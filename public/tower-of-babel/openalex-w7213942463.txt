Abstract
Some schizotypal-affective boundary presentations are clinically recognizable, yet difficult to place within DSM-5TR or ICD-11 categories. Patients who simultaneously show ego-structural fragility, concrete-mechanistic reasoning,
affective dysregulation, developmental adversity, and bizarre persecutory ideation may only be partially captured by
existing diagnostic categories considered in isolation. This narrative review aims to propose and discuss a hypothesisgenerating four-axis dimensional model — encompassing structural ego fragility, a relational causality deficit,
limbic/affective dysregulation, and developmental potentiation — that may help explain the formation of mechanistictechnological delusions in schizotypal-affective boundary presentations, offering an integrative but empirically
testable framework grounded in phenomenological psychopathology and contemporary social-cognitive and affective
neuroscience, rather than a validated diagnostic entity, case report, treatment guideline, or substitute for established
nosology. We propose a four-axis heuristic comprising: (I) structural ego fragility (Ich-Schwäche) related to impaired
consolidation of the secondary self; (II) a putative deficit in relational causality, involving impaired social-intentional
inference with possible mirror-neuron-system involvement; (III) limbic hyperactivation and thymic dysregulation
generating an anxiety–perplexity–paranoia cascade; and (IV) early adversity and protracted polysubstance use as
developmental potentiators. These axes are treated as interacting vulnerability dimensions, and their convergence is
proposed to produce a perplexity state requiring cognitive closure, assembled from the individual's premorbid
cognitive vocabulary when relational templates are unavailable. The resulting delusional content is formally coherent
but clinically bizarre and resistant to correction. The framework is offered as a testable hypothesis for future cohort,
phenomenological, neurocognitive, and treatment-response studies, not as a validated diagnostic entity or substitute
for established nosology.

1. Introduction
A history of psychiatric nosology is defined by a persistent tension between the pragmatic utility of categorical
diagnoses and the clinical reality of patients whose presentations cross rigid classification boundaries. The DSM-5-TR and
ICD-11 incorporate dimensional and spectrum-relevant elements, yet clinical use often requires decisions among categories
whose boundaries may be less distinct in practice than in formal criteria (American Psychiatric Association, 2022; World
Health Organization, 2019). Heterogeneity within diagnostic categories, as well as overlap across schizophrenia-spectrum,
affective, personality, and neurodevelopmental domains, has motivated transdiagnostic approaches such as the NIMH
Research Domain Criteria program (Cuthbert & Kozak, 2022; Insel et al., 2010).
A particularly demanding subgroup consists of patients with chronic persecutory ideation of bizarre or technologically
mediated content, preserved general cognition, partial or episodic occupational functioning, affective dysregulation, and
evidence of personality or self-structural disorganization. Schizophrenia, bipolar disorder with psychotic features,
schizoaffective disorder, delusional disorder, and schizotypal personality disorder may capture part of the presentation, but
none necessarily captures the organization of the whole (American Psychiatric Association, 2022; Murru & Carpiniello, 2018;
Tandon et al., 2009). Recent evidence on psychosis continua, transdiagnostic neurobiological markers, and the predictive
validity of schizotypy supports the clinical value of dimensional formulations (Barrantes-Vidal & Kwapil, 2025; Merola et al.,
2026; Saccaro et al., 2024).

Additional complexity arises when patients show long-standing interests or cognitive styles suggestive of schizotypal
or autistic spectrum traits before overt psychopathology. Circumscribed interests in mechanics, technology, astronomy, digital
systems, and bodily mechanisms may serve both as ordinary identity materials and, later, as the vocabulary through which
delusional content is constructed. The present review therefore asks not only which category is the most accurate, but also what
structure of psychic organization generates the symptomatic configuration.
A psychopathological approach grounded in the ontogeny of the self, phenomenology of delusion, and neuroscience
of social cognition offers one possible framework. Classical Continental psychopathology, including Jaspers' formal analysis
of experience and Conrad's account of nascent psychosis, can be placed in dialogue with contemporary works on anomalous
self-experience, attachment, aberrant salience, social cognition, and psychosis-spectrum dimensionality (Jaspers, 1963;
Conrad, 1958; Parnas & Henriksen, 2014; Fonagy & Target, 1997; Kapur, 2003; Garety et al., 2001). The following sections
articulate this dialogue as a four-axis dimensional model.
At the schizotypal-affective boundary, standard categories may be insufficient when each diagnosis explains only a
fragment of the clinical organization. A diagnosis of schizophrenia may be incomplete when general cognition and elements of
occupational function have been preserved over long periods and negative symptoms are minimal. Bipolar disorders with
psychotic features may be incomplete when persecutory ideation persists outside discrete mood episodes. Schizoaffective
disorder may not fully account for cases in which psychotic ideation appears continuous and structurally embedded rather than
episodically separable according to DSM-5-TR temporal criteria. Delusional disorders may be insufficient when beliefs are
explicitly bizarre, multidomain, and linked to broader self-structural disturbances (American Psychiatric Association, 2022;
Tandon et al., 2009).
A possible phenomenological marker of self-structural pathology is what we term structural dilettantism: the repeated
initiation and abandonment of academic, vocational, literary, artistic, or technical projects. In this formulation, structural
dilettantism does not indicate low intelligence. Rather, it may indicate difficulty in sustaining a stable secondary self capable of
micro-level routine, temporal continuity, and commitment. The patient may compensate through serial macro-projects that
function as external scaffolds for internal self-governance. This hypothesis helps explain the paradox of preserved higher
abilities alongside profound instability in employment, adherence, and long-term role continuity.
The aim of this review is therefore to propose and discuss a hypothesis-generating four-axis dimensional model —
encompassing structural ego fragility, a relational causality deficit, limbic/affective dysregulation, and developmental
potentiation — that may help explain the formation of mechanistic-technological delusions in schizotypal-affective boundary
presentations, offering an integrative but empirically testable framework grounded in phenomenological psychopathology and
contemporary social-cognitive and affective neuroscience, rather than a validated diagnostic entity, case report, treatment
guideline, or substitute for established nosology.

2. Methodology
A qualitative study (Risemberg et al., 2026a; Pereira et al., 2018) was conducted, characterized by limited
systematization and a narrative literature review approach (Ogassawara et al., 2025).
This article is a conceptual narrative review and theoretical synthesis consistent with the Review Article format. The
central question is: can mechanistic-technological delusions arising in schizotypal-affective boundary presentations be
understood through an integrated dimensional model linking self-structure, social inference, affective dysregulation, and
developmental adversity? The review does not attempt to estimate prevalence, effect size, diagnostic accuracy, or treatment

Targeted literature searches were performed and updated through May 2026 using PubMed, Scopus, and Google

and (d) aberrant salience, predictive coding in psychosis, limbic hyperactivation, childhood adversity and psychosis,

supplied the foundational constructs directly operationalized in the model.
Inclusion was guided by conceptual relevance to one of four domains: phenomenological psychopathology of self and
delusion, developmental and attachment-based self-organization, social-cognitive mechanisms relevant to interpersonal
inference, and affective/neurobiological models of psychosis and spectrum vulnerability. Peer-reviewed empirical studies,
systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and recognized classical sources were prioritized. Excluded sources were opinion pieces
without conceptual relevance to the proposed axes, studies focused exclusively on unrelated psychotic subtypes, and sources
whose claims could not be linked to the model's explanatory problem. Because the aim was theoretical integration across
heterogeneous literature, the synthesis was organized around explanatory convergence, conceptual coherence, and clinical
falsifiability rather than quantitative aggregation. No systematic review methodology, formal risk-of-bias assessment, or
PRISMA-based screening procedure was applied; therefore, the article makes no claim to exhaustive coverage of any literature
stream.
To minimize overstatement, the proposed model distinguishes established evidence from integrative inference and
speculative but testable propositions. The four axes are grounded in independent literature, but their interaction as a unified
model has not yet been prospectively validated. Throughout the manuscript, terms such as may, putative, heuristic, and
hypothesis are used deliberately to mark the difference between literature-supported constructs and the authors' integrative
proposal.

3.1 Axis I: Structural ego fragility and the secondary self
Ich-Schwäche, or structural weakness of the secondary self, designates the fragility of the reflexive and metacognitive
layers of identity that sustain self-observation, reality testing, affective regulation, and continuity across time (Jaspers, 1963).
Under ordinary developmental conditions, early attachment relationships help consolidate this layer by mirroring, naming, and
contextualizing affective and perceptual states (Fonagy & Target, 1997). When this process is disrupted by adversity,
dysregulated caregiving, or constitutional vulnerability, the self-world boundary may remain porous, and stimulus processing
is insufficiently buffered (Blatt & Lerner, 1983).
Clinical expressions may include identity diffusion, stimulus hypersensitivity, affective dysregulation, poor illness
awareness, and readiness to elaborate paranoid explanations under stress. Parnas and Henriksen's construct of ipseity

3.2 Axis II: Relational causality deficit and social inference
The mirror neuron system, involving inferior frontal and parietal regions, has been implicated in action recognition,
affective resonance, and aspects of social cognition (Iacoboni & Dapretto, 2006; Rizzolatti & Craighero, 2004). Because the
degree to which mirror neuron system activity specifically explains human theory of mind remains debated, we cautiously use
MNS-related language. In this model, the central clinical construct is broader than MNS dysfunction: a deficit in relational
causality, meaning a reduced availability of social-intentional templates by which interpersonal events are assigned plausible
causes.
When relational-causal learning proceeds adequately, children acquire implicit templates for interpreting others ("I
cried because I was hungry; my mother came because she heard me"), and later, "my brother said X because he feels Y." When
such templates are weak, unavailable, or overridden by hyperarousal, formal technological reasoning may fill the explanatory
gap — for example, "my brother has IT expertise; IT expertise enables remote access; remote access enables surveillance;
therefore he is monitoring me." Each inferential step may be locally intelligible; the deficit lies in interpersonal probability
weighting (Baron-Cohen et al., 1985; Corcoran et al., 1995; Garety et al., 2001). Evidence for MNS involvement in
schizophrenia-spectrum conditions is relevant but not decisive; a systematic review and qualitative meta-analysis support
altered mirror mechanisms in schizophrenia, while also leaving open broader social-cognitive interpretations (Valizadeh et al.,
2022).
3.3 Axis III: Limbic hyperactivation and the anxiety–perplexity–paranoia cascade
An inadequately consolidated secondary self may be less able to buffer sensory and interpersonal salience, resulting in
hypervigilance and primary anxiety. This state can resemble Conrad's Trema, a perplexity state in which the world feels
charged with imminent but not yet identifiable significance (Conrad, 1958). The psychic apparatus then presents an
explanation. In persons with adequate relational templates, explanations may remain interpersonal and corrigible; in those with
impaired relational causality, closure may be assembled from mechanistic or technological concepts.
Kapur's aberrant salience framework offers a complementary account: dysregulated dopaminergic transmission
assigns inappropriate motivational salience to neutral stimuli, and delusion formation represents an attempt to explain this
anomalous experience (Kapur, 2003). In the present model, Axis I contributes to inadequate buffering, Axis II shapes the form
and content of explanation, and Axis III supplies affective urgency. Once established, paranoid appraisal may intensify
anxiety, deepen perplexity, and drive further elaboration (Freeman & Garety, 2014), as illustrated in Figure 2.
Biological and iatrogenic factors may modulate Axis III, but this review does not treat them as established causal
components of the four-axis model unless directly supported by future studies. Sleep dysregulation, hormonal state,
anticholinergic burden, sedative use, and medication interactions should be clinically considered as potential modifiers of
arousal, cognition, and treatment tolerability. Specific claims regarding hyperandrogenism or individual drug interactions
should be tested rather than assumed.

3.4 Axis IV: Early adversity and polysubstance use as developmental potentiators
The fourth axis captures the compounding role of early adversity and protracted polysubstance use. Early adversity
may compromise attachment-based self-consolidation and affect neurodevelopmental processes relevant to prefrontal-limbic
regulation (Andersen, 2022; Frith, 2005). Longitudinal work has linked childhood adversity, identity disturbance, and later
psychosis symptoms, supporting the idea that self-organization may mediate part of the adversity-psychosis association
(Janssens et al., 2025).
Polysubstance use beginning in early adolescence may increase developmental risk. In some schizotypal or boundary
presentations, substances may be pursued not only for hedonic escape but also for the exploration of altered states of
consciousness, reflecting boundary porosity and curiosity regarding unusual experiences. Regardless of motivation, repeated
substance exposure during adolescence may lower the threshold for psychotic elaboration and undermine developmental
consolidation of self-regulation.
3.5 Convergence: perplexity, closure, and delusional content
The four axes are hypothesized to converge on a perplexity state characterized by disturbed self-world boundaries,
hypercharged salience, and an urgent demand for cognitive closure. The content of mechanistic-technological delusions —
chip implantation, satellite surveillance, IT-enabled remote access, electromagnetic manipulation, or bodily mechanical control
— should not be treated arbitrarily. The formal structure of delusions may be shaped by a psychotic mechanism, but its
specific imagery is often drawn from the patient's premorbid cognitive world (Schneider, 1959; Maher, 1974).
A technology-oriented patient may therefore produce technological delusions, while another patient may elaborate
genetic, religious, electromagnetic, or political explanations. Phenomenological analysis should attend not only to conviction
and falsity but also to the symbolic and cognitive materials from which delusional closure is built.

3.7 Clinical hypotheses and implications for future testing
Pharmacological implications as hypotheses, not guidelines
The model suggests that affective arousal, salience dysregulation, and thymic instability may be important treatment
targets for selected spectrum-boundary presentations. However, this is a clinical hypothesis rather than a treatment

recommendation. Evidence supports antipsychotic efficacy in schizophrenia-spectrum conditions and maintenance treatment,
but existing network meta-analyses have not validated the specific four-axis subgroup proposed here (Leucht et al., 2013;
Schneider-Thoma et al., 2022). Medication choice should therefore remain individualized, guideline-concordant, and based on
diagnosis, symptom profile, tolerability, adherence risk, comorbidity, and shared clinical judgment.
Long-acting antipsychotic formulations may be considered when adherence is compromised, whereas mood
stabilization may be relevant when affective or bipolar-spectrum dysregulation is prominent. The present framework does not
establish the superiority of any specific antipsychotic or mood stabilizer. Similarly, low-dose strategies, anticholinergic burden,
sedative co-medication, and hormonal or sleep-related modifiers should be systematically investigated rather than treated as
established mechanisms.
Psychotherapy: Axis I and relational causality
Structural ego-fragility is unlikely to be corrected by pharmacology alone. Psychotherapy therefore remains central,
particularly when it supports continuity of self, reflective function, interpersonal probability testing, and alliance formation
(Morrison et al., 2004). Direct confrontation of delusional convictions is often clinically unhelpful and may intensify defensive
or paranoid responses. A more useful strategy may begin by respecting the patient's cognitive style, while gradually expanding
the range of interpersonal explanations available.
A clinically important distinction concerns constitutional concrete-mechanistic cognition versus state-dependent
concreteness secondary to hyperarousal. Both may involve rule-based interactions, reduced spontaneity, and formal reasoning.
If warmth, initiative, and flexible social engagement emerge after stabilization, the presentation may include a reversible state
component with important prognostic implications (Attwood, 2007).
Brazilian context: dimensional formulation in resource-constrained settings
The Brazilian mental health context makes dimensional formulations clinically relevant. Brazil has a high burden of
anxiety and depressive disorders (GBD 2019 Mental Disorders Collaborators, 2022), and psychiatric reform has expanded
community-based CAPS networks while leaving ongoing challenges for severe and boundary presentations requiring
longitudinal formulation (Cordeiro et al., 2023; Loch et al., 2024). A four-axis formulation may help clinicians preserve
continuity across admissions, outpatient follow-up, family work, and community-based rehabilitation. This contextual
argument does not validate the model but indicates where it might be practically useful.
3.8 Alternative explanations and boundary conditions
Several alternative explanations should be considered. First, mechanistic-technological content may reflect cultural
availability rather than a specific relational-causality deficit: contemporary societies provide technological metaphors for
surveillance, control, contamination, and bodily intrusion, so technological delusions may emerge even without a four-axis
configuration. Second, predictive coding and Bayesian accounts may explain delusional convictions through altered precision
weighting without requiring a specific MNS-related mechanism (Kapur, 2003). Third, trauma-based models may interpret
persecutory ideation primarily as threat anticipation shaped by adversity rather than as a self-structured phenomenon

social reasoning independent of psychosis (Attwood, 2007).

3.9 From heuristic formulation towards empirical validation
The four-axis model requires prospective validation before it can have diagnostic or treatment-guiding significance.
Future work should develop or combine dimensional instruments to assess secondary-self consolidation, relational causality,
limbic/salience reactivity, and developmental loading. Existing instruments provide partial coverage: the Examination of
Anomalous Self-Experience may inform Axis I assessment; social-cognition batteries may inform Axis II; affective reactivity
paradigms and symptom monitoring may inform Axis III; and structured developmental histories may inform Axis IV.
Operational definitions should be specified before outcome testing, and inter-rater reliability should be assessed in
diagnostically heterogeneous samples.
Validation should proceed through longitudinal cohort studies of spectrum-boundary presentations, testing whether
four-axis profiles predict delusional content, categorical instability, treatment adherence, alliance formation, relapse patterns,
and functional outcomes above and beyond established diagnoses and symptom severity measures. Neurobiological studies can
examine social-cognition tasks, functional neuroimaging, diffusion imaging, and biomarkers relevant to stress, hormonal
modulation, and neuroinflammation. Cross-cultural studies are also needed to determine whether mechanistic-technological
content reflects the proposed configuration, local cultural vocabulary, or both. Treatment-response studies should be framed as
exploratory and hypothesis-generating until replicated.

3.10 Limitations
This study has several limitations. First, this model is a conceptual synthesis and theoretical proposal, not a validated
empirical framework; the axes are grounded in independent literature, but their interactions, as described, have not been tested
in cohort studies. Second, the MNS framework remains debated; the model therefore uses MNS as a possible contributor to
broader social-cognition impairment rather than as a precise neural mechanism. Third, relational causality deficit and structural
dilettantism require operational definitions, psychometric development, inter-rater reliability testing, and demonstration of
discriminant validity against existing constructs such as schizotypy, anomalous self-experience, autistic-spectrum cognition,
trauma-related threat anticipation, and predictive-coding accounts of delusion. Fourth, the pharmacological implications are
hypotheses and not practical guidelines. Fifth, the model was developed in a Brazilian clinical and educational context, and its
cross-cultural applicability remains unknown. Sixth, because no patient-level illustrative material was presented, the model
should be read as a literature-based conceptual framework requiring empirical validation rather than as evidence drawn from a
single case. Seventh, as a narrative review, the study may have been affected by selection bias and did not provide quantitative
evidence of prevalence, predictive validity, or treatment response.

4. Conclusion
This review proposes a hypothesis-generating four-axis dimensional model of psychopathological organization in
schizotypal-affective boundary presentations. The model integrates structural ego fragility, relational causality impairment,
affective/salience dysregulation, and developmental potentiation as interacting vulnerability dimensions that may converge on
mechanistic-technological delusional elaboration. Its central contribution is to formulate why, in selected boundary
presentations, particular delusional contents may be selected from the patient's premorbid cognitive world, rather than to
establish a new diagnosis or validated mechanism.
The proposed four-axis model should therefore be regarded as a heuristic framework requiring empirical validation
rather than as a diagnostic category, treatment algorithm, or substitute for established nosology. Its value depends on whether
future studies can operationalize each axis, distinguish the model from alternative explanations, and show that four-axis
profiles predict clinically meaningful outcomes beyond existing diagnostic and dimensional measures. If validated, the
framework may contribute to more precise dimensional formulation, improved longitudinal continuity of care, and a more
nuanced integration of phenomenological psychopathology with contemporary social-cognitive and affective neuroscience.
