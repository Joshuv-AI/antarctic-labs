Seeing is believing: visualizing spatiotemporal lipophagic flux in vivo with the tfLiveDrop reporter mice
Authors: Siqiao Gong, Chen Yang, Huafeng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLipophagy, the selective autophagic degradation of lipid droplets (LDs), is central to cellular lipid homeostasis, yet its dynamic regulation in living organisms has remained largely unobservable in real time. To overcome this barrier, we recently developed the mCherry‑eGFP‑LiveDrop (tfLiveDrop) reporter mice, in which a pH‑sensitive tandem fluorescent probe targeted to LDs via the GPAT4‑derived LiveDrop domain enables real‑time, single‑cell visualization of lipophagic flux in vivo. Using tfLiveDrop mice, we uncovered pronounced organ heterogeneity in basal lipophagy, identified an organ‑specific lipophagic inhibition in type 2 diabetes, and revealed a previously unrecognized developmentally programmed lipophagy induction that drives renal metabolic maturation. Together, these findings demonstrate that tfLiveDrop is a sensitive and versatile reporter for in vivo lipophagy research.

Lipophagy involves the sequestration of lipid droplets (LDs) into autophagosomes and their subsequent delivery to lysosomes for degradation. Its dysfunction has been linked to steatosis, diabetic complications, atherosclerosis, and neurodegeneration. Despite its importance, studying lipophagy in living organisms has been remarkably challenging. Conventional approaches, like electron microscopy, immunofluorescence, and bulk LD staining, provide only static snapshots and cannot visualize the dynamic process of lipophagic flux. A genetically encoded, sensitive reporter capable of dynamically visualize lipophagic flux at cellular resolution in vivo was urgently needed.

To address this gap, we generated the tandem fluorescent LiveDrop (tfLiveDrop) reporter mouse [1]. Similarly to the pioneering system developed to measure mitophagy in vivo, in mice, the design leveraged two key principles. First, we fused a pH‑sensitive mCherry‑eGFP tandem fluorophore to the LiveDrop domain, a compact LD‑targeting motif derived from GPAT4 (glycerol‑3‑phosphate acyltransferase 4) that anchors the fused protein to the LD surface without perturbing endogenous LD dynamics (Figure 1(A)). Second, the construct was placed under a Cre‑loxP‑dependent CAG promoter and knocked into the Rosa26 safe harbor locus, enabling cell‑type‑specific or systemic expression. The readout is straightforward: at neutral pH, i.e., cytosolic LDs or autophagosome‑encapsulated LDs, both mCherry and eGFP emit fluorescence, appearing as yellow puncta. Upon autophagosome-lysosome fusion, the acidic lysosomal environment quenches the fluorescence signal of eGFP while the one of mCherry remains stable, producing red‑only puncta (Figure 1(A)). Using tfLiveDrop, we mapped basal lipophagic activity across adult mouse organs. Hepatocytes displayed red puncta, reflecting a constitutive lipophagy flux consistent with the liver’s central role in lipid handling. Intestinal crypts, the stem cell niche, also showed clear activity. In striking contrast, kidney, heart, lung, and brain exhibit minimal basal lipophagy under chow diet. We interpret this pattern as indicating that lipophagy acts as a constitutive housekeeper in metabolically active tissues but remains largely quiescent in others, functioning as an “on‑demand” stress response (Figure 1(B, ⅰ).

We then subjected tfLiveDrop mice to a high‑fat diet/streptozotocin‑induced type 2 diabetes (T2D) model (Figure 1B, ⅱ). Remarkably, diabetic mice developed an organ‑specific lipophagic defect. In the liver and kidney, two key organs affected by diabetic lipotoxicity, massive yellow puncta failed to transition to red, alongside lost basal red-only signals. This disconnect between lipid load and clearance capacity underscores a true impairment of net lipophagic flux, independent of puncta density. By contrast, heart, brain, lung, and small intestine maintained predominantly red puncta, demonstrating intact flux. The yellow puncta in the liver and kidney colocalized with the LD coat protein PLIN2 and correlated with pathological lipid accumulation. These findings provide the first direct in vivo evidence that T2D impairs lipophagic flux in a tissue‑specific manner, identifying the liver and kidney as primary sites where massive LD biosynthesis couples with downstream clearance stagnation. We speculate that this block may arise from impaired autophagosome–lysosome fusion or lysosomal dysfunction, a hypothesis now testable using the tfLiveDrop mouse model.

Perhaps the most unexpected discovery came from examining renal development (Figure 1B, ⅲ). Using the tfLiveDrop mice, we identified a transient perinatal induction of lipophagic activity. At embryonic day 15.5, no reporter signal was detected in the kidney. At late embryonic and early postnatal stages (E18.5–P0), abundant yellow puncta appeared in AQP1 (aquaporin 1)-positive proximal tubules, by postnatal week 1, these puncta had predominantly converted to red. This progression demonstrates that developmental LD biogenesis is efficiently coupled to lipophagic clearance, rather than representing pathological accumulation. In adulthood, activity returns to baseline. A similar but weaker trend was observed in glomeruli, which are characterized by lower fatty acid metabolism. This programmed lipophagy induction coincided with the known metabolic switch of the developing kidney from glycolysis to fatty acid oxidation. Because cytosolic lipolytic enzymes are minimally expressed during embryogenesis, we propose that lipophagy serves as a critical compensatory pathway to clear perinatal lipid loads and support the energetic demands for tubular maturation. This finding broadens our understanding of how selective types of autophagy contributes to mammalian developmental programming, as observed for mitophagy and ER-phagy as well lipophagy in neutrophil differentiation.

Nevertheless, certain limitations of tfLiveDrop require consideration. First, it tracks cumulative flux but cannot differentiate selective lipophagy from bulk autophagic capture. Second, key lipid-metabolizing compartments, such as adipose tissues, have not yet been evaluated. Finally, since yellow puncta accumulation integrates both de novo LD biogenesis and flux inhibition, future investigations with autophagy inhibitors are required to fully decouple metabolic loading from clearance defects.

In conclusion, the tfLiveDrop mouse model enables spatially and temporally resolved visualization of lipophagic flux in living animals. Our findings reveal pronounced organ heterogeneity in basal lipophagy, an organ‑specific flux inhibition in T2D, and a developmentally programmed lipophagy induction that drives renal metabolic maturation. In future, the tfLiveDrop platform could be leveraged as a high‑content screening tool for identifying small‑molecule modulators of lipophagic flux. Furthermore, the ratiometric mCherry/eGFP readout is compatible with fluorescence‑activated cell sorting (FACS), enabling isolation of cell subpopulations based on their functional lipophagic state for downstream single‑cell multi‑omics analyses. The Cre‑dependent design also allows for systematic investigation of sex-dependent differences in lipophagy, as well as its contributions to organ development and metabolic diseases such as diabetic nephropathy and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis.
