A narrative review of mental health interventions and biopsychosocial risk factors in adolescent athletes
Authors: Sandeep Saroha, Deepak Bangari, Yuvraj Singh, Chandrabhan Seniya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adolescence is an important stage of development marked by quick changes in the brain and emotions. During this time, young people become more vulnerable to mental health issues. Taking part in organized sports can help build resilience, social connections, and self-esteem; however, new evidence shows that it might also create psychological stress. This review looks at mental health outcomes in adolescent athletes aged 12–19, based on peer-reviewed studies published from January 2000 to December 2025. We included and summarized 45 studies using a biopsychosocial and ecological approach to find key risk factors, protective mechanisms, and intervention strategies. The findings indicate that adolescent athletes face a variety of mental health challenges, such as anxiety, depression, burnout, and disordered eating. Reported rates indicate higher levels of anxiety (about 40–50%), disordered eating (up to 42% in aesthetic sports), and burnout (35–45% in endurance sports), while lower rates of anxiety and depression are seen in team sports (around 10–20%). Common risk factors include performance pressure, fear of failure, early specialization in sports, overtraining, injury-related identity changes, unhealthy perfectionism, controlling coaching styles, and body image issues. On the other hand, protective factors include supportive coaching, a strong team environment, family support, a balanced athletic identity, and good coping skills. In summary, organized sports can both pose risks and offer protection for young people’s mental health. This underscores the importance of developing comprehensive prevention and intervention strategies.

Introduction

Adolescence is an important stage of human development characterized by intense somatic growth, ongoing neurobiological development, and extensive psychosocial change. During this phase, stable patterns of identity development, autonomy, emotion regulation, and behavioural control emerge. Neurodevelopmental studies have shown that limbic and reward-related systems develop in advance of the prefrontal executive system, leading to a transient mismatch that increases emotional sensitivity, novelty-seeking behaviour, and stress vulnerability [1, 2]. This vulnerability, in part, accounts for the emergence of many prevalent mental health disorders, such as anxiety and mood disorders, before the age of early adulthood [3]. Thus, adolescence is both a time of vulnerability and a critical opportunity for preventive mental health care. Engaging in organized sports often occurs around these maturational events, placing sports as a highly influential environment for adolescent mental health [4, 5]. Sport offers organized opportunities for peer interaction, skill building, competence enhancement, teamwork, and social belonging. Large-scale population research suggests that participation in youth sport is linked to reduced depressive symptoms, improved self-esteem, and increased perceptions of social support [6–8]. In positive environments, sport builds resilience, coping skills, and character that generalize well beyond athletic contexts [9–11]. Modern conceptualization of positive youth development, therefore, positions sport not simply as competition but as a context for holistic growth. However, it is the same environments that offer benefits that also introduce substantial psychological challenges. The competitive sport environment is often characterised by intensive training programmes, frequent performance assessments, and constant comparison with peers, which together create sustained evaluative pressure that may contribute to performance anxiety driven by fear of failure. Research on training load indicates that cumulative fatigue, sleep disruption, and poor recovery contribute to both physiological and emotional dysregulation [12, 13]. Family expectations, particularly those from parents, can contribute to pressure experienced by adolescent athletes [14, 15]. It has been increasingly observed that young athletes experience various levels of anxiety, emotional distress, and depressive symptoms comparable to or, in some cases, higher than those of non-athletes [16, 17]. Psychological outcomes in youth sport are also affected by contextual and relational influences [18]. Additionally, parental expectations, coaching behaviour, organizational factors, and the growing role of digital and social media influence how adolescents perceive success and failure. Self-determination and motivational environment research indicate that controlling, outcome-orientated coaching is linked to emotional distress and poor well-being. In contrast, autonomy-supportive and mastery-orientated motivational environments may lead to intrinsic motivation and good psychological health [19–22]. Unattended pressures can impair healthy development and contribute to the risk of anxiety, depression, and maladaptive coping. Burnout is one of the most visible expressions of the imbalance between demands and recovery, which occurs when chronic stress exceeds coping resources [23]. It can be defined as emotional and physical exhaustion, feelings of reduced accomplishment, and sport devaluation [24]. High workload, perfectionism and controlling climate are important predictors of burnout symptoms in adolescents [25, 26]. Issues of similar concern have emerged in paediatric sports medicine, where specialization and year-round competition are associated with overtraining, injury risk, and mental exhaustion [27]. These results reinforce the interconnectedness of physical and psychological stress in a biopsychosocial model. Ecological and biopsychosocial approaches focus on the intersections of developmental risk and sport-specific relationships and practices [28, 29]. Disordered eating and relative energy deficiency in sport (RED-S) are considerations in aesthetic, endurance, and weight-class sports and have been reported to be higher in adolescent athletes than non-athletes. Weight management practices, performance standards, and body surveillance in sport cultures may normalize unhealthy behaviours and postpone the identification of symptoms [30, 31]. The trends suggest the need for systematic screening and early intervention at an organisational rather than individual level. The adolescent athlete’s mental health cannot be adequately conceptualised using individual-level variables. Therefore, biological, psychological, and environmental processes should be considered. For example, international consensus statements on mental health recommend multi-level strategies that include mental health education, early intervention, integrated psychological support, and collaborative responsibility between coaches, sports psychologists, and administrators [32].

This review paper integrates existing knowledge of mental health in adolescent athletes from a developmental and biopsychosocial perspective. Specifically, it examines the prevalence and nature of key psychological issues, including anxiety, depression, burnout, and disordered eating in sport; reviews biopsychosocial risk and protective factors spanning individual, relational, coaching, and sport-cultural domains; and integrates these findings into a conceptual framework explaining the interplay between developmental risk factors and sport demands. It also evaluates multi-level intervention and policy strategies to support the long-term well-being and performance of adolescent athletes. Further, this study aims to examine mental health challenges in adolescent athletes through a biopsychosocial and systems-based perspective. To achieve this, a qualitative thematic review approach was adopted to synthesise and critically interpret existing literature and identify key risk factors and research gaps, thereby informing the development of youth sports environments that support mental health and personal development.

Methodology of the review

Review design

This study was conducted as a structured narrative review incorporating systematic search and screening procedures to synthesise evidence on mental health in adolescent athletes. A narrative synthesis approach was adopted due to the methodological heterogeneity of the literature, including variation in study designs, outcome measures, and sport contexts. The review process was guided by PRISMA 2020 principles [33] to enhance transparency and reproducibility.

Databases and information sources

Electronic searches were conducted across Google Scholar, Taylor & Francis Online, ScienceDirect, and Wiley Online Library. These databases were selected to ensure broad multidisciplinary coverage of sport psychology, adolescent health, and sports medicine literature. Additional relevant studies were identified through manual screening of reference lists. The search included publications between January 2000 and December 2025 and was limited to peer-reviewed articles published in English.

Research strategy and inclusion criteria

The review includes original research articles, including longitudinal and intervention studies to understand mental health challenges among adolescent athletes. Peer-reviewed paper selection criteria

Study selection process

Study selection followed a multi-stage screening process. After removal of duplicates, titles and abstracts were screened for relevance, followed by full-text assessment based on predefined criteria. The search yielded 269 records, of which 59 duplicates were removed. Of the remaining 210 records, 111 were excluded during title and abstract screening. A full-text review of 98 articles resulted in the exclusion of 53 studies, with 45 retained for qualitative synthesis. Data extraction and synthesis

Data were extracted using a structured framework, including study characteristics and key findings. A qualitative thematic synthesis was conducted to identify recurring patterns across three domains: individual psychological factors, social and organisational influences, and broader sport-cultural contexts.

Cross-comparison across studies allowed an identification of recurring patterns, conceptual consistencies, methodological gaps, and emergent directions for future interdisciplinary research on adolescent athlete mental health.

Results and thematic synthesis

This section presents the findings of the qualitative thematic synthesis of the reviewed literature. The analysis identified several interrelated themes related to mental health in adolescent athletes. These themes include sport-specific stressors and biopsychosocial pathways that influence psychological outcomes. They are organized to reflect multi-level influences, such as individual, interpersonal, and sport-cultural factors. This arrangement highlights the complex interactions that shape mental health experiences in this group.

Sport-specific mental health risks and biopsychosocial pathways

Mental health challenges in adolescent athletes vary systematically across sport types, reflecting the interaction between developmental vulnerability and sport-specific demands. Anxiety is particularly prevalent in aesthetic and weight-class sports, where evaluative pressure, fear of failure, and maladaptive perfectionism are common [34]. Individual sports often show greater vulnerability to depressive symptoms than team-based, likely due to reduced peer buffering and heightened self-attribution of failure, whereas team-based ball sports typically demonstrate lower anxiety and depressive prevalence, with collective responsibility and social support functioning as protective factors [35, 36]. Depression frequently emerges following injury or prolonged absence from sport, especially when athletic identity is narrowly defined, and outcomes are further exacerbated by diminished social support. Endurance sports additionally present a moderate depressive risk associated with cumulative training fatigue and physiological strain. Furthermore, burnout represents a related but distinct consequence of these chronic stress exposures and is observed across all sports with highest rates in endurance and early-specialization contexts [24]. Excessive training loads, inadequate recovery, and monotony contribute to sustained emotional and physical exhaustion, while controlled coaching styles further heighten stress and disengagement. Disordered eating is most pronounced in the sport-specific pattern, particularly in aesthetic and weight-sensitive disciplines, where weight surveillance and appearance norms interact with pubertal maturation and body-shaping amplify risk [31]. Collectively, these patterns reinforce a biopsychosocial interpretation of adolescent athlete psychopathology. Moreover, biological strain, psychological characteristics, and social–cultural conditions do not operate independently; rather, they dynamically interact within sport environments that may either intensify or buffer vulnerability. Consequently, sport-sensitive screening, workload regulation, autonomy-supportive coaching, and targeted prevention strategies are essential for reducing anxiety, depression, burnout, and disordered eating across youth sport settings [37, 38]. Risk and protective factors influencing mental health in adolescent athletes

Studies included in this review suggest that performance anxiety is associated with factors such as unhealthy perfectionism, evaluative pressure, and expectations from parents and coaches [13, 24, 32]. Similarly, depression among adolescent athletes has been linked to injury experiences, social isolation, and a strong identification with the athletic role [13, 32]. Burnout has been reported in relation to overtraining, low autonomy, and controlling coaching environments [24, 27], while disordered eating behaviours are frequently associated with weight stigma, dieting culture, and appearance-related norms in sport [30, 46]. The thematic synthesis further indicates that mental health challenges arise not solely from individual vulnerabilities but from the interaction of psychological, environmental, and cultural factors. Evidence from the reviewed studies also suggests that sport structure and culture may influence psychological outcomes. For instance, sports emphasising individual accountability, aesthetic evaluation, or high physical demands have been associated with increased psychological distress, whereas team-based sports characterised by shared responsibility and social support may offer protective effects [13, 47, 48]. These findings highlight the importance of sport-specific screening, prevention strategies, and interventions that address both individual coping mechanisms and broader environmental influences to support the mental health of adolescent athletes.

Tables 2 and 3 suggest that sports structure and culture have an impact on mental health in adolescent athletes. Sports that demand individual accountability, aesthetic judgement or physical demand on athletes lead to higher-risk psychological distress, leading to depression. Whereas team sports that offer joint accountability and social support impose lower risk. The risk factors underscore the importance of sport-specific screening, prevention, and interventions that target both personal coping skills and environmental factors to protect the mental health of young athletes. These allocations are consistent with findings reported in the broader sport psychiatry and athlete mental health literature [49]. Athletes participating in sports that emphasize leanness, weight control, and subjective evaluation, such as gymnastics, wrestling, and figure skating, are at significantly greater risk of experiencing anxiety, depressive symptoms, and disordered eating compared to those involved in team sports [46]. Similarly, chronic training stress combined with limited improvement has been linked to emotional fatigue, reduced achievement and psychological fatigue, core features of athlete burnout [50]. Team-based sports often confer meaningful psychosocial protection by fostering a sense of belonging, shared responsibility, and consistent support from peers and coaches. Such environments help adolescents feel connected, valued, and autonomous, conditions that reduce perceived stress, strengthen coping capacity, and promote emotional well-being [51]. Empirical evidence indicates that autonomy-supportive coaching and cohesive peer relationships buffer psychological strain and are associated with lower levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms, underscoring the mental health benefits of collective sport participation [52]. In contrast, sporting contexts characterised by individual accountability, aesthetic judgement, or strict weight control may heighten self-scrutiny and performance pressure, increasing vulnerability to distress. Together, these observations highlight the need for targeted, developmentally sensitive prevention strategies, including balanced training and recovery cycles, regulation of weight-monitoring practices, routine mental health screening, and the integration of psychological support within youth sport systems.

Performance anxiety

Performance anxiety is a prevalent psychological concern among adolescent athletes and has been widely documented in the literature [13, 32]. It is commonly associated with cognitive and emotional factors such as fear of failure, maladaptive perfectionism, and heightened self-evaluative concerns [16–18], as well as external pressures arising from coaching behaviours, parental expectations, and competitive environments [14, 26, 53]. Empirical studies further indicate that motivational climate and coaching style play a significant role in shaping athletes’ anxiety responses, with controlling or ego-orientated environments exacerbating stress and performance-related worry [16, 34]. In adolescent populations, these pressures may be intensified due to developmental sensitivities related to identity formation, peer comparison, and social evaluation [54]. Collectively, the reviewed literature suggests that performance anxiety arises from the interaction of individual psychological characteristics and environmental demands within sport contexts, rather than from isolated factors alone.

Depression stigma

Depression in adolescent athletes is progressively recognised within sport psychology and sports medicine, yet it remains often underdiagnosed and undertreated. Signs are often masked by behavioural removal, fatigue, bad temper, or mood fluctuations and may be incorrectly attributed to overtraining, competitive stress, or prescriptive adolescent development [39, 55]. This symptomatic overlap can delay identification and intervention, allowing symptoms to progress unattended within sport settings. Many sport-specific and developmental stressors contribute to depressive vulnerability. Injury-related inactivity, extended social isolation, performance strain, identity foreclosure, and extreme athletic identity have all been recognized as significant risk factors [36, 40]. Adolescents who strongly describe self-worth among athletic success may experience declared psychological distress, ensuing setbacks or performance decay. Clinically, depressive presentations involve insistent sadness, anhedonia, focus problems, sleep disturbance, reduced motivation, disengagement from teammates, and, in severe cases, desperate ideation [56]. Such symptoms can damage both academic functioning and sport performance, heightening stress and reinforcing removal. Distinguishing clinical depression from transient adjustment difficulties or temporary fatigue is critical [53]. Contemporaneous suggestions emphasise active and systematic methods for early detection. Repetitive screening using validated tools such as the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), targeted psychoeducation for athletes and coaches, and the integration of sport psychologists inside athletic programmes are advocated to facilitate timely intervention, recovery, and resilience [57]. These plans position mental health monitoring as a requirement for safe and sustainable youth sport participation.

Burnout syndrome

Burnout is recognised as a main psychological syndrome in adolescent athletes and signifies a progressive, stress-related maladaptive reaction rather than an important event. It is a staged process beginning with high sport needs, including intensive training loads, performance pressure, and academic–sport conflict, which create continuing and unmanaged stress. Burnout is identified by three main dimensions: emotional and physical fatigue, sport devaluing (cynicism or detachment), and a reduced sense of achievement or efficiency [25]. When recovery is insufficient, this chronic stress accumulates and shows initially as emotional and physiological fatigue, improving to emotional exhaustion, monitored by cynicism and motivational disengagement, and eventually culminating in full burnout. This demand-stress-exhaustion pathway is consistent with stress–recovery and workload models in sport science, which present that sustained high training volume and unsatisfactory improvement raise allostatic load, disrupt sleep, and impair mood regulation [12, 51]. Empirical studies further identify controlling coaching climates, chronic evaluative pressure, perfectionistic concerns, and limited athlete autonomy as key psychosocial risk factors that accelerate this progression [26]. Similarly, reports that chronic imbalance between demands and coping capacity predicts emotional exhaustion and withdrawal behaviours in youth and elite athletes [24]. This pattern can also be understood through self-determination theory, which emphasises that young athletes stay motivated when they feel a sense of choice, confidence in their abilities, and connection with others. As a result, motivation gradually declines, enthusiasm fades, and some adolescents may emotionally detach or eventually withdraw from sport altogether. Protective factors, for example, resilience and coping skills, social support and team cohesion, autonomy-supportive coaching, adequate sleep and recovery can mitigate the impact of stress and facilitate psychological salvage. These factors determine whether athletes progress towards burnout or towards recovery. The biopsychosocial framework model emphasises the impact of environmental demands and resources on burnout and the importance of multi-level approaches in the prevention of psychological distress in youth sports. Evidence informed by self-determination theory suggests that young athletes are most likely to thrive when their sporting environments nurture three core psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Coaching styles that provide choice, acknowledge athletes’ perspectives, and cultivate supportive peer relationships help athletes feel valued and capable, which in turn promotes intrinsic motivation, sustained engagement, and emotional well-being. In contrast, overly controlling or pressurised climates, characterised by rigid expectations, excessive criticism, or limited athlete voice, can frustrate these needs, leading to emotional exhaustion, reduced motivation, and progressive disengagement from sport. Sufficient recovery time, sleep, and periodised training also reduce physiological strain and help maintain psychological functioning. In terms of prevention, burnout is a gradual process that may initially manifest as chronic fatigue, irritability, and enjoyment, followed by performance decline, depressive symptoms, sport withdrawal, and chronic disengagement from physical activity. Preventive approaches focus on balanced workload management, structured recovery, integrated psychological services, and regular screening using validated tools such as the Athlete Burnout Questionnaire [23]. These results support a biopsychosocial explanation in which both environmental needs and contextual supports determine whether adolescents adapt positively to sport stress or transition toward burnout (Fig. 3).

Multilevel determinants contributing to disordered eating risk among adolescent athletes

Evidence indicates that teenage changes, injury and fatigue, maladaptive perfectionism, external evaluation, and weight-focused norms converge to increase vulnerability to restrained eating, binge-purge behaviours, and compulsive exercise. These behaviours often result in low energy availability, impaired recovery, and decreased performance, which may further enhance body dissatisfaction and perceived pressure to control weight. Thus, disordered eating emerges as a self-reinforcing cycle, highlighting the need for multilevel preventive strategies rather than isolated individual interventions. Epidemiological research shows that disordered eating behaviours and clinical discord occur more often in athletic populations than in non-athletes. The risk appears to be particularly high among adolescent girls and in sports where performance is judged partly on appearance. Sociocultural body ideals, direct and indirect pressure from coaches, routine weight monitoring and internalised perfectionism may increase vulnerability to disordered eating [43]. These factors can lead to behaviour such as restrictive eating, purging, binge eating, excessive or compensatory exercise, and ongoing concern about body shape. Due to inadequate nutritional intake, behaviours can disrupt hormonal function, weaken bone health, reduce recovery, and affect long-term performance. They are also associated with psychological difficulties, including anxiety, low self-esteem, and depressive symptoms [49]. Research and expert consensus consistently stress the importance of early identification, as disordered eating behaviours are often normalised within sporting environments and may go unnoticed. To improve detection, routine screening with validated instruments such as the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q) is recommended. Structured psychoeducation for athletes, parents, and coaches can increase awareness of early warning signs and reduce stigma. When concerns arise, prompt referral to a multidisciplinary team, including physicians, dietitians, and mental health professionals, is considered best practice. This evidence supports the development of a proactive, sport-specific prevention approach that addresses individual risk factors and broader environmental contributors to eating pathology among adolescent athletes.

Conceptual model to manage mental health in adolescent athletes

Understanding mental health in adolescent athletes requires attention to multiple factors that shape development. Biological, psychological, social, and cultural influences do not operate independently; mental health reflects their ongoing interactions [61]. Adolescence is marked by neurobiological maturation, increased emotional sensitivity, and identity development. At the same time, young athletes face sport-specific demands, including intensive training, performance evaluation, and comparison with peers. These influences can create periods of both vulnerability and resilience. For this reason, athlete well-being cannot be explained solely by individual characteristics or isolated stressors but must be considered within the broader relational and orgnizational contexts in which sport takes place. The review, therefore, proposes an ecological-biopsychosocial model tailored to adolescent sport settings. The framework draws on Urie Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory (1979) and the contemporary biopsychosocial model of health and performance [62]. Within this approach, mental health is understood as arising from interactions among (i) biological processes, such as growth, fatigue, and injury; (ii) psychological processes, including coping skills, perfectionism, and self-concept; and (iii) social–environmental influences such as coaching style, peer norms, and family expectations. Sport culture, including training practices and organisational norms, shapes how these factors interact and may either reduce or amplify stress [63]. This perspective situates adolescent athletes within interconnected systems: the individual; immediate settings, teams and families; organisational structures, including clubs and schools; and the wider sociocultural environment. Research shows that supportive coaching, autonomy-promoting practices, and cohesive teams promote resilience and psychological safety. Whereas controlling coaching behaviours, early specialisation, and performance-focused cultures are linked to anxiety, burnout, and disordered eating. Viewing these influences together helps explain why athletes exposed to similar demands may experience different outcomes depending on context. Beyond the theoretical basis, this systems approach has practical implications. It is consistent with implementation frameworks that support multilevel, theory-informed interventions [64]. Effective strategies may therefore target individual skills, team climate and organisational policy simultaneously. By explicitly recognising the role of sport culture, this model provides a structured basis for prevention, early identification, and long-term mental health support in adolescent sport ecosystems.

Rationale for a biopsychosocial approach

Adolescence represents a sensitive period of development marked by substantial neurobiological, emotional, and psychosocial change. Ongoing brain maturation during this stage is characterised by earlier development of limbic and reward-related systems relative to prefrontal regions responsible for cognitive control and emotional regulation. This developmental imbalance is associated with heightened emotional reactivity, increased sensation seeking, and still-maturing executive functioning, which together may increase susceptibility to stress, anxiety, and mood-related difficulties. These vulnerabilities can become more pronounced when adolescents are situated in environments that emphasise evolution, comparison, and performance outcomes. Participation in competitive sport introduces a distinct set of demands that may interact with these normative developmental processes. Young athletes are routinely exposed to performance appraisal, intensive training programmes, injury risk, and, in some sports, pressures related to body weight or appearance [65]. When such sport-specific stressors coincide with developmental sensitivity, psychological strain may accumulate, and hence, emotional well-being is challenged. Mental health outcomes in adolescent athletes cannot be explained by any single risk or protective factor in isolation. Strong coping skills or resilience do not automatically ensure healthy adjustment, just as biological or genetic predispositions alone do not determine vulnerability. Rather, evidence increasingly supports an integrative biopsychosocial perspective in which biological sensitivity, cognitive emotional processes, and environmental contexts interact continuously over time to shape mental health trajectories. In practical terms, this means that young athletes’ well-being reflects the combined influence of their developing bodies, their thoughts and emotions, and the support or pressure they experience within their sporting and social environments. Such a dynamic view underscores the need for coordinated, multilevel approaches that address both the person and the context in which they train and compete [66]. For example, supportive coaching practices and strong relationships may attenuate stress responses, whereas controlled environments and excessive demands or limited social support may elevate risk, even among athletes who otherwise demonstrate high resilience. A biopsychosocial framework is therefore essential for understanding mental health in this population. Such an approach acknowledges that biological maturation, psychological characteristics, and social context operate dynamically and reciprocally to be competitive. Mental health arises from the combined effect of various interacting factors, not just one cause. Biological development, psychological skills, and social and sporting environments shape how young athletes see stress, handle challenges, and maintain well-being. This means that an athlete’s experiences on the field, their relationships with coaches and peers, and their ongoing growth all contribute to their emotional health. Therefore, effective support should include strategies that consider the whole athlete rather than focusing on individual symptoms.

Biological factors

Biological processes play a central role in adolescent mental health and are closely intertwined with the physical and performance demands of competitive sport. Puberty marked hormonal and somatic changes, including fluctuations in sex hormones such as oestrogen and testosterone, rapid growth, and changes in body composition [67]. These developmental shifts are commonly accompanied by increased emotional sensitivity, fluctuations in mood, and heightened body awareness. During a period already shaped by social comparison and identity development, such changes may increase variability to psychological distress. In sport settings, where physical appearance and performance are often closely monitored, these biological sensitivities can contribute to greater self-consciousness, stress reactivity, and related concerns. Injury is a particularly salient biological stressor for adolescent athletes. It is physical consequences, injury, that can disrupt an athlete’s sense of competence, belonging, and continuity, especially when self-worth is strongly linked to sports participation and achievement [68]. Extended time away from training and competition may weaken social connections and foster feelings of isolation, a process commonly described as athletic identity foreclosure. This experience has been associated with depressive symptoms, reduced self-esteem, and emotional withdrawal [69], indicating that effective rehabilitation requires attention to psychological as well as physical recovery. Overtraining provides a further example of how biological strain can influence mental health. Training loads that exceed an athlete’s capacity for recovery can disturb physiological regulation and result in persistent fatigue, irritability, sleep disruption, and declining performance [55]. These symptoms are frequently accompanied by elevated levels of stress, anxiety, and low mood, highlighting the close relationship between prolonged physiological stress and psychological functioning [70]. Genetic factors may also shape vulnerability during adolescence. A family history of mood or anxiety disorders has been associated with increased sensitivity to environmental and performance-related stressors in young athletes [71]. Developmental and performance-related stressors in young athletes’ transitions and environmental pressures can interact with inherited risk, increasing the likelihood of maladaptive outcomes [72]. These findings emphasise the importance of biological consideration within broader safeguarding efforts, including injury prevention strategies, structured rest and recovery, careful monitoring of training load, and awareness of family mental health [73]. Addressing biological factors alongside psychological and social influences is therefore essential for protecting the well-being of adolescent athletes.

Psychological factors

Psychological developments play a central role in deciding how adolescent athletes understand, assess, and respond to the demands of competitive sport. Mental health outcomes are not controlled merely by external stressors but are filtered through internal characteristics such as coping style, perfectionism, resilience, self-worth, and emotional regulation capacity. Differences in these psychological factors help explain why athletes exposed to comparable training loads or performance expectations can show very different patterns of adjustment or distress [74, 75]. Perfectionism is particularly prominent in high-performance and aesthetic sport contexts. Many adolescents develop rigid performance standards and come to link self-esteem closely with achievement, selection, or external validation [76]. This pattern is typically associated with self-criticism, fear of failure, and heightened concern over mistakes [77]. Research consistently shows that elevated perfectionistic concerns are associated with increased anxiety, emotional exhaustion, burnout, and disordered eating behaviours, especially in sports that emphasise weight, appearance, or technical precision [78, 79]. In their settings, efforts to maintain control and meet expectations may undermine psychological well-being. Coping strategies further distinguish vulnerability from resilience [80]. Athletes who rely primarily on avoidant or inflexible coping approaches tend to report greater stress and poorer psychological adjustment following challenges such as injury, non-selection, or competitive loss. In contrast, strategies, such as problem-focused coping, cognitive reappraisal, acceptance, and seeking social support, are associated with improved emotional regulation, quicker recovery from setbacks, and more stable mental health [81]. These findings suggest that coping flexibility is an important and modifiable target within youth sport intervention. Athletic identity also plays a role in psychological vulnerability [82]. Adolescents who define themselves almost exclusively through sport may experience heightened distress when participation or performance is disrupted, as such experiences threaten their broader sense of self [83]. By contrast, a more balanced identity that includes academic, social, and personal roles appears to provide protection and is associated with greater psychological resilience. Resilience and emotional adaptability moderate responses to competitive stress [84]. Athletes who adopt a growth-oriented approach and view challenges as part of the learning process tend to report levels of anxiety and burnout. Taken together, psychological characteristics operate in both risk-enhancing and protective ways. While maladaptive traits can increase vulnerability, adaptive skills support regulation and recovery. Strengthening these capacities through education, coaching practices, and structured psychological skills training, therefore, represents an important pathway for supporting sustainable mental health in adolescent athletes.

Social factors

Adolescence is a period in which social relationships and identity development are particularly salient, and the social environment plays a substantial role in shaping mental health. During this stage, young people become increasingly attentive to issues of belonging, acceptance, and evaluation, making their emotional well-being particularly responsive to interpersonal contexts. In sport settings, peer relationships, family influences, academic demands, and media exposure intersect to shape psychological outcomes, either increasing vulnerability or providing protection. Consistent with social identity and group-based models of mental health, adolescents derive meaning, confidence, and emotional stability from their social connections [85]. Peer groups often function as primary sources of belonging and everyday support. Positive team relationships can foster psychological safety, motivation, and shared purpose, which in turn support resilience and continued engagement in sport. In contrast, experiences of exclusion, hazing, bullying, or persistent rivalry, whether within teams or broader social settings, have been linked to higher levels of anxiety, depressive symptoms, and reduced self-esteem [86]. In competitive environments, frequent comparison with teammates may heighten feelings of inadequacy and threat, particularly among athletes who already exhibit high levels of self-criticism or perfectionism [87]. Parental and family contexts are similarly influential. Families that prioritize balanced development, enjoyment, and consistent emotional support tend to promote adaptive coping and psychological resilience. Excessive performance pressure, unrealistic expectations, or critical parental feedback can increase stress, undermining motivation and contributing to emotional distress [88, 89]. Family conflict or instability may further reduce the emotional resources available to adolescents as they manage the combined demands of sport and school. Academic commitments often intersect with athletic participation, adding to overall workload and fatigue. Educational settings that provide flexibility, acknowledge sporting commitments and offer accessible mental health support may help mitigate this strain. Highly competitive or inflexible school environments can exacerbate stress, burnout, and emotional exhaustion [90]. Digital and social media represent an additional influence in contemporary youth sport. Regular exposure to idealised representations of performance and appearance may contribute to increased self-criticism, perfectionism, and body dissatisfaction. Online scrutiny, public commentary, and social comparison can further intensify performance-related anxiety and emotional strain [91]. Supportive social networks act as protective factors. Cohesive teams, empathetic coaching practices, and positive peer and family relationships have been associated with lower levels of loneliness, more effective coping, and better emotional regulation [92]. Social environments can function either as sources of stress or as foundations for resilience, highlighting the importance of fostering inclusive, supportive, developmentally appropriate contexts within adolescent sport.

Sports culture

Sport culture encompasses the shared norms, values, beliefs, and practices through which athletes, coaches, and organisations define success, respond to adversity, and interpret psychological difficulty [93]. Rather than serving as a context, sport culture shapes how mental health concerns are recognised, discussed, and addressed. Research indicates that these cultural norms have a substantial influence on mental health outcomes in adolescent athletes, with routine interactions and implicit expectations often exerting a greater impact than formal policies or guidelines [94]. In high-pressure or unsupportive sport environments, where performance outcomes are prioritised over athlete well-being and emotional control is framed as a marker of toughness, mental health concerns are frequently minimised or stigmatised [95]. Practices such as public monitoring of body weight, authoritarian coaching, approaches, and conditional approval based primarily on performance can normalise fear of failure and constant self-monitoring. These conditions are associated with increased psychological strain and elevated risks of anxiety, burnout, and disordered eating. Over time, athletes in such environments may come to equate personal worth solely with performance outcomes, a pattern linked to maladaptive perfectionism, reduced intrinsic motivation, and heightened vulnerability to distress. By contrast, sport cultures that are developmentally supportive place greater emphasis on autonomy, inclusion, and long-term athlete development. Coaching practices that encourage athlete input, provide rationale for training demands, and allow open discussion of mental health concerns contribute to psychological safety and reduce barriers to help-seeking. Such environments support self-care, strengthen peer relationships, and facilitate the development of identities that extend beyond sport participation, thereby promoting resilience and emotional regulation [96]. Importantly, supportive sport cultures do not emerge by chance. They are shaped through leadership behaviours, organisational priorities, coach education, and the consistent modelling of athlete-centred values. Taken together, the evidence suggests that sport culture functions as a powerful contextual influence, with the capacity either to intensify psychological risk or to buffer vulnerability. As such, cultural change represents a critical and sustainable avenue for promoting mental health in adolescent sport settings.

Intervention strategies to manage mental health in adolescent athletes

Mental health difficulties in adolescent athletes arise from the interaction of biological development, psychological functioning, and the demands of contextual sport. As a result, prevention and intervention efforts cannot be limited to individual-level treatment alone. Approaches informed by biopsychosocial and system perspectives emphasise the need for coordinated strategies that reflect both developmental processes and the contexts in which young athletes train and compete. Given the multifactorial nature of mental health risk during adolescence, interventions must be developmentally appropriate, sensitive to sport-specific demands, and integrated within everyday sporting practice. Effective programmes are therefore best implemented within the sport setting and designed to address individual characteristics alongside team dynamics, organizational culture. In practical terms, this involves strengthening personal coping skills, supporting healthy relationships within teams, and establishing clear organisational safeguards. By combining individual, team, and policy-level actions, sport systems are better positioned to reduce psychological risk, support adaptive development, and promote sustainable well-being in adolescent athletes. Individual-level interventions

At the individual level, psychological interventions aim to strengthen coping skills, emotional regulation, and cognitive flexibility, enabling adolescent athletes to manage both developmental challenges and sport-related demands more effectively. CBT is the most extensively studied and widely implemented approach for reducing anxiety, depressive symptoms, and maladaptive perfectionism in youth and athletic populations [97]. Core CBT techniques, including cognitive restructuring, behavioural activation, graded exposure, self-monitoring, and problem-solving, can be adapted to sport contexts to address performance anxiety, negative self-talk, and rigid beliefs and achievement [98]. By targeting unhelpful thinking patterns and avoidance behaviours, these strategies support more balanced appraisals of competitive stress and promote emotional stability. Mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs), including Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) and Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR), have also received increasing empirical support in adolescent and elite sport settings. Evidence suggests that these interventions improve control, emotional awareness, and psychological flexibility while reducing rumination and performance-related anxiety [99]. Rather than focusing on symptom elimination, mindfulness approaches emphasise acceptance of internal experiences and engagement with valued actions. Incorporating brief mindfulness practices into regular training routines, rather than delivering them as standalone sessions, appears to improve uptake and long-term integration. Psychoeducation is an important complementary component of individual-level intervention. Structured education on mental health literacy, stress management, sleep nutrition, and recovery can help normalise emotional difficulties and reduce stigma associated with seeking support. Early identification using validated screening tools, together with clear referral pathways to appropriately trained professionals, further strengthens prevention and timely intervention. However, individual-focused approaches alone are unlikely to be sufficient when environmental stressors remain unchanged. Without parallel adjustments to coaching practices, training demands, and organisational expectations, gains achieved through individual interventions may not be sustained. Individual strategies are therefore most effective when implemented as part of a broader, coordinated framework.

Team-level interventions

The team environment plays a significant role in shaping how adolescent athletes experience and respond to sport-related pressure. Because young athletes spend much of their time in team settings, daily interactions, leadership behaviours and shared norms directly influence psychological safety and well-being. Team-level interventions, therefore, focus on modifying interpersonal dynamics and creating environments that support both performance and development. Autonomy-supportive coaching has emerged as one of the most consistently supported protective factors. Coaching behaviours that provide clear explanations, acknowledge athletes’ perspectives, encourage input and distinguish effort from personal worth are associated with greater intrinsic motivation and psychological security [100]. These practices align with self-determination principles and have been linked to improved well-being, stronger engagement, and reduced burnout risk. In contrast, controlling, punitive coaching approaches are associated with higher anxiety, emotional exhaustion, and disengagement from sport. The evidence suggests that leadership style is a modifiable influence on mental health rather than a neutral background factor. Peer relationships provide an additional source of support. Peer mentoring programmes, in which older or more experienced athletes support younger teammates, have been shown to enhance belonging, confidence, and social integration [101]. By sharing experiences and modelling adaptive coping, mentors can help normalise challenges and reduce feelings of isolation, particularly during transitions into more competitive environments. Team-based resilience programmes have also gained attention, particularly those that include shared goal setting, stress appraisal education, and group problem-solving. When embedded within routine training, adaptive coping norms strengthen cohesion and reduce stigma around help-seeking. Such approaches position the team environment as an important protective layer that moderates individual vulnerability.

Organizational and structural interventions

Organisational structures and policies play a decisive role in determining whether psychological risk is mitigated or allowed to accumulate. Individual coping skills and team-level plans are helpful; continued safety of adolescent mental health depends basically on organisational and policy-level liability. In other words, enduring change requires structural safeguards rather than reliance on athletes’ personal resilience. Sport organisations, therefore, take on a responsibility of care to design and implement clear policies that prioritise psychological well-being beside performance outcomes. At the essential level, suggested actions include implanting qualified or licensed mental health experts within teams or athletic departments to improve approach, continuity of care, and early intervention. Normalising regular mental health screening using validated tools, when shown ethically and privately and attended by recommendation pathways, helps timely identification of distress and reduces stigma-related difficulties to help-seeking. Workload regulation signifies another critical protective mechanism. Policies that systematically observe training volume, mandate recovery periods, and discourage early specialisation reduce the possibility of overtraining, injury, and burnout. Youth sport science indicates that extreme and hard training demands improve emotional exhaustion and dropout risk, but developmentally correct planning supports both performance and well-being. In weight-sensitive controls, removing public weigh-ins, preventing disciplinary weight-monitoring training, and adopting body-neutral or performance-based metrics can alleviate strains that contribute to body dissatisfaction and disordered eating. As a group, these measures signify a shift from reactive care to preventive design. Organizations adopting a “mental health in performance structure", where psychological well-being is given as integral rather than secondary to athletic success, stated improved athlete retention, engagement, trust, and long-term development. Attaining this cultural shift requires visible leadership responsibility, regular coach education, transparent defending procedures, and mechanisms that ensure policies are implemented consistently in daily practice.

Athlete-centred coping strategies

In addition to structured and system-level interventions, the reviewed literature highlights the importance of athlete-centred coping strategies in managing psychological stress among adolescent athletes. Psychological skills training represents a key approach in this regard, encompassing techniques such as relaxation (e.g., controlled breathing and progressive muscle relaxation), attentional control, and cognitive restructuring to regulate emotional and cognitive responses to performance demands. Evidence suggests that these strategies can help athletes manage anxiety, reduce maladaptive thought patterns such as fear of failure, and enhance self-regulation in competitive settings. Goal setting and self-monitoring further support adaptive coping by promoting a sense of control and competence. Within adolescent populations, the development of such skills is particularly important, given ongoing psychological and social maturation. Integrating athlete-level coping strategies alongside coaching, organisational, and clinical interventions may therefore contribute to a more comprehensive and sustainable approach to mental health in youth sport.

Policy and practice recommendation

Ecological recovery in adolescent athlete mental health cannot be attained amid individual-level interventions alone. While granting personal managing skills, counselling, and psychological therapies remain imperative, resilient change depends on the larger operational, institutional, and social stages that shape daily training and competition environments. Reliable with principles from health elevation research, expressive and fixed benefits appear when organization create protective settings rather than imposing the full load of change on young athletes themselves. Appropriately, interventions necessarily cover more than lonely athlete-focused plans to include matched policy and organisational amendment at the institutional and societal level. National governing bodies, schools, and sports clubs have not only an ethical duty but also a clear legal responsibility to safeguard the welfare of adolescents in organised sport. Embedding responsibility within formal governance structures, through clearly defined safeguarding guidelines, regulatory standards, and dedicated mental health protection policies, helps ensure that athlete well-being is treated as a core requirement of safe sport rather than an optional or supplementary consideration. By institutionalising these protections, organisations signal that psychological health is as essential as physical safety, creating environments where young athletes are consistently supported rather than relying on ad hoc or voluntary efforts.

Policy-level amendment is primary to implementing mental health safety inside youth sports organizations rather than handling it as a possible or responsive add-on. Basic duties are required to confirm that psychological well-being is spoken of beside physical conditioning and performance outcomes. A key initial point is the compulsory facility of mental health literacy practice for coaches, administrators, athletic trainers, and support staff. Such training provides adults who manage adolescent athletes with the skills to recognise primary caution indications, provide initial support, and stimulate suitable recommendation pathways. Discussion

Mental health outcomes in adolescent athletes should be interpreted as results of relating developmental and ecological practices alone or as signs of vulnerabilities. The synthesis accessible here shows that psychological functioning during adolescence is explained within a period of improved neurobiological sensitivity, self-formation, and social comparison, terms that inherently increase exposure to stress. When these regulating developmental challenges cross with the structured strains of competitive sport, mental health becomes mostly context-dependent [110]. Organised sport is regularly promoted as protective for youth well-being, but the current evidence suggests that its benefits are neither universal nor automatic. Sport seems to function as a conditional developmental setting, where outcomes depend mostly on how training environments are organised, and they adapt to adolescents’ psychological needs. The results of autonomy-supportive and relationally safe settings lead to improved self-esteem, belongingness, and emotional strength, while controlling and outcome-driven climates reduce risk for distress [8]. The reviewed literature shows that performance-related stress arises as a central helpful pathway. Adolescents are exclusively sensitive to evaluation and peer support, and useful sport amplifies these pressures through regular review by coaches, parents, judges, and digital audiences. This content study seems to intensify fear of failure and perfectionistic cognitions; tools are repeatedly connected with anxiety and motivational exhaustion. Chronic competitive anxiety can grow into burnout and final sport withdrawal [24], suggesting that unmanaged stress has values not only for well-being but also for long-term participation paths. From a developmental perspective, these results imply that youth sport methods prioritising performance over psychological safety can inadvertently weaken sustainability. The discussion of injury-related disorder supports the biopsychosocial nature of risk. Injury is not merely a physical effect but also a psychological and social rupture. Adolescents who strongly identify with the athlete role may experience injury as a risk to identity and go from casual to removal and depressive symptomatology [111]. This interpretation stresses the need to reconceptualise ’remedy’ as holistic management, including emotional and social help alongside physical recovery. Burnout and disordered eating illustrate how structural features of sport socialise over time. Burnout shows less as a sign of personal weakness than a signal of systemic difference, emerging when training loads, recovery deficits, and controlling coaching training exceed managing resources. Disordered eating remains too common in sports, emphasising strength and aesthetic evaluation [46], with a bigger consent indication linking weight-focused norms to both physiological health and psychological distress [30]. These conditions underscore how organisational practices can shape health risks. The evidence supports taking sport culture as a serious moderator. Environments characterised by penal leadership and stigma near help-seeking demonstrate higher psychological risk [96]; inclusive, autonomy-supportive climates foster resilience. Intrusions aiming at individuals alone are unlikely to achieve sustained change. Multilevel plans integrating psychological services, coach education, workload regulation, and safeguarding policies [13, 19] are consistent with the systemic nature of the problem. The discussion indicates adolescent sport can function either as a protective developmental asset or as a ramp of vulnerability, depending primarily on how its structures and cultures are designed and ruled.

Conclusion

Sport provides a strong, difficult environment for young people. However, when organized to meet their developmental needs, it can boost resilience, self-esteem, trust, social connections, and positive identity formation. When competitive pressures, constant evaluation, and restrictive norms dominate, sport participation may increase vulnerability to anxiety, depression, burnout, and disordered eating. The evidence reviewed here indicates that mental health outcomes in adolescent athletes are neither uniformly protective nor inherently harmful. They vary according to sport type, training culture, and organisational context. Higher levels of anxiety, eating-related concerns, and burnout have been reported in appearance-focused, weight-sensitive, and early specialisation sports, whereas team sports often show lower risk, potentially due to shared responsibility and peer support. These patterns suggest that performance pressure, overtraining, injury, perfectionism, controlling coaching practices, and weight-focused norms are key contributors to psychological risk during adolescence. Mental health outcomes, therefore, arise from the interaction of biological development, psychological coping, social relationships, and sport culture, rather than from individual strengths or weaknesses alone. Effective prevention requires coordinated, system-level strategies. Practical steps include embedding qualified mental health professionals within sport programmes, promoting supportive and athlete-centred coaching, regulating training load and recovery, conducting early screening, and eliminating harmful monitoring practices. Redefining success in youth sport to include well-being alongside performance is essential. Despite advances in understanding, important research gaps remain. Greater use of longitudinal designs, inclusion of underrepresented populations, and sport-specific analyses are needed. Emerging digital and psychophysiological tools also warrant further evaluation. Ultimately, when developmental needs are prioritized and supportive frameworks are built into sports systems, participation can promote both competitive growth and lasting mental health. In this way, youth sports can serve as a pathway to performance excellence and a foundation for lifelong psychological well-being and positive development.
