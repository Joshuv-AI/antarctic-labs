Wild plant trade in Mexican traditional market: an ethnobotanical review from a socio-ecological systems perspective
Authors: Jesús Alejandro Ríos-Solis, Manuel Gerardo Chávez-Ángeles, Luicita Lagunez-Rivera, Manuel Cuéllar-Martínez, Rodolfo Solano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditional markets in Mexico are longstanding centers of biocultural exchange where wild plants are commercialized for diverse purposes, with important socio‑ecological implications. However, research in this topic remains fragmented across disciplines. This review critically examines and synthesizes scientific literature on the trade of wild plants in traditional Mexican markets to assess accumulated knowledge, prevailing theoretical approaches, and the extent to which this activity has been conceptualized within a socio‑ecological systems (SES) framework.MethodsWe conducted a systematic search of major academic databases and analyzed studies documenting wild plant trade in traditional markets across Mexico. We identified traded species, their origin, and their uses, and examined reported social, ecological, and economic variables, emphasized their integration within an SES perspective. Ethnobotanical potential and knowledge gaps were also evaluated for each Mexican state.ResultsBased on 32 studies documenting 60 traditional markets, we recorded 902 species (528 genera, 131 families), primarily Orchidaceae, Asteraceae, Fabaceae, Cactaceae, and Bromeliaceae. Most of the species were native to Mexico and were frequently harvested from the wild, including numerous taxa listed under conservation status. Medicinal and food uses predominated, while ornamental and ritual uses reflected strong cultural drivers of demand. Although most studies reported variables relevant to SES analyses, they were largely descriptive and rarely applied integrative, hypothesis‑driven SES frameworks. Oaxaca, Chiapas, Veracruz, State of Mexico, and Puebla exhibited the highest ethnobotanical potential; while the highest ethnobotanical gap values were found in Chiapas, Veracruz, Guerrero, and San Luis Potosí.ConclusionsKey ethnobotanical theories guiding plant selection and use where identified, along with critical research gaps related to governance, consumer behavior, ecological impacts, and management practices. Traditional markets represent strategic settings for advancing SES‑based ethnobotanical research and promoting sustainability that balance biodiversity conservation, livelihoods, and cultural continuity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13002-026-00918-0.Keywords: Biocultural diversity, Ethnobotanical theories, Traditional management, Useful plants, Wildlife trade

Background

From pre‑Hispanic times to the present, traditional markets in Mexico have served as central hubs of social and economic life, where people gather to buy, sell, or exchange goods essential to daily life [1, 2]. These markets are cultural constructs that reflect the biocultural diversity of the environments in which they are established [3, 4] and serve as important repositories of ethnobiological and anthropological knowledge [5]. In Mexico, traditional markets include both open‑air markets held periodically, typically once a week, and permanent public markets that operate in fixed locations [6–8]. The variety of goods sold in these spaces reflects local agricultural and production practices, which are influenced by the available natural resources in the surrounding agroecosystems and forests, from which most goods originate [2, 8].

Within this broader context, traditional Mexican markets offer a wide array of products derived from forest and agroforestry systems, as well as cultivated crops—including plants, fruits, seeds, bark, charcoal, firewood, leaf litter, soil, insects, and fungi. Wild plants stand out in this assortment for their ornamental value [9]. Unlike many other goods, their supply often remains relatively constant throughout the year. The highest sales volumes typically involve plants extracted directly from their habitats and sold in traditional markets or through informal networks with limited regulatory oversight. Consequently, this activity is often considered a form of illicit trafficking of wild flora [10]. Prominent groups in this trade include orchids, cacti, bromeliads, cycads, and “elephant’s foot” [11–14]. These taxa are classified as non‑timber forest products (NTFPs). The commercialization of NTFPs provides rural households with subsistence income and generates employment for collectors, processors, traders, and vendors [15, 16]. Local demand is growing, and wholesale intermediaries are increasingly structuring the supply across market networks [17].

Markets where wild plants are traded represent valuable spaces for analyzing human–environment interactions, and offer insights into the social, economic, and cultural drivers of plant use [18]. This activity fits within the socio‑ecological systems (SES) paradigm, which views resource use as an emergent property of interconnected and co‑evolving human and biophysical subsystems [19]. Widely adopted in environmental governance, the SES framework emphasizes inter‑ and transdisciplinary integration to address complexity, resilience, and feedback processes [20–22]. SESs are characterized by nonlinear dynamics, multiscalar interactions, and cross‑level linkages among resource units, resource systems, governance structures, and user groups [23–25]. Within this framework, the analytical emphasis is on quantifying the provision of ecosystem services, identifying thresholds, and assessing how social demand, market pressures, and institutional arrangements shape ecological integrity and species persistence [26, 27].

From a theoretical perspective, SESs are defined by dynamic and reciprocal interactions between social actors and ecological components that are shaped by institutions, knowledge systems, and feedback mechanisms [20, 24, 28]. These interactions are structured within nested systems, in which human occupy a central position embedded in interconnected social and ecological subsystems [29]. Such subsystems generate essential ecosystem services that sustain social organization and economic activity [30]. Within this framework, socioecological practice comprises six interrelated types of human actions and social processes: planning, design, construction, restoration, conservation, and management [31].

Thus, analyzing traditional markets necessitates elucidating the interlinkages between social and ecological contexts within the practices and structures that govern plant trade [32, 33].

Empirical studies have documented significant ecological risks associated with wild plant extraction for trade. In China and India, for example, the rising demand for Panax ginseng and Curcuma longa has intensified harvesting pressure and caused population declines due to unsustainable extraction and habitat loss [34]. In South Africa, widespread reliance on traditional medicine has led to overexploitation of wild species in demand on the markets [35]. Similarly, increased demand for Thymus vulgaris, Matricaria chamomilla, and Valeriana officinalis in Albania and Bulgaria has led to overharvesting, posing risks to species conservation and supply‑chain sustainability [36]. Collectively, these patterns highlight the need of a robust SES‑based framework to assess human–ecological interactions and support conservation and governance initiatives aimed at mitigating biodiversity loss from wild plant extraction.

Mexico, recognized as a megadiverse country, harboring nearly 25,000 plant species [37, 38]. Of this flora, approximately 13% has ornamental, medicinal, food, and ceremonial uses [39, 40], many of which are traded in traditional markets. Given the country’s biological richness and the cultural importance of wild plants, applying SES theory is essential for generating testable hypotheses on human–plant interactions. This requires theoretical frameworks that explain how people select, value, and manage plant resources, thereby strengthening SES‑based interpretations of wild plant use and trade [41].

Accordingly, this review aimed to document and analyze research on the trade of wild plants in Mexico’s traditional markets by (1) assessing the richness and current state of knowledge, (2) evaluating the methodological and theoretical approaches employed, and (3) identifying gaps that limit a comprehensive understanding of this activity. Particular attention was given to proposed or tested hypotheses and their contributions to conceptualizing ornamental wild plant trade as a SES. Overall, this review seeks to identify key variables and information needs that can guide future interdisciplinary research and support more robust SES‑based analyses of this phenomenon.

Materials and methods

From December 2023 to February 2024, we conducted a systematic search for scientific publications using academic search engines, including Elsevier (https://www.elsevier.com), Google Scholar (https://scholar.google.es), Web of Science (https://www.webofscience.com), Scopus (https://www.scopus.com), Springer (https://link.springer.com), and SciELO (https://scielo.org/es/). Keywords in both English and Spanish were used: “wild plants,” “markets,” and “Mexico”. Boolean operators were used to specifically identify studies addressing the trade of wild plants in traditional markets in Mexico while excluding irrelevant results. Academic theses were excluded. To be included, publications had to include at least one of the search terms in their titles and/or keywords.

All retrieved documents were screened, and those not primarily focusing on the presence or commercialization of wild plants were excluded. Studies that met the selection criteria were analyzed to determine the extent to which they incorporated a SES perspective. To accomplish this, the variables reported and analyzed in each study were identified and classified into three categories: (a) social (e.g., age, schooling, and ethnicity), (b) ecological (e.g., number of species, vegetation type, seasonality, and plant parts used), and (c) economic (e.g., market value, income, and profits). For each study that met the selection criteria, the list of reported commercialized species was extracted and incorporated into a general database. The scientific names were reviewed to determine the accepted name for each species, according to Villaseñor [42] and the online Mexican flora portal (eFloraMex, https://efloramex.ib.unam.mx). Taxa that could not be identified at the species level in the reviewed studies were excluded. The taxonomic authorities for the accepted names were assigned using eFloraMex and IPNI (https://www.ipni.org). In cases where more than one name appeared for a given species, synonyms were removed, retaining only the accepted name. In addition, the origin of the traded plants was identified and classified as wild (harvested in natural ecosystems), cultivated (harvested in crops or agroecological systems), and naturalized (introduced, non-cultivated species) for each selected publication. The documented use reported in the literature for each species was also recorded, along with information on the species’ provenance, whether it is native or introduced to Mexico (Supplementary Material TS-1).

A word cloud was generated using the WordClouds platform (https://www.wordclouds.com) based on the titles and keywords of the selected studies, excluding the predefined search terms. This visualization provided an overview of the literature’s conceptual emphasis within the literature, with relative prominence reflecting their word frequency [43].

A selective and representative review of the main hypotheses in ethnobotany was conducted, with an emphasis on those grounded in SES theory, following Gaoue [41]. The selected studies were classified according to three guiding research questions: (1) How do people select plants for commercial use? (2) What are the ecological and social implications of plant selection? and (3) How do people mitigate the potential negative effects of plant use? For each hypothesis, the main prediction was identified, and the selected studies were evaluated in terms of how the hypothesis could be tested. The primary theories or hypotheses identified and examined in the reviewed studies are presented in the Supplementary Material TS-2.

An ethnobotanical potential index (P) was developed to evaluate the biocultural basis of each Mexican state by integrating floristic and ethnic diversity. Floristic diversity was represented by vascular plant richness [42] as an indicator of plant resources with potential use. Ethnic diversity was assessed using two variables: the percentage of the Indigenous population relative to the national total [44], as a proxy for traditional knowledge, and the number of ethnic groups [45], reflecting cultural diversity in plant-use practices. The index was calculated as the mean of these three normalized variables: P = (R + I + G) / 3, where R is normalized floristic richness, I is the normalized percentage of Indigenous population, and G is the normalized number of ethnic groups. All variables were normalized using min–max scaling: Xnorm = (Xi − Xmin) / (Xmax − Xmin), where Xi is the observed value, and Xmin and Xmax are the minimum and maximum values across states.

The normalized percentage of studied traditional markets was used as an index of scientific attention (A), representing the extent of documented plant trade per state. The ethnobotanical gap (B) was calculated as: B = P – A. This metric identifies mismatches between biocultural potential and research effort. Supplementary Material TS-3 contains the data used for these calculations.

Results

Geographic coverage and thematic scope of the review

A total of 263 articles containing the search terms in their titles or keywords were identified. Of these, 231 publications did not meet the inclusion criteria, which required a primary focus on wild plant species native to Mexico. Consequently, 32 articles published between 1966 and 2023 were retained for analysis (Table 2). These studies documented the trade of wild plants in 60 markets across eleven Mexican states (Fig. 1). The most frequently represented states were Queretaro, Puebla, the State of Mexico, and Oaxaca , whereas fewer studies were conducted in Veracruz, Jalisco, Nuevo Leon, Michoacan, Hidalgo, Mexico City, and Chiapas (Fig. 2). No studies meeting the criteria were found for the remaining Mexican states.

The 32 selected studies were published across 16 scientific journals. Twenty articles (64%) appeared in domestic journals, and the remainder appeared in international outlets. Among these, Polibotanica (national) and Economic Botany (international) were the most represented journals, with nine and six articles, respectively. Additionally, five of the journals focus on botany, while another five focus on the humanities, particularly anthropology and ethnoecology (Fig. 3).

Representation of the distribution of studies on wild plant trade in Mexican traditional markets by scientific journal. Square size is proportional to the number of published studies per journal

Eleven data collection techniques were identified across the 32 studies. Semi-structured interviews were the most commonly used method (46.8%), followed by interview-purchase techniques (9.3%), open interviews, general interviews, and structured interviews (6.2% each one). Several studies combined multiple data collection methods to obtain information.

Richness and origin of the commercialized plants

A total of 902 species traded in traditional markets were recorded, belonging to 528 genera and 131 families (Supplementary Material TS-1). The most species-rich families were Orchidaceae (23.7%), Asteraceae (6.8%), Fabaceae (4.6%), Cactaceae (4.5%), and Bromeliaceae (4.3%). Collectively, these families represent nearly 400 species traded in Mexican traditional markets (Table 3, Supplementary Material TS-4). The genera with the highest species richness that were commercialized were Tillandsia (33 species, Bromeliaceae), Epidendrum (23 species, Orchidaceae), Prosthechea (22 species, Orchidaceae), Mammillaria (11 species, Cactaceae), Encyclia (10 species, Orchidaceae), and Salvia (10 species, Lamiaceae).

Regarding species origin, native taxa predominated (68.3%) over introduced or naturalized plants (31.7%). These numbers reflect a strong representation of local flora in traditional trade systems.

Use and management of the commercialized plants

Regarding the management of traded plants (Fig. 4), the data show that 493 species of plants are collected from the wild, while 383 species are cultivated in agroecosystems. An additional 26 taxa correspond to naturalized plants in Mexico that are harvested for trade in traditional markets. Of the native species, 234 are obtained through both wild collection and cultivation in agroecosystems.

In terms of commercialized plant use (Fig. 5), ornamental applications were the most frequently documented, encompassing 598 species (54.9%) while 225 (20.7%) taxa have a medicinal use. This highlights their central role in traditional health practices. Food use was also prominent, involving 150 species, reflecting the importance of plant in local diets. Culturally embedded uses such as religious (68 species) and ceremonial (28 species) applications further highlight the symbolic and ritual significance of traded plants, while construction-related uses involved four species, representing more utilitarian functions. Additional uses included recreational (11 species), recreational (4 species), and ecological applications (1 species). Several species traded in markets were found to have multiple uses.

Ethnobotany theories and hypotheses identified in the studies reviewed

Of the 32 selected studies, three reported data for only one of the variable categories considered in Table 1. Eight studies provided information for two of the three variable groups, and 21 studies included data for all three groups, which comprise social, ecological, and economic variables.

The reviewed studies were classified according to the ethnobotanical theories and hypotheses proposed by Gaoue et al. (2017; Suppleentary Material TS-1). Eighteen of these studies provided sufficient information to be analyzed within five theoretical frameworks or hypothesis‑based approaches, and several could be assigned to more than one analytical category. These frameworks included: (I) versatility, availability, and diversification (five); (II) optimal foraging (six); (III) age, gender, and knowledge dynamics (eight); (IV) cultural keystone species (six); and (V) value of plant use (seven). Thirteen of the remaining 14 studies contained only partial information, which prevented their classification within these frameworks. One study was purely descriptive and did not provide analyzable data.

Finally, the titles of the selected studies contained a total of 154 distinct words in both English and Spanish. A word cloud analysis revealed the five most frequent terms: medicinal, Oaxaca, commercialized, traditional, and management. These terms summarize the studies’ dominant themes and point to key research areas related to the trade of Mexican wild plants in traditional markets.

In these states, high levels of floristic and ethnic diversity indicate a substantial biocultural heritage.

Regarding scientific attention (A) by the ethnobotanical community to traditional markets where wild plants are traded, the highest values were recorded in Querétaro (1.00), Puebla (0.86), Oaxaca (0.78), State of Mexico (0.65), and Chiapas (0.29, see Supplementary Material TS-3).

These results reveal persistent information gaps on wild plant trade in traditional markets, particularly in states with high floristic and cultural diversity.

Discussion

Geographic coverage and thematic scope of the review

The revised studies indicate that traditional markets in Veracruz, Oaxaca, Puebla, and Chiapas have the largest number of commercialized, wild‑collected plant species. Previously, it was mentioned that Veracruz is one of the states with fewer studies on plant trade; however, one of these documented the highest number of species recorded in traditional markets [13]. Notably, Chiapas, Oaxaca, and Veracruz are also among the most floristically rich states in Mexico, with more than 8,500 plant species each. Jalisco and Guerrero follow with 6,000–8,000 species each, Michoacán, San Luis Potosí, Puebla, and the State of Mexico follow with 5,000–6,000 species each [42, 74–76]. Most of the states where the studies reviewed here were conducted are located in the tropical region of Mexico, which is also characterized by a high floristic and ethnic diversity. These states encompass more than 30 of Mexico’s 68 officially recognized Indigenous Peoples [44]. Approximately 10% of these groups inhabit forested regions, where they possess extensive ecological knowledge and are actively involved in managing and using local plant resources [77].

In contrast, there is a notable scarcity, or even absence, of research in the Yucatan Peninsula, and the northern region of Mexico—areas that have not attracted the same level of interest from ethnobotanists. It is particularly noteworthy that this review did not identify any studies for the Yucatan Peninsula, despite the high proportion of indigenous people [44] in the region and the well‑recognized importance of plant resource use among the communities inhabiting it [78].

Most of the 60 traditional markets recorded in the review are concentrated in central and southern Mexico. Four states—Queretaro (14), Puebla (eight), the State of Mexico (nine), and Oaxaca (12)—account for 76% of these markets. Solís-López & Flores-López [79] have previously documented this uneven spatial distribution of traditional markets. This pattern reflects the structural role of traditional markets as nodes where economic activity, cultural identity, and social interaction converge. This sustains historical practices within contemporary urban contexts despite urban regeneration and gentrification processes [80–82].

Migration processes and biocultural diversity play a central role in shaping the dynamics and concentration of traditional markets. As a state that ranks fifth nationally in internal migration inflows [83], Queretaro likely experiences increased demand for culturally meaningful plant products as migrant populations seek to sustain traditional practices in new urban contexts. Cacti used in Christmas celebrations are one example of how these markets function as spaces of cultural continuity. They enable migrants to maintain foodways, ritual practices, and culturally significant consumption patterns amid urban expansion [79].

Oaxaca, with its exceptional biocultural diversity —characterized by high floristic richness and strong indigenous presence—facilitates intense exchanges of plant resources and knowledge. This reinforces the role of markets as interfaces between ecological availability (wild, cultivated or tolerated plants —i.e., species not intentionally cultivated but deliberately maintained due to its usefulness or cultural value), cultural use, and knowledge exchange [67]. Similarly, Puebla and the State of Mexico, as major economic and demographic hubs, sustain dynamic market systems. The convergence of diverse populations and production zones in these areas promotes the circulation, adaptation, and persistence of cultural practices based on its plant diversity [5, 62].

Richness and origin of the commercialized plants

The studies reviewed here primarily focus on documenting the number of plant species traded in traditional markets and commonly distinguish between cultivated, wild-harvested, and introduced species. Consequently, most of these papers adopt a predominantly descriptive approach [46–49, 55]. Despite their descriptive nature emphasis, these studies provide valuable insights into the diversity of Mexican flora commercialized in traditional markets. This is evident in the Supplementary Material TS-1, which includes the presence of a substantial number of species of conservation concern within the traditional market trade. This pattern has been documented by some authors [8, 13, 62].

Among the most prominently commercialized taxonomic groups of wild plants, orchids stand out, followed by bromeliads. From a biocultural perspective, species from these families are salient components of local vegetation and are deeply embedded in regional systems of knowledge, beliefs, and uses [8, 13, 59, 62]. Their high visibility in forests and strong aesthetic appeal reinforce their cultural value, and their religious significance places them within culturally structured use systems as keystone species. These systems transcend utilitarian functions, contributing to the symbolic and ritual dimensions of plant use [59, 84].

The increased demand for these taxa during their flowering periods, especially when these periods coincide with Catholic festivities, reflects seasonal surges in extraction and commercialization [8, 51, 59]. These dynamics align with socioecological systems theory, wherein which market demand, cultural calendars, and ecological phenology interact to influence harvesting pressure [85, 86]. However, the predominance of wild harvesting reported for most orchids, bromeliads and cacti could indicate a structural imbalance between extraction intensity and biological regeneration, raising concerns about long‑term sustainability [87]. The limited number of documented regulated management plans highlights the persistent gap between traditional market practices and formal conservation frameworks [88].

While previous studies have categorized plant uses in traditional markets trade at a general level, this review emphasizes medicinal and food uses due to their central role in sustaining local livelihoods and well‑being. These use categories are particularly relevant in ethnobotanical theory because they link plant trade not only to economic value, but also to food security, health systems, and cultural continuity. From a biocultural conservation standpoint, the importance of these uses underscores the need of reconciling biodiversity conservation with the social and cultural roles that traditional markets play in local socioecological systems.

Use and management of commercialized plants

This review shows that plant trade in Mexican traditional markets is sustained by a combination of wild harvesting, cultivation, and mixed management strategies. The predominance of species obtained directly from natural ecosystems—particularly those with medicinal, ornamental, and ritual value—highlights the markets’ dependence on natural ecosystems as primary sources of supply [13, 59]. In this context, the use and commercialization of plants respond not only to ecological availability but also to cultural and utilitarian criteria that guide their selection and persistence in the market [18, 53].

The large number of medicinal species traded emphasizes the significant role of traditional markets in local health systems, as well as the continuity of traditional knowledge that sustains the demand for therapeutic plants [47, 48]. These species are selected based on economic accessibility, perceived efficacy, and cultural value, which reinforces the continuity of local medical practices and the transmission of ethnobotanical knowledge [5, 89].

Nearly half of the recorded species are used for food, highlighting the importance of markets for food security, where wild‑harvested and cultivated edible plants converge [46, 67]. This dynamic system includes processes of incipient domestication, tolerance within agroecosystems, and small‑scale cultivation. These processes function as complementary strategies that reduce direct extractive pressure on wild plant populations [56, 63].

Species collected exclusively from natural ecosystems tend to be associated with specialized cultural uses, such as rituals or ornaments, as seen with bromeliads and orchids. These uses are characterized by marked seasonality in demand [51, 59]. In contrast, cultivated species are primarily linked to food and medicinal uses and are consumed more frequently. This differentiation suggests that the market–landscape system operates through feedback among cultural calendars, ecological cycles, and economic dynamics. This is consistent with a socio‑ecological systems perspective [20, 24].

Families such as Orchidaceae, Bromeliaceae, and Cactaceae contain species of high cultural and aesthetic value. The extraction of these species is driven by identity‑based and symbolic values rather than basic subsistence needs [62, 84]. Although mixed management processes are documented, the limited description of specific practices hinders assessments of their effectiveness in mitigating ecological impacts. This highlights the need for more integrative studies on management and sustainability [88].

Ethnobotany theories and hypotheses identified in the studies revised

As scholarly interest in the socioecological implications of wild plant use in Mexico expanded, later studies increasingly incorporated social, ecological, and economic variables into their analyses. Across the reviewed literature, there is a broad consensus on the importance of these core variables, whose systematic documentation is essential for understanding the trade of wild plants in traditional markets as a SES. Accordingly, such analyses require examining the linkages between social and ecological contexts that shape plant trade practices and structures [32, 33].

Many studies tend to address these dimensions individually rather than through an explicitly integrative SES framework [4, 5, 8, 51, 52, 57, 58, 60, 61, 63, 64, 66, 67]. While these approaches generate valuable descriptive and diagnostic information, they often overlook system‑level properties, such as feedback loops, cross‑scale interactions, and adaptive responses. These properties are considered central to the resilience and sustainability of SES [24, 28, 90].

Only a limited subset of studies explicitly incorporates social and cultural components in a manner consistent with SES theory. In several cases, however, the information these studies provide allows them to be situated retrospectively within an SES framework, even though this was not their primary analytical objective [12, 13, 53, 56, 59, 65]. These works offer important insights into the interrelationships among system components, such as cultural practices, extraction strategies, and market dynamics, thereby laying the groundwork for more explicitly integrative analyses grounded in SES theory.

Future research could build on these contributions by incorporating analytical approaches such as network analysis, participatory modeling, and actor‑oriented frameworks, to better account for the interactions among diverse stakeholders, institutions, and resource flows operating across multiple spatial and temporal scales [91–93]. However, approaches to SESs continue to vary widely due to differences in methodologies, conceptual frameworks, and definitions. These differences often result in analytical gaps, including the insufficient acknowledgment or inconsistent application of foundational SES concepts [21, 94].

Within this framework, socioecological practice comprises six interrelated types of human actions and social processes: planning, design, construction, restoration, conservation, and management [31]. Of these, five were identified in the reviewed studies on wild plants trade; restoration was the only type not documented. This absence likely reflects a lack of field‑based data, as management practices are often overlooked or insufficiently reported in market‑oriented ethnobotanical studies.

Several of the reviewed studies analyze the trade of wild plants using an integrated economic, social, and ecological approach, conceptualizing the trade as an interconnected system of components that interact with variables from multiple domains. Flores‑Palacios and Valencia‑Díaz [13] documented local extraction-sale networks, estimated sales volumes and profits, and identified instances of non‑compliance with environmental regulations. Linares and Bye [53] approached the trade from a social and organizational perspective, providing detailed information on market dynamics, species composition, collection sites, sales volumes, and buyer profiles. Arellanes et al. [56] examined the persistence of plant species in markets by conducting fieldwork at both points of sale and extraction sites and by integrating ecological, socioeconomic, and management variables. Similarly, Cruz‑García et al. [59] analyzed local extraction-sale networks, documenting species richness, sales volumes, and income generation, and identifying vulnerable social groups involved in the trade. Emeterio‑Lara et al. [12] and Jiménez‑López et al. [65] examined family‑based networks and gender‑specific roles among orchid vendors. They linked extraction practices with patterns of use and perceived aesthetic attributes such as floral attractiveness. Jiménez‑López et al. [8] noted that the primary extraction area was located within a protected natural area and that transportation infrastructure facilitated the movement of plants between extraction sites and markets.

Potential and knowledge gaps in the study of wild plant trade in traditional markets

The states with the highest ethnobotanical potential values are primarily concentrated in southern and southeastern Mexico (Oaxaca, Chiapas, Veracruz, and Guerrero), where high floristic richness coincides with a strong Indigenous presence, reflecting a robust biocultural relationship. This spatial overlap between biological and cultural diversity has been widely documented in Mexico, where regions of high species richness also tend to concentrate linguistic and cultural diversity [95]. Oaxaca and Puebla exhibit a clear correspondence between ethnobotanical potential and research effort, as both states show high values for the P and A indices. In contrast, Queretaro stands out as a case where relatively high research attention (A index) is not matched by its comparatively lower biocultural potential (P index).

Conversely, Chiapas, Veracruz, Guerrero, San Luis Potosi, Jalisco, Sonora, Yucatan, and Chihuahua are states with the highest ethnobotanical gap values, emerge as priority areas for future research. These states combine high floristic richness and significant Indigenous presence with limited or absent documentation of plants traded in traditional markets, highlighting substantial gaps in ethnobotanical knowledge. Previous studies have also shown that ethnobotanical research and market-based studies in Mexico are geographically uneven and concentrated in a few states, leaving extensive areas underdocumented [96].

Overall, the results reveal a consistent pattern in which states with greater biological and cultural diversity also exhibit higher ethnobotanical potential. These findings support the hypothesis that the presence of Indigenous peoples is closely linked to the generation, transmission, and persistence of knowledge related to the management and use of plant resources. Indigenous knowledge systems, developed through interactions with ecosystems, play a crucial role in the sustainable use and conservation of biodiversity [97].

The analysis also demonstrates that ethnobotanical research in Mexico focused on traditional markets, is unevenly distributed and does not align proportionally with the country’s biocultural potential. This mismatch underscores the existence of significant knowledge gaps in regions of high diversity and emphasizes the need to direct future research efforts toward these underrepresented yet bioculturally rich areas. Traditional markets themselves have been recognized as key socioecological systems that reflect regional biocultural diversity yet remain insufficiently studied in many parts of the country [63].

Future perspectives in the wild plant trade as SES

The review also reveals a substantial gap in studies advancing the understanding of the wild plant trade as a SES. Key areas requiring further examination include: (a) purchasing processes, (b) the social, economic, and cultural characteristics of consumers, (c) the governance and regulatory frameworks that shape this activity, (d) the ecological impacts of extraction on wild populations, and (e) the presence of in situ or ex situ management practices. Some studies have begun to address these gaps. For instance, Farfán‑Heredia et al. [63] examined the trade of wild plants and fungi, emphasizing the roles of traditional management practices and local demand in sustaining market supply. Consumers play a central role in shaping extraction and commercialization through supply-and-demand dynamics; however, this dimension remains understudied. In the context of e‑commerce for wild plants, for example, sellers have been shown to exert monopolistic control over digital platforms, enabling price inflation and speculative practices [98]. This introduces new governance challenges within the SES.

The trade of wild plants can be examined using hypotheses and theories commonly employed in ethnobotany, drawing on the variables documented in the reviewed studies. Several studies incorporate variables such as species richness, plant uses, extraction volume, and availability [8, 48, 49, 57, 61], which are aligned with the hypotheses of versatility, availability, and diversification. Other studies report economic variables, including plant value, extraction sites, seller income, and sale volumes, that are suitable for testing optimal foraging theory [8, 13, 59, 65].

Research focusing on the age and gender of sellers, knowledge transmission, and differentiated roles within trade activities supports the hypothesis of age, gender, and knowledge dynamics [51, 53, 58, 61, 63, 65]. Additionally, the theory of key cultural species—centered on plant use, cultural importance, conservation status, and normative restrictions—is reflected in other studies [4, 5, 13, 53, 58]. The hypothesis of plant use value, which relies on data regarding use, cultural relevance, and availability, is applicable to a wide range of ethnobotanical studies. Finally, the economic theory of consumption, though still underexplored, can be applied using data on economic variables and consumption preferences, as illustrated by Emeterio‑Lara et al. [12].

Conclusions

The trade of wild ornamental plants in Mexico’s traditional markets constitutes a complex socio‑ecological system where biodiversity, cultural practices, economic strategies, and governance processes intersect. This review highlights the remarkable richness of species involved in this activity, with a predominance of native taxa and a strong dependence on wild extraction, including species under conservation risk. This underscores both the ecological relevance of these systems and the potential pressures exerted on natural populations.

The results show that traditional markets serve as vital nodes of biocultural exchange where ecological knowledge, cultural identity, and livelihood strategies converge, functioning not only as commercial spaces. The predominance of medicinal and food uses, together with the presence of ritual and ornamental values, reflects the central role of these markets in sustaining local health systems, food security, and cultural continuity.

Despite the wide availability of ecological, social, and economic data in the reviewed literature, most studies remain predominantly descriptive and rarely integrate these dimensions within a structured socio‑ecological systems (SES) framework. This lack of integration limits the understanding of system‑level processes such as feedback mechanisms, resilience, and sustainability. Advancing this field requires hypothesis‑driven and interdisciplinary research approaches that explicitly integrate ecological impacts, economic incentives, social organization, cultural valuation, governance mechanisms, consumer dynamics, and management strategies.

Research efforts on plant traded in traditional markets are unevenly distributed across the country and do not correspond to Mexico’s biocultural diversity, leaving regions with high ethnobotanical potential insufficiently studied.

Mexico’s exceptional biocultural diversity makes its traditional markets offer a valuable empirical setting for advancing socio‑ecological theory and developing sustainable management strategies that balance biodiversity conservation with the livelihoods and cultural continuity of local communities.
