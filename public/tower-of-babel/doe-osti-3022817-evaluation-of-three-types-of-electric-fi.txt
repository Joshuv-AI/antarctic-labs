Evaluation of Three Types of Electric Field Sensors
Xianjin Yang, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory
Bill Junor, Los Alamos National Laboratory

Abstract
On June 3-4, 2025, we evaluated three types of electric-field (E-field) sensors: capacitive
electrodes, stainless-steel stakes, and porous pots - at two electromagnetic (EM) stations
(PWBVI and PWAOF) on Aqueduct Mesa at the Nevada National Security Site. We
compared calibrated time series and derived metrics including power spectral density,
root-mean-square (RMS) amplitude, signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and detectability of power-
line harmonics and Schumann resonance. The objective was to identify an alternative E-
field sensor with higher SNR than the existing capacitive electrodes. Our data
demonstrates that stainless-steel electrodes perform best, with the highest SNR and the
most consistent detection of power-line harmonics and Schumann resonance among the
three sensor types; they may be used as a supplement to, or replacement for, the existing
capacitive electrodes.

Introduction
EMX22 experiments demonstrated that (1) magnetic-field (B-field) data on Aqueduct Mesa
have a much higher signal-to-noise ratio than E-field data, and (2) the observed horizontal
E-field at some stations on the mesa disagrees in polarity with predictions from
electromagnetic (EM) propagation modeling (Figure 1, E_east). The surface EM sensors
have been unattended for almost 20 months since the PE1-A shot. On June 3-4, 2025, we
conducted an experiment to troubleshoot the wiring of the existing capacitive electrodes
and to compare the performance of three types of horizontal E-field sensors. We evaluated
the horizontal (east and north) E-field sensors at surface EM stations PWBVI and PWAOF.
The objective was to identify an alternative E-field sensor with improved signal-to-noise
ratio (SNR) and stability.

E-field (E_east) at PNADO.

Experiment Description
This experiment evaluates and compares three types of E-field sensors: the existing
capacitive electrode (CE), a stainless-steel (SS) stake electrode, and a porous-pot (PP)
electrode (Figure 2). SS and PP electrodes have been widely used for geophysical imaging
for decades. A CE sits on the ground surface and couples capacitively to the ground. SS
and PP electrodes contain no built-in electronics for signal amplification or conditioning,
cost less than CE sensors, and do not require a power supply, which facilitates field
deployment. However, SS and PP electrodes must be partially buried in moist soil to
establish galvanic contact with the ground. Frozen ground or dry soil can be highly resistive
and may open the measurement circuit. Standard practice is to water the electrodes (as in
this study) or to install them in moist clay (e.g., bentonite) for long-term monitoring.

Figure 2. Three E-field sensor types evaluated: capacitive electrode (CE) sitting on the soil
in a bucket, stainless-steel (SS) stake electrode, and porous-pot (PP) electrode.

Figure 3a shows three co-located sets of east-west (E-W) E-field sensors installed at
surface EM station PWBVI. The Phoenix Geophysics RXU-8A digitizer (Figure 3b) has eight
built-in channels. We retained the three existing B-field channels (H1-H3) and the two
existing horizontal E-field channels (E2 and E3; capacitive electrodes). The vertical E-field
channel (E1) and the accelerometer channel (E4) were disconnected. We then connected
the SS electrode pair to E1 and the PP electrode pair to E4; channel E5 was unused. We
collected 30 min of data at a sampling rate of 24 kHz for the three E-W electrode pairs. After
each acquisition, we powered down the digitizer, removed the SD card, inserted a new SD
card with an updated configuration file, disconnected the E–W electrode cables, and
connected the N–S electrode cables. We then collected another 30 min of data for the
three north–south (N–S) electrode pairs.

Figure 3. (a) Three E-field sensor types installed adjacent to one another at surface EM
station PWBVI. (b) Phoenix Geophysics RXU-8A eight-channel digitizer. The top row of
terminals comprises five E-field channels labeled E1–E5.

Data Analysis
The observed E-field data were calibrated to account for differences in channel gains,
electrode spacing, and time offsets. No digital filtering was applied. Calibrated E-field
amplitudes are reported in µV/m. We compare time series, power spectral density (PSD),

root-mean-square (RMS) amplitude, standard deviation (STD), and signal-to-noise ratio
(SNR) for the three sensor types at two surface EM stations (PWAOF and PWBVI).

Time Series
We compared the fine-scale waveform characteristics among the three sensor types at the
two EM stations using 10-ms time series (240 samples; Figure 4). The CE sensors are the
noisiest, whereas the SS sensors are the least noisy. The PP and SS sensors show similar
and correlated signatures, although PP is slightly noisier than SS. Figure 5 compares 60-s
time series and shows that the PWAOF CE sensors exhibit strong noise pulses every 5s in
both the north and east components; these sensors were sent to the vendor for
troubleshooting and repair. The east-component E-field at PWBVI is strongly correlated
among the three sensor types.

Figure 4. Comparison of 10-ms time series among three sensor types at two EM stations.
CE = capacitive electrode; PP = porous pot; SS = stainless-steel stake electrode. The top
row shows PWAOF data; the bottom row shows PWBVI data.

Figure 5. Comparison of 1-min time series for three E-field sensor types in two orientations
(east and north) at two EM stations (PWAOF and PWBVI).

Figure 6 shows cross-correlations of the 10-ms E-field segments (Figure 4) for three sensor
pairs (CE–PP, PP–SS, and SS–CE) in the east and north orientations at both stations. Strong
positive correlations between PP and SS data are observed for both stations and both
orientations. In contrast, the CE data show strong negative cross-correlation with SS or PP
at PWAOF (north) and weak negative cross-correlation with SS or PP at PWAOF (east) and
PWBVI (east), suggesting a possible polarity reversal (sign flip) in the CE measurements.

Figure 6. Cross-correlation for three pairs of E-field sensors (CE–PP, PP–SS, SS–CE) in two
orientations (east and north) at two EM stations (PWAOF and PWBVI).

Power Spectral Density
We computed power spectral density (PSD) from 1-min E-field segments using the Welch
method (scipy.signal.welch). The four panels in Figure 7 correspond to the two EM stations
(PWAOF and PWBVI) and two sensor orientations (east and north). Each panel compares
PSD across the three sensor types per station and orientation. All sensors at PWAOF and
PWBVI detected the 60-Hz power-line signal and its harmonics (e.g., 180, 300, and 420 Hz),
except that the east–west CE sensor at PWAOF shows only weak peaks due to potential
sensor malfunction. The 60-Hz sideband at PWAOF for both SS and PP sensors is wider
than that at PWBVI, suggesting a potential power-supply issue at PWAOF.

Figure 7. PSD of 60-s E-field data for three sensor types in two orientations (east and north)
at two EM stations (PWAOF and PWBVI).

Schumann Resonance
We computed Welch PSDs from 10-min E-field segments to evaluate detection of
Schumann resonance. All SS and PP sensors detected Schumann resonances at 7.8, 14.3,
and 20.8 Hz at PWAOF and PWBVI, except for the N-S PP sensor at PWBVI. All CE sensors
failed to detect Schumann resonance, an indication of pool sensor performance.

Figure 8. PSD of 10-min E-field data for detection of Schumann resonance.

Spectrogram
We computed spectrograms of 30-min E-field records using 2-s windows with 50% overlap
(scipy.signal.spectrogram). All E-field sensors at both PWAOF and PWBVI detected power-
line harmonics, except the CE sensors at PWBVI. The SS sensors exhibit the highest signal-
to-noise ratio apparently.

Figure 9. Spectrogram of 30-min E-field data for three sensor types in two orientations (east
and north) at two EM stations (PWAOF and PWBVI).

Root-mean-squares (RMS)
We computed root-mean-squares (RMS) of E-field amplitudes in a 5-s window for the 30-
min record at EM station PWBVI (Figure 10). RMS results for PWAOF are not shown because
the record contains numerous artifacts. In both north and east orientations, the CE sensors
have higher RMS values than the SS and PP sensors, indicating a higher noise floor in CE
sensors.

Figure 10. Root-mean-square (RMS) E-field in a 5-s window for the 30-min record at PWBVI.

Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
We compared the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) at 60 Hz among the three sensor types. SNR
was calculated as follows: (1) compute the PSD of 20-s E-field segments using the Welch
method; (2) estimate signal power by integrating the PSD from 59.5 to 60.5 Hz; (3) estimate
noise power by integrating the PSD in the left and right 1-Hz bands offset by 3 Hz; and (4)
express SNR in dB (Figure 11). The CE sensors have consistently lower SNR than SS and PP
sensors in all scenarios. At PWAOF (closer to the P-tunnel power network), PP sensors have
slightly higher SNR than SS sensors.

Figure 11. SNR (dB) at 60 Hz computed from 20-s E-field segments for three sensor types in
two orientations (east and north) at two EM stations (PWAOF and PWBVI).

Summary and Recommendation
Across both stations (PWAOF and PWBVI) and both orientations (east and north), the CE
sensors consistently underperform the PP and SS sensors in terms of detectability of
power-line harmonics and Schumann resonance, noise floor (RMS), and SNR, with very few
exceptions. PP and SS sensors produce strongly cross-correlated E-field data and perform
comparably overall, although Table 1 indicates small differences in 60-Hz SNR between PP
and SS sensors. SS stake electrodes are also cheaper and easier to install than PP
electrodes. Therefore, we rank the SS electrodes as the best-performing of the three sensor
types. The 1-min E-field data further show that the PWAOF CE sensors exhibit a noise pulse
approximately every 5 s, whereas the PP and SS sensors do not, suggesting a malfunction
in the PWAOF CE sensors.

We recommend (1) retaining the existing horizontal capacitive electrodes (CE) used for
EMX22 and PE1-A and (2) installing stainless-steel (SS) electrodes adjacent to the CE
sensors to provide two additional horizontal E-field channels per EM station. This duplicate
E-field deployment preserves backward compatibility with EMX22 and PE1-A data while
leveraging the improved performance of SS electrodes. The two new E-field channels can
be connected to a currently unused (spare) channel and the vertical E-field channel, which
provided limited information during EMX22 and PE1-A and could be disconnected. For

long-term monitoring, a stainless-steel electrode could be emplaced in a bentonite-filled
shallow small hole (e.g., 2 in diameter and 1-2 ft deep).
