Tropical curves with parallel rays
Song JuAe

Abstract
. In the previous works, the rational function semifields of abstract tropical curves were characterized. In this paper, we give a contravariant categorical equivalence between the category of abstract tropical curves with morphisms and the category of semifields over the tropical semifield 𝑻 \boldsymbol{T} characterized above with 𝑻 \boldsymbol{T} -algebra homomorphisms. The characterization tells us that the traditional definition of abstract tropical curves has a fatal flaw such that we are never able to deal with parallel rays, unlike the traditional tropical curves, which generally admit them. To address this flaw, we introduce a new notion of abstract tropical curves with parallel rays. Then we define the rational function semifields of these curves and give a characterization of them, and a variant of the categorical equivalence between their categories with a suitable notion of morphisms between these curves. Under the categorical equivalences, we translate several geometric notions for traditional or abstract tropical curves (with parallel rays) into algebraic ones, including weights on edges and the balancing condition.

1.
Introduction

Background

The definition of abstract tropical curves we give in Subsection
2.4
is very classical in tropical geometry.
These are (equivalence classes of) graphs endowed with a metric.
This definition traces its origins to
[
22
]
, with a version more closely resembling our own appearing in
[
29
]
.
Our definition of abstract tropical curves was also introduced in
[
23
]
under the name of metric graphs within the framework of quantum graphs.
The name “abstract tropical curve” was given in
[
28
]
, and in
loc. cit.
and
[
5
]
, using the Riemann–Roch theorem for finite graphs, which had been established earlier in
[
3
]
, the Riemann–Roch theorem for abstract tropical curves was proven independently.
This made abstract tropical curves attracted particular interest as objects of study in tropical geometry.

By Kapranov’s theorem (or its generalization, the fundamental theorem of tropical algebraic geometry, see
[
26
, Subsections 3.1 and 3.2]
), tropicalizations of algebraic curves or tropical plane curves, that is, objects that had traditionally been called tropical curves, can be viewed as abstract tropical curves by lattice length.
This justifies the name “abstract tropical curves”.

Another justification can be found in situations where tropicalization is considered via Berkovich spaces, as in
[
1
]
.
In such cases as well, it is natural to call the support of a connected one-dimensional polyhedral complex equipped with a metric an abstract tropical curve.

On the other hand, it should be noted that in this abstraction from classical tropical curves via lattice length, information such as edge weights and the balancing condition inherent to classical tropical curves is discarded.
Furthermore, phenomena that can never occur in classical tropical curves due to the balancing condition—such as the existence of leaves of finite length or the absence of leaves—are permitted within the framework of abstract tropical curves.
It seems that the motivation for introducing this broader framework, compared with the abstraction of classical tropical curves using lattice lengths, largely stems from the proof of the Riemann–Roch theorem mentioned above.

At this point, at least the following issue naturally arises:

(i) How can weights and the balancing condition be incorporated into the framework of abstract tropical curves?

There are broadly two possible approaches: an intrinsic one and an extrinsic one.
The former modifies the very definition of abstract tropical curves in order to build a framework that incorporates weights and the balancing condition, while the latter keeps the existing framework intact and describes weights and the balancing condition by imposing additional external conditions.

Research close to the former approach includes the study of tropical ideals introduced in
[
24
]
.
In this theory, tropical ideals are defined by abstracting the properties possessed by tropicalizations of ideals; that is, by imposing conditions on ideals to restrict the class under consideration,
[
25
]
shows that the varieties they define have structure of weighted polyhedral complexes satisfying the balancing condition, and then studies further properties.
In this paper, however, we pursue the latter approach.
Moreover, in view of the fact that tropical geometry is a form of algebraic geometry, this approach is realized within an algebraic framework:

(ii) How do we translate several geometric notions (containing weights and the balancing condition) for abstract tropical curves into algebraic ones?

To this end, we must first recall that for an abstract tropical curve
\varGamma
, its rational function semifield
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
is characterized as follows (for notations, see Section
2
):

Theorem 1.1

(
[
18
, Corollary 3.19]
, cf.
[
20
, Section 3]
)
.

Let
S
be a
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra.
Then
S
is isomorphic to
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
as a
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra with some abstract tropical curve
\varGamma
if and only if some (and any) surjective
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism
\psi
from a tropical rational function semifield to
S
satisfies the following five conditions:

(1)

\operatorname{Ker}(\psi)
is finitely generated as a congruence,

(2)

\operatorname{Ker}(\psi)=\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{V}(\operatorname{Ker}(\psi)))
holds,

(3)

\boldsymbol{V}(\operatorname{Ker}(\psi))
is connected,

(4)

\boldsymbol{V}(\operatorname{Ker}(\psi))
is of dimension zero or one, and

(5)
if
L_{1},L_{2}
are rays of
\boldsymbol{V}(\operatorname{Ker}(\psi))
with the same direction vector toward infinity, then
L_{1}\subset L_{2}
or
L_{1}\supset L_{2}
holds.

By the classical fact that over an algebraically closed field
k
, the category of smooth projective curves with dominant morphisms is contravariantly equivalent to the category of one-dimensional function fields with
k
-homomorphisms, together with Theorem
1.1
, we expect a contravariant equivalence between the category of abstract tropical curves and the category of semifields over
\boldsymbol{T}
in Theorem
1.1
.
If such an equivalence can be established, various notions associated with abstract tropical curves can be translated into algebraic notions of their rational function semifields, allowing abstract tropical curves to be studied within an algebro-geometric framework.

This categorical equivalence has already been partially resolved in
[
18
, Corollary 3.20]
and
[
20
, Section 3]
, and in this paper we aim to provide a complete resolution in Corollary
3.2
.
There are three points that deserve special attention here.

The first concern is the definition of morphisms between abstract tropical curves.
This notion was introduced by Chan in
[
4
]
, where only (finite) harmonic morphisms were considered, rather than general morphisms.
This is a reasonable assumption since finite morphisms between algebraic curves are sent to harmonic morphisms between abstract tropical curves under tropicalization as explained in
[
1
]
.
On the other hand, no justification had previously been given for why the definition of morphisms adopted in
[
4
]
should be used; the justification provided in Corollary
3.2
is the first of its kind.
Namely, morphisms between abstract tropical curves are geometric realizations of
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphisms between their rational function semifields.

The second point to note concerns condition
(5)
of Theorem
1.1
.
From the proof of this theorem, the following statement can be derived:

Proposition 1.2

(
[
18
, Proposition 3.18]
,
[
19
, Proposition 3.11]
)
.

Let
\varGamma
be an abstract tropical curve.
For
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)\setminus\{-\infty\}
, let
\theta:\varGamma\setminus\varGamma_{\infty}\to\boldsymbol{R}^{n};x\mapsto(f_{1}(x),\ldots,f_{n}(x))
.
Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
is isomorphic to
\overline{\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{X})}_{n}/\boldsymbol{E}(\operatorname{Im}(\theta))
as a
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra, and

(2)

\theta
is an injective local isometry and for any rays
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
of
\varGamma
, the images
\theta(L_{1}\setminus\varGamma_{\infty})
and
\theta(L_{2}\setminus\varGamma_{\infty})
do not contain rays with the same direction vector toward infinity.

Considering that, under the above definition of morphisms, the notion of isomorphism between abstract tropical curves coincides with that of isometries, this statement is rather surprising.
Indeed, if an abstract tropical curve
\varGamma
is “realized” (other than points at infinity) as the image of a map
\theta
consisting of a finite number of rational functions on
\varGamma
other than
-\infty
as in Proposition
1.2
, their “rational function semifields”
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
and
\overline{\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{X})}_{n}/\boldsymbol{E}(\operatorname{Im}(\theta))
should coincide, but this is not the case.
This discrepancy arises from a deficiency in the definition of abstract tropical curves, which extracts only the information of topology and lattice length in the abstraction of classical tropical curves, and the definition of morphisms between them that respect only their topologies and metrics.

In fact, for example, tropical plane curves can clearly have parallel rays that do not contain each other, but information about such parallelism—ultimately originating from lattice data—cannot be captured in the above category.
Similar issues concerning parallel rays also arise in higher-dimensional cases; see
[
2
]
for details.

The third concern is finitely generated subsemifields over
\boldsymbol{T}
of rational function semifields of abstract tropical curves.
This arises from condition
(5)
of Theorem
1.1
again.
The author explained in
[
19
, Section
1
]
that
\overline{\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{X})}_{n}/E
plays the role of coordinate rings for any congruence
E
on
\overline{\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{X})}_{n}
.
From such a perspective, we naturally expect that for an abstract tropical curve
\varGamma
, a finitely generated subsemifield over
\boldsymbol{T}
of
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
is again a rational function semifield of some abstract tropical curve.
However condition
(5)
of Theorem
1.1
clearly denies this.

(iii) How do we solve these problems arising from condition
(5)
of Theorem
1.1
?

Main results

In this paper, to solve them, we introduce an extension of the notion of abstract tropical curves that allows the presence of parallel rays, namely, abstract tropical curves with parallel rays.
We then define the rational function semifields of such curves and provide a characterization in Theorem
4.9
analogous to Theorem
1.1
, thereby establishing the aforementioned categorical equivalence for this extended category as well (Corollary
4.17
).
Corollary
4.12
and Remark
4.13
show that a finitely generated subsemifield over
\boldsymbol{T}
of the rational function semifield of an abstract tropical curve with parallel rays is again the rational function semifield of some abstract tropical curve with parallel rays.
We interpret weights as lattice dilations and define them via bijective morphisms between abstract tropical curves with parallel rays.
The balancing condition is understood through rational functions that are harmonic away from points at infinity.

Since harmonicity of rational functions at a point is a local property, we introduce localization at points in order to translate this notion into algebraic terms.

Taken together, these constructions yield a framework that treats lattice information in a more refined manner.
An advantage of this new framework is that, since everything is described algebraically, it provides a clear path toward higher-dimensional generalizations.
Although we do not pursue higher-dimensional cases in this paper, such generalizations appear feasible in light of the results of
[
2
]
.

2.1.
Semirings and algebras

In this paper, a
semiring

S
is a commutative semiring with the absorbing identity
0_{S}
for addition
+
and the identity
1_{S}
for multiplication
\cdot
.
If every nonzero element of a semiring
S
is multiplicatively invertible and
0_{S}\not=1_{S}
, then
S
is called a
semifield
.

A map between semirings
\varphi:S_{1}\to S_{2}
is a
semiring homomorphism
if for any
x,y\in S_{1}
,

\displaystyle\varphi(x+y)=\varphi(x)+\varphi(y),\ \varphi(x\cdot y)=\varphi(x)\cdot\varphi(y),\ \varphi(0_{S_{1}})=0_{S_{2}},\ \text{and}\ \varphi(1_{S_{1}})=1_{S_{2}}.

Given a semiring homomorphism
\varphi:S_{1}\to S_{2}
, we call the pair
(S_{2},\varphi)
(for short,
S_{2}
) a
S_{1}
-algebra
.
For a semiring
S_{1}
, a map
\psi:(S_{2},\varphi)\to(S_{2}^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})
between
S_{1}
-algebras is a
S_{1}
-algebra homomorphism
if
\psi
is a semiring homomorphism and
\varphi^{\prime}=\psi\circ\varphi
.
When there is no confusion, we write
\psi:S_{2}\to S_{2}^{\prime}
simply.

If a semiring homomorphism
\varphi:S_{1}\to S_{2}
is injective and both
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are semifield, then
S_{2}
is called a
semifield over
S_{1}
.
Then for
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in S_{2}\setminus\{0_{S_{2}}\}
, we write the smallest subsemifield over
S_{1}
of
S_{2}
that contains
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
as
S_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and call it the semifield over
S_{1}

generated by
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
(we do not use in this paper, but we can also define the same thing for any subset of
S_{2}\setminus\{0_{S_{2}}\}
).
If there exist finitely many
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in S_{2}\setminus\{0_{S_{2}}\}
such that
S_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=S_{2}
, then
S_{2}
is said to be
finitely generated as a semifield over
S_{1}
.

Let
\boldsymbol{R}
be the set of real numbers.
The set
\boldsymbol{T}:=\boldsymbol{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
with two tropical operations:

\displaystyle a\oplus b:=\operatorname{max}\{a,b\}\quad\text{and}\quad a\odot b:=a+b,

where
a,b\in\boldsymbol{T}
, becomes a semifield.
Here, for any
a\in\boldsymbol{T}
, we handle
-\infty
as follows:

\displaystyle a\oplus(-\infty)=(-\infty)\oplus a=a\quad\text{and}\quad a\odot(-\infty)=(-\infty)\odot a=-\infty.

Then
\boldsymbol{T}=(\boldsymbol{T},\oplus,\odot)
is called the
tropical semifield
.
In addition,
\boldsymbol{B}:=(\{0,-\infty\},\operatorname{max},+)
is a subsemifield of
\boldsymbol{T}
called the
Boolean semifield
.

The
tropical polynomials
(resp.
tropical Laurent polynomials
) are defined as polynomials (resp. Laurent polynomials) with respect to tropical operations
\oplus
and
\odot
, where the coefficients are taken in
\boldsymbol{T}
, and the set of all tropical polynomials (resp. tropical Laurent polynomials) in
n
-variables is denoted by
\boldsymbol{T}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
(resp.
\boldsymbol{T}[X_{1}^{\pm},\ldots,X_{n}^{\pm}]
).
It becomes a semiring with two tropical operations
\oplus
and
\odot
and is called the
tropical polynomial semiring
(resp.
tropical Laurent polynomial semiring
).
We often write
\boldsymbol{T}[\boldsymbol{X}^{\pm}]_{n}
instead of
\boldsymbol{T}[X_{1}^{\pm},\ldots,X_{n}^{\pm}]
.
By the same way, we obtain the subsemiring
\boldsymbol{B}[\boldsymbol{X}^{\pm}]_{n}
of
\boldsymbol{T}[\boldsymbol{X}^{\pm}]_{n}
consisting of tropical Laurent polynomials whose coefficients are in
\boldsymbol{B}
together with
-\infty
.
A
tropical Laurent monomial
means a tropical Laurent polynomial of the form
a\odot\boldsymbol{X}^{\odot\boldsymbol{i}}:=a\odot X_{1}^{\odot i_{1}}\odot\cdots\odot X_{n}^{\odot i_{n}}
with
a\in\boldsymbol{R}
and an integer vector
\boldsymbol{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})\in\boldsymbol{Z}^{n}
.
Note that
-\infty
is not a tropical Laurent monomial.
When
\boldsymbol{i}\in\boldsymbol{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
, we call
a\odot\boldsymbol{X}^{\odot\boldsymbol{i}}
a
tropical monomial
, and its
degree
is the sum
i_{1}+\cdots+i_{n}
.
A tropical Laurent polynomial
F
other than
-\infty
is written by the sum of finitely many tropical Laurent monomials, each of which is called a
term
of
F
.
When
F
is a tropical polynomial, its
degree
is defined by the maximum of the degrees of terms of
F
.
For
F=-\infty
, we define that it has degree
-\infty
.

2.2.
Congruences

A
congruence

E
on a semiring
S
is a subset of
S^{2}=S\times S
satisfying

(1)
for any
x\in S
,
(x,x)\in E
,

(2)
if
(x,y)\in E
, then
(y,x)\in E
,

(3)
if
(x,y)\in E
and
(y,z)\in E
, then
(x,z)\in E
,

(4)
if
(x,y)\in E
and
(z,w)\in E
, then
(x+z,y+w)\in E
, and

(5)
if
(x,y)\in E
and
(z,w)\in E
, then
(x\cdot z,y\cdot w)\in E
.

Applying the operations of
S
coordinatewise, the set
S^{2}
becomes a semiring and is a congruence on
S
.
This is called the
improper
congruence on
S
.
Congruences other than the improper congruence are said to be
proper
.
For a congruence
E
on
S
, by
(1),(2)
and
(3)
above, we have the quotient set
S/E
of
S
by
E
.
This forms a semiring by
(4)
and
(5)
above with the following two well-defined operations:
[x]+[y]:=[x+y]
and
[x]\cdot[y]:=[x\cdot y]
for
x,y\in S
, where
[z]
denotes the equivalence class of
z\in S
under
E
.
Then the natural surjection
\pi_{E}:S\twoheadrightarrow S/E;x\mapsto[x]
is a semiring homomorphism.

The intersection of (possibly infinitely many) congruences is again a congruence.
For a subset
T
of
S^{2}
, let
\langle T\rangle
be the smallest congruence on
S
containing
T
, i.e., the intersection of all congruences on
S
containing
T
.
A congruence
E
on
S
is
finitely generated
if there exist a finite number of elements
(a_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,(a_{n},b_{n})\in E
such that
\langle\{(a_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,(a_{n},b_{n})\}\rangle=E
.

For a semiring homomorphism
\psi:S_{1}\to S_{2}
, the
kernel congruence

\operatorname{Ker}(\psi)
of
\psi
is the congruence
\{(x,y)\in S_{1}^{2}\,|\,\psi(x)=\psi(y)\}
.
For semirings and congruences on them, the fundamental homomorphism theorem holds (
[
6
, Proposition 2.4.4]
).
Then, for the above
\pi_{E}
, we have
\operatorname{Ker}(\pi_{E})=E
.

A congruence
P
on a semiring
S
is
prime
if it is proper and
(x_{1}\cdot y_{1}+x_{2}\cdot y_{2},x_{1}\cdot y_{2}+x_{2}\cdot y_{1})\in P
implies that
(x_{1},y_{1})\in P
or
(x_{2},y_{2})\in P
holds.
The
Krull dimension
of
S
is defined by the maximum length of strict inclusions of prime congruences on
S
.
If
S
has no prime congruence, then we define that
S
has Krull dimension
-\infty
.

Let
\overline{E}:=\{(f,g)\in\boldsymbol{T}[\boldsymbol{X}^{\pm}]_{n}^{2}\,|\,\forall x\in\boldsymbol{R}^{n},f(x)=g(x)\}
.
Then
E
is a congruence on
\boldsymbol{T}[\boldsymbol{X}^{\pm}]_{n}
and we write the quotient semiring
\boldsymbol{T}[\boldsymbol{X}^{\pm}]_{n}/\overline{E}
as
\overline{\boldsymbol{T}[\boldsymbol{X}^{\pm}]}_{n}
.
It has the
tropical rational function semifield

\overline{\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{X})}_{n}
in
n
-variables as its semifield of fractions.
An element of
\overline{\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{X})}_{n}\setminus\{-\infty\}
is a
rational function
on
\boldsymbol{R}^{n}
, which is a real-valued function on
\boldsymbol{R}^{n}
defined by the fraction of two tropical (Laurent) polynomials in
n
-variables.
By abuse of notation, we write the image of
X_{i}\in\boldsymbol{T}[\boldsymbol{X}^{\pm}]_{n}
in
\overline{\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{X})}_{n}
again
X_{i}
.

For a subset
V\subset\boldsymbol{R}^{n}
, let
\boldsymbol{E}(V)
be the subset
\{(f,g)\in\overline{\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{X})}_{n}^{2}\,|\,\forall x\in V,f(x)=g(x)\}
, which is a congruence on
\overline{\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{X})}_{n}
.
Similarly, for a subset
T\subset\overline{\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{X})}_{n}^{2}
, let
\boldsymbol{V}(T):=\{x\in\boldsymbol{R}^{n}\,|\,\forall(f,g)\in T,f(x)=g(x)\}
.
Since
\boldsymbol{V}(T)=\boldsymbol{V}(\langle T\rangle)
holds by definition, we call
\boldsymbol{V}(T)
the
congruence variety associated with
T
.

2.3.
Polyhedral sets

A
polyhedral set
in
\boldsymbol{R}^{n}
is the solution set of a system of a finite number of linear inequalities (in the usual sense).
A
polyhedral complex

X
is a complex consisting of a finite number of polyhedral sets.
Each polyhedral set in
X
is a
cell
.
Its
support

|X|
is the union of its polyhedral sets.
When
|X|
is connected as a subset of
\boldsymbol{R}^{n}
, we say that
X
is
connected
.
A finite union of polyhedral sets in
\boldsymbol{R}^{n}
is the support of a polyhedral complex in
\boldsymbol{R}^{n}
, and vice versa (cf.
[
27
, Proposition 4.1.1(a)]
).

For a nonempty polyhedral set
P
in
\boldsymbol{R}^{n}
, its
dimension

\operatorname{dim}P
is defined by the dimension of the smallest affine subspace of
\boldsymbol{R}^{n}
containing
P
.
If
P
is empty, we define that it has dimension
-1
.
The
dimension

\operatorname{dim}X
of a polyhedral complex
X
consisting of polyhedral sets
P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}
is defined as
\operatorname{max}\{\operatorname{dim}P_{i}\,|\,i=1,\ldots,m\}
.
We say that
|X|
has dimension
\operatorname{dim}X
, i.e.,
\operatorname{dim}(|X|):=\operatorname{dim}X
.
Since
X
consists of only a finite number of polyhedral sets,
\operatorname{dim}(|X|)
is independent of the choice of its
polyhedral structures
, i.e., polyhedral complexes with
|X|
as their supports.

A polyhedral set
P
in
\boldsymbol{R}^{n}
is
\boldsymbol{R}
-rational
if
P
is of the form
\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\boldsymbol{R}^{n}\,|\,A\boldsymbol{x}\geq\boldsymbol{b}\}
with some
l\times n
-matrix
A
with rational entries and some vector
\boldsymbol{b}\in\boldsymbol{R}^{l}
.
A polyhedral complex is
\boldsymbol{R}
-rational
if it consists only of
\boldsymbol{R}
-rational polyhedral sets.

For a congruence on
\overline{\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{X})}_{n}
, if it is finitely generated as a congruence, then the associated congruence variety is a finite union of
\boldsymbol{R}
-rational polyhedral sets in
\boldsymbol{R}^{n}
by
[
18
, Corollary 3.5]
.
For a subset
V
of
\boldsymbol{R}^{n}
, the congruence
\boldsymbol{E}(V)
on
\overline{\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{X})}_{n}
is finitely generated if and only if the closure of
V
for the Euclidean topology of
\boldsymbol{R}^{n}
is a finite union of
\boldsymbol{R}
-rational polyhedral sets by
[
18
, Theorem 1.1]
.
By
[
20
, Corollary 3.30]
, for a nonempty finite union
V
of
\boldsymbol{R}
-rational polyhedral sets in
\boldsymbol{R}^{n}
, the Krull dimension of
\overline{\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{X})}_{n}/\boldsymbol{E}(V)
is equal to the dimension of
V
(as the support of a polyhedral complex) plus one.

For a tropical Laurent polynomial
F\in\boldsymbol{T}[\boldsymbol{X}^{\pm}]_{n}\setminus\{-\infty\}
, we define the
tropical hypersurface

\boldsymbol{V}(F)
defined by
F
as

\displaystyle\{x\in\boldsymbol{R}^{n}\,|\,F\text{ has at least two terms that take the value }F(x)\}.

For
F=-\infty\in\boldsymbol{T}[\boldsymbol{X}^{\pm}]_{n}
, we set
\boldsymbol{V}(-\infty)=\boldsymbol{R}^{n}
.
Note that if
F
is a tropical Laurent monomial, i.e., it has just one term, then
\boldsymbol{V}(F)=\varnothing
, and vice versa.
It is well-known that
\boldsymbol{V}(F)
has a connected weighted one-dimensional
\boldsymbol{R}
-rational polyhedral complex structure satisfying the balancing condition when
n=2
and
F
is not a tropical Laurent monomial or
-\infty
(see
[
26
, Proposition 3.1.6 and Theorem 3.3.5]
for more details and more general cases).
Here
weighted
means that its maximal cells (hence, in this case, one-dimensional cells) have positive integers called their
weights
.
In particular, the weights are naturally defined by
F
.
Also satisfying the
balancing condition
means that for any zero-dimensional cell
x
, if
P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}
are the one-dimensional cells incident to
x
, then the sum of primitive vectors from
x
to
P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}
times their weights, respectively, is the zero vector.
A vector in
\boldsymbol{R}^{n}
is
primitive
if it is in
\boldsymbol{Z}^{n}
and the greatest common divisor of the components is one.
For a primitive vector
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})\in\boldsymbol{Z}^{n}
and a nonnegative number
\lambda\geq 0
, the vector
\lambda\boldsymbol{v}=(\lambda v_{1},\ldots,\lambda v_{n})
has
\lambda
as its
lattice length
.

2.4.
Tropical curves

In this paper, a
graph
is an unweighted, undirected, finite, connected nonempty multigraph that may have loops.
For a graph
G
, the set of vertices is denoted by
V(G)
and the set of edges by
E(G)
.
A vertex
v
of
G
is a
leaf end
if
v
is incident to only one edge and this edge is not a loop.
A
leaf edge
is an edge of
G
incident to a leaf end.

An
abstract tropical curve
is the underlying topological space of the pair
(G,l)
of a graph
G
and a function
l:E(G)\to{\boldsymbol{R}}_{>0}\cup\{\infty\}
, where
l
can take the value
\infty
only on leaf edges, together with an identification of each edge
e
of
G
with the closed interval
[0,l(e)]
.
The interval
[0,\infty]
is the one-point compactification of the interval
[0,\infty)
.
We regard
[0,\infty]
not just as a topological space but as an extended metric space.
The distance between
\infty
and any other point is infinite.
When
l(e)=\infty
, the leaf end of
e
must be identified with
\infty
.
If
E(G)=\{e\}
and
l(e)=\infty
, then we can identify either leaf ends of
e
with
\infty
.
In what follows, we say abstract tropical curves just tropical curves.
When a tropical curve
\varGamma
is obtained from
(G,l)
, the pair
(G,l)
is called a
model
for
\varGamma
.
There are many possible models for
\varGamma
.
An
edge
of
\varGamma
means an edge of
G
for some model
(G,l)
for
\varGamma
.
We frequently identify a vertex (resp. an edge) of
G
with the corresponding point (resp. the corresponding closed subset) of
\varGamma
.
A model
(G,l)
is
loopless
if
G
is loopless.
For a point
x
of
\varGamma
, if
x
is identified with
\infty
, then
x
is called a
point at infinity
, otherwise,
x
is called a
finite point
.
Let
\varGamma_{\infty}
denote the set of all points at infinity of
\varGamma
.
If
x
is a finite point, then the
valence

\operatorname{val}(x)
is the number of connected components of
U\setminus\{x\}
with any sufficiently small connected neighborhood
U
of
x
; if
x
is a point at infinity, then
\operatorname{val}(x):=1
.
We construct a model
(G_{\circ},l_{\circ})
called the
canonical model
for
\varGamma
as follows.
Generally, we define
V(G_{\circ}):=\{x\in\varGamma\,|\,\operatorname{val}(x)\not=2\}
except for the following two cases.
When
\varGamma
is homeomorphic to a circle
S^{1}
, we define
V(G_{\circ})
as the set consisting of one arbitrary point of
\varGamma
.
When
\varGamma
has the pair
(T,l)
as its model, where
T
is a tree consisting of three vertices and two edges and
l(E(T))=\{\infty\}
, we define
V(G_{\circ})
as the set of two points at infinity and any finite point of
\varGamma
.
A
ray
of
\varGamma
is an edge of
G
of length infinity for some model
(G,l)
for
\varGamma
, so it contains a point at infinity.
A
subgraph
of
\varGamma
is a closed subset of
\varGamma
with a finite number of connected components.

2.5.
Rational functions and chip firing moves

Let
\varGamma
be a tropical curve.
A continuous map
f:\varGamma\to\boldsymbol{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
is a
rational function
on
\varGamma
if
f
is identically
-\infty
or a piecewise affine function with integer slopes, with a finite number of pieces and that can take the values
\pm\infty
at only points at infinity.
Let
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
denote the set of all rational functions on
\varGamma
.
For rational functions
f,g\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
and a point
x\in\varGamma\setminus\varGamma_{\infty}
, we define

\displaystyle(f\oplus g)(x):=\operatorname{max}\{f(x),g(x)\}\quad\text{and}\quad(f\odot g)(x):=f(x)+g(x).

We extend
f\oplus g
and
f\odot g
to points at infinity to be continuous on the whole of
\varGamma
.
Then both are rational functions on
\varGamma
.
Note that for any
f\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
, we have

\displaystyle f\oplus(-\infty)=(-\infty)\oplus f=f\quad\text{and}\quad f\odot(-\infty)=(-\infty)\odot f=-\infty.

Then
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
becomes a semifield with these two operations.
Also,
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
becomes a
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra with the natural inclusion
\boldsymbol{T}\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
.
Note that for
f,g\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
, the equality
f=g
means that
f(x)=g(x)
for any
x\in\varGamma
.

Let
\varGamma^{\prime}
be a subgraph of
\varGamma
which has no connected components consisting of only a point at infinity and
l
a positive number or infinity.
The
chip firing move
by
\varGamma^{\prime}
and
l
is defined as the rational function
\operatorname{CF}(\varGamma^{\prime},l)(x):=-\operatorname{min}\{\operatorname{dist}(\varGamma^{\prime},x),l\}
with
x\in\varGamma
, where
\operatorname{dist}(\varGamma^{\prime},x)
denotes the distance between
\varGamma^{\prime}
and
x
in
\varGamma
.

2.6.
Divisors and
\boldsymbol{T}
-modules

Let
\varGamma
be a tropical curve.
A
divisor
on
\varGamma
is an element of the free abelian group
\operatorname{Div}(\varGamma)
generated by the points of
\varGamma
.
For a divisor
D
on
\varGamma
and a point
x
of
\varGamma
, we write the coefficient of
D
at
x
as
D(x)
.
The sum of coefficients of
D
at all points is denoted by
\operatorname{deg}(D)
, and called the
degree
of
D
.
If all coefficients of
D
are nonnegative, then
D
is said to be
effective
and written by
D\geq 0
.

For a finite point
x
of
\varGamma
, a rational function
f\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)\setminus\{-\infty\}
and one outgoing direction at
x
, the
outgoing slope
of
f
at
x
is the ratio
\frac{f(y)-f(x)}{\operatorname{dist}(x,y)}
(in the usual sense) with a finite point
y
in a sufficiently small neighborhood of
x
in the direction.
If
x
is a point at infinity, then we regard the
outgoing slope
of
f
at
x
as the slope of
f
from
y
to
x
times minus one, where
y
is a finite point on the leaf edge incident to
x
such that
f
has a constant slope on the interval
(y,x)
.
In both cases, the definition of outgoing slope of
f
at
x
in the direction is independent of the choice of
y
.
The point
x
is a
zero
(resp.
pole
) of
f
if the sign of the sum of outgoing slopes of
f
at
x
is positive (resp. negative).
The absolute value of the sum is its
degree
.
By the definition of rational functions on tropical curves,
f
has at most finitely many zeros and poles.
For the zeros
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\varGamma
with degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
, respectively, and the poles
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\in\varGamma
with degrees
s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}
, respectively, of
f
, let
\operatorname{div}(f)
be the divisor
\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}x_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}s_{j}y_{j}\in\operatorname{Div}(\varGamma)
, which is called the
principal divisor
defined by
f
.
When
f\in\boldsymbol{R}
, we consider
\operatorname{div}(f)=0\in\operatorname{Div}(\varGamma)
, and for
f=-\infty
, we do not define its principal divisor.
For a divisor
D\in\operatorname{Div}(\varGamma)
, let
R(D):=\{f\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)\setminus\{-\infty\}\,|\,D+\operatorname{div}(f)\geq 0\}\cup\{-\infty\}
.
It is known that
R(D)
is a finitely generated
\boldsymbol{T}
-module by
[
8
, Theorem 6]
(in the case of excluding points at infinity,
[
14
, Theorem 3.15]
in the case of including such points).
Here a subset
R
of
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
is a
\boldsymbol{T}
-module
(in
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
) means that for any
f,g\in R
and
t\in\boldsymbol{T}
, the sum
f\oplus g
and the tropical scalar multiplication
t\odot f
are in
R
.
Also a subset
R
of
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
is
finitely generated as a
\boldsymbol{T}
-module
means that there exist finitely many
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
in
R
such that for any
f\in R
, there exist
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in\boldsymbol{T}
satisfying
f=t_{1}\odot f_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus t_{n}\odot f_{n}
.
Let
R
be a
\boldsymbol{T}
-module in
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
.
An element
f\in R\setminus\{-\infty\}
is an
extremal
if for any
g,h\in R
, the equality
f=g\oplus h
implies
f=g
or
f=h
.
By
[
8
, Proposition 8]
, any finitely generated
\boldsymbol{T}
-module
R\not=\{-\infty\}
in
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
is generated by extremals in
R
and this generating set is minimal and unique up to tropical scalar multiplication other than
-\infty
.

One might expect that for a tropical curve
\varGamma
, any finitely generated
\boldsymbol{T}
-module in
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
is of the form
R(D)
for some divisor
D
.
However, this is false:

Example 2.1
.

Let
\varGamma:=[-\infty,\infty]
, which is regarded as a tropical curve.

For the rational function
f(x):=2x
for
x\in\varGamma
, the
\boldsymbol{T}
-module
R
generated by
f
is not equal to
R(D)
for any
D\in\operatorname{Div}(\varGamma)
.
In fact, the map
\varGamma\setminus\varGamma_{\infty}=(-\infty,\infty)\to\boldsymbol{R};x\mapsto f(x)
is injective, and if
R=R(D)
holds for some divisor
D
, then
f
generates
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
as a semifield over
\boldsymbol{T}
by
[
18
, Corollary 3.21]
.
But this is a contradiction, clearly
f
does not generate
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
.

By the same reason, for
g(x):=-x
with
x\leq 0
and
g(x):=x
with
x>0
and
h(x):=0
with
x\leq 0
and
h(x):=x
with
0<x\leq 1
and
h(x):=1
with
x>1
, the
\boldsymbol{T}
-module generated by
g
and
h
is never equal to
R(D)
for any divisor
D
on
\varGamma
.

2.7.
Morphisms between tropical curves

Let
\varphi:\varGamma\to\varGamma^{\prime}
be a continuous map between tropical curves.
This
\varphi
is a
morphism
if there exist loopless models
(G,l)
and
(G^{\prime},l^{\prime})
for
\varGamma
and
\varGamma^{\prime}
, respectively, such that
\varphi
can be regarded as a map
V(G)\cup E(G)\to V(G^{\prime})\cup E(G^{\prime})
satisfying
\varphi(V(G))\subset V(G^{\prime})
and for
e\in\varphi(E(G))
, there exists a nonnegative integer
\operatorname{deg}_{e}(\varphi)
such that for any finite points
x
and
y
of
e
, the equality
\operatorname{dist}_{\varphi(e)}(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))=\operatorname{deg}_{e}(\varphi)\cdot\operatorname{dist}_{e}(x,y)
holds, where
\operatorname{dist}_{\varphi(e)}(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))
(resp.
\operatorname{dist}_{e}(x,y)
) denotes the distance between
\varphi(x)
and
\varphi(y)
in
\varphi(e)
(resp.
x
and
y
in
e
).
This integer
\operatorname{deg}_{e}(\varphi)
is called the
degree
of
\varphi
on
e
.

For a morphism
\varphi:\varGamma\to\varGamma^{\prime}
and
f^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma^{\prime})
, it is easy to check that the composition
f^{\prime}\circ\varphi
is a rational function on
\varGamma
.
The induced map
\varphi^{\ast}:\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma^{\prime})\to\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma);f^{\prime}\mapsto f^{\prime}\circ\varphi
is called the
pull-back map
or the
pull-back
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism
of
\varphi
, which is a
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism.

2.8.
Pseudodirect products and disconnected tropical curves

Let
S_{i}
be a semifield over
\boldsymbol{T}
.
The
pseudodirect product

S_{1}\bowtie S_{2}:=(S_{1}\bowtie S_{2},+,\cdot)
of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
is defined by

\displaystyle S_{1}\bowtie S_{2}

\displaystyle:=\{(s_{1},s_{2})\in(S_{1}\setminus\{0_{S_{1}}\})\times(S_{2}\setminus\{0_{S_{2}}\})\}\cup\{(0_{S_{1}},0_{S_{2}})\},

\displaystyle(s_{1},s_{2})+(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle:=(s_{1}+t_{1},s_{2}+t_{2}),\text{ and}

\displaystyle(s_{1},s_{2})\cdot(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle:=(s_{1}\cdot t_{1},s_{2}\cdot t_{2}).

This is a semifield over
\boldsymbol{T}
with the diagonal semiring homomorphism
\boldsymbol{T}\hookrightarrow S_{1}\bowtie S_{2};t\mapsto(t,t)
by
[
20
, Lemma 3.32]
and has the universal mapping property below.
This
S_{1}\bowtie S_{2}
is a subsemifield over
\boldsymbol{T}
of the
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra
S_{1}\times S_{2}
equipped with the operations of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
coordinatewise, respectively.
Note that
S_{1}\bowtie S_{2}
has the natural surjective
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism
\pi_{i}:S_{1}\bowtie S_{2}\twoheadrightarrow S_{i};(s_{1},s_{2})\not=(0_{S_{1}},0_{S_{2}})\mapsto s_{i},(0_{S_{1}},0_{S_{2}})\mapsto 0_{S_{i}}
by
[
20
, Lemma 3.33]
.
We also write
S_{1}\bowtie S_{2}
as
\bowtie_{i=1}^{2}S_{i}
.

The pseudodirect product was originally defined for more general semirings, see
[
7
, Chapter 2]
.

Proposition 2.2

(Universal mapping property)
.

In the above setting, for a semifield
T
over
\boldsymbol{T}
and a
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism
\psi_{i}:T\to S_{i}
with
i=1,2
, there exists a unique
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism
\psi:T\to S_{1}\bowtie S_{2}
satisfying
\psi_{i}=\pi_{i}\circ\psi
with
i=1,2
.

Proof.

For
a\in T
, if
\psi_{1}(a)=0_{S_{1}}
or
\psi_{2}(a)=0_{S_{2}}
, then
a
must be
0_{T}
.
In fact, since both
T
and
S_{i}
are semifields over
\boldsymbol{T}
and
\psi_{i}
is a
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism, the image
\psi_{i}(T)
is a subsemifield over
\boldsymbol{T}
of
S_{i}
, and hence
a
must be
0_{T}
when
\psi_{i}(a)=0_{S_{i}}
by
[
19
, Lemma 2.2]
.
Thus for any
a\in T\setminus\{0_{T}\}
, the pair
(\psi_{1}(a),\psi_{2}(a))
defines an element of
S_{1}\bowtie S_{2}
.
Defining
\psi
by the correspondence
a\not=0_{T}\mapsto(\psi_{1}(a),\psi_{2}(a)),0_{T}\mapsto(0_{S_{1}},0_{S_{2}})
, it becomes a
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism
T\to S_{1}\bowtie S_{2}
as
t\in\boldsymbol{T}\subset T
is mapped to
(t,t)\in\boldsymbol{T}\subset S_{1}\bowtie S_{2}
by
\psi
.
This
\psi
clearly satisfies
\psi_{i}=\pi_{i}\circ\psi
by definition.
Let
\psi^{\prime}:T\to S_{1}\bowtie S_{2}
be a
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorpshim satisfying
\psi_{i}=\pi_{i}\circ\psi^{\prime}
.
Then
\psi=\psi^{\prime}
holds since for any
a\in T\setminus\{0_{T}\}
, when
\psi^{\prime}(a)=(b_{1},b_{2})
, each

coincides with
\psi_{i}(a)
by
\psi_{i}=\pi_{i}\circ\psi^{\prime}
and so
\psi^{\prime}(a)=(b_{1},b_{2})=(\psi_{1}(a),\psi_{2}(a))=\psi(a)
and
\psi^{\prime}(0_{T})=(0_{S_{1}},0_{S_{2}})=\psi(0_{T})
.
∎

The following is clear:

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
S_{i}
be a semifield over
\boldsymbol{T}
.
Then the map
(S_{1}\bowtie S_{2})\bowtie S_{3}\to S_{1}\bowtie(S_{2}\bowtie S_{3});((s_{1},s_{2}),s_{3})\not=((0_{S_{1}},0_{S_{2}}),0_{S_{3}})\mapsto(s_{1},(s_{2},s_{3})),((0_{S_{1}},0_{S_{2}}),0_{S_{3}})\mapsto(0_{S_{1}},(0_{S_{2}},0_{S_{3}}))
is a
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra isomorpshim.

By Proposition
2.3
, we can write
(S_{1}\bowtie S_{2})\bowtie S_{3}
(or
S_{1}\bowtie(S_{2}\bowtie S_{3})
) just
S_{1}\bowtie S_{2}\bowtie S_{3}
.

For two tropical curves
\varGamma_{1}
and
\varGamma_{2}
, by
[
19
, Proposition 3.11]
and
[
20
, Lemma 3.41]
, we can regard
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1})\bowtie\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{2})
as the rational function semifield of the direct sum (as a topological space)
\varGamma_{1}\sqcup\varGamma_{2}
of
\varGamma_{1}
and
\varGamma_{2}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1}\sqcup\varGamma_{2}):=\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1})\bowtie\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{2}).

Here
\varGamma_{1}\sqcup\varGamma_{2}
has the same metric as
\varGamma_{i}
as in the image of the natural inclusion
\varGamma_{i}\hookrightarrow\varGamma_{1}\sqcup\varGamma_{2}
for each

.
We do not define the distance between the points of the image of
\varGamma_{1}

\varGamma_{2}
.
Thus, for a subgraph
\varGamma^{\prime}_{i}
of

(resp.

(\operatorname{CF}(\varGamma^{\prime}_{1},l),0)\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1}\sqcup\varGamma_{2})
(resp.
(\operatorname{CF}(\varGamma^{\prime}_{1},l),\operatorname{CF}(\varGamma^{\prime}_{2},l))\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1}\sqcup\varGamma_{2})
).
We write the addition and multiplication in
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1}\sqcup\varGamma_{2})
as

and

, respectively.
In
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1}\sqcup\varGamma_{2})
, the identity

with respect to

.

Note that
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1}\sqcup\varGamma_{2})
is finitely generated as a semifield over

by

, Theorem 1.1]
and
[

For a surjective

-algebra homomorphism

, the congruence variety
\boldsymbol{V}(\operatorname{Ker}(\psi))
is a finite union of

-rational polyhedral sets in

with two connected components each of which is isometric to

and
\varGamma_{2}\setminus\varGamma_{2,\infty}
with the lattice length, respectively, and

, Corollary 3.19]
and
[

, Corollary 3.43]
.

Remark 2.4
.

In the above setting, we call the direct sum
\varGamma_{1}\sqcup\varGamma_{2}
a
disconnected tropical curve
(with two connected components).
We can also define disconnected tropical curves with a finite number of connected components in the same way.
Then we can characterize the rational function semifields of such disconnected tropical curves as in
[
18
, Corollary 3.19]
or Theorem
1.1
without condition
(3)
by the same proofs in
[
18
, Section 3]
.
But since we do not use this fact in this paper, to avoid redundancy, we do not more discuss about these ones.

Note here that the definition of rational function semifields of disconnected tropical curves is provisional.
As an analogue of the classical algebraic geometry, it might be more natural employing the product

3.3

4.3
and

For a disconnected tropical curve

is a ray of a connected component of
\varGamma

Theorem 3.1

\varGamma

So far,

3.2.
Subgraphs

In this subsection, we investigate the relation between subgraphs (other than points at infinity) of tropical curves and surjective
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphisms between their rational function semifields.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\varGamma
be a tropical curve and
\varGamma^{\prime}
a connected subgraph of
\varGamma
that is not contained in
\varGamma_{\infty}
.
Then, regarding
\varGamma^{\prime}
itself as a tropical curve, the restriction
f|_{\varGamma^{\prime}}
on
\varGamma^{\prime}
for any
f\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
is in
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma^{\prime})
and the restriction map
\psi:\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)\to\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma^{\prime}):f\mapsto f|_{\varGamma^{\prime}}
is a surjective
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism.

Proof.

Since
\varGamma^{\prime}\not\subset\varGamma_{\infty}
, the restriction
f|_{\varGamma^{\prime}}
for any
f\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
is in
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma^{\prime})
, that is,
f|_{\varGamma^{\prime}}
is never a constant function of
\infty
.

Clearly
\psi
is a
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism and if
\varGamma^{\prime}=\varGamma
, then
\psi
is surjective.

Assume that
\varGamma^{\prime}\subset\varGamma\setminus\varGamma_{\infty}
.
Let
f^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma^{\prime})\setminus\{-\infty\}
.
Since
\varGamma^{\prime}
is a subgraph of
\varGamma
contained in
\varGamma\setminus\varGamma_{\infty}
, the rational function
f^{\prime}
takes some real minimum value
a\in\boldsymbol{R}
on
\varGamma^{\prime}
.
If there exists
f\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
such that
\psi(f)=a^{\odot(-1)}\odot f^{\prime}
, then
\psi(a\odot f)=f^{\prime}
holds as
\psi
is a
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism.
Hence it is enough to show the case
a=0
, and assume this.
For a negative integer
s
whose absolute value is sufficiently large, let
g
be the function of
\varGamma
such that
(1)

g
coincides with
f^{\prime}
on
\varGamma^{\prime}
and
(2)

g
has
s
as its slope in the outgoing direction at each boundary point
x
of
\varGamma^{\prime}
in
\varGamma
until it takes the value zero and
(3)

g
is constant zero the outside of the union of
\varGamma^{\prime}
and all such segments incident to each
x
.
Then
g\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
and
\psi(g)=f^{\prime}
hold by definition, and thus
\psi
is surjective in this case.

Assume that
\varGamma^{\prime}\not=\varGamma
and
\varGamma^{\prime}\cap\varGamma_{\infty}\not=\varnothing
.
Let
L^{\prime}\subset\varGamma^{\prime}
be a ray of
\varGamma
.
Then
\psi(\operatorname{CF}(\overline{\varGamma\setminus L^{\prime}},\infty))\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma^{\prime})
holds, where
\overline{\varGamma\setminus L^{\prime}}
denotes the closure of
\varGamma\setminus L^{\prime}
in
\varGamma
.
By the previous case and
[
15
, the proof of Lemma 1.4]
, the
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism
\psi
is surjective.
∎

The converse of Proposition
3.3
holds as follows.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\varGamma
and
\varGamma^{\prime}
be tropical curves and
\psi:\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma^{\prime})
a surjective
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism.
Then the corresponding injective morphism
\iota:\varGamma^{\prime}\hookrightarrow\varGamma
such that
\psi=\iota^{\ast}
is a local isometry and its image
\operatorname{Im}(\iota)
is not contained in
\varGamma_{\infty}
.

Proof.

The existence of the injective morphism
\iota
that satisfies
\psi(f)=f\circ\iota
for any
f\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
and
\operatorname{Im}(\iota)\not\subset\varGamma_{\infty}
is guaranteed by
[
19
, Proposition 3.11 and Theorem 3.14]
.
For any
x^{\prime}\in\varGamma^{\prime}\setminus\varGamma^{\prime}_{\infty}
, since
\psi
is surjective, there exists
f\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
such that
\operatorname{CF}(\{x^{\prime}\},\infty)=\psi(f)=f\circ\iota
.
For an edge
e^{\prime}
of
\varGamma^{\prime}
containing
x^{\prime}
and any point
y^{\prime}\in e^{\prime}
close enough to
x^{\prime}
, since
0=\operatorname{CF}(\{x^{\prime}\},\infty)(x^{\prime})=(f\circ\iota)(x^{\prime})=f(\iota(x^{\prime}))
and
-\operatorname{dist}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\operatorname{CF}(\{x^{\prime}\},\infty)(y^{\prime})=(f\circ\iota)(y^{\prime})=f(\iota(y^{\prime}))
, the slope of
f
from
\iota(x^{\prime})
to
\iota(y^{\prime})
is
-\frac{\operatorname{dist}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}{\operatorname{dist}(\iota(x^{\prime}),\iota(y^{\prime}))}\in\boldsymbol{Z}
.
As
\varphi
is an injective morphism, the inequality
\operatorname{dist}(\iota(x^{\prime}),\iota(y^{\prime}))\geq\operatorname{dist}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
holds, and so
\operatorname{dist}(\iota(x^{\prime}),\iota(y^{\prime}))
must be
\operatorname{dist}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, that is,
\iota
is a local isometry.
∎

By these two propositions, we can identify a connected subgraph
\varGamma^{\prime}
of a tropical curve
\varGamma
not contained in
\varGamma_{\infty}
with a surjective
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma^{\prime})
.
More generally, we can identify a (possibly disconnected) subgraph
\varGamma^{\prime}
of
\varGamma
whose every connected component
\varGamma_{i}^{\prime}
is not contained in
\varGamma_{\infty}
with a surjective
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism
\psi:\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)\twoheadrightarrow\bowtie_{i=1}^{l}\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{i}^{\prime})
as follows.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\varGamma
be a tropical curve and
\varGamma^{\prime}
a subgraph of
\varGamma
with connected components
\varGamma^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\varGamma^{\prime}_{l}
such that
\varGamma^{\prime}_{j}\not\subset\varGamma_{\infty}
for each
j=1,\ldots,l
.
Then the
l
-tuple
(f|_{\varGamma^{\prime}_{1}},\ldots,f|_{\varGamma^{\prime}_{l}})
for any
f\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
is in
\bowtie_{i=1}^{l}\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{i}^{\prime})
and the restriction map
\psi:\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)\to\bowtie_{i=1}^{l}\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{i}^{\prime});f\mapsto(f|_{\varGamma^{\prime}_{1}},\ldots,f|_{\varGamma^{\prime}_{l}})
is a surjective
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism.
Moreover for each natural surjective
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism
\pi_{j}:\bowtie_{i=1}^{l}\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{i}^{\prime})\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{j}^{\prime})
, the composition
\pi_{j}\circ\psi
coincides with the restriction map
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{j}^{\prime});f\mapsto f|_{\varGamma_{j}^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

The existence and definition of
\psi
is clear by Propositions
3.3
and
2.2
.
Since
\varGamma_{i}^{\prime}\cap\varGamma^{\prime}_{j}=\varnothing
for
i\not=j
, for any
f_{i}\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma^{\prime}_{i})\setminus\{-\infty\}
, there exists
g_{i}\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
such that
g_{i}|_{\varGamma^{\prime}_{i}}=f_{i}
and
g|_{\varGamma^{\prime}_{j}}=0
for
j\not=i
.
Then
\psi(g_{i})=(0,\ldots,0,f_{i},0,\ldots,0)
, where
f_{i}
is the
i
th component, and thus
\psi(g_{1}\odot\cdots\odot g_{l})=\psi(g_{1})\odot\cdots\odot\psi(g_{l})=(f_{1},0,\ldots,0)\odot\cdots\odot(0,\ldots,0,f_{l})=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{l})
.
This means that
\psi
is surjective.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\varGamma
and
\varGamma_{i}
be tropical curves and
\psi:\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)\twoheadrightarrow\bowtie_{i=1}^{l}\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{i})
a surjective
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism.
Then the corresponding injective map
\iota:\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{l}\varGamma_{i}^{\prime}\hookrightarrow\varGamma
satisfying
\psi(f)=f\circ\iota
for any
f\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
is a local isometry and each connected component of its image
\operatorname{Im}(\iota)
is not contained in
\varGamma_{\infty}
.

Proof.

It is clear by
[
20
, Lemma 3.41]
,
[
19
, Proposition 3.11 and Theorem 3.14]
and Proposition
3.4
.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

From Propositions
3.5
and

, we improve the definition of subgraphs of tropical curves by adding the condition that they have no connected components consisting of only one point at infinity.
In what follows, we always use the word “subgraphs of tropical curves” in this manner.

Also we can generalize these propositions by replacing

3.3.
Gluing and decomposition

In this subsection, we give the notion of gluing tropical curves along subgraphs, and translate this into algebraic language.

Definition 3.8
.

Let
\varGamma_{i}
and
\varGamma^{\prime}
be tropical curves, respectively, and
\psi_{i}:\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{i})\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma^{\prime})
a surjective
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism.
For the natural surjective
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism
\pi_{i}:\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1})\bowtie\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{2})\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{i});(f_{1},f_{2})\mapsto f_{i}
, we define

\displaystyle~\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1})\bowtie_{\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma^{\prime})}\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{2})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle~\{h\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1})\bowtie\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{2})\,|\,\psi_{1}(\pi_{1}(h))=\psi_{2}(\pi_{2}(h))\}.

As in the setting of Definition
3.8
, let
\iota_{i}:\varGamma^{\prime}\hookrightarrow\varGamma_{i}
be the injective morphism that is a local isometry such that
\psi_{i}=\iota_{i}^{\ast}
and
\varGamma_{1}\sqcup_{\varGamma^{\prime}}\varGamma_{2}
the tropical curve obtained by gluing
\varGamma_{1}
and
\varGamma_{2}
via
\iota_{1}
and
\iota_{2}
.
More explicitly,
\varGamma_{1}\sqcup_{\varGamma^{\prime}}\varGamma_{2}
is the quotient set of the disconnected tropical curve
\varGamma_{1}\sqcup\varGamma_{2}
by the equivalence relation
\sim
generated by

\displaystyle x_{1}\sim x_{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x_{1}\in\iota_{1}(\varGamma^{\prime})\text{ and }x_{2}=\iota_{2}(\iota_{1}^{-1}(x_{1}))

for
x_{i}\in\varGamma_{i}
.
Since
\iota_{i}
is an injective local isometry,
\varGamma_{1}\sqcup_{\varGamma^{\prime}}\varGamma_{2}
has the natural topology and metric structure that inherit those of
\varGamma_{i}
.

Proposition 3.9
.

In the above setting, the set
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1})\bowtie_{\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma^{\prime})}\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{2})
is a subsemifiled over
\boldsymbol{T}
of
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1})\bowtie\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{2})
isomorphic to
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1}\sqcup_{\varGamma^{\prime}}\varGamma_{2})
as a
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra.

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1})\bowtie\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{2})
is a semifield over
\boldsymbol{T}
via the diagonal map
\boldsymbol{T}\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1})\bowtie\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{2});t\mapsto(t,t)
and the composition
\psi_{i}\circ\pi_{i}
is a
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism,
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1})\bowtie_{\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma^{\prime})}\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{2})
contains
\boldsymbol{T}
naturally and is closed by the operations taking tropical sum
\oplus
, tropical multiplication
\odot
, and tropical division
⊙(-1)
, and thus is a subsemifiled over
\boldsymbol{T}
of
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1})\bowtie\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{2})
.

For
f\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1})\bowtie_{\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma^{\prime})}\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{2})
, let
\psi(f)
be the function on
\varGamma_{1}\sqcup_{\varGamma^{\prime}}\varGamma_{2}
defined by

\displaystyle\psi(f)=\begin{cases}\psi_{1}(\pi_{1}(f))\quad\text{on}\quad(\varGamma_{1}\setminus\iota_{1}(\varGamma^{\prime}))\subset\varGamma_{1}\sqcup_{\varGamma^{\prime}}\varGamma_{2},\\
\psi_{2}(\pi_{2}(f))\quad\text{on}\quad(\varGamma_{2}\setminus\iota_{2}(\varGamma^{\prime}))\subset\varGamma_{1}\sqcup_{\varGamma^{\prime}}\varGamma_{2},\\
\psi_{1}(\pi_{1}(f))\quad\text{on}\quad\iota_{1}(\varGamma^{\prime})=\iota_{2}(\varGamma^{\prime})\subset\varGamma_{1}\sqcup_{\varGamma^{\prime}}\varGamma_{2}.\end{cases}

This is well-defined and is a rational function on
\varGamma_{1}\sqcup_{\varGamma^{\prime}}\varGamma_{2}
by definition.
Clearly this map
\psi:\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1})\bowtie_{\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma^{\prime})}\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{2})\to\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1}\sqcup_{\varGamma^{\prime}}\varGamma_{2})
is a
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra isomorphism.
∎

By this proposition, we know that
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1})\bowtie_{\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma^{\prime})}\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{2})
is identified with the glued tropical curve
\varGamma_{1}\sqcup_{\varGamma^{\prime}}\varGamma_{2}
through Corollary
3.2
, and the natural inclusion
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1})\bowtie_{\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma^{\prime})}\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{2})\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{1})\bowtie\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{2})
is regarded as the
decomposition
of the tropical curve
\varGamma_{1}\sqcup_{\varGamma^{\prime}}\varGamma_{2}
into
\varGamma_{1}
and
\varGamma_{2}
when
\varGamma^{\prime}
is a point.
A variant of the notion of decomposition is explained in
[
24
, Example 2.7]
, where it is written by tropical ideals.

Remark 3.10
.

We do not give the proof, but the same thing is true by replacing
\varGamma_{1},\varGamma_{2}
and
\varGamma^{\prime}
with disconnected tropical curves, respectively, in Proposition
3.9

4.1.
(Abstract) tropical curves with parallel rays and rational function semifields

We give a characterization of rational function semifields of tropical curves with parallel rays in this subsection.
To do so, we first define these notions.

Definition 4.1
.

An
(abstract) tropical curve with parallel rays

\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
is an (abstract) tropical curve
\varGamma
whose rays have an equivalence relation
\sim
such that for rays
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
of
\varGamma
, if
L_{1}\subset L_{2}
, then
L_{1}\sim L_{2}
holds.
Then
\varGamma
is called its
underlying tropical curve
and a ray of
\varGamma
a
ray
of
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
.
Two rays of
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
are
parallel
if these are equivalent under
\sim
, otherwise, these are
nonparallel
.

For a tropical curve
\varGamma
, a
parallelization
of
\varGamma
is to give an equivalence relation
\sim
on rays of
\varGamma
satisfying the condition above.

In what follows, we just say tropical curves with parallel rays without “abstract”.
Clearly one tropical curve possibly has several parallelizations.

Remark 4.2
.

Clearly, for a tropical curve
\varGamma
, for rays
L_{1},L_{2}\subset\varGamma
, defining
L_{1}\sim_{\operatorname{triv}}L_{2}
if
L_{1}\subset L_{2}
or
L_{1}\supset L_{2}
, the relation
\sim_{\operatorname{triv}}
is an equivalence relation on rays of
\varGamma
and makes the pair
(\varGamma,\sim_{\operatorname{triv}})
a tropical curve with parallel rays.
Hence Definition
4.1
gives an extended notion of tropical curves.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
be a tropical curve with parallel rays and
\varGamma
the underlying tropical curve of
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
.
A function
f:\varGamma\to\boldsymbol{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
is a
rational function
on
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
if,
f\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
and
f
has the same slope at infinity along any pair of parallel rays of
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
, or
f\equiv-\infty
.
Let
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}})
denote the set of all rational functions on
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
, which is a subset of
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
.
We call
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}})
the
rational function semifield
of
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
.

Proposition 4.4

(cf.
[
15
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

For a tropical curve with parallel rays
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
and its underlying tropical curve
\varGamma
, the set
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}})
is a finitely generated subsemifield over
\boldsymbol{T}
of
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
.

Proof.

Clearly
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}})
is a subsemifield over
\boldsymbol{T}
of
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
.

Let
\varGamma^{\prime}\subset\varGamma
be a connected subgraph which has no rays and the closure of each connected component of
\varGamma\setminus\varGamma^{\prime}
is a ray of
\varGamma
.
Let
L_{11},\ldots,L_{1m_{1}},L_{21},\ldots,L_{km_{k}}
be all distinct rays of
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
, each of which is the closure of a connected component of
\varGamma\setminus\varGamma^{\prime}
and each two of

are parallel and
L_{i1}
and
L_{j1}
are nonparallel for any

.
By definition, the chip firing moves
g_{i}:=\operatorname{CF}\left(\overline{\varGamma\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{m_{i}}L_{ij}},\infty\right)
and
h_{ij}:=\operatorname{CF}\left(L_{ij},\varepsilon\right)
for
\varepsilon>0
are in
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}})
.
Let
x_{ij}
be the unique boundary point of
L_{ij}
in
\varGamma
.
By
[

, the proof of Lemma 1.4]
, for
f\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}})\setminus\{-\infty\}
, the subsemifield over
\boldsymbol{T}

4.3.
Disconnected tropical curves with parallel rays and direct sum

As explained in Subsection
2.8
, the notion of disconnected tropical curves is just defined by the directed sums of tropical curves.
This is so simple since we need not respect the parallelity of rays.
However, in respect of this property, the situation is more complicated, i.e., the direct sum of tropical curves with parallel rays can be regarded as a disconnected tropical curve with parallel rays, but the converse does not hold.
We explain this in this subsection.

Definition 4.22
.

A
disconnected tropical curve with parallel rays

\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
is a disconnected tropical curve
\varGamma=\varGamma_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\varGamma_{n}
possessing an equivalence relation
\sim
on rays of
\varGamma
such that for rays
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
of
\varGamma
, if
L_{1}\subset L_{2}
, then
L_{1}\sim L_{2}
holds.
The disconnected tropical curve
\varGamma
is called its
underlying disconnected tropical curve
.
We call a ray of
\varGamma
a
ray
of
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
.
Two rays of
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
are
parallel
if these are equivalent by
\sim
, otherwise, these are
nonparallel
.

This definition is just an extension of Definition
4.1
and it is quite natural from a geometric point of view.
Hence the following definition is also natural:

Definition 4.23
.

Let
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
be a disconnected tropical curve with parallel rays and
\varGamma
the underlying disconnected tropical curve of
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
.
A function
f:\varGamma\to\boldsymbol{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
is a
rational function
on
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
if,
f\in\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
and
f
has the same slope at infinity along any two parallel rays of
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
, or
f\equiv-\infty
.
Let
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}})
denote the set of all rational functions on
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
, which is a subset of
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
.
We call
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}})
the
rational function semifield
of
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
.

In the setting of Definition
4.23
, clearly
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}})
is a subsemifield over
\boldsymbol{T}
of
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma)
.

For a disconnected tropical curve with parallel rays
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
with connected components
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par},1},\ldots,\varGamma_{\operatorname{par},n}
, where each
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par},i}
is considered as a tropical curve with parallel rays inheriting the equivalence relation from that of
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
, one might expect that
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}})\cong\bowtie_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{\operatorname{par},i})
holds.
However, this is not the case.
See the following example.

Example 4.24
.

Let
\varGamma_{i}
be a copy of the interval
[-\infty,\infty]
.
For the disconnected tropical curve
\varGamma:=\varGamma_{1}\sqcup\varGamma_{2}
and rays

, we define

\displaystyle L_{1}\sim L_{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad-\infty\in L_{1},L_{2}\text{ or }\infty\in L_{1},L_{2},

which is an equivalence relation on rays of
\varGamma
.
Put
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
the disconnected tropical curve with parallel rays defined by the pair
(\varGamma,\sim)
and say
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par},i}
the tropical curve with parallel rays
(\varGamma_{i},\sim_{\varGamma_{i}})
, where
\sim_{\varGamma_{i}}
denotes the restriction of

on rays of
\varGamma_{i}
and it is an equivalence relation.
Note that
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{\operatorname{par},i})
is just
\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{i})
in this case.
Then for the rational function

\displaystyle f(t):=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\quad t<0;\\

\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{\operatorname{par},1})\bowtie\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{\operatorname{par},2})

The reason why such a thing happens is that in the above setting, the pseudodirect product
\bowtie_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Rat}(\varGamma_{\operatorname{par},i})
is isomorphic to the rational function semifield of the direct sum
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par},1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\varGamma_{\operatorname{par},n}
of
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par},1},\ldots,\varGamma_{\operatorname{par},n}
defined as follows:

Definition 4.25
.

Let
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par},i}
be the tropical curve with parallel rays defined by the pair
(\varGamma_{i},\sim_{i})
of a tropical curve
\varGamma_{i}
and an equivalence relation
\sim_{i}
on rays of
\varGamma_{i}
.
The

\varGamma_{\operatorname{par},1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\varGamma_{\operatorname{par},n}

\displaystyle L_{1}\sim L_{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\exists i:L_{1},L_{2}\subset\varGamma_{\operatorname{par},i},L_{1}\sim_{i}L_{2}.

In the setting of Definition

is a local isometry and it clearly induces a
\boldsymbol{T}
-algebra homomorphism

(cf.
[
20

)

In the above setting, the map

4.7.
Finitely generated
\boldsymbol{T}
-modules

In this subsection, we focus on finitely generated
\boldsymbol{T}
-modules in rational function semifields of tropical curves with parallel rays and define their degrees.

Proposition 4.45
.

Let
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
be a tropical curve with parallel rays.
For

, let

denote the
\boldsymbol{T}
-module generated by

.
For any point

and any generators

of

\boldsymbol{T}
-module, the equality

\displaystyle~\operatorname{min}\{\operatorname{div}(f_{1})(x),\ldots,\operatorname{div}(f_{n})(x)\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\operatorname{min}\{\operatorname{div}(g_{1})(x),\ldots,\operatorname{div}(g_{l})(x)\}

holds.

Proof.

If
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
consists of only one point

, then clearly
\operatorname{div}(f_{i})(x)=\operatorname{div}(g_{j})(x)=0
for any

.
Thus the assertion holds.

Assume that
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
does not consist of only one point.
As

and

generate

as a
\boldsymbol{T}
-module, there are
a_{j1},\ldots,a_{jn}\in\boldsymbol{T}
such that

and at least one of

is not
-\infty
.
Then, in any direction from

, the slope of

is greater than or equal to that of

such that

.
This implies that the inequality

\displaystyle~\operatorname{min}\{\operatorname{div}(f_{1})(x),\ldots,\operatorname{div}(f_{n})(x)\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\operatorname{min}\{\operatorname{div}(g_{1})(x),\ldots,\operatorname{div}(g_{l})(x)\}

holds.
As

are also generators of
R
as a
\boldsymbol{T}
-module, by the same argument, the opposite inequality holds.
∎

Note that for a point of
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
, it is a pole of some

if and only if it is a pole of some

by this proposition.
This guaranties that the following definition is independent of the choice of generators of

as a
\boldsymbol{T}
-module.

Definition 4.46
.

As in the setting of Proposition

, let

be the poles of

.

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\operatorname{min}\{\operatorname{div}(f_{1})(x_{i}),\ldots,\operatorname{div}(f_{n})(x_{i})\}

of
R
and write it as
\operatorname{deg}(R)
.
For

, let
\operatorname{deg}(\boldsymbol{T}):=0
and for

, let
\operatorname{deg}(\{-\infty\}):=-\infty
.

With this definition, in the context of
[
8
, Theorem 35]
, the degree of

coincides with
\operatorname{deg}(D)
, see Proposition
4.49
.
Thus, this is a notion that is already partially studied in
[
8
]
.

In addition, we know that this definition depends on the inclusion

.
More precisely, for a finitely generated
\boldsymbol{T}
-module

is isomorphic to

as a
\boldsymbol{T}
-module, these may have different degrees.
For example, for

with

generated by

If

As in the setting of Definition

4.8.
Harmonicity and balancing condition

In this subsection, we give an algebraic description of the balancing condition for tropical curves with parallel rays, as for tropicalized curves in
\boldsymbol{R}^{n}
, in terms of harmonicity.

We define harmonicity at a point of a rational function on a tropical curve with parallel rays.
This is an analogue of the notion of discrete harmonic functions introduced by Heilbronn in
[

]

Definition 4.47
.

Let

Note that the harmonicity at a point is a local property, and hence in fact we need not consider the parallelity of rays, and without the parallelity, the harmonicity is already described in
[

Let
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
be a tropical curve with parallel rays other than one point and

For

, let

-algebra homomorphism.

The following is clear by definition:

Proposition 4.48
.

In the above setting, for

(1)

is harmonic at

(2)

is in the kernel of
\omega
, i.e.,

Let
\varGamma_{\operatorname{par}}
be a tropical curve with parallel rays.

, let

denote the
\boldsymbol{T}
-module generated by

.

.

Assume that

Then there exists

For the last assertion,
