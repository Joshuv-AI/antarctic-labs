Selective isotope separation of 169Er for medical applications
Authors: M. V. Suryanarayana, Dipjyoti Balo Majumder, M. Sankari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We have theoretically investigated the three-step laser isotope separation (LIS) of the 169Er isotope from naturally occurring erbium, irradiated in a medium-flux nuclear reactor for 20 days. The LIS process was analyzed using the density matrix formalism to examine the influence of atomic, laser, and atomic beam source parameters, aiming to optimize the performance of the separation process. This method can be employed to produce 30 to 300 doses equivalent of 169Er / day, each with an activity of 7.4 GBq. The results demonstrate the potential to produce 169Er with high specific activity (1.3 × 10¹⁵ Bq/g), which could enhance the targeting of cancer cells with greater efficacy. This method offers a viable alternative for producing high-specific-activity 169Er without the need for high-flux reactors.

Introduction

177Lu, a radioactive isotope of Lutetium, is a critical component in targeted radionuclide therapy (TRT) for cancer treatment1–3. Widely utilized in theranostic applications, 177Lu serves both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. It has a half-life of 6.6443 days, which is ideal for therapeutic use, offering sufficient time for tumor targeting. The isotope primarily decays by β⁻ emission with a mean energy of 148.8 keV (79.44% of decays), which enables effective tumor cell irradiation along with minimizing radiation exposure to healthy tissues. Additionally, 177Lu emits γ-rays at energies of 112.9 keV (6.23%) and 208.4 keV (10.41%), which are valuable for imaging purposes in single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) scans. Due to its effectiveness in treating non-endocrine tumors (NETs), the demand for 177Lu is high, contributing to significant supply shortages. The primary cause of the 177Lu supply shortage is the limited availability of its enriched parent isotopes, namely,176Lu and 176Yb. Availability of either of these enriched isotopes is critical for the production of 177Lu through neutron activation in nuclear reactors. However, the production of enriched 176Lu and 176Yb is technically challenging and resource-intensive4–18, leading to a constrained supply chain.

On the other hand, 169Eris an isotope with decay characteristics that closely resemble those of 177Lu. A comparison of the properties of 169Erand 177Lu is presented in Table 1. An additional advantage of 169Er is its relatively long half-life, which enables the isotope to be transported over long distances, including to remote locations, without significant loss of activity. Recently19, we have studied enrichment of 168Er through atomic vapor laser isotope separation for the production 169Er isotope.

Previously, Formento-Cavaier et al.20 have investigated the production of 169Er with very high specific activity through mass separation by irradiating enriched 168Er in a high-flux nuclear reactor (a neutron flux of 1.2 × 10¹⁵ n/cm²/s). They achieved a specific activity of approximately 2.40 × 10¹⁴ Bq/g (theoretical no-carrier-added specific activity of 3.044 × 10¹⁵ Bq/g) for 169Er, corresponding to an isotopic purity of ~ 8%.In a similar approach, Zeynep Talip et al.21. employed laser ionization to achieve a separation efficiency of 0.5%.Both methods require two stages of isotope separation. The first stage involves the enrichment of 168Er from natural erbium, typically carried out by the commercial suppliers. In the second stage, mass separation of the 169Er isotope is carried out on the irradiated enriched 168Er. This dual-stage isotope separation process substantially increases the overall cost. Further, irradiation of enriched ¹⁶⁸Er in a high-flux reactor is essential for the production of high-specific activity ¹⁶⁹Er. As per the research reactor database 2025 data published by the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), only five high-flux reactors are currently operational globally for the production of isotopes. These reactors are all located in Western nations. Consequently, this approach is unlikely to meet the growing worldwide demand for the production of high-specific activity medical isotopes for cancer therapy. In contrast, there are 42 reactors worldwide capable of producing isotopes with a neutron flux of less than 1 × 10¹⁴ n/cm²/s. Therefore, developing an alternative method for the production of high-specific activity ¹⁶⁹Erusing low to medium flux reactors would significantly enhance global production capacity, thereby meeting the increasing demand for such crucial therapeutic isotopes.

In the present work, we have theoretically investigated the feasibility of employing single-stage isotope separation for the production of high-specific-activity 169Er from natural erbium irradiated in a medium-flux reactor (1 × 1014 n/cm²/s) and evaluate its suitability for medical applications. Specifically, we explore the atomic vapor laser isotope separation (AVLIS) of 169Er from irradiated natural erbium, using the density matrix formalism22, thereby eliminating the need for the enriched 168Er isotope.

Irradiation of natural erbium

Erbium has five natural isotopes namely 162Er (0.139%), 164Er (1.601%), 166Er (33.503%) 167Er (22.869%) 168Er (26.978%) and 170Er (14.910%). When one gram of natural erbium is irradiated in a low-flux (1 × 1013 n/cm2/s), medium-flux (1 × 1014 n/cm2/s) and high-flux (1 × 1015 n/cm2/s) reactors for 20 days(with no cooling), the amount of 169Er producedis6.7 µg, 70.5 µg and 940 µg respectively (Fig. 1A). Concurrently, it also produces other short lived isotopes namely 163Er (T1/2 = 73 min), 165Er (T1/2 = 10.36 h) and 171Er (T1/2 = 7.516 h) in relatively small quantities. Under the same irradiation parameters mentioned above; for the medium-flux reactor irradiation, the resulting quantities of these short-lived isotopes produced are 17.1 ng, 1.1 µg, and 5.1 µg, respectively (Fig. 1B), which are significantly smaller than the 70.5 µg of 169Er generated. Due to the small quantities produced and the relatively short half-lives of these isotopes, they decay rapidly. As a result, these isotopes have been excluded from studies on the laser isotope separation of 169Er. For the present investigations, we consider that the natural erbium is irradiated in a medium-flux reactor for 20 days with no cooling.

Laser photoionization of erbium

Erbium has a melting point of 1529 °C and a boiling point of 2868 °C. Heating the erbium metal to 1200 °C is required to produce a number density of 4 × 1013atoms/cm3at the oven orifice; during effusion, the density is reduced according to the source geometry. At this temperature, 99.2% of the population lies in the ground state 4f126s23H6 (0.0 cm− 1). Therefore, it is necessary to establish the photoionization schemes originating from the 4f126s23H6 (0.0 cm− 1) ground state. Laser separation of 169Er

In the three-step photoionization process under investigation, erbium atoms in the 4f¹²6s² ³H₆ (0.0 cm⁻¹) ground state are initially excited to the 4f¹¹5d6s², J = 7 (15,846.549 cm⁻¹) excited state by a laser tuned to the 631.052 nm transition. Atoms reaching this first excited state are then further excited to the 4f¹¹5d6s², J = 8 (32,884.867 cm⁻¹) level using a second laser, which is tuned to a 586.912 nm wavelength. In the final step, these excited atoms undergo ionization by being promoted to an auto-ionizing state at 50,552.6 cm⁻¹, induced by a third laser set to a 566.003 nm wavelength. For the purposes of this study, all lasers are assumed to have a Gaussian pulse width of 30 ns and a pulse repetition frequency (PRF) of 10 kHz. The lasers are considered to be co-propagating.

The laser-atom interaction dynamics in the three-step photoionization scheme can be accurately described by the density matrix elements, which have been previously reported by us14. These elements are numerically integrated over the entire pulse duration using standard numerical integration techniques. The laser pulses are considered to be having Gaussian temporal profiles, and it is further assumed that there is no temporal delay between the pulses.

At the end of the laser-atom interaction, atoms in the auto-ionization state undergo ionization. The population in this auto-ionization state is thus considered a measure of the ionization efficiency. Ionization efficiency contours

To achieve selective ionization of the target isotope, the lasers must be precisely tuned to the resonant transitions specific to that isotope at each excitation step. For odd-mass isotopes, which exhibit hyperfine structure, the lasers should ideally be tuned to the strongest hyperfine component in each step to maximize the ionization efficiency.

In the three-step photoionization of erbium via the 631 nm → 587 nm → 566 nm photoionization scheme; the five even-mass isotopes are ionized through J = 6 → 7 → 8 → 9 excitation pathway. In contrast, 167Er (nuclear spin I = 7/2) has 135 possible hyperfine pathways, while 169Er (I = 1/2) has 5 such pathways (Table 4). The ionization efficiency of constituent isotopes, under resonant or off-resonant excitation, is governed by a complex interplay of atomic, laser, and source parameters. Atomic properties include Einstein coefficients, decay rates to trapped states, ionization cross-sections, isotope shifts, and hyperfine structure. Laser parameters such as pulse width, pulse shape, inter-pulse delay, intensity, and spectral bandwidth, along with source characteristics including temperature, velocity distribution, Doppler broadening, and collimation, play a critical role. Systematic optimization of these parameters is essential to maximize ionization efficiency and isotopic enrichment of the target isotope.

To investigate the impact of off-resonant ionization of non-target isotopes when the lasers are tuned to the strongest hyperfine pathway (F = 13/2→15/2→17/2→19/2) of the target isotope 169Er, the ionization efficiencies of all constituent erbium isotopes were calculated under Doppler-free (i.e., disregarding the velocity distribution of atoms)excitation. These calculations involved systematically varying the frequencies of the first and second excitation lasers while fixing the third excitation laser to the frequency of F = 17/2→19/2 hyperfine transition of 169Er. The resulting ionization efficiency contour for irradiated erbium was obtained by summing the abundance-weighted ionization efficiencies of the constituent isotopes (Fig. 2).The resonance positions in Fig. 2have been identified according to the serial numbers in the two-photon framework (Table 5). The intensity of the strongest hyperfine transition pathway, F = 13/2→15/2→17/2→19/2, for the target isotope 169Er (denoted as 60 in two-photon reference frame), is relatively low owing to its low abundance. The resonance frequency positions observed for all even isotopes align with the expected values. In the ionization contour, the horizontal ridges correspond to the spectral lineshape of the first excitation step, while the vertical ridges represent the spectral lineshape of the second excitation transition. The diagonal ridges arise from coherent two-photon excitation, which occurs when the detunings of the two transitions are equal in magnitude but opposite in sign (Δ₁ = –Δ₂). The probability of coherent two-photon excitation decreases as the absolute value of the detuning (|Δ|) increases. A detailed discussion on the two-dimensional ionization efficiency contours can be found in Ref14,17,18.

An additional distinct resonance was observed for each even isotope (denoted with * in Fig. 2) at a detuned position within the second excitation transition. This can be explained as following. This process corresponding to coherent two-photon ionization from the first excited level, as described in the Type-3 process (Fig. 3). The observed resonance positions for even isotopes (Fig. 2) agree well with predicted values (Table 6). Intensity of the excitation lasers

Intensity of the lasers is detrimental to the efficiency of the laser isotope separation process. Low laser intensity leads to reduced ionization efficiency of the target isotope, resulting in a lower production rate. Conversely, at high laser intensity, the ionization efficiency of both target and non-target isotopes increases, leading to a reduced degree of enrichment. There exists an optimal range of laser intensities in which the production rate of the target isotope is sufficiently high without a significant reduction in the degree of enrichment. In order to find such optimalrange of laser intensities, a series calculations have been carried varying the intensity of all the lasers under the Doppler free excitation. When the atom source is heated to temperature T (˚K), the evaporated atoms have a most probable velocity determined by the expression\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{v}_{mp}=\:\sqrt{\frac{2{K}_{B}T}{m}}$$\end{document}; where KB is the Boltzmann constant (1.380649 × 10− 23m2.kg.s− 2.K− 1) and m is the atomic mass (kg). If the source temperature is 1200˚ C; the erbium most probable atomic velocity is 383 m/sec. The Doppler shift of the transition frequency due to the atomic velocity can be calculated using the expression\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varDelta\:\nu\:=\:{\nu\:}_{0}\left(\frac{{v}_{mp}}{c}\right)$$\end{document}, where ν0 is the rest frame transition frequency (Hz) and c is the velocity of the light (m/sec). Therefore, in the laser isotope separation process, when the tunable lasers are tuned the resonance transition of the target isotope, velocity induced Doppler shifts of the atoms of the ensemble cause reduction in the ionization of the target isotope. On the other hand, the non-target isotopes experience enhanced ionization due to velocity induced Doppler shift. As a result, the degree of enrichment of the target isotope decreases. Therefore, the Doppler broadening of the atomic transitions need to be controlled to obtain optimum ionization efficiency and the degree of the enrichment. In AVLIS experiments, Doppler broadening of atomic transitions is minimized by optimizing the effusion geometry of the atomic source. This is achieved through precise control of the atomic beam’s flow dynamics, as well as the incorporation of additional collimators concentrically oriented in a plane perpendicular to the effusion axis. These modifications effectively narrow the velocity distribution of the atomic ensemble, reducing the Doppler shift and thereby improving the degree of enrichment.

Effect of bandwidth of excitation lasers

Thus far, calculations have been carried out by varying the excitation laser intensities and the angular divergence of the atomic ensemble, while maintaining the bandwidths of all three lasers constant. In practice, however, the optimal bandwidth for each excitation is determined by the isotope shifts, hyperfine structure, and oscillator strength of the corresponding transition. Therefore, additional calculations were performed by varying the bandwidths of all three excitation lasers independently, while keeping the full-angle divergence fixed at 10° and the excitation laser intensities at 550, 300, and 100 W/cm², respectively. Under the conditions described above, setting the bandwidth of all excitation lasers to 20 MHz resulted in an ionization efficiency of 5 × 10− 2 and a degree of enrichment of 99.9%. Both values decreased to 2 × 10− 2 and 47.5%, respectively, when the laser bandwidths were increased to 240 MHz. Although the ionization efficiency of the target isotope remains largely unaffected, the co-enrichment of stable 168Er (20.9%) and 170Er (31.4%) isotopes significantly reduces the overall degree of enrichment. Consequently, the excitation laser bandwidths should be limited to 100 MHz, yielding a degree of enrichment of 98.1% with an ionization efficiency of 6.2 × 10− 2.

Production rate of target isotope

The production rate of the enriched radioisotope determines the number of doses of medicine that can be produced per day. The production rate can be determined using the following equation:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P\:\left(\frac{g}{hour}\right)=2.827\times\:{10}^{3}\times\:\left({b}^{2}\:p\:l\:d\:A\:{\eta\:}_{i}\frac{M}{{N}_{A}}PRF\:\right)$$\end{document}

Where b is the laser beam diameter (cm), pis the fractional population of the ground level, l is the length of the laser-atom interaction region (cm), d is the number density of atoms in the interaction region (atoms/ cm3),A is the fractional abundance of the target isotope, ηιis the ionization efficiency (derived from the density matrix calculations), M is the atomic mass of the target isotope (AMU), NA is the Avogadro number (6.02214076 × 1023)and PRF is the pulse repetition frequency of the lasers (Hz).

For the dimensions of the laser–atom interaction cell and the given laser parameters, the production rate is determined by the atomic number density in the interaction region and the ionization efficiency. Operating the atomic source at the highest feasible density is therefore desirable; however, at higheratomic number densities, photoions are increasingly susceptible to charge-exchange collisions prior to extraction. The probability of such collisions can be estimated using the following expression:

Where, σ is the resonant charge exchange cross-section (cm2), d is the distance traversed by photoions prior to collection at the ion collector (cm) and N is the number density of the atoms (atoms / cm3).

The resonant charge-exchange cross section can be determined24 from the following expression:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:\left(v\right)=\left(1.81\times\:{10}^{-14}-2.12\times\:{10}^{-15}{.log}_{10}v\right).{\left(\frac{IP}{13.6}\right)}^{-1.5}$$\end{document}

where v is the velocity of the ion in cm/sec and IP is the ionization potential of the element in eV. Based on the above formula, the resonant charge-exchange cross section corresponding to the most probable velocity of 383 m/s is calculated to be 4.2 × 10− 14 cm2. This value is good agreement with reported value of 2.8 × 10− 14 cm225.

As discussed earlier, in AVLIS experiments, it is desirable to operate the atomic source at the highest feasible number density in order to maximize the production rate of the target isotope. However, increasing the atomic number density and the laser beam diameter () adversely affects the achievable degree of enrichment due to charge exchange collisions. Therefore, it is necessary to identify an optimal operating regime in which the number density is sufficiently high while the degree of enrichment is not significantly degraded. Calculations were performed by varying the laser beam diameter () and the atomic number density (), and the results are presented in Fig. 6. It should be noted that increasing the laser beam diameter also necessitates operation of the lasers at higher average powers to maintain the required laser intensities. Considering these constraints, a laser beam diameter of 50 mm and an atomic number density of 4 × 1012 atoms/cm3 are identified as optimal operating conditions, yielding a degree of enrichment of 42.4% for 169Er isotope.

Conclusions

We have theoretically investigated the three-step laser isotope separation (LIS) of the 169Er isotope from naturally occurring erbium, irradiated in a medium-flux nuclear reactor for 20 days. The LIS process was analyzed using the density matrix formalism to examine the influence of atomic, laser, and atomic beam source parameters, aiming to optimize the performance of the separation process.

It has been shown that the present method can be employed to produce 30 to 300 doses equivalent of 169Er/ day, each with an activity of 7.4 GBq. The results demonstrate the potential to produce 169Er with high specific activity (1.3 × 10¹⁵ Bq/g), which could enhance the targeting of cancer cells with greater efficacy. This method offers a viable alternative for producing high-specific-activity 169Er without the need for high-flux reactors. It is anticipated that this approach will contribute to the increased availability of 169Er for the treatment of non-endocrine tumors.

The optimum conditions derived in the present work are contingent upon the atomic parameters reported by the research groups cited herein. Errors in the reported atomic parameter values could potentially alter the derived optimum system parameters. Consequently, experimental validation of the photoionization process may be a prerequisite for the design and implementation of a large-scale isotope separation system based on the current work.
