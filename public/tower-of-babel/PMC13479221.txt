Proposed model of empathic leadership to address crises in contemporary management
Authors: Jeremy Howick, Sean Heneghan, Jodi Halpern, Sue Carr, Sheona MacLeod, Rich Withnall
Published: 2026

The growing literature on empathic leadership

Research on empathic leadership is thriving. Forbes1 and the Harvard Business Review2 have extensively explored the concept, and there are a growing number of books on the topic.3 4 Yet, despite the increased recognition of empathic leadership, two significant gaps remain in current research. First, research on empathy’s benefits in healthcare (for both patients5 and healthcare practitioners)6 suggests potential applications in leadership, but this potential has not been applied to empathic leadership. Second, no model of empathic leadership has been developed, whereas such a model would be useful for explanatory and pedagogical purposes. Drawing on the proven benefits of empathy in healthcare, we propose a novel model to address these gaps. The model offers a potential solution to current leadership challenges while providing a foundation for future research and understanding. Although our primary emphasis is on leadership within healthcare, we believe the model could potentially offer insights applicable to other professional contexts. We present our empathic leadership model by examining limitations in current and traditional leadership approaches, defining empathic leadership and demonstrating its potential benefits for leaders, healthcare systems and patient outcomes. We propose that future research explore the effects of this new model in real-world settings.

Workforce changes leading to a crisis in leadership

Leadership faces several unprecedented challenges in the post-COVID-19 era. First, staff satisfaction in the National Health Service (NHS) has reached record lows. According to a King’s Fund report, the past decade has seen an alarming trend: the number of staff departing due to poor work–life balance, health issues or difficult working relationships has nearly doubled.7

Second, the modern healthcare workplace now spans four distinct generations—Baby Boomers, Generation X, Millennials (Generation Y) and Generation Z—each bringing different leadership expectations.3 This generational diversity creates unique challenges, as Baby Boomers typically embrace hierarchical, command-and-control leadership, younger generations gravitate toward collaborative, flexible and value-driven approaches. This stark contrast in preferences raises questions about the viability of any single leadership style.

Third, workplace gender and ethnic diversity has evolved significantly in recent decades—a positive development that nevertheless requires leaders to demonstrate exceptional communication agility and foster inclusive environments across demographic groups.

Finally, the post-COVID-19 era has forced organisations to grapple with defining a ‘new normal’. While clinical healthcare workers rarely have the option of remote work, the NHS has formally introduced hybrid working models for non-clinical staff.7 These flexible arrangements have introduced a potentially contentious aspect of workforce management. For example, one of us (SH) coached a team divided over remote work, with management requiring full office attendance and staff seeking complete flexibility. Through open dialogue, management explained the need to maintain their valued physical space, while acknowledging staff concerns about commuting costs. This led to a compromise that improved team dynamics.

The abovementioned challenges have contributed to rising rates of absenteeism, presenteeism and near-record levels of employee resignations.7

Limitations of traditional leadership styles

Leadership styles have been described in detail elsewhere,8 and the remarks here are limited to limitations of many past and contemporary leadership styles that are relevant to current challenges. Traditional leadership styles, such as the Great Man Theory or Trait Theory, emphasized top-down communication. These are usually too rigid for today’s dynamic and technologically innovative environment, as well as for younger generations of workers.8

Contemporary models, such as transformative or transactional leadership, have attempted to address the growing complexity of leadership but also have limitations in relation to the changed environment. Transformative leadership, which aims to inspire and motivate, can overwhelm younger generations who prioritise autonomy.9 Transactional leadership relies on rewards for performance and does not appeal to Millennials and Gen Z, who are focused on values and social impact as much as material rewards.

Other leadership approaches like servant and coaching leadership are theoretically well-suited to modern workforce demands. Yet, they have had limited impact, largely because they require exceptionally high levels of emotional intelligence, which could be elusive and seem to be declining.10 Failing to achieve or maintain requisite levels of emotional intelligence required to lead as coaches or servants, leaders can default to more authoritarian models.

Compassionate leadership is related to empathic leadership and involves deep listening, understanding, empathising, supporting others, as well as valuing and respecting team members.11 A problem with compassionate leadership is that it encourages emotional merging or “feeling the distress or frustration of those we lead”.11 Yet, actually feeling the distress of others can lead to compassion fatigue. Moreover, feeling what someone else feels could be inappropriate to mimic, for example, if a team member is angry or indeed feeling attraction towards a leader. Another problem is that the definition of compassionate leadership has become confusing. A recent review of compassionate leadership definitions concluded that it was not easily distinguishable from other leadership constructs such as servant leadership, benevolent leadership, transformational leadership or ethical leadership.12 The conceptual confusion surrounding compassionate leadership could partly explain why, despite nearly 15 years of compassionate leadership being taught in the NHS, the number of compassion-related failures in healthcare systems seems to have increased.13

Empathic leadership: a proposal to overcome current leadership challenges

Mirroring an accepted definition of therapeutic empathy,14 empathic leadership requires understanding, shared understanding and cooperative action, which together generate high-performing teams (see figure 1). This model is built on the evidence for the tangible benefits to patients5 and practitioners.6 Importantly, empathy—and hence empathic leadership—can be taught, often in a short period of time.

A model of empathic leadership.

Understanding

Leaders must first seek to understand the perspectives and motivations of their team members. Adequate understanding requires active listening and a commitment to gathering and processing diverse viewpoints. An example where there is a lack of understanding in healthcare relates to shift scheduling. Staff members often want more flexibility, whereas leaders experience the difficulties in managing changes to shift patterns while ensuring that the requisite number of staff are present to ensure patient safety. The lack of understanding from each side regarding how rotas are managed can cause job dissatisfaction and failure to retain junior doctors, leading to high costs of paying locum clinicians. To resolve this conflict, innovative NHS trusts have actively listened to junior doctors’ preferences regarding shifts. Armed with the information gained by listening, they introduced an electronic tool to assist with scheduling. For one trust, the new system generated a more efficient rota system, less spending on locum clinicians and an estimated saving of £456 000 in one year.15

One of us recently worked with an NHS Trust where the new deputy chief executive officer (CEO) walked around their ward on a regular basis, taking time to speak to staff, including junior staff and remembering most names. This coincided with a significant rise in staff well-being and patient satisfaction. The deputy CEO’s colleagues asked where they found the time to walk around the wards. The CEO replied that the increased understanding gained by walking around the wards often led to better decisions, reduced staff burnout and fewer patient complaints, and that overall, therefore, it saved time.

Shared understanding

Once individual perspectives are understood, the goal is to foster shared understanding among the entire team. In the example above over the difficulties resolving different perspectives related to shift patterns, both leaders and staff were able to understand the needs of the other group, and once this shared understanding was reached, a mutually acceptable solution was agreed. More importantly, tensions were resolved and the team’s functioning improved. Achieving shared understanding involves creating an environment where trust flourishes and team members feel comfortable expressing dissenting views.

Achieving shared understanding is helped by developing ‘empathic curiosity’ to understand the problems and feelings of others.16 It is better to respect that you do not know what it is like to be in the shoes of your employees but would like to know more. This model was originally developed for physicians listening to patients with problems the physician had not experienced.16 It requires active listening and imagining another person’s distinct experience.

Throughout the process of attaining shared understanding, the empathic leader must maintain a self-other distinction. This cognitive emphasis has been shown to deploy brain circuits that downregulate anxiety and distress in the listener, which makes empathic curiosity sustainable for leaders.17

Cooperation

Finally, empathic leadership emphasizes coordinated and focused action based on shared understanding. This cooperation generates synergy within teams, enabling them to achieve more than they could as a sum of their parts. An example of this, although from sports, is the Leicester City Football Club’s unlikely Premier League victory in 2016 despite being rated as having a 1 in 5000 chance of doing so at the beginning of the season and spending a third less on star players than major contenders. The head coach achieved his unlikely victory by “choosing a star team, not a team of stars”.18 By de-emphasising the role of the individual, he was able to focus everyone on a shared team goal. Synergies in sports teams have been studied and found to rely on several factors including having clear common goals and good cooperation. While sports analogies may appear overly simplified because the aim of a sports team is inherently clearer (win!), they offer a paradigm for which other organisations can approximate.

Everyone is a leader (and a follower)

Within today’s less hierarchical workplaces, shared understanding implies the need for everyone to be a leader, and everyone to be a follower at times. There is often an individual in a group who bears a disproportionate amount of responsibility for the overall success or even survival of an organisation. These people typically have appropriate titles (CEO, president, director, etc.) and have power which comes with the responsibility. However, the empathic leadership style requires that the title and power be used only sparingly. More importantly, depending on the requirements of the task or the circumstances, it could be appropriate for someone who does not bear such a title to be the leader, if only temporarily. The flip side of the fact that everyone is sometimes a leader is that everyone is sometimes a follower. Understanding when to follow and when to lead will promote healthy teams. This implies that followers (whoever they are at the time) must be empathic towards leaders since empathy is relational and requires all parties involved to understand others. For example, followers must have the courage to speak their minds and accept the results of shared decisions.

Anticipated benefits of empathic leadership

Empathic leadership has the potential to improve patient safety and staff well-being. High-profile failures, some that have led to avoidable mortality, found that a lack of empathy contributed to dysfunctional working and hundreds of avoidable deaths.13 Relatedly, empathic leadership has been linked to enhanced team performance.19

Additionally, empathic leaders will serve as role models who can catalyse positive behavioural change within clinical teams. Role modelling is a known predictor of behaviour, and by displaying empathy to their teams, clinical colleagues will be more likely to display empathy to their patients. Increased patient/practitioner empathy, in turn, will positively impact on patient outcomes, including reduced pain and increased patient satisfaction,20 and even lower mortality.21 Moreover, when practitioners enhance empathy towards their patients, practitioner well-being improves,6 so we may expect that when leaders display empathy towards team members, the well-being of the leaders will increase.

Research also suggests that empathic organisations consistently outperform their less empathetic counterparts.22 Microsoft provides a compelling case study: under Chief Operating Officer Satya Nadella’s leadership, an explicit focus on empathy coincided with a remarkable sevenfold increase in market capitalisation between 2014 and 2021. Healthcare organisations both in the USA and the UK must be economically efficient to survive, and the financial benefits of empathic leadership in healthcare could be similar to its benefits outside the healthcare setting.

We also hypothesise that the benefits of empathic leadership will do more than trickle down through role modelling. It might also contribute to empathy being systematically embedded throughout healthcare organisations. This could transform hierarchical structures and processes—including ostensibly blame-free incident reporting systems—that currently impede empathic practice.

Addressing challenges of empathic leadership

Empathic leadership is sometimes promoted as a panacea, devoid of a dark side. Yet, it can be particularly difficult for leaders of high-performing teams, who must balance the need for empathy with the demands of performance. For these reasons, stress levels are high and rising among many leaders.23 The stress response is an attenuated fight-or-flight response that makes people think about their own survival and makes it difficult and sometimes impossible to understand others’ perspectives.24 Indeed, beyond a certain threshold, stress causes some leaders to leave the profession which abruptly ends all opportunities for communication, including empathy.25 The empathic leader cannot allow this to happen: They must embody authentic empathy and maintain a culture of kindness.

To avoid the inadvertent deterioration of empathy due to stress, sustainable empathic leadership requires a practice of self-understanding and awareness. Leaders who learn to monitor their own emotions and take ready steps to reduce stress will maintain the ability to communicate with and understand others. Leaders who practise self-awareness will also act as positive role models for staff who may further improve their well-being.11 This could even have a cascade effect and influence the well-being of wider society. This may in turn improve population health. When leaders practise self-awareness, they can take concrete steps to sustain a culture of empathy in the workplace, even during stress. These steps may include encouraging active listening, openness to disagreement, sustaining a friendly tone, noticing and praising what is positive, and being clear about expectations.

Conclusion and next steps

As organisations face the challenges of a more diverse, dynamic and intergenerational workforce, many traditional leadership models have proven inadequate. Grounded in evidence from healthcare and communication research, empathic leadership offers a promising solution to current leadership challenges. By focusing on understanding, cooperation and self-awareness, this proposed model can help leaders create teams that are more unified, resilient, high performing, and even more financially sustainable. Future research should focus on testing the impact of empathy-based leadership training on team outcomes and overall organisational performance.
