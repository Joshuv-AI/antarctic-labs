A new and eco-friendly disinfectant for antimicrobial-resistant bacteria: ozone nano water
Authors: Wenhui Wang, Jie Chen, Xiaobao Wan, Muhammad Shahzad Rafiq, Asif Ali, Junhao Wang, Yufeng Gu, Sinuo Yang, Haihong Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEscherichia coli (E. coli) is a major foodborne pathogen that affects 2.8 million people globally. The widespread use of antibiotics and disinfectants in livestock production has contributed to the emergence of multidrug-resistant strains, underscoring the need for resistance surveillance and the development of alternative control strategies, such as ozone nano water (ONW). ONW has emerged as a promising disinfectant, particularly for controlling microbial contamination in food safety and medical applications. In this study, we evaluated the bactericidal activity of ONW against four E. coli strains, including three clinical isolates with multidrug-resistant (MDR) phenotypes, and assessed potential short-term effects on antimicrobial susceptibility.ResultsONW was physicochemically characterized (nano bubble size distribution, zeta potential, and stability under varied conditions). ONW caused rapid inactivation of all strains and markedly suppressed biofilm formation. Flow cytometry and microscopy (SEM and TEM) demonstrated that ONW induced significant cell membrane damage, resulting in bacterial death. Confocal Raman microspectroscopy revealed decreases in spectral features associated with membranes, cell walls, nucleic acids, and ATP, supporting a multi-target oxidative injury mechanism. Interestingly, ONW did not significantly alter the antimicrobial sensitivity of E. coli strains across three consecutive generations, suggesting its potential as a sustainable disinfectant without promoting resistance.ConclusionThese results indicate that ONW is an effective and potentially sustainable disinfectant against E. coli, including MDR strains, with applicability to medical and agricultural settings while minimizing concerns about resistance selection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05120-6.Keywords: Escherichia coli, Foodborne, Ozone nano Water (ONW), Multi-Drug Resistance

Introduction

Escherichia coli (E. coli) is a gram-negative bacterium commonly found in the intestines of humans and animals, and certain strains can cause serious foodborne illnesses [1]. It is a leading cause of gastrointestinal infections, with implications for public health, particularly in food safety, water quality, and environmental health [2]. While many E.coli strains are harmless, pathogenic variants, including enterohemorrhagic and enterotoxigenic strains, pose significant risks to food security and human health, often leading to outbreaks of foodborne diseases [3]. In particular, E. coli is a major concern in the food industry, where its presence in contaminated water, meat, and produce can result in widespread disease transmission. The China Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance System (CARSS) reported that in 2021, 1,026,214 cases were reported from 1910 hospitals, ranking first among all bacterial isolates. These surveillance data reflect overall clinical E. coli isolates (i.e., generic E. coli across pathotypes) rather than a single pathotype, such as STEC [4].

The rise of multidrug-resistant (MDR) E. coli strains further complicates the situation, as these pathogens are increasingly resistant to commonly used antibiotics, limiting treatment options and increasing the risk of severe infections [5]. Traditional disinfectants, such as chlorine, UV radiation, and alcohol-based solutions, have proven effective in controlling E. coli but often face limitations due to bacterial adaptation, resistance, or environmental factors that reduce their efficacy [6].

Ozone, a powerful oxidant, has gained attention for its potential as an antimicrobial agent, capable of inactivating a broad range of microorganisms, including E. coli [7]. In recent years, the advent of micro-nano bubble (MNB) technology has effectively addressed the drawbacks of gas forms. MNBs can significantly increase gas solubility and prolong residence time in water due to their small diameter, lower rise velocity in water, and high internal pressure [8]. Additionally, recent studies have demonstrated that ozone oxidation can inactivate drug-resistant bacteria and reduce antibiotic resistance genes [9]. However, there is a lack of research on the evaluation of ozone nano water (ONW) on foodborne bacteria and its effect on antimicrobial susceptibility.

Therefore, this study provides an integrated evaluation of ONW against E. coli strains, including a reference strain and three field isolates with distinct antibiotic-resistance phenotypes. Unlike prior studies that often rely primarily on culture-based killing metrics, we combine complementary readouts, including morphological observation, spectroscopic characterization, and flow cytometry-based membrane integrity assessment (Annexin V–FITC/PI), together with assays of biofilm formation and changes in antimicrobial susceptibility. Collectively, these data aim to clarify both the disinfectant efficacy and biological effects of ONW across different resistance phenotypes, supporting its potential application as a sustainable alternative to conventional disinfectants in food safety and related settings.

Methods and materials

Bacteria strains

E. coli ATCC 25,922 (quality-control strain) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. Three field isolates (GK-15, HZ-130, and SW2-1) were preserved in the strain repository of the National Reference Laboratory for Veterinary Drug Residues and were revived for this study.

Antibiotic susceptibility determination

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using broth microdilution according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [10] with commercial Shanghai Xingbai broth microdilution panels (AST panels F-1/F-2). The antibiotic concentration ranges tested were two-fold dilution series, as follows: ampicillin (0.25 to 512 µg/mL), amoxicillin–clavulanate (0.12/0.06 to 512/256 µg/mL), gentamicin (0.25 to 512 µg/mL), spectinomycin (0.25 to 512 µg/mL), tetracycline (0.25 to 512 µg/mL), florfenicol (0.25 to 512 µg/mL), sulfisoxazole (0.25 to 512 µg/mL), trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (0.06/1.2 to 32/608 µg/mL), ceftiofur (0.12 to 256 µg/mL), ceftazidime (0.12 to 256 µg/mL), enrofloxacin (0.015 to 32 µg/mL), ofloxacin (0.03 to 64 µg/mL), meropenem (0.008 to 16 µg/mL), apramycin (0.06 to 128 µg/mL), colistin (0.12 to 256 µg/mL), and mequindox (1 to 512 µg/mL). MDR was defined as reduced susceptibility to agents from three or more antimicrobial classes, according to CLSI interpretation where applicable.

Preparation of ONW

The ozone generator (air-cooled FG-L50G, China) was connected to the ultra-fine bubble generator (UFB-N4B, Japan) to prepare ONW. Four liters of double-distilled water (dd water) were added to the reaction tank, oxygen was released at a pressure of 0.5 MPa, and the ozone generator was turned on with an ozone pressure between 0.15 and 0.2 MPa. The gas flow rate was adjusted to 100 mL/min, and the gas was discharged at a flow rate of 30 s to confirm stable gas emissions. The gas volume was adjusted to obtain the ozone concentration required for the experiment, and the ultrasonic oscillator was turned on while the gas was discharged to prepare ONW. Determine the bubble size and Zeta potential of ONW

The characterization and measurement data of ONW were obtained using the nanoparticle size and Zeta potential analyzer of Marvin Company, UK [12]. The size distribution of nanobubbles was measured using dynamic light scattering, spanning 0.3 nm to 10 μm. Additionally, zeta potentials were calculated based on the measured electrophoretic mobility, in combination with the Smoluchowski equation as described previously [13].

Determine the stability of ONW

The freshly prepared ONW was placed at 4 ℃ and 25 ℃ for 80 min. The concentration was measured after every 20 min. Each experiment was repeated three times to obtain the average value. Additionally, freshly prepared ONW with different initial concentrations was stored for 80 min. Using the same method, we recorded the effect of initial concentration on ONW stability.

Determination of the antibacterial activity using flow cytometry

Bacteria in the logarithmic growth phase were adjusted to a concentration of 1.0 × 108 CFU/mL. Four E. coli strains were tested (ATCC 25922 and three clinical isolates: GK-15, HZ-130, and SW2-1). The bacteria suspension (5 mL) was exposed to freshly prepared ONW (45 mL) at low (2 mg/L), intermediate (4 mg/L), and high (8 mg/L) final concentrations for 10 min. The mixtures were centrifuged at approximately 3000 rpm for 3–5 min to pellet the cells. After removing the supernatant, the pellets were resuspended in pre-cooled phosphate buffered saline (PBS; 4 °C). This washing step was repeated twice to minimize potential carry-over effects from residual ozone prior to staining and acquisition. After the final wash, the cell pellets were resuspended in binding buffer. Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) were added in combination, and Annexin V–FITC/PI staining was used to assess bacterial membrane integrity and permeability by flow cytometry [14, 15]. Analysis was performed using a Beckman Coulter BC FC500 flow cytometer. Bacterial events were gated on FSC/SSC to exclude background noise and debris. A minimum of 7,152 total events was acquired per sample (range: 7,152–100,000 events).

Quantitative analysis of the bactericidal activity of ONW

According to Technical Standard For Disinfection (Ministry of Health of the People’s Republic of China, 2002), PBS containing 10 g/L sodium thiosulfate were prepared as the neutralizer. To determine the neutralization effects of the neutralizer on residual ONW, six sets of experiments were conducted to evaluate the neutralizer in accordance with the above standard. Because ONW decays over time, it was freshly prepared for each experiment and diluted immediately to the target concentrations prior to use.

E. coli ATCC25922 was selected for the quantitative bactericidal tests of the bacterial suspension, following the above standard. Considering the effect of concentration on the bactericidal effect, the concentrations of ONW were diluted to 0.42, 1.12, 1.98, 4.14, and 8.48 mg/L respectively. Meanwhile, E. coli ATCC 25,922 was treated with ONW at a concentration of 2.04 mg/L for 3, 10, 30, and 60 min, respectively, to explore the effect of reaction duration on the efficacy of disinfestation. Additionally, quantitative bactericidal experiments were extended to E. coli strains with different drug-resistant phenotypes (GK-15, HZ-130 and SW2-1) treated with ONW at concentrations of 0.72, 1.32, 3.24, and 5.28 mg/L. These concentration/time settings were designed to bracket bacteriostatic, bactericidal, and disinfectant-level performance as defined by the disinfection standard criteria (η and KL). The live bacterial concentration was calculated according to the following formula. When 50% ≤ Ƞ < 90% and KL < 1.00, it indicates that ONW has bacteriostatic effect; when Ƞ ≥ 90% and 1 ≤ KL < 5, it suggests that ONW has a bactericidal effect; and when Ƞ ≥ 99.99% and KL ≥ 5.00, the ONW complies with the disinfectant standard.

Determination of the anti-biofilm activity

To evaluate the effect of ONW on biofilm formation, four E. coli strains (ATCC 25922, GK-15, HZ-130, and SW2-1) were revived and grown in LB broth to the logarithmic phase. Bacterial suspensions were adjusted to approximately 1.0 × 10⁸ CFU/mL. For ONW exposure, 5 mL of the standardized suspension was mixed with 45 mL ONW (1.82 mg/L) and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. A bacteriostatic ONW concentration was used to avoid complete sterilization and to allow assessment of post-exposure biofilm-forming capacity. After exposure, 100 µL of the mixture was transferred into each well of a sterile 96-well plate preloaded with 100 µL fresh LB broth (final volume 200 µL/well), resulting in immediate dilution of residual ONW prior to incubation. The negative control (blank) contained LB broth only, and the control group contained untreated bacteria in LB broth. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24, 48, and 72 h. At each time point, the supernatant was removed and wells were gently washed three times with sterile PBS to remove planktonic cells. Biofilm biomass was quantified using the crystal violet method: adherent cells were stained with 0.1% (w/v) crystal violet for 30 min, washed, air-dried, and the bound dye was solubilized with 200 µL of 95% ethanol. Absorbance was measured at 600 nm (OD600) using a microplate reader [16, 17]. All experiments were performed in three independent experiments.

Determination of microscopic morphological changes

The samples for Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) were prepared as described previously [18]. Briefly, the bacterial precipitates were thoroughly washed with PBS. The bacterial concentration was then adjusted to approximately 1.0 × 108 CFU/mL and mixed with ONW at a concentration of 5.08 mg/L for 10 min. Glutaraldehyde was added to the processed bacterial sample, which was allowed to stand for 4 h. The bacteria were stained and absorbed onto the copper mesh following centrifugation and sedimentation, and changes in the appearance of the bacteria were observed. At the same time, the bacterial samples underwent ultra-thin section processing at room temperature to observe changes in the internal structure of the bacteria. Observation was conducted using transmission electron microscopy at100 kV (H7650, Hitachi, Japan).

To prepare Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) samples, bacterial precipitates were fixed with glutaraldehyde for 4–24 h, then dehydrated with 30%, 50%, and 70% alcohol sequentially. After freeze-drying under vacuum (11.3 ~ 13 Pa) for 6 ~ 24 h, the effects of 8 mg/L of ONW on the exterior three-dimensional structure of E. coli were observed using field emission scanning electron microscopy (model SU8010, Tianmei Scientific Instrument Co., China).

Laser confocal Raman spectroscopy detection

Bacteria were revived to the logarithmic phase, suspended in sterile PBS, collected by centrifugation at 4 °C, 4000 rpm, and washed twice with PBS to remove residual medium. The suspension was adjusted to 0.5 McFarland (approximately 1.0 × 10⁸ CFU/mL). Fresh ONW (8.0 mg/L; 45 mL) was mixed with bacterial suspension (5 mL) and incubated for 10 min; an untreated suspension served as the control. A 2-µL aliquot was spotted onto an Al-foil–coated slide and air-dried at room temperature. Single-cell Raman spectra were acquired using a confocal micro-Raman spectrometer equipped with a 532 nm Nd: YAG laser and a 600 g/mm grating, using a 50× objective (NA 0.50). The integration time was 35 s per spectrum and the laser power was set to 100%. Three independent experiments were performed for each strain and condition, and 3 single-cell spectra were collected per condition. Background spectra of the substrate (n = 3) were acquired and subtracted prior to spectral analysis.

MIC determination under ONW-associated conditions

MICs were determined by the broth microdilution method in Mueller–Hinton (MH) broth as described in Sect. 2.2.

ONW as a solvent for antibiotic preparation (four antibiotics). To evaluate whether ONW alters antibiotic activity through direct interaction with drug solutions (independent of bacterial adaptation), tetracycline, gentamicin, cefoxitin, and florfenicol were prepared using sterile water (control solvent) or ONW (8 mg/L) (test solvent). Drug solutions prepared with ONW were kept for 2 h prior to MIC testing.

To assess whether continuous exposure to ONW affects antibiotic susceptibility, each E. coli strain was exposed to its pre-determined bacteriostatic concentration of ONW for 120 min using the quantitative suspension method described in Sect. 2.7. After exposure, an aliquot (100 µL) was plated on nutrient agar and incubated at 37 °C for 16–24 h. This plating step diluted residual ONW and minimized potential carry-over effects. Surviving colonies were collected to initiate the next exposure cycle. This procedure was repeated for three consecutive passages (defined here as three generations of continuous ONW exposure). After the third passage, MICs were determined using the same broth microdilution protocol for a panel of 16 antibiotics, and compared with baseline MICs before ONW exposure.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed by using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 22 (IBM, US). For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied as appropriate. When ANOVA indicated significance, multiple comparisons were performed using Dunnett’s test (comparisons versus a single control) or Tukey’s test (all pairwise comparisons). Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments (n = 3). A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Effect of ONW on biofilm in E. coli

In the untreated control groups, biofilm biomass (OD600) generally increased over time, with OD600 values at 72 h being higher than those at 24 h across strains (Fig. 4). Among the four strains, GK-15 and SW2-1 showed stronger biofilm formation than ATCC 25,922, whereas HZ-130 exhibited relatively weaker biofilm formation (Fig. 4). Following ONW pre-treatment, biofilm formation was significantly reduced compared with the untreated controls at multiple time points for ATCC 25,922, GK-15, and SW2-1 (Fig. 4A, B and D). For HZ-130, a significant reduction was observed only at 72 h (Fig. 4C). Overall, these results indicate that incubation duration influences biofilm development and that ONW pre-treatment can inhibit biofilm formation in E. coli [19].

The microscopic morphological changes after exposure to ONW

SEM imaging revealed that E. coli ATCC25922 had a homogeneous, long rod-shaped structure with a relatively smooth surface (Fig. 5A). Interestingly, the three clinically resistant strains GK-15, HZ-130, and SW2-1 all exhibited short rod-shaped patterns, with slightly less plumpness compared to ATCC25922 (Fig. 5B and D). However, significant changes in the surface structure of E. coli with different drug-resistant phenotypes treated with ONW were observed. After treatment with 8 mg/L ONW, all experimental groups showed cell surface shrinkage, severe cell wall rupture, and destruction of the rod-shaped structure, indicating damage to the cell membrane. Based on these observations, it can be inferred that the inactivation of bacteria after exposure to ONW may result from the destruction of their bacterial membranes, leading to bacterial death.

As shown in Fig. 5, TEM imaging revealed disruption of the bacterial structure and loss of typical bacterial morphology in ATCC25922 treated with ONW. Meanwhile, GK-15 and SW2-1 exhibited a significant decrease in electron density, possibly due to the loss of cellular contents, leading to internal cavitation. An obvious lysis phenomenon was observed on the cell surface of HZ-130, illustrating a trend of substance loss. Likewise, compared to controls, lower electron densities were clearly detected using ultrathin section TEM. Significant changes were observed in HZ-130 treated with ONW at a magnification of 70 K, which exhibited stronger resistance than other strains. Taken together, these results suggest that both standard and drug-resistant strains treated with ONW experienced cell membrane rupture, resulting in the outflow of intracellular substances.

Antimicrobial sensitivity of E. coli after ONW treatment

As shown in Table S7, there was no change in the MIC results when ONW or water was used to dissolve the drug. These results indicate that, under the conditions tested, using ONW as a solvent did not measurably alter the apparent MIC values.

Across three successive passages under bacteriostatic ONW exposure, MICs for most antibiotics remained unchanged for all four E. coli strains (Tables S8a–S8d). Occasional MIC shifts of one two-fold dilution were observed for a subset of antibiotics, and these changes were not progressive across generations, suggesting minor variability and/or transient stress-associated phenotypic adaptation rather than stable resistance development. Notably, a ≥ 4-fold increase was observed for ceftazidime in strain SW2-1 by the third passage (64 to 256 µg/mL), and chloramphenicol in SW2-1 increased from 0.5 to 2 mg/L over passages. Overall, after three consecutive laboratory passages under bacteriostatic ONW exposure, no consistent progressive increase in MICs was observed across the antibiotic panel; however, isolated ≥ 4-fold shifts warrant further evaluation in longer-term passaging studies.

Discussion

In this study, ONW showed concentration-dependent bactericidal activity against four E. coli strains and inhibited biofilm formation in most strains tested, suggesting that ONW may be a promising and eco-friendly disinfectant. However, the extent to which ONW influences antimicrobial susceptibility and the durability of these effects requires further investigation under conditions that better mimic real-world settings.

Evaluation of the bactericidal performance of ONW against E. coli

Ozone is highly effective in controlling microbial contamination and preventing biofilm formation in the food industry [19]. Consistent with previous reports, we observed that while the four E. coli strains exhibited different biofilm-forming abilities, the amount of biofilm formation was reduced in all strains capable of biofilm formation after ONW treatment [20].

In this study, significant changes in the bacterial morphology of the four E. coli strains treated with ONW were observed. SEM imaging revealed a loss of bacterial integrity, with blurred edges following exposure to ONW. TEM analysis showed that, except for HZ-130, which exhibited clear cell wall rupture, the other strains primarily displayed a decrease in electron density, though their bacterial outlines remained relatively intact. This was interpreted as a change in cell membrane permeability, leading to the loss of intracellular substances and, ultimately, bacterial death [21]. It has been reported that E. coli exhibited deformation and cell wall loss under the combined treatment of ultrasound and ozone [22]. In this study, similar results were observed only in HZ-130. Given HZ-130’s poor capacity to form biofilm, we speculated that the cell wall rupture was due to the lack of protection from the cell membrane.

Oxidative stress causes damage to nucleic acids [23], which in turn impaired protein synthesis in cells [24]. To investigate the molecular response mechanism of E. coli to the strongly oxidizing ONW, laser confocal Raman spectroscopy experiments were conducted. The results revealed significant decreases in the Raman shifts at 918 cm− 1, 1338 cm− 1, and 1373 cm− 1 across the four E. coli strains, which corresponded to nucleic acid and protein components, respectively [25–27]. These results demonstrated that the loss of bacterial contents, along with the destruction of the bacterial membrane and cell wall structure, ultimately led to bacterial death.

Effectiveness of ONW on the elimination of four different antibiotic-resistant phenotypes of E. coli

ONW exhibits broad-spectrum bactericidal effects due to its strong oxidation capabilities. However, its efficacy may vary across different microorganisms [28, 29], particularly in bacteria that have developed resistance [30].

Flow cytometry quantifies cell characteristics primarily through visual means, recording measurements of individual cells, while processing thousands of cells in seconds. When combined with fluorescent staining, it provides valuable information for estimating and distinguishing various physiological characteristics of cells [31]. In this study, flow-cytometry analysis of membrane injury (Fig. 2) showed that ONW (up to 8 mg/L) markedly increased the proportion of membrane-compromised/non-viable bacterial events across all four E. coli strains, with no obvious strain-dependent differences at the same dose. Further analysis revealed that ONW’s impact on bacterial integrity was almost a direct shift in fluorescence intensity from the Q4 region to the Q1 region, indicating that the cells were primarily killed by lysis resulting from physical damage.

Effect of ONW on the antimicrobial sensitivity of E. coli

Disinfectants play a crucial role in preventing the spread of harmful bacteria and viruses [32, 33]. However, insufficient disinfectant dosage or inadequate cleaning can lead to bacteria adapting through chronic exposure to subinhibitory or sublethal concentrations of disinfectants, ultimately developing resistance [34, 35]. Previous studies have shown that bacteria exposed to disinfectants can develop adaptive responses or resistance, as well as cross- or co-resistance to other antimicrobials [36, 37]. Conventional disinfectants such as UV, chlorine, and ozone are among the most effective for eliminating drug-resistant bacteria and genes in water treatment [38, 39]. Prolonged treatment with ozonated water, for example, has been shown to reduce the abundance of genes associated with sulfamethoxazole- and methicarbamol-resistant E. coli [40].

From a mechanistic perspective, ONW is expected to impose oxidative stress that can damage bacterial envelopes (e.g., membrane lipids and proteins), alter membrane permeability, and affect physiological states that are relevant to antibiotic action. Such stress may transiently influence apparent susceptibility by modulating envelope integrity, biofilm-associated protection, and stress-response pathways, without necessarily producing stable, heritable resistance changes.

Because broth microdilution inherently allows variation of approximately one two-fold dilution, these minor MIC fluctuations may reflect assay variability and/or transient stress-associated phenotypic adaptation rather than stable, genetically fixed resistance. Notably, isolated ≥ 4-fold MIC shifts were observed for specific strain–drug combinations, although these changes were not progressive across passages. Overall, ONW treatment did not show a progressive upward MIC trend across generations after three consecutive laboratory passages under ONW exposure. This difference across studies may be related to differences in exposure design (e.g., ONW concentration, contact time, and surrounding matrix conditions) and the relatively short serial-passaging period in this work. Further investigations are required to explore whether ONW affects bacterial genotypes [41, 42].

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed an eco-friendly disinfectant, ozone nano water (ONW), and established an evaluation procedure to assess its characterization and bactericidal efficacy against four multidrug-resistant E. coli. The findings revealed that ONW effectively eliminated foodborne E. coli, and treatment of four strains of E. coli with ONW over three consecutive generations did not result in the exacerbation of drug resistance. These results provide a strong foundation for the potential application of ONW as a disinfectant for controlling a broad spectrum of foodborne pathogens in future practical settings.
