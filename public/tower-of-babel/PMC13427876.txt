CT-positive traumatic brain injury in patients ≥ 80 years: mortality and 6-months functional outcome
Authors: Mads Aarhus, D F Netteland, C Tverdal, V Stenset, P Rønning, E Helseth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTraumatic brain injury (TBI) in patients ≥ 80 years poses major clinical challenges, with limited evidence to guide neurosurgical decision-making. We evaluated mortality, functional outcomes, and prognostic factors in patients undergoing neurosurgical versus conservative management.MethodsThis cohort study included patients ≥ 80 years with imaging-verified TBI treated at a tertiary trauma centre. Demographics, injury characteristics, treatment, mortality, and 6-month outcomes were collected. Favourable outcome was defined as Glasgow Outcome Scale 4 and 5. Multivariable logistic regression identified predictors of mortality and unfavourable outcome, and Cox regression assessed 30, 180, and 365 days mortality.ResultsA total of 608 patients (median age 85.8 years; 52% female) were included; 84 (14%) underwent neurosurgical intervention. Thirty-day and one-year mortality were 24% and 42%, respectively. Neurosurgical intervention was associated with reduced 30-day mortality (HR 0.46, 95% CI 0.28–0.76; p = 0.002) but not one-year mortality (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.63–1.12; p = 0.23). Favourable 6-month outcome occurred in 29% of patients having intervention versus 34% of conservatively managed patients (p = 0.439). Increasing age, higher ASA class, dependent preinjury living, and injury severity were independently associated with unfavourable outcome. Acute craniotomy was associated with higher mortality and unfavourable outcome, whereas later trephination for subsequent chronic subdural haematoma was associated with lower mortality (5%) and better outcomes. Guideline adherence for ICP monitoring was 54%.ConclusionIn patients ≥ 80 years with TBI, neurosurgical intervention was associated with reduced early mortality but was not associated with improved 365-days survival or functional outcome at 6 months. Outcomes were largely determined by preinjury status and injury severity. These findings suggest selective surgical decision-making, with consideration of baseline function, comorbidity, and patient-centred goals of care.Keywords: Traumatic brain injury, Neurosurgery, Craniotomy, Octogenarian, Nonagenarian, Outcome

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a major contributor to the global burden of disease [37]. With increasing life expectancy and more active lifestyles, an increasing fraction of older adults sustaining TBI has been observed in high-income countries [8, 16, 33, 35].

The Brain Trauma Foundation guidelines for the management of TBI have gained wide acceptance helping clinicians with a systematic approach to patient care [2]. Acute neurosurgical intervention for TBI includes intracranial pressure monitoring (ICP), and evacuation of mass lesions via craniotomy or craniectomy. However, these guidelines do not incorporate age, comorbidity or frailty into decision making; factors known to influence outcomes [9, 11, 28].

There is an ongoing debate regarding the benefit of treatment in older patients, since frailty, higher risks of adverse events, more uncertain potential for rehabilitation and shorter life expectancy may limit beneficial treatment effects [13]. Given the highly invasive nature of neurosurgical intervention and the uncertainties regarding outcome, this debate is particularly relevant for TBI. Clinical decision-making may be influenced by a perceived poor prognosis in the very old, potentially leading to lower treatment intensity compared with younger patients [24]. Such practice may inadvertently contribute to a self-fulfilling prophecy, where high age alone becomes associated with worse outcomes due to systematically less aggressive care. Furthermore, rehabilitation after the acute phase is particularly limited for the oldest patients. In a previous study, we found that only 10% of patients > 65 years with moderate and severe TBI managed in our regional referral centre followed a direct pathway into rehabilitation after the acute intervention [29]. Consequently, such an imbalance between neurosurgical intervention and later rehabilitation limits the overall beneficial effects of the initial treatment.

Similarly, a recent European study on 27 patients ≥ 80 years treated with craniotomy for an acute subdural hematoma (ASDH) found a favourable outcome in only 22% [36]. The authors identified that Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score > 8, low comorbidity and a lower ASDH volume predicted a favourable outcome in univariable analysis. This is consistent with an American study of a large cohort of octogenarians with TBI and GCS < 9, where only 38% survived [1].

Against this background, we conducted a registry-based cohort study of patients ≥ 80 years with CT-positive TBI admitted to and treated at the regional trauma centre. The aim of the study was to establish the mortality and functional outcome 6 months after injury, and identify factors associated with outcome for patients who received neurosurgical intervention in comparison to conservatively managed patients.

Material and methods

Study design and data sources

Oslo University Hospital (OUH) is the only neurotrauma centre in the South-East region of Norway, covering 3.1 million inhabitants through a network of 19 referring hospitals. The neurosurgical department runs the prospective Oslo TBI Registry-Neurosurgery (OTBIR-N), where data collection commenced in January 2015. Patient inclusion criteria in the registry are: 1) TBI, 2) neuroimaging demonstrating acute traumatic changes, 3) admission to the trauma centre within 7 days after injury, and 4) a Norwegian social security number [19, 30, 31]. All four criteria must be met for inclusion. We retrieved patients ≥ 80 years with image verified TBI admitted to OUH between Jan 1, 2015, and Dec 31, 2024, from OTBIR-N. Neurosurgical intervention was defined as one or more of the following: acute craniotomy, acute craniectomy, ICP monitoring, external ventricular drainage (EVD), and later burr hole evacuation (trephination) of chronic subdural hematoma (CSDH) complicating the initial TBI.

TBI management

At OUH TBI management adheres to the BTF guidelines with local adaptations with no age limits [2]. The indications for craniotomy are defined separately for ASDH, epidural hematoma (EDH), and brain contusions. For patients with ASDH and a GCS < 14, we recommend craniotomy and evacuation if either hematoma volume is > 30 cubic centimetres (cc), the midline shift (MLS) is > 5 mm (mm) and/or the hematoma width is > 10 mm. EDH evacuation is recommended if GCS < 14, EDH volume is > 30 cc, MLS > 5 mm, and/or hematoma width is > 15 mm. For patients with brain contusions, we recommend craniotomy if GCS < 12, and if either the contusion volume is > 20 cc or the MLS is > 5 mm.

In selected cases, when the patient’s clinical condition permits, evacuation of an ASDH may be deliberately delayed allowing partial liquefaction of the hematoma. This strategy enables medically fragile patients to undergo trephination under local anaesthesia with light sedation, thereby avoiding the risks associated with general anaesthesia and craniotomy.

The indications for ICP monitoring are: 1) GCS < 9 and pathological head CT, 2) GCS < 9 and normal head CT and ≥ 2 of the following: age > 40 years, systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg, GCS motor < 4, 3) GCS < 13 and long duration surgery in other organ systems or expected long lasting ventilator treatment due to injury in other organ systems. Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) for adults is maintained > 60 mmHg. The target ICP threshold for escalating measures to reduce ICP is < 22 mmHg. The mean arterial pressure (MAP) zero level is standardized at the tragus.

Data collection and definitions

From the OTBIR-N we retrieved demographic data and details of the preinjury patient status, including functional living status (home independent/home assisted/institution), comorbidity (American Society of Anaesthesiologists Physical Status Classification System (ASA-PS) [17], and the use and type of antithrombotic medication. Comorbidity was dichotomized into low (ASA-PS 1 and 2) and high (ASA-PS > 2). We recorded mechanism of injury, trauma to other organ systems (multitrauma), lowest GCS score [27], Rotterdam CT Score [15], and Head Injury Severity Scale (HISS) [26]. Rotterdam CT Score was dichotomized as high (score 5–6) or low (score 1–4). HISS was dichotomized into complicated mild/moderate versus severe. Management measures included type of neurointervention, length of stay in the ICU, length of stay in hospital, and discharge destination. Outcome measures included mortality at 30, 180 and 365 days, and functional outcome in the form of Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) score [12] at 6 months after injury. GOS was dichotomized into favourable (GOS 4–5) and unfavourable outcome (GOS 1–3).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean (SD) or median (IQR), as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Group differences were assessed using the Student’s t test, Mann–Whitney U test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

To analyse the effect of neurosurgical intervention on outcome, we performed uni- and multivariable logistic regressions where 1) 30-day mortality and 2) dichotomized GOS were the dependent variables. Given the limited sample size, the number of covariates included in the multivariable model was restricted to reduce the risk of overfitting and unstable estimates. Variables were selected based on clinical relevance supported by univariable analyses, and included age, sex, comorbidity (dichotomized ASA-PS), living status (independent versus dependent), severe TBI according to HISS, and type of surgical intervention. We also calculated a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for the prediction model.

Time-to-event analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazards regression models to compare patients treated with neurosurgical intervention and patients managed conservatively, with time to death (all-cause mortality) as the outcome. We calculated separate hazard ratios for 30 days, 180 days and 365 days mortality, adjusted for age, sex, comorbidity, dependent living status pre-injury. All Cox models were stratified by injury severity (HISS), allowing separate baseline hazard functions across severity groups. To account for multiple comparisons across time points, Bonferroni correction was applied for each variable, with a significance threshold of p < 0.017 (0.05/3).

P values < 0.05 were considered significant. The statistical analysis was performed using Stata (StataCorp. 2023. Stata Statistical Software: Release 18. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC).

Results

During the ten-year study period, 608 patients ≥ 80 years with CT-positive TBI were admitted and treated at the trauma centre (Fig. 1).

Overview of the patients included in the study (n = 608)

Among these, 84 patients underwent neurosurgical intervention, and 524 were treated conservatively. The median age was 85.8 years (82.6–89.4) for the whole cohort, and 52% were female. The intervention group was characterized by more severe injuries at baseline, including lower GCS scores and higher HISS and Rotterdam CT scores, as well as slightly younger age and a higher frequency of independent living. The predominant CT verified pathology in the full cohort was ASDH which was present in 414/608 (68%) patients, followed by traumatic subarachnoid haemorrhage (tSAH) in 354/608 (58%) and brain contusions in 230/608 (38%) of the patients. Mortality analysis

The mortality rates for the complete cohort at 30, 180 and 365 days were 143/608 (24%), 215/608 (35%), and 257/608 (42%), respectively. For patients receiving neurosurgical intervention, the rates were 20/84 (24%), 38/84 (45%), and 44/84 (52%), respectively. Acute surgery was associated with significantly reduced 30-day mortality (HR 0.46, 95% CI 0.28–0.76, p = 0.002). At 6 months, the association was no longer statistically significant (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.51–1.09, p = 0.13). No association was observed with one-year mortality (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.63–1.12, p = 0.23) (Table 2). These findings suggest a time‑dependent effect of neurosurgical intervention, consistent with non‑proportional hazards. After Bonferroni correction, the association between neurosurgical intervention and mortality remained significant at 30 days only, whereas age and male sex remained significant predictors across all time points.

Functional outcome at 6 months after injury

GOS at 6 months after injury was available for 591/608 (97%) of the patients, where 195/591 (33%) had favourable outcome. Favourable outcome occurred in 24/82 (29%) of the patients managed with neurosurgical intervention, and in 171/509 (34%) of the conservatively treated patients (p = 0.439). In multivariable analysis, increasing age (OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.03–1.14, p = 0.002), ASA class 3–4 (OR 2.19, 95% CI 1.22–3.94, p = 0.009), dependent living (OR 6.68, 95% CI 4.01–11.13, p < 0.001), and injury severity (moderate HISS: OR 6.78, 95% CI 3.92–11.72; severe HISS: OR 43.30, 95% CI 15.98–117.29; both p < 0.001) were independently associated with unfavourable outcome. Acute surgery and male sex were not significant predictors (Table 3).

Neurosurgical intervention

Craniotomy with evacuation of haemorrhage was performed in 34 (40%) of the intervention patients, where ASDH was present in 32/34 (94%). ICP monitoring either alone or in combination with craniotomy occurred in 13 (15%) and 9 (11%), respectively. ICP monitoring of patients with GCS < 9 occurred in 14/26 (54%). For patients managed with ICP monitor only, 10/13 (77%) had ASDH. None of the patients in the total cohort were managed with either EVD or decompressive craniectomy. ICU management occurred in 65/84 (77%) patients, and the length of stay in the ICU was median 3 days (IQR 2–5).

Later trephination of CSDH complicating the initial TBI

Trephination and evacuation of a subsequent CSDH complicating the initial acute TBI (86% had ASDH; either pure ASDH or ASDH complicating a preexisting CSDH) was performed in 37 (44%) patients at a median of 18 days after injury (IQR 7–49). According to HISS, the frequencies of complicated mild, moderate and severe TBI were 26 (70%), 8 (22%), and 3 (8%), respectively. Readmission after the initial injury for subsequent trephination was necessary in 24/37 (65%). Consequently 35% were trephined at the initial admission. Among the remaining 5 of 37 patients (14%) who did not present with an ASDH on the initial CT scan, the predominant traumatic findings consisted of various combinations of cerebral contusions, skull fractures, and traumatic subarachnoid haemorrhage.

Factors associated with 30-day mortality and unfavourable outcome after neurosurgical intervention

The 30-day mortality rate was 20/84 (24%), with a male predominance 17/20 (85%, p = 0.006). The 30-day mortality differed significantly between the surgical strategies (acute craniotomy 38%, ICP alone 38%, trephination 5%, p = 0.002). Multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that male sex and patients undergoing emergency craniotomy were significantly associated with 30-day mortality (Table 4). The model demonstrated good discriminative ability with an AUC of 0.83.

Discussion

In this study of patients ≥ 80 years treated CT-positive TBI, the 30-day mortality rate was 24%. In the full cohort, neurosurgical intervention was associated with a significant reduction in 30-day mortality, but this effect was not sustained, with no significant impact on 6 months or one-year survival. One year mortality reached 42%, and 67% had unfavourable outcome at 6 months. Neurosurgical intervention was not associated with improved 6-month functional outcome. Among operated patients, later trephination for CSDH complicating the initial TBI was associated with a lower 30-day mortality and better functional outcome compared with those treated with acute craniotomy and ICP targeted therapy. These findings collectively underscore the substantial vulnerability after CT-positive TBI for this age group, despite active neurosurgical intervention.

Mortality and functional outcome

The observed early survival benefit without sustained effect suggests non-proportional hazards, indicating that the impact of neurosurgical intervention is primarily limited to the acute phase. The absence of benefit in functional outcome suggests that long-term prognosis is likely driven by patient-related factors and injury severity rather than the initial neurosurgical management alone. The intervention group had more severe injuries at baseline (lower GCS, higher HISS and Rotterdam CT scores, and slightly younger age), reflecting treatment selection and confounding by indication. Adjusted analyses showed no independent association between neurosurgical intervention and one-year mortality or functional outcome at 6 months. Thus, while acute surgery may reduce early mortality, it does not necessarily improve long-term functional recovery, highlighting the importance of patient selection and the multifactorial nature of outcomes following TBI. The observed effect of neurosurgical intervention on early mortality corroborates with a Spanish study of 301 TBI patients ≥ 65 years of age, although that study reported better 12-month functional outcome [3], possibly due to a younger cohort. Interestingly, our study is in line with a study comparing different strategies of ASDH management, where acute surgery versus conservative treatment did not result in a better outcome [34]. Ultimately, our study supports the assumption of an inherently high risk of death after CT-positive TBI in the elderly population. Therefore, preventive measures of TBI, like fall prevention strategies, are of utmost importance for the elderly [23, 25].

Neurosurgical intervention and outcome

Several findings emerged regarding neurosurgical intervention in this cohort. First, among patients undergoing emergency craniotomy, only 5 of 34 (15%) had a favourable outcome at 6 months, indicating limited benefit of this invasive procedure. In contrast, if the clinical situation allowed a subsequent trephination of a CSDH complicating the initial TBI, a favourable outcome occurred in 18/35 (51%). Notably, among the trephined patients, 32/37 (86%) had an ASDH verified on neuroimaging at first presentation, underscoring the radiological severity of the TBI. This substantial difference between acute craniotomy and trephination may suggest that clinical status at admission and procedure invasiveness plays a role in outcome. However, the difference in outcome between acute craniotomy and trephination likely reflects confounding by indication, as patients selected for delayed surgery typically have less severe primary TBI and a more benign clinical course, rather than an acute life-threatening mass lesion.

Second, 65% of patients undergoing trephination required readmission due to clinical deterioration and corresponding CT findings, with a median interval of 18 days after the initial TBI. These findings support routine scheduling of follow-up head CT within 2–3 weeks after CT-positive TBI, and clear instructions to subsequent care providers to ensure prompt readmission if clinical status worsens. Robust population-level data on the risk of CSDH after moderate or severe TBI in the elderly remain limited; most incidence studies focus on mild TBI, where the risk is low, and evidence for more severe injury is largely indirect [10]. In our cohort, a subsequent symptomatic CSDH developed in 5/37 (14%) of patients without ASDH on initial presentation. Although based on small numbers, these findings provide preliminary data that warrant further investigation.

Third, one-year mortality was high (52%) despite neurosurgical intervention, reflecting the overall severity of disease and emphasizing the importance of individualized clinical decision-making in this age group. Importantly, the study showed that patients eligible for later trephination had a significantly lower 30-day mortality rate at 5%, and a significantly longer survival. This supports an individualized, goal-oriented approach rather than routine escalation of care.

Fourth, none of the patients underwent decompressive craniectomy, possibly related to impact of recent studies indicating that very few patients > 60 years benefit these procedures. This trend is in line with an increasing body of literature suggesting that aggressive decompressive strategies may be of limited benefit in very elderly patients [6, 14, 20, 22].

Fifth, adherence to ICP monitoring guidelines was low (54%), consistent with prior reports [5, 21], and may reflect clinical judgment and uncertainty regarding benefit; improved adherence may indicate care quality rather than causal effect [7].

In summary, the observed effects of neurosurgical intervention should not be interpreted as evidence of harm from craniotomy or ICP guided management; however, the findings likely reflect confounding by indication, where patients receiving more aggressive interventions have more severe injuries not fully captured by measured variables, rather than a detrimental effect of the procedures themselves.

Clinical factors and outcome after neurosurgical intervention

In our study, 70% of patients undergoing neurosurgical intervention were living independently pre-injury, whereas only 11% were discharged directly home and 25% were dead or discharged to a nursing home after the acute phase. Pre-injury independent living was strongly associated with favourable outcome, with 92% of patients with favourable outcome living independently before injury. This supports that biological reserve, reflected by functional status, remains a key determinant of outcome in very elderly patients. Accordingly, pre-injury living status should inform acute treatment decisions, and in non-independent patients, neurosurgical intervention should be more restricted given the high risk of futility.

Comorbidity burden was substantial, with 83% receiving antithrombotic therapy and a median ASA-PS of 3. Higher comorbidity was associated with unfavourable outcome in both univariable and multivariable analyses, whereas antithrombotic use was not, likely due to its high prevalence. Thus, antithrombotic therapy should not be overemphasized in decision-making, while overall comorbidity, associated with more than a threefold increase in odds of unfavourable outcome, should be carefully considered.

In the model including age, sex, HISS, dependent living status, and surgical intervention, both dependent living status and acute surgery were associated with unfavourable 6-month outcome, with good discrimination (AUC 0.86). These findings indicate that living status and surgical strategy capture important prognostic information, although incremental value beyond the high baseline risk is uncertain. Surgical decision-making should therefore be individualized, with clear communication of prognosis to support shared decision-making with patients and relatives.

Future directions

TBI guidelines should incorporate data specific to patients ≥ 80 years and provide tailored recommendations for this group. The role of clinical frailty scales remains insufficiently explored and should be integrated into both research and clinical care [4]. Early goals-of-care discussions with patients and families are also recommended to guide treatment intensity in acute situations (18, 32), supporting more patient-centred and ethically aligned care.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-centre study including only patients admitted to a regional trauma centre, which may limit generalizability. However, as no other hospitals within the catchment area have the capacity to perform neurosurgical interventions, all patients eligible for such treatment were captured. In contrast, patients managed conservatively may not have been referred, introducing potential confounding by indication. In clinical practice, patients aged ≥ 80 years are generally not considered for neurosurgical intervention or transfer unless the likelihood of meaningful recovery is deemed acceptable. The cohort therefore represents a selected subgroup with relatively favourable prognostic profiles; outcomes in an unselected elderly population would likely be worse. Additional limitations include the lack of data on lesion localization and volumetry, as well as the retrospective observational design. The sample size was limited, restricting the number of variables in multivariable analyses on the effects of neurosurgical intervention. Nevertheless, the observed effects of comorbidity and severe TBI remained significant after adjustment for age and sex and are consistent with findings from comparable studies, supporting the robustness of the results despite these constraints.

Conclusion

In patients aged ≥ 80 years with CT-positive TBI, neurosurgical intervention was associated with reduced 30-day mortality but did not confer a benefit on one-year survival or 6-month functional outcome. Overall mortality and rates of unfavourable outcome remained high, reflecting the marked vulnerability of this population. Outcomes were primarily determined by baseline functional status, comorbidity, and injury severity. These findings support a individualized approach to neurosurgical decision-making, with careful consideration of preinjury status and realistic goals of care.
