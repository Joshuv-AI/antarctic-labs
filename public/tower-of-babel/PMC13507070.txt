Effect of olive paste pH during malaxation on virgin olive oil properties
Authors: Sıddıka Yusra Özkılıç, Esra Nida Öztürk, Semanur Buse Altınpulluk, Derya Arslan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study evaluated the influence of olive paste pH adjustment during malaxation on oil yield and quality parameters of virgin olive oil obtained from Olea europaea L. cv. Gemlik. The pH of olive paste was systematically adjusted to 2.7–6.7 using citric acid and NaOH solutions during malaxation. Oil yield, free fatty acidity (FFA), peroxide value (PV), total and individual phenolic compounds, DPPH radical scavenging activity, and color attributes (L*, a*, b*) were determined. Oil yield ranged from 28.67 to 45.11%, with the highest yield observed at pH 6.7. Total phenolic content(TPC)showed a clear pH-dependent pattern, reaching 678 mg GAE kg⁻¹ at pH 2.7 and decreasing to 281 mg GAE kg⁻¹ at pH 6.7. Moderately acidic conditions (pH 4.7) promoted elevated concentrations of secoiridoid derivatives, with oleacein (257 mg kg⁻¹) and oleocanthal (159.7 mg kg⁻¹) attaining their highest levels. Phenolic alcohols exhibited compound-specific pH responses, with tyrosol and hydroxytyrosol acetate reaching maximum levels at pH 4.7, while hydroxytyrosol peaked at pH 3.7 and declined thereafter. Flavonoids, such as apigenin, reached maximum concentrations (16.5 mg kg−¹) under mildly acidic conditions (pH 3.7). 3,4-DHPEA-EA decreased progressively with increasing pH, from 226 to 103 mg kg−¹, indicating that pH influences its stability and transformation pathways during malaxation. PV values were highest at pH 3.7 (8.4 meqO2kg⁻¹ oil) and 4.7 (7.2 meqO2kg⁻¹ oil). DPPH radical scavenging activity paralleled the phenolic profile, with oils obtained at lower pH exhibiting approximately twofold greater activity than those obtained at pH 5.7 and 6.7. Several quality parameters exhibited significant quadratic responses to pH variation. Color attributes were also influenced by pH, with acidic conditions producing brighter oils with reduced yellowness. These findings indicate that controlled pH modulation during malaxation can influence phenolic composition, antioxidant capacity, and visual characteristics of virgin olive oil, providing mechanistic insight into the pH sensitivity of its extraction and compositional changes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54300-x.Keywords: Virgin olive oil, pH adjustment, Malaxation process, Phenolic compounds, Antioxidant activitySubject terms: Biochemistry, Chemistry, Plant sciences

Introduction

The olive tree (Olea europaea L.) is one of the oldest cultivated species in the world. Global olive and olive oil production is concentrated mainly in Mediterranean countries, particularly Spain, Italy, Greece, and Türkiye, as well as Tunisia, Syria, and Morocco1. In Türkiye, olives are predominantly cultivated in the Mediterranean, Aegean, and Southeastern Anatolia regions2. Olive oil has long been preferred as a healthy dietary fat.

The olive fruit contains substantial amounts of oil and water, along with proteins, cellulose, sugars, hydrocarbons, phenolic compounds, minerals, and tocopherols3. Olive oil constituents are classified into saponifiable and unsaponifiable fractions. Triglycerides, free fatty acids, and phosphatides constitute the saponifiable fraction, while hydrocarbons, alcohols, sterols, pigments, and aromatic compounds comprise the unsaponifiable part4. Minor components include α-tocopherol, hydrocarbons, aliphatic alcohols, triterpenic alcohols, phospholipids, and chlorophylls5. During crushing and malaxation, mechanical forces and endogenous enzymes allow valuable compounds such as phenolics, squalene, vitamin E, pigments, triterpenes, and sterols to be released from olive tissues into the oil6. These components influence flavor, aroma, and overall oil quality, while factors such as cultivar, ripening stage, harvest, storage, and processing conditions also play a crucial role. Numerous studies have demonstrated beneficial health effects of these compounds, including antioxidant activity, cardiovascular protection, anti-inflammatory effects, and potential anticancer properties7,8.

Olive oil production begins with the removal of foreign materials and the washing of olives, followed by crushing to produce the olive paste. Modern extraction systems employ two-phase and three-phase decanters to enhance efficiency, improve yields, prevent oxidation, and extend shelf life. The difference between these systems mainly concerns the addition of water to the olive paste, and both the amount and temperature of added water affect oil composition9. Malaxation is a critical step during which microscopic oil droplets coalesce into larger ones, facilitating phase separation10. Water addition improves the breaking of the oil-water emulsion11. In the present study, 15% water was added during malaxation.

During processing, endogenous enzymes in olives play major roles in numerous chemical reactions. Acidic conditions during malaxation enhance the activity of key endogenous enzymes such as β-glucosidase and lipoxygenase, which promote the formation of secoiridoids and volatile compounds12,13. Therefore, environmental parameters affecting enzyme activity, such as temperature, pH, reaction time, substrate availability, and additives, have a direct influence on oil quality9. Oleuropein, a key phenolic compound in olives, is one of the most abundant secoiridoids in olive fruit, being present in olive paste, oil, and by-products14. Secoiridoids are formed during oil extraction through the enzymatic hydrolysis of oleuropein and ligstroside, leading to the formation of oleacein and oleocanthal as major compounds12. Previous studies have shown that pH plays a significant role in phenolic extraction, increasing extraction pH from 2.5 to 4.5 improved recovery of phenolic compounds from olive pomace15. Acidification of olive paste has been reported to significantly increase total phenolic content and antioxidant capacity without negatively affecting extraction yield or standard quality parameters12,16. Adjusting olive paste acidity during malaxation has been suggested as a means to increase the concentration of colloids (proteins, pectins, hemicelluloses, and other hydrophilic colloids) and improve phenolic content, which is important for both oxidative stability and sensory attributes17. For instance, lower pH values increased hydroxytyrosol concentration during malaxation but reduced storage stability18. Other studies have reported that decreasing paste pH enhances plant colloids and reduces phenolic oxidation19. Peralta et al.12 reported that the addition of organic acids during malaxation improved phenolic content and oxidative stability, highlighting the technological potential of pH modulation during olive oil extraction. However, comprehensive studies on this subject remain limited. As citric acid is known to complex heavy metals and inhibit oxidation, lowering pH may influence enzymatic oxidative processes, particularly those involving oxidation-related enzymes20. Moreover, olive paste pH has been shown to correlate with concentrations of total phenols, ortho-diphenols, and secoiridoid derivatives in the final oil, highlighting the importance of this parameter21. Beyond phenolics, changes in processing conditions such as malaxation parameters may also influence color, aroma, enzymatic and non-enzymatic reactions, and structural properties of the olive paste22.

Recent studies have demonstrated that the addition of organic acids to olive paste can enhance the concentrations of selected bioactive secoiridoids. Peralta et al.12 for example, reported improved oleacein enrichment following paste acidification with citric, ascorbic, and acetic acids. The present findings are consistent with this general tendency, particularly regarding the increased oleacein and oleocanthal levels observed under conditions ranging from acidic to moderately acidic. However, unlike previous studies that primarily focused on enrichment strategies, the current work provides a broader pH-response evaluation by simultaneously assessing extraction yield, oxidative indices, antioxidant activity, instrumental color measurements, and individual phenolic transformations across a systematically controlled pH range.

The aim of this study was to investigate the effect of olive paste pH on olive oil yield and quality during the malaxation step. To achieve this, different pH levels were obtained by adding food-grade citric acid to the olive paste, and the resulting oils were evaluated for their phenolic composition (particularly oleuropein-derived compounds), extraction yield, and key quality parameters such as FFA and peroxide value (PV). By clarifying how pH modification influences the release of phenolic compounds and mechanical oil recovery, this study aims to provide new insights that may support the production of higher-quality olive oils with improved functional properties.

Materials and methods

Materials

Olive (Olea europaea L.) fruits of the Gemlik cultivar were manually harvested near Mut, Mersin, Türkiye (36° 38′ 38.00″ N, 33° 26′ 19.86″ E) in early November 2023. The fruits were collected from a semi-mature rainfed orchard (200 trees/ha) with a mean annual rainfall of 1096 mm. Approximately 150 kg of healthy olives were selected for processing. Fruit maturity was determined according to the method described by Boskou23, and the ripening index was approximately 5.5. All chemicals used for analytical procedures were of analytical grade.

Experimental design

Freshly harvested Gemlik olives were stored overnight at 4 °C and then cleaned to remove leaves, branches, and other foreign materials before washing. Olive oil extraction was performed using a pilot-scale extraction system (Miniolive50Eko, Türkiye) consisting of crushing, malaxation, and decanting units.

Malaxation was performed in a conventional laboratory malaxer under atmospheric conditions, without inert gas protection, at a controlled temperature of 20–24 °C. The pH values reported correspond to measurements taken during the malaxation process. Olive paste pH was adjusted during malaxation using a 10% (w/v) citric acid solution for acidic treatments. For pH values above 4.7, a 0.5 N NaOH solution was used. The pH of the olive paste was adjusted by the gradual addition of 10% (w/v) citric acid solution or 0.5 N NaOH, depending on the target pH. The additions were performed stepwise under continuous mixing, and the pH was monitored throughout malaxation using a calibrated pH meter. The total volume of added solution did not exceed 150–200 mL and was adjusted to achieve the target pH. The amount of process water was correspondingly modified to maintain a constant total liquid content (15%, w/w) in the system. The electrode of the pH meter (Mettler-Toledo S220-K, USA) was immersed in the homogeneous sample to determine pH. The pH of the paste was adjusted to five levels between 2.7 and 6.7. For each batch, 15% water was added at ambient laboratory temperature (20–22 °C), and the paste was malaxed for 20 min. The initial pH of the olive paste (without added citric acid) was 4.7. In total, five treatments, including the control, were prepared in triplicate, resulting in 15 samples.

Following extraction, oils were not filtered prior to analysis. All analytical determinations were performed immediately after extraction, and no storage period was applied before analysis. All analytical measurements were performed on the oils in triplicate.

FFA, expressed as % oleic acid, was determined according to the official method of the AOCS Official Method Ca 5a-4024. PV was measured following ISO analytical procedures25.

Color measurement

The Oil color was measured with a Minolta CR-400 chromameter (Minolta Co., Osaka, Japan) using the Hunter L*, a*, b* color system. All measurements were carried out in triplicate26.

Determination of total phenolic content (TPC) and DPPH radical scavenging activity (DPPH-rsa)

Phenolic compounds were isolated from olive oil samples by liquid-liquid extraction. Briefly, oil samples were dissolved in hexane, and phenolics were extracted using a methanol: water mixture (80:20, v/v). Methanolic fractions were collected for TPC and DPPH-rsa analyses. TPC was quantified using the Folin-Ciocalteu colorimetric method as described by Caponio et al.27. Absorbance was measured spectrophotometrically, and the results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per kg of oil. DPPH-rsa was determined according to the procedure described by Roginsky and Lissi28. For analysis, 0.1 mL of the methanolic extract was mixed with 0.9 mL of HCl buffer (pH 7.4) and 2 mL of DPPH solution (Fluka, Germany). The mixture was incubated for 30 min in the dark at room temperature. Absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Biochrom Libra S22, UK). Results were expressed as percentage of DPPH inhibition.

HPLC analysis of phenolic compounds

Phenolic profiles were analyzed using an Agilent 1100 HPLC system equipped with an autosampler, quaternary pump, vacuum degasser, thermostatted column compartment, and diode array detector(DAD). Separation was achieved on an Inertsil ODS-3 column (25 cm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm; GL Sciences, Tokyo, Japan) maintained at 40 °C.

The injection volume was 40 µL, and chromatograms were recorded at 240, 280, and 320 nm. The mobile phases consisted of 3% aqueous formic acid (A) and methanol (B), with a total run time of 76 min following the gradient program as published earlier29.

The flow rate was 0.85 mL/min. Individual phenolic compounds were quantified using four-point external calibration curves prepared from commercial standards. Quantification of phenolic compounds was performed using external calibration with gallic acid as a reference compound, and results were expressed as gallic acid equivalents (mg kg⁻¹), due to the lack of commercially available standards for some secoiridoids (3,4-DHPEA-EDA, p-HPEA-EDA, 3,4-DHPEA-EA, p-HPEA-EA)30.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics software package (Version 22.0, IBM Corp., New York, USA). Differences among mean values were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan’s multiple range test for post-hoc comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Regression analysis was carried out to assess the linear and non-linear relationships between olive paste pH and key response variables, including oil yield, phenolic content, and oxidation parameters. Model adequacy was evaluated based on coefficient of determination (R²). Quadratic regression curves were used to describe the pH-dependent trends. Regression analyses were conducted using quadratic models to describe the dependence of the measured parameters on olive-paste pH. Quadratic models were selected when they adequately represented the data (R² ≥ 0.65). When the fit was insufficient (R² < 0.65), regression equations were not reported. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated, and statistical significance was assessed at p < 0.01 and p < 0.05. Two complementary approaches were applied. First, a global correlation analysis was conducted using the pooled dataset that included all pH treatments, with olive paste pH treated as a categorical processing factor. Second, pH-specific correlation analyses were performed by evaluating datasets for each pH level to assess pH-dependent variations in the magnitude and direction of correlations among variables. All results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation of triplicate measurements.

Results and discussion

Effect of olive paste pH on oil yield

Olive paste pH significantly influenced oil extraction yield (Fig. 1).

Effect of olive paste pH on oil yield (%) (Polynomial regression curves were used to describe the pH-dependent trends. Quadratic regression curves are included only for parameters demonstrating an adequate descriptive fit (R² ≥ 0.65)).

As pH increased from 2.7 to 3.7, oil yield increased, then showed a slight decrease at pH 4.7 and 5.7. The highest oil yield was observed at pH 6.7. At higher pH values, the weakening of hydrophilic colloidal structures and improved emulsion breakdown may facilitate oil droplet coalescence and more efficient mechanical separation, ultimately leading to higher extraction yields31. In contrast, intermediate pH conditions appear to favor the stabilization of oil-water emulsions through enhanced colloidal interactions, thereby limiting oil recovery and reducing extraction efficiency19.

Olive paste pH plays a critical regulatory role in the activity of oxidative enzymes such as polyphenol oxidase and peroxidase, whose catalytic efficiencies are strongly pH-dependent. A moderate reduction in pH has been shown to partially inhibit these enzymes, thereby limiting phenolic oxidation and favoring the retention of secoiridoid derivatives during malaxation17,19,20.

The higher oil yield observed at elevated pH values, despite the reduction in phenolic content, suggests a phase-dependent separation mechanism. Increased pH likely promotes emulsion destabilization and oil droplet coalescence, thereby improving mechanical extraction efficiency. However, many phenolic compounds exhibit hydrophilic characteristics and may preferentially partition into the aqueous phase under higher pH conditions. Therefore, while higher pH enhances oil recovery, it may simultaneously reduce phenolic retention in the oil fraction.

Effect of olive paste pH on FFA

FFA showed a non-monotonic response to pH modification (Fig. 2). The lowest FFA values were recorded at pH 4.7, while higher values were observed at pH 3.7 and pH 5.7. The minimum FFA observed at pH 4.7 may be attributed to partial inhibition of endogenous lipase activity, as lipase-mediated triglyceride hydrolysis is known to be strongly pH-dependent32,33. Deviations from this pH appear to enhance lipolytic activity, leading to increased FFA formation. Conversely, the reduction in FFA observed at pH 6.7 may be related to the relatively short malaxation time employed in this study, as well as the metal-chelating action of citric acid, which can limit the availability of metal ions involved in lipase activation34.

Effect of olive paste pH on PV

PV was strongly affected by the olive paste pH (Fig. 3). The highest PV was detected at pH 3.7 (8.4 meqO2kg⁻¹ oil), while a progressive decrease was observed from pH 4.7 to pH 6.7. The dependence of PV on pH could be described by a quadratic model (R² = 0.679).

Effect of olive paste pH on PV (meqO2kg− 1) (Polynomial regression curves were used to describe the pH-dependent trends. Quadratic regression curves are included only for parameters demonstrating an adequate descriptive fit (R² ≥ 0.65).

Elevated PV observed at lower pH may reflect accelerated primary oxidation reactions, as PV is a direct indicator of hydroperoxide formation during the early stages of lipid oxidation32. Intermediate pH levels may temporarily favor primary oxidation reactions during malaxation, whereas low (pH 2.7) or high pH conditions limit hydroperoxide formation, possibly through enzyme inhibition or enhanced antioxidant protection. As pH increased, the enhanced effectiveness of phenolic antioxidants together with the metal-chelating action of citric acid likely contributed to the suppression of hydroperoxide formation, leading to lower PV values20,34,35.

Effect of olive paste pH on TPC and DPPH-rsa

The pH of olive paste markedly influenced both total and individual phenolic compounds in the extracted oils. The highest TPC was observed at pH 2.7 (678 mg GAE kg⁻¹), while a substantial decrease was recorded at pH 6.7, where TPC reached its lowest value (281 mg GAE kg⁻¹). TPC exhibited distinct pH-dependent behavior (Fig. 4).

TPC was highest at pH 2.7, decreased at pH 3.7, reached a secondary maximum at pH 4.7, and declined sharply at pH 5.7 and 6.7. This relationship was modeled using the quadratic regression equation (R² = 0.733):

Under the atmospheric malaxation conditions applied, lower pH likely contributed to partial suppression of PPO-mediated oxidation, thereby favoring phenolic retention. However, at higher pH values, increased phenolic oxidation and increased migration into the aqueous phase during malaxation may explain the observed reduction in TPC.

These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that acidification of olive paste enhances the extraction and preservation of phenolic compounds. Peralta et al.12 demonstrated that organic acid addition during malaxation increased the concentration of bioactive phenolics in olive oil, supporting the role of pH as a key factor in phenolic transfer and stability.

The antioxidant capacity of olive oil, assessed by DPPH-rsa, closely followed the trend observed for TPC (Fig. 5). DPPH-rsa was highest at pH 2.7 and 4.7, and decreased markedly at pH 5.7 and 6.7. The relationship between pH and DPPH-rsa was described by a quadratic regression equation (R² = 0.811).

Effect of olive paste pH on DPPH-radical scavenging activity (% inhibition). Polynomial regression curves were used to describe the pH-dependent trends. Quadratic regression curves are included only for parameters demonstrating an adequate descriptive fit (R² ≥ 0.65)).

This strong correspondence confirms that phenolic compounds are the primary contributors to the antioxidant activity of olive oil. Higher pH values are likely to promote phenolic degradation and reduce the radical-scavenging efficiency. The DPPH-rsa results closely mirrored the phenolic profile. Oils obtained at lower pH values exhibited DPPH inhibition values exceeding 40%, which were approximately twofold higher than those of oils produced at pH 5.7 and 6.7. This strong enhancement in radical scavenging capacity corroborates the dominant role of phenolic compounds in determining antioxidant potential.

The non-linear responses observed in total phenolic concentration and DPPH-rsa indicate that the pH of olive paste modulates multiple partially competing mechanisms during malaxation. Moderately acidic conditions may limit enzymatic oxidation while maintaining sufficient phenolic solubility in the oil phase. Outside this range, either enhanced oxidative reactions or increased partitioning into the aqueous phase may contribute to phenolic losses31,36.

Collectively, the findings demonstrate that olive paste pH exerts a complex influence on oil quality parameters. While higher pH values tended to favor oil yield, lower and intermediate pH values supported greater phenolic retention and antioxidant activity. Among the tested treatments, pH 4.7 provided a balanced profile, combining relatively high concentrations of secoiridoid derivatives and elevated DPPH-rsa without severely compromising oil yield or increasing oxidation indices.

Effect of olive paste pH on color parameters (L*, a*, b*)

The olive oil lightness (L*) was significantly affected by olive paste pH (Fig. 6).

Changes in color parameters of virgin olive oil as affected by olive paste pH: lightness (L*). Quadratic regression curves are included only for parameters demonstrating an adequate descriptive fit (R² ≥ 0.65).

L* values increased from pH 2.7, reaching a maximum at pH 4.7, then decreased at pH 5.7 and partially increased at pH 6.7. The increase in L* values up to pH 4.7 suggests improved oil clarity and light reflectance, potentially resulting from reduced suspended solids and more effective oil-water phase separation during malaxation31. In contrast, the decrease observed at pH 5.7 may indicate increased pigment solubilization or the formation of stable micro-emulsions, which can reduce optical clarity. The partial recovery of L* at pH 6.7 may be attributed to enhanced oil droplet coalescence and reduced turbidity, consistent with previously reported effects of processing conditions on emulsion breakdown and oil clarification19.

The a* values of olive oil exhibited a clear pH-dependent trend (Fig. 7). The a* values increased (became less negative) from pH 2.7 to pH 4.7, then decreased at higher pH levels. The quadratic regression equation describing this relationship was (R² = 0.899).

Changes in color parameters of virgin olive oil as affected by olive paste pH: redness/greenness (a*). Correlation coefficients were calculated, with R² = 1.00 for all models. Quadratic regression curves are included only for parameters demonstrating an adequate descriptive fit (R² ≥ 0.65).

The shift toward less negative a* values observed at pH 4.7 indicates a reduction in green color intensity, which may be associated with partial chlorophyll degradation or altered pigment-protein interactions under mildly acidic processing conditions33,37. The subsequent decrease in a* values at pH 4–5 suggests enhanced green pigmentation, potentially due to increased chlorophyll stability or modified pigment partitioning between oil and aqueous phases at higher pH levels26,31,32.

The b* values were also significantly influenced by paste pH (Fig. 8). A pronounced decrease in b* was observed from pH 2.7 to pH 4.7, followed by an increase at pH 5.7 and 6.7. This behavior was well described by the quadratic regression (R² = 0.970).

Changes in color parameters of virgin olive oil as affected by olive paste pH: yellowness/blueness (b*). Correlation coefficients were calculated as R² = 1.00 for all the models. Quadratic regression curves are included only for parameters demonstrating an adequate descriptive fit (R² ≥ 0.65).

Lower b* values observed at pH 3.7 indicate reduced yellowness, which may be associated with altered carotenoid stability or their preferential migration into the aqueous phase during malaxation31,37. Conversely, the increase in b* values at higher pH levels suggests enhanced carotenoid retention within the oil phase or reduced oxidative degradation, consistent with previous reports on pigment behavior under varying processing conditions26,32,33.

Color attributes of olive oil are primarily governed by chlorophylls and carotenoids, whose stability and partitioning are highly sensitive to processing conditions32,37. The observed non-linear relationships between olive paste pH and L*, a*, and b* values indicate that pH modulates pigment behavior through multiple mechanisms, including enzymatic activity, pigment-protein interactions, and phase partitioning during malaxation26,31.

The convergence of maximum L* and minimum b* values at pH 4.7 suggests the production of a brighter and less yellow olive oil, a color profile often associated with enhanced sensory clarity and perceived freshness32,38. At higher pH values (pH 4–5), the increase in yellowness (b*) together with the recovery of green tones (more negative a*) may reflect improved pigment stability; however, this condition may also indicate increased susceptibility to photo-oxidation during storage due to the pro-oxidant role of chlorophylls in the presence of light26,33.

These findings demonstrate that olive paste pH is not only a determinant of chemical quality and antioxidant capacity but also a critical factor shaping the visual and sensory attributes of olive oil. Mildly acidic conditions(around pH 3–4)appear optimal for achieving balanced color characteristics, complementing the enhanced phenolic content and antioxidant activity observed at similar pH levels.

Effect of olive paste pH on individual phenolic compounds

Flavonoids: luteolin and apigenin

The concentrations of flavonoid compounds were markedly influenced by olive paste pH (Fig. 9). Luteolin content was highest at pH 2.7, decreased sharply at pH 3.7, increased again at pH 4.7, and progressively declined at higher pH values. This behavior was described using a quadratic regression model (R² = 0.727).

Influence of olive paste pH on the concentration (mg kg− 1) of individual phenolic compound in virgin olive oil. Quadratic regression curves are included only for parameters demonstrating an adequate descriptive fit (R² ≥ 0.65).

Apigenin showed a pronounced maximum at pH 3.7, followed by a sharp decrease at pH 4.7 and minimal levels at pH 5.7 and 6.7. The relationship between pH and apigenin concentration followed a regression model (R² = 0.669).

These results indicate that mildly acidic conditions favor flavonoid stability and extractability, whereas higher pH levels promote degradation or migration into the aqueous phase. The sharp decline observed beyond pH 4.7 suggests increased susceptibility of flavonoids to oxidative and enzymatic degradation under less acidic conditions.

Secoiridoid derivatives: oleacein, oleocanthal and their aglycones

Oleacein content exhibited a pronounced pH-dependent trend, increasing from pH 3.7 to a maximum at pH 4.7 (257 mg kg⁻¹), then sharply decreasing at pH 5.7 and 6.7 (Fig. 10). Similarly, oleocanthal exhibited its highest concentration at pH 4.7 (159.7 mg kg⁻¹), whereas pH values lower or higher than 4.7 resulted in reduced concentrations.

Influence of olive paste pH on the concentration (mg kg− 1) of Secoiridoid Derivatives: Oleacein, Oleocanthal and Their Aglycones in virgin olive oil. Quadratic regression curves are included only for parameters demonstrating an adequate descriptive fit(R² ≥ 0.65).

These findings suggest that moderate acidity (around pH 4.7) promotes the enzymatic hydrolysis of oleuropein and ligstroside into their corresponding dialdehydic forms, oleacein and oleocanthal, through enhanced β-glucosidase and esterase activity39,40. At higher pH values, increased oxidative degradation and enhanced partitioning of phenolic compounds into the aqueous phase may collectively account for the observed decrease in these secoiridoid derivatives19,21,41.

The observed increase in oleacein and oleocanthal under acidic conditions is consistent with previous findings, where acidification promoted β-glucosidase activity and enhanced secoiridoid formation12.

Phenolic alcohols: tyrosol, hydroxytyrosol and hydroxytyrosol acetate

Tyrosol concentration increased from pH 2.7, reached a maximum at pH 4.7, and declined thereafter (Fig. 11). This trend followed the regression (R² = 0.691).

Influence of olive paste pH on the concentration (mg kg− 1) of Phenolic Alcohols: Tyrosol, Hydroxytyrosol and Hydroxytyrosol Acetate in virgin olive oil. Quadratic regression curves are included only for parameters demonstrating an adequate descriptive fit (R² ≥ 0.65).

Hydroxytyrosol exhibited a distinct maximum at pH 3.7, followed by a sharp decrease at pH 4.7 and a slight recovery at higher pH values. Hydroxytyrosol acetate exhibited its maximum concentration at pH 4.7, with a pronounced decline at pH 5.7 and 6.7.

These results reflect the dynamic balance among hydrolysis, esterification, and oxidation reactions governing the fate of phenolic alcohols during olive oil processing. Lower pH values favor the formation of hydroxytyrosol through the hydrolytic cleavage of oleuropein derivatives, whereas intermediate pH conditions promote the stabilization and formation of acetylated phenolic forms39–41. In contrast, higher pH conditions may accelerate oxidative degradation pathways, leading to phenolic depletion19,32.

Oleuropein aglycone derivatives (3,4-DHPEA-EA and p-HPEA-EA)

The concentration of 3,4-DHPEA-EA decreased progressively with increasing pH, showing the highest levels at pH 2.7(Fig. 12), and this relationship showed a good fit to a quadratic model (R² = 0.878).

Influence of olive paste pH on the concentration (mg.kg− 1) of Oleuropein Aglycone Derivatives(3,4-DHPEA-EA and p-HPEA-EA)in virgin olive oil. Quadratic regression curves are included only for parameters demonstrating an adequate descriptive fit (R² ≥ 0.65).

In contrast, p-HPEA-EA exhibited a complex response, with relatively high levels at pH 2.7–3.7, a minimum at pH 4.7, and partial recovery at pH 5.7.

These opposing trends highlight the differential stability of oleuropein- and ligstroside-derived aglycones in response to pH changes, reflecting distinct enzymatic sensitivities and oxidation pathways.

These results demonstrate that olive paste pH modulates individual phenolic compounds through a complex interplay of enzymatic hydrolysis, oxidation, esterification, and phase partitioning during malaxation. While lower pH values (2.7–3.7) favor the preservation of simple phenolics and flavonoids, intermediate pH values (around 4.7) promote the formation of bioactive secoiridoid derivatives such as oleacein and oleocanthal. At higher pH values (5.7–6.7), oxidative degradation and phenolic losses predominate.

The secondary maximum of TPC observed at pH 4.7 suggests that moderately acidic conditions may create a favorable balance between enzymatic hydrolysis and phase partitioning during malaxation. Endogenous β-glucosidase and related hydrolases are known to exhibit higher activity under mildly acidic conditions, which may enhance the conversion of oleuropein and ligstroside derivatives into more oil-soluble phenolic forms. While a very low pH (2.7) likely limits oxidative degradation and preserves total phenolic content, excessive acidity may influence the solubility and partitioning of certain compounds between the aqueous and lipid phases. In contrast, at higher pH values, increased oxidative reactions or preferential migration of phenolics into the aqueous phase may contribute to the decline in TPC. Therefore, pH 4.7 may represent an intermediate condition in which hydrolytic release of phenolic compounds and their retention in the oil phase are relatively balanced.

PV was positively correlated with FFA, although the strength of this relationship decreased at pH 6.7, as reflected by lower coefficients of determination. Overall, correlation analysis indicated a moderate negative relationship between PV and acetoxypinoresinol (r = − 0.534, p < 0.05). In addition, PV exhibited a strong negative correlation with TPC at pH 6.7, suggesting enhanced oxidative susceptibility under higher pH conditions. The negative correlation between PV and phenolic compounds reflects the protective role of phenolics against primary lipid oxidation, as these compounds can chelate pro− oxidant metal ions and scavenge lipid radicals, thereby delaying hydroperoxide formation32,35. At higher pH values, the reduced inhibitory effect on oxidative enzymes may accelerate phenolic depletion, which in turn compromises oxidative stability and results in higher susceptibility to peroxide formation20.

A strong negative correlation between pH and TPC was observed ( r = − 0.939, p < 0.01), indicating that increasing paste pH led to a pronounced decrease in total phenolic concentration. This trend is consistent with the known instability of secoiridoid derivatives and phenolic glycosides under less acidic conditions, where hydrolytic and oxidative degradation reactions are favored. Lower pH values appear to enhance the preservation and extractability of phenolic compounds during malaxation.

DPPH− rsa showed a very strong positive correlation (r = 0.916, p < 0.01) with TPC, confirming the dominant contribution of phenolic compounds to the antioxidant capacity. Significant positive correlations were also detected between DPPH inhibition and individual phenolic compounds, including hydroxytyrosol acetate, oleacein, oleocanthal, 3,4− DHPEA− EA, and luteolin. The strongest DPPH–phenolic correlations were observed at pH 3.7, indicating optimal phenolic preservation at this pH level. DPPH− rsa was positively correlated with oleacein and inversely correlated with pH. The general correlation between DPPH− rsa and TPC was strong (r ≈ 0.72, p < 0.01), whereas DPPH− rsa also closely tracked oleacein levels across pH treatments. This finding highlights oleacein’s pivotal role in defining the antioxidant capacity of olive oil, a conclusion consistent with its well− documented radical− scavenging efficiency and biological activity.

Oleacein showed a very strong negative correlation with pH (general r ≈ − 0.99, p < 0.01), confirming that acidic paste conditions strongly favor oleacein formation and retention. This behavior can be attributed to enhanced enzymatic conversion of oleuropein aglycone into dialdehydic forms under acidic conditions and to reduced oxidative breakdown. The close alignment between TPC and oleacein suggests that oleacein is one of the dominant contributors to the total phenolic pool under controlled pH conditions. Secoiridoid derivatives such as oleacein and oleocanthal have been reported to contribute disproportionately to antioxidant activity due to their ortho− diphenolic structure, explaining their strong positive correlations with both total phenolics and DPPH inhibition observed in this study32,36.

The strong correlation between TPC and DPPH− rsa confirms that olive oil antioxidant capacity is predominantly governed by phenolic compounds, particularly secoiridoids and simple phenols, which act as efficient hydrogen and electron donors42,43.

Several individual phenolic compounds, namely hydroxytyrosol acetate, oleacein, p− DHPEA− EA, oleocanthal, acetoxypinoresinol, and luteolin, showed positive correlations with TPC, highlighting their quantitative contribution to the overall phenolic pool of olive oil.

Regarding color attributes, the lightness parameter (L*) exhibited positive correlations with TPC at pH 3.7 and 5.7, whereas L* exhibited a strong negative correlation with p− DHPEA− EA at pH 6.7. Overall, correlation coefficients revealed significant negative associations between L* and both p− DHPEA− EA and apigenin, indicating that olive oil progressively darkens as concentrations of these compounds increase. The inverse relationship between lightness (L*) and specific phenolic compounds suggests that increased concentrations of secoiridoids and flavonoids contribute to darker oil appearance, either through direct pigmentation effects or by promoting secondary oxidation products that affect color perception26,37.

The redness parameter (a*) increased with rising PVs and with higher concentrations of tyrosol and oleocanthal, suggesting a relationship between oxidative status and a chromatic shift toward red hues. In contrast, the yellowness parameter (b*) showed positive correlations with 3,4− DHPEA− EA, p− DHPEA− EA, and apigenin. Furthermore, a* values correlated positively with TPC at pH 4.7, while b* values correlated positively with TPC at pH 2.7 and 3.7.

Taken together, these correlations clearly demonstrate that paste acidification enhances phenolic preservation, particularly oleacein, which in turn drives antioxidant activity. The tight coupling between pH, TPC, oleacein, and DPPH− rsa supports a mechanistic pathway in which pH modulation governs enzymatic reactions and phenolic stability during processing, ultimately shaping both chemical quality and functional properties of olive oil.

The correlation analysis confirms that pH manipulation during olive paste processing is an effective technological tool for modulating phenolic composition and antioxidant capacity, with oleacein as a key marker compound. In the present study, causality is supported not only by correlation analysis but also by the experimental control of pH as an independent processing parameter and by the application of polynomial regression models, which collectively demonstrate structured and reproducible responses of phenolic compounds and antioxidant activity to pH variation.

Detailed supplementary data, including color parameters, individual phenolic compounds, and additional quality indices, are provided in the Supplementary Material (Tables S1 and S2 and Fig. S1).

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that olive paste pH during malaxation is a significant technological factor influencing oil yield, phenolic composition, antioxidant activity, PV, and instrumental color attributes of virgin olive oil. Systematic pH adjustment revealed clear non− linear responses in several key parameters, particularly TPC, DPPH− rsa, PV, a*, b*, and selected phenolic compounds, including apigenin, luteolin, tyrosol, and 3,4− DHPEA− EA.

Lower paste pH values favored the preservation of total phenolics and resulted in higher radical scavenging activity, with maximum TPC and DPPH− rsa observed at pH 2.7. In contrast, moderately acidic conditions (around pH 4.7) resulted in elevated levels of bioactive secoiridoid derivatives, such as oleacein and oleocanthal, while the highest oil yield was obtained at pH 6.7. These findings indicate a trade− off between extraction efficiency and phenolic quality across the tested pH range.

PV and color parameters were also significantly modulated by pH, indicating that paste acidity affects not only the phenolic transfer but also the oxidative behavior and the visual appearance of the extracted oils. Collectively, the results confirm that olive paste pH should be considered an independent technological variable capable of shaping the quality of virgin olive oil, and that it may serve as a useful tool for targeted process optimization, depending on the desired analytical profile.

Despite these promising findings, some limitations should be acknowledged. The experiments were conducted under controlled malaxation conditions, and direct extrapolation to continuous industrial processing requires further validation. In addition, the study focused on the immediate quality characteristics of freshly extracted oils, while long− term storage stability and sensory consequences of pH modulation were beyond the scope of the present work. Future studies should, therefore, evaluate the persistence of these compositional changes during storage and their practical relevance in industrial− scale production.
