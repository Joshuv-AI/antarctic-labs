Intraoperative and postoperative evaluation of temporomandibular joint disc repositioning and suturing via anteriorly based or posteriorly based approaches to the neurovascular bundle: a retrospective single-center cohort study
Authors: Ying Zhang, Zhenlong Liu, Li Deng, Wenchao Zhong, Zhiqiang Song, Qingbin Zhang, Zixian Jiao, Antong Wu, Wei Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate a modified posteriorly based approach of superficial temporal neurovascular bundle for temporomandibular joint (TMJ) disc repositioning and suturing in patients with anterior disc displacement without reduction (ADDWoR), and to assess its immediate clinical outcomes.Methods and materialsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 132 patients (218 joints) treated at the Affiliated Stomatology Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University between August 2023 and April 2024. All patients were diagnosed with ADDWoR corresponding to Wilkes–Bronstein stages III–V. Open TMJ disc repositioning and suturing were performed using either a posteriorly based or anteriorly based approach relative to the neurovascular bundle. Intraoperative parameters and immediate postoperative outcomes were compared between the two groups.ResultsPostoperative MRI confirmed successful disc reduction in 211 of 218 joints. Intraoperative bleeding was significantly higher in the posterior group (25.06 ± 8.00 mL) than in the anterior group (14.52 ± 4.83 mL). Operating duration was also longer in the posterior group (103.1 ±27.64 minvs.87.85 ± 23.93 min). No skin flap perforation occurred in the posterior group, whereas 5 cases were observed in the anterior group. The posterior group exhibited a higher postoperative infection rate (9.09%) than the anterior group, whereas transient facial nerve injury was more frequent in the anterior group (27.27% vs. 9.09%). Postoperative fever was more common in the posterior group (18.18% vs. 3.03%), and surgical site drainage was greater in the posterior group (13.87 ± 3.90 mL vs. 11.52 ± 3.52 mL). Perioperative inflammatory markers and hospitalization duration showed no significant differences between the two groups.ConclusionThe modified posteriorly based approach for TMJ disc repositioning and suturing is a safe and effective technique for treating ADDWoR. Compared with the anteriorly based approach, it offers advantages in reducing facial nerve injury and minimizing flap complications, despite longer operative time and increased intraoperative bleeding.Keywords: Temporomandibular joint, Anterior disc displacement without reduction, Superficial temporal artery, Neurovascular bundle

Introduction

Temporomandibular joint disorder (TMD) is a highly prevalent condition, with studies reporting that 12–87% of the population exhibit at least one clinical sign of temporomandibular joint (TMJ) dysfunction [1, 2]. TMD can manifest as pain, joint sounds, functional limitations, jaw dysfunction, osteoarthritis, and dento-maxillofacial deformities. In recent years, TMD has increasingly affected younger populations, with epidemiological data from China indicating that 71.6% of patients are between 15 and 35 years of age [3]. Among the various subtypes, anterior disc displacement without reduction (ADDWoR) is the most common and is strongly associated with progressive joint dysfunction. Previous studies have demonstrated that anterior disc displacement, particularly cases without reduction, is a major contributor to pain [4, 5]. Effective management of ADDWoR requires anatomical repair, including restoration of retrodiscal tissue, establishment of a fibrocartilaginous covering on the condyle, and re-establishment of appropriate disc–condyle and fossa–disc relationships [6]. Disc repositioning and suture stabilization are essential to alleviate symptoms, reduce condylar stress, and promote TMJ reconstruction [7].

Surgical repositioning and suture stabilization of the displaced TMJ disc constitute an effective strategy for TMD management. Currently, two main approaches are available for TMJ disc reduction and fixation: arthroscopic surgery (Yang’s arthroscopic disc repositioning and suturing, also referred as Yang’s arthroscopic surgery) and open surgery [7, 8]. Arthroscopic surgery is generally recommended for patients with a relatively short disease duration and well-preserved disc morphology, whereas open surgery is preferred for those with longer disease duration, disc perforation, or thickened posterior bands.

For many oral and maxillofacial surgeons, arthroscopic disc repositioning and suturing to the auricular cartilage remain technically challenging. In comparison, open surgery is technically easily, has broader indications, and is less restricted by intraoperative visualization issues such as bleeding [6]. Within open surgery, two types of primary procedures are used for disc fixation: mini-screw anchoring (MA) and open suturing (OS). The classical method involves using a microanchor to fix the disc to the condyle, thereby enabling disc–condyle complex movement. However, placement of a metallic anchor in hypoplastic condyles may compromise blood supply and lead to resorption [6]. To address this limitation, He et al. [7] introduced OS, a technique involving disc suturing through an open incision that minimizes the risk of condylar resorption. This method is particularly advantageous for patients with small, osteoporotic, or developing condyles, and is simpler to learn and apply.

Notably, arthroscopic training is hindered by technical difficulties, particularly bleeding that obscures visualization and may necessitate premature termination of the procedure [6]. In contrast, open approaches provide clearer operative fields and greater procedural reproducibility. Previous studies have reported high disc position stability (DPS) rates on MRI follow-up, ranging from 88% to 98.6% [9] as well as positive condylar bone remodeling (CBR) rates ranging from 30.1% to 86.7% [10]. Interestingly, arthroscopic disc repositioning has been reported to achieve superior CBR compared with MA. For open procedures, a modified preauricular approach, located anterior to the neurovascular bundle, is commonly used to expose the joint capsule. However, this traditional technique carries risks, including facial nerve injury, skin perforation, and other complications.

The purpose of this study was to introduce a modified retrovascular approach for TMJ disc repositioning and suturing in cases of ADDWoR and to evaluate its immediate clinical outcomes. We hypothesized that the choice of surgical approach—anteriorly based versus posteriorly based relative to the neurovascular bundle—would result in clinically meaningful differences in perioperative and postoperative outcomes. Accordingly, the specific aim of this study was to compare the clinical performance, safety profiles, and early postoperative outcomes of the anteriorly based and posteriorly based approaches in patients undergoing TMJ disc repositioning and suturing.

Methods and materials

Research design and grouping

This study was a retrospective observational investigation conducted at the Affiliated Stomatology Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University between August 2023 and April 2024. A total of 132 patients (218 joints) diagnosed with ADDWoR, corresponding to stage III–V internal derangement according to the Wilkes–Bronstein staging system, were included. Patients were allocated into two groups according to the surgical approach used for disc repositioning: the anteriorly based approaches to the neurovascular bundle group (anterior group) and the posteriorly based to the neurovascular bundle group (posterior group). Inclusion criteria: (a) Confirmed diagnosis of ADDWoR by both clinical and MRI examination. (b) Persistent TMJ pain or dysfunction for at least 3 month. Exclusion criteria: (a) History of prior TMJ surgery. (b) Presence of systemic inflammatory or autoimmune disorders affecting the TMJ. (c) Severe comorbidities precluding surgical intervention. All patients provided written informed consent. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Stomatology Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (No. LCYJ20250417001).

Special suturing device

The TMJ disc suturing device consisted of a 20-gauge needle, a dural puncture needle (Tuoren Medical Instrument Co. Ltd., Henan, China), and a self-designed needle with an exchangeable hook-type suture gripper (Shanghai Pu Wei Medical Instrument Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China). The disc suture material was a 2 − 0 polyester multifilament suture comprising two zones: a guiding zone and a functional zone. The guiding zone, located at each end of the suture, was reinforced with an adhesion agent to increase stiffness, whereas the central functional zone remained soft and non-reinforced (Shanghai Pudong Golden Ring Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Surgical approaches

The procedures were performed according to previously described methods [6, 7, 11] with modifications. Posteriorly based to the neurovascular bundle approach: A modified preauricular incision was made (Fig. 1A1). Depending on ear size and the need for surgical field exposure, the incision could be extended superiorly into the helix region. After incision of the skin and subcutaneous tissue in front of the tragus (Fig. 1A2), the base of the zygomatic arch and condyle were palpated. The SMAS layer was reached by careful dissection from the base of the zygomatic arch and condyle (Fig. 1A3). The superficial temporal arteries and veins were retracted anteriorly, and the SMAS was incised to expose the bony surface of the zygomatic arch base. Dissection proceeded to expose the joint tubercle and joint capsule fully (Fig. 1A4). Anteriorly based approaches to the neurovascular bundle approach: A similar preauricular incision was made (Fig. 1B1). After incision of the skin and subcutaneous tissue, the flap was elevated anteriorly. At the temporoparietal fascia, the superficial temporal vessels became visible (Fig. 1B2). A cut was made anterior to these vessels, followed by entry into the SMAS layer (Fig. 1B3). The superficial temporal vessels were then retracted posteriorly, and the SMAS was incised to expose the joint tubercle and joint capsule (Fig. 1B4).

(A2) Incision through the skin and subcutaneous tissue; the superficial temporal neurovascular bundle is relatively distant from the tragus. (A3) Dissection posterior to the superficial temporal vessels to access the SMAS fascia layer. (A4) Incision of the SMAS layer to expose the zygomatic arch and joint capsule. (A5) Loosening of the anterior disc attachment. (A6, A7) Fixation of the articular disc. (A8) Closure of the surgical wound. B Anteriorly based approach: (B1) Modified preauricular incision. (B5) Loosening of the anterior disc attachment. (B6, B7) Fixation of the articular disc. (B8) Closure of the surgical wound

TMJ exposure and anterior release

After injection of 1–2 mL of 2% lidocaine into the TMJ suprarticular cavity, dissection was performed along the inferior border of the zygomatic arch, ending at the anterior slope of the articular tubercle. The articular capsule was entered with a #15 blade. The disc was identified and inspected for shape, size, and perforations. Normally, the disc appeared white and firm, while the anterior and posterior attachments appeared pink and soft. The borders between the disc and anterior attachments were identified and carefully released. Following injection of 3–5 mL vasoconstrictor into the anterior recess, the anterior attachment was dissected approximately 2–3 mm anterior to the disc using scissors or an electrotome (Fig. 1A5, B5). The anterior release was extended sufficiently to permit passive repositioning of the disc.

Suturing the disc to the external auditory canal

A 20-gauge needle containing the suture was used to puncture the TMJ disc at the lateral one-third junction between the disc and the posterior band. The needle was first directed downward into the lower joint space, then advanced medially to the medial one-third junction, and finally passed upward through the disc into the upper joint space. The assistant held the puncture needle while the operator pulled the suture out with a mosquito hemostat. The needle was then withdrawn from the disc.

Next, the hook-type suture gripper was inserted through the external auditory canal (approximately 10 mm from the tragus tip) to grasp the first end of the suture and pull it externally. It was reinserted through the external auditory canal into the upper joint space at least 3 mm from the first puncture site to retrieve the second end of the suture (Fig. 1A6, B6).

The disc was repositioned, and its placement was verified. If needed, a second suture was placed. The suture ends were tied with six knots to prevent loosening, and the knot was buried beneath the skin of the external auditory canal (Fig. 1A7, B7). The disc was intentionally overcorrected so that the posterior band was positioned at approximately the 1- to 2-o’clock position over the condylar head. The skin incision of the external auditory canal was closed with 5 − 0 sutures (Fig. 1A8, B8). The endaural incision was then closed in layers with a mini-drain inserted. Preoperative and 1-month postoperative MRI scans were obtained to evaluate the condyle–disc relationship.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 9.0, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the quantitative Data. Depending on data distribution and type, the following tests were applied to evaluate differences between the two groups: independent samples t-test, χ² test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the study population

A total of 132 patients were enrolled, comprising 36 males (27.27%) and 96 females (72.73%) (Fig. 2A). The largest age group was 15–19 years, accounting for 58 patients (43.94%) (Fig. 2D, G). Eighty-six patients (172 joints) presented with bilateral TMJ disease, while 46 patients (46 joints) had unilateral involvement (Fig. 2B). All patients included in this study were diagnosed with TMJ disc ADDWoR, corresponding to stage III–V of TMJ internal derangement according to the Wilkes–Bronstein staging system (Wilkes III: n = 50, 37.88%; Wilkes IV: n = 78, 59.09%; Wilkes V: n = 4, 3.03%) (Fig. 2F). Based on surgical approach, patients were divided into two equal groups: (1) Posterior group (modified posteriorly based approach of superficial temporal neurovascular bundle): 66 patients (54 females, 12 males). (2) Anterior group (traditional anteriorly based approach): 66 patients (42 females, 24 males) (Fig. 2C, E).

Intraoperative bleeding, duration, and skin flap perforation

Intraoperative indicators showed that bleeding was significantly greater in the posterior group (25.06 ± 8.00 mL) compared with the anterior group (14.52 ± 4.83 mL; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3A). Samilarly, operative time was markedly longer in the posteriorgroup (103.1 ± 27.64 min) than in the anterior group (87.85 ± 23.93 min; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3B). Notably, 0 case of skin flap perforation occurred in the posterior group, whereas 5 cases were observed in the anterior group (p = 0.0246) (Fig. 3C).

Postoperative complications and outcomes

Analysis of postoperative outcomes revealed significant differences between the two surgical approaches. The posterior group demonstrated a higher incidence of postoperative infection (9.09%) compared with no cases in the anterior group (p = 0.0278) (Fig. 4A). Facial nerve injury was significantly more common in the anterior group (27.27%) than in the posterior group (9.09%) (p = 0.0119) (Fig. 4B). Postoperative fever occurred more frequently in the posterior group (18.18%) than in the anterior group (3.03%) (p = 0.008) (Fig. 4C). Postoperative pain, measured by VAS, did not differ significantly between groups (posterior: 2.833 ± 1.145 vs. anterior: 3.108 ± 1.107; p = 0.1497) (Fig. 4D). However, the posterior group exhibited greater surgical site drainage (13.87 ± 3.897 ml) compared with the anterior group (11.52 ± 3.524 ml; p = 0.0004) (Fig. 4E). The mean length of hospitalization (day) was comparable between groups (posterior: 6.477 ± 1.659 vs. anterior: 6.154 ± 1.326 days; p = 0.2223) (Fig. 4F). Analysis of perioperative inflammatory markers showed no significant differences in WBC count or neutrophil count between the posterior and anterior groups at baseline or after surgery (all p > 0.05) (Fig. 5A and B).

Postoperative MRI outcomes

The TMJ disc reduction outcomes were evaluated according to the criteria described by Zhang et al., which classify results as excellent, good, or poor [12]. Representative MRI images of ADDWoR cases in the posterior and anterior groups are shown in Fig. 6A. Preoperatively, the discs were compressed and folded, with the posterior margin displaced anterior to the condylar head. Postoperatively, the discs were restored to their normal anatomical position, demonstrating an improved disc–condyle relationship (Fig. 6B). Clinical evaluation demonstrated a total effectiveness rate of 98.2% in the posterior group, with 92.9% rated as excellent (105/113), 5.3% as good (6/113), and only 1.8% as poor (2/113). In the anterior group, the total effectiveness rate was 95.2% (100/105), with 4.8% of cases rated as poor (5/105) (Fig. 6C).

Discussion

Over the past 138 years, oral and maxillofacial surgeons have sought to develop minimally invasive surgical approaches for the management of TMJ ADDwoR [13]. The primary goals of disc repositioning surgery are to improve mandibular mobility, alleviate TMJ-related pain, prevent condylar resorption, and promote condylar bone regeneration [14, 15]. A common feature across various surgical techniques is the use of a traction suture placed through the posterior band of the TMJ disc to reposition it into a more physiologic location. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated a strong association between ADD, osteoarthritis, and condylar deformities in adolescents [8]. Open surgical repositioning of the TMJ disc was first reported by Annandale in 1887 [13]. Nearly a century later, in 1978, Wilkes elucidated the anatomy and function of the TMJ through arthrography, which established disc reduction as a widely accepted and improved treatment modality [16]. Since the 1990s, one of the present authors has adopted and refined techniques introduced by surgeons in the United States [17] and Japan, further modifying disc suturing by developing dedicated suturing devices to enhance arthroscopic repositioning outcomes [18]. A common feature of these approaches was the placement of a traction suture through the outer one-third of the posterior disc, with fixation to adipose tissue outside the articular capsule or to the facial skin. However, these methods were limited by suboptimal success rates and poor long-term stability. This was primarily due to two factors: (1) the pulling direction of the suture did not align with the actual displacement of the disc, and (2) the medial portion of the disc could not be adequately reduced with a single lateral suture. To address these limitations, Yang proposed a modification using two traction sutures, which reposition the disc more effectively and are fixed to the soft tissue beneath the auricular cartilage, thereby improving disc reduction stability [19]. However, performing disc repositioning within the small confines of the TMJ space remains technically challenging for most surgeons. Accurate orientation and puncture into the correct anatomical position is difficult, and intraoperative factors such as disc swelling or bleeding within the superior joint space further complicate the procedure. Therefore, open joint surgeries still have clinical value.Based on the anatomical characteristics of the temporomandibular joint, preauricular, intraural and postauricular incisions are used for routine and microsurgical procedures of the TMJ [20].To simplify the surgical process and reduce technical difficulty, an open surgical approach has been introduced. This approach employs a small incision along the course of the superficial temporal neurovascular bundle, avoiding the need for endoscopic assistance.

In this technique, the surgical pathway is developed posterior to the superficial temporal neurovascular bundle through the temporoparietal fascia, rather than anteriorly along the external auditory canal. The superficial temporal artery (STA) and vein, which are branches of the external carotid artery, are major vessels in the temporal region supplying the scalp, TMJ, adjacent muscles, and overlying skin. Dissection of the STA in a cadaveric specimen clearly demonstrated the STA, vein, and auriculotemporal nerve. Because of its accessibility and reliability, the STA is frequently used as a donor vessel in microvascular reconstruction, and it also plays an essential role in regulating blood flow to the scalp and periauricular region.

The modified approach described in this article utilizes the STA as a key surgical landmark. A modified endaural incision with a small posterior extension is made, and instead of dissecting anterior to the superficial temporal vessels along the external auditory canal, the dissection proceeds posterior to the vessels through the temporoparietal fascia. The temporal branch of the facial nerve courses anterior to the STA, allowing this approach to safeguard both the superficial temporal vessels and the temporal branch. Upon entering the superficial layer of the deep temporal fascia, the flap is reflected forward along the zygomatic arch. Compared with the conventional anteriorly based approach, this posteriorly based approach is further away from the facial nerve and offers superior facial nerve protection and provides more extensive exposure of the posterior joint and articular disc, thereby facilitating posterior release, disc repositioning, and suturing with greater ease.

Compared with disc suturing using open surgery with a mini-screw anchor in the condyle, the novel approach proposed in this study—employing a small incision anterior to the tragus and dissecting posterior to the superficial temporal vein—demonstrated superior outcomes in terms of surgical access, skin flap protection, and facial nerve preservation [1]. Disc retention method. Annandale [13] first described surgical repositioning of the displaced TMJ disc in 1887. Nearly a century later, Wilkes [16] delineated the anatomy, morphology, and function of the TMJ, which facilitated the widespread adoption of disc repositioning procedures. McCarty et al. [21] reported resection of the bilaminar zone and disc replacement with a 94% success rate, but this technique was not reproduced by other surgeons, prompting the development of numerous modified disc repositioning procedures with variable outcomes [22]. Wolford [1] was the first to describe fixation of the TMJ disc using a Mitek anchor inserted into the condyle. However, implantation of titanium mini-screws carries surgical risks, including screw loosening, condylar resorption, and postoperative pain. In contrast, the disc reduction technique described in the present study avoids implants altogether by suturing the disc to the external auditory canal rather than anchoring it to the condyle [7]. This approach is safer, preserves the structural integrity of the condyle, and better maintains its vascular supply—advantages that are particularly important in adolescent patients and in those with idiopathic condylar resorption (ICR) [23, 2]. Skin flap protection. The anteriorly based temporal approach requires wide flap elevation anterior to the superficial temporal arteriovenous bundle to achieve sufficient exposure. Because the skin layer in this region is thin, it is highly susceptible to perforation during flap elevation with electrocautery. In this study, no cases of skin flap perforation occurred in the posterior group, whereas five cases were documented in the anterior group out of 105 operated TMJs. However, it should be noted that intraoperative bleeding was significantly greater, and operative time was correspondingly longer, in the posterior group compared with the anterior group.

Postoperative parameters of TMJ disc repositioning and suturing surgery—including infection rate, postoperative fever, VAS pain scores, wound drainage, and length of hospitalization—are important indicators for comparing surgical approaches. In our study, these postoperative outcomes did not show correlation with patients’ age. Instead, intraoperative factors such as bleeding volume and prolonged surgical time may explain the relatively higher infection rate. Notably, release of the anterior disc attachments is technically more demanding via the posteriorly based approach. Because the joint capsule is exposed posteriorly, the posteriorly based approach is associated with relatively greater intraoperative bleeding and longer operative duration. Adequate release of the anterior attachments is essential to ensure successful disc reduction. Achieving sufficient release often requires dissection and separation of additional soft tissues during flap elevation at the deep temporal fascia layer, which may increase the risks of bleeding and prolonged operation time, thereby predisposing to higher rates of postoperative infection, increased drainage, postoperative fever, hematologic changes, and extended hospitalization.

Despite these disadvantages in exposing anterior attachments, the posteriorly based approach offers notable benefits. This technique provides superior exposure of the space posterior to the glenoid fossa, facilitating faster and more reliable fixation of the posterior disc. In particular, puncture and suture placement at the junction between the posterior band of the disc and retrodiscal tissue can be performed more easily with this limited-incision method.

Most importantly, the posteriorly based approach significantly reduces the risk of facial nerve injury. The facial nerve (cranial nerve VII) controls the muscles of facial expression, transmits taste sensations from the anterior two-thirds of the tongue, and provides parasympathetic innervation to the lacrimal and salivary glands. Its major branches (temporal, zygomatic, buccal, mandibular, and cervical) supply motor function and sensation across the face. Among these, the temporal branch is especially critical for eyebrow elevation, eye closure, and forehead movement. Injury to this branch can result in paralysis or weakness, producing obvious aesthetic and functional deficits [24]. In the modified approach described here, dissection proceeds posterior to the STA within the SMAS layer, thereby avoiding the temporal branch of the facial nerve, which courses anterior to the STA. Immediate postoperative follow-up showed a facial nerve injury rate of approximately 1%, markedly lower than that associated with the traditional anteriorly based approach. This reduction is clinically meaningful, as preserving temporal branch function—particularly eyebrow elevation—is essential for surgical acceptance, especially in female patients.

TMJ disc reduction is a critical determinant of the clinical prognosis following disc repositioning surgery. Qiu et al. [25] emphasized the importance of complete anterior attachment release and slight disc overcorrection to achieve stable long-term outcomes. Immediate postoperative results of disc repositioning can be reliably evaluated using MRI [12, 26]. Postoperative MRI is recommended to assess the success of disc reduction, as it provides detailed information on disc position, morphology, and potential perforations, as well as condylar parameters including height, shape, cortical continuity, and bone marrow blood supply [27, 28].

In the present study, the posteriorly based approach achieved a total effectiveness rate of 98.2%, with 92.9% of cases rated as excellent, 5.3% as good, and only 1.8% as poor. In comparison, the anteriorly based approach achieved a total effectiveness rate of 95.2%, with 4.8% rated as poor. These findings suggest that both surgical approaches can achieve the intended surgical goals; however, the posteriorly based approach demonstrated slightly higher effectiveness and stability.

TMJ disc repositioning by suturing through an open incision to the auricular cartilage is relatively straightforward to perform and has gained increasing acceptance among oral and maxillofacial surgeons as well as dentists. However, important questions remain regarding how best to preserve the vascular supply around the condyle and minimize surgical trauma. Based on our findings, both the anteriorly based and posteriorly based approaches have unique advantages. We observed that the distance between the external auditory canal and the superficial temporal arteriovenous bundle may serve as a valuable anatomical reference when selecting the surgical approach. Specifically, a shorter distance favors the anteriorly based approach, as this configuration allows better protection of the facial nerve, whereas a greater distance favors the posteriorly based approach, which provides superior preservation of the vascular supply.

Several limitations remain. Although the posteriorly based approach has previously been described for several TMJ procedures, including condylar mass resection and condylar fracture management, its application in TMJ disc repositioning and suturing has not been reported in the literature. He and Yang’s team first described TMJ disc repositioning and suturing in 2018 using an anteriorly based approach relative to the superficial temporal neurovascular bundle [7]. To our knowledge, the present study is the first to apply a posteriorly based approach to the superficial temporal neurovascular bundle for TMJ disc repositioning and suturing in patients with anterior disc displacement without reduction.

Another limitation of the present study is that the sample size was insufficient to support robust statistical analysis of this anatomical parameter. Future studies with larger cohorts, ideally through multicenter collaboration, are needed to validate these findings and refine surgical guidelines for approach selection.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the modified tragus incision combined with the posteriorly based approach offers favorable aesthetic outcomes and a high level of facial nerve safety, making it reliable option for TMJ disc reduction and fixation. When appropriately selected and applied, this combined approach preserves the vascular supply, reduces surgical trauma, and minimizes postoperative complications. These advantages collectively contribute to improved perioperative safety and enhance the overall prognosis of TMJ disc surgery.
