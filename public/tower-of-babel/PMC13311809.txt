Beyond Algorithms: Embodied Artificial Intelligence and the Future of Dentistry
Authors: Mohammed Turky, Paul MH Dummer
Published: 2026

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly becoming a pivotal force in shaping the future landscape of dentistry, with its applications expanding across various domains, including diagnosis, treatment planning, workflow optimisation, and personalised patient care.1, 2, 3 Current discourse surrounding dental AI has predominantly centred on cognitive systems that analyse vast arrays of data, generate predictive insights, and support clinical decision-making processes. However, an area that holds great promise, yet remains relatively underexplored in the dental literature, is a transformative frontier known as embodied artificial intelligence.

Embodied AI refers to intelligent systems that possess the ability to perceive and interact with their surroundings through a physical or simulated presence.4 This is distinctly different from conventional AI systems, which primarily generate computational outputs based on data analysis. Embodied AI merges sensory feedback mechanisms, spatial awareness capabilities, adaptive learning processes, and real-time interactive functionalities. While these concepts have gained traction in broader fields such as healthcare and robotics—where they are being utilised in innovative applications like robotic assistance, autonomous navigation, haptic guidance, and intelligent human-machine collaboration—their potential in dentistry remains largely untapped.4

Recent developments in dentistry already reflect early elements of embodied AI, particularly in guided implant surgery, robotic-assisted implant placement, robotic-driven minimally invasive management of calcified root canals, haptic navigation systems, and AI-assisted operative and endodontic procedures.3,5, 6, 7, 8 However, many of these technologies remain limited to specialised applications, early clinical adoption, or research settings. Importantly, these advances have largely been discussed as isolated technological innovations rather than as part of a broader transition toward embodied intelligence in dentistry. Recognising this conceptual shift may help contextualise future developments in intelligent instrumentation, adaptive procedural systems, and human–machine clinical interaction.

This emerging framework posits that intelligent systems can dynamically engage with clinical environments, utilising sensory feedback, adaptive learning skills, and physical engagement. This distinction is profoundly important given the procedural and tactile nature of dentistry, which heavily relies on accurate hand movements, visual guidance, and nuanced sensory perceptions during diagnosis and treatment. As such, the integration of embodied AI could potentially redefine how dental AI systems operate, shifting them from merely passive decision-support tools to active participants in procedural assistance and adaptive clinical interactions.

Potential transformative applications of embodied AI in dentistry could encompass a wide range of innovations capable of responding dynamically to biological, anatomical, and operator-related variables during treatment. Future technologies may extend beyond robotic assistance alone toward intelligent instruments that continuously interpret tactile resistance, dentine thickness, tissue characteristics, instrument stress, fluid dynamics, or patient movement in real time. Such systems could potentially modify procedural behaviour autonomously or semi-autonomously to improve precision, safety, and tissue preservation. Embodied AI may also contribute to the emergence of context-aware clinical environments in which imaging systems, operative instruments, navigation technologies, and digital patient data interact continuously during treatment. Although many of these applications remain speculative, they illustrate how embodied AI may ultimately transform dentistry from a discipline supported by static digital technologies into one characterised by intelligent, responsive, and interactive clinical systems.

Nevertheless, the rise of embodied AI is not without its challenges, as it brings forth critical ethical, educational, and regulatory considerations. As AI systems evolve from providing recommendations to actively engaging with patients and the broader clinical environment, the implications surrounding accountability, clinician oversight, safety validation, and professional autonomy become paramount. This evolution may necessitate the establishment of new governance frameworks, revised training protocols, and guidelines for the responsible integration of these technologies into clinical practice.

Currently, discussions surrounding AI in dentistry are largely focused on the automation of diagnostics and the development of predictive analytics. In light of this, it is imperative to recognise that embodied AI represents the next conceptual advancement in the evolution of dental AI, transitioning the field from a sole emphasis on computational intelligence to embracing intelligent physical interaction within clinical environments. By exploring this emerging paradigm proactively, the field of dentistry can better prepare for the next generation of AI-integrated patient care, ensuring that the integration of technology enhances not only treatment outcomes but also patient experiences.
