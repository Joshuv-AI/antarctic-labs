Role and mechanisms of moisture regulation in enhancing alfalfa silage quality
Authors: Guolin Yang, Heng Jiang, Haoran Wang, Zhennan He, Zhaoming Wang, Siyi Wang, Yuanyuan Jing, Fengqin Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundManaging moisture content is crucial for optimizing the fermentation quality of alfalfa silage; however, the underlying mechanisms of water spraying following wilting remain poorly understood. This study aimed to elucidate the microbial mechanisms through which this rehydration process enhances fermentation quality by modulating key microorganisms and metabolic pathways.ResultsIn this study, alfalfa was subjected to four moisture treatments: ND50 (wilted to 50% moisture content), ND65 (wilted to 65% moisture content), PS50 (wilted and water-sprayed to 50% moisture content), and PS65 (wilted and water-sprayed to 65% moisture content). Following 90 days of ensiling, changes in nutritional quality, fermentation characteristics, microbial communities, and metabolites were analyzed. Results showed that the PS65 treatment exhibited significantly lower dry matter (DM), water-soluble carbohydrates (WSC), pH, ammonia nitrogen content (NH3-N), amino acids (AA), and butyric acid (BA) than the ND65 group (P < 0.05). Similarly, DM, crude protein (CP), WSC, and pH were significantly lower in the PS50 treatment than in the ND50 group (P < 0.05). Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) exceeded 97% relative abundance across all treatments, with Lactobacillus predominating. The key differential microorganisms among the treatments were primarily Weissella and Exiguobacterium (P < 0.05). Furthermore, yeast counts in the water-sprayed treatments (PS65 and PS50) were significantly lower than those in the wilted treatments (ND65 and ND50) (P < 0.05). Compared with the ND50 group, both up and down-regulated metabolites in the PS50 treatment were predominantly phenolic acids. In contrast, the comparison between the PS65 and ND65 groups revealed 81 significantly up-regulated and 89 down-regulated metabolites, with lipids and flavonoids being the primary components, respectively. Furthermore, multiple metabolic pathways, including ascorbate and aldarate metabolism as well as phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, were significantly enriched in the water-sprayed treatment (PS65). Microbial traceability analysis revealed that the differential metabolites were mainly derived from Lactiplantibacillus, Lentilactobacillus, and Weissella.ConclusionWithin the appropriate moisture range for alfalfa silage, the PS65 treatment not only significantly altered the relative abundances of key bacteria, including Weissella and Exiguobacterium, and fungi, including Coprinellus and Cladosporium(P < 0.05), but also significantly decreased silage pH (P < 0.05) and increased Lactic acid (LA)content (P < 0.05) by modulating metabolic pathways such as phenylpropanoid biosynthesis.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Alfalfa, Silage, Free water, Fermentation quality, Microbial diversity, Metabolomics

Introduction

Ensiling forage crops, such as alfalfa, is a typical solid-state fermentation process crucial for long-term feed preservation. Within this system, moisture content serves as a critical factor that directly influences the water activity available to microorganisms, thereby dictating fermentation pathways and subsequent silage quality. As a vital pillar of the national economy, the livestock industry carries the strategic responsibility of securing the supply of animal-derived foods, including meat, eggs, and milk. Within forage production systems, alfalfa is widely recognized as the “king of forages” owing to its high protein content and exceptional fiber digestibility [1]. Converting alfalfa into silage not only effectively replaces a portion of expensive concentrate feeds, thereby alleviating the “human-livestock grain competition” dilemma, but also substantially reduces feeding costs. Consequently, extensive research has been conducted across various facets of the ensiling process, including mechanical harvesting, automated operations, and silage additives. However, given China’s vast territory and significant regional climatic variations, universal ensiling technologies still encounter localized challenges in practical application, such as inconsistent fermentation quality and substantial dry matter loss. Therefore, optimizing pre-ensiling pretreatment processes is of paramount importance to safeguard the robust development of herbivorous animal husbandry. In recent decades, extensive research has been conducted on moisture control in alfalfa silage. When the moisture content of the raw material is too high, water-soluble carbohydrates become insufficient, which can readily promote the rapid proliferation of harmful microorganisms such as clostridia [2]. High-throughput sequencing has demonstrated that the spoilage process in high-moisture alfalfa silage is closely associated with specific clostridial species. Clostridium sp. BTY5 and Garciella sp. GK3 have been identified as key spoilage bacteria driving butyric acid fermentation [3]. Adjusting the moisture content of the raw material to 50–65% results in a moderate water activity, which not only ensures the activity of lactic acid bacteria but also inhibits some acid-producing bacteria through osmotic pressure. This, in turn, increases lactic acid production, lowers pH, and better preserves nutrients such as protein [4, 5]. Liu et al. pointed out that a moisture content of 58%, although inhibiting harmful bacteria, may affect compaction density and delay the initiation of fermentation due to the high elasticity of the material [6]. In recent years, driven by advances in molecular biology techniques, research has expanded beyond traditional assessments of fermentation quality to elucidate microbial community composition and metabolic functions during silage production. Microbial growth and metabolic activity are highly dependent on the water status of the environment, and water requirements vary considerably among different microbial groups. The higher the water activity, the lower the degree of water binding to the substrate, and vice versa [7]. In alfalfa silage fermentation, the medium in which microorganisms reside is a primary concern. In mature plant cells, over 90% of the water is stored as free water in the central vacuole, surrounded by the vacuolar membrane, with the cell wall rich in cellulose and pectin forming the outer layer. This structural arrangement results in the intercellular and interleaf spaces being filled with gas as the continuous medium, thereby limiting water availability for lactic acid bacteria and hindering nutrient leaching and organic acid diffusion. The root cause of this physical barrier lies in the water state within the silage system. In freshly cut alfalfa, the released water is predominantly free water distributed in the intercellular spaces. Free water can dissolve nutrients and is directly available to microorganisms such as lactic acid bacteria; in contrast, bound water is tightly associated with components such as proteins and cellulose via hydrogen bonds, making it difficult for microorganisms to utilize directly [8]. Adding water prior to ensiling enhances the fermentation activity of lactic acid bacteria, increases lactic acid production, and causes a rapid drop in pH, thereby effectively inhibiting spoilage bacteria and molds [9]. Nora L. et al. found that mycelial growth may be influenced by both bound and free water [10]. Conversely, an excessively high proportion of bound water reduces water activity, limits water utilization by lactic acid bacteria, and impedes nutrient leaching and organic acid diffusion, thus delaying the fermentation process and reducing silage quality. Other studies have shown that different types of lactic acid bacteria bind water during starch degradation, which in turn affects water activity values. Metabolites produced by lactic acid bacteria enhance protein integrity during storage, inhibit the conversion of bound water to free water, and thereby improve moisture retention [11]. Therefore, regulating the ratio of free water to bound water in alfalfa silage production is a key factor in optimizing fermentation quality and enhancing the nutritional value of the feed. To investigate how moisture regulates key metabolic pathways and microbial composition to improve fermentation quality, this study used wilted forage evenly sprayed with sterile water as the treatment group. This study hypothesize that water spraying following wilting differentially modulates the microbial community compared with natural wilting, and activates key metabolic pathways in LAB, thereby yielding superior silage fermentation quality.

Materials and methods

Silage preparation

The plant material used in this study was the alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) cultivar WL343, which was provided by the Grassland Research Institute of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences and cultivated at the Mengniu Helin Base in Hohhot. The forage was harvested at the initial flowering stage on August 5, 2024. After mowing, the alfalfa was subjected to four moisture pretreatment treatments as follows: Natural drying to 65% moisture (ND65), in which alfalfa was naturally sun-dried until the moisture content reached approximately 65%; natural drying to 50% moisture (ND50), in which alfalfa was naturally sun-dried until the moisture content reached approximately 50%; spray to 65% moisture (PS65), in which alfalfa naturally sun-dried to approximately 50% moisture was sprayed with sterile water to increase the moisture content to approximately 65%; and spray to 50% moisture (PS50), in which alfalfa naturally sun-dried to approximately 35% moisture was sprayed with sterile water to increase the moisture content to approximately 50%. After pretreatment, the alfalfa was chopped into 1–2 cm lengths using a guillotine chopper. The silage starter culture used in the experiment consisted of two lactic acid bacteria: Lactobacillus plantarum (1.3 × 1010 CFU/g) and Lactobacillus buchneri (7 × 109 CFU/g), which were mixed at a 1:1 ratio at the time of inoculation. The silage inoculant (0.005 g/kg) and sucrose (2 g/kg) were separately dissolved in 5 mL/kg of sterile water. After thorough mixing, the solutions were evenly sprayed onto each treatment. The treated alfalfa was then packed into polyethylene bags (250 mm × 350 mm), vacuum-sealed, with each bag containing approximately 300 g of material. Each treatment was prepared in triplicate and stored at room temperature for 90 days. Samples were collected after ensiling for subsequent analyses.

The optimal moisture content for semi-dry alfalfa silage typically ranges from 50% to 65%; exceeding this range readily induces Clostridium fermentation, whereas lower moisture levels impede compaction and inhibit LAB activity [12]. To investigate the impact of the water state rather than the total moisture content on silage quality, this study employed a strategy of initial wilting to a lower moisture level followed by rehydration via water spraying, thereby creating raw materials with identical total moisture contents but distinct water states. A moisture content of 50% was selected as the baseline value because it represents the upper threshold for semi-dry silage, a stage at which plant cells remain partially viable and their cellular structures are largely intact. Adjusting the moisture content to 65% via spraying yields a substrate with a high proportion of exogenous free water. Conversely, a moisture level of 35% was selected as the target for severe wilting because, below 40%, plant cells are completely inactivated, cell membranes rupture, and intracellular nutrients are fully liberated [13]; rehydrating this severely wilted material back to 50% thus provides a raw material with nutrients that have been pre-released.

Sample collection and index measurement

After opening the silage bags, a portion of each sample was immediately frozen at − 20 °C in sealed plastic bags for subsequent chemical analyses. For extract preparation, 20 g of silage was mixed with 180 mL of distilled water, allowed to stand at 4 °C for 24 h, and then filtered through four layers of medical gauze. The filtrate was used for the determination of pH, ammonia nitrogen(NH3-N), and organic acids(OA). The pH was measured using an electronic pH meter (LAQUAtwin-pH-22, Horiba, Japan), and NH3-N concentration was determined using the phenol–hypochlorite method [14]. The filtrate (1500 µL) was filtered through a 0.22-µm aqueous filter, and oxalic acid (OA) was determined using a high-performance liquid chromatography system according to the method described in Wang et al.‘s report [15]. For microbial enumeration, 20 g of silage was homogenized with 180 mL of sterile saline solution and serially diluted. The populations of yeasts and molds, lactic acid bacteria, and coliform bacteria were determined using the agar plate method following the procedures described by Yan et al. [16]. The dry matter (DM) content of the remaining silage in each bag was determined after oven-drying at 65 °C for 72 h. Dried samples were then ground and passed through a 1.0 mm sieve for further analysis. Crude protein (CP) content was analyzed using the Kjeldahl method [17]. Neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF) contents were determined according to the method of Van Soest et al. [18]. Water-soluble carbohydrate (WSC) content was determined using the method described by Thomas [19].

Bacterial community composition analysis

Upon completion of silage fermentation, all samples were collected and thoroughly homogenized within each treatment group. Total microbial DNA was extracted using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega, USA). DNA concentration and purity were measured with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, and integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. For bacterial and archaeal community analysis, the V3–V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified with the universal primers 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). For fungal community analysis, the ITS1 region was amplified using the fungus‑specific primers ITS1F (5′-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3′) and ITS2 (5′-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3′). All PCR products were examined on 2% agarose gels, and target bands were excised and purified with the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit, followed by quantification using a Quantus™ Fluorometer (Promega, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared with the TruSeq™ DNA Sample Prep Kit and subjected to paired‑end sequencing on an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, USA).

Using fastp (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp, version 0.20.0) software to quality control of the original sequencing sequence, use FLASH (http://www.cbcb.umd.edu/software/flash, Version 1.2.7) software for Mosaic: use UPARSE software (http://drive5.com/uparse/, version 7.1), according to 97% of the similarity of sequence OTU clustering and eliminate chimeras. Species classification and annotation were performed for each sequence using RDP classifier (http://rdp.cme.msu.edu/, version 2.2), and the Silva 16 S rRNA database (version 138) was compared. The comparison threshold was set at 70%.

Metabolite analysis

Samples were vacuum freeze-dried using a freeze dryer (Scientz-100 F). The dried samples were ground into a fine powder using a mixer mill (MM400, Retsch) at 30 Hz for 1.5 min. Metabolites were extracted with a precooled 70% methanol–water solution containing internal standards at − 20 °C, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 3 min. The resulting supernatant was then used for UPLC–MS/MS analysis. Metabolomic data acquisition was performed using an ultra-performance liquid chromatography system (UPLC; ExionLC™ AD, SCIEX, https://sciex.com.cn/) coupled with a tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) system. UHPLC separation was performed using an Agilent SB-C18 chromatographic column (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm). The mobile phases were: Phase A, ultrapure water with 0.1% formic acid; and Phase B, acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The elution gradient was as follows: at 0.00 min, Phase B was at 5%; from 0.00 to 9.00 min, Phase B linearly increased to 95%, which was maintained for 1 min; from 10.00 to 11.10 min, Phase B decreased back to 5%, which was held until 14 min. The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min, and the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C. The injection volume was 4 µL.

The metabolomics data of these samples were qualitatively analyzed using the MetWare database independently developed by MetWare Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Jiaxing, China). The variable importance of predicted (VIP) ≥ 1.0 and absolute multiple change (FC) ≥ 5.0 was used as the criterion for the selection of differential metabolites. Compounds using KEGG database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/) for identification of the metabolites of comments, and then map the annotation of metabolites to KEGG pathways database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html).

Statistical analysis

The physical and chemical property data of silage were first organized using Microsoft Excel 2016 and then analyzed by analysis of variance (ANOVA) with IBM SPSS Statistics 26. Sample values were expressed as “mean ± standard deviation.” Microbial diversity data were analyzed using QIIME2 and visualized using R 4.4.2 and Python 3.5.

Results

Nutrient and fermentation characteristics

The nutritional and fermentation parameters of the alfalfa silage are presented in Table 1. The CP content in the PS50 treatment was significantly lower than that observed in the other treatments (P < 0.05). Significant differences were also found in WSC concentrations among the groups (P < 0.05), which followed the descending order of ND50 > PS50 > PS65 > ND65. In contrast, no significant differences were detected for NDF, ADF, or Ash content among the treatments (P > 0.05). In addition, Moisture content significantly affected all fermentation parameters of alfalfa silage. Compared with a moisture content of 65%, a moisture content of 50% significantly increased LA concentration, while significantly reducing AA, pH, NH3-N, propionic acid (PA), and BA concentrations (P < 0.05). In addition, the numbers of LAB and yeasts were also reduced at 50% moisture content. Distinct differences also emerged when comparing the water-spraying and wilting group. For both moisture levels, the water-spraying treatments (PS65 and PS50) resulted in significantly higher LA content and, significantly lower pH and yeast counts compared to their wilted counterparts (P < 0.05). In the PS65 treatmentNH3-N, AA, PA, and BA contents were all significantly lower than in the corresponding wilting group (P < 0.05). In contrast, at the lower moisture level of PS50, these parameters (NH3-N AA, and BA) did not differ significantly from the wilting treatment (P < 0.05).

Bacterial and fungal community

Bacterial diversity and community composition

The bacterial and fungal communities of alfalfa silage were characterized by sequencing 16 S rDNA and ITS regions. As shown in Fig. 1(a, b), the Ace and Shannon indices of all treatment groups were not significantly different (P > 0.05). The composition of the bacterial community in alfalfa silage is shown in Fig. 1(c, d). At the phylum level, Firmicutes was the dominant phylum in all treatments, with a relative abundance exceeding 97%. The dominant bacterial genera in each treatment group included Lactobacillus and Pantoea. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on the Bray–Curtis distance matrix is shown in Fig. 1e. The first two principal coordinates explained 93.88% of the total community variation, with PC1 accounting for 84.22% and PC2 accounting for 9.66%. Samples within the same treatment clustered closely, indicating high repeatability among replicates. ND65 and PS65 were clearly separated along PC1, reflecting pronounced differences in bacterial community composition between these treatments. Venn diagrams were used to illustrate the numbers of shared and unique bacterial OUTs among treatments. As shown in Fig. 1f, a total of 147 bacterial OUTs were detected. Compared with ND65, the PS65 treatment contained 26 shared OUTs and 51 unique OUTs. Similarly, compared with ND50, the PS50 treatment shared 55 OUTs, with 64 OUTs being unique to PS50. Significant differences in the relative abundances of Weissella, Exiguobacterium, Enterococcus, Microbacterium, Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus, Salana, Pseudoclavibacter, and Tianweitania were observed among treatments (P < 0.05)(Fig. 1g). Pairwise comparisons at the genus level (Fig. 1h) showed that the relative abundance of Weissella in the PS65 treatment reached 0.37%, which was significantly higher than that in the other treatments (P < 0.05). The relative abundance of Exiguobacterium in the PS50 treatment (0.07%) was significantly lower than that in the ND50 treatment (0.10%) (P < 0.05). In addition, the relative abundances of Microbacterium, Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus, Salana, Pseudoclavibacter, and Tianweitania in the PS50 treatment were significantly higher than those in the other treatments (P < 0.05).

Correlation analysis between microorganisms and silage fermentation quality

Correlation analysis between bacterial genera and physicochemical parameters of silage (Fig. 3a) showed that Lactobacillus was significantly positively correlated with pH (P < 0.05). Both WSC contents were significantly positively correlated with Exiguobacterium, Nocardioides, Paracoccus, Rhodococcus, Delftia, and Sphingomonas. Staphylococcus, Devosia, and Microbacterium were all significantly positively correlated with LA (P < 0.05). Conversely, significant negative correlations were found between AA and Exiguobacterium, Nocardioides, Microbacterium, and Pseudomonas (P < 0.05). Similarly, Exiguobacterium, Nocardioides, Paracoccus, Rhodococcus, Myroides, Delftia, and Sphingomonas were all significantly negatively correlated with BA (P < 0.05). Additionally, Exiguobacterium and Nocardioides were positively correlated with DM but negatively correlated with NH3-N and PA (P < 0.05). Regarding fungal genera Fig. 3b, Fusarium showed significant positive correlations with pH, AA, PA, BA, NH3-N, and Ash, but negative correlations with DM and water-soluble WSC (P < 0.05). In contrast, unclassified fungi exhibited the opposite pattern, being negatively correlated with pH, AA, PA, BA, and NH3-N, while positively correlated with DM and WSC (P < 0.05). Finally, Cutaneotrichosporon was positively correlated with NDF and ADF, while CP was positively correlated with Cladosporium but negatively correlated with unclassified_c__Sordariomycetes (P < 0.05).

Effects of moisture regulation on differential metabolites

As shown in Fig. 4, based on the UPLC–MS/MS platform, a total of 1,023 metabolites were detected in the 12 alfalfa silage samples in this experiment. These metabolites included 238 flavonoids, 157 phenolic acids, 113 lipids, 99 amino acids and their derivatives, 90 terpenoids, 89 alkaloids, 84 organic acids, 34 nucleotides and their derivatives, and 26 lignans and coumarins. These compounds endow alfalfa with a wide range of biological activities. The inclusion of alfalfa silage in the diet has been reported to increase feed intake and digestibility in dairy cows and to improve their production performance and metabolic capacity, which may be associated with the abundance of bioactive compounds in alfalfa. However, no tannin-like compounds were detected in the present study, which may be related to the characteristics of the raw materials used. The principal component analysis (PCA) of metabolites is shown in Fig. 4a. Samples within each treatment group were well clustered, indicating good experimental repeatability. Clear separation was observed among the different treatment groups. The two moisture content groups were distinctly separated from each other. Principal component 1 (PC1) explained 40.53% of the total variance, indicating that moisture content had a significant effect on the metabolite profiles of silage alfalfa. Differences were also observed between the natural drying and water spraying treatment groups. Principal component 2 (PC2) explained 18.36% of the total variance, suggesting that water spraying treatment also influenced silage metabolites, with a greater impact observed in the treatment groups with a moisture content of 65%.

To further investigate metabolic differences, volcano plot analysis was performed based on VIP values and log2 fold change (VIP > 1, |log₂FC| > 1). Results showed that a total of 988 metabolites were detected in the comparison between the ND65 and PS65 treatment groups, with 81 significantly up-regulated and 89 significantly down-regulated (Fig. 4b). Regarding the categorical distribution of these differential metabolites, lipids constituted the most abundant components among the up-regulated metabolites, whereas flavonoids predominated among the down-regulated metabolites. Specifically, the PS65 treatment significantly up-regulated 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid, methyl linolenate, 9-hydroperoxy-10E,12,15Z-octadecatrienoic acid, and LysoPE 16:1, with LysoPE 16:1 exhibiting the greatest increase. In contrast, the PS65 treatment led to a significant decrease in the relative abundances of various flavonoids, primarily involving glycosides (including apigenin-7-O-glucuronide and luteolin-7-O-glucuronide), free aglycones (prunetin, eriodictyol, and wighteone), and tricin-like compounds such as tricin-7-O-(2’’-feruloyl)glucoside and tricin-7-O-(2’’-sinapoyl)glucoside. Among these down-regulated metabolites, wighteone showed the most prominent decline. A total of 1,014 metabolites were detected between the ND50 and PS50 treatment groups, including 32 upregulated and 69 downregulated metabolites (Fig. 4c). In terms of metabolite class, phenolic acids were the most abundant among both the upregulated and downregulated differential metabolites, followed by flavonoids. Specifically, the PS50 treatment significantly upregulated a variety of phenolic acids, including mandelic acid, gallic acid, 1-O-p-coumaroylglycerol, 3-galloylshikimic acid, and 3-O-digalloylquinic acid, among which mandelic acid exhibited the most pronounced upregulation. In contrast, the PS50 treatment significantly downregulated phenolic acids such as cinnamic acid, hydrocinnamic acid, ethyl cinnamate, p-coumaric acid ethyl ester, ethyl caffeate, ethyl ferulate, benzyl glucoside, 1-O-galloyl-rhamnose, and protocatechuic acid-4-O-glucoside; among these, protocatechuic acid-4-O-glucoside* was the most downregulated metabolite. Furthermore, between different spraying treatments, 210 metabolites were upregulated and 73 downregulated (Fig. 4d); when naturally dried samples with 65% and 50% moisture contents were compared, 228 metabolites were upregulated and 100 downregulated (Fig. 4e), and among the differential metabolites, flavonoids were the most upregulated, while terpenoids were the most downregulated.

Discussion

Impact of water spraying on the fer mentation profile and nutritional quality of alfalfa silage

Significant differences in organic acid content were observed among the different moisture treatment groups. pH and NH3-N content are important indicators reflecting the fermentation quality of silage. Lower pH values and NH3-N content levels indicate greater inhibition of harmful microorganisms and reduced crude protein degradation. In the present study, silage with 50% moisture had significantly lower pH and NH3-N content contents than silage with 65% moisture (P < 0.05), which is consistent with the reduced abundance of undesirable microorganisms, such as yeasts, under low-moisture conditions. BA is the main product of Clostridium fermentation, and Clostridium species tend to proliferate in silage with high moisture content. For corn silage, a BA concentration below 1.0 g/kg DM is widely recognized as indicative of high‑quality silage [22], whereas the BA levels in the present study were consistently below 5 g/kg DM. This discrepancy is primarily attributable to the low sugar content and high buffering capacity characteristic of legume silages such as alfalfa, which inherently challenge the complete inhibition of clostridia. In fact, alternative studies and production standards classify BA level below 5.0 g/kg DM as acceptable or good fermentation [23]. Therefore, the BA concentrations observed in this study successfully met the quality requirements for well-fermented alfalfa silage In the present study, the LA content in the water‑spraying treatments (PS65 and PS50) was significantly higher than that in the corresponding wilting treatment groups (P < 0.05), whereas the pH values and yeast counts were significantly lower (P < 0.05). Mechanistically, LA production is intrinsically linked to the utilization of WSC. Sun et al. reported that lactate accumulation correlates with WSC consumption [24]. Consistently, the WSC contents in this study also differed significantly among the treatment groups(P < 0.05), suggesting that the efficiency of WSC utilization serves as a critical driver for the subsequent shifts in pH and LA levels. Furthermore, this metabolic variation may also involve the modulation of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. WGCNA revealed that pH was significantly correlated with the turquoise module (P < 0.05), in which fumaric acid was identified as a core metabolite. Subsequent enrichment analysis demonstrated that these pathways were significantly enriched in the TCA cycle (P < 0.05), directly involving fumaric acid. Consequently, this implies that the TCA cycle may modulate pH dynamics and LA accumulation through the mediation of fumaric acid.

Impact of water spraying on the diversity of the microbial community

In this study, LA levels were significantly higher in the PS65 treatment group (P < 0.05), which may be linked to the rapid proliferation of Weissella species during the early stages of silage fermentation, thereby creating favorable conditions for the growth of LAB and subsequent LA production [25]. Previous studies have reported a significant positive correlation between LA and Weissella abundance [26], which is consistent with the observations in the PS65 group. Huang et al. found that when Weissella abundance was low during ensiling, AA content was relatively high [27]. Correspondingly, in the present study, the ND65 group exhibited a significant decrease in Weissella abundance and a significant increase in AA content compared with the PS65 group (P < 0.05), aligning with their conclusion. The abundance of Exiguobacterium in the PS65 group was significantly lower than that in the other treatment groups, suggesting that this treatment effectively suppresses undesirable microorganisms during ensiling (P < 0.05). A meta-analysis by Ridwan confirmed that Exiguobacterium abundance is associated with poor storage conditions and that its reduction contributes to improved silage stability [28]. Therefore, the concurrent decrease in Exiguobacterium and increase in Weissella observed in this study suggest that water spraying is beneficial for enhancing silage quality. The higher Enterococcus abundance in the water-spraying groups (PS65 and PS50) indicates that water spraying promotes the proliferation of this bacterium. Furthermore, the increased abundance of Enterococcus in the water-spraying groups may be associated with shifts in the community niche resulting from the introduction of exogenous LAB. Under anaerobic conditions, Clostridium can ferment lactic acid, a primary fermentation product of LAB, into butyric acid, acetic acid, carbon dioxide, and hydrogen [29]. Their spores can survive within the gastrointestinal tract of dairy cows, and certain species are capable of producing highly pathogenic toxins that contaminate raw milk, leading to off-flavors and excessive gas formation in cheese [30]. Importantly, no Clostridium was detected in any of the treatment groups in the present study, signifying excellent silage fermentation quality.

At the genus level, the dominant fungal genera observed in this study were consistent with those previously reported for forages such as alfalfa, sweet sorghum, corn, and barley [31]. In the present study, the relative abundance of Cladosporium was significantly lower in the water‑spraying treatments (PS65 and PS50) than in the corresponding wilting treatments (ND65 and ND50) (P < 0.05). Cladosporium is a common spoilage fungus frequently encountered in silage; thus, suppressing its abundance aids in mitigating the risks of feed degradation and deterioration [32]. Conversely, significantly higher relative abundances of Lysurus and Coprinellus were observed in the PS65 and PS50 groups (P < 0.05), respectively. Both fungal genera are generally considered detrimental to the ensiling process. The proliferation of these fungi in their respective treatment groups suggests that localized adverse fermentation may have occurred, although the specific underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation.

Correlations between silage fermentation quality and the microbial community

Previous research has demonstrated that Lactobacillus produces LA by consuming substantial amounts of WSC, thereby lowering the pH [33]. In this study, the abundance of Lactobacillus was positively correlated with LA (P < 0.05); however, this correlation may be influenced by a time lag between microbial growth and metabolite accumulation during early silage fermentation. Furthermore, Staphylococcus and Microbacterium showed significant positive correlations with LA (P < 0.05), whereas Liu et al. reported that a low-pH environment inhibits the growth of Staphylococcus; this discrepancy might be attributed to differences in silage raw materials [34]. Meanwhile, both Exiguobacterium and Nocardioides were significantly positively correlated with WSC (P < 0.05), which is consistent with the findings of Lu et al. [35]. With regard to fungi, Fusarium was significantly negatively correlated with WSC (P < 0.05), suggesting that it can proliferate as WSC is consumed and organic acids accumulate. In contrast, Cutaneotrichosporon was significantly positively correlated with NDF and ADF (P < 0.05), while being significantly negatively correlated with ash (P < 0.05). Consistent with previous reports, the degradation of NDF and ADF has been associated with Pichia and Cutaneotrichosporon [36], implying that these fungi may play an important role in the breakdown of fibrous materials.

Differences in silage metabolites

Effects of moisture regulation on flavonoid and phenolic compounds in alfalfa silage

Extracellular hydrolases secreted by LAB, such as Lactobacillus plantarum, possess the capability to specifically cleave flavonoid glycosidic bonds [37]. In the present study, various flavonoid metabolites underwent marked alterations in the PS65 group. Specifically, the concentrations of glycosides, including apigenin-7-o-glucuronide and luteolin-7-o-glucuronide, as well as free aglycones such as prunetin, eriodictyol, and wighteone, exhibited a significant downward trend. This indicates that appropriate moisture adjustment, by facilitating anaerobic acidification, optimizes the activity of microbial enzymatic systems, thereby accelerating the degradation and biotransformation of flavonoid glycosides [38].Additionally, mechanical damage and residual oxygen during the initial stages of ensiling can trigger lipid oxidation pathways, driving the accumulation of oxylipins such as 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid [39]. In this experiment, the concurrent increase in 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid within the PS65 group suggested that water spraying might have exacerbated tissue disruption in the raw material or prolonged the initial aerobic phase. Furthermore, methyl linolenate is a key ester characterized by floral and fruity aromas; it is synthesized via microbial esterases during fermentation and contributes positively to the enhancement of the flavor profile [40]. The significant up-regulation of this compound in the PS65 group indicated that the acidic environment promoted the synthesis of this aroma-active metabolite.

Compared with natural wilting, mandelic acid was detected in the silage of the PS50 treatment. Mandelic acid possesses antimicrobial activity and can inhibit certain pathogens. Jeon et al. found in their study on foodborne pathogenic bacteria and lactic acid bacteria that in a 2 mg/mL mandelic acid solution containing 1.0 M NaCl, foodborne pathogenic Escherichia coli was inhibited, whereas Lactobacillus spp. were unaffected [41]. The present study observed that the PS50 group significantly promoted the upregulation of gallic acid in silage, which contrasted sharply with the trend of phenolic acid downregulation caused by conventional wilting [42]. This was mainly attributed to the optimized moisture environment altering the microbial community composition, resulting in a decrease in the abundance of undesirable bacteria such as Enterococcus. Gallic acid can effectively inhibit the proliferation of Coprinellus and Lysurus, indicating that water spraying, by driving phenolic acid metabolism toward gallic acid enrichment, achieved metabolic regulation while reducing nitrogen loss. Lactic acid bacterial strains possess the enzymatic capacity to reduce the C2–C3 double bond of cinnamic acid to the corresponding hydrocinnamic acid [43]. In the present study, cinnamic acid and hydrocinnamic acid were synchronously downregulated in the PS50 group, suggesting that their decrease in this treatment was not caused by changes in the abundance of LAB. Previous studies have shown that Weissella is positively correlated with hydrocinnamic acid [44]. In this study, hydrocinnamic acid exhibited the greatest downregulation in the PS50 group, a phenomenon that may be related to changes in the abundance of Weissella. Furthermore, cinnamic acid derivatives exert inhibitory effects on mycotoxin biosynthesis [45], which may be a key reason for the markedly lower abundance of Cladosporium in the PS50 group compared with the other treatments. Studies have found that erucic acid content in milk is positively correlated with dietary erucic acid intake, and erucic acid is partially hydrogenated or isomerized in the rumen and readily taken up by the mammary gland for milk fat synthesis [46]. In the present study, water spraying treatment led to an upregulation of erucic acid regardless of moisture level; however, the erucic acid content in silage remains to be measured, and its impact on silage quality requires further evaluation.

Effects of moisture regulation on metabolic pathways in alfalfa silage

The present study demonstrated that the PS65 treatment significantly modulated the ascorbate and aldarate metabolism pathway. This metabolic profile aligns with the finding that Lactobacillus plantarum can effectively activate this pathway, thereby enhancing silage fermentation quality [47]. The phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway is intimately associated with the production of phenolic acids [48]. Given that microorganisms under ensiling conditions lack the complete capacity for de novo phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, the marked changes in metabolites along this pathway in the PS65 group were primarily attributable to alterations in plant-derived metabolism. In the PS65 group, the rapid fermentation of soluble carbohydrates by lactic acid bacteria induced a sharp acidification of the system. This low-pH environment not only directly suppressed the physiological activity of plant cells but also restricted the activity of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), a key rate-limiting enzyme in the plant phenylpropanoid pathway [49], as evidenced by the pronounced accumulation of upstream lignin monomer precursors, such as p-coumaryl alcohol. Although the decreased synthesis of plant-derived phenolic acids (ferulic acid and sinapic acid) partially attenuated the system’s intrinsic antibacterial and antioxidant capacities, the rapidly established highly acidic environment was sufficient to substitute for these phenolic acids in exerting robust antimicrobial effects. Furthermore, previous studies have reported that metabolites such as sinapic acid and dihydroferulic acid influence lignin biosynthesis within the cell wall [50].

The accumulation of phenylpyruvate typically originates from deamination, a metabolic process that concomitantly releases ammonia. Notably, the NH3-N content in the PS65 group was significantly lower than that in the ND65 group (P < 0.05). This decrease is primarily attributable to the suppression of the phenylalanine metabolic pathway by the water-sprayed treatment, which aligns with the observed down-regulation of key downstream intermediates, namely phenylpyruvate, trans-cinnamate, and phenylpropanoate, under the PS65 treatment. It is speculated that appropriate water spraying optimizes the microenvironmental conditions during the early stages of ensiling to promote rapid LA production, thereby effectively inhibiting the proliferation of undesirable bacteria such as Enterococcus. Deng et al. reported that up-regulating the activities of both the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and phenylalanine metabolic pathways in silage can promote the synthesis of sweet and floral aroma compounds, whereas Enterococcus-dominated fermentation suppresses these pathways, resulting in an inferior flavor profile [51]. The present study found that although the PS65 treatment did not alter the concentration of α-linolenic acid, it exerted a significant influence on the levels of its downstream oxidative derivatives. This was characterized by a pronounced down-regulation of 9-hydroxy-12-oxo-10(E),15(Z)-octadecadienoic acid. Notably, previous research has documented a significant up-regulation of these compounds during tobacco curing, a metabolic process recognized as being regulated by the ADH gene family and highly sensitive to environmental shifts [52]. This suggests that the water sprayed treatment may have redirected the metabolic flux of α-linolenic acid by modifying the silage microenvironment, thereby leading to the differential accumulation of its downstream derivatives. Furthermore, while it has been reported that inoculating silage with Lactiplantibacillus plantarum can significantly enhance α-linolenic acid content [53], no such significant change was observed in this experiment. This discrepancy implies that the specific inoculation regimen employed in the present study did not impact the α-linolenic acid status of the silage. Although previous studies have established that nicotinate metabolism is crucial for maintaining rumen microbial community stability, enhancing host immunity, and improving feed conversion efficiency [54], the present study demonstrated that the PS65 treatment significantly down-regulated nicotinamide, a key intermediate in the nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism pathway, concurrent with a significant up-regulation of fumarate and maleate. Given that LAB possess nicotinamidase activity and are capable of converting nicotinamide to nicotinic acid during fermentation [55], this metabolic alteration suggests that the PS65 treatment may have redirected the metabolic flux of LAB, sacrificing a portion of the vitamin reserve to enhance the accumulation of TCA cycle intermediates. Consequently, the PS65 treatment appears to optimize silage quality primarily through the regulation of fermentative homeostasis.

As shown by the correlation heatmap between modules and treatment groups in the WGCNA analysis, In both the brown and turquoise modules, amino acids and their derivatives constituted the core metabolites [56]. In the present study, most amino acids and their derivatives were more abundant in silage with a moisture content of 50% than in silage with 65% moisture, which may be associated with the activity of plant enzymes and microorganisms. Guo et al. reported that inoculation of Lactobacillus buchneri into alfalfa silage increased the contents of polyols such as arabitol, erythritol, mannitol, and threitol, which differed from the sugar alcohols (maltitol, xylitol, mannitol, and threitol) detected in the present study [57]. This discrepancy may be related to differences in the lactic acid bacterial inoculants used. Biogenic amines produced via amino acid decarboxylation can pose risks to animal and human health, as they may react with nitrites to form carcinogenic nitrosamines [58]. In the present study, no significant differences in CP content were observed among the treatment groups; however, the NH3-N content in the water-sprayed treatment groups was significantly lower than that in the corresponding control groups (P < 0.05). This reduction suggests an alteration in protein degradation pathways, which exerts a positive effect on silage quality to some extent.

Traceability association between key metabolites and microorganisms

Traceability analysis of these metabolites indicated that metabolic changes in phenolic acids were closely correlated with the genus Lactiplantibacillus. The metabolism of phenolic acids by lactic acid bacteria is strain-specific, and phenolic acid decarboxylase, which catalyzes the decarboxylation of caffeic acid and ferulic acid, has been identified in Lactobacillus plantarum [59, 60]. In the present study, within the 50% moisture content treatment, phenolic acids represented the most significantly up- and down-regulated differential metabolites. This may be explained by the fact that water spray treatment altered the microbial community composition, which in turn influenced the activity of key enzymes such as phenolic acid decarboxylase, ultimately resulting in pronounced changes in phenolic acid metabolism. Some Weissella species can utilize α-cyclodextrin, fumaric acid, and glycyl-L-glutamine as carbon sources [61]. In this study, Weissella was the differential microorganism with the most significant change in relative abundance, which was notably increased in the water spray treatment (PS65) [62]. This finding might explain the observed difference in fumaric acid production in this treatment group. Furthermore, Weissella is associated with the generation of arginine, suggesting that the differences in arginine levels among treatments may be attributed to the varying abundance of this genus. In addition, specific lactic acid bacteria, such as Lactococcus lactis subsp. lactis, Enterococcus faecalis, and Lactobacillus plantarum, are also capable of degrading arginine [63]. In plant-associated lactic acid bacteria, this degradation primarily occurs via the arginine deiminase pathway. During this process, arginine is enzymatically degraded into citrulline and ornithine, which releases ammonia and ATP, thereby helping to alleviate environmental acid stress. In this study, arginine biosynthesis was significantly downregulated in the PS50 treatment group (P<0.05), which may be the result of the combined action of Lactobacillus and Weissella species.

Limitations and future research directions

Recent studies have shown that cell wall integrity can significantly affect the fermentation performance of leguminous forages [64–66]. The intact cellular structure provides an initial barrier against microbial access to entrapped nutrients [67]. The purpose of ensiling is to induce orderly cell death under anaerobic conditions, thereby facilitating the release and transformation of nutrients. During the first two days of fermentation, the rapid decrease in water-soluble carbohydrate (WSC) content indicates [68] that lactic acid bacteria primarily utilize soluble sugars from intercellular spaces and the cytosol, along with nutrients released from cell wall disintegration driven by acidification and mechanical compression. Plant cells do not die immediately at the onset of ensiling; they can continue to undergo respiration and enzymatic hydrolysis for up to 12 h. During this period, residual oxygen within the silo is consumed through the aerobic respiration of the plant cells [69]. In this study, based on the pH changes observed in the ND65 group during the early stage of ensiling, we speculated that the cells might be in a “semi-viable state.” Meanwhile, we considered that in the PS65 water-spraying group, the disruption of water balance led to loss of selective membrane permeability, leakage of ions and small organic molecules, and consequently improved substrate accessibility for LAB However, direct methods such as cell viability staining, membrane integrity fluorescence labeling, dynamic measurement of extracellular conductivity, or ultrastructural observation were not employed to verify cell survival status and membrane permeability changes during the early ensiling period (0–24 h). Therefore, the inferences regarding the “semi-viable state” and “cell leakage” were only indirect speculations based on physicochemical indicators. To address this limitation, future research will delve into the cellular level and intends to apply the above-mentioned direct methods to observe the damage status of cells at the initial stage of water spraying, aiming to gain a deeper understanding of the mechanism by which water regulation affects alfalfa ensiling fermentation.

It is well-established that bacteria generally require higher water activity for growth compared to yeasts, while molds have the lowest requirement [70]. Therefore, materials with the same total moisture content can differ in their ability to support microbial growth, depending on the ratio of free to bound water, which defines their water activity [71]. In this study, significant differences in bacterial and fungal microbial diversity were observed between the water-spraying treatment group and the wilting treatment group (P<0.05), and the wilting treatment group, and speculated that these differences were attributable to differences in water activity. The water-spraying treatment increased the free water content in the silage system, thereby creating a water activity environment that was more favorable for the growth of LAB while inhibiting fungal proliferation. Although the water-spraying treatment significantly reduced the ammonia NH3-N and PA content under the 65% moisture condition (P<0.05), suggesting that the metabolic activity of butyric acid-producing clostridia was suppressed, water activity was not directly measured in each treatment group in this study. Therefore, the inference that “the changes in microbial diversity are caused by differences in water activity” currently lacks direct evidence. To address this limitation, future research will directly measure the dynamic changes in water activity during the water-spraying treatment and combine these measurements with absolute quantitative analyses of key microbial groups, in order to more rigorously elucidate the microecological mechanisms by which water regulation affects alfalfa ensiling fermentation.

Conclusion

The water-sprayed treatment modulated the relative abundances of key bacterial genera, including Weissella and Exiguobacterium, as well as specific fungal taxa, including Coprinellus and Cladosporium, by altering the moisture state. At the metabolomic level, the PS50 group exhibited a predominance of phenolic acids among the differential metabolites compared with the ND50 group. In contrast, relative to the ND65 group, up-regulated metabolites in the PS65 group were primarily lipids, whereas down-regulated metabolites were mainly flavonoids. Further analysis indicated that the PS65 treatment significantly influenced key metabolic pathways, including phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and phenylalanine metabolism, leading to a significant reduction in pH (P < 0.05) and a concurrent increase in LA content (P < 0.05). Overall, silage quality was superior in the treatment where alfalfa was wilted to 50% moisture content and subsequently sprayed to 65%, highlighting the potential of this moisture regulation strategy to optimize the fermentation quality of alfalfa silage.
