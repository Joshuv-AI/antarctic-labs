EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The Biogas Utilization in Refuse Power Plants (BURP2) project evaluated the techno-economic
and life-cycle feasibility of co-firing biogas with waste coal while integrating carbon capture and
sequestration. The goal was to convert waste-coal piles from environmental liabilities into energy
assets capable of delivering net-negative emissions. The study examined biogas transport logistics,
potential efficiency gains from hot windbox repowering - a configuration in which gas-turbine
exhaust was directed into the boiler windbox to displace a portion of waste-coal combustion while
maintaining power output - and environmental impacts across global warming, acidification, and
eutrophication. The analysis considered two deployment pathways: a retrofit at the Grant Town
plant in West Virginia and a greenfield facility in Kentucky designed to integrate hot windbox
repowering from the outset, with geospatial mapping used to identify optimal siting near landfills,
anaerobic digesters, coal mines, and existing pipelines. For carbon capture and storage evaluation,
an amine solvent system served as the baseline due to its commercial maturity. The study also
examined solid sorbent capture systems, focusing on operational differences, fast response, low
thermal inertia, and simplified integration with gas engines or turbines, rather than a one-to-one
cost comparison.
The technoeconomic assessment showed that net-negative electricity generation was technically
achievable in the retrofit configurations. Retrofit levelized cost of electricity values ranged from
$60/MWh to $117/MWh (2024 dollars), with the lower end associated with low capture rates
(~30% CO2 capture) and the upper end corresponding to designs achieving up to 90% CO2 capture
and net-negative global warming potential. These levelized cost values represent the cost of
electricity without any policy incentives applied. Without incentives, these high-capture options
required substantial increases in electricity prices. For example, if all retrofit costs had been passed
directly to consumers, the breakeven price would have been ~$230/MWh, more than double the
2024 West Virginia retail average of ~$109/MWh. The 45Q tax credit ($85 per metric ton CO2)
materially improved feasibility: when applied, the derated high-capture retrofit could be operated
with no material change to regional retail price (i.e., effectively holding rates near the ~$109/MWh
reference) rather than the large increase observed without incentives. This indicated that, under
current policy and study assumptions, the configuration could be cost-neutral to consumers, though
it did not imply additional profit for the plant; implementation would still have depended on
regulatory approval and commercial arrangements.
Environmental outcomes for both retrofit and greenfield configurations depended strongly on the
selected biogas feedstock. In retrofit applications at existing facilities, co-firing biogas derived
from animal manure achieved the most significant net-negative global warming potential, ranging
from -191 to -498 kg CO2e/MWh. Biogas derived from municipal solid waste biogas similarly
demonstrated high reductions between -82 and -351 kg CO2e/MWh. Mixed biogas configurations
yielded smaller net-negative results (down to -130 kg CO2e/MWh), while wastewater sludge-
derived biogas produced only a marginal net-negative benefit of -13 kg CO2e/MWh. These
findings confirmed that while high capture rates were necessary, the magnitude of the benefit was
primarily determined by the feedstock’s avoided methane credit.
The greenfield assessment revealed more challenging conditions for achieving net-negative targets
compared to the retrofits. A 100 MWe greenfield facility co-firing 35 wt% animal manure-based
biogas with 95% carbon capture reached a net global warming potential of -634 kg CO2e/MWh.
However, when tri-combustion (blending waste coal, natural gas, and biogas) was utilized to

                                                                                              Page | iii

stabilize fuel supply in regions with scarce biogas resources, the global warming potential
outcomes were higher: Animal manure-based tri-combustion reached near-neutral levels at -22 kg
CO2e/MWh, while municipal solid waste- and wastewater sludge-based biogas sources resulted in
positive values of 38 and 55 kg CO2e/MWh, respectively. Without any biogas co-firing, a natural
gas and waste coal greenfield facility maintained a positive global warming potential of 178 kg
CO2e/MWh, even with 90% carbon capture.
Economically, retrofits offered a more viable immediate pathway than small-scale greenfield
developments due to existing infrastructure. A 100 MWe Kentucky greenfield facility would have
required approximately $370 million in new pipeline investment alone, driving the levelized cost
of electricity far above the regional benchmark of $125/MWh. While tri-combustion was identified
as a strategy to improve fuel flexibility, the research found that greenfield feasibility relied heavily
on economies of scale; 650 MWe facilities reduced capital costs per MW by approximately 50%
compared to 100 MWe plants. Ultimately, siting remained the decisive factor, as new facilities
needed to be located near dense biogas production clusters to avoid prohibitive pipeline costs.

1. Introduction
The primary motivation for this work was the development of the “Biogas Utilization in Refuse
Power Plants” (BURP2) concept, a strategic approach designed to achieve net‑negative carbon
emissions by co‑firing renewable biogas with waste coal in conjunction with integrated carbon
capture. This research was undertaken to address several critical challenges in the then‑current
energy and environmental landscape:
 1.1. Extending the Viability of Waste Coal Remediation
One of the most significant hurdles in waste coal reclamation was that individual waste coal piles
were finite resources. This limited lifespan often made it difficult to justify the heavy, long‑term
capital investments required for advanced environmental controls. By integrating biogas, the
facility could eventually transition to a purely renewable operation, effectively extending the
investment payback period for carbon capture and storage (CCS) equipment indefinitely.
 1.2. Preventing the Closure of Small‑Scale Facilities
Existing waste coal power plants were typically small (80-100 MWe) and lacked sophisticated
pollution control equipment. Under potential future mandates for CO2 capture, these facilities
would likely have been forced to shut down due to prohibitive costs; however, the BURP2 concept
provided the economic incentives and technological framework necessary for these plants to install
CCS while maintaining their rated capacities.
 1.3. Optimizing Environmental Trade‑offs
While other forms of biomass co‑firing (such as solid wood) could reach net‑zero targets, they
often led to increases in other environmental impacts like acidification, eutrophication, and ozone
depletion due to the energy required for processing and drying. Anaerobic digestion pathways for
biogas production can introduce their own burdens (e.g., fugitive methane, ammonia and nutrient
releases from digestate management), and the relative ranking of options is highly sensitive to
feedstock properties, transport distances, combustion conditions, and system-boundary choices
(including avoided-emission credits). These uncertainties make it worthwhile to investigate
whether the use of different biogas types could provide environmental trade-offs when used in the
BURP2 concept.
 1.4. Leveraging Existing Infrastructure
The research was driven by the potential to utilize existing biomass‑handling infrastructure, such
as landfills and wastewater treatment plants, to produce fuel. This eliminated the need for
expensive road‑based biomass transportation and allowed for the potential re‑purposing of natural
gas pipelines to deliver biogas to the facility.
Ultimately, this work was performed to quantify the bounding conditions, both economic and
environmental, under which integrating biomass with waste coal remediation could meet
ambitious climate goals while remaining a profitable and flexible part of the power grid.
The following sections describe the work completed to develop a comprehensive techno-economic
assessment (TEA) and life cycle assessment (LCA) for the BURP2 concept. This effort focused on
evaluating the feasibility of co-firing renewable biogas and waste coal alongside a 90-95% CO2
capture facility to achieve net-negative carbon emissions.

                                                                                          Page | 13

The subsequent discussions detail the results of the primary technical activities, including the
investigation of biogas pipeline infrastructure using Geographic Information Systems (GIS), the
development of the various TEA models, and the execution of cradle-to-grave LCA studies for
both retrofit and greenfield applications. While the technical results and environmental impacts
are discussed, tasks related to project management and planning were excluded from these
discussions.
2. Technical Project Summary
 2.1. GIS-Enabled Reasoning Behind the Analytical Approach
To establish a realistic foundation for the technical analysis, a geospatial understanding of
renewable-fuel availability, transportation infrastructure, and access to CO2 storage across the
study regions was established. Because the feasibility of biogas co-firing and carbon capture is
strongly constrained by location-specific resource availability and pipeline connectivity, a GIS-
based approach was adopted to ensure that all subsequent case development reflected physical,
spatial, and logistical realities.
The study investigated Grant Town, WV, as a retrofit scenario because of the existing Grant Town
Power Plant, which provided a realistic technical framework for mirroring an actual 80 MWe
configuration consisting of two circulating fluidized bed boilers. The research was expanded to
include a greenfield assessment in Harlan County, Kentucky, to evaluate the utilization of a low-
quality waste coal resource (estimated at roughly eight million tons) that was not currently being
used. To ensure the feasibility of these scenarios was grounded in location-specific logistical
realities, the team constructed a GIS model in ArcGIS Pro. This model incorporated mappable

 2.2. Integrated Process Configuration for BURP2 Simulations
To establish a realistic technical framework for the simulations, the process layout was designed
to be as similar as possible to the Grant Town power plant. The simulation mirrored the plant’s
actual configuration of 80 MWe capacity consisting of two 40 MWe Circulating Fluidized Bed
(CFB) boilers.13
The simulated retrofit included the construction of an upstream biogas turbine that had its hot flue
gas injected into the CFB’s secondary gas inlet in a process called hot windbox repowering. Figure
5 illustrates the major process flows associated with the BURP2 hot windbox configuration. For
the repowering portion, a Siemens SGT-70014 industrial gas turbine was selected because it offered
simple-cycle output in the ~32-33 MW range and was designed for operation on natural gas, liquid
fuels, dual-fuel, and other gases within specification, which aligned with the project’s need to fire
biogas and deliver a high-temperature exhaust stream for windbox injection.
The downstream retrofit consisted of two process areas. The first process area included an amine
absorption and stripping configuration to separate the carbon dioxide from the boiler’s flue gas.
The second process area consisted of an absorption and stripping configuration to remove excess
water from the carbon dioxide that was sequestered.

 Figure 5. Block flow diagram of proposed retrofitted power plant.

In this setup, biogas was first routed to a biogas turbine unit, where it was combusted to generate
power and produce a hot exhaust stream. The turbine exhaust was then directed to the steam power
plant and injected into the CFB boiler’s secondary air inlet as part of the hot windbox repowering
configuration.

                                                                                           Page | 17

Alongside the turbine exhaust, the power plant continued to receive its standard solid inputs of
coal and limestone, while producing CFB ash. The combined flue gas from the boiler was sent
downstream to the CCS unit. Within the CCS, an amine-based solvent contacted the flue gas to
remove CO2, while tri-ethylene-glycol (TEG) was used for water removal in the conditioning step.
The treated CO2 stream exited the system for sequestration, whereas the remaining CO2-lean flue
gas was vented through the clean gas outlet. Wastewater from the solvent and dehydration
processes was discharged as shown. The block-flow representation was intended to capture only
the primary material movements between the biogas turbine retrofit, the existing steam power
plant, and the CCS system without detailing internal equipment or secondary subsystems.
This integration mentioned above was central to the BURP2 approach for two reasons:
 i) Thermal efficiency - The added hot gas stream reduced the required coal firing rate while
 contributing additional useful energy to the steam cycle.
 ii) Maintaining capacity - To prevent the carbon capture system from reducing the plant’s net
 power output, the electricity generated by the biogas turbine would offset the carbon
 capture system’s power demand.
This layout ensured that the transition from the GIS-informed resource analysis to the technical
process simulation remained grounded in the physical realities of the Grant Town facility’s original
design and capacity.
To evaluate the technical and economic performance of the retrofit and greenfield systems, the
complete process model was executed using Aspen Plus software. Although the simulations did
not include complicated chemical kinetics, the accuracy of the processes was adequate to inform
process development and implementation using comparisons with literature for similar power
production processes. The simulation tool was subsequently used to develop different cases based
on biogas availability and varying degrees of carbon capture for both the retrofit and greenfield
scenarios. For the greenfield scenarios, a similar layout to the retrofit was assumed, but the costing
for the entire plant was included rather than only the modification costs considered in the retrofit
scenario. Outputs from the simulations were imported into the Aspen Process Economic Analyzer
(APEA), which determined optimized equipment sizing, capital costs, and operating requirements
based on the evolving process configuration. This TEA work is discussed in the following sections.
 2.3. Retrofit vs. Greenfield Co-firing TEAs
For the retrofit configurations, the analysis focused on incremental capital additions to the existing
Grant Town plant, primarily the biogas-fired turbine (SGT-700) and the amine-based CO2 capture
and conditioning system, along with the auxiliary modifications for heat integration and flue-gas
routing. To ensure consistent and comparable results, the costing followed NETL’s Quality
Guidelines for Energy System Studies: Cost Estimation Methodology for NETL Assessments of
Power Plant Performance (2021)15 as the standard framework for developing capital investments,
electricity prices, and related metrics. All costs were normalized to a 2024 cost basis to maintain
consistency across equipment estimates, escalated vendor quotes, and referenced cost models. All
process models assumed an 85% capacity factor.
For the greenfield configurations, the same NETL cost-estimation method was applied; however,
the scope included full plant construction rather than only modification and integration costs. This
encompassed the complete power-island and balance-of-plant requirements sized to the greenfield

                                                                                            Page | 18

designs, while preserving methodological consistency with the retrofit assessments for direct
comparison.
The NETL method calculates capital investments and electricity prices that can be used as common
metrics to compare alternative projects. The principal figure of merit was the Total As‑Spent Cost
(TASC), which includes Bare Erected Cost (BEC), Engineering, Procurement, and Construction
Costs (EPCC), Total Plant Cost (TPC), Total Overnight Cost (TOC), and interest during
construction. While no single value determines advantage in isolation, these standardized figures
enable like‑for‑like comparisons against other processes and industry benchmarks.
The capital costs of equipment were determined with a combination of calculated values, quotes,
and Aspen Process Economic Analyzer (APEA). Most of the power plant’s capital costs were
obtained utilizing the APEA of the completed simulations for each configuration which gave sizing
and costs for the associated operational demands. Although this methodology was accurate and
efficient, the turbine, CFB, and Steam Generator’s specifications were outside the bounds
considered by APEA and thus this method was not utilized for these major units so that more
accurate values could be obtained. The cost of this turbine was quoted as a Siemens Energy product
in Gas Turbine World16 and used in the techno-economic analysis. Pricing for the circulating
fluidized beds were extrapolated from the NETL’s Cost and Performance Baseline for Fossil
Energy Plants - Volume 3b: Low Rank Coal to Electricity: Combustion Cases (2011)17, which had
a similar design utilizing low grade coal. Similarly, the prices for the greenfield steam generators
were extrapolated from the NETL’s Technoeconomic and Life Cycle Analysis for Bio-Energy
with Carbon Capture and Storage (BECCS) Baseline Report (2021)18 to give accurate cost
estimates for these pieces of equipment.
In addition to the Aspen Plus simulations providing capital cost estimates, they also provided
operational quantities such as flow rates and utility costs. These values were then used alongside
quoted prices for chemicals and fuels to comprise the operational expenditure (OPEX) which is
the expected operational costs need to maintain production annually.

 2.3.1. Retrofit Case Designs
To evaluate the performance of the proposed retrofit technology, a series of process configuration
cases were developed. Across these cases, the key design variable was the amount of biogas
available to the plant, which directly determined the power that could be generated from the biogas
turbine, and, in turn, the amount of CO2 capture that could be supported without derating the plant.
Regional resource assessments indicated that approximately 21,000 kg/hr of biogas was available
within the West Virginia region for potential utilization.19, 20, 21

Using this value as the upper constraint, a set of cases was created to examine how increasing
biogas supply influenced the power available to operate the CCS system and the resulting CO2
capture rate. Because the CCS process was powered solely by the biogas turbine, the CO2 capture
capacity of each case was dictated by the biogas flow rate and its corresponding power output.
These case configurations were summarized in Table 1.

The objective of the first group of cases (Cases 1-5) was to establish a clear correlation between
biogas availability, CCS power demand, and CO2 capture percentage while maintaining a net plant
output of 80 MWe. Within this group, Case 1 served as the baseline scenario, representing the

                                                                                          Page | 19

operation of the existing plant without biogas co-firing, without CCS installation, and with no
change to fuel consumption or flue-gas handling. This baseline case provided the reference point
against which all retrofit performance improvements were evaluated. To avoid derating the
existing steam cycle, each case was constrained by the available biogas supply - thus, CO2 capture
could only increase as biogas availability increased.

A second set of cases (Cases 6-8) was developed to represent the conditions of current
infrastructure, where only a fraction of the regional biogas potential was realistically accessible.
Under this constraint, the CCS system was sized for a 30% capture rate, and that CCS size was
held constant even when additional biogas became available. In these cases, any surplus biogas
power beyond what the CCS required was used to displace steam‑turbine power, allowing the plant
to combust less waste coal while still maintaining the 80 MWe output.

Finally, Case 9 explored a scenario in which the plant was willing to operate at a slightly reduced
output to enable a much larger CCS system capable of achieving 90% CO2 capture, assuming the
same biogas availability as Case 8. This case demonstrated the upper limit of capture performance
achievable under the retrofit concept and illustrated how increased biogas utilization could support
near‑complete CO2 capture if future infrastructure or policy allowed for modest reductions in plant
output.

 2.3.2. Greenfield Case Designs
The primary focus of the greenfield assessment was to determine how a low-quality coal resource
in Harlan County, Kentucky, estimated at roughly eight million tons, could be used in a new plant
configuration in combination with biogas. A CFB boiler was selected for its fuel flexibility and
ability to burn low-heating-value fuels while maintaining stable combustion, and the configuration
was further optimized with hot windbox repowering to supply hot turbine exhaust to the CFB.
Co-firing pathways were evaluated with three gaseous options: biogas (landfill or anaerobic
digestion), natural gas, and a tri-combustion blend (methane-enriched biogas). Although the goal
was net-zero or net-negative operation using green fuels, natural gas was included to meet
requirements where biogas was scarce. Designs targeted 95% carbon capture, and the CCS process
area was consistent with the retrofit configuration.
Two plant scales were assessed: 100 MWe, sized to remediate the eight-million-ton pile over ~25
years, and 650 MWe, used to study economies of scale and provide benchmarks against other
biofuel co-firing technologies. For the 100 MWe plants, the required coal feed rate was roughly
42,000 kg/hr, sufficient to consume the coal within the plant’s expected operating life while
allowing time to transition toward more favorable fuels. Regional biogas scarcity and high
dedicated-pipeline costs led to evaluating natural gas as a co-firing option, as well as a
tri-combustion approach that blends natural gas with biogas to create methane-enriched biogas for
the turbine. The study therefore examined two fuel strategies: biogas co-firing and natural-gas

co-firing. The summary of cases is provided in Table 2, followed by a brief justification for each
configuration. All configurations were simulated in Aspen Plus.

 2.3.2.2. Comparative CO2 Capture Technology Assessment
To conclude the TEA section, the following paragraphs highlight the assessment of an alternative
capture technology considered in the analysis. The research included an evaluation of a patented
solid-sorbent CO2 capture technology to determine its effectiveness compared to traditional amine-
based systems.
The solid sorbent technology was identified as highly cost-competitive with, and in low-capacity
factor operation, favorable to conventional amine-based systems by offering operational
advantages under the more dynamic conditions driven by increasing variable renewable energy
(VRE) penetration.
When considering biogas cofiring, the variability in biogas production adds operational challenges
that create favorable conditions for the patented technology to handle fluctuating resources. This
is a definitive capability that amine-based systems generally cannot support. The technology
features an ultra-reactive sorbent and efficient gas-solid contacting, enabling fast start-up and
shutdown, and responsive transients for flexible load following, a stark contrast to solvent-based
systems.
Conventional amine systems are optimized for stable baseload operation and tend to face
performance or efficiency challenges when operated in turndown or ramping modes. By contrast,
the patented technology, based on a low-temperature hybrid sorbent system, is engineered for
flexibility. It allows capture rates to be regulated without significant energy or efficiency penalties.
For example, based on estimates for deployment in natural-gas combined-cycle (NGCC) power
plants, the patented capture system was shown to enable integration with power systems that

                                                                                                                Page | 35

cycled in response to VRE availability, a capability that conventional amine-based capture systems
were generally not designed to support.
This flexibility extends beyond just NGCC applications. In biogas repowering scenarios where a
consistent gas supply may be difficult to guarantee, the ability to ramp CO2 capture up or down
offers operational resilience. Similarly, for refuse-derived biogas projects that may be affected by
VRE-driven load fluctuations, the patented technology would provide a level of adaptability that
conventional capture technologies do not.
Lastly, the patented technology offers several technical advantages, including high capacity, fast
reactivity, low regeneration energy, and a low-cost, non-toxic, O2-tolerant sorbent. It is analogous
to proven SOx capture technology used in flue-gas desulfurization, adding to its practical
credibility. The flue gas is cooled indirectly to 60-80°C, after which the sorbent is dispersed in flue
gas ducts and captured in a baghouse. Induced-draft fans evacuate cleaned flue gas to the stack,
while the sorbent is conveyed to storage systems, decoupling adsorption and regeneration. Stored
sorbent is regenerated using either steam or waste heat, and the recovered CO2 is cleaned,
compressed, and sent to a pipeline. This configuration enables adsorber-regenerator decoupling,
which supports flexible operation.
An additional strength of the system is its ability to temporarily store CO2 within the system,
enabling regeneration during periods of low electricity prices or high renewable availability. In
practice, this could involve capturing CO2 during evening peaks and regenerating sorbents later at
night or midday to align energy use with grid dynamics and minimizing cost.

 2.4. Retrofit and Greenfield LCAs
The objective of the LCAs was to determine how combinations of biogas sourcing, co-firing levels,
and CCS influenced life-cycle greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and other environmental impacts
associated with electricity production.
To meet this objective, two studies were conducted that examined both retrofit and greenfield
configurations under consistent functional units, system boundaries, and impact-assessment
methods. The analysis integrated multiple biogas pathways, including landfill gas (LFG),
anaerobic digestion (AD) of animal manure (AM), municipal solid waste (MSW), and wastewater
sludge (WWS), along with two mixed-biogas availability scenarios representing operational
supply and potential future supply. These biogas pathways were combined with waste-coal feed
and varying CCS capture fractions to evaluate a range of operating modes.
Under the methodologies, the case descriptions defined the retrofit cases by biogas share and
capture rate, and the greenfield cases by biogas share, natural gas share, and capture rate. These
cases established the basis for comparing configurations across fuel supply and CCS settings.
The goals of the study were to:
 1. Quantify life-cycle GHG emissions for each configuration and identify conditions under
 which net-negative emissions could be achieved.
 2. Compare retrofit and greenfield designs and assess how design flexibility, fuel mixes, and
 CCS sizing affected environmental performance.

                                                                                             Page | 36

 3. Evaluate non-GHG environmental tradeoffs, including acidification, eutrophication, ozone
 depletion, particulate matter formation, and smog formation, under different biogas
 sourcing strategies.
 4. Identify the life-cycle stages that dominated impacts, including combustion, CCS, avoided
 emissions from waste management, and fuel-transport requirements.
The subsequent sections describe the methods used in the analysis, including the power-plant
models, fuel pathways, case descriptions, and impact-assessment steps, followed by the results and
the discussion of comparative findings.
       2.4.1. Methods
The LCAs for this study were conducted using the SimaPro PhD 10.1.0.6 software and the
ecoinvent database which provided background data resources for the power plant process models.
The LCA followed the principles set out in ISO-14040-14044. The key components of this
assessment include goal and scope definition, LCI analysis, life cycle impact assessment (LCIA),
and interpretation.

The global warming potential (GWP) impact category was assessed using the IPCC 2013 GWP
100a model in SimaPro PhD 10.1.0.6 software. The life cycle impact assessment was calculated
for the power plant model processes, including the avoided methane emissions from anaerobic
digestion and landfilling of wastes. The US EPA’s Tool for the Reduction and Assessment of
Chemical and Other Environmental Impacts (TRACI) 2.1 method was used to assess various
environmental impact categories for the four different sourced biogas co-firing retrofit scenarios.
TRACI provided characterization factors for LCA and measures the midpoint indicators that
describes impacts at some point between the inventory and eventual degradation of the
environment. The impact indicators calculated with the TRACI 2.1 method in SimaPro included
acidification potential, eutrophication potential, ozone depletion potential, particulate matter
formation potential and photochemical smog formation potential.

The scope spanned cradle-to-grave processes for fuels and materials and included avoided burdens
from methane avoidance in waste management and mineral fertilizer offsets from digestate use.
The functional unit for all scenarios was 1 MWh of net electricity delivered to the grid.

Across these results, the AM- and MSW-based biogas cases achieved the lowest GWP due to two
dominant drivers: the avoided emissions from diverting methane-generating wastes into controlled
anaerobic digestion, and the high capture rate (95%) applied to predominantly biogenic CO2. These
two forces together pushed several scenarios into low or net-negative carbon intensity, with Case 2
reaching -634 kg CO2e/MWh, representing a reduction of more than 1400 kg CO2e/MWh relative
to the baseline. Tri-combustion configurations that included NG provided an additional operational
benefit, maintaining low GWP while stabilizing fuel supply in cases where biogas availability was
limited.
However, the non-climate indicators revealed clear trade-offs. Acidification and smog formation
generally increased with higher biogas use, particularly for MSW due to emissions of SOx, NOx,
and VOCs from both upstream processes and combustion. Eutrophication demonstrated an
opposite trend, improving with higher biogas fractions and showing the strongest reductions for
WWS as nutrient discharges declined in the AD pathway. In most cases, tri-combustion scenarios
with NG reduced particulate matter formation and ozone depletion relative to dual-combustion
biogas cases, a trend consistent with NG’s cleaner combustion characteristics. These findings
aligned with trends observed in the retrofit system but highlighted an important distinction: in the
greenfield context, NG co-firing reduced several non-climate impacts while preserving credible
GWP reductions.
Overall, scenario selection depends strongly on the performance criteria. If minimizing GWP is
the primary objective, the AM-based and MSW-based biogas configurations with high CO2
capture deliver the most favorable outcomes. If minimizing acidification or particulate matter is
more important, NG-supplemented tri-combustion systems strike a better balance by reducing
these indicators while maintaining low GWP. In practice, the relative attractiveness of AM, MSW,
WWS, LFG, or NG blends depends on regional waste availability, transport distances, and
achievable capture rates, each of which reshapes the life-cycle contributions of transportation,
combustion, CCS credits, and avoided emissions.

3. Conclusions and Recommendations
The research demonstrated that the BURP2 framework is a technically viable strategy for achieving
net-negative carbon emissions by co-firing renewable biogas and waste coal. A key technical
success of the project was the implementation of hot windbox repowering, which directed gas-
turbine exhaust into the boiler windbox to displace a portion of waste-coal combustion while
maintaining net power output. However, siting remained the decisive factor for project success;
Geographic Information Systems mapping proved that facilities had to be located near dense
biogas production clusters to avoid prohibitive expensive pipeline costs, such as the $370 million
investment required for the Kentucky greenfield site.
Environmental performance was primarily dictated by feedstock selection rather than just capture
technology. While all configurations reduced carbon intensity, the magnitude of the net-negative
benefit was determined by the avoided methane credit of the fuel. Specifically, animal manure-
and municipal solid waste-derived biogases achieved the most significant net-negative results in
the retrofit configuration, reaching global warming potentials as low as -498 kg CO2e/MWh. While

                                                                                          Page | 53

these retrofits demonstrated a clear path to carbon negativity, the greenfield assessment revealed
more challenging conditions for achieving similar targets.
Specifically, a 100 MWe greenfield facility co-firing 35 wt% animal manure-based biogas with
95% carbon capture was able to reach a net global warming potential of -634 kg CO2e/MWh.
However, when tri-combustion, a blend of waste coal, natural gas, and biogas, was utilized to
stabilize the fuel supply in regions with scarce biogas resources, the outcomes were less favorable.
Under these tri-combustion conditions, animal manure-based setups reached near-neutral levels of
-22 kg CO2e/MWh, while municipal solid waste and wastewater sludge sources resulted in positive
values.
Beyond environmental performance, the greenfield path was further complicated by logistical and
economic burdens. The research found that a 100 MWe greenfield facility in Kentucky would have
required an estimated $370 million in new pipeline investment alone, driving the levelized cost of
electricity far above regional benchmarks. Consequently, while the greenfield configurations could
achieve deep net-negative emissions, their feasibility relied more heavily on economies of scale
and proximity to dense biogas production clusters to avoid prohibitive infrastructure costs.
Economically, the commercial viability of BURP2 would be contingent on federal policy,
specifically the IRA Section 45Q tax credit. Without such incentives, high-capture retrofits could
have increased electricity prices to approximately $230/MWh, which was more than double the
2024 West Virginia retail average. When the $85 per metric ton CO2 credit was applied,
configurations achieving 90% capture became cost-neutral for consumers and competitive with
non-capture facilities. Retrofits were more immediately viable than greenfield developments due
to existing infrastructure, as greenfield feasibility relied heavily on economies of scale, with 650
MWe facilities reducing capital costs per MW by approximately 50%.
The research also evaluated transitioning from traditional amine systems to solid-sorbent capture
technology, which the sources identified as a highly cost-competitive and flexible alternative.
Solid sorbents offered lower thermal inertia and faster response times, which allowed the plant to
handle fluctuating biogas supplies and load variations better than solvent-based systems.
Ultimately, it is recommended that developers prioritize retrofits with capture rates above 90%
and utilized Geographic Information Systems-based assessments to select feedstocks that align
with both carbon goals and local ecological priorities.
