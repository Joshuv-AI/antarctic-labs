Management of tetralogy of fallot in developing countries: opportunities and challenges for surgeons
Authors: Yuletta Adny Ambarsari, Yunanto Kurnia, Haryo Aribowo, Ihsanul Amal, Dicky Panditatwa Susantya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study evaluates outcomes of Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) correction, focusing on survival, complications, and management insights in a developing country setting.MethodsThis cohort retrospective study analyzed medical records of patients with TOF undergoing total correction (April 2024–April 2025). Variables included demographics, McGoon ratio, Cardiopulmonary Bypass (CPB), Aortic Cross Clamp (AOX), Length of Stay (LOS), morbidity, and mortality. Data were analyzed with chi-square and Mann-Whitney tests (p < 0.05).ResultsAmong 43 patients with TOF undergoing late total correction, mortality was 11.6%, higher than reported rates in low- and middle-income countries. Most cases presented late (mean age 9.19 years), underweight (65.1%), and hypoxemic (SpO₂ <90% in 79.1%). Predominant subvalvar stenosis led to prolonged CPB and AOX times. Deaths occurred only in children < 2 years. Limited Intensive Care Unit resources and delayed referral likely worsened outcomes, underscoring the need for early surgery, nutrition support, and infection control.ConclusionDelayed referral and limited early detection within the community should not preclude surgical correction in patients older than one year. With appropriate perioperative management, favorable outcomes remain achievable despite late presentation.Practical implicationsEnhancing preoperative optimization, perioperative management, and postoperative care could improve late-repair outcomes and align results with global standards.Keywords: Tetralogy of fallot, Surgical outcome, Total correction, Congenital heart defects

Background

Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) is the most prevalent form of cyanotic congenital heart disease. The estimated incidence is approximately 3.9 per 10,000 live births [1].

Over the past several decades, the operative mortality associated with TOF has markedly declined. In high-income countries, early diagnosis and timely surgical intervention have significantly improved outcomes, enabling effective anatomical correction during infancy. In developed countries, unrepaired TOF has become increasingly rare due to widespread neonatal screening and prompt surgical intervention. However, in many low- and middle-income countries (LMIC), a substantial number of TOF cases remain uncorrected into adulthood, contributing to ongoing morbidity and mortality. Without correction, however, TOF carries a poor prognosis: approximately 25% of affected individuals die within the first year of life, 40% by age three, and up to 70% by age ten [2].

Surgical management of TOF includes three main approaches: palliative procedures, staged repair, and primary complete repair. Currently, one-stage total correction is considered the gold standard, aiming to relieve right ventricular outflow tract obstruction, close the ventricular septal defect, and preserve pulmonary valve function, with elective repair typically performed within the first year of life [3, 4].

However, in resource-limited settings such as ours, delayed presentation, suboptimal preoperative condition, and limited access to early diagnosis often result in deviations from this ideal timing and strategy. Therefore, our study reflects the real-world application of primary repair in a cohort of patients with older age at presentation and more complex clinical conditions.

Recent studies reflect significant variation in postoperative outcomes. For instance, a study at Erasmus University Medical Center in Rotterdam reported an early postoperative mortality rate of 7.55% across both pediatric and adult cohorts [5]. Erdoğan et al. [6] observed a lower mortality rate of 3.1%, while Hashemzadeh et al. [7] reported an in-hospital mortality rate of 6.9%.

Advances in surgical technique and perioperative care have significantly reduced risk in congenital cardiac surgery, underscoring the advantages of early definitive repair over palliative strategies. Evidence suggests that two-stage approaches are associated with higher cumulative mortality compared to primary repair [4].

In addition to improved cardiac outcomes, early complete repair of TOF has demonstrated neuroprotective benefits, reducing the risk of chronic hypoxia-induced brain injury. Consequently, current best practice guidelines advocate for early surgical correction in children diagnosed with TOF [4]. Due to resource limitations in Indonesia, our tertiary care hospital frequently receives patients with TOF at an age older than one year. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the clinical outcomes of patients with TOF who undergo total surgical correction beyond the age of one year. The objective was to assess postoperative survival, and complications in this delayed-intervention population. Furthermore, this study aimed to provide comprehensive data on the clinical outcomes of patients with TOF who underwent total surgical correction at our center in a developing LMIC. Additional objectives were to evaluate postoperative results, including survival rates, complications, and to contribute to the understanding of TOF management outcomes in a limited resources setting.

Methods

Part of this research was previously shared as a poster presentation in Indonesia. This recent cohort study retrospectively collected data from the medical records of patients with TOF who underwent total corrective surgery at our center between April 2024 and April 2025. We collected data on mortality, morbidity, as well as intraoperative and postoperative variables as an epidemiological basis for future research.

Inclusion criteria included all patients diagnosed with TOF who underwent complete surgical correction at our center and had complete preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative medical records, including documented length of hospital stay. Preoperative variables included age, vital signs, body mass index (BMI), hemoglobin, hematocrit, and McGoon ratio. Intraoperative variables included operative findings, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) time, and aortic cross-clamp (AOX) time. Postoperative variables included length of stay (LOS) in the intensive care unit (ICU), total LOS, complications, and mortality. Exclusion criteria included incomplete data.

Preoperative evaluation was primarily based on transthoracic echocardiography and, in selected cases, cardiac catheterization to assess hemodynamics and pulmonary artery anatomy. Due to resource limitations, computed tomography (CT) was not routinely performed and was reserved for highly selected cases requiring additional anatomical clarification. Nutritional status was assessed using BMI according to the World Health Organization (WHO) age-specific BMI classification for under 2 years old. For subjects more than 2 years old we used CDC classification BMI for Age. Age was categorized according to standard pediatric and WHO-based definitions as follows preschool: 3–5 years, school-age: 6–12 years, early adolescence: 13–15 years, late adolescence: 16–18 years, and adults: >18 years.

Transannular patch repair was performed selectively in patients with significant valvar pulmonary stenosis and a small annulus. When the pulmonary valve was not stenotic, a valve-sparing approach was performed using a right ventricular outflow tract patch or a pulmonary artery patch.

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 16 (IBM Corp., Chicago, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic, clinical, intraoperative, and postoperative variables. Continuous variables (e.g., age, CPB time, and AOX time) were summarized using median and range. Categorical variables (e.g., sex, mortality, and comorbidities) were presented as frequencies and percentages. Associations between oxygen saturation, AOX time, CPB time, and malnutrition with mortality outcomes were analyzed using chi-square tests. A 95% confidence interval was applied for all statistical analyses with p < 0.05 considered significant.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Medical and Health Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine, Public Health and Nursing Universitas Gadjah Mada, with protocol number KE/FK/14,747/EC/2025. As a retrospective study using anonymized data, it posed no direct risk to patients. The findings were intended to contribute to improved perioperative risk management and clinical care for patients with TOF. The authors declared no conflict of interest.

Results

The findings indicated that most patients in this cohort presented with advanced disease characteristics, including older age at repair, a high prevalence of underweight status, and severe preoperative cyanosis (SpO₂ <90% in nearly 80% of cases). The predominance of subvalvar pulmonary stenosis suggests a tendency toward more complex surgical repairs. Additionally, the high proportion of patients with prolonged postoperative hospital stays (> 7 days) may reflect a greater burden of complications or slower recovery. Discussion

In this study of 43 patients, the majority were children, with 37.2% in the preschool age group and only a small proportion were adults. Male patients predominated (58.1%), consistent with previous reports of a slight male predominance in congenital heart disease (CHD), including TOF. Nutritional status was a significant concern, with 65.1% of patients classified as underweight and a median BMI of 15 kg/m². In addition, preoperative oxygen saturation was markedly low, with a median of 80% and nearly 80% of patients presenting with SpO₂ <90%.

Malnutrition in children with CHD is well-documented and represents an independent risk factor for adverse surgical outcomes. Poor nutritional status is associated with impaired immunity, reduced wound healing, and longer recovery times. Previous studies have demonstrated that preoperative malnutrition correlates with increased morbidity and mortality after pediatric cardiac surgery [8–9]. Although underweight patients in our cohort demonstrated higher crude mortality compared with those with normal nutrition, the difference did not achieve statistical significance (p = 0.324). This finding may reflect referral bias, a common issue in LMICs, where the most severely malnourished and critically ill children often die before reaching tertiary centers, resulting in selective survival among those referred for surgery [2]. Studies from Ethiopia and Indonesia highlight how delayed diagnosis, socioeconomic barriers, and limited access to specialized care contribute to late presentation and undernutrition among children with CHD [9–10].

Similarly, chronic hypoxemia predisposes patients to polycythemia, coagulation abnormalities, and organ dysfunction, all of which contribute to increased perioperative risk. The high prevalence of underweight status and hypoxemia in this cohort likely reflects delayed diagnosis and surgical intervention, which are common challenges in resource-limited settings [11]. Previous studies have consistently highlighted the prognostic value of preoperative oxygen saturation, with lower saturation levels often linked to adverse outcomes in congenital heart surgery. For example, Waqar et al. [12]. reported that desaturation below 90% was associated with increased early mortality after TOF repair. In contrast, our data did not show a statistically significant association (p = 0.72).

The mean CPB time was 112 min (range 69–253), with a median AOX time of 79 min (range 55–183). While comparable to previously reported series, the wide range indicates that certain patients required prolonged procedures, reflecting anatomical complexity. Prolonged bypass and cross-clamp times are well-established as predictors of postoperative complications, including low cardiac output syndrome and prolonged ICU stay [13–14]. Similarly, prolonged CPB time and AOX time are widely recognized as predictors of morbidity and mortality in pediatric cardiac surgery. Cardiac procedures with CPB time > 240 min and XCT > 150 min were associated with a higher risk of immediate postoperative adverse events. In our study, neither CPB time > 240 min nor cross-clamp time > 150 min reached statistical significance. This discrepancy may again be attributable to the small sample size and low event rate, as well as perioperative improvements in myocardial protection and postoperative care.

Anatomically, subvalvular pulmonary stenosis was the predominant type (74.4%), followed by valvular (20.9%) and supravalvular stenosis (4.7%). This distribution aligns with the classical pathophysiology of TOF, where infundibular narrowing is most common. Subvalvular stenosis frequently necessitates extensive resection or patch augmentation, which may contribute to prolonged bypass duration. The median ventricular septal defect (VSD) size was 2 cm² (range 0.5–9), consistent with the large defects typically seen in TOF. Larger VSDs are associated with significant right-to-left shunting and more severe preoperative cyanosis but remain surgically correctable with contemporary techniques [1].

Postoperative recovery showed a median ICU stay of 2 days (range 1–10) and a median overall hospital stay of 7 days (range 2–28). Notably, 65.1% of patients required hospitalization for more than 7 days, reflecting the burden of perioperative management in this high-risk group. Morbidity was relatively low, with only three complications (6%) observed—wound dehiscence, redo operation due to postoperative bleeding, and post-pericardiotomy syndrome, each occurring in one patient.

Despite the low morbidity, mortality was recorded in five patients (11.6%), which is higher than rates reported in high-volume centers with early interventions [15–16]. This mortality rate is higher than the rates reported in most recent multicenter and single-center studies from LMICs. The International Quality Improvement Collaborative (IQIC) database, which includes multiple LMIC centers, reported an operative mortality of 3.3% for primary TOF repair, with 54% of cases performed after 1 year of age and no significant difference in mortality between early and late repair groups (p = 0.21) [2].

The causes of mortality in our cohort were multifactorial and predominantly related to perioperative complications and preoperative clinical status. The most frequent causes included cardiogenic shock, sepsis with metabolic acidosis, pulmonary complications such as aspiration, and severe pulmonary hypertension. Cardiogenic shock reflects postoperative myocardial dysfunction, which may be associated with prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass time and the fragile physiological condition of patients at the time of surgery.

Sepsis and metabolic acidosis highlight the impact of pre-existing infection and poor nutritional status, which are common in our patient population and may impair recovery. Pulmonary complications, including aspiration and severe pulmonary hypertension, further contributed to adverse outcomes by increasing perioperative instability and respiratory failure risk.

In our cohort, mortality was significantly associated with younger age and lower body weight (both p = 0.002), indicating reduced physiological reserve and increased vulnerability to perioperative stress. This is consistent with LMIC reports, where younger patients often present with compromised preoperative conditions such as malnutrition and infection, which adversely affect outcomes. In addition, prolonged ICU length of stay (p = 0.020) and longer cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) time (p = 0.012) were significantly associated with mortality. Longer CPB duration has been linked to increased systemic inflammatory response, coagulopathy, and end-organ dysfunction, while prolonged ICU stay reflects a more complicated postoperative course, including hemodynamic instability and sepsis.

Single-center studies from Pakistan and Tanzania reported mortality rates of 1.3–9% for late repairs, with higher rates generally associated with poorer preoperative condition and lower institutional capacity [15–16]. Our study found that all mortality cases occurred in patients under 2 years of age. In contrast, adolescent patients who were already clinically eligible for surgery did not demonstrate an increased mortality rate. Additionally, limited pediatric ICU resources—such as mechanical ventilation capacity, inotropic support, and infection control—may have affected postoperative survival. While the 11% mortality observed in our study exceeds that of many published LMIC series, it remains comparable to the upper range reported from centers in the early stages of congenital cardiac program development [15]. Our findings emphasize the need for interventions targeting modifiable risk factors—particularly earlier detection and referral, nutritional optimization prior to surgery, infection prevention measures, and reduction of bypass/cross-clamp times through surgical planning and training.

Overall, our findings suggest that while oxygen saturation, CPB duration, AOX time, and nutritional status are biologically plausible and previously validated risk factors, their statistical impact could not be confirmed in this study. Larger, multi-center studies are needed to clarify these associations in similar populations.

Integrating these findings, it is clear that this study faced a dual burden of poor preoperative condition (malnutrition and hypoxemia) and intraoperative complexity (predominance of sub valvular pulmonary stenosis and large VSDs). These factors likely contributed to the variability in bypass duration and the relatively high mortality.

Strategies to improve outcomes should focus on early diagnosis and referral to avoid the long-term effects of hypoxemia, preoperative nutritional optimization to strengthen physiologic reserves prior to surgery, efficient surgical techniques to minimize CPB and AOX times, and enhanced perioperative care with emphasis on infection control, hemodynamic monitoring, and intensive care resources.

Although late repair remains feasible, outcomes are inferior to those achieved with early correction. Several studies have confirmed that early surgical intervention is associated with reduced mortality and better long-term survival [17]. Our findings highlight the challenges of TOF repair in resource-limited settings, where malnutrition, hypoxemia, and complex anatomy contribute to prolonged hospitalization and higher mortality. While our hospital mortality rate (11.6%) is higher than the 3.6% reported by the IQIC [2], the key risk factors are consistent: low oxygen saturation and poor nutritional status significantly increase perioperative risk. Importantly, age at repair beyond infancy was not independently associated with mortality, as also demonstrated in the IQIC study, underscoring that delayed repair can be safe when performed with adequate surgical and perioperative support. Strengthening preoperative optimization, infection prevention, and ICU capacity could narrow the outcome gap between low- and high-income settings.

Previous reports from LMICs have highlighted several additional challenges in patients with TOF, including prior palliative procedures, the presence of major aortopulmonary collateral arteries (MAPCAs), higher rates of endocarditis, neurological complications, arrhythmias, and coagulation abnormalities related to chronic hypoxia. These factors are often associated with delayed presentation and may increase surgical complexity and perioperative risk [18].

In contrast, in our cohort, despite delayed presentation and a proportion of older patients, none had undergone prior palliative procedures, and surgical management was performed as primary complete repair. In our country, limited access to early screening and delayed referral pathways to tertiary centers often result in late presentation, with many patients undergoing surgery at an older age. Although anatomical complexity such as MAPCAs may have been present, our outcomes remained acceptable. This finding suggests that, even in the absence of staged palliation, carefully selected patients with adequate pulmonary artery development can undergo successful primary repair.

Furthermore, our results support the notion that surgical outcomes in LMIC settings are not solely determined by age or anatomical complexity, but are more strongly influenced by preoperative clinical condition and perioperative management. This pattern may help to explain why, despite the presence of factors typically associated with increased risk in LMIC populations, our cohort still demonstrated relatively favorable outcomes.

Limitations

This study is limited by its retrospective design, relatively small sample size, and lack of detailed nutritional, biochemical parameters, and potential referral bias toward more complex patients. These factors restrict the ability to establish causality. Future research should include prospective multicenter studies with larger cohorts, detailed nutritional assessments, and evaluation of specific perioperative interventions.

Conclusions

The majority of patients in this study presented with undernutrition, chronic hypoxemia, and complex pulmonary stenosis anatomy. These preoperative and intraoperative challenges contributed to prolonged hospital stay and an elevated mortality rate despite relatively low morbidity. The higher-than-average early mortality in our study underscores the combined impact of patient, surgical, and systemic factors in LMIC settings. Strengthening preoperative optimization, perioperative protocols, and postoperative care infrastructure could help bring outcomes closer to international benchmarks, even when repair occurs after the first year of life.
