Twenty‐Four‐Hour Blood Pressure Variability Combined With Circadian Rhythm Patterns in Predicting Cardiovascular Events in Hypertensive Patients
Authors: Qin Ma, Shaoping Wang, Hua Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTShort‐term blood pressure variability (BPV) and circadian BP abnormalities are important cardiovascular risk predictors of mean BP levels, but their combined prognostic significance in essential hypertension remains incompletely understood. To evaluate the independent and combined prognostic value of 24‐hour BPV and circadian BP patterns for predicting major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in patients with essential hypertension. This retrospective cohort study included 500 adults with essential hypertension who underwent 24‐hour ambulatory BP monitoring. Short‐term BPV was assessed using the standard deviation of 24‐hour systolic BP. Circadian patterns were classified as dipper, non‐dipper, or reverse dipper based on nighttime systolic BP decline. The primary outcome was MACE, including myocardial infarction, stroke, hospitalization for heart failure, or cardiovascular death. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to evaluate associations with outcomes. During a median follow‐up of 4.2 years, 128 patients (25.6%) experienced MACE. High BPV was associated with a significantly greater incidence of MACE than low BPV (34.2% vs. 17.1%, p < 0.001) and remained an independent predictor (adjusted HR 1.89, 95% CI 1.32–2.71, p < 0.001). Reverse dipping was also associated with increased risk. Patients with both elevated BPV and abnormal circadian patterns had the highest risk. Elevated short‐term BPV and abnormal circadian BP patterns are independent predictors of MACE in essential hypertension. Their coexistence identifies a particularly high‐risk phenotype. ABPM‐based dynamic BP assessment may improve cardiovascular risk stratification beyond mean BP levels.Keywords: ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, blood pressure variability, cardiovascular outcomes, circadian rhythm, hypertension

Introduction

Hypertension is one of the non‐communicable diseases that are most prevalent in the world and is a primary cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Although there has been a significant improvement in the diagnosis, treatment, and guideline‐based care, a significant percentage of patients with hypertension still suffer adverse events of the cardiovascular system, despite the clinical blood pressure that might seem to be under control. This remaining residual risk highlights the weakness of using traditional office‐based blood pressure measurements as a cardiovascular risk assessment method alone [1, 2].

Blood pressure is a dynamic phenomenon which is regulated by autonomic mechanisms, behavior, physical activity, and sleep‐wake patterns, and stress factors of the environment. Consequently, blood pressure varies in the immediate periods of time and assumes an expected circadian distribution of 24 h. These temporal variations can be measured using twenty‐four hours ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM), which yields clinically meaningful information not just in terms of mean blood pressure values.

Acute changes in blood pressure have become progressively better recognized as a risk factor for vascular damage and cardiovascular hazard. Excess variability can expose the arterial wall to repeated mechanical forces, which favor endothelial dysfunction, arterial stiffening, inflammation and atherosclerosis development. Regular relationships have been indicated between high variances in blood pressure and poor outcomes such as stroke, myocardial infarction and heart failure [2, 3].

Another relevant aspect of blood pressure regulation is the circadian rhythm of blood pressure. The normal physiological response of nocturnal blood pressure in healthy people is that the pressure decreases by 10 to 20 percent compared to the daytime level, also referred to as the dipper pattern. Leaving this physiological profile, like non‐dipping, reverse dipping, or extreme dipping, has been attributed to the increased sympathetic activity, poor functioning of baroreflex and increased nocturnal burden of blood pressure. These abnormalities are linked to target‐organ damage, including left ventricular hypertrophy, renal injury, and cerebrovascular disease [4, 5, 6].

Although blood pressure variability (BPV) and abnormal circadian blood pressure patterns have each been associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes, evidence regarding their combined prognostic value in patients with essential hypertension remains limited. Evaluating these dynamic blood pressure characteristics together may improve cardiovascular risk stratification beyond conventional clinical and mean ambulatory blood pressure measurements. Therefore, this study aimed to determine whether 24‐hour systolic BPV and circadian blood pressure patterns were independently associated with major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and to assess whether their coexistence identified patients at particularly high cardiovascular risk during follow‐up.

Materials and Method

Study Design and Ethical Approval

This retrospective observational cohort study was conducted at a tertiary cardiovascular care center between January 2019 and December 2024. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Beijing Anzhen Hospital, Capital Medical University. The study was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Because the study utilized anonymized retrospective clinical data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

Study Population

The study included 500 consecutive adult patients with essential hypertension who underwent valid 24‐hour ABPM during the study period.

Patients with advanced chronic kidney disease were excluded because severe renal impairment is associated with substantial alterations in BPV, circadian blood pressure regulation, and cardiovascular risk, which could confound the associations under investigation.

Data Collection and Clinical Variables

Demographic characteristics, including age and sex, clinical variables including body mass index (BMI) and smoking status, comorbidities including diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and coronary artery disease, laboratory parameters, and antihypertensive medication use were extracted from electronic medical records. Follow‐up information was obtained from outpatient visits, hospital admissions, discharge summaries, and institutional clinical databases [8].

ABPM Protocol

Twenty‐four‐hour ABPM was performed using validated oscillometric devices in accordance with contemporary European Society of Hypertension recommendations. Blood pressure measurements were automatically recorded every 15–20 min during daytime hours and every 30 min during nighttime hours. Participants were instructed to maintain their usual daily activities during monitoring while avoiding unusually strenuous physical exertion.

Daytime and nighttime periods were defined according to each participant's self‐reported wake‐up and sleep times. ABPM recordings were considered valid when at least 80% of scheduled measurements were successfully obtained and available for analysis [9].

Assessment of BPV

Short‐term BPV was assessed using the standard deviation (SD) of 24‐hour systolic blood pressure measurements obtained from ABPM recordings. Systolic BP variability was selected because of its established association with cardiovascular outcomes in previous studies and its widespread use in clinical BP variability research.

Patients were stratified into high‐ and low‐BPV groups according to the median 24‐hour systolic BP SD observed in the study population. Although alternative indices such as weighted standard deviation and average real variability have been proposed, the 24‐hour systolic BP SD remains one of the most commonly reported measures of short‐term BP variability in epidemiological studies.

Classification of Circadian Blood Pressure Patterns

Circadian blood pressure patterns were classified according to the percentage decline in mean nighttime systolic BP relative to mean daytime systolic BP:

Outcome Measures

The primary study endpoint was the occurrence of MACE, defined as a composite of acute myocardial infarction, ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, hospitalization for heart failure, or cardiovascular death.

Cardiovascular events were identified through review of clinical records, diagnostic imaging reports, hospitalization records, and discharge diagnoses. All outcome events were adjudicated using available clinical documentation.

For the primary statistical analyses, patients with normal dipper and extreme‐dipper patterns were combined into a single dipper reference group because of the relatively small number of extreme dippers.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, depending on data distribution. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using Student's t‐test, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or the chi‐square test, as appropriate.

Event‐free survival was assessed using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, and differences between groups were evaluated using the log‐rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to identify predictors of cardiovascular events. Variables with established clinical relevance and potential confounding effects were included in multivariable analyses, including age, sex, diabetes mellitus, smoking status, mean 24‐hour systolic BP, and antihypertensive medication use.

Hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed prior to model construction. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version XX (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics

The study cohort consisted of 500 patients with essential hypertension, with a mean age of 56.8 ± 11.2 years; 270 patients (54.0%) were male. Common cardiovascular risk factors were prevalent within the study population, including diabetes mellitus in 190 patients (38.0%), dyslipidemia in 210 patients (42.0%), and current or former smoking in 145 patients (29.0%). Established cardiovascular disease was present in 105 patients (21.0%).

Based on circadian blood pressure classification, 180 patients (36.0%) exhibited a normal dipper pattern, 30 patients (6.0%) exhibited an extreme‐dipper pattern, 230 patients (46.0%) were classified as non‐dippers, and 60 patients (12.0%) were classified as reverse dippers. Because of the relatively small number of extreme dippers, normal and extreme dippers were combined into a broader dipper reference group for the primary analyses. Accordingly, the combined dipper group comprised 210 patients (42.0%). Short‐term BPV, assessed using the standard deviation (SD) of 24‐hour systolic blood pressure measurements obtained from ABPM, had a median value of 13.5 mmHg and was used as the threshold for stratification into low‐ and high‐BPV groups. Based on this cutoff, 240 patients (48.0%) were categorized as having high BPV (>13.5 mmHg), while 260 patients (52.0%) were classified as having low BPV (≤13.5 mmHg).

Overall, the two groups were broadly comparable; however, patients with high BPV tended to be older, had a higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus, and exhibited higher mean nighttime systolic blood pressure values compared with those with low BPV.

Incidence and Distribution of Cardiovascular Events

During a median follow‐up period of 4.2 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 3.1–5.0 years), 128 patients (25.6%) experienced at least one MACE. The distribution of cardiovascular events was as follows: stroke in 49 patients (38.3%), hospitalization for heart failure in 40 patients (31.3%), myocardial infarction in 27 patients (21.1%), and cardiovascular death in 12 patients (9.4%).

Stroke was the most frequently observed cardiovascular event, accounting for more than one‐third of all recorded outcomes. The cumulative incidence of MACE increased progressively over the follow‐up period, as demonstrated by Kaplan–Meier survival analysis.

Association Between BPV and Cardiovascular Outcomes

Patients with high BPV experienced a significantly greater incidence of MACE than those with low BPV (34.2% vs. 17.1%, p < 0.001). Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated significantly lower cardiovascular event‐free survival among patients with high BPV compared with those with low BPV (log‐rank p < 0.001) (Figure 3).

Combined Effect of BPV and Circadian Rhythm

To evaluate the combined prognostic significance of BPV and circadian blood pressure phenotype, patients were stratified according to BPV status and circadian BP pattern. The highest incidence of MACE was observed among patients with both high BPV and abnormal circadian BP patterns (non‐dipper or reverse‐dipper). In contrast, patients with low BPV and a normal dipper pattern exhibited the lowest event rate during follow‐up.

Specifically, MACE occurred in 14.1% of patients with low BPV and a dipper pattern, compared with 36.1% of patients with high BPV and abnormal circadian BP patterns (p < 0.001). Intermediate event rates were observed in patients with high BPV and a dipper pattern (22.0%) and those with low BPV and abnormal circadian BP patterns (26.5%), suggesting that both elevated BPV and circadian BP dysregulation contribute independently to cardiovascular risk.

Multivariate Predictors of Cardiovascular Events

Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of MACE during follow‐up. After adjustment for demographic characteristics, cardiovascular risk factors, and ambulatory blood pressure parameters, several variables remained significantly associated with cardiovascular outcomes (Table 4). Increasing age was independently associated with a higher risk of MACE, with each 10‐year increase conferring a 34% increase in cardiovascular risk (adjusted HR 1.34, 95% CI 1.11–1.61, p = 0.002). Diabetes mellitus (adjusted HR 1.49, 95% CI 1.07–2.09, p = 0.020) and smoking (adjusted HR 1.37, 95% CI 1.01–1.87, p = 0.044) also emerged as significant predictors of adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Among ambulatory blood pressure parameters, elevated 24‐hour systolic BPV remained a strong independent predictor of MACE (adjusted HR 1.89, 95% CI 1.32–2.71, p < 0.001) (Table 5). Similarly, abnormal circadian blood pressure phenotypes were associated with increased cardiovascular risk. Compared with patients exhibiting a normal dipper pattern, non‐dippers had a 56% higher risk of MACE (adjusted HR 1.56, 95% CI 1.08–2.24, p = 0.020), while reverse‐dippers demonstrated more than a two‐fold increase in risk (adjusted HR 2.03, 95% CI 1.29–3.18, p = 0.002) (Figure 5).

Notably, patients exhibiting both high BP variability and abnormal circadian BP regulation experienced the greatest cardiovascular risk. Association Between BP Variability, Circadian Pattern, and Specific Cardiovascular Outcomes

When individual cardiovascular outcomes were analyzed separately, high BPV and abnormal circadian blood pressure patterns remained consistently associated with increased risk across multiple event types (Table 6). Reverse dipper patterns and the combined phenotype of high BP variability with abnormal circadian rhythm demonstrated the strongest associations, particularly for stroke and heart failure hospitalization. These findings indicate that dynamic blood pressure abnormalities exert broad adverse cardiovascular effects rather than being limited to a single disease manifestation. Sequential model comparison demonstrated significant incremental improvement in cardiovascular risk prediction with the stepwise addition of ambulatory blood pressure parameters (Table 7). While mean 24‐hour systolic blood pressure modestly improved model discrimination, inclusion of BPV and circadian blood pressure pattern resulted in substantial gains in prognostic performance. The highest discrimination was observed when the combined BP variability–circadian phenotype was incorporated, supporting the additive clinical value of these dynamic ABPM measures beyond conventional risk factors.

Clinical and Translational Implications

These findings highlight the limitations of relying solely on clinic or mean ambulatory BP values and support a more comprehensive interpretation of ABPM data. Incorporating BP variability and circadian rhythm assessment may improve cardiovascular risk stratification and guide individualized treatment strategies, including long‐acting antihypertensive agents, chronotherapy, and closer follow‐up in high‐risk patients.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the independent and combined prognostic significance of short‐term BPV and circadian blood pressure patterns in a cohort of 500 patients with essential hypertension followed for a median of 4.2 years. Three principal findings emerged from our analysis. First, elevated 24‐hour systolic BPV was independently associated with an increased risk of MACE. Second, abnormal circadian BP phenotypes, particularly reverse‐dipper and non‐dipper patterns, were associated with worse cardiovascular outcomes compared with the normal dipper pattern. Third, patients exhibiting both elevated BPV and abnormal circadian BP patterns experienced the highest cardiovascular risk, suggesting an additive adverse effect of these two dynamic blood pressure characteristics.

Our findings regarding BPV are consistent with a growing body of evidence demonstrating that BP fluctuations provide prognostic information beyond mean blood pressure levels alone. Previous studies have shown that increased short‐term BPV is associated with a higher incidence of stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure, and cardiovascular mortality. Various pathophysiological mechanisms may explain these observations. Excessive BP fluctuations expose the vascular wall to repeated mechanical stress, contributing to endothelial dysfunction, arterial stiffness, oxidative stress, and accelerated atherosclerotic progression. In addition, increased BPV has been linked to impaired baroreflex sensitivity and heightened sympathetic nervous system activity, both of which may promote cardiovascular instability and adverse clinical outcomes [10, 11, 12, 13, 14].

Circadian blood pressure rhythm represents another important component of cardiovascular risk assessment. In healthy individuals, blood pressure typically declines during sleep as a consequence of reduced sympathetic activity and enhanced parasympathetic predominance. Failure of this normal nocturnal BP decline, manifested as non‐dipping or reverse‐dipping patterns, reflects autonomic dysregulation and persistent nocturnal hemodynamic burden. Consistent with previous reports, we observed significantly higher cardiovascular event rates among patients with abnormal circadian BP patterns, particularly those exhibiting reverse dipping. These findings support earlier studies demonstrating associations between abnormal nocturnal BP profiles and target‐organ damage, including left ventricular hypertrophy, renal dysfunction, cerebrovascular disease, and increased cardiovascular mortality [15, 16, 17].

Although BPV and circadian BP abnormalities have each been extensively investigated, relatively few studies have examined their combined prognostic value. This distinction is clinically important because these phenomena frequently coexist in patients with hypertension. Our study demonstrated that patients with both elevated BPV and abnormal circadian BP patterns experienced the poorest cardiovascular outcomes, with a risk of MACE exceeding that observed with either abnormality alone. This finding suggests that BPV and circadian BP dysregulation may represent complementary manifestations of underlying hemodynamic instability. The coexistence of exaggerated BP fluctuations and persistent nocturnal BP burden may amplify vascular stress, endothelial injury, sympathetic activation, and target‐organ damage, thereby accelerating cardiovascular disease progression [18, 19, 20, 21].

The relatively high incidence of cardiovascular events observed in our cohort may be explained by the characteristics of the study population. As a tertiary‐care referral cohort, a substantial proportion of patients had multiple cardiovascular risk factors, including diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and established cardiovascular disease. Consequently, the study population represented a relatively high‐risk hypertensive group, which may account for the observed event rate during follow‐up.

From a clinical perspective, our findings reinforce the importance of ABPM as a tool for cardiovascular risk stratification. Traditional hypertension management has largely focused on achieving target clinic or mean ambulatory BP values [22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. However, our results suggest that dynamic BP characteristics—including short‐term BPV and circadian BP patterns—provide additional prognostic information that may identify high‐risk individuals despite apparently adequate BP control. Incorporating these ABPM‐derived parameters into routine clinical assessment may facilitate earlier identification of patients who could benefit from more intensive monitoring and individualized therapeutic strategies [27, 28, 29, 30, 31].

The strengths of this study include the use of 24‐hour ABPM for comprehensive blood pressure assessment, evaluation of both BPV and circadian BP phenotypes, and a relatively large real‐world cohort with longitudinal follow‐up. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective single‐center design may limit generalizability and introduces the possibility of residual confounding. Second, BPV and circadian BP patterns were derived from a single ABPM recording and may not fully reflect long‐term blood pressure behavior. Third, BPV was assessed using the standard deviation of 24‐hour systolic BP, which may be influenced by day–night BP differences. Alternative measures such as weighted standard deviation or average real variability could provide additional information. Finally, despite multivariable adjustment, unmeasured confounding factors cannot be completely excluded.

Future research should focus on prospective multicenter validation, interventional trials targeting BP variability and circadian normalization, and integration of dynamic BP parameters into cardiovascular risk models. Mechanistic studies and advances in wearable BP monitoring may further clarify pathophysiological pathways and improve longitudinal risk prediction [30, 31, 32, 33, 34].

Conclusion

Elevated short‐term BPV and abnormal circadian blood pressure patterns are independent predictors of MACE in patients with essential hypertension. The coexistence of these abnormalities identifies a particularly high‐risk phenotype associated with substantially worse cardiovascular outcomes. These findings support the broader use of ABPM not only for assessing average blood pressure levels but also for evaluating dynamic blood pressure characteristics that may improve cardiovascular risk stratification and guide individualized hypertension management.
