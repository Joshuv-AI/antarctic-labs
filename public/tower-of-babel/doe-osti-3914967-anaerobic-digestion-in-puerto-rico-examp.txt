Executive Summary
This report assesses the potential of small-scale anaerobic digestion (AD) in Puerto Rico to
enhance energy security, boost agricultural productivity, and improve community self-
sufficiency, particularly during emergencies. By converting local organic waste into energy and
soil products, small-scale AD can reduce costs, reduce dependence on external inputs, and help
rural communities respond to hurricanes, flooding, and other events that often isolate remote,
hilly areas from critical infrastructure.

The analysis was developed in 2024–2025 by National Laboratory of the Rockies under the U.S.
Department of Energy’s Communities Local Energy Action Program (LEAP) in collaboration
with the Centro de Microempresas y Tecnologías Agrícolas Sustentables de Yauco Inc.
(CMTAS) and the Hispanic Federation.

The analysis focuses on three pathways for small-scale anaerobic digesters:

 • On-farm use of biogas for energy needs, such as cooking, water heating, and
 electricity

 • Compression and bottling of biogas for local sale

 • Production and use of digestate as a soil amendment, additive, or soil conditioner.

The report integrates technical, regulatory, financial, and research considerations to provide
communities, policymakers, and project developers with actionable guidance for future
biodigester projects. Specifically, it:

 • Evaluates alternative distribution models and end-use options for biogas and digestate
 using CMTAS’s operating sites as real-world examples (see Figure ES1)

 • Provides regulatory and permitting considerations for siting, construction, operation,
 and commercialization activities

 • Offers design considerations for CMTAS’s living laboratory to strengthen research
 credibility and attract new academic and technical partners

 • Identifies financing and funding options suited to small-scale AD projects in Puerto
 Rico.

The assessment centers on CMTAS’s current small-scale AD operations across five sites in
Puerto Rico and opportunities to scale up both for CMTAS and other communities across Puerto
Rico (Figure ES2). Key findings include:

 • Biogas production and use: CMTAS currently has eight anaerobic digesters installed at
 five sites in Puerto Rico: Quebradas de Yauco, “Yauco” (three digesters, cattle farm);
 Peñuelas (one digester, pig farm); Santa Isabel (one digester, fishing community);
 Guánica (one digester, fishing community); and Cabo Rojo (two digesters, urban waste).
 NLR evaluated the potential range of end uses for the biogas produced by the
 biodigesters—for example, on-site cooking, water heating, electricity generation, and
 bottling for off-site distribution—some of which (cooking) are already happening at a
 limited demonstration level at the CMTAS Yauco site.

 Figure ES2. Overview of CMTAS operations and digesters in Puerto Rico

  • Biogas distribution pathways: Once treated, biogas can be distributed off-site to support
 other end users, an option that was explored by CMTAS with support from NLR. Four
 distribution models—bagging, bottling, piping, and electricity generation—were
 analyzed for technical feasibility, safety, cost, and regulatory implications. Small-scale
 bottling systems were found to align with CMTAS’s goals of providing downstream
 community benefits but would require further investment and operator training to
 implement successfully.

  • Digestate as fertilizer: The chemical and physical compositions of the liquid fraction of
 the digestate from the CMTAS biodigesters were evaluated to explore potential pathways
 for commercializing the product as an alternative to commercial fertilizer, known as
 “BioFert.” While the macronutrient levels for the potential BioFert product are below the
 threshold for commercial fertilizers, there could be potential waivers and/or alternative
 applications that could be explored.

  • Living laboratory and research partnerships: An agricultural research plot at the
 CMTAS Yauco site has been developed to evaluate performance of the potential BioFert
 product, water use efficiency, and crop yields. This creates a potential to develop
 partnerships with local, regional, and mainland U.S. universities and other relevant
 entities to support agricultural research and student engagement.

  • Regulatory and permitting guidance: An analysis of U.S. federal, Puerto Rican, and
 local government laws, permitting, and regulatory requirements applicable for site/land
 access, construction, operations, and biogas/fertilizer production and sales for AD
 projects in Puerto Rico was conducted to inform future AD projects. Overall, small-AD
 systems do not fit neatly within existing frameworks and instead are subject to a complex
 framework of federal, territory/state, and local government permits and regulatory
 requirements that vary by jurisdiction.

 vii

 • Financing and scale-up: Small-scale AD systems often encounter barriers to securing
 financing. Projects of this scale are often too small for traditional infrastructure
 investment from banks and other investors yet potentially too capital-intensive for
 grassroots implementation without external support. Depending on the type of project, its
 size, and its location, there are a variety of financing providers that could potentially
 support AD projects in Puerto Rico, each with different requirements, focus and
 missions, and financial product offerings.

 • Risk management and mitigation: AD projects have specific risks for elements such as
 technology performance, safety, awareness and social acceptance, regulations/policy,
 markets, and environmental impacts that need to be managed with intentional design and
 maintenance of systems, specific training for operators, and proactive engagement with
 local officials and communities.

 • Strategic importance: The CMTAS projects offer a potential model for other small
 farms, agricultural collectives, fishing communities, and rural areas in Puerto Rico. These
 systems can deliver tangible benefits aligned with Puerto Rico’s policy goals around
 agricultural self-sufficiency, energy independence, and reliability.

This work serves as a technical and strategic reference for agricultural communities,
policymakers, funders, and other stakeholders exploring AD solutions in Puerto Rico and similar
contexts.

1 Introduction
The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Communities Local Energy Action Program (LEAP)
facilitates long-term economic benefits through locally driven energy planning and development.
Under the program, local governments and Tribes, along with local businesses, residents,
utilities, and other organizations, partner with technical assistance providers to assess energy-
related challenges and opportunities and develop strategies to advance energy projects for energy
security, reliability, affordability, and innovation.

Through LEAP, DOE provides customized technical assistance to local governments, businesses,
and residents to advance approaches for energy-related economic development. Coalitions of
local partners, each including at least one local government and one community partner,
contribute to project oversight and delivery. NLR is the primary technical assistance provider,
delivering technical expertise and supporting stakeholder engagement as well as managing the
overall network of technical assistance providers. Cohort communities can also strategize plans
to leverage funding or financing opportunities in collaboration with the public, private, and
philanthropic sectors.

Puerto Rico faces recurring challenges from extreme weather, energy infrastructure
vulnerabilities, and rural economic constraints. Communities often experience prolonged power
outages, disruptions to essential services, and limited access to reliable energy for agriculture and
households. At the same time, small farms and rural cooperatives face rising input costs, waste
management challenges, and increasing interest in locally driven solutions.

Small-scale anaerobic digestion (AD) offers one potential pathway to address these needs by
converting agricultural, organic, and other waste into biogas for energy and digestate for soil
enhancement; but questions remain about technical feasibility, regulatory requirements,
financing options, and long-term viability, particularly for small-scale or community-led
projects.

This report was developed in 2024–2025 by NLR under the DOE LEAP second cohort in
collaboration with the Centro de Microempresas y Tecnologías Agrícolas Sustentables de Yauco
Inc. (CMTAS) 1 and the Hispanic Federation.

2 Background
2.1 Disruptions and Vulnerabilities in Puerto Rico
Puerto Rico faces persistent challenges related to infrastructure vulnerabilities, economic
fragility, and exposure to natural hazards. Given its mountainous island geography, high
population density (~959 people per square mile—more than any other U.S. state or territory 2),
socioeconomic conditions (an estimated 43% of the population lives below federal poverty levels
[higher in rural areas]—more than three times higher than in the United States overall), 3 and
social vulnerability (more than 1.31 million people—about 40% of the population—have three or
more components of social vulnerability 4,5), Puerto Rico’s population is highly susceptible to
disruptions and damage, particularly from events such as extreme weather, tropical storms,
flooding, 6 landslides, 7 and earthquakes. 8

The U.S. Federal Emergency Management Agency conservatively estimates $2.9 billion in
average expected annual losses due to disasters across census tracts in Puerto Rico. 9 Puerto
Rico has had eight weather events with losses exceeding $1 billion in the last 40 years ($0.2
billion events per year on average). Most recently, Hurricanes Maria and Irma in 2017
collectively caused more than $90 billion in damage, leaving many communities without power
for up to 11 months and without critical services, such as road access and fuel delivery, for
extended periods. 10

Electricity and energy service disruptions are particularly common in Puerto Rico—even
without disaster events. For example, the typical customer in Puerto Rico experienced 1,572
minutes (or nearly 27 hours) of power interruptions in 2023—significantly exceeding the U.S.
national average of 342 minutes (approximately 5.7 hours). Even under the lower estimates,
territory-wide power outages could cost customers about $1 billion for a 1-day outage, $5 billion
for a 2-week outage, and $29 billion for a month-long outage. 11 The collective cost of these
disruptions to fuel, electricity, and household capabilities is heightened in rural communities.

Outage events, both big and small, can also disrupt household capabilities, such as cooling,
food and medicine refrigeration, lighting, water pumping and heating, and cooking, all of which
have been highlighted in a study 12 as key drivers of physical and mental health impacts for
Puerto Rican households, especially rural households. For example, an outage response survey in
Puerto Rico found that households lost an average of 2.8 capabilities per event and reported
spending an average of 11.6 additional hours and upward of $195 trying to address all the
disrupted capabilities. 13 At a minimum, this time and economic burden can exacerbate key
affordability challenges for rural communities—but if the disruptions impact key services, such
as healthcare and water availability, there can be broader public health implications, especially
among more at-risk populations.

Further, while accounting for less than 1% of the gross domestic product in Puerto Rico in
2020, 14 agriculture still plays a significant cultural and economic role, especially in rural
communities. Puerto Rico’s island geography makes maintaining local food production critical;
disruptions to agriculture mean greater dependence on imported food (currently about 85% of
food is imported 15), increasing costs for all Puerto Ricans and worsening affordability
challenges. In total, there are an estimated 7,600 farms 16 in Puerto Rico, with an average size of
63.1 acres 17; however 75% of farms are under 48.5 acres 18 and 55% are smaller than 18.4
acres. 19 Approximately 55% of farmers live off their farm, and farming is the primary occupation
for 60% of farmers. 20,21 Protecting and strengthening this local agricultural base is vital to
reducing food supply vulnerabilities and stabilizing costs for rural and urban communities alike.

These smallholder farms and farmers in particular are more vulnerable to impacts from
disaster events because they often operate with limited financial resources, smaller equipment
inventories, and fewer options to cover risks, such as insurance or diversified revenue streams.
Following Hurricanes Maria and Irma in 2017, the number of farms smaller than 10 acres
decreased by more than 50%, and the number of farms with 10 to 49 acres decreased by almost
one-third. 22 The loss of these farms not only disrupts local food production but also accelerates
dependence on imported food, contributing to higher food prices and reduced food security for
rural and urban households alike. 23

2.2 Overview of Anaerobic Digestion
2.2.1 Overview
AD is a biological process in which microorganisms convert organic matter (“feedstock”) from a
variety of sources (e.g., animal manure, food waste, fish discard, crop residues, human waste,
wastewater sludge) into biogas and digestate. Anaerobic digesters (biodigesters) are engineered
to create an oxygen-free environment to accelerate the process and capture biogas and other
valuable coproducts, such as digestate (Figure 1) 27; however, only roughly 5% of the billions of
tons of organic feedstock is currently being used for biogas production globally. 28

It is important to differentiate between large-scale AD systems and small-scale AD, as they
significantly differ in size, complexity, and purpose. For this report and the work with CMTAS,
the focus is on small-scale AD, which serves small farms or communities using on-site organic
waste. Unlike large, centralized AD plants that can feed electricity to grids or produce
commercial-grade fertilizers, small-scale systems are simpler, lower-cost technologies that
require less infrastructure and rely on community-level operations and maintenance. Small-scale
AD is one of many solutions that can help build localized energy security for cooking,
electricity, and heating and help strengthen rural energy access, food security, and reliability
following disruptions while improving agricultural waste management in remote areas.

2.2.2 Outputs: Biogas and Digestate
Biogas is a gaseous blend of roughly 50 to 70% methane (CH4) and 30 to 50% carbon dioxide
(CO2), with up to 10% water and traces of other gases, including nitrogen (N2), oxygen (O2),
hydrogen sulfide (H2S), and ammonia (NH3). 29 At the small-household and farm level,
biodigesters produce biogas that is typically used for cooking as a substitute for firewood or
imported liquified petroleum gas (LPG), especially in many small island and rural

3.2.2 Treatment
The treatment of biogas is an important step for ensuring safe and effective distribution and the
downstream sale of biogas. Biogas cleanup systems are used to remove trace components from
raw biogas and to improve the quality of biogas for distribution and broader end uses. Three
main categories of cleanup include:

 • Cleaning: Biogas filters (e.g., steel wool) are used to remove trace contaminants, such as
 hydrogen sulfide (H2S); ammonia (NH3); and volatile organic compounds from raw
 biogas (contaminant concentrations on the order of hundreds to thousands of parts per
 million). Exposure to these components can have negative health impacts and accelerate
 the corrosion of biogas equipment. 41
 • Moisture removal: Water vapor (up to 10% of biogas) reduces potential heating values
 and can accelerate the aging of equipment. Effective moisture removal is an important
 factor for biogas distribution. Common methods for moisture removal include the use of
 silica gel (adsorption) or more commonly cooling or chilling (condensation). Chilling
 biogas has additional benefits, including reduced compression temperature and the partial
 removal of contaminants including siloxane, NH3, and volatile organic compounds. 42 In
 piping systems, water can be removed by a water trap that can be placed underground 43
 or coupled to a chiller.

3.2.3 Distribution
Following treatment, an effective distribution strategy is key to maximizing the potential benefits
of biogas. CMTAS is currently exploring pathways to extend biogas use beyond the digester site,
both for on-farm use and in the surrounding community. To inform this decision, four potential
biogas distribution options were evaluated: bagging, bottling, piping, and electricity
generation. 44, 45 This section provides an overview of each option along with their respective
advantages and disadvantages.

 • Bagging: Biogas can be stored in flexible bags or balloons without compression and
 transported by car or even carried in backpacks. For example, in Zambia, communities
 have used biogas bags holding about 70 ft³, roughly the size of a household refrigerator,
 which one person could carry safely 46; however, the gas must be used quickly to avoid
 leakage, and because it is uncompressed, bagging requires the largest storage volume,
 involves significant manual handling, and provides the lowest energy density among
 distribution options.
 • Bottling: Compressing the same amount of biogas using electric or gas-powered
 compressors to fill bottles to distribute excess biogas can reduce its volume by 2- to 200-
 fold, depending on the pressure. At low pressures typical of small-scale applications
 (<580 psi), one or two standard LPG cylinders could store the same 70 ft³ of gas. Bottling
 allows longer storage and easier transport but requires additional measures—such as
 advanced cleanup, moisture removal, and safety precautions—to address risks, such
 as leakage and flammability. (See footnote for details.) 47 Pilot projects in India, Zambia,
 Kenya, Uganda, Thailand, and Mexico have demonstrated the feasibility of small-scale
 biogas bottling (Annex 3).
 • Piping: Small PVC or high-density polyethylene (HDPE) pipeline distribution systems
 developed to provide constant biogas to nearby locations. Directly piping biogas to

3.3.3 Comparison to Commercial Fertilizers
The CMTAS digestate samples from Yauco and Peñuelas were analyzed and compared with
commonly available commercial vegetable fertilizers to assess their nutrient content and
agronomic potential for desired crops (peppers, tomatoes, eggplant, cucumbers, and carrots—see
Living Laboratory section) as part of an effort to explore potential commercialization for the
digestate product (referred to as “BioFert” by CMTAS).

Results (see Table 5) showed that concentrations of key macronutrients—N, P, and K—were
significantly lower in the potential BioFert product, with the total macronutrient levels more than
two orders of magnitude below those found in commercial fertilizers (e.g., 0.01% to 0.02%
versus roughly 2% to 6% for nitrogen). Phosphorus levels were especially low, at nearly three
orders of magnitude below commercial benchmarks (e.g., 0.002% to 0.004% compared to 1% to
5%). This presents a major constraint for the potential commercialization of the potential BioFert
product given the intended use for high-nutrient-demand crops, such as tomatoes, peppers,
eggplants, and cucumbers. Note, however, that the testing was done on the liquid fraction of the
digestate because that is the portion currently used by CMTAS since the solid fraction is so small
and filtered out in current operations. Additional testing could be done specifically on the solid
portion to further understand potential digestate characteristics.

The BioFert product might also face regulatory barriers to commercialization including
obtaining formula and grade approval and complying with bonding and labeling
requirements.

Another process improvement option to minimize nutrient loss is reducing the digestate storage
time. Ammonia losses during storage can be substantial: For dairy cow digestate, they range
from about 6% to 40% of the total nitrogen content, and for pig slurry, they could reach 50%–
60%. 84 These losses increase with longer storage periods (and are further influenced by factors
such as pH, temperature, and surface exposure), suggesting that applying digestate to fields as
soon as practical might help retain more nutrients, particularly nitrogen.

While process improvements likely would have the greatest initial impact, additional gains could
be achieved through post-processing techniques that lower water content and concentrate
nutrients, such as filtering followed by evaporation pond concentration of liquids or air-drying of
solids. A final option is post-treatment blending with phosphorus-rich additives (e.g., colloidal
phosphate 85), but this is a higher-cost solution and is likely not practical for small-scale
production.

Commercializing the potential BioFert product from the CMTAS, or other AD sites, presents
several challenges, particularly given the small-scale of the production involved and the low
concentrations of N, P, and K found in the final digestate or BioFert product. Biofertilizer
compounds might also need to be treated to meet fertilizer regulations (see the regulatory and
permitting section) and reduce or eliminate pathogens (disease risk). The most common method
of doing this is heat treatment (pasteurization), where the digestate is heated to a specific
temperature (e.g., 70°C or higher) for a given time. For the solids fraction, heat treatment can be
achieved through composting. Other methods include ultraviolet radiation and chemical
treatments, but this could be expensive for small-AD applications.

In addition, the informal nature of small operations and the lack of a recognized brand or quality
assurance for the potential BioFert product might make it difficult to attract buyers accustomed
to standardized commercial products. There could be limited consumer willingness to pay for a
non-commercial, unregulated fertilizer product, particularly if nutrient performance is not
guaranteed.

As highlighted, the biggest barrier might be the regulatory requirements and approvals needed to
manufacture and distribute the potential BioFert product.

Despite these limitations, the commercialization of the potential BioFert product could offer
some opportunities if approval is received and the product is strategically positioned. The
product is organic, non-chemical, and locally produced—attributes that might appeal to growers
or consumers interested in supporting local economies and locally produced products. It could be
especially relevant for use on-site or within short distribution chains, where trust and
relationships can substitute for formal product validation. Home gardeners who are interested in
local, non-chemical alternatives might be the most appropriate initial user of the potential
BioFert product, given the characteristics, application method, and quantity produced; however
other potential users—such as organic farms or mission-driven institutions, such as schools,

A digestate-based fertilizer could also provide some benefits compared to the sale of manure as
an agricultural input. 86 Digestate can contain more readily available nitrogen (i.e., ammonium)

raw manure, but it can come with certain trade-offs, such as increased leaching of nitrates. 87

as a fertilizer supplement for crop production, especially if low levels of commercial fertilizer
are currently used. Filtering the digestate could still be required if the fertilizer is to be applied
through fertigation or spraying, as a slurry mix could clog emitters or equipment.

Overall, to develop successful commercial and/or on-site supplemental fertilizer production, the
process will need to be iterative and include experimentation with different methods and crops.
This could include testing out different retention times to understand the impact on biogas
production and nutrient levels, 88 experimenting with different co-digestates, and/or using a living
laboratory to test out different digestate products from different sites and feedstocks to evaluate
their potential commercialization as BioFert products.

To ensure statistical validity for research and comparisons, there are several key considerations
to keep in mind for the research design 92, 93, 94:

 • Establish a control plot for each direct comparison to the potential BioFert products—
 for example, a plot where commercial fertilizer is applied to the same crops, serving as a
 comparison to crops treated with BioFert.
 • Be consistent across treatments (only change one variable at a time)—for example, in
 line with these objectives, a single plot should only be changing water application rates,
 the type of fertilizer (conventional versus BioFert), or the BioFert application rates, not a
 combination of those variables. This plus the establishment of control plots helps ensure
 statistically relevant comparisons.
 • For each treatment, having at least 10 plants for each crop can help perform more
 robust statistical analysis and to account for any losses throughout the season (e.g., from
 pests, natural damage, human damage, bad seedlings/seeds).
 • Performing research over multiple seasons can help account for weather and
 pest/disease variability.
 • It can be useful to compare results across different crop varietals (e.g., multiple
 varieties of tomatoes).
 • Maintaining harvest schedules similar to farmers aligns research with established
 practices.

3.4.2 Research Methodologies and Questions
An overall research framework is needed to support data collection for the research plots. Some
key best practices include:

 • Labeling system. For effective data collection, label each raised bed, row, and plant
 (e.g., Bed 1, Row 2, Tomato 3) to track data on a per plant basis.
 • Data collection system. Whether using a spreadsheet, paper, or software, collect data on
 a per plant basis, include each research variable, and record the date.
 • Site journal. To keep track of important events throughout the season (e.g., day of

4 Regulatory and Permitting
Although regulatory and permitting requirements apply to biodigesters of all sizes, the permitting
process in Puerto Rico and the United States for small-scale operations is often easier and more
streamlined than for larger projects. The facility’s location and size, whether it is underground or
above ground, the type of feedstock used, and whether there is co-digestion are all factors that
dictate which permits and regulatory requirements apply. In most cases, biogas and digestate end
uses are also regulated. Whether federal or state funding (e.g., U.S. Department of Agriculture
[USDA] grant) is used for the project could also trigger regulatory review in some cases.

Using CMTAS’s current operations and goals as a baseline, an analysis of U.S. federal, Puerto
Rican, and local government laws and regulations was conducted to inform the expansion of on-
farm biogas use for different applications, the compression and bottling of biogas for sale, and
the production and sale of biofertilizers. The review focused on applicable legal requirements
and restrictions, guidance, and best practices for four categories: (1) facility siting, land use, and
land access; (2) facility construction; (3) facility operations; and (4) on-farm biogas use and the
commercialization of fertilizer (Figure 17). Details on individual regulatory and permitting
considerations relevant to on-farm micro- and small-scale AD operations in Puerto Rico and
additional guidance regarding bottling biogas for sale and the commercialization of biofertilizer
are included in Annex 6.

Analysis of five micro- and small-scale AD operations in the United States was also conducted
to provide regulatory and permitting insight for CMTAS’s future development opportunities and
operational scaling potential. The case studies provided permitting and regulatory insights to site,
construct, and operate digesters of different sizes and varying feedstocks as well as permitting
guidance for digestate end uses. A matrix detailing the individual case studies analyzed is

4.2 Spotlight: Fertilizer Manufacturing and Distribution
The formula of an AD digestate or biofertilizer product (e.g., potentially BioFert) manufactured
or distributed for use in Puerto Rico must first be approved by the Secretary of Agriculture. If the
formula is approved, then the Secretary, in consultation with the Fertilizer Board, determines the
biofertilizer’s grade (see Table 7), which then dictates which labeling and quality standards
apply. 102

The grade and formula of all commercial fertilizers or soil additives imported,
manufactured, or distributed in Puerto Rico must have their grade and formula approved
by the Secretary of Agriculture. 103,104

AD digestate or biofertilizer formulas typically must meet the standard nutrient requirements—
24% of the total product must constitute three primary macronutrients: nitrogen (N),
assimilable phosphoric acid (P2O5), and/or potash (K2O). 105,106,107 If the AD digestate or
biofertilizer does not meet nutrient standards, the Puerto Rico Secretary of Agriculture could, on
a case-by-case basis, allow for an exception (5 PR §526) or grant an exemption (5 PR §520(c)) to
the formula and grade approval requirement. 108 ,109 The Secretary of Agriculture, in consultation
with the Fertilizer Board, may allow an exception for specific fertilizers that do not meet the
standard nutrient requirements if they deem the fertilizer “necessary and convenient” to
manufacture or distribute for use in Puerto Rico. 110,111 Puerto Rico regulations provide that
“specialized fertilizers” or “raw materials for fertilizers” (defined in Table 7) are examples of
commercial fertilizers that might not meet the standard nutrient requirements but on a case-by-
case basis might be approved by the Secretary of Agriculture if they are considered “necessary
and convenient” to manufacture or distribute for use in Puerto Rico. 112

In addition, if an AD digestate or biofertilizer were manufactured for, or distributed to, a specific
consumer (according to that individual or class of consumer’s specifications), a consumer-
specific exemption to the grade approval requirement might be issued by the Puerto Rico
Secretary of Agriculture. 113 In all cases, whether the AD digestate or biofertilizer meets the
nutrient standards or if an exception or exemption might apply, the decision is made by the
Secretary of Agriculture, in consultation with the Fertilizer Board, on a case-by-case basis. For
example, manufacturers and distributors of commercial fertilizers or soil additives are required to
label each container with specific information, including “the minimum grade or guaranteed

 5 Financing, Funding, and Proposal Development
 Despite the potential impacts and alignment of AD with the challenges faced by communities in
 rural Puerto Rico, AD projects, particularly at the small scale, often encounter barriers to
 securing financing. Projects of this scale often fall into a financing gap—they are too small for
 traditional infrastructure investment from banks and other investors, yet they are potentially too
 capital-intensive for grassroots implementation without external support. This can create a
 funding void where small-scale projects struggle to take off beyond the conceptual or pilot
 phase.

 A major challenge when seeking this type of project financing is that many financing providers
 have limited familiarity with AD projects compared to other more widely deployed and
 commercialized technologies, including gas, generators, solar, wind, and energy efficiency.
 Financing providers are often hesitant to invest in projects that involve technologies or risks they
 do not fully understand, and the relatively lower standardization of project structure and
 financing in the AD space can exacerbate that hesitation. This means that developers often need
 to approach financing creatively and educate investors on how the project’s technical and
 financial elements work. This can include clearly communicating how biodigesters work, how
 biogas and other byproducts are used, and how the project generates economic and/or social
 returns over time.

 This section provides an overview of potential financing pathways for small-scale AD projects as
 well as key best practices for developing financing proposals for AD projects.

 variety of financing providers that could potentially support AD projects, each with different
 requirements, typical focuses and missions, and financial product offerings (e.g., grant, loan, or

7 Discussion, Key Takeaways and Considerations
7.1 Discussion
This assessment demonstrates that small-scale AD systems, when thoughtfully implemented, can
deliver benefits to rural and agricultural communities in Puerto Rico. Drawing from the CMTAS
experience, practical insights can be gleaned regarding technical performance, social and market
challenges, and regulatory/policy considerations surrounding small-scale AD projects.

Technical Considerations and Lessons Learned

Small-digester systems successfully convert a range of organic waste streams—such as livestock
manure, food scraps, fish discard, and crop residues—into usable biogas and digestate using
simple materials and processes. Field observations confirm that with simple training, local
operators can safely and effectively manage these systems, even without prior experience. The
digesters can help reduce on-farm waste, enhance waste management practices, and support
additional value streams for communities.

However, the analysis also highlighted some technical limitations. Nutrient concentrations in the
potential CMTAS BioFert product 146, especially phosphorus, are up to three orders of magnitude
lower than in commercial fertilizers, making the digestate less viable for stand-alone
commercialization, particularly for high-nutrient-demand crops. Potential pathways for
improving concentrations (e.g., co-digestion and post-processing treatment) will likely need
further exploration, and the final potential BioFert product could be evaluated in controlled trials
in the living laboratory to better understand the commercialization potential for the biofertilizer,
which could potentially provide an additional revenue stream for small-digester projects if the
regulatory requirements for sale can be met.

Social and Community Engagement

Learning from the CMTAS experience, community engagement has been found to be central for
successful project implementation, highlighting trust-building, clear communication, and hands-
on participation from community members and farm operators as essential for community
acceptance. Locally appropriate technologies, when paired with effective training and support,
can be transformative for communities. The social dimension can be as critical as the
engineering design in ensuring long-term adoption and success of an AD project.

Institutional and Regulatory Barriers

Small-digester projects potentially have a patchwork of regulatory and permitting requirements,
depending on local laws for the four potential project aspects: siting and land use, construction,
operations, and sales of biogas and fertilizer. Given the variability, those seeking to implement

AD projects need to proactively work to understand relevant requirements by engaging with

Although CMTAS was able to advance the Peñuelas pig farm AD project, broader and

permitting processes could help reduce delays and increase confidence among prospective
project developers. Community organizations such as CMTAS could work with local regulators
to develop a permitting template or handbook that could assist other farmers looking to adopt
this technology in the future. Technical studies like this, which highlight the regulatory
considerations and processes, can inform policy updates and help support wider adoption.

7.2 Key Takeaways
The following key takeaways summarize the broader significance of this project, the strategic
role of funders and institutions in scaling similar efforts, and the opportunity that small-scale AD
projects offer for strengthening communities in Puerto Rico, emphasizing its replicability and
alignment with national objectives.

 • Small-scale AD systems can offer locally managed solutions to meet critical energy
 and waste management needs. At CMTAS sites and across Puerto Rico, biodigesters
 can help transform organic waste into biogas, which could help provide critical cooking,
 backup electricity, and water heating services during grid outages and reduce reliance on
 propane deliveries disrupted by storms or fuel shortages. These projects can show how
 small-scale systems can deliver reliable, decentralized infrastructure in regions where
 hurricanes, power outages, and fuel disruptions are common.
 • Small-scale biodigesters can transform organic waste into usable products for
 farmers and communities. When deployed in rural or semi-rural settings, these systems
 can reduce on-farm waste volumes, improve sanitation, and produce nutrient-rich soil
 amendment products that can enhance soil quality and crop productivity. For farmers and
 cooperatives, this can create a closed-loop system where waste becomes a resource,
 supporting both agricultural efficiency and local economic value.
 • The CMTAS project offers one potential model for other small farms, agricultural
 collectives, fishing cooperatives, and other types of rural communities in Puerto Rico,
 and similar small islands. It demonstrates how locally appropriate technologies, when
 coupled with training and technical support, can deliver tangible benefits for
 communities.
 • Targeted support from institutions (e.g., for training, regulatory guidance, technical
 assistance) can be as important as direct investment in general. Many of the challenges
 observed for these small-scale projects can be surmountable through knowledge transfer
 and local capacity building.
 • Catalytic investment is critical to bridge the gap between early-stage pilots and broader
 adoption. For many farmers and cooperatives, access to upfront capital remains a
 significant barrier. There are potentially a variety of funders that could be interested in
 small-scale digesters—including microfinance institutions, credit unions/cooperativas,

 45

 loan funds, green banks, commercial banks, philanthropies, and the USDA, among
 others. Specific funding partners will vary depending on the planned technology and
 scale, site-specific context, the entity looking for financing, and other elements.
 • A coordinated finance and policy ecosystem is needed to support this new sector, from
 smallholder incentives and equipment financing to permitting assistance and institutional
 procurement programs. This could also include awareness building with local finance
 institutions in Puerto Rico to better understand AD technology and de-risk projects for
 investment.

7.3 Leveraging This Work
Looking ahead, several pathways can be considered for different stakeholders:

 • CMTAS (and other community-based project developers): CMTAS could document
 and share operational experiences to inform the refinement of both technical processes
 and community engagement strategies. This could include improving data collection
 protocols, defining metrics and completing third-party-verified studies to track biogas
 yield and digestate performance, and identifying operational bottlenecks on system
 performance. Lessons learned from the CMTAS sites could be synthesized and shared
 with potential adopters, including farmer cooperatives, communities, and
 nongovernmental organizations. Establishing performance benchmarks could also
 strengthen funding applications and partnerships.
 • Agricultural communities and smallholder farmers: For farmers, small-scale
 biodigesters offer a pathway to energy security during emergencies, enhanced waste
 management, and potentially soil enhancement to support different crops. These systems
 are particularly valuable for areas with unstable grid access or high agricultural input
 costs. Technical training and capacity building are essential to support adoption and
 successful operation over time.
 • Local communities: Wider community benefits include more effective organic waste
 management, improved air quality, and enhanced energy reliability in rural and semirural
 areas. Biodigesters can also support community kitchens, schools, or other shared-use
 facilities; however, successful community integration requires early engagement, clear
 definition of shared responsibilities, and strategies to build technical familiarity and trust
 among users.
 • Other rural or peri-urban communities: For communities beyond the current project
 sites, this report provides context and considerations that can be adapted based on
 feedstock availability, energy demand, land use, and local community dynamics. Locally
 appropriate feasibility studies could precede replication to evaluate the practicality of
 biodigesters based on technical, social, and economic conditions. Community interest and
 willingness to participate in ongoing maintenance and monitoring are essential to success.
 Collaborations with technical institutions and experienced implementers such as CMTAS
 can help streamline implementation and reduce risks.
 • Universities and research institutions: Academic institutions could integrate
 biodigester projects into curricula and research programs focused on agriculture, waste-
 to-energy systems, and community energy security strategies. Field-based testing, such as

 46

 CMTAS’s living laboratory, can support innovation in digester design, nutrient recovery,
 and biogas distribution. Universities can also play a key role in workforce development
 by offering technician training and internships linked to ongoing projects.
 • Municipalities and the government of Puerto Rico: Local governments stand to
 benefit from reduced solid waste volumes, reduced water quality impacts on waterways
 due to enhanced waste management, improved local air quality and odor, and
 decentralized infrastructure that lessens dependencies on utilities during emergencies.
 Municipalities can incorporate anaerobic digester projects into waste management plans
 or agricultural support programs, potentially linking them to local economic development
 or food system initiatives. Government agencies could align incentives, especially
 financial incentives, permitting processes, and technical assistance programs to facilitate
 wider adoption, particularly in farming and fishing communities.
 • Regulatory authorities: Regulators have a critical role to play in clarifying rules related
 to the safe use of biogas and the classification of digestate as a usable fertilizer. Current
 ambiguities in permitting and compliance can delay projects. Clear, risk-focused
 guidelines tailored to small-scale systems are needed to provide legal certainty while
 maintaining safety and environmental standards.
 • Ecotourism Operators
 Tourism operators can integrate small-scale AD systems into sustainability
 demonstrations that attract travelers. These systems offer practical examples of organic
 waste management, on-site energy generation, and emergency response. By incorporating
 anaerobic digesters into tours and educational programs, operators can strengthen their
 brand while also benefiting from lower fuel costs and organic waste disposal solutions.
Projects like CMTAS are not only technically viable but also socially and economically strategic.
With the right enabling environment and targeted funding support, these initiatives can become
replicable models across the island, contributing meaningfully to a more self-reliant and secure
Puerto Rico.

production rate inputs from the site itself and would not serve as an independent validation

 • Current digesters on-site: three digesters with two gasometers each at Yauco; one
 digester with two gasometers at Peñuelas

 area producing 1.3 m3 of biogas/day/gasometer (provided by CMTAS for the Peñuelas
 site)
 • Input: Based on the CMTAS Peñuelas site, 1:3 manure-to-wastewater ratio. The

Land Use Plans and Zoning

In Puerto Rico, land use planning is a collaborative process between the Government of Puerto
Rico and municipalities. The 2015 Land Use Plan 200 is a planning instrument that governs at the
macro level the public policy on the use of land in Puerto Rico, including designating lands for
development and conservation. A flood zone assessment is also required to ensure compliance
with the Puerto Rico Planning Board’s flood-prone zone regulation. Some local governments,
including Guánica and Yauco, also have territory-specific land use plans and regulate
development activities through zoning ordinances that require compliance with setback, odor and
noise requirements, and restrictions relevant to small-scale AD and biogas operations (Table 20).

In Puerto Rico, small-scale AD and biogas production operations at or below 2 tons/year of any

Puerto Rico’s clean air regulations. Criteria pollutants are limited to ozone, particulate matter,
carbon monoxide, lead, sulfur dioxide, and nitrogen oxides. HAP emissions listed in Appendix A
of PR Reg 5300 are not applicable. Methane and carbon dioxide are not criteria pollutants and

8.9 Annex 9: Pitfalls and Best Practices for AD Project Proposal
 Development
Across the development cycle for a biogas proposal, there are several potential pitfalls and
related best practices for the development of a successful proposal. These are synthesized from
the guidance, literature, and applied lessons included throughout the report, particularly Sections
5 (“Financing, Funding, and Proposal Development”) and 6 (“Risks and Mitigation”), as well as
NLR’s technical assistance experience supporting small-scale anaerobic digestion and
community energy projects globally. Although not direct quotations, these pitfalls and
recommendations also pull from international and U.S. sources, including US EPA AgSTAR 379,
FAO/IRENA Renewable energy for agri-food systems 380, Advancing Bioenergy for Sustainable
Development Guideline for Policymakers and Investors Volumes I, II, and III 381.

8.9.1 Designing and Planning the Project
Weak community engagement:
 • Pitfall: assuming communities or farm workers will support or maintain the system
 without their input or buy-in
 • Best practices:
 o Involve local stakeholders and beneficiaries early.
 o Include training, clear roles, and ongoing engagement during the development of
 your funding proposal.
Ignoring nonfinancial barriers:
 • Pitfall: focusing only on money and ignoring potential obstacles around access to land,
 technical knowledge, or permits
 • Best practices:
 o Build a comprehensive project readiness plan, not just a financial one.
 o Address potential project barriers early and often.
Lack of institutional alignment:
 • Pitfall: Projects are proposed without coordination with local governments or utility
 programs, causing legal or technical conflicts.
 • Best practices:
 o Review local regulations early.

Underestimating the total project costs:
 • Pitfall: only accounting for equipment costs without including installation, permitting,
 training, operation, maintenance, or contingencies in budget estimates
 • Best practices:
 o Always include a 10–20% contingency 382 and account for full life cycle costs
 from day one.
 o Adjust the budget as the project develops and cost uncertainty decreases.
Relying on a single source of funding:
 • Pitfall: assuming one grant or donor will provide sufficient funding to cover the entire
 project
 • Best practices:
 o Consider combining multiple sources (e.g., small grant + community contribution
 + technical assistance) to cover both project capital costs as well as ongoing
 operations and maintenance.
 o Prepare backup funding scenarios.
 o If seeking a loan, explore multiple lenders to ensure the best possible loan terms.
Applying to the wrong programs:
 • Pitfall: pursuing grants not designed for small projects or that require too much
 cofinancing.
 • Best practices:
 o Screen programs for size, eligibility, timeline, match requirements, and technical
 focus. Apply to programs with a strong fit.
 o Reach out directly to the program administrator early if you have any clarifying

8.9.3 Writing the Funding Application
Using vague or complicated language:
 • Pitfall: Proposals and communications that are too wordy, technical, or unclear can
 confuse application reviewers and weaken your message.
 • Best practices:
 o Write clearly and concisely.
 o Use plain language, when possible, so that non-technical audiences can
 understand.
 o Highlight key points and takeaways upfront.
Poor financial documentation:
 • Pitfall: submitting proposals without clear budgets, payback analysis, or revenue
 assumptions
 • Best practices:
 o Prepare a simple but clear project budget.
 o Ensure that all inputs and assumptions are clearly documented and supported.
 o Describe how savings or income will support the project’s financial sustainability.

8.9.4 Implementing and Operating the Project
Waiting too long to start:
 • Pitfall: delaying action until the funding is “perfect.”
 • Best practices:
 o Start small, build credibility via pilot projects, and document results.
 o Build proof and momentum for broader rollout and future scalability.
 o Scale projects modularly, proving what is possible as you go.
Overlooking operations and maintenance:
 • Pitfall: no budget or plan for ongoing operations and maintenance, leading to system
 failure or major gaps in operation
 • Best practices:
 o Include a clear operations and maintenance plan with roles, costs, and potential
 revenue sources (e.g., sale of fertilizer and/or biogas).
 o Plan for unforeseen decreases in revenue by creating a backup plan for funding
 operations and maintenance.
Failing to sufficiently de-risk the project:
 • Pitfall: project appears too risky to funders and partners, leading to funding rejection or
 hesitation

 86

 • Best practices:
 o Actively de-risk by identifying potential project risks (e.g., technical, feedstock)
 and presenting clear mitigation measures.
 o Start with small pilots and show alignment with proven approaches or successful
 partners.
Treating funders as one-time transactional partners:
 • Pitfall: only contacting funders when asking for money, which limits trust and long-term
 support
 • Best practices:
 o Cultivate relationships with funders by communicating regularly and
 transparently, not just when asking for money.
 o Be responsive and provide updates on successes and challenges.
