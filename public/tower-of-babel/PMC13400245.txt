Reasons for refusal of genetic testing in the evaluation of autism spectrum disorder and intellectual developmental disorder with longitudinal follow-up on test completion
Authors: Kai Russell, Kyla Arcebido, Andryella Maxie Ibarra, Sabrina Barella, Amelia Evans, Sohum Shinkre, Takahiro Soda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAutism and Intellectual and Developmental Disorder (IDD) are neurodevelopmental conditions influenced by genetic factors. Health organizations recommend genetic testing as part of medical care for individuals diagnosed with autism and IDD. Genetic etiologies can improve counseling accuracy and refine patient treatment, but despite the benefits, studies show that patients often decline clinically offered testing.MethodsWe analyzed data from 7539 health records of autistic individuals with IDD and identified 111 instances with documented refusal of genetic testing (GT). Then, we rechecked the charts with previously documented refusal 4 years later for subsequent acceptance.ResultsReasons for refusal were “access issues” (46.8%) predominantly related to “insurance/cost,” “personal concerns” (21.6%) regarding “procedure” or “no interest,” and “deferred” (19.8%); some refusals were “not specified” (13.5%). Of those individuals who initially declined genetic testing, 29 (26.1%) obtained testing upon follow-up review; the initial reason for refusal was not predictive of later completion of tests.ConclusionThese findings indicate a need to address external barriers, such as cost and insurance. Reducing discomfort and increasing accessibility to information regarding autism/IDD GT benefits, importance, and alternative DNA collection methods could also increase awareness, interest, and access to testing. Longitudinal results indicate that providers should follow up on GT for all autism/IDD patients who decline it.Keywords: Autism, Chart review, Intellectual/Developmental disorder, Genetic testing, Health disparities

Introduction

Autism is characterized by social communication challenges and repetitive patterns of behavior.1 It affects approximately 1 in 100 children globally and 1 in 31 in the US2,3 and is about 3.4 times more predominant in males.3,4 This neurodevelopmental condition encompasses a wide, complex spectrum of disability that manifests in an array of presentations unique to each patient, often with cooccurring conditions.5,6 It has high cooccurrence rates with genetic, neurological, sleep, gastrointestinal, and immune disorders.6

Although both genetic and environmental factors contribute to autism etiology, previous studies suggest that genetic factors play a more significant role as well.7, 8, 9, 10 Thus, genetic testing (GT)can help identify specific etiologies of autism, supporting more accurate diagnoses and tailored interventions.11,12 Currently, GT for autism is recommended by the American Academy of Pediatrics,13 the American College of Medical Genetics,14 and the American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry.15 Recommended tests include Fragile X, chromosomal microarray, and exome Sequencing15; however, as technology advances and costs decrease,16 guidelines increasingly favor exome and genome Sequencing.15,17 Identifying the etiology of the patient’s autism can refine treatment options,18 enable awareness of and early intervention for genotype-associated medical conditions,18 and facilitate more targeted services and support for patients and their families. GT can also help avoid unnecessary, costly, or uncomfortable diagnostic procedures.18

Despite recommendations made by these professional organizations and the multiple benefits, GT remains underutilized.19 Reasons for the low rates of GT may be because of barriers on both the patient’s side and the physician’s side. For example, physicians may not offer genetic tests because of limited awareness of recommendations or difficulty in ordering and interpreting them.19,20 Previous studies that interviewed caregivers and/or patients identified barriers to GT, including financial constraints, distrust in the medical system, psychological burden, privacy concerns, and possible family conflict.18,21,22 Adults with autism and some families of autistic individuals also believe that GT results would not improve quality of life or health outcomes, could create unnecessary stigma, and could negatively affect their insurance coverage.21,23

For younger patients, caregivers may want to defer GT until the patient is old enough to make an informed decision.21 Multiple studies have been performed addressing attitudes about GT and patient-side barriers to receiving GT.21,24 However, these studies particularly focused on why autistic individuals or their caregivers would decline GT when asked in a research-based context, rather than their reasons for declining a test when it is offered clinically.

Intellectual developmental disorder (IDD) is a neurodevelopmental condition marked by intelligence-related deficits and delays or deficits in adaptive behaviors.25 IDD impacts approximately 1% to 3% of the US population and has similar GT recommendations for clinical GT as autism populations. The cooccurrence of autism and IDD also increases the likelihood of an individual having received GT, as uptake in testing is 7 times more common in individuals diagnosed with both autism and intellectual disability than in those diagnosed with autism only, and 2 times more common than in those diagnosed with an intellectual disability only. Despite compounding effects and a slightly higher uptake in testing compared with autism-only populations, GT surrounding IDD remains underutilized, with testing completed in only 13.09% to 43.3% of cases.25,26 Even less research has been done on the reasons why this population declines GT.

Our study aims to analyze the reasons why patients and/or their caregivers have declined GT from their physician, and gain a deeper understanding of patient-side barriers to GT for autism and IDD.

Materials and Methods

A retrospective review was previously conducted on 7539 electronic health records (EHRs) of patients diagnosed with autism or IDD that interfaced with a large health care system across Florida and Georgia over a 7-month period (January-July 2021).20 If the free text and discrete elements of the EHR met the following criteria: (1) a diagnosis of autism and/or IDD, (2) clinical evidence of a genetic test being offered, and (3) evidence that the test was subsequently declined or expired (not completed within the year it was ordered), they were included in this analysis. The diagnoses of autism/IDD were recorded, as well as the patient’s age at the time of refusal and subsequent acceptance, if applicable.

Data were abstracted from the EHR and stored in REDCap,27,28 a HIPAA-compliant database shared only with active approved researchers in a data collection template (see “Supplementary Material”).20 A team of 8 researchers initially coded and analyzed each declined response. After initial coding, all researchers met to identify, analyze, and finalize codes by consensus. Some patients had more than one code assigned to them because of the multifaceted nature of their responses. Finalized codes were tallied to determine the total responses per code. A thematic analysis was conducted by 3 researchers, resulting in categories based on identified themes. Counts represent each code corresponding to a particular category. The EHR was reviewed again in December 2025 to ensure data were kept up to date and relevant.

In addition to the inclusion criteria, information on the patient’s gender, sex assigned at birth, race, ethnicity, and insurance status/type was also collected. Information was captured in 1 of 3 formats: multiple choice, multiple selection, and free response. If a test had been discussed by a clinician or ordered, this was recorded in a multiple-choice response format. A separate question documented when someone had declined GT. When patients/guardians declined GT, reasons were extracted from the EHR in free-response format, if available.

To maintain patient confidentiality, each patient was assigned a record ID that was not contained within the medical records. No other identifiers, such as doctors’ names, dates of birth, addresses, or any other information that was not related to the above demographics, were captured. Certain demographics in race and gender were further de-identified and grouped as “other” if there were fewer than 5 respondents in the original demographic.

Codes were created under overarching themes in people’s decision-making and were further categorized based on the shared features between codes. To establish credibility in our study, all final coding decisions were determined collectively during meetings to reduce bias. Other research on the refusal of GT was consulted to verify consistency.29

In December 2025, 2 researchers rereviewed the charts of those who had previously declined GT to determine if a test was later ordered and completed, and then tallied the completions into the previously declined categories. Results were grouped and analyzed based on the reasons recorded before declining GT. Upon identification, a 4 × 2 Fisher exact test was performed, comparing the total number of individuals who declined GT for each code category with the number of individuals who initially declined but later completed GT in the same category. The α value was adjusted for multiple comparisons (0.05/4 = 0.0125). We also compared individuals diagnosed with an IDD and individuals diagnosed with autism using a 2 × 2 Fisher exact test to assess if future receptivity to testing after declining differed between those with an IDD diagnosis and those without. Subsequent analysis using a 4 × 2 Fisher exact test was performed comparing adults vs children and autism vs. IDD vs both to determine if there was a significant difference in the distribution of reasons for refusal among these identifiers. Additionally, a chi-squared test was performed comparing the insurance types of those who declined GT because of insurance/cost to the original dataset published.20 All statistical analyses were done using R version 4.5.2.30

Results

We identified 111 individuals who were clinically offered GT and subsequently declined. Of the individuals identified, 36 were diagnosed with autism only, 12 with IDD only, and 63 were diagnosed with both. In addition, 93 identified individuals were children at the time of documented refusal, whereas 18 individuals were adults. Most child patients had both autism and IDD and were, on average, 8 years old when refusal was first documented. The age of refusal ranged from <1 year to 17 years. The adults with documented refusals mainly had autism only, without IDD, and had their first documented refusal at age 27 years. The first documented refusal ranged from 18 to 53 years. Analysis of code distribution demonstrated no significant difference in the distribution of reasons for refusal when comparing those with autism, IDD, and both diagnoses who declined testing (P = .3226), as well as those who later received testing (P = .09883). The distribution of codes and subsequent analysis between diagnoses is displayed in Table 1. The distribution of codes amongst adults and children is shown in Table 2. There was also no significant difference in the distribution of reasons for refusal amongst adults and children for both those who declined (P = .07063) and those who received testing later (P = .3042). Of those with an IDD diagnosis or both diagnoses, 32% of individuals later received testing, compared with 13.9% of individuals diagnosed with autism only. No significant difference was found in the likelihood of individuals with an IDD diagnosis or both vs those with an autism-only diagnosis to later pursue testing (P = .06353). The demographics of all individuals in the sample, the chart review, and the health care system are shown in Table 3. Most patients in our study were male gender and sex, white, non-Hispanic, and had Medicaid-type insurance. The number of patients’ reasons for declining GT for the categories and codes identified are shown in Figure 1. Of the 111 patients analyzed, 113 documented codes, 4 consolidated categories (“access issue,” “personal concern,” “deferred,” and “not specified”), and 11 individual code categories were identified.

Flowchartof the documented reasons why patients and/or their families declined genetic testing (GT) when it was offered as a part of the diagnostic process for autism and/or other intellectual developmental disorders. These reasons were thematically coded into 11 individual categories, then grouped into 4 major themes. The number of patients in each category is contained within parentheses.

The access issue” category was the largest barrier to GT for those who declined (46.8%). Access was further divided into 2 subcategories: “insurance/cost” and “GT process.” The “insurance/cost” subcategory was defined as the cost of GT not being covered by insurance or out-of-pocket costs being too expensive for the patient or family, regardless of insurance coverage. The main barrier that caused people to decline testing was insurance/cost issues. The following are sample responses from the “insurance/cost” subcategory: “inactive insurance,” “insurance denial (exome with copy number variant evaluation trio),” and “genetics has requested... exome sequencing, but insurance would not cover,” and “denial of GT by insurance.” The “GT process” subcategory was noted if a genetic test was not done because of procedural or physical barriers to obtaining GT after a recommendation. Examples of this code include “Mother of child lost prescription for chromosome study, and GT was never completed” and “exome sequencing had been discussed but not pursued because of the family’s upcoming move.”

The second category, “personal concern,” was divided into 2 subcategories: “no interest” and “procedure.” These subcategories were then divided further to account for the variety in each subcategory. “No interest (family)” was attributed to responses in which the family was not interested in pursuing GT. Examples include: “parents showed their lack of interest in pursuing neuroimaging and chromosome study,” “mother is not interested,” and “adoptive mom has declined any genetic workup since it wouldn’t change the treatment and doesn’t want to do blood work.” Another code was “no interest (patient),” in which the patient was not interested in pursuing GT. An example under this category is: “Hard to get consent from patient; compliance issues; patient is in clear disagreement with the family and the physician; and he questioned the clinician’s education level. It will be very difficult to get the patient to cooperate with the workup and treatment.”

The code “no interest (general)” corresponded to 1 patient, where it was unclear whether the lack of interest was on behalf of the patient or their caregiver.

The “procedure” code was further divided into “patient discomfort,” “familial discomfort,” and “physician preference.” “Patient discomfort” was detailed as the procedure of GT entailing discomfort for the patient. Examples include documentation such as “patient refuses to have his blood drawn,” “patient fainted before they could draw blood for Fragile X testing,” and “patient dislikes blood tests.” “Familial discomfort” was characterized as the family of the patient being uncomfortable with the GT procedure or uncomfortable with subjecting the patient to this procedure. Example responses in this category include “familial concerns over phlebotomy sample collection,” “mom worries about the blood test,” and “MOC does not want to overwhelm him or herself.” Lastly, “physician preference” was defined as the physician preferring a specific physician to conduct the GT or being concerned with conducting the GT with another physician. A response from this code is “Mother wanted to get recommended genetic tests done through the primary care provider.”

The “deferred” and “not specified” categories had no subcategories. Responses coded as “deferred” were defined as responses in which the family or patient wished to consider GT later and did not want to proceed with GT at that time. Examples of “deferred” responses include: “Mom will think about it and call back if she wants a genetics referral,” “they would like to hold off on this evaluation as they are unsure how it would potentially benefit her treatment,” and “family declined genetics consult or further workup at this time and would prefer to monitor.”

“Not specified” was characterized as having no reason given for the denial of GT, or very little reason for declining was provided. Table 4 shows the insurance types of patients who declined GT because of an access issue with insurance or cost. Among this group of patients, Medicaid was the most commonly recorded health insurance plan (67.4%). Private insurance was the second most common (37%). Insurance type was not predictive of GT refusal relative to its frequency in the population (P = .7459).

Discussion

Preexisting literature surrounding GT in the evaluation of autism and IDD is limited. Research regarding GT for autism focuses mainly on attitudes and perceived barriers reported by autistic individuals or their caregivers in a research context, which is not wholly applicable to the study of clinical GT. Within our dataset, we did not see instances of clinicians offering research as an alternative to clinical testing for declined reasons for clinical testing. The clinical practice in recommending testing is that opportunities to participate in research are mentioned but not presented as an alternative; such a suggestion would raise ethical concerns about undue influence to participate in research. Previous studies have noted concerns about genetic discrimination that may put individuals at risk of losing health insurance benefits, psychological burden, and privacy issues.21,24 Others report caregiver and patient hesitancy toward GT because of psychological burden, financial constraints, privacy concerns, and doubts about its impact on care. Similar studies regarding individuals with IDD focus predominantly on prenatal testing, not clinically-based GT.31 However, none of these studies directly assesses reasons for refusing testing when offered in a clinical setting.

In contrast, there is abundant literature that examines patients’ reasons for declining GT or counseling services for cancer. One study reported that some of the most common reasons patients declined cancer genetic counseling services included concern about health insurability, cost, no perceived benefit from having the service done, and concern about the emotional impact on the patient or family,32 whereas another study on genome sequencing refusal similarly reported concerns about privacy, discrimination, and psychological burden.29 A study on predictive GT for Lynch syndrome reported reasons including a lack of perceived benefit, insurance concerns, potential anxiety from results, inconvenience, and the test being a low-priority test.33

The most prevalent reason given by adults and caregivers of children with autism, IDD, or both for declining GT in our study was “access issues,” predominantly related to insurance and cost. Insurance coverage is particularly complex because of the delays in the authorization process for GT and meeting the restrictive timeframes for obtaining DNA samples required to receive coverage.34 Previous literature on autism and IDD-related GT also noted that insurance, cost, and health care accessibility are significant barriers influencing patients’ decisions to pursue GT.22,31,35 A retrospective chart review of patients who received genetic counseling for breast cancer noted that a significant percentage of patients declined GT because of financial hardship, with 66% of these patients covered by Medicare or Medicaid.36 In our study, about 67.4% of patients who declined GT because of “access issues” related to insurance or cost had Medicaid listed as their insurance, compared with 71.9% of individuals with autism/IDD whose charts were reviewed for completion of GT. Unlike GT for breast cancer, insurance type is not a significant determining factor in whether patients decline testing because of insurance/cost issues. The large proportion of individuals who declined testing because of insurance and cost, combined with these findings, highlights the need to address the financial aspect of GT. Coverage and out-of-pocket costs vary regardless of insurance type; therefore, reexamining policies regarding GT for all insurance types may help increase accessibility and facilitate patient health care. By increasing coverage for GT and counseling, health care providers can order necessary tests that allow patients and families to access appropriate care based on their needs. Although costs vary, patients who receive positive GT results can obtain appropriate monitoring and preventive services, which can reduce or avert long-term treatment costs. For patients and families concerned about insurance costs after undergoing GT, health care professionals can provide information on protective legislation, such as the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008, which prevents health insurance from adjusting premiums based on genetic information.37 In particular, for Florida, where this study primarily takes place, extra protective laws are in place, preventing discrimination related to genetic information for life insurance premiums, disability insurance, and long-term care policies. Other states also have varying levels of protection related to genetic information discrimination in insurance policies.38 Studies show that knowledge related to laws protecting against genetic discrimination by insurance policies is low, despite being ranked high in importance for most individuals.39,40 Assuaging these concerns can help individuals feel more comfortable pursuing essential health care without worrying about losing coverage.

The second most common reason reported in this study was personal concerns leading to refusal of recommended GT. Many responses indicated that patients or their decision-making family members were not interested in pursuing GT. Others communicated how patients or their family members were uncomfortable with the testing procedure. Our results corroborate previous literature that internal factors, such as general disinterest or fear of needles, can impact a patient’s decision to pursue GT. Studies on GT for cancer identified lack of interest as a reason for refusal,41 whereas other studies on chromosomal microarray testing cited fear of needles as a barrier.42 An additional aspect that may have contributed to personal concerns within our study is the current concerns surrounding GT for autism in adult populations and caregivers of children with autism. Studies have shown that many adults with autism express concerns about improper usage of genetic results, like eugenics, an increase in stigma against autism, and a perceived lack of personal benefit.23,43 Caregivers of autistic individuals, on the other hand, typically possess positive attitudes of GT on average and agree with the potential benefits.44 However, concerns regarding potential family conflicts, conflict with one’s religious or cultural beliefs, patient distress, lack of direct benefit, and causing more harm than good create hesitancy.44 These reasons highlight concerns that may be addressed by fully informing patients and families about clinical GT, its benefits, and potential physical and psychological risks, including uncomfortable procedures or emotional distress. Procedural concerns can be reduced by alternative sampling methods, such as buccal swabs, using discarded samples, or coordinating blood draws with other necessary phlebotomy procedures.

In our study, 22 patients chose to defer GT. For these patients, providers may offer the option to follow up on GT again in the future. Current insurance practices that only provide approvals for GT within a short timeframe are counterintuitive to these patients’ desires to defer such testing. Additional factors, such as the timing of preconsent, provider interactions, and rapport, can influence whether patients perceive testing as more burdensome than beneficial. Notable cases of deferrals in our study cite various reasons for deferring. One case mentioned that they wanted to “think about it” before deciding. Another deferred because they were “unsure how it would benefit treatment.” Several cases opted to “hold off” on testing because of timing, patient age, or wanting to monitor. Proper counseling regarding the potential benefits of testing and follow-ups with individuals at later dates, when circumstances change, may prove beneficial in creating interest and giving opportunities for individuals who defer testing to reconsider their initial deferral.

After conducting a prospective longitudinal follow-up of these individuals who initially declined GT, we found that 29 patients subsequently obtained GT after initially refusing. Those diagnosed with autism only compared with those with IDD or both, showed no significant difference in rates of later GT uptake. Additionally, those who reconsidered and later completed testing had a variety of reasons for refusal originally, not significantly belonging to one category of codes. This was unexpected, as previous studies have shown that individuals with IDD are more likely to receive GT than those with ASD only.25,26 It is also a reasonable assumption that patients who deferred would be more willing to reconsider GT than patients who refused, since deferrals mean that the patient was postponing their decision on testing rather than outright refusing it. In addition, the same could be said for “access issues,” as it is common for individuals to seek out insurance coverage that covers a particular procedure to access care. Financial status can also change with time, allowing patients to afford testing out of pocket. In addition, individuals can choose to work with different labs or utilize take-home kits. These results indicate that a significant proportion of such patients and families who initially declined GT may be willing to reconsider GT if they are provided with the option later, regardless of why they originally declined testing. Reasons for reconsidering can vary with each patient, such as establishing a trusting relationship with their provider, obtaining insurance that provides coverage for these tests, or having more time to process their diagnosis and the option for undergoing GT. Children can also have differing attitudes towards GT compared with their caregivers and may be willing to consider testing upon reaching adulthood. A similar study assessing patients’ willingness to reconsider cancer GT after initially refusing revealed that patients would choose to pursue it if they were offered the option again after a period of time.45 Several participants in a cancer study regretted initially declining GT; others reported feeling overwhelmed when initially offered, but would choose to undergo it now.45 Though our current study did not allow us to directly ask why individuals ended up getting tested, our results indicate that we should not take initial refusal as a permanent choice, regardless of initial reasons. When procedures are clinically indicated, as with autism and IDD GT, repeat interactions and follow-ups with providers can facilitate completion.

A particular strength of this study is the unique use of a retrospective chart review and nested naturalistic prospective follow-up to understand reasons for declining clinically recommended GT. The chart review captured provider-documented reasons and allowed longitudinal assessment of whether patients later received testing. This study can be expanded in the future to account for a more diverse and larger sample. Using a similar methodology to obtain information from health records from various geographical locations and backgrounds would allow the findings to better account for reasons behind GT refusal for a greater percentage of autistic individuals and individuals with IDD beyond the specific context of the current study. Broadening this study to include a variety of health care systems and settings would allow the measurement of differing ratios of responses or perhaps provide entirely different reasons as to why GT was declined, based on the increased diversity within the target population. In-depth qualitative studies can be conducted to account for the nuances of these findings, such as holding focus groups or conducting interviews with patients who decline GT in clinical settings.

This study also has limitations. Reasons were obtained from provider notes and may reflect the provider’s perspective, potentially paraphrasing the patient’s rationale. In addition, because we used an EHR, dot phrases may have been used. These are shortcuts used to populate prewritten phrases in clinical notes rapidly and can potentially limit our knowledge regarding reasons for refusal, though the variety of responses suggests that most of the documentation was not pre-stored text from the EHR. Because of our data collection method, it is possible that there may have been some ascertainment bias, as most patients interfaced with the health system multiple times. It is important to note that in other contexts outside our study, where repeated interactions with people who can order or recommend testing are lower, there may be fewer opportunities for testing to be recommended, artificially reducing opportunities for individuals to consider or refuse testing and limiting the applicability of our results. Similar things can be said for instances where patients are less medically inclined or generally have fewer interactions with health providers. We are also unaware of how providers counseled patients on GT. Thus, some patients may be more informed about GT than others, which can impact a patient’s decision to decline or pursue GT. Additionally, some reasons were initially interpreted differently by individual reviewers. We mitigated this by directly copying and pasting patient reasons into our database and working until consensus was obtained on an interpretation and categorical code. Our small sample size limited our ability to conduct statistical analyses on subgroups in our datasets. Many subgroups had either few patients or none at all, making it difficult to draw conclusions about or discuss them in depth. Qualitative analysis was done where possible, and unique cases were examined to highlight potential trends in this preliminary study.

This study identified several categories of reasons why patients and caregivers declined autism/IDD GT, as documented in the medical records, such as concerns about insurance, cost, location, lack of interest, and procedural discomfort. These reasons led individuals to decline GT or defer it to a later date after being offered by their providers. Understanding these reasons can help the medical community develop strategies that address these barriers and encourage patients to obtain testing. Autism/IDD GT needs to be made more accessible to patients by improving insurance processes, lowering the cost of testing, reducing risks and discomfort, increasing public awareness and interest, and conducting more research. Implementing these strategies can help increase the rates of GT and facilitate the overall improvement of autism/IDD diagnosis, treatment, and research. In addition, encouraging providers to follow up on GT interest after patients decline for any reason may help increase rates of testing and give patients more opportunities to consider autism/IDD GT if desired. Future research can focus on a larger and more diverse sample using in-depth qualitative data collection methods to produce more reliable and generalizable findings.
