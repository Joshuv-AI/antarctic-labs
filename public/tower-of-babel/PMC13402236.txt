Arthroscopic Primary Repair and Suture Augmentation for Combined Acute Femoral and Tibial Attachment Avulsion of Anterior Cruciate Ligament
Authors: Quanchao Li, Yujia Li, Yuchen He, Weihong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveCombined acute femoral‐ and tibial‐side avulsion injuries of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) are rare and present a distinct surgical challenge because both attachment sites require restoration while preserving the native ligament tissue. This study aimed to describe an arthroscopic ACL primary repair (ACLPR) technique with suture augmentation for the treatment of rare combined acute femoral‐ and tibial‐side ACL avulsion injuries and to present its short‐term clinical outcome.MethodsA 21‐year‐old male patient sustained a twisting injury to his right knee while landing during a basketball game 3 weeks before presentation. He subsequently developed right knee pain and recurrent episodes of giving way and was diagnosed with combined acute femoral‐ and tibial‐side ACL avulsion tears. An arthroscopic ACLPR technique reinforced with suture augmentation was performed. The procedure involved in situ suture capture of both avulsed ACL ends using circumferential cinch‐loop sutures. Femoral fixation was achieved with a polyetheretherketone (PEEK) anchor, while tibial fixation was performed through bone tunnels. A suture augmentation was incorporated to provide secondary stabilization and protect the repair during early healing. A transtibial pullout repair was performed to repair the lateral meniscus posterior root tear through a separate tibial tunnel. The surgical technique, including multi‐tunnel orientation, precise suture management, and secure extracortical fixation, is described in a step‐by‐step manner.ResultsThe technique allowed for anatomical reapproximation of the native ACL and stable fixation of both avulsion sites while minimizing graft harvest morbidity and optimizing biological healing conditions. At the 1‐year follow‐up, satisfactory clinical outcomes were observed, with restoration of knee stability and function, and no major complications reported.ConclusionArthroscopic primary repair with suture augmentation is an optional approach for combined acute femoral and tibial attachment avulsion of the ACL.Keywords: ACL avulsion, anterior cruciate ligament, meniscus tear, primary repair, suture augmentation

Introduction

The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is one of the most commonly injured ligaments in the knee, especially among young active individuals and athletes [1]. The reported annual incidence of ACL tears is about 68.6 per 100,000 person‐years [2]. In the United States, about 1 in 3500 people per year suffer from ACL injury [2, 3]. Current arthroscopic surgical treatment options for ACL injury include ACL reconstruction (ACLR) and ACL primary repair (ACLPR), with or without augmentation [4, 5, 6, 7]. ACL reconstruction remains the gold standard for most mid‐substance ACL tears and chronic injuries [4]. In this process, the torn ligament is replaced with an autograft or allograft to provide reliable restoration of knee stability and long‐term outcomes [8]. In contrast, ACLPR aims to preserve the native ligament by reattaching the torn fibers to their anatomical footprint [9]. It is generally reserved for acute proximal or distal avulsion‐type tears with good tissue quality [10].

Several modern techniques have emerged to advance ACLPR by enhancing biomechanical stability and promoting biologic healing [11], including Suture Anchor Primary ACL Repair (SAPAR) [12], Internal Brace Ligament Augmentation (IBLA) [13], and Dynamic Intraligamentary Stabilization (DIS) [11]. Collectively, these innovations reflect a growing trend toward preserving the native ACL in select cases, potentially narrowing the gap between repair and reconstruction outcomes [10, 14].

While modern arthroscopic ACLPR techniques have demonstrated promising clinical and functional outcomes in carefully selected patients, particularly when combined with augmentation strategies, their effectiveness appears context dependent. Current comparative evidence suggests a higher risk of clinical failure and revision compared with ACLR, although patient‐reported outcomes are often comparable between the two approaches [15, 16]. Challenges and controversies remain regarding ACLPR, particularly in the context of dual end of ACL tears [17].

Therefore, the purpose of this study is (i) to characterize the specific challenges associated with this rare dual‐end femoral and tibial ACL avulsion pattern; (ii) to present a detailed all‐arthroscopic primary repair technique with suture augmentation for the treatment of this special injury, highlighting its technical features and potential advantages; and (iii) to discuss the indications, limitations, and future clinical implications of this technique in the management of selected ACL injuries.

Case Presentation

Ethical Approval and Informed Consent

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of a tertiary medical institution (Approval No. LYF20250243). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient and the patient's legal guardian for participation in this study and for publication of related images and clinical data.

Patient History

A 21‐year‐old male patient presented with right knee pain sustained for 3 weeks after a basketball game. He landed awkwardly from a jump and felt an immediate popping sensation in the knee, followed by intense pain, swelling, and instability that forced him to stop playing. After receiving ice packs, immobilization, and acupuncture treatment, his swelling subsided. However, he still experienced pain and subsequently sought consultation with a sports medicine surgeon.

Clinical Examination and Imaging

On examination in the clinic, the knee was moderately swollen with an effusion and the range of motion was limited by pain, approximately 0°–100° of flexion. Tenderness was noted along the lateral joint line and at the lateral femoral condyle region. Clinical stability tests suggested significant ligamentous injury: the anterior drawer test and Lachman test were both markedly positive, indicating anterior translation of the tibia with a soft endpoint. A positive Pivot shift test indicated anterolateral rotatory instability associated with ACL insufficiency. Neurovascular examination of the limb was normal. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated a combined femoral‐ and tibial‐side avulsion pattern of the anterior cruciate ligament with preserved mid‐substance continuity, along with a LaPrade type 1 posterior root tear of the lateral meniscus (Figure 1).

Arthroscopic Technique With Photographic and Video Illustration

Patient Positioning and Preparation

The patient is placed supine on the operating table under general anesthesia with routine sterilization for arthroscopic examination.

Diagnostic Arthroscopy

A thorough diagnostic arthroscopy is performed through the anterolateral viewing portal. Arthroscopic evaluation demonstrated a combined avulsion of the ACL at both the femoral and tibial attachment sites involving the ligament bundles, with preserved but lax mid‐substance fibers rather than an isolated single‐bundle proximal avulsion. Although the posterior root lesion was not distinctly visualized on preoperative MRI, arthroscopic probing confirmed a partial detachment of the lateral meniscus posterior root consistent with a LaPrade type 1 tear (Figure 1 and Video S1).

Lateral Meniscus Root Repair

The root tear was repaired with a transtibial pullout suture technique, which allows reliable fixation of the meniscus root to the tibia. The FIRSTPASS MINI Suture Passer (Smith & Nephew Inc., USA) was placed 4–5 mm medial to the edge of the meniscus and passed the #2 ULTRABRAID Suture (Smith & Nephew Inc.) through the posterior horn of the meniscus. A suture loop was created, and the free end of the suture was pulled through this loop to form a double‐loop cinch construct on the meniscus. A second suture was placed using the same technique.

A standard ACL tibial guide set at 60° was used to create a tibial tunnel directed toward the anatomic insertion of the lateral meniscus posterior root. The tip of the guide was positioned precisely at the root attachment site under arthroscopic visualization. A 2.0 mm K‐wire was drilled along the guide and exited at the meniscus root footprint. A spinal needle was inserted through the K‐wire tunnel to deliver a polydioxanone (PDS) suture into the joint cavity. After retrieving the suture externally through the skin, a second PDS suture was looped and knotted at its midpoint to create a suture loop. This loop was used to shuttle the previously placed meniscal repair suture out through the tibial tunnel. The meniscal root sutures were fixed with a 4.5 mm FOOTPRINT ULTRA anchor (Smith & Nephew Inc.) on the tibial side after the fixation of ACL. ACL Repair and Fixation

Arthroscopic evaluation demonstrated a dual‐end avulsion pattern involving both the anteromedial and posterolateral bundles of the ACL, with preserved but lax mid‐substance fibers. The ligament tissue was of sufficient quality to permit in situ capture and reduction (Figures 2 and 3 and Video S1). Using a suture hook, the native ACL fibers were penetrated and a PDS suture was passed through the hook to serve as a shuttle. A high‐strength suture was pulled into the joint along this tract to complete the first stitch. For the second stitch, special attention was paid to ensure that the PDS suture for the new pass was looped around the suture limb of the first stitch, thereby creating a cinch loop around the ACL fibers. This configuration forms a secure locking loop around the ligament. The same method was repeated to place a second suture loop, resulting in double‐looped fixation of the ACL tissue. Another high‐strength suture was then used to repeat the process, enhancing stability and ensuring even distribution of fixation forces across the ligament. The femoral fixation sutures were secured with a single 4.5 mm FOOTPRINT ULTRA anchor (Smith & Nephew Inc.) positioned at the central region of the native ACL femoral footprint under direct arthroscopic visualization to restore anatomic tension across both bundles. A single anchor was considered sufficient because the continuous ligament remnant allowed balanced reduction without the need for separate bundle fixation. On the tibial side, high‐strength sutures were similarly passed through the distal ACL remnant. A tibial guide was positioned to the ACL tibial footprint, and a 2.0 mm K‐wire was drilled to create a tunnel. Care was taken to maintain appropriate tunnel orientation at different angles (55°, 60°, and 65°) to avoid convergence with adjacent tunnels. A spinal needle was used to deliver a PDS suture into the joint, and the tibial repair sutures were shuttled out through the tibial tunnel for external fixation. To further reinforce the construct, an additional tibial tunnel was created to retrieve the femoral repair sutures, forming a suture‐bridge augmentation spanning the native ACL. The femoral and tibial repair sutures were passed through a cortical button placed on the anteromedial tibial cortex. The corresponding suture limbs were then tied in pairs over the plate to complete fixation. The repair construct was cycled repeatedly to eliminate initial slack and creep. A full‐extension impingement test was performed to ensure the construct did not over‐constrain knee motion, and overall stability was verified using the Lachman and anterior drawer tests under anesthesia. Postoperative Rehabilitation and Follow‐Up

Postoperatively, the patient followed a structured rehabilitation protocol tailored to the procedure. During the initial 4 weeks, knee flexion was strictly limited to 30° to protect the repaired ligament attachments and meniscal sutures, with the range of motion gradually progressing under supervision. Partial weight‐bearing with crutch assistance was recommended to reduce axial load across the tibiofemoral joint and repair sites. Emphasis was placed on early quadriceps activation through isometric exercises, including quadriceps setting and straight leg raises performed in a knee brace locked in extension. Closed chain strengthening, resisted hamstring exercises, and deep knee flexion activities were avoided during this period to minimize stress on the repair constructs. This early protective phase aimed to promote biological healing while preventing stiffness and muscle atrophy.

MRI was performed on the first postoperative day to document the immediate structural status of the repair construct, including ACL continuity, anchor position, and meniscal root fixation (Figure 4). One year after surgery, the MRI scan showed that the ACL had good morphology and tension, and the lateral meniscus healed well. A physical examination 1 year post‐surgery showed full knee range of motion and stability compared with the contralateral side, with negative drawer and Lachman tests. The patient has returned to physical activity (Video S1).

Discussion

Challenges of This Rare Dual‐End Avulsion Pattern

Combined femoral‐ and tibial‐side avulsion of the ACL represents a rare injury pattern that poses unique diagnostic and technical challenges. Unlike typical proximal avulsion injuries, where one insertion site remains intact and provides a reference for reduction and tensioning, this dual‐end configuration lacks a stable anchoring point at either end of the ligament [19, 20]. As a result, achieving accurate anatomic reapproximation, restoring native ligament length, and maintaining appropriate tension become considerably more complex. In addition, concomitant injuries, such as the lateral meniscus posterior root tear in this case, further increase procedural complexity and require careful coordination of tunnel placement and fixation strategy to avoid tunnel convergence and construct interference.

From a biomechanical perspective, a true simultaneous complete avulsion of the entire ACL at both femoral and tibial insertions under a single loading condition is unlikely. A more plausible explanation is a sequential or asymmetric loading mechanism involving the two functional anteromedial (AM) and posterolateral (PL) bundles of ACL [21]. Given their distinct tensioning behavior, while the AM bundle is tighter in flexion and the PL bundle is tighter in extension, different knee positions during a high‐energy pivoting or hyperextension injury may result in uneven stress distribution [22, 23]. Under such conditions, one bundle may fail at its femoral attachment due to tensile overload, while the other may sustain a tibial‐side avulsion driven by combined shear and rotational forces.

Rationale for the Presented Repair Technique

Modern arthroscopic primary ACL repair is typically framed as a ligament‐preserving option for acute proximal avulsion‐type tears with good tissue quality [18]. In this article, we report a technique for ACLPR via reattaching both ligament ends with suture loops and reinforced with suture augmentation to address the absence of a stable insertion site and to restore both structural continuity and biomechanical function of the ACL. Traditional single‐sided ACL repair techniques typically rely on at least one intact insertion for guidance and tensioning and are therefore insufficient in this context [19, 20]. Our approach utilizes circumferential cinch‐loop suturing to capture both ligament ends, combined with independent fixation pathways to reestablish femoral and tibial attachment sites. The addition of suture augmentation serves as a protective internal stabilizer, reducing mechanical load on the repaired ligament during the early healing phase. Technically, the use of three independent tibial tunnels allows separate management of the ACL repair sutures, internal augmentation, and meniscal root repair, thereby minimizing tunnel convergence and ensuring construct integrity. The use of a 2.0‐mm Kirschner wire during suture passage reduces iatrogenic bone damage, while extracortical fixation enhances stability.

Several suture‐based ACL repair techniques have been described, but most have been developed for selected proximal ACL tears with adequate tissue quality. In these techniques, the torn ligament is reattached to the femoral footprint using sutures passed through bone tunnels or secured with suture anchors, with or without additional suture tape augmentation [24]. Suture augmentation or internal bracing provides an independent load‐sharing construct that protects the repaired ligament during early healing while preserving native ACL tissue and avoiding graft harvest [25]. However, these techniques generally depend on the presence of a repairable ligament remnant with an intact or relatively preserved opposite attachment site. Bridge‐enhanced ACL repair (BEAR) represents another ligament‐preserving strategy. In this technique, bioactive collagen scaffold combined with autologous blood is placed between the torn ACL ends to facilitate biological healing rather than relying solely on direct suture reapproximation [13, 26]. In contrast to these approaches, the present technique was specifically developed for simultaneous femoral‐ and tibial‐side ACL avulsion. Our technique independently captures and reattaches both ACL ends using site‐specific fixation pathways and incorporates a separate suture augmentation construct to protect the repaired ligament. Therefore, its principal distinction from previously reported suture‐based repair techniques lies in the simultaneous anatomical restoration of both ACL attachment sites while preserving the native ligament tissue. Therefore, compared with conventional single‐site repair, this technique is technically more demanding because it requires coordinated tensioning of both repaired ends, meticulous suture management, and careful tunnel orientation to avoid tunnel convergence.

Indications and Patient Selection

The indications for this technique are narrow and should be carefully considered. In our opinion, arthroscopic primary repair with augmentation is most appropriate for acute avulsion‐type ACL injuries with well‐preserved tissue quality that allow secure suture capture and anatomic reapproximation. Patient selection is critical, with tear pattern, tissue integrity, and timing of surgery serving as key determinants of repairability. Acute injuries with minimal retraction and good ligament quality are more likely to achieve favorable outcomes. Conversely, delayed or chronic injuries may be associated with ligament degeneration, scarring, and retraction, which compromise the ability to restore native anatomy and tension [10]. In addition, mid‐substance tears often lack sufficient tissue for secure fixation and are therefore less suitable for primary repair [10]. Intraoperative assessment remains essential to confirm the feasibility of repair and to guide decision‐making.

Limitations and Contraindications

Several limitations and contraindications of this technique should be acknowledged. First, this is a single‐case technical report, and the generalizability of the findings is limited. Second, the procedure is technically demanding, requiring advanced arthroscopic skills, precise tunnel planning, and meticulous suture management. Third, poor tissue quality, chronic injury patterns, and complex ligament disruption may preclude reliable repair and instead favor reconstruction. Multiligament knee injuries represent another important limitation. In such cases, global instability, increased biomechanical demands, and concomitant structural damage reduce the likelihood of successful isolated ACL repair. Furthermore, the long‐term durability of this technique remains uncertain, and further studies with larger cohorts and extended follow‐up are required to validate its reproducibility and clinical outcomes.

Contextual Comparison With ACL Reconstruction

Traditional ACLR remains the gold standard for most complete and chronic ACL tears, providing reliable restoration of knee stability and well‐established long‐term outcomes [4, 10]. However, reconstruction has inherent drawbacks. For example, it replaces the torn ligament with a graft, which involves tendon harvesting and loss of the native ligament's biology [27]. Over the past several decades, ACLPR has undergone a dramatic evolution in technique and acceptance. Early attempts at open ACL repair in the 1970s and 1980s showed unacceptably high failure rates, with the rerupture rates of up to 40%–50% at midterm follow‐up [28]. Consequently, direct repair was largely abandoned in favor of reconstruction for nearly 30 years [29]. Recently, however, there has been a resurgence of interest in ACL repair fueled by improved arthroscopic techniques and a better understanding of ACL biology. Modern arthroscopic primary repair techniques utilize suture‐anchor fixation, internal bracing, and biologic enhancements to enhance ACL reapproximation and healing have received promising results [15, 30, 31]. Consensus guidance supports early anatomic ACLR with concomitant treatment of associated injured structures when multiple ligament injury is present [32]. Regarding durability, recent comparative meta‐analyses suggest that, overall, ACLPR cohorts may have higher failure and revision risks than reconstruction, although functional outcomes can be comparable in selected patients [16]. Accordingly, we present this technique as an option for a narrow indication, acute, repairable avulsion‐type injuries with good tissue quality, rather than a replacement for reconstruction across broader tear patterns. Conclusion

Arthroscopic ACLPR with suture augmentation represents a viable option for the management of combined acute femoral and tibial attachment avulsion of the ACL in carefully selected patients. This technique enables restoration of native ligament anatomy while minimizing surgical morbidity and providing both biological and mechanical support for healing in 1‐year follow‐up period.
