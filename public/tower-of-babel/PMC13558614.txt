Protective effects of dietary omega-3 on cardiac and hepatic function, mortality, and gene expression in broilers under high stocking density
Authors: Shahgol Rahbari, Abdolreza Salehi, Seyed Davood Sharifi, Sara Pahlavan, Morteza Pashaei Jalal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the effects of dietary omega-3 fatty acids on cardiac and liver function, mortality rates, hematological parameters, tissue morphometric, and gene expression in broilers exposed to high stocking density (HSD). A total of 420 one-day-old Arian broiler chicks were allocated to a 2 × 2 factorial arrangements consisting of two stocking densities [standard stocking density (SSD; 9 birds/m2) and high stocking density (HSD; 16 birds/m2)] and two levels of omega-3 supplementation (0.057% basal vs. 0.5%) within a completely randomized design. At 40 days of age, birds reared under HSD and supplemented with 0.5% omega-3 showed significantly lower total ventricle-to-body weight (TV/BW) and right ventricle-to-body weight (RV/BW) ratios compared with the other groups (P ≤ 0.05). Additionally, omega-3 supplementation significantly reduced mortality rates in birds reared under both SSD and HSD (P ≤ 0.05). Omega-3 supplementation significantly reduced serum concentrations of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (P ≤ 0.05). Histological analysis showed that birds reared under HSD with omega-3 supplementation had thinner pulmonary arterial walls compared with those under HSD without omega-3, as well as those reared under SSD regardless of supplementation. The smallest cardiomyocyte diameter was recorded in birds reared under HSD and supplemented with 0.5% omega-3 (P ≤ 0.05). Gene expression analysis revealed that acetyl-CoA carboxylase alpha (ACACA) and Phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK1) were significantly down-regulated in the liver tissue of birds reared under HSD compared with those fed HSD diets supplemented with omega-3 (P ≤ 0.05). Overall, omega-3 supplementation was associated with reduced mortality, lower liver enzyme levels, attenuated histological alterations in the pulmonary artery and RV, and modulation of hepatic genes related to energy metabolism.

Introduction

High stocking density is a common practice in poultry production aimed at improving production efficiency; however, it may compromise bird welfare and health. Reducing the space available per bird can lead to increased mortality and reduced growth performance, despite the potential for higher profit per square meter1,2. For instance, experimental evidence indicates that elevated stocking densities significantly increase mortality and suppress growth. Conversely, another study found that increasing density from 12 to 18 birds per square meter reduced the incidence of sudden death syndrome in broilers, underscoring the need for careful management of stocking density (SD)3.

Environmental factors, such as poor air quality and competition for resources, can exacerbate stress and increase mortality. Moreover, HSD adversely affects growth and may induce cardiovascular disorders. These health challenges can ultimately reduce economic returns and increase mortality at slaughter2.

Diet plays a crucial role in preventing non-infectious diseases, including cardiovascular disorders4. Nutritional strategies, particularly the incorporation of omega-3 fatty acids, may help mitigate these problems. Omega-3 fatty acids possess anti-inflammatory properties and may contribute to the prevention of cardiovascular disorders by modulating various physiological processes, thereby supporting cardiac health and potentially reducing the risk of atherosclerosis. Consequently, dietary supplementation with omega-3 fatty acids may improve overall poultry health and production profitability5,6.

The liver of broiler chickens is highly sensitive to stress, a response largely regulated by glucocorticoids. These hormones play a central role in lipid metabolism and influence the expression of more than 50 hepatic genes. Glucocorticoids can induce metabolic shifts, including gluconeogenesis, and alter hepatic lipid composition. Omega-3 fatty acids may mitigate hepatic fat accumulation by promoting fatty acid oxidation and exerting beneficial anti-stress effects. Recently, the health-promoting effects of omega-3 fatty acids have been increasingly recognized in both humans and animals, as they modulate various biological processes and cellular functions7.

Hypoxia is a major concern in broiler production and is often associated with stress, contributing to the development of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH). Under hypoxic conditions, cells increasingly rely on glycolysis as a primary energy source. The PFK1 is a key regulatory enzyme in glycolysis, whereas ACACA plays a central role in fatty acid metabolism by regulating lipid synthesis and indirectly influencing fatty acid oxidation8,9. In addition, endothelin-1 (ET-1), a potent vasoactive peptide implicated in various cardiovascular disorders, contributes to endothelial dysfunction, which often precedes the onset of clinical manifestations10.

Despite extensive research on commonly used commercial broiler strains, relatively limited attention has been given to the Arian strain. Compared with widely studied strains such as Ross, Arian broilers have been reported to exhibit lower production performance and a greater susceptibility to cardiovascular-related mortality. Cardiovascular disorders have been estimated to account for up to 10% of total deaths in Arian broilers, whereas the proportion is generally lower in other commercial strains11. These complications are particularly evident in the paternal line (B), which is characterized by a rapid growth rate. The specific genetic background of the Arian strain, together with limited selective breeding focused on survival traits, may contribute to its increased vulnerability to cardiovascular dysfunction. Therefore, nutritional interventions aimed at improving cardiovascular and metabolic resilience may be particularly relevant in this strain.

Given the diverse metabolic benefits of omega-3 fatty acids, incorporating these nutrients into the diets of Arian broilers raised under HSD may enhance health and performance while potentially reducing mortality. We hypothesized that dietary omega-3 supplementation could alleviate stress associated with HSD by modulating metabolic and functional responses in cardiac and hepatic tissues. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of omega-3 fatty acid supplementation on cardiac and liver health, mortality, hematological parameters, tissue morphology, and the mRNA expression of selected genes in the heart and liver of Arian broilers subjected to HSD.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. All experimental procedures complied with established ethical standards for animal research and were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Department of Animal and Poultry Science, University of Tehran, Iran (Protocol No. 9918276; Date: May 31, 2021).

Birds and dietary treatments

A total of 420 one-day-old, unsexed Arian broiler chicks (initial body weight 41.4 ± 0.70 g; Dizbad Breeder Co., Tehran, Iran) were used in a 40-day experiment arranged in a 2 × 2 factorial design within a completely randomized design, with four treatments and five replicates per treatment. Two SD treatments were applied: a low-density group (16 birds/pen) and a high-density group (26 birds/pen). Birds were housed in 2 m2 floor pens (2 × 1 m) with wood shavings as bedding. To account for the area occupied by feeders and drinkers, SD was calculated based on usable floor space rather than total pen area, following the approach of Goo et al.12. Diets were formulated based on corn and soybean meal to meet the nutritional requirements of Arian broilers according to the Arian management guide. The starter, grower, and finisher diets provided 2870, 2950, and 3025 kcal AMEn/kg and 220, 200.5, and 180 g/kg crude protein (CP), respectively (Table 1). Two dietary omega-3 fatty acid levels were evaluated: 0% (control) and 0.5% supplementation. The basal concentration of omega-3 fatty acids in the control diet was 0.057%. Persia Fat (Kimiya Danesh Alvand Co., Tehran, Iran), a commercial supplement derived from salmon oil containing 20% total omega-3 fatty acids, served as the dietary source. The 0.5% inclusion level was selected based on its established efficacy in modulating physiological traits in poultry13–15. Birds were reared under environmentally controlled conditions. Ambient temperature was gradually reduced from 32°C at placement to 24°C at a rate of approximately 3.5°C per week. Continuous lighting (24 h light) was provided on day 1, followed by a 23L:1D (light: dark) schedule for the remainder of the experimental period.

All birds were vaccinated against infectious bronchitis (H-120; administered via spray at day 1 and via drinking water at day 18), infectious bursal disease (D-78; administered via drinking water at days 14 and 28), and Newcastle disease (B1 via eye drops at day 10 and LaSota via drinking water at day 22).

Measurement of organ weights, cardiac status, hematology, and tissue sampling

At the end of the 40‑day trial, one male broiler from each replicate pen (n = 5 per treatment) was selected for all organ, biochemical, hematological, and histological analyses. To obtain representative samples, all birds within each pen were weighed, and the bird whose body weight was closest to the pen mean was chosen. The selected birds were individually weighed, and blood samples (approximately 8 mL) were collected via jugular vein puncture into 10‑mL Vacutainer tubes without anticoagulant (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Samples were centrifuged at 2000 × g for 15 min at 4°C, and serum was stored at −20°C until biochemical analysis.

Serum ALT and AST activities were measured using commercial kits according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Hematological parameters, including hematocrit (HCT), hemoglobin (HGB), differential leukocyte counts (heterophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, and eosinophils), and the heterophil‑to‑lymphocyte ratio (H/L), were determined using an automated hematology analyzer (ADVIA 120, Bayer, Tarrytown, NY, USA) with appropriate commercial kits (Biorexfars, Shiraz, Iran), following Richmond16.

Immediately after blood collection, the birds were euthanized by cervical dislocation in accordance with AVMA guidelines17. The carcass, liver, heart, right ventricle (RV), and total ventricle (TV) were dissected and weighed to determine dressing percentage and to calculate the RV/TV ratio. An ascites heart index (AHI) ≥ 0.28 was considered indicative of pulmonary hypertension syndrome18. Birds with AHI < 0.28 and HCT < 0.36 were classified as healthy, whereas those with AHI ≥ 0.28 and HCT ≥ 0.36 were considered ascitic. Mortality was recorded throughout the experimental period.

For histological assessments, samples of the right ventricular myocardium, pulmonary artery, and liver were collected immediately after dissection. Pulmonary artery segments were excised from the pulmonary trunk, just distal to the right ventricular outflow tract, by removing a transverse ring approximately 3–5 mm thick. All tissues were fixed in 10% neutral‑buffered formalin and subsequently transferred to 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Routine histological processing included dehydration in graded ethanol solutions (70%–99.5%), clearing in xylene, embedding in paraffin, microtome sectioning, and hematoxylin and eosin staining19.

Morphometric evaluations were performed using an Olympus BX‑51 light microscope equipped with a digital camera and analyzed with Zeiss KS‑400.3 software. Measurements included pulmonary artery wall thickness (μm), cardiomyocyte diameter (μm), hepatocyte diameter (μm), and hepatocyte nuclear diameter (μm). For each parameter, at least 10 measurements were obtained from randomly selected microscopic fields, and the mean value for each bird was calculated. For all variables, the pen was considered the experimental unit for statistical analysis.

Real-time PCR analysis

The mRNA expression levels of ACACA, PFK1, and ET-1 in the right ventricle (RV) and liver were quantified using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) on an ABI Step One Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). Primers for broiler chicken ACACA (NM-205505.2), PFK1 (NM-204223.2), ET-1 (XM-418943.6), and GAPDH (NM-204305.2) were designed using Oligo 2 software (https://oligo-analyzer.software.informer.com/) and are presented in Table 2. Right ventricular and liver tissues (100 mg) were collected from five birds per treatment group at day 40. Total RNA was extracted from homogenized tissues using TRIzol reagent (Kiazol, Iran; Royan Institute for Stem Cell Biology and Technology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity and concentration were assessed by measuring the absorbance ratio at 260/280 nm using a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from the extracted RNA using a commercial reverse transcription kit (Parstous, A101161, Tehran, Iran) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed in a final reaction volume of 10 µL containing 2 µL cDNA, 3.5 µL SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, Japan), 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers, and nuclease-free water. All reactions were run in triplicate using gene-specific primers. Amplification was carried out under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 60 s. Fluorescence was recorded at the end of each cycle. Cycle threshold (Ct) values were obtained for each sample, and relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt2^{-\Delta\Delta Ct}2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH used as the reference gene20. Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using a 2 × 2 factorial arrangement within a completely randomized design. The normality of residuals was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Performance, physiological, morphometric, and biochemical variables were analyzed using the GLM procedure of SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., 2013). Mortality percentages were arcsine-transformed prior to analysis. When significant treatment effects were detected, means were separated using Tukey’s multiple range test. Statistical significance was declared at P ≤ 0.05. For real-time PCR analysis, gene expression was quantified as relative fold change using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Fold-change values were calculated by comparing the omega-3-supplemented group under HSD with the corresponding HSD control group, resulting in a pairwise comparison rather than a full factorial dataset. Statistical analysis was performed on ΔCt values after confirmation of normal distribution, using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, and differences were considered statistically significant at P ≤ 0.05. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 9.1.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Discussion

Evaluation of cardiac status and mortality in Arian broilers indicated that omega-3 fatty acid supplementation reduced cardiac size (TV and RV) and decreased mortality rates. However, omega-3 supplementation did not significantly affect the AHI in this strain. An AHI value exceeding 28% is generally considered indicative of RV hypertrophy17, and ascitic birds typically display higher AHI values than healthy counterparts21. In the present study, omega-3-supplemented broilers—particularly those reared under SSD—showed significant reductions in both TV and RV sizes, along with lower mortality rates compared with the non-supplemented groups. These results suggest that dietary omega-3 fatty acids may attenuate RV hypertrophy, alleviate pulmonary hypertension, and consequently improve the survival of broilers.

Given the significant reduction in mortality observed in these groups, the use of omega-3 fatty acids may be associated with decreased RV and TV sizes and reduced pulmonary blood pressure. Previous studies have reported similar outcomes; supplementation with linseed oil—a rich source of omega-3 fatty acids—has been shown to lower the RV-to-TV ratio and decrease mortality linked to pulmonary hypertension in broilers22. Likewise, the inclusion of linseed oil in broiler diets has been effective in reducing both mortality and the incidence of ascites23.

Research on the effects of omega-3 fatty acids on the incidence of ascites in broiler chickens remains limited. However, available evidence suggests that the metabolic conversion of omega-3 fatty acids into vasodilatory compounds may contribute to reducing the incidence of ascites and pulmonary hypertension syndrome23. In humans, dietary supplementation with fish-oil–derived omega-3 fatty acids have been shown to improve red blood cell deformability, a factor that may enhance microvascular perfusion and oxygen delivery24. Additionally, it has been reported that moderating growth rate can effectively lower the incidence of ascites in broiler chickens25.

In this context, vasodilation has been proposed as a consequence of dietary arginine through enhanced nitric oxide (NO) production26, as well as through the effects of dietary omega-3 fatty acids via the generation of bioactive metabolites derived from arachidonic acid23. Recent studies have further suggested that the inclusion of omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids in broiler diets may alleviate pulmonary hypertension and right ventricular dysfunction22. Accordingly, broilers fed linseed oil–enriched diets exhibited a significant reduction in the RV-to-TV weight ratio, accompanied by lower mortality associated with ascites. These effects have been attributed to increased circulating NO levels and the suppression of hepatic lipogenesis.

This study demonstrated that dietary supplementation with 0.5% omega-3 fatty acids significantly reduced serum ALT and AST concentrations in broilers. Consistent with these findings, the use of rice bran oil (RBO), a rich source of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), has also been shown to decrease serum ALT and AST activities without inducing adverse hepatic effects, as reported by Franciscato et al.27. The decline in transaminase activity observed in the present study suggests that omega-3 fatty acids may protect hepatocytes by mitigating tissue damage and minimizing metabolic stress. Given the central role of the liver in intermediary metabolism, serum transaminase levels (ALT and AST) are widely recognized as key biomarkers for evaluating hepatocellular injury, recovery, and overall hepatic health in poultry28.

Liver injury typically results in the leakage of intracellular enzymes into the circulation29. Consequently, increased plasma activities of hepatic enzymes such as AST, ALT, and GGT are recognized diagnostic indicators of hepatocellular damage30. In avian species, AST activity is particularly considered a sensitive marker of liver dysfunction. The reduction in AST levels observed in the present study may reflect the anti‑inflammatory properties of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and other long‑chain omega‑3 fatty acids31. Lower serum ALT and AST activities further indicate that omega‑3 supplementation alleviates hepatic injury and supports improved liver function in broilers. These protective effects are likely mediated through the antioxidant capacity of omega‑3 fatty acids, which limit lipid peroxidation and preserve hepatocellular integrity.

Furthermore, histological evaluation of the pulmonary artery and cardiac tissue revealed pronounced morphological differences among treatments. Broilers reared under HSD but supplemented with omega-3 fatty acids exhibited the smallest arterial wall thickness and cardiomyocyte diameter. In contrast, non-supplemented birds exposed to HSD showed marked hypertrophy of the pulmonary arterial wall, discontinuity of smooth muscle layers, and loose associations within the mesenchymal tissue (Fig. 3C). These pathological changes are consistent with previous reports describing pulmonary arterial hypertension in broilers—a condition characterized by elevated pulmonary vascular resistance and right-sided cardiac strain. Additionally, stressed birds displayed loosely organized myocardial fibers and poorly defined sarcolemma boundaries. Collectively, these findings indicate that cardiomyocyte structural disruption plays a central role in the pathogenesis of ascites syndrome. In line with earlier studies, omega-3 fatty acid supplementation appears to exert protective cardiovascular effects and may contribute to improved systemic health in both avian and mammalian species.

Under stressful conditions, omega-3 fatty acids appear to modulate pulmonary arterial remodeling. These fatty acids exert multiple physiological effects, including the suppression of arachidonic acid synthesis, competition with arachidonic acid for incorporation into cell membranes, and alteration of downstream eicosanoid pathways. Through these mechanisms, omega-3 fatty acids confer anti-inflammatory and anti-thrombotic properties and may also influence cardiomyocyte excitability by modulating ion channel activity. In the present study, the physiological disturbances induced by HSD increased broiler susceptibility to pulmonary arterial hypertension, ultimately contributing to elevated mortality. Although environmental stress is widely recognized as a risk factor for increased mortality in broiler production, our findings suggest that omega-3 supplementation may attenuate these adverse outcomes and help reduce associated economic losses for producers.

Histological evaluation of liver tissue demonstrated that neither SD nor omega-3 fatty acid supplementation significantly affected hepatocyte or hepatocyte nuclear diameter. Consistently, serum activities of hepatic enzymes, including AST and ALT—commonly used indicators of hepatocellular stress—remained unchanged under HSD conditions in Arian broilers.

Although studies specifically examining hepatic micromorphological changes in broilers exposed to different stocking densities are limited, experimental models in other species have shown that oxidative stress may contribute to liver injury32. The HSD is generally associated with elevated physiological stress, which could potentially impair hepatic function and performance. However, the present findings suggest that, in the Arian strain, HSD did not induce measurable structural alterations in hepatocytes nor biochemical evidence of hepatic stress. These results may indicate a degree of strain-specific resilience of hepatic tissue to stocking-density–related stress.

With respect to omega-3 supplementation, a numerical reduction in hepatocyte diameter was observed (P = 0.09). Although this difference did not reach statistical significance, the near-significant trend suggests a potential hepatoprotective effect of omega-3 fatty acids (Fig. 4, liver). Omega-3 fatty acids have been implicated in modulating hepatic metabolism by enhancing antioxidant capacity and attenuating inflammatory and stress-related responses33. These properties may contribute to the mitigation of environmentally induced hepatic stress in broilers exposed to HSD.

This study investigated the expression of key enzymes involved in glycolysis, fatty acid metabolism, and endothelin-1 signaling. In the liver, the expression of ACACA, a key enzyme in fatty acid synthesis, was elevated in broilers subjected to HSD, whereas dietary omega-3 supplementation significantly reduced its expression. These findings suggest that HSD may influence the transcriptional regulation of hepatic lipid metabolism, while omega-3 fatty acids may modulate these responses at the mRNA level. Furthermore, the significantly lower expression of PFK1 in the omega-3-supplemented HSD group (P ≤ 0.05) may indicate a regulatory shift in hepatic glucose metabolism.

The liver is highly susceptible to environmental stress, second only to the brain34. Such stress can induce metabolic alterations, including increased gluconeogenesis and changes in lipid metabolism, which may contribute to oxidative stress35. Consistent with previous reports, environmental stressors can exert direct or indirect effects on the expression of metabolic genes, particularly those involved in catabolic pathways7,36. Although no apparent phenotypic alterations were observed in the liver in the present study, the observed molecular changes may reflect early adaptive responses to HSD.

These transcriptional responses may be linked to several regulatory mechanisms described in the literature. For example, post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation play an important role in the regulation of glycolysis37. The enzyme PFKFB3 has been reported to be upregulated under hypoxic conditions38 and may regulate PFK1 activity. In addition, activation of AMPK can phosphorylate and inhibit ACC, thereby decreasing malonyl-CoA synthesis and promoting fatty acid oxidation through the activation of CPT-139.

Furthermore, it has been proposed that in stressed broilers, altered acetyl-CoA levels within cardiomyocytes may influence the rate of glucose metabolism40. Under hypoxic conditions, an imbalance between acetyl-CoA production (derived from glycolysis and fatty acid oxidation) and its utilization in the TCA cycle may lead to metabolic disturbances41. However, as the present study assessed only mRNA expression, further studies evaluating enzyme activity and metabolite concentrations are necessary to confirm these functional metabolic changes in broilers exposed to HSD.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides new insights into the molecular and physiological responses of broiler chickens reared under HSD and the potential mitigating role of omega-3 fatty acid supplementation. Using the Arian strain as a model, HSD was shown to increase mortality and alter the expression of key genes involved in hepatic energy metabolism. Dietary omega-3 supplementation improved survival, stabilized liver enzyme levels, and attenuated histological alterations in pulmonary arterial and cardiac tissues. Moreover, omega-3 fatty acids modulated the hepatic expression of genes associated with lipid and glucose metabolism, suggesting a protective effect against HSD-induced stress. These findings indicate that omega-3 supplementation may serve as a practical nutritional strategy to enhance broiler resilience and reduce the adverse physiological impacts associated with HSD.
