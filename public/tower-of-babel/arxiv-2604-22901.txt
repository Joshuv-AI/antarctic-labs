Accelerating Frequency Domain Diffusion Models with Error-Feedback Event-Driven Caching
Dong Liu, Haisheng Wang, Yanxuan Yu

Abstract
Diffusion models achieve remarkable success in time series generation. However, slow inference limits their practical deployment. We propose E 2 -CRF (Error-Feedback Event-Driven Cumulative Residual Feature caching) to accelerate frequency domain diffusion models. Our method exploits two structural properties: (1) spectral localization, where signal energy concentrates in low frequencies, and (2) mirror symmetry, which halves the effective frequency dimension. E 2 -CRF uses a closed-loop error-feedback system that adaptively caches transformer KV features across diffusion steps. We trigger recomputation using event-driven residual dynamics instead of fixed schedules. Our method selectively recomputes high-energy or rapidly-changing tokens while reusing cached features for stable high-frequency components. E 2 -CRF achieves 2.2× speedup while maintaining sample quality. We demonstrate effectiveness on 5 datasets. Our caching strategy naturally aligns with the diffusion process’s structure-to-detail progression. We include sufficient-condition error and complexity bounds under standard regularity assumptions (Appendix), alongside empirical validation. Our code is available at https://github.com/NoakLiu/FastFourierDiffusion and is also integrated in https://github.com/NoakLiu/FastCache-xDiT .

Introduction

When applying frequency-domain diffusion to time series generation, a natural acceleration strategy is to cache transformer key–value (KV) features across diffusion steps, since attention computation scales quadratically with sequence length and the same network is evaluated hundreds of times during sampling
(
5
;
40
;
20
;
16
;
21
)
. However, naïve caching fails catastrophically: cached features drift over diffusion steps, causing error accumulation that degrades sample quality beyond acceptable thresholds. This failure mode stems from ignoring the differential dynamics of frequency components during reverse diffusion—high-frequency tokens often evolve slowly while low-frequency tokens change rapidly—yet uniform caching treats all frequencies identically.

Why frequency-domain caching collapses.

The fundamental issue is a mismatch between the caching policy and the diffusion process’s structure-to-detail progression: early steps establish coarse, low-frequency structure, while later steps refine high-frequency details. Uniform reuse across steps makes high-energy low-frequency representations stale, corrupting the generation trajectory. For transformer-based score networks
(
40
)
, this manifests as increasingly inaccurate attention patterns, since cached KV pairs no longer reflect the current frequency representation state.

Existing accelerations and the missing piece.

The approximation error between the cached score and the fully recomputed score can grow unbounded when reuse is not carefully controlled, undermining the reverse trajectory. Most existing diffusion acceleration methods focus on reducing the number of sampling steps via improved solvers or implicit sampling
(
35
;
26
;
12
;
17
;
22
;
19
;
18
)
, rather than addressing per-step reuse. Meanwhile, recent frequency-aware caching for diffusion models
(
24
;
15
)
suggests that caching can be effective when aligned with spectral structure, but principled designs for
time-series
frequency diffusion remain limited.

Observation: energy distribution enables selective caching.

Real-world time series exhibit spectral localization: most energy concentrates in low frequencies, while high-frequency components carry less energy and evolve more smoothly across diffusion steps
(
13
)
. In addition, mirror (conjugate) symmetry of real-valued signals’ DFTs halves the effective frequency dimension, enabling cache compression without information loss
(
34
)
. Together, these properties suggest that selective caching is not only possible but necessary: high-frequency tokens (low energy, slow evolution) are natural candidates for reuse, while low-frequency tokens (high energy, rapid evolution) must be recomputed frequently. The central challenge is determining
when
cached tokens become stale and require refresh.

Solution: event-driven caching with error feedback.

We introduce
E
2
-CRF
(Error-Feedback Event-Driven Cumulative Residual Feature caching), a mechanism that monitors the residual dynamics of the score network’s internal representations to trigger selective recomputation. E
2
-CRF combines (i) an event-driven trigger that detects drift beyond acceptable thresholds and (ii) error-feedback correction that periodically recalibrates cached values via probe computations. This design adaptively adjusts caching aggressiveness across diffusion stages: early steps with high residual activity trigger frequent recomputation, while later steps allow more aggressive caching of stable high-frequency tokens. Our method exploits spectral localization
(
13
)
and mirror symmetry
(
34
)
, and is compatible with frequency-domain diffusion modeling
(
5
)
.

Our contributions.

(1) E
2
-CRF: Error-Feedback Event-Driven Caching.
We propose a principled, step-aware caching mechanism for frequency-domain diffusion time-series models.

(2) Speedup with Quality Preservation.
We show that E

-CRF achieves significant inference speedups while maintaining sample quality (Section 4).

(3) Theoretical Analysis.
We analyze how an event-driven trigger and error feedback jointly control cache-induced approximation error across diffusion stages.

2.1
Caching in Iterative Generative Inference

Diffusion models achieve strong generative performance across domains
(
6
;
9
;
37
;
4
)
, but remain inference-limited due to hundreds to thousands of score-network evaluations per sample.
This is especially costly for transformer score networks
(
40
)
, where self-attention recomputes highly similar key–value (KV) projections across adjacent diffusion steps.
While diffusion has been widely adopted for time-series generation
(
38
;
32
;
14
)
, acceleration is still a central bottleneck.

Caching is a natural way to amortize redundant computation, but diffusion differs from autoregressive decoding: the input state evolves at every step, so stale reuse can accumulate error and degrade samples.
Concretely, replacing
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\textbf{x},t)
with a cached
\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{\theta}(\textbf{x},t)
introduces an approximation error
\epsilon(\textbf{x},t)
that can compound along the reverse trajectory.
Accordingly, most diffusion accelerations reduce step count via improved solvers
(
35
;
26
;
12
)
, rather than enabling safe, step-aware feature reuse.
Recent frequency-aware caching for diffusion transformers
(
24
)
suggests that reuse can be effective when aligned with spectral structure, but principled designs for frequency-domain
time-series
diffusion remain limited.
Related training-free or proxy-based reuse has also been studied for image/video diffusion transformers (e.g., timestep/feature-driven caching
(
23
;
27
)
, fast or offset cache corrections
(
17
;
15
;
31
)
), and for diffusion LLMs via adaptive KV reuse
(
25
;
42
)
.
These settings differ in backbone, modality, and how hidden states evolve across denoising steps, so policies are not directly interchangeable with half-spectrum KV reuse for multivariate signals
(
5
)
.
Broader time-series diffusion models beyond the Fourier parameterization
(
43
;
8
;
1
)
are complementary generative baselines but do not isolate per-step KV caching for Fourier-domain scores; we therefore benchmark primarily against an uncached frequency baseline and controlled ablations.

2.2
Cache Reuse in Frequency-Domain Diffusion

Frequency-domain parameterizations provide an inductive bias for diffusion on temporal signals by exposing spectral structure and redundancy.
Fourier-space diffusion for time series has been studied as a modeling choice
(
1
;
5
)
, and more recent work further explores frequency-structured generation via wavelets and frequency-shaped conditioning
(
41
;
8
)
.
In parallel, frequency-domain perspectives have also been developed for diffusion beyond time series (e.g., Fourier-space analyses or wavelet-based generators), supporting the broader view that spectral coordinates can make diffusion dynamics more structured and controllable
(
7
;
30
)
.

Two properties are particularly relevant for caching.

(i) Spectral localization.
Real-world time series concentrate most energy in low-frequency components, while higher-frequency bands typically carry less energy and evolve more smoothly across diffusion steps.
As a result, the spectral energy density
\|\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{\kappa}\|_{2}^{2}
often decays quickly with frequency index
\kappa
, making high-frequency tokens natural candidates for reuse.

(ii) Mirror symmetry.
For real-valued signals, DFT coefficients satisfy conjugate symmetry, reducing the effective number of distinct frequency tokens by roughly half, and introducing built-in redundancy that can be exploited by cache compression.

Crucially, reverse diffusion exhibits a structure-to-detail progression: early steps establish coarse, low-frequency structure, whereas later steps refine high-frequency details.
This asymmetry implies that many high-frequency tokens change little over large portions of the trajectory.
Formally, for token
\kappa
at diffusion step
i
, the relative change

\delta_{\kappa}^{(i)}=\|\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{\kappa}^{(i)}-\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{\kappa}^{(i-\Delta)}\|_{2}/\|\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{\kappa}^{(i-\Delta)}\|_{2}

is typically smaller when
\kappa>K
than when
\kappa\leq K
for a threshold
K
.
Therefore, reuse can be structurally aligned with the generative process, provided that it is selective and guarded against drift—motivating event-driven, error-aware caching rather than uniform reuse schedules.

2.3
Cached Score-Based Diffusion with SDEs

In continuous-time diffusion modeling, we observe data
\textbf{x}(0)\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}
and define a forward noising SDE

\mathrm{d}\textbf{x}=\bm{f}(\textbf{x},t)\mathrm{d}t+\bm{G}(t)\mathrm{d}\bm{w},

(1)

with drift
\bm{f}
and diffusion
\bm{G}(t)
.
Sampling relies on the reverse-time SDE requiring the score
\mathbf{s}(\textbf{x},t)=\nabla_{\textbf{x}}\log p_{t}(\textbf{x})
:

\mathrm{d}\textbf{x}=\bm{b}(\textbf{x},t)\mathrm{d}t+\bm{G}(t)\mathrm{d}\hat{\bm{w}},

(2)

where
\bm{b}(\textbf{x},t)=\bm{f}(\textbf{x},t)-\bm{G}(t)\bm{G}(t)^{\!T}\mathbf{s}(\textbf{x},t)
.
With caching, we instead use
\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{\theta}(\textbf{x},t)
, yielding

\mathrm{d}\textbf{x}=\hat{\bm{b}}(\textbf{x},t)\mathrm{d}t+\bm{G}(t)\mathrm{d}\hat{\bm{w}},

(3)

with
\hat{\bm{b}}(\textbf{x},t)=\bm{f}(\textbf{x},t)-\bm{G}(t)\bm{G}(t)^{\!T}\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{\theta}(\textbf{x},t)
.
Caching is viable only if the induced error
\epsilon(\textbf{x},t)=\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\textbf{x},t)-\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{\theta}(\textbf{x},t)
is controlled; otherwise the reverse trajectory drifts from the target distribution.
Our error-feedback mechanism is designed to monitor and correct this error during sampling.

Cached denoising score matching.

Training commonly fits
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\textbf{x},t)
by denoising score matching
(
10
;
36
)
:

\displaystyle\theta^{*}=\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{t,\textbf{x}(0),\textbf{x}(t)}\!\left[\|\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\textbf{x},t)-\mathbf{s}_{t|0}(\textbf{x},t)\|^{2}\right],

(4)

where
\mathbf{s}_{t|0}(\textbf{x},t)=\nabla_{\textbf{x}(t)}\log p_{t|0}(\textbf{x}(t)\mid\textbf{x}(0))
and
t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T)
.
At inference time, replacing
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
by
\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{\theta}
effectively introduces an additional perturbation:

\displaystyle\|\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{\theta}(\textbf{x},t)-\mathbf{s}_{t|0}(\textbf{x},t)\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\textbf{x},t)-\mathbf{s}_{t|0}(\textbf{x},t)+\epsilon(\textbf{x},t)\|^{2},

(5)

highlighting that uncontrolled cache error shifts the effective objective away from what the model was trained for.
Error-feedback directly targets this term via probe recomputations and adaptive correction.

2.4
Frequency-Domain Representation with Cache

DFT and cache-enabling mirror symmetry.

Let
\tilde{\textbf{x}}=\mathcal{F}[\textbf{x}]=U\textbf{x}
be a unitary DFT mapping with
U^{*}U=I
, so
\textbf{x}=U^{*}\tilde{\textbf{x}}
.
For real-valued
x
, the frequency coefficients satisfy the mirror-symmetry constraint

\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{\kappa}=\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{N-\kappa}^{*},

(6)

implying that roughly half of the spectrum is redundant.
Thus we can cache only the non-redundant tokens (e.g.,
\kappa\leq\lfloor N/2\rfloor
), effectively halving cache size and reducing memory and compute overhead in transformer score networks.

Spectral energy localization for cache efficiency.

By Parseval’s theorem,
\|\textbf{x}\|^{2}=\|\tilde{\textbf{x}}\|^{2}
, and empirical time series often exhibit strong low-frequency energy concentration.
This supports aggressive reuse for high-frequency tokens (
\kappa>K
), whose relative change rates
\delta_{\kappa}^{(i)}
are typically smaller, while recomputing low-frequency tokens more frequently.
Moreover, the score in Fourier space inherits the same symmetry: if
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}(\tilde{\textbf{x}})=\nabla\log\tilde{p}(\tilde{\textbf{x}})
, then
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{\kappa}=\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{N-\kappa}^{*}
, further aligning caching with the representation’s intrinsic redundancy.

3.1
Frequency-Domain Diffusion SDEs

We start from a continuous-time forward diffusion in the time domain,

\mathrm{d}x=f(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\,\mathrm{d}w,

(7)

where
w
is a standard Brownian motion and
g(t)
is scalar for simplicity (the VP-SDE case used in experiments is a special case).
Applying the unitary DFT to (
7
) yields a forward diffusion in the frequency domain with a
mirrored
complex Brownian motion that preserves conjugate symmetry.

Proposition 3.1

(Diffusion in the frequency domain)
.

Assume the time-domain forward SDE (
7
) with isotropic diffusion, i.e.,
\mathbf{G}(t)=g(t)\mathbf{I}
(equivalently scalar
g(t)
as written there). Let
\tilde{x}=\mathcal{F}[x]
and define
\tilde{f}(\tilde{x},t)=\mathcal{F}\!\left[f(\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\tilde{x}],t)\right]
.
Then the forward process in the frequency domain is

\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}=\tilde{f}(\tilde{x},t)\,\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{v},

(8)

where
\tilde{v}
is a mirrored Brownian motion enforcing
\tilde{x}_{\kappa}=\tilde{x}^{*}_{N-\kappa}
.
The corresponding reverse-time SDE is

\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}=\Big(\tilde{f}(\tilde{x},t)-g(t)^{2}\,\Lambda^{2}\,\tilde{s}(\tilde{x},t)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\,\mathrm{d}\bar{v},

(9)

with
\tilde{s}(\tilde{x},t)=\nabla_{\tilde{x}}\log\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{x})
, mirrored reverse Brownian motion
\bar{v}
, and a diagonal scaling matrix
\Lambda
(defined in Appendix
A.4
).

Implementation note.

Proposition
3.1
implies that (i) one should only model/cache the half-spectrum tokens, and (ii) the stochastic increments must respect conjugate symmetry; otherwise, the inverse DFT may produce complex-valued signals.

3.2
Denoising Score Matching and a Time-Domain View

Let
\tilde{s}_{\theta}(\tilde{x},t)
be a neural score model in the frequency domain. Training uses denoising score matching (DSM) on
\tilde{x}(t)
sampled from (
8

\theta^{\star}=\arg\min_{\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{t,\tilde{x}(0),\tilde{x}(t)}\Big[\big\|\tilde{s}_{\theta}(\tilde{x}(t),t)-\tilde{s}_{t|0}(\tilde{x}(t),t)\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big],

(10)

where
\tilde{s}_{t|0}(\tilde{x}(t),t)=\nabla_{\tilde{x}(t)}\log\tilde{p}_{t|0}(\tilde{x}(t)|\tilde{x}(0))

The following equivalence connects frequency-domain DSM to an induced time-domain score, clarifying why frequency-domain sampling can still generate real time series after inverse DFT.

Proposition 3.2

(Score-matching equivalence)
.

Assume
\tilde{s}_{\theta}
satisfies conjugate symmetry. Define an induced time-domain score

s^{\prime}_{\theta}(x,t)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left[\tilde{s}_{\theta}(\mathcal{F}[x],t)\right]

Here
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
are applied along the temporal axis, independently for each feature channel of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}

Then minimizing (
10

s^{\prime}_{\theta}
in the time domain (details in Appendix
A.5

From mechanism to algorithm.

The above components jointly define a cache-aware reverse-time sampler for frequency-domain diffusion.
Algorithm
1
presents the complete E

event-driven recomputation, KV caching, and error-feedback correction are integrated into each diffusion step.

Tokenization and half-spectrum.

At step
i
, we represent each non-redundant frequency
\kappa\leq\lfloor N/2\rfloor
by a real token

z^{(i)}_{\kappa}\in\mathbb{R}^{2M}
formed by concatenating real/imag parts of
\tilde{x}^{(i)}_{\kappa}
.
All caching is performed over these half-spectrum tokens.

Cache target: cumulative residual features (CRF).

Instead of caching all intermediate activations, we cache a compact summary at the final layer,

z^{(i)}_{L}=\phi(\tilde{x}^{(i)},t^{(i)}),

(11)

which aggregates residual updates across layers (full form in Appendix
A.6
).
This choice substantially reduces memory footprint while retaining a faithful proxy of representation drift.

Event-driven recomputation.

We measure representation drift via an event intensity

r^{(i)}=\frac{\|z^{(i)}_{L}-z^{(i-\Delta)}_{L}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|z^{(i-\Delta)}_{L}\|_{2}^{2}+\eta},

(12)

and recompute only a subset of tokens

\mathcal{S}^{(i)}=\underbrace{\{0,\dots,K\}}_{\text{always refresh low-freq}}\ \cup\ \underbrace{\{\kappa:\delta^{(i)}_{\kappa}>\tau_{\kappa}\}}_{\text{high-change tokens}}\ \cup\ \underbrace{\mathcal{P}^{(i)}}_{\text{small random probes}},

(13)

where
\delta^{(i)}_{\kappa}=\|z^{(i)}_{\kappa}-z^{(i-\Delta)}_{\kappa}\|_{2}
and
\tau_{\kappa}
is energy-weighted (Appendix
A.6
).
The global intensity
r^{(i)}
uses a normalized squared-change ratio via
\|z^{(i-\Delta)}_{L}\|_{2}^{2}+\eta
, making it less sensitive to global feature scale; per-token decisions already rescale thresholds by spectral energy, so we keep
\delta^{(i)}_{\kappa}
in absolute units to avoid double-normalizing token-wise drift.
Intuitively, low frequencies capture global structure and are refreshed frequently; high frequencies are often stable and can be reused.

Error-feedback stabilization.

To prevent long-horizon drift from stale cached KV states, we periodically probe a small set
\mathcal{P}^{(i)}
and apply a correction

\hat{z}^{(i)}_{\kappa}\leftarrow\hat{z}^{(i)}_{\kappa}+\alpha^{(i)}\big(z^{(i)}_{\kappa}-\hat{z}^{(i)}_{\kappa}\big),

(14)

with step size
\alpha^{(i)}
increasing with
r^{(i)}
.
This closes the loop: caching is aggressive when drift is low, but automatically tightens when drift rises.

At each diffusion step, only a dynamically selected subset of frequency tokens

Discussion

In this work, we introduced E

-CRF to accelerate frequency-domain diffusion transformers for multivariate time series.
Across five public benchmarks, we observed consistent wall-clock speedups of
\sim
2.2
\times
relative to an uncached Fourier-domain baseline while keeping sliced-Wasserstein drift small.

Limitations.

(Scope)
We evaluate multivariate series along a single temporal axis (fixed
N,M
within each dataset after preprocessing), not image/video diffusion backbones where caching has been most heavily explored.

(Baselines)
We report comparisons to an uncached frequency baseline, naive KV reuse, step-reduced DDIM, and ablations; apples-to-apples wall-clock comparisons to image/video DiT cache systems (e.g.,
(
24
;
23
;
31
)
) or diffusion-LLM KV caches
(
25
;
42
)
would require matched model families and codebases, which we leave to future work.

(Memory)
E
2
-CRF retains per-layer KV tensors for half-spectrum tokens, so GPU memory grows with model depth and effective sequence length; scaling to very long horizons may need compression, eviction, or disaggregated KV strategies.

(Step reduction)
The largest latency gains in diffusion often come from aggressive step reduction or alternative parameterizations; caching is best viewed as a complementary per-step optimization when the step budget is fixed.

Data and ethics.

(

Appendix A
Mathematical details

A.1
Notation and Preliminaries

We establish the mathematical framework for our analysis of frequency-domain diffusion with error-feedback caching. Our analysis follows the rigorous style of stochastic differential equation theory, establishing convergence rates, error bounds, and computational complexity guarantees.

State and Representation Spaces
: Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
be the time-domain input space, where
N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
is the number of time steps and
M\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
is the number of features. Let
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}=\mathbb{C}^{N}
be the frequency-domain space after applying the discrete Fourier transform (DFT). The total dimension is
d_{X}=N\cdot M
, and the effective frequency dimension after accounting for mirror symmetry is
N^{\prime}=\lfloor N/2\rfloor+1
.

Diffusion Process
: The forward diffusion process in the time domain is defined by:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\textbf{x}=\bm{f}(\textbf{x},t)\mathrm{d}t+\bm{G}(t)\mathrm{d}\bm{w},

(15)

where
\bm{f}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{X}}\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{X}}
is the drift function,
\bm{G}(t)=g(t)I_{N}
is the diffusion matrix, and
\bm{w}
is a standard Brownian motion. The reverse process uses the score function
\mathbf{s}(\textbf{x},t)=\nabla_{\textbf{x}}\log p_{t}(\textbf{x})
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\textbf{x}=\bm{b}(\textbf{x},t)\mathrm{d}t+\bm{G}(t)\mathrm{d}\hat{\bm{w}},

(16)

where
\bm{b}(\textbf{x},t)=\bm{f}(\textbf{x},t)-\bm{G}(t)\bm{G}(t)^{T}\mathbf{s}(\textbf{x},t)
is the reverse drift.

Frequency Domain Transformation
: The DFT operator
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{X}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d_{X}}
maps time-domain signals to frequency domain via
\tilde{\textbf{x}}=U\textbf{x}
, where
U\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
is the unitary DFT matrix with elements
[U]_{\kappa\tau}=N^{-1/2}\exp(-i\omega_{\kappa}\tau)
and
\omega_{\kappa}=\frac{2\pi\kappa}{N}
.

Caching Components
:

•

\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{\tilde{\theta}}:\mathbb{C}^{d_{X}}\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d_{X}}
: frequency-domain score network

•

\hat{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}}_{\tilde{\theta}}
: cached score approximation using KV pairs

•

\mathbf{z}_{\ell}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
: cumulative residual features (CRF) at layer
\ell
and diffusion step
i

•

\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\ell}^{(i)}
: cached CRF approximation

•

\text{Cache}_{\ell}[k]=(K_{\ell}[k],V_{\ell}[k])
: cached key-value pairs for token
k
at layer
\ell

Error Quantities
:

•

\epsilon_{\ell}^{(i)}=\mathbf{z}_{\ell}^{(i)}-\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\ell}^{(i)}
: CRF approximation error

•

\tilde{\epsilon}(\tilde{\textbf{x}},t)=\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{\theta}(\tilde{\textbf{x}},t)-\hat{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}}_{\theta}(\tilde{\textbf{x}},t)
: score approximation error

•

\Delta\tilde{\textbf{x}}^{(i+1)}=\tilde{\textbf{x}}^{(i+1)}-\tilde{\textbf{x}}^{(i)}
: step-wise change in frequency representation

Mathematical Constants
:

•

\mathcal{B}_{d}(r)=\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\mathbf{z}\|_{2}\leq r\}
:
d
-dimensional ball of radius
r

•

\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{F}}
: supremum norm over function class
\mathcal{F}

•

C,c
: universal constants that may vary between contexts

•

L
: Lipschitz constant of the score network

•

\delta\in(0,1)
: spectral localization parameter (small fraction of energy in high frequencies)

A.2
Assumptions

Our theoretical analysis relies on the following standard assumptions:

Assumption 1 (Spectral Localization):
Real-world time series exhibit spectral localization: for frequency threshold
K=\lfloor N/10\rfloor
, there exists
\delta\ll 1
such that low-frequency components contain at least
(1-\delta)
fraction of the total energy:

\sum_{\kappa=0}^{K}\|\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{\kappa}\|_{2}^{2}\geq(1-\delta)\sum_{\kappa=0}^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}\|\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{\kappa}\|_{2}^{2}.

(17)

Assumption 2 (Bounded State Space):
The frequency-domain state space is bounded:
\tilde{\textbf{x}}(t)\in\mathcal{B}_{d_{X}}(R)
for all
t\in[0,T]
and some
R>0
.

Assumption 3 (Lipschitz Continuity):
The score network
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{\tilde{\theta}}
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous: for all
\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{1},\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{X}}
and
t\in[0,T]
,

\|\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{\tilde{\theta}}(\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{1},t)-\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{\tilde{\theta}}(\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{2},t)\|_{2}\leq L\|\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{1}-\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{2}\|_{2}.

(18)

Assumption 4 (Function Class Complexity):
The score network
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{\tilde{\theta}}
belongs to a function class
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}
with finite Rademacher complexity
\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F}_{\theta})=O(\sqrt{d/n})
, where
d
is the model dimension and
n
is the number of training samples.

Assumption 5 (Diffusion Schedule):
The diffusion schedule
g(t)
is bounded and smooth:
g_{\min}\leq g(t)\leq g_{\max}
for all
t\in[0,T]
, and
|g^{\prime}(t)|\leq G_{\max}
for some constant
G_{\max}
.

Assumption 6 (Error-Feedback Regularity):
The error-feedback correction mechanism satisfies:
\alpha^{(i)}\in[\alpha_{\min},1]
for some
\alpha_{\min}>0
, and corrections are applied at least every
R
steps with
R\geq 1
.

We present our main theoretical results, establishing convergence guarantees, error bounds, and computational efficiency for the E

(Convergence of Cached Score Matching)

and concentration parameter

, E

-CRF achieves:

Cache hit rate

\rho\geq 1-\delta

2.

A.4
Frequency-Domain Diffusion Derivations

A.4.1
Mirrored Brownian Motion and Conjugate Symmetry

Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the (unitary) DFT matrix acting along the temporal axis. For a real-valued process
x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
, its spectrum
\tilde{x}(t)=\mathcal{F}[x(t)]
satisfies
\tilde{x}_{\kappa}(t)=\tilde{x}^{*}_{N-\kappa}(t)
.
To preserve this constraint under stochastic increments, the driving noise in the frequency domain must be
mirrored
.

Lemma A.6

(Mirrored Brownian motion)
.

Let
w(t)
be a standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
and define
\tilde{v}(t)=\mathcal{F}[w(t)]
.
Then
\tilde{v}
is a complex Brownian motion whose increments satisfy conjugate symmetry:

\tilde{v}_{\kappa}(t)=\tilde{v}^{*}_{N-\kappa}(t)
for all
\kappa
.

Proof.

Because
\mathcal{F}
is linear and unitary,
\tilde{v}(t)
has independent Gaussian increments with covariance preserved by
\mathcal{F}
.
Conjugate symmetry follows from the standard real-signal DFT identity applied to each increment
\mathrm{d}w
:

\mathcal{F}[\mathrm{d}w]_{\kappa}=\mathcal{F}[\mathrm{d}w]^{*}_{N-\kappa}
.
∎

A.4.2
Proof of Proposition
3.1

Starting from (
7
), apply
\mathcal{F}
to both sides:

\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}=\mathcal{F}[\mathrm{d}x]=\mathcal{F}[f(x,t)]\,\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\,\mathcal{F}[\mathrm{d}w].

Define
\tilde{f}(\tilde{x},t)=\mathcal{F}\!\left[f(\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\tilde{x}],t)\right]
and
\mathrm{d}\tilde{v}=\mathcal{F}[\mathrm{d}w]
.
By Lemma
A.6
,
\tilde{v}
is mirrored, giving (

).

For the reverse-time SDE, we use the standard score-based SDE reversal result (e.g., Anderson, 1982; Song et al., 2020) applied to the frequency-domain forward process:

\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}=\Big(\tilde{f}(\tilde{x},t)-g(t)^{2}\,\nabla_{\tilde{x}}\log\tilde{p}_{t}(\tilde{x})\Big)\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\,\mathrm{d}\bar{v}.

The diagonal matrix

) accounts for representing complex variables via real coordinates (stacking real/imag parts) while preserving inner products; concretely, if we write

\mathrm{vec}(\tilde{x})\mapsto[\Re(\mathrm{vec}(\tilde{x}));\Im(\mathrm{vec}(\tilde{x}))]\in\mathbb{R}^{2NM}

Proof of Score-Matching Equivalence

A.5.1
Proof of Proposition
3.2

Let
\tilde{s}_{\theta}
be conjugate-symmetric. Define
s^{\prime}_{\theta}(x,t)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\tilde{s}_{\theta}(\mathcal{F}[x],t)]
.
Because
\mathcal{F}
is unitary,
\|a\|_{2}=\|\mathcal{F}[a]\|_{2}
and inner products are preserved.
Moreover, the conditional perturbation kernels satisfy
\tilde{p}_{t|0}(\tilde{x}(t)|\tilde{x}(0))=p_{t|0}(x(t)|x(0))
under the bijection
\tilde{x}=\mathcal{F}[x]
with mirrored noise.
Hence the DSM objective in the frequency domain equals the time-domain DSM objective of the induced score:

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\tilde{s}_{\theta}(\tilde{x}(t),t)-\tilde{s}_{t|0}(\tilde{x}(t),t)\|_{2}^{2}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\|s^{\prime}_{\theta}(x(t),t)-s_{t|0}(x(t),t)\|_{2}^{2}\big].

Therefore minimizing one is equivalent to minimizing the other.

A.6.1
CRF definition

For a transformer score network with residual blocks
F^{(\ell)}
, define the cumulative residual feature (CRF) at the final layer:

z^{(i)}_{L}=h^{(0)}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}F^{(\ell)}(h^{(\ell)},t^{(i)}),

where
h^{(0)}
is the token embedding and
t^{(i)}
is the diffusion time.
We cache the per-token KV states and the final-layer CRF
z^{(i)}_{L}
.

A.6.2
Energy-weighted thresholds

Let
E^{(i)}_{\kappa}=\|\tilde{x}^{(i)}_{\kappa}\|_{2}^{2}
be the token energy. We set

\tau_{\kappa}=\tau_{0}\cdot(\epsilon+E^{(i)}_{\kappa})^{-1},

so high-energy components use stricter thresholds and are refreshed more often.

A.7
Spectral Localization and Cache Efficiency

In this section, we establish the mathematical foundations that enable efficient caching in frequency domain diffusion. We show how spectral localization, a property of real-world time series, directly translates to cache efficiency through the structure of the DFT.

Lemma A.7

(Spectral Energy Concentration)
.

Let
\tilde{\textbf{x}}=U\textbf{x}
be the DFT of a real-valued time series
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{X}}
, where
U
is the DFT matrix. For any frequency threshold
K\in\{0,\ldots,\lfloor N/2\rfloor\}
, define the low-frequency energy as
E_{\text{low}}=\sum_{\kappa=0}^{K}\|\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{\kappa}\|_{2}^{2}
and the total energy as
E_{\text{total}}=\sum_{\kappa=0}^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}\|\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{\kappa}\|_{2}^{2}
. Then, under the spectral localization assumption that most energy is concentrated in low frequencies, there exists
\delta\in(0,1)
such that:

E_{\text{low}}\geq(1-\delta)E_{\text{total}}.

(22)

Proof.

By Parseval’s theorem and the unitarity of
U
(established in

Proposition A.8

(Mirror Symmetry and Cache Dimensionality)
.

Proposition A.10

is an energy-weighted threshold,

•

Random probes: A small fraction of high-frequency tokens are randomly selected for periodic recalibration.

A.11.3
Error Accumulation and Bounds

Let
\epsilon_{\ell}^{(i)}
denote the approximation error at layer
\ell
and step
i
. Under caching, tokens
k\notin S^{(i)}
reuse cached features
\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\ell}^{(i)}=\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{\ell}^{(i-1)}
, while tokens
k\in S^{(i)}
are recomputed. The error evolution follows:

\epsilon_{\ell}^{(i)}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }k\in S^{(i)}\text{ (recomputed)}\\
\epsilon_{\ell}^{(i-1)}+\Delta_{\ell}^{(i)}&\text{if }k\notin S^{(i)}\text{ (cached)},\end{cases}

(42)

where
\Delta_{\ell}^{(i)}=\mathbf{z}_{\ell}^{(i)}-\mathbf{z}_{\ell}^{(i-1)}
is the true change in CRF between steps.

Under the assumption that the score network
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{\tilde{\theta}}
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous with respect to its input, we can bound the error growth. Specifically, if
\|\nabla_{\tilde{\textbf{x}}}\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{\tilde{\theta}}(\tilde{\textbf{x}},t)\|_{2}\leq L
for all
\tilde{\textbf{x}}
and
t
, then:

Proposition A.18

(Error Bound Under Caching)
.

Let
\epsilon_{\ell}^{(i)}
be the approximation error at layer
\ell
and step
i
. If tokens are cached for at most
R
consecutive steps before recomputation, and the score network is
L
-Lipschitz continuous, then:

\|\epsilon_{\ell}^{(i)}\|_{2}\leq L\cdot R\cdot\max_{j\in[i-R,i]}\|\Delta\tilde{\textbf{x}}^{(j)}\|_{2},

(43)

where
\Delta\tilde{\textbf{x}}^{(j)}=\tilde{\textbf{x}}^{(j)}-\tilde{\textbf{x}}^{(j-1)}
is the change in frequency representation at step
j
.

Proof.

The error accumulates only for cached tokens. Since recomputation occurs at least every
R
steps, the maximum error accumulation is bounded by the product of the Lipschitz constant
L
, the caching window
R
, and the maximum change in input
\Delta\tilde{\textbf{x}}^{(j)}
over the caching window.
∎

A.11.4
Error-Feedback Correction

To mitigate error accumulation, we apply error-feedback correction as described in
Equation

14
. Periodically (every
R
steps) or when event intensity exceeds a threshold, we perform a probe computation to estimate the approximation error:

\epsilon_{k}^{(i)}=\mathbf{z}_{k}^{(i)}-\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{k}^{(i)},

(44)

and apply correction:

\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{k}^{(i)}\leftarrow\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{k}^{(i)}+\alpha^{(i)}\cdot\epsilon_{k}^{(i)},

(45)

where
\alpha^{(i)}\in[0,1]
is an adaptive step size. This correction reduces the error from
\epsilon_{k}^{(i)}
to
(1-\alpha^{(i)})\epsilon_{k}^{(i)}
, effectively suppressing error accumulation.

A.11.5
Cache Efficiency and Spectral Localization

The cache efficiency depends on the spectral localization property. Under the assumption that most energy is concentrated in low frequencies
\kappa\leq K
, high-frequency tokens
\kappa>K
change more slowly and are ideal candidates for caching. Formally, if the spectral density satisfies:

\sum_{\kappa=0}^{K}\|\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{\kappa}\|_{2}^{2}\geq(1-\delta)\sum_{\kappa=0}^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}\|\tilde{\textbf{x}}_{\kappa}\|_{2}^{2},

(46)

for some small
\delta>0
, then caching high-frequency tokens
\kappa>K
yields high cache hit rates while maintaining accuracy, since these tokens contribute less to the overall signal energy and change more slowly during diffusion.

A.11.6
KV Cache and Attention Computation

For each transformer layer
\ell
, we cache key-value pairs
\text{Cache}_{\ell}[k]=(K_{\ell}[k],V_{\ell}[k])
for all frequency tokens
k
, as defined in the main text. At diffusion step
i
:

•

If
k\in S^{(i)}
: Recompute
K_{\ell}[k]=W_{K}\mathbf{z}_{k}^{(i)}
and
V_{\ell}[k]=W_{V}\mathbf{z}_{k}^{(i)}
, where
W_{K}
and
W_{V}
are projection matrices.

•

If
k\notin S^{(i)}
: Reuse
\text{Cache}_{\ell}[k]
from the previous step.

The attention computation for cached tokens reduces from
O(d^{2})
(full recomputation) to
O(d)
(lookup), where
d
is the dimension. For a cache hit rate of
\rho
, the computational savings scale as
(1-\rho)\cdot C_{\text{recompute}}+\rho\cdot C_{\text{cache}}
, where
C_{\text{recompute}}
and
C_{\text{cache}}
are the costs of recomputation and caching, respectively.

A.11.7
Convergence Guarantees

Under mild assumptions, the cached diffusion process converges to the same distribution as the baseline (non-cached) process. Specifically, if the error-feedback correction is applied sufficiently frequently and the approximation error remains bounded (as guaranteed by
Proposition

A.18
), then the discrepancy between cached and baseline sampling scales with the maximum approximation error:

\text{TV}(\text{Cached}(\tilde{\textbf{x}}^{(T)}),\text{Baseline}(\tilde{\textbf{x}}^{(T)}))\leq O(\max_{i}\|\epsilon_{L}^{(i)}\|_{2}),

(47)

where TV denotes total variation distance and
\epsilon_{L}^{(i)}
is the final-layer approximation error at step
i
. This bound ensures that cached sampling maintains distributional fidelity as long as the approximation error is controlled.

B.2
Details on evaluation

Sliced Wasserstein distances.

The sliced Wasserstein distance
(
3
)
is a metric which can handle high-dimensional distributions. It is motivated by the fact that the Wasserstein distance is easy to compute when comparing two one-dimensional distributions. The idea of the sliced Wasserstein distance is to map the high-dimensional distributions of interest to one-dimensional distributions, by considering random projections on vectors of the unit sphere. For two distributions
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
, it can be written as:

SW_{p}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}W_{p}(P_{u}\#\mu_{1},P_{u}\#\mu_{2})du

(48)

where
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is the unit sphere in dimension
d
,
P_{u}(x)=u\cdot x
denotes the projection of
x
on
u
,
P_{u}\#\mu
is the push-forward of
\mu
by
P_{u}
, and
W_{p}
is the Wasserstein distance of order
p
. To estimate this quantity in practice, we sample
n=10,000
random vectors
\{u_{i}|i\in[n]\}
which follow a uniform distribution in
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and consider
p=2
. Hence, we can approximate
{SW}_{p}
by the Monte-carlo estimator:

\hat{SW}_{p}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{p}(P_{u_{i}}\#\mu_{1},P_{u_{i}}\#\mu_{2})

(49)

Marginal Wasserstein distances.

In addition to the sliced Wasserstein distance, we also consider the marginal Wasserstein distance. For any
j\in\{1,...,d\}
, the
j
-th marginal Wasserstein distance is defined as:

{MW_{p}}^{(j)}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=W_{p}(P_{e_{j}}\#\mu_{1},P_{e_{j}}\#\mu_{2})

(50)

where
e_{j}
is the
j
-th vector of the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Throughout our experiments in
Section

4
, we compute the Wasserstein distances with respect to
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{train}}
.
