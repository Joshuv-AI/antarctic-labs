Effects of extraction solvents on the recovery of bioactive compounds from Platycladus orientalis Leaves of different tree ages: a comparative metabolomics approach
Authors: Huayu Li, Bei Cui, Haoran Zhao, Guopeng Wang, Yuanyuan Wang, Sheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlatycladus orientalis foliage, referring to the dried twigs and leaves of the Cupressaceae plant P. orientalis, is a traditional Chinese medicine known for its anti-inflammatory and sedative properties. The composition and concentration of metabolites in P. orientalis foliage vary depending on extraction solvents and the age of the tree, which makes the selection of efficient solvents and appropriate tree age crucial.ResultsThis study evaluated solvent extraction efficiency for bioactive compounds from P. orientalis foliage and analyzed metabolic variations across trees aged 5-3000 years. Acetic acid (AcOH) achieved the highest extraction yield (14.17%), while Ethanol (EtOH) yielded the lowest (9.60%). EtOH extracts exhibited the highest total phenolic and flavonoid contents, with flavonoids increasing with tree age. Water and EtOH showed stronger DPPH scavenging than AcOH; water excelled in ·OH scavenging, whereas AcOH led in ABTS scavenging and total antioxidant activity. Older trees consistently outperformed younger ones in antioxidant assays. Both EtOH and AcOH inhibited E. coli and S. aureus; AcOH’s effect against E. coli strengthened with tree age. Water and essential oil showed no antibacterial effects, though all extracts, especially essential oil, inhibited Aspergillus niger. GC-MS identified 88 metabolites and 14 bioactive compounds. β-pinene and geraniol characterized trees under 1000 years, while caryophyllene oxide and α-terpineol accumulated in super-aged trees. These compounds exhibited multi-target pharmacological synergy via anti-tumor, analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective pathways. UHPLC-QE-MS suggested upregulated phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis in 3000-year-old trees, leading to accumulation of key antioxidants like isoeugenol, coniferin, myricetin, and quercetin.ConclusionsThese findings confirm that even at 3000 years of age, P. orientalis maintains a robust capacity to synthesize active metabolites, underscoring its considerable potential as a valuable resource for pharmaceutical research and development. This study highlights the synergistic effects of solvent and tree age on the bioactive compounds in P. orientalis foliage, providing a theoretical basis for optimizing extraction processes and developing high-value natural products. It also underscores the unique value of ancient tree resources in pharmaceutical applications, offering new strategies for plant-derived drug development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09199-x.

Introduction

Platycladus orientalis (L.) Franco, commonly known as oriental arborvitae, is a tree species whose dried twigs and leaves are widely distributed throughout China. In the Compendium of Materia Medica by Li Shizhen of the Ming Dynasty, it is recorded as “a wood of longevity, suitable for medicinal use. Taoists often brew it into decoctions for regular consumption.” Modern research has shown that the leaves of P. orientalis contain flavonoids, phenylpropanoids, terpenoids, volatile oils, and other compounds, which exhibit significant antioxidant, antibacterial, and anti-inflammatory properties [1]. A study found that people in a P. orientalis forest environment tend to experience emotional relaxation, feel refreshed, comfortable, and joyful, with an additional calming effect, suggesting that volatile compounds released from the leaves and bark of P. orientalis play a role in emotional regulation [2].

Research indicates that tree age significantly influences the accumulation of bioactive compounds in plants. In essential oils extracted from 30- and 80-year-old Phoebe zhennan trees, coumarin compounds (such as caryophyllene oxide, elemene, and piperitone) served as characteristic aroma components. Both the variety and relative content of these compounds were significantly higher than those in 10-year-old trees, demonstrating a clear age-dependent accumulation pattern [3]. Similarly, essential oils from the bark of Cinnamomum cassia trees aged 6 to 14 years and found that samples from 13- and 14-year-old trees had higher total flavonoid content and exhibited stronger in vitro antioxidant capacity [4]. Furthermore, P. orientalis shows a similar trend: essential oils extracted from older trees not only possess a richer aroma but also contain higher levels of active compounds and demonstrate superior antibacterial properties compared to those from younger trees [5]. These findings consistently indicate that trees continuously synthesize and accumulate bioactive secondary metabolites as they mature, making tissues from older trees potentially valuable for medicinal and aromatic applications. It is worth noting that, as mentioned earlier, ancient trees are not only important sources of bioactive compounds but also play an irreplaceable role in maintaining ecosystem stability and providing health benefits. Their comprehensive value extends far beyond timber or medicinal use alone.

Extraction is a key step in isolating bioactive compounds from plant materials. However, the composition and yield of extracted compounds can vary significantly depending on factors such as temperature, solvent choice, and operational conditions [6]. Therefore, selecting an appropriate solvent is crucial. Aqueous solutions, AcOH, and EtOH are among the most commonly used extraction solvents [7]. However, owing to the differences in physical and chemical properties of bioactive compounds across plant materials, it is often challenging to identify the most effective solvent [8]. Variations in extraction efficiency primarily stem from interactions between the solvent and the target compounds, guided by the principle of “like dissolves like.” Solvent polarity is a key factor; for instance, 50% acetone was found to be optimal for extracting phenolics from black tea due to its suitable polarity [9]. Additionally, specific molecular interactions, such as hydrogen bonding between EtOH and polyphenols, play an important role. EtOH, for example, has been shown to be more effective in extracting polyphenols and flavonoids from jute [10]. In practice, mixed solvent systems (e.g., EtOH-water) can enhance extraction efficiency by modulating overall polarity and improving penetration into plant cell walls.

To further investigate the synergistic effects of solvent and tree age on the bioactive compounds in P. orientalis leaves, this study analyzed extracts obtained using three different solvents in terms of yield, total phenolic and flavonoid content, and antioxidant activity. Antibacterial activity of the extracts was also compared. GC-MS was employed to examine variations in volatile pharmacological components and their concentrations in leaf extracts from trees of seven different ages (5, 50, 200, 500, 1000, 2000, and 3000 years). Additionally, a comparative metabolomic analysis was conducted on leaves from 1000- and 3000-year-old trees to identify differentially abundant metabolites with significant pharmacological relevance. This approach provides a more detailed understanding of the variations in bioactive compounds across tree ages and offers both theoretical and practical foundations for the comprehensive development and utilization of P. orientalis leaves.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation and pretreatment

P. orientalis leaf samples were collected in 2023 from trees of different ages in Huangling County, Yan’an City, Shaanxi Province, China (N 35°22’; E 109°41’). For all age groups, current-year disease-free leaves were collected from the middle layer of the canopy facing south and east. Fresh leaves were cleaned and air-dried at 50 °C. Following the drying process, the leaves were pulverized into a fine powder. This powder was then passed through an 80-mesh sieve and kept in storage at 4 °C. The age of the P. orientalis trees was determined by the Research Center for Engineering Technology of Ancient Tree Conservation and Breeding at Northwest A&F University. The age of P. orientalis trees was determined as follows: for 5- and 50-year-old trees, ages were confirmed based on afforestation and tending records from the local forestry department; for trees aged 200 years and above, ages were estimated using growth regression models combined with historical records, including local chronicles and ancient tree census archives. The collection of plant material complied with institutional, national, and international guidelines. Permission for sampling P. orientalis leaves in Huangling County was granted by the Administration of Huangdi Mausoleum (Huangling Management Bureau), the governing body responsible for the land.

Reagents and bacterial strains

Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd (China) provided the following six kits: plant flavonoid content assay kit (BC1330), plant total phenolic content assay kit (BC1340), DPPH free radical scavenging capacity detection kit (BC4750), hydroxyl free radical scavenging capacity detection kit (BC1320), ABTS free radical scavenging capacity detection kit (BC4770), and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) assay kit (BC1310). The Beijing Biological Preservation Center (Beijing, China) supplied the following four bacterial strains: Escherichia coli (ATCC 25922), Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 6538), Saccharomyces cerevisiae (ATCC 9763), and Aspergillus niger (CGMCC 3.3928).

Water, EtOH, and AcOH used for extraction were of analytical grade and purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Luria-Bertani (LB) broth, nutrient agar (NA), yeast extract peptone dextrose (YPD) medium, and potato dextrose agar (PDA) were obtained from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Methanol and acetonitrile used for chromatographic analysis were of HPLC grade and purchased from CNW Technologies (Shanghai, China). Ammonium acetate and AcOH used for mobile phase preparation were of mass spectrometry grade and purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Extraction method

Extraction conditions were optimized in preliminary experiments to determine the maximum saturation concentration for each solvent. For water, EtOH, and AcOH, a series of solid-to-liquid ratios (ranging from 1:50 to 5:50, w/v) were tested under the same extraction conditions (ultrasonic-assisted heating at 50 °C for 1 h). The maximum extraction yield was achieved at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 3:50 for water, 2:50 for EtOH, and 2:25 for AcOH; further increases in solid-to-liquid ratio did not significantly improve the extraction yield. Accordingly, based on methodologies described by Ivanov et al. [11], extraction was performed by immersing leaf powder of P. orientalis in 50 mL water, 50 mL EtOH, and 25 mL AcOH at solid-to-liquid ratios of 3:50, 2:50, and 2:25, respectively. The mixture was thoroughly blended, followed by ultrasonic-assisted heating at 50 °C for 1 h (200 W). The resulting extract was then filtered and collected by centrifugation at 5500 r/min for 15 min. For the antimicrobial activity assay, the final extract was concentrated by rotary evaporation, and residual solvent was removed using a nitrogen blowing apparatus. The dried extract was then redissolved in 50% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a stock solution at a concentration of 50 mg/mL for subsequent use. Before the antimicrobial assay, the stock solution was diluted with sterile water to obtain a final DMSO concentration of 1% (v/v). To determine the extraction yield, the final product was likewise concentrated by rotary evaporation, and any residual solvent was removed using a nitrogen blowing apparatus prior to yield calculation [11].

Essential oil extraction and preparation

Dried leaf powder (500 g) was soaked in distilled water at a liquid-to-material ratio of 7:1 (v/w) for 1 h at room temperature. The mixture was then transferred to a distillation apparatus and subjected to steam distillation for 3 h. The resulting distillate was collected and transferred to a separatory funnel, where it was allowed to stand for 1 h to achieve natural phase separation. The lower aqueous layer was discarded, and the upper essential oil layer was collected. The essential oil was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate to remove residual water, and the yield was calculated based on the dry weight of the plant material (mL/100 g). The essential oil was stored in a sealed brown glass vial at 4 °C until further use in antimicrobial assays.

Total flavonoid content

The total flavonoid content (TFC) in the crude extract was quantified using the NaNO2-Al(NO3)3-NaOH chromogenic system. Rutin standard solutions (0.02–1.5 mg/mL) were prepared via serial dilution of a 10 mg/mL stock solution with the standard diluent. Each diluted standard was sequentially treated with NaNO2, Al(NO3)3, and NaOH solutions. After the reaction was complete, the absorbance of each solution was measured at 470 nm. A standard curve was generated from the concentration-absorbance data, yielding the regression equation: Y = 0.6197X + 0.0059, with a coefficient of determination (R²) of 0.9997. Following the kit protocol, 200 µL of the P. orientalis leaf crude extract was analyzed under the same conditions, and the TFC was expressed as milligrams of rutin equivalents per gram of dry crude extract (mg RE/g).

Total phenolic content

The total phenolic content (TPC) in P. orientalis leaf crude extract was assessed using the Folin-Ciocalteu colorimetric method, in accordance with the commercial kit instructions. A standard series of gallic acid (0.15625–0.0025 mg/mL) was prepared by serial dilution of a 5 mg/mL stock solution with distilled water. Each standard was subsequently reacted with Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, Na₂CO₃ solution, and distilled water, followed by incubation in the dark for 10 min at room temperature (25 ± 3 ℃). Absorbance was measured at 760 nm for both the gallic acid standards and the plant crude extract. The standard curve constructed from these data followed the equation Y = 5.5245X + 0.0102, with a coefficient of determination (R²) of 0.9992. TPC results are expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents per gram of dry crude extract (mg GAE/g) [12].

Antioxidant activity

The radical scavenging activities of P. orientalis leaf crude extracts were determined using 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), hydroxyl radical (·OH), 2,2’-Azinobis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS), and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) assays, corresponding to kits BC4750, BC1320, BC4770, and BC1310 (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), respectively, in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. All assays were performed in triplicate.

In the DPPH assay, 25 µL of the crude extract was mixed with 975 µL of DPPH working solution and kept in darkness at ambient temperature (25 ± 3 ℃) for 30 min prior to absorbance measurement at 515 nm. For the ·OH scavenging assay, the detection reagent and 150 µL of crude extract were combined in a 1.5 mL tube and incubated with shaking in a 37 ℃ water bath for 1 h, after which the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 8 min. The resulting supernatant was collected for absorbance reading at 536 nm. In the ABTS assay, 50 µL of crude extract was allowed to react with 950 µL of ABTS working solution, and the absorbance was measured at 405 nm. The scavenging rate for each radical was determined by the decrease in absorbance compared to the control.

For the T-AOC assay, both the FeSO₄ standard solution and the plant crude extract were treated with ferric ion reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) working solution and distilled water [13]. After 10 min of reaction, absorbance was measured at 593 nm. The total antioxidant capacity was expressed as the equivalent concentration of ferrous ions (mmol/mL) required to produce the same absorbance change (ΔA) as the sample.

Antimicrobial assay

Antibacterial activity was assessed using the Oxford cup technique [14]. Briefly, bacterial strains were revived in 5 mL of Luria-Bertani (LB) broth at 37 ℃ with shaking at 200 rpm for 24 h, whereas S. cerevisiae was grown in YPD medium at 28 ℃ for 48 h. Each bacterial suspension was then adjusted to 1 × 10⁶ CFU/mL, and 200 µL was evenly spread on nutrient agar (NA) plates. In the case of A. niger, 200 µL of its spore suspension was applied to Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) plates. Surface-sterilized Oxford cups (0.78 cm in diameter) were placed on the inoculated agar plates. For solvent extracts, 100 µL of the prepared extract solution was added to each Oxford cup. The extract solution was prepared by redissolving the dried extract in 50% DMSO to a concentration of 50 mg/mL, followed by dilution with sterile water to obtain a final DMSO concentration of 1% (v/v). Sterile distilled water was used as the negative control. Prior to the formal antimicrobial assays, 1% DMSO was evaluated as a solvent control under the same experimental conditions, and no detectable inhibition zones were observed against any of the tested microorganisms. For essential oil, the undiluted oil was used directly without further dilution. Plates inoculated with E. coli and S. aureus were kept at 37 ℃ for 24 h, while those containing S. cerevisiae and A. niger were maintained at 28 ℃ for 48 h and 96 h, respectively. For E. coli, S. aureus, and S. cerevisiae, antimicrobial activity was evaluated by measuring the diameter of inhibition zones around the Oxford cups, with a zone size exceeding 1.2 cm regarded as indicative of such activity. For A. niger, due to the spreading growth pattern and sporulation characteristics of the fungus on PDA plates, the antifungal effect was assessed qualitatively by visual observation of mycelial growth and spore formation on days 2 and 3 of incubation.

GC-MS analysis

GC-MS analysis was performed using an Agilent 7890 A gas chromatograph coupled with an Agilent 5975 C mass spectrometer (Agilent, Germany). Compound identification was performed by comparing the obtained mass spectra with the NIST Mass Spectral Library (version 2.0). A compound was considered positively identified when the matching similarity index (SI) was ≥ 800. For key bioactive compounds, identification was further confirmed by comparison of retention indices with those reported in the literature. Retention indices were calculated using a series of n-alkanes (C8 -C40) analyzed under the same chromatographic conditions. The relative content of each identified compound was calculated by the peak area normalization method, expressed as the percentage of the individual peak area relative to the total peak area of all identified compounds in the chromatogram. Only compounds with a peak area percentage ≥ 0.1% were reported.

The GC analysis was performed on an HP-5MS flexible quartz capillary column (0.25 mm × 30 m, 0.25 μm) with high-purity helium as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL·min−¹. The injection volume was 0.8 µL, with the injector set at 230 ℃ and a split ratio of 50:1. The oven temperature program was set as follows: initial temperature of 60 ℃ held for 2 min, increased to 120 ℃ at 5 ℃·min−¹ and held for 2 min, then raised to 180 ℃ at 2 °C·min−¹ and held for 3 min, further elevated to 240 ℃ at 8 ℃·min−¹ and held for 5 min, and finally increased to 280 ℃ at 10 ℃·min−¹ with a final hold time of 5 min. For MS detection, an electron impact (EI) ion source was used with an electron energy of 70 eV. The ion source temperature was maintained at 230 ℃. Data were acquired in full scan mode with a mass range of m/z 30–445, and a solvent delay time of 2.5 min was applied.

Differential metabolomics research

The samples used for metabolomics analysis were the same dried leaf powder prepared as described in Sect. 2.1. For each age group, three individual trees were selected as biological replicates (n = 3). From each biological replicate, approximately 25 mg of dried leaf powder was accurately weighed and combined with 500 µL of extraction solution consisting of methanol and water (3:1, v/v) containing an isotope-labeled internal standard mixture. The mixture was homogenized via grinding at 35 Hz for 4 min, then underwent ultrasonic extraction for 5 min while placed in an ice-water bath. This combined grinding and sonication procedure was carried out three times. Next, the samples were incubated at 40 ℃ for one hour and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm under 4 ℃ for 15 min. The supernatant obtained was transferred to a fresh glass vial for later analysis.

Chromatographic separation was carried out on a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) fitted with a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm). The mobile phase included (A) aqueous solution containing 5 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 5 mmol/L AcOH, and (B) acetonitrile. The injection volume was 2 µL, and the auto-sampler was maintained at 4 ℃.

Raw data files were converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard and processed with an in-house R script based on the XCMS package for peak detection, extraction, alignment, and integration. Metabolite annotation was performed by matching the acquired MS/MS spectra against the BiotreeDB in-house MS2 database (version 2.1), with an annotation cutoff score set at 0.3. Compounds annotated with MS2 matching were used for subsequent analysis, and the MS2 score was used only as an indicator of database spectral matching quality and was not considered definitive evidence of metabolite identification. Compounds annotated solely by MS1 matching were considered tentative and were excluded from biological interpretation.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), derived from three independent replicates. The statistical evaluations were conducted with SPSS 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). To characterize the variation across the distinct extracts, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was employed.

For the extraction yield, total phenolic content, total flavonoid content, antioxidant activities, and antimicrobial activities, two-way analysis of variance (two-way ANOVA) was performed to evaluate the effects of tree age (A), extraction solvent (S), and their interaction (A × S). When significant differences were detected, Duncan’s multiple range test was used for post-hoc comparisons. In the figures, the significance levels of two-way ANOVA are indicated as follows: ns for no significant difference (P ≥ 0.05), * for P < 0.05, and ** for P < 0.01.

For comparisons among different treatment groups within each figure panel, different lowercase letters above the bars indicate significant differences at the P < 0.05 level (Duncan’s multiple range test). Bars sharing the same letter are not significantly different, whereas bars with different letters differ significantly.

Results and discussion

Yield of extract, total flavonoid and phenolic contents

As shown in Table 1, the extraction protocol using AcOH as the solvent achieved the highest yield at 14.17%, followed by the water-based protocol at 10.58% and the EtOH-based protocol at 9.60%. Previous research has suggested that the yield of plant extracts increases with the polarity of the solvent [15]. However, this relationship was not clearly observed in the extraction of P. orientalis leaves with different solvents in this study, such as water, which has higher polarity, resulted in a lower extraction rate (10.58%) compared to AcOH (14.17%).

As shown in Fig. 1(a) and 1(b), the extraction protocol using EtOH as the solvent consistently yielded higher total phenolic and flavonoid contents from P. orientalis leaves across different tree ages, compared to the protocols using water or AcOH. The AcOH extracts showed the lowest levels and were significantly different from those of other solvents. EtOH, as a medium-polarity solvent, efficiently extracts structurally diverse compounds from plants due to its ability to dissolve both polar and non-polar groups. The aromatic ring structures in phenolic and flavonoid molecules contribute to their high compatibility with EtOH [16], which may be the key mechanism behind the significantly elevated levels of these compounds in EtOH extracts.

The total flavonoid contents exhibited a stage-specific accumulation pattern with increasing tree age. At the juvenile stage, the levels of total flavonoids were relatively low. As the trees matured, the contents significantly increased during the middle-age stage. Although slight fluctuations were observed in the older-age stage, the overall levels remained high. No significant differences in total phenolic content were observed across trees of different ages. Research on Salix myrsinifolia has similarly indicated that leaf age does not significantly influence the inducibility of total phenolic compounds [17], a finding that provides important insight into the consistent levels of total phenolics across tree age classes. Although inducibility at the leaf level appears unaffected by developmental stage, the overall phenolic content may remain consistent across ages at the whole-tree level. This pattern may be attributed to the overarching role of environmental stress in regulating secondary metabolism, which can outweigh the influence of tree age. When trees of varying ages grow under similar habitat conditions, they are exposed to comparable abiotic stresses—such as intense light or drought. These pronounced environmental cues likely drive the synthesis of similar levels of total phenolics as a coordinated adaptive response, thereby masking any potential age-related differences at the macroscopic level.

It should be noted that the extraction conditions (solid-to-liquid ratios) were optimized separately for each solvent based on preliminary experiments to achieve maximum saturation concentration. Therefore, the differences observed among solvents reflect the combined effects of solvent properties and their respective optimal extraction conditions, rather than solvent type alone.

Antioxidant activity of the extract

As shown in Fig. 2, among the three solvent extracts, the AcOH extract demonstrated the strongest total antioxidant activity. There was a significant difference in DPPH free radical scavenging capacity between EtOH and AcOH extracts, and their total antioxidant capacities also diverged markedly in trees older than 500 years. However, no statistical difference was observed in their hydroxyl radical (·OH) scavenging efficiencies. Notably, although the AcOH extract exhibited lower total phenolic and flavonoid contents compared to the EtOH extract, it still displayed superior total antioxidant capacity. This apparent contradiction may be related to the content, composition, and interactions of specific flavonoid compounds [8]. For instance, Alimpić A et al. [18] found that while AcOH extracts had lower total phenolic content, they exhibited the strongest hydroxyl radical scavenging ability [18]. Conversely, the ethyl acetate extract of Salvia ringens (Lamiaceae), despite having the highest total phenolic and flavonoid contents, showed significantly weaker antioxidant activity than extracts obtained with other solvents, underscoring the decisive role of flavonoid specificity in determining bioactivity. Thus, this study speculates that AcOH extracts may be enriched with specific flavonoid subclasses possessing strong antioxidant potential.

Furthermore, the two-way ANOVA results indicated that both tree age and extraction solvent had highly significant effects on DPPH radical scavenging rate, ABTS radical scavenging rate, and total antioxidant capacity—though not on hydroxyl radical (·OH) scavenging rate (Fig. 2), further underscoring the important role of tree age in regulating antioxidant performance. The influence of tree age exhibited distinct patterns across different antioxidant evaluation systems. In the DPPH assay, EtOH extracts from 2000- and 3000-year-old trees showed significantly higher activity than those from younger trees, suggesting that advanced age may promote the accumulation of certain lipophilic antioxidant compounds. In contrast, for hydroxyl radical scavenging, aqueous extracts from 500-year-old trees performed best. Moreover, AcOH extracts from the same age group (500 years) also exhibited superior total antioxidant capacity compared to other ages. Overall, leaves from older trees (≥ 500 years) demonstrated advantages across multiple antioxidant metrics. This age-dependent activity profile offers new insights into the accumulation patterns of plant secondary metabolites and provides a scientific basis for the rational development and utilization of P. orientalis leaf resources.

Antimicrobial activity of extracts

As shown in Fig. 3; Table 2, the EtOH extracts exhibited certain inhibitory activity against both S. aureus and E. coli, but the inhibition intensity did not vary significantly across samples of different tree ages. This suggests that the antibacterial properties may not be directly related to tree age, or that the content of relevant active components in P. orientalis is minimally affected by tree age. The AcOH extracts also demonstrated inhibitory activity against both bacterial strains, with the antibacterial strength increasing in a concentration-dependent manner as tree age increased. This phenomenon may be related to changes in the content of the active component α-terpineol. Previous studies have confirmed that α-terpineol exhibits strong inhibitory effects against bacteria such as E. coli and S. aureus in a concentration-dependent manner [19]. Subsequent GC-MS analysis in this study confirmed that the content of α-terpineol in 3000-year-old P. orientalis leaves was relatively higher compared to samples from 1000-year-old trees, consistent with the enhanced activity of AcOH extracts from older trees in antibacterial assays. Additionally, no inhibitory activity was detected for aqueous extracts or essential oil extracts against either bacterial strain.

A. niger is frequently present in cereals, plant-based products, and soil, and is considered a key microorganism contributing to the spoilage of baked goods. It also causes mold development in grains when exposed to high moisture conditions [20]. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the growth inhibitory impact of four distinct extraction solvents on A. niger was monitored during the second and third days of cultivation. On the second day, spore production was visibly inhibited in all tree age groups, with the essential oil showing the best inhibitory effect. By the third day, although some treatment groups showed spore production, inhibition was still evident, with the essential oil demonstrating the strongest suppression and completely preventing spore formation. This may be because the essential oil was extracted using steam distillation, which more effectively preserved the volatile components in P. orientalis leaves [21]. These components often possess strong antimicrobial activity. In contrast, extracts obtained with other solvents may lose or degrade some volatile components due to the differences of extraction methods, thereby reducing their antimicrobial efficacy.

Chemical components of extracts

Through systematic analysis of P. orientalis extracts using GC-MS technology, a total of 88 bioactive metabolites were successfully identified. Based on pharmacological activity screening criteria, this study focused on 14 compounds with high abundance and well-established positive pharmacological effects. Among these, β-pinene and geraniol were enriched in extracts from 5-year and 1000-year-old trees, while caryophyllene oxide and L-α-terpineol showed enrichment in extracts from 1000-year and 3000-year-old trees (Fig. 5). The pharmacological synergy of these components and their potential in disease intervention warrant further investigation.

β-pinene, a monoterpenoid and one of the major components of turpentine, has been confirmed by multiple studies to possess significant antitumor potential. It exhibits strong inhibitory effects on human SW872 liposarcoma cells by inducing morphological changes such as cell shrinkage, rounding, and increased intercellular space, as well as triggering toxic responses and cell death [22]. Geraniol, another monoterpenoid, demonstrates broad pharmacological activities, including inhibition of pathogenic microorganisms, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antitumor proliferation, and neuroprotective effects [23]. Studies have shown that geraniol interferes with DNA synthesis and synergistically enhances the inhibitory effect of the chemotherapeutic drug 5-fluorouracil on human colon cancer xenografts. In cellular experiments, geraniol at a concentration of 150 µM reduced the expression levels of thymidylate synthase and thymidine kinase by approximately 50%, an effect not observed with 5-fluorouracil treatment alone. In animal models, the combined use of 20 mg/kg 5-fluorouracil and 150 mg/kg geraniol resulted in a 53% reduction in tumor volume; geraniol alone reduced tumor volume by 26%, while 5-fluorouracil alone showed no inhibitory effect [24].

Caryophyllene oxide, when combined with paracetamol (acetaminophen), demonstrated synergistic anti-nociceptive effects in CD1 mouse models, significantly enhanced the analgesic and gastric mucosal protective activities of the combination [25]. Furthermore, β-caryophyllene, the oxide precursor of caryophyllene, significantly improved neurological function in MCAO rats within a safe dosage range, effectively reducing cortical neuron damage and apoptosis, thus exhibiting protective effects against cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury [26]. α-terpineol alleviates cancer-related pain by modulating oxidative stress and inhibiting the production of inflammatory factors such as IL-1β [27]. The synergistic enrichment of caryophyllene oxide and α-terpineol in high-age P. orientalis reflects that P. orientalis leaves retain a robust capacity for secondary metabolite synthesis and accumulation even at 3000 years of age.

Comparative metabolomics of P. orientalis leaves from 1000- and 3000-year-old trees

Global metabolomic profiling of P. orientalis leaves from 1000- and 3000-year-old trees

Based on UHPLC-QE-MS technology, this study conducted metabolomic analysis on the leaves of P. orientalis with tree ages of 1000 and 3000 years. To investigate the metabolic differences between super-aged (3000 years) and ordinary ancient (1000 years) P. orientalis trees, untargeted metabolomics analysis was performed on dried leaf powder (prepared as described in Sect. 2.1) from three individual trees per age group (n = 3, each representing an individual tree). As shown in Appendix A2, the overall trends of the total ion chromatograms in both positive and negative ion modes were generally similar between the two age groups, with minor differences observed at specific time points, indicating stable overall analytical performance of the instrument.

Qualitative and quantitative analyses were conducted on the components of P. orientalis leaves from two age groups. After manual curation of the MS/MS-based annotations, a total of 253 putatively annotated differential metabolites were retained in the samples, including primary and secondary metabolites. The putatively annotated metabolites were classified and statistically analyzed based on their chemical categorization, and the proportions of each metabolite category are illustrated in Appendix A3. Representative MS/MS mirror plots of the key metabolites discussed in the manuscript are provided in Appendix A4, while examples of excluded questionable annotations are shown in Appendix A5.

Differential metabolite analysis of P. orientalis leaves from 1000- and 3000-year-old trees

Annotation and analysis of differential metabolites

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to gain an initial understanding of the overall metabolic differences between sample groups, as well as the variations observed within each group. As presented in Appendix A6a, the first two principal components contributed 29.6% and 24.2% of the total variance, respectively, revealing a distinct separation trend between groups and reflecting underlying differences in metabolite composition.

The OPLS-DA score scatter plot (Appendix A6b) further illustrates this separation, with distinct colors denoting different sample groups and showing clear segregation between them.

Both PCA and OPLS-DA score plots consistently indicate distinguishable clustering trends in metabolites extracted from P. orientalis leaves across different tree ages.

To further explore variations in secondary metabolites between the two age groups, leaves from 1000-year-old trees served as the control. Differential metabolites were screened by combining fold-change analysis with an OPLS-DA model based on data acquired in positive ion mode, using screening thresholds of VIP > 1 and a P-value < 0.05 from Student’s t-test. The resulting metabolites are illustrated in Appendix B1.

The differential metabolites were screened and visualized in a volcano plot (Fig. 6a). Each point in the volcano plot represents a metabolite, with the blue points indicating significantly down-regulated differentially expressed metabolites, the red points indicating significantly up-regulated differentially expressed metabolites, and the gray points representing metabolites with no significant difference in relative content. The results revealed that in the leaves of 3000-year-old P. orientalis, 199 metabolites were significantly up-regulated compared to those in 1000-year-old trees, while only 54 metabolites were down-regulated. Among these, 24 flavonoids, 15 organic acids, 15 phenylpropanoids,13 terpenoids, 12 lipids, 8 glycosides, 7 steroids, and 7 alkaloids showed higher relative abundances. The categories with the highest numbers of differential metabolites were flavonoids and terpenoids, followed by organic acids and phenylpropanoids.

Pathway enrichment analysis of differential metabolites

Through pathway enrichment analysis of 253 significantly differential metabolites using the KEGG database, it was found that 144 metabolites with significant differences between the leaves of 3000-year-old P. orientalis and those of 1000-year-old trees were annotated to 50 metabolic pathways (Fig. 6b). The most significantly enriched pathways were arachidonic acid metabolism, followed by tyrosine metabolism, amino acid biosynthesis, ABC transporters, phenylalanine metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, D-amino acid metabolism, flavonoid biosynthesis, among others. KEGG pathway annotation revealed that these pathways are primarily involved in three major biological processes: lipid signaling, amino acid metabolism, and the synthesis of secondary metabolites.

Analysis of major differential metabolite components

China hosts the world’s largest population of thousand-year-old P. orientalis, including five individuals over 5000 years old. The Huangling region alone contains more than 34,000 such trees exceeding a millennium in age. While thousand-year-old P. orientalis is relatively common in China, those reaching 3000 years are considerably rarer. To clarify the basal metabolite profile in leaves of 3000-year-old trees, we conducted untargeted metabolomics. This approach aimed to identify differences in bioactive compounds between super-aged (≥ 3000 years) and commonly encountered ancient trees, thereby providing a data foundation for better utilization of these ancient biological resources.

Metabolomics provides insights into the entire metabolic process of plants and validates the accuracy of previous physiological measurements. To investigate the metabolite differences in extracts from leaves of P. orientalis of two ages, this study analyzed key pharmacologically active metabolites such as flavonoids and terpenoids in 3000-year-old versus 1000-year-old samples. Flavonoids play a crucial role in plant defense responses both in vivo and in vitro. Previous physiological measurements had already indicated sustained high flavonoid content in older trees. Metabolomic analysis suggested that, in positive ion mode, pathways related to flavonoid synthesis—such as phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, phenylalanine metabolism, and flavonoid biosynthesis—were upregulated in 3000-year-old trees. Key metabolites showing increased abundance included chlorogenic acid, L-phenylalanine, isoeugenol, sinapyl alcohol, coniferin, L-tyrosine, 2-hydroxycinnamic acid, epiafzelechin, and myricetin. Similarly, in negative ion mode, elevated levels of myricetin, naringenin, epicatechin, 3-hydroxyphenylacetic acid, L-tyrosine, sinapic acid, and trans-cinnamic acid were detected in the 3,000-year-old group (Fig. 7).

Notably, isoeugenol, sinapyl alcohol, coniferin, myricetin, naringenin, and sinapic acid showed marked upregulation in 3,000-year-old leaves, potentially underpinning the high antioxidant capacity, antimicrobial activity, and medicinal potential observed in the older trees. Among these, isoeugenol is a natural aromatic compound with documented antibacterial effects against pathogens such as S. aureus [28]. Sinapyl alcohol, a key intermediate in the semi-synthesis of vanillin and a natural fragrance, exhibits inhibitory activity against S. aureus and E. coli, alongside antioxidant properties that mitigate oxidative cellular damage [29]. Coniferin acts as a natural antioxidant, scavenging free radicals and alleviating oxidative stress [30]. Myricetin, a natural flavonoid, mitigates oxidative stress and inflammation by neutralizing free radicals and modulating signaling pathways such as NF-κB. It also inhibits cancer cell proliferation and induces apoptosis, showing potential efficacy against liver, lung, and other cancers, alongside antimicrobial and antiviral activities [31]. Moreover, quercetin and its derivatives (e.g., quercetin-3-galactoside) were among the most significantly upregulated metabolites, forming a core component of the observed metabolic differences with clear biological relevance. Quercetin exerts dual mechanisms by arresting the cell cycle and activating apoptotic pathways. In vitro studies confirm its growth-inhibitory effects on diverse malignancies, including leukemia, gastric, colon, lung, and glioma cells, establishing it as a plant-derived antitumor agent of significant research interest [32]. In an in vivo study, Guo et al. (2012) administered quercetin to SD rats intracranially implanted with C6 glioma cells. Results demonstrated that quercetin significantly suppressed glioma growth, likely through the induction of apoptosis and inhibition of tumor cell proliferation [33]. Collectively, these findings indicate that even at 3,000 years of age, P. orientalis leaves maintain high levels of bioactive compounds, supporting strong antioxidant and antimicrobial functions. Although certain medicinal properties highlighted here remain to be fully validated, the notably high relative abundance of these metabolites underscores the potential of ancient trees as valuable resources for pharmaceutical and health-promoting applications.

It should be noted that the UHPLC-QE-MS-based metabolomics in this study was untargeted, and metabolite annotation was mainly based on MS/MS spectral matching against a database. Therefore, the annotated metabolites should be considered putative unless further confirmed by authentic standards.

Conclusions

This study extracted leaves of P. orientalis of different tree ages (5–3000 years) using three solvents: water, EtOH, and AcOH. Significant differences were observed among the various solvent extracts in terms of yield, chemical composition, antioxidant activity, and antibacterial activity. Under the extraction conditions optimized for each solvent, AcOH extraction achieved the highest yield (14.17%), while EtOH extracts contained the highest levels of total phenols and flavonoids, with flavonoid content showing an increasing trend with tree age. These differences reflect the combined effects of solvent properties and their respective optimal extraction parameters, rather than solvent type alone. Regarding antioxidant activity, water extracts exhibited the strongest hydroxyl radical (·OH) scavenging ability, EtOH showed significant DPPH radical scavenging effects, and AcOH performed best in ABTS scavenging and total antioxidant capacity. Furthermore, leaves from older trees (≥ 500 years) consistently demonstrated stronger antioxidant activity across multiple evaluation metrics. In antibacterial experiments, EtOH and AcOH extracts inhibited E. coli and S. aureus, with the inhibitory effect of AcOH on E. coli strengthening as tree age increased. Essential oil extracts demonstrated the best inhibitory effect on A. niger.

Based on GC-MS analysis, β-pinene and geraniol were relatively enriched in 5- and 1000-year-old P. orientalis leaves, while caryophyllene oxide and L-α-terpineol were relatively enriched in 1000- and 3000-year-old P. orientalis leaves. Untargeted metabolomics analysis further suggested that, compared with 1000-year-old trees, several putatively annotated secondary metabolites associated with the phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthetic pathways (such as isoeugenol, coniferin, quercetin, and myricetin) were higher in the leaves of 3000-year-old P. orientalis. These results indicate that even at 3000 years of age, P. orientalis leaves maintain a strong capacity for the synthesis and accumulation of secondary metabolites. These findings provide insights into the selection of solvents for specific purposes when detecting P. orientalis leaves, and offer a chemical and metabolomics basis for the further development and utilization of ancient tree resources for medicinal purposes.
