Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains the third most common malignancy worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1]. Approximately 5% of metastatic CRC cases exhibit the highly immunogenic microsatellite unstable (MSI) or deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) genotype which is highly responsive to immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) immunotherapy [2]. Conversely, the remaining ∼95% of patients present with a microsatellite stable (MSS) or proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) genotype [3], representing a cohort characteristically resistant to standard ICI regimens [4,5].

Patients with ICI-resistant mCRC who have progressed on standard oxaliplatin- and irinotecan-based chemotherapy are considered eligible for third-line salvage treatments. Current regulatory-approved third-line (3 L) interventions include regorafenib, trifluridine/tipiracil (TAS-102) administered alone or in combination with bevacizumab, and fruquintinib [6]. However, these established therapeutic options demonstrate marginal efficacy and carry cumulative systemic toxicities. Consequently, a pronounced clinical unmet need remains for innovative strategies to manage 3 L MSS/pMMR mCRC.

To address this challenge, we developed a translational protocol framework designed to alter the host immune environment in MSS/pMMR patients utilizing the immunomodulatory properties of an investigational, living allogeneic Th1 immune cell formulation (AlloStim®). Although typically classified as immunotherapy-refractory, advanced CRC lesions remain susceptible to graft-versus-tumor (GVT) immune dynamics [7]. The cellular protocol evaluates the reverse engineering of GVT mechanisms natively observed in non-myeloablative allogeneic stem cell transplantation [7,8]. This operational framework isolates favorable anti-tumor GVT cascades from systemic graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) pathologies, while omitting the prerequisites of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) tissue matching or host myeloablative pre-conditioning [[9], [10], [11]].

Overcoming resistance in "cold" MSS/pMMR tumors requires coordinated immunomodulation to transition the tumor microenvironment (TME) toward an inflamed state resembling responsive "hot" MSI/dMMR malignancies. To establish the design parameters of this protocol, the baseline physiological variations between responsive and non-responsive phenotypes were evaluated. A primary determinant of ICI responsiveness in MSI/dMMR tumors is an elevated tumor mutational burden (TMB), which inherently increases tumor immunogenicity [12]. However, because allogeneic cell therapies, such as AlloStim® do not alter host genomic TMB directly, our protocol development focused on the immunomodulation of downstream mechanisms associated with neoantigen expression and presentation.

Clinical observation indicates that a hypermutated genomic profile alone is insufficient for therapeutic response, as a subset of patients with high-TMB MSI/dMMR tumors still fail to benefit from ICI [13,14]. Comparative analyses of responsive versus non-responsive high-TMB cohorts reveal that elevated expression of mutation-associated neoantigens (MANA) strictly correlates with positive therapeutic outcomes [15,16]. In contrast, low-TMB MSS/pMMR tumors display restricted MANA presentation [17]. Given that exogenous upregulation of tumor neoantigens has shown limited clinical success [18], this protocol was structured to enhance host recognition of the constrained, endogenous neoantigens natively expressed within MSS/pMMR tumors.

Immune recognition within low-MANA environments depends heavily on the cellular composition of the TME. Infiltrated "hot" tumors are characterized by an accumulation of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (Tc1), CD4+ Th1 cells, M1 macrophages, natural killer (NK) cells, and IL-12+ dendritic cells (DC1), which heavily correlates with favorable prognosis [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. Therefore, converting a non-inflamed, "cold" microenvironment into an inflamed, cell-dense destination is a foundational element of the strategy [23].

Imprinting a "hot" inflammatory infiltrate upon a pre-existing "cold" tumor presents a distinct translational challenge. When the host immune system has previously encountered localized antigens without achieving clearance, subsequent chronic exposure typically results in functionally impaired anti-tumor effector cells [17]. This dysfunctional priming is associated with initial antigen exposure occurring within an immunosuppressive, tolerogenic TME [24], ultimately yielding clonal T-cell exhaustion and peripheral neoantigen tolerance [25].

The efficacy of ICI mandates the pre-existence of primed, functional, infiltrating effector cells within the TME [26]. In responsive "hot" tumors, these pre-existing cells possess the capacity to execute tumor clearance but are functionally restricted by inhibitory checkpoint signaling. Under these conditions, monoclonal antibodies that disrupt the negative signaling pathways can successfully rescue anti-tumor immunity [27]. However, in the absence of a baseline functional infiltrate, checkpoint blockade remains mostly ineffective [28].

To bypass the barriers of peripheral tolerance and limited mutational burden, the current translational framework utilizes a dual-route, sequential cellular delivery method designed to convert the local and systemic tumor microenvironments from "cold" to "hot." Rather than requiring complex ex-vivo antigen manufacturing, this strategy leverages the patient's own endogenous tumor tissue as an individualized antigen repository via a synchronized in situ vaccination (ISV) cascade. Neoantigen-based therapeutic vaccination strategies are capable of expanding the endogenous host T-cell repertoire to target specific malignant clones [29].

Therapeutic cancer vaccine strategies are being pursued which aim to identify tumor neoantigens and combine these with immunoenhancing adjuvants [30], including development of vaccination strategies against CRC [31]. Whole-exome and RNA sequencing protocols have identified immunogenic candidate neoantigens in CRC capable of driving tumor-specific cytotoxic clearance [32], indicating that targeted neoantigen engagement remains a viable anti-tumor approach.

However, identifying tumor neoantigens for use in cancer vaccines presents several challenges, including tumor heterogeneity and high degree of HLA polymorphism [33]. Selection of neoantigens to incorporate in a vaccine formulation requires that processed antigen epitopes are capable of binding to a patient’s own major histocompatibility complex (MHC) alleles [34], which further limits identification and selection of neoantigens for vaccine formulation.

Personalized formulations face significant logistical hurdles in late-stage mCRC, including the difficulty of isolating viable immunogenic candidates from a constrained mutational pool, navigating spatial tumor heterogeneity, and accommodating complex patient HLA polymorphisms. To overcome these barriers, the protocol aims to enhance cellular immune function and create an in-situ vaccination process [35] similar to the abscopal effect that can occur occasionally after radiotherapy [36], allowing the host immune system to select the neoantigens.

To elicit this mechanism (see Fig. 1 for illustration of the mechanism and Supplemental Note 1 for detailed analysis of the mechanistic details), this protocol incorporated three distinct operational steps utilizing the immunomodulatory properties of mismatched living allogeneic cell therapy [37]:

The following are the steps in the mechanism with the numbers corresponding to the number labels in the illustration: (1) the fully allogeneic AlloStim® cells are injected ID, the activated Th1 cells condition the local microenvironment by producing IFN-γ and expressing CD40L and within this inflammatory microenvironment are rejected by the host immune system; (2) the debris from the rejected cells is engulfed by local dendritic cells (Langerhans cells) in the skin and the alloantigens processed and presented on MHC molecules; (3) the DC mature in the inflammatory microenvironment to DC1, producing IL-12 and expressing CD80/86 co-stimulatory molecules and traffic to the draining lymph node; (4) cognate CD4+ cells interact with the DC1 and differentiate into allo-specific Th1 cells that enter circulation, multiple ID injections result in the differentiation of allo-specific Th1 memory cells; (5) the increase in circulating Th1 cells corrects the Th1/Th2 balance; (6) infusion of IV AlloStim® after ID priming creates a systemic rejection effect that releases IFN-. and IL-12, which serve to non-specifically activate allo-specific Th1 and NK cells; (7) the activated Th1 and NK cells extravasate and enter tumor lesions and produce inflammatory cytokines converting the tumor lesions from ‘cold’ to ‘hot’; (8) activated NK cells within the lesions cause immuno-logical cell death of some tumor cells releasing DAMP and inflammation which creates conditions for in-situ vaccination and de-novo tumor priming; (9) tumor debris resulting from NK rejection is processed by local DC which mature in the inflammatory microenvironment to DC1, producing IL-12 and ex-pressing CD80/86 co-stimulatory molecules and then traffic to the draining lymph node and interact with cognate CD8+ T-cells which mature to tumor-specific CTL; (10) tumor-specific CTL enter circulation; (11) additional ID AlloStim® injections amplify the circulating allo-specific Th1 in circulation; (12) additional IV AlloStim® infusion results in a rejection response that releases inflammatory cytokines which activates memory allo-specific Th1 cells, NK cells and memory tumor-specific CTL; (13) the activated memory allo-specific Th1, NK cells and tumor-specific CTL extravasate and infiltrate tumor lesions causing both tumor-specific and non-specific immunological cell death, inflammation and release of danger signals; (14) resulting tumor debris is engulfed by local DC and the tumor neoantigens processed in the context of inflammation; (15) the DC mature to DC1 and then traffic to the draining lymph node and interact with cognate CD8+ T-cells which mature to tumor-specific CTL; (16) de-novo tumor-specific CTL enter circulation increasing the CTL titer; (17) additional ID AlloStim® injections amplify allo-specific Th1 cells in circulation; (18) rejection of IV AlloStim® infusion releases cytokines which activate NK, allo-specific Th1 and tumor-specific CTL; (19) activated NK, allo specific Th1 and tumor-specific CTL extravasate and enter tumor lesions and cause amplified inflammation and tumor cell death.

Step 1: Intradermal (ID) Priming and Systemic Th1 Polarization. The mechanism initiates with repeated intradermal injections of allogeneic, activated memory Th1 cells (AlloStim®). The intradermal introduction of fully mismatched allogeneic cellular material triggers a potent host-versus-graft (HVG) rejection response at the injection site. This acute rejection creates a localized inflammatory center dominated by Type I cytokines, including interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and interleukin-12 (IL-12). This localized Th1-polarized microenvironment serves to reprogram circulating host antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and naive T-helper cells, effectively shifting the systemic host immune status away from a tumor-induced, immunosuppressive Th2 or regulatory T-cell (Treg) baseline and toward an active, defensive Th1 state through systemic population of alloantigen-specific Th1 cells.

Step 2: Intravenous Infusion and Tumor Microenvironment (TME) Targeted Inflammation. Following systemic priming, the protocol introduces the allogeneic cells via intravenous (IV) infusions. This initiates a HVG rejection which releases inflammatory cytokines which serve to activate host memory allo-specific Th1 cells and NK cells. These activated cells track toward highly vascularized metastatic beds, releasing a secondary wave of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This systemic and peri‑tumoral inflammation upregulates vascular adhesion molecules (such as VCAM-1 and ICAM-1) on the tumor endothelium, permitting host effector cells—previously barred from entering "cold" lesions—to extravasate directly into the TME, successfully converting the non-inflamed tumor into a "hot," cell-dense inflammatory zone.

Step 3: In Situ Vaccine Generation and Adaptive Immune Control. Once the TME is successfully inflamed, the final phase of the translational blueprint is realized: the generation of a durable, adaptive anti-tumor immune response. The influx of activated host NK cells, Th1 cells, cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), and M1 macrophages into the newly inflamed TME mediates targeted lysis of resident colorectal cancer cells. This localized tumor immunological cell death (ICD) liberates mutation-associated neoantigens (MANA) while complexed with endogenous cellular chaperones, specifically heat shock proteins (HSPs) such as gp96, HSP70, and HSP90.

Within this IFN-γ-rich microenvironment, mature host dendritic cells (DCs) capture these chaperoned tumor neoantigens via receptor-mediated endocytosis, facilitating efficient cross-presentation on host HLA Class I and Class II molecules. By presenting these previously tolerated neoantigens in the presence of strong “danger signals”, the protocol bypasses baseline peripheral tolerance. This initiates a de-novo, polyclonal adaptive immune response tailored to the patient’s evolving metastatic variants, establishing a state of immune-mediated tumor equilibrium designed to restrict disease progression.

To evaluate the operational feasibility, safety, and mechanistic validity of this framework to address tumor immunoavoidance and immunosuppressive limitations via an immunomodulatory cascade, we conducted an exploratory, single-arm, multi-center Phase 2B proof-of-concept study in heavily pre-treated third-line (3 L) MSS/pMMR mCRC patients. The study specifically investigated whether this multi-step translational framework could elicit an overall survival (OS) benefit within this refractory population, while characterizing the clinical and radiological discordance in converting “cold” tumors to “hot” tumors inherent to this immunomodulatory approach.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approvals

This exploratory, open-label, multi-center, single-arm Phase 2B study (“STIMVAX”) [38] was conducted across four outpatient oncology clinics in the United States to evaluate a sequential, dual-route allogeneic immunomodulatory protocol utilizing an investigational living allogeneic Th1 cell formulation called “AlloStim®” (Immunovative Therapies, Ltd., Jerusalem, Israel). Patient recruitment and enrollment spanned from June 30, 2021 through May 6, 2024, with the final data lock for survival follow-up occurring on January 16, 2026. The study adhered strictly to the guidelines of Good Clinical Practice (GCP) and the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The operational protocol was cleared by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) under an active Investigational New Drug (IND) application and registered prospectively (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04444622).

Prior to study initiation, all protocol documents, patient informed consent materials, and subsequent amendments were independently reviewed and approved by a central institutional review board (IRB) and relevant local IRBs. An independent Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) regularly reviewed unblinded safety profiles, treatment compliance, and donor batch integrity during the active trial phases. All participants provided written informed consent prior to the initiation of any protocol-specific screening, baseline imaging, or clinical procedure.

Cellular formulation manufacturing and product specifications

The experimental cellular agent, AlloStim®, used in this protocol consists of a living, activated, non-genetically modified, allogeneic Th1 cell formulation. Source whole blood was obtained from healthy, intentionally human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-mismatched blood donors. All donors underwent rigorous screening and testing in accordance with 21 CFR Part 1271 requirements for human cells, tissues, and cellular and tissue-based products (HCT/Ps) at a FDA-registered facility. Donor evaluations included a comprehensive medical history review, physical examination, American Association of Blood Banks (AABB) Donor History Questionnaire (DHQ v.4), travel and lifestyle history assessments, and clinical laboratory screening. FDA-licensed infectious disease testing was conducted at a CLIA-certified laboratory for: HIV-1, HIV-2, HTLV-1, HTLV-2, cytomegalovirus (CMV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), hepatitis B virus (HBV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), Treponema pallidum (syphilis), Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and West Nile Virus (WNV). Final donor eligibility was established by a designated qualified individual following regulatory review.

The Th1 cells were differentiated and expanded ex vivo from CD4+ T-cell precursors purified from buffy coat separated from the whole blood and further manufactured under current Good Manufacturing Practices (cGMP) regulations (21 CFR Parts 210, 211, and 600). Prior to batch release, the finished cellular product underwent validation for identity, functionality, and safety, including USP 〈71〉 sterility and USP 〈63〉 mycoplasma testing. The cellular product was formulated with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 monoclonal antibody-coated microbeads at a fixed 1:1 bead-to-cell ratio.

The bead-bound cells are suspended at a density of 1 × 107 cells/mL in a formulation buffer consisting of PlasmaLyteA (Baxter International) supplemented with 5% human serum albumin (HSA) and 2% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Aliquots were distributed into individual dose vials and cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen (LN2) for storage. Vials were transported to the clinical sites in validated LN2 dry shipper containers. Immediately prior to clinical administration, the vials were thawed in a dry bath without washing or secondary formulation.

The pre-cryopreservation and post-thaw cellular product demonstrated a consistent identity phenotype characterized as IFN-γ+, CD4+, CD45RO+, CD62Llo, CD40Lhi, CD25+ with post-thaw cellular viability consistently exceeding the release criteria threshold of 80%.

Patient eligibility

To facilitate contextual tracking against historical benchmarks for third-line salvage therapies, inclusion and exclusion criteria were aligned with populations studied in previous late-stage metastatic CRC registration trials [6].

Key inclusion criteria

Eligible patients were required to be between 18 and 80 years of age with histologically or cytologically confirmed metastatic colorectal adenocarcinoma with documented microsatellite stable (MSS) or proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) status. Candidates must have exhibited objective disease progression during or within 3 months following at least two prior standard systemic chemotherapy regimens for advanced disease, which explicitly included one oxaliplatin-containing regimen (e.g., FOLFOX) and one irinotecan-containing regimen (e.g., FOLFIRI). Prior therapy with an anti-EGFR monoclonal antibody, such as cetuximab or panitumumab, was mandatory for all individuals presenting with KRAS/NRAS wild-type tumors. Patients were required to possess an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0 or 1, having discontinued their prior lines of therapy due to documented disease progression or unacceptable, cumulative toxicity. Furthermore, participants had to demonstrate adequate baseline organ function at the start of the trial, defined as acceptable bone marrow, hepatic, and renal laboratory parameters.

Key exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded with a documented history of active autoimmune disease, an ongoing requirement for systemic immunosuppressive therapies, or confirmed positive serology for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis B virus (HBV), or hepatitis C virus (HCV). Candidates were also deemed ineligible if they presented with any uncontrolled or severe concurrent medical disorders, active systemic infections requiring intravenous antimicrobial therapy, or unstable cardiac disease that would compromise safe adherence to the protocol.

Clinical protocol

The operational framework evaluated a sequential, dual-route cell delivery schedule organized into three consecutive 28-day cycles (“STIMVAX”). Each cycle comprised weekly AlloStim® intradermal (ID) injections on days 0, 7, 14, and 21, culminating in a combined ID injection and intravenous (IV) infusion on day 28.

Cycle 1: Days 0, 7, 14, and 21 received 0.5 mL ID cellular formulation (5 × 106 cells). Day 28 received 0.5 mL ID injection and a concurrent 3.0 mL IV infusion 3 × 107 cells).

Cycle 2: Following a 14-day rest interval, treatments resumed on Day 42, Day 49, Day 56, and Day 63 with 0.5 mL ID injections. Day 70 administered the combined 0.5 mL ID and 3.0 mL IV doses.

Cycle 3: Following a 14-day rest interval, treatments resumed on Day 84, Day 91, Day 98, and Day 105 with 0.5 mL ID injections. Day 112 administered the final protocol-defined combined 0.5 mL ID and 3.0 mL IV doses.

For patients who successfully completed the three core cycles without experiencing limiting toxicity or clinical deterioration, monthly 3.0 mL IV booster infusions were made available as an optional extension for up to an additional 9 months. Discontinuation of the protocol occurred early for patients exhibiting acute clinical deterioration or unmanageable protocol-related toxicities, with all early withdrawals explicitly tracked in the trial's flow schema.

Radiological response evaluations were performed using contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scans of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis at baseline and within 7 days of completing Cycle 3 (Day 119 ± 3 days). For patients transitioning to the optional booster phase, maintenance CT scans were performed every 8 weeks ±1 weeks) thereafter. Tumor responses were evaluated by local site investigators for clinical assessment of response. Efficacy evaluation was conducted retrospectively by an independent radiologist reviewer in accordance with Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1.

Following the completion of Cycle 3, all patients entered a standardized long-term survival follow-up phase, regardless of booster status. Follow-up continued until death, unmanageable protocol-related toxicity, or withdrawal of consent. Patients received best supportive care throughout the study period. Concomitant administration of other investigational cellular therapies, approved cytotoxic chemotherapies, targeted biologics, or systemic immunotherapies was prohibited. Localized palliative radiation therapy for bone pain management was permitted when clinically indicated.

Statistical design and sample size considerations

This study was designed as an exploratory, single-arm, multi-center, Phase 2B proof-of-concept trial to evaluate the clinical feasibility and survival signal of the STIMVAX protocol in refractory MSS/pMMR mCRC. The primary efficacy endpoint was overall survival (OS), defined as the time from the first protocol-defined treatment administration to death from any cause.

The sample size of (N = 29) patients was determined based on a precision-oriented, exploratory framework rather than a formal hypothesis-driven power calculation, consistent with the proof-of-concept objectives of validating this translational cellular model. In this highly refractory 3 L population, historical registration data for approved agents (e.g., regorafenib, TAS-102, fruquintinib) established a benchmark median OS of approximately 5.0 to 7.0 months, with a 12-month survival rate of <30%. Enrolling 29 patients provided sufficient descriptive granularity to detect a clinically meaningful shift in median survival while providing an acceptable safety profile across multiple outpatient oncology settings.

Survival and efficacy analysis

Survival parameters were analyzed on an intent-to-treat (ITT) basis encompassing all 29 allocated patients. Time-to-event outcomes were calculated using the non-parametric Kaplan-Meier product-limit method, and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for median survival times were calculated using the linear Greenwood method.

To supplement the standard median survival calculations and rigorously account for the non-proportional hazards frequently encountered in cellular immunotherapy trials (e.g., due to delayed immune activation, immune-mediated tumor equilibrium, or pseudoprogression), Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST) was evaluated. RMST values were calculated as the area under the Kaplan-Meier survival curve up to a pre-specified temporal horizon (τ) which was set to the maximum observed patient follow-up time of 989 days (approximately 32 months). RMST directly quantifies the average life expectancy of the cohort within this tracking window. Missing data or patients alive at the time of the data cutoff were administratively censored at their last verified date of follow-up. All statistical analyses were executed using NCSS 2020 statistical software.

Overall survival outcomes

Efficacy analyses were performed on the complete intent-to-treat (ITT) cohort (N = 29). At the time of the designated data lock, 18 disease-related deaths (62.1%) had occurred, and 11 patients (37.9%) were administratively censored. The primary efficacy endpoint was met, demonstrating a median overall survival (OS) of 16.4 months (492 days/ 95% CI: 258–625 days/8.6–20.8 months). The cohort exhibited a 12-month survival rate of 60.0%, an 18-month survival rate of 39.0% and 24-month survival rate of 19% (see Fig. 3). This clinical signal represents a notable absolute increase when evaluated against historical registrational benchmarks for approved 3 L therapies in refractory MSS/pMMR mCRC, which traditionally exhibit a median OS of approximately 5.0 to 7.0 months and a 12-month survival rate below 30% [6].

To account for non-proportional hazards commonly associated with late-acting, cell-mediated immunotherapies, a non-parametric Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST) analysis was executed at a temporal horizon (τ) of 989 days (∼32 months). The RMST analysis demonstrated an average cohort life expectancy of 16.1 months (482.1 days/16.1 months; 95% CI: 369.9–594.2 days/12.3–19.8 months). This robust metric confirms that the survival advantage was consistently sustained across the majority of the study population throughout the tracking window, rather than being driven by a small subset of extreme outliers.

Radiological response and survival-progression discordance

Of the 29 patients in the intent-to-treat (ITT) population, 19 evaluable individuals possessed both baseline and scheduled Day 119 (±3 days) follow-up contrast-enhanced CT scans. The remaining 10 patients were non-evaluable for Day 119 radiological assessment due to early clinical dropouts (n = 9) or missing interval scans (n = 1), as tracked in the patient disposition framework. Among the 19 radiologically evaluable patients, 17 individuals (89.5%) were categorized as having progressive disease (PD) and 2 individuals (10.5%) exhibited stable disease (SD) in accordance with conventional RECIST 1.1 criteria.

Retrospective translational analysis revealed a profound, reproducible discordance between conventional RECIST 1.1 metrics and actual patient survival. Many cases scored as PD demonstrated significant increases in target lesion diameters, alongside the appearance of new punctate lesions and localized lymphadenopathy. Crucially, these structural alterations occurred in the absence of constitutional symptoms or functional decline, with patients maintaining stable ECOG performance statuses (0 or 1) and clinical baseline metrics. This clinical preservation is consistent with the hypothesis that the apparent radiological progression reflects a localized immune cell influx and intratumoral inflammatory swelling (clinically reminiscent of pseudoprogression) rather than true malignant cellular proliferation. However, in the absence of direct histopathological verification or localized cellular monitoring, true early progression followed by delayed treatment effect cannot be definitively ruled out.

To characterize the distinct kinetics of this survival-radiological paradox, longitudinal imaging tracking revealed three clear clinical phenotypes within the cohort. The first phenotype is defined by transient structural worsening followed by clinical survival prolongation, as exemplified by two representative patients presenting with lung metastases (Fig. 4). At Day 119, both individuals met formal RECIST 1.1 criteria for progressive disease (PD) due to a notable volumetric expansion of pulmonary lesions. However, these patients remained completely asymptomatic with a preserved performance status, ultimately achieving a prolonged survival of 313 days (Fig. 4) and 288 days (Fig. 5).

Safety evaluations were monitored continuously across the complete intent-to-treat population (N = 29). A total of 509 individual treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were documented during active therapy and long-term protocol monitoring. The overall safety profile was favorable, characterized primarily by mild-to-moderate, transient physiological shifts associated with localized immune activation (see Table 2).

Clinical investigators noted that distinguishing background disease progression from targeted, localized inflammatory flares was a central challenge during protocol management. Successful conversion of "cold" tumors to "hot" lesions triggers regional cell swelling, transient low-grade fevers, and secondary musculoskeletal discomfort. These phenomena inherently overlap with standard metastatic oncology symptoms, accounting for the elevated event density across the primary MedDRA SOC columns.

Discussion

The primary endpoint of this exploratory, Phase 2B open-label trial in refractory, 3 L metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) presenting with the microsatellite stable/proficient mismatch repair (MSS/pMMR) genotype was overall survival (OS). The AlloStim® intent-to-treat (ITT) cohort demonstrated an encouraging median OS of 16.4 months (492 days; 95% CI: 8.6–20.8 months). This outcome compares favorably to historical data from large, randomized controlled registration trials in this salvage setting. For example, the benchmark fruquintinib randomized, controlled registration study reported a median OS of 7.4 months (95% CI: 6.7–8.2) versus 4.8 months (95% CI: 4.0–5.8) in the placebo group [39].

However, a direct comparison is fundamentally limited by the non-randomized, single-arm design of this study. Variations in baseline patient characteristics, subsequent lines of therapy, and evolving standards of care over the chronological gap separating these trials can introduce confounding historical biases. Nevertheless, the 121.6% higher median OS for AlloStim® compared to fruquintinib suggests that AlloStim® immunotherapy may have activity in this immunotherapy-refractory indication that, if confirmed, would be clinically relevant.

The 95% CI for the median OS in the current single-arm study (8.6–20.8 months) displays no statistical overlap with the 95% CI for the active arm of the fruquintinib randomized, controlled study. While this non-overlapping observation is descriptive and cannot substitute for a formal, prospective comparative superiority test, it remains an important clinical indicator. The small sample size (N = 29) inherently widened the confidence limits, yet the lower boundary (8.6 months) exceeds the absolute median survival values of approved monotherapies in this setting, which is notable and suggests a potential substantial difference in survival outcomes and anti-tumor activity, especially when considering these results are for an immunotherapy in an immunotherapy-refractory indication.

One patient remained alive at the final data cutoff of January 16, 2026, contributing a long-term censored survival milestone at 990 days (∼33 months). The remaining 10 censored observations occurred early in the tracking timeline (between Days 60 and 150) and were driven by early withdrawals following off-protocol imaging, rather than late-stage dropouts. The fact that a patient achieved sustained clinical survival out to nearly three years provides translational evidence of a possible durable, immune-mediated anti-tumor effect rather than a temporary stabilization of disease.

When contextualizing these outcomes against other historical third-line benchmarks, certain multi-agent combinations have demonstrated extended survival metrics, predominantly within Asian patient populations. For example, in a real-life retrospective study of Chinese third-line metastatic CRC patients treated with regorafenib combined with PD-1 inhibitors was 19.2 months, TAS-102 combined with bevacizumab was 14.0 months, and fruquintinib combined with PD-1 inhibitors was 16.2 months, with no statistically significant differences between the groups [40].

As another example, the median OS benefit in the CORRECT registration trial for regorafenib was 6.4 months, which enrolled only 15% Asian patients, mostly from Japan [41]. Whereas, the CONCUR trial was a phase 3 clinical trial that investigated the efficacy and safety of regorafenib in Asian patients at 25 centers in mainland China, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Republic of Korea, and Vietnam. The median OS was 8.8 months in the regorafenib group in the CONCUR trial compared to 6.4 months in the CORRECT trial [42]. The apparent survival benefit for third-line agents in Asian patients may be due to genetics [43]. As the present study accrued 82.8% Caucasian patients and did not accrue any Asian patients, the OS comparisons to the CORRECT study were considered more relevant for historical comparison.

AlloStim® had an overall response rate (ORR) rate of 0%. The approved 3 L metastatic CRC agents also provide a minimal ORR ranging from 1% to 4% [41,42,44,45]. In a single-arm Phase II trial, the combination of regorafenib with the ICI, avelumab, did not report any objective responses, with stable disease as the best response in 53.5% of the 48 patients enrolled and a median OS of 10.8 months [46].

In this study, radiological progression led some investigators and patients to discontinue AlloStim® immunotherapy or to withdraw consent early in the study. However, as the trial progressed and patients with radiological progression remained clinically stable, investigators became more inclined to keep patients on the study and to treat past radiological progression. These early withdrawals were handled as censored observations in the ITT analysis, which contributed to the wide 95% CI. The disconnect between OS and radiological evaluation suggests that the inflammatory mechanism of the study drug may cause tumors to swell and appear larger than the actual tumor burden.

RECIST 1.1 response in cancer generally correlates with survival; however, ICI immunotherapy treatments can result in atypical response patterns, such as pseudoprogression and hyperprogression [47,48]. ICI treatment beyond RECIST 1.1-defined progression can subsequently result in a reduction of tumor size compared to baseline [49], suggesting that RECIST 1.1 criteria may underestimate the benefit of immunotherapy [50].

Pseudoprogression can be associated with inflammation of tumor lesions [51]. Generally, the prognosis for patients with pseudoprogression is better than those with true progression, but whether pseudoprogression is better than those with an objective response is not known. Often patients with pseudoprogression have increased OS compared to patients with progressive disease by RECIST criteria [50,52,53]. However, pseudoprogression after ICI immunotherapy is relatively rare; a pooled estimate of true pseudoprogression in 18 ICI immunotherapy clinical trials was 15% (95% CI: 8%–26%) [54].

Since MSS/pMMR CRC tumors do not accumulate mutations, TMB is low and the TME is not pro-inflammatory, defining these as “cold” tumors. Therefore, it is hypothesized that a potential conversion of these “cold” tumors to an inflamed phenotype might manifest as an increase in relative size by RECIST after immunotherapy. The baseline non-inflamed status of MSS/pMMR tumors presents a potential clinical rationale for why the apparent pseudoprogression incidence appears notably higher than previously reported for ICI immunotherapy. The baseline RECIST measurements would be expected to be smaller than the baseline of an inflamed “hot” tumor. As the baseline size of “cold” non-inflamed tumors is lower, the conversion of these “cold” tumors to “hot” tumors in this study would be expected to demonstrate an increase in relative size by RECIST after immunotherapy. Whereas, an inflamed “hot” tumor treated with ICI immunotherapy is already increased in size at baseline and would not be expected to swell additionally after immunotherapy. Therefore, the cold tumor baseline status of MSS/pMMR tumors in this study may explain why the pseudoprogression incidence may be so much higher than previously reported for ICI immunotherapy. Nevertheless, this phenotypic shift remains speculative as no serial tissue biopsies or correlative immune biomarkers were available to confirm direct microenvironment modification.

The high number of patients with radiological progression who experienced a survival benefit in this study is highly descriptive and points to the potential occurrence of pseudoprogression. In further alignment with this hypothesis, one of the subjects in this study that presented with radiological progression after AlloStim® immunotherapy, suspected to be due to pseudoprogression, was treated with ICI immunotherapy based on the clinical assumption that the increased tumor size reflected a transition of non-ICI responsive tumors to an ICI-responsive phenotype, subsequently reaching an objective response [55]. While encouraging, these anecdotal and indirect clinical observations require formal mechanistic validation.

The first CT scan in the study was scheduled for day 119, one week after the completion of the 3 treatment cycles. The timing of the CT scan procedure was longer than the 2–3 months standard of care practiced by most of the clinical sites. Therefore, many patients received off-protocol CT scans prior to day 119. When these off-protocol scans were interpreted as progressive disease (PD), this contributed to the initial early withdrawal from the study prior to the completion of the 3 treatment cycles.

There is a high need for timely diagnosis of pseudoprogression, but there currently is not a uniform standard for differentiating true progression from pseudoprogression. iRECIST criteria were established as a possible way to better identify pseudoprogression after ICI immunotherapy, which requires treating past an initial progression until progression is confirmed in a follow scan, which can result in a significant increase in time-to-progression (TTP) [56]. The shortening of the CT scan interval would enable iRECIST evaluations in future studies.

The withdrawal rate of 27% was an abnormally high rate. In an analysis of the rates of patient withdrawal of consent within 2 years of enrollment in cancer clinical trials, 1060 of 11,993 (9%) patients withdrew consent within 2 yr of enrollment (range, 5.7%–9.8%), with most during the early follow-up period [57]. Withdrawal rates in a separate study were 11.9% (IQR 0–55.1%) across 100 cancer clinical trials, with reasons for study withdrawal mainly disease progression (70.6%), followed by adverse events (10.4%) [58].

Although RECIST remains the main predictor of treatment benefit in most cancer settings, being closely associated with survival, this method may not be applicable to an immunotherapy that converts cold tumors to hot tumors. A comprehensive evaluation of other radiological characteristics could enrich the interpretation of treatment response in patients treated with immunotherapy agents that cause intratumoral inflammation. To date, little is known about the specific pattern of the response to inflammation-inducing immunotherapy, but some of the radiological characteristics noted in this study, such as heterogeneous enhancement, distributed necrosis, hemorrhage, edema, and peritumoral inflammation characteristics, deserve future consideration.

There are not any clear predictors of efficacy in 3 L metastatic CRC. Subgroup analyses in the REGONIVO and REGOTORI studies revealed varied ORRs. In the REGONIVO study, patients with lung metastases had a higher ORR (50%) than those with liver metastases (15.4%) [59]. In the REGOTORI study, patients with liver metastases had a lower ORR (8.7%) than those without (30.0%). Here, 24/29 (82.8%) presented with liver metastases, suggesting that the OS data was not biased by the location of the tumor lesions.

While adverse events (AE) are common in this indication due to the late stage of disease, the approved third-line therapies have a high incidence of ≥ Grade 3 (severe) events. Regorafenib presents with high rates of non-hematological toxicities, particularly palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia (hand and foot syndrome) and hepatotoxicity, while TAS-102 is primarily associated with hematologic toxicities, especially anemia, leukopenia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia. The fruquintinib safety profile is similar to regorafenib and is associated with hypertension, palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia, and hypothyroidism.

The AlloStim® AE profile was rather benign in comparison, with a predominance of the AEs scored as Grade 1 (mild) events. Only 4.1% of AEs were severe ≥Grade 3. A high frequency of AlloStim® AEs (31.8%) were scored as ‘possibly’ related to the study drug. This was mainly due to an inability to distinguish events that may have been due to progressive disease from those due to inflammation.

A primary limitation of the present study is the lack of direct mechanistic confirmation, such as sequential tumor biopsies, peripheral blood immune monitoring, or correlative biomarker evaluations. Consequently, our characterization of tumor phenotypic conversion from 'cold' to 'hot' and the high incidence of pseudoprogression cannot be definitively demonstrated. These interpretations must be viewed strictly as hypotheses supported by indirect clinical and radiological observations, intended to provide a basis for target validation in future prospective trials.

Conclusion

AlloStim® immunotherapy demonstrates encouraging potential survival benefits in immunotherapy-refractory, 3 L MSS/pMMR metastatic colorectal cancer. The findings from this intent-to-treat analysis provide clinical proof-of-concept for the immunomodulatory design and successful clinical translation of this sequential cell delivery schedule. While these exploratory phase 2B outcomes are encouraging, a prospective, randomized controlled clinical trial powered sufficiently to validate these findings against standard-of-care benchmarks remains a necessity.

If confirmed in a randomized setting, this allogeneic cell translational framework—either administered alone or deployed sequentially in combination with multikinase inhibitors or immune checkpoint inhibitors—could offer a meaningful new line of treatment for this refractory CRC population, while providing a possible therapeutic blueprint for managing other immunologically "cold" solid tumor indications.
