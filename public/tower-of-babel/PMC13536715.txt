Cross-system collaboration between the child welfare system and substance use treatment providers: a qualitative analysis of strategies, facilitators, and barriers
Authors: Allyson L. Dir, Brielle L. Batch, Matthew C. Aalsma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParental substance use in the child welfare system (CWS) is common, and collaboration between CWS and substance use treatment agencies is critical for parents’ success. Research on the challenges to cross-system collaboration exists, but much of the published literature is dated despite recent efforts to improve cross-system collaboration. The current manuscript seeks to understand collaborative strategies used between child welfare and substance use treatment providers and their perceived effectiveness, identify facilitators and barriers to collaboration, and understand the impact of collaboration on child welfare system case and parent outcomes.MethodsQualitative data analysis was conducted on semi-structured interviews, completed September 2023 – June 2024, with n = 31 CWS personnel (90.3% female; 96.8% White, Non-Hispanic) and n = 28 substance use service providers (96.4% female; 76% White) across Indiana.ResultsCollaborative strategies targeted facilitation of service referral and initial service connection between systems and ongoing care coordination. Beyond formal strategies, frontline personnel described informal efforts to improve collaboration. Facilitators to collaboration included positive attitudes towards collaboration and teamwork, such as understanding the importance of teamwork and respect for others’ roles and responsibilities. Barriers to collaboration included workforce turnover and large caseloads, competing perspectives on how to address substance use (SU), and competing expectations (i.e., CWS focus on child safety versus SU provider focus on parent).ConclusionFindings highlight the need for continued efforts to support collaboration between CWS and SU service agencies through organizational and broader policy and reiterate the benefits of collaboration on effectively supporting parents and families.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14973-6.

Background

Parental substance use is prevalent in the child welfare system (CWS), with estimates that up to two-thirds of parents involved in CWS have problems with substance use (SU) or substance use disorder (SUD; [1, 2]). CWS cases involving parental SU are more likely to result in out-of-home placements (representing 39.1% of out-of-home cases nationally and 67.1% in Indiana), foster care (e.g., non-kinship) placements, longer foster care placements, lower rates of reunification, and have a higher likelihood of repeat child maltreatment [3–10]. It is crucial that parents with SU receive SU treatment if needed (and is often a requirement by CWS), as receipt of SU treatment has been associated with better CWS outcomes, such as greater likelihood for family reunification and fewer days in foster care [11, 12]. However, challenges exist at multiple stages of the care process, from initial identification of the need for services, referring parents to services, successfully connecting them to care, and helping parents maintain engagement in services; on average, less than 20% of parents involved in CWS complete SU treatment [9, 13, 14]. The current manuscript focuses on the importance of cross-system collaboration and coordination between CWS and community service providers in supporting parents with SU from initial connection to services and through service engagement.

Collaboration between CWS and community service providers is essential for ensuring that parents are connected to services and supported throughout SU treatment and CWS involvement [15–19]; however, challenges to collaboration have been well documented in CWS literature. Cross-system collaboration can be understood within the theory of organizational social context, which explains how collaboration between agencies is influenced by characteristics at both the organizational and individual level [20–21]. At the organizational level, CWS and SU service providers have unique organizational policies and procedures, practice expectations, and regulations which influence collaboration. Moreover, such policies and procedures may or may not align; for example, with respect to SU, policies regarding confidentiality and data sharing related to SU are known to hinder collaboration [18, 22–24]. At the individual personnel level, one’s role in the organization, job stress, and attitudes towards collaboration (and SU) can also influence collaboration. With respect to SU, differences in goals and focus (i.e., provider focus on parent and CWS focus on child safety), attitudes regarding collaboration, and differing perspectives on SU and treating SU can hinder collaboration [17, 22–24].

Recognizing the challenges of and need for collaboration, the past two decades have seen an increase in the focus on improving coordination and collaboration between CWS and community service providers across the United States, and there have been significant efforts in implementation and quality improvement to improve collaboration between organizations [15, 17, 25]. Common strategies utilized include administrative strategies, such as memorandums of understanding (MOUs) for services, co-location of services, joint trainings, and data sharing procedures [26–28], as well as frontline strategies that target aligning case plans [15, 17, 25]. More targeted interventions and programs, such as Ohio’s Sobriety Treatment and Recovery Teams (START), have also been developed which entail fuller integration of CWS and SU services [29–31]. Despite these efforts, more recent research on the current state of collaboration between CWS and SU treatment providers is lacking, and there is a need for updated research to understand whether challenges persist. Moreover, as treatment and policy evolve and advance, there is a need for effective cross-system collaboration for successful implementation of novel policies and evidence-based practices targeting parental substance use [32].

The current manuscript builds on previous literature on cross-system collaboration and parental SU within the CWS to further understand how frontline personnel from CWS and SU service providers work together to support parents with SU. The focus is on CWS and SU agencies across Indiana, where 67.1% of cases of home removals involve parental SU [10]. Indiana’s CWS has standardized policies for identifying and addressing maltreatment due to parental SU ([33–34]); however, the focus of the manuscript is not on policies but rather on actual collaboration between agencies. The manuscript examines perspectives from CWS and community SU service agencies to: (1) specify use of collaborative strategies among CWS and community SU service providers and describe perspectives on their effectiveness; (2) identify facilitators and barriers to collaboration unique to supporting families with parental SU; and (3) understand the impact of collaboration on case outcomes. This manuscript seeks to update the literature and determine areas for needed improvement.

Methods

Procedure

Participants were CWS (n = 31) and community SU service providers (n = 28) across Indiana recruited through email sent to CWS listservs, SU treatment centers, and community mental health centers providing SU treatment. Purposive sampling was used to ensure a heterogenous sample of participants with respect to demographic variables, geographic region, and employment characteristics. We attempted to recruit participants according to CWS designated regions across the north, central, and southern parts of the state. We also attempted to “match” participants by geographic regions, so that both SU treatment agency and CWS perspectives were represented for a given county or larger region. Participants completed semi-structured interviews via telephone or videoconferencing, with interviews occurring from September 2023 – June 2024. All participants received a copy of the study information sheet and provided verbal consent to participate in a recorded interview. All recruitment and data collection procedures were submitted to and approved by the affiliated institution’s Institutional Review Board as an exempt study (IRB # 16674).

Measures

Demographics

Participants provided their age, sex, ethnicity, race, current job title, and the county in which they worked or served.

Qualitative interviews

Semi-structured interview guides were developed for both CWS personnel and SU service providers (See Supplementary File 1). Interview guide questions sought to understand standard work processes in working with parents with SU, facilitators and barriers to supporting parents, and specifics regarding collaboration and coordination between CWS and SU service providers. Questions specific to cross-system collaboration were based on organizational social context and sought to understand both individual-level and organizational-level factors that may impact collaboration [21]. Questions also asked participants to describe any collaborative strategies used at different points along the service cascade (e.g., initial service connection, ongoing service coordination; see 25) and facilitators and barriers to collaboration.

Sample

CWS personnel

Of the 31 CWS personnel, over three-quarters served as a family case manager (FCM; n = 25, 80.6%) with most reporting involvement in ongoing, or permanency case work (e.g., facilitating child family team meetings, conducting home visits, developing safety plans), and some participants noting a dual function of assessment (e.g., conducting initial safety assessment, recommending if a case should be substantiated) and permanency work (n = 23, 92.0%; n = 11, 44.0% assessment work; see Table 1). The remaining 6 participants noted roles as clinical coordinators or consultants (n = 2, 6.5%) and supervisors or directors (n = 4, 12.9%). Over a quarter of CWS personnel reported working within a Family Recovery Court (FRC; n = 7, 26.9%). Participants were predominantly White (n = 30, 96.8%), not Hispanic or Latino (n = 30, 96.8%), females (n = 28, 90.3%) with an average age of 33 (SD = 8.7, range = 23–65) years old. Most participants worked in a rural/mixed (n = 10, 40.0%) or urban (n = 10, 40.0%) county in the northern region of the state (n = 12, 48.0%). There were 19 unique counties represented amongst CWS personnel.

SU service providers

Of the 28 SU service providers, most served in supervisorial or directorial roles (n = 11, 39.3%; see Table 1). The remaining participants served within case management or skills management (n = 7, 25.0%), as peer recovery coaches (n = 6, 21.4%), or therapists (n = 4, 14.3%). Most participants worked at community mental health centers or SU-specific agencies that provided outpatient services (i.e., group intensive outpatient SU treatment, individual therapy, medication management (n = 13; 46.4%)); n = 7 (25%) worked at residential facilities, n = 4 (16%) worked at peer support service agencies (e.g., support groups, peer recovery coaching), and n = 4 (16%) at other agencies providing supportive services (e.g., housing, assessment). Less than one quarter of participants noted working with a FRC (n = 5, 17.9%). Participants were predominantly White (n = 19, 76.0%), not Hispanic or Latino (n = 25, 100%) females (n = 27, 96.4%) with an average age of 40 (SD = 13.0, 24–62) years old. Most participants worked within an urban (n = 17, 60.7%) county in the central region of the state (n = 14, 50.0%). There were 16 unique counties represented among SU service providers.

Data analysis

Constant comparative methods and iterative sampling were used to determine saturation and final sample size [35]; both code and thematic saturation were reached [36]. Interviews were deidentified and uploaded to NVivo for coding and analysis utilizing a thematic analysis approach. A qualitative codebook was developed with deductive codes corresponding directly to specific interview questions, based on categories of collaborative strategies previously identified, and categorized as facilitating initial service access or managing ongoing service and care coordination [25]. These codes were chosen based on the larger project goal of better understanding points along the SU service cascade for parents involved in CWS [37]; additional codes were identified from an inductive review of interview transcripts [37]. The first two authors completed independent open coding of a subset of interview transcripts to develop a codebook; they then met to finalize the codebook [38]. Following initial coding, focused coding was conducted independently with the finalized codebook and authors met regularly to ensure reliability and discuss discrepancies in coding. Broad study results were summarized and presented to the Indiana CWS birth parent advisory board to assess validity and discuss next steps for disseminating research findings.

Results

There was heterogeneity across both SU service providers and CWS personnel with respect to overall positive and negative experiences and perspectives of collaboration. There were no clear patterns in results across geographic region, rurality, role (i.e., leadership vs. frontlines), agency type, race, or ethnicity.

Collaboration strategies

Participants were not explicitly asked about specific collaborative strategies used, but across interviews, common collaborative strategies were discussed. Participants described formal strategies (i.e., formalized or required by their agency and part of policies and procedures) that were determined to either (1) facilitate initial service access or (2) manage ongoing service and care coordination.

Service access strategies

Participants across counties described (1) formalized contracts and service agreements between SU service agencies and CWS as well as (2) an electronic referral and data sharing system that facilitated service access and assisted individuals on the frontlines in the initial stages of referral, service connection, and treatment engagement. Most participants from SU treatment agencies reported working for agencies that had formalized contracts with CWS to provide services. Contracts provided a foundation for collaboration by establishing formalized procedures for service provision at the administrative level and set expectations for frontline workers in communicating and coordinating with each system. CWS generally described that having contracts with agencies facilitated effective communication with providers. For example, as one CWS assessment case manager described, “If CWS is contracted with [the provider], we don’t have too many other barriers that I’ve noticed. The people that I work with always seem to be very willing to communicate with me.” CWS also described how contracts helped accelerate the initial assessment and engagement process, while working with non-contracted providers often slowed the process. For example, one CWS assessment/permanency case manager explained:[Parents] have the ability to choose on their own who they want to go to. So, if we are not connecting with the service [provider] individually…depending on the parents and their level of communication with us, it can drag on a case.

[Parents] have the ability to choose on their own who they want to go to. So, if we are not connecting with the service [provider] individually…depending on the parents and their level of communication with us, it can drag on a case.

SU providers explained that having contracts helped establish rapport with parents and families; by going to contracted providers, parents understood expectations that SU providers would be in communication with CWS case managers. For example, one case manager at a community mental health center that provided parental SU services explained how contracts made it easy for her to be up front with parents, as she explained: “If you’re involved with [CWS], I’m going to tell you, like, ‘hey, I talked to your caseworker today.’” This helped to build rapport with parents understanding that communication was part of the process.

As part of formalized contracts, CWS and SU providers described using an electronic referral and data sharing system for CWS to submit service referrals and SU providers to document and track services provided. Participants described that the system created a centralized, standardized, and efficient way of information sharing which expedited service access. Most of the CWS personnel described the referral process as “pretty easy”. SU providers described how the system allowed for ease of receiving and identifying referrals specifically from CWS. One director of a small, community mental health center described that the referral system allowed their agency to easily organize CWS and other referrals: “If they’re coming through [CWS] that comes electronically…we get notified that there’s a new referral.” Providers from uncontracted agencies and without this system described how lack of a formalized referral system can delay communication with CWS. One family case manager at an uncontracted agency described: “If a client doesn’t say ‘hey, I’m an active [CWS] referral’, we don’t always know like to call and get the collateral.”

Care coordination strategies

Two formal strategies were discussed that facilitated ongoing care coordination: CWS liaisons and joint team meetings.

Some larger treatment agencies reported having a liaison designated to communicate with CWS regarding treatment status on behalf of the providers. The liaison served as a consistent, dependable representative that helped foster relationships with CWS front-line case managers. On the CWS side, most reported the convenience of having a specific individual that they could reach out to without having to navigate agencies with multiple providers to determine a parent’s provider. One CWS case manager from a large urban county explained: “We have a designated liaison that we go to to ask questions or if we have like an immediate concern, we can operate that way.” SU providers also found liaisons helpful; for providers with large caseloads and busy schedules, having someone with a flexible schedule and more readily available to be contacted and communicate with CWS in a timely fashion was convenient. The only limitation noted by CWS was related to the lack of more intimate understanding of parent status in treatment, as one CWS permanency case manager noted: “I don’t have like that firsthand feedback from the actual therapist or visit supervisor that’s meeting with the family.”

CWS personnel and SU providers also described child and family team meetings (CFTMs) which were mandatory meetings held every 90 days with the family, CWS case manager, and providers to discuss CWS treatment plans and progress. Both CWS and SU providers described these meetings as aligning CWS case managers and SU providers and ensuring parents have clear expectations from both parties. However, there were a few challenges. Some CWS explained hesitancy from parents to involve SU providers out of concern of what SU providers would disclose to CWS. One assessment/permanency case manager explained:A lot of clients don’t like to invite their substance use treatment providers…[treatment] is where they become the most vulnerable, and they open up about the most information, and so they don’t necessarily want those providers coming into the meetings and maybe divulging something they don’t want to talk about.

A lot of clients don’t like to invite their substance use treatment providers…[treatment] is where they become the most vulnerable, and they open up about the most information, and so they don’t necessarily want those providers coming into the meetings and maybe divulging something they don’t want to talk about.

Some SU providers described the challenges in coordinating and scheduling those meetings. One clinical manager at a large residential program noted: “We would run into a lot of cancellations of CFTMs or rescheduling things along those lines. I know that would be frustrating.”

Facilitators to collaboration

While these formalized, organization-level strategies provided a foundation for collaboration, they were not sufficient for building partnerships needed for effective collaboration and care coordination. Participants from both sides described the additional effort and time it takes to gain trust, establish rapport, and build partnerships for effective collaboration. Those who experienced positive, successful collaboration reported efforts they put in to foster relationships and encourage flexible, open communication. For example, one CWS liaison at a large community mental health center described the importance of making meaningful, personal connections to build a comfortable rapport:We’ve went into [CWS] staff meetings to say like, ‘hey, look at my face this is my phone number. Call me for everything, e-mail me for everything.’ And I would get a lot of, ‘I’m sorry to bother you’ and I was like, ‘this is my job. Like this is what I do. Like, please bother me.’

We’ve went into [CWS] staff meetings to say like, ‘hey, look at my face this is my phone number. Call me for everything, e-mail me for everything.’ And I would get a lot of, ‘I’m sorry to bother you’ and I was like, ‘this is my job. Like this is what I do. Like, please bother me.’

CWS personnel described similar efforts towards establishing effective working relationships as part of successful collaboration and acknowledged the time it takes to build rapport and the importance of a “consistent face they work with.”

Those reporting effective collaboration also described having a shared value of teamwork and mutual respect for one another’s roles. As one CWS assessment/permanency case manager in a rural county described:I’ve been very intentional about when I get a case and I make referrals that I’m following up, I’m inviting providers to team meetings, I’m including them in discussions about treatment goals and that sort of thing and just being respectful of their role in the process and acknowledging that while I’m the case manager and I’m coordinating things, I’m not a [therapist], I’m not a drug counselor. And we need to work as a team instead of being siloed.

I’ve been very intentional about when I get a case and I make referrals that I’m following up, I’m inviting providers to team meetings, I’m including them in discussions about treatment goals and that sort of thing and just being respectful of their role in the process and acknowledging that while I’m the case manager and I’m coordinating things, I’m not a [therapist], I’m not a drug counselor. And we need to work as a team instead of being siloed.

Some SU providers similarly shared the value of working as a team and described a “tag teaming” in which CWS and providers support clients with their respective roles.

Barriers to collaboration

Participants also noted barriers to collaboration despite facilitators and formal collaborative strategies.

Organizational barriers: workforce issues

At the organizational level, both SU providers and CWS shared how heavy caseloads and workforce turnover hindered collaboration. Both CWS and SU providers experienced large caseloads and recognized each other’s large caseloads and how this hindered timely communication regarding clients. One CWS permanency/assessment case manager in a rural county described how SU providers’ large caseloads delay effective communication:If I’m having like an issue with something that needs to be worked out, sometimes it’s not always like right away because there’s only two of them and they have like they said the update was like 99 people on their caseload.

If I’m having like an issue with something that needs to be worked out, sometimes it’s not always like right away because there’s only two of them and they have like they said the update was like 99 people on their caseload.

CWS also noted that SU providers’ heavy caseloads made it difficult to build rapport and establish personal working relationships. As one CWS regional provider described of a large treatment center they partner with: “I don’t feel like that connection, that personal or professional connection is there because they’re just, you know, they’re spinning their wheels of next client, next client, next client.” SU providers also acknowledged how their workload was a limitation to collaboration. One family case manager at a community mental health center noted:It was harder for me to be fully engaged and fully like updating them on everything because some things I just I didn’t know because I had it’s like 120 people on my caseload. So, it was a lot to keep track of…that kind of mess[es] with the relationship with [CWS] because we just didn’t have the time to even check our emails.

It was harder for me to be fully engaged and fully like updating them on everything because some things I just I didn’t know because I had it’s like 120 people on my caseload. So, it was a lot to keep track of…that kind of mess[es] with the relationship with [CWS] because we just didn’t have the time to even check our emails.

SU providers also similarly described challenges to collaborating due to CWS case managers’ workloads. One clinical manager at an outpatient SU service center acknowledged the limits that both systems face:I think it’s by and large that [CWS is] dealing with the same stuff. You know they’re understaffed and overworked and have way too big of caseloads to do their job well on each of them… it’s hard work and it’s not high paying work…and we get that at [SU service center] too, because it’s the same.

I think it’s by and large that [CWS is] dealing with the same stuff. You know they’re understaffed and overworked and have way too big of caseloads to do their job well on each of them… it’s hard work and it’s not high paying work…and we get that at [SU service center] too, because it’s the same.

Both SU service agencies and CWS were impacted by workforce turnover. SU providers described often working with multiple case managers on a single case due to turnover, which made it difficult to establish relationships. One peer recovery coach explained that building rapport on cases takes time, and when case managers change in the middle of a case, this can disrupt the relationship with both parents and the provider: “It’s almost like we’re starting all the way back over again.” CWS also shared that turnover is challenging; many CWS personnel described often trying to work with providers they’ve had a history with since it takes time to establish rapport. For example, one CWS permanency/assessment case manager said: “If there is a new provider…that communication lacks at the beginning just because we’re like kind of getting to know each other.”

Individual-level personnel barriers to collaboration

Addressing substance use

Providers and CWS personnel described how there were often differences and disagreements regarding how to address parental SU. Disagreements were described at the point of initial assessment when decisions were made regarding appropriate treatment needed as well as throughout treatment, such as disagreements regarding treatment expectations and addressing relapse or continued substance use.

CWS personnel and SU providers both described potential for disagreements in the initial assessment period. While contracts – as described above – helped to facilitate information sharing regarding cases, CWS personnel and SU providers described taking different approaches to determining service needs. Many SU providers emphasized relying on parent self-report for diagnosis and recommendations, while CWS described frustration when SU providers didn’t consider preliminary information collected by CWS to help providers make informed SU treatment decisions during the assessment phase. As one CWS assessment case manager described:Some providers don’t even take into account the history or the up-to-date information that we give them and strictly go off of what the parent is reporting and then say no recommendations needed. Well, they just tested positive for methamphetamine last week…So that’s a barrier and a frustration at the same time.

Some providers don’t even take into account the history or the up-to-date information that we give them and strictly go off of what the parent is reporting and then say no recommendations needed. Well, they just tested positive for methamphetamine last week…So that’s a barrier and a frustration at the same time.

Providers acknowledged this challenge, as one therapist at a community mental health center said: “It does get tricky because clients coming in for treatment, you know, their [CWS case manager] may know their history…we have to go off what they’re [the parents] are telling us as far as diagnosing and recommending that service.”

Both parties also described disagreements regarding treatment decisions and differences in CWS and SU treatment plans. While SU providers are primarily responsible for clinical decision-making, CWS personnel often have expectations regarding treatment that may not align with SU providers’ clinical recommendations. For example, some providers described how CWS may request a specific level of treatment with the referral before providers have completed an assessment. One clinical compliance manager at a large SU service agency with multiple levels of care explained: “Occasionally [CWS] will try to direct treatment, and you know, they’ll say X, Y, Z. Well and occasionally we kind of have to push back and say, well, we’re the provider.” SU providers also described how CWS treatment plans do not always align with SU treatment recommendations and treatment plans. For example, one peer recovery coach at a residential facility described misalignment between their standard programming and CWS’ treatment expectations:[The program] is 84 days… Some of these small counties, the [CWS] will be like, no they only need to do 45 days. So…did they complete it, or did they not complete it? Because [CWS] is only saying that they have to do 45 days, yet we require them to do 84 to complete it…I wish [we were] all on the same page.

[The program] is 84 days… Some of these small counties, the [CWS] will be like, no they only need to do 45 days. So…did they complete it, or did they not complete it? Because [CWS] is only saying that they have to do 45 days, yet we require them to do 84 to complete it…I wish [we were] all on the same page.

These differences in opinions and recommendations had the potential to delay treatment and the case process, create tension in the relationship, and cause lack of clarity for parents in treatment and case expectations.

CWS and SU providers also had different perspectives on how to respond to parents’ continued SU, which impacted collaboration. SU providers saw relapse or slip-ups as a common part of the treatment process, while CWS often had different expectations for addressing relapse. These disagreements and differences in expectations have the potential to be “very damaging” to the relationship, as one CWS case manager described. One FRC coordinator alluded to how CWS expects consequences for relapse, but this does not align with SU providers’ approach to treatment: “We’re here to help them [parents] and so [CWS] get[s] upset because they don’t feel like they’re, quote, unquote, punished enough.” Another clinical leader at a residential SU treatment facility in an urban county described differences in expectations for clients:Our team might feel that a little more progress is happening than maybe [CWS] does. I have seen [and] heard of some of those discrepancies that we think this person is making progress in X, Y, and Z. And [CWS] may have a different perspective.

Our team might feel that a little more progress is happening than maybe [CWS] does. I have seen [and] heard of some of those discrepancies that we think this person is making progress in X, Y, and Z. And [CWS] may have a different perspective.

This SU provider goes on to describe what happens if parents have a positive drug screen: “We just have a little bit of, I think, a different perspective, different understanding and belief that this is more likely to happen.”

Both CWS and SU providers were aware of their differences in perspective and even acknowledged that these differences had the potential to create collaboration barriers. CWS was less focused on overall treatment progress and more concerned about continued use due to their focus on child safety. As such, many CWS personnel had the perception that SU providers were often too lenient on parents. CWS described how these disagreements could often be “counterproductive.” As one CWS assessment/permanency case manager shared:I have run into that numerous times where the provider was like ‘ohh yeah they’re doing great’ and then I can sit here and be like ‘well no they they’ve done this. They’ve done that. You’re still gonna sit there and tell me that they’re doing great…’ It can be a barrier.

I have run into that numerous times where the provider was like ‘ohh yeah they’re doing great’ and then I can sit here and be like ‘well no they they’ve done this. They’ve done that. You’re still gonna sit there and tell me that they’re doing great…’ It can be a barrier.

Differential roles and clients

Even beyond situations specific to SU, both CWS and SU providers acknowledged that their different goals and expectations for clients and who was considered their client hindered relationships. While CWS’ focus is on child safety, treatment providers’ primary client is the parent. Impact of collaboration on outcomes

Both CWS and SU treatment providers agreed on the impact of effective collaboration on CWS case outcomes and treatment outcomes. CWS and SU providers described the importance of collaboration – highlighting both the negative impacts of poor collaboration as well as the benefits of effective collaboration.

Negative impacts of poor collaboration

CWS described how poor collaboration and breakdown in communication can impact parents’ motivation, progress, and case progression, as one CWS case manager explained: “If there’s not frequent communication, it’s harder to find like a potential issue earlier. And that can definitely like delay addressing the issue or prevent it from being adequately addressed, especially for working through like pretty strict timelines.” CWS also described the importance of working together as a team and presenting as having shared goals and expectations. One CWS assessment case manager discussed:If there’s not good communication between the FCM and the providers and they’re not on the same page, you know, for instance, if they’re telling families, this is the next milestone, you need to meet and then you know, [CWS] says, no, this is the next milestone, that obviously affects the overall progress of the family and their case but then can also be discouraging for the families.

SU providers similarly described the potential negative impact on collaboration and how this can impact parents and their understanding of their expectations and potentially their engagement in services. One prescriber in a large outpatient SU treatment program illustrated:When that communication is lacking, it’s kind of like having parents that are divorced and won’t speak to each other, and it’s like, ‘well, Mom’s saying one thing and Dad’s saying the opposite. Benefits of effective collaboration

Both CWS and SU providers described the benefits of collaboration as creating a safety net to ensure that parents stay engaged in care as well as ensuring that parents have clear expectations which helps them stay more motivated. CWS and SU providers described that working as a team makes it easier to identify if parents are struggling and provide resources or intervention as needed. SU providers and CWS have unique roles and resources that when used together can help parents. For example, one SU provider described:We have relationships with a lot of FCMs that really improve the parents’ opportunity to have more positive outcomes in their case… I think a lot of local FCMs really trust our ability to intervene. And really trust. Trust that when we are communicating concerns, it is at a level which is beyond our ability to intervene.

One supervisor at a program for mothers involved in CWS described of the importance of the relationship for her clients: “And I always remind my staff that, remember her best chance is that [CWS] and us are buddies. Discussion

Parental SU among families involved in CWS is common and requires coordination between SU service agencies and CWS to ensure that parents receive and stay engaged in SU services as part of their CWS treatment plans. The current study sought to update previous literature on cross-system collaboration between CWS and SU service providers and examine collaborative strategies, facilitators and barriers to effective collaboration, and perspectives on the impact of collaboration on parents’ participation in services. Findings replicate and expand on previous research.

Participants identified formalized collaborative strategies that functioned to facilitate service access as well as improve care coordination and service engagement, consistent with previous literature [17, 22, 25, 28, 39, 40]. Strategies that facilitated more efficient service connection and service referrals included formalized contracts between SU service agencies and CWS as well as an electronic referral and data sharing system between CWS and SU service agencies. Building on previous literature, findings point to the utility of these strategies in expediting the process of initial service referral, which is critical for parents involved in CWS given CWS case timelines. These strategies that bridge systems at the point of referral and initial service connection are also critical for treating SU, as this is often where individuals fall through the cracks due to challenges navigating services or ambivalence regarding treatment [41]. Strategies that helped facilitate ongoing care coordination included joint meetings with CWS, providers, and parents, as well as SU provider CWS liaisons. These strategies helped ensure that CWS and SU providers were aligned in CWS case plans and enabled communication to help support parents in treatment and through CWS processes. Such strategies have been recommended as best practice and are well-documented in the literature [25].

Despite the perceived benefits of these formal strategies, barriers to collaboration existed, and many barriers were consistent with what has previously been documented in the literature [22–24]. Findings are consistent with the theory of organizational social context, in that both organization-level and individual-level factors impact collaboration [20]. At the organizational level, workforce shortages, and, relatedly, large caseloads among both CWS case managers and SU providers hindered communication necessary for care coordination, and frequent workforce turnover made it difficult for individuals to establish working relationships. One of the purposes of cross-system collaboration is to help address insufficient organizational resources and reduce staff burden through resource sharing and increasing each organization’s capacity [17, 42, 43]; in fact, previous research found that CWS and SU treatment agencies with large caseloads used more intensive collaborative strategies as a way to compensate for stretched resources [17]. However, current findings are not consistent with this, as workforce shortage was a barrier to effective collaboration and hindered use of more intensive strategies. This could be due to the type of collaborative strategies used, or it could be due to organizational culture and attitudes regarding collaboration that impact frontline workers’ actions [44]. Nonetheless, findings reinforce well-documented issues in workforce shortage, burden, and turnover in CWS [45–47] and SU treatment fields [48–50] and the need for more attention towards policy and funding in order to help increase and sustain this crucial workforce.

At the individual level, differing perspectives on SU and how to address SU also emerged as a barrier to collaboration. CWS personnel and SU providers explained occasional disagreements regarding the level of treatment needed, consequences for parents’ continued SU and positive drug screens, as well as perspectives on parents’ progress in treatment. These differing perspectives can be attributed to differences in each system’s respective clients and expectations for clients (i.e., SU provider client is the parent vs. CWS client is the child), as well as individual attitudes towards SU and individuals’ perceptions of how the other system views addiction [22]. For example, while continued use or relapse is viewed as a normal part of SU treamtent, relapse can have significant consequences for CWS case outcomes given CWS policies regarding timelines for permanency plans and child safety (e.g., relapse can delay reunification or hinder parent-child visitation).

With respect to SU, collaborative strategies also differentially impact systems’ policies, procedures, and roles. For example, information sharing is an essential collaboration tool; however, information sharing may contradict SU agency policies regarding confidentiality and hinder rapport between SU providers and parents. On the other hand, lack of information sharing can create unhealthy dynamics in relationships between parents, CWS, and SU providers – as seen in results – and impact CWS monitoring of child safety. Most policies for information sharing and release of information vary across CWS and across service agencies; thus, agreement on and clarity in procedures and extent of information sharing between agencies, as well as guidelines for clearly outlining information sharing to parents (i.e., ensuring parents are aware of limits to confidentiality, etc.) is paramount [18, 25, 51]. Results illustrated how effective relationships between CWS and SU – in part through information sharing – was beneficial as it had the potential to foster effective, open, relationships between all parties. In summary, collaborative strategies should seek to complement both systems’ roles and procedures rather than place systems in contradiction to one another.

CWS and SU providers overwhelmingly agreed on the impact of collaboration on outcomes for parents. Providers across systems emphasized how collaborative relationships create an essential safety net that helps maintain parental engagement in services, consistent with findings for the impact of collaboration on receipt of services [17]. The united front presented through strong cross-system collaboration was identified as a key factor in sustaining parents’ motivation throughout treatment, while conversely, when collaboration breaks down and there are disagreements, this can damage parents’ relationships with both CWS and SU providers and weaken engagement.

These shared perspectives on the impact of collaboration also parallel the narratives of participants who reported positive, effective collaborative relationships. Those who reported effective collaboration conveyed a positive attitude regarding collaboration, commonly noting the importance of working as a team and acknowledging and respecting each other’s expertise and roles. They also noted the additional efforts beyond the formal strategies in place needed to establish partnerships and effective communication (e.g., building relationships outside of case collaboration, finding efficient modes of communication). Such efforts are particularly important in the context of parental SU: beyond the challenges of having different clients and competing organizational priorities, the stigma associated with SU and addiction and individuals’ personal connections to addiction can heighten conflicting perspectives. Thus, establishing relationships outside this context can be beneficial.

These narratives demonstrating effective collaboration, parallel interorganizational research regarding the impact of organizational culture and values on individuals’ attitudes and behaviors [52] and also point to some implications. For example, leadership has the potential to impact collaboration, as leadership is critical in diffusing values across an organization and sustaining organizational practices [44, 53, 54]. Organizational change interventions targeting leadership, such as the Leadership for Organizational Change Intervention (LOCI), have been effective in helping the implementation and sustainment of evidence-based practices and could be useful in maintaining collaborative strategies and promoting a culture of collaboration [55]. Other interagency strategies such as hosting joint supervision, co-locating employees, and ongoing training are also effective in further facilitating collaboration [25].

Moreover, there have been significant advancements to addressing parental SU since earlier research on cross-system collaboration between CWS and SU treatment agencies was published over 15 years ago. New programming exists that addresses many of these identified challenges to collaboration; many service models now focus on family preservation, which further facilitates shared goals between parents, CWS, and SU service providers [56, 57]. Other evidence-based programs – such as those aimed at preventing foster case placements [58] as well as family recovery courts [59] – are also commonly used models to address both SU and other family needs and require close coordination. These family-centered programs are ideal as they align with common goals of both CWS treatment plans (i.e., ensuring child safety) and SU treatment plans (i.e., supporting parents in recovery), naturally facilitating collaboration between CWS and SU providers. Work is needed in dissemination and implementation of these effective programs to further improve how parental SU is addressed.

Results also point to policy implications. Individual organizations need to ensure policies support cross-system collaboration and communication; it could even be helpful to enact shared or joint policies between agencies. Implications for much larger policy and legislation include the importance of continuing to support and advance policies such as the Family First Preservation Services Act which promote adoption of effective family preservation service models that inherently require cross-system collaboration.

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. First, there are limitations to generalizability of findings. We sampled individuals from across the state representing multiple SU treatment agencies and CWS county offices; thus, results are limited to the context of Indiana. The study found no clear pattern across geographic region, rurality, role (i.e., leadership vs. frontline), race, or ethnicity; however, it is unclear if this is representative of the state as a whole or simply the sample studied. Given the voluntary nature of the study, there may be bias in individuals who chose to participate, including their job function/role, and their experiences may not be generalizable. Nonetheless, interviews were conducted until saturation was reached. Second, participants were not explicitly asked to comprehensively or systematically identify collaborative strategies utilized, and most participants were frontline workers who might not have intimate knowledge of the extent of strategies implemented at the administrative level; thus, strategies described here were only those common across participants and there are likely others. Moreover, results do not speak to the effectiveness of collaborative strategies but instead shed light on individuals’ perspectives of their utility. Future research should systematically examine the use of formal collaborative strategies and measure their effectiveness. Lastly, given results for the impact of collaboration on outcomes for parents, a major limitation is the missing perspective of parents themselves and their perspectives on collaboration. Further, results are limited to discussion of coordination between SU services and CWS and do not consider additional services often needed to address co-occurring mental health and chronic health conditions seen with problem SU.

Conclusions

Study findings underscore the important role that collaboration plays in supporting parents with SU involved in CWS, as collaboration is critical for successful connection to and engagement in SU treatment and other CWS requirements. Formalized collaborative strategies – such as contracts, data sharing procedures, and frontline coordination – are necessary but not sufficient for effective collaboration: successful collaboration requires efforts by frontline staff and leadership beyond the scaffolding that these strategies provide. Moreover, competing perspectives on how to address parental SU still exist. Legislation and policy focused on family preservation is one way to continue to facilitate collaboration and shared goals between CWS and SU service providers.
