Self-esteem in patients post-percutaneous coronary intervention: a cross-sectional examination
Authors: Bo Chen, Yanbei Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPercutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is a widely used therapeutic modality for coronary heart disease (CHD). This study aimed to characterize self-esteem levels and identify key factors influencing self-esteem among CHD patients following PCI.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among CHD patients undergoing PCI at our hospital between February 2024 and January 2025. Self-esteem was assessed using the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES), a 10-item 4-point Likert scale with a total score range of 10–40 points (higher scores indicate greater self-worth). Clinical trial number: Not applicable.ResultsA total of 316 CHD patients were included. The study sample had a mean age of 62.3 ± 8.5 years, with 30.4% being female. The overall mean RSES score was 23.18 ± 3.05. Correlation analysis revealed significant associations between self-esteem and age (r = -0.610), educational level (r = 0.602), per capita monthly household income (r = 0.628), place of residence (r = 0.534), duration of CHD diagnosis (r = 0.614), and number of stents implanted (r = -0.631). Multiple linear regression confirmed these as independent factors: age (β = -0.142, 95% CI: -0.215 to -0.069), educational level (β = 0.876, 95% CI: 0.321 to 1.431), per capita monthly household income (β = 0.563, 95% CI: 0.189 to 0.937), place of residence (β = 1.245, 95% CI: 0.152 to 2.338), duration of CHD diagnosis (β = 0.987, 95% CI: 0.526 to 1.448), and number of stents implanted (β = -0.320, 95% CI: -0.451 to -0.189). Variance inflation factor (VIF) values ranged from 1.32 to 2.45 (no significant multicollinearity).ConclusionsCHD patients post-PCI exhibit self-esteem levels within the lower range of general population norms, with several factors independently influencing these scores. These findings highlight the need for targeted psychological interventions tailored to vulnerable subgroups to enhance post-PCI psychological well-being and optimize recovery outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05664-5.

Introduction

Coronary Heart Disease (CHD) is characterized by coronary artery stenosis, occlusion, or functional abnormalities leading to myocardial ischemia or infarction [1, 2], and remains a major public health challenge in China with substantial health and economic burdens [3, 4]. Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (PCI) is a widely adopted minimally invasive treatment for CHD, effectively improving myocardial perfusion due to its safety and rapid efficacy [5]. Despite technological advancements, postoperative complications and concerns about restenosis often contribute to negative psychological states in patients, including anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem [6–8]—factors that may hinder recovery and reduce quality of life [9].

With the shift toward positive psychology in CHD research [10], self-esteem—defined as a core evaluation of an individual’s self-worth—has emerged as a critical determinant of illness adaptation, aligning with Lazarus and Folkman’s stress-coping framework. According to this theoretical model, an individual’s appraisal of stressors (e.g., illness-related uncertainty, functional limitations post-PCI) and subsequent coping strategies are shaped by personal resources, among which self-esteem serves as a key psychological asset [11]. High self-esteem enhances individuals’ ability to engage in adaptive coping (e.g., seeking social support, adhering to medical recommendations) by fostering a sense of control and resilience, thereby supporting physical and mental health outcomes. In contrast, low self-esteem is associated with maladaptive coping patterns (e.g., avoidance, helplessness), social withdrawal, and compromised quality of life, as individuals may perceive illness-related stressors as overwhelming [12]. In CHD patients, lower self-esteem levels have been linked to impaired treatment adherence, reduced stress resilience, and adverse disease prognosis [13–15], underscoring its regulatory role in mediating the stress-response process and influencing disease progression and recovery.

However, existing literature predominantly positions self-esteem as a predictive variable for prognosis, with limited research investigating it as an outcome variable shaped by contextual and individual factors. Specifically, studies on self-esteem among post-PCI patients remain scarce, and the mechanisms through which factors such as rural residence (e.g., associated with reduced access to psychosocial resources, greater illness-related stigma) or educational level (e.g., influencing health literacy and stress appraisal) impact self-esteem remain underexplored. Guided by Lazarus and Folkman’s stress-coping framework [16, 17], this cross-sectional study aims to systematically assess self-esteem status in CHD patients following PCI and identify its influencing factors. By elucidating how individual (e.g., age, educational level) and contextual (e.g., place of residence, income) factors interact to shape self-esteem via stress appraisal and coping processes, this study seeks to provide empirical evidence for the development of targeted psychological nursing interventions to improve patient outcomes and quality of life. Methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted following a protocol that was reviewed and approved by the hospital’s ethics committee (approval number: 2025234). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants included in the study. The research protocol strictly adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

This study recruited patients with CHD who underwent PCI in our hospital from February 2024 to January 2025. Consecutive sampling was used to recruit participants from the Cardiology Department of our hospital, a tertiary cardiac care center located in Suzhou, China.The inclusion criteria for participants in this study were as follows: patients aged ≥ 18 years; confirmed diagnosis of CHD via coronary angiography [18] and subsequent treatment with PCI at our hospital; possession of normal reading and comprehension abilities, enabling effective communication with the researchers; and voluntary participation in the study with signed informed consent.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with cognitive impairments or psychiatric conditions that preclude participation in questionnaire surveys; patients with severe comorbidities such as hepatic or renal failure, severe congestive heart failure, significant cerebrovascular sequelae, malignant tumors, or those in the terminal stage of disease; and patients unwilling to participate in the study.

We collected following information regarding the characteristics of patients undergoing PCI: age, gender, body mass index (BMI), marital status, educational level, per capita monthly household income, place of residence, presence of hypertension, presence of diabetes mellitus, presence of hyperlipidemia, duration of CHD diagnosis, number of PCI, number of stents implanted.

In this study, we utilized the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES) to assess the self-esteem levels of patients. Originally developed by Rosenberg in 1965, the RSES was designed to evaluate adolescents’ sense of self-worth and self-acceptance [19]. Owing to its robust psychometric properties and user-friendly design, the RSES has been widely adopted in research across diverse populations, including patients with CHD. The questionnaire used in our study has previously been published elsewhere [20, 21]. The RSES consists of 10 items, with five items scored positively and five scored negatively. It employs a 4-point rating scale, yielding a total score ranging from 10 to 40 points. Higher scores indicate higher levels of self-esteem. The Chinese adaptation of the RSES used in this study was previously validated specifically in a sample of Chinese cardiac patients [22, 23]. In that validation study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.85) and construct validity, with confirmatory factor analysis supporting a single-factor structure (factor loadings ranging from 0.68 to 0.82), which is consistent with the original RSES. In the current study sample, we assessed the reliability of the scale and found a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.89, indicating excellent internal consistency. We also conducted exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to confirm the construct validity. The EFA results revealed a single factor that explained 68.2% of the total variance, with all 10 items loading strongly on this factor (loadings ranging from 0.71 to 0.83). These findings confirm that the Chinese version of the RSES is reliable and valid for assessing self-esteem among post-PCI patients.

In this study, data were collected using a questionnaire survey conducted on the day before discharge for patients with CHD following PCI. Prior to the survey, researchers provided detailed explanations of the study’s objectives and significance to patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Questionnaires were distributed only after obtaining written informed consent from the participants. For patients who were unable to read the questionnaire independently due to poor vision, low educational level, or other reasons but still met the inclusion criteria, a one-on-one interview method was employed. The researchers read each item aloud and recorded the patients’ verbal responses accurately. Upon questionnaire collection, researchers carefully checked for completeness. Any missing information was promptly completed on-site to ensure the integrity and validity of the data.

With regard to missing data handling, no missing data were observed for any of the variables reported in this study. During questionnaire collection, research staff conducted immediate completeness checks for each completed form; any omitted items were promptly completed on-site through direct clarification with the participating patients. This rigorous quality control procedure ensured the integrity of the dataset (n = 316) for all demographic, clinical, and psychosocial variables analyzed. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0. Normality of continuous data was assessed via the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as n (%). Intergroup comparisons of normally distributed continuous data were conducted using independent samples t-test (binary categories) or one-way ANOVA (multiple categories); categorical variables were analyzed with the chi-square test (χ² test) as appropriate. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to explore associations between self-esteem (normally distributed) and patient characteristics (categorical variables coded as dummy variables). Multiple linear regression analysis (stepwise selection) was employed to identify independent factors influencing self-esteem, with variables showing P < 0.1 in correlation analysis included in the model. Model assumptions (normality of residuals, no multicollinearity [VIF < 3.0], no heteroscedasticity) were verified. Subgroup analyses were further performed by stratifying data by age (< 60 years vs. ≥60 years) and place of residence (urban vs. rural) to validate result robustness. All tests were two-tailed, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Post-hoc power analysis (GPower 3.1) confirmed adequate statistical power (92%) for detecting small-to-medium effect sizes (f²=0.10) at α = 0.05.

Results

A total of 350 eligible patients were approached within 1–3 days post-PCI, of whom 316 provided written informed consent and completed the survey (response rate: 90.3%). The remaining 34 patients declined participation due to post-procedural fatigue (n = 18), anxiety about their cardiac condition (n = 10), or family-related time constraints (n = 6). No significant differences in demographic or clinical characteristics were observed between participants and non-participants (all p > 0.05), suggesting minimal selection bias.

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the 316 enrolled patients are summarized in Table 1. The cohort was predominantly male (69.6%), married (88.0%), and urban-dwelling (64.6%). Common comorbidities included hypertension (42.4%), diabetes mellitus (26.9%), and hyperlipidemia (22.1%). Most patients (72.2%) had undergone a single PCI procedure. The mean RSES score was 23.18 ± 3.05, with scores approximating a normal distribution (Fig. 2).

Bivariate analyses revealed significant differences in self-esteem across several sociodemographic and clinical subgroups (Table 1). Higher self-esteem was associated with younger age (< 60 years), higher educational attainment, greater household income (≥ 5000 CNY/month), urban residence, longer duration of CHD diagnosis, and fewer stents implanted (all p < 0.05). A graded positive association between educational level and self-esteem is illustrated in Fig. 3. In contrast, no significant differences in self-esteem were observed by gender, BMI, marital status, presence of comorbidities, number of prior PCI procedures, or lesion vessel distribution (all p > 0.05).

Correlation analyses (Table 2) demonstrated moderate to strong associations between self-esteem and several key factors, including age (r = − 0.610, p = 0.027), educational level (r = 0.602, p = 0.034), per capita monthly household income (r = 0.628, p = 0.010), place of residence (r = 0.534, p = 0.045), duration of CHD diagnosis (r = 0.614, p = 0.007), and number of stents implanted (r = − 0.631, p = 0.004). The inverse relationship between age and self-esteem is depicted in Fig. 4.

Given that some variables (e.g., educational level, per capita monthly household income) are ordinal, Spearman rank correlation analyses were conducted as a sensitivity check to verify the robustness of the correlation results. As shown in Supplementary Table 2, the Spearman correlation results were virtually identical to the Pearson correlation results in both magnitude and statistical significance, confirming that the associations between self-esteem and the key factors were robust to distributional assumptions.

Multiple linear regression analysis (Table 3; Fig. 5) identified six independent predictors of self-esteem: age (β = − 0.142, p = 0.018), educational level (β = 0.876, p = 0.032), per capita monthly household income (β = 0.563, p = 0.029), place of residence (β = 1.245, p = 0.039), duration of CHD diagnosis (β = 0.987, p = 0.003), and number of stents implanted (β = − 0.320, p = 0.002). The model accounted for 42.3% of the variance in RSES scores (adjusted R² = 0.402, p < 0.001). All regression assumptions were met, including normality of residuals and absence of multicollinearity (VIF range: 1.32–2.45). Subgroup analyses stratified by age and place of residence yielded consistent effect estimates, supporting the robustness of these findings (Supplementary Table 1).

Discussion

Self-esteem, defined as an individual’s positive evaluation and emotional experience of their self-worth and capabilities within specific sociocultural contexts, reflects the fundamental human pursuit of dignity and value [24]. As a core psychological resource, self-esteem is closely linked to mental health, adaptive behavioral patterns, social functioning, and illness recovery trajectories [25, 26]. The present study examined self-esteem levels and their correlates among patients with coronary heart disease (CHD) following percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). It should be noted that, in the absence of a comparison group comprising CHD patients not undergoing PCI or healthy controls, we cannot infer that PCI itself diminishes self-esteem. Rather, the relatively low self-esteem observed in this cohort suggests that many patients may experience deficits in self-worth evaluation, potentially lacking confidence in their capacity to manage their illness and navigate associated role demands. Such diminished self-esteem may not only undermine patients’ confidence in disease control and treatment adherence but also impede the broader recovery process. Accordingly, healthcare providers should attend to existing self-esteem concerns and address patients’ psychosocial needs. By identifying factors that influence self-esteem and developing targeted nursing interventions, clinicians can support patients in enhancing self-evaluation and self-acceptance, thereby promoting holistic recovery and psychological well-being.

Older age was associated with significantly lower self-esteem following PCI. This finding is consistent with research by Huang et al. [27], who reported that postoperative self-esteem in elderly patients was approximately 30% lower than in younger counterparts. While the overall mean RSES score in our sample falls within the lower range of general population norms (21–25 [19]), the marked age-related disparity remains clinically meaningful. Several mechanisms may underlie this association. Older adults commonly experience age-related declines in physical function and increased medication burden, which may diminish self-efficacy [28]. Additionally, shifts in social roles and reduced social participation following retirement can erode perceived self-worth [29]. Concerns about postoperative complications and long-term prognosis may further exacerbate psychological distress [30]. In clinical practice, comprehensive intervention strategies for older patients may include establishing peer support groups to strengthen social networks, implementing adaptive training programs to enhance self-management skills and psychological resilience, and providing individualized psychological counseling to address prognosis-related anxiety [31].

Educational attainment emerged as a significant determinant of self-esteem, with lower education levels predicting reduced self-worth. Prior research has indicated that individuals with lower educational attainment may have self-esteem levels approximately 40% lower than those with higher education [32]. This association may operate through several pathways: limited educational background can constrain patients’ capacity to comprehend and integrate disease-related information, thereby undermining confidence in participating in treatment decisions [33]. moreover, patients with lower education may lack effective coping strategies, rendering them more vulnerable to feelings of helplessness [34]. The relatively strong intercorrelations observed among education, income, and self-esteem (r ≈ 0.60–0.63) likely reflect shared variance among these indicators of socioeconomic position. In the Chinese context, educational attainment strongly predicts income level and urban residence, suggesting that these variables may collectively represent a broader latent construct of socioeconomic status. Although multicollinearity diagnostics remained acceptable (VIF < 2.5), these associations should be interpreted as interrelated rather than independent contributors to self-esteem. Healthcare institutions should therefore develop health education materials tailored to patients with varying educational backgrounds, utilizing multimedia approaches that combine visual and auditory elements to enhance comprehension and engagement [35]. Peer education programs may further empower patients to manage their conditions effectively, ultimately improving self-esteem and health outcomes.

Economic factors also demonstrated significant associations with self-esteem. Financial strain may impact psychological well-being through multiple mechanisms: the economic burden of medical treatment can evoke feelings of guilt and dependency [36]. limited income may restrict access to high-quality rehabilitation resources; and persistent financial worry can exacerbate anxiety, further compromising psychological health [37]. Integrating a multidisciplinary economic support system into nursing practice—including social worker involvement, medical insurance consultation, and connections to charitable resources—may help alleviate these burdens [38]. Importantly, Chinese cultural factors may shape the expression and experience of self-esteem in the post-PCI context and warrant consideration in clinical care. First, the cultural value of “face-saving” (Mianzi) may lead some patients to underreport psychological distress or low self-esteem, as these may be perceived as signs of personal weakness. Healthcare providers should cultivate non-judgmental, supportive environments that encourage open discussion of psychological concerns. Second, family-centric care is a core value in Chinese culture, and family members often play pivotal roles in patients’ recovery. Interventions engaging family members—such as family education sessions and family-centered support groups—may be particularly effective in enhancing self-esteem by strengthening social support networks. Third, traditional Chinese beliefs sometimes prioritize physical health over mental health, potentially leading patients to overlook or minimize psychological needs. Clinicians should normalize discussions about mental health and self-esteem, emphasizing their importance for overall recovery and quality of life. Finally, rural-urban disparities in healthcare access and social support systems may exacerbate vulnerability among rural patients, underscoring the need for targeted interventions such as telehealth support and community-based rehabilitation programs.

Consistent with this urban-rural disparity, patients residing in rural areas exhibited lower self-esteem compared to their urban counterparts. This finding likely reflects the limited availability of medical resources, inadequate rehabilitation support, and weaker social support networks characteristic of rural regions [39]. Research has demonstrated that rural patients are more susceptible to feelings of isolation and helplessness during postoperative recovery [40]. Nursing interventions addressing these challenges should emphasize the role of community nurses through regular home visits, the establishment of patient support groups, and the provision of telemedical support [41].

Contrary to the conventional expectation that longer disease duration would correlate with lower self-esteem—owing to cumulative physical and psychological burdens, diminished confidence in recovery, and persistent stress-related helplessness [42, 43]— our study identified an unexpected positive association between longer CHD duration and higher self-esteem. This finding aligns with the notion that prolonged engagement with a chronic condition may enable patients to develop adaptive coping strategies, gain confidence from sustained disease management success, and experience reduced uncertainty about long-term prognosis—all of which can foster improved self-esteem over time. This counterintuitive pattern does not negate the need for targeted psychological support in CHD patients but rather underscores the importance of tailoring interventions to disease duration. For newly diagnosed patients, who may face heightened anxiety and limited experience with disease self-management, rehabilitation plans should prioritize early psychological support to address initial distress, build self-efficacy, and prevent the development of negative self-perceptions. For those with longer disease duration, interventions can focus on reinforcing existing coping skills, sustaining treatment adherence, and addressing potential residual psychological needs—such as managing long-term complications or preventing complacency in self-care. Across all subgroups, personalized rehabilitation plans should integrate comprehensive psychological support (e.g., counseling, stress management techniques) and disease-specific education to empower patients with the knowledge and skills necessary for effective self-management [44], thereby promoting sustained psychological well-being irrespective of disease duration.

A greater number of stent implantations was significantly associated with lower self-esteem. Importantly, as noted, stent count serves as a surrogate marker for underlying disease severity rather than a direct consequence of PCI itself—reflecting more complex coronary pathology that may independently influence psychological outcomes. This association may be driven by heightened patient concerns about long-term prognosis, increased vigilance toward health status, and elevated risk of emotional distress; prior research has linked multiple stent implantations to greater anxiety and depression [45], which can further erode self-esteem through persistent psychological burden. Clinical interventions should prioritize targeted support for patients with more complex coronary disease (as indicated by higher stent counts). Providing detailed, personalized postoperative education—including clear explanations of disease severity, expected recovery trajectories, and strategies to manage potential complications—can help alleviate uncertainty and empower patients in their self-care [46]. Routine psychological screening for this high-risk subgroup may facilitate early identification of distress, enabling timely interventions (e.g., counseling, stress management techniques) to mitigate negative impacts on self-esteem. Notably, while variables such as age, income, and education are well-documented general predictors of self-esteem in broader populations, the unique association between stent number (as a marker of disease severity) and self-esteem underscores the need for PCI-specific care frameworks that address both the physiological and psychological dimensions of complex coronary disease.

Building on the identification of high-risk subgroups—including elderly patients, rural residents, low-income individuals, and those with low educational attainment—we propose the following practical interventions to enhance self-esteem and psychological well-being among post-PCI patients: [1] Implement routine self-esteem screening using the RSES during post-PCI hospitalization and at follow-up visits. For patients with scores in the lower range (≤ 22 points), refer them to psychological counseling services or cardiac rehabilitation programs with integrated psychological support [2]. Develop culturally tailored health education materials accessible to patients with low educational attainment, utilizing simple language, visual aids, and practical examples to facilitate understanding of their condition, treatment plan, and self-management strategies [3]. Establish peer support groups for post-PCI patients, either in-person or via online platforms, to provide emotional validation, shared coping experiences, and practical advice—all of which can enhance self-esteem and social connectedness [4]. Train cardiac nurses and other healthcare providers in basic psychological assessment and supportive counseling skills, including techniques for identifying low self-esteem, facilitating open discussions about psychological concerns, and providing empathetic support [5]. Integrate family members into the care process, particularly for elderly patients, to enhance social support, reduce feelings of isolation, and empower patients to engage in self-management behaviors—thereby positively influencing self-esteem.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include a relatively large sample size (n = 316), the use of consecutive sampling to minimize selection bias, and the adoption of the validated Chinese version of the RSES to ensure reliable assessment of self-esteem. However, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design prevents the establishment of causal relationships between the identified factors and self-esteem. We can only conclude that these variables are associated with self-esteem, not that they cause changes in self-esteem. Second, the study was conducted at a single tertiary care center in an urban area, which limits the generalizability of the findings to patients treated in rural hospitals, primary care settings, or other regions of China. Third, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce response bias, such as social desirability bias (patients may overreport positive self-evaluations to please the researchers). Fourth, we did not measure several potential confounders that may influence self-esteem, including social support, coping styles, medication adherence, and subjective perception of disease severity. Future studies should include these variables to better control for their effects. Fifth, the study did not assess changes in self-esteem over time, which would provide valuable insights into how self-esteem evolves during the recovery process and identify critical periods for intervention. Future studies should adopt longitudinal designs to explore the trajectory of self-esteem changes over time following PCI, which would help identify critical periods for intervention. Additionally, randomized controlled trials are needed to evaluate the efficacy of targeted interventions (e.g., cognitive-behavioral therapy, family-centered support programs) in improving self-esteem and clinical outcomes (e.g., medication adherence, quality of life) among post-PCI patients. Further research should also explore the role of unmeasured confounders such as social support and coping strategies in shaping self-esteem.

Conclusions

In conclusion, rather than characterizing the overall sample as having low self-esteem, our findings highlight clinically meaningful differences across patient subgroups. The 5.18-point difference in mean RSES scores between younger and older patients, the 5.79-point gap between low and high education groups, and the 3.54-point disparity between urban and rural residents all exceed the minimal clinically important difference reported for this scale in cardiac populations. These subgroup disparities—not the absolute population mean—underscore the need for targeted psychological support for vulnerable populations, including older adults, rural residents, and those with lower socioeconomic status. It is important to acknowledge the cross-sectional nature of this study, which precludes causal inferences between the identified factors and self-esteem. Future longitudinal research is warranted to explore how self-esteem evolves over the post-PCI recovery trajectory and to evaluate the efficacy of targeted interventions in improving psychological outcomes for this patient population.
