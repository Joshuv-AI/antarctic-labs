Progressive Giant Left Atrial Appendage Thrombus After Aborted Appendage Occlusion Despite Anticoagulation

History of Presentation

An 84-year-old man with permanent atrial fibrillation presented to a referring hospital with sudden left upper extremity pain and weakness of the fingers. Physical examination revealed coldness of the left upper extremity, poor palpation of the left brachial and radial arteries, and motor dysfunction of the left fingers. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography demonstrated occlusion at the origin of the left axillary artery, and the patient was referred to our hospital for emergency thrombectomy.

Emergency thrombectomy was performed, and the event was diagnosed as cardioembolic. Anticoagulation therapy was subsequently changed from warfarin to dabigatran (300 mg/d).

Three days after thrombectomy, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) revealed a small thrombus within the left atrial appendage (LAA) measuring 8.6 × 3.8 mm. The thrombus resolved under continued anticoagulation therapy.

Because the patient had experienced an embolic event despite anticoagulation therapy and had a CHA2DS2-VASc score of 5 and a HAS-BLED score of 4, left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) using the Watchman device was considered. The LAA morphology was consistent with a cactus-type appendage, and the appendage depth was shallow relative to the ostial diameter. Implantation was attempted using Watchman FLX Pro devices (40 mm and 35 mm), but stable positioning could not be achieved. Therefore, the procedure was aborted. During test deployment, marked blood stasis was observed within the device (Figure 1). Immediate postprocedural TEE showed no apparent thrombus in the LAA. Dabigatran was temporarily discontinued on the day of the procedure, and anticoagulation was managed with heparin under activated clotting time guidance during the procedure. Dabigatran was resumed the following morning and was continued without interruption thereafter. On the day following the procedure, transthoracic echocardiography suggested the presence of a thrombus within the LAA measuring 4.6 × 10.6 mm (Figure 2).

Past Medical History

The patient had permanent atrial fibrillation, hypertension, and a history of cardioembolic stroke. He had been receiving warfarin and clopidogrel therapy with therapeutic international normalized ratio control.

Differential Diagnosis

After the aborted LAAO procedure, the early echocardiographic finding within the LAA raised concern for recurrent native LAA thrombus. However, pectinate muscle disruption related to device manipulation, postprocedural structural change, and echocardiographic artifact were also considered.

Investigations

Despite continued dabigatran therapy, transthoracic echocardiography performed 76 days after the procedure demonstrated enlargement of the thrombus to 9.7 × 16.5 mm. Furthermore, TEE performed 98 days after the procedure revealed a giant thrombus measuring 20 × 25 to 30 mm within the LAA (Figure 3).

A giant thrombus measuring 20 mm × 25 to 30 mm with high echogenicity and limited mobility is observed, partially protruding from the left atrial appendage.

A formal hypercoagulable evaluation was not fully completed. Antithrombin III activity, protein C activity, protein S activity, anticardiolipin immunoglobulin G, anticardiolipin immunoglobulin M, and homocysteine levels were all within the normal range. However, additional thrombophilia testing, including factor V Leiden and prothrombin gene mutation, was not performed. Tumor marker testing was also not performed, although computed tomography did not reveal any evident malignancy.

Management

Because of the high risk of severe systemic embolization, minimally invasive cardiac surgery was performed 100 days after the aborted procedure to remove the thrombus and resect the LAA.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Gross examination of the surgical specimen revealed a large thrombus attached to pseudo-endocardial tissue within the LAA (Figure 4). Histopathological examination demonstrated fibrous thickening of the LAA endocardium. The pseudo-endocardial tissue consisted of fibrous tissue with fibroblast infiltration and neovascularization. The thrombus was predominantly fibrin-rich and consistent with a subacute thrombus (Figure 5). The postoperative course was uneventful. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography performed 9 days after surgery confirmed complete closure of the LAA. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 14. During outpatient follow-up, he has remained clinically stable without recurrent embolic events.

Discussion

The present case illustrates a potentially underrecognized clinical scenario: giant LAA thrombus formation after an aborted Watchman implantation despite continued anticoagulation therapy. Although device-related thrombus after successful LAAO procedures has been well described, the clinical course after aborted LAAO procedures remains poorly characterized.

LAAO using the Watchman device has been established as an alternative strategy for stroke prevention in patients with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation who are not suitable for long-term anticoagulation therapy. Randomized trials such as PROTECT AF (WATCHMAN Left Atrial Appendage System for Embolic Protection in Patients with Atrial Fibrillation) and PREVAIL (Prospective Randomized Evaluation of the Watchman Left Atrial Appendage Closure Device In Patients With Atrial Fibrillation Versus Long Term Warfarin Therapy) demonstrated the noninferiority of LAAO compared with warfarin therapy.1,2 In addition, the PRAGUE-17 (Left Atrial Appendage Closure vs Novel Anticoagulation Agents in Atrial Fibrillation) trial showed that LAAO is comparable to direct oral anticoagulants in high-risk patients.3 Based on these findings, current guidelines and expert consensus statements recognize LAAO as an important therapeutic option in appropriately selected patients.4,5

The procedural success rate of Watchman implantation has been reported to be approximately 90% to 98% in randomized trials and registry studies.6 However, unfavorable LAA anatomy may lead to procedural failure or abortion. Anatomical factors associated with unsuccessful implantation include shallow appendage depth, chicken-wing morphology, multilobed appendages, and prominent pectinate ridges. Preprocedural imaging using cardiac computed tomography and TEE is therefore important for appropriate procedural planning.7,8

Device-related thrombus is a recognized complication after successful LAAO procedures and occurs in approximately 3% to 7% of cases. Device-related thrombus has been associated with an increased risk of thromboembolic events.9,10 However, these reports have focused on patients in whom device implantation was successful, and little is known about thrombus formation after aborted LAAO procedures.

In general, thrombus formation after an unsuccessful or aborted LAAO attempt is not routinely observed, particularly in patients maintained on therapeutic oral anticoagulation.

To the best of our knowledge, thrombus formation within the native LAA after aborted Watchman implantation has not been previously reported.

In the present case, a small LAA thrombus that had previously resolved under dabigatran therapy reappeared after the procedure and progressively enlarged despite continued anticoagulation therapy. Temporary interruption of anticoagulation on the procedural day may have contributed to thrombus formation. In addition, marked blood flow stagnation within the device was observed during provisional deployment. This observation may provide mechanistic insight into thrombus formation after aborted LAAO procedures. Even without device implantation, transient alteration of LAA flow dynamics during attempted deployment may have induced local blood stasis, and repeated device manipulation may also have caused local endothelial disruption. These factors, possibly compounded by an underlying prothrombotic predisposition, may have overcome the protective effect of anticoagulation and promoted progressive thrombus formation. This suggests that aborted procedures may not be physiologically neutral events.

This case also highlights the clinical challenge of distinguishing early thrombus formation from postprocedural structural changes. The initial echocardiographic finding was interpreted as possible pectinate muscle disruption or procedural artifact, which influenced the follow-up strategy. This underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion and considering earlier or repeated imaging when thrombus cannot be definitively excluded.

This case may represent a high-risk intersection of permanent atrial fibrillation, a prior thromboembolic event despite anticoagulation, repeated deployment attempts, marked blood stasis during provisional deployment, and a possible prothrombotic milieu. In such a setting, patients undergoing aborted LAAO procedures may remain vulnerable to thrombus formation even when anticoagulation therapy is continued. Careful follow-up imaging should therefore be considered in such patients.

Current guidelines and consensus documents recommend postprocedural imaging surveillance after successful implantation to assess device-related thrombus and peri-device leak. However, they do not specifically address follow-up after aborted LAAO procedures in which no device is ultimately implanted. Our case suggests that such procedures may not be physiologically neutral and that careful follow-up imaging may be warranted in selected high-risk patients.

A formal hypercoagulable evaluation was not fully completed, and an underlying prothrombotic predisposition cannot be completely excluded.

In retrospect, closer imaging follow-up might have been warranted before surgery in this case. Fortunately, the patient did not experience any systemic embolic events during the preoperative observation period.

Conclusions

This case underscores that thrombus formation after aborted LAAO may not be benign and highlights the importance of careful postprocedural imaging surveillance and clinical vigilance in high-risk patients.
