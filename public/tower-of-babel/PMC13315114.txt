Controlling mountain-type zoonotic visceral leishmaniasis in China: Current progress and challenges through a One Health lens
Authors: Jingshu Liu, Zhengbin Zhou, Lulu Huang, Zhuowei Luo, Shenglin Chen, Peijun Zhang, Shuxun Wang, Yi Zhang, Junhu Chen, Xiaonong Zhou, Shizhu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDespite effective historical control, Mountain-Type zoonotic visceral leishmaniasis (MT-ZVL) resurged in China in the early 2000s, risking geographical expansion and increased incidence. This study assessed progress in applying the One Health approach for MT-ZVL control in endemic provinces and identified optimization for current control framework.MethodsSeven endemic provinces (2022 data) were assessed with a pre-developed evaluation tool by a weighted scoring method. Results were analyzed to identify progress and challenges and formulate recommendations.ResultsThe average total score was 47.77/100 (range: 38.59–61.72). Category scores ranked: Disease Control (58.28) > External Environment (50.22) > Internal Support (36.86). Within the External Environment, Social Conditions scored highest (90.43), while Natural Environment scored lowest (31.96). Within the Internal Support, Resource Allocation scored highest (75.71), and Financial Investment scored lowest (27.90). Within the Disease Control, Epidemic Surveillance and Reporting scored highest (72.00), while Control Effectiveness scored lowest (42.78).ConclusionThis study evaluated the current status of One Health implementation for MT-ZVL control in seven endemic provinces. The results indicate slow overall progress, with the lowest scores observed for operational capacity and the highest for disease control activities. To advance MT-ZVL control, endemic provinces in China should prioritize integrating the One Health concept into control policies, strengthening the establishment and implementation of multisectoral coordination mechanisms, promoting scientific and technological innovation, and optimizing key interventions such as canine management.

Background

The visceral leishmaniasis (VL) in China is caused by infections of Leishmania donovani and Leishmania infantum. There are 3 different types: anthroponotic visceral leishmaniasis (AVL) is primarily distributed in plains and transmitted by Phlebotomus longiductus and P. wui; the mountain-type zoonotic visceral leishmaniasis (MT-ZVL) is mainly found in mountainous areas and transmitted by P. chinensis, and the reservoir is dog; and the desert sub-type of zoonotic visceral leishmaniasis (DT-ZVL) is in desert areas and transmitted by P. wui and P. alexandri, and the reservoirs are dogs (not primary reservoir host) and unknown wild animals. [1], [2], [3]

As a neglected tropical disease, VL was one of the five major parasitic diseases severely prevalent in China in the 1950s. Through vigorous prevention and control efforts, it was effectively controlled by the 1980s, with only sporadic cases in several provinces [4]. Since the 20th century, compared to other types, there has seen a significant resurgence of prevalence and expansion of epidemic range in MT-ZVL, severely damaging public health in central and western regions. As the rural population declined and demographic structures shifted, the number of elderly and children in rural areas increased, who are proved to be more susceptible to MT-ZVL [5]. This shift has led to a rise in dog ownership, resulting in a substantial growth in dog population. Environmental protection efforts have restored the habitats of wild sandflies, contributing to a surge in its density, significantly raising infection rates in dogs and humans [6], [7]. Additionally, global warming has expanded their habitats northward [8]. In 2021 and 2022, the number of reported MT-ZVL cases in China's endemic areas was 180 and 191, respectively, exceeding the five-year average of 116 cases and accounting for over 70% of all reported VL cases (180/230, 191/239) [9], [10].

Since MT-ZVL is zoonotic, its transmission is influenced by various natural and social-economic factors. There is currently a broad consensus on applying the One Health approach to control VL [11], [12], [13]. China has adopted comprehensive practices to control several parasitic diseases [14], [15], [16], but the application of the One Health approach to control and eliminate MT-ZVL remains in its early stages. Though many studies have conducted risk assessment from integrated surveillance data, few studies have mapped the current progress of controlling MT-ZVL using the One Health approach in endemic areas. In response to this gap, our research team has developed an index system, which has been published [17], and applied it in this study to assess the One Health performance of seven endemic provinces in China from three aspects: external environment, internal support, and disease control actions. The results highlight the current progress and challenges faced by these provinces and offer actionable recommendations when using the One Health approach to control and eliminate MT-ZVL.

Methods

The study utilized the previously published index system (see Appendix 1 Table S1–1 for details) to conduct a cross-sectional survey of seven provinces in China where MT-ZVL is endemic (hereinafter referred to as Province 1 to Province 7). Offline surveys were conducted and data were collected at provincial Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDCs). Additionally, stakeholders were asked to add more information in the offline questionnaires about the progress and challenges when controlling MT-ZVL. Finally, combining the supplementary information of each province, scores of provinces were analyzed and policy recommendations for optimizing future One Health strategies were proposed.

Conceptual definition

The conceptual framework (see Appendix 1 Fig. S1–1 for details) developed by the research team defines what constitutes a good One Health performance on controlling MT-ZVL: i) although its natural conditions are highly conducive to disease emergence, the area should have a stable economic and social environment; ii) it should possess well-established internal conditions, such as the cross-sectoral coordination mechanism, a solid organizational structure, a regulatory framework, and efficient resource allocation and monitoring and evaluation mechanisms to support effective implementation; iii) it should effectively implement disease control measures, which are the core driving force for controlling and eliminating MT-ZVL.

Data collection

Based on the index system, the study designed a questionnaire focused on the indicators of B-Internal Support and C-Disease Control. Data for indicators of A-External Environment were collected from the 2022 provincial statistical yearbook.

The study conducted the survey on June 29, 2023, to collect the data in 2022. The questionnaire was distributed online to the staff at the provincial CDCs, with a request for feedback within one month.

Results

For quantitative data, we collected data of 50 indicators for seven provinces in 2022. The data completeness for six provinces was 100%. One province had missing data in 6 indicators (B6.4, C1.1, C1.2, C1.3, C3.1, C6.2), with a missing rate of 12%. We used the arithmetic mean of the scores of the corresponding indicators in other provinces for interpolation. For qualitative data, we summarized notes from CDCs staff and the data is shown in Appendix 2.

Discussion

The current progress of controlling MT-ZVL using the One Health approach in endemic provinces

In general, there is a large gap in controlling MT-ZVL with the One Health approach in endemic provinces, with the average total score of 47.77, less than half of maximum 100.00. C-Disease Control scored the highest among the three categories (57.28), and B-Internal Support scored lowest (35.83). Endemic provinces have undertaken many efforts in controlling MT-ZVL, but they still need to pay attention to operational capacity. Specifically, the essential resource supplies are relatively adequate (75.71), and the completion rate of routine surveillance is high (72.00). However, critical deficiencies persist in financial investment (28.08), and efforts are still needed in source control (47.37) and transmission pathway intervention (48.66). (see Appendix 4 for more detailed analysis of each province).

Firstly, most endemic provinces have strong capabilities in diagnosis, treatment, reporting, and epidemiological investigation. Performance in case diagnosis and treatment was notably high (C1-Surveillance and Response and C2-Diagnosis and Treatment were the top-scoring indicators in C-Disease Control). All provinces achieved C1.4 scores above 90.00, reflecting high completion rates for case reporting and epidemiological investigations.

Secondly, policy development is insufficient in most endemic provinces, and multi-sectoral mechanisms have yet to be established. The average score of B1-Policy Supply is 38.11. Except for Province 3 and Province 7, most provinces did not perform well in B1 and its sub-indicators. Most endemic provinces have established working groups, but have not issued effective documents to guide cross-sectoral actions. There is a lack of detailed planning for One Health action framework and clear division of responsibilities among different sectors. The shortcomings in policy supply may be due to several reasons: (i) the lack of local governmental engagement in multi-sectoral coordination mechanisms; (ii) the lack of national technical guidance; (iii) the lack of awareness in the importance of cross-sectoral collaboration.

Thirdly, the medical institution setup in most endemic provinces do not meet the needs for diagnosis and treatment of MT-ZVL. The average score of B2-Designated Treatment Facilities is only 32.78. Except for Province 1 (100.00) and Province 2 (47.78), other provinces scored below 13.77 in designated hospital setup (B2.1). Given the generally low economic income and limited medical resources in endemic provinces, they don't think it is necessary to set designated hospitals.

Moreover, operational optimization of zoonotic control measures is needed, particularly of the canine population management in high-transmission provinces. In terms of canine population management, the average score of C3-Source Control is 47.37. Some provinces showed significant weaknesses, such as Province 1 scoring 0.00 on C3.2 (registration of household dogs), Province 2 scoring 1.09 on C3.1 (canine serological screening), etc. Several provinces reported challenges in maintaining long-term control efforts, citing a lack of evidence-based canine population management. Underlying reasons include:•Public Opposition to Culling Campaigns: Increased public education levels and growing societal emphasis on animal welfare have fueled criticism through short video platforms and other multimedia channels, often escalating to formal complaints against governmental agencies.•Fragmented Canine Management Systems: The absence of a coordinated national framework for owned and stray dogs, coupled with inefficient resource allocation, has rendered control measures labor-intensive yet ineffective [18]. For example, Yangquan City in Shanxi Province has implemented a three-year program involving mandatory registration of owned dogs and large-scale stray dog round-ups for centralized confinement. However, this placed unsustainable burdens on local police departments.•Challenging Working Conditions for Field Personnel: Sero-surveillance requires technicians to conduct village-level blood sampling, exposing staff to high workloads and occupational hazards (e.g., dog bites) due to inadequate protective equipment.

Lastly, there have been gaps in professional training and workforce capacity. Provinces performed well in sub-indicators under B3-Workforce Capacity, particularly in CDC personnel training. However, scores were low for clinical doctor training (B3.3) and training for other relevant departments (B3.2, B3.5). Provinces face challenges such as underfunding for training, restrictive staffing quotas, and insufficient motivational mechanisms to engage clinicians and non-health departments in disease control. In addition, although the score of CDC personnel training is high, many provinces pointed out the critical staffing shortages in CDCs at grassroot level, as professionals are often assigned concurrent responsibilities for multiple diseases.

Although MT-ZVL transmission is influenced by ecological and environmental conditions, our findings indicate that ecological suitability does not directly correspond to disease control performance on the cross section. Provinces with higher natural suitability tended to allocate greater financial resources, suggesting risk-responsive investment. However, no consistent association was observed between ecological background and overall control effectiveness.

This supports the conceptual positioning of the present framework as an assessment of prevention and control capacity rather than transmission determinants. Ecological exposure represents contextual risk, whereas internal support mechanisms and operational implementation reflect system preparedness and governance performance. Future studies may integrate environmental suitability indices with capacity scores to develop a combined risk–capacity analytical model.

Four suggestions for improving the One Health efforts in endemic provinces

First, provinces need to enhance policy development. Strategic prioritization should focus on incorporating the One Health paradigm within existing MT-ZVL control frameworks. Evidence-based policy formulation can draw upon several key references, including the “National Malaria Elimination Action Plan (2010-2020)” [19], the “National Plan for the Prevention and Control of Zoonotic Diseases among Animals (2022-2030)” [20], the “Tripartite Guide on Zoonotic Diseases” [21]. National-level authorities should draft and issue comprehensive disease control plans. The China CDC and its subordinate agencies should provide technical support to develop corresponding guidelines. These guiding documents should support endemic provinces in conducting local epidemiological surveys and risk assessments, formulating evidence-based integrated control strategies, and evaluating cross-sectoral intervention outcomes while reporting progress.

Second, provinces should prioritize multi-sectoral coordination and cross-regional collaboration to enhance intervention efficiency targeting reservoirs and vectors. Our results indicate endemic provinces lack effective MT-ZVL control mechanisms. Yangquan City, Shanxi Province, demonstrates feasibility: its health institutions and local governments established consensus in 2020, issuing key documents (e.g., control plans, canine management regulations) defining stakeholder roles [22]. Establishing One Health committees, technical advisory panels, and data-sharing platform [21] could advance coordination. Cross-regional collaboration remains critically underdeveloped. Similar to Europe's experience with cross-border rabies transmission via canine and foxes [23], key questions persist—such as inter-county movement of infected dogs and potential parasite introduction from neighboring provinces—requiring urgent attention.

Third, provinces must enhance cross-sector workforce development and optimize resource allocation. Expanding professional teams (clinical, laboratory, and other sectors) and community workforce is essential. Implementing integrated multisectoral training will build stakeholder awareness and technical capacity for One Health MT-ZVL control. Furthermore, endemic provinces face significant challenges from insufficient funding and medical resources. Efforts should focus on sustaining interventions under constraints, including conducting health economic evaluations to identify cost-effective strategies, integrating resources with existing programs targeting rabies and echinococcosis [24], or optimizing resources based on the prevalence risk.

Last, provinces need to strengthen technological innovation and application nationally and locally. Critical domestic research gaps hinder evidence-based, systematic MT-ZVL control:•Source control: dog culling remains standard practice in most endemic provinces despite being challenged by scientific evidence and field experience [25], [26]. In addition, the low sensitivity of the rK39 strip test for canine screening likely underestimates dog infection rates.•Vector control: Current sandfly density reduction strategies lack effectiveness. Large-scale residual spraying in residences is often prohibitively costly [27], induces insecticide resistance [28], and poses human health risks. Crucially, control must be location-specific as conspecific sandflies occupy distinct ecological niches regionally [29].

Limitations

This study could not establish definitive thresholds for “qualified” or “excellent” performance, necessitating analysis based on relative performance levels. The COVID-19 pandemic during 2021–2022 disrupted routine control activities in many provinces, potentially leading to underestimation of their capabilities. Consequently, the findings may offer a partial perspective on the actual situation.

In addition, because this framework was designed as a capacity-oriented assessment, environmental and climatic factors were not explicitly incorporated as analytical variables. As a result, the present evaluation does not capture how climatic variation may shape the broader transmission context of MT-ZVL.

Furthermore, although the cross-sectional design is appropriate for assessing the current status of prevention and control capacity, repeated multi-year assessments would be more informative for evaluating the temporal stability and robustness of capacity scores. Prevention and control capacity is not static and may change over time due to variations in funding allocation, workforce availability, policy priorities, and responses to emerging public health events. A single time-point assessment may therefore not fully capture the dynamic evolution of institutional capacity.

Future studies may integrate environmental suitability indicators with system performance metrics and incorporate longitudinal observations to develop a more comprehensive and robust risk–capacity evaluation framework.

Conclusion

In summary, the overall progress in using the One Health approach to control MT-ZVL in endemic provinces needs to be improved. While they have made strides in regular interventions—including surveillance, epidemic focus management, and case report—significant challenges persist in multi-sectoral collaboration, workforce capacity building and resources allocation. Policymakers can prioritize three strategies: i) institutionalizing the One Health concept through legislative amendments that mandate integrated control of zoonotic disease or MT-ZVL; ii) adapting key interventions to local context including canine management and residual spraying; iii) accelerating researches targeting technological breakthroughs in reservoir control, vector control, and cost-benefit analysis.
