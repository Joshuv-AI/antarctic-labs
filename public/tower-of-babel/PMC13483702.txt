RNA methylation in cell death: mechanisms and clinical applications
Authors: Shuang Shang, Xianzhao Wang, Xin Xu, Yiwen Huang, Daolin Tang, Jiayi Wang, Lifang Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
RNA methylation represents a critical epi-transcriptomic modification that regulates cell fate by modulating diverse aspects of RNA metabolism, including splicing, export, translation, and degradation. Among the best-characterized modifications are N6-methyladenosine (m6A), 5-methylcytosine (m5C), N7-methylguanosine (m7G), and N1-methyladenosine (m1A), which are dynamically regulated by specific writer, eraser and reader proteins. Dysregulation of these modifications perturbs multiple regulated cell death pathways, such as apoptosis, autophagy, pyroptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, and cuproptosis, thereby promoting tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance. In this review, we summarize the molecular mechanisms by which RNA methylation shapes cell death programs and highlight its potential as a biomarker for cancer diagnosis and prognosis, as well as a promising target for therapeutic intervention.

Introduction

Epigenetic modifications, including DNA methylation, histone modification, chromatin remodeling, and RNA methylation, play fundamental roles in gene regulation and cellular homeostasis [1, 2]. The study of chemical modifications across diverse RNA species has given rise to the field of epi-transcriptomics, which has garnered increasing attention in recent years [3]. Among these modifications, RNA methylation represents a highly dynamic mechanism for controlling RNA function through post-synthetic covalent alterations. These marks influence multiple aspects of RNA metabolism.

Cell death is a fundamental biological process essential for development, tissue homeostasis, and protection against damage or infection. It can be broadly classified into accidental cell death and regulated cell death (RCD) [4]. In addition to classical forms such as autophagy, apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis, several metabolically driven modalities, including ferroptosis and cuproptosis, have recently garnered considerable research interest [5–7], making them promising therapeutic targets across different kinds of cancer [8–10].

Growing evidence highlights the significance of RNA methylation in regulating cell death phenotypes (Fig. 1). While significant progress has been made, the mechanistic basis linking specific RNA modifications to distinct RCD pathways remains incompletely understood. Unlike earlier reviews that largely focused on individual methylation types, this article provides an integrative perspective on how multiple RNA methylation marks collectively regulate cell death modalities. We first summarize the major RNA methylation types and their regulatory enzymes, followed by a discussion of their context-dependent functions in key forms of cell death under physiological and pathological conditions. Particular emphasis is placed on their roles in cancer, as well as their translational potential as diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets (Fig. 2). By consolidating recent advances, we aim to clarify the mechanistic interplay between RNA methylation and cell death and propose future directions for research and clinical application.

Classification and functions of RNA methylation

The most extensively studied types of RNA methylation include m6A, m1A, m5C, and m7G. These modifications are dynamically regulated by three major classes of proteins: methyltransferases (writers), which install the modifications; demethylases (erasers), which remove them; and RNA-binding proteins (readers), which recognize and interpret the marks. These regulators form a coordinated and reversible epi-transcriptomic system that fine-tunes RNA splicing, stability, translation, and degradation, thereby exerting profound effects on gene expression and cellular homeostasis. Dysregulation of RNA methylation is linked to oncogenesis, contributing to aberrant oncogene activation, silencing of tumor suppressors, and resistance to cell death. Consequently, RNA methylation represents a critical bridge between epigenetic regulation and cancer biology and is emerging as a promising target for therapeutic intervention.

m6A

m6A is the most abundant and extensively studied internal RNA modification [11]. It plays a pivotal role in regulating various aspects of RNA metabolism. Alterations in m6A levels have been linked to various tumors, where it can act as either an oncogenic driver or a tumor-suppressor depending on the cellular and molecular context. Accumulating evidence underscores the essential role of m6A in RNA fate determination and cellular homeostasis (Table 1), and ongoing studies are focused on decoding its mechanistic underpinnings and therapeutic potential.

Representative m6A regulators and their biological function

Dysregulated m6A modification has been implicated in diverse pathological processes. For instance, METTL3-mediated m6A promotes the degradation of oncogenic transcripts in bladder cancer [29], while FTO-dependent demethylation of Salt-Inducible Kinase 2 (SIK2) mRNA suppresses autophagy in renal cancer [30]. Together, these findings establish m6A as a context-dependent regulator of cell fate, with significant implications for its utility as both a diagnostic biomarker and a therapeutic target. The therapeutic potential of targeting m6A machinery is exemplified by STC-15, an METTL3 inhibitor undergoing clinical trials for solid tumors, including non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), melanoma, endometrial cancer, and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (NCT05584111, NCT06975293). Beyond METTL3, inhibitors targeting other m6A regulators, such as FTO, have shown promising preclinical efficacy in hematologic and solid malignancies; however, these agents have not yet been in clinical development. Looking ahead, these advances underscore the growing translational landscape of m6A-targeted therapies. Future efforts should prioritize the need for continued efforts to translate these epigenetic insights into clinically viable strategies for cancer diagnosis and treatment.

m1A

m1A was first reported in 1964 as a conserved RNA modification [31]. It has since been detected in multiple RNA classes, including transfer RNA (tRNA), ribosomal RNA (rRNA), and messenger RNA (mRNA) [32, 33]. m1A methylates the nitrogen at position 1, conferring a positive charge to the RNA molecule. This modification frequently occurs in critical regions for RNA stability, such as the T-loop of tRNAs or specific sites in rRNAs, where it contributes to ribosome assembly and function [34]. Beyond structural roles, m1A also regulates gene expression in a location-dependent manner. For example, m1A can disrupt Watson-Crick base pairing within coding sequences (CDS), thereby inhibiting translation [35, 36], whereas m1A in the 5′ untranslated region (UTR) enhances translation initiation [37] (Table 2).

Functions of m1A regulators

Dysregulation of m1A is linked to various human diseases, particularly cancer. Aberrant m1A methylation can alter the translation of oncogenes or tumor suppressors, thereby contributing to tumorigenesis. For instance, changes in ALKBH3 expression modulate m1A-dependent RNA metabolism and promote proliferation in breast and bladder cancers [45, 55]. These findings suggest that m1A serves as a regulatory node in cancer progression and highlights its potential as a therapeutic target. Further studies are needed to elucidate the spatial and temporal regulation of m1A and its mechanistic contributions to health and disease.

m5C

m5C, typically enriched in 3’UTR and CG-rich regions, is a prominent RNA modification involving the methylation of the fifth carbon of cytosine [56–58]. This modification has been identified across diverse RNA classes, including tRNA, rRNA, mRNA, and non-coding RNAs (ncRNA) [59, 60], and plays crucial roles in regulating RNA metabolism, such as protein synthesis, stability, and translation efficiency [61, 62] (Table 3).

Functions of m5C regulators

Functionally, m5C participates in regulating gene expression, cellular differentiation, and disease pathogenesis, with particularly strong implications in cancer. Aberrant m5C methylation patterns have been observed in a variety of malignancies, including breast, lung, and colorectal cancers [73–75]. Overexpression of NSUN2 is frequently associated with enhanced mRNA stability of oncogenic transcripts, promoting tumor cell proliferation and metastasis [74, 76]. These observations suggest that m5C exerts context-dependent effects on disease pathogenesis. However, the absence of well-characterized demethylases and the limited availability of real-time detection technologies hinder a full understanding of its functional roles. The development of refined and high-resolution profiling approaches will be essential to delineate the dynamics of m5C methylation and its contribution to health and disease.

m7G

As a ubiquitous epi-transcriptomic mark, m7G is predominantly located in the 5’ cap of mRNAs, but it also occurs internally within other RNA classes, including tRNAs, rRNAs, and ncRNAs [36, 77] (Table 4). The addition of m7G is catalyzed by specific methyltransferases that transfer a methyl group from S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) to the nitrogen at the seventh position of guanosine [79]. This modification is essential for the regulation of gene expression and in various cellular processes, such as RNA stability, processing, maturation, ribosome biogenesis, and translational control [80, 88–90].

Functions of m7G regulators

Beyond these fundamental regulatory roles, the dysregulation of m7G-mediated pathways has recently been identified as a critical driver in tumorigenesis. Emerging evidence suggests that m7G methylation is intricately implicated in tumorigenesis. Overexpression of METTL1 and WDR4 leads to elevated levels of m7G-modified tRNAs, which in turn enhance oncogenic translation programs [91–93]. Dysregulation of m7G can also disrupt ribosome biogenesis and stress adaptation, thereby exacerbating cancer progression. Despite these insights, research on m7G in human disease is relatively scarce. Future studies dissecting its cell type-specific distribution and regulatory networks will be crucial for clarifying its broader physiological and pathological relevance.

Overall, RNA methylation has emerged as a versatile regulatory layer that connects epi-transcriptomic dynamics with cellular fate decisions. Dysregulation of these modifications not only drives tumor progression, immune evasion, and metabolic imbalance but also uncovers vulnerabilities that may be therapeutically exploited. Importantly, although the biological functions of individual RNA methylation marks have been extensively characterized, increasing evidence suggests that these modifications may not act in isolation. Instead, potential crosstalk and coordinated regulation among different RNA methylation marks may collectively shape RNA fate and cellular responses. This raises the possibility of a broader epi-transcriptomic “code” that integrates multiple RNA modifications to fine-tune gene expression programs in cancer. As high-resolution mapping and functional tools continue to advance, a deeper understanding of RNA methylation will provide new opportunities for cancer stratification and the development of targeted interventions.

RNA methylation in cell death and cancer

Recent studies increasingly underscore the pivotal role of RNA methylation in orchestrating diverse cell death programs, particularly in cancer. Although different types of RNA methylation are involved in the regulation of multiple forms of cell death, increasing evidence suggests that they may share common mechanistic principles. Rather than acting as pathway-specific regulators, RNA methylation is better viewed as a dynamic post-transcriptional layer that broadly controls RNA stability, translation efficiency, and metabolic adaptation under cellular stress conditions, thereby influencing cell fate decisions. Across apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and cuproptosis, RNA methylation frequently affects core biological processes such as oxidative stress, energy metabolism, inflammatory signaling, and protein homeostasis, which ultimately shape cellular sensitivity to death signals. Therefore, despite the molecular differences among distinct cell death pathways, the regulatory logic of RNA methylation may rely on several shared biological mechanisms. Below, we summarize these recent advances in detail.

m6A-mediated regulation of cell death in cancer

m6A is crucial in regulating multiple forms of cell death (Fig. 3). As a dynamic and reversible RNA modification, m6A influences transcript stability, translation efficiency, and RNA-protein interactions, thereby modulating key death-related pathways in both physiological and pathological contexts.

Autophagy is primarily a cytoprotective process, enabling cells to degrade and recycle damaged organelles, misfolded proteins, and other cellular components. Under certain conditions, excessive or dysregulated autophagy can directly lead to cell death, often termed autophagy-dependent cell death [94]. It plays a dual role in disease, acting as a protective mechanism under stress while also contributing to pathological progression when dysregulated. In autophagy, m6A influences the stability and translation of key regulators in cancers (Table 5). In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), YTHDF1 enhances hypoxia-induced autophagy and tumor progression by promoting m6A-dependent translation of autophagy related 2 A (ATG2A) and autophagy related 14 (ATG14). Hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α) directly activates YTHDF1 transcription under hypoxic conditions, and YTHDF1 overexpression correlates with poor clinical prognosis, underscoring its potential as both a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target [100]. More broadly, METTL3 and YTHDF1 often promote autophagy by enhancing the expression of autophagy-related genes, whereas demethylases such as FTO and ALKBH5 suppress autophagic flux by destabilizing critical transcripts [30, 105, 106]. These findings highlight the context-dependent and dual role of m6A in autophagy, functioning as either a promoter or suppressor depending on the disease setting. A deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms will not only clarify its contribution to cancer progression but also support the development of therapeutic strategies that target autophagy through epi-transcriptomic regulation.

m6A regulators and autophagy-related genes in different diseases

In apoptosis, m6A exerts context-dependent dual roles, either promoting or suppressing cell death depending on the transcript involved (Table 6). For example, METTL3-mediated methylation can enhance the decay of pro-survival transcripts, thereby promoting apoptosis in certain tumors whereas FTO stabilizes anti-apoptotic mRNAs and confers resistance to therapy. In pancreatic cancer, depletion of METTL3 increases sensitivity to chemo- and radiotherapy, with apoptosis linked to downregulation of METTL3 [107]. Similarly, pharmacological inhibition of METTL3 with STM2457 reduces acute myeloid leukemia (AML) growth and enhances apoptosis, highlighting m6A as a promising therapeutic target [108]. The functional outcomes of m6A in apoptosis are mediated by reader proteins such as YTHDC1 and members of the IGF2BPs family, which bind to m6A-modified transcripts and modulate their stability or translation. In bladder cancer, reduced YTHDC1 expression downregulates phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) in an m6A-dependent manner, facilitating apoptosis evasion [116]. By contrast, elevated YTHDC2 expression in papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) promotes apoptosis through upregulation of pro-apoptotic factors such as cleaved caspase-3 and BAX and suppression of the anti-apoptotic protein B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) [117] (Table 7).

Necroptosis is a form of programmed necrosis mediated by receptor-interacting protein kinases receptor-interacting serine/threonine-protein kinase 1 (RIPK1) and receptor-interacting serine/threonine-protein kinase 3 (RIPK3), as well as mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL) [121, 122]. It results in plasma membrane rupture and the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), thereby eliciting strong inflammatory responses [123]. However, the downstream targets and context-specific functions of m6A remain incompletely defined, with most findings limited to specific disease models (Table 7). The temporal dynamics and cooperation between m6A readers and other RNA-binding proteins also warrant further investigation. Future studies should aim to map precise m6A sites and delineate causal mechanisms, which may reveal therapeutic strategies to either restore necroptosis in apoptosis-resistant cancer.

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent, non-apoptotic form of regulated cell death characterized by excessive lipid peroxidation and disruption of redox homeostasis [126–128]. In ferroptosis, m6A regulates iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation by targeting mRNAs such as solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), and ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1) (Table 8). METTL3 and METTL14 regulate ferroptosis through distinct downstream pathways, while the demethylase FTO frequently suppresses ferroptosis by stabilizing antioxidant transcripts [140]. In colorectal cancer (CRC), FTO enhances tumorigenesis by promoting m6A-YTHDF2-mediated upregulation of SLC7A11 and GPX4, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis. Pharmacological inhibition of FTO with compounds such as Mupirocin induces ferroptosis and suppresses CRC growth, suggesting a novel therapeutic strategy [141]. Reader proteins, including YTHDC1 and YTHDF2, also modulate ferroptosis by regulating transcript stability, turnover, or translation [137, 139, 142]. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), suppression of Dual Specificity Phosphatase 4 (DUSP4) enhances ferroptosis sensitivity by influencing Ferritin Light Chain (FTL)/Ferritin Heavy Chain 1 (FTH1) expression through YTHDC1-mediated mRNA nuclear export. Cuproptosis is a recently identified form of regulated cell death triggered by excess intracellular copper, which directly binds to lipoylated components of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, leading to proteotoxic stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. In cuproptosis, METTL3 and METTL16 interact with ferredoxin 1 (FDX1), dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT) and other copper-related transcripts (Table 9). In esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), METTL3 expression is markedly elevated and has been reported to regulate cuproptosis-related genes such as DLAT and Lipoic Acid Synthetase (LIAS), suggesting a novel therapeutic strategy for targeting ESCA [124]. In gastric cancer (GC), copper promotes METTL16 lactylation, which enhances its expression and increases m6A deposition on FDX1 mRNA, thereby linking RNA methylation to cuproptosis regulation [125]. In addition to the well-established cell death pathways, several non-canonical regulatory mechanisms have recently been identified. Given that m6A modification serves as a rapid stress-response factor, it is highly likely to orchestrate the cellular defense against the oxidative triggers. Oxeiptosis is induced by reactive oxygen species (ROS), occurring independently of caspases and mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization [143]. Although the role of m6A in oxeiptosis remains largely unexplored, oxidative stress has been shown to alter m6A dynamics in several models[144, 145]. Necrosis by sodium overload (NECSO) is triggered by persistent Na⁺ influx, which leads to osmotic stress and plasma membrane rupture [146]. While direct evidence linking m6A to NECSO is lacking, m6A-dependent regulation of transcripts involved in ion transport, osmotic sensing, and energy metabolism may modulate cellular susceptibility to sodium-induced necrosis [147, 148].

Overall, the role of m6A in emerging death modalities such as oxeiptosis and NECSO remains incompletely understood. Key challenges include defining its temporal and spatial regulation under stress, distinguishing direct from indirect targets, and clarifying its context-specific effects. Future studies integrating multi-omic approaches with disease models will be essential to uncover whether targeting m6A can provide therapeutic leverage in these newly described cell death pathways.

Other RNA methylation-mediated regulation of cell death in cancer

m1A has garnered increasing attention for its emerging roles in regulating cell fate under stress conditions. In HCC, differences in m1A modification patterns correlate with distinct autophagy clusters, suggesting that m1A may enhance autophagy, inhibit tumor proliferation, and serve as a prognostic biomarker [149]. m1A demethylase ALKBH3 facilitates the cleavage of tRNA to generate specific tDRs, thereby inhibiting apoptosis in multiple cancer lines [150]. Overexpression of ALKBH3 in cardiomyocytes enhanced ZBED6 translation, thereby suppressing PANoptosis [151]. TRMT6/TRMT61A was found to promote ferroptosis in graphene-induced toxic injury [152]. Collectively, current evidence suggests that m1A modification may may influence cellular adaptation to stress through post-transcriptional regulation of RNA metabolism and translation, although the precise downstream mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

The m5C modification also plays important roles in cell death pathways (Fig. 4). In recurrent implantation failure, NSUN2 overexpression elevates m5C levels and promotes autophagy [153]. In triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), spermidine SAT1 enhances YBX1-mediated m5C modification and stabilizes mTOR mRNA, thereby suppressing autophagy and driving tumor progression [73]. Conversely, NSUN2-mediated m5C modification of three prime repair exonuclease 2 (TREX2) promotes apoptosis in tumor cells [76]. In cervical cancer, NSUN2 stabilizes leucine rich repeat containing 8 A (LRRC8A) mRNA via m5C modification to inhibit apoptosis [154]. Similarly, overexpression of DNMT1 and NSUN2 in response to cisplatin or doxorubicin downregulates anti-apoptotic genes, thereby reducing resistance to apoptosis [155]. In NSCLC, elevated NSUN2 expression increases m5C modification of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) mRNA, conferring resistance to ferroptosis [156]. In endometrial cancer, NSUN2 overexpression promotes tumor progression, while its knockdown enhances sensitivity to ferroptosis by reducing m5C modification of SLC7A11 mRNA [157]. Additionally, depletion of METTL17 has been shown to induce ferroptosis by increasing intracellular and mitochondrial lipid peroxidation and ROS levels [158]. Elevated NSUN5 in cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) stabilized GLS transcript, thus inhibiting cuproptosis [159].

Taken together, these findings suggest that non-canonical RNA methylations such as m1A, m5C, and m7G represent emerging layers of epi-transcriptomic control in cell death, with context-dependent effects that warrant deeper investigation as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets. It is worth noting that while m6A, m5C, m1A, and m7G collectively contribute to the complexity of the epi-transcriptomic landscape, their functions may not be entirely independent. Instead, these modifications may converge on shared biological processes, particularly cellular stress responses, to coordinately influence cellular sensitivity to death signals. However, there is currently a lack of evidence for a convergent pathway where these diverse modifications directly crosstalk to regulate a single cell death event. Future research is needed to determine how different RNA modifications interact within broader regulatory networks and whether they function cooperatively under specific cellular contexts.

Clinical applications of RNA methylation in cell death

RNA methylation has emerged as a promising avenue for clinical translation, with potential applications as both diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets in cancer. Altered expression of methylation-related enzymes is frequently associated with tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapy resistance, suggesting that RNA methylation signatures may serve as indicators of cancer stage, prognosis, and treatment response. Beyond prognostic value, pharmacological modulation of these epi-transcriptomic modulators has shown potential to restore sensitivity to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and ICB by reactivating cell death pathways.

m6A modifications are the most extensively explored. Small-molecule inhibitors of m6A regulators, such as STM2457 targeting METTL3, have demonstrated antitumor efficacy in preclinical cancer models. In osteoarthritis (OA), METTL3-mediated destabilization of ATG7 mRNA reduces autophagy, whereas inhibition of METTL3 alleviates cartilage damage by disrupting the senescence-autophagy-SASP axis [96]. In small cell lung cancer (SCLC), METTL3 regulates DCP2-Pink1-Parkin-mediated mitophagy to promote chemotherapy resistance and STM2457 can reverse this resistance [162]. In CRC, METTL3 regulates the m6A-BHLHE41-CXCL1 axis, overcoming immune suppression and synergizing with anti-PD-1 therapy [163]. Other regulators, such as FTO, suppress ferroptosis and promote therapy resistance through m6A-dependent stabilization of SLC7A11 and GPX4 transcripts; inhibitors such as CS1/2 and FB23-2 restore ferroptosis sensitivity [133, 141, 164]. Reader proteins including YTHDFs and IGF2BPs are also being evaluated as prognostic biomarkers, as their expression correlates with stabilization of oncogenic or anti-death transcripts. Collectively, these findings highlight m6A regulators as attractive therapeutic targets and predictive markers of ICB outcomes, particularly via modulation of apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, and immune infiltration [165–167].

m5C modifications also play complex roles in oncogenesis and immune evasion. For example, NSUN2-mediated m5C of TREX2 activates the cGAS-STING pathway, promoting antitumor immunity [76, 168]. Targeting NSUN2 in a context-specific manner could fine-tune immunogenic cell death. DNA methyltransferase inhibitors, including Decitabine (DAC), 5-Azacytidine (AZA), Zebularine, and RG108, indirectly impact RNA methylation and cell death. DAC, for instance, restores chemosensitivity in bortezomib-resistant cells by enhancing apoptosis [169] and upregulates immune-related and chemokine genes, potentially converting “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors and improving ICB responses [170]. Furthermore, expression of m5C regulators correlates with immune cell infiltration, suggesting their potential as biomarkers for immunotherapy stratification.

m7G modifications are increasingly linked to therapy resistance. Upregulation of METTL1/WDR4 in HCC suppresses autophagy and confers resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as Lenvatinib [171–173]. In osteosarcoma, however, reduced METTL1 enhances chemosensitivity by suppressing FTH1 translation and promoting ferroptosis [160]. While no specific inhibitors of m7G regulators are yet available, therapeutic suppression of m7G-mediated translation represents an attractive future strategy.

m1A modifications have also been implicated in cancer progression and therapy resistance. For example, TRMT6/61A-driven m1A methylation stabilizes oncogenic transcripts and promotes bladder cancer growth [55]. The m1A demethylase ALKBH3 has been associated with tumor glycolysis and survival. Therefore, inhibiting ALKBH3 may restore apoptosis and enhance chemosensitivity [45, 174].

Given the emerging role of RNA modifications as potential biomarkers, the development of reliable detection technologies is critical for their clinical implementation (Fig. 6). Recent technological advances have accelerated the study of RNA methylation in clinical contexts. Methods such as MeRIP-seq, LC-MS/MS, and nanopore direct RNA sequencing enable quantitative and high-throughput profiling of RNA modifications [175–177]. Incorporating these approaches into liquid biopsy platforms could allow non-invasive monitoring of tumor evolution and therapeutic response [178].

Despite these advances, significant challenges remain for clinical translation, including tissue-specific regulation, mechanistic complexity, and the lack of selective small-molecule inhibitors or editing tools. Moving forward, highly sensitive detection technologies, modification-specific enzymatic profiling, and disease-relevant functional models will be essential. Ultimately, harnessing RNA methylation for precision medicine will require integrated strategies that combine epi-transcriptomic modulation with conventional and immunotherapeutic approaches.

Conclusion, challenges, and outlook

RNA methylation has emerged as a central layer of epi-transcriptomic regulation that intricately connects RNA metabolism with cell fate decisions. Unlike classical genetic or protein-based regulation, RNA modifications provide cells with a rapid and reversible mechanism to adapt to stress, fine-tune survival pathways, or commit to regulated death. This flexibility underscores why m6A, m1A, m5C, and m7G have been repeatedly linked not only to cell death but also to therapy resistance, immune evasion, and metabolic plasticity in diverse cancer contexts.

Yet, the field faces important challenges. First, the context-dependency of RNA methylation complicates interpretation: the same regulator may promote survival in one cancer but trigger death in another. Second, novel sequencing and profiling approaches are able to capture global patterns, but technical limitations often miss the temporal, spatial, and single-cell resolution which are needed to map modifications during dynamic stress responses. Third, many enzymes act on multiple RNA substrates, raising questions about substrate specificity and redundancy, while crosstalk among RNA modifications and with other epigenetic layers remains incompletely understood. Importantly, this lack of substrate specificity also raises concerns regarding potential off-target effects when therapeutically targeting RNA methylation regulators. Finally, the therapeutic landscape is still in its infancy. While inhibitors of METTL3, FTO, and DNMTs show preclinical promise, selective and clinically applicable tools for other regulators are largely lacking. Moreover, the heterogeneity of RNA methylation landscapes across tumor types and patients suggests that therapeutic responses are unlikely to be uniform, highlighting the need for robust patient stratification strategies. In addition, selective and clinically applicable tools for many RNA methylation regulators remain lacking.

Looking ahead, several directions stand out. Mechanistically, the field must move beyond correlative studies to causal dissection of how RNA methylation governs death pathways, especially in emerging modalities like oxeiptosis and NECSO. Technologically, single-cell and spatial epi-transcriptomic approaches will be essential to resolve the heterogeneity of RNA methylation across tissues and microenvironments. Therapeutically, integration of RNA methylation modulators with conventional chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy holds promise, particularly for overcoming resistance driven by disrupted cell death programs. Moreover, liquid biopsy-based detection of RNA modification signatures could enable real-time monitoring of cancer progression and treatment response.

In conclusion, RNA methylation represents both a fundamental regulator of cell death and a fertile ground for translational innovation. However, challenges related to specificity, safety, and patient selection must be carefully addressed before these strategies can be broadly applied. Addressing the mechanistic, technological, and therapeutic challenges ahead will be key to converting epi-transcriptomic insights into precision strategies for cancer, inflammation, neurodegeneration, and beyond.
