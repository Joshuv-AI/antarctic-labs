From Promises to Totality: A Framework for Ruling Out Quantum Speedups
Thomas Huffstutler ∗ Upendra Kapshikar † David Miloschewsky ∗ Supartha Podder ∗ ∗ Stony Brook University, USA † University of Ottawa, Canada ∗ {thuffstutler, dmiloschewsk, supartha}@cs.stonybrook.edu † ukapshik@uottawa.ca

Abstract
We study when partial Boolean functions can (and cannot) exhibit superpolynomial quantum query speedups, and develop a general framework for ruling out such speedups via two complementary lenses: promise-aware complexity measures and function completions . First, we introduce promise versions of standard combinatorial measures (including block sensitivity and related variants) and prove that if the relevant promise and completion measures “collapse”, then deterministic and quantum query complexities are necessarily polynomially related, i.e., D ⁡ ( f ) = poly ⁡ ( Q ⁡ ( f ) ) D(f)=\mathrm{poly}(Q(f)) . We then analyze structured families of promises, including symmetric partial functions and promises supported on Hamming slices, obtaining sharp (up to polynomial factors) characterizations in terms of a single gap parameter for the symmetric case and refined slice-dependent bounds for k k -slice domains. Next, we formalize completion complexity as the minimum of a measure over total completions of a partial function, and show that completability of a measure captures the possibility of superpolynomial quantum speedups. Finally, we apply this viewpoint to derive broad non-speedup criteria for some classes of functions admitting well-behaved completions, such as functions with low maximum influence on both the standard and p p -biased hypercubes and functions with efficiently identifiable domains, and then show some hardness results for general completion techniques.

Introduction

One of the central questions in quantum complexity theory is identifying problems which exhibit superpolynomial quantum speedups.
While polynomial quantum speedups are relatively common and can be constructed for many problems, finding exponential quantum speedups is significantly more challenging.
When it comes to showing exponential quantum speedups and/or designing quantum algorithms, the query complexity model is often the preferred choice, as it is simple enough to facilitate proving lower bounds, yet expressive enough to capture the varying levels of problem difficulty.
In this model, query access is given to an unknown
n

f
on

as the response. The goal is to minimize the number of queries necessary to compute

in the worst case. In the randomized setting, algorithms are allowed to output incorrect answers with inverse-polynomial (in the input size) probabilities. When we consider quantum algorithms in this model, we allow the algorithms to make queries in superposition, thus rendering the question of how much advantage quantum algorithms provide over classical algorithms in this setting.

Over the years, only a handful of structured problems—such as period-finding in Shor’s algorithm or Simon’s problem

1.2
Our techniques

Promise measures

To obtain the requirements for speedup using the two variations of promise measures, we follow the steps of the proof that for total functions
f
,
\mathrm{D}(f)=_{\mathrm{poly}}\mathrm{Q}(f)
. For a survey of the proofs, see
[
Bd02
]
. First, we define the alternative promise versions of
\mathrm{bs}(f)
and
\mathrm{C}(f)
,
\mathrm{bs}^{\prime}(f)
and
\mathrm{C}^{\prime}(f)
. As
\mathrm{bs}^{\prime}(f)
was already described alongside Result
1.1
, let us describe
\mathrm{C}^{\prime}(f)
. Standardly, a certificate

for a promise function certifies that each input in the promise which agrees with
c
has the same output. The alternative
\mathrm{C}^{\prime}(f)
is defined such that no input which agrees with
c
is outside of
\textup{Dom}\!\left(f\right)
.

Transferring the relationships from the total to our promise versions of the measures relies on the role of inputs outside of the promise. Specifically, many steps use the assumption that changing the input, for example by flipping multiple indices, keeps it in the promise, which is not necessarily true. For example, consider the proof that the size of a minimal block is bounded by sensitivity. If we attempt to show a promise-version of such a statement, meaning that the size of a minimal block
B
such that
x,x^{B}\in\textup{Dom}(f)
is bounded by its sensitivity, we find that removing an index
i
from
B
might take it out of the promise, meaning
x^{B\backslash\{i\}}\not\in\textup{Dom}(f)
and therefore
i
might not be a sensitive index. This is underscored by the fact that there are partial functions for which
\mathrm{s}(f)=0
, such as functions on the slice. On the other hand, using the same argument, we bound the size of a block which certifies that takes an output outside of the promise or changes its output by
\mathrm{s}^{\prime}(f)
(
\mathrm{bs}^{\prime}(f)
restricted to blocks of size
1

Efficiently identifying domains is sufficient

When trying to think of explicit constructions for a completion, the naïve idea is to just set all inputs not in the promise of a partial function to have an output of

\epsilon-

for

Characterizing the natural completion

A natural completion one can think of is based on the sign of an approximating polynomial. If we are given a partial function

We would like to characterize the notion of this “natural” completion more precisely and give a condition which captures when we can guarantee that

1.3
Discussion and further work

In this work we introduced two new frameworks for proving non-superpolynomial quantum speedups and analyzed the relationship between partial functions and
S_{n}
. Furthermore, we showed several applications of the completion technique. We also introduced a new technique using the smoothness of an approximating polynomial to prove that no superpolynomial speedup exists. We list some open problems that would further the usefulness of our techniques.

•

Completion complexity characterizes the possibility of superpolynomial speedups in comparison to deterministic query complexity. What is the analogous characterization when comparing quantum to randomized query complexity?

•

Since the completion of approximate degree captures the gap between deterministic and approximate degree – and consequently, between deterministic and quantum complexity – but not all functions exhibit quantum speedups, it follows that not all polynomials are extendable. What intrinsic properties of a polynomial determine whether it admits such a completion?

•

For what classes of functions is a low degree approximation of the indicator function achievable? An exact characterization would quantify precisely when the naïve completion is sufficient to prove no superpolynomial speedup.

•

Theorem
33
directly implies that partial functions on domains with a large promise (i.e., low number of undefined inputs) do not attain superpolynomial speedup. Can one define a measure of
connectedness
on the Boolean hypercube and show that on highly-connected domains one cannot obtain superpolynomial speedup?

•

Are there more fine-grained versions of the promise measures discussed in
Section

3
which allow us to show the non-existence of a superpolynomial speedup?

is labeled by a variable
x_{v}
, and each leaf
\ell
is labeled by a bit
b_{\ell}\in\{0,1\}
. The computation on input
x
proceeds as follows: It starts at the root of
\mathrm{T}
and follows a path based on queries. At each internal node
v
, the variable
x_{v}
is queried, and the computation moves to the left child if
x_{v}=0
and to the right child if
x_{v}=1
. When a leaf
\ell
is reached, the output is
\mathrm{T}(x):=b_{\ell}
, and the process terminates. The deterministic decision tree complexity of
f
, denoted
\mathrm{D}(f)
, is the depth of the shallowest decision tree computing
f
, equivalently stated as the worst-case number of queries made by the optimal decision tree.

Randomized Query Complexity.
A randomized decision tree
\mathcal{T}
is a probability distribution over deterministic decision trees
\mathrm{T}
. It is said to compute a Boolean function
f
if, for every valid input
x
,

\Pr_{\mathrm{T}\sim\mathcal{T}}[\mathrm{T}(x)\neq f(x)]\leq 1/3.

The randomized decision tree complexity of
f
, denoted
\mathrm{R}(f)
, is the minimum depth
d
for which there exists a randomized decision tree
\mathcal{T}
supported on deterministic trees of depth at most
d
that satisfies this condition.
Although the error bound of
1/3
may seem arbitrary, it can be replaced by any constant strictly smaller than
1/2
without affecting the complexity measure by more than a constant factor.

Quantum Query Complexity.
A quantum oracle for an input
x
is a unitary transformation
\mathrm{O}:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{m}
on an
m
-dimensional complex space, where
m\geq\lceil\log n\rceil+1
. It acts on basis states of the form
|i,b,z\rangle
, where
i
(an index in
{1,\dots,n}
) takes
\lceil\log n\rceil
bits,
b
takes 1 bit, and
z
is the remaining workspace. The oracle applies the transformation:

\mathrm{O}|i,b,z\rangle=|i,b\oplus x_{i},z\rangle.

By linearity, this defines
\mathrm{O}
on all of
\mathbb{C}^{m}
.

A quantum query algorithm with
q
queries is a unitary transformation

\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{U}_{q}\mathrm{O}\mathsf{U}_{q-1}\mathrm{O}\dots\mathrm{O}\mathsf{U}_{1}\mathrm{O}\mathsf{U}_{0},

where each
\mathsf{U}_{i}
is a fixed unitary independent of
x
. The algorithm’s output is determined by measuring the first qubit of
\mathsf{A}|0^{m}\rangle
.

The algorithm computes
f
with bounded error if, for every
x
, its output equals
f(x)
with probability at least
2/3
. The bounded-error quantum query complexity of
f
, denoted
\mathrm{Q}(f)
, is the minimum number of queries
q
needed for such an algorithm.

For an overview of key results and open problems in query complexity, see
[
Aar21
,
Amb18
,
BDW02
]
.

2.2
Definition of measures for partial functions

Due to the nature of partial functions, which are undefined on certain inputs, one must be careful when defining complexity measures.
Indeed, when it comes to partial functions, even for standard combinatorial functions, there is no
canonical choice
of complexity measure, and there are often multiple definitions in the literature
[
CGG+25
,
ABDK21b
]
.
Ideally, we would want these measures to be
meaningful
and to be related to some algorithmic measures, just like in the case of total functions.
For example, some combinatorial measures, such as approximate degree, adversary bound etc., of total functions lower bound quantum query complexity
\mathrm{Q}(f)
.
Therefore, a useful definition of these measures in the case of partial functions should also lower bound
\mathrm{Q}(f)
.
Here, we state the definitions we will use throughout the paper, and show that they satisfy the requisite properties. Note that various measures for partial functions were already considered in the literature; see
[
ABDK21b
,
CGG+25
,
BT22
]
for reference.

Definition 7

(Degree)
.

For a
(possibly partial)
Boolean function
f
, its degree is defined as the minimum degree of a real polynomial
p
that satisfies

p(x)=f(x)\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathrm{Dom}(f),

as well as

p(x)\in[0,1]\quad\text{for all }x\in\{0,1\}^{n}.

If
f
is a total function, then there is a unique polynomial
p
satisfying the above conditions.
We can similarly define approximate degree.

Definition 8

(Approximate degree)
.

For a (possibly partial) Boolean function
f
, its approximate degree measure, denoted by
\widetilde{\mathrm{deg}}_{\epsilon}(f)
, is the minimum degree of a real polynomial
p
that approximates
f
in the sense that

|p(x)-f(x)|\leq\epsilon\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathrm{Dom}(f).

Additionally,
p(x)
must satisfy

p(x)\in[0,1]\quad\text{for all }x\in\{0,1\}^{n}.

As it is common in the literature, we will use the shorthand
\widetilde{\mathrm{deg}}
to denote
\widetilde{\mathrm{deg}}_{\frac{1}{3}}
.

In a seminal result,
[
BBC+01
]
showed that one can lower bound quantum query complexity by the approximate degree, which leads to the following chain of inequalities.

Lemma 9

(
[
BBC+01
]
)
.

For every Boolean function
f
, we have,
\mathrm{D}(f)\geq\mathrm{R}(f)\geq\mathrm{Q}(f)\geq\Omega\left({\widetilde{\mathrm{deg}}(f)}\right)
.

The above results were initially proved for total functions, but as mentioned in
[
ABDK21b
]
, one can define these measures for partial functions, and the results also hold for them.

Definition 10

(Partial assignment)

A partial assignment

\alpha

or
\alpha_{i}=*

Support

Definition 11

(Certificate,

(Block notation)

2.3
Fourier analysis

We review some basic Fourier analysis. Refer
[
O’D21
]
for a more detailed treatment of the subject. We note that
S\ni i

that contain the index

Definition 20

(Discrete derivative)
.

Given a function
f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we define the discrete derivative of
f
for all

as

\displaystyle D_{i}f(x)=\frac{f(x)-f(x^{i})}{2}.

Definition 21

(Influence)
.

Given a function
f(x):\{-1,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we define the influence of the bit
i\in[n]
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Inf}_{i}[f]=\sum_{S\ni i}\hat{f}(S)^{2}.

Definition 22

(
p
-biased hypercube)
.

Let
\pi_{p}
denote the distribution over
\{0,1\}
with
\pi_{p}(0)=p
.
With slight abuse of notation, we extend the notation to
x\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
:
\pi_{p}(x)=\prod\limits_{i\in[n]}\pi_{p}(x_{i})
.
We will denote this distribution as
\{-1,1\}^{n}_{p}
.

Definition 23
.

Let
p\in[0,1]
.
Define the function
\phi:\{-1,1\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\phi(x_{i})=\frac{x_{i}-\mu}{\sigma}

where,

\mu=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{x_{i}\sim\pi_{p}}[x_{i}]=1-2p\quad\text{ and }\hskip 8.5359pt\sigma=\mathop{\mathrm{stddev}}\limits_{x_{i}\sim\pi_{p}}[x_{i}]=2\sqrt{p(1-p)}.

Definition 24

(Fourier basis for the
p
-biased hypercube)
.

We define the Fourier basis for functions on the
p
-biased hypercube as
\{\phi_{S}\}_{S\subseteq[n]}
where

\phi_{S}(x)=\prod_{i\in S}\phi(x_{i}).

The Fourier coefficients are defined as

\hat{f}(S)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{x\sim\pi_{p}^{n}}[f(x)\phi_{S}(x)]

and so we have the biased Fourier expansion

f(x)=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\hat{f}(S)\phi_{S}(x).

We also note the following property of smooth functions.

Definition 25

x
and

A function

where

Lemma 29

For any partial function

0
.

3.

Query the indices in
c_{i}
. If they agree with
c_{i}
, output
1
. Otherwise, add all the queried indices and their results to
p
.

Suppose the algorithm did not terminate. As will be shown below, for all
x,y\in\textup{Dom}(f)
which agree with
p
,
f(x)=f(y)
. Therefore, based on
p
, we may output
f(x)
(this may be hard-coded into the algorithm).

Let us explain the correctness of the algorithm. If we terminate during the
b
rounds, we output the correct result. To show that after
b
rounds all inputs which agree with
p
have the same output, assume the statement is false and there are two inputs
x,y\in\textup{Dom}(f)
which agree with
p
and
f(x)\neq f(y)
. Without loss of generality, assume
f(y)=0
. Let
B_{b+1}
be the block such that
x^{B_{b+1}}=y
. For each
c_{i}
, we may define a corresponding block
B_{i}
as the set of indices on which
y
and
c_{i}
disagree. Each
B_{i}
is non-empty, disjoint from all other
B_{j}
, and
f(y^{B_{i}})\in\{1,*\}
, meaning that
B_{1},\dots B_{b+1}
form a valid set of blocks. However, this would contradict the assumption that the block sensitivity
\mathrm{bs}^{\prime}(b)=b
. Thus, the algorithm must output the correct value. Items
2
and
3
follow analogously.
∎

Alternatively, we may bound
\mathrm{D}(f)
using
\mathrm{C}(f)
and
\mathrm{C}^{\prime}(f)
.

Lemma 31
.

For any partial function
f:\textup{Dom}(f)\rightarrow\{0,1\}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}(f)\leq\min\{\mathrm{C}^{1}(f)\mathrm{C}^{\{0,*\}}(f),\mathrm{C}^{0}(f)\mathrm{C}^{\{1,*\}}(f)\}

Proof.

Let us prove
\mathrm{D}(f)\leq\mathrm{C}^{1}(f)\mathrm{C}^{\{0,*\}}(f)
, the rest of the statement follows by symmetry. Fix some
x\in\textup{Dom}(f)
. Let us describe a deterministic algorithm which outputs
f(x)
using at most
\mathrm{C}^{1}(f)\mathrm{C}^{\{0,*\}}(f)
queries.

Let
p
be a partial assignment which is initially empty which records all bits of
x
queried so far. Additionally, assume there exists a fixed ordering of all
1
-certificates of
f
. Repeat the following for
\mathrm{C}^{\{0,*\}}(f)
rounds:

1.

If
p
contains a
\{0,*\}
-certificate, output
0
.

2.

Otherwise, choose the first
1
-certificate
c
consistent with
p
, query its indices and add them to
p
.

3.

If the updated
p
agrees with
c
, output
1
.

If the loop does not terminate early, output
0
.

Let us analyze the algorithm. Since the size of the certificate
c
queried each round is bounded by
\mathrm{C}^{1}(f)
, the query complexity is bounded by
\mathrm{C}^{1}(f)\mathrm{C}^{\{0,*\}}(f)
. Let us argue for correctness. If we terminate in Step 1, we are correct as since
x\in\textup{Dom}(f)
, a
\{0,*\}
-certificate implies that
f(x)=0
. On the other hand, if we continue to Step 2, we are guaranteed to find some
1
-certificate
c
which agrees with
p
as otherwise, we could create a
\{0,*\}
-certificate. Of course, if
p
agrees with
c
,
f(x)=1
and we output the correct value.

Lastly, let us show that if the loop terminates without an output, then
f(x)=0
. Let
c_{0}
be the minimum
\{0,*\}
-certificate for
x
, meaning that
\lvert c_{0}\rvert\leq\mathrm{C}^{\{0,*\}}(f)
. Additionally, notice that for each
c
in Step 2, there must exist at least
1
certificate on which
c
and
c_{0}
disagree as both cannot be true at the same time. Since
c_{0}
was not triggered in Step 1 and
c
can never be triggered in step 2, we must have queried this index. Therefore, at each round of the algorithm, we must query at least one index in
c_{0}
. Therefore, after
\mathrm{C}^{\{0,*\}}(f)
, we must have queried all of its indices and thus are in a
\{0,*\}
-case.
∎

Next, we find that
\mathrm{cbs}(f)
sits between
\mathrm{bs}(f)
and
\mathrm{bs}^{\prime}(f)
.

Lemma 32
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{bs}(f)\leq\mathrm{cbs}(f)\leq\mathrm{bs}^{\prime}(f)

Proof.

By definition, the completion of
f
must have more sensitive blocks than
f
itself, so
\mathrm{bs}(f)\leq\mathrm{cbs}(f)
. On the other hand, consider the completion
f^{\prime}
of
f
which achieves block sensitivity
c=\mathrm{cbs}(f)
and let
B_{1},\dots B_{c}
be its blocks. Then for that input
x
, each block either points to an input in the domain or not. Regardless, each block may be used as a block for
\mathrm{bs}^{\prime}(f)
. Thus,
\mathrm{cbs}(f)\leq\mathrm{bs}^{\prime}(f)
.
∎

Lastly, we prove a statement which shows how a polynomial relationship between various measures discussed in this section implies the impossibility of a quantum speedup.

Theorem 33
.

Let
f
be a partial function such that
at least
one of the following conditions is satisfied:

1.

\mathrm{cbs}(f)=_{\mathrm{poly}}\mathrm{bs}^{\prime}(f)

2.

\mathrm{cbs}(f)=_{\mathrm{poly}}\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{cri}}(f)

3.

\min\{\mathrm{C}^{1}(f),\mathrm{C}^{0}(f)\}\leq_{\mathrm{poly}}\mathrm{cbs}(f)
.

4.

\mathrm{C}(f)=_{\mathrm{poly}}\mathrm{C}^{\prime}(f)
and
\mathrm{bs}(f)=_{\mathrm{poly}}\mathrm{s}^{\prime}(f)
.

Then
\mathrm{D}(f)=_{\mathrm{poly}}\mathrm{Q}(f)
.

Proof.

Let us begin with Item
1
. Let
a
be some constant such that
\mathrm{bs}^{\prime}(f)\leq\mathrm{cbs}(f)^{a}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}(f)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{C}(f)\mathrm{bs}^{\prime}(f)

(from
Item

1
in Lemma
3
)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{bs}(f)\mathrm{s}^{\prime}(f)\mathrm{bs}^{\prime}(f)

(from Lemma
3
)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{bs}^{\prime}(f)^{3}

(from
Section

2.2
)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{cbs}(f)^{3a}

\displaystyle=O(\mathrm{Q}(f)^{6a})

(from
Section

2.2
)

This completes the proof that Item
1
is sufficient to ensure that
\mathrm{D}(f)=_{\mathrm{poly}}\mathrm{Q}(f)
.
The proof that
Items
2
and
3
are also sufficient follows similarly (due to Items
3
and
2
in Lemma
3
and
Section

2.2
, respectively).

Item
4
. Let
b
be the smallest constant such that
\mathrm{bs}(f)\leq\mathrm{s}^{\prime}(f)^{b}
and
\mathrm{s}^{\prime}(f)\leq\mathrm{bs}(f)^{b}
, while
c
is the smallest constant such that
\mathrm{C}^{\prime}(f)\leq\mathrm{C}(f)^{c}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}(f)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{C}(f)\mathrm{C}^{\prime}(f)

(from Lemma
3
)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{C}(f)^{c+1}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{bs}(f)^{(b+1)(c+1)}

(from Lemma
3
)

\displaystyle=O(\mathrm{Q}(f)^{8bc})

(from
Section

2.2
)

Theorem
33
implies that several structured domains cannot attain a speedup. For example, Item
1
implies that
\mathrm{bs}(f)=_{\mathrm{poly}}\mathrm{bs}^{\prime}(f)
leads to no superpolynomial quantum speedups.
It also implies that for a domain to attain speedup, there must exist at least one input
x
with many disjoint blocks
B
such that
x^{B}\not\in\textup{Dom}(f)
. Alternatively, if the graph induced by adjacent inputs in the domain is “well-connected” such that the degree of each node is closer to
n
than the block sensitivity of the function, it cannot attain speedup.

Item
2
of Theorem
33
is in line with
Section

5.1
where the main argument is that a completion of a partial function which does not blow-up a parameter leads to a polynomial relationship between
\mathrm{D}(f)
and
\mathrm{Q}(f)
. Let us consider an example of a domain where Item
2
applies. Fix some input
x^{*}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and consider the partial Boolean function
f
where
\textup{Dom}(f)=d(x^{*})=\{x:\forall i\in[n],x_{i}\leq x^{*}_{i}\}
. On this domain, we may define a completion
g
which achieves the minimal critical block sensitivity,
\max_{x\in\textup{Dom}(f)}\mathrm{bs}(g,x)=\mathrm{cbs}(f)=\mathrm{bs}(f)
, and does not increase the certificate complexity,
\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{cri}}(f)=\mathrm{C}(f)
. Specifically, let
g(x)=f(x\land x^{*})
where
(x\land x^{*})_{i}=x_{i}\land x^{*}_{i}
. Using this completion, all minimal sensitive blocks and certificates only rely on the indices such that
x_{i}^{*}=1
. Furthermore, this result can be improved to the case where the domain is the union
\cup_{i}d(x^{*}_{i})
as long as
\max_{i,j}\lvert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rvert
is constant. This is due to the fact that the indices on which the different inputs
x^{*}_{i}
differ may be queried in order to decide which subdomain the input is in.

Interestingly, the conclusion of Theorem
33
does not follow from
\mathrm{C}(f)=_{\mathrm{poly}}\mathrm{C}^{\prime}(f)
, meaning the second condition in Item
4
is necessary. This is due to the Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm which solves the problem of whether some input
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is constant (i.e.,
\lvert x\rvert=0
or
n
) or
\lvert x\rvert=n/2

[
DJ92
]
. In this case, all three of
\mathrm{D}(f),\mathrm{C}(f)
and
\mathrm{C}^{\prime}(f)
are
O(n)
, while
\mathrm{Q}(f)=1
.

4
Functions with
S_{n}
symmetry

In this section, we look at functions that have some invariance under the symmetric group
S_{n}
.
In the first part, we consider functions having the highest order of
S_{n}
symmetry (Definition
4.1
).
In the second part, we will consider a much broader class of functions, those defined on a single orbit of
S_{n}
, also known as the Boolean slice.
However, in this case, the function may be arbitrarily defined on the slice.

4.1
Symmetric functions

In this part, we will study functions that are symmetric as defined below.

Definition 34

(Symmetric function)
.

Let
\textup{Dom}(f)\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
.
We say that a function
f:\textup{Dom}(f)\rightarrow\{0,1\}
is symmetric under
S_{n}
(or simply symmetric) if for all
\sigma\in S_{n}
and for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
:

1.

x\in\textup{Dom}(f)
if and only if
\sigma(x)\in\textup{Dom}(f)
.

2.

f(x)=f(\sigma(x))
.

Note that for a symmetric function
f
, its evaluation at a point
x
depends only on
\mathrm{wt}(x)
.

Our main focus will be on characterizing various measures such as
\mathrm{D}
and
\mathrm{Q}
for partial symmetric functions.
This problem has already been considered for
\mathrm{R}
and
\mathrm{Q}
in
[
Cha19
]
, proving that there is no superpolynomial speedup between the two quantities. Furthermore,
[
PYY25
]
analyzed these quantities and proved that
\mathrm{Q}
can be written as a certain maximization problem, whereas our expression is free from such optimization and has a much simpler proof technique.
Specifically, we give a characterization of various measures (up-to a polynomial factor) using
\mathrm{GAP}
, a parameter for symmetric functions we define below. Additionally, we also consider the relationship between
\widetilde{\mathrm{deg}}
and
\mathrm{D}
, of which the former has been characterized for total Boolean functions by
[
Pat92
]
, and the latter is trivial; for total symmetric functions as it is
\Theta(n)
for non-constant functions
[
BDW02
]
. To the best of our knowledge, no earlier works attempt to analyze these measures for partial symmetric functions. Furthermore, our results exactly characterize when symmetric functions attain a superpolynomial separation between
\mathrm{Q}
and
\mathrm{D}
(or
\widetilde{\mathrm{deg}}
and
\mathrm{D}
).
As an added result, our proof also shows that
\widetilde{\mathrm{deg}}
and
\mathrm{Q}
are polynomially related for every such function, hence the polynomial method is tight for partial symmetric functions. Let us formally define
\mathrm{GAP}
.

Definition 35

(Gap of a symmetric function)
.

For any partial symmetric function
f:\textup{Dom}(f)\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, define
\mathrm{GAP}(f)
as

\mathrm{GAP}(f)=\min_{x,y:f(x)\neq f(y)}d_{H}(x,y)=\min_{x,y:f(x)\neq f(y)}|\mathrm{wt}(x)-\mathrm{wt}(y)|.

First, consider the following standard fact.

Fact 36
.

Let
p:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a real polynomial. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be a positive integer, and suppose that

p(x)\in[0,1]\text{ for all }x\in[0,n].

Then

\deg(p)\geq\sqrt{\frac{nc}{1+c}},\text{\hskip 28.45274pt where }c=\max_{x\in[0,n]}\left|p^{\prime}(x)\right|.

Next, let us characterize
\widetilde{\mathrm{deg}},\mathrm{Q}
and
\mathrm{R}
using
\mathrm{GAP}
.

Lemma 37
.

Let
f
be a symmetric function.
Then,
\widetilde{\mathrm{deg}}(f)=_{\mathrm{poly}}\mathrm{Q}(f)=_{\mathrm{poly}}\mathrm{R}(f)=_{\mathrm{poly}}\frac{n}{\mathrm{GAP}(f)}
.

Proof.

We will show this in two parts.
First,
\frac{n}{\mathrm{GAP}(f)}\leq_{\mathrm{poly}}\widetilde{\mathrm{deg}}(f)
.
Our proof will follow the standard symmetrization technique.
Consider
x,y
that achieve the minimization of
\mathrm{GAP}(f)
.
That is, let
x
and
y
be such that
f(x)=0
,
f(y)=1
and
|\mathrm{wt}(x)-\mathrm{wt}(y)|=\mathrm{GAP}(f)
.
Let
p(x)
be the optimal polynomial that approximates
f
.
That is,

1.

p(x)\in[0,1]
for all
x
.

2.

|p(x)-f(x)|\leq\frac{1}{3}
for all
x\in\textup{Dom}\!\left(f\right)
.

3.

\widetilde{\mathrm{deg}}(f)=\deg(p)
.

Since
f
is symmetric,
f(x^{\prime})=0
for all
x^{\prime}
such that
\mathrm{wt}(x^{\prime})=\mathrm{wt}(x)
.
Thus,
p(x^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{3}
. Similarly,
p(y^{\prime})\geq\frac{2}{3}
for all
y^{\prime}
that have the same Hamming weight as
y
.
Let
p_{0}
be the symmetrized version of
p
, then
p_{0}(\mathrm{wt}(x))\leq\frac{1}{3}
and
p_{0}(\mathrm{wt}(y))\geq\frac{2}{3}
.
Thus,
\max_{x\in[0,n]}|p_{0}^{\prime}(x)|\geq\frac{1}{3|\mathrm{wt}(x)-\mathrm{wt}(y)|}=\frac{1}{3\mathrm{GAP}(f)}
.
Using Fact
4.1
,
\deg(p_{0})\geq\sqrt{\frac{n}{3\mathrm{GAP}(f)}}
.
Thus, the standard symmetrization arguments gives us that
\widetilde{\mathrm{deg}}(f)=\deg(p)\geq\deg(p_{0})\geq\sqrt{\frac{n}{3\mathrm{GAP}(f)}}
.

For the other direction, we will give a randomized algorithm which outputs
f(x)
with query complexity
t=O\left(\frac{n^{2}}{\mathrm{GAP}(f)^{2}}\right)
. As
f(x)
depends only on the Hamming weight
\mathrm{wt}(x)
, it suffices to estimate the value of
\mathrm{wt}(x)
by taking uniformly random samples of indices of the input. Letting
\widehat{w}
denote the weight estimate, since the hamming weights of any two conflicting inputs (i.e.,
x,y\in\textup{Dom}\!\left(f\right)
with
f(x)\neq f(y)
) are at least
\mathrm{GAP}(f)
apart, we want to bound the probability
\widehat{w}
differs by more that
\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathrm{GAP}(f)
. Suppose the randomized algorithm uniformly samples
t
coordinates without replacement and queries them, meaning that
\widehat{w}
is the number of
1
’s among these samples, scaled by
n/t
. By a Chernoff bound, after
t
samples, the estimate satisfies

\Pr\!\left[\,|\widehat{w}-\mathrm{wt}(x)|>\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{GAP}(f)\,\right]\leq e^{-\Omega(t\,(\mathrm{GAP}(f)/n)^{2})}.

With
\sqrt{t}=\Theta\left(\frac{n}{\mathrm{GAP}(f)}\right)
, we can make the probability a small enough constant,
therefore,
\mathrm{R}(f)\leq t=\Theta\left(\frac{n^{2}}{(\mathrm{GAP}(f))^{2}}\right)
.
Thus all these measures are polynomially related. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lastly, we evaluate
\mathrm{D}
using
\mathrm{GAP}
.

Lemma 38
.

Let
f
be a symmetric function.
Then,
\mathrm{D}(f)=n-\mathrm{GAP}(f)+1
.

Proof.

Let
x,y
be points that achieve the
\mathrm{GAP}(f)
. Without loss of generality, assume that
\mathrm{wt}(x)\leq\mathrm{wt}(y)
.
Define the domain
\mathcal{D}^{*}=\{z\ :\ z=\sigma(x)\text{ or }z=\sigma(y)\}\subseteq\textup{Dom}\!\left(f\right)
and define
f^{*}:\mathcal{D}^{*}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
with
f^{*}(x)=f(x)
for
x\in\mathcal{D}^{*}
.
Since
f^{*}
is a subfunction of
f
, any algorithm that computes
f
must also compute
f^{*}
.
Hence,
\mathrm{D}(f^{*})\leq\mathrm{D}(f)
.
Moreover, by construction,
\mathrm{GAP}(f)=\mathrm{GAP}(f^{*})
.

We will show that
n-\mathrm{GAP}(f^{*})+1\leq\mathrm{D}(f^{*})
, establishing the lower bound.
Suppose an algorithm queries
t\leq n-\mathrm{GAP}(f)
positions.
Our idea will be to construct two inputs that return the same queries to the algorithm but have different outputs under
f
.
We will divide the proof into two cases.
In the first case, suppose
t\leq\mathrm{wt}(x)
.
Suppose the algorithm first queries position
i_{1}
, then set
x_{i_{1}}=1
.
Suppose then the algorithm (adaptively) queries
i_{2}
, then set
x_{i_{2}}=1
.
Continue this until
x_{i_{t}}=1
.
That is, suppose the transcript returns all
t
entries as 1.
Now let
x_{0}
be an input that is consistent with the above transcript and has
\mathrm{wt}(x)-t
exactly 1 in the unqueried entries.
Let
x_{1}
be an input that is consistent with the above transcript and has
\mathrm{wt}(y)-t
exactly 1 in the unqueried entries.
Note that this is possible to do since
t\leq\mathrm{wt}(x)\leq\mathrm{wt}(y)\leq n
.
Thus, we get
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
, two conflicting inputs with the same transcript; hence, the algorithm cannot distinguish them.
The second case
t\geq\mathrm{wt}(x)
can also be proven in a similar manner by flipping 1’s with zeros and stopping the 1’s at the
x_{i_{\mathrm{wt}(x)}}
-th entry.
That is, set
x_{i_{1}}=x_{i_{2}}=\ldots=x_{i_{\mathrm{wt}(x)}}=1
and set the rest of the queries up-to
t
to be 0.
Now, for
x_{0}
set all the unqueried positions to be
0
and for
x_{1}
set exactly
\mathrm{GAP}(f)
positions to be 1.
This is possible since the number of unqueried positions is
n-t\geq\mathrm{GAP}(f)
.
Now,
\mathrm{wt}(x_{0})=\mathrm{wt}(x)
and
\mathrm{wt}(x_{1})=\mathrm{wt}(y)
, making them conflicting inputs, but they have the same transcript; hence, again the algorithm cannot distinguish them.

Now, we consider the upper bound.
We will give a trivial algorithm that queries on
t=n-\mathrm{GAP}(f)+1
locations.
We will show that querying any
t
positions is enough to decide
f
, that is, there can not be two conflicting inputs with that transcript.
Suppose we query
t
positions and we get
t_{1}
1’s and
t-t_{1}
0’s in them.
For the sake of contradiction, assume that there are two conflicting inputs
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
with
f(x_{0})=0
and
f(x_{1})=1
that are consistent with the above transcript.
Now,
t_{1}\leq\mathrm{wt}(x),\mathrm{wt}(y)\leq t_{1}+(n-t)
.
The first inequality follows since there are already
t_{1}
1’s in the transcript, and the second inequality follows by noting that the unqueried part can contribute at most
n-t
.
Thus
|\mathrm{wt}(x)-\mathrm{wt}(y)|\leq t_{1}+n-t-t_{1}\leq\mathrm{GAP}-1
, contradiction.
∎

4.2
Functions defined on a slice

In this section, we will consider partial functions whose domain is a single slice of the Boolean cube.
The deterministic and quantum query complexities of such functions were already considered by
[
BD16
]
, who showed that
\mathrm{D}(f)=O(\mathrm{Q}(f)^{18})
.
Here, we prove an alternate bound on
\mathrm{D}(f)
.
Our main motivation is to fine-grain the bound based on the slice’s location.
As such, our result is not comparable to that of
[
BD16
]
.
Their result is tighter for slices near the edges, whereas our bound is tighter for slices near the middle.
In particular, we give a tighter bound for roughly balanced domains.

Definition 39

(Balanced blocks)
.

We say that a block
B\subseteq[n]
is balanced for
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
if

\ \lvert\{i\in[n]:i\in B\text{ and }x_{i}=1\}\rvert=\lvert\{i\in[n]:i\in B\text{ and }x_{i}=0\}\rvert.

Remark 40
.

For a function
f
defined on a slice,
x\in\textup{Dom}(f)
if and only if
x^{B}\in\textup{Dom}(f)
for every balanced block
B
. Hence, the block sensitivity
\mathrm{bs}(f)
(Definition
2.2
) can be restricted to only balanced blocks.

Lemma 41
.

For
f:\textup{Dom}(f)\rightarrow\{0,1\}
such that
\textup{Dom}\!\left(f\right)=\{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}:\mathrm{wt}(x)=k\}
for some fixed
k\in[n]
, we have,

\mathrm{D}(f)\leq\frac{3n}{\min\{k,n-k\}}\mathrm{C}(f)\mathrm{bs}(f)
.

Proof.

We follow a similar structure to the proof of
\mathrm{D}(f)\leq\mathrm{C}(f)\mathrm{bs}(f)
for total functions
[
BBC+01
]
. The main difference is that in order to turn the indices which disagreed with the queried certificate into blocks, by
Section

4.2
, we must ensure they are balanced. We “balance” them out by adding indices which were not queried by the algorithm.

We may assume
f
is non-constant and that
k\leq n/2
as the case for
k>n/2
follows by symmetry. Furthermore, one may observe that for all functions
f
on the
k

5.1

Lemma 48

Proof.

Since

Now define the polynomial:

5.3

A natural path when searching for completions of a partial function

5.4.2

We turn from the case of completing exact degree polynomials to a discussion about working with approximating polynomials. The reason for pivoting to approximating polynomials is the same as in
