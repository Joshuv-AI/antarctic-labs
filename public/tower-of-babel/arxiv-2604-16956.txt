Waves Everywhere: A Distributional Equation Approach to Front Propagation
Matthieu Jonckheere, Seva Shneer

Abstract
. We study reaction-diffusion particle systems with several interaction mechanisms. As the number of particles tends to infinity, the system admits a mean-field limit describing the bulk behaviour. We focus on determining the propagation speed and the particle distribution around the centre of mass, which corresponds to the travelling wave of the limiting equation. We introduce a probabilistic method to characterise these waves via tagged particle distributional equations. Our key technique connects these to linear distributional equations solvable using martingale limits from branching processes. We first demonstrate our approach on a general model where particles move via Lévy processes and synchronise at interaction moments (the lower particle jumps to the position of the higher one). Assuming the mean-field limit holds, we characterise its travelling-wave solutions. We then apply the method to two specific models with established mean-field limits. For Brownian particles, we recover known travelling-wave solutions of the F-KPP equation. For the compound Poisson model studied in [ 4 ] where particles perform random walks with exponential holding times and copy positions at interactions, we extend previous results beyond exponential jumps to arbitrary jump distributions. Finally, we analyse the power-of-2 growth model, where interactions add a random value to the lower particle. We characterise its travelling waves and discover a surprising connection to the synchronisation models. The distributional equations at the heart of our technique are of independent interest, and we identify connections and differences with related equations studied extensively in the literature.



1.
Introduction

Reaction-diffusion systems with mean-field interactions have attracted considerable attention in recent years due to their broad applicability across diverse scientific domains. In statistical physics, these systems model the collective behaviour of interacting particles where local interactions propagate to create macroscopic patterns
[
16
,
30
]
. In population dynamics, they describe the spatial spread of populations subject to reproduction, competition, and migration processes
[
3
,
22
]
. In queueing theory and communication networks, they represent the dynamics of job processing where interacting servers affect each other’s workloads through load-balancing mechanisms
[
38
]
.

The mean-field approximation, which becomes exact in the limit of infinitely many particles, transforms the complex microscopic interactions into a deterministic macroscopic description. This framework has proved to be remarkably powerful in elucidating how simple local rules can give rise to complex collective phenomena. A particularly striking emergent behavior is the formation of travelling waves—self-sustaining, shape-preserving fronts that propagate through the system at constant velocity.
The emergence of travelling waves in such systems represents a fundamental phenomenon of pattern formation and front propagation that transcends specific applications.

From a mathematical perspective, travelling waves provide a crucial link between microscopic stochastic dynamics and macroscopic deterministic equations
[
17
,
16
,
25
]
. They emerge as stationary solutions in moving reference frames, offering insights into the system’s long-term behaviour and stability properties. The study of these waves addresses fundamental questions about the selection of propagation speeds, the stability of wave profiles, and the conditions under which such coherent structures can form and persist.

Our principal contribution here lies in developing a novel probabilistic methodology for characterising travelling waves in mean-field interacting particle systems, with particular focus on systems governed by simple interaction rules like the ”power-of-2” protocol. More precisely, we study a class of reaction-diffusion particle systems where particles move via Lévy processes and interact through simple copying or growth mechanisms: synchronisation (particles jump to match the leader) or conditional jumps (where a random value is added to the state of the lower particle). Despite their simplicity, these models exhibit rich collective behaviour including travelling-wave propagation.

Unlike traditional approaches that typically analyse travelling waves through partial differential equations derived in the hydrodynamic limit, we establish direct connections between the wave profile and the microscopic particle dynamics via distributional fixed-point equations. This framework enables us to characterise travelling waves as solutions of non-linear distributional equations and their linear counterparts.

By linking these distributional equations to martingale limits of appropriately constructed branching processes, we obtain not only existence results but also powerful probabilistic representations of the wave profiles. This methodology opens the door to deriving finer properties of travelling waves that are often inaccessible through purely analytical methods. In particular, we can characterise their tail asymptotics thanks to the distributional characterisation. Moreover, our approach allows to reveal deep structural connections between seemingly unrelated models. In that vein, we show how the travelling waves for the power-of-2 model relate to those of the classical F-KPP equation through transformations of their characterising equations. This unification provides new insights into the universality classes of front propagation and offers a flexible toolkit for analysing a broad spectrum of interacting particle systems beyond the specific examples considered here.

Organisation and contributions

Section 2
establishes the general framework. We formalise the mean-field limits for reaction-diffusion particle systems, presenting three key examples: the F-KPP model with synchronisation, the Baryshnikov-Stolyar model combining independent jumps with copying, and the power-of-2 growth model. We then derive distributional equations characterising travelling waves assuming convergence of microscopic dynamics (Proposition 1).

Section 3
develops the core probabilistic methodology underpinning our analysis. We study non-linear distributional equations and establish a fundamental correspondence between these and associated linear equations solvable via branching processes (Lemma 2). We then recall existing results for existence and uniqueness conditions for solutions with both finite and infinite mean (Proposition 3).

Section 4
applies this framework to models with Lévy diffusions and synchronisation. After connecting the distributional equations to martingale limits, we demonstrate the technique on Brownian motion, recovering the classical F-KPP results. We then treat the compound Poisson case in detail, deriving the travelling wave characterisation of the Baryshnikov-Stolyar model (Theorem 4) and extending previous results to arbitrary jump distributions.

Section 5
analyses the power-of-2 model. We derive an equivalent distributional formulation (Proposition 5) revealing a surprising structural connection to synchronisation models. We then use the previous results to characterise the travelling wave via the linear equation (Theorem 6). We establish tail asymptotics (Corollary 7) and provide explicit solutions for special cases.

We consider a generic reaction-diffusion particle system where the diffusion dynamics are driven by a Lévy process

The

For simplicity, we omit the dependence of

Fisher-Kolmogorov-Petrovskii-Piskunov

\theta_{ij}(\xi)(k)=\begin{cases}\xi(j)&\text{ if $k=i$ and $\xi(i)<\xi(j)$},\\

Example 2

Model with independent jumps and copying (Baryshnikov-Stolyar model)

The model considered in

\theta_{ij}(\xi)(k)=\begin{cases}\xi(j)&\text{if }k=i\text{ and }\xi(i)<\xi(j),\\

Example 3
.

Quantile mechanisms and power of 2.

The model considered in this line of work consists of
N
particles on the real line, experiencing ”jump urges” at exponential times. The key feature is the mean-field interaction: the probability that a particle actually jumps upon an urge is a decreasing function
\eta(v)
of its quantile
v
within the current empirical distribution of all particle locations. When a jump occurs, its size is given by an i.i.d. non-negative random variable
Z
.

A specific example of those mechanisms is the so-called power of 2, which can be described as follows. There is a Poisson process associated to each particle. At the instances of the process, it chooses uniformly between all other particles and jumps (according to a fixed distribution) only if the position of the
chosen particle is bigger than its own position. In other words:

(7)

\theta_{ij}(\xi)(k)=\begin{cases}\xi(i)+X&\text{ if $k=i$ and $\xi(i)\leq\xi(j)$},\\
\xi(k)&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

with
X
a random variable with a finite first moment. We will assume, w.l.o.g., that
\mathbb{E}(X)=1
. Namely, given two labels
i,j
,
\theta_{ij}
pushes the position of the leftmost particle by
X
.

The foundational work of
[
22
]
established the convergence of the particle system’s empirical measure. However, this initial result relied on a restrictive
finite-dependence assumption

The convergence result under more general conditions was later established by
[
3
]
for a different model and has now been generalised for this system by
[
37
]
.

The question of the limiting systems admitting a travelling wave solution has been addressed in several stages:

•

Early Results:

[
23
]
made significant progress by studying the MFM directly. They proved that
if
a travelling wave exists, then it is typically unique (up to a shift), and the trajectories of the MFM are attracted to it as time
t\to\infty
. However, the fundamental question of the

of such a wave under general assumptions was left open.

•

Special Cases:
For specific types of MFMs and jump distributions, explicit travelling wave solutions have been found.
[
3
]
,
[
27
]
, and
[
26
]
obtained such explicit forms in certain special cases.

•

Existence:

[
37
]
(Theorem 3.1) finally resolved the existence question under general conditions. It was shown that a travelling wave
\phi
exists as long as the jump size has a finite second moment,
\mathbb{E}Z^{2}<\infty
. This result requires no other assumptions on the jump-size distribution, which is a significant generalisation.

[
23
]
showed that under certain conditions, the travelling wave is unique. Their analysis relied on demonstrating that the Wasserstein

[

A distributional equation for travelling waves

Usually the travelling waves are expressed in terms of stationary equations derived from the PDEs.
One looks for
\psi(x,t)=h(x-ct),

h(\infty)=0

\bar{\theta}(\xi_{1},\xi_{J})=\theta_{1J}(\xi)(1)-\xi_{1}

•

•

•

Let

We can write the dynamics of the Markov process

\displaystyle\xi^{N}_{1}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\xi^{N}_{1}(0)+\Gamma(\tau)+\bar{\theta}(\xi^{N}_{1}(\tau-),\xi^{N}_{J}(\tau-)),

(10)

\displaystyle\xi^{N}_{1}(\tau)

\displaystyle-c\tau=\xi^{N}_{1}(0)+\Gamma(\tau)-c\tau+\bar{\theta}(\xi^{N}_{1}(\tau-),\xi^{N}_{J}(\tau-)),

\displaystyle\xi^{N}_{1}(\tau)

3.
Distributional equations

In the Section, we will study a particular case of the equation above, namely, we will study equations

(12)

\xi\overset{d}{=}\max\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\}-A,

where
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}
are independent copies of
\xi

We now state a key lemma that establishes a correspondence between solutions of (

Lemma 2
.

There exists a solution to (

F(x):=\mathbb{P}(\xi\leq x)=\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-Ve^{-\gamma x}}\right)

if and only if there exists a positive random variable
V>0

\gamma>0
satisfying:

(13)

V\overset{d}{=}(V_{1}+V_{2})e^{-\gamma A},

where
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
are independent copies of
V
, and all random variables on the right-hand side are mutually independent.

Proof.

We prove both directions of the equivalence.

1. (
\Leftarrow
) Existence of
V
satisfying (
13
) implies existence of
\xi
.

Assume there exists
V>0
a.s. and
\gamma>0
satisfying (
13
). Define a random variable
\xi
via its cumulative distribution function

F(x):=\mathbb{P}(\xi\leq x)=\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-Ve^{-\gamma x}}\right).

First, note that
F(x)
is indeed a valid distribution function: it is increasing in

\lim_{x\to-\infty}F(x)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{x\to\infty}F(x)=1.

Let
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}
be independent copies of
\xi
, and let
A
be independent of
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
. For any

, we compute:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\}-A\leq x\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[F(x+A)^{2}\right].

Now, since

and
V_{2}

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-Ve^{-\gamma(x+A)}}\mid A\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-V_{1}e^{-\gamma(x+A)}}\mid A\right)\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-V_{2}e^{-\gamma(x+A)}}\mid A\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-(V_{1}+V_{2})e^{-\gamma(x+A)}}\mid A\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\}-A\leq x\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-(V_{1}+V_{2})e^{-\gamma(x+A)}}\mid A\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-(V_{1}+V_{2})e^{-\gamma A}e^{-\gamma x}}\right).

By (

),
V\overset{d}{=}(V_{1}+V_{2})e^{-\gamma A}

\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-(V_{1}+V_{2})e^{-\gamma A}e^{-\gamma x}}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-Ve^{-\gamma x}}\right)=F(x).

Thus,
\xi

\xi\overset{d}{=}\max\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\}-A
.

2. (
\Rightarrow
) Existence of

Assume
\xi

\gamma>0

\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-Ve^{-\gamma x}}\right)=F(x)=\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-(V_{1}+V_{2})e^{-\gamma A}e^{-\gamma x}}\right).

Define the function

.

Parts (i) and (ii): existence via the branching construction.

5.1.

Consider the power-of-2 model described in Example

Applying Proposition
1
yields the following characterisation of travelling wave solutions. A travelling wave profile (i.e., the stationary distribution in the moving frame with speed

), must satisfy the distributional equation:

(35)

\xi\overset{d}{=}\xi_{1}+{\bf 1}{\left\{\xi_{1}\leq\xi_{2}\right\}}X-cT+\Gamma(T),

where:

•

\xi_{1},\xi_{2},X,T
are independent random variables,

•

\Gamma(T)
in this case represents the displacement from a Brownian motion,

•

X
has distribution
F
with
\mathbb{E}[X]=1
,

•

\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi
have the same distribution
h
,

•

T\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1)
is the time between interactions.

A crucial simplification occurs by noting that for this model, the speed is necessarily

. This follows from conservation of mass and the fact that
\mathbb{E}[X]=1
, as shown in
[
37
]
.

We say that two distributional equations are
equivalent
if there exists a bijective transformation mapping solutions of one equation to solutions of the other. The following proposition provides a rather surprising way to connect travelling waves in the models with synchonisation from the previous section and the previously described power-of-2 interaction.

Proposition 5

(Equivalent
Formulation)
.

The distributional equation (
35
) with
c=1
is equivalent to

(36)

\xi\overset{d}{=}\min(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})+\overline{X}+Z,

where:

•

\overline{X}
is the integrated tail random variable with survival function

\mathbb{P}(\overline{X}>x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}\overline{F}(y)dy,\quad\text{where }\overline{F}(x)=1-F(x),

•

Z
is an independent random variable which is exponentially distributed with mean
\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}
for diffusion coefficient
\sigma^{2}
.

Proof of Proposition
5

For any random variable
\zeta
, let
\varphi_{\zeta}(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}\left(e^{i\lambda A}\right)
define its characteristic function. Then, taking transforms on both sides of (
35
), we have

\varphi_{\xi}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{1+i\lambda+\lambda^{2}a^{2}/2}\int_{y=-\infty}^{\infty}h^{\prime}(y)\left(\varphi_{X}(\lambda)\int_{z=-\infty}^{y}h^{\prime}(z)e^{\lambda z}dz+\int_{z=y}^{\infty}h^{\prime}(z)e^{\lambda z}dz\right)dy,

where the first term on the RHS is obtained by a straightforward calculation of the transform of
-T+\Gamma(T)
. Integrating by parts the integral on the RHS above, we can then rewrite the equation as

(1+i\lambda+\lambda^{2}a^{2}/2)\varphi_{\xi}(\lambda)=\varphi_{X}(\lambda)\int_{z=-\infty}^{\infty}h^{\prime}(z)e^{i\lambda z}dz-(\varphi_{X}(\lambda)-1)\int_{y=-\infty}^{\infty}h(y)h^{\prime}(y)e^{i\lambda y}dy.

Note now that

-\int_{y=-\infty}^{\infty}h(y)h^{\prime}(y)e^{i\lambda y}dy=\int_{y=-\infty}^{\infty}(1-h(y))h^{\prime}(y)e^{i\lambda y}dy-\int_{y=-\infty}^{\infty}h^{\prime}(y)e^{i\lambda y}dy

and, as
2(1-h(x))h^{\prime}(x)
is the density of
\min\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\}
, we have, continuing with equations for transforms above,

(1+i\lambda+\lambda^{2}a^{2}/2)\varphi_{\xi}(\lambda)=\varphi_{X}(\lambda)\varphi_{\xi}(\lambda)+(\varphi_{X}(\lambda)-1)\left(\varphi_{\min\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\}}(\lambda)-\varphi_{\xi}(\lambda)\right)

and finally,

\varphi_{\xi}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{1-i\lambda a^{2}/2}\frac{\varphi_{X}(\lambda)-1}{i\lambda}\varphi_{\min\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\}}(\lambda).

The proof is completed by noting that
\frac{1}{1-i\lambda a^{2}/2}
is the characteristic function of
\frac{a^{2}}{2}T
and
\frac{\varphi_{X}(\lambda)-1}{i\lambda}
is the characteristic function of
\overline{X}
.

The transformation from (
35
) to (
36
) reveals a fundamental structure: the travelling wave arises from the combined effect of taking the minimum of two independent copies (representing the interaction mechanism) plus two independent additive components representing the jump size and the Brownian displacement.

With notation
\eta=-\xi
, (
36
) can be rewritten as

(37)

\eta=\max\{\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\}-\overline{X}-Z,

a particular case of equation (
12
) with
A=\overline{X}+Z
, and thus all the results we derived are applicable in this case.

Remark 6
.

We could not find an equivalent form amenable to analysis for the case of a generic Lévy process.
The main difficulty is that our proof of Proposition
5
relies critically on the specific characteristic function of Brownian displacement over an exponential time:

\mathbb{E}[e^{i\lambda\Gamma(T)}]=\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}/2},

where
T\sim\text{Exp}(1)
. This simple form allows us to decompose
-cT+\Gamma(T)
into a sum of an exponential random variable and the integrated tail
\tilde{X}
, which is key to applying Lemma
2
.
For a general Lévy process
the analogous decomposition does not have a tractable closed form. Establishing the connection to the linear distributional equation would require new techniques out of the scope of the current work, possibly involving direct analysis of the Lévy-Khintchine representation.

5.2.
Characterisation of the travelling wave

Applying Lemma
2
to equation (
36
), we obtain the following characterisation:

Theorem 6

(Travelling Wave Characterisation)
.

For the power-of-2 model, the travelling wave profile
h(x)=\mathbb{P}(\xi>x)
admits the representation

(38)

h(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-Ve^{\gamma x}\right)\right],

where
\gamma>0
is a positive parameter which satisfies

(39)

\frac{1}{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\gamma Y\right)\right],

and
V>0
is a random variable satisfying the linear equation

(40)

V\overset{d}{=}(V_{1}+V_{2})\exp\left(-\gamma Y\right),

with
V_{1},V_{2}
independent copies of
V
, independent of
Y=\overline{X}+Z
.

Proof.

From Lemma
2
applied to equation (
36
), we have that the distribution of
\eta
satisfies

\mathbb{P}(\eta\leq x)=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-Ve^{-\gamma x}\right)\right].

Since
\eta=-\xi
, we obtain

h(x)=\mathbb{P}(\xi>x)=\mathbb{P}(-\eta>x)=\mathbb{P}(\eta<-x)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left(-Ve^{\gamma x}\right)\right],

which is the claimed representation (
38
).

It remains to show that the hypotheses of Proposition
3
are satisfied for
A=Y
and some
\gamma>0
.
The necessary condition (
39
) requires
\mathbb{E}[e^{-\gamma Y}]=\tfrac{1}{2}
. Define
\Phi(\gamma):=\mathbb{E}[e^{-\gamma Y}]
. Since
Y>0
a.s., the function
\Phi
is strictly decreasing on
[0,\infty)
with
\Phi(0)=1
and
\Phi(\gamma)\to 0
as
\gamma\to\infty
. By the intermediate value theorem, there exists a unique
\gamma^{*}>0
satisfying
\Phi(\gamma^{*})=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

We now check the supercritical condition of Proposition
3
,
\mathbb{E}[Ye^{-\gamma^{*}Y}]>0
. This holds automatically since
Y>0
a.s. and
e^{-\gamma^{*}Y}>0
a.s., so the integrand is strictly positive almost surely.
Therefore, there exists a unique (up to multiplicative constant) random variable
V>0
with
\mathbb{E}[V]<\infty
satisfying (
40
). This
V
can be constructed as the almost sure limit of the additive martingale associated with the branching random walk described in Section
4.1
, with displacements distributed as
\gamma Y
.

∎

5.3.
Properties of the Solution

The characterisation in Theorem
6
allows us to derive important properties of the travelling wave.

Corollary 7

(Tail Behavior)
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
6
, the travelling wave
h(x)=\mathbb{E}[e^{-Ve^{\gamma x}}]
satisfies

1-h(x)\sim\mathbb{E}[V]\,e^{\gamma x}\;\text{ as }x\to-\infty,\qquad h(x)\sim f_{V}(0^{+})\,e^{-\gamma x}\;\text{ as }x\to+\infty,

where
f_{V}
denotes the density of
V
, which exists and is continuous on
(0,\infty)
with
f_{V}(0^{+})>0
.

Proof.

Regularity of
f_{V}
.
Since
Y=\overline{X}+Z
has a density (as
Z
is exponential), the fixed-point equation
V\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(V_{1}+V_{2})e^{-\gamma Y}
falls within the scope of
[
31
, Theorem 1.1]
: under the conditions
\mathbb{E}[e^{-\gamma Y}]=\tfrac{1}{2}
and
\mathbb{E}[Ye^{-\gamma Y}]>0
(both verified in the proof of Theorem
6
),
V
has a continuous density on
(0,\infty)
with
f_{V}(0^{+})>0
.

Write
s=e^{\gamma x}
.

Left tail
(
s\to 0^{+}
).  Since
0\leq(1-e^{-Vs})/(Vs)\leq 1
and the ratio tends to
1
a.s., dominated convergence gives

\frac{1-h(x)}{s}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[V\cdot\frac{1-e^{-Vs}}{Vs}\right]\;\longrightarrow\;\mathbb{E}[V]\in(0,\infty).

Right tail
(
s\to+\infty
).  Substituting
v=w/s
in the Laplace integral:

s\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{-Vs}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-w}\,f_{V}(w/s)\,dw.

As
s\to\infty
, the integrand converges pointwise to
e^{-w}f_{V}(0^{+})
. Since
f_{V}
is bounded on compact sets, the integrand is dominated by
\|f_{V}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,M])}\,e^{-w}
on
[0,Ms]
for any fixed
M
, while the tail integral over
[Ms,\infty)
vanishes exponentially. By dominated convergence,

s\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{-Vs}\right]\;\longrightarrow\;f_{V}(0^{+}).

∎

5.4.
Connection to branching processes

Of course, we can again demonstrate that a solution to the linear equation can also be constructed using a branching process, similarly to the case of synchronisation interaction.

Consider a branching process starting with a single individual. Any individual’s lifetime is given by an independent copy of a random variable with the distribution of
Y
, and, upon death, very individual produces
2
offspring.
Let us denote by
Z(t)
the number of individuals alive at time
t

It is known that there exists a
\gamma>0
such that

\frac{Z(t)}{e^{\gamma t}}

is a martingale that converges to a limit, say,
W
. Conditioning on the first branching event clearly leads to

W\overset{d}{=}e^{-\gamma Y}(W_{1}+W_{2}).

5.5.
Explicit Solutions for Special Cases

In certain special cases,
\gamma
can be characterised explicitly:

Example 7

(Exponential Jumps with Brownian Motion)
.

If
X\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1)
and the displacement is Brownian motion with coefficient
\sigma^{2}
, then
\overline{X}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1)
and
\Psi(1)=\sigma^{2}/2
. In this case, equation (
39
) becomes

\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{1+\gamma}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\gamma},

which is a quadratic equation in
\gamma
. The positive solution is

\gamma=\frac{\sqrt{1+2\sigma^{2}}-1}{\sigma^{2}}.

6.
Concluding remarks and open problems

We proposed a new technique for characterising travelling waves in stochastic reaction-diffusion particle systems. The technique is probabilistic and allows to avoid working directly with PDEs describing the system’s mean-field limit.

We demonstrated the technique on models with synchronisation interaction where particles diffusions are either driven by Brownian motion (where speeds and shapes of travelling waves were known) or compound Poisson processes (where speeds and shapes were only known in one special case). We also demonstrated how our technique can be used to characterise speeds and shapes of travelling waves in the case of a general Levy diffusion, assuming some conditions can be verified.

We then considered a power-of-2 model where, upon interaction, a random number is added to the state of the lower particle. While the distributional equation for the travelling wave in this model is different, one can show, via transforms, that its tail satisfies
a structurally similar distributional equation, providing a characterisation of travelling waves in power-of-2 models which was not known previously.

The technique can be generalised to models where more than
2
particles interact, or even a random, independently selected from the same distribution every time, number of particles interact.

At the heart of our technique are non-linear distributional equations. We showed how connections to linear equations allows to construct solution but also to relate these solutions to branching processes. A remaining open problem is to study whether or not the non-linear equations may have other solutions.
