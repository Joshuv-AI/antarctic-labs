Self-rated fall risk and its associated factors among older adults: a community-based cross-sectional study in rural Minia, Egypt
Authors: Nehal Refaat Raouf, Chrestina Monir Fekry, Sara Ibrahim Sayed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFalls are a major cause of morbidity and mortality among older adults worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Rural older populations may be at increased risk due to environmental hazards, multimorbidity and limited access to preventive services. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of high fall risk and identify its associated factors among older adults living in rural Minia Governorate, Egypt.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 325 adults aged ≥60 years residing in two rural villages in Minia Governorate between September and December 2024. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire covering sociodemographic characteristics, health conditions, nutritional status, physical activity and environmental risk factors. Fall risk was assessed using the validated Arabic version of the Fall Risk Questionnaire (FRQ).ResultsMore than half of the participants (57.8%) were classified as having a high risk of fall. In univariate analysis, variant socioeconomic, health-related and environmental factors were associated with high fall risk. In multivariate analysis, age ≥75 years (OR=4.26), having three or more comorbidities (OR=21.69), poor self-rated health status (OR=7.36), physical inactivity (OR=2.79), and exposure to multiple environmental hazards (OR=2.79) emerged as independent predictors of high fall risk.ConclusionsThere is a high burden of fall risk among rural older adults in Minia Governorate and it is driven by a combination of health-related vulnerabilities and environmental hazards. Integrating fall risk screening, home safety assessment, management of chronic conditions and promotion of physical activity into rural primary healthcare services is essential to support healthy ageing and reduce fall-related burden.Keywords: Accidental Falls, Community Health, Cross-Sectional Studies, Age Factors, Risk Assessment

Introduction

Falls are considered a leading cause of disability and mortality among people aged 60 years and older. Fall rates have increased over the past decade, making falls a growing public health concern.1 2 Additionally, falls are the second leading cause of unintentional injury, accounting for the sixth leading cause of mortality among the aged worldwide.3 4 Fall risk is significantly influenced by housing and environmental factors, especially for older persons who live in underprivileged environments, according to recent worldwide burden estimates.5

The global population aged 60 years and older is rising. There was one billion individuals aged 60 years and older in 2020. By 2030 and by 2050, this figure will rise to 1.4 billion and 2.1 billion, respectively. Ageing of people began in high-income nations, but the biggest shift is currently occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Two-thirds of people over 60 worldwide will reside in LMICs by 2050, where health systems frequently struggle to manage health issues associated with ageing due to a lack of resources.6

The Central Agency for Public Mobilisation and Statistics estimates that by 2030, the aged will make up 11.6% of Egypt’s total population, up from 8.4% in 2024.7 Every year, millions of older adults experience falls. Although more than one in four older adults individually experience a fall every year, fewer than half report it to their physician. The likelihood of falling again is doubled after one fall.8

The risk of falls in older adults is influenced by a variety of factors, which include intrinsic and extrinsic factors. Age, gender, physical and mental health are examples of intrinsic factors. Behaviour, lifestyle, living conditions and living environment are examples of extrinsic factors that may affect the risk of falls.9 10 Older adults frequently fall due to a combination of circumstances rather than just one.11 12 Multimorbidity is a particularly strong predictor of fall-related injuries, according to evidence from LMICs, highlighting the cumulative effect of chronic conditions on functional decline and balance impairment.13

A large proportion of older adults living in rural regions are unaware of their personal propensity to falls, which may delay seeking appropriate care.14 15 Fall risk among elderly in rural locations is also influenced by environmental characteristics like uneven ground surfaces, wet circumstances, poor lighting, absence of handrails and overcrowded pathways.16 Age-inappropriate infrastructure, limited health literacy and restricted access to preventive and rehabilitative services may further exacerbate fall risk in rural areas.11 12

Fall Risk Questionnaire (FRQ) is a simple, brief and fast 12-item screening tool developed and validated by Rubenstein et al.17 It was incorporated in the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention to help older persons to evaluate their personal fall risk and increase their awareness of the need to seek medical attention. It may also be applied to the creation of algorithms that avoid falls.16 Many countries have also recognised the importance of such a tool, and it was translated into many languages and showed high validity and reliability.18 19

Despite the growing burden of population ageing and falls, evidence on fall prevalence and self-rated fall risk among Egyptian older adults, especially in rural Upper Egypt, such as Minia, remains limited. In addition, health inequities are expected to be present in rural areas with insufficient existing policies and surveillance systems to capture fall risk factors. This study aims to assess the prevalence of falls and analyse factors associated with self-rated fall risk among older adults living in rural Minia, Egypt.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Minia District, Minia governorate, Egypt, in two villages: Eldawadya and Nazlet Hussien. The study was conducted between September and December 2024 after approval of the ethical committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Minia University (Approval No.: 1274/09/2024).

Sampling technique and participants

The two villages, where the study was conducted, were chosen by a stratified random sample. Initially, Minia district was chosen randomly from the nine districts in Minia governorate, then Eldawadya and Nazlet Hussien were chosen randomly from the 42 villages in Minia District.

Households were visited by the research team and houses were selected by systematic random sample (every 10th house) to recruit the elderly people. This interval of houses was obtained according to data from previous surveys done by the local health unit workers of the villages to know the number of houses where elderly live. If no eligible older adult was present in the 10th house, the following houses were reviewed until finding a house with an older adult, then continuing according to the systematic sampling (every 10th house) from the last reviewed house.

Eligible participants included adults aged 60 years or older who were permanent residents of the selected villages and agreed to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria included individuals who were deaf or mute, emotionally unstable or unwilling to participate.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated by the Epi- info programme software version 7.2, depending on previously reported prevalence of fall among older adults (39.6%)20 . Considering the total number of older populations living in the selected two villages was 1908 with 95% CI and 5% margin of error. Accordingly, the sample size was calculated to be 309 elderly people. To account for a potential non-response rate of 5%, the final sample was increased to 325 older adults.

Data collection

Data collection was made by a team of undergraduate medical students and healthcare workers under the supervision of the researchers after training to fill out the questionnaire. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a structured Arabic questionnaire provided in ‘online supplemental file 1’. All participants were informed about the objectives of the study and gave consent to participate in this study.

Variables and measurements

The outcome variable of the study was self-rated fall risk, assessed using the Fall Risk Questionnaire (FRQ). Based on the total FRQ score, participants were classified into two categories: low fall risk (score <4) and high fall risk (score ≥4), in accordance with the recommended cut-off.

Independent variables included sociodemographic, health-related, behavioural and environmental factors. Sociodemographic variables comprised age (categorised into 60–64, 65–69, 70–74, and≥75 years), sex, educational level (illiterate, read and write, pre-university, and university or higher), marital status (married vs unmarried), current occupational status (working vs not working), perceived family income (sufficient and save, sufficient most of the time, or insufficient/in debt), and smoking status among males (current, ex-smoker, or never smoker).

Health-related variables included self-reported history of falls during the previous 12 months, number of comorbidities, number of medications taken daily, self-rated health status during the last month, nutritional status and physical activity. Comorbidities were assessed by asking participants whether they had been previously diagnosed by a physician with any of 21 chronic conditions; the total number of comorbidities was categorised as none, one, two, or three or more. Medication use was classified into none, 1–4, 5–8 and more than 8 medications per day. Self-rated health status was on a four-point scale (excellent, very good, good and poor).

Nutritional status was assessed using the MNA-SF and categorised as normal nutritional status (score >11), at risk of malnutrition (score 8–11), or malnourished (score <8). Physical activity was assessed by asking participants whether they engaged in regular physical activity and was dichotomised as physically active or inactive.

Environmental risk factors were assessed using a structured checklist covering common household hazards related to falls, including poor lighting, slippery floors or loose carpets, uneven floor surfaces, lack of handrails and unsafe bathroom conditions. The total number of environmental risk factors was calculated for each participant and categorised as none, one, two, or three or more environmental hazards.

Several measures were taken to minimise potential sources of bias. Selection bias was reduced through the use of systematic random sampling of households. Information and measurement bias were minimised by using validated Arabic versions of the Fall Risk Questionnaire and Mini Nutritional Assessment, as well as by training data collectors and conducting face-to-face interviews using a standardised questionnaire.

Despite these measures, some degree of recall bias may still be present due to reliance on self-reported information, particularly regarding history.

Statistical analysis

Missing data were handled using a complete-case analysis. Participants with missing information for a specific variable were excluded from analyses involving that variable only (only one variable with missing data (MNA)). The proportion of missing data was small and no imputation methods were applied.

The analysis of the data was carried out using the IBM SPSS 25.0 statistical package software (IBM; Armonk, New York, USA) and Microsoft Excel 2010 for figures. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. χ2 test/ Fisher exact test was used for comparison between qualitative data between two groups of risk. A two-step binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent predictors of high fall risk among older adults. Age and gender were entered in the first block using the method entered as common non-modifiable confounders.

In the second block, variables that showed significant associations with fall risk in the bivariate analysis were entered simultaneously and the Forward Likelihood Ratio method was used for selecting additional variables as it allowed for an efficient identification of significant predictors from the broader set of candidate variables. History of falls was not included in the regression model because it is closely related to the definition of fall risk and may introduce reverse causality. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered a cut-off point for significance in all statistical tests.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

A total of 325 older adults aged 60 years above participated in the study. Of them, 188 participants (57.8%) were classified as having a high risk of fall, while 137 (42.2%) had a low fall risk according to the Fall Risk Questionnaire.

Table 1 presents the association between fall risk and sociodemographic characteristics of studied population. High fall risk increased significantly with advancing age, with the highest proportion observed among participants aged 75 years and above compared with other age groups (p=0.004). The percentage of females who had a high risk of falling was significantly higher than males (p=0.001). In addition, significant differences were observed across educational level, occupation, marital status and perceived family income. Higher risk of fall percentages were also observed among illiterates, not working, unmarried and those who reported insufficient family income. Smoking status among males was not significantly associated with fall risk.

‘Poor’ self-rated health status independently predicted high fall risk, particularly among participants perceiving their health as ‘poor’ (OR=7.36, 95% CI 2.10 to 26.18). Physical inactivity was associated with nearly threefold increased odds of high fall risk (OR=2.79, 95% CI 1.49 to 5.25). In addition, exposure to multiple environmental risk factors remained a significant independent predictor, with participants exposed to three or more hazards having higher odds of high fall risk (OR=2.79, 95% CI 1.49 to 5.20).

The model indicates a good fit for predictors of high fall risk according to the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (χ² = 4.51, df=8, p=0.808). It explained 45.3% of the variance in fall risk (Nagelkerke R²=0.453).

As shown in ‘online supplemental figure 1’, among the high risk of fall group, the most prevalent environmental safety risk was poor lighting (48.4%) followed by slippery floor or moving carpets (42.6%), then unstraight floor (35.1%) and no ladder support (35.1%).

Discussion

Falls among elderly people is a serious public health concern, especially in areas with minimal resources like rural Egypt. This community-based cross-sectional study examined self-rated fall risk and its associated factors among older adults living in rural Minia Governorate, Egypt.

A sample was chosen in a random method, using a validated screening tool. It ensures external validity and provides a representative sample.

More than one-third of participants in the current study reported having experienced a fall in the previous year, and a significant percentage of them were classified as high-risk for falls. The FRQ captures both previous falls and perceived functional vulnerability, making it particularly suitable for rural settings where objective assessment tools may be limited.

A high percentage of older population that were classified as having a high risk of falls indicates a substantial and under-recognised public health problem in rural LMIC settings. Multivariable analysis identified advanced age, multimorbidity, poor self-rated health, physical inactivity and exposure to multiple environmental hazards as the strongest independent predictors of fall risk.

The high burden of fall risk observed in this study is consistent with previous community-based research conducted in rural Egypt. In Sharkia Governorate, nearly 62% of community-dwelling older adults reported at least one fall, with most events occurring indoors, particularly in bathrooms and on stairs24. Similarly, Mahmoued et al. in 2014 reported that nearly two-thirds of rural older adults in Zagazig district experienced previous falls, predominantly within the home environment.25 Evidence from rural primary care settings further supports these findings; in Fanara, integrating fall risk screening with home safety interventions significantly reduced falls, highlighting the importance of early identification and community-level prevention.26 Additional rural studies from El-Monofya and Dakahlia Governorates have also documented high baseline fall risk and identified environmental hazards, chronic diseases and functional dependency as key contributors.27 28

Environmental hazards emerged as a particularly strong predictor of fall risk in the present study. Poor lighting, slippery floors, uneven surfaces and unsafe bathroom design were common among high-risk participants, reflecting housing conditions typical of many rural communities. This finding is consistent with rural Egyptian intervention studies demonstrating significant reductions in fall risk following home safety education and environmental modification.26 28

Evidence from other LMICs reinforces this rural-specific pattern. A comparative rural–urban study from Indonesia showed that environmental factors were independently associated with falls in rural but not urban settings, likely reflecting infrastructural inequities and limited access to preventive services.29 Studies from rural China and Thailand also highlight the influence of living environment hazards on fall risk.15 30 These findings reinforce the importance of home safety assessments and structural modifications as a low-cost and effective component of fall prevention in rural communities.

Multimorbidity was another strong determinant of fall risk, with markedly increased odds among participants with three or more chronic conditions. This aligns with prior rural Egyptian studies linking the accumulation of chronic diseases to functional decline, impaired balance and polypharmacy,24 27 as well as broader evidence associating chronic conditions and medication use with increased fall risk.27 31 This may be explained by the cumulative effects of chronic diseases on balance, muscle strength and medical burden. Poor self-rated health also independently predicted fall risk, reinforcing its value as a simple indicator that captures both medical and social dimensions of vulnerability in resource-limited rural settings.

Physical inactivity independently predicted higher fall risk, consistent with findings from rural primary care and community-based studies showing that limited mobility and lack of structured exercise increase susceptibility to falls.26 29 In rural areas, barriers such as unsafe outdoor environments and the absence of organised programmes may further exacerbate inactivity.

Implications for practice and policy

These findings demonstrate that falls among rural older adults are driven by a combination of health-related vulnerabilities, environmental hazards and structural inequities. Context-specific strategies are therefore required, including routine fall risk screening, home safety assessment, management of multimorbidity and promotion of feasible physical activity programmes. The use of a self-rated fall risk questionnaire offers a practical and scalable approach for community-level screening and could be integrated into rural primary healthcare and family medicine services to support early identification and prevention.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. Its cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and the reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall or reporting bias. The study was conducted in only two rural villages in Minia Governorate, which may limit generalisability to other settings. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable community-based evidence on fall risk in an under-studied rural population.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study adds to the growing evidence of a high burden of fall risk among rural older adults in Egypt and other LMICs. Environmental hazards, multimorbidity, poor self-rated health and physical inactivity emerged as key modifiable risk factors. Targeted, context-specific interventions that integrate home safety, health promotion and functional rehabilitation are urgently needed to reduce fall risk and support healthy ageing in rural communities.
