Trends in the burden of low back pain attributable to occupational ergonomic factors among the global population aged 15–64 years (1990–2021): a study based on the GBD database
Authors: Haijing Ma, Sue Yuan, Hui Zhu, Yujia Chen, Ping Song, Yunxia Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLow back pain (LBP) is a major public health concern among the global working-age population and is closely associated with occupational ergonomic factors (OEF). However, comprehensive evidence on the global and regional burden of LBP attributable to OEF, as well as its long-term temporal trends in the working population, remains limited. Therefore, a systematic assessment of the burden and trends of OEF-related LBP is essential for informing occupational health policies and preventive strategies.MethodsThis study used the GBD 2021 database to analyze the Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) rate and numbers of LBP attributable to OEF across global and SDI regions. DALY counts and population data were aggregated across the age groups 15–19 to 60–64 years to estimate DALY rates and numbers for the working-age population (15–64 years). Age-standardization and time-trend analysis were applied, with Joinpoint regression used to identify inflection points and the rate of change in DALYs globally. Stratified analyses by age group were further performed to assess temporal patterns in LBP burden within the working-age population.ResultsFrom 1990 to 2021, the age-standardized DALY rate for LBP attributable to OEF among the global working-age population (15–64 years) decreased from 306.40 (95% UI: 270.5–346.2) per 100,000 population in 1990 to 275.80 (95% UI: 239.6–314.9) per 100,000 population in 2021. The age-standardized DALY rate declined from 1990 to 2021, with an annual average percentage change (AAPC) of -0.68 (95% CI: -0.72 to -0.63). Males experienced a faster decline than females. However, between 2019 and 2021, the downward trend stalled (APC = -0.39 for males; APC = -0.10 for females). Regionally, the highest DALY rates were in low-SDI areas, with 246.21 (95% UI: 333.60-174.75) for males and 292.34 (95% UI: 394.86-207.45) for females in 2021. The 50–54 age group had the highest burden, with females consistently showing higher DALY rates than males across all regions.ConclusionsOEFs are an important contributor to LBP in the working-age population, with significant gender, age, and regional disparities. Despite a global decline in DALY rates, the burden remains substantial, requiring stronger intervention measures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41043-026-01367-x.

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) is defined as discomfort, stiffness, or muscle tension in the area between the 12th rib and the iliac crest, extending to the gluteal folds. It may present as nonspecific pain and can occur with or without associated sciatica [1, 2]. Currently, LBP is the leading contributor to non-fatal disability globally and is a common health issue among the working-age population. Almost everyone will experience varying degrees of acute or chronic low back pain during their lifetime, significantly impacting patients’ mobility and work capacity and imposing substantial economic and healthcare burdens on individuals, families, and governments [3–5]. Globally, approximately 619 million people were affected by LBP in 2020, projected to increase to 843 million by 2050 [4]. Furthermore, the prevalence and incidence of LBP increase with age, particularly among individuals aged 15 years and older, especially in working populations.

Occupational ergonomic factors (OEFs) are recognized as one of the major modifiable risk factors contributing to the burden of LBP [6, 7]. Estimates from the GBD study indicate that OEF, high BMI, and smoking are the three major modifiable risk factors contributing to the global LBP burden. In 2020, 22.0% (95% UI: 20.4–23.4) of YLDs due to LBP were attributable to OEF, 12.5% (95% UI: 3.1–21.5) to smoking, and 11.5% (95% UI: 1.4–20.9) to high BMI [4]. Data suggest that approximately 25% of YLDs attributable to LBP may be related to ergonomic factors in the workplace. These factors include prolonged sitting or standing, frequent bending, and lifting, all of which can lead to musculoskeletal stress [4, 8].

In 2019, OEF was responsible for 126 million cases of LBP and 15.1 million YLD among the global working-age population, the economic loss reached $216.1 billion, with $47 billion attributed to healthcare costs, creating a heavy burden on both the health and economic systems [9]. With ongoing changes in work patterns, occupational environments, and population ageing, the burden of LBP attributable to OEF may vary across regions and demographic groups over time. Previous studies based on the GBD database have described the burden of LBP at regional or national levels [7, 10], and some studies have also focused on the role of occupational ergonomic factors in the global burden of LBP [9, 11]. However, studies that systematically characterize the long-term trends in LBP attributable to OEF in the global working-age population and further explore population disparities across multiple dimensions, such as sociodemographic index (SDI), sex, and detailed working-age groups, remain relatively limited.

This study used the GBD database to systematically estimate the burden of low back pain attributable to OEF at the global level and across different SDI regions from 1990 to 2021. Disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and age-standardized DALY rates were the primary outcome indicators. Stratified analyses were conducted among the working-age population (15–64 years) by sex and detailed age groups. In addition, Joinpoint regression was applied to characterize long-term temporal trends and identify key inflection points in global age-standardized DALY rates. The findings may help identify high-risk populations, evaluate occupational health inequalities, and provide evidence for targeted prevention and occupational health policy development.

Materials and methods

Data sources

The GBD is a comprehensive global database and an integrated analytical tool widely used in burden-of-disease research [12, 13]. It results from long-term collaboration between governments worldwide. In this study, data were extracted from the GBD 2021 database following a stepwise sequence. First, the “GBD Estimate” parameter was set to “Risk factor.” Second, the “Measure” parameter was set to “DALYs and YLDs.” Third, the “Metric” parameter was set to “rates” and “Number.” The “Risk” parameter was then set to “Occupational ergonomic factors,” and the “Cause” parameter to “Low back pain.” The “Location” parameter included “Global, High SDI, High-middle SDI, Middle SDI, Low-middle SDI, Low SDI.” The “Age” parameter included “All ages, Age-standardized, 15–39 years, 40–44 years, 45–49 years, 50–54 years, 55–59 years, 60–64 years.” The “Sex” parameter included “Both, Male, Female.” Finally, the “Year” parameter was set to “All years.” In addition, the corresponding total percentage change data were extracted. To estimate DALY rates for the working-age population (15–64 years), additional population data were extracted from the GBD 2021 database. The “GBD Estimate” parameter was set to “Population,” and the “Measure” parameter was set to “Population.” Population counts were obtained for standard GBD age groups (15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49,15–49, 50–54, 55–59, and 60–64 years) for subsequent aggregation and DALY rate calculations. Corresponding DALY counts for these detailed age groups were also extracted.

We used age-standardized DALY rates directly from the GBD 2021 database, which uses a standard world population for these calculations.

Sociodemographic index (SDI)

The SDI, a composite measure in the GBD study, assesses regional socioeconomic development on a scale from 0.0 (lowest) to 1.0 (highest) [14] It is based on the total fertility rate for women under 25, average years of schooling for those 15 and older, and per capita income adjusted for lag. Lower SDI values reflect high fertility, low education, and low income; higher values indicate the opposite. The GBD study groups 204 countries and regions into five SDI categories, from Low to High, excluding areas with fewer than 1 million people [14, 15].

Statistical analysis

This study defines the working-age population as people aged 15–64 years, as used by the International Labour Organization (ILO) and the United Nations [16]. Because the GBD database does not provide a predefined 15–64-year age category, estimates for the working-age population were calculated by aggregating DALY counts and population data across standard GBD age groups (15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49, 50–54, 55–59, and 60–64 years). In addition, sensitivity analyses were performed using an alternative broader age-grouping strategy (15–49, 50–54, 55–59, and 60–64 years) to evaluate the robustness of the estimates. The DALY rate for the 15–64-year population was calculated as follows:

DALY rate₁₅–₆₄ = (Σ DALYi) / (Σ Populationi) × 100,000,

where DALYi and Populationi represent the DALY counts and corresponding population counts for each age group, respectively. Age-standardized DALY rates (ASRs) were further calculated using the GBD world standard population:

ASR = Σ(ri × wi).

where ri represents the age-specific DALY rate, and wi represents the corresponding weight of the GBD world standard population. The results of the sensitivity analyses were highly consistent with the primary analyses (Supplementary Materials).

We analyzed DALY data by age group, sex, location, and year (1990–2021). Age groups included all ages, standardized, 15–39, 40–44, 45–49, 50–54, 55–59, and 60–64 years. Sex was categorized as male, female, or both. Locations included Global, High SDI, High-middle SDI, Middle SDI, and Low SDI. The GBD framework gives a 95% uncertainty interval (95% UI) to show data variability.

To assess temporal trends and gender differences in age-standardized DALY rates from 1990 to 2021, we conducted a Joinpoint regression analysis (version 5.1.0). Joinpoint regression is a statistical method based on segmented linear regression. It is primarily used to evaluate trends in disease burden or health indicators over time. This model uses least-squares fitting to identify significant inflection points, known as Joinpoints, in the time series. These points reveal turning points and the rate of change in different periods. The model calculates the annual percentage change (APC), average annual percentage change (AAPC), and their 95% confidence intervals (CI). We defined statistical significance as P < 0.05. This approach reduces potential bias associated with single linear trend analysis and improves the accuracy and objectivity of temporal trend assessments. This feature is valuable for analyzing long-term time series data, as it allows us to identify statistically significant trend changes. The number and location of joinpoints are automatically determined by the software using a grid search and permutation testing. Differences in temporal trends between males and females were evaluated by comparing the AAPC estimates and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals.

Following these analyses, all statistical computations and visualizations were performed using RStudio (version 4.4.2) and Joinpoint regression software (version 5.1.0).

Results

Age-standardized DALY rates attributable to OEF actors in global and SDI regions

In 1990, the age-standardized DALY rates for males and females were 222.49 (95% UI: 298.21, 158.01) and 231.99 (95% UI: 312.18, 160.64) per 100,000 population, respectively. By 2021, these rates had decreased to 172.12 (95% UI: 234.02, 120.13) per 100,000 population for males and 195.59 (95% UI: 261.85, 137.73) for females. From 1990 to 2021, the total percentage change was − 23% (95% UI: -20, -25) for males and − 16% (95% UI: -10, -21) for females per 100,000 population. In low-SDI regions, the male DALY rates in 1990 and 2021 were 310.09 (95% UI: 418.80, 220.89) and 246.21 (95% UI: 333.60, 174.75), respectively, while the female rates were 353.53 (95% UI: 469.01, 255.20) and 292.34 (95% UI: 394.86, 207.45) per 100,000 population. Overall, from 1990 to 2021, the DALY rates for LBP showed a downward trend across global and SDI regions, with significant reductions in the middle-SDI and high-middle-SDI regions. Female DALY rates were consistently higher than male rates, particularly in the middle-SDI and low-SDI regions. Detailed data are presented in Table 1.

Trends in the burden of LBP attributable to OEF globally

From 1990 to 2021, the total DALYs and age-standardized DALY rates attributable to OEF for LBP showed distinct trends across all age groups. Both male and female DALYs globally increased gradually, with growth slowing after 2005. Female DALYs remained higher than male DALYs throughout the period. Globally, age-standardized DALY rates declined for both males and females, with female rates consistently higher than male rates (Fig. 1).

Trends in the burden of LBP attributable to OEF in different SDI regions

Figure 2 shows a general decline in age-standardized DALY rates across all SDI regions from 1990 to 2021, though the rate of decrease varied. The most pronounced declines occurred in high-SDI and high-middle-SDI regions, with annual DALY rate reductions in these groups being substantially higher than in other regions. Changes in LBP DALYs for the global working-age population (15–64 Years) attributable to OEF

The trend of DALY rates for LBP attributable to OEF in the global working-age population (15–39, 40–44, 45–49, 50–54, 55–59, 60–64 years), stratified by gender, is shown in Fig. 3A. DALY rates for all age groups declined over the study period, although the rate of decline was gradual, especially in older age groups (55–64 years). Notably, female disease burden was consistently higher than male disease burden, with the most pronounced gender differences in the 40–64 year age range, and this disparity widened with age. The youngest age group (15–39 years) had the lowest DALY rate and the most significant decline over time. In contrast, the DALY rates for middle-aged groups (40–54 years) showed some fluctuations, with the most significant gender differences observed during specific time intervals. The most significant decline in DALY rates occurred between 1990 and 2000, with a slower decline after 2000. In 2021, the DALYs for both males and females peaked in the 15–39 age group (Fig. 3B). The DALY rate for LBP in working-age populations exhibited a “rise and fall” pattern, with a sharp increase in the 40–44 age group, peaking at 50–54 years, followed by a decline in the 60–64 age group. The gender difference was most significant in the 50–54 age group (Fig. 3C), with females experiencing a higher overall disease burden than males.

Joinpoint regression analysis of age-standardized DALYs globally

Joinpoint regression analysis demonstrated an overall declining trend in global age-standardized DALY rates for LBP attributable to OEF from 1990 to 2021 (Fig. 4; Table 2). Although the decline persisted throughout most of the study period, the rate of decrease varied across different time intervals, with several significant turning points identified. The between-sex difference in AAPC was statistically significant (P = 0.000222), indicating a steeper declining trend in males compared with females. In both sexes, the downward trend became less pronounced between 2019 and 2021, indicating a relative stagnation in the decline of DALY rates during the late study period.

Discussion

Our study systematically analyzed the global burden of LBP attributable to OEF among working-age adults (15–64 years) from 1990 to 2021. The findings revealed overall declines in global age-standardized DALY rates, with marked disparities across SDI regions, sexes, and age groups, and relatively higher burden observed in low SDI regions, females, and middle-aged to older working adults.

Global trends and burden

From 1990 to 2021, the age-standardized DALY rates for LBP attributable to OEF declined (AAPC = − 0.68, 95% CI: −0.72 to − 0.63, P < 0.0001). However, the total number of DALYs continued to increase over the same period. These findings suggest that although workplace ergonomic design and occupational health interventions have improved, the overall burden of LBP has not been substantially reduced. Between 1990 and 2021, the total DALYs for LBP among the working-age population (15–64 years) increased by approximately 4,095,170. Another study reported that global DALYs among individuals aged 45–59 years increased by 4,301,321 during the same period, and this trend is expected to persist [17]. An analysis of the global LBP burden in the 15–39 age group showed increasing incidence, prevalence, and mortality from 1990 to 2019 [18]. In 2021, approximately 62.8 million people worldwide were living with LBP, with 26.7 million new cases, and the DALY burden increased substantially [19]. These findings suggest that the persistently high prevalence of LBP among the working-age population remains a major public health challenge. In addition, with labour force expansion and population ageing, the number of individuals affected by LBP is expected to further increase in the coming decades [4, 20].

From an economic and social perspective, the escalating prevalence of LBP poses urgent and substantial challenges to labor productivity and the allocation of healthcare resources. For example, between 2012 and 2016, the total societal cost of LBP in Brazil soared to approximately USD 2.2 billion, with 79% attributable to productivity losses and 21% to healthcare expenditures, resulting in an alarming 59 million lost workdays [21]. In the United States, the annual economic burden is staggering at USD 40 billion, with an average annual cost of approximately USD 2,000 per patient, and this figure continues to rise at a worrying rate [22]. Studies from South Africa reveal that the average annual direct medical costs of LBP are around USD 5.4 million [23]. Collectively, these findings suggest that LBP imposes a substantial economic and social burden in multiple countries and may contribute to reduced workforce productivity and increased healthcare expenditures among the working-age population.

Regional and socioeconomic disparities

This study also found significant differences in age-standardized DALY rates for LBP due to OEF across SDI regions from 1990 to 2021. Specifically, DALY rates decreased more in high-SDI and high-middle-SDI regions. In contrast, middle-SDI, low-middle-SDI, and low-SDI regions saw slower changes, with some periods showing slight increases in DALY rates. Notably, the burden was heaviest in low-SDI regions. Such differences likely reflect multiple impacts of socioeconomic development on the LBP burden, including advances in medical technology, improved occupational health policies, and reductions in OEF from industrial upgrades [19]. However, analyses based on macro-level SDI regions may obscure major subnational differences. Previous studies demonstrate that, even within similarly developed countries, the burden of occupational low back pain varies across nations and industries. For instance, a systematic review in sub-Saharan Africa found large differences in LBP prevalence across countries and occupations, suggesting that labor structure and occupational exposure characteristics play key roles [24]. Likewise, a study from Bangladesh found farmers and rickshaw pullers had 4-fold and 3-fold higher LBP risks, respectively, compared with office workers. These findings highlight that differences in occupational ergonomic exposures within the same country can strongly shape LBP distribution [25].

In the GBD framework, OEF are modeled as a composite occupational exposure rather than as individually distinguishable ergonomic risk components. Therefore, the present study cannot determine the independent contribution of specific ergonomic exposures, such as prolonged sitting, heavy lifting, repetitive movements, or prolonged standing, to the burden of LBP.

At a more granular level, previous occupational studies have suggested that certain workplace ergonomic exposures, including heavy physical workload, sustained or awkward postures, repetitive movements, and prolonged sitting or standing, may be associated with increased LBP risk. A study of 5,700 older workers showed a clear dose–response relationship between early-career exposure to heavy physical work and an increased risk of LBP in later working life [26]. Among populations primarily engaged in computer work, approximately 54% experienced significant LBP symptoms [27], and further experimental research indicated that standing continuously for 30 min represented a critical threshold for the onset or exacerbation of LBP and ankle/foot pain in office workers [28]. Similar findings have been observed in industrial labor settings; for instance, chronic LBP prevalence among jute mill workers in India reached 55%, with handling loads over 20 kg and repetitive limb movements identified as primary contributing factors [29]. Although the GBD framework evaluates occupational ergonomic factors as a composite exposure without differentiating specific ergonomic risk types, the above evidence highlights that different workplace ergonomic exposures may play varying roles in LBP occurrence across occupations and settings. However, these specific exposure components could not be separately quantified within the current GBD-based analysis.

These subnational, occupational, and task-level differences may partly drive the ongoing accumulation of LBP burden in low- and middle-SDI countries. Over the next decades, LBP-related disability and economic burden in low-income countries may rise, as fragile healthcare and support systems may limit the response to increasing disease burden [3]. A review notes that, in low- and middle-income countries, hospitalization rates for LBP patients range from 13.4% to 18.7%, resulting in significant clinical and economic burdens [30]. A similar study using the GBD database found a positive correlation between age-standardized LBP and SDI YLD rates from 1990 to 2019 [8]. This aligns with the current study’s findings, which focus on LBP burden from OEF. The results suggest occupational ergonomic factors may contribute to regional LBP differences, especially across SDI levels. From a policy perspective, stronger occupational health education, improved working conditions, and greater workplace ergonomics training may help reduce the burden of LBP, particularly in middle- and low-SDI regions.

Impact of COVID-19 and changing work patterns

LBP is the most prevalent musculoskeletal disorder. The Joinpoint regression analysis in this study revealed that the age-standardized DALY rate for LBP nearly stagnated between 2019 and 2021 (Male APC = -0.39; Female APC = -0.10). This trend may be associated with changes in work patterns, lifestyle behaviours, and psychological stress during the COVID-19 pandemic [31]. During the pandemic, increased sedentary behaviour related to remote work, reduced physical activity, and non-ergonomic home working environments may have contributed to increased LBP risk. Previous studies have indicated that changes in work and study patterns during the COVID-19 pandemic may have increased the burden of LBP. These changes may have resulted in more unfavorable ergonomic exposures, such as prolonged sitting. For instance, a nationwide survey in Japan found that 31.3% of office workers reported increased time working from home during the pandemic. The incidence of new-onset LBP was significantly higher among those with increased telework compared to those without (6.3% vs. 3.1%). This suggests that COVID-19–related work arrangement changes may elevate LBP risk due to inadequate ergonomic environments [32]. Similar trends were observed among student populations. After implementing home confinement or remote teaching, the prevalence of LBP among Malaysian university students rose from 22.7% to 37.2% [33]. Brazilian university students experienced a prevalence as high as 91.0% during remote learning [34], much higher than the pre-pandemic prevalence of approximately 56.3% [35]. Collectively, these findings suggest that pandemic-related changes in study and work modalities may be associated with an increased burden of LBP.

In addition, social isolation, psychological stress, and mental health disorders such as anxiety and depression likely had a profound impact on LBP prevalence and pain intensity [36–38]. Studies indicate that, following COVID-19, the number of adults seeking healthcare for musculoskeletal pain nearly doubled compared to pre-pandemic levels [31]. Furthermore, LBP prevalence and pain intensity significantly increased during the pandemic compared to pre-pandemic levels, highlighting the multifaceted impact of COVID-19 on LBP burden [39, 40]. A large-scale cross-sectional study demonstrated that regular physical activity significantly reduced disability among LBP patients during the pandemic. Specifically, in individuals who engaged in ≥ 4 h of exercise per week, the odds ratio (OR) for severe disability decreased from 0.57 pre-pandemic to 0.29 during the pandemic (p = 0.002). Conversely, the negative impact of psychological factors, such as anxiety and depression, on LBP pain intensity and disability levels became more pronounced, with the OR increasing from 1.42 pre-pandemic to 1.73 during the pandemic (p < 0.001) [41].

Recent evidence suggests that COVID-19’s potential impact on low back pain may go beyond behavioural and psychological factors. Viral neuroinvasiveness and neuroimmune responses are possible biological mechanisms. SARS-CoV-2 may enter neural tissues by binding to angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptors on host cells. This may lead to abnormalities in the central and peripheral nervous systems and alter pain perception [42]. Virus-related neuroinflammation, glial activation, and disrupted nociceptive signalling may lead to abnormal pain modulation and central sensitization. As a result, these processes may be associated with chronic pain syndromes and worsening low back pain [43, 44].

Gender and high-risk population

Studies have shown that women exhibit significant differences compared to men in pain perception, reporting, and coping strategies. Women are more likely to report pain, seek medical attention, and demonstrate higher pain sensitivity [45]. This gender difference is also evident in the burden of LBP: women experience higher total DALYs and age-standardized rates than men [46, 47]. Similar patterns appear in other musculoskeletal disorders, including neck pain and rheumatoid arthritis [36, 37]. Several factors—such as physiological differences, occupational division, and social role disparities—may contribute to this phenomenon. The study indicates that this disparity in LBP burden spans all age groups, with the most pronounced difference observed in the 50–54 age group, a finding that aligns with previous research [19]. This may relate to the decline in estrogen levels in postmenopausal women, which triggers physiological changes such as increased osteoporosis and a higher risk of spine-related disorders [38, 39]. Supporting this pattern, a study on neck pain burden found that neck pain prevalence for women also peaked in the 50–54 age group [40].

Moreover, occupational division is critical to understanding gender differences in LBP. Women are more likely to be employed in sales, service, and clerical jobs, which often require prolonged standing or sustained postures, thereby significantly increasing the risk of LBP. In contrast, men are more likely to work in equipment operation, mechanical work, and physical labour, where the physical load may be higher; however, men tend to have greater muscle mass and strength, which can offer some protection against musculoskeletal strain [40, 41]. LBP has been identified as a significant health issue among the working-age population, especially in healthcare settings [48, 49]. Nurses, as the core workforce in healthcare, have a notably high prevalence of LBP [50]. Studies show that 72.9% of female nurses have experienced at least one day of LBP, and 36.2% have sought medical treatment due to the pain [51].

The nursing workforce is predominantly female in many countries, increasing the prevalence of LBP among women. Household responsibilities further contribute to this burden [52]. These findings highlight the impact of physiological differences and occupational roles on gender disparities in LBP. To address this, provide high-risk female occupational groups with ergonomically designed workstations, appropriate equipment, and improved work environments. Additionally, minimize strain from prolonged standing and repetitive tasks to help alleviate LBP in women.

For high-risk working populations, such as those in low SDI regions and female workers, evidence-based intervention strategies can provide practical guidance. In low SDI regions, measures such as conducting workplace ergonomic assessments and improvements, providing lumbar support devices, optimizing work processes, and implementing training and education programs can effectively reduce the risk of LBP and mitigate its burden [53, 54]. For high-risk female workers, policy-based mandatory measures, such as limiting lifting, can further reduce lumbar load [55]. In addition, empirically supported exercise interventions—such as lumbar stabilization training and lower extremity strength exercises—can enhance lumbar muscle strength and stability, further reducing the risk of LBP [56]. These targeted measures provide policymakers and workplace managers with actionable guidance to reduce the burden of LBP among workers in low SDI regions and high-risk female populations.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, although the GBD database provides valuable data for assessing global disease burden, its estimates are based on modeled data from multiple heterogeneous sources. This approach may introduce statistical uncertainty and potential estimation bias. Differences in data collection, regional coverage, and potential gaps could also affect the accuracy of the results. Second, within the GBD framework, exposure to OEF is a population-level composite. The database does not provide globally comparable, occupation-stratified estimates (e.g., healthcare workers, manual laborers, office workers). Nor does it allow distinctions among specific ergonomic risks such as prolonged sitting or heavy lifting. In addition, exposure estimates are derived from modeled risk attribution rather than direct individual-level measurement. This method may lead to misclassification of exposure. Third, the study could not conduct occupation-specific stratified analyses or evaluate individual ergonomic risks. This study focuses on global and regional–level analyses within the SDI. It does not examine country- or subnational-level differences. This scale may obscure regional heterogeneity from urban–rural disparities, economic structures, or occupational distributions. Finally, multiple stratified analyses across age, sex, and SDI groups were performed in this study. Although these analyses were conducted within a unified analytical framework, no formal multiple-testing correction was applied. This omission may increase the risk of type I error when interpreting individual subgroup results. Future research using country-level or subnational datasets with more detailed occupational and exposure information is needed to better inform targeted occupational health interventions.

Conclusion

LBP is a major global public health issue. Despite a decline in age-standardized DALY rates, the burden on the working-age population remains significant, with total DALYs continuing to rise. Gender, age, and regional disparities are pronounced, especially among females, low-SDI regions, and the 40–64 age group. LBP thus requires ongoing global attention and effective response. Future efforts should raise public and policymaker awareness of LBP caused by OEF, emphasizing gender, age, and regional differences. Interventions should include tailored ergonomic programs in workplaces, educational campaigns directed at high-risk groups, and policy measures focused on occupational health in low-SDI regions. These targeted strategies aim to reduce the future burden of LBP.
