Betel nut chewing influences pathologic response in oral squamous cell carcinoma treated by neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy
Authors: Ran Gao, Xu Zhang, Gang Li, Peng Li, Wei Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBetel nut chewing (BNC) is a prevalent risk factor for oral squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) in South and Southeast Asia, yet its influence on response to neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy remains unexplored. This study aimed to evaluate the association between BNC and pathologic response in oral SCC patients receiving neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 192 patients with primary oral SCC who received neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (docetaxel, cisplatin, and PD-1 inhibitors) followed by surgery between January 2020 and October 2024. Pathologic complete response (pCR), major pathologic response (mPR), objective response rate (ORR), clinical to pathological downstaging, adverse pathologic features, and disease-free survival (DFS) were compared between BNC (n = 42) and no-BNC (n = 150) groups.ResultsBNC patients demonstrated significantly lower pCR (16.7% vs. 34.0%; OR = 2.55, p = 0.031) and mPR rates (54.8% vs. 74.7%; OR = 3.17, p = 0.013) compared to no-BNC patients. Multivariable analysis confirmed absence of BNC as an independent predictor of both pCR (adjusted OR = 2.55, 95% CI: 1.20–7.65) and mPR (adjusted OR = 3.17, 95% CI: 1.32–8.53). BNC was also associated with lower ORR (73.8% vs. 86.7%, p = 0.045), reduced downstaging (71.4% vs. 90.0%, p = 0.002), and higher prevalence of adverse pathologic features (19.0% vs. 4.7%, p = 0.005). The poorest outcomes occurred in well/moderately differentiated tumors with BNC history. BNC did not significantly impact DFS (3-year DFS: 71.2% vs. 78.4%, p = 0.194). Number of neoadjuvant cycles did not influence pathologic response.ConclusionsBNC is an independent predictor of suboptimal pathologic response to neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy in oral SCC, although it does not adversely affect DFS. These findings may inform patient selection and treatment stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16348-w.Keywords: Oral squamous cell carcinoma, Neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy, Betel nut chewing, Cycle of neoadjuvant therapy, Pathologic response

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is the most common malignancy of the head and neck region. Its primary risk factors include tobacco use, alcohol consumption, betel nut chewing (BNC), and infections with HPV and EBV [1]. BNC is particularly prevalent in South and Southeast Asia, accounting for over half of oral and oropharyngeal SCC cases in these regions [2]. Worldwide, approximately 600 million individuals chew areca nut, making it the fifth most commonly used psychoactive substance after alcohol, nicotine, caffeine, and cannabis [3]. The dangers of BNC arise from both the physical irritation of coarse betel nut fibers and the chemical damage caused by arecoline.

The current standard of care for locally advanced resectable oral SCC typically involves upfront surgery followed by adjuvant radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy. However, this approach is associated with significant morbidity and suboptimal functional outcomes, particularly when extensive resections or flap reconstructions are required [4]. Notably, BNC-associated oral SCC may exhibit distinct biological behavior compared to tumors arising from tobacco or alcohol use. BNC-related tumors show low expression of Ki-67 and p53 proteins [5], poor responses to induction chemotherapy, and diminished progression-free survival and overall survival [6]. The mechanisms underlying this suboptimal response are not fully elucidated but may involve unique genetic mutation profiles and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment characterized by cancer-associated fibroblast populations that limit drug penetration and immune cell infiltration [7]. These factors collectively justify the exploration of more effective strategies in BNC-associated oral SCC.

Despite the survival advantage offered by immunotherapy in recurrent or metastatic head and neck SCC [8], and the promising outcomes of neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy trials [9–12], little is known about factors that influence pathologic responses in this setting. Specifically, no study has systematically evaluated the impact of BNC on pathologic response to neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy. A earlier report [6] has described a BNC history was associated with lower response rates to docetaxel, cisplatin, and fluorouracil (TPF) induction chemotherapy, and lymph node density but did not directly address BNC’s influence on treatment response. However, the TPF regimen is seldom used as a standalone neoadjuvant treatment in contemporary practice due to its limited survival benefit, and the role of BNC in the era of immunochemotherapy remains unexplored. To our knowledge, the present study is the first to specifically investigate the association between BNC and pathologic response to neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy in oral SCC, thereby addressing a significant gap in the literature.

Consequently, our objective was to evaluate the association between potential predictors with particular emphasis on BNC and pathologic response in oral SCC patients undergoing neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy.

Patients and methods

Ethical approval

This study received approval (No. 202533CGF) from Henan Cancer Hospital Institutional Research Committee, and written informed consent for medical research was obtained from all patients prior to the initiation of treatment. All methodologies were executed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines (Supplementary File 1).

Study design

To achieve our objective, we conducted a retrospective review of medical records from patients diagnosed with primary oral SCC who received neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy. A total of 220 patients were treated at a tertiary cancer hospital between January 2020 and October 2024. Of these, 20 cases were excluded because surgery was not performed: 15 patients declined surgical intervention after completing neoadjuvant therapy, 3 patients developed medical contraindications to surgery, and 2 patients were lost to follow-up before the planned procedure. An additional 8 cases were excluded due to a history of prior malignancies within the past 5 years. Following these exclusions, we meticulously extracted demographic, pathological, and treatment-related data from the remaining records.

Variable definition

Tumor and neck stages were established according to the 8th edition of the AJCC system. Pathological differentiation was categorized into three distinct classifications: well-differentiated, moderately differentiated, and poorly differentiated. Lymphovascular invasion (LVI) was considered positive when tumor cells were identifiable within the lymphatic vessels [13]. Perineural invasion (PNI) was acknowledged as present if tumor cells infiltrated nerve structures [14]. The evaluation of LVI and PNI was performed on hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections. Immunohistochemical staining was used to confirm ambiguous cases when necessary, whereas no routine immunohistochemical confirmation was performed for unequivocal hematoxylin and eosin findings. Extranodal extension (ENE) referred to the proliferation of cancer cells beyond the lymph node capsule into adjacent tissues [15]. The combined positive score (CPS) was employed to evaluate the proportion of PD-L1-positive tumor cells and infiltrating immune cells in relation to the total number of viable tumor cells [16]. Manual scoring was performed following standardized training and reference slides. Digital image analysis software (QuPath) was additionally employed for CPS quantification in a subset of cases to verify consistency.

All resected primary tumor specimens and regional lymph nodes were processed according to a standardized protocol. The tumor bed was serially sectioned at 3–5 mm intervals, mapped, and entirely submitted for histopathological evaluation. Hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections from all blocks were independently reviewed by two head and neck pathologists. pCR was defined as the absence of any residual viable tumor cells in the primary tumor bed and all resected lymph nodes. mPR was defined as ≤ 10% residual viable tumor cells in the primary tumor bed. In cases where tumor regression left only acellular debris, fibrosis, or foamy macrophages, no additional immunohistochemistry was routinely required. However, when scar-like areas or equivocal findings were present, immunohistochemical staining for pan-cytokeratin was performed to exclude scattered residual tumor cells [17]. No imaging studies (CT, MRI, or PET-CT) were used to define pCR or mPR. Secondary outcome variables encompassed the objective response rate (ORR), clinical to pathological downstaging, adverse pathologic features of PNI, LVI, and ENE, and disease-free survival (DFS). The objective response rate was defined as the percentage of patients achieving a predetermined level of tumor response, which includes complete response and partial response as assessed by the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors [18]. DFS indicated the duration following neoadjuvant therapy during which a patient remained free from disease recurrence or progression.

Data evaluation

All pathological responses and adverse pathological features were independently assessed by two experienced head and neck pathologists. Any disagreement between the two evaluators was resolved by consensus or, if necessary, by adjudication from a third senior pathologist. Inter-rater agreement between the two independent pathologists was excellent (κ = 0.85 for pCR and κ = 0.82 for mPR). For ORR, two independent radiologists, reviewed all pre- and post-neoadjuvant imaging studies. Discrepancies were resolved through joint review and consensus.Inter-rater agreement for ORR assessment was substantial (κ = 0.83).

Treatment protocol

The neoadjuvant therapeutic regimen comprised docetaxel administered at a dose of 75 mg/m², cisplatin at 75 mg/m², and Pembrolizumab or Sintilimab or Penpulimab or Tislelizumab at a dose of 200 mg of each three-week cycle, typically spanning two to three cycles. Surgical intervention was scheduled within one to four weeks following the conclusion of the neoadjuvant regimen. Surgical plans and resection margins were predetermined based on baseline evaluations conducted prior to the initiation of neoadjuvant therapy, remaining unaltered regardless of therapeutic response. Subsequent adjuvant therapy commenced within six weeks post-surgery, targeting the tumor bed with a margin of 1–2 cm. Adjuvant chemotherapy was determined based on clinical judgment and pathological characteristics, generally involving cisplatin administered over a duration of 4–6 cycles at a dose of 75 mg/m².

Statistical analysis

Primary outcomes were compared utilizing the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Categorical secondary outcomes were analyzed employing the same statistical tests, while survival-related secondary outcomes were assessed through univariate and Cox regression models, with results presented as hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 3.4.4, with a significance threshold set at a p-value of less than 0.05.

Results

Baseline data

In total, there were 144 male patients and 48 female patients. A history of alcohol consumption and tobacco smoking was recorded in 103 and 121 individuals, respectively. The primary anatomical sites were distributed as follows: the tongue in 72 patients, the floor of the mouth in 44 patients, the buccal mucosa in 43 patients, and the gingiva in 33 patients. The majority of cases (n = 129, 67.2%) were classified as stage III. Pathological differentiation was categorized as well-differentiated in 47 patients, moderately differentiated in 106 patients, and poorly differentiated in 39 patients. A total of 114 patients underwent two cycles of neoadjuvant therapy, while 78 patients received three cycles. The mean Ki67 index was 19.7% ± 9.9%. Among the entire cohort, 42 patients had a history of BNC, all of whom were male and demonstrated a younger age compared to those without a BNC habit (Supplementary Table 1, p < 0.001). Negative surgical margins were achieved in the entire cohort.

All patients underwent flap reconstruction, with 113 cases utilizing free flaps and 79 utilizing pedicled flaps. In the postoperative specimens, PNI was observed in 13 patients, LVI occurred in 10 patients, and ENE was identified in 8 patients.

Primary outcome

pCR was observed in 7 patients (16.7%) within the BNC population, compared with 51 patients (34.0%) in the no-BNC group (risk difference = 17.3% points; OR = 2.55, 95% CI: 1.20–7.65; Fig. 1, p = 0.031). The overall mPR rate was 70.3%, declining to 54.8% in the BNC cohort versus 74.7% in the no-BNC group (risk difference = 19.9% points; OR = 3.17, 95% CI: 1.32–8.53; Fig. 1, p = 0.013).

Pathologic complete response (pCR) and major pathologic response (mPR) in patients with or without betel nut chewing (BNC)

To further elucidate the independent impact of the BNC habit, multivariable analysis was conducted. In univariate analysis, age, sex, and pathological differentiation were found to be associated with both pCR and mPR; these variables, along with the BNC habit, were subsequently assessed in the multivariable analysis. Both poor differentiation and the BNC habit exhibited independent effects on pCR and mPR. Multivariable analysis confirmed that absence of BNC was independently associated with a 1.5-fold higher odds of pCR (adjusted OR = 2.55, 95% CI: 1.20–7.65) and a 3.2-fold higher odds of mPR (adjusted OR = 3.17, 95% CI: 1.32–8.53) relative to the BNC group (Tables 1 and 2).

Secondary outcome

The ORR was 73.8% (31/42) in the BNC group versus 86.7% (130/150) in the no-BNC group (risk difference = 12.9% points; OR = 2.34, 95% CI: 0.78–7.88; Fig. 2, p = 0.045). Clinical to pathological downstaging occurred in 30 patients (71.4%) in the BNC cohort versus 135 patients (90.0%) in the no-BNC cohort (risk difference = 18.6% points; OR = 3.00, 95% CI: 1.25–9.06; Fig. 2, p = 0.002). Adverse pathologic features (PNI, LVI, or ENE) were present in 8 BNC patients (19.0%) compared with 7 no-BNC patients (4.7%) (risk difference = 14.3% points; OR = 1.87, 95% CI: 0.27–7.66; Fig. 2, p = 0.005).

Further multivariable analysis indicated that the BNC habit was independently associated with clinical to pathological downstaging, but not with ORR or adverse pathological features. Compared to the BNC group, no-BNC patients had an OR of 3.00 (95% CI: 1.25–9.06) (Table 3). The results of other factors are delineated in Supplementary Tables 2–4.

In our follow-up, with a median duration of 2.9 years, we noted 46 adverse events. The 3-year DFS rate was 71.2% (95% CI: 56.8–85.6%) in the BNC group versus 78.4% (95% CI: 71.2–85.6%) in the no-BNC group (Fig. 3, log-rank p = 0.194). Multivariable analysis confirmed poor differentiation (adjusted HR = 2.01, 95% CI: 1.11–3.64, p = 0.021) and no pCR (adjusted HR = 2.18, 95% CI: 1.01–4.71, p = 0.047) as independent predictors of reduced DFS. BNC was not significantly associated with DFS in either univariate (HR = 1.42, 95% CI: 0.83–2.43, p = 0.194) or multivariable analysis (adjusted HR = 1.35, 95% CI: 0.77–2.37, p = 0.298) (Supplementary Table 5).

Subgroup analysis

Both poor differentiation and the absence of the BNC habit emerged as independent predictors of superior primary outcomes. We subsequently delved into the interaction between differentiation and BNC habits. The highest incidences (55.2% and 82.6%, respectively) of pCR and mPR were observed in patients with poorly differentiated cancer who did not engage in BNC, while the lowest rates (12.5% and 50.0%, respectively) of these outcomes were noted in patients with well or moderately differentiated cancer who participated in BNC. This disparity was statistically significant (Fig. 3, p = 0.004 for pCR and p = 0.006 for mPR, respectively).

Interaction analyses revealed no significant effect modification by smoking (p for interaction = 0.42 for pCR and 0.38 for mPR) or drinking status (p for interaction = 0.56 for pCR and 0.51 for mPR), indicating that the association between BNC and poor pathologic response was consistent across strata of tobacco and alcohol use.

Discussion

Our most noteworthy finding was that, in oral SCC, the BNC habit predicted a suboptimal response to neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy. This was reflected in lower rates of pCR, mPR, ORR, and clinical-to-pathological downstaging, as well as a higher frequency of adverse features. Importantly, however, BNC was not associated with reduced DFS. Moreover, neither Ki-67 expression nor the number of neoadjuvant therapy cycles influenced the pathological response. To our knowledge, this study may represent the first effort to elucidate factors affecting pathological response in oral SCC, highlighting key predictors and offering valuable insights for identifying patients most suitable for neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy.

The BNC habit is widely recognized as a significant risk factor for oral SCC; nevertheless, many young individuals remain ensnared by its allure due to a myriad of factors [19]. In a cohort study of 228 patients with oral SCC [20], a pronounced prevalence was observed among males, with the most commonly affected age group being those under 50 years. Areca nut usage was reported in 59.6% of cases, and the buccal mucosa emerged as the predominant site of involvement. BNC use was significantly associated with male gender, larger tumor size, increased depth of invasion, higher tumor and nodal stage, PNI, and a greater likelihood of recurrence. Similarly, another investigation [5] found that 46.6% of patients had a BNC habit, with 67.0% diagnosed with either tongue or buccal cancer. Notably, tumor location and degree of differentiation rather than Ki-67 expression were significantly correlated with the BNC habit, a finding that our own analysis corroborated. However, the impact of BNC on neoadjuvant therapy remains largely unclear. Wang et al. [21] sought to elucidate the relationship between lymph node density and chemotherapy response in a cohort from a BNC-endemic region with head and neck SCC. Among the subjects, 51 had their largest lymph node classified as hypodense, while 63 exhibited ENE in the associated lymph nodes. Notably, 59 patients reported a history of betel quid chewing. The chemotherapy response rate in lymph nodes was 35% (7 of 20) for the isodense group compared to 47.1% (24 of 51) for the hypodense group (P = 0.36). Nevertheless, the study did not address the specific influence of BNC on these associations. In a retrospective analysis of 162 smoking patients with head and neck SCC who underwent induction chemotherapy with TPF, Su et al. [6] reported overall response rates of 57% in patients with a BNC history versus 77% in those without (P = 0.038). The 2-year progression-free survival rates were 37% and 67% (P = 0.004), and the 2-year overall survival rates were 47% and 71% (P = 0.017), respectively. This study was pioneering in revealing the detrimental impact of betel nut consumption on induction chemotherapy. However, the TPF regimen is seldom used as a standalone preoperative treatment in contemporary practice due to its limited survival benefit.

Immunochemotherapy has garnered considerable attention for its promising role in the neoadjuvant setting, a notion validated by multiple clinical trials. Huang et al. [9] prospectively enrolled 20 patients, achieving a remarkable 100% completion rate for neoadjuvant therapy and subsequent R0 resection. The mPR rate was recorded at 60%, inclusive of a 30% pCR. Notably, mPR was attained in all four patients exhibiting a combined positive score of PD-L1 greater than 10. During a median follow-up of 23 months, disease-free survival reached an impressive 90%, while overall survival stood at 95%. In another trial involving 48 patients [12], 27 proceeded to surgical resection, while the remaining 21 received non-surgical therapy. The ORR among the 48 patients who completed neoadjuvant therapy was 89.6%. Among the 27 patients who underwent surgical intervention, 17 achieved either mPR or pCR, yielding a pCR rate of 55.6%. These encouraging outcomes align with findings from other reports [17, 22]. However, these studies were limited by their small sample sizes, which consequently precluded analysis of potential predictors of pathological response. Our findings indicated that the BNC habit was associated with a striking 60% or greater reduction in the likelihood of achieving pCR or mPR, or in clinical to pathological downstaging. This observation aligns with existing literature [7, 23], pointing to two main factors that may clarify this suboptimal response: first, the genetic mutations driving the occurrence of oral SCC linked to BNC may differ from those associated with smoking- or alcohol-related oral SCC; second, the tumor microenvironment in patients who consume BNC may encompass a distinct, immunosuppressive population of cancer-associated fibroblasts that actively limit drug penetration and immune cell infiltration. But these mechanisms are not yet fully elucidated, rendering them compelling, albeit partially speculative, hypotheses for our findings. Furthermore, the BNC group demonstrated a higher prevalence of adverse pathologic features, indicating that not only is the overall response to neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy attenuated in these patients, but the residual disease may also harbor more aggressive characteristics. This dual disadvantage highlights the need for closer postoperative surveillance and potentially intensified adjuvant strategies in BNC-associated oral SCC.

Interestingly, we observed that the lowest rates of pCR and mPR occurred among patients with well or moderately differentiated oral SCC who also had a history of BNC consumption. This finding raises the hypothesis that this particular subgroup may derive limited benefit from neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy. However, to our knowledge, no published study including those from other populations or other head and neck cancer subtypes has specifically examined whether tumor differentiation modifies the effect of BNC on pathologic response to neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy. Therefore, while our observation is provocative, it should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing at this stage. We caution against extrapolating these results to exclude patients from neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy solely based on differentiation status and BNC history, given the limited sample size and the absence of external validation. Future prospective studies with larger, multi-institutional cohorts are needed to confirm whether this subgroup truly exhibits resistance.

In clinical practice, the number of neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy cycles administered to most patients with oral SCC is typically two or three, determined by anecdotal experience rather than established guidelines. Notably, no comparative studies have specifically evaluated the efficacy of two versus three cycles in oral SCC. However, this scientific question has garnered substantial attention in other solid malignancies. A recent investigation [24] involving 108 patients with non-small cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC) found that those receiving two cycles had a significantly smaller diagnostic radiological tumor size (37.0 mm vs. 49.6 mm, p = 0.022) and a higher radiological tumor regression rate (36% vs. 49%, p = 0.007) compared to those receiving more than two cycles. Nevertheless, no statistically significant difference in pathological tumor regression rate was observed between the two groups. Conversely, other evidence suggests a negative correlation regarding cycle number. In a study of 176 patients with NSCLC [25], ORRs for those receiving two, three, four, and five or more cycles were 52%, 67%, 53%, and 50%, respectively. Post hoc analysis indicated that the number of treatment cycles bore no significant association with mPR or pCR. Furthermore, treatment cycles did not affect operational metrics such as operating time, postoperative drainage, or length of hospital stay. A meta-analysis [26] elucidated that neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy was associated with improved 2-year DFS and higher pCR rates in experimental versus control arms, with this association remaining unaffected by tumor- or treatment-related factors, including PD-L1 status, type of platinum-based chemotherapy, or number of cycles in NSCLC. Our study aligns with this perspective, demonstrating that the number of neoadjuvant therapy cycles does not significantly influence any outcome variables in oral SCC. Consequently, our findings provide initial evidence that two cycles may represent a rational and effective choice in this context.

The limitations of the current study must be acknowledged. Firstly, there exists an inherent selection bias characteristic of retrospective studies. Secondly, although we adjusted for alcohol consumption and tobacco smoking in our multivariable analysis, the concomitant use of these substances with BNC represents a potential source of residual confounding that could influence the interpretation of our results. The distinct contributions of each exposure to the suboptimal pathologic response cannot be fully disentangled in this cohort. Thirdly, we cannot exclude the possibility of unmeasured or unanalyzed confounding factors, such as detailed comorbidity indices, nutritional status, or socioeconomic variables, which may independently affect treatment tolerance and pathologic outcomes. Fourthly, our sample size, particularly within the BNC cohort (n = 42), was relatively modest, limiting the statistical power for some subgroup analyses. Lastly, this investigation was conducted at a single institution in a region where BNC is endemic, necessitating external validation in diverse populations with different risk factor profiles before broader clinical applicability can be established.

In conclusion, within the context of oral squamous cell carcinoma, BNC serves as a predictor of a suboptimal response to neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy, evidenced by diminished rates of pCR, mPR, ORR, and clinical to pathological downstaging as well as a higher prevalence of adverse pathologic features; importantly, however, this did not adversely impact disease-free survival. Furthermore, the number of neoadjuvant therapy cycles appeared to exert minimal influence on pathological response.
