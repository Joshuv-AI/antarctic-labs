A dynamic perspective on clinical treatment evaluation using dynamic structural equation modeling
Authors: Shuncheng He, Wooyeol Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConventional evaluations of psychological treatment effects have primarily focused on changes in average symptom levels, thereby overlooking intra-individual variability. However, evidence from intensive longitudinal data demonstrates that psychological states fluctuate dynamically over time, suggesting that treatment effects may manifest not only in mean levels but also in temporal instability. It is imperative to acknowledge the impact of variability on treatment efficacy, particularly in the context of clinical intervention research. Failure to account for this variability can lead to the misinterpretation of treatment outcomes, potentially compromising the accuracy of research findings.MethodsThe proposed methodology, under a randomized controlled trial design, is intended to facilitate the dynamic assessment of clinical treatment. The present method is based on a dynamic structural equation model framework, which allows individual-specific residual variances to be contingent on treatment allocation, thereby facilitating the direct evaluation of the impact of treatment on psychological instability. The proposed framework is illustrated with an empirical case, and the performance of dynamic treatment assessment parameters is systematically evaluated under different conditions using Monte Carlo simulations.ResultsEmpirical evidence has demonstrated the efficacy of the dynamic structural equation model in capturing variations in dynamic treatment effects between experimental and control groups. The simulation results further indicate that this method provides accurate parameter recovery and stable estimates under various conditions, thereby supporting its robustness and flexibility for simulating dynamic treatment effects in intensive longitudinal data settings.ConclusionsThe present study proposes a novel perspective on dynamic treatment evaluation based on a dynamic structural equation model framework. By conceptualizing residual variance as a dynamic outcome, this framework facilitates a more nuanced comprehension of clinical change and offers a significant extension to conventional treatment-evaluation methods in randomized controlled trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-026-02921-1.

Background

In recent years, the widespread use of electronic devices and the rapid development of mobile health technologies have made the collection of intensive longitudinal data (ILD) much more convenient in clinical settings [1]. Compared with traditional longitudinal designs, ILD allows for much more frequent assessments over short periods of time [2]. This enables researchers not only to capture trends in symptoms or momentary experiences but also to observe dynamic fluctuations within individuals (Fig. 1). Such within-person dynamics may indicate substantial changes in treatment adherence or increasing physiological instability. Therefore, clinical evaluations that rely primarily on changes in mean symptom levels may be insufficient to fully capture treatment-related processes.

When dynamic processes are considered in clinical research, temporal instability has typically been quantified using summary indices such as overall autoregressive parameters [3] and the mean squared successive difference (MSSD) [4]. These indicators have been widely applied in clinical and medical studies based on ILD, including assessments of affective and behavioral instability [5, 6]. However, temporal instability is not a unidimensional construct. Prior research has shown that instability can be decomposed into at least two distinct but complementary components, namely temporal persistence, and fluctuation magnitude [7, 8]. Temporal persistence is captured by autoregressive dynamics and reflects the extent to which deviations from an individual’s typical level carry over across time points. In contrast, fluctuation magnitude, commonly referred to as amplitude, reflects the intensity of short-term deviations around an individual’s typical level that are not predictable from prior states. Although both components relate to instability, they represent conceptually distinct dimensions with different predictive implications for health outcomes. Specifically, temporal dependency is more strongly associated with chronic health conditions, whereas fluctuation amplitude is more closely related to subjective somatic complaints [8]. Accordingly, amplitude can be regarded as a clinically meaningful form of within-individual variability, and reductions in amplitude, such as attenuated emotional or physiological reactivity, may indicate improved treatment response [9].

However, conventional statistical models generally assume constant residual variance, a limitation that curtails their ability to detect dynamic characteristics. When interpreted dynamically, residual variance represents the amplitude of short-term deviations from an individual’s expected level [4], with larger residual variance corresponding to greater fluctuation intensity. Whereas residual variance captures the magnitude of short-term, unpredictable fluctuations around an individual’s expected level, temporal dependency is governed by autoregressive dynamics, which reflect the degree of carryover from prior states. As demonstrated in Fig. 2, increases in residual variance result in larger amplitudes over time, underscoring the importance of modeling residual variance as an informative outcome to capture clinically relevant dynamics.

Dynamic structural equation modeling (DSEM) has been introduced in the field of psychology as a Bayesian hierarchical modeling 1framework for analyzing intensive longitudinal data [10–13]. This approach allows both the mean (location) and residual variance (scale) of outcomes to be modeled simultaneously. This methodological framework facilitates the examination of treatment effects not only on mean levels but also on amplitude, defined as residual variance. Whereas conventional mixed models for repeated measures (MMRM), commonly used in confirmatory clinical trials, primarily target mean trajectories across discrete follow-up visits and treat time as a design factor, DSEM is rooted in a time-series perspective in which dynamic process is explicitly modeled as a core feature of the design.

By linking the log-transformed residual variance to predictors through a structural equation, DSEM offers a flexible hierarchical framework for capturing intra-individual variability and clinical. This hierarchical formulation allows residual variance to be modeled as an outcome, rather than as a fixed nuisance parameter, thereby enabling direct inference on dynamic instability. Although this approach has not yet been widely applied in clinical and medical research, its univariate specification is consistent with the mixed-effects location–scale modeling (MELSM) framework [2]. The integration of time-series analysis with multilevel modeling is a methodological approach employed by DSEM, which enables the capture of both within- and between-individual variability. Modeling residual variance as a dynamic outcome provides a clinically meaningful way to capture the amplitude of treatment effects or moment-to-moment fluctuations in psychological states.

Although modeling residual variance as an index of amplitude and capturing dynamic treatment effects are theoretically relevant and practically feasible, their application remains limited in clinical research. Most conventional randomized controlled trials (RCTs), particularly those that employ infrequently spaced follow-up assessments, predominantly emphasize mean-level treatment effects at predefined primary endpoints. These trials frequently neglect to consider intra-individual variability. Conversely, trials that incorporate intensive longitudinal measurements provide an opportunity to examine dynamic or secondary outcomes reflecting within-person processes.

Recent work on distributional treatment effects [14] demonstrates that interventions can alter outcome variances and other distributional properties even when mean-level effects are modest, underscoring the importance of modeling residual variance to fully characterize treatment effects. Even within DSEM applications, emphasis is usually placed on modeling average trajectories and autoregressive effects, with little attention given to fluctuations in residual variance. Most extant work incorporates random residual variance (RRV) to enhance the accuracy of variance estimation or improve overall model fit [15]. However, there is a paucity of research that examines the substantive and clinical meaning of amplitude (i.e., residual variance) itself.

To address this gap, the present study adopts a DSEM framework to evaluate dynamic treatment effects in clinical settings and proposes residual variance (i.e., amplitude) as a clinically meaningful dynamic or secondary outcome metric in trials with intensive longitudinal designs. The DSEM is demonstrated to be a flexible method for estimating time-varying and group-specific dynamic effects. Empirical evidence shows that modeling changes in amplitude can reveal clinically meaningful dynamic treatment effects that may be overlooked in conventional clinical assessments. To assess the efficacy of the proposed model, a Monte Carlo simulation was employed to evaluate its performance under diverse conditions. This approach was undertaken to ascertain the circumstances under which dynamic treatment effects remain robust. This text provides practical guidelines for applied researchers seeking to use this approach in empirical studies.

Methods

By extending the DSEM and MLSM frameworks to include an RCT predictor at the between-individual scale level, this approach allows researchers not only to examine mean-level treatment effects but also to evaluate differences in amplitude variability between treatment and control groups. Such differences in variability serve as dynamic indicators of treatment effects.

Estimation

Conventional multilevel location-scale models are generally estimated using maximum marginal likelihood [17]. However, when a model includes a substantial number of random effects, maximum likelihood estimation often encounters convergence challenges, estimation instability, and difficulties with model identification, especially with limited sample sizes or high model complexity.

Therefore, this study employs the Bayesian Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method for model estimation. In comparison with conventional maximum likelihood estimation, Bayesian methods facilitate more stable and flexible inferences, particularly when the model incorporates multiple random effects or faces convergence difficulties [13]. The Bayesian MCMC framework offers a distinct advantage in its capacity to facilitate straightforward interval estimation for variance components. This functionality is frequently hindered by boundary issues and asymmetric sampling distributions, which are prevalent in maximum likelihood estimation. The incorporation of prior distributions into Bayesian MCMC facilitates the regularization of complex models and stabilizes parameter estimation in scenarios with limited sample sizes or elevated complexity [20]. Recent studies have demonstrated that MCMC-based Bayesian estimation is superior in recovering parameter values and provides more robust inference under conditions of non-normality or missing data [21].

All models in the present study were estimated in Mplus using the default Bayesian settings for DSEM [22]. Fixed-effect parameters were assigned diffuse normal priors, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N(0,\infty\:)$$\end{document}, while variance components, including random-effect variances and residual variances, were assigned noninformative inverse-gamma priors, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:IG(-\mathrm{1,0})$$\end{document}, as implemented by default in Mplus. For each model, two parallel Markov chains with distinct random seeds were executed, with each chain comprising 5,000 iterations. The initial iterations were designated as burn-in, in accordance with the default Bayesian procedure. Convergence was assessed using Mplus default diagnostics, including inspection of the potential scale reduction factor (PSR) and trace plots. All reported models showed satisfactory convergence, with PSR values close to 1 and stable trace behavior across chains.

Results

Simulation study

We conducted a Monte Carlo simulation to examine the performance of dynamic treatment effect modeling in RCT designs with ILD. Both models were then estimated for each simulated dataset, with the primary objective of evaluating their ability to recover the mean and dynamic treatment effects under varying data conditions. The simulation design was informed by previous studies on DSEM frameworks [12, 23] and adapted to the context of ILD and treatment evaluation.

Following these established frameworks, we implemented a fully crossed factorial design involving five manipulated factors (Table 2): sample size, number of time points, the between-group allocation ratio, RRV (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\tau\:}_{\sigma\:}^{2}\:$$\end{document}), and dynamic treatment effect (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\gamma\:}_{\sigma\:1}$$\end{document}). Specifically, sample sizes of N = 80 and N = 160 were selected based on prior methodological findings and the design of the present study. Preliminary studies indicate that DSEM generally necessitates sample sizes that exceed approximately N = 50 to attain sufficient statistical power and stable estimation [12]. Consequently, N = 80 was designated as a reasonable lower-bound condition, considering the RCT design and the possibility of between-group imbalance. To assess sensitivity to increased sample size, a commonly used doubling strategy was adopted, with N = 160 included as a comparison condition. Each participant was assessed at T = 15, 30, or 45 time points, corresponding to increasing measurement density. The minimum condition (T = 15) reflects empirical precedents in the DSEM literature, where dynamic parameters have been examined with short individual time series [7, 12]. The between-group allocation ratio was set to 1:1 and 0.3:0.7, representing balanced and moderately unbalanced group assignments commonly observed in RCTs. To account for inter-individual heterogeneity in within-individual variability, the RRV component was varied across two levels: 0.0 (homogeneous residual variance) and 0.4 (heterogeneity). The true dynamic treatment effect was specified at four levels (0, − 0.2, − 0.5, and − 0.8). These values were defined on the log-variance scale and correspond to no effect and progressively larger proportional reductions in within-individual amplitude (approximately 18%, 39%, and 55%, respectively), thereby capturing null, small, moderate, and substantively large dynamic treatment effects.

Simulation conditions

Crossing all levels of these factors yielded 96 simulation conditions. Eight hundred datasets were generated for each condition. The initial input data and all simulation outputs are available on the Open Science Framework (OSF) at https://osf.io/r3sbw/.

Evaluation

For each simulation condition, results from 800 replications were summarized from the Monte Carlo output generated by Mplus. For each parameter of interest, model performance was evaluated using five indices. Specifically, these indices capture aspects of parameter estimation accuracy, interval estimation quality, and inferential decision-making. The most critical parameter in this study is the dynamic treatment effect (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\gamma\:}_{\sigma\:1}$$\end{document}). The recovery of this parameter was evaluated using five performance indices: relative bias, root mean square error (RMSE), 95% coverage rate, Type I error rate, and power. The relative bias (RB) was evaluated across conditions, with values below 10% considered acceptable and values below 5% considered negligible [24]. RMSE, defined as the square root of the sum of the squared bias and variance, reflects the overall accuracy of the estimation. The magnitude of RMSE is inversely proportional to the precision of the recovery process. This makes it particularly useful for comparing conditions across varying sample sizes and time points.

The 95% coverage rate is defined as the proportion of replications in which the true parameter values fall within the estimated 95% confidence interval. Although Bayesian credible intervals do not guarantee nominal frequentist coverage, empirical coverage was examined as a diagnostic measure of interval performance across repeated samples, consistent with prior Bayesian Monte Carlo studies. A coverage rate ranging from 0.91 to 0.98 is considered acceptable [25]. The Type I error rate is defined as the proportion of replications in which a non-existent treatment effect is incorrectly detected. Finally, statistical power indicates the probability of correctly detecting a true dynamic effect. In accordance with established conventions, power values exceeding 0.80 and Type I error rates within the range [0.025, 0.075] are regarded as indicative of acceptable inference [26, 27].

Results

Application to a health study example

This study draws on ecological momentary assessment (EMA) intervention research [28], which examined the effects of feedback-based interventions on emotional experiences in patients with depression, to illustrate the clinical importance of amplitude as an outcome indicator. The present analysis is secondary, and all affect measures followed the original measurement protocol. At each EMA assessment, participants rated affect-related adjectives on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = “not” to 7 = “very”), yielding polytomous responses. Negative affect was operationalized as a composite score based on the mean of four items (lonely, anxious, guilty, suspicious), which formed a negative affect factor in the original study. Measurement error was not modeled explicitly in the present analysis.

This study used EMA data collected during the five days following treatment completion, with a particular emphasis on changes in negative affect. Participants were permitted to report their emotions at any time, resulting in unequal numbers of observations across individuals. The mean number of reports in the control group (n = 24) was 22.2 (SD = 13.4), whereas the mean number of reports in the treatment group (n = 25) was 19.8 (SD = 11.9). Participants were randomly assigned to either the treatment or control group. At the observation level, the control group exhibited a mean negative affect score of 2.36 (SD = 1.29), while the treatment group demonstrated a lower mean score of 2.13 (SD = 1.38).

DSEM requires data in long format, which is particularly advantageous because it allows the inclusion of participants with unequal measurement intervals and varying numbers of observations. All analyses were conducted using Mplus 8.7 [22]. Missing values were coded as 999 and treated as unknown quantities within the Bayesian estimation framework; they were sampled using a Gibbs sampler with Metropolis–Hastings steps [10].

Notwithstanding this limitation, the dynamic treatment effect on within-individual amplitude was statistically significant, indicating a reliable treatment-related difference in symptom variability over time. Instead of suggesting that mean-level effects are inconsequential, this pattern indicates that evaluations based exclusively on mean symptom changes may offer a limited representation of treatment-related processes. Consequently, the integration of dynamic assessments with mean-level effects may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the manifestation and evolution of intervention effects over time.

Supplementary simulation analysis

To further evaluate the performance of the proposed DSEM in estimating dynamic treatment effects under conditions comparable to the empirical application, we conducted a supplementary simulation study informed by the characteristics of the health study data. The sample size was set at N = 50. The simulation design was consistent with the model specification of the primary simulation study, with a particular emphasis on evaluating the estimation accuracy and robustness of the mean treatment effect and the dynamic treatment effect on within-individual amplitude.

The results of this supplementary simulation are summarized in Appendix B. The findings indicate that, except for conditions characterized by a minimal number of measurement occasions (T = 15) and the most negligible dynamic treatment effect (– 0.2), the proposed model yielded stable parameter estimates and reliable inference. It is important to note that as the magnitude of the dynamic treatment effect increased, adequate statistical power was achieved even under small-sample conditions (N = 50) and a limited number of measurement point (T = 15). These results provide further support for the applicability of the DSEM framework in practical clinical research settings.

Discussion

This study extends the DSEM to advance the evaluation of psychological dynamic treatment effects in RCTs. While traditional approaches to treatment evaluation have predominantly focused on mean-level changes over time, the DSEM offers a more comprehensive framework by simultaneously modeling within-individual variability (i.e., amplitude) as an indicator of dynamic change. This method allows us to examine not only whether a treatment reduces symptom severity but also whether it contributes to symptom or psychological stability at the individual level.

The DSEM incorporates individual-specific residual variance into the hierarchical structure of time-series data, providing a novel avenue for investigating treatment-related changes in temporal fluctuations. Changes in within-individual variability, such as reduced short-term fluctuations, may reflect clinically meaningful improvements in disease stability. For example, these changes may indicate more consistent symptom control or reduced physiological lability over time. This perspective acknowledges that treatment efficacy is not solely defined by reductions in symptom means. It is also defined by increased predictability and stability of clinical processes over time. By explicitly modeling dynamic treatment effects on variability, the present study enables the examination of whether interventions attenuate the amplitude of fluctuations rather than merely shifting average outcome levels. In this way, the proposed approach provides a more nuanced and ecologically valid assessment of treatment effects within the DSEM framework, particularly for ILD observed in clinical research.

Practical recommendations

Data sufficiency

When a new model is proposed, researchers are often concerned with the amount of data required to use it. The simulation results emphasize the importance of sample size (N) and measurement frequency (T) in accurately estimating and detecting dynamic treatment effects. Across all evaluation criteria—including model selection accuracy, parameter recovery, and statistical power—larger N and T values consistently improved performance. Specifically, increasing N and T reduces estimation error (RMSE) and increases the power to detect moderate treatment effects. Based on the results of the simulation study, we suggest that more than 1,200 data points are needed in equal-sized RCT groups to detect moderate treatment efficacy and individual heterogeneity (i.e., N = 80, T = 15).

In addition, in contrast to balanced RCT group allocations, unequal group sizes resulted in higher RMSE and lower power. However, increasing the overall sample size partially mitigated these adverse effects.

Asymmetric compensation between N and T

The simulation results indicated an asymmetric compensatory relationship between sample size (N) and the number of time points (T) in recovering the dynamic treatment effect parameter. In the context of residual variance heterogeneity (RRV = 0.4), the findings indicated that an increase in T resulted in a consistent reduction in RMSE, particularly in scenarios where sample size was constrained. To illustrate, the RMSE decreased from approximately 0.166 at N = 80, T = 15 to 0.156 and 0.154 at T = 30 and T = 45, respectively. Furthermore, upon attaining a moderate sample size (N = 160), subsequent increases in T resulted in enhanced estimation precision, as evidenced by a decline in RMSE from approximately 0.114 at T = 30 to 0.105 at T = 45.

A similar pattern was observed in the statistical power analysis. In a limited number of instances (T = 15), increasing the sample size from N = 80 to N = 160 modestly enhanced power. However, when a constant sample size of N = 80 was maintained, an increase in T from 15 to 30 and 45 resulted in substantial increases in power. The findings indicate that enhancing temporal density can significantly improve estimation precision and statistical power in detecting dynamic treatment effects, potentially compensating for reduced sample sizes.

Heterogeneity in dynamic processes

The increasing availability of time-series data has enabled researchers to examine dynamic processes in unprecedented detail, thereby attracting renewed attention to individual-level heterogeneity [30, 31]. However, in much of the clinical medicine literature, discussions of individual differences in treatment response remain largely focused on average treatment effects [16, 32], with limited attention paid to heterogeneity in intra-individual variability or changes in within-individual amplitude over time. In our study, we modeled residual variance as a random effect (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\tau\:}_{\sigma\:}^{2}$$\end{document}), which allowed for variability in intra-individual fluctuations across individuals. This approach not only relaxes the assumption of strict homogeneity but also enables the examination of potential issues that may arise when individual heterogeneity is ignored. Our findings indicate that the degree of individual variability moderates the treatment effect, supporting the argument that dynamic processes often differ across individuals and, therefore, require appropriate modeling strategies. We further hope that this approach can be more widely adopted in clinical treatment evaluation, thereby enabling a more comprehensive understanding of treatment effects.

Limitations and future directions

While this study contributes methodologically to modeling dynamic treatment effects, several limitations remain. First, the current modeling approach relies on between-group comparisons within an RCT framework. Although randomization is increasingly used in intervention studies, non-randomized single-arm trials remain common in many fields [33]. For instance, Rupp et al. (2019) employed EMA to assess treatment sensitivity by comparing pre- and post-intervention symptom levels in individuals with obsessive-compulsive disorder [34]. Future research could extend dynamic treatment effect modeling to non-randomized single-arm designs by incorporating within-individual changes over time. Moreover, hybrid designs that integrate RCTs with single-arm trials may provide additional flexibility for applying dynamic treatment effect modeling in clinical settings.

Second, the Bayesian estimation in this study relies on the Mplus platform, which introduces certain limitations owing to its closed-source nature. Although Mplus provides stable estimation and user-friendly model specifications, its proprietary structure restricts transparency and extensibility. To enhance transparency and replicability, future research may consider migrating the model implementation to open-source platforms such as RStan [35], which allows greater flexibility in model customization and broader evaluation approaches, including posterior predictive p-values [20] and Leave-one-out cross-validation [36].

Third, a significant methodological consideration pertains to the identifiability and interpretation of residual variance. Residual variance may be indicative of true within-person process variability, as well as measurement error, alterations in reporting behavior, or unmodeled temporal structure. The interpretability of residual variance as an indicator of symptom stability is contingent upon the accuracy of model specification and the availability of sufficient observations per individual. In scenarios where per-subject time series are constrained, permitting heterogeneity in residual variance can enhance the complexity of the parameters and potentially diminish estimation precision, as evidenced by the simulation outcomes.

Furthermore, changes in residual variance do not exclusively reflect increased or decreased symptom stability. Such effects may arise from altered sensitivity to situational influences, differential reactivity to unmodeled time-varying factors, or heterogeneous treatment effects across individuals or covariates. Consequently, even in randomized controlled trials, variance effects should be interpreted with caution and not as definitive evidence of improved stability. The contribution of this study lies not in treating reduced variance as a stand-alone clinical conclusion, but in demonstrating that within-person variability represents a distinct and empirically testable dimension of treatment effects that is not captured by mean-based analyses alone. Variance effects are best regarded as complementary dynamic indicators that may signal changes in within-person fluctuation patterns. This suggests that further investigation of potential moderators and alternative model specifications is warranted in future research. More broadly, this perspective aligns with a growing methodological shift that moves beyond average treatment effects to examine structural or dynamic features of psychological processes. For instance, recent work in network psychometrics highlights that treatment may alter symptom connectivity or system organization rather than solely mean symptom levels [37–39].
