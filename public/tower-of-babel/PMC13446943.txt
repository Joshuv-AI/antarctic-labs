Genomic Insights into p53 Alterations and Chromosome 1p Anomalies for Prognostic Stratification in Childhood Solid Tumors: A Pilot Study
Authors: Simmi K. Ratan, Nitin Jain, Dinesh Kumar, Nitesh Kumar Sharma, Sujoy Neogi, Rhea Ratan, Shasanka Shekhar Panda, Nita Khurana, Binita Goswami, Arka Banerjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:To analyze the frequency of p53 and 1p anomalies and to study the association of these genetic biomarkers with histopathological and clinical outcomes in childhood solid tumors.Materials and Methods:A pilot study was conducted on 28 children with solid tumors up to 12 years of age. Participants were subjected to a needle biopsy. The material was divided into two parts: one placed in saline, for studying 1p loss of heterozygosity (LOH); other placed in formalin fixed, for histopathological examination and p53 analysis. Clinical, histopathological, and outcome status were noted. Statistical methods were applied. Data were also analyzed as any positive (either positive) or no positive (neither positive) to p53 and 1p LOH.Results:Among 28 pediatric patients, p53 positivity was seen in 10 (36%) cases and 1p LOH in 14 (50%) cases, with 6 (20%) showing both. p53 positivity appeared more frequent in children <4 years in an exploratory subgroup analysis (P = 0.024). Tumors <5 cm were predominantly p53 negative (P = 0.019), while 1p LOH was significantly associated with larger tumors (>5 cm; P = 0.019). p53(-) status was seen in well-differentiated histology (P = 0.041). Either p53 or 1p LOH positivity was significantly linked to large tumors, while other tumor characteristics did not show any significant association. Mortality (n = 2) and relapse (n = 3) were observed only in patients positive for both markers.Conclusion:The chromosome 1p anomalies were relatively more frequent than reported. 1p LOH showed a significant association with larger tumor size, while p53-negative status was more frequently observed in smaller tumors and well-differentiated histology. However, tumor growth and adverse outcomes appeared more frequent when both markers were present concurrently, suggesting a possible synergistic adverse effect that requires confirmation in larger studies.

INTRODUCTION

Childhood cancers are rare; however, they remain the second-leading cause of death among the pediatric population. The annual incidence is estimated at approximately 14–15 cases per 100,000 children, representing about 1%–1.5% of all cancers in the general population. Among these, solid tumors account for approximately 60% of all childhood malignancies.[1] In recent years, biomarkers have superseded the histopathology/radiological criteria to determine the outcomes of solid tumors.[2] Among the various molecular markers studied, alterations in the TP53 tumor suppressor gene and loss of heterozygosity (LOH) involving the short arm of chromosome 1 (1p) have emerged as significant genetic alterations associated with tumorigenesis and poor outcomes in several malignancies. The TP53 gene produces a protein p53, often termed the “guardian of the genome,” which plays a crucial role in maintaining genomic stability by regulating cell cycle arrest and apoptosis; its mutation leads to uncontrolled cell proliferation and aggressive tumor behavior.[34] Such mutations have been reported in multiple pediatric solid tumors, including Wilms’ tumor, neuroblastoma, and primitive neuroectodermal tumors (PNET).[5] Similarly, chromosomal anomalies involving 1p – such as deletions, duplications, or translocations – have been linked to pediatric tumors like Wilms’ tumor, embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma, and neuroblastoma, as they disrupt normal gene function and contribute to malignant transformation.[6]

Alterations of the TP53 gene and LOH of chromosome 1p have been extensively studied in varieties of adult malignancies, but data on childhood malignancies are still limited. Through this pilot study, we aim to delineate the pattern of p53 and chromosome 1p abnormalities in a small subset of childhood solid tumors and to evaluate their association with histopathological and clinical outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A pilot study was conducted following Institutional Ethics Committee clearance on consecutive children up to 12 years of age, admitted with solid tumors in the department of pediatric surgery at a tertiary care center over 2 years. Those with metastasis/recurrence/relapse or already on chemotherapy at presentation were excluded. Based on a previous study published in 1996 by Lahoti et al.[7] and assuming a prevalence (P) of 10%, the calculated sample size was 55; however, 28 patients were included as a convenient sample during the pilot study period. All patients underwent needle biopsy, and freshly obtained tumor tissue was divided into two parts: one part placed in normal saline and subjected to fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) for the assessment of chromosome 1p abnormalities using a dual-color ZytoLight® SPEC 1p36/1q25 probe, while the second part was processed for histopathological examination and genomic DNA extraction from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue using a fully automated extraction system, with DNA quality assessed spectrophotometrically [Figure 1]. FISH analysis was performed on 3–4 μm sections using standard protocols [Figure 2], with signal enumeration in 20 tumor nuclei and calculation of the 1p/1q ratio according to the established criteria, and known tissue used as a positive internal control. TP53 analysis was performed using commercially available kits with exon-specific primers targeting exon 6 and showing 100% sequence identity with the National Center for Biotechnology Information reference sequence NM_000546.6, limited to the detection of the presence or absence of genetic alterations. Tumor size was determined radiologically, histopathology was categorized into well-differentiated and non-well-differentiated groups (comprising moderately and poorly differentiated histology), and clinical variables, including age (≤2 years, >2 years), tumor size (≤5 cm, >5 cm), and lymph node status, were recorded for statistical analysis, with outcomes assessed at 1-year follow-up.

The collected data were entered and cleaned in MS Excel. Both “either positive” (any positive) and “neither positive” (none positive) values were also analyzed as the data had a limited size. Either positive included p53(+), 1p(+), or (p53[+] +1p[+]) and neither positive included p53(-) +1p(-). After cleaning, the data were imported into SPSS version 25 (IBM SPSS version 25.0 [IBM Corp. (2017). IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.]) for analysis. The quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. The qualitative data were presented as percentage and frequency. For each variable, one overall Fisher’s exact test was performed to assess the overall association with biomarker status, followed by category-specific exploratory comparisons within individual categories. These analyses were performed for exploratory and descriptive purposes. All P < 0.05 (calculated using Fisher’s exact test) were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

The distribution of p53 expression and 1p LOH among patients (n = 28) with various pediatric solid tumors in our study is depicted in Table 1. The overall incidence of p53 (n = 10) and 1p (n = 14) anomalies in our study was 36% and 50%, respectively. Six tumors (21%) were positive for both p53 and 1p LOH. There were 20 females and 8 males. Age at presentation ranged from 9 days to 11 years, with a mean age of 3.5 years. In children <2 years, p53-negative cases were more frequent than p53-positive cases. A similar trend was observed in children ≥2 years; however, overall age was not significantly associated with p53 positivity (P = 0.236). Of ten p53(+) patients, 6 (60%) were younger than 4 years, while 4 (40%) were >4 years and p53 positivity appeared more frequent in children <4 years of age (P = 0.024) when a different cutoff for age (4 years rather than 2 years) was examined. Among 13 patients with tumor size of <5 cm, 11 (84.6%) were p53(-) (P = 0.018). On the contrary, among 15 patients with tumor size >5 cm although more subjects were p53(+), the association was not statistically significant, and a larger sample size would be required to confirm this observation. Lymph node status was not affected by p53 positivity, and almost double the numbers of p53(-) status subjects were seen both for lymph nodes positive or lymph nodes negative status. There were significantly more numbers of p53(-) patients for well-differentiated tumors. Among 16 patients of well-differentiated tumors, 13 (81.2%) were p53(-) and the association of this p53 status with well-differentiated tumors was found to be significant (P = 0.041). Among 12 patients with non-well-differentiated histological grading, 7 (58.3%) were p53(+), but this was not statistically significant. When differentiation status was related, the only association emerging was the presence of p53(-) status with well-differentiated tumors. Overall, p53 status in relation to histopathological differentiation was found to be significant (P = 0.039) [Table 2].

DISCUSSION

Although cancer is a major cause of disease-related mortality in children, pediatric tumors remain relatively rare compared with adult malignancies, accounting for only about 3% of all cancer cases. Childhood tumors comprise a variety of malignancies with incidences varying by age, sex, ethnicity, geography, and histology. Studying these factors alongside TP53 alterations and 1p LOH can provide valuable insights into cancer etiology and outcomes.

TP53 is a tumor-suppressor gene that codes for a DNA-binding protein p53 involved in cell cycle arrest, DNA repair, differentiation, and apoptosis. The mutation of TP53 and its role in genetic alterations that result in the ultimate carcinogenic event differs among tumor sites ranging from the earliest mutations that can be detected in premalignant cells to mutations that trigger malignant transformation of a benign neoplasm. The TP53 gene is mutated in approximately 50% of human cancers and in germline DNA with inherited cancer syndromes. Its mutations can cause expression of abnormal proteins or result in the complete absence of p53. For these reasons, the role of p53 genetic disruption has different implications in different tumor types and may vary depending on the effect of the mutation on p53 protein function.[8] The mutation of p53 has been associated with increased recurrence and poorer survival in several previous studies.[910] In our cohort, all cases of relapse and mortality occurred in patients positive for both p53 and 1p LOH, although the small sample size limits definitive conclusions.

Douglas et al.[11] found abnormalities of chromosome 1 in 32 of 46 pediatric solid tumors, including Ewing’s sarcoma, Wilms’ tumor, rhabdomyosarcoma, PNET, and hepatoblastoma. Their frequent occurrence indicates that they may endow a clonal advantage in the development of cancer. It is worth highlighting that our subjects had a higher incidence of 1p LOH positivity (50%) rather than p53 positivity (36%), contrary to adults, where the role of p53 mutation is already eminently documented.[10] This observation suggests that studying 1p LOH along with p53 positivity may help in understanding their role in pediatric solid tumor outcomes.

Overall, age was not significantly associated with p53 status (p = 0.236), with the majority of patients being p53 negative irrespective of age (n = 18 out of 28). Several studies have reported a positive association between adverse p53 alterations and increasing age; however, most of this evidence is derived from adult populations,[121314] with limited data in pediatric cohorts.[15] Stangel et al.[16] demonstrated a significant association between age and p53 status in childhood acute lymphoblastic leukaemia. Similarly, Pinto et al.[17] observed that pediatric adrenocortical tumors frequently harbour high-penetrance p53 mutations, predominantly in children younger than 4 years. Although some pediatric studies have suggested associations with older age groups,[1819] stronger correlations have more consistently been observed in younger children, particularly in adrenocortical tumors. In our study, an exploratory subgroup analysis using a higher age cutoff (4 years instead of 2 years) revealed that the majority of p53-positive cases were younger than 4 years (n = 6/10, 60%).

Grosfeld[20] reported an association between p53 mutations and lymph node metastasis as well as advanced disease stage in tumors such as Wilms’ tumor, neuroblastoma, and rhabdomyosarcoma. In our study, although p53 positivity was observed more frequently in larger tumors (>5 cm; n=8 out of 15), this association did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the limited sample size. Furthermore, no significant relationship was found between p53 status and lymph node involvement, with a predominance of p53-negative cases observed irrespective of nodal status. While studies in adult populations, such as Rodier et al.[13] have demonstrated a positive correlation between adverse p53 alterations and nodal metastasis, such an association was not evident in our cohort.

In our study, p53 status was significantly associated with histopathological differentiation (P = 0.039). Well-differentiated tumors were predominantly p53 negative (n= 13 out of 15, P = 0.041) where as non-well differentiated tumors were predominantly p53 positive (n=7 out of 12). These findings are consistent with previous reports, including Lemoine et al.,[21] who demonstrated increased p53 expression in poorly differentiated Wilms’ tumors, and Rivlin et al.[22] who emphasized the role of mutant p53 in tumor dedifferentiation and progression.

1p loss of heterozygosity (LOH) was not significantly associated with patient age in the current study. However, it was observed more frequently in tumors larger than 5 cm (n=10 out of 15, P = 0.019), without any significant correlation with lymph node involvement or tumor differentiation. Tsukamoto et al.[23] similarly reported an association between 1p loss and larger tumor size (>2 cm) as well as lymph node metastasis. In contrast, Grundy et al.[24] found that adverse 1p alterations were more common in anaplastic Wilms’ tumors and in advanced stages of disease with an associated increased risk of recurrence.

In our study, mortality was observed in two patients, and both were p53 and 1p LOH positive, while the relapse was seen in three patients and all were p53 and 1p LOH positive. Although the numbers were small, all cases of mortality and relapse occurred in patients positive for both p53 and 1p LOH. Similarly, in the systematic review by Cone et al.,[25] the authors reported an increased recurrence rate among Wilms’ tumor patients demonstrating p53 positivity and 1p LOH.

When the data were studied simultaneously, either positivity (1p, p53, or both p53 and 1p LOH+) led to a significantly increased number of patients with (>5cm; n=12 out of 15, P = 0.04). Associations between adverse p53 alterations and 1p anomalies with tumor size suggest that alterations of one or more tumor suppressor genes in this region may contribute to carcinogenesis and tumor growth. Other tumor characteristics, such as nodal status, histology, mortality, and relapse, did not show significant associations, probably due to the small sample size. Therefore, these findings provide hypothesis-generating observations and may help guide future larger multicenter studies.

Synergism for adverse genetic markers

The phenomenon of oncogene synergism is described for animal experiments and also mainly for neural tumors. In animal models, the synergistic effect of two oncogenes was observed in transgenic mice, in which RAS and MYC oncogenes were transfected. MYC oncogene caused tumorigenesis in only a small percentage, RAS oncogene caused tumorigenesis earlier than the MYC oncogene, in 50% of the transgenic mice developed a tumor. In transgenic mice with both RAS and MYC oncogenes, cancer developed in 100% of the transgenic mice and in a much shorter period.[26] In our study, among all patients, two patients (out of three) with Wilms’ tumor (blastemal dominant and anaplastic histology = 1 each) and two with non-Hodgkin lymphoma (both poorly differentiated) having both adverse p53 and 1p LOH had adverse outcomes. Relapses were seen in three patients, and mortality was observed in two patients. All cases of mortality and relapse occurred exclusively in patients positive for both TP53 and 1p LOH, suggesting a possible synergistic adverse effect.

CONCLUSION

In this pilot study, both 1p LOH and p53 positivity were observed more frequently in children with larger tumors and non-well-differentiated histology, and were associated with adverse outcomes, particularly when both markers were present concurrently. The intricate interplay between p53 alterations and 1p chromosomal anomalies in pediatric solid tumors underscores the complexity of these cancers. Continued research in this field holds promise for advancing our understanding of the underlying genetic mechanisms and developing more precise and effective treatment strategies.

Future areas for research

Although the presence of any one of the unfavorable markers was variably associated with selective adverse outcome, the effect was more marked when present together. Instead of a single gene, a panel of multiple genetic biomarkers may be identified for dictating a program as a futuristic approach.
