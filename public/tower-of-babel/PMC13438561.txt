Enzyme-inspired single-molecule photocatalyst enables singlet-oxygen-driven asymmetric epoxidation and sulfoximination
Authors: Yirui Chen, Wanting Tong, Xinhang Jin, Hangtao Wu, Xianghua Zhao, Dingguo Song, Weihui Zhong, Fei Ling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of catalytic systems for the enantioselective epoxidation of sterically hindered trisubstituted alkenes using molecular oxygen (O₂) as the terminal oxidant remains a formidable challenge. Here, we report a biomimetic bifunctional photocatalyst that integrates a chiral manganese center and a covalently tethered anthraquinone photosensitizer within a single molecular scaffold. This unified design emulates the enzymatic principle of spatially coupled oxygen activation and chiral induction. Under visible light and ambient O₂, the catalyst enables the asymmetric epoxidation of challenging trisubstituted alkenes, including cyclic sulfones and linear α,β-unsaturated carbonyls, in up to 99% enantiomeric excess (ee), and also facilitates the efficient conversion of sulfilimines to (chiral) sulfoximines. Mechanistic studies reveal a cascade involving singlet-oxygen-mediated oxidation, oxidative decarboxylation, and acid-assisted O–O bond heterolysis to generate a high-valent Mn⁴⁺=O species as the key active intermediate. By unifying light harvesting, O₂ activation, and asymmetric catalysis in a precious-metal-free platform, this work establishes a versatile, sustainable strategy for the selective oxidation of traditionally recalcitrant substrates.Subject terms: Asymmetric catalysis, Photocatalysis

Introduction

Biomimetic catalysis serves as both a conceptual bridge and a guiding paradigm in modern synthetic chemistry, providing a powerful framework for developing green, efficient, and selective transformations, with oxidation catalysis being a prime example1–6. Oxidation reactions are of paramount industrial importance, accounting for approximately 30% of global chemical production and underpinning the synthesis of high-value epoxides and sulfoximines7. Inspired by the remarkable efficiency of non-heme iron/manganese metalloenzymes in performing selective oxidations under mild conditions, chemists have, since the 1990s8, designed a wide array of non-heme Fe/Mn complexes that enable highly enantioselective epoxidation of diverse alkenes9–19. However, these systems almost universally rely on stoichiometric oxidants such as H2O2, iodosylbenzene (PhIO), or peracids, posing safety, environmental, and economic concerns due to hazards in storage and transport, byproduct generation, and high cost (Fig. 1a). Consequently, the development of biomimetic catalytic systems employing O2 as the terminal oxidant represents not only a fundamental alignment with green chemistry principles but also the ultimate goal for sustainable, selective oxidation20.

Nature’s blueprint for this objective lies in metalloenzymes themselves, which achieve regio- and stereoselective oxidations using O2. The key to their efficiency lies in the spatial coupling of O2 activation with chiral induction; O2 activation proceeds via proton-promoted O–O bond heterolysis to generate a highly reactive high-valent metal-oxo (M = O) species (Fig. 1b)21. This reactive high-valent metal-oxo (M = O) species serves as the key oxidant responsible for selective oxygen-atom transfer in biological systems1. This spatially coupled O–O heterolysis mechanism constitutes the chemical foundation of biological selective oxidation and provides a clear design principle for artificial systems.1a Current chemical strategies for O2 activation have yet to effectively emulate this enzymatic model22–27. In alkene epoxidation, two representative approaches have been established: one involves in situ generation of peracids via aldehyde-mediated autoxidation28, the other employs Ru-based catalysts operating through single-electron transfer (SET) pathways (Fig. 1c, left)29. Despite their elegant design, these methods remain limited by modest enantioselectivity and narrow substrate scope, typically applicable only to electron-rich disubstituted alkenes and incompatible with sterically hindered trisubstituted alkenes. By contrast, energy transfer (EnT)-mediated generation of 1O2 represents a biomimetically inspired and inherently green oxidation strategy30. However, as an electrophilic species, 1O2 readily reacts with electron-rich alkenes but typically yields mixtures of allylic oxidation products, [4 + 2] cycloadducts, and other byproducts31,32, and exhibits very low reactivity toward electron-deficient alkenes, making selective epoxidation challenging (Fig. 1c, right). To the best of our knowledge, 1O2-driven highly enantioselective epoxidation of electron-deficient trisubstituted alkenes has not been achieved yet. Therefore, a new catalytic strategy that mimics the enzymatic paradigm of spatially coupled O–O Bond heterolysis is urgently needed. Building upon our ongoing efforts in the design of novel ligands for asymmetric catalysis33–41, we reasoned that a unified molecular platform integrating a photosensitizer and a chiral manganese center might enable enzyme-inspired O–O bond activation via spatially coupled heterolysis under visible light.

In this work, we report a biomimetic bifunctional catalyst that unifies a chiral manganese center and a covalently linked anthraquinone (AQ) photosensitizer within a single molecular scaffold (Fig. 1d). This system enables highly enantioselective epoxidation of sterically hindered trisubstituted alkenes and selective sulfilimine oxidation using O2 as the sole oxidant under visible light, emulating the enzymatic principle of spatially coupled O–O bond heterolysis.

Results

Design and optimization of bifunctional catalysts

Chiral sulfones are privileged motifs in bioactive molecules and pharmaceuticals due to their stability and utility in downstream stereospecific transformations42, which has drawn significant attention to them in the field of asymmetric catalysis in recent years43–45. Although two asymmetric epoxidations of α,β-unsaturated sulfones using H2O2 as the oxidant have been reported46,47, these methods are restricted to less-hindered disubstituted alkenes and deliver only modest catalytic performance. To address this limitation, we selected the cyclic trisubstituted vinyl sulfone 2-phenylbenzo[b]thiophene 1,1-dioxide (1a) as a more challenging and representative model substrate for evaluating our catalytic system (Table 1). Its structural rigidity and prochiral sulfonyl group make it ideal for probing both reactivity and stereocontrol.

Inspired by our previous work36, we first evaluated the non-heme manganese catalyst Mn(EtPMB) in a photocatalytic oxidation system employing organic photosensitizers. In this system, visible-light irradiation (blue light) excites the photosensitizer to generate 1O2, which serves as the key reactive oxygen species (Fig. S2)48. Among the photosensitizers screened, salt-type sensitizers such as methylene blue exhibited negligible activity despite their known ability to produce 1O2, likely due to incompatibility with the manganese catalyst or unfavorable intermolecular interactions. In contrast, acetophenone- and carbazole-based photosensitizers showed moderate efficacy, with AQ delivering the best performance, affording the target epoxide 2a in 29% yield and 86% ee (Table 1, entry 1). To enhance enantioselectivity, the ethyl group in Mn(EtPMB) was replaced with a bulkier benzyl moiety, yielding Mn(BnPMB), which improved the ee to 91% (Table 1, entry 2). This improvement aligns with the arm effect, where increased steric bulk at the tertiary amine center modulates the metal coordination geometry and enhances chiral induction15. In contrast, alternative manganese complexes Mn(MCMB) and Mn(MPMB) exhibited inferior performance (Table 1, entries 3 and 4)9,49, further highlighting the unique advantages of the PMB ligand framework.

However, the poor solubility of AQ severely limited catalytic efficiency. To address this issue, we developed a key design strategy: covalently integrating the photosensitizing unit into the chiral catalyst scaffold to enable intramolecular cooperation and thereby improve overall catalytic performance. Specifically, the phenyl group in Mn(BnPMB) was replaced with an AQ moiety, affording the bifunctional catalyst C1. This molecularly integrated design not only significantly enhanced stereocontrol but also increased solubility in acetonitrile by approximately 62-fold, from 10 to 624 µmol/mL at 25 °C. This improvement effectively resolved the incompatibility caused by the physical separation of photosensitizer and catalyst in conventional systems. Under identical conditions, C1 delivered the epoxide in 80% yield with 96% ee (Table 1, entry 5), clearly demonstrating the synergistic benefits of the unified architecture. The enantiomeric catalyst C2 gave comparable results, with 79% yield and −96% ee (Table 1, entry 6), whereas the pyridine-based analogue C3 showed markedly reduced reactivity and enantioselectivity, providing the product in 61% yield with 78% ee (Table 1, entry 7). These results underscore the superior electronic and steric properties of the benzimidazole ligand11. Systematic optimization of reaction temperature (Table 1, entries 8 and 9), light intensity, andcarboxylic acid additives (Tables S1 and S2)50 led to the establishment of optimal reaction conditions. Control experiments under the finalized protocol (Table 1, entry 10) confirmed that the manganese catalyst, visible light, exogenous carboxylic acid, and O2 are all essential, as the omission of any single component completely abolished product formation. Notably, the alkene underwent exclusive epoxidation, with no detectable dihydroxylation or oxidative cleavage byproducts. In summary, this bifunctional catalyst integrates light harvesting, O2 activation, and asymmetric catalysis within a single molecular entity, achieving synergistic enhancement in both efficiency and selectivity, which embodies the core principle of biomimetic catalytic design.

Substrate scope for the epoxidation of 2-arylbenzo[b]thiophene 1,1-dioxides

Leveraging this efficient catalytic system, we examined the scope of 2-arylbenzo[b]thiophene 1,1-dioxides (Fig. 2). Diverse functional groups were tolerated, delivering epoxides in 53–91% yields and 86–99% ee. On the 2-aryl ring, electron-withdrawing substituents (halogens, –CF3, –CN; 2b–2 f, 2m–2p) generally delivered high enantioselectivity (up to >99% ee), whereas para-positioned electron-donating groups (–OMe, alkyl; 2h–2 l) typically afforded slightly lower ee values (86–90%). Notably, the ortho-methoxy-substituted substrate (2 g) exhibited >99% ee, which is significantly higher than that of its para isomer (2 h, 86% ee), likely due to steric constraints near the reaction site. For certain substrates, such as 2b, 2e, and 2 g, higher catalyst loading and longer reaction times were required due to factors like limited solubility, which enabled high yields to be achieved. In contrast, substituents on the benzo[b]thiophene core (2q–2x) had minimal impact on enantioselectivity. Remarkably, the diaryl-substituted analogue bearing an additional phenyl group (2x) also maintained excellent stereocontrol (>99% ee).

Overcoming isomerization barriers in trans-alkene epoxidation

Having established the catalytic system for cyclic alkenes, we next explored its applicability to more challenging linear internal alkenes. Thermodynamically stable trans-alkenes can undergo photosensitized isomerization under blue light irradiation and partially equilibrate with their cis-isomers. This process shares identical reaction conditions with our photocatalytic epoxidation system51. However, achieving complete and selective isomerization across diverse linear alkenes remains difficult, rendering stereodefined epoxidation particularly challenging. Previous studies suggest that non-heme metal-catalyzed epoxidation may proceed through an acyclic carbon-centered radical or carbocation intermediate52,53. In this context, the Cα–Cβ bond of the initially formed cis-configured intermediate can undergo partial rotation, enabling a fraction of the intermediate to adopt a trans-like conformation prior to ring closure (Fig. 3). Consequently, both cis- and trans-epoxides are generated, with the diastereomeric ratio dictated by the relative rates of bond rotation and cyclization. Notably, the choice of carboxylic acid ligand modulates these conformational dynamics, thereby fine-tuning the trans/cis product ratio.

The effect of light wavelength was first examined, as higher photon energy (shorter wavelength) might promote cis-alkene formation. Using chalcone (3a) as a model substrate, longer wavelengths were found to suppress cis-isomer generation: irradiation at 450–460 nm afforded a cis/trans ratio of 0.6:1 (Table S3). Carboxylic acid additives were subsequently screened because they are critical for active oxidant generation and stereoselectivity control (Table S4). DMBA delivered optimal results, affording trans-4a in 80% yield and 97% ee (trans/cis = 16:1), whereas acetic acid provided poor selectivity (10% yield, 80% ee, trans/cis = 1:1), likely due to its smaller steric bulk. Temperature exerted a dramatic influence: at -40 °C, cis-product formation was nearly suppressed (trans-4a, 87% yield, 97% ee, trans/cis > 20:1). Lower temperatures enhance stereocontrol by favoring the lower-energy transition state pathway10,12,15. Under cryogenic conditions, slowed ring closure allows rotation about the Cα–Cβ bond to outcompete cyclization, thereby suppressing cis-product formation.

Substrate scope for the epoxidation of α,β-unsaturated carbonyl derivatives

With the optimized protocol established, we investigated the substrate scope of the asymmetric epoxidation (Fig. 4). A variety of α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compounds were efficiently converted to the corresponding epoxides with excellent enantioselectivity (4a–4p, 93–97% ee), irrespective of the electronic nature of the substituents. As observed previously, ortho-substitution that introduced steric congestion led to reduced yields. Remarkably, even a trisubstituted cyclic enone was successfully epoxidized (4q). The methodology was further applicable to α,β-unsaturated esters (4ra–4rf) and amides (4sa–4sd), including derivatives of natural products and key intermediates toward bioactive molecules such as (–)-Balasubramide and (R)-Fluoxetine (4sa and 4sb)54,55, which were obtained in good yields and high enantiomeric excess. Significantly, a chromene lacking a carbonyl functionality (4t) also underwent smooth epoxidation, affording a precursor to Cromakalim with high stereoselectivity56. This outcome demonstrates that direct coordination between the carbonyl group and the metal center is not essential for effective stereocontrol, highlighting the versatility and robustness of this catalytic system.

Oxidative transformation of sulfilimines to sulfoximines

Sulfoximines are important scaffolds in medicinal chemistry and asymmetric catalysis, serving as chiral ligands and bioactive motifs57–62. The two principal synthetic routes involve sulfoxide amination or sulfimide oxidation. While the former is well developed, direct sulfimide oxidation remains underexplored, particularly in terms of sustainability. Existing methods typically rely on thermal activation with H2O263, stoichiometric oxidants (RuCl3/NaIO4)64, or hypervalent iodine reagents (PODIC)65, which are often associated with harsh conditions and significant waste generation. Notably, aerobic approaches using molecular oxygen as the terminal oxidant for sulfoximine synthesis, particularly enantioselective variants, are exceedingly rare. In response to the growing demand for green, catalytic oxidation methods, we envisioned a photocatalytic aerobic strategy for the direct conversion of sulfilimines to sulfoximines.

Initial evaluation of sulfimide 5a under visible-light irradiation at room temperature using Mn(BnPMB) and AQ afforded only trace amounts of the desired sulfoximine 6a (2, entry 1). In contrast, catalyst C1 enabled delivery of 6a in 32% isolated yield with 68% recovery of starting material (Table 2, entry 2). Catalyst C2 exhibited similar efficiency (33% yield, Table 2, entry 3). In contrast, C3 gave a lower yield (18%, Table 2, entry 4), indicating that the benzimidazole-based ligand framework enhances catalytic activity more effectively than its pyridine analogue. The symmetric bis-AQ complex C4 showed markedly reduced reactivity (5% yield, Table 2, entry 5), likely due to steric hindrance that impedes substrate approach or catalyst turnover. Solvent screening revealed that ethyl acetate (EA) led to a biphasic reaction mixture and provided only moderate yield (58%, Table 2, entry 8). Substitution with ethyl lactate improved the yield to 66% (Table 2, entry 10) but complicated purification. An optimal balance of homogeneity and reactivity was achieved in an EA/MeCN (4:1, v/v) mixture, affording 6a in 84% isolated yield (Table 2, entry 12). In contrast, alcohols, 1,4-dioxane, and dichloromethane resulted in significantly lower conversions (Table S5). Further optimization identified 390–400 nm as the optimal wavelength range, with reactivity declining at longer wavelengths (Tables S6 and S7). All reactions were conducted at room temperature for 48 h unless otherwise noted.

Substrate scope for the oxidation of sulfilimines

Under the optimized conditions, the substrate scope for sulfoximine synthesis was investigated (Fig. 5a). The reaction was sensitive to electronic effects: the unsubstituted substrate gave a yield of 84%. Electron-withdrawing substituents (e.g., halogen, nitro) on either the aryl ring attached to the sulfur atom or the N-benzoyl aryl ring generally afforded yields close to or slightly higher than that of the parent system (ranging from 74% to 92%). In contrast, electron-donating groups consistently led to reduced yields (mostly in the 60–70% range), indicating that electron-rich arenes are less reactive, whereas moderately electron-deficient arenes are well tolerated. Steric effects were also significant but site-dependent. ortho-Substitution on the sulfur-attached aryl ring completely suppressed reactivity, indicating severe steric constraints near the reaction center. In contrast, ortho-substitution on the benzoyl aryl ring markedly lowered yield but still allowed product formation, suggesting a more accessible environment at that site. The method was successfully extended to sulfonamides and cyclic sulfilimines, delivering structurally diverse sulfoximines. Notably, the phenyl-substituted product 6x, a known key intermediate in the synthesis of EphB4 receptor inhibitors66, was obtained in good yield, highlighting the potential of this approach for constructing pharmaceutically relevant scaffolds.

Kinetic resolution of scalemic sulfilimines with enantiopurity amplification

To evaluate the potential for accessing chiral sulfoximines, we first tested the asymmetric oxidation of racemic sulfimide 5a under standard conditions. The product was racemic, indicating no inherent enantioselectivity in the catalytic cycle. Notably, when the toluenesulfonyl-substituted analog 7a was used, low but detectable enantioselectivity (21% ee) was observed, although extensive optimization led to only modest improvement (48% ee; Fig. 5b, Tables S8 and S9). Given that chiral sulfoximines are often accessed via oxidation of enantiomerically enriched sulfilimines67, we hypothesized a substrate-controlled pathway. Indeed, oxidation of non-racemic 7a (74% ee) delivered the corresponding sulfoximine in high yield and with significantly enhanced ee (94%), demonstrating efficient chiral amplification (Fig. 5c). The toluenesulfonyl group can be readily removed under acidic conditions, enabling access to versatile intermediates for C2-symmetric bis(sulfoximine) ligands, which are widely employed in asymmetric catalysis, such as hetero-Diels-Alder reactions68. With this amplification effect established, we examined the generality using a range of non-racemic sulfilimines. Aryl- and naphthyl-substituted substrates were oxidized with >94% ee, regardless of electronic properties, indicating excellent stereocontrol and functional group tolerance. However, replacing the S-methyl group with ethyl led to reduced enantioselectivity (90% ee), revealing sensitivity to steric bulk at the alkyl substituent.

Reaction mechanism elucidation

Oxygen concentration experiments showed that the yield of product 4a increased significantly with rising O2 concentration, indicating a strong dependence of the reaction on molecular oxygen. Furthermore, 18O2 labeling experiments revealed 75% incorporation of 18O (Fig. 6a), supporting that the oxygen atom incorporated into the product likely originates predominantly from O2. To identify the reactive oxygen species, various inhibitors were tested; notably, the singlet oxygen traps 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF) and 9,10-diphenylanthracene (DPA) significantly suppressed epoxidation, concomitant with the formation of the characteristic singlet oxygen-derived products: the oxidative cleavage product 1,2-phenylenebis(phenylmethanone) (9a) from DPBF and the endoperoxide 9b from DPA (Fig. 6b)69. EPR analysis detected the 1O2–TEMPO adduct, further confirming 1O2 generation (Fig. 6c)70. Upon visible-light irradiation (λ = 390 nm), anthraquinone (AQ) absorbs photons and undergoes intersystem crossing to generate the triplet excited state (3AQ*), which transfers energy to O2 to produce 1O271–73. However, the photon energy at 390 nm is insufficient to directly excite stilbene. This mechanism is supported by the observation that Z/E isomerization of trans-stilbene occurs only in the presence of AQ and under irradiation, a hallmark of 1O2-mediated reactivity (Fig. 6d).

In non-heme oxidation systems, H218O isotopic labeling is commonly used to probe high-valent metal–oxygen intermediates (Fig. 6e)50,74,75. To assess whether a Mnn= O species is involved, we performed H218O labeling experiments during the formation of 3a and observed low levels of 18O incorporation (Fig. 6f: 2.4% at 0 °C and 0.5% at −40 °C). This result supports the assignment of a high-valent Mnⁿ = O species as the active oxidant. To elucidate the mechanism of O–O bond cleavage, we employed α,α-dimethylphenylacetic acid (AIPHA), which undergoes decarboxylative oxidation to generate an in situ peroxo intermediate structurally analogous to cumene hydroperoxide. The resulting product distribution, with alcohol and ketone formed in a 2.5:1.0 ratio, indicates preferential heterolytic O–O bond cleavage facilitated by the acidic reaction environment (Fig. 6g and h)75. This conclusion is further supported by competition experiments between alkene epoxidation and alcohol oxidation, where the alcohol-to-ketone ratio serves as a direct probe for the heterolytic/homolytic partitioning (Fig. 6i). Notably, exogenous cumene hydroperoxide (PHA) also undergoes predominantly heterolytic cleavage in the presence of AIPHA, reinforcing an acid-enabled heterolysis pathway (Fig. 6j)75,76. These results support the hypothesis that the proposed Mnn–alkyl peroxo intermediate undergoes acid-mediated O–O bond heterolysis to form the putative Mnn+2 = O species (Fig. 6k). UV-Vis spectroscopy shows that integrating the AQ photosensitizer into the ligand framework enhances absorption and red-shifts the onset, extending the photoresponse range and improving light harvesting (Figure. S10). On/off irradiation reveals no reaction in the dark, excluding a radical chain process (Fig. S11).

Enantioselectivity is an exquisitely sensitive probe of the energy difference between the transition states leading to the two enantiomeric epoxides (±1%), and therefore, ee values are highly diagnostic of the nature of the intermediate50,74. To distinguish between MnV = O75–77 and MnIV = O78,79 as the active oxidant, we compared the epoxidation of 3a using various oxidants, including H2O2 and t-BuOOH, which typically generate MnV-oxo species, against the O2 system. Under standard conditions, H2O2 and t-BuOOH afforded 98% and 97% ee, respectively, while the O2 system delivered 96% ee. These similarly high ee values hint at a potentially common high-valent manganese–oxo intermediate. However, temperature-dependent studies revealed a key divergence: upon warming, both enantio- and diastereoselectivity eroded more rapidly in the O2 system than in the H2O2 system. At 0 °C, the ee gap reached 3%, reflecting distinct transition-state energy landscapes (Fig. S12). Collectively, the data support MnIV = O as the operative oxidant, rather than MnV = O. The carboxylic acid likely binds within the chiral ligand cavity, where it modulates stereoselectivity through steric and electronic effects while simultaneously promoting heterolytic O–O bond cleavage via a cooperative mechanism. Notably, a similar N4–Mn/O2 system has also been reported to operate via a MnIV = O active species, further supporting the assignment of this intermediate in our system80.

Plausible mechanism

A plausible mechanism is proposed based on mechanistic studies, inspired by oxygenase enzymes (Fig. 7). The catalyst C1 first undergoes ligand exchange with a carboxylic acid to generate intermediate B. The MnII center in B is oxidized by photochemically generated 1O2, affording the MnIII–OO• species C, which structurally and functionally resembles reactive intermediates observed in enzymatic systems. Species C promotes oxidative decarboxylation of the coordinated carboxylate, releasing CO2 and an alkyl radical (R•). This radical rapidly recombines with the metal-bound oxygen atom to form the MnII–alkylperoxo species D81. Subsequently, a second molecule of carboxylic acid coordinates to the Mn center in a monodentate fashion through its carbonyl oxygen, forming ternary complex E. Concurrently, the hydroxyl group of this carboxylic acid engages in an intramolecular hydrogen bond with the distal oxygen of the Mn–OOR moiety. This dual interaction enhances the Lewis acidity of the Mn center and stabilizes the developing negative charge on the leaving group during O–O bond cleavage, thereby significantly lowering the barrier for heterolysis. As a result, acid-assisted heterolytic O–O bond scission occurs, generating the highly reactive MnIV = O oxidant F15,74,82, which is functionally analogous to high-valent metal–oxo intermediates in metalloenzymes. Species F then transfers its oxo group to alkenes or sulfinimines, yielding epoxides or sulfoximines, respectively, and regenerating the MnII catalyst. Notably, the bulky AQ ligand creates a spatially confined chiral pocket around the metal center, effectively mimicking the steric constraints of enzyme active sites. This biomimetic microenvironment enforces precise substrate orientation, which is crucial for achieving high enantioselectivity.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a biomimetic, bifunctional Mn–AQ photocatalyst that enables highly enantioselective epoxidation of sterically encumbered trisubstituted alkenes and selective oxidation of sulfilimines to sulfoximines, using O2 as the terminal oxidant under visible-light irradiation. The system triggered a cascade of oxygen activation, decarboxylation, and carboxylate-assisted O–O bond heterolysis. This MnIV = O oxidant serves as the common active intermediate for both transformations, establishing a unified platform for asymmetric oxygen-atom transfer. Critically, the covalent integration of the AQ photosensitizer not only enhances catalyst solubility and photosensitization efficiency but also rigidifies the chiral pocket, thereby amplifying stereocontrol. By spatially coupling light harvesting, O2 activation, and asymmetric catalysis within a single molecular scaffold, this design emulates the enzymatic principle of spatially coupled O–O heterolysis and chiral induction, offering a sustainable, precious-metal-free strategy for highly selective green oxidation.

General methods

Representative procedure for synthesis of chiral epoxide 2

In a custom-designed cryogenic photoreactor cooled by circulating ethanol, substrate 1 (0.1 mmol), C1 (5.0 μmol, 5 mol%, or 10.0 μmol, 10 mol%), DMBA (0.9 mmol, 9.0 equiv.), and dry MeCN (2 mL) were combined. The mixture was cooled to −35 °C and stirred under O2 atmosphere while being irradiated with a 25 W blue LED (λmax = 450–460 nm) for 36 h or 48 h. After completion, the solvent was removed under reduced pressure, and the residue was purified by silica gel column chromatography to afford the corresponding product 2.

Representative procedure for synthesis of chiral epoxide 4

In a custom-designed cryogenic photoreactor cooled by circulating ethanol, substrate 3 (0.1 mmol), C1 (5.0 μmol, 5 mol%), DMBA (0.9 mmol, 9.0 equiv.), and dry MeCN (2 mL) were combined. The mixture was cooled to -40 °C and stirred under O2 atmosphere while being irradiated with a 25 W blue LED (λmax = 450–460 nm) for 18 h. After completion, the solvent was removed under reduced pressure, and the residue was purified by silica gel column chromatography to afford the corresponding product 4.

Representative procedure for synthesis of sulfoximine 6

In a Schlenk tube, substrate 5 (0.1 mmol), C2 (10.0 μmol, 10 mol%), DMBA (0.9 mmol, 9.0 equiv.), and EA:MeCN (4:1, v/v, 2.5 mL) were combined. The mixture was stirred under an O2 atmosphere at 25 °C and irradiated with a 25 W purple LED (λmax = 390–400 nm) for 48 h. A fan was used to maintain the temperature by air cooling. After completion, the solvent was removed under reduced pressure, and the residue was purified by silica gel column chromatography to afford the corresponding product 6.

Representative procedure for synthesis of sulfoximine 8

In a Schlenk tube, substrate 7 (0.1 mmol), C2 (10.0 μmol, 10 mol%), DMBA (0.9 mmol, 9.0 equiv.), and EA:TFE (4:1, v/v, 2.5 mL) were combined. The mixture was stirred under an O2 atmosphere at 25 °C and irradiated with a 25 W purple LED (λmax = 390–400 nm) for 96 h. A fan was used to maintain the temperature by air cooling. After completion, the solvent was removed under reduced pressure, and the residue was purified by silica gel column chromatography to afford the corresponding product 8.
