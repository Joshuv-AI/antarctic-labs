A Linear Matching Bandit Approach to Online Multi-Human Multi-Robot Teaming
Yaohui Guo, X. Jessie Yang, Cong Shi

Abstract
We address the problem of online multi-human multi-robot teaming through the lens of a linear matching bandit framework, where a learner assigns robots with unknown features from a fixed pool to distinct sets of human agents over multiple rounds. To solve this problem, we propose LinMatch , an online learning algorithm that updates the confidence intervals of the unknown features and makes the optimistic matching under uncertainty. The contributions and novelty of this work are twofold. First, we recast the optimistic matching problem in each round as a linear program of maximum weighted matching, efficiently solvable by the celebrated Hungarian algorithm. Second, we provide novel bounds for matching with linear feature problems, showing an upper bound of O ~ ​ ( d ​ M ​ K ​ T ) \tilde{O}(d\sqrt{MKT}) and a minimax lower bound of Ω ⁡ ( d ​ M ​ K ​ T ) \Omega(d\sqrt{MKT}) , establishing a tight optimal regret rate of Θ ~ ​ ( d ​ M ​ K ​ T ) \tilde{\Theta}(d\sqrt{MKT}) . This demonstrates that LinMatch achieves strictly optimal achievable regret with respect to the total number of rounds T T , the feature dimension d d , and the matching parameters M M and K K . The proposed algorithm and bounds apply to a wide range of matching problems with applications beyond human-robot matching, such as housing allocation, recommendation systems, and more.

However, efficient teaming is challenged by unknown, heterogeneous agent characteristics. To address this, we investigate the multi-human-multi-robot teaming problem within an online learning bandit framework. As illustrated in Figure

Intractable arm space
: Due to varying human features and diverse robot capabilities, treating each unique matching as a bandit arm leads to exponential growth in the number of arms, limiting the transfer of insights from previous rounds.
Combinatorial complexity
: The decision process is inherently constrained by the matching requirements, which interconnect the rewards and complicate optimization.

In online multi-human multi-robot teaming, a fixed pool of

Each human-robot team will produce a reward, which can be observed by the learner. The learner’s goal is to maximize the total rewards by making the optimal matching in each round. We assume the expected outcome of each human-robot team is determined by the inner product of their features. The feature of each human agent is known, potentially learned from the human’s capability or task context, but the robots’ features are unknown due to the complexity of AI systems.

To address the first challenge, we assume a linear reward structure, a common approach in personalized problems like article recommendation
(
21
;
22
)
and healthcare
(

)
. In particular, we assume that each robot is characterized by a feature vector

such that the expected reward the robot produces when teaming with a human agent with feature

is
X^{\top}\theta
. In addition, we assume that the features of robots are
unknown
due to the increasing complexity and lack of transparency in advanced robotic systems; while human features are assumed
known
, which can be gleaned from user profiles or the context of the task at hand. Second, to manage the combinatorial complexity, we employ an upper-confidence-bound (UCB) method to balance exploration and exploitation. In particular, we propose the
LinMatch

Problem Setting

We consider a multi-human multi-robot team with
K
robots,
M
human agents, and
T
rounds. At round
t

M

X_{m,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathcal{X}_{t}:=\{X_{m,t}\}_{m=1}^{M}
of human agents, as well as the interaction history

A_{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{M,K}
according to its policy
\pi

\mathcal{A}_{M,K}
is the collection of all injection functions from
[M]
to
[K]
. This action
A_{t}
assigns each human
m
a robot
A_{t}(m)
. To ensure that each human is assigned a robot, we impose the condition that
M\leqslant K
. Upon completing the task, the learner receives a reward
Y_{m,t}
from team
m
, for all
m\in[M]
. We denote the entire reward vector for round
t
as
Y_{t}=(Y_{1,t},\dotsc,Y_{M,t})
. A summary of major notation is presented in Appendix
B

We assume linear realizability for the expected rewards: for all
k\in[K]
,
m\in[M]
, and
t\in[T]
such that
A_{t}(m)=k
, there exists some unknown vector
\theta_{k}^{*}\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
of robot
k
such that
Y_{m,t}=X_{m,t}^{\top}\theta_{k}^{*}+\epsilon_{m,t}
, where
\epsilon_{m,t}
is a
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian random noise.
The sub-Gaussian noise assumption is standard in bandit literature
(
19
;
23
;
25
)
.
Furthermore, we assume the noise terms
\{\epsilon_{m,t}\}_{m=1}^{M}
are independent of each other given the features
\mathcal{X}_{t}
, matching
A_{t}
, and history
\mathcal{H}_{t-1}

The optimal matching for round

A_{t}^{*}=\arg\max_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{M,K}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{m=1}^{M}Y_{m,t}\right]=\arg\max_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{M,K}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{m,t}^{\top}\theta_{A(m)}^{*}

Hence, we define the regret for policy
\pi

R_{T}\left(\pi,\{X_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}\right)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\max_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{M,K}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}X_{m,t}^{\top}\theta_{A(m)}^{*}-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{m=1}^{M}Y_{m,t}\right]

(1)

We note that our problem formulation accommodates adversarial matchings. Specifically, the feature set
\{\mathcal{X}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
may be selected and fixed by an oblivious adversary prior to the first round.

We make the following standard assumption on the magnitude of the feature vectors:

Assumption 1
.

\left\|\theta_{k}^{*}\right\|\leqslant S
for all
k\in[K]
,
\|X_{m,t}\|\leqslant L
for all
(m,t)\in[M]\times[T]
.

4.1
Constructing Confidence Intervals

We use ridge regression to construct confidence intervals of
\theta_{k}^{*}
’s. Since the number of robots is greater than the number of humans, a robot may not be assigned to a human in some rounds. Therefore, for each robot, we need to keep track of the rounds where it was assigned to a human. For each
k

\Psi_{k,t}=\left\{s\middle|s\in[t-1]\ \text{and}\ k\in A_{s}([M])\right\}

Theoretical Results

We analyze the

5.1
Regret Upper Bound

The following result gives an upper bound on the regret of the

Theorem 1
.

Assume
\left\|\theta_{k}^{*}\right\|\leqslant S
for all
k\in[K]
and
\|X_{m,t}\|\leqslant L
for all

The difficulties of analyzing the upper bound of the

First, the algorithm’s future actions depend on the confidence intervals constructed using past actions, making the determination of the confidence set a complex task. Previous works in linear bandits have developed two approaches to solve this problem, either utilizing the theory of self-normalized processes
(
31
;
1
)
or expressing the estimated reward as a linear combination of rewards from arms that are independent of the current arm
(
3

5
)

\theta

Second, the combinatorial nature of the matching problem makes it a complicated work to express the regret in terms of the confidence intervals. To illustrate, consider the matching problem with two robots and two human agents. The time index
t
is omitted for clarity. Suppose we have constructed the confidence intervals
\hat{\Theta}_{1}
and
\hat{\Theta}_{2}
and calculated the optimistic estimations
\hat{\theta}_{11}
,
\hat{\theta}_{12}
,
\hat{\theta}_{21}

\hat{\theta}_{22}

\hat{\theta}_{1}
) and human 2 (
X_{2}
) to robot 2 (
\hat{\theta}_{2}

R=\underbrace{\left(X_{1}^{\top}\theta_{2}^{*}+X_{2}^{\top}\theta_{1}^{*}\right)}_{\text{optimal reward}}-\underbrace{\left(X_{1}^{\top}\theta_{1}^{*}+X_{2}^{\top}\theta_{2}^{*}\right)}_{\text{actual reward}}=X_{1}^{\top}\left(\theta_{2}^{*}-\theta_{1}^{*}\right)+X_{2}^{\top}\left(\theta_{1}^{*}-\theta_{2}^{*}\right).

(6)

\displaystyle R=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left(X_{1}^{\top}\theta_{2}^{*}+X_{2}^{\top}\theta_{1}^{*}\right)}_{\text{optimal reward}}-\underbrace{\left(X_{1}^{\top}\theta_{1}^{*}+X_{2}^{\top}\theta_{2}^{*}\right)}_{\text{actual reward}}\leqslant\underbrace{\left(X_{1}^{\top}\hat{\theta}_{11}+X_{2}^{\top}\hat{\theta}_{22}\right)}_{\text{optimistic reward}}-\underbrace{\left(X_{1}^{\top}\theta_{1}^{*}+X_{2}^{\top}\theta_{2}^{*}\right)}_{\text{actual reward}}

5.2
Regret Lower Bound

We provide an instance-independent lower bound for the matching bandit problem with linear features, proving that the upper bound achieved by LinMatch is minimax optimal.

Theorem 3
.

For the linear matching bandit problem described in Section
3
, with
K
robots,
M
human agents per round, feature dimension
d
, and time horizon
T
, any matching policy
\pi
must suffer an expected cumulative regret bounded by:

\mathcal{R}_{T}^{*}(\pi)\geq\Omega(d\sqrt{MKT})

To establish this lower bound, we construct a hard adversarial environment utilizing a dense hypercube packing strategy over the parameter space
\Theta=\{-\Delta,\Delta\}^{d\times K}
, rather than a standard sparse basis construction. By leveraging Assouad’s Lemma and bounding the total variation distance between adjacent hypotheses via the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence of the Gaussian noise, we capture the necessary continuous exploration penalty across all
d
dimensions. Furthermore, by accounting for the
M
simultaneous semi-bandit observations per round and maximizing the adversary’s capability through a uniform allocation bound, the resulting divergence yields the tight
\Omega(d\sqrt{MKT})
bound. The full mathematical proof is detailed in the Appendix.

To show such a lower bound, we construct a family of similar bandit instances and lower bound the sum of their regret. The proof relies on the following variant of Pinsker’s inequality:

Lemma 1
.

For two probability measures
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
on the same probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
,

\mathbb{P}^{\prime}(A)\leqslant\mathbb{P}(A)+\sqrt{1-\exp\left(-\text{KL}\left(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}^{\prime}\right)\right)},\ \ \text{for all }A\in\mathcal{F},

(11)

where
\text{KL}\left(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}^{\prime}\right)
is the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
.

5.2.1
Decompose KL Divergence

To leverage Lemma
1
, we quantify the KL-divergence between two linear matching bandit instances. We first have to define the probability space and probability measure of the outcome of linear matching bandits.
Second, we derive a decomposition property of the KL divergence of two linear matching bandits so we can express the total divergence as a weighted sum of divergence between different matching.

We present the following result for decomposing the KL divergence and provide the details in Appendix

Theorem 4

Let
\nu=(\theta_{1},\dotsc,\theta_{K},\mathcal{X}_{1},\dotsc,\mathcal{X}_{t})

\nu^{\prime}=\left(\theta_{1}^{\prime},\dotsc,\theta_{K}^{\prime},\mathcal{X}_{1}^{\prime},\dotsc,\mathcal{X}_{t}^{\prime}\right)
be two linear matching bandits and
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{\prime}}
be the corresponding probability measures under the same policy
\pi

\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}^{\prime}\right)<\infty
and the noise
\{\epsilon_{m,s}\}_{m=1}^{M}

A_{s}

\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{\nu},\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{\prime}})=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}_{A_{s}\sim\nu\pi}\left[\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{m,A_{s}},\mathbb{P}_{m,A_{s}}^{\prime}\right)\right],

where
\mathbb{P}_{m,A_{s}}
is the distribution of
Y_{m,s}
given
A_{s}
.

When the noise
\epsilon_{m,s}
is Gaussian with zero mean and

variance, the distribution of

Let

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{\nu},\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{\prime}})=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\left(\left(X_{m,s}^{\top}\theta_{k}-X_{m,s}^{\prime\top}\theta_{k}^{\prime}\right)^{2}\mathbb{P}_{\nu}(A_{s}(m)=k)\right),

robot

7
Conclusion

We propose
LinMatch
, a UCB-like algorithm, to address the online multi-human multi-robot teaming problem in a linear matching bandit framework. The optimistic matching problem in each round can be reformulated as a linear programming problem of maximum weighted matching and thus can be efficiently solved by the celebrated Hungarian algorithm.

Our regret analysis shows that, for
T
rounds,
M
humans,
K
robots, and
d
dimensional feature vectors, the regret of
LinMatch
is bounded by
\tilde{O}\left(d\sqrt{MKT}\right)
and
\Omega\left(d\sqrt{MKT}\right)
, which implies that the algorithm achieves sublinear regret and is minimax optimal across all variables. Numerical experiments show that the theoretical constraint on the regularization factor could be practically relaxed to achieve lower regret, which suggests directions for future instance-dependent analysis.

In addition, the proposed algorithm utilizes the linear reward structure to recover the unknown feature vectors of the robots. Future studies can relax this assumption and assume more flexible forms of reward. For example, with a general reward structure, one can use the NeuralUCB algorithm
(
39
)
to compute the upper confidence bounds of the matching rewards and then make the optimistic matching.

Proof of Theorem
4

We first define probability measures on linear matching bandits. Consider the linear matching bandit
\nu=(\theta_{1},\dotsc,\theta_{K},\mathcal{X}_{1},\dotsc,\mathcal{X}_{t})
defined in Section
3
. Let
(A_{1},Y_{1},\dotsc,A_{t},Y_{t})
be the action-reward sequence produced by the interaction between
\nu
and a policy
\pi=(\pi_{s})_{s=1}^{t}
. We define the probability space as
\Omega_{s}=\left(\mathcal{A}_{M,K},\mathbb{R}^{M}\right)^{s}
and sigma algebra as
\mathcal{F}_{s}=\mathcal{B}(\Omega_{s})
for
s\in[t]
. A policy
\pi=(\pi_{s})_{s=1}^{t}
is a sequence such that
\pi_{s}
is a probability kernel from
(\Omega_{s-1},\mathcal{F}_{s-1})
to
(\mathcal{A}_{M,K},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{A}_{M,K}))
. The probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
on
(\Omega_{t},\mathcal{F}_{t})
satisfies

\mathbb{P}_{\nu}(A_{s}|A_{1},Y_{1},\dotsc,A_{s-1},Y_{s-1})=\pi_{s}(A_{s}|A_{1},Y_{1},\dotsc,A_{s-1},Y_{s-1})

and

\mathbb{P}_{\nu}(Y_{s}|A_{1},Y_{1},\dotsc,A_{s-1},Y_{s-1},A_{s})=P_{A_{s}}(Y_{s}),

where
P_{A_{s}}(Y_{s})
is the conditional distribution of the reward
Y_{s}
given the matching
A_{s}
.

Proof.

By its definition, the probability density function
p
of
\mathbb{P}
satisfies

p(a_{1},y_{1},\dotsc,a_{t},y_{t})=\prod_{s=1}^{t}p(a_{s}|a_{1},y_{1},\dotsc,y_{s-1})p(y_{s}|a_{1},y_{1},\dotsc,a_{s})=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\pi_{s}(a_{s}|a_{1},y_{1},\dotsc,y_{s-1})p_{a_{s}}(y_{s}).

Because
\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{\nu},\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{\prime}})<\infty
,
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{\prime}}
, and thus
\frac{p}{p^{\prime}}(A_{1},,\dotsc,A_{t},Y_{t})
is well defined. Therefore,

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{\nu},\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{\prime}})=\mathbb{E}_{\nu\pi}\left[\log\frac{p}{p^{\prime}}(A_{1},,\dotsc,A_{t},Y_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu\pi}\left[\log\frac{\prod_{s=1}^{t}\pi_{s}(A_{s}|A_{1},Y_{1},\dotsc,Y_{s-1})p_{A_{s}}(Y_{s})}{\prod_{s=1}^{t}\pi_{s}(A_{s}|A_{1},Y_{1},\dotsc,Y_{s-1})p_{A_{s}}^{\prime}(Y_{s})}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\nu\pi}\left[\log\frac{\prod_{s=1}^{t}\prod_{m=1}^{M}p_{m,A_{s}}(Y_{m,s})}{\prod_{s=1}^{t}\prod_{m=1}^{M}p_{m,A_{s}}^{\prime}(Y_{m,s})}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{A_{s}\sim\nu\pi}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\nu\pi}\left[\log\frac{\prod_{s=1}^{t}\prod_{m=1}^{M}p_{m,A_{s}}(Y_{m,s})}{\prod_{s=1}^{t}\prod_{m=1}^{M}p_{m,A_{s}}^{\prime}(Y_{m,s})}\middle|A_{s}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{t}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}_{A_{s}\sim\nu\pi}\left[\operatorname{KL}\left(P_{m,A_{s}},P_{m,A_{s}}^{\prime}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

D.4
Proof of Theorem
3

Proof.

We construct a hard adversarial environment utilizing a dense hypercube packing. Let the true parameter space for the robots be
\Theta=\{-\Delta,\Delta\}^{d\times K}
, where
\Delta>0
is an adversarial perturbation parameter. For each robot
k\in[K]
, its true parameter vector is
\theta_{k}\in\{-\Delta,\Delta\}^{d}
.

Assume the human context vectors
X_{m,t}
arriving at each round are drawn independently and uniformly from the unscaled hypercube
\{-1,1\}^{d}
. The observed reward for a matched pair is
Y_{m,t}=X_{m,t}^{\top}\theta_{A_{t}(m)}+\epsilon_{m,t}
, where
\epsilon_{m,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
denote the probability measure induced by policy
\pi
interacting with environment
\theta
, and let
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}
denote the corresponding expectation. By Assouad’s Lemma, the expected minimax regret is bounded by the sum of total variation distances between adjacent hypotheses. Let
\theta^{(k,j)}
be the hypothesis identical to
\theta
, except the sign of the
j
-th coordinate of robot
k
is flipped (
\theta_{k,j}^{(k,j)}=-\theta_{k,j}
). Since
X_{m,t}\in\{-1,1\}^{d}
, an error in estimating the sign of any coordinate incurs an expected instantaneous regret of
2\Delta
. Thus:

\mathcal{R}_{T}^{*}\geq\frac{\Delta}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left(1-\|\mathbb{P}_{\theta}-\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{(k,j)}}\|_{TV}\right)

Using Le Cam’s inequality, we can alternatively bound the total variation distance using the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence:

\|\mathbb{P}_{\theta}-\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{(k,j)}}\|_{TV}\leq 1-\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-KL(\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\|\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{(k,j)}})\right)

Let
N_{k}(T)
be the random variable denoting the total number of times robot
k
is matched over
T
rounds. By the chain rule for KL divergence, and noting that only observations from robot
k
differ between the two hypotheses, the divergence between the Gaussian reward distributions is:

KL(\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\|\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{(k,j)}})=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[(X^{\top}\theta_{k}-X^{\top}\theta_{k}^{(k,j)})^{2}\right]

Because
\theta_{k}
and
\theta_{k}^{(k,j)}
differ only in the
j
-th coordinate by exactly
2\Delta
, and
X_{j}\in\{-1,1\}
strictly implies
X_{j}^{2}=1
:

KL(\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\|\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{(k,j)}})=\frac{2\Delta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}

Substituting this back into the regret bound yields:

\mathcal{R}_{T}^{*}\geq\frac{\Delta d}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\exp\left(-\frac{2\Delta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\right)

The algorithm must match exactly
M
humans per round, strictly constraining the total matches:
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}=TM
. Applying Jensen’s inequality to the convex function
x\exp(-cx)
, the minimum is achieved when pulls are distributed uniformly,
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}=TM/K
:

\mathcal{R}_{T}^{*}\geq\frac{\Delta dTM}{2}\exp\left(-\frac{2\Delta^{2}TM}{\sigma^{2}K}\right)

We optimize the adversarial parameter
\Delta
by setting
\Delta=\sigma\sqrt{\frac{K}{4TM}}
. This bounds the exponential term by
e^{-1/2}
. Substituting
\Delta
:

\mathcal{R}_{T}^{*}\geq\frac{dTM}{2}\left(\sigma\sqrt{\frac{K}{4TM}}\right)e^{-1/2}=\frac{e^{-1/2}}{4}\sigma\cdot d\sqrt{MKT}

Thus, we conclude that
\mathcal{R}_{T}^{*}=\Omega(d\sqrt{MKT})
, strictly matching the upper bound up to logarithmic factors.
∎

Limitations

Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors?

Guidelines:

The answer

•

•

The paper should point out any strong assumptions and how robust the results are to violations of these assumptions (e.g., independence assumptions, noiseless settings, model well-specification, asymptotic approximations only holding locally). The authors should reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the implications would be.

•

The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was only tested on a few datasets or with a few runs. In general, empirical results often depend on implicit assumptions, which should be articulated.

•

The authors should reflect on the factors that influence the performance of the approach. For example, a facial recognition algorithm may perform poorly when image resolution is low or images are taken in low lighting. Or a speech-to-text system might not be used reliably to provide closed captions for online lectures because it fails to handle technical jargon.

•

The authors should discuss the computational efficiency of the proposed algorithms and how they scale with dataset size.

•

If applicable, the authors should discuss possible limitations of their approach to address problems of privacy and fairness.

•

While the authors might fear that complete honesty about limitations might be used by reviewers as grounds for rejection, a worse outcome might be that reviewers discover limitations that aren’t acknowledged in the paper. The authors should use their best judgment and recognize that individual actions in favor of transparency play an important role in developing norms that preserve the integrity of the community.
