Unified generalization analysis for physics informed neural networks
Yuka Hashimoto, Tomoharu Iwata1. NTT, Inc. 2. RIKEN AIP

Abstract
Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) and their variational counterparts (VPINNs) are neural networks that incorporate physical laws, making them useful for scientific problems. Existing generalization analyses for PINNs and VPINNs remain limited, often requiring restrictive assumptions such as stability conditions or linear ellipticity. In this paper, we derive generalization bounds for neural networks that involve differentiation with respect to input variables, covering PINNs and VPINNs under a unified framework. We apply Taylor expansion to represent nonlinear differential operators as linear operators on a high-dimensional space, enabling the use of Koopman-based analysis and showing that high-rank networks can generalize well even in settings involving differential operators. We also show that the nonlinearity of the differential operator exponentially enlarges the bound, highlighting its significant impact on generalization.



1
Introduction

Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) are a class of machine learning methods designed to solve differential equations by incorporating physical laws directly into the training process of neural networks
[
24
]
.
The key idea is to train a neural network to approximate the solution of a physical system while ensuring that it satisfies the underlying equations, boundary conditions, and initial conditions.
PINNs have gained attention as part of the broader field of scientific machine learning (SciML), offering a flexible framework for solving forward and inverse problems, including parameter estimation and system identification
[
25
]
.
They are particularly useful in areas such as fluid dynamics, solid mechanics, and diffusion processes, where traditional numerical methods may be computationally expensive or difficult to apply
[
8
,
7
]
.
Variational Physics-Informed Neural Networks (VPINNs) are a class of
PINNs that employs a weak (variational) formulation.
They are recognized as one of the more accurate and stable approaches in this
family of methods
[
6
]
.

Generalization error analysis of neural networks has been a major topic in machine learning for understanding the performance of models.
Generalization error measures the gap between the training error and the true error, and characterizes how well a model fits unseen test data.
A typical approach for deriving generalization bounds is bounding some complexity of networks
[
4
,
21
]
.
Classically, the VC-dimension theory
[
15
,
1
]
has been applied to show a large number of parameters makes the complexity and generalization error large.
For over-parameterized regimes, norm-based bounds have been investigated
[
23
,
3
,
12
,
22
,
27
,
28
,
19
,
18
,
29
]
.
These bounds do not depend on the number of parameters explicitly.
Another approach to tackle over-parameterized networks is a compression-based approach
[
2
,
26
]
, which explain the generalization of networks by investigating how much the networks can be compressed.
These bounds get small as the ranks of the weight matrices become small.
On the other hand, Hashimoto et al.
[
17
,
16
]
proposed Koopman-based bounds, which describes that high-rank networks can also generalize well.
They represent the composition structure of neural networks using linear operators called Koopman operators and derive a generalization bound by evaluating the norm of Koopman operators.
Using this approach, the determinants of weight matrices appear in the denominator of the bound, which implies high-rank weight matrices such as unitary matrices can also make the generalization error small.

The analyses in the previous paragraph are valid only for standard neural networks without differential operators since the loss function depends on the derivative of neural networks, instead of the original neural networks.
Generalization error analysis for PINNs has also been investigated to clarify how the number of collocation points and the architecture of the neural network affect the performance.
However, the applicability of existing analyses is limited.
For example, the stability assumption
[
20
]
, the linear elliptic assumption
[
30
]
are needed for deriving the bounds.
There are no unified analysis with general settings.
In particular, generalization error analysis for VPINNs is extremely limited.
Berrone et al.
[
5
]
analyzed VPINNs, but the linear elliptic assumption is also needed in this case.

In this paper, we derive a generalization bound for neural networks involving differentiations of neural networks with respect to input variables, including PINNs and VPINNs.
Our setting is suitable for various types of nonlinear differential equations, which can be approximated by polynomials.
We apply the Taylor expansion and describe the nonlinear differential operator with a linear operator on a high dimensional space.
This enables us to consider the “adjoint” of the operator and separate the model from the differential operator.
Building on this decoupled structure, we extend the Koopman-based framework to settings involving differential operators, thereby establishing new generalization bounds that reveal high-rank networks can generalize well even in the presence of differential operators.
In addition, the nonlinearity of the differential operator exponentially makes the bound large, which implies that the nonlinearity significantly affects the generalization property.
We primarily focus on VPINNs, but the model that we consider in this paper is interpreted as an approximation of the standard PINNs.
Thus, the results are expected to also be effective for the standard PINNs.
In addition, we can also apply our results to other neural networks involving differentia operators, such as Hamiltonian neural networks
[
14
]
and Lagrangian neural networks
[
10
]
.
Practically, our bound is useful to design regularization terms.
By learning neural networks so that our bound becomes small, we can obtain high test performances.
This paper sheds light on the generalization property of neural networks involving derivatives in a unified manner and shows the connection to the Koopman-based analysis.

Preliminaries

We review mathematical notions and existing results related to this paper.

2.1
Fréchet derivative and Taylor’s theorem for Banach spaces

We introduce the derivative of a map from a Banach space to a Banach space.
Let
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}
be Banach spaces, and let
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{F}
be an open subset.

Definition 1

A mapping
\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{E}
is said to be
Fréchet differentiable
at a point
u\in\mathcal{U}
if there exists a bounded linear operator
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E})
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{\|h\|_{\mathcal{F}}\to 0}\frac{\|\mathcal{D}(u+h)-\mathcal{D}(u)-\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(u)h\|_{\mathcal{E}}}{\|h\|_{\mathcal{F}}}=0.

The operator
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(u)
is called the
Fréchet derivative
of
\mathcal{D}
at
u
.
We denote by
\mathcal{D}^{(i)}
, the
i
th Fréchet derivative.
If
\mathcal{D}
is Fréchet differentiable at every point in
\mathcal{U}
, then
\mathcal{D}
is said to be Fréchet differentiable on
\mathcal{U}
.

We denote by
\mathcal{C}^{r}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{E})
the space of functions
\mathcal{D}
where
\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime},\ldots,\mathcal{D}^{(r-1)}
are Fréchet differentiable at each
v\in\mathcal{U}
,

\mathcal{D}^{(r)}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F}^{\otimes r},\mathcal{E})
is continuous. Here,
\mathcal{F}^{\otimes r}=\mathcal{F}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{F}
(
r
copies) and
\otimes
is the tensor product.

Similar to the case of the standard derivative, we have the following Taylor’s theorem with the Fŕechet derivative.

Proposition 2

Let
\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{C}^{r}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{E})
,

v\in\mathcal{U}
, and
u\in\mathcal{F}
so small that

\{v+tu\mid 0\leq t\leq 1\}\subset\mathcal{U}
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(v+u)=\sum_{i=0}^{r}\frac{\mathcal{D}^{(i)}(v)\,u^{\otimes i}}{i!}+S(v,u)\,u^{\otimes r},

where
S(v,u)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-t)^{r-1}}{(r-1)!}(\mathcal{D}^{(r)}(v+tu)-\mathcal{D}^{(r)}(v))\mathrm{d}t
is the remainder.

2.2
Koopman operator and Koopman-based Rademacher complexity bound

Koopman operator is a linear operator that represents the composition of nonlinear functions.
Since neural networks are constructed using compositions, Koopman operators play an essential role in analyzing neural networks.
Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Lebesgue measure space.
Koopman operators are defined as follows.

Definition 3
(Koopman operator)

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
.
The Koopman operator
K_{\sigma}
with respect to a map
\sigma:\tilde{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathcal{X}
is a linear operator from
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}})
to
L^{2}(\tilde{\mathcal{X}},\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}})
that is defined as
K_{\sigma}h(x)=h(\sigma(x))
for
h\in L^{2}(\tilde{\mathcal{X}},\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}})
.

We will consider the Koopman operators with respect to activation functions.
Throughout this paper, we assume these Koopman operators are bounded.
Indeed, we have the following lemma regarding the sufficient condition of the boundedness of Koopman operators.

Lemma 4

Assume
\sigma:\tilde{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathcal{X}
is bijective,
\sigma^{-1}
is differentiable, and the Jacobian of
\sigma^{-1}
is bounded in
\mathcal{X}
.
Then, we have
\|K_{\sigma}\|\leq\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}|J\sigma^{-1}(x)|^{1/2}
, where
J\sigma^{-1}
is the Jacobian of
\sigma^{-1}
.
In particular, the Koopman operator
K_{\sigma}
is bounded.

The following lemma is regarding the boundedness of well-known elementwise activation functions defined as
\sigma([x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}])=[\tilde{\sigma}(x_{1}),\cdots,\tilde{\sigma}(x_{d})]
for a map
\tilde{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 5

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}=[a_{1},b_{1}]\times\cdots\times[a_{d},b_{d}]\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded rectangular domain, and let
\mathcal{X}=\sigma(\tilde{\mathcal{X}})
.
If
\sigma
is the elementwise hyperbolic tangent defined as
\tilde{\sigma}(x)=\tanh(x)
, then we have
\mathcal{X}\subset[-1,1]^{d}
and
\|K_{\sigma}\|\leq(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\sup_{x\in\tilde{\sigma}([a_{i},b_{i}])}1/(1-x^{2}))^{1/2}
.
If
\sigma
is the elementwise sigmoid defined as
\tilde{\sigma}(x)=1/(1+\mathrm{e}^{-x})
, then we have
\mathcal{X}\subset[-1,1]^{d}
and
\|K_{\sigma}\|\leq(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\sup_{x\in\tilde{\sigma}([a_{i},b_{i}])}1/(x-x^{2}))^{1/2}
.

Hashimoto et al.
[
17
,
16
]
proposed applying Koopman operators to derive Rademacher complexity bounds of neural networks.
Let
d_{l}\in\mathbb{N}
,
D>0
, and
\Theta_{l}=\{W\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}\times d_{l-1}}\,\mid\,|\det{W_{l}^{*}W_{l}}|^{-1/4}\leq D\}
.
Here,
∗
represents the adjoint.
Let
\mathcal{X}_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
be the input space,
W_{l}\sigma_{l-1}(W_{l-1}\cdots W_{2}\sigma_{1}(W_{1}\mathcal{X}_{0}))\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{X}_{l}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}}
, and
\sigma_{l}(W_{l}\cdots W_{2}\sigma_{1}(W_{1}\mathcal{X}_{0}))\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{l}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}}
that satisfy
\mu_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}}}(\mathcal{X}_{l})>0
and
\mu_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}}}(\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{l})>0
.
Here,
\mu_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}}}
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}}
and
\sigma_{l}:\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{l}\to\mathcal{X}_{l}
is the activation function at layer
l
.
We assume the Koopman operator
K_{\sigma_{l}}
is bounded.
Let
v\in L^{2}(\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{L},\mathcal{X}_{L})
be the final nonlinear transformation.
Consider the neural network

\displaystyle u_{\theta}(x)=v(W_{L}\sigma_{L-1}(W_{L-1}\sigma_{L-2}(\cdots\sigma_{1}(W_{1}x))))

(1)

with learnable parameters
\theta=(W_{1},\ldots,W_{L})
.

Let
\alpha(h)=({\int_{W_{l}\mathcal{X}_{l-1}}|h(x)|^{2}d\mu_{\mathcal{R}(W_{l})}(x)}/{\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{l}}|h(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}}}(x)})^{1/2}
for
h\in L^{2}(\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{l},\mathcal{X}_{L})
.
Let
f_{l}=v\circ W_{L}\circ\sigma_{L-1}\circ\cdots\circ W_{l+1}\circ\sigma_{l}
.
They derived the following lemma regarding the
L^{2}
norm of
u_{\theta}
.

Lemma 6

We have

\displaystyle\|u_{\theta}\|\leq\frac{\|v\|\prod_{l=1}^{L-1}\|K_{\sigma_{l}}\|\alpha(f_{l})}{\prod_{l=1}^{L}|\det W_{l}^{*}W_{l}|^{1/4}}.

Let
p_{x}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}})
for
x_{n}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
, and consider a regularized model
V_{\theta}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{0}}u_{\theta}(y)\cdot p_{x}(y)\mathrm{d}y
.
Let
D>0
and
\mathcal{V}_{\Theta}=\{V_{\theta}\,\mid\,\theta\in\Theta\}
, where
\Theta=\{(W_{1},\ldots,W_{L})\,\mid\,W_{l}\in\Theta_{l}\}
.
Using Lemma
6
, they derive the following bound.

Proposition 7

Assume there exists
F>0
such that
\|p_{x_{n}}\|\leq F
.
Then, the empirical Rademacher complexity of
\mathcal{V}_{\Theta}
and samples
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{F\|v\|\prod_{l=1}^{L-1}\|K_{\sigma_{l}}\|\alpha(f_{l})}{\sqrt{N}\prod_{l=1}^{L}|\det W_{l}^{*}W_{l}|^{1/4}}.

(2)

The right hand side of (
2
) includes the determinants of the weight matrices.
Thus, the bounds can become smaller even if
W_{l}
has large singular values.
This result with Koopman operators describes why networks with high-rank weight matrices generalize well.

3
Problem setting

We consider a model with differentiations of a neural network with respect to inputs.
Typical examples are PINNs, Hamiltonian neural networks, and Lagrangian neural networks.
Let
d,\tilde{d}\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain,
C>0
, and
\mathcal{F}:=\{u\in H^{s}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}})\,\mid\,\|u\|_{H^{s}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}})}\leq C\}
.
Here,
H^{s}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}})
is the Sobolev space of order
s
from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}}
.
Let
\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{F}\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}})
be a differential operator of order
s
.
Note that
\mathcal{D}
can be nonlinear.
Let
\Theta
be a parameter space and consider a neural network
u_{\theta}\in\mathcal{F}
parametrized by
\theta\in\Theta
.
We denote by
\mathcal{U}_{\Theta}
the class of neural networks parametrized by
\Theta
.
Consider the differentiation of the neural network
\mathcal{D}(u_{\theta})
and its variational form:

\displaystyle V_{\theta}(x_{n})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{D}(u_{\theta})(y)\cdot p_{x}(y)\mathrm{d}y=\langle p_{x_{n}},\mathcal{D}(u_{\theta})\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{X})}\quad(n=1,\ldots,N),

where
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in\mathcal{X}
are collocation points and for
x_{n}
,
p_{x_{n}}\in H^{s}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}})
is a nonzero test function satisfying

p_{x_{n}}^{(r)}(x)=0
for
x\in\partial\mathcal{X}
,
n=1,\ldots,N
,
r=1,\ldots,s
and there exists
B>0
such that for
n=1,\ldots,N
,
\|p_{x_{n}}\|\leq B
.
We denote
\mathcal{V}_{\Theta}=\{V_{\theta}\,\mid\,u_{\theta}\in\mathcal{U}_{\Theta}\}
, the class of regularized models.
Consider a
L
-Lipschitz continuous loss function
\mathcal{L}_{n}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
for
n=1,\ldots,N
and minimize the mean loss:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}_{n}\bigg({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V_{\theta}(x_{n})}\bigg).

(3)

Typical examples of the test function
q_{x_{n}}
and the loss function
\mathcal{L}_{n}
are as follows.

Example 1

Let
q_{x_{n}}(y)=\mathrm{e}^{-1/(1-c^{2}\|y\|^{2})}
for
\|y\|\leq 1/c
and
q_{x_{n}}(y)=0
for
\|y\|>1/c
.
Let
\tilde{d}=1
and
p_{x_{n}}(y)=q_{n}(y)/(\int_{\mathcal{X}}q_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z
).
Then,
p_{x_{n}}
is infinitely differentiable.
Consider a PDE
\mathcal{D}(u)=f
for
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}})
.
Let
\mathcal{L}_{n}(z)=|z-\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(y)p_{x_{n}}(y)\mathrm{d}y|^{2}
.
Assume for some
D>0
and
E>0
,
|\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{D}(u_{\theta})(y)p_{x_{n}}(y)\mathrm{d}y|\leq D
and
|\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(y)p_{x_{n}}(y)\mathrm{d}y|\leq E
.
Then,
\mathcal{L}_{n}
is
2(D+E)
-Lipchitz.
Note that since
p_{x_{n}}
goes to the delta function as
c\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{c\to\infty}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{D}(u_{\theta})(y)p_{x_{n}}(y)\mathrm{d}y=\mathcal{D}(u_{\theta})(x_{n}).

(4)

In this case, Eq. (
3
) is the loss for the variational form such as VPINNs, but as
c\to\infty
, it becomes the loss for the strong form, such as PINNs for the interior collocation point
x_{n}
.

4
Generalization bounds

Our goal is to derive Koopman-based generalization bounds of the variational form
V_{\theta}
.
Since we have the differential operator
\mathcal{D}
in
V_{\theta}
and the loss function depends on the derivative of the neural network, not the neural network itself, the derivation is not straightforward.
This is the main difference between the existing setting in Proposition
7
and our setting.
To tackle this situation, we first consider a simple case with linear differential operator
\mathcal{D}
.
In this case, we can consider the adjoint of
\mathcal{D}
.
We then consider nonlinear cases.
Applying the Taylor expansion, we can regard
\mathcal{D}
as a linear operator on a higher dimensional space, which enables us to consider a similar notion of the adjoint of
\mathcal{D}
.

Since the loss function
\mathcal{L}_{n}
is Lipchitz continuous, we can derive a generalization bound by deriving a Rademacher complexity bound for the function class
\{\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{D}(u_{\theta})(y)\cdot p_{x_{n}}(y)\mathrm{d}y\,\mid\,u_{\theta}\in\mathcal{U}_{\Theta}\}

[
21
, Theorem 3.5]
.
Let
\Omega
be a probability space equipped with a probability measure
P
.
Let
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{N}:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
be i.i.d. Rademacher variables (random variables following the uniform distribution on
\{-1,1\}
).
For a measurable function
\epsilon:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
, we denote by
\mathrm{E}[\epsilon]
the integral
\int_{\Omega}\epsilon(\omega)\mathrm{d}P(\omega)
.
The empirical Rademacher complexity
\hat{R}(\mathcal{U},x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
of a function class
\mathcal{U}
is defined as
\hat{R}(\mathcal{U},x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\mathrm{E}[\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}u(x_{n})\epsilon_{n}]/N
.

4.1
Linear case

Assume
\mathcal{D}
is bounded and linear.
Then, by the Cauchy Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\langle p_{x_{n}},\mathcal{D}(u_{\theta})\rangle=\bigg\langle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{D}^{*}p_{x_{n}},u_{\theta}\bigg\rangle\leq\|u_{\theta}\|\,\bigg\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{D}^{*}p_{x_{n}}\bigg\|,

where
∗
means the adjoint.
This transformation enables us to force the action of
\mathcal{D}
to
p_{x_{n}}
, instead of
u_{\theta}
, and to apply the same analysis as the neural network
u_{\theta}
without
\mathcal{D}
.
Indeed, we have the following theorem.
The proof is documented in Appendix
A

Theorem 8

Let
\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}}=\mathcal{D}^{*}p_{x_{n}}
.
Assume there exists
F>0
such that for any
n=1,\ldots,N
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\|\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}}\|}\leq F
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\hat{R}(\mathcal{V}_{\Theta},x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\leq\frac{F}{\sqrt{N}}\sup_{u_{\theta}\in\mathcal{U}_{\Theta}}\|u_{\theta}\|.

4.2
Polynomial case

When
\mathcal{D}
is nonlinear, we do not have
\mathcal{D}^{*}
.
However, if
\mathcal{D}
is approximated by a polynomial, then we can construct a linear operator on a higher dimensional space.
Then, we have the adjoint of the linear operator.

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\in\mathcal{C}^{r}(\mathcal{F},L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R}^{d}))
be a differential operator whose remainder
S(0,u_{\theta})
of
r
-order Taylor approximation at
0
is bounded as
\|S(0,u_{\theta})\|\leq\epsilon
for some
\epsilon>0
.
Assume
\mathcal{D}
is in the following form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(u_{\theta})=g\circ u_{\theta}+\tilde{\mathcal{D}}(u_{\theta})

(5)

with
g:\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}}
.
In many real-world physical systems, such as fluid flows and diffusion-dominated problems, terms in
\mathcal{D}
with differentiations are polynomials and non-polynomial terms only depend on the solution
u
, not the derivatives of
u
.
In this case, we can regard
\mathcal{D}
as a linear operator on a higher dimensional space and we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\langle p_{x_{n}},\mathcal{D}(u_{\theta})\rangle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bigg(\left\langle p_{x_{n}},g\circ u_{\theta}\right\rangle+\sum_{i=0}^{r}\frac{1}{i!}\langle p_{x_{n}},\tilde{\mathcal{D}}^{(i)}(0)u_{\theta}^{\otimes i}\rangle+\langle p_{x_{n}},S(0,u_{\theta})u_{\theta}^{\otimes r}\rangle\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad=\bigg\langle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}},\bigoplus_{i=0}^{r}u_{\theta}^{\otimes i}\oplus u_{\theta}^{\otimes r}\oplus(g\circ u_{\theta})\bigg\rangle\leq\bigg\|\bigoplus_{i=0}^{r}u_{\theta}^{\otimes i}\oplus u_{\theta}^{\otimes r}\oplus(g\circ u_{\theta})\bigg\|\,\bigg\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}}\bigg\|,

where
\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}}=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{r}\frac{1}{i!}\mathcal{D}^{(i)}(0)^{*}p_{x_{n}}\oplus S(0,u_{\theta})^{*}p_{x_{n}}\oplus p_{x_{n}}
.
Using this perspective, we have the following theorem in the same manner as Theorem
8
.

Theorem 9

Let
\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}}=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{r}\frac{1}{i!}\mathcal{D}^{(i)}(0)^{*}p_{x_{n}}\oplus S(0,u_{\theta})^{*}p_{x_{n}}\oplus p_{x_{n}}
.
Assume there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\|S(0,u_{\theta})\|\leq\epsilon
.
Assume, in addition, there exists
F>0
such that for any
n=1,\ldots,N
,
\|\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}}\|\leq F
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\hat{R}(\mathcal{V}_{\Theta},x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{F}{\sqrt{N}}\sup_{u_{\theta}\in\mathcal{U}_{\Theta}}\bigg\|\bigoplus_{i=0}^{r}u_{\theta}^{\otimes i}\oplus u_{\theta}^{\otimes r}\oplus(g\circ u_{\theta})\bigg\|

\displaystyle=\frac{F}{\sqrt{N}}\sup_{u_{\theta}\in\mathcal{U}_{\Theta}}\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{r}\|u_{\theta}\|^{2i}+\|u_{\theta}\|^{2r}+\|g\circ u_{\theta}\|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}.

The order
r
represents the nonlinearlity of
\mathcal{D}
.
Thus, Theorem
9
shows that as the nonlinearlity of
\mathcal{D}
grows, the Rademacher complexity becomes large.

Remark 10

Since we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}},\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{r}\bigg\|\frac{1}{i!}\mathcal{D}^{(i)}(0)^{*}p_{x_{n}}\bigg\|^{2}+\|S(0,u_{\theta})^{*}p_{x_{n}}\|^{2}+\|p_{x_{n}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{r}\bigg\|\frac{1}{i!}\mathcal{D}^{(i)}(0)^{*}p_{x_{n}}\bigg\|^{2}+(\epsilon^{2}+1)\|p_{x_{n}}\|^{2},

the constant
F
does not depend on
\theta
.

Example 2

Let
d=\tilde{d}
. Consider the nonlinear convection operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(u)=(u\cdot\nabla)u=\sum_{k=1}^{d}u_{k}\,\partial_{k}u_{j}=[1,\ldots,1]\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{t_{1}}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\partial_{t_{d}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{u}_{11}&\cdots&\tilde{u}_{1d}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\tilde{u}_{1d}&\cdots&\tilde{u}_{dd}\end{bmatrix},

where
\tilde{u}_{ij}(s,t)=u_{i}(s)\,u_{j}(t)=(u_{i}\otimes u_{j})(s,t)

for
s=[s_{1},\ldots,s_{d}],[t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Note that this operator is the second order polynomial with respect to
u
.
This operator appears, for example, in the Navier–Stokes equation.

The adjoint
\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime}(0)^{*}
acts on
v
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime}(0)^{*}v

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{t_{1}}^{*}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\partial^{*}_{t_{d}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\vdots\\
1\end{bmatrix}[v_{1}\;\cdots\;v_{d}]=\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{1}^{*}v_{1}&\cdots&\partial_{1}^{*}v_{d}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\partial_{d}^{*}v_{1}&\cdots&\partial_{d}^{*}v_{d}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{1}v_{1}&\cdots&\partial_{1}v_{d}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\partial_{d}v_{1}&\cdots&\partial_{d}v_{d}\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime}(0)^{*}v\|^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\|\partial_{i}v_{j}\|^{2},

which shows that
\|\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime}(0)^{*}p_{x_{n}}\|
does not depend on
x_{n}
if the norm of the derivative of
p_{x_{n}}
does not depend on
x_{n}
.
For example, if
p_{x_{n}}
is shift-invariant, i.e.,
p_{x_{n}}(y)=\tilde{p}(y-x_{n})
for some
\tilde{p}
, then the norm of the derivative of
p_{x_{n}}
does not depend on
x_{n}
.
As a result, by Remark
10
, the assumption
\|\mathcal{D}_{x_{n}}\|<F
in Theorem
9
is satisfied.
Note that since
\mathcal{D}
is the second-order polynomial, the order
r
in Theorem
9
is
r=2
in this case.

4.3
Nonlinear transformation of a linear differential operator

Another important nonlinear form is the nonlinear transformation of a linear differential operator.
Let
\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{F}\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}})
be linear.
Assume
\mathcal{D}
is in the following form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(u_{\theta})=g\circ\tilde{\mathcal{D}}u_{\theta}

(6)

with
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}:\mathcal{F}\to L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{\hat{d}})
being bounded linear and
g:\mathbb{R}^{\hat{d}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}}
.
Typical examples are the Hamilton–Jacobi equations
[
9
]
and the Monge–Ampère equations
[
13
]
.
Assume there exists
\epsilon>0
, and assume
g\in\mathcal{C}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{\hat{d}},\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}})
whose remainder
S(0,\tilde{\mathcal{D}}u_{\theta}(x))
of the
r
-order Taylor approximation at
0
is bounded as
\|S(0,\tilde{\mathcal{D}}u_{\theta}(x))\|\leq\epsilon
for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
In this case, we can approximate
\mathcal{D}
as a polynomial of
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}u_{\theta}
, which is represented as a linear operator on a higher dimensional space.
We have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\langle p_{x_{n}},\mathcal{D}(u_{\theta})\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{r}\frac{1}{i!}\langle p_{x_{n}},g^{(i)}(0)(\tilde{\mathcal{D}}u_{\theta}(\cdot))^{\otimes i}\rangle+\langle p_{x_{n}},S(0,\tilde{\mathcal{D}}u_{\theta}(\cdot))(\tilde{\mathcal{D}}u_{\theta}(\cdot))^{\otimes r}\rangle\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{r}\frac{1}{i!}\langle p_{x_{n}},g^{(i)}(0)P^{i}\tilde{\mathcal{D}}^{\otimes i}u_{\theta}^{\otimes i}\rangle+\langle S(0,\tilde{\mathcal{D}}u_{\theta}(\cdot))^{*}p_{x_{n}}(\cdot),P^{r}\tilde{\mathcal{D}}^{\otimes r}u_{\theta}^{\otimes r}\rangle\bigg)

\displaystyle=\bigg\langle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}},\bigoplus_{i=0}^{r}u_{\theta}^{\otimes i}\oplus u_{\theta}^{\otimes r}\bigg\rangle\leq\bigg\|\bigoplus_{i=0}^{r}u_{\theta}^{\otimes i}\oplus u_{\theta}^{\otimes r}\bigg\|\,\bigg\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}}\bigg\|,

where
\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}}=\bigotimes_{i=0}^{r}1/(i!)(\tilde{\mathcal{D}}^{\otimes i})^{*}(P^{i})^{*}g^{(i)}(0)^{*}p_{x_{n}}\oplus(\tilde{\mathcal{D}}^{\otimes r})^{*}(P^{r})^{*}S(0,\tilde{\mathcal{D}}u_{\theta}(\cdot))^{*}p_{x_{n}}(\cdot)
,
P^{i}:L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}})^{\otimes i}\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X},(\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{d}})^{\otimes i})
is defined as
P^{i}u^{\otimes i}(x)=u^{\otimes i}(x,\ldots,x)=u(x)^{\otimes i}
, and for functions
u
and
v
of
x
,
u(\cdot)v(\cdot)
means
x\mapsto u(x)v(x)
.
In the same manner as Theorem
8
, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 11

Let
\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}}=\bigotimes_{i=0}^{r}1/(i!)(\tilde{\mathcal{D}}^{\otimes i})^{*}(P^{i})^{*}g^{(i)}(0)^{*}p_{x_{n}}\oplus(\tilde{\mathcal{D}}^{\otimes r})^{*}(P^{r})^{*}S(0,\tilde{\mathcal{D}}u_{\theta}(\cdot))^{*}p_{x_{n}}(\cdot)
.
Assume there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\|S(0,\tilde{\mathcal{D}}u_{\theta})\|\leq\epsilon
.
Assume, in addition, there exists
F>0
such that for any
n=1,\ldots,N
,
\|\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}}\|\leq F
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\hat{R}(\mathcal{V}_{\Theta},x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{F}{\sqrt{N}}\sup_{u_{\theta}\in\mathcal{U}_{\Theta}}\bigg\|\bigoplus_{i=0}^{r}u_{\theta}^{\otimes i}\oplus u_{\theta}^{\otimes r}\bigg\|

\displaystyle=\frac{F}{\sqrt{N}}\sup_{u_{\theta}\in\mathcal{U}_{\Theta}}\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{r}\|u_{\theta}\|^{2i}+\|u_{\theta}\|^{2r}\bigg)^{1/2}.

The order
r
represents the nonlinearity of
g
in this case.
Thus, Theorem
11
shows that as the nonlinearity of
g
grows, the Rademacher complexity becomes large.
Note that in the same manner as Remark
10
, the factor
F
does not depend on
\theta
.

4.4
Application of Koopman-based bounds

Since Theorems
8
,
9
, and
11
includes the term
\|u_{\theta}\|
, we can apply Lemma
6
to understand the relationship between the Rademacher complexity and the weight matrices.
We set
\mathcal{X}_{0}=\mathcal{X}
,
d_{L}=\tilde{d}
.
Consider the neural network (
1
).
Then, we obtain the following bound.

Let
\alpha(h)=({\int_{W_{l}\mathcal{X}_{l-1}}|h(x)|^{2}d\mu_{\mathcal{R}(W_{l})}(x)}/{\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{l}}|h(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}}}(x)})^{1/2}
for
h\in\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{l}
.
Let

\Theta=\{(W_{1},\ldots,W_{L})\,\mid\,|\det{W_{1}^{*}W_{1}}|^{-1/4},\ldots,|\det{W_{L}^{*}W_{L}}|^{-1/4}\leq D\}

Theorem 12

Assume
\mathcal{D}
is linear.
Let
\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}}=\mathcal{D}^{*}p_{x_{n}}
.
Assume there exists

such that for any

,
\|\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}}\|\leq F
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\hat{R}(\mathcal{V}_{\Theta},x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\leq\frac{F}{\sqrt{N}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{\|v\|\prod_{l=1}^{L-1}\|K_{\sigma_{l}}\|\alpha(f_{l})}{\prod_{l=1}^{L}|\det W_{l}^{*}W_{l}|^{1/4}}.

Theorem 13

Assume
\mathcal{D}
is in the form (

).

.
Assume there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\|S(0,u_{\theta})\|\leq\epsilon
.
Assume, in addition, there exists
F>0
such that for any

,
\|\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}}\|\leq F
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\hat{R}(\mathcal{V}_{\Theta},x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{F}{\sqrt{N}}\sup_{\theta\in{\Theta}}\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{r}\bigg(\frac{\|v\|\prod_{l=1}^{L-1}\|K_{\sigma_{l}}\|\alpha(f_{l})}{\prod_{l=1}^{L}|\det W_{l}^{*}W_{l}|^{1/4}}\bigg)^{2i}+\bigg(\frac{\|v\|\prod_{l=1}^{L-1}\|K_{\sigma_{l}}\|\alpha(f_{l})}{\prod_{l=1}^{L}|\det W_{l}^{*}W_{l}|^{1/4}}\bigg)^{2r}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\bigg(\frac{\|g\circ v\|\prod_{l=1}^{L-1}\|K_{\sigma_{l}}\|\alpha(f_{l})}{\prod_{l=1}^{L}|\det W_{l}^{*}W_{l}|^{1/4}}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}.

Theorem 14

Assume
\mathcal{D}
is in the form (
6
).
Let

\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}}=\bigotimes_{i=0}^{r}1/(i!)(\tilde{\mathcal{D}}^{\otimes i})^{*}(P^{i})^{*}g^{(i)}(0)^{*}p_{x_{n}}\oplus(\tilde{\mathcal{D}}^{\otimes r})^{*}(P^{r})^{*}S(0,\tilde{\mathcal{D}}u_{\theta}(\cdot))^{*}p_{x_{n}}(\cdot).

Assume there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\|S(0,\tilde{\mathcal{D}}u_{\theta})\|\leq\epsilon
.
Assume, in addition, there exists
F>0
such that for any
n=1,\ldots,N
,
\|\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}}\|\leq F
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\hat{R}(\mathcal{V}_{\Theta},x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{F}{\sqrt{N}}\sup_{\theta\in{\Theta}}\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{r}\bigg(\frac{\|v\|\prod_{l=1}^{L-1}\|K_{\sigma_{l}}\|\alpha(f_{l})}{\prod_{l=1}^{L}|\det W_{l}^{*}W_{l}|^{1/4}}\bigg)^{2i}+\bigg(\frac{\|v\|\prod_{l=1}^{L-1}\|K_{\sigma_{l}}\|\alpha(f_{l})}{\prod_{l=1}^{L}|\det W_{l}^{*}W_{l}|^{1/4}}\bigg)^{2r}\bigg)^{1/2}.

Since the determinants of weight matrices appear in the denominator of the bound, the bound implies that similar to the standard neural networks, for neural networks with differential operators, high-rank weight matrices such as unitary matrices can also make the generalization error small.
Regarding the factor

6
Conclusion and limitation

In this paper, we derived a new generalization bound for neural networks that involve differential operators with respect to input variables.
The proposed bound allows us to apply the Koopman-based approach, which shows that high-rank networks can generalize well even in settings involving differential operators.
It also shows that the nonlinearity of the differential operator exponentially enlarges the bound.

Our bound is a uniform bound that does not take the learning algorithm into account.
Deriving algorithm-dependent bounds using Koopman-based approach is left for future work.

Proof of Theorem

By the Cauchy–Schwartz inequality and the Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{u_{\theta}\in\mathcal{U}_{\Theta}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\langle p_{x_{n}},\mathcal{D}(u_{\theta})\rangle\,\epsilon_{n}\bigg]\leq\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{u_{\theta}\in\mathcal{U}_{\Theta}}\|u_{\theta}\|\,\bigg\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{D}^{*}p_{x_{n}}\epsilon_{n}\bigg\|\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sup_{u_{\theta}\in\mathcal{U}_{\Theta}}\|u_{\theta}\|\mathbb{E}\bigg(\bigg[\sum_{n,m=1}^{N}\langle\mathbb{D}_{x_{n}}\epsilon_{n},\,\mathbb{D}_{x_{m}}\epsilon_{m}\rangle\bigg]\bigg)^{1/2}

Appendix B
Regularization based on the proposed bound

We consider a regularization based on the bounds in theorems in Subsection
4.4
.
We set the space of pre-activation vectors as
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{l}=\|W_{l}\|[-1,1]^{d_{l-1}}+\|b_{l}\|_{\infty}
for

, where
d_{0}=d
and

is the output dimension of the

th layer for

.
According to Lemma

of
\|K_{\sigma_{l}}\|^{2}
.
To avoid that the upper bound becomes too large, we computed
\tilde{A}_{l}:=1/(1+1/A_{l})

A_{l}
.
The factor
\tilde{A}_{l}
increases monotonically as

Regarding the factor

, since

\displaystyle D_{l}=\Bigl(\prod_{k=1}^{R}\sigma_{k}\Bigr)^{1/R}=|\det W_{l}\bigr|^{1/R}=\exp\!\bigg(\frac{1}{R}\sum_{k=1}^{R}\ln\sigma_{k}\bigg)

By adding a regularization term

, we can make the factor
\|K_{\sigma_{l}}\|/|\det W_{l}^{*}W_{l}|^{1/4}
in the bound in Theorem
