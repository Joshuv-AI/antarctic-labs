Impaired behavioral inhibition in Fmr1 KO mice is linked to disrupted visual cortex theta oscillations
Authors: Michael P Zimmerman, Mowen Yin, Kevin R Cragg, Sanghamitra Nareddula, Varun M Kumar, Violeta Saldarriaga, Adriana Rotger, Rachel Lehman, Paige Edens, Caroline Powell, Jenna Barry, Sein Kim, Joseph G Makin, Alexander A Chubykin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYFragile X syndrome (FX) is associated with sensory processing and learning deficits. Visual familiarity evokes persistent theta oscillations during passive stimulus presentation in the primary visual cortex (V1) and the hippocampus (HPC), which are impaired in V1 of FX. How does this activity change during active behavior? To address this, we performed Neuropixels recordings in V1, HPC, and the prefrontal cortex (PFC) during Go/No-Go visual discrimination behavior. Theta oscillations are reduced in FX in both V1 and HPC and are abolished during No-Go trials, correlating with excessive, incorrect licking behavior. In wild-type (WT) mice, V1 theta power strongly correlates with correct behavioral outcomes. PFC shows significantly reduced cue-related responses in FX. Together, these findings show loss of behavioral inhibition in FX correlated with attenuated theta activity in V1 and HPC and deficient top-down control from PFC. This work sheds light on circuit-level impairments underlying behavioral deficits in FX for potential therapeutic interventions.

INTRODUCTION

Autism spectrum disorders (ASDs) are an extremely prevalent group of neurodevelopmental disorders that affect approximately 2% of the population worldwide while being four times more likely in boys than girls.1,2 A hallmark of ASDs is a disruption of sensory processing that is associated with either hyper- or hyposensitivity that can impact social interaction, daily functioning, and learning and memory.3–5 Sensory disruptions are associated with alterations in both local and long-range connectivity, with several reports demonstrating reduced long-range communication and increased local network connectivity.6–9

Fragile X syndrome (FX) is the most common inherited form of ASDs with a high degree of similarity with other types of ASDs. Four out of ten individuals with FX meet the criteria to also be diagnosed with another form of the disorder.10–12 FX is a monogenic disorder caused by a mutation of the FMR1 gene, which results in a subsequent decrease in the production of the Fragile X messenger ribonucleoprotein (FMRP).13,14 The lasting effects include impairments in both synaptic function15 and activity-dependent plasticity.16,17 More specifically, there is enhanced metabotropic glutamate receptor (mGluR)-dependent long-term depression (LTD)18,19 and impaired cortical long-term potentiation (LTP).17,20 In addition to the synaptic plasticity impairments, there are reports of altered excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) imbalance with a decrease in the number and density of mature parvalbumin (PV)-positive interneurons.21,22 Additionally, these PV interneurons are shown to be hypoactive and receive decreased excitatory connectivity, which may be contributing to the changes in E/I balance.15,19,22–24 Despite the reports of changes in the E/I balance and synaptic plasticity, a major question remains: what underlies the impairment to learning and memory that’s present in FX?

Memory is a complex cognitive process requiring the relay of information across the brain in an efficient manner. While the precise mechanism driving this is not fully understood, research has shown that theta (4–8 Hz) oscillations are critical for this processing.25–30 Early studies in rats attributed theta activity to fear-conditioned memory,31,32 while later work showed theta-modulated spike-timing-dependent plasticity.27,33,34 More recently, it has been shown to be associated with contextual learning, memory consolidation, and attention.35–38 Recent work has shown the emergence of visually cued theta oscillations in response to familiar stimuli in the primary visual cortex (V1).15,39,40 These visually cued oscillations correlate with elevated theta power within the hippocampus(HPC)41 and differ from the conventional theta oscillations described in HPC.42,43 After visual information is initially processed in V1, it is relayed to higher visual areas (HVAs), which are functionally specialized to process more specific aspects of vision, such as visual feature selectivity, receptive fields, and illusory contours.44–48 Interestingly, these HVAs show the emergence of familiarity-induced theta activity, which suggests an efferent relay of this activity from V1.44 However, theta activity in V1 emerges independent of HPC activity41 and may be synchronized through secondary areas, such as the retrosplenial cortex49–52 or medial entorhinal cortex,53–56 which also display theta oscillations.

Recently, it has been shown that theta activity is impaired within the context of passive visual familiarity in FX.15 More specifically, oscillations are frequency shifted and reduced in magnitude and duration. Additionally, the functional connections from layer-5 pyramidal cells to layer-4 fast-spiking (FS) interneurons are likewise weaker.15 FX mice show enhanced mismatch responses and reduced adaptation during an oddball paradigm, which might underlie the altered sensory perception phenotypical to the disorder.57 This could point toward impairments in the encoding of memory in FX and may even indicate similar impairments within other pathways. However, there is still much to be learned about these oscillations and the underlying mechanism driving this cognitive process. Other work has shown that HPC activity is disrupted in FX. Specifically, it has been shown that there is impaired plasticity, spatial learning, and memory reactivation; yet there is no investigation into HPC visually cued theta oscillations themselves.58–61 Studies have shown that in addition to declarative memory, HPC plays a role in perception of complex visual information and was found to be crucial in a simple geometric object discrimination task.62–69 Translating the passive familiarity results observed in FX into an active-learning paradigm and clarifying the role of visually cued theta oscillations in both V1 and HPC is critical to understanding the mechanism of learning, their impairments in FX, and bridging the gap between these theta oscillations and memory.

RESULTS

To establish whether visually evoked oscillations that emerge in V1 following passive familiarity are related to the encoding and recall of memory, and to test whether the impairments seen in cases of Fragile X syndrome are sustained during learning, we trained wild-type (WT) and FX mice to perform a visual discrimination, Go/No-Go task. The behavior during training was measured via an infrared beam break placed near the mouse’s jaw to detect each time the mouse licked (Figure 1A). During the recording sessions, a Neuropixels probe (Figure 1C) was inserted across all layers of the primary visual cortex (V1) and the hippocampus (HPC) and used to measure the local field potential (LFP) and single-unit activity. Following recordings, probe tracks were traced to ensure that proper insertion location and depth were achieved (Figure 1B). At the start of the study, mice underwent a head-plate surgery with three subsequent days of recovery before beginning habituation and training (Figure 1D). As mice showed licking behavior localized to the stimulus, they moved into the final, discrimination training between the Go and No-Go stimuli. To differentiate reward from expectancy, 20% of the Go trials withheld water delivery (Go, orange), and the other 80% were unchanged (Go+, green). In total, there are five different outcomes that may occur throughout a training session: Reward (Hit), Miss, No Reward (Expected), Timeout (False Alarm), and Correct Reject (Figure 1E).

Behavioral performance in FX mice shows impaired learning rates across training days

At the start of training, mice showed no apparent discrimination between the two stimuli, as they continued to lick throughout all trials, regardless of the stimulus shown (Figure 1F). With continuous days of training, WT and FX mice began to learn to perform the task appropriately as they would withhold licking on the No-Go trials. Additionally, both types of mice would localize their licks within each trial to the time window between the onset of the Go ± stimulus and reward delivery (Figure 1F). However, the lick raster plots also show that although FX mice respond appropriately to the Go trials, they are more active during the No-Go trials as well, unlike the WT mice. This is confirmed by quantifying the behavior using a training score (d’, discrimination index) for each day of training for each mouse (Figure S1). By looking at all days of training, we find that mice in both groups are able to show discrimination of the two stimuli for at least three consecutive days (d’ values >0), at which point they were deemed experts on the task and a recording was taken. When we look at the group performance throughout this task, we find that both WT and FX mice are able to show significantly increased d’ scores from the start to end of training (Figure 1G). Interestingly, when we look at an intermediate day of training, which is the halfway point between the start and end, we find that WT mice show increases in d’ from the start to intermediate (inter) days while FX mice did not. This indicates that the FX mice have a slower overall rate of learning compared to the WT group during the initial days of training but are still able to reach an appropriate performance level given enough time.

Reduced local field potential dynamics in V1 across conditions

After mice were given an ample amount of time to learn the discrimination task and show a high level of performance in the quantified training score, an acute recording was taken using the Neuropixels probe. Given the dense recording site geometry along the shank of the probe, all cortical layers of binocular V1 were recorded. On the recording day, a new stimulus was included in the recording (“Novel”: black and white checkerboard, 0.24 cpd, orientation = 0 deg.) as a control to show that the visually evoked oscillations emerge only after familiarization with a given stimulus. Visually evoked potentials (VEPs) were identified from layer 4 of V1 by selecting the activity on the channel displaying the strongest negative peak following the visual stimulus presentation. Consistent with previous reports,15,39 we find LFP oscillations in both the Go ± and No-Go conditions, as indicated by sustained oscillations (>2 periods), but not with the Novel stimulus (Figure 2A). We additionally observed differences between WT and FX in the amplitudes of the oscillation cycles (Figure 2B). In particular, we find that the WT mice have stronger (more negative) amplitudes, primarily seen in the 2nd and 3rd cycles of the response to Go stimuli, while the responses to No-Go stimuli only show a significant difference in the 2nd cycle. As expected, we found little difference in the Novel condition, which suggests that the FX impairment seen in the Go ± and No-Go conditions is the result of learning and not inherent visual processing.

We next turned our attention to the translaminar currents underlying the oscillations across cortical depth, applying current source density (iCSD) analysis to the averaged LFPs for each group across all conditions (Figure 2C). The most prominent activity was seen in layers 2/3 and 4 in both WT and FX mice, with more pronounced cycles shown in WT, especially during the persistent activity (after the visual stimulus is removed, [0.7–2.0] s). We sought to investigate any sustained (0.7–2.0 s) band-power differences between the groups across the various conditions by quantifying the oscillatory power relative to the baseline period (0.0–0.5 s). The frequencies were grouped together into multiple bands: theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), beta (12–30 Hz), low gamma (30–40 Hz), high gamma (50–70 Hz), and gamma (30–70 Hz) (Figures 2D and 2E). We found that in the Go−, No-Go, and Novel conditions, theta-band power was significantly lower in the FX group, while the Go+ showed no difference. Finding striking differences in the theta band is particularly interesting as this band is reported to relate to working memory and communication across brain regions for memory encoding and subsequent recall,70 and its impairment in FX mice may contribute to the weaker behavioral performance. More specifically, the loss of No-Go theta activity could indicate a lack of No-Go stimulus memory recall, leading to elevated licking and inability to withhold this behavior.

V1 single unit activity shows reduced persistent oscillatory activity in FX

To investigate cell-specific differences between groups following training, single-unit activity was analyzed across all cortical layers of V1. We first looked at the auto-correlograms of units to ensure the presence of oscillations following the onset of the visual stimulus (Figure S2). Next, we show the activity of visually responsive units as population heat maps (Figure 3A). Interestingly, similar to the LFPs, we find that the single unit activity has visually evoked oscillations that are impaired in the FX mice, specifically in terms of the total number of discernible cycles across conditions. This observation can be made more precise utilizing a peak detection algorithm that shows diminished, or lost, later cycles in FX compared to WT (Figure 3B). We then analyzed the power spectrum of each unit response and found strong theta activity across all conditions in both groups (Figures 3C and 3D). Interestingly, here we find a significant decrease in the theta power between WT and FX during the No-Go condition, consistent with the LFP results described earlier and providing further evidence of underlying network impairments and the resulting impairment in inhibitory behavioral control in FX.

As the Z-scored heat maps showed differences in the single-unit activity and oscillations between groups and across conditions, we sought to further identify these key differences by quantifying the oscillation duration and frequency for the visually responsive units. Both the distributions (Figure 3E) and the means (Figure 3G) of the unit durations are significantly lower in FX in all conditions except for the Novel. Similarly, the distributions (Figure 3F) and means (Figure 3H) of oscillation frequencies are significantly lower in FX mice in response to Go stimuli. For both the unit duration and frequency, we found no differences within the Novel condition, which strongly suggests that the effect of the FX mutation is on the learning that takes place over time and not on the visual response itself. To dissect layer-specific contributions to the observed impairments, we separated units based on layers in V1 (Figure S4). The results showed FX mice had significantly reduced oscillation peak number in L1&L2/3 for No-Go and L1&L2/3, L4, and L5 for Go+ (Figure S4). Importantly, despite both the Go and No-Go stimuli being familiar to the mice, they each elicited unique activity patterns (Figure S4F). The peak number difference in Go+ and No-Go was robustly observed in both groups across all layers, except in FX L4 (Figure S4F). Overall, the single-unit activity in V1 showed impaired FX responses under different conditions, which aligns with previous results,15,57 and these changes could be the underlying mechanism by which the learning disability manifests in FX syndrome.

Matching hippocampal LFP and single-unit activity between WT and FX

To investigate the relationship between familiarity-induced theta oscillations in V1 and the hippocampus (HPC), a region well known for its critical role in learning and memory,42,43 we conducted simultaneous recordings in both V1 and HPC. Using the Neuropixels probe, we were able to simultaneously record across all cortical layers of V1 (the results canvassed above) and the ventral region of CA1 (vCA1), which is shown to be critical for contextual information encoding and memory retrieval71–74 (Figure 4A). Matching the analysis in V1, we began characterizing HPC responses by investigating LFP activity (Figure 4B). Visually cued activity is present across all conditions, while smaller than V1, and could be visually identified in WT. To better quantify the theta oscillations, we computed power spectral densities of LFP under each condition. In all conditions, theta power was present (Figure 4C); however, unlike V1, we found no significant differences between WT and FX mice within the theta band (Figure 4D).

We next investigated the single-unit activity present within HPC and found defined cycles of the oscillations across conditions for both WT and FX mice (Figure 5A). The power-spectrum analysis of these HPC units showed significant differences in multiple frequency bands of all conditions (Figures 5C and 5D). Importantly, theta power was higher in all conditions, except Novel, which suggests theta impairments in FX are present not only in V1 but also additionally within HPC. As with V1, the patterns of activity across the conditions did not match, suggesting that different variations of the oscillations coincided with different stimulus-reward pairings, specifically between the Go ± and No-Go. The aggregate peak times from the peak-detection analysis also confirmed this finding (Figure 5B). Here, we were able to see similar activity between WT and FX mice within each condition but varying patterns within each group across the conditions. We further analyzed this oscillatory activity by quantifying the unit oscillation duration (Figures 5E and G) and frequency (Figures 5F and H). We found a decrease in the distribution of FX oscillation duration only for the Go+ and No-Go conditions, while WT mice showed a decrease in the Novel condition. By looking at the mean quantification, we only found a significant decrease in the Go+ condition for FX. From the frequency analysis, we found the Go ± conditions were significantly different between groups as the FX units had a higher frequency of oscillations in HPC compared to WT. An elevation in FX frequency was surprising but could be potentially explained by the distinct neural circuitry within V1 compared to HPC.

To investigate excitatory and inhibitory activities, we separated putative fast-spiking (FS) and regular-spiking (RS) units that showed strong oscillations in both V1 and the HPC (Figure S3). Here, we quantified the percentage of active FS interneurons in FX and WT mice. In V1, we observed a trend toward a decreased percentage of active FS units in FX mice compared to WT across all three stimulus conditions, although this did not reach statistical significance (Figure S3C). No obvious trend was seen between WT and FX mice for the HPC (Figure S3D). In V1, the differences in RS unit oscillations were observed for both Go+ and No-Go conditions, exhibiting decreased unit durations and shifted unit frequency, while FS units only showed a significantly shorter duration for the No-Go condition in FX (Figure S3E–S3G).

To further dissect the cellular mechanism of theta oscillations, we pharmacologically activated hM3Dq receptors in V1 PV+ interneurons using a Cre-dependent hM3D(Gq) designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADDs) in Fmr1KO/PV-Cre mice using the drug DCZ (Figures S5A and S5B). Mice were trained with a passive visual stimulation task for 4–6 days to establish familiarity. V1 activity was recorded at three different times: pre-training, post-training, and post-training with hM3Dq-DREADD activation. The two post-training recordings were taken consecutively, with only a 20-min gap for the DCZ injection and to let the activation reach its peak. This allowed us to track the same unit activity before and after activation.

We found a reduced number of oscillation cycles after DREADD activation (Figure S5C). Specifically, the 3rd and the 4th cycles after DREADD had a significant decrease in amplitude compared to the post. Time-frequency analysis revealed an increase in gamma-band power (Figures S5F and S5G), providing electrophysiological evidence of PV activation, which is known to drive gamma power through fast spiking. The significant difference in theta-band power only occurred between pre- and post-training before DREADD activation but not after activation.

We next separated putative FS and RS neurons to investigate single neuron activity further. FS were primarily separated from RS based on waveforms (Figure S5H). We found that the post-training condition had 4~5 peaks compared with a single peak in the pre-training condition, indicating the establishment of familiarity. Consistent with our LFP data, only two peaks were observed on average for both RS and FS units after DREADD activation (Figure S5I). We also saw a significant decrease in oscillation durations and an increase in frequency before and after DREADD activation for both FS and RS neurons (Figures S5J–S5M).

Inter-region coupling is decreased in FX while intra-region coherence matches WT

As a further investigation into differences in the oscillatory activity between WT and FX mice within the context of learning and memory, we sought to quantify the phase-amplitude coupling in V1 and HPC during the behavior experiment. We accomplish this by implementing a Gaussian-copula phase-amplitude coupling (gcPAC) between the theta phase and gamma amplitude within each region. This form of PAC is a rank-based implementation that is invariant to amplitude shifts in the data, meaning an increase in overall power does not correlate to an increase in coupling, which is beneficial for electrophysiological recordings and analysis.69 The heat maps for the gcPAC across the frequency bands showed differences between the WT and FX groups within V1 (Figure S6A). After quantification, we found decreased theta:low gamma (T-LG), theta:high gamma (T-HG), and theta:gamma (T-G) gcPAC in FX mice in all conditions except for the No-Go T-LG and Novel T-HG (Figure S6B). Interestingly, within HPC, we only find a significant decrease in FX mice for the Go+ T-G gcPAC (Figures S6C and S6D), despite the heat maps seeming to show a stronger difference. As PAC is shown to facilitate the integration of neural activity for coordinated working-memory storage75–78 and shows changes in diseases such as schizophrenia, Alzheimer disease, and Parkinson disease,79 these results additionally support the notion that altered theta oscillations shown in FX may contribute to the learning disability present in the disorder. Using Ai32/PV-Cre mice, we were able to optogenetically activate PV+ interneurons within the theta frequency (4 Hz) to see if it had a positive effect on theta-gamma PAC (Figure S7). Mice were exposed to a passive visual stimulus for 4–6 days. Once the stimulus was familiar, we recorded from V1 while presenting the familiar visual stimulus. We then recorded trials in which we activated PV + interneurons at a 4 Hz frequency using a 473 nm laser, while showing a gray screen only. PAC analysis for T-LG, T-HG, and T-G was performed during the post-stimulus window for both visual stimulus trials and laser stimulus trials. We saw that optogenetic activation of PV+ interneurons resulted in a significant increase in PAC for all three bands. These results indicate that enhancing inhibition of PV+ interneurons can increase theta-gamma coupling in the post-familiarity V1 circuit.

Additionally, we sought to describe any V1-HPC oscillation coherence during behavior and quantified the phase difference (PHD) and phase-locking values (PLV) of the LFP activity in both regions. Here, we split the oscillations into periods of visual stimulation (0.5–0.7 s) and sustained activity (0.7–1.5 s), as we predominately find differences between groups in the sustained period for the LFP and single-unit activity. The PHD is shown for the entirety of the recording period, with similar patterns of activity for the two groups (Figure S8A). When quantified, we similarly found overlap between the groups for both time windows across all conditions (Figures S8B and S8C). Similarly, the PLV analysis showed no significant differences across the frequency bands for all conditions except for the No-Go gamma band (30–70 Hz) in the sustained oscillation time window where the FX group is decreased (Figure S8F). Altogether, the PHD and PLV analyses suggest that both groups show similar levels of V1-HPC coherence. As a majority of the information from the cortex to deeper brain regions is not transmitted through direct connections, but rather through additional brain regions, such as the retrosplenial cortex and medial entorhinal cortex, more pronounced differences may be found in these additional brain regions. Further experiments would be needed to investigate this.

Previous research suggests that group 1 metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluR) may play an important role in the pathophysiology of Fragile X syndrome.18 Specifically, it has been shown that an mGluR5 antagonist, MPEP, can rescue several of the critical sensory and behavioral functions impaired in Fmr1 KO mice,80,81 such as audiogenic seizures and open field hyperactivity. To test whether MPEP administration can rescue the theta oscillation and phase amplitude coupling defects in V1, mice were subjected to a passive visual experience paradigm, where we recorded on the first and final days of training. In the post-training session, we recorded from V1 while presenting the familiar visual stimulus. Mice were then injected intraperitoneally with MPEP, after which we recorded from V1 while again presenting the familiar stimulus (Figure S9A). We found that post-MPEP delivery, there were significantly more oscillations than in the pre- and post-training conditions (Figure S9B). Quantification of the band-power differences between the three conditions revealed a trend of an increase in the power of the 4–8 Hz and 8–12 Hz frequency bands, although this did not reach significance (Figure S9D and SE). Similarly, PAC analysis showed a trend toward a general increase in theta-gamma coupling following MPEP delivery, though this also did not reach significance (Figures S9F and S9H).

Correlating behavior outcomes with theta oscillation activity

Our results so far have described oscillation differences between WT and FX mice across conditions, yet to investigate the role of theta oscillations and their association with learning and memory, we decided to take into consideration how theta oscillations correlate with the behavioral outcomes. We subdivided the recording day trials based on the licking behavior of each mouse. We separated behavioral trials into six behavioral outcomes: Go+ hit (H) & miss (M), Go− H & M, and No-Go correct reject (CR) and false alarm (FA). The Novel stimulus presentation trials were not analyzed as they had no association with reward delivery or timeout punishment. Interestingly, representative single-trial responses of the same neurons from WT and FX mice demonstrated vastly distinct patterns of activity for different behavioral outcomes (Figure 6A). To measure the strength of theta oscillations correlated with these behavioral outcomes, we sought to quantify single-trial theta power values using the power spectrum analysis previously used (Figure S10). The mean responses for each mouse were plotted and showed decreased distributions of FX theta power for the Go+ H, No-Go CR, and No-Go FA conditions in V1 (Figure 6B). To identify oscillating trials, we used a one-standard-deviation threshold and discovered significant differences between WT and FX responses for the No-Go conditions (Figure 6C). Interestingly, we observed a significant increase in theta power during correct responses (hits and correct rejections) in the WT group, but not the FX group. This finding suggested that strong theta activity could be tied to improved behavioral performance.

To test this hypothesis and test if the overall LFP oscillations were correlated with the behavioral outcomes, we performed a logistic regression analysis across all mice and all trial responses. Here, we utilized the LFP theta power measured on each individual trial as the dependent variable and classified behavioral responses as the categorical dependent variable (0: Miss or False Alarm; 1: Hit or Correct Reject). One benefit of utilizing this analysis was the inclusion of both the Go and No-Go responses. From the analysis, we found significant correlations of theta oscillation strength in WT V1 and HPC with the correct behavioral outcome (Table S1). Interestingly, neither brain region in FX demonstrated any significant correlations, which could be potentially explained by both weaker oscillations and poorer behavioral performance compared to WT.

To further analyze these results, we investigated the relationship between licking rates across all of the recording day trials against the theta power for correct and incorrect trials independently. Under this premise, we would expect to see an increase in theta power with an increase in correct licking rate and a decrease in theta power with an increase in incorrect licking. Linear regressions were fit to the data to visualize the overall trend of the two distinct groups, and we found that WT group displayed a positive correlation in V1 for both Go and No-Go correct behavioral outcomes, while FX group did not (Figure 6D). Of important note, for the Go condition, we found a significant difference in the distribution of responses between WT and FX. Incorrect trials yielded mixed results, making it more challenging to identify a clear correlation, as the behavior responses could be influenced by multiple additional factors, such as inattention or lack of motivation to complete the task, and not just theta power alone (Figure 6E). A matching analysis was done on the HPC data but did not yield any significant differences between groups or show correlations between theta activity and behavior for either WT or FX (Figure S11).

In addition, we wanted to know if there were any changes in the spike-phase coherence (SPC) within the groups or regions across the conditions. As SPC aims to investigate the synchrony between spiking activity and the phase of the LFP, it provides insight into how well neural activity is coordinated within the network, as high SPC is thought to be necessary for information consolidation and decision-making.82 We found that SPC in V1 was not significantly different between WT and FX for most conditions; however, there was a significant difference in SPC during No-Go CR responses (Figure S12). In the HPC, we found significant differences between WT and FX in Go+ H, Go+ M, and Go− M responses. Additionally, within FX, we saw a significant difference between SPC distributions for Go+ H vs. Go+ M responses, with hits showing a localization at one peak. Overall, only a select few conditions showed differences in the SPC within each group, suggesting that generally, SPC was maintained regardless of the behavior. Our results suggest that (1) a certain level of theta power correlated with increased behavioral performance, and (2) there was a positive linear relationship between theta power and correct behaviors in WT mice, which was reduced in FX.

To investigate how other brain regions might contribute to the impaired behavioral phenotype, we also recorded from a subregion of the prefrontal cortex (PFC), ventrolateral orbital area (vlORB), during the operant conditioning paradigm (Figures 7A and 7B). Both WT and FX mice were trained as described previously. Once mice had learned to discriminate between the Go and No-Go stimuli, we recorded in ORB using Neuropixels probes. Surprisingly, we did not see oscillations in both WT and FX mice (Figures 7E and 7F). Instead, we identified two groups of units demonstrating persistent non-oscillatory activity after either a visual stimulus or reward in both WT and FX mice (Figures 7C and 7D). In both FX and WT, Go stimulus- and reward-triggered persistent activity had significantly higher power compared to No-Go and Novel responses. However, between genotypes, No-Go-triggered persistent responses were significantly weaker in FX compared to WT mice. There was no difference between reward-related persistent responses in WT and FX mice.

Finally, to reveal the importance of V1 theta oscillations in behavior, we decided to determine if suppression of V1 theta oscillations was sufficient to disrupt behavior. We performed an experiment using an open-field visual discrimination paradigm (Figure S13). Freely moving Ai32/PV-Cre mice were trained on a Go/No-Go visual discrimination task, in which they learned to touch the Go stimulus for a water reward and to withhold touching the No-Go stimulus to avoid a time-out punishment. Once mice had sufficiently learned the task, we performed a test day where we inhibited the V1 oscillatory response by high-frequency 30 Hz laser activation of PV+ interneurons. The inhibition occurred after the visual stimulus had been shown, ensuring that only the sustained part of the visually cued oscillations was disrupted while preserving the initial visual response. These laser trials were pseudorandomly interspersed between trials with a visual stimulus only. We observed a significant decrease in the number of correct responses to the Go stimulus trials during laser stimulation compared to the visual-stimulus-only trials. However, there was no significant change in the number of touches to the No-Go stimulus, showing no decrease in activity. These results emphasize the importance of visually cued V1 theta oscillations for visual memory and learning and are vital for task performance.

Logistic regression model shows better classification with WT responses compared to FX

If the altered oscillations in FX mice do indeed impair the encoding of visual stimuli, we would expect to be able to decode stimulus classification more accurately from WT than from FX mice. To test this hypothesis, we fit logistic regressions from binned spike counts to stimulus class (Go and No-Go), separately for each strain of mouse (WT and FX) and each brain area (V1 and HPC) (Figure S14A). First, to identify what section of the trial encodes the maximum difference between stimuli, we fit a fixed-window decoder on four sections of the recording ([0, 0.5] s, [0.5, 0.7] s, [0.7, 1.5] s, and [1.5, 3] s). In V1 and HPC, we found that both WT and FX have significantly higher classification accuracies during the oscillatory activity ([0.5, 0.7] s and [0.7, 1.5] s) compared to the spontaneous times ([0, 0.5] s and [1.5, 3] s) (Figure S14A), as expected. However, if all recorded neurons were used as input to the decoder, decoding accuracies for WT and FX mice were not statistically distinguishable at any time window, in either V1 or HPC (Figure S14B). This suggests a more subtle impairment, which is consistent with the observation that both types of mice successfully learn to perform the task. In HPC, the overall accuracy trended to be lower (though not reaching significance) for WT compared to FX mice, which is likely explained by the fact that patterns of Go and No-Go activity were more distinct for FX than for WT mice. More specifically, FX showed a lack of later oscillation cycles in the No-Go condition compared to the Go as previously described, while WT did not (Figures 3 and 5).

To test for more subtle differences in stimulus encoding across mouse types, and in particular fine-grained temporal differences, we performed classification with a sliding input window (Figure S14E). In V1, we observed that both WT and FX mice achieved high accuracy in the stimulus presentation window ([0.5, 0.7] s) with a subsequent decay in performance as the oscillations go away (Figure S14E). Interestingly, the accuracy plots displayed an oscillatory pattern with delayed peaks for FX mice, which indicated that the decoder itself performed better at the neural oscillation’s peaks than it did between cycles.

If FX impaired but did not completely disrupt encoding across a population of neurons, we would expect the effects to be more pronounced for smaller neural populations. To test this hypothesis, we trained classifiers on random subsets of units and compared the performances for different sizes of these subsets within V1 and HPC (Figure S14F). In FX mice, larger sets of neurons were required to achieve an accuracy comparable to that for WT mice in V1. This could indicate that the neuronal recruitment in FX mice was suboptimal and less efficient as the network relied on more neurons to process the information. In HPC, surprisingly, the decoder showed that FX mice required fewer neurons to distinguish stimuli than the WT. As with the previous results mentioned, this was likely a byproduct of the fact that in FX, No-Go theta oscillations were lost in HPC, while WT showed similar activity for both the Go and No-Go conditions. This regression model performed better because the neural activity patterns were more distinct, which did not necessarily indicate that the FX HPC network was better at differentiating stimuli, but rather emphasized a loss of oscillations in one condition. A more extensive network is needed to better translate these differences into the observed behavior. However, it does indicate that a simple model could be a means of identifying how the theta activity is required and used by the brain to underwrite learning and memory.

DISCUSSION

Oscillatory activity is thought to have a crucial role in complex brain operations such as cognition.30 Of particular interest is the theta band frequency range as it has been related to learning and memory and is associated with synaptic plasticity.25 Specifically, theta oscillations may play a critical role in the mechanism of encoding and recall of stimulus-specific memory. Within V1, theta band activity emerges in a visually cued manner following familiarity with a visual stimulus and is otherwise absent under a novel condition.39,40 Additionally, following passive visual familiarity, these oscillations have been observed in the HPC as well and were found to originate locally and independently in both V1 and HPC while also being relayed to other brain areas.41,44 Interestingly, theta frequency oscillations are impaired in humans with ASD in the visual system as well as other brain areas. EEG recordings from occipital lobes of minimally verbal children with ASD show decreased inter-trial phase coherence and decreased spectral power in the theta frequency, during a visual-auditory coupling test.83 Further, theta oscillation differences related to face recognition have also been observed in correlation with aberrant social communication abilities in children with ASD.84,85 Similar theta oscillation impairments have also been identified in mouse models of FX. The observed visually cued theta oscillations in V1 were found to be weaker in amplitude, shifted in frequency, and shorter in duration in FX mice compared to WT controls15,57 and may contribute to the known learning deficits in FX mice.23

Recently, HPC has been found to play an important role in the perception of complex stimuli, especially involving stimuli with ambiguous merging of spatial features, suggesting a broader role for HPC in visual stimulus processing beyond just declarative memory.62–67 In a 2007 review written by Bussey and Saksida, the authors argue that HPC is involved in the processing of complex visual information vital for recognition and perception and should not be grouped into function-based memory consolidation alone.68 Another study showed that a task involving simple geometric object discrimination was dependent on the hippocampus.69 Additionally, predictive responses to temporally structured sequential stimuli in V1 are dependent on the HPC and are impaired when the HPC is damaged.86 Visual-familiarity-evoked theta oscillations in HPC may play a role in the contextual perception and integration of sensory experience with memory-related representations. Synchronized theta-band activity between HPC and V1 may support inter-areal communication and functional binding required for perceptual learning and behavioral discrimination. Here, we used an active visual discrimination task to determine how theta oscillations in HPC and V1 contribute to perceptual learning and behavioral inhibition and to test whether disrupted inter-areal theta dynamics are associated with impaired behavioral performance.

Using a Go/No-Go visual discrimination task with WT and FX mice, we find that both groups are able to learn the task, as the discrimination indices are able to reach values above the threshold. Interestingly, from the behavior alone, we see the learning disability in FX mice, as it took longer for them to show discrimination compared to the WT. This is primarily shown by the inability of FX mice to withhold licks during the No-Go condition and by the slower changes in the d’ performance values during training. After the mice showed discrimination, we performed acute silicon probe recordings. In V1, major takeaways include (1) diminished LFP oscillation amplitude for FX in all conditions except Novel, (2) weaker theta power for LFP and unit activity in No-Go, and (3) shifts in the unit duration and frequency in most conditions, except Novel. Furthermore, the functional importance of these oscillations for behavioral performance was demonstrated by optogenetic disruption of theta oscillations in V1 following visual stimulus presentation and during the memory-related delay period of an open-field visual discrimination task, which significantly impaired task performance. These findings suggest that persistent theta oscillations are not merely correlates of visual familiarity but actively contribute to memory-guided behavioral responses. Together, our results support the hypothesis that impaired theta oscillations in FX mice may directly contribute to their behavioral deficits.

The excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) ratio, the balance between excitatory and inhibitory strength, is critical for the brain’s ability to undergo effective long-term potentiation and depression.21,22 FX has been characterized by an altered E/I ratio and circuit hyperexcitability, with reduced inhibitory synaptic transmission, in addition to excessive excitatory connectivity in cortical brain regions.87 Our putative FS and RS unit analysis revealed a slight decrease in the percentage of active FS units in V1 of FX mice, although this did not reach significance. In addition, recent work involving targeted restoration of FMRP in PV+ interneurons of Fmr1 KO mice showed improvements in the V1 circuit theta oscillations, along with improved performance in a visual discrimination task and decreased hyperactivity and social anxiety behaviors.88 This emphasizes the role of E/I imbalance, causing impairments in V1 theta oscillations. Here, we also performed pharmacological treatment to induce hM3Dq-mediated enhancement of PV+ function. Surprisingly, this caused significant attenuation of V1 theta oscillations. LFPs show fewer oscillation cycles with a significant decrease in amplitude in trailing cycles. There was also a significant decrease in theta band power post-activation. These results indicate that DREADD activation of PV+ cells might lead to a more hyperactive response, resulting in a strong and sustained inhibition of the network. However, this increase in function might have a rescue effect during the formation of the familiarity circuit, and it would be of interest to study this in the future.

Visual-familiarity-evoked theta oscillations were not restricted to V1 but were also propagated to higher visual areas (HVAs) and additionally observed in RSC and HPC, suggesting the engagement of distributed cortical and memory-related networks during perceptual learning and familiarity processing.41,44 Here, by simultaneously recording from HPC, we were able to show visually cued responses in both the LFP and single-unit activity during an active visual learning paradigm. We found no differences between groups in the power spectrum analysis of the LFP traces but did see a decrease in unit activity theta power in FX mice for all conditions except for Novel. Furthermore, we found a decrease in the FX unit oscillation duration but surprisingly found an increase in the FX frequency. This increase in the HPC theta frequency could also be explained by an imbalance in the HPC E/I ratio due to reduced suppression of inhibitory synapses, which are known to maintain the frequency of oscillatory circuits.89 This may also lead to hyperexcitability and increased neural firing rates of the HPC network.90

We next sought to characterize any region-specific phase amplitude coupling (PAC) differences between groups. PAC is shown to facilitate memory encoding by coordinating the timing of oscillations and enhancing attention during a task, while impaired theta:gamma PAC is shown to be correlated with deficits in memory performance.75–78 Also, disruptions in PAC have been linked to various cognitive disorders that display memory impairments, such as Alzheimer disease and schizophrenia.79 Within V1, we found a significant decrease in theta:gamma PAC in all conditions for FX mice. In HPC, however, there was only a significant change in the Go+ trials. This suggests that cortical differences between the groups are more pronounced compared to the HPC. Interestingly, both the phase difference and phase locking values for V1-HPC were similar between the WT and FX groups, suggesting that the synchrony between the regions remains intact and unaffected. This maintained level of synchrony could explain the ability to learn the task in the FX mice, but it would be interesting to test this across different stages of training. Perhaps the delay in learning was due to an initially disrupted coherence that, given enough time, could potentially be restored in FX. Optogenetic activation of PV+ interneurons at a 4 Hz theta frequency results in a significant increase in PAC for all theta-gamma bands, indicating that enhancing inhibition from PV+ interneurons can increase coupling in the post-familiarity V1 circuit. PV+ interneuron dysfunction associated with FX may underlie PAC impairments seen here. Additionally, previous research has shown that mGluR5 antagonists have ameliorating effects on FX phenotypes in mice.13,80,91 Our treatment with the non-cell-specific mGluR5 antagonist MPEP in FX mice caused a slight increase in PAC, which did not reach significance. This suggests that targeted approaches specific to inhibitory neurons may have a more drastic effect on theta-gamma coupling in FX.

To further investigate the effect of theta oscillations on learning and memory, we performed logistic regression analysis using theta oscillations as an indicator of correct behavioral response. Interestingly, we find WT shows a significant correlation between the two variables in both V1 and HPC, while FX does not. This result suggests that theta power could be an indicator of behavioral responses, and the decreased FX performance could be attributed to the decreased theta strength in V1. Furthermore, we see weaker levels of FX theta power for Go+ hits and No-Go responses in V1 but no differences within HPC. By applying a theta power threshold, we demonstrate that elevated theta activity in V1 is significantly associated with correct responses (hits and correct rejects) in WT but not in FX mice. Lastly, we find that WT shows a positive correlation with lick probability and theta power in V1, while FX results are not as pronounced.

Although the association between increased theta activity in V1 and improved behavioral performance is compelling, understanding how visual information is transformed into an appropriate behavioral response requires studying other association areas involved in perceptual decision-making. One particularly important region is the prefrontal cortex (PFC), which plays a central role in executive control and adaptive decision-making by integrating sensory, contextual, and reinforcement-related information to guide behavior. PFC is also critically involved in behavioral inhibition, including the suppression of inappropriate licking responses during No-Go trials.92,93 In mouse models of ASD, studies revealed that the PFC showed reduced excitatory synaptic transmission and an altered synaptic E/I ratio correlated with cognitive impairments.94,95 In our PFC recordings, both visual and reward-related persistent responses were observed for both genotypes, but these responses were not oscillatory. Interestingly, we did see a significant difference between WT and FX genotypes in the visually cued responses to the No-Go stimulus in ORB. Interestingly, recent studies reveal that ORB plays a crucial role in suppressing compulsive behavior96 and modulating visual encoding.97 Although the mechanism of this action may be different from theta oscillations in V1 and HPC, our results indicate that the aberrant PFC activity in FX may contribute to an impairment of executive control and top-down modulation of V1.

Finally, we investigated the effects of FX on stimulus encoding by classifying stimuli from the neural responses they elicited. Stimulus classification revealed that both WT and FX mice obtained significantly higher classification accuracies during the oscillatory activity window compared to the spontaneous windows (which acted as controls for the decoder), unsurprisingly, suggesting that theta oscillations are essential for encoding the differences in visual stimuli. There were instances in which FX mice showed an overall increase in decoder performance, specifically in HPC; however, this is likely due to the fact that oscillations are nearly completely lost in FX for the No-Go stimulus. Additionally, we found that more neurons were required in FX mice to achieve the same classification accuracy as WT in V1, which may suggest decreased information content of neurons in FX or an inefficient neural processing architecture.

Altogether, our findings in V1 and HPC demonstrate (1) the emergence of visually experience-dependent theta oscillations during an active learning paradigm in both WT and FX mice, (2) a positive correlation between theta power and behavioral performance in V1, and (3) disrupted oscillatory dynamics and impaired task performance in FX mice.

Specifically, the reduced theta oscillations in V1 and impaired PFC activity observed during No-Go trials in FX mice may contribute to their inability to withhold licking, reflecting the role of these brain regions in inhibitory control. These results provide important insight into the role of theta activity in learning, perceptual decision-making, and behavioral control and further suggest that theta oscillations may represent a potential therapeutic target for neurodevelopmental disorders such as Fragile X syndrome. Future studies using causal approaches, including optogenetic restoration of theta synchrony and theta-gamma phase-amplitude coupling (PAC), as well as modulation of impaired non-oscillatory persistent activity in PFC during No-Go behavioral inhibition, will be essential for determining whether normalization of circuit dynamics can rescue behavioral impairments.

Limitations of the study

Although this study provides insight into the relationship between theta oscillations and behavioral performance during perceptual learning, several limitations should be considered. Our analyses primarily focused on interactions between V1 and the HPC, while the transformation of sensory information into behavioral responses involves a broader distributed network, including multiple brain areas such as PFC, RSC, thalamic nuclei, and higher visual areas. In addition, while we identified strong correlations between disrupted oscillatory dynamics and impaired behavior in WT mice, the precise causal contributions of inter-areal synchrony, theta-gamma coupling, and persistent activity across these regions to the observed impairments in FX are incompletely understood. Future studies combining simultaneous large-scale recordings, cell-type-specific manipulations, and circuit-level manipulations across multiple brain regions will be required to demonstrate the causality between the impairments found in familiarity-evoked oscillation and inhibitory control in FX mice. And more, determine the network mechanisms underlying perceptual learning and its disruption in Fragile X syndrome. In the present study, we did not perform sex-specific analyses. Although our previous study shows that both males and females of WT or FX mice show similar visual-familiarity-evoked theta oscillations in V1, future studies correlating the learning-related emergence of theta oscillations with behavior in both males and females would be important.15

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND SUBJECT PARTICIPANT DETAILS

All animal procedures carried out in this experiment were approved by Purdue University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC, protocol number 1408001112). To generate mice for this study, adult B6.129P2-Fmr1tm1Cgr/J (FX) mice (JAX Stock No:003024) and C57BL/6 wild-type (WT) mice (JAX Stock No:000664) were used. For all mice used in the DREADD experiment, we crossed B6.129P2-Fmr1tm1Cgr/J (Fmr1 KO) with B6.129P2-Pvalbtm1(Cre)Arbr/J (PV-Cre) mice (JAX Stock No. 008069). For all experiments involving optogenetic modulation, Ai32 mice (JAX Stock No:012569) were crossed with PV-Cre mice. All mice were group housed in a 12-h light/dark cycle with full access to food and restricted access to water throughout the duration of this experiment. We ensured mice received 1 mL of water each day through training or additional compensation depending on the number of correct trials performed. If compensation was required, mice were transferred to a temporary cage and given a dish of water to hit the 1 mL total after completing the training for the day. A total of 24 mice, approximately 2–4 months old, consisting of age-matched WT and FX male and female mice (homozygous) were used. For the entirety of the study, we were blinded to the genotype of the mice to remove unintentional biases between the groups.

METHOD DETAILS

Visual stimulation presentation

PsychoPy3, an open-source Python-based psychology software, was used to present visual stimuli. A default gray screen was created using the color space “gray.” The mean luminance of the monitors used throughout training and recordings was 73 cd/m2. During training and recording sessions, the Go ± stimulus was a drifting sinusoidal grating with the following specifications: spatial frequency (SF) = 0.04 cycles per degree (cpd) of visual angle, temporal frequency (TF) = 2 Hz, speed = 100°/second, oriented and drifting at an angle of 30°. The No-Go stimulus involved spatially filtered pink noise with SF = 0.12 cpd. The light intensity at each pixel of the filtered image changed in sinusoidal functions of time at a TF = 2 Hz. On recording days, a Novel checkerboard stimulus between white and black squares was presented and had a spatial frequency of 0.24 cycles per degree, and an orientation of 0° to the horizontal. In all instances of training and recordings, the stimuli were shown for 200 ms and trials had a variable inter-trial interval with the default gray screen that ranged from 7 to 13 s.

Surgical procedures

Anesthesia was induced at 5% inhaled isoflurane and maintained at 1.5–2.0% during surgery. Head-plate implantation involved head-fixing animals in a motorized stereotaxic apparatus and applying a protective ointment to the eyes before shaving and sterilizing the skin above the skull. A small incision was made along the midline, then expanded to expose lambda and bregma. The connective tissue was removed, and shallow lines were etched into the skull in a checkerboard pattern to promote stronger bonding of the head plate to the skull when cemented using Metabond bone cement. A grounding pin (1.5 mm tungsten reference wire soldered to a 0.79-mm-diameter gold-plated pin) was inserted through the skull and used as a reference for recordings. Neurostar stereo drive software was used to mark the recording sites on the skull according to the coordinates of the binocular visual cortex (from lambda: AP 0.8 mm, ML ± 3.2 mm) or ventral prefrontal cortex (from bregma: AP 2.2 mm, ML ± 1.1 mm). Finally, Metabond was used to anchor the head-plate and grounding pin, while also sealing the remaining exposed skull. Before recordings were taken, a cranial window was made around one of the previously marked recording sites. Artificial cerebral-spinal fluid (ACSF; 1.25 mm NaH2PO4, 26 mm NaHCO3, 10 mm dextrose, 124 mm NaCl, 1 mm CaCl2, 0.8 mm MgCL2, and 3.5 mm KCl) was continuously added dropwise to the cranial window to prevent cortical drying. After the recording session ended, the craniotomy was sealed with Kwik-Cast Silicone Elastomer and enclosed with Orthojet.

For surgeries with virus injection, procedures of anesthesia and skull exposure were conducted as mentioned above. The skull on the marked sites was thinned by a dental drill to the extent that it could be penetrated by an injection glass pipette, tip diameter 20 μm. The pipette was inserted stereotaxically 0.25 and 0.6 mm underneath the pial surface using injector Nanoject III. 250 nL of rAAV5/hSyn-DIO-hm3Dq-mCherry (North Carolina University Vector Core Facility, 6 × 1012 particles/mL) was released to express hm3Dq Cre-dependently in PV + interneurons using Fmr1 KO/PV-Cre mice throughout the cortical layers. After two to three weeks of injection, until viral expression plateaued, mice began habituation to the training and recording setup.

For surgeries to implant optic ferrules in mice V1, following skull exposure, a small hole was made with the dental drill over V1, and an optic ferrule was placed on the surface of the brain. The ferrules were sealed to the skull using Metabond.

Habituation and training

Habituation began three days after head-plate surgery to ensure full recovery. The habituation process progressively desensitized the mice to head fixation and immobilization on the training rig over five days. Each day, the computer monitor showed the default gray screen for the entire time mice were on the rigs. On days 1 and 2 the mice were habituated for 30 min, day 3 for 45 min, and days 4 and 5 for 60 min. During this time, mice also began water restriction. Their body weight was monitored daily and kept above 80% ad libitum weight. Mice were given 1 mL of water each day following the habituation session via a water dish in an isolation cage before being returned to their home cage.

Passive training paradigm

For the passive training paradigm, mice were presented with the same visual stimulus (visual features same as Go) 200 times a day, for 4–7 consecutive days. Recordings were taken before and after passive training, once the stimulus was familiarized.

Pre-training paradigm

Pre-training consisted of presenting mice with the Go ± stimulus for 100 trials. A central computer was used to control stimulus presentation, via PsychoPy3, during training and monitor the licking behavior of the mice. An infrared (IR) beam-break senor was fixed to a 2 mm diameter tube connected to a water reservoir and positioned beneath the jaw of the head-fixed mice so that when the mouse licked its tongue would break the beam. During all training and recording sessions, the time stamps of the IR beam breaking, the start, and the end of the visual stimulus during each trial were recorded and saved for subsequent analysis. Simultaneously, these time stamps were utilized in conjunction with an Arduino Uno board to determine the reward outcome. When the mice behaved appropriately, a 2-Way, Normally Closed Solenoid valve triggered by a Cool-Drive ONE valve driver would open to deliver a bolus of water (~10 μL) to the mouse. A ~10 μL water reward bolus was delivered immediately after the visual stimulus ended for each trial. This was to help acclimate the mice to the rigs and familiarize them with the location of water delivery. When mice showed licking on 80% or more of the trials, they were moved to the next stage of training.

Visual discrimination training paradigm

For the duration of the training period, an operant conditioning paradigm was used. Mice were shown both the Go ± and No-Go stimuli. Each day, mice were trained for a total of 150 trials. The stimuli were distributed pseudo-randomly throughout all trials to ensure mice were licking in response to the visual stimuli, rather than any other factors. The Go+ stimulus was presented for 80 trials, and the Go- was shown for 20 trials. If the mouse licked in response to the stimulus, it was deemed a “Hit”. The No-Go stimulus was shown for the remaining 50 trials. If the mice licked during this stimulus (deemed a “False Alarm”) a timeout (2–6 s) was incurred and applied in addition to the inter-trial interval. Open-field visual discrimination training paradigm

Mice were placed under water restriction and limited to 1 mL of water per day. The weights were recorded every single day throughout the experiment. Once mice dropped to 85% of their original weight, they were started on the operant conditioning paradigm in the open-field chambers. Training was done gradually over three stages. In stage 1, mice were given a water reward periodically to familiarize them with the water port. In stage 2, mice were given a water reward only if they touched a gray screen to familiarize them with the touchscreen and correlate that behavior with reward. Finally, on stage 3, mice were trained to the Go/No-Go visual discrimination task. Mice were given a water reward upon touching the screen during Go stimulus presentation (pink noise, sf = 0.12 cpd, tf = 0.75 Hz) and given a time-out punishment (10 s) if they touched during the No-Go stimulus presentation (pink noise, sf = 0.03 cpd, tf = 0.75 Hz). Both stimuli were presented for 5 s. Once mice had correctly touched 80% of the Go trials and incorrectly touched less than 20% of the No-Go trials, for three consecutive days, they were considered to be experts at the task. On the test day, laser activation trials were pseudo-randomly interspersed with visual stimulus only trials. Laser stimulation at 473nm wavelength was done at an output of 4–6mW. All data were analyzed using Python code from data exported through the open-field ABET II software.

Pharmacological interventions

DREADD activation experiment

For the DREADD activation experiment, 5 Fmr1 KO/PV-Cre mice underwent head-plate implantation surgery and received an rAAV5/hSyn-DIO-hm3Dq-mCherry injection and were allowed to express for two weeks.

The passive training paradigm was applied to these mice after 4–5 days of habituation. Extracellular recordings were taken at three different times: before passive training, after training without DREADD activation, and with DREADD activation, with the data acquisition setup mentioned above.

Deschloroclozapine dihydrochloride (DCZ, MedChemExpress, 1977-07-7) was used to activate the hM3D(Gq) receptor in PV + interneurons. DCZ powder was dissolved in Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at a 5 mg/mL concentration and stored at 80°C. On experiment day, the DCZ solution was diluted to 0.02 mg/mL with saline. After taking the first post-training recording, the DCZ solution was injected intraperitoneally at a dosage of 0.1 mg/kg (~130 μL per mouse) while the mouse was still fixed to the recording rig with the probe inserted. After allowing 20 min for DCZ diffusion, a second post-training recording was taken.

MPEP injection experiment

For this experiment, head-fixed Fmr1 KO mice were trained to the passive visual stimulus paradigm. Electrophysiological recordings were taken from V1 prior to visual stimulus familiarity. Post familiarity, mice were injected intraperitoneally with mGluR5 antagonist 2-Methyl-6-(phenylethynyl) pyridine hydrochloride (MPEP, Tocris, 219911-35-0) at a dose of 20 mg/kg (5 mM) while the mouse was still fixed to the recording rig with the probe inserted. The post-training recordings were performed before and 20 min after MPEP injection.

MPEP stock was prepared by dissolving MPEP powder into DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, D8418) at a concentration of 75 mM. This solution was diluted with sterile saline to 5 mM (6.67% V/V DMSO) and stored at 4°C until use.

Optogenetic activation of PV + interneurons

After passive visual experience, mice were recorded from V1 using an optrode. Recordings were taken during 20 trials of visual stimulus presentation, followed by 20 trials of 30 Hz stimulation of a 473 nm laser.

Data acquisition and preprocessing

All neural recordings were performed in awake head-fixed mice following the craniotomy procedure. Mice were allowed a 30-min period to fully awaken from anesthesia, during which a Neuropixels silicon probe was positioned normally (66° from horizontal) to the surface of the binocular area of the primary visual cortex and inserted 3 mm into the brain to span all layers of V1 and the hippocampus. After insertion, at least 15 additional minutes of recovery were allowed so the probe could settle before beginning the recordings. Neuropixels probes are ~1 cm in length and 70 μm in width, with electrodes staggered and separated 20 μm vertically. The first 384 channels along the tip of the probe were used during all sessions. Recordings were 3.0 s in duration and began 0.5 s before the onset of the visual stimulation to serve as a baseline of natural, spontaneous neural activity. Raw traces were acquired with OpenEphys acquisition hardware and software with LFP data sampled at a rate of 2500 Hz and single unit activity at 30,000 Hz. Raw traces and bandpass filtered units (300–6000 Hz) underwent a notch filter (60 Hz) to remove any noise associated with the recording rig and building. All data was plotted and analyzed using custom-written Python code that utilized open-sourced libraries, such as Pandas, Scipy, Matplotlib, Pickle, and Seaborn.

For recordings taken from the prefrontal cortex, Neuropixels probe was positioned normally to the surface of the prefrontal cortex (85° from horizontal) and inserted 3 mm into the brain to span all layers of ORB. Other setups and analyses are the same as mentioned above.

Local field potential (LFP) analysis

LFPs for both V1 and HPC were initially filtered (1–300 Hz bandpass and 60 Hz notch) and common average referenced (CAR) from the raw traces post hoc. Activity was compared between groups of mice by taking the channel with the strongest trial averaged visually evoked potential (VEP) elicited after visual stimulation per mouse in layer 4 of V1 and the channel at the center of mass for CA1 in the HPC. Since the probe was inserted at 66° from horizontal (perpendicular to the surface of the cortex) we were able to measure the current source density profile (CSD) of the visually evoked responses in V1. CSD analysis used the filtered, trial-averaged VEPs across the cortical depth using the spline iCSD method in Python on a single column of channels across the probe. Five time windows (VEP1: 0.55–0.70 s, VEP2: 0.71–0.85 s, VEP3: 0.86–1.00 s, VEP4: 1.05–1.15 s, VEP5: 1.20–1.40 s) were used to capture local minima in the VEP traces to determine the average amplitudes at different cycle peaks. For the MPEP experiment, the quantification of the number of oscillations was done by counting peaks crossing a −4.5 standard deviation threshold between 0.5 and 2.2 s calculated from the baseline LFP activity between 0.0 – 0.45 and 2.2–3.0 s. Time-frequency analysis in V1 and HPC used the complex wavelet convolution on the trial-averaged VEPs for each mouse. 40 frequencies were used across a logarithmic range from 2 to 80 Hz, with the number of cycles of the wavelet ranging from 3 to 10 as our time-frequency precision tradeoff. The resulting power was dB normalized to the baseline period before visual stimulation (0.0–0.5 s), and the mean power was calculated across the Theta (4–8 Hz), Alpha (8–12 Hz), Beta (12–30 Hz), Low Gamma (30–40 Hz), High Gamma (50–70 Hz), and Gamma (30–70 Hz) frequency ranges. All data was plotted and analyzed using custom-written Python code via Jupyter notebooks. To investigate the interareal synchronization (phase locking values, PHD and PLV) between V1 and HPC, the LFPs in both regions were filtered between 2 and 90 Hz using the Morlet wavelet method. Single unit analysis

Kilosort,101,102 a template-based clustering algorithm in MATLAB, was used to detect and sort spikes from the raw binary traces resulting from OpenEphys.103 This version is most suited for recording with the Neuropixels probe because it implements drift correction along the probe throughout the duration of the recording. Default parameters were used. Clusters were manually curated using the Phy2 template GUI (https://github.com/kwikteam/phy), with several criteria used to determine high quality units for further analysis. Peri-stimulus time histograms (PSTHs) of single-unit activity were computed using 10 ms bins and smoothed with a Gaussian Kernel with a width of 100 ms. A simple 2-cluster unbiased k-means clustering was used to separate units into visually-responsive and non-visually-responsive units, where the input was the PSTH Z score of single units across 0.5–0.7 s (visual stimulus presentation time window). For ORB units, visually-responsive units are selected using a 2-cluster unbiased k-means clustering with the Z score of single units across 0.5–1.7 s (time window for processing visual information). Similarly, reward-responsive units are selected with the input of the Z score of single units across 1.7–2.9 s (time window after reward delivery). To quantify the oscillation duration and frequency, a peak detection algorithm was implemented on the z-transformed PSTH with the following criteria: 1) peaks must be within 200 ms from one another, 2) the first peak must be within 100 ms of stimulus onset, and 3) the minimum peak height must be at least 1.5 * SD from baseline. The output of this peak detection yielded time stamps of each peak per unit. The timing of the first peak was then subtracted from the onset of the last to quantify the duration. The frequency of single-unit activity used the same peak detection parameters and divided the number of peaks by the oscillation duration for each unit. Power spectrum analysis was done using NumPy (numpy.fft), which calculates the one-dimensional n-point discrete Fourier Transform with the efficient Fast Fourier Transform algorithm across various frequencies. This was further quantified by calculating the area under the curve (AUC) for five distinct frequency bands (2–4, 4–8, 8–12, 12–30, and 30–70 Hz).

For spike phase coherence (SPC) analysis, the region-specific channel selected for LFP analysis (described above) was initially cut to a time window that fit the oscillations [0.5, 1.5] s. A Butterworth band-pass filter from 4 to 8 Hz was applied and a Hilbert transform was taken to convert the LFP into the phase domain. For each unit’s spike times, the corresponding LFP phase value was extracted, and a Rayleigh statistical test was run on the phase distributions to select for units showing significant phase preference (p < 0.05). For these units, the averaged phase value was calculated by taking the circular mean across trials.

Associating theta oscillations with behavior outcomes

To investigate whether the resulting behavior had any association with the theta oscillations, recording day lick activity was used to split the stimulus conditions with the mouse’s decision. A logistic regression was run using statsmodels Logit function in Python where LFP theta power across all mice and trials is the independent variable and behavior response is the dependent variable (0 = miss & false alarm, 1 = hit & correct reject). Power spectrum analysis was run on the LFP data for each individual trial and the area under the curve was calculated for the 4–8 Hz range. To further investigate prominent trials, a threshold was calculated as the mean +1 standard deviation within each genotype, stimulus, and brain region combination. Trials with a correct behavior response had their theta power quantified from the power spectrum analysis and were correlated with the correct rate of licking per mouse (number of correct responses divided by the total number of presentations of the stimulus). A linear regression was fit to the grouping using the scipy.stats.linregress function in Python to help visualize the groupings and general trends of the genotypes.

Perfusions and histology

Mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of 100 mg/kg ketamine and 16 mg/kg xylazine. After exposing the heart, mice were perfused with 1× PBS, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Extracted brains were stored in 4% PFA for 24–48 h, after which 75 μm thick coronal sections were made on a vibrating microtome (1000 Plus, Vibratome). Slices were mounted onto glass slides with anti-fading mounting media containing n-propyl-gallate, counterstained with DAPI in PBS to provide sufficient background, and then sealed with transparent nail polish. The electrode tracks were visualized using confocal microscopy to verify the electrode placement in V1 and HPC according to landmarks shown in mouse brain reference atlases (Neurostar stereotaxic mouse brain atlas, Allen mouse brain atlas). This was done by coating the probes with a red fluorescent lipophilic dye (Vybrant DiD) prior to insertion into the brain.

For immunohistochemistry, transcardial perfusion and brain fixation were performed as described above. Brains were cryoprotected by immersion in 30% sucrose solution for two days, then stored at −80°C in OCT (Fisher Scientific). 100 μm sections of V1 were obtained using a cryostat (Precisionary). For antibody staining of PV + interneurons, sections were first permeabilized for 30 min with 0.3% Triton X-PBS (PBST) and blocked for 1.5 h using 5% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) in PBST (Sigma Aldrich, A8806–1G). Slices were then incubated overnight in primary antibodies at 4°C (1:1000 anti-Parvalbumin Bosterbio, cat# m04041). For removing excess primary antibody, samples were washed 3 times for 15 min in blocking solution before being stained for 2 h with secondary antibodies (1:1000 Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-chicken IgG). Finally, slices were washed with PBS before being mounted in DAPI containing media (Vectashield, H-1500–10) and imaged using Zeiss 710 microscope.

For the fixed-window analysis, a logistic regression model was trained on each mouse on four discrete time windows ([0, 0.5] s, [0.5, 0.7] s, [0.7, 1.5] s, and [1.5, 3] s). This analysis was done for both V1 and HPC regions separately. To assess the temporal characteristics of stimulus encoding across the recording time, we trained a classifier on spike counts from a moving window for each mouse and brain region. The moving window had a width of 50 ms and an overlap of 40 ms between windows. For each mouse, we computed the 5-fold cross-validated balanced accuracy score averaged across all mice for both WT and FX.

For assessing the effect of number of neurons on classification performance, we calculated the accuracy of classification using a random subset of units (n = [2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 75] for V1 and n = [2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 50] for HPC) for a randomly selected mouse from each strain. For a robust estimate of this accuracy, we perform this random selection 2000 times. We only considered mice that had at least 75 visually responsive units for the V1 region (8/10 WT and 6/8 FX mice) and 50 units for the HPC region (9/10 WT and 7/8 FX mice).

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

All statistical analysis was performed in Python. Mann-Whitney U tests were used to compare behavior performance across training within each genotype, VEP peak amplitudes at each oscillation cycle between WT and FX, TF analysis within each frequency band, and the gcPAC between WT and FX. It was also used for comparing the V1-HPC PLV between WT and FX, the percentage of trials above the threshold split by behavior outcome, and the accuracy of the stimulus classification network between and within WT and FX. Two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) tests were used to compare distributions of oscillation duration and frequency for single unit analysis between genotypes, spike-phase coherence between and within genotypes, and theta power split by behavior outcome. A two-dimensional, two-sample KS test was used to compare distributions of WT vs. FX theta power and behavior performance. A Welch’s two-sample t test was used to account for unequal variances and unequal number of units between comparisons. It was used to compare the mean unit duration and frequency between genotypes. Linear mixed models (LMMs) were used to test power spectrum analysis of single unit activity between genotypes across conditions. Tukey HSD was utilized to adjust for multiple comparisons and look for differences between genotypes following LMMs. Spearman correlation coefficient with a pairing permutation was used to test the relationship between licking rate and Go & No-Go theta power. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for gcPAC. All tests were performed using available packages (scipy and statsmodels).
