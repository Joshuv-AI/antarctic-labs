Gauge Symmetry Beyond Perturbation Theory: BRST and anti-BRST Structure, Background Fields, and Infrared Dynamics of Yang–Mills Theory
Daniele Binosi

Abstract
We present a pedagogical and self contained account of the functional formulation of non-Abelian gauge theories, aimed at the construction of a process independent effective charge for Yang–Mills theory. Starting from the path integral quantization of gauge fields, we review gauge fixing and the emergence of Faddeev–Popov ghosts, illustrating how gauge invariance is preserved at the quantum level through Becchi–Rouet–Stora–Tyutin (BRST) symmetry. We then develop the BRST and anti-BRST formalisms and show how their simultaneous implementation leads to powerful functional identities that severely constrain the ghost and gluon sectors. Background field gauges are introduced as a natural framework in which these symmetries manifest themselves through Abelian like Ward identities, allowing for a transparent separation between quantum and background degrees of freedom. This structure makes it possible to define renormalization group invariant combinations of Green functions that generalize the QED effective charge to the non-Abelian case. The resulting effective charge is shown to be unique, gauge invariant, and process independent, providing a unified description of the theory from the ultraviolet down to the infrared. The interplay between functional identities, Dyson–Schwinger equations, and lattice results is discussed in detail, highlighting how dynamical mass generation and infrared saturation naturally emerge within this framework.

Introduction

One of the central goals in the study of non-Abelian gauge theories is the construction of physically meaningful quantities that remain well defined beyond perturbation theory. Among these, a process independent effective charge plays a distinguished role, as it provides a unified description of the interaction strength from the ultraviolet (UV) regime, where asymptotic freedom applies, down to the infrared (IR) region, where confinement and dynamical mass generation dominate. Unlike process dependent running couplings extracted from specific observables, such an effective charge must be gauge invariant, renormalization group invariant (RGI), and uniquely defined within the theory
Deur et al. 2024
.

The construction of a process independent effective charge in Yang–Mills theory is highly nontrivial. Gauge symmetry, while essential for the consistency of the theory, obscures the direct identification of Abelian like structures familiar from quantum electrodynamics. As a result, one must rely on a combination of symmetry principles and functional identities that reorganize Green functions in a way that preserves gauge invariance while allowing for physically transparent definitions. This discussion develops a systematic framework for achieving this goal.

In Sect.
2
, starting from the path integral formulation of
SU(N_{c})
Yang–Mills theory, we review the necessity of gauge fixing and the introduction of Faddeev–Popov ghosts. Explicit perturbative examples are used to illustrate how unphysical degrees of freedom cancel and how transversality of Green functions is maintained.

This sets the stage for the introduction of Becchi–Rouet–Stora–Tyutin (BRST) symmetry in Sect.
3
, which is shown to replace classical gauge invariance at the quantum level and to provide the algebraic backbone for all subsequent constructions. We then explore the combined BRST and anti-BRST formulation of the theory. The simultaneous implementation of these symmetries leads to additional functional identities, including linear ones, that tightly constrain the ghost sector and play a crucial role in the IR. These identities are shown to encode information that goes well beyond perturbation theory and are essential for establishing relations among Green functions that enter the definition of the effective charge.

A pivotal role in the construction is played by background field (BF) gauges discussed in Sect.
4
, which are shown to coincide, modulo the separation between background and quantum fields, with the BRST and anti-BRST invariant theory. The linear identities are then revealed to be induced by the residual background gauge invariance, which gives rise to Ward identities (WIs) when the divergence of background legs is considered. In contrast to the Slavnov–Taylor identities (STIs) of the conventional formulation, which generally involve intricate cancellations between gluon and ghost contributions, the WIs are satisfied independently within each class of diagrams. A general proof of this property for Green functions with an arbitrary number of background and quantum gluon legs, as well as ghost and anti-ghost pairs is provided; and the way they allow for symmetry preserving truncations of the Dyson–Schwinger equations (DSEs), also introduced in this Section, discussed. Finally, the background-quantum identities (BQIs), relating background and quantum Green functions in a precise manner are also presented.

Sect.
5
contains a short presentation of (some) lattice results for the gluon propagator, the ghost dressing function and key Green functions appearing in the BQIs, evaluated in both Landau and linear covariant gauges. These lattice results are discussed in close connection with the continuum framework developed in the previous sections, showing how the observed IR behavior is consistent with the symmetry relations found.

These simulations thereby provide direct nonperturbative evidence for the IR saturation of the gluon propagator and therefore for a mechanism that endow the gluon with a dynamically generated mass. Understanding the origin and consistency of this phenomenon is the scope of Sect.
6
. It turns out that BF gauges are particularly well suited for this purpose, as the WIs associated with background gauge invariance simplify considerably the treatment of intermediate steps, with BQIs relating the final results to conventional correlation functions.

It is only at this point that, building on the knowledge gained, we proceed to define, in Sect.
7
, a unique, gauge invariant, and process independent effective charge, which encapsulates the dynamics of the theory from the UV to the IR.

2
SU(N_{c})
Yang-Mills theory: gauge fixing and Faddeev-Popov ghosts

The action of an
SU(N_{c})
Yang-Mills theory reads

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{YM}}

\displaystyle=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,{\cal L}_{\mathrm{YM}}=-\frac{1}{4}\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,F^{a}_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}_{a};

\displaystyle F^{a}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\mu}A^{a}_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A^{a}_{\mu}+gf^{abc}A^{b}_{\mu}A^{c}_{\nu},

(1)

with
A^{a}_{\mu}
the gluon field, and
f^{abc}
the totally antisymmetric structure constants appearing in the commutation relations satisfied by the
SU(N_{c})
generators
t^{a}
:
[t^{a},t^{b}]=if^{abc}t^{c}
.

The action in Eq. (
1
) is invariant under the (infinitesimal) gauge transformations

\displaystyle\delta_{\theta}A^{a}_{\mu}

\displaystyle={\cal D}_{\mu}^{ab}\theta^{b};

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{\mu}^{ab}

\displaystyle=\delta^{ab}\partial_{\mu}-gf^{abc}A^{c}_{\mu},

(2)

with
{\cal D}_{\mu}^{ab}
the covariant derivative in the adjoint representation and
\theta^{b}
the infinitesimal gauge transformation parameters.

When attempting to quantize such theory, one encounters two (related) problems:

1.

The overcounting of physically equivalent gauge configurations,
i.e.
, the ones related by a local gauge transformations. Naively integrating over all field configurations
A^{a}_{\mu}
in the path integral

\displaystyle Z=\int\!{\cal D}A^{a}_{\mu}\exp\left(iS_{\mathrm{YM}}\right),

(3)

one would end up integrating over infinitely many physically equivalent copies and the integral would diverge by acquiring an infinite multiplicative factor corresponding to the volume of the
SU(N_{c})
gauge group.

2.

The impossibility of defining canonical equal-time commutation relations for the gluon field. The Yang-Mills lagrangian of Eq. (
1
) would in fact lead to canonical momenta

\displaystyle\Pi^{a}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial{\cal L}_{\mathrm{YM}}}{\partial(\partial_{0}A^{a}_{\mu})}=F^{a}_{0\mu},

(4)

implying
\Pi^{a}_{0}=0
.

These difficulties appear independently of the particular quantization scheme employed: the first manifests itself in the path-integral formulation, while the second arises in canonical quantization, and both ultimately reflect the gauge redundancy of the classical Yang–Mills action.

Throughout this work we will employ Minkowski-space notation, which is convenient for discussing the structure and symmetries of Green functions and their perturbative properties. It should be understood, however, that the Yang–Mills functional integral is most rigorously defined after a Wick rotation to Euclidean space, where the weight becomes
\exp(-S_{E})
. In particular, nonperturbative analyses such as those based on DSEs, bound-state equations, or lattice simulations are properly formulated in Euclidean space. Accordingly, statements concerning nonperturbative dynamics should be interpreted in this Euclidean sense, even when written in Minkowski notation for notational simplicity.

It turns out that both problems are simultaneously solved by a gauge-fixing procedure. Dividing out the gauge redundancy in the path integral in Eq. (
3
) requires selecting a single gauge configuration representative of each gauge orbit. Operationally, this is then done by introducing a gauge-fixing condition
{\cal F}^{a}=0
, with
{\cal F}^{a}={\cal F}^{a}[A]
the so-called gauge-fixing function, through the action

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{GF}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\xi}\int\!{\mathrm{d}}^{4}x\,{\cal F}^{a}{\cal F}^{a}.

(5)

To see how the gauge-fixing action of Eq. (
5
) emerges in the path integral quantization, one starts, at a formal level, by inserting in the path integral the unit representation

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\int\!{\cal D}\theta^{b}\,\delta({\cal F}^{a}[A_{\theta}])\mathrm{det}\left(\left.\frac{\delta{\cal F}^{a}[A_{\theta}]}{\delta\theta^{b}}\right|_{\theta=0}\right),

(6)

where:
A_{\theta}
is the gauge transformed field
A^{a}_{\theta\mu}=A^{a}_{\mu}+{\cal D}_{\mu}^{ab}\theta^{b}
; and the functional derivative
\frac{\delta{\cal F}^{a}[A_{\theta}]}{\delta\theta^{b}}
defines the so-called Faddeev-Popov operator
{\cal M}^{ab}
:

\displaystyle{\cal M}^{ab}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\delta{\cal F}^{a}[A_{\theta}(x)]}{\delta\theta^{b}(y)}\right|_{\theta=0}=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}z\,\left.\frac{\delta{\cal F}^{a}[A_{\theta}(x)]}{\delta A^{c}_{\theta}(z)}\right|_{\theta=0}\left.\frac{\delta A^{c}_{\theta}(z)}{\delta\theta^{b}(y)}\right|_{\theta=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\delta{\cal F}^{a}[A_{\theta}(x)]}{\delta A^{c}_{\theta}(y)}\right|_{\theta=0}{\cal D}_{\mu}^{cb}[A].

(7)

(Notice that in the following we will be somewhat more relaxed about the space-time dependence of functional differentiations).

The question is: what are we to do about the
\delta
function and the determinant in the path integral? Let’s treat the
\delta
first, leaving the determinant for later. Recall that the
\delta
function is the Fourier transform of unity, or (for each space-time point and color index)

\displaystyle\delta({\cal F}^{a})={\cal N}\!\int\!{\cal D}b^{a}\exp\left(\!i\!\int b^{a}{\cal F}^{a}\right).

(8)

Next, add and subtract a Gaussian in
b
with a positive parameter
\xi
to get

\displaystyle\delta({\cal F}^{a})={\cal N}\!\int\!{\cal D}b^{a}\exp\left[\!i\!\int\!{\mathrm{d}}^{4}x\,\left(b^{a}{\cal F}^{a}-\frac{\xi}{2}b^{a}b^{a}\right)\right]\underbrace{\exp\left(\frac{\xi}{2}\int\!{\mathrm{d}}^{4}x\,b^{a}b^{a}\right)}_{A\mathrm{-independent\ constant}}.

(9)

After completing the square in the exponent

\displaystyle-b^{a}{\cal F}^{a}+\frac{\xi}{2}b^{a}b^{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{\xi}{2}\left(b^{a}-\frac{{\cal F}^{a}}{\xi}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2\xi}{\cal F}^{a}{\cal F}^{a},

(10)

we can shift the
b
integration variable
b\to b^{\prime}=b-{\cal F}/\xi
to obtain

\displaystyle\delta({\cal F}^{a})={\cal N}^{\prime}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2\xi}\int\!{\mathrm{d}}^{4}x\,{\cal F}^{a}{\cal F}^{a}\right),

(11)

which gives the gauge-fixing term in Eq. (
5
). Notice that as the Yang-Mills action of Eq. (
1
) is gauge invariant, the argument of the gauge fixing function can be changed from the
A_{\theta}
in Eq. (
6
) to simply
A
.

Observe, finally, that we could have decided not to integrate over the
b
fields, in which case the gauge-fixing action would read

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{GF}}

\displaystyle=\int\!{\mathrm{d}}^{4}x\,\left(b^{a}{\cal F}^{a}-\frac{\xi}{2}b^{a}b^{a}\right).

(12)

The
b
s are the so-called Nakanishi-Lautrup (NL) multipliers
Nakanishi 1966
;
Lautrup 1966
; they have no dynamical content and can be eliminated through their (trivial) equations of motion to get back the gauge-fixing action of Eq. (
5
).

Linear covariant gauges are defined by the gauge-fixing function
Fujikawa et al. 1972

\displaystyle{\cal F}^{a}=\partial^{\mu}A^{a}_{\mu},

(13)

and give rise to the following Feynman rules:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Delta_{\mu\nu}^{ab}(q)&=-i\delta^{ab}\frac{1}{q^{2}}\left[P_{\mu\nu}(q)-\xi\frac{q_{\mu}q_{\nu}}{q^{2}}\right],\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Gamma^{(0)}_{A^{a}_{\mu}A^{b}_{\nu}A^{c}_{\rho}}(q,k_{1})&=gf^{abc}\Gamma^{(0)}_{\mu\nu\rho}(q,k_{1})\\
&=gf^{abc}[g_{\nu\rho}(k_{1}-k_{2})_{\mu}+g_{\mu\rho}(k_{2}-q)_{\nu}+g_{\mu\nu}(q-k_{1})_{\rho}],\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Gamma^{(0)}_{A^{a}_{\mu}A^{b}_{\nu}A^{c}_{\rho}A^{d}_{\sigma}}(q,k_{1},k_{2})&=-ig^{2}\Gamma^{abcd(0)}_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}(q,k_{1},k_{2})\\
&=-ig^{2}[f^{ade}f^{ecb}(g_{\mu\rho}g_{\nu\sigma}-g_{\mu\nu}g_{\rho\sigma})\\
&\mathrel{\phantom{=}}+f^{abe}f^{edc}(g_{\mu\sigma}g_{\nu\rho}-g_{\mu\rho}g_{\nu\sigma})\\
&\mathrel{\phantom{=}}+f^{ace}f^{edb}(g_{\mu\sigma}g_{\nu\rho}-g_{\mu\nu}g_{\rho\sigma})],\end{aligned}

where we have introduced the transverse projector

\displaystyle P_{\mu\nu}(q)

\displaystyle=g_{\mu\nu}-\frac{q_{\mu}q_{\nu}}{q^{2}};

\displaystyle q^{\nu}P_{\mu\nu}(q)

\displaystyle=0,

(14)

and, for a generic Green function
\Gamma_{\phi_{1}\phi_{2}\cdots\phi_{n}}
, we indicate only the momenta of the first
n-1
fields, as the one of the
n^{\mathrm{th}}
field is dictated by momentum conservation (we assume all momenta entering unless explicitly indicated,
e.g.
, in certain Feynman rules).

Suppose now we want to calculate the one-loop correction to the gluon propagator. This entails the calculation of the one-loop gluon self-energy, which, according to the Feynman rules stated above, comprises two terms

:

In the Feynman gauge (
\xi=1
) then we have

\displaystyle\Pi^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)=\Pi^{(1)\mathrm{gl}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}g^{2}C_{A}\int_{k}\!\frac{\Gamma^{\rho\sigma(0)}_{\mu}(q,k)\Gamma^{(0)}_{\nu\rho\sigma}(q,-k-q)}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}-g^{2}C_{A}(d-1)\int_{k}\frac{g_{\mu\nu}}{k^{2}},

(15)

where: the trivial color factor
\delta^{ab}
has been omitted;
C_{A}
is the Casimir eigenvalue of the adjoint representation of the
SU(N_{c})
gauge group (
C_{A}=N_{c}
); and we have introduced the dimensional regularization integral measure

\displaystyle\int_{k}=\frac{\mu^{\varepsilon}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\!\mathrm{d}^{d}k,

(16)

with

\mu

At the level of the classical action
S=S_{\mathrm{YM}}+S_{\mathrm{GF}}
the gluon is a massless gauge boson. Consequently, order by order in perturbation theory, gauge symmetry enforces the transversality of the gluon self-energy,

\displaystyle q^{\nu}\Pi^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)=0\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\Pi^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)=P_{\mu\nu}(q)\Pi^{(1)}(q;\mu).

(17)

However, owing to the dimensional regularization result

\displaystyle\int_{k}\frac{1}{k^{2}}=0,

(18)

the seagull term in Eq. (
15

i.e.

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(0)}_{\mu\rho\sigma}(q,k)\Gamma^{\rho\sigma(0)}_{\nu}(-q,k+q)

\displaystyle=4q^{2}g_{\mu\nu}+(d-6)q_{\mu}q_{\nu}+(4d-6)k_{\mu}k_{\nu}+(2d-3)k_{\mu}q_{\nu}

\displaystyle+(2d-3)q_{\mu}k_{\nu},

(19)

and therefore

\displaystyle q^{\nu}\Gamma^{(0)}_{\mu\rho\sigma}(q,k)\Gamma^{\rho\sigma(0)}_{\nu}(-q,k+q)

\displaystyle=(q^{2}+2k{\cdot}q)[(2d-3)k_{\mu}+(d-2)q_{\mu}]+q_{\mu}k{\cdot}q.

(20)

Writing
q^{2}+2k{\cdot}q=(k+q)^{2}-k^{2}

18

20

\displaystyle q^{\nu}\Pi^{(1)\mathrm{gl}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)=\frac{1}{2}g^{2}C_{A}\int_{k}\!\frac{q_{\mu}k{\cdot}q}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}\neq 0.

(21)

Using the dimensional regularization results:

\displaystyle\int_{k}\!\frac{1}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}

\displaystyle=f_{\epsilon}(q^{2};\mu^{2}),

(22a)

\displaystyle\int_{k}\!\frac{k_{\alpha}}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{q_{\alpha}}{2}f_{\epsilon}(q^{2};\mu^{2}),

(22b)

\displaystyle\int_{k}\!\frac{k_{\alpha}k_{\beta}}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{12}\left(g_{\alpha\beta}q^{2}+4q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}\right)f_{\epsilon}(q^{2};\mu^{2}),

(22c)

where

\displaystyle f_{\epsilon}(q^{2};\mu^{2})

\displaystyle=i(4\pi)^{\frac{\epsilon}{2}-2}\Gamma\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)\left(\frac{q^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{\epsilon}{2}};

\displaystyle f_{0}(q^{2};\mu^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{4\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{2}{\epsilon}-\gamma-\ln 4\pi-\ln\frac{q^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right),

it is immediate to show that indeed the 1-loop gluon contribution to the self-energy, Eq. (

\displaystyle\Pi^{(1)\mathrm{gl}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{g^{2}C_{A}}{12}\left(19g_{\mu\nu}-22\frac{q_{\mu}q_{\nu}}{q^{2}}\right)q^{2}f_{\epsilon}(q^{2};\mu^{2}).

(24)

This represents an anomaly: namely, the failure to elevate to the quantum level a classical symmetry (gauge symmetry in this particular case).

Obviously, this result has nothing to do with our choice of performing the calculation of the gluon-self-energy in the Feynman gauge
\xi=1
. Performing it for a generic value of the gauge fixing parameter, would reveal the presence of additional terms proportional to
(1-\xi)
and
(1-\xi)^{2}

(1-\xi)^{n}
are individually transverse as a result of gauge fixing parameter independence.

To rescue the situation we could assume that in evaluating the gluon self-energy the contribution of certain fictitious particles, that will be called ghosts, was left out
Feynman 1964
. Ghosts must contribute loops with opposite sign to ordinary bosonic fields, so that they can cancel the unphysical gluon polarizations in Feynman diagrams. This implies that ghosts must be anticommuting scalars (
i.e.
, Grassmann valued fields), interacting through the Feynman rules:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} D^{ab}(q)&=i\delta^{ab}\frac{1}{q^{2}},\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Gamma^{(0)}_{c^{c}A^{a}_{\mu}\overline{c}^{b}}(k_{2},q)&=gf^{abc}\Gamma^{(0)}_{\mu}(q,k_{2})\\
&=gf^{abc}k_{1\mu}.\end{aligned}

Then the ghost contribution to the gluon self-energy would be

or, using the corresponding Feynman rules (and sending
k\to-k
in the first diagram above)

\displaystyle\Pi^{(1)\mathrm{gh}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)

\displaystyle=-g^{2}C_{A}\int_{k}\!\frac{k_{\mu}(k+q)_{\nu}}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}.

(25)

Indeed, then the sum of the gluon and ghost contributions to the gluon self-energy is transverse:

\displaystyle q^{\nu}\Pi^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)=q^{\nu}[\Pi^{(1)\mathrm{gl}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)+\Pi^{(1)\mathrm{gh}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)]=0,

(26)

even though individually this is not true, since

\displaystyle q^{\nu}\Pi^{(1)\mathrm{gl}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)

\displaystyle\neq 0;

\displaystyle q^{\nu}\Pi^{(1)\mathrm{gh}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)

\displaystyle\neq 0.

(27)

To check the transversality property of Eq. (

\displaystyle\int_{k}\!\frac{k_{\mu}(k+q)_{\nu}}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}=\int_{k}\!\frac{k_{\nu}(k+q)_{\mu}}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}.

(28)

Contracting with

\displaystyle q^{2}\int_{k}\!\frac{k_{\mu}}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}=\int_{k}\!\frac{q_{\mu}k{\cdot}q}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}.

Next, consider the right-hand side of Eq. (

\displaystyle q^{\nu}\int_{k}\!\frac{k_{\nu}(k+q)_{\mu}}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{k}\!\frac{q_{\mu}k{\cdot}q}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{k}\!\frac{k_{\mu}[(k+q)^{2}-k^{2}-q^{2}]}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{k}\!\frac{q_{\mu}k{\cdot}q}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}q^{2}\int_{k}\!\frac{k_{\mu}}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{k}\!\frac{q_{\mu}k{\cdot}q}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}},

where in the last step we have used the result of Eq. (

\displaystyle q^{\nu}\Pi^{(1)\mathrm{gh}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)=-\frac{1}{2}g^{2}C_{A}\int_{k}\!\frac{q_{\mu}k{\cdot}q}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}=-q^{\nu}\Pi^{(1)\mathrm{gl}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu),

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{FP}}=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\overline{c}^{a}(-\partial^{\mu}{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu})c^{b}=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}y\,\overline{c}^{a}{\cal M}^{ab}c^{b},

In what follows we will thus restrict our attention to the linear covariant gauges of Eq. (

In so doing we will, for the moment, ignore the issue of Gribov copies
Gribov 1978
;
Singer 1978
. Strictly speaking, the complication described below arises within naive analyses of the gauge-fixing procedure, where the standard Faddeev–Popov construction does not guarantee a unique representative of each gauge orbit. As we will discuss at the end of our presentation, within the dynamical mass generation framework that will emerge later, the difficulties associated with Gribov copies are expected to be naturally avoided
Gao et al. 2018
.

To illustrate how this problem arises in the standard formulation, consider the geometric interpretation of the gauge-fixing condition. In principle, the hyper-surface defined by
{\cal F}^{a}=\partial^{\mu}A^{a}_{\mu}=0
should intersect each gauge orbit only once. Let us therefore consider a gauge transformed field

\displaystyle A^{a}_{\theta\mu}=A^{a}_{\mu}+{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}\theta^{b},

(40)

and ask under which conditions it satisfies the same gauge condition. One finds that
\partial^{\mu}A^{a}_{\theta\mu}=0
provided that

\displaystyle\partial^{\mu}{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}\theta^{b}=0\hskip 17.00024pt\Rightarrow\hskip 17.00024pt{\cal M}^{ab}\theta^{b}=0,

(41)

where
{\cal M}
is the Faddeev–Popov operator. Thus, if
{\cal M}
possesses zero-modes, there exist non-trivial
\vartheta^{b}
such that
A^{a}_{\mu}
and
A^{a}_{\vartheta\mu}
are distinct fields satisfying the same gauge condition. In this case the gauge orbit intersects the gauge-fixing hyper-plane more than once, and the path integral still integrates multiple times over the same orbit. Notice that in Abelian gauge theories (or in non-covariant gauges) the Faddeev-Popov operator is field independent and thus zero-modes can be removed through suitable boundary conditions.

If
\mathcal{M}
is positive definite, then the gauge fixing is locally unique in the neighborhood of that configuration. This observation leads to the definition of the first Gribov region
\Omega
: it is the set of all transverse gauge fields for which the Faddeev–Popov operator is strictly positive,

\displaystyle\Omega=\left\{A_{\mu}\;\big|\;\partial_{\mu}A_{\mu}=0\ \text{and}\ \mathcal{M}>0\right\}.

(42)

The boundary of this region, known as the Gribov horizon
\partial\Omega
, is reached when the smallest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{M}
vanishes. At this point, the gauge fixing hypersurface becomes tangent to the gauge orbit, and, as we have seen above, an infinitesimal gauge transformation exists that preserves the gauge condition. This signals the onset of Gribov copies: beyond the horizon, the Faddeev–Popov determinant changes sign and multiple gauge equivalent configurations proliferate. Although configurations inside
\Omega
are still not completely free of copies, the region is convex, bounded in all directions, and contains the perturbative vacuum, making it a natural and well defined domain to which the functional integral can be restricted. From a dynamical point of view, the Gribov horizon plays a crucial role in the IR. As the functional integral samples larger field amplitudes, it becomes increasingly sensitive to the accumulation of near zero modes of the Faddeev–Popov operator, causing typical configurations to lie close to
\partial\Omega
.

As anticipated, however, within the dynamical mass generation framework that we will develop, the difficulties associated with Gribov copies are avoided.

3
Becchi–Rouet–Stora–Tyutin (BRST) symmetry, anti-BRST symmetry, and the ghost sector of
SU(N_{c})
Yang-Mills theories

3.1
Becchi–Rouet–Stora–Tyutin symmetry

As we have seen, the quantization of a Yang-Mills theory requires fixing a gauge; and this operation modifies the action as

\displaystyle S=S_{\mathrm{YM}}+S_{\mathrm{GF}}+S_{\mathrm{FP}},

(43)

with each term given in Eqs. (
1
), (
5
) and (
34
) respectively. As already noticed, however, the gauge-fixing term can be written as in Eq. (
12
), since the NL multipliers satisfy the trivial equations of motion:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\cal L}}{\partial b^{a}}=0\quad\Rightarrow\qquad b^{a}=\frac{1}{\xi}{\cal F}^{a},

(44)

which gives back Eq. (
5
) once inserted in Eq. (
12
).

Now let us introduce the Becchi–Rouet–Stora–Tyutin (BRST) operator
s

Becchi et al. 1975
;
Becchi et al. 1976
;
Tyutin
such that

\displaystyle sA^{a}_{\mu}={\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}c^{b}.

(45)

Then, as the action of
s
on the gauge field coincides with its gauge transformation where the gauge parameter has been replace by the ghost field
c
, the gauge invariance of the Yang-Mills action, Eq. (
1
), implies also BRST invariance:

\displaystyle sS_{\mathrm{YM}}=0.

(46)

On the other hand, setting

\displaystyle s\overline{c}^{a}

\displaystyle=b^{a};

\displaystyle sb^{a}=0,

(47)

implies that the sum of gauge fixing and Faddeev-Popov ghost actions can be rewritten as a total BRST variation

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{GF}}+S_{\mathrm{FP}}

\displaystyle=s\Psi;

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\overline{c}^{a}\left({\cal F}^{a}-\frac{\xi}{2}b^{a}\right),

(48)

where
\Psi
is the so-called gauge-fixing fermion, and

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{GF}}

\displaystyle=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\left(b^{a}{\cal F}^{a}-\frac{\xi}{2}b^{a}b^{a}\right);

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{FP}}

\displaystyle=-\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\overline{c}^{a}s{\cal F}^{a}.

(49)

What is finally missing is to establish the BRST variation of the ghost field. This can be obtained considering the closure of the gauge (Lie) algebra, which states that the commutator of two gauge transformations must itself be a gauge transformation, with

\displaystyle[\delta_{\theta_{1}},\delta_{\theta_{2}}]

\displaystyle=\delta_{[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}=\delta_{\theta_{3}};

\displaystyle\theta^{a}_{3}

\displaystyle=gf^{abc}\theta_{1}^{b}\theta_{2}^{c}.

(50)

As
s
is Grassmann-odd, this implies the nilpotency of the BRST operator:

\displaystyle\{s,s\}

\displaystyle=0\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad s^{2}=0,

(51)

so that requiring
s^{2}A^{a}_{\mu}=0
yields the ghost transformation

\displaystyle sc^{a}=-\frac{1}{2}gf^{abc}c^{b}c^{c}.

(52)

Thus, the gauge-fixing procedure necessary to quantize the Yang-Mills action, Eq. (
1
), has generated the new action
S
, Eq. (
43
), that is invariant under BRST transformations:
sS=0
, with
s^{2}=0
and

\displaystyle sA^{a}_{\mu}

\displaystyle={\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}c^{b};

\displaystyle sc^{a}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}gf^{abc}c^{b}c^{c};

\displaystyle s\overline{c}^{a}

\displaystyle=b^{a};

\displaystyle sb^{a}=0.

(53)

In other words the gauge symmetry of the original action, Eq. (
1
), has been traded for the BRST symmetry of the gauge-fixed action, Eq. (
43
).

The path integral measure is also invariant under the BRST transformations of Eq. (
53
). To show that this is indeed the case, let’s consider an infinitesimal BRST change of variable

\displaystyle\Phi\to\Phi^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\Phi+\epsilon s\Phi;

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle=\{A^{a}_{\mu},c^{a},\overline{c}^{a},b^{a}\},

(54)

where
\epsilon
must be Grassmann-odd:
\epsilon^{2}=0
.
Then the measure transforms according to
{\cal D}\Phi^{\prime}=J{\cal D}\Phi
where the Jacobian
J
is given by

\displaystyle J=\mathrm{det}\left(\frac{\delta\Phi^{\prime}}{\delta\Phi}\right)=\mathrm{det}\left(1+\epsilon\frac{\delta s\Phi}{\delta\Phi}\right)=1+\epsilon\mathrm{Tr}\left(\frac{\delta s\Phi}{\delta\Phi}\right).

(55)

As we want to show that the
J=1
we need to prove that the condition

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\frac{\delta s\Phi}{\delta\Phi}\right)=0,

(56)

holds true for all
\Phi
. Indeed:

\displaystyle A^{a}_{\mu}:

\displaystyle\hskip 17.00024pt\mathrm{Tr}\!\left(\frac{\delta\,(sA^{a}_{\mu})}{\delta A^{b}_{\nu}}\right)=\left.gf^{acd}\frac{\delta A^{c}_{\mu}}{\delta A^{b}_{\nu}}c^{d}\,\right|_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=b\\
\mu=\nu\end{subarray}}=gf^{aad}g^{\mu}_{\mu}c^{d}=0,

(57a)

\displaystyle c^{a}:

\displaystyle\hskip 17.00024pt\mathrm{Tr}\!\left(\frac{\delta\,(sc^{a})}{\delta c^{b}}\right)=-\frac{1}{2}gf^{acd}\left.\frac{\delta(c^{d}c^{d})}{\delta c^{b}}\right|_{a=b}=-\frac{1}{2}g(f^{aad}c^{d}+f^{aca}c^{c})=0,

(57b)

\displaystyle\overline{c}^{a}:

\displaystyle\hskip 17.00024pt\mathrm{Tr}\!\left(\frac{\delta\,(s\overline{c}^{a})}{\delta\overline{c}^{b}}\right)=\left.\frac{\delta\,b^{a}}{\delta\overline{c}^{b}}\right|_{a=b}=0,

(57c)

\displaystyle b^{a}:

\displaystyle\hskip 17.00024pt\mathrm{Tr}\!\left(\frac{\delta\,(sb^{a})}{\delta b^{b}}\right)=0.

(57d)

Notice that if it turns out that
J\neq 1
, we get a non invariant measure
{\cal D}\Phi^{\prime}=(1+\epsilon\Delta){\cal D}\Phi
with
\Delta
a local functional; inserting it back in the path integral produces a breaking term

\displaystyle sS\sim\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\Delta,

(58)

which represents a gauge anomaly. The most famous example is the Adler-Bell-Jackiw (chiral) anomaly
Adler 1969
;
Bell and Jackiw 1969
, which is due to the non-unity value Jacobian from a chiral rotation, giving a non-vanishing
\Delta\sim\mathrm{Tr}(\gamma_{5})

Dittrich and Reuter 1986
.

In our case, however, the theory is non-anomalous and the invariance of the path integral measure has the important consequence that a total BRST variation integrates to zero. In fact, consider the path integral of a generic functional
F[\Phi]
; then under a BRST transformation

\displaystyle\int\!{\cal D}\Phi\,F[\Phi]=\int\!{\cal D}\Phi^{\prime}\,F[\Phi^{\prime}]=\int\!{\cal D}\Phi\,F[\Phi+\epsilon s\Phi]=\int\!{\cal D}\Phi\,F[\Phi]+\epsilon\int\!{\cal D}\Phi\,sF[\Phi],

(59)

and therefore

\displaystyle\int\!{\cal D}\Phi\,sF[\Phi]=0.

(60)

As we will see, this result will have important consequences in what follows.

The BRST symmetry of the gauge-fixed Yang-Mills action is most easily exposed through the so-called Batalin-Vilkoviski (BV) formalism
Batalin and Vilkovisky 1977
;
Batalin and Vilkovisky 1983
. To this end, we introduce for every field
\Phi=\{A^{a}_{\mu},c^{a},\overline{c}^{a},b^{a}\}
an antifield
\Phi^{*}=\{A^{*a}_{\mu},c^{*a},\overline{c}^{*a},b^{*a}\}
with: opposite Grassmann parity of the corresponding field; ghost charge
\mathrm{gh}(\Phi^{*})=1+\mathrm{gh}(\Phi)
, where
\mathrm{gh}(\Phi)=\mathrm{gh}(A^{a}_{\mu},c^{a},\overline{c}^{a},b^{a})=(0,1,-1,0)
; and, finally,
s\Phi^{*}=0
.

Next we add to the action in Eq. (
43
) a term coupling the antifields with the (BRST variation of the) corresponding field, obtaining the complete action

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{C}}

\displaystyle=S+S_{\mathrm{BV}};

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{BV}}

\displaystyle=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\sum\Phi^{*}s\Phi=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\left(A^{*a}_{\mu}sA^{a\mu}+c^{*a}sc^{a}+\overline{c}^{*a}b^{a}\right).

(61)

Notice that as
sb^{a}=0
there is no need for an antifield associated to the NL multipliers; in addition we can also write

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{C}}

\displaystyle=S_{\mathrm{YM}}+sX;

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\left[\sum(-1)^{\mathrm{gh}(\Phi^{*})}\Phi^{*}\Phi+\overline{c}^{a}{\cal F}^{a}-\frac{\xi}{2}\overline{c}^{a}b^{a}\right].

(62)

Both the BV action
S_{\mathrm{BV}}
and the sum of the gauge-fixing and Faddeev-Popov actions
S_{\mathrm{GF+FP}}=S_{\mathrm{GF}}+S_{\mathrm{FP}}
are
s
-exact (or pure gauge),
i.e.
, there exists a functional
X_{i}
such that
S_{i}=sX_{i}
(obviously
X=\sum X_{i}
), see, in particular, Eq. (
62
) above and Eq. (
48
). While this condition automatically ensures that
S_{i}
is also
s
-closed (or gauge invariant),
i.e.
,
sS_{i}=0
, it is a stronger condition than the latter. Being gauge invariant does not mean being pure gauge: think about
S_{\mathrm{YM}}
, which is clearly
s
-closed but not
s
-exact.

s
-exact terms do not change the theory’s partition function. In fact, with
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
consider

\displaystyle Z_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\Phi\,\exp\left[i(S+\lambda sX)\right];

\displaystyle\frac{dZ_{\lambda}}{d\lambda}

\displaystyle=i\!\int\!\mathcal{D}\Phi\,(sX)\,\exp\left[i(S+\lambda sX)\right].

(63)

Since
sS=0
and
s^{2}=0
, we have
s\left\{X\exp\left[i(S+\lambda sX)\right]\right\}=(sX)\exp\left[i(S+\lambda sX)\right]

and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{dZ_{\lambda}}{d\lambda}

\displaystyle=i\!\int\!\mathcal{D}\Phi\,s\left\{X\exp\left[i(S+\lambda sX)\right]\right\}.

(64)

On the other hand, we know from Eq. (
60
) that the path integral of a total BRST variation is zero; thus

\displaystyle\frac{dZ_{\lambda}}{d\lambda}=0\hskip 17.00024pt\Rightarrow\hskip 17.00024ptZ_{\lambda}=Z_{0}=\int\mathcal{D}\Phi\,\exp(iS).

(65)

This is sometimes expressed by saying that physical theories are determined solely by the cohomology class of the action (in ghost number zero); see,
e.g.
,
Henneaux and Teitelboim 1992
;
Piguet and Sorella 1995a
. Any two actions differing by an
s
-exact term are in the same cohomology class:

\displaystyle S^{\prime}=S+sX\hskip 17.00024pt\Rightarrow\hskip 17.00024pt[S^{\prime}]=[S]\in H^{0}(s),

(66)

and so we are free to shift the Yang-Mills action by
s
-exact terms, as done in Eqs. (
43
) and (
61
).

The same applies to the observables of the theory. Consider, in fact, the expectation value of an
s
-closed functional
{\cal O}[\Phi]
, and let’s show that it does not change when the action is shifted by an
s
-exact term. Defining

\displaystyle\langle{\cal O}\rangle_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{Z_{\lambda}}\int\mathcal{D}\Phi\,{\cal O}\exp\left[i(S+\lambda sX)\right],

(67)

the same reasoning above applied in the case of the partition function
Z_{\lambda}
, implies that whenever

\displaystyle\frac{d\langle{\cal O}\rangle_{\lambda}}{d\lambda}=0\hskip 17.00024pt\Rightarrow\hskip 17.00024pt\langle{\cal O}\rangle_{\lambda}=\langle{\cal O}\rangle_{0}=\langle{\cal O}\rangle.

(68)

Thus, within a BRST formulation of gauge theories: physical observables
{\cal O}
are
s
-closed,
s{\cal O}=0
; and since two observables
{\cal O}
and

differing by an

-exact term

have the same expectation value, they are physically equivalent. Thus also observables live in the (zero ghost charge) cohomology of the BRST operator:
[{\cal O}]\in H^{0}(s)
.

The complete action of Eq. (
61
) satisfies the master equation

\displaystyle\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\sum\frac{\delta S_{\mathrm{C}}}{\delta\Phi^{*}}\frac{\delta S_{\mathrm{C}}}{\delta\Phi}=0.

(69)

To verify the result above, observe that, the BRST differential can be written as

\displaystyle{\cal S}

\displaystyle=\sum\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,s\Phi(x)\frac{\delta}{\delta\Phi(x)}.

(70)

Then we have

\displaystyle{\cal S}(S_{\mathrm{C}})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\sum(s\Phi)\frac{\delta S_{\mathrm{C}}}{\delta\Phi}=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\sum\frac{\delta S_{\mathrm{C}}}{\delta\Phi^{*}}\frac{\delta S_{\mathrm{C}}}{\delta\Phi}=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\sum\frac{\delta S_{\mathrm{C}}}{\delta\Phi^{*}}\frac{\delta S_{\mathrm{YM}}}{\delta\Phi}+\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\sum\frac{\delta S_{\mathrm{C}}}{\delta\Phi^{*}}\frac{\delta S_{\mathrm{BV}}}{\delta\Phi}.

(71)

Now, on the one hand the first term on the right-hand side of the equation above is zero due to the BRST invariance of the Yang-Mills action

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\sum(s\Phi)\frac{\delta S_{\mathrm{C}}}{\delta\Phi}=\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,(sS_{\mathrm{C}})=0;

(72)

on the other hand, the second term is zero due to the nilpotency of the BRST:

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\sum\Phi^{*}\!\sum(s\Phi^{\prime})\frac{\delta(s\Phi)}{\delta\Phi^{\prime}}=\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\sum\Phi^{*}s^{2}\Phi=0.

(73)

Overall, we have then have
{\cal S}(S_{\mathrm{C}})=0
, which is just Eq. (
69
).

To elevate the BRST symmetry to the quantum level, one then invokes the quantum action principle (QAP) requiring
Piguet and Sorella 1995b

\displaystyle{\cal S}(\Gamma)=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\left(\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta A^{*a}_{\mu}}\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta A^{a\mu}}+\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta c^{*a}}\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta c^{a}}+b^{a}\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta\overline{c}^{a}}\right)=0,

(74)

where
{\cal S}
is the so-called Slavnov–Taylor identity (STI) functional and
\Gamma
is the full quantum effective action.

The QAP represents the statement that classical local variations (
i.e.
, gauge transformations) of the action correspond to local operator insertions (
i.e.
, BRST variations) in the theory’s quantum Green functions. It is what ensures that the symmetries and equations of motion survive renormalization; or tells exactly how they break when anomalies appear.

The structure of the STI functional in Eq. (
74
) can be simplified by noticing that the anti-ghost
\overline{c}
and NL multiplier
b
have a linear BRST transformation; thus, the complete action of Eq. (
61
) can be split into the sum of a minimal sector, which depends on the minimal variables
\{A^{a}_{\mu},c^{a},A^{*a}_{\mu},c^{*a}\}
, and a trivial sector, which depends on
\{\overline{c}^{a},\overline{c}^{*a},b^{a}\}
:

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{C}}=S_{\mathrm{min}}+\overline{c}^{*a}b^{a},

(75)

and similarly for the effective action
\Gamma
. Variables like
\overline{c}
and
b
, which are such that
s\overline{c}=b
and
sb=0
, are called BRST doublets or trivial pairs. The last term in Eq. (
75
) has no effect on the master equation of Eq. (
69
), which is satisfied by
S_{\mathrm{min}}
alone; and in what follows we will only consider the minimal sector of the action, with the STI functional given by

\displaystyle{\cal S}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\left(\frac{\delta\Gamma_{\mathrm{min}}}{\delta A^{*a}_{\mu}}\frac{\delta\Gamma_{\mathrm{min}}}{\delta A^{a\mu}}+\frac{\delta\Gamma_{\mathrm{min}}}{\delta c^{*a}}\frac{\delta\Gamma_{\mathrm{min}}}{\delta c^{a}}\right)=0.

(76)

Thus, the BRST differential can be decomposed as

\displaystyle{\cal S}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathrm{min}}\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta\Phi^{*}}\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta\Phi}+\sum_{\mathrm{doublets}}\hskip-1.42271pt\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,(s\phi)\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta\phi}.

(77)

Eq. (
76
) is one of a set of functional equations that we will need to understand the dynamics of the infra-red (IR) sector of Yang-Mills theories. Its functional differentiations with respect to combinations of different fields eventually evaluated at zero fields and antifields, give rise to the STIs, which encode existing symmetry relations among the theory’s Green functions.

In order to reach meaningful (nonzero) STIs one needs to keep in mind that:
{\cal S}(\Gamma)
is Grassmann-odd; and Green functions with nonzero ghost charge must vanish. This implies that nonzero expressions are obtained when taking differentiations with respect to field combinations containing: one ghost field; two ghost fields and one antifield; or a ghost antifield and three ghost fields.

In particular, as the linear part of the BRST transformation of a gauge boson fields is proportional to the divergence of a ghost field,
sA^{a}_{\mu}\sim\partial_{\mu}c^{a}
, STIs involving two or more gauge bosons are obtained by differentiating
{\cal S}(\Gamma)
with respect to a combination of fields in which one of the gluons is replaced by a ghost field.

As a first example of an STI, let’s study the gauge boson 2-point function
\Gamma_{AA}
(the self-energy). According to what is stated above we then have to consider the following functional differentiation:

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\delta^{2}{\cal S}(\Gamma)}{\delta A^{a}_{\mu}\delta c^{b}}\right|_{A,c=0}=0.

(78)

Then, using the minimal sector only,

\displaystyle\frac{\delta{\cal S}(\Gamma)}{\delta c^{b}}=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma_{\mathrm{min}}}{\delta c^{b}\delta A^{*c}_{\rho}}\frac{\delta\Gamma_{\mathrm{min}}}{\delta A^{c\rho}}+\cdots,

(79)

where the dots indicate terms that would give vanishing contributions to the final result. Next

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{2}{\cal S}(\Gamma)}{\delta A^{a}_{\mu}\delta c^{b}}=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma_{\mathrm{min}}}{\delta c^{b}\delta A^{*c}_{\rho}}\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma_{\mathrm{min}}}{\delta A^{a}_{\mu}\delta A^{c\rho}}+\cdots.

(80)

Then, setting to zero all fields and antifields, we obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\mathrm{min}}_{c^{b}A^{*c}_{\rho}}(q)\Gamma^{\mathrm{min}}_{A^{c\rho}A^{a}_{\mu}}(q)=0.

(81)

We now use Lorenz invariance to write
\Gamma_{c^{b}A^{*c}_{\rho}}(q)=q_{\rho}\delta^{ac}\Gamma_{cA^{*}}(q^{2})
; and since
\Gamma_{cA^{*}}(q^{2})
is non vanishing beyond tree-level, we obtain the transversality of gauge-boson 2-point function

\displaystyle q^{\nu}\Gamma^{\mathrm{min}}_{A^{a}_{\mu}A^{b}_{\nu}}(q)=0\hskip 17.00024pt\Rightarrow\hskip 17.00024pt\Gamma^{\mathrm{min}}_{A^{a}_{\mu}A^{b}_{\nu}}(q)=i\delta^{ab}P_{\mu\nu}(q)\Pi(q^{2}).

(82)

Additional functional equations can be obtained by studying the dependence of the complete action on the NL multiplier
b
and the anti-ghost field
\overline{c}
, both depending crucially on the form of the gauge-fixing function
{\cal F}
. In the first case, we immediately obtain the equation of motion of the
b
-field

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta b^{a}}+\xi b^{a}-{\cal F}^{a}=0.

(83)

In the second case, using the BRST differential of Eq. (
70
) we find that for any functional

such that
\mathrm{gh}(sF)=0
, the nilpotency of the BRST operator implies

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{\delta}{\delta\overline{c}^{a}},{\cal S}\right\}F

\displaystyle=\sum\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\left[\frac{\delta}{\delta\overline{c}^{a}}s\varphi(x)\right]\frac{\delta F}{\delta\varphi(x)}.

(84)

As the only dependence on the anti-ghost field is in the
s
-exact term of the action, one has

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta\overline{c}^{a}}=\frac{\delta}{\delta\overline{c}^{a}}(sX)=\sum\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\left[\frac{\delta}{\delta\overline{c}^{a}}\,s\varphi(x)\right]\frac{\delta X}{\delta\varphi(x)}-s\,\frac{\delta X}{\delta\overline{c}^{a}}.

(85)

Using Eq. (

), we then see that the first term on the right-hand side of the equation above is zero whereas for the second term we obtain

\displaystyle s\,\frac{\delta X}{\delta\overline{c}^{a}}

\displaystyle=s{\cal F}^{a}=\partial^{\mu}(sA^{a}_{\mu})=\partial^{\mu}\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta A^{*a}_{\mu}},

(86)

yielding the final ghost equation

\displaystyle{\cal G}^{a}\Gamma

\displaystyle=0;

\displaystyle{\cal G}^{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{\delta\overline{c}^{a}}+\partial^{\mu}\frac{\delta}{\delta A^{*a}_{\mu}}.

(87)

A more expeditious way to obtain the ghost equation Eq. (
87
) is, in this case, to observe that Eq. (

) implies that the entire action’s dependence on the anti-ghost field is contained in the term

\displaystyle\overline{c}^{a}s{\cal F}^{a}=\overline{c}^{a}\partial^{\mu}(sA^{a}_{\mu})=\overline{c}^{a}\partial^{\mu}\frac{\delta S_{\mathrm{C}}}{\delta A^{*a}_{\mu}}.

(88)

Passing then to the full quantum effective action

one obtains the same ghost equation of Eq. (

), namely

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta\overline{c}^{a}}+\partial^{\mu}\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta A^{*a}_{\mu}}=0.

(89)

The ghost equation of Eq. (
87
) can be used to understand the difference between considering the minimal or complete action functional when deriving STIs. In practice, STIs generated from the minimal action coincide with the ones obtained from the complete action after the implementation of Eq. (

To see this, let’s consider again the transversality of the gauge-boson two-point function, Eq. (

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{2}{\cal S}(\Gamma)}{\delta A^{a}_{\mu}\delta c^{b}}=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\left(\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma_{\mathrm{C}}}{\delta c^{b}\delta A^{*c}_{\rho}}\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma_{\mathrm{C}}}{\delta A^{c\rho}\delta A^{a}_{\mu}}+\frac{\delta b^{c}}{\delta A^{a}_{\mu}}\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma_{\mathrm{C}}}{\delta c^{b}\delta\overline{c}^{c}}\right)+\cdots,

yielding, after setting all the fields to zero,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\mathrm{C}}_{c^{b}A^{*c}_{\rho}}(q)\Gamma^{\mathrm{C}}_{A^{c\rho}A^{a}_{\mu}}(q)+\frac{q_{\mu}}{\xi}\Gamma^{\mathrm{C}}_{c^{b}\overline{c}^{a}}(q)=0.

Differentiating the ghost equation with respect to

we obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\mathrm{C}}_{c^{b}\overline{c}^{a}}(q)+iq^{\mu}\Gamma^{\mathrm{C}}_{c^{b}A^{*a}_{\mu}}(q)=0,

(92)

which, inserted back into Eq. (

3.2
Anti-BRST

In the BRST formulation of gauge theories, the ghost field
c
plays a particularly distinguished role. It effectively takes the place of the infinitesimal gauge transformation parameter that appears in the classical gauge symmetry. As we have glimpsed, the transformation properties of
c
can be understood as reflecting the cohomological structure of the Lie algebra associated with the gauge group (see,
e.g.
,
Nakanishi 1978
;
Barnich et al. 2000
).

By contrast, the anti-ghost
\overline{c}
and its BRST partner, the Nakanishi–Lautrup field
b
, enter the theory for a rather different reason. They act as auxiliary fields that implement the gauge-fixing condition
{\cal F}=0
and its BRST transform
s{\cal F}=0
at the level of the functional integral. In this sense, they serve as Lagrange multipliers ensuring the consistency of the chosen gauge. It is also important to note that, unlike the ghost field, the antighost
\overline{c}
is not the Hermitian conjugate of the ghost
c
and satisfies an equation of motion that differs from the one satisfied by
c
; the two are independent Grassmann variables.

Given these differences, one might expect that
c
and
\overline{c}
could never be interchanged in a meaningful symmetry. Surprisingly, however, it was shown long ago
Curci and Ferrari 1976
;
Ojima 1980
;
Baulieu and Thierry-Mieg 1982

that the BRST algebra admits an additional nilpotent symmetry, known as the anti-BRST symmetry, in which the roles of ghost and anti-ghost fields are precisely exchanged. In fact, the anti-BRST transformations can be obtained directly from the BRST ones of Eq. (
53
) by simply swapping
c
and
\overline{c}
;
that is one has

\displaystyle\overline{s}A^{a}_{\mu}

\displaystyle={\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}\overline{c}^{b};

\displaystyle\overline{s}\,\overline{c}^{a}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}gf^{abc}\overline{c}^{b}\overline{c}^{c};

\displaystyle\overline{s}c^{a}

\displaystyle=\overline{b}^{a};

\displaystyle\overline{s}\overline{b}^{a}

\displaystyle=0.

(95)

Additionally, to close the
s
and
\overline{s}
algebra two more transformations are needed:

\displaystyle s\overline{b}^{a}

\displaystyle=gf^{abc}\overline{b}^{b}c^{c};

\displaystyle\overline{s}b^{a}

\displaystyle=gf^{abc}b^{b}\overline{c}^{c}.

(96)

Clearly both
s
and
\overline{s}
are nilpotent:
s^{2}
=
\overline{s}^{2}=0
; then, the additional (natural) requirement that their sum is also nilpotent (or that
\{s,\overline{s}\}=0
), results in the constraint
Curci and Ferrari 1976

\displaystyle\overline{b}^{a}=-b^{a}-gf^{abc}c^{b}\overline{c}^{c}.

(97)

Use of the Jacobi identity,
f^{ade}f^{bce}+f^{bde}f^{cae}+f^{cde}f^{abe}=0
, shows that this constraint is consistent with the algebra closure of Eq. (

\displaystyle s\overline{s}A^{a}_{\mu}

\displaystyle={\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}b^{b}+gf^{abc}\left({\cal D}_{\mu}^{bd}c^{d}\right)\overline{c}^{c};

\displaystyle s\overline{s}c^{a}

\displaystyle=s\overline{b}^{a};

\displaystyle s\overline{s}\,\overline{c}^{a}

\displaystyle=-\overline{s}b^{a}.

(98)

To understand why the IR behavior of the
G
function is so important we need to go back to the cohomology of the BRST operator. Recall that the physical observables of a Yang-Mills theory live in the zero ghost charge cohomology of the BRST operator
H^{0}(s)
; this is also true for the asymptotic particles (the spectrum) of the theory.

Owing to Noether’s theorem, we know that associated to the BRST symmetry there is a conserved charge
Q_{B}
so that
Q_{B}^{2}=0
and for any physical state
|\mathrm{phys}\rangle
one has

\displaystyle Q_{B}|\mathrm{phys}\rangle=0.

(130)

In this way, two physical states that differ by a
Q_{B}
exact term are in the same cohomology class:

\displaystyle Q_{B}|\mathrm{phys}\rangle^{\prime}=Q_{B}\left(|\mathrm{phys}\rangle+Q_{B}|\chi\rangle\right)=Q_{B}|\mathrm{phys}\rangle=0.

(131)

A way to realize color confinement is then through the construction of a color global charge
Q^{a}
such that for every physical state

\displaystyle Q^{a}|\mathrm{phys}\rangle=0,

(132)

which would then imply that all states are color singlets. In particular, if we could write

\displaystyle Q^{a}=\{Q_{B},{\cal N}^{a}\},

(133)

for some
{\cal N}^{a}
then

\displaystyle Q^{a}|\mathrm{phys}\rangle=Q_{B}{\cal N}^{a}|\mathrm{phys}\rangle+{\cal N}^{a}Q_{B}|\mathrm{phys}\rangle=Q_{B}\left({\cal N}^{a}|\mathrm{phys}\rangle\right),

(134)

which implies that
Q^{a}
is BRST exact and therefore trivial in cohomology space.

Now it turns out that the equation of motion of the gauge field can be written as

\displaystyle J^{a}_{\mu}=\partial^{\nu}F^{a}_{\mu\nu}+\{Q_{B},{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}\bar{c}^{b}\},

(135)

where
J^{a}_{\mu}
is the Noether current associated with color symmetry. Additionally, the Noether current is not uniquely defined, since one can always add a term of the form
\partial_{\nu}f^{\mu\nu}
, where
f^{\mu\nu}
is an arbitrary local antisymmetric tensor. In other words, the modified current
J^{\prime a}_{\mu}\equiv J^{a}_{\mu}+\partial_{\nu}f^{\mu\nu}
is still conserved; and, moreover, the corresponding conserved charge generates the correct infinitesimal color transformation on any field operator.
Thus we can define the color charge
Q^{a}
as

\displaystyle Q^{a}

\displaystyle=\int\!{\mathrm{d}}^{3}x\,\left(J^{a}_{0}-\partial^{\nu}F^{a}_{0\nu}\right)=\int\!{\mathrm{d}}^{3}x\,\{Q_{B},{\cal D}^{ab}_{0}\bar{c}^{b}\},

(136)

which is exactly of the needed form of Eq. (
133
) once we proceed to the identification
{\cal N}^{a}=\{Q_{B},{\cal D}^{ab}_{0}\bar{c}^{b}\}
.

The problem here is that the definition in Eq. (
136
) does not lead to a well-defined color charge operator owing to the presence of massless one-particle poles, which render the 3-volume integration divergent. To see this, observe that the
b
-equation, Eq. (

\displaystyle\Gamma_{A^{a}_{\mu}b^{b}}=-iq_{\mu}\delta^{ab}\hskip 17.00024pt\Rightarrow\hskip 17.00024pt\langle T(A^{a}_{\mu}b^{b})\rangle=i\frac{q_{\mu}}{q^{2}}\delta^{ab}

.

(137)

In addition, ghost charge conservation implies that
\langle T(A^{a}_{\mu}\bar{c}^{b})\rangle=0
so that

\displaystyle s\langle T(A^{a}_{\mu}\bar{c}^{b})\rangle=0\hskip 17.00024pt\Rightarrow\hskip 17.00024pt\langle T[{\cal D}^{ac}_{\mu}c^{c})\bar{c}^{b}]\rangle=-\langle T(A^{a}_{\mu}b^{b})\rangle=-i\frac{q_{\mu}}{q^{2}}\delta^{ab}.

(138)

As the relations above are
exact
, they imply the existence of massless asympotic fields, the so-called quartet mechanism, see
Kugo and Ojima 1979
. Cancelling these poles requires a certain relation between the weights with which such fields contribute to the charge in Eq. (

). Indeed, one finds
Kugo and Ojima 1979
;
Kugo 1995

\displaystyle Q^{a}

\displaystyle=\int\!{\mathrm{d}}^{3}x\,\left(J^{a}_{0}+\frac{v}{w}\partial^{\nu}F^{a}_{0\nu}\right),

(139)

subjected to the constraint

\displaystyle v=-w+(1+u).

(140)

Then, if
1+u=1+G(0)=0
,

and one is left with the well defined color charge in Eq. (

), that coincides with Eq. (

).

), and the WI functional, Eq. (
113
). It turns out that there are gauges in which both a WI functional and a local anti-ghost equation (at least in the Landau gauge) also appear: background field (BF) gauges
Abbott 1981
;
Abbott 1982
.

In these gauges, one starts by splitting the gauge field into a background part,
\widehat{A}
, and a quantum part,
Q
, according to

\displaystyle A^{a}_{\mu}=\widehat{A}^{a}_{\mu}+Q_{\mu}^{a}.

(141)

Each term will then encode different gauge transformations:

•

The quantum field
Q
represents the variable in the path integral, so that we can define a gauge transformation that acts on
Q
(and leaves the path integral measure invariant) keeping
\widehat{A}
fixed

\displaystyle\delta_{\theta}\widehat{A}^{a}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=0;

\displaystyle\delta_{\theta}Q^{a}_{\mu}

\displaystyle={\cal D}_{\mu}^{ab}\theta^{b}.

(142)

•

The background field
\widehat{A}
represents instead a classical source, so that gauge invariance with respect to the field
A
can be thought of as a simultaneoeus transformation of both
\widehat{A}
and
Q
, according to

\displaystyle\delta_{\omega}\widehat{A}^{a}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}\omega^{b};

\displaystyle\delta_{\omega}Q^{a}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=gf^{abc}Q^{b}_{\mu}\omega^{c},

(143)

which implies the usual gauge transformation on the total field
A
:
\delta_{\omega}A^{a}_{\mu}={\cal D}_{\mu}^{ab}\omega^{b}
. Evidently, under a background gauge transformation the quantum part of the gauge field transforms as would a matter field in the adjoint representation.

Classically, ignoring gauge fixing, the path integral would be invariant under both kinds of transformations. In practice, however, we know that we need to fix the gauge; but we can do that only by breaking the quantum gauge invariance, Eq. (
142
), leaving the background gauge invariance of the action in Eq. (
143
) intact.

This can be achieved by choosing the gauge fixing function

\displaystyle\widehat{\cal F}^{a}=\widehat{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}Q^{\mu}_{b},

(144)

so that, under the background gauge transformation of Eq. (
143
), one finds the adjoint transformation

\displaystyle\delta_{\omega}\widehat{\cal F}^{a}=gf^{abc}\widehat{\cal F}^{b}\omega^{c}.

(145)

To explicitly check Eq. (
145
), observe that

\displaystyle\delta_{\omega}\widehat{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=gf^{abc}\widehat{\cal D}^{cd}_{\mu}\omega^{d};

\displaystyle\delta_{\omega}Q^{b}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=gf^{bde}Q^{d}_{\mu}\omega^{e},

(146)

which implies

\displaystyle\delta_{\omega}\widehat{F}^{a}

\displaystyle=gf^{abc}(\partial^{\mu}Q_{\mu}^{b})\omega^{c}+g^{2}(f^{abe}f^{bdc}+f^{bec}f^{adb})\widehat{A}^{d}_{\mu}\omega^{c}Q^{e}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=gf^{abc}(\partial^{\mu}Q_{\mu}^{b}+gf^{bde}\widehat{A}^{d}_{\mu}Q^{e}_{\mu})\omega^{c}

\displaystyle=gf^{abc}\widehat{\cal F}^{b}\omega^{c}.

(147)

Then, the gauge fixing action corresponding to Eq. (
144
) is invariant under background gauge transformations, since

\displaystyle\delta_{\omega}(\widehat{{\cal F}}^{a}\widehat{{\cal F}}^{a})\sim gf^{abc}\widehat{{\cal F}}^{a}\widehat{{\cal F}}^{b}\omega^{c}=0.

(148)

The ghost sector associated with the breaking of the quantum gauge invariance can be obtained by introducing the gauge-fixing fermion

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\overline{c}^{a}\left(\widehat{\cal F}^{a}-\frac{\xi}{2}b^{a}\right);

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{\mathrm{GF}}+\widehat{S}_{\mathrm{FP}}

\displaystyle=s\widehat{\Psi}.

(149)

with the additional BRST transformations

\displaystyle s\widehat{A}^{a}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=A^{\#a}_{\mu};

\displaystyle sA^{\#a}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=0;

\displaystyle sQ^{a}_{\mu}

\displaystyle={\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}c^{b}-A^{\#a}_{\mu},

(150)

where
\widehat{A}
is an anticommuting source which implements the equation of motion of the background part of the gauge field at the quantum level. As
\widehat{A}
and
A^{\#}
are introduced as a BRST-doublet/trivial pair, the physical observables of the theory are not affected. Background gauge invariance is then preserved if

\displaystyle\delta_{\omega}\varphi

\displaystyle=gf^{abc}\varphi^{b}\omega^{c};

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle=\{c,\overline{c},b,A^{\#}\}.

(151)

Indeed, one has (omitting the space-time integral)

\displaystyle\delta_{\omega}\widehat{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\omega}\overline{c}^{a}\left({\cal F}^{a}-\frac{\xi}{2}b^{a}\right)+\overline{c}^{a}\delta_{\omega}{\cal F}^{a}-\frac{\xi}{2}\overline{c}^{a}\delta_{\omega}b^{a}

\displaystyle=gf^{abc}\overline{c}^{b}\omega^{c}{\cal F}^{a}-g\frac{\xi}{2}gf^{abc}\overline{c}^{b}\omega^{c}b^{a}+\overline{c}^{a}gf^{abc}\overline{c}^{a}{\cal F}^{b}\omega^{c}-\frac{\xi}{2}\overline{c}^{a}gf^{abc}b^{b}\omega^{c}=0,

(152)

which implies
\delta_{\omega}s\widehat{\Psi}=s\delta_{\omega}\widehat{\Psi}=0
.

Thus we have that the action

\displaystyle\widehat{S}=S_{\mathrm{YM}}+\widehat{S}_{\mathrm{GF}}+\widehat{S}_{\mathrm{FP}},

(153)

is both BRST and background gauge invariant.

In addition to the usual Feynman rules involving quantum fields (which are obtained from the previously introduced ones through the replacement
A\to Q
),

\widehat{S}
gives rise to the following Feynman rules involving background fields:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Gamma^{(0)}_{\widehat{A}^{a}_{\mu}A^{b}_{\nu}A^{c}_{\rho}}(q,k_{1})&=gf^{abc}\widehat{\Gamma}^{(0)}_{\mu\nu\rho}(q,k_{1})\\
&=gf^{abc}[g_{\nu\rho}(k_{1}-k_{2})_{\mu}+g_{\mu\rho}(k_{2}-q+\frac{k_{1}}{\xi})_{\nu}\\
&\mathrel{\phantom{=}}+g_{\mu\nu}(q-k_{1}-\frac{k_{2}}{\xi})_{\rho}],\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Gamma^{(0)}_{\widehat{A}^{a}_{\mu}A^{b}_{\nu}\widehat{A}^{c}_{\rho}A^{d}_{\sigma}}(q,k_{1},k_{2})&=-ig^{2}[f^{ade}f^{ecb}(g_{\mu\rho}g_{\nu\sigma}-g_{\mu\nu}g_{\rho\sigma}+\frac{1}{\xi}g_{\mu\sigma}g_{\nu\rho})\\
&\mathrel{\phantom{=}}+f^{abe}f^{edc}(g_{\mu\sigma}g_{\nu\rho}-g_{\mu\rho}g_{\nu\sigma}-\frac{1}{\xi}g_{\mu\nu}g_{\rho\sigma})\\
&\mathrel{\phantom{=}}+f^{ace}f^{edb}(g_{\mu\sigma}g_{\nu\rho}-g_{\mu\nu}g_{\rho\sigma})],\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Gamma^{(0)}_{c^{c}\widehat{A}^{a}_{\mu}\overline{c}^{b}}(k_{2},q)&=gf^{abc}\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mu}(q,k_{2})\\
&=gf^{abc}(k_{1}+k_{2})_{\mu},\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Gamma^{(0)}_{c^{d}\widehat{A}^{a}_{\mu}A^{b}_{\nu}\overline{c}^{c}}(k_{2},q,k_{3})&=ig^{2}g_{\mu\nu}f^{ace}f^{ebd},\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Gamma^{(0)}_{c^{d}\widehat{A}^{a}_{\mu}\widehat{A}^{b}_{\nu}\overline{c}^{c}}(k_{2},q,k_{3})&=ig^{2}g_{\alpha\beta}(f^{ace}f^{ebd}+f^{bce}f^{ead}).\end{aligned}

Notice that there is a final vertex

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\widehat{A}^{a}_{\mu}Q^{b}_{\nu}Q^{c}_{\rho}Q^{d}_{\sigma}}(q,k_{1},k_{2})

\displaystyle=-ig^{2}\widehat{\Gamma}^{abcd}_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}(q,k_{1},k_{2});

(154)

at tree level, however, one has the identity

\Gamma^{(0)}_{\widehat{A}^{a}_{\mu}Q^{b}_{\nu}Q^{c}_{\rho}Q^{d}_{\sigma}}\equiv\Gamma^{(0)}_{Q^{a}_{\mu}Q^{b}_{\nu}Q^{c}_{\rho}Q^{d}_{\sigma}}
.

The seagull term vanishes as in the calculation of the conventional self-energy owing to the dimensional regularization result of Eq. (

\displaystyle\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{(1)\mathrm{gl}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}g^{2}C_{A}\int_{k}\!\frac{\widehat{\Gamma}^{(0)}_{\mu\rho\sigma}(q,k)\widehat{\Gamma}^{\rho\sigma(0)}_{\nu}(-q,k+q)}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}},

(155)

with

\displaystyle\widehat{\Gamma}^{(0)}_{\mu\rho\sigma}(q,k)\widehat{\Gamma}^{(0)\rho\sigma}_{\nu}(-q,k+q)

(156)

Notice that in the equations above the number of ‘hats’ on the corresponding Green function indicates the number of background fields we are considering.

The ghost contributions are instead given by the diagrams

Again the seagull term vanishes in dimensional regularization, whereas the first term reads

\displaystyle\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{(1)\mathrm{gh}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)

\displaystyle=-g^{2}C_{A}\int_{k}\!\frac{\widehat{\Gamma}^{(0)}_{\mu}(q,k-q)\widehat{\Gamma}^{(0)}_{\nu}(-q,k)}{k^{2}(k-q)^{2}}

(157)

where in the last step we have made the replacement

It is then immediate to realize that the transversality of the background gluon 2-point function is realized in a “block-wise” fashion
Aguilar and Papavassiliou 2006
;
Binosi and Papavassiliou 2009
,
i.e.
, independently for each gluon and ghost contributions,

\displaystyle q^{\nu}\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{(1)\mathrm{gl}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)

\displaystyle=0;

\displaystyle q^{\nu}\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{(1)\mathrm{gh}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)

\displaystyle=0,

(158)

as opposed to the “cross-talk” realization happening in the conventional case (where the cancellation happens because the gluon and ghost terms cancel each other). We will return to this important concept (and its generalization) later on.

To check Eq. (
158

156
) implies

\displaystyle\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{(1)\mathrm{gl}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)

\displaystyle\sim\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{(1)\mathrm{gh}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)+\mathrm{transverse\ terms}.

(159)

Then one has

\displaystyle q^{\nu}\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{(1)\mathrm{gh}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)\propto\int_{k}\!\frac{(2k+q)_{\mu}(q^{2}+2k{\cdot}q)}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}=\int_{k}\!\frac{(2k+q)_{\mu}[(k+q)^{2}-k^{2})}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}=2q_{\mu}\int\!\frac{1}{k^{2}}=0.

(160)

Since
\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}^{(1)\mathrm{gh}}
is transverse we then have

\displaystyle\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{(1)\mathrm{gh}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)

\displaystyle=P_{\mu\nu}(q)\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{(1)\mathrm{gh}}(q^{2};\mu^{2}),

(161)

with

\displaystyle\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{(1)\mathrm{gh}}(q^{2};\mu^{2})=-g^{2}C_{A}\frac{1}{d-1}\int_{k}\!\frac{(2k+q)^{2}}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}=g^{2}C_{A}\frac{q^{2}}{d-1}\int_{k}\!\frac{1}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}}.

(162)

Thus, also using Eq. (
156

\displaystyle\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{(1)\mathrm{gl}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{g^{2}C_{A}}{2}\left(\frac{7d-8}{d-1}\right)q^{2}P_{\mu\nu}(q)\int_{k}\!\frac{1}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}};

(163a)

\displaystyle\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{(1)\mathrm{gh}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{g^{2}C_{A}}{d-1}q^{2}P_{\mu\nu}(q)\int_{k}\!\frac{1}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}},

(163b)

which yield the final results

\displaystyle\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{(1)\mathrm{gl}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{10}{3}g^{2}C_{A}q^{2}P_{\mu\nu}(q)f_{\epsilon}(q^{2},\mu^{2});

\displaystyle\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{(1)\mathrm{gh}}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}g^{2}C_{A}q^{2}P_{\mu\nu}(q)f_{\epsilon}(q^{2},\mu^{2}).

(164)

Notice also that the conventional and background gluon one-loop self-energies are related through the equation

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mu\nu}^{(1)}(q;\mu)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)-2g^{2}C_{A}q^{2}P_{\mu\nu}(q)\int_{k}\frac{1}{k^{2}(k+q)^{2}},

(165)

which represents the one-loop expansion of a set of identities that will be presented later on and go under the name of background-quantum (BQ) identities
Binosi and Papavassiliou 2002
;
Grassi et al. 2001
. Using then the results Eqs. (
23
) and (
164
), we obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}(q;\mu)

\displaystyle=P_{\mu\nu}(q)\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{(1)}(q^{2};\mu^{2});

\displaystyle\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{(1)}(q^{2};\mu^{2})

\displaystyle=i\alpha(\mu^{2})\frac{\beta_{0}}{4\pi}q^{2}\left(\frac{2}{\epsilon}-\gamma-\ln 4\pi-\ln\frac{q^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right),

(166)

where
\beta_{0}=(4\pi)^{2}b=\frac{11}{3}C_{A}
is the one-loop coefficient of the QCD
\beta
function in the absence of quarks loops. Notice that, while we have obtained the result reported in Eq. (
166
) in the Feynman gauge, the latter is completely general. Then, dropping the divergent part (that will renormalize the tree-level propagator), we obtain at one-loop order in perturbation theory

\displaystyle q^{2}\hskip 3.69899pt\widehat{\hskip-3.69899pt\widehat{d}}\mbox{\ }^{(1)}(q^{2})=\alpha(\mu^{2})q^{2}\widehat{\widehat{\Delta}}\mbox{}^{(1)}(q^{2};\mu^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(\mu^{2})}{1+\frac{i}{q^{2}}\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{(1)}(q^{2};\mu^{2})}=\frac{\alpha(\mu^{2})}{1+\alpha(\mu^{2})\frac{\beta_{0}}{4\pi}\ln\frac{q^{2}}{\mu^{2}}}.

(167)

The denominator vanishes at the scale defined by

\alpha(\mu^{2})\frac{\beta_{0}}{4\pi}\ln\frac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}^{2}}{\mu^{2}}=-1
,
which introduces the RGI scale
\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}
. Expressing the result in terms of this scale, one obtains

\displaystyle q^{2}\hskip 3.69899pt\widehat{\hskip-3.69899pt\widehat{d}}\mbox{\ }^{(1)}(q^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\frac{\beta_{0}}{4\pi}\ln\frac{q^{2}}{\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}^{2}}}.

(168)

Thus, at least at one-loop, the
q^{2}\hskip 3.69899pt\widehat{\hskip-3.69899pt\widehat{d}}
combination involving the background propagator, is RGI and coincides with the theory’s effective charge, exactly as it happens in the case of quantum electro-dynamics, where the photon propagator at one-loop provides
\beta_{0}=-4/3
. As we will soon see, this is a completely general result solely owing to the background gauge invariance of the action and the corresponding WI this residual invariance generates.

To be sure this does not happen for the conventional (quantum) propagator, for which the expansion of Eq. (
33
) yields

\displaystyle\Pi^{(1)}(q^{2};\mu^{2})

\displaystyle=i\alpha\frac{5C_{A}}{12\pi}q^{2}\left(\frac{2}{\epsilon}-\gamma-\ln 4\pi-\ln\frac{q^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right).

(169)

In this case, however, RGI quantities are dictated by the vertex structure of the action and its BRST invariance, which leads to the relations

\displaystyle Z_{Ac^{2}}

\displaystyle=Z_{g}Z_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}Z_{c};

\displaystyle Z_{A^{3}}

\displaystyle=Z_{g}Z_{A}^{\frac{3}{2}};

\displaystyle Z_{A^{4}}

\displaystyle=Z^{2}_{g}Z_{A}^{2};

(170)

where
Z_{Ac^{2}}
,
Z_{A^{3}}
and
Z_{A^{4}}
are the renormalization constants of the ghost-gluon, three-gluon and four-gluon vertices respectively and

\displaystyle A_{0}

\displaystyle=Z_{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}A;

\displaystyle c_{0}

\displaystyle=Z_{c}c;

\displaystyle g_{0}

\displaystyle=Z_{g}g,

(171)

with the ‘0’ subscript indicating bare quantities. Standard calculations give (again in the Feynman gauge, for consistency)

\displaystyle F^{(1)}(q^{2};\mu^{2})

\displaystyle=i\alpha\frac{C_{A}}{8\pi}q^{2}\left(\frac{2}{\epsilon}-\gamma-\ln 4\pi-\ln\frac{q^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right)=\Gamma^{(1)}_{Ac^{2}}(q^{2};\mu^{2}),

(172)

where
\Gamma_{Ac^{2}}
is the ghost-gluon vertex dressing function. Then, looking this time at the (momentum-independent) renormalization constants rather than to the (momentum-dependent) finite pieces of the dressing functions, we get the one-loop result

\displaystyle Z_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=Z^{2}_{g}=Z^{2}_{Ac^{2}}Z_{A}^{-1}Z_{c}^{-2}=1-\alpha\frac{\beta_{0}}{4\pi}\frac{2}{\epsilon},

(173)

which, through the (one-loop) beta function

gives rise to the effective coupling

\displaystyle\alpha(q^{2})=\frac{1}{\frac{\beta_{0}}{4\pi}\ln\frac{q^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}_{\mathrm{QCD}}}},

(174)

as previously found in Eq. (

).

Similarly to what was done in the covariant gauges case, from the action of Eq. (
153
) we can construct the complete action according to

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{\mathrm{C}}=\widehat{S}+S_{\mathrm{BV}},

(175)

with the antifields transforming in the adjoint representation with respect to the background gauge transformations, so that
\widehat{S}_{\mathrm{C}}
is background gauge invariant too:

\displaystyle\delta_{\omega}\Phi^{*a}=gf^{abc}\Phi^{*b}\omega^{c}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\delta_{\omega}\widehat{S}_{\mathrm{C}}=0.

(176)

Given the additional BRST transformations Eq. (
150
), the effective background action
\widehat{\Gamma}
satisfies the STI

\displaystyle\widehat{\cal S}(\widehat{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=0;

\displaystyle\widehat{\cal S}(\widehat{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\left[\frac{\delta\widehat{\Gamma}}{\delta A^{*a}_{\mu}}\frac{\delta\widehat{\Gamma}}{\delta Q^{a\mu}}+\frac{\delta\widehat{\Gamma}}{\delta c^{*a}}\frac{\delta\widehat{\Gamma}}{\delta c^{a}}+b^{a}\frac{\delta\widehat{\Gamma}}{\delta\overline{c}^{a}}+A^{\#a}_{\mu}\left(\frac{\delta\widehat{\Gamma}}{\delta\widehat{A}^{a\mu}}-\frac{\delta\widehat{\Gamma}}{\delta Q^{a\mu}}\right)\right],

(177)

whereas the residual background gauge invariance gives rise to the WI

\displaystyle\widehat{\cal W}^{a}\widehat{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=0;

\displaystyle\widehat{\cal W}^{a}

\displaystyle=-{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}\frac{\delta}{\delta Q^{b}_{\mu}}-\widehat{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}\frac{\delta}{\delta\widehat{A}^{b}_{\mu}}+gf^{abc}\sum\phi^{c}\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi^{b}},

(178)

with

.

4.1
BF gauges and anti-BRST

The reader might have noticed that the background field,
\widehat{A}
, and the associated source,
A^{\#}
, have been denoted with exactly the same letters as, respectively, the gluon field BRST/anti-BRST and anti-BRST sources (see the discussion below Eq. (
98
)). To understand why we did that, observe that the BF gauge fixing function of Eq. (
144
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\widehat{\cal F}^{a}

\displaystyle={\cal F}^{a}-{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}\widehat{A}^{b\mu}.

(179)

Then we find
Binosi and Quadri 2013

\displaystyle s\widehat{\Psi}

\displaystyle=s\left(\overline{c}^{a}{\cal F}^{a}-\frac{\xi}{2}\overline{c}^{a}b^{a}\right)-s\left(\overline{c}^{a}{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}\widehat{A}^{b\mu}\right)=S_{\mathrm{GF}}+S_{\mathrm{FP}}+\widehat{A}^{a}_{\mu}s\overline{s}A^{a}_{\mu}+A^{\#a}_{\mu}\overline{s}A^{a\mu}.

(180)

To prove Eq. (
180
) we start by observing that, neglecting surface terms, one has

\displaystyle\Phi^{a}{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}\Phi^{\prime b}

\displaystyle=-(\partial_{\mu}\Phi^{a})\Phi^{\prime b}+gf^{abc}A^{c}_{\mu}\Phi^{b}\Phi^{\prime a}=({\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}\Phi^{a})\Phi^{\prime b},

(181)

which immediately provides the results

\displaystyle b^{a}{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}\widehat{A}^{b\mu}

\displaystyle=-\widehat{A}^{a\mu}{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}b^{b};

\displaystyle\overline{c}^{a}{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}A^{\#b\mu}

\displaystyle=A^{\#a\mu}{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}\overline{c}^{b},

(182)

where we have duly taken into account the statistics of the fields. Then, using the identities above, we find

\displaystyle-s(\overline{c}^{a}{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}\widehat{A}^{b\mu})

\displaystyle=-b^{a}{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}\widehat{A}^{b\mu}+\overline{c}^{a}[s{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}]\widehat{A}^{b\mu}+\overline{c}^{a}{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}A^{\#b\mu}

\displaystyle=\widehat{A}^{a\mu}{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}b^{b}+gf^{abc}\overline{c}^{b}{\cal D}^{cd}c^{d}+A^{\#a\mu}{\cal D}^{ab}_{\mu}\overline{c}^{b}=\widehat{A}^{a}_{\mu}s\overline{s}A^{a}_{\mu}+A^{\#a}_{\mu}\overline{s}A^{a\mu}.

(183)

This is a somewhat unexpected result as we see BF gauges emerge from the requirements of the simultaneous BRST and anti-BRST invariance of the Yang-Mills action:

\displaystyle\underbrace{S_{\mathrm{YM}}+s\Psi+S^{\prime}_{\mathrm{BV}}}_{\text{BRST/anti-BRST\ invariant}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{S_{\mathrm{YM}}+s\widehat{\Psi}+S_{\mathrm{BV}}}_{\text{BF\ gauge-fixed}}+\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\bigg(\underbrace{c^{\#a}\overline{s}c^{a}+b^{\#a}\overline{s}b^{a}+\overline{c}^{\#a}\overline{s}\overline{c}^{a}+\widehat{c}^{a}s\overline{s}c^{a}}_{\text{leftovers}}\bigg).

(184)

The background field
\widehat{A}
is therefore identified with the source of the BRST/anti-BRST variation of the gluon field, whereas its doublet partner
A^{\#}
is identified with the source associated with the anti-BRST variation of the gluon field. This explains why the STI in Eq. (
177
) is identical to the one in Eq. (
104
) when the background-quantum splitting is “undone”, and the WI Eq. (
178
) perfectly matches the one found in Eq. (
113
).

Since in the following we will not be concerned with the sectors of the theory where
c^{\#}
,
b^{\#}
,
\overline{c}^{\#}
or
\widehat{c}
play some role, these terms can be put to zero and one is left with the identity
Binosi and Quadri 2013

\displaystyle\text{BRST/anti-BRST\ invariance}\equiv\text{BF\ gauges}.

(185)

Not only does this equation give a meaning to the somewhat mysterious invariance of the action under the anti-BRST symmetry
Weinberg 1996
; it will also show that rendering the Yang-Mills action simultaneously invariant under both BRST and anti-BRST symmetry provides new and better ways to understand its IR dynamics, as we have already seen in the case of the ghost sector.

4.2
Background-quantum and Ward identities

Green functions involving background fields are related to the corresponding ones where one or more of those fields are replaced by quantum fields through a set of identities that go under the name of background-quantum identities (BQIs)
Binosi and Papavassiliou 2002
;
Grassi et al. 2001
. The most important of these relations are those satisfied by the gluon two-point function. To get them, we take the functional differentiation of the STI Eq. (
177
), with respect to one background source and one background field (which, incidentally, is also a
source
) or quantum field. Then, omitting the hat on both the STI and the effective action, owing to Eq. (
185
), we find the BQIs

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\delta^{2}}{\delta A^{\#}\delta\widehat{A}}{\cal S}(\Gamma)\right|_{\Phi,\Phi^{\#},\Phi^{*}=0}

\displaystyle=0;

\displaystyle i\Gamma_{\widehat{A}^{a}_{\mu}\widehat{A}^{b}_{\nu}}(q)

\displaystyle=[ig^{\rho}_{\mu}\delta^{ac}+\Gamma_{A^{\#a}_{\mu}A^{*c}_{\rho}}(q)]\Gamma_{Q^{c\rho}\widehat{A}^{b}_{\nu}}(q).

(186a)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\delta^{2}}{\delta A^{\#}\delta Q}{\cal S}(\Gamma)\right|_{\Phi,\Phi^{\#},\Phi^{*}=0}

\displaystyle=0;

\displaystyle i\Gamma_{\widehat{A}^{a}_{\mu}Q^{b}_{\nu}}(q)

\displaystyle=[ig^{\rho}_{\mu}\delta^{ac}+\Gamma_{A^{\#a}_{\mu}A^{*c}_{\rho}}(q)]\Gamma_{Q^{c\rho}Q^{b}_{\nu}}(q).

(186b)

Using the transversality of the gluon two-point functions, we see that the
L
term in the decomposition of Eq. (
120
) of the function
\Gamma_{A^{\#}A^{*}}
drops out and the BQIs above simplify to

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\widehat{A}_{\mu}\widehat{A}_{\nu}}(q)

\displaystyle=[1+G(q^{2})]\Gamma_{Q_{\mu}\widehat{A}_{\nu}}(q);

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\widehat{A}_{\mu}Q_{\nu}}(q)

\displaystyle=[1+G(q^{2})]\Gamma_{Q_{\mu}Q_{\nu}}(q).

(187)

The two identities above can be then combined in a unique relation:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\widehat{A}_{\mu}\widehat{A}_{\nu}}(q)=[1+G(q^{2})]^{2}\Gamma_{Q_{\mu}Q_{\nu}}(q),

(188)

which shows that the same function governing the dynamics of the ghost sector determines also how the gluon 2-point sector behaves. Notice that using Eq. (
94
) we can finally relate the background propagator with the conventional one:

\displaystyle\Delta^{-1}(q^{2})=\frac{\widehat{\Delta}\mbox{}^{-1}(q^{2})}{1+G(q^{2})}=\frac{\widehat{\widehat{\Delta}}\mbox{}^{-1}(q^{2})}{[1+G(q^{2})]^{2}}.

(189)

The importance of this equation will be soon clear.

Expanding Eq. (
188
) at one-loop and observing that

The combination of the BQI Eq. (
189

Recall the one-loop case: block-wise transversality implies that the gluon and ghost contributions to the gluon self-energy are individually transverse. Consequently, omitting one of them still yields a transverse tensor structure, albeit with an incorrect prefactor. This should be contrasted with the conventional formulation, where neglecting the ghost contribution leads to an unphysical, non transverse result. The same is true for the full DSE; for example we can consider retaining only the diagrams belonging to the 1-loop dressed gluon class, thus obtaining the (truncated) DSE

\displaystyle\widehat{\widehat{\Delta}}\mbox{}^{-1}(q^{2})\,P_{\mu\nu}(q)=q^{2}P_{\mu\nu}(q)+i\widehat{\widehat{\Pi}}\mbox{}^{1\ell;\mathrm{gl}}_{\mu\nu}(q).

The only problem with this line of reasoning, is that these DSEs do not constitute a genuine dynamical equation, since background fields act as sources and therefore cannot propagate inside loops. As a consequence, the propagator appearing on the left-hand side of Eq. (

\displaystyle\widehat{\widehat{\Delta}}\mbox{}^{-1}(q^{2})=q^{2}+\int_{k}\Delta(k)\,\Delta(k+q)\cdots+\int_{k}\Delta(k)\cdots,

Thus, the block-wise realization of WIs coupled to the propagator BQI, provides a systematic, gauge-invariant truncation scheme for the propagator’s DSE that preserves the transversality of the result. As a consequence, the number of coupled DSEs that must be retained in order to maintain gauge (or BRST) symmetry can drastically be reduced.

6
Dynamical gluon mass generation

6.1
Masslessness of the gluon propagator

The combined lattice evidence therefore supports a coherent IR picture characterized by a finite gluon propagator, a finite ghost dressing function, and a Kugo–Ojima function that falls short of the confinement condition
G(0)=-1
. This picture is robust under changes of dimension, lattice spacing, and inclusion of dynamical quarks, and extends smoothly from Landau gauge to small values of the gauge parameter in linear covariant gauges.

Taken at face value, these results are telling us that IR interactions generate dynamically a mass for the gluon. But how can this practically happen? We know that gauge symmetry forbids the addition of an explicit tree-level mass term
mA^{a\mu}A_{\mu}^{a}
in the Yang-Mills action Eq. (

Let us concentrate on the one loop dressed diagrams (recall that owing to the block-wise realization of the WI we can do this gauge invariantly). Then, for the gluon diagrams one has:

\displaystyle d\widehat{\Pi}^{i;j}(0)=g^{\mu\nu}\widehat{\Pi}^{i;j}_{\mu\nu}(0).

This inequivalence is illustrated by the integral

\displaystyle I_{\mu\nu}(q)=g_{\mu\nu}A(q^{2})+\frac{q_{\mu}q_{\nu}}{q^{2}}B(q^{2}),

\displaystyle B(0)=0.

6.2
Dynamical gluon mass generation

In order to obtain an IR-finite gluon propagator in a self-consistent manner, one must therefore allow for the appearance of massless pole terms in the relevant vertices. In particular, such poles must occur in the kinematic channel associated with the momentum
q
flowing into the gluon DSE, since this is the momentum carried by the vertices inserted in the corresponding diagrams. According to the conventions adopted so far, this requirement implies that (some of) the vertices
\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mu\nu\rho}
,
\widehat{\Gamma}_{\mu}
, and
\widehat{\Gamma}^{abcd}_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
must contain pole contributions of the form
q_{\mu}/q^{2}
.

Let us see why this is the case. According to Goldstone’s theorem
Nambu 1960
;
Goldstone 1961
;
Goldstone et al. 1962
, the spontaneous breaking of a continuous global symmetry is accompanied by the appearance of massless excitations, known as Goldstone particles. Although Goldstone’s theorem is most commonly realized through a scalar field acquiring a nonvanishing vacuum expectation value, Nambu and Jona-Lasinio
Nambu and Jona-Lasinio 1961a
;
Nambu and Jona-Lasinio 1961b
introduced the notion of a
dynamical
Goldstone boson, demonstrating that the Goldstone mechanism may operate even in the absence of fundamental scalar fields in the Lagrangian. In this case, when chiral symmetry is broken, the associated Goldstone boson (the pion) is not an elementary excitation but rather a quark–antiquark bound state
Maris et al. 1998
.

Goldstone’s theorem, however, does not apply when the symmetry that is spontaneously broken is local rather than global, and the theory contains a massless gauge boson mediating the interaction (equivalently, when gauge-invariant long-range forces are present). In this situation the Goldstone phenomenon is replaced by the Higgs mechanism: no massless excitations appear in the physical spectrum, because the would-be Goldstone bosons combine with the gauge field to provide the longitudinal degree of freedom of a massive spin-one particle
Higgs 1964a
;
Higgs 1964b
;
Englert and Brout 1964
;
Guralnik et al. 1964
;
Higgs 1966
. In addition, a massive scalar particle, the Higgs boson, emerges and plays a crucial role in ensuring the renormalizability of the theory.

Both mechanisms may coexist: if a local and a global symmetry are simultaneously broken, massive gauge bosons may appear together with massless Goldstone modes. The converse situation may also occur: the breaking of a global symmetry may generate Goldstone bosons which, in the absence of elementary scalar fields, are absorbed by gauge bosons, thereby rendering them massive.

Before turning to the specific case of non-Abelian gauge theories, it is useful to recall a different mechanism capable of generating masses for gauge bosons without introducing explicit mass terms in the Lagrangian.

Independently of these considerations, and prior to the formulation of the Higgs mechanism, Schwinger argued that gauge invariance of a vector field does not necessarily imply a vanishing mass for the associated particle, provided that the vector current coupling is sufficiently strong
Schwinger 1962a
. Schwinger’s key observation was that if the vacuum polarization tensor
\Pi_{\mu\nu}(q)
develops a pole at zero momentum transfer, then the vector boson becomes massive, even though a mass term is forbidden at the level of the fundamental Lagrangian by gauge symmetry
Schwinger 1962b
. Indeed, since

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mu\nu}(q)=-iP_{\mu\nu}(q)\frac{1}{q^{2}\left[1+i\Pi(q^{2})\right]},

(263)

it is evident that if
\Pi(q^{2})
possesses a pole at
q^{2}=0
with positive residue
m^{2}
, the vector boson acquires a mass
m
, even though it is massless in the absence of interactions (
g=0
,
\Pi=0
).

There is, in fact, no fundamental principle forbidding
\Pi(q^{2})
from developing such a pole. Since bound states are expected to occur in many physical systems and to generate poles in
\Pi_{\mu\nu}
at timelike momenta, one may envisage that sufficiently strong binding could drive the mass of a bound state to zero, thereby inducing a mass for the vector boson without violating gauge invariance.

A particularly clear realization of this mechanism occurs in two-dimensional massless QED, the so-called Schwinger model. In this theory the special properties of the Dirac algebra in
d=2
allow an exact solution. The vacuum polarization tensor
\Pi_{\mu\nu}
indeed develops a pole at
q^{2}=0
, and the photon acquires a mass
m^{2}=e^{2}/\pi
(in two dimensions the coupling
e
has dimensions of mass). The appearance of this pole is related to the axial anomaly: the anomalous divergence of the axial current prevents the appearance of a massless Goldstone boson and instead generates a mass for the gauge field. In this way Goldstone’s theorem is evaded, consistently with Coleman’s theorem
Coleman 1973
, which excludes Goldstone bosons in
d=2
.

From a broader perspective, the Higgs mechanism may be regarded as a particular realization of the Schwinger mechanism. In the Higgs case the pole required by the Schwinger mechanism is generated through the vacuum expectation value
v
of a scalar field, which produces tadpole contributions to
\Pi(q^{2})
. The residue of the pole is then saturated by
v^{2}
, yielding a gauge-boson mass
m^{2}=2g^{2}v^{2}
, while the would-be Goldstone bosons decouple from the physical spectrum.

We now return to the case of non-Abelian gauge theories, where the Schwinger mechanism may operate dynamically without the presence of elementary scalar fields.

In realistic field theories the crucial challenge in implementing the Schwinger mechanism is to demonstrate the dynamical generation of the required pole. While the Higgs mechanism ensures this at the classical or semiclassical level, its realization in the absence of fundamental scalar fields is subtler but also more conceptually appealing, as it avoids postulating elementary scalars not observed in Nature.

Motivated by this idea, considerable effort in the 1970s was devoted to combining the dynamical Goldstone mechanism,
i.e.
, the Nambu–Jona-Lasinio mechanism with composite Goldstone bosons, with gauge theories in order to generate gauge-invariant masses for vector bosons. In this two-step scenario, the spontaneous breaking of a global (typically chiral) symmetry produces composite Goldstone bosons, which supply the pole in
\Pi_{\mu\nu}
, while gauge invariance ensures that these massless excitations decouple from physical scattering amplitudes. The explicit realization of this decoupling proceeds through a subtle mechanism, illustrated in detail in a number of models
Jackiw and Johnson 1973
;
Cornwall and Norton 1973
.

Although much of this activity was originally motivated by alternatives to the Higgs mechanism in the electroweak sector and in grand unified model building, these ideas have had a lasting impact on our understanding of strong interactions. When applied to pure Yang–Mills theories, such as quarkless QCD, the underlying idea is the following
Eichten and Feinberg 1974
. In a strongly coupled non-Abelian gauge theory, longitudinally coupled, massless bound-state excitations may be dynamically generated. Demonstrating the existence of such bound states, particularly massless ones, is a highly nontrivial dynamical problem. In practice this question is typically investigated using Bethe–Salpeter equations and related continuum or lattice approaches
Poggio et al. 1975
. Strong coupling, arising from IR dynamics, is an essential ingredient in this scenario.

These excitations resemble Nambu–Goldstone bosons in that they are massless, composite, and longitudinally coupled; however, they differ crucially in origin, as they do not arise from the spontaneous breaking of any global symmetry. Their primary role is to activate the Schwinger mechanism by providing the necessary pole in the gluon self-energy, thereby generating a gauge-invariant dynamical mass for the gluons. A further essential step is to show that these Goldstone-like excitations are removed from the
S
-matrix, either through cancellations among massless poles or as a consequence of current conservation.

At the level of Green functions and the corresponding DSEs, such composite excitations manifest themselves as poles in off-shell Green functions associated with fields absent from the classical action. These poles arise dynamically as bound-state solutions of the DSEs and encode the nonperturbative physics underlying the Schwinger mechanism.

Instead of Conclusions

The reader that has made it this far, should now be able to engage in a meaningful discussion on the dynamical generation of a gluon mass, while avoiding the common misconceptions and pitfalls that often arise when addressing such a subtle and technically delicate subject.

The dynamical generation of a gluon mass is a cornerstone of the broader phenomenon known as emergent hadron mass (EHM)
Roberts 2020
. EHM describes how the vast majority of the mass in the visible Universe was generated approximately
10^{-6}

Although the QCD Lagrangian is written in terms of massless gluon and (in the chiral limit) quark fields, strong interactions, as we have explicitly seen at least in the case of gluons, dress these elementary degrees of freedom into complex quasiparticles. These quasiparticles are characterized by dynamically generated, momentum-dependent mass functions that become large in the IR, reaching values of roughly one-half (for gluons) and one-third (for quarks) of the proton mass,
\sim 1\,\mathrm{GeV}

Roberts et al. 2021
;

Subsequently, these massive quasiparticles bind to form hadrons, whose properties and observable characteristics carry the imprint of emergent hadron mass. In this way, mass generation in QCD is not the consequence of explicit mass terms in the fundamental Lagrangian, but rather the outcome of nonperturbative dynamics operating at IR scales.

We have only begun to uncover the full implications of EHM. It is our hope that some of you will now feel prepared to contribute to this ongoing effort, especially in view of the intense experimental program being pursued worldwide in hadron physics with the operation, construction and planning of facilities like the Jefferson Lab running at 12 GeV, AMBER at CERN, and the electron ion colliders planned in the USA (EIC) and China (EicC).
