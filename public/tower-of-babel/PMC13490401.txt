Prioritising construction accident risk factors using an integrated Random Forest, XGBoost, and ANN framework with sensitivity analysis
Authors: Abdulaziz Alotaibi, John Gambatese, Hossam Wefki, Abdulaziz Alqurashi, Mohamed T Elnabwy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConstruction safety remains a critical concern due to accidents influenced by multiple interacting factors. While previous studies have identified key risk drivers, robust, data-driven approaches for prioritising them remain limited. This study proposes an integrated machine learning and sensitivity-based framework to systematically prioritise construction accident risk factors. A dataset of key safety-related variables was analysed using Random Forest Regression, Extreme Gradient Boosting, and Artificial Neural Networks. Model performance was evaluated using standard metrics, and sensitivity analysis quantified the relative importance of each risk factor. Within the available dataset, the Random Forest model achieved the highest observed test correlation coefficient of 0.813; however, statistical testing did not indicate significant differences between model prediction errors. Sensitivity analysis revealed that physical strain sub-factors, particularly mechanical and electrical strain, gravity-related tasks, and motion-related exertion, collectively account for 33.74% of the total influence on predicted accident risk. Cognitive demand factors, specifically perceived time pressure and workflow interruptions, represent the most influential secondary drivers. The top five sub-factors alone account for nearly half of the cumulative risk impact, providing a preliminary, data-driven basis for targeted safety interventions within the studied context, subject to further validation with larger, more geographically diverse datasets. The proposed framework offers a promising decision-support tool for construction stakeholders, enabling data-driven prioritisation of safety risks and supporting more informed resource allocation for risk mitigation, subject to further validation in real industrial environments.Keywords: Accident risk factor, Construction safety, Machine learning, Random forest, XGBoost, Artificial neural network, Cognitive factors, Physical strain, Environmental factors, Risk prioritisationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Risk factors

Introduction

The construction industry is widely recognized for its inherent risks, where workplace accidents remain a significant concern despite various safety interventions1. Accidents on construction sites pose significant risks to workers’ health and safety and lead to substantial economic and operational impacts on projects. As construction remains one of the most hazardous industries globally, understanding the multifaceted factors contributing to accident risk is essential for improving site safety and minimizing incidents2,3. Due to the dynamic and often hazardous nature of construction sites, numerous factors interact in ways that can lead to unanticipated safety incidents. This complexity requires a more structured approach to understanding and mitigating accident risks. In this context, modeling accident risk in construction becomes essential to systematically understand how diverse elements contribute to unsafe conditions and outcomes2. Various components, including cognitive, physical, task-related, and environmental factors, interact on construction sites to affect workers’ exposure to hazards1. Identifying and analyzing these factors provides critical insights for implementing effective risk management strategies, ultimately reducing accident rates and enhancing site safety4.

Various techniques have been implemented to address ongoing safety challenges arising from the complex nature of the construction industry5. These include personal protective equipment (PPE), comprehensive safety training programmes, and the strategic deployment of construction machinery to minimise manual labour and related risks. However, these measures fall short of addressing the fundamental causes of accidents, which frequently stem from workers’ diverse cognitive responses to the unpredictable and intricate construction environment6. While conventional statistical approaches such as Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM) have been widely applied in construction safety research to validate structural pathways and examine relationships among latent risk constructs7, they are fundamentally constrained by linear assumptions and predefined structural paths8,9. Consequently, these traditional parametric models can obscure localised, non-linear interactions and complex interdependencies among low-level risk sub-factors. Existing machine learning applications in safety research have primarily focused on binary accident classification or macro-level outcomes, without offering an integrated framework capable of modelling, ranking, and interpreting sub-factor interactions10,11. This methodological gap motivates the present study.

ML techniques offer a more powerful alternative for modelling accident risk, as they capture complex, non-linear relationships among variables without imposing rigid structural assumptions10,11. Ensemble algorithms can simultaneously process multiple interacting risk factors, quantify their relative contributions to predicted accident risk, and generalise effectively across diverse datasets10. Recent advances in quantitative and machine learning approaches, including ensemble algorithms, game-theoretic frameworks, and explainable AI techniques, have further demonstrated significant potential for predicting occupational safety outcomes, managing construction risks, and enhancing the transparency of data-driven safety decision-making12–14. To address the identified gap, the proposed integrated framework offers three key methodological advantages over conventional PLS-SEM and single-model ML approaches. First, it captures non-linear and interdependent relationships among risk factors without imposing structural constraints. Second, it combines three complementary ML algorithms to ensure robust and unbiased predictions. Third, it employs sensitivity analysis validated through MDI, permutation importance, and SHAP values to translate model outputs into actionable, ranked safety priorities.

The key factor domains investigated in this study, cognitive, physical, task-related, and environmental, are examined for their role in influencing predicted accident risk (PAR)5. Cognitive factors refer to the mental processes of construction workers, including perception, decision-making, and awareness, which directly affect their responses to dynamic site conditions15. Differences in cognitive responses often contribute to variability in risk perception and subsequent behaviour, impacting safety outcomes. Physical factors address the physiological and ergonomic elements involved in construction work, such as manual handling, physical stamina, and exposure to physical hazards16. These factors are often linked to fatigue and physical strain, which can significantly increase the likelihood of accidents. Task factors relate to the nature and demands of specific construction activities, including task complexity, repetitiveness, and precision requirements17. Certain tasks inherently pose higher risks due to the skill level required or the use of hazardous tools and equipment. Environmental factors encompass external conditions that influence construction work, such as site layout, weather, and surrounding infrastructure, creating dynamic, unpredictable work environments that can amplify hazards and contribute to unsafe conditions.

This study advances the understanding of accident causation by examining these interconnected factors through an integrated machine learning framework. By applying Random Forest Regression (RFR), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), the study models and prioritises the relative influence of cognitive, physical, task-related, and environmental factors on PAR. Sensitivity analysis is subsequently used to rank risk sub-factors by their contribution to accident risk, providing an evidence-based foundation for targeted safety interventions. By identifying the dominant factors driving accident risk, this research aims to inform more effective, data-driven safety strategies that ultimately foster a safer, more resilient construction industry.

Accident risk in construction

The construction industry is recognized globally for its high rate of occupational injuries and fatalities. According to the International Labor Organization (ILO), construction accounts for a significant share of workplace accidents and deaths each year. Factors contributing to these incidents are often complex and interwoven, involving both intrinsic human elements and extrinsic environmental conditions. Construction sites are inherently dynamic, with various tasks, hazards, and changing environmental factors contributing to an elevated risk profile18.

Accident causation in construction is frequently attributed to multifaceted interactions between human behavior, physical demands, task characteristics, and environmental influences. In many cases, addressing these interactions can substantially improve safety outcomes. However, understanding these interactions requires rigorous data analysis methods to discern direct and indirect relationships among variables19. The following are the critical factors affecting safety modeling in construction sites based on existing studies.

The four risk factor categories examined in this study, including cognitive demand, physical strain, task characteristics, and environmental factors, were selected based on a systematic review of the construction safety literature, which consistently identifies these domains as the primary dimensions of construction accident risk5,20. This categorisation was further validated through expert consultation to ensure comprehensive coverage of the key risk dimensions relevant to the studied context.

Cognitive demand

Gaining insight into how construction workers interpret information and make decisions is essential for uncovering why they choose unsafe actions. Additionally, it highlights how the information they gather influences their decision-making process5. Cognitive aspects, including task complexity, interruptions, task switching, perceived time pressure, workers’ attention, perception, decision-making skills, and situational awareness, are crucial in predicting accident risk. Research shows that cognitive overload, distractions, and mental fatigue can impair a worker’s ability to recognize and respond to hazards effectively, contributing to higher accident rates20. Cognitive factors are critical as they can mediate the impact of other variables, such as environmental or physical risks.

Physical strain

Physical strain is a significant concern on construction sites, as it directly impacts workers’ safety and well-being5. Construction tasks often involve gravity, motion-related tasks, heat and temperature exposure, mechanical and electrical strain, heavy lifting, repetitive movements, awkward postures, and prolonged physical exertion, leading to fatigue, musculoskeletal disorders, and an increased risk of accidents. Factors such as inadequate ergonomic practices, environmental conditions, and insufficient rest periods can further exacerbate physical strain2. Understanding these contributing factors is crucial to developing strategies that minimize physical stress and promote a safer work environment for construction professionals21.

Task characteristics

Task-specific characteristics, such as task duration, task repetitiveness, working crowding, task pace, complexity, duration, repetitiveness, and risk level, directly impact safety2. Complex or repetitive tasks often require heightened focus and physical exertion, which, if not managed, may lead to errors or accidents. Task factors are closely tied to cognitive and physical variables, as high-complexity or high-stress tasks can exacerbate workers’ mental and physical strain22.

Environmental factors

The environmental conditions on construction sites, including weather, noise levels, lighting, worksite hazards, and temperature extremes, are also critical to safety. Adverse weather, poor lighting, and high noise levels can obscure hazards and reduce workers’ sensory perception, increasing accident risk. The environment interacts with cognitive and physical factors, as poor conditions may pose physical hazards and hinder cognitive processing23.

Predicted accident risk

The predicted accident risk on construction sites is influenced by various factors, such as task complexity, worker experience, equipment use, and the work environment24. High-risk activities, such as working at heights or with heavy machinery, inherently carry a greater potential for accidents6. Inexperienced workers or those unfamiliar with specific tasks are more likely to make errors, which can lead to accidents25. Additionally, improper use of equipment or failure to follow safety protocols can significantly increase the risk of accidents. The prediction of accident risk requires analyzing these factors collectively, as they interact and compound, making some situations inherently more dangerous than others.

In conclusion, modeling accident risk in construction highlights the significant influence of various cognitive, physical, task, and environmental factors. These elements include workers’ cognitive load and risk perception, physical strain and fatigue, task complexity and variability, and environmental conditions such as noise, lighting, and weather extremes. Each factor contributes to the overall risk profile, affecting the likelihood and severity of accidents. Understanding the interplay between these elements provides valuable insights for improving safety protocols and mitigating risks. Materials and methods

Study design and data collection

This study adopts a quantitative research design to model predicted accident risk (PAR) in construction sites by analysing cognitive, physical, task-related, and environmental factors. Data were collected through a structured questionnaire survey distributed via LinkedIn and targeted email outreach to construction professionals, including project managers, safety managers, and site supervisors. The questionnaire development followed a two-stage process. In the first stage, 16 sub-factors across four risk categories were identified through a systematic literature review. In the second stage, a panel of 10 construction safety professionals and academics with at least 10 years of industry experience validated the instrument for content relevance, clarity, and completeness. The expert panel comprised five construction safety practitioners, three construction safety academics, and two occupational health professionals. Each expert independently rated the relevance of each sub-factor using a structured feedback form, and items were retained based on content validity ratio (CVR) thresholds as recommended by Ayre and Scally41. Sub-factors receiving inconsistent or low relevance ratings were revised or removed accordingly, ensuring that only well-supported and clearly defined items were included in the final questionnaire. Following the expert validation, the reliability of the finalised instrument was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha for each construct. Cronbach’s alpha was calculated for each construct. Physical Strain Factors (α = 0.737) and Environmental Factors (α = 0.721) exceeded the widely accepted threshold of 0.70, confirming satisfactory internal consistency. Cognitive Demand Factors (α = 0.586) and Task Characteristics Factors (α = 0.636) fall slightly below this threshold, which is not uncommon in exploratory construction safety research with small samples and multidimensional constructs. These values are considered acceptable for an exploratory study of this nature, as thresholds between 0.60 and 0.70 are recognised as adequate in early-stage instrument development42. To assess the potential influence of common method bias, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted. The single factor accounted for 38.5% of the total variance, below the 50% threshold, indicating that common method bias does not pose a serious threat to the validity of the findings. Procedural remedies, including anonymised responses, clear item wording, and the separation of predictor and criterion variables, were also applied to minimise subjectivity.

Following data collection, after cleaning to remove incomplete and inconsistent responses, 73 valid responses were retained for analysis. The dataset was first split into training and test sets, and a conservative data augmentation strategy was then applied exclusively to the training set. Small Gaussian perturbations, bounded to ± 0.3 on the five-point Likert scale, were added to the original training responses, with all augmented values rounded to the nearest valid ordinal category to preserve the discrete structure of the data. This approach expanded the effective training dataset to 100 instances while ensuring that the held-out test set consisted entirely of original, unmodified responses, thereby preventing data leakage. While this augmentation approach is methodologically justified, it is acknowledged as a limitation, and findings should be interpreted accordingly. Although the final sample size remains modest, it is consistent with comparable ML studies in construction safety11, and future research is encouraged to validate the framework using larger and more diverse datasets.

The respondents were primarily based in Oregon and Washington, reflecting the geographic scope of the data collection effort conducted in collaboration with Oregon State University. While these regions host well-established construction industries and experienced safety professionals, future research should seek to engage respondents from more geographically diverse contexts to enhance the external validity of the findings.

The study was conducted in strict accordance with institutional and international ethical standards for research involving human participants. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Oregon State University (approval number: IRB-2021-1100) before data collection commenced. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, confidentiality of responses was rigorously maintained, and no animal subjects were involved in this research.

Machine learning framework

This study applies a machine-learning–driven approach to model and predict PAR using identified risk factor variables. The 16 sub-factors covering cognitive demands (CDF1–CDF4), physical strain (PSF1–PSF4), task characteristics (TCF1–TCF4), and environmental factors (EF1–EF4) serve as input variables, while PAR serves as the continuous dependent variable. Given the non-linear and interrelated patterns observed in this dataset, conventional linear analytical approaches such as regression were found to be insufficient to fully capture their combined influence on accident risk under the conditions of this study.

Four machine learning algorithms are investigated: Multiple Linear Regression (MLR), Random Forest Regression (RFR), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN). These models were selected based on their proven capability to handle small-to-medium sample sizes, non-linear interactions, and multicollinear predictor structures commonly observed in construction safety research11. Model performance is evaluated using the Correlation Coefficient (CC) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE). All model development, training, validation, and evaluation processes are implemented using Python-based libraries, providing a reproducible and efficient computational environment.

Random Forest Regression is an ensemble learning method that constructs multiple decision trees during training and outputs the mean prediction of the individual trees, thereby reducing overfitting and improving generalisation43. Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)

Sensitivity analysis

Following model training and validation, a sensitivity analysis is conducted to quantify the relative influence of each risk factor category: cognitive demands (CDF), physical strain (PSF), task characteristics (TCF), and environmental factors (EF) on predicted accident risk (PAR). Normalised importance scores are derived from the best-performing model and presented through a ranked table and cumulative Pareto chart. This analysis identifies the dominant risk drivers and provides a data-driven basis for prioritising targeted safety interventions. The sensitivity analysis directly replaces the path coefficient interpretation used in conventional PLS-SEM approaches, offering a more robust, non-linear account of factor influence.

Data exploration and preprocessing

Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) was conducted to examine the statistical relationships between the identified risk factor variables and the composite Predicted Accident Risk (PAR) score, and to assess interdependencies among the independent sub-factors before machine learning modelling. This step is critical for understanding data behaviour, validating modelling assumptions, and justifying the use of non-linear analytical techniques. Figure 2 illustrates the Pearson correlation matrix between all 16 risk sub-factors and PAR. Overall, the results reveal systematic positive correlations between most risk variables and PAR, confirming that higher perceived risk factor intensity corresponds to higher predicted accident risk. From a quantitative perspective, environmental and cognitive sub-factors demonstrate the strongest associations with PAR. Specifically, EF3 (Lighting Conditions, r = 0.37) and EF4 (Temperature, r = 0.43) exhibit the highest positive correlations with PAR, followed closely by CDF4 (Time Pressure, r = 0.55) and PSF4 (Mech/Elec Strain, r = 0.50). These values indicate that environmental conditions and physical strain exert a dominant influence on construction accident risk. Physical strain sub-factors display moderate positive correlations with PAR, with PSF1 (r = 0.51), PSF2 (r = 0.47), and PSF4 (r = 0.50) indicating that gravity-related, motion-related, and mechanical tasks remain relevant contributors. Task characteristics sub-factors show comparatively weaker yet non-negligible relationships, with TCF1 (r = 0.45) and TCF4 (r = 0.44) suggesting that task duration and task pace contribute meaningfully to accident risk dynamics.

Pearson correlation matrix of risk sub-factors and predicted accident risk (PAR).

In addition to factor PAR relationships, the correlation matrix shows substantial intercorrelations among the sub-factors. For example, PSF1 and PSF2 are strongly correlated (r = 0.75), while EF2 and EF3 show a high intercorrelation (r = 0.78), and CDF2 and CDF3 show a notable association (r = 0.53). These inter-factor correlations confirm the presence of multicollinearity and interdependent risk structures, where multiple sub-factors co-occur and reinforce one another rather than acting in isolation. This pattern is characteristic of non-linear, systemic risk behaviour that cannot be adequately decomposed through linear, single-factor analytical approaches. The heterogeneity and variability observed in factor–PAR correlation coefficients further confirm that risk factor influences on predicted accident risk are non-uniform and context-dependent. Collectively, these findings provide quantitative justification for the adoption of ensemble machine learning techniques in this study, which are better equipped to capture the complex, non-linear interactions and combined multi-factor effects observed in this dataset than conventional parametric approaches.

Baseline MLR model performance

To provide a conventional statistical benchmark before applying machine learning models, an MLR model was fit to the dataset using identical input variables and a train-test split. The MLR model assumes a linear relationship between the input sub-factors and the PAR and estimates regression coefficients via ordinary least squares. While MLR offers simplicity and interpretability, it is inherently limited in its ability to model interactions and non-linearities among predictors.

The MLR model achieved a training CC of 0.612 and training RMSE of 0.721, and a test CC of 0.489 and test RMSE of 0.798. The marginal gap between training and testing performance suggests that, while the model did not overfit, it consistently underperformed across both stages due to its inability to generalise from the complex relationships in the data. These results indicate that, within this dataset, the linear assumption underlying MLR was insufficient to fully capture the complex, non-linear, and interdependent relationships among the accident risk sub-factors identified in the EDA, where multiple correlated predictors collectively influenced PAR in ways that were not adequately represented through additive linear combinations. These findings motivated the application of three advanced machine learning models, as presented in the following section.

For RFR, the number of estimators was explored from 50 to 300, with an optimal value of 300 trees identified. The maximum tree depth was tested between 3 and 10, with a depth of 5 found to provide the best trade-off between model complexity and generalisation. The minimum samples per split was set to 2, and the number of features per split was optimised at 6. Bootstrap sampling was enabled throughout. This configuration demonstrates Random Forest’s ability to capture non-linear relationships among accident risk factors while avoiding overfitting under the limited sample conditions of this study.

For XGBoost, the learning rate was explored between 0.01 and 0.30, with an optimal value of 0.09. The number of boosting rounds was set to 100, and the maximum tree depth was optimised at 3. L2 regularisation was set to λ = 1.0, and the subsample ratio was fixed at 0.8 to reduce variance. This configuration reflects XGBoost’s ability to correct residual errors while maintaining robust generalisation through regularisation sequentially.

For the ANN ensemble, five networks were trained with architectures of three hidden layers each, with a 64-to-32-to-16 neuron configuration. Both sigmoid and ReLU activation functions were evaluated during training; the best predictive performance was achieved with ReLU applied to all hidden layers, as reported in Table 3. Learning rates ranged from 0.004 to 0.006, and training was conducted over 650–720 epochs, depending on the network configuration. Dropout regularisation (0.08–0.10) was applied during training to prevent overfitting, and a momentum optimiser (β = 0.9) with learning rate decay was used to ensure stable convergence. The ensemble approach, which averaged predictions across five independently initialised networks with varied seeds, significantly enhanced the model’s generalisation capability, reducing prediction variance on unseen test data.

To statistically assess the comparative model performance, a Wilcoxon signed-rank test was conducted on the cross-validated prediction errors. The test did not reveal statistically significant differences between model prediction errors (RFR vs. XGBoost: p = 0.533; RFR vs. MLR: p = 0.562), likely due to the modest sample size and the limited statistical power of the non-parametric test. Therefore, RFR should be interpreted as the model that achieved the strongest observed performance within this dataset rather than as statistically superior in a generalisable sense. Based on its highest observed test CC and lowest test RMSE, RFR was selected as the basis for the subsequent sensitivity analysis.

Overall, the findings suggest that machine learning models may complement traditional linear methods when analysing correlated and potentially interactive accident-risk factors in construction safety datasets. However, further validation using larger, independent, and geographically diverse datasets is required before broader conclusions can be drawn regarding model transferability or practical deployment.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was conducted to quantify the relative influence of individual risk sub-factors on PAR and to identify the priority drivers whose variation has the greatest impact on accident risk outcomes. The analysis is based on normalised Mean Decrease in Impurity (MDI) scores derived from a Random Forest model trained on the full dataset (300 trees). It is presented in a ranked importance table and a cumulative Pareto chart.

Table 5 presents the ranked sub-factors by importance score, percentage contribution, cumulative impact, and category classification. The results reveal a highly uneven distribution of influence, indicating that a limited number of sub-factors dominate the dynamics of accident risk. The three most influential sub-factors are all physical strain variables, namely PSF4 (Mech/Elec Strain, 12.11%), PSF1 (Gravity Tasks, 10.92%), and PSF2 (Motion Tasks, 10.71%), collectively accounting for 33.74% of the cumulative importance. This finding underscores the primacy of physical hazards, particularly exposure to mechanical and electrical energy, height-related gravity tasks, and repetitive motion, in driving accident risk on construction sites. The dominance of PSF sub-factors is consistent with the EDA correlation findings, where PSF1 (r = 0.51), PSF2 (r = 0.47), and PSF4 (r = 0.50) all showed moderate-to-strong associations with PAR. The fourth and fifth ranked sub-factors, CDF4 (Time Pressure, 8.32%) and CDF2 (Interruptions, 7.36%), highlight that cognitive demand factors, specifically perceived time pressure and workflow interruptions, represent the next most influential constraints on accident risk. Collectively, the top five sub-factors contribute approximately 49.42% of the cumulative importance, indicating that nearly half of the construction accident risk is governed by a small subset of highly influential physical and cognitive factors. As shown in Fig. 4, the cumulative curve demonstrates a steep initial slope, with approximately 80% of the total influence captured by the top ten sub-factors (cumulative = 80.48%). Beyond this point, the curve flattens noticeably, indicating diminishing marginal influence from lower-ranked sub-factors. This pattern reflects a classic Pareto-type structure, where a small number of dominant factors account for the majority of the observed impact.

The sensitivity analysis reveals that construction accident risk is governed by a concentrated and hierarchical factor structure rather than a uniformly distributed set of constraints. The dominance of physical strain and cognitive demand factors suggests that safety interventions focusing solely on environmental controls or task redesign are unlikely to yield substantial risk-reduction gains in the context examined. Instead, the findings indicate that targeted interventions addressing physical hazard exposure, particularly mechanical and electrical strain, gravity-related risks, and motion-related fatigue, alongside cognitive support mechanisms to manage time pressure and workflow interruptions, can produce disproportionately large improvements in safety outcomes. These results complement the EDA correlation findings and provide a more granular, data-driven ranking of risk drivers than what conventional path coefficient analysis can offer.

Theoretical and practical implications

Theoretical contributions

This study makes several important theoretical contributions to the construction safety and machine learning literature. The findings advance understanding of construction accident risk by demonstrating that risk factors operate as an interdependent, non-linear system rather than as isolated variables. The sensitivity analysis reveals that physical strain, cognitive demands, task characteristics, and environmental factors interact in complex, hierarchical ways to influence predicted accident risk (PAR), with physical strain sub-factors exerting a disproportionately large and previously underestimated influence at the individual indicator level.

The study challenges existing theoretical narratives in construction safety research that primarily attribute accident risk to environmental or cognitive factors at the construct level. While prior studies have consistently identified environmental conditions and cognitive demands as the dominant path-level predictors of perceived accident risk, the machine learning sensitivity framework adopted in this study reveals that at the sub-factor level, physical strain variables, particularly mechanical and electrical strain (PSF4), gravity-related tasks (PSF1), and motion-related exertion (PSF2), collectively account for the highest proportion of total influence on PAR at 33.74%. This distinction underscores the theoretical importance of disaggregating risk constructs to the indicator level when examining accident causation, as construct-level analyses can mask the relative contributions of individual risk drivers. Beyond factor ranking, the sensitivity analysis findings offer theoretical insights into the mechanisms of accident causation. The dominance of physical strain and cognitive demand sub-factors aligns with established human factors theories, suggesting that accident risk in construction environments is primarily driven by the cumulative interaction of biomechanical and cognitive stressors rather than isolated environmental or task-related triggers. The multi-layered and coupled interaction effects identified in the sensitivity analysis are consistent with findings from recent complex systems safety research, which has demonstrated that safety risks in diverse operational environments arise from interconnected system-level interactions rather than isolated variables45[,46.

Furthermore, the study contributes to the growing body of literature on data-driven safety research by establishing machine learning, specifically Random Forest Regression, as a robust and methodologically advanced alternative to conventional structural equation modelling for predicting and prioritising construction accident risk. The generalisation performance of RFR, with a test CC of 0.813 in this dataset, suggests that accident risk in the studied context is influenced by non-linear, interactive mechanisms that covariance-based or partial least squares approaches may not adequately capture under similar conditions. The application of an ANN ensemble with dropout regularisation further demonstrates that deep learning architectures can achieve competitive performance with a test CC of 0.737 even under limited sample conditions, provided that appropriate regularisation and ensemble strategies are adopted.

Finally, the study contributes a replicable, eight-phase methodological framework that integrates expert validation, survey data collection, exploratory data analysis, machine learning modelling, cross-validation, and sensitivity analysis into a coherent decision-support pipeline. While developed within the context of Pacific Northwest construction environments, this framework shows strong potential for transferability to other construction safety contexts, subject to appropriate validation and adaptation.

Practical contributions

Within the context of this study, the findings carry significant practical implications for construction safety managers, project leaders, and industry practitioners operating in similar construction environments.

First, the dominance of physical strain sub-factors in the sensitivity ranking, with PSF4, PSF1, and PSF2 collectively accounting for nearly 34% of total PAR influence, underscores the urgent need for targeted ergonomic and hazard control interventions in construction workplaces. Practical measures should prioritise the elimination or control of mechanical and electrical hazards through engineering controls, the provision of mechanical assistance for gravity-intensive lifting and handling tasks, and the introduction of structured task rotation schedules to mitigate the cumulative effects of motion-related fatigue. Investing in wearable monitoring technologies that track physical exertion, posture, and fatigue indicators in real time can provide early warning signals that enable proactive intervention before injury occurs.

Second, the high sensitivity scores assigned to CDF4 (Time Pressure, 8.32%) and CDF2 (Interruptions, 7.36%) highlight that cognitive overload and workflow disruption represent critical secondary risk drivers that are frequently overlooked in conventional safety programmes. Construction organisations should implement cognitive workload management strategies, including realistic scheduling and planning practices to reduce perceived time pressure, structured communication protocols to minimise task interruptions, and situational awareness training programmes to enhance workers’ capacity to identify and respond to hazards under cognitively demanding conditions.

Third, the moderate influence of task characteristics sub-factors, particularly TCF4 (Task Pace, 7.20%) and TCF3 (Worksite Crowding, 5.93%), indicates that site layout planning and workload distribution represent accessible, high-impact intervention areas. Addressing site congestion through optimised spatial planning, reducing task pace variability through standardised work sequencing, and aligning crew sizing with task complexity can meaningfully reduce accident risk without requiring significant capital investment.

Fourth, the proposed machine learning sensitivity framework provides construction organisations with a practical decision-support tool that enables evidence-based allocation of safety resources. Rather than distributing safety investment uniformly across all risk categories, the sensitivity ranking allows organisations to focus resources on the highest-impact sub-factors first, achieving greater risk reduction per unit of investment. The framework is scalable and can be updated as new safety data are collected, enabling continuous improvement of safety management systems over time.

Finally, the study findings are directly applicable to safety regulation and policy development. Regulatory bodies and industry associations can use the sensitivity rankings to prioritise inspection and enforcement activities, update occupational health and safety standards to reflect the disproportionate contribution of physical strain and cognitive factors to accident risk, and design targeted training and certification programmes that equip construction workers and supervisors with the skills required to manage the highest-impact risk drivers identified in this study.

Limitation and future work

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The sample size of 73 respondents, concentrated primarily in Oregon and Washington in the Pacific Northwest region of the United States, limits the geographic and organisational diversity of the findings. While the sample comprises experienced construction professionals, the results may not fully represent the diversity of construction practices, regulatory environments, and workforce characteristics present in other regions or countries. Additionally, the respondent sample is predominantly composed of project managers, safety managers, and site supervisors, with limited representation of field workers. Although managerial respondents possess relevant expertise in construction safety assessment, their risk perceptions may differ from those of field workers who are directly exposed to site hazards daily. A more balanced representation of both managerial and operational roles would enhance the representativeness and generalisability of future findings. Furthermore, the absence of external validation using an independent dataset represents a key limitation. Future research should validate the proposed framework using independent datasets from diverse geographic regions and construction contexts to confirm its broader applicability.

The cross-sectional survey design precludes causal inference regarding the temporal dynamics of risk factor accumulation. The data capture perceptions of risk factor intensity at a single point in time, which may not reflect the dynamic nature of accident risk as it evolves across project phases or seasonal conditions. Additionally, reliance on self-reported Likert-scale data may introduce response bias, particularly among experienced professionals whose perceptions of risk may differ systematically from objective hazard measures.

The data augmentation strategy employed to address sample-size limitations, while methodologically justified, introduces controlled variance that may not fully reflect the natural variability of construction risk factor distributions. The augmented dataset increases the effective sample size to 100 instances, which, while sufficient for the present modelling objectives, remains modest given the complexity of the relationships being examined.

Future research should address these gaps through several directions. Longitudinal data-collection designs that track the accumulation of risk factors over time and across project phases would provide a more dynamic understanding of accident-causation dynamics. Expanding the sample to include diverse construction contexts, such as residential, commercial, industrial, and infrastructure projects across multiple geographic regions, would enhance the framework’s generalisability. Integration of objective physiological and environmental sensor data from wearable devices and IoT-enabled site monitoring systems would further strengthen the predictive validity of machine learning models and reduce reliance on self-reported risk assessments. Finally, the proposed framework should be validated through cross-country comparative studies to assess its transferability across different regulatory environments, labour market conditions, and cultural attitudes towards safety.

Conclusions

This study examined factors influencing predicted accident risk (PAR) in the construction industry using an integrated, data-driven research design. By combining expert validation, empirical survey data from 73 construction professionals, machine learning modelling, and sensitivity analysis, the study provides a structured approach for examining and prioritising construction accident risk factors within the studied context.

The main contribution of this study is the development of a predictive and ranking-oriented framework for analysing construction accident risk factors. Rather than treating risk factors as isolated variables, the proposed approach examines their combined influence on PAR and quantifies their relative importance. This enables a clearer distinction between dominant and secondary risk drivers within the available dataset.

The results indicate that PAR is primarily associated with physical strain factors, followed by cognitive demand, task characteristics, and environmental factors. Among the individual sub-factors, PSF4, PSF1, and PSF2 collectively accounted for the largest proportion of total PAR influence at 33.74%, while CDF4 and CDF2 represented the most influential secondary drivers. Environmental factors showed lower aggregate sensitivity at the individual indicator level in this dataset, with EF2 exerting moderate influence.

In terms of model performance, Random Forest Regression achieved the highest observed test CC of 0.813 and a test RMSE of 0.432 within this dataset. However, because the Wilcoxon signed-rank test did not show statistically significant differences between model prediction errors, these results should be interpreted as dataset-specific rather than as evidence of general model superiority. The ANN ensemble also demonstrated competitive performance, while XGBoost showed weaker generalisation under the conditions of this study. Further validation using larger, independent, and geographically diverse datasets is required before broader conclusions can be drawn regarding model transferability or practical deployment.

In conclusion, within the geographic and sample boundaries of this study and based on self-reported data from construction professionals primarily located in the Pacific Northwest of the United States, the proposed framework provides a preliminary analytical basis for understanding and prioritising construction accident risk factors using ensemble machine learning techniques. The findings may support more informed and targeted safety management decision-making in similar construction contexts; however, their broader applicability should be confirmed through future validation using independent datasets from diverse geographic regions and construction environments.
