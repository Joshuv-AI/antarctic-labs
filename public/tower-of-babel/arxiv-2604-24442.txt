The Fragility of Learning LQG Controllers
Bruce D. Lee*, Anastasios Tsiamis*, Nikolai Matni, Manfred Morari, John Lygeros

Abstract
Learning methods are increasingly used to synthesize controllers from data, yet existing sample-complexity characterizations for continuous control are sharp only in the fully observed setting. This paper studies the partially observed case by deriving information-theoretic lower bounds for learning Linear Quadratic Gaussian (LQG) controllers from offline trajectories generated by a (linear) exploration policy. We prove an ε \varepsilon -local minimax excess-cost lower bound that applies to any algorithm mapping the offline dataset to a stabilizing linear controller. The bound is expressed in terms of the Hessian of the LQG cost with respect to model parameters and the inverse Fisher Information induced by the exploration policy. We further provide system-theoretic characterizations of these objects, enabling transparent construction of hard instances. Instantiating the bound on classical fragile robust-control examples, including variants of the Doyle LQG fragility counterexample and non-minimum-phase systems, demonstrates when fragile robust control problems translate into high sample complexity for learning-enabled control. These results suggest the asymptotic optimality of certainty-equivalent synthesis and motivate the importance of both task-directed experiment design and system co-design for sample-efficient learning in partially observed control.



I
Introduction

The problem of learning to control partially observed systems remains poorly understood, even for the relatively simple case of linear systems with quadratic costs and Gaussian noise (LQG). Although the robust control community has long recognized that partial observability introduces substantial additional subtleties
[
1
]
, this insight is largely absent from the recent literature on learning-based control and reinforcement learning.
In this paper, we fill this gap by examining the fundamental limits and inherent fragility of learning the optimal LQG controller. We characterize the complexity of this problem through its data requirements, that is, its statistical complexity.

I-A

Contributions

We make the following contributions:

•

We provide an information-theoretic lower bound on the sample complexity of learning LQG controllers from offline data. The bound applies to any algorithm mapping data to a controller, and thus shows that common practical strategies for mitigating partial observability in control and reinforcement learning (e.g., history stacking
[
2
]
) cannot circumvent the fundamental hardness of the problem.

•

We show the bound in terms of the underlying dynamical system, allowing the construction of ‘‘hard’’ instances.

Fundamental Limitations of Robust Control

A long line of work in robust control has shown that partially observed systems can exhibit fundamental fragility to model uncertainty.
The seminal paper
[
1
]
was the first to show that LQG could be arbitrarily fragile to model uncertainty, despite the guaraneed inherent robustness of the linear quadratic regulator (LQR). Subsequently, robust alternatives to LQG were developed, which could mitigate the issue
[

[

In

We provide theoretical justification for such observations by deriving lower bounds on the cost incurred by any learning algorithm applied to control a partially observed linear system. We instantiate our lower bound on several classical examples of fragile robust control problems, and demonstrate when they correspond to challenging learning-based control problems.

Review of LQG optimal controller

When the system

is the Kalman predictor gain.
The LQR gain is

\displaystyle F(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\Psi(\theta)^{-1}B(\theta)^{\top}P(\theta)A(\theta);

\displaystyle\Psi(\theta)

\displaystyle=B(\theta)^{\top}P(\theta)B(\theta)+R;

\displaystyle P(\theta)

\displaystyle=\texttt{DARE}(A(\theta),B(\theta),Q,R),

while the Kalman predictor gain is given by

\displaystyle L(\theta)

\displaystyle=A(\theta)\Sigma(\theta)C(\theta)^{\top}\Sigma_{e}(\theta)^{-1};

\displaystyle\Sigma_{e}(\theta)

\displaystyle=C(\theta)\Sigma(\theta)C(\theta)^{\top}+\Sigma_{v}(\theta);

\displaystyle\Sigma(\theta)

\displaystyle=\texttt{DARE}(A(\theta)^{\top},C(\theta)^{\top},\Sigma_{w}(\theta),\Sigma_{v}(\theta)).

The optimal LQG controller can also be implemented in the time-domain as

\displaystyle u^{\theta}_{t}=F(\theta)\hat{x}^{\theta}_{t|t-1},

III
Fundamental Limits

Our main result lower bounds the local minimax excess control cost of any learning algorithm. The lower bound is expressed in terms of two quantities:

The Fisher Information

corresponding to the dataset collected by policy
\pi_{\exp}
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{FI}^{\pi_{\exp}}(\theta)

\displaystyle\triangleq\E_{\mathcal{D}\sim\rho^{\pi_{\exp},1,T}(\theta)}\mathopen{}\left[\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\mathsf{nll}(\mathcal{D};\theta)\right]\mathclose{}.

(6)

Here,
\mathsf{nll}
denotes the negative log likelihood of the dataset for any parameter
\theta
, defined as

\displaystyle\mathsf{nll}(\mathcal{D};\theta)

\displaystyle\!\triangleq\!\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|y_{t}^{n}\!-\!\hat{y}^{n}_{t}(\theta)\right\|_{\Sigma_{e}(\theta)^{-1}}^{2}\!+\!\frac{T}{2}\log{\left|\Sigma_{e}(\theta)\right|},

\displaystyle\hat{y}^{n}_{t+1}(\theta)

\displaystyle\triangleq\sum_{k=0}^{t}C(\theta)A_{\mathsf{cl}}^{o}(\theta)^{t-k}\begin{bmatrix}L(\theta)\!\!\!&B(\theta)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}y_{k}^{n}\\
u_{k}^{n}\end{bmatrix}.

The Fisher information measures the signal-to-noise ratio and thus captures the “easiness” of estimating
\theta
.

The Hessian

of the objective under LQG policies:

\displaystyle H(\theta)\triangleq\nabla_{\tilde{\theta}}^{2}J(K_{\tilde{\theta}},\theta)|_{\tilde{\theta}=\theta}.

(7)

The Hessian captures the sensitivity of the control synthesis problem to the unknown parameters.

Theorem III.1

(Excess Cost Lower Bound)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be any algorithm that maps a dataset
\mathcal{D}
to a policy
K_{\mathcal{D}}\in\mathcal{K}
for a policy class
\mathcal{K}
that obeys Assumption
1
. Suppose that all instances in the ball

satisfy the stabilizability and detectability assumptions of
Section

II
. Suppose that the data is collected under an exploration policy
\pi_{\exp}
such that
\mathsf{FI}^{\pi_{\exp}}(\theta)
is Lipschitz continuous in
\theta
over
\mathcal{B}(\theta^{\star},\varepsilon)
and
\mathsf{FI}^{\pi_{\exp}}(\theta^{\star})\succ 0
.
There exist constants
c_{1}(\theta^{\star},\pi_{\exp},T,\alpha)
and
c_{2}(\theta^{\star},\pi_{\exp},T)
that depend on the nominal system, the exploration policy, the length of the experiment rollouts, and the parameter
\alpha
from Assumption
1
such that for
N\geq\max\mathopen{}\left\{c_{1}(\theta^{\star},\pi_{\exp},T,\alpha),c_{2}(\theta^{\star},\pi_{\exp},T)\varepsilon^{-2}\right\}\mathclose{}
, the following bound holds:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\theta^{\star},\varepsilon,\mathcal{A})\geq\frac{1}{4}\frac{\trace(H(\theta^{\star})\mathsf{FI}^{\pi_{\exp}}(\theta^{\star})^{-1})}{N}.

(8)

The main tool for establishing this result is the van Trees inequality
[
33
]
, a Bayesian version of the Cramer-Rao lower bound, which lower bounds the squared error in estimating a smooth function of the unknown parameter from data. To apply this result, we exploit the fact that the excess cost is a quadratic function of the Youla parameter for the estimated controller.
The argument is detailed in
Section

VI
.

The theorem lower bounds the excess cost of learning the LQG controller in terms of the Hessian
H(\theta^{\star})
(defined in (
7
)) multiplied by the inverse of the Fisher information for the policy used to collect the dataset,
\mathsf{FI}^{\pi_{\exp}}(\theta^{\star})
(defined in (
6
)). The Hessian captures the sensitivity of the control synthesis problem to the unknown parameters, while the inverse of the Fisher information measures the noise-to-signal ratio and thus captures the “difficulty” of estimating
\theta^{\star}
. The product of these quantities captures the fact that the excess cost due to learning a controller scales with the difficulty of the identification problem in the directions that are relevant to the cost.

Remark III.1

(Dependence on
T
, exploration policies)
.

The Fisher Information matrix depends on the length of the exploration episodes
T
. If the exploration policy
\pi_{\exp}
is linear and stabilizing, then it satisfies the smoothness requirement for
\mathsf{FI}^{\pi_{\exp}}(\theta)
(follows by Riccati perturbation arguments, see, for example, Appendix B of
[
6
]
). Moreover, asymptotically, the Fisher Information grows linearly with

(see

IV.2
). Thus, in the case of linear stabilizing policies, the lower bound scales inversely proportionally to the total amount of data
N\times T

The lower bound is valid only after

, and

For sufficiently small

\theta^{\star}
.
When

Notably, the burn-in time depends on the instance of interest, the exploration policy, the length of the exploration episodes, and the value
\alpha
from Assumption
1

Remark III.2

(Tightness of lower bound)
.

One can verify that the lower bound matches the asymptotic rate of decay for the model-based certainty equivalence algorithm. In particular, if the parameter
\hat{\theta}
is estimated from the collected dataset via maximum likelihood as
\hat{\theta}=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}}\mathsf{nll}(\mathcal{D},\theta)
, and if
\mathsf{FI}^{\pi_{\exp}}(\theta)\succ 0
, then it holds by Theorem 9.1 of
[

]
that

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}(\hat{\theta}-\theta)\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathsf{FI}^{\pi_{\exp}}(\theta)^{-1})\mbox{ as }N\to\infty.

If the controller is synthesized as though this estimate is ground truth, and as long as that controller is stabilizing, it holds by a Taylor expansion that

\displaystyle J(K_{\hat{\theta}},\theta)-J(K_{\theta},\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\hat{\theta}-\theta\right\|_{H(\theta)}^{2}+O\mathopen{}\left(\!\left\|\hat{\theta}-\theta\right\|^{2}\!\right)\mathclose{}.

By combining these facts, one achieves the following asymptotic characterization of the excess cost

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\!N\E\mathopen{}\left[\!J(K_{\hat{\theta}},\theta)\!-\!J(K_{\theta},\theta)\right]\mathclose{}=\frac{1}{2}\trace\mathopen{}\left(H(\theta)\mathsf{FI}^{\pi_{\exp}}(\theta)^{-1}\right)\mathclose{}.

This implies that our lower bound is tight and highlights the fact that certainty equivalence is asymptotically optimal.

The matching upper and lower bound suggest that to achieve sample efficient control, the exploration policy should be chosen such that
\mathsf{FI}^{\pi_{\exp}}
is as large as possible in the directions where objective is sensitive to the parameter estimates.
Practical limitations, such as actuator and energy bounds, typically prevent the experiment policy from injecting large inputs to achieve large signal-to-noise ratio.
In such cases, the learner should choose an exploration policy that maximizes the relevant information subject to constraints on the experimental procedure. This decision reduces to the canonical problem of experiment design

V-E

Loss of Persistent Excitation

The lower bounds and the above examples highlight the importance of the exploration policy. In particular, for the excess cost to decay to zero with an increasing number of experiments, the Fisher Information induced by the exploration policy must be positive when the Hessian is positive. Here, we review a situation where this is not the case. In particular, consider the fully observed setting by choosing
C(\theta)=I
and
\Sigma_{v}(\theta)\to 0
.

Consider playing a static exploration policy defined by the LQR gain
A_{\exp}=0
,
B_{\exp}=0
,
C_{\exp}=0
,
D_{\exp}^{y}=\tilde{F}
and
D_{\exp}^{\eta}=0
. In this case, the Fisher information of

IV.1

\displaystyle\mathsf{FI}^{\pi_{\exp}}=

\displaystyle\trace\mathopen{}\left(\!(\!\dot{A}\!+\!\dot{B}\tilde{F})\textnormal{{dlyap}}(A\!+\!B\tilde{F},\Sigma_{w})\!(\dot{A}\!+\!\dot{B}\tilde{F})^{\top}\!\Sigma_{w}^{-1}\!\right)\mathclose{}.

It holds that

\dot{A}=-\dot{B}F

\dot{B}\Delta F=0

By the discussion of

This hessian can be lower bounded as
H(\theta)\geq\lambda_{\min}(R)\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{w})2\left\|\dot{F}\right\|_{F}^{2}

V-F

Misspecification

The above examples studied classical fragile systems in the face of the excess cost lower bounds of
Theorem

III.1
. Critically, the lower bound elucidates the unavoidable excess cost of learning only in the presence of a well-specified problem: one where the parametric dynamics model captures the data generating process of (
1
). If one engages in model-based design, e.g. by following the certainty equivalent synthesis procedure, then they may only attain the stated bounds if the dynamics model class is appropriately selected. The primary issue of robust control is the uncertainty due to dynamics which are challenging to model, at least via simple linear systems. This induces some misspecification into the model class. To understand the roll such misspecification, observe that if the search space is restricted to a subset
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}
such that
\theta^{\star}\notin\Theta
, then the identification procedure will asymptotically converge to the best in class estimate
\hat{\theta}\in\Theta
that maximizes the likelihood under the exploration policy
\pi_{\exp}
[Theorem 8.2,
[
34
]
]. Such an estimate may have an identification error from the true underlying parameter that never vanishes, and which aligns with a sensitive direction of the control cost, as meaured by
H(\theta^{\star})
. This in turn leads to a poorly performing controller due to an incorrect parametric modeling assumption.

Consider for example the LQG instance of (
10
), but where the second element of the actuation matrix is incorrectly assumed to be
1+\varepsilon
, and the learner only fits the parameter for the first component of the actuation matrix: i.e.
\hat{B}=\begin{bmatrix}\theta\\
1+\varepsilon\end{bmatrix}
, even though the true system evolves under the actuation matrix
\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}
. The estimation for
\theta
turns out to be asymptotically unbiased, and therefore the resulting dynamics model is correct up to the misspecified second element in the actuation matrix. Then even asymptotically, it holds from a Taylor expansion that the learner suffers an excess cost that scales as
\varepsilon^{2}H
, for the Hessian
H
as in
Section

V-A
. This excess cost can be arbitrarily poor as the value of
\sigma
approaches zero.

VI-A

Review of Youla parameterization

Doubly co-prime factorizations enable us to parameterize all possible stabilizing controllers. They are defined as follows.

Definition VI.1

(Doubly co-prime factorization)
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{R}^{\mathsf{dy}\times\mathsf{du}}_{p}
be any system. We say that
M,U,N,V,\tilde{M},\tilde{U},\tilde{N},\tilde{V}\in\mathcal{R}\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
are a doubly co-prime factorization of
P
if and only if

•

P=NM^{-1}=\tilde{M}^{-1}\tilde{N}

•

\tilde{V}M-\tilde{U}N=I,\tilde{M}V-\tilde{N}U=I
(co-prime identity).

•

\tilde{V}U-\tilde{U}V=0,\tilde{M}N-\tilde{N}M=0
(orthogonality).

The parameterization of the stabilizing controllers is achieved via the celebrated Youla parameter.

Lemma VI.1
.

Consider any proper controller
K\in\mathcal{R}_{p}^{\mathsf{du}\times\mathsf{dy}}
that internally stabilizes a strictly proper system
P\in\mathcal{R}^{\mathsf{dy}\times\mathsf{du}}_{s}
. Suppose that
M,U,N,V,\tilde{M},\tilde{U},\tilde{N},\tilde{V}\in\mathcal{R}\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
is a doubly co-prime factorization of
P
.
Then there exists a unique stable transfer function
\mathcal{Q}
, called the Youla parameter, that is proper and satisfies
K=(U+M\mathcal{Q})(V+N\mathcal{Q})^{-1}=(\tilde{V}+\mathcal{Q}\tilde{N})^{-1}(\tilde{U}+\mathcal{Q}\tilde{M})
. In particular, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{V}&-\tilde{U}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}K\\
I\end{bmatrix}(I-PK)^{-1}\tilde{M}^{-1}.

(18)

If in addition
U
is strictly proper, then
K
is strictly proper if and only if
\mathcal{Q}
is strictly proper.

Proof.

Let
U^{\prime},V^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
be any right co-prime factorization (chapter 5 in
[
31
]
) of the controller, that is,
K=U^{\prime}V^{\prime-1}
. Following the same steps as in Theorem 12.17 of
[
31
]
, it follows that
K
is internally stabilizing if and only if there exist
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathcal{R}\mathcal{H}_{\infty},\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}\in\mathcal{R}\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
such that
K=(U+M\mathcal{Q})(V+N\mathcal{Q})^{-1}=(\tilde{V}+\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}\tilde{N})^{-1}(\tilde{U}+\tilde{}\mathcal{Q}\tilde{M})
.
Multiplying by
V+N\mathcal{Q}
and
\tilde{V}+\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}\tilde{N}
from the right and the left respectively, we obtain

\displaystyle(\tilde{V}+\tilde{}\mathcal{Q}\tilde{N})(U+M\mathcal{Q})=(\tilde{U}+\tilde{}\mathcal{Q}\tilde{M})(V+N\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\tilde{V}M\mathcal{Q}-\tilde{U}N\mathcal{Q}+\tilde{}\mathcal{Q}\tilde{N}U-\tilde{}\mathcal{Q}\tilde{M}V=0\quad\mbox{(orthogonality)}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}-\tilde{}\mathcal{Q}=0\quad\mbox{(co-prime identity)}.

This implies that
\tilde{}\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}
. It also implies uniqueness.

Define
Z=\tilde{M}V^{\prime}-\tilde{N}U^{\prime}
. Then, by inverting
U^{\prime}V^{\prime-1}=K=(\tilde{V}+\mathcal{Q}\tilde{N})^{-1}(\tilde{U}+\mathcal{Q}\tilde{M})
, we obtain the following explicit formula for the Youla parameter:

\mathcal{Q}=(\tilde{V}U^{\prime}-\tilde{U}V^{\prime})Z^{-1}.

Note that
Z^{-1}
is well-defined and in
\mathcal{RH}_{\infty}
(see Lemma 5.10 in
[
31
]
). Then, (
18
) follows from the identities

\begin{bmatrix}U^{\prime}\\
V^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}Z^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}K\\
I\end{bmatrix}V^{\prime}Z^{-1},\,V^{\prime}Z^{-1}=(I-PK)^{-1}\tilde{M}^{-1}.

Finally, let now
U
be strictly proper. Taking the limit
z\rightarrow\infty
, we obtain that

M(\infty)\mathcal{Q}(\infty)V^{-1}(\infty)=K(\infty).

M(\infty)
and
V^{-1}(\infty)
are both nonzero and finite by the co-prime property and the fact that both
P
and
K
are strictly proper.
Hence,
\mathcal{Q}(\infty)=0
if and only if
K(\infty)=0
.
∎

The optimal strictly causal LQG controller induces a doubly co-prime factorization.
Specifically, define the following transfer functions

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}M_{\theta}&U_{\theta}\\
N_{\theta}&V_{\theta}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\triangleq\left[\begin{array}[]{c|cc}A_{\mathsf{cl}}^{c}(\theta)&B(\theta)&L(\theta)\\
\hline\cr F(\theta)&I&0\\

(19)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{V}_{\theta}&-\tilde{U}_{\theta}\\

C(\theta)&0&I\end{array}\right],

where we define the closed loop matrix under the LQR optimal controller as

and recall the shorthand for the closed loop matrix under the Kalman predictor gain

VI-B

Performance Difference Lemma

We now proceed to study the performance gap between an arbitrary stabilizing controller and the optimal LQG policy. Such results are known in the literature as performance difference lemmas. Our first result expresses the performance gap in terms of control policies mapping a history of both measurements and inputs to future control inputs. Our second result shows that the gap is simply equal to a non-degenerate quadratic function of the Youla parameter. In order to state this result, we define
\tilde{}\mathcal{P}_{\theta}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}0&I\\
\mathcal{P}^{d}_{\theta}&\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{u}\end{bmatrix}
and
K_{\theta}^{y,u}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{U}_{\theta}&I-\tilde{V}_{\theta}\end{bmatrix}
.

Lemma VI.2

(Performance Difference Lemma)
.

Consider strictly proper stabilizing linear controller
K
. Let
K^{y,u}
be any transfer matrix of dimension
\mathsf{du}\times(\mathsf{dy}+\mathsf{du})
satisfying

\displaystyle K=K^{y,u}\begin{bmatrix}I\\
K\end{bmatrix}.

Then the excess cost of applying controller
K
to system (
1
) with parameter
\theta
is given by

\displaystyle J(K,\theta)-J(K_{\theta},\theta)

\displaystyle=\left\|\Psi(\theta)^{1/2}(K^{y,u}-K_{\theta}^{y,u})\begin{bmatrix}I\\
K\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{F}(\tilde{}\mathcal{P}_{\theta},K)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}^{2}

(21)

\displaystyle=\left\|\Psi(\theta)^{1/2}\mathcal{Q}^{K}_{\theta}\Sigma^{1/2}_{e}(\theta)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}^{2}.

(22)

As a result,

\displaystyle J(K,\theta)-J(K_{\theta},\theta)\geq\mu\left\|\mathcal{Q}_{\theta}^{K}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}^{2},

where

\mu={\lambda_{\min}(B(\theta)^{\top}P(\theta)B(\theta)+R)}{\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{e}(\theta))}>0.

Proof.

The lower bound involving
\mu

22

Part a): proof of (

By the performance difference lemma (Theorem 11.2 of

\displaystyle J(K,\theta)-J(K_{\theta},\theta)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{T}\frac{1}{T}\E_{\theta}^{K}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left\|\Psi(\theta)^{1/2}(u_{t}-u_{t}^{\theta})\right\|^{2},

where
u_{t}^{\theta}
is the action taken by the LQG controller for the system with dynamics
\theta
conditioned on the observed history of actions and observations
u_{0},y_{0},\dots,u_{t-1},y_{t-1}
:

\displaystyle u_{t}^{\theta}=F(\theta)\mathbf{E}_{\theta}\mathopen{}\left[x_{t}|y_{0:t-1},u_{0:t-1}\right]\mathclose{}.

By contrast,

is the control action taken by controller
K
. The expectation is taken under the distribution induced by the controller
K

We may write this in frequency domain by denoting

K\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{F}(\tilde{}\mathcal{P}_{\theta},K)\boldsymbol{d}
. Consequently,

\mathbf{u}=K^{y,u}\begin{bmatrix}I\\
K\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{F}(\tilde{}\mathcal{P}_{\theta},K)\boldsymbol{d}

satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\theta}

K\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{F}(\tilde{}\mathcal{P}_{\theta},K)\boldsymbol{d}.

\displaystyle J(K,\theta)-J(K_{\theta},\theta)

K\end{bmatrix}(I-\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{u}K)^{-1}\mathcal{P}^{d}_{\theta}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}^{2},

Recall that

and

\displaystyle\tilde{M}_{\theta}\mathcal{P}^{d}_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\!\begin{bmatrix}C(zI\!-\!A_{\mathsf{cl}}^{o})^{-1}\Sigma_{w}^{1/2}\!&\!(I\!-\!C(zI-A_{\mathsf{cl}}^{o})^{-1}L)\Sigma_{v}^{1/2}\end{bmatrix}.

\displaystyle(\tilde{M}_{\theta}\mathcal{P}^{d}_{\theta})(\tilde{M}_{\theta}P^{d}_{\theta})^{*}

\displaystyle-C\mathcal{R}L\Sigma_{v}-\Sigma_{v}L^{\top}\tilde{}\mathcal{R}C^{\top}+\Sigma_{v}.

\displaystyle C\mathcal{R}(\Sigma_{w}+L\Sigma_{v}L^{\top})\tilde{}\mathcal{R}C^{\top}

The proof of (

\mathcal{H}_{2}
norm and the cyclical invariance property of the trace operator.
∎

The quadratic structure of the performance gap is key to applying the van Trees inequality, which is suitable for obtaining lower bounds in quadratic estimation problems.

Lemma VI.4

(Closed-loop map is affine)
.

Recall the definition of the closed loop map
\mathcal{T}_{K}
of the plant
\mathcal{P}_{\theta^{\star}}^{u}

K

\displaystyle\tilde{V}

\displaystyle=\tilde{V}_{\theta^{\star}}+\mathcal{Q}^{K}_{\theta^{\star}}\tilde{N}_{\theta^{\star}},

\displaystyle\tilde{U}

\displaystyle=\tilde{U}_{\theta^{\star}}+\mathcal{Q}^{K}_{\theta^{\star}}\tilde{M}_{\theta^{\star}},

VII
Conclusion

This work provides a Cramer-Rao style lower bound for the excess cost achieved by an LQG controller learned from an offline set of interaction data from the system. The lower bound is expressed in terms of the Fisher Information for the offline dataset and the Hessian of the control objective under a certainty equivalent control policy, with respect to the system parameter estimate defining the certainty equivalent policy. Classic examples of fragile systems are examined in light of this lower bound, demonstrating when classically fragile control problems translate into problems of learning-enabled control with a large sample complexity.

The results suggest the asymptotic optimality of certainty-equivalent based synthesis policies, and also suggest that an objective for task-weighted optimal experiment design used for the fully observed case by
[
11
]
is also effective in the partially observed setting. By highlighting the potential tradeoff between the cost of control and the excess cost of learning to control, this work additionally suggests a theoretically-informed manner of system co-design for learning-enabled control. Such a strategy is previewed in
Section

V-D
. Further investigation is left for future work. Finally, this work may provide an effective way to construct benchmarks for reinforcement learning applied to continuous control tasks with a rigorous understanding of the complexity of the benchmark.
