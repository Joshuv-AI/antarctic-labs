On the structure of marginals in high dimensions
Daniel Bartl, Shahar Mendelson

Abstract
. Let G , G 1 , … , G N G,G_{1},\dots,G_{N} be independent copies of a standard gaussian random vector in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} and denote by Γ = ∑ i = 1 N ⟨ G i , ⋅ ⟩ ​ e i \Gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle G_{i},\cdot\rangle e_{i} the standard gaussian ensemble. We show that, for any set A ⊂ S d − 1 A\subset S^{d-1} , with exponentially high probability, sup x ∈ A 1 N ​ ∑ i = 1 N | ( Γ ​ x ) i ♯ − q i | ≤ c ​ 𝔼 ​ sup x ∈ A ⟨ G , x ⟩ + log 2 ⁡ N N . \sup_{x\in A}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big|(\Gamma x)^{\sharp}_{i}-q_{i}\big|\leq c\frac{\mathbb{E}\sup_{x\in A}\langle G,x\rangle+\log^{2}N}{\sqrt{N}}. Here each q i q_{i} is the i N + 1 \frac{i}{N+1} –quantile of the standard normal distribution and ( Γ ​ x ) ♯ (\Gamma x)^{\sharp} denotes the monotone increasing rearrangement of the vector Γ ​ x \Gamma x . The estimate is sharp up to a possible logarithmic factor and significantly extends previously known bounds. Moreover, we show that similar estimates hold in much greater generality: after replacing the gaussian quantiles by the appropriate ones, the same phenomenon persists for a broad class of random vectors.



1.
Introduction

The problem we explore here revolves around
structure preservation
: how much structure is preserved by i.i.d. sampling.
More accurately, let
\mathcal{F}\subset L_{2}(\mu)
be a class of mean-zero functions and set
\sigma=(X_{1},...,X_{N})
to be distributed according to
\mu^{\otimes N}
.
Given the sample
\sigma
, each
f\in\mathcal{F}
is associated with the random vector
P_{\sigma}f=\left(f(X_{1}),...,f(X_{N})\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and the question is whether
P_{\sigma}f
captures key features of
f
— uniformly in the class
\mathcal{F}
.

To give a flavour of why this question is of interest, let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, identify
x\in A
with the linear functional
f_{x}=\left\langle\cdot,x\right\rangle
and set
\mathcal{F}_{A}=\{\left\langle\cdot,x\right\rangle:x\in A\}
.
Consider a centred random vector
X
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
\Gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle X_{i},\cdot\right\rangle e_{i}
be the random matrix whose rows are
X_{1},...,X_{N}
— independent copies of
X
.
In this case,
P_{\sigma}f_{x}=\Gamma x
, and the question is whether the elements of
\Gamma A=\{\Gamma x:x\in A\}
capture the essence of the points in
A
; when that happens, the random image
\Gamma A
inherits much of
A
’s geometric structure.

Let us stress that even in this simplified setup, and in the best of scenarios — when
X
is the standard gaussian random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
—, the interplay between
\Gamma A
and
A
is not fully understood. Accurate information is known only for notions of structure preservation that are ‘coarse’.
For example, the ideal result for what is arguably the most natural notion of structure preservation—the
L_{2}
sense—implies that with high probability with respect to
\mu^{\otimes N}
, for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
,

(1.1)

\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f^{2}(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}f^{2}\right|\leq c\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\|f\|_{L_{2}}\cdot\frac{{\rm Comp}(\mathcal{F})}{\sqrt{N}}.

Here,
c
is an absolute constant and
{\rm Comp}(\mathcal{F})
captures the complexity of the class
\mathcal{F}
in some appropriate sense.
Taking into account the behaviour of the supremum as
N\to\infty
, one would hope that

{\rm Comp}(F)
is the expectation of the supremum of the canonical gaussian process indexed by
\mathcal{F}
; but for finite
N
such a gaussian bound is often false.

The reason that (
1.1
) is coarse is because it does not say much about the location of each
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}P_{\sigma}f
—only that for a typical sample it ‘lives’ in an annulus of radii
\|f\|_{L_{2}}\pm\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\|f\|_{L_{2}}\frac{{\rm Comp}(\mathcal{F})}{\sqrt{N}}
, nothing more.

Remark 1.1
.

One should keep in mind that ‘coarse’ does not mean ‘easy’ or ‘pointless’. Establishing (
1.1
) (and doing so with the right notion of
{\rm Comp}(\mathcal{F})
) is a notoriously difficult problem with a wide variety of important applications—see, for example,
[
14
,
18
,
20
]
.
In fact, satisfactory versions of (
1.1
) exist either when the class
\mathcal{F}
consists of light-tailed random variables—e.g., exhibiting a subgaussian tail decay, or when
\mathcal{F}
has plenty of symmetries. The most natural example of the latter is
\mathcal{F}_{S^{d-1}}=\{\left\langle\cdot,x\right\rangle:x\in S^{d-1}\}
, and then (
1.1
) captures the extremal singular values of the random matrix
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\Gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle X_{i},\cdot\right\rangle e_{i}
; see
[
2
,
3
,
12
,
21
,
26
]
and the references therein for some relatively recent results.

The notion of ‘structure preservation’ we are interested in here is distributional: that the distribution of the coordinates of each vector
P_{\sigma}f
resembles the true distribution of
f
, uniformly in
\mathcal{F}
. And since the joint distribution of the sample
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})
is invariant under coordinate permutations, it stands to reason that any meaningful notion of ‘distributional structure preservation’ should be formulated up to permutations. Thus, the shape of the vectors
P_{\sigma}f
should be studied after rearranging their coordinates, rather than following a specific ordering.

With that in mind, for a vector
v=(v_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
denote by
v^{\sharp}
its monotone nondecreasing rearrangement; in particular,
v^{\sharp}_{1}=\min_{i}v_{i}
and
v^{\sharp}_{N}=\max_{i}v_{i}
.
With minor abuse of notation, put
f^{\sharp}(X_{i})=v^{\sharp}_{i}
for
v=(f(X_{i}))_{i=1}^{N}
.
Setting
(Q_{f}(u))_{u\in(0,1)}
to be the quantiles of
f(X)
, the question is whether each reordered vector
(f^{\sharp}(X_{i}))_{i=1}^{N}
captures
(Q_{f}(u))_{u\in(0,1)}
, uniformly in
\mathcal{F}
.

The asymptotic picture for a single random variable is well understood when the distribution of
f(X)
is sufficiently regular—for example, when
f(X)
is gaussian. In such cases it is standard to verify (see, e.g.
[
29
]
) that almost surely, as
N\to\infty
,

f^{\sharp}(X_{\lfloor uN\rfloor})\to Q_{f}(u)\qquad\text{for every }u\in(0,1).

Unfortunately, even if this behaviour passes to a uniform estimate in
\mathcal{F}
(as is the case under minimal assumptions), this is an asymptotic estimate and provides little information on how well the finite-dimensional geometry of the vectors
\left\{(f^{\sharp}(X_{i}))_{i=1}^{N}:f\in\mathcal{F}\right\}
approximate the profiles of the true quantiles.

As we explain in what follows, existing non-asymptotic results are less than satisfactory. There are known quantitative estimates only for highly structured classes; while in more general scenarios—even when
X
is a standard gaussian vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{F}_{A}
consists of linear functionals indexed by a set
A\subset S^{d-1}
—, what is known is far from sharp.

Our main result is an optimal non-asymptotic bound that holds under minimal assumptions on the class and the underlying measure. Rather than introducing the result in full generality, it is instructive to see what happens in the gaussian case, and by how much the new estimate improves the current state of the art.

To formulate the gaussian version of our main result, let
F_{g}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-s^{2}/2}\,ds
be the standard gaussian distribution function, put
Q_{g}=F_{g}^{-1}
, and set
q\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
by

q_{i}=Q_{g}\!\left(\frac{i}{N+1}\right),\qquad i=1,\ldots,N.

Consider
G
, the standard gaussian vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
be independent copies of
G
, and put
\Gamma
to be the random matrix whose rows are
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exist absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that the following holds. For every set
A\subset S^{d-1}
, if

(1.2)

\displaystyle\Delta\geq c_{1}\frac{\max\left\{(\mathbb{E}\sup_{x\in A}\left\langle G,x\right\rangle)^{2},\log^{4}(N)\right\}}{N},

then with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{2}\Delta N)
,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in A}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big|(\Gamma x)^{\sharp}_{i}-q_{i}\big|\leq\sqrt{\Delta}.

In other words, with high probability, for every
x\in A
there is a rearrangement of the coordinates of the vector
\Gamma x
that ‘lives’ in a small
\ell_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
-ball centered in
q
.
As it happens, the only (potential) looseness is the
\log^{4}(N)
factor; the restriction on
\Delta
and the probability estimate with which (
1.3
) holds are optimal. Indeed, we show that if
N\geq c
, then for every
\Delta>0
and every fixed
x\in A
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big|(\Gamma x)^{\sharp}_{i}-q_{i}\big|\geq\sqrt{\Delta}\right)\geq\exp(-c^{\prime}\Delta N).

And also that with probability at least
0.99
,

\sup_{x\in A}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big|(\Gamma x)^{\sharp}_{i}-q_{i}\big|\geq c^{\prime\prime}\frac{\mathbb{E}\sup_{x\in A}\left\langle G,x\right\rangle}{\sqrt{N}}.

The proof of the optimality of Theorem
1.2
can be found in Section
4
.

In addition to being optimal, Theorem
1.2
is a substantial improvement on existing results: even in the gaussian setting the restrictions on
\Delta
known previously were considerably weaker.
For example (and ignoring logarithmic factors), there are sets
A\subset S^{d-1}
for which, thanks to Theorem
1.2
,
\Delta\sim 1/N
is a ‘legal choice’, but previously the best that one could hope for was
\Delta\gtrsim\sqrt{d}/N
, see
[
5
]
.
Moreover, all prior results (which are stated in or can be derived from
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
11
,
17
,
22
]
) were highly restricted—either requiring strong regularity assumptions on the set
A
, or requiring that
X
is light-tailed and the marginals have bounded densities. In contrast, the general version of Theorem
1.2
—formulated in the next section—see Theorem
2.6
—, is (almost) universal.

At the heart of the proof of Theorem
1.2
is a uniform concentration estimate on the Wasserstein distance between empirical distribution functions and their true counterparts (see Theorem
2.2
), a fact that is of independent interest and has several applications (see one example in Appendix
A
).

2.
A uniform estimate on the Wasserstein distance

Let
(\Omega,\mu)
be a probability space, set
X
to be distributed according to
\mu
, and let
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
be independent copies of
X
.
Consider a class of functions
\mathcal{F}\subset L_{2}(\mu)
, for
f\in\mathcal{F}
set
F_{f}(t)=\mathbb{P}(f(X)\leq t)
to be its distribution function, and let

F_{N,f}(t)=\frac{1}{N}|\{i\leq N:f(X_{i})\leq t\}|,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}

be the empirical distribution function of
f(X)
.
For two distribution functions
F
and
H
, denote their first order Wasserstein distance by

\mathcal{W}_{1}(F,H)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F(t)-H(t)|\,dt.

We refer to
[
10
,
28
]
for more information on the
\mathcal{W}_{1}
distance and its role in optimal transport.
Our main result follows from a uniform estimate on the
\mathcal{W}_{1}
distance between
F_{N,f}
and
F_{f}
.

Set
(G_{f})_{f\in\mathcal{F}}
to be the canonical gaussian process associated with
\mathcal{F}
(that is, the process is gaussian with mean zero and covariance
\mathrm{\mathbb{C}ov}(G_{f},G_{h})=\mathrm{\mathbb{C}ov}(f,h)
), and let

d_{\mathcal{F}}=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\|f\|_{L_{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad d^{\ast}(\mathcal{F})=\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}G_{f}}{d_{\mathcal{F}}}\right)^{2}

be the radius of
\mathcal{F}\cup\{0\}
and its critical (or Dvoretzky-Milman) dimension, respectively. For a function
f
, set

\|f\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}=\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f^{2}(X_{i})\right)^{1/2},

and the key assumption we require is that
\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}
are compatible in the following sense:

Assumption 2.1
.

There are
\theta>\mathbb{E}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}G_{f}
,
B\geq 1
and an event
\Omega_{0}
on which, for every
f,h\in\mathcal{F}\cup\{0\}
,

\|f-h\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}\leq B\|f-h\|_{L_{2}}+\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{N}}.

We show in Section
2.1
that Assumption
2.1
is always satisfied with
\theta\sim\mathbb{E}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}G_{f}
when the class
\mathcal{F}
is subgaussian, and in Appendix
B
that it is valid for other, more heavy-tailed classes as well.

The crucial estimate is as follows:

Theorem 2.2
.

For any
B\geq 1
there are constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
depending only on
B
for which the following holds.
If Assumption
2.1
is satisfied and

\Delta N\geq c_{1}\max\left\{\frac{\theta^{2}}{d_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}},\log^{4}(N)\right\},

then with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{2}\Delta N)-\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{0}^{c})
,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,f},F_{f})\leq d_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\Delta}.

Remark 2.3
.

The way
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
in Theorem
2.2
depend on
B
is of secondary importance. The proof we present implies that one may set
c_{1}=c_{1}^{\prime}B^{4}
and
c_{2}=c_{2}^{\prime}/B^{4}
for absolute constants
c_{1}^{\prime},c_{2}^{\prime}
, but that is likely to be suboptimal.

Remark 2.4
.

We may assume that each
f\in\mathcal{F}
has mean zero: the
\mathcal{W}_{1}
distance is invariant to centering.
We also ignore all questions related to measurability that can be resolved using standard methods (e.g., assuming that the class can be well-approximated by a countable subset).

As it turns out, the estimate in Theorem
2.2
is sharp—even in a gaussian setting and for classes of linear functionals
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{A}=\{\left\langle\cdot,x\right\rangle:x\in A\}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

There are absolute constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
for which the following holds.
Let
X=G
be the standard gaussian vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and set
A\subset S^{d-1}
.
Then for every
\Delta\geq\frac{1}{N}
and
x\in A
, with probability at least
\exp(-c_{1}\Delta N)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,x},F_{x})\geq c_{2}\sqrt{\Delta}.

Moreover, with probability at least
0.99
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in A}\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,x},F_{x})\geq c_{2}\frac{\mathbb{E}\sup_{x\in A}\left\langle G,x\right\rangle}{\sqrt{N}}.

The proofs of Theorem
2.2
and Lemma
2.5
are presented in Section
3
.

Theorem
2.2
leads to our main result (and to its gaussian version in Theorem
1.2
) thanks to the standard representation of the
\mathcal{W}_{1}
-distance using quantile functions: for any two distribution functions
F
and
H
,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{W}_{1}(F,H)&=\int_{0}^{1}\left|F^{-1}(u)-H^{-1}(u)\right|\,du.\\
\end{split}

Moreover, observe that for every
i=1,\dots,N
and
u\in[\frac{i-1}{N},\frac{i}{N})
,
F_{N,f}^{-1}(u)=f^{\sharp}(X_{i})
.
Thus, setting
q_{i}(f)=F_{f}^{-1}(\frac{i}{N+1})
for
i=1,\dots,N
, it follows from (
2.2
) that
\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,f},F_{f})
is almost equal to
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|f^{\sharp}(X_{i})-q_{i}(f)|
up to the discrepancy between
F_{f}^{-1}
and its discretization—which can be easily controlled (see Lemma
4.1
).
We thus have the following:

Theorem 2.6
.

For any
B\geq 1
there are constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
depending only on
B
such that the following holds.
If Assumption
2.1
is satisfied and

\Delta N\geq c_{1}\max\left\{\frac{\theta^{2}}{d_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}},\log^{4}(N)\right\},

then with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{2}\Delta N)-\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{0}^{c})
,

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big|f^{\sharp}(X_{i})-q_{i}(f)\big|\leq d_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\Delta}.

The proofs of Theorem
2.6
and Theorem
1.2
are presented in Section
4
.

2.1.
On Assumption
2.1

Consider first the case when the class
\mathcal{F}
is
L
-subgaussian; that is, for every
f,h\in\mathcal{F}\cup\{0\}
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\|f-h\|_{L_{p}}\leq L\sqrt{p}\|f-h\|_{L_{2}}\quad\text{for }p\geq 2.

Note that an equivalent formulation is that

\mathbb{P}\left(|f(X)-h(X)|\geq L^{\prime}\lambda\|f-h\|_{L_{2}}\right)\leq 2\exp(-\lambda^{2})\quad\text{for }\lambda\geq 0,

and then
L^{\prime}\sim L
; we refer e.g. to
[
27
]
for this and other basic facts about subgaussian distributions.

We then have the following result.

Lemma 2.7
.

There are absolute constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
for which the following holds.
If the class
\mathcal{F}
is
L
-subgaussian,
0\in\mathcal{F}
, and
\theta\geq c_{1}L\mathbb{E}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}G_{f}
, then Assumption
2.1
is satisfied with that
\theta
,
B=c_{1}L
, and an event
\Omega_{0}
of probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{2}\max\{N,\frac{\theta^{2}}{d^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}}\})
.

The (standard) proof is given in Appendix
B
.
Since
d^{\ast}(\mathcal{F}\cup\{0\})\sim\max\{1,d^{\ast}(\mathcal{F})\}
, Theorem
2.2
and Lemma
2.7
(applied with
\theta\sim d_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\Delta N}
) yield the following.

Corollary 2.8
.

There are constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
depending only on
L
such that the following holds.
If the class
\mathcal{F}
is
L
-subgaussian and

\Delta N\geq c_{1}\max\{d^{\ast}(\mathcal{F}),\log^{4}(N)\},

then with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{2}\Delta N)
,

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,f},F_{f})\leq d_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\Delta}.

We also show in Appendix
B
that Assumption
2.1
holds for various other (possibly heavy-tailed) classes of functions.

3.
Proofs of Theorem
2.2
and Lemma
2.5

The proof of Theorem
2.2
relies on a (non-standard) variant of Talagrand’s generic chaining theory.
For a detailed and illuminating exposition on generic chaining we refer to Talagrand’s treasured book
(see
[
25
]
).

Definition 3.1
.

An admissible sequence
(\mathcal{F}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
of the class
\mathcal{F}\subset L_{2}(\mu)
is a sequence of subsets
\mathcal{F}_{s}\subset\mathcal{F}
that satisfy
|\mathcal{F}_{0}|=1
and
|\mathcal{F}_{s}|\leq 2^{2^{s}}
.

Talagrand’s
\gamma_{2}
-functional (with respect to the
L_{2}(\mu)
distance) is

\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{F})=\inf_{(\mathcal{F}_{s})_{s\geq 0}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{s\geq 0}2^{s/2}\|f-\pi_{s}f\|_{L_{2}},

where
\pi_{s}f
is the nearest element to
f
in
\mathcal{F}_{s}
with respect to the
L_{2}(\mu)
distance and the infimum is taken with respect to all admissible sequences of
\mathcal{F}
.

Talagrand’s majorizing measures theorem
[
24
]
implies that
\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{F})
— seemingly, a purely metric object—, is actually a probabilistic entity: it is equivalent to the expectation of the supremum of the canonical gaussian process indexed by
\mathcal{F}
. Formally, there are absolute constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
for which, for every
\mathcal{F}\subset L_{2}(\mu)
,

c_{1}\mathbb{E}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}G_{f}\leq\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{F})\leq c_{2}\mathbb{E}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}G_{f},

where
(G_{f})_{f\in\mathcal{F}}
is the canonical gaussian process indexed by the class
\mathcal{F}
.

The second ingredient we require is the following dual representation of the
\mathcal{W}_{1}
-distance:
If
F
and
H
have finite first moments,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}(F,H)=\sup\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\,dF-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\,dH:\ \varphi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\text{ is $1$-Lipschitz}\right\}.

In particular, (
3.1
) immediately yields the following observation.

Lemma 3.2
.

For every two measurable functions
f
and
h
,

\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,f+h},F_{f+h})\leq\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,f},F_{f})+\|h\|_{L_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}+\|h\|_{L_{1}}.

Proof.

We assume without loss of generality that
f
and
h
have finite first moments.
Clearly, by the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,f+h},F_{f+h})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,f+h},F_{N,f})+\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,f},F_{f})+\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{f},F_{f+h}).

Next, let
\varphi
be any 1-Lipschitz function.
Then

\left|\mathbb{E}\varphi(f(X))-\mathbb{E}\varphi(f(X)+h(X))\right|\leq\mathbb{E}[|h(X)|]

and by (
3.1
),
\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{f},F_{f+h})\leq\|h\|_{L_{1}}
.
In a similar fashion,

\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,f+h},F_{N,f})\leq\|h\|_{L_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{N})},

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

We may assume that
\Delta\leq 1
.
Indeed, by Lemma
3.2
and Jensen’s inequality, for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
,

\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,f},F_{f})\leq\|f\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}+\|f\|_{L_{2}},

and clearly
\|f\|_{L_{2}}\leq d_{\mathcal{F}}
.
Moreover, by Assumption
2.1
, for every realization
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\in\Omega_{0}
,

\|f\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}\leq Bd_{\mathcal{F}}+\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{N}}

and thus
\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,f},F_{f})\leq c(B)d_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\Delta}
for every
\Delta\geq 1
satisfying the restriction from Theorem
2.2
, that
\Delta N\geq c^{\prime}\theta^{2}/d_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}
.

3.1.
Setting up the chaining argument

If Assumption
2.1
is satisfied for
\mathcal{F}
, it is also satisfied for
\mathcal{F}\cup\{0\}
, hence we may assume that
0\in\mathcal{F}
.

Observe that there is a set
\mathcal{F}^{\theta}\subset\mathcal{F}
with cardinality at most
2^{cN}
that is
\theta/\sqrt{N}
-separated (that is, for any distinct
f,h\in\mathcal{F}^{\theta}
,
\|f-h\|_{L_{2}}\geq\theta/\sqrt{N}
) and covers
\mathcal{F}
at scale
2\theta/\sqrt{N}
(that is, for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
there is
h\in\mathcal{F}^{\theta}
satisfying
\|f-h\|_{L_{2}}\leq 2\theta/\sqrt{N}
).
Indeed, by Sudakov’s inequality (see, e.g.,
[
23
, Theorem 5.6]
), if
\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon)
denotes the smallest cardinality of a set that covers
\mathcal{F}
at scale
\varepsilon>0
, then for
\varepsilon=\theta/\sqrt{N}

where the second inequality follows from the condition on
\theta
in Assumption
2.1
.
The claim now follows from the standard relation between packing numbers and covering numbers.
Finally, we may assume without loss of generality that
0\in\mathcal{F}^{\theta}
.

Next, let
(\mathcal{F}^{\theta}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
be an almost optimal admissible sequence of
\mathcal{F}^{\theta}
and since
\mathcal{F}^{\theta}\subset\mathcal{F}
, we have that
\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{F}^{\theta})\leq\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{F})
.
Denote by
s_{1}\geq 0
the smallest integer that satisfies
2^{2^{s_{1}}}\geq|\mathcal{F}^{\theta}|
; hence
2^{s_{1}}\leq c^{\prime}N
and we set
\mathcal{F}^{\theta}_{s}=\mathcal{F}^{\theta}
for
s\geq s_{1}
.
For
f\in\mathcal{F}
and
s\geq 0
, let
\pi_{s}f
be the closest element in
\mathcal{F}^{\theta}_{s}
to

.
In particular,
\pi_{s}f=\pi_{s_{1}}f
for

.

Combining these observations we have the following:

For every
f\in\mathcal{F}
,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\|f-\pi_{s_{1}}f\|_{L_{2}}\leq\frac{2\theta}{\sqrt{N}},

for
s\leq s_{1}
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\text{either }\|\Delta_{s}f\|_{L_{2}}=0\,\text{ or }\,\|\Delta_{s}f\|_{L_{2}}\geq\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{N}},

and

(3.4)

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{s\geq 0}2^{s/2}\|\Delta_{s}f\|_{L_{2}}\leq 4\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{F}^{\theta})\leq 4\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{F}).

Finally, recall that
\Delta\geq\frac{1}{N}
and set
s_{0}
to be the first integer that satisfies

.

Remark 3.4
.

In what follows we assume that
s_{1}>s_{0}
.
If
s_{1}\leq s_{0}
, the arguments presented below simplify considerably, as there is no need for chaining; we therefore omit the standard details.

The first step in the proof of Theorem
2.2
is the following reduction.

Lemma 3.5
.

Using the notation of Assumption
2.1
, for every
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\in\Omega_{0}
and every
f\in\mathcal{F}
,

\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,f},F_{f})\leq\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,\pi_{s_{1}}f},F_{\pi_{s_{1}}f})+(2+B)\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{N}}.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.2
, for every
f\in\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,f},F_{f})\leq\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,\pi_{s_{1}}f},F_{\pi_{s_{1}}f})+\|f-\pi_{s_{1}}f\|_{L_{1}}+\|f-\pi_{s_{1}}f\|_{L_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}.

Recall that

\|f-\pi_{s_{1}}f\|_{L_{1}}\leq\|f-\pi_{s_{1}}f\|_{L_{2}}\leq\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{N}},

and that by Assumption
2.1
,

\|f-\pi_{s_{1}}f\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}\leq B\|f-\pi_{s_{1}}f\|_{L_{2}}+\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{N}},

as required.
∎

The next step in the proof is truncation.
For each
m>0
define

\phi(\,\cdot\,;\,m)\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\phi(x\,;\,m)=\begin{cases}x&\text{if }|x|\leq m,\\
m&\text{if }x>m,\\
-m&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and set
\psi(\,\cdot\,;\,m)={\rm id}-\phi(\,\cdot\,;\,m)
.
Note that
|\psi(x\,;\,m)|=\max\{0,|x|-m\}
.

With a minor abuse notation, set for any function
h
,

\displaystyle\phi_{s}(h)

\displaystyle=\phi\left(h\,;\,\|h\|_{L_{2}}\sqrt{N/2^{s}}\right),

\displaystyle\psi_{s}(h)

\displaystyle=\psi\left(h\,;\,\|h\|_{L_{2}}\sqrt{N/2^{s}}\right),

and for
f\in\mathcal{F}
let

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=s_{0}}^{s_{1}-1}\phi_{s}(\Delta_{s}f)+\phi_{s_{0}}(\pi_{s_{0}}f).

The idea behind the introduction of
\mathcal{T}(f)
is a multi-level truncation.
Clearly
\pi_{s_{1}}f=\sum_{s=s_{0}}^{s_{1}-1}\Delta_{s}f+\pi_{s_{0}}f
; thus
\mathcal{T}(f)
is a truncation of
\pi_{s_{1}}f
that takes place for each ‘link’ separately, according to the level
s
and to the
L_{2}
-norm of the link.
This is essential in guaranteeing that the wanted degree of concentration is exhibited by all the links simultaneously—and is the reason behind the choice of the truncation level of an
s
-link at
\|\Delta_{s}f\|\sqrt{N/2^{s}}
.
Note that for
s>s_{1}
,
\sqrt{N/2^{s}}<1
, meaning that the truncation would be beyond a meaningful scale; that is the reason why the construction is terminated at the level
s_{1}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

There is an absolute constant
c
such that for
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\in\Omega_{0}
and every
f\in\mathcal{F}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,\pi_{s_{1}}f},F_{\pi_{s_{1}}f})\leq\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,\mathcal{T}(f)},F_{\mathcal{T}(f)})+cB^{2}\left(d_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\Delta}+\frac{\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{F})}{\sqrt{N}}\right).

Proof.

By Lemma
3.2
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,\pi_{s_{1}}f},F_{\pi_{s_{1}}f})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,\mathcal{T}(f)},F_{\mathcal{T}(f)})

\displaystyle\qquad+\left\|\pi_{s_{1}}f-\mathcal{T}(f)\right\|_{L_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}+\left\|\pi_{s_{1}}f-\mathcal{T}(f)\right\|_{L_{1}}.

To estimate
\|\pi_{s_{1}}f-\mathcal{T}(f)\|_{L_{1}}
, set
b_{s}=\|\Delta_{s}f\|_{L_{2}}\sqrt{N/2^{s}}
and by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality followed by Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\left\|\psi_{s}(\Delta_{s}f)\right\|_{L_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|\Delta_{s}f|1_{\{|\Delta_{s}f|\geq b_{s}\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\|\Delta_{s}f\|_{L_{2}}\sqrt{\mathbb{P}(|\Delta_{s}f|\geq b_{s})}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Delta_{s}f\|_{L_{2}}\sqrt{\frac{2^{s}}{N}}.

In a similar manner, using that
2^{s_{0}}\geq\Delta N
and that
\|\pi_{s_{0}}f\|_{L_{2}}\leq d_{\mathcal{F}}
,

\displaystyle\left\|\psi_{s_{0}}(\pi_{s_{0}}f)\right\|_{L_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\pi_{s_{0}}f\|_{L_{2}}\sqrt{\mathbb{P}(|\pi_{s_{0}}f|\geq\|\pi_{s_{0}}f\|_{L_{2}}/\sqrt{\Delta})}\leq d_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\Delta}.

Thus, by (
3.4
),

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\pi_{s_{1}}f-\mathcal{T}(f)\|_{L_{1}}&\leq\left\|\psi_{s_{0}}(\pi_{s_{0}}f)\right\|_{L_{1}}+\sum_{s=s_{0}}^{s_{1}-1}\left\|\psi_{s}(\Delta_{s}f)\right\|_{L_{1}}\\
&\leq d_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\Delta}+\sum_{s=s_{0}}^{s_{1}-1}\sqrt{\frac{2^{s}}{N}}\left\|\Delta_{s}f\right\|_{L_{2}}\leq d_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\Delta}+4\frac{\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{F})}{\sqrt{N}}.\end{split}

The analysis of the
\|\psi_{s}(\Delta_{s}f)\|_{L_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}
-term is slightly more involved.
Since
\mathcal{F}_{s_{1}}^{\theta}
is a
\theta/\sqrt{N}
-separated set, we either have
\Delta_{s}f=0
(in which case
\|\psi_{s}(\Delta_{s}f)\|_{L_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}=0
), or
\|\Delta_{s}f\|_{L_{2}}\geq\theta/\sqrt{N}
.
In the latter case, and since
|\psi_{s}(\Delta_{s}f)|=\max\{0,|\Delta_{s}f|-b_{s}\}
, tail-integration followed by Markov’s inequality show that

\displaystyle\left\|\psi_{s}(\Delta_{s}f)\right\|_{L_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}

\displaystyle=\int_{b_{s}}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{N}(|\Delta_{s}f|>t)\,dt\leq\int_{b_{s}}^{\infty}\frac{\|\Delta_{s}f\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}^{2}}{t^{2}}\,dt.

Moreover,
\|\Delta_{s}f\|_{L_{2}}\geq\theta/\sqrt{N}
and it thus follows from Assumption
2.1
that

\|\Delta_{s}f\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}^{2}\leq(B+1)^{2}\|\Delta_{s}f\|_{L_{2}}^{2}.

Therefore, by the choice of
b_{s}
,

\displaystyle\left\|\psi_{s}(\Delta_{s}f)\right\|_{L_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}

\displaystyle\leq(B+1)^{2}\|\Delta_{s}f\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\int_{b_{s}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{2}}\,dt

\displaystyle=(B+1)^{2}\|\Delta_{s}f\|_{L_{2}}\sqrt{\frac{2^{s}}{N}}.

The same arguments can be used to show that

\displaystyle\left\|\psi_{s_{0}}(\pi_{s_{0}}f)\right\|_{L_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}B^{2}d_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\Delta},

and following the same path as in (
3.5
),

\|\pi_{s_{1}}f-\mathcal{T}(f)\|_{L_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}\leq c_{2}B^{2}\left(d_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\Delta}+\frac{\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{F})}{\sqrt{N}}\right),

as required.
∎

3.2.
The heart of the proof

We are left to deal with
\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,\mathcal{T}(f)},F_{\mathcal{T}(f)})
, which is where the non-standard chaining argument is required.

For
f\in\mathcal{F}
set
S_{s_{0}-1}(f)=\phi_{s_{0}}(\pi_{s_{0}}f)
and for
r\geq s_{0}
let

S_{r}(f)=\sum_{s=s_{0}}^{r}\phi_{s}(\Delta_{s}f)+\phi_{s_{0}}(\pi_{s_{0}}f).

In particular
\mathcal{T}(f)=S_{s_{1}-1}(f)
is a telescopic sum:

\mathcal{T}(f)=\sum_{r=s_{0}}^{s_{1}-1}\left(S_{r}(f)-S_{r-1}(f)\right)+\phi_{s_{0}}(\pi_{s_{0}}f).

Moreover,

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T}(f)\leq t)=\sum_{r=s_{0}}^{s_{1}-1}\left(\vphantom{\big(}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{r}(f)\leq t\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(S_{r-1}(f)\leq t\right)\right)+\mathbb{P}(\phi_{s_{0}}(\pi_{s_{0}}f)\leq t),

and the same is true for
\mathbb{P}_{N}
as well.
Thus, setting
[\mathbb{P}_{N}-\mathbb{P}](A)=\mathbb{P}_{N}(A)-\mathbb{P}(A)
, we have that

(3.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,\mathcal{T}(f)},F_{\mathcal{T}(f)})&\leq\sum_{r=s_{0}}^{s_{1}-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\vphantom{\mathbb{P}^{(a)}}[\mathbb{P}_{N}-\mathbb{P}](S_{r}(f)\leq t)-[\mathbb{P}_{N}-\mathbb{P}](S_{r-1}(f)\leq t)\right|\,dt\\
&\qquad+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|[\mathbb{P}_{N}-\mathbb{P}](\phi_{s_{0}}(\pi_{s_{0}}f)\leq t)\right|\,dt\\
&=\mathcal{E}_{1}(f)+\mathcal{E}_{2}(f).\end{split}

3.3.
Estimating
\mathcal{E}_{1}(f)

Lemma 3.7
.

There is an absolute constant
c
such that with probability at least
1-\exp(-4\Delta N)
, for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
,

\mathcal{E}_{1}(f)\leq c\left(d_{\mathcal{F}}\frac{\log^{2}(e/\Delta)}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{F})}{\sqrt{N}}\right).

The crucial ingredient is a high probability estimate on each increment

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\vphantom{\mathbb{P}^{(a)}}[\mathbb{P}_{N}-\mathbb{P}](S_{r}(f)\leq t)-[\mathbb{P}_{N}-\mathbb{P}](S_{r-1}(f)\leq t)\right|\,dt:

Lemma 3.8
.

There is an absolute constant
c
such that the following holds.
For every
f\in\mathcal{F}
and
r\in\{s_{0},\dots,s_{1}-1\}
, with probability at least
1-\exp(-2^{r+5})
,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\vphantom{\mathbb{P}^{(a)}}[\mathbb{P}_{N}-\mathbb{P}](S_{r}(f)\leq t)-[\mathbb{P}_{N}-\mathbb{P}](S_{r-1}(f)\leq t)\right|\,dt\\
&\leq c\left(d_{\mathcal{F}}\frac{\log(e/\Delta)}{\sqrt{N}}+\|\Delta_{r}f\|_{L_{2}}\sqrt{\frac{2^{r}}{N}}\right).\end{split}

Let us show that Lemma
3.7
is an immediate outcome of Lemma
3.8
.

Proof of Lemma
3.7
.

Observe that

|\{(S_{r}(f),S_{r-1}(f)):f\in\mathcal{F}\}|\leq\exp(2^{r+4}).

Indeed, recall that
S_{r}(f)=\sum_{s=s_{0}}^{r}\phi_{s}(\Delta_{s}f)+\phi_{s_{0}}(\pi_{s_{0}}f)
and thus

\displaystyle|\{S_{r}(f):f\in\mathcal{F}\}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=s_{0}}^{r}|\mathcal{F}_{s+1}^{\theta}\times\mathcal{F}_{s}^{\theta}|+|\mathcal{F}_{s_{0}}^{\theta}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=s_{0}}^{r}2^{2^{s+1}}2^{2^{s}}+2^{2^{s_{0}}}\leq\exp(2^{r+3}).

Now consider
r\in\{s_{0},\dots,s_{1}-1\}
.
By Lemma
3.8
and the union bound over the pairs
(S_{r}(f),S_{r-1}(f))
, it is evident that with probability at least
1-\exp(-2^{r+4})
(
3.7
) is true uniformly for every pair
((S_{r}(f),S_{r-1}(f)))_{f\in\mathcal{F}}
.
And, by the union bound over
r
, with probability at least

1-\sum_{r\geq s_{0}}\exp(-2^{r+4})\geq 1-\exp(-2^{s_{0}+2}),

for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}(f)

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\sum_{r=s_{0}}^{s_{1}-1}\left(\|\Delta_{r}f\|_{L_{2}}\sqrt{\frac{2^{r}}{N}}+d_{\mathcal{F}}\frac{\log(e/\Delta)}{\sqrt{N}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\left(4\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{F})+(s_{1}+1-s_{0})d_{\mathcal{F}}\frac{\log(e/\Delta)}{\sqrt{N}}\right).

To complete the proof, note that

s_{1}-s_{0}\leq\log_{2}(4N)-\log_{2}(\Delta N)\leq c\log\left(\frac{e}{\Delta}\right).\qed

Next, fix
f\in\mathcal{F}
and
r\geq s_{0}
, and set

Z=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\vphantom{\mathbb{P}^{(a)}}[\mathbb{P}_{N}-\mathbb{P}](S_{r}(f)\leq t)-[\mathbb{P}_{N}-\mathbb{P}](S_{r-1}(f)\leq t)\right|\,dt.

We turn to the ‘main event’:
showing that
Z
concentrates around its expectation, and that the expectation is sufficiently small.

3.3.1.
Controlling
\mathbb{E}Z

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
m>0
and
M\geq 1
.
If
h
satisfies that
\|h\|_{L_{2}}\leq m
and
\|h\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq mM
, then

\mathbb{E}\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,h},F_{h})\leq 2m\frac{\log(eM)}{\sqrt{N}}.

Proof.

Set
w=h/m
, thus
F_{N,h}(t)=F_{N,w}(t/m)
and
F_{h}(t)=F_{w}(t/m)
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and
F_{N,w}(t)=F_{w}(t)\in\{0,1\}
for
|t|>M
.
By Fubini’s theorem followed by a change of variables,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,h},F_{h})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}|F_{N,h}(s)-F_{h}(s)|\,ds=m\int_{-M}^{M}\mathbb{E}|F_{N,w}(t)-F_{w}(t)|\,dt.

Clearly, for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}[|F_{N,w}(t)-F_{w}(t)|^{2}]\right)^{1/2}=\frac{(F_{w}(t)(1-F_{w}(t)))^{1/2}}{\sqrt{N}}.

Finally, by Markov’s inequality,

F_{w}(t)(1-F_{w}(t))\leq\|w\|_{L_{2}}^{2}t^{{-2}}\leq t^{{-2}},

and the claim follows because
\int_{1}^{M}\frac{1}{t}\,dt=\log(M)
.
∎

Lemma 3.10
.

There is an absolute constant
c
such that for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
and
r\in\{s_{0}-1,\dots,s_{1}-1\}
,

\mathbb{E}\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,S_{r}(f)},F_{S_{r}(f)})\leq cd_{\mathcal{F}}\frac{\log(e/\Delta)}{\sqrt{N}}.

In particular,

\mathbb{E}Z\leq 2cd_{\mathcal{F}}\frac{\log(e/\Delta)}{\sqrt{N}}.

Proof.

Observe that
\|S_{r}(f)\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq c_{1}d_{\mathcal{F}}/\sqrt{\Delta}
.
Indeed,

\|\phi_{s_{0}}(\pi_{s_{0}}f)\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq d_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\frac{N}{2^{s_{0}}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\|\phi_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}f)\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq\|\Delta_{\ell}f\|_{L_{2}}\sqrt{\frac{N}{2^{\ell}}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|S_{r}(f)\|_{L_{\infty}}&\leq\sum_{\ell=s_{0}}^{r}\|\phi_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}f)\|_{L_{\infty}}+\|\phi_{s_{0}}(\pi_{s_{0}}f)\|_{L_{\infty}}\\
&\leq\sum_{\ell=s_{0}}^{r}\|\Delta_{\ell}f\|_{L_{2}}\frac{2^{\ell/2}}{2^{s_{0}}}\sqrt{N}+d_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\frac{N}{2^{s_{0}}}}\\
&\leq\frac{4\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{F})\sqrt{N}}{\Delta N}+d_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\Delta}}\leq 5d_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\Delta}},\end{split}

where we used that
2^{s_{0}}\geq\Delta N

and that
\gamma_{2}^{2}(\mathcal{F})\leq d_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}\Delta N
.

Next, note that
\|S_{r}(f)\|_{L_{2}}\leq c_{2}d_{\mathcal{F}}
.
This holds because
\|\phi_{s_{0}}(\pi_{s_{0}}f)\|_{L_{2}}\leq\|\pi_{s_{0}}f\|_{L_{2}}
and
\|\phi_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}f)\|_{L_{2}}\leq\|\Delta_{\ell}f\|_{L_{2}}
; hence

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|S_{r}(f)\|_{L_{2}}&\leq\sum_{\ell=s_{0}}^{r}\|\phi_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}f)\|_{L_{2}}+\|\phi_{s_{0}}(\pi_{s_{0}}f)\|_{L_{2}}\\
&\leq\frac{4\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{F})}{2^{s_{0}/2}}+d_{\mathcal{F}}.\end{split}

Recalling that
2^{s_{0}}\geq\Delta N\geq\gamma_{2}^{2}(\mathcal{F})/d_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}
, it is evident that
\|S_{r}(f)\|_{L_{2}}\leq 5d_{\mathcal{F}}
.

The first part of the lemma follows from Lemma
3.9
and the second one since

\mathbb{E}Z\leq\mathbb{E}\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,S_{r}(f)},F_{S_{r}(f)})+\mathbb{E}\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,S_{r-1}(f)},F_{S_{r-1}(f)}).\qed

3.3.2.
Concentration around
\mathbb{E}Z

The argument is based on the generalization of the Efron-Stein inequality from
[
9
]
.

Set
X_{1}^{\prime},\dots,X_{N}^{\prime}
to be independent copies of
X
that are independent of
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
, and let

\mathbb{P}^{(j)}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\left(\delta_{X_{1}}+\cdots\delta_{X_{j-1}}+\delta_{X_{j}^{\prime}}+\delta_{X_{j+1}}+\cdots\delta_{X_{N}}\right).

Denote

Z^{(j)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|[\mathbb{P}^{(j)}_{N}-\mathbb{P}](S_{r}(f)\leq t)-[\mathbb{P}^{(j)}_{N}-\mathbb{P}](S_{r-1}(f)\leq t)\right|\,dt

and put

V=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Z-Z^{(j)}\right)^{2}\mid(X_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\right].

Here, the conditional expectation means that the expectation is taken only with respect to the
X_{j}^{\prime}
.

Using this notation, a direct consequence of Theorem 2 in
[
9
]
is the following:

Theorem 3.11

(
[
9
, Theorem 2]
)
.

For every
q\geq 2
,

\|Z-\mathbb{E}Z\|_{L_{q}}\leq 6\sqrt{q}\|\sqrt{V}\|_{L_{q}}.

The proof of Lemma
3.8
is based on Theorem
3.11
for
q=2^{r+5}
: by Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}\left(|Z-\mathbb{E}Z|\geq e6\sqrt{q}\|\sqrt{V}\|_{L_{q}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{e^{q}}=\exp(-2^{r+5}).

Remark 3.12
.

In light of the wanted chaining bound, we need to show that

\|\sqrt{V}\|_{L_{q}}\leq c\|\Delta_{r}f\|_{L_{2}}/\sqrt{N}

for that choice of
q
.
Such an estimate implies that
\|\sqrt{V}\|_{L_{q}}\sim\|\sqrt{V}\|_{L_{1}}
, and as the proof reveals, this stability is true only up
q\sim 2^{r}
; for higher moments
\|\sqrt{V}\|_{L_{q}}
grows with
q
.

Lemma 3.13
.

For every
q\geq 2
,

\|\sqrt{V}\|_{L_{q}}\leq\frac{2\|\Delta_{r}f\|_{L_{2}}}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{2}{N}\left\|\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\phi_{r}^{2}(\Delta_{r}f(X_{j}))}\right\|_{L_{q}}.

Proof.

The expectations appearing in
Z
and
Z^{(j)}
are the same, and by the triangle inequality

\displaystyle|Z-Z^{(j)}|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|[\mathbb{P}_{N}-\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(j)}](S_{r}(f)\leq t)+[\mathbb{P}_{N}-\mathbb{P}_{N}^{(j)}](S_{r-1}(f)\leq t)\right|\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\left(1_{\{S_{r}(f)(X_{j})\leq t\}}-1_{\{S_{r}(f)(X_{j}^{\prime})\leq t\}}\right)-\left(1_{\{S_{r-1}(f)(X_{j})\leq t\}}-1_{\{S_{r-1}(f)(X_{j}^{\prime})\leq t\}}\right)\right|\,dt.

Next,
S_{r}(f)=S_{r-1}(f)+\phi_{r}(\Delta_{r}f)
; therefore,

\{S_{r}(f)\leq t\}=\{S_{r-1}(f)+\phi_{r}(\Delta_{r}f)\leq t\},

and the Lebesgue measure of the set

\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}:1_{\{S_{r}(f)(X_{j})\leq t\}}-1_{\{S_{r-1}(f)(X_{j})\leq t\}}\neq 0\right\}

is
|\phi_{r}(\Delta_{r}f(X_{j}))|
.
Using an identical argument for
X_{j}^{\prime}
in place of
X_{j}
, it is evident that

|Z-Z^{(j)}|\leq\frac{1}{N}\left(|\phi_{r}(\Delta_{r}f(X_{j}))|+|\phi_{r}(\Delta_{r}f(X_{j}^{\prime}))|\right),

and as
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\|\phi_{r}(\Delta_{r}f)\|_{L_{2}}\leq\|\Delta_{r}f\|_{L_{2}}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Z-Z^{(j)}\right)^{2}\mid(X_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\right]\leq\frac{2}{N^{2}}\left(\phi_{r}^{2}(\Delta_{r}f(X_{j}))+\|\Delta_{r}f\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\right).

The claim now follows from sub-additivity of
a\mapsto\sqrt{a}
.
∎

With Lemma
3.13
in mind, all that remains is to establish a satisfactory estimate on

To that end, we use the following bound on moments of sums of independent random variables established in

Theorem 3.14

(

Then, for every

We also need the standard observation that if
\|Y\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq M
and
\|Y\|_{L_{1}}\leq m

Hence (
B.2
) follows by transforming back to the original coordinate system.

Step 2 (the estimate on
M
):

Set
Z=P_{M}X
and
Z_{i}=P_{M}X_{i}
, and put
\Sigma^{Z}=\mathrm{\mathbb{C}ov}[Z]
.
Observe that, since
(\lambda_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
is non-increasing,

\|\Sigma^{Z}\|_{\rm op}=\lambda_{K+1}\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\lambda_{i}\leq\frac{{\rm tr}(\Sigma)}{K}.

Assume first that
\lambda_{K+1}\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\rm tr}(\Sigma)}{K}
; we explain the minor changes needed in the general case at the end of the proof.

By interchanging two suprema,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in M:\|x\|_{2}\leq 1}\,\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle Z_{i},x\right\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in M:\|x\|_{2}\leq 1}\sup_{a\in S^{N-1}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\left\langle Z_{i},x\right\rangle\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{a\in S^{N-1}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}Z_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}=(\ast).

Following the notation used in
[
1
]
and setting
[N]=\{1,\dots,N\}
, let

f(s,[N])=\sup_{a\in S^{N-1}\,:\,|\{i\leq N:a_{i}\neq 0\}|\leq s}\,\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}Z_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}

for
s\geq 1
.
Note that
(\ast)=f(N,[N])
and that
f(N,[N])\leq\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}f(K,[N])
; hence to establish (
B.3
) it remains to show that with high probability,
f(K,[N])\lesssim R^{2}{\rm tr}(\Sigma)
.
To that end, we apply
[
1
, Theorem 3]
:

Theorem B.5

(
[
1
, Theorem 3]
)
.

There is an absolute constant
\beta\geq 1
and constants
c,c^{\prime}
depending only on
q
and
L
such that the following holds:
For all integers
N
and
s
that satisfy
\frac{1}{\beta}N\geq s\geq{\rm r}(\Sigma^{Z})
, with probability at least
1-\frac{c}{N}
,

\displaystyle f(s,[N])\leq c^{\prime}\left(\max_{i\leq N}\|Z_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Sigma^{Z}\|_{\rm op}\cdot s\left(\frac{N}{s}\right)^{4/(4+q)}\log^{4}\left(\frac{N}{s}\right)\right).

We claim that the condition
\frac{1}{\beta}N\geq s\geq{\rm r}(\Sigma^{Z})
in Theorem
B.5
is satisfied for
s=2K
.
Indeed, since
\|\Sigma^{Z}\|_{\rm op}=\lambda_{K+1}\geq\frac{{\rm tr}(\Sigma)}{2K}
, we have

{\rm r}(\Sigma^{Z})=\frac{{\rm tr}(\Sigma^{Z})}{\|\Sigma^{Z}\|_{\rm op}}\leq\frac{{\rm tr}(\Sigma)}{\lambda_{K+1}}\leq 2K,

and the claim follows since
K=\eta N
and
\eta=\frac{1}{2\beta}
.
Moreover, using that
\|\Sigma^{Z}\|_{\rm op}\leq\frac{{\rm tr}(\Sigma)}{K}
, that
\|Z_{i}\|_{2}\leq\|X_{i}\|_{2}
, and that
\max_{i\leq N}\|X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\leq R^{2}{\rm tr}(\Sigma)
with probability
1-\delta
by assumption, it is straightforward to verify that with probability
1-\delta
,

\max_{i\leq N}\|Z_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Sigma^{Z}\|_{\rm op}\cdot 2K\left(\frac{N}{2K}\right)^{4/(4+q)}\log^{4}\left(\frac{N}{2K}\right)\leq c_{5}(q,L)R^{2}\mathop{\rm tr}(\Sigma).

Finally,
f(K,[N])\leq f(2K,[N])
, which concludes the proof of (
B.3
).

Step 3 (putting the estimates together):

On the intersection of the events in which the assertions of Step 1 and Step 2 hold, set
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|x\|_{2}\leq 1
.
Write
x=y+z\in L\oplus M
and note that
\|\left\langle X,y\right\rangle\|_{L_{2}}\leq\|\left\langle X,x\right\rangle\|_{L_{2}}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\left\langle X,x\right\rangle\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}

\displaystyle\leq\|\left\langle X,y\right\rangle\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}+\|\left\langle X,z\right\rangle\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{N})}

\displaystyle\leq c_{6}(q,L)R\left(\|\left\langle X,y\right\rangle\|_{L_{2}}+\sqrt{\frac{{\rm tr}(\Sigma)}{N}}\right).

as claimed.

Step 4 (on the assumption that
\lambda_{K+1}\geq\frac{{\rm tr}(\Sigma)}{2K}
):

If the assumption is not satisfied, we proceed as follows.
Set
\xi=\frac{{\rm tr}(\Sigma)}{K}
, let
B
be the standard Bernoulli random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{K}
, independent of
X
, and put

\widetilde{X}=(X,\sqrt{\xi}B)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+K}.

Setting
\widetilde{\Sigma}=\mathrm{\mathbb{C}ov}[\widetilde{X}]
, we have
{\rm tr}(\widetilde{\Sigma})=2{\rm tr}(\Sigma)
and
\|\widetilde{\Sigma}\|_{\rm op}\geq\|\Sigma\|_{\rm op}
; thus
{\rm r}(\widetilde{\Sigma})\leq 2{\rm r}(\Sigma)
and
\lambda_{K}(\widetilde{\Sigma})\geq\xi=\frac{{\rm tr}(\widetilde{\Sigma})}{2K}
.
Moreover,
\widetilde{X}
satisfies
L_{q}-L_{2}
norm equivalence with constant
L+c(q)
, and, almost surely,
\|\widetilde{X}\|_{2}\leq\|X\|_{2}+\sqrt{{\rm tr}(\Sigma)}
and
\|P_{1:N}\widetilde{\Sigma}^{{-1/2}}\widetilde{X}\|_{2}\leq\|P_{1:N}\Sigma^{{-1/2}}X\|_{2}+\sqrt{K}
.
We repeat Step 1-3 for
\widetilde{X}
.
∎

B.3.
Classes of linear functionals indexed by
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

Let
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{A}=\{\langle\,\cdot\,,x\rangle:x\in A\}
where
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is isotropic.
Let
c_{0}
be a suitable absolute constant, set
w
to be a random variable with zero mean and unit variance that satisfies the following ‘local’ moment condition—that for some
\alpha>0
,

(B.4)

\displaystyle\|w\|_{L_{p}}\leq Lp^{1/\alpha}\|w\|_{L_{2}}\quad\text{for }2\leq p\leq c_{0}\log(ed),

and put
X=(w_{1},\dots,w_{d})
where the
w_{i}
’s are independent copies of
w
.

Remark B.6
.

Note that (
B.4
) is indeed much weaker than (
2.3
); for example
w
need not have any moments beyond
c_{0}\log(ed)
.

If the random vector
X
satisfies the local moment condition and
A
is not too small—in the sense that its critical dimension is at least logarithmic in
d
, then Assumption
2.1
is satisfied:

Lemma B.7
.

There are absolute constants
c_{0},c_{1},c_{2}
and, for every
L,\alpha>0
, there is a constant
c_{3}=c_{3}(L,\alpha)
such that the following holds.
Suppose that
w
satisfies (
B.4
) with
L
and
c_{0}
,
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies
0\in A
and
d^{\ast}(A)\geq c_{1}\log(ed)
, and
N\geq\log^{4/\alpha+1}(ed)
.
Put

\xi=\log^{2/\alpha+1}\left(\frac{ed}{d^{\ast}(A)}\right).

Then Assumption
2.1
is satisfied with set
\Omega_{0}
of measure
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{0})\geq 1-\exp(-c_{2}d^{\ast}(A))-d^{-10}
and constants
B=c_{3}
,
\theta=c_{3}\xi\gamma_{2}(A)
.

Corollary B.8
.

In the setting of Lemma
B.7
and using its notation: with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{2}d^{\ast}(A))-d^{-10}
,

\sup_{x\in A}\mathcal{W}_{1}(F_{N,x},F_{x})\leq c_{4}(L,\alpha)\frac{\max\{\xi\gamma_{2}(A)\,,\,\log^{2}(N)\}}{\sqrt{N}}.

The proof of Lemma
B.7
resembles that one of Lemma
2.7
but requires some preparations.
Denoting by
\Gamma
the matrix that has
X_{i}
as its rows, the central ingredient in the proof of Lemma
2.7
was to establish appropriate estimates on
\sup_{x}|\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\Gamma x\|_{2}^{2}-\|x\|_{2}^{2}|
, where the supremum is taken over
x
in localizations of
A
.
To establish those estimates, we will rely on the results in
[
4
]
on structure preservation of random matrices with i.i.d. columns.
Note that since each
X
has i.i.d. coordinates, the columns of
\Gamma
are i.i.d., and we denote them by
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, thus
\Gamma=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left\langle\cdot,e_{j}\right\rangle Z_{j}
.

We start by proving the (rather standard) result showing that
Z
inherits the local moment condition required in
[
4
]
.

Lemma B.9
.

There is an absolute constant
c
such that the following holds.
Let
w
be a random variable with zero mean and unit variance and set
Z=(w_{1},\dots,w_{N})
where the
w_{i}
’s are independent copies of
w
.
Then, for every
z\in S^{N-1}
and every
p\geq 2
,

\|\left\langle Z,z\right\rangle\|_{L_{p}}\leq c\sqrt{p}\|w\|_{L_{p}}.

Moreover, if
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d}
are independent copies of
Z
, then for every
p\geq 2
and for every
\lambda\geq 1
, with probability at least
1-d\lambda^{-p}
,

\max_{j=1,\dots,d}\left|\frac{\|Z_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{N}-1\right|\leq c\lambda\frac{\sqrt{p}\|w^{2}\|_{L_{p}}}{\sqrt{N}}.

Proof.

Let
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
be i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables that are also independent of
Z
.
By a standard symmetrization argument (see, e.g.,
[
16
, Lemma 6.3]
),

\|\left\langle Z,z\right\rangle\|_{L_{p}}=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}z_{i}\right\|_{L_{p}}\leq 2\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}z_{i}\right\|_{L_{p}}.

Moreover, Bernoulli random variables are subgaussian with an absolute constant, in particular
\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}v_{i}\|_{L_{p}}\leq c\sqrt{p}\|v\|_{2}
for every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
p\geq 2
.
Applying the latter inequality conditionally on
Z
,

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}z_{i}\right\|_{L_{p}}^{p}=\mathbb{E}_{Z}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}z_{i}\right|^{p}\leq\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left(c\sqrt{p}\right)^{p}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}^{2}z_{i}^{2}\right|^{p/2},

where here
\mathbb{E}_{Z}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}
denote the expectation with respect to
Z
and
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
, respectively.
Let
B_{1}^{N}
be the
\ell_{1}^{N}
-unit ball and observe that

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in B_{2}^{N}}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}^{2}z_{i}^{2}\right|^{p/2}=\sup_{v\in B_{1}^{N}}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}^{2}v_{i}\right|^{p/2}=\max_{v\in B_{1}^{N}}\psi(v).

As
\psi
is convex, it follows that
\max_{v\in B_{1}^{N}}\psi(v)
is attained at an extreme point of
B_{1}^{N}
, i.e. for
v
of the form
v=\pm e_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq N
; hence

\max_{v\in B_{1}^{N}}\psi(v)=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left|w_{i}^{2}\right|^{p/2}=\|w\|_{L_{p}}^{p}.

Combining all estimates,
\|\left\langle Z,z\right\rangle\|_{L_{p}}\leq 2c\sqrt{p}\|w\|_{L_{p}}
.

As for the second statement, by a standard symmetrization argument followed by the arguments presented in the previous step,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\|Z\|_{2}^{2}}{N}-1\right\|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{1}{N}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}^{2}\right\|_{L_{p}}\leq\frac{2c\sqrt{p}\|w^{2}\|_{L_{p}}}{\sqrt{N}}.

Hence, by Markov’s inequality, for every
\lambda\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\|Z\|_{2}^{2}}{N}-1\right|\geq\lambda\frac{2c\sqrt{p}\|w^{2}\|_{L_{p}}}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\leq\lambda^{-p},

and the claim follows from the union bound over
j=1,\dots,d
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
B.7
.

Set
Z=(w_{1},\dots,w_{N})
.
The first part of Lemma
B.9
implies that for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and every
2\leq p\leq c_{0}\log(d)
,

\|\left\langle Z,z\right\rangle\|_{L_{p}}\leq c_{1}Lp^{1/\alpha+1/2}\|z\|_{2}

and the second part of that lemma (applied with
\lambda=e^{11}
and
p=\log(d)
) shows that, with probability at least
1-d^{-10}
,

\max_{j=1,\dots,d}\left|\frac{\|Z_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{N}-1\right|\leq c_{2}L\frac{\log^{2/\alpha+1/2}(d)}{\sqrt{N}}.

Thus
Z
satisfies the assumption needed in
[
4
]
and it follows from Theorem 1.5 therein that for every set
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying
d^{\ast}(V)\geq\log(d)
, setting

\mathcal{E}(V)=\frac{\log^{2/\alpha+1/2}(d)}{\sqrt{N}}d_{V}^{2}+\log^{2/\alpha+1}\left(\frac{ed}{d^{\ast}(V)}\right)\left(d_{V}\frac{\gamma_{2}(V)}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{\gamma_{2}^{2}(V)}{N}\right),

with probability at least
1-d^{-10}-2\exp(-c_{3}d^{\ast}(V))
,

(B.5)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in V}\left|\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\Gamma x\right\|_{2}^{2}-\|x\|_{2}^{2}\right|\leq c_{4}(L,\alpha)\mathcal{E}(V).

From this point, the proof of the lemma follows from the same path as the one presented for Lemma
2.7
and we only sketch it:
Set
\xi=\log^{2/\alpha+1}(\frac{ed}{d^{\ast}(A)}),

and put
r=\xi\frac{\gamma_{2}(A)}{\sqrt{N}}
.
Apply (
B.5
) to

A_{r}=\left\{x\in{\rm conv}(\{y-z:y,z\in A\cup\{0\}\}):\|x\|_{2}\leq r\right\}.

The claim follows by noting that
d^{\ast}(A_{r})\geq c_{5}d^{\ast}(A)\geq c_{6}\log(d)
and that
\mathcal{E}(A_{r})\leq c_{7}(L,\alpha)r^{2}
, where the latter holds because
N\geq\log^{4/\alpha+1}(d)
.
∎
