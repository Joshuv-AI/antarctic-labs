On a conjecture of distance spectral extremal problems
Hongzhang Chen, Jianxi Li, Yongtao Li

Abstract
Brualdi and Hoffman proposed a well-known problem of determining the graph with maximum adjacency spectral radius among all graphs with given size m m . Early work by Friedland and Stanley addressed some specific cases. This problem was later completely solved by Rowlinson and recently revisited by Cheng and Weng. Pioneering work on the distance matrix was carried out by Graham and Pollak, as well as by Graham and Lovász. The distance spectral radius ρ ⁡ ( G ) \rho(G) of a connected graph G G is the largest eigenvalue of its distance matrix. In this paper, we completely solve the problem of characterizing the connected graph with minimum distance spectral radius among all graphs with size m m . Let 𝒢 ⁡ ( m ) \mathcal{G}(m) be the class of connected graphs with m m edges. For every m ⩾ 3 m\geqslant 3 , let n n be the unique integer satisfying ( n − 1 2 ) < m ⩽ ( n 2 ) \binom{n-1}{2}<m\leqslant\binom{n}{2} , and we write m = ( n − 1 2 ) + s m=\binom{n-1}{2}+s with 1 ⩽ s ⩽ n − 1 1\leqslant s\leqslant n-1 . Recently, Lin and Zhou [ Adv. in Appl. Math. 173 (2026) ] investigated the graph in 𝒢 ⁡ ( m ) \mathcal{G}(m) that minimizes ρ ⁡ ( G ) \rho(G) in the range s ⩾ max ⁡ { n − 6 2 , 1 } s\geqslant\max\{\frac{n-6}{2},1\} . However, the problem is much more difficult in the remaining range 1 ⩽ s ⩽ n − 7 2 1\leqslant s\leqslant\frac{n-7}{2} , and they conjectured that the unique minimizer is P n , s + 1 ¯ \overline{P_{n,s+1}} , the complement of a balanced disjoint union of paths. Using novel matrix analysis, we solve this conjecture in the affirmative. Moreover, we provide a new unified proof for the entire range 1 ⩽ s ⩽ n − 1 1\leqslant s\leqslant n-1 . The key ingredients in our proof argument include an innovative comparison principle for the distance spectral radius, an increment analysis of Φ \Phi -functions on paths and cycles, an argument for balancing path lengths, and a walk enumeration technique via the Neumann series.



Key words:
Distance spectral radius, Lin–Zhou’s conjecture.

Introduction

Let
G
be a simple connected graph with vertex set
V(G)
and edge set
E(G)
.
We denote by
n
and
e(G)
the
order
and
size
of
G
, respectively.
The complement of
G
is denoted as
\overline{G}
. For any vertex
u\in V(G)
, let

d_{G}(u)
and
N_{G}(u)
(or
d(u)
and
N(u)
for short) be the degree and the set of neighbors of
u
, respectively. Clearly,
d_{G}(u)=|N_{G}(u)|
.
We write
\mathbf{1}
for the all-ones column vector and
J=\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}
for the all-ones matrix. Let
I
be the identity matrix.
We denote by
C_{\ell}
the cycle on
\ell
vertices, and
P_{k}
the path on
k
vertices.
The adjacency matrix of an
n
-vertex graph
G
is defined as
A(G)=[a_{ij}]_{i,j=1}^{n}
, where
a_{ij}=1
if
ij\in E(G)
, and
a_{ij=0}
otherwise. The spectral radius
\lambda(G)
of a graph
G
is defined as the largest eigenvalue of its adjacency matrix
A(G)
.

1.1
The Brualdi–Hoffman type extremal problem

The study of bounding the spectral radius of a graph in terms of the number of edges has a long and rich history. It is well-known that
\lambda(G)<\sqrt{2m}
. Tracking back to 1985,
Brualdi and Hoffman
[
4
]
proved that if
G
has
m\leqslant{k\choose 2}
edges for some integer
k\geqslant 2
, then
\lambda(G)\leqslant k-1
. This bound was further extended by Friedland
[
7
]
, and later by Stanley
[
22
]
who showed that
\lambda(G)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{8m+1}-1)
, with equality if and only if
m={k\choose 2}
and
G
is a complete graph
K_{k}
. Here, we ignore the possible isolated vertices if there are no confusions.
We write
m={s\choose 2}+t
for some integers
s,t
with
0\leqslant t\leqslant s-1
.
Let
K_{1}\vee_{t}K_{s}
be the graph obtained from
K_{s}
by adding a new vertex joining

t
vertices of
K_{s}
. In 1988, Rowlinson
[
19
]
proved that if
G
has
m
edges, then

\lambda(G)\leqslant\lambda(K_{1}\vee_{t}K_{s})
,
with equality if and only if
G=K_{1}\vee_{t}K_{s}
.
This confirms a conjecture proposed by Brualdi and Hoffman
[
4
]
. Roughly speaking,
the graph with the largest adjacency spectral radius packs almost all edges into the clique, and then attaches the remaining edges to that clique.
We refer to
[
5
]
for a matrix realization of these spectral bounds.

Motivated by the Turán theorem, Nikiforov
[
18
]

significantly improved the above bounds by proving that if
G
is a
K_{r+1}
-free graph, then
\lambda^{2}(G)\leqslant(1-\frac{1}{r})2m
. In particular, this recovers a result of Nosal, saying that every
m
-edge triangle-free
G
satisfies
\lambda(G)\leqslant\sqrt{m}
.
For related results, we refer the readers to
[
12
,
11
,
13
]
.
Recently, Li, Liu and Zhang
[
14
]
proved that for any graph
F
with chromatic number
r+1\geqslant 3
, if
G
is an
F
-free graph with
m
edges, then
\lambda^{2}(G)\leqslant\big(1-\frac{1}{r}+o(1)\big)2m
. Extending Nikiforov’s theorem, Li, Liu and Zhang
[
15
]
proved that the
o(1)
error term can be removed for color-critical graphs
F
; and
\lambda(G)\leqslant\sqrt{m}+O(1)
for almost-bipartite graphs
F
.

1.2
The distance spectral extremal problem

The distance between two vertices
u,v
, denoted by
d_{G}(u,v)
, is the length of a shortest path connecting them. The
distance matrix
of
G
is defined as
D(G)=(d_{G}(u,v))_{u,v\in V(G)}
.
The
distance spectral radius

\rho(G)
is defined as the largest eigenvalue of
D(G)
.
Note that
D(G)
is nonnegative and irreducible. By the Perron–Frobenius theorem,
\rho(G)
is a positive simple eigenvalue, and there exists a unique positive unit eigenvector corresponding to
\rho(G)
.
The study of eigenvalues of the distance matrix of a connected graph dates back to the classical work
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
, where they provided an inequality related the number of negative distance eigenvalues
to the addressing problem in data communication system.
The properties of distance matrix turn out to be significant in the study of the isometric embedding problems from a connected graph to the squashed hypercube; see
[
23
,
21
,
1
]
.
We refer to the comprehensive surveys
[
2
,
16
]
for recent development.

For connected graphs of fixed order
n
, the extremal problems are classical: Ruzieh and Powers
[
20
]
and Stevanović and Ilić
[
24
]
showed that the complete graph
K_{n}
uniquely minimizes
\rho
, while the path
P_{n}
uniquely maximizes
\rho
.
In contrast, the adjacency spectral radius exhibits the opposite behavior: the complete graph
K_{n}
is the unique maximizer, and the path
P_{n}
is the unique minimizer.
Here, we provide an explanation for this reversed phenomenon.
The adjacency spectral radius
\lambda(G)
measures how well a graph expands or how strongly it is connected: a large value indicates a dense graph with many edges and small diameter (e.g., a clique). In contrast, the distance spectral radius
\rho(G)
measures the overall separation among vertices: a large value means many pairs of vertices are far apart, so the graph is sparse (e.g., a path or a star). Hence, dense graphs tend to have large
\lambda(G)
but small
\rho(G)
, while sparse graphs tend to have small
\lambda(G)
but large
\rho(G)
.

A much harder problem is to find the extremal graphs among all connected graphs with a prescribed number of edges
m
, which was initiated by Lin and Zhou
[
17
]
. Let
\mathcal{G}(m)
denote the class of such graphs. Throughout this paper, for a given
m\geqslant 3
, we denote

n:=\Bigl\lceil\tfrac{1+\sqrt{8m+1}}{2}\Bigr\rceil,\qquad s:=m-\binom{n-1}{2}\in\{1,2,\ldots,n-1\}.

(1)

For positive integers
N\geqslant c\geqslant 1
, we define

P_{N,c}\,:=\;\bigl(c+c\lfloor N/c\rfloor-N\bigr)\,P_{\lfloor N/c\rfloor}\;\cup\;\bigl(N-c\lfloor N/c\rfloor\bigr)\,P_{\lceil N/c\rceil},

the
balanced disjoint union
of
c
paths on
N
vertices (its parts differ in order by at most one).
By the definition, it is easy to verify that
\overline{P_{n,s+1}}
contains

{n\choose 2}-(n-s-1)={n-1\choose 2}+s=m
edges. Intuitively, we see that
\overline{P_{n,s+1}}
is close to almost-complete graph, which is obtained from the largest possible
K_{n}
by deleting the edges of those
s+1
balanced vertex-disjoint paths.

Recently, Lin and Zhou
[
17
, Theorem 1.3]
proved that if
\max\{\frac{n-6}{2},1\}\leqslant s\leqslant n-1
, then
\overline{P_{n,s+1}}
is the unique graph that minimizes the distance spectral radius over all graphs in
\mathcal{G}(m)
. In the concluding remarks of
[
17
]
, they conjectured that the same formula holds for all small
s
.

Conjecture 1.1

(Lin–Zhou
[
17
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph with
m
edges, and let
n
and
s
be defined as in (
1
). If

1\leqslant s\leqslant\frac{n-7}{2}
, then

\rho(G)\geqslant\rho\bigl(\overline{P_{n,s+1}}\bigr),

with equality if and only if
G\cong\overline{P_{n,s+1}}
.

In this paper, we resolve Conjecture
1.1
by showing the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G\in\mathcal{G}(m)
minimize
\rho
of graphs in
\mathcal{G}(m)
, and let
n=\lceil\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{8m+1})\rceil
and
s=m-\binom{n-1}{2}
.
If
1\leqslant s<\frac{n-2}{2}
, then
G\cong\overline{P_{n,s+1}}
. In particular, Conjecture
1.1
holds.

Remark.
In the case
s=0
, the above result is invalid. In this setting,
we have
m={n-1\choose 2}
, the minimizer is uniquely the complete graph
K_{n-1}
, instead of the expected graph
\overline{P_{n,1}}=\overline{P_{n}}
.

The range
\max\{\frac{n-6}{2},1\}\leqslant s\leqslant n-1
was already investigated by Lin and Zhou
[
17
]
, which together with the range in Theorem
1.2
yields the following
complete solution
of the distance spectral extremal problem for graphs with given size
m
.

Theorem 1.3
.

For every integer
m\geqslant 3
, let
n=\lceil\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{8m+1})\rceil
and
s=m-\binom{n-1}{2}
.
Then for all
1\leqslant s\leqslant n-1
, the graph
\overline{P_{n,s+1}}
uniquely minimizes
\rho
over all graphs in
\mathcal{G}(m)
.

As an application of our framework, we shall provide
a new self-contained proof
for
s\geqslant\frac{n-2}{2}
in Section
3
, thereby giving a unified treatment of Theorem
1.3
for the entire range
1\leqslant s\leqslant n-1
.

Before the proof, we first illustrate the difficulty for the small
s
.
To appreciate why the range of small
s
was much difficult and left open, we now recall how the proof argument of
[
17
, Theorem 1.3]
worked for large
s
.
A structural lemma (we state it as in Theorem
2.1
below) shows that any minimizer
G
has complement

\overline{G}=C_{\ell_{1}}\cup\cdots\cup C_{\ell_{t}}\cup P_{k_{1}}\cup\cdots\cup P_{k_{s+1}}
, where
\ell_{i}\geqslant 3
,
k_{j}\geqslant 2

and the number of path components equals
s+1
. The problem is therefore to show that the extremal graph is
t=0
together with the balanced path partition
P_{n,s+1}
.
Lin and Zhou’s argument proceeds by local edge-switching: given two candidate configurations, one exchanges a single edge and uses a Rayleigh quotient to show
\rho
strictly decreases. For large
s
, this works well because the number of candidate configurations is small and can be enumerated. In fact, when
s=\lceil\frac{1}{2}(n-5)\rceil
, the only cyclic candidate is
C_{3}\cup\tfrac{n-3}{2}K_{2}
, and a single edge-switch to
P_{n,(n-3)/2}
suffices.

However, when
s
is small, the number of possible cycle structures grows without bound, and as one can verify numerically, no single step edge-switch is monotone. There exist cyclic configurations
G
such that every local rearrangement, which breaks one cycle and reconnects one path, either fails to be admissible or increases
\rho
. The following example with
n=11
and
s=1
illustrates this.

\rho(\overline{C_{3}\cup P_{4}\cup P_{4}})\approx 11.65444,\qquad\rho(\overline{P_{3}\cup P_{8}})\approx 11.65452.

The natural operation, which breaks the
C_{3}
into
P_{3}
and merges the two
P_{4}
’s into
P_{8}
, increases the value of
\rho
, even though the minimum
\rho(\overline{P_{11,2}})=\rho(\overline{P_{5}\cup P_{6}})\approx 11.65442
is strictly smaller than both of the above two graphs.
In other words, the minimum can
not
be reached by a monotone sequence of single-step improvements of the edge-switch operation. To overcome the difficulty, a global argument to compare the distance spectral radii of graphs is required.

Our approach.

We get rid of the use of local edge-switching operations as it fails to be monotone for small integer
s
. Instead, we develop a new global comparison framework grounded from matrix analysis.
Our approach can be partitioned into three stages.

The first stage lies in linking the distance matrix
D(G)
to the adjacency matrix
A(H_{0})
of the complement
H_{0}=\overline{G}
. We show an identity
D(G)=J-I+A(H_{0})
, and that
\rho(G)
is the unique root of the equation
\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}((\rho+1)I-A(H_{0}))^{-1}\mathbf{1}=1
; see Lemma
2.3
.
For a graph
K
, we denote

\Phi_{K}(\lambda):=\bm{1}^{\mathsf{T}}(\lambda I-A(K))^{-1}\bm{1}
.
Let
\mathcal{C}(H_{0})
be the set of components of
H_{0}
. We define the
\Psi
-function of
G
as
\Psi_{G}(\lambda):=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{C}(H_{0})}\Phi_{K}(\lambda)
; see Definition
2.4
.
Under the above notation, we reduce the problem of comparing distance spectral radii to evaluating these
\Psi
-functions; see Theorem
2.5
.

The second stage is to compute the explicit formula of
\Phi_{K}(\lambda)
when
K
is chosen as a path and cycle; see Lemma
2.6
. Based on these formulas, we then perform a rigorous increment analysis of
\Phi_{K}(\lambda)
when
K
is a path. We provide an upper bound on the increment and establish a strict convexity property, showing that the map
k\mapsto\Phi_{P_{k}}(\lambda)
is strictly convex on
k
; see Theorem
2.7
.

The third stage is an argument for balancing path lengths.
The properties from the second stage allow us to formulate balancing principles, demonstrating
that the complement
H_{0}
of the extremal graph must consist exclusively of paths of nearly equal lengths; see Lemma
2.8
and Claim
2.1
. Moreover, we show that
H_{0}
must be devoid of any cycles; see Lemma
2.9
and Claim
2.2
.

Finally, we present a short self-contained proof in the range
s\geqslant\frac{n-2}{2}
.
By expanding the
\Psi
-function via the Neumann series, we see that

\Psi_{G}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{w_{k}(H_{0})}{\lambda^{k+1}}
,
where
w_{k}(H_{0})
denotes the total number of walks of length
k
in
H_{0}
, we integrate the comparison principle in Theorem
2.5
with walk enumeration to provide
a new unified proof
covering the entire range
1\leqslant s\leqslant n-1
.

We anticipate that the method developed in this paper will offer an efficient approach to solving additional extremal problems related to graphs and their distance spectral radii.

2
Proof of the main result

In order to prove Theorem
1.2
,
we need to present some preliminary results.
In the range
1\leqslant s<\frac{n-2}{2}
,
the expected extremal graphs in
\mathcal{G}(m)
are restricted by the following result.

Theorem 2.1

(Lin–Zhou
[
17
]
)
.

Let
G\in\mathcal{G}(m)
be a graph that minimizes
\rho
of graphs in
\mathcal{G}(m)
.
Then
|V(G)|=n
. In addition, if
1\leqslant s<\frac{n-2}{2}
, then each component of
\overline{G}
is either a cycle or a non-trivial path, and there are exactly
s+1
components being paths in
\overline{G}
.

Throughout the rest of the paper, we denote by

H_{0}=\overline{G}=C_{\ell_{1}}\cup\cdots\cup C_{\ell_{t}}\;\cup\;P_{k_{1}}\cup\cdots\cup P_{k_{s+1}},

where
t\geqslant 0,\ \ell_{i}\geqslant 3,\ k_{j}\geqslant 2
satisfying

N_{c}:=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\ell_{i}
and
N_{p}:=\sum_{j=1}^{s+1}k_{j}=n-N_{c}.

Based on this result, our goal is to prove that
t=0
(no cycles) and the path lengths
k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{s+1}
are as equal as possible. We point out that this is not as easy as it looks. To achieve this goal, we need to establish a series of important lemmas and theorems.
We will now present them one by one.

The following lemma from Matrix Analysis can be found in
[
3
, page 7]
.

Lemma 2.2

(Neumann series, e.g.,
[
3
]
)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be a square matrix.
If
\|A\|<1
for some matrix norm (or equivalently,
the spectral radius
\lambda(A)<1
), then
I-A
is invertible and

(I-A)^{-1}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}A^{i}=I+A+A^{2}+A^{3}+\cdots

where the series converges in any matrix norm.

2.1
New comparison principle via matrix analysis

The next lemma is the cornerstone of our approach.
We establish an interesting relation between the distance spectral radius
\rho(G)
and the adjacency matrix of
H_{0}
.
The diameter of a connected graph
G
is defined to be the maximum distance between any two vertices in
G
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
H_{0}
be a disjoint union of cycles and paths, with at least two components, and let
G=\overline{H_{0}}
on
n\geqslant 4
vertices. Let
A(H_{0})
be the adjacency matrix of
H_{0}
.
Then

(i)

G
is connected with diameter exactly
2
,
and
D(G)=J-I+A(H_{0})
.

(ii)

\rho:=\rho(G)
is the unique root in
(2,\infty)
of the equation

\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl((\rho+1)I-A(H_{0})\bigr)^{-1}\mathbf{1}=1.

(2)

(iii)

Every entry of
\bigl(\lambda I-A(H_{0})\bigr)^{-1}

is positive and strictly decreasing in
\lambda
on
(2,\infty)
. In particular, the left-hand side of (
2
) is strictly decreasing in
\rho
.

Proof of (i).

By definition, it is easy to see that
G
is a connected graph with diameter
2
.
For any two distinct
u,v\in V(G)
, we see that
d_{G}(u,v)=1
if and only if
uv\notin E(H_{0})
; and
d_{G}(u,v)=2
if and only if
uv\in E(H_{0})
. Thus, it follows that

d_{G}(u,v)=1+\mathbf{1}_{\{uv\in E(H_{0})\}}\;=\;(J-I)_{uv}+(A(H_{0}))_{uv}.

For
u=v
, both sides are
0
. Hence, we obtain
D(G)=J-I+A(H_{0})
, as needed.

Proof of (ii).
Since
D(G)
is irreducible and nonnegative, the Perron–Frobenius theorem yields a strictly positive eigenvector
\bm{x}>0
associated with
\rho
. Normalize so that
\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}=1
. From part (i),

\rho\,\bm{x}=D(G)\bm{x}=\big(J-I+A(H_{0})\big)\bm{x}=\mathbf{1}(\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x})-\bm{x}+A(H_{0})\bm{x}=\mathbf{1}-\bm{x}+A(H_{0})\bm{x}.

Rearranging the above gives

\bigl((\rho+1)I-A(H_{0})\bigr)\bm{x}=\mathbf{1}.

(3)

We next show that the matrix
(\rho+1)I-A(H_{0})
is invertible. Since every component
K
of
H_{0}
is a cycle or a path, the spectral radius
\lambda(H_{0})\leqslant 2
.
Since each vertex has
\deg_{H_{0}}(u)\geqslant 1
, we have
\deg_{G}(u)\leqslant n-2
.
On the other hand,
since
G
has diameter
2
, every row sum of
D(G)
equals to
2(n-1)-\deg_{G}(u)\geqslant n
.
By the Perron–Frobenius theorem, we have
\rho(G)\geqslant n\geqslant 4
. Therefore
\rho+1\geqslant n+1>2\geqslant\lambda(H_{0})
, and
(\rho+1)I-A(H_{0})
is invertible. Solving (
3
) yields

\bm{x}=\big((\rho+1)I-A(H_{0})\big)^{-1}\mathbf{1}.

multiplying
\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}
of both sides and using the assumption
\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}=1
gives the desired identity (
2
).

Proof of (iii).
For
\lambda>2\geqslant\lambda(H_{0})
, the Neumann series in Lemma
2.2
gives

\big(\lambda I-A(H_{0})\big)^{-1}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{A(H_{0})^{i}}{\lambda^{i+1}},

where the series converges entrywise. Since
A(H_{0})
is nonnegative,
each entry of the partial sums is a nonnegative, strictly decreasing function of
\lambda
, and the same holds in the limit. In particular, the left-hand side of (
2
) equals
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{-(i+1)}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}A(H_{0})^{i}\mathbf{1}
, whose
i=0
term
\frac{n}{\lambda}
is already strictly decreasing, so the whole sum is strictly decreasing in
\lambda
on
(2,\infty)
. This proves (iii), and together with the identity (
2
) at
\lambda=\rho+1
, also
show that (
2
) has a unique root in
(2,\infty)
.
∎

For each component
K
of
H_{0}
and
\lambda>2
, we define

\Phi_{K}(\lambda):=\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl(\lambda I-A(K)\bigr)^{-1}\mathbf{1}.

Next, we extend the notation to the graph
H_{0}
.
Since
A(H_{0})
is block-diagonal with each block corresponding to a component of
H_{0}
, its inverse matrix
(\lambda I-A(H_{0}))^{-1}
is also block-diagonal.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
H_{0}=\overline{G}
be a disjoint union of cycles and paths, with at least two components.
Denote
\mathcal{C}(H_{0})
the set of components of
H_{0}
. We define the
\Psi
-function of
G
as follows:

\Psi_{G}(\lambda):=\Phi_{H_{0}}(\lambda)=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{C}(H_{0})}\Phi_{K}(\lambda).

Thus, Lemma
2.3
(ii) gives
\Psi_{G}(\rho(G)+1)=1
.
Lemma
2.3
(iii) implies that
\Phi_{H_{0}}(\lambda)
is strictly decreasing in
\lambda\in(2,\infty)
.
By Definition
2.4
, we know that
\Psi_{G}(\lambda)

is also strictly decreasing in
\lambda
.
Comparing the distance spectral radius of two graphs reduces to comparing their
\Psi
-functions.
The following theorem can be viewed as a key innovation in this paper to solve Conjecture
1.1
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
G_{1},G_{2}
be two graphs satisfying the hypotheses of Lemma
2.3
, with corresponding
\Psi
-functions
\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}
, respectively. If
\Psi_{1}(\lambda)<\Psi_{2}(\lambda)
for every
\lambda>2
, then
\rho(G_{1})<\rho(G_{2})
.
More generally, if the strict inequality holds merely at

\lambda=\rho(G_{2})+1
, then
\rho(G_{1})<\rho(G_{2})
.

Proof.

Setting
\lambda_{i}:=\rho(G_{i})+1
, we have
\Psi_{i}(\lambda_{i})=1
.
The assumption implies

\Psi_{1}(\lambda_{2})<\Psi_{2}(\lambda_{2})=1=\Psi_{1}(\lambda_{1}).

Since
\Psi_{1}(\lambda)
is strictly decreasing by Lemma
2.3
, we have
\lambda_{2}>\lambda_{1}
, i.e.,
\rho(G_{2})>\rho(G_{1})
.
∎

Remark.

By applying Theorem

, we no longer compare the increment of the distance spectral radius via the entries of Perron eigenvector and the Rayleigh quotient of
\rho(G)
.

2.2
The increment of
\Phi
-functions on paths and cycles

We now compute
\Phi_{K}(\lambda)
in closed form for the two types of components relevant here.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\lambda>2
and denote
\theta:=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\lambda+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4}\bigr)>1
, so that
\lambda=\theta+\theta^{-1}
.

(i)

For any cycle
C_{\ell}
with
\ell\geqslant 3
, we have

\Phi_{C_{\ell}}(\lambda)\;=\;\frac{\ell}{\lambda-2}.

(ii)

For any path
P_{k}
with
k\geqslant 2
, we have

\Phi_{P_{k}}(\lambda)\;=\;\frac{k}{\lambda-2}-\frac{2}{\lambda-2}R_{k}(\theta),\quad\text{where}\quad R_{k}(\theta):=\frac{1}{\theta-1}\!\left(1-\frac{1+\theta}{1+\theta^{k+1}}\right).

Proof of (i).

Since
C_{\ell}
is
2
-regular, we have
A(C_{\ell})\mathbf{1}=2\mathbf{1}
. Hence

(\lambda I-A(C_{\ell}))\mathbf{1}=(\lambda-2)\mathbf{1}.

Since
\lambda>2
, the matrix
\lambda I-A(C_{\ell})
is invertible. So

(\lambda I-A(C_{\ell}))^{-1}\mathbf{1}=\frac{1}{\lambda-2}\mathbf{1}.

Recall that
\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{1}=\ell
. We obtain
\Phi_{C_{\ell}}(\lambda)=\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl(\lambda I-A(C_{\ell})\bigr)^{-1}\mathbf{1}=\frac{\ell}{\lambda-2}
.

Proof of (ii).

Suppose that
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})^{\mathsf{T}}
is a vector satisfying
(\lambda I-A(P_{k}))\bm{y}=\mathbf{1}
. We need to compute
\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{y}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}
.
For
2\leqslant i\leqslant k-1
, the equation
(\lambda I-A(P_{k}))\bm{y}=\mathbf{1}
at
i
-th row reads

-y_{i-1}+\lambda y_{i}-y_{i+1}=1.

At the first and last rows (
i=1
and
i=k
), the equations gives

\lambda y_{1}-y_{2}=1,\qquad-y_{k-1}+\lambda y_{k}=1.

Introducing auxiliary values
y_{0}:=0
and
y_{k+1}:=0
, so
y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{k},y_{k+1}
satisfy

-y_{i-1}+\lambda y_{i}-y_{i+1}=1\qquad(1\leqslant i\leqslant k).

(4)

The characteristic equation of the recurrence
-y_{i-1}+\lambda y_{i}-y_{i+1}=0
is given as
x^{2}-\lambda x+1=0
, which has two real roots
\theta
and
\theta^{-1}
, where
\theta=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\lambda+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4}\bigr)
.
A particular solution by setting
y_{i}\equiv c
, gives
(-1+\lambda-1)c=1
, i.e.,
c=\frac{1}{\lambda-2}
.
Thus, the general solution of (
4
) is given as

y_{i}=\frac{1}{\lambda-2}+C_{1}\theta^{i}+C_{2}\theta^{-i},\quad(0\leqslant i\leqslant k+1)

(5)

where
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
are some constants determined later.

The boundary condition
y_{0}=0
gives

0=\frac{1}{\lambda-2}+C_{1}+C_{2}.

(6)

The condition
y_{k+1}=0
gives

0=\frac{1}{\lambda-2}+C_{1}\theta^{k+1}+C_{2}\theta^{-(k+1)}.

(7)

From (
6
), we have
C_{2}=-\tfrac{1}{\lambda-2}-C_{1}
. Substituting into (
7
) yields

0=\frac{1}{\lambda-2}+C_{1}\theta^{k+1}+\Bigl(-\frac{1}{\lambda-2}-C_{1}\Bigr)\theta^{-(k+1)}.

Collecting terms, we have

C_{1}\bigl(\theta^{k+1}-\theta^{-(k+1)}\bigr)=-\frac{1}{\lambda-2}\bigl(1-\theta^{-(k+1)}\bigr).

Multiplying by
\theta^{k+1}
both sides, we obtain

C_{1}{(\theta^{2(k+1)}-1)}=-\frac{1}{\lambda-2}{(\theta^{k+1}-1)}
,
so

C_{1}=-\frac{1}{\lambda-2}\cdot\frac{\theta^{k+1}-1}{\theta^{2(k+1)}-1}=\frac{-1}{(\lambda-2)(1+\theta^{k+1})}.

(8)

Substituting back into (
6
), it follows that

C_{2}=-\frac{1}{\lambda-2}-C_{1}=\frac{-(1+\theta^{k+1})+1}{(\lambda-2)(1+\theta^{k+1})}=\frac{-\theta^{k+1}}{(\lambda-2)(1+\theta^{k+1})}.

(9)

Combining with (
5
), we conclude that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}=\frac{k}{\lambda-2}+C_{1}\,S_{1}+C_{2}\,S_{2},

where
S_{1}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\theta^{i}=\frac{\theta(\theta^{k}-1)}{\theta-1}
and
S_{2}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\theta^{-i}=\frac{\theta^{k}-1}{\theta^{k}(\theta-1)}
.

Using (
8
) and (
9
), we have

\displaystyle C_{1}S_{1}+C_{2}S_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{-(\theta^{k}-1)}{(\lambda-2)(\theta-1)(1+\theta^{k+1})}\cdot\Bigl(\theta+\frac{\theta^{k+1}}{\theta^{k}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{-(\theta^{k}-1)}{(\lambda-2)(\theta-1)(1+\theta^{k+1})}\cdot 2\theta=\frac{-2}{\lambda-2}\cdot\frac{\theta(\theta^{k}-1)}{(\theta-1)(1+\theta^{k+1})}.

We now show that

\frac{\theta(\theta^{k}-1)}{(\theta-1)(1+\theta^{k+1})}\;=\;R_{k}(\theta).

This can be verified by rewriting the left-hand side as

\displaystyle\frac{\theta(\theta^{k}-1)}{(\theta-1)(1+\theta^{k+1})}=\frac{1}{\theta-1}\cdot\frac{(1+\theta^{k+1})-(1+\theta)}{1+\theta^{k+1}}=\frac{1}{\theta-1}\!\left(1-\frac{1+\theta}{1+\theta^{k+1}}\right)=R_{k}(\theta).

Putting all the above identities together, we conclude that

\Phi_{P_{k}}(\lambda)=\bm{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\big(\lambda I-A(P_{k})\big)^{-1}\bm{1}=\bm{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\bm{y}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}=\frac{k}{\lambda-2}-\frac{2}{\lambda-2}R_{k}(\theta).

This proves the desired identity in part (ii).
∎

This section contains the main technical result of the paper.

Theorem 2.7
.

For every
\lambda>2
and
k\geqslant 2
, let
\theta>1
be the root of
\theta+\theta^{-1}=\lambda
. Then

\Phi_{P_{k+1}}(\lambda)-\Phi_{P_{k}}(\lambda)\;=\;\frac{1}{\lambda-2}\cdot\frac{(\theta^{k+1}-1)(\theta^{k+2}-1)}{(1+\theta^{k+1})(1+\theta^{k+2})}.

(10)

Consequently, we obtain the following two statements:

(i)

For all
k\geqslant 2
and
m\geqslant 1
, we have

0<\Phi_{P_{k+m}}(\lambda)-\Phi_{P_{k}}(\lambda)<\frac{m}{\lambda-2}
.

(ii)

The map
k\mapsto\Phi_{P_{k}}(\lambda)
is strictly convex on
\{k\in\mathbb{N}:k\geqslant 2\}
.

Proof.

Applying Lemma
2.6
(ii), it follows that

\Phi_{P_{k+1}}(\lambda)-\Phi_{P_{k}}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\lambda-2}-\frac{2}{\lambda-2}\big(R_{k+1}(\theta)-R_{k}(\theta)\big).

(11)

We denote

g(j):=\frac{1}{1+\theta^{j}}
.
By definition, we get
R_{k}(\theta)=\tfrac{1}{\theta-1}\bigl(1-(1+\theta)g(k+1)\bigr)
, so

R_{k+1}(\theta)-R_{k}(\theta)=-\frac{1+\theta}{\theta-1}\bigl(g(k+2)-g(k+1)\bigr).

(12)

We now compute the difference
g(j+1)-g(j)
and obtain

g(j+1)-g(j)=\frac{1}{1+\theta^{j+1}}-\frac{1}{1+\theta^{j}}=\frac{\theta^{j}-\theta^{j+1}}{(1+\theta^{j})(1+\theta^{j+1})}=-\frac{\theta^{j}(\theta-1)}{(1+\theta^{j})(1+\theta^{j+1})}.

Replacing with
j=k+1
, we get

g(k+2)-g(k+1)=-\frac{\theta^{k+1}(\theta-1)}{(1+\theta^{k+1})(1+\theta^{k+2})}.

Substituting into (
12
), we obtain

R_{k+1}(\theta)-R_{k}(\theta)=-\frac{1+\theta}{\theta-1}\cdot\left(-\frac{\theta^{k+1}(\theta-1)}{(1+\theta^{k+1})(1+\theta^{k+2})}\right)=\frac{(1+\theta)\,\theta^{k+1}}{(1+\theta^{k+1})(1+\theta^{k+2})}.

Now we need to compute
1-2(R_{k+1}(\theta)-R_{k}(\theta))
. Note that

\displaystyle 1-2(R_{k+1}(\theta)-R_{k}(\theta))=1-\frac{2(1+\theta)\theta^{k+1}}{(1+\theta^{k+1})(1+\theta^{k+2})}=\frac{(1+\theta^{k+1})(1+\theta^{k+2})-2(1+\theta)\theta^{k+1}}{(1+\theta^{k+1})(1+\theta^{k+2})},

where the numerator can be expanded as

1+\theta^{k+1}+\theta^{k+2}+\theta^{2k+3}-2\theta^{k+1}-2\theta^{k+2}=1-\theta^{k+1}-\theta^{k+2}+\theta^{2k+3}=(\theta^{k+1}-1)(\theta^{k+2}-1).

Therefore, we conclude that

1-2(R_{k+1}-R_{k})=\frac{(\theta^{k+1}-1)(\theta^{k+2}-1)}{(1+\theta^{k+1})(1+\theta^{k+2})}.

(13)

Combining (
13
) and (
11
) yields the expected identity (
10
).

Proof of (i).

For every
\theta>1
and
j\geqslant 1
, we have

0<\frac{\theta^{j}-1}{1+\theta^{j}}<1
.
Then

0\;<\;\frac{\theta^{k+1}-1}{1+\theta^{k+1}}\cdot\frac{\theta^{k+2}-1}{1+\theta^{k+2}}\;<\;1.

Combining this with (
10
), we have

0<\Phi_{P_{k+1}}(\lambda)-\Phi_{P_{k}}(\lambda)<\frac{1}{\lambda-2}.

Telescoping the above inequality from
k
to
k+m
, we get

\Phi_{P_{k+m}}(\lambda)-\Phi_{P_{k}}(\lambda)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\bigl(\Phi_{P_{k+j+1}}(\lambda)-\Phi_{P_{k+j}}(\lambda)\bigr)<\frac{m}{\lambda-2}.

Proof of (ii).

Using (
10
), we denote the increment

\Delta\Phi_{k}:=\Phi_{P_{k+1}}(\lambda)-\Phi_{P_{k}}(\lambda)=\frac{h(k)}{\lambda-2},

(14)

where

h(k):=\frac{(\theta^{k+1}-1)(\theta^{k+2}-1)}{(1+\theta^{k+1})(1+\theta^{k+2})}=\prod_{j=1}^{2}\frac{\theta^{k+j}-1}{\theta^{k+j}+1}=\prod_{j=1}^{2}\left(1-\frac{2}{\theta^{k+j}+1}\right).

For each
j\in\{1,2\}
, since
\theta>1
makes
\theta^{k+j}
strictly increasing in
k
, and
2/(\theta^{k+j}+1)
strictly decreasing, then the factor
1-\frac{2}{\theta^{k+j}+1}
is strictly increasing in
k
. Hence
h(k)
is strictly increasing in
k
, giving
\Delta\Phi_{k+1}>\Delta\Phi_{k}
.
Consequently, we obtain the convexity

\Phi_{P_{k+2}}(\lambda)+\Phi_{P_{k}}(\lambda)>2\Phi_{P_{k+1}}(\lambda)
.
∎

2.3
Balancing principles

We now derive two results: first, for a fixed total order and fixed number of parts, path lengths should be as balanced as possible (Lemma
2.8
); second, enlarging the total order monotonically enlarges each part of the balanced partition (Lemma
2.9
).

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
c\geqslant 1
and
N\geqslant 2c
be integers, and let
k_{1},\ldots,k_{c}\geqslant 2
be integers with
\sum_{i=1}^{c}k_{i}=N
. We denote
(k_{i}^{\ast})_{i=1}^{c}
the balanced partition of
N
into
c
parts, i.e., with
q=\lfloor N/c\rfloor
and
r=N-qc
, there are
r
copies of
q+1
and
c-r
copies of
q
. Then for every
\lambda>2
,

\sum_{i=1}^{c}\Phi_{P_{k_{i}}}(\lambda)\;\geqslant\;\sum_{i=1}^{c}\Phi_{P_{k_{i}^{\ast}}}(\lambda),

with equality if and only if
(k_{i})_{i=1}^{c}
is a permutation of
(k_{i}^{\ast})_{i=1}^{c}
.

Proof.

If
(k_{i})_{i=1}^{c}
is already a permutation of
(k_{i}^{\ast})_{i=1}^{c}
, then two sums
are equal. Otherwise, there exist indices
i\neq i^{\prime}
with
k_{i}\geqslant k_{i^{\prime}}+2
. We consider the operation: Replace

(k_{i},k_{i^{\prime}})
by
(k_{i}-1,k_{i^{\prime}}+1)
; the sum is changed only at these
two indices, and the constraint
k_{j}\geqslant 2
is preserved (since

k_{i}\geqslant k_{i^{\prime}}+2\geqslant 4
gives
k_{i}-1\geqslant 3
and
k_{i^{\prime}}+1\geqslant 3
). Thus, the effect on
these two indices is

\left(\Phi_{P_{k_{i}-1}}(\lambda)+\Phi_{P_{k_{i^{\prime}}+1}}(\lambda)\right)-\left(\Phi_{P_{k_{i}}}(\lambda)+\Phi_{P_{k_{i^{\prime}}}}(\lambda)\right)=\Delta\Phi_{k_{i^{\prime}}}-\Delta\Phi_{k_{i}-1},

where
\Delta\Phi_{k}
is defined as in (
14
).
Since
k_{i}-1\geqslant k_{i^{\prime}}+1>k_{i^{\prime}}\geqslant 2
, and
by Theorem
2.7
(ii), we know that

\Delta\Phi_{k}
is strictly increasing in
k
, we have
\Delta\Phi_{k_{i}-1}>\Delta\Phi_{k_{i^{\prime}}}
.
So we get

\Phi_{P_{k_{i}-1}}(\lambda)+\Phi_{P_{k_{i^{\prime}}+1}}(\lambda)\,<\,\Phi_{P_{k_{i}}}(\lambda)+\Phi_{P_{k_{i^{\prime}}}}(\lambda).

We conclude that the operation strictly decreases the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{c}\Phi_{P_{k_{i}}}(\lambda)
.

Using the above operation repeatedly, we can turn
any partition
(k_{i})_{i=1}^{c}
to a balanced partition.
Indeed,
we consider the quantity
Q:=\sum_{i=1}^{c}k_{i}^{2}
. Since
k_{i}\geqslant k_{i^{\prime}}+2
, we see that

\Delta Q=(k_{i}-1)^{2}+(k_{i^{\prime}}+1)^{2}-k_{i}^{2}-k_{i^{\prime}}^{2}=-2(k_{i}-k_{i^{\prime}}-1)\leqslant-2.

Thus,
Q
strictly decreases under each step of the operation.
Since
Q\geqslant 0
, the operation process thereby terminates at finitely many times. The terminal configuration admits no pair
(k_{i},k_{i^{\prime}})
with

k_{i}\geqslant k_{i^{\prime}}+2
, i.e., it is a balanced partition of
N
. Since each operation strictly
decreased the sum, the starting value
\sum_{i=1}^{c}\Phi_{P_{k_{i}}}(\lambda)
strictly exceeds the
final value
\sum_{i=1}^{c}\Phi_{P_{k_{i}^{\ast}}}(\lambda)
.
∎

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
c\geqslant 1
and
N_{1}<N_{2}
be integers with
N_{1}\geqslant 2c
. Write
(k_{j}^{(i)})_{j=1}^{c}
for the balanced partition of
N_{i}
into
c
parts, sorted in non-decreasing order. Then for every
j\in\{1,\ldots,c\}
,

k_{j}^{(1)}\;\leqslant\;k_{j}^{(2)}.

Proof.

We denote
N_{i}=q_{i}c+r_{i}
with
0\leqslant r_{i}<c
. The sorted balanced partition of
N_{i}
is

(k_{j}^{(i)})_{j=1}^{c}=(q_{i},\ldots,q_{i},\,q_{i}+1,\ldots,q_{i}+1),

where
q_{i}
appears
c-r_{i}
times and
q_{i}+1
appears
r_{i}
times.
Since
N_{1}<N_{2}
and
c
is fixed, we have
q_{1}\leqslant q_{2}
.
If
q_{1}<q_{2}
, then every entry of
(k_{j}^{(1)})
is at most
q_{1}+1\leqslant q_{2}
. Moreover, every entry of
(k_{j}^{(2)})
is at least
q_{2}
. Thus, we get
k_{j}^{(1)}\leqslant q_{1}+1\leqslant q_{2}\leqslant k_{j}^{(2)}
for every
j
.

If
q_{1}=q_{2}=q
, then
N_{1}<N_{2}
implies
r_{1}<r_{2}
.
In this case,
N_{1}
has the partition
\{q,\ldots,q,q+1,\ldots,q+1\}
, where
q+1
appears
r_{1}
times, and
N_{2}
has the partition
\{q,\ldots,q,q+1,\ldots,q+1\}
, where
q+1
appears
r_{2}
times. Since
r_{1}<r_{2}
, we see that

k_{j}^{(1)}\leqslant k_{j}^{(2)}
for all
j
.
∎

3
A new proof for the
range
s\geqslant\frac{1}{2}(n-2)

In previous section, we determined the spectral extremal graph in the difficult case for the range
1\leqslant s<\frac{n-2}{2}
, and thereby solved Lin–Zhou’s problem in Conjecture
1.1
.
The range
s\geqslant\max\{\frac{n-6}{2},1\}
was already confirmed by Lin and Zhou
[
17
]
by using the Rayleigh quotient technique.
In this range, we cannot apply the structural result stated in Theorem
2.1
, so that we cannot use the previous lemmas for the increment analysis of
\Phi
-functions on paths and cycles.

In this section, we provide another application of our approach developed in this paper.
Applying the comparison principle in Theorem
2.5
, we give a new proof of Lin–Zhou’s result for the range
s\geqslant\frac{n-2}{2}
by combining
\Psi
-functions with walk enumeration.
Counting the number of walks in a graph turns out to be powerful in studying spectral extremal problems; see, e.g.,
[
18
,
14
,
6
]
.

Before comparing the
\Psi
-functions, we need to bound the diameter of
G
.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
G
is a graph on
n
vertices with
m>\binom{n-1}{2}
edges, then
\mathrm{diam}(G)\leqslant 2
.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that
\mathrm{diam}(G)\geqslant 3
. There exist
u,v\in V(G)
such that distance
d_{G}(u,v)\geqslant 3
. Thus
u
and
v
are non-adjacent, and
N(u)\cap N(v)=\emptyset
. We now count the missing edges (non-edges) in
G
. Observe that
u
is non-adjacent to
n-1-d(u)
vertices. In addition,
v
is non-adjacent to
d(u)
vertices of
N(u)
. So the total number of non-edges of
u,v
is at least
(n-1-d(u))+d(u)=n-1
.
Then we have
e(G)\leqslant\binom{n}{2}-(n-1)=\binom{n-1}{2}
, contradicting with the assumption
m>\binom{n-1}{2}
. Thus, the diameter of
G
is at most
2
.
∎

Recall that
e(G)=m={n-1\choose 2}+s
.
Since
s\geqslant 1
, we have
m>\binom{n-1}{2}
.
Lemma
3.1
guarantees
\mathrm{diam}(G)\leqslant 2
, which implies for any two distinct
u,v\in V(G)
,
d_{G}(u,v)=1
if and only if
uv\in E(G)
; and
