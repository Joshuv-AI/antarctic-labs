Untreated mild-moderate mitral regurgitation after isolated aortic valve replacement for aortic stenosis

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative persistent/worsening mitral regurgitation (MR) is reported to be a prognostic marker in patients undergoing isolated aortic valve replacement (AVR). However, there is little evidence regarding the surgical indication for coexisting mild-to-moderate MR in patients undergoing isolated surgical AVR. This study evaluates the relationship between untreated mild-to-moderate MR and postoperative significant MR after surgical AVR.MethodsBetween 2016 and 2021, 430 patients with severe aortic stenosis underwent isolated surgical AVR with untreated mild-to-moderate MR. The primary endpoint was significant (moderate or greater) MR at 1 year after surgery. Composite endpoint (mortality and heart failure readmission) was also retrospectively reviewed. The mean follow-up duration was 5.0 ± 2.0 years.ResultsAt 1 year after surgery, 22 patients (5%) presented with moderate or greater MR. Background

In patients undergoing isolated aortic valve replacement (AVR), the presence of preoperative moderate or greater mitral regurgitation (MR) is associated with increased perioperative morbidity and mortality, and worse long-term survival compared to patients with no or mild MR [1–5]. In contrast to transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), surgical AVR offers the option to concomitantly intervene on coexisting mitral lesions. However, given the increased operative risk of double-valve surgery and the potential for spontaneous MR improvement [6–9], a conservative approach is often favored, especially in elderly or high-risk patients [2]. Actually, for most patients with aortic valve stenosis (AS), AVR theoretically relieves left ventricular outflow stenosis (LVOTS), reduces left ventricular pressure, and relieves MR. Besides, it is known that a proportion of patients fail to improve MR after surgical AVR for AS [3, 10–12]. For those unexpected to improve MR after surgical AVR, concomitant mitral surgery is considered; however, little is known about the predictors of post-op persistent/worsening MR, especially the effect of preoperative hemodynamic status of LVOTS on postoperative MR [12–17]. Careful preoperative assessment of patient risk factors is essential for optimal management, though evidence is limited for the relationships between predictors of persistent or worsening MR after surgical AVR and long-term clinical outcomes.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the clinical fate of untreated mild to moderate MR at the time of isolated surgical AVR for predominant AS etiology. Also, the relationships between preoperative AS parameters and postoperative significant MR were assessed.

Methods

Patients

This study included 430 patients who underwent isolated surgical AVR for severe aortic valve stenosis with untreated preoperative mild to moderate MR, which was assessed at postoperative 1 year by echocardiography, between 2016 and 2021. Patients with infectious endocarditis, rheumatic mitral change, none/trivial or severe MR at baseline, mortality within 1-year after surgery, and those without 1-year echocardiography follow-up were excluded. A flow diagram depicting the selection of the patients is illustrated in Fig. 1. This study was conducted with patient data retrieved from the ACTIVIST (Accelerating Conventional and Transcatheter Integration in Valvular Intervention STrategy) database, with approval by the institutional ethics committee of Osaka University Hospital, and written informed consent from each patient was waived for this retrospective study [approval number 20222(T2)].

Participant flow chart. AS, aortic stenosis; MR, mitral regurgitation; SAVR, surgical aortic valve replacement

Grading of mitral regurgitation

Two-dimensional and Doppler echocardiography were performed before the surgery (baseline) and at 1 year after the surgery (between six months and a year and a half after surgery) to assess the severity of MR, left ventricular (LV) function, and aortic valve parameters. The severity of MR was graded as 0 (absent), 1+ (trivial), 2+ (mild), 3+ (moderate), or 4+ (severe) based on the findings of color Doppler and the spatial distribution of the regurgitant jet relative to the left atrial area.

Follow-up

The primary endpoint was significant (i.e., moderate or severe) MR at 1 year after surgery. The secondary endpoint was all-cause mortality, the composite of all-cause death and readmission for heart failure. Follow-up was completed in all patients (100%) through a review of their clinical records for a total duration of follow-up of 5.0 ± 2.0 years.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using non-parametric tests for paired and unpaired data. Categorical variables are presented as frequency and proportion, and were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The correlation between the variables was tested with Pearson’s correlation analysis. Cumulative survival and composite endpoints were calculated with the Kaplan-Meier method, and log-rank testing was performed to compare the groups. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression models were used to determine the association between baseline characteristics and postoperative moderate or greater MR. The multivariable model was constructed using variables with a P-value < 0.05 in the univariable analysis. In addition, optimal cutoff values of the peak aortic velocity and left atrial dimension index to predict postoperative moderate or greater MR were determined by receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The optimal cutoff values were defined as those providing maximal accuracy to predict postoperative moderate or greater MR after surgery. Subgroup analyses were conducted with tests for interactions in the Cox’s regression model. The results are presented as hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was determined as p < .05. JMP version 13 (SAS institute Inc, Cary, NC, USA) software was used for the statistical analyses.

Postoperative changes of MR

There were 22 patients (5%) who presented with moderate or greater MR at post-surgery 1 year (Fig. 2). At baseline, patients who showed postoperative MR had more prevalence in chronic kidney disease (50% vs. 27%, P = .0244) and moderate MR (50% vs. 19%, P < .001), lower LVEF (59% vs. 65%, P = .0211), low aortic peak velocity (4.3 vs. 4.7 m/s, P = .0324), and larger left atrial dimension index (30 vs. 27 mm/m2, P < .001) as compared with those without postoperative MR (Table 1). Univariable logistic regression analyses showed LVEF, aortic peak velocity, moderate MR, and left atrial dimension index were significantly associated with postoperative significant MR at one year after surgery. After multivariable adjustment, aortic valve peak velocity (OR, 0.44; 95% CI, 0.22–0.89; P= .021), moderate MR (OR, 11.9; 95% CI, 3.80–38.5; P = < 0.001), and left atrial dimension index (OR, 1.12; 95% CI, 1.02–1.25; P= .020) remain significantly associated with postoperative significant MR (Table 2).

Risk factors for postoperative MR

ROC curve analysis demonstrated that the optimal cutoff value of the aortic peak velocity for predicting postoperative significant MR was 4.65 m/sec, which resulted in a sensitivity of 82% and specificity of 54% with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.65 (Supplementary figure (A)). The optimal cutoff value of the left atrial dimension was also obtained as 32 mm/m2, which resulted in a sensitivity of 41% and specificity of 87% with an AUC of 0.68 (Supplementary figure (B)). When an aortic peak velocity of > 4.65, left atrial dimension index of > 32 mm/m2, and moderate MR were used as the dichotomized risk factors to define patients susceptible to postoperative significant MR, there were 46 (11%) patients with two or more baseline MR risk factors (“high-risk” group) and 384 (89%) without (“low-risk” group). The number of patients who developed significant MR was 9 (20%) in the high-risk group and 13 (3%) in the low-risk group (P < .001).

Early and long-term clinical outcomes and MR risk factors

During follow-up, there were 39 mortalities. The cause of mortality was malignancy in 6 patients (15%), heart failure in 4 (10%), and infection in 4 (10%). Overall, the composite of freedom from death or unscheduled readmission for heart failure was significantly lower in the high-risk group Sthan in the low-risk group (log-rank P=.041; Fig. 3A), whereas cumulative survival rates were not different between the groups (log-rank P=.64; Fig. 3B).

Discussion

The major findings of this study can be summarized as follows. In a specific cohort of patients with severe aortic valve stenosis and untreated mild-to-moderate MR who underwent isolated surgical AVR, (i) 5% (22/430) patients had moderate or greater MR at 1-year after surgery; (ii) multivariable analysis demonstrated that higher aortic valve peak velocity, larger left atrial dimension index, and moderate MR at preoperative baseline were predictors of postoperative significant MR; (iii) patients with multiple risk factors for postoperative MR showed inferior heart failure hospitalization-free survival at 5 years as compared with those without.

Post-operative MR after isolated surgical AVR

Observational studies have demonstrated that a greater proportion of patients undergoing isolated AVR experiences reductions in MR grade after surgery. Harling and colleagues revealed in their review article that in 807 patients with aortic stenosis and concomitant MR from six studies, postoperative MR was improved in 55.5%, while unchanged in 37.7% and worsened in 6.8% [3]. The same degree of MR improvement was also confirmed in another recent study by Kato and colleagues [12]. They reviewed 234 patients with severe AS and concurrent significant MR defined as effective mitral regurgitant orifice area (ERO) ≥ 10 mm2. After surgical or transcatheter correction of AS, 59% of patients showed improvement in MR, 29% remained unchanged, and 12% worsened. In the current study, MR was improved in 58% (249/430), unchanged in 39% (168/430), and worsened in 3% (13/430). Our results were compatible with the results of Harling and Kato. Compared to Kato’s cohort, it is interesting to notice that postoperative aortic valve area seems smaller in our cohort (1.4 vs. 2.1 cm2), which may be attributed to the mode of intervention (all surgical AVR vs. mixed surgical and transcatheter AVR), though, peak velocity (2.3 vs. 2.4 m/sec) and transaortic gradient (11 vs. 13 mmHg) were comparable. The comparable degree of MR improvement can be explained by the comparable degree of improvement in postoperative aortic hemodynamics.

This study demonstrated that patients with higher preoperative peak aortic gradients exhibited a greater likelihood of MR improvement following isolated AVR. A higher preoperative aortic valve gradient may result in a greater reduction in left ventricular afterload following relief of aortic stenosis; therefore, in patients with a similar degree of preoperative MR, a greater decrease in afterload may contribute to a more pronounced reduction in MR severity. However, whether the severity of preoperative AS correlates with postoperative MR improvement remains controversial. As noted earlier, Kato et al. reported that in cases of functional MR, postoperative improvement was more likely in patients with a smaller preoperative indexed aortic valve area, a finding consistent with our results indicating that more severe preoperative AS was associated with greater MR improvement [12]. In contrast, Kaczorowski et al. found no association between MR improvement and either the preoperative mean aortic gradient or the change in mean gradient before and after surgery in a cohort of 462 patients undergoing AVR [15]. Compared with the present study, their cohort included patients with lower preoperative ejection fraction and lower mean aortic gradients. It is plausible that, because functional MR after isolated AVR is primarily driven by intrinsic left ventricular pathology, the hemodynamic benefit of relieving LVOTS may have had a relatively limited impact on MR improvement in their population.

In the current study, an enlarged left atrium, defined as left atrial dimension index > 32 mm/2, was identified as an independent predictor of postoperative MR. This result was also confirmed with a previous report from Waisbren and colleagues [17]. They reviewed 227 patients undergoing isolated AVR for stenotic aortic valve and concomitant less than severe MR. By multivariable analysis, an enlarged left atrium was shown to be an independent predictor of postoperative MR. The enlarged left atrium has been regarded as an indicator of chronically increased left ventricular end-diastolic pressure and diastolic dysfunction. Mosquera and colleagues demonstrated that in 2022 patients who underwent aortic valve surgery, preoperative indexed left atrial diameter was as an independent predictor of all-cause mortality, as well as the combined event, with a mean follow-up of 3.2 years [18]. In patients with chronic LV pressure overload due to aortic valve stenosis, the clinical importance of indexed LA diameter should be emphasized as a predictor of postoperative MR, as well as its long-term prognostic value.

Although AVR for AS theoretically reduces left ventricular pressure load and may consequently ameliorate MR, the degree to which relief of left ventricular outflow tract obstruction translates into reduced left ventricular pressure varies across patients. When aortic stenosis coexists with MR, it is therefore challenging to predict the extent of hemodynamic improvement achievable through isolated aortic valve treatment. In contrast to transcatheter AVR, surgical intervention enables simultaneous correction of both valves. Although combined valve surgery entails greater procedural risk, the present findings suggest that consideration of the factors identified here may aid in selecting appropriate candidates for more aggressive surgical management (Fig. 4).

Limitations

There are some limitations to our study. First, this study was retrospective in nature and included a small number of patients. Second, the database used in this study did not include information regarding the etiology of MR. Whether MR etiology influences the effect of isolated surgical AVR remains a matter of debate. Kato et al. reported that MR etiology is not strongly associated with prognosis [12]. Similarly, quantitative measurements of MR severity were unavailable in this study, and changes in MR were assessed using qualitative echocardiographic grading. Preoperative and postoperative echocardiography were performed and interpreted at the respective institutions where surgery was conducted, and interobserver variability was not adjusted for in this study. Despite these limitations, contemporary evidence evaluating the impact of isolated AVR for severe AS on postoperative MR remains limited. Accordingly, the factors associated with worsening MR identified in this study may offer valuable insight when considering treatment strategies.

Conclusion

In patients undergoing isolated surgical AVR with untreated mild-to-moderate MR, preoperative aortic velocity, left atrial dimension, and moderate MR were associated with persistent/worsening MR, as well as increased heart failure readmission rates. Risk factors for postoperative significant MR need to be considered in coexisting mild-to-moderate MR when treating aortic valvular lesions.
