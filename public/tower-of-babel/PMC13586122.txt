Thin cortical translucent area of the iliac wing: morphometric analysis and implications for surgical safety
Authors: Helin Yücedağ Gündoğdu, Bahattin Paslı, Burcu Erçakmak Güneş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe iliac wing is a frequently used fixation site in the surgical management of pelvic and acetabular fractures. Achieving safe implant placement in this region necessitates a comprehensive understanding of local morphology. However, the morphometric characteristics and surgical safety margins of the thin cortical translucent area (PLA) located in the central iliac wing have not been sufficiently defined. This may pose a significant challenge in evaluating the risk of cortical perforation, particularly during implant placement. This study aimed to evaluate the morphometric characteristics of the PLA and its relationship to fixation corridors.MethodsFifty-one unpaired dry hip bones were examined. The thin cortical translucent region was identified using photoluminescence. Photograph-based measurements were conducted utilising ImageJ software on standardised photographs. Dimensions, area, distances to reference landmarks and relationships with the iliac pillar and supraacetabular corridors were assessed. The safe angular deviation range for anterior inferior iliac spine (AIIS)-origin screw fixation was calculated. Cortical thickness was measured using a digital thickness gauge.ResultsThe vertical and horizontal diameters and area of the PLA were 56.24 ± 16.56 mm, 59.92 ± 24.83 mm and 28.10 ± 16.47 cm2, respectively. Mean distances to the anterior superior iliac spine, posterior superior iliac spine, iliac crest and acetabular roof were 62.12 ± 19.84 mm, 71.03 ± 12.17 mm, 19.88 ± 11.61 mm and 56.02 ± 9.09 mm, respectively. The safe angular deviation for AIIS-origin screw fixation was 16.94° ± 4.43°. The central cortical thickness was 2.07 ± 1.60 mm (range 0.3–5.9).ConclusionsThe PLA is a clinically important region characterised by marked central cortical thinning and individual variability. Individualised morphometric assessment and preoperative imaging may help define safer zones for fixation planning and donor-site procedures.Level of EvidenceLevel V.Keywords: Iliac wing, Translucent area, Iliac screw fixation, Iliac pillar corridor, Supraacetabular corridor, Safe zone

Introduction

Pelvic and acetabular fractures are serious musculoskeletal injuries with high morbidity and mortality rates. These injuries may lead to life-threatening consequences, particularly when accompanied by hemodynamic instability and multiple traumatic injuries [1, 2]. While high-energy trauma is the primary cause of these fractures in young adults, the ageing population and the increasing incidence of osteoporosis are also significantly increasing the frequency of pelvic fractures owing to low-energy trauma [3–5]. The primary objectives of treatment are to achieve stable internal fixation, allow early mobilisation and minimise complications [1, 6–8]. Successful surgical outcomes depend not only on the selection of the appropriate approach but also on a detailed understanding of pelvic morphology.

The iliac wing is one of the most frequently used anatomical regions for acetabular and posterior pelvic ring fixation, and it also serves as a major donor site for autologous bone grafting and bone marrow aspiration [6, 9–12]. However, its complex anatomy poses significant challenges for surgical planning. Despite the presence of a translucent and fragile region in the central iliac wing, the lateral parts undergo thickening in a parallel orientation to the muscle attachment areas, thereby forming an irregular, bowl-like geometry [8].

Previous anatomical studies have identified various osseous fixation corridors within the iliac wing and highlighted their importance for implant stability [5, 6, 8, 13]. However, the morphometric characteristics and surgical safety margins of the thin cortical translucent area (PLA) located in the central iliac wing have not been sufficiently clarified. This complicates the assessment of the risk of perforation and potential complications, particularly in procedures such as screw placement, bone grafting and bone marrow aspiration [9–11].

The objective of this study is to perform morphometric mapping of the PLA on dry bones, determine cortical thickness and area measurements, evaluate their relationships with osseous fixation corridors, and thereby contribute to the definition of safe margins in surgical procedures.

Materials and methods

Study design

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine at Hacettepe University (approval no: GO 21/21-28).

In this study, 57 unpaired dry hip bones of unknown age and sex, from the osteological collection of the Department of Anatomy at the Hacettepe University Faculty of Medicine , were examined. Specimens with preserved iliac wing morphology and clearly identifiable anatomical landmarks required for measurement were included in the study. Specimens with deformity, fracture, post-mortem damage, severe erosion or any structural alteration that could affect the measurement parameters were excluded. Accordingly, six specimens were excluded from the final analysis.

A standardised image acquisition protocol was implemented to ensure the accuracy and repeatability of measurements in photograph-based morphometric analysis. All bone samples were placed on a flat, stable surface and the camera was mounted on a tripod and oriented perpendicular (90°) to the measurement surface. To reduce perspective-related measurement errors, a metric scale was placed on each bone and included in every image. Although the use of a fixed-focus prime lens was not feasible because of physical constraints during image acquisition, distortion was minimised by using an optical zoom lens and avoiding close camera-to-object proximity. Furthermore, focus was maintained on the central region of the bone during photography. Lighting was standardised using a fixed external point light source to prevent artefacts caused by shadows and reflections that could affect the identification of anatomical landmarks. All images were acquired with a tripod-supported fixed camera (Sony HX350) while maintaining a consistent distance and angle.

The acquired images were transferred to ImageJ software. In ImageJ, the Straight-Line Tool was used to select a known length on a reference ruler, and the pixel size was matched to the actual length using the Set Scale command. Owing to the irregularity of the measurement surface, calibration was performed using reference scales at five different locations and the pixel/mm ratio was determined by averaging these measurements. Other measurements were calibrated using the nearest metric guide and recorded in millimetres. The measurement of cortical thickness was conducted utilising a digital thickness gauge. The measurements were performed by two independent researchers, and the inter-rater reliability, assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), was >0.90 for all parameters.

Parameters

Using the ImageJ software

Before measurements were obtained, the thin cortical region was standardised using reproducible anatomical landmarks. For this purpose, the ASPS line, which extends between the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) and the posterior superior iliac spine (PSIS), and is readily identifiable during interventional procedures was utilised. The region was delineated within a quadrilateral formed by lines drawn parallel and perpendicular to the ASPS line and tangent to its borders.

The location of the region was assessed relative to the proximal, middle and distal thirds of the ASPS line, and its vertical and horizontal diameters were measured. Utilising reference points of known clinical and anatomical importance (ASIS, PSIS, iliac crest and acetabular roof) served to provide a more precise definition of the location. Accordingly, the distances between anterior border-ASIS, posterior border-PSIS, superior border-iliac crest and inferior border-acetabular roof were measured (Fig. 1).

The distance of the region to the centreline of the iliac pillar corridor was also measured. In determining the safe angle range for fixation applications using the supraacetabular corridor, trajectories A and B were defined, tangent to the boundaries of the region from the anterior inferior iliac spine (AIIS). The angle between the supraacetabular corridor and trajectory A was measured and accepted as the safe angle. Furthermore, the closest distance of the region to the AIIS within the risky angle range was also recorded (Fig. 1). Additionally, the lengths of the iliac pillar and supraacetabular corridors, which are osseous fixation pathways, and the ASPS line were determined.

Cortical thickness was measured using an outside diameter digital thickness gauge at the superior, inferior, anterior and posterior borders of the region, as well as at its centre, where the thinnest cortical area was identified.

Statistical analysis

The descriptive statistics of the obtained data were calculated using SPSS v23.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA, 2015). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, percentiles, and minimum–maximum values. The normality of the parameters was evaluated using Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests, and histograms. For parameters exhibiting a normal distribution, the independent sample t-test was employed for intergroup comparisons, whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was utilised for parameters that were not normally distributed. For comparisons among multiple groups, either a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or a Kruskal–Wallis test was employed. The statistical significance level was accepted as p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 51 hip bones were included in the study, 26 from the right side and 25 from the left. As no statistically significant difference was identified between the sides in terms of the measured parameters, analyses were performed on the combined sample.

The inferior border of the PLA was located an average of 8.99 ± 6.81 mm above the ASPS line (range 1.90–26.90) in 23 specimens (45.1%), at the level of the line in 8 (15.7%), and 6.78 ± 4.85 mm below the line (range 1.50–16.50) in 20 (39.2%).

The vertical and horizontal diameters of the region were measured as 56.24 ± 16.56 mm and 59.92 ± 24.83 mm, respectively, and the average area was 28.10 ± 16.47 cm2. No statistically significant difference was found between vertical diameters when considering the position (above, at the border, below) relative to the ASPS line (p = 0.198). However, according to its relationship with the iliac pillar corridor, the horizontal diameters were 89.30 ± 11.05 mm in specimens “crossing the corridor” and 50.88 ± 20.47 mm in specimens “not crossing the corridor,” and this difference was found to be statistically significant (p < 0.001).

The anterior border-ASIS and posterior border-PSIS distances of the PLA were 62.12 ± 19.84 mm and 71.03 ± 12.17 mm, respectively. The superior border-iliac crest and inferior border-acetabular roof distances were determined as 19.88 ± 11.61 mm and 56.02 ± 9.09 mm, respectively. The inferior border-acetabular roof distance was recorded as 61.06 ± 8.20 mm in specimens located above the ASPS line and 49.94 ± 7.25 mm in specimens located below it, and this difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Discussion

In pelvic and spinal surgeries, the iliac wing represents a strategically significant anatomical region that is frequently utilised as both a fixation and donor site [2, 5, 7, 10, 14]. However, regional differences in bone quality and cortical integrity during implant placement and graft procurement can directly affect surgical safety [10, 14, 15]. While the current literature [8, 13, 16] describes safe osseous corridors in detail, quantitative data regarding translucent areas where cortical integrity is significantly weakened remain limited. This study evaluates the morphometric characteristics of this region from a surgical perspective.

While the cortical thickness at the boundary of the PLA averages approximately 5–6 mm, it decreases to 2.07 ± 1.60 mm in the centre and in some specimens to as low as 0.3 mm. This pronounced periphery to centre gradient suggests that the centre of the PLA forms a critical zone with low cortical reserve. Indeed, age-related osteoporotic changes may reduce cortical thickness, potentially increasing the susceptibility of the PLA to cortical perforation during surgical procedures. However, the absence of age and sex data limits the interpretation of whether this cortical thinning may be related to demographic factors or osteoporotic changes. Wang et al. described an “implant constriction” pattern in the iliac crest where the inner and outer cortices are significantly converging and the intercortical width is less than 5 mm in some segments [8]. Similarly, Hernigou et al. demonstrated that the bone stock is not homogeneous during bone marrow aspiration from the iliac crest, and that regional thinning does not completely eliminate the risk of cortical perforation despite technical care and experience. The occurrence of perforation in 114 out of 410 trocar insertions in cadaveric series and subsequent dissection revealed nine vascular or neurological lesions involving the external iliac artery, lateral femoral cutaneous nerve, inferior gluteal vessel and sacroiliac joint [10]. These findings indicate that cortical thinning of the iliac wing may have practical consequences beyond reduced bone stock, including donor-site morbidity and the risk of injury to adjacent neurovascular structures. Consequently, computed tomography (CT)-based preoperative morphometric characterisation of thin cortical regions may facilitate optimised fixation corridor planning and improve implant purchase, while also minimising the risk of cortical perforation and associated complications during donor-site procedures.

Von Glinski et al. suggested that the caudal boundary of the PLA is located above the ASPS line and that this line can provide a safe anatomical boundary for iliac screw placement [17]. By contrast, Schildhauer et al. reported that screws placed in the iliac region have an average diameter of 6–8 mm and that the risk of cortical perforation should be considered when performing procedures above the ASPS line [18]. In the present study, the mean length of the ASPS line was 180.34 ± 22.37 mm. This finding suggests that this line may provide a useful reference for long screw and pin applications. However, in 8 specimens, the lower boundary of the PLA was at the same level as the ASPS line, and in 20, it was an average of 7 mm below this line. Even in specimens located above the line, the mean distance was only 8 mm, suggesting a narrow safety margin. These findings demonstrate that the caudal boundary of the PLA exhibits significant anatomical variability among individuals. Consequently, the risk is not limited to procedures performed above the ASPS line but may also arise in cases extending below it. Especially when implant guidance is limited to two-dimensional fluoroscopy, fixed safety assumptions based on the ASPS line can increase the risk of malposition and cortical perforation. This risk may be even more relevant in cases of poor bone quality, where cortical thinning may be more pronounced. Consequently, individual anatomical variations must be considered when determining the screw trajectory and depth.

Furthermore, the findings of this study demonstrate that the PLA may approach the acetabular roof considerably in some individuals (61.06 ± 8.20 mm in specimens above the ASPS line; 49.94 ± 7.25 mm in those below; p < 0.001). Antoniades and Pellegrini defined the acetabular dome approximately 35 mm proximal to the teardrop reference and showed that the iliac bone stock can change significantly even within 1–2 cm proximal to the dome (42 ± 9 mm at the dome, 32 ± 6 mm at 1 cm proximal and 22 ± 4 mm at 2 cm proximal) [19]. These findings suggest that the supraacetabular ilium should not be regarded as a region of constant thickness and that local cortical reserve should be considered when planning screw placement adjacent to the joint. Particularly in periprosthetic acetabular reconstructions or fixations requiring supraacetabular extension, the risk is not limited solely to proximity to the joint and neurovascular structures. Cortical breach, inadequate purchase and related complications may also occur when the screw trajectory coincides with a region of reduced cortical thickness, particularly given that 6.5 mm screws with lengths ranging from 15 to 70 mm are commonly used in these procedures.

The increasing incidence of pelvic and acetabular fractures, particularly in the elderly population, has increased the need for minimally invasive fixation techniques, making the iliac pillar and supraacetabular corridors frequently used osseous pathways in surgical practice [2, 5, 7]. Although the iliac pillar, supraacetabular and iliac crest corridors have been described as safe osseous fixation pathways in the literature [6, 8], the morphometric features of the thin cortical translucent area related to these corridors have been largely overlooked. However, the accuracy of iliac screw trajectories is critical not only for mechanical implant fixation but also for clinical outcomes. A cortical breach or screw malposition has the potential to compromise adjacent neurovascular structures, which may result in postoperative symptoms including pain, gait disturbance and sensory deficits. Nguyen et al. [20] reported an iliac screw–related complication rate of 7.7%, a screw loosening rate of 3.5% and a sacroiliac joint pain rate of 14.2% in a series of 260 adult patients. Consequently, when screw trajectories approach low cortical reserve areas such as the PLA, the clinical risk is not limited to cortical perforation or fixation failure but may also include neurovascular injury and postoperative pain. In this context, the preoperative identification of low cortical reserve areas may contribute to safer trajectory planning and reduction of fixation-related complications.

In open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) techniques applied along the iliac crest, stabilisation is typically achieved with 3.5 mm interfragmentary lag screws [21]. However, previous studies have shown that the best bone stock is found along the iliac crest and adjacent to the pelvic brim, and that the bone between these two strong areas may become thin enough to compromise screw placement [21]. This surgical framework underscores the clinical significance of the proximity of the PLA to the iliac crest. In the present study, the projection of the PLA on the middle third of the iliac crest in most of the specimens suggests that the clinical risk may be particularly increased in this region. Furthermore, the mean distance from the PLA to the iliac crest was 19.88 ± 11.61 mm and was less than 10 mm in 9 specimens, indicating that, in some cases, the plate screws may approach a thin cortical zone (Fig. 2). The different screw lengths preferred in crest fixation, especially in applications extending to deeper bone segments, can increase the likelihood of the screw trajectory coinciding with zones showing low cortical thickness. This may result in a reduction in screw purchase, cortical perforation and subsequent loss of stability. On the other hand, considering that the iliac crest is also a frequently used site for autologous bone grafting and bone marrow aspiration [10, 11], it should be considered that PLA may increase the risk of perforation in these procedures. Therefore, clearly defining the boundaries of this area is clinically important not only for internal fixation of pelvic and acetabular fractures but also for donor site safety.

In the iliac screw technique used for spinopelvic stabilisation in sacral and lumbar pathologies such as trauma, deformity and tumours, the entry point is located at the posterior iliac crest/PSIS level, and the screw is advanced towards the AIIS. The intended trajectory passes above the acetabulum and remains approximately 2 cm superior to the sciatic notch [22]. Berry et al. described two basic trajectories for iliac screw/pin placement at the suprasciatic notch level and showed that the trajectory from the PSIS to the AIIS may provide more reliable implant purchase because it is longer than the trajectory from the PSIS to the superior acetabular rim. However, they also emphasised that in both approaches, the critical narrowing occurs just above the sciatic notch [23]. In this context, inferior deviation from the reference line increases proximity to the sciatic notch, whereas superior deviation may increase the likelihood of overlap with the PLA, where cortical integrity is markedly reduced within the iliac wing (Fig. 3). Previous clinical literature has shown that iliac screw breach may become clinically symptomatic rather than remaining merely a radiological finding. Lee et al. reported an overall breach rate of 12.4% among 193 iliac screws, whereas symptomatic violations requiring revision surgery occurred in 2.1% of screws. They further reported that lateral wall perforation was associated with gluteal muscle pain, while inferior wall perforation could result in sciatic nerve compression and severe radiating leg pain [24]. This concern is also supported by the S2-Alar-Iliac (S2AI) spinopelvic fixation literature, as this trajectory similarly terminates near the AIIS and requires passage through a narrow intraosseous pathway. Arora and Berven [25] noted that anterior or inferior violations during S2AI fixation may endanger major vascular and neural structures, including the iliac vessels, superior gluteal vessels, sciatic nerve and pudendal nerves. This is particularly relevant given the large-diameter screws commonly used in this technique, typically 7.0–8.0 mm [22]. In specimens where the PLA is located at or below the ASPS line, superior deviation exceeding approximately 12° (range 5.98–17.27) from the PSIS-AIIS reference orientation may increase the risk of PLA contact and subsequent cortical perforation. This finding supports the need for preoperative CT assessment of PLA position and individualised screw trajectory planning, even when the PSIS-AIIS approach is used. However, it should be noted that this angular relationship was obtained photogrammetrically from dry bone specimens; therefore, its clinical applicability and the potential influence of sex-related pelvic morphological differences should be evaluated in further CT-based studies.

The iliac pillar corridor is a preferred osseous pathway, especially in cases of isolated iliac wing fractures and unstable pelvic ring injuries requiring minimally invasive stabilisation in osteoporotic orgeriatric pelvis [5]. Von Glinski et al. reported that this corridor can exhibit a distinct “hourglass” morphology and that segmental narrowing along the corridor may be surgically significant [16].Our findings reinforce this conceptual framework. In the present study, the iliac pillar corridor length was 124.07 mm. It is recommended that caution be exercised when using screws longer than 12 cm to avoid acetabular penetration. The fact that this limit is reported as approximately 10 cm in the literature [16, 26] may be related to differences in pelvic morphology depending on the age, sex, and population characteristics of the specimens. However, the absence of age and sex data in the analysed specimens precludes a comprehensive evaluation of the potential influence of these demographic variables on iliac pillar corridor dimensions. Therefore, multicentre studies including diverse populations, demographic groups and bone-quality profiles would be valuable for assessing the generalisability of this anatomical relationship. In 12 specimens in which the PLA intersected this corridor, the thin cortical area was concentrated within the first half of the corridor, at a mean distance of 26–62 mm from its origin. This distribution supports the view that the narrowing segment of the hourglass architecture along the iliac pillar is located in the mid-portion of the corridor. Furthermore, the finding that the intercortical width measured could fall below 5 mm in this tapering segment suggests that the assumption that the iliac pillar offers high cortical reserve in every case may not be valid and could directly limit implant diameter in some patients (Fig. 2). This point is clinically critical because large-diameter implants, preferred in long-segment screw applications, can increase the risk of cortical perforation in the tapering segment. Moreover, given the recommendation of two-pin usage in certain instances, the proximity of the PLA to the iliac pillar, as revealed by our morphometric mapping, is of clinical significance [27]. Indeed, the fact that the anterior border of the PLA was located at an average distance of 1.7 cm from the iliac pillar midline in 76% of the specimens (n = 39) suggests that in some individuals, the pin placement may approach the PLA in cases where the pins are shifted posteriorly along the iliac crest (Fig. 2). At this point, given that the Schanz pins utilised are frequently in the 5–6 mm diameter range, it may be more meaningful to consider the distribution of cortical reserve to prevent possible complications [27].

Pelvic apophyseal avulsion fractures most frequently occur at the ASIS and AIIS levels, and most cases are managed conservatively; however, when significant displacement requires ORIF, the fragment is typically fixed with a 3.5 mm half-threaded cancellous screw placed according to the lag principle [28]. Although the screw trajectory may vary depending on the fracture line and fragment location, the mean distance from the PLA to the ASIS was 38 mm (range 23.40–65.40) in specimens in which the PLA crossed the iliac pillar corridor. This finding suggests that, especially in cases where different screw lengths in the 20–50 mm range are selected, the risk of possible overlap with the PLA should be considered when the chosen screw length and trajectory are combined with individual anatomical variations (Fig. 4).

In long intramedullary lag screw techniques extending from the AIIS to the posterior ilium, screws are generally reported to be longer than 150 mm, with a diameter of 4.5 mm being most commonly preferred [29]. This surgical context supports the clinical relevance of the morphometric findings reported in the present study. Although the AIIS to PSIS corridor is generally considered a safe osseous pathway, the limited safe angular deviation defined for AIIS-origin screw fixation (16.94° ± 4.43°), together with the shortest AIIS–PLA distance of 67.31 ± 13.92 mm when this angle was exceeded, suggests that cranial deviation from this trajectory may lead the implant towards a zone of low cortical reserve as it advances posteriorly (Fig. 5). Intraoperatively, this angular deviation may be approximated on iliac oblique fluoroscopic views using an imaginary AIIS-PSIS line as a reference and assessing the cranial orientation of the guidewire relative to this line. Notably, Deng et al. [30] reported implant-related problems in 8.0% of patients undergoing modified LC-II screw fixation, including hardware loosening and implant breakage, and emphasised that safe screw placement requires detailed knowledge of osseous corridor parameters. Accordingly, we suggest that CT-based preoperative assessment should extend beyond the length, diameter and angulation of the osseous corridor to include the position of the PLA and the distribution of cortical thickness when determining screw trajectory and length, to reduce potential fixation-related complications. Nevertheless, these findings were obtained from dry bone specimens using photogrammetric measurements and should therefore be interpreted as anatomical reference values. The safe angular range may vary according to sex-related pelvic morphology and interindividual differences in PLA position.

There are several limitations to this study that should be considered. Firstly, the morphometric measurements were obtained from photograph-based analyses and may therefore be affected by imaging geometry. Variables such as the distance between the camera and the specimen plane, focus variability, lens distortion and surface inclination, may introduce measurement errors, particularly on irregular or inclined bony surfaces. Although a standardised imaging protocol and multiple calibration points were used to minimise these effects, perspective and distortion related errors cannot be entirely excluded. Secondly, the study was performed on dry hip bones of unknown age and sex. Therefore, the potential effects of age-related osteoporotic changes and sex-related pelvic morphological differences on PLA morphology, cortical thickness and its relationship with fixation corridors could not be evaluated. This may limit the generalisability of the findings to different demographic groups, osteoporotic bone and clinical populations. Thirdly, given that this was an osteological study, it was not possible to directly assess cartilage, soft tissues, neurovascular structures, fracture-related deformity and intraoperative conditions. Consequently, the proposed safety margins and angular values should be interpreted as anatomical reference parameters rather than definitive operative thresholds. Future CT-based three-dimensional studies including larger multicentre datasets and diverse population samples, together with clinical validation, are required to confirm the applicability of these findings under clinical conditions and to determine their impact on implant placement accuracy and surgical outcomes.

Conclusions

The PLA represents a surgically significant thin cortical region of the iliac wing, characterised by marked central cortical thinning and anatomical variability. Its relationship to the iliac crest, iliac pillar and supraacetabular corridors suggests that it may affect the risk of cortical perforation and implant purchase during pelvic and acetabular fixation, and that safe corridor approaches based on fixed reference lines may not be applicable in every case. Consequently, individualised morphometric assessment should guide surgical planning. Preoperative CT-based assessment of PLA morphology and cortical thickness distribution can support individualised fixation planning and donor-site safety. These findings should be interpreted as anatomical reference data and require further validation through CT-based clinical studies.
