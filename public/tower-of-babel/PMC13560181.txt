Aetiology-dependent short- and long-term survival after paediatric out-of-hospital cardiac arrest: a multicentre, retrospective, cohort study
Authors: Romana Erblich, Matthias Noitz, Marius Knöll, Helmut Trimmel, Wolfgang Voelckel, Dominik Jenny, Jens Meier, Martin W Dünser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOut-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) in children is rare and associated with poor outcomes. Background

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) in children is a rare but devastating event. Its incidence is strongly dependent on age and ranges from 4 to 9 cases per 100,000 children [1–3]. The aetiology of paediatric OHCA differs substantially from that in adults [4, 5]. Whereas primary cardiac causes predominate in adult OHCA [6], respiratory failure, shock, and external causes are more common in children [7–9]. Short-term survival following paediatric OHCA remains poor, with reported hospital survival rates as low as 6.6% in infants and up to 18.5% in adolescents [5, 10]. Among children who survive to hospital discharge, however, long-term survival is high, with 95% surviving at least one year [11]. Despite these favourable long-term survival rates, functional outcomes remain disappointing, with only a small proportion of children achieving good neurological recovery [11–13].

During resuscitation of children in OHCA, it is essential for emergency physicians to be aware of the individual child’s likelihood of achieving return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) and survival. Established predictors of short-term survival in paediatric OHCA are largely comparable to those in adults and include a witnessed collapse, provision of bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), and an initial shockable electrocardiographic rhythm [8–14]. To date, however, limited data are available regarding differences in short- and long-term outcomes across the various aetiologies of paediatric OHCA. Since the aetiology of OHCA in children is often straightforward to define (e.g., trauma, drowning, asphyxiation, non-traumatic aetiologies), improved understanding of aetiology-specific outcomes may provide valuable prognostic information and assist clinicians in identifying patient groups, in whom prolonged resuscitation efforts or advanced interventions, such as extracorporeal CPR, could be warranted.

In the present study, we investigated differences in sustained ROSC, 30-day survival, and 1-year survival among 200 children experiencing OHCA of different aetiologies. In addition, we compared patient characteristics, OHCA-related factors, and resuscitation interventions across aetiology groups.

Methods

Study design

This multicentre, retrospective, observational cohort study analysed data from patients treated by teams of a nationwide helicopter emergency medical service (HEMS) (ÖAMTC Flugrettung) in Austria. The observation period extended from January 1, 2010 to December 31, 2019. The study formed part of a larger research project investigating the characteristics, management, as well as short- and long-term outcomes of patients with OHCA treated by HEMS teams of the ÖAMTC Flugrettung. The research project was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Federal State of Lower Austria (GS4-EK-4/703–2020). Owing to the retrospective design, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. Results from prior analyses focusing on outcomes in selected adult OHCA populations have been published previously [15–17]. The reporting of this manuscript follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines [18].

Study setting

The ÖAMTC Flugrettung is the sole organisation contractually mandated to provide nationwide HEMS coverage in Austria. During the study period, 16 HEMS bases were in operation, of which 13 contributed medical data to a central electronic database. Of these, twelve bases operated from dawn to dusk, while one base was operational for 24 h. HEMS teams consisted of an emergency physician (predominantly anaesthesiologists), a flight paramedic, and a pilot. In Austria, physician-staffed emergency medical service teams are dispatched to all unexpected OHCA events. Decisions regarding dispatch of a physician-staffed rapid response vehicle versus a HEMS team are made by regional ambulance control centres based on availability, accessibility, and estimated time to scene. During the study period, no HEMS-specific standard operating procedures for paediatric OHCA existed within the ÖAMTC Flugrettung. Nevertheless, all HEMS teams were trained and strongly encouraged to adhere to contemporaneous guidelines for paediatric OHCA management.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The central electronic database of the ÖAMTC Flugrettung was screened for primary missions with OHCA as the final diagnosis in the medical records. Children (< 18 years) were eligible for study enrolment. In line with the 2024 update of the Utstein OHCA Registry template [19], we excluded children with signs of life at emergency medical service (EMS) arrival and those with signs of irreversible death. In addition, newborns (age ≤ 28 days) with OHCA were also excluded from the analysis.

Study Variables

When available, the following variables were extracted from the electronic database: age, sex, presence and type of comorbid conditions, aetiology of OHCA, suspected suicide attempt, location of arrest, witness status, bystander CPR in patients experiencing OHCA before EMS arrival, bystander defibrillation in patients experiencing OHCA before EMS arrival, signs of irreversible death, ROSC prior to EMS arrival, HEMS response time, initial electrocardiographic rhythm, resuscitation interventions performed, and flight time to hospital (for patients transported from the scene). As a substantial proportion of children were transported to hospital under ongoing CPR, we defined the ROSC rate as the overall rate of sustained ROSC, encompassing ROSC achieved on scene, during HEMS transport, or in the emergency department. Sustained ROSC was defined as restoration of a palpable central pulse accompanied by an autonomous electrocardiographic rhythm for at least 20 min following cessation of CPR [20]. Thirty-day and 1-year survival outcomes were available only for Austrian nationals. In these subjects, binary survival status was obtained from the Austrian national death registry (Statistik Austria; Vienna, Austria).

OHCA aetiologies

The aetiology of OHCA was determined based on the final diagnosis documented in the electronic medical records by the attending emergency physician. In the 21 records, in which no explicit aetiology was recorded, classification was derived from contextual information documented in the medical records. Aetiologies were classified as non-traumatic or external/traumatic. The International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), was used to define external and traumatic causes of OHCA (codes S00–T98) [21, 22], which were further subdivided into asphyxiation, drowning, and trauma. Because poisoning could not be reliably confirmed in any study patient, it was not analysed as a separate external aetiology. Non-traumatic OHCA aetiologies were subdivided into suspected cardiac causes, non-cardiac causes, and Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS). Patients were classified as having SIDS if they were younger than 1 year, previously healthy, and found without signs of life during sleep, with no clinical evidence of trauma, infection, or airway obstruction.

Study objectives

The primary objective was to compare rates of sustained ROSC, 30-day survival, and 1-year survival between children with OHCA of different aetiologies. Secondary objectives included comparisons of patient characteristics, OHCA-related variables, and resuscitation interventions across aetiology groups. Additionally, we evaluated the relationship between patient age and intra-arrest HEMS transport to the hospital.

Data processing

Only relevant sections of the electronic medical records were transferred by the ÖAMTC Flugrettung data management team in strict accordance with data minimization and pseudonymization requirements. Following data extraction, quality control procedures were applied to identify and correct syntax or data entry errors where possible. No imputation methods were used to address missing data.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 30.0.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to report primary and secondary study endpoints. Categorical variables are presented as absolute numbers with percentages and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which demonstrated non-normal distributions of all variables. Accordingly, continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Log-transformed continuous variables were compared across the six aetiology groups using an analysis of variance. Categorical variables were compared between the six aetiology groups using chi-square tests. In order to determine the independent association between rates of sustained ROSC, 30-day survival, and 1-year survival and different OHCA aetiologies, binary logistic regression analyses were calculated with the OHCA aetiology as well as patient characteristics, OHCA-related variables, and resuscitation interventions as independent variables and each of the three outcome variables as dependent variables. We did not include variables that interacted with each other or were obviously associated with the three outcome variables (i.e., death on scene or transport to hospital) into the regression models. The relationship between age and intra-arrest HEMS transport to the hospital was assessed using bivariate correlation analysis. Spearman’s rho was used to quantify the strength of correlation. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Among 9,018 patients with OHCA, 247 [2.7% (95% CI, 2.2–3.1%)] were children. Two-hundred of these were included into the statistical analysis (Fig. 1). External or traumatic aetiologies accounted for 129 cases [64.5% (95% CI, 57.4–71.1%)], while non-traumatic OHCA occurred in 71 children [35.6% (95% CI, 28.9–43.6%)]. Trauma was the most frequent aetiology [n = 78; 39.0% (95% CI, 32.2–46.1%)], followed by drowning [n = 34; 17.0% (95% CI, 12.1–22.9%)], non-cardiac non-traumatic causes [n = 27; 13.5% (95% CI, 9.1–19.0%)] and suspected cardiac causes [n = 27; 13.5% (95% CI, 9.1–19.0%)] (Fig. 2). SIDS [n = 17; 8.5% (95% CI, 5.0–13.3%)] and asphyxiation were the least common aetiologies [n = 17; 8.5% (95% CI, 5.0–13.3%)]. Asphyxiation resulted from hanging or strangulation (n = 10), accidental inhalation of liquids or solid objects (n = 4), or burial (n = 3). Non-cardiac non-traumatic causes included respiratory failure (n = 20), infection (n = 5), anaphylaxis (n = 1), and unknown causes (n = 1).

Aetiology-dependent outcomes of paediatric OHCA

Thirty-day and 1-year survival data were available for 194 of 200 patients (97.0%). Rates of sustained ROSC (p < 0.001), 30-day survival (p < 0.001), and 1-year survival (p < 0.001) differed significantly across OHCA aetiologies (Fig. 3). Children with OHCA due to drowning exhibited the highest rates of sustained ROSC [77.4% (95% CI, 58.9–90.4%)], 30-day survival [55.9% (95% CI, 37.9–72.8%)], and 1-year survival [50.0% (95% CI, 32.4–67.6%)]. In contrast, outcomes were poorest among children with SIDS, with sustained ROSC achieved in only 20.0% (95% CI, 4.3–48.1%), and both 30-day and 1-year survival rates of 5.9% (95% CI, 0.2–28.7%). In the multivariable regression models, OHCA aetiology was independently associated with rates of sustained ROSC, 30-day survival, and 1-year survival (Electronic Supplementary Material Tables 1–3).

Differences in patient characteristics, OHCA characteristics, and resuscitation interventions

Patient age differed significantly across aetiology groups (Table 1) and demonstrated distinct distribution patterns within each group (Fig. 4). Male sex predominated in all aetiology groups except the non-cardiac non-traumatic group. Significant intergroup differences were observed in the prevalence and type of comorbid conditions, arrest location, witness status, suspected suicide attempts, and HEMS response time. Asystole was the most common initial rhythm across all aetiology groups (Table 1). The rates of vascular access type, fluid infusion, and chest decompression differed significantly by aetiology (Table 2). Similarly, the rate of the emergency physician’s decision to pronounce death on scene or transport patients to hospital under ongoing CPR varied significantly across aetiology groups. Intra-arrest HEMS transport to the hospital was significantly correlated with patient age [death on scene: 11 [2–16] years; intra-arrest HEMS transport: 3 [1–8] years; p < 0.001; Spearman-rho, -0.301]. Histograms of aetiology-dependent outcomes of the 58 children, who underwent intra-arrest HEMS transport, are summarized in Fig. 5.

Discussion

In this retrospective, multicentre cohort study, paediatric OHCA accounted for only a small proportion of all OHCA cases in our nationwide HEMS. We classified paediatric OHCA into six aetiological categories. Although rare causes such as electrocution were not observed and no cases of poisoning could be verified, the identified aetiologies are consistent with those reported in previous studies [2, 7–9] and underscore the heterogeneity of paediatric OHCA. In contrast to reports demonstrating a predominance (up to 66%) of non-traumatic aetiologies [2, 7, 8], traumatic and other external causes prevailed in our cohort. This likely reflects the mission profile of Austrian HEMS, which primarily operates in non-metropolitan regions and is commonly dispatched to patients with severe trauma [23]. Rates of sustained ROSC and 30-day survival were higher than those reported in prior cohorts [13]. Compatible with earlier analyses [11], most children who were alive at 30 days also survived at least one year following the event.

The most important finding of the present study is that the underlying aetiology was significantly associated with both short- and long-term survival after paediatric OHCA. Drowning was associated with the by far highest 1-year survival, with two-thirds of affected children surviving to one year. In contrast, only one in three children with OHCA due to non-cardiac, non-traumatic causes or asphyxiation was alive at one year. Children with OHCA of suspected cardiac origin, traumatic causes, or SIDS had the lowest survival probabilities. Although 1-year survival rates in these groups (5.9–14.8%) were significantly lower than in other aetiologies, they nonetheless exceeded those typically reported in adults with OHCA [6].

Our findings are in line with previous studies demonstrating higher survival rates in paediatric OHCA related to drowning, asphyxiation, or respiratory causes. In a retrospective analysis of paediatric OHCA managed by Dutch HEMS teams, 42% of drowning victims survived, followed by children with OHCA due to asphyxiation (21%) [24]. Similarly, an Australian study reported the highest ROSC rates in children with OHCA caused by poisoning (40.0%), followed by drowning (31.1%) and asphyxia (19.6%) [25]. A multicentre, retrospective cohort study from the United States identified OHCA due to drowning or asphyxia as one factor independently associated with survival after paediatric OHCA [26]. Interpreting our results together with the existing literature, these data highlight aetiology as a probable key prognostic determinant in paediatric OHCA. Knowledge of or suspecting the underlying cause may assist emergency physicians in prognostication and decision-making regarding the duration of resuscitation efforts or the application of advanced interventions, particularly in children with OHCA secondary to drowning, asphyxiation, or non-cardiac non-traumatic causes.

Another notable observation was the strong age dependence of paediatric OHCA aetiologies. By definition, SIDS occurred exclusively in children < 1 year of age, whereas OHCA due to non-cardiac non-traumatic causes or drowning peaked among those aged < 5 years. In contrast, OHCA of suspected cardiac origin demonstrated a bimodal age distribution. In younger children, OHCA due to suspected cardiac aetiologies was predominantly associated with congenital conditions, whereas in older children and adolescents, it may have been due to arrhythmogenic or hypertrophic cardiomyopathies and myocarditis [5, 27]. Suicidal hanging or strangulation was the most common mechanism of asphyxiation-related OHCA and occurred primarily in adolescents, while asphyxiation due to tracheal aspiration mostly occurred in infants. These findings are consistent with known age-dependent disease burdens and risk profiles [28].

Although our study cannot definitively explain the observed differences in short- and long-term survival across aetiologies, both patient-related factors (e.g., pre-existing conditions) and OHCA-related circumstances (e.g., unwitnessed collapse) likely contributed to these heterogeneous outcomes. Notably, bystander CPR rates were highest among drowning victims (68.8%). Together with the obligatory reversal of the underlying cause (i.e., rescue from water), this may partially explain why more than one third of drowning victims, who underwent bystander CPR, might have regained signs of life before EMS arrival. It is, however, important to note that since it was impossible to confirm that these children had sustained an actual cardiac arrest, they were excluded from the statistical analysis.

As expected, resuscitation strategies also differed according to OHCA aetiology and age. For example, the predominance of intraosseous vascular access in SIDS cases can be readily explained by the young age of these patients. A particularly noteworthy finding relates to the decision of HEMS physicians to transport children under ongoing CPR. A correlation analysis indicated that younger children were more likely to undergo intra-arrest HEMS transport. The outcomes analysis in this subgroup suggests two potential rationales for intra-arrest transport. First, the high rate of death in the emergency department may indicate that some children were transported to allow termination of resuscitation in a hospital setting, where bereavement support for families is often superior to that available on scene. Emotional, ethical, and medicolegal considerations are known to influence the decision to transport paediatric patients under ongoing CPR rather than declare death on scene [29]. Second, a subset of children may have been transported under ongoing CPR to maximize the likelihood of ROSC and survival. Although a prior study has reported that intra-arrest transport compared with lower continued on-scene CPR was not associated with survival to hospital discharge among children with OHCA [29], our data suggest that 1-year survival after intra-arrest HEMS transport remained substantial for certain aetiologies, particularly drowning (50%). These findings must, however, be interpreted with caution given the small number of patients in these subgroups.

Certain limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, survival at 30-days and 1-year was assessed as a binary outcome. We were unable to evaluate neurological or functional status among survivors, an outcome of central importance to patients and families [30]. Consequently, favourable survival rates, such as those observed after drowning, cannot be assumed to reflect good functional recovery. Indeed, current scientific evidence suggests that a substantial proportion of paediatric OHCA survivors experience unfavourable neurological outcomes [11–13]. Second, the retrospective design and inclusion of patients over a ten-year period introduce inherent limitations, including incomplete or missing data, misclassification of data, and time-dependent confounding, all of which may have affected the validity of our results. Potential misclassification of the aetiology of OHCA might have introduced a particularly relevant bias into our data. Misclassification could have occurred by either the attending emergency physician or from retrospective determination based on contextual information documented in the medical records. Finally, although the overall cohort was relatively large for paediatric OHCA, the small number of patients within individual aetiological subgroups resulted in wide 95% confidence intervals and considerable uncertainty around estimates of the primary outcome of this study.

Conclusions

In this nationwide HEMS cohort, paediatric OHCA was rare and demonstrated marked heterogeneity in both aetiology and outcomes. Short- and long-term survival rates varied significantly across OHCA aetiologies, with comparatively higher survival rates in OHCA due to drowning, non-cardiac non-traumatic aetiologies, or asphyxiation. Our results emphasize the prognostic relevance of OHCA aetiology in children and may inform clinical decision-making.
