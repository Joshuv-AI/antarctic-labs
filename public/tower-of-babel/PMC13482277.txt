Generation of human primordial germ cell-like cells from cumulus and blood cell-derived isogenic human induced pluripotent stem cells†
Authors: Silvia Llonch, Laurie Pinel, Tom Mattimoe, Jacqueline Severino, Filippo Zambelli, Anna Mallol, Karen Freire Carvalho, Laura Batlle-Morera, Rita Vassena, Bernhard Payer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe recent development of in vitro germ cell differentiation from pluripotent stem cells opened the human reproduction field to mechanistic investigations. In combination with induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC) reprogramming, in vitro gametogenesis provides an avenue for patient-specific disease modeling approaches. However, further advancements are required, as current protocols are limited to early stages of germ cell development. To get one step closer to this goal, here we aimed to identify optimal conditions for generating high-quality hiPSC lines capable of in vitro germ cell differentiation. We isolated two clinically available somatic cell types, peripheral blood-derived mononuclear cells and cumulus cells from the same female donor, and reprogrammed them into human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs). We then assessed their differentiation into ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm, and evaluated their X-chromosome inactivation status, a critical indicator of stem cell quality. Finally, we compared the capacity of PBMC- and cumulus-derived hiPSCs to differentiate into human primordial germ cell-like cells (hPGCLCs). We found that despite variability between cell lines, hiPSCs from both cell types were capable of generating hPGCLCs and that variations in X-chromosome state did not appear to generally interfere with the process. Our findings provide insight into germ cell differentiation from different clinically available starting materials guiding future patient-specific studies of fertility disorders.

Introduction

The fertility decline with advanced maternal age, the negative impact of lifestyle factors and environmental toxins on the reproductive system, and birth rates well below the replacement rate provide major challenges to modern society. Nevertheless, despite the urgent need for solutions to these problems, human reproduction and infertility disorders are mechanistically understudied subjects, in part due to a lack of good in vitro models.

Recent advances in the differentiation of primordial germ cell-like cells (PGCLCs) from pluripotent stem cells pose a promising avenue [1, 2]. Most progress so far has been achieved in the mouse model system, where functional eggs have been generated completely in vitro from pluripotent stem cells [3, 4]. Remarkably, functional eggs have even been derived from male embryonic stem cells (ESCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), by elegant conversion of sex-chromosome composition from XY into XX cells [5]. Nevertheless, it needs to be noted that the occurrence of live offspring from these in vitro-derived oocytes is still very low (around 5%) and improvements in the efficiency of the protocol will need to be made.

The success from the mouse has only been partially replicated in the human system, where in vitro differentiation of human ESCs (hESCs) and human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) into functional gametes is still far from being accomplished. While human primordial germ cell-like cells (hPGCLCs) have been derived successfully using various protocols [6–12], their further progression in differentiation has been limited to pre-meiotic stages such as migratory PGCs and oogonia [13–17]. A number of factors might play a role, such as the source of gonadal somatic cells to establish the correct environment for oocyte development. These are difficult to obtain in humans due to ethical and practical reasons, and therefore mouse somatic cells have been used in xenogenic reconstituted ovaries [13]. However, these might not provide the optimal niche for human oocyte development due to species differences. Another contributing factor for the failure to reach more advanced stages of oogenesis might be suboptimal starting cell material, from which hPGCLCs were derived. In the female case, epigenetically unstable pluripotent stem cells with eroded X-chromosome inactivation pose a problem, as they display abnormalities including reduced differentiation potential [18, 19]. Regarding this aspect, previous studies have come to contrasting conclusions, in which the X-chromosome state of hiPSCs either did [15, 20, 21] or did not [22] correlate with faithful in vitro germ cell development. Furthermore, the somatic cell type of origin, from which hiPSCs are reprogrammed, might potentially, through epigenetic memory, affect their capacity to form hPGCLCs and impact further progression towards meiosis and gametogenesis [22]. Further studies are needed to identify the cell type of origin and X-chromosome state, which provide the optimal conditions.

In this work, we aimed to investigate these issues and bring a more clinical perspective to the field of in vitro germ cell development. We thereby reprogrammed isogenic patient-derived somatic cells of two different origins, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and cumulus cells, into hiPSCs, evaluated their quality and X-chromosome status, and further differentiated them towards hPGCLCs. We did not detect consistent differences in X-chromosome state and hPGCLC differentiation potential between PBMC- and cumulus cell-derived hiPSCs and found both cell types equally suitable for modeling germ cell development in vitro. In conclusion, we generated new, high-quality hiPSC lines validated for human reproduction research and developed a workflow, which could provide the basis for future personalized disease modeling applications.

Materials and methods

Ethics

This project has received approval from the ethics committee of Clinica Eugin, the Generalitat de Catalunya, and the Instituto de Salud Carlos III, and procedures have been performed according to local and National regulations. The blood and cumulus cell donor provided written informed consent before being included in the study. Generated hiPSC lines will be deposited in the Spanish National Stem Cell Bank (BNLC), following the established legal and ethical guidelines of the Instituto de Salud Carlos III (ISCIII).

Somatic cell collection

The oocyte donor underwent hormonal stimulation with daily injections of 150–300 units of human Menopausal Gonadotropin (Menopur, Ferring). From day 7 of stimulation, a daily administration of 0.25 mg of GnRH antagonist (ganirelix, Orgalutran, Merck) was used for pituitary suppression. Ovulation was induced after the detection of three or more 18 mm-diameter follicles with a single injection of 0.2 mg of GnRH agonist triptorelin (Decapeptyl, Ipsen Pharma). Cumulus oocyte pickup was performed 36 hours after with ultrasound guidance. Cumulus oocyte complexes were then dissociated in 80 IU/mL hyaluronidase (Hyase-10×, Vitrolife, Sweden) in G-MOPS (Vitrolife, Sweden). Cumulus cells (CC) were then collected in 10 mL of DMEM/F12 (+L-Glutamine), 10% FBS, 1% Pen/Strep to inactivate the hyaluronidase, centrifuged and plated on matrigel-coated 4-well culture plates. Attached cells were then maintained at 37°C in normoxic conditions in the same media supplemented with 10 μM ROCKi for improving their survival. Medium was changed three times a week. Blood was collected in EDTA tubes at the same visit as cumulus-oocyte complexes, and processed within the next few hours.

Sendai-GFP infectivity test

Previous to the reprogramming of cumulus cells using Sendai viruses, we tested whether cumulus cells were prone to Sendai transduction. For that we used the Cytotune EmGFP Sendai Fluorescent Reporter (A16519, ThermoFisher). Cumulus cells were obtained as described above. On the day of Sendai emGFP transduction, three different multiplicity of infection ratios (MOIs) were tested (1, 3, 5). Cells were screened for emGFP expression 24 h and 48 h post-transduction using an EVOS imaging system.

Somatic cell reprogramming

Both peripheral blood mononuclear blood cells and cumulus cells were retrieved from the same female donor.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cell reprogramming

Erythroid progenitors, which represent a small fraction of the PBMCs population, were isolated and expanded using the Erythroid Progenitor Reprogramming Kit (StemCell Technologies, cat# 05924) as indicated by the manufacturer. Briefly, hematopoietic progenitor cells were pre-enriched from whole blood using a density gradient and cultured in Erythroid Expansion Medium for 7 days in order to expand the erythroid progenitor cells. After expansion, erythroid progenitor cells were transduced with the CytoTune-iPS 2.0 Sendai Reprogramming Kit (Invitrogen, cat# A16517) for the generation of hiPSCs. Again, the manufacturer’s recommended MOIs were used. Three days after transduction, erythroid progenitor cells were plated onto iMEFs-coated wells of a 6-well plate at two different densities, 1*104 and 1*105 cells/well. Two days later, the erythroid expansion medium was gradually changed to ReproTeSR (Stemcell Technologies cat# 5926). From day 8 after transduction onwards, media was changed daily and wells were monitored for colony formation. Colonies were manually picked and plated directly on Matrigel-coated plates in mTeSR-1 medium (Stemcell Technologies, cat# 85851 and 85852) or on iMEF-coated plates. Each clone was daily monitored and differentiating cells were manually eliminated from the culture. The best clones were passaged at least once with EDTA and expanded in Matrigel-coated 6-well plates before freezing.

C‌C reprogramming

After hyaluronidase dissociation, we plated cumulus cells onto 4 wells of a matrigel-coated 4-well plate and left them in culture for 2–3 days for cells to recover. From the original 4 wells, one was passage into 4 wells of a 12-well plate, two of which were coated with gelatin 0.1%, and the other two with Matrigel. For passaging, cells were dissociated with TrypLE, centrifuged for 5 min at 200 g, and re-plated 1:4 into 4 wells of a 12-well plate previously coated with gelatin or Matrigel. ROCKi was added to the media for cell survival. However, passaged cells did not proliferate enough for Sendai transduction. Two of the original 4 wells of cumulus cells, which presented different cell densities were directly transduced using the CytoTune-iPS 2.0 Sendai Reprogramming Kit (Invitrogen, cat# A16517) using the indicated MOIs (hKOS = 5, hc-MYC = 5, hKLF4 = 3) and the fourth well was left as a non-transduced control. The next day, the media was changed to remove the reprogramming factors. Transduced cells were kept in maintenance media for seven days and then passaged onto iMEFs. Over the next three days, media was gradually changed to ReproTeSR and small colonies started to appear. During the next 10–15 days colonies were manually picked and plated either on iMEFs or directly in Matrigel. After those 10–15 days, the well with the transduced cells was also dissociated using TrypLE and re-plated onto new iMEFs. Afterwards, over the next 15–20 days more colonies continued to appear that were manually picked and plated on Matrigel in mTeSR-1 medium. Each clone was daily observed and differentiating cells were manually eliminated from the culture. The best clones were passaged at least once with EDTA and expanded in Matrigel-coated 6-well plates before freezing.

hiPSC maintenance

The generated hiPSCs were regularly maintained on matrigel-coated plates and with the commercial media mTeSR-1 (Stemcell Technologies, cat# 85851 and 85852). Media was changed daily. Cells were passaged every 5–6 days using a non-enzymatic dissociation method. Briefly, cells were rinsed with PBS and incubated for a maximum of 3 min with 0.5 mM EDTA, pH 8. The dissociation solution was removed and cells were detached from the dish using mTeSR-1. Cell clumps were collected in 15 mL conical tubes and centrifuged for 5 min at 200 g. Cells were then plated using a 1:15 dilution and the rock inhibitor Y-27632 (Tocris Bioscience, cat# 1254) was added for the first 24 hours at a concentration of 10 μM.

Cumulus and blood-derived hiPSCs characterization

Differentiation into the three germ layers

Both PBMCs and CC-derived iPSCs were differentiated into ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm according to the protocol provided by STEMCELL Technologies with the STEMdiff™ Trilineage Differentiation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, cat# 05230) with the following modification: Day 0 iPSCs were plated on Corning® Matrigel® Growth Factor Reduced (Corning, cat# 356231).

FACS analysis of the trilineage differentiation

FACS analysis of intracellular ectodermal markers (PAX6, SOX2) was carried out on day 7. Cells were collected, washed once in PBS, and fixed in 4% Paraformaldehyde (Sigma, P6148) for 10 min at room temperature. After fixation, cells were washed twice with PBS and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sigma, X-100) for 10 min at room temperature. Cell types were stained with the respective antibodies (see Supplementary Table 1) for 1 hour at 4°C in FACS Buffer (0.1% BSA in PBS). Cells were then washed twice in FACS Buffer and analyzed using an LSRII flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

FACS analysis of mesodermal and endodermal surface markers (CD144, CD140b, SOX17, and CD184) was carried out on day 5. Cells were collected, washed once in PBS, and directly stained with the corresponding marker for 1 hour at 4°C in FACS Buffer (0.1% BSA in PBS). Cells were then washed twice in FACS Buffer and analyzed using an LSRII flow cytometer. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software v10.8.0.

Immunofluorescence

To assess pluripotency and proper differentiation into the three germ layers, cells were washed once in PBS and fixed in 4% Paraformaldehyde (Sigma, P6148) for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were fixed at day 5 for mesoderm and endoderm lineages and at day 7 for ectoderm lineage. After fixation cells were washed twice with PBS and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sigma, X-100) for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were blocked for 1 hour at 4°C in PBS + 10% BSA (Sigma, A2058). Cells were stained overnight at 4°C. Antibodies concentrations and references are reported in Supplementary Table 1. Primary antibodies LIN28A (ab63740) and NANOG (ab63740) were kindly provided by Jarek Sochacki, EMBL Barcelona, Spain. Nuclei were visualized with DAPI (Sigma, 1 mg/mL).

G-banded karyotyping

One 70% confluent 10 cm dish of both PBMCs and CC-derived iPSCs was arrested with 0.02 μg/mL KaryoMAX Colcemid (Gibco, cat# 15212012) for 4 hours at 37°C. Cells were carefully washed once with PBS (Gibco, cat# 14190–094) and harvested by Trypsin 0.05%-EDTA (Gibco, cat# 25300054) treatment for 5 min at 37°C. Trypsin was inactivated with DMEM (Gibco, cat# 10565–018) + 10% FBS (Thermos, cat# 10270-106) and centrifuged 120 g for 5 min at room temperature. The supernatant was aspirated and the cell pellet was gently resuspended in 10 mL of hypotonic solution (75 mM KCl). After incubation for 30 min at 37°C, 1 mL of freshly prepared methanol/acetic acid fixative (3:1, 4°C) was added dropwise to the swollen cells to pre-fix them. Cells were pelleted at 200 g for 10 min, and most of the supernatant was removed, leaving behind ~100 μL to resuspend the cells by gentle flicking. Then 10 mL of ice-cold fixative was added dropwise. Metaphase spreads were stained with Giemsa dye and scanned using a GSL-120 scanner (Leica Biosystems). G-banding analysis was performed using CytoVision software.

RNA-fish

The XIST probe consisting of 4 oligos (BD419: AAA GCA GGT ATC CGC GGC CCC GAT GG GC; BD420: GGT CAC TTA CAA TTG TGC ACC TTG ATT GTC CA; BD421: GTC TTA TGG AGT GGG CAC TCC CTG CTG G; BD422: GGG CCT TGG TGA TCA GCA CCC CTG CTG TAC TGC AA) with each a 3′-Cy5 modification was purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies. A bulk stock of XIST probe (10 ng/μL each oligo) was prepared in hybridization buffer (25% formamide, 10% dextran sulfate, 2× SSC, 20 mM RVC, 0.5 μg/μL yeast tRNA (Life Technologies, cat# 15401029), 0.08 μg/μL human COT DNA (Merck, cat# 11581074001)).

XACT and ATRX probes were synthesized from bacterial artificial chromosomes (BAC) RP11-35D3 and RP11-42 M11, respectively, as described below.

BAC amplification

Bacteria containing the BAC with the desired sequence (XACT/ATRX) were amplified from E. coli glycerol stocks. Briefly, glycerol stock of each BAC was smeared in LB plates with 12.5 μg/mL chloramphenicol. Plates were incubated at 37°C O/N. On the next day, two single colonies per clone were picked and used to seed 5 mL starter cultures. 8 hours later starter cultures were used to inoculate 250 mL cultures (LB + 12.5 μg/mL chloramphenicol), which were grown O/N in a shaking incubator at 37°C and 200 rpm. On the next day, fresh chloramphenicol-containing media was added and the cultures were grown for another 24 hours at 37°C, 200 rpm. Next, bacteria were harvested and BACs were purified using the NucleoBond Xtra BAC kit (Macherey-Nagel, cat# 740436.10), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Nick translation and probe purification

Nick Translation Mix (Roche, cat# 11745808910) was used to synthesize the XACT and ATRX RNA-FISH probes. Firstly, a fluorophore labeling mix was prepared according to the Nick Translation Mix instructions. We used Cy3-dUTP (ChemCruz, cat# sc-472858) to label both probes. Next, 1 μg of template DNA (isolated BAC as described in the section above), fluorophore labeling mix, and nick translation mix were added into a reaction tube and incubated for at least 1 hour and 30 min at 15°C. Once the probe had the desired size (200–500 bp), the reaction was stopped by adding 1 μL of 0.5 M EDTA and heating to 65°C for 10 min. To purify the probe, Sephadex G-50 columns were used (Illustra ProbeQuant G-50 Micro Columns, GE cat# 28903408). Finally, the probe was precipitated by adding 0.7× volumes of EtOH and 0.1× volumes of 3 M sodium acetate O/N at −80°C, and centrifuged for 1 hour at 4°C and maximum speed. After a wash with 70% EtOH, probes were resuspended in 50 μL of hybridization buffer (25% formamide, 10% dextran sulfate, 2× SSC, 20 mM RVC, 0.5 μg/μL yeast tRNA (Life Technologies, cat# 15401029), 0.08 μg/μL human COT DNA (Merck, cat# 11581074001)) and stored at −20°C until use.

Hybridization

Human pluripotent stem cells were grown on top of coverslips in 6-well plates. At confluence, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min at 4°C and stored at −20°C in 70% EtOH. Hybridization probes were thawed at room temperature and denatured for 10 min at 80°C followed by 30 min at 37°C. Timed with the denaturation step, cells were dehydrated by a serial treatment with 70-80%–100% EtOH, 2 min each. Last, cells were left to air-dry completely. Pre-annealed probes were briefly spun and pipetted directly onto a slide. Cover slides with cells facing the hybridization mix were placed on top of the slides, air bubbles were removed and rubber cement was used to seal. Slides were placed in a humid chamber and incubated at 37°C O/N. The next day, cells were washed 3 times for 5 min in 50% (vol/vol) formamide in 2× SSC at 45°C and 3 more times, 5 min each, in 2× SSC at 37°C. Last, cover slides were mounted in Fluoromount with DAPI (Life technologies, cat# 00–4959-52) and stored O/N at room temperature. The next day cells were imaged using a Leica Dmi8 fluorescence microscope or stored at 4°C until imaged. 200 cells were counted for each triplicate and each cell was classified into six different categories represented in Figure 3. All images were used for maximum projection on a LAS Leica software before counting.

hPGCLC induction

Human primordial germ cell-like cells were induced from our selected hiPSC clones mostly following a previously described protocol [23] with slight modifications. Briefly, hiPSCs were initially induced towards incipient mesoderm-like cells (iMeLCs). To do so, hiPSCs were dissociated into a single-cell suspension and plated onto human plasma fibronectin (Sigma, cat# F0895)-coated 12-well plates at a density of 3*105 cells/well and cultured for 42 hours in iMeLCs induction media, which consists of GSK15 [GMEM (Wisent, cat# 322–005-CL), 15% KSR (Gibco, cat# 10828–028), 1× NEAA (Gibco, cat# 11140050), 1× glutamax (Gibco, cat# 5050061), 1× sodium pyruvate (Gibco, cat# 113600709) and 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco, cat# 31350010)] supplemented with freshly added 50 ng/mL Activin A (Peprotech, cat# 120–14), 3 μM CHIR99021 (Sigma Aldrich, cat# SML1046), 10 μM Y-27632 (Tocris Biosciences, cat# 1254) and 50 mM PD173074 (Tocris Biosciences, cat# 3044). Next, iMeLCs were dissociated to a single cell suspension and plated on EZSPHERE microplates (ASM, cat# 4810-900SP) at a density of 1.125*106 cells/well with hPGCLCs-induction media [GSK15 + freshly added 0.01 μg/mL hLIF (Peprotech, cat# 300-05), 200 ng/mL BMP4 (R&D Systems, cat# 314-BP-050), 100 ng/mL hSCF (Peprotech, cat# 300-07), 50 ng/mL hEGF (R&D Systems, cat# 236-EG) and 10 μM Y-27632 (Tocris Bioscience, cat#1254)]. hPGCLCs aggregates were cultured for 6 days and fresh media was added daily.

Flow cytometry

Analysis

Pluripotent stem cells grown on Matrigel-coated plates as well as incipient mesoderm-like cells grown on human plasma fibronectin-coated plates were dissociated to single cells using TrypLE. After centrifugation, cells were resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS + 0.1% BSA) and filtered through a 40 μM cell strainer. Human primordial germ cell-like cell bodies were dissociated into single cells using Trypsin 0.05%-EDTA (Gibco, cat# 25300054) containing 1× DNaseI for 10–20 min. After centrifugation, cells were resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS + 0.1% BSA) and filtered through a 70 μM cell strainer. After filtration, all cell types were stained with the respective antibodies (see Supplementary Table 1) during 1 hour at 4°C. Samples were analyzed using an LSR II flow cytometer. Data was later processed using FlowJo software.

Sorting

Human primordial germ cell-like cell bodies were dissociated on day 6 as described above. After centrifugation, cells were directly stained with the desired antibodies (see Supplementary Table 1) for 1 h at 4°C. Cells were filtered through a 70 μM cell strainer right before sorting. Human primordial germ cell-like cells were separated from the rest using a BD FACSAria sorter at 12 psi and a 100 μm wide nozzle. Data was processed using FlowJo software.

RT-qPCR

RNA extraction

For gene expression analysis, pellets of hiPSCs, iMeLCs, and hPGCLCs (whole body and sorted) were collected, resuspended in RLT buffer (RNeasy Mini kit, Qiagen, cat#74106), vortexed and kept at −80°C until processing. RNA extraction was performed according to the instructions of the kit (RNeasy Mini kit, Qiagen, cat#74106).

cDNA synthesis

For cDNA synthesis, we used the QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen, cat# 205314) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 100 ng of RNA were added to the corresponding cDNA reaction mix. Using a thermocycler, cDNA synthesis was performed by incubating for 30 min at 42°C. To stop the reaction, samples were incubated for 3 min at 95°C and held at 4°C. 180 μL of RNase-free water was added to each sample to dilute the cDNA.

Quantitative PCR

Quantitative PCR was performed using Quantinova SYBR Green (Qiagen, cat# 208056). Primers used for qPCR can be found in Supplementary Table 2. Reactions were run on a QuantStudio Pro6 qPCR (Applied Biosystems) using the following program: 2 min at 95°C, next 5 sec at 95°C and 17 sec at 60°C for 45 cycles, reading fluorescence at the end of the combined annealing/extension step. The program continued with the melting analysis for 10 sec at 95°C, 1 min at 64°C, and 1 sec at 95°C, reading fluorescence at the end. GAPDH was used as a housekeeping gene and the delta–delta Ct method was used for the analysis.

Statistical analysis

All descriptive data are presented as mean ± SD. The number of biological replicates is indicated in the figure legends. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.6.1). Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test, Ordinary one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test or lineage contrast multiple comparisons test were applied where appropriate. Results were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05 (*).

Results

Generation of isogenic female hiPSCs clones from blood and cumulus cells

We aimed to evaluate the feasibility of modeling patient-specific germ cell development in vitro from hiPSCs derived from two different somatic cell types of origin available in the fertility clinic setting (Figure 1A). We therefore collected blood and cumulus-oocyte complexes from a single female donor.

Experimental overview and hiPSC reprogramming. A Schematic representation of the experimental workflow. Obtention of cumulus / erythroid progenitor cells → infection with Sendai virus expressing reprogramming factors (human KOS = KLF4, OCT4, SOX2; KLF4; c-MYC) → hiPSC reprogramming from PBMCs (peripheral blood mononuclear cells) or CCs (cumulus cells) → derivation of hPGCLCs. B, C Phase contrast images of different stages of hiPSC reprogramming from isolated PBMCs (B) / CCs (C) (left) to fully reprogrammed hiPSCs (right).

For blood reprogramming, we isolated PBMCs, which are a defined, easily obtainable, and commonly used starting cell type for hiPSC reprogramming [24]. We followed standardized PBMC isolation and non-integrating Sendai virus-based reprogramming protocols (see methods). Briefly, after separating PBMCs, we enriched and expanded erythroid progenitors, transduced them with viral particles harboring the reprogramming factors OCT4, SOX2, KLF4, and c-MYC (CytoTune-iPS 2.0 Sendai Reprogramming Kit, Thermo Fisher) and reprogrammed them under primed culture conditions in ReproTeSR (Stemcell Technologies) medium on inactivated mouse embryonic fibroblast (iMEF)-feeder layers (Figure 1B). In the end, we obtained a total of 16 clones, of which we selected three (Clones S12, S2 and S6) for further use based on colony morphology and growth.

Besides PBMCs, we chose cumulus cells for reprogramming, as they are a usually discarded and therefore easily available by-product of oocyte retrieval in IVF clinics. Considering the lack of literature on cumulus cell reprogramming using Sendai virus, we first tested whether cumulus cells could be efficiently infected by it. To do so, we took advantage of the Cytotune EmGFP Sendai Fluorescent Reporter (Thermo Fisher, see methods). We infected cumulus cells at different MOIs and scanned the cells for the presence of GFP 48 hours later. We observed, as expected, that cumulus cells were infected by Sendai Virus proportionally to the MOI used (Supplementary Figure 1A). The next set of cells collected was used for reprogramming (Figure 1C). To increase our chances of success, we tested different approaches. As passaging of cumulus cells onto matrigel or gelatine proved unsuccessful due to poor cell proliferation, we chose to directly transduce cumulus cells using the CytoTune-iPS 2.0 Sendai Reprogramming Kit (Thermo Fisher) at two different cellular densities. Seven days after transduction, we dissociated cumulus cells into single cells and plated them on iMEF-feeder layers. From the two wells transduced, only the one with a lower cell density at the moment of transduction gave rise to pluripotent colonies. By the end of the process, a total of nine clones had been generated, three of which we selected (Clones 5, 7, and 9) for follow-up based on colony morphology and growth.

Blood- and cumulus-derived hiPSCs show normal karyotype, pluripotency, and differentiation potential

From all the obtained hiPSC clones, we chose a total of six (three blood-derived and three cumulus-derived) for characterization prior to differentiation into human primordial germ cell-like cells (hPGCLCs). All clones were mycoplasma-free (Supplementary Figure 1B), had undetectable levels of Sendai virus and external reprogramming factor RNA after 3–5 passages (Supplementary Figure 1C), and were karyotypically normal (Figure 2A and Supplementary Figure 2A). Furthermore, all were positive for expression of pluripotency markers, such as the transcriptional regulator NANOG, the RNA-binding protein LIN28 and the cell surface markers SSEA4 and Tra-1-81 (Figure 2B and Supplementary Figure 2B, C). Interestingly, hiPSC clones derived from cumulus cells (particularly clone 5) appeared to express higher levels of TRA-1-81, in comparison to hiPSC clones derived from PBMCs (except for clone 12; Supplementary Figure 2C). Moreover, the six selected clones were successfully induced towards the three embryonic germ layers ectoderm (SOX2, PAX6, and NESTIN positive), mesoderm (CD140b and T/BRACHYURY positive but no CD144 upregulation (excluding a differentiation into vascular endothelial cells)) and endoderm (SOX17, CXCR4 and FOXA2 positive) (Figure 2C-E and Supplementary Figure 3). While we observed some clone-to-clone variability in terms of trilineage differentiation, no major overall differences between PBMC- or cumulus cell-derived hiPSCs were apparent. In summary, all our six newly-derived hiPSC clones showed normal karyotypes, pluripotency factor expression and trilineage differentiation independent of their cell type of origin.

The X-chromosome state of hiPSCs varies between clones and is independent of the cell type of origin

It has been previously shown that the X-chromosome status of human primed pluripotent stem cells can influence their differentiation capacity towards different cell lineages [18], but it has been under debate if it influences hPGCLC differentiation efficiency [20, 22]. Therefore we analyzed the X-chromosome status of our different patient-derived hiPSC clones at low passages p4/9 using RNA-FISH. We chose to study the expression of the long non-coding RNAs XIST and XACT, which coat the inactive and active X chromosomes in primed human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs), respectively, as well as nascent transcripts for the X-linked gene ATRX, which is subject to X-chromosome inactivation and only expressed from the active X chromosome. In primed cells with erosion of X-inactivation, which have been reported to exhibit poor differentiation potential [18, 20], XIST would become gradually downregulated from the inactive X (Xi), while XACT and ATRX would become expressed from the Xi [25]. We classified our cells into six different categories depending on whether XIST was expressed, and how many XACT/ATRX signals, from none to biallelic, we observed for each clone (Figure 3A-C). The percentage of cells displaying an XIST cloud, indicative of X-chromosome inactivation at the primed state of pluripotency, ranged from around 45% (PBMC clone S6) to 95% (PBMC clone S2). Erosion of X-inactivation as indicated by biallelic XACT expression affected between 0% (PBMC clone S2) to 18% (PBMC clone S6) of the cells, but never led to reactivation of ATRX expression in more than 3% of the cells (Supplementary Figure 4). Clone CC5 displayed a unique XIST/XACT co-expression on the same X chromosome (16%), which is a hallmark of ongoing X-erosion [25], while clone PBMC S6 showed the lowest expression of XIST RNA (50%), indicating initiation of erosion. We concluded that our hiPSC clones showed variable, but overall mild degrees of X-inactivation erosion, consistent with high-quality cells of low passage number and robust trilineage differentiation potential. Furthermore, the cell type of origin did not consistently affect the X-chromosome state of our hiPSC lines since no global difference was observed between the cells derived from PBMCs or cumulus cells.

Cumulus and blood-derived hiPSCs show equal potential for primordial germ cell-like cell differentiation

We then assessed the six selected hiPSC clones for their capacity to differentiate towards human primordial germ cell-like cells (hPGCLCs) following an established protocol [7, 23] with small modifications (see methods, Figure 4A). Briefly, each clone was differentiated first for 2 days towards iMeLCs and then for 6 days into hPGCLCs by the addition of cytokines. While most clones displayed similar morphologies throughout the protocol, cumulus cell (CC)-derived clone 5 appeared more dome-shaped, brighter and overall less healthy at the iMeLC stage (Figure 4B).

We then quantified the hPGCLC differentiation efficiency of the clones by FACS analysis of the cell surface hPGCLC markers CD38 and alkaline phosphatase [6, 22]. Both PBMC- and CC-derived hiPSCs successfully differentiated towards hPGCLCs, however, not all the clones showed the same efficiency and we also observed a variability within each clone across different experiments (Figure 4C and Supplementary Figure 5). While five of the six clones differentiated towards hPGCLCs with similar efficiency (~10%), cumulus clone 5 (CC5) was practically unable to form hPGCLCs (~1%). This was corroborated by qRT-PCR analysis, showing expression of primordial germ cell-specific genes (SOX17, BLIMP1, TFAP2C, and NANOS3) only in the PGCLC-marker sorted-positive cells population for all six clones (Figure 4D). To see if the low induction efficiency of CC5 could be due to an alteration at the hiPSCs or iMeLC state, we analysed pluripotency genes such as OCT4, NANOG and SOX2 and the lineage priming marker OTX2 at the hiPSCs stage and mesoderm markers such as EOMES, MIXL1 and NODAL at the iMeLC stage. Overall, clone CC5 showed a higher expression of pluripotency genes at the hiPSCs state, lower expression of mesoderm markers at the iMeLC state and higher expression of other lineage markers CDH1, FGF2 and SOX3 at the iMeLC state (Figure 4D and Supplementary Figure 6). Although several clones showed gene expression patterns in a subset of genes that significantly deviated from the collective mean of the other five clones, CC5 was the only clone that matched this specific profile (Supplemental table 3). In summary, with the exception of clone CC5, we were able to differentiate hPGCLCs with comparable efficiencies both from blood and cumulus cell-derived hiPSCs, suggesting that germ cell capacity is not dependent on the cell type of origin of hiPSCs.

Discussion

In this work, we provide a proof-of-concept study for deriving isogenic patient-specific hiPSCs from adult somatic cells of different origins for in vitro germ cell differentiation applications. Specifically, we chose two cell types that are easily obtained in a fertility clinic setting without the need for additional invasive procedures for the patient: PBMCs, which can be isolated during regular blood draws and are a common source for hiPSCs generation [24], and cumulus cells, which are usually discarded as a by-product during oocyte retrieval. We used Sendai virus as delivery method for the Yamanaka factors, as this provides a standardized, efficient reprogramming method without integration into the genome, thereby avoiding undesired mutations and prolonged expression of exogenous pluripotency factors. In total we generated six hiPSC lines (three PBMC-derived and three cumulus cell-derived), which presented a normal karyotype, expressed pluripotency markers, and showed differentiation potential towards the three embryonic germ layers ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm.

To generate hPGCLCs, several protocols have been previously described and we selected an established protocol allowing robust induction of hPGCLCs from hiPSCs in a primed pluripotent state through involving an intermediate mesoderm state [2, 23]. Using this approach, we were able to derive hPGCLCs from all six hiPSC lines generated in this study, albeit from one clone (CC5) with a very low efficiency. A previous study demonstrated that hPSC lines with low levels of endogenous NODAL may have higher capacity to undergo hPGCLC differentiation, and our analysis showed a higher expression of NODAL in clone CC5 compared to the others clones [12]. Another study using the same protocol with approximately nine hiPSC lines derived from PBMCs or fibroblasts demonstrated that hPGCLC differentiation efficiency positively correlates with high expression levels of MIXL1 and EOMES and negatively correlates with high levels of SOX3, CDH1 and FGF2 in iMeLCs [26]. Consistent with these findings, our results showed that iMeLCs derived from clone CC5 exhibited low expression levels of MIXL1 and EOMES and high expression levels of SOX3, CDH1, and FGF2 compared to the other clones, explaining CC5’s reduced capacity for hPGCLC differentiation.

We successfully obtained hPGCLCs from both cumulus cell and PBMC-derived hiPSCs, demonstrating their equal potential for in vitro germ cell development. This poses the question of which cell type of origin—PBMCs or cumulus cells—would be better suited as starting material for future applications, such as disease modeling of reproductive disorders in vitro. An advantage of PBMCs is that they can be obtained from any sex, and in women from both oocyte donors and infertile patients (including women without eggs), as blood sample draws are commonly performed before oocyte donation or fertility treatments. On the other hand, cumulus cells are limited in their availability as they can be only obtained from egg donors and patients during egg retrieval thereby restricting their application to women with eggs. While we did not detect in our analysis differences in germ cell differentiation capacity based on cell type of origin, a previous study did [22]. This might have been due to different cell types of origin (skin fibroblasts and kidney epithelia from urine) and reprogramming methods (RNA transfection, lentivirus, episomes and Sendai virus) used. Cell type of origin might thereby influence germ cell potential depending on the experimental setup. Another parameter, which could influence the suitability of cumulus- versus PBMC-derived hiPSCs is their somatic mutation rate. While we could not find a study comparing mutation rates between PBMCs and cumulus cells, it might be conceivable that differences exist and further analysis would be warranted in particular before considering clinical applications of these hiPSCs.

Apart from the cell type of origin, the epigenetic state of hiPSCs might impact their differentiation potential towards the germ cell lineage. Indeed, germ cell specification and competence are influenced by multiple layers of epigenetic regulation such as global and locus-specific DNA methylation patterns, histone modifications, imprinting status, and X-chromosome state [20, 27]. Since X-erosion is known to happen during hPSC cell culture [25, 28], we therefore studied the X-chromosome status of our different lines using RNA-FISH. Mild and heterogeneous X-chromosome erosion was observed across lines, with no global association with germ cell differentiation capacity. For example, the cumulus-derived hiPSC clone CC5, exhibiting the highest degree of XIST and XACT dual-coating, indicating initiation of erosion of the X chromosome [25], showed markedly reduced, if any, differentiation into hPGCLCs. The PBMC-derived clone S6, which displayed the biggest loss in XIST coating, indicating potentially a more advanced eroding X chromosome, retained hPGCLC differentiation efficiency comparable to other lines. These observations indicate that specific X-erosion states, rather than erosion per se, may be detrimental to germ cell differentiation, potentially accounting for discrepancies among previous reports [20, 22]. While the X-chromosome state might affect PGCLC specification to a lesser extent, it might influence more the further steps of germ cell progression, including epigenetic reprogramming, meiosis and oogenesis, as observed in the mouse, non-human primate and human systems [15, 21, 29]. Thus resetting of the X-chromosome state through transient naive culture of hiPSCs [30–33] could lead to a more homogenous, in vivo-like X-chromosome state beneficial for subsequent germ cell differentiation. The recent development of protocols that extend the culture period—such as reconstituted ovaries with somatic cells or co-culture with amnion-like cells—could build upon our findings [16, 17, 23]. These approaches would allow assessing whether hPGCLCs derived from hPSCs of different X-states display different capacities to enter and progress through meiosis, and mirror more faithfully the epigenetic dynamics observed during primordial germ cell development in vivo. Further optimizations in culture conditions will be needed to be able to precisely model the entire process of human oogenesis in vitro.

In summary, here we provide a proof-of-principle that hiPSCs of cumulus cell and PBMC origin can be used as the starting material for generating patient-specific hPGCLCs. This will offer a useful tool for future modeling of human germ cell development and infertility disorders in vitro.
