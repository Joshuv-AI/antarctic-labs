Multisuture craniosynostosis and acquired Chiari malformation secondary to vitamin D deficiency rickets: illustrative case

Arising from genetic mutations, metabolic disturbances, or mechanical factors, craniosynostosis represents a clinically and etiologically heterogeneous disorder. While congenital examples often present within the 1st year of life, acquired craniosynostosis associated with metabolic etiologies can exhibit a delayed onset. Rickets, a historically recognized metabolic cause of craniosynostosis, results from a defect in bone mineralization due to deficiencies in calcium, phosphorous, and/or vitamin D.1–4 Rickets can have genetic, nutritional, environmental, or metabolic causes. Despite significant global efforts to promote vitamin D supplementation, nutritional deficiency rickets remains a common preventable disease.5

Given the overlapping clinical features of metabolic craniosynostosis with genetic or syndromic forms, identification of the underlying cause fosters appropriate multidisciplinary care. Prompt correction of the metabolic disturbance may prevent further skeletal complications. Similarly, early detection and treatment of elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) (if present) may mitigate risks of visual or neurocognitive impairment.

In this case report, we describe a unique presentation of multisuture craniosynostosis with overt signs of elevated ICP and acquired Chiari malformation type I (CM-I) in the setting of nutritional rickets due to vitamin D deficiency. The patient underwent open mid- and posterior cranial vault expansion and bone-only posterior fossa decompression (PFD) to address both the restricted cranial volume and associated hindbrain herniation.

Illustrative Case

A 2-year-old boy with a past medical history of severe vitamin D deficiency rickets and asthma presented to the craniofacial and neurosurgery team clinic with the chief complaint of skull prominence at the bregma and headache. The diagnosis of rickets occurred around age 12 months when a chest radiograph obtained during a viral infection revealed osteomalacia and rachitic changes. At that time, the anterior fontanelle appeared large and open, without skull imaging performed. Following initiation of vitamin D, calcium, and phosphate supplementation, the patient’s electrolyte and vitamin D levels normalized prior to the craniofacial team clinic visit. Imaging (performed at the initial craniofacial clinic visit at age 2 years) demonstrated a slightly elongated cranial vault, multifocal scalloping along the inner calvaria, fusion of the sagittal and superior aspects of bilateral lambdoid sutures, and cranial elevation or prominence at the bregma (Fig. 1). Additional imaging included brain MRI with or without contrast and MR venography (MRV) that demonstrated normal to small ventricular size, optic nerve head elevation suggesting papilledema, and CM-I (Fig. 2). The brain MRI with contrast and MRV (performed to evaluate the dural venous sinuses and venous outflow) demonstrated mild decreased caliber of the left transverse and sigmoid sinuses. The constellation of imaging findings suggested the presence of elevated ICP. Ophthalmology examination at that time revealed bilateral papilledema with 360° of nerve elevation and superior disc edema. Based on thorough history taking and questioning, there were no developmental concerns reported. Skeletal survey imaging of knees and wrists showed resolution of the prior rachitic changes. With the concern for elevated ICP, the patient underwent initiation of acetazolamide as a medical temporization measure. Based on the above findings, the patient underwent evaluation by a medical geneticist. Given the lack of relevant medical history, physical and developmental findings typically associated with ­syndromic craniosynostosis, and contributory family history, ­molecular genetic testing was not pursued. Furthermore, the improvement of previous radiological rachitic changes with nutritional (vitamin D) supplementation likely negated genetic or other metabolic vitamin D–related causes.

Given the findings of sagittal and partial bilateral lambdoid multisutural craniosynostosis, often termed Mercedes Benz pattern craniosynostosis,6 and acquired CM-I, along with clinical symptoms and signs of elevated ICP, operative intervention was recommended. Surgical goals included cranial vault expansion and foramen magnum decompression for the acquired CM-I. The patient underwent middle and posterior cranial vault remodeling with elevation of occipital and parietal bone flaps and concurrent bone-only PFD (Fig. 3). The inner table of the calvaria showed extensive scalloping or “thumbprinting,” suggesting evidence of chronic ICP elevation. Otherwise, the cranial thickness and quality observed intraoperatively were unremarkable. The craniotomy bone flaps were expanded via outfracturing and fixated in routine fashion with resorbable hardware. Following standard postoperative care and unremarkable recovery, the patient underwent hospital discharge on postoperative day 3. With continued improvement in papilledema by postoperative day 14, the patient and family did not continue acetazolamide therapy following medication supply depletion (2 months total). The papilledema fully resolved by the next ophthalmological assessment (3 months postoperatively). The patient has not experienced recurrent headaches or other clinical symptoms of elevated ICP for at least 15 months postoperatively, with ongoing neurosurgical and craniofacial postoperative surveillance clinic visits. The patient has continued to meet developmental milestones postoperatively. Repeat brain MRI performed 15 months postoperatively demonstrated (compared to preoperative examination) decreased cerebellar tonsillar herniation (6–7 mm), resolved optic disc protrusion and optic nerve sheath dilation, improved caliber of the transverse and sigmoid sinuses, and increased CSF flow at the cervicocranial junction.

Discussion

Craniosynostosis with late onset, delayed presentation, multisuture involvement, or atypical features necessitates thorough evaluation and a broad differential diagnosis. Early identification of underlying metabolic etiologies may guide appropriate medical management and mitigate the risks of systemic involvement and/or neurocognitive impairments.

Observations

Metabolic Causes of Craniosynostosis

Nutritional vitamin D deficiency represents a rare cause of secondary or acquired craniosynostosis, marked by impaired bone mineralization during periods of rapid cranial growth. Insufficient vitamin D limits intestinal calcium absorption, leading to hypocalcemia and triggering secondary hyperparathyroidism. Elevated parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels promote bone resorption to maintain serum calcium, but this compensation further disrupts normal bone mineralization. In the developing calvaria, defective mineralization prevents osteoid maturation, altering the balance of osteoblastic and osteoclastic activity at the sutures.4 This dysregulated remodeling may cause the premature fusion of cranial sutures. The persistence of cranial suture fusion after resolution of other rickets-associated skeletal changes, as seen in our patient, represents an interesting contrast in the manifestation of this disease without a clear mechanism. There are a few other published cases of craniosynostosis due to vitamin D deficiency rickets7,8 in the literature. These include patients both with and without evidence of elevated ICP at presentation, and no prior patients with concurrent acquired CM-I.

Genetic forms of vitamin D deficiency and related craniosynostosis also exist, offering a distinct biological mechanism but with a similar clinical presentation. Vitamin D–dependent rickets type 1 (VDDR1) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic mutations in the CYP27B1 gene, leading to impaired 1-alpha hydroxylase activity and subsequent failure to convert 25-hydroxyvitamin D to its biologically active form.9,10 Affected individuals typically present in infancy with signs and symptoms of hypocalcemia. In contrast to nutritional rickets, VDDR1 does not respond to standard vitamin D supplementation due to the underlying enzymatic defect. Patients with VDDR1 may develop craniosynostosis, although the true incidence remains unknown due to the rarity of the condition.

Given their diverse pathophysiological mechanisms and potential for significant craniofacial and neurological sequalae, metabolic causes of craniosynostosis beyond VDDR should be considered in the diagnostic evaluation of craniosynostosis patients with atypical presentations (late onset, delayed presentation, multisuture involvement, atypical features). Among these causes, X-linked hypophosphatemic rickets (XLHR), resulting from pathogenic variants in the PHEX gene, represents the most extensively characterized.11 Loss of PHEX function disrupts phosphate homeostasis through excessive renal phosphate wasting, leading to defective skeletal mineralization and characteristic osseous deformities.12,13 Radiographic evidence of craniosynostosis, most commonly involving the sagittal suture, occurred in 59% of individuals with XLHR and available CT imaging.11

Hypophosphatasia represents another well-established metabolic cause of craniosynostosis. This rare inborn error of metabolism arises from mutations in the ALPL gene, which encodes the tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase enzyme essential for bone mineralization.14,15 Enzymatic deficiency results in impaired skeletal matrix deposition and accumulation of inorganic pyrophosphate, a known inhibitor of mineralization.16 Clinically, hypophosphatasia occurs with elevated serum calcium and phosphate levels and resulted in craniosynostosis in 7 of 20 patients in 1 case series.14

Other metabolic conditions, including pseudohypoparathyroidism and mucopolysaccharidosis (MPS), have been implicated in the pathogenesis of multisuture craniosynostosis. Pseudohypoparathyroidism, a disorder of end-organ resistance to PTH, results in impaired calcium and phosphate homeostasis, which disrupts normal bone remodeling and contributes to premature suture fusion.17,18 Similarly, MPS represents a heterogeneous group of lysosomal storage disorders caused by enzymatic deficiencies in glycosaminoglycan (GAG) degradation.19,20 Accumulation of GAGs within connective tissues results in progressive multisystem dysfunction, including skeletal dysplasia, airway obstruction, and neurocognitive impairment. Craniosynostosis may occur in severe forms of MPS (affecting 77% of all MPS patients reported at a single institution20) and frequently co-presents with thickened calvarial bones and shortened skull base structures.19

Features of Multisuture Craniosynostosis

Multisuture craniosynostosis, particularly when acquired and progressive, initiates a complex cascade of cranial morphological changes and neurological sequelae driven by impaired intracranial volume expansion. As multiple sutures fuse prematurely, the compensatory capacity of the calvaria to accommodate cerebral growth declines gradually, leading to elevated ICP and secondary deformation of the cranial vault. Radiographic findings including scalloping (or thumbprinting) of the inner calvaria and copper-beaten patterns—­characterized by impressions along the inner calvarial table—­represent classic markers of chronic ICP elevation.21 These indentations result from brain expansion against an increasingly rigid cranial vault and often co-present with effacement of cortical sulci on neuroimaging.22 The “volcano sign” refers to an osseous prominence at the bregma or anterior fontanelle and is thought to be a consequence of ICP elevation.23

Clinically, young children may present with nonspecific symptoms of elevated ICP, including irritability, headaches, vomiting, developmental delay, and poor feeding. As the condition progresses, more overt clinical signs such as frontal bossing, occipital flattening, or a prominent bregma may emerge, reflecting localized compensatory growth at sites of delayed suture closure.24 Ophthalmological assessment may also serve as a critical window into intracranial dynamics, with papilledema representing a potential marker of sustained ICP elevation. If left unrecognized, prolonged papilledema can result in optic atrophy and permanent vision loss.25

Progressive restriction of cranial growth may further alter posterior fossa anatomy, leading to acquired Chiari malformation.26 This condition involves inferior displacement of the cerebellar tonsils through the foramen magnum, disrupting normal CSF dynamics and potentially causing compression of the cervicomedullary junction. This acquired (rather than congenital) form of Chiari malformation has been reported in metabolic variants of craniosynostosis, including hypophosphatemic rickets.11 In these metabolic disorders, defective bone mineralization and progressive suture fusion result in reduced posterior fossa volume, amplifying the risk of hindbrain descent. The potential external morphological and neurological sequelae underscore the importance of early recognition, thorough clinical assessment, neuroimaging, and timely intervention in patients with suspected metabolic multisuture craniosynostosis and clinical signs or symptoms of elevated ICP. While evidence addressing whether early diagnosis and treatment of vitamin D deficiency can prevent the development of craniosynostosis remains limited, the potential for resolution likely depends on the timing and severity of presentation. Correction of the underlying metabolic abnormality may mitigate abnormal cranial remodeling if treatment occurs prior to established suture fusion. However, once true bony synostosis has developed, reversal with medical therapy alone remains unlikely, and surgical intervention may still be required.

Lessons

While subject to standard limitations of a singular descriptive case report including selection bias, this unique case illustrates the importance of multidisciplinary evaluation and management for children with secondary craniosynostosis, particularly due to metabolic causes. Secondary or postnatal craniosynostosis often does not present with characteristic head shape changes. However, it may be associated with elevated ICP and acquired CM-I, which may require surgical intervention. Late presentation or diagnosis of craniosynostosis in young children without typical features should prompt investigation for underlying disorders of bone metabolism. Additionally, elevation at the bregma in a child may represent an important secondary sign of intracranial hypertension and should prompt further imaging evaluation. In the developed world, vitamin D deficiency rickets can be associated with poor access to healthcare, which can make diagnosis of associated abnormalities challenging. The potential for a disorder of bone metabolism to affect neurocognitive development in early childhood should prompt examination of the skull or specialist referral in children diagnosed with rickets.
