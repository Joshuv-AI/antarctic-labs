Baby-Feed Pilot Trial for Improving Diet and Weight Gain in Infants and Toddlers
Authors: Jennifer Bolton, Wenjia Wang, Catherine Coccia, Vijaya Narayanan, Marcia Varella, Jean Hannan, Cristina Palacios
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo pilot test the initial efficacy of the Baby-Feed website for improving infant diet quality (main outcome) and weight gain (exploratory outcome) compared to usual care.MethodsThis was a 3-month randomized clinical trial, in which two healthcare providers and 50 caregivers of healthy-term infants (4–12 months) in a community health center were recruited. Participants were randomized to usual care or access to the Baby-Feed website, which assesses infants’ diets, provides automated results, and delivers feedback. Measures were assessed at baseline and at the end of the study: diet quality from a validated infant food frequency questionnaire (FFQ), self-reported weight/length to calculate rate of weight gain, and a validated self-efficacy questionnaire. Changes were compared using analysis of covariance or Chi-square/Fisher’s Exact test.ResultsBaseline characteristics were similar between groups (n = 25 intervention; n = 25 control). Most caregivers were mothers (92%) and Hispanic (86%). Change in total diet quality scores was higher in the intervention (-2.95 ± 6.53) vs. controls (-7.69 ± 7.84; p = 0.031). Healthy weight gain was higher in the intervention group (n = 21, 95%) vs. controls (n = 14, 58%; p = 0.006). Changes in the self-efficacy score for promoting healthy foods were associated with changes in the fruit score (r = 0.46; p = 0.03), and changes in the self-efficacy score for limiting unhealthy foods were associated with changes in the juice score (r = 0.45; p = 0.04) only in the intervention group.ConclusionsUsing Baby-Feed for 3 months improved diet quality and the rate of weight gain compared with controls in this small and short pilot trial. A larger, longer trial with a more diverse population is needed to confirm the results.Trial Registryhttps://clinicaltrials.gov/: NCT05062824.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10995-026-04282-z.

Introduction

Rapid infant weight gain increases the risk of obesity later in childhood (Arisaka et al., 2020). Certain feeding practices, such as low breastfeeding rates (Clayton et al. 2024a; Qiao et al. 2020), early introduction of complementary foods (Clayton et al. 2024a; Padhani et al. 2023; Pearce et al. 2013), and sugar-sweetened beverage intake (Clayton et al. 2024b; Pan et al. 2014; Robinson et al. 2022) increase that risk. Generally, studies have found low diet quality in infants (Amaro-Rivera et al., 2019; Au et al., 2023; Rios et al., 2016; Roess et al., 2018), which is even lower after the 1 st year (Amaro-Rivera et al., 2019) and is related to obesity (Au et al., 2023). This is important to address, as the complementary feeding period (6–23 months) is critical for meeting infants’ nutritional needs, beyond breast milk or formula, during this rapid growth period (Soriano et al. 2023; World Health Organization 2023), with little room for low-nutritional-value foods in their diet (Bailey et al., 2022).

There are several educational interventions delivered to caregivers of infants to improve diet quality and weight gain, with varying levels of success (Hunter et al., 2025; Johnson et al., 2022; Koplin et al., 2019). Interventions using technology devices may reduce some of these limitations, as these devices are available 24/7. To date, there are only a few interventions using technology among caregivers with infants (Fiks et al., 2017; Health Ontario 2021; Jiang et al., 2014; Karssen et al., 2022; Laws et al., 2021; Lozada-Tequeanes et al., 2024; Palacios et al., 2018), with significant improvements on diet (Fiks et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2014; Laws et al., 2021; Lozada-Tequeanes et al., 2024) and weight in some studies (Karssen et al., 2022) but not in others (Fiks et al., 2017; Palacios et al., 2018). However, most interventions focused on delivering social media messages with weekly videos (Fiks et al., 2017), email messages with access to videos and recipes (Røed et al., 2021), and/or text messages (Lozada-Tequeanes et al., 2024; Palacios et al., 2018).

Delivering interventions during routine well-child visits provides several opportunities for intervening. Although the available interventions have shown some success in improving diet and weight gain (Messito et al., 2020; Morandi et al., 2019; Schroeder et al., 2015; Taveras et al., 2011), these may not be easily translatable/maintained due to a lack of time and training on nutrition among healthcare providers (Bassin et al., 2020). Combining the delivery of the intervention in the healthcare setting with technology-based approaches has also shown some success in improving diet and weight gain (Heerman et al., 2024; Helle et al., 2019). But, to our knowledge, none of these interventions evaluate infants’ diets, providing automatic results and feedback (Johnson et al., 2022, 2025).

Therefore, we designed the Baby-Feed website as an easy-to-use online tool for healthcare providers and caregivers to assess infants’ diets, provide automated results, and deliver feedback to improve infants’ diets. This website was based on a validated tool for assessing infants’ diets (Palacios et al., 2017) and was developed using known behavioral theories (Palacios et al., 2023). The purpose of the present study was to pilot-test the initial efficacy of the Baby-Feed intervention to improve infants’ diet quality (main outcome) and weight gain, and caregivers’ self-efficacy (exploratory outcomes) compared with usual care in the healthcare setting using a randomized controlled trial (RCT). We hypothesized that the intervention would significantly improve diet quality and potentially improve the rate of infant weight gain and caregivers’ self-efficacy.

Methods

Design

This pilot trial tested whether using Baby-Feed for 3 months improved diet quality (main outcome) and weight gain (exploratory outcome) in infants compared with usual care in the healthcare setting using a randomized parallel-group controlled trial (RCT) with a convenience-sampling approach. We also explored changes in caregivers’ self-efficacy. We recruited 2 healthcare providers and 50 caregivers of infants 4–12 months from a community healthcare center in South Florida. After explaining the study, all participants signed an informed consent. The Florida International University Institutional Review Board approved all study procedures (Trial registration: NCT05062824). Patients or the public were not directly involved in the design, conduct, and reporting of the trial; however, we conducted a feasibility, acceptability, and usability pilot test among a group of healthcare providers and 25 mothers with infants from the same center (Palacios et al., 2023), and based on their suggestions, the Baby-Feed website was improved. There were no changes to the trial once it commenced. The study was conducted in 2021–2022.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

This study recruited caregivers of healthy and term infants aged 4–12 months. The caregiver needed to have internet access, provide informed consent, be willing to be randomized, and participate for 3 months. Those with special diets, limited mobility, preterm birth (< 37 weeks), and small or large for gestational age (birthweight < 10th or > 90th percentile) were excluded.

Recruitment and Enrollment

A convenience sample of 2 healthcare providers and 50 caregivers was recruited from Borinquen Health Centers, during well-child visits in September-November of 2021. Staff from the healthcare center helped recruit caregivers by briefly describing the study during reminder calls. If the caregivers agreed to participate, the research team met with them in the waiting area before their appointment to describe the study and complete a pre-screening form. Eligible caregivers were asked to sign the written informed consent (English/Spanish) via Qualtrics (a secure, web-based survey platform) using the study tablet. Caregivers were also recruited through study flyers (English/Spanish) posted in the medical centers’ waiting area. We used computer-generated block randomization with 1:1 allocation between intervention and control, stratified by healthcare provider. An equal number of participants (n = 25) were recruited for each of the 2 healthcare providers.

Study Groups

Intervention Group

The intervention (n = 25) received usual care during their well-child visits, plus full access to the Baby-Feed web application (English/Spanish). A detailed description of Baby-Feed is published elsewhere (Palacios et al., 2023). Briefly, Baby-Feed was developed by our group using the Social Cognitive Theory and Health Self-Empowerment Theory, and constructs from these theories were embedded into all of Baby-Feed’s features, as previously published (Palacios et al., 2023). Baby-Feed helps healthcare providers and caregivers of infants easily assess the infant’s diet and get instantaneous results and feedback. First, caregivers were asked to complete a short Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ), previously validated by our group (Palacios et al., 2017), on the Baby-Feed website. Immediately after completing this, they received immediate results showing the amounts consumed for each food group (milk, protein foods, whole and refined grains, fruits, vegetables, juices, sugary beverages, sweets, and salty snacks). Feedback was also provided using a simple color-coded system, highlighting the food groups consumed adequately (in green), above the recommendations (in red), or below the recommendations (in yellow), as developed by our group (see supplementary table) (Amaro-Rivera et al., 2019; Rios et al., 2016). These recommendations were based on the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, the American Academy of Pediatrics, the World Health Organization, and the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children (WIC) (American Academy of Pediatrics 2026; Geneva, 2000; U.S. Department of Agriculture and U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. 2020; WIC Program, 2009). Other Baby-Feed features included the “Recommendations” tab, which displayed the age-specific recommended amounts for each food group in practical household measurements (ounces and/or cups). These amounts are the same as those used to determine whether foods were consumed adequately, above, or below. Baby-Feed also includes the “Tracking” tab, a very short form that asks caregivers how well they followed the food group recommendations in the past week. Upon completion, it automatically showed a summary of which food groups were being consumed adequately, above, or below using the same color-coding system, and prompted caregivers to set a goal for the next week based on this feedback. Researchers sent the caregivers weekly text messages between well-child visits, encouraging them to use these features often.

The healthcare provider had full access to Baby-Feed’s Clinician Portal, which allowed them to view the FFQ results, the age-specific recommendations, and the results from the weekly tracking survey. Before the study started, the healthcare provider received in-service training on accessing and using the site and was asked to discuss the most relevant results from the FFQ and age-specific recommendations with caregivers during the well-child visit.

This website is hosted by [blinded] and is currently free for research participants participating in active projects.

Control Group

The control (n = 25) received usual care at their well-child visits, which included checking whether the infant was developmentally ready to eat solid foods and recommending the introduction of one or two single-ingredient foods each week, pureed or mashed into a very smooth texture (American Academy of Pediatrics, 2019). No written or online materials were provided. Control participants had limited access to Baby-Feed, in which they were asked to complete the FFQ (for assessment) without access to results, feedback, or other features.

Due to the nature of the intervention, full blinding was not feasible. Participants were not explicitly informed of their group assignment; however, the characteristics of the intervention may have made the group allocation apparent.

Measurements and Outcomes

Socio-demographic Questionnaire

All participants completed a socio-demographic questionnaire that asked about their age, relationship with the child, race/ethnicity, education, number of children, WIC participation, weight, height, and contact information.

Infant Diet Quality (Main Outcome)

Infant diet was assessed using a validated 55-item infant FFQ developed by our group (Palacios et al., 2017). Caregivers were asked to estimate the amount of food the infant had consumed over the past 7 days using typical household measures. It was completed online by all participants (English/Spanish) through the Baby-Feed website, using their cell phone or the study tablet before their well-child visits at baseline and at the end of the study (after 3 months). Based on the results of this FFQ, Baby-Feed automatically calculates the infant Diet Quality Index Score (DQIS), also developed by our group (Amaro-Rivera et al., 2019; Rios et al., 2016) (see supplementary table for more details). Briefly, for infants 0–5.9.9 months, milk is scored as 10 points if breastfeeding and 5 points if formula feeding only; for the other food groups, 5 points are given if the food group has not been introduced, and 0 points if it has been introduced. For 6–12 months, 5 points were assigned for appropriate intake, 2.5 points for intake slightly above or below recommendations, and 0 points for intake above or below recommendations. For grains, the score was equally divided between refined and whole grains, for a total of 5 points if the appropriate intake of both was met. For milk, a similar score was used for this age group. This scoring system is consistent with the amounts recommended on the Baby-Feed website. The total energy consumed was calculated via the FFQ. Those with extreme energy intakes (< 400 or > 2,250 calories) were excluded (Gomes et al., 2018).

Infant Weight Gain (Exploratory Outcome)

Infants’ weight and length were captured via a form given to caregivers at baseline and at the end of the study, which asked for the infant’s sex, age in months, weight in pounds, and length in inches as recorded from the well-child visit. Train nurses at [blinded] took these measurements using a digital pediatric scale and an infantometer. Researchers calculated the weight-for-length (WFL) z-score at each time point using the World Health Organization (WHO) Anthro anthropometric calculator version 3.2.2 (World Health Organization (WHO), 2018). WFL z-score was then categorized as severely wasted (<−3 SD), wasted (<−2 SD), healthy weight (± 1 SD), at risk of overweight (> + 1 SD), overweight (> + 2 SD), or obesity (> + 3 SD) (World Health Organization (WHO), 2016). Changes in WFL-z score between visits were calculated and categorized as ‘Healthy’ (−0.67-0.67 SD), ‘Slow’ (< −0.67 SD), or ‘Rapid’ (> 0.67 SD)(Ong et al., 1999).

Caregivers’ Self-efficacy (Exploratory Outcome)

To explore if the Baby-Feed web application improved self-efficacy, one of the constructs of SCT defined as “beliefs about personal ability to perform behaviors that bring desired outcomes” (Glanz et al., 2015), caregivers were asked to complete a validated self-efficacy questionnaire (Spence et al., 2014). This questionnaire asked how confident parents felt about promoting healthy foods, limiting unhealthy foods, and providing healthy eating settings for their infant.

The research team was not blinded, but all the measures were self-reported by caregivers; diet was completed directly on the website, weight/length was completed by blinded staff in the healthcare center, and the self-efficacy was self-reported by caregivers directly in Qualtrics.

Data Analysis

The assumption of normality was assessed by visually inspecting histograms of model residuals for a symmetric, bell-shaped distribution without substantial skewness or extreme outliers. We used descriptive statistics such as the mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequency for categorical variables. This study was designed as a pilot RCT to evaluate the intervention’s preliminary efficacy; therefore, a formal sample size calculation based on statistical power was not conducted. Primary analyses were conducted using a complete-case approach, including only participants with non-missing data for each variable. No imputation of missing data was performed. Differences in outcomes between groups at baseline and at the end of the study were assessed using Independent Samples t-tests, Chi-Square test, and Fisher’s Exact test, as appropriate. Differences in changes over time between groups were assessed using analysis of covariance, with baseline value as a covariate. Associations between the use of the additional Baby-Feed features and changes in DQIS or WFL z-score, and between changes in self-efficacy scores and changes in DQIS were assessed using Pearson correlations. All analyses were done using SPSS Statistics software. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 50 caregivers and their infants were recruited for this study. However, 3 caregivers (2 in the intervention and 1 in the control group) did not complete the last visit (Fig. 1). For the diet analysis, 2 participants (one in each group) were removed because the infant’s energy intake was outside the specified range. Baseline characteristics were similar between the groups (Table 1). Overall, most caregivers were mothers (92%), Hispanic (86%), had a high school education or higher (88%), were WIC participants (86%), had a mean age of 30.9 years, and had 2 children. The child had a mean age of 8.13 months, 54% were male, and a mean WFL z-score of 1.22 at baseline. Also, most infants were not breastfeeding at baseline (65.3%), with no significant differences between groups.

Table 2 shows the diet quality scores at each time point and changes over time. At baseline, the fruit juice score was significantly lower in the intervention group than in the control group and at the end of the study, the breast milk score was significantly higher in the intervention group (5.00 ± 5.12) compared to controls (1.90 ± 4.02; p = 0.034), with 11 (50%) infants in the intervention group partially or exclusively breastfed, compared to 4 (19%) in the control group. When results were analyzed by change over time, adjusting for baseline values, the change in the breast milk score was significantly greater in the intervention group (0.45 ± 3.75) than in controls (−1.50 ± 4.89; p = 0.046), indicating that the intervention was more compliant with the breast milk recommendation. The change in the sugary drinks score was significantly more negative (−1.25 ± 1.49) in the intervention group than in controls (−0.38 ± 1.22; p = 0.019), indicating that the intervention group was less compliant with the sugary drinks’ recommendation. Lastly, the change in total diet quality score was significantly less negative in the intervention group (−2.95 ± 6.53) than in controls (−7.69 ± 7.84; p = 0.031), indicating that the intervention group was more compliant with the overall diet recommendations than controls.

We also recorded the analytics for the other Baby-Feed components (Tracking, goal settings, and Diet recommendations) in the intervention group. Although all intervention participants accessed Baby-Feed to complete the infant FFQ, with immediate access to results and feedback, only 6 completed the goal-setting feature. Also, 11 caregivers completed the Tracking form 4.5 times on average, and only 8 caregivers read the diet recommendations tab, which was read 2 times on average. Fourteen participants did not use these additional features. Among those using these additional features, we found no significant correlations between the number of times each was used and changes in scores (data not shown).

There were no significant differences between groups in WFL z-score or the growth indicators at any time point (Table 3). However, there was a significantly higher proportion of infants with a healthy rate of weight gain in the intervention group (n = 21, 95%) than in controls (n = 14, 58%; p = 0.006) over the 3 months. Among those who completed the tracking, set a goal, or read the recommendation, we found no significant correlations between the number of times each was used and changes in WFL z-score.

Discussion

In this short pilot trial conducted with a convenience sample of caregivers in South Florida, we found that using Baby-Feed for 3 months led to small but significant improvements in overall diet quality compared with controls, indicating that the intervention group was more compliant with the website’s overall diet recommendations. This pilot trial also found a greater proportion of infants in the intervention group with a healthy weight-gain rate during this short period.

To our knowledge, this is the first pilot trial using a web-based intervention in the healthcare setting to quickly assess infants’ diets, provide automated results, and deliver feedback to improve (Johnson et al., 2022, 2025). Providing this type of feedback to caregivers is very important, as studies show that infant feeding is among their most pressing concerns and that accurate, evidence-based information is needed to support their infant feeding decisions (Dattilo et al., 2020). The overall improvements in diet quality observed in the present study could stem from the automatic results and feedback received after completing the FFQ, as this website highlighted the food groups that were fed adequately, above, or below the recommendations. Caregivers were encouraged to use this feedback to make improvements based on their situations and goals. For example, some children were fed milk above the recommendations (highlighted in red) or vegetables/fruits below the recommendations (highlighted in yellow). Caregivers were also encouraged to set a goal for the following weeks on which component to improve, though only 6 caregivers did so. Although no differences were observed in parenting self-efficacy between groups, the intervention group showed significant positive associations between changes in promoting healthy foods efficacy score and improvements in fruit score, and between changes in limiting unhealthy foods efficacy score and improvements in juice score. However, the intervention group had a significantly lower score for sugary drinks, which is surprising, as the website clearly states that infants should not consume this. Therefore, future modifications to this website should place greater emphasis on reducing sugary drink consumption and on its importance. These small but significant improvements in overall diet quality observed in this pilot trial are clinically relevant, given that infants have limited dietary intake capacity (Bailey et al., 2022) and high nutrient needs during this period of rapid growth, leaving little room for foods with low nutritional value (American Academy of Pediatrics Committee on Nutrition 2019; Soriano et al. 2023; World Health Organization 2023).

This pilot trial also found a greater proportion of infants in the intervention group with healthy infant weight gain compared to controls during this short period, although no significant differences between groups were observed in changes in WFL z-scores. As an exploratory outcome, these results inform the design of future adequately powered, longitudinal trials aimed at confirming these findings and evaluating the extent to which changes in the intervention’s underlying theoretical constructs mediate effects on infant weight gain.

Our results are consistent with other educational interventions that use technology to successfully improve infants’ diet (Fiks et al., 2017; Helle et al., 2019; Karssen et al., 2022; Lozada-Tequeanes et al., 2024; Røed et al., 2021). The INFANT program (Campbell et al., 2008) also found that parenting self-efficacy scores were significantly associated with infant diet quality (Spence et al., 2014). However, interventions using technology to improve the rate of infant weight gain have generally shown mixed results (Denney-Wilson et al. 2015; Karssen et al., 2022; Kok et al. 2016; Laws et al. 2018; Palacios et al., 2018; Prochaska and Velicer 1997). For example, “Samen Happie! App” trial, an app that included healthy energy balance-related behaviors modules, found that the intervention group had a smaller increase in BMI z-score after 6 months but a greater increase after 12 months compared to the control group (Karssen et al., 2022). Integrating such interventions into frequent well-child visits during the first 2 years of life may be more effective, as it provides several opportunities to intervene. However, limited studies have tested such interventions in the healthcare setting (Daniels et al. 2015; Messito et al., 2020; Morandi et al., 2019; Schroeder et al., 2015; Taveras et al., 2011). In general, they have shown improvements in diet but not in weight gain (Messito et al., 2020; Morandi et al., 2019; Schroeder et al., 2015; Taveras et al., 2011). Typically, these interventions are very resource-intensive, led by healthcare professionals, requiring extended time for preventive visits, which is not typical in well-baby care. Only a few interventions have combined technology components in the healthcare settings (Johnson et al., 2022, 2025). For example, the Greenlight Plus trial (Heerman et al., 2024), a multi-center trial delivering the intervention through primary care plus a website with tracking goal progress and a child’s color-coded growth chart with automated adaptive feedback, found significantly healthier weight gain at 4 and 24 months compared to controls. This overall evidence highlights the need to re-evaluate interventions to prevent rapid infant weight gain. Perhaps future studies should include focus groups or interviews with caregivers to learn how they are interacting with these platforms. Also, as recently shown in the largest meta-analysis of infant interventions (Hunter et al., 2025) with no significant differences in BMI z-scores between interventions and controls at 24 months, interventions may need to continue into childhood, based on behavior strategies, and take into consideration the constraints and stressors of caregivers.

There are a few strengths and limitations to highlight when interpreting the results from this small, short pilot trial. A strength was the RCT design involving healthcare providers during regular well-child visits. Also, the FFQ and the DQIS were developed and validated specifically for this group. We had a high retention rate (92%), which may reflect mothers’ desire to learn about healthy diets for their infants. However, this pilot trial had a small convenience sample that was not representative of these clinics, with a very short follow-up and only two time points to assess the rate of infant weight gain, limiting the interpretation of the growth trajectory over time. Most caregivers were low-income Hispanics, limiting the generalizability of the findings to other populations, but this is precisely the group in need of these kinds of interventions. Another limitation was the relatively low engagement of caregivers with the other Baby-Feed features, so future studies should assess the other theoretical constructs used in the design of this website and explore among caregivers how they used Baby-Feed to inform future improvements. Such improvements could focus on better operationalizing the theoretical constructs within its design, ensuring that all components are effectively utilized by users, optimizing the user interface for mobile accessibility, and integrating push notifications to remind users to use Baby-Feed. Additionally, expanding interoperability with electronic health record systems could enable healthcare providers to seamlessly incorporate Baby-Feed results into routine patient assessments.

In conclusion, this short pilot trial found that using Baby-Feed for 3 months among this group of healthcare providers and caregivers of infants at a community healthcare center in South Florida improved overall diet quality, with a higher proportion of infants achieving a healthy rate of weight gain compared to controls. Larger and longer studies are needed to confirm these results.

Implications for Research, Practice and Policy

Baby-feed could be used in healthcare settings as a companion tool for healthcare providers to help caregivers improve infants’ diets. However, enhancements to the website are needed to better operationalize the theoretical constructs within its design, ensuring that all components are effectively utilized by users. Lastly, further testing is needed before it can be translated into practice.
