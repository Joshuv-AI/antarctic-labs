Abstract
. We settle the long-standing open question whether there exists a 3 3 -ladder of cardinality ℵ 2 \aleph_{2} . Given a positive integer n n , an n n -ladder is a lower finite lattice whose elements have at most n n lower covers. In 1984, Ditor proved that every n n -ladder has cardinality at most ℵ n − 1 \aleph_{n-1} , and that this cardinal bound is sharp for n = 1 , 2 n=1,2 . He then raised the question of whether the bound is attained for n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 as well. An affirmative answer is known to be consistent with 𝖹𝖥𝖢 \mathsf{ZFC} . We prove, relative to the consistency of a Mahlo cardinal, that the question is independent of 𝖹𝖥𝖢 \mathsf{ZFC} . More precisely, we show that the nonexistence of a 3 3 -ladder of cardinality ℵ 2 \aleph_{2} is equiconsistent with a Mahlo cardinal.

Set Theory

The monographs
[

is stationary in

Now let us recall some basic properties of Lévy’s collapse forcing. Given two cardinals
\delta<\kappa

\mathrm{Coll}(\delta,{<}\kappa)
the forcing notion whose conditions are functions

\mathrm{dom}(p)\subseteq\kappa\times\delta

(1)

|\mathrm{dom}(p)|<\delta
,

(2)

,

ordered by reverse inclusion:
p\leq q
if and only if

. Here are the basic properties of this forcing—see, e.g.,
[
2
, §14]
and

Lemma 1.1
.

Given two regular cardinals
\delta<\kappa
, with
\kappa

(1)

\mathrm{Coll}(\delta,{<}\kappa)
has the
\kappa
-cc.

(2)

For every decreasing sequence
\langle p_{\alpha}:\alpha<\eta\rangle

\mathrm{Coll}(\delta,{<}\kappa)

(3)

\mathrm{Coll}(\delta,{<}\kappa)

1.2.
Join-semilattices

The monograph
[
10
]
is our reference for all classical definitions and notation in lattice theory.

Given a poset
(P,\leq)
and some
x\in P
, we denote by
P\downarrow x
and
P\uparrow x
the sets
\{y\in P\mid y\leq x\}
and
\{y\in P\mid y\geq x\}
, respectively. Sometimes, instead of
P\downarrow x
and
P\uparrow x
, we simply write
{\downarrow}x
and
{\uparrow}x
when no ambiguity arises. The set
{\uparrow}x
is called the
upper cone of
x
. Furthermore, given two elements
x,y\in P
,
y
is a
lower cover of
x
if
y<x
and there is no
z\in P
with
y<z<x
. A subset
C\subseteq P
is said to be
cofinal
in
P
if for every
x\in P
there is
y\in C
with
x\leq y
. Given two posets
P
and
Q
, a map
f:P\rightarrow Q
is
order-preserving
if
f(x)\leq f(y)
for every
x,y\in P
with
x\leq y
.

A
join-semilattice
is a nonempty set equipped with a binary operation, denoted by
\vee
, that is associative, commutative, and idempotent; it induces a partial order via
x\leq y\iff x\vee y=y
. Equivalently, a join-semilattice is a partially ordered set in which every pair of elements
x,y
admits a least upper bound, denoted by
x\vee y
. The dual notion is the
meet-semilattice
. We treat semilattices as algebraic structures or as posets depending on which representation is better suited to the given context.

A nonempty subset
S
of a join-semilattice
P
is called a
join-subsemilattice
of
P
if it is closed under binary joins. Meet-subsemilattices are dually defined. A downward closed join-subsemilattice is called an
ideal
. Note that
P\downarrow x
is an ideal of
P
for every
x\in P
; such ideals are known as
principal ideals
. An ideal that does not coincide with the whole poset is a
proper ideal
.

Given a nonempty subset
S
of a join-semilattice
P
and an element
x\in P
, we denote by
x\vee S
the set
\{x\vee y:y\in S\}
. Note that if
S
is a join-subsemilattice, then
x\vee S
is also a join-subsemilattice.

Given a join-semilattice
P
and a finite nonempty set
F=\{x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, we denote
x_{0}\vee x_{1}\vee\ldots\vee x_{n}
simply by
\bigvee F
. If we write
\bigvee\emptyset
, we are tacitly assuming that
P
has a least element
0_{P}
, and therefore that
\bigvee\emptyset=0_{P}
.

Let us briefly review the notion of quotient join-semilattice. Given a join-semilattice
P
, an equivalence relation
\sim
on
P
is a
congruence relation
if for all
x_{0},x_{1},y_{0},y_{1}
in
P
,

x_{0}\sim y_{0}\text{ and }x_{1}\sim y_{1}\Rightarrow x_{0}\vee x_{1}\sim y_{0}\vee y_{1}.

Given a congruence relation
\sim
on
P
, we can define the join operator
\vee
on the quotient
P/{\sim}
as follows: for every
x,y\in P
,

[x]_{\sim}\vee[y]_{\sim}\coloneqq[x\vee y]_{\sim}.

It is easy to check that this operation is well defined and makes
P/{\sim}
into a join-semilattice. The resulting join-semilattice
P/{\sim}
is called the
quotient join-semilattice of
P
modulo
\sim
. We denote the quotient map by
q_{\sim}:P\rightarrow P/{\sim}
.

Any ideal
I
of
P
induces the following natural congruence relation
\sim_{I}
on
P
: for
x,y\in P
,
x\sim_{I}y
if there exists
z\in I
such that
x\vee z=y\vee z
. In this case, we simply write
P/I
and
q_{I}
instead of
P/{\sim_{I}}
and
q_{\sim_{I}}
. Not every congruence relation on a join-semilattice is induced by an ideal.

Let us also recall the definition of breadth, a classical numeric invariant of lattice theory.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
P
be a join-semilattice and
n\in\omega
. We say that
P
has
breadth at most
n
if, for every nonempty finite subset
X
of
P
, there exists
Y\subseteq X
with at most
n
elements such that
\bigvee X=\bigvee Y
. The
breadth
of
P
, denoted by
\mathrm{breadth}(P)
, is the least
n\in\omega
such that
P
has breadth at most
n
, if such an
n
exists.

In fact, there is a more general notion of breadth which is self-dual and purely poset-theoretical
[
5
, §4]
. We say that a join-semilattice has
finite breadth
if it has breadth at most
n
for some
n\in\omega
. Furthermore, note that a join-semilattice
P
has breadth
0
if and only if
P=\{0_{P}\}
, and has breadth at most
1
if and only if it is a linear order. The next lemma is immediate from the definition.

Lemma 1.3
.

Given a join-semilattice
P
and
n\in\omega
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

P
has breadth at most
n
.

(2)

For every
X\in[P]^{n+1}
, there exists
Y\in[X]^{n}
such that
\bigvee X=\bigvee Y
.

Finally, let us introduce a nonstandard notation from
[
19
]
. Given a lower finite lattice
P
, an ideal
I\subseteq P
, and an element
x\in P
, let

\pi_{I}(x)\coloneqq\bigvee\{y\in I:y\leq x\}.

We say that
\pi_{I}(x)
is the
projection of
x
onto
I
. Since every lower finite lattice has a least element and
{\downarrow}x
is finite, the set
\{y\in I:y\leq x\}
is finite and nonempty; hence its join is well-defined. Equivalently,
\pi_{I}(x)
is the greatest element of
I
which is less than or equal to
x
, i.e.,

\pi_{I}(x)=\max\{y\in I:y\leq x\}=\max(I\cap({\downarrow}x)).

Fix a lower finite lattice
P
and an ideal
I\subseteq P
. The following properties of the map
\pi_{I}
follow directly from its definition:

•

\pi_{I}\upharpoonright I
is the identity;

•

\pi_{I}
is idempotent, that is,
\pi_{I}\circ\pi_{I}=\pi_{I}
;

•

\pi_{I}(x)\leq x
for every
x\in P
;

•

\pi_{I}
is order-preserving.

A self-map of a poset satisfying the last three properties is an
interior operator
, equivalently, a closure operator on the order dual. It is also sometimes called a
kernel operator
or a
projection

[
3
,
8
,
22
]
.

The following two lemmas from
[
19
]
establish two less immediate properties of the maps
\pi_{I}
.

Lemma 1.4
.

Given a lower finite lattice
P
, elements
x,y\in P
, and an ideal
I\subseteq P
,
\pi_{I}(x\wedge y)=\pi_{I}(x)\wedge\pi_{I}(y)
.

Proof.

Since
\pi_{I}(x)\leq x
and
\pi_{I}(y)\leq y
, we have
\pi_{I}(x)\wedge\pi_{I}(y)\leq x\wedge y
. As
I
is downward closed,
\pi_{I}(x)\wedge\pi_{I}(y)\in I
. We conclude
\pi_{I}(x)\wedge\pi_{I}(y)\leq\pi_{I}(x\wedge y)
.

Furthermore, since
\pi_{I}(x\wedge y)\leq x\wedge y\leq x,y
and
\pi_{I}(x\wedge y)\in I
, we conclude
\pi_{I}(x\wedge y)\leq\pi_{I}(x)\wedge\pi_{I}(y)
. Overall, we have
\pi_{I}(x\wedge y)=\pi_{I}(x)\wedge\pi_{I}(y)
.
∎

In other words, Lemma
1.4
tells us that
\pi_{I}:P\rightarrow I
is a meet-homomorphism. Moreover, if
x,y\in P
are such that
x\wedge y\in I
, we conclude from Lemma
1.4
that
x\wedge y=\pi_{I}(x)\wedge\pi_{I}(y)
.

Lemma 1.5
.

If
P
is a lower finite lattice and
I,J\subseteq P
are ideals with
I\subseteq J
, then
\pi_{I}\circ\pi_{J}=\pi_{I}
.

Proof.

Fix an
x\in P
. Since
\pi_{I}\circ\pi_{J}(x)\in I
and
\pi_{I}\circ\pi_{J}(x)\leq x
, we have
\pi_{I}\circ\pi_{J}(x)\leq\pi_{I}(x)
. Furthermore,
\pi_{I}(x)\leq\pi_{J}(x)
since
\pi_{I}(x)\leq x
and
\pi_{I}(x)\in I\subseteq J
. Therefore,
\pi_{I}(x)=\pi_{I}\circ\pi_{I}(x)\leq\pi_{I}\circ\pi_{J}(x)
. Overall,
\pi_{I}\circ\pi_{J}(x)=\pi_{I}(x)
.
∎

1.3.
Ditor’s Theorem

Ditor proved that the breadth, together with the cardinalities of the principal ideals, provides a neat upper bound on the cardinality of the join-semilattice.

Theorem 1.6

(Ditor,
[
5
, Theorem 5.2]
)
.

Let
n>0
and let
\kappa
be an infinite cardinal. If
P
is a join-semilattice of breadth at most
n
whose principal ideals have cardinality
<\kappa
, then

(a)

|P|\leq\kappa^{+(n-1)}
, and

(b)

|I|<\kappa^{+(n-1)}
for every proper ideal
I
of
P
.

As noted by Wehrung, the first inequality of Ditor’s Theorem is in fact a fairly direct corollary of Kuratowski’s Free Set Theorem
[
13
]
(see also
[
7
, Theorem 46.1]
).

Since every
n
-ladder is, in particular, a lower finite join-semilattice of breadth at most
n

[
5
, Proposition 4.1]
, it follows from Ditor’s Theorem that every
n
-ladder has cardinality at most
\aleph_{n-1}
.

Another consequence of Ditor’s Theorem that we shall use repeatedly is that, for every infinite cardinal
\kappa
and every
n>0
, every join-semilattice of cardinality
\kappa^{+(n-1)}
whose principal ideals have cardinality
<\kappa
has breadth at least
n
. In particular, for lower finite join-semilattices of cardinality
\aleph_{n-1}
, having breadth at most
n
is equivalent to having breadth exactly
n
. Thus, Theorem
A
could equivalently have been stated with “breadth
n
” in place of “breadth at most
n
”.

Finally, let us remark that Ditor also asked whether these more general cardinal upper bounds are sharp
[
5
, Question A]
:

Ditor’s Question A
.

For every
n>0
and every infinite cardinal
\kappa
, is there a join-semilattice of breadth
n
and cardinality
\kappa^{+(n-1)}
whose principal ideals have cardinality less than
\kappa
?

Ditor’s Question B on ladders, stated in the introduction, is a more demanding version of Question A when
\kappa=\aleph_{0}
. Indeed, every
n
-ladder is, in particular, a lower finite join-semilattice of breadth at most
n
. In this sense,
n
-ladders may be regarded as particularly tame examples of lower finite join-semilattices of breadth at most
n
.

Together with the consistent positive results recalled in the introduction, Theorem
A
implies that the instance of Ditor’s Question A corresponding to
\kappa=\aleph_{0}
and
n\geq 3
is independent of
\mathsf{ZFC}
, relative to the consistency of a Mahlo cardinal. The fact that Theorem
A
concerns lattices rather than join-semilattices is inessential: adjoining a least element to an infinite lower finite join-semilattice yields a lower finite lattice without changing its cardinality or breadth. See Section
5
for further discussion.

Recall that a
filter
on an infinite set

\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)
that satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

\emptyset\not\in\mathcal{F}
and
X\in\mathcal{F}
,

(2)

if
U\in\mathcal{F}
and
U\subseteq^{*}V\subseteq X
, then
V\in\mathcal{F}
,

(3)

if
U,V\in\mathcal{F}
, then
U\cap V\in\mathcal{F}
.

Given a filter
\mathcal{F}
, we denote by
\mathcal{F}^{+}
the family of all subsets
H\subseteq X
such that

. Note that
H\in\mathcal{F}^{+}
if and only if

is infinite for every
U\in\mathcal{F}
.

The filter of cofinite subsets of

is the
Fréchet filter

, we denote it by
\mathfrak{Fr}
. By definition, every filter contains the Fréchet filter

2

2

The filters considered here are precisely the

[

]

.

Let us recall the Rudin-Blass reducibility preorder on filters—see, e.g.,
[

,

]

Definition 1.7

Let
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
be filters on

and

, respectively. We say that
\mathcal{F}
is

if and only if

For notational clarity, the remainder of this subsection is stated for filters on
\omega

with its standard topology induced by the Cantor space

, we can ask topological questions about filters, seen as subsets of the topological space
\mathcal{P}(\omega)
. The following well-known result of Talagrand
[
24
]
gives a very useful combinatorial characterization of meager filters.

Lemma 1.8

(Talagrand)
.

The following are equivalent for a filter
\mathcal{F}
:

(1)

\mathcal{F}
is meager.

(2)

\mathfrak{Fr}\leq_{RB}\mathcal{F}
.

(3)

There exists a sequence
\langle A_{n}:n\in\omega\rangle
of finite, pairwise disjoint subsets of
\omega
such that, for every
U\in\mathcal{F}
,
U\cap A_{n}\neq\emptyset
for all but finitely many
n
.

In what follows, we will use only the equivalent combinatorial characterizations of meagerness given in conditions (2) and (3).

By Talagrand’s characterization, it is clear that the meager filters form an upward closed subset with respect to the Rudin-Blass reducibility preorder. However, the family of meager filters is also downward closed:

Lemma 1.9

(Folklore)
.

Given two filters
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}
with
\mathcal{F}\leq_{RB}\mathcal{G}
,
\mathcal{F}
is meager if and only if
\mathcal{G}
is meager.

Proof.

The “only if” is a direct consequence of Talagrand’s characterization. It remains to prove the “if” direction. Suppose that
\mathfrak{Fr}\leq_{RB}\mathcal{G}
. We must show
\mathfrak{Fr}\leq_{RB}\mathcal{F}
. Equivalently, we need to show that there exists a sequence
\langle A_{n}:n\in\omega\rangle
of finite, pairwise disjoint subsets of
\omega
such that every element of
\mathcal{F}
intersects all but finitely many of the sets
A_{n}
.

Fix two finite-to-one maps
f,g:\omega\rightarrow\omega
witnessing
\mathfrak{Fr}\leq_{RB}\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{F}\leq_{RB}\mathcal{G}
, respectively. We inductively define an increasing sequence
\langle k_{n}:n\in\omega\rangle
of natural numbers as follows. First let
k_{0}=0
. Now suppose that
k_{m}
is defined for every
m\leq n
. Since both
f
and
g
are finite-to-one, the set
g^{-1}(g[\bigcup_{m\leq n}f^{-1}(\{k_{m}\})])
is finite. Thus, we can pick
k_{n+1}>k_{n}
such that
f^{-1}(\{k_{n+1}\})
is disjoint from the latter set. This completes the recursive definition.

For each
n
, let
A_{n}\coloneqq g[f^{-1}(\{k_{n}\})]
. Since
f
is finite-to-one, the sets
A_{n}
are finite. Moreover, it follows directly from our construction that they are pairwise disjoint.

Pick some
U\in\mathcal{F}
. By the way we chose
f
and
g
,
g^{-1}(U)\in\mathcal{G}
, and therefore
f[g^{-1}(U)]\in\mathfrak{Fr}
. Let
m
be such that
\omega\setminus k_{m}\subseteq f[g^{-1}(U)]
. Then, for every
n\geq m
,
k_{n}\in f[g^{-1}(U)]
, so
f^{-1}(\{k_{n}\})\cap g^{-1}(U)\neq\emptyset
, and hence
A_{n}\cap U\neq\emptyset
. In particular,
U
intersects all but finitely many
A_{n}
.
∎

Finally, let us also recall a game-theoretic characterization of meager filters due to Laflamme
[
15
]
. For a given filter
\mathcal{F}
, consider the following game
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{F})
. In this game, at the
k
-th round, Player I plays a natural number
n_{k}
, and then Player II plays another natural number
m_{k}

I

n_{0}

n_{1}

n_{2}

…

II

m_{0}

m_{1}

m_{2}

…

with the rule:
n_{k}\leq m_{k}\leq n_{k+1}
for every
k\in\omega
. At the end of a play, Player II
wins
if and only if
\bigcup_{k\in\omega}[n_{k},m_{k})\in\mathcal{F}^{+}
.

Theorem 1.10

(Laflamme,
[
15
, essentially Theorem 2.12]
)
.

For a given filter
\mathcal{F}
, Player II has a winning strategy in
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{F})
if and only if
\mathcal{F}
is meager.

Before sketching a proof of Theorem
1.10
, let us briefly recall what a strategy is in this context. A strategy for Player II in Laflamme’s game
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{F})
is a function
\sigma
that assigns to each finite nonempty sequence
\langle n_{0},\ldots n_{k}\rangle
of natural numbers a natural number
\sigma(\langle n_{0},\ldots n_{k}\rangle)\geq n_{k}
. The strategy
\sigma
is
winning
if for every infinite sequence
\langle n_{k}:k\in\omega\rangle
of natural numbers satisfying
n_{k+1}\geq\sigma(\langle n_{0},\ldots n_{k}\rangle)
for every
k
, we have

\bigcup_{k\in\omega}[n_{k},\sigma(\langle n_{0},\ldots,n_{k}\rangle))\in\mathcal{F}^{+}.

Proof sketch of Theorem
1.10
.

The “if” direction is a direct consequence of Talagrand’s characterization. Indeed, if
\langle A_{n}:n\in\omega\rangle
witnesses Lemma
1.8
(3), then Player II wins by playing at each turn
k
, a natural number
m_{k}
big enough so that for some
n>k
,
A_{n}\subseteq[n_{k},m_{k})
.

For the “only if” direction, suppose that
\sigma
is a winning strategy for Player II and let us define inductively a sequence
\langle M_{k}:k\in\omega\rangle
of natural numbers as follows: first let
M_{0}=0
; then, for each
k\geq 0
, let

M_{k+1}=\max\big\{\sigma(\langle n_{0},\ldots,n_{i}\rangle):i\leq k\text{ and }\forall j\leq i\ (n_{j}\leq M_{k})\big\}.

Then, an argument by contraposition shows that the sequence
\langle[M_{k},M_{k+1}):k\in\omega\rangle
witnesses Lemma
1.8
(3). Indeed, if there were
U\in\mathcal{F}
and an increasing sequence
\langle k_{i}:i\in\omega\rangle
of natural numbers such that
U\cap[M_{k_{i}},M_{k_{i}+1})=\emptyset
for every
i
, then Player I would win against the strategy
\sigma
by playing the sequence
\langle M_{k_{i}}:i\in\omega\rangle
.
∎

Laflamme’s game will appear in Lemma
4.6
, where it is compared with an auxiliary game arising from the forcing argument for Theorem
A
.

2.
Local breadth and quotients

In this section we study the relationship between the finite breadth of a join-semilattice and its ideal quotients. Let us start by introducing a local version of breadth.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
P
be a join-semilattice and
n\in\omega
. Given
x\in P
, we say that
P
has
breadth at most
n
at
x
if, for every nonempty finite subset
X
of
P
with
\bigvee X=x
, there exists
Y\subseteq X
with at most
n
elements such that
\bigvee Y=x
. The
breadth
of
P

at
x
, denoted by
\mathrm{breadth}_{P}(x)
, is the least
n\in\omega
such that
P
has breadth at most
n
at
x
, if such an
n
exists.

For a given
x\in P
, note that
\mathrm{breadth}_{P}(x)=0
if and only if
x=0_{P}
, and
\mathrm{breadth}_{P}(x)=1
if and only if
x
is join-irreducible—i.e.,
x\neq 0_{P}
and
x=y\vee z
implies that either
x=y
or
x=z
. Furthermore, it follows readily that for every join-semilattice
P
of finite breadth

\mathrm{breadth}(P)=\max_{x\in P}\mathrm{breadth}_{P}(x).

The objective of this section is the proof of the following theorem. Recall that, given an element
x
of a join-semilattice
P
and an ideal
I\subseteq P
, the set
x\vee I
denotes the join-subsemilattice
\{x\vee y:y\in I\}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Given a join-semilattice
P
of finite breadth, an ideal
I\subseteq P
, and an element
x\in P
, there exists
y\in P
with
y\sim_{I}x
such that

\mathrm{breadth}(y\vee I)\leq\mathrm{breadth}(P)-\mathrm{breadth}_{P/I}([x]_{I}).

Let us start by recalling the following lemma due to Ditor. We omit its proof, since it uses essentially the same argument as the proof of Theorem
2.2
. The lemma implies the case of Theorem
2.2
in which
\mathrm{breadth}_{P/I}([x]_{I})=1
; in fact, in this case one may take
y=x
.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
5
, Lemma 5.1]
)
.

Given a join-semilattice
P
of finite breadth, a proper ideal
I\subsetneq P
, and an element
x\in P\setminus I
,
\mathrm{breadth}(x\vee I)<\mathrm{breadth}(P)
.

Before delving into the proof of Theorem
2.2
, let us record the following corollary of Lemma
2.3
, which was already anticipated in the introduction and allows us to reduce the statement of Theorem
A
to the case
n=3
.

Corollary 2.4
.

If there is no lower finite lattice of breadth
3
and cardinality
\aleph_{2}
, then, for every
n\geq 3
, there is no lower finite lattice of breadth
n
and cardinality
\aleph_{n-1}
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that if there is a lower finite lattice of breadth
n>0
and cardinality
\aleph_{k}
, for some
k>0
, then there exists a lower finite lattice of breadth at most
n-1
and cardinality
\aleph_{k-1}
. The result then follows by a straightforward induction.

Pick a lower finite lattice
P
of breadth
n
and cardinality
\aleph_{k}
. Pick a (proper) ideal
I\subseteq P
of cardinality
\aleph_{k-1}
—just let
I
be the ideal generated by some subset of
P
of cardinality
\aleph_{k-1}
. Fix also an
x\in P\setminus I
. By Lemma
2.3
, the join-subsemilattice
x\vee I
has breadth at most
n-1
. Moreover, note that
|x\vee I|=|I|
. Clearly,
|x\vee I|\leq|I|
; also, since
I
is included in the downward closure of
x\vee I
and
P
is lower finite, we have
|I|\leq|x\vee I|\cdot\aleph_{0}=|x\vee I|
. Overall,
x\vee I
is a lower finite join-semilattice of breadth at most
n-1
and cardinality
\aleph_{k-1}
. As every lower finite join-semilattice with a least element is a lattice,
x\vee I
is actually a lattice, and we are done.
∎

We are ready to prove Theorem
2.2
.

Proof of
Theorem
2.2
.

If
P
has breadth
0
, then the claim follows trivially. So fix a join-semilattice
P
of breadth
n>0
, an ideal
I\subseteq P
, and an element
x\in P
such that
\mathrm{breadth}_{P/I}([x]_{I})=m\in\omega
. We want to show that there is
y\in[x]_{I}
such that
\mathrm{breadth}(y\vee I)\leq n-m
.

First note that it follows directly from the definition of the quotient join operator that
\mathrm{breadth}(P/I)\leq\mathrm{breadth}(P)
, and hence
n\geq m
. Moreover, since
x\vee I
is a join-subsemilattice of
P
, we have
\mathrm{breadth}(x\vee I)\leq\mathrm{breadth}(P)
. In particular, our claim holds when
m=0
.

If
m=1
, then, by letting
y=x
, our claim follows directly from Lemma
2.3
. Indeed, note that
\mathrm{breadth}_{P/I}([x]_{I})>0
is equivalent to
x\not\in I
. Hence, from now on, we assume
m>1
.

Since
\mathrm{breadth}_{P/I}([x]_{I})=m
, we can fix some
F\subseteq P/I
of size
m
such that
\bigvee F=[x]_{I}
and such that for every
H\in[F]^{m-1}
,
\bigvee H<[x]_{I}
.

Pick
x_{0},\ldots,x_{m-1}\in P
such that
F=\{[x_{i}]_{I}:i<m\}
, and denote
\bigvee_{i<m}x_{i}
by
y
. By the way we chose
F
, we must have
x_{i}\not\in I
for every
i<m
. Moreover, it directly follows from the quotient map
q_{I}
being a join-homomorphism that
[y]_{I}=\bigvee F=[x]_{I}
. We claim that
y
satisfies the desired property, i.e.,
\mathrm{breadth}(y\vee I)\leq n-m
.

Fix
A\subseteq y\vee I
of size
n-m+1
and pick also a set
B\in[I]^{n-m+1}
such that
y\vee B=A
.

Consider the set
B\cup\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{m-1}\}
. Since
B\subseteq I
and
x_{i}\not\in I
for every
i<m
, we conclude that the set
B\cup\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{m-1}\}
has size
n+1
. Since
P
has breadth
n
, we can pick some
D\in[B\cup\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{m-1}\}]^{n}
such that
\bigvee D=y\vee\bigvee B=\bigvee A
.

Note that
x_{i}
must belong to
D
for every
i<m
. Otherwise, if there were some
i<m
such that
x_{i}\not\in D
, we would conclude that
x_{i}\leq(\bigvee_{j\neq i}x_{j})\vee\bigvee B
, and therefore, by applying the quotient map
q_{I}
to both sides, we would have
[x_{i}]_{I}\leq\bigvee(F\setminus\{[x_{i}]_{I}\})
, contradicting the way we chose
F
.

Since
D
has size
n
and every
x_{i}
belongs to it, there must be some
z\in B
such that
z\not\in D
. Hence

(1)

z\leq y\vee\bigvee(B\setminus\{z\}).

If
n=m
, then
B=\{z\}
and (
1
) is equivalent to
z\leq y
. In particular, by the arbitrariness of
A
, in this case we conclude that
y
is an upper bound of
I
, or, equivalently, that
y\vee I=\{y\}
, which proves our claim, as then
\mathrm{breadth}(y\vee I)=0=n-m
.

If
n>m
instead, (
1
) gives us

\bigvee A=y\vee\bigvee B=y\vee\bigvee(B\setminus\{z\})=\bigvee(y\vee(B\setminus\{z\})).

Therefore, the set
y\vee(B\setminus\{z\})
is a subset of
A
of size
n-m
whose join is the same as the join of
A
. This shows that
\mathrm{breadth}(y\vee I)\leq n-m
, and we are done.
∎

The element
y
in the statement of Theorem
2.2
cannot in general be taken to be
x
. Indeed, consider the join-semilattice
P
represented by the following Hasse diagram, where the dashed ellipse encloses an ideal
I
:

x

I

The join-semilattice
P
has breadth
3
, while
P/I
is isomorphic to the four-element Boolean lattice
\mathsf{B}_{2}
. Moreover,
[x]_{I}
is the top element of
P/I
, and hence
P/I
has breadth
2
at
[x]_{I}
. On the other hand,
x\vee I
consists of two incomparable elements and their join, so it also has breadth
2
. Therefore,

\mathrm{breadth}(x\vee I)=2\not\leq\mathrm{breadth}(P)-\mathrm{breadth}_{P/I}([x]_{I})=1.

We now turn to some consequences of Theorem
2.2
. First, recall that, as already noted in the proof of Theorem
2.2
, taking quotients cannot increase the breadth: given a congruence relation
\sim
on a join-semilattice
P
of finite breadth, the definition of the join operation on
P/{\sim}
immediately yields
\mathrm{breadth}(P/{\sim})\leq\mathrm{breadth}(P)
. Theorem
2.2
shows that this inequality is strict for quotients induced by unbounded ideals.

Corollary 2.5
.

Given a join-semilattice
P
of finite breadth and an unbounded ideal
I\subseteq P
,
\mathrm{breadth}(P/I)<\mathrm{breadth}(P)
.

Proof.

For every
x\in P
, we must have
\mathrm{breadth}(x\vee I)>0
; otherwise,
x\vee I
would be a singleton, and its unique element would be an upper bound of
I
, contrary to the assumption that
I
is unbounded. Therefore, it follows from Theorem
2.2
that
\mathrm{breadth}_{P/I}([x]_{I})<\mathrm{breadth}(P)
for every
x\in P
. Since
\mathrm{breadth}(P/I)=\max_{x\in P}\mathrm{breadth}_{P/I}([x]_{I})
, we conclude that
\mathrm{breadth}(P/I)<\mathrm{breadth}(P)
.
∎

Finally, let us prove the following result, obtained by combining Theorem
2.2
with Ditor’s Theorem
1.6
. It will play an important role in the next section.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
\kappa
be an infinite cardinal, let
n
be a positive integer, and let
P
be a join-semilattice of breadth
n
and cardinality
\kappa^{+(n-1)}
whose principal ideals have cardinality
<\kappa
. Then, for every ideal
I\subseteq P
of cardinality at most
\kappa
, there exists
x\in P
such that
x\vee I
is a chain.

Proof.

If
n=1
, then the result follows trivially, since
P
is itself a linear order. So let us assume
n>1
. For every
x\in P
,

[x]_{I}\subseteq\bigcup_{y\in I}{\downarrow}(x\vee y).

Since
I
has cardinality at most
\kappa
and the principal ideals of
P
have cardinality strictly less than
\kappa
, it follows that every equivalence class of
\sim_{I}
must have cardinality at most
\kappa
. Similarly, every principal ideal of
P/I
has cardinality
\leq\kappa
.

As we are assuming
n>1
,
\kappa^{+(n-1)}
is a regular cardinal. Since every equivalence class of
\sim_{I}
has cardinality at most
\kappa
,
P/I
has the same cardinality as
P
. Overall,
P/I
is a join-semilattice of cardinality
\kappa^{+(n-1)}
whose principal ideals have cardinality
\leq\kappa
. It follows from the first inequality of Ditor’s Theorem
1.6
that
\mathrm{breadth}(P/I)\geq n-1
. In particular, there must be some
x\in P
such that
\mathrm{breadth}_{P/I}([x]_{I})\geq n-1
. By Theorem
2.2
, there exists
y\in P
with
y\sim_{I}x
such that
\mathrm{breadth}(y\vee I)\leq n-(n-1)=1
or, equivalently, such that
y\vee I
is a chain.
∎

3.
The filter of projected upper cones

In this section, we study projected upper cones in lower finite lattices and the filters they generate. Given an infinite lower finite lattice
P
, we associate to each infinite ideal
I\subseteq P
the following family of subsets of
I
:

\mathcal{F}_{I}^{P}\coloneqq\big\{U\subseteq I:\exists x\in P\ (\pi_{I}[P\uparrow x]\subseteq U)\big\}.

When there is no risk of ambiguity, we just write
\mathcal{F}_{I}
instead of
\mathcal{F}_{I}^{P}
. This family is the filter on the set
I
generated by the projections of the upper cones of
P
onto the ideal
I
.

Lemma 3.1
.

\mathcal{F}_{I}
is a filter on
I
.

Proof.

Clearly,
I\in\mathcal{F}_{I}
,
\emptyset\not\in\mathcal{F}_{I}
, and
\mathcal{F}_{I}
is closed under taking supersets. Now let us show that
\mathcal{F}_{I}
contains the Fréchet filter on
I
. Let
F\subseteq I
be a finite set. Since
I
is infinite and
P
is lower finite, there exists
x\in I
with
\bigvee F<x
. Then, consider
\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x]
. As
x\in I
, it is easy to see that
\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x]=I\cap({\uparrow}x)
. Hence,
F\cap\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x]=\emptyset
. In particular,
I\setminus F\supseteq\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x]
, and thus
I\setminus F\in\mathcal{F}_{I}
.

Now pick
U,V\in\mathcal{F}_{I}
. We show that
U\cap V\in\mathcal{F}_{I}
. By definition, there are
x,y\in P
such that
U\supseteq\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x]
and
V\supseteq\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}y]
. But then,
\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}(x\vee y)]\subseteq\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x]\cap\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}y]
. Thus,
U\cap V\supseteq\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x]\cap\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}y]\supseteq\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}(x\vee y)]
and therefore
U\cap V\in\mathcal{F}_{I}
.
∎

We now prove some basic facts about the projections of upper cones.

Lemma 3.2
.

Given a lower finite lattice
P
, an ideal
I\subseteq P
, and an element
x\in P
, the following hold:

(1)

\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x]
is a meet-subsemilattice of
P
which is cofinal in
I
.

(2)

For every
y\in\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x]
,
y=\pi_{I}(x\vee y)
.

(3)

The map
\pi_{I}\upharpoonright(x\vee I)
is an isomorphism between
x\vee I
and
\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x]
with their induced orderings.

Proof.

That
\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x]
is a meet-subsemilattice of
P
follows immediately from the fact that
\pi_{I}
is a meet-homomorphism by Lemma
1.4
. That it is cofinal in
I
is also clear: for any
y\in I
, we have
y\leq\pi_{I}(x\vee y)\in\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x]
.

Now let us prove (2). Clearly,
y\leq\pi_{I}(x\vee y)
. Moreover, since
y\in\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x]
, there exists
z\geq x
such that
\pi_{I}(z)=y
. In particular, since
y\leq z
, we have
x\vee y\leq z
and hence
\pi_{I}(x\vee y)\leq\pi_{I}(z)=y
. Overall,

y\leq\pi_{I}(x\vee y)\leq\pi_{I}(z)=y,

and we conclude
\pi_{I}(x\vee y)=y
.

We now show (3). The map
\pi_{I}
is order-preserving. Moreover, it follows directly from (2) that
\pi_{I}[x\vee I]=\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x]
. Let us prove that
\pi_{I}\upharpoonright(x\vee I)
is injective.

Assume that
\pi_{I}(x\vee y)=\pi_{I}(x\vee z)
with
y,z\in I
. We show that
x\vee y=x\vee z
. The following inequalities hold

x\vee y\leq x\vee\pi_{I}(x\vee y)\leq x\vee y,

where the first inequality follows from
y\leq\pi_{I}(x\vee y)
, and the second one from
\pi_{I}(x\vee y)\leq x\vee y
. We conclude that
x\vee y=x\vee\pi_{I}(x\vee y)
, and similarly
x\vee z=x\vee\pi_{I}(x\vee z)
. Since
\pi_{I}(x\vee y)=\pi_{I}(x\vee z)
by assumption, we get
x\vee y=x\vee z
. Hence,
\pi_{I}\upharpoonright(x\vee I)
is injective.

What is the inverse of
\pi_{I}\upharpoonright(x\vee I)
? By (2), it is the map
y\mapsto x\vee y
, which is clearly order-preserving. We conclude that
\pi_{I}\upharpoonright(x\vee I)
is an isomorphism between
x\vee I
and
\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x]
with their induced orderings.
∎

By Lemma
3.2
(3), the meet-subsemilattice
\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x]
and the join-subsemilattice
x\vee I
are isomorphic with their induced orderings. We can therefore transfer the analysis of the breadth of
x\vee I
from the previous section to projected upper cones. In particular, Theorem
2.6
and Lemma
3.2
(3) yield the following direct corollary.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
P
be a lower finite lattice of breadth
n
and cardinality
\aleph_{n-1}
for some
n>0
. If
I\subseteq P
is a countably infinite ideal, then there exists
U\in\mathcal{F}_{I}
such that
U
is a chain.

Finally, let us prove two consequences of Theorem
3.3
. The first, which is employed in the proof of Theorem
A
, tells us that, under the relevant hypotheses, the map
I\mapsto\mathcal{F}_{I}
, which associates to each countably infinite ideal its filter of projected upper cones, is order-preserving with respect to the inclusion relation on ideals and the Rudin-Blass reducibility on filters. In particular, when
P
is a lower finite lattice of breadth
3
and cardinality
\aleph_{2}
, the following proposition, together with Lemma
1.9
, implies that if
\mathcal{F}_{I}
is meager for some countably infinite ideal
I
, then
\mathcal{F}_{J}
is meager for
every
countably infinite ideal
J
. Indeed, given countably infinite ideals
I
and
J
, the ideal generated by
I\cup J
is countably infinite and contains both
I
and
J
. In other words, the meagerness of the filter
\mathcal{F}_{I}
is independent of the choice of the countably infinite ideal
I
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
P
be a lower finite lattice of breadth
n>0
and cardinality
\aleph_{n-1}
, and let
I\subseteq J
be two countably infinite ideals of
P
. Then,
\mathcal{F}_{I}\leq_{RB}\mathcal{F}_{J}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.3
, we can pick an
x\in P
such that
D\coloneqq\pi_{J}[{\uparrow}x]
is a chain. The set
D
belongs to
\mathcal{F}_{J}
by definition, and moreover, by Lemma
3.2
(1),
D
is cofinal in
J
.

Let us first show that
\pi_{I}\upharpoonright D
is finite-to-one. Fix
y\in I
. We show that
\pi_{I}^{-1}(\{y\})\cap D
is finite. Since
I
is an infinite ideal and
P
is lower finite, there must be
z\in I
with
y<z
. Moreover, as
D
is cofinal in
J
and
z\in I\subseteq J
, there must be
u\in D
with
z\leq u
. Clearly,
\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}u]\subseteq{\uparrow}z
, and therefore
y\not\in\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}u]
. Furthermore, since
D
is a chain, we have

D=(D\cap({\uparrow}u))\cup(D\cap({\downarrow}u)).

We conclude that
\pi_{I}^{-1}(\{y\})\cap D\subseteq{\downarrow}u
, which is finite. Hence,
\pi_{I}\upharpoonright D
is finite-to-one.

Since
D
is an infinite subset of the countable set
J
, we can fix a finite-to-one map
f:J\rightarrow D
such that
f\upharpoonright D
is the identity. Let
\theta\coloneqq\pi_{I}\circ f
. Since both
f
and
\pi_{I}\upharpoonright D
are finite-to-one, so is
\theta
. Now pick some
U\subseteq I
and let us show that
U\in\mathcal{F}_{I}
if and only if
\theta^{-1}(U)\in\mathcal{F}_{J}
.

First suppose that
U\in\mathcal{F}_{I}
. We show that
\theta^{-1}(U)\in\mathcal{F}_{J}
. By definition, there exists some
y\in P
such that
U\supseteq\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}y]
. Then, the following holds:

\displaystyle\theta^{-1}(U)

\displaystyle\supseteq\theta^{-1}(\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}y])

\displaystyle=f^{-1}(\pi_{I}^{-1}(\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}y]))

\displaystyle\supseteq f^{-1}(\pi_{I}^{-1}(\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}y])\cap D)

\displaystyle\supseteq\pi_{I}^{-1}(\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}y])\cap D

\displaystyle=\pi_{I}^{-1}(\pi_{I}\circ\pi_{J}[{\uparrow}y])\cap D

\displaystyle\supseteq\pi_{J}[{\uparrow}y]\cap D\in\mathcal{F}_{J},

where the third inclusion follows from
f\upharpoonright D
being the identity and the second equality follows from Lemma
1.5
. In particular, we conclude that
\theta^{-1}(U)\in\mathcal{F}_{J}
.

Now suppose that
\theta^{-1}(U)\in\mathcal{F}_{J}
. We show that
U\in\mathcal{F}_{I}
. By definition, there must be some
y\in P
such that
\theta^{-1}(U)\supseteq\pi_{J}[{\uparrow}y]
. Replacing
y
by
x\vee y
if necessary, we may assume that
\pi_{J}[{\uparrow}y]\subseteq D
. Then,

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle\supseteq\theta\circ\pi_{J}[{\uparrow}y]

\displaystyle=\pi_{I}\circ f\circ\pi_{J}[{\uparrow}y]

\displaystyle=\pi_{I}\circ\pi_{J}[{\uparrow}y]

\displaystyle=\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}y]\in\mathcal{F}_{I},

where the second equality follows from
f\upharpoonright D
being the identity and
D\supseteq\pi_{J}[{\uparrow}y]
, while the last equality follows from Lemma
1.5
. In particular, we conclude that
U\in\mathcal{F}_{I}
. Overall,
\theta
witnesses
\mathcal{F}_{I}\leq_{RB}\mathcal{F}_{J}
.
∎

The second and final result of this section shows that, under the relevant cardinality hypotheses, the filters of projected upper cones on countably infinite ideals are all
\mathsf{P}
-filters. Recall that a filter
\mathcal{F}
is a
\mathsf{P}
-filter if, for every countable collection
\{U_{n}:n\in\omega\}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, there exists
U\in\mathcal{F}
such that
U_{n}\supseteq^{*}U
for every
n
. Although the next result establishes an important combinatorial feature of these filters, it will not be employed in the proof of Theorem
A
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
P
be a lower finite lattice of breadth
n>0
and cardinality
\aleph_{n-1}
. Then,
\mathcal{F}_{I}
is a
\mathsf{P}
-filter for every countably infinite ideal
I\subseteq P
.

Proof.

If
n=1
, note that
P
must be isomorphic to
\omega
with its usual ordering. The only infinite ideal of
\omega
is
\omega
itself, and
\mathcal{F}_{\omega}=\mathfrak{Fr}
, which is trivially a
\mathsf{P}
-filter. So we can assume
n>1
.

Pick a countably infinite ideal
I\subseteq P
and a countable family
\{U_{k}:k\in\omega\}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{I}
. By definition of
\mathcal{F}_{I}
, for each
k
we can pick
x_{k}\in P
such that
U_{k}\supseteq\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x_{k}]
. Arguing as in the proof of Theorem
2.6
, we have that the quotient
P/I
is a join-semilattice of cardinality
\aleph_{n-1}
whose principal ideals are countable. Moreover, as
P
is lower finite and
I
is infinite,
I
is unbounded, and we conclude by Corollary
2.5
that
P/I
has breadth at most
n-1
.

By
[
18
, Proposition 11]
, every join-semilattice of breadth (at most)
n-1
and cardinality
\aleph_{n-1}
whose principal ideals are countable is
\sigma
-directed—i.e., every countable subset has an upper bound. Thus,
P/I
is
\sigma
-directed, and we can pick
y\in P
such that
[x_{k}]_{I}\leq[y]_{I}
for every
k
.

By Theorem
3.3
, there exists
x\in P
such that
\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x]
is a chain. Consider the element
x\vee y
. We claim that
\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}(x\vee y)]\subseteq^{*}U_{k}
for every
k
. Since
\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}(x\vee y)]
belongs to
\mathcal{F}_{I}
, this claim will complete the proof.

Fix
k\in\omega
. We show that
\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}(x\vee y)]\subseteq^{*}U_{k}
. As
[x_{k}]_{I}\leq[y]_{I}
, there exists
a\in I
such that
x_{k}\leq a\vee y
. Thus,

({\uparrow}a)\cap\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}y]=\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}(a\vee y)]\subseteq\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x_{k}]\subseteq U_{k}.

By Lemma
3.2
(1),
\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}(x\vee y)]
is cofinal in
I
. Pick
u\in\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}(x\vee y)]
with
a\leq u
. Since
\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}(x\vee y)]\subseteq\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}x]
, the set
\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}(x\vee y)]
is also a chain. Thus,

\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}(x\vee y)]\setminus({\uparrow}a)\subseteq\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}(x\vee y)]\cap({\downarrow}u),

which is finite, as
P
is lower finite. Overall, we have

\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}(x\vee y)]=^{*}({\uparrow}a)\cap\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}(x\vee y)]\subseteq({\uparrow}a)\cap\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}y]\subseteq U_{k},

and we conclude
\pi_{I}[{\uparrow}(x\vee y)]\subseteq^{*}U_{k}
.
∎

4.
The main result

This section is devoted to the proof of the following theorem. By combining it with Corollary

A

for the relevant set-theoretic terminology and for the basics of Lévy’s collapse forcing

Theorem 4.1
.

If
\kappa
is a Mahlo cardinal and

is a
\mathsf{V}
-generic filter for
\mathrm{Coll}(\omega_{1},{<}\kappa)
, then in
\mathsf{V}[G]

and cardinality
\aleph_{2}
.

First, let us prove the following technical proposition.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
P
be a lower finite lattice of cardinality
\geq\aleph_{2}
and let
\mathbb{P}
be a forcing notion that preserves
\omega_{1}
. If
\dot{C}
is a
\mathbb{P}
-name such that

\Vdash\dot{C}\text{ is a cofinal subset of }P\text{ and }\dot{C}\text{ is a join-semilattice of breadth }2\\
\text{in the induced ordering},

then, for every

, there is

such that for every

The proof uses the following auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathsf{V\subseteq W}
be transitive class models of
\mathsf{ZFC}
with
\omega_{1}^{\mathsf{V}}=\omega_{1}^{\mathsf{W}}
, and let

be an uncountable lower finite lattice. Suppose that

is a cofinal subset of

2
in the induced ordering. Then, for every uncountable proper ideal

of

is not cofinal in

.

Proof.

Suppose, toward a contradiction, that there is an uncountable proper ideal

of
P
such that

is cofinal in
I
. In particular,
C\cap I
is a directed subset of

—i.e., any two elements of

have an upper bound in

. Moreover, as

is downward closed,
C\cap I
is also a downward closed subset of

. Overall,

, being a downward closed and directed subset of

with respect to the induced ordering.

Actually,

is a

. Indeed,
I
is a proper ideal of
P
, and therefore

, since
C
is a cofinal subset of

.

By the second inequality of Ditor’s Theorem

, every proper ideal of a lower finite join-semilattice of breadth

must be countable. Hence, by the preceding paragraph,

must be countable in

. Now, since

is cofinal in

—in general, every cofinal subset of a lower finite infinite poset must have the same cardinality as the whole poset. Thus,
|I|^{\mathsf{W}}=\aleph_{0}
, which is a contradiction, as
|I|^{\mathsf{V}}\geq\omega_{1}^{\mathsf{V}}=\omega_{1}^{\mathsf{W}}

∎
