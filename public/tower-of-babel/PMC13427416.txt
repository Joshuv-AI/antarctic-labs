The pseudocirrhotic liver: Hepar lobatum carcinomatosum in a patient with triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Nabil Ziani, Abderrahmane Ibenyahia, Zahra Hami, Soufiane Bigi, Houssam Rajad, Mohamed Ayez, Said Adnor, Zakaria Chahbi, Farah Boutaggount, Meryem Maskrout, Ghizlane Rais, Soukaina Wakrim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepar lobatum carcinomatosum (HLC) is a rare hepatic manifestation of metastatic malignancy that mimics cirrhosis in imaging and morphology but arises from entirely different pathophysiological mechanisms. Most commonly associated with breast cancer, particularly hormone-receptor-positive subtypes, HLC in the context of triple-negative breast cancer is extremely rare. This report presents the case of a 65-year-old woman with triple-negative breast cancer who developed progressive liver deformation consistent with HLC over a 12-month period, as documented by sequential computed tomography and Magnetic resonance imaging. Despite systemic chemotherapy, the liver showed marked lobulation, capsular retraction, and segmental atrophy in the absence of cirrhosis risk factors. Radiological features and clinical findings were analysed in light of current literature. The case emphasizes the need for heightened clinical suspicion and diagnostic accuracy to distinguish HLC from pseudocirrhosis and true cirrhosis, thereby improving patient management and prognosis.

Introduction

Hepar lobatum carcinomatosum (HLC) is a rare hepatic manifestation of metastatic malignancy, most frequently described in association with breast carcinoma. It is characterized by an irregularly lobulated liver, capsular retraction, and architectural distortion that can mimic cirrhosis both radiologically and macroscopically. Unlike true cirrhosis, these changes are secondary to metastatic infiltration, vascular obstruction, and subsequent fibrotic remodeling of the hepatic parenchyma [1,2].

Breast carcinoma, particularly invasive lobular subtype, is the most common malignancy associated with HLC. Reported mechanisms include tumor embolization of portal or hepatic veins, chemotherapy-related sinusoidal obstruction, and collapse of regressing metastases after treatment [3,4]. Clinically, patients may present with signs of hepatic dysfunction or portal hypertension, often in the absence of classical risk factors for chronic liver disease [5].

Although HLC has been well described in hormone receptor–positive breast cancers, its occurrence in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is exceedingly rare. TNBC represents an aggressive subtype of breast cancer, accounting for approximately 10%-15% of cases, with limited targeted treatment options and a higher propensity for visceral metastases. The coexistence of HLC in TNBC poses a unique diagnostic and clinical challenge, as the hepatic changes may mask disease progression or be misinterpreted as cirrhosis on imaging.

We report the case of a 65-year-old woman with triple-negative breast cancer who developed HLC, where radiological evaluation played a pivotal role in raising suspicion. The imaging findings, which closely mimicked cirrhosis, underscore the diagnostic challenges and highlight the importance of recognizing this rare hepatic manifestation in oncology practice.

Case presentation

A 65-year-old woman with no significant past medical history presented with a painless right breast mass that had been progressively increasing in size. Breast ultrasonography, performed on July 2024, revealed a lesion in the upper quadrant of the right breast measuring 29 × 22 × 15 mm, classified as BI-RADS 5, and associated with ipsilateral axillary lymphadenopathy. A core needle (tru-cut) biopsy confirmed an invasive ductal carcinoma, grade III according to the Scarff–Bloom–Richardson histological classification. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated a triple-negative phenotype. A contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan showed a right breast tumor with ipsilateral axillary lymphadenopathy and multiple hepatic nodules suspicious for secondary localization.

Physical examination revealed mild hepatomegaly without peripheral stigmata of chronic liver disease. Laboratory tests demonstrated elevated liver transaminases (AST 330 U/L, ALT 190 U/L) and hyperbilirubinemia (total bilirubin 158.77 mg/L; direct bilirubin 124.06 mg/L), while albumin and coagulation parameters remained within normal limits, indicating preserved hepatic synthetic function. Tumor markers (CA 15-3, CEA) were within reference ranges.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed in mid-2024 revealed a reduced liver volume with irregular and nodular contours, showing multiple confluent T2-hyperintense lesions with capsular retraction. The lesions appeared iso- to hypointense on T1-weighted images and demonstrated heterogeneous enhancement after gadolinium injection without definite washout. Diffusion-weighted sequences showed marked restricted diffusion, confirming the presence of active tumoral infiltration. PD-L1 expression and BRCA mutation testing were not performed, and neither immunotherapy nor PARP inhibitors were available in our setting. A baseline liver function assessment and viral hepatitis serology were performed, both showing no abnormalities. The case was discussed during a multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, and a consensus was reached to initiate systemic chemotherapy with paclitaxel and carboplatin.

After 4 therapeutic cycles, follow-up contrast-enhanced CT scan performed in January 2025 demonstrated progressive hepatic dysmorphism with a lobulated contour and multiple linear retractile hypodensities predominantly in segments IV, VII, and VIII. These findings were associated with segmental atrophy and relative hypertrophy of uninvolved parenchymal regions. The hepatic veins and main portal vein were patent, but the left portal branch appeared attenuated. A small perihepatic fluid collection and mild gallbladder wall thickening was also noted. The case was re-evaluated during a MDT meeting following radiologic evidence of hepatic progression, while liver function tests remained within normal limits. Based on the discussion, oral capecitabine was initiated as the next line of treatment.

After 3 months of oral capecitabine, a hepatic MRI performed in mid-2025 demonstrated a significant reduction in liver size (FH = 142 mm vs 175 mm in mid-2024), with irregular, bosselated contours. The liver parenchyma exhibited heterogeneous T2 signal intensity with multiple confluent nodular lesions, showing restricted diffusion and heterogeneous enhancement postcontrast. Some lesions displayed annular enhancement with central necrosis. Segmental atrophy of segment IV and relative hypertrophy of segment I were noted, indicative of secondary fibrotic remodeling. The largest lesion extended from segment II through VI and VIII, measuring up to 130 mm in transverse width and 91 mm in AP diameter. Collectively, the imaging findings demonstrated the evolution from localized metastatic deposits in 2024 to a diffusely infiltrative, retractile hepatic morphology in 2025, consistent with HLC. The diagnosis was supported by the absence of prior liver disease, the pattern of segmental capsular retraction, and the imaging progression (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Two months later, the patient developed clinical signs of hepatocellular failure and passed away, with an overall survival of 12 months.

Discussion

HLC is a rare acquired hepatic deformity caused by diffuse infiltration of metastatic carcinoma, most commonly breast cancer. Unlike cirrhosis, HLC does not result from chronic hepatocellular injury but from tumor infiltration, vascular compromise, and secondary fibrosis, producing a distorted and lobulated liver morphology. Its rarity and radiological resemblance to cirrhosis or pseudocirrhosis make diagnosis challenging, particularly in oncology patients where early recognition impacts management.

Pathophysiology

The development of HLC is attributed to tumoral embolization of intrahepatic vessels, particularly the portal and hepatic veins, leading to parenchymal ischemia, collapse, and fibrosis [1]. In addition, desmoplastic stromal reaction induced by tumor cells contributes to capsular retraction and distortion. Over time, the process results in pseudolobulated liver morphology, often accompanied by portal hypertension and ascites [6].

Clinical implications

From a clinical standpoint, patients with HLC often present with signs of portal hypertension (ascites, splenomegaly, and varices), while maintaining relatively preserved hepatic synthetic function compared with classical cirrhosis [5]. This discrepancy between severe radiological deformity and preserved function should alert clinicians to alternative diagnoses beyond cirrhosis.

Moreover, misinterpretation of HLC as progression of cirrhosis or treatment-related pseudocirrhosis can lead to delays in appropriate oncologic management. Several case series emphasize that radiologists and oncologists must consider HLC in patients with known breast carcinoma and atypical liver deformation on imaging [3,4].

Radiological features

Radiology is pivotal in the diagnosis of HLC. CT and MRI allow recognition of the characteristic features:•CT scan: Contrast-enhanced CT often demonstrates irregular liver contours with focal or diffuse capsular retraction, segmental atrophy interspersed with hypertrophy, and parenchymal heterogeneity. These findings can mimic macronodular cirrhosis but differ by the absence of classic regenerative nodules and the frequent presence of infiltrative hypodense areas corresponding to metastatic deposits [4,7].•MRI: Provides superior tissue characterization. Typical findings include T1 hypointensity and T2 hyperintensity of affected segments, architectural distortion, and retraction of the hepatic capsule. Dynamic contrast studies reveal heterogeneous enhancement due to fibrosis and tumoral infiltration. These MRI patterns are especially useful in differentiating HLC from benign fibrotic changes or chemotherapy-associated pseudocirrhosis [2,5].•Pitfalls: The radiological similarities between HLC and cirrhosis can lead to potential diagnostic pitfalls. Both CT and MRI in HLC may be misinterpreted as advanced cirrhosis with regenerative nodules, or alternatively as “pseudocirrhosis,” a term often used for chemotherapy-induced transient hepatic changes in metastatic breast cancer [8]. In our patient’s case, this distinction was particularly important. She had received systemic chemotherapy for TNBC, and patients with treated metastases can develop pseudocirrhotic changes (shrinkage of metastases with fibrotic scars). Indeed, some of her earlier imaging (mid-2024) showed a response of individual lesions to therapy. However, by early 2025 the development of new capsular retractions and diffuse nodularity indicated ongoing tumor activity rather than mere post-treatment scarring. One clue was that her disease was progressing on imaging despite normal tumor marker levels—a scenario where one might erroneously attribute the liver changes to benign treatment effects if relying only on labs. In HLC, tumor markers (like CA 15-3 in breast cancer) can be normal even as hepatic metastatic involvement worsens, as seen in our patient. Another clue was the discrepancy between the severe imaging appearance and the clinical profile: our patient had no history of chronic liver disease and lacked stigmata of cirrhosis (aside from mild jaundice), making a primary liver cirrhosis unlikely. Distinguishing HLC from chemotherapy-related pseudocirrhosis was critical here—misclassification could have led to complacency or incorrect management. In our case, recognizing the CT/MRI changes as HLC prompted continued aggressive oncologic therapy for active metastases rather than misinterpreting them as irreversible cirrhotic changes. This case highlights that, in a patient with metastatic cancer, new or worsening lobulated hepatic contour and capsular retractions should raise suspicion for active malignancy (HLC) over treatment-related changes, especially when clinical and laboratory features do not fully align with end-stage cirrhosis.

Literature comparison

In the largest published series, Alberti et al. [4] reported 5 cases of HLC secondary to breast carcinoma, all demonstrating irregular liver contour, lobulation, and capsular retraction on CT/MRI, closely mimicking cirrhosis. Similarly, Graber et al. described a case where HLC revealed an occult metastatic lobular breast carcinoma. Gravel et al. and Honma further highlighted the pathological basis of this entity, linking hepatic distortion to tumoral obstruction of vascular structures. More recently, Nejmeddine et al. [6] and Mathis et al. [5] documented cases where HLC was initially misdiagnosed as cirrhosis or idiopathic portal hypertension, underscoring persistent diagnostic pitfalls. A limitation of this report is the absence of histopathological confirmation. The diagnosis of HLC was based on serial CT and MRI findings, their characteristic evolution over time, and the clinical context of metastatic triple-negative breast cancer. Although liver biopsy could have provided definitive pathological confirmation, it was not performed because the imaging findings were considered highly suggestive and the result was unlikely to modify therapeutic management. Consequently, the diagnosis should be interpreted as a clinicoradiological diagnosis.

Conclusion

HLC remains a diagnostic masquerader that challenges radiologists and clinicians, especially in patients with metastatic cancer. This case highlights the importance of integrating imaging findings with clinical context to differentiate HLC from true cirrhosis or chemotherapy-induced pseudocirrhosis. By recognizing the liver changes on imaging as HLC, the misdiagnosis was avoided and appropriate oncologic management for active metastatic disease was ensured. In summary, HLC should be considered in any cancer patient who develops unexplained cirrhosis-like liver changes on imaging. Timely diagnosis of HLC is crucial, as it portends active tumor progression and enables clinicians to tailor therapy accordingly.
