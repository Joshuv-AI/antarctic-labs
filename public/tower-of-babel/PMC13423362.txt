Wnt/β-Catenin Signaling Pathway Is Involved in Phenotypic Transformation of Human Skin Fibroblasts Induced by IL-17A
Authors: Xiang He, Yunshu Yang, Jiaqi Liu, Dahai Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Hypertrophic scar (HTS) formation following severe trauma or burns remains a significant clinical challenge due to the lack of effective treatments, imposing substantial psychological burdens. Chronic inflammation is a key driver of HTS pathogenesis. Interleukin-17A (IL-17A), a critical inflammatory cytokine implicated in organ fibrosis, may play a role in HTS formation, but its function and mechanism in cutaneous scarring are not well defined.Methods:We collected 10 pairs of HTSs and normal skin tissues from patients with burns and examined the expression of IL-17A and Wnt signaling pathway–related molecules by immunohistochemistry and Western blot. These findings were further verified in rabbit ear scar experiments.Results:IL-17A protein expression was significantly higher in HTSs than in normal skin, whereas Wnt signaling pathway–related molecules were downregulated in the HTS group. In rabbit ear scar models, Wnt signaling inhibitors aggravated HTS development, whereas activators partially inhibited HTS formation.Conclusions:Our findings demonstrate that IL-17A may target cyclin D1 downstream of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. These findings represent a promising therapeutic target for the prevention and treatment of HTSs.

Takeaways

Question: How does interleukin-17A (IL-17A) contribute to hypertrophic scar (HTS) formation, and what role does the Wnt/β-catenin pathway play?

Findings: IL-17A was elevated in HTS tissues and promoted fibroblast transformation by inhibiting Wnt/β-catenin signaling, whereas Wnt activation reduced scarring in rabbit models.

Meaning: Targeting the IL-17A-Wnt/β-catenin axis may offer a novel therapeutic strategy for preventing HTSs.

INTRODUCTION

Tissue repair after trauma and burns is a complex process. Ideally, the healing process results in the formation of healthy scars that are inconspicuous; however, abnormal scars, such as keloids or hypertrophic scars (HTSs), can also develop.1 Approximately 70% of patients develop HTSs as burn wounds heal.2 Studies have reported that persistent myofibroblasts are a key factor in pathological fibrosis.3,4 Alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) is a major participant in the contractile forces of collagen and is an important indicator of the phenotypic transformation of fibroblasts.5 Continuous myofibroblast activation contributes to wound contraction and promotes extracellular matrix production, which aggravates HTS formation.6

Numerous studies have confirmed that interleukin-17A (IL-17A) plays an important role in tissue damage and infection,7 and chronic inflammation is one of the main reasons for HTS formation.1 In recent years, research on the roles of IL-17A in fibrosis has been increasing, especially in internal organs,8–12 but no studies have examined this in HTS tissues. Therefore, we examined the role of IL-17A in HTS formation.

The Wnt signaling pathway is also being increasingly linked to organ fibrosis. Yang et al13 found that downregulation of the classical Wnt pathway reduces collagen deposition in cardiomyocytes. Interestingly, another study reported that Wnt/β-catenin signaling played an important role in atrial fibrosis and atrial fibrillation.14 Alyoussef and Taha15 found that blocking the Wnt pathway attenuates the activation of skin cancer–induced profibrotic signaling.15 Only a few studies have linked classic Wnt/β-catenin signaling to HTS formation.16,17 However, interactions between IL-17A and the Wnt pathway have only been studied in orthopedics,18–21 and the upstream regulators that drive the dysregulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in HTSs, particularly within the context of chronic inflammation, remain largely unexplored. Therefore, our aim was to investigate whether the Wnt signaling pathway is involved in the IL-17A-mediated transformation of fibroblasts during HTS formation.

METHODS

Specimen Collection and RNA Sequencing

Healthy and HTS skin tissues were collected from the Department of Burns and Cutaneous Surgery at Xijing Hospital (Xi’an, Shanxi, China). All patients provided written informed consent and volunteered to provide their removed tissues. All experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fourth Military Medical University. We sent 3 of these tissues to Genergy Biotechnology (Shanghai, China) for RNA sequencing.

Cell Culture and Transfection

Sterile tissues were washed 3 times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), after which the epidermis and dermis were separated with Dispase II (0.25%, D4693-1G, Sigma) and digested for 6–8 hours. Next, the tissues were shredded and cultured in Dulbecco modified Eagle medium (DMEM), containing 10% fetal calf serum (BI, 04-001-1A, Israel), 1% penicillin, and 1% streptomycin, in a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37°C, and the fibroblasts were allowed to migrate by themselves. In all experiments, cells were used between passages 4 and 7. IL-17A small interfering (si)RNA was purchased from Biomics (Nantong, China). Control siRNA (siNT) and siIL-17A (50 nM) were transfected into fibroblasts using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). After 6 hours, the transfection solution was removed, and fresh complete medium was added. Recombinant IL-17A-expressing (AdIL-17A, Ubi-MCS-3FLAG-CBh-gcGFP-IRES-Puromycin) and control (AdNT, Ubi-MCS-3FLAG-SV40-Puromycin) lentiviral vectors were purchased from Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Fibroblasts were infected for 8 hours, and then the medium containing the lentivirus was removed, and fresh complete medium was added.

Real-time Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA was extracted from tissues or fibroblasts using TRIzol (Takara, Dalian, China). RNA (500 ng) was reverse transcribed to cDNA with PrimeScript RT Master Mix kit (Takara, Japan). Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction was performed in a 10-μL reaction volume (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA). Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase was used as an internal control. The primer sequences were as follows: 5′-CTCCTCCACCTTTGACGCTG-3′ and 5′-TCCTCTTGTGCTCTTGCTGG-3′ for glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase; 5′-ACTACCTCAACCGTTCCACG-3′ and 5′-ATGTGGTGGTCCAGCTTTCC-3′ for IL-17A. The data analysis method followed the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western Blot Analysis

Sterile tissues and cells were lysed with radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer containing 1% protease inhibitor. Proteins (50 μg) were resolved by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and then transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for at least 2 hours at 37°C and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C on a shaker. The next day, membranes were incubated with the corresponding horseradish peroxidase–linked secondary antibodies (1:3000; Boster, Wuhan, China) for 1 hour at 37°C. Protein bands were developed with an electrochemiluminescence kit (Millipore) and imaged using a FluorChem FC system (Alpha Innotech, San Leandro, CA). Primary antibodies were as follows: anti‐α‐SMA (Cell Signaling Technologies [CST], #19245, 1:1000), anti‐β-catenin (CST, #8480, 1:1000), anti‐phospho-glycogen synthase kinase 3β (p-GSK3β; Ser9; CST, #5558, 1:1000), anti‐Axin 2 (Abcam, ab32197, 1:1000), anti‐cyclin D1 (CST, #55506S, 1:1000), anti‐IL-17A (1:500; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), and anti‐β tubulin (Bioss, bs-4511R, 1:500).

Masson Trichrome Staining

Rabbit ear scar tissue was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours, then decalcified, embedded in paraffin blocks, sectioned at 5 μm, and stained with a Masson Trichrome Staining Kit (MM1007-400ML, MK Biological Co., Ltd., Shanghai). Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin and eosin.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 minutes and then blocked in 1% bovine serum albumin for 30 minutes at room temperature. Cells were incubated with rabbit antihuman α-SMA antibody (CST) overnight at 4°C. The next day, the primary antibody was removed, and the cells were incubated with antirabbit cyanine 3-linked secondary antibodies (BA1037, BOSTER, Wuhan, China) for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole. Images were acquired on Olympus TH4-200 Imaging Navigator (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunohistochemistry

IL-17A primary antibodies (1:100; Proteintech) were used to stain sections according to the instructions provided with the SPlink DAB Detection Kit (ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China). Images were acquired using an FSX100 Bio Imaging Navigator (Olympus).

Cell Viability Assays

Viability assays were performed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (Dalian Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Fibroblasts were seeded in 96-well plates at 4000 cells/well. The number of viable cells was measured at 450 nm using Infinite M200 PRO (Tecan, Switzerland).

Flow Cytometry Analysis

Fibroblasts were stimulated with 10% DMEM or DMEM plus 50 μg/mL IL-17A for 24 hours. The cells were detached with 0.25% trypsin and washed twice with PBS, then fixed in 75% ice-cold ethanol for more than 2 hours. After fixation, cells were washed once with PBS and resuspended in 400 μL of propidium iodide/RNase staining buffer (BD Biosciences) and incubated in the dark for 15 minutes at room temperature. For apoptosis assays, cells were washed twice with PBS and then resuspended in 1× binding buffer (BD Biosciences). Next, 100 μL of the solution was stained with 5 μL fluorescein isothiocyanate-annexin V and 5 μL propidium iodide for 15 minutes at room temperature. Then, 200 μL of binding buffer was added to each tube. Cell cycle and apoptosis analysis were performed on a BD Accuri C6 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

Three-dimensional Gel Assays

We purchased 10 rats from the Experimental Animal Center of the Fourth Military Medical University. All experimental procedures were approved by the ethics committee of the Fourth Military Medical University. Rat tail collagen was extracted from the tail according to the method of Bell et al.22 The concentration was determined to be approximately 1.2 mg/mL using a nucleic acid–protein analyzer, and 5× DMEM (Gibco) was prepared. The volume fractions of rat tail collagen, 5× DMEM, and a fibroblast suspension were mixed for 3-dimensional gels at a ratio of 7:2:1. The gels were treated with transforming growth factor β1 (TGF-β1; 10 ng/mL), IL-17A (50 ng/mL), XAV939 (20 μM), or SKL2001 (20 μM); a control gel was left untreated. Collagen shrinkage was measured at 6, 12, 24, and 48 hours, and images were acquired with a Canon 50D digital single lens reflex camera.

Rabbit Ear Scar Model

We purchased 12 New Zealand white rabbits weighing about 2.5–3 kg from the Experimental Animal Center of the Fourth Military Medical University. There were 6 females and 6 males. All experimental procedures were approved by the ethics committee of the Fourth Military Medical University. Intraperitoneal anesthesia was performed with sodium pentobarbital (30 mg/kg). Under sterile conditions, 6 round wounds with a diameter of 1 cm were created on the ventral side of each rabbit ear. The epidermis, dermis, and perichondrium were removed from each wound, and the wounds were covered with sterile gauze for 1 day. Subcutaneous injections of PBS (as a control), 50 ng IL-17A, 10 nM XAV939, or 10 nM SKL2001 were administered into the ear scars for 28 days (12 scars per group). We observed the rabbits for 28 days in a standard environment. Next, the rabbits were euthanized, and the thicknesses of the most prominent scar hyperplasia and adjacent healthy skin (HS) were measured. These values were used to calculate the scar index using the following equation: scar index = thickness where scar hyperplasia is most pronounced/thickness of adjacent HS.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (La Jolla, CA). The results were recorded as the mean ± standard error of the mean. Statistical differences between groups were analyzed using the Student t test and 1-way analysis of variance. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

IL-17A Expression Is Increased in HTS

Several studies have reported that IL-17A is closely related to organ fibrosis,9–12 so we explored whether it is involved in skin fibrosis. We examined IL-17A expression in HTS and HS tissues from patients with burns and trauma by immunohistochemistry, real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction, and Western blotting, and found that it was significantly increased at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 1A).

Expression of IL-17A and Wnt signaling pathway–related proteins in HTS and NS. Immunohistochemistry (A) and Western blot (B) were used to detect IL-17A expression in HTS and HS samples (n = 3). C, The protein expression of β-catenin, Axin 2, and p-GSK3β in HTS and HS samples (n = 4) was detected by Western blot. Each data point represents the mean ± standard error of the mean of 3 independent experiments and was normalized to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01. NS, normal skin.

Wnt Pathway–related Molecules Are Decreased in HTS

Studies implicating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in fibrosis are increasing. β-catenin is a key signal transduction molecule in classical Wnt signaling. In the basal state, cytoplasmic β-catenin resides mainly in the adenomatous polyposis coli–Axin–GSK3β complex, where it is phosphorylated by GSK3β and marked for degradation, thereby inhibiting Wnt signaling. When Wnt molecules bind frizzled receptors on the cell membrane, resultant signal transduction leads to the inhibition of GSK3β and the accumulation of β-catenin, which is translocated to the nucleus and regulates the transcription of downstream effector genes.23,24

We next tested the expression of β-catenin, Axin 2, and p-GSK3β, key molecules of the destruction complex regulating Wnt signaling, in HTS and HS samples (n = 4). The levels of all 3 Wnt pathway components were significantly reduced in HTS samples (Fig. 1B). This suggests that IL-17A inhibits Wnt signaling.

Effects of IL-17A on Fibroblasts

To systematically examine the effects of IL-17A on HTS, we performed RNA sequencing on paired HTS and HS tissues (n = 3). The results indicated that components of the IL-17A signaling pathway were differentially expressed between the 2 groups (Fig. 2A). Zhang et al25 found that persistently elevated IL17 levels aggravated the fibrogenesis process in HTS through its proinflammatory and profibrotic effects, and verified this in a mouse wound healing model. To examine the direct effects of IL-17A on fibroblasts, we stimulated cells with different concentrations (12.5, 25, 50, 75, and 100 ng/mL) of IL-17A for various time points (6, 12, 24, and 48 h) and measured their viability. The cells displayed the highest viability with 50 ng/mL IL-17A, 24 hours after stimulation. The results provided a reference for our subsequent experiments and confirmed that IL-17A had significant effects on fibroblast proliferation within a certain concentration range. Interestingly, there were more S/G2/M cells in the control group and fewer G0/G1 cells in the experimental group. Thus, IL-17A significantly shortened the cell cycle and accelerated cell proliferation (Fig. 2B) but had no significant effect on apoptosis (Fig. 2C).

Each data point represents the mean ± standard error of the mean (n = 3). *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01. cAMP, Cyclic adenosine monophosphate; ECM, extracellular matrix; PPAR, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor.

The Wnt Pathway Is Involved in the IL-17A-mediated Phenotypic Transformation of Fibroblasts

To verify the profibrotic effects of IL-17A, we stimulated healthy fibroblasts with 50 ng/mL IL-17A for 24 hours and then assayed the level of α-SMA, the main marker of fibroblast phenotypic transformation. As the TGF-β pathway is also important for fibrosis, α-SMA levels were also assessed after TGF-β stimulation. IL-17A promoted fibroblast phenotypic transformation similarly to TGF-β. Interestingly, IL-17A inhibited the expression of key molecules in the Wnt pathway, including the downstream target gene cyclin D1 (Fig. 2D). To confirm the involvement of the Wnt pathway in the IL-17A-induced phenotypic transformation of fibroblasts, we used XAV939 and SKL2001 to stimulate fibroblasts. Wnt inhibition and activation increased and decreased α-SMA expression, respectively (Fig. 2D). Taken together, these results indicate that IL-17A plays a key role in the phenotypic transformation of healthy fibroblasts, and that the Wnt pathway is involved in mediating these effects (Fig. 2D).

To confirm that the Wnt pathway is involved in the phenotypic transformation of IL-17A-induced fibroblasts, we used 3-dimensional gel shrinkage experiments, which reflect the α-SMA content of the fibroblasts. We used TGF-β and IL-17A to stimulate fibroblasts and found that IL-17A promoted gel contraction similarly to TGF-β. Figure 3 shows fibroblasts in the gel. Next, we used IL-17A to induce the phenotypic transformation of fibroblasts in the presence of activators and inhibitors of the Wnt pathway. Inhibition of the Wnt pathway aggravated gel contraction (Fig. 3). Consistent results were observed in scar fibroblasts (Fig. 3).

Effects of IL-17A on Fibroblast Wnt Signaling

We also examined α-SMA expression levels in healthy and HTS fibroblasts under different stimulation conditions through immunofluorescence (Fig. 4A). These results confirm that IL-17A promotes α-SMA production in fibroblasts and activates the Wnt signaling pathway, which can significantly reduce IL-17A-induced phenotypic transformation of fibroblasts.

B, Confirmation of IL-17A overexpression, and α-SMA and Wnt pathway–related molecule levels after IL-17A overexpression. C, Confirmation of IL-17A depletion, and α-SMA and Wnt pathway–related molecule levels after IL-17A depletion. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, relative to the control (by t test). Each data point represents the mean ± standard error of the mean (n = 3). Scale bar = 50 μm. DAPI, 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole; HC, hypertrophic cell; NC, normal cell.

To clarify the relationship between IL-17A and Wnt signaling in fibroblast transformation, we overexpressed IL-17A in fibroblasts and detected the levels of Wnt pathway indicators and α-SMA. IL-17A overexpression significantly increased α-SMA and decreased Wnt pathway–related molecules and cyclin D1 (Fig. 4B). We used siRNA to deplete fibroblasts of IL-17A and found that α-SMA was significantly decreased and Wnt pathway–related molecules were significantly increased (Fig. 4C). Therefore, IL-17A promotes the phenotypic transformation of fibroblasts by targeting the cyclin D1 expression to inhibit the Wnt pathway.

Wnt Pathway Activation Reduces the IL-17A-induced Phenotypic Transformation of Fibroblasts

Twelve rabbits were randomly divided into 4 groups. Rabbit ear wounds were injected with PBS, IL-17A, IL-17A and XVA939, or IL-17A and SKL2001. Each experimental group had 3 rabbits and each rabbit had 12 repetitive scars. We observed the rabbits for 28 days in a standard environment. Next, the rabbits were euthanized, and the scars were measured to calculate the scar index (Fig. 5A). We measured the maximum and minimum scars of each rabbit ear for recording. Interestingly, compared with the control group, SKL2001 significantly reduced the scar index of IL-17A-induced HTSs. Masson staining demonstrated that SKL2001 resulted in decreased collagen formation and increased collagen alignment, thereby inhibiting scar formation (Fig. 5B).

The ear scars of the rabbits were injected with PBS, IL-17A (50 ng), XVA939 (10 nM), or SKL2001 (10 nM), and scar indices (A, B and C) were calculated. D, Scar tissue specimens were stained with hematoxylin and eosin and Masson trichrome. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, relative to the control (by t test). Each data point represents the mean ± standard error of the mean (n = 3). Scale bar = 50 μm.

DISCUSSION

Several studies have established IL-17A as a requisite factor in organ fibrosis;26–35 yet, its role in cutaneous HTS remains unclear. Chronic inflammation and fibrosis are inextricably linked, and macrophages play a vital role in inflammation. As a pleiotropic cytokine, IL-17A is a key mediator of inflammatory and immune responses,36 and its role in fibrosis may represent a pathological extension of these functions within the wound microenvironment. Moreover, IL-17A can synergize with TGF-β to exacerbate fibrosis,37 suggesting that its inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin signaling may be 1 pivotal mechanism within a larger profibrotic network.

Studies on the Wnt signaling pathway in skin hair follicles and fibrosis are increasing,38,39 and studies describing interactions between IL-17A and the Wnt signaling pathway are extensive in orthopedics.40–43 A relationship between IL-17A and the Wnt pathway has also been reported in the microenvironments of intestinal tumors,44,45 and in pulmonary hypertension.46 However, their interplay in skin fibroblasts has not been reported. Our results indicate that IL-17A promotes HTS by driving fibroblast transformation through the inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, potentially via targeting cyclin D1. These findings reveal the Wnt signaling pathway as a downstream effector in IL-17A-mediated fibrosis and provide a novel therapeutic target for scar treatment. Previous study also demonstrated that pirfenidone inhibits Wnt/β-catenin signaling to ameliorate scarring,47 which may reflect the context-dependent roles of Wnt signaling during wound healing. The paradoxical role of Wnt signaling underscores its context-dependent nature and highlights the necessity for precise, disease-stage–specific therapeutic modulation. However, this study has several limitations, including its exclusive focus on fibroblasts, which neglects critical immune interactions, and the need to validate the translational relevance of the rabbit ear model for human hypertrophic scarring.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, our results indicate that IL-17A expression is increased in HTS tissue, whereas Wnt pathway–related molecules are decreased. Activation of the Wnt signaling pathway can reduce the phenotypic transformation of fibroblasts induced by IL-17A, possibly by targeting cyclin D1. Our results suggest that the Wnt signaling pathway is inhibited during the IL-17A-mediated phenotypic transformation of fibroblasts and provides new targets for HTS treatment.
