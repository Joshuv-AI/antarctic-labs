Human centric hybrid modeling of circular waste systems for urban transitions
Authors: R. Anitha, A. Parthiban
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUrban waste systems are shaped by interactions among household behavior, infrastructure capacity, and policy design, especially in rapidly urbanizing regions. This study develops a hybrid modeling framework integrating agent-based simulation, system dynamics, and graph theoretic analysis to examine municipal waste transitions under diverse interventions. Using a synthetic urban system grounded in national benchmarks, the model evaluates behavioral, economic, and infrastructural strategies across diversion, circularity, cost, emissions, and equity. Results show that integrated interventions improve diversion efficiency, reduce system costs and emissions, and enhance equity outcomes, while revealing critical feedback mechanisms. The framework captures behavioral heterogeneity, identifies spatial bottlenecks, and incorporates informal sector contributions and digital access. Its modular structure supports scalability across urban contexts. The approach provides a decision support tool for designing inclusive and effective waste management policies aligned with sustainability goals.

Introduction

Urban waste management is emerging as both a logistical challenge and a climate imperative, driven by rapid urbanization, growing resource constraints, and increasing climate vulnerability. Municipal solid waste (MSW) generation has increased sharply over the last two decades due to population growth, changing consumption patterns, and expanding urban footprints.1,2 In India alone, daily MSW generation exceeds 170,000 tonnes, with substantial disparities in collection, segregation, and treatment performance across states and municipalities. Despite regulatory frameworks such as the Solid Waste Management Rules (2016), implementation remains uneven, and progress toward circularity targets remains limited.3,4

Traditional waste planning approaches typically rely on static models or aggregate indicators, which are insufficient to represent the dynamic and decentralized nature of urban waste systems. These systems are shaped by complex interactions among household behavior, infrastructure capacity, policy design, and socioeconomic conditions.5 Additional layers of heterogeneity arise from informal sector participation, uneven digital access, and local governance arrangements, all of which are difficult to capture using conventional modeling techniques. The operational importance of informal waste workers in Indian cities has been widely documented, underscoring the need for inclusive and context-sensitive analytical frameworks.6,7,8,9 As cities strive to meet sustainable development goals (SDGs) related to sustainable consumption, climate action, and inclusive urbanization,10 there is a growing demand for modeling tools that support evidence-based policy exploration under realistic system constraints.

Hybrid modeling approaches that integrate behavioral, systemic, and spatial perspectives have gained attention as a means of analyzing complex urban systems. While such approaches have been successfully applied in domains such as energy transitions, water management, and urban mobility,11 their systematic application to MSW systems particularly in low- and middle-income country (LMIC) contexts remains limited.12,13 However, many existing waste management models focus on isolated components, limiting their usefulness for integrated policy analysis.14,15,16 Moreover, relatively few studies explicitly incorporate circular economy objectives or distributional equity outcomes, despite their growing relevance in sustainable urban planning.17,18

This study addresses these gaps by advancing a methodological contribution rather than proposing a new optimization algorithm. Rather than analyzing behavioral, systemic, or spatial dimensions in isolation, this work develops an integrated hybrid simulation framework to examine how household decision-making, infrastructure routing, and system-level feedback jointly shape urban waste outcomes. The framework is applied to a synthetic urban archetype designed to reflect common challenges observed in low-performing municipalities, rather than to predict outcomes for any specific city. This controlled setting enables systematic comparison of policy and infrastructure interventions while avoiding claims of direct empirical forecasting.

The framework evaluates intervention performance across multiple dimensions, including diversion, circularity, cost, emissions, and equity, thereby enabling the identification of trade-offs and synergies that are often overlooked in single-objective analyses. Importantly, the model explicitly examines how policy measures affect different socioeconomic groups, supporting the design of inclusive waste management strategies that move beyond one-size-fits-all assumptions. By embedding circular economy principles within an integrated urban systems perspective, the study positions municipal waste management as a strategic entry point for climate-responsive and socially inclusive urban governance.19,20

Problem statement

Urban waste systems in LMICs remain fragmented, reactive, and weakly aligned with circular economy principles. Despite regulatory mandates and technical developments, municipal systems face challenges in incorporating behavioral variability, physical limits, and informal sector dynamics into operational planning. Existing models often fail to integrate behavioral, infrastructural, and spatial dimensions, limiting their ability to simulate realistic transitions. Furthermore, infrastructure constraints, governance obstacles, and restricted digital access hamper the scalability of decentralized and inclusive waste solutions. The disconnect between conceptual modeling and operational viability hinders the development of robust, equitable, and data-driven urban waste systems.

Research motivation

This study responds to the gap between simulation research and operational urban waste planning in India’s socio-technical context. The study seeks to capture the complexity of household behavior, infrastructure routing, and policy feedback loops by combining agent-based modeling (ABM), system dynamics (SDs), and graph-theoretic optimization. The explicit inclusion of informal sector responsibilities, digital access zones, and circularity indicators is intended to ensure that the proposed framework is both technically robust and socially inclusive, equipping municipal planners, policymakers, and academics with a scalable decision-support tool aligned with climate goals, equity outcomes, and smart city ambitions.

Related works

Urban waste systems are increasingly recognized as complex, adaptive networks influenced by behavioral, infrastructural, and policy factors. Recent research emphasizes the importance of hybrid modeling approaches that combine agent-based logic, SD, and spatial optimization to represent realistic urban waste transitions.21,22 Hybrid forecasting models have been shown to better capture nonlinear waste generation trends than traditional statistical methods, subject to data availability and modeling assumptions.11 Policy reform, stakeholder alignment, and institutional preparation all contribute to the implementation of the circular economy in MSW management.23,24

Technological breakthroughs such as artificial intelligence (AI)-powered sorting and digital twins are transforming waste infrastructure design, but financial and governance constraints remain.25,26 Informal sector participation and decentralized composting have shown environmental and social benefits in urban India, especially when accompanied by inclusive governance frameworks.27 Graph-theoretic routing and geographical information system (GIS)-based optimization help identify spatial bottlenecks that hinder collection efficiency.28 These findings underscore the importance of hybrid, inclusive, and data-driven modeling frameworks for developing resilient urban waste systems in India and elsewhere. Despite these advances, several gaps remain in the existing literature. Most studies apply behavioral models, SD, spatial optimization, or machine learning techniques in isolation, with limited interaction across scales.37 Few frameworks explicitly integrate household-level behavioral heterogeneity, spatial routing efficiency, and system-level feedback loops within a single simulation architecture. Moreover, equity and circularity outcomes are often treated qualitatively or omitted altogether, particularly in low- and middle-income urban contexts.19 These limitations constrain the ability of existing models to support integrated, policy-relevant decision-making for urban waste transitions.

The reviewed literature demonstrates a clear evolution in urban waste modeling, with a greater emphasis on hybrid techniques that use machine learning, simulation, and decision-making frameworks. Studies conducted in a variety of locations, including Mexico, China, India, and Indonesia, illustrate the operational benefits of combining predictive analytics with spatial and behavioral logic. Across approaches such as deep learning classification, agent-based simulation (ABS), and SD, these models support forecasting, routing analysis, and policy exploration, contingent on contextual and data constraints. Notably, the incorporation of informal sector dynamics, digital technology, and circular economy concepts demonstrates a rising commitment to fairness and sustainability in waste governance.6 Together, these studies highlight the growing use of hybrid and data-driven approaches in urban waste management, while also revealing persistent limitations in cross-scale integration, behavioral representation, and outcome assessment.

National waste performance (2021–2022)

MSW systems in India are decentralized, with performance varying significantly across ULBs. However, implementation shortcomings exist, particularly in treatment infrastructure and behavioral compliance.38,39 In the fiscal year 2021–22, India generated 170,339 tonnes per day (TPD) of MSW.40 156,449 TPD was collected (92%), 91,511 TPD was treated (54%), and 41,455 TPD was disposed of (24%). The remaining 22% was unaccounted for, implying systemic leakage. Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) estimates that the waste sector emits approximately 9.6MtCO2−e, largely from methane generated during anaerobic decomposition in landfills (2022). Behavioral outcome effects

Behavioral interventions produced measurable changes in waste system performance by altering household segregation and participation patterns. Informational nudges increased awareness-driven compliance, while PAYT mechanisms introduced economic feedback that discouraged residual waste generation. When combined, these tools reinforced one another, leading to higher and more stable segregation rates than observed under single-policy scenarios. These behavioral shifts increased upstream segregated flows, improving treatment utilization and reducing landfill dependence. Importantly, these outcomes were not imposed ex ante but emerged endogenously from repeated household decision cycles interacting with system-level capacity constraints. The results demonstrate that behavioral levers influence waste outcomes most effectively when embedded within feedback-driven SD rather than applied in isolation.

Equity implications of policy scenarios

The equity index reveals that stronger policy interventions, particularly PAYT and hybrid strategies, are associated with modest declines in uniform participation across household zones. This occurs because economically and digitally advantaged households respond more readily to behavioral and financial incentives, while marginalized groups face adaptation barriers. From an SD perspective, these disparities are amplified over time as participation feedback loops reinforce early behavioral advantages. Such equity-efficiency trade-offs are well documented in environmental policy and reflect structural heterogeneity rather than model bias. Rather than optimizing for equity alone, the metric is used diagnostically to highlight where complementary support measures are required. These findings inform inclusive policy design by identifying the need for targeted interventions alongside system-wide efficiency gains.

System-level interpretation

Circular transitions are not binary shifts, but rather incremental reconfigurations of urban metabolism. The results highlight the need to balance short-term cost and emissions increases against long-term gains in circularity and diversion. The hybrid intervention resulted in nonlinear gains in circularity (+48%) and diversion (+7.6 pp), whereas cost and emissions increased moderately (+24.1% and 15.7%, respectively). scenario D (hybrid intervention) provided the highest level of circularity, while scenario E (co-located hubs) balanced performance with moderate cost and emissions. The results show that multi-lever solutions outperform single-policy approaches. When compared to national benchmarks, the synthetic intervention scenario brings the city closer to the CPCB’s reported 54% treatment rate (2022),40 implying scalability. The results also highlight equity considerations, informing inclusive waste system design aligned with SDG 11, SDG 12, and SDG 13.

Discussion

The contribution of this study lies in demonstrating how behavioral, spatial, and systemic dimensions of waste management interact when analyzed within a unified decision-support framework. The integrated hybrid modeling technique described here emphasizes the multifaceted nature of urban waste transitions. This work shows how agent-based, SD, and graph-theoretic methodologies can improve understanding and aid decision-making in complicated urban environments by combining validated national statistics with simulated city-level scenarios and policy interventions. Key findings indicate that multi-lever methods, which combine behavioral, infrastructural, and economic interventions, outperform single-policy approaches in terms of waste diversion, circularity and equity. The hybrid intervention consistently emerged as the strongest performer in terms of circularity, while co-located infrastructure strategies offered a more balanced trade-off between performance, cost, and emissions. The observed nonlinear benefits point to positive system feedback: minor focused interventions, such as informational nudges or incentive programs, can yield significant improvements when paired with enhanced waste collection and processing capacity.

The explicit modeling of household decision-making recognizes the significant behavioral variation found in metropolitan environments. This human-centric perspective is consistent with current requests to incorporate socio-technical modeling into urban sustainability research, ensuring that policy design considers the different motives and barriers that city people encounter.41,42 The graph-theoretic analysis of stakeholder and infrastructure networks strengthens the case for co-designing policies that address system bottlenecks while leveraging critical nodes for maximum impact.43 For example, hub co-location and decentralized facility placement have been shown to reduce transportation distances, optimize flows, and promote participation, mirroring successful practices in cities that have prioritized dispersed composting and local recycling projects. The findings also indicate that behavioral interventions such as nudges and PAYT are effective entry points in low-compliance settings, but infrastructural methods such as hub co-location have a stronger impact in more mature systems.

While the synthetic city serves as a controlled testbed, the framework’s modular design enables adaptation to real-world cities with different waste compositions, regulatory frameworks, and infrastructural maturity issues that have been well documented in practice and study. The necessity of context-specific solutions cannot be overstated: policy rollouts must consider aspects such as digital access, education, and local regulatory contexts to ensure optimal efficacy. Importantly, the connection of intervention outcomes with SDGs particularly SDG 11, SDG 12, and SDG 13 shows how local changes can assist larger global objectives. The concept and outcomes present urban waste management as not simply a technical challenge, but also an area of participatory governance and environmental justice. This study emphasizes the importance of hybrid, human-centered models as scientific and policy instruments for facilitating the co-design of resilient, inclusive, and sustainable urban waste systems. These results highlight the potential for scaling such hybrid approaches to real-world urban systems, aligning local waste transitions with broader sustainability and climate objectives.

Policy implications

The policy implications derived from this study should be interpreted as exploratory rather than prescriptive. Given the use of a synthetic urban archetype, the results are not intended to forecast outcomes for specific cities but to illustrate how different policy levers interact within an integrated waste management system. The framework provides simulation-based insights to support municipal decision-making within circular economy contexts, highlighting the importance of coordinated and adaptive strategies.

A key finding is the effectiveness of integrated hybrid strategies, where economic instruments such as PAYT, behavioral interventions, and infrastructural improvements are implemented in combination rather than isolation. Such bundled approaches enhance segregation efficiency, strengthen circular resource flows, and improve overall system resilience. Within this context, digital access emerges as a critical behavioral enabler, as app-based nudges and digital literacy initiatives can significantly improve household participation and enable real-time feedback for adaptive management.

From a spatial and infrastructural perspective, bin placement and accessibility play a crucial role in influencing segregation behavior. Increasing bin density, particularly in high-density urban areas, can improve service equity and recycling outcomes. Similarly, the development of decentralized and co-located treatment hubs can reduce transportation costs and emissions, supporting low-carbon urban transitions and efficient waste logistics.

The study also highlights important equity and governance considerations. While PAYT schemes are effective in incentivizing waste reduction, they must be carefully designed with transparent billing mechanisms and adequate infrastructure to avoid disproportionately impacting low-income households. In parallel, extended producer responsibility (EPR) mechanisms can reduce the financial burden on municipalities while encouraging sustainable production practices at the source.

In addition to policy and infrastructure interventions, analytical tools play a complementary role in decision-making. Simulation-based modeling serves as a strategic planning tool, enabling municipalities to evaluate policy trade-offs before implementation. Sensitivity analysis further indicates that digital access and bin placement are high-impact levers, suggesting that prioritizing these factors can yield significant improvements under resource constraints. Overall, the findings support a transition toward data-driven, equitable, and resilient waste management systems aligned with global sustainability objectives.

Future work

Building on the insights and limitations, future research should focus on improving the hybrid modeling framework’s empirical foundation, scalability, and inclusivity. Integrating more datasets from a variety of urban contexts-including informal settlements, peri-urban zones, and tier-2 cities-will improve model calibration and generalizability. Validating the framework using real-world case studies and participatory field trials can boost its operational relevance and policy adoption. Advancements in computing efficiency and modular framework design will be critical for scaling the model to broader regions and enabling real-time scenario testing. Incorporating tighter spatial and temporal granularity, such as ward-level flows and hourly decision cycles, could reveal localized dynamics and the timing of interventions. Expanding stakeholder involvement to include informal sector workers, private contractors, and community organizations would support the design of more equitable and context-sensitive policies. Future research should investigate adaptive policy sequencing, including how behavioral, infrastructural, and economic levers perform under different rollout schedules and governance contexts. Integrating hybrid modeling with new technologies like IoT, machine learning, and smart monitoring systems could improve predictive capacity and operational efficiency in urban waste management. This will enable cities to prioritize actions based on readiness, resources, and community engagement, ultimately promoting circularity and resilience in urban waste systems.

Overall, this study presents a hybrid modeling framework integrating agent-based behavior, SDs, and graph-theoretic analysis to examine urban waste transitions under diverse policy and infrastructure scenarios. The framework captures behavioral heterogeneity, system-level feedback, and spatial infrastructure interactions within a unified decision-support environment. Results from the synthetic urban archetype demonstrate that integrated behavioral, economic, and infrastructural interventions can significantly improve circularity, diversion, and equity outcomes. By linking local intervention strategies with broader sustainability objectives, the framework provides a scalable and flexible foundation for supporting resilient, inclusive, and climate-responsive urban waste management systems aligned with SDG 11, SDG 12, and SDG 13.

Limitations of the study

The model does not incorporate behavioral learning, habit formation, or social diffusion mechanisms. Household responses are represented as static probabilities conditioned on policy exposure, and therefore, the framework should be interpreted as an exploratory decision-support tool rather than a predictive behavioral model. Additionally, the use of a synthetic city limits the direct transferability of numerical results to real-world policy contexts. Urban waste systems vary widely in governance capacity, informal sector integration, and sociocultural practices; therefore, the findings are best interpreted as comparative insights into intervention dynamics rather than as city-specific policy prescriptions.

Despite the strengths of the hybrid modeling approach, several constraints must be considered. The accuracy of simulations is highly reliant on the availability and reliability of national and local waste management data, which may contain inconsistencies or gaps due to varying reporting standards across metropolitan local bodies. While the synthetic city provides a controlled environment for testing intervention dynamics, it does not fully capture the institutional and governance complexities of specific urban contexts. Behavioral probabilities and network features were derived from literature and national benchmarks, which may overlook localized socioeconomic shifts and evolving policy responses. The integration of agent-based, SD, and graph-theoretic components increases computational complexity, making it challenging to scale the framework to larger cities or regional applications without simplification or enhanced computational resources.

Furthermore, the model’s daily time-step and aggregated city-level resolution constrain its capacity to capture fine-grained spatial and temporal dynamics, such as neighborhood-level behaviors or hourly intervention impacts. Stakeholder participation is predominantly geared toward households and municipal infrastructure, with informal sector workers, private operators, and community actors under-represented despite their critical roles. Finally, while simulated treatments such as informational nudges, PAYT schemes, and hub co-location show theoretical efficacy, their real-world implementation may encounter sociopolitical obstacles, funding constraints, and enforcement challenges. Addressing these limitations through expanded empirical datasets, diverse case validations, deeper stakeholder integration, and methodological refinement will be essential to enhance the framework’s applicability, scalability, and effectiveness.

Method details

This study proposes a hybrid modeling framework integrating agent-based modeling (ABM), system dynamics (SD), and graph-theoretic modeling to simulate urban waste management systems under diverse intervention scenarios. The framework is designed to capture interactions across behavioral, infrastructural, and policy dimensions within a unified simulation environment. A synthetic urban system is constructed using national performance benchmarks to represent waste generation, collection, and processing dynamics. The system is initialized with parameters representing household behavior, infrastructure capacity, and policy conditions.

The ABM component models individual household agents, where each agent’s decision-making process is influenced by behavioral attributes such as compliance, awareness, and responsiveness to incentives. These agent-level interactions generate emergent patterns in waste segregation and disposal. The SD component captures aggregate system behavior using stock-flow structures, where waste accumulates in different system states such as collected, processed, and diverted waste streams. Feedback loops represent interactions between policy interventions and system performance, enabling dynamic adjustment over simulation time. The graph-theoretic modeling component represents the spatial structure of waste collection and redistribution networks. Nodes correspond to collection or processing points, and edges represent transportation links. Network connectivity and flow patterns are used to identify structural bottlenecks and optimize waste routing pathways.

The integration of ABM, SD, and graph-theoretic modeling enables simultaneous modeling of micro-level behavior, macro-level system dynamics, and spatial network interactions. The model operates iteratively, updating agent states, system flows, and network structures across simulation time steps to evaluate intervention scenarios.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Simulation experiments are conducted under multiple intervention scenarios, including baseline conditions, behavioral interventions, infrastructural enhancements, and integrated policy strategies. Model performance is evaluated using key metrics including waste diversion, circularity, operational cost, emissions, and equity. These metrics are computed at each simulation step and aggregated for comparative analysis. Relative improvements are assessed by comparing intervention scenarios against baseline conditions. The analysis focuses on identifying nonlinear system responses, trade-offs between performance metrics, and the overall effectiveness of integrated interventions. Comparative scenario analysis is used to evaluate system-level outcomes and identify dominant factors influencing performance improvements across different policy configurations.
