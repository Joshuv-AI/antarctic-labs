Lithological identification for mixed carbonate and clay sediments employing principal component analysis of geophysical logging
Authors: Jingzhe Guo, Liang Wang, Ya Li, Yuxia Wang, Lifa Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study proposes a novel method for lithological identification to address the challenge of accurate classification in complex sedimentary successions. Leveraging principal component analysis, the methodology was developed using Ordovician data from the Western Ordos Basin, where the lithological assemblage predominantly comprises of marine carbonate rocks, claystones, and their associated transitional rocks. Five well-log curves highly sensitive to lithology were selected as original variables, from which two principal components (PCs) were extracted to capture key compositional discrepancies. The first PC effectively differentiates clay and carbonate minerals, thereby enabling identification of transitional rocks between carbonates and claystones. The second PC reflects variations in calcite and dolomite contents, allowing discrimination of transitional lithologies between limestone and dolostone. Following the geological interpretation of these two principal components, a lithological cross-plot was constructed, which reveals distinct clustering among various rock types and supports a well-defined classification framework for transitional lithologies. The reliability and accuracy of the proposed method were validated against X-ray fluorescence element logging data from an active borehole. By prioritizing geological interpretability through feature transformation rather than treating the classification as a “black box”, this study establishes a transparent, observable, and highly practical framework for lithological identification.Keywords: Principal component analysis, Lithological identification, Mixed carbonate and clay sediment, Geophysical loggingSubject terms: Solid Earth sciences, Geology, Geophysics, Petrology

Introduction

Driven by the sustained global demand for energy resources, hydrocarbon exploration and development have progressively expanded into geologically and tectonically complex settings1–5. In recent years, significant natural gas discoveries have been proved within the Paleozoic strata of the Western Ordos Basin (WOB), emerging as a pivotal contributor to regional production output. In particular, the Ordovician successions in this region host substantial natural gas accumulations within marine carbonate rocks and claystones, highlighting an immense potential for continued resource appraisal and strategic development6–8.

Lithofacies identification plays a fundamental role in the exploration and development of geological resources. However, the mineral composition and texturing of subsurface rocks, especially complex carbonate successions, vary significantly due to factors such as sedimentary environment, tectonic evolution, and diagenesis, thereby increasing the complexity of lithofacies characterization9–11. In hydrocarbon exploration and development, lithological information is primarily derived from borehole data, with standard workflows relying on cuttings description, coring, and geophysical logging12–14. Cuttings description is highly dependent on operational conditions and site specifications, which can compromise interpretation accuracy15,16. Coring enables direct visual observation and quantitative mineralogical verification, yet its high cost involved and time-consuming nature make continuous or routine coring across multiple boreholes impractical12,17. To overcome these sampling limits, conventional geophysical logging provides continuous, high-vertical-resolution datasets along the wellbore to capture petrophysical responses reflecting lithology9,14,18–20. In modern operational workflows, logging while drilling is now widely deployed to deliver near-real-time petrophysical data, enabling more prompt and direct decision-making during drilling operations21–23. Beyond these standard tools, advanced signal-processing techniques—such as frequency decomposition via wavelet transform and empirical mode decomposition—have been increasingly applied to extract continuous multi-scale lithological features and stratigraphic cycles from log responses24–28. Concurrently, surface and core-based hyperspectral analysis has emerged as a robust, non-destructive tool for automated mineral mapping29–32.

Against the current backdrop of energy price volatility and the industry-wide push for cost reduction and efficiency, without relying on specialized, high-cost instrumentation, sophisticated processing software, or dedicated expertise, optimizing the use of existing conventional well-logging data to achieve reliable lithofacies interpretation provides a practical and viable pathway.

Conventional methodologies for well-log-based lithological identification primarily rely on curve overlays, feature parameters, and cross-plots16,19,33. However, with the accelerating shift toward unconventional hydrocarbon resources, reservoir lithologies have become increasingly heterogeneous and complex, confounding traditional classification frameworks. To surmount these challenges, advanced machine learning (ML) techniques have been extensively deployed. A variety of ML algorithms—including decision tree, artificial neural network, support vector machine, fuzzy inference systems, and random forest—have been implemented to map non-linear logging responses to specific rock types, enabling effective lithological identification16,34–36. Despite their efficacy, geological investigations frequently encounter few-shot classification scenarios. Under such small-sample constraints, conventional data-driven models are highly susceptible to overfitting and poor generalization, rendering their predictions unreliable. To mitigate these limitations, effective data preprocessing is critical for enhancing classification performance by reducing dimensionality and eliminating feature redundancy. Constructing cross-plots from these condensed, low-dimensional features offers an elegant solution. It allows for the direct visualization of mathematical decision boundaries within a concrete geological context, effectively transforming a “black-box” process into a highly interpretable and robust lithological identification framework.

To facilitate strategic feature engineering and data preprocessing, principal component analysis (PCA) is widely employed as a robust mathematical and statistical technique dedicated to mitigating the dimensionality of multidimensional datasets37–39. It plays a pivotal role in simplifying intricate data structures while preserving maximum underlying information. Through this approach, a set of correlated multidimensional variables is orthogonally transformed into a new set of linearly independent variables, designated as principal components (PCs)40–43. These PCs are linear combinations of the total variables, thereby safeguarding the integrity of the primary data43–45. Such transformations facilitates a more effective representation of the data, enabling visualization, analysis, and petrophysical interpretation. In recent years, researchers in hydrocarbon exploration and development have increasingly deployed PCA as a powerful asset for data-driven analysis. For instance, Ma integrated PCA with neural network to develop an advanced workflow for lithofacies clustering based on geophysical logging data46. Alaei applied PCA to evaluate reservoir parameters using conventional log suites47. Yang and Cheng leveraged PCA for lithological identification, benchmarking its performance against independent component analysis48. Similarly, Jahan et al. utilized PCA to fuse multiple seismic attributes, enabling effective fault detection49. Yang and Schulz employed PCA biplots to analyze the controlling factors influencing hydrocarbon generation potential50. Furthermore, Kang et al. utilized PCA to compress the dimensionality of input variables during the construction of a 3D geological reservoir models51, while Michael and Craigie processed geochemical data via PCA to facilitate the characterization and comparison of carbonate reservoirs52.

In this paper, PCA was deployed to characterize the lithologies of the Lower Paleozoic Ordovician successions in the WOB. The systematic workflow was executed through four progressive stages: First, the lithological types of core samples were quantitatively determined via X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. Second, f a suite of five standard well-log curves—comprising acoustic transit time (AC), compensated neutron logging (CNL), bulk density (DEN), gamma ray (GR), and true resistivity (RT)—was compiled to extract the PCs as linear combinations of these foundational petrophysical variables. Third, the geological significance of the extracted PCs was decoded by integrating their mathematical expressions with the diagnostic petrophysical response characteristics of various minerals. Finally, calibrated against the XRD-verified lithofacies, a precise lithological identification framework utilizing PCA on geophysical logging data was established. Here, unsupervised data preprocessing techniques were leveraged to condense the multidimensional log suites into intuitive, two-dimensional representations, thereby facilitating a streamlined and transparent approach to lithological classification via PCA cross-plots. This methodology explicitly enhances the transparency, interpretability, and simplicity of the classification pipeline. By implementing the proposed workflow during the early exploration phase of the WOB Ordovician reservoirs, a more comprehensive understanding of subsurface lithofacies variations can be achieved, thereby providing robust support for subsequent downstream reservoir characterization and development planning. Furthermore, within the broader domain of geophysical exploration, the conceptual framework and procedural workflow presented herein can be scaled to enhance the correlation and calibration between high-resolution but spatially discrete well-log data and low-resolution but spatially continuous seismic volumes, ultimately maximizing the practical utility and application value of integrated subsurface characterization53–56.

Methods

Lithological classification based on mineral composition

In this study, the sedimentary rock types of the retrieved core samples were preliminarily categorized into claystone, limestone, and dolostone through macro-core observations. Subsequently, XRD quantitative analyses were performed to determine their mineral compositions. Finally, the samples were classified into specific lithologies based on their quantitative mineralogical compositions.

Sedimentary rocks consist of diverse mineral constituents, whose types and relative abundances can be quantified using XRD. Because each mineral possesses a unique diffraction pattern and its concentration positively correlates with peak intensity, characteristic diffraction peaks are leveraged to quantify individual mineral contents57–60. The XRD analytical procedure is executed as follows: First, rock samples are cleaned, dried, crushed and ground into fine powder. Second, the generated XRD datasets are cross-referenced with standard mineralogical for phase identification databases. Third, the content of each identified mineral is calculated based on the intensities of its diffraction peaks.

Based on the quantitative mineral abundances derived from XRD analysis, rocks are classified and named according to their essential mineral constituents. Additionally, incorporating secondary minerals as modifiers within the rock nomenclature enhances the precision of lithological descriptions61. Within this classification scheme, an essential mineral is defined as constituting more than 50% of the rock composition, whereas a secondary mineral designates the most abundant phase following the essential mineral. If a secondary mineral accounts for less than 5%, it is excluded from the nomenclature. For concentrations ranging between 25 and 50%, the adjective form of the secondary mineral is prefixed as a modifier (e.g., dolomitic limestone). Conversely, when the secondary mineral content falls between 5 and 25%, a compound modifier consisting of the mineral name and the suffix “-bearing” is applied (e.g., dolomite-bearing limestone).

PCA

Results

Lithologic category

A total of 61 core samples were retrieved from Ordovician boreholes in the WOB. The lithological assemblages are predominantly composed of marine carbonate rocks and claystones, which constitute the diagnostic features of the Ordovician strata in this region. Driven by multi-factorial influences such as sedimentary environment and subsequent diagenesis, fluctuating concentrations of calcite, dolomite and clay minerals have given rise to various transitional lithofacies.

Based on macro-core observations, the samples can be qualitatively grouped into three lithological categories: limestone, dolostone, and claystone. Claystone, primarily dominated by clay minerals, typically exhibits a massive sedimentary structure, with occasional graptolite fossils observed (Fig. 1a and b). Both limestone and dolostone represent the major carbonate lithologies, wherein calcite and dolomite serve as the respective dominant mineral phases, frequently intermixed with terrigenous components such as clay minerals, quartz, and feldspar. Additionally, trace constituents including minor pyrite or gypsum may occur locally. Structurally, both limestone and dolostone display stratified, massive, or brecciated fabrics (Fig. 1c and d). The most distinctive diagnostic criterion distinguishing these two lithologies is their geochemical response to dilute hydrochloric acid: limestone reacts vigorously with profuse effervescence, whereas dolostone exhibits little to no reaction under ambient conditions.

Through microscopic thin-section analysis, the microtextural and mineralogical characteristics of these three lithological categories can be further observed (Fig. 2). Claystone exhibits a dense, fine-grained argillaceous matrix with micro-stratification (Fig. 2a), locally accompanied by occasional patches of siliceous and calcite cementation. In low-energy deep-water environments, limestone is formed by a silt-sized crystalline calcite matrix (Fig. 2b), which becomes intermixed with dispersed clay minerals during periods of enhanced terrigenous clastic supply. Alternatively, high-energy depositional events displays distinctive brecciated fabrics (Fig. 2c), featuring sand-textured intraclasts embedded within a dark micritic matrix. Within evaporative settings, dolostone is characterized by a tightly interlocking crystalline architecture, presenting a classic crystalline mosaic fabric composed predominantly of micritic to very fine-grained dolomite rhombs formed via penecontemporaneous dolomitization (Fig. 2d), with minor argillaceous laminations locally preserved.

Among the 61 sedimentary rock samples, 26 are classified as limestone, 19 as dolostone, and 16 as claystone. Their quantitative mineralogical compositions were determined via XRD to quantitatively distinguish transitional rock types (Table 1). Within the limestone cohort, the primary matrix is dominated by an average calcite content of 71.28%, with dolomite and clay minerals accounting for 12.16% and 12.83%, respectively. The dolostone samples exhibit a mean composition of 74.28% dolomite, 12.19% calcite, and 10.56% clay. In contrast, the claystone end-member contains an average of 65.75% clay and 18.85% calcite. Guided by these fluctuating mineral fractions, the transitional rock types are quantitatively differentiated into distinct lithofacies schemes (Table 1). Specifically, the limestone suite is partitioned into five subtypes: pure limestone, dolomite-bearing limestone, dolomitic limestone, clay-bearing limestone, and clayey limestone. In an analogous fashion, the dolostone group is subdivided into pure dolostone, calcite-bearing dolostone, calcitic dolostone, clay-bearing dolostone, and clayey dolostone. Lastly, the claystone entries are categorized into three types: pure claystone, calcite-bearing claystone, and calcitic claystone.

Geological characterization and discussion

Numerical characteristics of distinct lithologies

For each lithological category, the mean values of both the standardized log data and the extracted PC scores were calculated (Table 5). According to the Z-score normalization protocol (Eq. 12), original petrophysical values exceeding the dataset mean are transformed into positive standardized values, whereas those below the mean yield negative values. Mechanistically, claystone are characterized by positive ZAC, ZCNL, and ZGR values, coupled with negative ZDEN and ZRT responses. Conversely, carbonate rocks exhibit the opposing petrophysical signature, marked by suppressed ZAC, ZCNL, and ZGR values but elevated ZDEN and ZRT metrics. Furthermore, within the standardized carbonate suite, the most definitive diagnostic criterion distinguishing limestones and dolostone is registered in ZRT, followed secondarily by ZDEN. In terms of dimensionality reduction spaces, PC1 scores are higher for claystones and lower for carbonates. Meanwhile, PC2 differentiates the carbonate end-members, yielding high scores for limestone but relatively depressed values for dolostone.

Geological implications of the extracted principal components

To fully appreciate the efficacy of PCA in subsurface characterization, it is of paramount importance to decipher the geological meanings embedded within the mathematical PCs. In this study, initial five-dimensional well-logging suite was effectively condensed into two orthogonal components (PC1 and PC2). Consequently, elucidating the geological control mechanisms of these two dominant PCs is essential to bridge the gap between abstract mathematical dimensionality reduction and tangible petrophysical realities.

For PC1, as formulated in Eq. 16, the weight coefficients assigned to each standardized well-logging curve are 0.538, 0.503, − 0.444, 0.456, and − 0.227, respectively. Except for ZRT, the absolute magnitudes of these coefficients are remarkably comparable, demonstrating that PC1 predominantly integrates the petrophysical signals from ZAC, ZCNL, ZDEN, and ZGR, while ZRT plays a subordinate role. Statistically, when a lithofacies exhibits elevated ZAC, ZCNL, and ZGR values coupled with depressed ZDEN and ZRT responses, PC1 yields a high positive score. Conversely, an inverted logging pattern with low ZAC, ZCNL, and ZGR but high ZDEN and ZRT metrics results in a low or negative PC1 score. Therefore, PC1 serves as a numerical indicator that captures the fundamentally opposing petrophysical properties between claystone and carbonate matrices.

The distinction between claystones and carbonate rocks can be established via PC1, leveraging the contrasting response characteristics inherent to different sedimentary rock types (Table 5). Structurally, PC1 exhibits prominently elevated scores in claystones but subdued baseline scores in carbonate rocks. Crucially, evaluation of the data distribution reveals a complete absence of overlap between these two macro-lithological categories in the PC1 domain (Fig. 3a). Specifically, the calculated PC1 scores range from 1.5 to 4.5 for claystones, whereas they span − 2.5 to 0.5 for carbonate rocks. In contrast, individual conventional log parameters fall short of achieving reliable lithological separation. For instance, in the case of GR log, the data distributions suffer from cross-over and overlap within the range of 64–88API (Fig. 3b).

Limestones are biased toward higher PC2 values, whereas dolostones remain constrained within lower intervals. Lithological identification by PCs

Building upon the geological implications of PC1 and PC2, a two-stage hierarchical identification scheme can be deployed for reservoir lithofacies classification. In this predictive framework, PC1 functions as the primary partitioner, effectively segregating claystones and carbonate rocks. Concurrently, PC2 operates as an intra-carbonate classifier, resolving the mineralogical boundaries between limestones and dolostones.

To visualize the classification efficiency, a standardized cross-plot was constructed by mapping PC1 on the x-axis against PC2 on the y-axis (Fig. 7a). Guided by this dual-axis configuration, different lithofacies categories are segregated into well-defined, distinct clusters. The claystone data points aggregate seamlessly along the right side of the plot, occupying the high-value domain of PC1. On the flip side of this lithological partition, carbonate variants populate the low-value PC1 domain on the left. Within this carbonate zone, the limestones and dolostone end-members undergo a clean, non-overlapping divergence along the vertical axis. Limestones cluster within the upper-left region of the plot, a space defined by low PC1 values alongside elevated PC2 responses. Method applicability and limitations

The lithofacies identification workflow developed in this study effectively differentiates mixed carbonate and siliciclastic matrices resulting from continuous variations in calcite, dolomite, and clay mineral content. This approach is broadly applicable for characterising complex lithofacies assemblages across diverse sedimentary environments under varying hydrodynamic regimes, spanning from shallow-water high-energy to deep-water low-energy settings. Specifically, it can be reliably deployed in: carbonate platform facies dominated by mixtures of carbonate grains with minor clay matrix; platform foreslope facies containing sediment-gravity flows, and interbedded claystones; and basinal plain facies composed of alternating thin beds of pelagic claystones and micritic carbonates.

However, certain boundaries must be delineated. This predictive method is not intended for transitional marine-continental settings (such as deltaic and lagoonal environments) where sandstones and coal seams predominate, nor for purely continental settings (such as fluvial and lacustrine environments) that lack diagnostic carbonate accumulations. Furthermore, a key inherent limitation of this study—a common bottleneck in subsurface geological research—is the relatively constrained core sample dataset. As the volume of training data progressively expands through ongoing drilling, advanced supervised ML algorithms, such as K-Nearest Neighbors, can be seamlessly integrated into this architecture to further elevate the interpretation speed and predictive fidelity of subsequent lithofacies characterization.

Conclusion

The Ordovician strata within the WOB are characterized by a mixed lithofacies assemblage composed of marine carbonate rocks and claystones. Conventional well-logging responses show that the claystones exhibit geophysical signatures with high AC, CNL, and GR, paired with low DEN and RT, whereas the carbonate rocks display the opposite response patterns. XRD analysis confirms that variations in mineral compositions and abundance drive the development of transitional lithofacies in the study area. Through PCA, the eigenvalues of PC1 and PC2 are 3.200 and 0.958, accounting for 64.00% and 19.16% of VCR, respectively, with a total CVCR of 83.16%. PC1 captures the contrasting characteristics of ZAC, ZCNL, and ZGR with respect to ZDEN and ZRT, making it reliable for separating claystones from carbonate rocks. Meanwhile, PC2 integrates information from RT and DEN to differentiate limestones from dolostones. Within the integrated PC1–PC2 discriminatory framework, a clear zonation of transitional lithofacies is observed. For the validation borehole, the lithological profiles derived from XRF logging and the PCA-based method exhibit a high degree of correspondence, demonstrating the reliability of the approach. In the absence of specialized procedures such as coring, or geochemical logging, subsurface lithofacies successions can be identified using the PCA-based method, which relies on continuous conventional logs to capture mineralogical fluctuations within heterogeneous intervals.
