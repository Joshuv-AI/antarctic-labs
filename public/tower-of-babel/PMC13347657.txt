Mechanically Stable Metal/Silica Antireflective and Antidust Coatings with Controlled Optical Properties to Mitigate Dust Adhesion in Desert Conditions
Authors: Mohammad Istiaque Hossain, Alaa Elsafi, Puvaneswaran Chelvanathan, Brahim Aissa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The extreme conditions of deserts such as intense solar irradiation and elevated dust concentration in the air pose significant operational issues to photovoltaic (PV) modules, optical components, and exposed functional surfaces. This research paper has presented metal-integrated silica thin-film coating materials that can be used to address dust adhesion, optical reflection loss, and environmental degradation at the same time. In this study, we investigate the metal integrated silica coatings with controlled optical and morphological properties using RF magnetron sputtering. 100 nm-SiO2, 10 nm-Ti/100 nm-SiO2, and 10 nm-ITO/100 nm-SiO2 were developed and examined systematically to understand the dust adhesion mechanism for two months. The Ti/SiO2 optimized coatings showed lower dust adhesion, and this is explained by the combined effect of the morphology, wettability, and surface energy. The potential dissipation of the electrostatic charge is expected as one of the factors. In addition, surface morphology strongly depends on the types of materials deposited on silica layers as found by average roughness (R a). Contact angle measurements confirm that the hydrophilicity of such engineered coatings can be tuned through metal content in the films. Micromechanical properties of the coatings were thoroughly studied immediately after sputtering deposition and after 2 months of outdoor exposure using Vickers microindentation analysis. Among all, as-deposited Ti/SiO2 coatings show excellent mechanical durability after a period of 2 months. As studied computationally, the power conversion efficiency (PCE) is predicted to reach almost 27.28% in the case of 10 nm Ti/100 nm silica and decreases to less than 8% at 20 μm. Ti/silica layers of the device play a crucial role in device performance. Our investigation also found that the adhesion of the dust to the films reduces as the metal films are integrated into the silica layers, which confirms the intrinsic self-cleaning nature of the coatings. Our works also investigated that dust adhesion on the surface of these metal-integrated silica coatings is inversely proportional to the metal contents in the films, confirming the coatings’ self-cleaning properties. The hydrophilic characteristics of the sputtered thin films can limit the adhesion of particles to the surface, which is crucial to enhance the performance of photovoltaic systems in desert areas. Such a solution can enhance both optical and mechanical characteristics as well as the performance of PVs in harsh conditions.

Introduction

For the last decades, scientific and industrial communities have invested a lot in the development of multipurpose surface coatings that can be used to reduce optical reflection as well as minimize dust accumulation. − Such multifunctional coatings are widely used in various industries, including photovoltaic energy systems, laser optics, optical components employed in precision applications, solar thermal collectors, and architectural glass. Antisoiling capability is, in the case of photovoltaic technologies, a vital requirement in the determination of the output of energy in the devices under harsh conditions. − The dust particles, electrostatically charged particulates, deposit on the exposed surfaces in desert and arid climatic regions, leading to extreme optical losses and deterioration of the electrical performance. − As studied, dust incidences can reduce the output of photovoltaics by more than one-fifth, and it is highly recommended to develop antidust coatings. New coating technologies are, therefore, attempting to implement the antireflection and self-cleaning and dust-repellent properties in a single thin-film structure. Self-cleaning antireflective coatings have already shown great potential in improving the performance of photovoltaic modules by taking advantage of the optimum light tuning and minimum adhesion of particles. The design of efficient antireflective coating relies on the fact that it is possible to regulate the optical interference of many interfaces among the reflected light waves.

Ideally, destructive interference is produced when the thickness of the coating is a quarter of the incident wavelength multiplied by the refractive index of the coating substance. − Besides this, to have an optimal match refractive index, the refractive index of the coating should be of the order of the geometric mean of the substrate’s refractive index and that of the surrounding medium. This would be a perfect coating with a refractive index of 1.22 and coating thickness of 120 nm in the case of normal glass substrates with a refractive index in the order of 1.5. However, such low values of the refractive index are not common in dense natural materials, to which magnesium fluoride and silica are typically added as optical materials with a refractive index of approximately 1.39 and 1.46, respectively. Nanostructuring and controlled porosity result in refractive indices. , Silica nanoparticle-based silica coating, as an example, by means of sol–gel reaction, has been capable of attaining transmission values exceeding 96% as such surfaces can as well be made superhydrophilic due to the increased surface roughness. −

On dry surfaces, the airborne dust particles are normally electrically charged with the energy generated during triboelectric interactions between the moving sand and the different surfaces. − Antistatic versions of dust-mitigation coating have, as a result, received increasing popularity over regular antisoiling coating. − The working mechanism of antistatic coating is to reduce the amount of charge deposited on the surface and dissipate the electrostatic charges through the control mechanism of surface conductivity or redistribution of charges. In Qatar, the dominant nature of fine particles is with an average diameter of about 2 μm and a small percentage of nonuniformly shaped coarse particles with a size up to several tens of micrometers. The domination of fine particles in this aspect is high because the extent of dust adhesion, optical scattering, and electrostatic charging response depends greatly on the size of the particles. Previous studies have found that micrometer and submicrometer dust are more likely to be subjected to triboelectric charge by frictional forces of dust particles and air molecules as well as particle-to-particle interactions during transport in arid environments. − Fine particles (particularly the ones below 5 μm) obtain the negative surface charges much easier because such particles have a high surface-area-volume ratio and because they are more effective at capturing the electrons at surface defect sites. Moreover, mineral constituents that are usually common in desert dust such as silica-rich and aluminosilicate phases can also contribute to the ability to capture electrons in a dry atmosphere and, therefore, to the net negative charge of the airborne particulates. The fact that the climate conditions such as those of a desert such as in the case of Qatar make the humidity low and the wind carries the particles means that it only enhances the production of triboelectric charge. Thereafter, the negatively charged fine dust particles can get firmly attached to the photovoltaic glass surfaces through electrostatic attractions even in the absence of any moisture-enhanced adhesion processes. −

Although metal-integrated silica coating is usually associated with electrostatic losses associated with the dissipation of the charge because of its conductivity, it is also necessary to mention that no direct electrical measurements (e.g., surface resistivity or charge decay measurements) were performed in this work. Consequently, the decrease in dust adhesion of the Ti/SiO2 coatings is construed as composite surface-related effects such as morphology, surface energy changes, and enhanced wettability. The contribution of the electrostatic effects is thus viewed as a potential contributing mechanism, and an additional effort on electrical characterization will be needed in the future to confirm this factor.

In this work, RF magnetron sputtering was employed to deposit silica thin films to study the optical properties, surface wettability, and microstructure of silica films of about 100 nm thickness. The first objective was to ensure the deposition of porous silicon oxide films, which can be used in dual-function antireflective, antisoiling, and perhaps antistatic photovoltaic coating studies. Later, Ti and indium tin oxide (ITO) layers were developed and installed in the outdoor testing facility. The findings provide critical information on how to develop versatile silica coatings that can be incorporated to increase optical transmission, reduce dust adhesion, and facilitate mass production of photovoltaic modules (see Figure ).

Methodology

Indium tin oxide (ITO), silica (SiO2), and titanium (Ti) sputter targets were used in this work, and the diameter of the target was 200 mm, and the thickness of the sputter target was 6 mm. The deposition was carried out by an Oxford Instruments PlasmaPro 400D radiofrequency (RF) magnetron sputtering system. Thin films were deposited on 6-in., FTO glass. A turbomolecular pumping system was used to evacuate the chamber to a base pressure of less than 3 × 10 –7 Torr. Once the desired vacuum was achieved, ultrahigh-purity argon gas (99.999%) was injected and kept for about 5 min to stabilize the plasma condition and eliminate any contaminant. A 100 nm silicon dioxide (SiO2) was deposited without vacuum breaking before ITO and Ti deposition. The ITO and Ti layers were then deposited at 10 mTorr chamber pressures with the sputtering gas being argon at a fixed 25 sccm flow. The required pressure was automatically maintained by the system by adjusting the throttle valve and keeping the flow of the gas constant. Depositions were done at a total of room temperature, and the substrates were not rotated. The deposited films had an average thickness of about 10 nm. After deposition, the films were analyzed systematically to find out information about their structural, optical, and surface morphological properties. Samples were installed in the outdoor testing facility for 2 months as shown in Figure . Optimized sputtering conditions were obtained, and uniform film coverage was obtained over the entire wafer surface. The spectroscopic measurements of the optical characterization were carried out by UV–vis NIR spectroscopy (PerkinElmer Lambda 1050). The Kruss contact angle measurement system was used to determine hydrophilic and hydrophobic behavior by measuring the surface wettability. Moreover, Vickers indentation was used to measure the micromechanical properties of silica layers, which showed that the hardness was adjustable by changing the parameters of sputtering and growth conditions. Surface morphology was also studied under FESEM (JEOL 7610), and the topography at the nanoscale was observed/measured with atomic force microscopy (AFM, Bruker). The numerical simulations were performed using the one-dimensional Solar Cell Capacitance Simulator (SCAPS-1D). Experimentally measured optical parameters of the fabricated samples, including bandgap, absorption coefficient, carrier mobility, and carrier concentration, were used as inputs, while other parameters such as electron affinity, density of states, dielectric permittivity, and thermal velocity were taken from the literature. SCAPS solves the coupled semiconductor transport equations numerically to evaluate key device performance parameters, namely, open-circuit voltage, short-circuit current, fill factor (FF), and power conversion efficiency (PCE). In addition, internal parameters such as electric field distribution, carrier densities, recombination profiles, and current densities were also extracted.

Results and Discussion

Optical Measurements

Figures and show the spectral responses of the reference glass (RG), Ti/SiO2 bilayer, and ITO/SiO2 multilayer structures prior to exposure to the environment, respectively. All samples with coating are highly optically transparent across the visible spectrum (about 400–800 nm); nevertheless, the differences emerge in relation to each arrangement of coating. The Ti/SiO2 bilayer has the highest transmission in the pristine condition (C), with the highest transmission value near the region of 500–550 nm, near 90–98%. The single-layer coating of SiO2 also shows high transparency, and it can be compared to bare RG, which proves that the coating is relevant as an antireflective material. Conversely, the ITO/SiO2 stack exhibits less transmission, especially in the short wavelength below 450 nm. This may be due to more absorption and scattering caused by the conductive ITO film and potential interference caused by the multilayer setup. All samples show a noticeable decrease in transmission after exposure to the environment (D). The degradation effect is the strongest in the ITO/SiO2 structure, where the transmission is considerably low throughout the visible spectrum. The SiO2-coated specimen exhibits moderate transmission loss, and the Ti/SiO2 bilayer bears moderately higher transmission values than the other coated structures, which means that it has better optical resilience to environmental stress.

Reflectance of the (a) fresh and (b) exposed samples.

The antireflective performance of the multilayer coatings is a result of a combination of refractive index grading of the coatings and the thin-film interference governed by phase matching across interfaces. In general, the SiO2 layer has a refractive index in between that of air (n ≈ 1) and that of glass (n ≈ 1.5) and serves as a medium of matching impedance that minimizes Fresnel reflections at the air–substrate interface. Second, at the optical thickness of the dielectric layer where the condition of λ/4n is met, the phase difference between the reflected waves by the air/SiO2 and SiO2/substrate interfaces approaches 0, leading to destructive interference and the attenuation of reflected power. The Ti/SiO2 bilayer (C) in the as-deposited form has the lowest reflectance in the visible range, specifically between 400 and 600 nm, with the reflectance values lower than the reflectance values below the values of 10. The single-layer coating of SiO2 also lowers the reflectance of bare RG, but not as well as the Ti/SiO2 structure. The ITO/SiO2 structure has the highest increase in reflectance, especially in a short wavelength region where it reaches about 40–50% in some areas. It can be mainly due to the amount of dust accumulation as confirmed by other techniques. RG Bare also exhibits an increase in reflectance, implying that the surface of the substrate is affected directly by the factors in the environment. Though reflectance of Ti/SiO2 and SiO2 coatings increases with exposure, and they have relatively lower values when compared to ITO/SiO2, they exhibit greater optical stability.

Topological Results

The surface of the clean state (Figure a) differs in microstructure with a granular texture with irregular height changes. The average roughness (R a) of 11.9 nm is evidence of an average rough morphology. Morphological changes are seen to be significant after exposure to the environment (Figure b). As confirmed, there is much adhesion and accumulation of dust. The coarseness grows significantly between 11.9 and 23.9 nm. The heterogeneity of surface energy and the electrostatic interactions between the particles suspended in the air and the conductive ITO layer can also lead to a higher degree of dust adherence. The more intense roughening is the reason why the reflectance was greater and the transmission was lower after exposure. As confirmed by the roughness, less dust is accumulated on the surface. Figure shows the height profile of the samples. Morphological Study

The SEM micrographs of dust-contaminated reference glass (RG), SiO2-coated glass, ITO/SiO2, and Ti/SiO2 surfaces are shown in Figure . The bare glass substrate has a rather even distribution of finely dispersed particles on the surface. The particles are mostly less than a few micrometers to submicrometers in size with irregular aggregates at times. The total morphology suggests an average dust retention on untreated glass within the environmental conditions that were tested. The density of the particles attached to the SiO2 surface is slightly greater than that on the reference glass. Fibrous and flake-like contaminants are also visible in addition to fine particulates and form small microaggregates. The clustering phenomena seem to be more intense than bare glass, pointing to stronger interaction between particles and other particles. This can be explained by the fact that its surface is chemically unoccupied silica, which usually has hydroxyl groups (OH) that can form hydrogen bonds and promote capillary action and adhesion at normal humidity levels. Based on this, the SiO2 coating can be seen to promote better particle retention compared to the bare substrate. Conversely, the ITO/SiO2 stack has the worst dust accumulation of all samples. ITO is conductive and may well increase electrostatic attraction of particles in the air, and the intrinsic roughness of the ITO-bearing surface is relatively higher, which means that there are additional mechanical anchoring sites. All of these contribute to high adhesion and aggregation, and dust clusters can cover a considerable surface. This morphology will lead to strong optical scattering and high reflectance, which are in line with the postexposure degradation in transmission. Agglomerates are fewer in number and smaller on the Ti/SiO2-coated surface. The deposition of dust is also observed, although the particles are thinner and less concentrated compared to those on ITO/SiO2. It is also possible that the intrinsic morphology of the Ti/SiO2 structure is smoother, and therefore, mechanical interlocking is not so strong, which minimizes strong adhesion sites. Also, the oxide surface chemistry can stabilize electrostatic- and capillary-based attachment mechanisms. Consequently, particle retention is not as vicious, and the spatial distribution is more homogeneous, as well as the level of clustering. In general, the SEM observations indicate that the soiling behavior is obviously dependent on the surface morphology and composition. The more intrinsically rough and conductive surfaces also facilitate greater particle aggregation and clustering, whereas smoother oxide bilayers have a relatively better resistance to heavy dust deposition. As large agglomerates are effective scattering centers in the visible wavelength regime, the strong clustering of ITO/SiO2 is the reason why it exhibits a higher amount of optical degradation when exposed to the environment, whereas the Ti/SiO2 coating shows better morphological stability and possible enhanced long-term optical functionality in outdoor photovoltaic systems, as shown in Figure . Image analysis in ImageJ (Fiji) was used to determine the dust coverage. The SEM images were contrast-adjusted to facilitate the discernment between the dust particles and the underlying surface. To clearly visualize these areas of dust, the identified areas were divided and marked with a color overlay (red). The percentage dust coverage was calculated by dividing the area of the segmented particle by the total image area. There were several representative areas for each sample, and reported values are averaged to be consistent. It must be mentioned that the color difference (red areas) is an artificial overlay added when working with the images to show the identified dust particles and should not reflect an intrinsic characteristic of the material.

Microscopy images were used to estimate the dust coverage on the coated surfaces by analyzing several representative areas of each sample. Comparing dust particles with underlying surface images, thresholding techniques were employed to enable an approximate determination of surface coverage. An average was arrived at with the values obtained to give a semiquantitative comparison among various coatings. Although this technique is not absolutely precise, it allows a stable assessment of relative dust deposition and helps to confirm the trends of observation as the coverage of Ti/SiO2 coatings was lower in comparison to that of ITO/SiO2.

These findings are confirmed by the images of optical microscopes (Figure ) that reveal that the low-oxygen silica coatings had visually darker and more contaminated areas. The more oxygenic structures do not lose their uniformity and brightness, which means an improved retention of optical clarity upon exposure. These morphological variations emphasize the fact that the real-world performance of a coating is highly dependent on the deposition parameters. Figure shows the energy-dispersive (EDS) images of the clean and dust-contaminated samples of ITO, Ti, reference glass (RG), and SiO2 substrates. The spectra show that the elemental signatures of the substrate/coating materials and the environmental dust that has built up on the material were different after outdoor exposure. Strong peaks of oxygen (O KA, 0.52 keV) and silicon (Si KA, 1.74 keV) are prominent in all the samples, as would be the case with glass and silica-based coatings. Quantitative results obtained with EDS show that the deposition of dust-related elements on the coating surface differs significantly depending on the type of coating, with the Ti/SiO2 sample having relatively lower levels of accumulated dust-related elements. In the case of Ti/SiO2 coating, Na normalized values are 10.13% (clean) to 8.64% with the addition of 0.02% (dust), Ca normalized values are 8.12% (clean) to 7.26% with the addition of 0.02% (dust), and Mg has a slight decrease of 2.30% to 2.02% (dust). This indicates that there is less accumulation and, therefore, less retention of dust particles on the surface. On the contrary, the reference glass (RG) exhibits a great rise of Na between 1.54% and 3.42%, Ca between 0.60% and 1.91%, and the emergence of Mg at 1.13%, which confirms the high level of dust deposition. Likewise, SiO2 exhibits Na rise between 1.63% and 2.66% and Ca rise between 0.80% and 1.51%, whereas ITO reveals a distinct presence of Fe (1.29%) and Mg rise between 1.62% and 2.05%. The relatively smaller variation and lack of high elemental buildup in Ti/SiO2 as compared to the other samples suggest low dust deposition, which explains lower measured dust coverage and better surface characteristics. These quantitative data show that the Ti/SiO2 coating has better resistance to the deposition of mineral dust.

The existence of these peaks proves the integrity of the silica network and substrate composition before and after exposure. The relative intensity of the Si peak is high in all the samples, which means that the bulk substrate signal is still conspicuous even with the contamination of the surface. Interestingly, sodium (Na), magnesium (Mg), aluminum (Al), calcium (Ca), and iron (Fe) are observed, which is a sign of the existence of mineral-based particulates, typically linked to desert dust and atmospheric deposition. Na and Ca peaks are evident in the RG and SiO2 samples, which agree with the composition of soda-lime glass. The relative intensities of Ca and Mg are slightly increased after exposure, which may indicate that calcareous and silicate dust particles settle on the surface. The Fe peak that is observed at higher energy (∼6.4 keV) is typical of the dusts of iron-containing minerals, which independently indicates the environmental pollution. In comparison, spectra of the dust-exposed samples have a greater elemental diversity and a stronger intensity of the background in the low-energy (below 2 keV) range, indicating the introduction of surface contaminants. The presence of more alkaline and alkaline-earth elements (Na, Mg, Ca) indicates that the prevailing dust type is silicate- and carbonate-rich as it is expected of the desert atmospheric particles. Significantly, no new and unanticipated metallic peaks or phase transformation signatures are found in the coated samples, and this observation suggests that exposure to the environment causes surface contamination of the underlying coatings and not chemical degradation. The presence of high levels of Si and O in addition to the maintenance of coating-specific traces (In, Sn, Ti) is used to prove structural stability in the deposited films.

Figure shows the values of the contact angle of clean and dust-exposed samples of Ti/SiO2- and ITO/SiO2-coated surfaces. It is evident that there is a great alteration of surface wettability after outdoor exposure. In the case of the clean Ti/SiO2 surface (Figure a), the water contact angle is around 62–63, which is moderately hydrophilic. Upon exposure to dust (Figure b), the contact angle also reduces to about 40–42, indicating an increase in surface hydrophilicity. The ITO/SiO2 samples have a more noticeable effect. Figure c shows that the clean ITO/SiO2 surface has a relatively low contact angle of around 75–78, indicating that the intrinsic hydrophilicity of the ITO/SiO2 is relatively lower than that of Ti/SiO2. Nonetheless, once dust accumulation occurs (Figure d), the contact angle reduces considerably to an average of 31–34, which signifies a considerable increase in wettability. Two primary mechanisms could explain the decrease in the contact angle following exposure. The deposited mineral particulates (silicates, carbonates, and metal oxides) enhance the surface roughness and add hydrophilic chemical groups. Second, dust particles will have the ability to form capillary effects and microporous structures that aid water in spreading over the surface. This change of angles of contact to lower contact angles indicates that environmental contamination modifies the surface energy landscape. Comprehensively, the findings indicate that there is a significant impact of surface contamination on wettability behavior, and it should be considered when conducting a long-term outdoor performance of silica-based coatings in desert environments. The relationship between surface structure and wettability can be interpreted using classical contact angle models. For an ideal flat surface, the equilibrium contact angle θ is described by Young’s equation: cos⁡θ=γsv−γslγlv where γsv, γsl, and γlv represent the solid–vapor, solid–liquid, and liquid–vapor interfacial tensions, respectively. When surface corrugation or roughness is introduced, the effective wetting behavior is modified, as described by the Wenzel and Cassie–Baxter models. For patterned or corrugated surfaces, the apparent contact angle becomes dependent on the fractional surface coverage (φ), expressed as γlvcos⁡θ=ϕ(γsv−γsl)−(1−ϕ)γlv where φ represents the fraction of solid surface area in contact with the liquid. Increased surface roughness amplifies the intrinsic wetting behavior by increasing the effective surface area, leading either to enhanced hydrophilicity or enhanced hydrophobicity depending on the dominant surface chemistry. In the present case, oxygen incorporation modifies both chemical bonding (e.g., formation of Si–O–Si networks and surface hydroxyl groups) and nanoscale morphology, thereby influencing droplet behavior. The correlation between the surface roughness, dust coverage, and the contact angle of the ITO/SiO2 and Ti/SiO2 samples is shown in Figure , which provides insights on how oxygen incorporation affected the surface wettability and environmental performance of the sample. The ITO/SiO2 sample has more roughness (approximately 11.9 nm), dust coverage (approximately 21%), and contact angle (approximately 41°), which means lower hydrophilicity. The Ti/SiO2 sample, however, exhibits much less roughness (about 1.85 nm) and lower dust coverage (about 14.5%), with a smaller contact angle (about 32°), which is indicative of an improved surface in terms of wetting.

The outdoor dust exposure test gives realistic information regarding coating performance in adverse environmental conditions, and it is necessary to consider that there is always some variation between field and laboratory experiments. The absolute level of dust deposition can be affected by factors like the speed of wind, the dust composition, humidity, and time variations. In this study, this was done by studying several representative areas in each sample and comparing the relative tendencies between various coating architectures as opposed to absolute values. Although the environment varied, the trends were determined, especially the consistently lower dust on the Ti/SiO2 coating than on SiO2 and ITO/SiO2, as being reproducible across sample installation areas and also validated using additional characterization methods, such as SEM, AFM, and optical analysis. Thus, it is expected that there will be variability in the outdoor setting, but the comparative performance and trends in the background were still strong and consistent.

In the current study, environmental contamination, especially deposition of mineral-rich dust particles, is likely to contribute greatly to the change in the behavior of surface wetting. These dust particles can add hydrophilic chemical groups and alter the surface morphology to result in an increased water spread and decreased contact angles. Therefore, the postexposure hydrophilicity of the coated samples is thought to be a combined effect of both the intrinsic surface characteristics of the metal-incorporated silica coatings and nonspecific effects by dust contamination. The difference is essential to use the wettability findings in a precise manner in an actual outdoor scenario.

The Vickers hardness values reported in Table are expressed in units of HV (kgf/mm2). This value is very much thickness-dependent, which increases with increasing thickness. As reported, the reported values are SiO2: 1000 HV, ITO: 600 HV, and Ti: 300 HV. According to the results of the Vickers hardness, Ti multilayer films have the greatest resistance, which shows a high level of densification of the films and structural integrity. It is found that a steady decrease in hardness is common to dust-exposed samples, and this is an indication that the mechanical stability of the coatings is affected by environmental exposure. The great reduction in hardness of the SiO2(D) clearly shows the vulnerability of the silica-based layers to surface alteration in the outdoor environment. These results play an important role in the determination of the long-term stability of desert photovoltaic applications.

Microhardness Results

It is important to note that Vickers hardness measurements performed after outdoor exposure may be influenced by the presence of dust and surface contaminants. Since the indenter interacts with the topmost layer, loosely adhered particles and surface deposits can affect the measured values and therefore may not accurately represent the intrinsic mechanical properties of the underlying coating. In this study, the hardness values obtained from dust-exposed samples are considered indicative of the overall surface condition rather than a direct measure of coating hardness. Consequently, the intrinsic mechanical stability of the coatings is primarily assessed based on measurements from as-deposited samples, while the postexposure results provide qualitative insight into surface durability under environmental conditions (see Figure ).

Computational Analysis

As shown in Figure , the optical properties of the stacked antidust/antireflective coating (ADC/ARC) structures experimentally measured were taken as input parameters into the simulations to enable the effect of such coatings on the performance of the devices at the device level to be determined. The method is based on the conversion of optical losses and transmittance variations induced by the multilayer coatings into photovoltaic simulated parameters, especially the short-circuit-current density. The empirical parameters of the other layers were used from another work as published previously. The findings here are explicitly theoretical estimates of optical parameters on photovoltaic response and not the experimentally demonstrated efficiency improvements. The simulated trends are thus discussed qualitatively to understand the interpretation of optical data.

Device schematic of the simulated perovskite structure.

As shown in Figure , based on the computational analysis, the open-circuit voltage (V oc) reduces slowly from 1.24 V at 0.5 μm to about 1.00 V at 20 μm, which means that bulk recombination is increased at higher thickness. The predicted short-circuit current density, J sc (maximum) of current at the short circuit, decreases to approximately 27.28 mA cm–2. The fill factor (FF) has a similar pattern with a maximum of about 82% at lower thickness and a sharp reduction to about 43–45%. The power conversion efficiency (PCE) is predicted to reach approximately 27.28%, as obtained from simulation results in the case of 10 nm Ti/100 nm silica, and decreases to less than 8% at 20 μm. Ti/silica layers of the device play a crucial role in the device performance.

Conclusions

This paper presents the fabrication of metal-integrated silica thin films to eliminate dust deposition and optical reflection losses in severe environmental situations. RF magnetron sputtering was used to prepare three architectures with 100 nm SiO2, 10 nm Ti/100 nm SiO2, and 10 nm ITO/100 nm SiO2 and assessed after two months of outdoor exposure. The findings show that metal interlayers that are thin in nature play a crucial role in determining the morphology of surfaces, wettability, and adhesion of dust. The best of the studied structures was the Ti/SiO2 coating that had a low surface roughness, a higher hydrophilicity, and less coverage with dust than the single-layered silica and ITO/SiO2 coatings. The determination of the contact angle proved that it is possible to tune the wettability of the surface with the help of metal integration, and the analysis of microhardness revealed that no significant changes in terms of mechanical stability occurred before and after exposure to the environment. This decrease in dust deposition is due to joint surface actions, such as reduced morphology and altered surface energy, and the role of electrostatic interactions is only indirectly estimated in this experiment. Furthermore, the simulations of SCAPS were performed to investigate the possible impact of these coatings on photovoltaic performance, revealing the tendencies of the better device reaction that were linked to the advanced optical transmission. These results, however, are only theoretical estimates of the efficiency of the device and not experimentally tested values. Overall, these results indicate that metal-integrated silica-based coatings, especially Ti/SiO2, can be a viable solution to enhancing the survivability of surfaces and reducing losses caused by dust in photovoltaic systems under arid conditions. These findings will be helpful in the design of multifunctional coatings, yet more research, such as direct electrical characterization and device-level validation, is necessary to help define their practical effect fully.
