Adaptive cargo deletion as an evolutionary dead-end during phage-based directed evolution
Authors: Zidan Li, Ibrahim Al’Abri, Nathan Crook
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe purpose of this project was to evaluate the performance and reliability of the P1 phage-based inducible directed evolution (IDE) method for performing global transcriptional machinery engineering (gTME) to improve pH tolerance in Escherichia coli Nissle. Unlike prior gTME approaches that evolved single sigma factors, we simultaneously mutated three sigma factors associated with pH tolerance (rpoD, rpoN, and rpoH) through five rounds of mutagenesis and growth-based selection at pH 4.2.ResultsMutations in rpoD (V98E and R603C) emerged during early rounds and reduced the lag phase under acidic conditions. However, library improvement plateaued after the third cycle. In later rounds, an empty vector resulting from the deletion of plasmid-encoded sigma factors became dominant. This deletion of plasmid-encoded sigma factors improved low-pH fitness, but it prevented further mutations and led to an evolutionary dead end. Additionally, transcriptome analysis showed that the single mutations (V98E, R603C) that preceded sigma factor deletion improved fitness through mechanisms resembling those observed upon sigma factor deletion. Together, these findings indicate that phage-based and continuous directed evolution approaches may face challenges if the evolutionary substrate itself reduces performance relative to a wild-type strain.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13104-026-07913-4.

Introduction

Directed evolution is an advantageous technique for improving biological function. Among them, phage-based evolution methods [1, 2] use intracellular mutagenesis and phage-mediated transfer of evolved pathways to fresh hosts to enable orders-of-magnitude improvements in library size/diversity, screening throughput, and the number of evolutionary rounds [3–6]. A consequence of these innovations is a reduced capacity to inspect the quality of the libraries between rounds, particularly regarding the maintenance of cargo length and mutation rates, because certain time-consuming steps (e.g., gel electrophoresis and sequencing of mutated cargo) are not performed. Clearly, the prior success of phage-based evolution approaches means that this tradeoff is a non-issue in at least some settings. However, the emergence of “cheater” variants remains a common challenge inherent to phage‑based evolution [4, 7, 8], motivating consideration of cases in which phage-based directed evolution may not be appropriate.

In this study, we performed inducible directed evolution (IDE) [2], which uses plasmid-based mutagenesis with P1 phage-mediated DNA transfer, to perform global transcriptional machinery engineering (gTME) [9–11] to evolve three sigma factors (rpoD, rpoN, and rpoH) to improve low-pH tolerance. This strategy was unique, as prior efforts employing gTME have largely focused on mutating single sigma factors at a time. Over 5 rounds of evolution, we identified rpoD mutations (V98E, R603C) that reduced lag phase. Notably, a deletion of all three sigma factors from the plasmid emerged and became dominant after the third cycle. Interestingly, this complete deletion of sigma factors improved low-pH tolerance, and transcriptome analysis showed that the rpoD mutations observed in prior rounds enhanced fitness through mechanisms similar to those triggered by sigma-factor deletion. These results suggest that plasmid-based sigma factor expression imposes a fitness cost under low-pH conditions, leading to their elimination during phage-based evolution, highlighting the need to consider the regulatory and metabolic burden of the evolved cargo before applying phage-based evolution for future gTME campaigns.

Main text

We performed five iterative cycles of IDE on an operon containing three sigma factors (rpoD, rpoN, and rpoH) and monitored library performance after each round (Figure S1). We observed that the lag phase duration decreased progressively over the first three cycles (Fig. 1). Interestingly, no further reduction was observed in the fourth and fifth rounds; in fact, their lag phases slightly increased compared to the third-round library. However, these later libraries showed a higher intrinsic growth rate (Fig. 1B), representing an alternative strategy to enhance overall fitness. As a result, the overall performance of the fourth and fifth libraries was comparable to that of the third, and all three libraries reached the stationary phase simultaneously, indicating that evolutionary adaptations had plateaued.

Deep sequencing of each library revealed a total of 48 mutations in the three sigma factors or their promoter/RBS regions, of which 35 were in rpoD, 12 in rpoN, and 1 in the rpoH RBS region (Table S1). This indicated that rpoD was the primary target of mutations. Strikingly, deep sequencing of the parental strain and libraries from each round of directed evolution revealed distinct patterns of read coverage (Fig. 2). The parental strain and the first three evolved libraries showed uniform read coverage across the entire reference phagemid, including the region carrying the three sigma factors (3.0 kb − 9.2 kb). In contrast, beginning from the fourth round, a clear loss of coverage was observed specifically in the cargo region, and this missing coverage persisted through the fifth round. This loss of coverage suggests that large deletions encompassing the sigma factor insert region occurred, preventing further mutations and creating an evolutionary dead-end, thus explaining the plateau in library performance.

To assess the impact of these mutations on pH tolerance, we reintroduced five dominant mutations (R603C, V98E, and W433* in rpoD, E115E (GAA→GAG) in rpoN, and C→T in rpoH RBS region) into the phagemid backbone. W433* proved toxic to Nissle 1917, yielding no colonies after transformation, so we proceeded with the remaining four mutations. A synthetic empty phagemid was also included to evaluate the effect of sigma factor deletion (Fig. 3). All mutants exhibited a reduced lag phase, with V98E and R603C showing the strongest effect, reflected by the shortest lag times (Fig. 3B). We then combined these two mutations, but the double mutant performed similarly to the single R603C mutant. Interestingly, the empty phagemid showed comparable growth to both R603C and the double mutant under low-pH conditions.

Because V98E, R603C, V98ER603C, and deletion mutants exhibited similar growth rates under acid stress, we sought to determine whether their underlying gene expression profiles were also similar. We therefore performed RNA-seq to profile the transcriptomes of cells carrying mutated rpoD (V98E, R603C, and V98ER603C) or the empty vector, alongside the parental strain, during growth under low-pH conditions. Based on clustering and principal component analysis (PCA), all three mutants clustered with the empty vector group along PC1, suggesting that the mutants mimicked the transcriptional profile of the empty vector to gain fitness (Fig. 3C-D). Indeed, both rpoD mutations decreased rpoD expression compared to wild-type rpoD (Figure S2). Specifically, while an extra copy of wild-type rpoD increased expression by 2.58-fold compared to the empty vector, V98E and R603C reduced it to 2.14 and 2.03, respectively, and the double mutant V98E/R603C further lowered it to 1.71. Notably, the R603C mutation was previously identified in gTME experiments [10] and was shown to reduce transcriptional activity at most promoters tested [12, 13], resembling the effect of complete rpoD deletion observed here.

Discussion

Traditional global transcriptional machinery engineering (gTME) has usually been performed by mutating plasmidic sigma factors in strains retaining the unaltered chromosomal copy [9, 10]. This approach positions the mutant protein alongside its wild-type counterpart, often enabling the discovery of functional dominant mutants [9, 10, 14]. While some gTME studies [15–17] do include an empty vector as a control, many gTME studies [9–11, 14] do not, thereby overlooking the potential fitness cost associated with introducing an extra copy of an essential gene on a plasmid. Our results highlight that such a comparison is critical, as it reveals whether the observed mutations represent genuinely beneficial adaptations or merely reflect the cell’s response to offset the burden of additional sigma factor copies.

Previous gTME studies have also reported truncated variants of sigma factors that confer improved fitness, but not complete deletions [10, 11, 14, 15, 18]. Most of these studies relied on PCR-based library amplification followed by purification, and typically involved a limited number of evolution cycles (< 5). Such experimental setups likely eliminated clones carrying complete sigma-factor deletions either because quality-control steps were performed between rounds or because single full-length variants were selected as substrates for the next evolutionary round. In contrast, phage-based evolution approaches avoid PCR amplification between cycles, allowing all selected variants to progress to the next round. Consequently, sigma factor deletion mutants can arise and eventually dominate due to their high likelihood and their fitness advantage.

The high library sizes afforded by IDE made it conceivable, in principle, that nontrivial fitness-enhancing mutations would arise in this study. However, based on the outcomes we observed, we do not recommend performing phage-based evolution techniques when the evolutionary starting point is less fit than the chassis strain in the screen or selection of interest. Instead, rigorous quality control should be performed at regular intervals to ensure that loss-of-function variants do not dominate. While such checks are easily performed using traditional, error-prone PCR approaches, there may be opportunities to incorporate more stringent quality-control procedures in phage-based evolution. Looking ahead, high-throughput approaches to identifying and discarding low-quality sequences during selection should reduce failure rates and “cheaters” in phage-based and continuous evolution techniques.

Limitations

We posit several explanations for the failure to identify mutations that enabled higher pH tolerance than simple deletion. First, rpoD may not be an optimal engineering target for improving pH tolerance. In fact, evolving transcription factors does not always lead to an improved phenotype [19]. Yu et al. applied gTME to both rpoD and rpoS for hyaluronic acid production in E. coli, and only the rpoD mutants resulted in improved yields [15]. In contrast, Klein-Marcuschamer et al. reported that rpoD mutagenesis did not improve butanol tolerance, whereas modifications to rpoA were effective [20]. Expanding IDE-based gTME to include additional candidates, such as artificial transcription factors [21] or exogenous global regulators [17], may avoid disrupting the wider cellular network that sigma factors regulate. Second, the substantial fitness cost of harboring extra sigma factor copies could have made it evolutionarily favorable to completely delete the sigma factors from the phagemid instead of identifying variants with improved pH tolerance. Once those deletions occurred, it became impossible to identify further sigma-factor variants, leading to an evolutionary dead end. Alternatively, evolving these genes in the context of a genomic knockout or directly in the genome may be advantageous.
