Evaluating the safety of Janus kinase inhibitors in the management of severe immune-related adverse events
Authors: Eudora Lee, Ralina Karagenova, Lirit Fuksman, Priya Katyal, Tristan Lim, Viginia Sun, Kerry L Reynolds, Yevgeniy R Semenov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized the treatment of cancer. However, ICIs are associated with potentially life-threatening immune-related adverse events (irAEs). Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi) are a promising steroid-sparing agent for treating corticosteroid-refractory severe irAEs. However, little is known about their safety in the ICI population, limiting clinical adoption due to concerns of reduced anticancer immunity and ICI response. In this retrospective multi-institutional cohort study, we aim to evaluate the safety of JAKi in the management of refractory irAEs requiring escalation beyond the standard of care with systemic corticosteroid therapy. Patients who received both ICI and JAKi at Mass General Brigham Healthcare System and the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute between 2018 and 2025 were manually reviewed for irAE presence, severity, treatment response, and survival (n=33). A comparator cohort of patients with irAEs treated with systemic corticosteroids (n=99). JAKi-treated patients were matched 1:4 to controls using nearest-neighbor matching with a 0.4 caliper based on age, cancer type, number of organs affected by ICI toxicity, and ICI regimen, yielding a matched cohort of 27 cases and 83 controls. Associations between JAKi use, progression-free survival, and overall survival were evaluated with multivariable Cox regression with landmark analyses to account for immortal time bias. To address limited power for survival analyses, we performed an external validation using the TriNetX research network, generating a 1:1 matched multi-institutional cohort of 297 JAKi-treated and 297 non-JAKi-treated irAE patients. Kaplan-Meier analysis showed no statistically significant difference in overall survival (median 34 vs 45 months) or progression-free survival (median 5.6 vs 9.6 months) in JAKi-treated versus control groups. These findings remained consistent with multivariable Cox proportional hazard models. Across all landmark times, the association between JAKi use and survival outcomes remained similar between the two treatment groups. Similarly, in the larger TriNetX cohort, JAKi therapy was not associated with inferior overall survival compared with matched non-JAKi-treated patients. In these complementary multi-institutional cohorts, no large adverse signal was observed with JAKi versus standard-of-care systemic corticosteroid therapy for severe irAEs in this real-world cohort. These results merit further investigation in prospective studies.

Background

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed the treatment of cancer. However, up to 70% of treated patients develop immune-related adverse events (irAEs) across a broad range of organ systems.1 Between 15% and 23% of these irAEs can be severe2 3 and result in treatment interruption, life-long morbidity, and mortality.4

Currently, systemic corticosteroids are the primary treatment for severe irAEs,5 but are often insufficient.6 7 Additionally, there is growing evidence that systemic corticosteroids are associated with worse survival among ICI recipients, particularly if administered early in the ICI treatment course.8 9 Given these growing concerns about safety and efficacy of systemic corticosteroids, there is an urgent need to identify steroid-sparing alternatives for the management of severe irAEs.

Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi) are an emerging treatment modality typically used in the setting of severe or life-threatening, often corticosteroid-refractory irAEs.10 11 They have a multi-targeted mechanism of action blocking interleukin (IL)-6, IL-17, IL-12/23 signaling which play a role in lymphocytic and mixed innate and lymphoid toxicities. However, limited safety data in ICI-treated patients and incomplete understanding of their effects on irAEs and antitumor efficacy have restricted clinical adoption because of concerns about potentially worse cancer outcomes compared with corticosteroids.1214

To date, most of the existing evidence on the safety and efficacy of JAKi in managing irAEs stems from case reports and small case series.10 13 For example, in a single-center prospective cohort study in Canada evaluating 24 patients with grade ≥3 or persistent grade 2 irAEs unresponsive to systemic corticosteroids, 17 patients (70.8%) achieved clinical resolution of symptoms.11 Interestingly, response rates to JAKi were particularly high in severe cases of irAEs, such as arthritis (100%) and myocarditis (75%). However, this study lacked a comparator cohort, preventing direct comparison between JAKi and alternative first-line or second-line therapies, including systemic corticosteroids and biologics.

In this multi-institutional retrospective cohort study, we aim to evaluate the safety and efficacy of JAKi in the management of refractory irAEs requiring escalation beyond standard-of-care with systemic corticosteroid therapy. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine this important clinical question with benchmarking to a matched control population, thereby reducing the significant sources of confounding affecting prior studies.

Methods

Study population and cohort assembly

We conducted a multi-institutional retrospective cohort study of ICI recipients who received systemic JAKi therapy for the management of irAEs between 2018 and 2025, at the Massachusetts General Brigham Healthcare System and the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (MGBD). Patient demographics and medical history were extracted from the MGBD enterprise data warehouse. Manual chart review was conducted to extract ICI-related variables, including ICI type, whether the patient received more than one ICI, start and end dates, and age at initiation. Data were collected on irAE characteristics for which a JAKi was given, including irAE type, number of organ systems involved, organ system of most severe irAE, onset and resolution dates, treatments prior to JAKi, recurrence, and severity. Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) V.5.0 was used to grade irAEs.15 Patients whose irAE became grade 0 were considered complete responders; those whose irAE became grade 1 were considered partial responders. Both complete and partial responders were categorized as responders.

JAKi-related variables included type of JAKi, indication, start and end dates, JAKi adverse events, and response. Additional patient characteristics included cancer type, stage at diagnosis, and patient outcomes (cancer progression, vital status, cause of death, date of last contact).

Patients who did not receive systemic JAKi or who were given JAKi for a condition other than an irAE were excluded. The retained ICI recipients who were treated with a JAKi for the management of irAEs (JAKi group) were then compared with a control group from MGBD, which included ICI recipients who developed irAEs but were treated with systemic corticosteroids. Controls were selected using 1:4 nearest-neighbor matching with a 0.4 caliper based on age at ICI initiation, ICI type (combination vs non-combination), number of organ systems involved in irAE, and cancer type. Each case was matched with up to four controls, restricting matches to those within a caliper of 0.4 on the propensity score scale. The matching was performed using the MatchIt package (V.4.7.2) in R, and 27 JAKi-treated patients were matched to 83 control patients. Most covariates achieved adequate balance with absolute standardized mean differences (SMD) <0.1; however, moderate imbalance remained for certain cancer types (largest SMD ≈ 0.26). All models were adjusted for these variables.

To address limited power for survival analyses in our cohort, we performed external real-world validation using a multi-institutional cohort from the TriNetX research network. Patients receiving ICIs who developed an irAE were identified using methodology from Block et al 202516 (online supplemental table 2). Patients treated with a JAKi following irAE diagnosis were compared with those not treated with JAKi. Propensity score matching (1:1 greedy nearest neighbor) was performed based on age, sex, race, ethnicity, metastatic codes, Charlson Comorbidity Index comorbidities, ICI agent, and hospitalization status (online supplemental table 3). Adequate balance was achieved for all variables (SMD <0.1).

Analytical approach

Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared between groups using the log-rank test. Associations between JAKi use and survival outcomes were evaluated using multivariable weighted Cox proportional hazards models, adjusted for age, sex, cancer type, ICI regimen (combo vs non-combo), irAE severity (categorized as low (CTCAE grades 0–2) vs high (CTCAE grades 3–5)), and cancer stage at ICI initiation, stratified by the number of organs involved in irAEs. Landmark analyses were conducted to account for immortal time bias. The primary landmark time was set at 150 days after ICI initiation, chosen based on the observed distribution of time from ICI initiation to time of JAKi initiation. Approximately 50% of JAKi treatments were initiated by 66 days and 75% by 160 days. At the 150-day landmark, over 90% of patients remained at risk for OS and over 70% for PFS. Patients who experienced the event or were censored before the landmark were excluded, and exposure status was defined according to JAKi use by the landmark time. Multivariable weighted Cox proportional hazards models were fit from the landmark forward using the same covariate structure. Sensitivity analyses were performed using alternative landmark times of 100 and 180 days to evaluate the robustness of the results to the choice of landmark. Continuous variables were compared between groups using Wilcoxon rank-sum test with continuity correction. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. All analyses were conducted using R V.5.0.4.

In the TriNetX cohort, OS was estimated using Kaplan-Meier and the effect of JAKi on OS was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards model. To reduce potential bias from early mortality following ICI initiation, sensitivity analysis was performed at 90 days after ICI initiation.

With a median follow-up time of 51.7 months (reverse Kaplan-Meier), Kaplan-Meier analyses demonstrated no significant differences in OS between JAKi-treated patients and control population (p=0.84) (figure 1A). Median OS was 34 months (95% CI 17.3 to not available) in the JAKi group and 45 months (95% CI 25.2 to not available) in the control group. In multivariable analyses, JAKi use did not demonstrate worse OS by comparison to the corticosteroid-treated control population (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.40 to 1.98, p=0.77). Landmark analyses treating JAKi exposure as time-dependent similarly did not demonstrate significant differences in OS between JAKi and the control groups at 100, 150, or 180 days (primary landmark 150 days; HR 1.37, 95% CI 0.44 to 4.27, p=0.59), though the HR trended towards greater risk in the JAKi population. Cox model for PFS demonstrated an increased, though non-statistically significant, risk of progression in the JAKi group (HR 1.65; 95% CI 0.91 to 2.99, p=0.10). Landmark analyses at 150 days demonstrated similar results (HR 1.69, 95% CI 0.54 to 5.26, p=0.37). Although Kaplan-Meier analyses demonstrated that PFS differed significantly between JAKi and the control groups with median PFS of 5.6 months (95% CI 2.6 to 11.8) versus 9.6 months (95% CI 6.9 to 13.7) (p=0.017) (figure 1B), these estimates reflect raw differences between groups, not accounting for potential confounding. In contrast, the Cox proportional hazards model adjusted for covariates that may explain why the association between JAKi use and PFS was no longer statistically significant. Post hoc power calculations were conducted to characterize the range of detectable effect sizes given the observed number of events and matching weights. Using 80% power and two-sided α of 0.05, only large effect sizes were detectable with HR below 0.50 or above 1.99 for PFS and below 0.38 or above 2.61 for OS. As a result, our study was underpowered to detect small-to-moderate effect sizes but able to detect large clinically meaningful differences between the study groups. To address this limitation, additional analysis using TriNetX compared 297 JAKi-treated patients to 297 matched non-JAKi-treated irAE patients. In survival analysis indexed at ICI initiation, Kaplan-Meier analysis showed no statistically significant difference between the groups (p=0.14) (online supplemental figure 1). In the Cox proportional hazard model, there was no significant difference in OS between groups (HR 1.21, 95% CI 0.94 to 1.55, p=0.87). After excluding early deaths occurring within 90 days of ICI initiation, the difference in OS remained nonsignificant in the JAKi and non-JAKi cohorts (HR 1.09, 95% CI 0.82 to 1.44, p=0.24). These findings provide external validation to our internal cohort results.

Discussion

JAKi have shown promise in the treatment of severe or refractory irAEs. However, their utilization is limited due to insufficient information on their safety in the ICI population and concerns of worse cancer outcomes by comparison to systemic corticosteroids, the current standard of care for moderate to severe irAEs.1214 Moreover, current guidelines recommending systemic corticosteroids as first-line therapy for moderate-to-severe irAEs stem mainly from clinical experience and expert opinions,5 and large trials evaluating steroid-sparing strategies for irAEs are lacking.

In this multi-institutional retrospective cohort study, JAKi use for severe irAEs was associated with rates of irAE resolution comparable to those observed with other immunosuppressive strategies, though conclusions are limited by small sample size and confounding factors. Specifically, there was no statistical difference in the rates of irAE resolution and time from irAE onset to resolution between JAKi and non-JAKi groups. Of note, the marginal association of JAKi use with earlier irAE onset may reflect more severe or rapidly progressing toxicity for which treatment escalation to JAKi was warranted, rather than a causal effect of JAKi. In contrast, duration of follow-up differed markedly across groups, with significantly shorter follow-up among deceased patients treated with JAKi (p<0.001) (table 2). Similarly, we observed that JAKi utilization may result in moderate (though not statistically significant) differences in survival by comparison to patients that did not receive a JAKi (online supplemental table 2). When comparing cancer stage at diagnosis, the JAKi cohort had significantly fewer metastatic cases (22.2% vs 51.8%) (table 1). Moreover, though more patients in the JAKi-treated cohort died from irAEs, there was less cancer-related death as compared with the non-JAKi group. Of note, two patients in the non-JAKi group died from sepsis and COVID-19, as compared with none in the JAKi cohort. Despite a more favorable stage distribution and fewer cancer-related deaths, the JAKi cohort demonstrated numerically lower OS. This pattern argues against cancer progression as the primary driver of mortality and instead suggests competing risks related to severe irAEs. These observations are partially mitigated by the fact that JAKi are typically used to manage corticosteroid-refractory, and therefore more severe and life-threatening irAEs10 11 even among the population with high-grade toxicities examined in this study. Additionally, all patients in both the case and control groups received some form of systemic corticosteroids, with many of them also receiving targeted immunomodulators (infliximab, vedolizumab), prior to receiving JAKi as a second or third-line treatment option for refractory disease (table 3). As a result, it is very likely that some of the trend toward increased risk of worse PFS, and to a lesser extent OS, observed in this study is due to this source of selection bias that could not have been fully eliminated given the retrospective nature of the study. Importantly, these findings do not support a signal of worsened oncologic outcomes associated with JAK inhibition.

Similarly, in the larger, multi-institutional TriNetX cohort, JAKi therapy for irAEs was not associated with inferior OS compared with matched irAE patients not receiving JAKi. Although the timing of JAKi initiation relative to irAE onset was not explicitly modeled and may introduce confounding by indication, the consistency of survival outcomes across both cohorts is reassuring. Furthermore, while TriNetX lacks clinical granularity and detailed phenotyping available in our internal cohort, its substantially larger sample size enables greater statistical power to detect meaningful survival differences and validate our internal cohort findings. The concordance of findings across these complementary and independent (as MGBD does not participate in TriNetX) populations strengthens the validity and generalizability of our results.

Across the complementary multinational and analytical approaches, we did not observe a large adverse safety signal with JAKi versus standard-of-care therapy for severe irAEs that could be attributed to JAKi use. These findings provide real-world evidence suggesting that JAKi use for refractory irAEs is not associated with inferior oncologic outcomes, though confirmation in prospective studies is needed.

While these conclusions suggest a potentially acceptable safety, they do not provide definitive evidence of benefit. While our institutional cohort was limited in size, the addition of a large, matched external cohort substantially increases statistical power for survival analyses. Notably, JAKi is preferentially administered to patients with severe presentations (a source of confounding by indication) and thus are expected to have poorer cancer outcomes or experience toxicity-associated mortality. However, our observation that these patients requiring treatment escalation with JAK inhibition did not experience worse outcomes by comparison to patients not receiving JAK inhibition is reassuring. Prospective studies with greater event counts are needed to demonstrate the impact of JAKi therapy on cancer outcomes and to determine whether these agents confer survival neutrality or benefit when used for severe or refractory irAEs. Nevertheless, this study provides important real-world evidence on JAK inhibition in a vulnerable patient population that can be leveraged in treatment selection and patient counseling.
