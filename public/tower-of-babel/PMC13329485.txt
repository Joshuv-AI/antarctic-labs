Association of Neighborhood Socioeconomic Status With Time to Consultation and Surgery for Carpal Tunnel Syndrome
Authors: Jack T Franchino, Kathleen Jahnke, Shems Hamdan, Christian J Hausner, Richard C Sadler, Katie A Latack, Charles S Day
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeCarpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) accounts for substantial disability. Previous CTS studies have conflicting findings. Some studies at the ZIP-code level show that patients from socioeconomically disadvantaged communities present with worse function and face inequities in accessing care. Conversely, other studies using area deprivation index (ADI) demonstrated no differences in delays to care. Existing literature lacks evaluation of earlier stages in the clinical pathway using higher geographic granularity, which we aim to contribute. Specifically, we aim to answer: (1) is lower census tract-level (CT-level) socioeconomic status (SES) associated with longer delays from symptom onset to orthopedic hand-specialty consultation, and (2) is lower CT-level SES associated with longer delays from symptom onset to carpal tunnel release surgery?MethodsRetrospective chart review identified 276 patients who underwent carpal tunnel release (2022−2025). Time from symptom onset to consultation and surgery was linked to ADI and social vulnerability index, obtained from patient addresses. Linear regression tested associations between ADI, social vulnerability index and American community survey CT-level variables and time to consultation and surgery.ResultsMedian time from symptom onset to specialist consultation was 720.5 days, and surgery, 970.0 days, reflecting wide variability in patient-reported symptom chronicity. Area deprivation index was not associated with time to consultation or surgery. Adjusted models yielded similar findings, with confidence intervals excluding clinically meaningful delays (>6 months).ConclusionsNeighborhood deprivation was not consistently associated with clinically meaningful delays to consultation or surgery, suggesting no detectable clinically meaningful disparity across SES strata.Type of study/level of evidencePrognostic III.Key words: Access, Carpal tunnel, Delays, Disparities, Socioeconomic

Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is the most common entrapment neuropathy of the hand and wrist.1 Affecting millions of people annually, CTS bears substantial impacts on societal wellbeing and economic costs to individuals and health care systems.1 Among CTS patients, those with prolonged duration of symptoms and worse preoperative symptoms are afforded the least relief from carpal tunnel release (CTR), leading to delayed or incomplete recovery.2

Prior studies investigating socioeconomic status (SES) disparities in CTS care have produced conflicting findings. Classically, studies performed at the ZIP-code level have shown CTR patients from socioeconomically disadvantaged areas present with significantly worse baseline physical and mental health, including impaired upper-extremity function, greater pain interference, and higher rates of anxiety and depression.3 Furthermore, such studies have found that despite more severe injury, socioeconomically disadvantaged patients face lower odds of receiving surgery and longer delays among those who do receive it.4,5 These inequities suggest that lower SES patients not only begin at a disadvantaged baseline but may also be subjected to additional barriers that prolong access to care.3,6

Conversely, other studies have found no association between area deprivation index (ADI), a measure of SES, and CTS severity or treatment timing.7,8 Most existing studies on delays in CTS have focused on later stages of the care pathway, such as from diagnosis to surgery.8 However, this timeframe does not fully capture the clinically meaningful interval in which patients experience worsening symptoms.2,9 Specifically, referral-based and diagnosis-based timelines do not account for the more patient-centered timeframe of how long someone is in pain before it can be addressed by a specialist. This timeframe may also better detect SES associations with delays to care as delays in obtaining referral and/or diagnosis likely represent the primary challenge of initially entering the care pathway. Furthermore, prior analyses of SES in CTS have relied on ZIP code-level-indicators which can be geographically large and may misrepresent meaningful within-area SES variation, whereas census tract-level (CT-level) measures may more accurately reflect underlying SES disparities.4,10,11 In contrast, our study assesses earlier access points in the care pathway, evaluating time from patient-reported symptom onset to specialist consultation and surgery using social vulnerability index (SVI) and individual American community survey (ACS) variables as CT-level SES measures, which provide enhanced geographic granularity.12,13 This approach may allow us to better test whether socioeconomic disadvantage is associated with earlier and more actionable delays in the CTS care pathway.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study, conducted at a multihospital academic health care system in the Midwest, was approved by the institutional review board and the study protocol conformed to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained or waived per institutional standards. Patients who underwent endoscopic CTR from November 1, 2022, through March 31, 2025, performed by a fellowship-trained orthopedic hand surgeon were identified using Epic SlicerDicer software and filtering by procedure date and Current Procedural Terminology code 29848. Patients were included if they underwent primary unilateral endoscopic CTR. Exclusion criteria included age under 18 years, residence outside of the United States, bilateral CTR, additional procedures at time of CTR (including injections), or revision CTR. Those cases were excluded to reduce symptom onset ambiguity, ensure independent observations for each patient, minimize referral bias, and test one primary clinical course. Diagnoses were made clinically by the primary surgeon, based on symptom presentation and confirmatory studies, including EMG, as part of the primary surgeon’s standard clinical workflow when indicated.

Description of experiment, treatment of surgery

All included patients underwent endoscopic CTR using the treating surgeon’s standard technique. No additional procedures were performed at the time of surgery. At our institution, standard postoperative CTR care includes, but is not limited to, routine wound care, gradual return to light activity, and patient education with instructions for follow-up. Postoperative therapy is not standard, unless clinically indicated on a patient-by-patient basis.

Variables, outcome measures, data sources, bias

For each patient, we collected demographic variables (age at surgery, sex, race/ethnicity, insurance type, and address), initial consultation date and location, and surgery date and location. Primary outcomes were time from symptom onset to consultation (days) and symptom onset to surgery (days). Primary predictors were ADI and SVI linked to patient addresses at the CT level using publicly available data sets. Other predictors included (by census tract) percent below poverty, percent without high school diploma, unemployment rate, and percent of households without access to at least one vehicle. Covariates included age, sex, race/ethnicity, insurance type, and travel distance from home address to consult and surgery locations, respectively. Symptom onset dates were abstracted from patient-reported histories documented at the time of initial consult. To minimize data entry errors, all variables were confirmed by three reviewers prior to data submission for analysis. Patients with incomplete address data were excluded. This study adhered to the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational studies.

Study population

The median age was 63 years (interquartile range [IQR], 52−73 years), and 64% were women. Demographic variables (address, insurance, race/ethnicity, sex, and age at surgery), initial consult date, initial consult location, surgery date, and surgery location were recorded for all included patients. Patient demographics are summarized in Table 1. A total of 276 unique patients met the inclusion criteria for this study. Two patients were excluded who lived outside of the United States and 36 patients had cancelled, concurrent, or noninitial CTR surgeries, or were missing address data upon chart review.

Other methods

Symptom onset was abstracted from the initial consultation note in the electronic medical record. Of the 276 patients included, 47% (n = 129) had symptom onset documented in definite, numerical terms (eg, “3 weeks” or “15 days”) and 53% (n = 147) had symptom onset documented in structured approximate terms (eg, “a few weeks” or “a couple of months”). In the cases of structured approximations, we uniformly applied standardized conversions to ensure internal validity and replicability. Following a consensus meeting with the senior author, research team, and documenting surgeon, standardized conversions and rules were applied based on the surgeon’s real-world documentation syntax (Fig. 1). One month was defined as 30 days. A sensitivity analysis was used to test whether these conversions affected our results significantly. We tested the impact of removing all approximated patients on the outcomes of both duration of symptoms prior to initial consult and prior to surgery. This approach was selected to capture the full duration of patients’ symptoms, including prior to their first episode of care.

Standardized conversion of approximate symptom durations. Symptom duration documented in the electronic medical record was categorized by type. Approximate durations (53% of the cohort) were classified as standardized approximations or ranges. Temporal descriptors (eg, “A Couple”) were converted to numeric outputs using predefined, standardized rules based on the documenting surgeon's clinical documentation patterns. Ranges were converted to midpoint values. Exact numerical entries (47% of the cohort) were used as documented without conversion.

Statistical analysis, study size

Patient data and clinic locations were formatted for analysis and uploaded to .csv files for geocoding in ArcGIS Pro. The address locator had a 100% match rate, with all 276 patients and 11 clinics matched to their exact location. Each patient record contained a code with the consultation and surgery location. Closest facility analysis was run to compute the distance between an individual’s home and their surgery location. All continuous data was described using medians and IQR, while categorical data were described using counts and percentages; six patients had more than one surgery. For these patients, only their first surgery was included in the analysis. Univariable and multivariable linear regression were run to explore the associations between SES (represented by ADI and SVI) and both time from symptom onset to orthopedic hand-specialty consultation and time from symptom onset to CTR surgery, controlling for demographic variables and distance from the patient’s address to the initial consultation location and surgery location, respectively. Census tract-level variables were tested in univariate analysis, but because of being a part of the calculation for SVI, they were not included in the multivariable analyses. The analyses were performed again after removing the outliers for time to consultation and surgery, for the respective analyses. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. An a priori sample size calculation was not performed. However, with 276 patients included, the observed 95% confidence intervals excluded delays on the order of several months, suggesting that large, clinically meaningful disparities (>6 months) were unlikely within the cohort.

Results

Overall, CT-level SES measures showed no consistent relationship with duration of symptoms prior to initial specialist consultation. In the univariable regression analysis (Table 2), ADI was not associated with time from symptom onset to consultation (95% CI: −17.58 to 3.69, P = .200). Similarly, SVI was not associated with consultation time (95% CI: −14.31 to 2.93, P = .195). Unemployment rate was associated with time from symptom onset to consultation with a modest negative association (P = .034); however, this effect did not persist after excluding outliers (P = .278) (Tables 2 and 3). Census tract-level percent of households below the poverty line (P = .086), without a high school diploma (P = .760), and without at least one vehicle (P = .306) all demonstrated null associations when both including and excluding outliers (Tables 2 and 3).

In the univariable regression analysis (Table 4), ADI was not significantly associated with time from symptom onset to surgery (B = −5.97, 95% CI: −16.69 to 4.75, P = .274). Similarly, SVI was not significantly associated with surgical timing (B = −5.66, 95% CI: −14.35 to 3.03, P = .201). Census tract-level findings remained insignificant in association with delays to surgery, including while controlling for outliers as well (Table 4 and 5). Grouped analyses of ADI and SVI quintiles (Tables 6 and 7) likewise did not show a consistent gradient across categories. While the SVI quintile of 21% to 40% (P = .004), was significant, the absence of concordant associations across the broader SVI gradient and small sample size (n = 9) suggests this finding is more likely because of random variation than a true effect. Distance from a patient’s home to office and surgery locations demonstrated no significant association in delays to either initial consultation or surgery (Table 2, Table 3, Table 4, Table 5). In addition, higher unemployment rate was significantly associated with shorter time from symptom onset to surgery in univariable analyses (P = .034) (Table 4). However, this association did not remain significant after removing outliers (Table 5).

Furthermore, to assess for potential deviations in results because of the standardized approximation of 53% of patients’ symptom durations, a sensitivity analysis was performed that tested removing the approximated patients (Table 8). Across the sensitivity analysis, broadly similar findings were identified aside from an isolated statistically significant negative SVI association (B = −10.70, 95% CI: –20.7 to −0.71, P = .036) (Table 9). Area deprivation index remained nonsignificant (P = .313), as did race, insurance type, percent of households below poverty, percent of residents without a high school diploma, unemployment rate, percent of households without a vehicle, age, and distance to consultation and surgery locations (Tables 9 and 10). Female sex was associated with delays in time to initial consultation, but that association was not observed in delays to surgery, nor for either duration in the sensitivity analysis (Table 3, Table 5, Table 9, Table 10).

Discussion

Carpal tunnel syndrome is highly prevalent and imposes significant morbidity. Patients with longer symptom duration before treatment typically experience worse outcomes and slower or incomplete recovery.6 Prior research has demonstrated conflicting results, with some studies finding patients from socioeconomically disadvantaged areas presenting with poorer baseline health status, longer delays in care, and are less likely to receive definitive treatment.3,6 Other studies have found no measurable differences after adjusting for confounders.7,8 These inconsistencies raise the question about whether previously reported disparities reflect true differences in access to care or arise from methodological factors such as using ZIP-level SES data or only focusing on later stages in the care pathway.3,4 Our study helps clarify these conflicting findings by assessing whether neighborhood deprivation, as measured by CT-level ADI, SVI, and ACS variables, is associated with earlier delays in care. By investigating further upstream in the clinical care pathway, our data can help inform whether disparities originate before patients even access specialty care. We also investigated whether a particular CT-level ACS variable, such as access to a vehicle within a household, is a primary contributor to delays in care, if detected. By evaluating individual ACS variables, we aim to provide more actionable evidence that may be useful as health systems and governments consider which initiatives may provide the highest value—improvement in outcome per dollar spent—when looking to allocate resources to improve health equity for their patients.

The finding of this study should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, symptom onset was based on patient-reported histories obtained at the time of initial consultation. This introduces the possibility for recall bias, depending on the individual patient and the unique circumstances and symptoms they presented with. To minimize this, conversion rules, as defined by the primary surgeon, were used to convert standardized approximations to numeric values, when necessary (Fig. 1). A sensitivity analysis was also performed to ensure the accuracy and robustness of our findings, showing broadly similar findings, with ADI and ACS variables remaining nonstatistically significant (Tables 8−10). Social vulnerability index reached statistical significance demonstrating a negative association with delays to care (Tables 8−10). The negative SVI association was not consistent with expected relationships between SES and delays in access to care and this isolated finding may reflect statistical variability rather than a true difference in delays accessing care. This single inconsistency, observed with otherwise concordant nonsignificant findings, should be viewed within the broader context of the data.

Furthermore, patient-reported symptom duration may introduce measurement imprecision; however, any misreporting or misclassification is likely nondifferential across SES strata. This was a retrospective single-surgeon, single-institution study, which may limit its generalizability to other health systems and providers with possibly different referral coordination or other barriers to accessing care. Access disparities may also differ in other systems with fragmented referral pathways or greater interhospital variability. Additionally, residual unmeasured confounding, such as symptom severity at presentation, patient preferences in provider and/or facility, and patients’ occupations, may not be reflected in our analysis. However, our observed confidence intervals across the spectrum of our analyses make large disparities unlikely within this cohort, although smaller effects remain possible. The confidence intervals observed in this study reflect substantial real-world variability in patient-reported symptom duration rather than analytic instability alone. Carpal tunnel syndrome often develops insidiously, and patients present within a wide range of chronicity, as demonstrated by the broad interquartile ranges in time to consultation and surgery. We deliberately retained this variability in our analysis to preserve clinical representativeness as excluding patients with drastically prolonged symptom durations may have narrowed the distribution and provided “cleaner” results, but at the expense of reducing generalizability to real-world clinical practice. Finally, we also acknowledge the “ecological fallacy” in assigning area level characteristics to individuals and are cognizant of the need to treat all such findings as associations between groups and individuals.14

In this cohort, we did not detect a consistent clinically meaningful association between neighborhood-level SES measures with time from symptom onset to consultation for CTS (Tables 2 and 3). This finding contrasts with prior studies that have reported less frequent surgical treatment and longer delays in accessing care among disadvantaged patients.4,6 However, our results do align with Aloi et al,7 who measured the relationship between ADI and CTS severity. In their retrospective analysis, there was no significant difference in any of the outcome/severity measures across the ADI quartiles. Specifically, higher ADI (worse neighborhood deprivation) was not associated with worse severity of CTS at presentation. Wright et al3 used ADI to test whether symptom duration differed by social deprivation quartile. In that study, ADI was also used to measure worsening of mental and physical health, comorbidity, and substance use among patients presenting for CTS.3 It was found that patients in the most deprived ADI quartile who presented for evaluation of CTS had worse mean scores in all four patient-reported outcomes measurement information system domains as well as higher proportions of elevated anxiety and depression.3 However, it was also determined that there was no difference in symptom duration across ADI quartiles.3 Together, these previous findings support our data that time to initial consultation for CTS may not be consistently delayed by SES factors once symptoms develop. By testing from symptom onset, rather than referral or diagnostic intervals, our analysis helps reconcile prior conflicting results showing that disparities likely do not affect time from symptom onset to specialist consultation, once a patient has decided to follow-through on obtaining specialty care.

In this study, we did not detect a consistent association between neighborhood deprivation and time from symptom onset to surgery for CTS after adjusting for demographic and geographic factors (Table 5). However, smaller undetected effects remain possible. This finding contrasts with several other analyses that reported SES disparities affected surgical access and timing for CTS.4, 5, 6 Those studies relied on ZIP code-level data and found that patients from more deprived communities had lower rates of CTR surgery, longer delays between diagnosis and surgery, and more severe symptoms. However, other studies found that after applying multivariable regression controlling for demographics and comorbidities, ADI was not a significant predictor of time to surgery and concluded that higher social deprivation did not correlate with delays in the treatment timeline.8 Our findings reflect the latter study, detecting no consistent association between CT-level SES measures and surgical timing. Additionally, we also aimed to expand on these findings by looking even further upstream in the care pathway, beginning at date of symptom onset, rather than date of diagnostic testing or referral, to test whether a previously undetected delay had occurred in an earlier untested time interval. Our results add longitudinal depth to these prior findings, investigating previously unassessed early time intervals in the care pathway, with higher granularity, and help clarify the mixed evidence in the literature. Taken together, these findings suggest SES disparities in CTS care are less likely originating from surgical scheduling, but, instead, may stem from individual patient context and clinical decision-making.

As described, we observed no clinically meaningful delays to consultation or surgery among households without access to at least one vehicle (Table 2, Table 3, Table 4, Table 5). Historically, the literature has demonstrated that lack of transportation to medical appointments was an important contributor to SES inequities in access to medical care, including within orthopedics.15, 16, 17 In particular, Bernstein et al15 surveyed new patients at an orthopedics clinic and found that 399 of 9,057 respondents indicated transportation issues limited their ability to make appointments or obtain related medications. Wolfe et al16 analyzed 20 years of data from the National Health Interview Survey and reported 1.8% of the US population—approximately 5,800,000 people—delayed medical care because of lack of transportation.

Based on prior literature, many policies and institutions, including ours, have allocated resources to mitigating this barrier.18,19 Of interest, and consistent with our findings, some prospective studies, targeting Medicaid and Medicare Accountable Care Organization patient populations, reported no improvements in appointment no-show rates, inpatient admissions, emergency department visits, and significantly higher outpatient spending.18,19 Although those assessed outcomes differ from the focus of our study, they similarly suggest that transportation alone may not be the driving force leading to inequities in access to care. In this study, we focused on evaluating individual, actionable, policy-relevant associations at the CT-level—such as transportation and distance to care—which currently has limited data in the orthopedic literature. Prior ZIP-code level and composite (ADI/SVI) studies may reflect aggregation bias, with less specific and actionable outcomes. With our specific null findings and those of existing studies, implementing higher-value interventions to achieve health equity may require looking beyond household vehicle ownership and distance to care. We suspect factors such as regional differences, individual-level constraints and obligations, and access to reliable—and safe—public transportation may account for these broadly null real-world findings despite seemingly robust theoretical retrospective literature.

Overall, our findings suggest that once a patient pursues specialty care for CTS, access to consultation and surgery is relatively equitable across SES strata. In addition, the study expanded beyond just ZIP code-level analysis and employed CT-level SVI and ACS variables. These results add geographic precision, actionable health policy considerations, and longitudinal depth to the body of ongoing investigations in determining how best to deliver timely and cost-effective orthopedic specialty care for all patients.
