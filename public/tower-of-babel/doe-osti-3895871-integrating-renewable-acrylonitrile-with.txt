 Of the 3 potential substrates under exploration, MA has been demonstrated to provide the highest
 yields of AN at 97%. Using the experimental conditions outlined in Table 1, production of 100 g
 of AN ranges from 30-50 hours on stream. Additionally, active catalytic sites on the TiO2
 catalyst has been shown to deactivate after ~12 hours on stream, reducing yields and requiring
 additional time for replacement or regeneration. Using existing instrumentation, it takes
 approximately 24 hours to repack the catalyst bed with regenerated catalyst. To generate 100 g of
 AN using MA as the substrate, the catalyst would need to be replaced 4 times, adding 96 hours
 for a total of 136.5 hours, or ~5.5 days.

 As such, reducing catalyst deactivation is central to improving the efficiency of AN generation
 using this technology. Addressing this issue, preliminary studies on catalyst deactivation have
 shown AlPO4 to be a promising alternative to TiO2 due to significantly decreased rate of
 deactivation while maintaining a fast rate of initial AN formation, a key requirement to mitigate
 side-reactions on the catalyst side and subsequent deactivation and carbon losses. In these
 studies, AlPO4 was determined to have a deactivation rate constant (kd) of 0.08 h-1, while TiO2
 has a kd of 0.74 h-1, almost an order of magnitude higher. Under the conditions used for
 determination of these rate constants, AlPO4 allowed for a 4-fold increase in TOS before
 significant losses in AN yields.

 Experimental approach:

 Nitrilation experiments are carried out using a heated vapor-phase flow reactor with an in-line
 FTIR for direct product quantification. In a typical nitrilation experiment, the methyl acrylate
 substrate is injected into a heated nitrogen carrier stream (1000 sccm) at 0.25 mL/min. It is then
 mixed with NH3 gas (500 sccm) where it then passes over ~40 g of TiO2 catalyst in a heated
 reactor bed at 310 ⁰C. The exhaust gasses are then diluted with 2000 sccm N2 and passed through
 an FTIR cell for quantification. The gasses are then bubbled through a knockout pot (KOP)
 containing glass beads and chilled DMF (7 ⁰C), where the acrylonitrile (AN), water, methanol
 (MeOH), and unreacted MA are collected. Notably, any gasses which do not condense in the
 KOP are vented out. After the reaction is complete, the KOP is emptied and its contents are
 stored at 5 ⁰C without any additives. Products collected in the KOP are quantified using 1H NMR
 spectroscopy and compared to yields measured by FTIR to determine product recovery.

 Characterization of KOP contents:

 H NMR was selected for characterization of the crude contents of the KOP pot. Through
 comparison of the known DMF concentrations to the monomer peaks, the amount of monomer
 can be readily quantified. Notably, methanol and water signals are overlapped by the DMF and
 are unable to be quantified. A key challenge in accessing high product recovery stems from the
 high volatility of AN and MA, with a boiling point of 77 ⁰C and 80 ⁰C, respectively. MeOH also
 has a low boiling point (64.7 ⁰C), while water (100 ⁰C) is expected to be collected more
 efficiently due to a high boiling point.

 To evaluate the product recovery in the KOP, and as such the conditions necessary for
 polymerization of AN to PAN, different KOP conditions were tested. In KOP Solution #1 (Table
 4), 400 mL of DMF was used with 3 hours of time on stream using the model nitrilation
 conditions described above. After analysis by 1H NMR, 52% of the monomer products were
 recovered. By comparison, using a smaller amount of DMF in the KOP with otherwise similar
 conditions resulted in a reduction in product recovery to 24.5%.

 Development of methodology for polymerization of knock-out pot solutions:

 A key component of this project is to minimize energy consumption through direct
 polymerization of the contents of the knock-out pot (KOP), which contains a mixture of organic
 solvent, acrylonitrile (AN), methanol (MeOH), water, and unreacted methyl acrylate (MA). As
 such, we sought to evaluate different polymerization methodologies that might be compatible
 with the KOP contents and are also widely used industrially in the synthesis of
 poly(acrylonitrile) PAN that is suitable for carbon fiber. Solution-based free radical
 polymerizations (FRPs) are conducted in the presence of an organic solvent, a radical initiator,
 and monomer. The presence of water can be problematic due to the solubility of PAN, which can
 limit yields and molecular weights (MW).

 Alternatively, emulsion-based FRPs are conducted using excess water, a water-soluble initiator,
 monomer, and a surfactant. The surfactant limits coalescence of monomer/polymer micelles,
 resulting in small micellar structures which can polymerize rapidly to high MWs and high yields.
 Currently, recovery of AN produced from nitrilation requires a large amount of organic solvent,
 in the KOP. The presence of large amounts of organic solvent in an emulsion FRP can be
 problematic due to miscibility of the water/organic phases, resulting in low yields and low MWs.
 Another key motivation in defining the polymerization approach lies in the choice of KOP
 organic solvent. We were interested in replacement of DMF and DMSO with Cyrene, which is a
 bio-based and non-toxic solvent that possesses similar polarity characteristics.

 We initially explored a solution-based FRP using a variety of organic solvents, but in all cases
 found that the addition of MeOH and water led to low PAN MWs and subsequent unsuitability
 as a carbon-fiber precursor (Table 1). Interestingly, preliminary data suggests that higher yields
 and MWs are possible using Cyrene as the polymerization solvent.

 Since emulsion FRP in the presence of DMF appears promising as a methodology for crude KOP
 polymerization, we have also investigated the scalability of the reaction conditions which
 provide the highest yields of PAN. Initial results show that similar yields can be maintained after
 increasing reaction scales 5x with high Mw accessed as well. As such, we have identified
 polymerization conditions in the presence of DMF as ideal for application to direct KOP solution
 polymerization. Future methodology development involves the exploration of DMSO as the gas-
 stripping organic solvent in both Nitrilation and polymerization conditions.

 Polymerization of crude knock-out pot solutions:

 Previously, we have encountered low AN recoveries from the Nitrilation process, accessing
 approximately a maximum 56% recovery yield. Recent experiments have also shown difficulty
 in accessing KOP solutions which have sufficient AN concentration to be directly applied toward
 polymerization conditions without any subsequent purifications. To address this concern, we
 have designed a water-cooled condenser for the Nitrilation reactor. However, part backorders
 have delayed implementation and improvement of AN recoveries.

 9

 Initial direct polymerizations of KOP solutions containing AN concentrations ranging from 2%
 to 10% were conducted using the ideal conditions previously identified. Unfortunately, these
 polymerizations only resulted in trace amounts of products. To eliminate potential quenching of
 the polymerization by a contaminant present in the KOP we also conducted polymerizations
 using the same KOP solutions, but with added AN. Using this approach, products with similar
 yields and MWs were accessed, proving that there is nothing inhibitive present in the KOP.

 Previous work has shown spinning band distillation to be an effective way to separate AN from
 other Nitrilation products. Although this technique can be readily implemented to isolate
 Nitrilation-produced AN for PAN synthesis, lower-energy and scalable routes toward PAN
 generation are desirable. To further evaluate KOP solutions for polymerization under more
 desirable reaction conditions we “concentrated” the crude KOP solution using a rotary
 evaporator. In this process, the crude KOP solution was heated to 50 ⁰C and stirred under
 reduced pressure, where the volatiles were then condensed into a cooled collection flask. Using
 1
 H NMR spectroscopy, the contents of the collection flask were characterized, finding solutions
 with up to 30% AN, but also still containing significant amounts of DMF, MeOH, and water. In
 polymerization reactions we found that these concentrations were not sufficient to produce
 adequate PAN yields, only accessing products up to 5% yield.

 Polymer characterization:

 Compositional, molecular weight, and thermal analyses of the polymers synthesized has been
 carried out as a fast-response method toward relation of polymerization results toward carbon-
 fiber PAN suitability. Quantification of the relative amounts of AN and MA incorporated into
 the final polymer have been identified using 1H NMR spectroscopy, finding that despite its
 relatively higher rate of propagation, methyl acrylate is consistently incorporated proportionally
 to its intended stoichiometry. As has been discussed in previous reports, Gel Permeation
 Chromatography has been performed to determine the relative MWs and dispersities of the
 isolated polymers.

 To validate the isolate PAN materials as adequate precursors for carbon fiber, thermal
 gravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) of the polymers
 synthesized has been performed. TGA allows measurement of mass loss as specific
 temperatures, a key parameter to ensure the desired 50% carbon yield at 3000 ⁰C during carbon
 fiber graphitization. Interestingly, under the tested polymerization conditions emulsion
 polymerizations with no organic solvent had the lowest mass yield at 900 ⁰C, while
 polymerizations in the presence of DMF or Cyrene had yields ranging from 44 – 48% (Table 6).
 This indicates that various conditions do not have a substantial impact on thermal decomposition
 properties.

 Conclusions:

 • The key barrier to successful polymerization of crude KOP solutions lies in low product
 recoveries, which results in diluted solutions of AN that are unable to be efficiently
 polymerized to PAN in high yields. To increase the concentration of AN recovered in the
 KOP, installation of a water-cooled condenser onto the KOP is ongoing and will be
 completed shortly. Once installed, product recoveries will be determined.
 • If the addition of the condenser does not result in sufficient AN recoveries for crude
 polymerization, existing protocols for AN separation will be implemented. As such, there
 is minimal risk in the ultimate objective of this project, which is to evaluate PAN
 synthesized from this nitrilation technology as a precursor for carbon fiber.
 • Notably, studies of model polymerization conditions mimicking the KOP contents has
 resulted in determination of ideal KOP concentrations and has produced PAN with ideal
 molecular weight and thermal properties. This process has been proven to be
 reproducible at increased reaction scales.
 • PAN synthesized using model KOP reaction conditions has been sent to Zoltek for fiber
 spinning and further tensile, viscosity, and conductivity analysis.
 • Future optimization of the polymerization process will be an iterative approach, where
 targeted KOP concentrations will be adjusted depending on MW, thermal, and tensile
 analyses performed collaboratively between NLR and Zoltek.

 Provide key physical properties of PAN and compare to conventional PAN properties
 needed for carbon fiber production. Adjust reaction conditions / knock solution
 concentrations if needed.

 For this annual milestone, key physical properties of PAN produced from a concentrated
 nitrilation knockout pot solution have been measured and comparison to PAN produced under
 model conditions and metrics from PAN carbon fiber precursors is provided. Nitrilation of
 methyl acrylate to produce acrylonitrile was performed using optimized high-throughput
 conditions and collected by condensing into a chilled solution of DMF. The concentration of the
 AN in the knockout solution was determined to be too dilute for ideal polymerization conditions.
 Accordingly, the knockout solution was distilled under reduced pressure to concentrate the AN
 for polymerization. The AN collected from the distillation overhead, was polymerized to PAN
 using an emulsion-based free radical polymerization approach. The composition, polymer
 molecular weight, intrinsic viscosity, melting temperature, melting temperature heat flow, and
 degree of cyclization were determined and are presented relative to PAN synthesized under
 similar model polymerization conditions. Subsequent nitrilations and distillations were
 conducted to tune the concentrations of the AN solutions for polymerization, finding minimizing
 side reactivity through radical inhibition and minimization of methanol content to be key to
 accessing PAN materials with high MWs.

 Carbon fiber is broadly viewed as a key material for light-weighting vehicles and provides a
 pathway for significant reductions in fuel consumption without compromising vehicle safety.
 However, widespread implementation of carbon fiber remains challenging due to high prices and
 environmental footprints, largely stemming from the production of the base chemical,
 acrylonitrile. Today acrylonitrile is made from the ammoxidation of fossil derived propylene.
 The reaction is practiced at very large scales with individual plants capable of producing ACN at
 > 250 kTa. However, propylene ammoxidation is notoriously dirty having a carbon footprint of
 XXX / kg ACN and produces toxic hydrogen cyanide as a byproduct. The large GHG footprint
 of AN results in an even larger GHG footprint of carbon fiber, because in the production of
 carbon fiber, AN is polymerized to PAN and then carbonized to produce carbon fiber. This
 process requires ~ 2lbs of AN for every pound of carbon fiber. Accordingly, to lower the carbon
 footprint of carbon fiber a greener route to the base chemical AN is needed. NLR has developed
 an alternative chemistry to propylene ammoxidation dubbed “nitrilation.” The nitrilation reaction
 produces ACN from a variety of green precursors that are available on the open market.

 Carbon fiber is produced from precursor polymers which must possess specific properties to
 manufacture carbon fiber with sufficient mechanical properties for automotive applications.
 Poly(acrylonitrile) is the most common precursor polymer material and accounts for over 50% of
 the cost of carbon fiber. NLR has developed a new technology, termed “Nitrilation” which
 produces high-value acrylonitrile using a vapor-phase flow reactor.

 In the nitrilation process, methyl acrylate (MA) is converted to acrylonitrile (AN), water, and
 methanol (MeOH) in equimolar amounts using a TiO2 catalyst and ammonia gas (Figure 1). The
 products are collected through bubbling of the gasses through a knockout pot containing a chilled
 stripping solvent, DMF. The main advantages of this process lie in its inherently low toxicity and
 ease of integration into existing manufacturing processes for carbon fiber. Furthermore, PAN can
 be synthesized in conditions using either DMF or water as the primary solvent, offering
 opportunities for streamlined synthesis of PAN from AN generated via nitrilation without time
 and energy intensive separations that are required in the incumbent propylene ammoxidation

 Herein we present the results for the current milestone objective, which is to synthesize PAN
 from AN generated by nitrilation and to validate its properties relative to the PAN that is
 currently used for carbon fiber manufacturing.

 Methods

 The general process for the nitrilation, AN recovery, polymer synthesis, and polymer
 characterization is shown in Figure 3. The experimental details for each process step is further
 described in this section.

 Figure 3: General overview of process steps. The AN is first synthesized through nitrilation (blue)
 where it is collected with equimolar amounts of water and MeOH using a knockout pot containing
 DMF (yellow). The knockout pot contents are concentrated into polymerizable concentrations and
 then polymerized into PAN using emulsion polymerization (green). The PAN products are then
 characterized to evaluate suitability as a carbon fiber precursor.

 Nitrilation:

 Nitrilation experiments are carried out using a heated vapor-phase flow reactor with an in-line
 FTIR for direct product quantification. In a typical nitrilation experiment, the methyl acrylate
 substrate is injected into a heated nitrogen carrier stream (750 sccm) at 0.25 mL/min. It is then
 mixed with NH3 gas (500 sccm) where it then passes over ~40 g of TiO2 catalyst in a heated
 reactor bed at 310 ⁰C. The exhaust gasses are then diluted with 500 sccm N2 and passed through
 an FTIR cell for quantification. The gasses are then bubbled through a knockout pot (KOP)
 containing glass beads and chilled DMF (0 ⁰C) with a cooled condenser (0 ⁰C). Notably, any
 gasses which do not condense in the KOP are vented out. After the reaction is complete, the
 contents of the KOP are transferred into a storage vessel stored at 5 ⁰C without any additives.
 Products collected in the KOP are quantified using 1H NMR spectroscopy and compared to
 yields measured by FTIR to determine product recovery.

 14

 Characterization of KOP contents:
 1
 H NMR was selected for characterization of the crude contents of the KOP pot. Through
 comparison of the known DMF concentrations to the monomer peaks, the amount of monomer
 can be readily quantified. Notably, methanol and water signals are overlapped by the DMF and
 are unable to be precisely quantified. A key challenge in accessing high product recovery stems
 from the high volatility of AN and MA, with a boiling point of 77 ⁰C and 80 ⁰C, respectively.
 MeOH also has a low boiling point (64.7 ⁰C), while water (100 ⁰C) is expected to be collected
 more efficiently due to a high boiling point.

 Concentration of KOP solution by distillation:

 To increase the relative concentrations of AN to DMF for polymerization the contents of the
 KOP solution were distilled at 80 C under reduced pressure. DMF, MeOH, and AN are known to
 form a ternary azeotrope, which makes isolation of pure AN impractical. Under these conditions,
 solutions ranging from 2:1 DMF:AN to 1:1.4 DMF:AN were readily accessed.

 Polymerization:

 Typical polymerization experiments using model compounds are conducted using well-
 established emulsion free radical polymerization approaches with additional amounts of organic
 solvent to mimic knockout pot solutions. The contents of these reactions include water, AN,
 MeOH, DMF, ammonium persulfate (initiator) and DOWFAX 8390 as surfactant. In these
 procedures, all components are sparged with N2 and then loaded into a flask under N2
 atmosphere, followed by heating to 65 ⁰C for 24 hours. The polymer is isolated by washing with
 aqueous MgSO4 and filtration, the dried at 70 ⁰C until it reaches a constant weight.
 Polymerizations conducted from the concentrated KOP solutions are conducted in the same
 manner, with the exception that the KOP solution containing AN, MeOH, MA, and DMF is
 degassed while cooled to minimize volatilization and loss of the monomer.

 Polymer Characterization:

 The composition of the PAN is determined using H NMR spectroscopy. The molecular weight
 and branching analysis is performed using Size Exclusion Chromatography coupled with Multi-
 angle Light Scattering, Viscometry, and Refractive Index detector systems. The thermal stability
 is evaluated using Thermal Gravimetric Analysis. The degree of cyclization and temperature of
 cyclization is determined using Differential Scanning Calorimetry.

 To meet the objectives outlined in this milestone, high throughput conditions were developed for
 generating high quantities of AN while minimizing product losses due to uncondensed products
 in the knockout pot. The nitrilation process yields an equimolar amount of AN, MeOH, and
 water, as well as any unreacted MA. At steady-state conditions before catalyst deactivation
 yields typically range from 94-96%. As catalyst deactivation occurs, AN yields decrease while
 MA yields increase, as can be seen in Figure 4. Typical experiments are stopped when MA
 levels reach about 5%, in order to minimize the amount of MA incorporation into the PAN
 material and maintain suitable properties as a carbon fiber precursor.

 Figure 4: Results of nitrilation showing yields of acrylonitrile (blue) and methyl acrylate (red) as
 determined by online gas-phase FTIR.

 Efforts outlined in previous quarterly and milestone reports have highlighted the development of
 suitable polymerization conditions that can include both organic and aqueous conditions while
 producing PAN at high yields and with the desired properties. To summarize, we have found that
 we can produce PAN at 50-60% yield using emulsion polymerization conditions with up to 2
 volumetric equivalents of DMF to AN. However, it remains challenging to access sufficiently
 concentrated solutions off of the nitrilation reactor for direct polymerization.

 Distillation at moderate temperatures of these knockout solutions has readily provided solutions
 for direct polymerization. After optimization of distillation conditions, solutions containing a
 mixture of AN, MeOH, and DMF were readily obtained. It should be noted that due to the
 relatively lower boiling of MeOH to the other components, MeOH is recovered in relatively
 higher amounts to AN. Furthermore, while the relative molar amounts of the products can be
 readily quantified using 1H NMR spectroscopy, the precise masses of each component are
 difficult to determine.

 To evaluate the products of nitrilation as a drop-in feedstock for carbon fiber precursors we have
 synthesized PAN under model conditions (i.e. commercially-sourced AN) and compared the key
 physical properties to that of PAN synthesized from nitrilation products. In this case, the key
 physical properties being used as metrics for suitability as a carbon fiber precursor include
 molecular weight, branching, degree of cyclization and temperature of cyclization, and thermal
 stability. In general, high molecular weight (Mw > 250 kDa) is desired for PAN used in carbon
 fiber, but successful instances of lower Mw’s have been reported as well.iv Efficient cyclization of
 PAN during the oxidative stabilization step is key to minimizing defects and accessing robust
 tensile properties. The degree, or efficiency, of cyclization can be promoted by using PAN with
 minimal degrees of branching and measured using DSC. Finally, typical PAN precursors retain
 40-45% of their mass post-carbonization. Lower mass yields increase the overall cost of carbon
 fiber.

 PAN from nitrilation (blue) are shown. The results of GPC analysis in 4-A show that the model-

 PAN is a higher molecular weight (Mw = 297 kDa) while the nitrilation-PAN has a lower Mw at
 100 kDa. This decrease in molecular weight can be attributed to the relatively higher MeOH

 increase reaction yields but also produces polymers of lower molecular weight. The branching
 analysis, shown in 4-B, indicates that there is relatively less branching using the nitrilation-PAN,

 The thermal analysis of these PAN materials reveals that the nitrilation PAN cyclizes at lower

 adding in additional methyl acrylate during polymerization, which promotes polymer orientation

 Next Steps

 To meet the next objectives of this project, distillation protocols will be further developed in
 order to reduce the relative amounts of MeOH and increase overall recoveries and yields of AN.
 Large amounts of AN are required in order to generate 1 kg of PAN for carbon fiber, and so
 another focus will be to continue to minimize catalyst deactivation during nitrilation through
 modified reaction conditions.

 Conclusions

 In summary, we have successfully generated PAN from nitrilation products using minimal
 purification techniques. After characterization of this PAN and comparison to PAN synthesized
 under model conditions, we have found that largely it meets the requirements as a precursor for
 carbon fiber materials. Ongoing work is focused on polishing nitrilation solutions with less
 relative MeOH content in order to increase the PAN molecular weight.

 In these experiments, multiple conditions were observed that produced high AN yields without
 apparent catalyst deactivation. While the most important parameter appears to be weight hourly
 space velocity, Experiment #8 produced no observable deactivation through at least 20 hours run
 time at a flow rate of 0.109 mL/min, which representing a more than 40% increase in throughput
 compared to the baseline flow rate of 0.077 mL/min while maintaining similar yields of 90-92%.
 Additionally, these yields were maintained without catalyst regeneration, which was employed
 by Karp et al. every 12 hours. While the optimization is ongoing, the conditions of Experiment
 #8 were selected for the 100-g production run.

 The production run was conducted in two segments. The first, conducted over two days, fed ~82
 mL MA through the reactor, recovering 86 g combined product in the knockout pot (KOP) for
 ~79% recovery of the theoretical mass. This portion was separated from the N,N-
 dimethylformamide (DMF) recovery solution by spinning band distillation. The distillation cut
 collected distillate until the pot temperature reached 132 °C, with a corresponding vapor
 temperature of 98.2 °C. However, the distillation recovered only 32.8 g distillate, a recovery of
 only 38% of the product solution. The distillation mass balance was 99.2%, indicating that
 almost no AN was lost during distillation. Thus, the AN losses likely occurred mainly at the
 KOP. TEMPO was added to both the KOP and the distillation collection pot to inhibit losses by
 depolymerization; thus, the losses were likely due to evaporation from the KOP or
 polymerization before reaching the KOP.

 The second segment of the production run was also conducted over two days, but fed ~145 mL
 MA through the reactor. The KOP solution volume was increased to 300 mL DMF (from 200
 mL DMF in the first segment) to increase recovery, but the KOP solution recovered only 98.2 g
 product, a recovery of ~50% of the theoretical mass. The KOP solutions may thus be reaching a
 saturation point beyond which no further recovery occurs. However, the distillation of this
 second condensate collected 61.4 g in the distillate for a recovery of 62% by mass. The mass
 balance on the second distillation run was 99.6%, similar to the first run. Together, the
 acrylonitrile-rich distillates totaled 94.0 g, reasonably approaching the milestone target of 100 g.
 These distillates were combined and shipped to the University of Kentucky for polymerization
 experiments.

 The first segment of the production run produced a distillate that was monophasic when first
 collected, but formed two phases after storing in a cold room (~4 °C) overnight. The distillate
 from the second segment of the production run remained monophasic even in the cold room, and
 when all three phases were combined, the solution was also monophasic. The three phases were
 analyzed by NMR and GC-FID-MS.

 Next steps: While the nitrilation reaction itself operated as expected to complete this milestone,
 significant optimization remains to improve capture of the reaction products in the knockout pot
 and separation of the products from the DMF solvent. In particular, elucidating the saturation
 point of the products in the DMF solvent by collecting a small portion of the solvent at different
 points in the reaction could significantly improve the efficiency of reactor operations and inform
 whether DMF is the best solvent for this application. In the distillation of the products from the
 DMF solvent, establishing distillate composition as a function of temperature and pressure would
 inform the conditions at which to stop distillate collection to better tune the composition of the
 distillate. In the present distillation runs, while there were no clear distillation cuts, it was
 apparent by the end of the run that the distillate composition had changed to (presumably) mostly
 water as the distillate began to collect in narrower portions of the spinning band apparatus,
 indicative of a higher surface tension. Additionally, the present distillation was conducted at
 atmospheric pressure, while vacuum distillation could improve AN recovery and/or distillate
 composition, as could distillation from a solvent other than DMF.

 Conclusions: We have produced 94 g of acrylonitrile-rich distillate, reasonably approaching the
 milestone target of 100 g, and shipped the sample to the University of Kentucky for
 polymerization experiments. Notably, this sample was produced with 40% higher throughput and
 less catalyst deactivation than previously observed in this system. Analysis of the distillate
 suggested a composition leaner in acrylonitrile and richer in methanol and water than reported in
 the literature. The reasons for this difference could include a lower collection efficiency in the
 knockout pot (favoring methanol and water over acrylonitrile) and extending the distillation to a
 higher final temperature, thereby bringing more water into the distillate. In addition to further
 increasing reactor throughput, future work should focus on improving the efficiency and
 tunability of the product collection from the reactor and from the distillation step.

 Acrylonitrile is a commodity chemical produced at over 5 million tons per year, with a global
 market over $10 billion and a projected annual growth rate over 4%. It is a key intermediate in
 the synthesis of many chemicals and products, including polyacrylonitrile (PAN) fibers. PAN
 fibers are the primary precursor to carbon fibers, which itself is a market growing rapidly at
 10.9% per year and expected to exceed 250,000 tons per year demand by 2025.2, 3 Currently,
 most acrylonitrile is produced by ammoxidation of non-renewable propylene.4 The
 ammoxidation reaction produces toxic byproducts, such as HCN, and is highly exothermic,
 presenting reactor heat management challenges. Recently, Karp et al. demonstrated an alternative
 acrylonitrile synthesis from renewable esters by an endothermic nitrilation reaction.5
 Acrylonitrile production can take one of multiple pathways, depending on the substrate. From
 hydroxyacids, such as 3-hydroxypropanoic or lactic acid, acrylonitrile is produced in three steps.
 First, the hydroxyacid is converted to a corresponding ester by reactive distillation. Second, the
 hydroxyester is dehydrated to form an acrylate ester. Third, the acrylate ester undergoes
 aminolysis and dehydration to produce acrylonitrile.6-8 The related compound b-propiolactone

 22

 undergoes a similar ammonolysis and nitrilation reaction, and can be produced by carbonylation
 of renewable ethylene oxide that is produced from renewable ethanol,9-12 or electrochemically
 from CO2 and H2O.13 The chemistry of these pathways is shown in Figure 1. The overall process
 integrated within first- and second-generation biorefineries is shown in Figure .

 While the original nitrilation process envisioned production of highly pure (97%) acrylonitrile
 for the production of the polyacrylonitrile (PAN) precursor to carbon fibers, subsequent work
 demonstrated that the crude mixture recovered from distillation (consisting of acrylonitrile,
 methanol, and water) could serve as the polymerization feedstock. In this mode, methanol was
 able to function as a chain-transfer agent promoting polymerization and water assisted with
 emulsion formation. Removal of the two downstream distillation columns required to recover
 high-purity acrylonitrile was estimated to reduce heat burden by 35% and imported electricity by
 40%. A comparison of the two processes is shown in Figure 9.

 In the nitrilation concept producing highly pure acrylonitrile, greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions
 were significantly reduced compared to an ammoxidation process. Cellulosic sugars provided
 additional benefits compared to first-generation sugars derived from corn starch, but even first
 generation sugars reduced GHG emissions by almost 10%. In the follow-on concept employing
 emulsion polymerization, GHG emissions were not calculated. In this milestone, we have
 updated the GHG emissions calculation to reflect the downstream process improvements. The
 results are summarized in Figure 10.

 Compared to petroleum (3.34 kg CO2e/kg acrylonitrile), 1G and 2G sugars exhibit lower GHG
 emissions (3.02 and 2.87 kg CO2e/kg acrylonitrile), a reduction of 9.5% and 14.1%, respectively.
 In updating the process model, we made two primary changes. First, we used methanol rather
 than ethanol for the esterifying agent, producing methyl 3-hydroxypropionic acid rather than
 ethyl 3-hydroxypropionic acid and methyl acrylate rather than ethyl acrylate. This has the effect
 of reducing the temperature needed during distillation of the ester and reactive distillation of the
 acrylate dehydration product. Second, we removed the two final distillation columns necessary to
 reach 97% purity. The effect of these changes was, surprisingly, to increase the GHG emissions

 24

 to 3.34 kg CO2e/kg acrylonitrile, equivalent to petroleum-derived acrylonitrile. Possible
 contributing factors to this counterintuitive result include the following:

 1. Ethanol is sources from renewable resources, while methanol is produced from
 natural gas. Thus, the process benefits for reducing the energy required to distill the
 esterified intermediates was offset by the use of a fossil-based esterifying agent.
 2. Despite the estimations in Meek et al., revisiting the process model revealed that the
 final two distillation columns consumed a relatively small fraction of the overall heat
 demand, so removing these columns had minimal effect.
 3. Carbon intensities and the way they are calculated change over time. Thus, comparing
 the improved process in 2022 units to the original and petroleum-based processes in
 2016 units is not completely apples-to-apples. We note that the value for petroleum-
 derived acrylonitrile included in the literature was based on proprietary information
 (Eco-Invent database), which was not accessible to this project.

 Conclusions:

 We have updated process models to compare production of polymerization-grade acrylonitrile
 from petroleum via ammoxidation of propylene and from biomass via fermentation,
 esterification, and nitrilation. Specifically, the process models from the original concept were
 updated to reflect less stringent polymerization requirements, and to use methanol rather than
 ethanol as the esterifying agent. Surprisingly, the improved process resulted in an apparent
 increase in GHG emissions, with both petroleum-derived acrylonitrile and bio-based acrylonitrile
 emitting 3.34 kg CO2e/kg acrylonitrile. The original nitrilation concept was modeled to emit 2.87
 kg CO2e/kg acrylonitrile, a 14% reduction. There is some level of uncertainty in these values due
 to changes in the LCA calculations over time, but there are also opportunities to significantly
 improve the emissions of the bio-based processes.
