Approximating the Fourier Transform of Ring-Like Images: the Focal Expansion Preprint: APS/123-QED
Filip Niewinski, Michael D. Johnson

Abstract
We derive and showcase a novel approach to approximating Fourier transforms in higher dimensions, focusing specifically on the case of 2D radially concentrated (‘ring-like’) functions. We first reduce the problem to that of evaluating the Hankel transforms of each angular mode of the image and then use our focal expansion to approximate these remaining Hankel transforms. Our method provides a single approximation that remains accurate from small to large spatial frequencies, bridging regimes where moment-based or large-frequency asymptotic expansions are individually reliable. We explore a series of examples, showing that the leading focal term provides an accurate global approximation for a broad range of functions. We demonstrate the utility of this approximation by examining the interferometric response for toy models of a black hole’s “photon ring,” highlighting the application to experiments designed to measure this feature such as the Black Hole Explorer.

Introduction

I.1
Motivation

Fourier analysis is an indispensable part of the mathematical and physical sciences
9
;
25
;
27
. However, analytic Fourier transforms are often unavailable, especially in higher dimensions – a brief perusal of integral tables will show those containing one dimensional (1D) Fourier transforms to be the most expansive, with the length decreasing as we increase the dimensionality. Here, we focus on analytic approximations for the two-dimensional (2D) Fourier transform, with higher-dimensional generalization relegated to the appendices.

The motivation for our focus on the 2D Fourier transform comes from studies of black hole images, a new field prompted by the successful imaging of the nuclear supermassive black holes in the Milky Way and in the M87 galaxy using astronomical interferometry by the Event Horizon Telescope collaboration
(
3
;
2
, EHT;)
. These images appear as approximately circular rings, but with non-trivial radial and azimuthal structure. Moreover, these images are expected to include a series of exponentially sharper rings from strong gravitational lensing near the black hole
5
;
22
;
13
;
18
. This rich “photon ring” structure both cleanly encodes the black hole properties and dominates the interferometric response on long baselines, which sample corresponding large spatial wavenumbers of the image, providing a crisp motivation for improved interferometric studies of black holes
(
18
)
. Hence, deriving model-agnostic predictions for the interferometric response and understanding their connection to the image features is of intense interest in shaping and interpreting the results from future experiments with longer baselines such as the Black Hole Explorer
17
.

Specifically, the data one obtains from an astronomical interferometer is
not
the image as it appears on the sky but is instead a discrete (and often sparse) sampling of the image’s two-dimensional Fourier transform
28
. Given that we expect images of black holes to be ring-like this raises a natural question: what aspect of the measured Fourier transform carries information about this approximate circular symmetry of the image? And how do small perturbations away from a perfect circle (thickness, shape, etc.) manifest in the interferometric response? While the asymptotic Fourier response is straightforward to compute in certain toy models of black hole images
(
18
;
14
, e.g.,)
, the generalization to more realistic astronomical images with non-trivial radial and azimuthal structure is a surprisingly difficult problem to deal with analytically. In particular, the envelope of the periodic visibility response is especially challenging to compute in general cases of interest. For an example of some of the difficulties previous investigations have encountered when applying standard approximation techniques to this problem, see
16
.

Here, we present a new analytic approximation –
the focal expansion
– that resolves some of these difficulties.
We first split the function into angular modes, in 2D this amounts to expanding
f(r,\theta)
into a Fourier
series
at each radius

\displaystyle f(r,\theta)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}g_{n}(r)e^{in\theta}

(I.1)

with the coefficients now dependent on the distance away from the origin. One can then show (see section
II.1
below) that the 2D Fourier transform can be written as

\mathcal{F}[f](\rho,\phi)=2\pi\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{in(\phi-\pi/2)}\int_{0}^{\infty}rg_{n}(r)J_{n}(2\pi r\rho)\,dr

(I.2)

where
(\rho,\phi)
are spherical coordinates in Fourier space and the
J_{n}
are Bessel functions. The remaining integrals over the angular modes are the so called Hankel transforms
24
and the focal expansion is an analytic method for approximating them. Our approach provides a perturbative series with each term given by a superposition of Bessel functions, each with frequency determined by the radius of the ring and modulated in amplitude by functions encoding the radial profile of the ring. In addition, the focal expansion is
uniformly valid
, meaning it performs well over the
full
range of arguments, without uncontrollably diverging from the true function at either small or large wavenumber, in contrast to Taylor or asymptotic expansions respectively. In fact, although the focal expansion is a series of infinitely many terms, due to this universal validity we found that just the leading order term sufficed in most practical situations.

There are two important caveats to the statements above. The first is that the angular decomposition
eq.

I.1
might be difficult to perform analytically as it does involve evaluating Fourier series integrals. The second caveat is that we have not escaped taking a Fourier transform altogether – in the focal expansion the role of modulating the amplitude of the Bessel functions is played by, essentially,
one
dimensional Fourier transforms of the radial profiles
g_{n}(r)
of the image. What this means, however, is that our scheme offers a dimensional reduction: evaluating higher dimensional Fourier transforms (at least for functions which manifest approximate spherical symmetry) can be reduced to evaluating 1D Fourier transforms. Alternatively, one can think of the focal expansion as a way to exchange the relatively obscure Hankel transforms of
eq.

I.2
for 1D Fourier transforms. As we remarked earlier, the 1D Fourier transform is more analytically tractable than either its higher dimensional version or the Hankel transform, so this is already an improvement.

The outline of the rest of the paper is as follows. We first finish section
I
by providing a representative example highlighting the application and properties of our approach. We then derive the full focal expansion in section
II
and provide a series of examples probing the performance of our scheme in Section
III
. Finally, we offer some brief concluding remarks in Section
IV
.

Demonstration

We now provide an example of our method at work. Consider a distant observer looking at a black hole. Let

This motivates an interest in the 2D Fourier transform of a ring of radius

\displaystyle f(r,\theta)\propto\log\left(\frac{c}{|r-a|}\right)\quad\text{when }r\approx a,

(I.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&f(r,\theta)=\\
&\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}\sqrt{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{3}\sin\theta\right)e^{-\frac{1}{5}\left(\frac{a}{r}-1\right)}\log\left[1+\frac{a^{4}}{(r-a)^{4}}\right].\end{split}

To summarize, our procedure allows us to perform a type of dimensional reduction, providing a remarkably accurate and robust approximation that reduces the two dimensional Fourier transform to expressions in terms of a one dimensional Fourier transform. II
Derivation

II.1
Preliminaries

For the remainder of the main body of this note we focus on the 2D Fourier transform, delegating the higher dimensional generalizations to Appendix
A
. Using the spherical coordinates
(r,\theta)
we can expand any function
f(\vec{X})
into a
\theta
-Fourier
series

f(\vec{X})=f(r,\theta)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}g_{n}(r)e^{in\theta},

(II.1)

where
5
5

5

Since our ultimate interest lies in ring-like shapes one might wonder why we don’t exploit this fact from the start, as in
14
or
16
. The problem here is that one cannot unambiguously assign a ring of some variable thickness and shape to an image. The generality of
eq.

II.1
saves us from having to introduce such artificial complexities but comes at a cost of making non-circular shapes less natural – see section
III.1.3
.

g_{n}(r)\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(r,\theta)e^{-in\theta}d\theta.

(II.2)

We now use the Anger-Jacobi expansion
29

e^{iz\cos\theta}=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}i^{m}J_{m}(z)e^{im\theta},

(II.3)

where
J_{m}
is the
m

J_{-m}=(-1)^{m}J_{m}
. We will make repeated use of
eq.

II.3
, one can understand it concisely as the statement that
i^{m}J_{m}(z)
is the
m
-th coefficient of the
\theta
-Fourier series of
e^{iz\cos\theta}
, with

a constant. Taking

II.3
,
I.14
and
II.1
together we get

\mathcal{F}[f](\rho,\phi)=2\pi\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{in(\phi-\pi/2)}\int_{0}^{\infty}rg_{n}(r)J_{n}(2\pi r\rho)\,dr

(II.4)

and so we see that once we separate our function into angular modes the process of taking the Fourier transform is reduced to performing weighted integrals of Bessel functions. By itself this decomposition is not new, indeed, the integrals in
eq.

II.4
are commonly known as Hankel transforms
24
. The principal question we are interested in is this: what can be said about the general behavior of such integrals? For a generic
g_{n}(r)
it is unlikely the integral can be performed in closed form, the standard approach would then be to look for an asymptotic approximation of some kind (see for instance
8
). We will instead take a novel approach motivated by the following set of qualitative observations.

The leading-order term in
J_{n}(2\pi r\rho)
as
\rho\rightarrow\infty
is
29

\displaystyle\begin{split}J_{n}(2\pi r\rho)\;\Big|_{\rho\gg 1}\approx\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{r\rho}}&\cos\left(-2\pi r\rho+n\pi/2+\pi/4\right),\end{split}

(II.5)

provided

. Now, assume

is sufficiently tightly peaked around some radius

so that to leading

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}&rg_{n}(r)J_{n}(2\pi r\rho)\,\text{d}r\;\Big|_{\rho\gg 1}\\
&\approx\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{a}{\rho}}\,\text{Re}\left[e^{i\pi(n/2+1/4)}\int_{0}^{\infty}g_{n}(r)e^{-2\pi ir\rho}dr\right].\end{split}

(II.6)

The last integral is almost the Fourier transform of
g_{n}(r)
, only the integration limits are incorrect. To remedy this, define an extension
G_{n}(r-a)
of
g_{n}(r)
to all of
\mathbb{R}
, shifted to make our peak centered at the origin. We will have more to say about choosing an extension in Section

but for now note that one might pick, for instance, a trivial extension with
G_{n}(r)
vanishing for

(although we will often make a less trivial choice for the sake of analytic tractability, e.g., fig.
2
in Section

). We then have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}&rg_{n}(r)J_{n}(2\pi r\rho)\,\text{d}r\;\Big|_{\rho\gg 1}\\
&\approx\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{a}{\rho}}\,\text{Re}\left[e^{i\pi(n/2+1/4)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{n}(r-a)e^{-2\pi ir\rho}dr\right],\end{split}

(II.7)

where we assume the effect of extending
g_{n}
to
G
is negligible compared to the leading behavior.
Provided
G_{n}(r)
is an even function so that
\widehat{G}_{n}(\rho)
is real (we will analyze the general case later),
eq.

II.7
becomes approximately

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}&rg_{n}(r)J_{n}(2\pi r\rho)\,\text{d}r\;\Big|_{\rho\gg 1}\\
&\approx\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{a}{\rho}}\,\text{Re}\left[e^{i\pi(-2a\rho+n/2+1/4)}\widehat{G}_{n}(\rho)\right].\end{split}

(II.8)

Applying the Bessel asymptotic

II.5

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle rg_{n}(r)J_{n}(2\pi r\rho)\,\text{d}r\;\Big|_{\rho\gg 1}\approx a\widehat{G}_{n}(\rho)J_{n}(2\pi a\rho).

(II.9)

This simple result turns out to be more correct than one would expect, to see why we now look at the case

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}&rg_{n}(r)J_{n}(2\pi r\rho)\,\text{d}r\;\Big|_{\rho\ll 1}\\

&\approx aJ_{n}(2\pi a\rho)\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{n}(r-a)\,\text{d}r,\end{split}

(II.10)

where we once again assumed

\widehat{G}_{n}(0)
which is the same as

\widehat{G}_{n}(\rho)
to leading order for small

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle rg_{n}(r)J_{n}(2\pi r\rho)\,\text{d}r\;\Big|_{\rho\ll 1}\approx a\widehat{G}_{n}(\rho)J_{n}(2\pi a\rho).

(II.11)

We have thus reached the conclusion that the asymptotic behavior of our integral is

in both the
\rho\rightarrow 0
and
\rho\rightarrow\infty

eqs.

II.9
and
II.11

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}&rg_{n}(r)J_{n}(2\pi r\rho)\,\text{d}r\approx a\,\widehat{G}_{n}(\rho)J_{n}(2\pi a\rho),\end{split}

(II.12)

understanding the approximation to be

valid for all
\rho

The above result clearly motivates a strategy:
we will aim to develop a general asymptotic expansion based around the 1D Fourier transform of

with leading term given by
eq.

II.12

eqs.

II.9
and
II.11
into a single approximation

II.12
would then ideally yield an approximation without a regime of validity limited to small or large arguments and constrain the error in the intermediate regime between these two limits. Extending this behavior to subleading perturbative orders, however, is significantly more difficult and requires the development of several new analytic tools.

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{K,a}\left[f\right](\rho)=&\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x-a)K(2\pi x\rho)\,\text{d}x\\
=&\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{f}(2\pi p\rho)\widehat{K}(-p)e^{-i2\pi p(2\pi a\rho)}\text{d}p.\end{split}

(II.15)

Using
eq.

II.15
we can equivalently interpret our integral as a linear functional of
\hat{f}
instead of
f

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{K,a}[\widehat{f}\,](\rho)\equiv\mathcal{I}_{K,a}\left[f\right](\rho)

(II.16)

where we will distinguish between the two alternative interpretations using a hat. Now note that we seem to get an expansion akin to what we desired by Taylor expanding
\widehat{f}(2\pi p\rho)
inside the integral around
p=1/2\pi
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{K,a}\left[f\right](\rho)=&\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2\pi\rho)^{n}}{n!}\widehat{f}^{\,(n)}(\rho)\\
&\times\int_{\mathbb{R}}(p-1/2\pi)^{n}\widehat{K}(-p)e^{-i2\pi p(2\pi a\rho)}\,\text{d}p\\
=&\widehat{f}(\rho)K(2\pi a\rho)+\cdots.\end{split}

(II.17)

The first term is encouraging – indeed, if
f(x-a)=xg(x-a)
and
K=J_{n}
it contains precisely our desired leading term
eq.

II.12
– but we are not so lucky with all subsequent terms in the sum. The problem is the appearance of the bare factors of
\rho^{n}
in the non-leading terms of
eq.

II.17
. Being polynomial, they are badly behaved for large
\rho
.

Although the above expansion fails globally, it motivates the path forward; we must avoid all polynomial factors of
\rho
in our final expression as these are inherently local to the origin. We will instead make use of a special family of differential operators – which we name
focusing operators
6
6

6

We refer to these as focusing operators because they progressively isolate higher-order local information for a given function, but we largely picked the term for convenience and ease of use.
– and arrange the expansion around the action of these operators on
\widehat{f}(\rho)
and
K(2\pi a\rho)
. We name the resulting series a
focal expansion
. To fix the coefficients of this series we will Taylor expand it at
\rho=0
and match it to a Taylor expansion of
\mathcal{I}_{K,a}\left[f\right]
obtained directly from
eq.

II.15
.
While this last matching step seems inherently local to the origin, surprisingly it seems to capture global behavior of the integral when
K=J_{n}
(and, potentially, for a wider class of oscillatory kernels) as we will see when we consider examples of our approach in section
III
. We encourage a practically minded reader not interested in the abstract details of the matching procedure to skip to the summary of our method for the case
K=J_{n}
in section
II.6
.

We proceed by by first introducing the operators. Assuming
n\geq 0
we define the
n-th even and odd focusing operators
respectively as
7
7

7

Operators of this type are known to be relevant in Bessel function analysis – see
29
;
24
– and more broadly in studies of differential equations upon rewriting them using the Cauchy-Euler operator
z\partial
. We are, however, unaware of any prior work using them to create series expansions of integrals the way we do here.

\displaystyle\left(\partial\right)_{n}^{+}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\partial-\frac{n-1}{z}\right)\left(\partial-\frac{n-2}{z}\right)\cdots\left(\partial-\frac{1}{z}\right)\partial,

(II.18)

\displaystyle\left(\partial\right)_{n}^{-}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\partial-\frac{n}{z}\right)\left(\partial-\frac{n-1}{z}\right)\cdots\left(\partial-\frac{1}{z}\right),

(II.19)

where
z
is a generic variable (later we will need it to stand in for either of our
x
or
p
) and
\partial=d/dz
; we suppress
z
while writing
\partial
as the argument over which derivation is performed will always be clear from context. The notation is meant to call to mind the falling factorial
eq.

I.20
due to the visually similar product structure. For
n=0
we take the standard empty product convention so that
\left(\partial\right)^{\pm}_{0}=1
. When applying these to a function
f(z)
we will often refer to
\left(\partial\right)^{\pm}_{n}f
as the
n-th even/odd focus of f
or that we are
focusing f to n-th even/odd order
. Note also that
\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{n+1}=\left(\partial\right)^{-}_{n}\,\partial
. An alternative version with the operator products expanded can be shown to be

\displaystyle\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(n-1+k)!}{(n-k-1)!k!}\frac{1}{(-2z)^{k}}\partial^{n-k},

(II.20)

\displaystyle\left(\partial\right)^{-}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(n+k)!}{(n-k)!k!}\frac{1}{(-2z)^{k}}\partial^{n-k},

(II.21)

with
eq.

II.20
valid only for
n\geq 1
. Note that for large
z
, unless the derivatives of
f(z)
decay slower than their parent function,
eqs.

II.21
and
II.20
show the
n
-th foci of
f(z)
are both approximately equal to
f^{(n)}(z)
. This means that we do not suffer any additional power-law divergences at large argument not already present in
f(z)
or its derivatives when calculating the foci.

To understand what motivates the form and naming of
eqs.

II.19
and
II.18
, we apply
\left(\partial\right)^{-}_{2}
to an odd power series:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\partial\right)^{-}_{2}&\left(a_{0}z+a_{1}z^{3}+a_{2}z^{5}+\cdots\right)\\
=&\left(\partial-\frac{2}{z}\right)\left(0\cdot a_{0}+2a_{1}z^{2}+4a_{2}z^{4}+\cdots\right)\\
=&\,0\cdot a_{1}z+8a_{2}z^{3}+\cdots.\end{split}

(II.22)

Every factor of
\left(\partial-n/z\right)
simply lets us cancel the remaining leading term of the power series, the
n
being a combinatorial factor resulting from chaining multiple of these operator factors in a row. This behavior will continue to hold in general as long as we apply the odd/even operators to odd/even power series respectively. First note that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{n}z^{k}=&\frac{k!!}{(k-2n)!!}z^{k-n}\\
=&k(k-2)\cdots(k-2(n-1))\;z^{k-n},\end{split}

(II.23)

test

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\partial\right)^{-}_{n}z^{k}=&\frac{(k-1)!!}{(k-2n-1)!!}z^{k-n}\\
=&(k-1)(k-3)\cdots(k-2n+1)\;z^{k-n},\end{split}

(II.24)

both of which can be proved by induction in
n
. In particular
eqs.

II.23
and
II.24
imply that focusing a non-negative power of
z
of the same parity as the operator cannot lead to negative powers of
z
– the higher order foci will simply vanish:

\displaystyle\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{n}z^{2k}\Big|_{n>k}=\left(\partial\right)^{-}_{n}z^{2k+1}\Big|_{n>k}=0

(II.25)

so that the lowest powers one can obtain when focusing (with appropriate parity)
z^{2k}
and
z^{2k+1}
are
z^{k}
and
z^{k+1}
respectively. This will not be the case for powers of opposite parity to the operator,
\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{2}z=1/z
for instance, and is precisely the reason we introduced separate even and odd operators in the first place. Even functions should be focused at even order and odd functions at odd order to avoid introducing a divergence at the origin. Bringing together
eqs.

II.23
,
II.24
and
II.25
we see that

\displaystyle\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{n}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{2k}

\displaystyle=(2n)!!\,a_{n}z^{n}+O(z^{n+2}),

(II.26)

\displaystyle\left(\partial\right)^{-}_{n}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{2k+1}

\displaystyle=(2n)!!\,a_{n}z^{n+1}+O(z^{n+3}),

(II.27)

where in both cases the higher order terms only depend on
\{a_{k}\}_{k>n}
– all dependence on
\{a_{k}\}_{k<n}
has been removed in the process of focusing. The key takeaway from
eqs.

II.26
and
II.27
is that focusing operators isolate progressively higher orders of both the coefficients
a_{k}
and raw powers of
z
near the origin, all done in a manner that is well behaved for large arguments. These facts will prove crucial for approximating our integral,
eq.

II.15
.

We saw how raw powers of
z
behave when focused but, as we shall soon see, we will also need to describe how foci of an arbitrary function
h(z)
change when multiplied by a power. The relevant expressions are

\displaystyle\left(\partial\right)^{\pm}_{n}z^{2k}h(z)=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}(n)^{\downarrow}_{j}2^{j}\binom{k}{j}z^{2k-j}\left(\partial\right)^{\pm}_{n-j}h(z),

(II.28)

\displaystyle\left(\partial\right)^{\pm}_{n}z^{2k\mp 1}h(z)=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}(n)^{\downarrow}_{j}2^{j}\binom{k}{j}z^{2k-j\mp 1}\left(\partial\right)^{\mp}_{n-j}h(z),

(II.29)

both of which can be proven by induction in
n
. Note that multiplication by an even power leaves the parity of the foci of
h
involved in the expression invariant, while multiplication by an odd power flips it.

II.3
The Focal Expansion

Before we utilize focusing operators we first Taylor-expand the integral
eq.

II.15
at small
\rho
. Doing so will suggest how a focal expansion is to be assembled in the first place. We begin by assuming our kernel
K
is a purely even function – it will turn out the general case can be reduced to that of even kernels. Assume
\widehat{f}
and
K
to have Taylor series near the origin given by

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(z)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}z^{k},\quad K(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}z^{2k},

(II.30)

where the
c_{k}
must be understood as free parameters and the coefficients of the focal expansion cannot depend on them – we seek a series which, once found, is valid for any
\widehat{f}
without the need to recalculate any coefficients. By contrast, we assume the kernel is fixed so that every
\alpha_{k}
is a known constant on which the focal coefficients may depend. With that said, from
eqs.

II.15
,
II.13
and
II.30
we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{K,a}\left[f\right](\rho)=&\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}(2\pi\rho)^{k}\mathcal{F}\left[p^{k}\widehat{K}(-p)\right](2\pi a\rho)\\
=&\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}(i\rho)^{k}K^{(k)}(2\pi a\rho),\end{split}

(II.31)

where in the last line we also used the identity
\mathcal{F}\left[x^{n}h(x)\right](p)=(i/2\pi)^{n}\widehat{h}^{(n)}(p)
and, once again,
eq.

I.15
. Finally, using the Taylor expansion of the kernel we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{K,a}\left[f\right](\rho)=

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{k\geq 0}\;\sum_{m\geq k/2}c_{k}(2\pi a\rho)^{2m}\left[\alpha_{m}\,(2m)^{\downarrow}_{k}\left(\frac{i}{2\pi a}\right)^{k}\right].

(II.32)

The term in the square brackets should be understood as a constant for fixed
k
and
m
while the prefactor
c_{k}(2\pi a\rho)^{2m}
represents the functional behavior we need to match with the focal expansion. There are two independent dimensions at play here: we must match the
c_{k}
as well as the
\rho
behavior.

This is where the focusing operators come in. Note that using
eqs.

II.23
,
II.24
and
II.30
we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{k}\widehat{f}^{+}\right)(\rho)=&\sum_{l=k}^{\infty}c_{2l}\;\rho^{2l-k}2^{k}(l)^{\downarrow}_{k}\\
=&c_{2k}\;\rho^{k}\;(2k)!!+\cdots,\end{split}

(II.33)

filler

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\left(\partial\right)^{-}_{k}\widehat{f}^{-}\right)(\rho)=&\sum_{l=k}^{\infty}c_{2l+1}\;\rho^{2l+1-k}2^{k}(l)^{\downarrow}_{k}\\
=&c_{2k+1}\;\rho^{k+1}\;(2k)!!+\cdots,\end{split}

(II.34)

filler

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{k}K\right)(2\pi a\rho)=&\sum_{l=k}^{\infty}\alpha_{l}\;(2\pi a\rho)^{2l-k}2^{k}(l)^{\downarrow}_{k}\\
=&\alpha_{k}\;(2\pi a\rho)^{k}\;(2k)!!+\cdots,\end{split}

(II.35)

where we also used the fact that
(2l)!!/(2l-2k)!!=2^{k}(l)^{\downarrow}_{k}
. Matching the
c_{k}
’s can be done naturally by focusing
\widehat{f}
and we can then fix the
\rho
behavior at that
c_{k}
order through multiplication by an appropriate series in foci of
K
. For instance, say we want to match the
c_{0}
order of
section

II.3
:

\displaystyle c_{0}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{m}(2\pi a\rho)^{2m}.

(II.36)

Inspired by the multiplicative character of
eq.

II.12
we see this can be done via a term of the form

\displaystyle\left(\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{0}\widehat{f}^{+}\right)(\rho)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A^{+}_{0,n}\left(\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{2n}K\right)(2\pi a\rho),

(II.37)

provided the coefficients
A^{+}_{0,n}
are chosen so that the
\rho
-Taylor expansion of
eq.

II.37
matches that of
eq.

II.36
. Note that this is possible since
\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{2n}K(2\pi a\rho)
starts at order
\rho^{2n}
and so the matching is iteratively well defined;
A^{+}_{0,0}
fixes the
\rho^{0}
order,
A^{+}_{0,1}
fixes the
\rho^{2}
order, etc. Every term ‘overshoots’ at higher
\rho
orders, but that can be accounted for in subsequent steps. In a similar vein,
eq.

II.37
contains dependence on higher order
c_{2k}
’s beyond just
c_{0}
but we can fix that later with higher order foci of
\widehat{f}
. For instance, to fit the
c_{2}
order we must account for
section

II.3
but also cancel the spurious terms we introduced in
eq.

II.37
, together these contributions are

\displaystyle\begin{split}-c_{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}&\Big[\frac{n\left(n-1/2\right)}{\pi^{2}a^{2}}\alpha_{n}(2\pi a\rho)^{2n}\\
&+\rho^{2}A^{+}_{0,n}\left(\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{2n}K\right)(2\pi a\rho)\Big].\end{split}

(II.38)

From
eqs.

II.33
and
II.35
we see that we can fit
eq.

II.38
using

\displaystyle\left(\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{1}\widehat{f}^{+}\right)(\rho)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A^{+}_{1,n}\left(\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{2n+1}K\right)(2\pi a\rho),

(II.39)

where the
A^{+}_{1,n}
are once again picked to match the
\rho
-Taylor expansion of
eq.

II.38
. The key fact here is that
\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{1}\widehat{f}^{+}
has no dependence on
c_{0}
so we don’t disturb the lower order we had already fixed. Again, we overshoot at higher
c_{2k}
orders but can account for that once we reach them in the matching procedure. Extending this reasoning to all
c_{k}
orders of even and odd parity we can write the following ansatz:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{K,a}&\left[f\right](\rho)=\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{K,a}[\widehat{f}\,](\rho)=\\
&\sum_{k,n=0}^{\infty}A^{+}_{k,n}\left(\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{k}\widehat{f}^{+}\right)(\rho)\left(\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{2n+k}K\right)(2\pi a\rho)\\
+&\sum_{k,n=0}^{\infty}A^{-}_{k,n}\left(\left(\partial\right)^{-}_{k}\widehat{f}^{-}\right)(\rho)\left(\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{2n+1+k}K\right)(2\pi a\rho),\end{split}

(II.40)

where the first line matches all the
c_{2k}
and the second all the
c_{2k+1}
orders. We will refer to
eq.

II.40
as
the focal expansion
of
\mathcal{I}_{K,a}\left[f\right](\rho)
, this is the central tool that we have developed.

Before finding the coefficients in
eq.

II.40
we briefly discuss the case of kernels with non-definite parity. First note that we can always decompose
K=K^{+}+K^{-}
and write

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{K,a}\left[f\right](\rho)=\mathcal{I}_{K^{+},a}\left[f\right](\rho)+\mathcal{I}_{K^{-},a}\left[f\right](\rho),

(II.41)

meaning all we need is to develop a way to deal with purely odd kernels. Note, however, that any odd kernel
K^{-}(z)
with a Taylor series at the origin defines an even function with a Taylor series at the origin via
\frac{K^{-}(z)}{z}
. This means that we may write

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{K^{-}(z),a}&\left[f(x)\right](\rho)\\
&=2\pi\rho\,\mathcal{I}_{\frac{K^{-}(z)}{z},a}\big[(x+a)f(x)\big](\rho)\\
&=2\pi\rho\,\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{\frac{K^{-}(z)}{z},a}\left[\frac{i}{2\pi}\widehat{f}\,^{\prime}+a\widehat{f}\;\right](\rho),\end{split}

(II.42)

where in the last step we have used
eq.

II.16
. We have thus reduced the problem to the already analyzed case of even kernels. To summarize, for a generic kernel
K(z)
the focal expansion can be found by writing

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{K(z),a}&\,[f](\rho)=\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{K^{+}(z),a}\,[\widehat{f}\,](\rho)\\
&+i\rho\,\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{\frac{K^{-}(z)}{z},a}\,[\widehat{f}\,^{\prime}\,](\rho)+2\pi\rho a\,\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{\frac{K^{-}(z)}{z},a}\,[\widehat{f}\,](\rho)\end{split}

(II.43)

and applying
eq.

II.40
to each integral separately.

We now discuss finding the coefficients
A_{k,n}^{\pm}
. One must substitute the explicit expressions
eqs.

II.33
,
II.34
and
II.35
into
eq.

II.40
and equate the coefficients at each
c_{k}(2\pi a\rho)^{2m}
order to the known expansion
section

II.3
. We omit the tedious details and instead list the final recurrence relations:

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\alpha_{m}

\displaystyle(2m)^{\downarrow}_{2k}=\sum^{m-k}_{n=0}\sum_{l=0}^{k}(2\pi a)^{l}A_{l,n}^{+}\alpha_{l+n+m-k}

\displaystyle\times 2^{2(n+l)}(k)^{\downarrow}_{l}(l+n+m-k)^{\downarrow}_{2n+l},

(II.44)

filler

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}i\alpha_{m}

\displaystyle(2m)^{\downarrow}_{2k+1}=\sum^{m-k-1}_{n=0}\sum_{l=0}^{k}(2\pi a)^{l}A_{l,n}^{-}\alpha_{l+n+m-k}

\displaystyle\times 2^{2(n+l)+1}(k)^{\downarrow}_{l}(l+n+m-k)^{\downarrow}_{2n+l+1},

(II.45)

where the first recurrence is non-vanishing only for
m\geq k\geq 0
and the second only for
m>k\geq 0
(this is a result of the
m
lower bound in
section

II.3
). These recurrence relations can be solved iteratively.
Thinking of
A^{+}_{l,n}
as an infinite matrix, we see from
eq.

II.44
that to calculate
A^{+}_{k,m-k}
we must first know the values of all
other
coefficients contained in the finite
k
-by-
(m-k)
submatrix stretching from
A^{+}_{0,0}
to the desired
A^{+}_{k,m-k}
. The coefficient
A^{+}_{0,0}
is unique in that it doesn’t depend on any others, from
eq.

II.44
it can be seen to be
1
as long as
\alpha_{0}
is non-vanishing. Thus, there are different ‘paths’ one can take to find the desired
A^{+}_{k,m-k}
; one option would be to go row by row in the submatrix, calculating
A^{+}_{0,0},\cdots,A^{+}_{0,m-k}
one after another, then calculating the row below, and so on. Completely analogous logic follows for finding
A^{-}_{l,n}
using
eq.

II.45
. This iterative procedure also makes clear that for each kernel
K
the coefficients
A^{\pm}_{l,n}
, if they exist
8
8

8

We run into non-existence issues if any of the
\alpha
’s vanish as that sets to 0 a term we need for a particular iterative step, see Appendix
B
for more details.
, are uniquely fixed. As an example, here are the first several focal coefficients:

\displaystyle\begin{split}A^{+}_{0,0}&=1,\quad A^{+}_{0,1}=0,\quad A^{+}_{1,0}=\frac{-(\alpha_{0}+2\alpha_{1})}{8\,a\pi\,\alpha_{1}},\\
&\text{\phantom{filler}}\\
A^{-}_{0,0}&=i,\quad A^{-}_{0,1}=0,\quad A^{-}_{1,0}=\frac{-i(\alpha_{1}+12\alpha_{2})}{16\,a\pi\,\alpha_{2}}.\end{split}

(II.46)

One can keep going indefinitely, at least algebraically – we do not claim the focal expansion

II.40
converges for all possible kernels. See Appendix
D
for two explicit counterexamples with poor convergence behavior.

While finding a finite subset of focal coefficients can be easily performed algorithmically, finding closed form analytic solutions for all of the
A^{\pm}_{l,n}
presents a more formidable challenge. For a generic
K
we delegate results of this type to Appendix
B
since, as we show now, the problem simplifies dramatically for the case that interests us the most.

II.4
Bessel Function Kernels

In the special case of
K=J_{n}
we introduce the notation

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{J_{n},a}\left[f\right](\rho)\equiv\mathcal{J}_{n,a}\left[f\right](\rho).

(II.47)

We start by investigating
K=J_{0}
. The Taylor expansion of
J_{0}(z)
is
29

\displaystyle J_{0}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{4^{k}k!^{2}}z^{2k}

(II.48)

and so, in our earlier notation of
eq.

II.30
, we have
\alpha_{k}=(-1)^{k}/4^{k}\,k!^{2}
. Evaluating the first few focal coefficients iteratively we get what seems to be (for a different derivation see appendix
B
)

\displaystyle A_{l,n}^{+}=-iA_{l,n}^{-}=\frac{\delta_{n,0}}{(4\pi a)^{l}}\frac{(2l-1)!!}{l!},

(II.49)

where
\delta
is the Kronecker delta. Eq. (
II.49
) can indeed be seen to solve the recurrences
eqs.

II.44
and
II.45
, both of which reduce to the hypergeometric identity

\displaystyle\frac{(2m-1)!!}{2^{k}k!\,m!\,(2m-2k-1)!!}=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{l}(2l-1)!!}{2^{l}l!\,(k-l)!\,(l+m-k)!}

(II.50)

that can be proved via, for instance, Sister Celine’s method
26
. By uniqueness of the solutions to
eqs.

II.45
and
II.44
whenever none of the
\alpha_{i}
’s vanish we then see that, indeed, the guess
eq.

II.49
gives the focal coefficients for
K=J_{0}
.

There is another significant simplification that occurs for this kernel. Bessel functions obey the following recurrence relations
29
:

\displaystyle\frac{2n}{z}J_{n}(z)=

\displaystyle J_{n-1}(z)+J_{n+1}(z),

(II.51)

\displaystyle 2\partial J_{n}(z)=

\displaystyle J_{n-1}(z)-J_{n+1}(z),

(II.52)

using which one can prove that focusing
J_{0}
simply results in higher order Bessel functions:

\displaystyle\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{n}J_{0}=(-1)^{n}J_{n}.

(II.53)

Putting
eqs.

II.49
and
II.53
together we see that for this kernel the focal expansion
eq.

II.40
takes the drastically simplified form

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{J}_{0,a}\left[f\right](\rho)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2k-1)!!}{k!}\frac{1}{(-4\pi a)^{k}}\\
\times&\left[(\left(\partial\right)^{+}_{k}\widehat{f}^{+})(\rho)J_{k}(2\pi a\rho)-i(\left(\partial\right)^{-}_{k}\widehat{f}^{-})(\rho)J_{k+1}(2\pi a\rho)\right]\end{split}

(II.54)

with each sum corresponding to matching
\rho
powers at a given
c_{k}
order in
eq.

II.40
vanishing beyond the leading term.

When trying to apply the logic that led to
eq.

II.54
to higher-order Bessel function kernels we run into an immediate problem: the Taylor series of
J_{m}(z)
starts at order
z^{m}
which means that for

at least one of the
\alpha_{k}
of
eq.

II.30
vanish. This invalidates the iterative procedure of the previous section (see Appendix
B
for more details). There is, however, another way. With
eq.

II.54
we can calculate focal expansions for higher order Bessel function kernels indirectly. One begins by noting that
eq.

II.52
Fourier transformed and multiplied by

becomes precisely the recurrence relation for Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind

, with the same initial conditions save for a multiplicative factor of
\widehat{J}_{0}
. This means we have

Intuitively, we may think of a focal expansion as a cousin to the Fourier convolution theorem, albeit an unusual one that separates the angular and radial dimensions. The Fourier convolution theorem holds that, in any dimension, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left[f_{1}*f_{2}\right]=\mathcal{F}[f_{1}]\mathcal{F}[f_{2}]

This argument is of course a totally incorrect application of the convolution theorem, for one the functions being convolved in

Nevertheless one can intuitively think of the focal expansion as the rigorous reflection of this naive want of making 2D functions play by 1D convolution rules: it separates out the radial direction (represented by the 1D Fourier transforms of the profiles) from the angular (represented by the Bessel functions and the angular mode label

III
Examples

In this section, we benchmark and stress-test the focal expansion on several representative families of ring-like profiles. We start with
annulus rings
(section
III.1
), for which both the exact Fourier transform and the focal series can be obtained analytically, providing a controlled validation of the expansion. We then turn to
Gaussian rings
(section
III.2
), which probe two practical choices required by the method—the extension of the radial profiles to the full real line and the choice of shift parameter. Next we consider
logarithmic rings
(section
III.3
) motivated by gravitational lensing in a black hole’s photon ring; here the relevant Hankel-type integrals are evaluated numerically and the examples probe the behavior of the focal expansion in the presence of integrable divergences and slow tails. We also include a

II.5
for removing weight at the origin. Finally, we discuss
delta-function rings
of zero thickness (section
III.5
) with non-circular shapes, where the focal expansion remains accurate for individual angular modes but the full Fourier transform can involve nontrivial resummations over modes.

We open our analysis of functions with non-compact support by considering the family of Gaussian rings, defined as the convolution of a delta function ring of radius

\displaystyle\begin{split}f(r,\theta)=&\int_{0}^{\infty}|x|\,\text{d}|x|\int_{0}^{2\pi}\text{d}\varphi\,\gamma(\varphi)\delta(|x|-a)\\

\displaystyle\gamma(\theta)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{n}e^{in\theta}.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{F}\left[f\right]&(\rho,\phi)\\
=&(2\pi)^{3/2}a\sigma\,e^{-2(\pi\sigma\rho)^{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{n}(-i)^{n}e^{in\phi}J_{n}(2\pi a\rho)\end{split}

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{n}(\rho)=\gamma_{n}\sqrt{2\pi}a\sigma e^{-2(\pi\sigma\rho)^{2}}J_{n}(2\pi a\rho).

(III.29)

\displaystyle f(r,\theta)=\frac{a\sqrt{2\pi}}{\sigma}e^{-(a^{2}+r^{2})/2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{in\theta}\gamma_{n}I_{n}\left(\frac{ar}{\sigma^{2}}\right)

(III.30)

and so the angular modes are

\displaystyle g_{n}(r)=\gamma_{n}\frac{a\sqrt{2\pi}}{\sigma}e^{-(a^{2}+r^{2})/2\sigma^{2}}I_{n}\left(\frac{ar}{\sigma^{2}}\right).

(III.31)

Unlike the annular rings of the previous section, the focal expansion here is non-trivial. The problem is that the trivial extension of

III.31

g_{n}(r)
while also making their 1D Fourier transforms tractable. Since we do have the exact result

III.28

We begin by noting that the functions
I_{n}(z)

\displaystyle I_{n}(z)\big|_{z\gg 1}\approx\frac{e^{z}}{\sqrt{2\pi z}}

(III.32)

for large real

for small values of

\displaystyle I_{n}(ar/\sigma^{2})=e^{ar/\sigma^{2}}\left(I_{n}(ar/\sigma^{2})e^{-ar/\sigma^{2}}\right)

and then Taylor expand the term in parenthesis up to some finite order

\displaystyle g_{n}(r)\approx\gamma_{n}\frac{a\sqrt{2\pi}}{\sigma}e^{-(r-a)^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}q_{n,N}(r-a)

where

\displaystyle q_{n,N}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\frac{z^{j}}{j!}\left[\left(\frac{d}{dr}\right)^{j}I_{n}(ar/\sigma^{2})e^{-ar/\sigma^{2}}\right]\Bigg|_{r=a}.

(III.35)

Note that one could in principle calculate the Taylor coefficients in

\displaystyle 2\partial I_{n}(z)=I_{n+1}(z)+I_{n-1}(z).

(III.36)

We omit further details since, as it turns out,

We still need to extend
g_{n}(r)

If we remove the tails we still get the factor of

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{G}^{2}_{n}(\rho)=&\pi a\,q_{n,N}\left(\frac{i}{2\pi}\frac{d}{d\rho}\right)\Bigg[e^{-2(\pi\sigma\rho)^{2}}\\
\times&\left(1-\text{Erf}(i\sqrt{2}\pi\sigma\rho-a/\sqrt{2}\sigma)\right)\Bigg]\end{split}

with Erf being the error function. Note that if we take the limit

Having found these

III.5.1
General Calculations

For our final set of examples we analyze the case of rings of zero thickness – those whose radial profile is a Dirac delta. We specialize to curves whose radial distance from the origin can be written as a positive function of the angle
R(\theta)>0
(so, in particular, we do not allow the shape to ‘back up’ in angle). The function to be Fourier transformed is then

\displaystyle f(r,\theta)=\gamma(\theta)\delta(r-R(\theta))

\gamma(\theta)

\displaystyle\gamma(\theta)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{n}e^{in\theta}

(III.92)

\displaystyle g_{n}(r)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{m}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i(m-n)\theta}\delta(r-R(\theta))\,\text{d}\theta.

(III.93)

Because we assumed

One might be tempted to immediately use the delta function to evaluate the

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{g}_{n}(\rho)=&\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{m}\\

(III.94)

(III.95)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{g}_{n}(\rho)=&\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{m}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}r\,e^{-i2\pi r\rho}\sum_{j}\frac{e^{i(m-n)\theta_{j}(r)}}{|R^{\prime}(\theta_{j}(r))|},\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{g}_{n}(\rho)=\frac{e^{-i2\pi\rho a}}{2\pi}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}&\gamma_{m}e^{i(n-m)\delta}\\
\times&\int_{0}^{2\pi}\text{d}\theta\,e^{i(m-n)\theta-i2\pi\rho b\cos\theta}.\end{split}

(III.100)

where we continue to assume our limaçon has a non-trivial intensity profile given by
eq.

III.92
. The integral above is the
(n-m)
th Fourier coefficient of
e^{-i2\pi\rho b\cos\theta}
, but from the Anger-Jacobi expansion
eq.

II.3
we immediately see this is simply
(-i)^{m-n}J_{m-n}(2\pi\rho b)
. This means that

\displaystyle\widehat{g}_{n}(\rho)=

\displaystyle e^{-i2\pi\rho a}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{m}e^{i(n-m)(\delta+\pi/2)}J_{m-n}(2\pi\rho b).

(III.101)

We see that
a
should be chosen as the shift parameter (justifying the notation) so that

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{n}(\rho)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{m}e^{i(n-m)(\delta+\pi/2)}J_{m-n}(2\pi\rho b),

(III.102)

meaning that the leading focal order is

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{n}(\rho)\approx

\displaystyle\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\gamma_{m}e^{i(n-m)\delta}i^{m}\left(h_{n,m}+(-1)^{m}h_{n,m}^{*}\right)(\rho),

(III.103)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&h_{n,m}(\rho)\equiv\\
&\frac{(-i)^{n}a}{2}\left(J_{n}+\frac{i}{2}J_{n-1}-\frac{i}{2}J_{n+1}\right)(2\pi\rho a)\,J_{n-m}(2\pi\rho b),\end{split}

(III.104)

where we defined an axillary function
h_{n,m}
for brevity and where
*
denotes the complex conjugate. In other words, the angular modes of the Fourier transform of a limaçon will be approximately given by a linear superposition of products of two Bessel
J
functions. In particular, when the angular dependence is trivial
\gamma(\theta)=1
and the rotation angle
\delta=0
vanishes we have

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{n}(\rho)=(-i)^{n}J_{n}(2\pi\rho b)

(III.105)

and the leading focal order simplifies to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{J}_{2n}&\big|_{\gamma=1,\delta=0}(\rho)\approx(-1)^{\frac{n}{2}}a\,J_{n}(2\pi\rho b)\,J_{n}(2\pi\rho a),\\
\mathcal{J}_{2n+1}&\big|_{\gamma=1,\delta=0}(\rho)\approx\\
&(-1)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\frac{a}{2}\,J_{n}(2\pi\rho b)\,\left(J_{n-1}-J_{n+1}\right)(2\pi\rho a).\end{split}

(III.106)

Figure (
38
) shows the numerics for
\mathcal{J}_{0}
,
\mathcal{J}_{1}
, and
\mathcal{J}_{2}
of the limaçon seen in the middle column of fig. (
37
) compared to the Focal LO and S
2
LO. We see that, once again, the leading order provides a serviceable and the subsubleading order an excellent approximation across all of the plots.

Looking at the general case with arbitrary intensity variation,
eqs.

III.103
and
III.104
, it appears there is no obvious way to disentangle the angular intensity information (represented by factors of
\gamma_{m}
) from the information describing the shape of the curve (represented by the constants
a,b,\delta
). This is emblematic of a larger issue; In fact, it would be surprising if the two
could
be separated. For a generic ring-like function
f
there is no canonical way of separating angular intensity variation and ring shape: if the ring appears to our eyes to have some non-circular shape, it can nevertheless be described as a circular ring with appropriately chosen radial profiles for each of the angular modes
g_{n}(r)
. This is relevant even for a delta function curve as the radial profiles of its angular modes
eq.

III.93
will not in general be delta functions themselves. It is true that for many situations it would be abundantly clear for the human observer to separate out angular intensity dependence from shape, like in our limaçon, but the point is that this language is not mathematically natural and so we should not expect our calculations to yield to it.

There is, surprisingly, a little more we can do with the limaçon focal expansion. Due to the relative simplicity of the leading order
\mathcal{J}_{n}
it is actually possible to analytically perform the sum over
n
in
eq.

II.82
. One must repeatedly use Graf’s addition theorem
29
:

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}J_{N+k}(x)J_{k}(y)e^{ik(\varphi+\pi)}=\frac{(x+ye^{-i\varphi})^{N}}{\omega_{x,y}(\varphi)^{N}}J_{N}(\omega_{x,y}(\varphi))

(III.107)

where we define

\displaystyle\omega_{x,y}(\varphi)\equiv\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}+2xy\cos\varphi}.

(III.108)

Performing the sum over angular modes in
eq.

II.82
and simplifying one obtains

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{F}\left[f\right](\rho,\phi)\approx\pi\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}&\gamma_{m}i^{m}e^{-im\delta}\\
\times&\big[\Omega_{m}(\phi+\delta)+(-1)^{m}\Omega_{m}^{*}(\pi-\phi-\delta)\big]\\
&\phantom{filler}\end{split}

(III.109)

where we have defined the auxiliary functions

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Omega_{m}(\varphi)\equiv\left(\frac{-\omega_{a,b}(\varphi)}{b+ae^{-i\varphi}}\right)^{m}\times\\
&\Bigg[aJ_{m}+\frac{i\omega_{a,b}(\varphi)}{2}\Big(J_{m-1}-J_{m+1}\Big)\Bigg]\Big(2\pi\rho\,\omega_{a,b}(\varphi)\Big).\end{split}

(III.110)

Note in particular that we have recovered the expected
1/\sqrt{\rho}
asymptotic decay behavior, even though the individual angular modes all decay as
1/\rho
. This is all thanks to the non-trivial resummation performed by Graf’s theorem.

Comparing
eq.

III.109
to numerics one will notice a surprising problem. At first, for small values of
b
, it seems that the focal approximation performs well – see fig. (
39
) where we have plotted the real and imaginary components of the Fourier transform along the directions
\phi=0^{\circ}
and
\phi=90^{\circ}

b=0.1a
from the first column of fig. (

Pushing the resummed leading focal expansion

III.109

b=0.5a
results in large but somewhat expected amplitude errors along the
\phi=0^{\circ}
direction – see the first two rows of fig. (
40

\phi=90^{\circ}
direction, however, we get an asymptotically catastrophic frequency mismatch: our predicted frequency appears just barely too large, making our peaks lag behind those of the numeric results by an amount increasing with distance from the origin.
This behavior is particularly surprising as we saw no evidence of any frequency errors in any of the plots for the individual angular modes in fig. (
38
). We suspect that non-trivial resummations occur among the subleading focal orders to result in this slight frequency shift – as mentioned above, we expect delta function rings to be unique in that they severely accentuate these types of resummation issues. It would be very interesting to see if one can apply a similar derivation using Graf’s theorem to resum higher focal orders over all angular modes and whether doing so removes this frequency discrepancy. We leave this for future work and let this particular angular resummation figure as a cautionary tale for the time being: the focal expansions of sharply peaked functions still work well for individual angular modes, but adding all these modes together is not a trivial matter.

Before we conclude, it is important to note the above mentioned frequency discrepancy does not appear when one uses existing asymptotic techniques. For instance, according to Gralla
14
for
b/a=0.5
along the
\phi=90^{\circ}
line we should approximate

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left[f\right](\rho,90^{\circ})\approx\frac{C_{G}}{\sqrt{\rho a}}\cos\left(\pi d_{G}\rho a-\pi/4\right)

(III.111)

where one can find the constants to be

\displaystyle C_{G}

\displaystyle=\sqrt[4]{6+\frac{7\sqrt{3}}{2}}

(III.112)

\displaystyle d_{G}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{9}{4}+\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}}.

(III.113)

To find these one needs to solve
0=\frac{d}{d\theta}R(\theta)\sin\theta
, which is analytically tractable in the present simple case but can prove quite difficult in general without numerical methods. The comparison between
eq.

III.111
, the resummed focal expansion, and the numerical result is shown in fig. (
41
). We see that Gralla’s calculation breaks down near the origin, as is typical of asymptotic methods, but provides an excellent approximation for larger arguments. This raises the question of whether this alternative approach can be combined with the focal expansion in some generic way, mitigating the downsides of both approaches in the process. We leave such considerations for future work.

Taken together, the examples in this section support the main practical claims of the focal expansion: for smooth ring-like profiles with up to moderate width-to-radius ratio, the LO approximation is often already accurate over a wide range of
\rho
, and successive subleading orders improve the result without an apparent finite-
\rho
breakdown. The Gaussian and logarithmic examples also highlight two important implementation details: (i) the extension of
g_{n}(r)
to the full real line should not introduce artificial non-smoothness at
r=0
, since such discontinuities can dominate large-
\rho
behavior, and (ii) the shift parameter need only be chosen within a reasonable neighborhood of the dominant ring radius for the phase to remain correct. Finally, the delta-function examples emphasize that zero-thickness features can require nontrivial resummations over angular modes, and may benefit from hybrid treatments that combine the focal expansion with complementary asymptotic methods such as stationary phase.

IV
Concluding Remarks

In this work, we derived a new approximation – the “focal expansion” – for the Fourier transform of radially concentrated functions, focusing on the 2D case of “ring-like” images. We demonstrated that it provides accurate analytic approximations to the corresponding 2D Fourier transforms (interferometric visibilities) for several families of images.
The focal expansion is written in terms of Bessel-type oscillatory factors set primarily by the ring radius, modulated by the one-dimensional Fourier transform of the radial profile and the angular image modes.
Hence, the focal expansion offers a clean separation between angular and radial information. In many of the examples considered here, truncation of the focal expansion at leading order accurately reproduces the correct global behavior over the full range of spatial frequency, even for non-circular rings and those with fractional thickness of order unity.

As presented, this method is formulated primarily as a forward-modeling tool: it approximates the Fourier transform of a known, ring-like image model. A natural next step is to develop an inverse (reconstruction) counterpart that maps oscillatory Fourier-domain data to a ring-like image model while retaining the separation of angular and radial structure. Such an inverse formulation could be particularly valuable for interferometric applications where the measured data are naturally sparse samples in Fourier space, especially for space-VLBI missions that sample baselines dominated by a black hole photon ring, such as the Black Hole Explorer
17
;
23
.

On the analytic side, several questions remain open. Establishing convergence criteria and practical error estimates would clarify the domain of validity of the approximation and strengthen its reliability. In particular, Section
III.3.2
shows unexpectedly good numerical performance in a regime where the assumptions used in the derivation appear to be strained, while the worm-like nonuniform breakdown illustrated in fig. (
27
) is atypical compared to standard truncation errors and may point to a distinct mechanism of failure. Appendix
D
further indicates that straightforward generalizations to non-Bessel kernels can perform poorly, motivating modified kernels or resummations that preserve the favorable uniform behavior observed in Bessel-type cases.

Several extensions appear especially promising. First, specializing the framework to half-integer-order Bessel kernels (i.e., spherical Bessel functions) could simplify implementations in odd spatial dimensions, as discussed in Appendix
A
. Second, the apparent leading-order frequency offset observed for the lima0con example in Sec.
III.5.2
suggests that related reorganizations may apply to more general oscillatory integrals on
S^{1}
; understanding this effect may open a route to uniformly accurate approximations for generalized Bessel-type integrals.

In short, we expect the focal expansion to provide a practical analytic tool for modeling and interpreting Fourier-domain signatures of ring-like sources, especially for studies of black holes with radio interferometry, and we hope it stimulates further development of uniform approximation methods for oscillatory integrals in broader settings.

Appendix A
Angular Decomposition in Higher Dimensions

Using our methods for higher dimensional Fourier transforms necessitates the use of the generalization of spherical harmonics to an arbitrary number
N
of dimensions. For an elementary introduction see
10
, we only summarize the necessary details.

Let
\vec{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\hat{r}\equiv\vec{X}/r
. A spherical harmonic
Y_{n,l}(\hat{r})
of degree
n\in\mathbb{N}
in
N
dimensions is a restriction to the unit sphere
S^{N-1}
of a polynomial
H_{n,l}(\vec{X})
in the variables
X_{1},\cdots,X_{N}
such that

\displaystyle H_{n,l}(\lambda\vec{X})=\lambda^{n}H_{n,l}(\vec{X})

(A.1)

for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and

\displaystyle\Delta_{N}H_{n,l}=0

(A.2)

where
\Delta_{N}
is the
N
-dimensional Laplacian. For a fixed
n
there are only

\displaystyle\frac{2n+N-2}{n}\binom{n+N-3}{n-1}

(A.3)

spherical harmonics with that degree and so the allowed values for the index
l
come from a finite set. For
n\neq m
and any two
l,l^{\prime}
the spherical harmonics
Y_{n,l}
,
Y_{m,l^{\prime}}
are naturally orthogonal when integrated over the sphere
S^{N-1}
with the standard measure
d\Omega_{N-1}
. For fixed
n
one can then orthonormalize over the finite set of
l
values to ensure any two spherical harmonics are orthonormal.

Having set all that up, the set of all spherical harmonics (for all values of
n
and
l
) forms an orthonormal basis for functions on the unit sphere
S^{N-1}
. This means that any function
f(\vec{X})
can be expanded as

\displaystyle f(\vec{X})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{l}g_{n,l}(r)Y_{n,l}(\hat{r}).

(A.4)

Using this expansion in the Fourier transform of
f
written in generalized spherical coordinates yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{F}\left[f\right](\vec{P})=&\sum_{n,l}\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}r\,r^{N-1}g_{n,l}(r)\\
\times&\int_{S^{N-1}}\text{d}\Omega_{N-1}(\hat{r})\,Y_{n,l}(\hat{r})e^{-2\pi i\vec{X}\cdot\vec{P}}.\end{split}

(A.5)

Now, note that the Jacobi-Anger expansion
eq.

II.3
is symmetric under
\theta\rightarrow-\theta
and so it can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{split}e^{iz\cos\theta}=&\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}i^{m}J_{m}(z)\cos(m\theta)\\
=&\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}(2-\delta_{m,0})i^{m}J_{m}(z)T_{m}\left(\cos\theta\right)\end{split}

(A.6)

where the
T_{m}
are Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind. Using this in
eq.

A.5
yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{F}\left[f\right](\vec{P})=&\sum_{n,m,l}(-i)^{m}\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}r\,r^{N-1}g_{n,l}(r)J_{m}(2\pi r\rho)\\
\times&(2-\delta_{m,0})\int_{S^{N-1}}\text{d}\Omega_{N-1}(\hat{r})\,Y_{n,l}(\hat{r})T_{m}\left(\hat{r}\cdot\hat{\rho}\right)\end{split}

(A.7)

where
\hat{\rho}\equiv\vec{P}/\rho
(recall that
\rho\equiv||\vec{P}||
). One must now generalize the family of Legendre polynomials (figuring prominently in three dimensional spherical analysis) to an arbitrary number of dimensions. We call this family
P_{n}^{(N)}
. These are more commonly known, after the rescaling

\displaystyle C_{n}^{\left(\frac{N-2}{2}\right)}(t)=\binom{n+N-3}{n}P_{n}^{(N)}(t),

(A.8)

as Gegenbauer or ultraspherical polynomials
24
;
1
(
t
here is a generic variable). With these polynomials one can now transform the spherical integral in
eq.

A.7
using the Hecke-Funk Theorem, which states that for any unit vector
\hat{k}
and an arbitrary function
h:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{S^{N-1}}&\text{d}\Omega_{N-1}(\hat{r})\,h(\hat{r}\cdot\hat{\rho})Y_{n,l}(\hat{r})\\
=&\Omega_{N-2}Y_{n,l}(\hat{\rho})\int_{-1}^{1}\text{d}t\,h(t)P_{n}^{(N)}(t)\,(1-t^{2})^{\frac{N-3}{2}}\end{split}

(A.9)

where

\displaystyle\Omega_{N-2}=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{N-1}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{N-1}{2}\right)}

(A.10)

is the area of
S^{N-2}
. We see that to apply
eq.

A.9
in
eq.

A.7
we simply replace the function
f
by the Chebyshev
T_{m}
. We must therefore evaluate the integrals

\displaystyle\eta_{m,n}^{(N)}\equiv\int_{-1}^{1}\text{d}t\,T_{m}(t)P_{n}^{(N)}(t)\,(1-t^{2})^{\frac{N-3}{2}}.

(A.11)

The generalized Legendre polynomials are actually orthonormal over the measure
(1-t^{2})^{\frac{N-3}{2}}\,\text{d}t
and interval
t\in[-1,1]
so if we could expand a generic Chebyshev
T_{m}
in terms of the ultraspherical polynomials we would be essentially done. The key is to note the following properties of ultraspherical polynomials
24
:

\displaystyle C^{(\mu)}_{n}(t)=\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{\lambda+n-2l}{\lambda}\frac{(\mu)^{\uparrow}_{n-l}(\mu-\lambda)^{\uparrow}_{l}}{(\lambda+1)^{\uparrow}_{n-l}l!}C^{(\lambda)}_{n-2l}(t),

(A.12)

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu\rightarrow 0}\frac{n+\mu}{\mu}C^{(\mu)}_{n}(t)=2T_{n}(t)-\delta_{n,0},

(A.13)

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\text{d}t\,\left[C^{(\lambda)}_{n}(t)\right]^{2}(1-t^{2})^{\lambda-1/2}=\frac{2^{1-2\lambda}\pi\Gamma(n+2\lambda)}{(n+\lambda)\Gamma(\lambda)^{2}n!}.

(A.14)

Using these one can show that

\displaystyle\begin{split}m=&0:\qquad\eta_{0,n}^{(N)}=\delta_{0,n}2^{3-N}\pi\frac{\Gamma(N-2)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{N-2}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)}\\
m>&0:\qquad\eta_{m,n}^{(N)}=4\pi m\,\delta_{m,n}^{\text{mod }2}\frac{(N-3)!}{2^{N}\Gamma\left(\frac{N}{2}-1\right)^{2}}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\frac{\left(\frac{m+n}{2}-1\right)!\left(1-\frac{N}{2}\right)_{\frac{m-n}{2}}^{\uparrow}}{\left(\frac{m-n}{2}\right)!\left(\frac{N}{2}-1\right)^{\uparrow}_{\frac{m+n}{2}+1}}\end{split}

(A.15)

where we used
\delta^{\text{mod}2}_{m,n}
to indicate the expression vanishes unless
m,n
are of the same parity. Note that due to the factor of
\left(\frac{m-n}{2}\right)!
in the denominator,
\eta^{(N)}_{m,n}
vanishes unless
n\leq|m|
.

Bringing
eqs.

A.7
,
A.9
and
A.15
together we see that the Fourier transform is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{F}\left[f\right](\vec{P})=&\Omega_{N-2}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{l}(-i)^{m}\eta_{m,n}^{(N)}Y_{n,l}(\hat{\rho})\\
&\times(2-\delta_{m,0})\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}r\,r^{N-1}g_{n,l}(r)J_{m}(2\pi r\rho)\end{split}

(A.16)

where we accounted for the fact that
\eta_{m,n}^{(N)}
vanishes for
n>|m|
by adding summation limits. The remaining Bessel integral in
eq.

A.16
can then be approximated using precisely the same methods as derived in section
II
and summarized in section
II.6
. The higher dimensionality simply manifests as a different power of
r
in the integrands (which our method can handle without difficulty) and the fact that the sum over angular modes is more complicated, accounting for the extra spherical degrees of freedom in higher dimensions.

Note also that for
N=2
one must understand
eq.

A.15
in a limiting sense, doing so one will obtain

\displaystyle\Omega_{0}\,\eta^{(2)}_{m,n}=\pi\delta_{m,n}(1+\delta_{0,n}).

(A.17)

Noting then that for
N=2
we have
Y_{n,l}=e^{inl\phi}
with
\{l\}={+1,-1}
for
n>0
and
\{l\}=1
for
n=0
, we recover the expression
eq.

II.4
derived in the main text.

Note, however, that the form of
eq.

A.16
in
N>2
dimensions is somewhat unwieldy. In practical applications one will have to truncate the
m
sum and while in
N=2
the terms with fixed
m
correspond neatly with the
m
th order angular modes, the same is not true in higher dimensions: note for instance that the
n=0
angular mode
Y_{0}
contributions come in at
every

m
order. Unless the coefficient functions
g_{n,l}
decay rapidly with growing
n
this means one might encounter non-trivial resummations in this approach even at leading angular mode order. The rapid decay condition should be true of smooth functions encountered in practice but one must be mindful of this possible pitfall when applying the focal expansion for higher dimensional Fourier transforms.

There is an alternative to
eq.

A.16
but it comes at a cost. We could skip the Jacobi-Anger step and apply the Hecke-Funk theorem directly to
eq.

A.5
, this will yield

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{F}\left[f\right](\vec{P})=&\Omega_{N-2}\sum_{n,l}Y_{n,l}(\hat{\rho})\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}r\,r^{N-1}g_{n,l}(r)\\
\times&\int_{-1}^{1}\text{d}t\,e^{-2\pi i\rho rt}P_{n}^{(N)}(t)\,(1-t^{2})^{\frac{N-3}{2}}.\end{split}

(A.18)

Surprisingly, the integral over
t
can be evaluated using the identity
24

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\text{d}t\,e^{i\omega t}C_{n}^{(\lambda)}(t)\,(1-t^{2})^{\lambda-\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{2\pi i^{n}\Gamma(n+2\lambda)}{n!2^{\lambda}\Gamma(\lambda)}\frac{J_{n+\lambda}(\omega)}{\omega^{\lambda}}

(A.19)

which plays the role of Jacobi-Anger in introducing Bessel functions into our analysis. In the end we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{F}\left[f\right](\vec{P})=&\frac{2^{3-N}\pi\Omega_{N-2}}{(\pi\rho)^{\frac{N}{2}-1}}\frac{(N-3)!}{\Gamma\left(\frac{N}{2}-1\right)}\sum_{n,l}(-i)^{n}Y_{n,l}(\hat{\rho})\\
&\times\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}r\,r^{\frac{N}{2}}g_{n,l}(r)J_{n-1+N/2}(2\pi\rho r).\end{split}

(A.20)

In even dimensions,
eq.

A.20
is often preferable to
eq.

A.16
because the angular modes are cleanly separated and the remaining integrals are all integer-order Bessel integrals precisely of the kind we have studied in this paper with the focal expansion. In odd dimensions, however, we get integrals over half-integer order Bessel functions which we have not thoroughly studied. That is not to say we necessarily expect the focal expansion to fail for half-integer order Bessel kernels, but the analysis of section
II.4
would need to be repeated for this new case. In addition, some comparisons to numerics and simple analytically workable examples (in the spirit of section
III
) would need to be done to gain confidence in the convergence properties and behavior of our approach for this class of kernels. We leave this avenue for future work.

Appendix B
Solving the General Focal Recurrence

In this appendix we investigate ways to solve the recurrence relations

(B.3)

filler

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}i^{0_{\pm}}\alpha_{m}(2m)^{\downarrow}_{2k+0_{\pm}}.

Assume we have found the first

(B.5)

With this

B.2

\displaystyle\begin{split}Y^{\pm}_{k}(c)=&\sum_{l=0}^{k}N^{\pm}_{k,l}(c)B^{\pm}_{l}(c),\\

It remains to be seen if the general focal expansion is useful beyond the case of Bessel function kernels. We have seen in section
III
it works very well for integer order Bessel kernels and so intuitively we suspect that it will also work well for functions ‘close’ (in some sense) to integer order Bessels, but the two counter-examples of this appendix temper our hopes for generic kernels. At the very least, we see no reason to expect the ‘pincer maneuver’ reasoning present in the case of Bessel kernels is replicated for other generic kernels. Perhaps one can tweak the focusing operators or the exact form of our expansion based on what family of kernels one is dealing with, but we leave all such analysis to future work.
