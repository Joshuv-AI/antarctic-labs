Beyond the cruise ship: Andes hantavirus and the neglected one health dimensions of global outbreak preparedness
Authors: Chidozie Declan Iwu, Christina Pettan-Brewer, Chinwe Iwu-Jaja, Peter Rabinowitz, Julianne Meisner
Published: 2026

The ongoing multi-country outbreak of Andes hantavirus linked to an expedition cruise travel has drawn global public health attention. The outbreak was reported to the World Health Organization on 02 May 2026 after a cluster of severe respiratory illness occurred among passengers and crew aboard the MV Hondius, which travelled across remote regions of the South Atlantic following departure from Ushuaia, Argentina.1 As of 18 May 2026, the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) reported 12 cases, including nine confirmed, two probable, and one inconclusive case, as well as three deaths linked to the outbreak.2 While response efforts have appropriately focused on case detection, isolation, contact monitoring, and clinical management, the event highlights broader gaps in how emerging zoonotic threats are managed globally. Rather than viewing this solely as a cruise-associated outbreak, it should also be understood as a One Health warning signal emerging at the intersection of rodent ecology, environmental and climatic change, expedition tourism, and global human mobility.

Hantaviruses are rodent-borne pathogens transmitted primarily through exposure to infected rodent urine, saliva, or faeces.3 Andes virus, which circulates mainly in Argentina and Chile, is one of the few hantaviruses for which limited person-to-person transmission has been documented, particularly following prolonged close contact.4 In the Americas, Andes virus causes hantavirus pulmonary syndrome (HPS) characterized by fever, myalgia, gastrointestinal symptoms, and rapidly progressive respiratory failure.3 Although such transmission appears relatively uncommon, its possibility has heightened international concern surrounding the current outbreak.

Despite longstanding understanding of the ecological drivers of hantavirus transmission in South America, the current outbreak exposes important blind spots in preparedness strategies for zoonotic diseases linked to environmental exposure during travel. The practice of international travel medicine has historically focused on vaccine-preventable, foodborne, vector-borne (such as malaria), and respiratory infections.5 Comparatively, less attention has been paid to environmentally mediated zoonoses acquired directly or indirectly from animals during ecological tourism, contact with animal agriculture, wilderness excursions, or remote expedition activities. Yet, adventure tourism and ecotourism have expanded substantially over recent decades, bringing increasing numbers of travellers into close contact with wildlife habitats and environmentally sensitive regions.6 Therefore, there is a need for the clinical travel history to more comprehensively assess zoonotic disease risk.

The outbreak also highlights the importance of environmental and climatic drivers in shaping hantavirus transmission. Rodent population and hantavirus spread are strongly influenced by environmental conditions and climatic fluctuations, including changes in precipitation and vegetation growth that may increase opportunities for human exposure.7 As climate change continues to alter ecosystems globally, the distribution and spread of rodent-borne pathogens may become increasingly difficult to predict. Despite growing recognition of the relationship between environmental change and infectious disease emergence, these factors remain insufficiently integrated into many preparedness systems. Better understanding of these drivers may improve prediction and prevention efforts for hantavirus and other zoonotic pathogens.

The outbreak also highlights challenges in risk communication and global prioritization of emerging zoonotic threats. Although current evidence suggests that person-to-person transmission of Andes hantavirus is relatively uncommon,8 public concern surrounding pandemic potential has been considerable. At the same time, the outbreak exposes broader inequities in how global health systems prioritize zoonotic diseases. Hantavirus has affected communities across Latin America for decades, particularly in rural settings where human exposure to rodent reservoirs is common.9 However, international concern intensified only after infections involved travellers from high-income countries aboard an internationally visible cruise vessel. This reflects persistent inequities in global outbreak response, where zoonotic diseases affecting rural and historically underserved populations often receive limited visibility until they threaten international mobility or higher-income populations. As a result, preparedness efforts remain largely reactive, focusing on border measures and emergency response after international spread has occurred rather than sustained investment in upstream prevention and public health capacity within endemic communities.

Building on these gaps, preparedness for hantavirus and other rodent-borne zoonoses should be strengthened through integrated One Health approaches aligned with selected action tracks from the 2022–2026 One Health Joint Plan of Action,10 particularly those focused on strengthening health systems, reducing zoonotic epidemic risks, controlling endemic zoonoses, and integrating environmental considerations into preparedness efforts. This will require sustained investment in surveillance, diagnostics, laboratory preparedness, clinical readiness, travel medicine infrastructure, and equitable public health capacity in endemic communities historically affected by limited global attention. Preparedness should also shift beyond reactive travel-associated responses toward upstream prevention strategies, including ecological surveillance, rodent reservoir monitoring, climate-informed risk prediction, environmental risk reduction, and integrated early warning systems capable of identifying spillover risks before international dissemination occurs.
