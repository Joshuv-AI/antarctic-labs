Intensive care under uncertainty: a qualitative study of how older patients and their relatives wish to contribute to decision-making
Authors: Gabriele Leonie Schwarz, Britt Ågot Sjøbø, Elisabeth Skaar, Ingrid Miljeteig, Reidar Kvåle, Hans Flaatten, Karen E. A. Burns, Paul K. J. Han, Margrethe Schaufel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeGiven the importance of shared decision-making in critical illness in advanced age, we aimed to explore how very old patients and their next of kin envision their contribution to the decision-making process.MethodsFrom November 2021 to March 2023, we conducted a survey among Norwegian outpatients aged 80 and over and their next of kin. We studied their perspectives on hypothetical scenarios of acute, life-threatening illness warranting admission to the intensive care unit (ICU). In this sub-study, we conducted cross-case thematic analysis of free-text responses addressing patients’ and next-of-kins’ participation in shared decision-making.ResultsOf 202 very old and 153 next-of-kin respondents, 210 participants provided 339 free-text comments relevant to this research question. Vi identified five main categories, describing how participants wished to contribute to the decision process: (1) Seeking scientific knowledge, (2) Contributing personal knowledge, (3) Tailoring care to the heterogeneity of older age, (4) Improving the process of decision-making, and (5) Strengthening security. Applying a framework for medical uncertainty and uncertainty tolerance, we found that all five of them comprised ways of coping with or adapting to the multidimensional uncertainty characterising clinical decisions when very old patients fall critically ill.ConclusionThese findings highlight the need to include patients’ and family members’ personal knowledge in clinical decision-making, and to facilitate the presence of next of kin when very old patients require critical care.Trail registrationClinicalTrails.gov NCT05149040 (October 31, 2021)Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s41999-026-01523-w.

Introduction

Shared decision-making, the collaborative process of integrating the best available scientific evidence with patient values and preferences, is particularly important when very old patients fall critically ill, since often several levels of treatment intensity may be equally relevant options [1, 2]. Conversely, decision-making is particularly difficult because most older patients are incapacitated at the time of intensive care unit (ICU) admission [3], and measures to predict patient preferences (e.g., advance directives or substituted judgment) have important limitations [4–7].

The benefit of intensive care for very old patients (in this context, those aged 80 or older, hereafter called older) remains a subject of debate [8, 9], and prognostic uncertainty is often significant due to the inherent and multifaceted heterogeneity of advanced age [10–15]. However, even when optimism from a medical perspective is justified, the likely outcomes may not align with patients’ wishes and values for various reasons [16–18].

In a recent survey, we found that older patients’ ICU admission preferences varied widely and were not correlated with the most reliable markers of ICU survival. Moreover, their treatment preferences were difficult for their next of kin to predict, which contrasted with respondents’ high levels of confidence in both their treatment preferences and the accuracy of proxies’ predictions [19].

Participating in difficult decisions under medical uncertainty may have both positive and negative consequences, as it may offer an opportunity to personalise treatment or become a burden to vulnerable patients and their next of kin, or both at the same time [20–22].

Although shared decision-making for complex treatment decisions is increasingly promoted in intensive care, knowledge remains limited about how older patients and their families envision their involvement in the process. Therefore, we aimed to address this knowledge gap by analysing free-text survey responses from older adults and their next of kin regarding their contributions to shared decision-making in cases of critical illness in advanced age.

Methods

Study design and setting

We adopted a social constructivist approach to better understand these complex human interactions, to bracket our preconceptions, and to reflect on how our professional backgrounds may influence our interpretations of survey participants’ expressions. We approached the research question as a multidisciplinary team representing intensive care medicine (GLS, RK, HF, KB), intensive care nursing (BAS), geriatric medicine (ES), cardiology (ES, MAS), pulmonology (MAS), palliative medicine (MS, PH), medical ethics (IM, PH) and behavioural science (PH). We have all experienced high-stakes clinical decision-making, often burdened by substantial and multifaceted uncertainty. Our basic assumption was that integrating patient preferences into decisions regarding treatment intensity in the event of critical illness in advanced age could reduce uncertainty about the value of life-sustaining treatment interventions for individual older patients.

Power imbalances are inherent in patient-healthcare provider relationships. They also affect how clinicians and scientists perceive interactions between laypeople and medical staff, as well as how study participants respond to our questions about these interactions. We believe that high-stakes situations involving critical illness and matters of life and death are particularly sensitive to these power imbalances. Additionally, if we genuinely aim at improving shared decision-making in critical illnesses, we need deeper insight into patients’ and their families’ notions from within their own lifeworld.

The Norwegian healthcare system is characterised by universal health coverage and publicly funded services. An independent government agency (www.npe.no/en/) provides a free system for handling compensation claims, with very few cases proceeding to court. Trust levels are high in the Norwegian society, including in healthcare encounters, as evidenced by the absence of written consent for medical interventions [23–25]. Living wills and designated proxies are rare and not legally binding in Norway. When next of kin and healthcare professionals cannot agree on treatment limitations, Norwegian law grants decision-making authority to the treating medical team, although a recent study found that public attitudes do not fully align with this [26]. Older patients are admitted to the ICU in Norway as often as in Western countries, but decisions to limit life-sustaining treatment are made more frequently and earlier during an ICU stay [27, 28]. Nonetheless, survival rates and long-term outcomes for older critically ill patients are similar to those in other Western healthcare systems [27, 29, 30].

Data collection

The data in this study form a subset of data collected in the published study on ICU admission preferences among very old Norwegians and their next of kin in the hypothetical event of acute critical illness [19]. Participants in that study were purposively sampled from outpatients aged 80 years and older who would typically be considered potential ICU candidates in the Norwegian health care context, along with their next of kin. The older study participants were recruited from November 2021 to March 2023 at several outpatient clinics at Haukeland University Hospital, Bergen, Norway. The next-of-kin respondents were identified by older study participants as their likely proxy decision makers in the event of a health care emergency.

Older patient–next of kin dyads completed matching questionnaires to examine older patients' treatment preferences and their next of kin's understanding of these preferences in three hypothetical scenarios of acute, life-threatening illness. Respondents were instructed to assume that, with their own health and background, they were the patients described in the case vignettes. Scenarios were created using randomly selected cases of acute critical illness in advanced age. All cases required escalation from ward care to intensive care and involved sedation and invasive organ support modalities. The scenarios also included a brief description of treatment options and estimated short- and long-term outcomes. Additionally, we collected demographic and health-related participant data, along with information on advance care planning, shared decision-making, and family involvement. Questionnaire development and validation, as well as sample size calculation, have been published [19]. Examples of the questionnaires are provided in the electronic supplementary information.

The primary focus of the current sub-study was a free-text question: “Can you explain how you wish that your next of kin / you as the next of kin, may contribute to decision-making in such a situation?” Relevant responses to another free-text question asking for “additional comments” at the end of the questionnaire were also included in the current analysis.

Analysis

We summarised quantitative data to characterise the study population using SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 26.0, Armonk, NY, IBM Corp, 2019).

Qualitative data were processed in Microsoft Excel and Microsoft Word (Version 2402, Redmond, WA, Microsoft, 2024). We identified and included all free-text comments from older patients’ and next of kins' questionnaires that addressed aspects of shared decision-making.

Cross-case thematic analysis was conducted by Systematic Text Condensation (STC), a pragmatic method developed for the medical context within a moderate social constructivist tradition [31, 32]. We conducted the analysis manually and documented the process in an audit trail. During steps one and two of the analysis, we developed the main categories inductively from the empirical data. When we started to work on the data, we noticed the absence of verbal expressions of uncertainty. Notwithstanding, we found that all five main findings emerged as strategies for coping with medical uncertainty. Therefore, during steps three and four of the analysis, we drew upon the framework of uncertainty tolerance and adaptation by Paul Han [33, 34] in an editing analysis style [35].

Participants

Seeking scientific knowledge

Some respondents expressed a lack of knowledge regarding the critical conditions of older patients presented in the case vignettes. They found the medical information insufficient to express a treatment preference or to actively participate in decision-making. They requested more facts, including more accurate prognostic predictions, risk–benefit ratios and the burden of different treatment options.“Facts, facts, facts… fact-based information. The likelihood for survival in the actual case, not only overall assessments.” (Patient, 89 yrs, male)

“Facts, facts, facts… fact-based information. The likelihood for survival in the actual case, not only overall assessments.” (Patient, 89 yrs, male)

A few participants were also interested in their healthcare providers’ prognostic estimates based on their own personal experiences and clinical gestalt. Several respondents requested that all medical information be shared with the patient or the proxy decision-maker, frequently emphasising that this should be done respectfully and compassionately.“I would wish to be presented all possible alternatives and experiential trajectories/consequences for my patient.” (Daughter, 60 yrs)

“I would wish to be presented all possible alternatives and experiential trajectories/consequences for my patient.” (Daughter, 60 yrs)

One older respondent suggested that the next of kin should have access to electronic patient records to acquire sufficient knowledge to make a treatment decision.

Contributing personal knowledge

Many participants perceived their role as one of contributing personal information essential to making the right decision for the individual patient, drawing on prior knowledge and experience. They saw the next of kin as most capable of comprehending the full picture of general health and acute illness, including the older patient's values, personal and past health-related experiences, current general condition, and desired future outcomes. Frequently, the latter was legitimised by prior conversations about end-of-life issues.“I know how her situation was before she fell ill, I know how she used to be after recovering from illness, I know what is important for her, and what would constitute a good life for her after having gone through illness. I know her quite well, her mental and physical strength.” (Relative, 57 yrs, female)

“I know how her situation was before she fell ill, I know how she used to be after recovering from illness, I know what is important for her, and what would constitute a good life for her after having gone through illness. I know her quite well, her mental and physical strength.” (Relative, 57 yrs, female)

The prevailing view was that the patient or their next of kin should play a vital role in the decision-making process. Many explained that general biographical details, personal traits, and personal values of the older patient were important for adding context to health-related information. Several respondents mentioned that their knowledge would compensate for, or even replace, incomplete medical knowledge.“This is important, the next of kin knows the person/patient better and in a different way than an unfamiliar doctor […] who only sees the patient as a patient.” (Patient, 81 yrs, female)

“This is important, the next of kin knows the person/patient better and in a different way than an unfamiliar doctor […] who only sees the patient as a patient.” (Patient, 81 yrs, female)

Tailoring care to the heterogeneity of older age

Another common strategy among older patients and their next of kin when facing prognostic uncertainty was to find predictability by formulating a personal prognosis based on the individual patient’s life trajectory. They tried to answer whether the patient’s life was waning despite medical efforts. Many participants distinguished between fit and frail older patients, arguing that decisions to provide intensive care should not be based on age alone, but on the likelihood of an outcome that an individual older patient considered meaningful, based on their own level of physical and mental functioning.“There are huge differences among people in their 80s. Some behave as if they are 100 years old, others as if they were 20 years old, in terms of being active.” (Son, 40 yrs)

“There are huge differences among people in their 80s. Some behave as if they are 100 years old, others as if they were 20 years old, in terms of being active.” (Son, 40 yrs)

In this context, several respondents noted that a dignified closure would be the preferred outcome for frail older patients, while pursuing survival to independence and other indicators of good quality of life would be most relevant for fit older patients.“My answers have so far been based on the fact that she, for the time being, has a good quality of life, is independent and active, both physically and mentally. If she had a much worse quality of life, my answers would maybe be different.” (Daughter, 52 yrs)

“My answers have so far been based on the fact that she, for the time being, has a good quality of life, is independent and active, both physically and mentally. If she had a much worse quality of life, my answers would maybe be different.” (Daughter, 52 yrs)

Improving the process of decision-making

Open, honest, empathetic, and timely communication was frequently highlighted as an important, if not the most important, aspect of decision-making. Most older and next-of-kin respondents described a process where healthcare personnel provided medical information, while patients and family members contributed biographical and personal details. Regarding decisional authority, participants outlined the full range of options and suggested improvements. Those who believed healthcare personnel should make the decision emphasised that the patient and the next of kin should be listened to and kept informed. For those favouring shared decision-making, avoiding conflict was an important concern. And for those who thought that the patient or their proxy should be responsible for the decision, seeking advice from healthcare professionals was essential. Additionally, several respondents stated that the next of kin had a duty to act as the patient's advocate, particularly to ensure the patient was not in pain or distress. Only a few participants expressed that the next of kin should not be burdened with such high-stakes decisions.“Good and open communication between the ones who care for me, and my 3 children. That my children are taken on board as co-players and not left outside.” (Patient, 82 yrs, female)“I think it is wrong that my next of kin should be responsible for my life and death.” (Patient, 89 yrs, male)

The importance of being prepared for decision-making in an uncertain future was a common perception among older and next-of-kin respondents. However, many also noted that these are difficult questions that could be easily postponed. Despite this, they highlighted the value of having thought them through without the pressure of an emergency. Some participants acknowledged that one can never be certain how one will respond when such questions become real, even if they have been contemplated beforehand. Many referred to participating in the study as a starting point for discussion and conversation about these topics. Strengthening security

A common term used by both older and next-of-kin respondents to describe how they would contribute to shared decision-making in the event of a critical illness in advanced age was “trygghet”. This Norwegian term includes both objective security and the subjective feeling of being well cared for. Hereafter, we will use the English word “security”, recognizing that it does not fully capture the meaning of “trygghet.”

Many respondents offered strong descriptions of the crucial role that family members play in the lives of older people and, consequently, should also be involved in these decision-making processes. They stated that the family was most capable of creating a safe space when life or death was at stake, and that getting the decision “right” in terms of alignment with the older patient’s treatment preferences was less important.It is my daughter who has taken over many tasks I can no longer complete myself. She makes me feel safe, and I fully trust her. I also have a grandchild (her daughter) who is a nurse. I have close contact with her and a good relationship, too. I trust them completely to make the best decisions, even if these should differ from my own thoughts. (Patient, 88 yrs, male)

I have close contact with her and a good relationship, too. I trust them completely to make the best decisions, even if these should differ from my own thoughts. (Patient, 88 yrs, male)

Some respondents described the intensive care environment and personnel as technical, knowledgeable, and professional, but also as unfamiliar, emotionally cold, and sometimes frightening. Several respondents explained that family members’ involvement in critical care decisions is vital not only for the patient but also for relatives of critically ill patients, helping them cope with the situation. They emphasised that the contributions of next of kin are essential for maintaining humanistic care in these settings.“The doctors will decide what the right [action] is. In the medical sense. Not in the human [sense]. (Patient, 90 yrs, female)

Discussion

In this qualitative study of survey free-text comments, we explored how older Norwegians and their next of kin view their contribution to shared decision-making in the hypothetical event of acute critical illness. Using cross-case thematic analysis, we identified five main contributions they envisioned themselves making to the decision process. We interpreted those main result categories as ways of coping with medical uncertainty, applying a framework of uncertainty tolerance and adaptation [33, 34].

The nature of medical uncertainty

Undoubtedly, medical uncertainty resided in our minds when conceiving the study, designing the questionnaires and analysing the results. Drawing on our clinical experience and socialisation into a positivist medical-scientific tradition, we postulated that uncertainty about various scientific issues, including prognosis and treatment, was the primary concern in the intensive care of older patients. We found support for this notion in the growing literature on variation in intensive care provision to older patients [37–41].

Upon receiving the completed questionnaires, entering and analysing data, we were struck by the apparent incongruence between (i) medical uncertainty explicitly and repeatedly posed throughout the questionnaires, with wide variation and unpredictability in older patients’ treatment preferences, and (ii) high levels of confidence and the absence of expressions of uncertainty in participant responses. However, when viewing the responses through a medical uncertainty frame, we realised that both older and next-of-kin participants’ contributions to the shared decision-making process represented different strategies for managing medical uncertainty. Some participants proposed strategies to reduce medical uncertainty by either seeking additional medical knowledge or contributing their personal knowledge to medical decisions. In most responses, though, and in the qualitative “thicker” descriptions, the study participants accepted medical uncertainty as irreducible and focused on various strategies for adapting to it.

Reflecting on the different strategies patients and their next of kin use to manage medical uncertainty may help healthcare professionals understand the relational and co-constructed nature of uncertainty [36]. Patients and their families may not only have varying levels of awareness about scientific uncertainty but also experience medical uncertainty in different ways and hold their own independent epistemological justifications. Therefore, joint efforts towards a shared understanding of the uncertainties involved in ICU admission decisions for older critically ill patients are a crucial part of shared decision-making in these complex situations.

The opportunities of medical uncertainty

Many respondents in our survey suggested tailoring treatment intensity according to the patient's life trajectory. They focused not on estimated outcomes at the group level but on what they perceived as the appropriate action at the individual level, extrapolated from the older respondent’s life course, especially his or her unique way of ageing. These respondents often shared detailed narratives about the older patient’s life and perceived medical uncertainty about potential actions as opportunities to personalise care. In intensive care, medical uncertainty is typically addressed by efforts to reduce statistical uncertainty through improved predictive models and large-scale quantitative studies. Individual variations have usually been perceived as unintended results of biases [28, 40]. Conversely, in precision medicine, the relationship between medical uncertainty and personalised treatment, and its implications for clinical practice, have been widely studied [42, 43].

Another prevailing strategy among our survey respondents was to move beyond uncertain outcomes and focus on the quality of the decision-making process, patients' and relatives' preparedness for decision-making, and on ensuring honesty, empathy, and good communication. We find this pragmatic approach very much in keeping with the central aim of “uncertainty tolerance” as proposed by Han and Hofmann: “… a shift in focus in our efforts to manage uncertainty: from outcomes to the human capacities that enable them, from the important but unanswerable questions of what will happen and what to do to the equally important but answerable question of how to be.” [34] Han and Hofmann further propose a set of key virtues as instrumental capacities that enable tolerance of uncertainty (Fig. 3). A final important finding from our data was the contribution of patients and relatives in filling gaps in scientific knowledge through emotional support and loving presence. Many of our study participants accepted that no amount of additional knowledge could enable us to make treatment decisions with complete certainty in the event of acute life-threatening illness in advanced age. They offered strong descriptions of how longstanding, trusting family relationships could instead provide a comforting sense of security when facing inevitable medical uncertainty. Being together during life-threatening illness after having lived together for decades was a prevailing request, regardless of the preferred treatment intensity. We sadly realised that this most prominent wish, in many cases, could not be accommodated in the ICUs we know. ICUs are poorly designed, equipped and organised for facilitating older couples to spend what might be the last hours of life together, especially when the patient’s spouse or partner lives with reduced physical or cognitive function.

Adapting to medical uncertainty

Uncertainty tolerance has for some time been promoted as a means to explain variations in how medical professionals, patients, and their families cope with medical uncertainty, and as a framework for educating them and strengthening their resilience [44, 45]. In the medical field, recent insights into the more complex nature of uncertainty have shifted the focus towards uncertainty adaptation, suggesting that uncertainty is not solely negative and that effective responses to it may go beyond simply enduring it [34]. Many survey participants in our study contributed to high-stakes shared decision-making by adopting strategies to navigate medical uncertainty with a positive, creative, and empathetic attitude.

Strength and limitations

The free-text comments made by older survey respondents and their next of kin on their contribution to ICU admission decisions offer new insights into the importance and opportunities of shared uncertainty management as a key part of shared decision-making in cases of critical illness in advanced age. Although derived from a relatively homogeneous sample within a limited healthcare context, our findings highlight more fundamental aspects of the need to incorporate the human and familial experience of ageing and dying into medical standards for critical care in advanced age. They may, therefore, be transferable to other Western intensive care settings.

Survey research on such complex and sensitive topics is prone to sampling and response biases, as well as the inherent limitations of using hypothetical scenarios to elicit respondents’ thoughts and notions [46–49]. However, many of our participants referred to previous experiences with life-threatening illnesses, either as patients or close relatives, which informed their free-text responses and enhanced their validity.

The framing and placement of the free-text questions within the questionnaires may have influenced responses. Since we collected static written accounts, we could not personally engage with the respondents, limiting the depth of the data despite the large volume of text included in the analysis [31, 50]. We applied the factors suggested by Malterud and colleagues to determine information power [51]. The large, highly specific sample in relation to a narrow study aim, and the use of a clinically relevant theoretical framework, strengthened our study but did not fully offset the weaknesses which we attribute to the lack of dialogue, temporality and idiography.

Lastly, we consider both our own, the medical professionals’, and the patients’ and families’ knowledge as situated, embodied, partial, and shaped by a specific context [52]. We explored the boundaries of medical-scientific knowledge about the meaning of critical illness and the entanglement of life and death in older patients from within those boundaries. The power imbalances, as initially addressed, may limit the impact of our study, especially its ability to bring about genuine change [53].

Conclusion

In this qualitative study of survey free-text comments, we found that older adults and their next of kin wished to contribute in various ways to shared decision-making and to managing uncertainty in cases of critical illness. These included seeking medical knowledge and offering their own personal knowledge and ways of knowing, which were legitimised through emotional qualities, continuity and trustworthiness. They also contributed by fostering security and humanity during critical illness among older adults and their next of kin, building on the unique knowledge gained through longstanding, familiar relationships rather than medical knowledge. These findings highlight the need to include patients’ and family members’ personal knowledge in clinical decision-making, and to promote co-caring for older patients with their families, especially spouses or partners, in intensive care.
