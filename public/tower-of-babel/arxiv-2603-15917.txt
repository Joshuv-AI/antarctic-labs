Bayesian-guided inverse design of hyperelastic microstructures: Application to stochastic metamaterials Journal: Nuclear Physics B
Hooman Danesh, Henning Wessels

Abstract
From a given pool of all feasible design variants, our aim is to identify a structure that achieves a target macroscopic stress response. For each candidate design, the response is obtained from a high-fidelity oracle, in particular, time- and resource-intensive computational homogenization or experiments. We consider the case where (i) the geometry cannot be conveniently parameterized, rendering gradient-based optimization inapplicable, and (ii) brute-force evaluation of all candidates is infeasible due to the cost of oracle queries. To tackle this challenge, we propose a Bayesian-guided inverse design framework that proceeds as follows. First, the dimensionality of the design variants is reduced through statistical feature engineering, and the resulting low-dimensional descriptors are mapped to effective constitutive parameters describing the macroscopic hyperelastic response. This mapping is modeled using a multi-output Gaussian process surrogate that accounts for correlations between the parameters. The surrogate is trained using uncertainty-driven active learning under severe budget constraints, allowing only a very limited number of high-fidelity oracle evaluations. Based on surrogate predictions, a finite number of promising candidates are shortlisted. Since the surrogate accuracy is inherently limited, the final selection of the optimal design is performed through high-fidelity oracle evaluations within the shortlist. In numerical test cases, we consider a dataset of 50,000 candidate structures. Active learning requires labeling less than half a percent of the full dataset. Bayesian-guided inverse design under unseen loading conditions reaches a prescribed error threshold with only a handful of oracle evaluations in the majority of cases.

Introduction

Understanding and predicting the behavior of complex systems has long been a central objective across scientific and engineering disciplines. Considerable effort has therefore been devoted to developing accurate physical models and advanced experimental techniques that enable the efficient solution of
forward problems
, in which the response of a system is computed for a given set of input parameters or input functions. In contrast, the corresponding
inverse problems
, which aim to determine the design parameters required to achieve a desired target response, remain challenging to solve. The main difficulty arises from the fact that inverse design typically requires a large number of forward evaluations in order to identify suitable solutions. Such evaluations, whether obtained from high-fidelity numerical simulations or experimental measurements, are often computationally or experimentally expensive. Consequently, it is highly desirable to develop inverse design frameworks that can identify optimal designs while requiring as few forward evaluations as possible.

Traditional approaches to inverse design often rely on gradient-based optimization, where an objective function quantifying the mismatch between the obtained and desired response is minimized. While effective in many applications, these methods require the gradients of the system outputs with respect to the design parameters to be readily computable. In practice, this requirement is not always satisfied. Many engineering design problems involve complex geometries or discrete design variables that cannot be easily parameterized, making gradient-based methods difficult to apply. Furthermore, the enormous size of the design space often makes exhaustive exploration through brute-force evaluation infeasible. To address these challenges, several computational approaches have been proposed in recent years.

Surrogate models for inverse design

A common strategy for addressing expensive forward evaluations is the construction of surrogate models that approximate the underlying physics. Once trained, such models can rapidly predict system responses, enabling efficient exploration of the design space without repeatedly invoking high-fidelity simulations or experiments. Surrogate-based inverse design has been successfully applied across a wide range of architected material systems. For example, machine learning surrogates such as random forests have been used to approximate the mechanical response of parametrized auxetic unit cells
[
14
]
, while physics-augmented neural networks have been coupled with evolutionary strategies to recover anisotropic microstructural parameters
[
29
]
. Deep learning approaches have also enabled mappings from desired mechanical responses to structural architectures, including physics-constrained neural networks for truss metamaterials
[
7
]
, tandem neural networks for spinodoid microstructures
[
36
]
, and artificial neural networks linking target stress–strain curves to semi-auxetic microstructures
[
47
]
. More recently, surrogate models have been extended to geometry-rich representations, where the design variables correspond directly to structural images or graphs. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been used for direct inverse prediction of layered thin film materials
[
37
]
, while graph-based learning approaches such as graph neural networks (GNNs) have been employed to capture connectivity in helicoidal laminates
[
22
]
, irregular lattice materials
[
18
]
, and nonlinear truss metamaterials
[
43
]
.

Despite its advantages, surrogate-based inverse design does not by itself eliminate the central bottleneck of the problem: the surrogate must first be trained on sufficiently informative data. In practice, this often requires a large number of high-fidelity evaluations to cover the relevant design space with adequate accuracy, particularly when the input consists of complex microstructures rather than a few low-dimensional parameters. This trade-off is evident across recent applications, where the speed gained at inference time is preceded by a nontrivial cost in generating training data. As a result, surrogate models are highly effective once available, but their construction can remain expensive when the accessible design space is large, high-dimensional, or only weakly parameterized.

Bayesian optimization for data-efficient design

Bayesian optimization addresses the data-efficiency issue more directly by coupling a probabilistic surrogate, often a Gaussian process (GP), with an acquisition function that balances exploration and exploitation. This framework has been successfully applied to a variety of inverse design problems in materials science. Examples include the design of spinodoid metamaterials within parameterized spaces
[
55
]
, optimization of Bézier-parameterized auxetic unit cells with respect to mechanical performance and fatigue life
[
34
]
, inference of nonlinear interface laws in bio-inspired composites from target stress–strain curves
[
66
]
, complex block copolymer systems
[
19
]
, and discovery of process–microstructure relationships through batch Bayesian optimization
[
27
]
.

The primary strength of Bayesian optimization lies in its ability to locate optimal solutions using relatively few high-fidelity evaluations. However, its effectiveness is closely linked to its sequential and target-specific nature. In a typical workflow, the surrogate model is constructed and iteratively refined for a specific objective or target response. The surrogate therefore evolves specifically to guide the search toward this desired target response. When the target response changes, the optimization procedure must typically be restarted, requiring the construction of a new surrogate model and the repetition of the associated hyperparameter optimization. This process can become computationally demanding, particularly when the surrogate must be repeatedly rebuilt and refined for multiple inverse design queries. As a result, Bayesian optimization is particularly effective when addressing individual inverse design tasks under limited evaluation budgets, but it becomes less attractive in scenarios where many target responses must be explored within the same design space.

Generative models for design exploration

When the design variables correspond to complex geometries rather than low-dimensional parameter sets, inverse design becomes considerably more challenging. To address this issue, several studies have proposed generative modeling approaches that learn a low-dimensional latent representation of the design space. Optimization or sampling can then be performed in this latent space to generate candidate structures with desired properties. Examples include generative models for microstructure generation and inverse design based on variational autoencoders
[
60
,
42
,

,
68
,
50
,

]
, as well as normalizing flows for design of porous and stochastic microstructures

,

. Variational autoencoders combined with Bayesian optimization have further been used to explore latent spaces for designing manufacturable phononic metaplates
[
20
]
. Bayesian generative formulations combining latent representations with GP likelihood models have likewise been explored
[
23
]
. Generative models have also been integrated with active learning strategies for designing polycrystalline textures with target anisotropic responses
[
10
]
. A related but distinct line of work employs diffusion-based generative models rather than explicit latent space optimization; for instance, conditional diffusion has been used to generate level-set-parameterized lattice unit cells from target elastic properties
[
65

, while video denoising diffusion models have been used to generate metamaterial microstructures from nonlinear target stress–strain responses
[

These approaches provide a powerful mechanism for exploring high-dimensional geometric design spaces without requiring explicit parameterization of structures. However, they also introduce several practical challenges. Training generative models typically requires large and representative datasets in order to learn meaningful latent representations. In addition, the structures generated in latent space are not always guaranteed to satisfy manufacturability constraints or geometric integrity unless these constraints are explicitly embedded into the learning process. In many cases, additional reconstruction or post-processing steps are therefore required to obtain physically realizable geometries.

Our Bayesian-guided discrete inverse design framework

Motivated by the limitations of existing inverse design strategies, we propose a data-efficient framework for discrete inverse design under a limited budget of high-fidelity evaluations. A schematic overview of the proposed workflow is shown in
figure

1
. In essence, a data-efficient surrogate model is used to screen a large pool of candidate designs and identify a shortlist of promising structures, after which high-fidelity physics-based evaluations determine the final design. Rather than performing optimization in a continuous latent space, the proposed approach operates on a predefined pool of admissible candidate structures. This perspective is particularly suitable for problems where microstructural geometries cannot be conveniently parameterized and exhaustive evaluation of all candidates using high-fidelity evaluations is impractical. The framework is built upon the following key components:

Figure 1:
Schematic overview of the proposed Bayesian-guided discrete inverse design framework. A data-efficient surrogate model guides the exploration of a large candidate design space toward a shortlist of promising structures, while high-fidelity physics-based evaluations ultimately decide the final design.

•

Discrete design space formulation.

The inverse design problem is formulated as a
selection task
over a finite library of candidate structures. Since the design pool is defined
a priori
, geometric feasibility and manufacturability are guaranteed by construction. This avoids the need for microstructure reconstruction or generative modeling, which can introduce additional complexity and may not always guarantee physically realizable designs.

•

Compact structural feature representation.

As raw microstructure images are inherently high-dimensional, statistical feature engineering is employed to extract compact descriptors that retain the essential structural information. This representation enables efficient surrogate learning while avoiding the need to train complex neural networks to learn geometric embeddings or latent representations of the design space.

•

Probabilistic surrogate modeling with uncertainty-driven active learning.
A probabilistic surrogate model is constructed to approximate the structure–property relationship using only a small number of labeled samples. To minimize the required high-fidelity evaluations, uncertainty-driven active learning is employed, in which the predictive uncertainty of the surrogate guides the selection of new candidate structures whose evaluations provide the greatest information gain. In contrast to Bayesian optimization frameworks, where a surrogate model is typically constructed and iteratively refined for a specific target during the optimization process, the surrogate in the present framework is trained only once to represent the entire admissible design space. Once constructed, it can therefore be reused to support multiple inverse design queries without requiring repeated surrogate training or hyperparameter optimization.

•

Uncertainty-aware candidate screening.

Once trained, the surrogate efficiently evaluates the candidate pool during the inverse design stage. Promising structures are identified based on their predicted proximity to the target response, while predictive uncertainty is incorporated into the ranking to penalize unreliable predictions. In contrast to gradient-based optimization methods, which may converge to local minima in complex design landscapes, this screening approach enables robust exploration of the discrete design space.

•

Physics-based final decision.

The final design is always determined through a small number of high-fidelity oracle evaluations. In this way, the surrogate
guides the search
, while the governing physics ultimately
decides the final design
. This separation between surrogate guidance and physics-based validation leads to a non-intrusive framework in which the machine learning model does not need to explicitly enforce physical constraints or governing equations.

The proposed framework is applied to the inverse design of hyperelastic microstructures, where the objective is to identify a structure whose effective macroscopic stress response matches a prescribed target behavior. In this setting, the design space consists of a large pool of admissible stochastic metamaterials, from which the framework efficiently identifies candidates that achieve the desired response using only a small number of high-fidelity oracle evaluations.

Bayesian-guided discrete inverse design

In many applications, the admissible design space is known
a priori
, for instance, through combinatorial construction rules, manufacturing constraints, or a precomputed database of representative designs. This motivates a discrete inverse design formulation in which the goal is to
select
the best structure from a finite set of candidate structures
\mathcal{D}=\{\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{d}}
with the index set
\mathcal{I}=\{1,\dots,n_{d}\}
, where
n_{d}
is the total number of designs. Throughout this contribution, but without loss of generality, each design
\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}}
is a discretized microstructure represented as a 2D/3D bitmap with
n_{p}
pixels/voxels. The inverse design task is to identify the structure
\bm{\mathrm{M}}^{\star}
,

\bm{\mathrm{M}}^{\star}=\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i}\in\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{L}_{i},

(1)

whose response best matches a prescribed target first Piola–Kirchhoff stress response. To define this objective, let
\bm{\mathrm{P}}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
denote the target stress response over a prescribed set of deformation gradients
\mathcal{F}=\{\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{f}}
with
\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
, where
n_{f}
is the total number of deformation states. The loss
\mathcal{L}_{i}
is then defined as

\mathcal{L}_{i}=\frac{1}{n_{f}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{f}}\left\|\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{tar}}\Big(\bm{\mathrm{P}}^{\star}\big(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j}\big)\Big)-\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{tar}}\Big(\bm{\mathrm{P}}\big(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j};\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i}\big)\Big)\right\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\forall\,i\in\mathcal{I},

(2)

which averages the squared stress mismatch across the prescribed deformation set and provides a single scalar score for each discrete candidate. Here,
\bm{\mathrm{P}}(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j};\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is the effective homogenized first Piola–Kirchhoff stress response, and
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{tar}}:\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{tar}}}
extracts, scales, and vectorizes the active stress components chosen for inverse design, with
n_{\mathrm{tar}}
the number of target components. The oracle
\mathcal{O}
provides the high-fidelity homogenized response, typically through experiments or physics-based simulations:

\bm{\mathrm{P}}\left(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j};\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j};\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i}\right),\qquad\mathcal{O}:\mathcal{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}.

(3)

An ad-hoc procedure for the identification of
\bm{\mathrm{M}}^{\star}
from
equation

1
is to evaluate and rank the loss
\mathcal{L}_{i}
for each design
\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i}
. Over the full design set
\mathcal{D}
, this entails a prohibitively large number of oracle evaluations and is generally infeasible.

Effective macroscopic constitutive model

We therefore assume that the effective homogenized stress
\bm{\mathrm{P}}
can be approximated by an effective constitutive response
\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
. We postulate a stochastic observation model,

\bm{\mathrm{P}}\left(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j};\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i}\right)=\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}\left(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j};\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}\right)+\bm{\mathrm{\varepsilon}},

(4)

with
\bm{\mathrm{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
a noise term capturing discretization errors, solver tolerances, and model errors, thereby accounting for the fact that any effective model is only an approximation to the oracle. Throughout this work, noise is modeled as a random variable and further specified in
section

3
. Moreover,
\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}}
denotes a vector of random variables, namely the effective material parameters for the
i
-th design
\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i}
.

Following first principles, we postulate the existence of a scalar-valued hyperelastic strain energy density
\widetilde{W}(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j};\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i})
, whose derivative yields the stress response. Following the notation of
[
21
]
, the effective strain energy density can be written as a linear combination:

\widetilde{W}\left(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j};\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}\right)=\bm{\mathrm{Q}}\left(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j}\right)\cdot\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}-p\,\left(J-1\right).

(5)

Here,
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}(\bm{\mathrm{F}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}}
collects the chosen energy basis functions (e.g., functions of invariants or principal stretches),
J=\det\bm{\mathrm{F}}
denotes the Jacobian, and
p
is the hydrostatic pressure acting as a Lagrange multiplier enforcing incompressibility. The effective stress tensor is then obtained by differentiation:

\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}\left(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j};\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}\right)=\left.\frac{\partial\widetilde{W}\left(\bm{\mathrm{F}};\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}\right)}{\partial\bm{\mathrm{F}}}\right|_{\bm{\mathrm{F}}=\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j}}=\left.\frac{\partial\bm{\mathrm{Q}}\left(\bm{\mathrm{F}}\right)}{\partial\bm{\mathrm{F}}}\right|_{\bm{\mathrm{F}}=\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}-p\,\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j}^{-\top}.

(6)

Identification of the constitutive model structure and parameters
\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}
satisfying
equation

4
constitutes a calibration or model discovery task, which again relies on high-fidelity oracle evaluations. To reduce the computational cost, we are ultimately interested in a surrogate for the map
\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i}\mapsto\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}
. To enforce the positivity of material parameters, we consider the componentwise transformation

\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}=\log\Big(1+\exp\big(\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}_{i}\big)\Big).

(7)

Here, we introduce
\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}}
as a latent random variable, for which we train a GP surrogate using variational inference and active learning, minimizing the required oracle evaluations. Details can be found in
section

3
.

Shortlisting

Building on the trained probabilistic surrogate, we approximate the expensive oracle mismatch
equation

2
by a surrogate mismatch,

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{i}=\frac{1}{n_{f}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{f}}\left\|\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{tar}}\Big(\bm{\mathrm{P}}^{\star}\big(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j}\big)\Big)-\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{tar}}\Big(\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}\big(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j};\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}_{i})\big)\Big)\right\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\forall\,i\in\mathcal{I},

(8)

which allows us to efficiently screen the full design set. Since
\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}_{i}
is a random variable, the pushforward measure
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{i}
is random as well. Therefore, we first screen the full design set using a point estimate
\widehat{\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}}_{i}
obtained from passing the surrogate’s predictive mean
\mathbb{E}[\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}_{i}]
through the componentwise positivity map
equation

7
:

\widehat{\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}}_{i}=\log\Big(1+\exp\big(\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}_{i}\right]\big)\Big).

(9)

Note that, due to the nonlinearity of the positivity map
equation

7
, in general
\mathbb{E}[\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}]\neq\log(1+\exp(\mathbb{E}[\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}_{i}]))
, and the expectation
\mathbb{E}[\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}]
and its pushforward measures, such as the surrogate mismatch
equation

8
, are typically estimated via Monte Carlo sampling. Nevertheless, the point estimate
equation

9
provides a computationally inexpensive proxy for screening the full design set. Using this point estimate, we then evaluate the loss function for all candidates in the design set and retain a shortlist of size
E_{\max}
,

\mathcal{B}^{\star}=\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{I}\\
|\mathcal{B}|=E_{\max}\end{subarray}}\left.\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{i}\right|_{\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}=\widehat{\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}}_{i}},\qquad\forall\,i\in\mathcal{I},

(10)

with
\mathcal{B}^{\star}\subset\mathcal{I}
the set of indices for the most promising candidates according to the surrogate mean.

Uncertainty-aware scoring

The decision on whether a design is shortlisted depends solely on the surrogate’s predictive mean through the point estimate
\widehat{\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}}_{i}
defined in
equation

9
.
We therefore compute, for all shortlisted designs
\mathcal{B}^{\star}
, an uncertainty-aware score
\phi_{i}
that takes into account Monte Carlo estimates of both the mean and variance of the surrogate mismatch
equation

8
:

\phi_{i}=\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{i}\right]+\lambda\,\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{i}\right]},\qquad\forall\,i\in\mathcal{B^{\star}}.

(11)

The variance, weighted by
\lambda\geq 0
, penalizes candidates for which the surrogate prediction is accompanied by high epistemic uncertainty (cf.
section

3
). The risk parameter
\lambda
controls the influence of predictive uncertainty in the selection rule: when
\lambda=0
, the rule reduces to the pure exploitation of the surrogate mean, and when
\lambda\to\infty
, the selection becomes dominated by variance minimization, favoring the most confident predictions. To obtain a scale-consistent and problem-adaptive choice, we set
\lambda
based on the statistics of the shortlist
\mathcal{B}^{\star}
:

\lambda=\frac{\overline{\mu}}{\overline{\sigma}},\qquad\overline{\mu}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}^{\star}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}^{\star}}\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{i}\right],\qquad\overline{\sigma}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}^{\star}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}^{\star}}\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{i}\right]}.

(12)

This normalization balances the typical magnitude of the mean and variance terms within the shortlist, ensuring that neither component dominates the score purely due to scaling.

Oracle evaluations

Recall that the focus of the presented methodology is to minimize the number of oracle evaluations in inverse design. This premise motivates the postulation of an effective macroscopic constitutive model
equation

4
and the surrogate modeling strategy outlined in
section

3

equation

8
is an approximation that must only be used to screen the design set
\mathcal{D}
for potential candidates retained in the shortlist
\mathcal{B}^{\star}
. The final selection of a design, i.e., the approximate solution of
equation

1
, must be based on the oracle mismatch
equation

2
. Thus, we successively evaluate the oracle

3
for the shortlisted designs
\mathcal{B}^{\star}
, starting with the design associated with the minimum score

11
,

i_{e}^{\star}=\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{i\in\mathcal{B}^{\star}\setminus\mathcal{Q}_{e-1}}\phi_{i},\qquad\text{with }\mathcal{Q}_{e}=\mathcal{Q}_{e-1}\cup\{i_{e}^{\star}\}.

(13)

Here,
e\in\{1,\dots,E_{\max}\}
denotes the iteration index, and
\mathcal{Q}_{e}

Termination threshold

As evaluating the oracle across the entire shortlist would be unnecessarily costly, we use a stopping rule that allows early termination once adequate agreement with the target response is achieved. To quantify this agreement, we define a normalized mean absolute error (nMAE) for each target stress component across all sampled deformation states:

\mathrm{nMAE}_{p}\left(\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i_{e}^{\star}}\right)=\frac{\frac{1}{n_{f}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{f}}\left|\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{tar}}\Big(\bm{\mathrm{P}}^{\star}\big(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j}\big)\Big)_{p}-\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{tar}}\Big(\bm{\mathrm{P}}\big(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j};\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i_{e}^{\star}}\big)\Big)_{p}\right|}{\frac{1}{n_{f}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{f}}\left|\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{tar}}\Big(\bm{\mathrm{P}}^{\star}\big(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j}\big)\Big)_{p}\right|},\qquad p=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{tar}}.

(14)

The normalization by the target magnitude yields comparable errors across selected target components and different loading paths. To enable thresholding via a single scalar quantity, we aggregate the per-component nMAEs using the weighted average,

\overline{\mathrm{nMAE}}_{e}=\frac{\sum\limits_{p=1}^{n_{\mathrm{tar}}}\omega_{p}\,\mathrm{nMAE}_{p}\left(\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i_{e}^{\star}}\right)}{\sum\limits_{p=1}^{n_{\mathrm{tar}}}\omega_{p}},

(15)

where the weights
\omega_{p}
can emphasize components that are more critical for the design objective. The sequential oracle evaluations are terminated once
\overline{\mathrm{nMAE}}
falls below a prescribed threshold
\eta
, i.e.,
\overline{\mathrm{nMAE}}_{e}\leq\eta
. If the threshold is not met, the oracle evaluations continue until the maximum budget
E_{\max}
is exhausted.
We denote by
E_{\eta}
the oracle evaluation count required to meet the threshold
\eta
:

E_{\eta}=\min\left\{e:\ \overline{\mathrm{nMAE}}_{e}\leq\eta\right\},\qquad E_{\eta}\leq E_{\max}.

(16)

Final selection

The final inverse design is defined as the best microstructure identified under the prescribed threshold
\eta
and oracle evaluation budget
E_{\max}
. Specifically,

\bm{\mathrm{M}}^{\star}=\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i^{\star}},\qquad\text{with }i^{\star}=\begin{cases}i_{E_{\eta}}^{\star},&\text{if }E_{\eta}\leq E_{\max},\\[10.00002pt]
\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{i\in\mathcal{Q}_{E_{\max}}}\overline{\mathrm{nMAE}}_{i},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

(17)

If the threshold
\eta
is reached within the allotted evaluations, the corresponding design is accepted as a feasible inverse solution. Otherwise, once the oracle evaluation budget
E_{\max}
is exhausted, the procedure returns the budget-limited optimum, i.e., the candidate within the explored set that yields the smallest aggregated error
\overline{\mathrm{nMAE}}

. Thus,
equation

4
can be cast as

\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{(i)}_{j}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{obs}}\Big(\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}\big(\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}_{i});\,\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j}\big)\Big)+\varepsilon^{(i)}_{j},

(18)

where we assume homoscedastic Gaussian noise
\varepsilon^{(i)}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{obs}}}
with zero mean and variance
\sigma^{2}
,

\varepsilon^{(i)}_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{\mathrm{0}},\,\sigma^{2}\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n_{\mathrm{obs}}}\right).

(19)

3.2
Feature engineering

Microstructures are typically represented by high
-
dimensional pixel/voxel fields (often thousands to millions of variables), making direct surrogate modeling in microstructural image space computationally intractable and statistically redundant. We therefore construct a low
-
dimensional space by computing two
-
point correlation functions that summarize spatial statistics and applying principal component analysis (PCA) to obtain a compact yet informative set of salient features, as detailed in our previous work
[
11
]
. For brevity, the details of such a feature engineering approach are not presented here, and interested readers are therefore referred to prior studies
[
32
,
52
,
51
,
33
,
9
,
11
]
. Accordingly, we represent the combination of two
-
point correlations and PCA by the mapping
\mathcal{A}
,

\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}=\mathcal{A}\left(\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i}\right),\qquad\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{z}},

(20)

where
\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{z}}
denotes the low-dimensional descriptors for the
i
-th design, and
n_{z}\ll n_{p}
. In practice, this reduces the representation from thousands–millions of variables to tens or fewer, mitigating the curse of dimensionality while preserving the variability needed for accurate surrogate modeling.

3.3
Multi-output Gaussian process prior

With low-dimensional descriptors in hand, we now place a probabilistic surrogate on the latent parameters
\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}_{i}
. The surrogate maps the reduced features
\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}
to the latent GP outputs
\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}_{i}
:

\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}_{i}=\upxi\left(\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\right),\qquad\upxi:\mathbb{R}^{n_{z}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}}.

(21)

Rather than modeling each of the
n_{\theta}
outputs with independent single
-
output GPs, we construct a multi
-
output GP that explicitly captures correlation among them. Although the effective stress response
equation

6
is linear in parameters, the associated energy basis evaluated over the chosen deformation states is generally not composed of mutually independent basis functions. Under the available loading conditions, certain basis functions may be only weakly excited or nearly collinear, such that different parameter combinations can produce nearly indistinguishable stress responses. This limits the identifiability of parameters
\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}
and induces posterior correlations among them. We therefore adopt a linear model of coregionalization (LMC)
[
31
,
1
]
, which represents the multi-output covariance as a linear combination of
n_{r}
shared latent processes while allowing parameter-specific variability. Each latent process
f_{r}
is modeled as an independent GP,

f_{r}\left(\bm{\mathrm{z}}\right)\sim\mathcal{GP}\,\Big(0,\,k_{r}\big(\bm{\mathrm{z}},\bm{\mathrm{z}}^{\prime}\big)\Big),\qquad r=1,\dots,n_{r},

(22)

where
k_{r}(\bm{\mathrm{z}},\bm{\mathrm{z}}^{\prime})
denotes the scalar-valued covariance kernel of the
r
-th latent process over the input space. The latent parameters are then expressed as linear combinations of these latent processes:

\upxi_{m}\left(\bm{\mathrm{z}}\right)=\sum_{r=1}^{n_{r}}a_{mr}\,f_{r}\left(\bm{\mathrm{z}}\right),\qquad m=1,\dots,n_{\theta}.

(23)

The coefficients
a_{mr}
determine how strongly each latent process contributes to the
m
-th parameter and thereby encode inter-output correlations. This construction induces a multi-output covariance kernel of LMC form
\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}(\bm{\mathrm{z}},\bm{\mathrm{z}}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}\times n_{\theta}}
, expressed by

\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}\left(\bm{\mathrm{z}},\bm{\mathrm{z}}^{\prime}\right)=\sum_{r=1}^{n_{r}}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{r}\,k_{r}\left(\bm{\mathrm{z}},\bm{\mathrm{z}}^{\prime}\right),

(24)

where the coregionalization matrices
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}\times n_{\theta}}

are defined as

\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{r}=\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{r}\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{r}^{\top}+\kappa_{r}\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n_{\theta}}.

(25)

Here,
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{r}=(a_{mr})_{m=1}^{n_{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}}
collects the mixing
coefficients associated with the
r
-th latent process, and
\kappa_{r}\geq 0
denotes the diagonal coregionalization variance, which allows for output-specific variability
[
54
]
. We use the automatic relevance determination squared exponential (ARD-SE) kernel
[
61
]
with
n_{z}
length scales, given by

k_{r}\left(\bm{\mathrm{z}},\bm{\mathrm{z}}^{\prime}\right)=\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{z}}\frac{\big(z_{k}-z_{k}^{\prime}\big)^{2}}{\ell_{r,k}^{2}}\Big).

(26)

The length scales
\ell_{r,k}
allow each latent process to weight the low-dimensional descriptors differently and effectively perform automatic relevance determination in the reduced feature space. In summary, we collect the hyperparameters of our multi-output GP in the vector
\bm{\mathrm{\uppsi}}_{\mathrm{GP}}
, which entails the length scales, mixing coefficients, and diagonal coregionalization variances:

\bm{\mathrm{\uppsi}}_{\mathrm{GP}}=\left(\{\{\ell_{r,k}\}_{r=1}^{n_{r}}\}_{k=1}^{n_{z}},\{\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{r}\}_{r=1}^{n_{r}},\{\kappa_{r}\}_{r=1}^{n_{r}}\right).

(27)

3.4
Variational Bayesian inference

We now formalize the dataset and latent variables for variational Bayesian inference. Let
\bm{\mathrm{Z}}=\{\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}^{\top}\}_{i=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times n_{z}}
denote the input matrix for the observed design set, with
N
the number of observed designs. The corresponding latent parameters are collected in
\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}=\{\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn_{\theta}}
, and the physical parameters in
\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}=\{\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn_{\theta}}
. Let
n_{y}=n_{f}n_{\mathrm{obs}}
denote the total number of stress observations per design and across all deformation states. For each design, we collect all observations into
\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{i}=\{\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{(i)}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{f}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
, while
\bm{\mathrm{y}}=\{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn_{y}}
stacks these vectors over all observed designs, yielding the training set
\mathcal{T}=\{(\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

Given the multi-output LMC kernel
\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}
from
equation

24
, the GP prior over the latent parameters
\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}
is obtained from

p\left(\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{Z}}\right)=\mathcal{N}\Big(\bm{\mathrm{0}},\,\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}\big(\bm{\mathrm{Z}},\bm{\mathrm{Z}}\big)\Big).

(28)

The latent parameters
\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}
and the measured data
\bm{\mathrm{y}}
are connected via the observation model
equation

18
, which is used to define the likelihood as

p\left(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p\left(\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{i}\mid\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}_{i}\right),

(29)

assuming conditional independence of the observations given the latent parameters. Combining the prior
equation

28
and the likelihood
equation

29
yields the posterior
p(\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}\mid\mathcal{T})
via Bayes’ rule,

p\left(\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}\mid\mathcal{T}\right)=\frac{p\left(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}\right)\,p\left(\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{Z}}\right)}{p\left(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{Z}}\right)},

(30)

where
p(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{Z}})
is the marginal likelihood (evidence), which normalizes the posterior. The exact posterior is intractable since the positivity map
equation

7
makes the likelihood non-Gaussian, even though the effective constitutive model
equation

6
is linear in parameters
\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}
.

To approximate the intractable posterior
p(\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}\mid\mathcal{T})
, we introduce a Gaussian variational distribution
q(\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}})
of the form

q\left(\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}\right)=\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{\mathrm{\upmu}},\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}\right),

(31)

with
\bm{\mathrm{\upmu}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn_{\theta}}
the variational mean and
\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(Nn_{\theta})\times(Nn_{\theta})}
the variational covariance.
These variational parameters are determined by maximizing the evidence lower bound (ELBO), which follows from Jensen’s inequality
[
30
,
8
]
:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}})}\left[\log p\left(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}\right)\right]-\mathrm{KL}\Big(q\big(\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}\big)\,\|\,p\big(\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{Z}}\big)\Big).

(32)

Here,
\mathrm{KL}(\cdot\,\|\,\cdot)
denotes the Kullback–Leibler divergence, which measures the discrepancy between the variational distribution and the GP prior over the latent parameters, thereby enforcing regularization consistent with the assumed covariance structure. The first term is the expected log-likelihood, which measures agreement with the observed data and is approximated by Monte Carlo integration:

\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}})}\left[\log p\left(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}\right)\right]\approx\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\log p\Big(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}^{(s)}\Big),\qquad\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}^{(s)}\sim q\left(\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}\right),

(33)

where
S
is the total number of samples. Maximizing the
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}
therefore leads to a regularized optimization problem that balances agreement with the observed data and consistency with the GP prior. The corresponding optimal parameter vector is obtained by

\bm{\mathrm{\uppsi}}^{\star}=\mathop{\arg\max}\limits_{\bm{\mathrm{\uppsi}}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}},

(34)

where
\bm{\mathrm{\uppsi}}
collects all learnable parameters, including the GP hyperparameters
\bm{\mathrm{\uppsi}}_{\mathrm{GP}}
defined in
equation

27
, the observation noise variance
\sigma^{2}
appearing in the error model
equation

19
, and the variational mean
\bm{\mathrm{\upmu}}
and covariance
\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}
introduced in
equation

31
:

\bm{\mathrm{\uppsi}}=\left(\bm{\mathrm{\uppsi}}_{\mathrm{GP}},\ \sigma^{2},\ \bm{\mathrm{\upmu}},\ \{\Sigma_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{Nn_{\theta}}\right).

(35)

This joint optimization yields a consistent probabilistic model whose posterior approximation enables predictive evaluation at new configurations and systematic uncertainty propagation in subsequent computations.

3.5
Posterior predictive inference

With the variational posterior in hand, we obtain predictive distributions for unseen designs by propagating uncertainty from the latent parameters to stress space. For
N_{*}
unseen designs, let
\bm{\mathrm{Z}}_{*}=\{\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}^{\top}\}_{i=1}^{N_{*}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{*}\times n_{z}}
denote the input matrix of low-dimensional descriptors and
\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}_{*}=\{\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{*}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{*}n_{\theta}}
collect the corresponding latent parameters. For prediction, we marginalize the latent parameters at the observed inputs to obtain the predictive variational distribution,

q\left(\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}_{*}\right)=\int p\left(\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}_{*}\mid\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}},\bm{\mathrm{Z}}_{*},\bm{\mathrm{Z}}\right)\,q\left(\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}.

(36)

Since both the conditional GP prior and the variational posterior are Gaussian, the integral admits a closed form and yields a Gaussian predictive variational distribution
q(\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}_{*})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathrm{\upmu}}_{*},\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{*})
. Applying standard Gaussian conditioning identities gives

\bm{\mathrm{\upmu}}_{*}=\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}_{*}^{\top}\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{\upmu}},\qquad\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{*}=\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}_{**}+\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}_{*}^{\top}\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}^{-1}\left(\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}-\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}\right)\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}_{*},

(37)

which are the closed-form predictive mean
\bm{\mathrm{\upmu}}_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{*}n_{\theta}}
and covariance
\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N_{*}n_{\theta})\times(N_{*}n_{\theta})}
for the variational GP defined over the observed inputs. Here,
\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(Nn_{\theta})\times(Nn_{\theta})}
,
\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{(Nn_{\theta})\times(N_{*}n_{\theta})}
, and
\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}_{**}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N_{*}n_{\theta})\times(N_{*}n_{\theta})}
denote the covariance matrices evaluated at the observed and unseen inputs:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}(\bm{\mathrm{Z}},\bm{\mathrm{Z}}),

(38)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}_{*}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}(\bm{\mathrm{Z}},\bm{\mathrm{Z}}_{*}),

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}_{**}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}(\bm{\mathrm{Z}}_{*},\bm{\mathrm{Z}}_{*}).

The uncertainty in the latent parameters
\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}_{*}
is then propagated to the effective material parameters
\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{*}=\{\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{*}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{*}n_{\theta}}
via the positivity map
equation

7
. The forecasted stresses
\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{*}n_{y}}
follow accordingly from the macroscopic effective model
equation

6
:

\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}_{*}=\left\{\left\{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{obs}}\Big(\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}\big(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j};\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{i}\big)\Big)\right\}_{j=1}^{n_{f}}\right\}_{i=1}^{N_{*}}.

(39)

Given
S
Monte Carlo samples, the predictive moments are then estimated by

\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}_{*}\right]\approx\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}_{*}^{(s)},\qquad\mathrm{Var}\left[\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}_{*}\right]\approx\frac{1}{S-1}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Big(\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}_{*}^{(s)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}_{*}\right]\Big)\odot\Big(\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}_{*}^{(s)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}_{*}\right]\Big),

(40)

where
\odot
denotes componentwise multiplication. The samples
\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}_{*}^{(s)}
correspond to draws
\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}_{*}^{(s)}\sim q(\bm{\mathrm{\upxi}}_{*})
, mapped to
\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}_{*}^{(s)}
via the positivity transformation
equation

7
, from which the stress predictions are computed according to
equation

39
. This formulation ensures consistent propagation of latent uncertainty onto the predicted stress response and provides the probabilistic foundation for uncertainty-driven model exploration (
section

3.6
) and uncertainty-aware design decisions (
section

2
).

3.6
Uncertainty-driven active learning

With the predictive stress distributions available from the previous section, we now leverage uncertainty to guide data acquisition through an iterative active learning procedure
[
39
,
10
,
54
,
11
]
. The goal is to train the surrogate with as few oracle evaluations as possible by selecting, at each iteration, the designs expected to provide the greatest information gain. To formalize the iterative procedure, at iteration
t\in\{0,\dots,T_{\max}\}
, we partition the design index set
\mathcal{I}
into three subsets: the labeled set
\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{lab}}_{t}
, the unlabeled set
\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{unlab}}_{t}
, and the test set
\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{test}}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{lab}}_{t}\cup\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{unlab}}_{t}\cup\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{test}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I},

(41)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{lab}}_{t}\cap\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{unlab}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\emptyset,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{lab}}_{t}\cap\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{test}}

\displaystyle=\emptyset,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{unlab}}_{t}\cap\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{test}}

\displaystyle=\emptyset,\qquad\forall\,t.

The labeled set provides training data, the unlabeled pool provides candidates for acquisition, and the test set is held out to monitor predictive performance. We denote the current labeled training set, unlabeled pool, and fixed hold-out set by

\mathcal{T}_{t}=\left\{\left(\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{i}\right)\right\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{lab}}_{t}},\qquad\mathcal{P}_{t}=\left\{\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{unlab}}_{t}},\qquad\mathcal{H}=\left\{\left(\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{i}\right)\right\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{test}}}.

(42)

At iteration
t=0
, the initial index sets
\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{lab}}_{0}
,
\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{unlab}}_{0}
, and
\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{test}}
(and thus
\mathcal{T}_{0}
and
\mathcal{P}_{0}
) are formed by a chosen sampling strategy over
\mathcal{D}
, such as random sampling, Latin hypercube sampling, clustering-based selection, or other space-filling designs, to ensure a representative starting coverage of the feasible set. The initial labeled set is then populated by oracle evaluations for each
i\in\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{lab}}_{0}
, which yields the observed stresses
\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
needed to construct
\mathcal{T}_{0}
according to

\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{i}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{obs}}\Big(\left\{\mathcal{O}\big(\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i};\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j}\big)\right\}_{j=1}^{n_{f}}\Big).

(43)

The surrogate is then trained on
\mathcal{T}_{0}
by solving the ELBO optimization problem
equation

34
, providing the initial predictive model used for acquisition.

At iteration
t>0
, we evaluate an acquisition score for each candidate in the unlabeled pool
\mathcal{P}_{t-1}
by quantifying the predictive uncertainty of its stress response,

\alpha_{t}\left(\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\right)=\log\operatorname{det}\Big(\operatorname{diag}\big(\mathrm{Var}(\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}_{*}\mid\mathcal{T}_{t-1})\big)_{(i)}\Big),\qquad\forall\,i\in\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{unlab}}_{t-1}.

(44)

This acquisition criterion assigns higher scores to candidates exhibiting large predictive variance across stress components. This criterion has proven effective in practice
[
39
,
10
,
54
,
11
]
, particularly when the model noise is approximately uniform across the input space
56
. Since the predictive stress distribution is obtained as the pushforward of the variational approximation (cf.
equation

39
), the variance reflects epistemic uncertainty induced by limited training data. Maximizing the acquisition function therefore promotes sampling in regions where the current model exhibits the largest uncertainty in the predicted stress response, thereby targeting maximal expected information gain. At iteration
t
, the next configuration is selected by

i_{t}^{\star}=\mathop{\arg\max}\limits_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{unlab}}_{t-1}}\alpha_{t}\left(\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\right),

(45)

and then moved from the unlabeled set to the labeled set, yielding the updated index sets

\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{lab}}_{t}=\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{lab}}_{t-1}\cup\{i_{t}^{\star}\},\qquad\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{unlab}}_{t}=\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{unlab}}_{t-1}\setminus\{i_{t}^{\star}\}.

(46)

The oracle is evaluated for the selected design
\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i_{t}^{\star}}
to obtain new stress observations using
equation

43
. The resulting observations are then appended to the training set while the queried point is removed from the pool,

\mathcal{T}_{t}=\mathcal{T}_{t-1}\cup\left\{\left(\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i_{t}^{\star}},\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{i_{t}^{\star}}\right)\right\},\qquad\mathcal{P}_{t}=\mathcal{P}_{t-1}\setminus\{\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i_{t}^{\star}}\}.

(47)

The surrogate model is then re-trained using the augmented training set
\mathcal{T}_{t}
by re-optimizing the ELBO objective
equation

34
, while predictive accuracy is monitored on a fixed hold-out set via

\mathrm{MAE}_{t}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{test}}}\left\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{i}-\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}_{*}\mid\mathcal{T}_{t}\right]\right)_{(i)}\right\|_{1},\qquad\forall\,i\in\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{test}}.

(48)

The MAE evaluated on the hold-out set provides a consistent out-of-sample measure of predictive accuracy as the surrogate model is iteratively re-trained. The stopping criterion is defined in terms of the relative change in MAE computed over a sliding window
[
11
]
,

\Delta_{t}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{t=T-L+1}^{T}\frac{\big|\mathrm{MAE}_{t}-\mathrm{MAE}_{t-1}\big|}{\mathrm{MAE}_{t-1}},

(49)

where
L
is the window length and
T
denotes the current iteration index used to compute the sliding average. The active learning procedure is terminated when improvements fall below a prescribed threshold,
\Delta_{t}\leq\epsilon
, or when the oracle evaluation budget is exhausted (i.e.,
t\geq T_{\max}
). This rule prevents additional oracle evaluations once the surrogate exhibits negligible improvement in predictive accuracy.

4
Results and discussion

To assess the performance of the proposed framework, we apply it to a large and diverse design space of stochastic hyperelastic metamaterials. We first introduce the discrete design space (
section

4.1
) and the high-fidelity oracle used to compute the effective homogenized response (
section

4.2
). Subsequently, we define a physics-consistent effective constitutive model that approximates the high-fidelity solutions (
section

4.3
) and establish an appropriate sampling strategy in deformation gradient space (
section

4.4
). Based on these ingredients, we train a data-efficient surrogate model via active learning to infer the effective constitutive parameters from limited oracle evaluations in
section

4.5
. Finally, the trained surrogate is employed to perform Bayesian-guided discrete inverse design over the considered set of stochastic metamaterial unit cells (
section

4.6
).

4.1
Design space: stochastic metamaterial unit cells

We employ the dataset of periodic two-dimensional stochastic metamaterials introduced by
5
,
6
, comprising
53,019
unit cells represented on a
96\times 96
pixel grid. Each unit cell is denoted by
\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}}
, where
\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i}
is the vectorized representation of the pixel grid and
n_{p}=96\times 96=9,216
. In our previous work
[
11
,
12
]
, this dataset served as the foundation for developing reduced-order structure–property linkages. Each microstructure is generated by sampling a Gaussian random field followed by threshold-based binarization to obtain a solid–void representation. Admissible samples are mirrored to enforce periodicity, resulting in a geometrically diverse collection of stochastic unit cells (see
[
5
]
for more details).

The original dataset
[
5
,
6
]
intentionally includes geometries exhibiting instability modes in order to study buckling behavior. In the present work, we restrict the design space to a mechanically feasible subset by excluding configurations identified as buckling-prone, resulting in the design set
\mathcal{D}=\{\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{d}}
with
n_{d}=50,000
admissible unit cells. The geometric variability of this dataset was quantitatively assessed in our previous study
[
11
]
, where diversity metrics were reported to characterize coverage of the design space. Such broad geometric coverage is particularly important here, as the proposed inverse design strategy operates by selection over a finite candidate library rather than continuous optimization. Ensuring sufficient diversity within the discrete pool increases the likelihood that the prescribed target response can be approximated within the available design space. 4.2
Oracle: FFT-based computational homogenization

To obtain homogenized effective responses of the stochastic unit cells, a high-fidelity oracle is required. In principle, such an oracle may consist of experimental measurements or numerical simulations. In the present study, we employ computational homogenization to compute the effective stress response of each unit cell under prescribed deformation states. The solid phase is modeled as an incompressible Neo-Hookean material under a plane-stress formulation appropriate for thin metamaterial films. The shear modulus of the solid phase is set to 100 MPa, whereas the void phase is modeled as a compliant material with stiffness 1 MPa (contrast ratio of 0.01), consistent with the contrast employed for spinodoid architected structures in
[
55
,
53
]
. This moderate contrast mitigates convergence issues associated with extreme material contrasts in spectral solvers
40
,
13
.

Homogenization is performed using the Galerkin FFT method
[
58
,
59
]
, combined with a rotated finite difference discretization
[
62
]
and solved using the MINRES iterative solver, a combination suggested by
40
to adapt spectral solvers for metamaterial unit cells. This framework has been employed in our previous work to generate high-fidelity datasets, including homogenized elastic constants of auxetic unit cells
[
14
]
and the present stochastic dataset
[
11
]
in the small-strain regime. While those earlier studies focused on small-strain elasticity, the present work employs a finite-strain formulation
[
17
,
45
]
adapted to plane-stress assumption to capture the nonlinear response of thin hyperelastic metamaterial films. In this setting, the oracle evaluations defined in
equation

3
correspond to the effective first Piola stress
\bm{\mathrm{P}}(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j};\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i})
obtained from representative volume element (RVE) simulations of the unit cell geometry
\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i}
under prescribed macroscopic deformation gradients
\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{j}
. In what follows, the dependence on the particular RVE and loading state is omitted for notational simplicity.

To briefly summarize the homogenization procedure, let
\bm{\mathrm{X}}\in V_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
denote the material coordinate in the reference configuration of an RVE (i.e., a metamaterial unit cell in our case), with
V_{0}
the reference domain. The microscopic deformation gradient
\bm{\mathrm{F}}^{\mu}
is decomposed into a prescribed macroscopic part
\bm{\mathrm{F}}
and a fluctuation field

\bm{\mathrm{F}}^{\prime}(\bm{\mathrm{X}})
,

\bm{\mathrm{F}}^{\mu}\left(\bm{\mathrm{X}}\right)=\bm{\mathrm{F}}+\bm{\mathrm{F}}^{\prime}\left(\bm{\mathrm{X}}\right),

(50)

where the fluctuation field
\bm{\mathrm{F}}^{\prime}(\bm{\mathrm{X}})
has vanishing volume average over the RVE. Accordingly, the macroscopic deformation gradient equals
the volume average of the microscopic field,

\bm{\mathrm{F}}=\frac{1}{|V_{0}|}\int_{V_{0}}\bm{\mathrm{F}}^{\mu}\left(\bm{\mathrm{X}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}V.

(51)

Analogously, the effective first Piola stress
\bm{\mathrm{P}}
is obtained by volume
averaging the microscopic stress field
\bm{\mathrm{P}}^{\mu}
:

\bm{\mathrm{P}}=\frac{1}{|V_{0}|}\int_{V_{0}}\bm{\mathrm{P}}^{\mu}\left(\bm{\mathrm{X}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}V.

(52)

Importantly, the proposed inverse design framework is independent of the specific homogenization scheme. The Galerkin FFT solver serves solely as a provider of high-fidelity response data. In principle, the oracle could be replaced by alternative numerical methods or experimental measurements without altering the methodology. Detailed aspects of the Galerkin FFT formulation are therefore beyond the scope of the present work, and interested readers are referred to
[
58
,
59
,
62
,
14
,
40
,
17
,
45
]
for further discussion.

4.3
Effective macroscopic constitutive model

The unit cell generation strategy, including random construction combined with periodic mirroring (cf.
section

4.1
), induces an orthotropic effective response with orthogonal in-plane material axes. In particular, each generated cell is symmetric by construction with respect to reflections about these two axes, which implies that the homogenized behavior is direction-dependent and invariant under sign reversal (reflection) along each preferred axis. Following
[
25
,
26
,
44
]
, we denote the corresponding preferred directions by two orthogonal unit vectors
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, i.e.,

\|\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{0}\|_{2}=1,\qquad\|\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{0}\|_{2}=1,\qquad\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{0}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{0}=0,

(53)

which define an orthotropic symmetry class. In the present setting, these preferred directions coincide with the axes of the reference coordinate system due to the symmetric construction of the unit cells, such that
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{0}=\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{1}
and
\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{0}=\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{2}
. To represent this symmetry in an objective manner, we introduce the invariant basis

I_{1}=\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{\mathrm{F}}^{\top}\bm{\mathrm{F}}\right),\qquad I_{4}=\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{0}\cdot\left(\bm{\mathrm{F}}^{\top}\bm{\mathrm{F}}\,\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{0}\right)=\|\bm{\mathrm{F}}\,\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{0}\|_{2}^{2},\qquad I_{6}=\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{0}\cdot\left(\bm{\mathrm{F}}^{\top}\bm{\mathrm{F}}\,\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{0}\right)=\|\bm{\mathrm{F}}\,\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}.

(54)

Here,
I_{1}
captures the isotropic distortion, while
I_{4}
and
I_{6}
represent the squared stretches along the two preferred material directions.

Based on this orthotropic invariant basis, we define the effective constitutive model
equation

6
. For readability, we omit the design index
i
and deformation state index
j
and consider a representative structure under a generic deformation state. In such an anisotropic case, the effective strain energy depends not only on the deformation gradient but also on the preferred directions
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{0}
and
\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{0}
, i.e.,

\widetilde{W}\left(\bm{\mathrm{F}},\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{0},\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{0};\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}\right)=\widetilde{W}\left(I_{1},I_{4},I_{6};\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}\right).

(55)

Since the preferred directions
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{0}
and
\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{0}
are fixed by the symmetry of the unit cell, their explicit appearance will be omitted in the following notation for brevity. Specifying
equation

5
, we introduce a compact energy ansatz as a linear combination of invariant basis terms plus an incompressibility constraint,

\widetilde{W}\left(\bm{\mathrm{F}};\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}\right)=\bm{\mathrm{Q}}\left(I_{1},I_{4},I_{6}\right)\cdot\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}-p\,\left(J-1\right),\qquad\bm{\mathrm{Q}}\left(I_{1},I_{4},I_{6}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\left(I_{1}-3\right)\\
\left(I_{4}-1\right)^{2}\\
\left(I_{6}-1\right)^{2}\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}=\begin{bmatrix}\theta_{I_{1}}\\
\theta_{I_{4}}\\
\theta_{I_{6}}\end{bmatrix},

(56)

which can be written in expanded invariant form:

\widetilde{W}\left(\bm{\mathrm{F}};\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}\right)=\theta_{I_{1}}\,\left(I_{1}-3\right)+\theta_{I_{4}}\,\left(I_{4}-1\right)^{2}+\theta_{I_{6}}\,\left(I_{6}-1\right)^{2}-p\,\left(J-1\right).

(57)

The effective first Piola–Kirchhoff stress is then obtained from
equation

6
as

\widetilde{\bm{\mathrm{P}}}\left(\bm{\mathrm{F}};\bm{\mathrm{\uptheta}}\right)=2\,\theta_{I_{1}}\,\bm{\mathrm{F}}+4\,\theta_{I_{4}}\,\left(I_{4}-1\right)\,\bm{\mathrm{F}}\,\left(\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{0}\otimes\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{0}\right)+4\,\theta_{I_{6}}\,\left(I_{6}-1\right)\,\bm{\mathrm{F}}\,\left(\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{0}\otimes\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{0}\right)-p\,\bm{\mathrm{F}}^{-\top}.

(58)

Accordingly, to enforce incompressible plane-stress conditions, the hydrostatic pressure
p
is determined from the out-of-plane stress condition
P_{33}=0
, yielding

p=2\,\theta_{I_{1}}\,\frac{F_{33}}{\operatorname{cof}\left(\bm{\mathrm{F}}\right)_{33}}.

(59)

Remark

In this representation, the linear contribution in
I_{1}
, with parameter
\theta_{I_{1}}
, represents the isotropic part of the effective response, consistent with the Neo-Hookean constitutive law used for the solid phase in the high-fidelity oracle (cf.
section

4.2
). The additional quadratic terms in
I_{4}
and
I_{6}
, with parameters
\theta_{I_{4}}
and
\theta_{I_{6}}
, respectively, introduce directional stiffening along the two preferred material directions while preserving zero stress in the reference configuration. In this sense, the model can be interpreted as a minimal orthotropic extension of the underlying Neo-Hookean solid phase behavior to the homogenized unit cell response. Furthermore, one may additionally include a mixed interaction invariant of the form
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{0}\cdot(\bm{\mathrm{F}}^{\top}\bm{\mathrm{F}}\,\bm{\mathrm{b}}_{0})
. In the present work, however, we restrict the effective basis to
\{I_{1},I_{4},I_{6}\}
, since the axis-aligned deformation states introduced below render this interaction term identically zero. An analogous behavior is reported in orthotropic invariant formulations of textile structures under aligned biaxial loading
[
44
]
, where the interaction invariant vanishes for axis-aligned stretch states. The omission here is therefore a consequence of the chosen loading family rather than a restriction of the underlying orthotropic framework.

4.4
Deformation sampling

We consider two different deformation sampling strategies: one used to generate the
training data
for the surrogate model and another used to construct the
target data
to evaluate the performance of our inverse design strategy. This distinction separates the surrogate training phase from the assessment of the proposed inverse design methodology. The surrogate is trained on a structured set of deformation states aligned with the preferred material directions of the unit cell, enabling efficient identification of the constitutive response. In contrast, target responses are generated from deformation states that differ from those encountered during training, allowing us to systematically assess the ability of the inferred constitutive model and the inverse design framework to generalize to previously unseen loading configurations.

Training data

To identify the effective parameters reliably, the sampled deformation states must sufficiently excite the invariant contributions associated with
I_{1}
,
I_{4}
, and
I_{6}
. We therefore define a biaxial in-plane loading family analogous to that introduced in
[
44
]
, consisting of the five paths listed in
table

1
: Tension-x, Off-x, Equibiaxial, Off-y, and Tension-y. These paths vary the principal stretches in a structured manner, ranging from pure tension in one preferred direction to unbalanced and balanced biaxial tension, ensuring systematic excitation of the invariant contributions. Since the metamaterial thin films considered here exhibit out-of-plane instabilities under compressive or shear-dominated in-plane loading, the deformation sampling is restricted to tensile states.

(61)

Accordingly,
I_{4}
and

are directly controlled by the principal stretches, while
I_{1}

with

deformation increments per path. This structured sampling generates distinct trajectories in invariant space, which are visualized in

. The separation of these trajectories promotes independent variation of
(I_{1}-3)
,
(I_{4}-1)^{2}
, and
(I_{6}-1)^{2}
, thereby reducing parameter collinearity and improving identifiability of the effective orthotropic coefficients.

Under the considered biaxial loading configuration aligned with the preferred material directions (
section

4.3
), the imposed deformation gradient is diagonal in the material frame (i.e.,
equation

60
) and contains no shear components. Owing to the alignment of the loading axes with the orthotropic symmetry directions of the microstructure, the overall response remains coaxial with the imposed deformation. Consequently, the homogenized first Piola–Kirchhoff stress tensor is diagonal in the material frame, such that the only nonzero in-plane components are
P_{11}
and
P_{22}
, while all shear components vanish by symmetry. The out-of-plane stress component is also eliminated by enforcing the plane-stress condition
P_{33}=0
. In experimental settings, the corresponding nominal stresses
P_{11}
and
P_{22}
are computed from the measured reaction forces along the loading axes together with the associated reference cross-sectional areas. In the present work, the same quantities are obtained from physics-based computational homogenization (cf.
section

4.2
). These nominal stresses
P_{11}
and
P_{22}
are then used as the stress observations to train the surrogate.

Target data

The surrogate is trained using loading paths aligned with the preferred material directions of the unit cell. In practice, however, loading conditions are not necessarily aligned with these directions. To assess the performance of the proposed inverse design framework under such circumstances, we deliberately consider loading cases that were not encountered during training.

In experimental settings, rotating a specimen with respect to the applied loading direction is conceptually straightforward. In numerical simulations, however, such a strategy is not feasible, since rotating the discretized microstructure would interfere with the imposed periodic boundary conditions and alter the underlying grid representation. Instead, we mimic off-axis loading by rotating the deformation gradient while keeping the microstructure fixed. This operation does not correspond to a superposed rigid body rotation, but rather to a re-expression of the prescribed stretch in a rotated loading coordinate system. This preserves the material frame associated with the unit cell and provides a clean and controlled test of generalization to an unseen loading orientation.

To formalize this idea, we introduce a rotation of the loading frame by an angle
\beta
about the out-of-plane axis
\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{3}
. Let
\bm{\mathrm{R}}
denote the corresponding rotation tensor. The rotated deformation gradient is then obtained by a similarity transformation of the original deformation gradient
\bm{\mathrm{F}}
, i.e.,

\bm{\mathrm{F}}^{(\beta)}=\bm{\mathrm{R}}^{\top}\,\bm{\mathrm{F}}\,\bm{\mathrm{R}},\qquad\text{with }\bm{\mathrm{R}}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\beta&-\sin\beta&0\\
\sin\beta&\cos\beta&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix},

(63)

where
\bm{\mathrm{F}}^{(\beta)}
represents the deformation gradient expressed in the rotated loading frame. We focus in particular on the representative case
\beta=45^{\circ}
, which introduces a balanced coupling between the two in-plane directions and therefore departs most clearly from the axis-aligned loading states used during training. For this choice, the rotated deformation gradient takes the explicit form

\bm{\mathrm{F}}^{(45)}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}}{2}&\dfrac{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}}{2}&0\\[4.0pt]
\dfrac{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}}{2}&\dfrac{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}}{2}&0\\[4.0pt]
0&0&\left(\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\right)^{-1}\end{bmatrix},

(64)

with the corresponding invariants computed as

I_{1}^{(45)}=\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}+\left(\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\right)^{-2},\qquad I_{4}^{(45)}=\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}}{2},\qquad I_{6}^{(45)}=\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}}{2}.

(65)

Notably,
I_{4}^{(45)}
and
I_{6}^{(45)}
coincide, reflecting the symmetric contribution of the two preferred material directions under a
45^{\circ}
rotation. This condition therefore probes the inferred anisotropic response encoded by the trained surrogate in a configuration where the directional invariants become indistinguishable and provides a robust setting to assess the generalizability of the inferred hyperelastic parameters.

In addition, the rotated loading introduces nonzero off-diagonal terms in
\bm{\mathrm{F}}^{(45)}
and therefore induces in-plane shear. Since the surrogate is trained exclusively on axis-aligned states, for which only
P_{11}
and
P_{22}
are observed, the prediction of the induced shear component under
\beta=45^{\circ}
constitutes a genuine generalization test beyond the training distribution. In general, the first Piola–Kirchhoff stress is not symmetric. In the present setting, however, the unit cells exhibit orthogonal symmetry with respect to the material axes, and the prescribed macroscopic deformation gradient is symmetric, corresponding to pure stretch without superposed rigid body rotation. For the loading cases considered here, we consistently observe that the homogenized nominal stress is symmetric, with
P_{12}=P_{21}
up to machine precision. We therefore restrict attention to the independent in-plane components
P_{11}
,
P_{22}
, and
P_{12}
as targets for inverse design.

4.5
Data-efficient surrogate training via active learning

Direct surrogate training in image space is computationally prohibitive for microstructures
\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{9216}
. We therefore employ the reduced descriptors
\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}=\mathcal{A}(\bm{\mathrm{M}}_{i})
introduced in
section

3.2
, where
\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
. In our previous study on the same dataset
[
11
]
, we demonstrated that combining the two-point auto-correlations of the solid phase with the auto-correlations of the solid–void interface, followed by PCA retaining
n_{z}=6
dominant modes, provides a compact yet highly informative representation. This reduces the input dimensionality from
n_{p}=9,216
to
n_{z}=6
while preserving the essential morphological variability. Recalling the relation
\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}_{i}(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}_{i}(\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}))
, we construct a probabilistic GP surrogate for the map
\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}\mapsto\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and use the positivity transformation
equation

7
to compute effective parameters
\bm{\mathrm{\theta}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. To capture the correlation among these outputs, we employ a multi-output GP with LMC structure. We choose
n_{r}=3
latent GPs, providing a minimal coregionalized representation consistent with the three effective parameters.

Prior to GP training, the reduced inputs are standardized to have zero mean and unit variance using the mean
\bm{\mathrm{\upmu}}_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{z}}
and standard deviation
\bm{\mathrm{\upsigma}}_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{z}}
computed over the full design set
\mathcal{D}
. The standardized input features are therefore given by

\widehat{\bm{\mathrm{z}}}_{i}=\left(\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}-\bm{\mathrm{\upmu}}_{z}\right)\oslash\bm{\mathrm{\upsigma}}_{z},\qquad\forall\,i\in\mathcal{I},

(66)

where
\oslash
denotes componentwise division. The stress observations are also standardized in the same manner, with separate statistics computed for each stress component at each sampled deformation state using the initial training set
\mathcal{T}_{0}
. Recalling the observation vector
\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{(i)}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{obs}}}
introduced in
equation

18
, we denote by
\bm{\mathrm{\upmu}}_{y,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{obs}}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{\upsigma}}_{y,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{obs}}}
the mean and standard deviation computed from the initial labeled set
\mathcal{T}_{0}
for each fixed deformation state
j\in\{1,\dots,n_{f}\}
. The normalized observations are then written as

\widehat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}^{(i)}_{j}=\Big(\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{(i)}_{j}-\bm{\mathrm{\upmu}}_{y,j}\Big)\oslash\bm{\mathrm{\upsigma}}_{y,j}.

(67)

The normalization factors are then held fixed during active learning, and each newly acquired observation is standardized before being added to the training set. This keeps the observed responses on comparable numerical scales across the full loading history and supports stable GP training.

The model is implemented in GPyTorch and optimized using Adam with cosine-annealing learning rate scheduling and warm restarts at each active learning retraining step. The ELBO objective
equation

32
is approximated by Monte Carlo sampling for the expected log-likelihood term using
S=64
samples in
equation

33
; unless stated otherwise, the same sample count is used for all other Monte Carlo estimates reported throughout this work. An initial labeled training set of size
|\mathcal{T}_{0}|=10
and a fixed hold-out set of size
|\mathcal{H}|=500
are generated via Latin hypercube sampling, with the latter reserved to monitor out-of-sample performance through the MAE defined in
equation

48
.

To train the surrogate with as little oracle-labeled data as possible, we adopt the active learning workflow described in
section

3.6
. Starting from the initial set
\mathcal{T}_{0}
, the surrogate is first trained by maximizing the ELBO via
equation

34
. At each subsequent iteration, the predictive uncertainty over the unlabeled pool is quantified using the acquisition criterion in
equation

44
. The most informative candidate is selected according to
equation

45
, labeled by the high-fidelity oracle via
equation

43
, and appended to the training set following
equation

47
. The surrogate is then retrained, and the procedure is repeated up to
T_{\max}=230
iterations. Convergence is monitored using the stopping rule in
equation

49
with
\epsilon=10^{-3}
and
L=5
.

The active learning curve is shown in
figure

equation

5
Conclusions

In this work, we presented a data-efficient framework for the discrete inverse design of hyperelastic microstructures, combining probabilistic surrogate modeling, uncertainty-driven active learning, and Bayesian-guided exploration of a discrete design space. The proposed approach enables inverse design within large pools of feasible stochastic structures while requiring only a minimal number of labeled samples and very few expensive high-fidelity simulations. In essence, a cheaply trained surrogate model identifies promising regions of the discrete design space, after which a small number of high-fidelity oracle evaluations are used to iteratively determine the structure that best matches a prescribed effective mechanical response.

More precisely, the framework targets structural design problems in which the geometry cannot be conveniently parameterized, and continuous optimization therefore becomes impractical. Instead of searching a continuous parameter space, the method operates on a discrete pool of feasible candidate structures while avoiding the need to evaluate all candidates using expensive simulations or experiments. A probabilistic surrogate is constructed to capture the structure–property relation using only a small number of labeled designs. To minimize the required training data, uncertainty-driven active learning is employed, in which the predictive uncertainty of the surrogate guides the selection of new structures to be evaluated and added to the training set. To ensure physical consistency and interpretability, the surrogate does not directly predict the effective stress response. Instead, it maps structural descriptors to the parameters of an effective constitutive model describing the macroscopic behavior of the hyperelastic microstructure. As structural representations are inherently high-dimensional, statistical feature engineering is used to extract a compact set of reduced descriptors that retain the essential structural information while keeping surrogate learning tractable. Once trained, the surrogate efficiently explores the candidate pool during the inverse design stage and identifies promising structures. Predictive uncertainty is incorporated into the ranking of shortlisted candidates in order to penalize unreliable predictions. The final decision is then made through a small number of high-fidelity oracle evaluations, which ultimately determine the design that best matches the prescribed target response. Importantly, since the surrogate serves only as a guide for navigating the design space, the overall framework remains robust even when the surrogate is imperfect, for instance, due to limited training data or an insufficiently expressive constitutive ansatz. In such cases, inaccuracies in the surrogate merely affect the efficiency of the search, since the final selection of the optimal design is always resolved by the high-fidelity oracle.

To investigate the performance of the proposed framework, we employed it for inverse design in a dataset of stochastic hyperelastic metamaterials comprising
50,000
admissible candidate structures. In this setting, uncertainty-driven active learning proved particularly effective, making it possible to train the surrogate using only 0.4% of the full dataset while still achieving strong predictive performance. During the inverse design stage, the framework consistently identified candidates that matched the prescribed targets within the predefined error threshold using only a small number of high-fidelity oracle evaluations. In most cases, the target was reached in fewer than 10 oracle calls, while the threshold hit rate already exceeded 92% within 20 evaluations and surpassed 96% within 50 evaluations across the considered target combinations. Moreover, as the oracle budget increased, the hit rate approached 100%, demonstrating that the framework provides a clear and controllable trade-off between oracle cost and design accuracy. Importantly, the method also remains robust when the prescribed threshold cannot be met within the available budget. In such cases, it returns a budget-limited optimum, which was shown to remain close to the target and to incur only moderate error.

The present framework also has several limitations that merit discussion. First, the inverse design problem is posed over a known finite pool of candidate structures. Consequently, the framework can only identify the best design within this predefined set, and its success therefore depends on whether the candidate pool already spans sufficiently rich and relevant regions of the admissible design space. Second, the effective constitutive description relies on a prescribed hyperelastic ansatz. While this choice provides interpretability and enables efficient surrogate learning, it nevertheless introduces a modeling assumption regarding the admissible functional form of the constitutive response and may therefore limit expressiveness when the true material behavior requires a richer representation.

These limitations naturally point toward several promising extensions of the present framework. Rather than relying on a fixed library of candidate structures, future work could incorporate generative modeling approaches to construct larger and more diverse pools of feasible designs. In such a hybrid strategy, generative models, such as variational autoencoders
[
35
,
50
]
, generative adversarial networks
[
28
,
49
]
, diffusion models
[
41
,
4
]
, or related geometry generation techniques, would primarily serve as candidate generators that expand the accessible design space, while the inverse design itself would remain a discrete surrogate-guided search validated by high-fidelity oracle evaluations. In parallel, the constitutive modeling component could evolve beyond a fixed hyperelastic ansatz toward model discovery frameworks, in which suitable constitutive forms are automatically identified from a richer library of admissible terms
[
21
,
38
,
57
,
48
,
2
]
. Such approaches could allow constitutive relations to be discovered either for individual structures or for families of structures grouped according to their symmetry class, thereby yielding more expressive and better adapted surrogate representations. More broadly, the overall framework is not restricted to hyperelastic materials and could be extended to inelastic settings
[
24
,
64
,
15
]
, where history-dependent constitutive behavior must also be learned and incorporated within the inverse design process. Beyond solid mechanics, the proposed methodology provides a general blueprint for data-efficient inverse design across a wide range of scientific and engineering domains, provided that the underlying problem can be formulated within a similar surrogate–oracle framework.
