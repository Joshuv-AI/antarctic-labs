Lack of association between second‐degree tears and pelvic floor symptoms one year postpartum
Authors: Sophie Hu, Nathalie Auger
Published: 2026

Sir,

We read with interest Edqvist et al.'s article on pelvic floor symptoms 1 year postpartum. 1 The authors analyzed women who participated in a randomized trial of the effectiveness of two midwives to prevent severe perineal trauma. 2 They prospectively followed the women over time to assess pelvic floor dysfunction and quality of life 1 year after perineal trauma. While pelvic floor symptoms were prevalent, perineal trauma was not associated with the expected outcomes. In particular, major second‐degree tears were not associated with pelvic floor dysfunction or quality of life after 1 year.

Most studies suggest that extensive perineal tears, especially third or fourth‐degree tears extending into the anal sphincter, increase the risk of postpartum pelvic floor dysfunction. 3 Less is known about second‐degree tears, a more common complication in which the tear extends into the perineal muscle, where the extent of damage can vary. 4 Some second‐degree tears may be expected to impact pelvic floor function, especially extensive tears through the vaginal muscle that are close but may not meet the criteria for a third‐degree tear. As Edqvist et al. did not find an association with second‐degree tears, we were concerned by the possibility that the sample was not representative.

The women were initially recruited to a trial of the effectiveness of midwives in reducing the risk of severe perineal trauma during labor. 2 The intervention consisted of having 2 midwives assist during the second stage of labour versus standard care with 1 midwife. Owing to the study design, women in the intervention group were prevented from having severe tears. To some extent, tears may also have been prevented in the group receiving standard care as midwives could not be masked to an intervention arm. Midwives providing standard care were aware of the trial and likely also made efforts to prevent severe tears. The design of the trial may have resulted in fewer and less severe tears in both groups overall. As perineal trauma was likely attenuated in all women, it is not surprising that second‐degree tears were not associated with postpartum pelvic floor symptoms when the women were assessed 1 year later.

There may be additional setbacks of using data from trials. Individuals who are motivated to participate in trials tend to differ from the general population and may not be representative. 5 In addition, a large number of nonrespondents could not be included in the analysis for reasons that are unclear. The exclusion of nonrespondents, especially nonrespondents with major second‐degree tears, is another potential source of bias.

Edqvist et al.'s study provides interesting data on a low‐risk population in which second‐degree tears had little impact on subsequent pelvic floor function. The limited impact of tears is to be expected as the study used data from an unmasked randomized trial meant to minimize severe tears. Future studies using a representative population‐based design would be beneficial to assess the association between second‐degree tears and long‐term pelvic floor dysfunction.
