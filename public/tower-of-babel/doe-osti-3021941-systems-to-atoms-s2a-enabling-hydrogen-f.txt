Abstract
The project addresses a critical gap in hydrogen infrastructure by integrating system-level energy
models with atomic-scale material simulations in a unified Systems-to-Atoms (S2A) framework.
The motivation stems from the need to develop efficient, cost-effective, and durable hydrogen
transport and utilization technologies to support decarbonization of hard-to-electrify sectors
such as heavy-duty transportation. Current system models lack awareness of material
performance mechanisms, while material-scale models do not account for system-level usage
and variability. To bridge this divide, the team developed a co-simulation capability linking
techno-economic analyses, reactor/process-flow modeling, and molecular-scale catalysis
simulations. Applied to hydrogen delivery in California, the framework enabled comparative
evaluations of compressed, cryogenic, and liquid organic hydrogen carrier (LOHC) pathways,
highlighting how catalyst operation and unit process efficiency influence overall performance.
The results demonstrate that no single material or transport mode is universally optimal; instead,
heterogeneous solutions tuned to specific operational contexts deliver better performance. The
project delivers a new capability for cross-scale material co-design, advancing hydrogen
infrastructure readiness and informing DOE and LLNL missions in climate and energy resilience.

Background and Research Objectives
The transition to a carbon-emission-free energy system requires scalable hydrogen storage,
transport, and utilization technologies, yet significant challenges remain across materials,
components, and infrastructure. Compressed and cryogenic hydrogen face high energy costs,
degradation, and boil-off losses, while LOHCs offer stable storage but require efficient catalysts
for hydrogen release under real-world conditions. Existing material models inadequately
capture system-level constraints such as temporal demand fluctuations, regional heterogeneity,
and cost uncertainty. Conversely, system models ignore the complex degradation phenomena
that limit material performance.
This project was motivated by the hypothesis that hydrogen delivery solutions will not converge
to a single optimal pathway; rather, multiple material-based approaches are needed depending
on end use and regional context. The research objectives were to:
1. Identify parameters necessary for cross-scale exchange and connect system, component,
 and material models.
2. Develop parameter interdependencies and extend model versatility to capture usage
 growth and variability at hydrogen refueling stations.
3. Identify critical parameters and establish uncertainty ranges to enable co-design of
 materials and processes.

We developed a Systems-to-Atoms (S2A) framework (Fig. 1) to co-simulate material, device,
and system design for clean hydrogen storage and transport. S2A was demonstrated on a
hydrogen storage and transport scenario where hydrogen is distributed using a liquid organic
hydrogen carrier (LOHC) to 700 bar hydrogen refueling stations (HRS).

In this work, the optimization approach introduced for multiscale systems is applicable when
the system consists of distinct models coupled by cross-scale variables. We construct a linear
model decision tree for each component, which serves as a piecewise linear surrogate within
the optimization framework. To demonstrate its utility, we applied this technique to the co-
optimization of catalyst selection and process design for a formic acid dehydrogenation reactor.
This case study illustrates the broader applicability of our approach in coupling models based
on differing physical principles and implemented using diverse software platforms.
This work demonstrates how surrogates enable rigorous mathematical optimization across
distinct yet interrelated models. By treating each underlying model as a data-generating “black
box”, our approach abstracts away the model’s form and the software used. This ensures that
any choice of underlying model can be converted to piecewise linear constraints. This method
facilitates global optimization, uncovering more favorable optima than those found through the
sequential optimization of individual models.

3. Improvements in the ML surrogate quality for multi-scale optimization using hyperplane
 surrogates (enabled on GPU with PyTorch)
Multiscale optimization problems require the interconnection of several models of distinct
phenomena which occur at different scales in length or time. However, the best model for any
particular phenomenon may not be amenable to rigorous optimization techniques. For
instance, molecular interactions are often modeled by computational chemistry software
packages that cannot be easily converted into optimization constraints. Data-driven surrogate
models can overcome this problem. By choosing surrogates with functional forms that are
convertible to a mixed-integer linear model, one can connect and optimize these surrogates
instead of the underlying models.
Fig. 5 shows plots of the original/underlying function alongside surrogates generated using
three different modeling approaches: a traditional decision tree, a linear model decision tree
(LMDT), and a hyperplane tree (HT). The decision tree model will divide the data into
subdomains and approximate the response in each subdomain with a constant value. The LMDT
makes similar horizontal and vertical splits but uses a linear model of the response in each
subdomain. Finally, the hyperplane tree model enables the use of diagonal, or hyperplane,
splits. The surrogate’s fit to the original function improves as the complexity of the modeling
strategy increases. This progression demonstrates how techniques like LMDT and HT can enable
a better description of the underlying function.

 Fig. 5: Tridimensional plots of the underlying function and the decision tree-based surrogates. For the
 surrogates, different colors represent individual leaves.

The primary application of these surrogate modeling strategies, both LMDT and HT, is to
replace nonlinear, simulation-based, or otherwise difficult-to-formulate expressions in
optimization problems. The use of these surrogates may improve solution time, or even enable
solutions of problems that might otherwise be computationally intractable. The suitability of
linear or hyperplane tree models for a specific optimization problem can depend on many
factors. For instance, for a specific case, hyperplane tree models may be more accurate and/or
smaller, but their increased complexity can result in slower optimization solves.

Mission Impact
This work directly supports LLNL’s mission in climate and energy resilience by providing a new
modeling and simulation capability that bridges materials science and systems analysis. The S2A
framework positions LLNL as a leader in multi-scale integration, enabling rapid response to
environment-specific infrastructure needs. For DOE and NNSA missions, the project advances
critical infrastructure by informing cost-performance trade-offs, guiding material development,
and providing validated tools for decision-making to meet future national security challenges.
Beyond hydrogen, the framework can be generalized to other mission areas requiring system-
material co-design, such as energy resiliency management, advanced manufacturing, and
resource security. The project also fostered cross-directorate collaboration and workforce
development, integrating expertise from PLS, ENG, COMP, and GS, and establishing new
partnerships with academia and industry.

Conclusion
The project demonstrated that system-material co-design is essential to realizing hydrogen’s
role in a decarbonized energy system. The capability is well-positioned for transition to industry
through partnerships with hydrogen infrastructure stakeholders, while future collaborations
with DOE applied energy offices could support continued development. Ultimately, this work
lays the foundation for more agile, multi-scale simulation approaches that can accelerate
deployment of resilient, cost-effective clean energy technologies.
