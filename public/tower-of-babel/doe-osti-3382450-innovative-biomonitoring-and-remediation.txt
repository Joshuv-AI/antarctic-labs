Executive Summary

The Savannah River Site (SRS) contains legacy coal combustion product (CCP)
impoundments that are impacted by elevated concentrations of heavy metals and
radionuclides, posing long-term risks to soil health, ecosystem functioning, and
environmental sustainability. Traditional environmental monitoring approaches rely
primarily on chemical analyses to quantify contaminant concentrations but often provide
limited information regarding biological responses or ecosystem recovery. This project
addressed these limitations by integrating environmental chemistry, microbial ecology,
artiﬁcial intelligence (AI), and bioremediation into a comprehensive framework for
environmental diagnostics and restoration of contaminated soils.

The overarching goal of this collaborative project between Florida A&M University (FAMU)
and the University of Georgia's Savannah River Ecology Laboratory (SREL) was to develop
innovative biomonitoring and remediation strategies for heavy metal-contaminated CCP
impoundment sites at the Savannah River Site. Speciﬁcally, the project sought to (i)
characterize heavy metal contamination, (ii) determine microbial responses to
contamination, (iii) isolate indigenous heavy metal-resistant microorganisms for
remediation applications, (iv) develop a microbial ecological health index using machine
learning, and (v) optimize fungal-mediated bioremediation using artiﬁcial intelligence.

Environmental diagnostic assessments quantiﬁed 24 metals using inductively coupled
plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and identiﬁed ﬁve distinct geochemical clusters that
reﬂected differences in contaminant mobility, redox behavior, nutrient cycling, and
geological origin. Correlation analyses demonstrated that toxic and redox-sensitive metals,
including mercury, nickel, copper, arsenic, cadmium, chromium, and lead, were
signiﬁcantly enriched at contaminated sites compared with uncontaminated reference
locations. These ﬁndings established the geochemical baseline for subsequent biological
investigations.

Microbial community analyses using 16S and 18S amplicon sequencing demonstrated that
heavy metal contamination substantially altered soil microbiomes. Contaminated soils

 iii

exhibited reduced bacterial richness but increased fungal richness, indicating contrasting
adaptive responses between prokaryotic and fungal communities. Heavy metal stress also
shifted microbial communities from specialist-dominated assemblages toward ecological
generalists, reﬂecting strong environmental ﬁltering under chronic contamination. Beta
diversity analyses showed a clear separation between contaminated and reference
communities, while predicted functional analyses revealed signiﬁcant restructuring of
microbial metabolic potential and a reduced abundance of nitrogen-cycling genes,
suggesting impairment of critical ecosystem functions.

To identify microbial resources for remediation, indigenous heavy metal-resistant fungi were
isolated and characterized from contaminated soils. Molecular identiﬁcation revealed four
dominant genera: Penicillium, Aspergillus, Trichoderma, and Purpureocillium. All isolates
demonstrated substantial tolerance to environmentally relevant concentrations of nickel,
copper, and arsenic, with several strains exhibiting resistance well above background site
concentrations. Among the isolates, Trichoderma spp. demonstrated broad-spectrum
heavy metal tolerance and was selected for subsequent laboratory remediation studies.
Experimental results indicated that heavy metal removal involved both biosorption and
probable biotransformation mechanisms, highlighting the potential of indigenous fungi as
effective agents for sustainable remediation.

A major outcome of the project was the development of a machine learning-based microbial
ecological health index that translates microbial community composition into a quantitative
measure of ecosystem condition. Redundancy analysis demonstrated that heavy metals
signiﬁcantly inﬂuenced microbial community composition, with nickel exhibiting the
strongest relationship. Random Forest and logistic regression models identiﬁed the
microbial taxa most predictive of contamination status and achieved an overall
classiﬁcation accuracy of approximately 91%. These core microbial indicators were
integrated into a standardized ecological health index that provides a biologically
meaningful assessment of environmental quality and offers a practical tool for long-term
monitoring and restoration assessment.

 iv

Artiﬁcial intelligence was further applied to optimize fungal-mediated bioremediation.
Laboratory experiments using Trichoderma spp. were designed using Latin Hypercube
Sampling to efficiently evaluate combinations of incubation time, temperature, and fungal
dosage. Machine learning models successfully predicted simultaneous removal efficiencies
for copper, nickel, and arsenic, and multi-objective optimization identiﬁed operating
conditions predicted to achieve approximately 99.9% removal of all three contaminants
under laboratory conditions. These results demonstrate the potential of integrating
indigenous microorganisms with AI-driven optimization to enhance remediation efficiency
while reducing experimental effort.

Collectively, this project establishes a comprehensive framework that integrates
environmental chemistry, microbiome science, machine learning, and bioremediation for
assessment and restoration of heavy metal-contaminated environments. The study
demonstrates that microbial communities serve as sensitive indicators of ecosystem
health, that indigenous fungal populations possess signiﬁcant potential for heavy metal
remediation, and that artiﬁcial intelligence can substantially improve both environmental
diagnostics and remediation optimization. The microbial ecological health index and AI-
assisted remediation framework developed through this project provide innovative tools that
can support future environmental monitoring, restoration, and decision-making at the
Savannah River Site and other contaminated ecosystems.

1. Introduction
1.1 Background

Coal combustion remains one of the world's major sources of electricity generation, accounting
for nearly half of global energy production in many regions. However, the process generates large
quantities of coal combustion products (CCPs), particularly fly ash, which contain complex
mixtures of heavy metals, metalloids, and radio nuclides that can become mobile in the
environment and adversely affect soil quality, surface waters, and groundwater resources (Bagwell
et al., 2025). Although CCPs are commonly disposed of in engineered impoundments and ash
ponds to minimize environmental exposure, long-term weathering, leaching, and occasional
structural failures can release contaminants into surrounding ecosystems, posing persistent risks
to human health, wildlife, and microbial communities (Petrović et al., 2022).

The Savannah River Site (SRS), a former U.S. Department of Energy nuclear production facility,
contains multiple legacy coal combustion product impoundments that continue to serve as
important sources of environmental contamination. It is estimated that more than 1.4 million cubic
meters of coal ash and coal fines remain at the site from historical operations at coal-fired power
plants (McCaslin, 2024). Over decades of operation, contaminants released from these
impoundments have accumulated within surrounding soils, sediments, wetlands, and aquatic
environments. Elevated concentrations of heavy metals and radionuclides—including chromium
(Cr), cobalt (Co), zinc (Zn), manganese (Mn), nickel (Ni), cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), arsenic (As),
mercury (Hg), aluminum (Al), uranium (U), zirconium (Zr), rubidium (Rb), and magnesium
(Mg)—have been reported throughout portions of the SRS and its surrounding watersheds
(Nangeelil et al., 2024). These contaminants exhibit diverse geochemical behaviors, including
persistence, bioaccumulation, and toxicity, making long-term environmental monitoring and
remediation a continuing priority.

Traditional monitoring programs at contaminated sites primarily rely on chemical measurements
to quantify contaminant concentrations and assess regulatory compliance. While these approaches
provide essential information on contaminant distribution, they offer limited insight into the
biological consequences of contamination or the capacity of ecosystems to recover following
remediation. Microbial communities are highly responsive to environmental disturbances and play

 1

central roles in nutrient cycling, contamination transformation, and ecosystem resilience (Philippot
et al., 2021). Consequently, changes in microbial community composition and function provide
sensitive indicators of ecological condition that complement conventional chemical monitoring.

Bioremediation has emerged as one of the most promising strategies for managing heavy metal-
contaminated environments because it harnesses naturally occurring microorganisms to
immobilize, transform, or detoxify hazardous contaminants. Compared with conventional
physicochemical treatment methods, including precipitation, coagulation, adsorption, and
chemical stabilization, biological remediation is generally more sustainable, cost-effective, and
environmentally compatible, while minimizing the generation of secondary pollutants (Krishna
Raj et al., 2026). Previous investigations at the Savannah River Site have demonstrated that
contaminated soils support diverse bacterial and fungal communities capable of tolerating elevated
heavy-metal concentrations, suggesting considerable potential to exploit indigenous
microorganisms in remediation efforts (Pathak et al., 2020). Recent studies have further identified
microbial taxa possessing metabolic pathways capable of transforming or immobilizing hazardous
metals, highlighting the potential of microbiome-guided remediation strategies (Kommu et al.,
2024).

Despite these advances, significant challenges remain in translating microbiological knowledge
into practical remediation technologies. Conventional approaches such as bioaugmentation and
biostimulation often yield variable outcomes because they rely on limited knowledge of
indigenous microbial ecology and lack predictive tools to optimize remediation performance.
Moreover, current remediation programs rarely incorporate biological indicators that can track
ecosystem recovery in real time. Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning,
and environmental bioinformatics provide new opportunities to overcome these limitations by
integrating environmental chemistry, microbial community dynamics, and predictive modeling
into unified decision-support frameworks (Kashif et al., 2025). Such approaches can identify
microbial indicators of ecosystem health, predict contamination status, optimize remediation
strategies, and support adaptive management of contaminated sites.

To address these knowledge gaps, this project developed an integrated framework that combines
environmental diagnostics, microbiome characterization, indigenous microbial isolation, machine

 2

learning, and AI-assisted bioremediation to improve assessment and restoration of heavy metal-
contaminated coal combustion product impoundments at the Savannah River Site. The resulting
microbial ecological health index and AI-guided bioremediation framework provide innovative
tools for evaluating ecosystem conditions, monitoring restoration success, and supporting
sustainable remediation of legacy-contaminated environments.

1.2. Project Objectives

The overarching goal of this project was to develop an integrated framework that
combines environmental diagnostics, microbial ecology, artiﬁcial intelligence (AI), and
bioremediation to improve the assessment and restoration of heavy metal-
contaminated coal combustion product (CCP) impoundments at the Savannah River
Site (SRS).

The speciﬁc objectives were to:

 1. Characterize the geochemical environment of contaminated and reference sites
 by quantifying heavy metal concentrations, assessing metal bioavailability, and
 identifying contamination hotspots using multivariate statistical analyses.

 2. Characterize soil microbial communities using amplicon sequencing to
 determine how heavy metal contamination inﬂuences microbial diversity,
 community composition, ecological strategies, and functional potential, and to
 identify microbial indicators of ecosystem condition.

 3. Isolate and characterize indigenous heavy metal-resistant microorganisms from
 contaminated soils, evaluate their tolerance to environmentally relevant metal
 concentrations, and assess their potential for bioremediation.

 4. Develop a machine learning-assisted microbial ecological health index that
 integrates environmental chemistry and microbial community composition to
 quantitatively assess ecosystem health and contamination status.

 3

 5. Optimize fungal-mediated heavy metal bioremediation using artiﬁcial
 intelligence and machine learning to identify operating conditions that maximize
 simultaneous removal of multiple heavy metals.

 6. Provide interdisciplinary training for undergraduate, graduate, and postdoctoral
 researchers through research activities integrating environmental science,
 microbiology, artiﬁcial intelligence, and environmental biotechnology.

2. Materials and Methodology

2.0 Study Site

The study was conducted at the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Savannah River Site
(SRS), a 310-square-mile (approximately 803 km²) federal reservation located along the
Savannah River near Aiken, South Carolina, USA. Established in the early 1950s for the
production of nuclear materials, the SRS contains multiple industrial facilities, nuclear
reactor areas, waste management units, coal combustion product (CCP) impoundments,
wetlands, streams, and forested ecosystems. Decades of industrial and nuclear operations
have resulted in localized contamination by heavy metals, radionuclides, and other
hazardous constituents, making the site an important location for developing and evaluating
innovative environmental monitoring and remediation technologies (Nangeelil et al., 2024).

The SRS is divided into several operational areas, including major facility areas (A-, B-, D-,
F-, H-, M-, N-, S-, Z-, E-, and J-Areas) and reactor areas (C-, K-, L-, P-, and R-Areas) (Figure
1). Historical coal-ﬁred power plants and associated coal combustion product disposal
ponds have contributed to the accumulation of heavy metals in portions of the site.
Contaminants of particular concern include mercury (Hg), nickel (Ni), uranium (U), arsenic
(As), chromium (Cr), cadmium (Cd), copper (Cu), lead (Pb), zinc (Zn), and other trace metals
that persist in soils and sediments (Edwards et al., 2014).

This project focused on CCP-impacted environments and adjacent reference locations to
evaluate the effects of heavy metal contamination on soil chemistry, microbial community

 4

structure, and remediation potential. Sampling locations were selected to represent a
gradient of contamination, ranging from highly contaminated impoundment soils to
relatively uncontaminated reference sites. This gradient enabled comparison of
environmental conditions, microbial community responses, and remediation performance
across varying levels of heavy metal exposure.

The Savannah River Site provides a unique natural laboratory for investigating interactions
among environmental chemistry, microbial ecology, and remediation processes because of
its long history of contamination, extensive environmental monitoring infrastructure, and
diverse ecological habitats. The site's well-characterized contamination history makes it an
ideal setting for developing microbial bioindicators, ecological health indices, and artiﬁcial
intelligence-based decision-support tools to monitor ecosystem recovery and optimize
remediation strategies.

2.1 Soil Sampling

Soil samples were collected from contaminated and reference ﬂoodplain locations within
the Savannah River Site (SRS), selected based on prior geochemical characterization and
sufficient spatial separation to minimize cross-contamination while maintaining
comparable soil types, vegetation cover, and hydrological conditions. The sampling
locations and corresponding geographic coordinates are provided in Table 2.1.

Sampling was conducted seasonally during spring, summer, late summer (peak), fall, and
winter to capture temporal variations in environmental conditions and microbial community
dynamics. The peak sampling period corresponded to late summer when temperatures were
highest, and soil moisture was lowest.

At each sampling event, replicate topsoil cores (0–15 cm) were collected using sterile corers.
Individual cores from each sampling location were composited and homogenized in the ﬁeld
to reduce small-scale spatial variability. Samples were transported to the laboratory on ice
immediately after collection. Portions designated for molecular analyses were stored at
−80°C, whereas subsamples intended for geochemical analyses were air-dried prior to
processing.

2.2 Chemical Analysis of Soil Trace Metals
Trace metal concentrations were determined using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry
(ICP-MS) following microwave-assisted acid digestion. Approximately 0.5 g of homogenized air-
dried soil was digested with 10 mL of concentrated nitric acid (HNO₃) following the procedures
outlined in U.S. EPA Method 3051A (Kwon et al., 2024). The resulting acid extracts were analyzed
by ICP-MS in accordance with U.S. EPA Method 6020B.

The analytical procedure quantified the acid-extractable fraction of soil metals, representing the
environmentally available metal pool rather than total elemental concentrations. Consequently,
elements predominantly bound within silicate mineral matrices, such as aluminum (Al) and
titanium (Ti), may be underrepresented relative to their total concentrations (Bennett et al., 2015).

Metal concentrations were measured in duplicate using a PerkinElmer Optima 8000 ICP-MS
following calibration with certified multi-element standards. Instrument performance and
analytical quality were monitored throughout the study using calibration blanks, procedural blanks,
duplicate digestions, and certified reference materials, which were analyzed at regular intervals to
ensure analytical accuracy, precision, and reproducibility.

A total of 24 metals were quantified and subsequently classified, based on their biological
functions and toxicological characteristics, into essential nutrients, other metals, heavy metals, and
heavy metals of toxicological concern (Rojas-Lemus et al., 2026). This classification provided the
framework for subsequent environmental diagnostic analyses.

2.3 Correlation-Based Geochemical Analysis
Relationships among trace metals were investigated using pairwise correlation analysis to identify
metals exhibiting similar environmental behavior and geochemical controls. Pairwise correlations
were calculated across all soil samples, and statistically significant associations (p < 0.01) were
retained for further analysis.

Hierarchical clustering of the correlation matrix was subsequently performed to group metals into
geochemically related assemblages. Five correlation-based metal groups (Groups I–V) were
identified and used in subsequent analyses to evaluate cumulative metal concentrations across

 7

contamination status and sampling seasons. These groupings facilitated the interpretation of
contaminant mobility, redox behavior, nutrient cycling, and potential sources of contamination
throughout the study area.

2.4 DNA Extraction and Amplicon Sequencing
Total genomic DNA was extracted from frozen soil samples using the DNeasy PowerSoil Kit
(Qiagen, MD, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNA concentration and purity
were determined using the Qubit High Sensitivity (HS) Assay Kit (Invitrogen, CA, USA) prior to
library preparation.

Bacterial community composition was characterized by sequencing the V4–V5 region of the 16S
rRNA gene, amplified using the universal primer pair 515F (5′-GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-
3′) and 926R (5′-CCGYCAATTYMTTTRAGTTT-3′). Fungal communities were characterized by
sequencing the 18S rRNA gene using universal eukaryotic primers EUF (5′-
GTCAGAGGTGAAATTCTTGGATTTA-3′) and EUR (5′-AGGGCAGGGACGTAATCAACG-
3′). Both bacterial and fungal amplicon libraries were prepared using a two-step PCR protocol,
pooled, and sequenced on an Illumina platform at the University of Illinois at Chicago Research
Resources Center.

Raw amplicon sequence data generated during this study have been deposited in the NCBI
BioProject database (Accession No. PRJNA1242494).

2.5 Sequence Processing and Taxonomic Assignment
Raw bacterial and fungal amplicon sequences were processed independently using the DADA2
pipeline implemented in R (Version 4.5). Primer sequences were removed using Cutadapt,
followed by quality filtering, error correction, sequence denoising, paired-end read merging, and
chimera removal to generate high-resolution Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs). Only
sequences matching the expected amplicon lengths were retained for downstream analyses.

Potential contaminant ASVs were identified and removed using the decontam package with a
prevalence-based approach utilizing extraction and PCR negative controls (Davis et al., 2018).
Bacterial ASVs were taxonomically classified against the SILVA SSU rRNA database (Version
138.2), while fungal ASVs were assigned using the PR2 (Protist Ribosomal Reference) database,

 8

which provides comprehensive taxonomic coverage of eukaryotic microorganisms. Non-target
sequences, including chloroplasts, mitochondria, unassigned taxa at the phylum level, and ASVs
detected in only a single sample, were excluded prior to downstream analyses.

Following quality control, the bacterial dataset comprised 32,350,182 high-quality sequences, with
an average sequencing depth of 622,118 ± 82,016 reads per sample. The fungal dataset underwent
the same quality-control pipeline before diversity and community analyses.

2.6 Microbial Diversity and Community Composition Analyses
Microbial diversity analyses were conducted using the phyloseq and vegan packages in R. Alpha
diversity was evaluated separately for bacterial and fungal communities using ASV richness,
Shannon diversity, and Faith's phylogenetic diversity following rarefaction to the minimum
retained sequencing depth for each dataset. Differences between contaminated and reference sites
within each sampling season were evaluated using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.

Community composition (beta diversity) was assessed using Principal Coordinates Analysis
(PCoA) based on weighted UniFrac distances for bacterial communities and Bray–Curtis
dissimilarity for fungal communities after Cumulative Sum Scaling (CSS) normalization to
account for differences in sequencing depth.

Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with 1,000 permutations was
used to evaluate the effects of contamination status, sampling season, and their interaction on
microbial community composition.

2.7 Ecological Strategy Classification
To investigate ecological responses to heavy metal contamination, bacterial and fungal ASVs were
classified as generalists, intermediates, or specialists using a consensus niche-breadth framework
that integrated Shannon-based, Levins', and occurrence-based niche-breadth indices, calculated
with the EcolUtils package in R.

Differences in the relative abundances of ecological strategy groups between contaminated and
reference sites were assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests to evaluate shifts in microbial
adaptation along contamination gradients.

 9

2.8 Functional Prediction and Statistical Analyses
Potential functional profiles of bacterial communities were inferred from 16S rRNA gene data
using PICRUSt2, enabling prediction of metabolic pathways and nitrogen-cycling genes
associated with heavy metal contamination. Functional beta diversity analyses were subsequently
performed to compare predicted metabolic profiles between contaminated and reference soils.

Relationships between microbial communities and environmental variables were further evaluated
using multivariate statistical approaches, including redundancy analysis (RDA), PERMANOVA,
and correlation analyses, to identify environmental drivers of microbial community structure and
function.

2.9 Isolation and Characterization of Heavy Metal-Resistant Fungal Strains
Isolation of Heavy Metal-Resistant Fungi
Heavy metal-resistant fungi were isolated from contaminated Savannah River Site (SRS) soils
using Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) supplemented with environmentally relevant concentrations of
arsenic (As), nickel (Ni), and copper (Cu). Equal aliquots from twenty-two fungal enrichment
cultures were composited to generate a representative inoculum. Serial ten-fold dilutions (10⁻¹–
10⁻⁵) were prepared in sterile 0.85% sodium chloride solution, and aliquots from the 10⁻¹, 10⁻³,
and 10⁻⁵ dilutions were spread onto PDA plates containing heavy metals.

The culture medium consisted of Potato Dextrose Agar supplemented with agar for improved
solidification and amended with arsenic, nickel, and copper prior to plating. Plates were incubated
at 30°C until distinct fungal colonies developed. Individual colonies exhibiting vigorous growth
under heavy metal stress were repeatedly re-streaked on fresh PDA plates to obtain pure cultures
for molecular identification and physiological characterization.

Molecular Identification of Fungal Isolates
Genomic DNA was extracted from twenty-two purified fungal isolates using the
ZymoBIOMICS™ DNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) according to the
manufacturer's instructions.

The fungal internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region was amplified using the universal primer pair:

 10

• ITS-F1: 5′-GAAGTCGTAACAAGGTTTCC-3′
• ITS-R1: 5′-TCCTGGTTAGTTTCTTTTCC-3′

 PCR amplification was performed using a 20-µL reaction mixture containing 2× PCR Master Mix,
 forward and reverse primers, template DNA, and nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling conditions
 consisted of an initial denaturation at 92°C for 2 min, followed by 36 cycles of denaturation at
 94°C for 1 min, annealing at 55°C for 45 s, extension at 72°C for 45 s, and a final extension at
    72°C for 5 min.

 Amplified products were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis using 1% agarose gels in 1× TAE
 buffer and visualized under ultraviolet illumination. PCR products with the expected fragment size
 were submitted for Sanger sequencing to enable molecular identification.

 Phylogenetic Analysis
 Consensus ITS sequences were compared against the NCBI GenBank database using the Basic
 Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) to determine taxonomic identities. Phylogenetic
 relationships among the isolates were reconstructed using MEGA 11 software with the Neighbor-
 Joining algorithm and 100 bootstrap replicates to assess branch support.

 Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) Assay
 Heavy metal tolerance of the fungal isolates was evaluated using Minimum Inhibitory
 Concentration (MIC) assays. Pure fungal cultures were first grown in Potato Dextrose Broth (PDB)
 at 28°C with continuous shaking for 7 days to obtain actively growing inocula.

 Aliquots (10 µL) of each fungal suspension were inoculated onto PDA plates containing increasing
 concentrations of arsenic (As), copper (Cu), or nickel (Ni). Control plates lacking heavy metals
 were included for comparison. All cultures were incubated for 7 days, after which fungal growth
 was examined to determine the highest metal concentration supporting visible colony
 development.

 2.10 Development of the Microbial Ecological Health Index
 A machine learning-assisted framework was developed to construct a Microbial Ecological Health
 Index (MEHI) that quantifies ecosystem condition using microbial community composition. The
 workflow integrated microbial abundance data, environmental chemistry, multivariate statistics,
 and supervised machine learning to identify microbial taxa that best discriminated against heavy
 metal contamination status.

 Identification of Environmental Drivers

 Relationships between microbial community composition and environmental variables were
 evaluated using Redundancy Analysis (RDA) implemented in the vegan package in R. Statistical
 significance of individual environmental variables was assessed using permutation-based analysis
 of variance (ANOVA) with 999 permutations. Metals that contributed significantly to microbial
 community variation were retained for subsequent analyses.

 2.11 Machine Learning Optimization of Fungal Bioremediation
 Laboratory bioremediation experiments were conducted using the indigenous Trichoderma isolate
 identified in Objective 3 as the most promising heavy-metal-resistant fungal strain.
 Experimental Design
 Experimental conditions were generated using Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) to efficiently
 explore the multidimensional design space while minimizing the number of laboratory
 experiments required. Three experimental factors were investigated:

• incubation time (days),
• incubation temperature (°C), and
• fungal inoculum dosage.

 A total of 100 experimental combinations were generated using the LHS design, providing uniform
 coverage of the experimental space and reducing bias associated with conventional factorial
 designs.

 Laboratory Bioremediation Experiments
 Each experimental condition was evaluated for simultaneous removal of copper (Cu), nickel (Ni),
 and arsenic (As) under controlled laboratory conditions. Heavy metal removal efficiencies were
 determined following incubation by comparing residual metal concentrations with those of
 untreated controls.

 Machine Learning Model Development

 14

Experimental data were used to develop predictive machine learning models relating operating
conditions to heavy metal removal efficiencies. Input variables included incubation time,
temperature, and fungal dosage, whereas model outputs included the percentage removal of Cu,
Ni, and As.

Several regression algorithms were evaluated, and the optimal model was selected based on
predictive performance using the coefficient of determination (R²), mean absolute error (MAE),
and root mean square error (RMSE). Model performance was further assessed using parity plots,
residual analyses, and permutation feature importance to identify the experimental variables most
strongly influencing remediation efficiency.

Multi-Objective Optimization
Following model validation, desirability-based multi-objective optimization was performed to
simultaneously maximize removal efficiencies for copper, nickel, and arsenic. Predicted Pareto-
optimal solutions were generated from the trained machine learning model, and the experimental
condition with the highest overall desirability was selected as the optimal operating condition for
fungal-mediated remediation.

This integrated optimization framework provides a data-driven approach for identifying operating
conditions that maximize heavy metal removal while minimizing experimental effort and can be
readily adapted to other contaminated environments and remediation systems.

Statistical Analysis
Unless otherwise stated, statistical analyses were performed using R (Version 4.5) and Python
(Version 3.13). Differences between contaminated and reference groups were evaluated using non-
parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, while multivariate analyses employed PERMANOVA and
redundancy analysis. Machine learning model performance was assessed using independent test
sets and standard performance metrics, including classification accuracy, balanced accuracy,
precision, recall, F1 Score, coefficient of determination (R²), mean absolute error (MAE), and root
mean square error (RMSE). Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05.

3.1.4 Key Contaminants of Concern

Although 24 metals were quantiﬁed to provide a comprehensive geochemical assessment
of the study area, three contaminants—mercury (Hg), uranium (U), and nickel (Ni)—were
identiﬁed as the primary contaminants of concern for detailed investigation throughout this
project. Their selection was based on historical records of contamination at the Savannah
River Site (SRS), environmental persistence, ecological signiﬁcance, and their potential
impacts on microbial community structure and ecosystem function.

Mercury (Hg) remains one of the most environmentally signiﬁcant contaminants at the SRS
due to its long history of industrial release and its ability to undergo microbial
transformations that produce methylmercury, a highly toxic and bioaccumulative

 21

 compound. Because mercury speciation and mobility are strongly inﬂuenced by microbial
 activity, understanding microbial responses to mercury contamination was considered
 essential for evaluating ecosystem health and remediation potential.

 Uranium (U) was included because of its legacy as a radionuclide contaminant resulting
 from historical nuclear materials production at the SRS. Uranium mobility is governed by
 complex geochemical and microbial redox processes, making it an ideal target for
 investigating interactions between environmental conditions, microbial communities, and
 contaminant transport.

 Nickel (Ni) was selected because it is a persistent heavy metal commonly associated with
 coal combustion products and industrial activities. In addition to its environmental
 persistence, nickel exerts strong selective pressure on microbial communities and can
 inﬂuence numerous metabolic pathways involved in nutrient cycling and metal resistance.

 These contaminants collectively represent distinct classes of environmental stressors,
 including heavy metals and radionuclides, each with diverse geochemical behaviors and
 ecological impacts. Consequently, subsequent project activities focused on determining
 how microbial community composition, functional potential, and predictive machine-
 learning models respond to gradients in Hg, U, and Ni contamination. This targeted approach
 enabled the development of microbial bioindicators and environmental health prediction
 models to support future monitoring and phytoremediation strategies at legacy-
 contaminated sites.

 4. Integrated Discussion

 This project developed and validated an integrated framework that combines environmental
 chemistry, microbial ecology, artificial intelligence (AI), and fungal bioremediation to improve
 the assessment and remediation of heavy metal-contaminated ecosystems. Rather than treating
 environmental monitoring and remediation as separate activities, the project established a
 continuous workflow in which environmental diagnostics informed microbiome characterization,
 microbiome analyses guided the isolation of indigenous remediation organisms, and machine
 learning integrated these datasets into predictive tools for ecosystem health assessment and
 remediation optimization.

 The environmental diagnostic assessment established the geochemical foundation of the study by
 identifying distinct patterns of heavy metal contamination across the Savannah River Site (SRS).
 Twenty-four trace metals were quantified and grouped into five geochemical assemblages
 representing differences in contaminant mobility, redox sensitivity, nutrient cycling, and
 geological origin. The elevated concentrations of redox-sensitive metals and arsenic-associated
 elements at contaminated locations confirmed the persistence of legacy contamination from coal
 combustion products and highlighted the complex geochemical processes governing contaminant
 transport and bioavailability. These findings provided the environmental context necessary for
 interpreting subsequent biological responses.

 54

Microbial community analyses demonstrated that heavy metal contamination fundamentally
altered both bacterial and fungal communities. While bacterial richness declined under
contamination, fungal richness increased, suggesting contrasting adaptive strategies between
prokaryotic and fungal microorganisms. Bacteria appeared more susceptible to chronic heavy-
metal stress, whereas fungal communities were enriched with metal-tolerant taxa capable of
exploiting ecological niches created by environmental disturbance. This observation is consistent
with the known resilience of many filamentous fungi, whose extensive hyphal networks,
extracellular enzymes, and metal-binding mechanisms enhance survival in contaminated
environments (Priyadarshini et al., 2021).

Beyond changes in diversity, contamination also altered microbial community assembly. Both
bacterial and fungal communities shifted from specialist-dominated assemblages toward
communities dominated by ecological generalists. Specialists typically occupy narrow ecological
niches and perform highly specific ecological functions, whereas generalists possess broader
metabolic capabilities that enhance survival under environmental stress (Ma et al., 2025). The
observed transition toward generalist communities, therefore, reflects ecological filtering imposed
by long-term heavy metal contamination. Similar shifts were evident in community composition
and predicted metabolic functions, with contaminated soils forming distinct clusters and exhibiting
reduced abundances of nitrogen-cycling genes. These functional changes suggest that heavy metal
contamination not only alters microbial diversity but also compromises critical ecosystem
processes that sustain nutrient cycling and soil fertility.

The isolation and characterization of indigenous heavy metal-resistant fungi demonstrated that
contaminated ecosystems also harbor microorganisms with significant remediation potential. Four
dominant fungal genera—Penicillium, Aspergillus, Trichoderma, and Purpureocillium—were
successfully isolated and shown to tolerate heavy metal concentrations exceeding those measured
at the study sites. The direct recovery of these organisms from contaminated soils indicates that
long-term exposure has selected for microbial populations with physiological adaptations for
survival under multiple metal stresses. Among these isolates, Trichoderma exhibited broad-
spectrum tolerance to arsenic, copper, and nickel and demonstrated substantial metal removal
during laboratory experiments. The discrepancy between initial metal concentrations and the
quantities recovered through extracellular adsorption and intracellular accumulation further

 55

suggests that fungal-mediated remediation involved not only passive biosorption but also
biologically mediated transformation processes.

One of the most significant outcomes of this project was the demonstration that microbial
communities can serve as quantitative indicators of ecosystem conditions. Redundancy analysis
confirmed that heavy metals, particularly nickel, explained a significant proportion of the observed
variation in microbial community composition, providing strong evidence that microbial
communities respond predictably to contamination gradients. Building on this ecological
relationship, supervised machine learning identified the microbial taxa that most accurately
distinguished contamination levels, achieving approximately 91% classification accuracy. These
indicator taxa were subsequently integrated into a Microbial Ecological Health Index, providing a
biologically meaningful metric that translates complex microbial community data into a
standardized measure of environmental condition. Unlike conventional monitoring approaches
that rely exclusively on contaminant concentrations, the microbial index incorporates the
ecosystem's biological response and therefore provides insight into ecological health and recovery.

Artificial intelligence also proved valuable for optimizing remediation processes. Laboratory
experiments using Trichoderma were designed using Latin Hypercube Sampling to maximize
information obtained from a limited number of experiments. Machine learning models
successfully predicted heavy metal removal efficiencies across combinations of incubation time,
temperature, and fungal dosage and identified experimental conditions predicted to achieve nearly
complete simultaneous removal of copper, nickel, and arsenic. This optimization framework
demonstrates how AI can substantially reduce experimental effort while improving remediation
performance, thereby accelerating the development of practical remediation strategies.

Collectively, these findings illustrate the value of integrating environmental chemistry, microbial
ecology, and artificial intelligence into a unified environmental management framework. Chemical
analyses identify the nature and magnitude of contamination; microbiome analyses reveal the
biological consequences of contaminant exposure; indigenous microorganisms provide sustainable
remediation agents; and machine learning transforms these diverse datasets into predictive tools
that support environmental decision-making. Rather than functioning independently, these

 56

components reinforce one another to provide a comprehensive understanding of ecosystem
condition and remediation potential.

The framework developed in this project has applications that extend beyond the Savannah River
Site. Because microbial communities respond rapidly to environmental disturbance, microbial
ecological health indices have the potential to complement conventional chemical monitoring at a
wide range of contaminated sites, including mining areas, industrial facilities, coal combustion
product impoundments, and legacy nuclear installations. Similarly, integrating machine learning
with indigenous microbial remediation offers a scalable strategy to optimize remediation across
diverse environmental conditions. Together, these advances represent an important step toward
biologically informed, data-driven environmental management that supports both contamination
assessment and long-term ecosystem restoration.

8. Conclusions

This project established an integrated framework that combines environmental chemistry,
microbial ecology, artificial intelligence (AI), and fungal bioremediation to improve the
assessment and restoration of heavy metal-contaminated ecosystems at the Savannah River Site
(SRS). By integrating environmental diagnostics, microbiome analyses, indigenous microbial
isolation, machine learning, and process optimization, the project advances both the scientific
understanding of contaminated soil ecosystems and the development of practical tools for
environmental monitoring and remediation.

The environmental diagnostic component demonstrated that contamination at the SRS is
characterized by complex geochemical interactions among multiple heavy metals rather than by
isolated contaminants. Correlation analyses identified distinct geochemical assemblages that
reflect differences in contaminant mobility, redox behavior, and nutrient cycling, providing a
comprehensive understanding of the environmental factors influencing ecosystem health. These
findings establish an important geochemical baseline for long-term monitoring of legacy coal
combustion products (CCP) impoundments and similar contaminated environments.

Microbiome analyses revealed that heavy metal contamination fundamentally reshapes soil
microbial communities. Chronic contamination reduced bacterial richness while increasing fungal
richness, promoted a shift from ecological specialists to generalists, altered microbial community
composition, and reduced the abundance of predicted nitrogen cycling genes. Together, these
responses demonstrate that microbial communities provide sensitive biological indicators of
environmental disturbance and ecosystem function. Unlike conventional monitoring approaches
that rely solely on chemical measurements, microbiome-based assessments capture the biological
consequences of contamination and therefore provide a more comprehensive evaluation of
ecosystem conditions.

 58

The isolation and characterization of indigenous heavy metal-resistant fungi further demonstrated
that contaminated environments harbor microbial populations with significant remediation
potential. Four dominant fungal genera—Penicillium, Aspergillus, Trichoderma, and
Purpureocillium—were successfully isolated and shown to tolerate elevated concentrations of
arsenic, copper, and nickel. Among these, Trichoderma exhibited broad-spectrum metal resistance
and strong remediation potential under laboratory conditions. The observed metal removal
exceeded that attributable to biosorption alone, suggesting that metabolically mediated
transformation processes contributed to contaminant removal and highlighting the value of
indigenous microorganisms for sustainable remediation.

A major innovation of this project was the development of a Microbial Ecological Health Index
(MEHI) that translates microbial community composition into a quantitative measure of ecosystem
condition. By integrating redundancy analysis with machine learning, the project identified
microbial indicator taxa that accurately distinguished contamination levels and achieved
approximately 91% classification accuracy. The resulting health index provides an objective,
biologically informed metric that complements traditional chemical monitoring and can be used
to evaluate ecosystem degradation, monitor restoration progress, and support environmental
decision-making.

The project also demonstrated the transformative potential of artificial intelligence for
environmental biotechnology. Machine learning models successfully optimized fungal-mediated
heavy metal removal by identifying combinations of incubation time, temperature, and fungal
dosage predicted to maximize simultaneous removal of copper, nickel, and arsenic. This data-
driven optimization framework substantially reduces experimental effort while improving
remediation efficiency and illustrates how AI can accelerate the development of sustainable
remediation technologies.

Beyond the individual scientific advances, the greatest achievement of this project is the successful
integration of multiple disciplines into a unified environmental management framework.
Environmental chemistry defined the contamination landscape, microbiome analyses revealed
ecosystem responses, indigenous fungal isolates provided biological remediation agents, and
machine learning converted these datasets into predictive tools for environmental assessment and

 59

remediation optimization. This systems-based approach represents a significant advancement over
conventional remediation strategies that evaluate chemical contamination independently of
biological responses.

The outcomes of this research have broad applicability beyond the Savannah River Site. The
microbial ecological health index provides a transferable framework for biological monitoring of
contaminated ecosystems, while the AI-assisted optimization strategy can be adapted to a wide
range of remediation applications involving heavy metals, mining-impacted lands, industrial sites,
and legacy waste facilities. Furthermore, integrating microbial indicators with predictive analytics
offers opportunities to develop next-generation environmental monitoring systems that support
adaptive management and evidence-based remediation decisions.

Overall, this project demonstrates that combining microbial intelligence with artificial intelligence
can transform how contaminated ecosystems are monitored and restored. The methodologies,
predictive models, and ecological health assessment tools developed through this work provide a
strong scientific foundation for future research and practical implementation of biologically
informed, data-driven environmental management strategies. These advances directly support the
U.S. Department of Energy's mission to develop innovative technologies that improve
environmental stewardship, accelerate remediation, and enhance the long-term sustainability of
contaminated federal sites.
