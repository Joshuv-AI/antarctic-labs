Direct domain estimation via regression-tree-assisted estimators in the production of official statistics
Juan Pablo Ferreira Facultad de Ciencias Económicas y de Administración, Universidad de la República, Uruguay juanpablo.ferreira@fcea.edu.uy

Abstract
National statistical offices (NSOs) produce their estimates under a single weighting system (the uni-weight approach): one set of weights, independent of the variable of interest, is used to estimate multiple parameters and multiple subpopulations (domains). In this paper we study, within the family of model-assisted estimators and from a design-based perspective of direct estimation, the use of regression trees as the assisting model for estimating totals in unplanned domains. We distinguish two strategies: (i) fitting a single tree at the population level and deriving from it uni-weight weights applicable to any domain (RT U ), and (ii) fitting a domain-specific tree (RT D ). We show that both estimators can be written as weighted sums with weights that do not depend on y y , preserving the uni-weight property and additivity ( benchmarking ) with respect to the population total. Extending to trees the classical result of 6 , we argue why the estimator built from a population-level model tends to behave like the Horvitz–Thompson estimator within domains, whereas the domain-specific model can achieve substantial variance reductions. A simulation study based on microdata from the Uruguayan Continuous Household Survey (ECH) illustrates the behavior of the estimators at the population level and by department. Keywords: domain estimation; model-assisted estimators; regression trees; calibration; official statistics; uni-weight.



1
Introduction

Sample surveys are one of the main sources of information for the production of official statistics. In the practice of national statistical offices (NSOs), estimates are obtained by attaching a weight to each sampled unit and using a
single
weighting system to estimate different parameters (totals, means, ratios) and different subsets of the population. This approach, known as
uni-weight

(
6
)
, has decisive operational advantages: it simplifies the computation of point estimates, creates economies of scale, suits continuous surveys that must release results in a timely fashion, and guarantees internal consistency among the figures disseminated. The weights are typically built through regression or calibration estimators
(
5
;
18
)
, accounting for production needs.

The uni-weight approach is not, however, optimal for every parameter or domain. The efficiency of a model-assisted estimator depends on the predictive power of the assisting model for the variable of interest
(
16
;
1
)
; a single model, fitted to the whole population, does not necessarily capture the relationship between the auxiliary variables and the study variable within a particular subdomain. This tension between operational convenience and local efficiency is the starting point of this work.

Modern assisting models.

The recent literature has incorporated flexible prediction techniques as assisting models within the design-based framework.
1
review the use of modern prediction methods.
13
propose an estimator assisted by a regression tree and, building on
19
, establish its design consistency; the estimator admits an appealing interpretation as a post-stratified estimator whose post-strata are selected automatically by the recursive partitioning algorithm.
4
extend the approach to random forests and, relevantly for this work, distinguish estimators based on partitions built at the population level from those based on local partitions. The use of the lasso as a variable-selection mechanism was studied by
12
.

Domain estimation.

The problem of estimating parameters for subpopulations admits two broad families of solutions. On one hand,
model-based
or indirect small area estimation (SAE), based on random-effects models that borrow strength across domains
(
15
)
; recent work incorporates machine learning into this framework through mixed models of the random-forest type
(
8
)
. On the other hand,
design-based
or direct estimation, which uses only the observations within the domain (possibly augmented with auxiliary information) and preserves design properties such as asymptotic unbiasedness and design-based variance estimation.
17
compares both strategies as functions of sample size, sampling fraction, and departure from the model;
7
argue that, for a wide class of design-consistent estimators and under general conditions, “borrowing strength” is not the best technique. The comparison between area- and unit-level estimators has been systematized by
9
.

Contribution of this work.

Our goal is to study, within the uni-weight and design-based direct approach, the use of regression trees as the assisting model for estimating totals in unplanned domains. The contributions are:

(i)

to formulate the estimator assisted by a domain-specific regression tree (
RT
D
) and to show that it can be expressed as a linear combination of sample observations with weights independent of the study variable that estimate the domain population size without error;

(ii)

to characterize the estimator built from a population-level tree with uni-weight weights (
RT
U
), and to explain, extending to trees the argument of
6
, why it tends to behave like the Horvitz–Thompson estimator within domains;

(iii)

to discuss the
trade-off
between domain-level precision and population-level optimality, and its relevance for large-scale production;

(iv)

to illustrate empirically the behavior of the estimators using microdata from the Uruguayan Continuous Household Survey (ECH).

2
Estimation framework and notation

Let
U=\{1,2,\dots,i,\dots,N\}
be a finite population of
N
eligible units (for example, the working-age individuals of a country). The goal is to estimate the total of a variable of interest
y
,

Y=\sum_{i\in U}y_{i}.

(1)

In addition to the study variable, a vector of auxiliary variables
\bm{x}_{i}
is available (from censuses, registers, or other sources), known for all units of the frame or, at least, with known population totals
\bm{X}=\sum_{i\in U}\bm{x}_{i}
.

A sampling design is defined on the basis of the available information (strata, measures of size, etc.) that assigns each unit a positive first-order inclusion probability,

\pi_{i}=\Pr[i\in s]>0,\qquad\forall\,i\in U,

(2)

and second-order probabilities
\pi_{ij}=\Pr[i,j\in s]
. The sample
s
is selected and
y_{i}
(together with the auxiliary information) is observed for
i\in s
. The basic design weight is
w_{i}=\pi_{i}^{-1}
.

Horvitz–Thompson estimator.

The simple estimator uses only the information on the variable of interest from the units included in the sample
(
10
)
:

\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{HT}}=\sum_{i\in s}\frac{y_{i}}{\pi_{i}}=\sum_{i\in s}w_{i}\,y_{i}.

(3)

It is design-unbiased,
\mathbb{E}(\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{HT}})=Y
, with variance

\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{HT}})=\sum_{i\in U}\sum_{j\in U}(\pi_{ij}-\pi_{i}\pi_{j})\,\frac{y_{i}}{\pi_{i}}\,\frac{y_{j}}{\pi_{j}},

(4)

and, if the design allows it, an unbiased variance estimator

\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{HT}})=\sum_{i\in s}\sum_{j\in s}\pi_{ij}^{-1}(\pi_{ij}-\pi_{i}\pi_{j})\,\frac{y_{i}}{\pi_{i}}\,\frac{y_{j}}{\pi_{j}}.

(5)

3
Model-assisted estimation

3.1
From the difference estimator to the prediction estimator

Suppose a method
m(\cdot)
for predicting
y
is available that
does not depend on the sample
. With its outputs, the difference estimator is defined
(
3
)
:

\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{DIFF}}=\sum_{i\in U}m(\bm{x}_{i})+\sum_{i\in s}w_{i}\bigl(y_{i}-m(\bm{x}_{i})\bigr).

(6)

This estimator is unbiased regardless of the quality of
m(\cdot)
,
\mathbb{E}(\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{DIFF}})=Y
. Since the first term is not random, its design variance is

\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{DIFF}})=\sum_{i\in U}\sum_{j\in U}(\pi_{ij}-\pi_{i}\pi_{j})\,\frac{y_{i}-m(\bm{x}_{i})}{\pi_{i}}\,\frac{y_{j}-m(\bm{x}_{j})}{\pi_{j}}.

(7)

The variance will be smaller than that of
\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{HT}}
to the extent that the residuals
y_{i}-m(\bm{x}_{i})
have lower variability than the raw data
y_{i}
. If the method has poor predictive power, the variance approaches that of the Horvitz–Thompson estimator.

The practical problem is that one rarely has an
m(\cdot)
that is independent of the sample and has good predictive power. Model-assisted estimation solves this by introducing a superpopulation model

y_{i}=m(\bm{x}_{i})+\epsilon_{i},\qquad\mathbb{E}(\epsilon_{i})=0,

(8)

which need not be true for
U
, but whose usefulness increases with its fit. The method
m(\cdot)
is estimated using the sample data,
\widehat{m}(\cdot)
(which depends on
s
), and substituted into (
6
), yielding the prediction estimator:

\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{PRED}}=\sum_{i\in U}\widehat{m}(\bm{x}_{i})+\sum_{i\in s}w_{i}\bigl(y_{i}-\widehat{m}(\bm{x}_{i})\bigr).

(9)

\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{PRED}}
is asymptotically unbiased,
\mathbb{E}(\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{PRED}})\to Y
. It has no exact variance form; a common estimator is based on the difference-estimator formula, replacing the residuals by
e_{i}=y_{i}-\widehat{m}(\bm{x}_{i})
:

\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{PRED}})=\sum_{i\in s}\sum_{j\in s}\pi_{ij}^{-1}(\pi_{ij}-\pi_{i}\pi_{j})\,\frac{e_{i}}{\pi_{i}}\,\frac{e_{j}}{\pi_{j}}.

(10)

The precision of the estimator therefore depends on the predictive power of the model. Among the assisting models that have been used are linear regression
(
3
)
, logistic regression
(
11
)
, nonparametric calibration
(
14
)
, regression trees
(
13
)
, and random forests
(
4
)
.

3.2
The generalized regression estimator and calibration

When the assisting model is linear,
y_{i}=\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\beta}+\epsilon_{i}
with
\epsilon_{i}\sim(0,\sigma_{i}^{2})
, the estimator (
9
) takes the form of the generalized regression estimator (GREG):

\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{GREG}}=\sum_{i\in U}\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{\bm{B}}+\sum_{i\in s}w_{i}\bigl(y_{i}-\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{\bm{B}}\bigr),

(11)

where

\widehat{\bm{B}}=\Bigl(\sum_{i\in s}w_{i}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}/\sigma_{i}\Bigr)^{-1}\Bigl(\sum_{i\in s}w_{i}\bm{x}_{i}y_{i}/\sigma_{i}\Bigr).

A key property for production is that it only requires knowing
\bm{x}_{i}
for
i\in s
and the totals
\bm{X}=\sum_{i\in U}\bm{x}_{i}
(not the micro-level information for the entire population), so that

\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{GREG}}=\bm{X}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{\bm{B}}+\sum_{i\in s}w_{i}(y_{i}-\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{\bm{B}})

.

The estimator is expressed as a weighted sum

\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{GREG}}=\sum_{i\in s}w_{i}^{*}\,y_{i},

(12)

where
w_{i}^{*}=g_{i}w_{i}
and

g_{i}=1+(\bm{X}-\widehat{\bm{X}}^{\mathrm{HT}})^{\mathsf{T}}\Bigl(\sum_{i\in s}w_{i}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}/\sigma_{i}\Bigr)^{-1}\bm{x}_{i}/\sigma_{i}

The weights
w_{i}^{*}

do not depend on
y
and therefore serve different variables of interest (the uni-weight property). Moreover, the estimator is calibrated to the auxiliary information,

\sum_{i\in s}w_{i}^{*}\,\bm{x}_{i}=\sum_{i\in U}\bm{x}_{i},

(13)

which provides coherence with other sources and improves the credibility of the figures for users.

The calibration approach
(
5
;
18
)
reaches the same estimator from a different angle: seeking weights
w_{i}^{*}=g_{i}w_{i}
that minimize a distance
L(w^{*},w)=\sum_{i\in s}L(w_{i}^{*},w_{i})
subject to (
13
). For the least-squares distance
L(w^{*},w)=\sum_{i\in s}(w_{i}^{*}-w_{i})^{2}/w_{i}
one recovers the GREG,
\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{CAL}}=\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{GREG}}
, the standard strategy in NSOs.

Limitations of the GREG.

The estimator may exhibit (i) high variability of the weights
w_{i}^{*}
, which unnecessarily inflates standard errors; (ii) negative weights
w_{i}^{*}
; and (iii) null precision gains relative to
\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{HT}}
if the model is inadequate. Furthermore,
\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{GREG}}
can behave poorly when the number of auxiliary variables exceeds what
n
can support, and post-stratification (a special case of the GREG with categorical
\bm{x}
) becomes fragile when there are many qualitative variables with many categories whose interactions define the cells. These difficulties motivate the use of assisting models that automatically select the relevant structure.

4
Regression-tree-assisted estimator

A regression tree
(
2
)
recursively partitions the prediction space into nodes or boxes
B_{1},\dots,B_{l},\dots,B_{L}
so that the units within each box are homogeneous with respect to
y
. Fitted to the sample data through a design-consistent algorithm
(
19
)
, it gives rise to a special case of
\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{PRED}}
in which
\widehat{m}
is the sample fit of the tree. The resulting estimator
(
13
)
can be read as a post-stratified estimator with automatically chosen cells:

\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{RT}}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\frac{N_{l}}{\widehat{N}_{l}}\sum_{i\in s_{l}}w_{i}\,y_{i}=\sum_{i\in s}w_{i}^{*}\,y_{i},

(14)

where
w_{i}^{*}=\frac{N_{l}}{\widehat{N}_{l}}\,w_{i}\ \ (i\in s_{l})
,
s_{l}=s\cap B_{l}
,
N_{l}
is the population count of node
l
, and
\widehat{N}_{l}=\sum_{i\in s_{l}}w_{i}
its Horvitz–Thompson estimator. The weights
w_{i}^{*}
estimate the node counts without error and satisfy
N=\sum_{i\in s}w_{i}^{*}
; in particular, they
do not depend on
y
, so the estimator is compatible with the uni-weight approach.

Compared with classical post-stratification, the tree performs automatic selection: instead of building all possible interactions among qualitative variables, it includes only the variables (and interactions) that explain the variability of
y
, reducing the dimension of the model. The estimator can also be viewed as a calibrated estimator whose calibration equation is determined automatically by the algorithm. A variance estimator is obtained from (
10
) by replacing the residuals by

e_{i}=y_{i}-\Bigl(\sum_{i\in s_{l}}w_{i}\Bigr)^{-1}\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{HT}}_{l},

(15)

that is, the residual with respect to the weighted mean of the node to which the unit belongs. The estimator is asymptotically unbiased; the size of the tree (number of boxes) governs its variance, as illustrated in Section
6
.

Proposition 1

(Design consistency of the tree-assisted estimator)
.

Consider a sequence of nested finite populations and sampling designs in the standard finite-population asymptotic framework
(
16
)
, and assume:

(A1)

(moments)
the study variable and the auxiliary vector have uniformly bounded fourth moments across the sequence;

(A2)

(design)
the inclusion probabilities satisfy
\lambda\leq N\pi_{i}\leq\Lambda
for constants
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda<\infty
, and the design admits a design-consistent Horvitz–Thompson variance estimator;

(A3)

(tree)
the recursive-partitioning algorithm yields a sample-fitted partition that is design consistent for a fixed limiting partition
\{B_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
in the sense of
19
, with
L
bounded and expected within-node sample sizes
\mathbb{E}(n_{l})\to\infty
.

Then
\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{RT}}
is design consistent,
N^{-1}(\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{RT}}-Y)=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
, and the variance estimator defined by (
10
)–(
15
) is design consistent for the asymptotic variance of
\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{RT}}
.

Proof sketch.

Conditionally on the partition,
\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{RT}}
in (
14
) is the post-stratified estimator on the cells
\{B_{l}\}
. By (A3) the sample-fitted partition converges to the limiting partition, so
\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{RT}}
differs from the post-stratified estimator on
\{B_{l}\}
by
o_{p}(n^{-1/2})

(
19
)
. On the limiting partition the assisting function
m(\bm{x})=\sum_{l}\mathbf{1}\{\bm{x}\in B_{l}\}\,\bar{y}_{B_{l}}
(the population node means) is fixed and non-random, so
\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{RT}}
is asymptotically equivalent to the difference estimator (
6
) with this
m
. The difference estimator is design unbiased with the variance given in Section
3
, and the plug-in residuals (
15
) yield a design-consistent variance estimator under (A1)–(A2). This coincides with the result of
13
, who establish design consistency and asymptotic normality of the regression-tree estimator building on the design-consistency of sample-fitted trees of
19
.
∎

: keeping

bounded (or letting it grow slowly with
n
) preserves
\mathbb{E}(n_{l})\to\infty

5
Domain estimation

5.1
Ground rules

Let
U_{1},\dots,U_{d},\dots,U_{D}
be disjoint domains with
U=\bigcup_{d=1}^{D}U_{d}
and
N=\sum_{d=1}^{D}N_{d}
. We assume
unplanned
domains (they are not design strata), so the domain sample size
n_{d}=|s\cap U_{d}|
is random. The goal is to estimate

Y_{d}=\sum_{i\in U_{d}}y_{i},

(16)

by means of
direct
estimators
\widehat{Y}_{d}=\sum_{i\in s_{d}}w_{i}y_{i}
, with
s_{d}=s\cap U_{d}
.

The extended variable
y_{d}
is useful, defined by
y_{di}=y_{i}
if
i\in U_{d}
and
y_{di}=0
otherwise, so that
Y_{d}=\sum_{i\in U}y_{di}
and the Horvitz–Thompson estimator for the domain is

\widehat{Y}_{d}^{\mathrm{HT}}=\sum_{i\in s_{d}}w_{i}y_{i}=\sum_{i\in s}w_{i}y_{di},

(17)

unbiased, with a variance estimator obtained by replacing
y
with
y_{d}
in (
3
).

5.2
Two prediction strategies for domains

Strategy 1: population-level model (RT
U
).

A single model is fitted with the whole sample
s
, and the domain estimator is built with the predictions
\widehat{m}(\bm{x}_{i})
,
i\in U_{d}
, plus an adjustment term that protects the estimator:

\widehat{Y}_{d}^{\mathrm{PRED},U}=\sum_{i\in U_{d}}\widehat{m}(\bm{x}_{i})+\sum_{i\in s_{d}}w_{i}\bigl(y_{i}-\widehat{m}(\bm{x}_{i})\bigr).

(18)

This estimator is
not direct
in the strict sense (it uses
\widehat{m}
fitted outside the domain), but it satisfies the additivity or
benchmarking
property with respect to the population total, creates economies of scale (a single algorithm), and when
\widehat{m}
comes from a GREG or a tree, gives rise to a single set of weights
w_{i}^{*}
applicable to any domain (uni-weight). Its weakness is that the variance reduction relative to (
17
) may be null if the domain has its own characteristics not captured by the global model.

Strategy 2: domain-level model (RT
D
).

A specific model
\widehat{m}_{d}(\cdot)
is fitted using only the data in
s_{d}
:

\widehat{Y}_{d}^{\mathrm{PRED},D}=\sum_{i\in U_{d}}\widehat{m}_{d}(\bm{x}_{i})+\sum_{i\in s_{d}}w_{i}\bigl(y_{i}-\widehat{m}_{d}(\bm{x}_{i})\bigr).

(19)

This estimator
is direct
, can be expressed as
\sum_{i\in s_{d}}w_{i}^{*}y_{i}
when the method allows it (GREG, trees), and requires that
\mathbb{E}(n_{d})
be large enough to estimate
\widehat{m}_{d}(\cdot)
stably. When the domain has its own characteristics, the variance reduction relative to (
18
) can be substantial.

5.3
Domain-level regression tree

A domain-specific regression tree
\widehat{m}_{d}(\cdot)
is defined, which partitions a reduced prediction space into nodes
B_{d1},\dots,B_{dl},\dots,B_{dL_{d}}
. The estimator of the domain total is

\widehat{Y}_{d}^{\mathrm{RT}}=\sum_{i\in U_{d}}\widehat{m}_{d}(\bm{x}_{i})+\sum_{i\in s_{d}}w_{i}\bigl(y_{i}-\widehat{m}_{d}(\bm{x}_{i})\bigr)=\sum_{i\in s_{d}}w_{i}^{*}\,y_{i}=\sum_{i\in s_{d}}\frac{N_{dl}}{\widehat{N}_{dl}}\,w_{i}\,y_{i},

(20)

with
w_{i}^{*}=(N_{dl}/\widehat{N}_{dl})w_{i}
that estimate the domain node counts without error and satisfy

\sum_{i\in s_{d}}w_{i}^{*}=N_{d}.

(21)

If the domains are disjoint, the collection
\{w_{i}^{*}\}_{i\in s}
obtained by pooling the weights of all domains constitutes a single weighting system: it can be used to estimate at the level of
U
,
\widehat{Y}=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\sum_{i\in s_{d}}w_{i}^{*}y_{i}
, for different parameters, and for different domains. The price is a
trade-off
: obtaining more precise estimates within each domain does not imply having the “optimal” estimator at the population level (for example, relative to
\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{RT}}
with a single global tree). In large-scale surveys this cost is usually minor.

5.4
Why the population-level model behaves like Horvitz–Thompson within domains

The contrasting behavior of the two strategies admits a transparent explanation in the linear case, which extends heuristically to the tree. If the model is linear and estimated at the level of
U
, the domain estimator can be written as

\widehat{Y}_{d}^{*}=\sum_{i\in s_{d}}w_{i}^{*}y_{i}=\widehat{Y}_{d}^{\mathrm{HT}}+(\bm{X}-\widehat{\bm{X}})^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{\bm{R}}_{U},

(22)

where

\bm{R}_{U}=\Bigl(\sum_{i\in s}w_{i}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\Bigr)^{-1}\Bigl(\sum_{i\in s}w_{i}\bm{x}_{i}\,y_{di}\Bigr),

with

w_{i}^{*}=w_{i}\bigl[1+(\bm{X}-\widehat{\bm{X}}^{\mathrm{HT}})^{\mathsf{T}}(\sum_{i\in s}w_{i}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}\bm{x}_{i}\bigr].

A variance estimator for
\widehat{Y}_{d}^{*}
is

\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\widehat{Y}_{d}^{*})=\sum_{i\in s}\sum_{j\in s}\pi_{ij}^{-1}(\pi_{ij}-\pi_{i}\pi_{j})\,\frac{e_{i}}{\pi_{i}}\,\frac{e_{j}}{\pi_{j}},

(23)

where, crucially, the errors are

e_{i}=\begin{cases}y_{i}-\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{R}_{U},\ i\in U_{d},\\[2.0pt]
-\,\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{R}_{U},\ i\notin U_{d}.\end{cases}

(24)

The units
outside
the domain contribute residuals
-\bm{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{R}_{U}
that do not vanish: the population fit was not designed to make the variability within
U_{d}
small. As a consequence, the residual sum of squares is not reduced relative to the Horvitz–Thompson estimator, and
\widehat{Y}_{d}^{*}
performs similarly to
\widehat{Y}_{d}^{\mathrm{HT}}
, which uses no auxiliary information within the domains of interest. As
6
already noted, the level (population, domain, or intermediate) at which the auxiliary information is incorporated is a critical factor for the variance of the uni-weight estimator.

The argument carries over to the tree: a tree fitted at the level of
U
defines nodes that are homogeneous with respect to
y

in the population
, but those nodes need not be homogeneous
within
a domain that has its own structure. By contrast, the domain-specific tree seeks nodes that are homogeneous in
U_{d}
, which is the source of RT
D
’s precision gains.

Proposition 2

(The level at which the model is fitted)
.

Under the conditions of Proposition
1
applied to the extended variable
y_{d}
, and writing
\mathrm{AV}(\cdot)
for the asymptotic design variance:

(a)

(population-level fit, RT
U
)
if the assisting model is fitted on all of
s
, then, because
y_{di}=0
for every
i\notin U_{d}
, the fitted relationship for
y_{d}
is attenuated and

\mathrm{AV}\!\bigl(\widehat{Y}_{d}^{\mathrm{PRED},U}\bigr)=\mathrm{AV}\!\bigl(\widehat{Y}_{d}^{\mathrm{HT}}\bigr)\,(1+o(1)),

i.e. there is asymptotically no efficiency gain over Horvitz–Thompson for the domain total;

(b)

(domain-level fit, RT
D
)
if the assisting model is fitted within the domain using
s_{d}
, with
\mathbb{E}(n_{d})\to\infty
and within-domain predictive power
R_{d}^{2}\in(0,1]
, then

\mathrm{AV}\!\bigl(\widehat{Y}_{d}^{\mathrm{PRED},D}\bigr)=\bigl(1-R_{d}^{2}\bigr)\,\mathrm{AV}\!\bigl(\widehat{Y}_{d}^{\mathrm{HT}}\bigr)\,(1+o(1))\leq\mathrm{AV}\!\bigl(\widehat{Y}_{d}^{\mathrm{HT}}\bigr)(1+o(1)),

with the reduction governed by
R_{d}^{2}
.

Proof sketch.

Both estimators target
Y_{d}=\sum_{i\in U}y_{di}
, and their asymptotic variances are governed by the population residuals
e_{i}
of the assisting model for
y_{d}
, as in (
24
).
(a) When the model is fitted on all of
U
, the regression response
y_{d}
is identically zero on
U\setminus U_{d}
, a fraction
1-N_{d}/N
of the population; the fit (least squares or tree) is pulled toward the null model, the coefficient
\bm{R}_{U}
is attenuated, and the residual variation
\sum_{i\in U}e_{i}^{2}/\pi_{i}
equals
\sum_{i\in U}y_{di}^{2}/\pi_{i}
up to a term of smaller order. Since the latter is the leading term of
\mathrm{AV}(\widehat{Y}_{d}^{\mathrm{HT}})
, the variance ratio tends to
1
.
(b) When the model is fitted within
U_{d}
, the assisting function captures the within-domain relationship between
y
and
\bm{x}
; the domain residuals satisfy
\sum_{i\in U_{d}}(y_{i}-\widehat{m}_{d}(\bm{x}_{i}))^{2}/\pi_{i}=(1-R_{d}^{2})\sum_{i\in U_{d}}y_{i}^{2}/\pi_{i}+o(\cdot)
, while by construction the extended residuals vanish on
U\setminus U_{d}
. Hence the leading variance term shrinks by the factor
1-R_{d}^{2}
.
∎

Remark 2
.

For regression trees the same dichotomy holds with
R_{d}^{2}
replaced by the proportion of within-domain variance of
y
explained by the partition. A globally fitted tree assigns to each unit the
population
node mean; restricted to
U_{d}
these means are generally biased for the domain node means, so the within-domain residual variance is not reduced the mechanism behind the
\texttt{deff}\approx 1
of RT
U

Simulation study

6.1
Experimental design

We use public microdata from the Uruguayan Continuous Household Survey (ECH). The population
U
consists of working-age individuals. Two dichotomous variables of interest are considered:
y^{(1)}=1
if the person is employed (and
0
otherwise) and
y^{(2)}=1
if the person is unemployed (and
0
otherwise). The target parameters are the totals of employed and unemployed persons,
Y^{(1)}=\sum_{i\in U}y^{(1)}_{i}
and
Y^{(2)}=\sum_{i\in U}y^{(2)}_{i}
.

The auxiliary variables
\bm{x}
, available at the micro level and a priori good predictors, are: single-year age, sex (2 categories), highest educational level attained (5 categories), and race (3 categories).

R=1000

n=1000

6.3
Domain results

A similar scenario is replicated with minor changes: the goal is to estimate the total of employed persons by department,
Y_{d}=\sum_{i\in U_{d}}y_{i}
, and the country total
Y=\sum_{d=1}^{D}Y_{d}
. Samples of size
n=10000
are drawn under simple random sampling; the domains are unplanned, with
\mathbb{E}(n_{d})=(N_{d}/N)\,n
large enough that the direct estimators do not begin to fail. For each replicate, three estimators are computed:

•

HT
:
\widehat{Y}_{d}^{\mathrm{HT}}=(N/n)\sum_{i\in s_{d}}y_{i}
, and
\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{HT}}=(N/n)\sum_{i\in s}y_{i}
.

•

RT
U
: tree estimated at the
U
level, with uni-weight weights;
\widehat{Y}_{d}^{\mathrm{RT}*}=\sum_{i\in s_{d}}w_{i}^{*}y_{i}
.

•

RT
D
: domain-specific tree;
\widehat{Y}_{d}^{\mathrm{RT},D}=\sum_{i\in s_{d}}w_{i}^{*}y_{i}
, and
\widehat{Y}=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\sum_{i\in s_{d}}w_{i}^{*}y_{i}
.

The results are as expected from Section
5.4
: the RT

deff

1

D

7
Discussion and conclusions

The estimator assisted by a domain-specific regression tree (RT
D
) offers, within the design-based direct framework and the uni-weight approach, an attractive route to improving the precision of estimates in unplanned domains. It is asymptotically unbiased, admits a representation as a weighted sum with weights independent of
y
that estimate the domain count without error, and, by construction at the level of each disjoint domain, gives rise to a single weighting system applicable to the population and to different parameters. The experiment with the ECH confirms that, whereas the global tree (RT
U
) brings no gains relative to Horvitz–Thompson within domains, the domain-specific tree (RT
D
) markedly reduces the variance, in line with the residual mechanism of Section
5.4
and with the classical result of
6
;
7
.

The approach has limits worth making explicit. First, the improvement is concentrated in the variable used to build the model: for a
non-modeled
variable of interest, the uni-weight estimator based on a tree fitted for another variable does not necessarily improve on Horvitz–Thompson, as Table
1
shows. Second, growing the tree increases the Kish effect and may deteriorate the variance. Third, the estimator requires
\mathbb{E}(n_{d})
to be large enough; in very small domains, direct estimators lose stability and indirect model-based estimation becomes competitive again
(
17
;
15
)
. Finally, the local gain is obtained at the cost of not having the optimal estimator at the population level, although in large-scale surveys this cost is usually minor.

Several extensions follow naturally. The domain-specific construction carries over to random forests
(
4
)
, which may capture within-domain structure more flexibly at the cost of greater weight variability. Between the two extremes studied here lies a family of intermediate strategies in which the tree is fitted at a level of aggregation above the domain but below the population, for instance, at the level of geographic regions grouping several departments in line with the “intermediate level” already identified by
6
as a determinant of the variance of the uni-weight estimator. Finally, the choice among RT
U
, RT
D
, and indirect model-based estimation can be cast as a decision rule indexed by the expected domain sample size
\mathbb{E}(n_{d})
: RT
D
where
\mathbb{E}(n_{d})
is large enough for stable within-domain fitting, indirect SAE in the small-
\mathbb{E}(n_{d})
regime
(
15
)
, and RT
U
(or a global model) where benchmarking to the population total is the binding requirement.
