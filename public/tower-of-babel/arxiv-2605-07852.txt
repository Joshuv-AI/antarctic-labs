CHASM: Online Changepoint Detection in Temporal and Cross-Variable Dependence
Victor K. Khamesi, Edward A. K. Cohen, Niall M. Adams, Dean A. Bodenham

Abstract
Changepoint detection identifies times when the generative process of a time series changes, with applications in healthcare, cybersecurity, and finance. In multivariate settings, changes in cross-variable and temporal dependence are particularly challenging to detect, as they are often less pronounced than shifts in marginal statistics such as the mean or variance. Existing methods detect changes using reconstruction error, which provides only an indirect measure of dynamical change, or rely on scalar functionals that may be too coarse to capture global structure. We introduce CHASM, an online nonparametric method that monitors the truncated eigenvalue sequence of the recursively estimated dynamic mode decomposition operator. Designing such an approach raises two challenges: the permutation invariance of eigendecompositions, resolved via optimal linear assignment, and the lack of online changepoint methods for multivariate complex-valued time series, addressed through a novel augmented monitoring scheme. We study the theoretical properties of the dynamics estimator under the canonical vector autoregressive model, which directly motivates our algorithmic design. The proposed method achieves competitive or superior performance to modern competitors across synthetic and real-world data sets, including challenging settings in video and text data. It is unsupervised, depends on a small number of interpretable parameters, and requires no distributional assumptions beyond finite moments, making it readily deployable across scientific domains.



1
Introduction

Modern data streams arise across domains as diverse as finance
[
10
]
, climate monitoring
[
60
]
, neuroscience
[
62
]
, natural language processing
[
67
]
, and video understanding
[
71
]
. In each of these settings, the data-generating process is rarely stationary: market regimes shift, environmental conditions evolve, neural activity varies with cognitive state, text reflects changing topics, and video transitions between scenes. Detecting such changes promptly is critical for timely decision-making, such as rebalancing portfolios
[
4
]
or segmenting recordings
[
49
]
. Changepoint detection seeks to identify times at which the underlying generative model of a time series changes.

Two settings are commonly considered: offline approaches retrospectively locate changepoints given the full sequence, while online methods process observations sequentially and detect changes as quickly as possible after they occur. This paper focuses on the online regime, where algorithms must process each observation in constant time with memory independent of the sequence length. These computational constraints make online changepoint detection fundamentally more challenging.

In many applications, observations are multivariate and the structure of interest lies in the joint evolution of variables over time. The vector autoregressive (VAR) process is a standard model for such data, used across econometrics
[
61
]
, neuroscience
[
26
]
, biology
[
48
]
, and climate science
[
30
]
. The parameter of interest in a VAR process is the transition matrix, which encodes both temporal and cross-variable dependence. Changes in this matrix can be diverse or subtle, and need not necessarily produce clear shifts in the marginal distribution of individual variables.

In practice, the noise distribution is typically unknown and varies across applications, from heavy-tailed in finance
[
52
]
to Poisson in imaging
[
21
]
and correlated Gaussian in physical systems
[
40
]
. This motivates nonparametric, unsupervised methods that make minimal distributional assumptions and generalise across domains without relying on labelled data, which are rarely available.

1.1
Related work

Online changepoint detection has received considerable attention since the cumulative sum (CUSUM) method
[
51
]
. Extensions cover multivariate and high-dimensional settings via nonparametric methods
[
47
]
, Gaussian models
[
17
,
18
]
, and probabilistic approaches
[
1
,
36
]
. While these provide strong baselines, they target distributional rather than dynamical changes. More recently, a related method
[
2
]
constructs a subspace-based CUSUM statistic to detect changes in dynamics.

Changepoint detection in VAR processes focuses on the transition matrix. Offline methods locate changepoints via low-rank and sparse decompositions
[
5
,
6
]
or likelihood-ratio statistics over candidate segments
[
19
]
. In the online setting, Tian & Safikhani
[
65
]
estimate the transition matrix via
\ell_{1}
-penalisation
[
9
]
and construct a detection statistic from one-step-ahead reconstruction errors. While the method comes with theoretical guarantees and shows strong empirical performance, it assumes sub-Gaussian noise with diagonal covariance and detects changes indirectly, potentially making it difficult to distinguish dynamical from noise changes.

Dynamic mode decomposition (DMD) has also been applied to changepoint detection. In
[
33
]
, an online kernel DMD method is proposed with a CUSUM statistic based on prediction error, while in
[
64
]
an offline autoencoder-based variant is developed; in both works, changepoint detection is a secondary application rather than the primary contribution. Recently, in
[
34
]
, DMD is applied on time-delay embedded data and detects changes via reconstruction error in seasonal time series. Our work addresses the more general setting of arbitrary dynamical structure, and monitors the operator spectrum directly rather than through reconstruction error.

1.2
Contributions

We introduce the changepoint detection method CHASM. Our main contributions are as follows:

1.

We propose monitoring the dominant eigenvalues of a recursively updated dynamics operator, applying rank truncation as a post-estimation step. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first online changepoint method to directly track the operator spectrum. This naturally raises two challenges of independent interest which we address below.

2.

We introduce a method to first align consecutive vectors of complex eigenvalues via a transport argument and second to detect changes in this multivariate complex-valued sequence.

3.

We establish the consistency of the DMD estimator and characterise its bias under the canonical VAR model with no parametric noise assumptions. Consequently, we reveal a trade-off between estimation accuracy and detection sensitivity.

4.

We evaluate CHASM on synthetic time series covering diverse noise distributions, change magnitudes, and dimensions, and on real-world data sets spanning motion, image, and text modalities, showing competitive to superior performance against modern competitors. While our theoretical analysis is focused on VAR processes, it is shown that in practice CHASM has good detection performance beyond this framework.

2
Background

We study changepoint detection in temporal and cross-variable dependence, and present a general method for detecting such changes in Section
3
. For analytical tractability, we formulate the detection problem using VAR processes. The VAR framework is highly flexible; Wold’s decomposition theorem (see, e.g. Section 4.8 of
[
27
]
) shows that any stationary process can be approximated arbitrarily well by a VAR model. This framework enables our theoretical analysis in Section
4
, while our experiments demonstrate strong performance beyond settings typically modelled by VAR in Section
5
.

2.1
Vector autoregressive processes

A
d
-dimensional first-order vector autoregressive process,
\mathrm{VAR}_{d}(1)
, follows the recursion

x_{t}=\Theta\,x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t},\qquad t\in\mathbb{N},

(1)

where
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the unknown transition matrix and
\{\varepsilon_{t}\}
is a white noise process with
\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{t}\right]=0
and
\mathrm{Cov}\!\left(\varepsilon_{t},\varepsilon_{s}\right)=\mathbbm{1}_{t=s}\,\Sigma
, for some positive semi-definite matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. The process
\{x_{t}\}
is weakly stationary if and only if
\zeta(\Theta)<1
. Moreover, any
\mathrm{VAR}_{d}(p)
process of order
p>1
admits a standard companion-form representation as in Eq. (
1
). Therefore, we restrict attention to
\mathrm{VAR}_{d}(1)
throughout, without loss of generality, following
[
8
]
; see Appendix
A.1
for details.

Problem definition.

We study online changepoint detection in the transition dynamics of Eq. (
1
). Given a stream
x_{1},x_{2},\dots
, where
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we test
\mathcal{H}_{0}
against the single change alternative
\mathcal{H}_{1}

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}

\displaystyle:x_{t}=\Theta_{0}\,x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t}\text{ for all }t\in\mathbb{N},

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}

\displaystyle:\exists\,\tau\in\mathbb{N}\text{ such that }x_{t}=\begin{cases}\Theta_{0}\,x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t},&t\leq\tau\\
\Theta_{1}\,x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t},&t>\tau\end{cases},

with
\Theta_{0}\neq\Theta_{1}
unknown. Changepoint detection is concerned with estimating
\tau
as closely as possible. Evaluation metrics are defined in Section
5.1
. Although Eq. (
2
) formalises a single changepoint, our method extends to multiple changepoints by restarting after each detection; see Section
5.3
.

Crucially, a change in
\Theta
need not manifest in the marginal distribution of
x_{t}
. Two
\mathrm{VAR}_{d}(1)
processes with diagonal transition
\Theta_{0}=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\theta&0\\
0&\theta\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
and rotation transition
\Theta_{1}=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-\theta\\
\theta&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
share identical marginal covariances
(1-\theta^{2})^{-1}I_{2}
for any
\left\lvert\theta\right\rvert<1
, yet differ fundamentally in their dynamics. This motivates the development of statistics that directly track the operator rather than marginal properties.

Spectrum as a surrogate statistic.

Testing matrix equality is fundamentally different from vector comparisons since the rows and columns are structured via properties such as rank and eigenstructure, so treating a matrix as an unordered vector ignores those dependencies. This motivates the use of surrogate statistics. Reconstruction error
[
34
,
65
]
introduces an additional layer of indirection between the parameter of interest and the detection statistic. Scalar functionals
[
16
,
35
,
42
]
collapse the operator to a single number, potentially discarding directional and oscillatory structure. We propose to monitor the dominant eigenvalues of
\Theta
as an interpretable and direct operator-level summary that captures stability and oscillatory behaviour while reducing monitoring dimension; this trade-off constitutes a central methodological idea of this work.

2.2
Dynamic mode decomposition

Dynamic mode decomposition (DMD) is a data-driven method for modelling dynamical systems
[
57
]
that extracts a collection of modes each associated with a growth rate and an oscillation frequency.

Modes and dynamics.

Given observations
x_{1},\dots,x_{T}
, DMD seeks an operator
A
such that
Y=AX
, where
X=\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{T-1}\right)
and
Y=\left(x_{2},\dots,x_{T}\right)
. In the original formulation of
[
57
]
, developed for high-dimensional fluid systems, the matrix
A
is assumed to be low-rank. This motivates an approximation via the rank-truncated singular value decomposition (SVD) of
X=U_{r}S_{r}V_{r}^{\mathsf{H}}
, yielding the operator
A_{r}=U_{r}^{\mathsf{H}}YV_{r}S_{r}^{-1}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
, whose eigendecomposition defines the modes and dynamics; a full derivation of the modal decomposition is provided in Appendix
A.2
.

Online DMD.

The operator of
[
57
]
cannot be updated exactly without storing all past observations and recomputing the SVD at each time step on the full sequence. To address this,
[
68
]
drop the low-rank assumption and maintain sufficient statistics to update the least-squares estimate recursively. The resulting online operator is mathematically equivalent to the batch solution of
[
57
]
in the absence of rank truncation. In this work, we propose to combine the recursive estimation of
[
68
]
with the truncation principle of
[
57
]
, applying regularisation as a post-estimation step to the online operator rather than during batch decomposition. This decouples regularisation from estimation, yielding a compact spectral representation updated in a single sequential pass.

3
CHASM: Complex Hungarian-Aligned Spectrum Monitoring

3.1
Online dynamics learning

Consider a stream
x_{1},x_{2},\dots
, where
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
. We estimate a sequence of linear operators
\Theta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
using recursive least squares, corresponding to online DMD; see Section
2.2
and
[
68
]
. For a fixed forgetting factor
\rho\in(0,1]
, the update equations for
\Theta_{n}
are

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{1}{n-1}x_{n-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma_{n-1}^{-1}x_{n-1}\right)^{-1}\in\mathbb{R},

(3)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{\rho(n-1)}\left(\Gamma_{n-1}^{-1}-\frac{\gamma_{n}}{n-1}\Gamma_{n-1}^{-1}x_{n-1}x_{n-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma_{n-1}^{-1}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},

\displaystyle\Theta_{n}

\displaystyle=\Theta_{n-1}+\frac{\gamma_{n}}{n-1}\left(x_{n}-\Theta_{n-1}x_{n-1}\right)x_{n-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma_{n-1}^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.

Smaller values of
\rho
emphasise recent observations for faster adaptation, while values closer to one yield more stable estimates and retain longer-term information. The recursion of Eq. (
3
) is initialised by setting
\Theta_{0}
arbitrarily and
\Gamma_{0}^{-1}=\epsilon I_{d}
for small
\epsilon>0
, or warm-started from a batch of at least
d
observations. Higher-order dynamics are handled by concatenating the last
p\in\mathbb{N}
samples into an augmented state vector, following Lemma
4
. We restrict to order
p=1
throughout, which provides sufficiently strong empirical performance across all settings considered.

Post-hoc regularisation.

At each step, we compute a truncated eigendecomposition of
\Theta_{n}
, retaining the
r\leq d
dominant eigenvalues ordered by decreasing magnitude,
\lambda_{n}=(\lambda_{n}^{(1)},\dots,\lambda_{n}^{(r)})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
. The truncation level
r
acts as a regularisation parameter controlling the complexity of the spectral representation; unlike
[
65
]
, which regularise during estimation via
\ell_{1}
-penalisation without DMD, we leave estimation unconstrained and regularise post-hoc via spectrum truncation.

The stream
\{x_{t}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is thus transformed into spectral summaries
\{\lambda_{t}\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{r}
, on which changepoint detection is performed. Two challenges arise: the eigendecomposition returns eigenvalues in no canonical order, so in practice numerical estimates may be inconsistently ordered across time steps producing artificially discontinuous trajectories; and changepoint detection for multivariate complex-valued sequences remains largely underexplored. Sections
3.2
and
3.3
address these challenges.

3.2
Optimal eigenvalue alignment

Since numerical routines do not guarantee consistent ordering of eigenvalues across time, the resulting trajectories may be artificially discontinuous. This is particularly pronounced for complex conjugate pairs, where estimation noise, especially early in the recursion, can induce arbitrary switching. We formalise the alignment problem as optimal transport between consecutive spectra.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\nu_{n}
be the uniform empirical measure over
\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
, and define
\nu_{n-1}
analogously. The squared
2
-Wasserstein distance between
\nu_{n-1}
and
\nu_{n}
satisfies

W_{2}^{2}(\nu_{n-1},\nu_{n})=\min_{\pi\in S_{r}}\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left\lvert\lambda_{n-1}^{(i)}-\lambda_{n}^{\pi(i)}\right\rvert^{2},\qquad\nu_{n}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\delta_{\lambda_{n}^{(i)}},

where
\delta
denotes the Dirac measure and
S_{r}
the set of permutations of
\{1,\dots,r\}
.

Lemma
1
shows that the optimal coupling is a permutation map. Therefore, alignment reduces to a search over
S_{r}
. However, a brute force approach is expensive at
r!
evaluations, even for moderate
r
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\Lambda_{n}=\diag(\lambda_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
,
\mathcal{S}_{r}\subset\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
the set of permutation matrices in Eq. (
10
), and
\mathcal{B}_{r}\subset\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
the set of doubly stochastic matrices in Eq. (
9
). Then

\Pi_{n}=\argmin_{\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{r}}\left\lVert\Pi^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda_{n}\Pi-\Lambda_{n-1}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\argmax_{\Pi\in\mathcal{B}_{r}}\Tr{\!\left(\Pi\Rpart{\!\left(\lambda_{n}^{\ast}\lambda_{n-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)}\right)}.

(4)

Proposition
1
reformulates alignment as a linear assignment task, solvable in polynomial time by relaxing
\mathcal{S}_{r}
to its convex hull
\mathcal{B}_{r}
. The earliest method to solve the assignment problem in polynomial time is the Kuhn-Munkres or Hungarian algorithm
[
50
]
, which inspires the name of our approach and is
\mathcal{O}(r^{4})
. In our implementation, we use
[
31
]
which is
\mathcal{O}(r^{3})
. This result can independently be viewed as an extension of the orthogonal Procrustes problem to permutations over complex-valued points. Proofs of Lemma
1
and Proposition
1
are provided in Appendix
B.2
.

3.3
Complex multivariate changepoint detection

Following Sections
3.1
and
3.2
, the sequence of eigenvalue vectors
\{\lambda_{t}\}
is aligned and suitable for changepoint detection. We propose to extend the multivariate exponentially weighted moving average (MEWMA) scheme of
[
45
]
to the complex domain.

Rather than monitoring
\{\lambda_{t}\}
directly, we track the first-order differences
v_{n}=\lambda_{n}-\lambda_{n-1}\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
, which can be interpreted as the instantaneous spectral velocity. Under stationary dynamics,
v_{n}
fluctuates around zero with small magnitude and no preferred direction; when a change occurs, it induces a coherent directional shift detectable as a sustained deviation from zero. This behaviour is illustrated in Figure
2
.

The second-order statistics of the eigenvalue velocity
v_{n}
require both its Hermitian covariance
\Sigma_{v_{n}}=\mathbb{E}\left[(v_{n}-\mu_{v_{n}})(v_{n}-\mu_{v_{n}})^{\mathsf{H}}\right]
and pseudo-covariance
\tilde{\Sigma}_{v_{n}}=\mathbb{E}\left[(v_{n}-\mu_{v_{n}})(v_{n}-\mu_{v_{n}})^{\mathsf{T}}\right]
, where
\mu_{v_{n}}=\mathbb{E}\left[v_{n}\right]\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
and
\Sigma_{v_{n}},\tilde{\Sigma}_{v_{n}}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
are all estimated sequentially from the stream. We form the augmented vector
\underline{v}_{n}=(v_{n},v_{n}^{\ast})\in\mathbb{C}^{2r}
and apply the recursion

\underline{z}_{n}=(1-\alpha)\,\underline{z}_{n-1}+\alpha\,\underline{v}_{n},\qquad\alpha\in(0,1),

(5)

with augmented mean
\mu_{\underline{z}_{n}}=(\mu_{v_{n}},\mu_{v_{n}}^{\ast})\in\mathbb{C}^{2r}
and covariance

\Sigma_{\underline{z}_{n}}=\beta_{n}\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{v_{n}}&\tilde{\Sigma}_{v_{n}}\\
\tilde{\Sigma}_{v_{n}}^{\ast}&\Sigma_{v_{n}}^{\ast}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2r\times 2r},\qquad\beta_{n}=\frac{\alpha(1-(1-\alpha)^{2n})}{2-\alpha}\to\frac{\alpha}{2-\alpha}\text{ as }n\to\infty.

The changepoint statistic is defined as the squared Mahalanobis distance

\mathcal{D}_{n}^{2}=(\underline{z}_{n}-\mu_{\underline{z}_{n}})^{\mathsf{H}}\Sigma_{\underline{z}_{n}}^{-1}(\underline{z}_{n}-\mu_{\underline{z}_{n}}),

(6)

with stopping rule
\hat{\tau}=\inf_{n}\{\mathcal{D}_{n}^{2}>h\}
for some threshold
h>0
. Since
\Sigma_{\underline{z}_{n}}
is positive semi-definite,
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{2}
is guaranteed to be non-negative real despite the complex-valued inputs.

Lemma 2
.

Let
z_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
such that
z_{n}=(1-\alpha)\,z_{n-1}+\alpha\,v_{n}
, similarly to Eq. (
5
). Then,

\mathcal{D}_{n}^{2}=\frac{2}{\beta_{n}}\Rpart{\left((z_{n}-\mu_{v_{n}})^{\mathsf{H}}\mathcal{C}_{n}^{-1}(z_{n}-\mu_{v_{n}})+(z_{n}-\mu_{v_{n}})^{\mathsf{H}}\mathcal{Q}_{n}(z_{n}-\mu_{v_{n}})^{\ast}\right)},

where
\mathcal{C}_{n}=\Sigma_{v_{n}}-\tilde{\Sigma}_{v_{n}}(\Sigma_{v_{n}}^{\ast})^{-1}\tilde{\Sigma}_{v_{n}}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{n}=-\mathcal{C}_{n}^{-1}\tilde{\Sigma}_{v_{n}}(\Sigma_{v_{n}}^{\ast})^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
.

Lemma
2
, proved in Appendix
B.3
, reduces the
2r
-dimensional inversion to two
r
-dimensional operations, slightly reducing the computational cost. When
v_{n}
is proper complex, i.e.
\tilde{\Sigma}_{v_{n}}=0
,
\mathcal{Q}_{n}=0
and
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{2}
reduces to the standard Mahalanobis distance on
z_{n}
, recovering MEWMA
[
45
]
as a special case. The eigenvalue velocities are generically non-circular
[
59
]
since complex conjugate pairs impose linear constraints between
v_{n}
and
v_{n}^{\ast}
, making augmentation necessary in general.

Theoretical analysis

In this section, we consider a stationary
\mathrm{VAR}_{d}(1)

x_{t}=\Theta\,x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t}

\zeta(\Theta)<1
,
\{\varepsilon_{t}\}
is a martingale difference sequence with respect to the natural filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(x_{0},\dots,x_{t})

\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]=0
almost surely and
\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{t}\varepsilon_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\right]=\Sigma\succ 0
. The stationary covariance
\Gamma=\mathbb{E}\left[x_{t}x_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\right]
satisfies
\Gamma\succ 0

\Gamma=\Theta\Gamma\Theta^{\mathsf{T}}+\Sigma

B.4
.

4.1
Unweighted estimator

We study the online unweighted DMD operator
\Theta_{n}
, obtained from Eq. (
3

\rho=1
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
\varepsilon_{t}\perp\varepsilon_{s}
for
t\neq s
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\varepsilon_{t}\right\rVert^{8}\right]<\infty
. Then
\Theta_{n}
is consistent, i.e.
\Theta_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\Theta
, and

\mathbb{E}\left[\Theta_{n}\right]=\Theta+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.

The proof is provided in Appendix
B.4.1
. The eighth-moment condition controls higher-order terms and is standard in dependent-data asymptotics
[
7
,
70
]
; consistency of
\Theta_{n}
requires only finite fourth moments. This condition is nonparametric, strictly weaker than sub-Gaussian noise, and imposes no restriction on the covariance structure, in contrast to
[
65
]
. No structural assumption is imposed on
\Theta
beyond stationarity, which accommodates full-rank, low-rank, oscillatory, and sparse dynamics.

Theorem
1
shows that, under stationarity of the data-generating process, the estimator consistently learns the true in-control dynamics, with bias decaying at rate
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
. This guarantees that the estimated matrix provides a reliable baseline representation of the in-control process.

4.2
Weighted estimator

We now consider the online weighted DMD operator
\Theta_{n}
, given by Eq. (
3
) with a fixed forgetting factor
\rho\in(0,1)
. We define the effective sample size
n_{\mathrm{eff}}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\rho^{n-t}=\frac{1-\rho^{n}}{1-\rho}\to(1-\rho)^{-1}
as
n\to\infty
, which quantifies the contribution of recent observations relative to the distant past.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
\varepsilon_{t}\perp\varepsilon_{s}
for
t\neq s
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\varepsilon_{t}\right\rVert^{8}\right]<\infty
. Then for any
\rho\in(0,1)
, there exist
c,c_{e}>0
depending only on
\Theta
,
\Sigma
,
d
, such that, for the event
\mathcal{E}_{n}=\{\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma_{n})\geq\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma)/2\}
,

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Theta_{n}-\Theta\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}}\right]\right\rVert\leq c\,n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1},\qquad\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{n}\right)\geq 1-c_{e}\,n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1},

and consequently
\mathbb{E}\left[\Theta_{n}\right]=\Theta+\mathcal{O}(n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1})
as
n\to\infty
.

Theorem
2
mirrors Theorem
1
with the sample size
n
replaced by the effective sample size
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
. As
\rho\to 1^{-}
, one recovers
n_{\mathrm{eff}}\sim n
, and the unweighted regime is obtained as a limiting case. In contrast to the unweighted estimator,
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
remains bounded for fixed
\rho<1
, and the bias bound does not vanish but stabilises at order
n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}=(1-\rho)
. These results are illustrated in Figure
2
.

Effective sample size and detection.

Without forgetting, both bias and variance vanish asymptotically, yielding a concentrated estimator that may become insensitive to changepoints. With forgetting, the estimator can retain a non-degenerate variability driven by recent observations, thereby maintaining sensitivity to changes. The forgetting factor
\rho
controls this trade-off: larger values favour stability and accurate estimation of the in-control regime, while smaller values increase responsiveness to changes at the cost of higher variability. The effective sample size
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
can be interpreted as the number of observations in the estimate. Since the objective of this work is changepoint detection rather than optimal estimation of
\Theta
, maintaining a non-vanishing error provides a principled mechanism to balance stability and adaptivity. This trade-off justifies the role of
\rho
in Section
3
and is confirmed empirically by the
\mathtt{ARL}_{0}
results of Section
5
, where smaller
\rho
produces shorter in-control run lengths.

5.3
Real-world experiments

Data sets.

We evaluate on five real-world data sets across four modalities. HASC provides accelerometry signals; CIFAR-100 and 20 Newsgroups form piecewise i.i.d. sequences of CLIP embeddings
[
53
]
for images and text; UCF-Crime represents videos as sequences of per-frame CLIP embeddings; and WikiSection embeds articles at the token level using Qwen3-Embedding
[
69
]
, inducing sequential structure via the autoregressive language model. All data (except HASC) are converted to vector sequences via pretrained models in a fully online, real-time manner, without fine-tuning. As all samples contain annotated changes,
\mathtt{ARL}_{0}/\mathtt{ARL}_{1}
are not meaningful, and we report detection accuracy only. Full details are provided in Appendix
C.4
.

Scope and novelty.

These data sets extend evaluation beyond VAR, covering i.i.d. embedding shifts (CIFAR-100, 20 Newsgroups) and nonlinear temporal dynamics (UCF-Crime, WikiSection, HASC), up to
d=64
. None are naturally VAR-modelled, making this a novel and challenging setting for changepoint detection. All methods are applied fully unsupervised, without task-specific training.

reports

-scores on real data; see Appendix

for results per data set. CHASM is the only method consistently strong across modalities. It reaches clear superior performance on CIFAR-100 and 20 Newsgroups, showing generalisation beyond VAR. On UCF-Crime and WikiSection, with nonlinear temporal dependencies, CHASM again yields superior performance, indicating robustness beyond its assumptions. WikiSection is hardest, with all methods performing relatively poorly. HASC is the only exception, where CPDMD leads, likely due to periodic signals with clear changes in marginal distribution favouring reconstruction-based statistics. No competing method achieves strong performance across all modalities, whereas CHASM remains consistent throughout. Figures

reacts to changes in a synthetic and a real-world time series.

6
Conclusion

We introduce CHASM, an online method for detecting changes in the transition dynamics of multivariate time series. By monitoring the eigenvalue sequence of a dynamics operator, it yields a direct and computationally efficient operator-level detection statistic. The method requires no parametric noise assumptions or structural constraints on the dynamics. Experiments confirm robustness across diverse noise and regimes, with superior performance on real-world tasks beyond VAR settings.

Limitations.

While efficient, CHASM uses repeated eigendecompositions; first-order perturbation approximations may reduce cost without degrading performance, pending empirical validation. The optimal choice of
\rho
varies by setting, so an automatic tuning step would be a natural improvement.
Beyond dynamics operators, the framework of Sections
3.2
and
3.3
naturally extends to general matrix sequences, with applications to covariance or adjacency matrices as a promising direction.

Definition 1

A

, denoted
\mathrm{VAR}_{d}(p)

, is a discrete-time stochastic process
\{y_{t}\}
, where
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, following the recursion

y_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\Theta_{k}\,y_{t-k}+\varepsilon_{t}\qquad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{N},

(7)

where
\Theta_{1},\dots,\Theta_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are fixed. The sequence
\{\varepsilon_{t}\}
is a
d

t,s\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{t}\right]=0
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{t}\varepsilon_{s}^{\mathsf{T}}\right]=\mathbbm{1}_{t=s}\,\Sigma
, with
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
symmetric positive semi-definite.

Definition 2
.

A stochastic process
\{y_{t}\}
is weakly stationary if, for all
t,s\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{E}\left[y_{t}\right]
is finite and constant, and
\mathrm{Cov}\!\left(y_{t},y_{s}\right)
is finite and depends only on the lag
(t-s)
.

In the context of VAR processes, the general stationarity conditions of Definition
2
translates into a simple algebraic identity on the transition matrices
\Theta_{1},\dots,\Theta_{p}
.

Lemma 3
.

A
\mathrm{VAR}_{d}(p)
process with transition matrices
\Theta_{1},\dots,\Theta_{p}
is weakly stationary if and only if

\det\!\left(I_{d}-\sum_{k=1}^{p}\Theta_{k}\,z^{k}\right)\neq 0\qquad\text{for all }z\in\mathbb{C}\text{ such that }\left\lvert z\right\rvert\leq 1.

(8)

See
[
27
]
, Section 10.1.
∎

A.2
Dynamic mode decomposition

Dynamic mode decomposition (DMD)
[
57
]
is a data-driven spectral method for identifying the dominant spatiotemporal structures of a dynamical system from measurement data. Originally developed for high-dimensional fluid systems, it has since found applications in dimensionality reduction, forecasting, and control
[
58
]
, and admits an interpretation as a finite-dimensional approximation of the Koopman operator
[
14
]
. Unlike methods such as principal or independent component analysis, DMD enforces a joint representation of spatial structure and temporal evolution.

A.2.1
Modal decomposition of linear dynamics

We assume the system evolves according to an ordinary differential equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathcal{A}x,

where
x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the state and
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a linear operator. For a given initial condition
x(0)
, the solution is given by
x(t)=e^{\mathcal{A}t}x(0)
. In particular, if
\mathcal{A}=\Phi\,\Omega\,\Phi^{-1}
is diagonalisable, then

x(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\phi_{i}e^{\omega_{i}t}b_{i},

where
\Phi=\left(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{d}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
with eigenvectors
\phi_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
,
\Omega=\diag{\!(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{d})}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
with eigenvalues
\omega_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
, and
b=\Phi^{-1}x(0)\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
. In practice, data are observed at discrete times
t_{n}=n\Delta t
, yielding snapshots
x_{n}=x(t_{n})
. The continuous-time dynamics are approximated by discrete-time linear map

x_{n+1}=A\,x_{n},\qquad A=e^{\mathcal{A}\Delta t},

with solution
x_{n}=A^{n-1}x_{1}
for an initial condition
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If
A=\Phi\,\Lambda\,\Phi^{-1}
, then

x_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\phi_{i}\lambda_{i}^{n-1}b_{i},

where
\lambda_{i}=e^{\omega_{i}\Delta t}
and
b=\Phi^{-1}x_{1}
. Each eigenpair
(\phi_{i},\lambda_{i})
corresponds to a spatiotemporal mode:
\phi_{i}
encodes spatial structure and
\lambda_{i}
encodes temporal dynamics through its modulus (growth or decay rate) and argument (oscillation frequency).

\displaystyle=\Tr{\!\left(\Pi^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda_{n}^{\ast}\Lambda_{n}\Pi\right)}-\Tr{\!\left(\Pi^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda_{n}^{\ast}\Pi\Lambda_{n-1}+\Lambda_{n-1}^{\ast}\Pi^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda_{n}\Pi\right)}+\Tr{\!\left(\Lambda_{n-1}^{\ast}\Lambda_{n-1}\right)}.

For the first term, we note that

\Tr{\!\left(\Pi^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda_{n}^{\ast}\Lambda_{n}\Pi\right)}=\Tr{\!\left(\Lambda_{n}\Pi\Pi^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda_{n}^{\ast}\right)}=\Tr{\!\left(\Lambda_{n}\Lambda_{n}^{\ast}\right)}=\left\lVert\Lambda_{n}\right\rVert_{F}^{2},

which is independent of
\Pi
. For the cross term, we note that
\Lambda_{n-1}^{\ast}\Pi^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda_{n}\Pi=\left(\Pi^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda_{n}^{\ast}\Pi\Lambda_{n-1}\right)^{\ast}
, so using
\Tr{\!\left(M+M^{\ast}\right)}=2\Tr{\!\left(\Rpart{\!\left(M\right)}\right)}
,

\Tr{\!\left(\Pi^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda_{n}^{\ast}\Pi\Lambda_{n-1}+\Lambda_{n-1}^{\ast}\Pi^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda_{n}\Pi\right)}=2\Tr{\!\left(\Rpart{\!\left(\Pi^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda_{n}^{\ast}\Pi\Lambda_{n-1}\right)}\right)}.

Since
\Lambda_{n}
and
\Lambda_{n-1}
are diagonal,
\Pi^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda_{n}^{\ast}\Pi
is diagonal with
i
-th entry
\big(\lambda_{n}^{(\pi(i))}\big)^{\ast}
, and the product
\Pi^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda_{n}^{\ast}\Pi\Lambda_{n-1}
is diagonal with
i
-th entry
\big(\lambda_{n}^{(\pi(i))}\big)^{\ast}\lambda_{n-1}^{(i)}
. Therefore,

\Tr{\!\left(\Rpart{\!\left(\Pi^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda_{n}^{\ast}\Pi\,\Lambda_{n-1}\right)}\right)}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\Rpart{\!\left(\big(\lambda_{n}^{(\pi(i))}\big)^{\ast}\lambda_{n-1}^{(i)}\right)}=\Tr{\!\left(\Pi\Rpart{\!\left(\lambda_{n}^{\ast}\lambda_{n-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)}\right)},

where
\lambda_{n}^{\ast}\lambda_{n-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
is the outer product of the eigenvalue vectors, with
(i,j)
-th entry
\big(\lambda_{n}^{(i)}\big)^{\ast}\lambda_{n-1}^{(j)}
. Combining all three terms,

\left\lVert\Pi^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda_{n}\Pi-\Lambda_{n-1}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\left\lVert\Lambda_{n}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+\left\lVert\Lambda_{n-1}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}-2\Tr{\!\left(\Pi\Rpart{\!\left(\lambda_{n}^{\ast}\lambda_{n-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)}\right)}.

Since
\left\lVert\Lambda_{n}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+\left\lVert\Lambda_{n-1}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}
is constant with respect to
\Pi
,

\Pi_{n}=\argmin_{\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{r}}\left\lVert\Pi^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda_{n}\Pi-\Lambda_{n-1}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\argmax_{\Pi\in\mathcal{S}_{r}}\Tr{\!\left(\Pi\Rpart{\!\left(\lambda_{n}^{\ast}\lambda_{n-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)}\right)}.

The new maximisation problem is addressed by relaxing the feasible set
\mathcal{S}_{r}
into its convex hull. Since the objective is linear in
\Pi
, the optimal solution of this relaxed problem is guaranteed to lie on the boundary of the feasible region
[
11
]
. Therefore,

\Pi_{n}=\argmax_{\Pi\in\mathcal{B}_{r}}\left\{\Tr{\!\left(\Pi\Rpart{\!\left(\lambda_{n}^{\ast}\lambda_{n-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)}\right)}\right\},

where the maximum is now taken over the larger set
\mathcal{B}_{r}\supset\mathcal{S}_{r}
.
∎

Lemma 6
.

Under the model assumptions of Section
4
, the stationary covariance matrix
\Gamma=\mathbb{E}\left[x_{t}x_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\right]
is positive definite, i.e.
\Gamma\succ 0
.

Proof.

Recall the
\text{MA}(\infty)
representation of the stationary process
\{x_{t}\}
,

x_{t}=\sum_{j\geq 0}\Theta^{j}\varepsilon_{t-j}.

(11)

Define the
\ell_{p}
norm of a random vector
y
as
\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{p}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{p}\right]^{1/p}
. Since
\zeta(\Theta)<1
, the series
\sum_{j\geq 0}\Theta^{j}\varepsilon_{t-j}
converges absolutely in
\ell_{2}
, and

\Gamma=\mathbb{E}\left[x_{t}x_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\right]=\sum_{j\geq 0}\Theta^{j}\Sigma\left(\Theta^{j}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}=\Sigma+\sum_{j\geq 1}\Theta^{j}\Sigma\left(\Theta^{j}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}.

Therefore,
\Gamma\succeq\Sigma\succ 0
since all other terms are positive semi-definite.
∎

Lemma 7
.

Let
p\geq 1
. If the noise
\{\varepsilon_{t}\}
has finite
p
-th moments, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\varepsilon_{t}\right\rVert^{p}\right]<\infty
, then the state process
\{x_{t}\}
also has finite
p
-th moments, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert x_{t}\right\rVert^{p}\right]<\infty
.

Proof.

By Minkowski’s inequality for the
\ell_{p}
norm and the
\text{MA}(\infty)
representation of the stationary process
\{x_{t}\}
in Eq. (
11
),

\displaystyle\left\lVert x_{t}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\geq 0}\left\lVert\Theta^{j}\varepsilon_{t-j}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\geq 0}\left\lVert\Theta^{j}\right\rVert\left\lVert\varepsilon_{t-j}\right\rVert_{p}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\varepsilon_{0}\right\rVert_{p}\sum_{j\geq 0}\left\lVert\Theta^{j}\right\rVert,

where we used that
\left\lVert\varepsilon_{t-j}\right\rVert_{p}=\left\lVert\varepsilon_{0}\right\rVert_{p}
by stationarity of
\{\varepsilon_{t}\}
. Since
\zeta(\Theta)<1
, there exist constants
\alpha>0
and
\beta\in\left(\zeta(\Theta),1\right)
such that
\left\lVert\Theta^{j}\right\rVert\leq\alpha\beta^{j}
for all
j\geq 0
. Therefore,

\left\lVert x_{t}\right\rVert_{p}\leq\left\lVert\varepsilon_{0}\right\rVert_{p}\frac{\alpha}{1-\beta}<\infty,

which implies
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert x_{t}\right\rVert^{p}\right]<\infty
.
∎

B.4.1
Unweighted estimator

Throughout this section, we consider the updates of Eq. (
3
) with fixed
\rho=1
.

Lemma 8
.

For any
n
, the conditional bias of
\Theta_{n}
given
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
is non-zero almost surely. Specifically,

\mathbb{E}\left[\Theta_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]-\Theta=\Xi_{n}\Gamma_{n}^{-1}\neq 0\qquad\text{almost surely},

where

\Xi_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\varepsilon_{t}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad\Gamma_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}x_{t-1}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}.

Proof.

For any time step
n
, the updates of Eq. (
3
) compute the DMD matrix

\Theta_{n}=\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}x_{t}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}x_{t-1}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)^{-1}

exactly. Substituting the model dynamics
x_{t}=\Theta\,x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t}
from the
\mathrm{VAR}_{d}(1)
model,

\sum_{t=1}^{n}x_{t}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}=\Theta\sum_{t=1}^{n}x_{t-1}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}+\sum_{t=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{t}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}.

Right-multiplying by
\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}x_{t-1}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)^{-1}
, we obtain

\Theta_{n}=\Theta+\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{t}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}x_{t-1}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)^{-1}.

(12)

Conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
, the denominator
\sum_{t=1}^{n}x_{t-1}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}
is deterministic since
x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}
are all
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
-measurable. The conditional expectation of the numerator term can be written as

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{t}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}},

since
x_{t-1}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
-measurable for
t\leq n
. We evaluate the term
\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]
for different
t
.

•

For
t<n
, the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
contains
x_{t}
and
x_{t-1}
. Since
\varepsilon_{t}=x_{t}-\Theta\,x_{t-1}
, the noise realisation
\varepsilon_{t}
is fully determined by the information in
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
, hence

\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]=\varepsilon_{t}\neq 0\qquad\text{almost surely}.

•

For
t=n
, the noise
\varepsilon_{n}
occurs at time
n
which is not contained in
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
. By the martingale difference property,

\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]=0.

Substituting these back,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{t}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]=\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\varepsilon_{t}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\neq 0\qquad\text{almost surely}.

Therefore, the conditional bias

\mathbb{E}\left[\Theta_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]-\Theta=\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\varepsilon_{t}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}x_{t-1}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)^{-1}

depends on the realised history of the noise and is non-zero almost surely.
∎

Proposition 2
.

If
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\varepsilon_{t}\right\rVert^{4}\right]<\infty
, then
\Gamma_{n}
is a consistent estimator of
\Gamma
. In particular,
\Gamma_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\Gamma
, and

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\Gamma_{n}-\Gamma\right\rVert^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Proof.

Let
\Delta_{n}=\Gamma_{n}-\Gamma
, and consider the
(i,j)
-th entry of
\Delta_{n}
,

(\Delta_{n})_{ij}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\left(x_{t,i}x_{t,j}-\Gamma_{ij}\right).

Define
(Y_{t})_{ij}=x_{t,i}x_{t,j}-\Gamma_{ij}
, so that
\mathbb{E}\left[(Y_{t})_{ij}\right]=0
and
(\Delta_{n})_{ij}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}(Y_{t})_{ij}
. Then,

\mathrm{Var}\left((\Delta_{n})_{ij}\right)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{s,t=0}^{n-1}\mathrm{Cov}\!\left((Y_{s})_{ij},(Y_{t})_{ij}\right).

By stationarity,
\mathrm{Cov}\!\left((Y_{s})_{ij},(Y_{t})_{ij}\right)=\gamma_{ij}(t-s)
depends only on the lag. Hence,

\mathrm{Var}\left((\Delta_{n})_{ij}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{h=-(n-1)}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{\left\lvert h\right\rvert}{n}\right)\gamma_{ij}(h),

(13)

where
\gamma_{ij}(h)=\mathrm{Cov}\!\left(x_{0,i}x_{0,j},x_{h,i}x_{h,j}\right)
. Using (
11
), decompose
x_{h}
for
h\geq 1
as
x_{h}=u_{h}+v_{h}
, where

u_{h}=\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}\Theta^{k}\varepsilon_{h-k},\qquad v_{h}=\sum_{k\geq h}\Theta^{k}\varepsilon_{h-k}.

Here,
u_{h}
depends only on
\{\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{h}\}
and is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{0}
, while
v_{h}
depends only on
\{\varepsilon_{s}\}_{s\leq 0}
. Since
x_{h,i}x_{h,j}=u_{h,i}u_{h,j}+v_{h,i}v_{h,j}+u_{h,i}v_{h,j}+v_{h,i}u_{h,j}
and
u_{h}
is independent of
(\mathcal{F}_{0},v_{h})
, each cross-covariance
\mathrm{Cov}\!\left(x_{0,i}x_{0,j},\cdot\right)
reduces to one involving only
v_{h}
and
x_{0}
. By Cauchy-Schwarz and Lemma
7
,

\left\lvert\gamma_{ij}(h)\right\rvert\leq c_{1}\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{L^{4}}^{2}+c_{2}\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{L^{4}}

for constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
depending on
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert x_{0}\right\rVert^{4}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert u_{h}\right\rVert^{4}\right]
. By Minkowski’s inequality and
\left\lVert\Theta^{k}\right\rVert\leq\alpha\beta^{k}
for some
\alpha>0
and
\beta\in(\zeta(\Theta),1)
,

\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{L^{4}}\leq\left\lVert\varepsilon_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{4}}\sum_{k\geq h}\alpha\beta^{k}=\frac{\alpha\left\lVert\varepsilon_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{4}}}{1-\beta}\,\beta^{h}.

Therefore,
\left\lvert\gamma_{ij}(h)\right\rvert\leq\omega\beta^{\left\lvert h\right\rvert}
for some
\omega>0
using
\gamma_{ij}(-h)=\gamma_{ij}(h)
by stationarity, and

\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert\gamma_{ij}(h)\right\rvert\leq\frac{2\omega}{1-\beta}<\infty.

(14)

Since
\left(1-\frac{\left\lvert h\right\rvert}{n}\right)\leq 1
in Eq. (
13
), the absolute summability Eq. (
14
) gives

\mathrm{Var}\left((\Delta_{n})_{ij}\right)\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert\gamma_{ij}(h)\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Furthermore, as
\left\lVert\Delta_{n}\right\rVert^{2}\leq\left\lVert\Delta_{n}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\left(\Delta_{n}\right)^{2}_{ij}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\Delta_{n}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\mathrm{Var}\left((\Delta_{n})_{ij}\right)=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.

(15)

Finally, by Markov’s inequality applied to the non-negative random variable
\left\lVert\Delta_{n}\right\rVert^{2}
, for any
\epsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\Gamma_{n}-\Gamma\right\rVert>\epsilon\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\Delta_{n}\right\rVert^{2}>\epsilon^{2}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\Delta_{n}\right\rVert^{2}\right]}{\epsilon^{2}},

which shows
\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\Gamma_{n}-\Gamma\right\rVert>\epsilon\right)\to 0
and therefore
\Gamma_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\Gamma
as
n\to\infty
.
∎

Lemma 9
.

If
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\varepsilon_{t}\right\rVert^{4}\right]<\infty
, then
\mathbb{P}\left(\Gamma_{n}\succ 0\right)\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. In particular,
\Gamma_{n}
is eventually positive definite.

Proof.

By Weyl’s inequality (Lemma
5
) with
A=\Gamma
and
B=\Delta_{n}=\Gamma_{n}-\Gamma
,

\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma_{n})\geq\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma)+\lambda_{\min}(\Delta_{n})\geq\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma)-\left\lVert\Delta_{n}\right\rVert,

since
\lambda_{\min}(B)\geq-\left\lVert B\right\rVert
for any Hermitian matrix
B
. By Proposition
2
, for any

,

∎

Lemma 10
.

On the event
\left\{\Gamma_{n}\succ 0\right\}
,

\Gamma_{n}^{-1}=\Gamma^{-1}-\Gamma^{-1}\Delta_{n}\Gamma^{-1}+\Upsilon_{n},

where
\Upsilon_{n}=\Gamma^{-1}\Delta_{n}\Gamma_{n}^{-1}\Delta_{n}\Gamma^{-1}
with
\Delta_{n}=\Gamma_{n}-\Gamma
.

Proof.

The resolvent identity gives

\Gamma_{n}^{-1}-\Gamma^{-1}=-\Gamma_{n}^{-1}\left(\Gamma_{n}-\Gamma\right)\Gamma^{-1},

(16)

or equivalently,
\Gamma_{n}^{-1}=\Gamma^{-1}-\Gamma_{n}^{-1}\Delta_{n}\Gamma^{-1}
. Substituting this expression into itself,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{-1}-\left(\Gamma^{-1}-\Gamma_{n}^{-1}\Delta_{n}\Gamma^{-1}\right)\Delta_{n}\Gamma^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{-1}-\Gamma^{-1}\Delta_{n}\Gamma^{-1}+\Gamma_{n}^{-1}\Delta_{n}\Gamma^{-1}\Delta_{n}\Gamma^{-1}.

(17)

Then, by Eq. (
16
) and Eq. (
B.4.1
)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n}^{-1}\Delta_{n}\Gamma^{-1}\Delta_{n}\Gamma^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{n}^{-1}-\Gamma^{-1}+\Gamma^{-1}\Delta_{n}\Gamma^{-1}

\displaystyle=-\Gamma_{n}^{-1}\Delta_{n}\Gamma^{-1}+\Gamma^{-1}\Delta_{n}\Gamma^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\Gamma^{-1}-\Gamma_{n}^{-1}\right)\Delta_{n}\Gamma^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{-1}\Delta_{n}\Gamma_{n}^{-1}\Delta_{n}\Gamma^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Upsilon_{n}.

∎

Lemma 11
.

If
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\varepsilon_{t}\right\rVert^{8}\right]<\infty
, then
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\Delta_{n}\right\rVert^{4}\right]=\mathcal{O}(n^{-2})
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Since
\left\lVert\Delta_{n}\right\rVert^{4}\leq\left\lVert\Delta_{n}\right\rVert_{F}^{4}
and

\left\lVert\Delta_{n}\right\rVert_{F}^{4}=\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}(\Delta_{n})_{ij}^{2}\right)^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}(\Delta_{n})_{ij}^{4}+2\sum_{(i,j)<(k,\ell)}(\Delta_{n})_{ij}^{2}\,(\Delta_{n})_{k\ell}^{2},

it suffices to show
\mathbb{E}\left[(\Delta_{n})_{ij}^{4}\right]=\mathcal{O}(n^{-2})
for each
(i,j)
, noting that the cross terms satisfy

\mathbb{E}\left[(\Delta_{n})_{ij}^{2}\,(\Delta_{n})_{k\ell}^{2}\right]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[(\Delta_{n})_{ij}^{4}\right]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[(\Delta_{n})_{k\ell}^{4}\right]}

by Cauchy-Schwarz. Let

S_{n}=\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}(Y_{t})_{ij},\qquad(\Delta_{n})_{ij}=\frac{S_{n}}{n}

By the moment-cumulant relation for zero-mean random variables,

\mathbb{E}\left[S_{n}^{4}\right]=3\,\mathbb{E}\left[S_{n}^{2}\right]^{2}+\sum_{t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}=0}^{n-1}\kappa(Y_{t_{1}},Y_{t_{2}},Y_{t_{3}},Y_{t_{4}}),

(18)

where

\displaystyle\kappa(Y_{t_{1}},Y_{t_{2}}Y_{t_{3}},Y_{t_{4}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{t_{1}}Y_{t_{2}}Y_{t_{3}}Y_{t_{4}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{t_{1}}Y_{t_{2}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{t_{3}}Y_{t_{4}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{t_{1}}Y_{t_{3}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{t_{2}}Y_{t_{4}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{t_{1}}Y_{t_{4}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{t_{2}}Y_{t_{3}}\right].

The identity Eq. (
18
) is verified by direct substitution. From Proposition
2
,
\mathbb{E}\left[S_{n}^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}(n)
, thus
3\,\mathbb{E}\left[S_{n}^{2}\right]^{2}=\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
. For the cumulant term,
\kappa(Y_{t_{1}},Y_{t_{2}},Y_{t_{3}},Y_{t_{4}})=c(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3})
by stationarity and depends only on the lag differences
h_{k}=t_{k+1}-t_{1}
, for
k\in\{1,2,3\}
. Hence

\left\lvert\sum_{t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}=0}^{n-1}\kappa(Y_{t_{1}},Y_{t_{2}},Y_{t_{3}},Y_{t_{4}})\right\rvert\leq n\sum_{h_{1},h_{2},h_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert c(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3})\right\rvert.

The joint cumulant
c(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3})
involves expectations of the form
\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{0}Y_{h_{1}}Y_{h_{2}}Y_{h_{3}}\right]
, each bounded by
\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{0}^{4}\right]
via Cauchy-Schwarz. Then,
\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{0}^{4}\right]<\infty
follows from
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert x_{0}\right\rVert^{8}\right]<\infty
, which holds by Lemma
7
with
p=8
under the assumption
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\varepsilon_{t}\right\rVert^{8}\right]<\infty
. By the same
u_{h},v_{h}
decomposition used in Lemma
2
, the cumulant
c(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3})
decays geometrically in
\max(\left\lvert h_{1}\right\rvert,\left\lvert h_{2}\right\rvert,\left\lvert h_{3}\right\rvert)
, and thus

\sum_{h_{1},h_{2},h_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert c(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3})\right\rvert<\infty.

Therefore

\sum_{t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}=0}^{n-1}\kappa(Y_{t_{1}},Y_{t_{2}},Y_{t_{3}},Y_{t_{4}})=\mathcal{O}(n)\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty,

and combining with Eq. (

Lemma 12
.

For any

given

non-zero almost surely. Specifically,

\mathbb{E}\left[\Theta_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]-\Theta=\Xi_{n}\Gamma_{n}^{-1}\neq 0\qquad\text{almost surely},

where

\Xi_{n}=\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\rho^{n-t}\varepsilon_{t}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad\Gamma_{n}=\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\rho^{n-t}x_{t-1}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}.

Proof.

The updates in Eq. (
3
) compute the weighted DMD matrix

\Theta_{n}=\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\rho^{n-t}x_{t}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\rho^{n-t}x_{t-1}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)^{-1}

exactly. Substituting the model dynamics
x_{t}=\Theta\,x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t}
into the first term,

\sum_{t=1}^{n}\rho^{n-t}x_{t}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}=\Theta\sum_{t=1}^{n}\rho^{n-t}x_{t-1}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}+\sum_{t=1}^{n}\rho^{n-t}\varepsilon_{t}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}.

Right-multiplying by
\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\rho^{n-t}x_{t-1}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)^{-1}
, we obtain

\Theta_{n}=\Theta+\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\rho^{n-t}\varepsilon_{t}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\rho^{n-t}x_{t-1}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)^{-1}.

(24)

Conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
, the denominator
\sum_{t=1}^{n}\rho^{n-t}x_{t-1}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}
is deterministic since
x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}
are all
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
-measurable and the weights
\rho^{n-t}
are deterministic. The conditional expectation of the numerator term can be written as

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\rho^{n-t}\varepsilon_{t}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\rho^{n-t}\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}},

since
x_{t-1}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
-measurable for
t\leq n
. We evaluate
\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]
for different
t

•

For
t<n
, the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
contains
x_{t}
and
x_{t-1}
. Since
\varepsilon_{t}=x_{t}-\Theta\,x_{t-1}
, the noise realisation
\varepsilon_{t}
is fully determined by the information in

\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]=\varepsilon_{t}\neq 0\qquad\text{almost surely}.

•

For

\varepsilon_{n}

\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]=0.

Substituting these back,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{n}\rho^{n-t}\varepsilon_{t}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]=\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\rho^{n-t}\varepsilon_{t}x_{t-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\neq 0\qquad\text{almost surely}.

depends on the realised history of the noise and is non-zero almost surely.

\mathbb{E}\left[\Theta_{n}-\Theta\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Theta_{n}-\Theta\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Theta_{n}-\Theta\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right],

where the first term satisfies
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert\leq c\,n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}
by Eq. (
32
). For the second, as in Theorem
1
, we adopt the convention that
\Gamma_{n}^{-1}=0
on the event that
\Gamma_{n}
is singular, so that
\Xi_{n}\Gamma_{n}^{-1}
is well-defined everywhere. By Cauchy-Schwarz,

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Theta_{n}-\Theta\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right]\right\rVert\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\Xi_{n}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert\Gamma_{n}^{-1}\right\rVert^{2}\right]}\sqrt{\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}\right)},

and by a further application of Cauchy-Schwarz,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\Xi_{n}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert\Gamma_{n}^{-1}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\Xi_{n}\right\rVert^{4}\right]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\Gamma_{n}^{-1}\right\rVert^{4}\right]}.

We bound each factor separately.

Since
\left\lVert\Xi_{n}\right\rVert^{4}\leq\left\lVert\Xi_{n}\right\rVert_{F}^{4}
, it suffices to show
\mathbb{E}\left[(\Xi_{n})_{ij}^{4}\right]=\mathcal{O}(n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-2})
for each
(i,j)
, with cross terms handled by Cauchy-Schwarz. Let
Z_{t}=\varepsilon_{t,i}\,x_{t-1,j}
and
T_{n}=\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\rho^{n-t}Z_{t}
, so that we have
(\Xi_{n})_{ij}=T_{n}/n_{\mathrm{eff}}
. By the moment-cumulant relation,

\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}^{4}\right]=3\,\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}^{2}\right]^{2}+\sum_{t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}=1}^{n-1}\rho^{\sum_{k=1}^{4}(n-t_{k})}\kappa(Z_{t_{1}},Z_{t_{2}},Z_{t_{3}},Z_{t_{4}}).

From Eq. (
28
),
\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}(n_{\mathrm{eff}})
, so
3\,\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}^{2}\right]^{2}=\mathcal{O}(n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2})
. For the cumulant sum, since the
\varepsilon_{t}
are mutually independent,
\kappa(Z_{t_{1}},Z_{t_{2}},Z_{t_{3}},Z_{t_{4}})
depends only on the lag differences by stationarity and decays geometrically in
\max(\left\lvert h_{1}\right\rvert,\left\lvert h_{2}\right\rvert,\left\lvert h_{3}\right\rvert)
by the
u_{h},v_{h}
decomposition of Lemma
2
, with
\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{t}^{4}\right]<\infty
following from
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\varepsilon_{t}\right\rVert^{8}\right]<\infty
via Lemma
7
. Fixing
t_{1}
(contributing at most
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
) and summing absolutely over the remaining lag differences yields the cumulant sum as
\mathcal{O}(n_{\mathrm{eff}})
. Therefore
\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}^{4}\right]=\mathcal{O}(n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2})
, yielding

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\Xi_{n}\right\rVert^{4}\right]=\mathcal{O}(n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-2}).

We have
\left\lVert\Gamma_{n}^{-1}\right\rVert\leq 2/\lambda_{\min}(\Gamma)
with probability at least
1-c_{e}\,n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}
on the event
\mathcal{E}_{n}
. By convention, the inverse is set to zero when
\Gamma_{n}
is singular. On the complement
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}
, which occurs with probability at most
c_{e}\,n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}
, the inverse remains finite since singular cases have been treated. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\Gamma_{n}^{-1}\right\rVert^{4}\right]=\mathcal{O}(1).

Combining,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\Xi_{n}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert\Gamma_{n}^{-1}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq\sqrt{\mathcal{O}(n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-2})}\sqrt{\mathcal{O}(1)}=\mathcal{O}(n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}),

and with
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}\right)\leq c_{e}\,n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}
from Lemma
13
,

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Theta_{n}-\Theta\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}}\right]\right\rVert\leq\sqrt{\mathcal{O}(n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1})}\sqrt{\mathcal{O}(n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1})}=\mathcal{O}(n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}).

Together,
\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[\Theta_{n}\right]-\Theta\right\rVert\leq C\,n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}
for some
C>0
as
n\to\infty
.
∎

Appendix C
Experiment details

C.1
Performance metrics

We evaluate changepoint detection algorithms along two complementary aspects: detection speed and detection accuracy. Speed captures how quickly an algorithm responds to a change or raises a false alarm; accuracy captures how reliably true changes are found and false positives avoided. We use the same notation as in the problem definition of Section
2.1
.

C.1.1
Detection speed

Consider the stream
x_{1},x_{2},\dots
under
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, with a single changepoint at time
\tau
. Let
\hat{\tau}
denote the first time step at which the algorithm signals a change. Detection speed is characterised by the
average run lengths

[
51
]
: the expected time to false alarm
\mathtt{ARL}_{0}
and the expected detection delay
\mathtt{ARL}_{1}
, given by

\displaystyle\mathtt{ARL}_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\tau}\mid\mathcal{H}_{0}\right],

\displaystyle\mathtt{ARL}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\tau}-\tau\mid\mathcal{H}_{1}\right].

\mathtt{ARL}_{0}
is the average number of observations before the detector incorrectly signals a change under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
;
\mathtt{ARL}_{1}
is the average lag between the true changepoint and its detection under
\mathcal{H}_{1}
. A well-performing detector achieves high
\mathtt{ARL}_{0}
and low
\mathtt{ARL}_{1}
.

C.1.2
Detection accuracy

Average run lengths alone are insufficient: they do not capture what fraction of true changepoints are found, nor what fraction of detections are spurious. Following
[
66
]
and
[
35
]
, consider a sequence with
n_{t}
true change times
\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{n}
and
n_{d}
detections
\hat{\tau}_{1},\dots,\hat{\tau}_{m}
. The set of true positives is

\mathcal{TP}=\left\{\tau_{i}~:~\exists\,\hat{\tau}_{j}\text{ such that }-\Delta_{\ell}\leq\hat{\tau}_{j}-\tau_{i}\leq\Delta_{r}\right\},

where
\Delta_{\ell},\Delta_{r}\geq 0
define the permissible detection margins around each true changepoint. Precision
\mathtt{P}
, recall
\mathtt{R}
, and
\mathtt{F}_{1}
-score are then defined
1
1

1

We rely on the implementation of
[
66
]
, available at
github.com/alan-turing-institute/TCPDBench
.
as

\mathtt{P}=\frac{\operatorname{card}(\mathcal{TP})}{n_{d}},\qquad\mathtt{R}=\frac{\operatorname{card}(\mathcal{TP})}{n_{t}},\qquad\mathtt{F}_{1}=\frac{2\,\mathtt{P}\mathtt{R}}{\mathtt{P}+\mathtt{R}}.

As argued in
[
20
]
, an algorithm that fails to reliably detect a single changepoint is unlikely to perform well in the more complex multi-changepoint setting; we therefore restrict synthetic data evaluation to single-changepoint sequences, following the same practice as
[
34
]
and
[
12
]
. Given a sequence with true changepoint at
\tau
, let
\hat{\tau}
denote the first detection time, if any. Each sequence yields exactly one of the following outcomes: a true positive (
\mathtt{TP}
) if
\tau-\Delta_{\ell}\leq\hat{\tau}\leq\tau+\Delta_{r}
; a false positive (
\mathtt{FP}
) if
\hat{\tau}<\tau-\Delta_{\ell}
; a false negative with late detection (
\mathtt{FN}_{d}
) if
\hat{\tau}>\tau+\Delta_{r}
; or a false negative with no detection (
\mathtt{FN}_{\emptyset}
) if no alarm is raised. Aggregating over
N
independent sequences, we set
n_{t}=N
(one true changepoint per sequence) and
n_{d}=\operatorname{card}(\mathtt{TP})+\operatorname{card}(\mathtt{FP}_{d})+\operatorname{card}(\mathtt{FN}_{\emptyset})
(total number of detections), so that precision and recall reduce to

\mathtt{P}=\frac{\operatorname{card}(\mathtt{TP})}{n_{d}},\qquad\mathtt{R}=\frac{\operatorname{card}(\mathtt{TP})}{N}.

For real-world data, which contain multiple changepoints per sequence, we apply the margin-based
\mathcal{TP}
set defined above directly.

C.3
Synthetic data sets

C.3.1
Overview and common setup

All synthetic data sets simulate a
\mathrm{VAR}_{d}(1)
process with a single changepoint, given by

x_{t}=\Theta_{t}\,x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t},\qquad t=\{1,\dots,T\},

where

\Theta_{t}=\begin{cases}\Theta_{0}&t<\tau,\\
\Theta_{1}&t\geq\tau,\end{cases}

with
\Theta_{0},\Theta_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with all eigenvalues strictly inside the open unit disk, and
\{\varepsilon_{t}\}
a zero-mean white noise process whose distribution varies by data set. Each data set is generated as
N=1{,}000
independent replications. Two variants are produced for each experiment:

•

\mathtt{ARL}_{1}
data set: sequences of length
T=400
with a changepoint present. The change time is drawn from a discrete uniform distribution as
\tau\sim\mathrm{Uniform}\!\left(\lfloor 0.3T\rfloor,\,\lfloor 0.7T\rfloor\right)
. This data set allows the computation of detection accuracy metrics, such as precision, recall, and
\mathtt{F}_{1}
-score, as well as the detection speed metric
\mathtt{ARL}_{1}
(Appendix
C.1
using
\Delta_{\ell}=0
and
\Delta_{r}=50
).

•

\mathtt{ARL}_{0}
data set: sequences of length
T=10{,}000
with no changepoint, i.e.
\tau=T
, so that
\Theta_{t}=\Theta_{0}
throughout. This data set allows the computation of the detection speed metric
\mathtt{ARL}_{0}
, which estimates the expected time to false alarm.

C.3.2
Transition matrix parametrisation

Bivariate case
(d=2)
.

All bivariate data sets use the following parametrisation. Given parameters
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
a^{2}+b^{2}<1
, the transition matrix is given by

\Theta=\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\
b&a\end{pmatrix}.

This corresponds to the real canonical form of a complex multiplication by
\lambda=a+bi
, and its eigenvalues are
\lambda_{1,2}=a\pm bi
, with modulus
\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}<1
. Therefore,
\Theta
is stable and
\{x_{t}\}
is stationary.

Uniform sampling from the unit disk.

Each pair
(a,b)
is sampled uniformly from the closed unit disk
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\left\lvert z\right\rvert<1\}
via the following procedure. We independently let
u\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)
and
\varphi\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,2\pi)
and then set

r=\sqrt{u},\quad a=r\cos\varphi,\quad b=r\sin\varphi.

Since the area element in polar coordinates is
r\,dr\,d\varphi
, and the density of
r=\sqrt{u}
is
f_{r}(r)=2r
for
r\in(0,1)
, the joint density
f(r,\varphi)=2r/(2\pi)=r/\pi
is the uniform density on the unit disk (whose area is
\pi
). This ensures
a^{2}+b^{2}=r^{2}=u<1
almost surely.

Noise covariance generation.

Unless specified otherwise, the noise covariance matrix is generated as follows. Draw
S\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with entries i.i.d. from
\mathrm{Uniform}(-1,1)
, and set
\Sigma=S^{\mathsf{T}}S
. This procedure yields a random semi-definite matrix almost surely.

C.3.3
Data set descriptions

Bivariate VAR with Gaussian noise

Purpose.

Baseline evaluation on the standard
\mathrm{VAR}_{2}(1)
setting with Gaussian noise.

Generative process.

For each replication, draw
(a_{0},b_{0})
and
(a_{1},b_{1})
independently and uniformly from the unit disk (Appendix
C.3.2
), and form the transition matrices

\Theta_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}&-b_{0}\\
b_{0}&a_{0}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\Theta_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&-b_{1}\\
b_{1}&a_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

Draw the covariance matrix
\Sigma
as described in Appendix
C.3.2
. The noise process is independent and identically distributed multivariate Gaussian,

\varepsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma).

The two transition matrices
\Theta_{0}
and
\Theta_{1}
are sampled independently with no constraint on the distance between their eigenvalues; in particular, the change magnitude is unconstrained.

Bivariate VAR with Laplace noise

Purpose.

Evaluate robustness to heavier-tailed, non-Gaussian noise.

Generative process.

The transition matrices
\Theta_{0}
,
\Theta_{1}
, and the noise covariance
\Sigma
are generated identically to the Gaussian case (Appendix
C.3.2
). The noise is drawn from a multivariate Laplace distribution with covariance
\Sigma
via the following Gaussian copula construction:

1.

Compute the correlation matrix
R
as
R_{ij}=\Sigma_{ij}/\sqrt{\Sigma_{ii}\Sigma_{jj}}
.

2.

Draw
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R)
, a correlated standard normal vector.

3.

Transform to uniform marginals as
u=\Phi(z)
element-wise.

4.

Apply the inverse Laplace
x_{i}=F_{\mathrm{Lap}}^{-1}(u_{i}\mid 0,s_{i})
, with
s_{i}=\sqrt{\Sigma_{ii}}/\sqrt{2}
.

The result
\varepsilon_{t}=X
has marginal Laplace distributions with variance
\Sigma_{ii}
and cross-correlation structure induced by
R
. Note that the marginal variance of a
\mathrm{Laplace}(0,s)
distribution is
2s^{2}=\Sigma_{ii}
, so the covariance matrix of
\varepsilon_{t}
is
\Sigma
by construction.

Bivariate VAR with Student’s
\boldsymbol{t}
noise

Purpose.

Evaluate robustness across a range of noise tail weights, from heavy tails (
\nu=3
) to near-Gaussian (
\nu=30
).

Generative process.

The transition matrices and noise covariances are generated as in the Gaussian and Laplace noise data sets (Appendix
C.3.2
). The noise at each time step is drawn from a multivariate Student’s
t
distribution

\varepsilon_{t}\sim t_{\nu}(0,\Sigma),

generated via a scale-mixture of Gaussians: draw
y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
and
u\sim\chi_{\nu}^{2}
independently, and set
\varepsilon_{t}=y\sqrt{\nu/u}
. Note that for
\nu>2
,
\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_{t})=\frac{\nu}{\nu-2}\Sigma
.

Binning.

The
N=1{,}000
replications are divided equally into 10 bins of 100 samples each, corresponding to degrees of freedom

\nu\in\{3,4,5,6,8,10,12,15,20,30\},

sorted from heavy-tailed to near-Gaussian. Within each bin,
\Theta_{0}
,
\Theta_{1}
, and
\Sigma
are redrawn independently per replication.

Bivariate VAR with Huber-contaminated noise

Purpose.

Evaluate robustness to outlier contamination. The noise follows Huber’s
\epsilon
-contamination model: most observations are drawn from the nominal Gaussian, but with probability
\epsilon
an outlier is drawn instead. Changepoint detection in contaminated or adversarial noise settings has attracted interest, including the algorithm proposed by
[
43
]
.

Generative process.

The transition matrices are generated as in Appendix
C.3.2
. At each time step, the noise is drawn from the mixture

\varepsilon_{t}\sim(1-\epsilon)\,\mathcal{N}(0,I_{2})+\epsilon\,\mathcal{N}(0,9I_{2}),

where
9I_{2}=(3I_{2})^{\mathsf{T}}3I_{2}
corresponds to the contaminating observations with standard deviation three times the nominal. This is implemented as a Bernoulli indicator:
\varepsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{2})
w.p.
1-\epsilon
and
\varepsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,9I_{2})
w.p.
\epsilon
.

Binning.

The 10 contamination levels are

\epsilon\in\{0,0.01,0.02,0.05,0.10,0.15,0.20,0.25,0.30,0.40\},

each applied to
N/10=100
replications. At
\epsilon=0
, the model reduces to the standard Gaussian case.

High-dimensional VAR with sparse dynamics

Purpose.

Evaluate scalability and robustness to ambient noise dimensions. A
\mathrm{VAR}_{d}(1)
process is generated by embedding a bivariate dynamical system into a
d
-dimensional observation space, where the remaining
d-2
components evolve as pure noise with no cross-interaction. This simulates a realistic setting in which a low-dimensional signal is embedded in a high-dimensional noisy observation space.

Generative process.

A random permutation
\pi
of
\{1,\ldots,d\}
is drawn, and two coordinates
i=\pi(1)
,
j=\pi(2)
are selected. The
d\times d
transition matrix is

(\Theta)_{ab}=\begin{cases}a_{\mathrm{val}}&a=b\in\{i,j\},\\
-b_{\mathrm{val}}&a=i,b=j,\\
b_{\mathrm{val}}&a=j,b=i,\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
(a_{\mathrm{val}},b_{\mathrm{val}})
parametrises the
2\times 2
rotation block. The
d
-dimensional noise is
\varepsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
where
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is generated as
S^{\top}S
with
S\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(-1,1)^{d\times d}
.

Minimum change size.

To ensure the embedded dynamics change is non-trivial and detectable, the pair of eigenvalues
(z_{0},z_{1})
for
(\Theta_{0},\Theta_{1})
are drawn with a minimum Euclidean distance constraint:

\left\lvert z_{1}-z_{0}\right\rvert\geq d_{\mathrm{low}},

where
d_{\mathrm{low}}
is the 90th percentile of the Euclidean distance
\left\lvert z_{1}-z_{0}\right\rvert
between two points drawn independently and uniformly from the unit disk. This 90th percentile is estimated with reproducibility from
10^{6}
Monte Carlo samples. Note that here the Euclidean distance
\left\lvert z_{1}-z_{0}\right\rvert
is used directly (not symmetrised as in the controlled distance case), since both points are embedded at the same positions
i,j
.

Noise.

Gaussian
\varepsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
where
\Sigma=S^{\mathsf{T}}S
with
S\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(-1,1)^{d\times d}
, independently drawn per replication.

Binning.

Dimension is varied within 10 bins as

d\in\{2,4,6,10,15,20,25,30,35,40\},

each receiving
N=100
replications.

High-dimensional VAR with full-rank transition matrix

Purpose.

Evaluate performance on high-dimensional VAR processes where all dimensions participate in the dynamics, without sparsity assumptions. This contrasts with the previous data set by removing the sparse embedding structure and generating dense, non-diagonally dominant transition matrices.

Eigenvalue sampling.

To ensure conjugate closure (required for the transition matrix to be real-valued), eigenvalues are drawn as follows. First, sample
n_{\mathrm{pairs}}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1,\dots,\lfloor d/2\rfloor)
, giving
n_{\mathrm{real}}=d-2n_{\mathrm{pairs}}
. Then,

•

For each real eigenvalue, draw
(a,\cdot)
from the unit disk; add
\lambda=a\in\mathbb{R}
.

•

For each complex conjugate pair, draw
(a,b)
from the unit disk with
b>0
; add the pair
\lambda=a\pm bi\in\mathbb{C}
.

Real canonical form.

Construct the block-diagonal matrix
D_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
as

D_{c}=\mathrm{blockdiag}\!\left(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n_{\mathrm{real}}},\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&-b_{1}\\
b_{1}&a_{1}\end{pmatrix},\ldots,\begin{pmatrix}a_{n_{\mathrm{pairs}}}&-b_{n_{\mathrm{pairs}}}\\
b_{n_{\mathrm{pairs}}}&a_{n_{\mathrm{pairs}}}\end{pmatrix}\right).

Similarity transform.

A random invertible matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with bounded condition number is constructed as follows. Draw
G\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries and compute the singular value decomposition
G=U\diag(s)V^{\mathsf{T}}
. Clip the singular values as

s_{\mathrm{clipped},i}=\max\!\left(s_{i},\frac{s_{1}}{\kappa_{\max}}\right),\qquad\kappa_{\max}=15,

and set
P=U\diag(s_{\mathrm{clipped}})V^{\mathsf{T}}
. This preserves the random orientation of
U
and
V
(uniformly distributed on the orthogonal group) while bounding the 2-norm condition number
\kappa(P)\leq\kappa_{\max}
, controlling the spread of entries in the resulting transition matrix.

Transition matrix.

The final transition matrix is given by

\Theta=PD_{c}P^{-1},

which as the prescribed spectrum
\sigma(\Theta)=\sigma(D_{c})
and is real-valued by construction.

Noise.

Gaussian
\varepsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
where
\Sigma=S^{\mathsf{T}}S
with
S\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(-1,1)^{d\times d}
, independently drawn per replication.

Binning.

Dimension is varied within 10 bins as

d\in\{2,4,6,10,15,20,25,30,35,40\},

each receiving
N=100
replications.

C.4
Real-world data sets

C.4.1
Overview

Real-world data sets are used to evaluate CHASM on naturally occurring changepoints across diverse modalities (motion, image, text, video). For each data set, the time series
\{x_{t}\}
is a sequence of feature vectors (pre-trained model embeddings or hand-crafted features) at consecutive time steps, and the ground-truth changepoints are derived from provided annotations. All data sets are used in online changepoint detection protocol supporting multiple sequential changepoints.

Novelty.

Text and topic segmentation are predominantly studied in an offline setting. For example,
[
29
]
assume access to the complete document and perform batch inference to identify segment boundaries at the sentence level. In contrast, online changepoint detection methods for text data such as
[
67
]
are comparatively less explored, and typically operate at the coarser document level, as in the 20 Newsgroups experiment. Online changepoint detection at finer granularities, such as the word or token level, remains largely unexplored. To the best of our knowledge, there is limited work on causal, real-time detection of topic boundaries directly from token streams. We argue that this formulation has several advantages: it enables fine-grained, online detection of changes directly at the token level, while remaining computationally efficient and thus allow applications such as authorship change detection, which can arguably be more challenging than document-level segmentation. In this experiment, we rely on autoregressive large language models, which generate tokens sequentially. We interpret the resulting embedding sequence as a dynamical system and detect changes in the effective dynamics of the token-generation process induced by the model’s hidden representations.

Dimensionality reduction.

For data sets whose ambient dimension exceeds a practical threshold, we reduce to
d

C.3.4
, competing approaches can rely on matrix inversions or SVD, making high-dimensional inputs such as flattened image pixels or raw CLIP embeddings computationally prohibitive; this work does not specifically target high-dimensional regimes. Reducing dimension is standard practice in changepoint detection applied to high-dimensional data; for instance,
[
56
]
apply pixel subset selection when processing image streams. Among the alternatives (subset selection, random projection, and offline fine-tuning of a smaller projection head) PCA requires no offline training, is computationally lightweight, and empirically generalises well across modalities. Crucially, the same projected sequence is fed to all competing methods, preserving a fair comparison.

C.6
Computing resources

Experiments were conducted on a high-performance computing cluster comprising 325 compute nodes, each with two AMD EPYC 7742 processors (128 cores, 1 TB RAM per node). Jobs used up to 100 cores in parallel with up to 200 GB of memory, varying with the evaluated method; see Appendix
C.3.4
for per-method time and memory complexity. Most jobs complete within a few hours. The exception is
\mathtt{ARL}_{0}
simulation for BOCPDMS, which requires on the order of days due to its increased computational cost.
