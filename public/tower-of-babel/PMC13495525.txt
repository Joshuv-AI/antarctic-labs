β3-adrenergic blockade targets fatty acid oxidation to induce ferroptotic vulnerability in pediatric T-ALL
Authors: Cristina Banella, Serena Travaglini, Francesco Carrozzo, Agnese Roveta, Francesco Pegoraro, Gianluca Mattei, Rachele Amato, Maria Ascone, Federica De Luca, Aurora Chinnici, Cinzia Marchi, Elena Chiocca, Annalisa Tondo, Marinella Veltroni, Maura Calvani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPediatric T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) accounts for approximately 15% of childhood ALL. It is associated with a high risk of relapse, with ~25% of patients failing conventional therapy. Resistance is driven by pro-survival signaling, impaired apoptosis, and metabolic adaptations that sustain leukemic proliferation under stress. Herein, we investigate the role of β3-adrenergic receptor (β3-AR) antagonist SR59230A signaling in the metabolic reprogramming and therapeutic vulnerability of pediatric T-ALL. β3-AR expression and transcriptomic profiling following SR59230A exposure were assessed in T-ALL cell lines by RNA sequencing, followed by gene set enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology and Hallmark pathways. Metabolic alterations were validated by Seahorse analyses of mitochondrial respiration, glycolysis, fatty acid oxidation (FAO), and fuel dependency. Systemic iron metabolism was evaluated by ferritin and free iron quantification using COBAS8000. β3-AR was markedly upregulated in T-ALL cells compared with normal hematopoietic counterparts, identifying a selective metabolic vulnerability. Pharmacologic inhibition of β3-AR with SR59230A affected mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, predominantly complex I, and suppressed FAO. The metabolic collapse disrupted bioenergetic flexibility and triggered ferroptotic cell death. This was accompanied by modulation of ferritin and transferrin levels, suggesting their potential role as biomarkers of metabolic response. Importantly, β3-AR blockade sensitized T-ALL cells to oxidative phosphorylation inhibition, resulting in synergistic cytotoxicity in refractory models. Collectively, these findings identify β3-AR as a central regulator of metabolic plasticity in pediatric T-ALL highlighting metabolic and iron-dependent vulnerabilities as potential combined targets for high-risk disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00850-z.Key Words: Acute lymphoblastic leukemia, Metabolism, Ferroptosis, Fatty acid oxidation, Adrenergic receptor

Introduction

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) represents the most common malignancy in childhood, with T-cell ALL (T-ALL) accounting for approximately 10–15% of cases [1]. Despite historically considered an aggressive subtype, the survival rate has improved over the years, with a 5-year event-free survival of 70–80% in recent patients treated with MRD-guided intensive therapy [2–4]. This progress reflects improved multi-agent therapies, refined risk stratification, early treatment response assessment, and advances in supportive care. Nevertheless, treatment resistance remains a major clinical challenge. Approximately 25% of pediatric T-ALL patients relapse, often with a more aggressive and treatment-resistant disease, resulting in a very poor prognosis [5–7]. Resistance arises from multiple mechanisms, including alterations of drug targets, pro-survival signaling, defective cell death induction, and metabolic adaptation of leukemic cells [8–11]. Therefore, the identification of novel therapeutic targets might enhance the efficacy of current treatments and help to tailor therapeutic intervention to overcome resistance.

In recent years, increasing evidence has highlighted cellular metabolism as a key regulator of leukemic cell survival, transformation, and disease progression. Metabolic adaptations allow leukemic cells to survive therapeutic pressure and dynamic microenvironmental conditions, supporting tumor maintenance and relapse [12–15]. Accordingly, metabolic targeting strategies have been investigated in hematologic malignancies. In acute myeloid leukemia (AML), IDH1/2 inhibitors [16–18] and venetoclax [19–21] have validated metabolic targeting as a therapeutic strategy. Venetoclax also demonstrates preclinical activity in T-ALL [22–27], highlighting the relevance of mitochondrial metabolism in this disease. Although metabolism-targeting agents such as antifolates are central to ALL therapy, the broader metabolic landscape underlying treatment response and resistance remains poorly defined [28].

Among emerging metabolic regulators, the β3-adrenergic receptor (β3-AR) is increasingly linked to the regulation of lipid metabolism and glucose homeostasis in cancer cells. β3-AR signaling has been implicated in several aspects of malignant transformation, including cell proliferation, apoptosis, chemotaxis, angiogenesis, tumor growth, and modulation of the immune response [29–33]. Notably, a selective β3-AR antagonist (SR59230A) has shown direct anti-leukemic activity by promoting apoptosis, particularly under hypoxic conditions [34]. In this study, we aimed to characterize the functional role of β3-AR in the pathogenesis and treatment response of pediatric T-ALL. We investigated β3-AR expression in T-ALL models and primary samples, assessed the impact of β3-AR inhibition on cell viability, and characterized associated metabolic alterations using transcriptomic and functional analyses. By elucidating the contribution of β3-AR–driven metabolic plasticity, this work provides new insights into previously underexplored metabolic vulnerabilities in pediatric T-ALL. These findings may uncover how leukemic cells escape current therapeutic strategies and support the development of personalized approaches to overcome drug resistance in pediatric T-ALL.

Materials and methods

Primary patients samples and controls

The bone marrow (BM) sample was collected from a patient with newly diagnosed de novo T-ALL (Supplemental Table 1) admitted at the Meyer Children’s Hospital IRCCS in Florence, following a collection of informed consent from parents under ethical committee approval (CET_14/2023). Age matched of 14 healthy donor Normal Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells (PBMCs), were used as controls.

Cell lines and cell culture

CCRF-CEM, Molt-3, and Jurkat human T-cell leukemia lines derived from a pediatric T-ALL exhibiting an immature T-cell phenotype were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) (Manassas, VA, USA). Cell details are listed in supplemental Methods (Table 2).

Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Euroclone, Pero, MI, Italy) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; GIBCO-BRL), 20 mM HEPES, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (GIBCO-BRL). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Drug treatments

Cells were treated with SR59230A (10–50 µM for 24, 48 0 r 72 h, or with RLS3 0,3 µM for 3 h) to induce ferroptosis. Where indicated, cells were also exposed to the iron chelator deferoxamine (DFX) 0,1 µM, alone or in combination with SR.

Cell viability

A CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay was used to assess cell viability. T-ALL cells were seeded in 96-well plates (1.5–2 × 10 4 cells/well) and treated with SR59230A (10–50 µM) for 24, 48, or 72 h. For the MTS-based assay, 5 µL of reagent was added and absorbance measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific™ Varioskan™ Flash Multimode Reader, Waltham, MA, USA).

Protein extraction and Western blot analysis

Cell pellets were lysed in RIPA buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to membranes, and probed with antibodies listed in supplemental Methods (Table 3). Detection was performed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and chemiluminescence. Signals were quantified with ImageJ and normalized to total protein per lane. Full experimental details are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Cytofluorimetric analysis

The markings were performed using 0.5 × 106 cells resuspended in a volume of 100 µL of current buffer (PBS + 2% FBS + 0.5% EDTA 500 mM). Cells were labeled with anti-β3-PE (Biorbyt Cat# orb124479, RRID:AB_2783863). The analysis of the samples was performed using MACSQuant Analyzer 10 Flow Cytometer (Miltenyi Biotec, Gladbach, Germany). Data were processed using Flowlogic software (Miltenyi Biotec, Gladbach, Germany).

Metabolic assays

T-ALL cell lines and primary blasts were seeded in precoated XFe96 plates and treated with drugs as indicated. Mitochondrial respiration, glycolysis, ATP production, and fuel flexibility were assessed using the Seahorse XFe96 analyzer (Agilent Technologies). Detailed protocols, drug concentrations, and assay parameters are listed in Supplemental Methods.

Quantitative real-time expression analysis

Quantitative Reverse Transcription-Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR) was used to evaluate the expression level of GPX4, FPS1 and NRF2 (Table SM 5). See supplemental Methods for detail.

RNA sequencing

Transcriptome sequencing was performed by BGI Genomics (BGI, China). See supplemental Methods for detail.

Cobas 8000 analyzer assays

Ferritin quantification was performed using the Cobas® 8000 e602 module with electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) technology (Elecsys Ferritin, ref. 07027273190). Free iron was measured photometrically on the c702 module at 700/570 nm using the FerroZine method (IRON2, Iron Gen2, ref. 05169291190).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software INc., San Diego, CA, USA). Data were presented as the mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments, and each experiment was performed in triplicate. Statistical analyses were performed using the unpaired two-tailed t-test with Welch’s correction, ordinary one-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s, Tukey’s or Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test or two-way ANOVA followed by Dunett’s multiple comparisons test, as indicated. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05.

Results

The β3-adrenergic receptor is highly expressed in T-ALL and represents a selective therapeutic vulnerability

To characterize β3-AR expression in T-ALL, we performed flow cytometric analysis in CCRF-CEM, Molt-3, and Jurkat cell lines and compared receptor levels with healthy PBMCs. T-ALL cell lines displayed significantly higher β3-AR expression than normal PBMCs (Fig. 1A), with β3-AR positivity of approximately 65% in CCRF-CEM, 40% in Molt-3, 35% in Jurkat cells, and only ~5% in control PBMCs.

We subsequently evaluated the therapeutic potential of the selective β3-AR antagonist SR59230A. Increasing concentrations of SR59230A (0–50 µM) for 24, 48, and 72 hours induced a significant reduction in cell viability in T-ALL cells, in a dose- and time-dependent fashion, while normal PBMCs were unaffected under the same conditions (Fig. 1B), indicating selective cytotoxicity toward leukemic cells. Notably, the anti-leukemic activity of SR59230A was further enhanced under fetal bovine serum (FBS)-deprived conditions, consistent with a role for β3-AR in fatty acid metabolism and bioenergetics (Fig. 1C).

To further investigate SR59230A specificity, we measured adrenergic receptor family members following treatment (15 µM) for 24, 48, and 72 hours. Despite SR59230A shows higher affinity for β3-AR (IC50 ≈ 40 nM), it retains measurable activity toward β1 and β2 isoforms (IC50 ≈ 408 nM and ≈648 nM, respectively). No significant changes in ADRB1 or ADRB2 transcripts were observed at any time point (Fig. 1D–E). In contrast, ADRB3 was significantly upregulated in CCRF-CEM cells at 24 hours (p < 0.01), in Jurkat cells at 24 and 48 hours (p < 0.001 and p < 0.01, respectively), and in Molt-3 cells at 48 hours (p < 0.0001), while decreasing in Molt-3 at 72 h (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1F).

At the protein level, β3-AR expression was significantly modulated in all three T-ALL cell lines following SR exposure, in agreement with transcriptional changes and consistent with ligand-induced receptor downregulation (Fig. 1G and Fig S3C-E). While changes in receptor expression do not prove isoform-selective inhibition, it supports preferential engagement of the β3-AR axis in T-ALL cells.

SR59230A triggers convergent mitochondrial suppression and metabolic rewiring in T-ALL

To investigate the transcriptional consequences of SR59230A, we performed RNAseq in T-ALL models after 24 h of treatment with 15 µM SR59230A. DESeq2 analysis revealed distinct transcriptional responses across the three cell lines (Fig S1A). GSEA of Hallmark pathways revealed a partially overlapping metabolic response together with lineage-specific response to SR59230A. Several pathways related to stress response, cellular signaling, and metabolic regulation were differentially modulated across the three models, reflecting context-dependent remodeling of transcriptional programs (Fig. 2A).

Rnaseq analysis following β3-AR inhibition in T-ALL cell lines. Hallmark pathway enrichment analysis (GSEA) across the three T-ALL cell lines. Box plots display normalized enrichment scores (NES) of significantly modulated pathways. (B) Schematic representation of the mitochondrial respiratory chain highlighting leading edge genes contributing to OXPHOS pathway enrichment. (C) Venn diagram illustrating the overlap of DEGs across the three cell lines upon β3-AR inhibition (filtering criteria: p-value < 0.05; FDR < 0.1)

Oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) was the only metabolic pathway consistently downregulated in all three cell lines, with a stronger decrease in CCRF-CEM and Molt-3 cells than in Jurkat. This attenuated transcriptional response in Jurkat may reflect lower β3-AR expression and intrinsic metabolic flexibility. Fig S1B shows the databases GO Biological Processes, GO Cellular Components, and Hallmarks related to the mitochondria that are commonly upregulated in CCRF-CEM and Molt-3 cells, highlighting the stronger transcriptional response in these lines. Overall, these data reveal a convergent inhibition of mitochondrial respiration with lineage-specific metabolic adaptations.

To further dissect OXPHOS signature, we examined leading edge genes contributing to pathway enrichment. Shared components included multiple subunits of mitochondrial respiratory chain Complex I (NDUFA1, NDUFA2, NDUFA3, NDUFA4, NDUFA6, NDUFB1, NDUFB2, NDUFB3, NDUFB4, NDUFB6, NDUFAB1, and NDUFS4), Complex III (UQCRB, UQCRQ, UQCR10, and UQCRFS1), Complex IV (COX5B, COX6A1, COX6C, COX7A2, COX7B, COX7C, COX8A, and COX17), and ATP synthase subunits (ATP5F1E, ATP5ME, ATP5MG, ATP5PF, and ATP5PO) (Fig. 2B), together with genes involved in mitochondrial transport, redox homeostasis (VDAC2, SLC25A5, MPC1, TIMM8B, PRDX3, and GPX4), protein import and iron–sulfur cluster biology (HSPA9, FXN, and ISCA1). This profile indicates that SR59230A affects the structural and functional integrity of the entire mitochondrial respiratory machinery.

Applying refined filtering criteria (p-value < 0.05 and FDR < 0.1), we identified 6 Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) shared by all the cell lines (Fig. 2C), including three upregulated genes (KLHL24, MFGE8, SLC5A3) and three downregulated genes (ALAS1, THOC6, RGL4) (Fig. 2D). Notably, ALAS1, a key regulator of heme biosynthesis, was part of the OXPHOS leading-edge subset, supporting mitochondrial and iron-related metabolic involvement, while genes such as SLC5A3 suggest the activation of adaptive stress responses.

Together, these data demonstrate that SR59230A induces a convergent suppression of OXPHOS across T-ALL models, alongside context-dependent metabolic rewiring.

SR59230A selectively impairs mitochondrial and glycolytic metabolism in T-ALL cells models

To functionally validate the metabolic impact of SR59230A, we assessed mitochondrial and glycolytic functions using Seahorse metabolic assays.

SR59230A significantly reduced basal respiration (Fig. 3A), maximal respiration (Fig. 3B), and spare respiratory capacity (Fig. 3C), leading to a marked decrease in ATP production rate (Fig. 3D) in all three leukemic models, indicating broad impairment of mitochondrial bioenergetic capacity upon SR59230A administration.

We subsequently evaluated the impact of SR59230A on glycolytic metabolism. SR59230A significantly reduced basal glycolysis in CCRF-CEM and Molt-3 cells (p < 0.001 and p < 0.0001, respectively), whereas Jurkat cells displayed increased basal glycolysis (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3E). Similarly, compensatory glycolysis was reduced in CCRF-CEM and Molt-3 cells, while no significant changes were detected in Jurkat cells (Fig. 3F). These findings indicate that SR59230A selectively disrupts metabolic homeostasis in leukemic cells, without affecting normal PBMCs, and identify Jurkat cells as the least sensitive model, likely because compensatory glycolysis partially offsets mitochondrial ATP loss.

The same analyses were performed in PMBCs from ten healthy donors. Following 24 hours of 15 µM SR59230A, a modest reduction in basal respiration was observed (p < 0.01) (Fig S2A) with no changes in maximal respiration (Fig S2B), spare respiratory capacity (Fig S2C), or total ATP production (Fig S2D). SR59230A also resulted in a significant reduction in proton leak (p < 0.01) (Fig S1E), suggestive of improved mitochondrial efficiency and reduced uncoupled proton efflux (Fig S2F). No significant alterations were observed in glycolytic parameters (Fig S2G–I) or in mitochondrial and glycolytic ATP production rates (Fig S2J-L).

SR59230A impairs mitochondrial bioenergetics in primary T-ALL blasts: a proof-of-concept analysis

To assess the translational relevance of our findings, we next investigated the effects of SR59230A in primary T-ALL blasts isolated from the bone marrow of a pediatric patient at diagnosis. Treatment with SR59230A (15 µM) for 24 hours significantly reduced basal respiration (p < 0.001), maximal respiration (p < 0.001), spare respiratory capacity (p < 0.01), and mitochondrial ATP production (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4A–D). In contrast, SR59230A treatment resulted in a significant increase in basal glycolysis (p < 0.0001), with no detectable changes in compensatory glycolysis, and a concomitant reduction in the proton efflux rate (PER) derived from glycolysis (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4E–G). ATP Rate Assay analysis showed that the reduction in mitochondrial ATP production (Fig. 4H) was only partially compensated by the increased glycolytic ATP generation (Fig. 4I), leading to a significant decrease in total cellular ATP levels. These findings provide preliminary evidence that SR59230A can induce a bioenergetic deficit in primary T-ALL blasts, potentially not fully compensated by glycolytic reprogramming.

SR59230A disrupts fatty acid oxidation and exposes T-ALL to a metabolic vulnerability

Considering the marked impairment of mitochondrial respiration following SR59230A treatment, we next assessed whether this bioenergetic defect was driven by altered fatty acid oxidation (FAO).

Using the XF Long Chain Fatty Acid Oxidation Stress Test under starvation conditions to promote fatty acid uptake and HEPES (1 M) to limit extracellular acidification, we observed that SR59230A significantly reduced basal and maximal respiration, spare respiratory capacity, and mitochondrial ATP production across all three T-ALL cell lines, both in BSA and palmitate-supplemented conditions (Fig. 5A–C).

The comparable reduction in OCR in both settings indicates that SR59230A impairs oxidation of both endogenous and exogenous fatty acids, providing a mechanistic basis for the mitochondrial dysfunction observed in Fig. 3.

SR59230A couples fatty acid oxidation blockade to ferroptotic cell death in T-ALL

Because FAO suppression can promote the accumulation of peroxidation-prone lipids, we evaluated whether SR59230A-mediated inhibition of FAO and iron release create a permissive environment for ferroptosis in T-ALL cells. Consistent with this model, transcript analysis revealed a significant downregulation of key ferroptosis-protective genes, including GPX4, FSP1, and NRF2, after 72 h of SR59230A treatment in CCRF-CEM and Molt-3 cells (Fig. 6A–C). No significant changes were observed in Jurkat cells (Fig. 6C).

At the protein level, ferritin heavy chain (FTH1) expression remained largely unchanged in CCRF-CEM and Jurkat cells, whereas Molt-3 cells exhibited a transient increase at 24 and 48 hours of treatment (Fig. 6D and Fig S4D-G). In parallel, the transferrin receptor CD71 (TFRC), a key mediator of cellular iron uptake and a determinant of intracellular iron availability, was significantly downregulated in CCRF-CEM and Molt-3 cells after 48 and 72 hours of SR59230A treatment, while it was upregulated in Jurkat cells (Fig. 6D and Fig S4H-L). In support of increased cellular damage, lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) expression, while not specific for ferroptosis, was significantly increased in CCRF-CEM and Molt-3 cells, but remained unchanged in Jurkat cells, further underscoring the heightened sensitivity of CCRF-CEM and Molt-3 to SR59230A -induced metabolic perturbation (Fig. 6E and Fig S5D-F).

Intracellular ferritin and free iron levels were quantified using the COBAS 8000 clinical diagnostic platform [35–38], routinely employed in standard patient care. Following assay optimization, treatment with the ferroptosis inducer RSL3 (0.3 µM for 3 hours) caused a significant reduction in ferritin levels across T-ALL models, confirming the suitability of this platform for detecting ferroptosis-associated alterations in iron homeostasis (Fig. 6F). We next assessed ferritin levels following treatment with 15 µM SR59230A or the iron chelator deferoxamine (DFX; 0.1 µM) for 24 h. Both treatments resulted in a significant reduction in ferritin levels in CCRF-CEM and Molt-3 cells, whereas Jurkat cells remained unaffected, consistent with their lower ferroptotic susceptibility and reduced basal FTH1 expression (Fig. 6G).

Given the sensitivity of CCRF-CEM cells, we next performed a time-course analysis of FTH1 and iron homeostasis following SR59230A treatment to directly assess whether SR59230A progressively perturbs intracellular iron handling. We observed a progressive, time-dependent decrease in ferritin levels (Fig. 6H), accompanied by a concomitant increase in intracellular free iron (Fig. 6I).

To further define the engagement of ferroptotic signaling, GPX4 expression was evaluated in all three T-ALL models treated with SR59230A (15 µM) for 24, 48, and 72 hours. A significant reduction of GPX4 protein levels was observed in CCRF-CEM cells at 24 hours (p < 0.01), which became more pronounced at 72 hours (p < 0.0001), whereas the other models showed no consistent modulation (Fig. 6J and Fig S3F-H), underscoring the selective ferroptotic vulnerability of CCRF-CEM cells.

Finally, to functionally validate ferroptosis involvement, we evaluated whether iron chelation could mitigate SR59230A-induced cytotoxicity. Although DFX did not completely rescue cell viability under standard culture conditions (Fig. 6K), it partially reversed SR59230A-mediated loss of viability under nutrient-starved conditions after 24 hours of treatment (Fig. 6L) [39]. Interestingly, the analysis of deregulated genes in the ferroptosis pathway within T-ALL cell line revealed markedly elevated expression of genes promoting iron metabolism-dependent ferroptosis, such as TF, TFRC, STEAP3, and FTL in CCRF-CEM and Molt-3 (Fig. 6M), as observed in AML primary blasts [39].

Taken together, these data indicate that SR59230A induces a ferroptosis-prone state through coordinated disruption of FAO, iron homeostasis, and GPX4-dependent antioxidant defenses.

Discussion

Despite recent improvements in survival rates, approximately 25% of children with T-ALL experience relapse. Relapsed T-ALL is typically associated with a more aggressive clinical course and poor prognosis, highlighting a clinical need for reliable molecular markers and novel therapeutic targets capable of selectively exploiting vulnerabilities in leukemic cells.

Our work investigates the β3-AR as a previously underexplored regulator of metabolic plasticity in pediatric T-ALL. Β3-AR is robustly expressed in pediatric T-ALL models, and pharmacologic targeting of the β- adrenergic axis with SR59230A reveals metabolic liability. Notably, although SR59230A is widely used as a β3-AR antagonist, pharmacological studies indicate limited subtype selectivity at human β-adrenoceptors, with measurable affinity also for β1- and β2-adrenergic receptors [40–43]. Accordingly, in this study SR59230A should be considered as a pharmacological probe of the β-adrenergic axis rather than a fully selective β3-AR inhibitor. While our data support a predominant involvement of the β3-AR pathway, contributions from other adrenergic receptor subtypes cannot be excluded. Thus, the results are best interpreted as reflecting preferential engagement of β3-AR–associated signaling. Future studies will address the specific contribution of individual receptor subtypes. In this scenario, SR59230A induces OXPHOS transcriptional reprogramming leading to a functional collapse of mitochondrial bioenergetics, with reduced mitochondrial ATP production and FAO. SR59230A significantly affects mitochondrial respiration in CCRF-CEM and Molt-3 cells while sparing healthy counterparts. In leukemic models, substrate-specific analyses reveal that SR59230A markedly diminishes FAO, generating energetic stress that translates into heightened metabolic vulnerability. Conversely, Jurkat cells displayed relative resistance to SR59230A-induced metabolic and ferroptotic stress. This phenotype may reflect lower β3-AR expression and enhanced metabolic flexibility, allowing compensation for mitochondrial impairment through alternative substrates such as glucose and glutamine, a recognized mechanism of therapeutic resistance. These features suggest a metabolic phenotype characterized by reduced dependence on β3-AR–associated signaling and increased adaptability to bioenergetic stress, potentially limiting the effectiveness of β3-AR–targeting strategies in similar T-ALL contexts.Real-time bioenergetic profiling and RNA-sequencing identify respiratory complex I as the main mitochondrial target of SR59230A. Complex I transcripts are consistently deregulated across all T-ALL models, underscoring coordinated mitochondrial disruption. This is highly relevant given increasing evidence that complex I inhibition (e.g., IACS-010759, EVT-701, mubritinib, or phenformin) reduces OXPHOS, rewires mitochondrial metabolism, and enhances chemosensitivity in AML, including increased cytarabine sensitivity and reduced PD-L1 and CD39 expression [44–46]. In defined genetic contexts, complex I inhibition reveals specific vulnerabilities: increased susceptibility in IDH1-mutant AML and reversal of oncogene-driven metabolic reprogramming in NOTCH1-mutant T-ALL [47, 48]. Consistently, complex I inhibitors such as metformin and intervenolin suppress tumor growth in solid tumor xenografts through tumor acidification and S6K1 dephosphorylation, independently of ATP depletion [49, 50]. Our previous work in AML demonstrated that OXPHOS-high cells preferentially survive chemotherapy, and that buformin, a potent metformin analog targeting complex I, suppresses this high-OXPHOS compartment [51]. The MCL1/HK2 axis further illustrates how leukemia cells coordinate mitochondrial and glycolytic programs to endure metabolic stress [52].

Within this framework, SR59230A disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis in T-ALL models, primarily targeting complex I and impairing OXPHOS. Metabolic analyses confirmed reduced basal respiration and spare respiratory capacity. FAO-dependent CCRF-CEM cells were particularly sensitive, whereas metabolically flexible Jurkat cells showed relative resistance, indicating that baseline fuel dependency shapes response to SR59230A. This metabolic collapse is accompanied by altered iron homeostasis, including progressive ferritin reduction and accumulation of labile iron detected by the clinically validated COBAS 8000 platform, together with downregulation of key ferroptosis defenses (GPX4, FSP1 and NRF2). Collectively, these changes create a redox and lipid metabolic landscape permissive to ferroptosis, driving SR59230A-induced leukemic cell death.

Clinically, dysregulated iron metabolism and expanded labile iron pools promote oxidative stress in acute leukemias. In ALL, reliance on antioxidant systems such as glutathione and FSP1 creates ferroptotic vulnerability that can be therapeutically exploited by iron modulation (e.g., deferoxamine, deferasirox) or inhibition of SLC7A11 and GPX4 [39, 53–56]. Similar approaches in AML and solid tumors further support iron-dependent lipid peroxidation as a therapeutically actionable axis [57–59]. In our model, SR59230A disrupts FAO, mitochondrial respiration, and iron homeostasis, promoting lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic cell death as therapeutically exploitable vulnerability.

Modulation of the transferrin receptor (CD71) further underscores the complexity of iron handling under metabolic stress. Although reduced CD71, SR59230A-sensitive cells show FTH1 depletion and labile iron accumulation, linking diminished iron import to ferroptotic vulnerability via mitochondrial dysfunction and lipid peroxidation [60, 60–62]. Partial rescue by iron chelation supports this mechanism and highlights ferritin-dependent iron buffering as a therapeutically actionable axis. Consistently, reduced FTH1 expression sensitizes leukemic stem cells to iron-targeting interventions and mitochondrial injury, thereby identifying ferritin-dependent iron buffering as a therapeutically exploitable axis [63–66].

Importantly, integration of clinically validated iron parameters into our framework establishes a direct translational bridge, enabling extension of these mechanistic insights to patient-derived samples explored in a clinical context.

Clinically, the use of an automated COBAS 8000 platform anchors these mechanistic findings in diagnostically accessible metrics. Although the COBAS 8000 platform is primarily validated for serum and plasma analytes, we employed it here as an exploratory strategy to obtain clinically relatable readouts of intracellular ferritin and iron changes. This may represent a useful translational direction, particularly in the context of increasing interest in ferroptosis-based vulnerabilities in resistant cancer cells. Because cell lysates are a non-standard matrix for this platform, these measurements were interpreted in comparison with matched control samples processed in parallel. Further matrix-specific work will be useful to define assay performance in lysates more precisely.

However, our findings are largely based on established T-ALL cell line models, which may not fully capture disease heterogeneity and microenvironmental context, and the number of patient-derived samples is limited. In addition, while SR59230A was used to interrogate β3-AR function, potential off-target effects cannot be excluded. These aspects will be the focus of future studies.

In conclusion, SR59230A induces a metabolic crisis in pediatric T-ALL characterized by complex I–driven OXPHOS disruption, impaired FAO, iron homeostasis perturbation, and suppression of ferroptosis defense. The convergence of mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid metabolic stress, and iron dysregulation sensitizes leukemic cells to ferroptotic death, positioning B3AR blockade as a potential metabolic-oriented therapeutic strategy in pediatric T-ALL.
