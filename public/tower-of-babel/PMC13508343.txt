MuSA: a Nextflow pipeline for deep, reproducible annotation and clinical ranking of genomic variants
Authors: D Scognamiglio, E Bonetti, A Moroni, L Sangiorgi, E Pedrini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate clinical interpretation of genetic variants requires integration of functional predictions, evolutionary constraint, population allele frequencies, and clinical evidence from heterogeneous resources. Conventional workflows based on standalone tools such as ensembl variant effect predictor (VEP) and ANNOVAR require complex manual configuration of plugins and databases, generate verbose transcript-level outputs unsuitable for clinical review, and rely on ad hoc scripts for format conversion and prioritization. These limitations hinder reproducibility and scalability, making data interpretation a major bottleneck in genomic medicine.ResultsWe present MuSA (Multi-Source variant Annotation), an nf-core–compliant Nextflow pipeline that automates germline variant annotation from resource setup to clinical interpretation. MuSA supports both a streamlined basic mode for diagnostic workflows and an extended deep-annotation mode for comprehensive analyses. The pipeline integrates Ensembl VEP with 22 curated plugins (including AlphaMissense, CADD, SpliceAI, and Enformer), ANNOVAR, a standalone pre-configured dbNSFP distribution, the RENOVO pathogenicity predictor, and automated ACMG/AMP classification via GeneBe and InterVar. MuSA standardizes input VCFs, executes parallel annotation branches in a fully containerized workflow, and consolidates results into richly annotated mutation annotation format (MAF) files (up to 920 columns per variant), alongside interactive HTML reports tailored for clinical review with HPO-matched gene panels. Benchmarked on a WES-like dataset of 22,705 variants derived from the public GIAB NA12878/HG001 GRCh38 benchmark VCF, MuSA completes full extended-mode annotation in approximately 20 min on a 64-core server. Systematic comparison with nf-core/sarek and nf-core/variantprioritization demonstrates that MuSA uniquely combines automated resource management with YAML-based version tracking and SHA-256 integrity verification, native dbNSFP integration, RENOVO-based VUS prioritization, HPO-driven gene panel filtering, and a clinically oriented interactive HTML report; those features are mostly absent in existing nf-core annotation pipelines. Containerization through Docker/Singularity and predefined execution profiles support reproducible deployment across workstations, HPC clusters, and cloud environments.ConclusionsMuSA provides an end-to-end framework for clinically oriented germline variant annotation and prioritization, addressing key limitations of manual and general-purpose workflows. Its dual-output design bridges research (machine-readable MAF files compatible with downstream tools such as maftools) and clinical diagnostics (interpretation-ready HTML reports), supporting reproducible and standardized variant interpretation across teams. Current limitations include restriction to germline small variants on hg38, a substantial storage footprint (up to 223.5 GB for extended mode), and dependence on external APIs for ACMG/AMP classification and phenotype-driven filtering. The RENOVO-based VUS prioritization module is experimental and requires expert review before clinical interpretation.Keywords: Variant annotation, Clinical genomics, Nextflow pipeline, ACMG/AMP guidelines, Nf-core community

Background

The challenge of variant interpretation

The exponential growth in genomic sequencing has shifted the primary bottleneck in precision medicine from data generation to data interpretation [1]. Accurate clinical interpretation of genetic variants requires synthesizing information from diverse sources: predicted functional consequences, evolutionary constraint metrics, population allele frequencies, splice-impact predictions, regulatory annotations, and curated clinical evidence [2]. However, integrating these heterogeneous resources into a unified, reproducible workflow remains technically challenging.

Limitations of current annotation approaches

The Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) [3] and ANNOVAR [4] are established annotation tools, yet their deployment in clinical and large-scale settings presents substantial limitations. VEP plugin installation is complex and error-prone, often requiring manual compilation of dependencies [5]. Default VEP configurations produce highly verbose, transcript-level outputs that demand extensive post-processing before clinical review. ANNOVAR simplifies database management but collapses all transcript annotations into single rows without MANE [6] (Matched Annotation from NCBI and EMBL-EBI) transcript designation [6], limiting clinical utility. Neither tool natively integrates comprehensive pathogenicity prediction models like RENOVO [7], nor do they provide automated ACMG/AMP [2] evidence assignment or phenotype-driven prioritization.

Most clinical genomics workflows rely on homemade scripts to bridge these gaps—custom code for VCF preprocessing, format conversion, frequency filtering, gene panel matching, and report generation. Such ad-hoc solutions are institution-specific, poorly documented, and difficult to reproduce or scale across heterogeneous computational environments [8]. Furthermore, the absence of workflow management systems in many pipelines limits parallelization, resource optimization, and deployment portability [8].

The need for workflow-based annotation

Nextflow’s dataflow model supports implicit parallelization and native container support [9] (Docker [10] and Singularity [11]), ensuring reproducibility and isolation of software dependencies. The nf-core community initiative [12] provides curated, validated pipelines following stringent best practices, facilitating collaborative development and institutional adoption.

However, existing Nextflow pipelines in genomics have focused primarily on upstream processing—alignment, quality control, and variant calling (e.g., nf-core/sarek [13])—with limited attention to comprehensive variant annotation and clinical prioritization. No current pipeline simultaneously addresses: (1) automated setup of annotation resources; (2) integration of deep functional annotation sources; (3) native incorporation of machine-learning pathogenicity predictors; (4) automated ACMG/AMP evidence assignment for germline pathogenicity variant prediction/prioritization; (5) phenotype-driven filtering; and (6) dual output formats serving both research (cohort-level MAF files) and clinical (interpretation-ready HTML reports) needs.

A further deliberate design choice in MuSA is the adoption of the Mutation Annotation Format (MAF) as the primary consolidated output, even though the current version of the pipeline is restricted to germline variants. MAF was originally popularized in somatic cancer genomics, but it offers several practical advantages that translate directly to germline diagnostics and research: it enforces a one-row-per-variant representation with canonical transcript selection, reducing the transcript-level redundancy that characterizes VCF-based VEP output and simplifying manual review; it provides a stable, column-based schema that is naturally suited to integration of hundreds of heterogeneous annotations (population frequencies, pathogenicity predictors, splice scores, clinical evidence) and to export into tabular formats for downstream statistical or machine-learning analyses; it provides a sample-aware, row-based schema, with each record carrying a sample identifier, that enables straightforward downstream cohort-level aggregation: per-sample MAFs produced by MuSA can be merged without format conversion and are directly compatible with tools such as maftools, which support cohort-level summaries and variant spectrum analyses; such operations are considerably more laborious to perform directly on multi-sample VCFs, which require explicit parsing of FORMAT fields and are sensitive to genotype encoding conventions. By standardizing on MAF, MuSA thus provides a uniform representation that is immediately usable for germline diagnostic workflows, directly compatible with existing somatic-variant tooling, and naturally extensible to future releases of the pipeline that will include somatic and structural variant annotation. In other words, choosing MAF now avoids locking the pipeline into a germline-only data model and ensures that today’s germline-centric use cases can seamlessly evolve into broader, mixed-cohort analyses.

Furthermore, the increasing adoption of ACMG/AMP guidelines, the availability of large-scale population datasets (e.g., gnomAD [14] v4.1 with 730,947 exomes and 76,215 genomes), and the emergence of machine-learning pathogenicity predictors such as RENOVO create opportunities for more comprehensive and automated annotation strategies.

MuSA: a dual-purpose solution

To address these gaps, we developed MuSA (Multi-Source variant Annotation), a Nextflow pipeline that provides both:

Streamlined basic annotation for standardized diagnostic workflows requiring core clinical information (transcript and protein consequences, population frequencies, ClinVar[15] classifications, PubMed references, basic clinical evidences)

Streamlined basic annotation for standardized diagnostic workflows requiring core clinical information (transcript and protein consequences, population frequencies, ClinVar[15] classifications, PubMed references, basic clinical evidences)

Ultra-deep extended annotation for biomarker discovery, complex case resolution, and machine-learning applications, incorporating 22 curated VEP plugins, native pre-configured dbNSFP, RENOVO pathogenicity scoring, and comprehensive regulatory annotations [16].

MuSA is nf-core–compliant, adopting the community’s modular architecture for process atomicity, configuration management, and extensibility. The pipeline fully automates resource setup (including VEP caches, ANNOVAR databases, and the dbNSFP distribution), orchestrates parallel annotation branches within containerized processes, and produces both machine-readable MAF files (up to 920 columns) and interactive HTML reports with HPO [17]-matched gene panels (Fig. 1). This dual-output design bridges the gap between research-oriented cohort analyses and clinical interpretation, enabling systematic computational analyses while providing clinicians with concise, evidence-rich candidate lists.

Implementation

Pipeline architecture

MuSA is implemented in Nextflow (v25.10.2) using modular processes connected by dataflow channels, enabling parallel execution across samples and annotation steps. Each module wraps a single tool or script (e.g., VEP, ANNOVAR, bcftools, GATK) within a Docker or Singularity container to ensure reproducibility. Execution profiles support local and HPC environments (e.g., SLURM, PBS, SGE).

The pipeline follows nf-core principles, with modular, reusable components that can be easily extended or replaced [12]. MuSA adopts a multi-container design (Table 1), where each functional module runs in a dedicated, version-pinned image, ensuring consistent behavior across environments and compatibility with both Docker and Singularity/Apptainer.

The pipeline is organized into two main workflows: a setup workflow for resource preparation and an annotate workflow for variant processing and interpretation.

The setup workflow automates the download and configuration of external resources required for variant annotation. Users can select a basic subworkflow, which retrieves core databases for routine clinical diagnostics, or an extended subworkflow, which additionally fetches curated VEP plugins and supplementary resources for comprehensive variant characterization.

Basic mode provides essential clinical information with minimal computational overhead, making it suitable for high-throughput diagnostic laboratories requiring standardized variant interpretation.

It has to be noted that MuSA is, to our knowledge, the first Nextflow pipeline to integrate a native, pre‑configured dbNSFP distribution, eliminating the manual database preparation and custom scripting typically required for dbNSFP annotation outside VEP. The dbNSFP branch annotates variants with extensive functional predictions, conservation metrics, and gene‑level annotations from more than one hundred computational tools, thereby providing a dense feature space for downstream prioritization and machine‑learning applications, including: (i) ensemble predictors such as MetaSVM [19] and REVEL [20]; (ii) classical deleteriousness scores such as SIFT [21] and PolyPhen‑2 [22]; (iii) newer or deep‑learning‑based models such as AlphaMissense; and (iv) conservation metrics such as phyloP [23] and GERP++ [24].

Extended resources include: (i) pathogenicity and functional-effect plugins (for example AlphaMissense and CADD [25]); (ii) splicing-impact plugins (for example SpliceAI [26]); (iii) regulatory and non-coding context plugins (for example Enformer and UTRAnnotator); (iv) conservation and ancestry plugins (for example AncestralAllele); and (v) experimental and utility plugins (for example MaveDB-based scores and SpliceRegion), with the full plugin list and versions summarized in Table 2.

MuSA’s basic and advanced resources

The modular design allows users to choose between basic and extended configurations to balance computational resource usage (total storage footprint: 223.5 GB for extended mode) with annotation depth. Extended mode is designed for research cohorts requiring high-dimensional variant annotation, producing a pre-formatted tabular feature matrix, covering over 120 pathogenicity prediction scores from dbNSFP and functional annotations from 22 VEP plugins, that can be used as input for downstream machine learning models of variant pathogenicity.

Resource management in the setup workflow is coordinated through a versioned YAML manifest file, hosted on GitHub (https://github.com/davide-scognamiglio/test-datasets) and fetched at pipeline initialization. Each entry in the manifest describes a single genomic resource with the following fields: dbname, version, out (expected local filename), url, method (one of curl, wget, or rsync), and two SHA-256 checksum fields—expected_sha256, fixed at release time, and computed_sha256, initially empty. As each resource is downloaded, the corresponding module computes the SHA-256 digest of the retrieved file and writes the result back into computed_sha256. The per-module manifests are subsequently merged into a single consolidated manifest, which serves as a permanent audit record of the exact database versions and file integrity at setup time. A companion script parses this merged manifest and produces an HTML integrity report in which each resource is labeled as VERIFIED, MISMATCH, or PENDING, enabling straightforward post-hoc inspection of the setup environment (Fig. 2). A future release will allow users wishing to update database versions or redirect downloads to institutional mirrors by supplying a custom manifest without modifying pipeline code, ensuring flexibility while preserving reproducibility guarantees.

RENOVO Scoring: the RENOVO model computes a pathogenicity likelihood score ∈ [0,1] specifically designed for evaluating variants of uncertain significance (VUS). RENOVO integrates over 11 features including conservation scores, predicted structural impacts, and functional annotations, and has demonstrated superior performance compared to traditional ensemble predictors in VUS reclassification tasks [7]. RENOVO is one of the few tools to have undergone a real-world evaluation in 2024, achieving an overall balanced accuracy of 82.6% on variants that were classified as VUS in 2020 and later reclassified as pathogenic or benign [16]. RENOVO scores are later used in a dedicated missense VUS prioritization step to provide additional decision-support evidence for variants classified as VUS. This module should be considered experimental: it has not yet been clinically validated as an automated reclassification procedure within MuSA and its output requires expert review before any clinical interpretation.

The annotate workflow of MuSA produces two complementary outputs. First, an interactive HTML report provides a clinical-friendly interface comprising a summary panel and a sortable and filterable variant table displaying gene symbol, HGVS [36] nomenclature, VEP consequence, ClinVar significance and review star rating, gnomAD population-maximum allele frequency, and RENOVO pathogenicity score of filtered variants (Fig. 3). Second, MuSA generates deeply annotated Mutation Annotation Format (MAF) files. Although originally developed for somatic mutations, MAF is deliberately adopted here for germline variants because it offers a standardized, cohort-level representation with one entry per variant and canonical transcript selection. Unlike VCF, which can contain multiple records per variant due to transcript multiplicity and multiallelic representation, MAF consolidates each variant into a single row, reducing redundancy and simplifying downstream analyses. MuSA’s MAF files can include up to 920 columns per variant, organized into thematic categories spanning variant consequence, population frequency, functional and pathogenicity prediction scores, splicing impact, regulatory context, and clinical classification. This produces a high-dimensional, consistently formatted feature matrix that supports both clinical interpretation and data-driven research, and is natively compatible with downstream tools such as the Bioconductor package maftools [37].

Nf-core/sarek is a mature, well-maintained pipeline whose primary focus is variant calling: it covers read alignment, preprocessing, and multi-caller SNV/INDEL and somatic calling, with VEP-based annotation available as a terminal step. In its default configuration, Sarek invokes VEP with basic parameters, producing an annotated VCF in which all consequence annotations are embedded as a semicolon-delimited CSQ field within the INFO column. While this format is technically complete, it is not directly amenable to clinical review or cohort-level tabular analysis without dedicated post-processing: variant consequences across multiple transcripts are concatenated in a single cell, no canonical or MANE transcript is pre-selected into a dedicated row, and no HTML report or per-sample MAF is generated. Extending Sarek’s annotation depth to include additional VEP plugins requires users to manually download each plugin data file, configure the associated paths, and pass them as individual command-line parameters—a process that scales poorly with the number of plugins and provides no automated mechanism for verifying resource integrity. Native dbNSFP annotation, ClinVar field normalization, automated ACMG/AMP evidence assignment, RENOVO scoring, and HPO-driven gene panel filtering are absent from the standard Sarek workflow. Notably, if a user were to annotate the same benchmark dataset via Sarek with all 22 VEP plugins and the full dbNSFP database manually configured to reproduce MuSA’s annotation depth, the resulting output would remain a raw annotated VCF with the same structural limitations described above: the deep functional information would be present but embedded inside unparsed INFO fields, without the one-row-per-variant MAF consolidation, RENOVO-based VUS reclassification, or interactive HTML report that MuSA provides natively.

Nf-core/variantprioritization is more closely scoped to annotation and prioritization and incorporates VEP with configurable filtering steps, producing a tabular TSV output with 276 columns. However, inspection of its output reveals that its design is specifically oriented toward inherited cancer susceptibility and cancer syndrome interpretation: dedicated columns for TCGA driver status and mutation frequency, COSMIC identifiers, Cancer Gene Census (CGC) tier, tumour suppressor gene (TSG) support, oncogene ranking, IntOGen driver classification, TCGA pancancer counts, and cancer predisposition gene source (CPG_SOURCE) are prominently present and central to its prioritization logic. This makes variantprioritization well-suited to hereditary cancer panels but limits its applicability to the broader landscape of germline disease—including skeletal dysplasias, neurodevelopmental disorders, connective tissue conditions, and other Mendelian diseases—which require disease-agnostic phenotype-driven prioritization. Moreover, similarly to Sarek, all annotation databases and plugin data files must be manually provisioned before execution; there is no automated setup workflow, no manifest-based version tracking, and no cryptographic verification of resources. RENOVO scoring, automated ACMG/AMP evidence assignment via GeneBe, and HPO-based gene panel filtering are absent, and the pipeline does not produce MAF-format output or an interactive clinical HTML report.

In contrast, MuSA addresses annotation as its primary objective across the full spectrum of germline disease. It provides a dedicated setup workflow automating the download of all required resources—including the VEP cache, 22 plugin data files, the full dbNSFP distribution, ANNOVAR databases, and the reference genome—recording the SHA-256 checksum of each file in a versioned YAML manifest. MuSA’s extended mode produces a consolidated MAF file with 920 columns per variant (compared to 276 in variantprioritization and an unparsed INFO field structure in Sarek), with each variant resolved to a single MANE-selected transcript row, integrating functional predictions, splicing scores, population frequencies from gnomAD v4.1, ClinVar classifications with automated ACMG/AMP evidence from GeneBe, and RENOVO pathogenicity scores. An interactive HTML report provides a clinical-friendly summary with HPO-matched gene panel filtering and a sortable variant table. These capabilities position MuSA as a complement rather than a competitor to upstream pipelines such as Sarek: MuSA is designed to receive VCF output from any calling pipeline and deliver a deeply annotated, clinically oriented result—applicable to any Mendelian disease context—without requiring extensive manual configuration of annotation resources.

Conclusions

MuSA provides a reproducible, end-to-end framework for germline variant annotation and ranking built on the Nextflow and nf-core ecosystem. By integrating VEP (with 22 curated plugins), ANNOVAR, native dbNSFP distribution, RENOVO, and automated ACMG/AMP evidence into a single containerized workflow, MuSA overcomes major limitations of traditional, manually assembled annotation pipelines.

The dual-mode architecture, offering streamlined basic annotation for standardized diagnostics and ultra-deep extended annotation, positions MuSA as a versatile tool serving both routine clinical laboratories and research-oriented genomics centers. The nf-core-compliant modular design facilitates community-driven extensions and institutional customization.

The dual-output design bridges bioinformatics research and clinical diagnostics, enabling systematic computational analyses (via MAF files) while providing clinicians with concise, evidence-rich candidate lists (via HTML reports).

Current limitations include support for germline small variants on hg38 only, a relatively large storage footprint driven by local copies of extensive annotation resources and container images, which may be constraining for some installations, and dependence on external API services, GeneBe and HPO, for ACMG/AMP classification and phenotype-driven filtering. MuSA does not currently support hg19/GRCh37 input files, and native support for this reference genome build is not planned, because maintaining a consistent annotation workflow across multiple genome builds is challenging when several integrated resources are build-specific or have uneven support across reference assemblies. To improve accessibility for users with legacy hg19/GRCh37 datasets, we plan to evaluate an optional preprocessing liftover step that would convert hg19/GRCh37 VCF coordinates to hg38 before annotation. An additional limitation is that the RENOVO-based VUS prioritization module is currently experimental. Although RENOVO itself has been previously described and evaluated, the MuSA-specific linear transformation and its integration with ACMG/AMP-oriented reporting have not yet been validated on an independent public dataset containing VCFs, phenotype annotations, and known causal variants. Therefore, this module should be interpreted as a decision-support and prioritization feature rather than as a clinically validated automated reclassification system. Additionally, while splicing impact is assessed through dedicated annotation resources including SpliceAI and related plugins, the current implementation prioritizes MANE/canonical transcript representation in the final MAF and HTML outputs and does not systematically report alternative transcript consequences. In future releases, we plan to introduce configurable transcript selection strategies, allowing users to retain or prioritize alternative transcripts for specific gene panels or loci where isoform-level interpretation is clinically relevant.

Planned extensions include: (1) support for somatic and structural variants; (2) compatibility with the T2T reference build; (3) optional liftover-based preprocessing for legacy hg19/GRCh37 VCFs; (4) native support for gVCF inputs and trio-aware analysis modes to enable cohort-level annotation; and (5) further expansion of the annotation resource library; (6) improved management of alternative transcripts annotations. Owing to its modular nf-core-oriented design, MuSA is well positioned for community-driven enhancements and institutional standardization. As genomic sequencing becomes routine in clinical practice, tools that bridge the gap between raw variant calls and actionable clinical evidence will be increasingly critical, and MuSA is designed to grow alongside this need.
