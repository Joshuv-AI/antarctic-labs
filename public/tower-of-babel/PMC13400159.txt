Negative effects of semaglutide on bone in obese mice
Authors: Katelyn Eisner, Stewart Jeromson, Kawkab Tahboub, Elizabeth A Zimmerman, David C Wright
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGLP1 receptor agonists, such as semaglutide, are used in the management of obesity. While causing weight loss, these drugs reduce both fat and lean mass. The impact of GLP1 receptor agonists on bone health has not been explored. Femur mass, stiffness, and strength are lower in obese male C57BL/6J mice treated with semaglutide, and these effects are more pronounced than in mice that are calorically restricted to the same extent. Decrements in bone are paralleled by lower circulating levels of markers of osteoblast activity. In an additional cohort, femur weight, strength, and stiffness are the same 6 weeks following the discontinuation of semaglutide compared to vehicle. Our findings provide evidence that bone mass and strength decrease with semaglutide and that this effect is dependent upon continued exposure to the drug. Clinical investigations are needed to determine if GLP1 receptor agonists have similar impacts on bone in patient populations.Keywords: semaglutide, bone metabolism, obesity, mice

Introduction

The glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP1ra) semaglutide has emerged as an efficacious treatment for obesity, with recent studies demonstrating 15%–20% reductions in body weight in individuals with obesity.1,2 The effects of semaglutide on weight loss are primarily driven by increases in satiety and reductions in food intake.3,4 Interventions that lead to reductions in food intake and, subsequently, weight, such as caloric restriction, cause reductions in not only adipose tissue but also lean mass.5 Given this, concerns have been raised about the loss of muscle mass during semaglutide treatment. In this regard, we, and others, have demonstrated that semaglutide treatment reduces muscle mass and strength in mice with diet-induced obesity.6,7,8,9 GLP1ra treatment does not appear to result in a disproportionate loss of lean and skeletal muscle mass, as mice pair-fed the same amount of calories as those treated with semaglutide lost similar amounts of lean tissue.7,9 The effects of semaglutide and pair feeding on skeletal muscle reversed when treatment was discontinued with lean mass, skeletal muscle mass, and grip strength being indistinguishable from previously vehicle-treated mice 6 weeks after treatment was stopped.7

A somewhat underappreciated effect of weight loss is the impact on bone density and strength. In both obese and non-obese mice, caloric restriction reduces bone density and suppresses bone formation.10 A 10% reduction in body weight results in approximately a 1%–2% reduction in bone mineral density with a greater impact of weight loss in lean individuals compared with overweight individuals.11 While semaglutide causes marked weight loss, there is surprisingly little data exploring the effects of semaglutide on bone health. One clinical study found increased circulating C-linked telopeptide of collagen (CTX1), a marker of bone breakdown, in individuals with increased fracture risk treated with semaglutide.12 In contrast, preclinical studies using GLP1ra’s, such as liraglutide and exendin-4, have shown positive benefits on bone turnover in mouse models of osteoporosis.13,14

In the current report, we sought to further understand the impact of semaglutide on bone. We hypothesized that (1) semaglutide treatment would result in reductions in bone strength, (2) that this effect would be recapitulated by restricting caloric intake, and (3) that decrements in bone health would require continued treatment with semaglutide. To do so, we assessed bone biomechanical properties in femurs from male C57BL/6J mice with diet-induced obesity immediately following 4 weeks of either semaglutide treatment or caloric restriction (experiment 1). In an additional cohort (experiment 2), obese mice were treated with semaglutide or were calorically restricted for 4 weeks, and bone was assessed 6 weeks following the discontinuation of treatment (Figure 1).

Results

Semaglutide treatment results in loss of bone weight and bone strength

Semaglutide and pair feeding resulted in significant body weight loss with semaglutide inducing greater weight loss than calorically restricted pair-fed mice (see Jeromson et al.7 for further details). Dry femur weight decreased with semaglutide treatment by ∼9%, with a trend toward reduced femur weight in the pair-fed group (Figure 2A). After observing this reduction in bone weight, we sought to examine if the biomechanical properties were altered by pharmacological and caloric restriction-driven weight loss. A subset of femurs underwent 3-point bend testing to assess bone strength and stiffness. Bone strength, defined as the mechanical force required to break the femur during the 3-point bend testing, was reduced ∼13% by semaglutide compared to vehicle controls (Figure 2B). In line with the greater decrease in body weight during semaglutide treatment, we observed a significant difference in bone strength between semaglutide and the pair-fed group, which were comparable with vehicle controls. Bone stiffness, which assesses resistance to deformation when placed under a load, also tended to be reduced with semaglutide (Figure 2C). Femur weight (r = 0.4747, p = 0.0080) and stiffness (r = 0.3687, p = 0.0412), but not strength (r = 0.3133, p = 0.0918), were associated with body weight when pooled data across all three groups were analyzed.

Together, our data suggest that semaglutide treatment leads to weakened structural properties in bone to a greater extent than caloric restriction alone. To gain mechanistic insight into whether changes in bone breakdown or formation were driving loss of bone weight and bone strength, we assessed circulating markers of bone resorption and bone formation. At the end of semaglutide treatment, there were no differences in serum CTX-1 concentrations between groups, suggesting that bone breakdown was not altered by treatment (Figure 2D). Serum bone alkaline phosphatase (BALP), osteocalcin, and procollagen type 1 N-propeptide (PINP), which are indicators of osteoblast activity and bone formation, were all reduced by semaglutide (Figures 2E–2G). Only BALP was reduced in the pair-fed group (Figure 2E). The attenuation of bone formation markers suggest that the observed reductions in bone weight and bone strength are driven by attenuations in bone formation and not changes in bone breakdown.

Discontinuation of semaglutide normalizes bone weight and bone strength

There is evidence that reductions in bone density during weight loss do not recover with subsequent weight regain.15 To assess the potential longer term impact of semaglutide-driven weight loss, we investigated bone properties following the discontinuation of semaglutide treatment or caloric restriction. Mice rapidly regained body weight once treatment was stopped and returned to pre-treatment weights within 6 weeks.7 Dry femur weight, bone strength, and bone stiffness were similar between previously semaglutide-treated or pair-fed mice and vehicle controls (Figures 3A–3C). Serum CTX-1 levels were reduced following the withdrawal period in both the semaglutide and pair-fed groups (Figure 3D), suggesting a shift toward greater formation of new bone, perhaps driven by the greater mechanotransductive forces placed on bone with weight regain. Markers of bone formation (BALP, osteocalcin, and PINP) were not different between groups (Figures 3E–3G).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that semaglutide-induced weight loss results in the loss of bone mass and a reduction in bone strength and bone stiffness and that these effects appeared to reverse following the discontinuation of treatment. We observed decreases in multiple circulating markers of bone turnover with semaglutide treatment. This suggests that semaglutide reduces the rate of new bone formation without altering the rate of bone breakdown. Over a prolonged treatment period, this shift in the balance between breakdown and new formation likely leads to a reduction in bone mass. While we found that bone-related alterations were greater in semaglutide-treated compared to calorically restricted mice, the differential weight loss between the semaglutide and pair-fed groups7 makes it difficult to discern whether the greater loss of bone strength is driven by differences in weight loss or direct effects of semaglutide on bone metabolism. With this said, we found positive associations between femur weight and femur stiffness and terminal body weight, suggesting that reductions in body weight could be driving changes in bone.

After treatment cessation, skeletal mass and mechanical properties rapidly recover, indicating that the effects of semaglutide are likely reversible and largely influenced by body weight loss. Similarly, in this study, bone properties were similar between all groups 6 weeks post-treatment when body weights were similar. This suggests that the degree of weight loss is a major influence on the reduction in bone mass and bone strength. Further work is necessary to elucidate the central and direct contributions to changes in bone metabolism following treatment with semaglutide and other GLP1ra’s.

Limitations of the study

Our findings highlight that semaglutide-related weight loss is not isolated to reductions in adipose tissue and skeletal muscle mass but also results in reductions in bone mass and bone strength. With this said, there are several limitations that need to be addressed. First, our study used relatively young and otherwise healthy obese mice. Further work is needed to examine the impacts of GLP1ra’s on bone health in conditions of increased fracture risk, such as in elderly individuals. Second, while bone weight and bone strength seem to track with body mass, further work is needed to elucidate if there are direct effects of GLP1ra’s on bone or if the effects are driven exclusively by weight loss. Third, while we demonstrate that bone weight and bone strength are similar between mice previously treated with semaglutide and vehicle controls, we are not able to assess bone strength serially within the same animal and thus cannot be certain that the observed lack of differences at the end of the discontinuation period is due to a true “recovery” of bone mass and strength within each animal. Lastly, future studies are needed to determine if GLP1ra’s have similar effects on bone in clinical populations.

Experimental model and study participant details

All experimental procedures were approved by University of British Columbia Animal Care Committee (A22-0063) and followed Canadian Council on Animal Care guidelines. 10–12-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME, CAT# 000664) and group-housed throughout treatment. Rooms were kept at an ambient temperature of 22°C and a 12:12 h light:dark cycle. Mice were allowed to acclimate to the facility for a minimum of 1 week before experiments and given ad libitum access to water and standard rodent chow (Teklad CAT #2018).

Method details

Mice were fed a high-fat diet (45% kcal fat, 35% kcal carbohydrate, 20% kcal protein, Research diets #D12451) for 12 weeks to induce obesity. Mice were then weight-matched and assigned to vehicle, semaglutide and pair-feeding groups (n = 20 per group). Mice received daily subcutaneous semaglutide or vehicle injections for 4 weeks. A ramp protocol was used for semaglutide (MedChemExpress #HY-114118) dosing with mice receiving a 3 nmol/kg body weight dose on day 1, 6 nmol/kg dose on day 2 and 10 nmol/kg on day 3 for the remainder of the treatment period. The daily caloric intake of pair-fed mice was matched to that of the semaglutide group. For the semaglutide treatment discontinuation experiments, mice underwent the same high-fat diet and treatment periods as described above, after which semaglutide and pair-feeding were discontinued and mice given ad libitum access to high fat diet for 6 weeks (n = 10 per group).

Mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (5 mg/100 g bw) and were killed by exsanguination. Cardiac blood was collected and allowed to clot at room temperature then put on ice until further processing. Serum was collected by centrifuging blood samples at 5000 g for 10 min and stored at −80°C until further analysis. Both femurs were dissected and remaining muscle and connective tissue were carefully removed. Femurs were then left to dry at room temperature before being weighed. Data is presented as the average weight of both femurs. For bone strength testing, dissected femurs were wrapped in gauze soaked in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and stored frozen at −20°C. For at least 12 h prior to testing, samples were thawed by hydrating in PBS at 3°C–4°C. On the day of testing, femurs were cleaned of any remaining soft tissue. Samples were tested to failure in three-point bending using a mechanical testing system (Mach-1, Biomomentum, Quebec, Canada) with a 25,000 gram-force load cell, an 8-mm span, and a displacement rate of 0.1 mm/s. The load was applied to the posterior surface of the femoral midshaft. Load and cross-displacement were recorded during testing. Maximal load (N) and stiffness (N/mm) were measured and reported according to Jepsen et al. (2015).16

All assays were analyzed using a Versamax Tunable Microplate Reader and SoftMax Pro Software (Molecular Devices). Serum Bone alkaline phosphatase (Biomatik #EKF58147), C-linked telopeptide of collagen (Novus Biologicals #NBP3-11802), Osteocalcin (Novus Biologicals #NBP2-68151), and procollagen type 1 N-propeptide (Novus Biologicals #NBP2-76466) were measured using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical tests were completed using GraphPad Prism v.11.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). All data was analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by a Tukey’s post hoc test if there was a significant effect. Pearson’s correlation coefficient analysis was used to examine the associations between body weight and bone weight, strength or stiffness. Data are presented as means ± SD and individual data points are shown. A relationship was considered significant when p < 0.05.
