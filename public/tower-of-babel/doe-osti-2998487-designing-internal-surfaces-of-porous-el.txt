1 Executive Summary
 This project was carried out by the PIs at West Virginia University Research Corporation
(WVURC), in collaboration with manufacturers of oxygen-ion-conducting Solid Oxide
Electrolysis Cells (SOECs), to dramatically improve performance for highly efficient, durable
hydrogen production.
 Solid Oxide Cells (SOCs) can operate as solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs) by oxidizing a fuel to
produce electricity and as SOECs by electrolyzing water to produce hydrogen and oxygen gases.
SOEC systems essentially adopt well-developed SOFC systems to shorten SOEC development
time. Operating SOEC stacks at current densities above 0.75A/cm2 and a degradation rate below
0.5%/1000 h, as stated in this FOA, could enable cost-competitive production of synthetic
hydrocarbon fuels without consuming fossil fuels. However, SOEC typically exhibits more severe
degradation than SOFC, and its degradation depends heavily on operating conditions. Most
importantly, SOEC degradation is rooted in the electrode and varies with the electrode's cell
structure. Understanding electrode degradation mechanisms and implementing feasible mitigation
approaches are crucial to enabling commercial SOFCs for direct SOEC applications. This is one
of the most critical issues to be addressed for the development of SOECs and reversible SOCs.
 This project focused on addressing the critical need to improve the electrodes of state-of-the-
art and commercially available SOFCs to enable their direct application in SOECs. This project
was based on WVURC's granted and pending patent technology and aimed to develop and
implement highly active, robust nanoscale coating layers on the internal surface of the porous
electrode. We developed these coating layers using the additive manufacturing process of Atomic
Layer Deposition (ALD) and applied them directly to the internal surfaces of porous electrodes in

2 Project Overview
 This project, led by the PIs at West Virginia University Research Corporation (WVURC),
collaborated with industry manufacturers to develop oxygen-ion-conducting Solid Oxide Cells
(SOCs) that have significantly enhanced electrolysis performance for more efficient and durable
hydrogen production. This project is based on WVURC's granted and pending patent technology,
which applies highly active, robust nanoscale coating layers to the internal surface of the porous
electrode. We developed this coating layer using the additive manufacturing process of Atomic
Layer Deposition (ALD), depositing it directly on the internal surfaces of as-fabricated commercial
cells. The commercial cells used in this study consist of LSM/YSZ and LSCF/SDC oxygen
electrodes that have been developed and matured worldwide over the past two decades. Our target
material systems are commercial LSM/YSZ and LSCF/SDC composite electrodes, respectively,
with an emphasis on applications at 650-800 °C. For porous electrodes, the ALD coating layer will
incorporate innovative, conformal surface nanoionics and nanocrystalline, ion-conducting
materials. An ALD-conformal nanoionics layer provides structural protection, increasing
durability against both intrinsic and extrinsic degradation. Most importantly, ALD-enabled surface
nanoionics is expected to significantly lower resistance and increase the number of active
electrochemical reaction sites on the electrodes for hydrogen production via electrolysis. On the
internal surface of the LSM/YSZ oxygen electrode, a dual-layered nanoionics structure, with
subjacent ZrO2 nanoionics and superjacent electrocatalytic nanoionics, has been applied. On the
LSCF/SDC backbone, the electrode has been further activated by implanting nanocatalysts.
 Overall, this research aims to achieve highly efficient and durable hydrogen production. The
approach is to reduce electrode/cell resistance, increase the hydrogen production rate, and improve
durability.
 The elements and materials used to engineer nano-architecture on the electrode surface are
commercially mature/available, low-cost, environmentally friendly, and fully chemically and
thermally compatible with materials used in state-of-the-art commercial cells. The project

4 Development of ALD Coating on Ni/YSZ Fuel Electrode (SOPO Task 2)
4.1 Fuel electrode: Ni migration & loss of TPBs and percolating network

 SOC electrochemical reactions occur on the internal surface of the engineered porous electrode and at

the triple-phase boundaries (TPBs), where ionic-conducting phases (YSZ, SSZ, or GDC), electronically

conducting phases (Ni), and the gas phase meet.1,2 YSZ stands for yttria-stabilized zirconia, a key ceramic

material used in solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs). At its core, it is zirconium dioxide (ZrO₂) doped with

yttrium oxide (Y₂O₃). The yttria stabilizes zirconia in its cubic crystal structure, which is essential because

this phase supports high oxide ion (O²⁻) conductivity at elevated temperatures (typically 600–1000 °C).

SSZ typically refers to scandia-stabilized zirconia. This is zirconium dioxide (ZrO₂) doped with scandium

oxide (Sc₂O₃). Like YSZ, the dopant stabilizes zirconia in the cubic fluorite structure, but SSZ exhibits

significantly higher oxide ion conductivity, especially in the intermediate temperature range (~500–800 °C).

GDC in solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs) stands for gadolinium-doped ceria (also written as
GdₓCe₁₋ₓO₂₋δ, commonly ~10–20 mol% Gd). It is a ceria-based mixed ionic conductor in which
doping CeO₂ with Gd³⁺ creates oxygen vacancies, enabling fast oxide-ion (O²⁻) transport.
Compared with YSZ, GDC has much higher ionic conductivity at intermediate temperatures
(~500–800 °C), which is why it is widely used in lower-temperature SOFC designs.

 The high-performance electrode requires high porosity, high surface area, high-density
TPBs,3,4,5,6,7,8,9 sufficient electrical and ionic conductivity, and high catalytic activity. TPB stands
for triple-phase boundary. It refers to the microscopic regions in an electrode where three phases
meet simultaneously: Electronic conductor (e.g., Ni in Ni–YSZ anode), Ionic conductor (e.g., YSZ
or GDC), Gas phase (fuel or oxidant). These TPB sites are where electrochemical reactions
actually occur. For example, at the fuel electrode (anode), hydrogen oxidation happens only where
all three are present: H₂ gas reaches the site O²⁻ ions arrive through the ionic conductor; Electrons
are transferred through the electronic conductor.

 13

 The state-of-the-art hydrogen electrode for SOECs is Ni-based cermet electrodes, such as Ni-
YSZ and Ni-GDC. One of the distinct features of hydrogen electrodes under the SOEC operation
conditions is high steam concentration, which is much higher than that of the SOFC operation.
High steam concentration has a severely detrimental effect on the structural stability of Ni-based
hydrogen electrodes. As the steam/H2 ratio increases, the partial pressure of oxygen, pO2, is more
than three orders of magnitude higher than that of the SOFC operation. This increased partial
pressure of oxygen would lead to the formation of nickel hydroxide or nickel oxide compounds
and accelerate the Ni surface transport, which in turn could cause the agglomeration and/or
evaporation of Ni, 10 resulting in the loss of the percolating Ni network and TPB. The larger
magnitude of Ni migration in electrolysis mode is not seen to the same extent in cells tested in fuel
cell mode under similar test conditions. Thus, the local pO2 in the fuel electrode and the resultant
Ni migration along the Ni/YSZ surface are the critical causes for SOEC degradation.

 As mentioned, the local pO2 in the fuel electrode and the resulting Ni migration along the
Ni/YSZ surface are critical parameters for SOEC degradation. In other words, the Ni-YSZ
hydrogen electrodes will undergo surface oxidation and agglomeration in a high-steam atmosphere,
thereby losing its electrical and catalytic activity, leading to poor redox stability and eventually
degrading cell performance. The development of Ni-free oxide ceramics is actively pursued by the
SOC community. However, the currently promising Ni-free ceramic oxide-based hydrogen
electrodes generally have low electrical conductivity, which can significantly increase the cell's
resistance. Accordingly, the well-developed Ni/YSZ is currently still the best-performing fuel
electrode for SOEC applications.

 For Ni/YSZ, because degradation is due to Ni surface migration and subsequent loss of
electrical and catalytic activity, surface modification of Ni/YSZ is a viable approach to achieve
durable SOEC operation, and liquid solution-based infiltration seems the most straightforward.
Nevertheless, for the surface modification to be effective, the liquid solution needs to penetrate the

 14

current-collecting layer and deliver the infiltrated materials to the active layer adjacent to the
electrolyte. Depending on the fabrication methods, the commercial fuel electrode support cells
usually have dense fuel electrodes. For example, the fuel electrode support button cells fabricated
by NexTech (fuelcellmaterials.com) have an anode thickness of ~0.3 mm and a porosity of ~35%.
Accordingly, the primary concern with liquid solution infiltration is its penetration into the active
layer of the fuel electrode in commercial cells.

 Furthermore, the liquid solution exhibits an uncontrolled microstructure within the infiltrated
materials, resulting in discrete particles rather than a percolating conducting network. For the
liquid solution infiltration of Ni, Co, and Fe nano-catalysts into Ni/YSZ electrode. 10 When those
discrete particles landed on the Ni surface, they could alloy the Ni grains and quickly lose their
nanograined features and advantageous catalytic activity. On the other hand, when those discrete
Ni, Co, and Fe nano-catalysts landed on the YSZ surface, those particles were not activated for
electrochemical reactions due to the lack of electrically conductive pathways on the pure ionic-
conducting YSZ substrate. As such, the infiltration of the liquid solution into the Ni/YSZ fuel
electrode faces challenges in terms of (1) the feasibility for practical applications in commercial
cells, and (2) its resultant technical effectiveness due to the lack of a conducting percolation
network within the infiltrated materials.

 As such, an ALD coating of a composite comprising the ionic conductor and electrocatalysts
was applied to the Ni/YSZ fuel electrode of an inherently functional commercial SOFC in this
project.

4.2 Experimental section
 Commercially available, anode-supported solid oxide button cells fabricated by
Chemtronergy LLC (CTG, Salt Lake City, UT) were employed for all the experiments described
in this paper. CTG cells are composed of five layers as follows, starting from the anode: ~0.9 mm
thick Ni/YSZ cermet layer, which supports the cell structure; 15 μm thick Ni/YSZ active layer;

 15

~12 μm thick YSZ electrolyte; ~15 μm thick LSM/YSZ active layer; and 50 μm thick, pure LSM
current collecting layer. The active area (limited by the air electrode) of the cell is 2 cm2. The
anode's exposure area to fuel is about 3.5 cm2. We applied the ALD coating only to the reduced
porous fuel electrode, while masking the air electrode to prevent ALD vapor penetration.
 The ALD coatings were performed in a commercial GEMStar-8 ALD reactor from Arradiance
Inc. The precursors used in this study were all purchased from Strem Chemicals, Inc. This is a
simple one-step process for as-received cells, in which the chemistry of the ALD layer is altered
through computer-controlled, automatic switching of the precursors. No surface pretreatment or
heat treatment was applied to the cells before or after ALD coating. The cell electrochemical
operation was performed immediately after the ALD coating.
 All cell tests were performed on a test stand. The platinum mesh was used for the anode and
cathode lead connections. The fuel and air stream flow rates were controlled separately using mass
flow controllers. Cell testing was performed at 750 ºC. During the operation, air and fuel flow rates
of 600 mL/min each were used. Before any electrochemical measurements, all cells were current-
treated for approximately 16 h under a small current density of 0.1 A/cm2 to ensure they were
activated. After that, all samples were loaded at a constant current of 0.3 A/cm2 for the desired
periods. The cell performance was examined using a TrueData-load modular electronic DC load,
which guarantees voltage and current accuracies of 0.03% FS of the range selected +/-0.05% of
the value. The cell impedance spectra were examined using a potentiostat/galvanostat (Solartron
1287A) equipped with a frequency response analyzer (Solartron 1260). Impedance measurements
were carried out using a Solatron 1260 frequency response analyzer in a frequency range from 50
MHz to 100 kHz. The impedance spectra and resistance (Rs and Rp) presented are those measured
under a DC bias current of 0.3 A/cm2. On a Nyquist plot, Rs is determined by the intercept at the
higher frequency end, and the distance between the two intercepts determines Rp.
 ALD-coated cells were sectioned and subjected to nano-structural and crystallographic

 16

examination using high-resolution (HR) Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and scanning
TEM (STEM). All the TEM examinations were conducted in the cathode active layer. TEM
samples were prepared by mechanical polishing and ion milling in a liquid-nitrogen-cooled holder.
Electron diffraction, diffraction contrast, and HRTEM imaging were performed using a JEM-2100
operated at 200 kV.
4.3 Improved electrochemical performance and reduced resistance induced by ALD
coating

 As stated previously, Ni migration at the Ni/YSZ interface is the primary cause of SOEC
degradation. As shown in Figure 4-1 of this project, Ni migration and oxidation directly led to the
formation of Ni(OH)2 or NiO within the original pore region, thereby blocking the original TPB,
reducing catalytic activity, and decreasing conductivity. To mitigate such Ni migration, this project
has also applied redox-stable, refractory mesoporous ZrO2 nanoionics to the surface of the porous
Ni/YSZ fuel electrode in a commercial SOFC. During electrochemical operation, the refractory
and redox-stable ZrO2 ALD layer formed a discrete nanocrystalline porous network on the surface
of the electrode backbone, as shown in Figure 4-1. Such an ALD-deposited ZrOx layer on the Ni
surface will prevent Ni from migrating and coarsening.

 Figure 4-1. Left: Illustration of the original TPB; Middle: NiO formation blocking the original TPB;
 Right: ALD coating of ZrO2 on the Ni surface to potentially prevent the Ni migration.

 The main methodology of this project was to enhance SOFC performance, as improvements
observed in cells, such as reductions in ohmic and polarization resistance, typically translate across

 17

operating modes, provided there is no catastrophic delamination when switching from SOFC to
SOEC. There are three sets of evaluations that were performed on the cells with different baseline
performance to evaluate the impact of the ALD coating on the fuel electrode.

(1) For the first batch of cells, under SOEC operation at 750°C, the ALD-coated cell with YSZ,
Ru, and Ni on the fuel electrode reaches a current density of 0.991 A/cm² at 1.3 V when operated
with a 70 % steam and hydrogen mixture, with an ASR value of 0.413 Ω cm². When the steam
content is reduced to 50 %, the cell delivers 0.808 A/cm² at 1.3 V, with an ASR value of 0.472 Ω
cm², demonstrating strong electrochemical activity across both operating conditions. For this set
of experiments, the uncoated cell was not tested under SOEC conditions, and we currently do not
have a corresponding data set to directly compare the coated cell performance in SOEC mode.
Additionally, no short-term or long-term durability tests were conducted, and the electrolysis I–V
curves presented here serve primarily to illustrate the performance enhancement imparted by ALD
nano-catalyst coatings, which further activate the ZrO₂-based nanoionics within the Ni/YSZ fuel
electrode. Additional tests were conducted on nanocatalysts, mainly in the form of a Ru coating

4.4 Section Summary
 This project demonstrated an ALD-coated nanocomposite comprising a ZrO₂ ionic conductor
and nano-catalysts on the porous Ni–YSZ fuel electrode. The electrochemical performance of the
commercial cells demonstrated simultaneous increases in performance and decreases in total
resistance (ASR). These results were achieved by applying a minute ALD coating to the very thick,
dense fuel electrode of commercial fuel electrode–supported cells. The impact of the ALD coating
on the fuel electrode in metal-supported cells is expected to be greater.
 In metal-supported cells, the electrode architecture is typically more heterogeneous, with
larger pore-size distributions, higher tortuosity, and a significant metal–ceramic interfacial area.
These features exacerbate transport limitations and interfacial polarization losses, which
conventional infiltration or bulk-modification techniques do not fully address. By contrast, Atomic
Layer Deposition (ALD) enables angstrom-level thickness control and exceptional conformality,
allowing uniform coating of internal pore surfaces and triple-phase boundaries even in highly
porous and irregular structures. This ensures continuous ionic conduction pathways (via ZrO₂)
while precisely dispersing catalytically active nanoparticles without pore blockage. Moreover,
ALD coatings can stabilize the metal surface against coarsening, oxidation, and chromium

 28

poisoning—degradation mechanisms that are more pronounced in metal-supported configurations.
The low loading requirement minimizes mass transport penalties while maximizing functional
surface area. As a result, ALD is uniquely suited to enhance reaction kinetics and improve long-
term durability in metal-supported cells beyond what conventional fuel electrode–supported
designs can achieve.

5.1 Catastrophic Electrode Delamination for the SOEC Cells with LSM-Based Air-
Electrode
 SOCs can operate as solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs) by oxidizing a fuel to produce electricity
and as SOECs by electrolyzing water to produce hydrogen and oxygen gases. SOEC systems are
adapting well-developed SOFC systems to shorten SOEC device development. Operating SOEC
stacks at current densities above 0.75 A/cm2 and with a low degradation rate of less than
0.5%/1000 h, as stated in this FOA, could enable cost-competitive production of synthetic
hydrocarbon fuels without consuming fossil fuels.11 However, SOEC usually shows more severe
degradation than SOFC, and SOEC degradation depends strongly on electrolysis operating
conditions, as shown in Figure 5-1.

Figure 5-1. Cell-level improvements. (A) Current-voltage curves for cells fabricated in 2006 and
2020 with data from at 750 °C, measured in H2O/H2 = 1 or CO2/CO = 1. (B) Durability test of H2O
electrolysis at 1 A/cm2 on a cell fabricated in 2005, measured at 850 °C, and a cell fabricated in
2015, estimated at 800 oC. All cells were supported by a Ni-YSZ electrode and had an active area
of 16 cm2.
 Extensive (> 9000 h) long-term SOEC testing has been performed in Europe, as shown in

 30

Figure 5-1. It was demonstrated that significant degradation occurred during the initial operation
(first ~ 500 h) for various SOEC operations, regardless of temperature and cell type. Degradation
is severe, especially at higher production rates, with the current density exceeding 1 A/cm²,12,13,14
as shown in Figure 5-2.

Figure 5-2. SOEC degradation as a function of SOEC operation current density and operation
duration.
 Most importantly, the SOEC degradation is rooted in both the fuel and oxygen electrodes and
varies based on the cell structure of the electrode. At temperatures above 850 ºC, LSM-based
materials are the most popular choice for SOFC because of their high chemical and thermal
compatibility with conventional YSZ electrolyte, adequate electrochemical performance, and
superior stability during long-term operation. 15 However, LSM possesses negligible ionic
conductivity and low oxygen surface exchange and thus restricts the electrochemically active sites
to the very limited TPBs. More severely, in the SOEC mode, the limited ionic conductivity of LSM
resulted in the immediate buildup of oxygen at the electrolyte-oxygen electrode interface, leading
to catastrophic delamination, as shown in Figure 5-3.16,17,18

 31

Figure 5-3. LSM/YSZ Oxygen electrode, severe delamination taking place at the electrolyte and electrode interface.

 For robust SOECs subjected to long-term electrochemical operations at elevated temperatures,
the electrode must exhibit high structural stability and chemical compatibility with the cell
components. It needs to satisfy strict physical property requirements, including sufficient electrical
and ionic conductivity and high catalytic activity. Accordingly, the use of new electrode materials
in cells for practical applications faces various challenges. On the other hand, electrochemical
reactions occur on the electrode's inner surface. For well-developed, mature SOC technology, the
internal surface of the as-fabricated electrode, engineered to be porous to access the reactant gas,
provides additional design space to further improve the performance and stability of the as-made
cells.
 Based on the critical issues listed above for different electrodes, this project aims to develop
coating layers on the internal surfaces of SOCs to enable efficient, cost-effective hydrogen
production. Such a coating layer will be applied using the additive manufacturing process of ALD.
It will be directly deposited onto the internal surfaces of the porous electrodes in the as-fabricated
commercial cells.

5.2 Experimental section
 Commercially available, anode-supported solid oxide button cells fabricated by

 33

Chemtronergy LLC (CTG, Salt Lake City, UT) were employed for all the experiments described
in this paper. CTG cells are composed of five layers as follows, starting from the anode: ~0.9 mm
thick Ni/YSZ cermet layer, which supports the cell structure; 15 μm thick Ni/YSZ active layer;
~12 μm thick YSZ electrolyte; ~15 μm thick LSM/8YSZ active layer; and 50 μm thick, pure LSM
current collecting layer. The active area (limited by the air electrode) of the cell is 2 cm2. The
anode's exposure area to fuel is about 3.5 cm2.
 The ALD coatings were performed in a commercial GEMStar-8 ALD reactor from Arradiance
Inc. The precursors used in this study were all purchased from Strem Chemicals, Inc. This is a
simple one-step process for as-received cells, in which the chemistry of the ALD layer is altered
through computer-controlled, automatic switching of the precursors. No surface pretreatment or
heat treatment was applied to the cells before or after ALD coating. The cell electrochemical
operation was carried out directly after the ALD coating.
 All cell tests were performed on a test stand. The platinum mesh was used for the anode and
cathode lead connections. The fuel and air stream flow rates were controlled separately using mass
flow controllers. Cell testing was performed at 750 ºC. During the operation, air and fuel flow rates
of 600 mL/min each were used. Before any electrochemical measurements, all cells were current-
treated for approximately 16 h at a low current density of 0.1 A/cm2 to ensure activation. Afterward,
all samples were loaded at a constant current of 0.3 A/cm2 for the desired durations. The cell
performance was examined using a TrueData-load modular electronic DC load, which guarantees
voltage and current accuracies of 0.03% FS of the range selected +/-0.05% of the value. The cell
impedance spectra were examined using a potentiostat/galvanostat (Solartron 1287A) equipped
with a frequency response analyzer (Solartron 1260). Impedance measurements were carried out
using a Solatron 1260 frequency response analyzer in a frequency range from 50 MHz to 100 kHz.
The impedance spectra and resistance (Rs and Rp) presented are those measured under a DC bias
current of 0.3 A/cm2. On a Nyquist plot, Rs is determined by the intercept at the higher frequency

 34

end, and the distance between the two intercepts determines Rp.
 ALD-coated cells were sectioned and subjected to nano-structural and crystallographic
examination using high-resolution (HR) Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and scanning
TEM (STEM). All the TEM examinations were conducted in the cathode active layer. TEM
samples were prepared by mechanical polishing and ion milling in a liquid-nitrogen-cooled holder.
Electron diffraction, diffraction contrast, and HRTEM imaging were performed using a JEM-2100
operated at 200 kV. The STEM imaging is performed at Thermo-Fisher Spectra 300 Probe-
Corrected S/TEM operated at 300 kV. Chemical analysis was carried out under TEM using energy-
dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS).

5.6 Section Summary
 For the first time in the field of SOEC, this project demonstrated that ALD coating of the ionic
conductor YSZ on the LSM/SSZ air electrode of commercial SOFC immediately mitigates
catastrophic electrode delamination under SOEC operation. The ALD coating decreases both the
ohmic and polarization resistance. Most importantly, for the commercial SOFC that could not
sustain SOEC operation at all, the ALD coating of YSZ onto the air electrode LSM/SSZ led to the
SOEC with a current density of 1.8-2.2 A with a low voltage of ~ 1.2 V, which exceeds the DOE
2025 program goal of 1.5 A/cm2. Furthermore, applying the YSZ and CoOx nanocomposite via
ALD on LSM/SSZ further improved SOEC performance. These results from this project
effectively validated the feasibility of using ALD coatings to enhance both the durability and
performance of the SOEC. It opens new directions for further improving SOEC performance
through ALD coating of mixed conductors, especially electrocatalytic mixed conductors.

6 Development of dual-layer coating of nanoionics on LSM/SSZ (SOPO Task 3)
6.1 ALD coating of electrocatalytic mixed conductor
 The SOFCs chosen for implementing nanoionics have composite air electrodes consisting of

LSM, a pure electronic conductor lacking ionic conductivity, balanced with the ionic conductor

scandium-stabilized Zirconium (SSZ) oxide. We performed ALD to deposit conformal thin films,

intended to subsequently form surface nanoionics on the internal surface of the porous air electrode,

where electrochemical reactions occur. This surface nanoionics thus experiences additional

thermodynamic and kinetic driving forces from the electrochemical reactions, which could further

assist in triggering thermally activated crystal grain growth at elevated temperatures. We applied

an ALD coating of a minute amount of the transition-metal electrocatalyst PrOx, which has

electronic conductivity four orders of magnitude lower than that of LSM and ionic conductivity

three orders of magnitude lower than that of SSZ at the bulk scale. Yet, PrOx has proven to be an

effective electrocatalyst due to its mixed-valence state and high oxygen surface exchange rate.

6.2 Experimental section
 Commercially available, anode-supported solid oxide button cells fabricated by

Chemtronergy LLC (CTG, Salt Lake City, UT) were employed for all the experiments described

in this paper. CTG cells are composed of five layers as follows, starting from the anode: ~0.9 mm

thick Ni/YSZ cermet layer, which supports the cell structure; 15 μm thick Ni/YSZ active layer;

~12 μm thick YSZ electrolyte; ~15 μm thick LSM/8YSZ active layer; and 50 μm thick, pure LSM

current collecting layer. The active area (limited by the air-electrode) of the cell is 2 cm2. The

exposure area of the anode to fuel is about 3.5 cm2.

 The ALD coatings were performed in a commercial GEMStar-8 ALD reactor from Arradiance

Inc. The precursors used in this study were all purchased from Strem Chemicals, Inc. This is a

simple one-step processing of as-received cells, and the change in the chemistry of the ALD layer

 42

was achieved through computer-controlled, automatic switching of the precursors. No surface

pretreatment or heat treatment was applied to the cells before or after ALD coating. The cell

electrochemical operation was carried out directly after the ALD coating.

 All cell tests were performed on a test stand. The platinum mesh was used for the anode and

cathode lead connections. The fuel and air stream flow rates were controlled separately using mass

flow controllers. Cell testing was performed at 750 ºC. During the operation, air and fuel flow rates

of 600 mL/min each were used. Before any electrochemical measurements, all cells were current-

treated for approximately 16 h under a small current density of 0.1 A/cm2 to ensure they were

activated. After that, all samples were loaded at a constant current of 0.3 A/cm2 for the desired

periods. The cell performance was examined using a TrueData-load modular electronic DC load,

which guarantees voltage and current accuracies of 0.03% FS of the range selected +/-0.05% of

the value. The cell impedance spectra were examined using a potentiostat/galvanostat (Solartron

1287A) equipped with a frequency response analyzer (Solartron 1260). Impedance measurements

were carried out using a Solartron 1260 frequency response analyzer in a frequency range from 50

MHz to 100 kHz. The impedance spectra and resistance (Rs and Rp) presented are those measured

under a DC bias current of 0.3 A/cm2. On a Nyquist plot, Rs is determined by the intercept at the

higher frequency end, and the distance between the two intercepts determines Rp.

 ALD-coated cells were sectioned and subjected to nanostructural and crystallographic

examination using high-resolution (HR) Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and scanning

TEM (STEM). All the TEM examinations were conducted in the cathode active layer. TEM

samples were prepared by mechanical polishing and ion milling in a liquid-nitrogen-cooled holder.

Electron diffraction, diffraction contrast, and HRTEM imaging were performed using a JEM-2100

operated at 200 kV. STEM imaging is performed on a Thermo Fisher Spectra 300 Probe-Corrected

S/TEM operated at 300 kV. Chemical analysis was carried out under TEM using energy-dispersive

 43

X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS).

6.3 ALD coating of electrolytic mixed conductor on LSM/SSZ air electrode
 The nano-scale ALD coating layer developed in this project appears to exhibit superior nano

structural stability and remains uniformly conformal even after 1000 h of electrolysis at 850 °C.

The thermal and electrochemical stability of such nanostructures was confirmed through

transmission electron microscopy over a large scale of ~5 microns. The typical ALD coating layer

is shown in Figure 6-1 of this report.

 Figure 6-1. Electrolyte and the active layer of the Oxygen electrode (ALD coating on the oxygen electrode) after
                                            1000 h SOEC at 850 °C.

 This conformal electrocatalytic layer activates the entire internal surface of the porous oxygen

electrode as an active site for electrochemical reactions. Implementing such a conformal ALD

layer on the internal surface of the oxygen electrode alone significantly lowered both the ohmic

and polarization resistance of the entire cell, as shown in Figure 6-2. We quantified the reductions

in ohmic and polarization resistance by comparing the high-frequency and low-frequency

intercepts of the Nyquist plots before and after modification. A leftward shift of the entire

impedance spectrum indicates reduced ohmic resistance, while a contraction of the impedance arc

reflects decreased polarization resistance. Together, these changes indicate lower internal

resistance, faster electrochemical reaction kinetics, and improved overall SOEC performance.

 One of the key features of the ALD-coated layer of nano-grains in this project is the formation
of a mixed conductor, as the high-density grain boundaries within the ALD layer exhibit both
electrical and ionic conductivity. In solid oxide cells (SOFCs), a mixed conductor (more precisely,
a mixed ionic–electronic conductor, or MIEC) is a material that can simultaneously transport oxide
ions (O²⁻) and electrons (or holes). This dual conductivity fundamentally changes how
electrochemical reactions occur: (a). In a pure ionic conductor (e.g., YSZ), reactions are confined
to narrow triple-phase boundary (TPB) regions where gas, ionic conductor, and electronic
conductor meet. (b). In a mixed conductor (MIEC), both ions and electrons can move through the
same solid phase, so the reaction can occur over a much larger surface area, not just at TPBs.
Mixed conductors are significant to solid oxide cells, as they immediately lead to (1). Expanded
reaction zone: Oxygen reduction (cathode) or fuel oxidation (anode) can occur across the entire
electrode surface exposed to gas. (2). Lower polarization resistance: Reduced reliance on TPB
density improves kinetics. (3). Better performance at intermediate temperatures: Especially
important as SOFCs move toward 500 800 °C operation.

7.1 LSCF air electrode degradation under SOED operation
 Switching from SOFC to SOEC for Hydrogen, Oxygen, and carbon monoxide
production, with the desired high electrolysis current density and high gas production rate, is not
straightforward. By contrast to the LSM-based electrode that undergoes catastrophic delamination,
cobaltite-based perovskites, including LSCF, exhibit mixed ionic and electronic conductivity, and the TPB

region of the oxygen electrode is effectively expanded across the entire LSCF surface. The LSCF is

currently utilized in most commercial SOCs, especially for SOEC. However, LSCF is susceptible to Sr

surface segregation60 and presents a high degradation rate. Sr surface segregation further degrades the cell

when LSCF is exposed to Cr contamination. Under SOEC operating conditions, the dominant product of

the Cr contamination reaction is SrCrO4, as anodic polarization promotes the surface segregation of SrO.61

As a result of the formation of the SrCrO4 on the electrode surface, there is the extraction of Sr across the

entire LSCF electrode. Because Sr doping plays a key role in promoting the electrocatalytic activity and

electrical conductivity of LSCF perovskite, the loss of Sr at the A-site in the active reaction region of LSCF,

and subsequent LSCF phase decomposition, deteriorates the electrocatalytic activity for the oxygen

evolution reaction.

7.2 Experimental section
 Commercially available anode-supported solid oxide button cells fabricated by Chemtronergy
LLC (CTG, Salt Lake City, UT) were employed in all experiments described in this paper. CTG
cells are composed of five layers as follows, starting from the anode: ~0.9 mm thick Ni/YSZ
cermet layer, which supports the cell structure; 15 μm thick Ni/YSZ active layer; ~12 μm thick
YSZ electrolyte. The active area (limited by the air-electrode) of the cell is 2 cm2. The anode's
exposure area to the fuel is about 3.5 cm2.
 The ALD coatings were performed in a commercial GEMStar-8 ALD reactor from Arradiance

 62

Inc. The precursors used in this study were all purchased from Strem Chemicals, Inc. This is a
simple one-step process for as-received cells, and the change in the ALD layer chemistry was
achieved through computer-controlled precursor switching. No surface pretreatment or heat
treatment was applied to the cells before or after ALD coating. The cell electrochemical operation
was carried out directly after the ALD coating.
 All cell tests were performed on a test stand. The platinum mesh was used for the anode and
cathode lead connections. The fuel and air stream flow rates were controlled separately using mass
flow controllers. Cell testing was performed at 750 ºC. During the operation, air and fuel flow rates
of 600 mL/min each were used. Before any electrochemical measurements, all cells were current-
treated for approximately 16 h at a low current density of 0.1 A/cm2 to ensure activation. Afterward,
all samples were loaded at a constant current of 0.3 A/cm2 for the desired durations. The cell
performance was examined using a TrueData-load modular electronic DC load, which guarantees
voltage and current accuracies of 0.03% FS of the range selected +/- 0.05% of the value. The cell
impedance spectra were examined using a potentiostat/galvanostat (Solartron 1287A) equipped
with a frequency response analyzer (Solartron 1260). Impedance measurements were carried out
using a Solatron 1260 frequency response analyzer in a frequency range from 50 MHz to 100 kHz.
The impedance spectra and resistances (Rs and Rp) presented were measured under a DC bias
current of 0.3 A/cm2. On a Nyquist plot, Rs is determined by the intercept at the higher frequency
end, and the distance between the two intercepts determines Rp.
 ALD-coated cells were sectioned and examined for nanostructure and crystallography using
high-resolution (HR) Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and scanning TEM (STEM). All
the TEM examinations were conducted in the cathode active layer. TEM samples were prepared
by mechanical polishing and ion milling in a liquid-nitrogen-cooled holder. Electron diffraction,
diffraction contrast, and HRTEM imaging were performed using a JEM-2100 operated at 200 kV.
STEM imaging is performed on a Thermo Fisher Spectra 300 Probe-Corrected S/TEM operated at

 63

300 kV. Chemical analysis was carried out under TEM using energy-dispersive X-ray
Spectroscopy (EDS).

8.2 Technology Maturation Plan (TMP SOPO Deliverable)

 The solid oxide electrolysis cell (SOEC), the reverse operation of SOFCs for hydrogen
production, studied through this project, represents an emerging yet rapidly growing technology
segment spanning stationary, transportation, and portable applications. The global SOFC market
is projected to grow from approximately USD 2.98–3.85 billion in 2025 to USD 11.6–18.2 billion
by 2030, driven by decarbonization and distributed energy demand. In parallel, the SOEC market
is expected to expand dramatically, potentially reaching over USD 11 billion by 2032, with very

 69

high growth rates driven by deployment in the hydrogen economy. Market expansion is primarily
driven by improvements in power density, durability, and reductions in fabrication and
maintenance costs.

 A major technical barrier in SOFC/SOEC development is electrode performance and
degradation. Conventional solution-based infiltration methods can enhance cathode activity by
introducing nanoscale catalysts into porous electrode scaffolds such as LSM and LSCF. However,
these methods face significant limitations: poor penetration into deep, porous structures (~50 μm),
high catalyst-loading requirements, cost and scalability challenges, and nanoparticle coarsening
and sintering under high-temperature operation, which reduce long-term stability.

 Atomic layer deposition (ALD) is presented as a superior alternative coating technology. ALD
enables atomic-scale, conformal, and highly uniform coatings over complex porous electrode
architectures, making it uniquely suited for SOFC/SOEC systems. It also offers scalable batch
processing and precise thickness control. However, prior studies have shown mixed results, as
certain ALD chemistries (e.g., Al₂O₃, CeO₂, SrO) can degrade electrochemical performance if
improperly selected, highlighting the importance of interface engineering and materials design.

 This project demonstrates a significant breakthrough in ALD-enabled electrode engineering.
ALD coatings applied to commercial SOFC/SOEC electrodes successfully mitigate long-standing
challenges such as electrode delamination, achieving stable electrolysis operation for up to 1000
hours, whereas uncoated cells fail within ~26 hours. The ALD-modified electrodes suppress
degradation mechanisms including Sr surface segregation, chromium contamination, and
interfacial instability. The resulting nanostructured interfacial layers enhance oxygen reduction
reaction (ORR) kinetics by increasing grain boundary density, strain effects, and surface catalytic
activity, while also acting as diffusion barriers that stabilize electrode composition.

 70

 Performance improvements are substantial. ALD-coated commercial cells achieve hydrogen
production current densities of ~2.5 A/cm² at ~1.2 V, approximately doubling the performance of
state-of-the-art cells (~1 A/cm² at ~1.3 V). This improvement translates directly into system-level
benefits, including more than a twofold reduction in stack size, balance-of-plant footprint, and
capital cost.

 SOFC/SOEC system capital costs are typically dominated by balance-of-plant (BOP)
components, which often account for ~70–90% of total system cost in kW-scale analyses, while
stack costs represent a smaller fraction (~10–30%), depending on scale and design maturity.63.
Increasing power density directly reduces cost per kilowatt, enabling approximately threefold
reductions in system-level capital cost when power density is tripled. Additionally, because BOP
lifetimes (~10 years) exceed stack lifetimes (~5 years), higher power density also doubles lifetime
energy or hydrogen output.

 The project advances ALD-coated SOFC/SOEC technology from TRL 2 to TRL 3,
demonstrating analytical validation and laboratory-scale proof of concept. The ALD process is
described as scalable, repeatable, and compatible with commercial cells without altering core
electrode compositions, enabling straightforward integration into existing stack manufacturing
pathways. Technology has already yielded patentable inventions and has attracted attention from
industry stakeholders in the fuel cell and ALD manufacturing sectors.

 Overall, this work establishes ALD-enabled surface engineering as a transformative approach
for next-generation SOFC/SOEC systems, enabling higher efficiency, improved durability,
reduced degradation, and significant reductions in system-level cost, particularly for hydrogen
production and distributed energy applications.
