Sex disparities in pre-hospital delay in patients with acute myocardial infarction: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Danqing Hu, Ying Duan, Jing Song, Lili Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPre-hospital delay is a critical determinant of outcomes in acute myocardial infarction (AMI). While sex-based differences in time to presentation have been documented, evidence regarding the association between sex and pre-hospital delay remains inconsistent. This study aimed to determine whether female sex is associated with prolonged pre-hospital delay among patients with AMI.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies published between 2000 and 2025, retrieved from PubMed and Web of Science. Included studies reported multivariable-adjusted odds ratios (ORs) for the association between sex (female vs. male) and pre-hospital or patient delay in AMI. Risk of bias was assessed using the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality checklist. Summary ORs were calculated using inverse variance-weighted random-effects models. Heterogeneity was evaluated using I²and Cochran’s Q statistics. Publication bias was assessed via funnel plots and Egger’s test. ResultsTwenty-nine studies involving 127,409 AMI patients were analysed (21 evaluated pre-hospital delay, 8 focused on patient delay). Female sex was significantly associated with prolonged pre-hospital delay (OR = 1.17, 95% CI = 1.11 to 1.23, I2=46.3%) and demonstrated an even stronger association with patient delay (OR = 1.23, 95% CI = 1.10 to 1.38, I2=11.9%). These associations persisted across AMI subtypes and after adjustment for confounding factors. ConclusionSignificant sex disparities exist in pre-hospital delay among AMI patients, with women experiencing longer delays than men. Addressing these gender disparities is crucial for improving timely recognition and management of AMI, ultimately leading to better clinical outcomes and reduced gender-based healthcare inequalities.Trial registrationPROSPERO (ID: CRD420251022182).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05995-3.

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains a leading cause of cardiovascular mortality [1]. While timely reperfusion therapy is essential to improve outcomes, pre-hospital delay continues to constitute the largest component of total ischemic time and critically impacts prognosis [2, 3], despite substantial improvements in in-hospital management such as reduced door-to-balloon times. Pre-hospital delay refers to the total time from symptom onset to hospital arrival and includes both patient delay and system delay.

Although women generally experience longer pre-hospital delays than men, it remains debated whether this disparity is attributable to sex itself or to confounding factors such as older age and greater comorbidity burden among women with AMI [4–7]. For instance, data from the AMIS Plus registry indicated that sex-based differences in delay time became non-significant after multivariate adjustment [8], whereas some studies have reported persistent disparities [9–12]. These inconsistencies may stem from variations in study design, definitions of delay, and adjustment for confounders. Furthermore, female-specific characteristics, such as atypical symptom presentation, differences in symptom perception, and healthcare-seeking behaviour, may also contribute to prolonged delays.

To resolve this uncertainty, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizing evidence from the past two decades to determine whether female sex is associated with pre-hospital delay in AMI. By harmonizing definitions and comprehensively adjusting for confounders, this study aims to provide robust, generalizable evidence to inform clinical practice and public health strategies aimed at reducing sex-based disparities in AMI care and improving outcomes.

Method

Study design

This meta-analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The study protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (Registration number: CRD420251022182). As this research involved secondary analysis of published data, patient consent and ethics committee approval were not required.

Literature search

A systematic and comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed and Web of Science for studies published from January 1, 2000, to March 31, 2025. Only English-language publications were included. The same search strategy was applied across both databases: (“Acute Myocardial Infarction” OR “AMI” OR “ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction” OR “STEMI” OR “Non-ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction” OR “NSTEMI”) AND (“Prehospital Delay” OR “Pre-hospital Delay” OR “Delay in Seeking Care” OR “Time to Treatment” OR “Treatment Delay” OR “Patient Delay” OR “Decision Delay” ) AND (“woman” OR “man” OR “women” OR “men” OR “Female” OR “Male”). Reference lists of relevant systematic reviews and included studies were manually screened to identify additional eligible studies.

Study selection

Studies were included if they examined the association between sex and pre-hospital delay in patients with AMI and reported multivariable-adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Studies reporting only unadjusted estimates were excluded. Conference abstracts, unpublished studies, and studies without sufficient data to estimate effect sizes were also excluded. When multiple publications originated from the same cohort, the study providing the most comprehensive data and greatest relevance to our objective was selected. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts of all retrieved records, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible studies based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. The study selection process is shown in Fig. 1.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers using a standardized data extraction form. The following information was extracted from each eligible study: first author, publication year, study location, sample size, AMI subtype, definitions and cut-off values for pre-hospital delay, the most comprehensively adjusted ORs with 95% CIs for the association between female sex and prolonged pre-hospital delay, and the covariates adjusted for in multivariable analyses, as studies reporting only unadjusted estimates were excluded to reduce the potential influence of confounding. Detailed adjustment variables for each study are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was evaluated using the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) methodology checklist for observational studies. This tool was selected as it is specifically designed for cross-sectional and retrospective observational studies and allows systematic assessment of key domains including selection methods, measurement quality, and control of confounding. The detailed assessment results for each study are presented in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4. Overall, most studies adequately reported data sources, inclusion criteria, and confounding adjustment.

Statistical analysis

Pooled associations between female sex and prolonged pre-hospital delay were estimated using inverse variance-weighted averages of log-transformed adjusted ORs with 95% CIs. For analytic purposes, a separate meta-analysis was conducted for patient delay. Patient delay refers to the patient-related component of pre-hospital delay, typically defined as the time from symptom onset to first medical contact for self-presenting patients, or from symptom onset to the decision to seek care for patients admitted via emergency medical services.

For studies reporting ORs for male versus female patients, ORs were inverted to reflect female versus male, with corresponding 95% CIs adjusted accordingly. Forest plots were used to display study-specific and pooled estimates. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test. A random-effects model was used for all analyses to account for between-study heterogeneity.

Potential publication bias was evaluated through visual inspection of funnel plots and the Egger regression test [13]. Sensitivity analyses were performed using a leave-one-out approach to assess the robustness of pooled estimates. Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the consistency of the association between sex and pre-hospital delay across different study-level characteristics, stratified by AMI type, cut-off values used to define prolonged delay (< 2 h, = 2 h, > 2 h), and adjustment for potential confounders including age, educational level, and mode of admission. In addition, to account for heterogeneity in the definition of patient delay, studies were further stratified according to how patient delay was defined, including time from symptom onset to decision to seek care and time from symptom onset to first medical contact. Univariable meta-regression using restricted maximum likelihood in STATA (“metareg” command) was applied to explore heterogeneity by AMI type, delay definition, and covariate adjustment. Covariates with significant associations were entered into multivariable meta-regression. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 14.0, with two-sided p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Included articles

A total of 1,045 records were identified through the PubMed database and 1,470 through the Web of Science database. After removing duplicates, 1,932 studies remained. Following title and abstract screening, 332 studies were selected for full-text review. Ultimately, 20 articles comprising 21 individual studies related to pre-hospital delay were included in the meta-analysis, as one article reported separate results for patients with STEMI (ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction) and non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) [3]. Three studies used overlapping populations from the Korean AMI registry [14–16]. We retained the study with the broader age range and no additional comorbidities [15]. In addition, 8 studies that specifically focused on patient delay were also included, yielding a total of 29 studies for analysis. The detailed study selection process is presented in Fig. 1.

Study characteristics

The 29 studies included 127,409 patients. Of these, 20 articles comprising 21 individual studies evaluated the association between female sex and pre-hospital delay in patients with AMI, with a combined sample size of 113,876 patients [15, 17–30]. These studies were conducted across North America, Europe, Asia, Oceania, and South America. Regarding AMI types, 10 studies included all AMI patients [4, 15, 17, 19–22, 24, 30, 31], 9 studies focused specifically on STEMI patients [3, 18, 23, 25–29, 32], and 2 on NSTEMI patients (Table 1) [3, 5].

Female sex and prolonged pre-hospital delay

Among the 21 studies evaluating pre-hospital delay, 10 reported a significant association between female sex and prolonged delay, whereas 11 studies found no significant relationship. The pooled analysis showed that female sex was associated with an increased likelihood of prolonged pre-hospital delay in patients with AMI after multivariable adjustment (OR = 1.17, 95% CI = 1.11 to 1.23), with moderate heterogeneity across studies (I² = 46.3%, P = 0.011) (Fig. 2). Focusing specifically on patient delay, 5 of 8 studies identified a significant association between female sex and prolonged delay, while 3 reported no significant difference. The corresponding pooled analysis consistently showed that female sex was associated with increased odds of prolonged patient delay (OR = 1.23, 95% CI = 1.10 to 1.38), with low heterogeneity (I² = 11.9%, P = 0.337) (Fig. 3). To facilitate interpretation, a schematic illustration of the components of pre-hospital delay and the phase in which sex disparities appear most pronounced is presented in Fig. 4.

Subgroup meta-analyses

Subgroup analyses were performed based on AMI type, cut-off value for prolonged pre-hospital delay, and adjustment for potential confounders, and different definitions of patient delay. The association between female sex and prolonged pre-hospital delay was significant in studies including all AMI patients (OR = 1.14, 95% CI = 1.03 to 1.25; I² = 64.4%), STEMI patients (OR = 1.21, 95% CI = 1.13 to 1.29; I² = 16.7%), and NSTEMI patients (OR = 1.19, 95% CI = 1.10 to 1.29; I² = 0) (see Supplementary Figure S1). This association remained consistent across studies using different cut-off values for pre-hospital delay: less than 2 h (OR = 1.35, 95% CI = 1.02 to 1.79; I² = 19.5%), 2 h (OR = 1.14, 95% CI = 1.08 to 1.21; I² = 30.5%), and greater than 2 h (OR = 1.19, 95% CI = 1.07 to 1.32; I² = 62.5%) (see Supplementary Figure S2).

Among the 21 studies stratified by age adjustment,19 adjusted for age and 2 did not. The association between female sex and prolonged pre-hospital delay remained significant in studies adjusting for age (OR = 1.16, 95% CI = 1.11 to 1.22; I² = 43.8%), whereas no significant association was observed in those without adjustment (see Supplementary Figure S3). Subgroups stratified by education level and mode of admission produced similar findings, with female sex consistently associated with increased risk (see Supplementary Figure S4 and S5).

When stratified by how patient delay was defined, the association between female sex and prolonged delay was observed both in studies defining delay as time from symptom onset to decision to seek care (OR = 1.45, 95% CI = 1.03 to 2.04; I²= 64.4%) and in those defining delay as time from symptom onset to first medical contact (OR = 1.19, 95% CI = 1.02 to 1.38; I²= 0), with consistent directions of effect (see Supplementary Figure S6).

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

No evidence of publication bias was detected. Funnel plot assessing the association between female sex and prolonged pre-hospital delay appeared symmetrical, and Egger’s test indicated no significant small-study effects (P = 0.931) (see Supplementary Figure S7).

Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the overall results. Excluding any individual study did not significantly alter the pooled estimate (see Supplementary Table S5). Univariable meta-regression analyses showed no significant sources of heterogeneity, including AMI type (P = 0.424), cut-off value for prolonged pre-hospital delay (P = 0.976), publication year (P = 0.190), and adjustments for age (P = 0.314), admission mode (P = 0.992), or education level (P = 0.792).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis, conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines, provides robust quantitative evidence that female sex is associated with prolonged pre-hospital delay among patients with AMI. By synthesizing data from 29 studies, we found that women had 18% higher odds of delayed hospital presentation compared with men, with an even greater disparity observed in the patient-related phase. These findings underscore that sex-based disparities in care-seeking behavior persist despite advances in AMI management, highlighting a critical unmet need in cardiovascular care.

Our results help resolve longstanding inconsistencies in the literature. For example, the AMIS Plus registry indicated that sex disparities in delay had narrowed and were no longer significant after adjustment [8]. In contrast, Weininger et al. reported longer delays in women, largely attributable to atypical symptoms and lower recognition of cardiac origin [32]. Other studies from diverse populations have similarly reported conflicting results, reflecting heterogeneity in study design, adjustment strategies, and definitions of delay [9–12]. The strength of the present meta-analysis lies in its large scale, multinational coverage, and exclusive focus on studies that provided multivariable-adjusted estimates, thereby providing higher-level evidence that female sex is associated with prolonged delay, particularly during the patient-initiated phase.

Pre-hospital delay comprises patient-related delay and system-related delay; however, only patient-related delay was analyzed in this meta-analysis. The patient-related phase, spanning from symptom onset to the decision to seek care or first medical contact, emerged as a key locus of sex-based disparity. This delay may be attributable to several sex-specific factors. Women more frequently present with non-cardiac or atypical symptoms such as fatigue, back pain, or nausea alongside or instead of classic chest pain [39, 40], which can reduce recognition of cardiac origin both by patients themselves and by healthcare professionals [41]. In addition, differences in symptom interpretation and help-seeking behaviors also play a role. Women are more likely to experience premonitory symptoms, such as fatigue, days before the onset of acute infarction, which may obscure the recognition of an impending cardiac event and delay the decision to seek care [39, 42].

These findings have important clinical implications. In this study, women with AMI were more likely to experience prolonged pre-hospital delay, particularly during the patient-related phase. To address this disparity, targeted interventions may be warranted, including public awareness campaigns tailored to women to improve recognition of atypical symptoms, as well as clinician education to enhance early identification and appropriate triage of AMI in female patients. Clinicians should maintain a lower threshold for considering AMI in women presenting with non-classic or atypical symptoms and ensure timely triage and diagnostic evaluation. At the system level, incorporating prompts or protocols to better identify high-risk female patients with non-specific symptoms may further support early recognition and response. In addition, integrating sex-sensitive strategies into pre-hospital care and emergency care pathways may help reduce delays in real-world settings. Greater recognition of this disparity may help inform future efforts to reduce treatment delay and improve timely access to care.

This study has several limitations. First, moderate statistical heterogeneity was observed across the included studies. This may be partly explained by variation in healthcare-seeking behavior and healthcare system structures across study settings, which could limit the generalizability of the findings. However, these potential sources of heterogeneity should be interpreted with caution, as the lack of clearly identified sources may partly reflect limited statistical power rather than a true absence of association. Second, recall bias in reporting symptom onset and the decision to seek medical care may have affected the accuracy of pre-hospital delay estimates, particularly among women with atypical symptom presentation. Third, all studies included were observational in nature, and variation in adjustment sets, outcome definitions, and cut-off thresholds across studies may have influenced the pooled estimates, limiting causal interpretation. The predominance of binary definitions of delay and inconsistent reporting of continuous measures also limited further analysis using continuous outcomes. In addition, differences between self-presenting patients and those transported by emergency medical services, which may reflect distinct patient behaviors and healthcare system processes, could not be examined due to limited reporting in the included studies. Finally, due to lack of subscription access, the literature search was restricted to PubMed and Web of Science and did not include Embase, Scopus, or the Cochrane Library, which may have affected the completeness of the evidence. In addition, only English-language publications were included, which may have introduced language bias and affected the completeness of the evidence.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis suggests that women are more likely to experience prolonged pre-hospital delay in AMI, with the most pronounced disparity observed during the patient-related phase. Further studies are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying these sex differences and to inform strategies aimed at reducing delay.
