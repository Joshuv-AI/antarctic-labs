Multicritical Scaling Limit of Shifted Schur Measure
Haruna Aida, Taro Kimura

Abstract
. We investigate the multicritical scaling limit of the shifted Schur measures. Under an appropriate scaling limit and specific conditions on the continuous parameters, we explicitly determine the limit shape of strict partitions distributed according to the shifted Schur measure. We then show that, under a multicritical condition, the edge scaling limit of the correlation function converges to a determinant of the higher-order Airy kernel. This rigorously demonstrates a transition from a Pfaffian point process to a determinantal distribution in the scaling limit.



1.
Introduction

A
random partition
is a random variable that takes a value in the set of partitions.
In the large-scale limit, the profile of partitions, distributed under an appropriate measure, converges to a continuous curve known as the
limit shape
.
In this context, fluctuation in the vicinity of the profile edge is particularly interesting, since it typically shows a universal structure observed in various probabilistic and statistical models.
The limit to focus on the edge fluctuation is called the
edge scaling limit
.

The limit shape was first discovered for the Plancherel measure by Logan and Shepp
[
LS77
]
and Vershik and Kerov
[
VK77
]
.
Via Kerov’s transition measures
[
Ker93
]
, this limit shape matches Wigner’s semicircle of Gaussian random matrices.
In fact, this convergence can be viewed as the free central limit theorem in free probability
[
Bia98
]
.
Moreover, as shown by Baik, Deift, and Johansson
[
BDJ98
]
, Borodin, Okounkov, and Olshanski
[
BOO99
]
and Johansson
[
Joh99
]
, the edge scaling limit of the random permutations and the Plancherel measure yields the
Tracy-Widom distribution
, originally introduced to describe the fluctuation of the largest eigenvalue of the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble
[
TW92
]
.
As these examples suggest, different probability models often share the universal behaviour in the edge scaling limit.

We may regard the Plancherel measure as a special case of the
Schur measures
introduced by Okounkov
[
Oko99
]
.
The Schur measure assigns to each partition
\lambda
a probability proportional to the product of two Schur functions:

\mathbb{P}_{\text{S}}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{Z_{\text{S}}}s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{y}),

where
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
are sets of parameters, and
Z_{\text{S}}
is a normalisation constant depending on
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
. This algebraic definition naturally yields a
determinantal point process
, whose correlation functions are given by determinants constructed from a correlation kernel, and satisfy the KP (or 2D Toda lattice) hierarchy
[
Oko99
]
.
In the context of the boson-fermion correspondence of type
A_{\infty}
, the Schur measure is described via a
coherent state
(a simultaneous eigenstate of annihilation operators) of
charged
free fermions. The variables
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
(or their power-sum counterparts, called the Miwa variables) then represent the eigenvalues of this state. To determine the limit shape and the edge scaling limit explicitly, we need to evaluate these eigenvalues for a specific choice of parameters.

Betea, Bouttier, and Walsh
[
BBW23
]
(announced in
[
BBW20
]
) studied a specialisation called the
multicritical Schur measures
, finding both the limit shape and the edge scaling limit.
The edge scaling limit corresponds to the higher-order Tracy-Widom distribution
[
LDMS18
]
, which can be obtained by replacing the Airy function in the definition of Tracy–Widom distribution with the
higher Airy function
.

]
). We follow the convention in
[
KZ22
]
.

[

; see also
[

.

Summary of results

In this paper, we investigate the multicritical scaling limit for the
shifted Schur measures

[
TW02
]
. They are rooted in the boson-fermion correspondence of type
B_{\infty}
for
neutral
(or
Majorana
) fermions
[
JM83
]
. Algebraically, for a
strict
partition
\lambda
, the shifted Schur measure is given by

\mathbb{P}_{\text{SS}}(\lambda;t)=\frac{1}{Z}P_{\lambda}(t)Q_{\lambda}(t),

where
P_{\lambda}(t),Q_{\lambda}(t)
are Schur’s
P,Q
-functions, respectively, depending on
t=(t_{1},t_{3},\dots)
a set of infinite parameters, and
Z
is a normalisation constant. See
Section 3
for details. This change in the underlying fermionic structure—from charged to neutral—is reflected in the stochastic properties of the measure: it gives rise to a
Pfaffian point process
, and its correlation functions satisfy the BKP hierarchy rather than the KP hierarchy
[
Mat03
,
WL18
]
.
Our first main result describes the limit shape of strict partitions under the shifted Schur measure:

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem
4.1
)
.

Subject to specific conditions on the parameter
t=(t_{1},t_{3},\dots)
, and under an appropriate scaling limit, the limit shape of a partition distributed according to the shifted Schur measure is given by

x+\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\max\{D(\theta)-x,0\}{\rm d}\theta\quad(x\geq 0),

where
D(\theta)=4\sum_{n:\text{odd}}nt_{n}\cos{n\theta}
.

In order to discuss the limit shape itself, the multicriticality condition is not yet necessary.
Although it may not be immediately obvious from the formula, this limit shape is non-differentiable at the point
x=4\sum_{n:\text{odd}}nt_{n}
, which is identified as the profile edge. We provide visual examples of these shapes in
Figure 3
.

We now shift our focus to the fluctuations near the edge, specifically in the specific scaling limit.
More generally, we study the edge scaling limit of the
N
-point correlation function:

\rho_{\rm SS}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N};t)=\sum_{\lambda\supset\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\}}\mathbb{P}_{\rm SS}(\lambda;t).

To investigate the edge scaling behaviour, we prescribe the
p
-multicritical condition on the parameter
t
for
p\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.
Our second main result is stated as follows:

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem
5.2
)
.

Suppose the parameter
t=(t_{1},t_{3},\dots)
satisfies specific conditions including the
p
-multicritical condition.
Then, under an appropriate scaling, the edge scaling limit of the
N
-point correlation function is given by

\det_{1\leq i,j\leq N}K_{p\text{-Airy}}(a_{i},a_{j}),

where
K_{p\text{-Airy}}
denotes the
p
-Airy kernel (see
Appendix A
).

The case
p=2
was obtained by Matsumoto
[
Mat03
]
. Remarkably, although the shifted Schur measure itself is a Pfaffian point process, its edge scaling limit converges to the
determinant
of the
p
-Airy kernel. This transition arises because the
N
-point correlation function can be expressed as the Pfaffian of a
2N\times 2N
matrix; as we take the scaling limit, the diagonal blocks of this matrix vanish. Consequently, the Pfaffian reduces to a determinant via the identity:

\mathrm{Pf}\begin{pmatrix}O&A\\
-A^{\top}&O\end{pmatrix}=(-1)^{\binom{N}{2}}\det A.

The proof of this theorem provides a rigorous foundation for the approach suggested in
[
KZ20
,
KZ22
]
.

Organisation of the paper

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.

Section 2
briefly reviews the theory of neutral fermions and the associated boson-fermion correspondence, following
[
WL18
]
.
In
Section 3
, we formulate the shifted Schur measures in terms of neutral fermions, showing that a correlation function are given by a Pfaffian, and deriving a double contour integral representation for the correlation kernel.

Section 4
is devoted to the limit shape analysis of the shifted Young diagrams, where we prove our first main theorem (Theorem
4.1
).
In
Section 5
, we focus on the multicritical edge scaling limit and prove our second main theorem (Theorem
5.2
), establishing the convergence of the correlation function to the determinant of the
p
-Airy kernel.
Finally,
Appendix A
summarizes the fundamental properties of higher Airy functions.

2.
Neutral Fermions and Their Boson-Fermion Correspondence

To pave the way for the definition of the shifted Schur measure, we first recall the essentials of the theory of neutral fermions (also known as Majorana fermions) and the associated boson-fermion correspondence, following the treatment in
[
WL18
]
.

Let
\psi_{a},\psi_{a}^{*}\,(a\in\mathbb{Z})
denote the charged fermions. They satisfy the standard anti-commutation relations

[\psi_{a},\psi_{b}]_{+}=0,\quad[\psi^{*}_{a},\psi^{*}_{b}]_{+}=0,\quad[\psi_{a},\psi^{*}_{b}]_{+}=\delta_{a+b,0},

where
[A,B]_{+}=AB+BA
denotes the anti-commutator.
Let
W_{A}
be the complex linear space spanned by
\{\psi_{a},\psi_{a}^{*}\}_{a\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Then, these relations naturally give rise to the Clifford algebra
Cl(W_{A})
associated with the charged fermions.

The algebra
Cl(W_{A})
admits a canonical representation constructed as follows.
Consider a linear space spanned by the basis vectors
\{e_{a}\}_{a\in\mathbb{Z}}
, and let
\mathcal{F}_{A}
be the Fock space generated by infinite wedge of the form

(2.1)

\cdots\wedge e_{a_{3}}\wedge e_{a_{2}}\wedge e_{a_{1}},\quad a_{1}>a_{2}>a_{3}>\cdots,

subject to the condition that the set of negative integers omitted from
\{a_{k}\}_{k=1,2,\ldots}
is finite.
We define the representation
\varrho_{A}\colon Cl(W_{A})\to\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{F}_{A})
by its action:

\displaystyle\varrho_{A}(\psi_{a})(\bullet)

\displaystyle=\bullet\wedge e_{-a},

\displaystyle\varrho_{A}(\psi^{*}_{a})(\bullet\wedge e_{a})

\displaystyle=\bullet.

Upon interpreting the vector (
2.1
) as a quantum state where the sites
\{a_{k}\}_{k=1,2,\ldots}
on a one-dimensional lattice are occupied by charged fermions,
\psi_{a}
and
\psi^{*}_{a}
act as creation and annihilation operators, respectively.

The neutral fermions
\phi_{a}\,(a\in\mathbb{Z})
are characterised by the following anti-commutation relations:

(2.2)

[\phi_{a},\phi_{b}]_{+}=(-1)^{a}\delta_{a+b,0}.

These operators can be expressed in terms of the charged fermions
\psi_{a},\psi^{*}_{a}
via the relation

(2.3)

\phi_{a}=\frac{\psi_{a}+(-1)^{a}\psi^{*}_{a}}{\sqrt{2}}.

Letting
W_{B}
denote the subspace of
W_{A}
spanned by
\phi_{a}\,(a\in\mathbb{Z})
, we obtain the Clifford algebra
Cl(W_{B})
of neutral fermions as a subalgebra of
Cl(W_{A})
.

The term ‘neutral’ comes from the fact that creation and annihilation operators are linearly combined on the right-hand side of (
2.3
).
In what follows, we shall be concerned with a representation in which
\phi_{a>0}
and
\phi_{a<0}
act as creation and annihilation operators, respectively.
To this end, consider a linear space spanned by
e_{a}\,(a\geq 0)
, and let
\mathcal{F}_{B}
be its exterior algebra.
A basis of
\mathcal{F}_{B}
is given by

(2.4)

e_{a_{l}}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{a_{1}},\quad a_{1}>\cdots>a_{l},

where the case
l=0
is permitted (corresponding to the scalar
1\in\mathbb{C}
).
We define the representation
\varrho_{B}\colon Cl(W_{B})\to\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{F}_{B})
by the action:

\displaystyle\varrho_{B}(\phi_{a\geq 0})(\bullet)

\displaystyle=\bullet\wedge e_{a}

\displaystyle\varrho_{B}(\phi_{-a\leq 0)})(\bullet\wedge e_{a})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{a}\bullet.

Analogous to the charged fermion case, the vector (
2.4
) can be interpreted as a quantum state in which the sites indexed by
a_{1},\ldots,a_{l}
on a one-dimensional lattice are occupied by neutral fermions.

For convenience, we shall henceforth suppress the symbol
\varrho_{B}
and set
|0\rangle=1\in\mathcal{F}_{B}
.
The vector (
2.4
) is then written as

\phi_{a_{1}}\cdots\phi_{a_{l}}|0\rangle.

Representing the state in which no neutral fermions are present,
|0\rangle
is referred to as the vacuum state.

The linear map

\varrho_{B}(\cdot)(|0\rangle):Cl(W_{B})\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}_{B}

induces a vector space isomorphism

\mathcal{F}_{B}\simeq Cl(W_{B})\Big/Cl(W_{B})\Big(\sum_{a<0}\mathbb{C}\phi_{a}\Big).

Dually, we define the space
\mathcal{F}_{B}^{*}
as the quotient

\mathcal{F}_{B}^{*}\coloneqq Cl(W_{B})\Big/\Big(\sum_{a>0}\mathbb{C}\phi_{a}\Big)Cl(W_{B})

and denote by
\langle 0|\,\in\mathcal{F}_{B}^{*}
the residue class of
1\in Cl(W_{B})
.
Since the multiplication in
Cl(W_{B})
descends to the map

\mathcal{F}_{B}^{*}\times\mathcal{F}_{B}\to Cl(W_{B})\Big/\Big(\sum_{a>0}\mathbb{C}\phi_{a}\Big)Cl(W_{B})\Big(\sum_{a<0}\mathbb{C}\phi_{a}\Big)\simeq\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}\phi_{0},

we obtain a bilinear pairing
\mathcal{F}_{B}^{*}\times\mathcal{F}_{B}\to\mathbb{C}
by imposing

\langle 0|1|0\rangle=1,\quad\text{and}\quad\langle 0|\phi_{0}|0\rangle=0.

The pairing is referred to as the vacuum expectation value.

Next, we introduce the operator
H_{n}
defined by

H_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{a+1}\phi_{a}\phi_{-a-n}.

This is termed the Hamiltonian, a choice motivated in part by the following commutation relation:

(2.5)

[H_{n},\phi_{a}]=\begin{cases}\phi_{a-n}&\text{for $n$ odd}\\
0&\text{for $n$ even}.\end{cases}

By assigning an energy
a
to each neutral fermion
\phi_{a}
, the action of
H_{n}
on
\phi_{a}
can be seen to decrease the energy by
n
.
In this setting, the operators
H_{n}
vanish for even
n
, whilst the remaining operators
\{H_{n}\mid n\in 2\mathbb{Z}+1\}
generate a Heisenberg algebra satisfying

(2.6)

[H_{m},H_{n}]=\frac{m}{2}\delta_{n+m,0}.

This correspondence yields a bosonisation of the neutral fermions.

Further to the above, we introduce the formal variables
t=(t_{1},t_{3},\dots)
(often referred to as
Miwa variables
) and a complex variable
z\in\mathbb{C}
.
We define

H_{+}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1,\text{odd}}t_{n}H_{n},\quad\phi(z)=\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}}\phi_{a}z^{a},\quad\xi(t,z)=\sum_{n\geq 1,\text{odd}}t_{n}z^{n}.

These definitions lead to the identity

e^{H_{+}(t)}\phi(z)e^{-H_{+}(t)}=e^{\xi(t,z)}\phi(z).

We are now in a position to state the boson-fermion correspondence for neutral fermions
[
JM83
]
.
First, we partition the Fock space into its even and odd components:

\mathcal{F}_{B}^{0}=\text{span}\{\phi_{a_{1}}\cdots\phi_{a_{2k}}|0\rangle\},\quad\mathcal{F}_{B}^{1}=\text{span}\{\phi_{a_{1}}\cdots\phi_{a_{2k-1}}|0\rangle\}.

We define the linear maps

(2.7)

\begin{split}\sigma^{i}\colon\mathcal{F}_{B}^{i}&\to\mathbb{C}[t_{1},t_{3},\ldots]\\
|U\rangle&\mapsto\langle i|e^{H_{+}(t)}|U\rangle,\end{split}

where we set
|1\rangle=2^{\frac{1}{2}}\phi_{0}|0\rangle
.
It is worth noting that although
e^{H_{+}(t)}
is a formal power series, the image
\sigma^{i}(|U\rangle)
is always a polynomial in
t
; this is guaranteed by the relation (
2.5
).

Theorem 2.1

(Boson-fermion correspondence for neutral fermions
[
JM83
]
)
.

The homomorphism
\sigma^{i}
constitutes an isomorphism of representations between

(\mathcal{F}_{B}^{i},H_{n\geq 1},H_{n\leq-1})\quad\text{and}\quad(\mathbb{C}[t_{1},t_{3},\ldots],\partial/\partial t_{n},(n/2)t_{n}).

Moreover, the fermionic action on the Fock space is intertwined with the action of the vertex operator

(2.8)

V(z)=V^{-}(z)V^{+}(z),

on
\mathbb{C}[t_{1},t_{3},\dots]
via the relation

\sigma^{i}\circ\phi(z)=2^{-\frac{1}{2}}V(z)\circ\sigma^{1-i.},

where the components of
V(z)
are given by

\displaystyle V^{-}(z)=\exp\left(2\sum_{n\geq 1,\text{odd}}\frac{z^{n}}{n}\frac{n}{2}t_{n}\right)=\exp\xi(t,z),\quad V^{+}(z)=\exp\left(2\sum_{n\geq 1,\text{odd}}\frac{z^{-n}}{-n}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{n}}\right).

3.
Shifted Schur Measures in Terms of Neutral Fermions

A
strict partition
is a strictly decreasing sequence of strictly positive integers
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{l})
, i.e.,
\lambda_{1}>\dots>\lambda_{l}>0
.
We denote the set of all strict partitions by
\mathcal{D}
. For each
\lambda\in\mathcal{D}
, we define

\alpha(\lambda)=\begin{cases}0&l(\lambda)\text{ is even},\\
1&l(\lambda)\text{ is odd},\end{cases}

where
l(\lambda)=l
signifies the length of the partition.

Recalling that
|1\rangle=2^{\frac{1}{2}}\phi_{0}|0\rangle
, it follows that for any
\lambda\in\mathcal{D}
, the state
\phi_{\lambda_{1}}\cdots\phi_{\lambda_{l}}|\alpha(\lambda)\rangle
lies within the even component
\mathcal{F}_{B}^{0}
of the Fock space.
We define the
Schur
Q
-function

Q_{\lambda}(t)
by

(3.1)

Q_{\lambda}(t)=2^{\frac{l(\lambda)}{2}}\sigma_{B}^{0}(\phi_{\lambda_{1}}\cdots\phi_{\lambda_{l}}|\alpha(\lambda)\rangle)\rvert_{t=2t}=2^{\frac{l(\lambda)}{2}}\langle 0|e^{H_{+}(2t)}\phi_{\lambda_{1}}\cdots\phi_{\lambda_{l}}|\alpha(\lambda)\rangle,

where the second equality is a consequence of (
2.7
).
Furthermore, we set
P_{\lambda}(t)=2^{-l(\lambda)}Q_{\lambda}(t)
.

A straightforward calculation using Wick’s theorem yields

\langle 0|e^{H_{-}(2t)}\phi_{\lambda_{1}}\cdots\phi_{\lambda_{l}}|\alpha(\lambda)\rangle=(-1)^{|\lambda|}\langle\alpha(\lambda)|\phi_{-\lambda_{l}}\cdots\phi_{-\lambda_{1}}e^{H_{-}(2t)}|0\rangle,

where
|\lambda|=\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{l}
denotes the size of the partition and
H_{-}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1,\text{odd}}t_{n}H_{-n}
.
This relation implies that the value of
Q_{\lambda}(t)
is proportional to the coefficient of the basis vector
\phi_{\lambda_{1}}\cdots\phi_{\lambda_{l}}|\alpha(\lambda)\rangle
in the expansion of the state
e^{H_{-}(2t)}|0\rangle
.

The state
e^{H_{-}(2t)}|0\rangle\in\mathcal{F}_{B}^{0}
is a
coherent state
, in the sense that it is a simultaneous eigenvector of the operators
H_{n}\,(n\geq 1)
.
Indeed, using (
2.6
), one finds the eigenvalue relation

H_{n}e^{H_{-}(2t)}|0\rangle=nt_{n}e^{H_{-}(2t)}|0\rangle,

whilst the size is given by

\langle 0|e^{H_{+}(2\overline{t})}e^{H_{-}(2t)}|0\rangle=e^{[H_{+}(2\overline{t}),H_{-}(2t)]}=\exp{\textstyle\left(\sum_{n\ge 1, \text{odd}}2n\, |t_n|^2\right)},

where
\overline{t}=(\overline{t}_{1},\overline{t}_{3},\dots)
denotes the complex conjugate of
t
.
Consequently, the assignment

\mathcal{D}\ni\lambda\mapsto\frac{|\langle 0|e^{H_{+}(2t)}\phi_{\lambda_{1}}\cdots\phi_{\lambda_{l}}|\alpha(\lambda)\rangle|^{2}}{\langle 0|e^{H_{+}(2\overline{t})}e^{H_{-}(2t)}|0\rangle}=\frac{P_{\lambda}(\overline{t})Q_{\lambda}(t)}{\exp{\left(\sum_{n\ge 1, \text{odd}}2n\, |t_n|^2\right)}}

defines a probability measure on
\mathcal{D}
, which we call the (Hermitian)
shifted Schur measure
.
We denote this measure by
\mathbb{P}_{\rm SS}(\lambda;t)=\frac{1}{Z}P_{\lambda}(\overline{t})Q_{\lambda}(t)
, with the partition function
Z=\exp\left(\sum_{n\geq 1,\text{odd}}2n\,|t_{n}|^{2}\right)
.

For a finite set
A\subset\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, we define the
correlation function
,

(3.2)

\rho(A;t)=\mathbb{P}_{\rm{SS}}(\{\lambda\in\mathcal{D}\mid A\subset\lambda\};t)=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\lambda\supset A}P_{\lambda}(\overline{t})Q_{\lambda}(t).

In terms of the neutral fermions,
\mathbb{P}_{\rm{SS}}(\lambda;t)
represents the probability that the fermions occupy exactly the sites corresponding to
\lambda
in the coherent state
e^{H_{+}(2t)}|0\rangle
, whereas the correlation function
\rho(A;t)
signifies the probability that at least the sites in
A
are occupied.
Let us define the correlation kernel
K(a,b;t)
by the vacuum expectation

K(a,b;t)=\langle 0|e^{H_{+}(2\overline{t})}e^{-H_{-}(2t)}\phi_{a}\phi_{b}e^{H_{-}(2t)}e^{-H_{+}(2\overline{t})}|0\rangle.

For a set
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\}
of size
N
, we introduce a skew-symmetric matrix
M(A;t)
of order
2N
, whose entries are prescribed by

\displaystyle M(A;t)_{i,j}=\begin{cases}K(a_{i},a_{j};t)&0<i<j\leq N,\\
(-1)^{a_{2N-j+1}}K(a_{i},-a_{2N-j+1};t)&0<i\leq N<j\leq 2N,\\
(-1)^{a_{2N-i+1}+a_{2N-j+1}}K(-a_{2N-i+1},-a_{2N-j+1};t)&N<i<j\leq 2N.\end{cases}

Theorem 3.1

(
[
Mat03
,
WL18
]
)
.

The correlation function (
3.2
) is given by a Pfaffian,

\rho(A;t)=\mathrm{Pf}(M(A;t)),

hence the shifted Schur measure induces a
Pfaffian point process
.

To clarify the structural origin of the matrix
M(A;t)
, we observe that the correlation function admits the representation

\displaystyle\rho(A)

\displaystyle=\langle 0|\Phi_{a_{1}}(-1)^{a_{1}}\Phi_{-a_{1}}\cdots\Phi_{a_{N}}(-1)^{a_{N}}\Phi_{-a_{N}}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle 0|\Phi_{a_{1}}\cdots\Phi_{a_{N}}(-1)^{a_{N}}\Phi_{-a_{N}}\cdots(-1)^{a_{1}}\Phi_{-a_{1}}|0\rangle,

where
\Phi_{a}=G\phi_{a}G^{-1}
with
G=e^{H_{+}(2\overline{t})}e^{-H_{-}(2t)}
(cf.
[
WL18
, Theorem 3.3]
).
Since each
\Phi_{a}
is a linear combination of neutral fermions
\{\phi_{a}\}_{a\in\mathbb{Z}}
, it naturally decomposes into creation and annihilation parts, alongside the zero mode
\phi_{0}
.
By defining the sequence of operators

(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{2N})=(\Phi_{a_{1}},\ldots,\Phi_{a_{N}},(-1)^{a_{N}}\Phi_{-a_{N}},\ldots,(-1)^{a_{1}}\Phi_{-a_{1}})

and applying Wick’s theorem, we obtain

\rho(A;t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{2N},\\
\sigma(1)<\sigma(3)<\cdots<\sigma(2N-1),\\
\sigma(2i-1)<\sigma(2i)\end{subarray}}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{N}\langle 0|\eta_{\sigma(2i-1)}\eta_{\sigma(2i)}|0\rangle.

The matrix
M(A;t)
is then recovered as the skew-symmetric extension of the values
\langle 0|\eta_{i}\eta_{j}|0\rangle
for
i<j
.

Notably, the correlation kernel
K(a,b;t)
admits a double contour integral expression that is amenable to asymptotic analysis.
Following the approach in
[
Mat03
]
, we introduce an auxiliary function, which we call the wave function

(3.3)

\mathbb{J}(z;t)=e^{2\xi(\overline{t},z)}e^{2\xi(t,-z^{-1})}.

Using the transformation property
G\phi(z)G^{-1}=\mathbb{J}(z)\phi(z)
, the kernel is expressed as

\displaystyle K(a,b;t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\rm{i})^{2}}\oiint_{|z|>|w|}\langle 0|G\phi(z)\phi(w)G^{-1}|0\rangle\frac{{\rm d}z{\rm d}w}{z^{a+1}w^{b+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\rm{i})^{2}}\oiint_{|z|>|w|}\mathbb{J}(z)\mathbb{J}(w)\langle 0|\phi(z)\phi(w)|0\rangle\frac{{\rm d}z{\rm d}w}{z^{a+1}w^{b+1}}

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(2\pi\rm{i})^{2}}\oiint_{|z|>|w|}\mathbb{J}(z)\mathbb{J}(w)\frac{z-w}{z+w}\frac{{\rm d}z{\rm d}w}{z^{a+1}w^{b+1}},

where the final equality is a direct consequence of the anti-commutation relations (
2.2
).

4.
Limit Shape Analysis

In this section, we introduce the shifted Young diagram, an effective tool to visualise configurations of neutral fermions.

To construct a shifted Young diagram for a strict partition
\lambda
, we take a standard Young diagram and indent its
i
-th row to the right by
i-1
boxes.
To facilitate our limit shape analysis, we transpose this diagram, rotate it by
135^{\circ}
, and re-orient it, as illustrated in
Figure 1
.

Figure 1
.
Shifted Young diagram for the strict partition
\lambda=(9,6,3,2)
.

We then map the resulting boundary to a sequence of black and white stones on the lattice
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
(the
Maya diagram
) following a simple geometric rule: a white stone corresponds to a southeast-oriented boundary segment, whilst a black stone indicates a northeast-oriented one as shown in
Figure 2
.

Figure 2
.
The correspondence between a shifted Young diagram and its associated Maya diagram.

By inspection, for a shifted diagram associated with a strict partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{i})
, the positions of the white stones coincide exactly with the parts of the partition
\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots\}
.
We associate these white stones with the neutral fermions.
More formally, the stone configuration corresponds to the even state
\phi_{\lambda_{1}}\cdots\phi_{\lambda_{l}}|\alpha(\lambda)\rangle\in\mathcal{F}_{B}^{0}
.

The shifted Young diagram is central to the combinatorial definition of the Schur
Q
-function
Q_{\lambda}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
.
Under the Miwa transformation
t_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}x_{i}^{n}
, this combinatorial definition neatly coincides with the algebraic expression in (
3.1
) (see, for instance,
[
TW02
]
).

We now turn to the limit shape of the shifted Young diagram as the lattice mesh size
\varepsilon
tends to zero (the scaling limit).
Concurrently, we scale the Miwa variables
t
by
1/\varepsilon
, ensuring the number of boxes in the diagram grows at a rate of
O(\varepsilon^{-2})
.

The profile (or height function) of a shifted Young diagram for a strict partition
\lambda
is characterised by:

\psi_{\lambda,1}(x)=x+2\#\{i\mid x<\lambda_{i}\},\quad x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

Rescaling the box size by
\varepsilon
yields the profile function:

\psi_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x)=x+2\varepsilon\#\{i\mid x<\lambda_{i}\varepsilon\},\quad x\in\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

Our primary interest lies in its expectation value, which can be expressed in terms of the one-point correlation function:

\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x)]=x+2\varepsilon\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}+k;\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\right).

This expectation is taken with respect to the scaled shifted Schur measure
\mathbb{P}_{\rm SS}^{\varepsilon}(\lambda)=\mathbb{P}_{\rm SS}(\lambda;t/\varepsilon)
.

Constraints on the Miwa Variables

To ensure a well-defined limit shape with specific critical behaviour, we assume that the Miwa variables
t
satisfy the following conditions:

(i)

Real-valuedness:

t_{1},t_{3},\dots
are all real numbers.

(ii)

Finite support:

t_{n}=0
except for a finite number of
n
.

(iii)

p
-Multicriticality:
There exists a positive constant
a>0
such that the function

\varphi(\theta)=4\sum_{n>0,\text{odd}}t_{n}\sin n\theta-a\theta,

possesses a Taylor expansion at
\theta=0
of the form

(4.1)

\varphi(\theta)=-\frac{\theta^{p+1}}{p+1}+O(\theta^{p+3}),

for a given positive even integer
p
.

(iv)

Non-degeneracy:
The variables satisfy

4\sum_{n>0,\text{odd}}n^{2}t_{n}\sin n\theta>0\quad\text{for all }\theta\in(0,\pi).

These constraints mirror the conditions established in
[
BBW23
]
for the multicritical Schur measure.
In particular, condition (iii) represents a system of
p/2
linear equations and a linear inequality in
t
.
By choosing
t_{1},t_{3},\ldots,t_{p-1}
to satisfy these equations and setting
t_{p+1}=t_{p+3}=\cdots=0
, the variables naturally satisfy conditions (i)–(iv).
We refer to this specific configuration as the
minimal
p
-multicritical shifted Schur measure
.
For instance, when
p=2
, the choice
t_{1}=1/2
,
t_{n>1}=0
, and
a=2
neatly satisfies all the above criteria.

Before proceeding to the limit shape analysis, we outline several consequences of conditions (i)–(iv) that will be essential for our later derivations.

Consequences of the Constraints

First, assume the Miwa variables
t
satisfy conditions (i)–(iii).
The Taylor expansion (
4.1
) implies that
\varphi^{(k)}(0)=0
for
k=0,1,\dots p
.
Specifically, the condition
\varphi^{\prime}(0)=0
leads to the identity:

a=4\sum_{n:\text{odd}}nt_{n},

which subsequently yields
\varphi^{\prime}(\pi)=-2a
.
If the non-degeneracy condition (iv) also holds,
\varphi^{\prime}(\theta)
decreases monotonically from
\varphi^{\prime}(0)=0
to
\varphi^{\prime}(\pi)=-2a
(assuming a multicritical setting with a fixed
a
).
Moreover, there exists a constant
C>1
such that

(4.2)

\varphi^{\prime}(\theta)\leq-\frac{\theta^{p}}{C}\quad\text{on}-\pi\leq\theta\leq\pi.

Next, consider the case where
t
satisfies (i), (ii), and (iv).
We define the function
D(\theta)=4\sum_{n:\text{odd}}nt_{n}\cos{n\theta}
.
Since
D(\theta)
decreases monotonically from
D(0)=b
to
D(\pi)=-b
, where

b\coloneqq 4\sum_{n:\text{odd}}nt_{n}>0,

it follows that for any
x\in[-b,b]
, there exists a unique
\chi(x)\in[0,\pi]
such that

(4.3)

D(\chi(x))=x.

From this point onward, we take conditions (i) and (ii) as our baseline assumptions for the Miwa variables
t
.

Integral Representation of the Profile Function

With these preparations, we return to the expectation value of the profile function.
The Pfaffian expression for the correlation function (Theorem
3.1
) allows us to write the scaled one-point function as follows,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\rho\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}+k;\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{x/\varepsilon+k}}{2(2\pi\rm{i})^{2}}\oiint_{|z|>|w|}\mathbb{J}(z)\mathbb{J}(w)\frac{z-w}{z+w}\frac{{\rm d}z{\rm d}w}{z^{x/\varepsilon+k+1}w^{-x/\varepsilon-k+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{x/\varepsilon+k}}{2(2\pi\rm{i})^{2}}\oiint_{|z|>|w|}e^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[S(z,x)+S(w,-x)]}\frac{z-w}{z+w}\frac{{\rm d}z{\rm d}w}{z^{k+1}w^{-k+1}},

where the action function
S(z,x)
is defined by

(4.5)

S(z,x)=\xi(2t,z)+\xi(2t,-z^{-1})-x\log z.

Utilising the identity
e^{S(w,-x)/\varepsilon}=e^{-S(-w,x)/\varepsilon}(-1)^{x/\varepsilon}\,\,(x/\varepsilon\in\mathbb{Z})
, the expression (
4.4
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\rho\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}+k;\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{2(2\pi\rm{i})^{2}}\oiint_{|z|>|w|}e^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[S(z,x)-S(-w,x)]}\frac{z-w}{z+w}\frac{{\rm d}z{\rm d}w}{z^{k+1}w^{-k+1}}

(4.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(2\pi\rm{i})^{2}}\oiint_{|z|>|w|}e^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]}\frac{z+w}{z-w}\frac{{\rm d}z{\rm d}w}{z^{k+1}w^{-k+1}}.

Finally, by summing over
k\in\{1,2,\dots\}
, we arrive at a double contour integral representation for the expectation of the profile function:

(4.7)

2\varepsilon\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}+k;\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\right)=\frac{\varepsilon}{(2\pi\rm{i})^{2}}\oiint_{|z|>|w|}e^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]}\frac{z+w}{z(z-w)^{2}}{\rm d}z{\rm d}w.

The following theorem characterises the deterministic limit of the profile function under the bulk scaling limit.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
b=4\sum_{n:\text{odd}}nt_{n}
.
Suppose the Miwa variables
t
satisfy the conditions (i),(ii) and (iv). Then, we have

(4.8)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}2\varepsilon\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}+k;\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\right)=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\chi(x)}(D(\theta)-x){\rm d}\theta&0\leq x\leq b,\\
0&b\leq x.\end{cases}

It follows that the limit shape of the shifted Young diagram is described by the deterministic function

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x)]=\begin{cases}x+\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\chi(x)}(D(\theta)-x){\rm d}\theta&0\leq x\leq b\\
x&b\leq x.\end{cases}

To gain further intuition, let us consider specific choices for the Miwa variables.

Example 4.2
.

Consider the standard case where
t_{1}=1/2,t_{3}=t_{5}=\cdots=0
and
a=b=2
.
In this setting, the relation (
4.3
) yields
\chi(x)=\arccos{x/2}
.
A straightforward integration then yields the limit shape:

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x)]=\begin{cases}x+\frac{2}{\pi}(\sqrt{4-x^{2}}-x\arccos{\frac{x}{2}})&0\leq x\leq 2\\
x&2\leq x\end{cases}

which is the Logan-Shepp-Vershik-Kerov curve.

Proof of
Theorem 4.1
.

We restrict our attention to the case
0\leq x\leq b
, as the logic for
x\geq b
follows naturally from identical arguments.

We deform the contour of integration in (
4.7
) so that the integral vanishes as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
This deformation inevitably picks up a residue from the
\frac{1}{(z-w)^{2}}
pole in the integrand, which precisely yields the right-hand side of (
4.8
).

Differentiating
S(re^{{\rm i}\theta},x)
with respect to
r
at
r=1
yields

\operatorname{Re}S(re^{{\rm i}\theta},x)=(r-1)[D(\theta)-x]+O((r-1)^{2}).

Thus, by deforming the integration contours to
c_{z}
and
c_{w}
as are illustrated in
Figure 6
,
one can ensure that
\operatorname{Re}[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]<0
everywhere except at the points
z,w=e^{\pm{\rm i}\chi(x)}
.

We carry out this deformation as follows.
Starting from the integration paths
\absolutevalue{z}\equiv\text{const.}>1
and
\absolutevalue{w}\equiv 1
, we rewrite the integral (
4.7
) as a repeated integral
\int{\rm d}w\int{\rm d}z\cdots
.
First, we deform the path for
z
to
c_{z}
.
We partition the path for
w
into
c_{\chi}
and
c^{\prime}_{\chi}
, as illustrated in
Figure 4
.

Figure 4
.

and
c^{\prime}_{\chi}

When
w\in c_{\chi}
, the contour for
z
is forced to cross the point
w
, which incurs a residue (see
Figure 5
):

\operatorname*{Res}_{z=w}\left\{e^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}S(z,x)-S(w,x)}\frac{z+w}{z(z-w)^{2}}\right\}=\frac{2}{\varepsilon}S^{\prime}(w,x)-\frac{1}{w}.

Conversely, when
w\in c^{\prime}_{\chi}
, no such obstruction occurs.

w\in c_{\chi}

(b)

w\in c^{\prime}_{\chi}

With the deformation for

complete, we subsequently deform the path for

to

as shown in

.

Summarizing the deformation procedure, we obtain

\displaystyle 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}+k;\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\rm{i})^{2}}\oiint_{|z|>|w|}e^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]}\frac{z+w}{z(z-w)^{2}}{\rm d}z{\rm d}w

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\rm{i}}\int_{w\in c_{\chi}}\operatorname*{Res}_{z=w}e^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]}\frac{z+w}{z(z-w)^{2}}{\rm d}w

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{(2\pi\rm{i})^{2}}\oiint_{z\in c_{z},w\in c_{w}}e^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]}\frac{z+w}{z(z-w)^{2}}{\rm d}z{\rm d}w

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\rm{i}}\int_{c_{\chi}}\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}S^{\prime}(w,x)-\frac{1}{w}\right){\rm d}w

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{(2\pi\rm{i})^{2}}\oiint_{z\in c_{z},w\in c_{w}}e^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]}\frac{z+w}{z(z-w)^{2}}{\rm d}z{\rm d}w.

It follows readily that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi\rm{i}}\int_{c_{\chi}}\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}S^{\prime}(w,x)-\frac{1}{w}\right){\rm d}w

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\chi(x)}(D(\theta)-x){\rm d}\theta.

So our goal is to show

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\oiint_{z\in c_{z},w\in c_{w}}e^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]}\frac{z+w}{z(z-w)^{2}}{\rm{d}}z{\rm{d}}w=0.

Since the integral is analytic in
1/\varepsilon
, the left-hand side, if it exists, is equal to

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}(1/\varepsilon)}\oiint_{z\in c_{z},w\in c_{w}}e^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]}\frac{z+w}{z(z-w)^{2}}{\rm{d}}z{\rm{d}}w.

Differentiating under the integral sign, it suffices to show

(4.9)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\oiint_{z\in c_{z},w\in c_{w}}[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]e^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]}\frac{z+w}{z(z-w)^{2}}{\rm{d}}z{\rm{d}}w=0.

Because
S(z,x)
and
S(w,x)
are multi-valued, we divide the paths
c_{z}
and
c_{w}
into sub-arcs
c_{z}^{i}
and
c_{w}^{j}
to establish single-valued branches.
We select these branches such that their arguments coincide at the intersection points
z=w=e^{\pm{\rm i}\chi(x)}
.
Consequently, the kernel
[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]\frac{1}{(z-w)^{2}}
exhibits a simple pole of order
1
at
z=w
, rendering it integrable within the iterated integral.

Case 1.

c_{z}^{i}\cap c_{w}^{j}=\emptyset
.
Let

M\coloneqq\sup_{z\in c_{z}^{i},w\in c_{w}^{j}}\absolutevalue{\frac{z+w}{z(z-w)^2}}<\infty.

Since
\operatorname{Re}[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]<0
, it follows that

\absolutevalue{
[S(z,x)-S(w,x)] e^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]} \frac{z+w}{z(z-w)^2}
}\leq\norm{S(z,x)-S(w,x)}_{\infty}\cdot M.

Moreover, as
\varepsilon\to+0
,

\absolutevalue{e^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon} [S(z,x)-S(w,x)]}}\leq e^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\operatorname{Re}[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]}\to 0.

Therefore, by the bounded convergence theorem, the integral (
4.9
) over these sub-arcs vanishes.

Case 2.

c_{z}^{i}\cap c_{w}^{j}\neq\emptyset
.
Without loss of generality, let us assume
c_{z}^{i}\cap c_{w}^{j}=\{e^{{\rm i}\chi(x)}\}
.
We parametrise each sub-arc as follows:

\begin{cases}c_{z}^{i}:z=z(t),&z(0)=e^{{\rm{i}}\chi(x)}\quad(t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{1}),\\
c_{w}^{j}:w=w(s),&w(0)=e^{{\rm{i}}\chi(x)}\quad(s_{0}\leq s\leq s_{1}).\end{cases}

Since
S(z(t),x)-S(w(s),x)=O(z(t)-w(s))
, there exists
M>0
such that

\absolutevalue{[S(z(t),x)-S(w(s),x)]\frac{z(t)+w(s)}{z(t)(z(t)-w(s))^2}}\leq\frac{M}{|t|+|s|}.

This implies that the integrand is bounded by an
L^{1}(c_{z}^{i}\times c_{w}^{j})
function.
Meanwhile, we have

\absolutevalue{e^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon} [S(z,x)-S(w,x)]}}\leq e^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\operatorname{Re}[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]}\to 0\quad\text{a.e.}

Invoking Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem, we obtain

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{(2\pi{\rm{i}})^{2}}\oiint_{z\in c_{z}^{i},w\in c_{w}^{j}}[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]e^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]}\frac{z+w}{z(z-w)^{2}}{\rm{d}}z{\rm{d}}w=0.

This completes the proof.

∎

Bulk scaling limit

By arguing precisely the same way as in the proof of Theorem
4.1
,
we obtain the following scaling limit:

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}(-1)^{x/\varepsilon+t}K\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}+r,-\frac{x}{\varepsilon}-s;\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\right)=\begin{cases}\delta_{r,s}&x\leq-b\\
\frac{\sin{\chi(x)(r-s)}}{\pi(r-s)}&-b\leq x\leq b\\
0&b\leq x.\end{cases}

Indeed, the left-hand side is written as

(-1)^{x/\varepsilon+t}K\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}+r,-\frac{x}{\varepsilon}-s;\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\right)=\frac{1}{2(2\pi{\rm i})^{2}}\oiint_{\absolutevalue{z}>\absolutevalue{w}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]\right)\frac{z+w}{z-w}\frac{{\rm d}z{\rm d}w}{z^{r+1}w^{-s+1}}.

When
-b\leq x\leq b
, for example, deforming the integration contour
\absolutevalue{z}>\absolutevalue{w}
to
z\in c_{z},w\in c_{w}
costs

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\cdot 2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{c_{\chi}}\operatorname*{Res}_{z=w}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}[S(z,x)-S(w,x)]\right)\frac{z+w}{z^{r+1}w^{-s+1}}{\rm d}w

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{-{\rm i}(r-s)\theta}{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin{\chi(x)(r-s)}}{\pi(r-s)}.

On the other hand, the integral on the deformed contour
z\in c_{z},w\in c_{w}
converges to
0
essentially because, although the integrand contains a simple pole of order 1 at
z=w
, it is integrable since the integration is repeated twice.
This proves our assertion.

As a corollary, we have the scaling limit of the one-point function,

(4.10)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\rho\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon};\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\right)=\frac{\chi(x)}{\pi},\quad 0\leq x\leq b.

Moreover, by deforming both the integration contour for
z
and
w
into
c_{z}
in (i), or
c_{w}
in (ii), we obtain

(i)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}K\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}+r,\frac{x}{\varepsilon}+s;\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\right)=0,

(ii)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}(-1)^{\frac{x}{\varepsilon}+r+\frac{x}{\varepsilon}+s}K\left(-\frac{x}{\varepsilon}-r,-\frac{x}{\varepsilon}-s;\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\right)=0.

Therefore, using Theorem
3.1
, we obtain the following result.

Proposition 4.3
.

For
0\leq x\leq b
, we define the sine kernel by
K_{\sin}(r,s)=\frac{\sin{\chi(x)(r-s)}}{\pi(r-s)}
.
Then, the correlation function in the bulk scaling limit is given by a determinant of the sine kernel,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\rho\left(\left\{\frac{x}{\varepsilon}+k_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{N};\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\right)=\det_{1\leq i,j\leq N}K_{\sin}(k_{i},k_{j}).

5.
Multicritical Edge Scaling Limit

In this section, we turn our attention to the edge behaviour of the previously established limit shape.
By construction, the limit

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}+k;\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\right)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\chi(x)}(D(\theta)-x){\rm d}\theta,\quad 0\leq x\leq b

can be interpreted as the (scaled) expected total number of neutral fermions lying in the interval
(x,\infty)
.
Consequently, the negative of its derivative,

-\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\chi(x)}(D(\theta)-x){\rm d}\theta=\frac{\chi(x)}{\pi}

naturally identifies with the fermion density function.
Under the
p
-multicriticality condition (iii), we find
D(\theta)=a-\theta^{p}+O(\theta^{p+1})
.
This implies that, near the edge
x=a
(where
a=b
), the density scales proportionally to
(a-x)^{\frac{1}{p}}
.

The right-hand side of the above formula appears in the bulk scaling limit (
4.10
).
Thus, evaluating the limit

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\rho\left(\frac{a+\varepsilon^{p}x}{\varepsilon^{p+1}};\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\right)

should yield precise insights into the microscopic fluctuations of the profile function
\psi_{\lambda,\varepsilon}
near the edge
x=a
.
In what follows, we determine the more general scaling limit of the
N
-point function,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{N}}\rho\left(\left\{\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{k_{i}}{\varepsilon}\right\}_{i=1}^{N};\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\right)

under this
p
-multicritical condition.

Let us define

J(i;t)=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{1}{z^{i+1}}\mathbb{J}(z;t){\rm{d}}z.

Using (
3.4
), the correlation kernel can then be expressed as

K(i,j;t)=\frac{1}{2}J(i;t)J(j;t)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}J(i+k;t)J(j-k;t).

Furthermore, by introducing the shifted phase function

\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\theta)=\varphi(\theta)-\varepsilon^{p}x\theta,

the scaled coefficients neatly take the integral form

(5.1)

J\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{x}{\varepsilon};\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\right)\mathrm{d}\theta.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assuming conditions (i)–(iv) hold, we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}J\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{x}{\varepsilon};\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\right)=\mathrm{Ai}_{p}(x),

where
\mathrm{Ai}_{p}
denotes the
p
-Airy function (see
Appendix A
).

Proof.

Our strategy is to isolate the saddle points of the phase function
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
from (
5.1
) within an interval of width
O(\varepsilon)
.
The dominant contribution from this central interval will naturally yield the
p
-Airy function.

Let
C
denote the constant introduced in (
4.2
).
We fix a real number
T
strictly greater than
\sqrt[p]{|Cx|}
, and choose
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small so that
T\varepsilon<\pi
.
Since the derivative satisfies
\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\theta)\leq-\theta^{p}/C-\varepsilon^{p}x
, any existing zeros of
\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\theta)
must have an absolute value bounded by
\sqrt[p]{|Cx|}\varepsilon
, which is strictly less than
T\varepsilon
.
By scaling the integration variable as
\theta=\varepsilon u
, we can partition the integral as follows:

\frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\theta)\right)\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\int_{-\pi/\varepsilon}^{-T}+\int_{-T}^{T}+\int_{T}^{\pi/\varepsilon}\right)\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon u)\right)\mathrm{d}u,

where, crucially, the derivative
\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\varepsilon u)
is non-vanishing on the outer domains
u\in(-\pi/\varepsilon,\pi/\varepsilon)\setminus(-T,T)
.

As
\varepsilon\to 0
, we observe the asymptotic behaviour of the scaled phase:

\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon u)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\left(-\frac{(\varepsilon u)^{p+1}}{p+1}+O((\varepsilon u)^{p+3})-\varepsilon^{p+1}xu\right)\to-\frac{u^{p+1}}{p+1}-xu.

Invoking the dominated convergence theorem for the central interval, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-T}^{T}\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon u)\right)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-T}^{T}\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\frac{u^{p+1}}{p+1}-\mathrm{i}xu\right)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{T}\cos\left(\frac{u^{p+1}}{p+1}+xu\right)\mathrm{d}u.

Conversely, for the outer region
[T,\pi/\varepsilon]
, integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\quad\,\,\int_{T}^{\pi/\varepsilon}\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon u)\right)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\qquad=\left[\frac{\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon u)\right)}{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\varepsilon u)}\right]_{T}^{\pi/\varepsilon}+\int_{T}^{\pi/\varepsilon}\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon u)\right)\frac{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon^{p-1}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(\varepsilon u)}{\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\varepsilon u)\right)^{2}}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\qquad=O(\varepsilon^{p})+O\left(\frac{1}{T^{p}}\right),

with an entirely analogous estimate holding for the integral over the negative outer region
[-\pi/\varepsilon,-T]
.

Taking the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
, followed by the limit as
T\to\infty
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi/\varepsilon}^{\pi/\varepsilon}\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon u)\right)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{T}\cos\left(\frac{u^{p+1}}{p+1}+xu\right)\mathrm{d}u+O\left(\frac{1}{T^{p}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Ai}_{p}(x).

∎

Theorem 5.2
.

Assuming conditions (i)–(iv) hold, for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathrm{(i)}&\quad\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}K\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{x}{\varepsilon},\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{y}{\varepsilon};\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\right)=0,\\[4.30554pt]
\mathrm{(ii)}&\quad\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(-1)^{\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{y}{\varepsilon}}K\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{x}{\varepsilon},-\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}-\frac{y}{\varepsilon};\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\right)=K_{p\text{-}\mathrm{Airy}}(x,y),\\[4.30554pt]
\mathrm{(iii)}&\quad\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(-1)^{\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{x}{\varepsilon}+\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{y}{\varepsilon}}K\left(-\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}-\frac{x}{\varepsilon},-\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}-\frac{y}{\varepsilon};\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\right)=0.\end{cases}

Consequently, we obtain the determinantal structure in the limit:

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{N}\mathrm{Pf}\left(M\left(\left\{\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{k_{i}}{\varepsilon}\right\}_{i=1}^{N};\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\right)\right)=\det_{1\leq i,j\leq N}K_{p\text{-}\mathrm{Airy}}(k_{i},k_{j}).

Remark 5.3
.

The theorem is stated for a pair of numbers
x,y
such that there exists a sequence of positive numbers
\varepsilon_{1}>\varepsilon_{2}>\cdots\to 0
with
\frac{a}{\varepsilon_{i}^{p+1}}+\frac{x}{\varepsilon},\frac{a}{\varepsilon_{i}^{p+1}}+\frac{y}{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
However, the left-hand sides admit double contour integral (with respect to
z,w
) expressions, which do not contain the sign (cf. (
4.6
)).
Then, by dividing the contours into subarcs and fixing branches for
\arg z,\arg w
, we obtain continuations of the left-hand sides for arbitrary
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
(cf. proof of
Theorem 4.1
).
Since the convergence is uniform when
x,y
range over a compact set, we obtain the same results for arbitrary
x,y
by incorporating the floor function; for example,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(-1)^{\left\lfloor\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{y}{\varepsilon}\right\rfloor}K\left(\left\lfloor\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right\rfloor,-\left\lfloor\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{y}{\varepsilon}\right\rfloor;\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\right)=K_{p\text{-}\mathrm{Airy}}(x,y).

Lemma 5.4
.

There exists a positive constant
C
such that

\left|\frac{1}{\varepsilon}J\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{x}{\varepsilon};\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\right)\right|\leq Ce^{-x},\quad x>0

for all
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small.

Proof.

By applying the residue theorem to (
3.3
), we have

\displaystyle J(m;t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{|z|=e^{\varepsilon}}\exp\left(2\sum_{n:\text{odd}}t_{n}(z^{n}-z^{-n})\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z^{m+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\exp\left(2\sum_{n:\text{odd}}t_{n}(e^{n\varepsilon}e^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}-e^{-n\varepsilon}e^{-\mathrm{i}n\theta})\right)e^{-m\varepsilon}e^{-\mathrm{i}m\theta}\mathrm{d}\theta

yielding

\left|J\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{x}{\varepsilon};\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\right)\right|\leq\frac{e^{-x}}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\left\{2\sum_{n:\text{odd}}t_{n}(e^{n\varepsilon}-e^{-n\varepsilon})\cos n\theta-a\varepsilon\right\}\right)\mathrm{d}\theta.

It thus suffices to show that the integral on the right-hand side, multiplied by
1/\varepsilon
, remains bounded as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Let us observe that

(5.2)

\displaystyle\quad\,\,2\sum_{n:\text{odd}}t_{n}(e^{n\varepsilon}-e^{-n\varepsilon})\cos n\theta-a\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\left\{4\sum_{n:\text{odd}}nt_{n}\cos n\theta-a\right\}+\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{3!}\left\{4\sum_{n:\text{odd}}n^{3}t_{n}\cos n\theta\right\}+\cdots

\displaystyle=\varphi^{\prime}(\theta)\varepsilon-\varphi^{(3)}(\theta)\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{3!}+\cdots+(-1)^{\frac{p}{2}+1}\varphi^{(p-1)}(\theta)\frac{\varepsilon^{p-1}}{(p-1)!}+O(\varepsilon^{p+1}).

By virtue of (
4.2
), for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, we obtain

2\sum_{n:\text{odd}}t_{n}(e^{n\varepsilon}-e^{-n\varepsilon})\cos n\theta-a\varepsilon\leq-\frac{\varepsilon\theta^{p}}{2C}.

Introducing the scaled variable
\theta=\varepsilon u
, we find

\displaystyle\quad\,\,\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\exp\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\left\{2\sum_{n:\text{odd}}t_{n}(e^{n\varepsilon}-e^{-n\varepsilon})\cos n\theta-a\varepsilon\right\}\right)\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\pi/\varepsilon}^{\pi/\varepsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{u^{p}}{2C}\right)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=O(1)\quad(\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0).

This bounds the integral and completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.2
.

Rescaling the variables in equation (
3.4
) according to (ii) and employing the parity relation
J(-i)=(-1)^{i}J(i)
, we write

\displaystyle\quad\,\,\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(-1)^{\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{y}{\varepsilon}}K\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{x}{\varepsilon},-\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}-\frac{y}{\varepsilon};\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}J\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{x}{\varepsilon};\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\right)\right)\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}J\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{y}{\varepsilon};\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}J\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(x+i\varepsilon);\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\right)\right)\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}J\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(y+i\varepsilon);\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\right)\right).

This infinite sum may be interpreted as the Riemann sum of a step function.
By Lemma
5.4
, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, this step function is uniformly bounded by an exponentially decaying profile.
Furthermore, by Theorem
5.1
, the step function converges pointwise to the product of
p
-Airy functions.
Invoking Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem, we thus deduce that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(-1)^{\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{y}{\varepsilon}}K\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{x}{\varepsilon},-\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}-\frac{y}{\varepsilon};\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm Ai}_{p}(x+z){\rm Ai}_{p}(y+z){\rm d}z.

It remains to establish (i) and (iii). We shall detail the proof of (i) alone, as the argument for (iii) is entirely analogous. From equation (
3.4
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}K\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{x}{\varepsilon},\frac{a}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}+\frac{y}{\varepsilon};\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(2\pi{\rm i})^{2}\varepsilon}\oiint_{|z|=e^{2\varepsilon},|w|=e^{\varepsilon}}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}(S(z,a)+S(w,a))+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(x\log z+y\log w)\right\}\frac{z-w}{z+w}\frac{{\rm d}z{\rm d}w}{zw}.

Let us observe that, by (
5.2
),

\operatorname{Re}S(e^{\varepsilon+{\rm i}\theta},a)=\varphi^{\prime}(\theta)\varepsilon-\varphi^{(3)}(\theta)\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{3!}+\cdots+(-1)^{\frac{p}{2}+1}\varphi^{(p-1)}(\theta)\frac{\varepsilon^{p-1}}{(p-1)!}+O(\varepsilon^{p+1}).

We fix a constant
\delta\in(0,1)
such that
p\delta>p-2
, and define the
central region

I
as

I=\{(z,w)\mid\absolutevalue{z}=e^{2\varepsilon},\absolutevalue{w}=e^{\varepsilon},\absolutevalue{\arg z},\absolutevalue{\arg w}<\varepsilon^{1-\delta}\}.

We denote its complement on the integration contours by
I^{c}=\{\absolutevalue{z}=e^{2\varepsilon}\}\times\{\absolutevalue{w}=e^{\varepsilon}\}\setminus I
.

Case 1.

(z,w)\in I^{c}
. Without loss of generality, let us assume
\absolutevalue{\arg z}>\varepsilon^{1-\delta}
. Under this assumption, equation (
4.2
) yields

\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\operatorname{Re}S(z,a)\leq\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(\theta)}{\varepsilon^{p}}\leq-\frac{\theta^{p}}{C\varepsilon^{p}}\leq-\frac{\varepsilon^{-p\delta}}{C}.

Meanwhile, since

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\varepsilon}\operatorname{Re}S(e^{\varepsilon+{\rm i}\theta},a)=\varphi^{\prime}(\theta)+O(\varepsilon^{2})\leq O(\varepsilon^{2}),

the mean value theorem provides

\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}\operatorname{Re}S(w,a)=O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p-2}}\right)=o(\varepsilon^{-p\delta}).

Furthermore, we note that

\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(x\log z+y\log w)=O(1),\quad\frac{z-w}{z+w}=O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right).

Consequently, we deduce

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\oiint_{(z,w)\in I^c}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}(S(z,a)+S(w,a))+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(x\log z+y\log w)\right\}\frac{z-w}{z+w}\frac{{\rm d}z{\rm d}w}{zw}}

\displaystyle\qquad=O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{-p\delta}}{C}\right)\right)\to 0.

Case 2.

(z,w)\in I
. In this regime, bounding the terms as

(5.3)

\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\operatorname{Re}S(e^{\varepsilon+{\rm i}\theta},a)\leq-\frac{\theta^{p}}{C\varepsilon^{p}},\quad\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(x\log z+y\log w)=O(1),\quad\frac{z-w}{z+w}=O(1),

yields

\displaystyle\quad\,\,\absolutevalue{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\oiint_{(z,w)\in I}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p+1}}(S(z,a)+S(w,a))+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(x\log z+y\log w)\right\}\frac{z-w}{z+w}\frac{{\rm d}z{\rm d}w}{zw}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{-\varepsilon^{1-\delta}}^{\varepsilon^{1-\delta}}\exp\left(-\frac{\theta^{p}}{C(2\varepsilon)^{p}}+O(1)\right){\rm d}\theta\cdot\int_{-\varepsilon^{1-\delta}}^{\varepsilon^{1-\delta}}\exp\left(-\frac{\phi^{p}}{C\varepsilon^{p}}+O(1)\right){\rm d}\phi

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\int_{-\varepsilon^{-\delta}}^{\varepsilon^{-\delta}}\exp\left(-\frac{u^{p}}{2^{p}C}+O(1)\right){\rm d}u\cdot\int_{-\varepsilon^{-\delta}}^{\varepsilon^{-\delta}}\exp\left(-\frac{v^{p}}{C}+O(1)\right){\rm d}v

\displaystyle\to 0,

where we have introduced the scaled variables
\theta=\varepsilon u
and
\phi=\varepsilon v
.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

The line of reasoning employed in the proof of (i) breaks down for case (ii).
This fundamentally occurs because the bounded estimate
\frac{z-w}{z+w}=O(1)
in (
5.3
) is replaced by the singular behaviour
\frac{z+w}{z-w}=O(\varepsilon^{-1})
.

Gap probability

To conclude this section, let us briefly touch upon the gap probability.
Let
I\subset\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be a discrete interval.
We consider the gap probability with respect to
I
, such that no point is found in
I
,

P(I)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{x_{1}\in I}\cdots\sum_{x_{n}\in I}\rho(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};t)\,.

In the case of the shifted Schur measure, the Pfaffian formula of the correlation function (Theorem
3.1
) yields the Fredholm Pfaffian formula for the gap probability.
Let
K
be a linear operator acting on
\ell^{2}(I;\mathbb{R}^{2})
,

K:\begin{pmatrix}f(x)\\
g(x)\end{pmatrix}\longmapsto\sum_{y\in I}\begin{pmatrix}K(x,y)&(-1)^{y}K(x,-y)\\
(-1)^{x}K(-x,y)&(-1)^{x+y}K(-x,-y)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}f(y)\\
g(y)\end{pmatrix}\,,

and also

J:\begin{pmatrix}f(x)\\
g(x)\end{pmatrix}\longmapsto\sum_{y\in I}\begin{pmatrix}0&\delta_{x,y}\\
-\delta_{x,y}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}f(y)\\
g(y)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}g(x)\\
-f(x)\end{pmatrix}\,.

Then, the gap probability is given by the Fredholm Pfaffian,

P(I)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{x_{1}\in I}\cdots\sum_{x_{n}\in I}\operatorname*{Pf}_{1\leq i,j\leq 2n}M(\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\})_{i,j}=\operatorname{Pf}(J-K)_{\ell^{2}(I;\mathbb{R}^{2})}\,.

Arguing the same way as in
[
BBW23
, Theorem 1]
, we obtain the following result.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
p\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Under the multicritical condition of degree
p
, we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{\lambda_{1}-a/\varepsilon^{p+1}}{\varepsilon}\leq s\right]=F_{p}(s)\,,

where
F_{p}
is the degree-
p
Tracy–Widom distribution,

\displaystyle F_{p}(s)

\displaystyle=\det(1 - K_{p\text{-Airy}})_{L^{2}([s,\infty))}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\int_{s}^{\infty}\cdots\int_{s}^{\infty}\det_{1\leq i,j\leq n}K_{p\text{-Airy}}(x_{i},x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{n}\,.

The simplest case
p=2
was proved by Matsumoto
[
Mat03
]
, where
F_{2}(s)
agrees with the GUE Tracy–Widom distribution
[
TW92
]
.

Appendix A
Higher Airy Functions

For any even integer
p\geq 2
, the
p
-Airy function

\mathrm{Ai}_{p}(x)
is defined by the integral

\mathrm{Ai}_{p}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos\left(\frac{t^{p+1}}{p+1}+xt\right)\mathrm{d}t,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

The convergence of this integral is verified via integration by parts:

\displaystyle\int^{R}\cos\left(\frac{t^{p+1}}{p+1}+xt\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int^{R}\frac{1}{t^{p}+x}\mathrm{d}\left(\sin\left(\frac{t^{p+1}}{p+1}+xt\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\sin\left(\frac{t^{p+1}}{p+1}+xt\right)}{t^{p}+x}\right]^{R}+\int^{R}\frac{pt^{p-1}}{(t^{p}+x)^{2}}\sin\left(\frac{t^{p+1}}{p+1}+xt\right)\mathrm{d}t.

To obtain the analytic continuation of
\mathrm{Ai}_{p}
into the entire complex plane, we substitute
\zeta=\mathrm{i}t
, which yields

(A.1)

\mathrm{Ai}_{p}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{\mathrm{i}\infty}\exp\left((-1)^{\frac{p}{2}+1}\frac{\zeta^{p+1}}{p+1}-x\zeta\right)\mathrm{d}\zeta.

Consider the integral

I(R)=\int_{\gamma}\left|\exp\left((-1)^{\frac{p}{2}+1}\frac{\zeta^{p+1}}{p+1}-x\zeta\right)\right||\mathrm{d}\zeta|,

where the contour
\gamma
is parameterised by
\gamma(\theta)=\mathrm{i}Re^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\theta}{p+1}}
for
0\leq\theta\leq\pi
.

Applying Jordan’s inequality, we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle I(R)

\displaystyle=\frac{R}{p+1}\int_{0}^{\pi}\exp\left(-\frac{R^{p+1}}{p+1}\sin\theta-xR\sin\frac{\theta}{p+1}\right)\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\pi R}{p+1}\exp\left(-\frac{R^{p+1}}{p+1}\frac{2}{\pi}+|x|R\right).

It follows that
I(R)
vanishes as
R\to\infty
.
Consequently, the integration contour in (
A.1
) may be deformed into any path originating at infinity within the sector
-\frac{\pi}{2}\leq\arg\zeta\leq-\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{\pi}{p+1}
and terminating at infinity in the conjugate sector
\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{p+1}\leq\arg\zeta\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
.

For a contour
\mathscr{L}
that originates in the sector
-\frac{\pi}{2}+\delta\leq\arg\zeta\leq-\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{\pi}{p+1}-\delta
and terminates in
\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{p+1}+\delta\leq\arg\zeta\leq\frac{\pi}{2}-\delta
for some
\delta>0
, we define

(A.2)

\mathrm{Ai}_{p}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathscr{L}}\exp\left((-1)^{\frac{p}{2}+1}\frac{\zeta^{p+1}}{p+1}-z\zeta\right)\mathrm{d}\zeta,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

At the extremities of
\mathscr{L}
, the term
\exp(\zeta^{p+1}/(p+1))
dominates
\exp(-z\zeta)
, ensuring that the integral (
A.2
) converges absolutely and uniformly on any compact subset of
\mathbb{C}
. This provides the analytic continuation of
\mathrm{Ai}_{p}
as an entire function. Henceforth,
\mathrm{Ai}_{p}
shall refer to this holomorphic function.

By differentiating under the integral sign, it is evident that
\mathrm{Ai}_{p}
satisfies the
p
-Airy equation

\left[(-1)^{\frac{p}{2}+1}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{p}}{\mathrm{d}z^{p}}-z\right]u=0.

This equation possesses
p
linearly independent solutions. For each root
\tau
of
\tau^{p}=(-1)^{\frac{p}{2}+1}
, there exists a solution with the asymptotic growth rate
\log u(x)\sim-\frac{p}{p+1}(\operatorname{Re}\tau)x^{\frac{p+1}{p}}
as
\mathbb{R}\ni x\to\infty
(see, e.g.,
[
Was65
]
). A standard application of the saddle point method shows that

(A.3)

\log\mathrm{Ai}_{p}(x)\sim-\frac{p}{p+1}(\operatorname{Re}\tau_{*})x^{\frac{p+1}{p}}\quad\text{as }x\to\infty,

where
\operatorname{Re}\tau_{*}=\max\{\operatorname{Re}\tau\mid\tau^{p}=(-1)^{\frac{p}{2}+1}\}
.
Accordingly,
\mathrm{Ai}_{p}
is the fastest decaying real-valued solution of the
p
-Airy equation as
x\to\infty
.

By virtue of the asymptotic estimate (
A.3
), we define the
p
-Airy kernel
as

K_{p\text{-}\mathrm{Airy}}(z,w)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{Ai}_{p}(z+x)\mathrm{Ai}_{p}(w+x)\mathrm{d}x.
