Curvature of optimal transport with respect to the cost and applications to inverse optimal transport
Gabriel Peyré, Clarice Poon, Oscar Tron

Abstract
We study the inverse optimal transport problem of recovering the ground cost from an optimal transport plan. In discrete settings, this problem reduces to inverse linear programming and is intrinsically ill-posed, exhibiting non-identifiability and flat directions. We show that in the continuous setting, the regularity of the marginals fundamentally alters the structure of the inverse problem. Assuming smooth positive densities for the source and target measures, we characterize the second variation of the optimal transport functional with respect to the ground cost in Hölder spaces. In particular, we show that it is non-degenerate modulo the natural transport invariances, yielding a strict curvature property that is absent in discrete transport. As a consequence, we obtain local identifiability and stability results for inverse optimal transport. For the structured family of bilinear costs (i.e. Mahalanobis parametrizations), the ground cost can be uniquely recovered—up to the intrinsic invariances—from a single optimal coupling under a natural spanning condition. We further show that this identifiability property is generic under arbitrarily small perturbations of the marginals, while settings where the optimal transport map is affine (for instance Gaussian or elliptical marginals) remain degenerate. Finally, we establish precise bounds on the bias and statistical variance of inverse optimal transport under entropic regularization. These results reveal a structural parallel between forward and inverse optimal transport: regularity of the marginals ensures smooth optimal maps in the forward problem, while non-degeneracy of the induced transport plan yields curvature and local invertibility in the inverse problem.

Introduction

Optimal transport (OT) is now a central modeling tool
36
to compare and couple probability distributions, with applications spanning economics, imaging, generative modeling, and computational biology
32
. In many modern pipelines, however, the transport cost is not known a priori and must be inferred from data. This leads to
inverse optimal transport
(iOT): recover the ground cost (or metric parameters) from an observed optimal coupling. Existing methodologies for iOT typically exploit entropic regularization due to the availability of efficient numerical algorithms
16
;
6
.

This paper studies iOT in a regime that is both mathematically delicate and practically unavoidable: the entropic regularization level
\varepsilon
is small (approaching the unregularized OT model), while the coupling is observed through a finite sample of size
n
. A larger
\varepsilon
improves convexity and numerical stability but introduces bias; a smaller
\varepsilon
is closer to the true OT geometry but amplifies conditioning and statistical variance. Understanding this
bias–variance geometry
in the joint
(\varepsilon,n)
regime is the main motivation of our analysis.

Optimal transport

Let

. For probability measures

and
\beta\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
and a cost

, optimal transport is defined by

\mathtt{OT}_{\alpha,\beta}(c)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\alpha,\beta)}\int c(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y).

(OT)

Here
\Pi(\alpha,\beta)
denotes the set of couplings with marginals
\alpha
and
\beta
.

Inverse optimal transport

In inverse OT (iOT), one observes i.i.d. samples

(x_{i},y_{i})\overset{iid}{\sim}\hat{\pi},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

where
\hat{\pi}
is a minimizer to (
OT
) for some cost
c
.
The central question of inverse optimal transport is:

Can one recover the ground metric
c
from samples from an optimal transport plan
\hat{\pi}
?

At first sight, this problem is fundamentally ill-posed, even if there is a unique minimizer to (
OT
), some immediate issues include

•

Scale invariance:
\mathtt{OT}\left(\lambda c\right)=\lambda\mathtt{OT}(c)
, so
c
is identifiable only up to scaling.

•

Additive invariance:

and
c(x,y)+f(x)+g(y)
induce the same optimal plans.

•

Discrete degeneracy: when
\alpha,\beta
are discrete, (
OT
) is a linear program; the set of costs compatible with a single optimal plan is typically a high-dimensional cone.

Thus, without additional structure, identifiability fails.
In this article,
we focus on the recovery of bilinear costs

c_{A}(x,y)=-x^{\top}Ay,\qquad A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.

Due to additive invariance, the quadratic cost
\frac{1}{2}\left\|x-y\right\|^{2}
is equivalent to

. Hence, recovering

amounts to recovering a Mahalanobis metric.

Our main contributions are the following.

•

Second-order geometry of OT with respect to the cost.

Theorem

(Section

This yields strict curvature in directions transverse to transport invariances.

•

•

Local quadratic stability for bilinear inverse OT.

Theorem

•

Deterministic and statistical guarantees in the small-entropic regime.

In Section

•

Explicit degenerate counter-regimes.

1.1
Previous works

Forward OT and entropic regularization

OT provides a variational geometry on probability measures and a mature analytical framework
36
. It is also a central computational toolbox in modern applications, especially in machine learning
32
. Entropic regularization was introduced in modern ML practice to make OT scalable through Sinkhorn iterations
11
, building on the original matrix-scaling algorithm
38
. On the modeling side, this regularization connects OT to Schrödinger bridge formulations
24
, and to a broader stochastic-control perspective
31
. The stabilization induced by this regularization explains why most practical inverse-OT procedures are built from entropic objectives rather than from the raw Kantorovich problem.

Inverse OT problem

Inverse OT can be viewed as a special case of inverse linear optimization
45
;
2
. In economics, the entropic inverse model is motivated by random utility and leads to likelihood-based convex estimation
19
;
16
. On the methodological side, SISTA-type formulations add sparse structure while preserving convexity and computational tractability
6
;
3
. In discrete settings, recent analyses make explicit the geometric degeneracies of this inverse problem
8
.

A major open difficulty is the
vanishing-regularization
limit. Fixed-
\varepsilon
estimators can be stable, but their constants typically deteriorate as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, exactly where one wants to recover unregularized OT structure. Identifiability theory also remains fragmented: one line of work proves nonlinear identifiability from richer observation models (multiple marginals or value data)
23
, while another recovers geometric structure on manifolds when many optimal maps are available
44
. The single-coupling regime is therefore still largely open, and this is the main gap addressed here.

Sample complexity of forward OT

One of our goals is to understand the discretization of the inverse OT problem using
n
samples. For the forward OT problem, the sample complexity is now well understood. For unregularized OT costs (i.e. estimation of
\mathtt{OT}_{\alpha,\beta}(c)
with its
n
-sample empirical counterpart
\mathtt{OT}_{\alpha^{n},\beta^{n}}(c)
obtained with empirical measures
\alpha^{n},\beta^{n}
), sharp sample-complexity analyses highlight severe high-dimensional effects and the curse of dimensionality
43
. For map estimation, the same curse appears and rates depend on structural assumptions on Brenier potentials
12
;
27
; alternative smooth-map estimators further clarify this bias–variance tradeoff
39
. Entropic regularization modifies this landscape: Under a
fixed
regularization strength
\varepsilon>0
, Sinkhorn divergences admit favorable sample complexity
21
, and non-asymptotic/statistical bounds for entropic OT are now well developed
28
. However, for estimation of the map, the constants exhibit exponential dependency on
1/\varepsilon

34
, unless under stronger smoothness assumptions on the densities
\alpha,\beta

33
. In our work, we transpose this forward program to iOT and quantify how these errors propagate to cost recovery as a function of both
n
and
\varepsilon
.

Quantitative stability

Obtaining rates in iOT requires sensitivity analysis with respect to the cost parameter. Quantitative stability of the dual potential or the transport map with respect to marginals is comparatively well understood, while sensitivity with respect to the cost variable remains much less explored.
To our knowledge, the main work studying stability with respect to the cost is a non-quantitative study on the smoothness of optimal transport maps
7
.
In the unregularized map setting, quantitative bounds with respect to the measures
\alpha,\beta
were established in
29
;
13
, and more recent work extends these results via gluing arguments
25
, and complementary results cover transport plans directly
26
. For entropic OT, Lipschitz bounds are available
5
, as well as quantitative convergence and algorithmic-stability analyses
17
;
14
. Stability of entropic dual potentials has also been studied from a functional-analytic viewpoint
30
. Finally, sharp small-
\varepsilon
control for entropic Brenier maps is obtained under stronger regularity assumptions in
15
. This corresponds to the regime we study for inverse OT when one wants both computational tractability and faithful recovery of the unregularized model.

2
Curvature of optimal transport

We study the second-order structure of the optimal transport functional

c\mapsto\mathtt{OT}_{\alpha,\beta}(c).

While first-order properties of optimal transport are classical, its second-order structure with respect to the cost variable has received little attention.

Dual formulation of OT

Define the semi-dual functional

F(c,\varphi)\coloneqq\int_{X}\varphi\,d\alpha\;-\;\int_{Y}\varphi^{c}\,d\beta,\qquad\varphi^{c}(y)\;=\;\inf_{x\in X}\{c(x,y)+\varphi(x)\}.

(S)

Then, (
OT
) has the dual formulation

\mathtt{OT}_{c}(\alpha,\beta)=\sup_{\varphi\in L^{1}(\alpha)}F(c,\varphi),

and an optimal solution
\varphi
is a Kantorovich potential.

Uniqueness of OT plans is typically guaranteed through the TWIST condition, that is, the cost
c
is differentiable with respect to
x
and
y\mapsto\nabla_{x}c(x,y)
is injective (or
\nabla^{2}_{x,y}c(x,y)
is invertible for all
x,y
)
(
42
, pg. 234)
. For the setting of bilinear costs, this corresponds to
A
being invertible. Moreover, under the twist condition on
c
and absolutely continuous densities,
\varphi
is uniquely defined up to a constant, so we assume throughout
\int\varphi(x)d\alpha(x)=0

(
42
, Remark 10.30)
.

Assumption A1
.

X,Y
are connected bounded uniformly convex domains with
C^{k+2}
boundary.

Assumption A2
.

\alpha,\beta
admit
C^{k,\kappa}
densities bounded from below defined on the compact sets
\overline{X}
and
\overline{Y}
.

As a slight abuse of notation, since
\alpha,\beta
are assumed to admit densities, we wrote

and

to denote their densities.

We will establish regularity of the maps
c\mapsto\varphi_{c}
and
c\mapsto\mathtt{OT}(c)
locally around a cost

satisfying the twist condition:

Assumption A3
.

c_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{k+2,\kappa}(X\times Y)
is such that
\nabla_{x,y}^{2}c_{0}(x,y)
is invertible for all
x\in X
and
y\in Y
.

(Curvature of OT for general costs)

Remark 1
.

The only directions of degeneracy of the second variation correspond precisely to transport-invariant perturbations of the cost. In all transverse directions,
\mathtt{OT}_{\alpha,\beta}
exhibits strict negative curvature.
Indeed,

,
c
and
\varphi_{c}
are related by

\partial_{1}c(x,T^{c}(x))=\nabla\varphi_{c}(x).

If

is a gradient field, then

\partial_{1}c_{\varepsilon}(x,T^{c}(x))=\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)

for

and
\varphi_{\varepsilon}=\varphi_{c}+\varepsilon\psi
. Thus, perturbation along

does not change the OT value. Section
3
applies this characterization to understand the issue of uniqueness and stability for inverse optimal transport.

Proposition 2

(First variation)
.

Since

3.1
Preliminaries on the Brenier map

We begin by recalling some well-known results about regularity properties of optimal transport.

In the following theorem, we recall that
for the classical Euclidean setting (i.e.
A=\mathrm{Id})
, Brenier’s theorem (see for instance
(
42
, Theorem 10.28)
) guarantees that the optimal transport plan
\pi
is supported on the graph of
T=\nabla\Psi
where
\Psi
is a convex function unique up to an additive constant, under the assumption that
\alpha
has a density wrt Lebesgue. We will make use of an analogous statement for an invertible matrix
A
, which is a restatement of Brenier’s theorem by a simple change of variables:

Proposition 6

(Existence)
.

Assume that
A
is invertible and that
\alpha
has a density wrt Lebesgue. Then there exists
\Psi_{A}
, a convex function, such that the optimal transport map for the cost
c_{A}
is
T_{A}(x)=\nabla\Psi_{A}(A^{\top}x)
. Moreover,
\Psi_{A}
is unique up to an additive constant. We will call
T_{A}
the Brenier map and
\Psi_{A}
a Brenier potential.

Proof.

The idea is to make the change of variable
A^{\top}\#\alpha
to reduce the problem to the Euclidean one. Indeed we know that
\pi\to(A^{\top},\mathrm{Id})\#\pi
is a bijection from
\Pi(\alpha,\beta)
to
\Pi(A^{\top}\#\alpha,\beta)
, therefore :

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\alpha,\beta)}-\int x^{\top}Ay\;d\pi(x,y)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(A^{\top}\#\alpha,\beta)}-\int x^{\top}y\;d\pi(x,y)

From Brenier’s theorem for the Euclidean cost, since
A^{\top}\#\alpha
has a density wrt Lebesgue, there exists a convex function
\Psi_{A}
such that

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(A^{\top}\#\alpha,\beta)}-\int x^{\top}y\;d\pi(x,y)=-\int x^{\top}\nabla\Psi_{A}(x)\;d(A^{\top}\#\alpha)(x)=-\int x^{\top}A\nabla\Psi_{A}(A^{\top}x)\;d\alpha(x).

Therefore the Brenier map is
T_{A}(x)=\nabla\Psi_{A}(A^{\top}x)
by identification. Indeed, the Brenier map is still unique with
c_{A}
through the TWIST condition.
(
42
, pg. 234)
.
∎

3.2
Uniqueness

Identifiability of the cost matrix
\hat{A}
is delicate: in the setting when

The main result of this section is Theorem

Theorem 2

(Identifiability)
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{P}(X)

\beta\in\mathcal{P}(Y)

Assume:

•

A Brenier map exists between
\alpha
and
\beta

•

The Brenier map is of the form

•

The spanning condition

\Span\{\nabla^{2}\Psi_{\hat{A}}(\hat{A}^{\top}x):x\in\Spt\alpha\}\supseteq\mathbb{S}_{0}^{d}\coloneqq\left\{B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\;;\;B^{\top}=B,\mathrm{Trace}(B)=0\right\}.

\mathcal{S}_{0}(\hat{\pi})=\{\lambda\hat{A}:\lambda>0\}.

(Generic identifiability by arbitrarily small perturbations)

\Spt(\alpha)
contains a nonempty open set
\mathcal{U}

to

,
where
\Psi_{A}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is convex.

Then there exists a convex function

such that, defining

1.

The map

is the optimal transport from
\alpha
to
\beta_{\delta}
for the cost
c_{A}
.

2.

The family of Hessians

\left\{\nabla^{2}(\Psi_{A}+\delta\tilde{\Psi})(A^{\top}x)\;:\;x\in\Spt(\alpha)\right\}

spans the space
\mathbb{S}_{d}
of symmetric
d\times d
matrices (identifiability condition).

Moreover,
\tilde{T}_{A}
belongs to
L^{p}(\alpha)
for all
p\geq 1
, and the Wasserstein-2 distance between
\beta
and
\beta_{\delta}
satisfies

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\beta,\beta_{\delta})\;\lesssim\;\delta\,\|\tilde{T}_{A}\|_{L^{2}(\alpha)}.

Proof.

Let
p\coloneqq\dim\mathbb{S}_{d}=d(d+1)/2
.

Step 1: spanning
\mathbb{S}_{d}
by rank-one matrices.

Since
\mathcal{U}
is open, the family
\{xx^{\top}:x\in\mathcal{U}\}
spans
\mathbb{S}_{d}
. Indeed, if there existed

such that

then the polynomial

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which contradicts

.
Therefore, there exist points

such that

\Span(x_{1}x_{1}^{\top},\dots,x_{p}x_{p}^{\top})=\mathbb{S}_{d}.

Step 2: construction of the perturbation directions.

Choose pairwise disjoint intervals

and smooth functions

such that
h_{k}\geq 0
,
\supp(h_{k})\subset I_{k}
, and
h_{k}(t_{k})=1
for some

Then

is convex and satisfies

Define

Then

, and

\Span(u_{1}u_{1}^{\top},\dots,u_{p}u_{p}^{\top})=A^{-1}\Span(x_{1}x_{1}^{\top},\dots,x_{p}x_{p}^{\top})A^{-\top}=\mathbb{S}_{d}.

Step 3: definition of the perturbation.

Define

\tilde{\Psi}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{p}g_{k}(u_{k}^{\top}x),\qquad\tilde{T}_{A}(x)=\nabla\tilde{\Psi}(A^{\top}x).

Since each
g_{k}
is convex,
\tilde{\Psi}
is convex, hence
\Psi_{A}+\delta\tilde{\Psi}
is convex for all
\delta>0
.
By the characterization of optimal maps for the cost
c_{A}
, the map
T_{A}+\delta\tilde{T}_{A}
is optimal from
\alpha
to
\beta_{\delta}
.

Step 4: generic spanning of Hessians.

For all

\nabla^{2}(\Psi_{A}+\delta\tilde{\Psi})(A^{\top}x)=\nabla^{2}\Psi_{A}(A^{\top}x)+\delta\sum_{k=1}^{p}u_{k}u_{k}^{\top}\,g_{k}^{\prime\prime}(u_{k}^{\top}A^{\top}x).

In particular, for

\nabla^{2}(\Psi_{A}+\delta\tilde{\Psi})(A^{\top}x_{k})=\nabla^{2}\Psi_{A}(A^{\top}x_{k})+\delta\,u_{k}u_{k}^{\top}.

Fix a linear isomorphism

and consider the matrix

Q(\delta)\coloneqq\big[L(\nabla^{2}\Psi_{A}(A^{\top}x_{1})+\delta u_{1}u_{1}^{\top})\mid\cdots\mid L(\nabla^{2}\Psi_{A}(A^{\top}x_{p})+\delta u_{p}u_{p}^{\top})\big].

Then

is a real polynomial of degree at most
p
.
Its leading coefficient equals

\det\big(L(u_{1}u_{1}^{\top}),\dots,L(u_{p}u_{p}^{\top})\big)\neq 0,

since
\{u_{k}u_{k}^{\top}\}_{k=1}^{p}
spans
\mathbb{S}_{d}
.
Therefore,

is not identically zero and vanishes for only finitely many values of
\delta

For all other
\delta>0

\left\{\nabla^{2}(\Psi_{A}+\delta\tilde{\Psi})(A^{\top}x_{k})\right\}_{k=1}^{p}

are linearly independent in
\mathbb{S}_{d}
, which implies the spanning (identifiability) condition.

Step 5: integrability and Wasserstein control.

Since each
g_{k}^{\prime}
is bounded,
\tilde{T}_{A}
is bounded on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, hence belongs to
L^{p}(\alpha)
for all
p\geq 1
.
The Wasserstein bound follows from the standard estimate

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\beta,\beta_{\delta})\leq\delta\|\tilde{T}_{A}\|_{L^{2}(\alpha)}
.

1.

c(x,y)=h(x-y)
. Then,
\partial_{cc}\mathtt{OT}(h)[\delta h,\delta h]=0
if and only if there exists
\psi
such that

\nabla\delta h(x-T(x))=-\nabla\psi(x)

which is iff

\nabla^{2}\delta h(x-T(x))DT(x)

is symmetric. Thus, the question of identifiability corresponds to understanding conditions on

under which
\nabla^{2}\delta h(x-T(x))DT(x)=DT(x)^{\top}\nabla^{2}\delta h(x-T(x))
for all

.

2.

Suppose we are interested in parameterized costs of the form

\partial_{c}c\mathtt{OT}(c_{\theta})[c_{\delta\theta},c_{\delta\theta}]=0
if and only if there exists
\psi

\sum_{i}\delta\theta_{i}(\partial_{11}h_{i}(x,T(x))+\partial_{12}h_{i}(x,T(x))DT(x))=\nabla\psi(x),\quad\forall x.

Identifiability, therefore, corresponds to understanding when

\bigcap_{x}\mathrm{Ker}\left(\delta\theta\mapsto\sum_{i}\delta\theta_{i}S_{i}(x)\right)=\mathbb{R}\theta,

4
Entropic regularization and sample complexity

The curvature result of Theorem
3
shows that the unregularized optimality gap
\mathcal{L}_{0}

has strictly positive transverse curvature near
A_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(\hat{\pi})
.
In particular, locally, the feasible set reduces to a single ray. This therefore, opens the possibility of stable recovery methods by combining

\mathcal{L}_{0}
with regularization functionals. In this section, we consider entropic approaches, as this is the most common setting in the inverse OT literature.

Entropic iOT in the literature.

Inverse optimal transport was introduced and studied primarily through its entropic formulation, notably in the work of Galichon and collaborators
16
, where the entropy-regularized problem arises as a maximum likelihood estimator under a random utility model.
In this framework, the cost is estimated by minimizing the entropic gap functional

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(A)=\left\langle c_{A},\,\hat{\pi}\right\rangle-\mathtt{OT}^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha,\beta}(c_{A}),

(
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
)

where the entropic optimal transport between
\alpha,\beta
given ground cost
c
is defined as

\mathtt{OT}^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha,\beta}(c)\coloneqq\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\alpha,\beta)}\Big(\left\langle c,\,\pi\right\rangle-\varepsilon\mathrm{KL}(\pi|\alpha\otimes\beta)\Big)

(
\mathrm{OT}_{\varepsilon}
)

Most numerical implementations of iOT rely on Sinkhorn-type solvers and therefore operate within this entropic framework.
Thus, understanding the properties of
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
is central to the existing theory and practice of inverse OT. Throughout this section, we denote the minimizer of (

OT
ε

) with cost
c_{A}
by
\pi_{\varepsilon}(A)
.

Regularized entropic objective.

If the data are generated exactly from an iOT model, that is,
\hat{\pi}=\pi_{A_{0}}
for some
A_{0}
, then minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(A)
without additional regularization is in general, hopeless.

Indeed, while
\mathcal{L}_{0}
has transverse curvature, the entropic relaxation introduces new degeneracies.
The dual formulation shows that enforcing the moment constraint

\int xy^{\top}\,d\pi=\int xy^{\top}\,d\hat{\pi}

may force couplings to become singular if
\hat{\pi}
is singular with respect to
\alpha\otimes\beta
— this is incompatible with entropic regularization.
As a consequence,
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(A)
can be unbounded from below, even in a neighborhood of
\mathcal{S}_{0}(\hat{\pi})
. We demonstrate this explicitly for the Gaussian setting in Section
5.2
where the moment condition can only be satisfied by couplings
\pi
that are singular with respect to
\alpha\otimes\beta
(so that
\mathrm{KL}(\pi\mid\alpha\otimes\beta)=+\infty
).
Thus, entropic smoothing alone does not resolve the intrinsic degeneracy of the inverse problem.

To obtain a well-posed problem, we consider the regularized functional

\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(A)=\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(A)+\varepsilon\lambda_{0}R(A),

(
\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}
)

where
R
is a regularizer.

To make sure that
\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}
is convex and coercive, we need the following assumption:

Assumption A4
.

Assume that
R
is convex with
R\geq\kappa\left\|\cdot\right\|
where
\kappa>0
and
\left\|\cdot\right\|
is any norm. Assume as well that
R

and that the ground truth

is symmetric positive definite.

The behavior of

as

is well understood on positive definite matrices, and Theorem
12
states that it’s lower bounded by the
\log
of the eigenvalues of
A^{-1}
. This is why the scaling by
\varepsilon
ensures that the regularization vanishes in the zero-temperature limit while preventing the entropic objective from escaping to
-\infty
.

The following theorem shows that this procedure preserves the geometric structure identified earlier.

Theorem 4

(Deterministic consistency and bias)
.

Assume

A1
,
A2
with

and

. Assume also that (

We now consider the case where we observe

(ii)

Now,
According to (

Moreover, by minimality of

0\geq\varepsilon(F(A_{\varepsilon})-F(A_{0}))-\varepsilon^{2}(C+C_{A_{0}})

5
Degenerate settings of iOT

In this section, we discuss two key degenerate settings of iOT. First, in the discrete setting, the set of admissible costs is a polyhedral set, and we show in Proposition
22
that the set of admissible costs is always a cone of dimension larger than one when the underlying dimension
d
is large with respect to the number of samples (see also Remark
23
). Second, when
\alpha
and
\beta
are two elliptical distributions with the same base, the set of admissible costs is a
d
-dimensional cone (Proposition
25
). These are two contrasting settings, where issues identified in Proposition
22
are resolved as the number of samples
n\to\infty
, and on the other hand, for elliptical distributions, even as we let
n\to\infty
, the problem remains degenerate.

5.1
The discrete setting

Let
\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
and
\beta=\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}\delta_{y_{j}}
, and let

\mathcal{U}(a,b):=\{P\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p\times q}:P\mathbf{1}=b,\ P^{\top}\mathbf{1}=a\}

be the transportation polytope. For a bilinear cost
c_{A}(x,y)=-x^{\top}Ay
, define the cost matrix

C(A)_{ij}:=-x_{i}^{\top}Ay_{j},\qquad\langle C(A),P\rangle:=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{q}C(A)_{ij}P_{ij}.

In this case, (
OT
) can be written as the linear program

\min_{P\in\mathcal{U}(a,b)}\ \langle C(A),P\rangle.

(14)

Dual formulation and certificates.

The dual of (
14
) reads

\max_{f\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\ g\in\mathbb{R}^{q}}\ a^{\top}f+b^{\top}g\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad f_{i}+g_{j}\leq C(A)_{ij}\quad\forall i,j.

(15)

A plan
\hat{P}\in\mathcal{U}(a,b)
is optimal for cost
C(A)
if and only if there exist dual potentials
(f,g)
such that

\begin{cases}f_{i}+g_{j}\leq C(A)_{ij}&\forall i,j,\\
f_{i}+g_{j}=C(A)_{ij}&\forall(i,j)\in\supp(\hat{P}),\end{cases}

(16)

where
\supp(\hat{P}):=\{(i,j):\hat{P}_{ij}>0\}
.

Given an optimal coupling
\hat{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
,
the set of admissible costs for which
\hat{P}
is optimal is

\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}_{0}(\hat{P}):=\{A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}:\ \hat{P}\in\mathop{\argmin}_{P\in\Pi(a,b)}\langle C(A),P\rangle\}.

(17)

We characterize the non-identifiability below, giving a direct specialization of the inverse-LP fact that the set of objectives making a fixed primal solution optimal is the (polyhedral) normal cone of the feasible polytope at that solution; intersecting that cone with the bilinear parameterization
A\mapsto C(A)
yields non-identifiability whenever the restriction map
A\mapsto(C(A))_{i,j\in\Supp(\hat{P})}
has nontrivial kernel.

Proposition 22

(Local invariance of optimality under support-orthogonal perturbations)
.

Fix marginals
a\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}
,
b\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{q}
be probability vectors and let
\hat{P}\in\mathcal{U}(a,b)
.
Assume there exist
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and dual potentials
(f,g)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}
such that

\begin{cases}f_{i}+g_{j}<C(A)_{ij}&\forall(i,j)\not\in\Supp(\hat{P})\\
f_{i}+g_{j}=C(A)_{ij}&\forall(i,j)\in\Supp(\hat{P}),\end{cases}

(18)

where
C(A)_{ij}=-x_{i}^{\top}Ay_{j}
. We in particular suppose
strict complementarity holds off the support
.

Let
M_{ij}:=x_{i}y_{j}^{\top}
. If

\mathrm{dim}\left\{M_{i,j}\;;\;(i,j)\in\Supp(\hat{P})\right\}<d^{2},

(19)

then
\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{0}(\hat{P}))\geqslant 2
.

Proof.

From (
19
), there exists a non-trivial
H\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that

\langle H,M_{ij}\rangle=0\qquad\forall(i,j)\in\supp(\hat{P}).

(20)

We will show that
\hat{P}
remains optimal for the perturbed cost
C(A+tH)
for all
t
sufficiently small.

For
(i,j)\in\supp(\hat{P})
, (
20
) implies

C(A+tH)_{ij}=-\langle A+tH,M_{ij}\rangle=-\langle A,M_{ij}\rangle=C(A)_{ij},

so the equalities
f_{i}+g_{j}=C(A+tH)_{ij}
remain valid on
\supp(\hat{P})
.

For
(i,j)\notin\supp(\hat{P})
, define the (positive) slack
s_{ij}:=C(A)_{ij}-(f_{i}+g_{j})>0
.
Since there are finitely many such indices, let

s_{\min}:=\min_{(i,j)\notin\supp(\hat{P})}s_{ij}\;>\;0\quad\text{and}\quad L_{\max}:=\max_{(i,j)\notin\supp(\hat{P})}|\langle H,M_{ij}\rangle|\;<\;\infty.

If
L_{\max}=0
the dual inequalities remain feasible for all
t
. Otherwise, choose

\varepsilon:=\frac{s_{\min}}{2L_{\max}}.

Then for any
|t|\leq\varepsilon
and any
(i,j)\notin\supp(\hat{P})
,

C(A+tH)_{ij}-(f_{i}+g_{j})=s_{ij}-t\langle H,M_{ij}\rangle\geq s_{\min}-|t|L_{\max}\geq\frac{s_{\min}}{2}>0,

so
f_{i}+g_{j}\leq C(A+tH)_{ij}
holds for all off-support indices as well. Hence
(f,g)
is dual-feasible for
C(A+tH)
and satisfies complementary slackness with
\hat{P}
. By strong duality,
\hat{P}
is optimal for
C(A+tH)
.
∎

Remark 23

(Non-identifiability.)
.

In general, a basic feasible plan has

|\supp(P)|\leq p+q-1
.
Indeed, a nondegenerate basic feasible plan corresponds to a spanning tree in the bipartite graph on
p+q
nodes which has exactly
p+q-1
edges/support. Thus, when
d
is moderate with
d^{2}>p+q-1
, we cannot expect identifiability due to (
19
).

Remark 24

(Identifiability is unstable when it occurs.)
.

In principle, uniqueness up to scaling could occur if
\{M_{ij}\}_{(i,j)\in\supp(\hat{P})}
spans
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and if the active-set structure in (
16
) is nondegenerate (e.g. strict inequalities off-support). However, both properties are unstable under perturbations of the empirical plan: small changes in
\hat{P}
(or its support pattern) can change which dual inequalities bind, thereby changing the face of the normal cone associated with
\hat{P}
. As a result, the set-valued map
\hat{P}\mapsto\mathcal{S}_{0}(\hat{P})
is generally discontinuous, and any procedure that selects a single representative
\hat{A}
from
\mathcal{S}_{0}(\hat{P})
(e.g. by normalization or tie-breaking) may exhibit
argmin jumps
under arbitrarily small perturbations of
\hat{P}
.

We provide in the next subsection (Figure
2
) a numerical illustration of the polyhedral nature of the loss
\mathcal{L}_{0}
for a discrete measure with a low number of atoms. We observe that even if the continuous distributions satisfy (
U
), the problem remains degenerate with a low number of samples.

5.2.1
Elliptical distributions

In this section, we consider the setting where
\alpha
and
\beta
are elliptical distributions, where the iOT problem can be studied explicitly.

An elliptical distribution of mean
\mu
, variance
\Omega\Omega^{\top}\coloneqq\Sigma
and base
g
is written
\mathcal{E}(\mu,\Omega,g)
and has a density
f
with respect to Lebesgue of the form

f(x)=|\Sigma|^{-1/2}g((x-\mu)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu))\quad\text{where}\quad\Sigma=\Omega\Omega^{\top}

and where
g
is a centered radial density, i.e.

g(Rx)=g(x)\quad\forall\,R\in\textbf{O}_{d}\;,\quad\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim g}[Z]=0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim g}[ZZ^{\top}]=\mathrm{Id}

A typical example of radial base is
g(x)=(2\pi)^{-d/2}e^{-\left\|x\right\|^{2}/2}
. In this case
\mathcal{E}(\mu,\Omega,g)=\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)
where
\Omega\Omega^{\top}\coloneqq\Sigma
.

Equivalently we say that
X\sim\mathcal{E}(\mu,\Omega,g)
if

X=\mu+\Omega Z\quad\text{where}\quad Z\sim g

In this subsection we assume that
\alpha
and
\beta
are elliptical with the same base distribution
g
i.e.

\alpha=\mathcal{E}(\mu_{1},\Omega_{1},g)\quad\text{and}\quad\beta=\mathcal{E}(\mu_{2},\Omega_{2},g)

Equivalently we say that
X\sim\alpha
and
Y\sim\beta
if and only if

X=\mu_{1}+\Omega_{1}Z\quad\text{and}\quad Y=\mu_{2}+\Omega_{2}Z\quad\text{with}\quad Z\sim g

Proposition 25
.

Assume that
\alpha
and
\beta
have the same base
g
. Given
\hat{A}
an invertible matrix, the OT map
T_{\hat{A}}
for the cost
c_{\hat{A}}
is affine with

T_{\hat{A}}(x)=\mu_{2}+\Omega_{2}\hat{V}\hat{U}^{\top}\Omega_{1}^{-1}(x-\mu_{1})\quad\text{where}\quad\Omega_{1}^{\top}\hat{A}\Omega_{2}\overset{SVD}{=}\hat{U}\hat{S}\hat{V}^{\top}

Moreover

S_{0}(\hat{\pi})=\left\{A:\Omega_{1}A\Omega_{2}^{\top}=\hat{U}\diag(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\hat{V}^{\top},\;x_{i}>0\right\}

Proof.

Assume that
\hat{A}=\mathrm{Id}
then we know from
20
that
T_{\mathrm{Id}}
is affine with

T_{\mathrm{Id}}(x)=\mu_{2}+\Omega_{2}\Omega_{1}^{-1}(x-\mu_{1})

Now, for a given invertible
\hat{A}
, after performing the linear change of variable
\hat{A}^{\top}\#\alpha
one recovers the Euclidean transport where the Brenier map is affine. Since the change of variable is linear,
T_{\hat{A}}
is also affine with
T_{\hat{A}}(x)=b+Mx
. The map
T_{\hat{A}}
solves the following optimization problem

\max_{T\#\alpha=\beta}\mathbb{E}[X^{\top}\hat{A}T(X)]

Matching first moments gives
T(x)=\mu_{2}+M(x-\mu_{1})
. Matching second moments gives
M\Sigma_{1}M^{\top}=\Sigma_{2}
where
\Sigma_{i}=\Omega_{i}\Omega_{i}^{\top}

After canceling the constant terms, the optimization problem becomes,

\max_{M\Sigma_{1}M^{\top}=\Sigma_{2}}\Tr(\hat{A}M\Sigma_{1})

Write
M=\Omega_{2}Q\Omega^{-1}_{1}
for some matrix
Q
. Then the constraint becomes

M\Sigma_{1}M^{\top}=\Sigma_{2}\iff Q\in\textbf{O}_{d}

Rewriting the optimization in term of
Q\in\textbf{O}_{d}
gives

\max_{Q\in\textbf{O}_{d}}\Tr(\Omega_{1}^{\top}\hat{A}\Omega_{2}Q)

which is solved by

Q^{*}=\hat{V}\hat{U}^{\top}\quad\text{where}\quad\Omega_{1}^{\top}\hat{A}\Omega_{2}\overset{SVD}{=}\hat{U}\hat{S}\hat{V}^{\top}

Therefore

T_{\hat{A}}(x)=\mu_{2}+\Omega_{2}\hat{V}\hat{U}^{\top}\Omega_{1}^{-1}(x-\mu_{1})\quad\text{where}\quad\Omega_{1}^{\top}\hat{A}\Omega_{2}\overset{SVD}{=}\hat{U}\hat{S}\hat{V}^{\top}

Hence, the Brenier map
T_{\hat{A}}
and thus
\hat{\pi}
is uniquely determined by the singular vectors of
\Omega_{1}^{\top}\hat{A}\Omega_{2}
and not its singular values. We conclude that

S_{0}(\hat{\pi})=\left\{A:\;\Omega_{1}^{\top}A\Omega_{2}^{\top}=\hat{U}\diag(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\hat{V}^{\top},\;x_{i}>0\right\}

∎

5.2.2
Radial densities

Here we consider
\alpha,\beta

Proposition 26

(Identifiability for radial densities with distinct bases)
.

Let
\alpha=\mathcal{E}(0,\mathrm{Id},f)
and
\beta=\mathcal{E}(0,\mathrm{Id},g)
have radial densities
f
and
g
and suppose
\hat{A}=\mathrm{Id}
. Then, the OT map satisfies

T(x)=b(\left\|x\right\|)\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|}

If
\alpha
has full support and
b
is not linear, (
U
) holds.

Proof.

Since
\alpha,\beta
have radial densities,
f(Rx)=f(x)
and
g(Rx)=g(x)
for all
R\in\textbf{O}_{d}
. Given a map
T
that pushes forward
\alpha
to
\beta
, for all
R\in\textbf{O}_{d}
the map
\tilde{T}_{R}(x)=R^{-1}T(Rx)
pushes
\alpha
to
\beta
as well. Since the OT map is unique,

RT(x)=T(Rx)

It the follows that
T(x)=b(\left\|x\right\|)x/\left\|x\right\|
.

Given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
r=\left\|x\right\|
and
u=x/r
. Then,

\nabla T(x)=b^{\prime}(r)uu^{\top}+\frac{b(r)}{r}(\mathrm{Id}-uu^{\top})=\left(b^{\prime}(r)-\frac{b(r)}{r}\right)uu^{\top}+\frac{b(r)}{r}Id.

Note that

(i.e.
b(r)=e^{C}r

) implies that

is constant and hence (
U
) fails.

On the other hand, if
b
is not linear, then
\nabla T(x)=a(r)uu^{\top}+b(r)/r\mathrm{Id}
for some function

that is not identically equal to zero. If
\alpha
has full support then for all

on the sphere,

uu^{\top}-vv^{\top}\in\Span\{\nabla T(x),\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}

which guarantees the condition (
U
).

5.2.3
The entropic gap loss for Gaussians

We now turn to the behavior of the entropy gap loss in the Gaussian setting. The following result presents the closed form expression of the loss

L
ε

and its corresponding minimizer
A_{\varepsilon}
.

Proposition 27
.

Let
\alpha=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\alpha})
and
\beta=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\beta})
be two centered Gaussian distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\hat{\pi}
be the plan between
\alpha
and
\beta
with respect to the cost
\hat{A}
and let
\hat{K}=\int xy^{\top}d\hat{\pi}(x,y)
. Write
\hat{G}=\Sigma_{\alpha}^{-1/2}\hat{K}\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1/2}
. Then, for an invertible matrix
A
,

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(A)=-\langle\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}A\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2},\hat{G}\rangle-\inf_{\left\|G\right\|_{op}\leq 1}-\langle\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}A\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2},G\rangle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\log(\mathrm{Id}-GG^{\top}).

(21)

Moreover,
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
is unbounded from below.

On the other hand, the conjugated nuclear norm regularized problem for
\lambda\in(0,1]

\min_{A\succ 0}\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(A)\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(A)\coloneqq\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(A)+\lambda\left\|\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}A\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}\right\|_{*}

admits a unique solution. For
\lambda=\lambda_{0}\varepsilon
, the minimizer
A_{\varepsilon}
satisfies

B_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}A_{\varepsilon}\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}=\frac{(1-\lambda_{0}\varepsilon)}{\lambda_{0}(2-\lambda_{0}\varepsilon)}\hat{U}\hat{V}^{\top}

where
\hat{U},\hat{V}
are the left and right singular vectors of
\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}\hat{A}\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}
.

Moreover, if
A_{0}
is defined by

A_{0}\coloneqq\underset{A\in S_{0}(\hat{\pi})}{\arg\min}-\frac{1}{2}\log\det A+\lambda_{0}\left\|\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}A\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}\right\|_{*}

Then
\displaystyle\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}A_{0}\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}=\frac{1}{2\lambda_{0}}\hat{U}\hat{V}^{\top}

and hence,
\left\|A_{0}-A_{\varepsilon}\right\|\sim\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Let’s first study the term
\mathtt{OT}^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha,\beta}(A)
. It is known that when
\alpha
and
\beta
are Gaussian,
\Pi(\alpha,\beta)
is structured as

\Pi(\alpha,\beta)=\left\{\pi=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma),\quad\text{where}\quad\Sigma=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Sigma_{\alpha}&K\\
K^{\top}&\Sigma_{\beta}\end{array}\right)\succ 0\right\}

Note that
K=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[XY^{\top}]
is the cross variance of
\pi
, hence
\langle c_{A},\pi\rangle=-\langle A,K\rangle
.

Using a Schur complement argument gives

\left\{K:\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Sigma_{\alpha}&K\\
K^{\top}&\Sigma_{\beta}\end{array}\right)\succ 0\right\}=\left\{\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}G\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2},\;\left\|G\right\|_{op}\leq 1\right\}

Moreover, a direct computation of the KL leads to

\mathrm{KL}(\pi|\alpha\otimes\beta)=-\frac{1}{2}\log(\mathrm{Id}-GG^{\top})\quad\text{where}\quad K=\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}G\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}\quad\text{with}\quad\pi=\mathcal{N}\left(0,\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Sigma_{\alpha}&K\\
K^{\top}&\Sigma_{\beta}\end{array}\right)\right)

Therefore, we have the following reformulation

\mathtt{OT}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\varepsilon}(A)=\inf_{\left\|G\right\|_{op}\leq 1}-\langle\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}A\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2},G\rangle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\log(\mathrm{Id}-GG^{\top})

which gives the expression for
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(A)
in (
21
).

For the linear term, observe that using proposition
25
, one can show that
\hat{G}=\hat{U}\hat{V}^{\top}
where
\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}\hat{A}\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}\overset{SVD}{=}\hat{U}\hat{S}\hat{V}^{\top}
. Indeed, since
\hat{A}
is supposed to be invertible, we know that

T_{\hat{A}}(x)=\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}\hat{V}\hat{U}^{\top}\Sigma_{\alpha}^{-1/2}x\quad\text{where}\quad\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}\hat{A}\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}\overset{SVD}{=}\hat{U}\hat{S}\hat{V}^{\top}

Then, by definition,

\hat{K}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\alpha}[XT_{\hat{A}}(X)^{\top}]=\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}\hat{U}\hat{V}^{\top}\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}

hence
\hat{G}=\Sigma^{-1/2}_{\alpha}\hat{K}\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1/2}=\hat{U}\hat{V}^{\top}
.

Now, to study the minimizer of
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
, notice that for any
B
invertible,

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(\Sigma_{\alpha}^{-1/2}B\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1/2})=-\langle B,\hat{G}\rangle-\inf_{\left\|G\right\|_{op}\leq 1}-\langle B,G\rangle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\log(\mathrm{Id}-GG^{\top})

Because minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
is equivalent to minimizing
B\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(\Sigma_{\alpha}^{-1/2}B\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1/2})
, we will define

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}(B)\coloneqq\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}(\Sigma_{\alpha}^{-1/2}B\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1/2})=-\langle B,\hat{G}\rangle-\inf_{\left\|G\right\|_{op}\leq 1}-\langle B,G\rangle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\log(\mathrm{Id}-GG^{\top})

Now let’s study the minimizer
G_{\varepsilon}^{B}
given by the second term. Write
B=\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}A\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}
and its SVD
\displaystyle B=U\diag(b_{i})V^{\top}
with
b_{i}>0
. By orthogonal invariance, the unique minimizer
G_{\varepsilon}^{B}
giving the second term aligns with
B
as :

G_{\varepsilon}^{B}=U\diag(g_{\varepsilon}(b_{i}))V^{\top}

where
g_{\varepsilon}(b)
is solution of

\min_{|g|<1}\{-bg-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\log(1-g^{2})\}

First order optimality leads to
-b+\varepsilon g/(1-g^{2})=0
, hence for
b>0

g_{\varepsilon}(b)=\frac{2b}{\varepsilon+\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}+4b^{2}}}\in[0,1),\quad g_{\varepsilon}(0)=0

Putting this expression into the value leads to

\inf_{\left\|G\right\|_{op}\leq 1}-\langle B,G\rangle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\log(\mathrm{Id}-GG^{\top})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}f_{\varepsilon}(b_{i})

where

f_{\varepsilon}(b)=-\frac{2b^{2}}{\varepsilon+\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}+4b^{2}}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(\log(\varepsilon+\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}+4b^{2}})-\log(2\varepsilon)\right)

Then

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}(B)=-\sum_{i=1}^{d}(b_{i}+f_{\varepsilon}(b_{i}))\quad\text{where}\quad B\overset{SVD}{=}U\diag(b_{i})V^{\top}

which diverges to
-\infty
as, for instance,
b_{1}\to\infty
.

Then, to make the optimization problem feasible, we need to regularize. Given
\lambda\in(0,1]
define

\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\varepsilon}(B)=\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}(B)+\lambda\left\|B\right\|_{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(-b_{i}-f_{\varepsilon}(b_{i})+\lambda|b_{i}|\right)

so that
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\varepsilon}(\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}A\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2})=\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(A)
.

Let’s first show that
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\varepsilon}
admits an invertible minimizer.
Around
+\infty
, the scalar loss behaves as

-b-f_{\varepsilon}(b)+\lambda|b|\sim\lambda b-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\log b\to+\infty

then
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}
is coercive. We have seen that it is convex as well.

When
\lambda\in(0,1]
, the slope of
b\mapsto\lambda|b|-b-f_{\varepsilon}(b)
is strictly negative at
0^{+}
, therefore the minimum of
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\varepsilon}
cannot have a singular value equal to 0. Then by coercivity, convexity, and degeneracy around non-invertible matrices,
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}
admits a unique invertible minimizer
B_{\varepsilon}
. Because it is invertible, at
B_{\varepsilon}
the minimizer
G_{\varepsilon}^{B_{\varepsilon}}
, giving the second term, is unique and is written as

G_{\varepsilon}^{B_{\varepsilon}}=U_{\varepsilon}\diag(g_{\varepsilon}(b_{i,\varepsilon}))V_{\varepsilon}^{\top}\quad\text{where}\quad B_{\varepsilon}\overset{SVD}{=}U_{\varepsilon}\diag(b_{i,\varepsilon})V_{\varepsilon}^{\top}

Then, thanks to the envelope theorem, the first-order condition is

-\hat{G}+G_{\varepsilon}^{B_{\varepsilon}}+\lambda U_{\varepsilon}V_{\varepsilon}^{T}=0

which can be written as

\hat{U}\hat{V}^{\top}=U_{\varepsilon}\diag(g_{\varepsilon}(b_{i,\varepsilon})+\lambda)V_{\varepsilon}^{\top}

Multiplying by
U_{\varepsilon}^{\top}
and
V_{\varepsilon}
gives

\diag(g_{\varepsilon}(b_{i,\varepsilon})+\lambda)=U_{\varepsilon}^{\top}\hat{U}\hat{V}^{\top}V_{\varepsilon}

Hence
U_{\varepsilon}^{\top}\hat{U}\hat{V}^{\top}V_{\varepsilon}
is an orthogonal diagonal matrix with positive coefficient (indeed
g_{\varepsilon}(b_{i,\varepsilon})+\lambda\geq 0
) it must be equal to
\mathrm{Id}
.
Thus

U_{\varepsilon}V_{\varepsilon}^{\top}=\hat{U}\hat{V}^{\top}\quad\text{and}\quad g_{\varepsilon}(b_{i,\varepsilon})=1-\lambda\;\forall i=1,\dots,d

Now, solving
g_{\varepsilon}(b_{i,\varepsilon})+\lambda=1
through

\frac{2b_{i,\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon+\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}+4b_{i,\varepsilon}^{2}}}=1-\lambda,\quad b_{i,\varepsilon}\geq 0

gives

b_{i,\varepsilon}=\frac{\varepsilon(1-\lambda)}{\lambda(2-\lambda)}

Consequently

B_{\varepsilon}=\frac{\varepsilon(1-\lambda)}{\lambda(2-\lambda)}\hat{U}\hat{V}^{\top}

Notice that
B_{\varepsilon}
converges if and only if
\lambda=\lambda_{0}\varepsilon
. If so, it converges toward
B_{0}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\lambda_{0}}\hat{U}\hat{V}^{\top}
. Now let’s prove that
B_{0}=\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}A_{0}\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}
where

A_{0}\coloneqq\underset{A\in S_{0}(\hat{\pi})}{\arg\min}-\frac{1}{2}\log\det A+\lambda_{0}\left\|\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}A\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}\right\|_{*}

Note that this problem is equivalent to the following one by cancelling the constant terms :

A_{0}\coloneqq\underset{A\in S_{0}(\hat{\pi})}{\arg\min}-\frac{1}{2}\log\det\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}A\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}+\lambda_{0}\left\|\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}A\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}\right\|_{*}

Hence, thanks to the description of
S_{0}(\hat{\pi})
given by proposition
25
, performing the change of variable
B=\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}A\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}
gives

\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}A_{0}\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}B=\hat{U}\diag(x_{i})\hat{V}^{\top}\\
x_{i}>0\end{subarray}}{\arg\min}-\frac{1}{2}\log\det B+\lambda_{0}\left\|B\right\|_{*}

This problem is equivalent to

\arg\min_{x_{i}>0}\sum_{i=1}^{d}-\frac{1}{2}\log(x_{i})+\lambda_{0}x_{i}

which has a unique solution given by
x_{i}=\frac{1}{2\lambda_{0}}\;\forall i=1,\dots,d
. Hence,
\Sigma_{\alpha}^{1/2}A_{0}\Sigma_{\beta}^{1/2}=\frac{1}{2\lambda_{0}}\hat{U}\hat{V}^{\top}=B_{0}

∎

Conclusion

This work establishes a sharp contrast between inverse OT regimes: from a single coupling, the problem is intrinsically degenerate in discrete/polyhedral or highly symmetric settings, while smooth continuous marginals can restore local well-posedness through transport curvature. Theorem
1
characterizes the second-order geometry of OT with respect to the cost function. As a result, Theorems
2
,
3
,
4
, and
5
provide a complete chain from identifiability to quantitative recovery.

Beyond theory, these findings are directly relevant for practical iOT deployment. In realistic pipelines, one works with finite samples and entropic surrogates; our analysis clarifies how
\varepsilon
controls a bias–conditioning tradeoff and how this interacts with sampling noise. This gives principled guidance for choosing regularization and for interpreting recovered costs.

A key application domain is single-cell genomics, where OT-type couplings are used to relate cell populations across time or conditions
37
;
4
. In inverse formulations, the learned cost can encode biologically meaningful relations between gene programs or latent cellular states
35
. Our results indicate when such inferred costs are structurally identifiable and when they may instead reflect degeneracies (symmetry, discretization, or over-regularization). This opens a path toward more interpretable and robust inverse-OT analyses in computational biology.

Appendix A
Sample complexity of entropic OT

The main result of Weed and Mena
28
establishes the sample complexity of entropic optimal transport, and their results are given
in expectation
. In this section, we convert their result to a
high probability
statement.

Theorem A.1

(Sample complexity of entropic optimal transport)
.

Assume that
\alpha
and
\beta
are sub-Gaussian.
Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{\left\|A\right\|\leqslant B}\left\lvert\mathtt{OT}^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}}(c_{A})-\mathtt{OT}^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha,\beta}(c_{A})\right\rvert\leqslant\frac{C_{\varepsilon,\delta}}{\sqrt{n}}

where
C_{\varepsilon,\delta}
is polynomial in
1/\varepsilon
and logarithmic in
1/\delta
.

A.1
Useful tools on sub-Gaussian and sub-Exponential variables

We are going to handle sub-Gaussian and sub-exponential random variables. We use in the following a series of useful tools, which are detailed in Sections 2.7, 2.8, and 3.4 of
41

Definition A.1

(Orlicz’s sub Gaussian norm)
.

Let
Z
be a scalar random variable. We define the Orlicz’s sub Gaussian norm of
Z
to be

\left\|Z\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\inf\{c>0,\mathbb{E}_{P}[\exp(Z^{2}/2c^{2})]\leq 2\}

Z
is said to be
\sigma^{2}
-sub Gaussian if
\left\|Z-\mathbb{E}_{P}[Z]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\sigma
.

For a random vector we said that
Z
is
\sigma^{2}
-sub Gaussian if all its centered projection are
\sigma^{2}
-sub Gaussian, or equivalently if :

\left\|Z-\mathbb{E}Z\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\coloneqq\sup_{\left\|u\right\|=1}\left\|u^{\top}(Z-\mathbb{E}Z)\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\sigma

Here we suppose that our densities
\alpha
and
\beta
are
\sigma^{2}-
sub Gaussian which means for
X\sim\alpha
and
Y\sim\beta
we have
\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{2}},\left\|Y\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\sigma
.

Remark A.1
.

For all invertible matrices
A
, given
\alpha
a
\sigma^{2}-
sub Gaussian measure,
A\#\alpha
is at least
\left\|A\right\|_{op}^{2}\sigma^{2}-
sub Gaussian. We will therefore write
\sigma_{A}=(1\wedge\left\|A\right\|_{op})\sigma
so that
A\#\alpha
is
\sigma_{A}^{2}-
sub Gaussian.

Definition A.2

(Orlicz’s sub exponential norm)
.

Let
Z
be a scalar random variable. We define the Orlicz’s sub exponential norm of
Z
to be

\left\|Z\right\|_{\psi_{1}}=\inf\{c>0,\mathbb{E}_{P}[\exp(Z/2c)]\leq 2\}

Z
is said to be
\sigma-
sub Exponential if
\left\|Z-\mathbb{E}_{P}[Z]\right\|_{\psi_{1}}\leq\sigma
.

We will only use the sub-exponentiality for a scalar random variable here. These Orlicz norms are indeed norms, and they satisfy a few useful properties stated below:

Lemma A.2
.

Let
Z_{1},Z_{2}
be two scalar random variables. Then,

\displaystyle\left\|Z_{1}Z_{2}\right\|_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|Z_{1}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\left\|Z_{2}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle\left\|Z_{1}-\mathbb{E}Z_{1}\right\|_{\psi_{a}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|Z_{1}\right\|_{\psi_{a}}\quad\text{where}\quad a=1,2

Lemma A.3
.

If
X
is a
\sigma^{2}
-sub Gaussian random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
then
\left\|\left\|X\right\|^{2}\right\|_{\psi_{1}}\leq d\sigma^{2}

A well-known concentration bound for a sub-exponential random variable is the Bernstein inequality. We manage the first term by using it.

Lemma A.4

(Bernstein inequality)
.

Let
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
be iid sub Exponential random variable such that for all
i=1,\dots n
,
\left\|Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]\right\|_{\psi_{1}}\leq K
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}]\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-cn\min\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{K^{2}},\frac{t}{K}\right\}\right)

In our framework, we are going to consider a small deviation, so
t\to 0
, therefore the term in
t^{2}
dominates.

Proof of Theorem

(Sample complexity of entropic OT)

, which states a similar bound in expectation assuming

and

are

\left\|A_{t}\right\|^{2}=\left(\frac{\left\langle A_{t},\,B\right\rangle}{\left\|B\right\|^{2}}+t\right)^{2}+\left\|V\right\|^{2}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle(*)

\displaystyle=\left\|A_{t}\right\|^{2}-\left(\left\langle\frac{B}{\left\|B\right\|},\,A_{t}\right\rangle\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|A_{t}\right\|^{2}\left(\left\langle\frac{B}{\left\|B\right\|},\,A_{0}\right\rangle+t\left\|B\right\|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|A_{t}\right\|^{2}-\left(\left\langle\frac{B}{\left\|B\right\|^{2}},\,A_{0}\right\rangle+t\right)^{2}\left\|B\right\|^{2}=\left\|V\right\|^{2}.

Therefore,

\left\|P_{A_{t}}^{\perp}B\right\|^{2}=\frac{\left\|B\right\|^{2}}{\left\|A_{t}\right\|^{2}}\left\|V\right\|^{2}.

So,

\inf_{t\in[0,1]}\left\|P_{A_{t}}^{\perp}B\right\|^{2}=\frac{\left\|B\right\|^{2}\left\|V\right\|^{2}}{\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left\|A_{t}\right\|^{2}}.

By convexity of
t\mapsto\left\|A_{t}\right\|^{2}
, the supremum is achieved at 0 or 1. So,

\inf_{t\in[0,1]}\left\|P_{A_{t}}^{\perp}B\right\|^{2}=\min\{\left\|P_{A_{0}}^{\perp}A\right\|^{2},\left\|P_{A}^{\perp}A_{0}\right\|^{2}\}.

Finally,

\left\|P_{A_{0}}^{\perp}A\right\|^{2}=\left\|A\right\|^{2}\left(1-\left(\left\langle\frac{A}{\left\|A\right\|},\,\frac{A_{0}}{\left\|A_{0}\right\|}\right\rangle\right)^{2}\right)
.
∎
