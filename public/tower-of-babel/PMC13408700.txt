Association of the cardiometabolic index and depressive symptoms with cardiovascular disease in cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome stages 0–3: A prospective cohort study
Authors: Yuehua Li, Yang Ding, Jing Yang, Junpeng Zhai, Wei Tian, Yizhan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe progressive nature of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome (CKM) allows early intervention during stages 0–3. However, the role of the cardiometabolic index (CMI) across CKM stages 0–3 and its potential synergy with depressive symptoms remain unclear. We followed 5,556 CHARLS participants (aged ≥ 45, CKM stages 0–3) for 7 years. Higher CMI and cumulative average CMI (CumCMI) were independently associated with CVD (HR for Q4 vs. Q1: 1.27, P trend = 0.009; HR for high vs. low CumCMI: 1.28, P trend < 0.001). Nonlinear thresholds were 0.47 (CMI) and 2.08 (CumCMI). Among 2,595 participants with complete depressive symptoms data, those with high CumCMI alone (HR = 1.61) or depressive symptoms alone (HR = 1.43) had elevated risk, whereas those with both factors had the highest risk (HR = 2.08). Baseline AUC was 0.651; adding CMI alone yielded no improvement (0.651), whereas adding depressive symptoms alone raised it to 0.657; both together gave 0.657–0.658. However, additive interaction was not statistically significant, and the AUC improvement from adding depressive symptoms to a model already containing CMI was minimal (ΔAUC ≈ 0.007), suggesting that this combined indicator may be more suitable for population-level risk stratification than individual clinical prediction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-63006-z.Keywords: Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome, Cardiovascular disease, Cardiometabolic index, Depressive symptomsSubject terms: Biomarkers, Cardiology, Diseases, Endocrinology, Risk factors

Introduction

Accounting for approximately one-third of all deaths worldwide, cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the principal cause of death, especially among older adults with multiple chronic conditions. Common chronic diseases such as hypertension, lipid disorder, diabetes, and adiposity, each also a risk factor for CVD, tend to cluster1,2. The cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome, introduced by the American Heart Association in 2023, results from pathophysiological interplay among adiposity, type 2 diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and CVD3,4. Patients with CKM face a 2- to 4-fold higher risk of myocardial infarction, heart failure, and CVD death than those without risk factors5. Stages 0–3 represent a preclinical window spanning from metabolic health to the emergence of hypertension, dyslipidemia, or kidney impairment, during which early identification can delay or prevent CVD6,7. Thus, a composite indicator to predict CVD risk in stages 0–3 is of substantial clinical value.

The cardiometabolic index (CMI) integrates visceral adiposity and lipid dysregulation8,9. Unlike traditional single metrics, CMI captures metabolic dysfunction more comprehensively and predicts risk across multiple conditions, including metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, atherosclerosis, and CVD10–13. CMI reflects visceral fat accumulation and lipid abnormalities, key drivers of insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction, all of which underlie CKM syndrome14. Although CMI independently predicts CKM staging15, its ability to stratify CVD risk specifically in patients with CKM stages 0–3 remains unclear. Most prior studies have relied on static or two-time-point averaged CMI, failing to capture long-term cumulative burden16,17. Given the progressive nature of cardiometabolic disease, this methodological gap leaves the role of CMI in CVD risk stratification largely unaddressed.

Beyond metabolic dysregulation, psychological factors, notably depressive symptoms, may modulate CVD risk18. Depression affects 5.7% of adults globally19. Among CKM patients, the prevalence of depressive symptoms rises markedly with stage, from 3.4% to 13.9% in early stages to 18.5%–30.0% in advanced stages, with late-stage patients at approximately twice the risk of those without risk factors20. Depressive symptoms and CKM engage in a bidirectional vicious cycle, and depressive symptoms shares pathophysiological pathways with the metabolic abnormalities captured by CMI (insulin resistance, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction), thereby synergistically accelerating CVD progression21,22. However, no study has specifically examined their synergistic effects on CVD risk in the CKM population, and the underlying mechanisms remain unexplored.

Accordingly, using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), this study examines, among patients with CKM stages 0–3, the independent effects of CMI quartile groups and cumulative CMI on CVD incidence, as well as their combined effects with depressive symptoms. Further analyses aim to evaluate the potential value of integrating these markers for population-level risk stratification in primary CVD prevention targeting metabolically diverse communities.

Methods

Study design and participant cohort

This study used data from CHARLS (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en), a national-level longitudinal investigation representative of Chinese adults 45 years and above. Launched in 2011 and conducted biennially through 2020, the survey covered 150 county-level and 450 village-level units, enrolling 19,000 respondents from 12,400 households. The dataset includes individual, family, and community-level indicators on health status, economic conditions, social security, physical examinations, and biological biomarkers23. The original protocol obtained approval from the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University (IRB 00001052–11015). Each participant provided a written statement of informed consent. Our investigation was designed, performed, and reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines for observational epidemiological studies. We used the 2011 CHARLS survey as baseline (11,847 participants), with follow-up assessments in three time points: 2013, 2015, and 2018. Displayed in Fig. 1 is the sample screening process. Subjects were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) had missing age or were aged < 45 years; (2) had missing CKM data or were classified as CKM stage 4 at baseline; (3) had missing baseline CMI; (4) had prevalent CVD at baseline; (5) had missing CVD information in 2011; or (6) had missing CVD data during any follow-up wave. We did not exclude participants with cancer, liver disease, or psychiatric disorders, as CHARLS lacks detailed clinical data to identify active cases, and exclusion based solely on binary indicators could introduce selection bias. Inflammatory diseases and glucocorticoid use were not measured in CHARLS and therefore could not be accounted for13. Additionally, CHARLS captures only clinically manifest CVD without subclinical biomarkers, so we restricted exclusion to overt cases. A cohort of 5,556 participants (CKM stages 0–3) constituted the first sample, which served to explore the link between CMI and CVD. To further assess the synergistic effect of CMI and depressive symptoms, we further removed cases lacking baseline depressive symptoms data, resulting in a subsample of 2,595 individuals.

Calculation of the CMI and cumulative

In the CHARLS survey, fasting blood samples and comprehensive physical examinations were collected at the 2011 baseline survey and the 2015 follow-up survey; blood samples were not collected in the 2013 wave, and only questionnaire-based data were gathered. Therefore, CMI could only be calculated at these two time points. To capture the cumulative metabolic burden over time, we calculated the cumulative CMI (CumCMI): CumCMI = [(CMI₂₀₁₁ + CMI₂₀₁₅) / 2] × (2015–2011). This formula represents the area under the CMI trajectory curve over the 4-year exposure window, reflecting the cumulative metabolic exposure burden24.

Assessment of depression-related symptoms

The CES-D-10 scale was used to assess depressive symptoms at baseline and follow-up within the CHARLS cohort25. It comprises ten items, each offering four response options about the respondent’s experiences during the preceding seven days. The overall score ranges between 0 and 30, with elevated scores corresponding to increased severity of depressive symptoms. The CES-D-10 is a screening instrument rather than a diagnostic tool for major depressive disorder. The scale has shown good internal consistency and validity among Chinese middle-aged and older adults, covering two dimensions: negative affect and positive mental state. Furthermore, cross-population screening validity of the CES-D-10 is supported by global meta-analyses. Following previous CHARLS-based studies in Chinese middle-aged and older adult populations, we used a score of ≥ 12 as the primary cut-off to define clinically relevant depressive symptoms. This cut-off has been validated in Chinese older adult populations, with demonstrated sensitivity of 0.76 and specificity of 0.5526, and has been widely applied in epidemiologic studies of depressive Symptoms and CVD among Chinese older adults, enabling direct comparability with prior CHARLS-based findings27.

Descriptive criteria for CKM syndrome (phases 0–3)

In our study, CKM syndrome stages 0 through 3 were strictly defined according to the American Heart Association (AHA) Presidential Advisory Statement28 and operationalized using baseline (2011) CHARLS data. Stage 0 was defined as the absence of any CKM health risk factors, meaning participants did not meet any of the criteria for Stages 1–3. Stage 1 (excess or dysfunctional adiposity and/or prediabetes) required at least one of the following: (a) body mass index (BMI) ≥ 24 kg/m²; (b) waist circumference ≥ 102 cm for men or ≥ 88 cm for women; or (c) HbA1c between 5.7% and 6.4%. Stage 2 (metabolic risk factors or kidney disease) was defined by the presence of any of the following: (a) hypertension (self-reported physician diagnosis, or measured systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, or measured diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg); (b) type 2 diabetes mellitus (self-reported physician diagnosis, or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL); (c) hypertriglyceridemia (triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL); or (d) chronic kidney disease (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²)29. Stage 3 (subclinical CVD or high-risk CKD) was defined by the presence of either: (a) very high-risk chronic kidney disease (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m², corresponding to KDIGO stages G4 or G5)29; or (b) high predicted 10-year CVD risk (Framingham Risk Score ≥ 20%)30.

All staging was performed retrospectively using available baseline variables from the CHARLS database; the data sources and measurement methods for each criterion are summarized in Table S1.

Assessment of outcomes

In the CHARLS cohort, incident CVD was based on self-reported physician diagnosis of heart disease or stroke, ascertained through biennial structured interviews. For participants who were deceased or unable to respond, proxy reports were obtained and supplemented by linkage to the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) mortality database31,32. Validation studies have demonstrated substantial agreement between self-reported questionnaires and medical records for myocardial infarction and stroke33,34. CHARLS captures only clinically manifest CVD and does not include subclinical biomarkers; therefore, exclusion was restricted to clinically overt cases. The biomarker and anthropometric data used for CMI calculation were derived from the 2011 and 2015 surveys, and CVD outcomes were recorded at the 2013, 2015, and 2018 follow-ups.

Variables

Data were gathered on participants’ demographic characteristics, physical assessment results, laboratory values, and clinical history. The demographic information captured age, sex, location (urban vs. rural), marital status (married, defined as living with a spouse, vs. other), and education category (elementary or lower contrasted with secondary or higher). Smoking and drinking status were each assessed as binary variables (yes/no). Physical assessment captured the following: body mass index (BMI, kg/m²), body weight (kg), standing height (cm), and waist circumference (WC, cm). BMI = weight (kg) / [height (cm)/100]². Systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP and DBP, mmHg) was measured three times, and the mean value was calculated. Biochemical blood markers included several categories: glycemic indicators (fasting plasma glucose [FPG] and glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c]); lipid parameters (total cholesterol [TC], triglycerides [TG], high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C], low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C]); kidney function indices (serum creatinine [Scr], blood urea nitrogen [BUN], estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR]); and other markers (uric acid [UA], C-reactive protein [CRP], white blood cell count [WBC], platelets, hemoglobin). Medical history was recorded for diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension, and hyperlipidemia, defined by either a self-reported diagnosis of the respective disease, or the disease-specific criteria listed below. For diabetes mellitus (DM): use of insulin or oral antidiabetic drugs, or any of the following: FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%. For hypertension: use of antihypertensive medication, or any of the following: average SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg. For hyperlipidemia: use of statins or other lipid-lowering agents, or any of the following: TC ≥ 6.2 mmol/L, TG ≥ 2.3 mmol/L, LDL-C ≥ 4.1 mmol/L, or HDL-C < 1.0 mmol/L.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0, with statistical significance defined as two-sided P < 0.05. Missing data were addressed through multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE). Baseline characteristics were described according to CVD status, CMI quartiles, and the binary classification of depressive symptoms. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality of continuous variables; data following a normal distribution were reported as mean (SD), whereas non-normally distributed data were presented as median (IQR). Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages (n (%)). Group comparisons employed the chi-square test (for categorical variables), the t-test (for normally distributed continuous data), and the Mann–Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed continuous data). CMI was examined in quartiles and also as a dichotomized cumulative CMI (CumCMI). The optimal cut-off for CumCMI (Fig. S1) was determined using the “survminer” package via maximally selected rank statistics, which assigned participants to high- versus low-exposure categories. Cumulative CVD incidence was estimated with Kaplan–Meier curves, and between-group differences were tested using the log-rank test. The association between CMI and CVD was evaluated with a Cox proportional hazards model across three specifications: Model 1 (crude), Model 2 (demographically adjusted), and Model 3 (adjusted for metabolic comorbidities). Restricted cubic splines (RCS) with knots at the 0.05, 0.35, 0.65, and 0.95 quantiles were applied to examine non-linear relationships, and the Wald test was used to assess non-linearity. ROC curves were generated to evaluate the discriminative ability of CMI, depressive symptoms, and their combination. To further evaluate the additive interaction between high CumCMI and depressive symptoms, we calculated RERI, AP, and SI using a logistic regression model adjusted for the same covariates as Model 3. RERI was defined as HR₁₁ − HR₁₀ − HR₀₁ + 1; a RERI confidence interval crossing 0 or an SI deviating from 1 was considered evidence of no additive interaction. To assess the robustness of our findings, we performed two sensitivity analyses. First, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding metabolic covariates from Model 3 to evaluate whether the CMI–CVD association was independent of its overlap with CKM components, and performed a VIF analysis to assess multicollinearity (GVIF^(1/(2×Df)) > 5 indicating multicollinearity). Second, we repeated the main analyses using the median CMI value (0.48) as the cut-off. Subgroup analyses were stratified by age, gender, educational level, marriage condition, residential location, hypertension, lipid disorder, diabetes, and body mass index (BMI). Interaction terms in Cox models were evaluated with likelihood ratio tests, and forest plots displayed subgroup-specific hazard ratios (HRs) along with interaction P-values.

Results

Participant baseline characteristics

From the 2011 CHARLS wave, 11,847 subjects were enrolled; 5,556 met the eligibility criteria (mean age 57 years; 2,571 males [46.3%], 2,985 females [53.7%]). Over a median follow-up of 79.58 months, 1,283 individuals developed CVD (23.09%). Baseline characteristics categorized according to CVD presence or absence are displayed in Table 1. Compared with non-CVD participants, the CVD group was older, had a greater likelihood of being female, and exhibited elevated BMI and WC, and showed higher rates of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes. CMI was significantly higher in the CVD group (0.54 vs. 0.45; p < 0.001). Among CVD cases, 29.3% were at CKM stage 2 and 60.7% at stage 3. Marked differences were observed in blood pressure indices (SBP, DBP), glycemic parameters (FPG, HbA1c), lipid profile (TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C), as well as eGFR and WBC, along with platelets, BUN, and CRP (all p < 0.001), but not in Scr, UA, or hemoglobin.

Combined effects on CVD risk of CMI and depressive symptoms

Figure 4 presents Kaplan–Meier curves for cumulative CVD incidence by CMI and depression status. Participants with both high CumCMI and depressive symptoms had the highest risk, which was confi rmed by thefully adjusted Cox model (Table 6). In this model, individuals with both high CumCMI and depressive symptoms showed the highest risk (HR = 2.08, 95% CI: 1.49–2.92), followed by those with high CumCMI alone (HR = 1.61, 95% CI: 1.14–2.28) and then depressive symptoms alone (HR = 1.43, 95% CI: 0.98–2.09). After full adjustment in Model 3, this estimate decreased but remained approximately twofold higher (HR = 2.08, 95% CI: 1.49–2.92).

We further evaluated the predictive value of CMI and depressive symptoms for CVD in CKM stage 0–3 patients using ROC curve analysis (Fig. 5). Discussion

Based on data from the CHARLS, the associations of CMI and depressive symptoms with new-onset CVD in middle-aged and elderly adults with CKM syndrome stages 0–3 were systematically examined in this study. Both baseline high-quartile CMI and high time-cumulative CMI (CumCMI) were strongly associated with an increased risk of CVD, exhibiting a nonlinear dose–response relationship. These associations remained significant after adjustment for established CVD risk factors. In fully adjusted Cox models, the combination of high CumCMI and depressive symptoms was associated with the highest excess risk, with hazard ratios exceeding those of either factor alone. However, additive interaction analysis revealed no statistically significant synergy (RERI = 0.166, 95% CI: -0.468 to 0.800; AP = 0.073; SI = 0.131), and the AUC improvement from adding depressive symptoms was minimal (ΔAUC = 0.007). These findings were further reinforced by subgroup and sensitivity analyses in terms of robustness.

In patients with CKM syndrome stages 0–3, CMI was significantly associated with CVD risk (highest vs. lowest quartile: HR = 1.27, 95%CI:1.06–1.51, P = 0.008), with a clear dose–response trend (P for trend < 0.01).Previous studies have shown that several alternative metabolic indicators, such as the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, remnant cholesterol (RC), and non-HDL-C, are independently associated with CKM stage and CVD outcomes [35–42]. In our cohort of patients with CKM stages 0–3, we found that baseline high‑quartile CMI and high time‑cumulative CMI (CumCMI) were strongly associated with an increased risk of CVD, thereby extending the predictive value of CMI to this early-stage population.

To capture long-term metabolic burden beyond single measurements and to formally evaluate the combined effect of CMI and depressive symptoms, we introduced a time-cumulative CMI (CumCMI). Using maximally selected rank statistics, we identified an optimal cutoff of 1.48, which maximizes risk discrimination while avoiding misclassification from fixed percentiles. Participants with CumCMI ≥ 1.48 had a significantly elevated CVD risk. Unlike previous studies that only reported quartile- or continuous-based risk estimates13,16, our study provides a practical reference cutoff (1.48) for CumCMI to facilitate risk stratification. Of note, this cutoff requires further validation in independent cohorts.

Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a nonlinear association between CMI and CVD risk, with an inflection point at 0.47 (HR = 1), consistent with previous RCS-based findings by Cui et al. and Ren et al.13,40 The dose–response curve showed an overall upward trend, with the slope flattening at moderate-to-high CMI levels. A similar pattern was observed for CumCMI, with an inflection point at approximately 2.08. For both indicators, risk increased sharply below the inflection point and gradually slowed thereafter. Of note, these inflection points were derived from the present dataset and should be interpreted as exploratory findings, warranting further validation in independent cohorts to confirm their stability and applicability. A possible explanation for this pattern relates to the dynamic nature of CKM progression. In early stages (1–2), CMI may serve as a core driver of risk by reflecting visceral obesity, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia. In stage 3, as multi-organ involvement (hypertension, diabetes, kidney disease, and CVD) becomes established, CMI may shift from a core driver to a contributing factor. This transition may involve persistent low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress, wherein early metabolic disturbances directly damage the vascular endothelium, as supported by studies on GPER-mediated endothelial protection and glycocalyx degradation in early vascular injury43,44, and drive rapid risk escalation, whereas late-stage structural changes (atherosclerosis, calcification, and hypertrophy) may reduce cardiovascular responsiveness, slowing the rise in risk, a pattern consistent with the obesity paradox45. These mechanistic interpretations, however, remain speculative and require further investigation. We further assessed whether CMI provides prognostic information beyond its conceptual overlap with CKM components. VIF analysis showed no substantial multicollinearity between CMI and CKM components (all GVIF^(1/(2×Df)) < 1.54), and excluding metabolic covariates from Model 3 did not materially alter the association between high CumCMI and CVD (HR = 1.86, 95% CI: 1.53–2.27, P < 0.001), supporting the independent predictive value of CMI beyond CKM staging. This sensitivity analysis not only confirms the robustness of our findings but also suggests that the predictive information captured by CumCMI is not fully accounted for by its individual metabolic components, reinforcing its value as an integrated metabolic burden marker.

Beyond these metabolic drivers, emerging evidence highlights the significant role of psychological factors, particularly depressive symptoms, in modulating CVD risk, potentially through shared pathophysiological pathways. Previous studies have shown that depressive symptoms increases CVD risk and is associated with cardiometabolic abnormalities46,47. Our study extends this finding to the CKM stage 0–3 population, demonstrating a combined effect of high CumCMI and depressive symptoms on CVD risk, with an effect size exceeding that of CumCMI alone. Depressive symptoms promotes insulin resistance, visceral obesity, and endothelial dysfunction via HPA axis overactivation, sympathetic excitation, inflammatory responses, and adverse behavioral patterns, and calcium signaling dysregulation may represent a shared molecular mechanism linking depressive disorders to metabolic disturbances48.

. When it coexists with metabolic abnormalities, these pathological processes may be further exacerbated49,50. Notably, in our cohort, depressive symptoms alone was not statistically significantly associated with CVD risk, which differs from some previous findings13,45,46. A possible explanation is that depression shares multiple pathophysiological pathways with CKM, including HPA axis activation, chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and insulin resistance, and its effect is mediated by metabolic indicators51. This is consistent with the report by Cui et al. that depressive symptoms influence CVD indirectly through CMI13.

However, the clinical implications of these findings warrant careful interpretation. Adding depressive symptoms to models already adjusted for CMI and other covariates improved the AUC only marginally, from 0.651 to 0.658 (ΔAUC = 0.007), and the additive interaction metrics did not support a synergistic effect (RERI = 0.166, 95% CI: -0.468 to 0.800; AP = 0.073; SI = 0.131). Thus, this combined indicator is not suitable for individual CVD risk prediction; its utility, if any, lies at the population level. CMI is derived from routine blood tests (TG, HDL-C, and waist-to-height ratio) and, when combined with brief depressive symptoms screening tools such as the PHQ-2, could serve as a low-cost initial triage tool in primary care settings to identify high-risk subgroups for targeted interventions. This hypothesis, however, requires further validation through prospective studies and cost-effectiveness analyses.

Subgroup analyses showed that the combined effect of high CumCMI and depressive symptoms was consistent across age, sex, education, residence, and metabolic factors (all interaction P > 0.05), indicating good robustness. Notably, hypertension showed a significant interaction (P = 0.041), but hazard ratios were similar between hypertensive and non-hypertensive individuals (HR = 1.24 vs. 1.31), suggesting that hypertension does not substantially alter the combined effect. The combined effect was also significant in females, those with higher education, rural residents, and overweight/obese individuals (BMI ≥ 24 kg/m²), further supporting the main findings. We acknowledge that the multiple subgroup analyses performed increase the risk of false-positive findings due to multiple comparisons. Therefore, interaction P values near the significance threshold (e.g., P = 0.041 for hypertension) should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory rather than confirmatory. Nevertheless, the consistent direction of effect across most subgroups supports the overall robustness of our findings. However, Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, CHARLS lacked detailed medication data and relied on self-reported diagnoses, which may have introduced residual confounding and outcome misclassification15,17,23,31,32. Second, CKM staging was a pragmatic approximation, as cystatin C and imaging data were unavailable, potentially underclassifying some stage 3 participants; generalization to full AHA criteria should be cautious. We also acknowledge that subclinical cardiovascular disease could not be assessed or excluded due to the absence of relevant biomarkers in CHARLS, which may have influenced baseline metabolic or psychological status. Third, CES-D-10 was administered at a single time point, capturing symptom burden rather than clinical diagnosis25–27, and CumCMI was based on only two waves, which may have introduced reverse causation. Fourth, CVD was treated as a binary outcome, potentially obscuring subtype heterogeneity; spline-derived inflection points require external validation13,16; and competing-risk models could not be implemented due to the lack of cause-specific mortality data. Despite adjusting for available proxies, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusion

In patients with CKM stages 0–3, the joint presence of high CMI and depressive symptoms was associated with elevated CVD risk, although its predictive gain was limited (ΔAUC = 0.007), suggesting its primary value lies in population-level risk stratification rather than individual clinical prediction. As a low-cost indicator derived from routine blood tests and brief screening scales, this combined marker shows potential for primary care screening, but its clinical application requires further validation in prospective interventional studies.
