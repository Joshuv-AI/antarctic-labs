Abstract

x_{1}^{+2}=x_{2}^{-}

x_{2}^{+2}=x_{1}^{-}
. But then

Subcase 3c

and the corollary below that the graphs satisfying (iii) and (iv) but not (i) or (ii) of Theorem

Corollary 11
.

Let
G
be a graph with maximum degree
\Delta
. Then its coline graph
L
is not tough if and only if
G
either

(i)

has less than
2\Delta
edges;

(ii)

has exactly
2\Delta
edges and two vertices of degree
\Delta
that are adjacent;

(iii)

is one of the graphs in Figure
3
.

Proof.

The reverse implication is immediate for (i) and (ii) from Lemma
5
(i) and (ii), respectively, since any graph satisfying (ii) must be type-
A
. The fact that no graph in Figure
3
is tough is immediate from each of the cutsets depicted and Lemma
5
(iii)-(vi).

For the forward implication we assume that
G
has a non-tough coline graph
L
which doesn’t satisfy (i) or (ii).
Then
L
has a cutset
S
for which
c(L-S)>|S|
and so
G^{\prime}=G-S
, the graph formed from
G
by removing the edges in
S
, must have a disconnected coline graph, namely
L-S
.
So
G^{\prime}
must be an exceptional graph and as
L
doesn’t satisfy (i) or (ii),
G^{\prime}
satisfies one of (iii)-(vi) of Lemma
5
.
So we consider each of these in turn. Let
G^{\prime\prime}
be the subgraph of
G
consising of the edges in
E(G)-E(G^{\prime})
.

If
G^{\prime}=C_{4}
, then
G
must have at most
5=\rho(C_{4})-1
edges by Lemma
5
(iii) and at least 5 edges, since
G
doesn’t satisfy (ii). So
G
must have exactly 5 edges and
G^{\prime\prime}
must consist of a single edge disjoint from
G^{\prime}
, as otherwise,
G
would satisfy (i). So
G=C_{4}\cup K_{2}
, which is one of the graphs in Figure
3
.

If
G^{\prime}=F_{k}
for some
k\geq 2
, then by Lemma
5
(iv),
G
must have at most
k+3=\rho(F_{k})-1
edges and must have at least
2k
edges, because
G
doesn’t satisfy (i).
So
2k\leq k+3
which implies that
k\leq 3
.
If
k=2
and therefore
G
has at most 5 edges, then
G
must have maximum degree 2 as it doesn’t satisfy (i). Then
G
must in fact have exactly 5 edges as it doesn’t satisfy (ii) and has two adjacent vertices of degree 2.
Hence,
G^{\prime\prime}
must consist of two edges disjoint from
G^{\prime}
as otherwise
G
would satisfy (i). Thus
G
is either
K_{3}\cup 2K_{2}
or
K_{3}\cup P_{3}
, which are graphs in Figure
3
.
If
k=3
, then
G
has maximum degree at least 3 and so has 6 edges because
G
does not satisfying (i).
Therefore, at most 1 edge in
G^{\prime\prime}
is incident to a vertex in
G^{\prime}
and if such an edge exists, then it must be incident to the unique one in
G^{\prime}
with degree 1 as otherwise
G
would either have maximum degree at least 4 or have two adjacent vertices of degree 3, contrary to
G
not satisfying (i) and (ii), respectively.
Thus
G
must be one of the 4 graphs in the second last row of Figure
3
.

If
G^{\prime}=K_{4}^{-}
, then
G
must have at least 7 edges as otherwise it satisfies either (i) or (ii). By Lemma
5
(v),
G
has at most 7 edges and so
G
has exactly 7 edges. No edge in
G^{\prime\prime}
can be incident to one of the vertices of degree 3 in
G^{\prime}
as otherwise
G
would satisfy (i). Similarly, only 1 edge in
G^{\prime\prime}
can be adjacent to each of the 2 vertices in
G^{\prime}
of degree 2. Thus
G
must be one of the 6 graphs in Figure
3
whose exceptional graph is
K_{4}^{-}
or
G
must be
K_{4}\cup K_{2}
.

Finally if
G^{\prime}=K_{4}
, then
G
must have at least 7 edges as it doesn’t satisfy (ii) and has at most 8 edges by Lemma
5
(vi). If
G
has 7 edges, then
G
has maximum degree at least 4, and would therefore satisfy (i), unless
G=K_{4}\cup K_{2}
, which is one of the graphs in Figure
3
. If
G
has 8 edges, then at most one edge in
G^{\prime\prime}
is incident to a vertex in
G^{\prime}
as otherwise
G
would have maximum degree 5 or have two adjacent vertices of maximum degree 4, contrary to
G
not satisfying (i) and (ii), respectively. Thus
G
must be one of the first 4 graphs in Figure
3
.
