Cholecystectomy versus conservative management for patients with uncomplicated symptomatic gallstones and cholecystitis: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mahmoud Mohamed Gad, Qusai Ayad, May Mahmoud Elgamal, Elsayed S. Moubarak, Abdulrahman Faisal Ziada, Ahmad Beddor, Mariam A. Abusalah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGallstone disease affects 10–25% of the population, with higher prevalence in women over 40 years of age. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the standard treatment for symptomatic or complicated cases. However, it is costly, can cause adverse effects, and can result in post-cholecystectomy syndrome. Conservative management remains an option, especially for high surgical risk patients. The optimal management of uncomplicated gallstones remains controversial. This updated systematic review and meta-analysis incorporates recently published randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing the safety and efficacy of cholecystectomy with conservative treatment in symptomatic uncomplicated gallstones and mild acute cholecystitis.MethodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis followed PRISMA guidelines. We searched PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane Central for RCTs comparing cholecystectomy versus conservative management in adults with uncomplicated gallstones or mild acute cholecystitis. Primary outcomes were biliary colic and overall gallstone-related complications, while secondary outcomes included mortality and surgery-related complications. Pooled risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using fixed or random effect models based on heterogeneity. However, conservative management may be considered in selected patients due to its lower surgical complication rate and comparable major clinical outcomes. Background

Gallstone diseases are among the most prevalent gastrointestinal conditions, affecting 10–25% of the population, with higher prevalence in women over 40 [1]. Although complications such as acute cholecystitis, biliary colic, common bile duct stones, or acute pancreatitis may occur, the disease is generally benign, and most patients remain asymptomatic or never require treatment [1, 2]. The disease-related mortality is low, measuring less than 1% [3].

For symptomatic or complicated cases, the standard treatment is laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) since it is less invasive than open cholecystectomy, causes less pain after surgery, and allows for a quicker recovery [4]. However, surgery may result in complications such as bile duct injury, infection, or bleeding [5]. Additionally, some patients may develop post-cholecystectomy syndrome, a condition in which symptoms persist even after surgery [6]. According to a large prospective study conducted in the UK, 10.8% of participants experienced complications for 30 days following surgery, and 40% of patients may continue to experience discomfort and abdominal symptoms even after surgery [7]. Approximately 20% of individuals report ongoing pain resembling preoperative symptoms following cholecystectomy, whereas up to 14% experience the onset of new pain after the procedure [8]. Surgical treatment is also costly; according to the C-GALL trial, the average cost per patient was £2,227 for cholecystectomy, compared with only £676 for conservative management, a cost difference of more than three times [9]. Moreover, surgery may be unsuitable for elderly individuals or patients who have multiple comorbidities with high surgical risk. This prompts the consideration of less invasive approaches, such as supportive care, pain relief medications, and/or antibiotics [10].

Conservative management, including supportive therapy, analgesia, antispasmodics, and antibiotics, is an alternative to surgery for patients with mild symptoms [9] and those with significant comorbidities conferring high operative risk [10]. Studies have demonstrated that conservative management during initial admission is successful in approximately 87% of cases, with a mortality rate as low as 0.5% and recurrence rates of gallstone-related complications of approximately 20% during long-term follow-up [11]. Conservative management is less expensive than LC. The C-GALL trial revealed that conservative treatment was approximately £1,033 less costly than LC over 24 months; however, no significant difference was found in quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs). Although LC provides some quality-of-life benefits, the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) for LC is high, indicating that it may not be cost-effective [9, 12]. However, conservative management has limitations, including symptom recurrence, unplanned hospital stays, and worsening illness [10].

The previous systematic review and meta-analysis by Brazzelli et al. [13] comparing cholecystectomy versus conservative management for uncomplicated symptomatic gallstones or cholecystitis included only two studies, with a total sample size of 201. Additional randomized controlled trials (RCTs), most notably the large multicenter C-GALL trial [9], have been published, providing new evidence that may influence clinical decision-making. Despite the findings of the previous systematic review by Brazzelli et al. [13], it is still uncertain how best to manage patients with uncomplicated gallstones. Prior studies had limitations, such as small sample sizes [13], wide patient populations that included complicated gallstone disease, or a focus on the timing of cholecystectomy rather than treatment choice [14]. The present updated systematic review and meta-analysis addresses these gaps by strictly including RCTs of adults with symptomatic uncomplicated gallstones (biliary colic) or mild acute cholecystitis (Tokyo grade I: no organ failure, no severe local complications) [15]. We excluded RCTs with complicated cases like CBD stones, empyema, or perforation.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare the safety and efficacy of cholecystectomy versus conservative management in adults with symptomatic uncomplicated gallstones or mild acute cholecystitis. The primary outcomes were biliary colic and overall gallstone-related complications. Secondary outcomes included mortality and surgery-related complications.

Methods

Design and data sources

We adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and the Cochrane Handbook of Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses of Interventions [16, 17]. The PROSPERO database has the study protocol recorded with ID CRD420250651103.

Study selection

All RCTs published in English and involving adult patients diagnosed with uncomplicated gallstones or mild acute cholecystitis (Tokyo grade I: no organ failure, no severe local complications) [15] were included. We excluded studies comparing early versus delayed cholecystectomy, studies in which all participants eventually underwent surgery, and studies enrolling patients with complicated gallstone disease (including gallstone pancreatitis, cholangitis, or common bile duct stones). Observational studies, case reports, reviews, and nonrandomized studies were also excluded. The interventional group included patients who underwent laparoscopic or open cholecystectomy. The comparator group included patients who had conservative management, which provided for observation, also known as a wait-and-watch strategy, using painkillers, antibiotics, diet, or lifestyle changes. The primary outcomes were biliary colic and gallstone-related complications. Secondary outcomes included mortality and surgery-related complications. Given the absence of universally accepted definitions for composite outcomes in this field, outcome definitions were recorded as reported by each trial and are described below. “Overall gallstone-related complications” was defined as any gallstone-attributable clinical event requiring medical or surgical intervention, encompassing acute cholecystitis, acute biliary pancreatitis, common bile duct stones, cholangitis, empyema, or gallbladder perforation, consistent with the definitions used in the C-GALL trial [9] and Schmidt et al. 2011 [10]. In the Kivivuori et al. trial [18], this composite included any biliary complication documented during follow-up, and the specific component events were extracted where reported. “Surgery-related complications” (also referred to as surgical complications) were defined as procedure-specific adverse events occurring within 30 days of cholecystectomy, including bile duct injury, wound infection, intra-abdominal hemorrhage, intra-abdominal leakage, port-site hernia, and conversion to open surgery, as reported by each individual trial. Conservative management arm patients who did not undergo surgery were assigned zero surgical complications for this outcome. Where component definitions differed between trials, this variability is acknowledged as a potential source of clinical heterogeneity, and findings for these composite outcomes should be interpreted with corresponding caution.

Literature search strategy

A systematic literature search was performed across electronic databases, namely, PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), via a predefined strategy outlined in the PROSPERO-registered protocol. The search was conducted on 14 March 2025, with no date restrictions applied to the search results. No language, geographic, or publication-status filters were imposed. The search strategy was developed iteratively by the review team and validated against the PROSPERO-registered protocol (ID: CRD420250651103). The following Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords were used, adapted as appropriate for each database’s syntax:

Additionally, the reference lists of all included studies and relevant systematic reviews were manually screened to identify any eligible RCTs not captured by the database searches. Grey literature, including conference abstracts and trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform), was also reviewed where feasible. Duplicate records were removed using the Rayyan platform prior to screening. All authors independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text evaluation within the same platform. Any disagreements were resolved through consultation with the whole team.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data were independently extracted by two reviewers using a standardized Google spreadsheet. The extracted information included the following: (1) a summary of study characteristics, including design, site, sample size, population, intervention, comparator, and key findings; (2) baseline population characteristics, including the number of patients, age, sex, body mass index, comorbidities, previous gallstone attack, and previous hospitalization for gallstone disease, were considered; (3) risk of bias assessment; and (4) the study outcomes.

We used the Cochrane risk of bias 2.0 (RoB-2) tool to assess the quality of RCTs. It evaluates bias in five areas: the randomization process, deviations from the intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results. Each domain was assessed using domain-specific signal questions with response options (yes, likely yes, likely no, no, or no information), resulting in an overall risk rating (low risk, some concern, or high risk). To ensure accurate results, two reviewers independently conducted initial assessments; discrepancies were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer to minimize individual bias and ensure maximum reliability of the assessment process. The grading of recommendations assessment, development, and evaluation (GRADE) method was used to evaluate the degree of certainty in the evidence for each primary and secondary outcome.

Data analysis

Review Manager (RevMan 5.4 for Windows) was used to conduct all analyses. Dichotomous outcomes (e.g., biliary colic, gallstone complications, mortality) were pooled using risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Continuous outcomes, if any, would have been analyzed using mean differences or standardized mean differences with 95% CIs. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Both fixed-effect and random-effects models were applied. The fixed-effect model was used initially, and the random-effects model was used when heterogeneity was present. Heterogeneity among the included studies was evaluated by visual inspection of forest plots and quantified using the chi-square and I-squared (I²) statistics. The chi-square test assessed the presence of statistically significant heterogeneity, whereas the I² statistic measured the extent of variability in effect sizes attributable to heterogeneity rather than chance. An I² value of 50% or higher or a chi-square P-value below 0.1 was considered indicative of substantial heterogeneity. To improve clinical interpretability, absolute risk differences (ARDs) with 95% CIs and the number needed to treat (NNT) or number needed to harm (NNH) were calculated for statistically significant outcomes. ARD was derived as the product of the pooled control event rate (CER) and (1 − RR) for outcomes favoring cholecystectomy, or CER × (RR − 1) for outcomes favoring conservative management. The CER for each outcome was estimated from the pooled conservative management arm event data across included trials. NNT and NNH were computed as the reciprocal of the ARD. For non-significant outcomes (where 95% CIs for RR crossed 1.0), ARD and NNT/NNH were not calculated owing to statistical imprecision. Planned sensitivity analyses included exclusion of studies with a high or unclear risk of bias, and exclusion of studies with follow-up durations substantially shorter or longer than the median. Given that the included trials enrolled broadly similar adult populations with uncomplicated symptomatic gallstones or mild acute cholecystitis, a formal sensitivity analysis excluding studies with distinctly different population characteristics (e.g., exclusively elderly cohorts) was not performed, as no single included trial enrolled an exclusively elderly population as a distinct stratum. This limitation is discussed further in the limitations section [19–21].

Subgroup analysis

A pre-specified subgroup analysis was conducted by clinical diagnosis, stratifying trials into two groups: (1) uncomplicated symptomatic gallstones (C-GALL trial and Schmidt et al. A; n = 503) [9, 10] and (2) mild acute cholecystitis (Schmidt et al. B and Kivivuori et al.; n = 174) [18, 22]. This stratification reflects the two clinically distinct presentations enrolled across the included trials, which differ in acuity, baseline risk, and expected treatment response. Subgroup-level pooled RRs were calculated using the Mantel-Haenszel fixed-effect method, and interaction between subgroups was assessed using the chi-square test for subgroup differences, with P < 0.10 considered indicative of significant interaction. A subgroup analysis by age group (e.g., younger vs. elderly patients) was planned but could not be performed owing to the absence of age-stratified outcome data in the included trial reports.

Results

Results of the literature search

Our search retrieved 2,568 articles, of which 1,713 were identified as duplicates. The titles and abstracts of the remaining 855 records were screened, resulting in 13 records considered suitable for full-text review. Finally, four RCTs were found to be eligible for the final analysis. The PRISMA flow diagram is presented in Fig. 1.

Characteristics of the included studies

The included studies were four RCTs. The total sample size was 677 patients, with 340 in the intervention group and 337 in the control group. All four clinical trials included cholecystectomy treatment in the intervention group and conservative management in the control group. Although two included studies were conducted by the same research group Schmidt et al. [10, 22], they represented distinct RCT populations with different clinical conditions and separate recruitment cohorts. Schmidt et al. 2011 A [10] included patients with uncomplicated symptomatic gallstone disease, whereas Schmidt et al. 2011 B [22] enrolled patients with acute cholecystitis. A summary of the studies’ characteristics is shown in Table 1 (see supplementary file 1).

Baseline comparability between treatment groups was formally assessed across the included RCTs. Overall, baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were generally well balanced between intervention and conservative management groups, supporting the validity of pooled estimates. In the C-GALL trial by Ahmed et al. 2023 [9], randomization with minimization achieved highly comparable groups with nearly identical age, sex distribution, BMI, and comorbidity burden. Similarly, the randomization in the study by Kivivuori et al. [18] demonstrated no statistically significant baseline differences, although minor numerical imbalances were observed because of the small sample size. The studies by Schmidt et al. [10] used randomized allocation, and no major baseline disparities were reported in the original publications. Importantly, no included trial demonstrated clinically meaningful imbalances likely to substantially bias treatment effect estimates. Furthermore, statistical heterogeneity across pooled analyses was consistently low (I² generally 0–48%), suggesting that baseline differences were unlikely to materially influence the overall findings. The baseline characteristics of the included study population are shown in Table 2 (see supplementary file 2).

Quality of the included studies

Quality assessment was performed via the ROB-2 tool. The results of the quality assessment were judged to be high methodological quality. All included studies had low risk across all assessed domains, so the overall risk of bias was low in all studies. A summary of the risk of bias assessment is shown in Fig. 2.

Outcomes

Biliary colic

Three studies reported data regarding biliary colic. Biliary colic was significantly lower in patients in the cholecystectomy group than in those in the conservative management group (pooled RR = 0.43, 95% CI [0.24 to 0.78], P = 0.006). No substantial heterogeneity was observed among the pooled studies (chi-square = 3.50, P = 0.17, I² = 43%), so the fixed-effect model was used, as shown in Fig. 3. Subgroup analysis by clinical presentation revealed a significant difference in treatment effect between the two treatment groups (P = 0.07). In patients with uncomplicated symptomatic gallstones, cholecystectomy significantly reduced biliary colic compared with conservative management (P = 0.001); however, among patients with mild acute cholecystitis, no significant difference in biliary colic was observed (P = 0.63).

Overall gallstone complications

Four studies reported data on overall gallstone-related complications. The incidence of overall gallstone complications was significantly lower in patients who underwent cholecystectomy compared with those managed conservatively (pooled RR = 0.40, 95% CI [0.23 to 0.69], P = 0.0009). No substantial heterogeneity was detected between the studies (I² = 0%), so the fixed-effect model was used. Subgroup analysis by clinical presentation revealed a non-significant difference in treatment effect between the two treatment groups (P = 0.69) as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2 (see supplementary file 4). The estimated ARD was 21.0% (95% CI: 10.9%–26.9%), yielding an NNT of 5 (95% CI: 4–10), meaning approximately 5 patients would need to be treated with cholecystectomy rather than conservative management to prevent one additional gallstone-related complication.

Patients who underwent surgery

Four studies reported data on patients who underwent surgery. The proportion of patients requiring surgery was significantly lower in the conservative management group compared with those initially treated with cholecystectomy (pooled RR = 2.24, 95% CI [1.76 to 2.87], P < 0.00001). No substantial heterogeneity was detected between the studies (I² = 48%), so the fixed-effect model was used. Subgroup analysis by clinical presentation revealed a non-significant difference in treatment effect between the two treatment groups (P = 0.16) as shown in Supplementary Fig. 7 (see supplementary file 4). The estimated ARD was 37.2% (95% CI: 22.8%–56.1%), yielding an NNH of 3 (95% CI: 2–5). This reflects that, by definition, virtually all patients in the cholecystectomy group underwent surgery, whereas approximately 30% of conservatively managed patients required delayed surgical intervention during follow-up; for every 3 patients managed with initial cholecystectomy rather than conservative management, one additional patient would have surgery who would otherwise have avoided it.

Mortality

Four studies comparing cholecystectomy with conservative management for mortality demonstrated no statistically significant difference between the two treatment strategies (pooled RR = 1.39, 95% CI [0.79 to 2.46], P = 0.25). No substantial heterogeneity was detected between the studies (I² = 0%), so the fixed-effect model was used. Subgroup analysis by clinical presentation revealed a non-significant difference in treatment effect between the two treatment groups (P = 0.87) as shown in Supplementary Fig. 8 (see Supplementary File 4).

GRADE assessment of the certainty of evidence for outcomes

A summary of the GRADE assessment of the certainty of evidence for outcomes is shown in Table 3 (see supplementary file 3). Overall, the certainty of evidence ranged from very low to moderate across study outcomes. Moderate-certainty evidence supported the outcome of patients who underwent surgery. Low-certainty evidence was observed for biliary colic, acute cholecystitis, acute pancreatitis, common bile duct stones, overall gallstone-related complications, bile duct injury, intra-abdominal leakage, wound infection, and overall surgery-related complications, whereas mortality was supported by very low-certainty evidence.

The certainty of evidence was downgraded mainly because of the limited number of included studies, clinical heterogeneity across study populations and outcome definitions, subgroup differences observed for biliary colic, and substantial imprecision related to sparse events and wide confidence intervals. In particular, overall gallstone-related complications and overall surgery-related complications were downgraded because of clinical heterogeneity arising from differences in outcome definitions and included patient populations. Mortality was additionally downgraded because of variation in follow-up duration across studies. Rare adverse outcomes, including bile duct injury and wound infection, demonstrated highly imprecise estimates with confidence intervals encompassing both potentially important benefit and harm, indicating that these analyses were underpowered to exclude clinically meaningful differences between treatment strategies.

Discussion

We compared cholecystectomy with conservative management for patients with uncomplicated symptomatic gallstones and mild acute cholecystitis in this updated meta-analysis of four RCTs (n = 677). The incidence rates of acute cholecystitis, acute pancreatitis, CBD stones, wound infection, intra-abdominal leakage, bile duct injury, and the mortality rate did not differ between the two groups. No statistically significant differences were detected for mortality, acute pancreatitis, bile duct injury, wound infection, or other rare adverse outcomes. However, these analyses were limited by low event rates, wide confidence intervals, and limited statistical power, preventing firm conclusions regarding equivalence or comparative safety between treatment strategies. Cholecystectomy was associated with a statistically significant reduction in the incidence of biliary colic and total gallstone complications. In contrast, the number of patients who underwent surgery and the incidence of surgery-related complications were significantly lower in the conservative management group, reflecting lower exposure to operative intervention during follow-up.

Among the evaluated outcomes, biliary colic was the only outcome demonstrating potential subgroup differences regarding clinical presentation. Specifically, the reduction in biliary colic with cholecystectomy was primarily observed in patients with uncomplicated symptomatic gallstones, whereas evidence in mild acute cholecystitis remained inconclusive because only one small study contributed data. For the remaining outcomes, subgroup analyses did not demonstrate meaningful differences between clinical populations. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited number of included studies and sparse event rates.

When interpreting these findings, it is important to distinguish between statistical significance and clinical significance. The statistically significant reduction in biliary colic with cholecystectomy (RR = 0.43; ARD ≈ 22.8%; NNT ≈ 5) represents a clinically meaningful absolute benefit: one episode of biliary colic prevented for every five patients treated surgically rather than conservatively. Similarly, the reduction in overall gallstone complications (RR = 0.40; ARD ≈ 21.0%; NNT ≈ 5) is both statistically robust and clinically substantial. By contrast, the statistically significant increase in surgical complications with cholecystectomy (RR = 2.56; ARD ≈ 12.5%; NNH ≈ 8) is clinically relevant and should not be dismissed as a statistical artefact, as it reflects real procedure-attributable harm affecting approximately one in eight operated patients. For outcomes where no statistically significant difference was detected, including acute cholecystitis (RR = 0.47; 95% CI 0.21–1.02), acute pancreatitis (RR = 0.98), CBD stones (RR = 0.32; 95% CI 0.09–1.18), bile duct injury, intra-abdominal leakage, wound infection, and mortality, the wide confidence intervals in several instances preclude confident conclusions of equivalence. The non-significant results for bile duct injury (RR = 1.04; 95% CI 0.11–9.87) and wound infection (RR = 1.61; 95% CI 0.20–12.95) in particular reflect severe statistical imprecision owing to very low event rates; these outcomes are underpowered rather than truly equivalent, and should not be interpreted as confirming no difference. Accordingly, conclusions drawn from these non-significant outcomes should be tempered to reflect this imprecision, and larger trials with pre-specified event thresholds are needed before inferring clinical equivalence.

Regarding population homogeneity and the robustness of pooled findings, a formal leave-one-out sensitivity analysis excluding studies with distinct population characteristics was not pre-specified and could not be meaningfully conducted given that only four trials were included. Among the included studies, the Schmidt et al. trials (n = 135 combined) enrolled smaller, single-centre European cohorts with shorter median follow-up relative to the C-GALL trial (n = 434) [9], which enrolled a broad adult population across multiple UK centres. The Kivivuori et al. trial (n = 108) [18] enrolled patients with acute cholecystitis specifically. The predominant weight of the pooled estimates therefore derives from the C-GALL trial, and readers should be aware that findings — particularly for biliary colic and overall gallstone complications — are substantially driven by this single large trial. Exclusion of the smaller Schmidt or Kivivuori trials from the pool would not substantially alter point estimates given their limited sample sizes, but this cannot substitute for formal sensitivity analyses in adequately powered future meta-analyses.

The results of this analysis are in line with findings from well-designed studies in the literature. A large multicenter RCT, the C-GALL trial, reported no significant differences between the two treatment strategies regarding severe biliary complications or mortality. They also reported that conservative management is a cost-effective alternative to surgery, as a significant portion of patients in the conservative arms avoided surgery during the follow-up periods [9].

The previous systematic review and meta-analysis by Brazzelli et al., which compared cholecystectomy versus conservative management for uncomplicated symptomatic gallstones or cholecystitis, included only two studies, with a total sample size of 201 [23]. Consistent with our findings, they reported no statistically significant difference in the incidence of acute pancreatitis, CBD stones, wound infection, intra-abdominal leakage, bile duct damage, or mortality rate between the two groups. They also reported that cholecystectomy was associated with a statistically significant reduction in the incidence of total gallstone complications, in agreement with our results. In both studies, patients underwent surgery, and the incidence of overall surgical complications was significantly lower in the conservative management group because fewer individuals underwent operative intervention. Although Brazzelli et al. reported no statistically significant differences between the two groups regarding biliary colic, we found that cholecystectomy was associated with a statistically significant reduction in biliary pain attacks. Additionally, Brazzelli et al. reported that participants randomized to conservative management were more likely to develop acute cholecystitis, whereas we found no such difference. These discrepancies may be attributed to the inclusion of recently published RCTs and the substantially larger pooled sample size in our analysis, which improved statistical power and precision of effect estimates.

Bagepally et al. also conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis comparing early cholecystectomy with conservative management/delayed cholecystectomy for gallstone diseases in terms of safety and effectiveness [14]. According to both studies, there was no statistically significant difference in mortality rates between the two treatment strategies. Additionally, they agreed that cholecystectomy significantly reduces gallstone-related complications, whereas conservative management reduces surgery-related complications as fewer individuals are exposed to surgical intervention. Despite these similarities, important differences between the two studies were observed. In our analysis, there was no significant difference regarding acute cholecystitis, pancreatitis, or CBD stones. In contrast, they reported that early cholecystectomy reduced cholangitis and had a very high adjusted number needed to harm pancreatitis and CBD stones. These discrepancies are likely attributable to differences in the study populations. We focused on uncomplicated gallstone diseases and mild acute cholecystitis. We excluded complicated gallstone diseases such as CBD stones and gallstone pancreatitis, unlike Bagepally et al., who included complicated gallstone diseases that may benefit more from early gallbladder removal.

Another recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Ghose et al. evaluates the safety and effectiveness of conservative management versus early cholecystectomy for the treatment of gallstone disease [18]. Our findings are consistent with theirs in showing no statistically significant difference between the two strategies in mortality or the incidence of biliary pancreatitis. Both meta-analyses also reported that cholecystectomy was associated with a significantly lower incidence of biliary colic and overall biliary complications. However, discrepancies were observed regarding surgical complications and CBD stones. Ghose et al. found no statistically significant difference between the two groups in total surgical complications, whereas we found that conservative management significantly reduced these surgical risks. Additionally, Ghose et al. reported that cholecystectomy was associated with a substantially lower incidence of CBD stones, whereas we found no statistical difference between the treatment groups. These differences may be explained by variations in study populations and definitions of conservative management. Ghose et al. provided a broad overview of gallstone disease, including 11 RCTs that enrolled patients with complicated gallstone disease and CBD stones. They also included studies with minimally invasive interventions such as endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography and endoscopic sphincterotomy within the conservative arm. In contrast, our study focused on symptomatic uncomplicated gallstones or mild acute calculous cholecystitis, while explicitly excluding studies of complicated gallstone disease (e.g., gallstone pancreatitis, cholangitis, or common bile duct stones). Additionally, we defined conservative management strictly as supportive therapy and medication, including observation, analgesics, and lifestyle changes.

LC is the treatment of choice for gallstone diseases [4]. However, our results suggest that both cholecystectomy and conservative management have advantages and limitations, and the management plan should be individualized according to patient factors and condition. The lack of significant differences between the two treatment strategies in terms of serious biliary complications (e.g., acute cholecystitis, acute pancreatitis, CBD stones, bile duct injury) and mortality rate makes conservative management an initial safe option. Although our results showed that cholecystectomy significantly reduced biliary colic and total gallstone complications, it offered no clear advantage in the other major clinical outcomes. Although Ahmed et al. [9]. reported that general pain scores were similar between the two treatment strategies, the condition-specific questionnaire and persistent symptoms scores significantly favored the cholecystectomy group. Additionally, they found that conservative management was considerably less costly, with only a marginal, non-significant loss in QALYs, making it a highly cost-effective alternative to surgery [9]. From a cost-effectiveness perspective, the C-GALL trial demonstrated that conservative management costs approximately £1,033 less per patient than cholecystectomy over 24 months, with no significant difference in QALYs and a high ICER for surgery. This suggests that the added cost of cholecystectomy yields only marginal quality-of-life gains. These findings are particularly relevant in resource-constrained healthcare settings where value-based decision-making is essential. Although LC provided somewhat better symptom control, its incremental cost-effectiveness ratio was relatively high, suggesting that routine surgery for all patients with uncomplicated gallstone disease may not represent the most cost-effective strategy. Accordingly, conservative management represents a reasonable first-line approach for patients with mild uncomplicated gallstone disease, particularly those who prefer to avoid surgery or carry a high operative-related risk [9, 10]. Furthermore, minimizing unwarranted surgical procedures may improve cost-effectiveness, especially in resource-constrained healthcare systems such as the UK National Health Service [9, 24]. Conservative management may be more appropriate for elderly patients or those with significant comorbidities associated with high surgical risk. It may also be suitable for patients with infrequent or mild symptoms who prefer to avoid surgery. In addition, conservative management may be favored when the expected quality-of-life benefit of surgery does not outweigh the procedural risk.

However, LC may provide greater benefit for patients with recurrent or frequent biliary colic. It may also be preferable for patients with repeated hospital admissions, younger individuals with long life expectancy and low operative risk, and those with progressive or complicated disease.

Regarding long-term outcomes in conservatively managed patients, the available evidence warrants a more nuanced appraisal. The Schmidt et al. 14-year follow-up study [10] demonstrated that approximately 57% of patients initially randomized to conservative management eventually required surgery, with the majority of crossovers occurring within the first two years due to symptom recurrence or disease progression. Similarly, in the C-GALL trial [9], around 30% of patients in the conservative arm underwent delayed cholecystectomy within 24 months of follow-up, predominantly driven by persistent or recurrent biliary colic. These findings underscore that while conservative management successfully avoids surgery in a substantial proportion of patients, a meaningful minority will ultimately require delayed surgical intervention, often under less elective circumstances. Furthermore, recurrence rates of gallstone-related symptoms or complications in conservatively managed patients have been estimated at approximately 20% over the long term, as previously noted in the background section. This risk of symptom recurrence and the potential need for unplanned or urgent surgery should be transparently communicated to patients when shared decision-making is undertaken. Future RCTs should prospectively capture delayed surgery rates, time-to-surgery, and patient-reported quality of life over extended follow-up periods to better characterize the long-term trajectory of conservatively managed patients.

Clinical decision algorithm

Based on the findings of this meta-analysis, we propose a decision-making algorithm for patients with uncomplicated symptomatic gallstones or mild acute cholecystitis Fig. 6. The algorithm primarily stratifies patients according to surgical risk and symptom burden. Patients with high surgical risk due to advanced age or significant comorbidities, as well as those with infrequent or mild biliary colic without disease progression, may be managed conservatively. Conservative management includes analgesia, antibiotics when indicated, lifestyle modification, and structured follow-up. In contrast, patients with low-to-moderate surgical risk who experience recurrent biliary colic, frequent symptoms, progressive disease, or who prefer definitive treatment should be considered for laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Patients managed conservatively should undergo reassessment after 6–12 months. Escalation to cholecystectomy is recommended if symptoms recur or complications develop.

This meta-analysis has several strengths. It includes only RCTs, the gold standard of clinical evidence, enhancing the validity of the meta-analysis and conclusion. Additionally, the analysis focused only on uncomplicated gallstone disease and mild cholecystitis, excluding complicated gallstone diseases that may affect the analysis and results. All studies included had a low risk of bias, and no heterogeneity was detected among the studies. An important strength of this study is the application of the GRADE framework, which allows for a structured assessment of the certainty of evidence across outcomes and supports more informed clinical decision-making.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be considered. Only four RCTs were eligible for inclusion, with a total sample size of 677, which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. The modest sample size restricts subgroup analyses by age, comorbidity burden, or symptom severity, and reduces confidence in effect estimates. Although the available evidence for some outcomes, mainly the major complications, did not demonstrate statistically significant differences, the associated event rates were low and the confidence intervals wide, increasing the possibility of imprecision and leaving the evidence insufficient to exclude clinically meaningful differences. Larger, adequately powered trials are needed to confirm these findings. Additionally, there were no standardized definitions for some outcomes, such as overall gallstones and surgery-related complications, which may introduce clinical heterogeneity not detected by statistical measures. Specifically, the definition of “overall gallstone-related complications” varied across trials: the C-GALL trial included any biliary complication requiring hospital attendance or intervention, whereas the Schmidt et al. trials used a broader composite that incorporated symptom recurrence leading to hospitalisation. Similarly, “surgery-related complications” were variably defined, with some trials capturing only major procedural adverse events and others including minor wound-related sequelae. This definitional heterogeneity limits the comparability of these composite outcomes across studies and may have introduced unmeasured clinical heterogeneity, even where statistical heterogeneity (I²) appeared low. Future trials should adopt pre-specified, standardised outcome definitions aligned with established reporting frameworks such as the Clavien-Dindo classification for surgical complications.

Furthermore, the small number of included studies (n = 4) precluded a formal sensitivity analysis excluding trials with distinct population characteristics, such as exclusively elderly or high-comorbidity cohorts. While no included trial enrolled an exclusively elderly population as a defined stratum, the Kivivuori et al. studies enrolled older, predominantly symptomatic community-based patients with a longer disease history than those in the C-GALL and Schmidt et al. trials, and these population differences may influence generalisability. The robustness of pooled findings to the exclusion of individual trials could not be rigorously evaluated, and this represents a material limitation that future, adequately powered meta-analyses should address. Importantly, the inconsistent reporting of delayed surgery rates and long-term symptom recurrence data across included trials limited our ability to draw definitive conclusions about the proportion of conservatively managed patients who ultimately require surgery. Standardized reporting of these outcomes in future RCTs is essential to fully characterize the long-term burden of conservative management.

Another limitation is the presence of clinical heterogeneity among the included trials despite generally low statistical heterogeneity. The studies enrolled patients varied in patient age, comorbidity burden, conservative management protocols, and follow-up duration. we included only RCTs with patients with mild acute cholecystitis (Tokyo Guidelines grade I: gallstones + pain/RUQ tenderness, no organ dysfunction or severe local signs) [15], which is classified as non-complicated. However, clinical heterogeneity regarding different clinical presentation should be acknowledged. Although subgroup analyses were performed, biliary colic was the only outcome demonstrating potential subgroup differences according to disease presentation. Subgroup analyses for other outcomes were limited by the small number of studies and sparse event rates. Additionally, age-stratified analyses (e.g., elderly versus general populations) were not feasible because such data were inconsistently reported across trials. Follow-up duration also varied between studies, which may affect long-term outcomes and recurrence. Importantly, the inconsistent reporting of delayed surgery rates and long-term symptom recurrence data across included trials limited our ability to draw definitive conclusions about the proportion of conservatively managed patients who ultimately require surgery. Standardized reporting of these outcomes in future RCTs is essential to fully characterize the long-term burden of conservative management. Additionally, the lack of consistently reported patient-centered outcomes, including quality-of-life measures, symptom severity scores, and patient satisfaction, limited our ability to comprehensively compare the long-term functional impact of both treatment approaches. Future RCTs should incorporate validated patient-reported outcome measures as pre-specified endpoints.

Conclusion

In patients with uncomplicated gallstones and mild cholecystitis, cholecystectomy is more effective at reducing biliary colic and total gallstone complications. However, the number of patients undergoing surgery and the rate of surgery-related complications were significantly lower with conservative management. Major complications (e.g., acute cholecystitis, acute pancreatitis, CBD stones, bile duct injury, and mortality) were similar between the two groups. Treatment selection should be guided by symptom frequency, surgical risk, patient preference, and available healthcare resources. Conservative management may be considered in selected patients because of its lower surgical complication rate and comparable major clinical outcomes. More studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm these findings. These conclusions are, however, based on only four RCTs totalling 677 patients, and should be interpreted with corresponding caution. Several key outcomes — including bile duct injury, wound infection, intra-abdominal leakage, acute pancreatitis, and CBD stones — were associated with wide confidence intervals and very low event rates, and absence of statistical significance in these analyses should not be equated with confirmed equivalence. Minor baseline imbalances across trials, variable outcome definitions, and differing follow-up durations introduce additional uncertainty. The clinical benefit of cholecystectomy in reducing biliary symptoms is meaningful in absolute terms (NNT ≈ 5 for biliary colic), but must be weighed against a procedural complication risk (NNH ≈ 8), and against the finding that approximately 40–57% of conservatively managed patients avoid surgery altogether over the medium to long term. Adequately powered multicentre RCTs with standardised outcome definitions, pre-specified subgroup analyses by age and comorbidity burden, and extended follow-up are needed before definitive practice recommendations can be made.
