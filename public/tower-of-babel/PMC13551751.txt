Perceptions and implementation challenges of venous thromboembolism prevention among neurosurgery operating room nurses: a descriptive qualitative study
Authors: Huan Wang, Ran Zhang, Xiaonan Liu, Zhen Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVenous Thromboembolism (VTE) prevention is essential for patient safety during neurosurgery, with operating room nurses playing a key role in its implementation. However, a discrepancy exists between nurses’ recognition of the importance of VTE and their practical ability to implement preventive measures effectively. This study examined neurosurgical operating room nurses’ perceptions, preventive practices, and implementation challenges related to intraoperative VTE prevention.MethodsSemi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted in two tertiary general hospitals in China between June and September 2025. Data were analyzed using a descriptive qualitative approach with content analysis guided by the Graneheim and Lundman framework, including repeated reading of transcripts, initial coding, categorization, theme development, and validation.ResultsA total of 20 neurosurgical operating room nurses who met the eligibility criteria participated in the study. The analysis identified four main themes: (1) a positive attitude toward VTE prevention; (2) limited understanding of VTE; (3) deficient practical competency in VTE prevention; and (4) multiple challenges in implementing VTE preventive care. While these findings reveal a consistent willingness among nurses to engage in prevention, they also highlight significant knowledge, practical ability, and systemic support gaps that hinder effective implementation. This highlights the need for structured education and competency training, as well as optimized clinical protocols, to translate positive attitudes into reliable practice and enhance patient safety in neurosurgical settings.ConclusionsIn this study, neurosurgical operating room nurses exhibited limited VTE expertise and faced challenges in preventive care. The findings underscore the need for enhanced training, stronger accountability, the development of a specialty-specific intraoperative risk assessment tool, and improved interdisciplinary collaboration to effectively reduce the incidence of VTE.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04924-1.

Introduction

Venous Thromboembolism (VTE), including deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, represents a major global public health challenge. Epidemiological data indicate that VTE affects approximately 10 million people worldwide each year, ranking as the third most common cardiovascular disease globally [1]. It is recognized as a leading cause of preventable hospital death and imposes a substantial economic burden on global healthcare systems due to prolonged treatments and readmissions [2]. In the perioperative context, VTE is a common and potentially serious perioperative complication associated with increased morbidity, mortality, prolonged hospitalization, and greater nursing workload [3–6]. In neurosurgical patients, the risk of VTE is particularly high because of disease-related hypercoagulability, prolonged anesthesia, special surgical positioning, restricted mobility, and possible neurological dysfunction [7–8]. Therefore, effective VTE prevention is essential for improving patient safety and postoperative recovery in neurosurgical settings.

Moreover, VTE often occurs within 6 months after tumor diagnosis and surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, with a cumulative incidence rate of up to 45.3% within 1 month after surgery [9–11]. The occurrence of VTE not only may aggravate neurological function damage and delay the rehabilitation process but also increase the risk of readmission, having a profound impact on the prognosis of patients. Therefore, VTE prevention is of great significance in reducing the burden on nursing and improving the quality of life of patients.

Neurosurgical operating room nurses play a crucial role in the clinical practice of VTE prevention. Currently, providing systematic and clear suggestions for VTE prevention during the perioperative period of patients, such as through the implementation of standardized risk assessment, mechanical preventive measures, and health guidance, can significantly reduce the risk of VTE occurrence and has an irreplaceable role in improving the surgical safety and postoperative rehabilitation quality of patients [12]. However, despite the existence of a large number of evidence-based guidelines recommending standardized prevention strategies, some measures have not been effectively translated into clinical practice, resulting in low implementation rates and deviations from guideline recommendations [13]. Additionally, deficiencies exist in the clinical translation of dynamic risk assessment. In practice, due to nurses’ heavy workload and the complexity of assessment tools, approximately 60% of patients do not complete dynamic assessment in time, resulting in delayed adjustment of preventive measures [14]. These factors collectively contribute to the unsatisfactory status of VTE prevention in neurosurgical patients during surgery, highlighting the significant gap between guidelines and practice. However, it remains unclear how neurosurgical operating room nurses perceive intraoperative VTE prevention, how they apply preventive measures in this specific surgical context, and what practical or organizational barriers they encounter during implementation. Therefore, the current practices and perceptions of neurosurgical operating room nurses when carrying out VTE prevention nursing practices must be explored in depth, including the cognitive and behavioural challenges they face and the situational factors that influence their decisions. Through a rigorous qualitative analysis of nurses’ perceptions, preventive practices, and implementation challenges, this study can provide key insights into the insufficient clinical translation of preventive measures, low implementation rates, and poor guideline compliance and can lay the foundation for formulating targeted training strategies and nursing management pathways [15].

Based on the above clinical predicament, this study adopted a descriptive qualitative design to explore the neurosurgical operating room nurses’ perceptions and implementation challenges related to intraoperative VTE prevention. The findings may provide empirical evidence for improving the quality of VTE preventive nursing in neurosurgery and promoting the effective translation of evidence-based practice into clinical care [16].

Methods

Study design

A descriptive qualitative study utilizing content analysis, guided by the framework of Graneheim and Lundman [17], was conducted in two tertiary hospitals in China between June and September 2025.

Participants

Participants were recruited through purposive sampling with the assistance of nursing managers in the two participating hospitals. The managers helped identify nurses who met the inclusion criteria, and the research team then approached eligible nurses individually to explain the study purpose, procedures, voluntary nature of participation, and confidentiality measures. Nurses were given sufficient time to consider participation before providing written informed consent. Participation was entirely voluntary, and refusal to participate had no influence on their work or professional evaluation. A total of 20 eligible nurses were invited and all agreed to participate. No participants withdrew from the study after enrollment. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) possession of a valid registered nurse license; (2) at least one year of experience in supporting neurosurgical procedures; (3) informed consent and voluntary participation. Nurses who were on an internship or on sick/personal leave were excluded.

Research instruments

Self-designed demographic questionnaire

Participants’ demographic characteristics, including age, gender, marital status, educational attainment, professional title, and years of work experience, were collected using a questionnaire developed for this study (Supplementary File S1).

Semi-structured interview outline

The interview guide was developed through an iterative process based on the study objectives. After a literature review and team discussions, a preliminary guide was created. This draft was piloted with two operating room nurses and subsequently refined based on their feedback. Finally, the guide was reviewed and finalized by two qualitative research experts. The core interview questions addressed: (1) understanding of VTE and its sources; (2) current VTE prevention practices in the operating room; (3) perceptions and practical challenges in implementing these practices; (4) challenges encountered and corresponding coping strategies, with examples; (5) the personal or professional impact of engaging in VTE prevention; (6) perceived needs for support in this practice area. See Supplementary File S2 for details. Although the interview guide included several predefined questions to ensure that key topics were covered, it was used flexibly during the interviews rather than as a rigid questionnaire.

Research team

The study was conducted by a team with expertise in both qualitative methodology and clinical nursing. The core research team comprised two principal investigators with substantial experience in qualitative inquiry, who were responsible for the overall study design, conducting the interviews, and leading the data analysis.

The interviews were conducted by the principal researcher, who was female, had a nursing background, and had received formal training in qualitative research methods and interview techniques. She was familiar with the neurosurgical operating room context but had no prior personal, supervisory, or direct working relationship with the participants. Before data collection, participants were informed of the interviewer’s identity, professional background, and the purpose of the study.

The principal investigators were supported by two research assistants who had undergone systematic training in qualitative methods. The assistants’ roles included taking field notes, transcribing the interviews verbatim, and assisting with preliminary data management. All members received dedicated training on the specific methodological approach of this study. Throughout the research process, the team engaged in reflexive discussions to identify and minimize the potential influence of their clinical background, assumptions, and preconceptions on data collection and interpretation. This combination of methodological expertise, clinical nursing experience, and reflexive practice was essential to ensuring the rigor and trustworthiness of the research process.

Data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face in-depth interviews, with interview locations determined by participant preferences. Participants were informed of the study’s purpose, significance, and voluntary nature. Each interview was conducted by the principal researcher, while the assistant researcher took notes. During the interviews, the guide was used flexibly to facilitate discussion rather than to impose a fixed sequence of questions. The interviewer adjusted the order and wording of questions according to participants’ responses and used probing and follow-up questions to encourage participants to elaborate on their perceptions, practices, and challenges. Interviews lasted between 30 and 60 min, and with participants’ consent, were recorded and transcribed within 24 h. Data collection continued until saturation was reached, defined as no new significant information emerging after three consecutive interviews [18]. After the 17th interview, no substantially new codes, categories, or themes emerged. Three additional interviews were conducted to confirm saturation, and these interviews did not generate new significant information. Therefore, the final sample size was determined as 20 participants.

Data analysis

Following data collection, all audio recordings were transcribed verbatim within 24 h. The transcripts were managed and analyzed using NVivo 12.0 software. To ensure methodological transparency and rigor, the data were analyzed using qualitative content analysis guided by the established framework of Graneheim and Lundman [17]. Following this framework, the analytical process involved: (1) immersing in the data through repeated readings of the transcripts to obtain a sense of the whole; (2) identifying and extracting significant statements relevant to the research objective as meaning units; (3) condensing these meaning units and labeling them with initial codes; and (4) comparing, categorizing, and abstracting the codes to synthesize them into broader sub-themes and main themes based on their underlying meanings. These themes were then rigorously reviewed, refined, and clearly defined. To ensure trustworthiness, member checking was employed by inviting participants to review the thematic findings for accuracy. Furthermore, the coding framework and thematic structure were critically examined by three independent qualitative research experts to enhance the analytical rigor. During coding and team discussions, the research team kept analytical notes to record preliminary interpretations, coding decisions, disagreements among coders, and the rationale for refining categories and themes. These notes were reviewed during subsequent discussions and helped the researchers compare codes, clarify category boundaries, and refine the final thematic structure. Representative examples of field notes and analytical memos are provided in Supplementary File S3 to enhance transparency regarding the analytical process.

Rigor

The trustworthiness of this study was ensured by adhering to Lincoln and Guba’s framework [19]. Credibility was established through prolonged engagement with participants to build rapport, triangulation of analysis among four researchers, and member checking. Transferability was supported by providing thick descriptions of the research context, participant profiles, data collection procedures, and representative quotations. Dependability was achieved by maintaining a detailed audit trail that documented recruitment, interview procedures, coding records, analytical notes, team discussions, and decisions regarding category refinement and theme development. Confirmability was addressed through researcher reflexivity, regular team debriefings, and consultation with external experts to minimize the influence of preconceptions [20]. During reflexive discussions, the researchers examined how their clinical nursing background and assumptions about VTE prevention might influence interviewing, coding, and interpretation.

Ethical considerations

In this study, all procedures were conducted in accordance to the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and its further modifications. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University (KY2019-034-02). After providing written informed consent, participants’ data were processed with stringent measures: all information was anonymized, pseudonyms with numerical identifiers were used when presenting participant quotations, and audio/text materials were kept on secure, access-restricted devices until their mandated deletion post-study.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

A total of 20 clinical nurses participated, including 2 males and 18 females. The mean age was 35.9 years (SD = 6.3), with ages ranging between 26 and 48 years. Their work experience varied from 3 to 17 years, averaging 13.9 years (SD = 6.8). In terms of education, 85% held a bachelor’s degree, and 40% possessed an intermediate or higher professional title. Perceptions, preventive practices, and implementation challenges of venous thromboembolism prevention

The analysis ultimately converged on four main themes, developed from nine sub-themes (Fig. 1). To enhance the transparency and rigor of the inductive content analysis process, Supplementary File S4 presents the development of all themes and subthemes from representative meaning units, initial codes, and categories. While nurses’ attitudes toward VTE prevention were generally favorable, the findings revealed deficits in understanding and practical readiness, compounded by significant implementation barriers.

Thematic framework illustrating neurosurgical operating room nurses’ perceptions, competencies, and implementation challenges related to intraoperative VTE prevention

Theme 1: Positive attitude toward VTE prevention

This theme indicates that neurosurgical operating room nurses did not reject or underestimate VTE prevention at the attitudinal level. Instead, they generally recognized its importance for patient safety and were aware of the potential severity of VTE-related adverse events. However, their positive attitude was accompanied by an implicit need for clearer professional positioning. The findings suggest that favorable attitudes alone are insufficient to ensure preventive practice unless nurses’ intraoperative responsibilities are explicitly defined and reinforced within perioperative care pathways.

Sub-theme 1: The need for enhanced role awareness

All participating nurses were cognizant of the significance of perioperative VTE prevention. Nevertheless, they reported that existing training programs predominantly addressed preoperative and postoperative care, with scant attention to intraoperative nursing responsibilities. This gap consequently marginalizes the role of operating room (OR) nurses in VTE prevention. Enhancing OR nurses’ awareness of their specific intraoperative role is therefore crucial for ensuring the continuity of preventive care throughout the entire perioperative journey. While the immediate risk of VTE during surgery is acknowledged to be low, its potential occurrence persists. Strengthening this professional role awareness also helps nurses in mitigating associated medico-legal responsibility risks.

Sophia (N12)

We conduct regular nursing rounds on VTE prevention for neurosurgical patients to raise nurses’ sense of responsibility and crisis awareness (female, 31 years old, nurse practitioner, 5 years of experience).

Sarah (N18)

I once encountered a fatal pulmonary embolism case. Fortunately, I recognized the patient’s high VTE risk and chose an upper limb venous access; otherwise, the consequences would have been unthinkable (female, 43 years old, staff nurse, 21 years of experience).

Theme 2: Limited understanding of VTE

This theme reveals a gap between general awareness of VTE and a deeper understanding of intraoperative prevention. Although nurses were familiar with VTE as a clinical risk, many participants tended to associate prevention mainly with preoperative or postoperative ward care rather than with intraoperative nursing practice. This finding suggests that VTE prevention had not yet been fully integrated into nurses’ intraoperative risk perception. As a result, some nurses underestimated the relevance of VTE prevention during surgery or regarded it as peripheral to their immediate surgical assistance responsibilities.

Sub-theme 1: Neglect of intraoperative prevention

While all of the nurses interviewed reported having a basic understanding of VTE, most of them believed that there were limited opportunities for preventive nursing actions during the intraoperative phase. Furthermore, the statements of some nurses reflected a lack of understanding regarding intraoperative VTE risks. Nevertheless, six participants explicitly emphasised that prevention during surgery remains equally critical.

Lily (N7)

Neurosurgical patients usually take antiplatelet drugs preoperatively, so the probability of VTE is relatively low (female, 25 years old, registered nurse, 3 years of experience).

Sarah (N18)

I know ward nurses use compression stockings or pumps postoperatively, but we rarely use these devices during surgery (female, 43 years old, staff nurse, 21 years of experience).

Sub-theme 2: Deficient sense of responsibility for intraoperative prevention

Several nurses indicated that in neurosurgery, where patient conditions are often complex and volatile, their primary duty is to provide precise intraoperative support to surgeons. They attributed their relatively weak sense of responsibility for intraoperative VTE prevention to this focus, generally viewing it as a duty that falls primarily within the domain of post-operative ward nurses.

Olivia (N3)

Although I know pulmonary embolism is devastating, focusing on key steps like craniotomy and tumor resection consumes all my attention. It’s hard to spare extra mental effort for VTE prevention (female, 35 years old, staff nurse, 13 years of experience).

Grace (N6)

Ward nurses handle prevention pre- and post-op. During surgery, we focus more on vital signs, pressure injuries, and venous access (female, 29 years old, staff nurse, 17 years of experience).

Theme 3: Deficient practical competency in VTE prevention

This theme demonstrates that the main barrier to intraoperative VTE prevention was not only insufficient awareness but also limited practical readiness. Nurses reported uncertainty about how to assess VTE risk during neurosurgery, when to apply mechanical prophylaxis, and whose instructions should guide preventive actions. These findings suggest that the absence of neurosurgery-specific assessment tools, clear protocols, and competency-based training made it difficult for nurses to transform general preventive knowledge into standardized clinical actions.

Sub-theme 1: Deficiency in risk assessment practice

The Caprini risk assessment model is currently employed for VTE screening upon patient admission. However, there is a notable absence of dedicated assessment tools for the intraoperative period, specifically tailored to neurosurgical patients. Most nurses expressed uncertainty about how to effectively evaluate VTE risk during surgery.

David (N15)

We routinely assess pressure injury risk intraoperatively as it’s common in neurosurgery, but we don’t conduct VTE assessments. We rarely see DVT cases clinically (male, 38 years old, staff nurse, 19 years of experience).

Ava (N4)

General surgery or orthopedic VTE criteria don’t fit neurosurgery. Without a unified hospital protocol, we can’t just assess arbitrarily (female, 28 years old, staff nurse, 6 years of experience).

Sub-theme 2: Incomplete mastery of preventive measures

A key finding was that while intermittent pneumatic compression devices and compression stockings are standard equipment, most nurses lacked a clear understanding of their proper operation, optimal timing for application, and the source of requisite medical orders.

Zoe (N8)

We typically administer heparin anticoagulation preoperatively as ordered, but we are unsure when to use these physical prevention devices or whose orders to follow (female, 30 years old, registered nurse, 11 years of experience).

Hannah (N13)

Some pediatric patients are too young to wear properly fitted compression stockings (female, 41 years old, staff nurse, 19 years of experience).

Theme 4: Multiple challenges in implementing VTE preventive care

This theme highlights that intraoperative VTE prevention was shaped not only by individual nurses’ knowledge and skills but also by the organizational and clinical environment in which they worked. Participants described barriers arising from surgical priorities, patient positioning requirements, limited access to risk information, and inadequate availability of preventive equipment. These findings indicate that improving VTE prevention cannot rely solely on individual nurse education; it also requires workflow optimization, information system integration, equipment support, and multidisciplinary collaboration.

Sub-theme 1: Tension between surgical assistance and nursing judgment

Neurosurgical procedures place stringent demands on nursing coordination, characterized by prolonged patient immobilization and the use of specialized positions, which consequently elevate the risk of VTE. Although nurses are cognizant of these hazards, the imperative for seamless surgical assistance and strict adherence to aseptic protocols frequently hinders the effective execution of intraoperative VTE prevention measures.

Nora (N11)

In certain neurosurgical positions, patients’ limbs are nearly immobile. While we know prolonged immobilization may cause thrombosis, we cannot adjust positions due to surgical needs (female, 30 years old, nurse practitioner, 8 years of experience).

Sub-theme 2: Inadequate connectivity of hospital information systems

Nurses reported that the hospital information system lacks full integration; consequently, operating room staff do not have direct access to the VTE risk assessment results documented at patient admission.

Claire (N14)

The operating room system is not connected to the ward system. Once patients enter the operating room, we cannot confirm if they are high VTE risk (female, 39 years old, staff nurse, 17 years of experience).

Grace (N6)

We can only indirectly assess VTE risk by reviewing patients’ B-ultrasound reports or relying on anesthesiologists to inquire about a history of lower extremity thrombosis (female, 29 years old, staff nurse, 17 years of experience).

Sub-theme 3: Inadequate equipment allocation

Nurses identified two key resource issues: an inadequate number of intermittent pneumatic compression devices to cover all operating rooms, and a lack of clarity on their storage locations.

Sophia (N2)

Our operating room has only two pneumatic compression devices, and I haven’t paid attention to where they are stored (female, 41 years old, nurse practitioner, 19 years of experience).

Olivia (N3)

Usage of pneumatic compression devices is rarely emphasized in daily training. I know the equipment exists but am unsure when or for which patients to use it (female, 35 years old, staff nurse, 13 years of experience).

Discussion

This qualitative study identified four interrelated themes among neurosurgical operating room nurses regarding VTE prevention: a positive attitude toward VTE prevention, limited understanding of VTE, deficient practical competency in VTE prevention, and multiple challenges in implementing VTE preventive care. These findings concurrently unveiled systemic deficiencies in current cognition, practice, and institutional support for intraoperative VTE prevention. Although nurses’ positive attitudes serve as a pivotal foundation for improvement, the translation of such attitudes into consistent preventive practice was constrained by cognitive gaps, insufficient practical preparedness, and systemic barriers. To address these issues, a multifaceted strategy encompassing tiered training programs, evidence-based practice protocols, standardized workflows, and a multidisciplinary collaborative model is needed. These measures may directly address the research-practice gap in guideline implementation and improve holistic perioperative VTE management, thereby reducing surgical VTE risk for patients.

Positive attitude toward VTE prevention: A foundation for practice improvement

In this study, the first theme showed that neurosurgical operating room nurses generally held a positive attitude toward VTE prevention and recognized its significance for patient safety. This positive attitude provides an important basis for improving preventive practice. However, the findings also suggest that positive attitudes must be transformed into clearly defined role responsibilities and standardized actions within intraoperative nursing practice. This may explain why nurses could recognize the seriousness of VTE prevention but still not consistently translate this awareness into concrete intraoperative actions. In the operating room, nurses’ preventive intentions may remain implicit if they are not supported by explicit task allocation, workflow prompts, and institutional expectations. Thus, a positive attitude should be regarded as a starting point rather than a sufficient condition for practice change.

Notably, a widespread receptiveness to training among nurses presents a pivotal opportunity for educational intervention. Given nurses’ positive attitudes but limited understanding of intraoperative VTE prevention, nursing managers should establish a systematic, competency-based training program. This system should be structured, tiered, and recurring, encompassing VTE pathophysiology, risk assessment, and multimodal prevention strategies [21]. To enhance engagement and retention, diversified pedagogical approaches are encouraged, blending online and offline modules with case studies and simulations [22]. Integrating assessment and performance feedback is crucial to bridge the theory-practice divide. This process may elevate the safety and standardization of intraoperative care, thereby fostering a more scientific and refined VTE prevention framework in neurosurgery.

Limited understanding of VTE: The need for theoretically informed training

In this study, the second theme revealed a limited and uneven understanding of VTE among neurosurgical operating room nurses, characterized by a grasp of basic facts without a deeper comprehension of etiology, risk factors, or prevention mechanisms [23]. This limited cognition highlights a significant gap between theory and practice. According to cognitive-behavioural theory, an individual’s cognitive processes shape their emotional and behavioural responses. Therefore, nurses’ incomplete understanding of the importance and necessity of VTE prevention may hinder their precise adherence to established nursing practice guidelines [24]. VTE prevention is often more visible in ward-based care, where nurses are directly involved in admission assessment, postoperative observation, anticoagulation management, and mobilization guidance. By contrast, operating room nurses interact with patients during a shorter and more procedure-focused period, which may make intraoperative VTE prevention appear less central to their daily responsibilities [25]. This role boundary may contribute to the perception that VTE prevention mainly belongs to ward nurses rather than operating room nurses.

Furthermore, individuals’ appraisal of their environment is a critical determinant of behavioural decisions. A deficient sense of responsibility among operating room nurses regarding intraoperative VTE prevention may distort this appraisal, hindering the integration of preventive awareness into surgical workflows [26]. Additionally, nurses’ awareness levels vary according to seniority and role, suggesting that experiential accumulation does not reliably translate into evidence-based prevention awareness. This is consistent with previous research indicating that, in neurosurgery, the implementation rate of intraoperative mechanical VTE prevention measures remains very low [27]. Overall, this suggests that prevention awareness is weak. Underlying causes may include the absence of standardized protocols, insufficient managerial prioritization, and a lack of targeted training [28].

Deficient practical competency in VTE prevention: Promoting evidence-based practice and confidence

In this study, the third theme demonstrated that nurses’ difficulties were not limited to cognitive gaps but also involved deficient practical competency in VTE prevention. Specifically, nurses lacked confidence in implementing intraoperative VTE prevention and were concerned about increased workload. Since personal beliefs and intentions are key behavioural determinants [29], and given that nurses’ self-efficacy and attitudes significantly influence their preventive practice [30], addressing these perceptions is crucial. This finding indicates that nurses’ difficulties were not merely related to whether they valued VTE prevention, but also to whether they knew how to operationalize prevention under real surgical conditions. Uncertainty about risk assessment, device indications, timing of intervention, and responsibility for implementation may reduce nurses’ confidence and make them hesitant to act. In this sense, practical competency serves as the bridge between awareness and actual preventive behavior [31].

Given nurses’ uncertainty regarding intraoperative VTE risk assessment and preventive measures, nursing managers should strengthen evidence-based practice by systematically disseminating updated intraoperative VTE guidelines and consensus [32]. This can enhance nurses’ confidence in executing tasks and their self-efficacy, facilitating a shift from passive compliance to active implementation and ultimately improving the compliance rate and quality of VTE preventive measures [33].

To address nurses’reported practical competency gaps, a structured approach is recommended to standardise VTE prevention care. Firstly, incentive and participatory mechanisms should be established to integrate nurses’ feedback into protocol development and recognise practitioners who comply with and proactively contribute to the protocol, thereby fostering a sense of ownership [34]. Secondly, health information technology should be used to provide automated risk alerts, equipment prompts, and monitoring feedback, while incorporating standardised templates to reduce cognitive load and ensure consistency [35]. Finally, a supportive team climate should be cultivated to give nurses a clear voice, facilitate regular case reviews and experience sharing, and publicly reinforce exemplary performance, thereby amplifying the normative influence of positive attitudes and elevating the overall standard of preventive care [36]. This is also supported by healthcare workforce studies showing that leadership support, high-involvement work practices, job crafting, and psychological empowerment can promote nurses’ proactive and innovative work behaviours [37–38].

Multiple challenges in implementing VTE preventive care: Process optimization and collaborative support

In this study, the fourth theme identified multiple systemic barriers hindering intraoperative VTE prevention by operating room nurses, including constraints from surgical priorities, a lack of real-time data sharing in hospital information systems, and practical burdens due to equipment shortages and fitting difficulties. These findings align with literature noting persistent challenges in selecting devices, integrating prophylaxis into surgical workflows, and managing related complications [39–40]. Therefore, a coordinated, system-level response is required. Surgical priorities may limit opportunities for position adjustment or device application, while a heavy workload may make preventive actions less visible unless they are embedded in standard workflows. At the same time, limited access to preoperative VTE risk information weakens continuity between ward-based assessment and intraoperative care, making individualized prevention more difficult.

Given the constraints of surgical priorities and unclear role boundaries reported by participants, we recommend establishing a formal multidisciplinary protocol that clearly delineates the roles of the surgical, anesthesia, and nursing teams in initiating measures such as intermittent pneumatic compression and positional adjustments [41]. This can reduce decision-making delays and standardize preventive care. Standardization can be achieved through a multifaceted approach. Firstly, detailed operating procedures for intraoperative VTE prevention should be codified to define objectives and standards, enhancing workflow quality [42]. Secondly, it is crucial to integrate VTE monitoring and intermittent pneumatic compression data into the hospital information system for real-time recording and care continuity [43]. Thirdly, in response to reported equipment shortages and unclear storage locations, equipment logistics must be optimized through risk-based allocation of intermittent pneumatic compression devices and ensuring the availability of accessories. Finally, systematic training via simulation, coupled with a robust audit and feedback mechanism, is essential to consolidate competencies and drive sustained practice improvement.

Limitations

While this study provides valuable insights, it was conducted in only two tertiary hospitals in Beijing, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Additionally, while the use of a detailed, predefined interview guide ensured thorough data collection, it inherently introduced a degree of structure that might have constrained the spontaneous emergence of completely unanticipated themes, representing a potential methodological limitation. Given the substantial regional variations in economic development, culture, and healthcare resources across China, future research should expand the sampling framework to include participants from diverse geographic locations and hospital tiers. Although analytical notes and team memos were used during data analysis, the memoing process was not initially designed as a fully structured procedure. This may have limited the completeness of documentation regarding some analytical decisions.

Conclusions

This study employed semi-structured interviews to deeply explore the perceptions, preventive practices, and implementation challenges of operating room nurses regarding VTE prevention in neurosurgical patients. The findings provided valuable insights into the cognitive and practical challenges experienced by this specialized group of nurses. In order to translate these insights into practice, it is crucial to reinforce theoretical education and practical procedural training. This will enhance nurses’ cognitive mastery and self-efficacy in implementing intraoperative VTE prevention. Furthermore, the establishment of standardized workflows and evaluation metrics is crucial to optimize existing practices and ultimately improve the compliance rate of preventive measures.
