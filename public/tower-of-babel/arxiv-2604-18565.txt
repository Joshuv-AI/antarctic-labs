Detectability of minority communities in networks
Jiaze Li, Leto Peel

Abstract
Community structure is prevalent in real-world networks, with empirical studies revealing heterogeneous distributions where a few dominant majority communities coexist with many smaller groups. These small-scale groups, which we term minority communities , are critical for understanding network organization but pose significant challenges for detection. Here, we investigate the detectability of minority communities from a theoretical perspective using the Stochastic Block Model. We identify three distinct phases of community detection: the detectable phase , where overall community structure is recoverable but minority communities are merged into majority groups; the distinguishable phase , where minority communities form a coherent group separate from the majority but remain unresolved internally; and the resolvable phase , where each minority community is fully distinguishable. These phases correspond to phase transitions at the Kesten-Stigum threshold and two additional thresholds determined by the eigenvalue structure of the signal matrix, which we derive explicitly. Furthermore, we demonstrate that spectral clustering with the Bethe Hessian exhibits significantly weaker detection performance for minority communities compared to belief propagation, revealing a specific limitation of spectral methods in identifying fine-grained community structure despite their capability to detect macroscopic structures down to the theoretical limit.



I
Introduction

Community structure, characterized by groups of nodes that are more densely connected to each other than to the rest of the network, is prevalent in a wide range of real-world networks.
These communities typically reveal important functional or organizational units within systems.
These properties have drawn significant attention to community structure and its detection.
For instance, in brain functional networks, researchers have discovered modular structures that correspond to distinct communities
13
, such as those governing visual or auditory processing.
In social networks
17
;
32
, connections among friends tend to be stronger and denser compared to those between strangers.
Understanding community structure provides a clearer view of network organization and enables a deeper analysis of the underlying systems.

Numerous methods have been developed to detect communities in networks
16
, including algorithms based on modularity optimization
30
, statistical inference
22
, and spectral clustering
37
;
11
;
38
.
Essentially, these algorithms partition a network’s nodes based on the differences in their connection patterns, e.g., identifying assortative communities with dense intra-community and sparse inter-community connections.
Empirical studies of real-world networks identify heterogeneous distributions of community sizes, where a few dominant
majority
communities coexist with many smaller groups
25
.
Examples of these include scientific collaboration networks
31
, musician networks
19
, and email-based social networks
20
.
In this study, we define these small-scale groups that comprise only a small fraction of the total node population as
minority communities
.
Here, we explore the detection of these minority communities from a theoretical perspective, aiming to quantify the limits of their recovery.

Here we frame the problem of community detection as one of statistical inference.
We assume that the observed network is generated from an underlying data generating process that depends on some latent community assignment.
A popular model for this process is the Stochastic Block Model (SBM)
21
, in which edges are generated between nodes with a probability that is conditioned on their community memberships.
In this setting, the community detection problem can be viewed as recovering the latent community assignments from the relative density of connections among different node groups.
The detectability of communities relates to the noisy-channel coding theorem
39
, which states that information transmitted through a channel is unrecoverable if the channel’s capacity is below the transmission rate.
For communities to be detectable, the signal provided by the community structure must be sufficiently strong relative to the noise introduced by random connections.
Communities are information-theoretically undetectable when the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the relative edge densities falls below a certain threshold
2
.
However, above this information-theoretic limit, there exists a
computational
threshold known as the Kesten-Stigum bound
23
. In most cases, between these two thresholds lies a
hard regime
where detection is information-theoretically possible but believed to be computationally intractable—no known polynomial-time algorithm can succeed
12
.
Here, we consider this computational limit as the detectability threshold and anything below it as “undetectable,” meaning that no algorithm can recover the community structure with an accuracy better than random guessing.

The detectability threshold has been derived for a variety of different generative models, starting with the symmetric SBM—a model with equal-sized groups and uniform connection probabilities within and between communities
12
. For this model, the Kesten-Stigum threshold marks the boundary below which efficient algorithms fail.
Abbe et al.
2
derived an explicit expression for this threshold for a more general SBM, free from parameter restrictions.
Comprehensive reviews of the research on detectability can be found in Abbe
3
and Moore
28
.
Building on these foundational results, subsequent research has explored the detectability threshold for community structures in various other settings,
including in networks that have hierarchical
33
or overlapping community structure
45
and in directed
44
and dynamic networks
18
.
Community detectability has also been considered in the context of hypergraphs
5
;
9
;
36
;
26
.
Further explorations into the detectability threshold of the SBM have uncovered intriguing phenomena.
For instance, Chin et al.
8
find that when the number of communities is sufficiently large, detection may be possible even in the hard regime where
\mathrm{SNR}<1
.
Furthermore, the asymptotic analysis of Decelle et al.
12
has been complemented by work from Young et al.
46
, who conducted a finite-size analysis of the detectability threshold.

Above the detectability threshold, efficient algorithms exist that can identify communities that correlate positively with the planted partition
27
;
29
;
2
.
However, being above the detectability threshold does not guarantee that all communities are equally detectable.
A network may contain communities of varying sizes and connection densities, leading to heterogeneous recovery across different groups.
The weak recovery condition of detecting communities better than random guessing can be achieved through, for instance, the identification of a coarser partition that correlates with a finer planted partition
33
.
This distinction is critical: a network may possess a community structure that is considered detectable even if certain fine-grained groups remain indistinguishable.
For instance, consider a network composed of two large communities and one small community.
The small community may be too small or sparsely connected to be identified as a distinct community.
However, an algorithm might still perform better than random guessing by overlooking the small group and absorbing its nodes into the two larger communities.

This difficulty of detecting minority communities parallels the well-known resolution limit in modularity optimization
15
, which tends to overlook communities smaller than approximately
\sqrt{|\mathcal{E}|}
and often merges them into larger groups.
Our observations show that even when
\mathrm{SNR}>1
, community detection methods, including those theoretically capable of reaching the detectability threshold, may still fail to identify smaller,
minority
communities.
Nodes within these minority communities may either be uniformly distributed among majority communities or merged into a new aggregated community.
We derive explicit conditions that determine the detectability phase of such minority communities under spectral clustering.
Furthermore, we demonstrate that spectral clustering exhibits significantly weaker detection performance for minority communities compared to belief propagation—an asymptotically optimal community detection method
18
.
This indicates a specific limitation of spectral clustering in identifying minority communities, despite its ability to detect macroscopic community structures down to the theoretical detectability limit
37
;
24
.

In this work, we first review the detectability threshold for the stochastic block model.
Building on this foundation, we introduce two specific SBM configurations that explicitly incorporate minority communities of relatively smaller size.
Subsequently, we present results on synthetically generated networks to demonstrate that some community detection methods systematically fail to identify minority communities, even when the network communities are above the detectability threshold.
We identify the existence of three distinct phases of detectability for minority communities and specify the conditions under which each phase occurs under spectral clustering using the Bethe Hessian.
Finally, we conclude with a discussion of our findings and suggest directions for future research.

II
Detectability threshold for community detection

Here we briefly introduce the stochastic block model (SBM) and the detectability threshold.
The SBM is a probabilistic generative model in which edges are generated conditionally independently, given the community assignments of the nodes.
For simplicity, we restrict our attention to undirected and unweighted networks, although the SBM can be easily extended to more general cases
4
;
44
.
We generate a network with
n
nodes and
q
communities using the SBM by first assigning each node
i
to a community
\psi_{i}\in\{1,2,...,q\}
.
Then, according to a
q\times q
affinity matrix
\bm{\Omega}
, for each pair of nodes
(i,j)
, we assign an edge between them with a probability
\bm{\Omega}_{\psi_{i}\psi_{j}}
, which is determined by their respective community assignments.

The symmetric SBM, often referred to as the planted partition model
7
;
42
;
10
, assumes all communities to be of equal size, such that each community contains the same number of nodes.
The affinity matrix
\bm{\Omega}
consists of two values: a diagonal value
p_{\rm in}
, representing the intra-community edge probability, and an off-diagonal value
p_{\rm out}
, representing the inter-community edge probability.
For a network generated by the symmetric SBM to be detectable, the difference between
p_{\rm in}
and
p_{\rm out}
must be sufficiently large.
Formally, we can describe the detectability condition using the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) written in terms of the expected number of edges within the same community,
d_{\rm in}=\frac{n}{q}p_{\rm in}
, and to nodes in other communities,
d_{\rm out}=\frac{n}{q}p_{\rm out}
:

\mathrm{SNR}:=\frac{(d_{\rm in}-d_{\rm out})^{2}}{d}=1\kern 5.0pt,

(1)

where
d=d_{\rm in}+(q-1)d_{\rm out}
is the expected degree.

For a general SBM without any constraints on community sizes and the affinity matrix
\bm{\Omega}
, its detectability threshold
\mathrm{SNR}=1
is derived from the spectrum of the signal matrix
\bm{Q}

2
;
41
,

\bm{Q}=\bm{N}\bm{\Omega}\kern 5.0pt,

(2)

where
\bm{N}
is a diagonal matrix with entries
N_{rr}
representing the number of nodes in community
r
:

\bm{N}_{rr}=|\left\{i\mid\psi_{i}=r\right\}|\kern 5.0pt.

(3)

Using the first- and second-largest eigenvalues of
\bm{Q}
, denoted as
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
respectively, the SNR for the general SBM is given by
3
;
2
:

\mathrm{SNR}=\frac{\lambda_{2}^{2}}{\lambda_{1}}\kern 5.0pt.

(4)

The SNR expression in Eq. (
4
) for the general SBM reduces to that of the symmetric SBM.
In the symmetric SBM, we have
\bm{N}=\frac{n}{q}\mathbf{I}_{q}
and
\bm{\Omega}=(p_{\rm in}-p_{\rm out})\mathbf{I}_{q}+p_{\rm out}\mathbf{J}_{q}
,
where
\mathbf{I}_{q}
is the
q
-dimensional identity matrix and
\mathbf{J}_{q}
is the
q
-dimensional all-ones matrix.
Then the signal matrix becomes:

\bm{Q}=\frac{n}{q}((p_{\rm in}-p_{\rm out})\mathbf{I}_{q}+p_{\rm out}\mathbf{J}_{q})\kern 5.0pt.

(5)

Since the matrix
\bm{Q}
takes the form
a\mathbf{I}_{q}+b\mathbf{J}_{q}
, its eigenvalues ordered by non-increasing magnitude can be derived:

\begin{aligned} &\lambda_{1}=\frac{n}{q}(p_{\rm in}+(q-1)p_{\rm out})=d_{\rm in}+(q-1)d_{\rm out}=d\\
&\lambda_{2}=\frac{n}{q}(p_{\rm in}-p_{\rm out})=d_{\rm in}-d_{\rm out}\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt.

(6)

Substituting these into equation (
4
) yields the SNR expression for the symmetric SBM as shown in (
1
).

III
Detectability of minority communities

In this section, we model minority communities using the SBM and derive the detectability thresholds for these communities under spectral clustering.
Figure
1
illustrates the two community classes by size that we assume:

•

the minority communities
\cal S
constitute a set of
q_{s}
communities, each with fewer nodes than those in the majority class.

•

the majority communities
\cal B
constitute a set of
q_{b}
communities with comparatively larger sizes,

where
q_{s}+q_{b}=q
.
Let
\rho
denote the total proportion of nodes allocated to all minority communities in
\mathcal{S}
, so each minority community holds
\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}
proportion of nodes and each majority community holds
\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}
proportion of nodes. If all
q
communities were equally sized, each would hold
\frac{1}{q}
of nodes, and the
q_{s}
minority communities would collectively hold
\frac{q_{s}}{q}
. Since minority communities must be smaller than average, their total node share must fall below this equal-split baseline:

\rho<\frac{q_{s}}{q}\kern 5.0pt.

(7)

We capture the per-community size discrepancy as
\epsilon
, the difference in node proportion between a minority and a majority community,

\epsilon=\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}-\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\kern 5.0pt,

(8)

which implies that
\epsilon<0
to satisfy condition (
7
) that minority communities are indeed smaller than majority communities.
Then the matrix of community sizes
\bm{N}
is given by

\begin{aligned} \bm{N}=n\cdot\mathrm{diag}\bigg(\bigg[\underbrace{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}},\ldots,\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}}_{q_{s}},\underbrace{\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}},\ldots,\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}}_{q_{b}}\bigg]\bigg)_{q\times q}\kern 5.0pt\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt.

(9)

Similar to the symmetric SBM, we specify the unique elements of the affinity matrix
\bm{\Omega}
:
p_{\rm in}
for the intra-community edge probability, and
p_{\rm out}
for the inter-community edge probability.

(a) A network with two minority communities and three majority communities.
(b) An illustration of the expected adjacency matrix of the network shown in (a). Here, two minority communities share a total proportion of
\rho

,
p_{\rm out}^{(1)}
, and

In this case, the affinity matrix
\bm{\Omega}
takes the form of the planted partition model:

\bm{\Omega}=(p_{\rm in}-p_{\rm out})\mathbf{I}_{q}+p_{\rm out}\mathbf{J}_{q}\kern 5.0pt,

(10)

and we can express the signal matrix
\bm{Q}=\bm{N}\bm{\Omega}
as a block matrix:

\bm{Q}=n\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\bm{\Omega}_{q_{s}}&\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}p_{\rm out}\mathbf{J}_{q_{s}\times q_{b}}\\
\hline\cr\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}p_{\rm out}\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}\times q_{s}}&\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\bm{\Omega}_{q_{b}}\\
\end{array}\right]_{q\times q}\kern 5.0pt,

(11)

where
\bm{\Omega}_{q}
is the planted partition affinity matrix from Eq. (
10
) with dimension
q
.

Each eigenvalue of
\bm{Q}
exceeding
\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}
in magnitude corresponds to an eigenvector encoding a detectable
community contrast
—a bipartition of the
communities into two groups
24
.
When
q
such eigenvalues exist, collecting their eigenvectors into a
q
-dimensional embedding captures the full community structure, and spectral clustering in this space reliably distinguishes all communities down to the detectability threshold
6
;
1
.
When communities are of equal size (
\epsilon=0
), the signal matrix
\bm{Q}
has only two distinct eigenvalues (
\lambda_{1}
and a repeated
\lambda_{2}
of multiplicity
q-1
), so the single condition
\mathrm{SNR}=\lambda_{2}^{2}/\lambda_{1}>1
determines whether all communities are detectable.
With minority communities (
\epsilon<0
), this degeneracy is broken: as we show below, the repeated eigenvalue splits into distinct values, each associated with a community contrast that distinguishes between minority and majority blocks, or within either block.

In the following subsections, we derive the eigenvalues of the signal matrix
\bm{Q}
and label them with non-increasing magnitude:
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\lambda_{3}\geq\lambda_{4}
. We then identify that the condition

\frac{\lambda_{r}^{2}}{\lambda_{1}}=1,\quad r>2\kern 5.0pt,

(12)

which follows the form of the SNR in Eq. (
4
), marks the phase transition boundaries for detecting each community contrast via spectral clustering with the Bethe Hessian matrix.

III.1
Eigenvalues of the signal matrix

To identify the eigenvalues of the signal matrix, we work with the scaled matrix
\bm{Q}/n
, which is independent of network size and simplifies subsequent algebraic derivations.
We make this change without loss of generality since the eigenvalue ratios determining detectability are invariant under scaling.
To find its eigenvalues, we find the roots of the characteristic polynomial
\det(\bm{Q}/n-\lambda\mathbf{I}_{q})=0
.
This determinant can be expressed using the block structure:

\displaystyle\det

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\bm{Q}}{n}-\lambda\mathbf{I}_{q}\right)

(13)

\displaystyle=\det\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\bm{\Omega}_{q_{s}}-\lambda\mathbf{I}_{q_{s}}&\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}p_{\rm out}\mathbf{J}_{q_{s}\times q_{b}}\\
\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}p_{\rm out}\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}\times q_{s}}&\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\bm{\Omega}_{q_{b}}-\lambda\mathbf{I}_{q_{b}}\end{array}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\det\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\hat{\bm{A}}&\hat{\bm{B}}\\
\hat{\mathbf{C}}&\hat{\bm{D}}\end{array}\right]\right)\kern 5.0pt.

According to the determinant formula for a block matrix
40

\hat{\bm{A}}
is invertible, then

\det\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\hat{\bm{A}}&\hat{\bm{B}}\\
\hat{\mathbf{C}}&\hat{\bm{D}}\end{array}\right]\right)=\det(\hat{\bm{A}})\det(\hat{\bm{D}}-\hat{\mathbf{C}}\hat{\bm{A}}^{-1}\hat{\bm{B}})\kern 5.0pt.

(14)

We derive the eigenvalues of the scaled signal matrix
\bm{Q}/n

non-increasing order as
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\lambda_{3}\geq\lambda_{4}

Our derivation proceeds by exploiting the block structure: we identify the contributing eigenvector from the minority submatrix
\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\bm{\Omega}_{q_{s}}

\lambda\neq\lambda_{4}

The submatrix
\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\bm{\Omega}_{q_{s}}
has two distinct eigenvalues: one
corresponding to the
uniform

q_{s}-1

\lambda_{4}=\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\left(p_{\rm in}-p_{\rm out}\right)=\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta\kern 5.0pt,

(15)

such that
\delta=p_{\rm in}-p_{\rm out}

The eigenvalue corresponding to the uniform eigenvector only extends to the full matrix under specific conditions (see Appendix
B

For
\lambda\neq\lambda_{4}

\det(\hat{\bm{D}}-\hat{\mathbf{C}}\hat{\bm{A}}^{-1}\hat{\bm{B}})=0\kern 5.0pt,

(16)

(17)

where

\displaystyle Q(\lambda)=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta-\lambda\right)\!\left(\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta+\rho p_{\rm out}-\lambda\right)

\displaystyle+(1-\rho)p_{\rm out}\left(\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda\right).

(18)

From Eq. (

\lambda_{2}=\frac{(1-\rho)}{q_{b}}\delta\kern 5.0pt,

with multiplicity

III.2
Phase transition thresholds for detectability

The eigenvalue structure reveals two critical phase transitions governing community detectability. These thresholds follow directly from the SNR condition
\mathrm{SNR}=\lambda_{r}^{2}/\lambda_{1}>1
established in Eq. (
4
), where each eigenvalue
\lambda_{r}
corresponds to a distinct community contrast.

The first transition occurs when:

\frac{\lambda_{3}^{2}}{\lambda_{1}}=1\kern 5.0pt,

(30)

marking the onset of the
distinguishable phase
, beyond which minority communities become spectrally distinguishable from the majority as a collective entity. At this threshold, the signals from all minority communities merge into one coherent mode, collectively separated from the majority but not yet separated from each other. This condition also emerges naturally when merging all minority communities into a single group and comparing against the merged majority, where the SNR for that configuration coincides with
\lambda_{3}^{2}/\lambda_{1}
.

The second transition occurs when:

\frac{\lambda_{4}^{2}}{\lambda_{1}}=1\kern 5.0pt,

(31)

marking the onset of the
resolvable phase
, beyond which each minority community becomes spectrally resolvable as a distinct entity. At this threshold, each minority community develops its own independent signal, allowing clustering algorithms to resolve them separately from both the majority and from one another.

V
Comparison of detection methods

In this section, we compare five combinations of community detection and
order-selection methods for detecting minority communities in networks
generated by the stochastic block model. We focus on two detection algorithms:
Bethe Hessian spectral clustering (BH)
37
, using Algorithm
1
, and belief propagation (BP)
12
. BP is known to achieve asymptotically
optimal accuracy down to the detectability threshold
47
. We pair these two detection methods with three order-selection strategies: negative-eigenvalue counting (NEC), minimizing free-energy (MFE), and minimum description length (MDL).

Order selection is important here because, unlike in standard detectability analysis where
q
treated as known, minority community detection involves identifying different phases corresponding to detecting distinct numbers of communities: all communities separately, the minorities combined separate from the majority communities, or the minorities merged into majority groups.
The choice of order determines which phases are observed.
We present results under two experimental settings: consistent inter-community edge probability and consistent average degree.

V.1
Order-selection methods

We select the order using the NEC method by simply counting the number of negative eigenvalues of the Bethe Hessian matrix
\mathbf{BH}_{\sqrt{d}}
(and
\mathbf{BH}_{-\sqrt{d}}
for disassortative structures) to estimate
q
. This approach is simple and requires no additional computation beyond spectral decomposition.

The MFE method involves
q
by minimizing free energy of the SBM using the Bethe approximation
12
. We iteratively learn the SBM parameters
\bm{N}
and
\bm{\Omega}
using expectation-maximization (EM)
14
for each candidate
q
, compute the minimum free energy
f_{q}^{\rm min}
, and select the smallest
q
that minimizes
f_{q}^{\rm min}

q^{*}=\mathrm{argmin}_{q}(f_{q}^{\rm min})\kern 5.0pt.

(35)

The relationship between
q
and

typically exhibits an elbow shape. Determining

requires comparing successive
f_{q}^{\rm min}
values (full details in Appendix
A

The MDL method allows us to select

by minimizing the description length

For the SBM, the description length L is defined as

where

is the proportion of edges between community

and

denotes the proportion of nodes in community

VI
Conclusion

Here we establish that minority communities in SBM networks exhibit additional phase transitions beyond the standard Kesten-Stigum threshold. Using eigenvalue analysis of the signal matrix, we derived explicit conditions for three distinct phases under Bethe Hessian spectral clustering. The detectable phase corresponds to recoverable overall community structure, where minority communities are merged into majority groups. The distinguishable phase marks when minorities separate from the majority as a collective. The resolvable phase indicates when each minority community is individually recoverable. Our experiments confirm these predictions. We also observe that belief propagation exhibits the same three-phase structure, performing at least as well as spectral clustering across all regimes.

A key takeaway is that both belief propagation and Bethe Hessian spectral clustering achieve the theoretical detectability threshold for community detection, yet they diverge when it comes to identifying communities at finer resolutions. This discrepancy reveals that reaching the information-theoretic limit does not guarantee equivalent performance across all aspects of community recovery. We view these findings as opening several promising directions for future research. The observed gap between the two methods raises questions about the trade-offs between computational efficiency and detection accuracy. Spectral methods offer computational advantages, while belief propagation provides superior fine-grained resolution. One promising direction is hybrid algorithms that combine spectral initialization with lightweight refinement, or modified spectral embeddings that better capture size heterogeneity; such methods could achieve both efficiency and accuracy. These developments would strengthen our ability to detect minority communities in large real-world networks where small but significant groups can carry critical information.

Appendix C
Derivation of quadratic factor
Q(\lambda)

Here we derive the explicit form of the quadratic factor
Q(\lambda)
, which appears in the factored characteristic polynomial of
\bm{Q}/n
(equation (
III.1
)). The factor arises from the Schur complement of the minority block
\hat{\bm{A}}
in
\bm{Q}/n-\lambda\mathbf{I}
; we compute it by inverting
\hat{\bm{A}}
via the Sherman-Morrison formula and then evaluating the resulting determinant.

The quadratic factor
Q(\lambda)
in equation (
III.1
) expands to the determinant expression in equation (
17
), namely

\det(\hat{\bm{D}}-\hat{\mathbf{C}}\hat{\bm{A}}^{-1}\hat{\bm{B}})\kern 5.0pt.

(50)

We begin by computing the inverse of the matrix

\hat{\bm{A}}=\left[\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}(p_{\rm in}-p_{\rm out})-\lambda\right]\mathbf{I}_{q_{s}}+\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}p_{\rm out}\mathbf{J}_{q_{s}}\kern 5.0pt.

(51)

Using the Sherman-Morrison formula
35
, which states that for an invertible matrix
\mathbf{M}
and vector
\bm{u}
and
\bm{v}
:

(\mathbf{M}+\bm{u}\bm{v}^{T})^{-1}=\mathbf{M}^{-1}-\frac{\mathbf{M}^{-1}\bm{u}\bm{v}^{T}\mathbf{M}^{-1}}{1+\bm{v}^{T}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\bm{u}}\kern 5.0pt,

(52)

we express
\hat{\bm{A}}
in the form
a\mathbf{I}+b\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{1}^{T}
, where

\begin{aligned} a=\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda,b=\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}p_{\rm out}\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt.

(53)

Here,
\delta=p_{\rm in}-p_{\rm out}
and
\mathbf{1}
denotes the all-ones vector.
Applying the Sherman-Morrison formula (
52
) on
\hat{\bm{A}}
yields:

\begin{aligned} \hat{\bm{A}}^{-1}&=\frac{1}{a}\mathbf{I}-\frac{\frac{1}{a}\mathbf{I}\cdot b\mathbf{J}\cdot\frac{1}{a}\mathbf{I}}{1+\mathbf{1}^{T}\cdot\frac{1}{a}\mathbf{I}\cdot b\mathbf{1}}\\
&=\frac{1}{a}\mathbf{I}-\frac{\frac{1}{a^{2}}b\mathbf{J}}{1+\frac{b}{a}q_{s}}\\
&=\frac{1}{a}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{b}{a+bq_{s}}\mathbf{J}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda}\left(\mathbf{I}_{q_{s}}-\frac{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}p_{\rm out}}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda+\rho p_{\rm out}}\mathbf{J}_{q_{s}}\right)\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt.

(54)

From the block matrix structure defined in equation (
13
), the submatrices are:

\begin{aligned} \hat{\bm{B}}&=\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}p_{\rm out}\mathbf{J}_{q_{s}\times q_{b}}\\
\hat{\mathbf{C}}&=\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}p_{\rm out}\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}\times q_{s}}\\
\hat{\bm{D}}&=\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\bm{\Omega}_{q_{b}}-\lambda\mathbf{I}_{q_{b}}\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt.

(55)

We now compute the product
\hat{\mathbf{C}}\hat{\bm{A}}^{-1}\hat{\bm{B}}
:

\begin{aligned} &\hat{\mathbf{C}}\hat{\bm{A}}^{-1}\hat{\bm{B}}\\
=&\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}p_{\rm out}\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}\times q_{s}}\hat{\bm{A}}^{-1}\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}p_{\rm out}\mathbf{J}_{q_{s}\times q_{b}}\\
=&\frac{(1-\rho)\rho p_{\rm out}^{2}}{q_{b}q_{s}}\frac{1}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda}\left(q_{s}-\frac{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}p_{\rm out}}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda+\rho p_{\rm out}}q_{s}^{2}\right)\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}\times q_{b}}\\
=&\frac{(1-\rho)\rho p_{\rm out}^{2}}{q_{b}}\frac{1}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda}\frac{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda+\rho p_{\rm out}}\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}\times q_{b}}\\
=&\frac{(1-\rho)\rho p_{\rm out}^{2}}{q_{b}}\frac{1}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda+\rho p_{\rm out}}\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}\times q_{b}}\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt,

(56)

Next, we evaluate the expression inside the determinant:

\begin{aligned} &\hat{\bm{D}}-\hat{\mathbf{C}}\hat{\bm{A}}^{-1}\hat{\bm{B}}\\
=&\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}(\delta\mathbf{I}_{q_{b}}+p_{\rm out}\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}})-\lambda\mathbf{I}_{q_{b}}-\\
&\frac{(1-\rho)\rho p_{\rm out}^{2}}{q_{b}}\frac{1}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda+\rho p_{\rm out}}\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}}\\
=&\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta-\lambda\right)\mathbf{I}_{q_{b}}+\\
&\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}p_{\rm out}-\frac{(1-\rho)\rho p_{\rm out}^{2}}{q_{b}}\frac{1}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda+\rho p_{\rm out}}\right)\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}}\\
=&\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta-\lambda\right)\mathbf{I}_{q_{b}}+\frac{(1-\rho)p_{\rm out}}{q_{b}}\frac{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda+\rho p_{\rm out}}\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}}\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt.

(57)

Since the determinant of a matrix equals the product of its eigenvalues, we compute the eigenvalues of
\hat{\bm{D}}-\hat{\mathbf{C}}\hat{\bm{A}}^{-1}\hat{\bm{B}}
. This yields:

\begin{aligned} \det(\hat{\bm{D}}&-\hat{\mathbf{C}}\hat{\bm{A}}^{-1}\hat{\bm{B}})\\
=&\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta-\lambda\right)^{q_{b}-1}\\
&\times\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta-\lambda+\frac{(1-\rho)p_{\rm out}(\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda)}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda+\rho p_{\rm out}}\right)\\
=&\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta-\lambda\right)^{q_{b}-1}\times\frac{1}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta+\rho p_{\rm out}-\lambda}\times\\
&\left[\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta-\lambda\right)\left(\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta+\rho p_{\rm out}-\lambda\right)\right.\\
&\left.\quad+(1-\rho)p_{\rm out}\left(\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda\right)\right]\\
=&\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta-\lambda\right)^{\!q_{b}-1}\frac{Q(\lambda)}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta+\rho p_{\rm out}-\lambda}\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt.

(58)

Appendix D
Alternative method for deriving eigenvalues of
\bm{Q}/n

Here we derive the eigenvalues of
\bm{Q}/n
using an alternative approach to that of Appendix
C
. Rather than factoring via the Schur complement, we apply elementary column operations directly to
\bm{Q}/n-\lambda\mathbf{I}
to reduce it to upper triangular form, then read off the eigenvalues as the diagonal entries.

We solve the characteristic equation

\det(\bm{Q}/n-\lambda\mathbf{I})=0\kern 5.0pt.

(59)

First, we perform some elementary column addition operations to transform
\bm{Q}/n-\lambda\mathbf{I}
to an arrow-shaped matrix

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}p_{\rm in}-\lambda&-\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta+\lambda&...&...&...&...&...\\
\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}p_{\rm out}&\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda&0&...&0&...&...\\

\vdots&\vdots&...&...&...&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&...&...&...&...&\ddots\\
\end{array}\right].

(60)

Then we perform some elementary column addition operations to transform (
60
) to an upper triangular matrix. For simplicity, we do not show the final matrix. The determinant of the final matrix is the product of its diagonal elements:

\displaystyle\det\biggl(\frac{\bm{Q}}{n}

\displaystyle-\lambda\mathbf{I}\biggr)

(61)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda\right]^{q_{s}-1}\cdot\left[\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta-\lambda\right]^{q_{b}}\cdot

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}p_{\rm in}-\lambda-\frac{-\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta+\lambda}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda}\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}p_{\rm out}(q_{s}-1)\right.

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda\right]^{q_{s}-1}\cdot\left[\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta-\lambda\right]^{q_{b}}\cdot

\displaystyle\left[\left(\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta+\rho p_{\rm out}-\lambda\right)\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta-\lambda\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left[\lambda^{2}-\left(\left(\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}+\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\right)\delta+p_{\rm out}\right)\lambda+\right.

The characteristic equation (
59
) therefore has root

(equation (
15

, and

Here we describe the method for generating synthetic networks under the consistent

The network

In

11

After generating
\mathcal{G}_{f}
, we form
\mathcal{G}
by selecting nodes as follows: from each of
q_{s}
designated minority communities, we sample a fraction
\rho_{f}
of nodes;
from each of the remaining
q_{b}
communities, we sample a fraction
1-\rho_{f}
of the nodes.
To ensure that the final network
\mathcal{G}
contains
n

\rho
of nodes in minority communities, we set

\begin{aligned} \rho_{f}&=\frac{q_{b}\rho}{q_{b}\rho+q_{s}(1-\rho)}\\
n_{f}&=n\frac{(q_{s}+q_{b})(q_{b}\rho+q_{s}(1-\rho))}{q_{s}q_{b}}\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt.

(62)

Constructing a network
\mathcal{G}
with minority communities by sampling from a larger symmetric SBM network
\mathcal{G}_{f}
.

Let
d
denote the average degree of network
\mathcal{G}
. We derive
d
as

\begin{aligned} d=&\frac{1}{n}\left\{n(1-\rho)\left[\frac{n(1-\rho)}{q_{b}}(p_{\rm in}+(q_{b}-1)p_{\rm out})+n\rho p_{\rm out}\right]+\right.\\

Appendix F
Eigenvalues of signal matrix under consistent average degree

Here we derive the eigenvalues of the signal matrix
\bm{Q}
in the consistent average degree scenario introduced in Section
V.3
. In this scenario the two inter-community edge probabilities
p_{\rm out}^{(1)}
and
p_{\rm out}^{(2)}
are distinct, so the block structure of
\bm{Q}
differs from the consistent
p_{\rm out}
case. We apply the block determinant formula and Sherman-Morrison inversion to factor the characteristic polynomial, then establish the ordering of the four eigenvalues. We also derive the eigenvalues for the case where all majority communities are merged into one.

In the consistent average degree scenario, where
\delta=p_{\rm in}-p_{\rm out}^{(1)}
, the signal matrix is:

\begin{aligned} \bm{Q}=&n\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\bm{\Omega}^{(1)}_{q_{s}}&\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}p_{\rm out}^{(2)}\mathbf{J}_{q_{s}\times q_{b}}\\
\hline\cr\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}p_{\rm out}^{(2)}\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}\times q_{s}}&\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\bm{\Omega}^{(1)}_{q_{b}}\\
\end{array}\right]_{q\times q}\\
\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt,

(66)

where
\bm{\Omega}^{(1)}_{q}=(p_{\rm in}-p_{\rm out}^{(1)})\mathbf{I}_{q}+p_{\rm out}^{(1)}\mathbf{J}_{q}
is the community structure matrix using
p_{\rm out}^{(1)}
.

To compute the eigenvalues of
\bm{Q}
, we need to find the roots of the characteristic polynomial
\frac{1}{n}\bm{Q}-\lambda\mathbf{I}
, which can be written in block-matrix form:

\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\bm{A}}&\hat{\bm{B}}\\
\hat{\mathbf{C}}&\hat{\bm{D}}\end{pmatrix}\kern 5.0pt,

(67)

with the blocks defined as:

\begin{aligned} \hat{\bm{A}}=&\left(\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda\right)\mathbf{I}_{q_{s}}+\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}p_{\rm out}^{(1)}\mathbf{J}_{q_{s}}\\
\hat{\bm{B}}=&\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}p_{\rm out}^{(2)}\mathbf{J}_{q_{s}\times q_{b}}\\
\hat{\mathbf{C}}=&\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}p_{\rm out}^{(2)}\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}\times q_{s}}\\
\hat{\bm{D}}=&\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta-\lambda\right)\mathbf{I}_{q_{b}}+\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}p_{\rm out}^{(1)}\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}}\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt.

(68)

Using the determinant formula for block matrices
40
, we have:

\det(\frac{1}{n}\bm{Q}-\lambda I)=\det(\hat{\bm{A}})\det(\hat{\bm{D}}-\hat{\mathbf{C}}\hat{\bm{A}}^{-1}\hat{\bm{B}})\kern 5.0pt.

(69)

Applying the Sherman–Morrison formula
35
as given in equation (
52
), we compute:

\hat{\bm{A}}^{-1}=\frac{1}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda}\left(\mathbf{I}_{q_{s}}-\frac{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}p_{\rm out}^{(1)}}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda+\rho p_{\rm out}^{(1)}}\mathbf{J}_{q_{s}}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

(70)

Then,

\begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf{C}}\hat{\bm{A}}^{-1}=&\frac{\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}p_{\rm out}^{(2)}}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda}\left(\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}\times q_{s}}-\right.\\
&\left.\frac{\rho p_{\rm out}^{(1)}}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda+\rho p_{\rm out}^{(1)}}\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}\times q_{s}}\right)\\
=&\frac{\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}p_{\rm out}^{(2)}}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda+\rho p_{\rm out}^{(1)}}\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}\times q_{s}}\\
\hat{\mathbf{C}}\hat{\bm{A}}^{-1}\hat{\bm{B}}=&\frac{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}p_{\rm out}^{(2)}\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}p_{\rm out}^{(2)}q_{s}}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda+\rho p_{\rm out}^{(1)}}\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}}\\
=&\frac{\frac{\rho(1-\rho)}{q_{b}}p_{\rm out}^{(2)}p_{\rm out}^{(2)}}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda+\rho p_{\rm out}^{(1)}}\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}}\\
\hat{\bm{D}}-\hat{\mathbf{C}}\hat{\bm{A}}^{-1}\hat{\bm{B}}=&\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta-\lambda\right)\mathbf{I}_{q_{b}}+\\
&\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}p_{\rm out}^{(1)}-\frac{\frac{\rho(1-\rho)}{q_{b}}p_{\rm out}^{(2)}p_{\rm out}^{(2)}}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda+\rho p_{\rm out}^{(1)}}\right)\mathbf{J}_{q_{b}}\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt.

(71)

Since the determinant of a matrix equals the product of its eigenvalues, we obtain:

\begin{aligned} \det(\hat{\bm{A}})=&\left(\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda\right)^{q_{s}-1}\left(\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda+\rho p_{\rm out}^{(1)}\right)\\
\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt,

(72)

and

\begin{aligned} &\det(\hat{\bm{D}}-\hat{\mathbf{C}}\hat{\bm{A}}^{-1}\hat{\bm{B}})\\
=&\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta-\lambda\right)^{q_{b}-1}\\
&\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta-\lambda+(1-\rho)p_{\rm out}^{(1)}-\frac{\rho(1-\rho){p_{\rm out}^{(2)}}^{2}}{\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda+\rho p_{\rm out}^{(1)}}\right)\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt.

(73)

\begin{aligned} &\det(\frac{1}{n}\bm{Q}-\lambda\mathbf{I})\\
=&\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta-\lambda\right)^{q_{b}-1}\left(\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda\right)^{q_{s}-1}\cdot\\
&\left\{\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta-\lambda+(1-\rho)p_{\rm out}^{(1)}\right)\left(\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta-\lambda+\rho p_{\rm out}^{(1)}\right)-\right.\\
&\left.\rho(1-\rho){p_{\rm out}^{(2)}}^{2}\right\}\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt.

(74)

The last term is a quadratic in
\lambda
:
a\lambda^{2}+b\lambda+c
, with coefficients:

\begin{aligned} a=&1\\
b=&-\left(\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}+\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\right)\delta+p_{\rm out}^{(1)}\right)\\
c=&\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta+(1-\rho)p_{\rm out}^{(1)}\right)\left(\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta+\rho p_{\rm out}^{(1)}\right)-\\
&\rho(1-\rho){p_{\rm out}^{(2)}}^{2}\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt.

(75)

Based on (
42
), the discriminant
\Delta=b^{2}-4ac
is:

\begin{aligned} \Delta=&b^{2}-4ac\\
=&\left(\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta+(1-\rho)p_{\rm out}^{(1)}\right)-\left(\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta+\rho p_{\rm out}^{(1)}\right)\right)^{2}+\\
&4\rho(1-\rho){p_{\rm out}^{(2)}}^{2}\\
=&\left(\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}-\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\right)\delta+\right.\\
&\left.(1-2\rho)\left(\frac{d}{n}-\frac{1}{1-2\rho}\left(\frac{(1-\rho)^{2}}{q_{b}}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{q_{s}}\right)\delta\right)\right)^{2}+\\
&4\rho(1-\rho){\left(\frac{d}{n}+\frac{\rho(1-\rho)}{1-2\rho}\left(\frac{1}{q_{b}}-\frac{1}{q_{s}}\right)\delta\right)}^{2}\\
=&\left((1-2\rho)\frac{d}{n}+\rho(1-\rho)\left(\frac{1}{q_{b}}-\frac{1}{q_{s}}\right)\delta\right)^{2}\left(1+\frac{4\rho(1-\rho)}{(1-2\rho)^{2}}\right)\\
=&\left(\frac{d}{n}+\frac{\rho(1-\rho)}{1-2\rho}\left(\frac{1}{q_{b}}-\frac{1}{q_{s}}\right)\delta\right)^{2}\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt.

(76)

Then the roots of this quadratic equation are:

\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{2}\left\{\left(\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}+\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\right)\delta+p_{\rm out}^{(1)}\right)\pm\sqrt{\Delta}\right\}\\
=&\frac{1}{2}\left\{\left(\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}+\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\right)\delta+p_{\rm out}^{(1)}\right)\pm\right.\\
&\left.\left(\frac{d}{n}+\frac{\rho(1-\rho)}{1-2\rho}\left(\frac{1}{q_{b}}-\frac{1}{q_{s}}\right)\delta\right)\right\}\\
=&\frac{1}{2}\left\{\left(\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}+\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\right)\delta+\frac{d}{n}-\frac{1}{1-2\rho}\left(\frac{(1-\rho)^{2}}{q_{b}}-\frac{\rho^{2}}{q_{s}}\right)\delta\right)\pm\right.\\
&\left.\left(\frac{d}{n}+\frac{\rho(1-\rho)}{1-2\rho}\left(\frac{1}{q_{b}}-\frac{1}{q_{s}}\right)\delta\right)\right\}\\
=&\frac{1}{2}\left\{\left(\frac{d}{n}+\frac{\rho(1-\rho)}{1-2\rho}\left(\frac{1}{q_{s}}-\frac{1}{q_{b}}\right)\delta\right)\pm\right.\\
&\left.\left(\frac{d}{n}+\frac{\rho(1-\rho)}{1-2\rho}\left(\frac{1}{q_{b}}-\frac{1}{q_{s}}\right)\delta\right)\right\}\\
=&\frac{d}{n}\text{ or }\frac{\rho(1-\rho)}{1-2\rho}\left(\frac{1}{q_{s}}-\frac{1}{q_{b}}\right)\delta\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt.

(77)

Including the two eigenvalues from the first two factors of (
74
):
\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}\delta
and
\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\delta
, and multiplying all eigenvalues by
n
,
we obtain the eigenvalues of
\bm{Q}
:

\begin{aligned} \lambda_{1}=&d\\
\lambda_{2}=&\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}n\delta\\
\lambda_{3}=&\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}n\delta\\
\lambda_{4}=&\frac{\rho(1-\rho)}{1-2\rho}\left(\frac{1}{q_{s}}-\frac{1}{q_{b}}\right)n\delta\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt.

(78)

For the case
\delta>0
and
q_{s}<q_{b}
, and given the consistent average degree
d
from (
40
) and the minority condition
\epsilon<0
from (
8
), we have

\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\lambda_{3}\kern 5.0pt.

(79)

Since
q_{s}<q_{b}
and
\epsilon<0
, it follows that
\rho<\frac{q_{s}}{q}<\frac{1}{2}
, ensuring
\lambda_{4}>0
. The difference between
\lambda_{4}
and
\lambda_{3}
is:

\begin{aligned} \lambda_{4}-\lambda_{3}=&n\delta\left(\frac{\rho(1-\rho)}{1-2\rho}\frac{1}{q_{s}}-\frac{\rho(1-\rho)}{1-2\rho}\frac{1}{q_{b}}-\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}\right)\\
=&n\delta\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{1-2\rho}\frac{1}{q_{s}}-\frac{\rho(1-\rho)}{1-2\rho}\frac{1}{q_{b}}\right)\\
=&n\delta\frac{1-\rho}{1-2\rho}\epsilon<0\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt.

(80)

Therefore, the eigenvalues are ordered as:

\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\lambda_{3}>\lambda_{4}\kern 5.0pt.

(81)

A Heuristic Approach to Minority Community Detectability

If we merge all majority communities into a single community while maintaining a consistent average degree, the intra-community edge probability of the merged majority community must be set as:

\frac{p_{\rm in}+(q_{b}-1)p_{\rm out}^{(1)}}{q_{b}}\kern 5.0pt.

(82)

We continue to use the block matrix approach to derive the eigenvalues of
\bm{Q}
, modifying only the blocks
\hat{\mathbf{C}}
and
\hat{\bm{D}}
as follows:

\begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf{C}}=&(1-\rho)p_{\rm out}^{(2)}\mathbf{J}_{1\times q_{s}}\\
\hat{\bm{D}}=&\left(\frac{1-\rho}{q_{b}}(\delta+q_{b}p_{\rm out}^{(1)})-\lambda\right)\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt.

(83)

Following a similar derivation, we find the first term in (
73
) vanishes, while the remaining terms stay unchanged.
This yields the following set of eigenvalues:

\begin{aligned} \lambda_{1}^{(m)}=&d\\
\lambda_{2}^{(m)}=&\frac{\rho}{q_{s}}n\delta\\
\lambda_{3}^{(m)}=&\frac{\rho(1-\rho)}{1-2\rho}\left(\frac{1}{q_{s}}-\frac{1}{q_{b}}\right)n\delta\end{aligned}\kern 5.0pt.

(84)

We can observe that
\lambda_{2}^{(m)}
in the merged community structure corresponds to
\lambda_{3}
in (
78
) for the unmerged case.
This could be a hint that the detectability threshold of minority communities is related
\lambda_{3}
of signal matrix
\bm{Q}
.

Appendix G
Supplementary experiments on phase transition of minority communities

Here, we present additional experimental results to further validate the phase transitions described in Section
III.2
under the setting of a consistent inter-community edge probability
p_{\rm out}
.
The experiments are conducted with parameters
n=6000
,
d=50
.
The interval of
\delta
is carefully selected such that all
(\rho,\delta)
pairs yield valid probabilities satisfying
p_{\rm in},p_{\rm out}\in[0,1]
.

We also examine disassortative cases where
\delta<0
, which further generalizes the detectability phase transitions identified in Section
III.2
.
The results confirm that:

•

When
\rm SNR>1
and
\frac{\lambda_{3}^{2}}{\lambda_{1}}<1
, the minority communities cannot be identified even as a single aggregated group;

•

If
\frac{\lambda_{3}^{2}}{\lambda_{1}}>1
and
\frac{\lambda_{4}^{2}}{\lambda_{1}}<1
, the minority communities become distinguishable as a distinct group but cannot be separated from one another;

•

If
\frac{\lambda_{4}^{2}}{\lambda_{1}}>1
, the minority communities are fully resolvable and can be resolved individually.
