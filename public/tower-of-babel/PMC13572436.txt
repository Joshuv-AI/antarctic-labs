A circular economy framework to mitigate water scarcity and river pollution in Delhi, India
Authors: Radhey Shyam Tyagi, S K Singh, P K Goyal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDelhi faces a drinking water deficit of nearly 2000 Million Liters per Day (MLD) driven by rapid population growth, climate variability, and dependence on interstate sources. This study proposes a phased, circular economy–based roadmap to achieve water self-reliance by 2045. Using official datasets, field inspections, consultations, and scenario modelling, both conventional and non-conventional resources are quantified. Current filtered water supply from surface and groundwater is 4540 MLD against demand of 6540 MLD. Under projected population of 54.4 million by 2045, demand could rise to 14,797 MLD at the DJB norm or 10,880 MLD at the CPHEEO norm. Integrated measures such as wastewater reuse, NRW reduction, rainwater harvesting, groundwater recharge, and related interventions could raise total supply to about 14,928 MLD. Sensitivity analysis identifies population growth, NRW reduction, and reuse adoption as the most influential factors. Monte Carlo analysis shows a 90% confidence range from a deficit of 1200 MLD to a surplus of 2500 MLD. The results suggest that combined legal, technical, and behavioral measures can sharply reduce water stress and pollution, but full self-reliance will depend on strong implementation and favorable demographic trends.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56891-x.Keywords: Best water management practices, Circular economy, Groundwater recharge, Scenario analysis, Urban water resilience, Wastewater recyclingSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Hydrology, Water resources

Introduction

Global water scarcity has emerged as one of the most critical sustainability challenges of the twenty-first century. Chaudhuri et al.1, Herrera2, projected that by 2025 nearly 40% of the world’s population, approximately 7.2 billion people, would face serious difficulties in accessing freshwater for human consumption. Groundwater already plays a vital role in meeting water demand, accounting for 10–20% of total water withdrawals in developing countries3. Future projections are even more concerning: by 2050, an estimated 3.1 billion urban residents are expected to experience seasonal water shortages, with nearly 1 billion people facing chronic or perpetual water scarcity, significantly constraining urban growth and sustainable water supply systems4–6.

In many cities worldwide, groundwater constitutes the backbone of urban drinking water supply. For instance, Berlin relies almost entirely on groundwater to meet its potable water demand7 Click or tap here to enter text. However, rapid urbanization, climate change, inadequate groundwater recharge, pollution, and inefficient water governance have placed unprecedented stress on urban aquifers. Satellite-based studies have revealed substantial groundwater depletion across Asia’s major cities, where population pressure, climate variability, and aging infrastructure intersect to exacerbate water insecurity8,9. Climate change further intensifies these pressures by altering precipitation regimes, increasing drought frequency, and reducing glacier-fed river flows that traditionally support urban water systems10,11.

Cities across the globe have responded to water scarcity with markedly different resilience pathways. Singapore, facing severe natural freshwater constraints, has emerged as a global benchmark by integrating NEWater, recycling more than 40% of its wastewater, alongside desalination and demand management to approach water self-sufficiency12,13. Barcelona, following the 2008 drought, permanently incorporated desalination, wastewater reuse, and aggressive demand-side management into its urban water portfolio14. In contrast, Mexico City exemplifies the risks of a linear water management paradigm, where heavy reliance on groundwater abstraction has resulted in widespread land subsidence and aquifer depletion, threatening long-term urban resilience15,16. Similarly, Cape Town’s 2015–2018 drought, which nearly culminated in the unprecedented “Day Zero” shutdown of municipal water supply, underscored the vulnerability of cities dependent on limited surface storage, prompting a rapid transition toward demand reduction, aquifer recharge, reuse, and diversification of supply sources17,18.

The novelty of this study lies in proposing a comprehensive circular-economy-based urban water resilience framework for Delhi that simultaneously addresses water scarcity and pollution. Unlike prior studies, this research integrates legal, technical, institutional, and behavioral dimensions into a phased roadmap extending to 2045, quantitatively estimating contributions from wastewater reuse, non-revenue water reduction, rainwater harvesting, groundwater recharge, and non-conventional sources, while benchmarking Delhi’s pathway against global cities.

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate and propose a comprehensive framework for making Delhi a self-resilient city in terms of drinking water availability by the year 2045. Specifically, the study aims to: Assess current water supply–demand balance and quantify the existing deficit; Evaluate existing water infrastructure and identify inefficiencies in surface, groundwater, and recycled water use; Quantify the potential of non-conventional sources (rainwater harvesting, wastewater reuse, desalination, recharge); Propose legal, institutional, and technological measures for sustainable water augmentation; Model future water demand under climate change and population growth scenarios; Recommend best water management practices (BWMP) for demand-side conservation, and Integrate technical, governance, and behavioral strategies into a phased water resilience roadmap.

Methodology

Figure 1 outlines the stepwise approach adopted in this study, beginning with data collection, followed by stakeholder consultations, demand–supply gap analysis, scenario-based modeling, conservation potential estimation, legal and institutional review, community behavior assessment, and finally synthesis into an integrated strategic framework. A demand–supply gap analysis was then performed using population norms and existing resource availability, which formed the basis for scenario-based modelling of non-conventional water sources. The methodology further incorporates conservation potential estimation through reduction of non-revenue water and efficiency measures, a legal and institutional review of agreements and governance challenges, and a community behaviour assessment to capture social acceptance and willingness to adopt reuse and conservation practices. Finally, all findings were synthesized into an integrated strategic framework that combines technical, legal, and behavioral interventions to achieve long-term water self-reliance for Delhi.

Data collection, filed inspection, observations, and stakeholders’ consultations

Quantitative and qualitative data were obtained from official sources including the Delhi Jal Board (DJB), Central Ground Water Board (CGWB), Central Water Commission (CWC), and published reports from the Ministry of Jal Shakti followed by physical inspection of all the ten water treatment plants and allied infrastructure of different capacities, running from different water sources. Abandoned water bodies and mines within NCT-Delhi boundaries having potential for rainwater storage were also visited. These datasets provided estimates of existing surface and groundwater allocations, treatment plant capacities and utilization, recycled water volumes and losses during treatment, and historical and projected population growth and per capita water demand. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 12 DJB engineers, 8 water treatment plant operators, and 25 residents using community-level water sources. These interactions yielded insights into operational bottlenecks, policy limitations, and public satisfaction.

Scenario-based modeling for future water availability

To systematically evaluate Delhi’s future water security under varying policy and implementation scenarios, a scenario-based modeling approach was adopted. This methodology enabled the projection of water availability up to the year 2045 by quantifying contributions from both conventional and non-conventional sources under different levels of intervention intensity and implementation success. Three distinct scenarios were developed to capture a range of plausible futures:

Business-as-Usual (BAU) Scenario

Assumes continuation of current trends with minimal adoption of new policies, limited investment in non-conventional sources, and slow progress in demand management. Non-Revenue Water (NRW) remains high, and large-scale reuse and recharge projects see limited implementation.

Progressive Implementation (PI) Scenario

Represents a moderate, realistically optimistic pathway. Assumes concerted government efforts, partial success in legal and institutional reforms, and gradual public acceptance of reuse and conservation measures. Key infrastructure projects are completed but often with delays.

Circular and Resilient (CR) Scenario

An ambitious, transformative pathway embodying the full adoption of circular economy principles. Assumes aggressive policy implementation, optimal utilization of all non-conventional sources, high public participation, and successful execution of all legal and technical interventions.

Model validation

The water balance model was validated by back-casting to the year 2010. Using 2010 population data (16.75 million), historical supply estimates (4,200 MLD), and the CPHEEO norm (200 LPCD), the model predicted demand of 3,350 MLD. Actual reported deficit for 2010–2011 was 300–400 MLD23 with actual demand estimated at 3,500-3,800 MLD, providing acceptable model performance (error < 10%).

Multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA) for intervention prioritization

To enable a holistic evaluation and ranking of the various water augmentation and management interventions, a Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA) was conducted using the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP).

Criteria Selection and Weighting:

Criteria weights were elicited using AHP with a panel of 12 experts: 4 from DJB, 4 from academic institutions, 2 from CGWB, and 2 from environmental NGOs(Non-Government Organizations). Five key criteria were selected to evaluate each intervention. Weights were assigned to each criterion based on their relative importance for long-term urban water resilience, determined through a combination of literature review and expert judgment. The criteria and their weights are: (1). Economic Viability (Weight: 25%): Implementation reality. (2) Water Augmentation Potential (Weight: 35%): The primary objective of study. (3) Pollution Reduction Co-Benefit (Weight: 20%): The intervention’s effectiveness in reducing pollutant loads (e.g., BOD) in the Yamuna River or groundwater. (4) Implementation Feasibility (Weight: 15%): Ease of implementation, considering technical readiness, institutional, political, and social constraints. (5) Co-Benefits & Circularity (Weight: 5%): Additional benefits such as energy recovery, groundwater recharge, or job creation.

Scoring and Normalization:

Each intervention was scored on a scale of 1 (Least Favorable) to 5 (Most Favorable) for each criterion. The scores were then normalized to a 0–1 scale using the linear max-min method.

Results

Baseline scenario: current water deficit and pollution footprint

The baseline analysis for the year 2025 quantifies the severity of Delhi’s dual crisis of water scarcity and environmental pollution. Against a daily demand of 6,540 MLD (based on 33 million population × 200 LPCD CPHEEO norm), the current usable supply from all conventional sources amounts to only 4540 MLD, resulting in a significant water deficit of approximately 2000 MLD. This water deficit is compounded by a substantial pollution footprint. With approximately 3632 MLD (80% of water supplied i.e. 4540 MLD) of sewage generated and only 2724 MLD receiving treatment (of varying efficacy), a large volume of untreated or partially treated wastewater is discharged into the Yamuna River26,27. Based on our pollutant load estimation, this results in an estimated daily organic pollutant load of approximately 1600 tons of Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) and approximately 3200 tons of Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD). This massive influx of pollutants is the primary cause of the severely degraded water quality in the Yamuna, transforming it into an ecologically dysfunctional water body for its stretch through Delhi24,26,27.

Projected population and water demand for 2045

Using population projections (54.4 million at 2.53% annual growth from a 2025 base of 33 million worked out as ), projected water demand at 200 LPCD (CPHEEO norm) is 10,880 MLD. At 272 LPCD (DJB norm), demand reaches 14,797 MLD. All subsequent analyses use the CPHEEO norm (200 LPCD) as the nationally recognized standard, with DJB norm presented for sensitivity.

Projected water availability and intervention contributions

The implementation of the proposed Circular & Resilient framework reveals a transformative potential for Delhi’s water future by 2045. The model projects that the total potential water availability can be increased to 14,928 MLD under the CR scenario. This comprises the current base supply (6,050 MLD) plus additional contributions from non-conventional sources as shown in Tables 3 and 4 (under different scenario).

Pollution load reduction potential

Under the CR scenario, large-scale wastewater reuse (4540 MLD) would prevent approximately 1140 tons of BOD and 2270 tons of COD from entering the Yamuna River daily, representing a reduction of over 70% of the current organic pollutant load. This level of abatement is sufficient to shift the river from its current hyper-eutrophic state toward a condition that can support aquatic life and reduce public health risks24,27.

MCDA results

The Table 2 in “Multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA) for intervention prioritization” depicts the MCDA ranking, which reveals a clear strategic priority. Wastewater Reuse emerges as the top-ranked intervention due to its unparalleled combination of high-water augmentation potential and a direct, high-magnitude pollution reduction co-benefit, aligning perfectly with the circular economy paradigm. NRW Reduction ranks second, justified by its exceptional economic viability and significant water saving potential. Rainwater Harvesting ranks third, scoring highly on feasibility and co-benefits (flood mitigation, groundwater improvement). Notably, large-scale infrastructure projects like the Upper Yamuna Storage Dams rank lower (5th) due to low scores on implementation feasibility (interstate complexities) and economic viability (high capital cost), despite their high-water potential. The water efficient fixture has gained rank 4th as it is achievable with the political intervention. This analysis provides a powerful, multi-faceted justification for prioritizing demand management and circular economy interventions over traditional supply-side projects for achieving a sustainable and resilient water future for Delhi.

Sensitivity analysis results

To test the robustness of the proposed water resilience framework, a sensitivity analysis was performed by varying key parameters within realistic ranges. The sensitivity analysis (Fig. 4) shows that population growth rate has the largest impact on net water balance, followed by NRW reduction and wastewater reuse adoption. Under pessimistic assumptions (high population growth of 3.0%, weak NRW control with only 25% reduction achieved, and low reuse adoption at 50% of target), the net balance could shift to a deficit of approximately 1200 MLD by 2045. Conversely, strong governance, technological execution, and public participation could increase the surplus beyond 5000 MLD. Discussion

Interpretation of findings

This study demonstrates that Delhi’s path to water resilience is not predicated on discovering new distant water sources but on a fundamental restructuring of its urban water metabolism from a linear to a circular model. Under the ambitious CR scenario, the net balance is a modest surplus of approximately 4048 MLD, and under pessimistic assumptions, a deficit remains possible. The uncertainty analysis confirms that the probability of achieving surplus is approximately 65%, not a certainty. Crucially, this potential surplus is primarily driven by internal loops, wastewater reuse and NRW reduction, which are within Delhi’s jurisdictional and managerial control, thereby reducing its vulnerability to interstate hydrological politics23. This finding aligns with a global paradigm shift where cities like Singapore have demonstrated that sovereignty over water is increasingly defined by the efficiency of the urban water cycle itself12.

The dual role of wastewater reuse

The most significant finding of this research is the dual role of wastewater reuse as the cornerstone for both water security and river restoration. The quantification in “Pollution load reduction potential” reveals that achieving the full reuse potential of 4540 MLD would directly abate over 70% of the current organic pollutant load into the Yamuna. This transforms the perception of sewage from a liability to the most viable resource for “cleaning” the river. This aligns with the circular economy principle of “designing out” waste28,29.

Real-world implementation barriers and mitigation strategies

Translating the proposed technical roadmap into reality faces four principal categories of barriers, which must be acknowledged and addressed.

Institutional and political barriers: The most significant risk to the roadmap is the reliance on interstate cooperation. The projected 1,820 MLD from Upper Yamuna Storage Dams (Renuka, Lakhwar, Kishau) has been under negotiation for decades. The MCDA (Table 2) correctly ranks this intervention low on feasibility. Mitigation: Delhi must treat these projects as a political priority but cannot wait for them. The framework’s strength lies in prioritizing wastewater reuse and NRW reduction (Rank #1 and #2), which are within Delhi Jal Board’s direct control, thus decoupling resilience from interstate uncertainty.

Financial barriers and investment models: The upfront capital cost for tertiary treatment plants, dual-piping infrastructure, and smart metering for NRW control will run into billions of USD. Mitigation: Innovative financing is required, including (a) municipal green bonds, (b) public-private partnerships (PPPs) for large-scale reuse plants where the “product” (recycled water) is sold to industries, and (c) integrating water infrastructure into broader climate resilience funding streams.

Social and behavioral barriers: Public acceptance of recycled water for non-potable uses is generally high in global studies, but acceptance for potable reuse (even indirect) is low30,31. Mitigation: A phased, transparent public engagement campaign is essential, starting with visible non-potable reuse in parks and public buildings. This should be coupled with a strong communication narrative linking “every drop reused to a cleaner Yamuna.”

Energy-intensity trade-off: Advanced wastewater treatment for reuse is energy intensive, typically requiring 0.6–1.2 kWh/m³ for secondary treatment and 1.0-1.5 kWh/m³ for advanced tertiary treatment. The projected 4,540 MLD reuse would require approximately 1.6–2.5 TWh per year. Mitigation: This trade-off can be partially offset by sludge-to-energy synergy (capturing methane from anaerobic digestion). Furthermore, the energy cost must be compared to the avoided energy of importing water over long distances or pumping groundwater from deeper, depleting aquifers.

Global comparative analysis

This study shows that the findings position Delhi’s potential pathway within a global context. Unlike Singapore, which has achieved high-tech, centralized reuse, Delhi’s scale and governance challenges suggest a more hybrid approach, combining centralized plants with decentralized systems for large campuses and industries13,29. The comparison with Cape Town’s “Day Zero” crisis serves as a stark warning; delaying demand-side management and reuse until a crisis point imposes severe socio-economic costs17,18. Delhi’s opportunity lies in proactive, rather than reactive, investment in circular infrastructure.

Circular economy synergies

The transition from a linear to a circular water economy generates significant co-benefits beyond water augmentation, creating a positive feedback loop that improves air quality, reduces greenhouse gas emissions, and enhances soil health. The supplementary Table S2 summarizes these key synergies.

This synergy analysis demonstrates that the circular framework is a powerful tool for integrated environmental management. For instance, wastewater reuse is not just a water strategy; it is also a pollution control and energy conservation strategy. Similarly, sludge-to-energy transforms a waste management problem into a source of renewable energy and sustainable agriculture, addressing the water-energy-food nexus25,32.

Conclusion and policy implications

This study clearly shows that an additional 8878 MLD can be mobilized through a phased strategy, with wastewater reuse (4540 MLD) and NRW reduction (2120 MLD) offering the largest contributions. This can create a surplus of ~ 4048 MLD by 2045 with projected population of 54.4 million, using CPHEEO norms, this yields a potential surplus of approximately 4048 MLD, with a 90% confidence interval ranging from deficit of 1200 MLD to surplus of 2500 MLD. The water surplus is highly sensitive to NRW reduction and reuse adoption, while pollution reduction is exclusively dependent on maintaining high wastewater treatment efficiency. Success hinges on merging legal interventions (securing interstate shares), technological deployment (reuse, RWH), and behavioral change (public acceptance of reuse) into a single, coherent governance framework. The primary trade-off is high upfront cost and energy use, offset by long-term water security, massive pollution abatement, and energy recovery.

The energy implications of advanced wastewater treatment and reuse are a critical consideration in the proposed circular water pathway. Advanced secondary and tertiary treatment processes typically require 0.6–1.2 kWh m⁻³ of treated wastewater, depending on treatment configuration and effluent quality targets, while advanced tertiary treatment for high-grade non-potable or indirect potable reuse may increase energy demand to approximately 1.0–1.5 kWh m⁻³. Applying these ranges to Delhi’s projected large-scale wastewater reuse potential of 4,540 MLD, the associated electricity demand would be on the order of 1.6–2.5 TWh per year under full-scale implementation.

Limitations of the study and future research directions

While this research provides a robust strategic framework, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the financial analysis, while accounting for capital and operational expenditures, did not conduct a full Life Cycle Cost Analysis (LCCA) or a detailed cost-benefit analysis. The high upfront capital requirements of large-scale reuse and treatment infrastructure were noted but not weighed against the long-term economic benefits of averted water scarcity, reduced health costs, and environmental restoration. Second, the environmental assessment of interventions, though qualitatively described, lacked a quantitative Life Cycle Assessment (LCA). The energy footprint and potential trade-offs, such as increased greenhouse gas emissions from advanced treatment processes, were not quantified, presenting an incomplete picture of the environmental nexus. Third, the quantification of health risks associated with material degradation and leaching (e.g., microplastics from HDPE pipes, heavy metals from corroding steel) were discussed descriptively but not monetized or integrated into the cost model. Incorporating these public health externalities would provide a more comprehensive socio-economic justification for material selection and infrastructure investment. Finally, the model’s projections, while based on best-available data, require validation through real-time performance monitoring and pilot-scale studies. The assumptions on using entire recycled water 1510 MLD, public acceptance and behavioral change, though informed by surveys, need to be tested through large-scale demonstration projects to accurately gauge adoption rates and social feasibility.

To build upon this work and address its limitations, the following research directions are proposed: Comprehensive Life Cycle Sustainability Assessment: Future studies should conduct an integrated Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) and Life Cycle Cost Analysis (LCCA) of the proposed interventions. This would quantify the full environmental footprint (carbon, energy, water) and economic viability, providing a definitive comparison of the circular pathway against business-as-usual scenarios. Monetization of Externalities and Health Co-Benefits: Research is needed to monetize the positive externalities of the proposed framework, such as the economic value of a cleaner Yamuna River (e.g., tourism, property value increase, biodiversity) and the public health cost savings from reduced waterborne diseases and pollution-related illnesses. This would create a stronger economic case for investment. Hydro-Economic and Climate Resilience Modeling: Employ integrated modeling tools (e.g., WEAP-MODFLOW coupling) to simulate the hydro-economic impacts of the interventions under various climate change scenarios. This would enhance the framework’s dynamism and ability to support adaptive, climate-resilient planning. Socio-Technical Studies on actual recycled water being used against potential utilization, Public Acceptance: In-depth socio-technical research is crucial to understand the drivers of and barriers to public acceptance of recycled water for both non-potable and, eventually, potable uses. This includes designing and evaluating the effectiveness of different communication strategies and incentive structures.
