Dental caries prevalence and associated factors among 12-year-old children in Shanghai: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Qingqing Xu, Huning Wang, Jin Yu, Hao Zhang, Dongxin Da, Yiwei Jiang, Ying Zhang, Jing Zhu, Xiaoli Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDental caries remains one of the most prevalent chronic diseases among children globally. This study aimed to determine the current prevalence of dental caries and identify key factors associated with caries among 12-year-old children in Shanghai based on a theory-informed analytical framework, to inform targeted prevention strategies.MethodsA school-based cross-sectional survey was conducted in 2024. A total of 2570 children aged 12 years were selected from 16 districts of Shanghai using a multi-stage, stratified, cluster sampling method. Data were collected through oral clinical examinations performed by calibrated dentists using WHO criteria and a structured questionnaire covering socio-demographics, dietary habits, oral hygiene behaviors, knowledge, attitudes, and dental service utilization. Caries experience was assessed using the DMFT index. Univariate analyses (Chi-square test, Mann-Whitney U test, Kruskal-Wallis H test) were conducted. For multivariable analysis, covariate sets were determined using a directed acyclic graph, and modified Poisson regression with robust variance was employed to estimate prevalence ratios. Cross-tabulation and the Chi-square test were used to analyze the distribution differences in the intake frequency of five major cariogenic foods between genders.ResultsThe overall caries prevalence was 19.65%, with a mean DMFT of 0.41 ± 1.04. Multivariable analysis using modified Poisson regression revealed that lower paternal education level (prevalence ratio [PR] = 1.344, P = 0.012), female sex (PR = 1.524), suburban residence (PR = 1.238), and higher frequency of dessert and candy intake were significantly associated with increased caries. Compared with children consuming desserts ≤ 1 time/week, those consuming desserts ≥ 1 time/day had a higher caries prevalence (PR = 1.391, P = 0.003), with a dose-response gradient observed across intake categories. A similar pattern was found for candies (≥ 1 time/day vs. ≤1 time/week: PR = 1.458, P = 0.001). Brushing time and brushing specific surfaces were not significantly associated with caries. Children without fissure sealants had a lower caries prevalence than those with sealants (PR = 0.749, P = 0.001). Higher oral health knowledge was paradoxically associated with higher caries prevalence (PR = 0.735, P = 0.027). Girls had higher intake of solid sweets, while boys consumed more sugary beverages.ConclusionsThe caries prevalence among 12-year-old children in Shanghai is relatively low, yet significant differences exist by sex, region, and socioeconomic factors. Dietary behaviors—particularly frequent dessert and candy intake—were the primary modifiable factors. The inverse association between fissure sealants and caries likely reflects indication bias. The paradoxical knowledge–caries association suggests reverse causality. Bridging the knowledge–behavior gap remains a priority for prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08848-9.Keywords: Dental caries, Children, Associated factors, Cross-sectional study, Oral health, China

Background

Dental caries, recognized by the World Health Organization (WHO) as one of the three major non-communicable diseases, poses a significant global public health challenge. It not only severely affects children’s oral health and masticatory function but also has long-term negative impacts on their growth, development, psychological well-being, and overall quality of life [1]. The age of 12, marking the establishment of the permanent dentition (excluding third molars), serves as a key indicator for monitoring the oral health status of populations in various countries and regions [2].

Globally, the prevalence of dental caries in children exhibits significant disparities, showing a dual imbalance between developed and low- to middle-income countries, as well as within different regions of the same nation. For instance, the caries prevalence among 12-year-olds in Arab countries is as high as 72.62%, with a mean DMFT of 1.675, highlighting a substantial regional disease burden [3]. In contrast, data from Germany demonstrates a significant decline in the average DMFT for 12-year-olds from 2.4 (1994/95) to 0.4 (2016), with a caries prevalence dropping to 21.2%, reflecting effective caries control in developed nations with well-established oral public health systems [4].

According to the Fourth National Oral Health Epidemiological Survey Report, the caries prevalence of permanent teeth among 12-year-old children in China was 38.5% [5], indicating a persistent disease burden, coupled with a low filling rate of only 6.9%, which reveals a considerable “treatment gap.” Particularly prominent are the significant geographical inequalities in caries prevalence within China [6]. Data from various provinces illustrate this variation: Gansu Province in the west reported a caries prevalence of 38.6% [7]; Jilin and Liaoning provinces in the northeast reached 40.8% and 53.65%, respectively [8, 9]; in the eastern coastal area, Zhejiang Province reported 44% [10], while its neighbor Jiangsu Province had a relatively lower rate of 34.98% [11]; in the south, the caries prevalence among 12-year-olds in Hong Kong was only 17.68%, with a DMFT of 0.29 [12].

Shanghai, a mega-city in China, has long been committed to children’s oral public health, systematically implementing comprehensive interventions including pit and fissure sealants, topical fluoride applications, and school-based health education. Previous monitoring data indicated that the caries prevalence of permanent teeth among 12-year-old children in Shanghai was 41.1% (DMFT = 1.13) in Pudong New Area in 2016 [13], and 31.25% (DMFT = 0.65) in Minhang District in 2017 [14]. A study by Pan et al. in 2025 [15] reported a caries prevalence of 26.7% among 12-year-olds in Shanghai, suggesting a significant declining trend over time and reflecting the positive effects of public health interventions.

Although the aforementioned studies reported the caries prevalence among children in Shanghai, there is a lack of systematic analysis based on recent data regarding which specific behavioral factors remain the primary drivers of current caries occurrence, and whether their influence is mediated or moderated by factors such as knowledge and attitudes. For example, while Liu et al. [13] indicated a correlation between gender and caries risk, the study did not delve into the specific behavioral pathways behind it by combining detailed dietary preferences and brushing quality data.

Therefore, to more precisely identify the key behavioral risk points at the current stage, this study aims to: (1) obtain the latest baseline data on the permanent tooth caries status of 12-year-old children in Shanghai; (2) systematically analyze the impact of multidimensional factors on caries, including socio-demographics, dietary intake (involving the frequency of five major cariogenic foods), oral hygiene behaviors (brushing frequency, duration, and most importantly, thoroughness), health knowledge, and attitudes; (3) specifically focus on significant associated factors identified in multivariate analysis (e.g., gender), to investigate whether they are driven by specific dietary or behavioral patterns, thereby providing an empirical basis for developing more precise and targeted intervention strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted as part of the 2024 Shanghai Oral Health Epidemiological Survey, which monitors the oral health status of key populations. The survey targeted 12-year-old school students in Shanghai. A multi-stage stratified cluster sampling method was employed. In the first stage, Shanghai’s 16 administrative districts were selected as strata. In the second stage, one junior high school (including both public and private schools) was randomly selected from each stratum. In the third stage, 5–6 classes were randomly selected from the first-grade (12-year-old) students of the chosen schools, and all eligible students in these classes were included, with a target sample size of approximately 200 students per school. The primary sampling unit was the school. In the statistical analysis, clustering effects were accounted for, with district as the stratification variable, to obtain robust standard errors.

This study was approved by the independent Ethics Committee of Shanghai Stomatological Hospital. The approval number is Shanghai Stomatological Hospital Ethics Review [2022] No. 022 (Hukouyuan Lunshen [2022] No. 022).All data collection and processing procedures strictly adhered to the principle of informed consent.

Inclusion criteria

Children aged 12 years (turned 12 on the survey day, or had turned 12 before the survey and not yet turned 13). Informed assent from the students and written informed consent from their parents or guardians were obtained. Present at school on the survey day and able to complete the entire oral clinical examination and questionnaire survey.

Exclusion criteria

Children with mental illnesses.

Survey content and methods

The survey comprised two parts: an oral health clinical examination and a questionnaire survey.

Oral health clinical examination

Six dentists, trained and calibrated according to WHO oral health survey standards (post-training Kappa value > 0.8, meeting the quality requirements for the survey), served as examiners.

Under artificial light, using standard dental probes (CPI probes) and mouth mirrors, they conducted full-mouth permanent tooth examinations in accordance with the diagnostic criteria outlined in the WHO Oral Health Surveys: Basic Methods (5th edition) [5].

This study recorded caries status in both primary and permanent teeth separately, but only permanent tooth caries data were included in the calculation of caries prevalence.

Questionnaire survey

The Oral Health Questionnaire for Children and Adolescents in Shanghai was developed by adapting and supplementing the WHO Oral Health Survey questionnaire and the 4th National Oral Health Survey questionnaire. Trained investigators distributed the questionnaires collectively in classrooms, guided students to complete them independently, and collected them on-site. The questionnaire content covered socio-demographic information, dietary habits, oral hygiene behaviors, preventive measures and history, self-assessment, dental service utilization pattern, oral health knowledge and attitudes. The English version of the questionnaire is provided in Additional file 1.

Quality control

Examiner Calibration and Training: Six dentists with professional qualifications and at least three years of clinical experience served as examiners. All participating dentists received unified theoretical and clinical examination training based on the WHO criteria. After training, an inter-examiner reliability test was conducted, and the Kappa value was calculated. The assessment standard required a Kappa value of greater than 0.8. Examiners who did not meet this standard underwent re-training until they passed, ensuring highly consistent diagnostic criteria among all examiners.

On-site Quality Supervision: Furthermore, a quality control team was established within the survey sample of each school to supervise every aspect of the data collection process and evaluate the reliability of the data.

Statistical analysis

Data Management: Two researchers independently entered the data using EpiData (EpiData Association, Odense, Denmark), and the two datasets were compared for consistency.

Statistical Methods: Data analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics (Version 26.0, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). Dental caries prevalence was determined based on the DMFT index. Variables were classified as follows: dietary intake (desserts, candies) into ≤ 1/week, 2–6/week, ≥ 1/day; brushing time into ≥ 2 vs. <2 min; oral health knowledge (0–8) into low (0–2), medium (3–5), high (6–8); attitude (0–4) into low (0–2) vs. high (3–4); paternal education into junior college or below, undergraduate or above, and unknown. Scores for oral health knowledge and attitudes were calculated as follows: correct/positive = 1 point, incorrect/negative/other = 0 points. Missing data were minimal (< 1%) and were handled by complete-case analysis.

Descriptive and Inferential Statistics: Categorical variables were described using frequencies (n) and percentages (%). For group comparisons, the Chi-square test, Mann-Whitney U test (for two groups), or Kruskal-Wallis H test (for multiple groups) was used as appropriate.

Covariate Selection and Multivariable Analysis: Covariate selection was guided by a directed acyclic graph (DAG) constructed based on existing literature and subject-matter knowledge (see Supplementary Material for full DAG specification). Overadjustment by including mediating variables can bias effect estimates [16]. Variables were classified into three temporal tiers: (1) distal background factors (sex, area, paternal education level); (2) behavioral and cognitive mediating factors (dietary habits, oral hygiene behaviors, oral health knowledge); and (3) disease consequence variables (history of caries/fillings, toothache experience, self-rated oral health, self-rated general health, and dental service utilization pattern). Disease consequence variables were excluded from all predictive models to avoid reverse causality bias. Behavioral and cognitive mediating variables were not adjusted for when estimating the total effect of distal background factors, to avoid overadjustment. Distinct covariate sets were specified for each exposure variable based on its causal position.

Multivariable associations were estimated using modified Poisson regression (generalized linear model with Poisson distribution, log link function, and robust variance estimator) to obtain prevalence ratios (PRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) [17]. The complex survey design was accounted for in the analysis, with school as the primary sampling unit and district as the stratification variable, to obtain robust standard errors adjusted for clustering. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). Unless otherwise specified, the significance level (α) was set at 0.05, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the prevalence and multifactorial influences of dental caries among 12-year-old children in Shanghai. The key findings reveal that despite a relatively low overall caries prevalence, the disease burden is unevenly distributed and influenced by a complex interplay of sociodemographic, behavioral, and cognitive factors.

Overall characteristics and distribution of caries prevalence

The Significant Caries Index (SiC Index) of 1.23 in this study, which is approximately three times the overall mean DMFT of 0.41, clearly indicates that the caries burden is concentrated in a specific high-risk subgroup. This uneven distribution is a well-recognized pattern [18]. To effectively assess and quantify this inequality, the Significant Caries Index (SiC Index) was introduced by Bratthall [19]. This index, which calculates the mean DMFT of the one-third of the population with the highest caries scores, has been widely used to identify and monitor the subgroup bearing the highest disease burden [20]. The overall caries prevalence (19.65%) and mean DMFT we found are notably lower than the national average reported in the Fourth National Oral Health Epidemiological Survey Report [5]. This may reflect the positive impact of systematic public health interventions implemented in Shanghai, as advocated in the “Healthy Shanghai 2030” policy initiative [21].

Analysis of caries by tooth position revealed that the lesions were highly concentrated in the first molars and other teeth with deep pits and fissures [22]. The mandibular first molars were the most affected teeth (#46: 11.83%, #36: 11.40%), unequivocally identifying these sites as the primary targets for clinical prevention and early intervention.

Sex and spatial disparities in caries prevalence

This study reaffirms female sex and suburban residence as factors associated for caries. The detailed analysis of dietary behaviors offers a plausible explanation for the sex disparity. Girls reported a significantly higher proportion of medium- and high-frequency intake of solid sweets like cakes and candies, whereas boys showed a higher proportion of high-frequency intake of soft drinks. While sugary drinks are well-established associated factors, sticky solid sweets are more likely to adhere to tooth surfaces, particularly pits and fissures, providing a sustained substrate for cariogenic bacteria and potentially exerting a stronger local cariogenic effect [23, 24]. Therefore, girls’ higher preference for multiple high-risk sticky sweets may be a key behavioral driver behind their greater overall caries prevalence. Additionally, the earlier eruption timing of permanent teeth in girls [25, 26] leads to a longer cumulative exposure of less mature, and thus more caries-susceptible, enamel to the oral environment, constituting an underlying physiological basis for their higher risk [18].

The higher caries prevalence among children in suburban areas profoundly reflects the influence of social determinants of health [27]. Evidence consistently shows that lower socioeconomic status is a strong determinant of caries, and this inequality often manifests as geographical disparities in access to services [28]. In China, socioeconomic inequalities in childhood caries have persisted, with evidence suggesting that disparities in untreated caries may be widening in rural areas [29]. This is supported by anther comparative study, which found differences in the caries experience of the first permanent molar among schoolchildren in Shanghai and Jiangxi Province, correlating with factors such as eating sweets before bedtime and parental education levels [30]. In the present study, children whose fathers had lower education levels had significantly higher caries prevalence, further supporting the role of socioeconomic factors. In China, where community water fluoridation is not widespread, the equity of access to professional preventive services becomes paramount. The uneven distribution of oral health resources between urban and suburban areas is a global challenge [31], which may limit suburban children’s access to essential prevention, thereby exacerbating health inequalities. Therefore, our findings call for optimizing the spatial allocation of preventive resources and strengthening school-based oral health programs in underserved areas to tackle the oral health inequalities rooted in social determinants.

Dietary and behavioral factors in caries occurrence

The DAG-informed analysis revealed significant dose-response relationships between dietary behaviors and caries. Higher frequency of dessert and candy intake was significantly associated with increased caries prevalence, with a clear gradient: children with higher intake frequency had progressively higher caries prevalence. Compared with children consuming desserts ≤ 1 time/week, those consuming desserts ≥ 1 time/day had a higher caries prevalence, with a similar dose-response gradient observed for candies. These findings reaffirm the central role of free sugar intake frequency in caries etiology [32] and support WHO recommendations on limiting free sugar consumption to less than 10% of total energy intake [33]. The identification of these associations—which were not apparent when disease consequence variables were included in the model—underscores the importance of appropriate covariate selection to avoid masking true dietary effects [34].

Regarding oral hygiene behaviors, commonly recommended indicators—including brushing duration and cleaning of specific tooth surfaces—showed no significant independent association with caries in the current analysis. Failure to clean occlusal surfaces, previously identified as a significant factor, was no longer significant after excluding disease consequence variables. This suggests that the earlier finding may have been confounded by the inclusion of variables such as caries history and toothache. The lack of significant associations for brushing behaviors may also reflect the limitations of self-reported oral hygiene measures [35, 36] and the cross-sectional design, which cannot capture the temporal relationship between brushing habits and caries development. Furthermore, in 12-year-old children, caries occurrence in pits and fissures may be more strongly determined by tooth morphology and past fluoride exposure than by brushing behavior alone [37, 38] .

Variables previously reported as risk factors—history of caries/fillings, toothache experience, self-rated general health, and dental service utilization—were identified by the DAG as potential consequences of caries rather than upstream causes. They were therefore excluded from the multivariable predictive models. Their strong associations with caries in univariate analysis reflect the fact that children with caries are more likely to have fillings, experience toothache, rate their health poorly, and seek treatment. While these variables are not etiological factors, they remain valuable for risk stratification in clinical and public health settings. A simple screening approach combining caries history and recent toothache can help identify children in need of prioritized care [39–41].

Paradoxical associations: fissure sealants and oral health knowledge

Two findings in this study warrant cautious interpretation due to their unexpected direction. First, children without fissure sealants had a lower caries prevalence than those with sealants. This inverse association likely reflects indication bias inherent in the cross-sectional design [42]. In school-based sealant programs, children with deep pits and fissures, early enamel demineralization, or other high-risk characteristics are more likely to be screened and selected for sealant placement. Consequently, the sealant group comprises children with inherently higher caries risk, creating a spurious positive association between sealants and caries. The well-established efficacy of pit and fissure sealants in preventing caries has been confirmed by multiple randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews [43–45]; the current finding should not be interpreted as evidence against sealant use. Longitudinal studies with information on the timing of sealant placement relative to caries onset are needed to accurately evaluate sealant effectiveness in real-world settings.

Second, higher oral health knowledge was paradoxically associated with higher caries prevalence. Children in the low knowledge group had a lower caries prevalence than those in the high knowledge group. This counterintuitive finding most likely reflects reverse causality [46, 47]: children who have experienced caries, toothache, or dental treatment are more likely to have received oral health information during clinical visits, thereby acquiring higher knowledge scores. In a cross-sectional study, the temporal sequence between knowledge acquisition and caries occurrence cannot be disentangled. Additionally, oral health knowledge does not automatically translate into sustained behavioral change—a phenomenon well-documented as the “knowledge-behavior gap” [48, 49]. Recent evidence from structural equation modeling further supports that oral health literacy influences caries only indirectly through behavioral pathways such as brushing frequency, rather than exerting a direct effect [50]. Children may possess adequate knowledge yet fail to implement recommended behaviors consistently. Future longitudinal studies should assess whether improvements in oral health knowledge precede reductions in caries incidence, and investigate the barriers to translating knowledge into effective oral health practices.

Implications for clinical and public health practice

WHO’s 2023–2030 global oral health targets aim to reduce the burden of dental caries and implement prevention, early diagnosis and effective management of caries as part of essential oral health care for all [51]. Building on the findings of this study and guided by the organizational framework for addressing oral health inequalities proposed by Patrick et al. [52]—and based on China’s national comprehensive oral health intervention program [53]—the following implications for caries prevention and control practice are proposed.

At the clinical level, dental professionals should integrate dietary behavior assessment, particularly the frequency of dessert and candy intake, and socioeconomic indicators such as paternal education level to quickly identify high-risk children. Children with high free sugar intake frequency and those from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds warrant prioritized preventive intervention. Subsequently, they should formulate and track personalized health management plans, facilitating a shift towards “personalized risk management.”

At the public health level, policy should prioritize ensuring that standardized, school-based pit and fissure sealant and fluoride programs are accessible to all children, particularly those in suburban areas and those with lower parental education levels. Although the cross-sectional association between sealants and caries was in an unexpected direction in this study—likely reflecting indication bias—the effectiveness of sealants in preventing caries is well established in randomized trials and systematic reviews [43–45]. Programs should also prioritize reducing frequent free sugar intake, given the clear dose-response relationships observed for both desserts and candies. For children with frequent sticky sweet intake, specialized dietary guidance should be implemented. For suburban areas, it is essential to strengthen the development of the primary-level oral disease prevention service system to fundamentally reduce health inequalities.

Finally, through the close integration of precise clinical interventions and systemic public health support, a comprehensive, multi-level integrated prevention and control system for dental caries in children should be established.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships between the identified factors and dental caries; the temporal sequence of exposures and outcomes cannot be determined. However, as noted in the literature, rigorously conducted observational studies can provide meaningful evidence for causality when randomized trials are infeasible [54, 55]. Second, data on dietary habits, oral hygiene behaviors, and dental visits were self-reported, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Third, the sample was drawn exclusively from Shanghai, and the findings may not be generalizable to other regions of China. Fourth, the “unknown” category of paternal education level encompasses diverse family backgrounds, and its interpretation warrants caution. Fifth, the inverse association between fissure sealants and caries likely reflects indication bias inherent in the cross-sectional design, and the true preventive effect of sealants cannot be evaluated in this study. Sixth, the paradoxical association between oral health knowledge and caries may reflect reverse causality, which cannot be resolved without longitudinal data. Future research should employ longitudinal designs and incorporate more objective measures, such as biomarkers for sugar intake or direct observation of oral hygiene behaviors, to further elucidate the dynamic relationships between various factors and the development and progression of dental caries.

Conclusion

The prevalence of dental caries among 12-year-old children in Shanghai is relatively low; however, the disease burden is concentrated in high-risk subgroups. The DAG-informed multivariable analysis identified female sex, suburban residence, lower paternal education level, and higher frequency of dessert and candy intake as factors associated with increased caries prevalence. Brushing behaviors were not significantly associated with caries, while higher oral health knowledge was paradoxically associated with higher caries prevalence, likely reflecting reverse causality. The inverse association between fissure sealants and caries likely reflects indication bias in this cross-sectional design.

Targeted strategies for caries prevention and control should focus on socioeconomically disadvantaged groups and children with high free sugar intake frequency. Public health efforts should prioritize reducing the frequency of free sugar consumption, particularly sticky sweets, and equitably expanding school-based pit and fissure sealant and fluoride programs to underserved areas. Health education should shift from merely imparting knowledge to fostering effective behavior change, bridging the persistent “knowledge-behavior gap.” Longitudinal research is needed to clarify the temporal relationships between oral health knowledge, dietary behaviors, and caries development.
