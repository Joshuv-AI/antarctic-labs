Localization and universality of three-dimensional pseudospin- s s fermions
Arpan Gupta, Gargee Sharma

Abstract
Quantum interference of electrons in disordered conductors is a sensitive probe of the internal structure of quasiparticles, revealing universal signatures of symmetry through weak localization (WL) and weak antilocalization (WAL). While these phenomena are well understood for the conventional Schrödinger and Dirac-Weyl fermions, their fate in the broader class of multifold chiral fermions remains largely unexplored. We develop a unified framework for semiclassical transport and quantum interference in three-dimensional disordered fermions with an arbitrary pseudospin s s . Starting from a general short-range matrix disorder ℳ \mathcal{M} , we derive compact expressions for elastic lifetimes and ladder vertex corrections for arbitrary pseudospin with multiband effects, and then show that in the scalar-disorder limit while the Drude conductivity is strongly pseudospin and helicity dependent, in contrast, the leading quantum interference correction exhibits a striking universality: its magnitude remains identical to that of conventional diffusive metals and Weyl fermions, while its sign is determined solely by the parity of 2 ​ s 2s , placing half-integer pseudospins in the symplectic class (WAL) and integer pseudospins in the orthogonal class (WL). We also analyze the role of interband and intervalley scattering for s = 3 / 2 s=3/2 . By solving the resulting coupled Bethe-Salpeter equations, we demonstrate that channel mixing suppresses WAL and drives a crossover toward localization. Our results establish a general theory of localization across the full pseudospin hierarchy, revealing an interplay between internal geometry, symmetry class, and transport universality.

Introduction

An exact quantum-mechanical treatment of electrons undergoing multiple scattering from a random distribution of impurities is, in general, an intractable many-body problem. The first key conceptual breakthrough came with Anderson
Anderson 1958
;
Abrahams et al. 1979
, who showed localization in a disordered tight-binding model using non-perturbative, self-consistent arguments based on the suppression of wavefunction amplitudes.
Since this seminal work, localization theory has become a cornerstone of condensed matter physics. It is now well established that sufficiently strong disorder can localize electronic wave functions, leading to a disorder-driven metal–insulator transition known as Anderson localization (AL). In the weak-disorder regime, and at sufficiently low temperatures where inelastic scattering is suppressed, a systematic perturbative expansion around the classical Drude conductivity reveals that even the leading quantum correction encodes remarkably rich physics. Specifically, constructive interference between time-reversed scattering paths enhances the return probability, giving rise to weak localization (WL), widely regarded as the precursor to strong localization
Bergmann 1984
;
Lee and Ramakrishnan 1985
;
Akkermans and Montambaux 2007
;
Altshuler et al. 1980
;
Chakravarty and Schmid 1986
. Remarkably, this interference can instead become destructive when the internal geometric structure of Bloch-states imprints a nontrivial phase on time-reversed paths. This possibility was first elucidated by Hikami, Larkin, and Nagaoka in the context of spin-orbit coupled metals
Hikami et al. 1980
, and has since been predicted and observed in systems ranging from graphene to topological insulators and Weyl semimetals
Lu and Shen 2015
;
Suzuura and Ando 2002
;
Khveshchenko 2006
;
McCann et al. 2006
;
Gorbachev et al. 2007
;
Wu et al. 2007
;
Gorbachev et al. 2007
;
Tikhonenko et al. 2008
;
Tikhonenko et al. 2008
;
Tkachov and Hankiewicz 2011
;
Lu et al. 2011
;
Lu et al. 2013
;
Lu and Shen 2014
;
Fu et al. 2019
. The resulting destructive interference manifests as weak antilocalization (WAL). These twin phenomena are now understood as universal infrared signatures of symmetry, encoded in the structure of the
Cooperon
, which is the propagator that describes the coherent return probability via time-reversed paths
Hikami et al. 1980
.

In topological Weyl and Dirac semimetals, the low-energy quasiparticles are no longer conventional Schrödinger electrons with a quadratic dispersion, but are instead massless Dirac fermions possessing a well-defined chirality and a nontrivial topological structure
Castro Neto et al. 2009
;
Armitage et al. 2018
;
Vafek and Vishwanath 2014
. A defining feature of these systems is spin–momentum locking, which perfectly suppresses exact backscattering in the presence of smooth, long-range disorder that preserves chirality. More precisely, intravalley scattering processes acquire a geometric phase of
\pi
between time-reversed paths, leading to destructive interference and hence WAL. In contrast, short-range disorder induces intervalley scattering between nodes of opposite chirality, restoring channels where this phase cancellation is absent and thereby driving the system towards WL-like behavior
Lu and Shen 2015
;
McCann et al. 2006
. Closely related physics arises in three-dimensional topological insulators, where surface states described by a single massless Dirac cone exhibit robust WAL due to spin–momentum locking, but in sufficiently thin films, hybridization between the top and bottom surfaces generates a finite Dirac mass, opening a gap and modifying the Berry phase, leading to a crossover from WAL to WL
Lu et al. 2011
;
Rosen et al. 2019
.

In recent years, a remarkable zoo of gapless fermionic excitations has been discovered in condensed matter systems, which transcends the conventional spin-
1/2
Dirac and Weyl varieties familiar from high-energy physics
Bradlyn et al. 2016
. In crystalline solids, certain symmetries can stabilize multifold band degeneracies (three-, four-, six-fold and so on), whose low-energy descriptions are generalized chiral Hamiltonians characterized by an effective pseudospin-
s

Bradley 1972
;
Wieder et al. 2016
;
Ezawa 2016
;
Bradlyn et al. 2016
;
Chang et al. 2018
. These higher-fold chiral fermions, which have no direct counterpart in relativistic vacuum field theory, exhibit qualitatively new features emerging from their enlarged internal Hilbert space. In particular, they can carry higher topological charge, display unconventional optical selection rules, and host anomalous transport responses that go beyond those of simple Weyl fermions
Ahmad and Sharma 2025
. Such quasiparticles have been experimentally realized in CoSi
Takane et al. 2019
, RhSi
Sanchez et al. 2019
, and AlPt
Schröter et al. 2019
, where spectroscopic probes have investigated the corresponding topological surface states. Complementary platforms have also emerged in engineered systems: for instance, pseudospin-1 excitations, often termed Maxwell fermions, have been proposed and realized in photonic and cold-atom optical lattice settings
Zhu et al. 2017
.

This development raises a natural and largely open question: how does quantum localization evolve across the full pseudospin-
s

This question is compelling not only because multifold fermions continue to proliferate in topological materials, but also because the pseudospin-
s

s

In this work, we develop such a framework for three-dimensional disordered pseudospin-
s
fermions. Starting from a rotationally invariant low-energy Hamiltonian of the form
H\sim\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{S}
, we formulate the problem in the helicity basis, and treat short-range disorder in its most general form rather than helicity preserving. We show that the disorder matrix elements acquire a universal angular structure that can be expressed in terms of Wigner rotation functions

This formulation allows us to obtain compact general expressions for the elastic lifetime, the transport lifetime, and the ladder vertex renormalization for arbitrary pseudospin and helicity bands in the presence of general disorder. In the scalar disorder limit, we find that the semiclassical (Drude) response is strongly pseudospin dependent– increasing

Our work thus provides a unified analytical framework for localization and quantum interference across the full pseudospin-
s

II
Model and symmetry

We consider a system hosting low-energy fermionic excitations of arbitrary pseudospin
-s
, in which the spin degrees of freedom are linearly coupled to the particle momentum. The corresponding low-energy effective Hamiltonian is given by

H_{s}(\mathbf{k})=\hbar v\,\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{S},

(1)

where
v
is a characteristic velocity, and
\mathbf{S}=(S_{x},S_{y},S_{z})
denotes the spin-
s
representation of the SU(2) generators that satisfy the algebra
[S_{i},S_{j}]=i\epsilon_{ijk}S_{k}
, while spin conservation implies
S^{2}=s(s+1)\mathbb{I}
.
Owing to its rotational invariance, the Hamiltonian
depends only on the relative orientation between the spin and momentum degrees of freedom.
One can re-express the Hamiltonian as

H_{s}(\mathbf{k})=\hbar v\,k\,(\hat{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathbf{S});

(2)

the energy spectrum is determined by the
eigenvalues of the spin projection operator and takes the form

E_{s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})=\hbar v\,s^{\prime}k,\qquad s^{\prime}=-s,-s{+}1,\ldots,s-1,s.

(3)

The eigenstates of the Hamiltonian are thus essentially helicity eigenstates. The spectrum consists of
2s{+}1
linearly dispersing bands that intersect at a single
degenerate point
\mathbf{k}=0
. For integer values of
s
, a dispersionless (flat) band
appears at zero energy corresponding to
s^{\prime}=0
, whereas for half-integer
s
, such a band is absent.

The helicity eigenstates, defined up to a momentum-dependent phase, are constructed as

\ket{\mathbf k;s,s'}=R(\hat{\mathbf{k}})\ket{s,s'}=\sum_{m=-s}^{s}D^{s}_{ms^{\prime}}(\phi,\theta)\ket{s,m},

(4)

where the operator
R(\hat{\mathbf{k}})=e^{-i\phi S_{z}}e^{-i\theta S_{y}}
, aligns the
z
axis with the direction
\hat{\mathbf{k}}=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta)
;
D^{s}_{ms^{\prime}}(\phi,\theta)=e^{-im\phi}d^{s}_{ms^{\prime}}(\theta)
, and the reduced Wigner functions
d^{s}_{ms^{\prime}}(\theta)
obey the following orthogonality relations
Sakurai and Napolitano 2020
;
Varshalovich et al. 1988
:

\displaystyle\sum_{m=-s}^{s}d^{s}_{ms^{\prime}}(\theta)d^{s}_{ms^{\prime\prime}}(\theta)=\delta_{s^{\prime}s^{\prime\prime}},

\displaystyle\sum_{s^{\prime}=-s}^{s}d^{s}_{ms^{\prime}}(\theta)d^{s}_{m^{\prime}s^{\prime}}(\theta)=\delta_{mm^{\prime}}.

For the topmost band
s^{\prime}=s
,

d^{s}_{ms}(\theta)=\sqrt{\binom{2s}{s+m}}\!\left(\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\right)^{s+m}\!\left(\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\right)^{s-m},

(5)

whose coefficients are Pascal’s triangle numbers. The general relation for arbitrary
s
and
s^{\prime}
is given in Eq.
56
.
Note that each eigenstate is a coherent superposition of
2s{+}1
spin projections whose relative weights follow Eq.
4
. With increasing
s
, the spinor texture becomes progressively smoother, and the pseudospin winding on the Fermi surface becomes more densely packed. Therefore, near-backscattering (
|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|\sim\pi-\epsilon
where
\epsilon
is a small number) diminishes systematically with increasing
s
, specifically,
\left|\langle\mathbf{k};s,s^{\prime}\mid{\mathbf{k}^{\prime};s,s^{\prime\prime}}\rangle\right|^{2}\sim\left({\epsilon}/{2}\right)^{2|s^{\prime}+s^{\prime\prime}|}
. This trend that is also explicitly reflected in the transport and vertex results we derive later.

It is also fruitful to comment on the behavior of pseudospins by evaluating their fluctuations about the mean value. While the pseudospin has a well-defined projection along its mean direction, the transverse components exhibit fluctuations that grow as
\sim\sqrt{s}
.
Since the magnitude of the pseudospin itself scales as

s
, the relative fluctuations decrease as
\sim 1/\sqrt{s}
As a result, in the large-
s
limit the spinor becomes increasingly sharply localized around its mean direction, recovering the behavior of a spinless metal.

III
Elastic disorder and scattering rates

We introduce a disorder described by

\displaystyle U(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{i}u_{0}\,\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{R}_{i})\mathcal{M},

\displaystyle\qquad\overline{U(\mathbf{r})U(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}=n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\,\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\mathcal{M}^{2},

(6)

where
n_{i}
is the impurity density,
u_{0}
is the scattering strength, and
\mathcal{M}
is a general square matrix of dimension
(2s+1)
and couples the different bands due to its off-diagonal structure.
We expand
\mathcal{M}
in terms of irreducible tensor operators
Edmonds 1996
;
Varshalovich et al. 1988
;
Sakurai and Napolitano 2020
:

\mathcal{M}=\sum_{L=0}^{2s}\sum_{Q=-L}^{L}M_{LQ}\,T_{Q}^{(L)},

(7)

where the tensor operators satisfy

\langle s,s^{\prime\prime}|T^{(L)}_{Q}|s,s^{\prime}\rangle=\sqrt{2L+1}\,(-1)^{s+s^{\prime\prime}-L}\begin{pmatrix}s&L&s\\
s^{\prime}&Q&-s^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix},

(8)

(\cdots)
being the Wigner’s 3-j symbol, which is related to the Clebsch-Gordon coefficients via

\displaystyle C^{\,s\,m^{\prime}}_{\,s\,m,\;L\,Q}=(-1)^{s-L+m^{\prime}}\,\sqrt{2s+1}\,\begin{pmatrix}s&L&s\\
m&Q&-m^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}.

(9)

The disorder-averaged Green’s functions for helicity
s^{\prime}
are given by:

G^{R/A}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k},E)=\frac{1}{E-\hbar vs^{\prime}k\pm i\hbar/(2\tau_{s^{\prime}})},

(10)

where the scattering rate
1/\tau_{s^{\prime}}
is, includes both intra- and interband processes. The self-energy in the first Born approximation is

\Sigma^{R}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})=n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\left|\langle\mathbf{k}^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}|\mathcal{M}|\mathbf{k},s^{\prime}\rangle\right|^{2}G^{0}_{s^{\prime\prime}}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}),

(11)

where
G^{0}_{s^{\prime\prime}}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})
is the bare Green’s function, and the corresponding scattering rate is given by

\frac{1}{\tau_{s^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{k},\omega)=-\frac{2}{\hbar}\mathrm{Im},\Sigma^{R}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k},\omega).

(12)

The overlap can be written in terms of the Wigner
D
-matrix as
Sakurai and Napolitano 2020
;
Varshalovich et al. 1988
;
Edmonds 1996

\langle\mathbf{k}^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}|\mathcal{M}|\mathbf{k},s^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}D^{s*}_{m_{1}s^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime})\,{M}_{m_{1}m_{2}}\,D^{s}_{m_{2}s^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})

(13)

where
M_{m^{\prime}m}=\langle s,m^{\prime}|\mathcal{M}|s,m\rangle
. Employing the properties of Wigner-D functions, and the Tensor operators, we evaluate the following closed-form expression for the scattering time (see Appendix
I
for derivation):

\frac{1}{\tau_{s^{\prime}}}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\frac{1}{(2s+1)^{2}}\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}}N_{F}^{(s^{\prime\prime})}\sum_{Q}|\mathcal{A}_{s^{\prime}}(Q)|^{2},

(14)

where

\mathcal{A}_{s^{\prime}}(Q)=\sum_{L}\sqrt{2L+1}\,M_{LQ}\,C^{\,s,\;s^{\prime}+Q}_{\,s,\;s^{\prime},\;L,\;Q},

(15)

and
N_{F}^{(s^{\prime})}
is the density of states in the helicity band, given by

\displaystyle N_{F}^{(s^{\prime})}=\frac{E_{F}^{2}}{2\pi^{2}(\hbar v)^{3}|s^{\prime}|^{3}}.

(16)

Interestingly, after angular averaging, the scattering process no longer retains information about the specific final helicity state, and the scattering rate is governed only by the phase space available through the density of states. The helicity dependence is instead carried by the symmetry-resolved angular momentum transfer channels
Q
via the Clebsch-Gordon selection rules.

For scalar disorder (
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{I}
),
L=Q=0
are the only nonzero components, and therefore
\mathcal{A}_{s^{\prime}}(Q)=\delta_{Q,0}\,M_{00}\,C^{\,s,\,s^{\prime}}_{\,s,\,s^{\prime},\,0,\,0}
, and

\frac{1}{\tau_{s^{\prime}}}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\frac{1}{(2s+1)}\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}}N_{F}^{(s^{\prime\prime})}.

(17)

The transport scattering time involves an additional angular weighting factor
(1-\cos\theta)
that suppresses forward scattering
Bruus and Flensberg 2004
. For scalar disorder, the valley resolved time is

\frac{1}{\tau^{\mathrm{tr}}_{s^{\prime\prime}\to s^{\prime}}}=2\pi n_{i}u_{0}^{2}N_{F}^{(s^{\prime})}\int d\Omega\,\big|d^{s}_{s^{\prime\prime}s^{\prime}}(\theta)\big|^{2}(1-\cos\theta).

(18)

The angular integrals can be evaluated using the algebra of Wigner

, yielding

\frac{1}{\tau^{\mathrm{tr}}_{s^{\prime}}}=\frac{1}{\tau_{s^{\prime}}}\Big(1-\frac{s^{\prime 2}}{s(s+1)}\Big).

(19)

This reproduces familiar limits:

\tau^{\mathrm{tr}}=(3/2)\tau
for
s=\tfrac{1}{2}
, and

\tau^{\mathrm{tr}}=2\tau
(
\tau^{\mathrm{tr}}=\tau
) for the
s=1
, respectively.
As
s
increases, the prefactor approaches unity, indicating that forward
scattering dominates for higher pseudospin and backscattering
is geometrically suppressed.

IV
Vertex corrections and Ward identity

The impurity potential not only broadens the quasiparticle spectrum but also renormalizes the current operator through multiple scattering.
This renormalization is required by charge conservation and is formally encoded in the Ward identity, which relates the self-energy and the current vertex
Velickỳ 1969
;
Rammer and Smith 1986
.
Diagrammatically, this corresponds to summing the ladder series of impurity lines in the particle–hole channel, leading to a Bethe–Salpeter equation for the dressed current vertex as shown in Fig.
1
(b).

The Ward identity for a disordered system may be written as

\displaystyle\Omega\,\Gamma^{0}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k},\omega;\mathbf{q},\Omega)-\mathbf{q}\cdot\bm{\Gamma}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k},\omega;\mathbf{q},\Omega)=

\displaystyle G^{-1}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\omega+\Omega)-G^{-1}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k},\omega),

(20)

which expresses charge conservation by relating the density and current components of the full vertex to the single-particle Green’s function. In the static, long-wavelength limit, this reduces to

\bm{\Gamma}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})\propto\frac{\partial G^{-1}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k},\omega)}{\partial\mathbf{k}},

(21)

ensuring that the vertex corrections consistently incorporate the same scattering processes that determine the quasiparticle lifetime.

The disorder kernel that enters the self-energy also enters the Bethe-Salpeter equation for the vertex. Defining

W_{s^{\prime}s^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}},\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime})\equiv\big|\langle\mathbf{k},s^{\prime}|\mathcal{M}|\mathbf{k^{\prime}},s^{\prime\prime}\rangle\big|^{2},

(22)

the ladder equation for the
\beta
-component of the vertex reads

\displaystyle\Gamma_{s^{\prime},\beta}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle=v_{s^{\prime},\beta}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle\quad+n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}}\mathcal{J}_{s^{\prime\prime}}\int\frac{d\Omega^{\prime}}{4\pi}\,W_{s^{\prime}s^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}},\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime})\,\Gamma_{s^{\prime\prime},\beta}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}),

(23)

with

\mathcal{J}_{s^{\prime}}=\int\frac{k^{d-1}dk}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,G^{R}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k},E_{F})G^{A}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k},E_{F})=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}N_{F}^{(s^{\prime})}\tau_{s}^{\prime}.

(24)

Within the same Born-plus-ladder approximation, the self-energy and vertex are therefore built from the same impurity correlator, so the construction is conserving and compatible with the Ward identity
Rammer 1991
;
Mahan 2013
.
For a generic matrix disorder
\mathcal{M}
, the kernel
W_{s^{\prime}s^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}},\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime})
is not purely a function of the relative angle, and the dressed vertex is not characterized by a single scalar renormalization factor. The natural generalization is therefore to expand the vertex in spherical harmonics:

\Gamma_{s^{\prime},\beta}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})=\sum_{\ell m}\eta^{(\beta)}_{s^{\prime};\ell m}\,Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}),

(25)

while the bare velocity contains only the
\ell=1
sector. Equation (
23
) then becomes a linear system in the combined band-harmonic space,

\eta^{(\beta)}_{s^{\prime};\ell m}=\eta^{(\beta,0)}_{s^{\prime};\ell m}+\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\mathcal{K}^{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}_{s^{\prime}s^{\prime\prime}}\,\eta^{(\beta)}_{s^{\prime\prime};\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}},

(26)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}_{s^{\prime}s^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\,\mathcal{J}_{s^{\prime\prime}}\int\!\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{d\Omega^{\prime}}{4\pi}\,Y_{\ell m}^{*}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})\,W_{s^{\prime}s^{\prime\prime}}^{\hat{\mathbf{k}},\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}\,Y_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{and\quad}\eta^{(\beta,0)}_{s^{\prime};\ell m}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\,v_{s^{\prime},\beta}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}).

(27)

Thus, the general vertex renormalization is a matrix

\eta^{(\beta)}=\left(\mathds{1}-\hat{\mathcal{K}}\right)^{-1}\eta^{(\beta,0)},

(28)

rather than a single number.
Since our system is isotropic, the dressed vertex remains parallel to the bare velocity and only the
\ell=1
component survives. Then one may write

\Gamma_{s^{\prime},\beta}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})=\eta_{s^{\prime}}\,v_{s^{\prime},\beta}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}),

(29)

and Eq. (
23
) reduces to

\eta_{s^{\prime}}=1+n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\mathcal{J}_{s^{\prime}}\ \int\frac{d\Omega^{\prime}}{4\pi}\,W_{s^{\prime}s^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{k}})\,\cos\theta^{\prime}\,\eta_{s^{\prime}}.

(30)

and the velocity correction factor can be written compactly as

\eta_{s^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{1-\mu_{s^{\prime}}},\qquad\mu_{s^{\prime}}\equiv\frac{\displaystyle\int\frac{d\Omega^{\prime}}{4\pi}\,W_{s^{\prime}s^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{k}})\,\cos\theta^{\prime}}{\displaystyle\int\frac{d\Omega^{\prime}}{4\pi}\,W_{s^{\prime}s^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{k}})}.

(31)

We find the following closed-form of a

3
for complete derivation)

\mu_{s^{\prime}}=\frac{s^{\prime}}{s(s+1)}\,\frac{\sum_{Q}(s^{\prime}+Q)\,|\mathcal{A}_{s^{\prime}}(Q)|^{2}}{\sum_{Q}|\mathcal{A}_{s^{\prime}}(Q)|^{2}}

(32)

where
\mathcal{A}_{s^{\prime}}(Q)
is define in Eq.
15
.

For scalar disorder (
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{I}
), we obtain

\mu_{s^{\prime}}=\frac{s^{\prime 2}}{s(s+1)},\qquad\eta_{s^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{1-\dfrac{s^{\prime 2}}{s(s+1)}}.

(33)

For the outermost helicity band
s^{\prime}=s
,

\eta_{s}=s+1,\qquad\tau_{s}^{\mathrm{tr}}=(s+1)\tau_{s}.

(34)

Focusing on the intraband cases, for
s=\tfrac{1}{2}

\eta=3/2

. For
s=1

\eta=2

Substituting the dressed vertex into the Kubo formula,

\sigma^{(s^{\prime})}_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{e^{2}}{2\pi}\int\!\frac{d^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,v_{\alpha}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})\,G^{R}_{s^{\prime}}\,\Gamma_{s^{\prime},\beta}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})\,G^{A}_{s^{\prime}},

and for a system characterized by an arbitrary pseudospin

,

\displaystyle\sigma_{s^{\prime}}^{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{d}\,v^{2}\,N_{F}^{(s^{\prime})}\,\tau_{s^{\prime}}\,\eta_{s^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=e^{2}N_{F}^{(s^{\prime})}\,\frac{v^{2}\tau^{\mathrm{tr}}_{s^{\prime}}}{d}\equiv e^{2}N_{F}^{(s^{\prime})}D_{s^{\prime}},

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}\hbar v}{6\pi n_{i}u_{0}^{2}}(2s+1)\eta_{s^{\prime}}

(36)

where the transport time is

\tau^{\mathrm{tr}}_{s^{\prime}}=\eta_{s^{\prime}}\,\tau_{s^{\prime}},

(37)

This reproduces the Boltzmann result with the diffusion constant

D_{s^{\prime}}=v^{2}\tau^{\mathrm{tr}}_{s^{\prime}}/d
.
This consistency with the Ward identity reflects the conserving nature of the ladder approximation, ensuring that the same scattering processes that determine the quasiparticle lifetime also control the current response.

V
Diffusion modes and quantum interference

Quantum corrections to the Drude conductivity arise from coherent
backscattering of time-reversed trajectories. The corresponding
diagrams Fig.
1
(c) involve the maximally crossed impurity lines and are described
by the Cooperon propagator, which captures the diffusive dynamics of two
particles moving along time-reversed paths. Unless otherwise specified, from now on, we will restrict our discussion to the presence of a scalar disorder and consider only intraband scattering. In the next section, we discuss the effect of interband as well as intervalley scattering.

Summary of universality

Time-reversal symmetry plays a central role in determining the structure of
quantum-interference corrections to transport. For generalized spin-
s
fermions
with pseudospin
s
, time reversal is implemented as
\mathcal{T}=e^{-i\pi S_{y}}\,\kappa,
where
\kappa
denotes complex conjugation. This operator reverses the
pseudospin according to
\mathcal{T}\,\mathbf{S}\,\mathcal{T}^{-1}=-\mathbf{S}
,
and satisfies
\mathcal{T}^{2}=(-1)^{2s}
.
The sign of the quantum-interference correction is controlled by the factor

\zeta_{s}=\mathrm{sgn}\!\left(\mathcal{T}^{2}\right),

(44)

which distinguishes constructive from destructive interference between
time-reversed scattering paths in the Cooperon channel. Explicitly,

\zeta_{s}=\begin{cases}-1,&s\ \text{is integer (weak localization)},\\[4.0pt]
+1,&s\ \text{is half-integer (weak antilocalization)}.\end{cases}

(45)

For integer pseudospin
s
, one has
\mathcal{T}^{2}=+1
, and the system belongs
to the orthogonal symmetry class. In this case, time-reversed paths interfere
constructively, leading to an enhanced return probability and hence weak
localization correction. In contrast, for half-integer pseudospin
s
, time-reversal
symmetry satisfies
\mathcal{T}^{2}=-1
, placing the system in the symplectic
class. The resulting
\pi
Berry phase accumulated along closed
time-reversed trajectories leads to destructive interference, suppressing
backscattering and giving rise to a weak antilocalization correction.
Importantly, the classification depends only on the parity of
2s
and is
independent of spatial dimensionality, disorder strength, or microscopic
details of the band structure. As a result, the symmetry class and the
corresponding localization behavior are universal features dictated solely by
the algebraic properties of the time-reversal operator. Consequently, the
sequence of localization behavior alternates with increasing pseudospin,
exhibiting WL for
s=1,2,\ldots
and WAL for
s=\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{3}{2},\ldots
. Furthermore,
the magnitude of the correction is independent of the pseudospin
s
or the band index
s^{\prime}
.

Eq. (
36
) demonstrates that the Kubo conductivity is explicitly sensitive to both the pseudospin magnitude and the band index, unlike the magnitude of the localization correction, which is insensitive to both these details. Specifically,
s=s^{\prime}
, yields a scaling relation of
\sigma_{D}^{ss^{\prime}=s}\propto(2s+1)(s+1)
.
Our results indicate that as the pseudospin increases, the Drude conductivity is significantly enhanced relative to conventional
s=1/2
Weyl fermions. This suggests a robust, universal nature of quantum transport corrections in higher-pseudospin systems. We also illustrate this in Fig
2
.

An experimental test of the universality may be realized in multifold chiral semimetals such as CoSi, RhSi, and related compounds that host multiple symmetry-protected multifold fermions. By engineering chemical-potential-tuned series (via substitution, gating, or stoichiometric control), such that transport is dominated by different Fermi-surface pockets with distinct symmetry and angular-momentum structure, as the dominant transport channel is tuned across different pseudospin sectors, the Drude conductivity should vary significantly, but in contrast, the magnitude of the localization correction, extracted from the low-field magnetoconductivity cusps, should remain robust (up to the symmetry-controlled sign and dephasing scale). This separation would manifest as a strongly varying baseline conductivity accompanied by an approximately invariant low-field interference cusp.

VI.1
Effect of interband and intervalley scattering

Until now, we have considered scattering channels that are diagonal, i.e., the band and/or the valley indices do not change. This is not true in general, as disorder can, in principle, couple various bands and valley indices, which changes the helicity and/or chirality states of the pseudospin. For example, the Weyl fermion with pseudospin-
s
, with multiple chirality flavors, may exist at different valleys as expected by the Nielsen-Ninomiya theorem
Nielsen and Ninomiya 1981
, whereas the Weyl fermion with pseudospin-
3/2
has multiple bands crossing the Fermi level at an arbitrary value of chemical potential. This immediately complicates the structure of quantum interference. In the presence of intervalley and interband scattering, the Cooperon is no longer a single scalar object associated with a fixed channel, but instead becomes a matrix in the combined band/valley space. Different scattering processes mix these channels, and the interference correction is governed by the coupled dynamics of all such modes. Consequently, the problem acquires an internal structure analogous to a multi-component diffusion process, where each component corresponds to a distinct sector.

In such a situation, the standard ansatz employed for diagonal scattering, where one assumes a single diffusive pole structure for the Cooperon, ceases to be valid
Akkermans and Montambaux 2007
. The key obstruction is that impurity scattering now generates off-diagonal correlations between different channels, so that a single-mode description cannot capture the full interference physics. Instead, the Cooperon must be determined self-consistently as a matrix object whose components are dynamically coupled through repeated scattering processes.

This naturally leads to a Bethe–Salpeter formulation in which the Cooperon is built up iteratively from the bare disorder correlators. Physically, each iteration corresponds to adding an impurity line, thereby dressing the interference propagator and progressively incorporating the mixing between different band and valley sectors.
In the present notation, the relevant Cooperon propagators
\Gamma^{m,n}_{n,m}
and
\Gamma^{n,n}_{m,m}
(with valley/band indices
m,n
) satisfy

\displaystyle\Gamma^{m,n}_{n,m}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})

\displaystyle=\gamma^{m,n}_{n,m}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})+\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{\nu=m,n}\gamma^{m,\nu}_{n,\bar{\nu}}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle\quad\times G_{\mathbf{k}}^{i\epsilon_{n}}G_{\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{k}}^{i\epsilon_{n}-i\omega_{m}}\Gamma^{\nu,n}_{\bar{\nu},m}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}_{2}),

\displaystyle\Gamma^{n,n}_{m,m}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})

\displaystyle=\gamma^{n,n}_{m,m}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})+\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{\nu=m,n}\gamma^{n,\nu}_{m,\bar{\nu}}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle\quad\times G_{\mathbf{k}}^{i\epsilon_{n}}G_{\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{k}}^{i\epsilon_{n}-i\omega_{m}}\Gamma^{\nu,n}_{\bar{\nu},m}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}_{2}),

(48)

where
\gamma^{\nu\bar{\nu}}_{\bar{\nu}\nu}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})
and
\gamma^{\nu\nu}_{\bar{\nu}\bar{\nu}}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})
are the bare disorder correlators defining the zeroth-order Cooperons:

\displaystyle\gamma^{\nu\bar{\nu}}_{\bar{\nu}\nu}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})

\displaystyle\equiv\langle U^{\nu\bar{\nu}}_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}}\,U^{\nu\bar{\nu}}_{-\mathbf{k}_{1},-\mathbf{k}_{2}}\rangle,

\displaystyle\gamma^{\nu\nu}_{\bar{\nu}\bar{\nu}}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})

\displaystyle\equiv\langle U^{\nu\nu}_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}}\,U^{\bar{\nu}\bar{\nu}}_{-\mathbf{k}_{1},-\mathbf{k}_{2}}\rangle.

VII
Summary and Outlook

In this work, we have developed a unified theory of semiclassical transport and quantum interference for three-dimensional chiral fermions with arbitrary pseudospin-
s
. A central element of our approach is that the disorder is treated at the level of a general matrix structure in pseudospin space, yielding compact arbitrary-
s
expressions for scattering and vertex renormalization, from which the scalar-disorder universality follows as a controlled limit. While the Drude response is strongly pseudospin dependent, reflecting a geometric suppression of backscattering and a systematic enhancement of transport with increasing
s
, the leading quantum-interference correction retains a universal magnitude identical to that of conventional diffusive metals and conventional Weyl-Dirac fermions, with its sign determined solely by the parity of
2s
, placing half-integer (integer) pseudospins in the symplectic (orthogonal) class. Therefore, only a single diffusive mode survives in the long-wavelength limit despite the enlarged internal Hilbert space. At the same time, this universality is fragile, because interband and intervalley scattering render the Cooperon intrinsically multichannel, and we show explicitly for
s=3/2
that channel mixing suppresses weak antilocalization and drives a crossover toward localization, with a scale set by the underlying backscattering probability. These findings place multifold fermions within a coherent localization framework that connects band geometry, symmetry class, and disorder, while opening directions for future work, including the role of electron-electron interactions, anisotropy, and realistic disorder, as well as experimental tests in chiral topological materials such as CoSi, RhSi, and AlPt, where magnetotransport may directly probe the predicted pseudospin-dependent interference physics.

Appendix I
General formalism

1
Hamiltonian and the Wigner-
D
functions

We consider a system with arbitrary pseudospin
s
in which the spin degrees of freedom are linearly coupled to the particle momentum. The corresponding effective Hamiltonian is given by

H_{s}(\mathbf{k})=\hbar v\,\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{S},

(53)

where
\mathbf{S}=(S_{x},S_{y},S_{z})
denotes the spin-
s
representation of the SU(2) generators
and
v
is a characteristic velocity. The eigenspectrum of the Hamiltonian is determined using the rotational invariance

E_{s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})=\hbar v\,s^{\prime}k,\qquad s^{\prime}=-s,-s{+}1,\ldots,s,

(54)

and the eigenstates of
H_{s}
are helicity eigenstates. The spectrum thus consists of
2s{+}1
linearly dispersing bands that intersect at a single
degeneracy point at
\mathbf{k}=0
. For integer values of
s
, a dispersionless (flat) band
appears at zero energy corresponding to
s^{\prime}=0
, whereas for half-integers
s
the spectrum is symmetric about zero energy, and the helicity eigenstates are

\ket{\mathbf k;s,s'}=R(\hat{\mathbf{k}})\ket{s,s'}=\sum_{m=-s}^{s}D^{s}_{m{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s^{\prime}}}(\phi,\theta,0)\ket{s,m},

(55)

with
D^{s}_{ms^{\prime}}(\phi,\theta,0)=e^{-im\phi}d^{s}_{ms^{\prime}}(\theta)
. The reduced Wigner functions
d^{s}_{ms^{\prime}}(\theta)
obey the orthogonality relations

\displaystyle\sum_{m=-s}^{s}d^{s}_{ms^{\prime}}(\theta)d^{s}_{ms^{\prime\prime}}(\theta)=\delta_{s^{\prime}s^{\prime\prime}},

\displaystyle\sum_{s^{\prime}=-s}^{s}d^{s}_{ms^{\prime}}(\theta)d^{s}_{m^{\prime}s^{\prime}}(\theta)=\delta_{mm^{\prime}}.

The closed form of the reduced Wigner functions is:

d^{s}_{ms^{\prime}}(\theta)=\sum_{p}(-1)^{p+m^{\prime}-m}\frac{\sqrt{(s{+}m)!(s{-}m)!(s{+}m^{\prime})!(s{-}m^{\prime})!}}{(s{+}m^{\prime}{-}p)!\,p!\,(m{-}m^{\prime}{+}p)!\,(s{-}m{-}p)!}\big(\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)^{2s+m-m^{\prime}-2p}\big(\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)^{2p+m^{\prime}-m},

(56)

and for the diagonal entries
m=m^{\prime}=s^{\prime}
, the compact ‘Pascal’ form

d^{s}_{s^{\prime}s^{\prime}}(\theta)=\sqrt{\binom{2s}{s{+}s^{\prime}}}\,\big(\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)^{s+s^{\prime}}\big(\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)^{\,s-s^{\prime}}.

(57)

We provide below the explicit polynomials
|d^{s}_{s^{\prime}s^{\prime}}(\theta)|^{2}
up to
s=2
(in
c\equiv\cos\theta
).

\displaystyle s=\tfrac{1}{2}:~

\displaystyle s^{\prime}=\tfrac{1}{2}:~|d|^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+c).

\displaystyle s=1:~

\displaystyle s^{\prime}=\pm 1:~|d|^{2}=\tfrac{1}{4}(1+2c+c^{2}),\qquad s^{\prime}=0:~|d|^{2}=c^{2}.

\displaystyle s=\tfrac{3}{2}:~

\displaystyle s^{\prime}=\tfrac{3}{2}:~|d|^{2}=\big(\tfrac{1+c}{2}\big)^{3}=\tfrac{1}{8}(1+3c+3c^{2}+c^{3}),

\displaystyle s^{\prime}=\tfrac{1}{2}:~|d|^{2}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{(3c-1)^{2}}{4}\cdot\frac{1+c}{2}}.

\displaystyle s=2:~

\displaystyle s^{\prime}=2:~|d|^{2}=\big(\tfrac{1+c}{2}\big)^{4}=\tfrac{1}{16}(1+4c+6c^{2}+4c^{3}+c^{4}),

\displaystyle s^{\prime}=1:~|d|^{2}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Big[\tfrac{1}{4}(4c-2)\cdot(\tfrac{1+c}{2})^{2}\Big]^{2}}

\displaystyle s^{\prime}=0:~|d|^{2}=\tfrac{1}{4}(3c^{2}-1)^{2}=\tfrac{1}{4}(9c^{4}-6c^{2}+1).

Density of States:
The density of states (DOS) at the Fermi surface is calculated as

\displaystyle N_{F}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\delta(E_{F}-\epsilon_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\phi}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\sin\theta\,d\theta}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{k^{2}\,dk}{2\pi}\delta(E_{F}-\epsilon_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}})

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi}{8\pi^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}k^{2}\,dk\delta(E_{F}-\epsilon_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}})

(58)

Since DOS varies by band. In general, we consider the dispersion relation in Eq.
3
as

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=s^{\prime}\hbar\vartheta k

\displaystyle d\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=s^{\prime}\hbar\vartheta dk

Put the value of
\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}
and
d\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}
in Eq.
58
,

\displaystyle N_{F}^{s^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int\frac{\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}d\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}}{(s^{\prime}\hbar\vartheta)^{3}}\delta(E_{F}-\epsilon_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}})

\displaystyle N_{F}^{s^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{E_{F}^{2}}{2\pi^{2}(s^{\prime}\hbar\vartheta)^{3}}

(59)

2
Scattering time in the presence of general disorder
\mathcal{M}

The spinor overlap between the states
\mathbf{k}
and
\mathbf{k^{\prime}}
, reflecting the rotation of one helicity state into another, is defined as:

\displaystyle W_{s^{\prime\prime}s^{\prime}}(\theta,\phi)=\left|\langle\mathbf{k}^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}|\mathcal{M}|\mathbf{k},s^{\prime}\rangle\right|^{2},

(60)

where
\mathcal{M}
is the impurity matrix, and
|{\mathbf{k},s,s^{\prime}}\rangle
is a helicity eigenstate, given in Eq.
4
.
The overlap can be written in terms of the Wigner
D
-matrix as
Sakurai and Napolitano 2020

\langle\mathbf{k}^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}|\mathcal{M}|\mathbf{k},s^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}D^{s*}_{m_{1}s^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime})\,{M}_{m_{1}m_{2}}\,D^{s}_{m_{2}s^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})

(61)

where
M_{m^{\prime}m}=\langle s,m^{\prime}|\mathcal{M}|s,m\rangle
.
The scattering rate for
|\mathbf{k},s^{\prime}\rangle
in the first-Born approximation is

\frac{1}{\tau_{s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}}\int\frac{d^{3}k^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{3}}W_{s^{\prime\prime}s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{k})\delta(\epsilon_{s^{\prime\prime}}(k^{\prime})-\epsilon_{s^{\prime}}(k)).

(62)

Since our system is isotropic, this becomes

\frac{1}{\tau_{s^{\prime}}}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}}N_{F}^{(s^{\prime\prime})}\int\frac{d\Omega_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{4\pi}\left|\langle(\mathbf{\hat{k}}^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}|\mathcal{M}|\mathbf{\hat{k}},s^{\prime}\rangle\right|^{2}.

(63)

Using Eq.
61
, we obtain

\frac{1}{\tau_{s^{\prime}}}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}}N_{F}^{(s^{\prime\prime})}\int\frac{d\Omega_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{4\pi}\left|\sum_{m^{\prime},m}D^{s*}_{m^{\prime}s^{\prime\prime}}(\mathbf{\hat{k}}^{\prime})M_{m^{\prime}m}D^{s}_{ms^{\prime}}(\mathbf{\hat{k}})\right|^{2}

(64)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau_{s^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}}N_{F}^{(s^{\prime\prime})}\int\frac{d\Omega_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{4\pi}\left|\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}D^{s*}_{m_{1}s^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime})\,M_{m_{1}m_{2}}\,D^{s}_{m_{2}s^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}}N_{F}^{(s^{\prime\prime})}\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}}M_{m_{1}m_{2}}M^{*}_{n_{1}n_{2}}D^{s}_{m_{2}s^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})D^{s*}_{n_{2}s^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})\int\frac{d\Omega_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{4\pi}D^{s*}_{m_{1}s^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime})D^{s}_{n_{1}s^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}).

(65)

Now, using the angular orthogonality relation

\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}D^{s*}_{ms^{\prime\prime}}(\Omega)\,D^{s}_{ns^{\prime\prime}}(\Omega)=\frac{\delta_{mn}}{2s+1},

(66)

the
m_{1},n_{1}
sums collapse and we get

\frac{1}{\tau_{s^{\prime}}}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\frac{1}{2s+1}\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}}N_{F}^{(s^{\prime\prime})}\sum_{m_{1}}\sum_{m_{2},n_{2}}M_{m_{1}m_{2}}M^{*}_{m_{1}n_{2}}D^{s}_{m_{2}s^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})D^{s*}_{n_{2}s^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}).

(67)

Defining
(\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M})_{n_{2}m_{2}}=\sum_{m_{1}}M^{*}_{m_{1}n_{2}}M_{m_{1}m_{2}},
, we get

\frac{1}{\tau_{s^{\prime}}}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\frac{1}{2s+1}\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}}N_{F}^{(s^{\prime\prime})}\sum_{m_{2},n_{2}}D^{s}_{m_{2}s^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})(\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M})_{n_{2}m_{2}}D^{s*}_{n_{2}s^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}).

(68)

Equivalently, we may write

\frac{1}{\tau_{s^{\prime}}}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\frac{1}{2s+1}\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}}N_{F}^{(s^{\prime\prime})}\langle{\hat{\mathbf{k}},s^{\prime}}|\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M}|{\hat{\mathbf{k}},s^{\prime}}\rangle.

(69)

Since the system is isotropic, the scattering time should not depend on the direction
\hat{\mathbf{k}}
and hence we may choose
|\hat{\mathbf{k}},s^{\prime}\rangle=|s,s^{\prime}\rangle
.
Then Eq. (
69
)
reduces to

\frac{1}{\tau_{s^{\prime}}}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\frac{1}{2s+1}\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}}N_{F}^{(s^{\prime\prime})}\langle s,s^{\prime}|\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M}|s,s^{\prime}\rangle.

(70)

We now expand the impurity matrix in irreducible tensor (or polarization) operators
Varshalovich et al. 1988
,

\mathcal{M}=\sum_{L=0}^{2s}\sum_{Q=-L}^{L}M_{LQ}\,T_{Q}^{(L)},

(71)

where
M_{LQ}
are the corresponding tensor amplitudes. Using
Varshalovich et al. 1988

\langle s,m^{\prime}|T_{Q}^{(L)}|s,m\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{2L+1}{2s+1}}\,C^{\,s\,m^{\prime}}_{\,s\,m,\;L\,Q},

(72)

where

\displaystyle C^{\,s\,m^{\prime}}_{\,s\,m,\;L\,Q}=(-1)^{s-L+m^{\prime}}\,\sqrt{2s+1}\,\begin{pmatrix}s&L&s\\
m&Q&-m^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}

(73)

is the Wigner-3j symbol.
Using
T_{Q}^{(L)\dagger}=(-1)^{Q}T_{-Q}^{(L)}

Varshalovich et al. 1988
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\langle s,s^{\prime}|\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M}|s,s^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{LQ}\sum_{L^{\prime}Q^{\prime}}M_{LQ}^{*}M_{L^{\prime}Q^{\prime}}\langle s,s^{\prime}|T_{Q}^{(L)\dagger}T_{Q^{\prime}}^{(L^{\prime})}|s,s^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{LQ}\sum_{L^{\prime}Q^{\prime}}M_{LQ}^{*}M_{L^{\prime}Q^{\prime}}(-1)^{Q}\langle s,s^{\prime}|T_{-Q}^{(L)}T_{Q^{\prime}}^{(L^{\prime})}|s,s^{\prime}\rangle.

(74)

Now, inserting a complete set of
|s,m\rangle
states,

\langle s,s^{\prime}|T_{-Q}^{(L)}T_{Q^{\prime}}^{(L^{\prime})}|s,s^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{m=-s}^{s}\langle s,s^{\prime}|T_{-Q}^{(L)}|s,m\rangle\langle s,m|T_{Q^{\prime}}^{(L^{\prime})}|s,s^{\prime}\rangle.

(75)

Using the Clebsch-Gordan selection rules, we have

m=s^{\prime}+Q=s^{\prime}+Q^{\prime}
, hence
Q^{\prime}=Q
. Therefore,

\langle s,s^{\prime}|\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M}|s,s^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{Q}\sum_{L,L^{\prime}}M_{LQ}^{*}M_{L^{\prime}Q}\,\mathcal{K}_{LL^{\prime}}^{(s,s^{\prime};Q)},

(76)

where

\mathcal{K}_{LL^{\prime}}^{(s,s^{\prime};Q)}=\frac{\sqrt{(2L+1)(2L^{\prime}+1)}}{2s+1}\,C^{\,s,\;s^{\prime}+Q}_{\,s,\;s^{\prime},\;L,\;Q}\,C^{\,s,\;s^{\prime}+Q}_{\,s,\;s^{\prime},\;L^{\prime},\;Q}.

(77)

Hence, the scattering rate becomes

\frac{1}{\tau_{s^{\prime}}}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\frac{1}{(2s+1)^{2}}\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}}N_{F}^{(s^{\prime\prime})}\sum_{Q}\sum_{L,L^{\prime}}\sqrt{(2L+1)(2L^{\prime}+1)}\,M_{LQ}^{*}M_{L^{\prime}Q}\,C^{\,s,\;s^{\prime}+Q}_{\,s,\;s^{\prime},\;L,\;Q}\,C^{\,s,\;s^{\prime}+Q}_{\,s,\;s^{\prime},\;L^{\prime},\;Q}.

(78)

This can be further simplified to

\frac{1}{\tau_{s^{\prime}}}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\frac{1}{(2s+1)^{2}}\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}}N_{F}^{(s^{\prime\prime})}\sum_{Q}|\mathcal{A}_{s^{\prime}}(Q)|^{2},

(79)

where

\mathcal{A}_{s^{\prime}}(Q)=\sum_{L}\sqrt{2L+1}\,M_{LQ}\,C^{\,s,\;s^{\prime}+Q}_{\,s,\;s^{\prime},\;L,\;Q},

(80)

We may now apply the above result to some specific cases:

(i) Scalar disorder:
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{I}_{2s+1\times 2s+1}

For scalar disorder, only the
L=0

Q=0

M_{00}\neq 0,\qquad M_{LQ}=0\ \text{otherwise}.

(81)

Thus,

\mathcal{A}_{s^{\prime}}(Q)=\delta_{Q,0}\,M_{00}\,C^{\,s,\,s^{\prime}}_{\,s,\,s^{\prime},\,0,\,0}.

(82)

Using
C^{\,s,\,s^{\prime}}_{\,s,\,s^{\prime},\,0,\,0}=1
,
we obtain

\mathcal{A}_{s^{\prime}}(0)=M_{00}=1,\qquad\mathcal{A}_{s^{\prime}}(Q\neq 0)=0.

(83)

Therefore,

\frac{1}{\tau_{s^{\prime}}}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\frac{1}{(2s+1)}\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}}N_{F}^{(s^{\prime\prime})}.

(84)

(85)

For

\displaystyle=M_{00}+\sqrt{3}\,M_{10}\,C^{\,\tfrac{1}{2},\,\tfrac{1}{2}}_{\,\tfrac{1}{2},\,\tfrac{1}{2},\,1,\,0},

(86)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{3}\,M_{1,-1}\,C^{\,\tfrac{1}{2},\,-\tfrac{1}{2}}_{\,\tfrac{1}{2},\,\tfrac{1}{2},\,1,\,-1}.

(87)

For

\displaystyle=M_{00}+\sqrt{3}\,M_{10}\,C^{\,\tfrac{1}{2},\,-\tfrac{1}{2}}_{\,\tfrac{1}{2},\,-\tfrac{1}{2},\,1,\,0},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{-}(+1)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{3}\,M_{1,+1}\,C^{\,\tfrac{1}{2},\,\tfrac{1}{2}}_{\,\tfrac{1}{2},\,-\tfrac{1}{2},\,1,\,+1}.

(89)

3
Vertex corrections and Kubo conductivity for general disorder
\mathcal{M}

For the short-range disorder potential, the renormalized vertex for helicity
s^{\prime}
is found by solving the ladder equation,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{s^{\prime},\beta}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})=v_{s^{\prime},\beta}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})\quad+n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}}\mathcal{J}_{s^{\prime\prime}}\int\frac{d\Omega^{\prime}}{4\pi}\,W_{s^{\prime}s^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}},\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime})\,\Gamma_{s^{\prime\prime},\beta}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}),

(106)

with

\mathcal{J}_{s^{\prime}}=\int\frac{k^{d-1}dk}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,G^{R}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k},E_{F})G^{A}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k},E_{F})=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}N_{F}^{(s^{\prime})}\tau_{s}^{\prime}.

(107)

Within the same Born-plus-ladder approximation, the self-energy and vertex are therefore built from the same impurity correlator, so the construction is conserving and compatible with the Ward identity
Rammer 1991
;
Mahan 2013
.
For a generic matrix disorder
\mathcal{M}
, the spinor overlap
W_{s^{\prime}s^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime},\hat{\mathbf{k}})
, Eq.
60
, is not purely a function of the relative angle, and the dressed vertex is not characterized by a single scalar renormalization factor. The natural generalization is to expand the vertex in spherical harmonics,

\Gamma_{s^{\prime},\beta}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})=\sum_{\ell m}\eta^{(\beta)}_{s^{\prime};\ell m}\,Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}).

(108)

The bare velocity transforms as a vector under rotations and therefore resides entirely in the
l=1
angular momentum sector; this follows from the fact that the components of
\hat{k}
are linear combinations of the spherical harmonics
Y_{1m}
.
Equation (
106
) becomes:

\eta^{(\beta)}_{s^{\prime};\ell m}=\eta^{(\beta,0)}_{s^{\prime};\ell m}+\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\mathcal{K}^{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}_{s^{\prime}s^{\prime\prime}}\,\eta^{(\beta)}_{s^{\prime\prime};\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}},

(109)

with kernel

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{\ell m,\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}_{s^{\prime}s^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=n_{i}u_{0}^{2}\,\mathcal{J}_{s^{\prime\prime}}\int\!\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\frac{d\Omega^{\prime}}{4\pi}\,Y_{\ell m}^{*}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})\,W_{s^{\prime}s^{\prime\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}},\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime})\,Y_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}),

(110)

and

\eta^{(\beta,0)}_{s^{\prime};\ell m}=\int\frac{d\Omega}{4\pi}\,v_{s^{\prime},\beta}(\hat{\mathbf{k}})Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\mathbf{k}}).

(111)

In general, the scattering amplitude between the initial and final states is calculated as

Figure 5:
(a) Magnetoconductivity for pseudospin

fermions with intervalley scattering, (b) Magnetoconductivity for pseudospin
s=1
fermions with intervalley scattering. The black curve corresponds to the

.

In the absence of intervalley scattering (
\eta_{I}=0
), the pseudospin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
Dirac system, which belongs to the symplectic class (
\mathcal{T}^{2}=-1
), exhibits the familiar weak-antilocalization (positive magnetoconductivity) at low magnetic field. In contrast, the pseudospin-1 system is in the orthogonal class (
\mathcal{T}^{2}=+1
) and shows a weak-localization (negative magnetoconductivity) in the intravalley channel. These behaviors are evident in Fig. 1(a, b), where the black intravalley curve for pseudospin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
has a sharp positive peak, whereas for pseudospin-1 it has a negative cusp.

When intervalley scattering is turned on (
\eta_{I}>0
), the low-field quantum correction is steadily suppressed. For the symplectic pseudospin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
case, the WAL peak is gradually reduced, and eventually changes sign: we find a WAL
\rightarrow
WL crossover around
\eta_{I}\approx 0.71
. By contrast, the orthogonal pseudospin-1 system simply loses its interference correction as
\eta_{I}\rightarrow 1
, with no sign change. Thus, the pseudospin-1 magnetoconductivity is monotonically suppressed to zero with increasing
\eta_{I}
, consistent with earlier results
Miao et al. 2023
;
Lu and Shen 2015
.
