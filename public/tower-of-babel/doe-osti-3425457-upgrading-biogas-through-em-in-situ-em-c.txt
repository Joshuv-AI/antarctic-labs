Executive Summary:
 Organic waste streams generated by wastewater treatment plants, agricultural
operations, and food processing industries represent an important yet underutilized
opportunity for renewable energy production in the United States. Through anaerobic
digestion, these waste streams can produce biogas, a mixture primarily composed of
methane (CH4) and carbon dioxide (CO2), that can be upgraded to pipeline-quality natural
gas. However, most existing upgrading technologies remove CO2 from biogas rather than
utilizing it, leaving a significant portion of the potential energy unused. This project
investigates a novel biological upgrading approach that converts CO2 into additional CH4
by supplying hydrogen (H2) to specialized microorganisms capable of performing
hydrogenotrophic methanation.
 The main challenges associated with biological biogas upgrading are related to
hydrogen supply, gas-liquid mass transfer, and process stability. First, due to the high
cost of hydrogen gas, it is preferable that H2 be produced on-site using renewable energy
sources such as wind or solar power. Second, hydrogen has low solubility in liquids, which
limits its availability to microorganisms and requires strategies to improve gas dissolution
and transfer within the reactor. Third, process inhibition may occur as a result of increased
pH caused by CO2 consumption or elevated H2 partial pressure, both of which can
negatively affect methanogenic activity. Although research in these areas has advanced
during the course of this project, these challenges have not yet been fully resolved. To
date, the biological systems that have achieved the highest methane concentrations are
typically ex-situ reactors, where operational conditions can be more easily controlled. For
this reason, the findings of the present project remain highly relevant.
 The project goal was to develop an innovative system that can accomplish biogas
upgrading via biological conversion of CO2 to CH4, in a novel hybrid approach that
combines the advantages of both in-situ and ex-situ systems. The proposed system
employs a three-phase upflow anaerobic bioreactor with H2 delivery through a gas-
permeable membrane, enabling efficient hydrogen transfer and microbial conversion.
Under optimized operating conditions, the system achieved 99% H2 consumption and
90% CO2 conversion. A subsequent gas cleaning stage was implemented to further
improve gas quality and meet target purity standards. The upgraded gas composition
reached 97.7% CH4, 2.2% CO2, and 0.97% O2, while H2S concentrations remained below
detection limits. In addition, a flue gas-driven inorganic thermoelectric generator (TEG)
system was designed and experimentally validated as a potential source of electricity for
H2 production. The system consisted of six TEG modules connected in series and
achieved an open-circuit voltage of 4.5 V and a maximum power output of 224 mW at a
temperature difference of approximately 53.5 °C, demonstrating effective conversion of
waste heat into electrical power under simulated flue gas conditions. Finally, a
comprehensive techno-economic analysis was completed to evaluate the capital and
operating costs associated with the proposed system. The results provide important
insights to guide future scale-up, optimization, and potential deployment of integrated
biological biogas upgrading technologies.

1. Background
 Anaerobic digestion (AD) is the most popular method for converting organic wastes
into bioenergy in the form of biogas. Energy production via biogas combustion releases
CO2 at a rate of 84 kg per GJ, much lower than 741 kg per GJ from diesel, 733 kg per GJ
from crude oil, or 774 kg per GJ from fuel oils. Thus, biogas-based energy can reduce
greenhouse gas emission compared to fossil fuels. According to American Biogas
Council, the U.S. has nearly 2,500 biogas systems across all the 50 states, with potential
to add another (nearly) 17,000 AD systems to increase biogas production 1. However, the
energy content of biogas (20-25 MJ/m3) is lower than that of methane (36 MJ/m3 at STP),
requiring significant purification and upgrading to be used as transportation fuel or
supplied to natural gas pipelines. Biogas typically consists of 50-70% CH4 and 30-50%
CO2 with minor concentrations of other gases, and CO2 must be reduced or removed to
increase biogas heating capacity. There has been an increasing interest in upgrading
biogas to have >95% CH4, named “biomethane” or “renewable natural gas (RNG)”.
According to Renewable Natural Gas Database developed and maintained by Argonne
National Laboratory, by the end of 2023, there are 305 RNG plants under operation and
237 plants under construction/being planned in the U.S. 2.
 Nowadays, most RNG plants use chemical or physical processes for biogas
upgrading, such as pressure swing adsorption (PSA), chemical scrubbing, or membrane
separation, all of which remove CO2 that must undergo further post-treatment. A more
sustainable approach to biogas upgrading is to convert CO 2 into CH4, thereby reducing
greenhouse gas emissions and increasing energy content. This conversion can be
achieved either chemically or biologically 3. Chemical conversion is based on the Sabatier
reaction in which metal catalysts drive the reaction of CO2 and H2 to form CH4 under a
high temperature/pressure. Biological conversion is based on the addition of external
hydrogen to promote the activity of hydrogenotrophic methanogens, which biologically
convert CO2 and H2 into CH4 under mild conditions through microbial metabolism 4. The
conversion of CO2 to CH4 can be performed in-situ or ex-situ. Although the ex-situ
conversion exhibits a higher conversion efficiency, the in-situ conversion has a much
smaller footprint and capital investment because the same reactor is used for both AD
and biogas upgrading. A lower capital cost is especially important to promoting new
technology.
 Pilot-scale demonstrations of in-situ and ex-situ biological biogas upgrading have
been conducted in Germany, Denmark, Japan, and Switzerland. Among these, ex-situ
configurations achieved the highest methane concentrations (~97%) 5,6, while in-situ
systems reached around 83.2% CH4, primarily due to incomplete hydrogen utilization 7.
To enhance H2 uptake, studies have combined the use of small-diameter spargers, which
reduce H2 bubble formation, with gas recirculation to maximize H2 mass transfer to the
liquid phase 8,9. These approaches achieved about 98% H2 consumption, resulting in a
CH4 content of ~91% 8,9, emphasizing the need for proper H2 delivery methods. Other
studies applied overpressurization to address the solubility limitation, enabling CH 4
concentrations of 92.9% 10.
 In addition to the low solubility of H2, in-situ upgrading also faces challenges related
to process inhibition. Elevated H2 partial pressure can inhibit hydrogenotrophic activity,
and the upgrading process itself shifts the carbonate equilibrium by consuming dissolved
CO2, which leads to H+ consumption, alkalinity depletion, and an increase in pH, which

can further suppress methanogenic activity 11. Strategies to address this issue include
gradual microbial adaptation to elevated pH and H2 levels, and manual pH adjustment
through the addition of HCl. However, relying on these strategies at full scale may be
problematic due to the high chemical demand and associated operational costs.
Moreover, the source of H2 remains one of the main bottlenecks in biological biogas
upgrading, as the process relies on large and continuous amounts of H 2. For example, if
a digester produces 100 Nm3/h of biogas with 40% CO2, converting all the CO2 would
theoretically require 160 Nm3/h of H2, and producing and supplying this volume of H2 at
scale is difficult and costly.
 Despite some success with the in-situ biological conversion of CO2 to CH4, these
challenges still limit full-scale applicability. To address these limitations, this project
proposes and validates a three-phase biogas upgrading reactor that integrates the
advantages of in-situ and ex-situ configurations in a novel hybrid approach. While
previous studies have explored hybrid systems by coupling an in-situ reactor with a
separate ex-situ unit 8, the proposed design introduces a new concept in which the
digestate itself serves as the nutrient medium for the “ex-situ” upgrading process within a
second compartment of the same reactor.
 For a low-cost H2 source, the system also integrates an energy recovery strategy
in which a portion of the produced biomethane is used for heat generation. This heat is
recovered through an organic thermoelectric generator (TEG) that will be coupled to a
microbial electrochemical system (MES), of which microbial electrolysis cell (MEC) is the
specific configuration employed in this study. This integrated approach has the potential
to directly address one of the main challenges faced on a pilot scale, which is the lack of
a low-cost hydrogen source. In this system, hydrogen can be produced on-site by utilizing
waste heat as a renewable energy source for green H2 production.

2. Project Objectives:
2.1. Goals and Expected Outcomes
 The project goal was to develop an innovative system that can accomplish biogas
upgrading via biological conversion of CO2 to CH4, in a novel hybrid approach that
combines the advantages of both in-situ and ex-situ systems. To achieve this goal, we
created different function zones within an anaerobic digester (AD) that can perform biogas
production, CO2 conversion, and gas/liquid separation without significantly affecting each
other. The project integrated several existing processes/technologies, such as membrane
supplied H2, thermoelectricity, and microbial electrochemical systems, in innovative and
synergistic ways to ensure both novelty and a high chance of success.
 The expected outcome was to have a scalable and innovative biogas upgrading
system at TRL-4, that can produce pipeline quality RNG containing >97% CH4 via two
steps: biological CO2 conversion to CH4 that generates a biogas of >95% CH4 and gas
cleaning that reduces impurities and further enhances the CH4 content to >97%. The
target CO2 concentration in the final RNG is <1%. The H2S content will be kept below 5.7
mg/Nm3 (or 0.25 grain/110 scf).
 By the end of the project, a comprehensive framework integrating both the biofilm
and system models is expected to be developed. Here, the biofilm refers to the microbial
community attached to the gas-permeable silicone membrane within the reactor, where
hydrogen is supplied. This immobilized microbial community is expected to support high

metabolic activity and serve as a retention mechanism for indigenous methanogens that
directly consume H2. In addition, the LCA and TEA models will be finalized to provide
insights that can guide the next stage of development.

2.2. Impact on U.S. Clean Energy and Economic Benefits
 This approach can improve the energy efficiency of waste-to-energy systems and
contribute to national efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and advance the
transition toward net-zero emissions by 2050. In addition to environmental benefits, the
application of biological upgrading can also provide economic advantages. For
illustration, a typical mid-size WWTP producing approximately 280 Nm3/h of biogas may
generate a gas stream composed of about 65% CH4 and 35% CO2. Under conventional
upgrading processes, the CO2 fraction is removed, resulting in a methane output of
approximately 182 Nm3/h. However, if the CO2 were biologically converted into CH4
instead of being separated from the gas stream, this could theoretically generate an
additional 80-95 Nm3/h of CH4, increasing total methane production to approximately 260-
275 Nm3/h. This represents a potential increase of around 45-50% in methane yield from
the same biogas stream, which could substantially improve the overall energy recovery
and economic value of anaerobic digestion systems.

3. Project Results and Discussion:
 This section summarizes the technical progress achieved during the reporting period
in relation to milestones defined in the Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO).

3.1. Task 1: Initial technical verification
 At the beginning of the project, the baseline data and project targets were verified
through literature analysis and limited experiments. Baseline technologies include AD
with non-membrane supplied H2 (for biogas upgrading), commercially available activated
carbon and molecular sieve adsorbents (for gas cleaning), and poly(3,4-
ethylenedioxythiophene)-poly(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT; for TEG). Because the

 Page 9 of 52

 Zhen He

proposed system is innovative and had not been demonstrated before, determination of
baseline performance was based on the analysis of individual unit (state of art
performance) and reasonable estimation of the system performance after individual units
are synergistically connected. The individual units analyzed included AD, biological CO 2
conversion to CH4, gas cleaning, TEG, and MEC. Limited experiments about AD, MEC
and TEG were conducted at the on-site verification visit to replicate the benchmark data.
Upon successful completion of the initial verification effort and Go/No-Go decision point,
the project commenced with work as discussed in the following tasks. The milestone 1.1
was to complete literature analysis, establishment of baseline performance for CO2
conversion to CH4 and biogas cleaning, and verification of research facility/capability for
conducting the proposed research. The Budget Period 1 Go/No-Go Decision Point was
based on the baseline performance metrics: 80% CO2 conversion, 80% H2 utilization,
zT>0.1; in RNG: CO2 < 5%, O2 < 2%, and H2S < 20 mg/Nm3.

3.2. Task 2: Demonstration of in-situ CO2 conversion to CH4
 Task 2 was composed of two subtasks. Task 2.1 focused on the development of
the biogas upgrading reactor in a unique design that combines both “in situ” and “ex situ”
conversion in the same reactor and included a total of six milestones (Milestone 2.1.1 to
Milestone 2.1.6). Task 2.2 focused on microbiome characterization across the different
reactor zones, as well as analysis of the biofilm formed on the permeable membrane used
for H2 delivery. A detailed description of the results and milestone achievements is
provided below for each subtask.

[Milestone 2.1.1] Completion of system setup with gas-permeable membrane unit
to create different operational zones

 The reactor setup was completed at the end of 2022 and comprised three distinct
zones: (1) anaerobic digestion, (2) membrane (bioconversion zone), and (3) gas-liquid
separation as shown in Figure 1. The reactor was inoculated with anaerobic sludge
obtained from an anaerobic digester at the Missouri River Wastewater Treatment Plant
(St. Louis, MO, USA). Synthetic brewery wastewater was initially used as the feed; it
mainly consisted of sucrose and nutrients, resulting in a COD of 4950 mg/L and a pH of
7.9. The influent was introduced at the lower side of the reactor and withdrawn from the
top of the bioconversion zone in continuous mode at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. The
effective working volume was 2.8 L, corresponding to a hydraulic retention time (HRT) of
3.5 days. The short HRT is attributed to the readily biodegradable, low-solids synthetic
feed, which enables faster degradation compared to conventional feedstocks that contain
more complex structures, where hydrolysis is the rate-limiting step. A detailed description
of the reactor setup can be found in the paper entitled “Biological upgrading of biogas
assisted with membrane-supplied hydrogen gas in a three-phase upflow reactor,”
published in Bioresource Technology 12.

increase in CH4 concentration. The system operated stably under varying substrate, HRT,

[Milestone 2.1.4] Establish the system coordination between TEG and MEC

 The organic Thermoelectric Generator (TEG) developed in Task 3 was originally
intended for integration with MEC. However, due to logistical constraints, a commercial
module was used to demonstrate the coupling. The organic TEG from task 3 achieved an
open-circuit voltage (OCV) of 5 V and approximately 2 V under load at a temperature
difference of 100 °C, exceeding the voltage requirements for effective MEC operation. To
emulate this performance under controlled conditions, the commercial module was
systematically characterized across a range of operating conditions, including different

a more scalable solution for on-site H2 generation. Unlike biological routes, electrolysis is
not constrained by microbial activity and has already been demonstrated at industrially

[Milestone 3.1.1] Virtually screen the conjugated polymers using supervised

 The original plan for this milestone was to perform virtual screening of conjugated
polymers by constructing a dataset from polymer databases (e.g., PoLyInfo), generating
molecular fingerprints, and applying machine learning models to predict thermoelectric
properties. The objective was to identify candidate materials with optimized performance
for enhanced zT, but this approach was limited by the scarcity of reliable datasets for
thermoelectric properties and the limited availability of stable high-performance n-type
systems. Therefore, the project direction shifted toward experimental validation of well-
established material systems at an early stage. During the candidate selection phase, a
reliable measurement platform was established to accurately characterize electrical
conductivity and Seebeck coefficient (Figure 6). In combination with the lab’s Transient
Thermal Grating (TTG) system (Figure 7) for in-plane thermal conductivity measurement,
this enabled direct and comprehensive evaluation of thermoelectric performance, rather
than relying solely on simulation or predictive models.

 Despite these improvements, the overall zT did not reach 0.1. These results

 Further testing with varying CO2 partial pressure revealed further insights into the
sorbent behavior. The CO2 adsorption capacity was found to increase with CO2 partial
pressure, as shown by comparing the blue and oranges bars in Figure 22. This is
presumably due to the increase in driving force for CO2 diffusion into the pore space.
 Interestingly, a substantially higher adsorption capacity was measured upon
replacement of N2 with CH4 (to simulate biogas). The measured capacity (~9.5 mmol
CO2/g) is on par with the best available materials (see Table 4) and well above the final
performance target of 2.0 mmol CO2/g. The working hypothesis for this result is that
                                      Page 30 of 52

methane is reactive at the surface of MgO and catalyzes the carbonation reaction: MgO
+ CO2 ↔ MgCO3. There is also evidence in the literature that moisture assists with
carbonation reactions. Therefore, prior testing under dry CO2/N2 environments may have
resulted in incomplete chemisorption. This could explain the relatively modest result
obtained by TGA earlier in the project (~ 1 mmol/g) which is on par with physical
adsorption on activated carbon.

 Testing also revealed issues with maintaining high adsorption capacity upon
cycling (for example, see comparison between the orange and green bars, and the purple
and red bars in Figure 22). We believe this is not caused by material degradation, but
rather due to unoptimized procedures for the desorption step. We hypothesize that
moisture or methane may be needed during desorption to catalyze the reverse
carbonation reaction, just as it does the forward reaction. Measurements of CO2
concentrations during desorption (not shown) confirm that regeneration was not
complete. Further testing is needed to improve decarbonation. Nonetheless, the results
demonstrate effective CO2 removal in simulated biogas, and the potential for high
adsorption capacity. Unfortunately, the project period ended before our MgO pellets could
be directly tested on biogas produced from the AD.
 In parallel to the sorbent synthesis and characterization work, the overall approach
to final biogas purification using sorbents was tested on real biogas using commercial
sorbents. A three-stage sorbent column was integrated with the AD reactor. The first
stage consisted of silica gel to remove moisture (after cold trap to promote condensation).
The second stage consisted of Zeolite 13X to target CO2. The final stage consisted of a
relatively small bed of copper-impregnated activated carbon to remove all other trace
contaminants and H2S. This column was integrated with the reactor for much of the overall
project test campaign. The concentrations of methane, carbon dioxide, and H 2S in the
biogas over time, as measured by GC-TCD, at the inlet and outlet of the cleaning column
are presented in Figure 19 (Milestone 4.2.2). Periods of elevated CO2 in the final RNG
correspond to periods when the sorbent was saturated and ready for regeneration (by
heating to approx. 100 oC under vacuum). The results shown in Figures 19(C) and 19(D)

3.5. Task 5: Modeling, Life cycle, and techno-economic assessment of the

 Task 5 consisted of two subtasks. Subtask 5.1 focused on the life-cycle analysis
(LCA) of the proposed treatment systems and included 4 milestones. Subtask 5.2
involved techno-economic analysis (TEA) to evaluate the economic feasibility of the
proposed system, comprising of 5 milestones. The results achieved for all milestones are
described below.

[Milestone 5.1.1]: Draft version of GREET with pathways for the proposed
technology

 A life cycle assessment (LCA) framework was developed using Argonne National
Laboratory’s R&D GREET model to evaluate the proposed system. The model was
initially configured for an ex-situ biomethanation system and later updated to represent
in-situ biomethanation once complete inventory data became available. Four hydrogen
supply scenarios were analyzed: (1) market-purchased hydrogen (baseline), (2)
hydrogen from MEC supplemented with market hydrogen, (3) combined MEC and water
electrolysis hydrogen, and (4) fully on-site hydrogen via water electrolysis (Figure 23).
Different hydrogen sources are essential for evaluating the environmental performance
of biogas upgrading, aiming to identify the most efficient and cost-effective approach for
RNG production from brewery wastewater.
 For any given energy system, the R&D GREET model can calculate the total
energy consumption, fossil fuel use, GHG emissions (carbon intensity), air pollutant
emission levels, and water consumption. In this study, the main environmental impact
assessment criteria that we have used is the life cycle GHG emissions (carbon intensity),
measured in grams of carbon dioxide equivalent (g CO2 eq.) which is based on the global
impact category of Global Warming Potential (GWP). The GWP is calculated by
converting all greenhouse gases into carbon dioxide equivalents (CO 2 eq.) which is
characterized mathematically by multiplying the life cycle inventory results by its
corresponding specific CO2 characterization factor. The characterization factors used in
this study are obtained from the sixth assessment of the Intergovernmental Panel on
Climate Change (IPCC GWP AR6) with GWP factor values of 29.8 for Methane and 273
for Nitrous Oxide (N2O). The resulting impact indicators are then combined to produce an
overall GWP indicator which is measured in g CO2 eq. 16,17.

                                       Page 32 of 52

 Zhen He

 The functional unit of 1 MJ of RNG produced was used in this analysis and the
system boundary was inclusive of a Well-to-Wheels analysis of the fuel produced which
considers the emissions impacts from raw material extraction to the combustion of RNG
in a small industrial boiler. The energy content of natural gas (NG) is measured using the
low heating value (LHV) for the calculation of the carbon fuel intensity. The main goal of
the analysis is to evaluate the life cycle environmental impacts (measured in g CO 2
eq./MJ) and identify environmental hotspots for the proposed system.

 The biogas upgrading facility was assumed to be co-located with the brewery
wastewater source, eliminating feedstock transportation. Upstream energy impacts were
based on U.S. average GREET conditions, with 60.3% fossil contribution to the electricity
mix, and steam supplied by NG industrial boilers. GHG emissions from RNG
transportation accounted for pipeline fuel use (50 miles), compression energy, and 1%
CH4 leakage. Similarly, AD management included emissions from digestate handling and
transport over 50 miles, consistent with typical waste management practices 18. Since the
RNG is derived from organic matter present in brewery wastewater, the CO2 emissions
from its combustion were considered biogenic and carbon-neutral, as part of the short-
term carbon cycle. Material inventory data were obtained from bench-scale experiments
and literature, with GREET default factors applied for PSA purification and a 3.5%
upgrading leakage rate.
 Given the dual function of RNG production and wastewater treatment, the
displacement method was applied to account for avoided emissions from conventional
municipal treatment. These credits were derived from the avoided counterfactual
treatment of brewery wastewater at municipal wastewater treatment plants 19. This

                                      Page 33 of 52

simplification introduces uncertainty due to differences in influent composition and energy
demand.
 A key component in scenarios 1 and 2 of the system is the use of hydrogen
purchased externally. High temperature gas-cooled reactors (HTGR) electrolysis was
selected since Small Modular Reactors (SMR) were not considered suitable for the
integrated system 20. More specifically, using an SMR would require the proposed system
to rely on an upstream source of NG while also producing RNG, thereby creating a circular
loop. Using purchased hydrogen from electrolysis is a good alternative option, this was
included in Scenarios 3 and 4 21.

 A single-point sensitivity analysis evaluated the impact of key operational and
material parameters on life cycle GHG emissions across the four scenarios (Figure 25).

provides the largest emissions benefit, achieving reductions of roughly 53% in Scenario
1 and up to about 54-55% in Scenarios 3 and 4. This highlights the dominant role of

operated at 200 m3/h (4,800 m3/d), which was substantially smaller than typical
commercial facilities, averaging about 55,621 m 3/d for landfill-derived RNG and 12,563
m3/d for livestock/agriculture-based RNG, where economies of scale reduce marginal
costs 55.
 Sensitivity analysis revealed that the MSPR was most sensitive to three key
parameters: methane yield per gram of COD removed, the cost of the hydrogen delivery
membrane, and the price of MEC electrode materials. Methane yield directly affects total

                                       Page 43 of 52

 Zhen He

methane output, where higher CH4 production substantially lowers the MSPR. In contrast,
the H2 delivery membrane and MEC electrodes represent major contributors to capital
investment, thus exerting strong influence on the overall process economics. The H2
delivery membrane was identified as the most critical cost driver, as in-situ
biomethanation depends on it for both CH4 production and upgrading. It accounted for
the largest share of the in-situ biomethanation capital cost and, in some cases, the
majority of the total capital investment. The MEC system was another major contributor
to capital cost in scenarios 2 and 3, significantly impacting their MSPR values. In all
scenarios, increases in electricity and steam prices caused only a slight rise in MSP,
indicating that these utilities have relatively minor effects compared with key process
parameters. In scenario 3, where TEGs were used to offset electricity demand, the
material cost of TEGs also showed noticeable sensitivity, accounting for about 22.7% of
the total equipment cost. By contrast, the cost of MgO showed little sensitivity because it
is used only in the biogas purification step, where its consumption and unit price are low
and it can be regenerated after CO2 adsorption. Overall, these results highlight how both
process performance and cost structure interact to determine RNG economics, offering
useful guidance for improving CH4 production efficiency and reducing the MSP through
targeted process and cost optimizations.

scenarios 1-4, respectively, bringing the MSPR in all scenarios closer to or below the
current RNG market value. These results highlight the substantial potential of targeted
technological advancements to improve economic feasibility and further lower the MSPR.

4. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:

Significant accomplishments achieved in this project are summarized below:
 1. Three-phase bioreactor for in-situ biogas upgrading: Enabled stable H2 injection
 without process inhibition, demonstrating successful integration with anaerobic
 digestion.
 2. High CO2 conversion and H2 uptake: Confirmed efficient hydrogenotrophic
 methanation under continuous operation.
 3. Spatially resolved reactor microbiome: Identified distinct microbial communities,
 including dominance of hydrogenotrophic methanogens in the bioconversion zone,
 linking microbiome structure to function.
 4. Organic TEG fabrication and validation: Demonstrated stable voltage generation
 from low-grade waste heat using low-cost materials.
 5. Pipeline-quality biomethane production: Achieved effective gas cleaning meeting
 practical quality standards.
 6. TEA and LCA completion: Established economic and environmental feasibility,
 supporting scale-up and future commercialization.
 Key challenges included limited performance of organic thermoelectric devices
compared to inorganic systems, intrinsic trade-offs between electrical conductivity and
Seebeck coefficient, limited mechanical robustness of organic materials, and low H 2
production in the MEC, requiring external H2 supplementation. Data limitations in life-
cycle inventory (LCI) development increased uncertainty and made the analysis more
challenging but did not prevent its completion. Lessons learned indicate that system
performance requires co-optimization of materials, device architecture, and thermal
design. Targeted doping, morphology control, and interface engineering are necessary to
improve power output. Mechanical stability depends on improved substrates and
fabrication strategies. For system integration, alternative H2 supply pathways are
required. Transparent and well-documented methodologies are essential to manage data
gaps in environmental assessments.

5. Path Forward:

 It is recommended to further investigate reactor performance and system
integration under more complex substrates to assess the robustness and scalability of
the proposed hybrid reactor. Additional research on improving hydrogen production
efficiency in MEC should focus on operation at larger scales, as many systems remain at

                                      Page 46 of 52

 Zhen He

small laboratory volumes (typically tens to hundreds of milliliters), making scale-up within
lab conditions still highly relevant. Efforts should also target continuous hydrogen
production, including optimization of electrode materials, configurations, and operating
conditions. Additional work is recommended to deepen the understanding of microbial
ecology in AD under biogas upgrading conditions, including validation of the hypothesis
of elevated hydrogen partial pressure in the hydrogen delivery zone through modeling
and simulation. Further investigation is recommended to improve the performance and
scalability of organic TEGs, focusing on co-optimization of material properties and device
architecture to enhance electrical conductivity, Seebeck coefficient, and power output
under low temperature gradients. Studies on device reliability, long-term stability, and
scalable fabrication methods are also recommended to support practical deployment,
while addressing current limitations in power density and durability.
 At the system level, it is recommended to evaluate renewable energy integration
pathways, such as solar PV and hybrid systems, to reduce life-cycle greenhouse gas
emissions. Optimization of waste heat recovery and increased RNG yields through
improved COD removal efficiencies are also recommended to enhance overall
sustainability. In addition, further investigation of low-cost hydrogen production pathways
is recommended, given its impact on process economics, along with continued
optimization of key parameters identified in sensitivity analyses, including AD
performance, hydrogen delivery membrane cost, and MEC electrode materials.
