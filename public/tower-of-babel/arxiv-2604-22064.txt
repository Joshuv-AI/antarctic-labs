Probabilistic Abduction in a Fuzzy Logic Framework
Tommaso Flaminio, Katsumi Inoue, Daniil Kozhemiachenko

Abstract
We study the problem of explaining observations about the probabilities of events such as ‘it rains 20 % 20\% of the time’, ‘rain and snow are equally likely’, etc. We explain these statements with a probability distribution or a statement about probabilities of (other) events that are consistent with our knowledge and entail the observation. We formalise this problem in a fuzzy probabilistic logic 𝖥𝖯 \mathsf{FP} . We define and motivate the notions of abduction problems and their solutions. Our main technical contribution is a comprehensive study of the complexity of solution recognition and existence for a given abduction problem in 𝖥𝖯 \mathsf{FP} for the case of full language and its disjunctive-clause fragments. We also obtain a translation of classical probabilistic abduction (finding the most likely explanation of a given event) to 𝖥𝖯 \mathsf{FP} .

Deduction, induction, and abduction are the main forms of reasoning

. Abduction, or inference of

,
4
,
10
,

,

]
, commonsense reasoning
[

,

]
, formalisation of scientific reasoning

]
, and machine learning
[

, in particular, explainable AI

In logic-based abduction
[
9
]
, the reasoning is usually conducted in the framework of
abduction problems
that contain a
theory

\Gamma

observation

\delta
. The goal is to find an

of
\delta

relative to

\Gamma
using a pre-defined set of

\eta

\Gamma,\eta\not\models\bot

\Gamma,\eta\models\delta

In practice, however, our data may be unreliable, and thus the knowledge might bear a degree of uncertainty. In such contexts, one might search for
probabilistic
explanations of an observation. Hence, to provide explanations in the context of uncertainty, one may employ
probabilistic abduction

Probabilistic Abduction

Probabilistic abduction was first introduced by Poole (
?
) as probabilistic Horn abduction (PHA). This framework computes probabilities of Bayesian networks by definite clauses with probabilistic hypotheses, but does not compute probabilities based on probability measures over possible worlds as defined by Fenstad (
?
).

Sato (
?
) uses definite clauses with probabilistic hypotheses like PHA, but proposes the distribution semantics, which defines probabilistic measures over possible worlds without assumptions of PHA. Sato et al. (
?
) extended this framework to allow arbitrary clauses in the background theory and any ground formulas in observations as constraint-based probabilistic modelling. The distribution semantics were also adopted in probabilistic logic programming. In particular, de Raedt et al. (
?
) present ProbLog, which considers a probability for each definite clause, i.e., a probabilistic hypothesis to select each clause, and Azzolini et al. (
?
) propose algorithms for computing explanations to probabilistic logic programs.

Other frameworks include a Bayesian view of prior distributions in abduction
[
8
]
and abduction in Markov logic networks
[
23
]
. They, however, do not strictly provide a logical formalisation of abduction to explain observations.

Probabilistic Reasoning in Fuzzy Logics

Probabilistic abduction requires formalisation of reasoning about uncertainty. One of the approaches to this task is via the employment of so-called
probabilistic logics
. Probabilistic logic in the context of AI was first proposed by Nilsson (
?
). The propositional fragment of Nilsson’s logic was then formalised by Fagin et al. (
?
). Hájek et al. (
?
;
?
) formalised reasoning about probabilities using
fuzzy logics
, i.e., logics where formulas have values in the real-valued interval
[0,1]
. The intuitive idea was to consider a (non-nesting) modality
\mathsf{Pr}
such that, for a classical formula
\phi
, the modal formula
\mathsf{Pr}(\phi)
is interpreted as the probability measure of the event corresponding to
\phi
.

Connections between probabilities and fuzzy logics have been extensively studied. Hájek and Tulipani (
?
) considered the complexity of probabilistic reasoning, in particular, with conditional probabilities. Flaminio (
?
) proposed a simpler formalisation of reasoning with conditional probabilities. Baldi et al. (
?
;
?
) showed that fuzzy probabilistic logics and the probabilistic logics proposed by Fagin et al. have the same expressive power. Corsi et al. (
?
) studied epistemic probabilistic logics and Kozhemiachenko and Sedlár (
?
) considered formalisation of reasoning about probabilities and actions.

Abduction in Fuzzy Logics

Abduction in various fuzzy logics has long attracted interest. The first formal presentation was given by Yamada and Mukaidono (
?
) in the framework of Łukasiewicz logic. Vojtaš (
?
) expanded this approach to Gödel and Product logics. Miyata et al. (
?
) considered abductive reasoning with observations of different degrees. Solutions to abduction problems in multiple fuzzy logics were further systematised by d’Allones et al. (
?
) and Chakraborty et al. (
?
). Recently, Inoue and Kozhemiachenko (
?
) provided a comprehensive study of the complexity of abduction in Łukasiewicz logic.

Abduction in fuzzy logic has found numerous applications. Namely, Vojtaš (
?
) and Ebrahim (
?
) proposed fuzzy logic programming. Bergadano et al. (
?
) studied fuzzy abduction in the context of machine learning. Among other applications are decision-making and learning in the presence of incomplete information
[
27
,
37
]
and robot perception
[
35
]
.

Contributions

Although probabilistic abduction and applications of fuzzy logics to probabilistic reasoning have been extensively studied, there has been, to the best of our knowledge, no formalisation of probabilistic abduction in a fuzzy logic setting. Furthermore, in most probabilistic abduction frameworks, the main task is to find
the most likely explanation
of an observed event. On the other hand, in some situations, we are interested in
why the event occurred with the observed frequency
. In such contexts, an explanation can be a
probability distribution
consistent with our knowledge about probabilities of events that explains the frequency of the occurrence of the observed event. Consider the following running example for an illustration.

Example 1
.

We study weather records of a town and notice that there was very bad weather (it rained and was cold on the same day) at least
20\%
of the time. The records are incomplete, so we do not know how likely it was to rain or to be cold on each given day. Moreover, there could be cloudy days without rain and days with mild weather (i.e., events ‘it rains’ and ‘it is sunny’ as well as ‘it is cold’ and ‘it is warm’ are
not complementary
). We know, however, that good weather (sunny and warm days) was twice as rare as bad weather (rainy or cold days). Furthermore, cold days were
1.5
times more often than warm days, and sunny days were rarer than rainy days. Now, we aim to
explain the observed frequency
of days with very bad weather.

We consider probabilistic abductive reasoning within the framework of a fuzzy probabilistic logic
\mathsf{FP}
that extends Łukasiewicz logic. We will formally define these logics in the next section. In our proofs, we will rely on the results for abduction in (propositional) Łukasiewicz logic
[
20
]
.
Our contribution is threefold. (1) We define and motivate abduction problems and their solutions that adequately represent the contexts, as in the example above. (2) We study the complexity of probabilistic abduction in the full logic and its disjunctive-clause fragments. We consider
solution recognition
(given a solution candidate
\zeta
and a problem
\mathbb{P}
, check if
\zeta
solves
\mathbb{P}
) and
solution existence
(given
\mathbb{P}
, check if it has solutions). (3) We formalise finding most likely explanations in
\mathsf{FP}
.

Omitted proofs can be found in the appendix of the technical report
[
15
]
.

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathsf{rain}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{matrix}\mathsf{Pr}(s\!\wedge\!w)\!\leq\!\overline{\tfrac{1}{2}},\mathsf{Pr}(w)\!\leq\!\overline{\tfrac{2}{3}},\neg\triangle(\mathsf{Pr}(r)\!\rightarrow\!\mathsf{Pr}(s)),\\

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Pr}(c\!\wedge\!r)\!\geq\!\overline{\tfrac{1}{5}};~{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathsf{rain}}=\{s,w,s\wedge w\};

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathsf{rain}}

\displaystyle=\{\tfrac{i}{100}\!\mid 0\leq i\!\leq\!100\}

Let us briefly discuss the formalisation. By Definitions
1
and
4

\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\neg\triangle(\alpha\rightarrow\beta))=1
iff
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\alpha)>\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\beta)
.
Thus,
\neg\triangle(\mathsf{Pr}(r)\!\rightarrow\!\mathsf{Pr}(s))
means ‘the probability of a rainy day is greater than the probability of a sunny day’.
\mathsf{Pr}(s\!\wedge\!w)\!\leq\!\overline{\tfrac{1}{2}}
and
(\mathsf{Pr}(s\wedge w)\oplus\mathsf{Pr}(s\wedge w))\leftrightarrow\mathsf{Pr}(c\vee r)
imply
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}(s\wedge r))=\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}(c\vee r))\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}
, i.e., rainy or cold days are two times likelier than days with warm and sunny weather.

We also add an auxiliary variable

to express
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}(w))=2\cdot\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}(x))

(\mathsf{Pr}(x)\oplus\mathsf{Pr}(x))\leftrightarrow\mathsf{Pr}(w)
and
\mathsf{Pr}(w)\!\leq\!\overline{\tfrac{2}{3}}

\mathsf{Pr}(c)\leftrightarrow(\mathsf{Pr}(w)\oplus\mathsf{Pr}(x))
. To provide an informative explanation of the frequency of days with cold and rainy weather, we need hypotheses

,
w
, and
s\wedge w
allowing us to express probabilities of sunny and warm days. Finally, we choose
{\mathcal{V}}
expressing percentages.

Now, to explain the observed frequency, we will need to assume probabilities of sunny and warm days. This means that the solution to an
\mathsf{FP}
-abduction problem will not be a conjunction of hypotheses, but
a statement about probabilities of hypotheses
. Note that we need
s{\wedge}w
because, in general, probability measures are not truth functional. In particular,
\mu(\|s{\wedge}w\|_{\mathfrak{M}})
is not uniquely determined by
\mu(\|s\|_{\mathfrak{M}})
and
\mu(\|w\|_{\mathfrak{M}})
. E.g., consider
\mathbf{V}=\{s,w\}
,
\mathfrak{M}_{1}=\langle 2^{\mathbf{V}},\mu_{1}\rangle
, and
\mathfrak{M}_{2}=\langle 2^{\mathbf{V}},\mu_{2}\rangle
being such that
\mu_{1}(\{s,w\})=\mu_{1}(\{s\})=\mu_{1}(\{w\})=\mu_{1}(\{\varnothing\})=\tfrac{1}{4}
,
\mu_{2}(\{s,w\})=\mu_{2}(\{\varnothing\})=\tfrac{1}{2}
, and
\mu_{2}(\{s\})=\mu_{2}(\{w\})=0
(recall that the measure is defined on
2^{2^{\mathbf{V}}}
). One can see that
\mu_{1}(\|s\|_{\mathfrak{M}_{1}}){=}\mu_{1}(\|w\|_{\mathfrak{M}_{1}}){=}\mu_{2}(\|s\|_{\mathfrak{M}_{2}}){=}\mu_{2}(\|w\|_{\mathfrak{M}_{2}})=\tfrac{1}{2}
, but
\mu_{1}(\|s\wedge w\|_{\mathfrak{M}_{1}})=\tfrac{1}{4}
and
\mu_{2}(\|s\wedge w\|_{\mathfrak{M}_{2}})=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

In
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{\mathsf{Pr}}
, statements about probabilities of events can be formalised using formulas of the form
\mathsf{Pr}(\phi)\geq\overline{\mathsf{c}}
(‘probability of
\phi

\mathsf{Pr}(\phi)\rightarrow\mathsf{Pr}(\chi)
(‘probability of
\phi

\displaystyle{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathsf{Pr}}(\mathsf{Pr}(\tau)\diamond\overline{\mathsf{d}})

\displaystyle=\!\{\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}(\tau))\!\mid\!\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}(\tau)\diamond\overline{\mathsf{d}})\!=\!1\}

Given
\mathbf{V}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\}
and
{\mathcal{V}}=\{0,\tfrac{1}{n-1},\ldots,1\}
, we call a PIT
\theta=\bigodot^{k}_{i=1}\mathsf{Pr}(\tau_{i}){\leq}\overline{\mathsf{c}}_{i}\odot\bigodot^{k}_{i=1}\mathsf{Pr}(\tau_{i}){\geq}\overline{\mathsf{c}}_{i}

\langle\mathbf{V},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
-complete
if
\mathsf{Var}[\tau_{i}]=\mathbf{V}
and
c_{i}\in{\mathcal{V}}
for every

, and
\sum^{k}_{i=1}c_{i}=1
.

Observe briefly that all
\langle\mathbf{V},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
-complete PITs are
\mathsf{FP}
-satisfiable. Let us now define the notion of a solution to an
\mathsf{FP}
-abduction problem.

Definition 8
.

Let
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\delta,{\mathsf{H}},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
be an
\mathsf{FP}
AP and let further
\mathbf{V}=\mathsf{Var}[\mathbb{P}]
.

•

A
sufficient solution
is a PIT
\eta=\bigodot^{n}_{i=1}\mathsf{Pr}(\tau_{i})\diamond\overline{\mathsf{c}}_{i}
s.t.
\mathcal{E}(\eta)\subseteq{\mathsf{H}}
,
\overline{\mathsf{c}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{V}}
for every
\overline{\mathsf{c}}_{i}
, and
\Gamma,\eta\models^{\mathsf{cons}}_{\mathsf{FP}}\delta
.

•

A
full solution
is a
\langle\mathbf{V}\!,\!{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
-complete PIT
\theta
s.t.
\Gamma,\!\theta\models^{\mathsf{cons}}_{\mathsf{FP}}\delta
.

To guarantee the finiteness of the set of solutions, we restrict the set of
values
of probabilistic atoms containing hypotheses. Intuitively, this can be interpreted as an explanation with a given precision. Furthermore, full solutions correspond to
probability distributions
on
2^{\mathbf{V}}
. Hence, there is
only one
probabilistic model that satisfies
\theta
. I.e., if
\theta
is a full solution, there is no full solution
\theta^{\prime}
s.t.
\theta^{\prime}\models_{\mathsf{FP}}\theta
and
\theta\not\models_{\mathsf{FP}}\theta^{\prime}
. Besides, a full solution
\theta
is not necessarily a sufficient solution since in general,
\mathcal{E}(\theta)\not\subseteq{\mathsf{H}}
. Moreover, while the length of sufficient solutions is bounded by
\mathcal{E}[\mathbb{P}]
(i.e., is linear in the length of
\mathbb{P}
), the length of full solutions is not. One could interpret
full solutions
as probabilistic counterparts of
most specific explanations (MSEs)

[
31
,
36
,
33
]
. The difference is that MSEs specify as many values of hypotheses as possible, and full solutions specify probabilities of
every event
.

Let us now solve the problem from Example
2
.

Example 3
.

We begin with a sufficient solution:

\displaystyle\eta_{\mathsf{rain}}

\displaystyle=(\mathsf{Pr}(w){\approx}\overline{0.4})\odot(\mathsf{Pr}(s){\approx}\overline{0.4})\odot(\mathsf{Pr}(s\wedge w){\approx}\overline{0.3})

By Definition

, we obtain
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}(c))\!=\!\tfrac{3}{5}
,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}(r))\in(\tfrac{2}{5},\tfrac{3}{5}]
, and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}(c\!\vee\!r))\!=\!\tfrac{3}{5}
from Example

. Thus,
\mathcal{I}(\mathsf{Pr}(c\wedge r))>\tfrac{2}{5}
in every probabilistic model
\mathfrak{M}
that satisfies
\Gamma_{\mathsf{rain}}
and
\eta_{\mathsf{rain}}
. Note that
\eta_{\mathsf{rain}}
is not a full solution: we cannot infer probabilities of
all
events over
\{c,r,s,w,x\}
. But
\eta_{\mathsf{rain}}

can be extended to a full solution
, e.g., as follows:

\displaystyle\theta_{\mathsf{rain}}=

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pr}(s\wedge w\wedge\neg x\wedge\neg c\wedge\neg r){\approx}\overline{0.3}\odot

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pr}(\neg s\wedge\neg w\wedge\neg x\wedge c\wedge r){\approx}\overline{0.5}\odot

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pr}(s\wedge\neg w\wedge x\wedge c\wedge\neg r){\approx}\overline{0.1}\odot

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pr}(\neg s\wedge w\wedge x\wedge\neg c\wedge r){\approx}\overline{0.1}

By Definitions

,

, and

. Clearly,

correspond to some

and the values of

.

We finish the section with a short observation. From Definition

, one can see that if a problem has a sufficient solution, then it has a full solution. On the other hand, the converse is not necessarily true. E.g., if we modify the set of hypotheses in Example

as follows:
{\mathsf{H}}^{-}_{\mathsf{rain}}=\{w\}
,

will not have sufficient solutions. Still

4
Complexity of
\mathsf{FP}
-Abduction

Before proceeding to establish the complexity of abduction in
\mathsf{FP}
, let us first define some preliminary notions. Since most of our hardness results will be obtained by reductions from
Ł
-abduction, we recall here the definitions of
Ł
-abduction problems and their solutions from
[
20
]
.

Definition 9

(
Ł
-abduction problems and their solutions)
.

•

An
Ł
-abduction problem
is a tuple
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Phi,\chi,{\mathsf{H}}\rangle
with
\Phi\cup\{\chi\}\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
being finite and
{\mathsf{H}}
a finite set of
interval literals
s.t.
\mathsf{Var}[{\mathsf{H}}]\subseteq\mathsf{Var}[\Phi\cup\{\chi\}]
. We call
\Phi
a
theory
,
\chi
an
observation
, and members of
{\mathsf{H}}

hypotheses
.

•

An
Ł
-solution
(simply called a solution) of
\mathbb{P}
is an
interval term

\tau
composed of hypotheses s.t.
\Phi,\tau\models^{\mathsf{cons}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}\chi
.

We note that
{{\textsf{\L}}}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{\triangle}
is the fragment of
\mathsf{FP}
with
atomic
events. The following statement is a straightforward generalisation of
[
12
, Proposition 5.6]
and
[
13
, Theorem 5.3]
.

Definition 10
.

For
\phi\in\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
,
\sharp\in\{\neg,\!\triangle\}
, and
\circledast{\in}\{\odot,\!\oplus,\!\rightarrow\}
, we define the
probabilistic counterpart

\phi^{\mathsf{Pr}}
as follows:

\displaystyle p^{\mathsf{Pr}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Pr}(p)

\displaystyle(p\diamond\overline{\mathsf{c}})^{\mathsf{Pr}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Pr}(p)\diamond\overline{\mathsf{c}}

\displaystyle(\sharp\chi)^{\mathsf{Pr}}

\displaystyle=\sharp(\chi^{\mathsf{Pr}})

\displaystyle(\chi\circledast\psi)^{\mathsf{Pr}}

\displaystyle=\chi^{\mathsf{Pr}}\circledast\psi^{\mathsf{Pr}}

For
\Phi\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
, we set
\Phi^{\mathsf{Pr}}=\{\phi^{\mathsf{Pr}}\mid\phi\in\Phi\}
.

Proposition 2
.

For all
\Phi\cup\{\chi\}\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
, it holds that
\Phi\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}\chi
iff
\Phi^{\mathsf{Pr}}\models_{\mathsf{FP}}\chi^{\mathsf{Pr}}
.

We are now ready to consider the complexity of abductive reasoning in
\mathsf{FP}
. We will deal with two problems:

•

solution recognition
— given a problem
\mathbb{P}
and a PIT
\zeta
, determine whether
\zeta
is a (sufficient or full) solution to
\mathbb{P}
;

•

solution existence
— given a problem
\mathbb{P}
, determine whether it has (sufficient or full) solutions.

4.1
Solution Recognition and Existence

We begin by determining the complexity of solution recognition. The next two statements show that while recognition of
sufficient
solutions is
\mathsf{DP}
-complete (same as in classical or Łukasiewicz logic), recognising a
full
solution is polynomially tractable.

Theorem 1
.

Given an
\mathsf{FP}
AP
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\delta,{\mathsf{H}},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
and a PIT
\eta
, it is
\mathsf{DP}
-complete to check if
\eta
is a sufficient solution to
\mathbb{P}
.

Theorem 2
.

Given an
\mathsf{FP}
AP
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\delta,{\mathsf{H}},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
with
\mathbf{V}=\mathsf{Var}[\mathbb{P}]
and a
\langle\mathbf{V},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
-complete PIT
\theta
, it requires polynomial time (w.r.t. the total number of symbols in
\theta
and
\mathbb{P}
together) to check if
\theta
is a full solution to
\mathbb{P}
.

The reason for this discrepancy is that a
full
solution determines
exactly one
probabilistic model where
\Gamma
and
\delta
are true. Moreover, a full solution
\theta
corresponds to a probability distribution on
2^{\mathbf{V}}
. Hence, to calculate the value of
\mathsf{Pr}(\phi)
using
\theta=\bigodot^{k}_{i=1}\mathsf{Pr}(\tau_{i}){\leq}\overline{\mathsf{c}}_{i}\odot\bigodot^{k}_{i=1}\mathsf{Pr}(\tau_{i}){\geq}\overline{\mathsf{c}}_{i}
, one needs to compute
\sum_{\tau_{i}\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}\phi}c_{i}
. Since
\tau_{i}
’s determine the values of all variables,
\tau_{i}\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}\phi
can be decided in polynomial time, and this sum takes polynomial time to compute. On the other hand,
sufficient
solutions can leave probabilities of some events undefined and thus do not correspond to a unique probability distribution. This means that we need two
\mathsf{NP}
-oracle calls: to verify
\Gamma,\eta\not\models_{\mathsf{FP}}\bot
and
\Gamma,\eta\models_{\mathsf{FP}}\delta
. Since these calls are independent,
\mathsf{DP}
-membership follows.

Lastly,
full
solutions are
\langle\mathbf{V},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
-complete PITs, i.e., they can be exponentially long w.r.t.
\mathsf{Var}[\mathbb{P}]
(and
\mathcal{E}[\mathbb{P}]
). Thus, we take into account lengths of both
\theta
and
\mathbb{P}
in Theorem
2
.

Let us now turn to the complexity of
solution existence
. As expected from the results in the previous section, determining the existence of
sufficient
solutions will be harder than determining the existence of
full
solutions. Namely,
\Sigma^{\mathsf{P}}_{2}
-hardness of
sufficient
solution existence follows from Proposition
2
and
\Sigma^{\mathsf{P}}_{2}
-hardness of solution existence for
Ł
-abduction problems
[
20
, Theorem 5]
.
\Sigma^{\mathsf{P}}_{2}
-membership can be obtained from Proposition
1
using a ‘guess-and-verify’ procedure.

Theorem 3
.

Given an
\mathsf{FP}
AP
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\delta,{\mathsf{H}},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
, it is
\Sigma^{\mathsf{P}}_{2}
-complete to determine whether it has sufficient solutions.

Full solution existence for an
\mathsf{FP}
AP
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\delta,{\mathsf{H}},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
can be seen as
\mathsf{FP}
-satisfiability of
\bigodot_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\gamma\odot\delta
provided that the model satisfying
\Gamma\cup\{\delta\}
should evaluate all probabilistic atoms over
{\mathcal{V}}
, not
[0,1]
. It is known from
[
17
]
that
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{\mathsf{Pr}}
is
\mathsf{FP}
-satisfiable iff it is satisfiable in a probabilistic model
\mathfrak{M}=\langle 2^{\mathsf{Var}(\alpha)},\mu\rangle
s.t.
\mu(\{w\})>0
holds for at most
|\mathcal{E}(\alpha)|+1
states. Thus, we will consider full solutions
\theta=\bigodot^{n}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}
s.t.
n\leq 2\cdot|\mathcal{E}[\mathbb{P}]|+2
(recall that
\mathsf{Pr}(\pi){\approx}\overline{\mathsf{c}}
is a shorthand for
(\mathsf{Pr}(\pi){\geq}\overline{\mathsf{c}})\odot(\mathsf{Pr}(\pi)\leq\overline{\mathsf{c}})
). We will call such solutions
concise
to differentiate them from full solutions of arbitrary length.

Definition 11
.

Given an
\mathsf{FP}
AP
\mathbb{P}
and its full solution
\theta=\bigodot^{n}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}
, we say that
\theta
is
concise
iff
n\leq 2\cdot|\mathcal{E}[\mathbb{P}]|+2
.

\mathsf{NP}
-membership of concise full solution existence for
\mathsf{FP}
APs now follows from Theorem
2
. For
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness, we can provide a polynomial reduction from
\mathsf{CPL}
-satisfiability.

Theorem 4
.

Given an
\mathsf{FP}
AP
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\delta,{\mathsf{H}},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
, it is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete to determine whether it has concise full solutions.

4.2
Prioritisation of Solutions

Up to now, we have been considering
arbitrary
full and sufficient solutions. Note, however, that usually we have some preference relation between different solutions. In particular, a usual requirement is that a solution should be as simple as possible. One of the most straightforward choices to formalise simplicity in logic-based abduction is to utilise entailment. If
\zeta_{1}
and
\zeta_{2}
are two solutions s.t.
\zeta_{1}\models\zeta_{2}
and
\zeta_{2}\not\models\zeta_{1}
, then
\zeta_{2}
is simpler because it makes fewer assumptions and thus
\zeta_{1}
is more preferable than
\zeta_{1}
. In classical logic, this is usually formulated in terms of
\subseteq
-minimality

[
9
]
(given
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{CPL}}
-terms
\sigma
and
\tau
,
\sigma\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}\tau
iff all literals in
\tau
belong to
\sigma
) and interpreted as preferring solutions without
redundant hypotheses
: if both
\tau\wedge h
and
\tau
are solutions, then
h
is redundant
[
29
]
.

An advantage of
entailment-minimal
solutions is that they can be easily generalised to abduction in non-classical logics (cf., e.g., works by Bienvenu et al. (
?
;
?
) and Inoue and Kozhemiachenko (
?
)). In the framework of
\mathsf{FP}
-abduction, we can employ
\models_{\mathsf{FP}}
-minimality to prioritise
sufficient
solutions. On the other hand, different
full
solutions are either equivalent or incompatible. Hence, we need another approach to prioritisation. Since full solutions correspond to probability distributions on
2^{\mathbf{V}}
, it makes sense to employ the
principle of maximum entropy
by Janes (
?
;
?
) (cf. a discussion by Williamson (
?
)) to establish a preference relation between them. The principle states that the probability distributions that best represent our knowledge are those with the highest entropy. Thus, given an
\mathsf{FP}
AP, we prefer full solutions with higher entropy. Entropy was used to prioritise probabilistic explanations by Valkovsky et al. (
?
) in the context of constraint programming, and Dubois et al. (
?
). There, however, the explanation was not a probability distribution itself but an event.

We will thus consider two classes of preferred solutions to
\mathsf{FP}
APs:
entropy-maximal
and
\models_{\mathsf{FP}}
-minimal
(entailment-minimal). We begin with entropy-maximal full solutions.

Definition 12
.

Let
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\delta,{\mathsf{H}},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
be an
\mathsf{FP}
AP with
\mathsf{Var}[\mathbb{P}]=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\}
and
\theta=\bigodot^{n}_{i=1}\mathsf{Pr}(\tau_{i}){\approx}\overline{\mathsf{c}}_{i}
its full solution s.t.
c_{i}>0
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. The
entropy of
\theta
(
\mathbb{H}(\theta)
) is defined as follows:
\mathbb{H}(\theta)=-\sum^{n}_{i=1}c_{i}\cdot\log_{2}c_{i}
.

Definition 13
.

Let
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\delta,{\mathsf{H}},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
be an
\mathsf{FP}
AP, that contains
k
unique probabilistic atoms. We say that
\theta
is a
concise entropy-maximal
(CEM) solution to
\mathbb{P}
iff
\theta
is a full solution, and there is no full solution
\theta^{\prime}
containing at most
2{\cdot}k{+}2
PITs s.t.
\mathbb{H}(\theta){<}\mathbb{H}(\theta^{\prime})
.

Note that CEM solutions have bounded length. This is done for the following reason. Intuitively, the higher the entropy of a full solution
\theta
, the closer it is to a uniform probability distribution. In other words, the higher
\mathbb{H}(\theta)
is,
the more
\theta
contains terms
\mathsf{Pr}(\tau){\approx}\overline{\mathsf{c}}
with
c\neq 0
. Thus, solutions with higher entropy could be exponentially long w.r.t. the size of the problem. For example, if
\phi
is a tautology and
\mathsf{Var}(\phi)=\mathbf{V}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}
, then the (non-concise) entropy-maximal explanation of
\mathsf{Pr}(\phi){\geq}\overline{1}
is the uniform probability distribution on
2^{\mathbf{V}}
expressed as
\bigodot^{2^{n}}_{i=1}\mathsf{Pr}(\tau_{i}){\approx}\overline{\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}}
. To circumvent this problem, we will only consider ‘concise’ full solutions (recall Definition
11
).

Example 4
.

Consider
\theta_{\mathsf{rain}}
. It contains
8
PITs (note that
\mathsf{Pr}(\tau){\approx}\overline{\mathsf{c}}
’s are shorthands for
\mathsf{Pr}(\tau){\leq}\overline{\mathsf{c}}\odot\mathsf{Pr}(\tau){\geq}\overline{\mathsf{c}}
) and is concise. By Definition
12
,
\mathbb{H}(\theta_{\mathsf{rain}})\approx 1.685
. Recall from Example
2
that
\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{rain}}
contains
8
unique probabilistic atoms. Thus, we can consider full solutions to
\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{rain}}
that contain up to
18
terms (i.e., up to
9
events).

Now, to produce a probability distribution with a higher entropy than
\theta_{\mathsf{rain}}
, we ‘spread’ the probability of
\neg s\wedge\neg w\wedge\neg x\wedge c\wedge r
to other events. Consider:

\displaystyle\theta^{\Uparrow}_{\mathsf{rain}}=

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pr}(s\wedge w\wedge\neg x\wedge\neg c\wedge\neg r){\approx}\overline{0.3}\odot

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pr}(\neg s\wedge\neg w\wedge\neg x\wedge c\wedge r){\approx}\overline{0.35}\odot

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pr}(s\wedge\neg w\wedge\neg x\wedge c\wedge r){\approx}\overline{0.15}\odot

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pr}(s\wedge\neg w\wedge x\wedge c\wedge\neg r){\approx}\overline{0.1}{\odot}

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pr}(\neg s\wedge w\wedge x\wedge\neg c\wedge r){\approx}\overline{0.1}

Using Definition

, i.e.,
\mathbb{H}(\theta^{\Uparrow}_{\mathsf{rain}})>\mathbb{H}(\theta_{\mathsf{rain}})
. Thus,
\eta^{\Uparrow}_{\mathsf{rain}}
is a more preferable full solution to

than

.

We can now show that recognising concise entropy-maximal solutions is
\mathsf{coNP}
-complete. Indeed, we can solve the complementary problem in
\mathsf{NP}
time: given a
\langle{\mathcal{V}},\mathbf{V}\rangle
-complete PIT
\theta
, we guess another (polynomially large) solution candidate
\theta^{\prime}
and check whether
\mathbb{H}(\theta)<\mathbb{H}(\theta^{\prime})
in polynomial time. If the check succeeds,
\theta

is not a CEM solution
. To show
\mathsf{coNP}
-hardness, we can construct a polynomial-time reduction from
\mathsf{CPL}
validity to the recognition of concise entropy-maximal solutions.

Theorem 5
.

Given an
\mathsf{FP}
AP
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\delta,{\mathsf{H}},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
with
\mathbf{V}=\mathsf{Var}[\mathbb{P}]
and a
\langle\mathbf{V},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
-complete PIT
\theta
, it is
\mathsf{coNP}
-complete to determine whether
\theta
is a CEM solution.

Let us now consider entailment-minimal solutions. We adapt the analogous notion introduced for
Ł
-abduction problems in
[
20
]
.

Definition 14
.

Let
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\delta,{\mathsf{H}},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
be an
\mathsf{FP}
AP. We say that
\eta
is an
\models_{\mathsf{FP}}
-minimal
(entailment-minimal) solution iff it is a sufficient solution, and there is no other sufficient solution
\zeta
s.t.
\eta\models_{\mathsf{FP}}\zeta
but
\zeta\not\models_{\mathsf{FP}}\eta
.

To illustrate the notion of
\models_{\mathsf{FP}}
-minimal solutions, we come back to Examples
2
and
3
. We show how to construct an
\models_{\mathsf{FP}}
-minimal solution from a given sufficient solution.

Example 5
.

Recall first a sufficient solution
\eta_{\mathsf{rain}}
to
\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{rain}}
. One can easily check that it is
not

\models_{\mathsf{FP}}
-minimal. We can, however, transform it into an entailment-minimal solution by increasing intervals of permitted values for each literal.

In fact, it turns out that
\mathsf{Pr}(s\wedge w){\approx}\overline{0.3}
is
redundant
and
\eta_{0}{=}\mathsf{Pr}(w){\geq}\overline{0.4}{\odot}\mathsf{Pr}(s){\approx}\overline{0.4}

\Gamma_{\mathsf{rain}},\!\eta_{0}\not\!\models_{\mathsf{FP}}\!\bot
.
Moreover, by Definition
4
, we obtain from
\Gamma_{\mathsf{rain}}
(cf. Example

\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}(w)){\geq}0.4

\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}(c)){\geq}0.6

\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}(s)){=}0.4

\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}(r)){>}0.4
. Additionally,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}(s\wedge w))\leq 0.4
because
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}(s)){=}0.4
, and hence
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}(c\vee r))\in[0.6;0.8]
. Thus,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}(c\wedge r)){>}0.2

\Gamma_{\mathsf{rain}},\eta_{0}\models_{\mathsf{FP}}\mathsf{Pr}(c\wedge r){\geq}\overline{\tfrac{1}{5}}
, as required. Now observe that neither of the following PITs solve

:

\displaystyle\eta_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Pr}(w){\geq}\overline{0.39}\odot\mathsf{Pr}(s){\approx}\overline{0.4}

\displaystyle\eta_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Pr}(w){\geq}\overline{0.4}\odot\mathsf{Pr}(s){\geq}\overline{0.4}\odot\mathsf{Pr}(s){\leq}\overline{0.41}

\displaystyle\eta_{3}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Pr}(w){\geq}\overline{0.4}\odot\mathsf{Pr}(s){\geq}\overline{0.39}\odot\mathsf{Pr}(s){\leq}\overline{0.4}

Thus, we cannot increment the sets of permitted values of PILs in

\mathsf{Pr}(s){\approx}\overline{0.4}

\mathsf{Pr}(s){\geq}\overline{0.4}\odot\mathsf{Pr}(s){\leq}\overline{0.4}
. Hence, we can weaken
\mathsf{Pr}(s){\leq}\overline{0.4}
to
\mathsf{Pr}(s\wedge w){\leq}\overline{0.4}
. One can check that the resulting PIT

\displaystyle\eta^{\min}_{\mathsf{rain}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Pr}(w){\geq}\overline{0.4}\odot\mathsf{Pr}(s){\geq}\overline{0.4}\odot\mathsf{Pr}(s\wedge w){\leq}\overline{0.4}

is a sufficient solution to

-minimal solution since we can neither increase intervals nor weaken the remaining events.

Now notice that, in contrast to classical and Łukasiewicz logics, the satisfiability and entailment of PITs are not polynomially decidable. It is easy to see that
\mathsf{FP}
-satisfiability of arbitrary probabilistic atoms is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete. By Definition
4
,
\phi
is
\mathsf{CPL}
-satisfiable iff
\mathsf{Pr}(\phi)
is
\mathsf{FP}
-satisfiable. Even the
\mathsf{FP}
-satisfiability of PITs is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete (and entailment is
\mathsf{coNP}
-complete) because we can encode DNF-non-validity in
\mathsf{CPL}
using PITs.

Proposition 3
.

1.

Given a PIT
\zeta=\bigodot^{n}_{i=1}(\mathsf{Pr}(\tau_{i})\diamond\overline{\mathsf{c}}_{i})
, it is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete to check whether
\zeta
is
\mathsf{FP}
-satisfiable.

2.

Given two PITs
\zeta_{1}
and
\zeta_{2}
it is
\mathsf{coNP}
-complete to check whether
\zeta_{1}\models_{\mathsf{FP}}\zeta_{2}
.

Still, even though entailment between PITs is not polynomially tractable, recognising
\models_{\mathsf{FP}}
-minimal solutions is
\mathsf{DP}
-complete. For
\mathsf{DP}
-hardness, we construct a polynomial reduction from the recognition of entailment-minimal solutions of
Ł
-abduction problems
, which is
\mathsf{DP}
-complete
[
20
, Theorem 3]
to the recognition of
\models_{\mathsf{FP}}
-minimal solutions. For
\mathsf{DP}
-membership, we show that given a PIT
\eta
and an
\mathsf{FP}
AP
\mathbb{P}
, there are only polynomially many ‘next weakest’ terms that we need to check. If none of them is a sufficient solution to
\mathbb{P}
, but
\eta
is, then
\eta
is a
\models_{\mathsf{FP}}
-minimal solution. These ‘next weakest’ PITs can be built as shown in Example
5
where we transformed
\eta_{\mathsf{rain}}
into
\eta^{\min}_{\mathsf{rain}}
.

Theorem 6
.

Given an
\mathsf{FP}
AP
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\delta,{\mathsf{H}},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
and a PIT
\eta
, it is
\mathsf{DP}
-complete to check whether it is an
\models_{\mathsf{FP}}
-minimal solution to
\mathbb{P}
.

5
Abduction in Fragments of
\mathsf{FP}

As we saw in the previous section, abduction in
\mathsf{FP}
is intractable in the general case (especially when it comes to
sufficient solutions
). Thus, it makes sense to consider fragments of
\mathsf{FP}
where abductive reasoning is polynomially decidable or at least simpler than in the general case.

In this section, we will consider two fragments of
\mathsf{FP}
that generalise the
simple clause fragment
of Łukasiewicz logic
[
3
]
and the
cover-free fragment
of
{{\textsf{\L}}}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{\triangle}

[
20
]
. Note, however, that we need to restrict not only the shape of outer formulas, but also the shape of inner formulas. Indeed, as we noted in Section
3
, satisfiability of a single formula
\mathsf{Pr}(\phi)
or
\mathsf{Pr}(\phi)\diamond\overline{\mathsf{c}}
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard if
\phi
is arbitrary. Moreover, the events should be syntactically simple, yet expressive enough to reason about probabilities of non-trivial events. The next definition presents such a restriction.

Definition 15

(Chained inner-positive theories)
.

•

\Phi\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{CPL}}
is
chained positive
(CP) iff

–

\pi=\bigwedge^{m}_{i=1}p_{i}
or
\pi=\bigvee^{n}_{i=1}q_{i}
for every
\pi\in\Phi
;

–

for every
\pi,\sigma\in\Phi
, it holds that either
\pi\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}\sigma
, or
\sigma\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}\pi
, or
\mathsf{Var}(\pi)\cap\mathsf{Var}(\sigma)=\varnothing
.

•

\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{\mathsf{Pr}}
is
chained inner-positive
(CIP) if
\mathcal{E}[\Gamma]
is chained positive.

Example 6
.

Recall the theory
\Gamma_{\mathsf{rain}}
from Example
2
. Observe that it is
not CIP
. Indeed,
\mathcal{E}[\Gamma]
contains
c\vee r
,
c
, and
r
. Clearly,
c\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}c\vee r
,
r\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}c\vee r
, but
c\not\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}r
and
r\not\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}c
. However
\Gamma^{\prime}_{\mathsf{rain}}\cup\{\delta_{\mathsf{rain}}\}
is CIP for
\Gamma^{\prime}_{\mathsf{rain}}=\Gamma_{\mathsf{rain}}\setminus\{\neg\triangle(\mathsf{Pr}(r)\rightarrow\mathsf{Pr}(s))\}
. Indeed, we have two ‘chains’ of events in
\Gamma^{\prime}_{\mathsf{rain}}
:
s\wedge w\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}w
and
c\wedge r\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}c\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}c\vee r
. Note, however, that
\eta_{\mathsf{rain}}
(cf. Example
3
) is
not
a solution to
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\mathsf{rain}}=\langle\Gamma^{\prime}_{\mathsf{rain}},\delta_{\mathsf{rain}},{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathsf{rain}},{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathsf{rain}}\rangle
. In fact, since
r\notin\mathcal{E}[\Gamma^{\prime}_{\mathsf{rain}}]
,
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\mathsf{rain}}

has no sufficient solutions
. Still,
\theta_{\mathsf{rain}}
is a full solution to
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\mathsf{rain}}
.

An important property of CIP theories is that they can be translated into
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
in polynomial time only. In fact, given a CIP theory
\Gamma
, it suffices to construct an
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
theory
\Psi_{\Gamma}
that encodes (classical) entailment between events in
\Gamma
. In this case, an
Ł
-valuation satisfying
\Psi_{\Gamma}
will induce a map coherent with a probability measure on
2^{2^{\mathsf{Var}[\Gamma]}}
.

The next definition is adapted from
[
7
]
.

Definition 16
.

Let
\mathbb{E}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{CPL}}
be finite,
\mathfrak{p}:\mathbb{E}\rightarrow[0,1]
, and
\mathfrak{M}=\langle 2^{\mathsf{Var}[\mathbb{E}]},\mu\rangle
be a probabilistic model. We say that
\mu
is coherent with
\mathfrak{p}
iff
\mu(\|\phi\|_{\mathfrak{M}})=\mathfrak{p}(\phi)
for all
\phi\in\mathbb{E}
. The map
\mathfrak{p}:\mathbb{E}\rightarrow[0,1]
is
coherent
if there exists a probabilistic model
\mathfrak{M}=\langle 2^{\mathsf{Var}[\mathbb{E}]},\mu\rangle
such that
\mu
is coherent with
\mathfrak{p}
.

Proposition 4
.

For every finite CIP theory
\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{\mathsf{Pr}}
, there is an
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
-theory
\Psi_{\Gamma}
s.t.

1.

\Psi_{\Gamma}
is
Ł
-satisfiable iff
\Gamma
is
\mathsf{FP}
-satisfiable;

2.

\Psi_{\Gamma}
is constructible in polynomial time w.r.t. the size of
\Gamma
.

5.1
Simple Clause Fragment

Let us now consider the probabilistic simple clause fragment of
\mathsf{FP}
. Recall from
[
3
]
that (propositional) simple clauses are formulas of the form
\bigoplus^{n}_{i=1}l_{i}
with
l_{i}
’s being propositional variables and their negations. As one can check from Definition
1
,
\bigoplus^{n}_{i=1}l_{i}
can be equivalently represented as
\bigodot^{k}_{i=1}\neg l_{i}\rightarrow\bigoplus^{n}_{j=n-k}l_{j}
for every
k\leq n
. Thus, simple clauses can be used to express fuzzy (disjunctive) rules. Moreover, in contrast to classical disjunctive clauses,
Ł
-satisfiability of sets of simple clauses is
polynomially decidable
, whence, simple-clause abduction turns out to be
\mathsf{NP}
-complete
[
20
]
, while classical abduction in theories represented as sets of disjunctive clauses is
\Sigma^{\mathsf{P}}_{2}
-hard in general
[
9
]
.

Thus, in the context of
probabilistic
abduction, it makes sense to consider an
\mathsf{FP}
-counterpart of simple clauses. The following definitions present
probabilistic
simple clauses (PSCs) and PSC abduction problems.

Definition 17

(Probabilistic simple clauses)
.

•

A
probabilistic simple clause
(PSC) is a formula of the form
\bigoplus^{m}_{i=1}\mathsf{Pr}(\sigma_{i})\oplus\bigoplus^{n}_{i=1}\neg\mathsf{Pr}(\tau_{i})
with
\sigma
and
\tau
being conjunctions or disjunctions of variables.

•

A
PSC theory
is a CIP theory
\Gamma
s.t. every
\gamma\in\Gamma
is either a PSC or has a form
\mathsf{Pr}(\tau)\diamond\overline{\mathsf{c}}
with
\tau
being a conjunction or a disjunction of variables.

Let us illustrate the expressivity of PSC theories with an example. Note, in particular, that we can formalise some statements about probabilities of events containing
\neg
. Moreover, formulas
\mathsf{Pr}(\tau)\diamond\overline{\mathsf{c}}

do not need to be PILs
.

Example 7
.

Consider the following statements:

\alpha
:

the probability of precipitation (rain, snow, or hailstorm) tomorrow is
70\%
;

\beta
:

the probability of the rain tomorrow is at least as high as the probability of neither snow nor rain happening tomorrow.

We set
\alpha=\alpha_{1}\odot\alpha_{2}
with
\alpha_{1}=\mathsf{Pr}(r\vee s\vee h){\geq}\overline{0.7}
and
\alpha_{2}=\mathsf{Pr}(r\vee s\vee h){\leq}\overline{0.7}
and
\beta=\mathsf{Pr}(\neg r\wedge\neg s)\rightarrow\mathsf{Pr}(r)
. In its current form,
\{\alpha,\beta\}
is not a PSC theory because
\mathcal{E}[\{\alpha,\beta\}]
is not chained positive. Note, however, that by Definition
4
,
\mathsf{Pr}(\neg\phi)
and
\neg\mathsf{Pr}(\phi)
are equivalent. Thus,
\beta
is equivalent to
\beta^{\prime}=\mathsf{Pr}(r\vee s)\oplus\mathsf{Pr}(r)
and
\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta^{\prime}\}
is a PSC theory.

Definition 18
.

A
PSC abduction problem
is an
\mathsf{FP}
AP
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\delta,{\mathsf{H}},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
s.t.
\Gamma\cup\{\delta\}
is a PSC theory and
\mathcal{E}[\mathbb{P}]
is CP.

Note that the observation of a PSC AP can have various types, depending on the constraints we want to explain using the theory. Furthermore, we need to list
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{CPL}}
-terms in
{\mathsf{H}}
and cannot allow arbitrary conjunctions of hypotheses: while
\{a,b\}
is CP,
\{a,b,a\wedge b\}
is not. Defining
{\mathsf{H}}
as a set of terms (not literals) and demanding that
\mathcal{E}[\mathbb{P}]
be chained positive ensures that given a
sufficient
solution
\eta
for
\mathbb{P}
,
\Gamma\cup\{\eta,\delta\}
is a CIP theory. We can use this together with Propositions
2
and
4
to show the following statement.

Theorem 7
.

Given a PSC AP
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\delta,{\mathsf{H}},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
and a PIT
\eta
, it is
{\mathsf{P}}
-complete under logspace reductions to check if
\eta
is an (
\mathsf{FP}
-minimal) sufficient solution of
\mathbb{P}
.

As expected from the previous statement, determining the existence of sufficient solutions is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete. Somewhat surprisingly, the existence of concise
full solutions
is also
\mathsf{NP}
-complete as in the general case. This is because we can reduce classical satisfiability of CNFs to the existence of full solutions to PSC APs.

Theorem 8
.

Given a PSC AP
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\delta,{\mathsf{H}},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
, it is:

1.

\mathsf{NP}
-complete to check if it has
sufficient
solutions;

2.

\mathsf{NP}
-complete to check if it has
concise full
solutions.

5.2
Interval Clause Fragment

Let us now consider the probabilistic interval clause fragment of
\mathsf{FP}
. Propositional interval clauses (i.e., formulas of the form
\pi_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\pi_{n}
with
\pi
’s being
interval literals
) were introduced by Inoue and Kozhemiachenko (
?
) to formalise statements such as ‘if the value of
p
is at least
0.5
, then the value of
q
is at most
0.6
’ —
p{\geq}\overline{0.5}\rightarrow q{\leq}\overline{0.6}
or, equivalently
p{<}\overline{0.5}\oplus q{\leq}\overline{0.6}
. In this section, we introduce the
\mathsf{FP}
-counterparts of interval clauses and investigate the complexity of abductive reasoning with
probabilistic interval clauses
.

Definition 19
.

A
probabilistic interval clause
(PIC) is a formula
\bigoplus^{n}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}
with every
\lambda
having the form
\mathsf{Pr}(\phi)\diamond\overline{\mathsf{c}}
with
\phi\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{CPL}}
. A
PIC theory
is a set of PICs.

It is known from
[
20
]
that the complexity of abductive reasoning with interval-clause theories is the same as with arbitrary
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
-theories. Thus, by Proposition
2
, the same would hold w.r.t. abductive reasoning with PIC theories. Still, the interval clause fragment of
{{\textsf{\L}}}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{\triangle}
can be restricted to the so-called
cover-free
fragment, over which solution recognition was polynomial and solution existence
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.

Definition 20
.

Let
\Phi\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
be a set of interval clauses represented as
\bigodot^{m}_{i=1}(p_{i}\diamond\overline{\mathsf{c}}_{i})\!\rightarrow\!(q\diamond\overline{\mathsf{d}})
and
\bigodot^{m}_{i=1}(p_{i}\diamond\overline{\mathsf{c}}_{i})\!\rightarrow\!\bot
.
\Phi
is
cover-free
(CF) if no pair of interval literals
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
over the same variable occurs in
\Phi
s.t.
{{\textsf{\L}}}\models\pi\oplus\pi^{\prime}
.

By Proposition
2
, it would follow from
[
20
, Theorem 16]
that solution existence is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard for
\mathsf{FP}
APs
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Phi^{\mathsf{Pr}},\lambda^{\mathsf{Pr}},{\mathsf{H}},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
with
\Phi
being a CF set of
interval clauses
and
\lambda
an interval literal. It is thus instructive to see whether we can find a subclass of CF theories where solution existence can be determined in polynomial time. The definition below introduces
short
probabilistic cover-free (SPCF) theories. We use this name to indicate that
n=1
in the implications of the form
\bigodot^{n}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}\rightarrow\lambda
(i.e., clauses with a non-empty head must be
binary
).

Definition 21
.

An SPCF theory is a CIP theory
\Gamma
s.t.

•

\Gamma
is a set of PICs represented as
\bigodot^{n}_{i=1}\mathsf{Pr}(\pi_{i})\diamond\overline{\mathsf{c}}_{i}\rightarrow\bot
or
\mathsf{Pr}(\pi)\diamond\overline{\mathsf{c}}\rightarrow\mathsf{Pr}(\varrho)\diamond\overline{\mathsf{d}}
;

•

for every pair of probabilistic atoms
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
occurring in
\Gamma
, it holds that
\mathsf{FP}\not\models\lambda\oplus\lambda^{\prime}
.

Consider now the following example.

Example 8
.

Assume that
\Gamma
contains the following relations between the frequencies of different events. If black ice (
b
) or thick fog (
f
) occurs at least
40\%
of the time, then traffic accidents (
a
) will happen at least every other day. If traffic accidents or snowdrifts (
s
) happen at least twice a week, then major traffic jams (
j
) will occur at least once a week.

We can formalise
\Gamma
as follows:
\mathsf{Pr}(b{\vee}f){\geq}\overline{\tfrac{2}{5}}\rightarrow\mathsf{Pr}(a){\geq}\overline{\tfrac{1}{2}}
,
\mathsf{Pr}(a{\vee}s){\geq}\overline{\tfrac{2}{7}}\rightarrow\mathsf{Pr}(j){\geq}\overline{\tfrac{1}{7}}
. Now, to explain why traffic jams happen every week, we can, for example, assume that thick fog occurred at least
40\%
of the time:
\eta=\mathsf{Pr}(f)\geq\overline{\tfrac{2}{5}}
. Note that
\Gamma,\eta\models^{\mathsf{cons}}_{\mathsf{FP}}\mathsf{Pr}(j){\geq}\overline{\tfrac{1}{7}}
and
\Gamma\cup\{\eta,\mathsf{Pr}(j){\geq}\overline{\tfrac{1}{7}}\}
is SPCF.

The next definition introduces the class of
\mathsf{FP}
abduction problems whose theories are SPCF. Note that we demand that the set of
all events
occurring in the problem be CP.

Definition 22
.

An
SPCF abduction problem
is an
\mathsf{FP}
AP
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\mathsf{Pr}(\chi)\diamond\overline{\mathsf{d}},{\mathsf{H}},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
s.t.
\Gamma\cup\{\mathsf{Pr}(\chi)\diamond\overline{\mathsf{d}}\}
is an SPCF theory and
\mathcal{E}[\mathbb{P}]
is chained positive.

The polynomial decidability of the sufficient solution existence follows from the fact that the problem of determining the existence of solutions to SPCF APs is reducible to determining the existence of solutions to classical abduction problems whose theories contain so-called
{\textsf{IHS-B}^{-}}
-clauses (literals —
p
and
\neg q
— and implications of the form
p\rightarrow q
and
\bigwedge^{n}_{i=1}p_{i}\rightarrow\bot
) proposed by Khanna et al. (
?
). As determining the existence of solutions to
{\textsf{IHS-B}^{-}}
abduction problems is
polynomially tractable

[
5
, Proposition 12]
, the existence of sufficient solutions to SPCF APs can also be decided in polynomial time. On the other hand, determining the existence of
full
solutions to SPCF APs is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard because we can construct a reduction from
\mathsf{CPL}
-satisfiability of CNFs. Thus, SPCF theories manifest an unusual behaviour: it is
harder
to check whether a full solution exists than whether a sufficient solution exists.

The intuitive reason behind this is that solutions to
\mathsf{CPL}
abduction problems with
{\textsf{IHS-B}^{-}}
theories and observations in the form of a single variable can be w.l.o.g. assumed to contain one literal only. Thus (after translating the original problem into its
\mathsf{CPL}
counterpart), it suffices to check hypotheses one by one. Moreover, for any candidate
\eta
for a sufficient solution to
\mathbb{P}{=}\langle\Gamma,\mathsf{Pr}(\chi)\diamond\overline{\mathsf{d}},{\mathsf{H}},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
,
\Gamma\cup\{\eta,\mathsf{Pr}(\chi)\diamond\overline{\mathsf{d}}\}
is a CIP theory. However, to check if a full solution exists, one needs to verify whether there is a suitable
\langle\mathbf{V},{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
-complete term, which is independent from
{\mathsf{H}}
and is not necessarily CP.

Theorem 9
.

For a SPCF AP
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\!\mathsf{Pr}(\chi)\diamond\overline{\mathsf{d}},\!{\mathsf{H}},\!{\mathcal{V}}\rangle
, it is

1.

decidable in polynomial time if it has sufficient solutions;

2.

\mathsf{NP}
-complete to check if it has concise full solutions.

6
Modelling Most Likely Explanations in
\mathsf{FP}

Until now, we have interpreted probabilistic explanations as statements
about probabilities
that explain the observed frequency of events, and showed how one can use
\mathsf{FP}
to model such explanations. A more traditional perspective on probabilistic explanations is to consider
the most likely explanations
of events. More formally, one can represent this task as follows: given a theory
\Phi
, an observation
\chi
, and probability assignments of (some) events, one has to come up with an explanation
\tau
s.t. there is no other explanation
\sigma
whose probability is higher than that of
\tau
. In this section, we will demonstrate how to model this reasoning task in
\mathsf{FP}
.

Definition 23
.

A
probabilistic abduction problem
(PrAP) is a tuple
\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{p}}=\langle\Phi,\chi,{\mathsf{H}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{p}\rangle
s.t.

•

\Phi\cup\{\chi\}\cup\mathbb{E}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{CPL}}
is finite and
\mathsf{Var}[\mathbb{E}]\subseteq\mathsf{Var}[\Phi\cup\{\chi\}]
;

•

{\mathsf{H}}
is a finite set of literals s.t.
\mathsf{Var}[{\mathsf{H}}]\subseteq\mathsf{Var}[\Phi\cup\{\chi\}]
;

•

\mathfrak{p}:\mathbb{E}\rightarrow[0,1]_{\mathbb{Q}}
.

We call
\Phi
,
\chi
,
{\mathsf{H}}
,
\mathbb{E}
, and
\mathfrak{p}

theory
,
observation
,
hypotheses
,
events
, and
value assignment
, respectively.

Note that PrAPs extend the (propositional fragments of the) frameworks in
[
30
,
34
]
, as we allow arbitrary (not just Horn) theories. Besides, PrAPs are similar to abduction problems with preferences on hypotheses
[
9
, §4.4]
. The difference is that
\mathfrak{p}
can be undefined on some hypotheses or their conjunctions and that we use numerical values, not a qualitative preference.

To determine solutions to PrAPs, we will consider probabilistic models whose measures are coherent with
\mathfrak{p}
. For simplicity, we will represent
\Phi
as
\bigwedge_{\phi\in\Phi}\phi
and w.l.o.g. assume that theories of PrAPs contain only one formula.

Definition 24

(Solutions to PrAPs)
.

For a PrAP
\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{p}}=\langle\{\phi\},\chi,{\mathsf{H}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{p}\rangle
and
\mathbf{V}=\mathsf{Var}[\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{p}}]
, we define the following.

•

A
solution
to
\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{p}}
is an
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{CPL}}
-term
\tau
built from
{\mathsf{H}}
s.t.
\phi,\tau\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}\chi
and there is a probabilistic model
\mathfrak{M}=\langle 2^{\mathbf{V}},\mu\rangle
s.t.
\mu
is coherent with
\mathfrak{p}
and
\mu\left(\left\|\phi\wedge\tau\right\|_{\mathfrak{M}}\right){>}0
.

•

A solution
\tau
is
preferred
iff for every other solution
\sigma
, there is a probabilistic model
\mathfrak{M}=\langle 2^{\mathbf{V}},\mu\rangle
s.t.
\mu
is coherent with
\mathfrak{p}
and
\mu(\|\tau\|_{\mathfrak{M}})\geq\mu(\|\sigma\|_{\mathfrak{M}})
.

Note that
\mathsf{CPL}
abduction problems can be seen as PrAPs with
\mathbb{E}=\{\phi\}
and
\mathfrak{p}(\phi)=1
(i.e., every statement in the theory has probability
1
). Indeed, there is
\mathfrak{M}=\langle 2^{\mathbf{V}},\mu\rangle
s.t.
\mu\left(\left\|\phi\wedge\tau\right\|_{\mathfrak{M}}\right)>0
iff
\phi\wedge\tau
is
\mathsf{CPL}
-satisfiable. Observe also, the notion of preferred solutions refines that of
\subseteq
-minimal solutions: if
\sigma
and
\tau
are
\subseteq
-minimal, we can still prefer
\tau
if there is a probabilistic model
\mathfrak{M}=\langle 2^{\mathbf{V}},\mu\rangle
s.t.
\mu(\|\tau\|_{\mathfrak{M}})\geq\mu(\|\sigma\|_{\mathfrak{M}})
. Solutions to PrAPs can also be characterised in terms of
conditional probability
as follows.

Definition 25
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}{=}\langle 2^{\mathbf{V}},\mu\rangle
be a probabilisitic model,
\mathsf{Var}[\{\phi,\chi\}]{\subseteq}\mathbf{V}
, and
\mu(\|\chi\|_{\mathfrak{M}}){>}0
. The
conditional probability of
\phi
given
\chi
is defined as
\mathfrak{Pr}_{\mu}(\phi{\mid}\chi)=\tfrac{\mu(\|\phi\wedge\chi\|_{\mathfrak{M}})}{\mu(\|\chi\|_{\mathfrak{M}})}
.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{p}}=\langle\{\phi\},\chi,{\mathsf{H}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{p}\rangle
be a PrAP,
\mathbf{V}=\mathsf{Var}[\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{p}}]
, and
\tau
an
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{CPL}}
-term. Then
\tau
is a solution to
\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{p}}
iff the following conditions hold:

1.

\mu\left(\left\|\phi\wedge\tau\right\|_{\mathfrak{M}}\right)\leq\mu(\|\chi\|_{\mathfrak{M}})
in all probabilistic models;

2.

there is a probabilistic model
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}=\langle 2^{\mathbf{V}},\mu^{\prime}\rangle
s.t.
\mu^{\prime}
is coherent with
\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathfrak{Pr}_{\mu^{\prime}}\left(\chi\mid\phi\wedge\tau\right)=1
.

Let us now establish a translation of classical probabilistic abduction into
\mathsf{FP}
. As solutions to PrAPs are
events
and not
assertions about their probabilities
, we will show the correspondence between pairs of PrAPs and their solutions and
\mathsf{FP}
-theories that express the probabilities coherent with
\mathfrak{p}
.

Definition 26
.

Let
\mathbb{E}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{CPL}}
be finite and
\mathfrak{p}:\mathbb{E}\rightarrow[0,1]_{\mathbb{Q}}
. The
\mathsf{FP}
-counterpart of
\mathfrak{p}
is the following set of
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{\mathsf{Pr}}
-formulas:

\displaystyle\Xi_{\mathfrak{p}}

\displaystyle=\{\mathsf{Pr}(\phi){\approx}\overline{\mathsf{c}}\mid\phi\in\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{p}(\phi)=c\}

Theorem 10
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{p}}=\langle\{\phi\},\chi,{\mathsf{H}},\mathbb{E},\mathfrak{p}\rangle
be a PrAP,
\tau
an
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{CPL}}
-term, and
\mathbf{V}=\mathsf{Var}[\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{p}}]
. Then it holds that:

1.

\tau
is a solution to
\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{p}}
iff
\Xi_{\mathfrak{p}},\neg\triangle\neg\mathsf{Pr}(\phi\wedge\tau)\not\models_{\mathsf{FP}}\bot
and
\mathsf{FP}\models\mathsf{Pr}(\phi\wedge\tau)\rightarrow\mathsf{Pr}(\chi)
;

2.

\tau
is a preferred solution to
\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{p}}
iff, in addition to that,
\Xi_{\mathfrak{p}},\mathsf{Pr}(\sigma)\rightarrow\mathsf{Pr}(\tau)\not\models_{\mathsf{FP}}\bot
for every solution
\sigma
to
\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{p}}
.

Conclusion and Future Work

We considered probabilistic abduction formalised in a fuzzy probabilistic logic
\mathsf{FP}
. Our study provides a comprehensive picture of the complexity of full and sufficient solution recognition and existence. We also formalised
classical
probabilistic abduction in
\mathsf{FP}
, thus connecting two interpretations of probabilistic explanations: (1) the explanation
why the given event has the observed probability
and (2) a most likely explanation
why the given event occurs
.

We also observe that the results in Section
4
can be straightforwardly extended to abductive reasoning with
conditional
probabilities. For that, one can consider abduction in the logic
\mathsf{FP}_{k}(\mathsf{R}{{\textsf{\L}}}_{\triangle})

[
14
]
that extends
\mathsf{FP}
with multiplication by a rational number from
[0,1]
. Since
\mathsf{FP}_{k}(\mathsf{R}{{\textsf{\L}}}_{\triangle})
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete, the complexity is preserved. Even more expressive propositional ground logics can be employed to deal, for instance, with product operation. In this case, we should move to the probability logic
\mathsf{FP}({{\textsf{\L}}}\Pi)
based on the combination of Łukasiewicz and Product logics by Esteva et al. (
?
). Besides the increased expressive power, it is known from
[
17
]
that the satisfiability of
\mathsf{FP}({{\textsf{\L}}}\Pi)
is in
\mathsf{PSACE}
, which would make abductive reasoning in it highly intractable.

Several questions, however, remain open. First, we did not consider determining the relevance and necessity of hypotheses. That is, given an abduction problem
\mathbb{P}
and a hypothesis
h
, determine if
h
occurs in some (respectively, all) solutions of
\mathbb{P}
. We conjecture that the complexity of relevance and necessity of hypotheses to solutions of
\mathsf{FP}
APs would coincide with that of
Ł
-abduction problems. It also makes sense to consider relevance and necessity of PILs w.r.t.
full solutions
. Additionally, we plan to establish the exact complexity of recognising preferred solutions to PrAPs.

Another important task would be to provide a translation of
\mathsf{FP}
-abduction into
Ł
-abduction. Flaminio (
?
) constructed an embedding of
deductive
reasoning in fuzzy probabilistic into deductive reasoning in fuzzy propositional logic. Thus, it makes sense to extend their results and obtain a similar correspondence for abduction.

A further connection to investigate would be the one between abduction in
\mathsf{FP}
and alternative notions of explanations. In particular,
contrastive explanations

[
18
]
and
extended abduction
studied by Inoue and Sakama (
?
;
?
;
?
;
?
). Intuitively, a contrastive explanation of
\phi
is telling
why
\neg\phi
did not happen
. In extended abduction, an explanation entails the observation but not some undesired consequence. In a similar fashion, in
\mathsf{FP}
-abduction, an explanation why
\phi
has probability
20\%
also tells why
\phi

does not have
probability
25\%
.

Another question is that of
prioritisation
of full solutions. We considered preference based on the
principle of maximal entropy
, but there are other alternatives discussed by Dubois et al. (
?
). One of them is the principle of
maximal likelihood
studied by Inoue et al. (
?
) and Ishihata et al. (
?
). It would be instructive to consider the prioritisation of full solutions according to the maximal likelihood principle.

Finally, we plan to consider other notions of uncertainty. First, more expressive logics that can reason about independent events and conditional probabilities
[
17
]
or belief functions
[
16
]
. Second, fuzzy logics for
qualitative
probabilities
[
2
]
. For the latter, it will make sense to establish a correspondence between qualitative probabilistic abduction and preference abduction
[
21
,
25
]
where the explanation is a
comparative
statement about likelihoods of events.

{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathsf{Pr}}(\mathsf{Pr}(\pi)\diamond\overline{\mathsf{c}})\neq\varnothing

\diamond\in\{\leq,<\}

Observe that it takes polynomial time to verify that

\mathsf{FP}\models\mathsf{Pr}(\pi)\diamond\overline{\mathsf{c}}

For the converse direction, let
\mathsf{FP}\models\mathsf{Pr}(\pi)\diamond\overline{\mathsf{c}}
and assume for the sake of contradiction that neither of conditions (i)–(iii) holds. This means that

\mathsf{CPL}
-satisfiable but not
\mathsf{CPL}
-valid and
{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathsf{Pr}}(\mathsf{Pr}(\pi)\diamond\overline{\mathsf{c}})\neq[0,1]

there is an probabilistic model

-validity of

Finally, to check the
\mathsf{FP}

\mathsf{Pr}(\pi)\blackdiamond\overline{\mathsf{c}}

\mathsf{FP}
-valid. By the previous paragraph, this is decidable in polynomial time.

\diamond^{\prime}
