The effect of shade on steer performance after terminal sort
Authors: Madeline R Mancke, Brad J White, Eduarda M Bortoluzzi, Brian V Lubbers, Robert L Larson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeat stress negatively impacts performance and welfare and occurs when total environmental and metabolic thermal load exceeds an animal’s ability to dissipate heat. Access to shade has been associated with reductions in body temperature and respiratory rate in cattle, but previous findings on cattle performance has been inconsistent. This pen-level randomized controlled trial evaluated the effects of shade (2.8 m2/head, 100% solar block; n = 12 pens) versus no shade (n = 12 pens) on steer performance following terminal sort at a commercial feedyard in the Pacific Northwest, where animals were housed on roller-compacted concrete floors. Data collected included water consumption, feed delivery, disease events, open mouth breathing (OMB) behavior, and carcass traits. Shade reduced water consumption by 15% (P < 0.05), and pens without shade showed a 6% decrease in feed delivery when the previous day’s temperature-humidity index (THI) was ≥ 80 (P < 0.05). Additionally, shade reduced OMB behavior (P = 0.09), the number of culled steers (P < 0.05), and the incidence of dark cutters (P < 0.05). A greater proportion of steers in shaded pens graded choice and prime (93%), compared to those in unshaded pens (91%) (P < 0.05). Inclusion of shade improved animal health, welfare, carcass quality, and environmental stewardship during the summer months in a commercial feedyard setting. Shade should be considered a valuable management tool for mitigating heat stress in finishing cattle.Keywords: feedlot, heat mitigation, heat stress, shade, steers

Introduction

Heat stress occurs when total heat gain, both environmental and metabolic, exceeds the heat loss capabilities of the animal (Hahn and Becker 1984). To maintain homeostasis in response to heat stress, cattle respond behaviorally and physiologically. A review focused on dairy cattle by Polsky and Von Keyserlingk (2017) found that increased environmental heat load leads to increased hunger, thirst, frustration, aggression, and malaise, increased panting and decreased performance, as well as increased grouping and shade seeking behavior. An animal’s difficulty or failure to cope with their environment can be an indicator of poor welfare, becoming detrimental to their well-being (Broom 1986). Heat stress is a major concern in feedyards during the summer months due to increased environmental heat load. Physiologic responses in beef cattle due to increased heat load include increased body temperature (Robertshaw 1985), panting scores and breaths per minute (Gaughan et al. 2000; Mader et al. 2006) and sweating (Mader et al. 2010). Behaviorally, changes can be seen with increased standing time, time spent under shade or seeking shade, and body splashing at the water trough (Young and Hall 1993). Cattle under heat stress consume smaller meals and shift their feed intake to cooler parts of the day (Brown-Brandl et al. 2005; Ray and Roubicek 1971). Overall dry matter intake, growth, and feed efficiency is affected by high heat load (Brown-Brandl et al. 2005, 2006; Eigenberg et al. 2005). Regarding finishing cattle, water intake has been seen to increase 87.3% in the summer compared to the winter (Arias and Mader 2011). Decreased animal welfare and performance contribute to economic losses at the feedyard. In 2003, an economic assessment determined heat stress in the beef cattle industry is responsible for a U.S.$369 million loss (St-Pierre et al. 2003). Beef cattle at higher risk of heat stress include dark-hided cattle, cattle with higher body condition scores, and immunocompromised animals (Brown-Brandl et al. 2006).

Multiple methods exist to help mitigate heat stress in feedyards, such as nutritional management (Mader et al. 2002; Davis et al. 2003), and physical environmental modifications, such as water misting and shade (Edwards-Callaway et al. 2021; Azevedo et al. 2024). However, widespread adoption of shade in feedyards in the United States has been slow. Two surveys, conducted at feedyards in the High Plains region of the United States and of consulting cattle nutritionists, respectively, found only 17% of feedyards reported the use of shade structures in their pens (Samuelson et al. 2016; Simroth et al. 2017). This is much lower than the adaption of shade in other parts of the world. A recent report from Australia states that as of June 2024, there was an estimated 70.4% of feedlot cattle on feed that had access to shade, with a projected 83.5% of total capacity having access to shade by the end of 2026 (Meehan AgriBusiness Solutions 2026). Factors, such as high initial and maintenance cost of the shade, relatively short life span, and being an obstacle when cleaning pens, are common reasons for not providing shade to cattle.

Although substantial research has evaluated the effects of heat stress and shade in beef cattle, the dairy industry has generally received greater attention in this area, likely due to more immediate, detectable production responses to heat stress. Heat stress in dairy cattle is potentially easier to recognize than heat stress in beef cattle, as milk production losses are observed rather quickly after a heat event (Collier et al. 1981). In feedyards, a response to heat, seen in dry matter intake, can be noticed within 3 to 5 days (Curtis et al. 2017), however, performance differences in carcass traits (beef yield and quality) are not observed until harvest, if observed at all. Currently, published literature has been inconsistent with determining the effects of shade on cattle performance and behavior, as well as carcass traits.

A meta-analysis performed by Edwards-Callaway et al. (2021), using fifteen shade vs. no shade published manuscripts, found cattle provided shade had a significantly higher final bodyweight, greater average daily gain (ADG), and gain efficiency compared to cattle without shade; however, there were not any differences seen in dry matter intake. The authors believe this has to do with the type or breed of study cattle, frequency, duration, and severity of heat events, as well as the type of shade provided (amount and solar block). There has been no published literature determining the effects of shade on steer performance in the Pacific Northwest region of the United States, or on cattle housed on roller-compacted concrete. Concrete has relatively high thermal conductivity and heat capacity compared with many natural ground surfaces, allowing it to absorb and retain heat for extended periods (Asadi et al. 2018). Roller compacted concrete has been implemented into feedyard pen construction recently, and one question posed is how the concrete pen floors will impact thermal load. The objective of this study was to compare performance and behavior outcomes of terminally sorted steers allowed shade to steers allowed no shade housed on roller compacted concrete in a commercial feedyard in the Pacific Northwest. The authors hypothesize that performance and behavior outcomes will be positively impacted in steers allowed shade.

Materials and methods

All procedures described within this study were evaluated by the Animal Care and Use Committee at Kansas State University (IACUC-5013-DFT). Sample size was determined by logistical constraints of the feedyard and the number of shaded pens available.

Animals and study timeline

This study was performed at a commercial feedlot in the Pacific Northwest region of the United States. The feedyard location is in a dry, arid region, with an annual rainfall between 200 and 230 mm and an average temperature in the month of July being around 33 degrees Celsius (Climate-Charts.com 2025). The Köppen climate classification describes this area as a cold, semi-arid climate (Peel et al. 2007). The cattle used in this study were predominantly crossbred Bos taurus beef steers with variable breed composition that were scheduled for terminal sort, approximately 60 days from their lots projected feedyard exit date. Steers were enrolled in this randomized controlled trial between 11 June and 2 July 2024. Terminal sorting events allow the feedyard to manage and market a more homogenous group, based on hip height and weight of the animal. Six pens of cattle were enrolled weekly, on a rolling basis, for a total of four weeks (blocks), totaling 24 pens. At terminal sort, each steer was reimplanted (200 mg trenbolone acetate and 28 mg estradiol benzoate, Synovex Plus, Zoetis, Parsippany, NJ). Body weight and hip measurements were used (PenPoint Sort, Elanco Animal Health, Greenfield, IN) to determine the respective terminal sort group for that individual animal; heavy (T3), medium (T2), or light (T1). The sorting system used was set to allow each terminal sort group to have similar head counts.

After each individual animal was given its respective sort group number, they were then randomly allocated to shade (S) or no shade (NS) treatment groups using a random number generator (Microsoft Excel, version 2503, Microsoft Corp, Redmond, WA). Each week (block, n = 4), six pens were enrolled: T3 shade, T3 no shade, T2 shade, T2 no shade, T1 shade, T1 no shade. After four weeks, 24 pens total had been enrolled in the trial (S; n = 12, NS; n = 12). A visual depiction of pen enrollment is shown in Fig. 1. Pen-location within block was not adjacent in order to keep cattle with the same lot visual identifiers separated in case of broken cable fencing and potentially mixed pens. Pen size allowed for approximately 350 animals, with 13.9 m2 of pen space per head. All study pen floors were roller-compacted concrete (RCC). The shaded pens had permanent steel roofed shade structures (100% solar block) in the center of the pen, 15.2 m wide and 30.5 m long, with the height of the roof spanning from 4.3 m tall to 6.7 m tall (16% grade). This allowed for approximately 2.8 m2 of shade per animal, and no heat trapped under the roof due to the slope. Due to the nature of the shade structure, blinding of treatment groups in this study was not possible. Pen served as the experimental and observational unit.

Visualization of pen enrollment to shade or no shade. Pen enrollment visual, including block, pen, and treatment group, depicting pens enrolled at terminal sort either into shade or no shade at a commercial feedyard in the Pacific Northwest in the summer of 2024.

Steers were fed a finishing diet of flaked corn, high moisture corn, potato byproduct, alfalfa hay, wet distillers grain, liquid finisher, and micro ingredients (56.5% DM, 13.1% DM protein, 62.7 Mcal/cwt net energy for gain) for the duration of the study. Lubabegron (Experior, Elanco Animal Health, Greenfield, IN) was fed for the last 42 days before the pens projected ship date, with a wash out period of at least 5 days before harvest. All study animals were harvested at a commercial beef packing plant approximately 96.5 kilometers from the feedyard between 4 August and 9 October, 2024. Paired pens (example: block 1 T1 S and block 1 T1 NS) were shipped to harvest on separate days of the same week. Carcass reports were collected from the packing plant to determine final live weight (FLW), hot carcass weight (HCW), quality grade, and yield grade. Pen level average daily gain (ADG) was calculated using the average FLW minus average enrollment weight, and average study days on feed (DOF). Pen level feed conversion (F:G) was calculated using the average FLW minus average enrollment weight, divided by the average sum of feed delivered on a dry-matter basis (DM). These calculations followed standard beef cattle performance calculations (Nutrient Requirements of Beef Cattle 2015). Any study pen with a head count discrepancy more than 15% from enrollment head count was removed from further statistical analysis (n = 4 S and 1 NS). This threshold was chosen to avoid potential bias from pens where a substantial number of carcass reports were missing due incomplete data from the packing plant. To ensure that the carcass data represented a reliable sample of the original pen population, pens with greater than a 15% head count discrepancy were removed from further analysis.

Daily sampling

Daily data collections included water consumption, feed delivery, disease events, and open mouth breathing (OMB) behavior. Pens had fence line waterers, with both pens on each side of the water being from the same treatment group. Each waterer had a flow meter, allowing water flow to be measured in total liters, and extrapolated to water consumption. Water consumption was recorded starting when the first pen to use the waterer was enrolled until August 4. Each day, at 1400 h, water consumption was recorded for each waterer, as well as the waterers respective pens head count and approximate body weight. Any water leak visible at the 1400 h was noted. Daily liters consumed per 453.6 kg of body weight was used as the outcome. Head count and estimated daily body weight (determined through the feedyards management software system) were used to determine the approximate total kg of animal for each waterer. Temperature-humidity index (THI) was determined by an equation previously published (THI = [0.8 × ambient temperature] + [(% relative humidity ÷ 100) × (ambient temperature − 14.4)] + 46.4) (Mader et al. 2006) and recorded daily at 1600 h by a weather station located at the feedyard. A THI threshold of greater than or equal to 80 was used to differentiate the potential for heat stress. This cutoff was determined based on industry guidance previously described (Ishmael 2011; Texas A&M AgriLife Extension 2022). For all statistical analyses, THI was binomialized to below 80 THI, or greater than or equal to 80.

Pen level feed delivery was collected, daily, through the feedyards data management system (Micro Technologies, AmerisourceBergen, Amarillo, TX) on a DM per head basis for the entirety of the study. Disease event outcomes were also collected, daily, through the feedyards management system (Micro Technologies, AmerisourceBergen, Amarillo, TX) by recording the number of morbidities, mortalities, and culling for the entirety of the study. Culling was defined in this study as any animal that shipped to harvest or culled before the projected ship date for that pen. Feedyard personnel evaluated cattle daily for signs of illness and all treatment and culling decisions were based on existing protocols in place by the consulting veterinarian.

Open mouth breathing (OMB) behavior was determined by the proportion of OMB within study pens from day of enrollment to August 4 at 1600 h. Due to time constraints, each pen was randomly allocated to one of four days (six pens per day) for OMB assessment. Every four days, the same pen was reassessed. One picture was taken from the front of the pen, and one picture was taken from the back of the pen. A single, trained observer then counted the number of animals in each picture that were standing in that half (front or back) of the pen, with their faces and mouths fully visible. Due to assessment through still pictures, only OMB was able to be recorded. The case definition of OMB in this study included standing, mouth open, saliva present, and neck extended. This relates closest to a panting score of 4 in regards to Mader et al. (2006). This represents severe heat stress only, and does not account for total heat stress behavior.

Statistical analysis

Statistical models were built using R Studio (version 2024.12.0, PBC, Boston, MA). Pen served as the experimental and observational unit. Linear mixed effects models (“lme” function of R Studio) were utilized for continuous outcomes (identity link function): enrollment total pen weight, enrollment head count, enrollment average weight per head, water consumption, feed delivery, FLW, HCW, ADG and F:G. Generalized linear mixed effects models (logit link function), using events over trials (“glmer” function of R Studio) were used for disease event outcomes, OMB behavior, yield grades, quality grades, and dark cutter count. Treatment group (S or NS) was considered a fixed effect for all outcomes. Terminal sort group nested within week of enrollment (block) was considered a random effect to account for the lack of independence between groups; however, sort group was removed for the enrollment weight models because they were purposefully enrolled at different weights. For the water consumption and OMB models, THI category of that day (<80 or ≥ 80) was included as a fixed effect, and waterer or pen number were treated as a random effect to account for repeated measures. For the feed delivery model, the previous day THI was used in the statistical model because feed calls are based on previous day intake and pen number was treated as a random effect to account for repeated measures. For the OMB behavior model, location of picture taken (front or back) was considered a random effect as well as pen number as a random effect to account for repeated measures. For repeated measure outcomes (water consumption, feed delivery, OMB), a repeated covariance structure was used, including calendar date to account for serial dependence. Yield grade counts were categorized for statistical analysis as yield grade 1, 2, and 3 versus 4 and 5. Quality grade counts were categorized as prime and choice versus select. To stabilize variance and better meet the assumptions of normality for parametric analyses, a Box-Cox transformation was applied to the dependent variable, determined by a Shapiro-Wilks test and visually examining histograms. The optimal lambda (λ) value was determined using maximum likelihood estimation. Box-Cox transformations were performed on water consumption, ADG, and F:G to obtain normality. For transformed data, the least square means is reported as the back-transformed value, with the standard error of the mean (SEM) reported as the transformed value. In each model, factors were considered significant if p ≤ 0.05. For significant interaction effects, Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons were performed within levels of the interacting factors.

Economic analysis

An economic analysis was performed on a per head basis for each treatment group using a similar method previously published (Dennis et al. 2020). Net returns were calculated by using total revenue minus feeder cost, yardage cost, feeding cost, health cost, and interest cost. Initial cost and maintenance costs of the shade were not included in the economic analysis; therefore, this is based on returns based on production outcomes. Total revenue was calculated using the fed cattle price ($/kg) for the week of 2 September 2024 (Cattle Markets 2024), which was the middle of the shipping period, average finish weight of treatment group, a 5% shrink, and the average weight of the culled animals, with their return set at 75% of the fed cattle sale price. Feeder cost was calculated on a slide using average differences of weight groups of the feeder price ($/kg) for the week of 17 June 2024 (USDA National Feeder Cattle Summary 2024), which was the middle of the enrollment period, the average enrollment weight, and the number of head enrolled in each treatment group. Yardage cost was determined by a national average of USD$0.30/head day and the DOF for each treatment group. Feeding cost was calculated by using the average price of corn for the week of 22 July 2024 (Agricultural Prices 2024), which was halfway through the study period, calculated average feed conversion, finish weight, and percent mortality and railers. Health costs were determined by the percent morbidity and cost, using USD$23.60/treatment (USDA 2013). Interest rate was calculated using the monthly prime rate for the month of July 2024 (FedPrimeRate.com).

Discussion

The objective of the current study was to compare performance and behavior outcomes after terminal sort between steers allowed access to shade and steers without shade housed on roller compacted concrete in a commercial feedyard in the Pacific Northwest. As evidenced in Fig. 2, it was a moderate summer regarding heat, with no extreme heat events occurring. Results show beneficial impacts to carcass quality, environmental sustainability, and animal welfare and health.

In the current study, there was a statistically significant interaction between the treatment group (S or NS) and THI groups regarding feed delivery. Steers in the NS group were shown to have a 3% decrease in feed delivery when the THI of the previous day was above the threshold of 80, whereas no differences were observed in the S group. Because the cattle are limit fed, we are assuming that feed delivery and intake are similar. Previous studies show that beef cattle allowed shade had a higher dry matter intake compared to cattle not allowed shade (Mader et al. 1999; Mitlöhner et al. 2001, 2002; Barajas et al. 2013; Hagenmaier et al. 2016), however, other studies show no difference in dry matter intake (Mader et al. 1999; Boyd et al. 2015; Hayes et al. 2017). One study reported the interaction of feed intake and different categories of THI between a shade and no shade group and results show that the no shade group had a higher intake in the “normal” THI category compared to the shaded group, however the reverse was true in the “emergency” THI category, with the shaded group having a higher intake than the no shade group (Brown-Brandl et al. 2005). Differences seen in literature, and described here, can be affected by cattle type, severity and duration of heat events or time of year, and type of shade (shade area and solar block).

Cattle daily response to thermal load was assessed by proportion of OMB and the NS group tended to have a greater probability of OMB compared to the S group. Though the probability was small in the NS group (approximately 0.9% OMB), this may be the result of multiple factors, such as logistical constraints of monitoring OMB; pictures were taken, and only extreme panting (OMB) would be noted. Therefore, this does not account for total heat stress behavior. Another factor leading to the low reported OMB may have been the low number of days during the study period where THI was not elevated enough to exceed the cattle comfort threshold, causing severe enough heat stress to exacerbate OMB in the pens. The current results are similar to previously published literature, with animals in the unshaded groups panting more or having an increased respiration rate compared to the shaded group (Brown-Brandl et al. 2005; Gaughan et al. 2010; Aengwanich et al. 2011; Blaine and Nsahlai 2011; Sullivan et al. 2011; Van Laer et al. 2015; Hagenmaier et al. 2016; Hayes et al. 2017). The differences seen in the current study prove the benefits of shade on animal welfare, by seeing decreases in culling, dark cutters, and OMB behavior.

Behavioral and physiological processes involved in heat dissipation result in water loss, therefore water intake becomes crucial to prevent dehydration and aid in a small amount of thermal heat mitigation (Gebremedhin et al. 2008). The inclusion of shade in pens was shown to significantly impact water consumption. In the current study, the S group had a 15% decrease in daily water consumption compared to NS group. This decrease was seen without subsequent changes identified in feed delivery or overall performance between the two treatment groups. Two previously published studies reported information concerning water consumption. One study noted a decrease in daily water consumption with the addition of shade compared to the non-shaded group (53.1 and 49.3 L/d, respectively) (Gaughan et al. 2010). Another study found no differences between shaded groups (square meters of shade per animal), but a significant decrease in water usage by cattle allowed shade versus not allowed shade (Sullivan et al. 2011). With increased concern regarding the environmental impacts of the feedyard and the limited water availability in certain areas of North America, shaded pens show a beneficial impact on water conservation and thus environmental sustainability. As thirst is a negative affective state in animals, a decrease in water consumption with the addition of shade proves beneficial to the animals’ affective states.

In the current study, no differences were observed between the S and NS group in morbidity, mortality, or did not finish (mortalities and culls combined). However, there was a 50% decrease in the count of culls in the S group compared to the NS group. Though blinding was not possible in the current study, feedyard staff were making the culling decisions at the hospital, away from the study pens. One potential explanation for this finding is the lack of shade may have increased heat load and previously diseased or immuno-compromised animals may have decreased chances for recovery with increased thermal stress, as seen in Brown-Brandl et al. (2006) as a risk factor for heat stress. A decrease in the number of culls with the inclusion of shade benefits production and animal welfare. Cattle’s capacity for thermoregulation is compromised when there is an active disease process (Lemal et al. 2023), in turn impacting the animals affective states. Therefore, culling becomes a humane option while considering the prognosis of these animals. To the authors’ knowledge, there are no other published manuscripts evaluating the effect of shade on culling rates.

Regarding steer performance, the current study only found statistically significant differences when comparing the proportion prime and choice versus select between the S and NS groups. Pens with the inclusion of shade were seen to have a higher proportion of steers grading prime and choice compared to non-shaded pens. Authors believe the higher proportion of quality grade prime and choice is due to the consistent feed intake seen in the S group compared to NS. Shade showing improvements to quality grade was also seen in another study comparing shade to no shade with beef cattle performance (Mitlöhner et al. 2002), though many studies did not find a difference in quality grade (Mitlöhner et al. 2001; Blaine and Nsahlai 2011; Hagenmaier et al. 2016). The current study did not find any differences when comparing FLW, HCW, yield grade, ADG, and F:G. Previously published literature had inconsistent results on the effect of shade on steer performance with some studies illustrating no detectable differences in FLW (Mader et al. 1999; Sullivan et al. 2011; Rovira, 2014), HCW (Mader et al. 1999; Sullivan et al. 2011; DiGiacomo et al. 2014; Boyd et al. 2015; Hagenmaier et al. 2016), ADG (Rovira and Velazco 2010; Brown-Brandl 2013; Rovira 2014; Hagenmaier et al. 2016; Hayes et al. 2017), or feed conversion (Mader et al. 1999; Mitlöhner et al. 2001, 2002; Boyd et al. 2015). However, some studies identified differences in FLW (Mitlöhner et al. 2001, 2002; Blaine and Nsahlai 2011; Boyd et al. 2015), HCW (Gaughan et al. 2010; Blaine and Nsahlai 2011), ADG (McIlvain and Shoop 1971; Mitlöhner et al. 2001, 2002; Gaughan et al. 2010; Blaine and Nsahlai 2011; Barajas et al. 2013; Boyd et al. 2015), or feed conversion (Gaughan et al. 2010; Barajas et al. 2013), showing performance benefits in the shaded groups.

The probability of dark cutters was significantly different, with S pens having approximately 50% lower dark cutters compared to NS groups. This finding was also observed in a previous study, where the proportion of dark cutters in heat-stressed heifers allowed shade was half of the dark cutters from the heat-stressed heifers not allowed shade (Mitlöhner et al. 2002). Prolonged, or chronic, stress before harvest is considered to be the primary cause of dark cutters, with a variety of events, such as environmental stress, playing a role (Scanga et al. 1998; Ponnampalam et al. 2017). Chronic stress that leads to detrimental effects on muscle glycogen depletion, that lead to observing dark cutters, has been well documented in slaughter facilities (Edwards-Callaway et al. 2025). A study composed of nine commercial feedlots over a three-year period showed the incidence of dark cutters rises with increasing temperatures (Scanga et al. 1998), while a study conducted in Australia found that higher maximum ambient temperature, prior to feedlot exit, increased the incidence of dark cutters throughout a 3-, 7-, 14-, and 28-day lag period, though the differences were minimal (Steel et al. 2022). Decreasing the number of dark cutters not only improves quality grade and financial returns, but also shows an improvement in the welfare of these animals before harvest.

One major concern for producers is the cost and financial return from the shade structures. A study estimated the initial cost of investing in artificial shade structures to be USD$90.00 per head (Maia et al. 2023). The economic analysis performed for this study showed beneficial to the S group, with a $11.00 increase in net return per head for each steer enrolled at terminal sort. This shows potential for a large enough difference in the net returns to allow the increase in cattle performance to help offset the cost of the shade. However, these economic calculations were limited to the last 70 days on feed, using national averages for feed costs and cattle prices during that period. Therefore, these results only provide a short-term economic perspective and do not include broader sensitivity analyses of feed or cattle price variation.

Limitations in this study include a specific study population, mild heat during the study period, and limited power to detect differences in treatment groups. Native beef steers, including both light and dark hided, were enrolled at terminal sort, approximately 60 days from harvest. There were only 19 days that passed the THI threshold of 80 (“danger” or “emergency” zone of the beef cattle temperature humidity chart) during the study period, proving the summer was milder compared to previous years. This study was a pen level study, with 12 S and 12 NS pens. The numbers per treatment group was determined based on feedyard logistics and how many pens had the permanent shade. After the exclusion of 4 S and 1 NS pen due to the lack of carcass reports obtained, power to detect differences was lessened. Pen exclusions occurred due to reasons unrelated to treatment effects, and therefore are unlikely to have introduced systematic bias. Data were analyzed using mixed models that accommodates unequal numbers of pens per treatment. Nonetheless, this potentially limited the ability to detect statistically significant differences in performance and carcass traits, if there truly was a difference between groups. A greater sample size may possibly be able to detect significant differences in more performance and carcass outcomes. Limitations regarding open mouth breathing behavior include the observation of only a subset of pens each day and the use of photographic assessment detecting more severe panting behavior. However, due to the large number of cattle in each pen (approximately 350 steers), panting behavior was evaluated using pen-level photographic scan sampling, which minimized animal disturbance and allowed observations to be collected consistently and within a similar environmental time frame across pens.

Further research is warranted on other heat stress mitigation strategies that could be used alone, or in combination, to help mitigate heat stress, such as misting cattle, fans, bedding type, or different feeding strategies. The implementation of shade in the U.S. feedyard sector is slow, despite the multiple benefits on beef cattle performance and welfare. Common reasons for not providing shade include the initial cost and investment for the structures, as well as the questionable need for shade in some regions of the United States. The current study demonstrates the benefits of shade on carcass quality, animal welfare, and environmental sustainability under moderate heat stress conditions in a temperate region. Producers should treat shade as being another tool in the toolbox for mitigating heat stress, especially while prioritizing shade to the higher risk cattle for heat stress (eg dark-hided, later DOF).
