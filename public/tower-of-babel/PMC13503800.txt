Development and validation of the Life after Stroke questionnaire as a patient-reported outcome measure for stroke survivors living at home
Authors: Wolfgang Brunner, Christoph Zenzmaier, Nina Huber, Julian Granna, Kurt Mölgg, Benjamin Dejakum, Silvia Felicetti, Anel Karisik, Stefan Kiechl, Christian Boehme, Michael Knoflach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerebrovascular disease is a leading cause of death and permanent neurological impairment worldwide. The goal of this study is to develop and validate a patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) that evaluates the daily lives of stroke survivors living at home. The questionnaire was developed using a four-phase procedure based on guidelines from the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Group. The phases included a literature review, semi-structured interviews with stroke patients, construction of the questionnaire, pre-testing with 20 patients, and validation with 216 patients. The statistical analyses included descriptive statistics, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), Cronbach’s alpha, and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients. The questionnaire demonstrated high test-retest reliability and strong correlations with established measures (SIS and EQ-5D-3L). The final questionnaire covered topics such as independence in daily activities, psychological and social well-being, and quality of life. The developed PROM “Life after Stroke” is a comprehensive and practical tool for evaluating the lives of stroke survivors who live at home. Its validity and reliability make it suitable for research and routine assessments (initial assessment and documentation of progress in long-term care) by healthcare providers.Trial registration: The study was registered in the German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS Deutsches Register Klinischer Studien) on September 1st, 2022 (DRKSID DRKS00029484). Introduction

Stroke is one of the leading causes of death worldwide and, more importantly, of permanent acquired disability1. In Europe, approximately 1.5 million people suffer a stroke each year2 and absolute numbers are going to rise due to the ageing population. Structured post-stroke disease management programs supporting patients and their caregivers are urgently needed and resemble one of the aims of the Stroke Action Plan for Europe3. Supporting patients after a stroke is challenging due to magnitude of challenges that can arise ranging from medical complications, rehabilitation demands, social4,5 and psychological problems6, secondary prevention issues to nursing demands7–9. Numerous checklists and assessment tools are available. Many of the commonly used scales, such as the modified Barthel Index (mBI)10, Functional Independence Measure (FIM), and Functional Assessment Measure (FAM)11, modified Rankin Scale (mRS)12 are based on external assessments by medical professionals (e.g., nurses, physical therapists, occupational therapists, physicians). Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are increasingly recognized as valuable tools for collecting information about the impact of a disease and its treatment from the patient’s perspective13. The EQ-5D-3L and the SF–36 are commonly used PROMs for assessing the health-related quality of life of stroke patients14,15. The German version of the Stroke Impact Scale Version 3.0 (SIS) is a reliable and valid PROM that assesses abilities, participation, and well-being in various relevant areas16. The PROMs mentioned above examine the degree of impairment, but they do not differentiate between the extent to which this impairment is compensated for using care aids. This information is essential for optimizing the care and support (e.g. by community nurses, home health care nurses, primary care physicians) of those affected in their home environment. This study aimed to develop and validate a structured questionnaire focusing on coping strategies and nursing demands to fill this gap. The primary purpose of the “Life after Stroke” (LaS) questionnaire is to support structured assessment and follow-up of post-stroke patients in the home setting. It is intended to guide healthcare professionals (e.g. mobile nursing services, primary care physicians) in identifying patients’ needs and to support decision-making regarding appropriate nursing and rehabilitative interventions, while also enabling longitudinal monitoring over time. Additionally, the LaS could serve as a tool in future research projects addressing the living situation and needs of stroke survivors in their home environment.

Methods

The questionnaire was developed based on the four-phase process for creating questionnaire modules by the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Group17, as described by the EORTC18. The development process comprised four phases: (I) creation of a comprehensive list of relevant topics covering all areas of interest through literature research and semi-structured interviews; (II) creation of an item list based on the literature review and interviews; (III) testing of the questionnaire; and (IV) field testing and validation19. The study was conducted at the Department of Neurology at the Medical University of Innsbruck. Patients were recruited via the post-stroke disease management program based on the STROKE-CARD concept20. The inclusion criteria for all phases were: (i) a diagnosis of ischemic stroke, (ii) a minimum age of 18 years, (iii) no or mild cognitive impairment that allows the questionnaire to be answered, (iv) fluency in German, and (v) a signed informed consent form. The study was approved by the local ethics committee of the Medical University of Innsbruck (App. No. 1456/2021). All patients gave written consent to participate in the study.

Phase I

Phase I aimed to create a comprehensive list of relevant aspects concerning stroke survivors living at home through literature research and semi-structured interviews. A systematic literature search was conducted by two researchers in the databases CINAHL Complete & Ultimate and MEDLINE via EBSCO Host, as well as MEDLINE via PubMed21. Additionally, literature was searched via ScienceDirect and Google Scholar. Further research was carried out in the libraries of the Health University of Applied Sciences Tyrol and the Medical University of Innsbruck. After removing duplicates, studies eligible for full‑text review were selected based on the screening of their titles and abstracts. Following a first full‑text analysis, additional studies were excluded based on their content. The results and discussion sections of the remaining studies were independently examined by four researchers using an inductive approach to identify relevant aspects as objectively and unbiasedly as possible. All identified aspects were subsequently organized into topics, which were discussed among the researchers until full consensus was achieved. Based on this review, a semi-structured interview guide was designed to identify additional topics related to the patient’s needs with a focus on coping strategies. The interview guide was designed to be as open as possible and comprised the following categories: (i) Coming home, (ii) Discharge planning, (iii) Coping with everyday life, (iv) Social environment, (v) psychological aspects and (vi) an open question of what the participants would recommend to stroke patients who have just been discharged home. Eight patients with a broad array of neurological deficits in their activities of daily living (ADLs) were selected for this phase. The interviews were transcribed using F422 and analyzed by four researchers using deductive (based on the topics identified in the literature review) and inductive content analysis to reveal potential additional topics relevant to stroke survivors. Based on the findings from the interviews and the literature review, the topics to be addressed in the LaS questionnaire were developed by consensus in several team meetings.

Phase II

The relevant aspects identified in Phase I were clustered into four topic areas by a team of five researchers, who discussed and refined the categorizations across several meetings. It was decided to begin the LaS with items assessing socio-demographic characteristics and the need for support at home (Topic Area A). Categories related to stroke‑related functional impairments (e.g., activities of daily living, sleep quality) and associated coping strategies were grouped into Topic Area B. As the LaS was specifically developed as a tool for the initial assessment and ongoing documentation of progress in the long‑term home care of stroke survivors, Topic Area B examines in greater detail those impairments for which community nurses, home healthcare nurses, and other healthcare providers can offer direct support. For each life domain surveyed, it assesses the perceived degree of independence (e.g., need for assistance from others, use of specific aids) and whether unresolved difficulties in daily life remain.

Topic Area C was designed to include general questions on health and well‑being, additional single‑item questions concerning mental and physical well‑being and social life (Block C3; without structured assessment of coping strategies), as well as an overall quality‑of‑life rating covering the main domains represented in Topic Areas B and C. Block C3 addresses aspects of daily living for which a community nurse or home healthcare nurse is typically unable to provide a sustainable solution independently. Consequently, these aspects are not assessed in detail. If a stroke survivor experiences difficulties in these areas, the involvement of specialized healthcare providers — such as consulting physicians, clinical psychologists, or psychotherapists — is recommended.

Topic Area D was planned to consist of a single open‑ended question allowing participants to provide any additional information they wished to add.

When formulating individual items, attention was given to clarity of expression, logical arrangement, and anticipated response time. The structure and wording of the initial questionnaire were reviewed and discussed within the research team until full consensus was achieved.

Phase III

The objective of Phase III was to pre-test the initial LaS with a group of stroke survivors who provided feedback to identify potential issues (e.g. linguistic clarity, question order, missing questions, identification of irrelevant questions) with the questionnaire. Furthermore, the test determined whether the participants were able to devote sufficient attention and interest to the task at hand.

The preliminary questionnaire was completed by a group of 20 stroke patients not involved in Phase I. Subsequently, the participants were invited to provide their feedback on the feasibility of the questionnaire. Therefore, an additional column was added to each individual item to assess its comprehensibility (participants were asked to indicate whether they found each item understandable by selecting “Yes” or “No”). Participants were asked to provide further feedback through the completion of five open-ended debriefing questions:

Phase IV

The objective of Phase IV was to analyze reliability, validity, and applicability of the questionnaire in a larger sample. Deviating from the EORTC Guidelines17, Phase 4 was planned and conducted as a monocentric test. The questionnaires for the first assessment of the test-retest were sent to the patients’ home addresses, and the patients were asked to complete the questionnaires shortly before their routine outpatient visits three or twelve months after the ischemic stroke. To assess applicability, the following debriefing questions were added at the end:

In addition, participants completed the Stroke Impact Scale (SIS) and EQ-5D-3L. It was anticipated that all questionnaires would require approximately 60 min to complete. For test-retest reliability, the LaS, enclosed and franked, was given to the participants for completion at home. Over a period of seven to ten days, participants were contacted by phone to provide further information and to remind them to complete the questionnaire and return it to the researchers. A sample size of 200 was proposed based on the recommendation for case and variable studies for Phase IV23.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.324. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the study cohort in the Phase IV validation study. Test-retest reliability was assessed using the two-way random-effects model, absolute agreement, single measurements intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC)25 using the psych package. The interpretation of ICC values followed the guideline by Koo and Li (2016), where values below 0.50 indicate poor reliability, between 0.50 and 0.75 indicate moderate reliability, between 0.75 and 0.90 indicate good reliability, and values above 0.90 indicate excellent reliability26. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s α for the ten multiitem blocks of questions in topic Area B. In accordance with Nunnally and Bernstein (1994), a Cronbach’s α value of ≥ 0.70 was considered to indicate acceptable internal consistency27; no Cronbach’s alpha was calculated for topic area C because it consists of individual items that are not organized into predefined blocks of questions. Item-total correlations (corrected for part-whole overlap) were computed for each block of questions in topic area B to assess the discriminatory power of each item. Items with a corrected item-total correlation below 0.30 were considered insufficiently discriminatory and removed from their respective blocks of questions. The SIS and EQ-5D-3L were chosen as comparator instruments during the study planning phase because of their established role in stroke outcome research and conceptual relevance for the LaS questionnaire domains. The direction of associations was defined beforehand (i.e., stronger correlations between related domains and weaker correlations between unrelated domains). However, the assignment of LaS blocks and items to specific comparator domains was not fully predefined and was partly refined after finalizing the questionnaire based on conceptual considerations. Construct validity was evaluated by calculating Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients between the ten blocks of questions of topic area B and the corresponding dimensions of the SIS, as well as between each item in topic area C and the dimensions of the SIS and EQ-5D-3L. The strength of the monotonic relationship was interpreted using Cohen’s criteria28, where coefficients of ρ ≥ 0.10, ≥ 0.30, and ≥ 0.50 represented small, medium, and large effect sizes, respectively. Since the LaS is intended to investigate the needs and applied coping strategies in detail, no total scores for the individual topics in area B or the overall questionnaire were calculated. The LaS should be evaluated on a single item basis.

Results

Phase I

A literature search conducted between February and April 2022 identified 211 publications. After removing duplicates and excluding studies based on title and abstract screening, 42 studies were selected for full-text review. Of these, 13 studies were identified as relevant for exploring life at home after stroke. From these studies, 12 topics (Limitations include motor skills (hemiplegia); communication; dysphagia; urination control; defecation control; personal hygiene; dressing; mobility; cognitive abilities; transferring to the shower or toilet; perception; social isolation) were derived that provide information on potential problems at home. Additional topics were derived from eight semi-structured interviews conducted with stroke survivors (four men, four women, one of which was severely affected). These interviews revealed additional topics that potentially affect life at home, in particular psychological aspects, such as loss of physical closeness, fear of recurrence, worries about relatives, sadness, or depression that were subsequently summarized in six additional topics (Information and preparation for life at home; concern for relatives; loss of social contacts; fear of another stroke; coping with illness; loss of ability to function after the event). The 18 topics developed from the literature review and the interviews were used to design the initial version of the LaS in Phase II.

Phase II

Based on the literature search and the interview transcripts from Phase I, a team of five researchers discussed and clustered the findings in several meetings to finally establish the preliminary questionnaire.

The LaS generated based on the 18 categories is divided into four Topic Areas as outlined in the methods section. Topic Area A contains questions on the living situation: demographic data, level of education and level of care (independence, partial care by relatives or care services, full-time care by relatives or care services). Topic Area B analyses the effects of stroke on various areas of life and is divided into ten question blocks (B1 – B10): B1 Rest and sleep, B2 Mobility and movement, B3 Personal hygiene, B4 Dressing, B5 Language Communication, B6 Food preparation, B7 Food and fluid intake, B8 Excretion, B9 Household management and B10 Medication. Within each question block, the items represent different, non-ordinal categories of coping and support (e.g., experienced difficulties, reliance on support from others, use of assistive devices, or absence of a coping strategy/unsolved problems). The order of items does not imply increasing severity or support needs. Each item is interpreted independently, with the four-point response scale (strongly agree, somewhat agree, somewhat disagree, strongly disagree) reflecting the perceived relevance of the statement for the respondent. The question blocks of the initial version consisted of five to seven items per block. Topic Area C begins with two questions on the state of health and well-being (C1 and C2) and a subjective assessment using a five-point Likert scale. Question block C3 comprises nine items on the topics of psychological and physical well-being, social relationships, sexuality, coming to terms with the stroke and looking to the future. The items are asked for using a four-point Likert scale (strongly agree, somewhat agree, somewhat disagree, strongly disagree). Question Block C4 assesses perceived quality of life related to the question blocks from topic Area B and the question block C3, again using a four-point Likert scale. Topic Area D consists of a field for personal concerns and can be freely formulated. The questionnaire was designed as a PROM, whereby support from relatives or carers is possible in the event of motor impairments during completion or limitations in reading the questions. The principal structure on the questionnaire was identical with the final version shown in Supplemental Files 1 and 2, containing additional 12 items in Topic Area B.

Phase IV

A total of 216 stroke patients took part in this phase of the study. Of these, 171 completed the questionnaire at 2 time points (test - retest). The average age was 68 years (range 30–92 years). Most of the participants were male (61%), 48% were interviewed 3 months and 52% 12 months after stroke. At the time of the survey, 70% of the participants were coping with their daily lives without the support of relatives or carers (Table 2).

Of a total of 216 participants, 164 completed the questionnaire independently, 44 with support from relatives and 2 with support from carers (no response n = 6). 29.6% of the validation cohort needed support at home, with 7.9% full-time support by relatives or nursing services. Modified Rankin Scale (mRS) data were available for a subsample of 164 participants. 161 recorded scores were ≤ 3, indicating that the study population predominantly consisted of individuals with mild to moderate functional impairment. The participants stated that they needed a median of 20 min to answer the questions (n = 140). There was no feedback on the debriefing questions on comprehensibility.

Topic Area B of the LaS measured the degree of independence in coping with everyday life and was divided into 10 question blocks, each with 5 to 7 items regarding the individual areas. To assess discriminatory power of the individual items, the item-total correlation (including part-whole correction) within the individual question blocks was calculated. This revealed that 1 to a maximum of 3 items per question block had a discriminatory power of < 0.3. The items in question were discussed by the research team regarding comprehensibility and content. 12 items allowed a misleading interpretation and were sufficiently covered in other items. They were therefore removed from the questionnaire, and the item-total correlation was recalculated based on the remaining items (Table 3), with 40 of 43 items having a discriminatory power > 0.3. To determine the validity of the topic Area C of the LaS, the individual items were correlated with the dimensions of the SIS and the dimensions of the EQ-5D-3L questionnaire (Table 7). The dimension of strength of the SIS correlated most strongly with satisfaction with quality of life in the items of mobility (C4.2 and C4.3) and dressing (C4.5). The memory dimension correlated with the items of language and communication (C4.6) and housekeeping (C4.10), as well as with the item on the ability to pay attention and concentrate (C3.3). The emotion dimension correlated most strongly with the items on mental health (C4.12), optimism (C3.9) and sadness (C3.2). The communication dimension correlated most strongly with the items of language and communication (C4.6), attention and concentration (C3.3). The dimension IADL correlated with many items on IADL, with mobility outside one’s own living area (C4.3) and housekeeping (C4.10). In addition to the information on mobility (C4.2 and C4.3), the mobility dimension also correlated with housekeeping (C4.10), personal hygiene (C4.4) and impairment due to pain (C3.1). The hand dimension correlated most strongly with the information on the items of mobility (C4.2 and C4.3), housekeeping (C4.10), dressing (C4.5) and personal hygiene (C4.4). The participation dimension correlated with almost all items from part C4, mobility outside one’s own living area (C4.3) and social participation (C4.13).

Discussion

In this study, the LaS questionnaire for assessing the life situation of stroke survivors at home was developed and tested for reliability and validity. The development of the questionnaire was based on the EORTC guidelines17. Two established questionnaires were used for validation (SIS, EQ-5D-3L). Test-retest analyses were conducted to examine temporal stability. According to feedback from a total of 236 participants in Phase III and Phase IV, the developed questionnaire is user-friendly, and all questions were clearly formulated. Participants stated that they needed a median of 20 min to answer the questions. To streamline the instrument without compromising content coverage, 12 items with low discriminatory power were removed. Without these items, moderate to good test-retest reliability was observed in the individual question blocks of Topic Area B. In Topic Area C, all but two items showed moderate to good stability. The reduced test–retest reliability observed for these two items likely reflects restricted response variance (floor/ceiling effects), which may be related to the underrepresentation of severely impaired participants in the development and validation cohorts. Notably, item C4.8 demonstrated an ICC close to the moderate range (ICC = 0.48). By contrast, the low temporal stability observed for item C3.5 (global adaptation; ICC = 0.30) may be partly attributable to overly broad/abstract wording and the absence of a defined recall period. We therefore plan to examine alternative item formulations in future research.

Construct validity was examined through correlations with established PROMs (SIS and EQ-5D-3L). We expected that LaS Topic Area B blocks capturing everyday activity limitations would show moderate to strong associations with conceptually corresponding SIS dimensions (convergent validity), and weaker associations with non-corresponding dimensions (discriminant validity). Likewise, LaS Topic Area C items addressing health and well-being were expected to align with related SIS/EQ-5D-3L constructs. Using Spearman’s rank correlations, the observed pattern of associations was largely consistent with these expectations, supporting convergent construct validity. While the construct validity analysis was guided by general a priori expectations regarding the direction of associations, the detailed domain-level correlations should be interpreted as partly exploratory. This should be considered when interpreting the strength and specificity of the observed associations. Further analyses (e.g., responsiveness to change; severity-stratified performance) are warranted. The full correlation matrices are provided in Tables 6 and 7.

To obtain a holistic overview of stroke survivors’ situations in the home environment, assessment should encompass daily functioning alongside psychological and social aspects. Tools frequently used in clinical practice primarily focus on independence in ADLs. Instruments such as the UK Functional Assessment Measure11 or the SIS, include social and emotional status, but not in detail and some essential aspects are not assessed (e.g. anxiety, sexuality). The LaS addresses these areas and thus provides a comprehensive picture of stroke survivors living at home. Another strength of the LaS is assessing the ability to use aids effectively and self-assessment regarding unresolved problems in the individual areas. It is therefore suitable as a patient-centered assessment and follow-up tool for primary care physicians and mobile care services, both for initial assessment after discharge and for documenting change over time. The data collected can be used for further research into life after stroke in the home environment.

PROMs are typically developed for specific purposes and settings; therefore, instruments differ in scope, level of detail, and feasibility for routine use. Compared with existing patient-reported outcome measures, the available instruments capture important but partly distinct aspects of post-stroke care and long-term support. The EDAQ-SS provides very comprehensive coverage (160 items across 15 domains and dual assessment with/without assistive devices) but may be challenging to implement in routine nursing practice due to the time required (approximately 40–60 min)31.

The Lawton–Brody Instrumental Activities of Daily Living Scale (IADL) is a well-established measure of instrumental activities across eight domains; however, it does not capture psychological dimensions or patients’ subjective appraisal of quality of life33. The SSSMQ (28 items; 6-point Likert) demonstrates high reliability and focuses on self-perceived self-management, addressing a related but different construct than the LaS and thus allowing only partial comparability34. The PROMIS-10 (Global Health), recommended by the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement (ICHOM), offers a brief global assessment of health status and quality of life across key dimensions (e.g., pain, fatigue, mental health, social participation)35. However, due to its brevity, it provides less granularity for profiling activity-specific limitations and domain-level needs. In contrast, the LaS was designed to bridge these perspectives by combining a structured assessment of everyday functioning in the home context with coping-related and patient-perceived nursing/rehabilitation needs, while remaining feasible for repeated use in long-term follow-up.

A standardized development process of the questionnaire with early input from patients and healthcare professionals is a major strength of the present study. Given its nature as a PROM, practical use of the LaS is restricted to individuals with sufficient cognitive ability to understand and answer the questions appropriately. Nevertheless, PROMs are an essential means to assess health across physical, psychological, and social levels and to document change after interventions29. As the German-language version of the LaS was evaluated, current validation is limited to German-speaking settings.

Information on stroke severity based on the mRS was available for a subsample of participants. The vast majority of recorded mRS scores were ≤ 3, indicating that the study population consisted predominantly of individuals with mild to moderate post-stroke impairment. The small number of patients with severe disability (mRS ≥ 4) represents an important limitation, as the findings cannot be generalized to more severely affected populations. Consequently, the applicability and measurement properties of the LaS questionnaire in patients with severe impairment remain to be established in future studies.

Furthermore, although general expectations regarding the relationship between LaS domains and established PROMs were defined a priori, the detailed mapping of individual items and blocks was partly exploratory. This limits the extent to which the observed correlations can be interpreted as confirmatory evidence of construct validity. In addition, the structural properties of the LaS questionnaire were not examined using exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis. This reflects the conceptual design of the instrument, which was not developed as a scale to measure latent constructs through aggregated scores, but rather as a clinically oriented, item-based tool capturing heterogeneous and context-specific aspects of everyday functioning, coping, and perceived needs. In particular, the items within the individual domains represent qualitatively different categories (e.g., experienced difficulties, use of assistive devices, need for support), which are not assumed to reflect a single underlying latent dimension. Nevertheless, further research in larger and more diverse samples should explore the structural properties of the questionnaire using exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis to complement the current findings.

The LaS is a patient-reported instrument designed to support structured initial assessment (post-discharge from hospital and any inpatient rehabilitation) and longitudinal follow-up in the home context. It facilitates clinical reflection, shared goal-setting, and multidisciplinary coordination; it is not intended to quantify personnel-related nursing demand or to derive staffing requirements, nor does it prescribe specific intervention pathways. Stakeholder involvement may vary by impairment severity (e.g., nursing professionals, physiotherapy/occupational therapy, speech therapy, social work, informal caregivers).

A key feature of the LaS is its emphasis on the proximal, home-based environment, reflecting the everyday realities of stroke survivors and supporting its intended home-centered use. Broader environmental and macro-structural determinants of participation (e.g., barriers in public space, access to services, community participation) are less comprehensively represented and should be considered when interpreting findings primarily related to activity limitations and domestic participation. Future research may therefore evaluate whether an additional module addressing participation in public and community contexts would enhance the instrument’s ability to capture participation restrictions at the societal level.

Although the LaS may be used within clinician-led, home-based follow-up, it is not designed as a performance-based measure of skill acquisition or proficiency. Instead, it adopts a patient-reported perspective to document how individuals experience their functional situation, coping strategies, and environmental influences over time. In line with the ICF framework, disability is thus understood as an interaction between health condition, activities, participation, and environmental factors, as perceived by the individual. The environmental dimensions addressed in the questionnaire primarily refer to the proximal home and family context. This focus allows an in-depth exploration of how everyday environments shape post-stroke functioning and coping, while acknowledging that broader societal and macro-structural determinants of participation are beyond the primary scope of the current instrument. To facilitate comparison of the LaS with other PROMs or assessment tools, the individual items were assigned to the categories of the Comprehensive ICF Core Set Stroke30–32 (Supplemental File 3). The items in the LaS were mainly assigned to classifications from dimensions b (body functions), d (activities and participation), and e (environmental factors).

The LaS supports patient-centered assessment and monitoring in outpatient rehabilitation and care. Next, we will implement the instrument across multiple sites to analyze the living situation of a larger cohort of stroke survivors in their home environment, determine the frequency of specific problem areas and the extent to which these are being addressed satisfactorily, and evaluate responsiveness to change These data may inform the implementation of appropriate regional care measures.

Conclusion

In summary, the LaS questionnaire was developed to capture a broad range of challenges experienced by stroke survivors in the post-hospital home setting. As a PROM, it supports structured assessment and documentation of post-stroke issues in routine care and research, including initial assessment and longitudinal follow-up.

The added value of the LaS lies in (i) its explicit focus on the home context after hospital discharge, (ii) the combination of functional domains with coping strategies and patient-perceived support needs, and (iii) its feasibility for repeated administration to document change over time. While existing PROMs provide valuable information, they often differ in scope (e.g., global health vs. activity-specific detail) and feasibility for routine implementation. The LaS aims to complement these instruments by offering domain-specific profiling of everyday functioning alongside perceived needs within a practicable format.

Overall, the LaS demonstrated good comprehensibility and promising psychometric properties in this study, supporting its use as a patient-centered tool in outpatient rehabilitation and follow-up care. Future studies should further examine its applicability and measurement properties in more severely affected populations and across broader environmental contexts.
