The stochastic block model has the overlap graph property for modularity
Shankar Bhamidi, David Gamarnik, Remco van der Hofstad, Nelly Litvak, Paweł Prałat, Fiona Skerman, Yasmin Tousinejad

Abstract
The overlap gap property (OGP) is a statement about the geometry of near-optimal solutions. Exhibiting OGP implies failure of a class of local algorithms; and has been observed to coincide with conjectured algorithmic limits in problems with statistical computational gap. We consider the Stochastic Block Model (SBM), where the graph has a planted partition with k k equal-size blocks which form the ‘communities’, and where, for parameters p > q p>q , vertices within the same community connect with probability p p , while vertices in different communities connect with probability q q , independently across pairs of vertices. Modularity–based clustering algorithms have become ubiquitous in applications. This article studies theoretical limits of local algorithms based on the modularity score on the SBM. We establish that modularity exhibits OGP on the SBM. This rules out a class of local algorithms based on modularity for recovery in the SBM, and shows slow mixing time for a related Markov Chain. Theoretically this is one of the few instances where OGP has been established for a ‘planted’ model, as most such analyses to date consider the ‘null’ model. As part of our analysis, we extend a result by Bickel and Chen 2009, who established that with high probability, the modularity optimal partition of SBM is o ⁡ ( n ) o(n) local moves away from the planted partition, where n n is the graph size. We show that, with high probability, any partition with modularity score sufficiently near the optimal value is close to the planted partition.

Introduction

Modularity maximisation
[
28
,
29
]
is one of the main methods used for finding clusters and communities in networks. Modularity maximisation is NP-hard
[
7
]
; in practice, modularity has a complex landscape, and there are many high scoring solutions
[
18
]
. On the other hand, one major advantage of modularity is that its local updates are easy to compute. Naturally, local updates form the initial phase in the go-to algorithms for modularity maximisation: the Louvain algorithm
[
5
]
and its improved version, the Leiden algorithm
[
35
]
. However, as already observed in
[
5
]
, algorithms based on local updates get trapped in a local maximum. To avoid this, both Louvain and Leiden include an additional non-local phase. An alternative approach, proposed by Wang and Kolter
[
36
]
via the Locale algorithm, escapes local maxima by replacing the local update with low-cardinality embedding.

Given the abundance of applications of modularity maximisation and Louvain and Leiden algorithms, theoretical understanding of modularity–based algorithms is surprisingly scarce. Cohen-Addad et al.
[
9
]
proved that in the Stochastic Block Model (SBM) with sufficient signal and two equal-sized blocks, a local algorithm, very similar to the local phase of Louvain, initiated on a random bisection, recovers the correct partition with high probability. See Section
6
for further discussion including how this relates to our result.

The question remains whether local updates lead to a global optimum in this random model with more than two communities or different initialisation. In this work, we answer this question negatively. Specifically, we establish that in SBM with
k\geq 3
blocks, modularity exhibits the
overlap gap property
(OGP).
The OGP is a statement of the geometry of near-optimal solutions and is considered a signature of algorithmic hardness—see Definition
1
.
Indeed, for many problems known to exhibit a computational-statistical gap, the threshold for OGP coincides with the conjectured algorithmic threshold
[
14
,
15
]
. The idea has origins in statistical physics, and can be used to prove failure of greedy and Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithms in average case problems. We show OGP in a planted random model as in
[
16
,
13
]
, though the theory is usually applied to optimisation problems in null models
[
14
]
.

The practical implication of this result is that in networks with more than two communities, the local updates of Louvain or Leiden can get stuck in a local maximum even for a random graph model with clear communities. The same holds for other algorithms based on local updates. For example, Gösgens et al.
[
19
]
offer a new interpretation of modularity as an angular distance in a high-dimensional hypersphere, thus establishing equivalence of modularity maximisation and nearest-neighbour search. Then, OGP implies that greedy algorithms for nearest-neighbour search may return a
local
rather than the
global
maximum. Furthermore, using the OGP we prove that a natural generalisation of the greedy algorithm, namely the Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithm, mixes exponentially slowly, and, in particular, takes an exponential time to reach partitions close to the planted partition with high probability.

While there are few theoretical results for modularity–based algorithms, there are positive results for recovering ground truth partitions using local-updates based on modularity in the SBM. As mentioned earlier, Cohen-Addad et al.
[
9
]
showed that for the SBM with sufficient signal and two equal-sized blocks, a local algorithm recovers the communities with high probability. This was further extended to general
k
by giving an algorithm with parallel local updates based on the modularity function
[
10
]
. For more details and a
discussion of how these relate to our OGP results, see Section
6
.

More is known about the behaviour of modularity on random models; we briefly review this for context.
Considering first random graph models without planted structure, the main results focus on the setting of growing average degree
\bar{d}
for the random
d
-regular graph
[
24
,
25
,
30
]
and the Erdős-Rényi random graph
[
26
,
31
]
, which are likely to have modularity of the order
1/\sqrt{\bar{d}}
. For the Preferential Attachment (PA) model
[
2
]
, very recently, this same behaviour was established up to log factors
[
32
]
.
For models with planted structure, break-through results were given for the SBM by Bickel and Chen
[
3
]
showing that the modularity-optimal partition is close to the planted partition. These were extended to the degree-corrected SBM in
[
37
]
. See Theorem
4
for details. Finally, the modularity in the Artificial Benchmark for Community Detection (ABCD) model
[
21
]
, a model similar to the well-known LFR model
[
22
]
used by practitioners, was studied in
[
20
]
.

2.1
Definitions

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with
m=|E|\geq 1
edges and
n=|V|\geq 1
nodes. For a partition
\mathcal{A}
of the vertices of
G
, the
modularity score
of
\mathcal{A}
on
G
is defined as

\displaystyle q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{u,v\in A}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{\{uv\in E\}}-\frac{d_{u}d_{v}}{2m}\right)=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{e(A)}{m}-\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\frac{{\rm vol}(A)}{{\rm vol}(V)}\right)^{2},

(1)

where
d_{u}
denotes the degree of node
u
,
e(A)
denotes the number of edges within
A\subseteq V
and
{\rm vol}(A)=\sum_{v\in A}d_{v}
denotes the (degree) volume of the set
A
. The
modularity
of a graph
G
is defined as
q^{\star}(G)=\max_{\mathcal{A}}q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)
, where the maximum is taken over all partitions
\mathcal{A}
of the nodes of
G
. It will be useful to express modularity as the difference of the
edge-contribution
or
coverage

q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}
and the
degree-tax

q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}
of the partition
\mathcal{A}
, defined as

q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}e(A)/m\quad\mbox{and}\quad q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(G)=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}{\rm vol}(A)^{2}/{\rm vol}(G)^{2}.

(2)

The modularity score, introduced by Newman and Girvan in
[
29
]
, is a quality function of many popular community detection algorithms such as Louvain
[
5
]
or Leiden
[
35
]
. Indeed, the modularity score favours partitions of the set of nodes of a graph
G
in which a large proportion of the edges fall entirely within the partition, but benchmarks it against the expected number of edges one would see in the same partition in a corresponding Chung-Lu random graph model
[
8
]
with expected degree sequence taken to be identical to the degree sequence of the observed graph
G
.

Fix
k\geq 2
denoting the number of communities. We now formally define the
stochastic block model
(SBM) which will be the focus of this paper. This model is also called the
planted partition model
. The set of nodes
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
is partitioned into
k
communities
P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}
of as equal sizes as possible; that is,
\lfloor n/k\rfloor\leq|P_{i}|\leq\lceil n/k\rceil
for all
i\in[k]
. For simplicity, often we will assume that
n
is divisible by
k
, but our main result is stated and holds without this restriction. We will refer to this
planted partition

(P_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
as
\mathcal{P}
. The probability of observing an edge between two nodes of the same community is equal to
p
; otherwise, it is equal to
q
. We will use the notation
G\in\mathcal{G}(n,k,p,q)
whenever a random graph
G
is generated with this probability distribution.

Results in the paper are in the large network limit
n\rightarrow\infty
. In particular, we will assume that both
p=p(n)
and
q=q(n)
are functions of
n
, and
n
is large enough for certain statements to be true. On the other hand, the parameter
k\geq 2
is an arbitrary but
fixed
integer. We emphasise that the notations
o(\cdot)
and
O(\cdot)
refer to functions of
n
, not necessarily positive, whose growth vanishes, respectively, is bounded.

We will establish that modularity exhibits OGP in the SBM. To prove this, we will obtain a more detailed result that characterises the maximum modularity of any partition with given distance to the planted partition. We define the distance as the classification error—see, for example, (4.25) in
[
1
]
; in case of two communities, this distance is equal to the ‘imbalance’ in
[
9
]
. Define the distance between a
k
-part partition
\mathcal{A}=(A_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
and the planted partition
\mathcal{P}=(P_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
in SBM as

d(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})=1-\frac{1}{n}\max_{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{k}|A_{\sigma(i)}\cap P_{i}|,

(3)

where the maximum is taken over all permutations
\sigma:[k]\to[k]
of
[k]=\{1,\ldots,k\}
that govern how parts of
\mathcal{A}
are aligned with the ones of
\mathcal{P}
.
Note that this
d(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})
has a natural interpretation: it is the minimum proportion of nodes that need to be re-shuffled to transform the candidate partition
\mathcal{A}
into the ground truth or planted partition
\mathcal{P}
. Indeed, for a given permutation
\sigma
, one can keep nodes in
A_{\sigma(i)}\cap P_{i}
where they are and move other nodes to the appropriate parts.

Now that we have a notion of distance between partitions, we may formally define the
overlap gap property
(OGP).
The definition of the OGP pertains to a particular instance
G_{n}
of a distribution over graphs
\mathcal{G}_{n}
.

Definition 1

(
Overlap gap property – planted model).

For a graph
G_{n}
with planted partition
\mathcal{P}
, the optimisation problem
\max_{\mathcal{A}}q_{\mathcal{A}}(G_{n})

exhibits OGP with values
\mu>0
and
0\leq\nu_{1}<\nu_{2}
if the following holds: For any partition
\mathcal{B}
for which
q_{\mathcal{B}}(G_{n})\geq q^{\star}(G_{n})-\mu
, it holds that either
d(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{P})\leq\nu_{1}
, or that
d(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{P})\geq\nu_{2}
. Furthermore,
d(\mathcal{B}^{\prime},\mathcal{P})\geq\nu_{2}
does indeed occur for some partition
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
with
q_{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}(G_{n})\geq q^{\star}(G_{n})-\mu
.

Modularity exhibits OGP

Theorem
2

(
Modularity has OGP).

Fix real numbers

Intuition.

Consider a ‘decoy’ partition
\mathcal{D}
in which two of the planted blocks are clustered together in the same part, and each of the remaining planted blocks forms its own part. (There are
\binom{k}{2}
such decoy partitions by symmetry.) We call
\mathcal{D}
a ‘decoy’ partition since it has a ‘misleadingly’ high modularity score given its distance to the planted partition
\mathcal{P}
; see Figure
1
.
(We emphasise that the decoy
\mathcal{D}
is an alternate partition of the nodes rather than an alternate random graph model to generate the graph.)

Given the decoy partition
\mathcal{D}
, the parameters for OGP are set as follows.
We set
\mu=q^{\star}(G)-q_{\mathcal{D}}(G)+\eta
for some small

Then we set
\nu_{2}=d(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{D})-\delta=1/k-\delta

so that the decoy partition and those very close to it will fall within the second interval. Lastly, we need to set
\nu_{1}
. Notice that, for small distances from the planted partition, the modularity score of partitions decreases with distance, so we set
\nu_{1}
to be some distance by which the likely modularity score has dipped below

2
.

As an implication of the OGP we show that a natural algorithm based on Markov Chain
Monte Carlo (MCMC) updates takes exponential time to reach any proximity of the

B

Theorem 3

(Informal)
.

For every
\zeta>0
, there exists a large enough inverse temperature parameter

\beta>0
, such that when the chain is initiated at distance
at least
1/k
from the ground truth, the time to reach a partition close
to the ground truth is at least
\exp(\Theta(n))
with high probability.

n
large enough, then the following holds with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
provided
n
is large enough.
Let
G\in\mathcal{G}(n,k,p,q)
and denote the planted partition by
\mathcal{P}
. Then,

\left|q_{\mathcal{P}}(G)-\frac{a-b}{a+(k-1)b}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

(5)

and

\Big|q^{\star}(G)-q_{\mathcal{P}}(G)\Big|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(6)

As a result,

\left|q^{\star}(G)-\frac{a-b}{a+(k-1)b}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\right|\leq\varepsilon.

Note that, as expected,
q^{\star}(G)\approx\frac{a-b}{a+(k-1)b}\,h(0)
, where
h(\cdot)
is defined in Theorem
6
.

3
Ingredients of the proof
: Distances to the planted partition
\mathcal{P}

For now, for simplicity of exposition, we assume that
n
is divisible by
k
, as this will be assumed in the first lemma, Lemma
8
. However, in the proof of Theorem
6
, we relax this condition and deal with the general case.

To prove our main result, we need to investigate a family of partitions of
[n]
with a given distance to the planted partition
\mathcal{P}=(P_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
. It will be convenient to represent partitions as
k\times k
matrices that capture the way how these partitions overlap with
\mathcal{P}
. Let
\mathcal{A}=(A_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
be any partition of
[n]
into
k
parts. Then, the
signature
of
\mathcal{A}
(with respect to
\mathcal{P}
) is defined as the matrix
X=X(\mathcal{A})=(x_{ij})_{1\leq i\leq j\leq k}
, where

x_{ij}=\frac{|A_{i}\cap P_{j}|}{n/k}.

(7)

Note that for the signature to be well defined, one needs to fix the labelling of both
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{A}
. To compute the distance between
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{A}
, one needs to align these labels as best as possible (see Lemma
8
below), but the modularity score clearly does not depend on the way the labels are aligned (see Lemma
9
below).
Note also that, trivially,
x_{ij}\geq 0
for all
i,j\in[k]
. Moreover, since
A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{k}=[n]
, for any
j\in[k]
,

\sum_{i\in[k]}x_{ij}=\frac{1}{n/k}\sum_{i\in[k]}|A_{i}\cap P_{j}|=\frac{|P_{j}|}{n/k}=1.

(8)

Finally, note that the signature of the planted partition
\mathcal{P}
is the matrix
Y^{\mathcal{P}}=(y_{ij})_{1\leq i\leq j\leq k}
with
y_{ii}=1
for all
i\in[k]
and
y_{ij}=0
for all
i\neq j
.

In our first lemma, we rewrite the distance between any partition
\mathcal{A}
and the planted partition
\mathcal{P}
in a convenient form, namely, as a function of the signature of
\mathcal{A}
. The proof is short so we give it below.

Lemma 8
.

Suppose that
n
is divisible by
k
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be any partition of
[n]
with
k
parts, and let
\mathcal{P}
be the planted partition.
Let
X=X(\mathcal{A})=(x_{ij})_{1\leq i\leq j\leq k}
be the signature of
\mathcal{A}
. Then,

d(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})=1-\tfrac{1}{k}\max_{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{\sigma(i)i},

(9)

where the maximum is taken over all permutations of
[k]
.

Moreover,
d(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})\leq 1-1/k
and this upper bound is sharp, that is, there exists a partition
\mathcal{A}
such that
d(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})=1-1/k
, provided that
n
is divisible by
k^{2}
.

Proof of Lemma
8
.

Equality (
9
) follows immediately from the definition of the signature in (
7
) and the definition (
3
) of the distance between the two partitions. Indeed,

d(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})=1-\frac{1}{n}\max_{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{k}|A_{\sigma(i)}\cap P_{i}|=1-\frac{1}{n}\max_{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{\sigma(i)i}\cdot(n/k)=1-\frac{1}{k}\max_{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{\sigma(i)i}.

It remains to show that
d(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})\leq 1-1/k
or, equivalently, that
\max_{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{\sigma(i)i}\geq 1
.

Consider the sum
S=\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{\sigma(i)i}
, where the outer sum is taken over all
k!
permutations of
[k]
. Clearly, there are
k!\cdot k
terms in
S
and for each
a,b\in[k]
, the term
x_{ab}
occurs exactly
(k-1)!
times (
i
has to be equal to
b
and there are
(k-1)!
permutations that map
b
to
a
). Hence, for each
j\in[k]
, the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{ij}
(which is equal to 1 by (
8
)) occurs
(k-1)!
times. We conclude that
S=k\cdot(k-1)!=k!
. By an averaging argument, there exists a permutation
\hat{\sigma}
for which
\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{\hat{\sigma}(i)i}\geq 1
. Hence,
\max_{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{\sigma(i)i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{\hat{\sigma}(i)i}\geq 1
, thus the desired inequality holds.

Assume now that
n
is not only divisible by
k
but, in fact, it is divisible by
k^{2}
. We construct a partition
\mathcal{A}
by partitioning each
P_{j}
into
k
equal parts and then picking
1/k
fraction of each
P_{j}
to form
A_{i}
of size
n/k
. This partition has signature
X
with
x_{ij}=1/k
for all
i,j\in[k]
. We get that
\max_{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{\sigma(i)i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}1/k=1
, which shows that the upper bound is sharp. This finishes the proof of the lemma.
∎

4
Ingredients of the proof: Concentration

The signature of
\mathcal{A}
not only determines the distance between
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{P}
but, more importantly, it predicts (up to an arbitrarily small error in a sufficiently dense graph

is well-approximated by a scaling of

of

g(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq k}(x_{ij}-x_{ij^{\prime}})^{2}.

(10)

Lemma 9

(
Concentration of modularity).

Fix
a>b>0
and integer

. Let

and

. For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists

\omega>c
for

1-\varepsilon
provided

Let

. For all

and

is defined in (

Recall that the planted partition
\mathcal{P}

As one might want to use this result for other purposes, we state it (and prove it, of course) without assuming that

Ingredients of the proof: Optimisation

We have now established that, up to small errors, we may consider

as our objective function, and have re-written our distance to the planted partition in terms of

Lemma 10
.

Let
0\leq t<1
.
Define

\displaystyle x_{ij}\geq 0,

\displaystyle\mbox{for all $i,j\in[k]$},

\displaystyle\sum_{i}x_{ij}=1

\displaystyle\mbox{for all $j\in[k]$},

(11)

Let

be defined as in (

\max_{X\in\mathcal{X}_{k}(t)}g(X)=k(k\!-\!1)-2t\big(k\!-\!t(k\!-\!1)\big)\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\arg\max_{X\in\mathcal{X}_{k}(t)}g(X)=\{X_{k}^{(ij)}(t)\;:\;i\neq j\},

Note that in the optimiser

Lemma 11

Consider

6
Discussion and the case of balanced partitions.

The literature contains positive results, with algorithms using local updates based on the modularity function to recover communities in the SBM
[
9
,
10
]
. This paper proves OGP, a signature of algorithmic hardness, for such algorithms.

Of course this does not give rise to a contradiction, since the setups in
[
9
,
10
]
and ours are subtly different and also OGP has been exhibited for problems known to be easy
[
23
]
. However, it leads to interesting open questions, probing which of the differences in the two setups are important. We describe this now in more detail.

As mentioned earlier, for
k=2
communities and starting with a random partition into equal-size parts, a local algorithm based on the modularity function was shown to recover the planted communities
[
9
]
. The local moves for this algorithm take the form of a
swap
, which takes pairs of vertices, one from each part, and swaps them if this increases the modularity score. This naturally maintains equal-size parts.
For general
k
, an algorithm with parallel local updates based on the modularity function was shown to recover the ground truth partition
[
10
]
. We note that this parallel algorithm has a random balanced start and also a mechanism to maintain the balanced sizes of parts during the algorithm.

For the overlap gap property that we establish, it was important that the ‘decoy’ partition
\mathcal{D}
, which is an
unbalanced
partition into
k-1
parts, has a ‘surprisingly high’ modularity given its distance of
1/k
from the ground truth partition
\mathcal{P}
. Furthermore, at distances
0<d<1/k
from
\mathcal{P}
modularity, optimal partitions are increasingly unbalanced, with part sizes
(1/k+d,\;1/k-d,\;1/k,\ldots,1/k)
; see Lemma
10
.

Perhaps a crucial difference in the two setups is that the positive results of
[
9
,
10
]
had
balanced
partitions, both in the initialisation as well as during the algorithm. We finally show that if we introduced this balanced condition into our optimisation problem then it no longer exhibits OGP. In particular, when considering
\max_{\mathcal{A}}q_{\mathcal{A}}
where the maximisation is over
balanced

k
-part partitions instead of all
k
-part partitions, we see that this no longer exhibits OGP.
We should note that this by itself does not imply

success of particular greedy algorithms – and hence fast algorithms.
More work needs to be done to exhibit one (as done in
[
9
,
10
]
). In a similar vain, for the problem of sparse regression
[
16
]
, in the regime where OGP ceases to hold a greedy type algorithm was established to be effective. The construction is not based on OGP. Instead it relies directly on the properties of the model, which is typically the case.

To state this result, we fix the random graph
G\in\mathcal{G}(n,k,p,q)
under the conditions of Theorem
6
. It turns out that the maximum modularity of
G
only over
balanced
partitions at distance about
0<d<1-1/k
from the planted partition
\mathcal{P}
concentrates about some function that now
is
decreasing in
d
:

Proposition 12
.

Let
\mathcal{X}_{t}^{\prime}
be defined as in (
11
) with the additional restriction that
\sum_{j}x_{ij}=1
for all
i\in[k]
. Then
g^{\rm bal}(t)=\max_{X\in\mathcal{X}_{t}^{\prime}}g(X)
is strictly decreasing on
0<t<k-1
.

Proposition
12
implies an analogue of Theorem
6
, optimising now only over
balanced partitions
, for
0<d<1-1/k
, now with decreasing
\tilde{h}(d),
contrary to

h(d)
.

Appendix B
Failure of the Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithm

Throughout this section we consider
k
-partitions with
k
fixed. We denote
q_{\mathcal{A}}=q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)
,
q^{\star}=q^{\star}(G)
for brevity.
The OGP immediately implies the failure of a natural Greedy algorithm for finding the planted partition
\mathcal{P}
when
started from the decoy partition
\mathcal{D}
. More specifically, Greedy is an algorithm resulting in a sequence of partitions
\mathcal{A}_{t}
built
as follows: We initialise the Greedy algorithm in the decoy partition, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\mathcal{D}
. Given
\mathcal{A}_{t-1}
, the partition
\mathcal{A}_{t}
is obtained
by changing the membership of at most one node
u\in[n]
such that the resulting modularity strictly increases.
That is,
\mathcal{A}_{t}
is any partition satisfying

d(\mathcal{A}_{t},\mathcal{A}_{t-1})={1/n}
and

q_{\mathcal{A}_{t}}(G)>q_{\mathcal{A}_{t-1}}(G)
. If no such node
u
exists, then the algorithm stops and outputs the partition obtained
in the final step. By the OGP, the Greedy algorithm, initiated at the decoy partition
\mathcal{D}
, terminates at this partition with distance at least
1/k
from the ground truth.

A natural generalisation of the Greedy algorithm, which is guaranteed (as we will show) to output a partition approximately
matching the planted partition
\mathcal{P}
, is the well-known Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm which we now describe.
Our main result in this section is the proof of slow mixing of this MCMC. In particular, we will show that the time it takes for the algorithm to approximately produce an
planted partition is exponentially large in
n
.

We begin by describing the MCMC algorithm. A parameter usually called the
inverse temperature

\beta
is fixed. The algorithm proceeds as a Markov
chain moving according to the following rules. Given any partition
\mathcal{A}
, consider any partition
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
with

d(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}^{\prime})={1/n}
. Then the algorithm moves from
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
with probability proportional
to
\exp(\beta nq_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}})
, namely with probability

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\right)\triangleq\frac{\exp(\beta nq_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}})}{\sum_{\mathcal{B}}\exp(\beta nq_{\mathcal{B}})},

where here the sum is over all partitions
\mathcal{B}
with
d(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})={1/n}
. (Note that we choose this parametrisation so that the stationary distribution is informative.)
It is known that the unique stationary
distribution of this chain is the so-called
Gibbs distribution
given by

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{\rm Gibbs}(\mathcal{A})=\frac{\exp(\beta nq_{\mathcal{A}})}{Z},

where
Z=\sum_{\mathcal{B}}\exp(\beta nq_{\mathcal{B}})
is the normalising constant, which is also called the
partition function
, and now the sum is over
all
partitions
\mathcal{B}
.

We first show that this algorithm is sound, in the sense that for large enough
\beta
it produces partitions that are close to the ground truth
\mathcal{P}
.

Proposition 13
.

For every
\varepsilon>0,\zeta>0
, there exists large enough
\beta>0
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{A}\colon d(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})\leq\zeta}{\mathbb{P}}_{\rm Gibbs}(\mathcal{A})\geq 1-\varepsilon.

In words, the probability mass of partitions with distance at most
\zeta
from the planted partition constitutes
at least a
1-\varepsilon
fraction of the total probability mass.
Thus, if the algorithm were to run until stationarity, then a partition sampled according to the Gibbs distribution is likely to be at most

close to the ground truth, modulo an at most

Proof of Proposition

Given any

.
Fix

small enough so that, with probability at least
1-\varepsilon/2
,
the event occurs that every

satisfies
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{E}(\zeta)

On this event,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{A}\notin\mathcal{E}(\zeta)}\exp\left(\beta nq_{\mathcal{A}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq k^{n}\exp(\beta n(q^{\star}-\delta)).

Fix

(any strictly negative constant will do). Then,
this sum is at most

, so that

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{A}\notin\mathcal{E}(\zeta)}{\mathbb{P}}_{\rm Gibbs}(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle\leq{\exp(-n)\exp(\beta nq^{\star})\over Z}

\displaystyle\leq{\exp(-n)\exp(\beta nq^{\star})\over\exp(\beta nq^{\star})}

\displaystyle=\exp(-n)\leq\varepsilon/2,

Our main result, described next, shows that unfortunately the time to stationarity is exponentially large in
n
when the chain
is initiated at a large enough distance from the partition, appropriately defined.
Worse than that, the time to reach even

partition close to the ground

n
, again when the chain is initiated at a large distance from the ground truth. This means that the set of such starting points acts as a

. See
[

for a discussion on metastability, and
[

]
for an example of metastability in random regular graphs.

In preparation for the proof of this claim, we recap some of the properties implied by the OGP
and an auxiliary lemma: there exist
0<\nu_{1}<\nu_{2}<1
and
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such
that the following holds. Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\rm close}

\displaystyle=\{\mathcal{A}\colon d(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})\leq\nu_{1}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\rm far}

\displaystyle=\{\mathcal{A}\colon d(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})\geq\nu_{2}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\rm btw}

\displaystyle=\{\mathcal{A}\colon d(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{A})\in(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})\}.

Then,
by Theorem
2
and Lemma
11
with probability at least
1-\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\arg\max q_{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{\rm close},

(12)

\displaystyle\max_{\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{E}_{\rm far}}q_{\mathcal{A}}\geq q^{\star}-c_{1},

(13)

\displaystyle\max_{\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{E}_{\rm btw}}q_{\mathcal{A}}\leq q^{\star}-c_{1}-c_{2}.

(14)

We now state our lower bound result.

Theorem 14
.

Consider the MCMC initiated at the Gibbs distribution conditioned on being in
\mathcal{E}_{\rm far}
. Namely, suppose

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{A}_{0}=\mathcal{A}\right)={{\mathbb{P}}_{\rm Gibbs}(\mathcal{A})\over\sum_{\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{E}_{\rm far}}{\mathbb{P}}_{\rm Gibbs}(\mathcal{B})},

for all

Let

. There exist

such that
with probability at least

(with respect
to the randomness of the graph)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau\geq\exp(c_{3}n)\right)\geq 1-\exp(-c_{4}n),

where the probability is with respect to the random choices of the MCMC.

Proof.

We assume that the events (

)-(

) hold, which is the case with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
.
We note that the events
\mathcal{A}_{0}\in\mathcal{E}_{\rm far},\mathcal{A}_{\tau}\in\mathcal{E}_{\rm close}

imply the existence of
s<\tau
such that
\mathcal{A}_{s}\in\mathcal{E}_{\rm btw}
.
For every positive integer

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{A}_{t}\in\mathcal{E}_{\rm btw}|\mathcal{A}_{0}\in\mathcal{E}_{\rm far}\right)

\displaystyle={{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{A}_{t}\in\mathcal{E}_{\rm btw},\mathcal{A}_{0}\in\mathcal{E}_{\rm far}\right)\over{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{A}_{0}\in\mathcal{E}_{\rm far}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq{{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{A}_{t}\in\mathcal{E}_{\rm btw}\right)\over{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{A}_{0}\in\mathcal{E}_{\rm far}\right)}.

We have

C.1
Intermediate result on weighted graphs

The definition for modularity extends naturally to
weighted
graphs, and indeed is often used for clustering weighted graphs in applications. Suppose that the edge-weights
w
are such that

0\leq w_{uv}=w_{vu}\leq 1
for all nodes
u
and
v
, and assume that
w
is not identically zero.
Define the (weighted) degree of a node

by setting
d_{u}^{(w)}=\sum_{v\in V}w_{uv}

by
{\rm vol}_{w}(X)=\sum_{u\in X}d_{u}^{(w)}
, and let
e_{w}(X)=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{u,v\in X}w_{uv}
be the total edge-weight.

For a given partition
\mathcal{A}
of
V
, define the
modularity score
of
\mathcal{A}
on

by

\displaystyle q_{\mathcal{A}}(w)=\frac{1}{{\rm vol}_{w}(V)}\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{u,v\in A}\left(w_{uv}-\frac{d^{(w)}_{u}d^{(w)}_{v}}{{\rm vol}_{w}(V)}\right)=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{e_{w}(A)}{e_{w}(V)}-\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\frac{{\rm vol}_{w}(A)}{{\rm vol}_{w}(V)}\right)^{2},

(15)

and the
modularity
of
w
by
q^{\star}(w)=\max_{\mathcal{A}}q_{\mathcal{A}}(w)
.

As in the original modularity function, we express the modularity score as the difference between the edge contribution
q^{E}_{\mathcal{A}}(w)
and the degree tax
q^{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(w)
– see (
2
).
Note that if the edge weights in
w
are
\{0,1\}

q_{\mathcal{A}}(w)
is the usual modularity score.

Given a probability weight function
w
, let
G_{w}
be the random (unweighted) graph obtained by considering each pair of nodes
u,v
independently, and including edge
uv
with probability
w_{uv}
.
Lemma
9
will be proven in the following way. We first calculate the modularity score of a partition with a given signature in a deterministic weighted graph (see also Lemma
15
). Secondly, we show that the random graph

is likely to have the property that the modularity score of any
k
-part partition is within a small window of the corresponding modularity score in the deterministic weighted graph. For the latter we use a concentration result from
[

]
, and this is done in Appendix

Lemma 15
.

Fix integer
k\geq 2
. For integer
n\geq k
that is divisible by
k
, let
P_{1}\cup\cdots\cup P_{k}
be a partition of
V=[n]
, where
|P_{j}|=n/k
for all
j\in[k]
.
Let
w=w(n,k,p,q)
be the weight function on node set
V
with
w_{uv}=p
if
u
and
v
are in the same part
P_{j}
for some

and with
w_{uv}=q
otherwise. Then, for all
k
-part partitions
\mathcal{A}
with signature

,

q_{\mathcal{A}}(w)=(1+O(1/n))\,\frac{p-q}{p+(k-1)q}\cdot\frac{g(X)}{k^{2}},

where
g(X)
is defined as in (
10

Proof of Lemma
15
.

To simplify the notation, in this proof we will use
f(n)\sim f^{\prime}(n)
to denote that
f(n)=(1+O(1/n))f^{\prime}(n)
.

First, note that the weighted degree is the same for each node, namely, for any
u\in V
,

d_{u}^{(w)}=(n-1)q+(n/k-1)(p-q)\sim n\left(q+\frac{p-q}{k}\right)=\frac{n}{k}\Big(p+(k-1)q\Big).

As a result,
{\rm vol}_{w}(V)\sim\frac{n^{2}}{k}(p+(k-1)q)
, and so
e_{w}(V)=\frac{1}{2}{\rm vol}_{w}(V)\sim\frac{n^{2}}{2k}(p+(k-1)q)
.

Let us now fix
i\in[k]
and aim to find an asymptotic value of
e_{w}(A_{i})
. Note that there are two types of contributions to consider, namely, the weight of edges within each planted part
P_{j}
and the weight of edges between planted parts
P_{j}
and
P_{j^{\prime}}
. First, note that

|A_{i}|=\sum_{j=1}^{k}|A_{i}\cap P_{j}|=\frac{n}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}.

(Recall the definition of the signature:
x_{ij}=|A_{i}\cap P_{j}|/(n/k)
.) We get that

\displaystyle e_{w}(A_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{|A_{i}|}{2}q+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{|A_{i}\cap P_{j}|}{2}(p-q)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\frac{n}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}\right)^{2}q+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{n}{k}\,x_{ij}\right)^{2}(p-q)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}}{2k^{2}}\left(q\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}\right)^{2}+(p-q)\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}^{2}\right),

and so the edge contribution is equal to

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{e_{w}(A_{i})}{e_{w}(V)}\sim\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{q}{p+(k-1)q}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}\right)^{2}+\frac{p-q}{p+(k-1)q}\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}^{2}\right).

(16)

On the other hand, again using the fact that the weighted degree is the same for each node,

{\rm vol}_{w}(A_{i})\sim|A_{i}|\frac{n}{k}\Big(p+(k-1)q\Big)=\left(\frac{n}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}\right)\frac{n}{k}\Big(p+(k-1)q\Big)=\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}}\Big(p+(k-1)q\Big)\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij},

and so

\left(\frac{{\rm vol}_{w}(A_{i})}{{\rm vol}_{w}(V)}\right)^{2}\sim\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}\right)^{2}.

We again use
{\rm vol}_{w}(V)\sim\frac{n^{2}}{k}(p+(k-1)q)
to conclude that the degree tax is equal to

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{{\rm vol}_{w}(A_{i})}{{\rm vol}_{w}(V)}\right)^{2}\sim\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}\right)^{2}.

(17)

Combining (
16
) and (
17
), we get that

\displaystyle q_{\mathcal{A}}(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{e_{w}(A_{i})}{m}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{{\rm vol}_{w}(A_{i})}{{\rm vol}_{w}(V)}\right)^{2}

(18)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\left(\frac{q}{p+(k-1)q}-\frac{1}{k}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}\right)^{2}+\frac{p-q}{p+(k-1)q}\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k^{2}}\cdot\frac{p-q}{p+(k-1)q}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(k\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}^{2}-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}\right)^{2}\right).

Finally we note that

\displaystyle k\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}^{2}-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(k-1)\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}^{2}-2\sum_{1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq k}x_{ij}x_{ij^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq k}\left(x_{ij}^{2}-2x_{ij}x_{ij^{\prime}}+x_{ij^{\prime}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq k}\left(x_{ij}-x_{ij^{\prime}}\right)^{2}.

Using this substitution in (
18
), we get the desired asymptotic value of
q_{\mathcal{A}}(w)
and the proof of the lemma is finished.
∎

$x_{ij}=0$ for all $(i,j)$ with $i\neq j,\;(i,j)\neq(2,1),(a,b)$}.

Notice that this fixes the value of all
k^{2}-k-1
variables:
x_{ab}=t
and all remaining variables take the value
0
, and that this assignment satisfies the remaining inequalities in (
22
). The value of
\tilde{g}_{2}(x)
, attained is that of
g(X)
with
x_{ab}=t
(and
x_{ij}=0
for all
(i,j)
with

, and
x_{ii}=\sum_{i^{\prime}:i^{\prime}\neq i}x_{i^{\prime}i}
for all

X^{(a,b)}(t)
, and, moreover, that again
g(X^{(a,b)}(t))=k(k\!-\!1)-2t\big(k\!-\!t(k\!-\!1)\big)

D.2
Proof for the general case
d>1/k

Here we prove Lemma
11
, which will allow us to infer that partitions
\mathcal{A}
with high modularity scores must be at distance
d\leq 1/k
from the planted partition.

Proof.

Consider any partition
\mathcal{A}
with signature
X=(x_{ij})_{1\leq i\leq j\leq k}
that is at distance more than
1/k
from the planted partition
\mathcal{P}
. Our goal is to show that
g(X)\leq k(k-1)-2
.

Suppose that there exists a column
j\in[k]
(say,
j=1
) in
X
with at least two non-zero entries (say,
x_{11}>0
and
x_{21}>0
). Consider a family of signatures
X(s)
that is parametrised by variable
s
, as follows: for a given
s\in[-x_{11},x_{21}]
, matrix
X(s)
is exactly the same as
X
but
x_{11}
is replaced with
x_{11}+s
and
x_{21}
is replaced with
x_{21}-s
. Of course,
X=X(0)
.

Note that
g(X(s))
is a quadratic function of
s
with a positive coefficient in front of
s^{2}
. As a result, at least one of the following two properties holds:
(i)
g(X(s))
increases as
s
increases from
s=0
to
s=x_{21}
(which is equivalent to transferring a weight from
x_{21}
to
x_{11}
); or
(ii)
g(X(s))
increases as
s
decreases from
s=0
to
s=-x_{11}
(which is equivalent to transferring a weight from
x_{11}
to
x_{21}
).
Note that it might be the case that
g(X(s))
attains its local minimum at
s=0
and both properties hold at the same time.
Regardless, we may start from
s=0
and either increase or decrease
s
to gradually increase
g(X(s))
. While we do this, the distance to the planted partition
\mathcal{P}
does not need to behave monotonically. However, it is easy to see (but it is crucial for the argument) that the distance to
\mathcal{P}
is a continuous function of
s
. Once we are done with transferring the weight, we get an additional zero entry in our signature matrix, and we can move on to the next pair of non-zero entries, possibly in a different column
j
.

We need to consider two cases now. Suppose first that during the above process the distance to
\mathcal{P}
is equal to
1/k
. We prematurely stop the process at the very first time this happens, and let
Y
be the signature we have at that moment. Since we kept increasing the function
g
along the way, it follows from Lemma
10
(applied with
t=1
) that

g(X)<g(Y)\leq\max_{X\in\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)}g(X)=k(k-1)-2,

which finishes the proof of the lemma in this case.

Suppose now that the distance from
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{P}
was always more than
1/k
, but we had to stop the above process of transferring weights at some point because each column had exactly one non-zero entry (of course these non-zero entries must be equal to one). This means that each part of
\mathcal{A}
is a union of some planted parts, and the distance between
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{P}
is equal to
i/k
for some integer
i\geq 2
.

Now, take any part which is a union of
r\geq 2
planted parts and split it into two parts, consisting of
r-1
and a single planted part, respectively. After such partition refinement, the function
g
increases. Indeed, before splitting the part with
r
planted parts, the contribution of that part to the function
g
is
r(k-r)
, but after splitting, the two resulting parts contribute
(r-1)(k-r+1)+(k-1)
, which is equal to
r(k-r)+2(r-1)>r(k-r)
. We continue such refinements of partitions until the distance to
\mathcal{P}
is
1/k
. At that point, the partition
\mathcal{A}
with signature
Y
has one part consisting of two planted parts, while the remaining parts consist of just one planted part. The conclusion is as before:

g(X)<g(Y)=\max_{X\in\mathcal{X}_{k}(1)}g(X)=k(k-1)-2.

This finishes the proof of Lemma
11
.
∎

Appendix E
Proof of Theorem
2
: SBM has OGP

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We will prove Theorem
2
using Theorem
6
, our concentration result in Lemma
9
, and the optimisation result in Lemma
11
.

First, we will set up the range for the distances. Recall that
h(d)
is a quadratic function of
d
, with minimum at
\frac{1}{2(k-1)}
. See also Figure
2
. Take any
\nu\in\left(\frac{1}{2(k-1)},\frac{1}{k}\right)
, choose

\nu^{\prime}=\frac{2}{3}\cdot\nu+\frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{k}
, and let
\nu^{\prime\prime}\in\left[0,\frac{1}{2(k-1)}\right)
be the unique number such that
h(\nu^{\prime\prime})=h(\nu^{\prime})
. Let
n_{0}
be the smallest integer such that
1/\sqrt{n_{0}}<\nu^{\prime}-\nu<\frac{1}{2(k-1)}-\nu^{\prime\prime}
.

Next, we define
\mu=\frac{a-b}{a+(k-1)b}\big(h(0)-h(\nu^{\prime})\big)
and
note that
\frac{a-b}{a+(k-1)b}\,h(0)-\mu=\frac{a-b}{a+(k-1)b}\,h(\nu^{\prime})
. Choose a positive
\varepsilon<\frac{a-b}{a+(k-1)b}\,(h(\nu^{\prime})-h(\nu))
. Then, by Theorem
6
, we obtain that parts (i) and (ii) below hold with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
, and for
n>n_{0}
large enough:

(i)

For all partitions
\mathcal{A}
at distance
d\in[0,\nu^{\prime}-\nu)
and at distance
d\in(1/k-(\nu^{\prime}-\nu),1/k]
, it holds that

q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)>\frac{a-b}{a+(k-1)b}\,h(d)-\varepsilon>\frac{a-b}{a+(k-1)b}\,h(\nu^{\prime})=\frac{a-b}{a+(k-1)b}\,h(0)-\mu.

(ii)

For any partition
\mathcal{A}
at distance
d\in\left(\frac{k}{2(k-1)},\nu\right)\subset\left[0,\frac{1}{k}\right]
,

q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)<\frac{a-b}{a+(k-1)b}\,h(d)+\varepsilon<\frac{a-b}{a+(k-1)b}\,h(\nu^{\prime})=\frac{a-b}{a+(k-1)b}\,h(0)-\mu.

Together, (i) and (ii) establish Theorem
2
.
∎

Appendix F
Proof of Theorem

We prove the following more detailed proposition, which implies Theorem
5
.

Proposition 19

(
Partitions with near-optimal modularity are close to the planted).

Fix
a>b>0
and integer
k\geq 2
. Let
p=p(n)=\omega\;a/n
and
q=q(n)=\omega\;b/n
. For any
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta<\frac{a-b}{a+(k-1)b}\cdot\frac{2}{k^{2}}
, there exists
c=c(\varepsilon)
such that, if
\omega>c
, then the following holds with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
provided
n
is large enough.

Let
G\in\mathcal{G}(n,k,p,q)
with the planted partition
\mathcal{P}
.
Then,
q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)>q^{\star}(G)-\delta
for some
k
-part partition
\mathcal{A}
implies
d(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})<\delta^{\prime}+\varepsilon
,
where
\delta^{\prime}=\delta^{\prime}(\delta)\in\big(0,\frac{1}{k(k-1)}\big)
.

In fact,
\delta^{\prime}=\delta^{\prime}(\delta)
is the unique value in the interval
\big(0,\frac{1}{k(k-1)}\big)
that satisfies

\frac{a-b}{a+(k-1)b}h(\delta^{\prime})=\frac{a-b}{a+(k-1)b}h(0)-\delta,

which is equivalent to

\delta=\frac{a-b}{a+(k-1)b}\Big(2\delta^{\prime}(1-\delta^{\prime}(k-1))\Big).

The intuition for the proof is that
if
the modularity score of a partition is sufficiently high, i.e., at least
q^{\star}(G)-\delta
,
then
, by our concentration results, we may translate this to a lower bound on
g(X)
for
X
the signature of
\mathcal{A}
. Since Lemma
11
upper bounds the possible values of
g
for signatures far from the planted partition, we may then infer that
X
must be within distance
d\leq 1/k
of the planted partition. Then, by our detailed results in Theorem
6
, we may conclude that the distance of
X
to the planted partition is within
\delta^{\prime}
since
g(X)
attains such a high value.
We now provide the details.

Proof.

Let
s_{\delta}=\delta\frac{a+(k-1)b}{a-b}
, and pick
\varepsilon<\frac{2}{k^{2}}-\delta
.
We first note that, by Lemma
9
, we may take
\omega
large enough so that with probability at least
1-\varepsilon/4k^{2}
for all partitions
\mathcal{A}
, we have that for the corresponding signature
X
,

|q_{\mathcal{A}}(G)-\frac{a-b}{a+(k-1)b}\frac{g(X)}{k^{2}}|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4k^{2}}.

Recall also that with probability at least

Hence, since

Appendix G
The case of balanced partitions

Theorem
6
shows that the OGP holds when optimising over all
k
-part partitions. If, instead of optimising over
all

k
-part partitions at a certain distance from the planted partition, we optimise only over
balanced
such partitions, then the behaviour is markedly different. See the discussion in Section
6
. We now prove Proposition
12
which we restate below.
See
12

For the proof we recall the notion of circulation on directed graphs and the following classic result on cycle decompositions of such graphs – see for example
[
17
, Theorem 1]
or
[
34
, Theorem 11.1 and (11.3)]
. Let
G=(V(G),\vec{E}(G))
be a directed graph. A function
b:\vec{E}(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a
circulation
on
G
if for every vertex
v\in V(G)
it holds that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\;:\;\vec{vu}\in\vec{E}(G)\end{subarray}}b_{vu}\;=\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\;:\;\vec{uv}\in\vec{E}(G)\end{subarray}}b_{uv},

i.e., the total outflow from
v
equals the total inflow into
v
. Circulations have the following structure related to directed cycles:

Lemma 20

(Cycle decomposition of a nonnegative circulation)
.

Suppose
B=(b_{ij})_{i,j\in[k]}
is a circulation and satisfies
b_{ij}\geq 0
for
i\neq j
and
b_{ii}=0
.
Then, for some
m\leq|(i,j):b_{ij}>0|
,
there exist
simple directed cycles

C_{1},\dots,C_{m}
in
G
and weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{m}>0
such that, for every
i\neq j
,

b_{ij}\;=\;\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}w_{\ell}\,\mathbf{1}\{\vec{ij}\in C_{\ell}\}.

Proof of Proposition
12
.

Fix
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<k-1
, and let
X^{*}\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}_{k}(t_{2})
be such that
g(X^{*})=g^{\rm bal}(t_{2})
. Note that it suffices to construct
\widetilde{X}\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}_{k}(t_{1})
such that
g(\widetilde{X})>g(X^{*})
since then we would have

g^{\rm bal}(t_{1})\geq g(\widetilde{X})>g(X^{*})=g^{\rm bal}(t_{2}).

(23)

The intuition for the proof is that we can regard the non-diagonal entries of
X^{*}
as a directed graph; and they form a non-negative circulation. We can then apply Lemma
20
to get a directed cycle decomposition
\{(C_{\ell},w_{\ell})\}_{\ell}
such that these sum to the off-diagonal entries of
X^{*}
. For any directed cycle and
\varepsilon\leq w_{\ell}
, we may modify an
X\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}(t)
by deleting an
\varepsilon
weight from any
x_{ij}
with
\vec{ij}
in the cycle, adding
\varepsilon
weight to
x_{ii}
. We call this a
(C,\varepsilon)
-transfer move, and we will see that this preserves the properties in (
11
), and will thus yield an element of
\mathcal{X}(t^{\prime})
for
t^{\prime}<t
. We will show that the
g
value has strictly decreased by this operation. Starting from
X^{*}
, we use the cycle decomposition to perform a sequence of transfer moves which allows us to construct the required
\widetilde{X}
in (
23
). Let us now give the details.

We begin by defining the
(C,\varepsilon)
-transfer move on matrix
X
. Given a directed cycle
C
and
\varepsilon>0
define
X^{(\varepsilon)}=T_{C,\varepsilon}(X)
by setting

\displaystyle x^{(\varepsilon)}_{ii}

\displaystyle:=x_{ii}+\varepsilon,\qquad\text{ for all }i\in V(C),\qquad\qquad x^{(\varepsilon)}_{ij}:=x_{ij}-\varepsilon,\qquad\text{ for all }\vec{ij}\in\vec{E}(C),

and leaving all other entries unchanged.

Claim 21
.

We
claim
that if
X\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}_{k}(t)
,
C
is a directed cycle with
V(C)\subseteq[k]
and
0<\varepsilon\leq\min_{\vec{ij}\in\vec{E}(C)}x_{ij}
, then, for
X^{(\varepsilon)}=T_{C,\varepsilon}(X)
,

•

g(X^{(\varepsilon)})>g(X)
and

•

X^{(\varepsilon)}\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}_{t}(t^{\prime})
where
t^{\prime}=t-|C|\varepsilon
.

{claimproof}

We first prove that
g
is strictly increased by the transfer move, i.e.,
g(X^{(\varepsilon)})>g(X)
. Notice that we may rewrite
g(X)
using the Frobenius norm. Fix a row
i
, then, since
\sum_{j}x_{ij}=1
,

\sum_{1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq k}(x_{ij}-x_{ij^{\prime}})^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}(x_{ij}-x_{ij^{\prime}})^{2}=k\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}^{2}-\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}\Big)^{2}=k\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{ij}^{2}-1,

so that

g(X)=k\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}^{2}-k

(24)

Hence, showing
g(X^{(\varepsilon)})>g(X)
is equivalent to showing
\sum_{i,j}(x^{(\varepsilon)}_{ij})^{2}>\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}^{2}
.
Note that only
2|C|
entries change in the transfer move. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\big(x^{(\varepsilon)}_{ij}\big)^{2}-\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in V(C)}\Big((x_{ii}+\varepsilon)^{2}-x_{ii}^{2}\Big)+\sum_{\vec{ij}\in\vec{E}(C)}\Big((x_{ij}-\varepsilon)^{2}-x_{ij}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=2\varepsilon\Big(\sum_{i\in V(C)}x_{ii}-\sum_{\vec{ij}\in\vec{E}(C)}x_{ij}\Big)+2|C|\varepsilon^{2}.

(25)

To see that (
25
) is positive, we argue as follows. Let
\pi\in S_{k}
be the permutation that maps
j\mapsto i
for directed edges
\vec{ij}
on the cycle, and fixes all other vertices.
Then
\sum_{j}x_{\pi(j),j}=\sum_{\vec{ij}\in\vec{E}(C)}x_{ij}+\sum_{i\notin V(C)}x_{ii}
.
However, by the alignment constraint of (
11
), which states that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{ii}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{\sigma(i)i}
for all permutations
\sigma
, we have that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{ii}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{\pi(i)i}
, so that

\sum_{i\in V(C)}^{k}x_{ii}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{ii}-\sum_{i\notin V(C)}^{k}x_{ii}\;\geq\;\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{\pi(j),j}-\sum_{i\notin V(C)}^{k}x_{ii}=\sum_{\vec{ij}\in\vec{E}(C)}x_{ij}.

Thus, (
25
) is at least
2|C|\varepsilon^{2}>0
, so we have
g\big(X^{(\varepsilon)}\big)>g(X)
, as required.

To see the second part of the claim, note first that
x^{(\varepsilon)}_{ij}\geq 0
for all
i,j\in[k]
by construction.
Also, row sums remain
1
since, for rows
i
with
i\in V(C)
, we decrease one entry by
\varepsilon
and increase the diagonal
entry by the same amount, and other row sums do not change. The same argument applies to column sums,
and thus
X^{(\varepsilon)}
is doubly stochastic. Moreover,

\sum_{i\neq j}x^{(\varepsilon)}_{ij}\;=\;\sum_{i\neq j}x_{ij}-|C|\varepsilon\;=\;t-|C|\varepsilon=t^{\prime}.

Lastly, for any permutation
\sigma
,
we note that by the definition of the transfer move, for
i
such that
\sigma(i)\neq i
, it holds that
x_{ii}^{(\varepsilon)}\geq x_{ii}
and
x^{(\varepsilon)}_{\sigma(i)i}\leq x_{\sigma(i)i}
. Hence we have

\sum_{i}x^{(\varepsilon)}_{ii}-\sum_{i}x^{(\varepsilon)}_{\sigma(i)i}=\sum_{i\;:\;\sigma(i)\neq i}(x_{ii}^{(\varepsilon)}-x^{(\varepsilon)}_{\sigma(i)i}).

Thus since
\sum_{i}x_{ii}\geq\sum_{i}x_{\sigma(i),i}
held for
X
, it still holds for
X^{(\varepsilon)}
. We conclude that
X^{(\varepsilon)}
satisfies the constraints (
11
) and
X^{(\varepsilon)}\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}(t^{\prime})
which completes the proof of the claim.

We now return to constructing
\widetilde{X}
to establish (
23
). Recall that
X^{*}\in\mathcal{X}_{k}(t_{2})
is such that
g(X^{*})=g^{\rm bal}(t_{2})
.
Define
B=(b_{ij})
by
b_{ij}=x^{*}_{ij}
for
i\neq j
and
b_{ii}=0
; and note that
B
is a circulation on
[k]
. To see this we need to check that the outflow from any vertex
i\in[k]
equals the total inflow into
i
. Since the
i
th row and column both sum to
1

,

\sum_{j\;:\;i\neq j}b_{ij}\;=\;\sum_{j\;:\;i\neq j}x^{*}_{ij}\;=\;1-x^{*}_{ii}\;=\;\sum_{j\;:i\neq j}x^{*}_{ji}\;=\;\sum_{j\;:i\neq j}b_{ji},

as required, confirming that
B
is a circulation.
Therefore, by Lemma
20
, there exist simple directed cycles
C_{1},\dots,C_{m}
in
G

and weights

such that for every

,

b_{ij}\;=\;\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}w_{\ell}\,\mathbf{1}\{\vec{ij}\in C_{\ell}\}.

In particular, the total off-diagonal mass decomposes as

t_{2}\;=\;\sum_{i\neq j}x^{*}_{ij}\;=\;\sum_{i\neq j}b_{ij}\;=\;\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}w_{\ell}\,|C_{\ell}|.

(26)

Hence, we may choose

\sum_{\ell<L}w_{\ell}\,|C_{\ell}|<t_{2}-t_{1}\leq\sum_{\ell\leq L}w_{\ell}\,|C_{\ell}|
.
Define

, and

\varepsilon_{L}\ :=\ \frac{t_{2}-t_{1}-\sum_{\ell<L}w_{\ell}\,|C_{\ell}|}{r_{L}},

.
Now define a sequence of matrices by

and, for

X^{(\ell)}\ :=\ T_{C_{\ell},\varepsilon_{\ell}}\big(X^{(\ell-1)}\big)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\widetilde{X}:=X^{(L)}

, each transfer move preserves nonnegativity, double stochasticity, the alignment constraint,

. Hence for

.
