A decade of renal dietetic staffing and malnutrition prevalence in dialysis patients: An observational study in a large dialysis population
Authors: Anne Snelson, Jenny Wei, Stephanie Greco, Sajani Wijekoon, Marcus Connor, Susannah King, Raisa Shaikh, Lillian King
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThis study explores the maintenance of dietetic full time equivalent staffing alongside malnutrition rate in a metropolitan renal dialysis service in Victoria, Australia. Objectives were to: (1) describe changes in dietitian staff‐to‐patient ratios over time; (2) evaluate dietetic service delivery against best practice guidelines and (3) report malnutrition prevalence over a 10‐year period.MethodsData were collated from nine retrospective point prevalence audits conducted between 2009 and 2024 across satellite, home haemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis modalities. Nutritional status was primarily assessed using the Subjective Global Assessment, with the Mini Nutritional Assessment and Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition criteria introduced in 2024. Malnutrition prevalence was analysed across gender, age and dialysis vintage. Staffing levels were mapped against patient growth, with a target of 1 full time equivalent dietitian per 143 patients (1:143). A decade of renal dietetic staffing and malnutrition prevalence in dialysis patients: An observational study in a large dialysis populationResultsBetween 2014 and 2024, dietitian staffing levels increased proportionally with patient numbers, maintaining dietitian staff‐to‐patient ratios between 1:126 and 1:157. Nutritional assessments completed in the home haemodialysis population rose from 25% to 96%. Malnutrition prevalence declined from 25% to 18% in the haemodialysis and from 22% to 11% in the home haemodialysis populations. These improvements were sustained over time. Malnutrition prevalence was higher in older adults, females and individuals on dialysis for less than 1 year.ConclusionThis audit observed that maintaining dietetics staffing in line with recommendations aligned with sustained reductions in malnutrition prevalence. These findings support the integral role of renal dietitians in multidisciplinary renal care.

INTRODUCTION

The demand for dialysis for people with stage 5 chronic kidney disease (CKD) has risen significantly over the past two decades. 1 Between 2003 and 2022, the number of Australians requiring dialysis has doubled, driven by increasing rates of diabetes, hypertension, obesity, an ageing population and limited access to kidney transplants. 1 This has placed significant burden on patients and the health care system, resulting in an increased demand for specialised renal clinicians. 2 , 3

Renal dietitians play a key role in improving clinical outcomes for patients on dialysis through assisting patients to understand and manage their fluid and electrolyte restrictions, achieve their goal weight for surgical approval to be waitlisted for transplant, and identifying and treating patients at risk of malnutrition. 4 International guidelines recommend all patients on dialysis receive a full nutrition assessment at least annually, 5 with evidence suggesting dietetic assessments every 6 months are effective in detecting malnutrition and enabling early, targeted education and intervention. 6

Malnutrition is a significant complication commonly associated with CKD, including people on dialysis. Malnutrition is associated with increased mortality and morbidity, poorer clinical outcomes and decreased quality of life. 7 Globally, the prevalence of CKD‐related malnutrition has been estimated at 42.7%, 7 while rates in Australian dialysis units vary from 3% to 43.5%. 2 , 6 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 The Subjective Global Assessment (SGA) tool, 12 validated for use in dialysis populations, is widely used to assess and diagnose malnutrition. 5 , 13 Many factors can contribute to malnutrition for people on dialysis, including anorexia and inadequate oral intake caused by uraemia, and nutrient losses from dialysis. 14 Evidence supports the effectiveness of dietetic services at identifying and reducing malnutrition rates in dialysis units 2 , 6 , 11 through individualised nutrition plans and targeted interventions. This not only benefits patients but is cost‐effective for the healthcare system, with hospital admissions being more common, lasting longer and costing more in malnourished patients. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 Addressing malnutrition in dialysis populations is not only a clinical imperative but also a strategic priority for sustainable healthcare.

While there are established guidelines for renal dietetic workforce recommendations, 19 , 20 dietetic staff‐to‐patient ratios vary across Australian dialysis units. 21 Further evidence on dietetic staff to patient ratios in Australian dialysis units, combined with clinical outcome measures such as nutritional status, is needed to support benchmarking and advocate for adequate dietetic services within dialysis units.

Few studies have explored long‐term trends in both malnutrition and dietetic staffing within a single health service. This retrospective cohort study aims to: (1) describe the dietitian staff‐to‐patient ratio within a large metropolitan dialysis service in Victoria, Australia over time; (2) assess the extent of dietetic service delivery against best practice guidelines and (3) report malnutrition prevalence in the dialysis population of the service over a 10‐year period. The overarching goal of this study is to provide benchmarking data to support health care service planning and enable meaningful comparisons across different health services.

METHODS

This study reports on the nutritional status of dialysis patients within Monash Health's Nephrology service, a large metropolitan multi‐site tertiary hospital network in Melbourne, Australia, serving a population of approximately 2800 people with CKD stages 4 and 5, including transplant recipients. In 2024, the adult dialysis population included 780 patients across five internal satellite haemodialysis (HD) units, external satellite dialysis units, home haemodialysis (Home HD) and peritoneal dialysis (PD). Over 80 patients were dialysing in‐centre or as transient patients. Transient patients are those without a fixed dialysis site who rotate between units based on availability, are typically new to dialysis and awaiting permanent placement. Their increasing numbers reflect limited service capacity to accommodate new satellite HD patients. Home HD and PD are collectively referred to as home dialysis patients. Patients dialysing at external health care services under the care of this organisation's Nephrologists were excluded, as they were managed by the dietitians at those sites.

Dialysis prescriptions were individualised. Most satellite HD patients received 5‐h dialysis treatments, three times per week using bicarbonate base dialysate. Home HD patients typically dialysed for 6 h every second day, with variations ranging from 5 h thrice weekly to 8 h second daily. PD patients typically used automated PD with an icodextrin day dwell, unless minimal dialysis was required. Dialysis prescriptions were adjusted on an individual basis by the treating team of nephrologists and nursing staff. Nutritional supplements were funded by the Nephrology department for dialysis patients identified as requiring them by renal dietitians.

Nine retrospective point prevalence audits were conducted in 2009, 2014, 2015, 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2021 and 2024. These audits assessed malnutrition prevalence and dietetic service delivery against best practice guidelines relevant at the time. 5 , 14 From 2014 to 2019, guidelines recommended a comprehensive nutrition assessment, including malnutrition assessment, evaluation of weight trends, fluid status and biochemistry, at least every 6 months. 14 In 2021 and 2024, updated guidelines 5 recommended annual assessments, with increased frequency for patients with identified nutrition‐related concerns.

While SGA remained the primary malnutrition assessment tool used. In 2024, the Mini Nutrition Assessment (MNA) and the Global Clinical Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM) criteria were added to support remote consultations, where physical exams were not feasible, noting that GLIM requires further validation in renal dialysis populations. This shift reflected patient preference for telephone‐based reviews and allowed application of malnutrition diagnostic criteria without physical assessment. Tool usage was tracked in the 2024 audit. All dietitians completed competency‐based SGA training. In 2024, staff received training to implement GLIM criteria following an organisation‐wide adoption.

Prior to 2009, dietetic services were limited to one satellite dialysis unit. Funding was extended to all four dialysis units in 2009, with dietetic staffing levels based on the British Renal Allied Health workforce algorithm, 20 targeting 1 full time equivalent (FTE) dietitian per 143 patients (0.007 FTE/patient), funded by the Nephrology Department. This FTE per patient was consistent and, with each unit expansion, nephrology executives supported resourcing to ensure maintenance of this ratio. This FTE was quarantined and not merged with other acute FTE. Malnutrition rates were recorded at service commencement and 6 months post‐implementation.

From 2014, dietitians monitored all permanent satellite HD patients. Transient patients were seen if attending a dialysis unit when a dietitian was present, but audits of their nutritional status and dietetic service delivery began in 2016. In 2015, the home dialysis service transitioned from being part of an acute dietitian caseload and triaged against adult inpatients to a dedicated renal dietitian service. Service delivery and patient handover processes evolved, with initial monitoring conducted at individual sites. From 2022, a unified handover database was introduced for all patients, streamlining both handover and audit processes.

Audit objectives consistently focussed on malnutrition prevalence and service delivery against guidelines, 5 , 14 with later audits identifying high risk subpopulations for targeted interventions. Data were de‐identified and collated from medical records, capturing a cross‐sectional snapshot of nutritional status and dietitian service delivery. Demographic data, including gender, age, body mass index (BMI) and dialysis vintage, were included from 2019 onwards. Dialysis vintage refers to how long a person has been receiving dialysis treatment.

Collected data included patient numbers per dialysis unit, SGA assessments and malnutrition classifications (SGA‐A: well nourished, SGA‐B/C: malnourished). Malnutrition rates were calculated as proportions of the total cohort. Subgroup analyses by sex, age and dialysis vintage were conducted in 2019, 2021 and 2024. For each audit, dietetic service provision was described, including FTE‐to‐patient ratios and documented completion of a comprehensive nutrition assessment consistent with best practice guidelines. Individual assessment domains were not extracted or reported separately.

A Kruskal‐Wallis test was used to compare gender, age, BMI and dialysis vintage between dialysis modalities for years 2019, 2021 and 2024. Where significant differences were found, Dunn's test was applied for pairwise comparisons, with Bonferroni adjustment to control for multiple comparisons.

This study adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines as outlined by the EQUATOR Network. The study protocol was approved by the Monash Health Human Research Ethics Committee Monash Health Reference Number: RES‐25‐0000‐027Q.

Prior to the 2009 dietetic service expansion, malnutrition prevalence across satellite dialysis sites was 26%, and it dropped to 18% within 6 months of service. Similarly, following the introduction of a dedicated renal dietitian service for Home HD patients in 2015, malnutrition rates declined from 22% to 11%. Transient dialysis patients consistently exhibited the highest malnutrition prevalence (25%–33%, except 9% in 2017), while Home HD patients maintained the lowest rates post‐service implementation (4%–11%).

Figure 2 presents malnutrition rates by age, gender and dialysis vintage (2019, 2021 and 2024). Older patients (>80 years) consistently showed higher prevalence (14.8%–19.2%) than younger patients (<40 years: 6.8%–10.9%). Patients dialysing for less than 1 year had higher prevalence (16.9%–19.2%) than those with 1–6.9 years on dialysis (8.5%–12%). A spike in malnutrition in patients dialysing greater than 7 years was seen in 2019 (17.1%), but not in subsequent years (7.7%–9.4%). In all years, females had higher malnutrition prevalence than males, most notably in 2021 (17.2% in females vs. 8.8% in males).

Malnutrition Rates by Age, Dialysis Vintage and Gender (2019, 2021 and 2024). Figure 2 shows collated malnutrition rates for satellite haemodialysis, transient haemodialysis, home haemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis by age group, dialysis vintage and gender for 2019, 2021 and 2024.

Between 2014 and 2021, the SGA was the sole tool used for malnutrition assessment. In 2024, SGA remained the primary tool (79%), followed by GLIM (13%) and MNA (8%). SGA was most used for permanent and transient satellite dialysis patients (both 79%), while PD and Home HD patients had more varied tool use: SGA (56%–58%), GLIM (26%–34%) and MNA (11%–15%). Overall, 80% of malnutrition diagnoses in 2024 were made using SGA, with GLIM and MNA contributing to 12% and 8% respectively, paralleling the use of each assessment tool.

DISCUSSION

This study provides a 10‐year overview of malnutrition prevalence and dietetic staffing across this organisation's dialysis population. Patients dialysing at satellite units had more consistent access to dietetic services compared to transient and home dialysis patients; however, these gaps have narrowed over time. Malnutrition was most prevalent among transient patients, likely due to less consistent access to dietetic care and frequent site transitions, disrupting continuity. Many were in their first year of dialysis, and the absence of a pre‐dialysis dietetic service during the audit period may have contributed to poor nutritional status, especially among those with uremic symptoms affecting appetite. Malnutrition is significantly more prevalent in pre‐dialysis populations without nutrition intervention, 22 suggesting poor nutritional status prior to commencing dialysis may influence malnutrition diagnoses when starting dialysis. With increasing strain on dialysis capacity 3 transient patient numbers are expected to rise. Strategies such as establishing a pre‐dialysis dietetic service, improving handover processes to highlight patients at risk, and reducing patient movement between sites should be considered to support this high‐risk group.

Contrary to existing literature linking longer dialysis vintage with increased malnutrition risk, 23 , 24 our data showed the highest prevalence of malnutrition was in the first year of dialysis. This may reflect clinical instability during dialysis initiation, lack of pre‐dialysis dietetic support, higher proportions of transient dialysis patients and the dietetic service model with routine surveillance and assessment of all patients once established on dialysis. Sustained dietetic care appears beneficial for patients with longer dialysis vintage, reinforcing the importance of early and ongoing intervention.

The 2021 audit showed reduced malnutrition assessments across all cohorts, likely due to Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID‐19) related service changes. This included a shift to remote consultations that limited physical assessments required for SGA, which was the only nutrition assessment tool in use by the renal dietitians within the organisation at the time. Remote services for satellite patients were discontinued post‐pandemic, but a dedicated telephone clinic was introduced in 2022 for home dialysis patients. This clinic incorporated alternative nutrition assessment tools which were less reliant on physical assessments, contributing to the improvement in service delivery in home therapies patients in the 2024 audit.

After the 2021 audit, integrating MNA and GLIM tools alongside the SGA led to increased nutrition assessment coverage; PD rose from 56% to 97% and Home HD from 64% to 96%. GLIM was used more than MNA, likely due to MNA's age eligibility criteria (>65 years) which excluded younger Home HD patients. However, tool choice can influence malnutrition diagnosis. 25 The MNA may be less sensitive than SGA in detecting moderate malnutrition in HD patients, 26 and overall diagnostic agreement between GLIM and SGA in HD patients is low. 25 A study found 86% of GLIM‐defined malnutrition in HD patients was based on reduced muscle mass, 25 raising concerns about under‐diagnosis in virtual settings where physical assessment is limited.

In our study, malnutrition diagnosis rates were comparable for each tool, rather than any one tool showing superior sensitivity. In 2024, only 55% of Home HD and 58% of PD patients had an SGA completed, consistent with historic trends at this organisation. While GLIM may risk underdiagnosing malnutrition without physical assessment, its introduction helped to close gaps in nutrition assessment coverage and contributed to increased diagnosis of malnutrition compared to years relying solely on SGA. Further research comparing the diagnostic performance of SGA, MNA and GLIM, particularly with and without muscle mass assessment, is required to determine their reliability in remote or virtual care settings. Face‐to‐face assessments remain important when body composition evaluation is indicated, especially when video consultations are insufficient.

Lower malnutrition prevalence was observed following the introduction of a dedicated renal dietetic service. In 2009, satellite HD patients saw a decrease in malnutrition from 26% to 18% within 6 months of the introduction of dietetic services. Similarly, in 2015, Home HD patients experienced a reduction from 22% to 11% after a dedicated renal dietitian was introduced. These findings align with previous studies showing reduced malnutrition rates with dietetic services for HD patients. 2 , 6 , 11 For example, a model with one FTE dietitian per 100 HD patients saw malnutrition drop from 28% to 20% over 12 months 2 ; another reported a reduction from 43.5% to 28.9% after 6 months of dietetic service. 11 Aligning dietetic service delivery with best practice guidelines can also reduce malnutrition rates. 6 , 8

Malnutrition prevalence varies across Australian dialysis units. More research on key demographic features that are linked to higher malnutrition rates would assist in targeting nutrition interventions and resources. Given the anticipated influx of new, unstable or recently hospitalised patients in dialysis services, some malnutrition is inevitable, and rates alone should not be used to assess unit performance. 27 However, sustained dietetic input and interventions in line with evidenced‐based guidelines are associated with lower prevalence of malnutrition.

Renal dietitian workforce recommendations vary based on patient numbers, new patient intake and complexity of patients. 19 , 20 , 28 An Australian study reported a median FTE‐to‐patient ratio of 1:127 (range 1:36–1:207), using a benchmark of 1:100. 29 More recent benchmarking with health services of similar size to this organisation in Australia shows variation of FTE‐to‐patient ratios of 1:250 to 1:355 (personal communication). Benchmarking with other dialysis units in Victoria, Australia shows variation of FTE‐to‐patient ratios from 1:175 to 1:480 (personal communication) highlighting service inequalities within the state. Our results ranged from 1:126 in 2015 to 1:157 patients in 2024, with staffing growing alongside patient numbers, demonstrating sustainability.

Strengths of this study include the length of time that service provision has been tracked, the size of the service and that all dialysis modalities were included, increasing the generalisability of the results. Several limitations are acknowledged. This study used historical audit data from annual point prevalence assessments, providing population‐level snapshots rather than longitudinal follow‐up, so changes in malnutrition after intervention could not be assessed. Malnutrition was reported as a proportion of the total dialysis cohort, regardless of nutrition assessment completion, limiting comparability across years. Diagnoses based on GLIM and MNA were not validated against SGA, which may have affected consistency. Detailed data on dietetic review frequency and duration were not captured, limiting the ability to inform workforce planning. Potential confounding factors include changes in patient demographics, dialysis modality mix, nutrition assessment tools, guideline recommendations and concurrent service delivery developments over time.

Future research using prospective and longitudinal designs is required to evaluate individual nutritional trajectories, malnutrition resolution and patient‐centred outcomes such as quality of life and functional status. This audit observed low malnutrition rates in renal dialysis services supported by adequate dietetic staffing, with timely nutrition interventions and comprehensive malnutrition assessments routinely undertaken. These findings reinforce the important role of renal dietitians and support the integration of routine dietetic evaluation within multidisciplinary renal care.
