From Genetic Variation to Biological Function: GBE Virtual Collection on Functional Genomics
Authors: Pedro Andrade
Published: 2026

A central challenge in evolutionary biology is understanding how genetic variants influence biological functions and, ultimately, phenotypic diversity and adaptation. In recent decades, important strides have been made in inferring function from genome-scale data, particularly in nonmodel organisms—even as the debate rages over basic questions such as the relative proportions of functional to nonfunctional DNA in genomes (eg see Galeota-Sprung et al. 2020; Fagundes et al. 2022; Adey et al. 2026). Yet, the field remains driven by studies of easier-to-study organisms and phenotypes; furthermore, obtaining a broad, coherent picture of organismal function must go beyond solely associating phenotype to genotype.

In a recent Perspective in Genome Biology and Evolution (Kassen 2026), Rees Kassen of McGill University (Canada) identifies critical issues in the field. Specifically, although the relationships between fitness increases and genetic/phenotypic change are well understood from a statistical perspective, a general understanding of how genetic changes translate into genome function remains elusive. In other words, Kassen argues for the necessity of a general theory of adaptation that predicts the functional properties of the genes or other genomic features involved in adaptation.

In light of the growing interest and uncertainties in the field, GBE has prepared a virtual collection on “Functional Genomics.” This virtual collection hosts a selection of recent articles including macroevolutionary studies that survey patterns of protein evolution across major branches of the tree of life, detailed studies on gene families known to impact critical phenotypes, and gene expression studies that advance our understanding of the regulatory landscape of genomes. This selection of articles is by no means exhaustive, but rather a demonstration of the myriad approaches being used to reveal the functional genome across the tree of life.

The virtual collection leads with the Perspective by Kassen (2026). In it, the author reviews the extensive body of work amassed from years of experimental evolution in the lab on microbial systems; in these experiments, microbial cultures are exposed to novel environments by being serially transferred to new media. Across studies, Kassen (2026) repeatedly found evidence that selection on gene regulation contributes to adaptation. Kassen argues that these patterns may reflect the modular hierarchical nature of gene expression. In gene expression networks, genes encoding structural proteins are controlled by local regulatory elements; these, in turn, are controlled by global regulatory genes that control more fundamental aspects of gene regulation, such as the expression of transcriptional machinery itself (eg RNA polymerases).

Kassen (2026) also notes evidence that global regulators are likely to be involved in the initial phases of adaptation. Global regulators, which are transcription factors responsible for regulating large numbers of genes, are predicted to be highly pleiotropic. It has been argued on theoretical grounds that initial mutations in an adaptive walk will be less pleiotropic, keeping the organism close to a gradually changing fitness optimum. However, some studies on microbial systems show that first-step mutations have larger pleiotropic effects and can frequently be linked to genes encoding global regulators (Kassen 2026). The extent to which this insight applies across species is yet to be determined.

Identifying genetic changes with implications for organismal function is a traditional pursuit, but validating their function is often a challenging follow-up. Some studies in our virtual collection show how researchers can identify candidate causal variants and infer their effect on protein function. Casillas et al. (2026) investigated the genetic basis of color pattern phenotypes in the European adder, identifying a candidate missense variant at the premelanosome protein gene (PMEL), a gene frequently implicated in pigmentation disorder phenotypes. Fajkus et al. (2026) demonstrate how experimental approaches may be used to validate a gene's function, focusing on telomerase activity in a model beetle species. After identifying paralogs of telomerase RNA, a component of the telomerase ribonucleoprotein, the authors expressed a recombinant telomerase in vitro and demonstrated its catalytic potential. When direct experiments are not possible, computational approaches can also be used to infer functional evolution. This was shown by Krishnaswamy and Seshasayee (2025), who combined phylogenetics and sequence conservation analyses to reconstruct the evolution of functional residues of bacterial transcription factors that mediate DNA binding.

As Kassen (2026) noted, understanding a gene's regulatory landscape is essential to understanding how genetic variation shapes adaptive outcomes. But where does regulatory function reside in a genome? Benton et al. (2023) combined chromatin conformation and gene expression data from human tissues to investigate how cis-regulatory elements relate to tissue-specific gene expression patterns. The authors found that cis-regulatory element activity is associated with gene expression across tissues, but relationships are not always predictable. This study shows why linking regulatory elements to gene function is not straightforward—gene regulation depends on cellular context and the broader regulatory network. This observation is particularly relevant for multicellular organisms, for whom transcriptomic and epigenomic data at single-cell resolution are revealing important insights. Ren and Liu (2024), for example, used single-nucleus Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) of mouse cerebellum to demonstrate that most cis-regulatory elements are active in multiple tissues throughout cell differentiation, suggestive of high pleiotropy.

Ultimately, the goal of functional genomics is to link functional genetic diversity to the processes governing adaptation in natural systems—thus revealing their evolutionary history. To this end, phylogenomic and comparative genomic approaches have helped integrate information derived from detailed gene studies into broader evolutionary scales. For example, Bosi et al. (2024) generated a pan-genome from the genomes of 141 Vibrio species from deep-sea hydrothermal vents, highlighting that deep-sea lineages share virulence and antibiotic resistance genes with surface lineages that infect humans and aquatic animals, reframing our expectations on adaptive evolution in human pathogens. At a much shallower evolutionary scale, population genetics approaches analyzing gene flow, population structure, or changes in allele frequencies between populations also help elucidate adaptation. Rajpara et al. (2026) demonstrate this approach by correlating the presence of Neanderthal- and Denisovan-introgressed haplotypes in modern human samples to higher DNA virus loads; some of these segments have evidence of shifting modes of selection, suggesting that natural selection has contributed to their persistence in modern humans.

The studies in the GBE virtual collection on “Functional Genomics” are but a selection of many important studies published in recent years on this topic in our journal and elsewhere. Together, they show that a single approach is not sufficient to tackle what remains one of the most elusive questions in evolutionary biology. As we release the collection on this timely topic, we hope to stimulate discussion on the important questions raised by Kassen (2026) and invite colleagues working on functional genomics—particularly on understudied taxa—to contribute to these debates by submitting their latest research to GBE.
