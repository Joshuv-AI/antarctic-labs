Background

The food you eat is full of things your body needs to survive, called “nutrients,” and things it cannot use, called “waste.” Your body gets rid of waste by making “stool,” or poop. This happens when the food you eat travels from your stomach through your intestines (figure 1). You have two kinds of intestines, called small and large, which both do different things. By the time your food reaches the end of the large intestine, your body has taken out all the nutrients it can, and only waste is left. The waste is ready to leave the body. This happens through the very end of your intestines, called the rectum and, finally, the anus.

An ostomy is an opening in the belly made during operation that allows poop to leave the body before it can exit out your bottom. You may need an ostomy if part of the intestine is damaged or sick, such as in diverticulitis, cancer, injury, or inflammatory bowel disease (eg, ulcerative colitis).

There are different types of ostomies, such as a “colostomy,” which connects to the large intestine or colon, and an “ileostomy,” which connects to the last part of the small intestine called the ileum.

The opening of an ostomy is also called a “stoma.” A bag (also called an ostomy appliance) is attached to the skin around the stoma. Poop then leaves the body through the stoma and into the bag.

What happens in the hospital

Operation

Ostomies are made by dividing or cutting the intestine, bringing it outside the body, and attaching it to the skin. The operation is done in two main ways: minimally invasive or open.

Minimally invasive means the surgeon will use small “incisions” or cuts, long instruments, and a camera to see inside the belly on a screen. Other words to describe minimally invasive operation are “laparoscopic” or “robotic,” depending on the surgeon’s tools. Open surgery, on the other hand, means the surgeon will make a bigger cut on the belly to see directly inside the body. Both types of operation are safe and effective. Deciding which technique to use depends on many factors, like age, other illnesses, urgency of operation, and history of other belly operations.

Sometimes, you may be asked to do a “bowel prep” before operation, which cleans out the intestines. A “bowel prep” is usually done with antibiotics and medications like laxatives and stool softeners.

Possible complications after operation

Possible risks of the operation include bleeding or infection, although these are rare. Sometimes, when you have operation on your intestines, it may take some time to start working well after operation. This is called an “ileus” and typically resolves by not eating and letting your intestines rest. There can also be problems with the stoma after operation, such as narrowing, pushing out too far (also known as “prolapse”), falling under the skin, or separating from the skin. If you notice stool leaking from the edge of the bag or if it does not sit well on the skin, your skin may become red and painful, or you may get an infection. Your body heals from operation by scarring, similar to a scar you might see from a cut on your skin. Longer-term risks include blockage of the intestines from scar tissue and a hernia around the stoma.

Care after operation

Before leaving the hospital, the doctor or nursing team will explain to you how and when to empty and change the bag. They will also tell you how to care for the skin around the stoma, and when to call the doctor. They will make sure you get all the supplies you need for the ostomy. Before leaving the hospital, everyone caring for the ostomy should get to practice emptying and changing the bag.

What happens next

Ostomy care

Ostomy bags or appliances may come in one or two pieces and may be one-time use only or reusable. For all appliances, there is a “wafer” or sticker that goes around the stoma and sticks to the skin to protect it, and a bag or pouch, which stool empties into (figure 2).

Empty the bag when it is 1/3 of the way full. Depending on the type of stoma you have, it may need to be emptied anywhere from once a day to 4–5 times a day. When the bag is closed, it should not smell. Keeping the bag clean, sealing it well, and changing the bag a few times a week are ways to avoid strong smells.

Having all the supplies you need to change the ostomy appliance can make it easier. Clean the skin around the stoma with a washcloth and warm water, and pat the skin dry. The stoma itself does not need to be cleaned. Sometimes, if the wafer does not stick well to the skin, trimming the hair around the stoma helps. Always use an ostomy measuring tool to cut the wafer to the right shape and size. If you follow these steps, very little of your skin should be exposed around the stoma.

Ostomy characteristics

You should check on your stoma often. It should be pink or red and moist. The skin around it should be healthy and look like other skin on your belly. Any redness or skin breakdown may mean your bag does not fit well, and you have an infection. Other things to notice are whether the stoma is larger or smaller than usual, if there is more leakage than normal, or if you do not need to empty the bag as much because you are making less poop or gas. Ostomies should not be painful. Belly pain, bloating, nausea, throwing up, and no longer pooping or passing gas may be signs of a problem. If you are worried about your ostomy, please contact your doctor.

Living with an ostomy

Most patients with ostomies live a healthy and active lifestyle. The bag fits under clothes and is usually not visible. Patients can take baths or showers with or without their bag. Patients with an ostomy can swim, travel, play sports, and have sex.

Patients with colostomies may have softer poop than before operation. Ileostomies tend to make large, more liquid stool. Your stool may become thinner or thicker depending on what you eat. The best texture to aim for is like applesauce to avoid dehydration. If you have an ileostomy and you make more than 1 L of stool per day or need to empty your bag more than 4–5 times per day, contact your doctor.

Diet

Most people do not need to follow a special diet. Eating foods with fiber and drinking plenty of water helps avoid constipation, especially with a colostomy. For ileostomies, be sure to drink plenty of fluids to stay hydrated. Eating smaller meals more often during the day may also help you decrease how much stool you make. You may notice that the amount of output and what it looks like changes based on what you eat. Some foods may make your output smell worse or increase gas and bloating. There is no “right” or “wrong” thing to eat, but it is a trial-and-error process that is different from person to person.

Ostomy reversal

Some ostomies are placed for a short time until the body heals, whereas others can be permanent and are needed lifelong. To “reverse” your ostomy, you will need an operation where the ends of your intestines are sewn back together. After this operation, you will be able to poop from your bottom like you did before your ostomy. Your doctor will help decide if an ostomy is no longer needed.
