Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract:Vaccination-related distress (VRD) encompasses a range of stress reactions associated with vaccination, including pain, needle fear, and stress. VRD is a significant barrier to immunization, contributing to up to 8% of vaccination refusals in children and even higher rates in under-vaccinated populations. Beyond immediate discomfort, VRD can lead to long-term psychological and physical consequences, underscoring the need for effective VRD-mitigation strategies. The updated knowledge synthesis and clinical practice guideline by the HELPinKids&Adults team provides evidence-based recommendations organized using the 5P framework: process, procedural, physical, pharmacological, and psychological interventions. These interventions target different user groups, including children, their support persons, and health care professionals. Multicomponent (complex) interventions, such as the Canadian CARD system (Comfort-Ask-Relax-Distract), integrate education with multiple VRD-reducing strategies. These multicomponent (complex) interventions have been shown to be effective in reducing VRD and promoted the adoption of other VRD-reducing interventions. However, the efficacy of a multicomponent (complex) intervention in the Canadian context does not guarantee its applicability in the Dutch setting. Consequently, the adaptation of such an intervention for the Dutch National Immunization Program necessitates a systematic implementation approach. Using the EPIS framework (Exploration-Preparation-Implementation-Sustainment), we explain our experiences to date with the cultural adaptation of the CARD system, the engagement of user groups, and the preparation of a Dutch version of the CARD system for pilot testing within the Dutch vaccination context.

Vaccination-related distress (VRD) is an umbrella term encompassing a spectrum of stress reactions associated with vaccination, including vaccination-related pain, needle fear, nausea, stress, syncope, hyperventilation, and anxiety.1 In the general public, ∼8% of all vaccination refusals in children could be allocated to VRD, and in under-vaccinated populations, this percentage could even rise to 18.3%.2 Beyond immediate discomfort, VRD can lead to long-term adverse effects for individuals, including chronic pain, disability, and psychological distress.3 Therefore, various interventions have been studied to reduce both the occurrence and severity of VRD in infants, children, and adults.

The Canadian Help ELiminate Pain in Kids & Adults (HELPinKids&Adults) team reviewed the world evidence and formulated the first clinical practice guideline (CPG) in 2015 to provide guidance for VRD management across the lifespan, and bridge the gap between research and clinical practice.4 This knowledge synthesis and CPG received an update in 2025 and 2026. The main finding of the update addressed in this commentary related to the effectiveness of multicomponent (complex) interventions, which have primarily been studied within the Canadian vaccination service.5

In Canada, vaccinations are delivered across multiple settings, including school‑based and pharmacy‑based clinics, where they are typically administered by a health care professional (HCP) who routinely provides immunizations. Selected members of the HELPinKids&Adults team created a Canadian multicomponent (complex) intervention to translate the 2015 CPG into action. This Knowledge Translation tool, called the CARD system (Comfort-Ask-Relax-Distract), has been shown to effectively reduce pain, fear, and dizziness during vaccinations.6–8 Nevertheless, this intervention has not yet been the subject of an assessment in other vaccination settings, such as the Dutch National Immunization Program (NIP). The recommendations in the updated CPG include multicomponent (complex) interventions and offer the possibility of being tailored to the specific elements of the Dutch NIP (explained below). Importantly, the aim of the Dutch NIP needs to shift from a service that is only focused on achieving the highest frequency of provided vaccinations in the shortest time period to a service that is also aimed at doing everything within its power to reduce the presence and the impact of VRD in children.9 This commentary article reflects on the updated CPG from the perspective of Dutch vaccination services, aligning scientific and cultural-specific insights with the updated HELPinKids&Adults knowledge synthesis and CPG, providing core recommendations to Dutch vaccination services.

THE DUTCH NATIONAL IMMUNIZATION PROGRAM

Vaccinations included in the Dutch NIP are administered in different settings, the main components being individual consultations at (preventive) child health clinics (aged 0 to 4), group vaccination sessions (aged 5 to 18), or individual consultations (aged 5 to 18).10 Most children (aged 5 to 18) receive vaccinations during large group sessions held in spacious venues. During group vaccination sessions, private vaccination rooms are available for children who do not tolerate the group setting (among others due to VRD). Individual consultations are available for those who missed group sessions, had unsuccessful attempts, or prefer a more private setting. At the time of writing, children and parents do not receive specific information on how to prepare optimally or deal with eventual VRD. HCPs administering vaccinations are not systematically trained in applying VRD-mitigating interventions.

REDUCING VACCINATION-RELATED DISTRESS WITH THE 5P MODEL

The updated knowledge synthesis and CPG categorize effective VRD-reducing interventions according to the 5P framework: process, procedural, physical, pharmacological, and psychological.11 However, each category may address distinct user groups, such as children, support persons (parents and caregivers), and HCPs. Process interventions address all people involved, whereas procedural interventions are directed specifically at HCPs responsible for organizing and providing vaccination care. Physical, pharmacological, and psychological interventions are intended for children and their support persons, who may choose among the available options based on individual needs and preferences. A summary of interventions included in the updated CPG is described in the following paragraph.

Effective process interventions highlighted in the updated CPG include providing education for HCPs, support persons, and vaccination recipients, as well as implementing multicomponent (complex) interventions that engage all user groups.5 The focus of procedural interventions is on optimizing the vaccine injection technique. This is achieved by avoiding aspiration and ensuring rapid administration, administering vaccinations in accordance with the anticipated distress levels of the recipient, and minimizing fear cues before vaccine delivery.12 Physical interventions include both oral interventions (for infants), as well as other physical interventions. Effective oral interventions for infants include breastfeeding, bottle feeding, non-nutritive sucking, and the administration of sweet-tasting substances such as sucrose or glucose/dextrose solutions.13 Other physical interventions for infants include manual tapping. For older recipients of vaccinations, interventions include manual tapping as well as other manual or device-delivered pressure, cold application, vapocoolant, and vibration (with or without cold). Effective pharmacological interventions to reduce VRD are centered around topical anesthetics.14 Psychological interventions to reduce VRD in infants include letting the support person sing and offering toys as distraction. Psychological interventions to reduce VRD in older individuals include offering music, breathing activities with toys, digital and nondigital activities or games, virtual reality, playing with toys, and videos and movies.15

Allowing the child and their support persons to choose the preferred intervention is also highly recommended and potentially the most prominent factor in reducing VRD.5,13–15 However, successful adoption of these interventions requires a clearly articulated multilevel implementation strategy, given the multitude of different interventions, the importance of shared decision making, differences in vaccination practices, variations in user group support, and existing cultural diversity. A multicomponent (complex) intervention could preselect feasible interventions, overcome potential barriers, and initiate a paradigm shift. The systematic review on process interventions showed that multicomponent (complex) interventions reduce distress and are likely to increase the use of VRD-reducing interventions.5 Four of the 5 studies evaluating multicomponent (complex) interventions examined the CARD system in Canada,6–8,16 while the remaining study examined a 3-component intervention in Australia.17 Owing to their multicomponent structure, these interventions provide a flexible framework for integrating other VRD-reducing interventions and adapting them to cultural contexts, thereby supporting a shift toward greater emphasis on procedural comfort.9

The CARD system has been included in the Dutch NIP Guideline as an effective intervention for reducing VRD since 2024, where the system is categorized as a psychological intervention under the subheading “Vaccination to Technique”. However, guidance on the operationalization of the CARD system within Dutch NIP settings remains limited at the time of writing.10 Furthermore, an explanation of how to apply the CARD system as a multicomponent (complex) intervention within a local vaccination setting is also missing. This lack of guidance may be attributable to the fact that the CARD system has not yet been adapted to the specific characteristics of Dutch children, the organization of vaccination services, and the broader cultural context. Importantly, successful implementation of any complex intervention requires careful tailoring to the local setting, target population, and cultural environment.18

HOW TO INTEGRATE THE CARD SYSTEM IN THE NETHERLANDS

Currently, there is no documented evidence of how to adapt the CARD system for use in vaccination systems outside Canada. This paper used the evidence-based Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, and Sustainment (EPIS) framework to guide the pilot study evaluating the CARD system within the Dutch NIP. EPIS is a widely utilized framework that facilitates the implementation of evidence-based interventions into other settings. It is designed to support the systematic assessment of the context, as well as the planning of the implementation itself.18,19 In collaboration with the HELPinKids&Adults team, the authors initiated an adaptation process in the preparation phase that includes both cultural adaptations (modifications to ensure alignment with local norms, communication styles, and expectations) and practical adaptations (to fit the workflows and organizational structures of the Dutch NIP). This was needed to ensure that the CARD system can be appropriately applied and empirically tested within the Dutch setting.20

The exploration phase involved assessing existing vaccination practices within public health services, paying particular attention to the current approach of managing VRD. This phase began with a collective dialog between children, support persons, and HCPs. A paper describing this process is currently under review. The aim of this dialog was to explore which ideas, needs, possibilities, and current practices align with reducing VRD. This dialog revealed a strong emphasis on the foundations on which the CARD system is built, and the need for full system-level implementation of VRD-reducing techniques, to ensure that they benefit all user groups.21 To test the CARD system in the Netherlands, a detailed Dutch adaptation was needed. In the preparation phase, we used the ISPOR’s translation and cultural adaptation guideline to create a Dutch version of the CARD system. By applying the 10 steps of the ISPOR Guideline (Fig. 1), including forward and backward translation, cognitive debriefing with children and their support persons, and proofreading with HCPs, we created an adaptation of the CARD system that is suitable for piloting in the Dutch NIP.20,22

Adaptation process of the CARD method for integration into the Dutch NIP, based on Wild et al.20

We named this Dutch system FAVO, short for favorite in Dutch; the acronym is derived from the words “fijn” (Dutch for pleasant), “afleiden” (to distract), “vragen” (to ask), and “ontspannen” (to relax). Educational modules will be developed in 2026 for HCPs, with an emphasis on the core CARD principles and effective communication strategies.23 Also, communication materials will be developed to assist children and their support persons in preparing for vaccination with the assistance of FAVO. In this phase, resource planning is a pivotal component, addressing time allocation, staff capacity, and the availability of supporting materials, such as distraction tools and visual aids.

When the steps described above are completed, the implementation will include a small-scale trial to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of FAVO within a specific public health service that executes the Dutch NIP. The application of predefined evaluation criteria and the establishment of feedback mechanisms will be undertaken to ensure implementation fidelity.19 The data collection process encompasses patient-level outcomes, educational outcomes, and implementation outcomes. Comparative analyses of findings between FAVO and the Canadian studies have the potential to both inform the contextual adaptation of CARD further and justify CARD use in different settings and countries. Collaboration with other public health organizations and the Dutch National Institute for Public Health and the Environment (In Dutch: RIVM) will be essential to facilitate integration of FAVO as a multicomponent (complex) intervention.10

CONCLUSIONS

Transferring an evidence-based intervention developed in a different context to the Dutch setting requires a systematic and comprehensive implementation strategy. Such a strategy includes clearly defined objectives that align with the overarching goal of reducing VRD. Successful adaptation extends beyond linguistic translation; it necessitates cultural tailoring to ensure that the intervention resonates with local norms, values, and needs. This process should be guided by collaboration with international experts familiar with the intervention, as well as active engagement of local user groups, including children, support persons, and HCPs. Their involvement is essential to achieve both fidelity to the core components of the intervention and contextual appropriateness, thereby optimizing its effectiveness in reducing VRD in the Dutch vaccination system.
