  1. BACKGROUND
  1.1 Introduction
  This report gives an account of the Software Tools Ecosystem Project
  (STEP) during calendar year 2025 [1]. STEP seeks to ensure the
  stewardship and continued advancement of tools and supporting
  software for monitoring, analysis, and diagnosis of performance and
  behavior. Performance tools must proactively adapt to the challenges
  presented by emerging platform technologies such as new accelerators,
  storage devices, network technologies, and smart devices. Further
  challenges arise from changes to software stacks (e.g., vendor GPU
  monitoring libraries) and emerging application use cases, including
  advanced machine learning and workflow frameworks. STEP enables
  these tools to continue meeting the needs of application scientists and
  to provide a strong foundation for future Advanced Scientific Computing
  Research activities.
  STEP is a component of the Next Generation Scientific Software Technologies (NGSST) program and the
  Consortium for the Advancement of Scientific Software (CASS). The leadership team consists of three
  funded roles:
 • STEP Director: Terry Jones, Oak Ridge National Laboratory
 • STEP Deputy Director: Mike Jantz, University of Tennessee (since FY2026) 1
 • STEP Events & Workforce Expertise: Suzanne Parete-Koon, Oak Ridge National Laboratory

  1.2 Overall Project Goals and Opportunities
  STEP is a purpose-driven activity focused on achieving the following project goals:
 • Deliver software tools that achieve greater effectiveness and efficiency for applications running
 on a spectrum of computing systems within the DOE complex – from laptops to leadership
 class machines
 • Grow workforce expertise and shared experience
 • Gain leverage with partners who have similar requirements
 • Serve as a nexus of communication and information for tools stakeholders
  We seek to be an agile project that can work effectively across a broad range of funding levels. At present,
  we are pursuing strategies that accommodate very limited resources. We are prioritizing essential tasks,
  utilizing creative solutions, and maintaining clear communication with stakeholders to ensure we're
  delivering the most impactful results.

  If given the opportunity, our long term vision is to grow the scope of STEP by fostering engagement
  between community stakeholders and vendors to codesign hardware monitoring interfaces and software
  that affect tools, with the aim of enhancing efficient utilization of low-level performance monitoring and
  analysis capabilities. Moreover, we seek to support work through ongoing collaborative efforts by cross-
  cutting teams to address shared concerns across multiple tools and enhance long-term sustainability.
  We wish to support incubation of new technology as well as agile short-duration activities that have high
  potential to address rapidly evolving needs and opportunities. Finally, to the extent possible, we aim to
  support community-wide stewardship and advancement activities with broad impact on the tools
  ecosystem, e.g. standards, with the aim of enhancing performance portability and enabling efficient use
  of diverse hardware platforms.

  2. Software Package Status
  2.1 HPCToolkit

  HPCToolkit is an integrated suite of tools for measurement and analysis of application performance on
  computers ranging from multicore desktop systems to GPU-accelerated supercomputers running
  applications at exascale. On CPUs, HPCToolkit profiles and traces applications by using statistical
  sampling of timers and hardware performance counters. On GPUs, HPCToolkit leverages vendor
  software frameworks for monitoring and analysis of GPU operations and instruction-level monitoring
  within GPU kernels.
  Context
  HPCToolkit is dependent upon vendor provided interfaces for measuring code performance on GPUs.
  Over the last 12 months, the HPCToolkit project team has engaged with both AMD and Intel about bugs
  and missing capabilities in their measurement interfaces.
  The project team provided extensive feedback to AMD about pre-releases of their emerging ROCprofiler-
  SDK interface, which supports profiling and tracing of GPU operations, time-based PC sampling within
  GPU kernels, and support for using hardware performance counters to measure events as kernels
  execute. AMD improved ROCprofiler-SDK based on feedback from Rice, released version 1.0.0 in March
  2025, and version 1.1.0 in November 2025.
  The project team engaged Intel about their emerging PTI-GPU measurement substrate for profiling and
  tracing GPU operations and provided feedback that PTI-GPU’s initial support for synchronous callbacks
  when GPU operations are launched could not be used to supply correlation IDs needed to attribute costs
  of GPU operations to their invocations. In the fall of 2025, Intel added this missing capability.

  • Work with AMD to finalize ROCprofiler-SDK support for MI300. Implement HPCToolkit support for PC
 sampling on MI300, including collection and attribution of information about instruction stalls.

 AMD updated support for PC sampling in their March and November releases of Rocprofiler-sdk. The
 2025.1.0 release of HPCToolkit in December 2025 included new support for both software and
 hardware (stochastic) PC sampling. This is a significant milestone as it began with convincing AMD

 to add hardware support for PC sampling into their GPUs in 2019 and working with them over the
 years since on their tools SDK that provides access to this capability.

  • Work with Intel to resolve showstoppers that present post-mortem analysis of instruction-level
 measurements on Aurora.

 Rice has continued a dialog with Intel about the fact that their tool support in Level Zero supplies fully-
 linked GPU executables back to tools and that these executables often differ across MPI ranks. These
 differences occur because GPU executables are fully linked by each MPI rank and linking by a rank
 incorporates addresses that depend upon the state of its memory allocator. Intel has neither fixed the problem
 nor provided a suitable workaround. As a result, it is only practical to use HPCToolkit to perform PC
 sampling on small-scale executions on Aurora.

  • Deploy new HPCToolkit capabilities for instruction-level performance monitoring at scale on Aurora,
 Frontier, and El Capitan.

 The 2025.1.0 release of HPCToolkit includes new support for hardware PC sampling on the Intel Data
 Center GPU Max Series GPUs in Aurora, software support for PC sampling in the AMD MI250X GPUs
 in Frontier, and both hardware and software support for PC sampling in AMD MI300A GPUs in El
 Capitan.

  • Improve HPCviewer user interface to better support analysis of large-scale performance data.

 Performance analysis of HPCToolkit’s HPCviewer user interface identified that when analyzing
 performance data for complex applications, filtering of Calling Context Tree (CCT) nodes (e.g. to hide
 unwanted details of measurements within implementations of MPI primitives) was very sluggish.
 Replacing an O(n²) algorithm for matching CCT nodes during metric filtering with an O(n) algorithm,
 reduced the time required to apply filters on large calling context trees by as much as 10x.

  • Mentor developers in hackathons and trainings at DOE laboratories.

 The HPCToolkit project team mentored participants in the 2025 El Capitan hackathon at Lawrence
 Livermore National Laboratory in January 2025, the Argonne Leadership Computing Facility INCITE
 GPU Hackathon inMay 2025, the Argonne Training Program on Extreme-Scale Computing in August,
 2025, and the Argonne Leadership Computing Facility Hands-on High Performance Computing
 workshop in October, 2025. (Participation and travel for these hackathons were funded by LLNL and
 ANL and not STEP.)

  2.2 PAPI

  PAPI (Performance API) is a portable software library that provides a consistent interface for accessing
  hardware performance counters across diverse computing architectures. It addresses the long-standing
  challenge of vendor-specific and platform-dependent performance interfaces by enabling application
  developers and performance tools to collect meaningful performance data in a portable and reusable
  manner.
  Over time, PAPI has evolved beyond CPU counters to support GPUs, power and energy measurements,
  network interconnects, I/O activity, and software-defined events. By abstracting hardware heterogeneity,
  PAPI enables sustainable and portable performance monitoring across generations of HPC systems.
  Context
  PAPI serves as foundational middleware infrastructure for performance analysis in DOE HPC
  environments. It is widely used indirectly through performance tools such as TAU, Score-P, HPCToolkit,
  Caliper, CrayPat, and Vampir, and has been deployed for decades on leadership-class DOE systems.
  It enables:
 • Portable performance and energy measurement across architectures
 • Scientific application scalability and optimization
 • Architecture evaluation and co-design efforts
 • Performance portability across CPU and GPU generations
  Its role as enabling infrastructure allows scientific teams to focus on domain science while relying on a
  stable, vendor-neutral performance interface.

  The primary objectives for CY2025 focused on expanding architecture support, improving usability, and
  enhancing sustainability of the PAPI ecosystem.
  Key activities and accomplishments include:
 • Major Releases:
             o PAPI 7.2.0b1 (Aug 2024)
             o PAPI 7.2.0b2 (Mar 2025)
             o PAPI 7.2.0 (Jun 2025)

 • Expanded Hardware Support:
 o Full AMD GPU support via ROCprofiler-SDK (including sampling and multi-GPU)
 o Support for AMD Zen5 and latest Intel CPUs (Sapphire Rapids, Granite Rapids, etc.)
 o Support for heterogeneous CPU architectures (P-cores and E-cores)
 o Early ARM Neoverse V2 and RISC-V support

  2.3 Dyninst

  Dyninst (and its related toolkits) is a binary code and instrumentation facility. It provides the ability to
  analyze binary code (both control and dataflow analyses) instrument the code efficiently. Dyninst also
  allows for modification of the executable. It supports such use cases as performance profiling,
  debugging, testing, tracing, security auditing and hardening, and architectural simulation. Its primary
  CPU platforms are x86, ARM, and Power, with recent new support for RISC-V. Dyninst also provides code
  analysis for AMDGPU, with instrumentation for that platform in progress.
  Currently, Dyninst provides binary analysis support for HPCToolkit and (in some modes) analysis and
  instrumentation for TAU. It is also the foundation for the LLNL STAT scalable debugger (in production
  and demonstrated effective at 5 million cores). Cray/HPE tools, including the ATP (abnormal
  termination processing debugger) and differential debugger are based on our toolkits. In addition, the
  Red Hat SystemTap monitoring tool is based on Dyninst, as is the new AMD Omnitrace. Currently in
  flight is support for the AMD GPUs allowing all current Dyninst tools to move to this platform.
  Context
  The Dyninst project is focused on binary code analysis and instrumentation for CPU and GPU systems.
  CPU code analysis and instrumentation is well established, with the main goal of adapting to new CPU
  designs as they are released, especially those associated with the leadership class systems. GPU
  analysis and instrumentation for the AMD platforms is underway.
  The Dyninst project continues to work closely with AMD, but response time from AMD continues to be a
  challenge. Our effort with them to get a machine-readable instruction semantics definition for AMDGPU
  has the challenge of long delays within AMD. We have worked with them to define a format and give
  immediate feedback on intermediate versions, but their response time is quite slow.
  We are also working with AMD on basic features needed for tool support on their GPUs. Currently, this
  support is quite primitive. We continue to work with them to understand the undocumented (or limited
  documented) features that we need to support Dyninst.
  As for the other STEP projects, the Dyninst project continues to operate at a funding level below what is
  needed to both innovate and support the new technologies and hardware that continue to emerge and
  be deployed.

  • Finalized instruction machine-readable instruction syntax specification with AMD. We are now in a
 mode where we update Dyninst as new definitions are released.

  • Control flow analysis for SIMD primitives based on execution masks is in progress. This analysis

 instrument vector code (an innovation).

  2.4 TAU

  TAU is performance evaluation tools that supports parallel profiling and tracing on HPC systems. It
  requires no changes to the application source code, build system, or binary. It also supports
  instrumentation using a plugin for the LLVM compiler, source instrumentation using the Program
  Database Toolkit (PDT), and binary rewriting of application binaries and DSOs using DyninstAPI. It
  supports automatic instrumentation for parallel programs written in C++, C, Fortran, Python, Julia, Java,
  Chapel, and UPC. Instrumentation can also be inserted using third party timing libraries such as GPTL,
  CAMTIMERS, PETSc, Perfstubs, Caliper, PhiProf, ROCTx, NVTx, and Score-P are supported. It supports
  instrumentation of OpenMP using OMPT including target offload directives, ROCm using ROCprofiler-
  SDK, CUDA using CUPTI, Kokkos, OpenCL, Intel Level Zero, and Python. TAU interfaces with PAPI to

  2.5 Darshan

  Darshan is a lightweight I/O characterization tool that captures concise summaries of I/O behavior for HPC
  applications. It provides detailed insights into application usage across various layers of the I/O stack.
  Darshan is popular among HPC application and I/O library developers, system administrators, and other I/O
  practitioners as it’s able to seamlessly quantify I/O characteristics of their jobs.

  Context

  To provide comprehensive understanding of HPC I/O behavior, tools like Darshan must capture details
  about I/O workloads across the entire storage stack (including high-level libraries, low-level system
  interfaces, and file system-specific interfaces) and summarize/present this information to users. The
  focus of this year's work has been on improving sustainability and documentation practices, and the
  development of a methodology for publication of Darshan logs from production applications. These
  developments are crucial as they facilitate understanding application I/O behavior to inform storage
  system research and optimization strategies.

  Develop processes to anonymize/publish Darshan log data captured in production for sharing with the
  broader HPC I/O research community, modernize and improve Darshan's documentation and release
  processes, and productize Darshan mechanisms for instrumenting I/O workloads at thread-level
  granularity. We will work with the STEP leadership to refine our metrics and identify methods to clearly
  demonstrate how our efforts lead to measurable increases.

  3. Aggregated Project Status
  3.1 Project Impact on HPC Community
  Our expansive vision is to grow the scope of STEP by fostering engagement between community
  stakeholders and vendors to codesign hardware monitoring interfaces and software that affect tools,
  with the aim of enhancing efficient utilization of low-level performance monitoring and analysis
  capabilities. Moreover, we seek to support work through ongoing collaborative efforts by cross-cutting
  teams to address shared concerns across multiple tools and enhance long-term sustainability. We also
  wish to support the incubation of new technology, as well as agile, short-duration activities with high
  potential to address rapidly evolving needs and opportunities. Finally, to the extent possible, we aim to
  support community-wide stewardship and advancement activities with broad impact on the tools
  ecosystem, e.g. standards, with the goal of enhancing performance portability and enabling efficient use
  of diverse hardware platforms.
  Given the constant rate of technical advances in high-performance computers, our topic area demands
  constant interactions with both vendors and users of leadership-class machines. While such technology
  tracking presents significant challenges, it also offers important opportunities -- especially for
  engagement. Given our deep understanding of the latest hardware and system-level software, we can be
  an asset to many who benefit from this expertise. Working together, we aim to develop a proactive stance
  toward the rapidly evolving hardware landscape.
  One example is the HPCToolkit collaboration with the Ellora Project. The Ellora project is an LLNL-led
  project that leverages AI for productive and trustworthy HPC software. The sophisticated measurement
  and analysis capabilities of HPCToolkit are being used to teach Large Language Models (LLMs) how to
  rewrite HPC software for improved performance and power efficiency.
  At the consortium level, STEP played a key role in the formulation of the Consortium for the Advancement
  of Scientific Software. Terry Jones is a founding member of the steering committee, and Mike Jantz is
  active in CASS as well. STEP also leads two CASS activities (including the vendor forum) and participates

 4. Report Conclusion
 The Software Tools Ecosystem Project (STEP) is a collaborative initiative funded by the U.S. Department of
 Energy to maintain and advance critical software for high-performance computing. This annual report presents the
 progress of five core performance tools—HPCToolkit, PAPI, Dyninst, TAU, and Darshan—which enable the
 monitoring, analysis, and diagnosis of complex scientific applications. The project focuses on adapting these tools
 to modern GPU-accelerated architectures and emerging AI/ML workloads while fostering a skilled workforce. By
 coordinating between national laboratories, universities, and industry partners like NVIDIA and AMD, STEP ensures
 that researchers can optimize code efficiency across exascale supercomputers. The report outlines significant
 technical milestones, such as improved hardware counter support and automated quality assurance, alongside
 strategic goals for long-term software sustainability. Despite its successes, the report highlights that stable
 funding and early access to hardware remain vital for keeping pace with rapidly evolving technology.
