Introduction
The most signiﬁcant advantage of Distributed Fiber Optic Sensing (DFOS) is its ability to leverage
ordinary telecommunications ﬁber optic cables, turning kilometers of ﬁber commonly used for data
communications into thousands of virtual vibrational sensors. The details in ﬁelding and operation
are left for future discussions, but the capability exists, easy to implement, and ﬁber access points
exist throughout the world. Distributed Acoustic Sensing (DAS) is one DFOS technology that
records a strain, and from rock mechanics, shear strain is proportional to particle velocity divided
by shear wave speed. Given this, the nuclear explosion monitoring community could
opportunistically utilize DAS on existing telecom ﬁber and analyze this data for location,
discrimination, and yield estimation as if DAS data were seismometer data (e.g., Mellors et al, 2021;
Gok et al., 2024). The two main limiting factors with DAS are noise levels and emplacement
conditions (or ground-to-cable coupling) that are typically not encountered in non-opportunistic
and costly seismic instrumentation sites. In this report, we will report on:

 (1) progress of data analysis with the Fiber Utilization Sensing Experiment (FUSE) dataset in
 making signal detections for feature extraction and machine learning/deep learning
 algorithm training datasets,
 (2) leveraging earthquake data collected on dark and installed ﬁbers at RNCTEC to study
 the e[ect of ground to cable coupling from 3 emplacement methods,
 (3) ﬁnally, report on PE-1B instrumentation support with DAS ﬁber deployment along P-
 tunnel road performed under add-on CR 252.

Background
The DAS interrogator unit (IU) is the size of desktop computer that repeatedly launches pulses of
light using a laser down a connected ﬁber optic cable. The IU then measures the changes in the
backscatter of light from the laser pulses using a photo detector. The backscatter is caused by
natural imperfections in the glass ﬁber. Vibrations traveling through the earth stretch and compress
the buried ﬁber changing the positions of these scattering points. The interrogator then converts
these changes of the backscatter between 2 points along the ﬁber into units of strain at a position
along the ﬁber based on the time of ﬂight of light. DAS turns ordinary ﬁber-optic telecommunication
cables into thousands of virtual seismic sensors, sensitive to sub nanometer scale strains, sensing
vibrations from vehicles, machinery, and even structural changes in roads, tunnels, and bridges as
well as earthquake or explosion generated ground motions. The beneﬁt from DAS over traditional
seismic sensors comes from densely recording the entire waveﬁeld solving spatial aliasing
common with sparsely positioned single point seismometers.

Fiber Utilization Sensing Experiment (FUSE)
Introduction
The Fiber Utilization Sensing Experiment (FUSE) was a demonstration of Distributed Acoustic
Sensing (DAS) technology on telecommunications ﬁber in a major urban corridor. Lawrence
Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL), Optical Fiber Communications Network (OFCnet), Viavi,
Stanford University, and Luna Innovations interrogated approximately 80 km of unused
telecommunications ﬁber from Moscone Center in San Francisco to Sunnyvale, CA. We recorded
50TB for 40 days continuously between February 20 to April 3, 2025. This dataset is rich in
vibrational signals from a complex noisy environment caused by trains, cars, buses, rainfall, and
earthquakes. Table 1 provides details of the optical recording parameters. We recorded using a
gauge length of 100 m and then for the second half of the experiment recorded at 50 m to improve
resolution but observed higher noise levels toward the end of the ﬁber.

 Technical Report 4

An hour of DAS data sampled at 1kHz across 8000 channels takes about ~200 gigabytes to store;
therefore, loading, simple processing, and visualization is beyond the memory resources of most
PC and even HPC systems. Reviewing the data quality, requires alternative methods like chunking
in time and space but gives too limited view of the data. We use a rolling window time-domain root-
mean-square to process raw data, remapping it into an expanded 24-hour view. We computed
RMS amplitudes in 2-minute windows, shifted by 1-minute (overlap). The quietest background
levels occur during nighttime hours, between 08:00 UTC (midnight local time) and 14:00 UTC (6:00
AM local time). Reducing the GL from 100 m to 50 m increased noise levels, particularly along the
final 60 km of the cable due to a 0.4 dB/km optical loss inherent to single-mode fiber at a 1550 nm
wavelength. This is evidenced by random optical speckle noise appearing beyond channel 7000 in
the 50 m GL data, whereas these noise features remain relatively absent in the 100 m GL baseline
as previously mentioned.

Geolocating FUSE ﬁber
Modeling, inversion, and interpretation of DAS data requires mapping of channel numbers to real
world geographic coordinates. Tap-testing has been a reliable method of ﬁber geolocation using
small, controlled experiments to “tap” on the ﬁber optic cable, which is the passive sensor, and
record the time and location of each tap. Then an analyst identiﬁes these signals in the DAS data as
spikes of energy associating the time and channel number to the surveyed location. Telecom
companies do not reveal the location of their ﬁber optic cables because, under presidential EO
13010 (1996), are categorized as critical infrastructure. DAS is a very sensitive technology and is
often used to help telecom industry rediscover lost information about ﬁber cable locations. This
makes tap-testing a necessary step before any interpretation of the data can be made.

DAS is a very sensitive and simply touching an exposed cable can impart enough strain to generate
a strong spike, but for buried telecom ﬁber optic cables, hitting manhole covers with a hammer is
also e[ective in generating DAS signals. However, with 80 km of ﬁber, using dynamic loading of a
vehicle with GPS is more e[ective and less time-consuming (e.g., Biondi et al. 2022). There are
limitations to this approach, one is that the vehicle needs to be moving at a high rate of speed
(approximately > 20 to 30 mph), because stopped or slow-moving vehicles do not generate
signiﬁcant ground strain loads. The other limit is conﬂicting tra[ic which can confuse the signal
association process, without any additional visual proof. Therefore, driving at night is preferred
when there are fewer cars on the roadways.

We drove a medium-size pickup truck ﬁtted with GPS Dashcam to conduct a controlled tap-testing
experiment. We also observed the signals streaming from the DAS interrogator on a tablet device
using a cellular internet connection. This allowed us to ﬁnd ﬁber and document, DAS signals from
our vehicle, other passing vehicles in both directions, and manhole covers on the roadway, giving us
ground-truth information. Articulated buses and big-rigs (18-wheelers or semitrucks) resulted in
higher amplitude and longer duration signals relative to passenger cars. Two cars traveling < 50-100

The manhole covers in the roadway associated with the strength of DAS signals provided some

trenches or conduits connected by underground vaults accessible from the surface by manholes.
In multilane roadways, DAS signals are strongest when driving directly over the ﬁber and driving 1 or
2 lanes away results in a signiﬁcant drop in signal displayed in real-time waterfall plots. When
driving in opposite direction for divided roads these signals completely disappear. To see these
signals away from the ﬁber required reprocessing the raw data, zooming or clipping down to see the
faint signals, especially in times of heavy cross tra[ic. Cars crossing perpendicular the roadway
with the ﬁber often disappeared within about 100 m. Slack loops are commonly used by utilities to
allow for further expansion or excess to repair damage to cables and consists of 10’s to 100’s
meters of excess cable coiled in underground vaults. These loops are poorly coupled to the ground
and show up as faint signals with no moveout like “gaps” in the DAS waterfall plots. The sight of
manhole covers on the roadway was often associated with both slack loops and/or transfers of
cables from one side of the roadway to another.

FUSE detection and feature extraction
In the goal of developing and training an AI/ML model (i.e., Convolutional Autoencoder) for
classifying DAS signals, we ﬁrst perform data preprocessing and exploratory data analysis. The
speciﬁc steps involve detection of signals, extracting features, and encoding features with labels
based on ground-truth information (e.g., class labels: car, truck, bus, train, earthquake).

We used DASCore Python libraries (Chambers et al., 2024) to compute channel detections using
energy ratios commonly known as short term average and long-term average ratios (STA/LTA).
Thirty-minute segments were loaded over a stride of 1000 channels (~10 km distance), converted to
single channel streams, and processed using Obspy Python libraries (Beyreuther et al., 2010). We
use Linux WS networked to a 60 TB RAID-5 mass data storage server. We pad each 30-minunte time
interval with 60-seconds of data to avoid edge e[ects, decimate the data by factor or 4 (sampling
rates decimated from 1000 Hz to 250 Hz), and ﬁnally bandpass ﬁlter between 1 and 80 Hz. The STA
window is 2 sec, and the LTA window is 10 sec, typical for local to regional detection processing,
with a max time length of 5 sec (triggering turns o[). The triggering threshold was 2. For ~8000
channels, it takes about 1 hour to process 1 hour of data. Figure 4A shows a density image of
776,397 detections over just 30 mins during the weekday evening commute, while Fig 4B shows a
time interval at 2 am with about half the number of detections. These two-time intervals were
selected because of an earthquake that are observed as horizontal features in both A and B.

NNSS DAS Deployments
RNCTEC telecom and installed ﬁbers
We deployed a DAS interrogator at the Radiological/Nuclear Countermeasures Test and Evaluation
Complex (RNCTEC) within NNSS to collect data on 1 km length telecom ﬁber and 1.5 km of
armored ﬁber that was shallowly buried using a plow method with some surface exposed sections.
The main purpose of the data collected was for facility monitoring. In the process of recording
background noise levels over 1 week prior to the experiment, we also recorded several
earthquakes, making this one of the ﬁrst examples of recording earthquakes using DAS on telecom
ﬁbers at NNSS. We have permission from the NA-22 Federal Program Manager, to leverage this
dataset under LYNM Sensing Venture, as a dual purpose for a natural lab to examine DAS cable to
ground coupling from the 3 di[erent emplacement conditions.

During the time of recording background signals, we recorded 7 earthquakes and potentially a
mining blast (Fig 6A). These earthquakes ranged in epicenter distance from ~30 km to 380 km and
ranged in size between magnitude from 2.5 to 3.8. The mining blast was near Baker, CA, 168 km
away with an equivalent earthquake magnitude of 1.8. We recorded on two of the same
interrogators (LLNL purchased, and LANL rented) located in the training trailer outside of the
complex. The 2 identical models of the interrogators were set with the same optical settings, i.e.,
ping-rate 1000 Hz decimated to a sampling rate of 500Hz, 1 m spatial sampling, and 10m GL. Fiber
1 was telecom ﬁber optic cable, mostly aerial on 12 poles; however, a small section was under the
pavement (ﬁber-1 ch 900 - black area in Fig 6B). We located Fiber 1 channel 900 by hitting a rock on
a manhole cover during recording and associated the spike in strain to this channel. We assume
that the ﬁber at this location was professionally installed (known as Outside Plant (OSP)) and
construction codes typically require cables buried > 1m and grouted in conduit (metal pipe) like
those exposed near the base of the poles at ch 740. Fiber-2 was a 4-strand armored cable, installed
by NNSS using an ATV rigged with a cable plow. This buried the length of 1.5 km cable about 1 ft
deep in just under a few hours. Fiber-2 channel 8000 was in this buried section. Fiber-2 channel
1200 was surface exposed due to environmental concerns.

Physics Experiment 1B P-tunnel road DAS and seismometers
We assisted LANL’s DAS deployment under Physics Experiment 1-B Change Request 252 mainly in
the capacity of providing an interrogator and real-time remote communications using an ATT
cellular modem. The interrogator is installed in trailer 12-B229328 “glass house” on the P-tunnel
apron. The LANL 4-strand armored cable was prepared and emplaced along P-tunnel road over 5
km. The interrogator was deployed at the time of this report; however, we are pleased to note that
the deployment went well, and currently recording continuously as of 9/3/2026 after some initial
power outages on the apron due to power upgrades. Prior to the outage we were able to record a
magnitude 1.7 earthquake 76 km from the apron. Work continues to bury noisy sections of the
cable to protect from wind noise. We also installed 4 broadband seismometers alongside the DAS
ﬁber. We were able to install these spaced in 1 km increments along the cable to directly compare
with DAS signals.

Conclusions
Leveraging 10-100’s km of preexisting telecom ﬁber optic cables takes full advantage of the beneﬁts
from distributed acoustic sensing technology reducing the cost of installing cables. However, we
see with the FUSE dataset that the coupling can greatly vary across the ﬁbers due to slack loops in
underground vaults and di[erences due to emplacement in either direct bury versus grouted

conduit and buried versus aerial ﬁbers. Additionally, there is the requirement to geolocate the ﬁber
since it is considered critical infrastructure. Overall, telecom ﬁber is still better than installing ﬁbers
using plow method, or surface exposed ﬁbers.
