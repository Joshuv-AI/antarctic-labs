 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 Under the proposed effort in partnership with Hyundai Motor Company (HMC), the National
 Laboratory of the Rockies’(NLR) will cooperate with HMC to develop mathematical models for
 battery cells and modules for simulating abuse response in batteries subject to the type of
 mechanical crushing that can occur in a full motor vehicle crash.

 CRADA benefit to DOE, Participant, and US Taxpayer:

 • Assists laboratory in achieving programmatic scope.
 • Enhances the laboratory’s core competencies.
 • Enhances U.S. competitiveness by utilizing DOE developed intellectual property and/or
 capabilities.

 Summary of Research Results:

 WP1. Construct the simulation model (LS-DYNA) for cell level test and compare the test
 results

 Description of the material cards (LS-DYNA MAT) of the cell components. Description of
 failure model of the cell components. Description of element size and type dependence of cell
 components. Identify battery modeling approach.

 Owing to the complexity of coupling mechanical and electrical solvers for finite element analysis
 (FEA) of mechanical abuse of a battery, the simulation should be performed by incorporating a
 local short circuit model activated based on the status of battery elements. However, the
 approach should also consider various other conditions of the battery simulated, such as the
 initial state of charge (SOC), for precise simulations of the internal short. This study proposes an
 approach developed with a mechanical model and single-particle battery model, along with a
 reaction kinetics model for thermal abuse using LS-DYNA. Several key parameters were
 selected, such as the activation criteria, kinetic abuse and element integration points during the
 internal short circuit induced by the indenter. The impact of these key parameters in the FEA on
 thermal runaway response for various initial SOCs from 25 to 100 % were analyzed. The FEA
 results obtained were compared against experimental results with various parameters such as
 activation criterion of internal short, kinetic abuse based on accelerating rate calorimetry (ARC)
 data. Finally, the root causes of the discrepancy between the FEA and experimental results are
 discussed regarding integration point what solver can make difference during calculation.

 Fig 1: Comparison of numerically predicted and experimental voltage-time responses for two
 activation criteria (left graph), contour of each criterion before short circuit activation (middle
 picture) current density contour (right picture) after short circuit activation using stress (right up)
 and strain (right down) criteria, and comparison of voltage-time responses between FEA and
 experiments using strain criteria

 An activation criterion case study was conducted to determine whether the criterion is
 appropriate for an SP model. Fig. 1 shows the voltage drop curves for the two activation criterion
 candidates when the initial SOC was 100. The stress and strain for the criterion were obtained
 from the FEA when the indenter deformed the same displacement for the internal short circuit in
 the experiment, as shown in Fig. 1. The curve of Fig. 1 shows that the voltage dropped sharply
 when strain was used as the activation criterion.

 This study proposed and validated a new mechanical–electrochemical–thermal multiphysics
 model to describe the internal short of a high-capacity cell in FEA. This study simulated the
 thermal runaway of a high-capacity cell by adding kinetic abuse using a sub-chemical model to
 the mechanical–electric coupling model. This study mainly focused on the adoption of kinetic
 abuse obtained from the ARC test and its use of the degree of conversion, α_i, between thermal
 steps. In addition to the kinetic abuse, additional parameters, such as activation criteria and
 integration point setting, were proposed to describe the internal short circuit in the battery. The
 simulation results were validated with experiments. The key results of this study are as follows:

 (1) The activation criteria were selected between similar levels of stress and strain such that the
 best parameter which could describe the short experiment was selected. Effective strain and
 effective stress were used for the activation criterion that adds a virtual short circuit in a cell.
 Owing to its highly compressive characteristics of the material, the number of elements in the
 activation criterion increased more rapidly than those in the stress. This increased the current
 rapidly, which is similar to the experimental results.

 3

 (2) The kinetic abuse model in the thermal solver, the main concept of this study, was studied.
 When the electrochemical heat was high owing to the high SOC, it induced an additional
 chemical reaction. Chemical reaction is the reaction of the SEI film in the battery. This model
 uses the virtual degree of conversion, in the thermal solver rather than make the SEI film a mesh,
 so the computation efficiency is significantly improved comparing with conventional
 approaches. Kinetic abuse occurs using the electrochemical heat produced during voltage
 change. Moreover, an increase in temperature can be depicted in the FEA with kinetic abuse
 parameters from the ARC test. The well-matched results show that the thermal runaway heat
 source from the internal short due to deformation was the same as the battery pouch cell self-heat
 due to the SEI film.

 (3) The integration point must be determined because the LS-DYNA calculates kinetic abuse at
 the integration point. Irregular thermal propagation and an inverted vertical relationship between
 TC1 and TC2 were observed with a reduced integrated point. This tendency was opposite to the
 experimental results. This is because the heat from the reduced integration translates to a higher
 heat source than that from a fully integrated one. This is because the reduced integration element
 dealt with the mean temperature at each node. The differences between the reduced and fully
 integrated systems were significant when the initial SOC was 100, but the difference decreased
 as the SOC decreased.

 WP2: Construct the simulation model (LS- DYNA) for module level test

 Use cell-level test results (or simulation results from WP2) as input to build finite element
 modeling techniques for modules for application in pre-existing full vehicle crash simulation
 models.

 Figure 3. Coupled Multiphysics response upon impact simulation (a) current density in positive
 and negative current collectors (b) Voltage prediction from multiple internal short circuit
 instantiations

 Developed mechanical model has been extended with methods to simulate internal and external
 short circuits in battery modules and the simulations were run with coupled mechanical,
 electrochemical, thermal models. Results indicate voltage starts quickly tapering when the first
 element shorts at around 1.2 ms. Voltage drops much faster when the second location shorts with
 a larger contact area. Relatively uniform state of charge is observed during short circuit form
 impact. All cells experience a soft short whereas current desist profiles are noticeable only in 4
 cells. Few milliseconds during the impact do not provide enough time for thermal propagation.

 Higher indenter radius initiated short faster. Dependence of voltage on short circuit resistance is
 as expected and needs to be calibrated to the experiments. Both short circuit initiation strain and
 short circuit resistance are important to predict the voltage response correctly. Model predicts
 that no short circuit will occur in orientation 2 and 3 despite having a higher peak force in the
 mechanical response. This is because jellyroll experiences very low strain as most of the stress is
 taken by the structural components of the modules. It is necessary to change orientation of
 modules for the second case or increase the mass of indenter for a short circuit to occur.

 5

 Figure 4: Internal short circuit thermal runaway model results compared with experimental data

 Calibrating both voltage and temperature data is extremely difficult based on the initial
 comparison with models. Model has been improved to include thermally initiated short circuits.
 This results in a more realistic voltage drop like experimental observations along with thermal
 propagation results. Comparison of temperature is shown in the figure. Damaged area is quite
 large in the Through-thickness tests causing significant erosion and distortion.

 Overall, module level models with a homogenized jellyroll assumption were completed. Initial
 velocity of impact is calculated based on drop height and the rigid indenter is gravity loaded to
 fall onto the module under specified orientations. The battery module deformation and force
 response is calculated the model using previously obtained material properties from cell level
 testing. Mechanical only models for RS-RVE can be extended to include electro-chemical and
 thermal models to better predict the buckling response of the cells. In-plane loading leads to
 severe buckling in cells which is challenging to capture using a homogenized model.

 There is considerable interest in developing computer aided engineering (CAE) tools to study the
 response of the batteries. With gasoline-powered conventional vehicles, there are well
 established CAE tools that simulate various crash response scenarios. However, this knowledge
 base is still being developed for electric vehicles. Developing models for battery powered
 vehicles is complicated: besides mechanical and thermal response, we should also consider
 chemical reactions, high-voltage implications as well as varied length scales and response times
 for the different physical phenomena. ‘Sandwich’ level tests for anisotropic modeling were
 performed in Tension and Compression conditions along with individual components and cell
 level tests. The cell level mechanical abuse models were correlated with testing data and
 calibrate the results.

 Compression curve of sandwich compared against individual components. Tests are very
 repeatable with precise response. The composite curve obtained from sandwich tests is slightly
 stiffer than that obtained from cell level tests. Tension Test is highly stochastic with a wide
 window for each failure. The regions of failure match the component tests but with increased
 variance in sandwich level tests. The data is useful for more accurate modeling of failure. These
 tests provide insights into the anisotropic response of effective jellyroll within pouch format
 lithium-ion batteries.

 Figure 6. (a) Mesh and input setup for the drop test simulation model for modules (b) strain
 distribution results obtained upon impact in multiple orientations.

 Deformation predicted by using homogenized model shows few eroded elements. Short circuit
 criterion of effective-stress and element-erosion based are used. Initial simulations indicate that
 the peak forces on the module will be less than 10 kN in all orientations of impact. From
 previous experiments, this can mean that the probability of short circuits is low. This suggests
 increasing mass required in experimental matrix. Using a layered model is possible with minimal
 modification. This allows for a more realistic buckling mode and prevents overprediction of
 force.

 WP6. NLR will integrate simultaneous coupling subroutines into LS-DYNA

 NLR will work with the LS-DYNA team to integrate NLR subroutines for the electrochemical-
 thermal simulations on to LS-DYNA software platform. This task requires NLR’s coordination
 with the LS-DYNA team, facilitated by HMC. NLR will support integration on to LS-DYNA to
 verify functionality.

 The schematic below shows how user-defined simultaneous coupling subroutines were
 integrated into LS-DYNA. The numerical model used for simulation consists of various sub-
 models that solve corresponding physical processes. Each cell is modeled as a homogenized
 continuum using solid hexahedral elements. For mechanics, an Isotropic Crushable Foam model
 (MAT_063 on LS-DYNA) is used for the cells. This model has been used by previous studies for
 modeling battery deformation and can accurately predict compression behavior. For the purpose
 of this study, a single particle electrochemical model was selected and implemented using user-
 defined subroutines within LS-DYNA’s BatMac framework. Implementation details regarding
 coupling with LS-DYNA EM solver and comparison of electrical-thermal responses between
 electrochemical models have been discussed in a previous paper.
