Optimization of Analytical Performances and Routine Laboratory Implementation of NPM1 Mutation Detection by Digital PCR in the Diagnosis and Monitoring of NPM1 ‐Mutated Acute Myeloid Leukemias
Authors: Chloé Ferrari, Brice Coulleray, Pauline Kerneves, Carole Geara, Dina Naguib, Guénaelle Levallet, Elizabeth Battersby, Sylvain Chantepie, Yohann Repessé, Coralie Nectoux, Elsa Maitre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionQuantification and monitoring of NPM1 mutations represent a key tool in the management of acute myeloid leukemia (AML), offering strong prognostic value and serving as a marker of minimal residual disease (MRD). Digital PCR (dPCR) provides absolute quantification with high sensitivity and specificity, offering advantages over conventional qPCR‐based methods, particularly for rare or atypical NPM1 mutations.MethodsWe adapted and evaluated a digital PCR strategy including both monoplex and multiplex assays targeting the most common NPM1 mutations (types A, B, D) as well as rare variants. A touch‐down PCR protocol was optimized to harmonize the dPCR protocol and enhance specificity. Assay performance was evaluated in terms of limit of detection (LoD), limit of quantification (LoQ), linearity, and specificity, and compared to quantitative PCR (qPCR).ResultsThe multiplex dPCR assay allowed detection of all clinically relevant NPM1 mutations with an LoD of 0.01%, while monoplex type A, B and D assays reached 0.001% for type A, B and D. The method showed strong linearity (R 2 > 0.99 for all targets) and high concordance with qPCR (R2 = 0.9967). In patient samples, dPCR demonstrated improved sensitivity over qPCR, detecting low‐level NPM1 mutations in samples previously negative by qPCR, and enabled early relapse detection.ConclusionThe approach enabled early relapse prediction in selected patients and improved molecular follow‐up, even for rare NPM1 mutations not covered by standard qPCR panels. It simplifies laboratory routine and improves MRD assessment, according to current ELN guidelines and the growing need for personalized molecular monitoring in AML.Keywords: acute myeloid leukemia, digital PCR, minimal residual disease, NPM1, personalized monitoring, rare mutations

Introduction

NPM1‐mutated acute myeloid leukemia (AML) accounts for approximately 30% of all AML cases [1, 2]. Isolated NPM1 mutation is considered a favorable prognostic marker according to the European LeukemiaNet (ELN) [3] and defines a distinct entity since the 2022 WHO classification requiring only 10% bone marrow blasts at diagnosis [4]. More than 50 different NPM1 mutations have been identified, with types A, B, and D being the most common [5, 6]. Each involves a four‐base pair insertion at the same position: Type A (c.860_863dup), Type B (c.863_864insCATG), and Type D (c.863_864insCCTG). Together, these account for approximately 95% of all NPM1 mutations [7]. The remaining 5% are classified as “rare” mutations [8], most of which also involve four‐base pair insertions at the same position (c.863_864insXXXX), representing 2%–3% of all cases [9].

To evaluate the measurable residual disease (MRD), the ELN recommends targeted monitoring using PCR‐based techniques whenever possible, specifically quantitative PCR (qPCR) or digital PCR (dPCR), focusing on non‐germline, stable, and clonal mutations [3]. A minimum limit of detection (LoD) of 10−3 is recommended [10]. Minimal residual disease (MRD) monitoring is therefore performed on the NPM1 mutation whenever possible. Prior to the implementation of digital PCR, RT‐qPCR was used at Caen University Hospital, targeting only A, B, and D mutations, with a theoretical sensitivity of 10−5 (0.001%) [11, 12]. This approach covered approximately 95% of NPM1 mutations.

Digital PCR offers several advantages. Its sensitivity is enhanced through sample partitioning [13, 14, 15], allowing the detection of rare events. It provides absolute quantification [14, 15], without the need for a calibration curve, with results corrected according to Poisson statistics [16]. Additionally, it is easy to implement and has a reduced hands‐on time. Digital PCR enables monitoring of A, B, and D mutations using three separate monoplex PCR assays. Mencia‐Trinchant et al. published an elegant approach with a multiplex digital PCR approach for MRD monitoring [17]. The “multiplex” primer targets a conserved four‐base pair motif located at the same position as the major NPM1 mutations. Its degenerate sequence also enables the detection of rare NPM1 mutations, provided they involve a four‐base insertion at codon c.863. Taken together, the monoplex and multiplex primers cover approximately 98% of known NPM1 mutations.

Currently, only next‐generation sequencing (NGS)‐based MRD approaches allow universal NPM1 mutation monitoring. However, this technology is technically demanding, time‐consuming, and challenging to implement at sensitivity thresholds of 10−3 to 10−4.

For these reasons, and to streamline the laboratory workflow, the aim of this study was to optimize and implement the digital PCR method by harmonizing the monoplex and multiplex PCR protocols, and to compare their performance to RT‐qPCR.

Materials and Methods

Patients Samples and Control Materials

All patients and healthy donors were adults, and were recruited from Caen University Hospital. All biological samples used in this study were collected as part of routine clinical care, with no additional sampling performed for research purposes. In accordance with French regulation and institutional policy, patients could express their opposition to the possible reuse of their biological samples (non‐opposition procedure). A total of 112 distinct patient samples and 28 negative controls from 7 different pools were used. Samples were obtained from peripheral blood or bone marrow aspiration. RNA extraction was performed using the Maxwell device (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin, USA) with the Maxwell RSC simplyRNA Cells kit (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin, USA) according to the manufacturer's specifications. RNA quality was deemed satisfactory with concentration greater than 30 ng/μL, and optical density (OD) ratios of 260/280 ≥ 1.6 and 260/230 ≥ 1.5. cDNA synthesis was performed using SuperScript Reverse Transcriptase II (SSII, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, USA) according to EAC (Europe Against Cancer) protocol [18].

The mutations considered were types A, B, and D, as well as all four‐base pair insertions (c.863_864insXXXX).

Plasmids (Quiagen) were also used during method validation to ensure a known NPM1 positivity rate, independent of sensitivity and potential technical issues with RT‐qPCR.

qPCR

qPCR NPM1 type A B D, were performed on Light‐Cycler 480 (Roche, Bâle, Suisse) as previously described [17, 19].

Instruments

The Bio‐Rad (Hercules, California, USA) QX200 Droplet Digital PCR System was used. Droplet generation was performed using the Automatic Droplet Generator (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, California, USA). After emulsion generation, PCR was carried out on the C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, California, USA). Fluorescence reading was performed using the QX200 Droplet Reader (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, California, USA). Data analysis was performed using QXManager software (version 2.2, Bio‐Rad, Hercules, California, USA). PCR replicates were manually merged to calculate total copy numbers and the (NPM1/ABL1)*100 ratio.

We used probes and primers previously published [17, 19] (Table 1) synthesized by ThermoFisher (Waltham, Massachusetts, USA).

PCR And Program Optimization

The dPCR reactions were performed using 1X ddPCR Supermix for Probes (No dUTP, Bio‐Rad, Hercules, California, USA) 500 nM of primer and 250 nM of probe were used for each point. To achieve a sensitivity of 0.001% (NPM1/ABL1 ratio*100), at least 100 000 copies of ABL1 are required [21]. Each sample was analyzed in duplicate, that is, in two wells, with at least 50 000 copies of ABL1 per well. For monitoring, 150 ng of cDNA per well was used, while at diagnosis, 6 ng was used to avoid droplet saturation.

After optimization, a modified version of the Touchdown PCR was developed [22, 23] using a Veriti thermocycler (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, California, USA), incorporating two annealing and elongation temperatures. The optimal temperature program is summarized (Table 1).

Limits of Blank, Detection, Quantification, and Linearity

The limit of blank (LoB) was measured using n = 7 negative controls in duplicate, from 7 different pools for each primer. The limits of detection (LoD) and quantification (LoQ) were calculated using the formulas =LoB+3σand LoQ=LoB+10σ [15].

Results

Limits of Blank, Detection, Quantification, and Linearity

The limit of detection (LoD), determined using background levels from n = 7 normal sample, was 0,001% for types A, B and D, and 0.01% for the multiplex assays. The limit of quantification (LoQ) was 0.004% for monoplex type A, B D, and 0.035% for the multiplex assay. On average, 0.15 copies of NPM1 were detected for type A and D [min 0—max 1.08]; 0 for type B, and 1.72 copies for the multiplex assay [min 0—max 4.40] (Figure 1A).

The sensitivity of the method is therefore 10−5 for the monoplex assay and 10−4 for the multiplex assay, in accordance with the ELN recommendations [10].

The linearity was measured using plasmids mixture of NPM1 and ABL1. Serial dilutions were prepared at the following concentrations: 0%, 0.001%, 0.01%, 0.1%, 1%, 10%, 100%. The resulting R2 values were: NPM1A: R 2 = 0.9974; NPM1B: R 2 = 1; NPM1D: R 2 = 0.9999; NPM1 Multiplex: R 2 = 0.9997. (Figure 1B).

The comparison of patient samples was conducted for all four mutation types at three ratios levels (low, medium, and high). The few discrepancies observed occurred at very high values and had no clinical impact, as further discussed in the Discussion section. The obtained R 2 values were as follows: Type A = 0.9958, Type B = 0.9971, D = 0.9249, and Multiplex = 0.9516. (Figure 2A).

Monoplex and Multiplex Specificity

The comparison between the multiplex PCR and each monoplex PCR was performed on 30 samples. No clinically relevant discrepancies were observed, except at very high values, which had no clinical impact, as further discussed in the Discussion section. It is also important to consider the difference in LoQ between the monoplex and multiplex assays. The comparison yielded an R 2 value of 0.9967.

The specificity of the monoplex PCR assays was further evaluated by testing known samples containing a specific mutation type with primers targeting other types. Some cross‐reactivity was observed. (Figure 2B).

Comparison to qPCR

The comparison of patient samples between dPCR and qPCR using the Bland–Altman statistical method demonstrated good agreement across all four primer sets (Figure 3A).

Additionally, dPCR was also performed on 17 patient samples undergoing routine MRD monitoring that had previously shown undetectable levels by qPCR. Among these, two samples that were negative by qPCR tested positive with the monoplex dPCR assay, enabling earlier detection of relapse (Figure 3B).

Furthermore, three patients with rare NPM1 mutations (4‐base pair insertions at position c.863_864) can now be monitored using the digital PCR approach.

Discussion

The evaluation of MRD in AML is crucial during treatment and at the end of chemotherapy. Monitoring by NPM1 is recommended by the ELN 202110 and should be prioritized over other targets or techniques. The high sensitivity of NPM1‐based monitoring allows for the detection of molecular relapse at an early stage, enabling timely and optimized therapeutic interventions. Despite the availability of published protocols, technical optimization was necessary to emphasize a laboratory routine work. By using a touch‐down PCR approach, we not only streamlined the workflow with a single PCR program but also significantly improved sensitivity compared to the initial setup, achieving a limit of detection of 0.01% in multiplex and 0.001% in type A monoplex assays (versus 0.024% and 0.01%, respectively, in initial experiments) [17].

The principle of touch‐down PCR is to start with an annealing temperature higher than the optimal annealing temperature, then gradually decrease it with each cycle until the optimal temperature is reached. A modified approach with two annealing and extension temperatures was chosen. The first, a higher temperature, enhances specificity by improving primer‐template binding. The second, a lower temperature, is optimized for the amplification of mutants. During specificity testing between the monoplex primers and the multiplex primer, no clinically relevant discrepancies were observed. The main discrepancies concerned very high values (647.8% with the multiplex primer versus 715.5% with the monoplex primer). However, targeted PCR should be favored to ensure the best possible limit of detection (LoD). The difference in the limit of quantification (LoQ) between the two approaches should also be taken into account. Nevertheless, given the similarities between the different mutations (only 1–2 nucleotide differences), absolute primer specificity was unlikely. The results confirm this hypothesis. The monoplex primers are not specific to their respective targets.

The implementation of digital PCR in the laboratory has improved MRD detection through enhanced sensitivity (LoD at 0.001%) and quantification limit (LoQ at 0.004%). It allows monitoring of lower values and earlier relapse prediction. Additionally, there has been a reduction in the number of repeats. In January 2023, 13.8% of samples had to be retested, compared to no retests in January 2025.

The outliers observed in the method comparison correspond to very high values at diagnosis, and this difference has no clinical or therapeutic impact, it belongs to a same log range. Because of the sensitivity achieved, the MRD analyze is very subject to contamination, to avoid this, diagnosis samples were diluting to 2 ng/μL, it both prevent contamination and saturation of the droplet.

Conclusion

NPM1 mutation quantification and monitoring is a powerful marker for early relapse detection, and MRD assessment is recommended by the ELN guidelines [3, 10]. The development of new targeted therapies, such as menin inhibitors, further emphasizes the importance of response monitoring and early relapse detection (< 3–5 days) [3, 24, 25]. Digital PCR (dPCR) is a robust technique that enables absolute quantification through direct copy counting. By simplifying the PCR protocol using a single touch‐down thermal cycling program, we have implemented dPCR quantification through both monoplex and multiplex approaches.

This strategy defines a new routine workflow for the molecular profiling of AML in our laboratory: first, NPM1 screening by amplified fragment length polymorphism (AFLP), as described by Kottaridis et al. [26] If the screening is positive, quantification is performed using multiplex NPM1 dPCR. This is followed by mutation typing via next‐generation sequencing (NGS). In cases where the mutation is identified as type A, B, or D, follow‐up monitoring is conducted using type‐specific monoplex dPCR. For other mutation types, monitoring is continued using multiplex NPM1 dPCR (Figure 4).

Switching to monoplex dPCR for types A, B, or D remains necessary due to its higher sensitivity. Nevertheless, the use of multiplex NPM1 dPCR allows us to provide high‐quality molecular monitoring for patients with rare NPM1 mutations who were previously ineligible for qPCR‐based follow‐up and also enables rapid initial quantification without requiring prior mutation typing by NGS.
