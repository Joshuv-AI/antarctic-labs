 Introduction
The debundling team recommended two solutions for implementing fuel debundling into a recycling
scheme in the United States at the conclusion of FY25 [1]. The first was a combined fuel debundling and
recycling facility, where the debundling fuel rods were fed directly into the recycling process. The second
was to separate debundling from the recycling facility. The motivation between the latter was to remove
the need to meet Category I or II nuclear material accountancy and control (NMAC) requirements
established by the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) in 10 CFR 74. Although the recommendation
in FY25 was to pursue the second option, the team was redirected to pursue the first option due to costs
and logistics associated with two separate facilities.

With the redirection, the team needed to begin by reassessing the NRC regulations to understand the best
options for implementing debundling in a combined debundling and recycling facility. Detector
alternatives were then revisited to determine if the FY25 recommendations were still the right choice.
Initial design considerations for the Fuel Debundling Detector (FUDD) were determined. The activities
from FY26 are reported here along with planned activities for FY27.

 NRC Regulations
The fuel debundling operation involves removing fuel rods from spent fuel assemblies during the initial
phase of a reprocessing facility's operation, before subsequent fuel dissolution and recycling. Initially, the
defueling operation can be performed as an independent, item-only facility. The NMAC program for
such a facility configured in this approach would be relatively straightforward, as spent fuel rods remain
intact throughout all operations. This facility thus needs only to comply with the NRC NMAC
requirements under Subpart B of 10 CFR Part 74. However, given the costs and logistics of two separate
facilities, a fuel debundling facility combined with a recycling facility enables the direct transfer of
debundled fuel rods into recycling operations without interruption, particularly avoiding interim storage
of spent fuel. Consequently, this integrated debundling and recycling facility must meet the NRC's
Category II NMAC requirements as stipulated in Subpart D of 10 CFR Part 74.

The NMAC requirements for a Category II recycling facility in Subpart D of Part 74 are substantially
more stringent than those for an item-only facility in Subpart B of Part 74. Consequently, NMAC
requirements should be considered to ensure that Category II reprocessing facilities produce only
enriched uranium of moderate strategic significance or Category II material, consistent with the existing

 1

requirements for Category II fuel cycle facilities. The NMAC approach should be compatible with the
performance-based NMAC requirements for a Category II reprocessing facility, with the alternative of
modifying and expanding them to suit the needs of a Category I facility when recycling operations exceed
the Category II quantity limits. This places more stringent requirements on the facility’s overall NMAC
program, which is evaluated for material production within the appropriate Category II level range. A
previous report provided a comprehensive overview and crosswalk of the NMAC program requirements
for all Category I and II facilities [1].

Within the NMAC requirements for Category II nuclear material of moderate strategic significance, the
measurement system and measurement control program must accommodate facility throughput and
achieve the more stringent measurement uncertainties required for the Category II facility. Under current
NRC reporting requirements, operator declarations serve as acceptable shipper’s values for input
accounting in recycling operations. However, these accepted values are based on burnup calculations
rather than independent measurements of fuel assemblies. Moreover, fuel rods will be separated during
debundling; thus, assembly-declared values applied to individual rods are no longer valid. While an
accurate accountability measurement is normally obtained via the first dissolution stage of all recovered
fuel pellets from the sheared fuel rods, independent mass estimates of individual fuel rods would be
obtained during debundling activities to enhance accountability at the dissolver and support input
accounting for downstream recycling operations. Design considerations and experimental testing of the
FUDD remain to assess the availability, applicability, and limitations of measurement methods and
techniques within the debundling operation and to extend them to the entire recycling process to achieve
the desired precision and accuracy of measured values, including measurement uncertainties and bias
corrections, in meeting the measurement and measurement control program for the integrated debundling
and recycling facility.

 Initial Design Considerations
 Need for Gamma-Ray Detector

Development of the FUDD included considerations for gamma and neutron non-destructive assay (NDA)
measurements to quantify the Pu mass in spent nuclear fuel rods. The system is expected to implement
passive neutron coincidence counting to measure Singles and Doubles for an individual spent fuel rod and
relate those measurements to the rod’s Pu mass. Since Doubles quantifies neutron pairs that are produced
by the same event (i.e. spontaneous fission (SF)), it can be related to the quantity of spontaneously
fissioning nuclides in an item. In spent nuclear power reactor fuel rods, isotopes of Pu and Cm are the
main contributors to the SF neutron emission rate.

A dataset of simulated burned PWR fuel assemblies with a large range of burnups, initial enrichments,
and cooling times was used to establish expectations for the material composition of spent power reactor
fuel rods [2]. To determine if Pu mass of a spent fuel rod could be determined using measured Doubles,
the Pu masses for each rod in the simulated PWR fuel database were plotted as a function of that rod’s SF
neutron emission rate, as shown in Figure 1. The scatter plot demonstrates that for a SF neutron emission
rate between 150,000-250,000 n/s, the Pu mass in a spent fuel rod could vary from 12.95 g to 18.90 g
when not accounting for spent fuel parameters. Initial enrichment, burnup and cooling time information
for a fuel rod may help to reduce the uncertainty in Pu mass for a given Doubles measurement.

 Figure 1 Total Pu mass in simulated PWR fuel rods plotted as a function of the SF neutron emission rate.

Figure 2 plots the same data as Figure 1 with different-colored data series grouped by fuel assembly
burnup. The different-colored series plotted in Figure 2 show the range of potential Pu masses associated
with SF neutron emission rates of fuel rods with the same burnup. Increasing the burnup of a fuel rod also
increases the inventory of spontaneously fissioning actinides in the fuel material, meaning the buildup of
additional Cm in the fuel increases the spontaneous fission neutron emission rate without an equivalent
increase in the total Pu mass. Independent knowledge of a fuel rod’s burnup can reduce the range of total
Pu mass values associated with a given Doubles measurement. Verifying the burnup of a fuel rod using
gamma ray measurements could reduce the uncertainty of total Pu masses measured with the combined
FUDD system. For example, for fuel rods with a burnup of 60 GWd/MTU, the range of total Pu masses
for a SF neutron emission rate between 150,000-250,000 n/s is reduced by 36%. Note that the
stratification in emission rate of the all the data in Figure 2, especially visible for the 60 GWd/MTU data
points, is due to the discrete spacing of the simulated parameters of the spent fuel, specifically initial
enrichment (IE), burnup (BU), and cooling time (CT).

 Measurement Dependence on Position

Segments of three Westinghouse 17×17 PWR fuel pins from the Byron Nuclear Generating Station (Ogle
County, IL, USA), operated by Constellation Energy, were measured in August 2026 to understand if and
how the neutron count rate changes along the axial length of the fuel rod. The data and preliminary results
from the campaign are described here. Further analysis is expected in FY27.

 Measurement Setup

The measurement campaign was performed in the east neutron radiography station of the Neutron
Radiography Reactor (NRAD) in the Hot Fuel Examination Facility (HFEF) at Idaho National Laboratory
(INL). Two High Dose Neutron Detector (HDND) pods were used to acquire neutron measurements
[3][4]. Each HDND pod has six cells of boron-lined parallel plates with high density polyethylene
(HDPE) between the cells for moderation.

List mode data were acquired using an advanced list mode module (ALMM), providing data from each
plate individually. The ALMM was operated from a laptop running the newly released INCC 6.0.1, which
has list mode data acquisition capabilities. The signal from plate 3 in pod 3 was rejected for this
campaign, because the observed count rate was significantly higher than all other plates combined when
measuring a spent fuel segment. The cause of the malfunction is unknown at this time. Measurements of
fuel rods were acquired in 30-s cycles with the number of cycles varied as necessary to achieve a relative
uncertainty of 5% or less in the measured Doubles.

A platform was erected in the east radiography station to place the HDND pods around the elevator tube
that fuel segments are lowered through from the HFEF main hot cell. The detector configuration in the
NRAD cell is shown in Figure 3 and Figure 4.

 HDND Characterization

Neutron Efficiency

Detection efficiency was determined by measuring two 252Cf sources of centered between the two pods
separated by about 30 cm, as shown in Figure 8. The combined neutron emission rate from the 252Cf
sources was 3.22 ·107 n/s at the time of measurement. The measurement yielded an efficiency of about
0.9%. Although the detection efficiency is relatively small compared to typical passive neutron counters,
it is large enough to measure statistically significant coincident rates from spent fuel.

 Figure 8. HDND Pod testing with 252Cf sources in the lab.

High Voltage Plateau

A high-voltage plateau was acquired to determine the optimal high-voltage setting to maximize detection
efficiency while rejecting gamma-ray pile-up events in the neutron signal. Segment 3 of fuel rod SXM
was used, because it had the highest burnup and shortest cooling time, resulting in the highest gamma-ray
dose rate. It was lowered to an elevation of 55 inches above the zero point. A series of measurements was
acquired from 600 – 940 V while recording the Singles rates at each voltage. The resulting plateau is
shown in Figure 9. The plateau demonstrates gamma ray pile-up events being recorded as neutron events
above 900 V. The HDND pods were consequently operated at 870 V to minimize gamma ray pile-up
events contributing to the neutron signal for these measurements.

Die-away Time

The die-away time, τ, for the HDND pods was determined from a single list-mode measurement of a
spent fuel segment by varying the gate width parameter for multiple analyses of a single recorded pulse
stream. The gate width parameter, GW, was adjusted between 20 – 128 µs and the Doubles were plotted
as a function of the gate width, as shown in Figure 10. A fitting program was used to fit a functional
curve given in Eq. (1).

Measurements (a), (e), and (f) in Figure 11 were compared to determine if measurement position
significantly affected the resulting count rate. Measurement (f)—the middle measurement of segment 4—
represented the center of the fuel rod for this analysis, because neither of the segment 3 measurements
filled the detector field-of-view due to operational uncertainty of rod position at the time of measurement.
The Singles across the length of the fuel rod differed by up to 7%, indicating that position along the
length of the fuel rod could matter for the measurement. However, the magnitudes of Singles did not
follow a logical pattern with respect to axial position. The expectation was that Singles would be smaller
near the bottom of the fuel pin and larger in the middle, such that Singles would be largest for
measurement (a) and followed by (f) and then (e). However, Singles were largest for measurement (f),
followed by (e) and then (a). This could have been caused by segment 4 being closer to the HDND pods
in the y-direction than segment 2. The slot in the elevator plate, shown in Figure 7, allows positioning
variability in this direction, and likely contributed to this unexpected trend. Given the potential variability
of the fuel segments in the y-direction, the only comparable data are from the same fuel segment. In this
case, the comparable data points were acquired for segment 4, which do follow the logical pattern based
on vertical location of the measurements.

The Doubles decrease between measurements (a) and (f) when compared to (e) is not statistically
significant at the 2- σ level. It is possible that a statistical difference could be observed if measurement
times were extended to obtain lower uncertainties. However, it is difficult to draw a conclusion for this
fuel pin without any valid segment 3 measurements. Note that the variability in the y-direction likely also
impacted the Doubles, but the effect is not as clear due to larger uncertainties in the Doubles
measurement. Ultimately, fuel positioning will be an important consideration when designing the FUDD
to reduce position-related uncertainty. The best method to accomplish this will be to limit the variability
of the fuel rod location and/or surround the fuel rod with detectors during measurement.

The 137Cs gamma data for each fuel pin was acquired in the HFEF main cell prior to sectioning using a
high-purity germanium (HPGe) detector in the highly collimated Precision Gamma Scanner (PGS) [6].
Several gamma ray lines that are proportional to the burnup were recorded as a function of position.
Figure 12 shows the net 662 keV gamma ray counts are relatively constant over the height of the fuel pin
but have several significant dips that generally coincide with the locations of the fuel assembly grid
spacers. The different colored dashed and solid lines above the 137Cs data in Figure 12 depict where the
different sections of the fuel pin were cut and the lettered rectangles show where the HNDN neutron
measurements were made on those individual sections. The dips in the 137Cs gamma ray intensity indicate
that a lower burnup was achieved for those fuel pellets adjacent to the grid spacer locations and could
account for the variability in the observed neutron count rates. However, there is not a perfect correlation
between burnup and neutron intensity, which is due primarily to curium and plutonium isotopes as
detected by the HDND pods.

 Number of Pins to Measure

Fuel assemblies do not necessarily build-up Pu uniformly across all fuel pins, as shown by the gradient in
the heat map in Figure 16 for a PWR 17×17 fuel assembly. Given the limited time for measuring each
fuel pin, it may be more beneficial to measure select fuel pins from a fuel assembly for a longer time than
all fuel pins for a short time. A longer count time typically reduces measurement uncertainty, especially
when a measurement can only be acquired for a couple of minutes like in fuel debundling.

High burnup assemblies from the simulated spent PWR fuel database were analyzed to determine Pu
mass range in each rod in an assembly. An assembly with high burnup is expected to have the most Pu
buildup because it was in the reactor for a longer duration. The range of fuel rod masses in a high burnup
fuel assembly was highlighted in Figure 17 for 60 GWd/MTU assemblies with cooling times of 0 days

Although this difference represents 25-33% of the total Pu mass in a fuel rod, NDA measurements may
not provide the fidelity to statistically differentiate mass estimates within that range when the count time
can only be about 60-s per fuel rod. Measuring a select few fuel rods from an assembly for a longer time
may provide an average Pu mass per fuel rod with smaller uncertainty than measuring all fuel pins for a
short time, ultimately improving NMAC in a debundling facility. This will need to be investigated during
the FUDD design to determine which methodology would achieve better NMAC outcomes.

 Pu Mass Algorithm
 Assay Concept

The point-model equations (PME) as applied to neutron multiplicity counting provide a useful analytical
framework for passive neutron assay of relatively compact, homogeneous plutonium oxide items [5]. For
such materials, Singles, Doubles, and Triples can be related to plutonium mass using a limited set of
effective parameters, including detection efficiency, neutron leakage multiplication, spontaneous-fission
(SF) multiplicity moments, induced-fission (IF) multiplicity moments, and the relative contribution of
(α,n) neutrons to the observed count rates.

Applying this approach to spent fuel is substantially more difficult because the item is an extended,
spatially heterogeneous source with a complex and evolving isotopic inventory. The emitted neutron
signal includes SF from both plutonium and curium, as well as (α,n) neutrons from many transuranic
isotopes. While in the traditional PME methodology, the parameters like detection efficiency,
multiplication, and other source-dependent quantities like the neutron detection efficiency (ε) are single-
valued and constant, for the case of a spent fuel pin that extends outside of the field of view of the neutron
counter, these parameters can be functions of fuel history, axial position, measurement geometry, and
neutron spectrum. Consequently, the conventional PME methodology must be modified before being
applied to spent fuel.

A quasi-point-model equation (qPME) treatment is under development for spent fuel that will provide
relevant effective parameters from depletion and transport calculations based on the specific fuel
parameters of initial enrichment (IE), burnup (BU), and cooling time (CT) for specific measurement
configurations. These “effective parameters” are those that violate the assumptions of the PME, such as
the detection efficiency and leakage multiplication being constants as a function of position. In this
respect, the qPME will be applicable only to the system being modeled instead of a general solution for a
variety of multiplicity counting systems. For this initial development of the qPME methodology only the

 17

Singles were considered in the analysis. Extension to Doubles will be pursued in FY27. This work was
done separately and in parallel to the Byron fuel rod measurement campaign reported on in Section 3.

 Model Inputs and Effective Parameters

The buildup and depletion code SCALE was used to generate a fuel library for Westinghouse 17×17
Pressurized Water Reactor (PWR) fuel over a set of representative combinations of IE, BU, and CT [7].
The modeled parameter ranges were:

    •   IE = 2.0%; BU = 15 – 30 GWd/MTU; CT = 10 – 80 years
    •   IE = 2.5%; BU = 15 – 40 GWd/MTU; CT = 10 – 80 years
    •   IE = 3.0%; BU = 15 – 50 GWd/MTU; CT = 10 – 80 years
    •   IE = 3.5%; BU = 15 – 55 GWd/MTU; CT = 10 – 70 years
    •   IE = 4.0%; BU = 15 – 60 GWd/MTU; CT = 10 – 70 years
    •   IE = 4.5%; BU = 15 – 65 GWd/MTU; CT = 10 – 60 years
    •   IE = 5.0%; BU = 15 – 65 GWd/MTU; CT = 10 – 60 years

These ranges are intended to span representative commercial PWR fuel histories while avoiding
combinations that are operationally unrealistic, e.g. a 2.0% IE fuel assembly burned to 60 GWd/MTU, or
4.5% IE fuel that has been cooled for 80 years. BU was incremented in steps of 5 GWd/MTU, and CT
was incremented in steps of 10 years. Across this analysis domain for each IE-BU-CT point, the SCALE
code calculations provided isotopic ratios, total Pu mass, Pu-240 effective fraction, SF and (α,n) neutron
source terms, an MCNP material card for the fuel pin, and a corresponding (α,n) neutron spectrum.

 Detector response parameters

A standard High-Level Neutron Coincidence Counter (HLNCC) was used for initial algorithm
development, because it is a well-characterized detection system [3]. Fixed-source MCNP calculations for
the fuel pin inside the HLNCC provided the effective response quantities needed for both the SF and (α,n)
neutron source terms at each IE, BU, and CT triplet [8]. These quantities included: detection efficiency,
the second neutron capture moment for a given pre-delay and gate width, and the effective spontaneous
fission and induced fission factorial moments. A separate set of MCNP KCODE calculations provided the
leakage-multiplication at each IE, BU, and CT triplet. Together the SCALE and MCNP simulations
provided a set of source and detector response parameters as a function of the full phase space of IE, BU,
and CT:

 • w: Pu fraction of the total SF rate
 • α: the ratio of (α,n)-to-SF neutron-emission
 • ε: effective neutron detection efficiency for the modeled pin-counter geometry
 • M: effective leakage multiplication
 • ν̄s1,eff: MCNP-derived effective first SF neutron factorial moment
 • ν̄s2,eff: MCNP-derived effective second SF neutron factorial moment
 • ν̄i1,eff: MCNP-derived effective first induced fission (IF) neutron factorial moment
 • ν̄i2,eff: MCNP-derived effective second IF neutron factorial moment
 • f240,eff: Pu-240 effective fraction of total Pu mass
 • mPu: total plutonium mass in the fuel pin.

 18

These are effective, case-specific quantities that depend on the unique combination of isotopes present in
the spent fuel pin, which are dependent on IE, BU, and CT, and the design of the neutron counter.
Furthermore, ε, M, and all the ν̄x#,eff values should not be interpreted as universal detector or material
constants.

NOTE: the discussion of the detector response parameters above includes the second neutron capture
moment for a given pre-delay and gate width which would be used to calculate Doubles in the HLNCC
and the second order SF and IF neutron factorial moments which appear in the PME for the Doubles.
Those parameters are left here for completeness, but they will not be used until FY27 when the Doubles
will be incorporated into the qPME analysis.

 Singles-Only Pu Mass Algorithm

The standard PME for the background corrected Singles, S, for a plutonium sample placed fully inside
any characterized neutron counter is given by Eq. (2).

                                      S = F ε M ν̄s1 (1 + α)                                             (2)

where, F is the SF rate of the 240Pueff mass, ε is the detection efficiency from characterization of the
neutron counter using a 252Cf source, M is the leakage multiplication of the sample, ν̄s1 is the average
number of neutrons emitted from the SF of 240Pu, and α is ratio of (α,n)-to-SF neutrons. In this equation
there are three unknowns about the sample: F, M, and α, where F is proportional to the mass of 240Pueff.
To solve for these unknowns requires the use of higher order equations for the Doubles and Triples,
making educated assumptions about those unknowns, or a combination of both. For samples of relatively
pure plutonium oxide this analysis is straightforward, and the results can be quite accurate.

In spent fuel, however, the SF rate is largely a function of plutonium and curium isotopic contributions.
The qPME method separates the total SF rate (Fₜ) into two components, plutonium and curium, and
defines the fraction of the SF rate that is due to plutonium.

 Fₜ = FPu + FCm (3)

                                      w = FPu / Fₜ                                                       (4)

Here FPu and FCm are the plutonium and curium portions of the total SF rate, respectively, and w is the
fraction of the total SF rate attributable to plutonium. In subsections 4.2 and 4.3, the development of the
different parameters needed was outlined to relate measured Singles in each neutron counter
configuration to Fₜ. Those parameters will be used here in the development of the algorithm to calculate
plutonium mass from measured Singles for a PWR 17×17 fuel pin in an HLNCC.

The derivation for determining Pu mass using Singles begins with the qPME for the background corrected
Singles:

                                      S = Ft ε M ν̄s1,eff (1 + α)                                        (5)

Rearranging Eqn. 5 to solve for the total SF rate gives

                                      Fₜ = S / [ε M ν̄s1,eff (1 + α)]                                    (6)

Combining Eqns. 3 and 6, the plutonium SF rate is then
 19

                             FPu = wFₜ = wS / [ε M ν̄s1,eff (1 + α)]                                      (7)

The plutonium SF rate can then be converted to mass by using Pu-240 effective-mass conversion factor of
473 fissions s⁻¹ g⁻¹, written as

                                      m240,eff = FPu / 473                                                (8)

and conversion from Pu-240 effective mass to Pu mass, written as

                                      mPu = m240,eff / f240,eff                                           (9)

Combining equations 7, 8, and 9 yields the working assay equation

                            mPu = w S / [473 ε M ν̄s1,eff (1 + α) f240,eff]                           (10)

 Testing and Implementation

The parameters on the right-hand side of Eq. 10 represent quantities that have been derived, or are a
combination of parameters derived, from the SCALE and MCNP simulations discussed before and are
functions of IE, BU, and CT. There are 409 combinations of IE, BU, and CT for which each of the
parameters in Section 4.3 have been determined. Each parameter, with the exception of mPu, was fitted
with complex mathematical expression that is a function of IE, BU, and CT such that, for any given IE,
BU, and CT the user can calculate all parameters needed to solve Eq. (10) for the mass of plutonium in
the fuel pin.

 Limitations and testing

True validation of this method and algorithm is not possible without actual measurement data of full-
length Westinghouse 17×17 PWR spent fuel pins placed in an HLNCC. However, further testing of the
algorithm can be conducted by applying the algorithm to a different set of IE, BU, and CT triplets than
those used in the calibration. A new set of 120 simulations was randomly selected from the original set of
SCALE calculations performed for Westinghouse 17×17 fuel to determine the total plutonium masses for
those pins. The fitting equations for the variables in Eq. 10 were used at the new IE, BU, and CT points
to calculate those variables which were then used to calculate the total plutonium mass for each of the
new 120 IE, BU, and CT triplets. The plot of calculated to declared total plutonium mass for these new
points are shown in Figure 20 and show very good agreement. These data were determined using the
fixed detector efficiency of 2.27% and n in place of w in Eq. 10. Finally, a different set of 100 IE, BU,
and CT triplets were simulated in SCALE using the Westinghouse 14×14 PWR model, the fixed detector
efficiency of 2.27%, and n in place of w in Eq. 10. These results, as seen in Figure 21, are equally good,
which indicates that this methodology has the potential to be more widely applicable.

Within the qPME parameter space, the initial Singles-only algorithm reproduced simulated plutonium
masses with moderate systematic bias. An empirically modified implementation, using a fixed detector
efficiency and the plutonium fraction of total neutron emission rather than the plutonium fraction of total
spontaneous-fission rate, substantially improved agreement for both the original modeled cases and
additional simulated test cases. The physical basis for this improvement has not yet been established and
requires further investigation.

These results demonstrate promising performance within the simulation framework, including for
withheld 17×17 cases and independent simulated 14×14 fuel cases, however, they do not constitute
experimental validation. Validation will require measurements of spent-fuel pins with independently
established plutonium mass. The practical application of the method will also depend on obtaining initial
enrichment, burnup, and cooling-time, information with sufficient accuracy, potentially through gamma-
ray spectroscopy and/or verified reactor operating records.

 Conclusions
The applicable NRC regulatory framework was reassessed to identify workable pathways for debundling
operations after being redirected to work within a combined facility. This reassessment defined the
material accountancy, measurement control, and safeguards requirements that constrained subsequent
technical work for designing the FUDD.

Initial design considerations for the FUDD identified the need for a companion gamma-ray detector, since
the neutron measurement alone does not provide the information required to constrain initial enrichment,
burnup, and cooling time on a rod-by-rod basis. The next design consideration centered on rod
positioning with respect to the detector. Measurements of the Byron fuel rod segments showed that
Singles and Doubles can change significantly depending on where the detector is positioned along the
length of the fuel rod. Repeatable positioning will need to be considered when designing the FUDD.
FY27 will continue with designing the FUDD based on the constraints determined in FY26. A neutron
counter tailored for high-gamma-dose spent-fuel measurements will be developed to improve
coincidence-counting performance to hit future throughput targets. A second measurement campaign is
also planned to investigate axial response, positioning effects, and the data needed for experimental
validation of the mass-estimation method.

Development of the qPME methodology established a viable approach for relating initial enrichment (IE),
burnup (BU), and cooling time (CT) to measured neutron response. Multivariable fitting expressions—
ranging from third-order polynomials to nonlinear models with exponential output transformations—were
generated and evaluated in Python. Initial results were promising for Singles, but the method needs to be
expanded to Doubles in FY27.
