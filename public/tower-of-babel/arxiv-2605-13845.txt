Quantitative Linear Logic for Neuro-Symbolic Learning and Verification
Thomas Flinkow, Ekaterina Komendantskaya, Matteo Capucci, Rosemary Monahan

Abstract
Differentiable Logics are deployed in neuro-symbolic learning tasks as a way of embedding logical constraints in the training objective of neural networks. A differentiable logic consists of a syntax to write logical properties and a semantics to interpret them as real-valued functions to be folded in the loss function. A defining trade-off of the field is that between logical properties of the connectives, and analytic concerns for the semantics, with both aspects being relevant in applications. At one extreme we find fuzzy logics, that have well-established algebraic and proof-theoretic foundations, and at the other ad-hoc differentiable logics like Fischer’s DL2, conceived for deep learning applications. However, no satisfactory foundation has emerged yet. We propose a resolution to this long-standing tension via a novel logic, QLL ( QLL ), with foundational ambitions. Our design is driven by naturality —the idea that, since logical constraints are translated to losses, the semantics of the connectives should be pertinent operations used in ML practice (that is, sum and log-sum-exp) on additive quantities (like logits). We then judge the result on two aspects: logical adequacy —that they satisfy most of the standard logical laws of Linear Logic; and empirical effectiveness —test-time performance (as measured by adversarial attacks) is well-correlated to the actual verification of the logical constraints (as measured by off-the-shelf neural network verifiers), which makes QLL stand out among SoTA techniques.

Background: Differentiable Logics and Property-Driven Training

Throughout the paper, we assume supervised machine learning scenarios that feature a data set
D
with input-output pairs
(\hat{\bm{x}},\hat{\bm{y}})

\mathbin{\cup^{p}}/\mathbin{\cap^{p}}
to their non-smooth counterparts
\min/\max
:

Proposition 3.2

(Shadow-lifting)
.

For
p<\infty
, the partial derivatives of
\mathbin{\cap^{p}}
and
\mathbin{\cup^{p}}
are strictly positive for all finite values—no operand is ever ‘shadowed’ by the other.

Together, these properties give rise to what we call
adversarially informative gradients
.
In adversarial min-max optimisation, gradients must decrease as constraints become satisfied, yet not vanish entirely so as to still expose nearby violations.
As a consequence, gradient signal persists even for satisfied constraints, enabling gradient-based methods such as
PGD
to find violations while still guiding optimisation towards satisfaction.

4.3
Effect of the Interval Choice

Prior differentiable logic design made different choices for the interval on which loss functions are defined: fuzzy differentiable logics
(
48
)
work on the interval
[0,1]
, DL2
(
17
)
—on
[0,\infty)
, and STL
(
49
)
—on
(-\infty,\infty)
. Recall that in QLL, the choice of the interval
[-\infty,+\infty]
is motivated by naturality, specifically that it is the range for cross-entropy, which is used as prediction loss.
Thus, the choice of the interval and the choice of operations are—via the naturality principle—the two sides of one coin. To test the effect of the interval choice alone, it suffices to compare the performance of STL/QLL vs DL2 on the strong classification robustness property that does not involve any conjunctions or disjunctions (all three logics interpret comparisons in the same way).

As
and
show, STL and QLL consistently out-perform DL2 on SCR.
We hypothesise that two factors are at play—both the range of the interval that matches better the standard cross-entropy loss, but also avoidance of
0
gradients. Note that in DL2 and fuzzy logics, as soon as a constraint is satisfied, gradients vanish.
Indeed, note that the inner maximisation step in (
2
) requires informative gradients to discover nearby violations even when the constraint is currently satisfied for one data point.
QLL and STL avoid this problem, by having the entire sub-interval
[-\infty,0]
standing for satisfaction (recall
Theorem

3.7
).

4.4
Effect of Convergence and Softness Parameters

The next property of

that we hold valuable is the fact that non-linear connectives in both logics converge to

and
\max
as

.

STL
shares the same feature, whereby its connectives are parameterised by a parameter

with the same limit as
\nu\rightarrow\infty
.
Our hypothesis is that this property conveys better the effect of classical connectives at training time.

and
show that having additive approximations of
\min
/
\max
is useful: on three out of four properties, STL outperforms most other logics and QLL outperforms all other logics.

We note, however, that current experiments are inconclusive when we try to isolate the effect of the interval choice from the effect of
\min
and
\max
approximations, where the former might overshadow the latter. This can be seen on the fuzzy logics, where Gödel directly uses
\min
and
\max
, Yager smoothly approximates
\min
and
\max
, and product uses neither. Still, all three fuzzy logics perform similarly.

5
Limitations and future work

From a theoretical standpoint, we left open various issues.
The biggest limitation is being not able to adequately explain for the empirical behaviour of
QLL
: intuitively,
QLL
better manages the gap between the soft spec
\llbracket\phi\rrbracket
and the hard one
\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{\mathbb{B}}
because the soft connectives approximate the hard ones and due to logical adequacy.
However, we do not prove rigorous
bounds
linking the test-time logical constraint loss value to the satisfaction of the hard spec.
Since verification is a much more expensive process compared to training, having guarantees on verification by evaluating the loss only would be of great practical value, evenmoreso for complex models.

The ideas of
8
suggest this requires a first-order extension of
QLL
.
DL2 is superior in this sense, having a first-order language, but not a proper first-order logic (with proof rules, model theory, etc.)
From an empirical standpoint, our experiments are purely demonstrative and cannot be considered to reflect real-world applications, based on model and dataset size as well as complexity of the logical constraint.

A.1
Residuated Lattices

Section

3
introduced the semantics of
QLL
in
[-\infty,+\infty]
as exhibiting a ‘quantitative’ version of a
residuated lattice
.
This appendix unpacks what that means.
We reference
18
and forthcoming work on the proof and model theory of
QLL

(
7
)
.

Residuated lattices are the standard algebraic semantics of substructural logics (like linear and fuzzy logic), and the laws collected in
Section

3
are quantitative instances of laws that hold in any residuated lattice.

Lattices.

A
partially ordered set
, or
poset
, is a pair
(L,\leq)
where
\leq
is reflexive (
a\leq a
), transitive (
a\leq b
and
b\leq c
implies
a\leq c
), and antisymmetric (
a\leq b
implies
b\nleq a
).
Given two elements
a,b\in L
, their
join

a\vee b
is their least upper bound and their
meet

a\wedge b
is their greatest lower bound, when these exist.

The ‘least upper bound’ property of the join
a\vee b
is characterized by two clauses:

\displaystyle c\leq a\ \text{or}\ c\leq b\quad

\displaystyle\text{then}\quad c\leq a\vee b,

(upper bound)

\displaystyle a\leq c\ \text{and}\ b\leq c\quad

\displaystyle\text{then}\quad a\vee b\leq c.

(minimality)

The first says
a\vee b
dominates each of
a
and
b
; the second says it is the smallest such element.
The meet
a\wedge b
is characterized dually as a greatest lower bound.

Then, a
lattice
is a poset in which every pair of elements admits both a join and a meet.

Two examples of lattices relevant to this work are the extended reals
([-\infty,+\infty],\geq)
, so that
a\vee b=\min(a,b)
and
a\wedge b=\max(a,b)
; and the Booleans
\mathbb{B}=\{{\bf false},{\bf true}\}
with
{\bf false}\leq{\bf true}
, where
\vee
is logical disjunction and
\wedge
is logical conjunction.

The main idea behind the algebraic semantics of
QLL
is to replace the order
\leq
with a quantitative predicate instead, which is
\multimap
in
[-\infty,+\infty]
.
This makes it a kind of ‘asymmetric’ metric space, as observed already by
27
.
Thus (
upper bound
) and (
minimality
) become the clauses appearing in
Section

3
as the
disjunction rules
of
QLL
.
The same happens for the
conjunction rules
, which are in fact formal duals.

Residuated lattices.

A (commutative)
residuated lattice
adds to a lattice a second pair of operations: a ‘multiplication’ and an ‘implication’.
Concretely, it is a structure
(L,\vee,\wedge,\otimes,i,\rightarrow)
where

1.

(L,\vee,\wedge)
is a lattice;

2.

(L,\otimes,i)
is a commutative monoid, i.e.
\otimes
is associative, commutative, and has
i
as a two-sided neutral element (
a\otimes i=i\otimes a=a
);

3.

(
mix
)
(a\leq b)
and
(c\rightarrow d)
imply
(a\otimes c)\leq(b\otimes d)
,

4.

\rightarrow
is the
residual
of
\otimes
, meaning that the two operations are linked by the
residuation property

a\otimes b\leq c\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a\leq b\rightarrow c.

(9)

Equation (
9
) pins down
\rightarrow
uniquely given
\otimes
and the order:
b\rightarrow c
is the largest element
a
such that
a\otimes b\leq c
.
When
\otimes
happens to coincide with the meet
\wedge
, the residual
\rightarrow
is exactly the implication of
Heyting algebras
, the algebraic semantics of intuitionistic propositional logic.

[-\infty,+\infty]
is a commutative residuated lattice with
\otimes=+
as defined in
Table

1
, and
\mathbb{B}
is too with
\otimes=\land
—in fact,
\mathbb{B}
is an Heyting algebra.

Again, in
QLL
we treat the laws quantitatively.
In
[-\infty,+\infty]_{\mathbin{+}}
, unitality, associativity, and commutativity of
\otimes=+
still hold in the traditional sense, while our ‘mix’ and ‘residuation’ rules from
Table

2
are a quantitative instantiation of the same rules we just introduced for residuated lattices.
In particular, note that for
[-\infty,+\infty]_{\mathbin{+}}
,
\multimap
is both the quantitative order and the residuation
\rightarrow
(compare the situation with
\mathbb{B}
, in which also order and residuation are given by classical material implication ‘
\Rightarrow
’).

Often the lattice carries an order-reversing involution
a\mapstochar\rightarrow a^{*}
so that

a\otimes b\leq c^{*}\iff a\leq(b\otimes c)^{*}

(10)

This is the case for both our running examples, where
(-)^{*}
is
-(-)
for
[-\infty,+\infty]
and
\neg(-)
for
\mathbb{B}
.
Then we can prove ‘contraposition’:

a\rightarrow b=b^{*}\rightarrow a^{*}.

A.2
Proof of
Theorem

3.7

We proceed by induction on the structure of
\phi
, recalling that at
p=\infty
the non-linear connectives become
\llbracket\phi\mathbin{\land}\psi\rrbracket_{\infty}=\max(\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{\infty},\llbracket\psi\rrbracket_{\infty})
and
\llbracket\phi\mathbin{\lor}\psi\rrbracket_{\infty}=\min(\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{\infty},\llbracket\psi\rrbracket_{\infty})
.

Propositional variables.

For
\phi=y_{i}
we have
\llbracket y_{i}\rrbracket_{\mathbb{B}}=(\pi_{i}\leq 0)=(\llbracket y_{i}\rrbracket_{\infty}\leq 0)
by definition; the cases
\phi=\hat{y}_{i}
and
\phi=r
are identical.

Negation.
By definition
\llbracket\neg\phi\rrbracket_{\mathbb{B}}=(\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{\infty}\geq 0)
, which is equivalent to
-\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{\infty}\leq 0
, i.e.
\llbracket\neg\phi\rrbracket_{\infty}\leq 0
.

Conjunction.

By induction hypothesis applied to
\phi
and
\psi
,

\llbracket\phi\mathbin{\land}\psi\rrbracket_{\mathbb{B}}\;=\;\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{\mathbb{B}}\land\llbracket\psi\rrbracket_{\mathbb{B}}\;\iff\;\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{\infty}\leq 0\;\text{and}\;\llbracket\psi\rrbracket_{\infty}\leq 0\;\iff\;\max(\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{\infty},\llbracket\psi\rrbracket_{\infty})\leq 0,

and the right-hand side is
\llbracket\phi\mathbin{\land}\psi\rrbracket_{\infty}\leq 0
.

Disjunction.

Symmetrically, by induction hypothesis,

\llbracket\phi\mathbin{\lor}\psi\rrbracket_{\mathbb{B}}\;=\;\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{\mathbb{B}}\lor\llbracket\psi\rrbracket_{\mathbb{B}}\;\iff\;\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{\infty}\leq 0\;\text{or}\;\llbracket\psi\rrbracket_{\infty}\leq 0\;\iff\;\min(\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{\infty},\llbracket\psi\rrbracket_{\infty})\leq 0,

and the right-hand side is
\llbracket\phi\mathbin{\lor}\psi\rrbracket_{\infty}\leq 0
.

Implication.

By definition
\llbracket\phi\multimap\psi\rrbracket_{\mathbb{B}}=(\llbracket\psi\rrbracket_{\infty}\leq\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{\infty})
, which is equivalent to
\llbracket\psi\rrbracket_{\infty}-\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{\infty}\leq 0
, i.e.
\llbracket\phi\multimap\psi\rrbracket_{\infty}\leq 0
.
∎.

Formalisations of Properties

Strong Classification Robustness.

In the following,
m=28\times 28
and
n=10
for MNIST and Fashion-MNIST.
For convenience, we also replaced data points
(\hat{\bm{x}},c)
with
(\hat{\bm{x}},\hat{\bm{y}})
where
\hat{\bm{y}}
is the logits of the one-hot distribution corresponding to the true class.

Strong classification robustness

(
17
;
9
)
requires that the logit corresponding to the true class must be predicted with degree above some threshold
\delta
, informally:

\text{for all $\bm{x}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\hat{\bm{x}})$},\ f_{\theta}(\bm{x})_{c}\geq\delta.

(14)

Note that
c
, in fact, depends on
\hat{\bm{x}}
, and thus one cannot be translated as a valid formula in the grammar we described in
Section

3.1
.
However, in
QLL
one can instead use the following formula, where
L=\{0,\ldots,9\}
is the set of MNIST/Fashion-MNIST labels:

\text{Strong classification robustness}:=\ \bigwedge_{c\in L}\hat{y}_{c}\multimap(\delta\multimap y_{c}).

(15)

The formula above thus says ‘for every class
c
, if it is the true class for the data point under consideration, then the network must predict
c
with degree at least
\delta
’.

As a sanity-check, the additive semantics of (
15
) at
\hat{\bm{x}}
equals the semantics of
\delta\multimap y_{c}
for
c
the true class of
\hat{\bm{x}}
:

\llbracket\text{Strong classification robustness}\rrbracket(\bm{y},\hat{\bm{y}})=\llbracket\delta\multimap y_{c}\rrbracket(\bm{y},\hat{\bm{y}}).

(16)

We call this the ‘simplified’ formula.
In this form, it can be handled by
DL2
and
STL
which lack an implication operator but have a comparison one—in the simplified formula, we can indeed interpret
\multimap
as
\geq
.

Classification Robustness.

Classification robustness

(
9
)
is a stronger notion of robustness than strong classification robustness, as the latter only imposes an arbitrary bound on a single logit and does not prevent label changes.
Classification robustness requires that the logit corresponding to the true class should remain the largest under all allowed perturbations, in symbols:

\text{for all $\bm{x}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\hat{\bm{x}})$},\ \text{for all $i\in L$},f_{\theta}(\bm{x})_{c}\geq f_{\theta}(\bm{x})_{i}

(17)

In
QLL
, this is formalized as

\text{Classification robustness}:=\ \bigwedge_{c\in L}\left(\hat{y}_{c}\multimap\bigwedge_{i\in L}(y_{c}\multimap y_{i})\right)

(18)

Similar considerations as above apply for the translation of this to
DL2
and
STL
, with the additional caveat that for
STL
we cannot use
\bigwedge_{i\in L}(y_{c}\geq y_{i})
due to ill-behaved semantics when one of the conjuncts is
0
, which always occurs for
c=i
.

Semantic Relations on Fashion-MNIST.

A more interesting constraint concerning semantic relations between classes can be encoded on Fashion-MNIST.
Here we partition the labels
L
into sets
C=\{0,2,3,4,6\}
for
clothing
items (t-shirt/top, pullover, dress, coat, shirt) and
F=\{5,7,9\}
for
footwear
(sandal, sneaker, ankle boot).
Then, given a data point
(\hat{\bm{x}},c)
with true class
c
, our specification is to require the logit corresponding to the ground-truth to exceed every logit of the opposite class, informally:

\begin{cases}\text{for all $i\in F$,}\ f_{\theta}(\bm{x})_{c}\geq f_{\theta}(\bm{x})_{i}&\text{if $c\in C$}\\
\text{for all $i\in C$,}\ f_{\theta}(\bm{x})_{c}\geq f_{\theta}(\bm{x})_{i}&\text{if $c\in F$}.\end{cases}

(19)

We can formalize this in
QLL
as follows:

\text{Clothing/Footwear}:=\left(\bigwedge_{c\in C}\left(\hat{y}_{c}\multimap\bigwedge_{i\in F}y_{c}\multimap y_{i}\right)\right)\land\left(\bigwedge_{c\in F}\left(\hat{y}_{c}\multimap\bigwedge_{i\in C}y_{c}\multimap y_{i}\right)\right).

(20)

A.6
Evaluation Metrics

Following
9
, we briefly recap the formal definitions of the three different measures we use to evaluate satisfaction of constraints:

(i)

Does the constraint hold? This is a binary measure, and the answer is either true or false.

(ii)

If the constraint does not hold, how easy is it for an attacker to find a violation?

(iii)

If the constraint does not hold, how often does the average user encounter a violation?

Based on these measures, we define three concrete metrics:

constraint satisfaction
,
constraint security
, and
constraint accuracy
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be the test set,
B_{\varepsilon}(\hat{\bm{x}})=\{\bm{x}\in\real^{m}\mid\lVert\hat{\bm{x}}-\bm{x}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon\}
be the
\varepsilon
-ball around
\hat{\bm{x}}
, and
P
be the right-hand side of the implication of a constraint
\phi
of the form
\forall\bm{x}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\hat{\bm{x}})\ldotp P(\bm{x})
.
Let
\mathbbm{1}_{\phi}
be the standard indicator function which is
1
if constraint
\phi(\bm{x})
holds and
0
otherwise.
The
constraint satisfaction
metric measures the proportion of the (finite) test set for which the constraint holds.

Definition A.1

(
CSat
(
CSat
))
.

\text{CSat}(\mathcal{X})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|}\sum_{\hat{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbbm{1}_{\forall\bm{x}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\hat{\bm{x}})\ldotp P(\bm{x})}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|}\sum_{\hat{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\inf_{\bm{x}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\hat{\bm{x}})}\mathbbm{1}_{P(\bm{x})}

In contrast,
constraint security
measures the proportion of inputs in the dataset such that an attack is unable to find an adversarial example for constraint
P
. In our experiments we use the
PGD
attack, yielding a set (usually singleton) of adversarial examples
A(\bm{x})\subseteq B_{\varepsilon}(\hat{\bm{x}})
, although in general any sufficiently strong attack can be used.

Definition A.2

(
CSec
(
CSec
))
.

\text{CSec}(\mathcal{X})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|}\sum_{\hat{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbbm{1}_{\neg\exists\bm{x}\in A(\hat{\bm{x}})\ldotp\neg P(\bm{x})}

Note that since
\neg\exists\bm{x}\in A(\hat{\bm{x}})\ldotp\neg P(\bm{x})=\forall\bm{x}\in A(\hat{\bm{x}})\ldotp P(\bm{x})
, and since
A(\bm{x})\subseteq B_{\varepsilon}(\hat{\bm{x}})
, we know in general that CSat implies CSec but we cannot conclude the converse (since our attack might not find a counterexample even if it exists).

We evaluate
CSec
under two different attack settings:

(a)

self oracle
: adversarial samples are found using a
PGD
attack with a loss built from the same differentiable logic used in training; and

(b)

QLL
oracle
: adversarial samples are found using a
PGD
with a
QLL
-based loss for all experiments.

The latter is not a definite measure of constraint satisfaction (as explained above) but only provides a more uniform evaluation setting, reducing the risk that weaker attack losses overestimate constraint security by failing to find counterexamples.

Finally,
constraint accuracy
estimates the probability of a random user coming across a counter-example to the constraint, usually referred as
1
- success rate
in the robustness literature.
Let
S(\hat{\bm{x}},n)
be a set of
n
elements randomly uniformly sampled from
B_{\varepsilon}(\hat{\bm{x}})
. Then constraint accuracy is defined as:

Definition A.3

(
CAcc
(
CAcc
))
.

\text{CAcc}(\mathcal{X})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|}\sum_{\hat{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\bm{x}\in S(\hat{\bm{x}},n)}\mathbbm{1}_{P(\bm{x})}\right)

This is the least reliable metric, as random sampling in the
m
-dimensional space is not likely to find adversarial samples.

The Dice dataset is a small custom multi-label dataset consisting of
340
RGB images of size
28\times 28
.
Each image shows three visible faces of a die.
Labels are multi-hot vectors in
\{0,1\}^{6}
, where each ground-truth label contains exactly three positive entries.
The dataset was created by randomly rolling and photographing physical dice and manually annotating the visible faces in each image.
As a result, the label distribution is reasonably balanced across the six labels (c.f.
Table

8
).
We randomly split the dataset into
80\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
training and
20\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
test data, resulting in
272
training and
68
test images.
Given the small training set size, we augment the training images using colour jitter to increase variability.
We use a batch size of
16
.
We formulate the Dice task as a standard multi-label classification problem rather than predicting ordered triples of visible faces, allowing us to express relationships between labels explicitly as constraints.

For MNIST and Fashion-MNIST, we use a small convolutional network with two strided
3\times 3
convolutional layers with
4
and
8
channels, respectively.
The first convolutional layer is followed by a
\operatorname{ReLU}
activation.
The resulting representation is flattened and passed through a fully-connected layer with a
\operatorname{ReLU}
activation, followed by a final linear layer mapping to
10
logits.

The Dice dataset uses a similar architecture, except that the first convolutional layer takes RGB images as inputs, and the final linear layer maps to
6
logits.
