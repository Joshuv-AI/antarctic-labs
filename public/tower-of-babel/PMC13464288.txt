History of Present Illness

A 60-year-old woman presented to her cardiologist with palpitations, shortness of breath, and dyspnea on exertion for few months. On physical examination, she was tachycardic with a heart rate of 120 beats/min, normal S1 and S2, and no murmurs. She was fully saturated, and there was no evidence of cyanosis, clubbing, or erythrocytosis to suggest right-to-left shunt. Electrocardiographic tracing demonstrated atrial flutter with 2:1 atrioventricular block and ventricular rate of 120 beats/min. She underwent cardioversion after transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) (to exclude thrombi), which revealed right atrial dilation, moderate tricuspid regurgitation, normal right ventricle size, normal right ventricle pressure, remnants of a large unroofed coronary sinus, and a persistent left superior vena cava (LSVC) draining into the left atrium (LA). Right atrial dilation and tricuspid regurgitation on the TEE were suggestive of a left-to-right shunt.

Past Medical History

The patient was born at term without any pregnancy complications. Her only medical history included surgical repair of atrial septal defect (ASD) at 29 years of age.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included post–ASD repair complications and congenital heart diseases affecting the right side, particularly those characterized by right-to-left shunting or anomalous systemic or pulmonary venous return to the right atrium. Notable considerations included unroofed coronary sinus, total anomalous pulmonary venous return, partial anomalous pulmonary venous return, and ASDs. However, based on the initial TEE findings, Raghib syndrome remained a leading diagnostic consideration. The primary clinical concern was accurately assessing the extent of systemic desaturation and the resulting degree of cyanosis.

Investigations

A contrast study with saline administration though the left arm vein catheter was performed along with transthoracic echocardiography. The majority of the bubbles appeared within the coronary sinus before the right heart, consistent with persistent LSVC. After the bubbles appeared in the coronary sinus, they were visualized in the LA, consistent with an unroofed coronary sinus (Video 1). The patient underwent an echocardiogram with a stress test to assess oxygen saturation levels during exercise. Although her functional capacity was below average for her age, she did not undergo any desaturation events. The anatomy was confirmed by computed tomography (CT), demonstrating a normal connection of the pulmonary veins to the LA. The patient's cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a bilateral superior vena cava (SVC) with a small bridging vein, normal biventricular size and systolic function, and biatrial enlargement without valvulopathy. An LSVC was shown to connect directly to the LA in the absence of an intact coronary sinus, confirming Raghib syndrome (Figure 1). The right SVC drained normally into the right atrium. The interatrial septum was intact, with thickening in the midanterior-inferior region of the septum, suggesting the previous patch closure of the ASD.

Four-dimensional MRI (Figure 2A) was obtained to better characterize the flow direction within the LSVC and revealed a predominantly (97%) upward flow through the LSVC (left to right) with minimal (3%) downward flow through the LSVC (right to left), with a multiphasic pattern. The pulmonary-to-systemic flow ratio was ∼1. The imaging showed 2 downward flow dips: one related to the downward motion of the closed mitral valve plane in early systole, effectively drawing some blood in after it, and another related to the rapid filling of the expanding left ventricle in early diastole, again effectively drawing some LSVC blood down with it. Overall, however, the net flow into the LSVC was predominantly upward (left to right), accounting for the lack of desaturation in this patient (Figure 2B).

Management (Medical/Interventions)

Despite the presence of a bridging vein, a device occlusion of the distal LSVC was deferred given the lack of clinically significant symptoms or right-to-left shunting. The decision was made to place the patient on anticoagulation and follow closely. The patient has followed up with our adult congenital heart disease clinic regularly and has not encountered any other complications.

Discussion

Raghib syndrome is a rare congenital anomaly, and prompt recognition is crucial for effective management. The hallmarks of this syndrome include an unroofed or absent coronary sinus, ASD, and abnormal drainage of the LSVC into the LA. The underlying embryological cause of Raghib syndrome is thought to stem from the failure of the left cardinal vein to regress, resulting in the persistence of the LSVC, in combination with unroofing—absence of the coronary sinus that causes drainage of the LSVC to the LA. Under normal circumstances, the LSVC drains into the right atrium via the coronary sinus. However, in Raghib syndrome, the absence of the coronary sinus disrupts this normal drainage pathway, leading to the LSVC emptying directly into the LA. This abnormal connection allows deoxygenated systemic venous blood to mix with oxygen-rich blood in the LA, causing systemic desaturation. The presence of an ASD further complicates this condition by facilitating additional shunting between the atria.1

Patients with Raghib syndrome may present with a wide range of symptoms, with some being asymptomatic. Potentially severe complications include paradoxical embolism, cryptogenic stroke, and pulmonary hypertension. In the present case, Raghib syndrome was incidentally diagnosed after the patient presented with arrhythmia. Remarkably, the patient exhibited minimal signs of systemic desaturation and did not experience pulmonary hypertension or right heart failure, likely given closure of the ASD during adulthood. Importantly, this case represents a persistent LSVC draining into a partially unroofed coronary sinus with an interatrial communication (Raghib syndrome), where a dilated coronary sinus ostium was misdiagnosed as a secundum ASD. Closure of this communication eliminated the left-to-right outlet, resulting in obligatory LSVC drainage into the LA. Prior surgical repair in adulthood likely contributed to the misdiagnosis, highlighting the importance of multimodal imaging in such patients, where echocardiography may miss key structures.

Diagnosis of Raghib syndrome typically begins with clinical evaluation focusing on the effects of right-to-left shunting, followed by the different cardioimaging modalities. Initial assessment often involves transthoracic echocardiography followed by TEE to delineate the abnormal venous connections. Common diagnostic errors in systemic venous anomalies can result from incomplete assessment of venous structures on echocardiography. A persistent LSVC, unroofed coronary sinus, and other systemic venous anomalies may be overlooked when right atrial dilation is attributed to other causes. These errors can be clinically significant because unrecognized anomalies can lead to detrimental clinical symptoms like right-to-left shunting, paradoxical embolism, and incomplete surgical planning. This highlights the importance of comprehensive evaluation of systemic venous anomalies with multimodal imaging, particularly in patients with prior congenital heart disease.2

Additional imaging modalities, such as cardiac MRI and CT, further offer detailed 3-dimensional anatomical visualization.3,4 Cardiac catheterization for hemodynamics can be used to assess the magnitude of shunting and pulmonary vascular resistance. For those with a significant shunt or symptoms, surgical intervention may be required, which typically involves rerouting the persistent LSVC through a baffle to the right atrium and closing the ASD. Recent reports also include device occlusion of the persistent SVC.5 In this report, we illustrate the predominance of left-to-right shunting (97%) over right-to-left shunting (3%) through the LSVC in Raghib syndrome, using cardiac MRI flow mapping, which explains the lack of significant net desaturation and the clinical decision-making of continuing the anticoagulation and not pursuing surgical repair or transcatheter occlusion.

Conclusions

We present a case of Raghib syndrome, a rare congenital anomaly involving a persistent left superior vena cava draining into the left atrium, absent coronary sinus, and an atrial septal defect. Clinical manifestations vary and may include thromboembolic events, pulmonary hypertension, or arrhythmias, as seen in this patient. CT and MRI revealed predominantly left-to-right shunting, supporting a conservative, nonsurgical management approach. In this case, we highlight the importance of multimodality imaging to determine anatomy and guide management.
