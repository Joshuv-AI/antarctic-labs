Generating Place-Based Compromises Between Two Points of View
Sumanta Bhattacharyya, Francine Chen, Scott Carter, Yan-Ying Chen, Tatiana Lau, Nayeli Suseth Bravo, Monica P. Van, Kate Sieck, Charlene C. Wu

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) excel academically but struggle with social intelligence tasks, such as creating good compromises. In this paper, we present methods for generating empathically neutral compromises between two opposing viewpoints. We first compared four different prompt engineering methods using Claude 3 Opus and a dataset of 2,400 contrasting views on shared places. A subset of the generated compromises was evaluated for acceptability in a 50-participant study. We found that the best method for generating compromises between two views used external empathic similarity between a compromise and each viewpoint as iterative feedback, outperforming standard Chain of Thought (CoT) reasoning. The results indicate that the use of empathic neutrality improves the acceptability of compromises. The dataset of generated compromises was then used to train two smaller foundation models via margin-based alignment of human preferences, improving efficiency and removing the need for empathy estimation during inference.



1
Introduction

Living in liberal, plural societies requires compromise and consensus. While these two components are essential for healthy deliberation and decision-making, they are not the same. For example, while multiple parties in one household could prefer different types of meat for dinner (e.g., chicken, beef, lamb), they might come to a consensus that they all want meat for dinner, but they may also compromise by agreeing to delay eating beef and lamb for another time and eating chicken tonight, because there is only chicken in the refrigerator.

Recent research shows that LLMs can promote democratic deliberation by fostering consensus (developing statements different participants can agree on) and reducing polarization, often outperforming human mediators in clarity and inclusiveness
Tessler et al. 2024
. Additionally,
Bakker et al. 2022
show that fine-tuning LLMs to generate consensus statements from diverse individual preferences enhances group acceptance of the statements more efficiently than traditional negotiation processes.

However, these examples concern using LLMs to develop
consensus
statements across broad viewpoints. Developing
compromises
between different and opposing viewpoints, on the other hand, requires a greater degree of social intelligence that LLMs may lack
Xu et al. 2024
.
Although LLMs can produce empathetic responses
Welivita et al. 2021
;
Sabour et al. 2022
;
Majumder et al. 2020
and can generate consensus statements, they have not been used to generate neutral compromises.
Generating compromises are fundamentally different, as successful compromises (i.e., acceptable to both parties) require proposing resolutions that make both parties better off compared to in the absence of any compromise
Van Parijs 2012
. This thus requires attending to a solution space that allows for differing viewpoints to both find positives compared to their original stances.
Recent studies show that while LLMs can analyze disputes and generate intervention messages, their effectiveness as neutral mediators remain inconsistent
Tan et al. 2024
. Similar concerns arise in humanitarian frontline negotiations, where neutrality and sensitivity are critical. Interviews with practitioners highlighted that, although LLMs can synthesize large amounts of information, they may be biased, which can lead to mixed outcomes when supporting neutral negotiation processes
Ma et al. 2024
.

Example showing two contrasting views, View_A and View_B, two compromises generated for the two views using prompt engineering, and notes about each compromise. Compromise 1 balances both views and is empathicly neutral, while Compromise 2 does not.

Inspired by these earlier works,
this paper explores whether LLMs can generate
neutral compromises for contrasting views (i.e., generate compromises that do not favor either view). This ability would be valuable in scenarios where impartiality
is crucial, such as collaborative decision-making and conflict resolution.
Particularly, we investigate the following research questions:

RQ1:
Can prompt engineering be used to generate compromises that are empathically neutral between two diverse viewpoints (about a place)?

RQ2:
Can smaller, open-source LLMs replicate empathically neutral compromise generation capabilities based on a larger, proprietary model, and remove the need for explicit empathy estimation during inference?

To address these questions, we propose a task that focuses on generating compromises from a pair of contrasting views (Figure
1
). As a first exploration of this task, we use pairs of contrasting positive and negative viewpoints about safety and welcomingness of public spaces from
Chen et al. 2025
. The positive views (view_A) are about feeling either safe or welcome and the negative views (view_B) are about feeling less safe or excluded. Each viewpoint includes a participant’s proposal for improving the safety or welcomingness of a place.
In our task, generated compromises for improving safety or welcomingness between a pair of positive and negative views should:

(i)

consider both suggestions and

(ii)

be empathically neutral

.
Since greater empathy helps people to understand each other’s opinions and viewpoints, empathy is important to compromise, which requires concessions from the parties involved. While individual parties agree to a compromise because they would be worse off without it
Van Parijs 2012
, if two views are judged to
be similarly empathetic towards a compromise, or
empathically neutral
,
the probability of finding a suggested compromise acceptable should naturally increase.

To address RQ1, we explore several
prompt-based controls to guide the generation process. We investigated guiding the model toward empathic neutrality by providing explicit feedback on the empathic similarity
Shen 2023
between each of the two views and the generated compromise (Section
3.2
). When the empathic similarity scores across both views are balanced, the generated compromise is considered
empathically neutral
. A 50-participant human evaluation of a subset of the compromises was conducted to assess the acceptability of the different compromise generation approaches and showed that compromises generated by the model informed with empathic neutrality was highly preferred.

To address RQ2,
we use the compromises generated in RQ1 to explore an alignment technique to teach empathetically neutral compromise generation abilities to smaller, pretrained language models
Liu et al. 2023
;
Huang et al. 2024
Motivated by how humans internalize social norms not just by observing conforming behavior, but by distinguishing instances of norm conformation from instances of norm violation
Bicchieri 2005
and fair and unfair outcomes
Fehr and Fischbacher 2004
, we use NCE-based alignment to explicitly contrast validated compromises against inadequate alternatives.
Our experiments show that the approach improved the quality of smaller LLM-generated compromises over fine-tuning alone, which only exposes models to acceptable compromises.

In this paper we present a method that uses empathic neutrality to enhance prompt engineering to generate compromises. These compromises are then used, together with poorer quality compromises, to teach an alignment model to align smaller models.
In the following sections, we describe the different prompt-based models that we investigated and the automatic and human evaluation of the generated compromises. We also describe the method for aligning smaller language models to generate empathically neutral compromises that we investigated.

Related work

;

This study focuses on aligning model outputs in the context of neutral compromise generation. Prompt engineering is guided by the empathic similarity between viewpoints and generated compromise, and this setup is used to generate synthetic training data. This data is used for fine-tuning and aligning pretrained LLM to generate these compromises.

3.2
Empathic similarity model

We adopt the empathic similarity framework from
Shen 2023
, which computes an empathic similarity score
sim(f_{\theta}(s_{1}),f_{\theta}(s_{2}))
between pairs of stories
(s_{1},s_{2})
, where higher scores indicate greater alignment in empathic content. While
Shen 2023
used Sentence-BERT
Reimers 2019
, we replace it with the e5-large model
Wang et al. 2022
for improved sentence representation.
To adapt the similarity model to our scenario, we combined 2,000 annotated story pairs from Shen’s EMPATHICSTORIES dataset
Shen 2023
with 1,000 story pairs from the ContrastingViews dataset.
Prior work
Schopf et al. 2023
;
Hupkes et al. 2022
has shown that such pretrained encoders can support semantic generalization even when exposed to mismatched discourse styles, such as narrative and structured text.
The combined dataset of 3,000 pairs is used to train the similarity model with a 75/5/20 train/validation/test split.
During inference, this model estimates empathic similarity between a generated compromise and each of its corresponding pair of viewpoints.

3.3
LLM generation of compromise data

We aim to generate compromises for each given pair of positive and negative views for a place. Each view consists of

(i)

description of the place

(ii)

reason of the view

(iii)

suggestions of improvement

. In order to use all this information as a context for feeding into an LLM, we follow a prompt template similar to the Alpaca prompt
Taori et al. 2023
. Recent work suggests that proprietary LLMs excel at synthetic dataset generation
Cascante-Bonilla et al. 2023
;
Lei et al. 2023
;
Shi et al. 2023
with the help of task specific prompt based strategies, providing an alternative to human data collection. We use Claude 3 Opus
Anthropic 2023
for generating compromise data.

Using a single compromise as the target constrains model generalization capabilities
Gong et al. 2019
. We generated multiple candidate compromises per view pair, enhancing the model’s ability to learn diverse resolution patterns. Additionally, we observed that single prompts were inadequate (see section
E
of the appendix) for our task as they frequently focus on only one perspective, which necessitated our exploration of more sophisticated prompt engineering techniques. All three CoT-based strategies share a common structural principle designed to promote balance between the two viewpoints. Each begins by separately extracting the suggestions from view_A and view_B before identifying commonalities. This explicit decomposition ensures that both perspectives are represented in the model’s reasoning context prior to compromise generation, preventing the model from anchoring on one viewpoint, a failure mode we observed frequently with single prompts (Section
H
). The strategies differ in how they reinforce this balance using empathic similarityafter initial generation, as described below.

Chain of Thought (CoT)
: We initially used CoT
Chu et al. 2024
;
Wei et al. 2022
to identify suggestion similarities and derive compromises, as illustrated in Figure
2
. This approach, however, proved ineffective, the generated text either emphasizing a single suggestion too heavily or extending beyond their intended scope, ultimately leading to less suitable compromises.

CoT+LLM
:
LLMs have demonstrated the potential to refine their outputs through self-generated feedback, closely aligning with human refinement techniques
Madaan et al. 2024
. In this approach, we integrated LLM-based self-evaluation scores (Figure
2
). These scores measure empathic similarity between the generated compromise and each view, ideally achieving high similarity for both perspectives with minimal discrepancy. Although self-evaluation scores showed improvement, we observed negligible gains in actual compromise quality, suggesting that these scores may not reliably enhance response quality.

CoT+Feedback
: To provide more reliable feedback for refining responses than LLM-based self-evaluation, we utilize empathic similarity scores from the trained empathic similarity model (Section
3.2
). Note that while view-view pairs are used to train empathic similarity, for CoT+Feedback, the empathic similarity model is used to score a novel generated compromise against each view.
The 480 pairs of views used for evaluation of all prompting models were separate from the 1,000 ContrastingViews pairs used to train the similarity model.
The predicted scores, along with generated responses (step 1 in Figure
3
), serve as iterative feedback to improve response quality. Across iterations, both response quality and empathy similarity scores show consistent improvement, indicating greater utility for refinement than self-evaluation alone.

Please refer to Appendix
H
for the qualitative analysis of the generated collected compromises across different prompting strategies.

3.4
Candidate compromise selection

For each prompting strategy, four compromises are collected per view pair (resulting in
4\times 4=16
compromises per view pair across four prompting strategies: Single Prompt, CoT, CoT+LLM and CoT+Feedback). From this set of 16, the top four responses are selected as candidate compromises (step 2 in Figure

score_A
and
score_B
, respectively. Then empathic neutrality is computed as the absolute difference in empathic similarity
|score_{A}-score_{B}|
In an ideal state of empathic neutrality, the absolute difference between these scores approaches zero.

4
Human Evaluation of Compromise Methods

The previous evaluation indicates how well the generated compromises achieve empirical empathic neutrality as measured by comparing the empathic similarity of two opposing viewpoints. We next conduct a human evaluation.

4.1
Evaluation Methodology

For this, we asked 50 humans to rate compromises generated by the Single Prompt,
COT
, and
COT+Feedback
methods. The participants were shown pairs of statements about modifications proposed for a place, where one statement was written by someone who thought positively about the place and the other statement was written by someone who felt negatively about the place. Instead of asking directly how fair a compromise was, which could be biased by the rater’s personal perspective and from cognitive priming and carryover effects of viewing two viewpoints sequentially
Epley and Gilovich 2006
, a rater was asked to take the viewpoint of either the person who felt positively or who felt negatively for all pairs of statements presented (assigned viewpoint). The number of raters taking the positive and negative viewpoints was balanced.

For each pair of statements, raters were asked to rate on a scale of 1-100 the acceptability of each of five compromises from their assigned viewpoint. One of the “compromises” was the positive or negative statement that they were not to identify with (‘Opposing View’). The four other compromises were generated: one by Single Prompt, one by
COT
, and two by the
COT+Feedback
method. Each participant rated the compromises for five pairs of statements, providing ratings from the positive and negative viewpoints for a total of 100 pairs of statements and 400 generated compromises.
For details about the collection of compromise ratings, see Appendix
D
.

5
Aligning Foundation Models for Empathically Neutral Compromise Generation

The prompt engineered pipeline described in Section 3 demonstrates that external empathic feedback improves compromise quality. However, this pipeline relies on iterative similarity scoring at inference time, requiring access to both a proprietary LLM and a trained similarity model. In human mediation training, practitioners learn to generate fair proposals not by consulting external fairness metrics in real time, but by building internal representations of what balanced perspective-taking feels like. We pursue an analogous goal computationally: aligning smaller, pre-trained language models so that empathically neutral compromise generation becomes an intrinsic capability rather than an externally guided one.

Instead of using standard fine-tuning to teach a model to imitate target outputs, we use an approach that teaches a model to distinguish good compromises from poor ones. This choice is motivated by cognitive research on social norm acquisition showing that humans learn evaluative standards through contrastive experience, e.g., comparing norm-conforming behavior against violations
Fehr and Fischbacher 2004
;
Bicchieri 2005
.

We adopt Noise Contrastive Estimation (NCE)
Gutmann and Hyvärinen 2012
;
Chen et al. 2024
, which directly optimizes the absolute likelihood of preferred outputs without requiring a separate reference model. This is important for our task because the model must retain broad world knowledge to generate contextually appropriate compromises. In contrast, alignment methods that optimize likelihood ratios, such as DPO
Rafailov et al. 2024
, risk suppressing the absolute likelihood of even preferred outputs, degrading generation quality.

5.1
NCE based alignment

We train an NCE model using the candidate compromises preferred by human evaluators (Section
4.2
) as positive examples to align the model with verified human social preferences. The negative examples are the fine-tuned base model’s outputs, which serve as contrasting cases.

For training efficiency, all parameters are frozen except the last three layers. Let s_target denote the sum of log-probabilities that the model assigns to the tokens of the candidate compromise, and let s_hypo denote the corresponding sum for the fine-tuned base model’s own generated output. The NCE loss (Equation
1
) consists of two terms:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{nce}}=-\log\left(\frac{1}{1+e^{-s_{\text{target}}}}\right)-\log\left(\frac{1}{1+e^{s_{\text{hypo}}}}\right)

(1)

The first term encourages the model to increase s_target, raising the likelihood of producing the validated compromise. The second term encourages the model to decrease s_hypo, lowering the likelihood of generating its current, less neutral output. Together, the two terms train the model to discriminate between good and poor compromises, internalizing the preference signal that was previously provided externally by the similarity model.

Experimental setup on alignment

Datasets:
We use the ContrastingViews dataset together with the synthetic compromises generated by CoT+Feedback, described in Section
3
, to validate our alignment targets. Our smaller models are trained, when given a pair of opposing views, to mimic the compromises, whose quality was evaluated by humans in Section
4
. Thus our alignment process optimizes towards a socially valid objective. The ContrastingViews dataset together with the genereated compromises were split into training (75%), development (5%), and test (20%) subsets.

Setup:
For all the experiments, we use Llama-3.1 8B
Dubey et al. 2024
and mistral-7B instruct
Jiang et al. 2023
as base models (referred as Llama and Mistral-7b). We initially fine-tune the base models for one epoch and use this fine tuned model for further alignment tasks. We implemented a linear warm-up and cosine decay scheduler to dynamically adjust the learning rate with an initial learning rate of
3e^{-5}
. Optimization is performed using the Adam optimizer with
\beta_{1}=0.90
and
\beta_{2}=0.99
. Through empirical tuning, we found that a weight margin value of 10 was optimal for task-based loss alignment. We train for 8 and 12 epochs for the NCE-based alignment and task- loss based alignment, respectively.

(ii)

Empathic Neutrality:
We measure the "neutrality gap" (

8
Conclusion and Future Work

Our work presents an initial framework for improving the social intelligence of LLMs for the novel task of empathically neutral compromise generation. We propose and conduct human evaluation of the effectiveness of several prompting strategies, and show that use of empathic similarity improves performance. We also show that alignment methods enable smaller, open-source models (Llama-3.1 8B and Mistral-7B) to approach the compromise generation quality of Claude 3 Opus, the proprietary model used to generate the training data. To extend this work, we hope to explore socially intelligent tasks beyond place-based conflicts, including ethical or policy disagreements, and develop methods that further maximize empathy toward both viewpoints. This would help to broaden the framework’s applicability to real-world mediation scenarios.

Appendix A
Limitations

The COT+feedback approach sought to produce compromise text that is equally acceptable to both viewpoints. In the future, we would also like to include consideration of maximizing the empathy of each viewpoint to the compromise.

Our collection of compromises was constrained to keep the data collection required manageable. The compromises are generated based on two different human viewpoints for a similar type of place. Although many of the comments about a place apply generally to a place type, i.e., trails and fencing in a park, in a real scenario, the expressed viewpoints are about the same type of place, rather than exactly the same. We chose this approach because collecting compromise data for the same place would be a much larger undertaking. The prompts were based on feeling safe/unsafe or welcome/excluded. Generalization to other prompts was not done because this would have also increased the required size of the negative viewpoints. These generalizations are left for future work.

While this place based focus on safety and inclusivity enables a controlled exploration of empathy-neutral compromises, it inherently limits the framework’s immediate generalization to broader real-world scenarios, such as interpersonal conflicts, policy debates, or collaborative decision-making in non-spatial domains (i.e., ethical dilemmas in team projects or ideological disagreements in community forums). The reliance on physical "places" as the locus of conflict may overlook nuances in abstract or multifaceted disputes, potentially reducing the framework’s utility for diverse mediation contexts where viewpoints diverge on intangible issues.

To enhance generalizability, future work could expand the dataset to include contrasting viewpoints on non-place-based topics, such as political policies (i.e., environmental regulations) or social norms (i.e., workplace diversity initiatives), by scaling negative viewpoint collection through crowdsourcing platforms or synthetic augmentation techniques. This would test the framework’s robustness across domains, potentially incorporating multi-turn dialogues to simulate ongoing negotiations.

While our neutrality metric captures empathic balance, it does not account for societal biases (i.e., race, gender, class) present in inputs or LLM generations. LLMs may overlook power asymmetries or ethical subtleties due to limited social reasoning. Moreover, the dataset’s narrow domain limits generalizability. Future work should explicitly evaluate and mitigate such biases.

The Human Study comparing different compromise generation texts presented only a sample of 4 of the generated texts per viewpoint pair, rather than all 16, because humans would have difficulty rating all items reliably.

Appendix B
Ethical Considerations

Our goal in this work was to generate compromises that are equally acceptable to the two parties with different viewpoints. Similar to the general dangers of some people trusting all results generated by an LLM, the generated compromises should be viewed as suggestions for discussion areas, rather than a compromise that each person should agree to.

Our work is a first step . In the future, it should be used as an aid rather than being relied upon directly. However, a possible future misuse is in sensitive conflict scenarios, where the compromises are not used as suggestions but instead as direct solutions. This is a societal problem, similar to other issues that generative AI has created, such as the generation of fake videos of real people and the generation of mental health advice.

This section describes the iterative prompt engineering procedure used to generate collected compromises guided by a similarity model. The process consists of a fixed prompt component (computed once) and an iterative refinement loop that reuses these fixed outputs.

Output 2:

Output 3:
Identify similarities in suggestion between view_A and view_B

suggestions_A, suggestions_B, similarities (from fixed prompt)

score_A
,
score_B

Step 2: Iterative Refinement

Using the same fixed outputs, iteratively refine the compromises:

Refinement Prompt - Step 2 (Iterative)

Input:

•

suggestions_A, suggestions_B, similarities (from fixed prompt)

•

compromise (from previous iteration)

•

score_A, score_B (from similarity model)

Appendix L
Task-loss based alignment

The NCE objective (
5.1
) teaches the model to distinguish good compromises from poor ones
similar to to how exposure to norm-conforming and norm-violating behavior builds evaluative standards
Bicchieri 2005
.
However, recognizing a good compromise is not the same as producing one.
In human mediation, the ability to generate fair proposals requires more than ranking. it requires an internalized sense of what balanced proposals substantively contain. Similarly, discrimination alone can drift in a model setting i.e., a model may learn to assign high likelihood to outputs that are stylistically similar to good compromises without preserving their semantic content.

To capture this, we introduce a complementary task-loss objective that enforces not only preference ordering but also semantic fidelity to the validated compromise.

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\text{tbl}}=\max(0,s_{hypo}-s_{target}+\\
|target_{rouge}-hypo_{rouge}|\cdot w_{\text{margin}})\end{split}

(2)

The quantities
s_{target}
and
s_{hypo}
denote the model’s scores (log-likelihoods) assigned to the target compromise and the generated hypothesis, respectively.
The required preference gap is determined by the ROUGE difference. The loss encourages the target and the reference compromise and remain semantically close to it. Because both scores are produced by the same model parameters, increasing the likelihood of the target also makes the model more likely to generate target-like hypotheses over time.

In this formulation,
target_{rouge}
denotes the ROUGE score of the candidate compromise with respect to itself (used as reference), while
hypo_{rouge}
is the ROUGE score of the fine-tuned model’s generated hypothesis with respect to the corresponding candidate compromise. The term
w_{\text{margin}}
(weight margin) modulates the influence of ROUGE difference during training. Note that while ROUGE is non-differentiable, our objective avoids direct backpropagation through the metric. The term
(target_{rouge}-hypo_{rouge})
is computed during the forward pass and treated as a static scalar margin detached from the computation graph. This effectively scales the gradient applied to the log-probabilities, penalizing hypotheses proportional to their semantic deviation from the target without requiring differentiable approximations.
