1.0 Executive Summary

Produced water is a byproduct of oil and gas extraction and is known to contain lithium, a critical
mineral. Produced water can also contain other minerals and commodities that are recoverable
and have economic value. Here we focus on estimating the total value proposition of produced
water in two important areas of oil and gas production. That is, we assess the potential ‘sale price’
of all commodities that could be reasonably extracted from a unit volume of produced water. This
value estimate helps better understand the economic and technological opportunities associated
with produced water. We do not explicitly consider the cost of extraction because many
technologies are still in development. We focus our analysis on the Permian Basin and Marcellus
Formation partly because their compositions are known to differ greatly from one another such
that their waters may represent compositional endmembers. Challenges in performing our
estimates include the variability in water composition. We also make assumptions about the form,
and thus value, in which various elements can be extracted, and do not explicitly consider the cost-
savings associated with water disposal. We find that Permian Basin and Marcellus Formation
produced water have potential values of $2.00/barrel water and $6.80/barrel water, respectively,
because of the critical minerals and other commodities they contain. Water treatment and reuse
can create an additional value per barrel water in the Permian Basin by reducing the need for water
injection. Collectively, water treatment and commodity extraction can supply critical minerals while
reducing the need for wastewater injection and disposal.

We estimate the total value proposition of produced water from the Permian Basin and Marcellus
Formation based on the composition of the water and value (price) of extractable commodities.
We find that the commodities of highest value in an average barrel of produced water from both
the Permian and Marcellus are salts (NaCl, CaCl2, and MgCl2), lithium, bromine, and iodine. Unlike
the Marcellus Formation, the Permian Basin produces excess water that could be sold for
agricultural or non-oil and gas purposes if the water was sufficiently treated. This agricultural
water represents 18% of the total value of Permian Basin produced water in our estimate.
However, we do not yet consider the cost-savings associated with reducing costs for water
disposal (see section 3.1).

Marcellus Formation produced water has a higher potential value per barrel water ($6.80/bbl)
compared to the Permian Basin ($2.00/bbl) largely due to its higher concentration of all
commodities: salts, lithium, bromine, and iodine. Despite the variable composition of produced
water between locations, we find that salts make up approximately half of the total potential value
of produced water for the Marcellus and Permian. Lithium, bromine, and iodine comprise 26%, 14%,
5% and 9%, 9%, and 5% of the potential value for the Marcellus Formation and Permian Basin
produced waters, respectively.

3.0 Discussion

Although we do not link our analysis to produced water volumes, large volumes of produced water
are generated daily: ~250 million bbl/day globally (Amakiri et al., 2022) and ~50 million bbl/day in
the United States (Jiang et al., 2022). For context, this means that the U.S. produces more than
3,000 Olympic size swimming pools of produced water per day. Thus, produced water treatment
and commodity extraction, when scaled up, can have large impacts.

3.1 Water injection cost-savings

The produced water values presented here do not yet include the cost-savings associated with
wastewater injection into saltwater disposal (SWD) wells. Currently, most produced water that is
not reused by oil and gas operations for enhanced oil recovery or fracking is injected into the
subsurface SWD wells (e.g., 36% of produced water to SWD in Texas in 2017; Jiang et al., 2022)
(Neff et al., 2011; Amakiri et al., 2022). Water disposal by injection is costly, $0.50- $3.00/bbl, and
can involve transporting water large distances (e.g., Jiang et al., 2022; Morris, 2025).
Produced water disposal and injection can also create earthquakes (Elsworth et al., 2016; Keranen
and Weingarten, 2018) that can be hazardous for people and infrastructure and can impact
groundwater flow (e.g., Wang et al., 2017). Several states such as Oklahoma and New Mexico
restrict produced water injection because of injection-induced seismicity (e.g., Scanlon et al.,
2019). Texas recently created new regulations related to wastewater disposal well pressures and
these new regulations may impact disposal and associated costs in the Periman Basin
(Mccartney, 2025). Increasing water reuse and decreasing the need for produced water disposal is,
therefore, important for reducing induced seismicity risks and for the continuation and economics
of oil and gas operations.

Treatment of produced water for reuse outside of the oil and gas sector (e.g., agricultural and
industrial reuse) is happening but technical and economic challenges remain to be overcome.
Several recent papers have chronicled the challenges and opportunities of removing constituents
such as total dissolved solids, dissolved organic and inorganic compounds, oil and grease, and
polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons from produced water (Amakiri et al., 2022; Scanlon et al., 2020).
Despite these challenges, produced water treatment for beneficial reuse outside the oil and gas
sector is already occurring in some locations and constitutes approximately 1-2% of produced
water use in the U.S. (Jiang et al., 2022). For example, in California produced water from
conventional oil drilling is used for irrigation (Jiang et al., 2022). In the Marcellus Basin, Eureka
Resources had produced water treatment facilities that that recovered sodium chloride, calcium
chloride, and lithium carbonate from produced water for commercial sales (Jiang et al., 2022;
Scanlon et al., 2020), although their facilities were recently idled (Bense, 2024). The costs
associated with desalination and wastewater treatment can be higher than disposal in injection
wells (e.g., $7/bbl versus $0.75/bbl in Oklahoma; Jiang et al., 2022). However, extraction of
commodities such as lithium, bromine, salts, and iodine may help the economics of water
treatment and beneficial reuse projects.

Additionally, the amount of produced water generated per barrel of oil or gas varies greatly and
depends on the location, age of the well/reservoir, and extraction methods. For example, in Texas,
the location of most of the Permian Basin, seven barrels of water are produced per barrel of oil
(Jiang et al., 2022). In contrast, in the Marcellus most water is reinjected for oil and gas production,
rather than disposed of (e.g., Jiang et al., 2022). Overall, the economic balance between
commodity recovery and reinjection cost-savings varies from basin to basin and through time.

3.2 Measurement variability

In addition to variability between produced water basins, there is variability in the composition
(concentration of salts and metals) within produced water basins and formations. For example, in
the Marcellus, the median lithium concentration is estimated to be 65 mg/L while the 95th
percentile lithium concentration is 214 mg/L (Marza et al., 2024). The differences in concentration
matter for those pursuing mineral extraction, where critical mineral production wells may seek to
target specific brines within sedimentary strata. Recent work has started to illuminate the
geological and fluid flow processes that control lithium variability. For example, high lithium brines
have been associated with evaporation of paleo-seawater and fluid-rock reactions in some basins
(Marza et al., 2024). Additional work to understand the specific geological units that host brines
with high critical mineral contents matters for commodity extraction. That said, the representative
compositional values utilized here (Fig. 1) demonstrate the general characteristics of Permian
Basin and Marcellus Formation produced water.

3.3 Market considerations

The cost-savings associated with reduced water reinjection may eclipse the value of commodities
within a barrel of produced water in some cases. However, extraction of all commodities may help
boost the total value proposition of produced water. That is, turning waste products or

Finally, this assessment does not include the costs associated with commodity extraction or
directly consider the technical recoverability of commodities. We also do not perform full-market
analyses to understand how new supplies might affect the price of a commodity. For example,
approximately half the value of a water barrel is derived from salts (Fig. 1), and it is possible that
large volumes of salt from produced water could greatly impact the salt market. Regardless,
however, we find that the above assessment of total value proposition helps frame the potential
for produced water to provide commodities of value.

4.0 Methods

To calculate the total value proposition of produced water, we multiply the representative
concentration of critical material in the feedstock, w, by the price of the commodity that contains
the critical material

                                          𝑉(𝑖) = 𝑤(𝑖) × 𝑝𝑒 (𝑖),

where V(i) is the value of any contained element, i, in dollars per liter (later converted to dollars per
barrel), w(i) is the concentration of the element in gram/L, and pe(i) is the price of the element in
$/gram. Inherent in this calculation is the assumption that 100% of critical minerals in a feedstock
can be extracted. The total value proposition of any feedstock is thus the sum of the value of all
contained critical minerals

                                             𝑇 = ∑𝑛𝑖=1 𝑉(𝑖),

and represents the total potential economic value of a unit volume of feedstock.

A challenge in performing this calculation is that, while the elemental concentrations of critical
minerals can be measured, critical minerals are rarely sold or utilized in their elemental form. For
example, lithium is typically used and sold as lithium carbonate, LiCO3, rather than elemental Li. In
addition, most critical minerals have multiple forms in which they can exist or be extracted, and the
various forms have different processing steps and unique economic values. For example, sodium
chloride is worth $56/ton if extracted as low purity road salt versus $230/ton if extracted as higher
purity salt pan quality salt (USGS, 2025).

Our choice of commodity form used here thus has a fundamental effect on price and the total
value proposition. Table 1 shows our choice of commodity for each critical mineral used in this
study. We attempt to choose commodities forms that can be extracted with the least level of
processing and thus might be considered the “base forms” of a product. That said, these choices
are somewhat subjective because technologies to extract critical minerals are still in development
in some cases. We must also balance selection of base commodities against the availability of
price data. Price data is typically available for more processed commodity forms (USGS, 2025). We
primarily rely on publicly available price data from the 2025 USGS Mineral Commodities
Summaries (Table 1; USGS, 2025).

Water Treatment & Speciation (NEWTS) dashboard (Blondes et al., 2023; Justman et al., 2023). We
calculated representative elemental concentrations of critical minerals, w(i), by applying a
Montecarlo simulation to the produced water data from the Permian and Marcellus and following
the methods of Wendt et al. (2025) and Creason et al. (2025).
