Ibero‐Balearic plant disjunctions: genomic support for rapid and recurrent long‐distance colonizations of the endangered Diplotaxis ibicensis (Brassicaceae) despite no dispersal syndromes
Authors: L. Bezares, M. Fernández‐Mazuecos, E. Cano, J. Borràs, J. Cerca, J. Cursach, P. Vargas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Diplotaxis ibicensis is a western Mediterranean plant endemic to the Balearic Islands and a small area of the eastern Iberian Peninsula.This study investigates the biogeographical relationships among 16 populations across its entire distribution using two complementary approaches: (i) an ITS‐based phylogenetic analysis to reconstruct Diplotaxis species relationships and divergence times, and (ii) phylogenomic and population genomic analyses of D. ibicensis via genotyping‐by‐sequencing (GBS) to assess divergence among populations.The ITS phylogeny supports D. ibicensis as a monophyletic group closely related to the western Mediterranean species D. siettiana, D. brevisiliqua and D. ilorcitana. A time‐calibrated tree estimates the most recent common ancestor of these four species in the Pliocene‐Pleistocene (3.68–0.77 Ma), well after the Messinian Salinity Crisis (5.96–5.3 Ma). GBS analyses identified six well‐supported clades of D. ibicensis individuals, four of them restricted to single islands and the mainland, while two clades contained populations from different islands. These results suggest that rapid long‐distance dispersal (LDD) events occurred across permanent marine barriers, followed by secondary colonizations among distant islands, but strong isolation in others. The highest genetic diversity (π) occurs on the island of Ibiza, while the greatest genetic differentiation (F ST) is found between mainland and Cabrera island populations.Recurrent LDD events contrast with the absence of specialized LDD traits in pollen, fruits and seeds. A review of studies of Ibero‐Balearic species reveals similar patterns of LDD across the so‐called Ibiza Channel. Conservation strategies are proposed based on patterns of genetic diversity and differentiation.

INTRODUCTION

The Mediterranean Basin has a complex biogeographical history shaped by pivotal geological events (Nieto‐Feliner 2014; Vargas 2020). The Messinian Salinity Crisis (MSC) (5.96–5.33 Ma) was a fundamental Miocene event triggering isolation of the Mediterranean Sea from the Atlantic Ocean (Roveri et al. 2025). This isolation caused extreme evaporation, leading to near‐complete desiccation of the basin and facilitating the migration of terrestrial flora across exposed land bridges (Llorens & Gil 2017). Following the MSC, the Zanclean flood at the Pliocene's onset (~5.33 Ma) reconnected the Mediterranean with the Atlantic via the Strait of Gibraltar (Thompson 2020). This marine inundation fragmented former highlands into islands, isolating landmasses such as Mallorca, Sicily, Corsica and Sardinia from the European and African continents (Médail 2022). Further land connection between the Balearic Islands and the Iberian Peninsula (Ibiza Channel, hereafter) does not appear to have taken place, despite the fact that glaciations during the Late Pleistocene (~0.8–0.01 Ma) decreased sea levels and created land bridges and island chains in some other parts of the Mediterranean (Cattaneo et al. 2020). These geologic processes profoundly influenced the region's biodiversity, fostering endemism in island ecosystems.

The resulting sea barriers across the Mediterranean Basin offer an ideal geographical setting to test two major biogeographical hypotheses: vicariance and long‐distance dispersal (hereafter LDD) (Sanmartín 2014). In particular, the Ibiza Channel (a minimum distance of c. 85 km) has been a long‐term sea barrier separating the floras of the Balearic Islands and the Iberian Peninsula in the last five million years. This spatio‐temporal context offers the opportunity to evaluate whether disjunct distributions of plant species are the result of sea fragmentation of a former wider land distribution (vicariance) or more recent dispersal and colonization events (LDD). A result of long‐term isolation is the high number of taxa endemic to the Balearic Islands (180 species and subspecies in Médail & Quezel 1997; 173 species, subspecies and varieties in Rita & Payeras 2006; 103 species in Buira et al. 2017; 154 species and subspecies in Guardiola & Sáez 2023). Also interesting from a biogeographical standpoint are 22 species (Buira et al. 2017) with disjunct distributions across the Ibiza Channel, which provide opportunities to test historical connection between these islands and the eastern Iberian Peninsula. One of them is Diplotaxis ibicensis (Pau) Gómez Campo, which exhibits a fragmented distribution across the mainland (Alicante province) and several islands of the Balearic Archipelago (Formentera, Ibiza, Cabrera and Mallorca), as well as nearby islets (Moreno Saiz 2011) (Fig. 1A).

Phylogenetic and phylogeographical tools have been instrumental in reconstructing the colonization history of populations across the Ibero‐Balearic sea barrier. Indeed, several studies focused on flowering‐plant species have already described patterns of population disjunctions: Medicago citrina (Font Quer) Greuter, Silene hifacensis Rouy ex Willk., Silene cambessedesii Boiss. & Reut. and Carduncellus dianius Webb (see references in Table 1). In the last three decades, phylogenetic and phylogeographic studies have relied on fingerprinting markers (RAPDs, SSRs, ISSRs, among others) and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from few DNA regions (nuclear, plastidial) (see review in Catalán & González‐Candelas 2014). For the genus Diplotaxis, the most recent phylogeny (Tripodi 2023) used sequences from five DNA regions and included 17 species. However, this study failed to resolve species relationships due to low statistical support. As a result of new technical advances, it is now possible to access genome‐wide data through various next‐generation sequencing (NGS) techniques. One of them, genotyping‐by‐sequencing (GBS), generates numerous SNPs by sequencing regions adjacent to restriction enzyme cutting sites, which are distributed across the entire genome. As a result, high phylogenetic resolution has been obtained in fast‐evolving groups of plants (Fernández‐Mazuecos et al. 2018). In addition, the GBS approach is frequently used to infer the phylogeography and population structure at the intraspecific level (e.g., Rahmati et al. 2024).

In this study, we focused on D. ibicensis. The main goal was to analyse the genetic diversity and colonization history of D. ibicensis across its entire distribution range. We chose D. ibicensis because of its fragmented distribution across mainland and insular territories, which is ideal for biogeography hypothesis testing. Besides, its scattered populations are isolated by marine barriers, which act as strong dispersal filters for terrestrial plant species. Our working hypotheses were: (i) all populations of D. ibicensis distributed on both sides of the Ibiza Channel are the result of a single origin (monophyly); (ii) divergence between mainland and island populations coincided with the end of the Messinian due to vicariance; (iii) populations occurring in close geographic proximity share a single genetic origin due to a lack of LDD syndromes on fruits or seeds; and (iv) populations on islets harbour lower genetic diversity than those from the mainland or large islands. As some populations are endangered (Rita & Capistrós 2017), we also analysed the GBS data to evaluate their genetic diversity and differentiation, which can help propose specific conservation measures. Indeed, Diplotaxis ibicensis is a species of high conservation concern, as it is listed as a species of Community interest (Annex II of Council Directive 92/43/EEC, 21 May 1992) and included in the Spanish List of Wild Species under Special Protection (Royal Decree 139/2011, 4 February).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study species

The genus Diplotaxis (Brassiceae, Brassicaceae) comprises 32–34 species (Raza et al. 2020), most of them endemic to the Mediterranean floristic region and Cape Verde (Grillo et al. 2012; Franzke et al. 2017). Diplotaxis ibicensis (Fig. 2A) is a perennial plant with a 20–50 cm stem and capsular fruits with linear‐oblong valves that release dry seeds with no noticeable LDD syndromes (Grillo et al. 2012). A short fruit rostrum with 1(2) ovules is a key morphological character used in the past to consider this plant a variety or subspecies under D. catholica (var. ibicensis Pau and subsp. ibicensis (Pau) Font Quer) (Martínez‐Laborde 1993).

Inflorescence of Diplotaxis ibicensis (A). Habitat of D. ibicensis (B). Photographs provided by Joana Cursach.

Diplotaxis ibicensis occurs in coastal habitats of eastern Iberia (Alicante province), four islands of the Balearic Archipelago and their satellite islets. Fragmented populations are found along the coasts of Formentera (northern areas), Ibiza (northern, eastern and southern areas), Mallorca (western and southern areas), Cabrera and several satellite islets. However, there are no records of this species in Menorca and remaining islets of the Archipelago. The small populations in Alicante are located near Xàbia (Rita & Capistrós 2017) (Fig. 1B–D). As D. ibicensis is a coastal species found in sandy soils, dune systems, slopes and rocky cliffs, it is typically associated with nutrient‐rich substrates due to the presence of guano from seabird colonies (Rita & Capistrós 2017) (Fig. 2B).

Sampling

A total of 94 individuals from 16 populations (three from Alicante and 13 from the Balearic Islands) of D. ibicensis representing the entire distribution range of the species were collected in May–June 2023 (Table S1), and their leaves were dried in silica‐gel. For the phylogenetic analysis based on ITS sequences, we selected 16 samples (one per population) of D. ibicensis and added sequences from 25 Diplotaxis species and Moricandia arvensis, taken from the GenBank database (Table S2). The latter species was selected as the outgroup based on the sister relationships between Moricandia and Diplotaxis according to Hendriks et al. 2023.

For the GBS analysis, all the 94 sampled individuals of D. ibicensis were used, and one sample of D. catholica was collected and used as the outgroup because of its availability and the relatively close relationship with D. ibicensis (see below). Six individuals were sampled from each D. ibicensis population, except for a small population (Na Foradada) of Cabrera, where four individuals were sampled (Table S1). Sampling was conducted in Spain under authorization from the competent authority of the Regional Government of Balearic Islands and the Regional Government of Valencian Community, in accordance with Spanish legislation on protected species. Access to genetic resources complied with Royal Decree 124/2017 and Regulation (EU) No. 511/2014 implementing the Nagoya Protocol. The study was carried out exclusively for non‐commercial research purposes.

DNA extraction

DNA extraction was performed using commercial kits, specifically the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit and the DNeasy Plant Pro Kit (QIAGEN; Hilden, Germany). For this process, 15–20 or 30–50 mg of dry leaves were weighed, respectively. The protocol provided by the manufacturer was followed, except for the heating block incubation time, which was extended from 10 min to 3 h to increase the DNA concentration in the final volume of 200 μl. DNA concentrations were quantified using a Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen; Carlsbad, CA, USA) with the 1X dsDNA high‐sensitivity assay kit.

ITS sequencing and phylogeny

PCR amplification of the ITS region was implemented in an Eppendorf Mastercycler Epgradient S thermocycler using primers ITS5 (Sang et al. 1995) and ITS4 (White et al. 1990) and BIOTAQ polymerase (Bioline; London, UK). The reaction mixture for each sample included 0.3 μl of polymerase (5 U/μl), 0.5 μl of each primer (P1A and P4), 1 μl of DNA template, 1.4 μl of MgCl2 (50 mM), 1.6 μl of dNTPs (2.5 mM), 2.5 μl of buffer (10X), 1 μl of BSA (1 mg/ml) and 16.2 μl of H2O, resulting in a total reaction volume of 25 μl. The PCR protocol consisted of an initial denaturation of 2 min at 95 °C, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation for 1 min at 95 °C, annealing for 1 min at 56 °C and extension for 1 min at 72 °C, with a final extension of 10 min at 72 °C. The PCR products were sequenced using Sanger sequencing by Macrogen (Seoul, Korea). Electropherograms were edited and assembled using Geneious Prime version 2024.0.5. Assembled sequences were deposited in the GenBank database (accession Nos. PZ288090‐PZ288105).

Newly generated sequences and those downloaded from GenBank were aligned using MUSCLE (Edgar 2004) on the MEGA11 platform (Tamura et al. 2021). The best‐fitting substitution model was selected using jModelTest v. 2.1.10 (Darriba et al. 2012), by applying the Akaike information criterion (AIC), the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), the K parameter and the delta value. The optimal model for this dataset was SYM + G. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using maximum‐likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) methods. For ML, the IQ‐TREE web server (Trifinopoulos et al. 2016) was used. The substitution model SYM + G was selected, and bootstrap support values were calculated with 1,000 replicates. For the BI analysis, MrBayes v. 3.2.7 (Ronquist et al. 2012) was employed with two independent runs, each consisting of four MCMC chains run for 1,000,000 generations, sampling every 500 generations and discarding the first 10% as burn‐in. The SYM + G substitution model was also used. It was ensured that the average standard deviation of split frequencies was lower than 0.01, and the results were visualized in Tracer v. 1.7.2 (Rambaut et al. 2018) to confirm that the effective sample sizes (ESS) were greater than 200 for all parameters after combining the two runs. The resulting trees were visualized and edited in FigTree v. 1.4.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/).

We estimated divergence times among Diplotaxis species based on the ITS dataset using the software BEAST v. 2.7.5, which implements Bayesian dating (Bouckaert et al. 2019). To generate the input file, BEAUti v. 2.7.5 (Bouckaert et al. 2019) was used. The BEAST Model Test (Bouckaert & Drummond 2017) was selected to estimate the mutation rate and to apply a relaxed molecular clock model during the analysis. The birth–death model was chosen for tree topology, which accounts for speciation and extinction processes (Gernhard 2008). A secondary calibration point was employed based on Willis et al. (2014), who estimated that the age of the most recent common ancestor of the genera Diplotaxis and Moricandia was within the range 28–16 Ma. This prior was implemented as a uniform distribution. An additional prior was included to constrain the genus Diplotaxis as monophyletic, with Moricandia arvensis as the outgroup. The analysis was run in BEAST for 10 million generations, sampling every 1,000 generations. The results were visualized in Tracer to confirm that the ESS values exceeded 200 for all parameters. To achieve this, a 20% burn‐in was applied. A maximum clade credibility tree was generated using TreeAnnotator v. 2.7.5 (Bouckaert et al. 2019), with median node ages.

GBS data and analyses

The GBS library was prepared following the protocol by Fernández‐Mazuecos et al. (2018), which was adapted from Elshire et al. (2011). A different volume of each sample was selected to achieve the required 500 ng of DNA, for a total of 104 samples, including 9 replicates to assess reproducibility. A volume of 6 μl of a specific working adapter stock, with a particular barcode sequence, was added to each sample. The plate was centrifuged for 1 min at 2,000 rpm and samples were dehydrated for 24 h at 37 °C in a thermal block. For digestion, 20 μl of a reaction mixture were added to each sample, consisting of 0.2 μl of PstI‐HF restriction enzyme 20,000 units/ml (NEB, USA), 2 μl of 10X NEBuffer 4 and 17.8 μl of H2O. After vortex mixing, samples were incubated at 37 °C in a thermocycler overnight. For ligation, 30 μl of a reaction mixture were added to each sample, including 1 μl of T4 ligase 400,000 units/ml (NEB, USA), 5.6 μl of 10X Ligase Buffer and 23.4 μl of H2O. Samples were incubated at room temperature for 4 h, after which the ligase was inactivated in a thermocycler at 65 °C for 10 min. Then, 5 μl of each sample were pooled in a single Eppendorf tube, and 50 μl of the pool were purified using the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter; Brea, CA, USA) at a 1:1 ratio. The DNA concentration of the purified pool was quantified using Qubit, resulting in a concentration of 9.04 ng/μl. PCR amplification of the library was performed in a volume of 100 μl, including the volume corresponding to 70 ng of DNA (7.75 μl), 3.6 μl of GBS primers, 50 μl of NEB Taq 2X Master Mix and 38.65 μl of H2O. The PCR protocol consisted of the following steps: 68 °C for 5 min, 95 °C for 1 min, 19–23 cycles at 95 °C for 30 s, 65 °C for 30 s and 68 °C for 30 s, and a final step at 68 °C for 5 min. PCR products for 19, 21 and 23 cycles were purified with AMPure beads at a 0.8:1 ratio. DNA concentrations of the purified libraries were quantified with Qubit: 7.76 ng/μl, 9.44 ng/μl and 8.22 ng/μl, respectively. For quality control, 1 μl of each library was analysed using a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies; Santa Clara, CA, USA) with a High‐Sensitivity DNA assay. The library resulting from 21 PCR cycles was selected based on the absence of adapter and primer dimers and a suitable fragment size range (200–500 bp). This library was submitted to Macrogen (Seoul, Korea) for 150‐bp paired‐end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq X platform. The results of Macrogen's quality control were satisfactory, with a final DNA concentration of 8.05 ng/μl and an average fragment size of 401 bp.

Data processing was carried out on the cluster of the Centre for Scientific Computing of the Universidad Autónoma de Madrid (CCC‐UAM) using the ipyrad v.0.9.84 pipeline (Eaton & Overcast 2020). The following parameters were chosen: 90% for the clustering threshold (parameter c) and 21 for the minimum number of samples with data (parameter m) (Fernández‐Mazuecos et al. 2018). Demultiplexed sequences were deposited in the Sequence Read Archive (NCBI) under BioProject ID PRJNA1455886. Individuals of D. ibicensis with fewer than 500 loci were discarded (32 samples), as well as the nine replicates after confirming reproducibility in preliminary analyses. This resulted in a final matrix including 63 individuals, in which all sampled populations were represented. With the assembled sequences, phylogenetic and population genetic analyses were performed to evaluate the hypotheses. A phylogenetic tree was generated using a concatenation‐based maximum‐likelihood approach. This analysis was performed in RAxML via the CIPRES portal (Miller et al. 2010) with the GTRGAMMA model and an appropriate number of bootstrap replicates determined during the analysis. A distance‐based phylogenetic network was generated using SNPs and the NeighborNet algorithm (Bryant & Moulton 2004) in SplitsTree (Bryant & Moulton 2002). A Bayesian Analysis of Population Structure (BAPS) (Corander et al. 2008) was performed to discern genetic groups based on unlinked SNPs using BAPS v.6.0 (Corander et al. 2004). The BAPS files were generated in PGDSpider v.2.1.1.1 (Lischer 2017). All the parameters recommended in the software's manual by Corander et al. (2013) were used, and four successive hierarchical analyses were conducted. Finally, genetic diversity and differentiation among populations and among genetic groups were quantified in DnaSP 6.12.03 (Rozas et al. 2017). Genetic diversity within populations or genetic groups was estimated based on nucleotide diversity (π; Nei 1987), while genetic differentiation between populations or genetic groups was estimated based on the fixation index (F ST; Hudson et al. 1992).

RESULTS

ITS phylogenetic relationships and divergence times

The length of the ITS alignment for 44 individuals of Diplotaxis and one individual of Moricandia was 617 bp, including 189 variable and 118 informative sites. The two phylogenetic trees generated using ML and BI identified D. ibicensis as a strongly supported monophyletic group with high support (bootstrap support, BS = 91%; posterior probability, PP = 1) (Figs. S1 and S2). Both analyses also recovered strong support for a close relationship between D. ibicensis and three other western Mediterranean species (D. siettiana Maire, D. brevisiliqua (Coss.) Mart.‐Laborde and D. ilorcitana (Sennen) Aedo, Mart.‐Laborde & Muñoz Garm.) (BS = 100%; PP = 1) (Figs. S1 and S2, Fig. 3). These three species formed a clade that was supported in the ML analysis (BS = 85%), but not highly supported in the BI analysis (PP = 0.63).

The time‐calibrated Bayesian phylogeny showed a divergence time between D. ibicensis and its sister group (stem age for D. ibicensis populations) estimated between 3.68 and 0.77 Ma (Fig. 3), while the divergence time of the most recent common ancestor of all D. ibicensis samples (crown age; PP = 1) was estimated to be 1.58–0.26 Ma (Fig. 3).

GBS phylogeny

After GBS data filtering, a matrix was obtained for 63 individuals with coverage between 576 and 6,275 loci. The GBS phylogenetic tree generated in RAxML revealed six well‐defined genetic groups (BS ≥89%), although the relationships among them were unsupported, including those between mainland and insular populations (Fig. 4). Clade I is composed of two populations occurring on two satellite islets of Cabrera (Na Foradada and Na Redona). Clade II includes one population from Ibiza (Ses Balandres) and two from Mallorca (Es Marquès and Es Malgrats). Clade III integrates mainland populations from Alicante (Cap Prim‐Cala Barraca, Cap Negre, Portitxol Island). Clade IV is formed by two distant populations from Ibiza (Sa Roca Llisa and Es Vedrà). Clade V comprises four geographically close populations from Ibiza (Na Bosc, s'Espartar, Es Bosc, Conillera). Finally, clade VI consists of one population from Mallorca (Punta del Toro) and one from Formentera (Es Caló) (Fig. 4). The same genetic groups were retrieved in the NeighborNet phylogenetic network. In this case, the outgroup sample of D. catholica was connected to D. ibicensis through the mainland samples of clade III (Fig. 5).

Population genetic analysis

The four hierarchical BAPS analyses retrieved six genetic groups, which matched those obtained in previous analyses (Fig. 6). No admixture among groups was detected. At the population level, nucleotide diversity (π) values ranged from 0.0017 (Na Foradada) to 0.0061 (Sa Roca Llisa) (Table S3). The highest genetic differentiation (F ST = 0.524) was found between Na Foradada (Cabrera) and Es Malgrats (Mallorca), whereas the lowest one (0.094) was found on Ibiza, between Sa Roca Llisa and Es Vedrà (Table S4). At the genetic group level (considering the groups obtained from the phylogenetic tree, phylogenetic network and BAPS analysis; Figs. 4, 5, 6), the highest π value (0.00583) was found in clade IV (Table 2), composed of the populations Sa Roca Llisa and Es Vedrà, which also displayed the highest π values (Table S3). The lowest π value (0.00312) was found in clade I, composed of Cabrera islet populations. In general, F ST values were high across all pairs of genetic groups, with the highest value (0.373) observed between clades I (Na Foradada and Na Redona) and III (Cap Prim‐Cala Barraca, Cal Negre, Portitxol Island), and the lowest value (0.128) between clades V (Na Bosc, s'Espartar, Es Bosc and Conillera) and IV (Sa Roca Llisa and Es Vedrà) (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

The remarkable plant diversity observed across the Mediterranean Basin appears primarily driven by three interconnected factors: the influence of geographical restriction on diversification rates, differential clade ages and spatio‐ecological isolation (Rundel et al. 2016). These factors, related to the region's unique topography (i.e., islands, rugged peninsulas, elevated mountains), created strong isolation in the Neogene (Médail & Diadema 2009; Vargas 2020). However, geographical constraints appear to have been frequently overcome by LDD, which facilitated plant exchanges across western Mediterranean areas, particularly overcoming the significant sea barrier imposed by the Ibiza Channel, separating the Iberian Peninsula and the Balearic Islands (Nieto‐Feliner 2014; Mas et al. 2018).

The two alternative hypotheses (vicariance vs LDD) posited for D. ibicensis in our study were successfully tested based on monophyletic groups obtained by analyses of ITS and GBS data. On the one hand, the ITS phylogeny supported the hypothesis (i) of monophyly of D. ibicensis because all the ITS sequences of D. ibicensis formed a well‐supported group. On the other hand, the GBS analysis offered further resolution by grouping together individuals and populations into six well‐defined clades. However, the time‐calibrated analysis does not support the hypothesis (ii) on the temporal origin of D. ibicensis populations predating the opening of the Strait of Gibraltar about 5 million years ago. These results suggest LDD is the primary factor shaping the biogeography of this species.

Recurrent plant dispersal during the Pleistocene

The time‐calibrated phylogeny reveals that the most recent common ancestor of all populations of D. ibicensis occurred in the Pleistocene (1.58–0.26 Ma, crown node), with a divergence time with the sister group between the Late Pliocene and Pleistocene (3.68–0.77 Ma; stem node) (Fig. 3). Thus, the hypothesis of vicariance, caused by the split between mainland and island populations at the end of the Messinian Salinity Crisis (5.96–5.33 Ma), is rejected because divergence times, including the origin of this species, are estimated to have occurred much later. Estimated divergence times compatible with colonization through Messinian land bridges followed by post‐Messinian divergence have been reported for some Balearic paleoendemics, such as the monotypic genera Naufraga (Fernández‐Mazuecos et al. 2014) and Femeniasia (Villanueva Raisman et al. 2025). In D. ibicensis, however, population disjunctions appear to be the result of post‐Messinian LDD across the western Mediterranean Sea, overcoming the marine barrier of the Ibiza Channel. This interpretation is consistent with other phylogeographical studies in the western Mediterranean that infer relatively recent colonization of the Balearic Islands, underscoring the importance of LDD in the region. A LDD pattern of plants traversing the Ibiza Channel after the Messinian has been previously reported for Carduncellus dianius (Herrando‐Moraira et al. 2019) and Cheirolophus intybaceus s.l. (Garnatje et al. 2013), and suggested for Silene hifacensis (Prentice et al. 2003), Medicago citrina (Juan et al. 2004) and Silene cambessedesii (del Valle et al. 2019). Similarly, phylogeographical analyses in Cymbalaria indicate a Corsica–Sardinia origin for a polyploid clade followed by LDD events to the Balearic Islands (Carnicero et al. 2018). In addition, studies on the Helichrysum pendulum complex suggest that the occurrence of this group in the Balearic Islands is best explained by stochastic LDD events, reinforcing the importance of dispersal processes in the assembly of western Mediterranean floras (Herrando‐Moraira et al. 2017). In some other cases, such as evergreen oaks, both LDD and vicariance have been proposed (López‐de‐Heredia et al. 2005). The possibility of post‐Messinian land bridges between the Iberian Peninsula and the Balearic Islands appears unlikely. Nevertheless, eustatic changes caused by Quaternary glaciations led to lowering of sea level, reducing the distance between the coastlines of the Iberian Peninsula and the Balearic Islands (Fraile‐Jurado et al. 2023) and potentially favouring island colonization. In any case, LDD would be required to explain colonization between the Iberian Peninsula and the Balearic Islands. There is geologic evidence of land connection within the Pityuses group (Ibiza and Formentera) and within the Gymnesians group (Mallorca, Menorca and Cabrera) when the sea level dropped in the Last Glacial Maximum, but not between both island groups (Cattaneo et al. 2020). Our GBS data suggests that several LDD events are needed to account for the genetic relationships among populations and islands of the same clade: clade II includes populations from Mallorca and Ibiza; and clade VI includes populations from Mallorca and Formentera (Figs. 4, 5, 6). From a different perspective, independent clades are found on two large islands: three clades on Ibiza and two clades on Mallorca (Figs. 4, 5, 6).

Multiple LDD despite lack of diaspore syndromes

Since the early 21st century, evidence provided by molecular markers, together with phylogenetic and phylogeographical tools, have supported frequent LDD across biogeographical barriers. In particular, this approach has helped estimate a minimum number of colonization events for populations of the same species across the Mediterranean basin (López‐de‐Heredia et al. 2005; Guzmán & Vargas 2009; Coello et al. 2022). In D. ibicensis, strong genetic evidence for the six GBS clades, but lack of support for the relationships among them, is interpreted as multiple dispersal events followed by establishment in a short period of time (rapid colonizations). When combining the GBS results (Fig. 4) and the time‐calibrated ITS phylogeny (Fig. 3), the pattern of multiple LDD events and colonizations is evident not only between the Balearic Islands and the eastern Iberian Peninsula, but also among islands and islets. As a result, a minimum of three colonization events are interpreted from our genetic data: one between the Balearic Islands and the eastern Iberian Peninsula and two between eastern Balearic Islands (Mallorca‐Cabrera group) and western Balearic Islands (Ibiza‐Formentera group). This main result contrasts with the lack of an LDD syndrome for D. ibicensis, which displays dry fruits that release dry seeds with no noticeable traits favouring LDD (Grillo et al. 2012). In addition, no sticky mucilage secretion was observed following a week of hydration of seeds collected in 2024 and 2025 tested during this study (pers. obs.). Interestingly, lack of an LDD syndrome in fruits and seeds was also found in some plants displaying disjunctions across the Ibiza Channel, such as Silene hifacensis and Silene cambessedesii (dry capsules and seeds) or Medicago citrina (spiral‐shaped legumes with no spines or hooks), while Carduncellus dianius displays anemochorous fruits (achenes with pappus) related to LDD (see Table 1). The LDD pattern is more common in plants with no LDD diaspore syndrome than traditionally assumed, particularly when analysing entire mainland and island floras. Over half (51.3%) of the native flora of Europe and its islands produce diaspores without traits clearly associated with dispersal facilitation (Vargas et al. 2023), indicating that specialized morphologies are not needed for dispersal and colonization. In other words, many dispersal agents (birds, water and wind) can disperse seeds and fruits to remote territories irrespective of their morphological and physiological traits (Heleno & Vargas 2015). Recent studies propose that local conditions for establishment, such as habitat suitability and low insular competition, are equally critical as dispersal‐enhancing traits after LDD (Guzmán & Vargas 2009; Green et al. 2022). This change of paradigm in LDD (see Heleno & Vargas 2015) is exemplified by D. ibicensis, which thrives in coastal scrub with soils rich in guano deposited by bird colonies (Rita & Capistrós 2017), counterbalancing the absence of specialized dispersal traits (Cardona et al. 2025). Interestingly, a germination study of D. ibicensis revealed that the species exhibits restricted salinity tolerance and that the presence of potassium nitrate in the substrate mitigates the negative effect of sodium chloride on seed germination (Cardona et al. 2025), as also observed in other species that inhabit halonitrophilous areas (Atriplex prostrata, Khan & Ungar 1984; Crithmum maritimum, Atia et al. 2009). All these findings suggest a new case of recurrent LDD mediated by birds, particularly seagulls, which may have facilitated plant connectivity between coastal populations. In addition, seagulls may have paved the way to colonization through the deposition of nutrient‐rich guano.

Evidence for rapid colonization followed by strong isolation

Recurrent colonization by D. ibicensis appears to have taken place in a relatively short period of time (estimated crown age of 1.58–0.26 Ma). Indeed, we interpret that lack of support for basal clade relationships in both the ITS (Fig. 3) and GBS (Fig. 4) phylogenies is the result of rapid lineage divergence. There are two main causes that account for low support for clade relationships (Coddington & Scharff 1996). On the one hand, a limited amount of nucleotide substitutions can lead to low resolution in phylogenetic trees due to insufficient molecular variation (soft polytomy). On the other hand, swift patterns of colonization from a common ancestor – similar to evolutionary radiation – can produce multiple lineages with low clade support, irrespective of the molecular technique used (hard polytomy). In the case of D. ibicensis, a relatively low number of ITS sequence substitutions (one base pair) at the population level and a relatively low number of SNPs to resolve the relationships among the six clades of D. ibicensis lead us to interpret rapid dispersal and establishment processes.

Despite a pattern of recurrent colonization for D. ibicensis, most populations remained isolated since dispersal as revealed not only by two of the six well‐defined clades of the GBS phylogeny (Fig. 4), but also by moderate to high differentiation values (F ST > 0.25 in 102 of the 120 population comparisons). These results are congruent with an initial founder effect and primary restriction of subsequent immigration, which is an expected pattern according to the theory of island biogeography (Alsos et al. 2015). Nevertheless, two of the six clades encompass populations occurring on different island pairs (Mallorca‐Ibiza, Mallorca‐Formentera), which indicate a relatively recent inter‐island connection between geographically distant populations.

Conservation measures

Conservation monitoring of islands and islets is of paramount importance, since they constitute major refuge areas for endangered species (Médail 2013). The moderate to high genetic differentiation found in D. ibicensis (F ST: 0.112–0.498; see Freeland et al. 2011 for categorization values) indicates the presence of unique genotypes that may confer ecological distinctiveness to each insular population. However, it is important to emphasize that genetic units do not correspond to specific islands or geographical areas in all cases, which may complicate the application of effective conservation measures. Although the IUCN and Spanish Red lists consider this species as Least Concern (LC), regional conservation strategies should consider these genotypes as potential management units.

Regarding genetic diversity, previous studies (Ai et al. 2014) estimated that the average nucleotide diversity (π) of populations in species classified as Least Concern is 0.00395, while populations with geographically restricted distributions have an average π of 0.00368. Based on these criteria, 9–10 out of the 16 populations studied show a genetic diversity below the expected average (Table S3). A comparison of Balearic endemic species showed different genetic diversity levels (Fernández‐Mazuecos et al. 2014). Some species, such as Crepis triasii (Cambess.) Nyman (Mayol et al. 2012), Femeniasia balearica (J.J.Rodr.) Susanna (Vilatersana et al. 2007; Villanueva Raisman et al. 2025) and Naufraga balearica Constance & Cannon (Fernández‐Mazuecos et al. 2014) exhibit moderate to high variation, while low variation was found among populations of Daphne rodriguezii Texidor (Calviño‐Cancela et al. 2012), Coristospermum huteri (Porta) L.Sáez & Rosselló (López‐Pujol et al. 2013) and Silene migjornensis L.Sáez, Guasp, P.P.Ferrer, López‐Alvarado & Rosselló (Herrero et al. 2026). While our study addresses only phylogenetic divergence and genetic diversity for D. ibicensis, further investigation of insular attributes, such as lineage singularity, morphological diversity and ecological conditions, is necessary for appropriate conservation measures (Patiño et al. 2017; Vargas 2023).
