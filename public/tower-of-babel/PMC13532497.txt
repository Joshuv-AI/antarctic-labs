Highly Entangled Bottlebrush Polymer Networks
Authors: Myoeum Kim, Baiqiang Huang, Shiwang Cheng, Li‐Heng Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEntanglements are topological constraints that govern the dynamic mechanical behavior of polymer networks. Linear polymers inevitably entangle at relatively low molecular weights, whereas bottlebrush polymers—consisting of a long backbone densely grafted with many relatively short side chains—suppress entanglements, enabling solvent‐free networks with tissue‐like softness. Yet, the same steric crowding pre‐strains the backbone and renders such networks brittle. Here, we report highly entangled bottlebrush elastomers that combine extreme softness and toughness. Using short polyethylene glycol side chains, we synthesize high molecular weight bottlebrush polymers (>3 × 106 g/mol) that remain amorphous at room temperature. We identify an entanglement threshold of 2.4 × 106 g/mol with an entanglement modulus of ∼1.3 kPa, nearly 1000 times lower than that of linear counterparts. While unentangled bottlebrush networks exhibit strain‐stiffening, entangled bottlebrush networks display pronounced strain‐softening followed by delayed stiffening due to entanglement slippage. Despite their low modulus (∼1 kPa), these elastomers stretch up to ∼1800% and show a fatigue threshold of ∼63 J/m2, comparable to natural rubber. Their intrinsic fatigue strength—fatigue threshold normalized by modulus—surpasses that of highly entangled linear polymer networks by >40‐fold. These results establish a new class of soft yet tough polymer networks and provide a model system for understanding nonlinear mechanics in architecturally complex polymers.

Introduction

In a network formed by crosslinking long linear polymers, network chains impose topological constraints on each other because they cannot cross (Figure 1a) [1]. These topological constraints—called entanglements—represent a central concept in polymer physics. Similar to permanent covalent bonds, unrelaxed entanglements function as crosslinks, thereby setting up the lower bound of network stiffness (∼106 Pa) [1]. Unlike permanent covalent bonds, however, entanglements can slide along network strands. This dynamic nature governs the viscoelastic response and mechanical behavior of entangled polymer networks. For example, while unentangled polymer networks typically exhibit strain‐stiffening at large deformations, entangled polymer networks often exhibit pronounced strain‐softening prior to strain‐stiffening. When entanglements greatly outnumber covalent crosslinks (Figure 1b), the resulting linear polymer networks exhibit exceptional durability characterized by low friction, resistance to fracture, and enhanced fatigue tolerance [2].

Unlike conventional linear polymers, a bottlebrush polymer has a long linear backbone densely grafted with many relatively short linear side chains [3, 4, 5]. Analogous to sausage rather than spaghetti, a bottlebrush polymer is essentially a type of ‘fat’ linear polymer that is difficult to entangle [6]. This feature prevents the formation of entanglements, enabling extremely soft, solvent‐free networks with tunable, tissue‐mimicking stiffness (102–105 Pa) [5, 7] (Figure 1c). Yet, this very feature also makes it challenging to create entangled bottlebrush networks.

Here, we report highly entangled bottlebrush elastomers that combine extreme softness and high toughness. Using short poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) side chains (480 g/mol), we synthesize high–molecular weight (>3 × 106 g/mol) bottlebrush polymers that remain amorphous at room temperature. This contrasts with conventional linear PEG polymers, which inevitably crystallize starting at a relatively low molecular weight (MW) (∼1,000 g/mol) [8]. Rheological measurements and theoretical analyses reveal an entanglement threshold of 2.4 × 106 g/mol with an entanglement modulus of ∼1.3 kPa, nearly 1000 times lower than linear PEG at 413 K (1.8 MPa). We develop a one‐pot method to synthesize bottlebrush PEG elastomers with entanglement‐to‐crosslink ratio (ce /cx ) from ∼0.02 to 40 (Figure 1d). Unentangled bottlebrush networks exhibit linear elasticity at low strains and strain‐stiffening at large strains, reflecting reduced chain flexibility due to bottlebrush architecture. Entangled bottlebrush networks show remarkable strain‐softening from entanglement slippage. The softening diminishes at low strain rates, where stretchability decreases. Nevertheless, highly entangled bottlebrush PEG elastomers reach ∼1800% elongation at 1 s− 1 strain rate, and despite their low modulus (∼103 Pa), exhibit a fatigue threshold of 63 J/m2, comparable to natural rubber (Figure 1e and Table S1). Their intrinsic fatigue strength—fatigue threshold normalized by modulus—surpasses that of highly entangled linear polymer networks by >40‐fold (Figure 1f). Our discovery establishes a new class of soft, tough polymer networks and provides a model system for understanding nonlinear mechanics in architecturally complex polymers.

Results

Molecular Design, Synthesis, and Entanglement Threshold of Bottlebrush Networks

We design and synthesize bottlebrush PEG elastomers through free radical polymerization of acrylate‐functionalized linear PEG chains. Monofunctional PEG acrylate (MW = 480 g/mol) serves as the side chain, and a diacrylate PEG (MW 575 g/mol) acts as crosslinker (Figure 2a). Under UV irradiation in ambient air, these reactive PEG precursors copolymerize in the presence of lithium phenyl‐2,4,6‐trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) to form a bottlebrush PEG hydrogel. The hydrogel is subsequently dried in a heating oven and then a vacuum oven to remove residual water, producing solvent‐free networks, as confirmed by thermogravimetric analysis in Figure S1. The average number of monofunctional PEG side chains per network strand, nsc , corresponds to the molar ratio of side chain to crosslinker (Figure 2b).

To determine the number of side chains per entanglement, ne , we examine the dependence of network shear modulus on nsc , where entanglements remain trapped by permanent covalent crosslinks. We characterize the linear viscoelasticity of each bottlebrush PEG network to extract its equilibrium shear modulus. Using parallel‐plate geometry, we perform small amplitude oscillatory shear (SAOS) measurements from 0.1 to 100 rad/sec across various temperatures. Then, we use time‐temperature superposition [1] to construct master curves of storage, G′(ω), and loss, G′′(ω), moduli as a function of oscillatory shear frequency ω. For networks with larger nsc , we extend the temperature range to ensure that G′ reaches a clear plateau at low ω (Figure 2c and Figure S2). The plateau value of G′ at the lowest accessible frequency is taken as the equilibrium network shear modulus, G 0.

We vary nsc by more than three orders of magnitude from 102 to 2 × 105, and observe two distinct regimes in the dependence of G 0 on nsc (Figure 2d). At nsc < 5000, G 0 decreases nearly linearly with the increase of nsc , from ∼23 kPa at nsc = 100 to ∼2 kPa at nsc = 5000. By contrast, at nsc ≫ 5000, G 0 approaches a constant value of 1.3 kPa. Alternatively, we attempt to determine the entanglement threshold by measuring linear viscoelasticity of ultra‐high MW bottlebrush PEG polymers. Using reversible addition‐fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT) polymerization, we synthesize a bottlebrush PEG with nsc = 6800 and MW 3.2 × 106 g/mol (Figure 2e, proton nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H NMR) in Figures S3 and S4, gel permeation chromatography (GPC) in Figure S5). Uniaxial Tensile Behavior

Unlike crosslink points formed through covalent bonding, entanglements are topological constraints that can slide along network strands upon large deformation. To elucidate how entanglements influence mechanical behavior, we perform uniaxial tensile tests on molded dog‐bone shaped samples at a constant strain rate of 0.02/sec. In each test, the two ends of the sample are glued onto hard cardboard pieces, which are then clamped to the gripper of a tensile machine; this avoids potential damage from direct compression by the grips (Figure 3a and Movie S1).

The elastomers become increasingly stretchable as crosslink density decreases, as shown by the dependence of nominal stress σ on elongation λ in Figure 3b. Interestingly, the network stretchability exhibits two distinct regimes depending on nsc . In unentangled networks (nsc < ne ), the elongation at break, λmax, increases dramatically from 8 to 13 as nsc increases five‐fold from 1000 to 5000. By contrast, for highly entangled networks (nsc > 50, 000), λmax saturates at ∼17 (Figure 3c). This value is far below the theoretical limit for a bottlebrush network with nsc = 50, 000 side chains, λmax ≈ (nsc /nK )1/2 ≈ 62. This difference is likely because of inhomogeneity in network strand size and defects in single‐network elastomers. During deformation, stress concentrates near these defects or along shorter strands, resulting in premature network failure. Nevertheless, the stretchability of entangled bottlebrush elastomers significantly exceeds that of conventional single‐network elastomers and even highly entangled single‐network elastomer (λmax ≈ 11) [2].

The difference between unentangled and entangled bottlebrush elastomers is further manifested by their elasticity under large deformations, which can be appreciated by plotting the reduced stress, σ/(λ − 1/λ2), against reciprocal elongation, 1/λ. Unentangled elastomers exhibit linear elasticity at small deformations, followed by strain‐stiffening under large deformations (filled symbols, Figure 3d). By contrast, entangled bottlebrush elastomers exhibit pronounced strain‐softening before strain‐stiffening (open symbols, Figure 3d). The onset elongation of strain‐stiffening, λ*, increases by nearly two‐fold, from 2.5 to 4.8, as nsc increases five‐fold, from 1000 to 5000. For entangled bottlebrush polymers, increasing nsc 40‐fold from 5000 to 200,000 results in only a modest increase in λ* from 4.8 to 5.5. In this regime, λ* corresponds to a minimum reduced stress, marking the point of strongest strain‐softening (Figure 3e). The pronounced strain‐softening at intermediate deformations is attributed to the slippage of entanglements, a hallmark of entangled polymer networks [1].

Strain‐rate Dependent Mechanical Properties

To further explore the roles of entanglements in network mechanics, we quantify the stress‐elongation behavior under various strain rates. We choose a sample with nsc = 5 × 104, well above the entanglement threshold (ne ≈ 5000) and perform uniaxial tensile test at strain rates ranging three orders of magnitude from 10−3 to 2 s−1 (Figure 4a,b). The apparent Young's moduli at small strains increase dramatically with strain rate from ∼4 to 17 kPa (blue squares, Figure 4c). This behavior is common to elastomers, whose apparent modulus is determined by the number of unrelaxed modes, which increases at shorter time scales. Notably, the apparent Young's modulus increases sharply (from ∼12 to 17 kPa) at relatively high strain rates (from 100 to 2 s−1), whereas at low strain rates (10−3 to 10−1 s−1), the modulus increases only gradually (from ∼4 to ∼11 kPa). At the lowest strain rate 10−3 s−1, the apparent modulus approaches 3Ge , indicating equilibrium state of the network. This observation is consistent with the estimated relaxation time of a bottlebrush network strand, τx≈τe(nsc/ne)3≈τ0(nenK)2(nscne)3≈10 sec, where τ e is the relaxation time of an entanglement strand, τ0≈ηsbeff3/(kBT)≈9×10−8 sec is relaxation time of an effective bottlebrush PEG Kuhn monomer with beff≈2lp≈3.3 nm (Equation (2)), and η s ≈ 10−2 Pa∙s is the monomer viscosity. Consistent with this understanding, linear viscoelastic spectra reveal that the average relaxation time of the network strands is 1–10 s, as indicated by the onset of the rubbery plateau (arrow, Figure 2c).

The entangled bottlebrush elastomer becomes more stretchable at higher strain rates. As the strain rate increases from 10−3 to 2 s−1, λmax increases significantly from 10 to ∼19 (red circles, Figure 4c). This behavior reflects the dynamic nature of entanglements, which are topological constraints that cannot relax in covalently crosslinked networks but can slip along the contour of network strands, effectively acting as slip‐links connecting neighboring chains. Without deformation, these slip‐links are homogeneously distributed throughout the network. Under deformation, the slip‐links slide along the contour of entanglement tube; this process allows the slip‐links to redistribute, following the heterogeneity of covalent crosslinks. Because this slippage takes time, at higher strain rates, it is less likely for the initially homogeneously distributed slip‐links to adapt the heterogeneous distribution of covalent crosslinks. As a result, the slip‐links more effectively redistribute stress throughout the whole polymer network; this process prevents localized, amplified stress and effectively delays network failure. Conversely, when the slip‐links slide, they cannot sustain stress as effectively as covalent crosslinks, leading to strain‐softening. Consistent with this understanding, as the strain rate increases, the entangled bottlebrush elastomers exhibit more pronounced strain‐softening (Figure 4d), reflecting enhanced apparent Young's modulus at small deformations (right axis, Figure 4c) and delayed strain‐stiffening due to suppressed slip‐link sliding. Indeed, the onset of strain‐stiffening (λ*) increases from 3.8 to 7.2 at high strain rates (Figure 4e).

Energy Dissipation, Fatigue, and Strength

To evaluate the mechanical resilience of entangled bottlebrush elastomers, we perform cyclic tensile tests on the sample with nsc = 50, 000 at a fixed strain rate of 0.02/sec and progressively increasing elongation amplitudes. The stress–elongation curves show hysteresis during loading and unloading (Figure 5a). We define the energy dissipation efficiency as the ratio of the hysteresis loop area to the area under the loading curve. The energy dissipation efficiency is relatively high of ∼38% at small deformation (λ = 1.8) but decreases rapidly to ∼13% at moderate deformation (λ = 7). At large deformations (λ > 7), the energy dissipation efficiency stabilizes at ∼10% until fracture (Figure 5b). This behavior contrasts sharply with conventional polymer networks, which typically exhibit linear elasticity with negligible dissipation at small deformations but high dissipation at large deformations due to network strand rupture. This distinctive behavior is likely because of entanglements, which form an intertwined, topologically constrained network. At small deformations, the entanglements readily slide along network strands; this process generates chain friction and helps redistribute stress throughout the network, enabling efficient energy dissipation. At large deformations, however, entanglements become locked, and their fluctuations are suppressed, such that they effectively behave as permanent crosslinks. Consequently, entangled bottlebrush networks are highly elastic and exhibit minimal energy dissipation under large deformation.

The entangled bottlebrush elastomer can sustain repeated large deformations without degradation in mechanical performance. We perform cyclic tensile tests at a strain rate of 0.1/sec and fixed elongation of λ = 11, comparable to the tensile breaking strain of recently reported highly entangled linear polymer networks [2]. In the first cycle, the maximum nominal stress reaches ∼18 kPa, slightly higher than the ∼15 kPa observed in subsequent cycles. Remarkably, the stress‐elongation curves remain nearly identical over 500 loading‐unloading cycles, the maximum tested (Figure 5c). The corresponding energy dissipation efficiency is initially ∼20% but rapidly decreases and stabilizes at ∼8% from cycle 10 onward (Figure 5d). These results show that, despite its extreme softness (G∼1.3 kPa), the entangled bottlebrush elastomer possesses exceptional mechanical resilience, maintaining structural integrity and elasticity under repeated large deformations without measurable damage.

To further assess the mechanical robustness of the entangled bottlebrush elastomer, we quantify its fatigue resistance using the Rivlin‐Thomas method [12]. Rectangular specimens (30 × 5 × 2 mm, width× height ×thickness) are prepared in notched and un‐notched pairs, with the notched samples containing a 3 mm edge cut (inset, Figure 5e). We apply cyclic loading between λ0 and λ0 + Δλ, where λ0 is the initial elongation, at a constant strain rate, ε.. We measure the crack growth for up to 10,000 cycles (∼6 h), with the sample removed every 2000 cycles for optical measurement of the crack length (lc ) (Figure 5e). In parallel, un‐notched sample is stretched at the same strain rate until fracture. The energy release rate Γ is calculated as Γ(λ) = HW(λ), where H is specimen height and W(λ) is the strain energy density obtained by integrating the stress‐strain curve between λ0 and λ0 + Δλ.

We explore the dependence of fatigue strength on nsc , λ0, and ε.. We begin with conditions commonly used in literature [12], where the sample is not pre‐stretched (λ0 = 1) and the strain rate is fixed at 1/sec. For the network with nsc = 50, 000, no crack propagation occurs below an energy release rate of Γ = 54.6 J/m2 (Figure 5e). Above this threshold, crack extends gradually, and linear regression of the data yields a fatigue threshold of Γ th = 63.3 ± 0.9 J/m2 (Figure 5f). Increasing nsc to 200, 000 does not yield noticeable increase in fatigue strength with Γ th = 59.4 ± 0.8 J/m2 (Figure S6a).

Interestingly, increasing the strain rate ε. from 1/sec to 2/sec results in nearly 20% increase in Γ th to 73.6 ± J/m2 (nsc = 200, 000, λ0 = 1; Figure S6b). This behavior may be partially due to increased energy dissipation efficiency at higher strain rates (Figure S7) [13]; it is also consistent with reduced damage zone under higher loading rates in double‐network hydrogels [14]. By contrast, pre‐stretching the networks (λ0 = 2, ε. = 1/sec) decrease the fatigue strength by nearly 30%, yielding 41.1 ± 2.8 J/m2 for nsc = 50, 000 and 46.8 ± 2.2 J/m2 for nsc = 200, 000 (Figure S6c,d). These results highlight the influence of loading history and deformation conditions on fatigue resistance. Nevertheless, the fatigue strengths of highly entangled bottlebrush networks remain comparable to that of natural rubber (∼50 J/m2) [15].

Although these values are lower than that of highly entangled linear polymer networks (Figure 1e), the entangled bottlebrush elastomer is nearly 1000 times softer, with a Young's modulus of E∼4 kPa compared to that of the entangled linear polymer networks of E∼3 MPa. According to the Lake‐Thomas model, the fatigue threshold scales with the energy required for chain scission across the crack plane [15]. When a load‐bearing strand breaks, the total bond energy stored along the whole stretched chain is irreversibly dissipated. As a result, the minimum energy, Ux , required to break a network strand scales with its contour length or the number of monomers per chain, Nx : Ux∼Nx. Simultaneously, the area density of network strands is coupled with network modulus E, Σ∼nx(bNx1/2)∼Nx−1/2, where bNx1/2 is the network strand size, b is the monomer size, and nx∼E∼1/Nx is the density of network strands (or crosslink density). Combining these relations leads to a scaling relation Γth∼UxΣ∼Nx1/2∼E−1/2, indicating that softer networks with longer strands exhibit higher fatigue thresholds. This scaling applies when elasticity is dominated by permanent crosslinks (Gx ≫ Ge ), such that the modulus scales with crosslink density (E = 3G ≈ 3Gx ).

In highly entangled networks, however, the modulus is dominated by entanglements (Ge ≫ Gx ) and is nearly constant; therefore, the Γ th ∼ E −1/2 scaling no longer applies. To compare mechanical robustness of highly entangled elastomers, we define intrinsic fatigue strength, Γ th /E, which accounts for the entanglement density and describes the characteristic length scale associated with energy dissipation or stress de‐concentration (highlighted shaded regions in Figure 5g). Remarkably, the entangled bottlebrush elastomer exhibits an intrinsic fatigue strength more than 40 times higher than highly entangled linear polymer networks (Figure 1f).

The exceptional combination of softness and toughness in entangled bottlebrush elastomers likely originates from the extraordinarily high molecular weight of bottlebrush entanglements. For networks of similar stiffness or network strand density, toughness depends on the ability of polymer chains to dissipate energy by redistributing stress near the crack tip. In conventional linear polymer networks, short strands at the crack tip can only redistribute stress over limited distances (Figure 5g, i). In a highly entangled linear polymer network, longer strands deconcentrate stress more effectively, and this effect is further enhanced by entanglement slippage (Figure 5g, ii) [2]. Nevertheless, the network strand size remains relatively small. For instance, given the entanglement molecular weight of linear poly(ethylene oxide) is 2000 g/mol [1], for an entanglement to covalent crosslink ratio of 100, the network strand molecular weight is on the order of 2 × 105 g/mol. By contrast, the entanglement molecular weight of a bottlebrush polymer is extremely large. In our system, it is ∼2.5 × 106 g/mol, over 1000 times larger than that of typical linear polymers [1]. These exceptionally long network strands redistribute stress across much larger length scales, enabling elastomers that are both extremely soft and remarkably tough (Figure 5g, iii).

Conclusion

We have developed highly entangled bottlebrush PEG elastomers that combine exceptional softness, stretchability, and toughness. Using short PEG side chains, we synthesize high molecular weight (>3 × 106 g/mol) bottlebrush polymers that remain amorphous at room temperature and form solvent‐free networks spanning from unentangled to highly entangled regimes. Rheological and theoretical analyses reveal an entanglement modulus Ge ≈ 1.3 kPa, nearly three orders of magnitude lower than that of linear polymers. Unentangled networks exhibit linear elasticity followed by strain‐stiffening, whereas entangled networks display pronounced strain‐softening due to entanglement slippage. This strain‐softening diminishes at low strain rates, indicating relaxation of topological constraints.

The entangled bottlebrush elastomers show exceptional mechanical resilience and fatigue resistance. They sustain repeated large deformation (λ = 11) for over 500 cycles without measurable damage and exhibit a fatigue threshold of Γ th ≈ 63 J/m 2, comparable to that of natural rubber, despite being nearly 1000 times softer. Note that the fatigue strength is determined at relatively high strain rates, at which the networks become more dissipative and often exhibit higher fatigue strength [13]. Performing measurements at low strain rates near equilibrium conditions would allow quantifying the contribution from viscoelastic dissipation, yet this is experimentally challenging because it requires long measurement times. Nevertheless, the strain rates in our study are comparable, if not lower than, those used for highly entangled linear networks [2]. The intrinsic fatigue strength, Γ th /E, surpasses that of highly entangled linear networks by more than 40‐fold. These properties arise from the extraordinarily large entanglement molecular weight (2.5 × 106 g/mol), which enables stress redistribution over much larger length scales than conventional linear polymers.

Future directions include leveraging these highly entangled, photo‐curable bottlebrush PEG elastomers for biomedical applications, owing to inherent biocompatibility of PEG, and for advanced additive manufacturing of soft devices [16, 17]. In addition, the highly entangled bottlebrush elastomers may serve as versatile matrices for polymer nanocomposites, enabling integration of the electric, magnetic, optical, or plasmonic functionalities of inorganic nanoparticles [18]. Finally, unlike typical linear polymer network strands with molecular scale dimensions (∼10 nm), the contour length of bottlebrush entanglements reaches the micrometer scale (∼1 µm), comparable to critical fracture size occurs in hard, brittle materials [19]. Thus, entangled bottlebrush networks may serve as a model system to explore the intriguing fracture mechanics in soft materials [20]. Together, our findings establish a molecular design principle for creating soft yet mechanically robust materials by exploiting high entanglement molecular weight. This concept bridges molecular architecture with macroscopic performance, opening new avenues for next‐generation flexible electronics, bio‐integrated devices, tissue‐mimicking elastomers, and fundamental studies of nonlinear mechanics in soft materials.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Polyethylene glycol methyl ether acrylate (mPEG‐AC, 480 g/mol), polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA, 575 g/mol), 4‐((((2‐carboxyethyl)thio)carbonothioyl)thio)‐4‐cyanopentanoic acid (TTC, ≥ 95%), lithium phenyl‐2,4,6‐trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP, ≥ 95%), phenylbis(2,4,6‐trimethyl benzoyl)phosphine oxide (BAPO, ≥ 97%), 2,2’‐azobis(2‐methylpropionitrile) (AIBN, 99%), tetrahydrofuran (THF, ≥ 99.9%, anhydrous) are purchased from Sigma–Aldrich. mPEG‐AC for synthesis is purified using basic alumina columns to remove inhibitors. All other chemicals are used as received.

Synthesis of Bottlebrush PEG Melts

We synthesize bottlebrush PEG polymers following our previously reported method [21]. Briefly, mPEG‐AC (10.0 g, 20.8 mmol, 10000 equiv), TTC (0.64 mg, 1 equiv), and AIBN (0.14 mg, 0.2 equiv) are dissolved in THF (20 mL) in a 50 mL Schlenk flask. The solution is degassed by three freeze–pump–thaw cycles, sealed under N 2, and reacted at 70°C. The reaction is quenched with liquid nitrogen, yielding a product with 68% monomer conversion and a degree of polymerization, DP = 6800, as confirmed by 1H NMR (Figures S3 and S4). The polymer is purified by precipitation in diethyl ether three times.

Fabrication of Entangled Bottlebrush PEG Elastomers

We prepare bottlebrush PEG elastomers by mixing mPEG‐AC, PEGDA, and LAP in deionized water with the LAP photoinitiator fixed at 0.2 wt% relative to the monomers. The mixture is a colorless, transparent, and low‐viscosity solution. As an example, the sample with nsc = 5 × 103 is prepared by dissolving mPEG‐AC (2 g, 4.2 mmol), PEGDA (48.3 µg, 0.084 µmol), and LAP (4 mg) in 2 mL water. We sonicate the mixture for 30 min to obtain a homogeneous solution, pour it into a Teflon mold, and cover it with a glass slide to minimize evaporation. The solution is then cured under UV light (405 nm, 14 mW/cm2) at room temperature for 30 min to form a hydrogel. All samples are prepared in ambient air without oxygen protection.

To obtain a solvent‐free polymer network, we dry the hydrogel first in a heating oven at 50°C overnight, followed by additional drying in a vacuum oven at room temperature overnight. Complete solvent removal is confirmed by the absence of further mass loss upon extended drying.

Note that PEG is prone to absorbing moisture in ambient air, which is known to change the mechanical properties of linear PEG networks dramatically. This is because linear PEG with relatively high MW (>1000 g/mol) crystallizes at room temperature without solvent, and a small amount of solvent such as water would dramatically affect crystallinity of PEG and thus its mechanical properties. By contrast, in bottlebrush PEG networks, the network strands are amorphous regardless of water solvent. Consequently, the stretchability of solvent‐free networks increases only slightly by <10% compared to hydrogels with 30% water content, as shown in our prior study [17].

1H NMR Characterization

Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy was performed using a Varian NMRS 600 MHz spectrometer. We use deuterated water (D2O) as the solvent.

Gel Permeation Chromatography

We conduct gel permeation chromatography (GPC) measurements by the TOSOH EcoSEC HLC‐8320 GPC system equipped with a refractive index detector, a TSKgel SuperAWM‐H (4.6 mm × 3.5 cm, 9 µm diameter beads) mixed‐bed guard column, and two TSKgel SuperAWM‐H (6 mm × 15 cm, 9 µm diameter beads) mixed‐bed columns. 2,2,2‐trifluoroethanol (TFE) with 0.02 M sodium trifluoroacetate (NaTFAc) is used as the eluent with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. We dissolve samples at a concentration of 2 mg/mL with an injection volume of 20 µL. The polydispersity index (PDI) is determined relative to poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) standards.

Rheological Characterization

We characterize the linear viscoelastic properties of bottlebrush elastomers using a stress‐controlled rheometer (MCR 302, Anton Paar) equipped with a Peltier system (PTD200) and enclosed in an environmental chamber. The sample is prepared following the same procedure described in Section 3 (Materials and Methods). The precursor solution is poured into a Teflon mold covered with a glass slide with spacers to define a thickness of 0.8 mm. The solution is then cured for 30 min in a UV box (405 nm, 14 mW cm−2) at room temperature and subsequently dried to obtain a solvent‐free elastomer with a final thickness of approximately 0.5 mm. For the measurements, the fully cured elastomer sample is placed on the bottom plate of the rheometer. We use an 8 mm rough‐surfaced parallel‐plate geometry to minimize slippage. After lowering the upper geometry to make contact, the sample edges are trimmed to match the geometry diameter. The chamber is purged with dry nitrogen (N 2) for 20 min to prevent the adsorption of moisture to the sample. Frequency sweep measurements are performed over 0.1–100 rad s− 1 at a strain amplitude of 0.5% under N 2. Time–temperature superposition (TTS) is applied to construct master curves.

Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA)

We perform TGA using a PerkinElmer Pyris TGA 7. About 16 mg of PEG bottlebrush elastomer is loaded into a platinum pan. The sample is equilibrated at 30°C under a nitrogen atmosphere (20 mL/min) and heated from 30°C to 700°C at a rate of 10°C/min. The sample weight is continuously recorded as a function of temperature.

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC)

The thermal transitions (Tg , Tc , and Tm ) of the bottlebrush PEG melt with nsc = 6800 are measured using a temperature‐modulated differential scanning calorimeter (TMDSC, DSC250, TA Instruments). Measurements are conducted over a temperature range of 315 to 185 K at a cooling rate of 2 K min− 1 with a modulation amplitude of 1 K every 60 s. Prior to measurement, the sample is dried under vacuum at 353 K overnight. A standard aluminum DSC pan is used for the measurements. The Tg is determined from the peak of the derivative of heat capacity with respect to temperature (inset of Figure 2g), while Tc and Tm are determined from the corresponding peaks in the heat capacity curve shown in Figure 2g.

Tensile Test

We use Instron (Model No. 5966) with a 10 N load cell to measure the mechanical properties of the elastomers. We cut elastomers into dumbbell shapes with a gauge section 18 mm in length, 3 mm in width, and ∼2 mm in thickness. Uniaxial tensile tests are performed in ambient air at a strain rate of 0.02/s. Nominal stress is calculated as the tensile force divided by the initial cross‐section area of the gauge section. The same setup and conditions are used for strain‐rate‐dependent and cyclic tensile tests.

Fatigue Test

Rectangular specimens (30 mm × 5 mm × 2 mm; width × height × thickness) are prepared in both notched and unnotched forms, and a 3 mm edge cut is introduced into the notched samples. To prevent adhesion between crack surfaces during cyclic loading, mineral oil is applied to the notched surfaces prior to testing. We perform cyclic loading‐unloading between λ0 and λ0 + Δλ at strain rates of 1 and 2 s− 1 for up to 104 cycles. Various loading conditions, including tests with and without pre‐stretching, are summarized in Table S5. Crack propagation is monitored using an optical microscope, and the crack length is recorded every 2000 cycles. The corresponding results are shown in Figure S6.
