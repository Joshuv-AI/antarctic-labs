A Gas Chromatography‐Based Workflow Leads to the Detection of Hydrogenase Activity in Native Bacteria

Marius Stoeckle, Kersten S Rabe, Christof M Niemeyer

Abstract

Functional assessment of hydrogenase activity in native bacterial systems is often complicated by the requirements associated with enzyme purification, heterologous expression, and cofactor maturation. Here, we present a standardized gas chromatography‐based workflow for the direct assessment of hydrogenase activity in crude membrane and lysate fractions without enzyme purification or genetic manipulation. The workflow was applied to a defined panel of phylogenetically distinct bacterial strains. Hydrogen oxidation activity was detected in Shewanella acanthi and Insolitispirillum peregrinum. Burkholderia plantarii exhibited low but reproducible hydrogen oxidation activity despite the absence of a clear hydrogenase candidate in the annotation‐based search applied here. This observation prompted further screening of Paraburkholderia mimosarum, P. xenovorans, P. kururiensis, P. phenoliruptrix, and P. phymatum, revealing hydrogen oxidation activity across these related strains. Among the initially investigated nonmodel organisms, I. peregrinum exhibited the highest hydrogen oxidation activity and showed prolonged activity, partial oxygen tolerance, and substantial activity at elevated temperatures. Hydrogen production was consistently lower than hydrogen oxidation under the applied assay conditions. Overall, the workflow provides a practical approach for comparative screening of hydrogen‐converting activity in native bacterial fractions and can support the selection of candidate systems for further biochemical characterization.
A standardized gas chromatography–based workflow enables direct functional screening of hydrogenase activity in crude membrane and lysate fractions from native bacteria. Without purification or heterologous expression, hydrogen oxidation is detected across phylogenetically diverse organisms, highlighting the value of activity‐based screening for functional enzyme discovery.

Introduction

The global transition toward a sustainable energy economy necessitates the development of efficient, carbon‐neutral energy carriers []. Molecular hydrogen (H2) is of particular interest due to its high energy density and clean conversion into water []. The controlled oxidation of H2 is relevant for applications such as fuel cells, where its chemical energy is converted into electricity. In this context, biocatalytic H2 oxidation offers an attractive alternative to conventional noble‐metal‐based catalysis as highly efficient conversion can be achieved under mild reaction conditions while reducing the dependence on expensive metals such as platinum [, ]. Central to this approach are hydrogenases, metalloenzymes capable of catalyzing the reversible oxidation of molecular hydrogen into protons and electrons. Among these, [NiFe]‐hydrogenases are particularly notable for their preference for H2 oxidation and for their O2‐tolerance [].

The rapid expansion of genomic databases has led to the identification of a vast number of putative hydrogenase sequences across phylogenetically diverse microorganisms []. Large‐scale bioinformatic resources such as HydDB have further facilitated the classification and annotation of hydrogenases based on sequence similarity []. However, sequence‐based annotation alone does not guarantee functional activity [], and a substantial gap remains between predicted hydrogenase genes and experimentally validated enzymes. This gap is particularly pronounced because hydrogenase activity depends not only on the structural integrity of the enzyme but also on correct cofactor maturation, the presence of accessory proteins required for metal insertion, and, in many cases, stringent control of oxygen exposure [].

Heterologous expression provides an established route for the production and functional characterization of hydrogenases but can be experimentally demanding because activity often depends on correct cofactor assembly and the presence of specific maturation and accessory proteins. Common hosts such as Escherichia coli may therefore require co‐expression of the hydrogenase structural genes together with the corresponding maturation machinery to obtain active recombinant enzymes [, , , ]. Functional heterologous expression and purification have been successfully demonstrated for several hydrogenases, although the required systems can involve the coordinated expression of numerous genes and considerable experimental optimization [, ]. Direct activity screening in native cellular fractions provides a complementary approach that allows the hydrogen‐converting activity to be assessed without first establishing a heterologous expression system.

To address this challenge, a range of experimental approaches has been developed to assess hydrogenase activity. Photometric assays based on artificial electron acceptors such as benzyl viologen (BV) or methyl viologen (MV) are widely used due to their simplicity and compatibility with high‐throughput screening formats [, , ]. However, these assays provide only an indirect readout of hydrogenase activity and are therefore inherently limited in specificity []. In whole‐cell or crude extract formats, the measured signal can be influenced not only by enzymatic activity itself but also by enzyme maturation state, metal availability, growth phase, and the accessibility of the mediator to the enzyme. Moreover, other redox‐active enzymes can reduce viologens, leading to false‐positive signals [, , ].

Electrochemical approaches provide a more direct mechanistic readout by coupling hydrogenases to electrode surfaces and monitoring catalytic currents under controlled potentials [, ]. While highly informative, these methods typically require enzyme immobilization on tailored electrode materials and often involve specific surface functionalization strategies. As a result, the catalytic performance can be strongly influenced by the electrode interface, and experimental setups are not easily standardized. This limits comparability across studies and complicates routine measurements of crude or heterogeneous samples.

Taken together, currently available assay systems differ substantially in terms of specificity, experimental complexity, and compatibility with native biological samples. Photometric methods offer high throughput but rely on indirect readouts, whereas electrochemical approaches provide mechanistic insight at the expense of experimental accessibility and standardization. Gas chromatography (GC) represents a complementary strategy that enables direct quantification of molecular hydrogen while remaining compatible with complex biological samples.

Here, we establish a standardized GC‐based workflow for screening hydrogen‐converting activity in crude membrane and lysate fractions derived from native bacterial samples (Figure ). The workflow directly quantifies H2 consumption or production under defined assay conditions without requiring enzyme purification or the prior establishment of a heterologous expression system. It was applied to a panel of bacterial strains representing different hydrogenase backgrounds and benchmarked using well‐characterized reference organisms. Importantly, the workflow is intended as a functional screening approach and does not by itself identify the underlying hydrogenase genes, determine the enzyme expression or maturation status, or establish the molecular origin of the observed activity. Instead, it provides a standardized means to identify native bacterial fractions exhibiting hydrogen‐converting activity and thereby select candidate systems for subsequent biochemical and molecular characterization.

Results and Discussion

Strain Selection and Growth Analysis

To systematically evaluate the applicability of the GC‐based workflow across different biological contexts, a set of bacterial strains was selected comprising both annotation‐supported hydrogenase systems and an exploratory case lacking a clear sequence‐based prediction (Table ). The E. coli strains MC4100 and FTD147 were included as well‐characterized reference systems. MC4100 possesses functional hydrogenases, whereas FTD147 lacks hydrogenases 1–3 and served as a low‐background control under the applied conditions. Annotation‐based inspection of the genomes of Insolitispirillum peregrinum and Shewanella acanthi identified putative [NiFe]‐hydrogenases (Table ). In I. peregrinum, two distinct hydrogenase candidates were detected, corresponding to a membrane‐associated and a soluble variant, whereas S. acanthi encoded a single putative membrane‐associated hydrogenase. In contrast, no clearly annotated hydrogenase candidate was identified in Burkholderia plantarii. Nevertheless, B. plantarii was included as an exploratory strain based on the indications from independent bioinformatic analyses. Recent advances in protein embedding‐based targeted function discovery illustrate the potential of such approaches to identify hydrogenase candidates that may escape conventional sequence‐ and profile‐based annotation methods [].

Following strain selection, cultivation conditions were systematically evaluated to establish reproducible growth protocols enabling biomass generation for hydrogenase activity assays. S. acanthi is known to grow in conventional LB medium [], which yielded sufficient biomass under the tested conditions.

Two closely related strains, I. peregrinum and I. peregrinum subsp. integrum were initially evaluated to identify suitable cultivation conditions and determine which strain provided sufficient biomass for subsequent hydrogenase activity screening. Phenotypic differences between closely related bacterial strains or subspecies, such as Parageobacillus thermoglucosidasius [] and Alcaligenes eutrophus [], have been reported previously, supporting the experimental evaluation of both strains rather than assuming comparable cultivation behavior. As no growth was observed in LB or TB medium, four recommended DSMZ media (7, 37, 790, and 830) were evaluated. I. peregrinum showed superior growth under the tested conditions and was therefore selected for the subsequent hydrogenase activity assays.

Due to substantially reduced growth under anaerobic conditions, both Insolitispirillum strains were cultivated under atmospheric conditions. All experiments were performed at 30 °C as preliminary tests at 40 °C did not support growth. Cultures were inoculated to a standardized starting optical density (OD600 = 0.05), and growth performance was assessed by endpoint biomass determination after 24 h and by high‐resolution growth monitoring over 50 h (Figure S1).

Both Insolitispirillum strains reached the highest OD600 values in DSMZ medium 37 and showed moderate growth in media 790 and 830, while only minimal biomass formation was observed in medium 7. Media 37 and 790 contain elevated peptone concentrations (10 g L−1), suggesting a preference for complex nutrient sources, whereas media 7 and 830 provide only low glucose concentrations (≤1 g L−1), likely limiting biomass accumulation.

High‐resolution growth analysis provided additional insight into growth dynamics. Both strains exhibited negligible lag phases due to precultivation in the respective media. I. peregrinum displayed sustained exponential growth for approximately 15 h, followed by a stationary phase extending to approximately 40 h after inoculation. A slight increase in OD600 during late cultivation was observed, potentially associated with the formation of coccoid bodies under nutrient‐limited conditions []. In contrast, I. peregrinum subsp. integrum exhibited a shorter exponential phase of approximately 10 h and a decline in OD600 after approximately 30 h. Based on these results, DSMZ medium 37 and I. peregrinum were selected for all subsequent experiments.

A comparison of OD600 values between the two cultivation formats revealed a notable difference. The growth curves were recorded in 96‐well microplates, whereas the endpoint measurements were obtained from cultures grown in Erlenmeyer flasks. These cultivation formats differ in parameters such as aeration, gas–liquid transfer, and mixing efficiency, which can affect bacterial growth. The lower OD600 observed in the microplate cultivation may therefore result from differences in the cultivation conditions, including reduced aeration and mixing compared with flask cultivation.

Hydrogen Oxidation

Following the establishment of reproducible cultivation conditions, all strains yielded sufficient biomass for hydrogenase activity measurements. To validate the reliability and specificity of the GC‐based assay and to define suitable experimental conditions, membrane fractions of two well‐characterized E. coli reference strains were analyzed.

The strain MC4100 possesses functional hydrogenases, whereas the mutant strain FTD147 lacks hydrogenases 1–3 (ΔhyaB, ΔhybC, ΔhycE) and retains only the hyd‐4 operon (hyf). Consistent with its genotype and the applied fermentative growth conditions [, ], FTD147 showed only minimal hydrogen oxidation under all tested conditions and was therefore used as a low‐background control (Figure S2). FTD147 nevertheless provides an important reference because it was derived directly from E. coli MC4100, thereby enabling a direct comparison with the hydrogenase‐positive parental strain. In addition, Bacillus subtilis was included as an independent negative control lacking annotated hydrogenases. B. subtilis showed negligible H2 oxidation under standard assay conditions and only a low background signal at increased H2 availability.

In the assay, molecular hydrogen was oxidized to protons and electrons, and the electrons were transferred to benzyl viologen (BV; 5 mM), which was used as the standard artificial electron acceptor. Hydrogen oxidation was quantified directly by GC as the decrease in headspace H2. Calibration with defined gas standards enabled reliable quantification over a range from approximately 4 μM to 5 mM H2 (Figure S3).

Compared to direct gas purging, the use of ambient hydrogen in an anaerobic chamber (~1000 μM H2) substantially simplified handling, enabled simultaneous incubation of multiple samples under uniform conditions, and prevented oxygen exposure. This setup provided a practical balance between analytical sensitivity and experimental throughput.

In addition, GC‐based analysis could be applied directly to crude membrane and lysate fractions without prior purification, thereby reducing limitations associated with restricted accessibility of externally supplied electron acceptors, such as BV, to hydrogenases in intact cells []. The use of cellular fractions may additionally reduce physiological constraints arising from differences in the growth state or cellular metabolism [].

Sample preparation was performed under atmospheric conditions to simplify handling and broaden the applicability of the screening workflow without requiring anaerobic equipment during cell disruption and fractionation. However, this procedural choice may reduce or abolish the detectable activity of strongly O2‐sensitive hydrogenases and therefore represents a limitation when comparing hydrogenases with different O2 sensitivities. All prepared fractions were subsequently incubated under anaerobic conditions for 3.5 h prior to activity measurements; while this establishes anaerobic assay conditions, irreversible O2‐induced inactivation during sample preparation cannot be excluded. Accordingly, the workflow identifies hydrogenase activity retained after the standardized preparation procedure rather than the maximum intrinsic activity of the respective hydrogenase system.

Using MC4100 as a reference, reaction time and protein concentration were systematically evaluated (Figure ). Hydrogen consumption was detectable after 1 h, whereas 24 h of incubation yielded a substantially stronger signal and was therefore selected as the standard reaction time. This extended incubation was particularly advantageous for detecting weaker activities in nonmodel strains that might remain below the detection threshold at shorter incubation times. Protein concentrations were normalized using the Bradford assay, and decreasing protein input resulted in reduced hydrogen oxidation. Based on these results, a standard condition of 5 mg of protein mL−1 in a 5 mL reaction volume was selected for all subsequent experiments. Although normalization to total protein likely underestimates specific hydrogenase activities, it enabled rapid and standardized comparison across crude cellular fractions.

Following assay optimization, the workflow was applied to S. acanthi and I. peregrinum to evaluate its applicability across phylogenetically distinct organisms (Figure ). For S. acanthi, only the membrane fraction was analyzed, which is consistent with the presence of a single predicted membrane‐associated hydrogenase. In I. peregrinum, both soluble lysate and membrane fractions were examined, reflecting the presence of two distinct hydrogenase candidates (Table ).

Comparative analysis revealed higher hydrogen oxidation activity in the membrane fraction of I. peregrinum than in the soluble lysate fraction, with both fractions exhibiting higher overall activity levels than those of S. acanthi (Figure ). Heat‐denatured control samples showed no detectable hydrogen oxidation (Figure S4), confirming that the observed signals originate from enzymatically active proteins.

Although the primary objective of this study was to investigate H2 oxidation by [NiFe]‐hydrogenases, Clostridium pasteurianum and Nitratidesulfovibrio vulgaris Hildenborough were additionally included. These strains are known to possess distinct types of hydrogenases [, ] and thus serve as reference organisms to assess the applicability of the workflow to strains harboring different hydrogenase types, including [NiFe]‐ and [FeFe]‐hydrogenases (Figure ). In addition, the model membrane‐bound hydrogenase of Cupriavidus necator was investigated to further validate our GC‐based screening approach. The strain was cultivated as previously described [], and the membrane fraction showed comparable activity to N. vulgaris (Figure ).

The successful detection of hydrogenase activity in phylogenetically diverse organisms demonstrates the robustness of the GC‐based workflow and highlights its suitability for the comparative functional assessment of hydrogen‐converting enzymes in native cellular fractions.

Biochemical Characterization of the I. peregrinum Hydrogenase

As I. peregrinum exhibited the highest hydrogen oxidation activity, it was selected for further biochemical characterization. The suitability of different artificial electron acceptors was first evaluated (Figure ). In addition to BV, methylene blue and neutral red were tested as alternative mediators []. BV supported more than twofold higher hydrogen oxidation activity compared to the alternative electron acceptors, consistent with its more negative redox potential, which thermodynamically favors electron transfer from hydrogenases [, ].

Temperature‐dependent measurements demonstrated that the maximal hydrogen oxidation activity coincided with the optimal growth temperature of I. peregrinum (Figure ). Notably, approximately 50% of the maximal activity remained detectable at 65 °C, indicating considerable thermal robustness. Such residual activity at elevated temperatures is consistent with previous observations that hydrogenases from mesophilic organisms can exhibit substantial thermostability []. The gradual decline in activity at higher temperatures is likely influenced not only by enzyme stability but also by reduced gas solubility, lowering substrate availability.

Subsequent experiments focused on parameters relevant for potential bioelectrochemical applications, in particular, long‐term stability and oxygen tolerance (Figure ) [, ]. To assess long‐term stability, membrane and soluble fractions were incubated under strictly anaerobic conditions and analyzed after 3.5 h and 7 days. For each sample, the activity measured after 3.5 h was defined as the reference (100%), and the activity measured after 7 days was expressed relative to this initial value. Thus, the values represent the fraction of the initial activity retained during storage rather than a comparison of absolute hydrogen oxidation activities between the strains. Both hydrogenase‐containing fractions from I. peregrinum retained approximately 40% of their initial activity after 7 days, whereas the E. coli sample retained approximately 30% (Figure ). Long‐term retention of hydrogenase activity is an important consideration for biotechnological applications, and high operational stability has been demonstrated in hydrogenase‐based catalytic systems []. The robustness of E. coli Hyd1 has likewise been demonstrated over extended reaction times and a broad temperature range []. Nevertheless, reported stabilities of [NiFe]‐hydrogenases vary considerably, ranging from nearly 100% activity after 45 days [] to a 50% activity loss within 6 h [].

In addition to long‐term stability, oxygen tolerance was evaluated as a second key parameter. Due to the explosion risk associated with direct mixing of H2 and O2, an indirect approach was employed, adapted from previously described methods [, ]. Oxygen‐saturated buffer was prepared separately, transferred gas‐tight into an anaerobic chamber, and mixed with anaerobic buffer to generate defined relative oxygen levels prior to sealing. The indicated values (0%, 5%, and 50%) refer to the fraction of oxygen‐saturated buffer in the reaction mixture rather than to the gas‐phase oxygen concentration. This corresponds to approximate dissolved oxygen concentrations of ~0 μM (0%), ~55–60 μM (5%), and ~550–600 μM (50%) at room temperature []. Across all tested conditions, E. coli exhibited the highest activity levels (Figure ), consistent with the known oxygen tolerance of its native hydrogenases []. Hydrogen oxidation activity of I. peregrinum remained detectable at elevated oxygen levels, indicating partial oxygen tolerance and suggesting potential adaptation to microaerobic conditions. However, as measurements were performed using crude cellular fractions rather than purified enzymes, contributions from auxiliary components cannot be excluded.

Detection of Hydrogen Oxidation Activity in (Para‐)Burkholderia Strains

In contrast to S. acanthi and I. peregrinum, B. plantarii represented an atypical case within the selected strain panel as no hydrogenase candidate was identified based on annotation (Table ). B. plantarii is primarily known as a rice‐associated phytopathogen with a distinct repertoire of virulence and quorum‐sensing‐associated genes []. The strain was included as an exploratory case to assess whether the workflow can detect hydrogen‐converting activity in organisms lacking clear annotation‐based indications.

When the standardized assay established in Section was initially applied, only low hydrogen oxidation activity was detected in membrane fractions. Since hydrogenase expression is often associated with nutrient limitation [], the cultivation time was extended from 27 to 72 h. Under these conditions, measurable hydrogen consumption was observed under ambient H2 in the anaerobic chamber (Figure ). Although activity remained lower than that of E. coli MC4100 and I. peregrinum, it was reproducible and clearly distinguishable from the background signal of FTD147 and B. subtilis (Figure S2). The amount of oxidized H2 increased over time, indicating the presence of biologically relevant hydrogen‐oxidizing activity.

To further assess this activity, the influence of the protein concentration was examined (Figure ). Hydrogen oxidation increased with increasing protein input, supporting the conclusion that the observed signal originated from a biological catalyst present in the cellular fraction rather than from analytical noise. However, the increase was not strictly proportional, indicating that the assay did not operate under ideal substrate‐saturating conditions across the investigated protein concentration range. Previous studies have shown that apparent hydrogenase activity can depend strongly on enzyme concentration and assay geometry, particularly in systems involving gas–liquid H2 transfer [, ]. In the present assay, additional factors such as H2 availability, gas–liquid mass transfer, accessibility of the hydrogenase within the cellular fraction, and electron transfer to BV may therefore influence the observed H2 conversion.

To evaluate the effect of substrate availability, experiments were repeated under H2‐purged conditions. Increasing the H2 concentration from ~1000 to ~4500 μM resulted in enhanced hydrogen oxidation by B. plantarii (Figure ). Under these conditions, E. coli MC4100 showed rapid hydrogen oxidation within 1 h (0.75 μmol h−1 mg−1 protein), consistent with the literature values obtained using photometric assays [, ]. FTD147 exhibited minimal activity (Figure S2), confirming its suitability as a control.

Together, these results demonstrate that the GC‐based assay is sufficiently sensitive to detect low levels of hydrogen oxidation activity in organisms lacking clear annotation‐based predictions. In the case of B. plantarii, such activity would likely remain undetected in strictly annotation‐driven workflows. While the genome of B. plantarii contains an annotated nitrogenase, and nitrogenases are capable of interacting with H2 [], no physiological hydrogen oxidation rates comparable to those of hydrogenases have been reported. Accordingly, the observed activity should be interpreted conservatively as functional evidence for hydrogen oxidation rather than as a definitive assignment to a specific enzyme class.

This activity prompted us to investigate whether related members of the family Burkholderiaceae show comparable hydrogen oxidation activity. HydDB contains several Burkholderia‐lineage genomes with annotated [NiFe]‐hydrogenases. These strains are now assigned to the genus Paraburkholderia based on phylogenomic analyses separating predominantly environmental species from the mainly pathogenic lineages retained within Burkholderia []. Paraburkholderia species are metabolically versatile bacteria associated with soil and plants, and many are capable of beneficial plant interactions, including biological nitrogen fixation [, ]. Their occurrence in plant‐associated and nitrogen‐fixing systems makes hydrogenase activity particularly relevant as H2 metabolism may be linked to H2 production by nitrogenases [].

Based on their hydrogenase annotations and their ecological relevance [, ], P. mimosarum, P. xenovorans, P. kururiensis, P. phenoliruptrix, and P. phymatum were selected for experimental characterization. Following acquisition from the DSMZ, the strains were cultivated under the recommended conditions to obtain sufficient biomass (Table ) and subsequently analyzed using the standard hydrogenase activity screening (Figure ). According to the predicted hydrogenase localizations, membrane fractions were analyzed for potentially membrane‐bound hydrogenases, whereas the lysate was analyzed for soluble hydrogenases (Table ). P. kururiensis showed the highest activity of all strains tested, exceeding that of B. plantarii, which served as the reference strain (Figure ).

For P. phymatum, previous studies have demonstrated the expression of the hydrogenase genes, providing evidence for the presence of an active hydrogenase system [, ]. Our results now provide direct experimental evidence that this hydrogenase is functionally active. A closer examination of the physiological conditions described in previous studies may help to identify cultivation conditions that further promote hydrogenase expression and activity and could thus increase the activity observed in this assay.

The low activities of P. mimosarum and P. phenoliruptrix may reflect suboptimal cultivation or induction conditions rather than the absence of functional hydrogenases. Hydrogenase expression and maturation depend on physiological conditions, oxygen availability, medium composition, and nickel availability [, ]. Future optimization should therefore evaluate these factors together with the potential role of the hydrogenases in recycling nitrogenase‐derived H2 during nitrogen fixation.

The detection of hydrogen oxidation activity across different (Para‐)Burkholderia strains illustrates the value of combining standardized functional assays with broader organism selection and demonstrates the applicability of the workflow to bacterial strains with different levels of prior hydrogenase annotation.

Hydrogen Production

Motivated by the reversible catalytic nature of hydrogenases [] and to complement the hydrogen oxidation data, hydrogen production was evaluated across the investigated strains. Initial experiments focused on B. plantarii and E. coli as a reference system, followed by comparative analysis of the remaining organisms.

Hydrogen production was quantified using MV reduced by sodium dithionite as an electron donor (Figure ). For E. coli, production rates of 3.5 μmol h−1 mg protein−1 were observed, consistent with literature values ranging from 2.7 [] to 6.6 [] μmol h−1 mg protein−1. FTD147 did not show detectable hydrogen‐producing activity under the tested conditions (data not shown).

To characterize H2 production at early time points, initial measurements were conducted after 1 and 2 h of incubation (Figure ). Substantial H2 production by the E. coli reference sample was already observed within this period. These measurements were intended to assess early H2 production rather than to determine the time point of maximum production. For comparative screening across different protein concentrations and bacterial samples, a standardized 24 h endpoint was subsequently used (Figure ), providing consistent assay conditions for comparison across the investigated samples.

A clear dependence of hydrogen production on the protein concentration was observed for E. coli, confirming the reliability of the assay across varying enzyme levels. In contrast, production rates in B. plantarii showed only a weak dependence on the protein concentration, consistent with its overall lower catalytic activity. Based on these observations, subsequent assays were performed at a standardized protein concentration of 5 mg mL−1, consistent with the oxidation experiments.

Comparison with hydrogen oxidation activities revealed consistently lower production rates across all investigated strains. In particular, hydrogen production by B. plantarii (7 nmol h−1 mg protein−1; Figure ) remained substantially below its corresponding oxidation rate (~39 nmol h−1 mg protein−1; Figure ). A similar trend was observed for I. peregrinum, whereas S. acanthi exhibited activity exclu‐sively in the direction of hydrogen oxidation (Figures and ).

These results indicate a pronounced functional bias toward hydrogen oxidation among the investigated systems. This observation highlights the importance of assessing both reaction directions in functional screening workflows as analysis restricted to a single reaction mode may lead to incomplete characterization of hydrogen‐converting activity.

Conclusion

The present study establishes a standardized GC‐based workflow for the direct assessment of hydrogenase activity in native cellular fractions, enabling functional validation across phylogenetically diverse organisms without the need for enzyme purification or heterologous expression. By applying this approach to a defined panel of bacterial strains, hydrogen oxidation activity was detected in S. acanthi, I. peregrinum, and B. plantarii, demonstrating that functionally relevant hydrogen conversion can be identified even in cases where sequence‐based evidence is limited or ambiguous.

Among the investigated strains, I. peregrinum exhibited the highest hydrogen oxidation activity and showed favorable operational properties, including prolonged activity, partial oxygen tolerance, and substantial activity at elevated temperatures. These characteristics highlight its potential as a source of hydrogen‐converting biocatalysts. At the same time, E. coli consistently outperformed all newly analyzed organisms and served as an important benchmark for assay validation and performance comparison.

The ability to detect hydrogen‐converting activity directly in crude membrane and lysate fractions underscores the sensitivity and robustness of the presented workflow. In particular, the detection of low but reproducible activity in B. plantarii illustrates the value of complementing annotation‐based approaches with systematic functional screening.

Taken together, this work demonstrates that standardized, fraction‐based activity assays provide a powerful strategy for functional validation of hydrogen oxidation. Expanding this approach to a broader range of organisms and cultivation conditions is expected to further extend the accessible diversity of hydrogen‐converting systems.

In addition, the workflow provides a basis for targeted assessment of functional properties relevant to bioelectrochemical applications, including O2 robustness [, , , ]. For example, comparing hydrogenase activity following controlled aerobic and strictly anaerobic sample preparation could enable the identification of candidates that retain activity after defined O2 exposure. Such comparative screening would complement bioinformatic approaches for identifying potentially interesting hydrogenase candidates and provide a rational basis for selecting enzymes for subsequent detailed biochemical characterization and biotechnological application.

Experimental Section

Selection of Strains and Hydrogenase Systems

A set of bacterial strains was selected to enable experimental validation of hydrogenase activity across both annotation‐supported and exploratory cases.

E. coli strains MC4100 and FTD147 were included as well‐characterized reference systems. MC4100 possesses functional hydrogenases, whereas FTD147 (MC4100 ΔhyaB ΔhybC ΔhycE) lacks hydrogenases 1–3 and served as a low‐background control under the applied conditions [, , ].

S. acanthi (taxon ID: 2864212) and I. peregrinum (taxon ID: 80 876) were selected as nonmodel organisms with annotated hydrogenase‐related genes identified by querying their respective NCBI genome entries using the keyword “hydrogenase”. In I. peregrinum, two distinct [NiFe]‐hydrogenase candidates were identified, whereas S. acanthi encoded a single putative membrane‐associated hydrogenase.

In contrast, no clearly annotated hydrogenase candidate was detected in the genome of B. plantarii (taxon ID: 41899) based on this approach. Nevertheless, B. plantarii was included as an exploratory case following indications from independent bioinformatic analyses suggesting that this strain might represent an interesting candidate for functional hydrogenase screening [].

Bacterial Growth

For each strain, 100 mL of preculture was prepared in the respective growth medium and incubated overnight at 180 rpm under atmospheric conditions. On the following day, 40 mL of the preculture was used to inoculate 2 L of fresh medium. All strains used in this study and their corresponding cultivation conditions for hydrogenase production are summarized in Table .

LB and TB media were obtained commercially. The cultivation of C. necator [] and N. vulgaris Hildenborough [] was performed as previously described. The compositions of DSMZ (German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures) media are as follows:

DSMZ medium 37: 10 g L−1 peptone, 1 g L−1 succinic acid, 1 g L−1 (NH4)2SO4, 1 g L−1 MgSO4 × H2O, 0.002 g L−1 FeCl3 × 6 H2O, and 0.002 g L−1 MnSO4 × H2O; pH 6.8.

DSMZ medium 830: 0.5 g L−1 peptone, 0.5 g L−1 yeast extract, 0.5 g L−1 casamino acids, 0.5 g L−1 glucose, 0.5 g L−1 starch, 0.3 g L−1 Na‐pyruvate, 0.3 g L−1 K2HPO4, and 0.05 g L−1 MgSO4 × 7 H2O; pH 7.2.

DSMZ medium 7: 1 g L−1 peptone, 1 g L−1 glucose, and 1 g L−1 yeast extract; pH 7.0.

DSMZ medium 790: 10 g L−1 peptone, 1 g L−1 yeast extract, 2 g L−1 MgSO4 × 7 H2O, and 2 g L−1 (NH4)2SO4; pH 7.0.

DSMZ medium 1: 5 g L−1 peptone and 3 g L−1 meat extract; pH 7.

DSMZ medium 545: 2.5 g L−1 K2HPO4, 5 g L−1 NaCl, 2.5 g L−1 glucose, 3 g L−1 soy peptone, 17 g L−1 casein peptone; pH 7.3.

DSMZ medium 220: 5 g L−1 NaCl, 5 g L−1 peptone from soymeal, and 15 g L−1 peptone from casein; pH 7.3.

DSMZ medium 54b: 20 g L−1 glucose, 10 g L−1 yeast extract, and 20 g L−1 CaCO3; pH not adjusted.

After cultivation, cells were harvested under atmospheric conditions by centrifugation (10,000 × g, 10 min), resuspended in 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (KPi; K2HPO4/KH2PO4, pH 8.0), and stored at −80 °C until further use.

For high‐resolution growth curve analysis, cultures were grown overnight in the respective medium at 180 rpm. Cultures were then diluted to an OD600 of 0.05, and 150 μL aliquots were transferred into a 96‐well plate. Measurements were performed at 30 °C using a Synergy H1 plate reader (BioTek Instruments, Germany).

Sample Preparation and Cell Fractionation

Sample preparation was performed under atmospheric conditions. A total of 10 mL of thawed cell suspension was treated with approximately 1 mg each of DNase and lysozyme and incubated for 30 min at room temperature on a rotary mixer. Cells were lysed by ultrasonication on ice using a Bandelin Sonopuls device (308 μm amplitude, ~50% power) in three cycles of 30 s, each followed by 30 s of cooling on ice.

The resulting suspension was centrifuged at 45,000 × g for 60 min at 4 °C to separate the membrane fraction (pellet including cell debris) from the soluble lysate fraction (supernatant). The membrane fraction was resuspended in 4 mL of 100 mM KPi buffer.

Protein concentrations of membrane and lysate fractions were determined using a Bradford assay, as described in Section , to enable analysis of hydrogenase activity in native cellular fractions.

Protein Quantification

Protein concentrations in membrane and lysate fractions were determined using a commercial Bradford assay [] (Bio‐Rad) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Bovine serum albumin (BSA) was used to generate a calibration curve for each experiment.

Hydrogen Oxidation Assay

Hydrogen oxidation assays were performed using crude membrane and lysate fractions prepared as described in Section . Samples were transferred into an anaerobic chamber (Figure S5A; 0 ppm O2, approximately 1000 μM H2, and the rest N2) and pre‐incubated for 210 min. This condition was defined as the standard method as molecular hydrogen was supplied directly from the chamber atmosphere without the need for additional gas handling.

Reactions were set up in 20 mL GC vials (Figure S5B; Agilent) containing 5 mL of 100 mM Britton–Robinson buffer (pH 6.0) []. Protein concentrations were adjusted to final concentrations of 1, 2.5, or 5 mg mL−1. Reactions were initiated by the addition of an electron acceptor dissolved in ddH2O. BV was used as the standard electron acceptor unless stated otherwise; neutral red and methylene blue were tested as alternatives. Electron acceptors were applied at a final concentration of 5 mM.

Reaction vials were sealed gas‐tight inside the anaerobic chamber and subsequently incubated at 37 °C and 180 rpm outside the chamber until analysis (see Section ). Control samples containing buffer and an electron acceptor but no protein were prepared and handled identically.

For experiments with increased hydrogen availability, samples were flushed with a gas mixture containing approximately 4500 μM H2 (rest argon) for 120 s prior to sealing.

For oxygen tolerance experiments, buffer was pre‐saturated with O2, transferred into the anaerobic chamber, and mixed with anaerobic buffer to obtain defined oxygen concentrations prior to sealing.

Hydrogen Production Assay

Hydrogen production assays were performed using a protocol based on a method previously described []. Crude membrane and lysate fractions were prepared, as described in Section . Prior to the assay, samples were degassed by flushing with pure argon for at least 120 s to remove residual hydrogen and ensure anoxic conditions.

Reactions were set up in 20 mL GC vials containing 5 mL of 100 mM Britton–Robinson buffer (pH 6.0). Protein concentrations were adjusted as described for hydrogen oxidation assays (Section ).

Reactions were initiated by the addition of methyl viologen (MV; 10 mM) and sodium dithionite (100 mM), supplied as 1000 mM stock solutions prepared in water. These concentrations have been commonly used in previous studies [, ]. For a final reaction volume of 5 mL, 50 μL of the MV stock solution and 500 μL of the sodium dithionite stock solution were added.

Reaction vials were sealed gas‐tight and incubated at 37 °C and 180 rpm until analysis (see Section ). Control samples lacking protein were prepared and handled identically.

Hydrogen Quantification

Hydrogen was quantified in the headspace using an Agilent 8860 gas chromatograph equipped with a molecular sieve 5A column (mesh 80/100, 1.5 m × 1/16′′ × 1 mm). Argon was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 24 mL min−1, with nitrogen as the makeup gas at 1 mL min−1. The oven and detector temperatures were set to 50 and 220 °C, respectively.

Hydrogen concentrations were determined quantitatively using a calibration curve generated with defined H2 concentrations in argon (Figure S3). Chromatogram peak areas (arbitrary units, AU) were converted to H2 concentrations using the linear regression obtained from the calibration data according to the following equation: H2 (μM) = [(peak area × 13.70725) + 54.824]/22.43, where 13.70725 and 54.824 represent the slope and intercept of the linear calibration, respectively, and 22.43 corresponds to the molar gas volume under standard conditions (22.43 L mol−1).

For hydrogen oxidation assays, the H2 concentration measured in each sample was compared with the corresponding control lacking the protein. The amount of oxidized H2 was calculated as the difference between the H2 concentration in the protein‐free control and that remaining in the sample after incubation [oxidized H2 = H2(control) − H2(sample)]. Thus, “oxidized H2” refers to the decrease in H2 relative to the corresponding protein‐free control and not to the residual H2 concentration in the vial. Apparent negative values after correction were set to zero. No corresponding reference correction was applied to the hydrogen production experiments.

To calculate the absolute amount of H2, a headspace volume of 15 mL was assumed for all reactions. Hydrogenase activity was calculated from the amount of H2 consumed or produced, the reaction time, and the protein amount used in the assay and was reported as μmol H2 h−1 mg protein−1.

Relative activity was calculated by setting the amount of oxidized H2 under the respective reference condition to 100% and expressing all other measurements relative to this value according to the following equation: relative activity (%) = [oxidized H2(sample)/oxidized H2(reference)] × 100. The respective reference condition is specified in the corresponding figure caption.
