Expression of APOL1 and NOTCH2 genes in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus attending babcock University Teaching Hospital, Ogun State, Nigeria
Authors: Oluwatofunmi Jemima Bankole, Olalekan Ademola Kemiki, Olalekan John Okesanya, Jessica Modupe Adeboyejo, Tolutope Adebimpe Oso, Olawunmi Feyisayo Onifade, Ojerinde Amos Olakunle, Jane Udoka Eguzoraku, Afis Akanni Agboola, Esther Ngozi Adejumo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Type 2 diabetes mellitus is a complex metabolic disorder with rising prevalence in Nigeria. APOL1 and NOTCH2 have been implicated in glucose metabolism, adiposity, and diabetic complications, necessitating comparison of APOL1 and NOTCH2 gene expression in patients with T2DM and healthy controls. A case–control study was conducted among 50 participants attending Babcock University Teaching Hospital, Nigeria. Participants were consecutively recruited based on eligibility and sample availability during the study period. Sociodemographic, anthropometric, and clinical data were collected. Biochemical analyses included fasting plasma glucose, lipid profile, urea, creatinine, glycated haemoglobin, serum insulin, and insulin resistance estimated using Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR). Gene expression of APOL1 and NOTCH2 in peripheral blood mononuclear cells was quantified using real-time reverse transcription quantitative PCR. Group comparisons and correlation analyses were performed using parametric or non-parametric statistical methods, including Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation tests, depending on data distribution. Participants with T2DM demonstrated significantly higher fasting glucose, HbA1c, triglycerides, urea, and HOMA-IR compared with controls (p < 0.05). Cases exhibited significantly higher fasting glucose, HbA1c, triglycerides, urea, and HOMA-IR, indicating poor glycaemic control and increased insulin resistance. APOL1 expression was significantly lower in T2DM cases compared with controls (p < 0.05), whereas NOTCH2 expression did not differ significantly between groups. APOL1 expression correlated positively with adiposity measures but showed significant inverse associations with fasting glucose, HbA1c, and urea. NOTCH2 expression was negatively associated with age and fasting glucose and positively correlated with hip circumference. A moderate positive correlation was observed between APOL1 and NOTCH2 expression. These findings highlight the importance of integrating molecular markers with clinical and biochemical data to improve understanding of T2DM in African populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58768-5.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterised by persistent hyperglycaemia resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both. It represents one of the most pressing global public health challenges, with projections estimating that approximately 693 million adults will be living with diabetes by 2045. Among the various forms of diabetes, Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) accounts for about 90–95% of all cases globally and continues to rise disproportionately in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Nigeria1,2. T2DM is a heterogeneous disorder characterised by complex interactions among genetic, environmental, and metabolic factors, with impaired insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells and insulin resistance in peripheral tissues, leading to dysregulation of glucose homeostasis. T2DM is not a single disease entity but rather a spectrum of metabolic phenotypes with varying clinical presentations, complications, and responses to therapy3. Similarly, studies from other African populations have reported a growing burden of diabetes and associated cardiovascular risk factors among inmates in the Yaoundé Central Prison in Cameroon, with a diabetes prevalence of 9.4%, while sedentary lifestyle, hypertension, obesity, smoking, and alcohol consumption were identified as major associated risk factors, further highlighting the increasing metabolic disease burden within sub-Saharan Africa4.

Despite advances in glycaemic control strategies, a substantial proportion of individuals with T2DM develop acute and chronic complications, including diabetic ketoacidosis, cardiovascular disease, nephropathy, neuropathy, and retinopathy. The presence of comorbidities such as hypertension, obesity, dyslipidaemia, chronic vascular disease, and chronic kidney disease (CKD) significantly worsens clinical outcomes and contributes to increased morbidity and mortality5. These comorbidities may be concordant, sharing overlapping pathophysiological pathways with diabetes, or discordant, arising independently but compounding disease burden. Genetic factors play a critical role in the development and progression of T2DM, particularly when individuals are exposed to obesogenic and sedentary environments. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified numerous loci associated with glucose metabolism and insulin resistance, including Transcription Factor 7 Like 2 (TCF7L2), Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor Gamma (PPARG), Fat Mass and Obesity-Associated Gene (FTO), Potassium Inwardly Rectifying Channel Subfamily J Member 11 (KCNJ11), and Notch Receptor 2 (NOTCH2)6,7. However, common genetic variants explain only a modest proportion of disease heritability, suggesting that rare variants, gene–gene interactions, and differential gene expression patterns may substantially contribute to disease susceptibility and complications, intensifying interest in exploring gene expression changes associated with T2DM8.

Apolipoprotein L1 (APOL1) is a component of high-density lipoprotein encoded by the APOL1 gene located on chromosome 22. Variants of this gene, particularly the G1 and G2 renal-risk alleles, have been strongly associated with an increased risk of kidney disease, especially among individuals of African ancestry9. Given that diabetic nephropathy is a major microvascular complication of T2DM, altered expression of APOL1 may play a significant role in mediating renal injury and disease progression in diabetic patients10. The Notch signaling pathway is a highly conserved cell–cell communication system involved in cellular differentiation, proliferation, apoptosis, and tissue homeostasis. NOTCH2, one of the major receptors within this pathway, has been implicated in pancreatic β-cell development, maintenance of insulin secretion, adipocyte differentiation, and inflammatory signalling pathways associated with metabolic disease. Dysregulation of Notch signaling has been associated with impaired insulin sensitivity, β-cell dysfunction, chronic low-grade metabolic inflammation, and cardiovascular complications in individuals with T2DM11,12. Experimental studies have further demonstrated that altered Notch signaling may influence glucose homeostasis through modulation of pancreatic endocrine function and inflammatory responses within metabolic tissues, suggesting a potential role in diabetes pathogenesis and progression13,14. However, data regarding NOTCH2 gene expression in African populations living with T2DM remain limited.

In Nigeria, and particularly in semi-urban communities such as Ilishan-Remo in Ogun State, there is a growing burden of T2DM and its associated comorbidities. Despite this, molecular studies exploring gene expression patterns relevant to disease progression and complications are scarce. The lack of population-specific data limits the identification of novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets that could support personalised diabetes management strategies15. Thus, this study investigates the expression of APOL1 and NOTCH2 genes in patients with T2DM attending Babcock University Teaching Hospital, Ogun State, Nigeria.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a case–control design to evaluate the expression of APOL1 and NOTCH2 genes among individuals with T2DM compared with apparently healthy controls. The study was conducted at the Diabetes Clinic of Babcock University Teaching Hospital (BUTH), Ilishan-Remo, Ogun State, Nigeria—a tertiary healthcare facility serving communities within Ogun State and its environs—between June and August 2023. A total of 50 participants were recruited, comprising 30 individuals living with clinically diagnosed T2DM (cases) and 20 apparently healthy non-diabetic volunteers (controls). Participant recruitment was conducted consecutively during the study period based on eligibility and willingness to participate. Controls were frequency-selected from apparently healthy volunteers but were not individually matched to cases for age or hypertension status. The study was designed as an exploratory pilot molecular profiling investigation aimed at generating preliminary evidence on APOL1 and NOTCH2 gene expression patterns in a Nigerian population. Sample size determination was guided by standard statistical approaches for case–control studies, assuming 80% study power, a 5% level of significance, and an expected difference in gene expression between groups, yielding a minimum estimated sample size of approximately 50 participants16. The final recruitment size also reflected participant availability and the feasibility of molecular analyses within the study timeline.

Eligibility criteria

Participants were eligible if they provided informed consent and were either confirmed T2DM patients attending the BUTH diabetes clinic or apparently healthy individuals without a diagnosis of diabetes. Individuals who had undergone surgery within the previous three months or who declined consent were excluded.

Data and sample collection

Demographic and clinical information was obtained through interviewer-administered questionnaires. Physical activity level was assessed using a structured self-reported questionnaire adapted from standard physical activity assessment approaches. Participants were broadly categorised into sedentary, light, moderate, or vigorous activity groups based on their reported routine daily, occupational, and recreational activities, taking into consideration the frequency and perceived intensity of physical activity. Anthropometric measurements, including body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, hip circumference, neck circumference, wrist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), were obtained using standardised procedures. Blood pressure was measured using a calibrated Omron digital sphygmomanometer (Omron Healthcare Co., Kyoto, Japan) after participants had rested in a seated position for at least 5 min. Two measurements were obtained at 5-minute intervals, and the average of the readings was used for analysis. Venous blood samples (12 mL) were collected aseptically from each participant by trained laboratory personnel. Two millilitres (2 mL) were dispensed into a fluoride oxalate bottle for glucose analysis, and 5 mLs into a plain bottle for lipid profiles and ELISA. The remaining 5 mL was dispensed into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) bottles for glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) and molecular analysis. Samples were centrifuged at 3000 revolutions per minute for 5 min. Plasma, serum and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were separated into sterile Eppendorf tubes for biochemical and molecular analysis and stored at -20 °c for biochemical assays and − 80 °C for RNA-based molecular analysis until processing.

Biochemical analysis

Biochemical analyses were performed on fasting venous blood samples using standardised enzymatic and colourimetric methods in accordance with the manufacturers’ protocols (Randox Laboratories Ltd., Crumlin, United Kingdom). Plasma glucose was determined by the glucose oxidase–peroxidase method, measured spectrophotometrically at 560 nm24. Serum total cholesterol was assayed using the cholesterol esterase–cholesterol oxidase enzymatic method, while triglycerides were measured using the glycerol-3-phosphate oxidase method25,26. High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) was quantified following selective precipitation of non-HDL lipoproteins with phosphotungstic acid and magnesium ions, after which cholesterol in the supernatant was measured enzymatically27,28. Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) was calculated using the Friedewald equation when triglyceride levels were below 400 mg/dL29. Plasma creatinine was estimated using the Jaffe kinetic method based on the formation of a creatinine–picrate complex in alkaline medium with absorbance monitored at 500 nm30, while serum urea was measured using the urease–glutamate dehydrogenase enzymatic method, which quantifies the oxidation of reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) following urease-mediated hydrolysis of urea31. Glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) was determined using a direct enzymatic assay involving proteolytic digestion of haemoglobin and oxidation of glycated valine residues by recombinant fructosyl-valine oxidase, with results expressed as percentage HbA1c according to standardised calibration and international diagnostic criteria32,33.

Immunochemical assays (ELISA)

Serum insulin concentrations were quantified using a solid-phase sandwich ELISA according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Calbiotech Inc., California, USA). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and insulin concentrations were calculated from a standard calibration curve. Insulin resistance was estimated using the HOMA-IR, calculated as fasting insulin (mU/L) × fasting plasma glucose (mmol/L) / 22.5, with values > 2.5 indicating insulin resistance34,35.

Molecular analysis

PBMCs were isolated from EDTA-anticoagulated whole blood using density gradient centrifugation. Total RNA was extracted from 200 µL of PBMCs using the Norgen Biotek RNA extraction kit (Norgen Biotek Corporation, Ontario, Canada; Lot No. 603936) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Extracted RNA was stored at − 80 °C until analysis. RNA quality, purity and concentration were assessed spectrophotometrically using NanoDrop measurements with acceptable absorbance at 260/280 nm (A260/A280) purity ratios ranging from approximately 1.8 to 2.0 were considered suitable for downstream molecular analysis. RNA integrity was additionally evaluated prior to amplification. RT-qPCR analysis was performed using RNA obtained from independent biological samples collected from all study participants. Gene expression analysis of mRNA NOTCH2 and APOL1 was performed using one-step real-time reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) with the Luna Universal One-Step RT-qPCR Kit (New England Biolabs, Massachusetts, USA; Lot No. 10167670). Amplification was carried out on a LineGene Bioer 9600 RT-PCR system under optimised cycling conditions, in accordance with previously described methods36. All reactions were performed in triplicate to improve analytical reproducibility. Primer specificity was confirmed through melt curve analysis following amplification. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method, with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) serving as the endogenous reference gene for normalisation. The primer sequences used were as follows: APOL1 forward 5′-TGATAATGAGGCCTGGAACG-3′ and reverse 5′-TACTGCTGGCCTTTATCGTG-3′; NOTCH2 forward 5′-GTGTTGACTTCTGCTCTCTC-3′ and reverse 5′-AGTTGGACCTTCTCACTCA-3′; and GAPDH forward 5′-TTGGCATTGTGGAGGGTCTTA-3′ and reverse 5′-CTTGGCTGCTTCATAGCTCC-3′.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS statistical software. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. Normally distributed variables were summarised as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range). Comparisons between cases and controls were performed using the independent Student’s t-test for parametric data and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-parametric data. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way ANOVA (parametric) or Kruskal–Wallis tests (non-parametric) were applied as appropriate. Correlation analysis was conducted using either Pearson’s correlation coefficient for normally distributed variables or Spearman’s rank correlation for non-normally distributed variables; these tests were not applied concurrently to the same dataset but were selected based on data distribution. Given the exploratory nature of this pilot molecular profiling study and the relatively small sample size, formal multivariable-adjusted analyses were not performed. However, potential confounding effects of variables such as age, adiposity, and hypertension were considered during interpretation of the findings. In addition, adjustments for multiple testing were not performed for the correlation analyses; therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously as hypothesis-generating. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations and disposal

Ethical approval was obtained from the Babcock University Health Research Ethics Committee (BUHREC 569/23) before study commencement. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and confidentiality of personal and clinical information was strictly maintained throughout the study. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant institutional guidelines and regulations and in compliance with approved ethical standards. All biological samples were handled as potentially infectious materials in accordance with biosafety guidelines. Used materials were disinfected, autoclaved, and disposed of by incineration following laboratory safety protocols.

Results

Table 1 shows that participants in the case group were significantly older than those in the control group, with a higher proportion of individuals aged > 40 years among cases (72.5%) compared with controls (27.5%) (p < 0.001). Hypertension was also significantly more prevalent among cases than controls (81.8% vs. 18.2%; p = 0.019). There were no statistically significant differences between cases and controls with respect to gender (p = 0.815), ethnicity (p = 0.678), religion (p = 0.157), or family medical history (p = 0.295). The majority of the study participants had a moderate level of physical activity from self-assessment. For the case participants, 6.67% were classified as sedentary and 16.67% were classified as light based on their level of physical activity, while for the control participants, 5% of them were classified as vigorous and 20% were classified as light based on their level of physical activity (Supplementary Figures A and B). The case group demonstrated significantly higher mean systolic blood pressure values compared with the control group (p < 0.05). However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because the study populations were not matched for age, sex, or hypertension status (Supplementary Table A). Table 2 demonstrates that participants with T2DM had significantly higher fasting plasma glucose, triglyceride levels, urea, HbA1c, and HOMA-IR compared with controls (p < 0.05 for all). Mean fasting plasma glucose (8.10 ± 3.50 vs. 5.09 ± 0.48 mmol/L) and HbA1c (7.89 ± 2.59 vs. 5.49 ± 0.24%) were markedly elevated among cases. Triglyceride levels and insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) were also significantly higher in cases than controls, while total cholesterol, HDL-cholesterol, LDL-cholesterol, creatinine, and serum insulin showed no statistically significant differences between the two groups (p > 0.05). Supplementary Figures C and D present the gene expression comparisons between participants with T2DM (cases) and controls. APOL1 expression was significantly lower in cases compared with controls (p < 0.05), whereas NOTCH2 expression did not differ significantly between the two groups.

Correlation analysis (Supplementary Table B) indicated that systolic blood pressure was positively associated with serum insulin and HOMA-IR, while diastolic blood pressure correlated with HbA1c. BMI and total body fat mass were significantly related to serum insulin, with fat mass also linked to HOMA-IR. HOMA-IR showed strong correlations with fasting glucose, triglycerides, and serum insulin. Other anthropometric measures, including weight, waist circumference, wrist, neck, waist-to-hip ratio, and waist-to-height ratio, were not significantly correlated with the biochemical parameters (p > 0.05). Spearman correlation analysis showed that APOL1 gene expression was positively associated with measures of adiposity, including body weight (r = 0.320, p = 0.028), total body fat mass percentage (r = 0.316, p = 0.031), and total body fat mass in kilograms (r = 0.357, p = 0.014), while no significant correlations were observed with age, blood pressure, BMI, waist, hip, or other anthropometric indices (p > 0.05) (Table 3). In contrast, APOL1 expression was inversely correlated with key glycaemic and renal biochemical parameters, showing significant negative associations with HbA1c (r = − 0.469, p = 0.001), urea (r = − 0.437, p = 0.002), and fasting glucose (r = − 0.680, p < 0.001). No significant correlations were found between APOL1 expression and lipid profile components, creatinine, serum insulin, or HOMA-IR (p > 0.05). NOTCH2 gene expression correlated negatively with age (r = − 0.343, p = 0.026) and positively with hip circumference (r = 0.402, p = 0.046) among anthropometric measurements (Table 4), while no significant correlations were observed with other anthropometric indices, including blood pressure, weight, BMI, waist, wrist, neck, waist-to-hip ratio, waist-to-height ratio, or total body fat (p > 0.05). Regarding biochemical parameters, NOTCH2 expression was significantly negatively correlated with fasting glucose (r = − 0.311, p = 0.045), whereas no significant associations were found with HbA1c, urea, lipid profile components, creatinine, serum insulin, or HOMA-IR (p > 0.05). Figure 1 shows a moderate positive correlation (r = 0.424, p = 0.006) between the fold changes of NOTCH2 and APOL1.

Discussion

This exploratory case–control study evaluated APOL1 and NOTCH2 gene expression among Nigerians living with T2DM and examined their associations with anthropometric measurements, biochemical indices, insulin resistance, and selected comorbidities. The findings demonstrate that participants with T2DM exhibited altered glycaemic indices, elevated systolic blood pressure, increased triglyceride levels, and higher insulin resistance compared with controls. In addition, distinct association patterns were observed between APOL1 and NOTCH2 gene expression and metabolic parameters, suggesting potential involvement of these genes in metabolic homeostasis and glucose regulation within this population. Importantly, the study contributes preliminary molecular data from an African population that remains underrepresented in diabetes-related genomic and transcriptomic research37.

Hypertension was the most prevalent comorbidity among participants with T2DM, aligning with the established clustering of cardiometabolic risk factors, as T2DM and hypertension frequently coexist due to shared pathophysiological mechanisms, including insulin resistance, endothelial dysfunction, and chronic low-grade inflammation38. Other comorbidities such as dyslipidemia, obesity, and renal damage were also observed, reflecting the multifactorial nature of T2DM and underscoring the importance of comprehensive risk assessment, as the presence of comorbidities amplifies cardiovascular and renal complications39,40. Biochemical analysis revealed pronounced metabolic dysregulation among cases. Participants with T2DM exhibited significantly higher fasting plasma glucose and HbA1c, indicative of poor long-term glycemic control. Elevated fasting glucose and HbA1c levels are hallmarks of T2DM, reflecting impaired insulin secretion and insulin resistance5. Elevated HOMA-IR values further support the presence of impaired insulin sensitivity and altered glycaemic regulation typical of T2DM41. Triglyceride levels were elevated among cases, consistent with the hypertriglyceridemia commonly associated with insulin-resistant states, while other lipid parameters remained relatively preserved within this cohort. Lipid profile and renal function markers were relatively preserved, although the rise in urea levels among cases may indicate altered metabolic or renal-related physiological processes in individuals living with T2DM. However, urea is a non-specific marker and may also be influenced by factors such as hydration status, dietary protein intake, muscle metabolism, and catabolic states. Correlation analyses provided further insight into the interplay between anthropometric measures, biochemical indices, and gene expression, which are consistent with the well-documented association between hypertension and insulin resistance in T2DM42.

The association between BMI, serum insulin, and total body fat mass emphasised the contribution of adiposity to insulin resistance, while other anthropometric indices did not show significant associations with metabolic parameters, suggesting that total adiposity, rather than regional fat distribution, may be more closely linked to insulin resistance in this population43. HOMA-IR correlations with fasting glucose, triglycerides, and serum insulin highlight its validity as a marker of insulin resistance and a central mediator of dysglycemia and hypertriglyceridemia in T2DM44. Similar observations have been reported in African populations by Tekoh et al., Omodanisi et al., and Ezekpo et al., who demonstrated significant associations between adiposity, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and poor glycaemic control among individuals living with T2DM45–47. In the present study, elevated systolic blood pressure was additionally associated with serum insulin and HOMA-IR, reinforcing the clustering of cardiovascular and metabolic risk factors commonly observed in T2DM. Collectively, these findings emphasise the importance of integrated cardiometabolic risk assessment in diabetes management.

The observed APOL1 expression pattern provides additional insight into the potential metabolic relevance of this gene beyond its established association with renal disease in individuals of African ancestry9,10. APOL1 expression was positively associated with measures of adiposity, including body weight and total body fat mass, while inversely associated with fasting glucose, HbA1c, and urea levels. Rather than implying a protective or compensatory role, these findings suggest that APOL1 expression may be associated with glycaemic regulation and metabolic adaptation in individuals living with T2DM. Previous studies have primarily focused on APOL1 renal-risk alleles and kidney disease susceptibility among African populations; however, the present findings indicate that APOL1 expression may also be linked to broader metabolic processes, including adiposity and glucose homeostasis48,49. The inverse association between APOL1 expression and glycaemic markers may reflect altered transcriptional responses occurring alongside impaired glucose metabolism, although the underlying biological mechanisms remain unclear. Furthermore, the inverse association with urea levels may indicate potential interactions between APOL1 expression and early metabolic alterations associated with renal dysfunction in T2DM, although renal-specific biomarkers such as albuminuria and eGFR were not evaluated. Consequently, these observations should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as exploratory rather than mechanistic evidence.

NOTCH2 expression demonstrated a more selective pattern of association with anthropometric and biochemical variables. NOTCH2 expression was inversely associated with age and fasting glucose, while a positive association was observed with hip circumference. These findings suggest that NOTCH2 may be linked to glucose metabolism and adipose tissue distribution in individuals living with T2DM. The Notch signalling pathway has previously been implicated in pancreatic β-cell biology, insulin signalling, inflammatory pathways, and adipocyte differentiation11,12,50. However, unlike APOL1, NOTCH2 expression in this study was not associated with several metabolic indicators, including HOMA-IR, serum insulin, lipid parameters, or renal markers, suggesting that its role in T2DM may be more limited or context-dependent within this cohort. The inverse association with fasting glucose may indicate a potential relationship between reduced NOTCH2 expression and impaired glycaemic control, although causal inferences cannot be made due to the cross-sectional design. Similarly, the observed age-related decline in NOTCH2 expression may reflect alterations in transcriptional activity associated with ageing or metabolic stress. Importantly, mechanistic conclusions should be avoided, as gene expression patterns alone do not establish functional biological effects. The relationship between gene expression and comorbidities was explored to assess whether APOL1 and NOTCH2 could serve as markers of disease severity or risk clustering in T2DM; however, none of the differences observed reached statistical significance (p > 0.05). These findings may indicate that while APOL1 and NOTCH2 may respond to metabolic stress associated with obesity and dyslipidemia, their expression is not markedly altered by the presence of hypertension or renal impairment in this cohort, highlighting the complex regulation of these genes and the potential influence of additional genetic, environmental, or epigenetic factors51,52.

The integration of these findings underscores the multifactorial nature of T2DM, wherein age, adiposity, hypertension, and metabolic dysregulation collectively contribute to disease pathophysiology. The positive associations between adiposity and APOL1, alongside the inverse relationship with glycemic markers, may suggest a dual role for APOL1 in both energy storage and glycemic control. Meanwhile, NOTCH2 may have a more specific relationship with peripheral fat distribution and fasting glucose, with an age-dependent component. The moderate correlation between APOL1 and NOTCH2 suggests that these genes may act in concert or share upstream regulatory pathways that influence metabolic homeostasis, insulin sensitivity, or adipocyte function14,53. From a clinical perspective, these results have several implications. First, the significant correlations between blood pressure, adiposity, and insulin resistance reinforce the importance of comprehensive cardiovascular and metabolic risk assessment in T2DM management. Second, the distinct patterns of APOL1 and NOTCH2 expression suggest their potential utility as biomarkers for identifying individuals at risk for poor glycemic control or obesity-related complications. Third, the lack of significant differences in gene expression between participants with and without comorbidities indicates that gene expression alone may not reflect clinical severity but could provide mechanistic insight into metabolic adaptations in T2DM.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, while the relatively small sample size may have reduced the statistical power to detect subtle associations, particularly in the gene expression, comorbidity, and correlation analyses, thereby increasing the possibility of both Type I and Type II errors. In addition, controls were not individually matched to cases for variables such as age and hypertension status, which may have introduced residual confounding. Although these factors were considered during interpretation, formal multivariable adjustment analyses were not performed due to the exploratory pilot nature of the study and the limited sample size; therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Renal damage was inferred using available biochemical markers, as albuminuria and eGFR assessments were not performed, which likely underestimated the true burden of diabetic kidney disease within the study population, particularly because early renal impairment in individuals living with T2DM may occur despite relatively preserved serum creatinine levels. Effect size estimates and confidence intervals were not comprehensively reported for all analyses, which may limit interpretation of the magnitude and precision of some observed associations. The study did not assess APOL1 high-risk variants (G1 and G2), which are known to influence susceptibility to kidney disease in African populations and may have contributed to inter-individual variability in renal and metabolic outcomes. In addition, protein-level validation and functional assays were not performed; therefore, the biological activity and mechanistic significance of the observed gene expression patterns could not be fully established. Furthermore, self-reported physical activity data may be subject to recall bias and may not accurately reflect the true intensity or duration of activity. The study also did not account for potential confounding factors such as dietary patterns, medication use, or other environmental exposures that could influence metabolic parameters and gene expression.

Despite these limitations, the study provides important insights into the relationships between anthropometric indices, biochemical parameters, and gene expression, while identifying potential molecular targets for future investigation. An important strength of this study is its contribution to molecular and metabolic research within an African population. Despite the disproportionately increasing burden of T2DM and related cardiometabolic disorders across Africa, African populations remain markedly underrepresented in genomic, transcriptomic, and biomarker discovery studies. Most molecular studies investigating APOL1, Notch signalling, insulin resistance, and metabolic dysfunction have been conducted in European, Asian, or North American populations, limiting the generalizability of findings to African settings. The present study, therefore, provides valuable preliminary evidence regarding the association between APOL1 and NOTCH2 expression and metabolic parameters among Nigerians living with T2DM. Integrating anthropometric, biochemical, and molecular data may improve understanding of population-specific metabolic phenotypes and support future precision medicine approaches tailored to African populations. However, larger multicenter longitudinal studies incorporating broader genomic and transcriptomic approaches, larger sample sizes, and functional assays are required to validate these findings, elucidate causal relationships and gene–environment interactions, and determine the translational relevance and clinical potential of these molecular markers54,55. Future studies incorporating APOL1 genotyping, proteomic analyses, and functional validation experiments are warranted to strengthen the translational relevance of these findings.

Conclusion

This exploratory study demonstrated that participants living with T2DM exhibited altered glycaemic indices, elevated triglyceride levels, increased insulin resistance, and distinct patterns of APOL1 and NOTCH2 gene expression compared with non-diabetic controls. APOL1 expression was associated with adiposity and glycaemic markers, while NOTCH2 expression showed selective associations with age, hip circumference, and fasting glucose. The moderate positive association observed between APOL1 and NOTCH2 suggests possible interaction or shared regulatory pathways in metabolic homeostasis. These findings contribute preliminary molecular evidence from an underrepresented African population and highlight the importance of integrating anthropometric, biochemical, and molecular data in understanding T2DM pathophysiology, while incorporating functional and multi-omics approaches are required to validate these observations and clarify their potential clinical relevance.
