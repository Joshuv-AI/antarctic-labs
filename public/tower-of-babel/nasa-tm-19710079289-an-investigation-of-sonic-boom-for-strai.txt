 ABSTRACT

 Wind tunnel tests were conducted to determine the sonic boom
 characteristics of straight-wing and delta-wing space shuttle orbiters
 during reentry into the earth's atmosphere. Two different trajectories
 were analyzed for the delta-wing orbiter while one trajectory was
 considered for the straight-wing orbiter. Both trajectory and angle of
 attack were found to have strong effects on the level of sonic boom
 overpressure under the flight path of the vehicle .

 SUMMARY

 An experimental investigation was conducted to determine the level
of sonic boom overpressure generated by space shuttle orbiters during
reentry into the earth's atmosphere. Two configurations were studied.
The first, a straight-wing orbiter, was tested at Mach numbers of
1.2, 1.68, 2.17 and 2.7. The second, a delta-wing orbiter, was tested
at Mach numbers of 1.68, 2.17 and 2.7. The sonic boom overpressures'
created by the straight-wing orbiter were found to vary from approximately
 2 2
100N/m (2 psf) at M = 1.2 to about 40 N/m (.9 psf) at M = 2.7 for a
trajectory based on a constant angle of attack of 60 degrees during
reentry. The level of sonic boom overpressure for the delta~wing
orbiter was found to be approximately the same as that for the straight-
wing orbiter for a 60 degree angle of attack trajectory. If the
trajectory was changed to permit a constant angle of attack of 25 degrees
during reentry, the level of sonic boom overpressure was less than
48 N/m 2 (1 psf) for the range of Mach numbers investigated.

 INTRODUCTION

 For several years a considerable effort has been expended in the
design and testing of different reusable space shuttle booster and
orbiter configurations. Some of the more important uses of a space
shuttle orbiter would include delivery of experimental hardware to and
from space stations; launching satellites; transporting manned orbital
observation teams to and from space; retrieving reusable satellites
from orbit and military logistics support. A desirable characteristic
of the space shuttle is that the orbiter be capable of operating from
present day airports. Since many of these airports are located near
or in major metropolitan areas an investigation has been conducted to
define the level of sonic boom overpressure generated during reentry
into the Earth's atmosphere near such densely populated areas. Two
different orbiter configurations, one having a delta wing and one having
a straight wing were studied during this investigation. Different flight
profiles were studied and techniques for alleviating the sonic boom
problem were considered.

 METHOD

 Because of the inability of the Whitham theory (reference 1) to
 calculate an F-fm1ction for a vehicle with detached shock waves it
 was necessary to employ a method which utilizes an F-function experimentally
 determined from wind tunnel tests (reference 2).

 This approach consists of measuring the sonic boom pressure signature
 at two distances, h and h , from the model in a wind tunnel. The
 1
 signature obtained at h is2 then used to calculate an F-function. The
 F-function so determinea can then be used to predict the pressure sig-
 nature at h . If the agreement between the predicted and the measured
 2
 signature at h is acceptable it is then possible to use either signature
 2
 to calculate, with some confidence, the level of overpressure at various
 flight altitudes.

 -3-

 Since the method of reference 2 is applicable to level, unaccelerated
 flight in a uniform atmosphere it is necessary to correct the level of
 overpressure for a non-uniform atmosphere (reference 3), deceleration
 (reference 4) and flight path angle. The procedure used in correcting
 for inclined flight paths is given in the Appendix.

 TEST CONDITIONS AND APPARArus

 A 0.254 meter (10 inch) model of a delta-wing orbiter (figure 1)
 and a 0.178 meter (7 in.) model of straight-wing orbiter (figure 2)
 were tested at Mach numbers of 1.68 and 2.17 in the Ames 2.74 x 2.13
 meter (9 x 7 ft.) wind tunnel and at a Mach number of 2.7 in the Ames
 2.44 x 2.13 meter (8 x 7 ft.) wind tunnel. The straight-wing orbiter
 was also tested at M = 1.2 in the Ames 3.35 x 3.35 meter (11 x 11 ft.)
 wind tunnel. The total pressure used was 508 mm-hg (20 in-hg) in all
 tunnels. The delta-wing model was tested at angles of attack of 25
 and 60 degrees, while the straight-wing model was tested at 60° angle of
 attack, The two angles of attack for the delta-wing configuration were
 obtained by attaching the model to its sting support in either of two
 appropriately inclined holes on the top of the fuselage (see figure l (a)).

 The models were mounted on a linear actuator (figure 1 (b) which
 permitted them to be translated longitudinally in the wind tunnels by
 as much as O. 736m (29 in). Pressures were measured with c·apacitance-
 type pressure transducers. The model flow field pressures were detected
 by a 2 degree included angle, conical, static pressure probe mounted
 on the wind-tunnel wall centerline as shown in figure l(b). The probe
' shown in the figure near the top of the wall was outside the model flow
 field and was used to detect the free stream static pressure.

 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

 Two trajectories were studied for the delta-wing orbiter while one
 was analyzed for the straight-wing orbiter. Details of the trajectories
 are shown in figures 3 and 4. The angle of attack for the first delta-
 wing trajectory is a constant 60 degrees while a constant angle of
 attack of 25 degrees is employed for the second delta-wing trajectory.
 An angle of attack of 60 degrees was used for the straight-wing orbiter
 calculations. Because of the shapes of the vehicles and the large
 angles of attack the bow shocks were detached as shown in figure 5.
 The large shock strengths associated with the detached bow waves made it
 necessary, to establish the validity of the extrapolation technique
 described in reference 2. This.was accomplished, as described in the
 METHOD section, for both 25- and 60-degrees angle of attack for the
 delta and at 60 degrees for the straight-wing orbiter at a Mach number
 of 2.. 7. The results are shown in figure 6. As can be seen, the positive

 The signatures shown in figure 7 were used to predict the maximum
sonic boom overpressures for flight conditions (see figure 8). The pre-
dicted levels of overpressure are between 40 and 100 N/m 2 (about 1 to
2 psf) under the flight path for the 60 degree angle-of-attack trajectories
for either configuration. It is interesting to note that the difference
between the overpressure level for the 25- and 60-degree trajectories for
the delta vehicle decreases rapidly with increasing Mach number. If
both orbiters are allowed to fly the trajectory for the delta-wing orbiter
at 60° angle of attack regardless of angle of attack the overpressure
characteristics shown in figure 9 result. It can now be seen from a
comparison of figures 8 and 9 that the level of overpressure is as strong
a function of flight profile as angle of attack, and that the straight-
wing orbiter produces lower values of overpressure than the delta-wing
orbiter if both vehicles fly the same flight profile. However, the
altitude/length ratio would be greater for the straight-wing orbiter
when flying at the same altitude since it has a shorter length. The
levei of overpressure for the 25 degree trajectory of the delta-wing
orbiter shown in figure 8 is below 50 N/m 2 (1 psf) over the Mach number
range shown.

 The data presented in figures 8 and 9 include corrections for flight
path angle, non-uniform atmosphere, and deceleration. In accordance with
experience, measured pressure signatures have been multiplied by a factor
of 1.8 to account for reflection of the incident shock wave by the earth's
surface. The effect,of rate of change of flight path angle was •
investigated by the method of reference 5 and found to be negligible for

these trajectories. The effects of bank angle and rate of change of
heading angle were not investigated in this study. However, future
effort should be devoted to a study of these effects since the sonic boom
characteristics of space shuttle vehicles might be affected by these
parameters.

 No data were obtained at a Mach number of 1.2 for the delta-wing
orbiter. because of unsteady flow conditions (possibly leading to questionable
results) observed at this Mach number during the straight-wing orbiter
tests.

 CONCLUDING REMARKS

 The sonic boom characteristics of a delta-wing orbiter have been
studied at Mach numbers of 1.68, 2.17 and 2.7 for trajectories flown at
25 and 60 degrees angle of attack; and at Mach numbers of 1.2, 1.68,
2.17 and 2.7 for a 60 degree angle of attack flight profile for a straight-
wing orbiter. It has been shown that by careful selection of the mission
profile and orbiter attitude it should be possible to keep the bow-shock
overpressure below 48 N/m 2 (1 psf); a level which may be acceptable to
communities under the flight path.

 Further work is needed to define the effects of bank angle and rate
of change of heading angle on the level of sonic boom overpressure.
