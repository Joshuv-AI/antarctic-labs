Hydromorphone-induced choreiform dyskinesia in a patient with homozygous sickle cell disease: a reversible adverse effect
Authors: Sakeena Saife, Michael El Masry, Islam Rajab, Rayan A Rahman, Nader Ghobrial
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSickle Cell Disease (SCD) is a type of chronic hemoglobinopathy with vaso-occlusive pain crises that requires aggressive pain management with opioids.Hydromorphone is commonly used in the management of pain in patients with SCD, particularly when vaso-occlusive crises require prolonged opioid therapy. While opioid-induced neurotoxicity is a well-recognized complication, usually presenting as delirium, agitation, or myoclonus, choreiform dyskinesias are rare and only infrequently reported. New-onset involuntary movements in patients with SCD may be concerning for cerebrovascular or metabolic complications, prompting extensive diagnostic evaluations. Recognition of opioid-induced choreiform dyskinesia is important, since symptoms are usually reversible with opioid dose reduction or discontinuation, thus offering early management and the avoidance of unnecessary investigations.Case ReportA 33-year-old African American woman with homozygous sickle cell disease (HbSS) was admitted for pleuritic chest pain, fever, abdominal pain, and sweating, suspected to be acute chest syndrome or a vaso-occlusive pain crisis. She had a history of recurrent vaso-occlusive crises requiring hospitalizations and opioid therapy. She had no history of cerebrovascular events, movement disorders, psychiatric illness, or exposure to dopamine-modulating medications. She had no significant family history.On day 25 of hospitalization, she developed new, involuntary, non-rhythmic choreiform movements affecting the upper limbs and face while on high-dose IV hydromorphone. She remained alert and oriented without any focal neurologic signs or metabolic issues. The hydromorphone was stopped, and the movements completely resolved within 24 hours. When trying a lower dose, the movements did not recur. Neurology advised that no further imaging or testing was needed given the rapid, full improvement.ConclusionsDyskinesia is a rare but reversible complication of opioid therapy and may be caused by hydromorphone. It is important to recognize this side effect to prevent unnecessary testing, particularly in patients with sickle cell disease, who may otherwise undergo an extensive neurologic workup.Keywords: hydromorphone, dyskinesia, opioid-induced neurotoxicity, chorea, sickle cell disease

Introduction

SCD is a life-long inherited hemoglobinopathy, the result of a β-globin mutation, causing erythrocyte sickling, hemolysis, and multi-organ damage. The global burden of SCD is substantial, with an estimated 300 000 affected births annually, predominantly in sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East, and South Asia [1]. The most common complications include recurrent painful VOCs, acute chest syndrome, and chronic organ injury; these usually require potent analgesia [2]. Opioids remain the mainstay for managing pain of moderate to severe intensity, and hydromorphone is often chosen due to its high potency and good efficacy [3]. The typical opioid side effects include somnolence, nausea, constipation, and pruritus. Neuroexcitatory effects of opioids, though infrequent, may manifest as agitation, tremors, myoclonus, or seizures [4]. These are generally attributed to accumulation of neuroexcitatory metabolites, most notably hydromorphone-3-glucuronide (H3G) in the case of hydromorphone [5]. While opioid-induced neurotoxicity presenting as myoclonus or delirium is well-described, choreiform dyskinesia remains exceedingly rare and under-reported [6]. Critically, new-onset involuntary movements in patients with SCD may raise concern for cerebrovascular accident, metabolic encephalopathy, or CNS infection, prompting extensive and potentially unnecessary diagnostic evaluation. We present the first documented case of hydromorphone-induced choreiform dyskinesia in a patient with homozygous SCD, highlighting its complete reversibility and emphasizing the importance of prompt recognition to avoid unnecessary investigations and guide appropriate management. Case Presentation.

A 33-year-old African American female who is known to have HbSS sickle cell disease, presenting to the ER with complaints of sudden pleuritic chest pain, fever, abdominal pain, and heavy sweating. She was tachycardic at 133 bpm, satting 98% on room air, and in marked distress. Exam of the lungs was clear, but her abdomen was diffusely tender. Labs showed hemoglobin 9.2 g/dl, WBC 2.9 K/μl, sodium 130 mEq/l, potassium 3.3 mEq/l. Chest X-ray had no infiltrates. Thus, she was admitted with suspected acute chest syndrome or an acute vaso-occlusive episode and started on IV fluids, ceftriaxone, azithromycin, and IV hydromorphone for pain.

Over the next three weeks, she required increasingly higher doses of hydromorphone to control stubborn pain. Concurrent medications throughout the admission included ceftriaxone, azithromycin (later discontinued after a 5-day course), scheduled acetaminophen, ketorolac (used intermittently for adjunct analgesia), ondansetron for nausea, and hydroxyurea (the patient’s home medication). No dopamine-antagonist antiemetics, antipsychotics, or other dopaminergic-modulating agents were administered during the admission. The pain trajectory was notable for persistent severity, and alternative modalities were considered: exchange blood transfusion was discussed by the hematology team but was ultimately deferred given the absence of acute chest syndrome on imaging and the patient’s hemodynamic stability. Simple transfusion was also considered but withheld due to prior alloimmunization. Non-opioid adjuncts, including acetaminophen and ketorolac, were utilized throughout, but were insufficient to achieve adequate pain control independent of opioids.

On hospital day 25, she developed new involuntary, irregular, non-rhythmic choreiform movements affecting her upper limbs and facial muscles. The movements lasted for several hours and were seen by the staff though the patient did not notice them and reported no discomfort. Neuro exam showed that she was fully awake and oriented, cranial nerves intact, no weakness or sensory loss and no signs of meningeal irritation. She denied headaches, vision changes, or slowed thinking. Vitals remained stable. Labs showed normal electrolytes, glucose, kidney, and liver function. These abnormal movements were suspected to be related to opioid-induced dyskinesia because the timing coincided with prolonged high-dose opioid exposure. Hydromorphone was stopped and replaced with fentanyl. Fentanyl was selected because it does not produce neuroexcitatory glucuronide metabolites and has a more favorable neurotoxicity profile in the context of acute opioid rotation, despite isolated case reports of fentanyl-associated movement disorders [7]. About 24 hours later, the movements disappeared completely. Neurology evaluated her and advised no urgent neuroimaging since symptoms had resolved and there were no focal deficits pointing to structural pathology. Hydromorphone was later restarted at a much lower dose without the movements recurring. The patient had no prior history of movement disorders, psychiatric illness, stroke, or use of dopamine-modulating medications, and her family history was unremarkable.

Discussion

Opioid-induced neurotoxicity is an important but under-recognized clinical entity, particularly relevant in patients with SCD who require prolonged, high-dose opioid therapy for recurrent VOCs [8]. Hydromorphone undergoes extensive hepatic glucuronidation to produce two principal metabolites: hydromorphone-3-glucuronide (H3G), which has no analgesic activity but potent neuroexcitatory properties, and hydromorphone-6-glucuronide (H6G), which retains partial opioid agonist activity.5 H3G antagonizes inhibitory glycine and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptors in the central nervous system, and at elevated concentrations may produce a spectrum of neuroexcitatory phenomena including myoclonus, tremor, agitation, seizures, and as illustrated in the present case choreiform dyskinesia [9].

A critical mechanistic consideration in the present case is why H3G reached a neurotoxic threshold despite the patient having normal renal and hepatic function. H3G accumulation is most commonly described in the setting of renal impairment, which reduces its renal clearance. However, accumulation can occur via two alternative mechanisms in patients with preserved organ function. First, prolonged high-dose opioid exposure over 25 days creates a sustained, high substrate load for hepatic glucuronidation, resulting in progressive H3G accumulation even when clearance mechanisms are intact. Second, individual pharmacogenomic variability in UGT2B7 the primary enzyme responsible for hydromorphone glucuronidation—may lead to a higher H3G-to-parent drug ratio in susceptible individuals, lowering the effective neurotoxic threshold. 5, [10]. In this case, the cumulative dose burden over an extended hospitalization, in the absence of an identifiable clearance deficit, is the most parsimonious explanation for H3G-mediated neurotoxicity.

The possibility of drug–drug interactions contributing to dyskinesia merits consideration. The concurrently administered medications in this patient included acetaminophen, ketorolac, ondansetron, and hydroxyurea. Ondansetron, a 5-HT3 antagonist, has been rarely implicated in extrapyramidal reactions, however, the patient had received it throughout the admission without incident, and the onset of dyskinesia coincided with peak cumulative opioid exposure rather than with any change in ondansetron dosing. No dopamine-antagonist antiemetics or antipsychotics the drug classes most commonly associated with drug-induced choreiform movements were administered. While a minor pharmacokinetic interaction between ondansetron and hydromorphone cannot be entirely excluded, the temporal pattern and resolution upon opioid discontinuation alone strongly support a primary opioid-mediated mechanism.

Three clinical features in the present case strongly support a causal relationship between hydromorphone and the observed dyskinesia: (1) the temporal onset following 25 days of progressive dose escalation, (2) complete resolution within 24 hours of hydromorphone discontinuation, and (3) absence of recurrence upon reintroduction at substantially lower doses—a pattern consistent with dose-dependent H3G accumulation.

Comparison with previously reported cases reveals a consistent pattern of opioid-induced dyskinesia (Table 1). Martin et al. (2018) described hydromorphone-induced choreoathetosis in a palliative care patient, which resolved upon opioid rotation [6] a presentation closely mirroring the current case. Gagnon et al. (2013) reported tremor and agitation following low-dose intravenous hydromorphone in a patient with renal dysfunction, reinforcing the role of H3G accumulation in neurotoxicity [9]. Watari and Tokuda (2015) documented drug-induced hemichorea, illustrating that choreiform presentations can result from a variety of pharmacological agents via dopaminergic or other CNS pathway dysregulation [11]. Importantly, while fentanyl was used as the rotation agent in the current case, fentanyl itself has been associated with opioid-induced neurotoxicity in isolated reports [7] likely via an indirect mechanism of altered dopaminergic signaling rather than glucuronide metabolite accumulation, given that fentanyl does not undergo glucuronidation. Fentanyl was selected for opioid rotation in this patient given its distinct metabolic pathway, shorter half-life, and absence of neuroexcitatory glucuronide metabolites, while acknowledging the need for clinical vigilance. This case is notable as the first report of hydromorphone-induced choreiform dyskinesia in a patient with homozygous SCD, where the chronicity and severity of VOC-associated pain creates a unique clinical context for prolonged, high-dose opioid exposure and cumulative metabolite accumulation [8].

Awareness of opioid-induced movement disorders is essential to prevent potentially harmful over-investigation including neuroimaging, lumbar puncture, and escalation of implicated medications in patients whose symptoms are entirely reversible with dose modification or opioid rotation. Opioid class effects, likely mediated by neuroexcitatory metabolite accumulation, have been described with morphine (morphine-3-glucuronide), hydromorphone (H3G), and, less consistently, methadone and fentanyl [4, 8]. The underlying mechanisms remain incompletely characterized and likely involve both receptor-level and metabolite-mediated pathways.

Several limitations of this report deserve acknowledgment. As a single case report, it is inherently subject to the constraints of anecdotal evidence, causal inference is supported by clinical reasoning and temporal association, but no formal pharmacokinetic analysis (e.g. H3G serum concentrations) was performed to confirm the diagnosis biochemically. The absence of serum H3G measurement represents a gap in evidence, though such assays are not routinely available in clinical settings. Additionally, the contribution of genetic variability in drug-metabolizing enzymes cannot be assessed without pharmacogenomic testing. These limitations reinforce the need for prospective pharmacovigilance studies of opioid-induced neurotoxicity in the SCD population.

Conclusion

This report describes the first documented case of hydromorphone-induced choreiform dyskinesia in a patient with homozygous sickle cell disease a population at particularly elevated risk due to the chronic, severe nature of VOC-associated pain and its requirement for prolonged high-dose opioid therapy. The case demonstrates that H3G-mediated neuroexcitatory toxicity can occur even in the setting of preserved renal and hepatic function when cumulative opioid exposure is sufficiently prolonged. Prompt recognition of this adverse effect enabled timely intervention, complete symptom resolution, and avoidance of extensive neurological investigation.

Clinicians managing pain in sickle cell disease should maintain a high index of suspicion for hydromorphone-induced choreiform dyskinesia when patients develop unexplained involuntary movements during prolonged opioid therapy. Early dose reduction or opioid rotation guided by the neurotoxicity profile of available alternatives leads to rapid resolution and prevents unnecessary diagnostic workup. Routine assessment of cumulative opioid exposure, patient-specific risk factors for metabolite accumulation, and concurrent medications should be incorporated into the clinical monitoring of SCD patients receiving sustained opioid therapy. Future prospective studies and pharmacovigilance efforts are warranted to better characterize the incidence, risk factors, and optimal management strategies for opioid-induced neurotoxicity in the sickle cell disease population.
