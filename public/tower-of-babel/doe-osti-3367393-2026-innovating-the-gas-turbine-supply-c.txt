 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The 2026 Innovating the Gas Turbine Supply Chain Workshop was convened at the Oak Ridge National
Laboratory’s Manufacturing Demonstration Facility (MDF) in Knoxville, Tennessee, on March 31–April
1, 2026. Organized by ORNL, NETL, and the Gas Turbine Association (GTA) including gas turbine
original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) GE Vernova, MHI, Siemens Energy, and Solar Turbines. The
workshop brought together approximately 70 participants representing OEMs, supply chain companies,
national laboratories, universities, and federal government agencies. Edgar Lara-Curzio (ORNL) served
as Workshop Chair.

The workshop was motivated by a convergence of forces reshaping the demand landscape for industrial
gas turbines. Accelerating electrification across heavy industry, transportation, and residential heating
sectors is a global long-term structural driver. The explosive growth of artificial intelligence (AI)-driven
data centers — next-generation facilities projected to require three to five gigawatts each — is generating
near-term demand for firm, dispatchable power at a pace that has no historical precedent in the power
generation industry. Against this backdrop, market intelligence presented at the workshop documented
approximately 103 GW of global gas turbine orders in 2025 alone — a near-record figure representing a
72% year-over-year increase in megawatts ordered — with a ten-year baseline forecast of $354 billion in
new orders for packaged power generation systems and over $437 billion in aftermarket service revenue
between 2026 and 2035.

 “The workshop was motivated by a convergence of forces reshaping the demand
 landscape for industrial gas turbines.”

The supply chain that must deliver on this demand is struggling to keep up. Lead times for new large gas
turbines now extend to five years or more. The installed fleet of units commissioned around 2000 is
simultaneously reaching end-of-life, creating a structural double bind: OEMs and suppliers must serve
surging new-build demand and sustain and upgrade an aging installed base with the same constrained
supply base. Raw material supply chains for critical alloy inputs — cobalt, yttrium, hafnium, rhenium,
tungsten carbide — are geopolitically exposed, with prices for some elements quadrupling within months
of a supply disruption. The supply chain for castings and forgings is shared with an aerospace industry
carrying a backlog of over 16,000 commercial aircraft and 32,000 engines. And the workforce of
experienced engineers and technicians in specialized disciplines — design for manufacturability, single-
crystal casting, thermal spray, precision forging, repair and lifecycle management — is retiring faster than
it is being replenished.

The 2026 Innovating the Gas Turbine Supply Chain Workshop was designed to produce a clear,
actionable characterization of these gaps and the opportunities to address them, drawing on the direct
experience of the major gas turbine OEMs and other participants across the full value chain. Its findings
are intended to inform decision-makers in the federal government about where research investments could
have the greatest impact. A parallel objective was to identify concrete opportunities for industry to
leverage the unique capabilities of national laboratories and universities to address these challenges in the
near term to advance US competitiveness.

The workshop was structured around five plenary keynote presentations, four expert panel discussions,
and four parallel breakout sessions organized around the themes of Advanced Materials and Coatings;
Digital Technologies and Predictive Maintenance; Manufacturing Technologies; and Repair,
Refurbishment, and Lifecycle Management. The findings documented in this report represent the

synthesis of all three formats: the strategic framing of the keynotes, the expert perspective of the panels,
and the participant-generated, structured findings of the breakout sessions.

Key Findings

Six cross-cutting findings emerged consistently across the keynote presentations, panel discussions, and
breakout sessions.

 1. The gas turbine supply chain is operating near its limits, and the current demand surge has both
 structural and cyclical components. This supercycle is driven by multiple forces — acute power
 demand for data centers, industrial electrification, aging fleet replacement, and global energy
 security considerations — that will sustain elevated market pressure into the future. The supply
 chain was not sized for this environment. New-build lead times of five or more years, combined
 with the simultaneous demand for major overhauls of units commissioned around 2000, create a
 dilemma that could have a negative effect on US competitiveness in facing today’s commercial
 opportunities. Until a fundamental expansion of domestic manufacturing infrastructure — in
 castings, forgings, specialty alloys, and thermal spray — occurs, there is an immediate need for
 investments that improve yields in constrained areas, develop rapid prototyping capabilities to
 optimize or replace traditional casting, enhance repair for performance, accelerate
 industrialization of advanced manufacturing technologies, and broadly optimize existing supply
 chain capabilities.

 2. Critical raw material supply chain vulnerability is an immediate and a longer term risk. Cobalt,
 yttrium, hafnium, rhenium, and tungsten carbide powders — all essential to gas turbine alloys and
 coatings — have experienced supply disruptions and price spikes that have already forced
 reactive responses from OEMs and material suppliers. Approximately 70% of the carbide powder
 production that once occurred domestically has migrated to China. Accelerated applied research
 in material systems development, innovative coating techniques, and optimized use and recycling
 of critical materials, are necessary in the face of supply challenges.

 3. Additive manufacturing and other emerging manufacturing technologies have demonstrated
 compelling value in specific applications but there remains tremendous unrealized potential. The
 clearest opportunities are in legacy part production, complex internal geometry components, rapid
 tooling for investment casting, and repair — applications where AM’s geometric freedom and
 tooling independence outweigh its dimensional precision challenges. The future application space
 for these technologies extends across the entire gas turbine, offering the potential to dramatically
 improve performance and ease severe supply chain constraints. Areas for near-term investment
 would include opportunities to improve high temperature creep capability in the 1200F and
 higher temperature range, speed up build times by 50%, and enable rotating hardware rig and
 engine demonstrations to accelerate commercialization. Because industrializing these
 technologies is highly proprietary, individual OEMs or suppliers are generally best positioned to
 drive these efforts.

 4. Digital technologies and condition-based lifecycle management offer high potential for leverage
 across the gas turbine life cycle — but deployment needs to be accelerated. Physics-informed
 digital twins, surrogate sensing, in-situ manufacturing process monitoring, and predictive
 maintenance tools are technically available today in more advanced forms than the industry has
 widely deployed. OEMs with mature platforms have begun demonstrating condition-based
 maintenance and life extension of in-service components which in turn directly relieves pressure
 on an overloaded new-build supply chain. Under the condition-based lifecycle management
 framework, investments could be directed towards creating a factory digital twin capturing key

 xiv

 process inputs, improving real-time identification and mitigation of defects in additive
 manufacturing, and developing and deploying predictive maintenance capabilities.

 5. Repair, refurbishment, and lifecycle management represent the most immediate supply chain
 relief valve available — and they are underinvested. Every component that can be repaired rather
 than replaced relieves pressure on an already stressed new-make pipeline. In addition repair today
 is not merely remediation. It represents an opportunity to return components to service with
 superior cooling geometries, refined microstructures, and improved coatings relative to the
 original design. But realizing this potential requires investment in robust residual life assessment
 models, retrofitting the most advanced technologies in the installed gas turbine fleet, additive
 repair for life extension. Retrofitting new technologies in the installed base requires both gas
 turbine level development and validation, and also system level studies of total plant level
 impacts.

 6. Workforce pipeline erosion is a compounding, long-lead constraint that is already manifesting as
 an operational reality. The retirement wave among experienced practitioners in specialized
 disciplines is not a future risk — it is reducing effective capacity today. University and
 community college curricula can be improved to produce graduates with the applied judgment
 these roles require. The depth of knowledge held by retiring engineers and technicians in design
 for manufacturability, thermal spray, casting, forging, and repair is not being systematically
 captured. Rebuilding these pipelines requires curriculum reform, industry-sponsored experiential
 learning, apprenticeship frameworks, knowledge-capture initiatives, and new credentialing
 structures. Near-term investments in programs like University Turbine Systems Research (UTSR)
 can be utilized to enable universities to advance the state of the art in gas turbine technologies
 with direct guidance from major OEMs to maximize effectiveness.

Priority Recommendations
Based on the full body of workshop discussions, the following priority recommendations are offered for
consideration:
 1. Scale Advanced and Additive Manufacturing (AM) to Bypass Bottlenecks
 Mechanisms such as cooperative funding agreements should be directed toward accelerating the
 industrialization of additive manufacturing and rapid prototyping. The goal is to alleviate some of
 the current five-year lead times for traditional castings and forgings by using AM for complex
 geometries, legacy part production, and rapid tooling. R&D examples could include improving
 high-temperature creep capability (1200°F+), increasing build speeds by 50% to make AM a
 viable alternative for rotating hardware and similar topics to accelerate industrialization.
 2. Secure Critical Material Supply Chains through R&D
 The DOE should fund applied research into alternative material systems and innovative coating
 techniques to reduce dependency on geopolitically exposed elements like cobalt, yttrium,
 hafnium, and rhenium. This includes developing advanced recycling technologies for specialty
 alloys and tungsten carbide powders to recapture essential materials from retired components,
 ensuring a more resilient domestic "circular" supply chain.
 3. Deploy Digital Twins and In-Situ Process Monitoring
 To improve manufacturing yields and quality, R&D should be focused on "factory digital twins"
 and in-situ monitoring. By capturing real-time metadata during the manufacturing process
 (especially in AM and thermal spray), OEMs can identify and mitigate defects as they happen.
 This "digital thread" should follow the component throughout its lifecycle, using physics-

 xv

 informed models to optimize performance and maintenance.Investments in accelerating AI-
 enabled modeling can also unlock improved efficiency and alternative system architectures.

 4. Optimize the "Supply Chain Valve" through Advanced Repair and Life Extension
 Since repairing a component is faster than building a new one, the workshop recommends heavy
 R&D investment in advanced repair technologies. This includes using AI-enabled federated
 learning to standardize repairs across global networks, developing robust residual life assessment
 models, and using additive repair to return components to service with better-than-original
 performance (e.g., improved cooling geometries and coatings).
 5. Rebuild the Specialized Workforce Pipeline
 To counter the "retirement wave" of experienced engineers, the government should modernize
 specialized curricula and apprenticeship frameworks in disciplines like single-crystal casting and
 thermal spray. Funding should be directed toward programs like the University Turbine Systems
 Research (UTSR) to ensure that university R&D is aligned with OEM needs and is introducing
 university students to relevant technology development, while also implementing systematic
 "knowledge-capture" initiatives to preserve the expertise of retiring practitioners.

 1. PLENARY PRESENTATION 1 —OVERVIEW OF GAS TURBINE SUPPLY CHAIN
 CHALLENGES AND TECHNOLOGY PATHWAYS

The gas turbine market recorded its second-highest megawatt total ever sold last year, with sales spanning
every technology class — E-class, F-class, advanced class, and aeroderivatives — signaling that demand
is broad-based and structurally driven, not cyclical. Critically, the current surge differs from the bubble of
2000, which was caused by a single factor (natural gas pricing) and concentrated in one technology class.
Today's drivers are multiple and durable: data center electrification, broader industrial electrification,
retirement of coal capacity, and growing international demand. Even if data center growth were to
moderate, the remaining demand vectors alone would sustain elevated market pressure for years.

while advanced-class units, though fewer in number, account for roughly two-thirds of all megawatts

sold, making them the central focus for utilities and the most consequential segment for technology and
supply chain investment.

1.2 SUPPLY CHAIN BOTTLENECKS AND COST PRESSURES

Lead times for new gas turbines now extend to five years or more for large units. Cost escalation is severe
and broad, affecting not just OEM pricing but Engineering, procurement, and construction (EPC)
contracts, which Bobby identified as a particularly acute and underappreciated source of inflation. Tariff
uncertainty adds a further layer of unpredictability that complicates planning at every level of the supply
chain.

Compounding new-build challenges is the installed fleet: the large cohort of units commissioned around
2000 is reaching end-of-life or requiring major life extension work — rotors, casings, and other long-lead
components that compete directly with parts needed for new units. This creates a structural double bind:
OEMs and suppliers must simultaneously serve new-build demand and sustain an aging installed base
with the same constrained supply base. Further, utilities currently ordering large batches of gas turbines
risk a synchronized major-maintenance surge 10–15 years from now, underscoring the need to begin
staggering operational profiles today to distribute future service demand.

EPRI's AM3 initiative (Advanced Manufacturing Methods and Materials) represents a multi-year, multi-

frontier is using AM for component repair (tip repair and full blade build-up), which would reduce
dependence on replacement castings and extend the useful life of existing hardware.

1.4 STANDARDS, QUALIFICATION, AND THE PATH TO SCALE

A key barrier to realizing AM's full supply chain potential is the qualification and standards landscape.
EPRI is actively engaged with SAE on powder feedstock specifications and participates in ASME boiler
and pressure vessel codes, recognizing that without qualification frameworks, new manufacturing
methods cannot replace conventional supply channels regardless of their technical merits. The America
Makes program, in which EPRI plays a leading role, has generated lessons learned — particularly in
powder bed fusion — that are being fed back into evolving industry specifications. The consensus:
standards are still in early revisions, and the window for shaping them is now.

1.5 GAPS AND OPPORTUNITIES

Several cross-cutting gaps emerged as priorities for collaborative action among OEMs, the supply chain,
national laboratories, and universities:

• Supplier qualification and diversification: Expanding the approved supplier base is universally
 desired but technically and procedurally complex. Coordinated frameworks for qualification —
 drawing on national laboratory capabilities and academic research — could accelerate this without
 compromising reliability.

• Rotor and long-lead component supply: Rotors represent one of the most acute bottlenecks across all
 gas turbine classes. New manufacturing approaches, material innovations, and coordinated capacity
 investments are needed.

• Life extension and fleet optimization: Maximizing the output and lifespan of the existing fleet —
 through capacity uprates, materials upgrades, and advanced coatings — reduces pressure on new-
 build supply chains and defers capital expenditure. EPRI's ongoing work on mission-profile-based
 capacity assessments (in collaboration with John Crane) provides a quantitative basis for prioritizing
 these investments.

• Fuel flexibility and materials readiness: Despite shifting U.S. policy, global demand for hydrogen and
 low-carbon fuel capability remains real. Materials and coatings R&D must continue to address the
 distinct degradation mechanisms of alternative fuels, particularly for turbine hot-section components.

• Digital tools and AI integration: Digital twins, AI-assisted manufacturing process control, and
 predictive maintenance represent under-leveraged tools for improving throughput and reliability
 across the supply chain.

• Heat recovery steam generator (HRSG) and balance-of-plant alignment: As gas turbine performance
 advances, heat recovery steam generators and balance-of-plant components risk becoming the binding
 constraint. Materials specification compliance along the full EPC supply chain needs closer oversight.

1.6 CONCLUSION

Bobby closed with a call to collective action. EPRI's research — while substantial — is, in their own
framing, a starting point rather than a solution. The value of convening OEMs, suppliers, national
laboratories, and universities in the same room is to catalyze a coordinated roadmap: one that addresses
qualification, capacity, materials science, and operational strategy simultaneously. The storm, as the
presenter put it, is already here. The task of this workshop is to build the shelter.

 2. PLENARY PRESENTATION 2 — OEM PERSPECTIVE ON GAS TURBINE SUPPLY
 CHAIN CHALLENGES AND OPPORTUNITIES

 Figure 2. Presenter: Jeremee Wetherby (GE Vernova).

Jeremee opened with a striking reminder of the industry's foundation: gas turbines built decades ago are
still running — some for 40 years — and they remain among the fastest, most power-dense sources of
electricity available anywhere on the planet. Far from being a legacy technology in decline, the installed
base of gas turbines represents an enormous and often underappreciated strategic asset. The central
message of his presentation was both urgent and optimistic: the demands being placed on these machines
are changing faster than the supply chain can currently respond, and closing that gap will require
unprecedented collaboration between OEMs, suppliers, national laboratories, and universities.

2.1 A SHIFTING OPERATIONAL LANDSCAPE

The traditional categories of baseload and peaking operation are giving way to entirely new duty cycles.
Units are now being continuously ramped up and down — not shut off — to follow the intermittent
output of renewable energy sources and to serve the insatiable power demands of AI-scale data centers.
Amazon Web Services facilities once drew 300–500 megawatts; next-generation AI data centers are
projected to require three to five gigawatts — comparable to the output of a small city. This
transformation is creating wear-out mechanisms that the industry has never encountered before:
components designed for steady-state or periodic cycling are now experiencing fatigue patterns for which
there is no established engineering playbook. Jeremee flagged synchronous condenser operation and
extended standby on turning gear as two specific new modes demanding immediate research attention.
Developing the materials science, operational models, and predictive diagnostics to manage these novel
duty cycles is an open problem that no single OEM can solve alone — it is precisely the kind of

 5

foundational, pre-competitive research where national laboratories and universities can provide critical
leverage.

2.2 PARTS: THE DEFINING BOTTLENECK

No theme recurred more forcefully in Jeremee's presentation than the parts shortage. Advanced hot gas
path components — turbine blades, nozzles, and vanes cast from single-crystal superalloys — are
produced by a vanishingly small number of casting houses worldwide, and every major OEM is
competing for the same limited capacity. The challenge is not simply one of market demand; it is
structural. These components require exotic alloys, complex ceramic molds for intricate internal cooling
passages, and extraordinarily tight tolerances. Yield rates remain stubbornly low, and any defect caught
late in a multi-step manufacturing process wastes enormous time and material.

  “Advanced hot gas path components — turbine blades, nozzles, and vanes cast
  from single-crystal superalloys — are produced by a vanishingly small number
 of casting houses worldwide, and every major OEM is competing for the same
 limited capacity.”

Additive manufacturing — particularly directed energy deposition and powder bed fusion — holds
genuine promise for increasing throughput, enabling rapid prototyping, and eventually restoring worn
rotating components without full replacement. However, Jeremee was candid that gaining engineering
confidence in additively manufactured rotating parts, which bear centrifugal loads of extraordinary
magnitude, will require rigorous qualification frameworks. This is a natural domain for collaboration:
OEMs can define the performance envelopes and failure modes, while national labs and universities
contribute independent characterization, non-destructive evaluation methods, and the computational
models needed to certify additive repairs. Faster prototyping cycles — reducing the time from concept to
tested hardware — were identified as equally important, since the current iteration loop is far too slow to
keep pace with the rate at which new operational challenges are emerging.

2.3 LARGE STRUCTURAL COMPONENTS AND GEOPOLITICAL RISK

As gas turbines have grown larger — Jeremee noted that the jump from F-class to H-class represents
roughly a 40% increase in physical size — the number of forge shops and foundries capable of producing
the necessary wheels, discs, and casings has shrunk, not grown. There are only a handful of facilities on
earth that can manufacture the forgings required for today's largest machines. When geopolitical
disruption strikes — as it has repeatedly over the past decade — OEM supply chains can be compromised
nearly overnight. Diversifying and expanding domestic forging and casting capability is therefore not
merely an economic optimization problem; it is a matter of energy security. Jeremee identified this as one
of his supply chain team's highest priorities, and it represents a clear opportunity for public-private
investment in manufacturing infrastructure and workforce development.

2.4 ADVANCED MANUFACTURING, AUTOMATION, AND AI QUALITY CONTROL

Jeremee described a compelling near-term opportunity to deploy robotics, automation, and AI-driven in-
process inspection to improve manufacturing yield and reduce scrap. The core insight is that defects
caught at step one of a complex manufacturing process cost a fraction of what they cost when discovered
at step nine. Embedding machine vision and AI quality control throughout the production workflow —
not just at final inspection — could significantly increase effective throughput without adding physical

 6

capacity. This is an area where collaboration between OEMs and research institutions is especially
natural: the underlying algorithms for anomaly detection, process monitoring, and predictive quality
control are broadly applicable, and their development need not be proprietary to any single manufacturer.

2.5 CRITICAL AND ALTERNATIVE ALLOYS: AN EMERGING VULNERABILITY

Perhaps the most urgent systemic risk Jeremee raised was the fragility of the supply of critical alloys.
Yttrium, hafnium, and other rare or specialty elements are essential to the thermal barrier coatings and
superalloy chemistries that allow hot gas path components to function at firing temperatures that would
otherwise destroy them. When a new industrial consumer enters a market for one of these materials — as
happened with hafnium — prices can increase tenfold or more in a matter of days, forcing OEMs to
scramble for alternative chemistries under severe time pressure. Jeremee argued that the industry cannot
continue to respond reactively. There is a compelling need for proactive materials research — mapping
potential substitutes, building redundant supply chains, and developing next-generation alloy systems
before the next disruption arrives. National laboratories, with their unique capabilities in materials
synthesis, characterization, and computational alloy design, are natural partners for this work. Universities
can contribute through fundamental research into high-entropy alloys, environmental barrier coatings, and
accelerated materials qualification methodologies.

 “There is a compelling need for proactive materials research — mapping
 potential substitutes, building redundant supply chains, and developing next-
 generation alloy systems before the next disruption arrives.”

2.6 THE OPPORTUNITY AHEAD

Jeremee closed with a call to action grounded in hard-won optimism. Thirty years ago, a 60% efficient
gas turbine was considered physically impossible. Today, combined-cycle plants routinely exceed 64%
efficiency — an achievement built on exactly the kind of sustained, collaborative innovation between
OEMs, materials scientists, and government-funded researchers that this workshop embodies. The
challenges ahead — novel duty cycles, parts shortages, forging constraints, critical alloy vulnerability,
and the need for dramatically faster innovation cycles — are formidable. But Jeremee framed each as an
opportunity: every gas turbine in the installed base has untapped value waiting to be unlocked, and the
engineers in the room have both the skills and the urgency to unlock it. The market is changing too fast
for any single organization to keep pace alone. The institutions that come together now — sharing pre-
competitive knowledge, co-investing in manufacturing infrastructure, and building the workforce of the
next generation — will define the trajectory of reliable power for decades to come.

 3. PLENARY PRESENTATION 3 — THE 2026 INDUSTRIAL GAS TURBINE MARKET—
 DEMAND, DISRUPTION, AND SUPPLY CHAIN CONSEQUENCES

 Figure 3. Presenter: Anthony Brough (Dora Partners & Company LLC).

Anthony Brough of Dora Partners & Company gave a sweeping assessment of the industrial gas turbine
(IGT) market, drawing on Dora Partners’ proprietary global database—the only commercially available
dataset tracking gas turbines from 600 kW through 300+ MW, spanning over 56,000 installed units and
including orders not publicly reported by OEMs. His presentation provided the market intelligence
foundation for everything the workshop examined: the forces generating unprecedented demand, the
structural questions that could reshape that demand over the coming decade, and the supply chain realities
that make meeting it so difficult.

Brough was candid from the outset about the inherent limitations of forecasting, noting that any model is
wrong the moment it is finalized. However, he offered conclusions about where the market is headed and
what the industry must do to keep pace. His message for this workshop was direct: the gas turbine supply
chain is operating under extraordinary strain, the window for action is measured in years not decades, and
the magnitude of the opportunity—and the risk of missing it—is larger than at any prior point in the
industry’s history.

3.1 A MARKET AT RECORD-BREAKING SCALE

Brough opened with order data that set an immediate tone of urgency. In 2025, global gas turbine orders
reached approximately 103 GW—a figure he described as nearly record-breaking—comprising well over
1,000 units. On a year-over-year basis, 2025 saw a 72 percent increase in MW orders and a 39 percent
increase in unit orders compared to 2024. He further estimated that between 15 and 30 GW of additional
orders likely occurred in 2025 but had not yet been publicly announced, suggesting the true scale of
demand may be even larger than reported figures indicate.

Critically, Brough emphasized that these figures are not merely a transitory spike. His firm’s ten-year
baseline forecast projects $354 billion in new packaged gas turbine genset orders between 2026 and
2035—a number he described as something he would never have predicted even a few years ago. The
upside case, if current market dynamics persist, pushes that figure above $400 billion. The aftermarket

 9

picture is equally dramatic: Dora’s baseline projects over $437 billion in aftermarket service revenue over
the same period, driven by rising utilization rates, escalating parts pricing, and the growing fleet of
turbines that will begin consuming replacement components in earnest over the coming years. In
combination, new unit sales and aftermarket services represent the strongest ten-year outlook Dora
Partners has ever produced.

These numbers carry a direct implication for the supply chain. A market of this magnitude, sustained over
a decade, cannot be served by the supply chain as it currently exists. Every level of the supply chain—
from raw materials and castings through completed components, subsystems, and final assembly—faces
demand it was not sized to meet. Brough’s data provided a quantitative backdrop for many of the
discussions held at the workshop.

 “A market of this magnitude, sustained over a decade, cannot be served by the
 supply chain as it currently exists. Every level of the supply chain—from raw
 materials and castings through completed components, subsystems, and final
 assembly—faces demand it was not sized to meet.”

3.2 FIVE FORCES SHAPING THE DECADE AHEAD

Brough structured his market outlook around five major forces that his firm has incorporated into its
scenario-based forecasting model. He noted that in prior years, historical order data alone could explain
roughly 80 percent of future market behavior. That figure has now declined to approximately 62 percent,
with market dynamics—the five forces below—accounting for a full 30 percent of the forecast direction.
This shift in forecasting methodology itself reflects how structurally unusual the current moment is.

3.2.1 Artificial Intelligence and Data Center Demand

The most dramatic near-term driver of gas turbine demand is the explosion in data center construction and
AI compute infrastructure. Brough reported that gas turbine orders for data centers and distributed power
applications increased by more than twenty times in 2025 compared to 2024, measured on a total plant
capacity basis. The number of operational US data centers grew by 853 facilities in a single year.
Goldman Sachs Research projects that by 2027, AI server racks will require approximately 50 times more
power than equivalent cloud infrastructure did in 2022. Bloomberg estimates US data center energy
demand alone could exceed 106 GW by 2035.

Brough’s assessment of how long this driver sustains itself was nuanced. For large gas turbines (150–300
MW and above), he expects OEM orders to remain strong through 2026–2028, with a potential
moderation in order activity beginning around 2029—though he stressed this is a moderation in new
orders, not in deliveries or utilization. For smaller turbines (1–100 MW), he sees demand remaining
robust well beyond 2029. He also acknowledged a genuine uncertainty: local government resistance to
data center siting and pressure over rising utility rates is growing in many communities, and this could
shift data center investment toward other geographies, including Asia.

3.2.2 Geopolitical Events and Oil & Gas Sector Demand

A point Brough made with particular emphasis—and one he felt had been underappreciated in the
morning’s earlier discussions—is that approximately 50 percent of all industrial gas turbines in the world

 10

serve the oil and gas industry. Any analysis of the IGT market that does not account for oil & gas (O&G)
sector dynamics is, in his view, missing half the picture.

Geopolitical developments in Venezuela and Iran represent potential near-to-medium term demand
catalysts. Venezuela in particular carries significant aftermarket opportunity: decades of deferred
maintenance on a large legacy fleet of gas turbines means that any normalization of trade and investment
relationships could generate substantial demand for overhauls, upgrades, and replacement units, primarily
in the 1–20 MW and 20–100 MW categories. Upstream development in both countries would take five to
seven years of Front-end engineering and design (FEED) studies and project development before
generating new unit orders, meaning the near-term effect is concentrated in aftermarket and mid-sized
turbines. Brough was appropriately measured about the long-term trajectory of either situation, noting that
regime change and diplomatic normalization are genuinely unpredictable.

3.2.3 Small Modular Reactors and Coal Plant Policy

Small modular reactors (SMRs), generally below 300 MW in capacity, face no major unresolved
technical challenges—the physics and engineering are well understood. The barriers are commercial and
regulatory: permitting timelines, financing structures, and public acceptance. Brough anticipates the first
commercial SMR operation around 2029, with meaningful market expansion not arriving until 2030–
2035. In the short term, he sees no material impact on IGT demand from SMR deployment.

On coal plant policy, the current US administration’s rollback of EPA regulations around mercury
emissions, toxic air pollutants, and coal ash standards effectively extends the operating life of older coal
plants that would otherwise have been forced to retire by the late 2030s. Brough views this as real and
durable for at least the next three years. However, he was careful to note that the global picture differs:
outside the United States, the march toward coal plant retirement continues, and international markets—
which represent a major portion of industrial gas turbines demand—remain on a trajectory where gas
turbines benefit from coal’s declining role. Longer term, he acknowledged that SMR deployment at scale
could reduce demand for large gas turbines, but this effect falls well outside the current workshop’s
planning horizon.

3.2.4 Grid-Scale Renewable Energy

Brough presented the renewable energy picture with unusual directness. Utility-scale solar photovoltaic
is, in 2025–2026, the single lowest-cost source of new electricity generation in most of the world, even
without subsidies. According to Lazard’s June 2025 Levelized cost of energy (LCOE) analysis,
unsubsidized utility-scale solar costs between $38 and $78 per MWh—a levelized cost approximately $20
per MWh below that of a new combined cycle gas plant, and roughly $150 per MWh below a gas peaking
unit. Utility-scale solar outpaced all other new generation capacity additions globally in 2025.

Brough was clear that ignoring this reality would be a mistake, and he said so plainly: “any industry
participant not paying attention to grid-scale renewables is ignoring reality.” At the same time, his
forecast conclusion is that large gas turbines will not see meaningful demand erosion from renewables
until 2028 or beyond. For smaller gas turbines under 100 MW, renewables are generally complementary
rather than competitive—smaller turbines serve as flexible peaking and balancing resources that pair well
with intermittent solar and wind capacity. The net effect is that renewables represent a long-term
structural headwind for large baseload turbines, but not a near-term one.

 11

3.2.5 Non-OEM Market Entrants

Perhaps the most provocative element of Brough’s presentation was his discussion of non-traditional
market entrants—companies that are not conventional gas turbine OEMs but are entering the industrial
power generation market using converted or repurposed aerospace engines. He identified three companies
as emblematic of this trend: Boom Supersonic, which has committed a 42 MW engine (not yet in metal at
the time of the workshop) to a 1.2 GW data center power supply contract with Crusoe Energy; ProEnergy,
which has successfully commercialized the PE6000—a 50 MW power plant built around retired GE CF6
aero engines—and reportedly took orders for over 30 units in 2025; and FTAI Power, which is converting
retired CFM56 engines into approximately 25 MW ground power units with plans for up to 100 mobile
gensets in 2027.

Brough assessed the near-term impact of these entrants as limited, given the overall strength of demand
across all market segments. But his longer-term view was emphatic: this is a potential game-changer,
particularly for gas turbines in the 20–50 MW range. The supply of retired aero engines available for
conversion is effectively inexhaustible over a 5–15 year horizon—there are tens of thousands of CFM56
and CF6 engines that will retire from commercial aviation. If non-OEM entrants establish themselves in
the industrial market, they will compete on price and delivery time in ways that conventional OEM
product lines cannot easily match, putting significant pressure on volumes and margins for medium-sized
turbines.

3.3 THE SUPPLY CHAIN: SCALE, STRUCTURE, AND STRESS

Having established the scale of demand, Brough turned to supply chain structure and the challenge of
meeting that demand. He described the IGT supply chain in four tiers. Level 0 encompasses raw
materials—nickel and other specialty alloys, integrated circuits, and other high-demand inputs. Level 1
covers forgings, castings, coated components, sensors, seals, and high-demand consumables. Level 2
includes completed components and subsystems—blades, vanes, disks, rotors, fuel nozzles, actuators,
valves, casings, and control systems. Level 3 represents final assembly products, packaged gensets and
compressor sets, installation services, and aftermarket supply.

A critical structural feature of this supply chain, Brough emphasized, is that Levels 0, 1, and 2 are shared
with the aerospace industry. This is not a minor overlap: as of March 2026, over 16,000 commercial
aircraft were in backlog, representing at least 32,000 gas turbine engines awaiting production. The IGT
backlog adds an estimated 2,200 to 2,600 additional units. Critically, the components required for a single
large industrial gas turbine—a GE 7H, a Siemens SGT6-8000H, a Mitsubishi J-series—are vastly larger
and more material-intensive than an aircraft engine component. The consequence is that even though the
IGT backlog is numerically a fraction of the aerospace backlog, its demands on raw material and forging
capacity are disproportionately large.

Brough’s conclusion on the supply chain was blunt: the combined impact of COVID-era disruptions, the
aerospace supercycle, and the IGT demand surge has created a distressed supply chain with no clear near-
term resolution. New unit pricing has risen dramatically since 2023—driven by a combination of genuine
cost increases and, he acknowledged, some degree of margin expansion—and Dora Partners projects that
prices are unlikely to return to 2023 levels. Some price moderation is anticipated beginning around 2029
as supply and demand move toward normalization, but elevated pricing is expected to persist for the
remainder of the decade.

On the aftermarket specifically, Brough noted that the growing sophistication and financial backing of
independent third-party repair and overhaul providers is creating competitive pressure for OEMs. These
companies are increasingly able to offer high-quality repairs at competitive prices, and the availability of

 12

retired aero engine assets for conversion gives them additional leverage. OEMs must navigate the
challenge of supporting their installed base while competing for aftermarket revenue against well-
resourced independents.

3.4 IMPLICATIONS FOR THE WORKSHOP: GAPS AND OPPORTUNITIES

Brough’s presentation, while framed as a market overview rather than a technical research agenda, carried
several important implications for the gaps and opportunities that the rest of the workshop addressed.

• The business case for supply chain investment is enormous. The quantitative scale of demand—$354
 billion in new units and $437 billion in aftermarket services over ten years—establishes the business
 case for supply chain investment at every tier. This is not a marginal market. The economic returns
 from solving supply chain bottlenecks are commensurate with the magnitude of the opportunity, and
 the case for sustained R&D investment, workforce development, and manufacturing capacity
 expansion is strong.

• Oil and gas applications cannot be overlooked. Brough’s explicit point that 50 percent of the global
 IGT fleet serves oil and gas applications is a reminder that supply chain solutions must be designed
 for the full market. Pipeline compressors, offshore platform power generators, and liquified natural
 gas (LNG) plant drivers place different demands on materials, coatings, and maintenance cycles than
 utility-scale power generation turbines. National laboratory and university research programs that
 focus exclusively on large power generation turbines are addressing only half the relevant design
 space.

• Aerospace-IGT supply chain overlap is both a constraint and a platform for collaboration. The shared
 supply chain between aerospace and industrial gas turbines creates both a constraint and an
 opportunity. It is a constraint because aerospace demand crowds out IGT supply at Levels 0, 1, and 2.
 It is also an opportunity because technologies, process improvements, and materials advances
 developed for one sector can benefit the other. Research institutions and national laboratories are
 well-positioned to facilitate this cross-sector technology transfer in ways that individual companies,
 competing with each other for the same supply base, cannot.

• Non-OEM entrants are a long-term structural risk that the supply chain must anticipate. The
 emergence of non-OEM entrants using converted aerospace engines is a structural development that
 OEMs and their supply chains need to take seriously. These entrants are not constrained by the
 qualification and certification timelines that govern new OEM products, and they have access to a
 large and growing pool of retired aero engines. The supply chain implications are significant: if non-
 OEM platforms scale successfully, they will create demand for aftermarket parts and services that
 does not flow through OEM channels, potentially disrupting the business models of both OEMs and
 their tier-one suppliers.

• Long-term electrification trends sustain the demand signal. Brough’s discussion of electrification
 trends—the global growth in electric vehicles, expanding middle-class energy consumption, and
 increasing industrial electrification—as a factor distinct from but additive to data center demand
 points toward a long-duration demand signal for gas turbines as firm power providers. This sustained
 demand justifies long-term investment in supply chain capacity, materials research, and workforce
 development that might otherwise be difficult to justify given the cyclical history of the power
 generation market.

3.5 CONCLUSION

Anthony’s plenary talk provided a clear and well-evidenced foundation for the workshop’s central
purpose. The industrial gas turbine market is, by every quantitative measure Dora Partners can construct,
at or approaching an all-time peak in terms of order volume, market value, and aftermarket opportunity.
The forces driving this demand—AI infrastructure, electrification, geopolitical realignment in oil and gas,
and the persistence of gas turbines as essential firm power providers—are durable enough to sustain
elevated demand for the better part of a decade.

The supply chain, however, was not built for this moment. It is shared with an equally stressed aerospace
industry, dependent on raw materials with constrained domestic supply, and staffed by a workforce whose
expert knowledge is eroding faster than it is being replenished. The gap between what the market requires
and what the supply chain can currently deliver defines the central challenge for OEMs, suppliers,
national laboratories, and universities alike. Brough’s data made clear that the stakes are high enough to
justify the kind of sustained, cross-institutional collaboration that this workshop was convened to
advance.

  4. PLENARY PRESENTATION 4 — BRIDGING RESEARCH AND MANUFACTURING:
 LESSONS FROM THE START LAB ON COOLING TECHNOLOGY DEVELOPMENT

 Figure 4. Presenter: Karen Thole (University of Michigan).

Professor Karen Thole — one of the foremost researchers in gas turbine heat transfer and cooling —
opened her remarks with a candid observation that framed the entire discussion: the gas turbine
community understands the physics of advanced cooling technologies far better than it understands how
to manufacture them. Drawing on more than a decade of work through the Steady Thermal Aero Research
Turbine (START) Lab, she presented a compelling body of evidence that manufacturing variability and
process maturity represent the most significant barriers to deploying next-generation turbine cooling
technologies — and, by extension, to improving turbine efficiency, durability, and fuel flexibility.

4.1 THE TECHNOLOGY-TO-PRODUCTION TIMELINE PROBLEM

Professor Thole introduced a striking data-driven observation: from the moment foundational physics
research begins on a new cooling concept, it typically takes 15 to 20 years before that technology reaches
a production turbine. More sobering is that it takes roughly twice as long to solve the manufacturing
challenges as it does to understand the underlying aerothermal physics. This asymmetry is a critical gap
with direct implications for the workshop's focus on supply chain readiness.

  “it takes roughly twice as long to solve the manufacturing challenges as it does
 to understand the underlying aerothermal physics”

 15

The gas turbine community is, in many cases, ahead of its own manufacturing ecosystem. Addressing this
imbalance — accelerating the transition from physics discovery to manufacturable, producible hardware
— is one of the most important challenges for OEMs, suppliers, national laboratories, and universities to
tackle collaboratively.

4.2 THE START LAB AND THE NATIONAL EXPERIMENTAL TURBINE (NEXT BLADE)

The START Lab has been uniquely positioned to test real engine parts under engine-representative
conditions — matching Reynolds number, Mach number, and Biot number — while simultaneously
studying manufacturing effects at the level of individual film cooling holes on spinning blades. The lab's
signature capability is full-field infrared thermography of rotating blades, providing spatially resolved
temperature maps with sufficient resolution to detect manufacturing-induced variations in cooling
performance hole by hole.

A landmark outcome of the START Lab is the National Experimental Turbine (NeXT) blade — a fully
cooled vane and blade geometry developed collaboratively with Honeywell, Siemens, Pratt & Whitney,
and Solar Turbines. The NeXT blade incorporates all modern cooling features identified as important by
industry partners, belongs to no single company, and serves as a shared, open testbed. Data is shared
broadly with government agencies and industry partners, making it a model for the kind of pre-
competitive collaboration this workshop aims to foster. The NeXT blade has been used both for
aerothermal and durability studies and, critically, as a manufacturing testbed — a role that has proven as
valuable as its original aerothermal purpose.

4.3 THE REALITIES OF MANUFACTURING ADVANCED COOLING TECHNOLOGIES

Thole's team undertook a multi-year program — spanning the careers of four sets of graduate students
and running from 2016 to 2023 — to transition research cooling technologies funded under DOE's
University Turbine Systems Research (UTSR) program into a real Pratt & Whitney airfoil. The effort
incorporated novel hole geometries (including the anti-vortex hole), trailing-edge pin fins, and varied
electrical discharge machining (EDM) methods, and was manufactured in partnership with Hitchiner, an
emerging casting company willing to engage with university-scale development programs where
established single-crystal foundries would not.

The lessons were largely manufacturing lessons. Core shifts, hole-to-hole flow variability, EDM-induced
geometric defects, and cooling flow non-uniformity were pervasive in a development environment.
Professor Thole showed flow parameter data with variations large enough — and linked to thermal
gradients sharp enough — to reduce blade life by approximately 50% in some configurations. While
production blades benefit from tighter process control, these findings underscore that development-phase
manufacturing variability can fatally compromise the ability to evaluate and de-risk a new cooling
technology before committing to a full casting development cycle.

4.4 ADDITIVE MANUFACTURING: A POWERFUL TOOL WITH SIGNIFICANT
 UNRESOLVED CHALLENGES

A central theme of the presentation was the role of additive manufacturing (AM) in accelerating cooling
technology development — and the stubborn barriers that remain. The START Lab demonstrated that the
NeXT blade could be built using AM in a fraction of the time required for traditional casting, enabling
rapid screening of cooling geometries. A compelling database of heat transfer performance versus
pressure drop across multiple internal cooling channel geometries — representing years of graduate

 16

research — was assembled in a compressed timeframe using AM coupons, illustrating AM's power as a
discovery tool.

However, Professor Thole was direct about AM's current limitations for turbine applications:

• Surface roughness on internal features (turbulators, pin fins, serpentine passages) is dramatically
 higher than in cast hardware, and cannot be remediated by post-processing. This makes direct
 performance comparisons between AM and cast configurations fundamentally ambiguous.

• Cooling hole manufacturability remains an unresolved problem — feature sizes at the scale of film
 cooling holes are, in many cases, still beyond reliable AM capability, requiring a secondary drilling
 step regardless.

• Vendor-to-machine-to-operator variability is severe. An inter-laboratory round-robin study —
 sending identical wavy-channel coupon geometries to multiple vendors with different (and same)
 machines — produced dramatically different surface morphologies and correspondingly wide scatter
 in heat transfer and pressure drop performance. This variability is not yet predictable from first
 principles.

• Workforce and supply chain instability: Professor Thole observed that just as the START Lab would
 build confidence with a capable AM vendor, that vendor would be acquired by an OEM or lose key
 personnel — forcing the team to restart qualification from scratch. This is a systemic supply chain
 issue that extends well beyond academia.

A key unresolved research question crystallized from this work: Can an AM blade reliably de-risk a
cooling technology for subsequent investment in a cast development blade? The honest answer, based on
current data, is not yet with confidence — a finding with direct relevance to OEM R&D investment
strategies and to the role national laboratories might play in establishing AM process standards.

4.5 RAPID CASTING AND THE PATH FORWARD

Professor Thole presented a promising near-term bridge: rapid casting, in which AM is used not to make
the blade directly but to produce the tooling for investment casting. Working with Honeywell, the team
produced a NeXT blade in approximately seven weeks using this approach — a dramatic compression of
the traditional tooling timeline. This hybrid pathway retains the material properties and geometric
precision of single-crystal casting while leveraging AM's speed and flexibility for tooling, and may
represent the most practical near-term route to faster cooling technology insertion.

4.6 RELEVANCE TO THE WORKSHOP: GAPS AND OPPORTUNITIES

Professor Thole's presentation surfaces several themes directly relevant to the workshop's focus on supply
chain gaps and collaborative opportunities:

For OEMs and the supply chain: The shortage of casting foundries willing to engage in development-
phase programs — highlighted by the START Lab's struggle to find single-crystal casting partners —
points to a gap in the supply chain for pre-production development work. Investment in supplier
development and in shared development vehicles like the NeXT blade could help bridge this gap.

For national laboratories: The unresolved problem of AM process standardization and certification —
including establishing process-property relationships that are vendor-agnostic and machine-independent
— is a natural role for national laboratories. A community-level certification effort for AM in gas turbine

 17

applications, combining the process expertise of national labs with the application knowledge of OEMs
and the experimental infrastructure of university facilities like START, could substantially accelerate
AM's reliable use as a de-risking tool.

For universities: The START Lab and NeXT blade exemplify a model of open, pre-competitive
infrastructure that serves the entire community. Sustained DOE investment in such facilities — and in
maintaining their accessibility to industry partners for both shared and proprietary studies — is essential.
The 15-to-20-year physics-to-production timeline also underscores the need for long-duration, patient
research funding that bridges fundamental heat transfer physics and manufacturing process development,
a combination universities are uniquely positioned to provide.

On workforce: Implicitly throughout the presentation, Professor Thole highlighted that AM capability is
deeply tied to individual expertise — a workforce challenge that intersects directly with supply chain
resilience.

In closing, Professor Thole challenged the room to take seriously the manufacturing half of the
technology development equation. The gas turbine community has made remarkable progress in
understanding the physics of advanced cooling. The next frontier — and the next bottleneck — is
building the manufacturing science, supply chain depth, and shared infrastructure to turn that
understanding into reliable, producible hardware at the pace the energy transition demands.

5. PLENARY PRESENTATION 5 — QUALIFYING METAL ADDITIVE MANUFACTURING
 FOR FLIGHT — THE AIR FORCE RESEARCH LABORATORY'S PATH FORWARD

 Figure 5. Presenter: Mark D. Benedict (Air Force Research Laboratory).

Mark opened his talk with a deceptively simple provocation: how many metal additive manufacturing
process-material combinations currently exist at Technology Readiness Level 8 or higher? The audience's
guesses ranged widely, but Benedict's answer brought the challenge into sharp focus — the real number is
somewhere between two and five, perhaps as many as a handful by a generous count. Against the
backdrop of a modern turbine engine that may incorporate thousands of distinct metal compositions, alloy
states, and material conditions, that figure is not just modest — it is a strategic constraint of the first
order.

To illustrate just how consequential material maturity can be, Benedict reached back to the Messerschmitt
262, the world's first production jet-powered aircraft. Its Junkers 004 engine was originally designed
around a nickel-chromium-molybdenum superalloy — but wartime supply chain interdictions forced Nazi
Germany to substitute a manganese-titanium-chromium alloy instead. The consequence was an engine
with a service life of roughly ten hours before mandatory overhaul. When postwar investigators rebuilt
the engine using the intended alloy, service life jumped to 150 hours. The lesson Benedict drew was
pointed and directly applicable to the present day: in the additive manufacturing space, as in 1944, the
question of whether you have the right alloy — not just a serviceable one — is a fundamental determinant
of what is achievable.

5.1 A NEW POLICY FRAMEWORK FOR ADDITIVE QUALIFICATION

The centerpiece of Benedict's talk was the Air Force's recently published Flight System Bulletin EN-FSB-
25-1, the service's first and only significant airworthiness policy change of the past year. Its subject is
preliminary qualification for metal additive manufacturing — a structured, time-bounded, and
deliberately modest process designed to give program offices a credible on-ramp for considering AM
components in flight-critical applications, including turbine engines.

The fundamental problem the bulletin addresses is one of qualification economics and timeline.
Transitioning a metal AM component to a flight-worthy application through the traditional pathway —
full C- or D-basis material property development, comprehensive testing campaigns, extended design and
evaluation cycles — can take a decade and cost many millions of dollars. That timeline is incompatible
with the supply chain urgency the workshop has been examining, and it dramatically discourages program

 19

offices from even considering additive as an option. At the same time, jumping directly from early-stage
material characterization to full qualification is a leap of faith that the Air Force's airworthiness
framework cannot sanction.

The bulletin creates an intermediate step. Designed to be completed in roughly three to four months at a
cost on the order of $150,000 to several hundred thousand dollars depending on material and testing
approach, the preliminary qualification assessment is explicitly framed as a knowledge check rather than
a data mountain. The Air Force is not asking vendors to demonstrate that they have achieved mastery —
it is asking them to demonstrate that they understand their material system well enough to be worth
investing in further. The questions are pointed: Does the vendor know what to look for in a
metallographic evaluation? Can they interpret crack growth test data, or are they simply forwarding
specimens to a test house without understanding what comes back? Do they have volumetric inspection
capability — CT or equivalent — and can they make sense of the results? Can they articulate,
quantitatively, what a good micrograph looks like versus a bad one?

 “The Air Force is not asking vendors to demonstrate that they have achieved
 mastery — it is asking them to demonstrate that they understand their material
 system well enough to be worth investing in further.”

This emphasis on demonstrated knowledge rather than demonstrated compliance reflects a hard-won
lesson from Benedict's two decades of working with additive manufacturers: the weakest link in the
qualification chain is frequently not the data itself, but the vendor's capacity to generate and interpret data
meaningfully. A vendor who has purchased a printer, adopted a canned parameter set, and gotten lucky on
tensile coupons is a very different proposition from one who understands the relationship between process
variables, microstructure, and mechanical performance. The bulletin is designed to distinguish between
the two.

The document is process-agnostic — applicable to powder bed fusion, directed energy deposition, wire-
based processes, and emerging methods like friction stir additive — and is explicitly tailorable to the
specifics of the material, the process, and the application. It calls for a defined minimum set of
mechanical tests (tensile, bearing, shear, compression, with provisions for stress corrosion cracking and
intergranular corrosion sensitivity as warranted) and an initial metallographic characterization, but it does
not require the full sweep of dynamic properties and design allowables that a mature qualification
campaign would demand. It is, in Benedict's framing, a 90% solution — enough to say with confidence
that a vendor is worth investing in, and enough to give a program office the basis it needs to authorize that
investment.

Two companies have now completed the process and received endorsement from the Air Force; Benedict
was unable to name the programs due to their classification status, but noted that the cleanest path through
the bulletin has been with new program starts, where vendors can design their quality systems to the new
requirements from the outset rather than retrofitting historical data into a framework it was not designed
to meet.

5.2 THE ALLOY GAP AND THE ROLE OF COLLABORATIVE INVESTMENT

Beyond the qualification framework itself, Benedict identified a deeper structural challenge: the profound
narrowness of the alloy space that additive manufacturing has matured to flight-applicable standards. The
industry has appropriately led with its strongest materials — titanium 6-4, Inconel 718, 625, and cobalt-

 20

chrome being the primary examples — but these represent a tiny slice of the alloy landscape required for
high-performance turbine applications. Maturing new alloys through the full qualification pyramid is
enormously expensive and time-consuming when done in isolation by individual companies or programs.

This is where Benedict sees a clear and urgent role for coordinated government investment, working
alongside OEMs and research institutions. The transfer learning embedded in the hard-won experience of
qualifying existing alloys — the test methodologies, the failure modes encountered, the process-
microstructure relationships established — should be systematically leveraged to accelerate the
maturation of new materials. Rather than each new alloy-process combination starting from scratch, a
coordinated database of qualification precedents and failure lessons could dramatically compress the
timeline. Benedict noted that government-supported manufacturing institutes are already beginning to
surface demand for maturing alternative materials, and he sees the Flight Systems Bulletin as a tool that
can focus and accelerate those investments by providing a clear, agreed-upon target for what "sufficiently
mature" means at each stage of the qualification spectrum.

5.3 TOWARD AGILE, DISTRIBUTED MANUFACTURING

Benedict closed with a strategic vision that extends well beyond the qualification document itself. The Air
Force is increasingly interested in manufacturing models that are less dependent on hard tooling,
centralized megafactories, and years-long infrastructure investments. The "Endless Forge" solicitation —
publicly available on SAM.gov — reflects this direction, targeting small production networks capable of
responsive, regionalized manufacturing that can adapt to operational requirements faster than the
traditional defense acquisition cycle allows.

Additive manufacturing is central to this vision not only for its geometric freedom and tooling
independence, but for its relative accessibility. Standing up an additive capability can be accomplished in
months at capital costs the commercial market can sustain, and the resulting capacity can be positioned
closer to points of need — whether that means Australia, the Gulf Coast, or a European theater. In the
context of turbine engines specifically, where smaller units power many of the unmanned and expendable
systems the Air Force is increasingly relying upon, the ability to produce and repair components at or near
the operational edge is not a theoretical advantage but an emerging operational requirement. The
qualification framework Benedict described is, in this light, not merely a compliance tool — it is the
foundational infrastructure for a fundamentally different manufacturing posture.

5.4 IMPLICATIONS FOR THE GAS TURBINE SUPPLY CHAIN

Benedict's talk surfaced several themes of direct relevance to the broader workshop conversation. The
acute shortage of flight-qualified AM process-material combinations is not a distant research problem; it
is a present-day supply chain constraint that limits the options available to OEMs and program offices
trying to manage long lead times and component shortages. Closing that gap requires structured
investment — in material characterization, in process control, in vendor knowledge development — and a
qualification framework that makes the return on that investment visible and predictable.

The bulletin's emphasis on vendor knowledge over data volume also carries an important message for
university and national laboratory partners: the research community's most leverageable contribution may
not be generating more material data in isolation, but rather developing the tools, models, and inspection
methodologies that help vendors understand and control what their processes are producing. Physics-
based models of microstructure evolution, validated non-destructive evaluation (NDE) techniques for
complex AM geometries, and data-driven methods for interpreting inspection results are all areas where
research institutions can directly accelerate the qualification pipeline. The Air Force has created the

 21

policy framework; filling it with technically mature, economically accessible qualification pathways is a
collaborative challenge that no single actor in the supply chain can meet alone.

 PANEL DISCUSSIONS AND BREAKOUT SESSIONS
 6. DISCUSSION PANEL 1: ADVANCED MATERIALS AND COATINGS

Figure 6. Moderator: Jonathan Li (Siemens Energy). Panelists: Chantal Sudbrack (NETL), Sanjay Sampath
 (Stony Brook University), Brett Tossey (Haynes International), Thomas Williams (Carpenter Technology).

The opening panel of the 2026 Innovating the Gas Turbine Supply Chain Workshop brought together a
cross-sector group of experts to examine one of the most technically demanding dimensions of supply
chain resilience: the materials and coatings that enable gas turbines to operate at extreme temperatures
and for extended service lives. Moderated by Jonathan Li of Siemens Energy, the discussion featured
perspectives from a national laboratory scientist, an academic researcher, and two specialty alloy
manufacturers. Together, they painted a picture of a sector under simultaneous pressure from surging
demand, constrained raw material supplies, an aging expert workforce, and technology development
timelines that rarely align with the urgency of near-term production needs.

The panel was framed around a central tension: the gas turbine industry needs to scale production rapidly
over the next three to five years, yet the materials science and process knowledge required to develop,
qualify, and manufacture new alloys and coatings operates on timescales measured in decades.
Navigating this tension—without sacrificing reliability or safety—is among the defining challenges
facing OEMs, suppliers, national laboratories, and universities alike.

 23

6.1 CONVERGING DEMAND PRESSURES AND A STRAINED SUPPLY CHAIN

Thomas Williams of Carpenter Technology set an important context at the outset: the current surge in
demand for industrial gas turbines is not occurring in isolation. It coincides with a major aerospace
supercycle, with over 30,000 firm orders for jet engines outstanding as the commercial aviation industry
recovers from COVID-era disruptions. Both sectors draw from the same pool of premium vacuum-melted
specialty alloys, the same qualified forge shops, the same machining capacity, and the same skilled
workforce. The result is a supply chain under compression from multiple directions simultaneously.

Brett Tossey of Haynes International reinforced this point from the perspective of a specialty nickel and
cobalt alloy producer. Lead times that stood at 24 weeks recently have stretched past 40 weeks, and the
challenge is not simply one of production rate. Raw material inputs—including cobalt, tungsten, yttrium,
and various rare earth elements essential to both alloys and coatings—face their own sourcing constraints,
many of which reflect decades of offshoring. Professor Sanjay Sampath of Stony Brook University noted
that much of the carbide powder production that once occurred domestically has migrated to China, and
recent geopolitical disruptions have caused prices for some materials to quadruple in a matter of months.
The panel broadly agreed that for the near-term horizon of one to five years, the primary lever available to
manufacturers is straightforward but difficult to execute: make more, and get in line now.

Carpenter Technology has committed approximately $450 million in new investment to expand premium
melting capacity, and Haynes is doubling its vacuum induction melting capability and adding large-scale
rotary forging equipment. These are significant commitments, but both panelists emphasized that capital
investment alone cannot solve the problem. Workforce capacity—the specialized metallurgical and
process knowledge held by experienced engineers—represents an equally critical bottleneck, one that has
been eroded by retirements and the COVID-era disruption to the technical labor pipeline.

6.2 MATERIALS AND COATINGS BEYOND THE HOT SECTION: THE COLD SIDE AND
 STRUCTURAL COMPONENTS

Moderator Jonathan Li deliberately opened the technical discussion by asking panelists to look beyond
the hot section—the combustor and turbine blades that typically dominate conversations about advanced
materials—and to consider compressor components, casings, bearings, and other structural elements. The
responses revealed that supply chain vulnerabilities extend well beyond the high-temperature alloys most
commonly associated with gas turbine technology.

Chantal Sudbrack of NETL highlighted compressor coatings as an area deserving greater attention.
Erosion and foreign object damage in the compressor require low-cost, durable surface treatments, yet the
number of qualified coating suppliers for these applications is limited, and requalification processes are
time-consuming. The bottleneck is not always the coating material itself, but the ecosystem of qualified
suppliers and the institutional knowledge required to validate alternatives.

Professor Sampath drew attention to the thermal spray coatings used extensively on rubbing and sliding
surfaces throughout the machine, including abradable seals and wear-resistant surfaces in the compressor.
These coatings are applied not primarily by OEMs but by a large and geographically dispersed network of
small and medium-sized maintenance, repair, and operations (MRO) shops. The cobalt-based carbide
powders they rely upon—particularly tungsten carbide-cobalt—have become a critical supply chain
vulnerability, illustrating how disruptions in a seemingly peripheral material category can cascade into
production and maintenance delays across the fleet.

Thomas Williams identified bearing steels as another underappreciated constraint, particularly for
aeroderivative turbines that use roller and ball bearings rather than the pad bearings common in large

 24

frame machines. These ultra-high-cleanliness, double-vacuum-melted steels are in direct competition with
the aerospace supply chain, and machining capacity for bearing components is severely limited. This is an
example of where the convergence of two industries—power generation and commercial aviation—
creates compounding stress on a shared, narrow supply base.

6.3 THE HOT SECTION: WHERE COATINGS AND ALLOYS MEET THE LIMITS OF
 PHYSICS

When the discussion moved to the combustor and turbine hot section, the panel reflected on one of the
most remarkable achievements in engineering history: that gas turbines now operate at flame
temperatures well above the melting point of the metallic alloys from which their components are made.
This has been accomplished through the layered application of thermal barrier coatings (TBCs),
sophisticated internal cooling architectures, and bond coat systems that together protect base alloys from
thermal, oxidative, and corrosive attack.

 “Gas turbines now operate at flame temperatures well above the melting point of
 the metallic alloys from which their components are made”

Professor Sampath provided a detailed account of the TBC supply chain, noting that approximately three
million pounds of yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) are plasma-sprayed globally each year. A current
shortage of yttrium—a rare earth element used to stabilize the zirconia phase—has created significant
concern across the industry. His point was not merely that a single element is difficult to source, but that
the entire TBC system has been optimized around this specific material over fifty years of development.
Replacing yttria with an alternative stabilizer is not a matter of finding a chemical substitute; it requires
re-establishing the fundamental understanding of how cracks form, how thermal cycling stresses are
accommodated, how bond coats interact with new ceramic chemistries, and how hundreds of thermal
spray booths worldwide can reproducibly deposit a new material system. That process, he noted, can take
decades.

Professor Sampath also highlighted an important asymmetry in how coatings and structural alloys are
qualified. Because many coatings are considered “life extension” technologies—meaning the component
does not immediately fail if the coating degrades—there may be more latitude to experiment with new
coating compositions and architectures than with primary structural materials. This opens a potential
pathway for faster innovation: strategically engineered multi-layer coating architectures that use more
readily available materials in carefully selected locations, reducing reliance on constrained inputs without
compromising performance. Advances in robotics and digital process control could make such
architectures manufacturable at scale. However, he was clear that even this faster pathway requires a solid
scientific foundation; trial and error in the coating shop will not be sufficient.

Sudbrack brought the fuel flexibility dimension into the hot section discussion, noting that the push
toward hydrogen blending and eventually higher hydrogen concentrations places new demands on
injector materials and combustor coatings. Additive manufacturing was identified as particularly well-
suited to the complex internal geometries required for hydrogen-capable injectors, a theme that recurred
throughout the workshop. She also raised the issue of rare earth supply in alloy design, noting that
elements like yttrium and rhenium—critical to oxidation resistance and creep strength respectively—are
embedded in superalloy compositions refined over generations, and that substitution is not
straightforward.

 25

Williams and Tossey both offered a candid assessment of the alloy development horizon. New alloy
concepts, including refractory high-entropy alloys and oxide-dispersion-strengthened materials, represent
genuine long-term opportunities, but their development cycles are measured in eight to ten years and tens
of millions of dollars of investment. For the data center power demand surge expected over the next three
to five years, these technologies are not on the table. Ceramic matrix composites (CMCs), already
deployed in some aero engine hot sections, offer a longer-range opportunity for land-based turbines where
environmental conditions can be more tightly controlled, but they too require their own supply chain
development and rare earth inputs.

 “New alloy concepts, including refractory high-entropy alloys and oxide-
 dispersion-strengthened materials, represent genuine long-term opportunities,
 but their development cycles are measured in eight to ten years and tens of
 millions of dollars of investment.”

6.4 KEY GAPS IDENTIFIED BY THE PANEL

The panel coalesced around several interconnected gaps that cut across organizational and sectoral
boundaries:

• Domestic raw material sourcing: The US has allowed critical raw material supply chains—carbide
 powders, rare earth processing, cobalt refining—to migrate offshore, leaving the gas turbine industry
 exposed to geopolitical and market disruptions with no rapid domestic alternative.

• Coating process science and metrology: Thermal spray is widely used but poorly understood at a
 fundamental level in industrial practice. Standardized measurement tools, process controls, and
 scientific frameworks are lacking, making it difficult to qualify new materials or suppliers
 consistently across the distributed MRO network.

• Accelerated testing infrastructure: The gap between laboratory coupon testing and engine-level
 validation is wide and expensive. Intermediate-scale test rigs and field trial pathways are needed to
 compress the qualification timeline for new materials and coatings without requiring full engine
 campaigns.

• Workforce and knowledge continuity: A wave of retirements among senior materials scientists and
 process engineers has created knowledge gaps that are not easily addressed through hiring alone. The
 tacit knowledge embedded in experienced practitioners—how to run a mill, how to set up a forge,
 how to troubleshoot a thermal spray process—takes years to develop and is not captured in
 publications.

• Qualification bottlenecks for new suppliers: The qualification process for new alloy suppliers and
 coating applicators is rigorous and time-consuming by design, but it also creates rigidity that can
 inhibit supply chain diversification. Novel approaches to qualification—potentially involving digital
 process records and data-driven equivalency assessments—could help.

• Integration of computational tools with experimental validation: AI and machine learning tools offer
 promise for accelerating alloy and coating design, but they cannot substitute for physical validation.
 The challenge is building the institutional infrastructure—and the OEM trust—needed to move from a
 computational prediction to a prototype material in a relevant environment.

 26

6.5 OPPORTUNITIES FOR COLLABORATION ACROSS SECTORS

Despite the sobering assessment of near-term constraints, the panel identified several areas where
coordinated action between OEMs, material suppliers, national laboratories, and universities could yield
meaningful results within the workshop’s strategic horizon.

6.6 STRATEGIC MATERIAL ARCHITECTURE IN COATINGS

Professor Sampath’s concept of “strategic layering”—engineering coating architectures that use more
available materials in non-critical locations while preserving performance where it matters most—
represents a near-to-medium term opportunity that does not require new alloy development. Realizing this
opportunity requires closer collaboration between university researchers who understand the physics,
national laboratories with characterization and prototype capability, and OEMs who can provide the
performance specifications and field validation pathways. The Consortium for Thermal Spray Technology
at Stony Brook, which has operated this kind of knowledge-transfer model for nearly 25 years, offers a
working template.

6.7 PROTOTYPE ALLOY DEVELOPMENT AT NATIONAL LABORATORIES

NETL’s Advanced Alloy Signature Center provides capability to prototype alloys at up to 500-pound
heats, bridging the gap between laboratory synthesis and commercial melting. This facility could play an
important role in de-risking new alloy compositions before suppliers are asked to commit commercial
capacity. Closer engagement between NETL, material suppliers like Carpenter and Haynes, and OEMs
around specific alloy development priorities could accelerate the transition of promising concepts toward
commercial readiness.

6.8 ADDITIVE MANUFACTURING FOR COMPLEX COMPONENTS

Additive manufacturing emerged as a cross-cutting opportunity, particularly for fuel injectors and other
combustor components with complex internal geometries that are difficult or impossible to produce
through conventional casting and machining. The Manufacturing Demonstration Facility at Oak Ridge
National Laboratory and similar national laboratory facilities have demonstrated significant capability in
this area. The challenge now is moving from demonstration to qualified production, which requires
engagement with OEM qualification teams and investment in process control and non-destructive
evaluation methods.

6.9 DEMAND SIGNALING AND LONG-RANGE SUPPLY PLANNING

It was stressed that material suppliers need earlier and more reliable demand signals from OEMs to make
investment decisions. Long-term supply agreements, rather than spot purchases, allow specialty alloy
producers to plan capital investment, workforce development, and raw material procurement. The panel
suggested that OEMs and the broader industry could benefit from a more structured demand forecasting
mechanism that accounts for both the aerospace and power generation build cycles simultaneously.

6.10 DOMESTIC RAW MATERIAL INVESTMENT AND POLICY ALIGNMENT

The panel agreed that rebuilding domestic supply chains for critical raw materials—yttrium, cobalt,
tungsten, rhenium, and rare earth elements used in both alloys and coatings—is a national-level challenge
that extends beyond what any single company or institution can address. This is an area where federal
policy, DOE programmatic investment, and industry coordination must work together. National

 27

laboratories can contribute by characterizing substitute materials, developing new processing routes, and
providing the foundational data that industry needs to justify qualification investments.

6.11 CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

The materials and coatings panel made clear that the gas turbine supply chain faces layered challenges
that cannot be resolved through any single intervention. In the near term—the next one to three years—
the priority must be maximizing output from existing qualified supply chains: expanding melting and
forging capacity, securing long-term material agreements, and building production workforce capability.
Looking out to a five-to-ten-year horizon, the focus should shift to building the scientific and industrial
infrastructure needed to diversify material sources, accelerate coating qualification, and introduce the next
generation of alloys and CMC components in a disciplined, data-driven way.

The panel consistently returned to one theme: the value of sustained, long-term relationships between
OEMs, material producers, national laboratories, and universities. The knowledge required to make these
systems work does not live in any single organization. It is distributed across this ecosystem, and
unlocking it requires trust, open communication about performance requirements and supply constraints,
and a shared commitment to the underlying science. In the words of Professor Sampath, reflecting on his
own experience: “you cannot simply develop a material and expect it to work. You have to concurrently
think about design, materials, and process—and you need much better collaboration with the OEM,
because without that engagement, even the best science will not penetrate at market scale.”

Specific recommendations emerging from the panel include:

• Establish a cross-sector working group on critical material inputs (yttrium, cobalt, tungsten, rhenium)
 to map domestic sourcing gaps and develop mitigation strategies in coordination with federal
 agencies.

• Invest in intermediate-scale testing infrastructure—burner rigs, oxidation furnaces, and field trial
 protocols—that can compress the timeline from laboratory coating or alloy concept to engine-relevant
 validation.

• Develop standardized process monitoring and metrology tools for thermal spray operations, enabling
 consistent quality control across the distributed MRO supplier network and facilitating the
 qualification of alternative coating materials.

• Expand the use of NETL’s Advanced Alloy Signature Center and similar national laboratory
 prototyping facilities to de-risk new alloy compositions prior to commercial scale-up, with OEM
 participation in defining target properties and validation criteria.

• Support workforce development programs that bridge university materials science education and the
 practical process knowledge of specialty alloy and coating production, including partnerships
 between universities, national laboratories, and material suppliers.

• Encourage OEMs to provide longer-range demand forecasts and enter into multi-year supply
 agreements with specialty material producers, enabling capital investment decisions that are currently
 impeded by market uncertainty.

 8. DISCUSSION PANEL 2: DIGITAL TECHNOLOGIES & PREDICTIVE MAINTENANCE

This panel brought together perspectives spanning industrial and aeroderivative gas turbines, aerospace
and defense, advanced manufacturing research, and OEM supply chain strategy to explore how digital
technologies and predictive maintenance tools can address the gas turbine supply chain's most pressing
bottlenecks. The conversation was energetic and candid, surfacing not only what is technically possible
today but — perhaps more importantly — the structural gaps that still stand between current capability
and the transformation the industry urgently needs.

8.1 THE DIGITAL TWIN: A VALUE CHAIN, NOT A SINGLE TOOL

Ramesh Subramanian of Siemens Energy set the conceptual tone early by reframing the digital twin not
as a single technology but as a connected chain of digital representations spanning the entire value chain
— from customer power plants to component condition monitoring, to supply chain risk mapping, to
supplier process analytics, to design and materials informatics. Siemens Energy's Omnivise platform, he
noted, currently ingests data from close to five million sensors daily across the global installed fleet, and
that ocean of operational data has become the foundation for machine learning tools that predict
remaining component life, guide repair-versus-replace decisions, and increasingly inform supply chain
activation. The insight here is significant: life extension of in-service components, enabled by high-

 35

fidelity condition monitoring, is not merely a customer service offering — it is a direct lever for relieving
pressure on a parts supply chain that is already strained to its limits by new build demand.

 “life extension of in-service components, enabled by high-fidelity condition
  monitoring, is not merely a customer service offering — it is a direct lever for
 relieving pressure on a parts supply chain that is already strained to its limits by
 new build demand.”

Christopher Meyer of Solar Turbines reinforced this framing from a lifecycle perspective. Solar operates
as both an OEM and a major overhaul service provider, which gives the company a rare closed-loop view:
digital twin predictions made years in advance can be validated when the physical parts come back for
inspection. Meyer described sectioning returned components to confirm predicted crack locations and
material degradation — and finding that the models are holding up. This kind of empirical validation, he
argued, is what builds the customer trust necessary for operators to act on digital advisements rather than
defaulting to conservative fixed-interval maintenance schedules. He also raised a point with direct supply
chain implications: as parts become serialized and their full processing and operational histories travel
with them through their entire lifecycle, engineers will gain the metallurgical context needed to make far
more precise fitness-for-service assessments — potentially recovering significant remaining life from
components that would otherwise be scrapped on schedule.

Leo Christodoulou of MMI Consultants brought the aerospace and defense lens to bear, drawing on his
experience leading DARPA's landmark Prognosis program, which demonstrated condition-based life
management of rotary-wing gearboxes and fixed-wing airframes. His central point was one of both
technical optimism and institutional candor: the tools for reliable prognosis — Bayesian updating,
surrogate sensor monitoring, edge computing — are no longer experimental. What continues to impede
their adoption is not the technology but the regulatory and standards environment, which in many cases
still mandates fixed-interval maintenance protocols regardless of actual component condition.
Christodoulou urged the community to invest energy in engaging standards bodies and regulators to
accept condition-based evidence, arguing that this organizational and policy work may ultimately be
harder than the engineering. He also made a compelling case for information architecture: different
stakeholders need different outputs from the same underlying data. A plant operator needs to know
whether the machine is safe to run today; an OEM engineer needs the signal that might inform a next-
generation component redesign. Conflating these information streams — or failing to parse them
intelligently — degrades the value of even the best predictive models.

Vladimir Orlyanchik of ORNL grounded the discussion in the specific digital tools being developed and
deployed at ORNL’s Manufacturing Demonstration Facility. He described three complementary
platforms: Peregrine, which provides in-situ detection of process and build defects in powder bed fusion
additive manufacturing and incorporates microstructure and property prediction; Pelican, its directed
energy deposition counterpart; and Myna, a digital thread data management framework that aggregates
data from disparate sources — characterization instruments, process sensors, inspection results — into a
unified, queryable record. With approximately 100 active Peregrine licenses already in use across
government, industry, and laboratory partners, Orlyanchik's observation was direct: the industry is
"thirsty" for these tools, and adoption is accelerating. The challenge ahead is not developing the capability
— it is ensuring that the data flows into these platforms consistently, and that the sensor infrastructure
exists in supplier facilities to feed them in the first place.

8.2 THE SUPPLY CHAIN'S DIGITAL READINESS GAP

If there was a single theme that united the panel's most candid moments, it was the wide gap between the
digital sophistication of the OEM and national laboratory community and the practical reality of the
supplier base that actually fabricates the components. Subramanian put it plainly: many smaller suppliers
do not yet understand what metadata models or digital thread frameworks are, let alone have the
infrastructure to implement them. He invoked the cartoon of a man pulling a cart on square wheels while
someone stands beside him offering a round one — unable to even explain what the round wheel does
while the cart still needs to move. The point was not dismissive but diagnostic: data literacy across the
supplier base must be treated as a community-wide infrastructure challenge, not a problem each OEM can
solve bilaterally with its own vendors.

Singledecker introduced the concept of “Smart Factories” as a forward-looking vision for the gas turbine
supply chain, framing digital transformation as a progression along a maturity path — from basic
connected and fixed-process states, through learning and intelligent capabilities enabled by data analytics,
machine learning, and AI/AR tools, toward the long-term aspiration of a fully autonomous, digitally
distributed factory. Key enablers highlighted include real-time closed-loop quality control, predictive
analytics, and integrated management systems. A critical gap identified was that many sub-tier suppliers
have yet to achieve even a basic "connected" state, despite the availability of technologies that could
accelerate their advancement to learning and intelligent stages today. The workshop emphasized that
closing this gap requires a collaborative, supply-chain-wide adoption of digital tools to unlock benefits
such as rapid cross-qualification, improved quality assurance, and reduced lead times.

Christodoulou echoed this urgency and proposed a structural remedy: manufacturing digital thread

pay per use rather than bearing the cost of licensing, maintaining, and updating models in-house. This
vision — of a manufacturing cloud that puts ORNL-class process modeling within reach of a precision
machining shop in the Midwest — represents one of the most actionable near-term opportunities
identified in the workshop. National laboratories, with their unique combination of production-scale
equipment and publicly accessible mandates, are natural anchors for such an infrastructure.

8.3 QUALIFICATION STANDARDS: THE HIDDEN BOTTLENECK

Subramanian raised a gap that received insufficient attention in the broader workshop but deserves to be
treated as a first-order priority: the absence of digital fingerprint qualification standards for manufactured
components. Today, qualification is built around physical inspection — cutting parts, examining
microstructures under a microscope, measuring dimensions. There is no accepted framework for saying
that a digital signature — the combination of in-process sensor streams, laser scan data, thermal imagery,
and process parameters — constitutes a sufficient quality record to qualify a part or a new supplier. Until
that framework exists, the data being generated by advanced manufacturing processes cannot be fully
leveraged for qualification, and the speed advantage of digital manufacturing cannot be realized in a
regulatory context. Developing such standards is a natural area for collaboration between OEMs, national

 37

laboratories, standards bodies, and academia — and it is work that, unlike new capital investment, can
begin immediately with existing expertise and data.

8.4 THE NEW OPERATIONAL FRONTIER: DATA GAPS IN NOVEL DUTY CYCLES

Both Subramanian and Meyer raised a challenge that cuts across the entire digital toolkit: the machine
learning models and digital twin frameworks that have been trained on years of baseload and traditional
cycling data are now being asked to make predictions for operating regimes they have never seen.
Behind-the-meter data center applications, rapid-cycling grid support, and extended standby followed by
fast dispatch are all creating conditions that fall outside the training distributions of even the most
sophisticated existing models. This is not a failure of the tools; it is a fundamental data gap. Closing it
will require coordinated efforts to instrument turbines operating in these new modes, share operational
data on a pre-competitive basis, and develop purpose-built model architectures suited to the lower-
volume, higher-variability nature of industrial operational data — which, as Subramanian noted, behaves
very differently from the large, relatively clean datasets on which most commercial AI tools are built.

8.5 A CALL FOR PARTNERSHIP

The panel closed with a theme that was as much cultural as technical. Subramanian argued that the supply
chain crisis has, paradoxically, created the conditions for a healthier OEM-supplier relationship than the
industry has historically sustained. The traditional dynamic — in which OEMs leveraged their purchasing
power to squeeze suppliers on lead time and cost — is no longer viable when supplier capacity is the
binding constraint. The moment calls for treating capable suppliers as partners in the digital
transformation: co-investing in their data literacy, bringing them along on the AI journey, and sharing the
tools and frameworks that will allow them to survive and grow in an era of accelerating technological
change. Christodoulou framed the same aspiration in terms of democratizing knowledge: forty years of
hard-won understanding about how materials behave through processing should not remain locked inside
a handful of large institutions. Getting that knowledge into the hands of the people who actually make
parts — in accessible, serviceable, cloud-based form — is among the highest-leverage investments the
community can make.

The overarching message of the panel was one of tempered urgency: the tools exist, the data is
accumulating, the computing power is available, and the need has never been more acute. What remains
to be built is the connective tissue — the shared infrastructure, the updated standards, the supplier
capability, and the cross-institutional partnerships — that will allow these capabilities to transform the
supply chain at the speed and scale the energy transition demands.

9. BREAKOUT SESSION 2: DIGITAL TECHNOLOGIES & PREDICTIVE MAINTENANCE

 Figure 9. Moderator: John Shingledecker (EPRI).

The Digital Technologies and Predictive Maintenance breakout session brought together OEM engineers,
utility operators, suppliers, national laboratory researchers, and academics to identify critical gaps and
opportunities at the intersection of digitalization and gas turbine operations. The discussion was wide-
ranging, covering predictive maintenance, digital twins, smart manufacturing, additive manufacturing
quality and qualification, sensor economics, data sharing, and the workforce readiness needed to make
these technologies operational across a diverse supply chain.

Four organizing themes structured the conversation: predictive maintenance and sensor systems; digital
twin development and deployment; smart factory and advanced manufacturing quality; and the enabling
conditions—data standards, workforce literacy, and business case clarity—that determine whether any of
these technologies can scale beyond early adopters. A consistent tension ran through all four areas: the
technology is advancing faster than the frameworks needed to deploy, trust, and sustain it across a
heterogeneous industry.

9.1 THEME 1: PREDICTIVE MAINTENANCE — FROM SCHEDULED HOURS TO
 CONDITION-BASED OPERATIONS

The most urgent challenge in predictive maintenance, as framed by participants, is the industry's
continued reliance on fixed maintenance intervals defined by hours and starts rather than actual
component condition. The data and modeling tools needed to move toward true condition-based
maintenance exist in various forms—but they are not yet connected, trusted, or economically justified at
the scale required.

A central discussion focused on surrogate sensing: the use of externally accessible measurements—such
as blade tip timing, exhaust temperature profiles, or shaft dynamics—as proxies for internal quantities that
cannot be directly observed in an operating turbine. Participants noted that this approach requires robust
physics-based models to translate surrogate signals into reliable estimates of damage accumulation,
fatigue life, and remaining useful life. Pure data-driven or machine-learning approaches were viewed
skeptically: without an underlying physical model to constrain and interpret the data, AI-generated
predictions lack the confidence required for high-value asset decisions.

A recurring obstacle is the cost justification for sensor installation. Participants acknowledged that the
return on investment from additional sensing is real but difficult to quantify in advance, particularly when

 39

sensors must be proven over long service cycles before their predictive value is established. This creates a
chicken-and-egg dynamic: instrumented test beds generate the data needed to build models, but
operational units—where the data ultimately matters—are rarely instrumented to the same degree.
Bridging this gap will require both technical work and creative business model development, such as
sensor-as-insurance arrangements or shared industry test facilities.

Participants also raised the challenge of moving from static to dynamic maintenance scheduling in a
practical operational context. Users running large fleets want to push or adjust maintenance intervals
based on actual operating profiles, particularly when units are subjected to highly variable loads driven by
grid integration demands. Building the confidence for operators and insurers to act on model-driven
maintenance recommendations is itself a significant research and communication challenge.

9.1.1 Key Gaps and Opportunities

• Development and validation of surrogate sensing approaches that estimate internal component
 condition from externally accessible measurements, coupled with physics-based models for damage
 accumulation

• Hybrid modeling frameworks that combine physics-based foundations with data-driven
 augmentation, with explicit uncertainty quantification to give operators confidence in predictions

• Instrumented test bed programs co-funded by OEMs, national laboratories, and utilities to generate
 the field-correlated data needed to validate maintenance models

• New business models—such as sensor-as-service or consortium-owned fleet data aggregation—that
 make the ROI for condition monitoring more legible and actionable

• Industry consensus on what constitutes sufficient model confidence to act on a condition-based
 maintenance recommendation, reducing the regulatory and liability ambiguity that currently
 discourages adoption

9.2 THEME 2: DIGITAL TWINS — ENABLING TECHNOLOGY OR OVERUSED
 CONCEPT?

Digital twins emerged as the most discussed technology concept in the session, but participants were
careful to distinguish between the aspiration and the reality. A true digital twin—a dynamic, physics-
faithful representation of an individual asset updated continuously from field data—offers transformative
potential for maintenance prediction, operational optimization, and life extension. But the gap between
that vision and current capability is substantial.

Several specific applications were identified as near-term anchors for digital twin development. One
participant highlighted the value of gas turbine digital twins wrapped for grid model integration, enabling
simulation of how turbines will respond to the rapidly changing demands of modern power grids. Data
centers now represent gigawatt-scale loads capable of swinging hundreds of megawatts in milliseconds;
turbines operating in these environments face mechanical stresses that existing qualification frameworks
were not designed to anticipate. Digital twins that bridge electrical grid models and mechanical
component models could help operators and ISOs understand equipment limits before those limits are
discovered destructively in the field.

Another discussed application is using digital twins to close the validation loop for new materials and
manufacturing processes. Participants proposed that combining digital twin frameworks with field

 40

deployment of new technologies—under monitored, consented arrangements with operators—could
accelerate the learning cycle that currently makes qualification so slow and expensive. The ability to bring
real-world operational data back into the twin, compare it against predictions, and refine the model
represents a fundamentally faster path to confidence than laboratory testing alone.

A significant gap identified by the group is the absence of common, open digital twin platforms or
interfaces for OEM-DOE-university collaboration. Proprietary models are difficult to share or federate;
there is no standard API or data wrapper that would allow a physics-based model developed by a national
laboratory to interoperate with an OEM's performance model or an operator's supervisory control and data
acquisition (SCADA) system. The Model Context Protocol (MCP) was raised as a potential framework
for this kind of interface standardization, enabling proprietary models to participate as black boxes while
exposing standardized inputs and outputs. This could enable an agentic AI ecosystem in which digital
tools—maintenance planners, grid models, manufacturing process controllers—can call each other's
models without requiring full disclosure of proprietary content.

9.2.1 Key Gaps and Opportunities

• Open or federated digital twin platforms that allow OEM, national laboratory, and university models
 to interoperate through standardized interfaces, without requiring disclosure of proprietary content

• Digital twins of gas turbines wrapped for grid integration studies, enabling analysis of equipment
 behavior under the rapidly changing load profiles driven by data centers, variable renewables, and
 grid stability requirements

• Validation loop programs in which new materials or technologies are deployed in consented field
 units, with digital twin predictions compared against real-world performance to accelerate
 qualification

• Standardized model interfaces (e.g., MCP-like API wrappers) that enable physics-based models to
 participate in broader digital ecosystems without full model transparency

• Investment in methods for quantifying and communicating model uncertainty, so that operators and
 decision-makers can assess the confidence level of digital twin predictions

9.3 THEME 3: ADDITIVE MANUFACTURING — QUALITY, QUALIFICATION, AND THE
 POST-PROCESSING GAP

Additive manufacturing (AM) occupied a central place in the session's manufacturing discussion, with
participants offering an unusually candid assessment of where the technology delivers on its promise and
where it continues to fall short. The consensus was that AM has passed the tipping point for a specific
class of applications—legacy parts, near-net-shape components, and parts with complex internal
geometries—but that the industry has also oversold its capability for high-tolerance, high-performance
components, and has not invested enough in the post-processing steps that determine final part quality.

The most frequently cited technical gap was manufacturing variation: machine-to-machine and batch-to-
batch differences in AM output that make it difficult to guarantee dimensional tolerances and
microstructural properties at scale. Participants noted that while the functional performance of AM
parts—particularly cooling channel geometry—is often excellent, the dimensional consistency required to
fit into downstream machining and assembly steps designed for castings is frequently not achieved. The
root cause, as one participant articulated, is that post-processing toolpaths, fixturing, and stock allowances
were developed for cast parts and have never been redesigned for AM geometries and tolerances. The

 41

result is a compounding mismatch: AM parts that are functionally sound but fail to pass through supply
chain steps built for a different process.

The qualification framework for AM was also critiqued as poorly calibrated. Current standards enumerate
over fifty statistical process variables, an approach that may be appropriate for a fully understood process
but becomes prohibitive for the iterative development cycles AM requires. Participants drew an analogy
to casting: the industry does not characterize every possible casting variable; it identifies the three or four
parameters that most strongly control quality and sets acceptance bounds around those. A similar
philosophy applied to AM—using physics-informed models to identify the dominant process drivers
rather than attempting exhaustive characterization—could dramatically accelerate qualification timelines.

Closed-loop control for AM was identified as the most impactful long-term capability, but participants
were clear that closed-loop control is only as good as the in-situ defect detection that feeds it. Without
reliable real-time identification of defects as they form—porosity, cracking, delamination—closed-loop
control cannot guarantee a good part, only a part produced within set process bounds. Development of in-
situ non-destructive evaluation (NDE) techniques that can detect defects during the build, not just after, is
the foundational research need that enables the rest of the closed-loop vision.

Despite these challenges, participants were optimistic about AM's role in repair—using additive processes
to restore worn or damaged components rather than manufacture new ones from scratch. This application
leverages AM's geometric flexibility without requiring the same dimensional precision as original
equipment manufacturer, making it more immediately deployable and economically attractive.

9.4 Key Gaps and Opportunities

• Development of in-situ NDE and defect detection techniques capable of identifying flaws during the
 AM build process, as the foundational enabler of closed-loop control

• Redesign of post-processing steps—fixturing, machining, stock allowances, geometric dimensioning
 and tolerancing (GD&T) specifications—for AM-produced parts rather than forcing AM output
 through workflows designed for castings

• Qualification philosophy shift toward identifying the few dominant process control parameters
 (analogous to casting practice) rather than attempting full characterization of all process variables

• Physics-informed process models that can run backwards from desired part properties to identify the
 controlling process parameters and their acceptable variation bounds

• Expanded use of AM for repair and life extension of existing turbine hardware, where geometric
 flexibility outweighs dimensional precision requirements

• Industry-wide pilot programs demonstrating multi-site AM qualification to build confidence that
 process parameters established at one facility can be transferred to others

9.5 THEME 4: SMART FACTORY AND DATA INFRASTRUCTURE — THE SUPPLY
 CHAIN'S DIGITAL READINESS GAP

The smart factory discussion revealed a fundamental asymmetry in digital readiness across the gas turbine
supply chain. OEMs and large Tier 1 suppliers are actively investing in data collection, digital
frameworks, and manufacturing intelligence. But the broader supplier base—Tier 2 and Tier 3 shops,
specialty fabricators, job shops, and small-volume manufacturers—is operating with equipment and data

 42

infrastructure that in some cases dates to the 1950s, with no clear path to digitalization and no business
case strong enough to self-fund the transition.

Participants described this gap in concrete terms: suppliers do not have standardized data structures, do
not collect process data systematically, and are often unable to share what data they do have because of
security concerns, proprietary worries, or simply the absence of compatible interfaces. A programmable
logic controller (PLC) running a machining center may generate useful process data, but without data
standards and accessible collection interfaces—an industrial equivalent of on-board diagnostics (OBD)
diagnostics—that data is trapped in the machine. One participant described the current state as analogous
to the early automotive telematics era: the information is there, but the ecosystem to extract, interpret, and
act on it does not yet exist for the manufacturing floor.

Several participants argued that the solution is not to wait for suppliers to self-fund digitalization, but to
establish a minimum viable digital framework—a supply chain digital literacy standard—that OEMs
could collectively agree on and use as a qualification baseline. This would tell suppliers exactly what
sensors to deploy, what data to collect, how to format it, and what interfaces to use, removing ambiguity
and enabling OEM procurement teams to point to a single reference rather than imposing 500-page
supplier-specific requirements. A consortium approach, bringing together major OEMs to agree on this
minimum standard, was seen as both feasible and high-impact.

Cybersecurity was raised as an increasingly serious concern as factories become more connected.
Participants noted that small suppliers are particularly vulnerable: they lack the IT resources to manage
cyber risk, yet they are increasingly being asked to connect their equipment to broader digital networks.
The risk of an undetected intrusion corrupting a manufacturing process—producing parts that appear
compliant but are subtly defective—was identified as a gap that the industry has not yet systematically
addressed.

AI was discussed as a potential bridge technology: rather than waiting for full interoperability standards
to emerge, AI tools can already connect disparate data systems, reconcile incompatible formats, and
surface insights from heterogeneous data sources. This does not eliminate the need for standards, but it
may reduce the urgency of requiring full standardization before gaining value from existing data.

9.5.1 Key Gaps and Opportunities

• Development of a minimum viable digital manufacturing framework—a supply chain digital literacy
 standard—agreed upon by major OEMs and used as a supplier qualification baseline for data
 collection, sensor deployment, and interface compatibility

• Accessible, open-source predictive maintenance toolkits designed for small and mid-sized suppliers,
 enabling condition monitoring of manufacturing equipment without requiring in-house digital twin
 expertise

• Data standards for manufacturing process data, analogous to industrial OBD interfaces, that allow
 process data to be extracted from equipment and shared in standard formats without disclosing
 proprietary process details

• Tenant-based data collection and upload architectures that give suppliers confidence their proprietary
 process data is secure while enabling anonymized fleet-level learning

• Cross-OEM cybersecurity baseline requirements for Tier 2 and Tier 3 suppliers, with national
 laboratory support for implementation guidance and verification

 43

• AI-assisted data integration tools that allow heterogeneous existing systems to interoperate without
 requiring full upfront standardization

• Workforce development programs—including cyber and digital literacy training—integrated into
 supplier qualification and development pathways

9.6 THEME 5: OVERARCHING CHALLENGES — ROI, TRUST, AND THE PATH TO
 ADOPTION

Running beneath each of the specific technical themes was a more fundamental challenge: the difficulty
of building a business case for digital investment that is compelling enough to move organizations from
pilot to production deployment. Participants described a pattern they have seen repeatedly—technologies
that work in the laboratory or in a funded demonstration, but that fail to cross the threshold into
widespread adoption because the ROI is real but deferred, the uncertainty is front-loaded, and the
decision-makers who control capital are not the engineers who understand the technology's potential.

Several structural features of the gas turbine industry make this particularly acute. Assets are extremely
expensive, service life is measured in decades, and failures—or false positives that trigger unnecessary
maintenance—carry enormous financial and reputational consequences. This creates strong risk aversion
that manifests as demand for certainty before investment, even when the expected value of the investment
is clearly positive. Participants noted that the traditional ROI framing—three years of negative returns
before breakeven—does not work with supplier procurement departments or utility CFOs who have
quarterly reporting obligations.

A consortium model was repeatedly proposed as the most promising structural solution. If OEMs,
suppliers, national laboratories, and utilities can share the cost and risk of technology development,
demonstration, and validation, the burden on any individual actor drops to a manageable level. The DOE
and Gas Turbine Association were identified as natural conveners for this kind of collaboration, with
national laboratories serving as neutral ground for sharing data and models that individual companies
would not share bilaterally.

9.6.1 Key Gaps and Opportunities

• Consortium-based funding and risk-sharing models that allow OEMs, suppliers, and utilities to jointly
 develop, demonstrate, and validate digital technologies without requiring any single actor to bear full
 cost or competitive risk

• New ROI frameworks and business case templates that account for deferred value, fleet-level
 benefits, and avoided downtime in ways that are legible to non-technical decision-makers

• National laboratory roles as neutral data custodians and model validators, enabling anonymized fleet
 data to be pooled and used for model training without exposing competitive information

• Joint industry-government programs that demonstrate condition-based maintenance or smart
 manufacturing at scale in real operating environments, generating the track record that shifts
 organizational risk tolerance

9.8 CONCLUSION

The Digital Technologies and Predictive Maintenance breakout session surfaced a technology landscape
that is rich with potential but constrained by structural gaps that no individual actor can close alone. The
enabling technologies—physics-informed digital twins, surrogate sensing, in-situ NDE, closed-loop AM
process control, smart factory data infrastructure—are advancing rapidly. What is lagging is the
ecosystem of standards, business models, workforce capabilities, and collaborative institutions needed to
translate these technologies from demonstration into routine industrial practice.

The most consistent message from participants was the need for a consortium approach: a deliberate,
structured collaboration among OEMs, suppliers, national laboratories, and universities that shares cost,
risk, and data in ways that make the collective investment viable and the results trustworthy. National
laboratories are uniquely positioned to serve as neutral conveners and model validators in this ecosystem,
holding anonymized fleet data and hosting interoperability frameworks that no individual company could
justify building or maintaining alone.

The participants expressed strong interest in continued engagement and asked that the results of this
session inform the Gas Turbine Association's advocacy priorities and DOE's planning for future research
investments. The session's outputs—captured in the sticky-note clusters and in this summary—represent a
clear map of where collaborative action can have the greatest impact on the gas turbine supply chain's
digital future.

 10. DISCUSSION PANEL 3: MANUFACTURING TECHNOLOGIES

Figure 10. Moderator: Mike Kirka (ORNL). Panelists: Mark Douglass (Lincoln Electric Additive Solutions),
 Dan Johnson (Solar Turbines), David Meek (Siemens Energy), Carl Popelar (SwRI).

The panel on Manufacturing Technologies brought together a diverse group — an OEM with a decade of
operational AM experience, a large-scale wire-arc additive manufacturer serving heavy industry and
defense, a robotics and automation technology developer at one of the world's largest turbine OEMs, and
a materials characterization researcher managing a new additive-centric demonstration facility.
Moderated by Michael Kirka of ORNL's Manufacturing Demonstration Facility, the discussion ranged
from the practical realities facing small suppliers trying to adopt advanced manufacturing tools, to
unresolved questions about IP governance, data standards, and the path from laboratory qualification to
field-proven hardware. What emerged was a candid, street-level view of where the manufacturing
ecosystem stands today — and a clear articulation of the collaborative infrastructure needed to move it
forward.

10.1 ADDITIVE MANUFACTURING AS A SUPPLY CHAIN BYPASS — WITH CAVEATS

The panel opened with a direct question: can advanced manufacturing — and additive manufacturing in
particular — help bypass the bottlenecks in global supply chains for castings and forgings, without
requiring the reconstruction of manufacturing infrastructure the U.S. no longer possesses? The consensus
answer was yes, conditionally — and the most important condition is time.

Dan Johnson of Solar Turbines framed AM's supply chain value through the lens of a decade of
operational experience. The clearest benefit is collapsing the supply chain back in-house: fewer casting
vendors, less machining, reduced tooling, and the ability to iterate on designs at a pace that simply cannot
be matched by traditional casting queues. The key advantage is not necessarily cost — AM feedstock and

 47

process costs often run higher than equivalent castings — but the ability to move quickly, catch design
issues early in the development cycle, and reduce the overall risk of committing to expensive tooling. The
"train leaving the station" problem is real: once a part has been qualified through a traditional supply
chain, the switching costs to AM are prohibitive unless forced by a field failure. This means the window
of opportunity to introduce AM is at the beginning of a part's lifecycle, requiring AM-capable suppliers
and OEM manufacturing engineers to be at the design table from day one.

 “the window of opportunity to introduce AM is at the beginning of a part's
 lifecycle, requiring AM-capable suppliers and OEM manufacturing engineers to
 be at the design table from day one.”

Mark Douglass of Lincoln Electric reinforced the time argument from the perspective of large, structural
components — what he called "big, chunky metal parts" produced via wire arc deposition. For pressure
vessels, pump casings, hydro components, and naval hardware, the same global supply chain chokepoints
apply: a small number of foundries and forge shops, long lead times, and no appetite for the capital
investment required to build new domestic capacity. In this environment, additive manufacturing —
particularly wire-based directed energy deposition (DED) processes that can produce large structural
components rapidly from a "box of wire" — offers a compelling alternative path for components that
don't require the dimensional precision or thermal performance of first-stage turbine hardware. The cost
comparison must be made on a lifecycle basis, not unit price: late castings with weld repairs and
reinspection cycles carry hidden costs that sticker-price comparisons consistently ignore.

10.2 BARRIERS FOR LOWER-TIER SUPPLIERS: CAPITAL, KNOWLEDGE, AND
 INCENTIVES

Perhaps the most practically important part of the discussion centered on what prevents lower-tier
suppliers from adopting these technologies — and what the broader ecosystem can do about it. The panel
identified three distinct but interrelated barriers.

The first is simply awareness. Douglass noted bluntly that a large fraction of smaller manufacturers —
and even some larger ones — remain unaware of what additive manufacturing can actually produce today
in terms of part quality, material properties, and production reliability. The gap between public perception
of AM as a prototyping technology and the reality of production-qualified components running in
operational environments is wide, and closing it requires deliberate outreach and education.

The second barrier is total cost misunderstanding. Buyers who see the unit price of an AM part and
compare it to the catalog price of a casting are making a fundamentally flawed comparison. They are not
accounting for the casting's real delivery timeline, its weld repair history, reinspection costs, and schedule
impact on downstream assembly. The problem is structural: procurement staff are often incentivized on
unit buy rates, not total program cost. Until financial incentives are realigned — particularly in
government contracting contexts like Navy submarine supply chains, where schedule pressure is acute,
but cost structures haven't caught up — AM will continue to struggle for adoption even when it offers the
superior solution on a systems basis.

The third barrier, articulated by David Meek of Siemens Energy from the robotics and automation
perspective, is capital cost combined with integration complexity. A single industrial robot costs between
$30,000 and $250,000 depending on configuration — but the robot is frequently the least expensive major
capital item in a functional automation cell. The sensor systems, integration software, programming, and

 48

ongoing maintenance represent the true cost of ownership, and many smaller shops lack the software
engineers and robotics expertise needed to sustain these systems after purchase. Worse, Meek described
walking into supplier facilities to find expensive robots sitting idle in corners — purchased in good faith,
but inadequately supported by the integrators who sold them. The ethics of the robot integration industry,
he noted, are uneven, and smaller shops without technical sophistication are vulnerable to being oversold
capabilities that don't exist in commercially deployable form. The implication is that OEMs and national
laboratories have a role to play not just in technology development but in supplier support and vetting —
helping the supply base navigate a complex and sometimes predatory vendor landscape.

Carl Popelar of Southwest Research Institute added a fourth barrier from the perspective of standing up a
new additive facility: qualification and certification burden. Because additive manufacturing
simultaneously creates the material and the component, every new application requires demonstrating
material properties, process repeatability, and regulatory compliance under quality systems that were
written for conventional manufacturing. For a small supplier without dedicated quality engineering staff,
this burden can be prohibitive. Popelar observed that OEM commitment and customer support during the
qualification process is not just helpful — it is often the difference between a supplier succeeding or
failing to enter the AM supply base.

Johnson summarized the imperative simply: "We live by our suppliers. Helping them with the business
case — that's going to work better for everyone. There's too easy a tendency to toss a spec over the wall
and say, do it. If we do that, we're never going to get through this together."

 “We live by our suppliers. Helping them with the business case — that's going to
 work better for everyone”

10.3 FROM LAB TO PRODUCTION: THE DATA ADVANTAGE OF ADDITIVE

A recurring theme throughout the panel was the distinctive data richness of additive manufacturing
compared to castings and forgings — and the unrealized potential of that data to transform qualification,
inspection, and supply chain confidence. Where a foundry might deliver a part with sparse thermocouple
records from the mold, an AM build generates continuous in-situ monitoring data: layer-by-layer thermal
histories, powder lot traceability, build plate positioning, machine parameters, and statistical records of
process consistency. Johnson noted that Solar serializes every AM part and can link it back to its
complete build pedigree.

Popelar argued that realizing this potential requires a shift in mindset: from verifying quality after the part
is built to assessing quality continuously during manufacture. In-situ monitoring, coupled with the ability
to integrate downstream steps like depowdering and geometry scanning into a single workflow, creates
the possibility of automated quality disposition in near real time. The digital thread — a continuous,
machine-readable record of the entire manufacturing process from powder to finished part — is the
infrastructure that makes this possible, and it represents a significant research and engineering challenge
that spans OEMs, equipment manufacturers, software developers, and national laboratories.

Kirka raised the intellectual property (IP) dimension of this data richness as a live tension: when build
files and process parameters constitute competitive IP, how do OEMs share work with outside vendors
without surrendering their manufacturing secrets? Johnson described the "golden build file" problem — a
customer requesting the ability to manufacture a qualified Solar part in their own facility without Solar's
presence, ostensibly by following the digital recipe. Solar's current position is to decline: the quality of an

 49

AM part is inseparable from the process control environment in which it is produced, and standing behind
a part manufactured outside that environment is not possible. Meek described creative workarounds for
defense applications where final computer-aided design (CAD) geometry is classified — developing
surrogate models, segregated black-box cells with no network connectivity, and carefully scoped data
streams that provide quality assurance without exposing protected design information. The broader point
is that as customers become more sophisticated about AM, IP governance frameworks will need to
become correspondingly more sophisticated — a challenge that likely requires industry-wide solutions
rather than part-by-part negotiations.

10.4 SHARED MATERIAL DATA: ELIMINATING REDUNDANT QUALIFICATION WORK

One of the most actionable observations of the entire discussion came from Johnson near the close of the
panel. Many AM practitioners in the gas turbine industry, he observed, is independently characterizing
the same materials on the same machines to meet similar qualification requirements. Test bars for nickel
superalloys, stainless steels, and titanium alloys are running in laboratories and test houses across dozens
of organizations, generating substantially overlapping datasets that are then sequestered as proprietary.
This is an enormous and unnecessary waste of research resources, and it was described as a "resounding
theme" at recent AM industry conferences.

The solution — a shared, trusted material properties database populated through coordinated testing
programs — is conceptually straightforward but organizationally difficult. Douglass noted that Lincoln
Electric has invested significantly in generating material data for ASME code qualification, including
funding independent testing programs, because the return on investment in terms of market access was
clear. He pointed to the National Institute for Aviation Research at Wichita State (NIAR) and ORNL's
MDF as existing resources doing some of this work for DoD-relevant materials. But the gas turbine
community — particularly for the specific alloys, thermal environments, and cyclic loading conditions
relevant to land-based and aero-derivative turbines — needs a coordinated equivalent. National
laboratories, with their existing roles in materials characterization and their independence from
competitive dynamics, are natural candidates to host and steward such a database in partnership with
OEMs and equipment manufacturers.

 “The solution — a shared, trusted material properties database populated
 through coordinated testing programs — is conceptually straightforward but
 organizationally difficult.”

10.5 STANDARDS PROGRESS: MEANINGFUL BUT INCOMPLETE

Douglass provided an important update on the regulatory and standards landscape. Recent additions to
ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code — specifically new code cases in Section 8 (pressure vessels),
Section 3 Division 1 (nuclear), and progress in B31.1 (power piping) — now provide formal regulatory
pathways for wire DED components in power generation and nuclear applications. This is meaningful
progress that directly supports the energy sector's ability to use AM components in code-governed
applications. However, the standards landscape remains incomplete, particularly for powder bed fusion
processes and for higher-temperature, rotating turbine hardware. The concept of interchangeability —
formally certifying that an AM part produced by a qualified process is interchangeable with its cast or
forged equivalent, thus avoiding costly functional test programs for every new application — was
identified as a key near-term target for standards development.

 50

10.6 FIELD PERFORMANCE: CAUTIOUS OPTIMISM

The panel closed with a question from Professor Thole about field experience with AM components —
whether surprises, positive or negative, had emerged from parts in service. The honest answer across all
panelists was measured confidence tempered by incomplete data. Solar has hundreds of AM parts and
millions of operating hours in the field, primarily on static components, with no major failures reported.
Siemens' failure analysis group occasionally sees AM parts return for examination and finds
microstructural features — small pores, surface anomalies — that attract attention but have not been
found to drive failures at service-relevant sizes. Lincoln Electric showed a comparison between an AM
pump casing and the corroded, voided casting it replaced for a Navy nuclear application, noting that the
bar for demonstrating superiority in many real-world applications is not especially high.

The common caveat was that most deployed AM parts are not yet at the end of their design lives, and that
fatigue and creep behavior under multi-overhaul cyclic conditions — the true long-term test — remains to
be demonstrated. Johnson was direct: "Time will tell. We'll see." This is not a counsel of doubt, but an
honest acknowledgment that the evidence base for AM in high-cycle, thermally demanding applications is
still accumulating.

10.7 GAPS AND OPPORTUNITIES FOR THE COMMUNITY

The manufacturing technologies panel crystallized several specific gaps and collaborative opportunities
with direct relevance to the workshop's supply chain focus:

For OEMs, the imperative is to engage the supply base proactively — at the design stage, with technical
support, and with lifecycle cost frameworks that make the business case for AM adoption legible to
procurement organizations. The "spec over the wall" model will not build the AM supply chain the
industry needs.

For lower-tier suppliers, the barriers of capital, knowledge, and qualification burden are real but
surmountable with structured support. Targeted programs combining equipment access, workforce
training, and guided qualification support — potentially co-designed by OEMs and national laboratories

For national laboratories, the highest-leverage opportunities are in shared material characterization
infrastructure, standards development support, and the kind of pre-competitive AM process
characterization work that individual companies cannot justify independently. ORNL's MDF was cited
multiple times as a model for this role, and its expansion to encompass the full process continuum —
from powder characterization through in-situ monitoring, heat treatment, and post-process inspection —
would address gaps explicitly identified by multiple panelists.

 “For national laboratories, the highest-leverage opportunities are in shared
 material characterization infrastructure, standards development support, and the
 kind of pre-competitive AM process characterization work that individual
 companies cannot justify independently”

For the community as a whole, the development of a shared, curated material properties database for gas
turbine-relevant AM alloys — governed in a way that protects individual contributions while making
validated data broadly accessible — may be the single intervention most likely to reduce redundant

qualification costs and accelerate the industry's collective ability to deploy AM in production applications
with confidence.

 11. BREAKOUT SESSION 3: MANUFACTURING TECHNOLOGIES

 Figure 11. Moderator: Mike Kirka (ORNL).

The Manufacturing Technologies breakout session convened OEM engineers, national laboratory
researchers, university faculty, and supply chain participants to examine the most pressing bottlenecks in
gas turbine manufacturing and to identify opportunities where targeted research, investment, and
structural reform could most rapidly improve supply chain performance. Discussion spanned a wide arc—
from the fundamental constraints of single-crystal casting and large-component forging, to the evolving
role of additive manufacturing (AM) and hybrid processes, to the systemic challenges of supplier base
fragmentation and the cost and knowledge barriers that prevent smaller manufacturers from participating
in advanced manufacturing.

Three organizing themes emerged from the brainstorm and discussion: the structural and capacity
constraints that define today's manufacturing bottlenecks; the unresolved technology and qualification
questions surrounding additive and hybrid manufacturing; and the supply chain ecosystem issues—
including workforce, data standards, and business model innovation—that determine whether new
manufacturing capabilities can actually scale across the industry. Throughout the session, participants
returned to a recurring tension: the gas turbine industry needs to both solve immediate capacity problems
and build the long-term manufacturing infrastructure to support next-generation, higher-efficiency
machines, and the investments required for each are not always the same.

11.1 THEME 1: MANUFACTURING CAPACITY CONSTRAINTS AND LEAD TIME
 BOTTLENECKS

The session opened with a candid inventory of the manufacturing constraints participants considered most
acute. Long lead times for parts and tooling were the single most commonly cited challenge across the
sticky-note clusters, and participants connected this directly to the fundamental architecture of the turbine
manufacturing supply chain: a small number of highly specialized facilities producing complex, low-
volume components to extraordinarily tight tolerances, with limited ability to flex capacity in response to
demand surges.

 53

Single-crystal casting was highlighted as a particularly severe chokepoint. The number of facilities
worldwide capable of producing single-crystal nickel superalloy blades and vanes for high-performance
turbines is small, and the process—directional solidification in precisely controlled furnaces, followed by
complex inspection—cannot be meaningfully accelerated or distributed without major capital investment.
Similarly, large forging capacity for disks and structural components has contracted over decades as the
number of forge shops with the press tonnage and alloy expertise required for turbine-grade components
has declined. Participants noted that competition from other industries—aerospace, nuclear, defense—
further strains this shared capacity base.

Tooling was identified as a compounding constraint, with one participant observing that three of the most
important needs at their facility were tooling, tooling, and tooling. The lead time for complex dies,
fixtures, and specialized machining tooling can rival that of the parts themselves, and the limited number
of toolmakers with turbine-specific expertise makes this difficult to address through market mechanisms
alone. The complexity of cooling passage geometries—which can approach a hundred features per
blade—adds further difficulty, as even small geometric changes can require extensive tooling updates.

Supply chain constraints for large components were noted explicitly, with one participant highlighting the
difficulty of sourcing very large forgings and castings—including large turbine blades and casings—
where supply chain capacity is genuinely limited and cannot easily be expanded without sustained
investment over multi-year horizons. Participants also noted that competition from other industries for
castings and forgings using the same alloys and the same specialized facilities will only intensify as
electrification, aerospace, and nuclear energy demand grows.

The rapid turnaround of parts and tooling—reducing the cycle from design change to qualified
production—was flagged as a near-term opportunity. Digital tools, rapid casting approaches, and closer
integration between OEM design teams and supplier shops could compress some of these cycles, but this
requires both technical investment and relationship infrastructure that does not currently exist at scale
across the supply chain.

11.1.1 Key Gaps and Opportunities

• Mapping and transparent characterization of single-crystal casting and large forging capacity,
 including cross-industry competition for shared resources, to enable better long-range planning and
 targeted capacity investment

• Investment in rapid tooling technologies—including AM-enabled die and fixture production—to
 reduce the tooling lead time that compounds part lead time across the supply chain

• Exploration of cross-industry collaboration models to share high-capital manufacturing infrastructure
 (casting furnaces, large presses) across sectors with complementary demand cycles

• Government support for domestic capacity expansion in bottleneck processes—single-crystal casting,
 large forging, rare earth processing—where market incentives alone are insufficient to drive
 investment at the required scale and timeline

• Supply chain weak-point mapping as a near-term action, identifying the specific facilities, processes,
 and part families where a single disruption would have the largest systemic impact, and developing
 contingency strategies for each

11.2 THEME 2: ADDITIVE MANUFACTURING — PROMISE, LIMITATIONS, AND THE
 PATH TO BROADER DEPLOYMENT

Additive manufacturing occupied the largest share of the technical discussion, reflecting both its potential
and the unresolved questions that continue to limit its role in the gas turbine supply chain. Participants
brought a mix of enthusiasm and hard-won skepticism, and the discussion was notably candid about
where AM delivers and where it continues to fall short.

The most important point of consensus was that the gas turbine industry needs to accept the fundamental
difference between AM and conventional manufacturing rather than continuing to treat AM as a drop-in
replacement for castings and forgings. As one participant put it, the industry keeps trying to use AM as a
substitute for conventional approaches and then measuring it by conventional standards, when in fact AM
enables geometries, design freedoms, and near-net-shape production economics that are genuinely
different and should be assessed on their own terms. This philosophical shift—from AM as a substitute to
AM as a distinct manufacturing paradigm—was seen as a prerequisite for realizing its full value.

Specific technical gaps discussed included: dimensional consistency and process variation across
machines and build sites; the qualification burden for AM parts, which participants questioned whether it
needed to be as extensive as current standards require; the question of whether AM parts have lower
projected lifecycles, particularly for heavy repair applications; and the implications of AM part lifecycles
for fatigue-based application design in combined-cycle versus simple-cycle service. Laser beam shaping
was raised as a potential enabler that could open the AM process window—expanding the range of
materials and geometries that can be reliably produced—and participants asked whether this and similar
process innovations were being sufficiently pursued.

Powder availability was flagged as a growing constraint, with the observation that AM demand is driving
more printer installations, which in turn is creating supply pressure on specialty powder producers—a
dynamic that could become a bottleneck in its own right if not anticipated. The supply chain for AM
powder, including international dependencies and the limited number of producers capable of meeting
aerospace-grade specifications, was identified as a vulnerability requiring attention analogous to the raw
material supply issues affecting conventional manufacturing.

Hybrid manufacturing processes—combining AM with conventional subtractive, forming, or joining
steps—were identified as an underexplored opportunity with potentially strong business cases,
particularly for parts that are difficult to produce entirely by either approach. Participants asked which
hybrid manufacturing processes currently have acceptable business cases and which remain economically
marginal, and noted that this question is rarely asked systematically across the industry.

A recurring theme was the need for industry-level lifecycle knowledge, design guidance, and workforce
capability to accompany material testing results—not just data on AM material properties, but integrated
understanding of how those properties translate into part performance, how designs should be adapted to

The franchise model analogy captured one of the session's most practical proposals: national laboratories
and OEMs establish the hard technical work—process parameters, qualification frameworks, design
guidelines—and then structure this as a deployable package that smaller shops can adopt with manageable

it. This would dramatically lower the barrier to entry for Tier 2 and 3 suppliers interested in AM, without

11.3 THEME 3: SUPPLY CHAIN ECOSYSTEM — FRAGMENTATION, ACCESS, AND
 STRUCTURAL REFORM

The supply chain ecosystem discussion addressed the structural conditions that determine whether
individual manufacturing technology advances can actually diffuse across the broader supplier base—and
participants were sobering about the barriers involved. The core problem, as one OEM participant framed
it, is that the gas turbine supply chain is served substantially by smaller, specialized shops that lack the
capital, technical resources, and market pull to adopt advanced manufacturing technologies or to comply
with increasingly demanding quality and traceability requirements, even when they have the fundamental
manufacturing capability to do so.

Capital intensity is a central barrier. Advanced computer numerical control (CNC) machining centers
suitable for turbine components cost over a million dollars each, and a properly equipped machine shop
requires multiple such systems. AM systems for production-grade turbine work carry similar or higher
capital costs. For a small or mid-sized supplier serving a turbine OEM alongside other customers in
aerospace, defense, or energy, the business case for this level of capital investment depends critically on
utilization—and utilization depends on a steady flow of qualified work, which in turn depends on having
the equipment and certifications to attract that work. Breaking this cycle requires either direct capital
support, shared-equipment models, or demand aggregation mechanisms that create the utilization needed
to justify investment.

 56

International supply chain dependencies were raised as a structural vulnerability, with participants noting
that multiple change-of-hands across international boundaries for a single part creates both traceability
risk and geopolitical exposure. As one sticky note captured: international supply chain in manufacturing
with multiple change of hands for a single part is a compounding risk that is difficult to manage and even
harder to reverse once established.

Data format standardization emerged as a near-term, tractable intervention that could have outsized
supply chain impact. The absence of a common data format or portal standardized among OEMs means
that suppliers producing the same types of parts for multiple customers must comply with incompatible
data requirements, reporting formats, and quality management systems—multiplying their administrative
burden and reducing their effective capacity. A standardized data format agreed among OEMs was
proposed as a high-value, achievable action that could meaningfully improve supplier productivity and
reduce error rates across the supply chain.

Participants also discussed the challenge of expanding the supplier base to include newer and smaller
companies. How do we expand the supplier base across the U.S. to include these newer companies—can
we enable them to be reliable suppliers? was the explicit question on one sticky note. The answer requires
not only technical onboarding support but also the development of graduated qualification pathways that
allow promising suppliers to enter at an appropriate level and build toward full qualification over time,
rather than facing a binary all-or-nothing certification requirement that most small shops cannot meet
without OEM support.

The role of alternative feedstock and raw material production technologies—wire arc, submerged arc,
electroslag remelting, and related approaches—was raised in the context of small supplier raw material
access. Large suppliers and OEMs have sufficient market pull to source specialty plate, bar, and billet
stock from primary producers; small shops often do not, and are subject to long lead times and minimum
order quantities that make just-in-time production difficult. Alternative near-net-shape feedstock
production methods that these smaller shops could operate themselves—producing material that meets
specification without depending on primary mill supply chains—were proposed as a research and
demonstration priority.

11.3.1 Key Gaps and Opportunities

• Development of a standardized data format and portal for part production documentation, agreed
 upon among major OEMs, to reduce supplier administrative burden and improve traceability across a
 heterogeneous supply base

• Supply chain weak-point identification across the manufacturing ecosystem, analogous to what the
 Defense Logistics Agency has conducted for defense applications, to surface single points of failure

• Graduated supplier qualification pathways that allow smaller, emerging suppliers to enter the supply
 chain at an appropriate capability level and build toward full qualification with structured OEM or

• Shared equipment and co-investment models—potentially facilitated by national laboratories or
 regional manufacturing institutes—that enable smaller suppliers to access advanced manufacturing

11.4 THEME 4: WORKFORCE, KNOWLEDGE INFRASTRUCTURE, AND INSTITUTIONAL
 COLLABORATION

Workforce and knowledge infrastructure was a cross-cutting theme that surfaced across all three of the
primary discussion areas. Participants noted that the manufacturing challenges facing the gas turbine
supply chain are not only about equipment and process capability—they are equally about the human
expertise needed to operate, qualify, and continuously improve that capability across a distributed,
heterogeneous supply base.

The workforce discussion echoed concerns raised in other breakout sessions about the retirement of
experienced technicians and engineers in specialized disciplines—single-crystal casting, thermal spray,
precision forging—and the difficulty of transferring the tacit knowledge embedded in those careers
through conventional training programs. But participants in the manufacturing technologies breakout
session added a dimension specific to advanced manufacturing: the challenge of building a new
workforce competency in AM and hybrid processes that does not yet have established apprenticeship
traditions, standardized curricula, or industry-recognized certification pathways.

The comparison to the robotics education ecosystem was raised here as in other sessions: the success of
open-source, accessible learning platforms in expanding the pipeline of people capable of working with
industrial robotics suggests a similar model could work for AM and advanced machining. The challenge
is that gas turbine manufacturing involves a degree of materials science depth—understanding how
process parameters affect microstructure, how microstructure determines service life, how damage
mechanisms evolve under realistic operating conditions—that goes beyond what a general robotics kit can
impart. A more sector-specific curriculum, developed in collaboration between OEMs, national
laboratories, and universities, is needed.

The consortium model for workforce and knowledge infrastructure was the most consistently proposed
structural solution. Several participants referenced the Air Force Material Affordability Initiative as a
precedent: a concentrated, government-enabled effort that brought together OEMs, research institutions,
and educational programs around a shared technical agenda, producing both near-term capability
improvements and longer-term workforce pipeline development. A similar structure for gas turbine
manufacturing—focused on AM and hybrid process qualification, lifecycle knowledge, and supplier
onboarding—was proposed as an action item for the Gas Turbine Association and DOE.

• Development of a gas turbine manufacturing workforce consortium—modeled on the Air Force

11.6 CONCLUSION

The Manufacturing Technologies breakout session surfaced a picture of an industry whose manufacturing
infrastructure is under pressure from multiple directions simultaneously: surging demand for turbine
capacity, constraints on the specialized manufacturing base that produces critical components, an
incomplete transition to additive and hybrid manufacturing processes that have been in development for
decades, and a supplier ecosystem that is structurally ill-equipped to absorb the pace of change being
demanded of it.

The most consistent message from participants was that the industry cannot solve these challenges
through incremental, company-by-company investment decisions. The capital intensity, the knowledge
depth, and the qualification burden involved in advanced gas turbine manufacturing are collectively too
great for any single actor to address efficiently. What is needed is a coordinated, government-enabled
consortium effort—bringing OEMs, national laboratories, universities, and supply chain participants
together around shared technical agendas, common standards, and integrated workforce development—
that treats manufacturing capability as a shared industrial asset rather than a source of competitive
differentiation.

National laboratories are positioned to anchor this effort: as neutral conveners of multi-OEM
collaboration, as developers of the core technical frameworks that suppliers can adopt, and as the research
institutions best equipped to bridge the gap between materials science fundamentals and manufacturing
process engineering. Universities bring the long-term workforce pipeline and the fundamental research
that keeps the knowledge base current. OEMs bring the demand signal, the application knowledge, and
the procurement leverage to drive supplier adoption. The path forward requires all three, working together
with more deliberate coordination than the gas turbine manufacturing ecosystem has historically
achieved.

 12. DISCUSSION PANEL 4: REPAIR, REFURBISHMENT, AND LIFECYCLE
 MANAGEMENT

 Figure 12. Moderator: Jason Wilkes (SwRI). Panelists: Manish Kumar (Siemens Energy), Tom Kurfess
 (Georgia Tech), Jonathan Lomas (GE Vernova), Brandon Ruiz (Mitsubishi Power Systems).

The fourth and final panel of the workshop brought together OEM engineers, a leading academic, and a
representative of an independent, nonprofit R&D organization to examine one of the most immediately
consequential dimensions of the gas turbine supply chain challenge: keeping existing assets operational.
Moderated by Jason Wilkes of the Southwest Research Institute (SwRI), the discussion surfaced a rich set
of technical gaps, workforce realities, and collaborative opportunities that cut across industry, academia,
and the national laboratory ecosystem.

12.1 REPAIR IS NOT REPAIR

The panel opened with a thought-provoking insight from Manish Kumar of Siemens Energy that set the
intellectual tone for the entire discussion: the word "repair" is, in his framing, a profound understatement
of what actually happens to a turbine component when it returns from service. A blade that enters a repair
facility and exits it is not the same part — it is a more deliberately engineered piece of hardware,
potentially incorporating advanced cooling schemes, next-generation coatings, and functionally graded
materials that were unavailable when the original casting was made. Jonathan Lomas of GE Vernova
reinforced this point by describing the repair technology space as a fusion of old and new — a
'playground,' in his words, for the engineers and technicians doing development work. Practitioners must
be equally fluent in fleet hardware and technologies of all vintages, applying that knowledge with
discipline to select the right combination of processes and materials to restore each component to full
function. For customers, the result is not merely a repaired component — it is hardware that benefits from
generations of lessons learned.

 61

 “A blade that enters a repair facility and exits it is not the same part — it is a
 more deliberately engineered piece of hardware, potentially incorporating

This reframing carries important implications for how the industry, universities, and national laboratories
should think about the problem. Repair is not remediation; it is an opportunity for incremental
advancement of the fleet. Components can leave a repair cycle with superior tip materials, refined
microstructures, or improved cooling geometries relative to the original design. Tom Kurfess of Georgia
Tech noted that additive manufacturing is not simply a tool for new production — it is arguably better
suited to the repair context, where only small amounts of material need to be deposited and the geometric
starting point is a real, service-aged part rather than a nominal CAD model. The practical challenge is
getting there: manual repair inherently carries process variation, and the complexity of advanced turbine
hardware further compounds this. Machine-enabled processes such as directed energy deposition can
substantially reduce variation in the repetitive elements of repair tasks — freeing repair technicians to
focus their expertise on the work that remains difficult to automate — and that work is only growing more
complex and demanding. This underscores the enormous value of continued investment in automation
and process control in this space.

12.2 THE LIFECYCLE MANAGEMENT GAP

Perhaps the most urgent unmet need identified by the panel was in residual life assessment. Kumar
articulated the stakes with clarity: operators and OEMs are routinely making decisions — sometimes in
real time — about whether a component can safely run for one more cycle, one more day, or whether it
must be retired immediately. These decisions involve enormous financial and reliability consequences,
yet the tools available to support them remain imprecise. The grain structure evolves during service in
ways that are not fully captured by existing models; microcracking that appears alarming may, in some
cases, actually reflect a material that has stabilized and can be safely operated further. Kumar offered an
explicit call to action for the research community: develop validated, high-fidelity residual life assessment
tools that can integrate inspection data — CT scanning, X-ray, metallurgical analysis — and return a
defensible, quantitative answer about remaining useful life.

Brandon Ruiz of Mitsubishi Power Systems connected this to a broader supply chain imperative. The
materials available for repair and new manufacture are finite and constrained. Every component that can
be repaired rather than scrapped relieves pressure on an already stressed new-manufacture pipeline. The
economic calculus is straightforward — repair is faster and less costly than new production — but it
requires investment in the engineering and data infrastructure to support confident life extension
decisions. Lomas added that deeper collaboration with plant operators is essential to this effort:
understanding each customer's operating profile, mission requirements, and flexibility creates the
foundation for genuinely optimized lifecycle management. Investments in repair technology made with
that full lifecycle context in mind have the potential to deliver compounding benefits — not just to
individual components, but to fleet performance and grid reliability..

12.3 TECHNICAL DATA PACKAGES AND INSTITUTIONAL KNOWLEDGE

Kurfess raised a challenge that resonated broadly with the audience: the fragility of technical knowledge
over time. Turbine components embody decades of accumulated process know-how that is frequently
embedded in the expertise of individuals rather than formally codified. A technical data package that was

 62

adequate when a blade was first manufactured may be wholly insufficient to reproduce or repair that
blade years later, because the tacit knowledge of the craftspeople who originally built it has retired along
with them. This problem — sometimes called "Bubba's retirement" in the panel's memorable shorthand
— is accelerating. The name may sound casual, but the expertise it represents is anything but — these are
the skilled welders, machinists, and repair technicians whose hard-won abilities underpin the industry,
and the risk that their knowledge may not successfully make a generational transition deserves to be
treated as the critical challenge it is.

The panel identified two distinct but related responses to this challenge. The first is automation: robotics,
CNC systems, laser welding, and automated inspection can encapsulate process knowledge in repeatable
machine behavior, reducing dependence on individual skill while improving consistency and safety. The
second is data: the thousands of scanned blade profiles accumulating in OEM databases represent an
underutilized asset. Kurfess noted that this corpus of geometric and performance data, if properly mined,
could support predictive modeling of long-term component behavior, inform redesigns that minimize
warping and degradation, and eventually underpin the kind of real-time, physics-informed decision-
making that operators need for lifecycle management. National laboratories like Oak Ridge National
Laboratory— whose manufacturing demonstration capabilities were cited approvingly by multiple
panelists — are natural partners in developing the data pipelines, machine learning frameworks, and
validated process models that can make this vision operational.

12.4 WORKFORCE: A PARALLEL CHALLENGE

The panel's most animated exchange centered on workforce development, with a university researcher in
the audience pressing the panelists to think not about today's workforce needs but about what engineers
and technicians will look like in five to ten years. Ruiz stressed that the ramp rate of gas turbine
deployment is real and immediate, and that the pipeline of skilled labor — engineers capable of
programming automation systems, technicians capable of operating advanced inspection cells, operators
who understand both the physical process and the digital infrastructure around it — is already strained.
The problem is not hypothetical; it is manifesting now in every discipline from welding to quality control.

Kumar offered a counterintuitive perspective: it may be hands-on practical skills, not fundamental
science, that are more vulnerable to automation and AI displacement. The wisdom embedded in deep
physical and materials understanding is harder to replicate algorithmically than the execution of a well-
defined repair procedure. His implied curriculum recommendation: lean into fundamentals — materials
science, thermodynamics, failure mechanics — while ensuring students understand the broader systems
context, including resource constraints, recycling, and the full material lifecycle. Kurfess translated this
into a concrete pedagogical principle: students who have operated a lathe, run a hydrogen furnace, or
TIG-welded a superalloy have a qualitatively different understanding of manufacturing constraints than
those who have only worked in simulation. That tactile literacy shapes how they design, how they
communicate with technicians, and how they diagnose failure.

Lomas distilled the workforce challenge to a cultural one: the ability of PhD-level researchers and

12.5 OPPORTUNITIES FOR COLLABORATION

Across all of these themes, the panel converged on several areas where OEM-laboratory-university
collaboration could meaningfully accelerate progress. Residual life assessment tools that integrate multi-
modal inspection data with physics-based and data-driven models represent a high-value target where
national laboratory capabilities in materials characterization and computational modeling align directly
with industry need. Process development for automated repair — particularly for complex geometries,
variable defect morphologies, and dissimilar material interfaces — is another domain where university
research in additive manufacturing and machine learning, combined with laboratory-scale demonstrations,
could support industry adoption. The development and curation of technical data packages, including the
codification of process knowledge currently held by experienced practitioners, is a preservation challenge
with both near-term economic consequences and long-term national security implications. Finally,
advanced coating recycling and the characterization of coating condition on service-aged parts were
identified as emerging topics where the research community has an opportunity to get ahead of a growing
operational need.

Bob Slattery, Manager of Industrial Collaborations at ORNL’s Manufacturing Demonstration Facility
noted at the close of the session that DOE's Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies Office
has recently established a dedicated funding stream for repair and refurbishment collaboration at the MDF
— a timely signal that the national laboratory ecosystem is positioned to engage seriously on these
problems. The panel's collective message was clear: the urgency is real, the technical challenges are
tractable, and the path forward runs directly through the kind of sustained, trust-based collaboration
between OEMs, universities, and national laboratories that workshops like this one are designed to
cultivate.

 13. BREAKOUT SESSION 4: REPAIR, REFURBISHMENT, AND LIFECYCLE
 MANAGEMENT

 Figure 13. Co-Moderator: Manish Kumar (Siemens Energy).

The Repair, Refurbishment, and Lifecycle Management breakout session convened OEM engineers,
MRO service providers, utility operators, materials scientists, and university faculty to examine one of the
most consequential near-term levers in the gas turbine supply chain: the ability to extend the productive
life of hardware already in the field. With new hardware capacity severely constrained and lead times for
original equipment stretching to multi-year horizons, the capacity to repair, refurbish, and intelligently
manage turbine components through multiple service cycles has moved from a cost optimization exercise
to a strategic supply chain imperative.

Four interconnected themes shaped the discussion: the technical and data infrastructure needed to support
condition-based lifecycle management; repair and refurbishment technologies and their current limits; the
manufacturing metadata gap that undermines both repair quality and lifecycle prediction; and the
workforce and curriculum changes needed to sustain these capabilities over the long term. Across all four,
participants identified a common structural gap—the absence of shared standards, data frameworks, and
collaborative incentives that would allow OEMs, repair providers, operators, and research institutions to
act on what they collectively already know.

13.1 THEME 1: LIFECYCLE MANAGEMENT — FROM FIXED INTERVALS TO
 CONDITION-BASED LIFE

The session opened with a frank discussion of how gas turbine maintenance intervals are currently
determined and why that approach is increasingly misaligned with operational realities. Standard
maintenance schedules—defined by hours, starts, or cycles—were designed for relatively predictable
operating conditions. But turbines today are being dispatched in patterns that bear little resemblance to the

 65

steady-state baseload profiles those intervals assumed: cycling more frequently to balance variable
renewables, ramping more aggressively to serve data center loads, and running at higher average
utilization as demand growth outpaces new capacity additions.

The result is that some units are consuming their maintenance intervals far faster than the schedule
implies, while others—operating in more benign conditions—could safely extend between service events.
Fixed intervals fail both groups: they impose unnecessary cost and downtime on one, and potentially
underinvest in inspection and remediation for the other. Participants argued that the industry needs to
move toward condition-based lifecycle management, in which actual operating history, component
inspection data, and physics-based models of damage accumulation together inform individualized
maintenance decisions.

A key enabler discussed was the development of cost-function frameworks that let operators see the true
economic value of flexible maintenance scheduling. If shutting down a unit early for inspection costs X in
lost generation revenue but saves Y in avoided forced outage or premature hardware retirement, that
calculation can and should be made explicit. OEM digital service platforms were noted as a natural
vehicle for this kind of decision support—but participants observed that few operators currently have
access to the full lifecycle cost functions needed to optimize those tradeoffs, and OEMs are
understandably cautious about fully delegating those recommendations to automated systems.

A secondary discussion addressed the potential for credit mechanisms—analogous to manufacturer trade-
in programs—to incentivize operators to return hardware for refurbishment before it reaches end-of-life,
rather than running components to the point where repair is no longer viable. Remanufactured content
credit systems, tiered across the industry, were proposed as a way to align OEM and operator incentives
around maximum hardware reuse.

• OEM digital service platforms extended to include condition-based maintenance recommendation

 behind recommendations

• Industry-wide remanufactured content credit frameworks that incentivize operators to return hardware

 equipment

• Flexible maintenance schedule standards that allow certification and warranty coverage for condition-

13.2 THEME 2: REPAIR AND REFURBISHMENT TECHNOLOGIES — CAPABILITY,
 GAPS, AND PRIORITIZATION

The repair and refurbishment discussion covered a wide range of technologies and process capabilities,
organized by the participants around an informal risk-versus-reward framework that surfaced on the
sticky-note clusters. High-reward, near-term opportunities were distinguished from longer-horizon
investments that carry more technical risk—a framing that proved useful for identifying where
collaborative R&D investment could have the greatest near-term supply chain impact.

Rapid casting was identified as a high-reward capability with significant potential to reduce lead times for
replacement hardware, particularly for legacy parts that can no longer be sourced through original supply
chains. The ability to produce cast replacements faster—potentially leveraging additive manufacturing
patterns or tooling—could materially shorten the supply gap for aging turbine fleets.

Electrical discharge machining (EDM) was highlighted as a critical process for repair operations that
participants felt deserved more focused research attention, particularly in the context of how EDM
interacts with the microstructure and fatigue life of repaired components. Understanding and controlling
the heat-affected zone and surface integrity of EDM-repaired features—especially in nickel superalloys—
was identified as an underinvested area relative to its importance in the MRO workflow.

Material joining was identified as a research priority that cuts across multiple repair scenarios. The ability
to reliably join dissimilar or degraded superalloy materials—through brazing, transient liquid phase
bonding, friction welding, or emerging adhesive ('glue') technologies for lower-stress structural repairs—
determines the range of damage states from which a component can be economically recovered.
Participants called for prioritized metallurgical studies on joining methods and materials, including
systematic characterization of joint properties under realistic service conditions. Structural repair of
blades using additive or deposition methods (AF/AM) was noted as a longer-horizon but high-value
capability that warrants continued investment.

Non-destructive evaluation (NDE) was woven throughout the repair discussion as the foundational
capability on which all other repair decisions rest. Without reliable, cost-effective NDE, repair providers
cannot confidently assess the condition of incoming hardware, cannot certify repaired components, and
cannot build the data histories needed to improve lifecycle models. Specific NDE gaps identified
included: ductility and toughness characterization of components with uncertain prior history; fatigue life
assessment for components that have accumulated damage in unknown operating conditions; and the
potential for combining AI-assisted image analysis with disassembly inspection data to characterize non-
connected (unmonitored) units. The combination of external 3D scanning, ultrasonic testing for internal
geometry, and integrated modeling was highlighted as a deployable, near-term approach to significantly
improve repair decision quality.

13.3 THEME 3: MANUFACTURING METADATA — THE MISSING LINK IN THE REPAIR
 CHAIN

One of the most practically grounded discussions in the session centered on a gap that participants
described as both well understood and frustratingly persistent: the absence of standardized manufacturing
metadata that travels with a part through its entire service life. When a turbine component arrives at a
repair facility, the technician often knows very little about how that part was actually made—what
specific lot of alloy was used, what the precise thermal processing history was, what dimensional
measurements were taken at manufacture, or what early-life inspection results looked like.

This matters enormously for repair quality and lifecycle prediction. A single-crystal turbine blade's
fatigue resistance depends on its crystallographic orientation, which varies from part to part within the
acceptable tolerance band. A coating's adhesion and durability depend on the substrate surface condition
at the time of original application. A disk's remaining crack propagation life depends on the grain size
distribution that resulted from its specific forging and heat treatment process. Without access to this
information, repair providers must either apply conservative blanket assumptions—which may result in
premature retirement of hardware that has significant remaining life—or accept greater uncertainty in
their return-to-service assessments.

Participants argued that the solution is a serialized part metadata standard: a requirement that suppliers
capture and transfer key manufacturing process data in a standardized digital format, linked to a unique
part identifier, such that this information is accessible to downstream repair and lifecycle management
operations. The analogy offered was a part 'fingerprint'—a compact, standardized record of the
manufacturing conditions and inspection results that define that specific component's starting state.

Several participants noted that OEMs individually possess pieces of this information but have not
standardized how it is captured, structured, or transferred. The competitive sensitivity of detailed process
parameters was acknowledged, but participants argued that the core metadata—alloy lot, heat treatment
batch, dimensional inspection results, coating application conditions—does not constitute the proprietary
process knowledge that OEMs need to protect. What is needed is a government-supported consortium
effort that brings OEMs together to agree on the minimum viable metadata standard and fund the tooling
and infrastructure required to implement it across the supply chain.

13.3.1 Key Gaps and Opportunities

• Development of a serialized part metadata standard specifying the minimum manufacturing process
 data—alloy lot, heat treatment, dimensional inspection results, coating history—that must travel with
 each turbine component through its service life

13.4 THEME 4: WORKFORCE DEVELOPMENT — BUILDING CAPABILITY FOR
 CONDITION-BASED AND REPAIR OPERATIONS

Workforce development for repair, refurbishment, and lifecycle management drew engaged discussion, with
participants from both industry and academia exploring how educational programs and training pipelines
need to evolve to support a more data-intensive, condition-based approach to turbine maintenance.

A consistent observation was that materials science and mechanical engineering curricula at the
undergraduate level provide the foundational knowledge relevant to lifecycle and repair—failure
mechanisms, fracture mechanics, heat transfer, materials degradation—but rarely integrate these topics in
ways that connect to real-world maintenance decision-making. Students learn strength of materials and
fatigue theory as separate modules, but are rarely asked to apply them together to a realistic component
lifecycle question. The result is that new engineers entering MRO or lifecycle engineering roles have the
theoretical tools but lack the applied judgment that comes from seeing how those fundamentals interact in
a real damaged component.

Participants proposed several mechanisms to close this gap. Industry-sponsored internships and senior
design projects focused specifically on digital twin development and lifecycle management were
identified as high-leverage interventions: they give students exposure to real operational data, real
damaged hardware, and the practical challenge of building condition assessment algorithms that actually
work. Industry-run design challenges—modeled on the case competition format common in business
schools, with real operational datasets, modest prize incentives, and an emphasis on learn-by-doing—
were proposed as an accessible, scalable complement to traditional coursework.

Materials science curriculum reform was identified as a specific need: the integration of condition-based
life concepts—damage accumulation, remaining useful life estimation, the connection between
microstructure and service-induced degradation—into standard undergraduate coursework. This is not
about making every materials science graduate a lifecycle engineer, but about ensuring that the
foundational mental models for condition-based thinking are in place before students enter industry.

On the trades and technician side, participants noted the analogy to the robotics education ecosystem: the
success of platforms like Raspberry Pi and Arduino in making electronics and programming accessible to
a broad audience suggests a similar open-source, challenge-kit model could work for industrial
maintenance skills, including thermal spray, joining, and non-traditional machining. Connecting these kit-
based learning experiences to gas turbine-specific 'specials'—the highly specialized process skills that are
hardest to find in the workforce—was seen as a longer-term but important pipeline investment.

13.6 CONCLUSION

The Repair, Refurbishment, and Lifecycle Management breakout session made clear that this domain is
one of the highest-leverage intervention points in the gas turbine supply chain. The hardware already in
the field represents an enormous, embedded investment, and the ability to recover, extend, and
intelligently manage that investment is not merely an economic optimization—it is, in the current supply
environment, a near-term operational necessity. New equipment cannot arrive fast enough to meet
demand; every additional service cycle extracted from existing hardware through better repair and
lifecycle management directly addresses the capacity gap.

What constrains progress is not primarily technical capability—the repair technologies, the NDE tools,
the modeling frameworks largely exist in some form—but rather the data infrastructure, standards, and
collaborative arrangements needed to deploy them consistently and confidently across a heterogeneous
industry. The manufacturing metadata gap is emblematic of this broader challenge: a known problem, a
tractable solution, but no mechanism to coordinate the agreement and investment required. A
government-supported consortium effort, bringing OEMs, repair providers, operators, and national
laboratories together around common metadata standards and shared lifecycle modeling frameworks, was
the most consistently proposed structural solution across the session.

National laboratories are well positioned to serve as conveners and technical anchors for this work:
neutral enough to hold shared data and standards without competitive concern, capable enough to develop
the underlying models and characterization databases, and connected enough to both industry and
academia to translate research into deployable tools. Universities bring the workforce pipeline and the
basic research capacity that sustains the field over the long term. The urgency is clear; the path forward
requires only the coordination mechanisms to take the first steps.
