Background

Primary adrenal lymphoma (PAL) is an exceedingly rare and aggressive malignancy, accounting for less than 1% of all non-Hodgkin lymphomas and a small fraction of adrenal tumors [1, 2]. Unlike adrenal cortical carcinoma or pheochromocytoma, PAL lacks distinctive biochemical markers or imaging findings, making early diagnosis particularly challenging. The tumor often presents with nonspecific symptoms, such as abdominal pain, fatigue, weight loss, or adrenal insufficiency in bilateral cases [2, 3]. Most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage due to the deep location of the adrenal glands and lack of early clinical signs.

The pathogenesis of PAL remains unclear, although it is believed to arise de novo from hematopoietic elements within the adrenal gland. The most frequently encountered histological subtype is diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), followed by rarer types, such as high-grade B-cell lymphoma and follicular lymphoma [3, 4].

Imaging studies, such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), are commonly used to characterize adrenal masses; however, they cannot reliably distinguish PAL from other adrenal tumors. In addition, fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG PET/CT) may contribute to disease staging and evaluation of extranodal involvement, although definitive diagnosis still requires histopathological confirmation. Because radiologic findings may mimic adrenal cortical carcinoma or metastatic disease, unnecessary adrenalectomy may occur in the absence of tissue diagnosis. Therefore, tissue biopsy is essential for a definitive diagnosis, especially in atypical presentations or when surgical intervention is considered [4, 5].

Treatment is primarily based on systemic chemotherapy, with the rituximab plus cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (R-CHOP) regimen being the most widely used first-line treatment for CD20-positive B-cell lymphomas. However, due to the aggressive nature of the disease and its frequent bilateral involvement, prognosis is often poor, with median survival ranging from several months to two years in most series [2, 6]. Favorable outcomes have been reported in selected cases with early diagnosis, unilateral involvement, and a good response to chemotherapy.

Herein, we present a case of a 71-year-old man with unilateral primary adrenal high-grade B-cell lymphoma who achieved complete remission after CHOP chemotherapy and remained disease-free for two years. In addition, we provide a focused review of previously reported cases of unilateral primary adrenal lymphoma treated with systemic chemotherapy. This case highlights the importance of multidisciplinary evaluation and underscores the value of radiologic interpretation and tissue confirmation in avoiding unnecessary surgeries.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old man presented to the urology outpatient clinic with a 6-month history of left upper quadrant abdominal pain and progressive fatigue over the past 3 months. His medical history included hypertension, coronary artery disease, benign prostatic hyperplasia, and hyperthyroidism; he had not undergone any prior surgical interventions. He was regularly treated with ramipril, acetylsalicylic acid, doxazosin, and methimazole.

On physical examination, a non-tender, palpable mass was detected in the left flank. Laboratory findings showed normocytic anemia with a hemoglobin level of 8.9 g/dL, hematocrit of 27.1%, and thrombocytopenia with a platelet count of 95,000/µL. Biochemical and hormonal evaluations, including adrenal function tests, were within normal limits. Serum cortisol, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), and routine biochemical adrenal function parameters showed no evidence of adrenal insufficiency or hormonally active adrenal disease.

Abdominal ultrasonography revealed a large, heterogeneous, hypoechoic mass in the left adrenal gland. Contrast-enhanced C of the abdomen revealed a well-defined, heterogeneous, hypodense left adrenal mass with necrotic and cystic areas, measuring approximately 16 cm in its longest diameter. There was also suspicion of local invasion of adjacent structures, such as the pancreas (Fig. 1). Additional adrenal protocol CT or MRI was not performed because the lesion was initially considered highly suspicious for adrenal malignancy requiring surgical evaluation.

Axial contrast-enhanced CT images showing a large, heterogeneous mass in the left adrenal region (marked by an arrow), with features suggestive of necrosis and suspected invasion of adjacent tissues

Initially, the lesion was considered to be a primary adrenal carcinoma, and surgical resection was planned. However, a detailed radiologic review by an experienced radiologist raised the possibility of primary adrenal lymphoma. Based on this suggestion, the decision was made to proceed with a CT-guided biopsy rather than immediate surgery.

A percutaneous CT-guided core needle biopsy of the adrenal mass was performed. No formal staging evaluation, such as PET/CT or Ann Arbor classification, was performed at the time of diagnosis. Histopathological examination revealed dense infiltration of atypical lymphoid cells with high mitotic activity and a diffuse growth pattern (Fig. 2). Immunohistochemical staining was strongly positive for Cluster of Differentiation 20 (CD20/ B-cell surface marker) (Fig. 3) and B-cell Lymphoma 6 Protein (BCL-6/ transcriptional repressor involved in B-cell development) (Fig. 4), confirming the diagnosis of a high-grade B-cell lymphoma. The Ki-67 proliferation index was approximately 80%, indicating high proliferative activity. Bone marrow aspiration and biopsy revealed normocellular marrow without lymphoma infiltration in the patient.

Following diagnosis, the patient received six cycles of standard R-CHOP chemotherapy (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone). The treatment course was well tolerated, without major complications. Post-treatment imaging with contrast-enhanced CT showed complete resolution of the adrenal mass, with no residual lesion or signs of local invasion.

The patient has been followed regularly with clinical evaluation and imaging for two years and remains in complete remission without any evidence of disease recurrence.

Discussion and conclusions

PAL is a rare and clinically challenging malignancy, representing less than 1% of all extranodal non-Hodgkin lymphomas and a very small proportion of adrenal tumors [1, 4]. The condition most frequently affects elderly males and often presents with nonspecific symptoms, such as abdominal discomfort, fatigue, or weight loss. Adrenal insufficiency is common, particularly in cases with bilateral adrenal involvement [3, 7].

The most prevalent histological subtype is DLBCL, whereas high-grade B-cell and follicular variants are significantly less common [3]. Diagnosis is often delayed due to the absence of specific biochemical or imaging markers, and many patients are initially misdiagnosed with more common adrenal pathologies, such as adrenocortical carcinoma or pheochromocytoma. This diagnostic uncertainty may lead to unnecessary adrenalectomy, as documented in multiple studies [2, 8, 9].

Our case is distinctive in that surgical resection was avoided owing to multidisciplinary collaboration and radiologic expertise. Although the initial radiologic interpretation favored primary adrenal carcinoma, re-evaluation by an experienced radiologist raised suspicion of PAL. This prompted a CT-guided biopsy, which confirmed the diagnosis of high-grade B-cell lymphoma. This strategy enabled the timely initiation of systemic chemotherapy and spared the patient unnecessary surgical morbidity.

CT-guided adrenal biopsy has been increasingly emphasized in recent years as a pivotal step in the diagnostic work-up of large adrenal masses, particularly in the absence of endocrine activity. Harada et al. [10] reported five cases of PAL diagnosed via image-guided biopsy, highlighting its importance in excluding adrenal insufficiency and guiding management. Similarly, Ezer et al. [11] and Aravamudan et al. [12] demonstrated that early biopsy can redirect the clinical approach and eliminate the need for surgery in patients with lymphomas.

Systemic chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of treatment, with R-CHOP regimens being the most commonly utilized [13–15]. Despite treatment, the overall prognosis is poor, particularly in older patients, those with bilateral disease, and cases involving adrenal insufficiency. Li et al. [6] reviewed 136 patients and found that age > 70 years and bilateral involvement were independent predictors of worse survival. Additional risk factors include Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) positivity and co-expression of Myelocytomatosis oncogene (MYC) and B-cell Lymphoma 2 Protein (BCL2/ Anti-apoptotic protein associated with poor prognosis in lymphomas), which are associated with aggressive disease and poor response [6, 16, 17].

Although PET/CT and Ann Arbor staging are commonly recommended in lymphoma evaluation, formal staging assessment was not performed in the present case because the patient was initially evaluated with a presumptive diagnosis of primary adrenal carcinoma rather than lymphoma. Nevertheless, bone marrow aspiration and biopsy demonstrated no evidence of lymphoma infiltration, and the patient achieved complete radiologic remission following systemic therapy.

Remarkably, our patient, despite being 71 years old, achieved complete remission following six cycles of R-CHOP chemotherapy and remained recurrence-free for 24 months. This favorable outcome may be attributed to several factors, including unilateral adrenal involvement, preserved adrenal function, absence of high-risk immunohistochemical markers, and timely diagnosis prior to clinical progression. In addition, the Ki-67 proliferation index was approximately 80%, consistent with the aggressive biological behavior of high-grade B-cell lymphoma. Comparable long-term remission outcomes have been reported by Kim et al. [14] and Zhang et al. [18]. Kim et al. described a 66-year-old patient who achieved complete remission with R-CHOP and remained disease-free for 18 months. Zhang et al. reported a series of 14 patients, among whom a subset with unilateral disease achieved prolonged remission, particularly when the Ki-67 index was below 60%.

While surgery may still be indicated in selected cases, particularly for diagnostic uncertainty, tumor rupture, or bleeding, most studies support a chemotherapy-first approach for histologically confirmed PAL [1, 5, 7]. Our case adds to the growing body of literature advocating for early biopsy and systemic therapy in the management of large adrenal masses of uncertain origin.

PAL should be considered in the differential diagnosis of large adrenal masses, particularly in elderly patients presenting with nonspecific abdominal symptoms. This case highlights the pivotal role of multidisciplinary evaluation and CT-guided biopsy in achieving an accurate diagnosis and avoiding unnecessary surgical intervention. In selected patients with unilateral disease and preserved adrenal function, systemic chemotherapy may provide excellent long-term results. Early identification and individualized management remain essential for optimizing outcomes and preventing overtreatment in patients with atypical adrenal masses.
