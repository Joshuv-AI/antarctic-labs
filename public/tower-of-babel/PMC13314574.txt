Simultaneous ultrasound and needle electromyography recording of fasciculations in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis
Authors: Takamichi Sugimoto, Keisuke Tachiyama, Akemi Hironaka, Hiroyuki Naito, Masahiro Nakamori, Shiro Aoki, Yu Yamazaki, Hirofumi Maruyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFasciculations can be detected using both muscle ultrasonography and needle electromyography, yet the correspondence between ultrasonographically observed fasciculations (U-fas) and needle electromyography–detected fasciculation potentials (N-fas) has not been clarified. This study investigated their correspondence using fully synchronized recordings.MethodsAdult patients showing fasciculation-like contractions on muscle ultrasonography were enrolled; all were subsequently diagnosed with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Ultrasound and needle electromyography were recorded simultaneously in up to three muscles per patient, with a recording duration of 3 min per muscle. For each ultrasonographically observed fasciculation, the presence of a corresponding electromyographic event and contraction duration assessed by M-mode imaging were evaluated.ResultsTen patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis were included. A total of 472 focused U-fas events were analyzed. Corresponding N-fas were detected in 437 events, yielding an overall concordance rate of 92.6% (95% confidence interval, 90.2–95.0%). U-fas contraction duration ranged from 343 to 971 ms, whereas N-fas duration ranged from 10.9 to 76.4 ms. The number of phases of N-fas observed during U-fas events ranged from 1 to 10.ConclusionsMost ultrasonographically observed fasciculations corresponded to electromyography-detected events on simultaneous recording.SignificanceUltrasonographically detected fasciculations may serve as a supplementary indicator of lower motor neuron involvement in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.Keywords: Fasciculation, Ultrasonography, Electromyography, Motor neuron disease, Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis

Introduction

Fasciculation is defined as a random spontaneous twitching of a group of muscle fibers belonging to a single motor unit (American Association of Neuromuscular and Electrodiagnostic Medicine, 2015), and it is widely recognized as a lower motor neuron (LMN) sign (Shefner et al., 2020). Fasciculations can be observed in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), benign fasciculation syndrome, radiation-induced neuropathy, and Isaacs syndrome (Mills, 2010; Cai et al., 2016; Matsui et al., 2025). In particular, fasciculation potentials recorded by needle EMG play an important role in demonstrating LMN involvement in ALS, and were regarded as a significant diagnostic feature in the updated Awaji criteria (Geevasinga et al., 2016).

It has been reported that fasciculations can be visualized using muscle ultrasonography (Misawa et al., 2011). Previous studies have reported that muscle ultrasonography may detect fasciculations with higher sensitivity than electromyography, possibly due to its larger field of observation (Walker et al., 1990). However, the electrophysiological nature of these ultrasonographically detected contractions has not been fully established. Muscle ultrasonography is a non-invasive technique that allows real-time visualization of muscle activity and can be repeatedly performed without discomfort to the patient. However, the correspondence between muscle contractions identified as fasciculations on ultrasonography and fasciculation potentials detected by needle electromyography (EMG)—that is, the correspondence between these two modalities—has not been sufficiently investigated. Although several studies, such as those by O'Gorman et al. (2017) and Bokuda et al. (2020), have compared EMG and ultrasonographic findings in the same muscle, these evaluations were not performed under simultaneous recording conditions. To date, the only report that fully synchronized both modalities is that of Regensburger et al. (2018), who reported the feasibility of synchronized ultrasound–EMG recordings and primarily investigated the spatial detection radius of EMG for fasciculations. However, event-level correspondence between ultrasonographically observed fasciculations and needle EMG–detected fasciculation potentials has not been systematically evaluated.

In this study, we simultaneously recorded muscle ultrasonography and needle EMG to examine how fasciculations observed on ultrasonography are represented as spontaneous activity on EMG. Simultaneous recording enables direct temporal alignment between ultrasonographic and electromyographic findings, allowing assessment of whether visually detected muscle contractions correspond to electrophysiological fasciculation potentials. We aimed to examine the event-level correspondence between ultrasonographically observed fasciculations and needle EMG–detected fasciculation potentials using fully synchronized recordings.

Patients and methods

This study was conducted with the approval of the Epidemiology and Ethics Committee of Hiroshima University Hospital. From April 2023 to March 2024, adult patients (≥18 years old) who were under diagnostic evaluation at our hospital for suspected neuromuscular disorders, including conditions potentially associated with fasciculations, were consecutively screened. Muscle ultrasonography was performed during the diagnostic work-up, and patients in whom muscle contractions considered to represent fasciculations were identified in at least one of the following muscles were included in the study: trapezius, biceps brachii, triceps brachii, flexor digitorum superficialis, flexor carpi ulnaris, first dorsal interossei, vastus medialis, and tibialis anterior. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Needle electromyography was performed as part of the routine diagnostic work-up. All enrolled patients fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for ALS at the time of recording, and the diagnosis was confirmed within a few days as part of the same diagnostic work-up.

Ultrasound examinations were performed using a linear array probe (10–15 MHz) attached to a LOGIQ Fortis LCD system (GE Healthcare Japan). Ultrasound and EMG examinations were performed by KT under the supervision of TS, with support from AH, in all patients. The imaging depth and field of view were initially set to 4 cm × 4 cm, while the gain setting was kept constant throughout the examination. However, during M-mode recording, the field of view was adjusted as needed to focus on the muscle contractions of interest. Ultrasound-detected fasciculations (U-fas) were defined as spontaneous, localized muscle contractions observed in real time using ultrasonography.

Needle EMG was performed using a Neuropack MEB-2306 system (Nihon Kohden, Japan) with a concentric needle electrode (NM series, Nihon Kohden, Japan; diameter 0.45 mm). For simultaneous recording of ultrasound and EMG, a Dual HDMI Capture device (GV-LSU200, I—O DATA, Japan) was used. During the examination, U-fas were displayed on the monitor, and the needle tip was positioned so that it could be visualized in close proximity to the targeted fasciculation. The needle was positioned near the site of visible fasciculations and kept in place during the recording period, allowing repeated events to be captured. When multiple ultrasonographically observed muscle contractions were present within the same field of view, analysis was focused on the contraction located closest to the needle tip. The presence or absence of a corresponding needle EMG–detected fasciculation potential (N-fas) was assessed specifically for this targeted ultrasonographic event. In some events, a corresponding N-fas was judged to be present, but its waveform parameters could not be reliably measured. These included cases in which the EMG waveform was truncated at the margins of the recording window (laterally or vertically), consisted of closely spaced double discharges, or was contaminated by voluntary activity. Such events were counted as N-fas present but were excluded from duration-based analyses. The number of phases was defined as the number of baseline crossings of the EMG waveform, consistent with standard electrophysiological definitions.

At screening, up to three muscles per patient that were considered suitable for detecting U-fas were selected for simultaneous recording. When fasciculations were observed in four or more muscles, priority for selection was given in the following order: biceps brachii, vastus medialis, tibialis anterior > triceps brachii > trapezius > flexor carpi ulnaris, flexor digitorum superficialis. Muscle selection was based on practical and clinical considerations, including patient comfort, ease of examination, and suitability for simultaneous ultrasonography and needle EMG recording, to minimize patient burden. When multiple muscles were eligible, a predefined priority order was applied for consistency. Needle insertion was performed under ultrasound guidance. If multiple fasciculations were observed within the same field of view, the site most suitable for EMG evaluation (i.e., where muscle architecture was preserved and needle insertion was feasible) was selected. The recording duration for each muscle was set at 3 min. Focused U-fas were defined as ultrasonographically visible fasciculation events occurring during the simultaneous recording period in close proximity to the needle tip; intervals in which no such events occurred were not included in the event-level correspondence analysis. Because the analysis was designed to assess event-level correspondence triggered by visually confirmed fasciculation events adjacent to the needle tip, recordings without focused U-fas yielded no analyzable events and were therefore treated as zero-event recordings.

Clinical data collected included sex, age, disease duration, ALS Functional Rating Scale–Revised (ALSFRS-R), and diagnostic classification according to the updated Awaji criteria (Geevasinga et al., 2016).

Statistical analyses were performed using JMP 19 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) and IBM SPSS Statistics 25 (IBM corp., Armonk, NY). The concordance rate was calculated as the proportion of U-fas events with corresponding N-fas, and its 95% confidence interval was estimated based on a binomial distribution.

Discussion

This study shows a high event-level concordance between U-fas and N-fas under fully synchronized recording conditions. This finding suggests that muscle contractions identified as U-fas in patients with ALS correspond to fasciculation potentials of lower motor neuron origin, rather than nonspecific muscle movements. Importantly, the present study assessed correspondence under conditions in which the needle was positioned in close proximity to the visualized contraction, and thus reflects local correspondence between the two modalities.

Previous work by Regensburger et al. (2018) reported that U-fas and N-fas can be captured simultaneously in patients with ALS, providing important foundational evidence for synchronized ultrasound–EMG assessment. Their study also reported concordance between the two modalities, and our findings likewise support a high concordance rate, suggesting that U-fas observed in patients with suspected ALS often reflect the electrophysiological events represented by N-fas. Notably, the study design differed between the two studies. Regensburger et al. evaluated EMG detection probability as a function of distance from the fasciculation source, showing a distance-dependent limitation of EMG sensitivity. In contrast, the present study employed ultrasound-guided needle placement to position the needle in close proximity to the target contraction, with the aim of capturing corresponding events under optimized conditions. This methodological difference may partly explain the higher concordance observed in the present study and should be considered when interpreting the results. Accordingly, the observed concordance rate should be interpreted as reflecting local correspondence under ultrasound-guided and spatially optimized recording conditions, rather than global agreement between ultrasonography and EMG across the muscle. In addition, the present findings do not allow inference regarding diagnostic performance or comparative sensitivity between ultrasonography and needle EMG.

The number of phases of N-fas observed during U-fas events ranged from 1 to 10 in our study. As previously reported, the number of phases is not a defining criterion for fasciculation potentials (de Carvalho, 2000; Higashihara et al., 2012; de Carvalho and Swash, 2013).

Previous studies have reported that the duration of U-fas ranges from 0.2 to 0.5 s (Reimers et al., 1988; Reimers et al., 1996). In the present study, while many U-fas events fell within this previously reported range, we also observed U-fas with longer contraction durations than those commonly described in earlier studies. The limited description of such longer-duration events in prior reports may be attributable to differences in patient populations, disease stage, or observation conditions.

Several studies have examined the observation time required for detecting U-fas using ultrasound (Takamatsu et al., 2016; Noto et al., 2017), and Noto reported that a 60-s observation period was used for U-fas detection. In the present study, we employed a 3-min observation period. Because our primary objective was not simply to determine the presence or absence of U-fas, but rather to assess the concordance between U-fas and N-fas, we considered a longer recording time to ensure sufficient capture of analyzable events and therefore used a 3-min observation period.

Beyond diagnosis, ultrasonography may have potential applications in the assessment of disease activity and longitudinal monitoring of fasciculation patterns, given its non-invasive and repeatable nature. However, these applications were not addressed in the present study and require further investigation. In addition, further studies are needed to clarify the relationship between U-fas duration and the morphology of N-fas. We recently reported a non-ALS case in which ultrasonographically observed muscle contractions appeared morphologically similar to fasciculations, whereas simultaneous EMG recordings demonstrated low-frequency myokymic discharges (Sugimoto et al., 2026). This observation suggests that similar ultrasonographic muscle movements may reflect different electrophysiological mechanisms depending on the underlying disease context. Ultrasonography primarily reflects morphological muscle movement, whereas EMG provides direct functional electrophysiological information. Therefore, ultrasonography should be interpreted as a complementary modality rather than a replacement for needle EMG.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not examine in detail the positional relationship between the needle electrode and the fasciculation site. In this study, we attempted to keep the needle tip visible within a fixed 4 cm × 4 cm ultrasound field of view and positioned it as close as possible to the observed muscle contraction. However, a strict quantitative assessment of the exact distance was not performed, as ultrasonography provides a two-dimensional representation of a three-dimensional structure, making precise spatial measurement difficult. Nevertheless, our results indicate that U-fas could be sufficiently visualized under these needle-placement conditions. Second, the present analysis was designed as an event-driven approach triggered by U-fas, with the primary aim of examining whether corresponding N-fas were simultaneously present. In addition, precise spatial correspondence between U-fas and N-fas could not be fully ensured, as ultrasonography provides a two-dimensional representation of a three-dimensional structure, limiting accurate alignment between the two modalities. Therefore, spontaneous N-fas occurring in the absence of detectable U-fas were not included in the present analysis. This design may increase the likelihood of detecting corresponding N-fas and thus influence the observed concordance rate. This design was chosen to specifically address the correspondence between visually detectable muscle contractions and their electrophysiological correlates, rather than to comprehensively quantify all EMG-detected fasciculation activity. Smaller or less complex fasciculation potentials may be more likely to fall below the detection threshold of EMG, which could influence the observed correspondence. Prolonged or atypical ultrasonographic muscle contractions may require further validation to exclude potential misclassification. The present study was not designed to compare the detection sensitivity of ultrasound and needle EMG, and such comparisons cannot be inferred from the present data. Third, although a large number of U-fas events were analyzed, the number of patients from whom these data were obtained was small. Additional validation or follow-up studies will be important to further support the robustness of our findings. In addition, the potential influence of clinical phenotype, including PMA, on fasciculation detectability and concordance between ultrasonography and EMG was not evaluated due to the small sample size. Further studies will be required to clarify possible phenotype-dependent differences in these findings. Fourth, simultaneous recording of U-fas with M-mode and N-fas was not performed in the present study, which may have provided further insight into the relationship between U-fas duration and N-fas morphology. Fifth, this study focused exclusively on patients with ALS. Therefore, it does not guarantee that muscle contractions resembling U-fas in other neuromuscular disorders would necessarily correspond to N-fas. The interpretation of U-fas–N-fas correspondence should always consider the underlying disease context. Finally, this study included only selected muscles based on practical and clinical considerations, including ease of examination, patient comfort, and suitability for simultaneous ultrasonography and needle EMG recording. Deep and facial muscles were not included, although these regions may offer additional insights when combining ultrasonography and EMG and warrant further investigation. These limitations indicate that several important questions remain unresolved, including EMG-triggered simultaneous analysis, distance-dependent analysis of the relationship between the fasciculation source and the needle tip, and detailed morphology-based subgroup analyses. Further studies using different study designs and acquisition strategies will be required to address these issues appropriately.

In this study, we analyzed more than 400 U-fas under fully synchronized ultrasound and needle EMG recording, thereby characterizing the relationship between ultrasonographically detected fasciculations and their electrophysiological correlates. The high concordance observed in patients with ALS suggests that U-fas may provide complementary information in the assessment of lower motor neuron involvement, particularly when N-fas cannot be readily detected. With appropriate consideration of the underlying disease context, our findings support further exploration of ultrasonographic fasciculation assessment within the diagnostic evaluation of ALS.
