Clinical and genomic features of mitis group streptococcal bacteremia in patients with febrile neutropenia
Authors: Yu Arakawa, Yusuke Yagi, Yoshie Nishida, Saya Iwame, Hiroshige Mikamo, Yuka Yamagishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundViridans group streptococci (VGS) can cause the life-threatening viridans streptococcal shock syndrome (VSSS) in patients with febrile neutropenia (FN). The Mitis group, a major subgroup of VGS, is frequently implicated in these severe infections, but its specific clinical and genomic characteristics remain incompletely characterized, particularly in patients with FN. This study aimed to systematically describe these features in this population.MethodsIn this single-center retrospective study, we compared the clinical data and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) results of Mitis group streptococcal isolates from patients with and without FN. Background

Febrile neutropenia (FN) is a critical complication of cancer therapy and carries a heightened risk for severe infections. While empirical treatment has traditionally focused on Gram-negative bacteria such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Gram-positive cocci, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, have also emerged as clinically significant pathogens in the context of FN [1].

Among Gram-positive bacteria, viridans group streptococci (VGS) are a common cause of bloodstream infections in patients with FN [1]. Although typically regarded as commensals, VGS can cause serious complications in immunocompromised hosts, including acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and septic shock—a constellation referred to as viridans streptococcal shock syndrome (VSSS) [2–7]. The reported incidence of VSSS in patients with FN ranges from 7% to 39%, with associated mortality rates of 2% to 21% [7].

Recent advances in phylogenetic analyses have led to the reclassification of VGS into distinct groups based on genetic relatedness. Notably, the Mitis group represents a well-defined clade within the VGS, including Streptococcus mitis, S. oralis, and S. sanguinis [8]. These species are dominant colonizers of the human oral cavity and are frequently implicated in systemic infections in patients with neutropenia. Importantly, several reported VSSS cases have involved species within the Mitis group [7, 9, 10], rather than across all VGS [11], suggesting a potential clinical relevance of this subgroup.

Despite increasing clinical concerns, detailed epidemiological and pathogenic studies focusing on the Mitis group streptococci in FN remain limited. This study investigated the clinical and microbiological characteristics of Mitis group streptococcal bacteremia in patients with FN. Specifically, we performed a comparative analysis of virulence gene profiles between strains isolated from patients with FN, including those who developed VSSS, and strains from individuals without FN. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was used to explore genomic diversity and to screen for candidate loci potentially associated with severe clinical manifestations. By integrating molecular and clinical data, this study aimed to provide a more detailed characterization of these infections.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection

This retrospective study was conducted at the Kochi Medical School Hospital between January 2007 and December 2024. Patients in whom the Mitis group was identified in blood cultures were included in the analysis. Clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records using the CLISTA® clinical data search system (Medical Engineering Institute, Inc., Tsu, Japan). For this study, the Mitis group streptococci were defined based on phylogenetic classification, and included 13 species: S. australis, S. cristatus, S. gordonii, S. infantis, S. mitis, S. oligofermentans, S. oralis, S. parasanguinis, S. peroris, S. pneumoniae, S. pseudopneumoniae, S. sanguinis, and S. tigurinus [8]. However, because S. pneumoniae and S. pseudopneumoniae are clinically and genetically distinct from other oral streptococci and have well-established pathogenic roles in non-FN populations, they were excluded from the present analysis. Therefore, isolates belonging to the remaining 11 species were categorized into the Mitis group. Species identification was performed using the API 20 Strep system (BioMérieux Inc., Marcy-l’Étoile, France) until July 2022; from October 2022 onward, matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS, ver. 11.0.0.0; Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA, USA) was used. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted using the WalkAway® system (Beckman Coulter, Inc., Brea, CA, USA). Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) breakpoints were interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100, 35th edition (2025). The collected clinical information included age, sex, underlying diseases, presence of hematologic malignancies, FN status, complications, Pitt bacteremia score (PBS), Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), administered antimicrobial therapy, and clinical outcomes.

Whole-genome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on Mitis group streptococcal isolates that had been stored at − 80 °C after initial identification. Genomic DNA was extracted using the Quick-DNA™ Fungal/Bacterial Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Library preparation was conducted using the Illumina DNA Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and unique dual indexing was achieved using the IDT for Illumina DNA/RNA UD Index Set A. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was performed on the MiSeq platform (Illumina).

Raw read quality was assessed using FastQC (v0.12.1). Sequencing depth was estimated by dividing the total number of sequenced bases (sum of forward and reverse reads) by the assumed genome size of Mitis group streptococci (2.1 Mbp). Based on this calculation, the raw sequencing depth ranged from 31.5× to 170.3× (median, 92.6×) across the 30 isolates.

As an initial screening step, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and virulence factor (VF) genes were detected using SRST2 (v0.2.0) on the Illumina BaseSpace Sequence Hub [12]. AMR genes were identified by mapping reads to the ARG-ANNOT reference database obtained from the SRST2 GitHub repository (commit dccf72909e110e914bc7fc9eb3965a3be241aa44). VF genes were screened using the VFDB-based reference set distributed with SRST2 (generated on May 10, 2022). Default SRST2 thresholds of ≥ 90% sequence identity and ≥ 90% coverage were applied.

To further validate the SRST2-based findings and to address the limitations of reference-based mapping approaches, de novo genome assembly was subsequently performed for all isolates using SPAdes (v3.15.2). The assembled contigs were annotated with Prokka (v1.14.6), and VF screening was repeated using ABRicate (v1.0.1) with the VFDB. Assembly quality metrics, including total assembly length, number of contigs, N50, and GC content, are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

For loci showing discordant or absent detection between reference-based and assembly-based approaches, additional BLASTn analyses were performed using the assembled contigs to assess sequence similarity and allelic diversity. This step was primarily applied to the rfb locus to evaluate the extent of genetic divergence among clinical isolates.

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) was attempted using SRST2; however, most isolates yielded “not found” or novel profiles. This likely reflects the extreme genetic diversity and frequent recombination of the Mitis group, which may not be fully captured by the SRST2 database version from May 10, 2022 [12]. While a dedicated S. mitis scheme was subsequently established in 2023 [13, 14], the SRST2 pipeline has not been updated to include this version. Consequently, the high rate of non-typeable results made the MLST data uninformative for assessing genetic relatedness, and these findings were excluded from downstream analyses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). For clinical variables, categorical data were compared using Fisher’s exact test, and statistical significance was set at a two-sided p-value of < 0.05.

Given the limited sample size for genomic analyses, particularly for the VSSS subgroup (n = 1), and the large number of genomic targets examined, genomic comparisons were treated as exploratory. For these analyses, results are presented descriptively as frequencies and proportions, and no formal hypothesis testing or multiple-comparison–adjusted inferential statistics were applied.

Definitions

FN was defined as a single oral temperature of ≥ 38.3 °C or a sustained temperature of ≥ 38.0 °C for > 1 h, in combination with an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) of < 500/µL, or an ANC expected to decrease to < 500/µL within the following 48 h, in accordance with established clinical guidelines. VSSS was defined as a bloodstream infection caused by members of the Mitis group, accompanied by shock or ARDS. In this study, we aligned our definitions with international consensus criteria. Septic shock was defined, in line with criteria used in similar studies, as hypotension (systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg) despite adequate fluid resuscitation or the need for vasopressor support [15]. ARDS was defined according to the new global definition [16].

Inappropriate empiric antibiotic therapy was defined as the administration of an empiric antimicrobial agent to which the isolated strain was subsequently found to be non-susceptible, based on in vitro susceptibility testing according to CLSI M100-ED35 breakpoints.

Contamination was defined as isolation from a single blood culture in the absence of clinical signs of infection, or where a clear alternative etiology was evident. Clinical significance was independently adjudicated by two infectious disease specialists (YA and YK), and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion to reach a consensus.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Kochi Medical School (approval number: 2022139). Patient data were anonymized, and all analyses were conducted in accordance with the institutional ethical guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Study population

Ninety-five cases of bloodstream infections caused by the Mitis group of streptococci were identified at Kochi Medical School Hospital between January 2007 and December 2024. Clinical data were available for all 95 patients, and preserved isolates suitable for genomic analysis were available for 30 patients collected from 2017 onward.

FN was observed in 61 (64.2%) patients. The most frequently isolated species was S. mitis (n = 54), followed by S. oralis (n = 30), S. sanguinis (n = 9), and S. gordonii and S. cristatus (each identified in one case).

Of the 30 isolates available for genomic analysis, 12 were obtained from patients with FN and 18 from those without. These isolates were subsequently used for a descriptive comparison of AMR and VF gene profiles between these two patient groups (Fig. 1).

Patients in the FN group were significantly younger than those in the non-FN group, with a median age of 54.0 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 38.0–63.0 years) compared to 72.5 years (IQR: 66.3–79.8 years) in the non-FN group (p < 0.001), while sex distribution did not differ between the two groups. Malignancy was markedly more frequent in the FN group, with 59 of the 61 patients (97%) affected, comprising 58 cases of hematologic malignancies and one solid tumor. In contrast, malignancies were present in 12 of 34 patients (35%) in the non-FN group, including nine solid tumors and three hematologic malignancies. Malignancy was significantly more prevalent in the FN group (p < 0.001).

Prior antibiotic exposure within 90 days was also more common in patients with FN (91.8% vs. 41.2%, p < 0.001). The CCI was significantly higher in the non-FN group, with a median of 5 (IQR: 4–6), compared with 3 (IQR: 2–4) in the FN group (p < 0.001). Conversely, PBS tended to be higher in the FN group (PBS ≥ 3: 13.1% vs. 2.9%), although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.208). Serum albumin levels were significantly higher in the FN group (mean: 3.49 vs. 3.09 g/dL, p = 0.003), while C-reactive protein (CRP) levels showed no significant difference.

S. mitis was the most frequently isolated species in both groups. It was numerically more prevalent in the FN cohort (63.9%) than in the non-FN cohort (44.1%), although this individual comparison was not statistically significant (p = 0.066). As shown in Table 1, the overall distribution of all identified species did not differ significantly between the two groups (p = 0.144). S. oralis and S. sanguinis were detected in both groups.

The presumed source of infection differed notably between groups. The focus in the FN group was classified as “primary bacteremia” in the vast majority of cases (60/61, 98.4%). In contrast, the non-FN group had more diverse sources, including infective endocarditis (12/34, 35.3%) and skin and soft tissue infections (7/34, 20.6%). VSSS was observed exclusively in the FN group, affecting 7 of 61 patients (11.5%).

Fourteen-day and thirty-day mortality rates were higher in the FN group (6.6% and 11.5%, respectively) than in the non-FN group (2.9% and 5.9%, respectively); however, these differences were not statistically significant.

Empirical antibiotic treatment also differed between the groups. Cefepime was the most frequently selected in the FN group (67.2%), while patients without FN were more often treated with penicillins or third-generation cephalosporins, such as ceftriaxone (Supplementary Table S2).

In terms of antimicrobial susceptibility, isolates from patients with FN showed significantly higher resistance to levofloxacin and erythromycin than those from patients without FN (p < 0.05). Although differences in susceptibility to penicillin G and cefotaxime were not statistically significant, the observed trends suggested reduced antimicrobial susceptibility in the FN group (Table 2).

Whole-genome sequencing–based initial screening (SRST2)

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed for 30 isolates, including 12 from patients with FN and 18 from patients without FN. As an initial step, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and virulence factor (VF) genes were screened using the reference-based mapping approach implemented in SRST2.

Distribution of AMR determinants

With respect to AMR determinants, genes associated with resistance to macrolides (e.g., ermB, msrD), β-lactams (e.g., pbp1b), and tetracyclines (e.g., tetM) were variably detected across isolates. Overall review of the screening results did not suggest consistent group-level patterns in AMR gene distribution between FN and non-FN isolates. Accordingly, these findings are presented descriptively. A detailed overview of the prevalence of individual AMR genes identified by SRST2 is provided in Table 5.

Distribution of virulence factor genes

For VF genes, a total of 31 loci included in the VFDB-based reference set were screened. Several genes, such as eno and plr/gapA, were detected in nearly all isolates, suggesting that these loci are broadly conserved within this dataset. In contrast, other VF genes showed heterogeneous detection patterns across isolates. For example, pavA and slrA were detected in a subset of isolates from both groups, whereas rfb-related genes were detected only in a subset of non-FN isolates in this initial screening.

The overall distribution of VF genes identified by SRST2 is summarized in Table 5, and the corresponding heatmap is shown in Fig. 2. These heterogeneous detection patterns prompted further investigation using assembly-based approaches to evaluate whether they reflected true biological variation or methodological constraints.

Assembly-based reanalysis and validation

Rationale for assembly-based reanalysis

To determine whether the heterogeneous gene detection patterns observed in the initial SRST2-based screening reflected true biological variation or methodological constraints, we performed additional assembly-based analyses. De novo assemblies were generated for all 30 isolates, followed by annotation using Prokka and virulence factor screening using ABRicate with the VFDB (Supplementary Table S3). This reanalysis aimed to reassess genes that appeared to show group-level differences in the initial mapping-based screening, including pavA, slrA, and rfb-related loci.

Reassessment of virulence gene detection

Compared with the mapping-based SRST2 approach, the assembly-based workflow improved the detection of several virulence-associated loci. However, rfb-related genes were still not consistently identified by the VFDB-based ABRicate pipeline, even in isolates in which these loci were suspected to be present.

Because both SRST2 and ABRicate rely on reference-based matching against curated databases, this observation raised the possibility that rfb-related loci in our clinical isolates might be highly divergent from sequences represented in VFDB or not included in the database, rather than being truly absent.

Prokka-based annotation and BLAST validation

To further investigate this discrepancy, we focused on rfbA (also annotated as rmlA) as a representative locus within the rfb-associated pathway, because it showed the clearest divergence pattern in the initial SRST2-based screening.

Using Prokka-based annotation, rfbA-related sequences were identified in 22 of 30 isolates (73.3%), suggesting that their apparent absence in the initial SRST2 screening was unlikely to reflect true gene loss.

To clarify the extent of sequence divergence, we performed manual BLAST-based pairwise sequence comparisons. These analyses revealed substantial allelic diversity among rfbA-related sequences, with identities ranging from 78 to 95% between clinical isolates. In contrast, similarity to the reference rfbA sequence from SVGS_061 [17] consistently showed extremely low query coverage (1–3%), despite high local identity.

Together, these results indicate that the failure of reference-based pipelines to detect rfbA in many isolates was likely driven by limitations of available reference databases and extreme sequence divergence, rather than by true gene absence.

Genomic profile of the VSSS isolate

The isolate derived from the patient with VSSS (SRR35387687) exhibited a distinct genomic profile in the assembly-based analysis. While canonical adhesion-associated genes such as pavA (99.64%), psaA (100.00%), and sipA (100.00%) were detected, this isolate also harbored lytA (99.27%) and lytC (98.01%), which were not consistently identified across other isolates in this cohort.

Conversely, the VSSS isolate lacked srtG1 and srtG2, which were frequently detected in other strains. Notably, even after assembly-based refinement and sequence-level validation, this isolate remained negative for the common rfbA variants observed among other clinical isolates.

Data integrity and assembly quality

To exclude the possibility that these findings were driven by technical artifacts, sequencing quality and assembly metrics were evaluated. FastQC confirmed consistently high base-call quality, with Phred scores exceeding 30 for the majority of reads. Assemblies also showed high contiguity (mean N50: approximately 350 kb), indicating that gene detection results were unlikely to be attributable to insufficient sequencing depth or excessive fragmentation.

Discussion

This study investigated the clinical and microbiological characteristics of streptococcal bacteremia in patients with FN, with a specific focus on comparative VF gene profiling of FN and non-FN isolates. Our findings highlight distinct clinical patterns associated with neutropenic status and underscore the complexity of VSSS, which appears to arise from multifactorial host–pathogen interactions rather than from a single microbial determinant.

Consistent with previous reports, patients in the FN cohort were younger, had a high prevalence of hematologic malignancies, and predominantly presented with primary bacteremia. These features align with a large 20-year epidemiological study from Australia, which demonstrated that viridans group streptococci are a more frequent cause of bacteremia in neutropenic patients with hematologic malignancies than in non-neutropenic populations (4.4% vs. 1.2%) [18]. The high rate of primary bacteremia in the FN group is likely attributable to chemotherapy-induced oral mucositis, which damages the mucosal barrier and facilitates the translocation of oral commensals into the bloodstream, a well-documented mechanism in this population [4]. In contrast, the non-FN cohort was older, had a higher burden of comorbidities, as reflected by the CCI, and presented with more varied infectious foci. This heterogeneity in the non-FN population is consistent with findings from other studies that have struggled to identify specific risk factors for VGS bacteremia in patients without neutropenia, suggesting that underlying comorbidities, rather than a single unifying factor, such as neutropenia, may predispose these individuals to infection [19].

Another critical finding of the present study was the poor prognosis of VSSS, even in patients who received empirically appropriate antibiotics. This severe outcome, observed despite appropriate antimicrobial coverage, warrants a closer examination of the syndrome’s complex pathophysiology. A direct comparison of VSSS incidence is complicated by the significant heterogeneity in diagnostic criteria across studies, which has resulted in a wide range of reported rates (7% to 39%) [4, 7, 20]. The 11.5% incidence observed in our FN cohort should be interpreted within this context of definitional variability. The mortality rate in our VSSS subgroup was 42.9% over 14 days. However, this figure is based on a very small number of cases (n = 7) and must be interpreted with caution. A direct comparison to the 2%–21% mortality range cited in literature reviews is challenging, given our small sample size and the aforementioned definitional inconsistencies [7, 15]. Furthermore, the true lethality of the syndrome may be underestimated in the literature due to reporting practices. For example, a recent study noted that while an overall 30-day mortality of 20% was reported for VGS bacteremia, shock and ARDS were documented as separate complication rates of 25% and 17%, respectively [15]. This practice of not specifying the mortality rate within the severe subgroup can obscure the actual fatality rate of VSSS. This severity, observed despite appropriate antimicrobial coverage, suggests that the drivers of mortality are more complex than those of the pathogen resistance profile. In fact, it has long been known that clinical outcome is not dictated by cephalosporin susceptibility, and recent studies have reaffirmed this, showing no significant correlation between prognosis and non-susceptibility to penicillin or ceftriaxone [15, 21, 22].

To explore possible microbial contributors to these clinical differences, we performed comparative genomic analyses. Initial SRST2-based screening suggested differential detection of certain VF genes, including pavA, slrA, and rfb-related loci, between FN and non-FN isolates. However, these findings must be interpreted cautiously. Large-scale genomic surveys have suggested that some of these genes, including pavA and slrA, are widely distributed across S. mitis strains from both commensal and invasive contexts [10, 23]. This apparent discrepancy prompted us to perform assembly-based re-analysis.

A notable feature of the SRST2-based analysis was the uniform non-detection of rfb-related genes, including rfbA, rfbB, and rfbD, in FN isolates. At face value, this finding would appear paradoxical, because the dTDP-L-rhamnose synthesis pathway encoded by the rfb operon plays a critical role in cell wall biogenesis in many streptococci, making true gene loss biologically implausible [24]. Subsequent assembly-based reanalysis, focusing on rfbA (also annotated as rmlA) as a representative locus, clarified that rfbA-related sequences were present in most isolates but exhibited substantial allelic divergence. This observation is consistent with the known genetic plasticity of S. mitis, particularly in surface polysaccharide-associated loci, which are subject to strong selective pressures from the host immune system [25, 26]. Such diversity can impede detection by reference-based tools, including SRST2, resulting in apparent “false-negative” patterns [27]. Therefore, the observed non-detection of rfb-related genes in the initial screening is more plausibly attributable to methodological constraints combined with extreme sequence divergence, rather than to true gene absence.

While these findings provide insight into the genomic diversity of Mitis group streptococci, they do not imply that specific bacterial genes alone determine the development of VSSS. VSSS is increasingly understood as a host-driven syndrome, characterized by an exaggerated proinflammatory cytokine response—including the overproduction of TNF-α and IL-8—alongside endothelial activation and capillary leak, often mediated by ICAM-1 up-regulation. Previous experimental studies have demonstrated that cytokine-inducing capacity among viridans streptococci is not species-specific and varies substantially between strains [28]. These findings suggest that the association between S. mitis and VSSS may be driven more by host vulnerability and epidemiologic factors than by intrinsic microbial virulence. Our genomic data are consistent with this interpretation, as we did not identify any species- or group-specific virulence determinants that clearly segregated with clinical severity.

Nevertheless, it remains plausible that progression to the most severe phenotypes, such as VSSS, may require the presence of additional virulence-associated features. Prior genomic analyses of S. mitis isolates from VSSS patients have identified genes encoding pneumococcal toxins, including pneumolysin (ply) and autolysin lytA [17]. In the present study, the single VSSS isolate also harbored several known virulence-associated genes, including lytA and lytC, although these were not unique to this isolate. Because only one VSSS isolate was available for sequencing, we were unable to perform a meaningful comparative analysis. Accordingly, these observations should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, this was a retrospective, single-center study, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other clinical settings. Second, whole-genome sequencing was restricted to isolates preserved since 2017, resulting in a reduced genomic cohort (n = 30) and introducing the possibility of selection bias. In particular, only a single isolate from a patient with VSSS was available for sequencing, which precluded meaningful comparative analyses aimed at identifying VSSS-specific genomic features.

Third, our initial genomic screening relied on reference-based approaches, including SRST2 and VFDB-based ABRicate. These tools depend on curated databases and predefined similarity thresholds, which may fail to detect highly divergent alleles. Given the well-documented genetic plasticity of the Mitis group, especially in loci related to cell surface structures and host interactions, false-negative results due to extreme sequence divergence cannot be excluded. Although we attempted to mitigate this limitation through assembly-based reanalysis and manual sequence validation, our findings should therefore not be interpreted as definitive evidence of true gene absence.

Furthermore, this assembly-based reanalysis identified substantial allelic divergence in the rfb locus among our isolates, but the factors associated with this variation remain unclear. Because this study was retrospective and included a limited number of isolates, we could not determine whether the observed divergence was related to host conditions in febrile neutropenia, prior antimicrobial exposure including fluoroquinolone prophylaxis, or both, as these factors often overlapped in our cohort.

Fourth, our genomic analyses were largely limited to presence/absence-based comparisons and did not systematically assess allelic variation, gene expression, or functional activity. As such, subtle but biologically relevant differences between strains may not have been captured. Moreover, because of the small sample size and the large number of genomic targets examined, these analyses were exploratory in nature.

Finally, direct comparison of our clinical outcomes with those reported in the literature is complicated by substantial heterogeneity in the diagnostic definitions of VSSS. As previously reported, estimated mortality rates may vary considerably depending on whether narrowly or broadly defined criteria are applied [6]. Therefore, the observed mortality rates in our cohort should be interpreted with caution.

Together, these limitations highlight the need for larger, multicenter, prospective studies integrating high-resolution genomic analyses with detailed host-response profiling to more fully elucidate the complex pathogenesis of VSSS.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study describes the clinical features of Mitis group streptococcal bacteremia in patients with febrile neutropenia and explores the genomic characteristics of a subset of available isolates. The results suggest differences in clinical presentation between FN and non-FN patients, while genomic analysis showed variation among isolates, particularly in genes related to surface-associated structures.

We did not identify a clear genomic feature specific to VSSS. Given the limited number of VSSS isolates available for genomic analysis, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Nevertheless, the results support the possibility that VSSS may not be fully explained by a single microbial factor and may instead reflect interactions between bacterial factors and host immune conditions.

Future studies will require standardized diagnostic criteria for VSSS and larger multicenter cohorts to allow consistent case identification and more reliable comparative analyses. Studies that examine bacterial genomic data together with host immune responses may provide a better understanding of VSSS.
