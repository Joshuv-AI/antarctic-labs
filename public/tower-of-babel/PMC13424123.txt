A high-resolution (500 m) dataset for mapping key agronomic growth stages of maize in Northeast China
Authors: Huizhu Wu, Mingchang Wang, Bingxue Zhu, Cuiping Li, Kaishan Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate monitoring of agronomic phenology is essential for yield estimation and food security assessment. However, currently available maize phenology datasets usually represent only a limited number of growth stages, restricting their application in process-based crop modeling and stage-specific agricultural management. Here, we present a high-resolution maize phenology dataset for Northeast China spanning 2001–2024 at 500-m spatial resolution and daily temporal resolution. By coupling MODIS spectral information with meteorological drivers in an energy-driven XGBoost framework, we retrieved eight key agronomic stages: Emergence, Three-leaf, Seven-leaf, Jointing, Flowering, Silking, Milking, and Maturity. Validation against observations from 91 agrometeorological stations during 2009–2024 demonstrates robust performance, with an overall RMSE of less than 5 days and R² values greater than 0.63 across all stages. Beyond overall accuracy, the dataset shows strong spatial consistency and temporal stability, preserves coherent regional phenological gradients, and captures interannual variations over the 24-year period. This long-term, multi-stage dataset provides a valuable benchmark for crop model calibration, climate change impact assessment, and the development of adaptive agricultural strategies in one of the world’s major maize-producing regions.

Background & Summary

Maize is one of the world’s most important staple, feed, and bioenergy crops, and its production patterns and stability directly affect global food and agricultural security1. Northeast China is a major maize-producing region and contributes a substantial share of China’s annual maize production. Reliable information on maize phenological stages, such as Emergence, Jointing, Silking, and Maturity, is therefore essential for precision fertilization, irrigation management, pest and disease control, yield forecasting, and assessment of climate change impacts2,3. Agronomic and remote sensing studies have consistently shown that accurate phenological identification improves crop monitoring and supports agricultural management decisions1.

Traditionally, crop phenology has been determined through ground observations, field surveys, and meteorological records4,5. However, these approaches are limited by sparse spatial coverage, temporal discontinuity, and high labor and cost requirements, making them unsuitable for large-scale monitoring. Advances in satellite remote sensing have enabled access to extensive time-series observations, providing an important basis for phenological research over large regions6. Vegetation indices (VIs) derived from satellite observations have been widely used to track vegetation growth dynamics1,7. Phenological transitions can be identified by analyzing temporal features and threshold values of VI time series2,8–10, or by aligning VI trajectories with ground observations to infer crop-specific phenological stages11–15. In addition, integrating crop growth models with remote sensing observations enables large-scale phenological monitoring and can improve phenological date estimation compared with purely remote sensing–based approaches13,16,17. Nevertheless, traditional methods often struggle to represent the complex nonlinear relationships between crop physiological development and fluctuating environmental drivers1. Recently, machine learning (ML) algorithms have shown strong potential for phenology retrieval by integrating multi-source data and capturing nonlinear patterns18. Gradient boosting frameworks, particularly XGBoost19, have been widely used in agricultural applications because they can handle correlated predictors and reduce overfitting through regularization18.

Advances in data acquisition and retrieval methods have facilitated the development of several large-scale and long-term phenology datasets in recent years. For example, Friedl, et al.20 derived global land surface phenology metrics from MODIS EVI2 time-series data using multiple threshold definitions, including the onset, midpoint, and end of greening, peak greenness, senescence onset, midpoint of yellowing, and dormancy. Similarly, Zhang21 used VIIRS observations to generate vegetation phenology metrics, including six transition dates: start of greening, onset of maximum greenness, start of greenness decline, onset of minimum greenness, mid-season date, and senescence. Bornez, et al.22 systematically compared vegetation variables and phenology retrieval methods using SPOT-VEGETATION and PROBA-V time-series data from the Copernicus Global Land Service (CGLS) for 1999–2017, showing that satellite-derived start-of-season (SOS) and end-of-season (EOS) indicators were reliable. The Copernicus Land Monitoring Service (CLMS) has also released the High-Resolution Vegetation Phenology and Productivity (HR-VPP) product, which provides detailed land surface phenology metrics across Europe at 10-m spatial resolution. In addition, several studies have produced global long-term land surface phenology records23,24.

Despite their broad spatial coverage, these global products mainly focus on ecological indicators of land surface greenness, such as SOS and EOS, rather than specific agronomic growth stages. This limits their direct application in crop management and process-based crop modeling, where stage-specific information is required. To connect satellite-derived phenological metrics with crop developmental stages, multi-scale validation is also needed. Ground-based PhenoCam networks, for example, provide high-frequency phenological observations that can help bridge the scale gap between site-level station records and satellite pixels, thereby supporting the validation of satellite-derived phenology products25.

Beyond global-scale initiatives, multiple country- and region-specific phenology datasets have emerged in recent years. For example, Luo, et al.26 developed 1-km phenology datasets for maize, rice, and wheat across China using leaf area index (LAI) time series. Niu, et al.27 generated a national maize phenology product for China at 30-m spatial resolution for 1985–2020 by leveraging Landsat imagery through the Google Earth Engine platform. Their dataset includes the V3 stage, defined as the third fully expanded leaf, and Maturity, defined as the date when kernel dry weight first reaches its maximum. Li, et al.28 also developed a Landsat-based vegetation phenology dataset for the United States, providing indicators such as SOS and EOS.

Despite these advances, several gaps remain in existing large-scale and long-term remote sensing phenology datasets. First, many products focus on seasonal transition metrics such as SOS and EOS, which do not correspond directly to detailed physiological stages of crop development, such as Jointing, Flowering, Silking, and grain filling. This limits their agronomic applicability. Second, remote sensing products require extensive ground-based data for validation, but multi-year and multi-region ground observations are often sparse or inconsistent, introducing uncertainty into product evaluation. Third, models trained in specific regions or periods may have limited transferability across years and geographic zones, which can hinder accurate phenological retrieval.

Although high-resolution crop-specific products, such as the product developed by27, have advanced maize phenology mapping, they usually characterize only a limited number of stages. Comprehensive quantification of the crop lifecycle from vegetative to reproductive stages is not simply an expansion in the number of variables, but is necessary for process-based crop modeling and stage-specific agricultural analysis. By providing a continuous eight-stage lifecycle sequence from Emergence to Maturity, our dataset enables the calculation of specific growth-phase durations, such as the grain-filling period, which are important for yield formation and climate sensitivity analysis but are not available from existing limited-stage products. In addition, our retrieval framework uses an energy-domain formulation based on ΔAARPD to support temporally consistent phenology reconstruction across the 24-year study period. For Northeast China, a dataset that combines long-term temporal continuity from 2001 to 2024 with a multi-stage physiological growth sequence has remained unavailable.

To address this gap, we generated a comprehensive maize phenology dataset for Northeast China at 500-m spatial resolution by integrating daily reflectance and meteorological data with observations from 91 agrometeorological stations. Building on our recently developed energy-driven phenology retrieval framework29, we operationalized and extended this approach for long-term regional dataset production from 2001 to 2024, enabling the reconstruction of eight key phenological stages: Emergence, Three-leaf, Seven-leaf, Jointing, Flowering, Silking, Milking, and Maturity. Rather than introducing a completely new algorithm, the main contribution of this study is the generation, validation, and public release of the first long-term, fine-resolution, multi-stage maize phenology dataset for Northeast China. Detailed metadata are provided to facilitate reuse. This open-access dataset offers a consistent benchmark for crop model calibration, climate change impact assessment, and regional food security analysis.

Methods

Study area

The study area covers the broadly defined black soil region of Northeast China (Fig. 1a), including Heilongjiang, Jilin, and Liaoning Provinces, as well as the eastern part of Inner Mongolia (DSM), specifically Hulunbuir, Tongliao, Chifeng, and Hinggan League. The region has a temperate continental monsoon climate, with long, cold winters and warm, humid summers. Precipitation is concentrated from June to August and gradually decreases from southeast to northwest. Accumulated temperature and the length of the frost-free period increase from north to south, and abundant solar radiation provides favorable conditions for maize production.Fig. 1Study area, agrometeorological station distribution, and maize phenological periods. Maize cultivation is mainly distributed across several major plains, including the Songnen Plain in western Heilongjiang and central–northern Jilin, the Sanjiang Plain in northeastern Heilongjiang, the Liaohe Plain in central and southern Liaoning, and the agro-pastoral transition zone of eastern Inner Mongolia, including valleys in Hulunbuir and the western Liaohe River Basin. The green areas in Fig. 1a represent maize cultivation zones derived from the national crop distribution map released by Peng, et al.30. To ensure spatial consistency with the reflectance data, the original 30-m maize distribution map was aggregated to 500-m resolution using an area-density aggregation method. A 500-m pixel was retained in the maize mask only when maize coverage within the pixel exceeded 75%. This threshold was applied to reduce spectral contamination from mixed pixels and improve the reliability of phenology retrieval in fragmented agricultural landscapes.

Ground-based phenological data were obtained from 91 agrometeorological stations distributed across the major maize-producing zones, as indicated by red stars in Fig. 1a. Each station recorded the DOY of maize growth stages from sowing to Maturity during 2009–2024. Panels b–e in Fig. 1 illustrate the temporal duration and regional variation of maize phenological stages, with observed DOY ranges of 130–282 in Heilongjiang, 129–286 in Jilin, 114–288 in Liaoning, and 123–286 in eastern Inner Mongolia.

Reflectance data

In this study, the date when GRVI equals zero was selected as the temporal baseline for extracting multi-source remote sensing features. GRVI is sensitive to the transition between soil and vegetation signals. During the early growth stage of maize, strong soil background effects in the visible bands often result in negative GRVI values, which gradually approach zero as canopy cover increases. Therefore, the DOY when GRVI = 0 provides a standardized temporal anchor across different locations and years. Reflectance data were obtained from the MODIS surface reflectance product MOD09A131. Linear interpolation was applied to generate continuous time series for four primary spectral bands: red (RED, 620–750 nm), near-infrared (NIR, 750–1400 nm), green (GREEN, 495–570 nm), and shortwave infrared (SWIR, 1400–3000 nm).

To capture canopy structure and chlorophyll-related information at this baseline, we calculated five widely used vegetation indices (VIs): Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), Wide Dynamic Range Vegetation Index (WDRVI), Green Chlorophyll Vegetation Index (GCVI), Green Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (GNDVI), and Optimized Soil-Adjusted Vegetation Index (OSAVI). The DOY when GRVI = 0 was used as the temporal input feature, while the four spectral bands and five VIs extracted on that date served as optical input features for the phenology retrieval model.

Meteorological data

In this study, we selected three variables: daily mean 2-m air temperature, total daily precipitation, and total downward surface solar radiation. To characterize the thermal and radiative conditions associated with maize development, these daily variables were converted into cumulative metrics. Model dataset construction

The final model dataset was constructed by systematically integrating reflectance, meteorological, and ground-observed phenological data through site-year matching. First, a maize cultivation mask was generated by spatially aggregating the 30 m maize distribution map to the 500 m MODIS resolution using an area-weighted aggregation method. To reduce mixed-pixel effects and ensure radiometric purity, a stringent maize fraction threshold of 75% was applied, and only pixels exceeding this threshold were retained.

For these maize-dominant pixels, we extracted three groups of input features: the temporal feature, defined as the DOY when GRVI reached zero; the optical features, represented by the reflectance values on that date; and the meteorological features, including AGDD, APrec, and AARPD, extracted for the same date. These features were then paired with ground-observed phenological dates by matching the geographic coordinates and observation years of the 91 agrometeorological stations with the corresponding pixel-level records. This site-year alignment ensured a precise correspondence between the environmental drivers used as model inputs and the observed phenological dates used as target outputs. Phenology retrieval framework based on energy increment

We developed a biophysically based, energy-driven framework to identify eight key agronomic growth stages of maize in Northeast China (Fig. 2). The core rationale of this framework is to use a physiological baseline to reduce systematic biases associated with variations in planting dates and early-season thermal accumulation. First, we determined the DOY when GRVI first reached zero DOYGRVI=0 through linear interpolation and used this date as a physiological temporal anchor.Fig. 2Biophysically based energy-driven framework for maize phenology retrieval.

Biophysically based energy-driven framework for maize phenology retrieval.

This threshold represents the transition from soil-dominated to vegetation-dominated reflectance during the early growth stage of maize. As maize enters this phase, GRVI changes from negative to positive, indicating the onset of active vegetative growth and providing a standardized temporal reference. Based on this anchor, we extracted a 13-dimensional feature vector as the model input, including DOYGRVI=0, four-band reflectance, five vegetation indices, and three cumulative meteorological variables evaluated at the baseline date: AGDDGRVI=0, APrecGRVI=0, and AARPDGRVI=0.

To visually illustrate this mechanism, Fig. 3 shows the temporal evolution of GRVI and NDVI across the four sub-regions. The GRVI = 0 transition generally occurs before the Jointing stage, indicating that it provides a relatively stable temporal reference for subsequent phenology retrieval under different geographical conditions.Fig. 3Provincial mean temporal profiles of GRVI and NDVI and observed maize phenological stages in 2024. Here, AARPDGRVI=0 represents the cumulative radiative condition at the physiological baseline and is used as an input feature, whereas ΔAARPD represents the additional cumulative radiative energy required to reach each target phenological stage and is used as the prediction target. This formulation links the model target more directly to crop developmental progression than absolute calendar dates. Meteorological features were calculated from the first date on which daily mean temperature exceeded 10°C, ensuring consistency in the environmental forcing background. Based on the nonlinear relationships between spectral signals, meteorological conditions, and energy accumulation, the models were trained to estimate the energy increments corresponding to each phenological stage.

To evaluate the adopted target formulation, we additionally constructed a direct DOY prediction benchmark under the same GRVI-based temporal anchor, predictor set, and training/testing split, with the target variable changed from ΔAARPD to phenological DOY.

Finally, phenological dates were retrieved by mapping the model predictions from the energy domain back to the temporal domain. After the trained model predicted ΔAARPD for each pixel, this value was added to the cumulative energy at the baseline date to obtain the absolute AARPD threshold. The final DOY was then determined by linear interpolation against the annual AARPD time series. This energy-to-time mapping mechanism constrains the retrieved dates according to the actual radiative and thermal trajectories of each growing season.

To optimize model hyperparameters and improve prediction accuracy, we employed a randomized search strategy combined with cross-validation (CV). During hyperparameter tuning, 10-fold cross-validation was performed using the root mean square error (RMSE) as the evaluation metric. The randomized search was repeated 200 times, with different parameter combinations randomly sampled, trained, and evaluated in each iteration. The parameter configuration that produced the lowest RMSE was selected as the optimal hyperparameter set. Evaluation indicators

Model performance was evaluated using in situ phenological observations recorded at agrometeorological stations. Two statistical indicators were used to assess the accuracy of the retrieved DOY for each phenological stage: the coefficient of determination (R2) and the root mean square error (RMSE).

Data Record

The maize phenology dataset for Northeast China generated in this study is publicly available from the Figshare repository34. The dataset covers the period from 2001 to 2024 and is provided in GeoTIFF format at a 500 m spatial resolution in the MODIS sinusoidal projection. It is organized into eight folders corresponding to the eight maize phenological stages: Emergence, Three-leaf, Seven-leaf, Jointing, Flowering, Silking, Milking, and Maturity.

Each folder contains 24 annual GeoTIFF files, named by year, such as 2001.tif and 2002.tif. Each file is a single-band raster in which pixel values represent the retrieved day of year (DOY) for the corresponding phenological stage. This dataset provides a long-term, stage-resolved spatiotemporal record for assessing maize development patterns and phenological variability across Northeast China.

Technical Validation

Accuracy assessment

Model performance on training data

Figure 4 presents the comparison between observed and predicted ΔAARPD values across the eight maize phenological stages. Overall, the models showed strong positive agreement between observed and predicted energy increments for most stages.Fig. 4Observed versus predicted ΔAARPD values across the eight maize phenological stages.

Observed versus predicted ΔAARPD values across the eight maize phenological stages.

For the early phenological stages, including Emergence, Three-leaf, Seven-leaf, and Jointing, the models achieved relatively high consistency. The best performance was observed for Emergence, with an R2 of 0.85, followed by Seven-leaf, with an R2 of 0.81. For the middle growth stages, namely Flowering and Silking, model performance was slightly lower, with R2 values of 0.74 for both stages.

Among the late phenological stages, Milking showed the weakest performance, with an R2 of 0.52 and a relatively large RMSE of 1.75 × 103 W/m². This indicates greater uncertainty in estimating the energy increment for this stage. Maturity achieved an R2 of 0.66, although minor overestimation was observed in the low-value range.

This evaluation design ensured that the reported accuracy metrics reflected model reliability when applied to unseen data.

Model robustness and transferability

Evaluation of model temporal transferability via back-testing

To address the potential risk of applying the model to earlier periods with possible differences in maize cultivars and management conditions, we conducted an independent temporal cross-validation experiment. Specifically, the XGBoost models were trained using data from the most recent nine years, 2016–2024, and were tested on the earlier period of 2009–2015, which served as an independent historical evaluation dataset.

As shown in Fig. 10, the back-testing results indicate generally stable performance across the eight maize phenological stages. For the vegetative growth stages (Fig. 10a), the models showed relatively high predictive accuracy. Emergence and Jointing exhibited the best performance, with R2 values of 0.84 and 0.76, respectively, and RMSE values below 3 days. Although Three-leaf and Seven-leaf showed slightly greater dispersion, with R2 values of 0.69 and 0.65, respectively, most predictions remained close to the 1:1 line.Fig. 10Temporal cross-validation of retrieved and observed DOY values for maize phenological stages. (a) Vegetative stages. (b) Reproductive stages.

Temporal cross-validation of retrieved and observed DOY values for maize phenological stages. (a) Vegetative stages. (b) Reproductive stages.

For the reproductive stages (Fig. 10b), the models maintained acceptable performance, although uncertainty increased for the late-season stages. Silking showed a clear correlation between retrieved and observed DOY values, with an R2 of 0.68 and an RMSE of 3.21 days. Milking and Maturity exhibited higher errors, with RMSE values of 5.01 and 4.32 days, respectively, but a large proportion of the data points still fell within the ±7-day error band.

These results suggest that the trained models retained reasonable temporal transferability when applied to an earlier independent period. This supports the use of the framework for long-term maize phenology reconstruction, while also highlighting the need to account for potential uncertainties related to cultivar changes, management differences, and interannual environmental variability.

Model stability under interannual climatic variability

To further investigate the reliability of the reconstructed dataset, we analyzed retrieval errors under different thermal backgrounds. Using long-term meteorological records from 2001 to 2024 as the reference baseline, we calculated annual anomalies in accumulated growing degree days (AGDD) to characterize interannual thermal variability across the study area. This analysis was used to assess whether retrieval accuracy remained stable under varying environmental conditions.

The interannual variation in retrieval errors was analyzed together with AGDD anomalies from 2009 to 2024 (Fig. 11a). Annual RMSE values ranged from 2.37 to 6.10 days, indicating generally stable retrieval accuracy over the 16-year evaluation period. In 2009, Northeast China experienced a strong negative AGDD anomaly of −619.30 °C d, representing a relatively cold thermal background. Nevertheless, the corresponding RMSE was 4.55 days, suggesting that retrieval accuracy did not deteriorate substantially in that year. This result indicates that the energy-driven framework can partly reduce the influence of interannual thermal variability by using a physiological temporal anchor and energy-increment formulation rather than relying directly on fixed calendar dates.Fig. 11Interannual variation in phenological retrieval errors under different thermal conditions. (a) Annual RMSE and AGDD anomalies from 2009 to 2024. (b) RMSE distributions under Cold, Normal, and Warm thermal regimes.

To provide an additional statistical comparison, we categorized the evaluation years into three thermal regimes: Cold, Normal, and Warm years, based on a threshold of ±0.5 standard deviations of the AGDD anomalies. Limitations and prospects

Despite the generally reliable performance of the developed maize phenology dataset, several sources of uncertainty should be acknowledged. First, the spatial scale mismatch caused by aggregating 30 m maize masks to the 500 m MODIS resolution may introduce mixed-pixel effects. To reduce this influence, a stringent maize purity threshold of 75% was applied, so that only maize-dominant pixels were retained. The evaluation using observations from 91 agrometeorological stations (Fig. 5) showed an average RMSE of 4.33 days, suggesting that the dataset maintained acceptable accuracy across heterogeneous agricultural landscapes. Nevertheless, residual sub-pixel heterogeneity may still contribute to retrieval uncertainty, particularly in fragmented cultivation areas.

Second, resampling the 0.1° ERA5-Land meteorological data to 500 m may overlook local microclimatic variability. The interannual stability analysis (Fig. 11) showed that retrieval errors remained within a moderate range under different thermal backgrounds. For example, in 2009, when the AGDD anomaly reached −619.30 °C d, the retrieval RMSE was 4.55 days. This result suggests that the ΔAARPD-based framework can partly reduce the influence of interannual thermal variability through its energy-increment formulation. However, uncertainties related to meteorological resampling and local environmental heterogeneity cannot be fully eliminated.

Third, extrapolating the model to the period 2001–2008, when station records were limited, may introduce additional uncertainty. The multi-year error analysis for 2009–2024 (Fig. 11a) showed no evident degradation in retrieval accuracy over the 16-year validation period, with annual RMSE values ranging from approximately 2.4 to 6.1 days. This temporal consistency supports the use of the framework for earlier-year reconstruction. However, the reliability of the 2001–2008 estimates should still be interpreted with caution, because cultivar changes, management practices, and environmental conditions may have differed from those represented in the training and validation records.

Regarding explanatory variables, the current model prioritizes temperature, precipitation, and solar radiation because they represent major biophysical drivers of maize development in Northeast China. Other factors, such as wind speed, soil moisture, management practices, and explicit extreme climate indices, were not included in the current framework to maintain model parsimony and ensure data availability over the full study period. This omission may limit the model’s ability to capture anomalous phenological responses under extreme events, such as prolonged heat stress or excessive precipitation. Future versions of the framework could incorporate these additional variables to better characterize crop responses to climate variability and long-term environmental change.

Finally, the multi-step transformation from GRVI = 0 to ΔAARPD prediction and then to final DOY retrieval may introduce propagated uncertainty. Future work should explore direct end-to-end retrieval models and integrate higher-resolution satellite observations, such as Sentinel-2 and Landsat, to reduce intermediate uncertainty. Incorporating variety-specific parameters and soil moisture information may also improve model generalization in fragmented cultivation zones.
