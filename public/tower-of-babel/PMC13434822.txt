Extending the tendency involvement experience model in street food experience
Authors: Yusuf Bayraktar, Zühal Aksakallı Bayraktar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Street food constitutes a culturally embedded and economically significant component of tourism destinations, yet the process linking consumption intention to experiential outcomes remains theoretically underexplored. This study extends the Tendency–Involvement–Experience (TIE) model by integrating it with the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) to explain tourists’ street food experiences. Data were collected from 310 tourists in Türkiye using a structured questionnaire and analyzed through Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM). Attitudes and subjective norms significantly and positively influenced intention to consume street food, whereas perceived behavioral control did not exert a significant effect. Intention significantly predicted both food involvement and food experience. Moreover, food involvement mediated the relationship between intention and experience, confirming the sequential mechanism proposed in the extended model. The findings demonstrate that experiential outcomes in street food consumption are primarily driven by attitudinal and normative evaluations rather than control perceptions. By empirically validating the indirect pathway from intention to experience through involvement, the study proposes an Extended Tendency–Involvement–Experience Model and refines the application of TPB in experiential consumption contexts.

Introduction

Street food represents an important experiential component of tourism destinations. These foods are typically derived from local culinary heritage and are considered an important component of regional cuisine1. Tourists seek diverse experiences during their travels, and culinary culture serves as a significant attraction for them in a destination. Consuming local food at a destination is akin to experiencing cultural traditions for tourists2. The sector relies on local resources and contributes directly to regional economies. Moreover, street food creates new employment opportunities for the local population. Street food appeals to travelers seeking authentic and unique experiences. It also enhances culinary diversity at destinations. Street food environments also facilitate social interaction among travelers3.

The Tendency–Involvement–Experience (TIE) model has rarely been applied to explain tourist behavior. Xu, Zhang4, who examined this model in the context of cultural heritage, focused on the relationship between tourists’ cultural tendencies and experiences. In their study, they highlighted the significant role of tourist involvement, and thus, the Tendency-Involvement-Experience Model was introduced into the literature. This study specifically addressed tourists’ travels to cultural heritage sites. The model introduced by Xu, Zhang4 forms the foundation of this research. However, the model has not been examined in the context of gastronomic experience. This study applies the TIE framework to street food consumption and operationalizes it using intention, food involvement, and food experience.

While the Tendency–Involvement–Experience framework provides a sequential perspective on how motivational predispositions evolve into experiential outcomes, it does not explicitly incorporate a well established intention formation theory4. Conversely, the Theory of Planned Behavior explains how intention is formed but does not sufficiently address how intention translates into experiential value5. Therefore, integrating these two frameworks offers a theoretically coherent structure that connects motivational antecedents with experiential consequences in street food consumption contexts.

Despite the Tendency–Involvement–Experience (TIE) model’s promise for explaining how tendency and involvement shape tourism experiences, it has been primarily examined in heritage settings and remains underexplored in gastronomic and street food contexts4. Street food studies frequently rely on the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) to predict intention, yet seldom specify the process through which TPB based intention translates into involvement and experiential value. Addressing these gaps, we extend TIE by operationalizing tendency as TPB based street food consumption intention and by testing a sequential mechanism linking intention to food involvement and food experience (CI→FI→FE). This integrative perspective connects intention formation to experiential outcomes and complements recent TPB evidence in street food settings6 and involvement experience research7.

Although the street food sector has grown steadily and constitutes a culturally and economically significant component of tourism destinations, it has not received proportionate academic attention relative to its scale and complexity8. Existing research has primarily examined street food consumption within the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) framework, focusing predominantly on intention formation rather than on the experiential processes that follow intention. At the same time, while the Tendency–Involvement–Experience (TIE) model offers a sequential logic for understanding tourism experiences, its application to gastronomic and street food contexts remains limited. Consequently, significant theoretical gaps persist regarding how consumption intention translates into involvement and experiential outcomes in street food settings. Addressing this limitation requires an integrative perspective that connects motivational antecedents to experiential consequences. Building on these theoretical and empirical gaps, the present study integrates TPB with the TIE framework and proposes an Extended Tendency Involvement Experience Model tailored to street food consumption. By linking intention formation to involvement and experiential outcomes, the proposed model aims to provide a more comprehensive explanation of street food consumption behavior and to contribute to the refinement of experiential consumption theory in tourism research.

Accordingly, the research design includes a detailed elaboration of the theoretical framework, literature review, hypothesis development, methods and procedures, results, discussion, theoretical implications, practical implications, limitations, and suggestions for future research.

Theoretical framework

Street food constitutes a culturally embedded and economically significant component of tourism destinations. Beyond its functional role, it reflects local culinary heritage and everyday cultural practices, thereby contributing to regional identity and informal food economies1,3. In tourism contexts, street food is increasingly positioned as an experiential domain where authenticity, social interaction, and emotional responses intersect8,9. As such, street food consumption represents not merely a behavioral act but a culturally meaningful and psychologically embedded experience.

Although prior research has examined tourists’ local food consumption in relation to satisfaction and behavioral intention10,11, the process through which consumption tendencies translate into involvement and experiential outcomes remains theoretically underexplored. To address this gap, the present study draws upon two complementary theoretical foundations: The Tendency–Involvement–Experience (TIE) model and the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB). The following subsections introduce these frameworks and clarify their integration within the context of street food consumption.

Tendency-involvement-experience model

The Tendency–Involvement–Experience (TIE) model conceptualizes tourism experience as a sequential process in which motivational predispositions evolve into engagement and ultimately into experiential outcomes4. Within this framework, tendency refers to an individual’s motivational orientation toward a particular domain, involvement captures the degree of psychological engagement with that domain and experience represents the evaluative and affective outcomes emerging from this engagement12.

In food related contexts, involvement has been associated with the personal importance individuals attach to food, the pleasure derived from consumption, and curiosity toward new culinary experiences11–13. Individuals with higher food involvement attribute deeper symbolic and cultural meanings to food beyond its functional role14. Such engagement is particularly relevant in tourism settings, where gastronomic experiences are linked to memorability and authenticity15,16.

Pine and Gilmore17 conceptualized experience as a distinct economic value, while Schmitt18 emphasized the emotional dimension of consumption, reinforcing the importance of experiential interpretation in tourism contexts. Xu, Zhang4 introduced the TIE model in the context of heritage tourism, demonstrating that tourists’ tendencies influence their experiences through involvement as a mediating mechanism. Building on this foundational logic, the present study adopts the sequential structure of the TIE model to examine how motivational predispositions toward street food may translate into involvement and experiential outcomes.

Theory of planned behavior

The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), developed by Ajzen and Madden5, explains behavioral intention through three core psychological determinants: attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control. Attitude reflects an individual’s positive or negative evaluation of performing a behavior, subjective norm refers to perceived social pressure to perform or avoid the behavior, and perceived behavioral control represents the perceived ease or difficulty of performing the behavior. Together, these components shape intention, which is considered the most proximal predictor of behavior.

TPB has been widely applied in tourism research to explain a variety of consumption related behaviors. Prior studies have used TPB to examine participation in tourism activities, destination visitation intentions, and food related behavioral intentions11,19. In gastronomy and hospitality contexts, TPB has been employed to investigate tourists’ intentions toward local food consumption, healthy eating, and street food purchasing behavior6. These applications demonstrate the robustness of TPB as a framework for understanding intention formation in tourism and food consumption settings.

Model integration

Although the Tendency–Involvement–Experience (TIE) model provides a sequential logic explaining how motivational predispositions evolve into experiential outcomes4, it does not explicitly specify the psychological mechanisms underlying the formation of the “tendency” component. In contrast, the Theory of Planned Behavior5 offers a well established framework for explaining intention formation through attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. However, TPB primarily focuses on predicting behavioral intention and does not extend its explanatory scope to experiential consequences following intention enactment.

Integrating these two models allows for a theoretically coherent and complementary structure. Within this framework, the “tendency” dimension of TIE is operationalized as consumption intention derived from TPB, while the sequential logic of TIE (tendency → involvement → experience) explains how intention translates into deeper engagement and experiential value. In this sense, TPB explains why tourists intend to consume street food, whereas TIE clarifies how this intention develops into involvement and ultimately shapes the street food experience.

To the best of current knowledge, the TIE model has not previously been integrated with TPB in the context of gastronomic or street food experiences. This integration therefore extends TIE at the antecedent level by embedding a validated intention formation mechanism into its structure and refines TPB by linking intention to experiential outcomes beyond behavioral prediction. Accordingly, the present study proposes an Extended Tendency–Involvement–Experience Model tailored to street food consumption.

Literature review and hypothesis development

In tourism consumption contexts, behavioral intention has consistently been identified as a key predictor of participation decisions. Within the Theory of Planned Behavior framework, intention is shaped by attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control5. In food and hospitality research, these determinants have been shown to significantly influence tourists’ consumption related decisions. In tourism and hospitality research, TPB has been applied to explain various food related and consumption based behaviors. Prior studies demonstrate that attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control significantly predict tourists’ intentions toward food consumption and related experiential behaviors6,11.

While the Theory of Planned Behavior has been applied to various tourism experiences, it has also been extensively used to examine tourists’ eating behaviors. Bayraktar, Oral20 analyzed tourists’ intentions towards healthy eating within the framework of the Theory of Planned Behavior, finding that this inclination is explained by attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. The eating attitudes and behavioral intentions of Muslim tourist profiles can also be explained using the premises of the Theory of Planned Behavior21. Additionally, there are numerous studies that examine tourists’ traditional consumption behaviors based on organic and local food using the Theory of Planned Behavior22–25. Furthermore, studies that address tourists’ street food consumption behaviors within the context of the Theory of Planned Behavior are also available. Neves, Eves26 analyzed street food consumption using the determinants of the Theory of Planned Behavior. Tacardon, Ong27 investigated tourists’ street food purchasing behaviors and found that attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control significantly and positively influenced this intention. Based on these insights, the following hypotheses have been developed for the study:

H1: The attitude towards street food positively and significantly affects the intention to consume street food.

H2: The subjective norm related to street food positively and significantly affects the intention to consume street food.

H3: The perceived behavioral control regarding street food positively and significantly affects the intention to consume street food.

The Tendency–Involvement–Experience (TIE) model proposes that motivational predispositions influence experiential outcomes through the mediating role of involvement (Xu et al., 2022). Empirical findings from heritage tourism contexts demonstrate that tourists’ tendencies significantly affect their experiences, with involvement functioning as a key mechanism in this relationship. Building on this logic, the present study applies the sequential structure of the TIE model to the street food context.

Food experiences are noted by tourists to represent meanings beyond mere nourishment. Walter28 associates food experiences with local cultural interaction, socialization, and intense sensory and emotional experiences. Carvalho, Kastenholz29 have tackled the topic of creative tourism experiences in food and wine tourism, where interaction, participation, and immersive experiences are considered critical precursors. Mawroh and Dixit16 state that tourists are motivated by authentic ethnic food behaviors related to a destination prior to experiencing street food. This motivation can be considered an intention shown by travelers before experiencing street food. Dutra14 evaluates street food as a source of entertainment for tourists, essentially framing it as a gastronomy tourism experience. In light of this information, it is thought that travelers’ intentions to consume street food may explain their dining experiences. Consequently, the following hypothesis has been developed:

H4: The intention to consume street food positively and significantly affects the food experience.

Within the Tendency–Involvement–Experience logic, intention reflects motivational readiness that should translate into deeper cognitive affective engagement with the focal domain; therefore, stronger street food consumption intention is expected to increase food involvement. Empirical tests of the TIE framework show that tendency significantly increases involvement, supporting an intention like disposition as a precursor to involvement4. Consistent with this view in food-tourism settings, involvement has been modeled as a key engagement mechanism linking tourists’ local food evaluations to behavioral intention11, and has been shown to align with memorable food based experiences and intention-related outcomes30. Moreover, on-site involvement is positively associated with experiential outcomes such as memorability and authenticity7, reinforcing the theoretical expectation that higher consumption intention fosters greater involvement. Accordingly, hypothesis H5 was developed.

H5: The intention to consume street food positively and significantly affects the level of food involvement.

Seyfi, Hall31 argue that evaluating tourism experience not only in terms of service or quality but also considering the memorability of the experience is a crucial factor in explaining tourism experience. de Bruin and Jelincic32 address tourism experience in the context of the tourist’s level of involvement. According to them, participatory tourism experience represents a more active involvement in tourist activities and is also considered an extension of creative tourism. Shin, Kim33 have examined tourism experiences in smart tourism destinations, emphasizing the importance of the memorability level of the experience.

While tourism experience is a common topic across various fields, studies also explore tourists’ eating behaviors within this scope. Kokkranikal and Carabelli15 have addressed the gastronomy tourism experience from the perspective of tourists engaged in cooking activities. In this context, the memorability of the cooking experience in gastronomy tourism is said to depend on factors like participation, hedonism, local culture, and knowledge. In this case, it is thought that food involvement can explain the food experience. Accordingly, hypothesis H6 has been developed.

H6: The level of food involvement in street food positively and significantly affects the food experience.

TIE model implies that participation translates the motivational effect of intention into a richer experience. In our case, a stronger intention to consume street food should lead to higher food involvement, which in turn should enhance the experience. This logic is consistent with recent findings. Xu, Zhang4 found that cultural “tendency” (similar to intention) significantly increased involvement in tourism experiences and led to greater experiential outcomes; Zatori, Smith7 showed that on site participation positively influenced experiential memorability and authenticity; Di-Clemente, Hernández-Mogollón30 showed that tourists’ food involvement changed with memorable food experiences and enhanced intention related outcomes; and Rehman, Samad11 modeled participation as a key engagement mechanism linking involvement of local food to behavioral intentions. Collectively, these results confirm that a higher intention to consume translates into higher involvement, leading to a more intense food experience. This information justifies hypothesis H7.

H7: Food involvement mediates the effect of the intention to consume street food on the food experience.

Materials and methods

Research model and hypotheses

The research model was designed based on the Tendency-Involvement-Experience Model4 and the Theory of Planned Behavior5, leading to the proposal of the “Extended Tendency-Involvement-Experience Model.” The model has been analyzed using the Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) technique and implemented through the SmartPLS4 software. Sample and data collection

To test the research model, data were collected directly from human participants. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for the research protocol was obtained from the [Atatürk University Social and Human Sciences Ethics Committee (Ref. 5/60–2024)]. All procedures involving human participants were carried out in compliance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians prior to participation in the study. Prior to data collection, the questionnaire was reviewed to ensure clarity and linguistic appropriateness for the target sample. The questionnaire was prepared bilingually (Turkish and English), as the original measurement scales were in English. The Turkish version was translated by the authors, who have extensive domain expertise in gastronomy tourism research and are fluent in both languages. This approach was intentionally adopted to minimize potential meaning shifts in context-specific terminology and experiential constructs. To enhance objectivity and ensure semantic equivalence, the bilingual questionnaire was evaluated by three independent field experts rather than solely by the researchers themselves. These experts have substantial experience in gastronomy tourism research and high language proficiency. The comprehensibility of the survey questions, their suitability to the field of eating behavior, and the use of appropriate and contextually accurate language were carefully examined. In addition, the survey form was structurally designed to include an optional “I did not understand this item” response option at the end of each scale item. This option was not mandatory and was provided as a procedural safeguard to detect potential comprehension problems during data collection. No such responses were recorded in the final dataset, indicating that participants did not report difficulties in understanding the items and supporting the linguistic clarity of the instrument. The survey comprises two sections: the first section contains four demographic and descriptive questions, while the second section includes a total of 34 scale items corresponding to the variables in the research model. To ensure temporal clarity and construct validity, participants were explicitly instructed to respond to all experience related items by reflecting on the most recent street food they had consumed during their travel. In addition, respondents were asked to specify the last street food they experienced in an open-ended question. Participation was subject to explicit inclusion criteria. Respondents were required to1 have traveled in Türkiye2, have consumed at least one street food item during their travel, and3 specify their most recent street food experience in an open-ended question before proceeding to the scale items. Responses that did not include a concrete street food example or that were incomplete were excluded from the dataset. This procedure ensured experiential verification rather than reliance on self-categorized tourist identity. This procedure ensured that all participants had prior street food consumption experience and that experiential evaluations were anchored to a concrete and recent consumption episode rather than a hypothetical or generalized perception. Given the cross-sectional nature of the study, the structural pathways indicated in the model represent theoretically grounded directional relationships rather than temporal causal effects. Experience refers to the participants’ most recent consumption event, while intention reflects a future oriented behavioral tendency; however, both constructs were measured concurrently as cognitively accessible psychological representations at the time the survey was administered. Therefore, the indicated relationships should be interpreted relationally and theoretically, not as evidence of chronological causality. In this context, causal inference remains limited. The theoretical target population of the study consists of tourists who have experienced street food during their travel in Türkiye. However, due to the absence of an accessible sampling frame covering all such tourists, participants were reached through industry gatekeepers, including hotel and travel agency managers who were in direct contact with active tourists. The distribution process was conducted through hotel and travel agency managers with whom the researchers were professionally connected. These industry representatives shared the survey link with tourists currently traveling in Türkiye via email, WhatsApp groups, and their official social media communication channels. The researchers did not conduct an open public call; instead, the survey was circulated through controlled professional networks to ensure access to actual tourists. A non-probability convenience sampling technique was employed, based on access to tourists through professional hospitality and travel networks. The sample size was determined based on current PLS-SEM methodological guidelines. Earlier literature suggested a “10-times rule”; however, recent research clarifies that the minimum sample size should be calculated based on the maximum number of structural paths pointing at any latent construct34. According to Hair, Risher34, the PLS-SEM technique can be employed with a smaller number of samples. In the present model, the construct with the highest number of incoming paths is Consumption Intention, which receives three direct paths. Accordingly, the minimum recommended sample size is 3 × 10 = 30 observations. With 310 valid responses, the sample size substantially exceeds this threshold. Therefore, the study satisfies current PLS-SEM sample adequacy requirements.

Since all variables were collected through a single self reported questionnaire administered at one point in time, potential common method bias (CMB) was assessed. Measures

All measurement instruments employed in this study were previously validated scales drawn from established research in tourism and food consumption contexts. Therefore, the present study did not involve new scale development but rather context-specific adaptation and revalidation within the street food setting. In the research model, variables related to attitudes towards street food, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, intention to consume street food, food involvement, and food experience were measured using scales whose validity and reliability have been established in the literature. All variables have been adapted specifically for street food consumption and consist of a single dimension. The variables of attitude towards street food, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and intention to consume street food were measured using scales adapted by Tacardon, Ong27 within the framework of the Theory of Planned Behavior. The attitude scale consists of 4 items, the subjective norm scale has 6 items, the perceived behavioral control scale comprises 5 items, and the scale for intention to consume street food includes 7 items. The food involvement variable was measured using the food involvement scale used by Kim, Suh12 as a factor of personality traits related to food, which consists of 5 items. The food experience variable was measured using the food experience scale from Mawroh and Dixit16 which contains 7 items. The original Food Involvement scale developed by Kim, Suh12 employed a seven-point Likert scale. In contrast, the other adapted scales were originally measured using five-point Likert formats. In the present study, all constructs were measured using a unified five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree) to ensure measurement consistency across variables and to reduce respondent cognitive burden. Prior methodological research indicates that five and seven-point Likert scales yield comparable reliability and validity outcomes in behavioral research36.

The abbreviations used for the variables constituting the research model and the factor loadings of the scale items are shown in Table 3. Upon examination of the factor loadings and indicator reliability, several items were removed in a stepwise refinement process to enhance the robustness of the measurement model. In the initial assessment, items SN1, PBC2, and PBC5 were excluded due to inadequate loadings and their negative impact on construct validity. In a subsequent re-estimation of the model, items ATT4 and FI2 were also removed, as their outer loadings were below the recommended threshold and their exclusion improved the AVE and composite reliability values of their respective constructs. After these refinements, all retained indicators met acceptable loading criteria and the measurement model demonstrated satisfactory reliability and convergent validity37. Although item PBC5 met the sufficient factor loading criterion, it was found to disrupt the factor structure. Upon examination, it was determined that the item was not consistent with the perceived behavioral control dimension and was more of an attitude-based item. Accordingly, it was observed that removing the item improved the other fit values of the PBC dimension. Following this refinement process, factor loadings and VIF values were recalculated, and the updated results are presented in Table 3. When all other items related to the scales are analyzed, it is seen that the factor loadings are above 0.50. In PLS-SEM analysis, the fact that the correlations of the indicators with each other are very high reveals the problem of multicollinearity. Accordingly, the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values of the items were examined. VIF values below 5.0 indicate acceptable fit, while VIF values below 3 indicate perfect fit37. All items exhibit VIF values below 3. Accordingly, all items meet the VIF criterion. Therefore, it was determined that there is no multicollinearity problem in the research model. Discussion and Conclusion

This study examines tourists’ street food intentions, involvement, and experience within the Tendency–Involvement–Experience framework, integrating the Theory of Planned Behavior into the tendency dimension. The findings indicate that attitudes and subjective norms significantly and positively influence tourists’ intention to consume street food, whereas perceived behavioral control does not have a statistically significant effect. Intention, in turn, significantly predicts both food involvement and food experience. Moreover, food involvement exerts a significant indirect effect by transmitting the influence of intention on experience. These results support the proposed Extended Tendency–Involvement–Experience Model and demonstrate that involvement functions as a key mechanism linking intention to experiential outcomes in the context of street food consumption. It is important to clarify that although experience refers to respondents’ most recent street food episode and intention reflects a future oriented tendency, the structural relationships tested in this study are interpreted as associative rather than strictly temporal causal. In cross-sectional psychological modeling, both constructs represent contemporaneous cognitive representations measured at the same time point. Therefore, the CI → FE pathway should be understood as reflecting a motivational evaluative linkage rather than a chronological influence of future intention on past experience.

This study strengthens the Tendency–Involvement–Experience (TIE) logic by operationalizing the “tendency” component through the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB). In interpreting the structural relationships, both statistical significance (p values) and substantive effect sizes (f²) were considered in order to distinguish between statistical detectability and theoretical magnitude. In line with TPB’s core proposition that intentions are shaped by attitudinal and normative appraisals41, the findings indicate that tourists’ attitudes and subjective norms are robust antecedents of intention to consume street food. Notably, the effect of subjective norms on consumption intention is not only statistically significant but also substantively strong (f² = 0.500), indicating that normative influences constitute a dominant driver of street food consumption intention in this context. Similarly, the effect of attitudes on consumption intention demonstrates a moderate to strong substantive magnitude (f² = 0.263), suggesting that positive evaluative appraisals of street food meaningfully contribute to intention formation, albeit to a lesser extent than normative pressures. This pattern is consistent with meta-analytic evidence demonstrating that attitudes and subjective norms are reliable predictors of intention across behavioral domains42,43, and with food tourism research linking tourists’ evaluations of local food to downstream behavioral intentions44. The influence of subjective norms on behavioral intention in the context of street food consumption has also been documented across different geographical settings. In a study conducted in the Philippines, the extended Theory of Planned Behavior demonstrated a significant relationship between subjective norms and consumption intention6. This evidence indicates that the SN → CI pathway identified in the present study is consistent with the international literature.

In contrast, perceived behavioral control (PBC) did not significantly predict intention in this street-food context. This result highlights an important boundary condition for applying TPB to low-effort, highly accessible consumption behaviors. TPB theorizing suggests that PBC becomes less diagnostic when the target behavior is largely under volitional control or when control perceptions exhibit limited variance5,41. Consistent with this reasoning, empirical work in convenience food settings shows that perceived control may fail to add explanatory power, particularly when routine or habitual processes dominate decision making45,46. In the tourism and hospitality context, several empirical studies have reported that perceived behavioral control does not always significantly explain behavioral intention47. Particularly in routinized or low barrier behaviors such as food consumption, the explanatory power of PBC may weaken. For example, in a study examining repurchase intention toward food truck products, the effect of perceived behavioral control was found to be non-significant48. The negligible effect size (f² = 0.001) further supports the interpretation that control perceptions play a minimal role in highly volitional consumption contexts. Moreover, meta analytic evidence points to heterogeneity in the magnitude of PBC effects across behaviors and operationalizations49,50, and indicates that PBC may operate more consistently as a moderator of intention enactment than as a stable determinant of intention formation50,51. There is evidence suggesting that perceived behavioral control (PBC) may operate differently across product categories. For instance, in a model examining sustainable biscuit purchase intention, PBC was found to be non significant, illustrating potential heterogeneity in its explanatory role52. Therefore, rather than assuming PBC as a universal determinant of intention, it may be more theoretically appropriate to interpret it as context-dependent. Moreover, within the TPB framework, PBC may function not only as a direct antecedent of intention but also as a moderating control interaction that conditions the effects of attitudes and subjective norms. Empirical research on control interactions indicates that PBC can moderate the influence of attitudinal and normative components on intention. This perspective supports the theoretical plausibility that PBC may not emerge as a significant direct predictor in certain contexts53.

Comparative street food research further supports the context dependence of PBC. For example, when monetary considerations, utilitarian value, and contextual barriers are salient, PBC has been found to significantly predict street food consumption intention6, and in some local food tourism contexts PBC can emerge as a dominant predictor54. Taken together, these findings refine TPB based theorizing in street food tourism by suggesting that, in destinations where street food is ubiquitous and easy to access, intention formation may be primarily attitudinal and normative rather than control driven6.

Beyond intention formation, the study extends the TIE model by demonstrating that intention functions as an experiential catalyst: it predicts both food involvement and the subsequent street food experience. The effect of consumption intention on food involvement is substantively large (f² = 0.555), indicating that motivational readiness strongly translates into deeper cognitive and affective engagement with street food experiences. In addition, the direct effect of consumption intention on food experience is also substantively large (f² = 0.442), suggesting that intention not only fosters involvement but also exerts a meaningful experiential influence in its own right. Furthermore, the effect of food involvement on food experience demonstrates a substantively meaningful magnitude (f² = 0.379), reinforcing the role of involvement as a central experiential mechanism within the Extended TIE framework. This contribution resonates with the view that food consumption can constitute either a supporting consumer experience or a peak touristic experience depending on situational conditions55. In addition, the results align with tourism research positioning involvement as a key driver of evaluative and place related outcomes56, suggesting that involvement is a critical mechanism through which motivational predispositions are converted into richer tourism experiences4,55.

Finally, food involvement mediates the intention-experience relationship, implying that tourists who intend to consume street food are more likely to invest cognitive and affective resources in food related activities, thereby amplifying their experience. This is theoretically consistent with the conceptualization of food involvement as the personal relevance attached to food acquisition, preparation, and consumption57, as well as with integrative evidence showing that involvement influences food related choices and responses58. In the formation of experience, involvement may function not merely as an antecedent but as a mechanism operating within the experiential process itself. Zatori, Smith7 demonstrated that on site experience involvement is significantly associated with experiential outcomes such as memorability and authenticity, thereby supporting this internal mechanism perspective. This evidence strengthens the theoretical justification for the FI → FE pathway identified in the present study. In gastronomy tourism, involvement may enhance the memorability of the experience and shape its behavioral consequences. Di-Clemente, Hernández-Mogollón30 identified a significant relationship between involvement and memorable food-based experiences, further supporting the involvement driven experiential mechanism. These findings allow the effect of involvement on experience observed in this study to be positioned within the broader gastronomy tourism literature. Moreover, the formation of intention and its translation into behavioral and experiential outcomes in tourism consumption can be explained through multiple psychological inputs. In the present model, intention serves as a motivational driver that unfolds into behavioral engagement (involvement) and experiential outcomes, a structure consistent with prior tourism research59. Therefore, conceptualizing intention as a gateway leading to both involvement and experience is theoretically coherent and empirically grounded. Overall, the findings advance an “Extended TIE” account in which TPB-based tendency (primarily attitudes and norms) fuels intention, while involvement operates as the experiential conduit leading to favorable street food experiences57.

Theoretical implications

This study advances theory by proposing an Extended Tendency–Involvement–Experience (TIE) framework that integrates a validated intention formation mechanism into the TIE sequence. Building on the original TIE logic, which conceptualizes experience as an unfolding process from motivational predispositions to engagement and experiential outcomes4, the present model extends TIE at the antecedent level by embedding the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) as a formally specified basis for the “tendency” component. This extension clarifies the theoretical structure of “tendency” and strengthens the explanatory chain from motivational antecedents to experiential value.

Second, the study makes a specific conceptual contribution by operationalizing tendency as TPB based consumption intention. In TPB, intention functions as the most proximal motivational antecedent of behavior and is theoretically determined by attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control5,41. By positioning intention as the operational core of tendency, the model provides a sharper and more theory consistent specification of the motivational construct within TIE. This improves conceptual precision by moving “tendency” from a broad predispositional label to a well-defined intention construct grounded in social psychology.

Third, the framework clarifies involvement as a transmission mechanism rather than merely a parallel predictor. Within experiential consumption theory, involvement represents the personal relevance and cognitive-affective investment individuals attach to a consumption domain57. In the Extended TIE structure, involvement functions as the experiential conduit that translates motivational readiness (intention/tendency) into experiential evaluation, thereby formalizing a process-based mechanism that links motivational cognition to experiential outcomes. This mechanism is consistent with experience oriented perspectives that conceptualize consumption as value creation through engagement, affect, and interpretation17,18.

Finally, by explicitly modeling the pathway from intention (tendency) to involvement to experience, the study offers a coherent theoretical template for examining experiential consumption in gastronomy tourism. The Extended TIE formulation preserves the sequential logic of TIE while strengthening its explanatory foundations through TPB’s intention architecture, thereby providing a structured approach for theorizing how motivational dispositions are converted into experiential value in food related tourism contexts.

Practical implications

Findings offer actionable guidance for destination managers and street food stakeholders seeking to enhance tourists’ street food experiences. First, because attitudes and subjective norms are the strongest predictors of consumption intention in our model, destination marketing efforts should prioritize “meaning based” positioning of street food as an authentic cultural asset rather than merely a convenient and inexpensive option. Prior destination marketing research shows that food can differentiate destinations when it is strategically embedded into positioning and communicated with appropriate cultural and market knowledge60. In parallel, supply side coordination and governance are critical to sustain a coherent culinary narrative and avoid fragmented offerings61. Second, the significant role of subjective norms implies that social proof mechanisms (e.g., UGC, influencer content, and peer recommendations) should be systematically leveraged. Experimental evidence indicates that positive, popular social media narratives can jointly increase visiting attitudes and intentions while lowering perceived destination risk62. Since online reviews are influential yet asymmetrically processed, review management protocols should also be institutionalized: expert reviewers tend to be perceived as more credible, and negative reviews can carry stronger credibility signals, requiring rapid and professional responses63. Third, because intention predicts both food involvement and experience, and food involvement further enhances experience, stakeholders should design “involvement building” products that convert intention into richer on-site engagement, such as curated street food trails, tastings with storytelling, cooking demonstrations, and cocreated microexperiences. Prior experience design research shows that interaction and customization strengthen experience-involvement and contribute to memorability and authenticity7, and visitor engagement can translate into eWOM and revisit intentions through memorable tourism experiences64. Finally, although perceived behavioral control did not significantly predict intention in our model, visible safety and hygiene practices remain strategically important because they indirectly protect tourists’ attitudes and social approval perceptions65. For example, standardized uniforms, visible handwashing stations, transparent food preparation areas, certification badges displayed at stalls, and digital screens showing recent inspection results can reinforce perceptions of professionalism and trust. Such visible signals help sustain positive evaluations (attitudes) and reduce potential negative word of mouth that could weaken subjective norms. In addition, structured vendor training programs and periodic public hygiene audits can institutionalize safety standards while simultaneously communicating accountability to consumers. By making hygiene practices visible and communicable, vendors can safeguard the attitudinal and normative foundations that drive intention and, ultimately, involvement and experiential outcomes66. Collectively, these actions can strengthen intention formation, increase involvement, and ultimately enhance tourists’ street food experiences.

Social Implications

The findings of this study carry important social implications regarding the normalization and cultural embedding of street food consumption in urban tourism contexts. The significant effects of attitudes and subjective norms on consumption intention suggest that street food is not merely an individual consumption choice but a socially constructed and culturally shared practice. Subjective norms, in particular, indicate that street food consumption is socially approved and embedded within everyday social environments. This aligns with previous research demonstrating that local food consumption is closely linked to social identity, destination image, and collective meaning making processes30,44. The nonsignificant effect of perceived behavioral control further implies that street food consumption is perceived as easily accessible and socially normalized rather than constrained by structural barriers. In this sense, street food appears to function as an ordinary yet culturally symbolic component of urban life, reinforcing communal interaction and shared experiential spaces.

Moreover, the confirmed mediation effect of food involvement (CI → FI → FE) highlights the role of personal engagement as a mechanism through which social meanings are transformed into experiential value. Involvement enables tourists to cognitively and emotionally invest in food related activities, thereby strengthening memorability and authenticity perceptions7. As involvement increases, street food becomes more than a transactional act; it evolves into a participatory cultural experience embedded in local heritage and social interaction. This finding supports the idea that gastronomy tourism contributes to cultural sustainability by fostering deeper engagement with local culinary traditions29. Therefore, street food can be interpreted not only as an economic or marketing asset but also as a socially integrative practice that enhances cultural continuity and collective urban experience.

Limitation and future research

The findings of this research should be evaluated within the framework of some limitations. First, the study employs a cross-sectional and self reported design, which restricts causal inference. Although the model specifies directional paths, causal inference cannot be established due to the cross-sectional nature of the data. Although the proposed model demonstrates strong predictive power, longitudinal research would provide deeper insight into how intention evolves into involvement and experience over time. Second, the nonsignificant effect of perceived behavioral control (PBC) represents an important contextual boundary of the model. While TPB posits PBC as a determinant of intention, its explanatory role may depend on the nature of the behavior examined. In the context of street food consumption, an activity generally characterized by high accessibility and low structural barriers, perceived control may exhibit limited variance. Therefore, the absence of a significant PBC effect may reflect contextual characteristics rather than theoretical inadequacy. Future research should test the model in settings where street food consumption is more regulated, costly, or constrained to determine whether PBC regains explanatory power under different environmental conditions. In addition, although composite reliability and AVE values for PBC met recommended thresholds, Cronbach’s alpha remained at a moderate level due to the reduced number of retained indicators. In reflective measurement models with few items, alpha may underestimate internal consistency. Therefore, the reliability of PBC should be interpreted with caution. Future studies may benefit from respecifying or expanding the measurement of perceived behavioral control to capture broader control dimensions in street food contexts. Third, the sample is limited to tourists who experienced street food in Türkiye and was obtained through convenience sampling. Cultural norms, food safety perceptions, and urban street food infrastructure may vary across destinations. Comparative cross-cultural studies would help clarify whether the relative dominance of attitudinal and normative drivers over control-based drivers is context specific. Furthermore, the questionnaire did not distinguish between domestic and international tourists. Although all participants were visitors who had experienced street food during their travel in Türkiye, the absence of nationality-based differentiation limits the ability to assess potential cross-cultural variation in intention, involvement, and experiential evaluation. Future research should explicitly compare domestic and international tourist segments to examine whether the proposed structural relationships differ across cultural backgrounds. Fourth, although participants were required to have prior street food experience, the study did not include control variables such as frequency of prior consumption, familiarity with street food, or length of stay. These factors may influence experiential evaluations and should be incorporated in future research to enhance model robustness. Finally, the model focuses on intention rather than actual behavioral outcomes. Future research may incorporate objective consumption behavior, revisit intentions, or expenditure data to examine the intention–behavior relationship within the Extended TIE framework.
