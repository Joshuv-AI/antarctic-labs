Prevalence, Antimicrobial Resistance, and Genetic Diversity of Non‐Typeable Streptococcus pneumoniae Carriage: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Robert K Reeks, Onyansaniba K Ntim, Eric S Donkor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNon‐typeable Streptococcus pneumoniae (NT‐Sp) strains lack detectable capsules and are often missed by traditional serotyping. While associated primarily with asymptomatic carriage, NT‐Sp has emerged as a key player in antimicrobial resistance (AMR) gene dissemination and serotype replacement in the post‐vaccine era. This systematic review and meta‐analysis synthesize global evidence on NT‐Sp carriage, its prevalence, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and genetic diversity.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, and ScienceDirect was conducted up to May 2025. Eligible studies reporting NT‐Sp carriage, molecular typing, or AMR profiles were included. Data extraction and quality assessment followed PRISMA guidelines. A meta‐analysis was performed to estimate pooled prevalence of NT‐Sp carriage and resistance, with subgroup and meta‐regression analyses exploring heterogeneity. Methodological quality was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale adapted for cross‐sectional studies. Only open‐access and free full‐text articles were included, which may have introduced selection bias.ResultsForty studies from 23 countries were included. The pooled NT‐Sp carriage prevalence was 4.64% (95% CI: 2.78%–6.92%), with substantial heterogeneity (I 2 = 97.1%, 95% CI: 96.6%–97.5%). Subgroup analyses revealed geographic variation, with Oceania showing the highest prevalence (7.24%, 95% CI: 1.39%–17.04%) and North America the lowest (0.44%, 95% CI: 0.05%–1.11%). Age‐stratified analysis showed that studies, including all ages had the highest estimate (29.88%, 95% CI: 0.00%–100.00%), while children had a pooled prevalence of 4.05% (95% CI: 0.00%–15.80%). Vaccination era did not significantly influence NT‐Sp carriage (p = 0.312). Resistance was highest for co‐trimoxazole (67.21%, 95% CI: 49.92%–82.73%), penicillin (44.57%, 95% CI: 24.45%–65.50%), and erythromycin (39.85%, 95% CI: 19.58%–61.77%). Meta‐regression identified the “all ages” participant category as a significant predictor of lower NT‐Sp prevalence (p = 0.034). Molecular tools revealed substantial genomic diversity, with dominant clonal complexes varying by region.ConclusionNT‐Sp is a globally distributed and genetically diverse pneumococcal subset with considerable AMR potential. Its persistence post‐PCV introduction and resistance gene carriage highlights the need for improved detection, molecular surveillance, and consideration in next‐generation vaccine development. Specifically, clinical laboratories in high‐prevalence settings should adopt molecular methods (e.g., PCR or WGS) to detect NT‐Sp in carriage surveillance; empirical antibiotic guidelines for pediatric respiratory infections should consider NT‐Sp resistance patterns, particularly the high prevalence of co‐trimoxazole resistance (67%) and next‐generation PCV trials should include NT‐Sp carriage as an exploratory endpoint. Integrating NT‐Sp into global pneumococcal control strategies is essential.Keywords: antimicrobial resistance, meta‐analysis, non‐typeable carriage, sequence type, Streptococcus pneumoniae

Introduction

Streptococcus pneumoniae is a Gram‐positive pathobiont that colonizes the nasopharynx asymptomatically [1] yet remains a leading cause of invasive pneumococcal diseases (IPD), including pneumonia, meningitis, and bacteremia, particularly in children, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals [2]. Globally, pneumococcal infections account for over 1.6 million deaths annually, with the highest burden in low‐resource settings where vaccine access is limited [3, 4]. Key risk factors for severe IPD include young age, older age (≥ 65 years), asplenia, sickle cell disease, HIV infection, chronic heart or lung disease, and limited access to healthcare in low‐resource settings [4, 5]. While over 100 serotypes have been identified based on capsular polysaccharides [5], non‐typeable pneumococci (NT‐Sp), strains lacking detectable capsules, represent a unique subset that challenges conventional serotyping methods and plays a complex role in carriage dynamics and disease [6, 7]. Although NT‐Sp rarely causes invasive pneumococcal disease, several studies have documented its involvement in conjunctivitis outbreaks and occasional invasive disease cases [6, 7].

NT‐Sp are frequently overlooked in surveillance due to their inability to elicit a Quellung reaction or agglutinate with standard antisera [6]. However, molecular techniques such as S2‐gene sequencing and multilocus sequence typing (MLST) have confirmed their genetic identity as S. pneumoniae, revealing a prevalence of up to 55% in infant carriage studies [8]. Unlike encapsulated strains, NT‐Sp rarely cause IPD but are increasingly recognized as reservoirs for antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes and contributors to serotype replacement post‐vaccination [9]. Their high recombination rates and genomic plasticity may facilitate the transfer of virulence factors or resistance determinants to encapsulated strains, complicating efforts to control pneumococcal disease [7, 9]. For example, Tavares et al. (2014) demonstrated that Portuguese NT‐Sp strains (NCC2 clonal complex) carried the same pneumococcal surface protein A (PspA) family and pilus islets as encapsulated clones, suggesting frequent horizontal gene transfer. Similarly, Dayie et al. (2019) identified NT‐Sp isolates in Ghana harboring mefA and ermB macrolide resistance genes also found in vaccine serotypes.

The rise of pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) has dramatically reduced carriage of vaccine‐serotype pneumococci [10], creating an ecological niche for non‐vaccine strains. Within this niche, NT‐Sp has emerged as a persistent colonizer, with several studies documenting stable or increased NT‐Sp carriage post‐PCV introduction [8, 11, 12]. This persistence may be explained by unique genetic features that enhance NT‐Sp adaptation to the nasopharynx, including frequent absence of the piaB gene (a marker for iron acquisition) and presence of zmpC (associated with mucosal colonization) [7]. These features, combined with high recombination rates, position NT‐Sp as a potential reservoir for AMR genes and a contributor to serotype replacement.

Despite their clinical and epidemiological significance, NT‐Sp remain understudied compared to encapsulated serotypes. Existing data on their sequence types (ST's), geographic distribution, and AMR profiles are fragmented, hindering a unified understanding of their public health impact. This systematic review synthesizes global evidence on NT‐Sp carriage. By integrating molecular and clinical data, this review aims to inform vaccine strategies, antimicrobial stewardship, and surveillance protocols for NT‐Sp in the PCV era.

Methods

This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) standards [13]. This review was not registered in PROSPERO; however, the protocol followed PRISMA guidelines throughout. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1) with the meta and metafor packages. The analysis plan followed the SAMPL (Statistical Analyses and Methods in the Published Literature) guidelines for reporting of statistical methods. Reporting of this systematic review followed the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) guidelines where applicable. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and all tests were two‐sided. p‐values were reported as follows: p < 0.001 as “p < 0.001”; p values between 0.001 and 0.01 to the nearest thousandth; p‐values ≥ 0.01 to the nearest hundredth; and p‐values > 0.99 as “p > 0.99”. The meta‐analysis methodology adhered to the recommendations of Assel et al. (2019) [14] for reporting of statistics in clinical research. As this is a systematic review and meta‐analysis of published literature, ethical approval and informed consent were not required.

Literature Search Strategy and Selection Process

Using keywords associated with Streptococcus pneumoniae non‐typeable, a comprehensive literature search was carried out in Google Scholar, PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and ScienceDirect. Only free full‐text or open‐access research articles published in English were included in the search, which ran until May 2025 (Table 1). Records that were retrieved were exported in CSV and RIS formats.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligible studies were observational (cross‐sectional, cohort, or surveillance) or clinical trials that reported carriage or antimicrobial resistance (AMR) of non‐typeable Streptococcus pneumoniae (NT‐Sp). Participants included healthy or infected individuals of any age. Included studies had to identify S. pneumoniae using phenotypic methods (e.g., Quellung reaction and optochin sensitivity) or molecular approaches such as PCR, MLST, or whole‐genome sequencing (WGS), and provide explicit NT‐Sp data, including resistance rates and multidrug resistance profiles.

For this review, NT‐Sp was operationally defined as pneumococcal isolates lacking detectable capsular serotype by phenotypic serotyping methods (e.g., Quellung reaction or latex agglutination) and/or isolates confirmed as non‐encapsulated through molecular approaches such as cpsA‐negative PCR, lytA‐positive PCR, MLST, or WGS. Variability in diagnostic criteria across studies was considered a potential source of heterogeneity.

Excluded studies were case reports, reviews, editorials, comments, non‐peer‐reviewed sources (e.g., theses, dissertations, and preprints), and studies that focused on other serotypes or did not provide specific NT‐Sp carriage data.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Two researchers (R.K.R. and E.S.D.) independently assessed the methodological quality of included studies using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale [16] (NOS) adapted for cross‐sectional studies. The NOS evaluates studies across three domains: selection (representativeness, sample size, non‐respondents, and exposure ascertainment), comparability (control for confounding), and outcome (outcome assessment and statistical analysis). A maximum of 8 stars could be awarded, with studies scoring 7–8 stars classified as high quality, 5–6 stars as moderate quality, and ≤ 4 stars as low quality. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through consensus. Of the 40 included studies, 34 (85%) were rated as high quality (7–8 stars), and six (15%) were rated as moderate quality (5–6 stars). No studies were rated as low quality. The most common methodological limitations were lack of non‐respondent description and limited control for confounding factors. Statistical Analysis

Meta‐analysis was conducted using a random‐effects model to estimate pooled prevalence and resistance proportions because substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity was anticipated across studies, including differences in study populations, geographic settings, vaccination eras, and laboratory identification methods. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I 2 statistic. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger's regression test. Subgroup analyses (by age, region, and vaccination era) and meta‐regression were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. All analyses were conducted in R using the meta and metafor packages.

Results

Search Results and Selection Process

A total of 985 records were identified from all four electronic databases. Two hundred ninety duplicate records were removed, leaving 695 unique records to be screened. Of the 695 unique records, 566 were excluded based on their title, abstract, and language, and the remaining 129 records were sought for full‐text retrieval. After full‐text retrieval (n = 129), 67 articles were excluded following the full‐text eligibility assessment. Finally, 40 studies were included in the systematic review (Figure 1).

PRISMA flow diagram of the study selection process.

Description of Included Studies

This systematic review included 40 studies from 23 countries across six continents (Table 3). The geographic distribution revealed a dominance of African (n = 13) and European (n = 12) studies, followed by South America (n = 5), Asia (n = 6), Oceania (n = 3), and North America (n = 1). Ghana contributed the most studies (n = 4), followed by Brazil (n = 3) and Portugal (n = 2). Most investigations were single‐center studies focusing on community or hospital‐based sampling.

The study populations were diverse: 10 studies sampled only children, six included both children and adults, one focused exclusively on adults (≥ 16 years), and two used mother–infant pairs. The remaining studies included mixed populations or did not explicitly stratify by age. Over 70% of the included studies were conducted post‐PCV introduction, spanning PCV7, PCV10, and PCV13 eras. Sample sizes ranged from under 100 to over 8000 individuals.

Non‐typeable Streptococcus pneumoniae (NT‐Sp) carriage prevalence ranged from 0.19% to 73.55%, with higher rates generally observed among infants and in pre‐vaccine populations.

Prevalence of Non‐Typeable Pneumococcal Carriage in the Isolates

The pooled prevalence of non‐typeable Streptococcus pneumoniae (NT‐Sp) carriage was estimated using a random‐effects meta‐analysis. Figure 2 presents the overall pooled prevalence together with the individual study estimates and measures of between‐study heterogeneity.

Forest plot showing the pooled prevalence of NT‐Sp carriage from 40 included studies using a random‐effects model. Pooled prevalence: 4.64% (95% CI: 2.78%–6.92%). Heterogeneity: I 2 = 97.1% (95% CI: 96.6%–97.5%), τ 2 = 0.0035, p < 0.0001. Prediction interval: 0.00%–25.90%. Each horizontal line represents the confidence interval (CI) for an individual study, while the diamond indicates the overall summary estimate.

Sensitivity Analysis

A leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the robustness of the pooled prevalence estimate and determine whether any individual study had a disproportionate influence on the overall results. The findings are summarized in Figure 8.

Sensitivity analysis plot showing the effect of removing each study one at a time on the overall pooled estimate. Leave‐one‐out estimates ranged from 3.73% to 4.83%, all within the 95% CI of the original estimate (4.64%, 95% CI: 2.78%–6.92%). The consistency across iterations confirms that the meta‐analysis result is robust and not disproportionately influenced by any single study.

Meta‐Regression

Meta‐regression analyses were performed using univariable random‐effects models to explore potential sources of heterogeneity in NT‐Sp carriage prevalence. Covariates included age category, vaccination period, geographic region, detection method, and sample size. Categorical variables were dummy‐coded prior to analysis. Due to the limited number of studies within some subgroups, multivariable modeling was not performed to avoid model overfitting and unstable estimates.

Molecular Tools and Genetic Diversity

Molecular characterization of NT‐Sp isolates revealed substantial diversity across geographic regions. Whole‐genome sequencing (WGS), multilocus sequence typing (MLST), and PCR‐based assays were the predominant molecular tools used. These methods enabled confirmation of pneumococcal identity in cpsA‐negative strains and differentiation from other commensal streptococci.

Clonal complexes such as ST66 were identified in Brazil, and ST320 in Ghana [26], illustrating regional specificity. In Southeast Asia and parts of Africa, studies noted the presence of non‐encapsulated clones lacking traditional capsule biosynthesis genes but retaining key virulence and resistance determinants.

Genomic analyses demonstrated that NT‐Sp strains shared up to 90.6% core genome similarity with encapsulated strains, though lacking capsule synthesis loci. The absence of the cps locus (capsule biosynthesis genes) is the defining molecular feature of NT‐Sp, consistent with observations that capsule loss can occur through recombination‐mediated deletion events [7, 12]. This genetic adaptation may represent an evolutionary strategy to evade vaccine‐induced immunity while retaining colonization fitness. Accessory gene content, including prophages, surface proteins, and AMR genes, varied significantly, reflecting adaptation to specific ecological niches. Some NT‐Sp strains carried resistance determinants such as mefA, ermB, and tetM, further underscoring their role in antimicrobial resistance gene dissemination.

Standardization remains a challenge, as older serotyping methods frequently misclassified NT‐Sp. The review highlights the need for consistent application of WGS and MLST, especially in low‐resource settings, to ensure accurate surveillance.

Discussion

This systematic synthesis highlights the global distribution and characteristics of non‐typeable Streptococcus pneumoniae (NT‐Sp), an often‐overlooked component of pneumococcal ecology. Drawing from 40 studies across six continents, this review offers the most comprehensive overview of NT‐Sp carriage to date. While traditionally marginalized in surveillance due to their lack of polysaccharide capsules, NT‐Sp strains emerge here as resilient colonizers with significant implications for antimicrobial resistance (AMR), vaccine strategy, and diagnostics [6, 7, 30, 33, 34]. The pooled global NT‐Sp carriage prevalence of 4.64% (95% CI: 2.78%–6.92%) confirms that NT‐Sp is a widespread colonizer, though the substantial heterogeneity (I 2 = 97.1%, 95% CI: 96.6%–97.5%) underscores the influence of geographic, demographic, and methodological factors on observed prevalence estimates (Figure 2).

Carriage prevalence varied significantly by geographic region (p < 0.001). Oceania showed the highest pooled prevalence at 7.24% (95% CI: 1.39%–17.04%) (Figure 3), though with wide confidence intervals reflecting heterogeneity across studies from Papua New Guinea and Australia. Asia followed closely at 6.46% (95% CI: 4.30%–9.01%), with particularly high rates in Southeast Asia especially along the Thailand–Myanmar border where Turner et al. [8] reported NT‐Sp carriage exceeding 50% among infants. This exceptionally high prevalence likely reflects multiple interacting factors: high transmission intensity due to crowding and large household sizes, limited PCV access during the study period (2007–2008), and the young infant cohort (0–24 months) that is most susceptible to pneumococcal acquisition. In contrast, European and North American studies reported the lowest NT‐Sp carriage at 3.12% (95% CI: 1.92%–4.58%) and 0.44% (95% CI: 0.05%–1.11%), respectively, attributable to high PCV coverage (≥ 85% in most countries), better hygiene infrastructure, smaller household sizes, and potentially different circulating clones with lower colonization fitness. West African countries (The Gambia, Ghana, and Nigeria) showed intermediate prevalence ranging from 5% to 15% in individual studies, reflecting moderate PCV coverage but persistent high transmission intensity in urban and peri‐urban settings. Brazil showed varied prevalence (2%–10%) depending on pre‐ vs. post‐PCV10 sampling periods [20, 28, 39, 45], suggesting that PCV introduction may have influenced NT‐Sp ecology in complex ways.

The age‐stratified analysis revealed that the “All ages” category had the highest pooled estimate at 29.88% (95% CI: 0.00%–100.00%) (Figure 4), though this finding must be interpreted with extreme caution due to the very wide confidence interval reflecting substantial heterogeneity (I 2 = 99.7%) and limited sample size (n = 1126). This category was primarily driven by a single study from The Gambia [11] which reported 89 NT‐Sp isolates among 121 samples (73.55%), substantially inflating the pooled estimate. Studies focusing exclusively on children showed a pooled prevalence of 4.05% (95% CI: 0.00%–15.80%), while adults showed 3.38% (95% CI: 0.00%–17.99%), and studies, including both children and adults showed 3.06% (95% CI: 0.00%–12.99%). The test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (p = 0.659), indicating that age group categorization alone does not explain the observed heterogeneity. However, the adult subgroup showed no heterogeneity (I 2 = 0%, p = 0.334), suggesting consistent NT‐Sp carriage estimates across adult study populations. Across included studies, NT‐Sp carriage was consistently highest among infants under 24 months of age [8, 11], consistent with the broader epidemiology of pneumococcal carriage where children serve as the primary reservoir for transmission due to immature immune systems and frequent close contact in daycare and household settings.

Vaccination era did not significantly influence NT‐Sp carriage (p = 0.312), with post‐vaccination studies showing 5.72% (95% CI: 2.29%–10.50%) (Figure 5) compared to pre‐vaccination studies at 3.87% (95% CI: 1.89%–6.48%) and mixed‐era studies at 2.91% (95% CI: 1.74%–4.37%). The non‐significant difference between pre‐ and post‐vaccination periods suggests that PCV introduction has not consistently reduced NT‐Sp colonization, unlike its dramatic impact on vaccine serotypes. This persistence may be explained by several mechanisms. First, ecological replacement—the removal of vaccine serotypes creates a niche that NT‐Sp (and other non‐vaccine serotypes) can fill, as documented in The Gambia where Usuf et al. [11] reported that NT‐Sp carriage remained stable despite 10 years of PCV pressure while vaccine serotypes nearly disappeared. Second, capsule loss—some NT‐Sp strains represent encapsulated serotypes that have lost capsule expression through genetic mutations in the capsule locus; Sá‐Leão et al. [12] identified NT‐Sp isolates in Portugal with disrupted capsule loci but otherwise identical genetic backgrounds to serotype 14 strains, suggesting capsule loss as a mechanism of vaccine escape. Third, intrinsic colonization fitness—NT‐Sp strains may possess genetic features that enhance mucosal colonization independent of capsule, including frequent absence of the piaB gene and presence of zmpC [7, 32]. However, studies from Norway [33] and the UK [41] observed declining or stable low NT‐Sp carriage post‐PCV, indicating that ecological replacement is not universal and may depend on baseline carriage prevalence, population structure, and transmission dynamics. While NT‐Sp is rarely implicated in invasive disease, its presence in vaccinated communities underscores its adaptability and the potential for immune evasion.

The high antimicrobial resistance rates observed in NT‐Sp isolates are concerning: co‐trimoxazole (67.21%, 95% CI: 49.92%–82.73%), penicillin (44.57%, 95% CI: 24.45%–65.50%), and erythromycin (39.85%, 95% CI: 19.58%–61.77%) resistance far exceeds typical rates in encapsulated pneumococci in many settings (Figure 9). The overall pooled resistance across all antimicrobials was 46.05% (95% CI: 35.97%–56.26%, I 2 = 80.0%). Several mechanisms may explain why NT‐Sp strains harbor resistance at rates comparable to or higher than encapsulated strains. First, the lack of a capsule may reduce the fitness cost associated with carrying resistance genes, allowing NT‐Sp to maintain resistance determinants even without antibiotic selection pressure; in encapsulated strains, resistance‐conferring mutations or plasmids often impose metabolic costs, and the absence of capsule biosynthesis may spare metabolic resources, enabling NT‐Sp to carry multiple resistance genes more stably. Second, co‐selection plays a role: NT‐Sp strains frequently carry resistance genes (e.g., ermB and tetM) on the same mobile genetic elements (such as Tn916‐like transposons), as documented by Dayie et al. [26] in Ghana, meaning that exposure to one antibiotic (e.g., tetracycline) could maintain resistance to another (e.g., erythromycin). Third, the high recombination rate of NT‐Sp may facilitate acquisition of resistance cassettes from other streptococci (e.g., Streptococcus mitis and S. oralis) that share the nasopharyngeal niche, with concerning implications for horizontal gene transfer to encapsulated strains that cause invasive disease. Fourth, persistent NT‐Sp carriage—potentially longer duration than encapsulated strains in some settings—provides a greater window for antibiotic exposure and resistance selection. Similar phenomena have been observed in non‐typeable Haemophilus influenzae, suggesting a broader pattern among non‐encapsulated respiratory pathobionts where capsule loss allows persistence in the face of vaccine pressure but may also facilitate resistance acquisition. The high co‐trimoxazole resistance (67%) is particularly concerning because co‐trimoxazole remains widely used for pediatric respiratory infections in many low‐resource settings, suggesting that empirical co‐trimoxazole may be ineffective for pneumococcal infections in these regions, necessitating alternative antibiotics or diagnostic confirmation.

Detection method significantly influenced reported NT‐Sp carriage (p < 0.001), with multiplex PCR detecting the highest pooled prevalence at 9.48% (95% CI: 7.35%–11.85%), followed by multiple detection tools at 4.67% (95% CI: 2.69%–7.14%, I 2 = 97.3%), PCR at 4.26% (95% CI: 2.09%–7.12%), and WGS at 1.06% (95% CI: 0.02%–3.16%) (Figure 7). The substantially lower prevalence detected by WGS likely reflects more stringent confirmation of pneumococcal identity and exclusion of non‐pneumococcal isolates, particularly among viridans group streptococci such as Streptococcus mitis that can be misidentified as pneumococci by phenotypic methods. This finding underscores the importance of standardized molecular confirmation for accurate NT‐Sp surveillance and highlights that many older studies relying on Quellung reaction alone may have significantly underestimated NT‐Sp prevalence. Studies using enrichment culture plus PCR consistently detected higher NT‐Sp carriage than those relying on Quellung reaction alone, reinforcing that detection methods are a critical determinant of reported prevalence and that molecular approaches should be prioritized, especially in low‐resource settings.

Meta‐regression analysis identified participant category “All ages” as the only statistically significant predictor of NT‐Sp carriage prevalence (coefficient: 0.4776, 95% CI: 0.0360–0.9193, p = 0.034) (Table 4). Studies that included participants of all ages reported significantly lower NT‐Sp prevalence compared to studies focusing exclusively on children (the reference category). This finding is consistent with the age‐stratified subgroup analysis, which showed that children have the highest NT‐Sp carriage rates, and including adults in study populations dilutes the overall prevalence estimate. No other covariates reached statistical significance, though several trends were observed: Asian studies showed a trend toward higher prevalence (coefficient: 0.0957, p = 0.260), North American studies showed a trend toward lower prevalence (coefficient: −0.0766, p = 0.648), multiplex PCR detection showed a trend toward higher prevalence (coefficient: 0.1660, p = 0.322), and WGS detection showed a trend toward lower prevalence (coefficient: −0.0336, p = 0.844). Vaccination period showed no association with NT‐Sp prevalence (coefficient: −0.0523, p = 0.426 for pre‐vaccination vs. post‐vaccination). The lack of statistically significant findings for most covariates underscores the complexity of NT‐Sp epidemiology, where unmeasured factors (e.g., host immunity, local antibiotic use, socioeconomic status, and transmission dynamics) likely play critical roles in explaining the substantial heterogeneity observed across studies.

Molecular tools provided key insights into the genetic diversity and evolution of NT‐Sp. MLST and WGS confirmed region‐specific clones and demonstrated that NT‐Sp strains share most of their core genome with encapsulated lineages [26, 28]. Clonal complexes such as ST66 were identified in Brazil [28], and ST320 in Ghana [26], illustrating regional specificity and geographic structure, likely reflecting local transmission dynamics, antibiotic selection pressures, and vaccine introduction histories. In Southeast Asia and parts of Africa, studies noted the presence of non‐encapsulated clones lacking traditional capsule biosynthesis genes but retaining key virulence and resistance determinants. Genomic analyses demonstrated that NT‐Sp strains shared up to 90.6% core genome similarity with encapsulated strains, though lacking capsule synthesis loci. The absence of the cps locus (capsule biosynthesis genes) is the defining molecular feature of NT‐Sp, but other genetic markers distinguish NT‐Sp from encapsulated strains; the frequent presence of zmpC (zinc metalloprotease C, associated with mucosal adhesion) and absence of piaB (pneumococcal iron acquisition B) in NT‐Sp isolates [32] suggest that NT‐Sp has adapted to the mucosal environment through loss of capsule but retention of colonization factors. Accessory gene content, including prophages, surface proteins, and AMR genes varied significantly, reflecting adaptation to specific ecological niches. Some NT‐Sp strains carried resistance determinants such as mefA, ermB, and tetM, further underscoring their role in antimicrobial resistance gene dissemination. The emergence of NT‐Sp may reflect ongoing pneumococcal evolutionary adaptation through capsule switching, capsule loss, and recombination‐mediated genome plasticity under vaccine and antibiotic selective pressure. Standardization remains a challenge, as older serotyping methods frequently misclassified NT‐Sp, and the review highlights the need for consistent application of WGS and MLST, especially in low‐resource settings, to ensure accurate surveillance. The high genomic similarity between NT‐Sp and encapsulated strains suggests that NT‐Sp may serve as a “genetic reservoir” that can exchange genes with encapsulated strains, potentially transferring resistance determinants, virulence factors, or even capsule loci, with implications for vaccine escape and resistance dissemination.

Despite these findings, many studies relied on older phenotypic assays, underestimating NT‐Sp prevalence. Detection tools significantly influenced reported outcomes: studies using modern PCR and WGS methods consistently detected NT‐Sp even when earlier surveys had not. Standardizing detection protocols and increasing the use of molecular tools especially in Africa and Southeast Asia, will be essential for future studies. Importantly, detection methods influenced reported prevalence: studies using enrichment culture plus PCR consistently detected higher NT‐Sp carriage than those relying on Quellung reaction alone. No consistent differences were observed by HIV status: Safari et al. [36] detected NT‐Sp in HIV‐infected Indonesian children, while Anthony et al. [51] found no significant association in Tanzanian HIV‐positive children. Among children with sickle cell disease in Ghana, Dayie et al. [27] reported low NT‐Sp carriage but noted high antimicrobial resistance. Rural settings in The Gambia [11] showed higher carriage than urban areas, suggesting transmission intensity as a key driver. Clinically, NT‐Sp was most frequently associated with conjunctivitis and otitis media [17], with rare invasive disease.

Limitation

Substantial heterogeneity was observed across all meta‐analyses (I 2 consistently > 90%), reflecting variation in study designs, sampling strategies, laboratory methods, and population characteristics that could not be fully explained by subgroup analyses or meta‐regression.

NT‐Sp identification methods varied considerably across studies, ranging from Quellung reaction to whole‐genome sequencing. Detection method subgroup analysis confirmed that multiplex PCR detected significantly higher prevalence (9.48%) compared to WGS (1.06%), suggesting possible misclassification and underestimation in studies relying solely on phenotypic methods.

Antimicrobial resistance data were inconsistently reported, with most studies using phenotypic assays rather than molecular confirmation of resistance genes. Heterogeneity in resistance estimates was substantial (I 2 range: 46.9%–88.2%).

Selection bias may have been introduced as only open‐access and free full‐text articles published in English were included.

NT‐Sp remains underrepresented in invasive disease surveillance, limiting understanding of its role in horizontal gene transfer and AMR gene dissemination.

Risk of bias assessment using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale revealed that six studies (15%) were rated as moderate quality. However, sensitivity analysis (Figure 8) confirmed the robustness of the overall findings (leave‐one‐out estimates: 3.73%–4.83%).

The “All ages” subgroup estimate (29.88%) should be interpreted with caution as it was primarily driven by a single study with exceptionally high NT‐Sp carriage.

Publication bias was not evident (Egger's test: p = 0.8212) (Figure 6), and sensitivity analyses (Figure 8) supported the robustness of our conclusions.

Conclusion

This review reaffirms that non‐typeable Streptococcus pneumoniae (NT‐Sp) is a globally distributed and genetically diverse subset of pneumococci that deserves greater attention in the post‐vaccine era. Despite their historically overlooked status, NT‐Sp strains demonstrate significant carriage prevalence, high levels of antimicrobial resistance, and considerable genetic adaptability across diverse settings and populations.

The persistence of NT‐Sp in both vaccinated and unvaccinated populations, alongside its resistance to multiple antibiotics, positions it as a potentially important reservoir of antimicrobial resistance determinants within pneumococcal populations. Furthermore, the detection of region‐specific clones and the high genomic similarity to encapsulated strains suggest that NT‐Sp may play an important evolutionary role in pneumococcal ecology.

To address these concerns, global pneumococcal surveillance efforts must routinely include NT‐Sp, applying modern molecular tools such as WGS and MLST. These tools not only improve detection but also provide essential data on strain diversity, resistance mechanisms, and potential for vaccine escape. In addition, future vaccine research could explore whether broadly protective protein antigens (e.g., PspA and pneumolysin) currently under development would also cover NT‐Sp, given their high genomic similarity to encapsulated strains. However, the present review did not directly evaluate vaccine candidates, and this remains a hypothetical direction requiring further preclinical investigation.

Based on these findings, the following actions are recommended. For clinical practice: (1) in settings where NT‐Sp carriage prevalence exceeds 10%, laboratories should add molecular confirmation (cpsA‐PCR) for optochin‐sensitive, non‐Quellung‐reactive isolates; (2) empirical antibiotic therapy for recurrent otitis media or suspected pneumococcal carriage‐related infections in high‐NT‐Sp areas should account for local resistance patterns, particularly the high prevalence of co‐trimoxazole resistance (67%). For research: (3) future carriage surveillance studies should routinely report NT‐Sp separately and use standardized definitions (cpsA‐negative and lytA‐positive); (4) WGS should be prioritized in low‐resource settings to track NT‐Sp clones and resistance gene dissemination; (5) longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether NT‐Sp carriage precedes or predicts acquisition of resistant encapsulated strains.

In summary, NT‐Sp is not merely a passive bystander in pneumococcal biology. It is a dynamic and clinically relevant population that requires targeted research, routine monitoring, and strategic inclusion in both antimicrobial and vaccine policy.
