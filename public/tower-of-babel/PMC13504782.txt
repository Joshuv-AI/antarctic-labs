The association between triglyceride-glucose index and major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events in patients with heart failure
Authors: Ling Ren, Dawei Liu, Bo Zhou, Jie Liu, Changqing Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to verify the association between triglyceride-glucose (TyG) and major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) in patients with heart failure (HF).MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study enrolled 927 patients with HF. Participants were categorized into four groups based on TyG index quartiles: Q1 (n = 227), Q2 (n = 237), Q3 (n = 234), and Q4 (n = 229). According to the occurrence of MACCE, they were further classified into non-MACCE (n = 847) and MACCE (n = 80) groups. Cox regression, subgroup analysis, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, Kaplan-Meier survival curves, and restricted cubic spline (RCS) plots were employed to evaluate the association between the TyG index and MACCE risk.ResultsMultivariable Cox regression analysis demonstrated that the TyG index remained independently associated with the risk of MACCE after adjusting for lifestyle factors, comorbidities, treatments, and key laboratory and echocardiographic parameters. Each 1-unit increase in TyG was associated with an 85.4% increase in MACCE risk (HR: 1.336–2.572, P < 0.001). Patients in the highest TyG quartile (Q4) had a 3.679-fold higher risk of MACCE compared to those in the lowest quartile (Q1) (HR: 1.702–7.956, P = 0.001). Subgroup analyses revealed that the association was more pronounced in females, patients aged > 75 years, those with chronic HF, hypertension, and those without chronic kidney disease (P for interaction = 0.021 and 0.035 for gender and hypertension, respectively). Sensitivity analyses addressing endpoint heterogeneity showed a stronger association between TyG and “hard endpoints” (HR per unit: 2.01, P < 0.001), with significant net reclassification improvement at 12 months. ROC analysis indicated a modest predictive value of TyG for MACCE (AUC = 0.587, 95% CI: 0.525–0.649, P = 0.010). Kaplan-Meier curves showed significant differences in cumulative event risk across TyG groups (Log-rank P = 0.025). RCS analysis indicated a significant positive linear dose-response relationship between TyG and MACCE risk (P for nonlinearity > 0.05).ConclusionThe TyG index exhibits a significant positive linear association with the risk of MACCE in patients with HF. As a simple and readily accessible metabolic marker, the TyG index may provide supplementary prognostic information when considered alongside established clinical risk factors in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-026-01369-9.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a clinical syndrome resulting from abnormal cardiac structure and function caused by various reasons, which leads to impaired cardiac pumping function and inability to meet the metabolic needs of tissues, and thus causes damage to the patient’s health and quality of life [1]. It has the characteristics of high incidence and high mortality [1]. According to statistics, as of 2019, there were approximately 56.19 million HF patients worldwide, among which China had approximately 8.9 million, accounting for 15.6% of the global HF patients, which not only affects the physical health and quality of life of patients but also brings a huge economic burden to the nation [2, 3]. Currently, significant progress has been made in the treatment of HF, such as sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), vildagliptin, and cardiac resynchronization therapy, etc [4]. However, the prognosis of patients is still not satisfactory, especially the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) has been increasing year by year, which is one of the main reasons for the death and disability of HF patients.

Current evidence suggests that insulin resistance (IR) plays a very important role in the development of HF [5]. The triglyceride-glucose index (TyG), an important surrogate marker for reflecting IR, can be calculated from fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and triglyceride (TG) results, and it has been found to be closely associated with the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and its adverse outcomes [6–8]. For example, the study by Kurniawan et al. found that the TyG index can not only assess metabolic syndrome but also serve as a CVD predictor, and it is significantly associated with the prognosis of CVD [7]. In addition, the study by Tan et al. showed that the TyG index is closely related to the incidence of atherosclerosis and hypertension [9]. Moreover, some studies have found that there is a certain association between the TyG index and the risk of HF and cardiovascular events [10, 11]. However, currently, there are few studies on the association between the TyG index and HF, and no study has systematically explored the association between the TyG index and MACCE in HF patients. Therefore, based on the above research foundation, this study aimed to evaluate the association between the TyG index and MACCE in HF patients, providing reference value for clinical diagnosis and treatment.

Methods

Research subjects

In this single-center retrospective cohort study, 927 patients with HF who visited the People’s Hospital of Bishan District, Chongqing from January 2021 to December 2024 were included. Inclusion criteria: (1) Age ≥ 18 years; (2) Clear diagnosis of HF, including chronic HF and acute HF [12]. Exclusion criteria: (1) Severe liver and kidney dysfunction; (2) Severe mental and neurological diseases, patients who could not cooperate with follow-up; (3) Patients with intellectual disability who could not cooperate; (4) Patients without FPG and TG detection data; (5) Patients with incomplete clinical data; (6) Patients who were lost to follow-up. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Bishan Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, and the study protocol conformed to the basic principles of the Helsinki Declaration. Since this was a retrospective study, all data were anonymous, and therefore the informed consent of patients was waived by the Ethics Committee of Bishan Hospital of Chongqing Medical University.

Data collection

This study collected demographic data, anthropometric data, comorbidities, drug treatment data, and blood biomarker data for statistical analysis from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. Demographic data included age, gender, and smoking status. Anthropometric data included height, weight, body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and heart rate. Comorbidities included family history of coronary heart disease (CHD), diabetes, hypertension, CHD, atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease (CKD), hyperlipidemia, and HF type. Previous medications included hypoglycemic drugs, lipid-lowering drugs, antihypertensive drugs, and antithrombotic drugs.

Blood hematology indicators were measured in the hospital’s laboratory by trained laboratory professionals according to standard experimental procedures. Blood routine indicators included white blood cell count (laser flow cytometry + fluorescence staining detection technology, 3.5–9.5 × 109/L), neutrophil count (laser flow cytometry + fluorescence staining detection technology, 1.8–6.3 × 109/L), monocyte count (laser flow cytometry + fluorescence staining detection technology, 0.1–0.6 × 109/L), lymphocyte count (laser flow cytometry + fluorescence staining detection technology, 1.1–3.2 × 109/L), hemoglobin (chemical colorimetry, 130–175 g/L), hematocrit (shank resistive impedance method, 40%-50%), platelet count (shank resistive impedance method, 125–360 × 109/L). Routine biochemical indicators were determined using the ARCHITECT c16000 automatic biochemical analyzer, including TG (0-1.7 mmol/L, enzymatic method), total cholesterol (0-5.18 mmol/L, enzymatic method), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) (0-3.37 mmol/L, enzymatic method), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) (1.16–1.42 mmol/L, enzymatic method), apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1) (1.2–1.6 g/L, immunoturbidimetry), apolipoprotein B (ApoB) (0.6–1.2 g/L, immunoturbidimetry), lipoprotein(a) (0-300 mg/L, latex-enhanced immunoturbidimetry), alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (9–50 U/L, enzymatic method), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (14–50 U/L, enzymatic method), total bilirubin (0–26 umol/L, vanadium oxidation method), albumin (40–55 g/L, bromocresol green method), FPG (3.9–6.1 mmol/L, hexokinase method), uric acid (uricase method, 208–428 umol/L), urea nitrogen (urease-glutamate dehydrogenase method, 3.6–9.5 mmol/L), creatinine (enzymatic method, 57–111 umol/L), potassium (indirect ion-selective electrode method, 3.5–5.5 mmol/L), calcium (azinocresol III method, 2.11–2.52 mmol/L), and sodium (indirect ion-selective electrode method, 137–147 mmol/L). In addition, the following parameters were also collected: high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (Hs-CRP) (immuno-turbidimetric assay, 0–3 mg/L), glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) (high-performance liquid chromatography, 3.6%-6.0%), fibrinogen (coagulation method, 1.8–3.5 g/L), D-dimer (immuno-turbidimetric assay, 0.00-0.55 mg/L), N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) (electrochemiluminescence method, 0-450 pg/mL), creatine kinase isoenzyme (CKMB) (immunosuppression method, 25–58 ng/mL), troponin T (electrochemiluminescence method, 0–14 pg/mL). The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated based on the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) formula [13].

In this study, TyG was regarded as an exposure factor: TyG = Ln(FPG (mg/dL) * TG (mg/dL) / 2) [14]. According to the quartiles of TyG, the subjects were divided into four groups: Q1: TyG ≤ 8.26 (n = 227), Q2: 8.26 < TyG ≤ 8.60 (n = 237); Q3: 8.60 < TyG ≤ 9.02 (n = 234); Q4: TyG > 9.02 (n = 229).

Echocardiography was evaluated by cardiologists specializing in cardiology. Using the GE vivid E9 color Doppler echocardiography system (with probe M5S, frequency 1.7–3.3 MHz), the cardiac structure and function of patients with HF were assessed. All examinations were conducted by dedicated echocardiographers. In case of inconsistency, a third expert was sought for evaluation. If still inconsistent, the average value of the three was taken. This study mainly included the left atrial diameter (LAD), left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVDD), interventricular septal thickness diameter (IVSTD), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and aortic valve calcification (AVC) for analysis.

Follow-up

All patients were followed up through telephone calls, outpatient visits, and inpatient records, starting from the date of discharge and continuing until death or December 31, 2024. The follow-up outcome was MACCE, which included cardiac death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, non-fatal cerebral stroke, unplanned revascularization, rehospitalization due to worsening HF, rehospitalization due to worsening angina pectoris. Cardiac death was defined as death due to cardiac causes or death without a clear cause. Non-fatal myocardial infarction was defined as myocardial necrosis due to coronary artery obstruction, but the patient was able to survive the acute phase through timely treatment or the establishment of collateral circulation, and did not directly die from this myocardial infarction. Non-fatal cerebral stroke was defined as a type of cerebrovascular disease caused by sudden rupture or obstruction of cerebral vessels, resulting in local brain tissue damage, and the patient survived after the onset. Non-planned revascularization was defined as revascularization through non-coronary artery intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) after which, due to stent thrombosis, restenosis, or occlusion of the bypass vessel, the patient experienced myocardial ischemia symptoms again or imaging examination showed evidence of myocardial ischemia, and required urgent or elective revascularization. Rehospitalization due to worsening HF was defined as patients previously diagnosed with heart failure who were discharged after stable treatment and whose symptoms and signs of HF worsened again due to various reasons and required further treatment at the hospital. Rehospitalization due to worsening angina pectoris was defined as patients previously diagnosed with angina pectoris who were discharged after relatively stable treatment and whose symptoms of angina pectoris recurred and worsened, the frequency increased, the duration prolonged, or the activity level that triggered angina pectoris decreased significantly, and thus required further examination, diagnosis, and treatment at the hospital. Recognizing the clinical heterogeneity among the components of the composite MACCE endpoint, we performed supplementary analyses to explore the association of the TyG index with different event types. Specifically, we categorized the endpoints into ‘hard endpoints’ (a composite of cardiac death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, and non-fatal stroke) and ‘soft endpoints’ (a composite of unplanned revascularization, rehospitalization due to worsening heart failure, and rehospitalization due to worsening angina). The distribution of the first MACCE event is presented in Supplementary Table 1. The associations of the TyG index (both as a continuous variable and across quartiles) with these categorized endpoints were analyzed using Cox proportional hazards models with the same adjustment sets as the primary analysis (see Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).

Relevant definitions

Smoking: Continuous or cumulative smoking for ≥ 6 months throughout one’s lifetime. Family history of CHD: Presence of CHD in a first-degree relative. Diabetes: (1) FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL); (2) Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) 2-hour postprandial blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL); (3) Random blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) accompanied by typical symptoms (such as polydipsia, polyuria, weight loss); (4) HbA1c ≥ 6.5% [15]. Hypertension: Three measurements of SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg under non-consecutive conditions [16]. CHD: Heart disease caused by coronary artery stenosis or obstruction, resulting in insufficient myocardial blood supply and clinical manifestations such as angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, etc [17]. Atrial fibrillation: Disordered atrial electrical activity, leading to rapid and irregular atrial contractions, manifested by disappearance of normal sinus P wave and substitution with irregular baseline fluctuations (f waves) on electrocardiogram, with a frequency typically ranging from 350 to 600 beats/min, and irregular R-R intervals [18]. CKD: Abnormalities in renal structure and function caused by various reasons, or eGFR decline of unknown cause, with a disease duration of more than 3 months [13]. Hyperlipidemia: Total cholesterol ≥ 6.2 mmol/L, or (and) TG ≥ 2.3 mmol/L, or (and) LDL-C ≥ 4.1 mmol/L [19]. HF: A group of syndromes characterized by impaired ventricular filling and/or ejection capacity due to any cardiac structural or functional abnormalities, resulting in pulmonary and/or systemic congestion and insufficient blood perfusion to organs and tissues as clinical manifestations [12].

Statistical methods

All data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.4.3. Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation if normally distributed (assessed by Shapiro-Wilk test) or as median (interquartile range) otherwise. Comparisons across TyG quartiles were performed using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as n (%) and compared using the chi-square test.

Univariate Cox regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with MACCE. To evaluate the independent association between the TyG index and MACCE, we constructed three multivariable Cox proportional hazards models with progressive adjustment: Model 1: Adjusted for basic lifestyle and treatment factors (smoking, antihypertensive drugs, lipid-lowering drugs). Model 2: Further adjusted for major cardiovascular comorbidities and interventions (coronary heart disease, atrial fibrillation, antithrombotic drugs, percutaneous coronary intervention). Model 3 (Primary fully adjusted model): Additionally adjusted for key laboratory and echocardiographic prognostic indicators: urea nitrogen (renal function), glycosylated hemoglobin (metabolic control), fibrinogen (coagulation status), left atrial diameter, and left ventricular ejection fraction (cardiac structure and function). These covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and their established association with outcomes in heart failure. In Model 3, there were 80 MACCE events and 12 adjusted covariates, yielding an events-per-variable (EPV) ratio of approximately 6.7. To address potential overfitting, we also performed a Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) Cox regression analysis with 10-fold cross-validation.

Subgroup analyses were performed by stratifying patients based on age, gender, heart failure type, hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease. The predictive performance of the TyG index for MACCE was assessed using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and the incremental prognostic value was quantified by calculating the Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) at 12 months, comparing a baseline clinical model with a model that included the TyG index. Cumulative event risks across TyG quartiles were visualized using Kaplan-Meier survival curves and compared with the log-rank test. The shape of the dose-response relationship between the continuous TyG index and MACCE risk was examined using restricted cubic splines (RCS) with four knots.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics according to TyG grouping

As shown in Table 1, there were 927 individuals in the total population. According to the TyG index, they were divided into four groups: Q1 (n = 227), Q2 (n = 237), Q3 (n = 234), and Q4 (n = 229). The results indicated that the probabilities of diabetes, CHD, hyperlipidemia, hypoglycemic drugs, lipid-lowering drugs, antithrombotic drugs, and PCI were statistically different among the four groups (P < 0.05). Age, BMI, white blood cell count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, platelet count, TG, total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, ApoB, AST, total bilirubin, albumin, blood calcium, FPG, HbA1c, D-dimer, and troponin T levels also showed statistically significant differences among the four groups (P < 0.05). However, the remaining indicators showed no statistically significant differences among the four groups (P > 0.05).

Association between TyG index and MACCE

In the fully adjusted Model 3, we included 12 covariates against 80 observed MACCE events, yielding an events-per-variable (EPV) ratio of approximately 6.7. As shown in Table 2, in Model 1 which only adjusted for smoking, antihypertensive drugs, and lipid-lowering drugs, for every unit increase in TyG, the risk of MACCE increased by 48.5% (HR: 1.091–2.022, P = 0.012); in addition, the risk of MACCE in Q3 and Q4 groups was 2.294 times (HR: 1.055–4.986, P = 0.036) and 2.700 times (HR: 1.263–5.773, P = 0.010) that of Q1 group. In Model 2 which further adjusted for CHD, antithrombotic drugs, PCI, and atrial fibrillation, TyG was still significantly correlated with the risk of MACCE, where for every unit increase in TyG, the risk of MACCE increased by 76.6% (HR: 1.286–2.424, P < 0.001). Moreover, the risk of MACCE in Q2, Q3, and Q4 groups was 2.475 times (HR: 1.137–5.385, P = 0.022), 2.541 times (HR: 1.169–5.524, P = 0.019), and 3.727 times (HR: 1.737–7.995, P = 0.001) that of Q1 group. In Model 3 which further adjusted for urea nitrogen, HbA1c, fibrinogen, LAD, and LVEF based on Model 2, the TyG index was still independently associated with the risk of MACCE, where for every unit increase in TyG, the risk of MACCE increased by 85.4% (HR: 1.336–2.572, P < 0.001), and the risk of MACCE in Q4 group was 3.679 times (HR: 11.702–7.956, P = 0.001) that of Q1 group.

As shown in Table 3, the subjects were stratified into 12 subgroups based on gender, age, type of HF, diabetes, hypertension, and CKD. Sensitivity analyses using categorized endpoints

To address the heterogeneity of the composite MACCE endpoint, sensitivity analyses were performed using categorized endpoints (see Supplementary Table 1 for event distribution). When analyzed as a continuous variable, the TyG index showed a strong and statistically significant association with the risk of hard endpoints across all adjustment models (fully adjusted Model 3: HR 2.01 per 1-unit increase, 95% CI 1.33–3.04, P < 0.001). The association with soft endpoints was positive but attenuated and of borderline statistical significance in the fully adjusted model (Model 3: HR 1.38, 95% CI 0.99–1.92, P = 0.056) (Supplementary Table 2). Analysis by TyG quartiles revealed a significant dose-response relationship for both hard and soft endpoints (P for trend = 0.002 and 0.035, respectively). Notably, patients in the highest TyG quartile (Q4) had a nearly 4-fold increased risk of hard endpoints compared to those in the lowest quartile (Model 3: HR 3.95, 95% CI 1.82–8.58, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 3). We further assessed the improvement in risk prediction afforded by the TyG index using reclassification statistics at 12 months (Supplementary Table 4). The addition of the TyG index to the baseline clinical model resulted in a statistically significant net reclassification improvement for both hard endpoints (NRI = 0.152, 95% CI: 0.085–0.219, P < 0.001) and soft endpoints (NRI = 0.072, 95% CI: 0.006–0.138, P = 0.032). The integrated discrimination improvement was significant for hard endpoints (IDI = 0.0083, P = 0.017) but not for soft endpoints (IDI = 0.0038, P = 0.229). Results of the LASSO Cox regression, a method designed to prevent overfitting, are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index was retained as a significant predictor in the final model. The cross-validated concordance index (C-index) for this model was 0.723.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the association between the TyG index and the risk of MACCE in patients with HF. Using multiple complementary analytical approaches, we demonstrated that the TyG index was independently associated with MACCE across fully adjusted models. Higher TyG levels were consistently associated with an increased risk of MACCE, with patients in the highest TyG quartile exhibiting a markedly elevated risk compared with those in the lowest quartile. These findings suggest that the TyG index, as a simple and readily available metabolic marker, may provide complementary prognostic information to established clinical risk factors in patients with HF. However, given the observational design, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require validation in external cohorts before any clinical application can be recommended.

In recent years, numerous studies have focused on the predictive role of the TyG index in diseases such as atherosclerosis, coronary artery disease, and stroke [20–22]. However, most of these studies have concentrated on the general population or individuals with metabolic syndrome, hypertension, or diabetes. In contrast, research specifically targeting the unique population of HF patients remains relatively scarce. For example, in a prospective cohort study involving 7,335 elderly individuals, Dui et al. found a significant association between the TyG index and the risk of HF, suggesting that the TyG index could serve as an effective tool for screening high-risk HF among older adults in the community [23]. Similarly, in a cross-sectional study of 13,825 participants with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, Zhang et al. reported a positive association between elevated TyG index levels and HF risk [24]. After full adjustment for confounding variables, each one-unit increase in the TyG index was associated with an approximate 5.7% increase in HF risk, indicating its potential as a key predictive factor and target for intervention. In addition, a retrospective study by Chen et al., which included 1,537 patients with both HF and CKD, demonstrated that a higher TyG index was an independent risk factor for all-cause mortality both during hospitalization and within one year post-discharge [25]. This relationship showed a non-linear positive association, highlighting the prognostic value of the TyG index as a practical marker for mortality risk assessment in this high-risk population. Furthermore, Zhou et al., in a retrospective study of 1,329 patients with severe chronic HF, found that the TyG index was significantly associated with 5-year all-cause mortality [26]. Although the TyG index did not significantly enhance the predictive power of the baseline model, it showed greater potential for risk stratification and prognostic evaluation, suggesting its role as a superior insulin resistance-related predictor of mortality in critically ill chronic HF patients.

Building upon existing evidence, the present study introduces the TyG index into the field of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular event research among HF patients. In contemporary clinical practice, prognostic stratification in acute cardiovascular conditions often relies on multivariable clinical risk scores integrating demographic characteristics, comorbidities, and laboratory parameters. Previous studies have shown that such validated risk scores, including those originally developed for heart failure, may retain prognostic value when applied to other acute cardiovascular settings. Notably, the heart failure–derived MAGGIC risk score has been reported to predict outcomes in patients with acute pulmonary embolism, illustrating the cross-context applicability of established prognostic models [27]. Through rigorous statistical analyses, it systematically reveals a significant positive association between the TyG index and the risk of MACCE, thereby expanding the clinical applicability of the TyG index. In this context, the TyG index should be regarded not as a substitute for multivariable clinical risk scores, but as a simplified metabolic marker reflecting insulin resistance and systemic metabolic stress, which may provide complementary prognostic information beyond traditional clinical variables.

To further explore the potential heterogeneity of the TyG-MACCE association, we conducted comprehensive subgroup analyses. The results suggested that the positive association might be more pronounced in specific populations, such as females, elderly patients (> 75 years), and those with hypertension but without CKD. Notably, interaction tests indicated that the effect modification was statistically significant for gender and hypertension status. Stratified analysis by diabetes status further demonstrated that an elevated TyG index was an independent risk factor for MACCE among patients with diabetes, whereas no significant association was observed in non-diabetic patients. This finding suggests that the prognostic impact of the TyG index may differ according to underlying metabolic status, potentially reflecting distinct pathophysiological mechanisms in diabetic and non-diabetic HF populations. These findings are consistent with previous studies highlighting the impact of sex differences and metabolic-inflammatory aging on HF outcomes [28, 29]. However, some subgroups contained a limited number of MACCE events, increasing the risk of type I error. Furthermore, except for gender and hypertension, the interactions for other stratifying factors were not statistically significant. Therefore, these subgroup results should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. In particular, the absence of a significant association in non-diabetic patients should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited number of events in this subgroup, and requires validation in larger cohorts.

Additionally, this study employed Kaplan-Meier curves and RCS plot to visually analyze the risk trends of MACCE across different TyG index levels, making the conclusions more intuitive and compelling. Furthermore, to address the clinical heterogeneity within the composite MACCE endpoint, we conducted sensitivity analyses by categorizing events into ‘hard endpoints’ and ‘soft endpoints’. Notably, the TyG index exhibited an even stronger association with hard endpoints (fully adjusted HR 2.01 per unit increase) than with the overall MACCE, and patients in the highest TyG quartile faced a nearly 4-fold increased risk of hard endpoints. This pattern suggests that the prognostic power of the TyG index is robust for predicting severe, irreversible cardiovascular complications in HF patients. Based on these findings, this study not only fills a gap in HF-related research but also provides a theoretical foundation for individualized risk assessment strategies.

The TyG index, as a surrogate marker of IR, is closely associated with various adverse cardiovascular mechanisms. IR can promote the occurrence of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events through multiple pathways, including the induction of chronic inflammation, exacerbation of oxidative stress, impairment of endothelial function, and facilitation of atherosclerosis development [30–32]. Moreover, elevated TyG levels typically reflect states of hypertriglyceridemia and hyperglycemia, both of which can lead to the production of small, dense low-density lipoprotein (sdLDL), thereby increasing atherogenic potential. In patients with HF, myocardial metabolic disturbances are already prominent. When accompanied by metabolic disorders represented by a high TyG index, this may further aggravate myocardial energy deficiency and structural remodeling, thereby increasing the risk of MACCE. In the present study, the association between TyG and MACCE was particularly significant in the elderly population, which may be closely related to their reduced metabolic adaptability and elevated baseline levels of inflammation. Li et al., in the Kailuan study, also pointed out that the age of HF onset is strongly associated with all-cause mortality, suggesting that patients with HF at different ages may have distinct metabolic profiles and outcome patterns—findings that provide mechanistic support for the heightened risk observed in the elderly subgroup in our study [33]. Although the underlying biological mechanisms require further validation, these insights offer a strong theoretical foundation for understanding the link between the TyG index and MACCE. After exploring the potential mechanisms, it is also important to acknowledge some limitations inherent in this study.

Several important limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively limited sample size (n = 927), which may introduce selection bias and limits the generalizability of our findings. Second, despite extensive multivariable adjustment, residual confounding remains a major concern. The TyG index is closely related to multiple metabolic factors, including diabetes status, triglyceride and glucose levels, lipid metabolism, body mass index, medication use (hypoglycemic and lipid-lowering agents), and overall metabolic health. Even with adjustment for these factors, unmeasured or incompletely measured confounders—such as dietary habits, physical activity level, medication adherence, and duration of metabolic abnormalities—may partly explain the observed association. Third, while the TyG index serves as a surrogate marker of insulin resistance, it does not directly measure insulin sensitivity, and the lack of HOMA-IR data in this cohort precluded direct comparison between the TyG index and more established IR metrics. Fourth, the absence of an external validation cohort means that the clinical applicability of the TyG index for risk stratification remains uncertain, and the modest AUC (0.587) suggests that TyG alone is insufficient for outcome prediction. Fifth, the composite MACCE endpoint is clinically heterogeneous; although we performed sensitivity analyses for hard vs. soft endpoints, the number of events in some subgroups was limited, leading to wide confidence intervals and potential instability of estimates. Sixth, because echocardiographic data were incomplete in certain subgroups and event numbers were limited, we were unable to perform robust stratified analyses according to HF phenotype (HFrEF vs. HFpEF). Finally, the observational design precludes any causal inference; the associations reported here should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Future prospective, multicenter studies with larger sample sizes and external validation are needed to confirm these findings. Recognizing these limitations not only aids in a more balanced interpretation of the study’s conclusions but also provides clear directions for improving the design of future research.

Conclusion

This study identified a positive association between the TyG index and the risk of MACCE in patients with HF. These findings suggest that the TyG index, as a simple and readily accessible metabolic marker, may provide complementary prognostic information when considered alongside established clinical risk factors. However, given the observational, single-center design and the modest discriminatory ability of the TyG index (AUC = 0.587), the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Several key directions for future research include: First, large-scale, multicenter, prospective studies with external validation cohorts are needed to confirm these associations; Second, investigations integrating the TyG index with other biomarkers and established risk scores to assess its incremental prognostic value; Third, exploration of the underlying molecular mechanisms linking insulin resistance to adverse cardiovascular outcomes in HF patients. At present, the TyG index should not be promoted as a standalone clinical tool, but rather as a potential adjunctive metabolic indicator for research purposes until its clinical utility is validated in further studies.
