A comparison of human and LLM-simulated participants in a writing style task
Felix Gröner, Erin K. Chiou

Abstract
Because large language models (LLMs) can produce natural language that is sometimes indistinguishable from texts produced by people, some researchers are starting to consider replacing human participants with LLM simulations. In this study, we test the extent to which the findings of a simulation with an LLM prompted to act as a synthetic participant match those obtained from 30 human participants. In our experiments, we evaluated how well writing style preference inference algorithms adapted to a participant over repeated interactions, compared to a baseline. We discover hints of bias and a lack of depth in GPT-4o’s text generation and judgement that prevent it from accurately simulating people’s behavior. Our results also hint at human biases that highlight the importance of considering human factors in the evaluation of systems that depend on human-automation interaction. Rather than treating these discrepancies as evidence for or against the validity of LLM-simulated participants, we present this study as a case analysis of methodological and design challenges.



1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) can produce texts that are superficially indistinguishable from those produced by people. This raises the question whether their reasoning, decision-making, and behavior are sufficiently similar that simulations with LLM-simulated participants lead to the same findings and conclusions as experiments with human participants. If this were the case, LLM-powered simulations could drastically lower the cost and increase the speed of research. Some research groups have started to empirically test novel algorithms and systems in simulations in which LLMs are prompted to act like people interacting with the system. However, we are concerned to what extent such simulations and the findings derived from them generalize to a population of real people.

To investigate this issue, we compared the results of a simulation and an experiment with human participants using a writing style preference inference paradigm. This use-case is particularly interesting for our research question because it places higher requirements on LLMs to faithfully replicate people’s behavior.

We found that our synthetic participants could not sufficiently simulate people’s writing and editing behaviors. In our setup, GPT-4o failed to replicate human biases and introduced its own biases, leading to a discrepancy between the findings from the simulation and the experiment with human participants. We present our findings not as a general rejection of LLM simulations as a research method but as a cautionary tale of experimental design issue surrounding them.

2
Related work

2.1
LLMs simulating people’s behavior

Several studies have tested the viability of LLM-powered simulations for replicating human participants. A common question is how closely the responses and behaviors of LLMs resemble those of people. A review by
Lu et al., 2024
provides several examples of experiments that were successfully replicated by LLM agents. LLMs were also able to match people’s responses in economic games
(
Horton, 2023
;
Xie et al., 2024
)
, movie selections
(
Wang et al., 2025a
)
, moral judgements
(
Dillion et al., 2023
)
, and political settings
(
Argyle et al., 2023
)
. A common finding is that simulations come close, but are not perfect. They might correctly predict the effect direction but not its strength
(
Manning et al., 2024
)
or have impressive but insufficient accuracy
(
Aher et al., 2023
;
Cui et al., 2025
;
Petrov et al., 2024
)
. Many studies have also reported significant differences between models
(
Aher et al., 2023
;
Petrov et al., 2024
;
Xie et al., 2024
)
.

Most of these studies aimed to replicate general, not individual, human behavior on a rather superficial level.
Petrov et al., 2024
showed that GPT-4 fails to replicate a specific individual’s responses to psychometric questionnaires when provided with the person’s extensive biographic information. Simulated users may not reflect real human variability, biases, motivations, or constraints
(
Cheng et al., 2023
)
. In fact, they are likely to introduce their own biases and constraints, such as sensitivity to changes in prompts, hyper accuracy, and biases stemming from the training data as well as the network architecture
(
Aher et al., 2023
;
Anthis et al., 2025
;
Wang et al., 2025b
;
Yang et al., 2024
;
Ye et al., 2024
;
Zheng et al., 2023
)
.

These insights from related work lead us to hypothesize that current state-of-the-art LLMs cannot accurately simulate human behavior in all domains and settings. We aim to establish whether this is the case for writing style preferences in article summarization tasks.

2.2
Preference inference for LLM personalization

The purpose of personalization is to provide a better user experience by producing responses tailored to a specific individual rather than generic ones. In other words, the desired output for the same prompt may differ amongst individuals. Thus, a one-size-fits-all output introduces inefficiencies by requiring some people to spend additional editing effort until the generic output matches the personally desired output. Several approaches have been attempted to personalize the output of LLMs, which can be roughly divided into fine-tuning
(
Stiennon et al., 2020
;
Tan et al., 2025
;
Zhuang et al., 2024
)
and in-context prompting
(
Dong et al., 2024
;
Lin et al., 2023
;
Salemi et al., 2024
;
Shen et al., 2024
)
. More rigorously developed taxonomies can be found in dedicated reviews
(
Liu et al., 2025
;
Zhao et al., 2025
;
Jiang et al., 2024
;
Zhang et al., 2024
)
.

To evaluate how well a system can infer people’s implicit writing styles,
Gao et al., 2024
introduced the PRELUDE (PREference Learning from User’s Direct Edits) framework in which an LLM observes an individual’s revisions across multiple writing tasks (e.g., summarizing articles, composing emails) and adapts future outputs with the goal to minimize the editing effort. They compare different algorithms and find that a combination of retrieving context-specific examples and inferring preferences in a separate step before generating the summary resulted in the lowest editing effort
(
Gao et al., 2024
)
.

This setup has been criticized for merely requiring participants to edit summaries rather than write them from scratch
(
Aroca-Ouellette et al., 2024
)
. The core argument is that pre-existing summaries will prime people and heavily influence their stylistic choices. This form of the anchoring bias would lead individuals to come to an acceptable outcome rather than the best outcome with the least amount of effort. It seems plausible that this effect would be stronger for human editors than for simulated editors. However, requiring people to write summaries from scratch to optimize the inference process is likely to worsen the user experience.

3
Experiment 1: Simulation

To identify possible shortcomings of LLM simulations, and gather evidence for or against the validity of such research methods, we conducted two similar experiments with synthetic participants (i.e., an LLM prompted to simulate a user) and real participants. This study is heavily inspired by the work of Gao et al., although it is not a faithful replication. While we used the performance of different algorithms as the dependent variable in both experiments, this comparison was not our main focus. Instead, our research question is to what extent the findings of the two experiments match and in which details they differ. Therefore, we used simplified versions of the PRELUDE framework and preference inference algorithms.

3.1
Methods

For the first experiment, we set up a simulation with synthetic participants to compare different personalization algorithms. The three core steps repeated in the simulation were as follows:

1.

The system generates a summary for an article.

2.

The user checks whether the summary is appropriate for their target audience.

3.

If the user decides that the summary must be edited, they generate a revised version.

4.

Depending on the algorithm, the pair of original and edited summaries is used to infer preferences which are taken into consideration for future summary generations.

We collected 20 articles from Wikipedia and edited them to be of approximately equal length (ranging from 136 to 293 words; full list is provided in Appendix
A
). The order of articles was randomized for each simulation run. We used OpenAI’s
gpt-4o-2024-08-06

(
OpenAI, 2024
)
with a temperature of 1.0 for all generations. The simulation has two components: first, the system that generates the summaries, observes the changes made by the user, infers the writing style preferences, and then takes those into consideration for the next summary; and second, the synthetic user who receives a summary and edits it if it does not match their preferences.

For each article, an ad-hoc summary was generated using one of three algorithms, which are described in detail below. The summary was then passed on to a simulated user who was tasked with ensuring that it is appropriate for one of four target audiences, which are also described below. We repeated this to have three runs across the 20 articles for each of the four target audiences for each of the three algorithms. The personas and prompt templates are heavily inspired by those originally used by
(
Gao et al., 2024
)
as well as revisions in
(
Aroca-Ouellette et al., 2024
)
, and can be found in Appendices
B
and
C
.

3.1.1
Synthetic participants and target audiences

We followed Gao et al.’s two-step design for the editing agents. In a first step, the LLM is prompted to decide whether a given original summary is appropriate for its designated target audience. If it responds with “no,” then it is prompted to edit the summary to make it appropriate.

Each synthetic user is assigned to one target audience and does not change their style preferences over the course of the 20 articles. This is a simplification of the PRELUDE setup in Gao et al. but is sufficient for our purposes. The four target audiences “young children watching TV,” “high-school students in class,” “managers and CEOs,” and “young adults on social media” were designed and accompanied by instructions to induce between-group variance in appropriate writing styles.

3.1.2
Summary-generating system

We compared two preference inference algorithms (in-context learning and rolling preferences) and a baseline (no personalization). We curated this selection to include conditions for which prior work found significant performance differences
(
Aroca-Ouellette et al., 2024
;
Gao et al., 2024
)
. The no-personalization baseline simply produces a summary without inferring or considering the user’s preferences. The two preference inference algorithms are illustrated in Figure
1
. For in-context learning (ICL), the five previous pairs of original and edited summaries were inserted into the prompt as examples to guide the generation of the next summary. This format leverages LLMs’ ability to infer patterns and rules from examples
(
Brown et al., 2020
)
. In this algorithm, the preferences are never explicitly stated.

Rolling preferences (RP) is prompted in three steps. It keeps a list of the inferred preferences for a specific user (initially empty) and considers them when generating the summary. It is then prompted with the original and edited versions of a summary to infer the preferences from the edits made by the user in this specific case. Finally, the general preferences of this user are updated by consolidating them with the newly inferred preferences.

Note that these two inference algorithms are similar to those presented in prior works
(
Aroca-Ouellette et al., 2024
;
Gao et al., 2024
)
but do not reflect the state-of-the-art. For simplification, we omitted the context-relevant retrieval used in these studies to adapt to changing preferences.

3.1.3
Metrics

Figure 1:
Comparison of preference inference algorithms.

We used two metrics to evaluate the different algorithms: normalized Levenshtein distance and BERTScore. The two reference texts were always the original and edited summaries. Levenshtein distance is defined as the minimal number of operations (insertions, deletions, and substitutions) required to change one text to another
(
Levenshtein, 1966
)
. We calculate this on the token level using
cl100k_base
encoding. This can be interpreted as a proxy for the user’s editing effort to convert the initial summary into an appropriate style. BERTScore is a measure of semantic similarity between two texts
(
Zhang et al., 2020
)
. In this study, we used the F1 score produced by the
roberta-large
model. This can be interpreted as reflecting the match of information included in the summaries. Using these metrics allowed us to evaluate the hundreds of summary pairs from this experiment more quickly, cheaply, consistently, and objectively than manual scoring.

4
Experiment 2: Human participants

We replicated the setup of the first experiment with human participants instead of simulated users. We used the insights from the simulation to reduce the scope (number of conditions and workload for participants) of this experiment.

4.1
Methods

4.1.1
Procedure

Participants were tasked with reading 10 ad-hoc generated summaries and revising them if necessary to make them appropriate for a specific target audience. We opted for this reduced number of summaries after observing the early stagnation in the simulations. These articles were drawn from the original set of articles (indicated in Appendix
A
) and made available to participants even though they were not necessary to complete the task.

Participants accessed the online study through a custom-built platform. We did not monitor or restrict the environment in which participants completed the study or which devices they used. On each page, participants were presented with the original article, the ad-hoc generated summary, and a reminder of their target audience. Participants rated the AI summary’s appropriateness for their assigned audience on a 0–100 scale, then revised the summary in a pre-filled text box to better suit their audience’s needs. Instructions given to participants are provided in Appendix
H
and a screenshot of participants’ view during the experiment is shown in Figure
9
of Appendix
I
.

4.1.2
Participants and conditions

We recruited 76 English-speaking adults (aged 18 years or older) from the United States to participate in this online study. Sixty participants were recruited through Prolific and paid $6 for a 30-minute task. The remaining 16 participants were drawn from a pool of undergraduate students and compensated with course credit. This research complied with the American Psychological Association Code of Ethics and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Arizona State University. Informed consent was obtained digitally from all participants by requiring them to check a box before continuing with the study.

We reduced the number of conditions to a
3\times 2
design (
algorithms\times target~audiences
). Participants were randomly distributed across three conditions that implemented the baseline, ICL, and RP algorithms, and assigned to one of two target audiences. The two target audiences for this experiment, “kids watching TV” and “busy managers”, were taken from the ones in the simulation and slightly adapted to include the most salient aspects of the omitted ones. Appendix
B
provides their descriptions.

Of the recruited participants, 64 completed the study. We excluded 18 people who completed the task unreasonably quickly (<5 minutes). This would not have been enough time to read the instructions and all 10 AI summaries, even if they were already in an appropriate style. An additional 16 participants were excluded for not making any changes to the first summary, which we took as an indicator that they did not take the task seriously. We assume that the first summary (for which there are no inferred preferences yet) always required edits. To minimize concerns that this assumption compromises the experiment, we provide the results of less conservative filtering in Appendix
J
, showing that conclusions are not significantly impacted.

The final sample size was 30 participants. These exclusions disproportionally affected the RP conditions, especially for the target audience of children. This might be due to longer loading times that participants experienced because this algorithm requires an additional inference step. In our recruitment, we aimed to even out the distribution of participants across groups, which resulted in the three group sizes ranging from 8 to 13. On average, participants took 28.24 minutes (
SD=19.59
) to complete the study.

4.2
Results

A repeated-measures correlation analysis
(
Vallat, 2018
)

r(269)=-0.32

This discrepancy raises the question to what extent BERTScore and Levenshtein distance capture the complex concept of perceived appropriateness. For this reason, and because there is no comparable measurement in the first experiment, we will leave this here as a tangential insight but not leverage it for further analysis.

5
Discussion

Figure
6
combines the data of Figures
3
and
4
, highlighting the divergence between the results of the simulation and experiment with human participants. This is in line with prior findings in related work
(
Aher et al., 2023
;
Cui et al., 2025
;
Manning et al., 2024
;
Petrov et al., 2024
, e.g.,)
that simulations can be used to predict a rough trend (e.g., preference inference algorithms outperforming the baseline) but fail to predict the strength and small but important details (e.g., the development of ICL over time). Because we saw no significant improvement with ICL and are not confident in the findings for RP, our conclusion from the experiment with human participants is that the preference inference algorithms did not provide any benefit and are thus not worth the additional computational overhead. This is in direct contradiction to the findings of the simulation.

From our quantitative and qualitative findings, we conclude that our implementation of the simulated participant is poorly suited to realistically simulate people’s editing behavior. The writing styles generated by GPT-4o are at the same time more extreme and more consistent than what people exhibit. The tireless nature of LLMs also makes them likely to put more effort into their revisions than people do. This exaggerated behavior makes it easier to infer the stylistic preferences of simulated users. Consequently, GPT-4o only needed a single contrastive example to adapt its writing style to a point where no further improvements could be made over the remaining 19 iterations. This indicates the threat that simulated users are likely to produce exaggerated results.

Figure 6:
Comparison of algorithm performance between the simulation and human trials.
Similarity scores were calculated by comparing the summary originally generated by the algorithms to the edited version generated by the human participant. Error bars indicate 95% confidence intervals. ICL = in-context learning, RP = rolling preferences.

The LLM-based synthetic participants as well as the preference inference algorithms missed the detail, depth, and motivation of people’s preferences. GPT-4o particularly failed to infer and replicate human imperfections that one naturally integrates when talking to certain audiences (e.g., children). The rolling preferences algorithm produced mostly descriptive style preferences that often missed the motivation behind the changes as well as the context to anchor them. We also found that a common revision by human participants is to merely cut out parts without rephrasing anything. This supports
Aroca-Ouellette et al., 2024
’s argument that being presented with a pre-generated summary induces complacency and a satisficing behavior in which participants make minimal revisions that satisfy the requirements
(
Moray, 2003
)
.

GPT-4o, on the other hand, seemed overeager to revise, rephrasing even if the original summary was already appropriate for the target audience. This behavior was corrected through the two-stage design of the simulated user
(
Gao et al., 2024
)
, indicating that smart design choices can improve the realism of synthetic participants. It is unlikely that off-the-shelf LLMs accurately simulate human nature. More research needs to be done to identify engineering approaches that replicate these important nuances we observe in human participants. At the same time, researchers must be careful to avoid introducing biases when engineering their simulated users to be more complex in an attempt to create more realistic simulations.

Diverging results open the question whether the simulation or the experiment with human participants produced more useful results for real-world applications. We were concerned about participant fatigue but found that this did not lead participants in the baseline condition to make fewer edits towards the end of the 30-minute experiment. In the rolling preferences condition, however, our implementation might have caused a worse user experience, leading to increased fatigue and a survivorship bias in favor of low-effort participants who were not too invested in the task in the first place. This is a plausible explanation for the significant improvement in the rolling preferences condition that we found. This insight highlights the importance of considering human factors in the evaluations of algorithms. An algorithm that performs well in simulations might not be preferred by people in real-world use-cases if it lacks usability because it is cumbersome to use. Researchers also need to consider human factors for the choice of evaluation metrics. In our experiment, no changes made to an original summary could indicate either a high-quality summary or a low-effort revision.

However, our method shows how simulations can be used as a time- and cost-effective method to compare and select algorithms or prompt variations. We were able to show significant benefits of some algorithms over the baseline and compared different algorithms and target audience descriptions. This helped us prepare for the experiment with human participants.

6
Limitations

One clear limitation of our study is its small scope. We recruited a relatively small sample and spread participants across three different conditions. We did not force participants to make edits to keep the task setting more realistic and explicitly instructed them to “not take too long for each summary” to prevent them from exceeding the expected 30 minutes for the experiment. However, this also resulted in a large share of our participants applying minimal to no changes, reflecting a natural tendency
(
Moray, 2003
)
that nonetheless affects the interpretation of our results.

We acknowledge that our simulation is limited to a single LLM and that other models, especially those from other providers, might be more capable of capturing and applying stylistic details. Our findings are also limited to the domain of style preference inference over repeated interactions. With the collected data we cannot claim whether similar problems exist for LLM simulations in other domains, although we believe this to be highly likely. We believe that the research issue of LLM simulation fidelity benefits from insights in a broad variety of task domains.

We also reduced the number of tasks, scenarios, and target audiences of the PRELUDE framework, and omitted the mechanism that retrieves similar examples
(
Gao et al., 2024
)
. We did this to demonstrate the discrepancy between simulated and real people with limited resources. Our findings regarding the performance of specific algorithms is thus not applicable. These simplified versions should be expected to perform worse which might have led to a deteriorated user experience. We attribute the significant findings of the rolling preferences condition mostly to such uncontrolled aspects of our experiment. This and the high exclusion rate threaten the validity of our experiment with human participants. However, we are confident that our main argument still holds because we show a clear discrepancy between the findings of the two experiments. This raises the question whether the simulation or the experiment with human participants is closer to the truth and provides more valuable insights. Biases could be introduced in both, the experimental setting as well as the simulation design. This further emphasizes the need for researchers to make their experimental and simulation designs transparent for the research community to discuss and criticize.

7
Conclusion

In this study, we used the PRELUDE framework
(
Gao et al., 2024
)
to evaluate different writing style preference inference algorithms. We compared the results of a simulation in which GPT-4o was tasked to emulate human behavior to the results of a replication experiment with human participants. Our comparison revealed a large discrepancy between the results of the two experiments. The simulation revealed biases and other aspects of LLMs that skew the performance evaluations, highlighting the threat to ecological validity if conclusions are solely based on the behavior of simulated users.

The replication with real people, on the other hand, demonstrated how aspects of human nature, such as satisficing behavior, intent of language, and usability concerns, threaten the validity of findings derived purely from simulations. Researchers need to account for the human factors when studying human-LLM interaction. Relevant biases of people and LLMs might only be revealed in studies where they actually interact. We suspect that our experimental design and setup further exacerbated the divergence of behavior and thus caution future work to carefully consider and discuss the validity of synthetic participants wherever they are used as a research method.
