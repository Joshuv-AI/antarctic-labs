High-Probability Guarantees for Random Zeroth-Order Gradient Descent on Smooth Functions
Haishan Ye

Abstract
Randomized zeroth-order methods are classically analyzed in expectation, but a black-box Markov conversion can give misleading high-probability guarantees, in particular by forcing the finite-difference smoothing radius to shrink with the confidence parameter. This paper gives a direct finite-horizon high-probability analysis of a two-query Gaussian finite-difference method for deterministic objectives with Lipschitz gradients. The method uses the classical two-point estimator together with the normalized stepsize η t = 1 / ( 4 ​ L ​ ‖ 𝒖 t ‖ 2 ) \eta_{t}=1/(4L\left\|\bm{u}_{t}\right\|^{2}) . We prove that it finds an ε \varepsilon -suboptimal point with probability at least 1 − δ 1-\delta using 𝒪 ⁡ ( ( d ​ L / μ ) ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) + log ⁡ ( 1 / δ ) ) \mathcal{O}((dL/\mu)\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log(1/\delta)) function queries under strong convexity, subject to an explicit finite-difference smoothing-radius condition. We also establish high-probability guarantees for smooth convex objectives under a level-set distance-to-solution radius condition and a pathwise smoothing-radius condition. For lower-bounded smooth non-convex objectives, the trajectory average is certified in stationarity with 𝒪 ⁡ ( L ​ Δ 0 ​ ( d + log ⁡ ( 1 / δ ) ) / ε ) \mathcal{O}(L\Delta_{0}(d+\log(1/\delta))/\varepsilon) function queries. The proofs combine lower-tail bounds for adaptive sums of Gaussian directional projections with upper-tail bounds for accumulated finite-difference smoothing errors.



1
Introduction

Zeroth-order optimization studies the minimization of an objective function when the
algorithm can only query function values. This oracle model is natural in
black-box simulation, hyperparameter tuning, simulation-based engineering design,
and derivative-free numerical optimization
(
Conn et al. 2009
)
, where
gradients may be unavailable, unreliable, or too expensive to compute. It also
appears in modern large-scale applications such as memory-efficient fine-tuning of
large language models
(
Malladi et al. 2023
)
, black-box adversarial
attacks
(
Ilyas et al. 2018
)
, reinforcement learning
(
Choromanski et al. 2018
)
,
optimization with bandit feedback
(
Bubeck and Cesa-Bianchi 2012
;
Wang 2023
)
, and
distributionally robust optimization
(
Lam and Zhang 2024
)
. A standard randomized method samples a
direction, evaluates the function at two nearby points, forms a finite-difference
gradient surrogate, and descends along this surrogate. Even for a deterministic
objective, the sampled directions create an adaptive random trajectory, and the
finite-difference smoothing error must be controlled along the whole path.

Most analyses of randomized zeroth-order methods are expectation based

(
Ghadimi and Lan 2013
;
Duchi et al. 2015
;
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
)
. Such results
explain the average behavior of random finite-difference estimators, but they do not
directly give fixed-confidence guarantees for a single run. Markov’s inequality gives
a simple conversion: if

\mathbb{E}[f(\bm{x}_{T})-f^{*}]\leq\delta\varepsilon
, then

f(\bm{x}_{T})-f^{*}\leq\varepsilon
with probability at least
1-\delta
. This conversion
is often too pessimistic. For example, in the smooth strongly convex setting,

Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
imply, up to constants,

N_{\rm exp}^{\rm sc}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{dL}{\mu}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),\qquad\alpha_{\rm exp}^{\rm sc}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu}}{dL}\right),

where
\alpha
is the finite-difference smoothing radius. The Markov conversion
replaces
\varepsilon
by
\delta\varepsilon
, and hence gives

N_{\rm Markov}^{\rm sc}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{dL}{\mu}\log\frac{1}{\delta\varepsilon}\right),\qquad\alpha_{\rm Markov}^{\rm sc}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\delta\varepsilon\mu}}{dL}\right).

Thus the admissible smoothing radius is smaller than the expectation-level one by a
factor
\sqrt{\delta}
, or equivalently the expectation-level radius is larger by

\delta^{-1/2}
. This artificial confidence dependence does not reflect the
behavior one expects from a deterministic black-box problem, where the only
randomness is the algorithm’s own search direction.

The difficulty is geometric. At iteration
t
, descent depends on the squared
normalized projection of the Gaussian direction onto the current gradient direction,

\zeta_{t}=\left(\frac{\bm{u}_{t}^{\top}\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})}{\left\|\bm{u}_{t}\right\|\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|}\right)^{2},

when
\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\neq 0
. Conditionally on the past,
\zeta_{t}
has a Beta-type
distribution with mean
1/d
. This mean is exactly what makes expectation analyses
clean. However, no useful high-probability lower bound can hold for a single
projection, since one sampled direction may be nearly orthogonal to the gradient. A
direct high-probability proof must therefore show that enough descent accumulates
over many adaptive steps, while also controlling the nonnegative smoothing error
coming from the two-point finite-difference approximation.

This paper provides such a direct analysis for deterministic smooth objectives. We
study the classical two-query estimator

\bm{g}(\bm{x}_{t})=\frac{f(\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha\bm{u}_{t})-f(\bm{x}_{t}-\alpha\bm{u}_{t})}{2\alpha}\,\bm{u}_{t},\qquad\bm{u}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{I}_{d}),

with the normalized adaptive step size
\eta_{t}=1/(4L\left\|\bm{u}_{t}\right\|^{2})
. Thus the
estimator is the usual two-point Gaussian estimator, while the stepsize is a
direction-length-normalized variant rather than the fixed
O(1/(dL))
stepsize
often used in expectation analyses. The normalization separates the random length
of
\bm{u}_{t}
from its random direction, which exposes the Beta-type projection
structure. We prove finite-horizon high-probability rates by combining lower-tail
bounds for adaptive sums of directional projections with upper-tail bounds for
accumulated smoothing errors.

Our main contributions are as follows.

1.

Strongly convex objectives.

For deterministic
L
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex objectives, the method
finds an
\varepsilon
-suboptimal point with probability at least
1-\delta

using

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{dL}{\mu}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)

function queries, up to the stated finite-difference smoothing condition. The
leading smoothing scale remains
\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu}/(dL)
, unlike the Markov
conversion which introduces the extra
\sqrt{\delta}
factor; the full
admissible radius also contains a logarithmic high-probability cap.

2.

Smooth convex objectives.

Under a level-set distance-to-solution radius
R_{\varepsilon}
, we obtain a
high-probability query complexity

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{dLR_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{\varepsilon}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

This guarantee uses the pathwise finite-difference condition

\alpha=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon/(dLT)})
; for

T\asymp dLR_{\varepsilon}^{2}/\varepsilon+\log(1/\delta)
, this is the
radius condition stated explicitly in Corollary
4
. In the
leading accuracy regime it becomes
\alpha=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon/(dLR_{\varepsilon}))
,
which explains the linear dependence on the target accuracy in the convex case.

3.

Smooth non-convex objectives.

For lower-bounded
L
-smooth objectives, without assuming uniformly bounded
gradients, we prove a trajectory certificate

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon

with probability at least
1-\delta
using

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{L\Delta_{0}(d+\log(1/\delta))}{\varepsilon}\right)

function queries.
For the same horizon, the admissible smoothing radius scales as

\alpha=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon}/(L\sqrt{d(d+\log(1/\delta))}))
, hence as

\mathcal{O}(1/d)
in dimension at constant confidence and fixed stationarity
tolerance.
This smoothing radius is
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d})
larger than the one in
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
holding in expectation.

1.1
Related Works

The closest expectation-based theory is the Gaussian-smoothing framework of

Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
; we refer to this work as Nesterov–Spokoiny (NS) in the
comparisons below. It analyzes random gradient-free methods for smooth convex and
strongly convex optimization. The same expectation-based viewpoint also appears in
stochastic and non-convex zeroth-order optimization

(
Ghadimi and Lan 2013
;
Duchi et al. 2015
)
. These analyses exploit the property
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\bm{g}(\bm{x})\right\|\right]=\mathcal{O}(d\cdot\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|^{2})
, but do not provide direct fixed-confidence bounds for the
normalized two-query trajectory studied here.

Several related algorithms use different feedback models. Random Pursuit

(
Stich et al. 2013
)
optimizes over a random one-dimensional subspace at
each iteration via a line-search oracle. Recent high-probability zeroth-order
optimization based on randomized sketching and multi-point feedback

(
Ye et al. 2025
)
makes per-iteration descent hold with high probability by
using more than two function queries per step. In contrast, we keep the classical
two-query Gaussian finite-difference estimator and use a normalized random stepsize.

There is also a large deterministic derivative-free optimization literature based on
direct search and model-based trust-region methods

(
Kolda et al. 2003
;
Conn et al. 2009
)
. Worst-case complexity results for
smooth non-convex derivative-free methods were developed by

Vicente 2013
and
Cartis et al. 2012
. High-probability results have
been obtained for direct search with probabilistic descent directions

(
Gratton et al. 2015
)
, trust-region methods with probabilistic models

(
Gratton et al. 2018
)
, and direct search in random reduced spaces

(
Roberts and Royer 2023
)
. These works provide important high-confidence
derivative-free theory, but their algorithms poll over direction sets, enforce
sufficient decrease, or build local models, rather than following a single Gaussian
two-query gradient-estimator step.

The lower-tail arguments used here are also related in spirit to randomized
coordinate-descent analyses, where descent accumulates over random coordinate choices

(
Nesterov 2012
;
Richtárik and Takáč 2014
;
Richtárik and Takáč 2016
)
. The
difference is that the present method samples a continuous Gaussian direction, so the
relevant descent variable is a Beta projection rather than a discrete coordinate
selection indicator.

Another closely related recent method is the stochastic three points algorithm of

El Bakkali El Kadi and Saadi 2025
. For deterministic objectives, it compares

\bm{x}_{t}
,
\bm{x}_{t}+\alpha_{t}\bm{s}_{t}
, and
\bm{x}_{t}-\alpha_{t}\bm{s}_{t}
, then moves to the best
point. Its guarantees are almost-sure and asymptotic, whereas our results are
finite-horizon and fixed-confidence.

2
Assumptions and Preliminaries

2.1
Assumptions

We first introduce the atomic assumptions used in the convergence analysis. Each
assumption states only one structural property, so common requirements such as
smoothness are not repeated across convexity classes.

Assumption 1

(
L
-Lipschitz gradient)
.

The function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is differentiable and has an
L
-Lipschitz
gradient: for all
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\left\|\nabla f(\bm{y})-\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|\leq L\left\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\|.

This implies the two-sided Taylor remainder bound: for all
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\left|f(\bm{y})-f(\bm{x})-\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rangle\right|\leq\frac{L}{2}\left\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\|^{2}.

(2.1)

In particular, the descent lemma

f(\bm{y})\leq f(\bm{x})+\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\left\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\|^{2}.

(2.2)

holds.

Assumption
1
is used in all three regimes in
Section
3
and in the corresponding proofs in
Sections
4.1
,
4.2
, and
4.3
. It gives the
descent lemma, controls the two-point finite-difference error through the two-sided
Taylor remainder bound (
2.1
), and underlies the one-step
inequalities used before the high-probability martingale arguments.

L
-Lipschitz gradients are a standard baseline in first-order optimization

(
Nesterov 2004
;
Nocedal and Wright 2006
;
Bubeck 2015
)
, in
zeroth-order convex optimization
(
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
;
Duchi et al. 2015
;
Shamir 2017
)
,
and in zeroth-order non-convex complexity analysis

(
Ghadimi and Lan 2013
;
Cartis et al. 2012
;
Conn et al. 2009
)
.

Assumption 2

(
\mu
-strong convexity)
.

The function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\mu
-strongly convex: for all

\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

f(\bm{y})\geq f(\bm{x})+\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\|^{2}.

(2.3)

Assumption
2
is used only in the strongly convex results in
Section
3
and their proof in Section
4.1
. Together with
Assumption
1
, it yields the function-value contraction and the gradient
growth relation used to prove Theorem
1
and Corollary
2
.
For finite differentiable objectives on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, this condition also ensures a unique
minimizer, so the strongly convex part does not need the separate
Assumption
4
.
Strong convexity is one of the canonical assumptions for linear convergence of
first-order methods
(
Nesterov 2004
;
Nocedal and Wright 2006
;
Bubeck 2015
)

and is also commonly imposed in high-accuracy or strongly convex zeroth-order analyses

(
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
;
Duchi et al. 2015
;
Shamir 2017
)
.

Assumption 3

(Convexity)
.

The function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex: for all
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

f(\bm{y})\geq f(\bm{x})+\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rangle.

(2.4)

Assumption
3
is used in the smooth convex results in
Section
3.1
and their proof in Section
4.2
. It is
used to replace strong convexity by

f(\bm{x})-f^{*}\leq\dist(\bm{x},\mathcal{X}^{*})\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|
, which is the key inequality behind
Theorem
3
and Corollary
4
. Convexity is the
standard model assumption in deterministic first-order complexity theory

(
Nesterov 2004
;
Bubeck 2015
)
and in two-point zeroth-order convex
optimization
(
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
;
Duchi et al. 2015
;
Shamir 2017
)
.

Assumption 4

(Existence of minimizers)
.

The solution set is nonempty:

\mathcal{X}^{*}:=\arg\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\bm{x})\neq\emptyset,\qquad f^{*}:=\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\bm{x}).

Assumption
4
is used in the smooth convex results in
Section
3
and the proof in Section
4.2
to define
the optimality gap, the distance
\dist(\bm{x},\mathcal{X}^{*})
, and the level-set radius. This
solvability condition is routine in convex optimization analyses whenever the guarantee
is stated relative to a minimizer or a solution set

(
Nesterov 2004
;
Nocedal and Wright 2006
;
Bubeck 2015
)
. It also appears
implicitly or explicitly in coordinate-descent and zeroth-order convex analyses that use
initial-distance or level-set radii

(
Nesterov 2012
;
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
;
Cai et al. 2021
)
.

Assumption 5

(Finite enlarged level-set radius)
.

Under Assumptions
3
and
4
, fix an enlargement
level
B\geq 0
. The corresponding distance-to-solution-set radius

R(B):=\sup\left\{\dist(\bm{x},\mathcal{X}^{*}):f(\bm{x})\leq f(\bm{x}_{0})+B\right\}

is finite. We refer to this as the
B
-instance of the assumption.

Assumption
5
is used in the smooth convex part of
Section
3.2
, specifically with
B=A_{\alpha,T}(\delta)
in
Theorem
3
and with
B=\varepsilon/4
in
Corollary
4
; the proof uses the same condition in
Section
4.2
. Each result invokes only the single
B
-instance
that it needs. This assumption keeps the convex proof local to the
enlarged initial level set and is what turns the convexity inequality into a usable
gradient lower bound along the trajectory. Similar level-set radii are widely used in
randomized coordinate-descent analyses
(
Nesterov 2012
;
Richtárik and Takáč 2014
;
Richtárik and Takáč 2016
)
,
in inexact coordinate descent
(
Tappenden et al. 2016
)
, and in zeroth-order
block-coordinate descent
(
Cai et al. 2021
)
. Bounded initial level sets are also
standard in classical descent analyses
(
Nocedal and Wright 2006
)
, and error-bound or
quadratic-growth conditions imply such radius bounds

(
Luo and Tseng 1993
;
Drusvyatskiy and Lewis 2018
)
.

Remark 1

(Reasonableness of the level-set radius assumption)
.

The finite-radius condition should be understood as a restricted radius condition on
the enlarged initial level set. It is not the same as strong convexity, nor is it the
same as a finite initial-distance parameter
\left\|\bm{x}_{0}-\bm{x}^{*}\right\|
: the latter controls
only the starting point relative to one minimizer, while Assumption
5

controls all points in the relevant enlarged sublevel set through their distance to

\mathcal{X}^{*}
. The condition can hold even when the sublevel set itself is unbounded,
because it controls only
\dist(\bm{x},\mathcal{X}^{*})
, not the diameter of the whole sublevel
set. It is automatically satisfied if the relevant sublevel set is bounded, if the
method is projected onto a compact convex domain, or under a quadratic-growth/error-bound
condition. The radius is used only in the performance bound and in the choice of a
sufficiently small smoothing radius; the algorithmic update itself does not require
computing
R(B)
. Thus
R_{\alpha,T}(\delta)
and
R_{\varepsilon}
should be
read as problem-dependent complexity parameters, analogous to but generally different
from an initial-distance parameter.

Assumption 6

(Lower boundedness)
.

The function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is lower bounded:

f^{*}:=\inf_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\bm{x})>-\infty.

Assumption
6
is used in the smooth non-convex stationarity
guarantees in Section
3.3
and in the proof in
Section
4.3
. Together with Assumption
1
, it makes

\Delta_{0}=f(\bm{x}_{0})-f^{*}
finite and allows the descent inequalities to telescope,
which proves Theorem
5
and
Corollary
6
. Lower boundedness is the standard baseline in
non-convex gradient-method complexity theory and derivative-free non-convex analysis

(
Ghadimi and Lan 2013
;
Cartis et al. 2012
;
Conn et al. 2009
;
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
)
.

2.2
Preliminaries and Notation

The two-point zeroth-order estimator used in Algorithm
1
can be written
compactly as follows. Generate
\bm{u}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{I}_{d})
, query the two points

\bm{x}+\alpha\bm{u}
and
\bm{x}-\alpha\bm{u}
, and set

\bm{g}(\bm{x})=\frac{f(\bm{x}+\alpha\bm{u})-f(\bm{x}-\alpha\bm{u})}{2\alpha}\bm{u}.

(2.6)

Algorithm
1
applies a normalized gradient-descent step with this estimator.

We use the following notation throughout the finite-horizon analysis. The natural
filtration generated by the random search directions is

{{\mathcal{F}}}_{-1}:=\{\emptyset,\Omega\},\qquad{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t}:=\sigma(\bm{u}_{0},\ldots,\bm{u}_{t}),\quad t\geq 0.

Thus
\bm{x}_{t}
,
\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})
, and
\eta_{t}
are
{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}
-measurable, while

\bm{u}_{t}
is independent of
{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}
. Whenever the relevant optimal value is
finite, write

f^{*}:=\inf_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\bm{x}),\qquad\Delta_{t}:=f(\bm{x}_{t})-f^{*},\qquad\Delta_{0}:=f(\bm{x}_{0})-f^{*}.

Under Assumption
4
, this
f^{*}
is the attained minimum and

\mathcal{X}^{*}
is the solution set. Under Assumption
6
, the same
notation denotes the finite infimum used in the non-convex stationarity result.
Finally, for
0<\delta<1
and horizon
T\geq 1
, define the common
finite-difference accumulation scale

\tau_{\delta}:=\log\frac{2}{\delta},\qquad U_{T}(\delta):=dT+2\sqrt{dT\tau_{\delta}}+2\tau_{\delta},\qquad A_{\alpha,T}(\delta):=\frac{L\alpha^{2}}{16}U_{T}(\delta).

(2.7)

3
Main Results

This section states the main high-probability guarantees and their
query-complexity consequences.

3.1
Strongly Convex Objectives

We first state the strongly convex guarantee for Algorithm
1
.

Theorem 1
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold.
Given
0<\delta<1
and an integer
T\geq 1
, run Algorithm
1
with

\eta_{t}=1/(4L\left\|\bm{u}_{t}\right\|^{2})
. If
\bm{x}^{*}
is the unique minimizer of
f
,
then the output
\bm{x}_{T}
satisfies

\displaystyle f(\bm{x}_{T})-f(\bm{x}^{*})\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\mu}{8L}\left(\frac{T}{2d}-\frac{6\log(3/\delta)}{d}\right)\right)\cdot\Big(f(\bm{x}_{0})-f(\bm{x}^{*})\Big)

(3.1)

\displaystyle+\frac{dL\alpha^{2}}{16}\left(1004+1000\left(\log\frac{3}{\delta}+\log\log(2T)\right)+\frac{32dL}{\mu}+3\log\frac{3}{\delta}\right),

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Remark 2

(Order form and constants)
.

The numerical constants in Theorem
1
are not optimized; they are kept only
to make the finite-horizon statement fully explicit. In order notation, the bound has
the form

\displaystyle f(\bm{x}_{T})-f(\bm{x}^{*})\leq

\displaystyle\exp\!\left\{-c\frac{\mu}{L}\left(\frac{T}{d}-\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{d}\right)\right\}\bigl(f(\bm{x}_{0})-f(\bm{x}^{*})\bigr)

\displaystyle+CdL\alpha^{2}\left(1+\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log\log T+\frac{dL}{\mu}\right)

for universal constants
c,C>0
.

The following corollary gives the corresponding query complexity.

Corollary 2
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold and parameters of Algorithm
1
be set as Theorem
1
.
Given
0<\delta<1
and the target precision
0<\varepsilon<1
, there exists
a universal constant
c_{\rm sc}>0
such that the following holds. If the
integer
T
satisfies

T\geq\frac{16dL}{\mu}\log\frac{2\Big(f(\bm{x}_{0})-f(\bm{x}^{*})\Big)}{\varepsilon}+12\log\frac{3}{\delta},

(3.2)

and the smoothing parameter
\alpha
satisfies

0<\alpha\leq c_{\rm sc}\min\left\{\frac{(\varepsilon\mu)^{1/2}}{dL},\;\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{dL\left(1+\log(1/\delta)+\log\log T\right)}\right)^{1/2}\right\},

(3.3)

then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the output
\bm{x}_{T}
satisfies

f(\bm{x}_{T})-f(\bm{x}^{*})\leq\varepsilon.

Remark 3

(Strongly convex comparison)
.

Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
analyze a random gradient-free method for
smooth strongly convex objectives in expectation. Translating their notation to ours
by setting
n=d
,
L_{1}(f)=L
, and
\tau(f)=\mu
, and denoting their smoothing
parameter
\mu
by
\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}
, their expected guarantee gives, up to
constant factors,

N_{\mathrm{NS}}^{\mathrm{sc}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{dL}{\mu}\log\frac{L\left\|\bm{x}_{0}-\bm{x}^{*}\right\|^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right),\qquad\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu}}{dL}\right).

Since
Z=f(\bm{x}_{N})-f(\bm{x}^{*})\geq 0
, Markov’s inequality gives

\mathbb{P}\{Z>\varepsilon\}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}Z}{\varepsilon}.

Thus an expectation guarantee with accuracy
\delta\varepsilon
implies an

\varepsilon
-accurate guarantee with probability at least
1-\delta
. Applying this
conversion to the above expectation result yields

N_{\mathrm{NS,HP}}^{\mathrm{sc}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{dL}{\mu}\log\frac{L\left\|\bm{x}_{0}-\bm{x}^{*}\right\|^{2}}{\delta\varepsilon}\right),\qquad\alpha_{\mathrm{NS,HP}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\delta\varepsilon\mu}}{dL}\right).

Compared with this Markov conversion, Corollary
2
keeps the same
dimension, condition-number, and accuracy dependence in the leading term, but its
confidence cost is the additive term
\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\delta))
in (
3.2
), rather than
a condition-number-weighted
\log(1/\delta)
. Its leading smoothing scale

\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu}/(dL)
also does not shrink by the Markov factor
\sqrt{\delta}
,
although the second term in (
3.3
) reflects the high-probability control of the
accumulated finite-difference error.

3.2
Smooth Convex Objectives

We next give the deterministic high-probability convergence guarantee for
Algorithm
1
when the objective function is only convex and
L
-smooth.
Compared with the strongly convex case, the condition

\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|^{2}\geq 2\mu\bigl(f(\bm{x})-f^{*}\bigr)

is no longer available. The replacement is a level-set radius bound, obtained from the
fact that the accumulated zeroth-order smoothing error is controlled with high
probability.

Throughout this section, we work under Assumptions
1
,

3
, and
4
. We use the optimality gap

\Delta_{t}=f(\bm{x}_{t})-f^{*}
and the common quantities

U_{T}(\delta)
and
A_{\alpha,T}(\delta)
from (
2.7

Define the relevant level-set radius

R_{\alpha,T}(\delta):=\sup\left\{\dist(\bm{x},\mathcal{X}^{*}):f(\bm{x})\leq f(\bm{x}_{0})+A_{\alpha,T}(\delta)\right\},

(3.4)

where

\dist(\bm{x},\mathcal{X}^{*}):=\inf_{\bm{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}^{*}}\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{*}\right\|.

The theorem below uses the
B=A_{\alpha,T}(\delta)
instance of
Assumption
5
. If
R_{\alpha,T}(\delta)=0
, then the relevant
level set is contained in the solution set and the conclusion is trivial.

Theorem 3

(High-probability convergence under smooth convexity)
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
3
, and

4
hold and let
\{\bm{x}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
be generated by
Algorithm
1
with
\eta_{t}=1/(4L\left\|\bm{u}_{t}\right\|^{2})
. Given
0<\delta<1
,
define
A_{\alpha,T}(\delta)
and
R_{\alpha,T}(\delta)
by
(
2.7
) and (
3.4
). Assume the relevant instance of
Assumption
5
holds, namely

R_{\alpha,T}(\delta)<\infty
, and

T>12\log\frac{2}{\delta}.

(3.5)

If

, then

with probability at least

1-\delta

R_{\alpha,T}(\delta)>0
, then, with probability at least

1-\delta

\displaystyle f(\bm{x}_{T})-f^{*}\leq 2A_{\alpha,T}(\delta)+\left(\frac{1}{\Delta_{0}+A_{\alpha,T}(\delta)}+\frac{T-12\log(2/\delta)}{128dLR_{\alpha,T}^{2}(\delta)}\right)^{-1}.

(3.6)

Consequently, the simpler bound

f(\bm{x}_{T})-f^{*}\leq 2A_{\alpha,T}(\delta)+\frac{128dLR_{\alpha,T}^{2}(\delta)}{T-12\log(2/\delta)}

(3.7)

holds with probability at least
1-\delta
. Here, if

\Delta_{0}+A_{\alpha,T}(\delta)=0
, the inverse term in
(
3.6
) is interpreted as
0
.

Corollary 4

(Query complexity under smooth convexity)
.

Let
0<\varepsilon<1
and define

R_{\varepsilon}:=\sup\left\{\dist(\bm{x},\mathcal{X}^{*}):f(\bm{x})\leq f(\bm{x}_{0})+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right\}.

(3.8)

Assume the
B=\varepsilon/4
instance of Assumption
5
holds,
namely
R_{\varepsilon}<\infty
. If
R_{\varepsilon}=0
, then the relevant enlarged
level set is contained in
\mathcal{X}^{*}
and the conclusion is immediate. Otherwise,
there exists a universal constant

c_{\rm cvx}>0
such that if

T\geq\frac{512dLR_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{\varepsilon}+24\log\frac{2}{\delta},

(3.9)

and the smoothing parameter is chosen as

0<\alpha\leq c_{\rm cvx}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{dLT}\right)^{1/2}

(3.10)

then Algorithm
1
satisfies

f(\bm{x}_{T})-f^{*}\leq\varepsilon

with probability at least
1-\delta
. Since each iteration uses two function queries,
the query complexity is

2T=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{dLR_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{\varepsilon}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

(3.11)

When
R_{\varepsilon}>0
and

T\asymp dLR_{\varepsilon}^{2}/\varepsilon+\log(2/\delta)
, the smoothing condition
(
3.10
) is implied by

\alpha\leq c_{\rm cvx}\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{dLR_{\varepsilon}},\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{dL\log(2/\delta)}\right)^{1/2}\right\}.

Remark 4

(Smooth convex comparison)
.

For smooth convex objectives without strong convexity,
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017

obtain an expectation bound for the best iterate of their simple random
gradient-free method. With
n=d
,
L_{1}(f)=L
,
R=\left\|\bm{x}_{0}-\bm{x}^{*}\right\|
, and with
their smoothing parameter
\mu
written as
\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}
, the extracted
complexity and smoothing scale are

N_{\mathrm{NS}}^{\mathrm{cvx}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{dLR^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right),\qquad\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{d}\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{L}}\right).

The result is in expectation. Markov’s inequality therefore turns it into a
probability-
1-\delta
guarantee by replacing
\varepsilon
with

\delta\varepsilon
, which gives

N_{\mathrm{NS,HP}}^{\mathrm{cvx}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{dLR^{2}}{\delta\varepsilon}\right),\qquad\alpha_{\mathrm{NS,HP}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{d}\sqrt{\frac{\delta\varepsilon}{L}}\right).

The different powers of
\varepsilon
come from the different error term that is
being controlled. In the NS expectation proof, after choosing

N\asymp dLR^{2}/\varepsilon
, the finite-difference contribution in the expected
objective gap is of order
Ld^{2}\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}^{2}
. Requiring this term to be
at most
O(\varepsilon)
gives

\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}=\mathcal{O}(d^{-1}\sqrt{\varepsilon/L})
. If the same expectation
bound is converted to probability
1-\delta
by Markov’s inequality, the target
inside the expectation must be
\delta\varepsilon
, and the same calculation gives

\alpha_{\mathrm{NS,HP}}=\mathcal{O}(d^{-1}\sqrt{\delta\varepsilon/L})
.

Corollary
4
instead gives the direct high-probability query
complexity

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{dLR_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{\varepsilon}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right),

where
R_{\varepsilon}
is the radius of the slightly enlarged initial level set in
(
3.8
). Thus the direct high-probability analysis avoids the multiplicative

1/\delta
loss from Markov’s inequality. The price is that a single path must
keep the accumulated finite-difference error small. In Theorem
3

this error is

A_{\alpha,T}(\delta)=\frac{L\alpha^{2}}{16}U_{T}(\delta),\qquad U_{T}(\delta)\asymp dT+\log(2/\delta)

up to constants. Since
2A_{\alpha,T}(\delta)
appears directly in the final
objective gap, it is enough to impose
A_{\alpha,T}(\delta)\leq O(\varepsilon)
,
which yields

\alpha=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{L(dT+\log(2/\delta))}}\right).

Substituting the convex horizon

T\asymp dLR_{\varepsilon}^{2}/\varepsilon+\log(2/\delta)
gives

\alpha=\mathcal{O}\left(\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{dLR_{\varepsilon}},\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{dL\log(2/\delta)}\right)^{1/2}\right\}\right).

Thus, when
d,L,R_{\varepsilon}
, and
\delta
are treated as fixed, the leading
accuracy dependence of the present pathwise condition is
\alpha=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
,
whereas the NS expectation condition is
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
and its Markov
high-probability conversion is
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\delta\varepsilon})
. This comparison
should be read together with the query guarantees: the direct proof asks for a
smaller finite-difference radius in high accuracy regimes, but it avoids the

1/\delta
Markov loss in the number of queries.

3.3
Smooth Non-Convex Objectives

We finally state the deterministic non-convex result under
Assumptions
1
and
6
. The objective is no longer
assumed to be convex or strongly convex; instead, we assume only smoothness and a finite
lower bound. The output criterion is therefore stationarity, measured by the average
squared gradient norm along the trajectory.

Theorem 5

(High-probability average stationarity for non-convex objectives)
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
6
hold and run
Algorithm
1
for

T\geq 1
iterations with
\eta_{t}=1/(4L\left\|\bm{u}_{t}\right\|^{2})
. Given

0<\delta<1
, use
\Delta_{0}
,
U_{T}(\delta)
, and

A_{\alpha,T}(\delta)
as defined in Section
2.2
.
Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{L\bigl(32d+16\log(2/\delta)\bigr)}{T}\left(\Delta_{0}+A_{\alpha,T}(\delta)\right).

(3.12)

Consequently,

\min_{0\leq t\leq T-1}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{L\bigl(32d+16\log(2/\delta)\bigr)}{T}\left(\Delta_{0}+A_{\alpha,T}(\delta)\right)

(3.13)

with the same probability.

Corollary 6

(Stationarity complexity)
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
6
, assume

\Delta_{0}>0
and choose
\alpha
so that

A_{\alpha,T}(\delta)\leq\Delta_{0}
; for example,

0<\alpha\leq 4\sqrt{\frac{\Delta_{0}}{L\,U_{T}(\delta)}}.

Since
U_{T}(\delta)=\mathcal{O}(dT+\log(2/\delta))
, there is a universal constant

c_{\rm nc}>0
such that it is sufficient to require

0<\alpha\leq c_{\rm nc}\left(\frac{\Delta_{0}}{L\left(dT+\log(2/\delta)\right)}\right)^{1/2}.

(3.14)

In particular, for

T\asymp\frac{L\Delta_{0}\left(d+\log(2/\delta)\right)}{\varepsilon},

this becomes

0<\alpha\leq c_{\rm nc}\min\left\{\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{L\sqrt{d\left(d+\log(2/\delta)\right)}},\left(\frac{\Delta_{0}}{L\log(2/\delta)}\right)^{1/2}\right\}.

If

T\geq\frac{2L\bigl(32d+16\log(2/\delta)\bigr)\Delta_{0}}{\varepsilon},

then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon,\qquad\min_{0\leq t\leq T-1}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon.

Since each iteration uses two function queries, the query complexity is

2T=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{L\Delta_{0}\bigl(d+\log(1/\delta)\bigr)}{\varepsilon}\right).

If
\Delta_{0}=0
, then
\bm{x}_{0}
is a global minimizer and

\nabla f(\bm{x}_{0})=0
, so a stationary point is already available. For running the
finite-difference trajectory anyway, one should use Theorem
5

directly instead of the simplified sufficient condition

A_{\alpha,T}(\delta)\leq\Delta_{0}
.

Remark 5

(Smooth non-convex comparison)
.

Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
also give an expectation guarantee for smooth
non-convex objectives. Rewriting their stationarity tolerance in the same squared
gradient form used above, namely targeting

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon,

their smooth non-convex estimate gives

N_{\mathrm{NS}}^{\mathrm{nc}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{dL\Delta_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right),\qquad\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{d^{3/2}L}\right),

where their smoothing parameter
\mu
is again denoted here by

\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}
. A Markov conversion gives the high-probability version

N_{\mathrm{NS,HP}}^{\mathrm{nc}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{dL\Delta_{0}}{\delta\varepsilon}\right),\qquad\alpha_{\mathrm{NS,HP}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\delta\varepsilon}}{d^{3/2}L}\right).

The
d^{-3/2}
scaling in NS is caused by the conversion from the stationarity of
the Gaussian-smoothed objective back to the stationarity of the original objective.
In our notation,
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
gives, up to constants,

\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|^{2}\leq 2\left\|\nabla f_{\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}}(\bm{x})\right\|^{2}+L^{2}\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}^{2}d^{3}.

The factor
d^{3}
is the Gaussian sixth-moment factor that enters this smoothing
bias. Thus, to certify

\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\|^{2}\leq O(\varepsilon)
, the bias term itself must satisfy

L^{2}\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}^{2}d^{3}\leq O(\varepsilon)
, which gives

\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{Ld^{3/2}}\right).

The Markov conversion again replaces
\varepsilon
by
\delta\varepsilon
, yielding
the displayed

\alpha_{\mathrm{NS,HP}}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\delta\varepsilon}/(Ld^{3/2}))
.

Corollary
6
gives, directly with probability at least

1-\delta
,

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{L\Delta_{0}\bigl(d+\log(1/\delta)\bigr)}{\varepsilon}\right)

queries. The proof does not first prove stationarity for a smoothed objective and
then translate it to
f
. Instead, it decomposes the two-point estimator into the
true directional-gradient part plus a finite-difference remainder, and the normalized
stepsize makes the accumulated remainder appear through

A_{\alpha,T}(\delta)=\frac{L\alpha^{2}}{16}U_{T}(\delta),\qquad U_{T}(\delta)\asymp dT+\log(2/\delta).

Requiring
A_{\alpha,T}(\delta)\leq O(\Delta_{0})
gives

\alpha=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Delta_{0}}{L(dT+\log(2/\delta))}}\right).

For the stationarity horizon

T\asymp L\Delta_{0}(d+\log(2/\delta))/\varepsilon
, this becomes

\alpha=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{L\sqrt{d\bigl(d+\log(1/\delta)\bigr)}}\right)

up to the additional cap in (
3.14
). Therefore, for
fixed
\varepsilon
,
L
,
\Delta_{0}
, and constant confidence, the present
condition scales as
\alpha=\mathcal{O}(1/d)
, while the NS condition scales as

\alpha_{\mathrm{NS}}=\mathcal{O}(1/d^{3/2})
. The missing factor
\sqrt{d}
is exactly the
cost of the NS smoothing-gradient conversion; the direct pathwise proof avoids that
conversion and pays instead for the accumulated finite-difference remainder along
the realized trajectory.

4.1
Strongly Convex Objectives

This subsection develops the strongly convex convergence analysis for

Lemma 1
.

Let Assumption
1

\{\bm{x}_{t}\}

f(\bm{x}_{t+1})\leq f(\bm{x}_{t})-\frac{\eta_{t}(\bm{u}_{t}^{\top}\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}))^{2}}{2}+L\eta_{t}^{2}\left\|\bm{u}_{t}\right\|^{2}\cdot\Big(\bm{u}_{t}^{\top}\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\Big)^{2}+\Delta_{\alpha,t},

(4.1)

where

(4.2)

The next lemma specializes the one-step inequality to the normalized stepsize and
iterates the resulting contraction over
T
steps.

Lemma 2
.

Assume that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold.
For an arbitrary
{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}
-measurable unit vector
\bm{a}_{t}
, define

\zeta_{t}:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\left(\frac{\bm{u}_{t}^{\top}\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})}{\left\|\bm{u}_{t}\right\|\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|}\right)^{2},&\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\neq 0,\\[13.99995pt]
\displaystyle\frac{(\bm{u}_{t}^{\top}\bm{a}_{t})^{2}}{\left\|\bm{u}_{t}\right\|^{2}},&\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})=0.\end{cases}

(4.3)

With the stepsize
\eta_{t}=\frac{1}{4L\left\|\bm{u}_{t}\right\|^{2}}
in Algorithm
1
,

\displaystyle f(\bm{x}_{T})-f(\bm{x}^{*})\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\mu}{8L}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\zeta_{t}\right)\cdot\Big(f(\bm{x}_{0})-f(\bm{x}^{*})\Big)

(4.4)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\exp\left(-\frac{\mu}{8L}\sum_{t=k+1}^{T-1}\zeta_{t}\right)\cdot\Delta_{\alpha,k}.

Remark 6
.

The definition (
4.3
) does
not
need to assume

\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|\neq 0
. If
\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})=0
, strong convexity implies

\bm{x}_{t}=\bm{x}^{*}
and the descent term in Lemma

is multiplied by the
zero optimality gap. The auxiliary vector
\bm{a}_{t}
is used only to keep the beta
projection martingale well defined.

Remark 7
.

Lemma
2
is the key to the high-probability analysis. Rather than
lower bounding descent at a single step, it tracks the accumulated decay over

T
updates.
At the one-step level, an appropriate stepsize gives a bound of the form

f(\bm{x}_{t+1})-f(\bm{x}^{*})\leq f(\bm{x}_{t})-f(\bm{x}^{*})-\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(\bm{u}_{t}^{\top}\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}))^{2}}{dL}\right).

(4.5)

For a single step, the term

(\bm{u}_{t}^{\top}\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t}))^{2}
behaves like

\left\|\nabla f(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|^{2}\chi^{2}(1)
, and no useful high-probability lower
bound is available for one draw. Expectation analyses avoid this issue through

\mathbb{E}[\chi^{2}(1)]=1
. Here the remedy is different: the proof lower bounds the
sum of many normalized projection terms along the adaptive trajectory.

Remark 8
.

Lemma
2
uses a stepsize of order

1/(L\left\|\bm{u}_{t}\right\|^{2})

scale commonly used in

(

. With this normalization,

when

\zeta_{t}=1
when

.
This beta-projection structure is the basis for the high-probability convergence
analysis.

Lemma 3
.

Let Assumption

\Delta_{\alpha,t}\leq\frac{L\alpha^{2}\left\|\bm{u}_{t}\right\|^{2}}{16}.

(4.6)

5
Conclusion

This paper established high-probability guarantees for a normalized two-query
Gaussian finite-difference method in deterministic smooth optimization. Unlike
expectation-based analyses and their Markov-converted high-probability counterparts,
our results control the random zeroth-order trajectory directly. For smooth strongly
convex objectives, the method achieves the standard zeroth-order query dependence
on
d
,
L/\mu
, and
\varepsilon
, with only an additive logarithmic confidence
term and without the leading
\sqrt{\delta}
shrinkage of the smoothing radius that
comes from Markov’s inequality. For smooth convex objectives, we obtain a
finite-horizon guarantee under a level-set radius condition, and for lower-bounded
smooth non-convex objectives we prove high-probability average stationarity without
assuming uniformly bounded gradients.

The proof mechanism separates two sources of randomness: the descent obtained from
Gaussian directional projections and the error introduced by finite-difference
smoothing. Lower-tail bounds for adaptive sums of Beta-type projection variables
provide accumulated descent, while upper-tail bounds for Gaussian norms control the
pathwise smoothing error. This explains why the normalized two-query method studied
here can enjoy fixed-confidence guarantees with logarithmic confidence overhead rather
than the polynomial losses suggested by a black-box expectation-to-probability
conversion. Natural next steps include sharpening the smoothing-radius conditions,
removing remaining logarithmic factors, and extending the same direct
high-probability approach to stochastic, constrained, or nonsmooth zeroth-order
problems.

Useful Lemmas

Lemma 11

(Weighted chi-square bound
(
Laurent and Massart 2000
)
)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{K}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and let

w_{1},\ldots,w_{K}\geq 0
be deterministic weights. Denoting

w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{K})^{\top}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the following
bound holds:
the standard weighted chi-square consequence of the Laurent–Massart inequality:

\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}X_{k}^{2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}+2\sqrt{\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}^{2}\right)\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}+2\max_{1\leq k\leq K}w_{k}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

Specifically, if
X\sim\chi^{2}(k)
with
k>0
, then for any
\tau>0
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(X\geq k+2\sqrt{k\tau}+2\tau\right)\leq\exp(-\tau),

\displaystyle\Pr\left(X\leq k-2\sqrt{k\tau}\right)\leq\exp(-\tau).

Lemma 12

(Ville’s inequality)
.

For any non-negative supermartingale
\{Z_{n}\}
with
0\leq n\leq N
, then it holds for any
y>0
:

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq n\leq N}Z_{n}\geq y\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[Z_{0}]}{y}=\frac{1}{y}.

Lemma 13

(Freedman’s inequality)
.

Let
\{Y_{t}:t\geq 0\}
be a real-valued martingale adapted to a filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
, with
Y_{-1}=0
. Let
Z_{t}=Y_{t}-Y_{t-1}
denote the martingale difference sequence. Assume the sequence is uniformly bounded from above:

Z_{t}\leq R\quad\text{a.s. for all }t\geq 0.

Define the predictable quadratic variation process
W_{k}
as:

W_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}[Z_{j}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{j-1}].

Then, for any
\tau\geq 0
and
\sigma^{2}>0
:

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists t\leq T:Y_{t}\geq\tau\text{ and }W_{t}\leq\sigma^{2}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\tau^{2}}{2(\sigma^{2}+R\tau)}\right).

Equivalently, the same exponential-supermartingale argument gives the linear-bound
form: for any
0<\lambda<3/R
and
0<\delta<1
, with probability at least

1-\delta
,

Y_{T}\leq\frac{\lambda}{2(1-\lambda R/3)}W_{T}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\frac{1}{\delta}.

Appendix B
Properties of Beta Distribution

Lemma 14
.

Let
\bm{u}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{I}_{d})
be a
d
-dimensional standard Gaussian random vector, and let
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a fixed constant vector of unit length (
\|\bm{a}\|_{2}=1
). Then, the random variable defined by the squared normalized inner product,

Y=\frac{\langle\bm{u},\bm{a}\rangle^{2}}{\|\bm{u}\|_{2}^{2}},

equals
1
almost surely when
d=1
. When
d\geq 2
, it follows a Beta distribution with parameters
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
and
\beta=\frac{d-1}{2}
, that is,
Y\sim\text{Beta}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)
.

Proof.

The case
d=1
is immediate because
\bm{a}=\pm 1
and

Y=\bm{u}^{2}/\bm{u}^{2}=1
on the probability-one event
\bm{u}\neq 0
. We now assume

d\geq 2
.
Because
\bm{a}
is a unit vector, we can construct an orthogonal matrix
\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that its first row is
\bm{a}^{\top}
. Equivalently, this means
\bm{Q}\bm{a}=\bm{e}_{1}
, where
\bm{e}_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0)^{\top}
is the first standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Let us define a newly rotated random vector
\bm{z}=\bm{Q}\bm{u}
. Because the standard multivariate Gaussian distribution is invariant under orthogonal transformations, the transformed vector
\bm{z}
is also a standard Gaussian random vector, i.e.,
\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{I}_{d})
. Consequently, its components
z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{d}
are independent and identically distributed standard normal random variables,
z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

We can now express both the numerator and the denominator of
Y
in terms of
z
. For the numerator, we use the orthogonal invariance of the inner product:

\langle\bm{u},\bm{a}\rangle=\langle\bm{Q}^{\top}\bm{z},\bm{a}\rangle=\langle\bm{z},\bm{Q}\bm{a}\rangle=\langle\bm{z},\bm{e}_{1}\rangle=z_{1}.

Thus, the numerator is simply
z_{1}^{2}
.

For the denominator, the Euclidean norm is preserved under orthogonal transformations:

\|\bm{u}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\bm{Q}^{\top}\bm{z}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\bm{z}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{2}.

Substituting these expressions into the definition of
Y
gives

Y=\frac{z_{1}^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{2}}=\frac{z_{1}^{2}}{z_{1}^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}z_{i}^{2}}.

Let
U=z_{1}^{2}
and
V=\sum_{i=2}^{d}z_{i}^{2}
. We analyze the distributions of
U
and
V
:

•

Since
z_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, its square follows a chi-squared distribution with
1
degree of freedom:
U\sim\chi^{2}_{1}
.

•

Since
z_{2},\dots,z_{d}
are
d-1
independent standard normal variables, the sum of their squares follows a chi-squared distribution with
d-1
degrees of freedom:
V\sim\chi^{2}_{d-1}
.

•

Because the components
z_{i}
are mutually independent,
U
and
V
are strictly independent.

A fundamental property of the Beta distribution states that if

U\sim\chi^{2}_{\nu_{1}}
and
V\sim\chi^{2}_{\nu_{2}}
are independent, then

U/(U+V)
is Beta distributed with parameters

\nu_{1}/2
and
\nu_{2}/2
. Applying this property with

\nu_{1}=1
and
\nu_{2}=d-1
gives

Y=\frac{U}{U+V}\sim\text{Beta}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right).

∎

Lemma 15

(Moments of the Beta distribution)
.

Let
X\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
with parameters
\alpha,\beta>0
.
Then the mean and variance of
X
are

\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta},\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\mathrm{Var}(X)=\frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^{2}(\alpha+\beta+1)}.

Moreover, for every integer
m\geq 1
, the
m
-th raw moment is

\mathbb{E}[X^{m}]=\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{\alpha+k}{\alpha+\beta+k}.

|\beta|\leq\frac{L\alpha}{2}\left\|\bm{u}\right\|^{2}
.

Proof.

Define
D(\bm{x},\alpha\bm{u})
and
D(\bm{x},-\alpha\bm{u})
by

\displaystyle D(\bm{x},\alpha\bm{u})=

\displaystyle f(\bm{x}+\alpha\bm{u})-(f(\bm{x})+\alpha\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{u}\right\rangle)

\displaystyle D(\bm{x},-\alpha\bm{u})=

\displaystyle f(\bm{x}-\alpha\bm{u})-(f(\bm{x})-\alpha\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{u}\right\rangle).

Then

\displaystyle\frac{f(\bm{x}+\alpha\bm{u})-f(\bm{x}-\alpha\bm{u})}{2\alpha}=

\displaystyle\frac{2\alpha\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{u}\right\rangle+D(\bm{x},\alpha\bm{u})-D(\bm{x},-\alpha\bm{u})}{2\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{u}\right\rangle+\frac{D(\bm{x},\alpha\bm{u})-D(\bm{x},-\alpha\bm{u})}{2\alpha}.

By the two-sided Taylor remainder bound (
2.1
),

\displaystyle|D(\bm{x},\alpha\bm{u})|\leq\frac{L\alpha^{2}}{2}\left\|\bm{u}\right\|^{2}.

The same argument gives

\displaystyle|D(\bm{x},-\alpha\bm{u})|\leq\frac{L\alpha^{2}}{2}\left\|\bm{u}\right\|^{2}.

Combining these two bounds with the definition of
\bm{g}(\bm{x})
in
Eq. (
2.6
) and

\beta=\frac{D(\bm{x},\alpha\bm{u})-D(\bm{x},-\alpha\bm{u})}{2\alpha}
, we get

|\beta|\leq\frac{|D(\bm{x},\alpha\bm{u})|+|D(\bm{x},-\alpha\bm{u})|}{2\alpha}\leq\frac{L\alpha}{2}\left\|\bm{u}\right\|^{2},

which proves the result.
∎

Lemma 17

(Unweighted lower bound for Beta projections)
.

Let
\{\bm{u}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
be independent standard Gaussian vectors in

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, equipped with the natural filtration convention from
Section
2.2
.
Let
\bm{a}_{t}
be

{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}
-measurable unit vectors and define

\zeta_{t}:=\frac{(\bm{u}_{t}^{\top}\bm{a}_{t})^{2}}{\left\|\bm{u}_{t}\right\|^{2}}.

Then for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\zeta_{t}\geq\frac{T}{d}-\frac{2\sqrt{2T\log(1/\delta)}+2\log(1/\delta)}{d}.

(D.1)

Proof.

If
d=1
, then
\zeta_{t}=1
almost surely and the claimed lower bound is
immediate because the error term on the right-hand side is nonnegative. Hence assume

d\geq 2
.
Define

Z_{t}:=\frac{1}{d}-\zeta_{t},\qquad Y_{s}:=\sum_{t=0}^{s-1}Z_{t},\qquad\mathcal{G}_{s}:={{\mathcal{F}}}_{s-1},\qquad s=0,\dots,T.

Since
\bm{u}_{t}
is independent of
{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}
and
\bm{a}_{t}
is

{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}
-measurable, it follows from Lemma
14
that

\zeta_{t}\mid{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}\sim\mathrm{Beta}\!\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right).

Lemma
15
gives

\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{t}\mid{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}]=\frac{1}{d},\qquad\mathrm{Var}(\zeta_{t}\mid{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1})=\frac{2(d-1)}{d^{2}(d+2)}\leq\frac{2}{d^{2}}.

Accordingly,

\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}\mid{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}]=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}^{2}\mid{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}]\leq\frac{2}{d^{2}}.

Thus
\{Y_{s},\mathcal{G}_{s}\}_{s=0}^{T}
is a martingale.

Also, since
0\leq\zeta_{t}\leq 1
, we have

Z_{t}=\frac{1}{d}-\zeta_{t}\leq\frac{1}{d},\qquad\forall\,t=0,\dots,T-1,

and the predictable quadratic variation satisfies

V_{T}:=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}^{2}\mid{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}]\leq\frac{2T}{d^{2}}.

Applying Freedman’s inequality (Lemma
13
) with
R=1/d
and

\sigma^{2}=2T/d^{2}
, and solving the resulting Bernstein tail bound at

x=2\sqrt{2T\log(1/\delta)}/d+2\log(1/\delta)/d
, gives, with
probability at least
1-\delta
,

Y_{T}\leq\frac{2}{d}\log(1/\delta)+\frac{2\sqrt{2T\log(1/\delta)}}{d}.

On the other hand,

Y_{T}=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(\frac{1}{d}-\zeta_{t}\right)=\frac{T}{d}-\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\zeta_{t}.

Rearranging yields (
D.1
).
∎

Appendix E
Uniform lower bound for all suffix sums

Lemma 18

(Maximal Bernstein Inequality for Martingales)
.

Let
\{D_{t},{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t}\}
be a martingale difference sequence. Suppose there are
constants
v>0
and
b>0
such that, for all
m\geq 2
, the conditional
moments satisfy the Bernstein condition

\mathbb{E}[|D_{t}|^{m}\mid{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}]\leq\frac{m!}{2}v^{2}b^{m-2}.

Let
M_{n}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}
. Then, for any
x>0
and integer
N>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq n\leq N}M_{n}\geq x\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2(Nv^{2}+bx)}\right).

Proof.

Fix
\lambda\in(0,1/b)
. Taylor expansion gives

e^{\lambda D_{t}}=1+\lambda D_{t}+\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{m}D_{t}^{m}}{m!}.

Taking conditional expectations and using

\mathbb{E}[D_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=0
, we get

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda D_{t}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq 1+\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{m}}{m!}\mathbb{E}[|D_{t}|^{m}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}].

Substituting the Bernstein moment condition

\mathbb{E}[|D_{t}|^{m}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq\frac{m!}{2}v^{2}b^{m-2}
gives

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda D_{t}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq 1+\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{m}}{m!}\left(\frac{m!}{2}v^{2}b^{m-2}\right)=1+\frac{\lambda^{2}v^{2}}{2}\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}(\lambda b)^{m-2}.

Since
\lambda<1/b
,
\lambda b<1
, and therefore

\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}(\lambda b)^{m-2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\lambda b)^{k}=\frac{1}{1-\lambda b}.

Thus

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda D_{t}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq 1+\frac{\lambda^{2}v^{2}}{2(1-\lambda b)}

Finally, using the elementary inequality
1+y\leq e^{y}
, we bound the conditional moment generating function as follows:

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda D_{t}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq\exp(g(\lambda))\quad\text{where}\quad g(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}v^{2}}{2(1-\lambda b)}

(E.1)

Define the process

Z_{n}=\exp\left(\lambda M_{n}-n\cdot g(\lambda)\right).

Since
M_{n}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}D_{t}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda M_{n-1}+\lambda D_{n}-n\cdot g(\lambda)\right)\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(\lambda M_{n-1}-n\cdot g(\lambda)\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda D_{n}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n-1}]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:exp_up}}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\exp\left(\lambda M_{n-1}-n\cdot g(\lambda)\right)\cdot e^{g(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(\lambda M_{n-1}-(n-1)\cdot g(\lambda)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Z_{n-1}.

Thus
\{Z_{n}\}
is a nonnegative supermartingale with
Z_{0}=1
.

If
\max_{1\leq n\leq N}M_{n}\geq x
, then for some
n\leq N
,

M_{n}\geq x
. Since
g(\lambda)>0
,

\lambda M_{n}-n\cdot g(\lambda)\geq\lambda x-n\cdot g(\lambda)\geq\lambda x-N\cdot g(\lambda).

Therefore
Z_{n}
satisfies

Z_{n}\geq\exp\left(\lambda x-N\cdot g(\lambda)\right),

which implies the event inclusion

\left\{\max_{1\leq n\leq N}M_{n}\geq x\right\}\subseteq\left\{\max_{1\leq n\leq N}Z_{n}\geq\exp\left(\lambda x-N\cdot g(\lambda)\right)\right\}.

By Ville’s inequality (Lemma
12
) with threshold
y=\exp(\lambda x-N\cdot g(\lambda))
:

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq n\leq N}M_{n}\geq x\right)\leq\frac{1}{\exp(\lambda x-N\cdot g(\lambda))}=\exp\left(-(\lambda x-N\cdot g(\lambda))\right),

(E.2)

where we used
Z_{0}=1
.

Set
\lambda=\frac{x}{Nv^{2}+bx}
, which satisfies

\lambda b=\frac{bx}{Nv^{2}+bx}<1
and

\lambda\in(0,1/b)
. Plugging this
\lambda
into Eq. (
E.2
)
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq n\leq N}M_{n}\geq x\right)\leq\exp\left(-(\lambda x-N\cdot g(\lambda))\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\left(\frac{x^{2}}{Nv^{2}+bx}-\frac{x^{2}}{2(Nv^{2}+bx)}\right)\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2(Nv^{2}+bx)}\right),

which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 19
.

Let
\{\bm{u}_{t}\}
be generated by Algorithm

, and let

\bm{a}_{t}
be
{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}
-measurable unit vectors.

If

For

Then, since
Z_{t}=\frac{1}{d}-X_{t}

\sum_{t=T-n}^{T-1}X_{t}=\frac{n}{d}-S_{n}.

(E.4)

Conditioned on
{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}

\bm{a}_{t}

\bm{u}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{I}_{d})

{{\mathcal{F}}}_{t-1}

Then (

We first bound

\bm{\mathcal{H}}_{j,0}={{\mathcal{F}}}_{a_{j}-1}

L_{j}

Proof of Lemma

.

By Lemma

, write

\eta_{t}=1/(4L\left\|\bm{u}_{t}\right\|^{2})
, the coefficient of

Proof of Lemma
8
.

Define the tail error

r_{t}:=\sum_{i=t}^{T-1}\epsilon_{i},\qquad y_{t}:=h_{t}+r_{t}.

Then
r_{t}=\epsilon_{t}+r_{t+1}
and (
4.13
) gives

y_{t+1}\leq y_{t}-a_{t}h_{t}^{2}.

Thus
y_{t}
is nonincreasing. If
y_{T}\leq 2E
, then

h_{T}\leq y_{T}\leq 2E
, and (
4.14
) follows.

It remains to consider the case
y_{T}>2E
. Since
y_{t}
is nonincreasing,

y_{t}\geq y_{T}>2E
for all
t
. Also
r_{t}\leq E
, so

h_{t}=y_{t}-r_{t}\geq y_{t}-E\geq\frac{y_{t}}{2}.

Therefore

y_{t+1}\leq y_{t}-\frac{a_{t}}{4}y_{t}^{2}.

Because
y_{t+1}\geq 0
, the right-hand side is nonnegative. Hence

\frac{1}{y_{t+1}}\geq\frac{1}{y_{t}-a_{t}y_{t}^{2}/4}\geq\frac{1}{y_{t}}+\frac{a_{t}}{4}.

Summing over
t=0,\ldots,T-1
yields

\frac{1}{y_{T}}\geq\frac{1}{y_{0}}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}a_{t}.

Since
y_{0}=h_{0}+E
and
h_{T}\leq y_{T}
, we get

h_{T}\leq\left(\frac{1}{h_{0}+E}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}a_{t}\right)^{-1}.

This also implies (
