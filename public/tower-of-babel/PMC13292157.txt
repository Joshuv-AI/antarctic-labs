Patient Perspectives on Palliative Care in Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension in the United States
Authors: Karim El‐Kersh, Rebecca Crawford, Hilary DuBrock, Peter Classi, Phuong Phan, Lori McLeod, Benjamin Wu, Denise Sese
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPalliative care can be offered concurrently as a treatment option to patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) to relieve symptoms and improve quality of life. However, it is infrequently used and is associated with misconceptions. Limited studies have reported patients' perspectives of palliative care as a treatment option for PAH. We conducted a web‐based survey, informed by a brief targeted literature review and input from patients and clinicians, which was completed by adults (≥ 18 years) in the United States (US) with self‐reported PAH. Patients reported demographic and disease variables and their perspectives and experiences with palliative care. Patients self‐reported their Pulmonary Hypertension Functional Class Self‐Report class, EmPHasis‐10, and 6‐min walk test. A total of 200 respondents (86.5% female; mean age 58.5 years) from 42 US states completed the survey between 19 September and 3 October 2023. Most respondents described palliative care as “end‐of‐life care” (n = 43, 25.7%) and associated it with the need for extra support (n = 111, 55.5%). Many respondents (n = 171, 85.5%) had not discussed palliative care services, nor had a palliative care consultation with their healthcare provider (HCP); most had not been offered a consultation (n = 109/171, 63.7%). Despite this low frequency of discussions, respondents reported feeling comfortable discussing palliative care with their HCPs (n = 177, 88.5%). The survey results demonstrated that these patients with PAH had limited experiences with palliative care, where palliative care was frequently subject to misinterpretation. These findings emphasize the need for proactive engagement between patients and HCPs to ensure palliative care is optimally used and understood.

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a chronic and progressive subtype of pulmonary hypertension (PH), resulting from restricted flow through the pulmonary arterial circulation [1, 2, 3]. It can lead to increased pulmonary vascular resistance and right heart failure, and if left untreated, death [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Common symptoms include dyspnea, fatigue, edema, heart palpitations, lightheadedness, and syncope [3]. Additionally, patients suffer with psychosocial symptoms, including depression, anxiety, and stress, which collectively impair health‐related quality of life (HRQOL) [7, 8, 9, 10].

Although pharmacological products are the mainstay of treatment, palliative care—which aims to relieve physical and emotional symptoms of patients with serious illnesses like PAH—can be provided concurrently [6, 11, 12]. The goal of palliative care is to improve the HRQOL of both the patient and their families and can be provided in either an inpatient or outpatient setting [6, 11, 12]. However, despite these benefits, it is frequently underused; referral in the inpatient setting was reported in only 2.2% of patients between 2001 and 2017 [6, 13]. The lack of access to palliative care in some healthcare systems restricts the availability of treatment to patients [14], and the misconception surrounding the use of palliative care is also a barrier that limits its use. Additional barriers include misconceptions around the utility of palliative care, including the association with end‐of‐life care, as well as some physicians believing that patients cannot receive both palliative care and another PAH therapy simultaneously [6, 14, 15]. Patients who experience greater symptom burden with PAH have been reported to have more negative emotional reactions towards palliative care; their worsening physical condition may heighten fears of death, loss of control, and hopelessness, which is potentially exacerbated by misconception that palliative care is an admission of defeat rather than a supportive measure [16]. Nevertheless, evidence suggests that despite this misconception, some patients may still be willing to discuss it as a treatment option with their physician [17, 18]. Currently, only small, single center studies have assessed the patient perspective toward palliative care as a treatment option for PAH [16]; thus, the aim of the study was to conduct a larger, national study with a broad recruitment of patients with PAH to explore their current perspectives on palliative care.

Methods

Study Design

This was a non‐interventional, cross‐sectional, web‐based survey of adult patients with self‐reported PAH in the United States (US). An electronic survey tool was developed comprising closed‐ended and 4 open‐ended questions on demographic and disease variables, palliative care perspectives and experience, and a standardized patient‐reported outcome instrument, EmPHasis‐10, an HRQOL prognostic marker in patients with PAH, predictive of mortality [19]. The survey content was informed by a brief targeted literature review on palliative care in PAH; 5 key publications were identified: Swetz et al. (2012) [18]; Anand et al. (2020) [13]; Constantine et al. (2021) [20]; Brown et al. (2023) [17]; and Rohlfing et al. (2023) [11]. Common themes that emerged from the selected publications included the low use of palliative care by patients with PAH as well as several misperceptions of palliative care (e.g., patients need to be very sick to receive palliative care, hospice care is equivalent to palliative care, and receipt of palliative care would stop pharmacological treatment of PAH). The survey underwent an iterative process, including review and input from clinicians and patients with PAH, and was developed in US English. Patients' functional class was self‐reported vita the Pulmonary Hypertension Functional Class Self‐Report (PH‐FC‐SR), a patient‐reported version of the World Health Organization functional classification measure, to evaluate symptoms and physical activity impacts relevant to patients [21]. Patients' HRQOL was assessed using EmPHasis‐10, which comprises 10 items that capture PH‐related symptoms, emotional/psychological impacts, and functional limitations [22]. A semantic 6‐point scale (0–5) was used for EmPHasis‐10, with a total maximum score of 50; higher scores correspond to worse quality of life/symptom burden. Respondents were asked to recall the results of their last 6‐min walk test (6MWT) [23], their PAH risk status [24], and whether they were currently enrolled in the Pulmonary Hypertension Association Registry (PHAR). Participants' zip codes were used to derive RTI Rarity Local Social Inequity (LSI) scores, which assess community‐level risks associated with various health outcomes. The RTI Rarity™ project, developed by RTI International, uses artificial intelligence and machine learning to analyze local social determinants of health at the neighborhood level across the US. The RTI Rarity risk score ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values corresponding to greater social risk and a more socially vulnerable area. RTI's institutional review board (STUDY00022600) reviewed and approved this study.

Study Population

Respondents included adults aged ≥ 18 years based in the US, with a self‐reported diagnosis of PAH. All participants were required to speak and comprehend the web‐based survey conducted in English and provide electronic consent. Participants were recruited via a US‐based PAH patient advocacy organization: the Pulmonary Hypertension Association.

Analyses

Quantitative analysis of closed‐ended question data was descriptive with no inferential statistical tests performed and was conducted using Microsoft Excel 2013 (Microsoft Corporation; Redmond, Washington). Data from 200 participants who completed the survey were analyzed as observed, with no imputation of missing data, except in the case of EmPHasis‐10, for which missing data was handled according to its scoring guidelines. EmPHasis‐10 was scored following its standardized scoring guidelines, which included the treatment of individual item non‐response. A qualitative description approach was used for the analysis of the data derived from the open‐ended questions and for the analysis of the open text responses to the “other” category for the close‐ended questions, where appropriate. Information on individuals' experiences in relation to a specific topic was collected to describe what respondents say, rather than to extrapolate or make conceptual inferences [25, 26].

Results

Overall, 200 respondents from 42 US states completed the survey between 19 September and 3 October 2023. Most respondents were female (n = 173, 86.5%), identified as White (n = 164, 82.0%), and had a mean (standard deviation [SD]) age of 58.5 (14.3) years (Table 1). Most respondents had achieved at least some college education or higher (n = 180, 90.0%), and approximately half of the respondents were married, living as married, or in a civil partnership (n = 102, 51.0%); the other half were single, divorced, separated, or widowed (n = 96, 48.0%). Most respondents reported that in the 7 days prior to completing the survey, they had experienced symptoms of either mild (n = 80, 40%) or moderate (n = 89, 44.5%) severity; fatigue (n = 83, 42.1%), shortness of breath (n = 76, 38.6%), and edema (n = 21, 10.7%) were reported as the most bothersome PAH symptoms experienced. The majority of respondents (n = 187, 93.5%) reported that their PAH had impacted their life to some degree in the 7 days prior to survey completion. Only 21.5% (n = 43) of respondents could recall that they were currently enrolled in PHAR, compared with nearly half (n = 98, 49%) who could not remember if they were currently enrolled. The top 3 reported treatments currently used by respondents included diuretics (n = 123, 62.1%), oxygen therapy (n = 85, 42.9%), and tadalafil (n = 84, 42.4%). The most common PAH‐specific medication class was phosphodiesterase‐5 inhibitors and soluble guanylate cyclase stimulators (n = 148, 74.7%) (e‐Table 1); one‐fifth of patients were on parenteral prostacyclin therapy (n = 38, 19.2%). One‐third of patients were on dual therapy (n = 67, 33.8%), while nearly another third of patients were on triple therapy (n = 60, 30.3%).

Perspectives on Palliative Care

Knowledge and Interpretation of Palliative Care

Overall, most respondents (n = 167, 83.5%) provided their own definition of palliative care; the most frequent interpretations described palliative care as either “end‐of‐life care” (n = 43, 25.7%), “comfort care” (n = 22, 13.2%), or support (n = 16, 9.6%) (Table 3). Other respondent descriptions included specialized care, care for chronic/serious/terminal conditions, and care designed to improve/maintain HRQOL and manage symptoms. For the 167 respondents who were familiar with palliative care, the most common source of information pertaining to palliative care services identified by respondents was provided by hospital medical staff (doctors, nurses, allied health workers) (n = 39/167, 23.4%), the internet/social media (online patient forums) (n = 32/167, 19.2%), and family/friends (n = 32/167, 19.2%). Nearly 25% of respondents reported that they had not received any information about palliative care services or were not sure of the information source (n = 41/167, 24.6%). Over 50% of respondents associated palliative care with the following statements: the need for extra support (n = 111, 55.5%), that more support would result in improved well‐being (n = 110, 55.0%), and that the patient's PAH is a terminal, incurable disease (n = 107, 53.5%) (e‐Table 2).

Palliative Care Experience and Discussions With Healthcare Professionals (HCPs)

Most respondents (n = 171) had not discussed palliative care services nor had a consultation with their HCP about palliative care; almost two‐thirds of these respondents reported that a palliative care consultation had not been offered (n = 109/171, 63.7%), or they were not “sick enough” for palliative care (n = 80, 46.8%) (Figure 3; Table 4). Despite this, the majority (n = 177, 88.5%) stated they would feel comfortable discussing palliative care with their HCPs. For the 15 respondents who indicated that they would not feel comfortable discussing palliative care with their HCP, several themes emerged: preferring not to think about palliative care or not feeling mentally ready to think about palliative care (n = 4), lack of knowledge about palliative care (n = 3), and disease stability (i.e., palliative care not currently required) (n = 3). Palliative care discussions with HCPs engendered a range of emotions for respondents; feeling “anxious” (n = 85, 42.5%) and “hopeful” (n = 61, 30.5%) were the most frequently reported emotional impacts.

Over 50% of respondents selected the response options “help me prepare for the future” (n = 109, 54.8%) and “provide emotional support” (n = 105, 52.8%) in terms of how palliative care could help them now in terms of their current PAH severity (e‐Table 3). Discussion

This study provides insights into the current perspectives on palliative care among patients with PAH, including patients' interpretations, experiences, discussions with HCPs, and views on palliative care as a support for patients. The results provided emphasis on the importance of including patient‐reported outcomes in clinical practice for understanding patient experiences and preferences, guiding holistic treatment decisions, and enhancing patient‐centered care in PAH management.

The majority of the respondents included in this survey were currently taking combination PAH‐specific therapy, consistent with current recommendations from the European Society of Cardiology and European Respiratory Society guidelines [27]. The high prevalence of combination PAH‐specific therapy among respondents underscores the advanced disease severity and associated symptom burden within this population. Given the complex treatment regimens and potential for significant adverse effects [28, 29], there is a clear role for early integration of palliative care to address physical symptoms, enhance quality of life, and support psychosocial needs. However, the most common self‐reported definition of palliative care by respondents was “end‐of‐life care” and was mainly associated with being an “additional support,” a key theme reported by respondents for the need for palliative care. These findings may explain why half of the respondents preferred the term “supportive care” to describe palliative care. Previous studies have also reported that patients negatively perceive palliative care as comparable with end‐of‐life care, equating it with hospice care, “giving up,” or “dying” [16, 17, 18]. Despite this, patients have been shown to be open to discussing palliative care with their physician despite these negative perceptions [17, 18]. These negative associations may be influenced by patients' limited understanding of palliative care or never having heard the term, which was the case for some respondents in this survey. However, when respondents were presented with the definition of palliative care, they became open to it as a treatment option, which may be a contributing factor to the misconception of palliative care. Collectively, these findings indicate that greater education and awareness of palliative care are needed to positively influence the patient perspective on palliative care.

With regard to discussing palliative care with HCPs, most respondents had not discussed palliative care services, nor had they had a consultation with their HCP. In addition, the inability of nearly half of respondents to recall their enrollment in PHAR may indicate limited patient engagement or awareness regarding registry participation. This highlights potential areas for improving communication between HCPs and patients regarding the purpose and importance of PHAR. Indeed, there is no established clinical criteria for referral to palliative care for PAH patients; historically, palliative care has been reserved for the advanced stages of PH [13, 20]. In some cases, it is only administered at the end of a patient's life when a patient is significantly ill, resulting in the need for a longer hospital stay [11, 13]. This is partially due to physicians' association of palliative care with end‐of‐life care, despite the World Health Organization emphasizing the need for palliative care at the early stages of disease [11, 13, 30]. Therefore, untimely utilization of palliative care by physicians restricts the accessibility of it as a treatment option for patients with PAH. These associations may explain why some patients feel that they are not “sick enough” to receive palliative care—as reported by many respondents in this survey.

Despite respondents self‐reporting good general health, many reported the presence of PAH symptoms and impaired HRQOL. Self‐reported physical symptoms associated with PAH in this study, like fatigue, have previously been documented in other PAH studies [21, 31]. Additionally, the respondent‐recalled 6MWT scores and results of the EmPHasis‐10 revealed that higher PH‐FC‐SR classifications are associated with greater impairment of walking and HRQOL. This trend was consistent with associations observed between 6MWT, EmPHasis‐10, and physician‐assessed World Health Organization functional classification [19]. Taken together, these findings support the potential utility of patient‐reported outcomes for remotely assessing PAH severity in settings where physician‐based evaluations are not feasible.

This study directly collected information on patients' perspectives and experiences with palliative care in PAH at scale via a standardized approach. The results have highlighted areas for improvement in educating patients and HCPs about palliative care services, as well as the need for improvement in communication between patients and HCPs. However, study limitations should be considered, including the self‐selecting nature of the recruitment process and the patient self‐reported diagnosis and disease severity. Due to the topic of the survey, response bias—specifically social desirability bias—has the potential to affect the reliability of the data collected. However, the survey was anonymously completed, which may reduce social desirability bias. Notably, digital exclusion was a limitation because only patients with access to email and web‐based surveys were able to participate, as well as being required to be proficient in English. Furthermore, a mixture of close‐ended and open‐ended questions was included to capture nuanced experiences, but these may not fully capture nuanced patient knowledge and experiences associated with palliative care. Open‐ended questions were strategically selected to allow greater depth for respondent feedback on key areas of interest, and although the survey content underwent rigorous testing with HCPs and patients, it is possible that respondents who are less familiar with certain medical terminology may misunderstand or misinterpret questions. While the majority of our patients (64%) reported receiving double or triple combination therapy consistent with the management of group 1 PAH, it is possible that individuals from other groups, such as those with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction‐associated PH (HFpEF‐PH), may have also been included in this study.

The findings from this survey suggest that respondents are aware of the benefit of palliative care as a resource to help manage their PAH. Although previous research has demonstrated that patients perceive palliative care as comparable with end‐of‐life care, this study suggests the understanding of palliative care by patients with PAH goes beyond the common misconception of perceiving it as only comparable with end‐of‐life care. This survey highlights the need for proactive engagement between patients and their HCPs to ensure palliative care is optimally used and understood by both HCP and patient to benefit patients' well‐being and ability to manage their PAH.
