Stochastic threshold dynamics of an HIV model with latent cell activation under mean-reverting environmental noise
Authors: Zhongwei Cao, Yuchen Jiang, Zhenfeng Shi, Daqing Jiang, Su Wei, Yaxin Zhou
Published: 2027

ABSTRACT
Motivated by the impact of environmental variability on within-host HIV dynamics, we develop a stochastic HIV model incorporating latent cell activation. Infection and activation rates are modeled as logarithmic mean-reverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes, ensuring positive parameters and biologically realistic fluctuations. We prove the existence and uniqueness of a global positive solution and derive stochastic thresholds characterizing persistence and extinction. If the persistence threshold exceeds one, the model admits a stationary distribution, indicating sustained viral presence; conversely, sufficiently strong environmental noise can drive latent cells, actively infected cells, and free virus to extinction almost surely, even when the deterministic model predicts persistence. Analysis of the associated Fokker-Planck equation provides explicit expressions for the covariance structure near quasi-equilibrium, quantifying stochastic fluctuations. Numerical simulations support the theoretical findings and illustrate how environmental noise reshapes HIV dynamics. Our results highlight the crucial role of stochastic variability in modulating viral persistence and latent reservoir dynamics.

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) is a retrovirus that targets the human immune system and has constituted a major global public health challenge since its discovery. Globally, HIV has resulted in approximately 40.4 million deaths and infected about 85.6 million individuals (UNAIDS, 2023). After entering the host, HIV primarily infects CD4+ T lymphocytes. Through reverse transcription, viral RNA is converted into DNA and subsequently integrated into the host genome. The infected cells then exploit the host cellular machinery to produce new virions, ultimately leading to cell destruction (Deeks et al., 2015). As CD4+ T cells are progressively depleted, HIV-infected individuals become increasingly susceptible to opportunistic infections. Consequently, many HIV-related complications, including mortality, are primarily attributable to secondary microbial infections rather than the direct cytopathic effect of the virus itself (Nowak & McMichael, 1995).

Mathematical modeling has become an indispensable tool for elucidating the within-host dynamics of infectious diseases. By constructing appropriate mathematical frameworks, it is possible to quantitatively describe viral replication, immune responses, and treatment effects, thereby providing theoretical insights into disease progression and control strategies. Since the early development of HIV research, numerous mathematical models have been proposed to investigate viral dynamics within the human body (Merrill, 1989, 2018, pp. 371–385, pp. 59–75; Tan & Wu, 1998). Classical HIV models typically consist of three compartments: uninfected target cells, infected cells, and free virus particles. For example, Elaiw (Elaiw, 2010) studied the global dynamics of an HIV model incorporating an exposed class and a nonlinear incidence rate. By constructing suitable Lyapunov functions, the global asymptotic stability of the disease equilibria was rigorously established.

Despite their success in capturing the fundamental mechanisms of HIV infection, classical models overlook an important biological feature: not all infected cells actively produce virus particles. Experimental studies indicate that a proportion of CD4+ T cells enters a long-lived dormant state following infection, during which viral replication is effectively suppressed (BAGASRA & POMERANTZ, 1993). These cells, commonly referred to as latent infected cells (Pace et al., 2011), form a persistent viral reservoir characterized by slow turnover. Although latent infected cells do not continuously produce free virions, they retain the capacity to rapidly release virus upon activation, particularly after ∗∗the∗∗ interruption of antiretroviral therapy. Moreover, because latent reservoirs evade immune surveillance and therapeutic elimination, complete viral eradication within the host's lifetime remains unattainable.

In recent years, increasing attention has been devoted to the role of latent infected cells in HIV dynamics. A variety of mathematical models incorporating latency and activation mechanisms have been developed (Pankavich, 2016; Wang et al., 2017, 2021). For instance, Wang (Wang et al., 2015) proposed an HIV model with latent infected cells and a saturated incidence rate, while Rong (Rong & Perelson, 2009) further investigated antigen-induced activation of latent cells by extending earlier models (Jones & Perelson, 2007a; Kim & Perelson, 2006). These studies demonstrate that latent infection plays a crucial role in viral persistence and treatment outcomes.

Recent developments in mathematical modeling of HIV dynamics have increasingly focused on linking within-host processes to population-level outcomes and intervention strategies. For instance, Wei et al. proposed a compartmental community transmission model incorporating HIV detection and monitoring, demonstrating that improved detection and monitoring rates can raise the stability threshold and help control infection spread in a community setting (Wei et al., 2026). Similarly, stochastic transmission models with protection awareness and fluctuating contact behaviors have been analyzed to understand how awareness and therapy can influence long-term epidemic outcomes (Zhai et al., 2023). In addition, models that explicitly incorporate HIV testing and contact tracing mechanisms have shown that early detection at the population level can significantly reduce hidden transmission and influence overall epidemic trajectories (Ouyang et al., 2025). These studies illustrate the importance of integrating detection mechanisms, stochastic effects, and behavioral factors into HIV models, suggesting promising avenues for extending our current within-host framework to multiscale, population-level settings where detection, monitoring, and intervention strategies play a central role.

From a mathematical standpoint, incorporating a latent compartment introduces multiple time scales into the system, reflecting the fast dynamics of viral replication and the slow evolution of the latent reservoir. Furthermore, activation of latent infected cells acts as an endogenous source of actively infected cells, even when viral loads are substantially suppressed. Consequently, the qualitative analysis of such models becomes significantly more intricate, and analytical techniques developed for standard three-dimensional HIV models are not directly applicable. In particular, the presence of latency may modify the basic reproduction number, influence the global stability of equilibria, and generate more complex long-term dynamical behaviors.

Motivated by the above biological and mathematical considerations, we formulate and analyze a deterministic HIV model that explicitly incorporates latent infected cells. The model describes the interactions among uninfected target cells, latent infected cells, actively infected cells, and free virus particles, thereby providing a tractable framework for investigating the impact of latency on within-host HIV dynamics.

Although the deterministic model captures the essential mechanisms of within-host HIV dynamics, real biological systems are inevitably subject to environmental fluctuations arising from variability in immune responses, treatment adherence, and other external factors. Incorporating stochastic perturbations is therefore necessary for a more realistic and mathematically robust description of disease evolution.

Stochastic epidemic models have been extensively studied in recent years (Cheng et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2019). For example, Gao (Gao et al., 2019) showed that environmental noise may significantly alter disease transmission patterns, while Cai (Cai et al., 2018) demonstrated that mean-reverting stochastic perturbations can suppress outbreaks. Qi (Qi & Meng, 2021) further observed that sufficiently strong noise may drive HIV to extinction. These results highlight that stochasticity can fundamentally change the long-term behavior predicted by deterministic models.

In the literature, environmental noise is typically introduced either through linear white-noise perturbations (Mao et al., 2002; Meng et al., 2018) or via mean-reverting Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) processes (Shi & Jiang, 2023; Zhou et al., 2022). Compared with white noise, OU processes incorporate mean reversion and temporal correlation, providing a more realistic description of regulated biological parameters while maintaining analytical tractability (Shi & Jiang, 2023).

From a mathematical perspective, the use of logarithmic OU processes offers two additional advantages. First, it guarantees parameter positivity without imposing artificial constraints. Second, it transforms multiplicative environmental fluctuations into additive noise in logarithmic coordinates, which facilitates Lyapunov analysis and the derivation of stationary distributions.

Although environmental variability may affect all parameters, we restrict stochastic perturbations to two key quantities that dominate HIV dynamics: the infection rate k(t) and the activation rate of latent infected cells a(t). The infection rate directly determines viral transmission efficiency, while the activation rate governs the release of virus from the latent reservoir, a central obstacle in HIV eradication strategies. Introducing stochasticity into these parameters allows us to quantify how environmental fluctuations influence both viral persistence and extinction.

To preserve positivity and incorporate mean-reverting fluctuations, we assume that k(t) and a(t) satisfy the logarithmic Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes:(1.4)dlogk(t)=λ1logk¯−logk(t)dt+σ1dB1(t),dloga(t)=λ2loga¯−loga(t)dt+σ2dB2(t),where B1(t) and B2(t) are independent standard Brownian motions, and λi > 0, σi > 0 (i = 1, 2) denote the mean-reversion rates and noise intensities, respectively.

Under this formulation, the resulting system becomes a nonlinear stochastic differential equation with correlated multiplicative effects and multiple time scales. The presence of stochastic activation of latent cells introduces additional analytical challenges, particularly in establishing global positivity, identifying stochastic threshold parameters, and characterizing invariant probability measures. These features distinguish the present model from classical three-dimensional stochastic HIV systems and motivate the theoretical analysis developed in the subsequent sections.

Accordingly, the stochastic counterpart of model (1.1) is formulated as follows:(1.5)dT(t)=λ−dTT(t)−(1−ϵ)k(t)V(t)T(t)dt,dL(t)=αL(1−ϵ)k(t)V(t)T(t)−(dL+a(t))L(t)dt,dT∗(t)=(1−αL)(1−ϵ)k(t)V(t)T(t)−δT∗(t)+a(t)L(t)dt,dV(t)=NδT∗(t)−cV(t)dt,dlogk(t)=λ1logk¯−logk(t)dt+σ1dB1(t),dloga(t)=λ2loga¯−loga(t)dt+σ2dB2(t).

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents preliminary definitions and auxiliary lemmas. Section 3 establishes the existence and uniqueness of a global positive solution and derives an invariant region for the stochastic model. Section 4 provides sufficient conditions for the existence of a stationary distribution via Lyapunov function techniques. Section 5 derives explicit expressions for the probability density function in a neighborhood of the quasi-equilibrium point. Section 6 establishes sufficient conditions for the exponential extinction of latent infected cells, actively infected cells, and free virus particles. Section 7 presents numerical simulations to illustrate the theoretical findings. Finally, concluding remarks and future research directions are discussed.

Existence and uniqueness of a global solution

Depending on the biological meaning represented by T, L, V, T∗, k and a, they should all be non-negative. Therefore, we need to give the existence and uniqueness of the global positive solution.Theorem 3.1For any initial value T(0),L(0),T∗(0),V(0),k(0),a(0)T∈R+6, there exists a unique solution for model (1.5) on t ≥ 0 and the solution T(t),L(t),T∗(t),V(t),k(t),a(t)T will remain in R+6 with probability one.Proof. Since all the parameters of the stochastic model (5) are locally Lipschitz continuous, then model (5) has a unique local solution T(t),L(t),T∗(t),V(t),k(t),a(t)T on 0,τe, where τe is an explosion time. If we prove that τe = ∞ a.s., then the stochastic solution T(t),L(t),T∗(t),V(t),k(t),a(t) is global. Choose a sufficiently large constant n0 ≥ 1, which guarantees that T(0), L(0), T∗(0), V(0), k(0) and a(0) all lie in the interval 1n0,n0. Conclusion

In this study, we analyzed a stochastic within-host HIV model incorporating latent cell activation, with infection and activation rates subject to environmental fluctuations modeled by logarithmic mean-reverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes. These results show that environmental variability fundamentally reshapes within-host HIV dynamics. Noise acting on key parameters can suppress viral persistence or induce extinction, emphasizing the importance of incorporating stochastic effects in models with latent reservoirs.

Mathematically, the study extends deterministic threshold theory to a stochastic framework with latent infection, multiplicative mean-reverting noise, and multi-scale dynamics. The distinct stochastic thresholds R0s and R0e highlight how environmental noise modifies the effective reproduction mechanism. Future work may incorporate stochasticity in additional biological parameters (Kang et al., 2026; Ma & Wang, 2026), explore non-Gaussian or state-dependent noise, and develop a unified framework linking deterministic and stochastic reproduction numbers.
