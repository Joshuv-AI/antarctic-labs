Targeted cryostimulation of myofascial trigger points modulates exercise-induced physiological tremor in adolescent swimmers: a randomized counterbalanced crossover trial
Authors: Szymon Kuliś, Maciej Skorulski, Bianca Callegari, Przemysław Pietraszewski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysiological tremor is known to increase following intense exercise due to neuromuscular fatigue and altered sensorimotor control. While cryotherapy is widely used in sports medicine to modulate peripheral excitability and afferent feedback, its potential role in attenuating exercise-induced tremor has not been directly examined. This study investigated whether targeted cryostimulation of myofascial trigger points (MTrPs) can modulate lower-limb physiological tremor in adolescent swimmers.MethodsThirty-four competitive adolescent swimmers completed a randomized counterbalanced crossover trial involving two conditions: 60-s localized cryostimulation of identified MTrPs and a control condition without intervention. Tremor of both lower limbs was recorded using accelerometry at baseline and after a standardized 7-min recovery period following a 30-s maximal tethered swimming test. Log-transformed tremor spectral power [L(2–5 Hz), L(9–14 Hz)] and tremor frequency parameters [F(2–5 Hz), F(9–14 Hz)] were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA. ResultsExercise increased tremor spectral power, whereas MTrP cryostimulation attenuated the post-exercise response in the 9–14 Hz frequency band (Condition × Time interaction, p = 0.010). The modulatory effect was observed predominantly in tremor spectral power, whereas frequency outcomes were more heterogeneous: F(2–5 Hz) did not show a consistent overall post-exercise shift, whereas F(9–14 Hz) increased following exercise and remained lower in the cryostimulation condition. ConclusionsTargeted pre-exercise localized cooling of MTrPs may partially modulate selected post-exercise neuromuscular responses in adolescent swimmers, particularly within the 9–14 Hz tremor frequency band, although the functional relevance of these effects for sport-specific performance remains to be established.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT07480200, registered 13 March 2026 (retrospectively registered)Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-01839-x.Keywords: Physiological tremor, Cryostimulation, Myofascial trigger points, Neuromuscular fatigue, Peripheral sensory modulation, Adolescent athletes

Introduction

Physiological tremor and fatigue

Physiological tremor, a fundamental characteristic of human movement, arises from the complex interplay of mechanical and neural oscillators within the neuromuscular system [1]. Contemporary neurophysiological models describe tremor as an emergent phenomenon arising from sensorimotor loop dynamics, including motor unit discharge variability, reflex gain, and afferent feedback integration [2]. Defined as an involuntary, low amplitude, high frequency oscillation of body parts in healthy individuals [3], it is generally benign but can be exacerbated by various factors, including fatigue, stress, anxiety, caffeine, or other stimulants [1]. Exercise induced fatigue, in particular, increases motor unit synchronization and variability of discharge rates, which contributes to amplified tremor spectral power [4]. Strenuous activity, for example, leads to increased muscle shaking due to neuromuscular fatigue; high-intensity or fatiguing workouts such as swimming are known to elevate tremor spectral power [5]. Studies in athletes confirm that post-exercise tremor spectral power is significantly higher than at rest [6]. This transient increase serves as a normal physiological response to muscle fatigue and an indicator of neuromuscular strain or insufficient recovery [7]. Consequently, monitoring tremor parameters in the frequency domain offers a non-invasive method for evaluating the body’s immediate response to physical exertion in athletic populations [5, 8]. While previous research has established the utility of accelerometric measurements for tracking tremor fluctuations following maximum intensity efforts on arm ergometers [5], the specific impact of therapeutic interventions such as local cryostimulation on modulating these post-exercise neuromuscular responses remains underexplored. Physiological tremor can be objectively quantified using accelerometry, which captures small involuntary oscillatory limb movements with high temporal resolution. Accelerometric assessment has become a widely accepted method for investigating fatigue-related changes in neuromuscular control because it provides a non-invasive measure of motor output variability [9]. Importantly, physiological tremor is composed of several oscillatory components that arise from different physiological mechanisms. Therefore, frequency-domain analysis based on power spectral density (PSD) is commonly used to characterize tremor behavior within specific frequency bands.

The low-frequency range (2–5 Hz) is generally associated with mechanical and postural control mechanisms, including limb biomechanics and fatigue-related changes in motor control. In contrast, oscillations within the 9–14 Hz range are thought to reflect central neural drive, stretch-reflex loop activity, and sensorimotor integration processes [10]. Previous studies investigating physiological tremor in athletes have demonstrated that these frequency bands are particularly sensitive to exercise-induced neuromuscular fatigue and recovery-related changes [11–13]. Therefore, analysis of tremor spectral power within the 2–5 Hz and 9–14 Hz bands was selected to evaluate potential effects of cryostimulation on fatigue-related neuromuscular responses.

Myofascial trigger points and tremor

One promising avenue for such interventions involves targeting myofascial trigger points, which are hyperirritable spots in taut bands of skeletal muscle fibers [14, 15]. They often manifest as tender “knots” in the muscle and are associated with local pain and stiffness [16]. Beyond pain, MTrPs can impair muscle function – for instance, latent MTrPs have been linked to subtle motor control issues like increased antagonist muscle activity during movement [17]. Consequently, in adolescent athletes, repetitive muscle use may have led to the development of MTrPs in the lower limb muscles [18], potentially affecting muscle coordination or contributing to post-exercise tremor [19]. Myofascial trigger points are associated with altered sensory input, increased nociceptive activity, and changes in local neuromuscular function [20].These factors may have influenced motor control and reflex regulation, particularly following strenuous exercise when neuromuscular fatigue is present. Cryostimulation applied directly to trigger points may reduce local sensory input, modify afferent feedback, and alter spinal reflex excitability. Consequently, targeted cooling of MTrPs could potentially influence physiological tremor, which is thought to arise from interactions among central neural drive, reflex mechanisms, and peripheral biomechanical factors. Although cryotherapy has been widely investigated in the context of recovery, pain reduction, and fatigue management, its potential influence on exercise-induced physiological tremor has received little attention. Furthermore, previous studies have generally focused on cooling larger anatomical regions rather than clinically identified trigger points [21, 22]. Therefore, the effects of targeted MTrP cryostimulation on post-exercise tremor remain unclear.

Cryotherapy mechanisms

Local cryostimulation therapy is a common sports medicine intervention to alleviate muscle pain, inflammation, and spasms [23]. Cryotherapy can reduce nerve conduction velocity and muscle spindle activity, producing a short-term calming effect on muscle tissue [24]. By modulating peripheral excitability, this cooling effect may theoretically influence the amplitude and frequency characteristics of physiological tremor, particularly in the context of exercise induced neuromuscular fatigue [25]. In trigger point therapy, cooling (e.g. ice massage or spray and stretch techniques) is used to relieve MTrP pain and muscle tightness [26]. Studies report that applying ice can rapidly decrease trigger-point pain and improve joint range of motion in athletes or patients [27]. However, the specific physiological mechanisms by which local cryostimulation at myofascial trigger points might modulate exercise-induced tremor in adolescent swimmers have not been fully elucidated, warranting further investigation into this therapeutic approach [15].

Knowledge gap and study novelty

Despite knowledge that exercise augments tremor and that MTrP therapy (including cryotherapy) can alter muscle condition, no prior studies have directly examined whether treating MTrPs with local ice can affect physiological tremor [16, 28]. The present study was designed to investigate this effect of local cryostimulation of myofascial trigger points on the physiological tremor of the lower limbs in adolescent swimmers. Using a crossover design, each athlete underwent both conditions (cryostimulation vs. control) with measurements taken at baseline and post-exercise. This design allowed us to compare tremor changes with and without MTrP cryostimulation within the same individuals, controlling for inter-subject differences. Although local cryotherapy has been extensively used in sports medicine to reduce pain, inflammation, and perceived fatigue, its potential influence on exercise-induced physiological tremor remains poorly understood. Furthermore, most previous studies have applied cooling to large anatomical regions or entire muscle groups. In contrast, the present study focused on targeted cryostimulation of clinically identified myofascial trigger points (MTrPs), which represent localized areas of altered neuromuscular function and increased sensory input. This targeted approach may provide a more specific modulation of peripheral sensory feedback and neuromuscular control than conventional local cryotherapy, thereby potentially influencing post-exercise tremor responses.

Aim and hypothesis

Various recovery strategies, including stretching, massage, foam rolling, and cryotherapy, have been proposed to alleviate post-exercise neuromuscular disturbances. Among these approaches, cryostimulation is particularly attractive because it can be applied rapidly and locally before exercise as a brief sensory-modulation procedure, potentially influencing peripheral sensory input and neuromuscular function during the subsequent fatigue response, while directly influencing peripheral sensory input and neuromuscular function. However, the effects of targeted cryostimulation applied to clinically identified myofascial trigger points on exercise-induced physiological tremor remain unknown. We hypothesized that local cryostimulation of MTrPs would attenuate the exercise-induced increase in physiological tremor of the lower limbs. Specifically, compared with the control condition, cryostimulation was expected to result in a smaller PRE-to-POST increase in tremor spectral power, particularly within frequency bands sensitive to fatigue-related neuromuscular responses.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study employed a randomized counterbalanced crossover design. Each participant completed two experimental conditions: (1) local cryostimulation of myofascial trigger points (MTrPs) and (2) a control condition without cryostimulation. The order of experimental conditions was randomized using a computer-generated randomization list and counterbalanced across participants to minimize potential order and carryover effects. In each condition, physiological tremor of the lower limbs was assessed at baseline and following a standardized exercise protocol. This study was reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 guidelines for randomized trials.

Participants

Thirty-four adolescent male competitive swimmers (N = 34) voluntarily participated in the study. The participants were healthy, free from diagnosed neuromuscular or musculoskeletal disorders, and reported no lower limb injuries within the six months preceding the experiment. Their mean age was 15.0 ± 2.0 years, body height 172.4 ± 7.8 cm, and body mass 63.5 ± 8.6 kg. Twenty-nine participants declared right limb dominance, whereas five reported being non right limb dominant. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before participation. In the case of minors, written informed consent was additionally obtained from their parents or legal guardians. The study protocol was approved by the Bioethics Committee at the Józef Piłsudski University of Physical Education in Warsaw (approval number SKE 01–60/2024). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki as amended.

Sample size estimation

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1) to determine the minimum required sample size. The calculation was performed for a repeated measures ANOVA with within-subject factors, corresponding to the primary hypothesis concerning the interaction between exercise condition (baseline vs. post-exercise (after the standardized 7-min recovery period)) and intervention condition (cryostimulation vs. control). Because no previous studies had specifically investigated the effects of targeted cryostimulation of myofascial trigger points on exercise-induced physiological tremor, an empirical effect size estimate was not available. Therefore, a medium effect size (f = 0.25) was selected according to Cohen’s conventional benchmarks for ANOVA-based designs and was considered a reasonable and conservative assumption for sample size estimation [29]. The analysis assumed an alpha level of 0.05 and a desired statistical power of 0.80. Four repeated measurements were specified (control–baseline, control–post-exercise, cryostimulation –baseline, cryostimulation –post-exercise), with an assumed correlation of 0.5 among repeated measures and a nonsphericity correction factor (ε) of 1.0. Based on these assumptions, the required total sample size was estimated at 24 participants, yielding an actual statistical power of 0.82. To account for potential exclusions or dropouts, a total of 34 participants were recruited and included in the study.

Cryostimulation procedure and anaerobic test protocol

Local cryostimulation of myofascial trigger points (MTrPs) was performed immediately after the baseline tremor assessment and prior to the exercise bout in order to acutely modulate peripheral neuromuscular input before fatigue induction. Trigger points were identified using the flat palpation technique within the anterior (quadriceps femoris) and posterior (hamstring) thigh muscle groups. Myofascial trigger points were identified according to established clinical diagnostic criteria, including the presence of a palpable taut band, a hypersensitive tender spot within the taut band, and reproduction of local tenderness upon palpation [20, 30]. All participants presented identifiable trigger points within the examined muscle groups. If no palpable trigger points had been identified, the participant would not have been eligible for the cryostimulation procedure. All palpation procedures were conducted by the same licensed physiotherapist experienced in MTrP assessment to ensure methodological consistency and reduce inter-examiner variability. Two MTrPs were identified bilaterally within the quadriceps femoris and two bilaterally within the hamstring muscle group, resulting in eight marked trigger points per participant. Cryostimulation was applied simultaneously to all identified MTrPs using cold packs for 60 s. A thin layer of dry paper towel was placed between the cold pack and the skin to prevent excessive local cooling and reduce the risk of cold-related skin irritation. Cold packs were manually held in firm contact with the marked trigger points by the same examiner throughout the 60 s application period. Although application pressure was not instrumentally quantified, it was kept consistent across participants by the same experienced physiotherapist to minimize inter-session variability. The cold packs were stored in a sealed polystyrene container filled with ice cubes, with the internal temperature maintained between − 8 °C and − 1 °C throughout the Cryostimulation session. Skin temperature was not directly measured; however, the short 60 s exposure duration was selected to induce transient sensory modulation without deep tissue cooling. The 60 s exposure duration was selected to induce acute modulation of cutaneous and intramuscular afferent input without producing prolonged reductions in nerve conduction velocity or impairing subsequent motor performance. This duration is considered sufficient to transiently influence muscle spindle sensitivity and peripheral sensory feedback mechanisms, which are known contributors to physiological tremor regulation. Previous evidence suggests that short-duration cooling can influence peripheral sensory feedback, muscle spindle sensitivity, and neuromuscular regulation, whereas longer cooling periods may produce more pronounced inhibitory effects on neuromuscular function. Therefore, a 60-s application was considered appropriate for eliciting transient sensory modulation before the exercise bout [31]. Immediately after the intervention, participants performed a 30 s all-out tethered swimming test designed as a Wingate-like anaerobic protocol adapted to aquatic conditions. The test required maximal lower-limb effort for 30 s while the swimmer was attached via a tether to a starting block. A dynamometer was integrated into the tethering system; however, it was not used for quantitative force analysis in this study. Its function was solely to provide controlled tethered resistance and ensure standardized exercise conditions. Following the exercise bout, a 7 min recovery period was implemented in accordance with the recommendations of Gajewski [5]. Subsequently, physiological tremor measurements were repeated using the same protocol as applied at baseline. This design enabled the evaluation of post-exercise tremor spectral power as a function of altered peripheral sensory input following localized cryostimulation.

Outcome measures

Physiological tremor of the left and right lower limbs was recorded using a triaxial accelerometer attached distally to the limb under standardized postural conditions. Acceleration signals were sampled at 200 Hz. Only the vertical acceleration component was used for further analysis. The testing procedure for physiological tremor of the lower limbs consisted of two measurements: at baseline (pre-exercise) and post-exercise (after the standardized 7-min recovery period). The exercise consisted of a 30-s bout of maximal tethered front crawl swimming. Each tremor measurement was conducted with the participant in a supine position on a mat. The upper limbs were abducted to 90° at the shoulder joints and externally rotated, with the dorsal side of the hands not in contact with the ground. The lower limb under examination was positioned with 90° flexion at both the hip and knee joints, while the ankle joint was maintained in a neutral position. Physiological tremor was recorded using a triaxial accelerometer (REJ 0006 J; JBA ZB. Staniak, Warsaw, Poland) characterized by a high signal-to-noise ratio, ensuring that predominantly involuntary oscillations were captured. The accelerometer was attached to a 1-kg inertial load positioned approximately 1 cm above the lateral malleolus [12, 32]. Based on previous methodological work, this inertial load, corresponding to less than 5% of the maximal voluntary contraction (MVC) of the knee flexors was selected to enhance measurement stability without inducing fatigue or interfering with the physiological tremor signal, even in younger or less physically developed participants. The duration of each tremor recording was 34 s for each limb. Including the 30 s inter-limb interval and participant positioning, the tremor assessment required approximately 3–4 min per time point, resulting in a total assessment duration of approximately 6–8 min per Cryostimulation session (baseline and post-exercise). The fixed recording duration (34 s) was selected to ensure sufficient signal length for reliable frequency-domain analysis while minimizing postural instability. Participants were instructed to maintain the tested limb as still as possible throughout the measurement. A 30-s interval was allowed between testing the right and left lower limbs. A 31.72-s segment of the vertical acceleration component was extracted for further analysis using the ZPP-3D/BC Acceleration Measurement Set (JBA ZB. Staniak, Poland). The primary outcome was exercise-induced change in tremor power within the 2–5 Hz band. Time-domain acceleration amplitude was not included as an outcome because the primary objective of the study was to quantify exercise-induced changes in physiological tremor within established frequency bands associated with neuromuscular and reflex-related oscillatory activity. Frequency-domain analysis based on power spectral density (PSD) allows separation of tremor components occurring in distinct physiological frequency ranges and has been widely recommended in previous tremor research. In contrast, time-domain amplitude measures provide only a global estimate of signal magnitude and do not allow differentiation between frequency-specific mechanisms contributing to physiological tremor. Secondary outcomes included tremor power in the 9–14 Hz band and tremor frequency parameters. The experimental setup and accelerometer placement are illustrated in (Fig. 2).

Signal processing

The vertical acceleration component was extracted for further analysis because it represented the primary direction of postural oscillation during the tremor assessment task. Prior to spectral analysis, signals were visually inspected and segmented into artifact-free epochs. The analyzed signal consisted of 6144 samples and was divided into six consecutive segments of 1024 samples each. A cosine window was applied to each segment before fast Fourier transform (FFT) computation to reduce spectral leakage [5]. Power spectral density (PSD) estimates obtained from all segments were subsequently averaged to generate a representative spectrum for each recording. Signal processing was performed using the proprietary TDA1v0 software (JBA Poland) [6, 11], which automatically processed the recorded accelerometry signals according to the manufacturer’s signal-analysis procedure. The acceleration raw signal was sampled at 200 Hz and subsequently subjected to spectral analysis using the fast Fourier transform (FFT) [9]. The present study focused on tremor spectral power within the 2–5 Hz and 9–14 Hz frequency bands because these ranges have previously been associated with physiological tremor and exercise-related neuromuscular responses. Therefore, interpretation of the results was restricted to these physiologically relevant frequency ranges. No additional low-pass or band-pass filtering was applied before FFT analysis; instead, the full recorded accelerometry signal was transformed and interpretation was restricted a priori to the 2–5 Hz and 9–14 Hz bands.

Frequency-domain analysis

The selected frequency bands were based on previous physiological tremor research in athletic populations. Spectral power within the 2–5 Hz range was interpreted as reflecting predominantly mechanical and postural oscillatory components, whereas the 9–14 Hz range was considered to represent neural and reflex-related components of physiological tremor. Separate analysis of these bands allowed evaluation of potential frequency-specific effects of exercise and cryostimulation [33].

For each recorded waveform, indicators describing tremor spectral power and frequency characteristics were computed from the PSD function according to the recommendations of Mazur-Różycka and Gajewski [32]. Because tremor power variables typically exhibit a log-normal distribution, logarithmic transformation was applied to all spectral power measures in accordance with previous methodological recommendations Gajewski et al. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA). Physiological tremor parameters were analyzed separately for tremor power (L2–5 and L9–14) and tremor frequency (F2–5 and F9–14). Prior to inferential analyses, normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test for each dependent variable. Visual inspection of residuals and distributional characteristics was additionally performed. The assumptions of RM-ANOVA were evaluated before model estimation. Sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test, and when the assumption of sphericity was violated, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied.

For each dependent variable, a three-way RM-ANOVA was conducted with exercise condition (baseline vs. post-exercise after the standardized 7-min recovery period), limb side (left vs. right lower limb), and intervention condition (cryostimulation vs. control) as within-subject factors. This fully repeated 2 × 2 × 2 design allowed assessment of main effects as well as two- and three-way interaction effects. The study followed a randomized counterbalanced crossover design. All participants (N = 34) completed both intervention conditions. To account for potential order effects, sequence (cryostimulation–control vs. control–cryostimulation; n = 17 per sequence) was included as a between-subject factor in supplementary analyses to test for possible carryover or order influences on the outcomes. Effect sizes for ANOVA effects were expressed as partial eta squared (ηp²) and interpreted according to conventional benchmarks (0.01 = small, 0.06 = medium, 0.14 = large effect) [29].

Planned contrasts derived a priori from the ANOVA model were used to decompose significant main effects and interactions, including comparisons between baseline and post-exercise (POST–PRE), left versus right limb, and cryostimulation versus control conditions. No exploratory post hoc multiple-comparison procedures were applied, as analyses were restricted to hypothesis-driven contrasts defined before data analysis.

To examine whether the main findings were robust to potential baseline differences between intervention conditions, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed using PRE-to-POST change scores. For each outcome, change scores were calculated as Δ = POST − PRE separately for each intervention condition and limb side. These change scores were analysed using a repeated-measures model with intervention condition (cryostimulation vs. control) and limb side (left vs. right) as within-subject factors. The main effect of intervention condition was interpreted as the difference in exercise-induced change between the cryostimulation and control conditions. The intervention × limb side interaction was used to assess whether the intervention-related change differed between limbs. Sequence was also examined as a between-subject factor to verify whether the observed effects were influenced by the order of experimental conditions. This additional change-score analysis was conducted as a sensitivity analysis to determine whether the primary conclusions remained unchanged after accounting for baseline differences.

For visualization purposes, estimated marginal means (EMMs) obtained from the RM-ANOVA models were plotted to illustrate interaction effects. Error bars in figures represent 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using Statistica (version 13.3; TIBCO Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA) and jamovi (version 2.7.18; The jamovi project, Sydney, Australia).

Tremor power in the L(2–5) band

Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of side, with higher tremor power observed in the left compared with the right lower limb (F(1,66) = 140.99, p < 0.0001, ηp² = 0.681). In addition, a significant side × intervention condition interaction was found (F(1,66) = 15.60, p = 0.0002, ηp² = 0.191), indicating that limb asymmetry differed between the cryostimulation and control conditions. A significant main effect of exercise condition was also observed (F(1,66) = 64.53, p < 0.0001, ηp² = 0.494), reflecting an overall increase in low-frequency tremor power following the 30-s tethered swimming effort. The exercise condition × intervention condition interaction did not reach statistical significance (F(1,66) = 3.83, p = 0.055). Furthermore, a significant side × exercise condition interaction was detected (F(1,66) = 22.21, p < 0.0001, ηp² = 0.252), indicating that post-exercise changes differed between the left and right lower limbs. This effect was further modulated by group, as evidenced by a significant side × exercise condition × intervention condition interaction (F(1,66) = 19.77, p < 0.0001, ηp² = 0.230). Planned contrasts derived from the ANOVA model indicated a significant increase in tremor power from baseline to post-exercise (after the standardized 7-min recovery period) across conditions (POST–PRE: t = 8.03, p < 0.0001). In addition, overall tremor power was significantly lower in the cryostimulation condition compared with control (t = -3.30, p = 0.0016). Estimated marginal means showed that, at baseline, tremor power was lower in the cryostimulation condition (9.13 ± 0.10) than in control (9.53 ± 0.10). Following exercise, tremor power increased in both conditions but remained lower under cryostimulation (9.51 ± 0.14) than in control (10.16 ± 0.14). Tremor power in the L(9–14) band

Repeated-measures ANOVA demonstrated a significant main effect of side, with higher tremor power observed in the left compared with the right lower limb (F(1,66) = 42.44, p < 0.0001, ηp² = 0.391). This effect was accompanied by a significant side × intervention condition interaction (F(1,66) = 40.75, p < 0.0001, ηp² = 0.382), indicating that limb-related asymmetry differed between the cryostimulation and control conditions. A significant main effect of exercise condition was also found (F(1,66) = 48.52, p < 0.0001, ηp² = 0.424), reflecting an overall increase in tremor power in the 9–14 Hz band following the 30-s tethered swimming effort. In contrast to the low-frequency band, the exercise condition × intervention condition interaction was statistically significant (F(1,66) = 6.96, p = 0.0104, ηp² = 0.095), suggesting that the magnitude of post-exercise changes differed between the cryostimulation and control conditions. Furthermore, a significant side × exercise condition interaction was observed (F(1,66) = 11.53, p = 0.0012, ηp² = 0.149), indicating differential post-exercise responses between the left and right lower limbs. The side × exercise condition × intervention condition interaction did not reach statistical significance (F(1,66) = 3.68, p = 0.059), although a trend toward group-dependent modulation was evident. Planned contrasts revealed a significant increase in tremor power from baseline to post-exercise (after the standardized 7-min recovery period) across conditions (POST–PRE: t = 6.97, p < 0.0001). In addition, tremor power was significantly higher in the left compared with the right lower limb (LEFT–RIGHT: t = 6.51, p < 0.0001). No significant overall difference between the cryostimulation and control conditions was observed when averaged across time points (Cryostimulation–Control: t = − 1.48, p = 0.144).

Tremor frequency in the F(2–5) band

Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of side, with higher tremor frequency observed in the right compared with the left lower limb (F(1,66) = 37.92, p < 0.0001, ηp² = 0.365). This effect was accompanied by a significant side × intervention condition interaction (F(1,66) = 36.11, p < 0.0001, ηp² = 0.354), indicating that limb-related differences in tremor frequency differed between the cryostimulation and control conditions. No sig-nificant main effect of exercise condition was observed for tremor frequency in the 2–5 Hz band (F(1,66) = 0.46, p = 0.499, ηp² = 0.007), indicating that, when averaged across groups and sides, the 30-s tethered swimming effort did not induce a uniform shift in low-frequency tremor oscillations. However, a significant exercise condition × intervention condition interaction was found (F(1,66) = 10.09, p = 0.0023, ηp² = 0.133), suggesting that post-exercise changes differed between the cryostimulation and control conditions. The side × exercise condition interaction was not statistically significant (F(1,66) = 2.44, p = 0.123). In contrast, a significant side × exercise condition × intervention condition interaction was observed (F(1,66) = 35.49, p < 0.0001, ηp² = 0.350), indicating a complex, group-dependent modulation of post-exercise tremor frequency responses between the left and right lower limbs. Planned contrasts demonstrated a significant difference between limbs, with lower tremor frequency in the left compared with the right lower limb (LEFT–RIGHT: t = − 6.16, p < 0.0001). In contrast, no significant overall change from baseline to post-exercise (after the standardized 7-min recovery period) was detected when averaged across conditions (POST–PRE: t = 0.68, p = 0.499). The contrast comparing groups across time points indicated a trend toward higher tremor frequency in the cryostimulation condition compared with the control condition (Cryostimulation–Control: t = 1.92, p = 0.059). Estimated marginal means showed divergent post-exercise responses between conditions: in the control condition, tremor frequency slightly decreased following exercise (PRE: 3.13 ± 0.03; POST: 3.08 ± 0.03), whereas in the cryostimulation condition a modest increase was observed (PRE: 3.15 ± 0.03; POST: 3.22 ± 0.03).

Tremor frequency in the F(9–14) band

Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a very strong main effect of side, with significantly higher tremor frequency observed in the right compared with the left lower limb (F(1,66) = 284.31, p < 0.0001, ηp² = 0.812). This effect was accompanied by a significant side × intervention condition interaction (F(1,66) = 36.13, p < 0.0001, ηp² = 0.354), indicating that limb-related asymmetry differed between the cryostimulation and control conditions. A significant main effect of exercise condition also emerged (F(1,66) = 32.65, p < 0.0001, ηp² = 0.331), reflecting an overall increase in high-frequency tremor following the 30-s tethered swimming effort. Group further modulated this effect, as demonstrated by a significant exercise condition × intervention condition interaction (F(1,66) = 8.15, p = 0.0058, ηp² = 0.110), suggesting that the magnitude of post-exercise frequency changes differed between the cryostimulation and control conditions. Moreover, a significant side × exercise condition interaction emerged (F(1,66) = 64.53, p < 0.0001, ηp² = 0.494), indicating differential post-exercise responses between the left and right lower limbs. Group further influenced this effect, as evidenced by a significant side × exercise condition × intervention condition interaction (F(1,66) = 25.32, p < 0.0001, ηp² = 0.277). Planned contrasts confirmed significantly lower tremor frequency in the left compared with the right lower limb (LEFT–RIGHT: t = − 16.9, p < 0.0001). A significant increase in tremor frequency from baseline to post-exercise (after the standardized 7-min recovery period) also emerged across conditions (POST–PRE: t = 5.71, p < 0.0001). In addition, overall tremor frequency was significantly lower in the cryostimulation condition compared with control when averaged across time points (Cryostimulation–Control: t = − 12.8, p < 0.0001). Estimated marginal means showed that at baseline tremor frequency was lower in the cryostimulation condition (11.1 ± 0.08) than in control (11.6 ± 0.08). Following exercise, tremor frequency increased in both conditions but remained lower under cryostimulation (11.4 ± 0.10) compared with control (12.6 ± 0.10). Change-score sensitivity analysis

Because baseline values differed between intervention conditions for several outcomes, an additional sensitivity analysis was conducted using PRE-to-POST change scores (Δ = POST − PRE). This analysis was performed to verify whether the main conclusions remained unchanged after accounting for baseline differences.

For log-transformed tremor spectral power in the 9–14 Hz band, the change-score analysis confirmed a significantly smaller PRE-to-POST increase in the cryostimulation condition compared with the control condition. When averaged across limbs, tremor spectral power increased by 0.768 ± 0.569 in the control condition and by 0.346 ± 0.739 in the cryostimulation condition. The intervention-related difference in change scores was − 0.422, indicating an attenuated post-exercise increase following cryostimulation (F(1,33) = 5.27, p = 0.028, ηp² = 0.138). This finding supports the primary interpretation that cryostimulation attenuated the exercise-induced increase in tremor spectral power in the 9–14 Hz band.

For log-transformed tremor spectral power in the 2–5 Hz band, the increase was also numerically smaller in the cryostimulation condition than in the control condition. When averaged across limbs, tremor spectral power increased by 0.635 ± 0.376 in the control condition and by 0.386 ± 0.639 in the cryostimulation condition. However, the intervention-related difference in change scores did not reach conventional statistical significance (difference = − 0.249; F(1,33) = 3.92, p = 0.056, ηp² = 0.106). Therefore, the 2–5 Hz spectral-power findings should be interpreted cautiously.

For tremor frequency in the 9–14 Hz band, the PRE-to-POST increase was significantly smaller in the cryostimulation condition than in the control condition. When averaged across limbs, tremor frequency increased by 0.964 ± 1.296 Hz in the control condition and by 0.322 ± 0.206 Hz in the cryostimulation condition. The intervention-related difference in change scores was − 0.642 Hz (F(1,33) = 8.21, p = 0.007, ηp² = 0.199), indicating an attenuated high-frequency tremor response following cryostimulation.

In contrast, tremor frequency in the 2–5 Hz band showed a heterogeneous response. When averaged across limbs, the control condition showed a small decrease from PRE to POST (Δ = −0.046 ± 0.148 Hz), whereas the cryostimulation condition showed a small increase (Δ = 0.070 ± 0.153 Hz). The intervention-related difference in change scores was significant (difference = 0.116 Hz; F(1,33) = 13.38, p = 0.001, ηp² = 0.289), but the direction of this effect does not indicate attenuation. Therefore, the frequency-domain findings in the 2–5 Hz band were interpreted as heterogeneous rather than as evidence of a consistent cryostimulation-related reduction.

Overall, the change-score sensitivity analysis confirmed that the most robust intervention-related effects concerned the 9–14 Hz frequency band. The main conclusion regarding attenuation of post-exercise tremor spectral power in the 9–14 Hz band remained supported after accounting for baseline differences, whereas the 2–5 Hz spectral-power outcome was weaker and should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

The present findings partly supported our hypothesis. Physiological tremor parameters increased after exercise, particularly in the control condition, whereas targeted cryostimulation of myofascial trigger points modulated selected post-exercise tremor responses. Importantly, the additional PRE-to-POST change-score sensitivity analysis indicated that the most consistent intervention-related effects were observed in the 9–14 Hz frequency band. Specifically, cryostimulation was associated with a smaller post-exercise increase in L(9–14 Hz) tremor spectral power and F(9–14 Hz) tremor frequency compared with the control condition. In contrast, the L(2–5 Hz) spectral-power outcome showed only a borderline intervention-related effect, and F(2–5 Hz) demonstrated a more heterogeneous response. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as evidence that cryostimulation may selectively modulate fatigue-related tremor components rather than uniformly attenuate all post-exercise tremor outcomes.

Previous investigations have consistently shown that acute fatigue and high-intensity exercise increase physiological tremor spectral power, likely reflecting elevated motor unit variability and altered neuromuscular control under fatigue conditions [7, 33]. While the present findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating exercise-induced increases in physiological tremor, several important differences should be noted [34]. Earlier studies primarily examined tremor responses following fatiguing exercise without applying any recovery intervention and were frequently conducted in adult populations or under laboratory-based exercise conditions [34]. In contrast, the present study investigated adolescent competitive swimmers using a randomized crossover design and evaluated the effects of targeted cryostimulation applied to clinically identified myofascial trigger points [31]. Therefore, the present findings extend existing knowledge by suggesting that peripheral sensory modulation strategies may influence selected fatigue-related tremor responses beyond the effects of exercise alone.

The intervention-related effects were most evident in the high-frequency tremor range. The significant interaction effects observed for the 9–14 Hz outcomes suggest that local cryostimulation may have altered the typical post-exercise tremor response, particularly in frequency components that are thought to reflect neural and reflex-related mechanisms. However, these effects should not be interpreted as a uniform reduction of tremor across all frequency bands. The lower-frequency 2–5 Hz outcomes were less consistent, indicating that the influence of cryostimulation may depend on the physiological characteristics of the tremor component being analysed. A particularly noteworthy finding was the consistent side-related asymmetry observed across outcomes, although its direction depended on the variable analyzed. Tremor spectral power tended to be higher in the left lower limb, whereas tremor frequency was higher in the right lower limb. This pattern suggests that side-related neuromuscular asymmetry in adolescent swimmers may be outcome-specific rather than uniformly expressed across all tremor measures.

Interlimb asymmetries are frequently reported in both general and athletic populations and may reflect chronic exposure to asymmetric mechanical loading or functional dominance [35, 36]. The present findings suggest that cryostimulation effects may interact with such pre-existing asymmetries, indicating a limb-dependent modulation of post-fatigue neuromotor behavior. Importantly, several side × condition and three-way interactions indicate that the effect of cryostimulation was not uniform across limbs, suggesting that peripheral sensory modulation may interact with pre-existing asymmetry.

The observed attenuation of tremor spectral power following cryostimulation is consistent with the established sensitivity of physiological tremor to short-term shifts in neural drive and afferent feedback [37]. Cooling has been shown to reduce nerve conduction velocity and alter sensory transmission, particularly within peripheral afferent fibers, which may contribute to modified reflex excitability and motor output [38, 39]. Additionally, cryotherapy has been associated with transient changes in neuromuscular function without detrimental effects on subsequent performance [40]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that localized cryostimulation of myofascial trigger points could acutely modulate exercise-induced physiological tremor in a crossover design. The findings extend current understanding of peripheral contributions to tremor regulation and suggest that targeted sensory interventions may influence fatigue-related motor instability in young athletes. Building on this, our findings align with prior re-search on exercise-induced tremor while offering novel insights. Previous studies have shown that vigorous exercise or fatigue reliably increases physiological tremor spectral power, as observed in our control condition [41]. Yet, no prior work has examined interventions to mitigate such tremor [42] representing the first demonstration that targeted MTrP cooling can temper post-exercise tremor. Several mechanisms may potentially explain this dampening effect. It is possible that cooling altered muscle spindle activity by modifying afferent feedback. Given that physiological tremor may be partly related to stretch-reflex oscillations, changes in spindle sensitivity could have contributed to the observed reduction in tremor; however, the present study was not designed to directly assess these mechanisms. Additionally, lower tissue temperatures slow nerve conduction and muscle contraction speeds, potentially reducing motor unit firing rates and smoothing high-frequency fluctuations that contribute to tremor. Cold exposure also activates temperature-sensitive ion channels such as TRPM8, which participate in peripheral sensory transduction and may influence neuromuscular regulation through altered afferent signaling [43]. Cryotherapy may also activate cutaneous cold receptors, promoting reflex muscle relaxation and reducing central drive or sympathetic outflow via cold-induced analgesia [44]. Collectively, these mechanisms may partly explain the lower post-exercise tremor response observed in the cryostimulation condition compared with control. These findings suggest that MTrP cryotherapy may influence factors related to neuromuscular stability in young athletes exposed to high training loads; however, further research is needed to confirm its practical relevance. Frequency outcomes were more heterogeneous than spectral power outcomes. F(2–5 Hz) did not show a uniform overall post-exercise shift when averaged across conditions, whereas F(9–14 Hz) increased following exercise and remained lower in the cryostimulation condition. This pattern suggests that the intervention primarily affected the magnitude of tremor oscillations, with a secondary effect on high-frequency tremor characteristics.

Limitations

However, limitations warrant caution. Because participants were aware of the intervention, expectancy-related effects cannot be entirely excluded; placebo-controlled recovery research has demonstrated that perceived recovery modalities may influence physiological responses [45]. In addition, the absence of participant blinding limits the ability to fully distinguish specific sensory-modulatory effects from expectancy-related influences. Although complete blinding is difficult in cryotherapy-based interventions because cold has distinctive sensory effects, a room-temperature pack or non-cooled compress could serve as a partial sham condition in future studies. In the present study, standardized instructions, identical assessment procedures, and the crossover design may have reduced inter-individual expectancy bias, but such effects cannot be eliminated. The sample comprised adolescent competitive swimmers, which may limit generalizability to other ages, sports, or skill levels, and although the crossover design controlled for individual differences, the modest sample size typical of pilot crossover studies warrants replication in larger cohorts. Tremor measurements were obtained under static postural conditions and reflected only acute responses in resting lower limbs; therefore, the persistence of the observed attenuation and its transfer to dynamic sport-specific tasks remain unknown. Furthermore, identification of myofascial trigger points relied on palpation-based clinical assessment, which, despite being performed by the same experienced physiotherapist to minimize variability, remains inherently examiner-dependent and does not fully exclude the possibility that observed effects may reflect general cooling rather than strictly MTrP-specific mechanisms. Future studies should extend these findings by including sham-controlled conditions, objective temperature monitoring, and complementary electrophysiological measures (e.g., EMG or reflex assessments) to better characterize the underlying mechanisms.

Moreover, physiological tremor is influenced not only by neural factors but also by peripheral and biomechanical mechanisms, including limb mechanics, tissue stiffness, viscoelastic properties, and sensory feedback from the musculoskeletal system. Consequently, the observed changes in tremor characteristics should be interpreted as reflecting a complex interaction of neuromuscular and biomechanical processes rather than exclusively neural adaptations. In addition, several factors known to influence physiological tremor, including sleep quality, caffeine consumption, nutritional status, psychological arousal, and training load preceding testing, were not formally controlled or quantified. Although the crossover design reduced the influence of inter-individual variability by allowing each participant to serve as his or her own control, the potential contribution of these factors cannot be completely excluded. An additional limitation is that the trial was registered retrospectively. Although the study protocol, outcome measures, and analytical approach were established before data analysis, prospective registration would have further strengthened the methodological transparency of the investigation. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted in light of this limitation. Although a 24-h washout period was implemented between experimental conditions, it is possible that residual fatigue, delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS), or other exercise-related adaptations were not completely resolved in all participants. Therefore, the potential influence of incomplete physiological recovery between sessions cannot be entirely excluded. The study included only male swimmers; therefore, the findings may not be directly generalizable to female athletes. Longitudinal designs may further clarify whether repeated application influences performance, recovery, or injury-related outcomes [46].

Practical implications

From an applied perspective, the present findings suggest that targeted local cryostimulation may represent a practical adjunct strategy for managing acute post-exercise neuromuscular instability. In adolescent swimmers and potentially other athletic populations, brief pre-exercise application of ice to identified MTrPs may attenuate subsequent post-exercise increases in physiological tremor. The observed modulation of selected tremor parameters may indicate changes in post-exercise neuromuscular control; however, whether these changes translate into improved sport-specific performance or recovery remains unknown. While these practical implications should be interpreted cautiously, they highlight the potential relevance of peripheral sensory modulation strategies within structured recovery routines. Further research is warranted to determine the long-term functional and performance implications of this intervention.

Conclusions

The findings suggest that targeted cryostimulation of myofascial trigger points may acutely modulate selected components of exercise-induced neuromotor instability in adolescent swimmers. The most consistent effects were observed in the 9–14 Hz frequency band, supporting the potential contribution of peripheral sensory mechanisms to the regulation of physiological tremor under fatigue-related conditions. In contrast, lower-frequency outcomes should be interpreted more cautiously. Targeted cooling of myofascial trigger points appears to be a feasible short-term intervention for influencing post-exercise tremor responses, although its functional relevance for sport-specific performance and recovery requires further investigation. These findings also suggest that tremor analysis may serve as a sensitive outcome measure for evaluating neuromuscular responses to recovery or sensory-modulation strategies in adolescent athletes.
