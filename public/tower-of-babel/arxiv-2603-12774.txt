Synchronization by noise for stochastic differential equations driven by fractional Brownian motion
Alexandra Blessing Neamţu and Mazyar Ghani Varzaneh

Abstract
We investigate synchronization by noise for stochastic differential equations (SDEs) driven by a fractional Brownian motion (fbm) with Hurst index H ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) H\in(0,1) . Provided that the SDE has a negative top Lyapunov exponent, we show that a weak form of synchronization occurs. To this aim we use tools from stochastic dynamical systems, random dynamical systems and a support theorem for SDEs driven by fractional noise. In particular, we characterize the support of an invariant measure of a random dynamical system in a non-Markovian setting.



Keywords:
stochastic dynamical system, random dynamical system, fractional Brownian motion, negative Lyapunov exponent, synchronization by noise.

60G22, 60F99, 37H30.

Introduction

The main goal of this work is to prove synchronization by noise for stochastic differential equations (SDEs) driven by fractional Brownian motion (fbm). In particular, we consider SDEs with additive noise

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&{\textnormal{d}}Y_{t}=F(Y_{t})\penalty\ {\textnormal{d}}t+\sigma{\textnormal{d}}B^{H}_{t}\\
&Y_{0}=x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
d\geq 1
,
(B^{H}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a
d
-dimensional fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index
H\in(0,1)
, the drift term
F
satisfies a suitable dissipativity and monotonicity assumption and
\sigma
denotes the intensity of the noise.

Synchronization means that trajectories of (
1.1
) starting from different initial data converge in a certain sense to a single trajectory given the same realization of the noise.
In the language of random dynamical systems, this property can be viewed as convergence towards a unique random equilibrium point (called singleton attractor). In the Markovian case, a powerful tool for the analysis of the long-time behavior of (
1.1
) is to associate to the random dynamical system a Markov semigroup and relate its invariant measure to a statistical equilibrium, see
[
18
]
and Remark
2.13
. This is one of the main challenges which one has to overcome in the non-Markovian setting.

Referring to
[
28
]
, synchronization occurs if there exists a weak attractor (as introduced in Subsection
2.3
) for the random dynamical system generated by (
1.1
) consisting of a single point. As argued in
[
28
]
, it is meaningful to weaken this concept and impose the existence of a minimal weak point attractor consisting of a single point. This is called weak synchronization and can be verified under natural assumptions on the underlying dynamical system established in
[
28
,
27
]
. In the Markovian case, if the associated Markov semigroup is strongly mixing, there is a connection between its invariant measure and the weak point attractor
[
28
]
which is not available in our setting.

In this work, we also focus on a modified concept of weak synchronization tailored to the non-Markovian dynamics induced by (
1.1
). To this aim, we follow the recent approach developed in
[
9
]
that
constructs a random dynamical system (RDS) given a stochastic dynamical system (SDS) as introduced in
[
44
]
. In this framework,
[
9
]
established that (
1.1
) has a negative top Lyapunov exponent increasing the noise intensity
\sigma
. Here we go one step further and prove that the trajectories of (
1.1
) converge in a certain sense to a random fixed point independently on the initial datum. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result on synchronization by noise for SDEs driven by fbm in dimensions higher than one, i.e. beyond the order-preserving case investigated in
[
27
, Subsection 4.1]
. Notably, our concept of weak synchronization is natural in a non-Markovian setting but is slightly different than the one defined in
[
28
,
27
]
. As already stated, this means that the minimal weak point attractor of the RDS associated to (
1.1
) is a singleton.

In the following, we briefly explain our concept of synchronization and its connection to
[
28
,
27
]
. To this aim, we first recall that a major step for the analysis of non-Markovian dynamics was introduced in
[
44
]
, where it was shown that enlarging the state space incorporating the noise, once can obtain a Markov process on the extended space. Using tools from Markov processes, one can establish the existence of a unique invariant measure on this extended space. We refer to
[
44
,
40
,
41
,
43
]
for more details on this procedure and further results. Under certain assumptions, the projection of this measure onto the original state space is mixing, which was used in
[
28
]
to prove synchronization for one-dimensional SDEs driven by fbm. Moreover, a key feature of the projected measure is that it admits a density with respect to Lebesgue measure and satisfies Gaussian-type upper and lower bounds
[
52
]
. Such bounds were used in
[
9
]
to prove that the top Lyapunov exponent of (
1.1
) is negative.

In this work, we follow a complementary approach. We do not consider the projected measure but instead we disintegrate it with respect to the Wiener measure. This leads to a family of random measures
\{\mu_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
, (where
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
is a suitable probability space) on the original state space which are shown to be invariant under the underlying dynamics. These random measures can be viewed as analogues of statistical equilibria in the Markovian setting. When the top Lyapunov exponent of (
1.1
) is negative, we show that these measures are discrete similar to the Markovian situation discussed in
[
28
]
. For a strongly mixing, white-noise RDS, it is known that the family
\{\mu_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
constitutes a weak point attractor as established in
[
28
]
. In the absence of the Markov property, we cannot prove an analogous result. Instead we show that the support of these measures is contained in the weak point attractor. This motivates us to introduce the following formal notion of synchronization, see Definition
4.16
for the precise statement.

Definition 1.1

We say that weak synchronization occurs if there exists a random point
b:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\text{supp}(\mu_{\omega})=\{b(\omega)\},\hskip 2.84526pt\mathbb{P}-a.e.\hskip 2.84526pt\omega\in\Omega.

At an informal level, our main result Theorem
4.25
reads as follows.

Theorem 1.2

The SDE (

) exhibits weak synchronization in the sense of Definition
1.1
provided that

In this setting we are able to show that (

Literature

The phenomenon of synchronization (by noise) was investigated for numerous stochastic systems in finite and infinite-dimensions ranging from SDEs on compact manifolds
[

to singular SPDEs
[

,

and the references specified therein. For more details on synchronization and an overview of the available results we refer to

. We mention that order-preservation holds under restrictive conditions on the drift term in dimensions higher than one. The tools developed in this manuscript are applicable beyond order-preserving white-noise RDS. Notably, as already mentioned, synchronization attracted lots of interest also in the context of singular stochastic partial differential equations. For example synchronization by noise for the
\Phi^{4}_{2}
and
\Phi^{4}_{3}
equation on the torus was derived in
[
34
]
. Moreover, synchronization for the KPZ equation on the torus (referred to as one-force one-solution principle) was proved in
[

]
. We also mention other approaches to synchronization by noise based on large deviations which consider a small noise regime
[
58
,
6
,
7
]
.

Finally, we point out that synchronization can provide a pathwise coupling mechanism used to establish ergodicity. However, even for Markovian systems, proving ergodicity only via synchronization is technically challenging. Consequently, many works establish ergodicity using alternative coupling methods
[

,
24
,
50
]
. The non-Markovian nature of the systems leads to additional technical difficulties

Moreover, there has been a growing interest in the dynamics of stochastic systems with fractional Brownian motion or other types of non-Markovian noise. In particular, stationary solutions for SDEs driven by fbm together with Gaussian bounds for their densities have been established in
[

whereas rates of convergence of fractional SDEs towards their equilibria and related aspects have been investigated in

[

random dynamical systems given stochastic dynamical systems generated by SDEs with additive fractional Brownian motion. This framework enabled us to show in a certain regime that the top Lyapunov exponent of such an SDE is negative. This assumption is heavily used in this work to show synchronization. The complementary regime in which the top Lyapunov exponent is positive, is expected to lead to entail a chaotic behavior of the systems together with random measures exhibiting SRB-type properties. This will be analyzed in a forthcoming work.

Structure of the paper

In Section
2
we collect basic concepts from stochastic dynamical systems introduced in
[
44
]
, invariant measures, fractional Brownian motion, random dynamical systems and attractors which are used to define (weak) synchronization. Moreover, we state some results established in
[
9
]
on the generation of an RDS given an SDS. In Section
3
we thoroughly discuss the concepts of invariant measures and attractors for RDS. A particular interest is given by the support of the disintegrated invariant measure of a stochastic dynamical system constructed in Lemma
2.12
provided that the top Lyapunov exponent of the underlying system is negative. For Markovian systems, it is known that the corresponding measure is atomic, see
[
27
, Lemma 2.19]
. We establish in Proposition
3.4
an analogue result using a stable manifold theorem. Furthermore, we introduce in Subsection
3.2
a concept of a random attractors for non-Markovian systems, which we call P-attractor. This depends only on the past of the noise and is motivated by the theory of SDS which filter out the future of the noise. In Theorem
3.7
we provide the existence of such an attractor for the RDS generated by (
1.1
).
Section
4
contains the main result on synchronization by noise for (
1.1
). Since in the Markovian case, there exists a one-to-one correspondence between invariant measures of RDS and pullback attractors
[
18
]
, we investigate such questions for the disintegrated measure of the SDS and the P-attractor.
Therefore, Subsection
4.1
provides a connection between the support of the invariant measure of the RDS and the introduced weak point attractor. Namely, we show that the support of this measure is contained in the P-attractor, see Proposition
4.3
for a precise statement. To the best of our knowledge, this relationship was known so far for strongly mixing, white-noise RDS as investigated in
[
28
,
27
]
.
Based on these insights, Theorem
4.25
establishes synchronization by fractional noise.
One essential ingredient for this argument is that the support of the invariant measure of the RDS contains finitely many discrete points and their distance is always bounded from above by a deterministic constant. This holds due to the assumptions imposed on the drift, particularly a contraction property on large sets, and the fact that we work with additive noise. We emphasize that the tools for synchronization developed in this work are not restricted to order-preserving random dynamical systems. Section
5
provides an example of an SDE for which synchronization holds.
We conclude with Appendix
A
with a result from measure theory required for the proof of Corollary
3.3
.

Let

2.2
Stochastic and random dynamical systems

Let us now briefly outline the general framework of this work which relies on the setting recently developed in
[
9
]
. We begin by recalling the basic definitions related to the theories of random dynamical systems and stochastic dynamical systems. The theory of random dynamical systems
[
3
]
is largely inspired by the framework of deterministic dynamical systems and ergodic theory. In contrast, the stochastic dynamical systems developed in
[
44
]
rely on probabilistic tools.

Definition 2.3

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
be a measurable space, and let
\mathbb{T}
denote either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, each equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra.
A family of mappings
\theta=\{\theta_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
on
\Omega
is called a
measurable dynamical system
if the following conditions hold:

•

The mapping
(t,\omega)\mapsto\theta_{t}(\omega)
is measurable with respect to
\sigma(\mathbb{T})\otimes\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}
.

•

The identity condition is satisfied at
t=0
, i.e.,
\theta_{0}=\mathrm{Id}
.

•

The semigroup property holds:
\theta_{s+t}=\theta_{s}\circ\theta_{t}
for all
s,t\in\mathbb{T}
.

If there exists a probability measure
\mathbb{P}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
such that

(\theta_{t})_{\ast}\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P},\quad\forall\,t\in\mathbb{T},

then the quadruple
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},\theta)
is called a
measurable metric dynamical system
.
Furthermore,
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},\theta)
is said to be ergodic, if for every
t\in\mathbb{T}\setminus\{0\}
, the transformation
\theta_{t}
is ergodic, meaning that for any
A\in\mathcal{F}
,

\mathbb{P}\big(A\triangle\theta_{t}^{-1}(A)\big)=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathbb{P}(A)\in\{0,1\}.

Now, we can define the concept of a random dynamical system.

Definition 2.4

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a Polish space equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\sigma(\mathcal{X})
.
An
(ergodic) measurable random dynamical system
on
(\mathcal{X},\sigma(\mathcal{X}))
consists of an (ergodic) measurable metric dynamical system
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},\theta)
together with a measurable function

\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathcal{X}\times\Omega\to\mathcal{X},

satisfying the
cocycle property
.
More specifically, for each
(t,x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathcal{X}\times\Omega
, set
\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x):=\Phi(t,\omega,x)
.
Then the following conditions hold:

\Phi^{0}_{\omega}=\operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal{X}}\quad\text{for all }\omega\in\Omega,

and

\Phi^{t+s}_{\omega}(x)=\Phi^{s}_{\theta_{t}\omega}\big(\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)\big),

for all
s,t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and
\omega\in\Omega
.
The function
\Phi
is called a
cocycle
.
Given the condition that, for each
\omega\in\Omega
, the map
(t,x)\mapsto\Phi(t,x,\omega)
is continuous, the function
\Phi
is called a continuous random dynamical system.
Moreover, for
k\geq 1
, we call
\Phi
a
C^{k}
-random dynamical system if for every
t\geq 0
and
\omega\in\Omega
, the function

x\mapsto\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)

is
C^{k}
-Fréchet differentiable.

Definition 2.5

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},\theta)
be a measurable metric dynamical system, and let
\mathcal{X}
be a Polish space.

•

A random variable
Y:\Omega\rightarrow(0,\infty)
is called
tempered
if

\displaystyle\limsup_{|t|\to\infty,\,t\in\mathbb{T}}\frac{1}{|t|}\log^{+}Y(\theta_{t}\omega)=0,\quad\text{for }\mathbb{P}\text{-almost every }\omega\in\Omega,

where
\log^{+}x:=\max\{\log x,0\}
.

•

Let
\mathcal{E}=\{\mathcal{E}(\omega)\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
be a family of non-empty subsets of
\mathcal{X}
such that, for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the function
\omega\mapsto d(x,\mathcal{E}(\omega))
is measurable, and with probability one each
\mathcal{E}(\omega)
is bounded in
\mathcal{X}
. We call
\mathcal{E}
a
tempered set
if the random variable

r(\mathcal{E}(\omega)):=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{E}(\omega)}d(x,x_{0})

is tempered for some fixed reference point
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
. The collection of tempered sets in
\mathcal{X}
is denoted by
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{X})
.

Remark 2.6

According to
[
3
, Theorem 4.1.3]
, the random variable
Y:\Omega\rightarrow(0,\infty)
is tempered if

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in\mathbb{T}\cap[0,1]}Y(\theta_{t}\omega)\right]<\infty.

Let us now state the definition of a stochastic dynamical systems.

Definition 2.7

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a Polish space equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\sigma(\mathcal{B})
.
A quadruple
\left(\mathcal{B},\{P_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathbf{P},\{\vartheta_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}\right)
is called a stationary noise process if the following properties hold.

(i)

The measure
\mathbf{P}
is a probability measure on
(\mathcal{B},\sigma(\mathcal{B}))
. Also, for each
t\geq 0
, the maps

P_{t}:\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}
and

\vartheta_{t}:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}

are measurable, and for every
\omega^{-}\in\mathcal{B}
, the random variables

P_{t}(\omega^{-},\cdot)
and
\vartheta_{t}
are independent.

(ii)

The family
\{\vartheta_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
forms a semiflow on
\mathcal{B}
, i.e.

\vartheta_{t+s}=\vartheta_{t}\circ\vartheta_{s}
, and preserves
\mathbf{P}
, that is
(\vartheta_{t})_{\star}\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{P}
.

(iii)

For
\omega^{-}\in\mathcal{B}
and

, define

\mathcal{P}_{t}(\omega^{-};\cdot):=(P_{t}(\omega^{-},\cdot))_{\star}(\mathbf{P}).

The family
\{\mathcal{P}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

\mathcal{B}
.
Moreover,
\mathbf{P}
is the unique invariant measure of

(iv)

For all
\omega^{-},\omega^{+}\in\mathcal{B}
and
t,s\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\vartheta_{t}\circ P_{t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})=P_{t}(\vartheta_{t}\omega^{-},P_{t}(\omega^{+},\omega^{-}))=\omega^{-},

\displaystyle P_{t+s}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})=P_{t}(P_{s}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+}),\vartheta_{s}\omega^{+}).

This leads to the following definition.

Let
\left(\mathcal{B},\{P_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathbf{P},\{\vartheta_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}\right)

\mathcal{X}
be a Polish space.
A map

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{X},\\

(2.1)

is called a continuous stochastic dynamical system if

•

For every
T>0
,
\omega\in\mathcal{B}
, and

\tilde{\varphi}_{T}(x,\omega)(t):=\varphi^{t}_{\vartheta_{T-t}\omega}(x),\quad t\in[0,T],

, and

to
C([0,T],\mathcal{X})
.

•

For all

,
\omega\in\mathcal{B}
, and

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\varphi^{0}_{\omega}(x)=x,\\

(2.2)

•

If
\mathcal{X}

\Phi

Let

•

Theorem 2.11

3.

Suppose that

In this manuscript, we work with RDS generated by SDEs driven by fractional Brownian motion making their long-time behavior a challenging task. We provide a short summary of some well-known concepts for Markovian dynamics for a better comprehension of our results.

Remark 2.13

For SDEs driven by Brownian motion, the corresponding RDS denoted by
\overline{\varphi}
,
where

and

is a separable Banach space,
is defined on a measurable metric dynamical system

associated with the law of the Brownian motion.
This RDS is a white-noise RDS (see
[

, Section 1.1]
for the definition).
It is adapted to a suitable filtration and satisfies the usual independence and shift-invariance properties of the Brownian motion,
see
[
16
, Section 1.1]
for further details.
The RDS

induces a Markovian (Feller) semigroup

for every bounded and measurable function

.
If

admits an invariant probability measure

,
by the martingale convergence theorem
(see e.g.
[
18
, Corollary 4.6]
and
[

exists.
More precisely, for every sequence

\overline{\mu}_{\overline{\omega}}:=\lim_{t_{k}\to\infty}\big(\overline{\varphi}(t_{k},\overline{\theta}_{-t_{k}}\overline{\omega},\cdot)\big)_{\ast}\overline{\mu}

The correspondence between invariant measure of a Markov process and statistical equilibrium for the corresponding white-noise RDS does not hold in the non-Markovian case considered here. Therefore we follow the approach developed in Lemma

.

2.3
Pullback attractors

An important topic in the theory of random dynamical systems is the concept of pullback attractors. Before stating it, we need the following definition.

Definition 2.14

Let
\Phi
be an RDS and
\mathcal{A}=\{\mathcal{A}(\omega)\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
be a family of non-empty subsets of
\mathcal{X}
.

1)

The family
\mathcal{A}
is called a random closed set (random compact set) if, with probability one, each
\mathcal{A}(\omega)
is closed (compact), and for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the function
\omega\mapsto d(x,\mathcal{A}(\omega))
is
\mathcal{F}
-measurable.

2)

We say that
\mathcal{A}
is invariant under
\Phi
if for all
t\geq 0
,

\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(\mathcal{A}(\omega))=\mathcal{A}(\theta_{t}\omega)

holds for
\mathbb{P}
-almost every
\omega\in\Omega
.

3)

The set
\mathcal{A}
is called random absorbing if,
for every
\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{X})
, there exists a (random) time

T=T(\mathcal{E},\omega)
such that

\varphi(t,\theta_{-t}\omega,\mathcal{E}(\theta_{-t}\omega))\subset\mathcal{A}(\omega),\quad\forall\,t\geq T(\mathcal{E},\omega),

for
\mathbb{P}
-almost every
\omega\in\Omega
.

Now, we can give the definition of a pullback attractor.

Definition 2.15

Let
\Phi
be an RDS. A random compact set
\mathcal{A}
is
called pullback attractor
if

1)

\mathcal{A}
invariant under
\Phi
.

2)

For every compact set
K\subseteq\mathcal{X}

we have

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\sup\limits_{x\in K}d(\Phi^{t}_{\theta_{-t}\omega}(x),\mathcal{A}(\omega))=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mathbb{P}-a.s.

We call
\mathcal{A}
a weak attractor if the convergence in 2) holds in probability instead of almost surely. The set
\mathcal{A}
is called a (weak) point attractor if it satisfies the properties stated above, assuming that the compact set
K
in condition 2) consists of a single point. A (weak) point attractor is said to be minimal if it is contained in every other (weak) point attractor.

Remark 2.16

•

Obviously, every pullback attractor is a weak attractor but the converse is not true, see
[
57
]
.

•

In
[
18
, Definition 3.9]
, the second property in Definition
2.15
is stated under the stronger assumption that
K
is bounded. When
\mathcal{X}
is a finite-dimensional Banach space, these two concepts are equivalent.

For more details on the construction and properties of random attractors and related concepts, in particular where the set
K
above is replaced by a random bounded or compact set, we refer to
[
18
,
29
,
33
,
32
,
31
]
. Finally, we point out the following well-known fact for the weak point attractor of a white-noise RDS in the Markovian case.

Remark 2.17

A Markovian semigroup
(\overline{P}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with invariant measure
\overline{\mu}
is called strongly mixing if

\overline{P}_{t}f(x)\longrightarrow\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(y)\,\overline{\mu}(dy)\quad\text{as }t\to\infty,

for every bounded and continuous function
f
and all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
Similarly, an RDS
\overline{\varphi}
is called strongly mixing if the law of
\overline{\varphi}(t,\cdot,x)

converges to
\overline{\mu}
for
t\to\infty
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. For a strongly mixing, white-noise RDS, it is well-known that the family
\{\overline{\mu}_{\overline{\omega}}\}_{\overline{\omega}\in\overline{\Omega}}

constitutes a weak point attractor, where
\overline{\Omega}
is the set introduced in Remark
2.13
. For more details, see
[
28
, Proposition 2.20]
and
[
49
, Theorem 2.4]
.

Definition 2.18

.

•

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=F(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}B^{H}_{t},\\

\mu_{\omega^{-}}
. Moreover, we prove the existence of random attractors which follows from the dissipativity of
F
stated in Assumption
2.21
. Moreover, we explore the connection between invariant measures and attractors recall Remark
2.13
and
2.17
.
We fix here some useful notations that will be used further on.

Notation 3.1

•

We fix

and write
\mathcal{B}

3.1
Random invariant measures

From Lemma
2.12
, one can disintegrate the invariant measure of the SDS to obtain a family of random measures on the phase space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The aim of this subsection is to investigate the structure of these random measures, when the top Lyapunov exponent is negative.
We recall some preliminary results in this direction following
[
9
]
, in particular the existence of a local stable manifold.

Theorem 3.2

There exists a sequence of deterministic values, called Lyapunov exponents

\lambda_{k}<\cdots<\lambda_{1},\qquad\lambda_{i}\in[-\infty,\infty),

and a set of full measure
\tilde{\Omega}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}
with respect to
\mu\times\mathbf{P}
such that

1.

The set
\tilde{\Omega}
is
\Theta_{t}
-invariant for every
t\geq 0
.
For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, define

G_{\lambda}(\omega,x):=\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\big|\mathrm{D}_{x}\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(z)\big|\leq\lambda\right\}.

Then
G_{\lambda_{1}}(\omega,x)=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and for every
1\leq i<k
,

z\in G_{\lambda_{i}}(\omega,x)\setminus G_{\lambda_{i+1}}(\omega,x)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\big|\mathrm{D}_{x}\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(z)\big|=\lambda_{i}.

Moreover, for all
z\in G_{\lambda_{k}}(\omega,x)\setminus\{0\}
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\big|\mathrm{D}_{x}\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(z)\big|=\lambda_{k}.

2.

Suppose
\lambda_{1}<0
and that for some
r\in(0,1]
and all
y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
there exist constants
\overline{C}_{F}>0
and
p_{1}\geq 1
such that

\|\mathrm{D}_{y}F-\mathrm{D}_{z}F\|_{L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\overline{C}_{F}(1+|y|+|z|)^{p_{1}}|y-z|^{r}.

(3.1)

Then there exists a full-measure set
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega
such that, for every
0<\nu<-\lambda_{1}
, there exists a positive random variable

\displaystyle R^{\nu}:\mathcal{M}\to(0,\infty)

(3.2)

with the property that for all
(x,\omega)\in\mathcal{M}
and all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying
|y|\leq R^{\nu}(x,\omega)
,

\sup_{t\geq 0}\exp(t\nu)\,\big|\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x+y)-\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)\big|<\infty.

Proof.

See
[
9
, Propositions 4.13 and 4.18]
.

This leads to the following consequence.

Corollary 3.3

Let the hypotheses of the second statement of Theorem
3.2
hold. Then for every
\epsilon>0
, we can find a random, non-empty, open set
U(\omega)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that for

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}:=\left\{\omega:\ \ \lim_{n\to\infty}\text{\emph{diam}}\left(\Phi^{n}_{\omega}(U(\omega))\right)=0\right\},

(3.3)

we have
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{C})>1-\epsilon
, where the diameter of a subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined as
\text{\emph{diam}}(S)=\sup\{|x-y|:x,y\in S\}
.

Proof.

Let
R^{\nu}
be the random variable obtained in Theorem
3.2
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{n}:=\left\{(x,\omega)\in\mathcal{M}:R^{\nu}(x,\omega)\geq\frac{1}{n}\right\}.

By construction, the set
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{n}
is measurable and satisfies

\lim_{n\to\infty}(\mu\times\mathbf{P})(\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{n})=1.

Since
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega
is a Polish space, every probability measure on it is regular. Hence, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a compact subset
\mathcal{M}^{n}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{n}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}(\mu\times\mathbf{P})(\mathcal{M}^{n})=1.

In particular, the projection
\Pi_{\Omega}(\mathcal{M}^{n})
is compact and thus a measurable subset of
\Omega
, so we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\Pi_{\Omega}(\mathcal{M}^{n})\right)=1.

(3.4)

Furthermore, for every
\omega\in\Pi_{\Omega}(\mathcal{M}^{n})
, the fiber

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}^{n}}(\omega)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:(x,\omega)\in{\mathcal{M}^{n}}\right\}

(3.5)

is a compact and measurable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By Lemma
A.1
, there exists a Borel-measurable function
\mathcal{K}:\Pi_{\Omega}(\mathcal{M}^{n})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\mathcal{K}(\omega)\in\mathcal{M}^{n}(\omega)
for every
\omega\in\Pi_{\Omega}(\mathcal{M}^{n})
.
Moreover, by definition

R^{\nu}(\mathcal{K}(\omega),\omega)\geq\frac{1}{n},\quad\text{for all }\omega\in\Pi_{\Omega}(\mathcal{M}^{n}).

We further define the open ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

U(\omega):=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left|x-\mathcal{K}(\omega)\right|<R^{\nu}(\mathcal{K}(\omega),\omega)\right\}.

Due to (
3.4
) and Theorem
3.2
, we conclude that the claim holds for the family
\{U(\omega)\}_{\omega\in\Pi_{\Omega}(\mathcal{M}^{n})}
, provided that
n
is chosen sufficiently large.

Recalling Remark
2.13
, we know that we can construct an invariant measure for a white-noise RDS in the Markovian setting. In that case, the sign of the first Lyapunov exponent determines the nature of the associated random measures: negative exponents yield atomic measures, while positive ones lead to SRB measures.
Since we assume a negative top Lyapunov exponent, it is natural to expect that the corresponding measure is atomic which is what we prove next. The foundation of our argument originates from
[
38
]
, albeit with several important modifications. We also refer to
[
28
, Lemma 2.19]
, where a related problem is studied in the context of Markov processes based on the techniques from
[
38
]
.

Proposition 3.4

Let the hypotheses of the second statement in Theorem
3.2
hold. Then there exist
p\in\mathbb{N}
and random variables
\{b_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq p}
, with
b_{i}:\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that

\mu_{\omega^{-}}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_{\{b_{i}(\omega^{-})\}},

where
\mu_{\omega^{-}}
denotes the disintegrated invariant measure obtained in Lemma
2.12
. In particular, this representation shows that the disintegrated measures are discrete.

Proof.

For
(x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega
, we define

\mathcal{H}(x,\omega^{-},\omega^{+}):=\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\{x\}).

(3.6)

Note that since
\Phi^{t}_{\omega}
is injective, we have
(\Phi^{t}_{\omega})^{-1}\big(\{\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)\}\big)=\{x\}
. Thus, by property
5
of Lemma
2.12
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\left(\Theta_{t}(x,\omega)\right)

\displaystyle=\mu_{P_{t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}\left(\{\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)\}\right)=\left(\Phi^{t}_{\omega}\right)_{\star}\mu_{\omega^{-}}\left(\{\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)\}\right)

\displaystyle=\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\Phi^{t}_{\omega})^{-1}(\{\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)\})=\mathcal{H}(x,\omega).

Since for every
t>0
,
\Theta_{t}
is ergodic, the function
\mathcal{H}
must be constant on a set of full measure with respect to
\mu\times\mathbf{P}
. There are two possible scenarios:
\mathcal{H}\neq 0
or
\mathcal{H}=0
. We treat these two cases separately. First, we show that assuming
\mathcal{H}\neq 0
proves our claim and second we demonstrate that the case
\mathcal{H}=0
can be ruled out.

Case I
. This is similar to the argument provided in
[
28
, Lemma 2.19]
. However, since in our setting
\Phi
is not Markov, we provide more details for the sake of completeness. We prove the statement by contradiction and
assume that
\mathcal{H}\neq 0
. Then, almost surely for
\omega^{-}\in\mathcal{B}
, the support of
\mu_{\omega^{-}}
must consist of finitely many discrete points. To see this, we define

N(\omega^{-}):=\begin{cases}\#\operatorname{supp}(\mu_{\omega^{-}}),&\text{if }\#\operatorname{supp}(\mu_{\omega^{-}})<\infty\\[6.0pt]
+\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega}\mathcal{H}(x,\omega)\,(\mu\times\mathbf{P})(\mathrm{d}(x,\omega))=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\{x\})\,\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}x)\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-}).

Since
\mathcal{H}(x,\omega)
is constant
\mu\times\mathbf{P}
-almost surely, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\{x\})\,\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}x)\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})=\mathcal{H}^{2}\int_{\mathcal{B}}N(\omega^{-})\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-}).

We know that
\mathcal{H}\neq 0
. Thus

\int_{\mathcal{B}}N(\omega^{-})\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}}<\infty.

Moreover by Fubini’s theorem, the function
\omega^{-}\mapsto N(\omega^{-})
is measurable. Since
\mathcal{H}(x,\omega)
is constant with respect to
\mu\times\mathbf{P}
, this indicates that
N(\omega^{-})
is
\mathbf{P}
-almost surely equal to some
p\in\mathbb{N}
and so
p=\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}}
.
Consequently, on a set of full measure in
\mathcal{B}
, we have

\mu_{\omega^{-}}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_{\{b_{i}(\omega^{-})\}},

where
(b_{i}(\omega^{-}))_{1\leq i\leq p}
are points in the support of
\mu_{\omega^{-}}
.

Case II.
Now, we consider the case
\mathcal{H}\neq 0
. Then, almost surely, all random measures contain no point masses and are therefore diffuse. More precisely, there exists a set of full measure in
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
such that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{\omega^{-}}\left(\{x\}\right)=0.

We prove that this leads to a contradiction. Consequently, the assertion follows from the previous case.
Let

\displaystyle\Delta:=\left\{(x,x)\mid x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right\}

be the diagonal in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then we can find a measurable function

G:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Delta\to[0,\infty)

such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\setminus\Delta}G(x,y)\,\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}x)\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}y)\ \mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})\\
&=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\setminus\Delta}G(x,y)\,\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}x)\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}y)\ \mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{+})<\infty\end{split}

and

\displaystyle G(x,y)\to\infty\quad\text{as}\quad|x-y|\to 0.

(3.7)

For the construction of such a function we refer to
[
28
, Lemma 2.19]
.
We further set

\displaystyle R(\omega^{-},\omega^{+}):=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\setminus\Delta}G(x,y)\,\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}x)\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}y).

Then, from Lemma
2.1
and the fact that
(\theta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is
\mathbf{P}\times\mathbf{P}
invariant, it follows for every
t>0
that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\setminus\Delta}G(x,y)\,\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}x)\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}y)\ \mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{+})\\
&=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}R(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\mathbf{P}\times\mathbf{P}\left(\mathrm{d}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right)=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}R\left(\theta_{t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right)\mathbf{P}\times\mathbf{P}\left(\mathrm{d}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right).\end{split}

(3.8)

From Lemma
2.12
we have
(\Phi^{t}_{\omega})_{\star}\mu_{\omega^{-}}\;=\;\mu_{P_{t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}.

With a slight abuse of notation, this yields

(\Phi^{t}_{\omega})_{\star}\left(\mu_{\omega^{-}}\times\mu_{\omega^{-}}\right)=\mu_{P_{t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}\times\mu_{P_{t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\begin{split}&R\left(\theta_{t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right)=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\setminus\Delta}G(x,y)\,(\Phi^{t}_{\omega})_{\star}\left(\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}x)\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}y)\right)\\
&=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\setminus\Delta}G\left(\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x),\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(y)\right)\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}x)\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}y).\end{split}

Consequently, from (
3.8
) we deduce that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\setminus\Delta}G(x,y)\,\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}x)\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}y)\ \mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{+})\\
&=\quad\int_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\setminus\Delta}G\left(\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x),\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(y)\right)\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}x)\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}y)\ \mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{+})<\infty.\end{split}

(3.9)

In particular, for every
\epsilon>0
, we can choose
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large such that for
\mathcal{M}:=\mathcal{M}^{n}
, with
\mathcal{M}^{n}
constructed in the proof of Corollary
3.3
, we have

(\mu\times\mathbf{P})(\mathcal{M})>1-\epsilon
. Note that we have:

1.

R^{\nu}(x,\omega)\geq\frac{1}{n}
for every
(x,\omega)\in\mathcal{M}
,

2.

\Pi_{\Omega}(\mathcal{M})
is measurable.

Thus, by Fubini’s theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Pi_{\Omega}(\mathcal{M})}\mu_{\omega^{-}}\left(\mathcal{M}(\omega)\right)\ \mathbf{P}\times\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+}))>1-\epsilon.

(3.10)

Since
\mu_{\omega^{-}}
does not have point masses, it follows once again from Fubini’s theorem that

(\mu_{\omega^{-}}\times\mu_{\omega^{-}})(\Delta)=0\quad\mathbf{P}\text{-a.s.}

Let
\{D_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
be a countable family of rectangles covering
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, each with diameter less than
\frac{1}{n}
such that

\displaystyle D_{i}\cap D_{j}\subset\partial D_{i}\cap\partial D_{j},\quad i\neq j.

Since
\mu_{\omega^{-}}
has no atoms, it follows from Fubini’s theorem for every
i\geq 1
that

\displaystyle(\mu_{\omega^{-}}\times\mu_{\omega^{-}})\left(\partial D_{i}\right)=0\quad{\mathbf{P}}\text{-a.s.}

(3.11)

We set for
i\geq 1

\displaystyle H_{i}(x,y,\omega):=\chi_{D_{i}}(x,y)\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}G\left(\Phi^{n}_{\omega}(x),\Phi^{n}_{\omega}(y)\right).

Note that
R^{\nu}(x,\omega)\geq\frac{1}{n}
. Thus, for every
(x,\omega)\in\mathcal{M}
, it follows from the second assertion of Theorem
3.2
and (
3.7
) that

\displaystyle H_{i}(x,y,\omega)=\begin{cases}\begin{split}&\infty,\quad\text{if}\quad x,y\in D_{i},\\
&0,\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{split}\end{cases}

(3.12)

From
3.9
, (
3.11
), Fatou’s Lemma and the monotone convergence theorem, we deduce that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 1}\int_{\Pi_{\Omega}(\mathcal{M})}\int_{(\mathcal{M}(\omega)\times\mathcal{M}(\omega))\setminus\Delta}H_{i}(x,y,\omega)\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}x)\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}y)\ \mathbf{P}\times\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+}))<\infty.

From (
3.12
) and using again the fact that almost surely
\mu_{\omega^{-}}
has no point masses, we conclude that for
\mathbf{P}\times\mathbf{P}
-almost every
\omega\in\Pi_{\Omega}(\mathcal{M})
, we have

\displaystyle(\mu_{\omega^{-}}\times\mu_{\omega^{-}})\left(\left((\mathcal{M}(\omega)\times\mathcal{M}(\omega))\setminus\Delta\right)\cap D_{i}\right)=(\mu_{\omega^{-}}\times\mu_{\omega^{-}})\left(\left(\mathcal{M}(\omega)\times\mathcal{M}(\omega)\right)\cap D_{i}\right)=0.

(3.13)

Recalling (
3.11
) and the fact that
\bigcup_{i\geq 1}D_{i}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, this further implies that

\displaystyle(\mu_{\omega^{-}}\times\mu_{\omega^{-}})\left(\left(\mathcal{M}(\omega)\times\mathcal{M}(\omega)\right)\right)=\mu_{\omega^{-}}^{2}(\mathcal{M}(\omega))=0\quad{\mathbf{P}\times\mathbf{P}}\text{-a.s.}

which contradicts (
3.10
). In conclusion, it is not possible for the almost surely constant function
\mathcal{H}
to be equal to zero. Therefore the claim follows from
Case I
.

3.2
Random attractors

We now establish the existence of pullback attractors To this end, we introduce the following definition tailored to the construction of a random dynamical system provided in
[
9
]
.
To distinguish between this definition and Definition
2.15
, we use the term
\mathrm{P}
-pullback attractor to emphasize that this depends only the past of the noise, which is natural since the SDS filters out the future of the noise.

Definition 3.5

A random compact set
\mathcal{A}=\{\mathcal{A}(\omega^{-})\}_{\omega^{-}\in\mathcal{B}}
is called a
\mathrm{P}
-pullback attractor for the SDS
\varphi
if the following conditions are satisfied.

1.

For every
\omega^{+}\in\mathcal{B}
, we have

\Phi^{t}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(\mathcal{A}(\omega^{-}))=\mathcal{A}(P_{t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})).

2.

For every compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

\lim_{t\to\infty}\sup_{x\in K}d(\varphi^{t}_{\omega^{-}}(x),{\mathcal{A}}(\omega^{-}))=0\quad\mathbf{P}\text{-a.s.}

Remark 3.6

1.

Similar to Definition
2.15
, other notions such as
\mathrm{P}
-weak attractor or
\mathrm{P}
-weak point attractor can be analogously defined.

2.

Note that the definition of the
\mathrm{P}
-pullback attractor is essentially the same as the definition of a pullback attractor given in Definition
2.15
.
In particular, one can see that

\displaystyle\Phi^{t}_{\theta_{-t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(x)=\varphi^{t}_{\omega^{-}}(x),

(3.14)

meaning that the second property in Definition
3.5
is the pullback attraction as stated in Definition
2.15
.
However, the main motivation for using this definition is the fact that the pullback attractor
\mathcal{A}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})
depends only on
\omega^{-}
, in the spirit of
[
17
, Corollary 4.5]
.
Thus, thanks to (
3.14
) and to the fact that the SDS filters out the future of the noise, it is natural to formulate the invariance property in Definition
3.5
in terms of the SDS and not of the RDS as in Definition
2.15
.

3.

For the uniqueness of attractors, we refer to
[
20
, Section 5]
, in particular
[
20
, Corollary 5.8]
.

We can prove the following statement.

Theorem 3.7

There exists a
\mathrm{P}
-pullback attractor
\mathcal{A}
for the SDS
\varphi
generated by the SDE (
2.17
).

Proof.

From Theorem
2.22
, it follows that for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi^{t}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(x)=\int_{0}^{t}F\left(\Phi^{s}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(x)\right)\mathrm{d}s+x-\sigma(\mathcal{D}_{H}P_{t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+}))(-t),

where we recall that
B^{H}_{t}\bigl(P_{t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\bigr)=-\bigl(\mathcal{D}_{H}P_{t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\bigr)(-t)
.
We define the stationary fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

\displaystyle Z_{t}(\omega):=\sigma\int_{-\infty}^{0}\exp(-(t-\tau))\mathrm{d}(\mathcal{D}_{H}\omega^{-})(\tau)+\sigma\int_{0}^{t}\exp(-(t-\tau))\mathrm{d}B^{H}_{\tau}(P_{\tau}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})).

(3.15)

To see that this is well-defined as a Young integral on a fixed time interval, we use Definition
2.18
together with Lemma
2.19
which ensure that the mapping

\tau\mapsto B^{H}_{\tau}\bigl(P_{\tau}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\bigr)

is
\frac{H}{2}
-Hölder continuous. In (
3.17
), we show that the improper integral in (
3.15
) is well defined. Keeping these facts in mind, it follows from (
3.15
) that
Z
is the stationary solution of the SDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Z_{t}(\omega)=-Z_{t}(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}B^{H}_{t}\big(P_{t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\big).

Consequently, for every
0\leq s\leq t

\displaystyle Z_{t}(\omega)-Z_{s}(\omega)=-\int_{s}^{t}Z_{\tau}(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\sigma\left(B^{H}_{t}\left(P_{t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right)-B^{H}_{s}\left(P_{s}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right)\right).

Recall that for
t\geq 0
, we have
\theta_{-t}\omega=\left(\vartheta_{t}\omega^{-},P_{t}(\omega^{+},\omega^{-})\right)
. Thus, from (
2.15
) and
(iv)
in Definition
2.7
, it follows that for all
0\leq\tau\leq t

\displaystyle\begin{split}&B^{H}_{\tau}\left(P_{\tau}\left(\vartheta_{t}\omega^{-},P_{t}(\omega^{+},\omega^{-})\right)\right)=-\left(\mathcal{D}_{H}P_{\tau}\left(\vartheta_{t}\omega^{-},P_{t}(\omega^{+},\omega^{-})\right)\right)(-\tau)\\
&=\mathcal{D}_{H}P_{t}\left(\vartheta_{t}\omega^{-},P_{t}(\omega^{+},\omega^{-})\right)(\tau-t)-\mathcal{D}_{H}P_{t}\left(\vartheta_{t}\omega^{-},P_{t}(\omega^{+},\omega^{-})\right)(-t)\\
&=(\mathcal{D}_{H}\omega^{-})(\tau-t)-(\mathcal{D}_{H}\omega^{-})(-t).\end{split}

This further implies using (
2.16
) that

\displaystyle Z_{s}(\theta_{-t}\omega)

\displaystyle=\sigma\int_{-\infty}^{0}\exp(-(s-\tau))\mathrm{d}(\mathcal{D}_{H}\vartheta_{t}\omega^{-})(\tau)

\displaystyle+\sigma\int_{0}^{s}\exp(-(s-\tau))\mathrm{d}B^{H}_{\tau}\left(P_{\tau}(\vartheta_{t}\omega^{-},P_{t}(\omega^{+},\omega^{-}))\right)=\sigma\int_{-\infty}^{s-t}\exp(t-s+\tau)\mathrm{d}(\mathcal{D}_{H}\omega^{-})(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sigma\int_{-\infty}^{0}\exp(\tau)\mathrm{d}(\mathcal{D}_{H}\vartheta_{t-s}\omega^{-})(\tau)=:\tilde{Z}_{s-t}(\omega^{-}).

(3.16)

which shows the stationarity of
Z
.
From (
2.10
), (
2.16
), and Lemma
2.19
, we infer that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{D}_{H}\vartheta_{t-s}\omega^{-}\|_{\frac{H}{2};[-n-1,-n]}

\displaystyle=\sup_{u,v\in[-n-1,-n]}\frac{\bigl|\mathcal{D}_{H}\omega^{-}(s-t+u)-\mathcal{D}_{H}\omega^{-}(s-t+v)\bigr|}{|u-v|^{\frac{H}{2}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{1+n+t-s}\,\|\mathcal{D}_{H}\omega^{-}\|_{\mathcal{B}_{1-H}}.

Hence, we can estimate the Young integral by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\bigl(\mathcal{D}_{H}\vartheta_{t-s}\omega^{-}\bigr)(\tau)\right|&\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left|\int_{-n-1}^{-n}e^{\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\bigl(\mathcal{D}_{H}\vartheta_{t-s}\omega^{-}\bigr)(\tau)\right|\\
&\lesssim\|\mathcal{D}_{H}\omega^{-}\|_{\mathcal{B}_{1-H}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-n}\sqrt{1+n+t-s}.\end{split}

(3.17)

Now for
\kappa_{t}(\omega,x):=\Phi^{t}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(x)-Z_{t}(\omega)
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left|\kappa_{t}(\omega,x)\right|^{2}&=2\left\langle F\left(\kappa_{t}(\omega,x)+Z_{t}(\omega)\right)-F(Z_{t}(\omega)),\kappa_{t}(\omega,x)\right\rangle\\
&+2\left\langle Z_{t}(\omega)+F(Z_{t}(\omega)),\kappa_{t}(\omega,x)\right\rangle\\
&\leq 2C_{1}^{F}-2C_{2}^{F}\left|\kappa_{t}(\omega,x)\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{C_{2}^{F}}\left|Z_{t}(\omega)+F(z_{t}(\omega))\right|^{2}+C_{2}^{F}\left|\kappa_{t}(\omega,x)\right|^{2}\\
&=-C_{2}^{F}\left|\kappa_{t}(\omega,x)\right|^{2}+2C_{1}^{F}+\frac{1}{C_{2}^{F}}\left|z_{t}(\omega)+F(Z_{t}(\omega))\right|^{2}\\
&\leq-C_{2}^{F}\left|\kappa_{t}(\omega,x)\right|^{2}+\bar{C}^{F}(1+|Z_{t}(\omega)|)^{2N},\end{split}

(3.18)

for an arbitrary constant
\bar{C}^{F}
. Hence, by Grönwall’s inequality

\displaystyle\left|\kappa_{t}(\omega,x)\right|^{2}\leq\exp(-C_{2}^{F}t)|x-Z_{0}(\omega)|^{2}+\bar{C}^{F}\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(-C_{2}^{F}(t-\tau)\right)\left(1+|Z_{\tau}(\omega)|\right)^{2N}\mathrm{d}\tau.

Thus replacing
\omega
by
\theta_{-t}\omega
, we obtain based on (
3.16
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left|\phi^{t}_{\omega^{-}}(x)\right|\leq\exp(-\frac{C_{2}^{F}}{2}t)\left(|x|+|Z_{0}(\theta_{-t}\omega)|\right)\\
&+|Z_{t}(\theta_{-t}\omega)|+\sqrt{\bar{C}^{F}}\sqrt{\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(-C_{2}^{F}(t-\tau)\right)\left(1+|Z_{\tau}(\theta_{-t}\omega)|\right)^{2N}\mathrm{d}\tau}\\
&=\exp(-\frac{C_{2}^{F}}{2}t)\left(|x|+|\tilde{Z}_{-t}(\omega^{-})|\right)+|\tilde{Z}_{0}(\omega^{-})|+\sqrt{\bar{C}^{F}}\sqrt{\int_{-t}^{0}\exp\left(C_{2}^{F}\tau\right)\left(1+|\tilde{Z}_{\tau}(\omega^{-})|\right)^{2N}\mathrm{d}\tau}\\
&\leq\exp(-\frac{C_{2}^{F}}{2}t)\left(|x|+|Z_{-t}(\omega^{-})|\right)+|\tilde{Z}_{0}(\omega^{-})|+\sqrt{\bar{C}^{F}}\sqrt{\int_{-\infty}^{0}\exp\left(C_{2}^{F}\tau\right)\left(1+|\tilde{Z}_{\tau}(\omega^{-})|\right)^{2N}\mathrm{d}\tau},\end{split}

(3.19)

where we used the following elementary inequalities

\displaystyle\lvert X\rvert-\lvert Y\rvert\leq\lvert X-Y\rvert\leq\lvert X\rvert+\lvert Y\rvert,\quad\text{and}\quad\sqrt{\lvert X\rvert+\lvert Y\rvert}\leq\sqrt{\lvert X\rvert}+\sqrt{\lvert Y\rvert}.

We further set

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}(\omega)=\rho(\omega^{-}):=|\tilde{Z}_{0}(\omega^{-})|+\sqrt{\int_{-\infty}^{0}\bar{C}^{F}\exp\left(C_{2}^{F}\tau\right)\left(1+|\tilde{Z}_{\tau}(\omega^{-})|\right)^{2N}\mathrm{d}\tau}.

Note that
\tilde{\rho}(\omega)
is tempered. To see this, by Remark
2.6
, it suffices to verify that

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\tilde{\rho}(\theta_{t}\omega)\right]<\infty.

This follows directly from (
3.17
) together with Fernique’s theorem
[
51

.
Since for the first term in (

) we have for every
\omega^{-}\in\mathcal{B}
that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\exp(-\frac{C_{2}^{F}}{2}t)\left(|x|+|\tilde{Z}_{-t}(\omega^{-})|\right)=0,

we get that

B(0,\epsilon+\rho(\omega^{-}))
is a random absorbing set for
\phi
. Then according to
[
26
, Theorem 3.5]
, the pullback attractor is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\omega^{-})=\bigcap_{s\geq 0}\bigcup_{t\geq s}\Phi^{t}_{\theta_{-t}\omega}\left(B(0,\epsilon+\tilde{\rho}(\theta_{-t}\omega))\right)=\bigcap_{s\geq 0}\bigcup_{t\geq s}\phi^{t}_{\omega^{-}}\left(B(0,\epsilon+\rho(\vartheta_{t}\omega^{-}))\right).

(3.20)

Remark 3.8

It is straightforward to verify that the collection of tempered sets
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is inclusion-closed
[

. Consequently, for the

-pullback attractor established in Theorem

, we conclude that it attracts tempered sets and not only deterministic compact sets.

4
Synchronization by noise

We are now ready to present the main result of this manuscript. We first show that the support of the disintegrated measures
(\mu_{\omega^{-}})_{\omega^{-}\in\tilde{B}}
is contained in any
\mathrm{P}
-weak point attractor. We then explore this relation further with the minimal
\mathrm{P}
-weak point attractor. Finally, we prove weak synchronization under certain conditions.

4.1
Support of the invariant measure of the RDS

We begin with the following useful lemma that relates times averages with ensamble averages in our setting.

Lemma 4.1

Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
G\in\mathrm{BL}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{B}}G(\varphi^{s}_{\omega^{-}}(x_{0}),\omega^{-})\ \mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d\omega^{-}})\mathrm{d}s=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{B}}G(x,\omega^{-})\mu\left(\mathrm{d}(x,\omega^{-})\right).

Proof.

We prove this by contradiction. Assume that for some fixed
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\tilde{G}\in\mathrm{BL}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there exists an increasing sequence
\{t_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
converging to infinity and a constant
\delta>0
such that for every
t_{n}

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{t_{n}}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\tilde{G}(\varphi^{s}_{\omega^{-}}(x_{0}),\omega^{-})\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})\,\mathrm{d}s-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{B}}\tilde{G}(x,\omega^{-})\,\mu(\mathrm{d}(x,\omega^{-}))\right|>\delta.

(4.1)

From (
2.18
), we have for
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\Phi^{t}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(x_{0})\right)\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}\left(\Pi_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\right)_{\star}\mu,

in total variation. Since for every
t\geq 0
, the random variables
\Phi^{t}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(x_{0})
and
\Phi^{t}_{\theta_{-t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(x_{0})=\varphi^{t}_{\omega^{-}}(x_{0})
have the same law, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\varphi^{t}_{\omega^{-}}(x_{0})\right)\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}\left(\Pi_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\right)_{\star}\mu,

(4.2)

in total variation. Let
\{\nu_{t_{n},x_{0}}\}_{t_{n}\geq 0}
be a family of measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{B}
defined by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\nu_{t_{n},x_{0}}(\Gamma):=\frac{1}{t_{n}}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}\int_{\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}}\chi_{\Gamma}\left(\varphi^{s}_{P_{s}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(x_{0}),P_{s}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right)\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{d}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+}))\,\mathrm{d}s\\
=\frac{1}{t_{n}}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\chi_{\Gamma}(\varphi^{s}_{\omega^{-}}(x_{0}),\omega^{-})\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})\,\mathrm{d}s,\end{split}

for
\Gamma\in\sigma(\mathbb{R}^{d})\otimes\sigma(\mathcal{B})
. Equivalently, this can be written using the transition semigroup
\{\mathcal{Q}_{t}\}_{t>0}
as

\displaystyle\nu_{t_{n},x_{0}}(\Gamma)=\frac{1}{t_{n}}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{Q}_{s}\left((x_{0},\omega^{-});\Gamma\right)\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})\,\mathrm{d}s.

From Definition
2.10
, we conclude that for every
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\mathcal{Q}_{t})_{\star}\nu_{t_{n},x_{0}}(\Gamma)&=\frac{1}{t_{n}}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{Q}_{t+s}\left((x_{0},\omega^{-});\Gamma\right)\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&=\nu_{t_{n},x_{0}}(\Gamma)+\frac{1}{t_{n}}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n}+t}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{Q}_{s}\left((x_{0},\omega^{-});\Gamma\right)\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\quad-\frac{1}{t_{n}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{Q}_{s}\left((x_{0},\omega^{-});\Gamma\right)\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})\,\mathrm{d}s.\end{split}

(4.3)

Note that
\left(\Pi_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\right)_{\star}\mu
is a probability measure and hence regular. Thus, from (
4.2
), we deduce that for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists a compact subset
K_{\epsilon}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a time
t_{\epsilon}>0
such that for all
t\geq t_{\epsilon}
,

\displaystyle\nu_{t,x_{0}}(K_{\epsilon}\times\mathcal{B})>1-\epsilon.

(4.4)

This implies that the family
\{\nu_{t_{n},x_{0}}\}_{n\geq 0}
is tight in
\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
according to
[
21
, Definition 4.2]
. By Prokhorov’s theorem for random measures
[
21
, Theorem 4.4]
, it follows that this family is relatively compact with respect to the narrow topology on
\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
which by
[
21
, Corollary 4.10]
is generated by the class of mappings

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{B}}G(x,\omega^{-})\,\nu(\mathrm{d}(x,\omega^{-})),

for all
G\in\mathrm{BL}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Let
\nu
be a limit point of
\{\nu_{t_{n},x_{0}}\}
. From (
4.3
), it follows that
\nu
is invariant under the transition semigroup
\{\mathcal{Q}_{t}\}_{t>0}
. Therefore, by the uniqueness of the invariant measure of the SDS
\mu
, we conclude that
\nu=\mu
.
Since
\mu
is the unique limit point of the family
\{\nu_{t_{n},x_{0}}\}
with respect to the narrow topology, we deduce that for a subsequence
\{t_{n_{k}}\}_{k\geq 0}\subseteq\{t_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{t_{n_{k}}\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{B}}\tilde{G}(x,\omega^{-})\nu_{t_{n_{k}},x_{0}}\left(\mathrm{d}(x,\omega^{-})\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t_{n_{k}}\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{n_{k}}}\int_{0}^{t_{n_{k}}}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\tilde{G}(\varphi^{s}_{\omega^{-}}(x_{0}),\omega^{-})\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{B}}\tilde{G}(x,\omega^{-})\,\mu(\mathrm{d}(x,\omega^{-})).

This contradicts (
4.1
).

Remark 4.2

Note that time averaging is a standard technique used to ensure that the family of tight measures
\nu_{t_{n},x_{0}}
converges to
\mu
. Although the family of measures

\int_{\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{Q}_{s}\left((x_{0},\omega^{-});\Gamma\right)\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-}),\quad s\geq 0,

is tight, it is not evident that
\mu
arises as a limit point of this family.
For a strongly mixing, white-noise random dynamical system this holds true, see
[
48
]
for details.
We expect that an analogous result should be valid in our setting as well, although the proof seems challenging in the absence of the Markov property.

Proposition 4.3

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a
\mathrm{P}
-weak point attractor. Then

\displaystyle\mu_{\omega^{-}}\big({\mathcal{A}}(\omega^{-})\big)=1\quad\mathbf{P}\text{-a.s.},

(4.5)

which in particular implies that

\displaystyle\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{\omega^{-}})\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}(\omega^{-})\quad\mathbf{P}\text{-a.s.}

(4.6)

Proof.

For an arbitrary
\epsilon>0
we define

\displaystyle\begin{split}G_{\epsilon}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\tilde{\mathcal{B}}&\longrightarrow[0,1],\\
(x,\omega^{-})&\longrightarrow 1-\min\left\{\frac{d(x,\mathcal{A}(\omega^{-}))}{\epsilon},1\right\}.\end{split}

(4.7)

Here we recall that
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
is the measurable subset of
\mathcal{B}
introduced obtained in Lemma
2.12
.
It is straightforward to verify that
G_{\epsilon}\in\mathrm{BL}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Hence, by Lemma
4.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{B}}G_{\epsilon}(\varphi^{s}_{\omega^{-}}(x_{0}),\omega^{-})\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{B}}G_{\epsilon}(x,\omega^{-})\,\mu\left(\mathrm{d}(x,\omega^{-})\right).

(4.8)

Let
0<\delta<1
be arbitrarily small. Then it is clear that

\displaystyle(1-\delta)\chi_{\mathcal{N}(\omega^{-},\epsilon\delta)}(x)\leq G_{\epsilon}(x,\omega^{-})\leq\chi_{\mathcal{N}(\omega^{-},2\epsilon)}(x),

(4.9)

where
\mathcal{N}(\omega^{-},\alpha)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:d(x,\mathcal{A}(\omega^{-}))<\alpha\right\}
for
\alpha>0
. Therefore, combining (
4.8
) and (
4.9
) and using that
\mathcal{A}
is a
\mathrm{P}
-weak point attractor, we obtain for
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that

\displaystyle(1-\delta)

\displaystyle=(1-\delta)\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\left(1-\mathbf{P}\left(d\left(\varphi^{s}_{\omega^{-}}(x_{0}),\mathcal{A}(\omega^{-})\right)\geq\epsilon\delta\right)\right)\mathrm{d}s

(4.10)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\mu_{\omega^{-}}\left(\mathcal{N}(\omega^{-},2\epsilon)\right)\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})\leq 1.

(4.11)

Thus, since
0<\delta<1
can be chosen arbitrarily small, we obtain

\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\mu_{\omega^{-}}\left(\mathcal{N}(\omega^{-},2\epsilon)\right)\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})=1.

Obviously
\mu_{\omega^{-}}\left(\mathcal{N}(\omega^{-},2\epsilon)\right)\leq 1
and
\mathbf{P}(\tilde{B})=1
. Hence

\displaystyle\mu_{\omega^{-}}\left(\mathcal{N}(\omega^{-},2\epsilon)\right)=1,\quad\mathbf{P}\text{-a.s.}

(4.12)

Since
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary and using the fact that
\mathcal{A}(\omega^{-})
is closed, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\omega^{-})=\bigcap_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{N}\left(\omega^{-},\tfrac{1}{n}\right).

(4.13)

This together with (
4.12
) implies (
4.5
).

Remark 4.4

From the definition, the
\mathrm{P}
-pullback attractor is also a
\mathrm{P}
-weak point attractor. Therefore, the claim of Proposition
4.10
is in particular valid for the
\mathrm{P}
-pullback attractor. However, since we plan to show a weak type of synchronization, we further focus on weak point attractors.

Let us continue with the following lemma.

Lemma 4.5

Let
\epsilon>0
and define

G_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{B}\longrightarrow[0,1]

by

G_{\epsilon}(x,\omega^{-}):=\begin{cases}\min\left\{\dfrac{d\left(x,\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{\omega^{-}})\right)}{\epsilon},1\right\},&\text{if }(x,\omega^{-})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\tilde{\mathcal{B}},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then
G_{\epsilon}\in\mathrm{BL}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and is jointly measurable.

Proof.

First, it is not hard to see that for every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

|G_{\epsilon}(x,\omega^{-})-G_{\epsilon}(y,\omega^{-})|\leq\frac{\lvert x-y\rvert}{\epsilon}.

To prove the measurability of
G_{\epsilon}
with respect to
\omega^{-}
, it suffices to show that for every
r>0
,

\left\{\omega^{-}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}:d\left(x,\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{\omega^{-}})\right)<r\right\}\in\sigma(\mathcal{B}).

To see this, note that by the definition of the support of a measure and Lemma
2.12
, we have

\left\{\omega^{-}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}:d\left(x,\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{\omega^{-}})\right)<r\right\}=\left\{\omega^{-}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}:\mu_{\omega^{-}}(B(x,r))>0\right\}\in\sigma(\mathcal{B}).

Therefore,
G_{\epsilon}\in\mathrm{BL}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The joint measurability of
G_{\epsilon}
follows from
[
21
, Lemma 1.1]
.

Remark 4.6

From Proposition
4.3
, for every
\mathrm{P}
-weak point attractor
\mathcal{A}
, it follows that

\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{\omega^{-}})\subseteq\mathcal{A}(\omega^{-}).

A natural question is whether the family

\{\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{\omega^{-}})\}_{\omega^{-}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}

itself constitutes a
\mathrm{P}
-weak point attractor.
According to Remark
2.17
, once we have a strongly mixing white-noise RDS, this property holds.

The following proposition provides a partial answer to the this question.

Proposition 4.7

Let
\overline{\mathcal{A}}=\left\{\overline{\mathcal{A}}(\omega^{-})\right\}_{\omega^{-}\in\mathcal{B}}
be defined by

\overline{\mathcal{A}}(\omega^{-}):=\begin{cases}\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{\omega^{-}})&\text{if }\omega^{-}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}},\\
\{0\}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then, for every
\epsilon>0
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{P}\left(d\left(\varphi^{s}_{\omega^{-}}(x_{0}),\overline{\mathcal{A}}(\omega^{-})\right)\geq\epsilon\right)\mathrm{d}s=0.

(4.14)

Proof.

Let
G_{\epsilon}
be as in Lemma
4.5
. From Lemma
4.1
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{B}}G_{\epsilon}(\varphi^{s}_{\omega^{-}}(x_{0}),\omega^{-})\ \mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d\omega^{-}})\mathrm{d}s=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{B}}G_{\epsilon}(x,\omega^{-})\mu\left(\mathrm{d}(x,\omega^{-})\right)\\
&=\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\int_{\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{\omega^{-}})}G_{\epsilon}(x,\omega^{-})\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}x)\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})=0.\end{split}

(4.15)

Thus, from the definition of
G_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{P}\left(d\left(\varphi^{s}_{\omega^{-}}(x_{0}),\overline{\mathcal{A}}(\omega^{-})\right)\geq\epsilon\right)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad\leq\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{B}}G_{\epsilon}(\varphi^{s}_{\omega^{-}}(x_{0}),\omega^{-})\ \mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d\omega^{-}})\mathrm{d}s=0.

This concludes the proof.

Remark 4.8

Unfortunately, Proposition
4.7
does not allow us to conclude that
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
is a
\mathrm{P}
-weak point attractor. Nevertheless, it is evident that every
\mathrm{P}
-weak point attractor satisfies the condition (
4.14
). This observation motivates the conjecture that
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
is a
\mathrm{P}
-weak point attractor and hence minimal.

Let us now examine how Proposition
4.7
provides a partial answer to the fact that
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
is the minimal
\mathrm{P}
-weak point attractor.

Proposition 4.9

Consider the setting of Proposition
4.7
. Let
(x_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be an arbitrary sequence in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then there exists an increasing sequence
(t_{n})_{n\geq 1}
with
t_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, such that

\displaystyle\lim_{t_{n}\rightarrow\infty}\mathbf{P}\left(d\left(\varphi^{t_{n}}_{\omega^{-}}(x_{i}),\overline{\mathcal{A}}(\omega^{-})\right)\geq t\right)=0,

for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
t>0
.

Proof.

First, note that Proposition
4.7
implies that for

\mathcal{R}_{m}(s,\omega^{-}):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j,k\leq m\end{subarray}}\mathbf{P}\left(d\big(\varphi^{s}_{\omega^{-}}(x_{k}),\overline{\mathcal{A}}(\omega^{-})\big)\geq\frac{1}{j}\right)

we have

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{R}_{m}(s,\omega^{-})\,\mathrm{d}s=0.

Therefore, we can find a sequence
(t_{n}^{m})_{n\geq 1}
such that

\lim_{t_{n}^{m}\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{R}_{m}(t_{n}^{m},\omega^{-})=0.

Now, applying a standard diagonal argument to the sequence of increasing functions
(\mathcal{R}_{m})_{m\geq 1}
completes the proof.

We further investigate the structure of
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
.

Lemma 4.10

Consider the setting of Proposition
4.7
. Then there exists a deterministic constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\left(\mu_{\omega^{-}}\times\mu_{\omega^{-}}\right)(\Gamma_{C})\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})=0,

where

\Gamma_{C}:=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x-y|>C\right\}.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and define

\kappa^{t}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(x,y):=\Phi^{t}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(x)-\Phi^{t}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(y).

Then, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left|\kappa^{t}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(x,y)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\left\langle F\left(\Phi^{t}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(x)\right)-F\left(\Phi^{t}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(y)\right),\kappa^{t}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(x,y)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}^{F}-C_{2}^{F}\left|\kappa^{t}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(x,y)\right|^{2}.

Thus, by Grönwall’s inequality,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\Phi^{t}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(x)-\Phi^{t}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(y)\right|^{2}&\leq\exp\left(-C_{2}^{F}t\right)|x-y|^{2}+C^{F}_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(-C_{2}^{F}(t-\tau)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\\
&\leq\exp\left(-C_{2}^{F}t\right)|x-y|^{2}+\frac{C_{1}^{F}}{C_{2}^{F}}.\end{split}

(4.16)

Note that for every
\Gamma\in\sigma(\mathbb{R}^{d})\otimes\sigma(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, analogously to (
3.8
) and (
3.9
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\scalebox{1.2}{$\chi_{\Gamma}$}(x,y)\,\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}x)\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})\\
&=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\scalebox{1.2}{$\chi_{\Gamma}$}\left(\Phi^{t}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(x),\Phi^{t}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(y)\right)\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}x)\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{+}).\end{split}

(4.17)

Setting

C:=\frac{C_{1}^{F}}{C_{2}^{F}},

it follows from (
4.16
) and the dominated convergence theorem that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\scalebox{1.2}{$\chi_{\Gamma_{C}}$}\left(\Phi^{t}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(x),\Phi^{t}_{(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})}(y)\right)\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}x)\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{+})=0.

This, together with (
4.17
), yields that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\scalebox{1.2}{$\chi_{\Gamma_{C}}$}(x,y)\,\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}x)\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})\\
&\quad=\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\left(\mu_{\omega^{-}}\times\mu_{\omega^{-}}\right)(\Gamma_{C})\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})=0.\end{split}

(4.18)

Now, we can state the following result.

Corollary 4.11

Assume the hypotheses of the second statement of Theorem
3.2
together with Proposition
4.7
hold. Then there exists an integer
p
and a collection of random variables
\{b_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq p}
, where each
b_{i}:\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that:

1)

The family
\overline{\mathcal{A}}=\{\overline{\mathcal{A}}(\omega^{-})\}_{\omega^{-}\in\mathcal{B}}
is given by

\overline{\mathcal{A}}(\omega^{-})=\begin{cases}\left\{b_{i}(\omega^{-})\right\}_{1\leq i\leq p},&\text{if }\omega^{-}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}},\\
\{0\},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Moreover, for every
\omega^{-}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}

\mu_{\omega^{-}}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_{\{b_{i}(\omega^{-})\}}.

2)

For every
\epsilon>0
and every
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{P}\left(d\left(\varphi^{s}_{\omega^{-}}(x_{0}),\overline{\mathcal{A}}(\omega^{-})\right)\geq\epsilon\right)\,\mathrm{d}s=0.

3)

For the constant
C
given in Lemma
4.10
, we have

\max_{1\leq i\neq j\leq p}\left|b_{i}(\omega^{-})-b_{j}(\omega^{-})\right|\leq C,\quad\mathbf{P}\text{-a.s.}

Proof.

The first two claims follow from Propositions
3.4
and
4.7
. Note that the existence of the random variables
\{b_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq p}
follows from any measurable selection theorem applied to the family
\{\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{\omega^{-}})\}_{\omega^{-}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}
. Given this collection of random variables, it follows from (
4.18
) that

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\scalebox{1.2}{$\chi_{\Gamma_{C}}$}(x,y)\,\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}x)\mu_{\omega^{-}}(\mathrm{d}y)\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i,j\leq p\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p^{2}}\int_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}\scalebox{1.2}{$\chi_{\Gamma_{C}}$}\big(b_{i}(\omega^{-}),b_{j}(\omega^{-})\big)\,\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega^{-})

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i,j\leq p\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p^{2}}\mathbf{P}\left(\left|b_{i}(\omega^{-})-b_{j}(\omega^{-})\right|>C\right)=0,

which proves the last claim.

4.2
Weak synchronization

In Subsection
4.1
, we investigated the relationship between random measures and weak point attractors. In particular, we observed in Propositions
4.7
and
4.9
that one can interpret the support of the random measures as minimal
\mathrm{P}
-weak point attractors in an averaged sense. Moreover, when the top Lyapunov exponent is negative, the support of these random measures is discrete. The question which arises is whether this support consists of a single point, as investigated in
[
28
]
for white-noise RDS. To address this issue in the non-Markvoian case, we first introduce an additional assumption on the drift.

Assumption 4.12

In addition to Assumption
2.21
, we assume that the drift term
F
is eventually strictly monotone. That is, there exist constants
R>0
and
C_{4}^{F}>0
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|x|,|y|\geq R
,

\displaystyle\langle F(x)-F(y),x-y\rangle\leq-C_{4}^{F}|x-y|^{2}.

(4.19)

We further impose the following conditions:

Assumption 4.13

1)

There exists an integer
p
and a collection of random variables
\{b_{i}:\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}\}_{1\leq i\leq p}
such that for every
\omega^{-}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
, the support of
\mu_{\omega^{-}}
is given by

\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{\omega^{-}})=\left\{b_{i}(\omega^{-})\mid 1\leq i\leq p\right\}.

2)

There exists a deterministic constant
C>0
and a set

\tilde{\Omega}\subset\Omega:=\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\times\mathcal{B}
of full measure such that for every

\omega=(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\in\tilde{\Omega}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\omega^{-}):=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i,j\leq p\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\left|b_{i}(\omega^{-})-b_{j}(\omega^{-})\right|\leq C.

(4.20)

Furthermore, for every
t\geq 0
, the set
\tilde{\Omega}
is invariant under
\theta_{t}
meaning that

\theta_{t}(\tilde{\Omega})\subseteq\tilde{\Omega}.

Remark 4.14

The eventually strict monotonicity condition on the drift
F
is necessary to show that the top Lyapunov exponent of the SDE (
2.17
) is negative for increasing
\sigma
, as established in
[
9
]
. This implies condition 1) of the previous assumption, as shown in Proposition
3.4
. This assumption on
F
will also be used to prove synchronization, in particular in Lemma
4.22
.

Remark 4.15

Condition 2) of Assumption
4.13
is implied by the assumptions of Corollary
4.11
. More precisely, Corollary
4.11
yields the existence of a subset

\overline{\mathcal{B}}\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
such that

\mathbf{P}(\overline{\mathcal{B}})=1
and

\max_{i\neq j}\lvert b_{i}(\omega^{-})-b_{j}(\omega^{-})\rvert\leq C,\qquad\forall\,\omega^{-}\in\overline{\mathcal{B}}.

By considering

\overline{\Omega}:=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z},\\
j\in\mathbb{N}\end{subarray}}\theta_{\tfrac{k}{2^{j}}}(\overline{\mathcal{B}}\times\mathcal{B}),

we obtain a full measure set that is
\theta_{\tfrac{k}{2^{j}}}
-invariant for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Now, define

\displaystyle\tilde{\Omega}:=\bigl\{\omega\in\overline{\mathcal{B}}\times\mathcal{B}:\forall t\in\mathbb{R},\;\mathcal{R}(P_{t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+}))\leq C\bigr\}.

(4.21)

By definition,
\tilde{\Omega}
is
\theta_{t}
-invariant for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\tilde{\Omega}\subseteq\overline{\Omega}
.
For every
\omega\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\times\mathcal{B}
, we have

\Phi^{t}_{\omega}\left\{b_{i}(\omega^{-})\right\}_{1\leq i\leq p}=\left\{b_{i}(P_{t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+}))\right\}_{1\leq i\leq p}.

Thus, the map

\displaystyle t\mapsto\mathcal{R}\!\left(P_{t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right)

(4.22)

is continuous.
Moreover, by the definition of
\overline{\Omega}
on the dense set

\{t_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}=\bigl\{\tfrac{k}{2^{j}}:k\in\mathbb{Z},\,j\in\mathbb{N}\bigr\}\subseteq\mathbb{R},

we have
\mathcal{R}(P_{t_{i}}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+}))\leq C
.
Together with the continuity of the map (
4.22
), this implies that

\overline{\Omega}\subseteq\tilde{\Omega}

yielding that
\tilde{\Omega}
has full measure.

We now define a weak type of synchronization in our non-Markovian setting.

Definition 4.16

We say that weak synchronization holds if there exists a random point
b:\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for
\mathbf{P}
-almost every
\omega^{-}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{\omega^{-}})=\{b(\omega^{-})\}.

Remark 4.17

In
[
28
, Definition 2.16]
, weak synchronization means that the minimal point attractor is a singleton. As already stated in Remark
2.17
, for strongly mixing white-noise RDS, the random measures
\mu_{\omega^{-}}
are minimal point attractors. In our case, we only know that
\text{supp}(\mu_{\omega^{-}})\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{A}}(\omega^{-})
, which motivates our definition. Consequently, when weak synchronization in the sense of
[
28
, Definition 2.16]
holds, i.e.
\overline{\mathcal{A}}(\omega^{-})=b(\omega^{-})
, this implies that each random measure is a Dirac measure sitting on the equilibrium, i.e.
\mu_{\omega^{-}}=\delta_{b(\omega^{-})}
.

The following result provides a necessary and sufficient condition to verify weak synchronization.

Lemma 4.18

Let
\eta>0
and suppose that

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\chi_{B(0,\eta)}\!\left(\mathcal{R}\!\left(P_{n}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right)\right)=1,\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}.

(4.23)

Then

\mathbf{P}\big(\mathcal{R}(\omega^{-})\leq\eta\big)=1.

In particular, weak synchronization follows if (
4.23
) holds for every
\eta>0
.

Proof.

From the assumption and Fatou’s lemma, we have

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\chi_{B(0,\eta)}\left(\mathcal{R}\left(P_{n}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right)\right)\right)\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\chi_{B(0,\eta)}\left(\mathcal{R}\left(P_{n}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{R}\left(P_{n}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right)\leq\eta\right)=\mathbf{P}\left(\mathcal{R}(\omega^{-})\leq\eta\right),

where in the last step we used the fact that the family
(\theta_{t})_{t\geq 0}

is
\mathbb{P}
-invariant (see Theorem
2.11
). Now let
\mathcal{B}_{\eta}
denote the set of
\mathbf{P}
-full measure such that for each
\omega\in\mathcal{B}_{\eta}
one has
\mathcal{R}(\omega^{-})\leq\eta
. It then follows that

\mathbf{P}\left(\bigcap_{m\geq 1}\mathcal{B}_{\frac{1}{m}}\right)=1,

and for each
\omega^{-}
in this intersection, we have
\mathcal{R}(\omega^{-})=0
. This yields weak synchronization.

Therefore it suffices to establish (
4.23
) in order to prove weak synchronization. Since the noise is additive, the main idea is to prove synchronization for an ODE with a suitable deterministic forcing term and then to transfer this to the SDE based on a support theorem. To this end, we outline the strategy as follows.

Step 1).
Let
R
and
C
be the constants given in Assumptions
4.12
and
4.13
. For a fixed
T>0
, define the function
h^{v}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle h^{v}(t)=vt\sigma^{-1}e_{1},

(4.24)

where the positive parameter
v\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
will be chosen sufficiently large and
e_{1}
denotes the first standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We consider the ODE

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=F(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}h^{v}(t),\\
Y_{0}=x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{cases}

(4.25)

which is obtained by replacing the stochastic driving term in the SDE (
2.17
) with the deterministic control
h^{v}
.
Then, for every initial condition
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the solution of the ODE lies for most of the time
t\in[0,T]
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R+C)}
. This follows increasing the parameter
v
which amplifies the effect of
h^{v}
, enabling it to dominate any attractive effect of
F
within compact sets. Furthermore, the monotonicity condition on
F
will ensure the synchronization of the trajectories of (
4.25
) which belong to
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R+C)}
.

Step 2).
Let
h^{\nu}
be as in the previous step. By applying the support theorem
[
45
, Proposition 5.8]
to the SDE (
2.17
), we obtain that in a neighborhood of
h^{v}
, there exists a measurable set
\overline{\Omega}_{T}
of positive probability such that for every element of
\overline{\Omega}_{T}
and every initial condition
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the solution of (
2.17
) lies in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R+C)}
for most of the time
t\in[0,T]
. We then use (
4.19
), which implies that if two solutions driven by the same realization of the noise but starting from different initial data are both in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R)}
for most of the time
t\in[0,T]
, then they become exponentially close and therefore synchronize.

Step 3).
Due to (
4.20
) and in view of the invariance property

\Phi^{t}_{\omega}\left\{b_{i}(\omega^{-})\right\}_{1\leq i\leq p}=\left\{b_{i}(P_{t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+}))\right\}_{1\leq i\leq p},

we can ensure that all these random variables belong to
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R)}
for most of the time
t\in[0,T]
whenever
\omega\in\overline{\Omega}_{T}
. Using the fact that
\overline{\Omega}_{T}
has positive measure, the trajectory
t\mapsto\theta_{t}\omega
returns infinitely often to this set by Poincaré’s recurrence theorem. Finally, since
T>0
is arbitrary, we iterate the same procedure for large values of
T
to verify the assumption of Lemma
4.18
.

Having outlined the main strategy, we make these steps rigorous.

Remark 4.19

Although we can increase
h^{v}
in any direction, we use for simplicity the form
h^{v}(t)=vt\sigma^{-1}e_{1}

which increases
h^{v}
in the direction of
e_{1}
.

We begin with the following lemma which verifies
Step 1)
.

Lemma 4.20

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
v\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
h
as introduced in (
4.24
) and let

\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{v}(x)
be the solution to the ODE (
4.25
) with initial datum
x
. We introduce the set

\tilde{K}(T,x,v):=\left\{t\in[0,T]:\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{v}(x)\in\overline{B(0,R+2C)}\right\}.

Then,

\lim_{v\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu_{\mathrm{Leb}}\big(\tilde{K}(T,x,v)\big)=0,

where
\mu_{\mathrm{Leb}}
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

Proof.

By (
4.24
), we have

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{v}(x),e_{1}\rangle-\langle x,e_{1}\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}\langle F\big(\tilde{\Phi}^{\tau}_{v}(x)\big),e_{1}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\tau+vt.

(4.26)

This implies that for every compact ball
\overline{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one can choose
v
sufficiently large such that the following condition holds

(enumi)

the map

t\longmapsto\langle\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{v}(x),e_{1}\rangle,\quad t\in[a,b]\subseteq[0,T],

is increasing whenever
\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{v}(x)\in\overline{K}
for all
t\in[a,b]
.

Moreover, choosing
v
sufficiently large, the continuity of
F
ensures that the first component of the vector field
y\mapsto F(y)+ve_{1}
dominates the other components on the set
\overline{K}
. To be more precise, let
v
be sufficiently large so that condition
(enumi)
holds and

v>\sup_{y\in\overline{K}}|F(y)|
.
For
z\in\operatorname{int}(\overline{K})
, define

T_{z,v,\overline{K}}:=\sup\{t:\tilde{\Phi}^{s}_{v}(z)\in\overline{K}\ \ \forall s\in[0,t]\}.

For
1<i\leq d
and for every interval
[a,b]\subseteq[0,T_{z,v,\overline{K}}]
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\left|\langle\tilde{\Phi}^{b}_{v}(z),e_{i}\rangle-\langle\tilde{\Phi}^{a}_{v}(z),e_{i}\rangle\right|}{\left|\langle\tilde{\Phi}^{b}_{v}(z),e_{1}\rangle-\langle\tilde{\Phi}^{a}_{v}(z),e_{1}\rangle\right|}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|\int_{a}^{b}\langle F(\tilde{\Phi}^{\tau}_{v}(z)),e_{i}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right|}{\left|\int_{a}^{b}\langle F(\tilde{\Phi}^{\tau}_{v}(z)),e_{1}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\tau+v(b-a)\right|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sup_{y\in\overline{K}}|F(y)|}{v-\sup_{y\in\overline{K}}|F(y)|},

which can be made small increasing
v
. Therefore,
for every
R_{2}
such that
R_{2}\gg R+2C
, define

\displaystyle M_{R_{2}}:=\sup_{y\in\overline{B(0,3R_{2})}}\lvert F(y)\rvert.

(4.27)

Furthermore, we choose
v\gg M_{R_{2}}
sufficiently large such that for every
z\in\overline{B(0,R+2C)}
, there exists
\overline{T}_{z,v}\in(0,T]
satisfying:

(1)

\langle\tilde{\Phi}^{\overline{T}_{z,v}}_{v}(z),e_{1}\rangle=2R_{2}
,

(2)

\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{v}(z)\in\overline{B(0,3R_{2})}
for all
t\in[0,\overline{T}_{z,v}]
,

(3)

the function
t\mapsto\langle\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{v}(z),e_{1}\rangle
is increasing on
[0,\overline{T}_{z,v}]
.

Given this condition on
v
, for
z\in\overline{B(0,R+2C)}
define

\tilde{T}_{z,v}:=\sup\left\{s\geq 0:\langle\tilde{\Phi}^{s}_{v}(z),e_{1}\rangle\leq R+2C\right\}.

By definition
\tilde{T}_{z,v}<\overline{T}_{z,v}
. Moreover, by construction
\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{v}(z)
belongs to
\overline{B(0,R+2C)}
for
t\in[0,\tilde{T}_{z,v}]
, whereas for all
t\in[\tilde{T}_{z,v},\overline{T}_{z,v}]
it lies in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R+2C)}
. Therefore, our aim now is to find an upper bound for
\frac{\tilde{T}_{z,v}}{\overline{T}_{z,v}-\tilde{T}_{z,v}}
in order to determine how much time the trajectory
\tilde{\Phi}^{\cdot}_{v}
spends in
\overline{B(0,R+2C)}
during
[0,T]
.

To this end, note that from (
4.26
) and (
4.27
), together with (1)-(3) above, we obtain the following for all

z\in\overline{B(0,R+2C)}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(v-M_{R_{2}}\right)\,\tilde{T}_{z,v}&\leq\left|\int_{0}^{\tilde{T}_{z,v}}\langle F\big(\tilde{\Phi}^{\tau}_{v}(z)\big),e_{1}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\tau+v\tilde{T}_{z,v}\right|\\
&=\left|\langle\tilde{\Phi}^{\tilde{T}_{z,v}}_{v}(z),e_{1}\rangle-\langle z,e_{1}\rangle\right|\\
&\leq 2(R+2C).\end{split}

(4.28)

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle 2R_{2}-(R+2C)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\langle\tilde{\Phi}^{{\overline{T}}_{z,v}}_{v}(z),e_{1}\rangle-\langle\tilde{\Phi}^{\tilde{T}_{z,v}}_{v}(z),e_{1}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\tilde{T}_{z,v}}^{{\overline{T}}_{z,v}}\langle F\big(\tilde{\Phi}^{\tau}_{v}(z)\big),e_{1}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\tau+v\big({T}_{z,v}-\tilde{T}_{z,v}\big)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(v+M_{R_{2}}\right)\,\big({\overline{T}}_{z,v}-\tilde{T}_{z,v}\big).

Consequently

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{T}_{z,v}}{{\overline{T}}_{z,v}-\tilde{T}_{z,v}}\leq\frac{v+M_{R_{2}}}{v-M_{R_{2}}}\cdot\frac{2(R+2C)}{2R_{2}-(R+2C)}=:\kappa(v,R_{2}).

(4.29)

Therefore, after exiting
\overline{B(0,R+2C)}
, the trajectory
(\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{v}(z))_{0\leq t\leq T}
remains in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R+2C)}
for some time before possibly re-entering the ball
\overline{B(0,R+2C)}
.
By the flow property of (
4.25
), we have

\tilde{\Phi}^{t+u}_{v}(z)=\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{v}\!\big(\tilde{\Phi}^{u}_{v}(z)\big),\quad\forall\,t,u\geq 0.

This implies that if at some time
u\in[0,T]
, the trajectory returns to the closed ball
\overline{B(0,R+2C)}
, we can repeat the same procedure with the initial value
\tilde{\Phi}^{u}_{v}(z)\in\overline{B(0,R+2C)}
on the interval
[u,T]
(unless
T-u<\frac{2(R+2C)}{v-M_{R_{2}}}
, which can be made arbitrarily small by (
4.28
)).
This procedure can be iterated up to time
T
. Our argument also yields that the ratio between successive time intervals spent by the trajectory in
\overline{B(0,R+2C)}
and those spent in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R+2C)}
during
[0,T]
is bounded above by
\kappa(v,R_{2})
.

Finally, by the flow property, the same procedure applies to an arbitrary initial datum
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If there exists a time
t_{0}\in[0,T]
such that the trajectory
(\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{v}(x))_{t\in[0,t_{0})}
belongs to
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R+2C)}
and enters the closed ball
\overline{B(0,R+2C)}
at time
t_{0}
,
we can apply the procedure above and use the flow property to extend the argument up to time
T
.

In conclusion, choosing the parameters
R_{2}
and
v
such that

\displaystyle R_{2}

\displaystyle\gg R+2C,

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle\gg M_{R_{2}}=\sup_{y\in\overline{B(0,3R_{2})}}\lvert F(y)\rvert,

the Lebesgue measure of the set
\tilde{K}(T,x,v)
which is the total time the trajectory
(\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{v}(x))_{t\in[0,T]}
spends in
\overline{B(0,R+2C)}
during the interval
[0,T]
, becomes arbitrarily small, uniformly with respect to the initial datum
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

So far, we have constructed a smooth path
h^{v}
for which the corresponding solution of the ODE (
4.25
) belongs to
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R+2C)}
for most of the time independent of the choice of the initial datum.
Our aim now is to construct a suitable subset of
\Omega
with positive probability for which the trajectory of the SDE (
2.17
) has the same property.
Namely, by the support theorem, one expects that in a neighborhood of the smooth path
h^{v}
, the solution of the SDE belongs to
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R+2C)}
independent of the initial datum
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This forms the plan for the next result.

Lemma 4.21

For every
T>0
and
0<\epsilon<1
, there exists a measurable set
\tilde{\Omega}_{T,\epsilon}\subset\Omega

with
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{\Omega}_{T,\epsilon})>0
such that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and every
\omega\in\tilde{\Omega}_{T,\epsilon}
, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{Leb}}\big(K(T,x,\omega)\big)<\epsilon,

where

K(T,x,\omega):=\left\{t\in[0,T]:{\Phi}^{t}_{\omega}(x)\in\overline{B(0,R+C)}\right\}.

Proof.

For
v\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, let
h^{v}:[0,2T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be defined by

h^{v}(t)=vt\sigma^{-1}e_{1}.

Thanks to the support theorem
[
45
, Proposition 5.8]
, for every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
there exist
\delta_{1}(v),\delta_{2}(v)>0
with

\delta_{2}(v)\leq\frac{\delta_{1}(v)}{2}
such that for
i\in\{1,2\}
,

\Omega_{i,\delta_{i}(v),v}=\left\{\omega\in\Omega:\sup_{t\in[0,iT]}\left|B_{t}^{H}\bigl(P_{t}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\bigr)-h^{v}(t)\right|\leq\delta_{i}(v)\right\}

satisfies the following properties:

(1)

\Omega_{2,\delta_{2}(v),v}\subset\Omega_{1,\delta_{1}(v),v}
and

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\Omega_{2,\delta_{2}(v),v}\bigr)>0
.

(2)

For every
\omega\in\Omega_{i,\delta_{i}(v),v}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in[0,iT]\\
x\in\overline{B(0,R+C)}\end{subarray}}\left|\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)-\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{v}(x)\right|\leq C.

(4.30)

We consider two cases.

Case I
. Let
x\in\overline{B(0,R+C)}
. Suppose that
\omega\in\Omega_{1,\delta_{1}(v),v}
for an arbitrary
v\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Then from (
4.30
) we have

|\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{v}(x)|\leq C+|\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)|,\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,T].

This implies

\Big\{t\in[0,T]:\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)\in\overline{B(0,R+C)}\Big\}\subseteq\Big\{t\in[0,T]:\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{v}(x)\in\overline{B(0,R+2C)}\Big\}.

From Lemma
4.20
and by choosing
v(\epsilon)>0
sufficiently large, we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu_{\mathrm{Leb}}\big(\tilde{K}(T,x,v)\big)<\epsilon,\qquad\forall v\geq v(\epsilon).

(4.31)

In conclusion, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{Leb}}\big(K(T,x,\omega)\big)\leq\mu_{\mathrm{Leb}}\big(\tilde{K}(T,x,v(\epsilon))\big)<\epsilon,\qquad\forall\,\omega\in\Omega_{1,\delta_{1}(v(\epsilon)),v(\epsilon)},\forall\,x\in\overline{B(0,R+C)}.

(4.32)

Case II
. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R+C)}
. We show that

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{Leb}}\big(K(T,x,\omega)\big)<\epsilon,\qquad\forall\,\omega\in\Omega_{2,\delta_{2}(v(\epsilon)),v(\epsilon)}.

(4.33)

To this end, note that there exists
t_{0}>0
such that the trajectory

\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R+C)}

for all
t\in[0,t_{0})
.
If
t_{0}\geq T
, then
\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R+C)}
for all
t\in[0,T]
, and hence (
4.33
) follows.
It remains to treat the case
t_{0}<T
.

1.

Let
t>0
such that
t+t_{0}\leq 2T
. By the cocycle property we have
\Phi^{t+t_{0}}_{\omega}(x)=\Phi^{t}_{\theta_{t_{0}}\omega}\circ\Phi^{t_{0}}_{\omega}(x)
. Now, we know that for all
s\in[0,t_{0})
, we have
\Phi^{s}_{\omega}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R+C)}
and

\Phi^{t_{0}}_{\omega}(x)\in\overline{B(0,R+C)}
.

2.

We claim that
\theta_{t_{0}}\omega\in\Omega_{1,\delta_{1}(v(\epsilon)),v(\epsilon)}
.
Indeed, by (
2.13
), (
2.14
), and the definition of
B_{H}
in Lemma
2.20
, we obtain

\displaystyle B_{t}^{H}\left(P_{t}\left(\vartheta_{t_{0}}\omega^{-},P_{t_{0}}(\omega^{+},\omega^{-})\right)\right)=-\left(\mathcal{D}_{H}P_{t+t_{0}}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right)(-t)

\displaystyle=-\left(\mathcal{D}_{H}P_{t+t_{0}}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right)(-t-t_{0})+\left(\mathcal{D}_{H}P_{t_{0}}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right)(-t_{0})

\displaystyle=B_{t+t_{0}}^{H}\left(P_{t+t_{0}}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right)-B_{t_{0}}^{H}\left(P_{t_{0}}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right).

Since
\omega=(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\in\Omega_{2,\delta_{2}(v(\epsilon)),v(\epsilon)}
,
t+t_{0}\leq 2T
and
h^{v}
is linear, we have

\displaystyle\left|B_{t}^{H}\left(P_{t}\left(\vartheta_{t_{0}}\omega^{-},P_{t_{0}}(\omega^{+},\omega^{-})\right)\right)-h^{v}(t)\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left|B_{t+t_{0}}^{H}\left(P_{t+t_{0}}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right)-h^{v}(t+t_{0})\right|+\left|B_{t_{0}}^{H}\left(P_{t_{0}}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right)-h^{v}(t_{0})\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\delta_{2}(v(\epsilon))\leq\delta_{1}(v(\epsilon)),

which proves that
\theta_{t_{0}}\omega\in\Omega_{1,\delta_{1}(v(\epsilon)),v(\epsilon)}
.

Consequently, together with (
4.32
), we have

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{Leb}}\big(K(T,x,\omega)\big)\leq\mu_{\mathrm{Leb}}\big(K(T,\Phi^{t_{0}}_{\omega}(x),\theta_{t_{0}}\omega)\cap[t_{0},T-t_{0}]\big)<\epsilon.

In particular, this also yields (
4.33
) in the situation where
t_{0}<T
.

Recall that

\Omega_{2,\delta_{2}(v(\epsilon)),v(\epsilon)}\subset\Omega_{1,\delta_{1}(v(\epsilon)),v(\epsilon)}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}\bigl(\Omega_{2,\delta_{2}(v(\epsilon)),v(\epsilon)}\bigr)>0.

Therefore, from (
4.32
) and (
4.33
), the claim holds setting

\tilde{\Omega}_{T,\epsilon}:=\Omega_{2,\delta_{2}(v(\epsilon)),v(\epsilon)}.

Let us now state a useful lemma, which together with Lemma
4.21
, forms the main ingredient of
Step 2)
.

Lemma 4.22

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and assume that
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{2m+1}=T

is a sequence of times such that for every
i\in\{0,1,\dots,m-1\}
and all
t\in[t_{2i+1},t_{2i+2}]
, it holds that

\displaystyle\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x),\ \Phi^{t}_{\omega}(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R)}.

(4.34)

Then we have

\displaystyle\left|\Phi^{T}_{\omega}(x)-\Phi^{T}_{\omega}(y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\exp\!\left(C_{3}^{F}\sum_{i=0}^{m}(t_{2i+1}-t_{2i})-C_{4}^{F}\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}(t_{2i+2}-t_{2i+1})\right)\,\left|x-y\right|.

Proof.

For every
t\in[0,T]
, it follows from Assumption (
2.21
) 2) and (
4.19
) that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left|\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)-\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(y)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\left\langle F(\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x))-F(\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(y)),\,\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)-\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(y)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}-2C_{4}^{F}\left|\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)-\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(y)\right|^{2}&\text{if }\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x),\,\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R)},\\[4.0pt]
2C_{3}^{F}\left|\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)-\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(y)\right|^{2}&\text{always}.\end{cases}

Therefore, for every subinterval
[a,b]\subset[0,T]
, by applying Grönwall’s inequality we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left|\Phi^{b}_{\omega}(x)-\Phi^{b}_{\omega}(y)\right|^{2}=\left|\Phi^{b-a}_{\theta_{a}\omega}\big(\Phi^{a}_{\omega}(x)\big)-\Phi^{b-a}_{\theta_{a}\omega}\big(\Phi^{a}_{\omega}(y)\big)\right|^{2}\\[4.0pt]
&\leq\begin{cases}\exp\!\left(-2C_{4}^{F}(b-a)\right)\,\left|\Phi^{a}_{\omega}(x)-\Phi^{a}_{\omega}(y)\right|^{2}&\text{if }\forall\,t\in[a,b],\ \Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x),\,\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R)},\\[4.0pt]
\exp\!\left(2C_{3}^{F}(b-a)\right)\,\left|\Phi^{a}_{\omega}(x)-\Phi^{a}_{\omega}(y)\right|^{2}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}\end{split}

(4.35)

Based on (
4.34
), we can prove the claim by applying (
4.35
) successively on each subinterval
[t_{i},t_{i+1}]
.
Now we can establish
Step 2)
.

Corollary 4.23

Consider the setting of Lemma
4.21
and let
\omega\in\tilde{\Omega}_{T,\epsilon}
with
2\epsilon<T
. Assume that for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\lvert\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)-\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(y)\rvert\leq C.

(4.36)

Then it holds that

\displaystyle\lvert\Phi^{T}_{\omega}(x)-\Phi^{T}_{\omega}(y)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq C\exp\!\left(2\epsilon(C_{3}^{F}+C_{4}^{F})-TC_{4}^{F}\right).

Proof.

Consider the trajectory
\big(\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)\big)_{0\leq t\leq T}
.
Since
\omega\in\tilde{\Omega}_{T,\epsilon}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R+C)}
is an open set, there exists a partition of the interval
[0,T]
given by
\{t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{2m+1}=T\}

such that

•

\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R+C)}
for all
t\in[t_{2i+1},t_{2i+2}]
and for every
i\in\{0,1,\dots,m-1\}
.

•

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{m}\big(t_{2i+1}-t_{2i}\big)\leq 2\epsilon
.

Therefore, by (
4.36
) since
\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R+C)}
we get that

\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R)}.

In conclusion, for every
i\in\{0,1,\dots,m-1\}
and for all
t\in[t_{2i+1},t_{2i+2}]
, we get that

\displaystyle\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(x),\;\Phi^{t}_{\omega}(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B(0,R)}.

(4.37)

Hence, by Lemma
4.22
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\Phi^{T}_{\omega}(x)-\Phi^{T}_{\omega}(y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\exp\!\left(2\epsilon(C_{3}^{F}+C_{4}^{F})-TC_{4}^{F}\right).

This completes the proof.

We now justify
Step 3)
. To this aim we first state a result based on Kingman’s subadditive ergodic theorem.

Lemma 4.24

Let
\Omega_{1}\subset\tilde{\Omega}
with
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{1})>0
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\chi_{\Omega_{1}}(\theta_{i}\omega)=\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{1})\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

Proof.

Define

S_{n}(\omega):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\chi_{\Omega_{1}}(\theta_{i}\omega).

Then, for every
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
we have

S_{n+m}(\omega)=S_{n}(\omega)+S_{m}(\theta_{n}\omega).

Thus, by the ergodicity of the shift map
\theta
and Kingman’s subadditive ergodic theorem, the following limit

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}S_{n}(\omega)

exists almost surely and is deterministic.
Since
\theta
is measure-preserving, a straightforward application of the dominated convergence theorem shows that this limit equals
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{1})
. This completes the proof.

We are now ready to prove weak synchronization.

Theorem 4.25

For every
\eta>0
,

\mathbf{P}\!\left(\mathcal{R}(\omega^{-})\leq\eta\right)=1,

implying weak synchronization for (
2.17
).

Proof.

Let
0<\epsilon<1
. By Lemma
4.21
, for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a measurable set
\tilde{\Omega}_{m,\epsilon}

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu_{\mathrm{Leb}}\big(K(m,x,\omega)\big)<\epsilon,\qquad\forall\omega\in\tilde{\Omega}_{m,\epsilon}\cap\tilde{\Omega}.

Recall that, by Assumption
4.13
, the set
\tilde{\Omega}
has full measure and is
\theta_{t}
-invariant for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus, by Lemma
4.24
, for almost every
\omega\in\tilde{\Omega}
we can find a (random) sequence
(m_{k})_{k\geq 1}
such that
m_{k}\to\infty
and
\theta_{m_{k}}\omega\in\tilde{\Omega}_{m,\epsilon}\cap\tilde{\Omega}
.
Given (
4.21
), this yields

\mathcal{R}\!\big(P_{m_{k}+s}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\big)\leq C,\qquad\forall s\in[0,m].

Therefore, by Corollary
4.23
and the cocycle property, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\!\left(P_{m_{k}+m}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\exp\!\left(2\epsilon(C_{3}^{F}+C_{4}^{F})-mC_{4}^{F}\right).

(4.38)

For every
\eta>0
, we can choose
m\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large such that

\displaystyle C\exp\Big(2\epsilon(C_{3}^{F}+C_{4}^{F})-mC_{4}^{F}\Big)<\eta.

For this choice of
m
and using the fact that
m_{k}\to\infty
in (
4.38
), we obtain

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{R}\!\left(P_{n}(\omega^{-},\omega^{+})\right)<\eta.

Thus, by Lemma
4.18
and since
\eta
is arbitrary, we conclude that

\mathcal{R}(\omega^{-})=0\quad\mathbf{P}\text{-a.s.}

Therefore weak synchronization holds.

5
Applications

We now show how to verify weak synchronization for the SDE (
2.17
). As already stated, the first step in this direction is given by the negativity of the top Lyapunov exponent. This holds true once the diffusion coefficient
\sigma
is sufficiently large, as established in
[
9
]
.

Definition 5.1

For
\kappa>0
and
\theta\geq 1
, define

T_{\theta,\kappa}:=\left\{\sigma\in GL(d,\mathbb{R})\;\middle|\;\|\sigma\|\geq\kappa,\;\|\sigma\|\,\|\sigma^{-1}\|\geq\theta\right\},

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the standard operator norm induced by the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
GL(d,\mathbb{R})
is the space of invertible
d\times d
matrices.

Proposition 5.2

Consider the SDE (
2.17
) under Assumptions
2.21
, together with the additional requirement that condition 4) is satisfied for every
H\in(0,1)
. Moreover, suppose that Assumption
4.12
holds. Then, for every
\theta>0
, there exists a constant
\kappa_{\theta}>0
such that for each
\sigma\in T_{\theta,\kappa_{\theta}}
, the top Lyapunov exponent
\lambda^{\sigma}_{1}
satisfies

\displaystyle\lambda^{\sigma}_{1}<0.

Proof.

See
[
9
, Theorem 5.30]
.

In particular, this yields the following result.

Corollary 5.3

Let
\lambda^{\sigma}_{1}<0
and assume that the hypotheses of Theorem
3.2
are satisfied. Then weak synchronization holds for (
2.17
).

Proof.

Thanks to Corollary
4.11
and Remark
4.15
, one can verify Assumption
4.13
. The claim follows from Theorem
4.25
.

Example 5.4

We provide an example of a drift term
F
satisfying the conditions of Proposition
5.2
and Corollary
5.3
. To this aim we consider a
C^{2}
-function
F
defined for some
0<R_{1}<R_{2}
as

\displaystyle F(x)=\begin{cases}&x-x|x|^{2},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ |x|<R_{1},\\
&H(x)-x,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ |x|>R_{2},\end{cases}

where

The function

Remark 5.5

From Proposition
5.2
and Corollary
5.3
, it follows that both the drift and diffusion coefficients contribute to synchronization. Furthermore, based on an interplay between drift and diffusion,
there might exist positive Lyapunov exponents, which would imply a chaotic behavior of the system. This question will be pursued in a future work.

Remark 5.6

In
[
27
]
, conditions for weak synchronization by noise for order-preserving random dynamical systems are provided. In particular, for the SDE (
2.17
) in dimension one, weak synchronization holds in the sense that the weak point attractor

is a singleton.
Moreover, we also expect that top Lyapunov exponent is negative in this case.

Appendix A
A measure theoretical result

The following result allows us to construct a measurable selection map which is required for the proof of Corollary
3.3
.

Lemma A.1

Assume that
\mathcal{Z}
is a Polish space endowed with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra, and let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{Z}
be a compact set. For each
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, define the fiber

A(z):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid(y,z)\in A\}.

Then, there exists a Borel-measurable function

K:\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}}(A)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d},

such that
K(z)\in A(z)
for every
z\in\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}}(A)
. Moreover, this map admits a Borel-measurable extension
\tilde{K}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

Note that since
A
is a compact subset of the Polish space
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{Z}
, it is sequentially compact, so
\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}}(A)
is closed. Also, for each
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, the fiber

A(z):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid(y,z)\in A\}

is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We claim that the family of compact sets
\{A(z)\}_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}
is weakly measurable, meaning that for every open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the set

\{z\in\mathcal{Z}\mid A(z)\cap U\neq\emptyset\}=\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}}\big(A\cap(U\times\mathcal{Z})\big)

belongs to
\sigma(\mathcal{Z})
.
To verify this, observe that every open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be written as a countable union
U=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}K_{n}
, where each
K_{n}
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then,

\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}}\big(A\cap(U\times\mathcal{Z})\big)=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}}\big(A\cap(K_{n}\times\mathcal{Z})\big).

Each set
\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}}\big(A\cap(K_{n}\times\mathcal{Z})\big)
is closed in
\mathcal{Z}
, which can be shown using a standard sequential compactness argument: a convergent sequence of points from this projection has a limit that must also lie in this set, due to the compactness of both
A
and
K_{n}
.
Thus, the weak measurability of the family
\{A(z)\}_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}
is established. Moreover, since
\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}}(A)
is closed, we have

\sigma\left(\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}}(A)\right)=\sigma(\mathcal{Z})\cap\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}}(A),

implying that
\sigma\left(\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}}(A)\right)\subseteq\sigma(\mathcal{Z})
.
By the Kuratowski-Ryll-Nardzewski measurable selection theorem
[
1
, Theorem 18.13]
, we can therefore construct a Borel-measurable function

\mathcal{K}:\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}}(A)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}

such that
\mathcal{K}(z)\in A(z)
for every
z\in\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}}(A)
. Furthermore, we may extend this function to a Borel-measurable map
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
by defining

\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(z):=\begin{cases}\mathcal{K}(z),&\text{if }z\in\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}}(A),\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The closedness of
\Pi_{\mathcal{Z}}(A)
ensures that
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
is Borel-measurable.
