A semicontinuous relaxation of Saito’s criterion and freeness as angular minimization
Tomás S. R. Silva

Abstract
We introduce a nonnegative functional on the space of line arrangements in ℙ 2 \mathbb{P}^{2} that vanishes precisely on free arrangements, obtained as a semicontinuous relaxation of Saito’s criterion for freeness. Given an arrangement 𝒜 \mathcal{A} of n n lines with candidate exponents ( d 1 , d 2 ) (d_{1},d_{2}) , we parameterize the spaces of logarithmic derivations of degrees d 1 d_{1} and d 2 d_{2} via the null spaces of the associated derivation matrices and express the Saito determinant as a bilinear map into the space of degree n n polynomials. The functional then admits a natural geometric interpretation: it measures the squared sine of the angle between the image of this bilinear map and the direction of the defining polynomial Q ⁡ ( 𝒜 ) Q(\mathcal{A}) in coefficient space, and equals zero if and only if its image contains the line spanned by Q ⁡ ( 𝒜 ) Q(\mathcal{A}) . This provides a computable measure of how far a given arrangement is from admitting a free basis of logarithmic derivations of the expected degrees. Using this functional as a reward signal, we develop a sequential construction procedure in which lines are added one at a time so as to minimize the angular distance to freeness, implemented via reinforcement learning with an adaptive curriculum over arrangement sizes and exponent types. Our results suggest that semicontinuous relaxation techniques, grounded in the geometry of polynomial coefficient spaces, offer a viable approach to the computational exploration of freeness in the theory of line arrangements. This manuscript is a working paper, and an updated version will be posted later.

Introduction

Hyperplane arrangements lie at a rich intersection of algebraic geometry, combinatorics, and singularity theory. Among the many properties an arrangement may possess,
freeness
occupies a central role. Introduced by Saito
35
, freeness is defined in terms of the module of logarithmic derivations associated with the defining polynomial of the arrangement: an arrangement is free when this module splits as a direct sum of graded components of prescribed degrees. This splitting condition is closely related to the existence of polynomial vector fields satisfying divisibility constraints along the arrangement; see, for instance,
31
;
15
;
44
;
22
.

Despite the simplicity of its definition, freeness remains difficult to characterize, detect, and construct in a systematic way. For arrangements with fixed combinatorics – encoded by the intersection lattice – the set of free realizations forms a Zariski open subset of the corresponding parameter space
45
, but this subset may be empty or proper, and determining which case holds is in general a hard problem. A central open question in this area is Terao’s conjecture
40
, which predicts that freeness of an arrangement is determined solely by its intersection lattice. Even for line arrangements in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, this conjecture remains widely open, and much recent work has focused on clarifying the relation between combinatorial data and freeness-related algebraic invariants such as the module of logarithmic derivations and the minimal degree of Jacobian relations
36
;
14
;
11
;
13
;
29
;
2
;
9
;
26
;
27
;
32
;
33
.

From both the computational and theoretical viewpoints, constructing line arrangements with prescribed algebraic properties is a difficult problem. The configuration space grows rapidly with the number of lines, while freeness is a highly non-generic property: free arrangements appear comparatively sparsely within the full space of configurations. In practice, explicit examples are usually obtained through geometric insight, addition–deletion arguments, or symbolic computations tailored to specific families (see e.g.
11
;
1
;
32
;
23
;
30
), and no general classification is known. On the computational side, large-scale investigations of realizable rank-three matroids have pushed the verification of Terao’s conjecture in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
up to
14
lines
5
, but exhaustive enumeration becomes infeasible as the number of lines grows. A method capable of generating free candidates automatically would therefore provide a genuinely useful new source of examples for the theory.

This is precisely the viewpoint adopted in this paper. Rather than treating freeness as a property to be checked
after
an arrangement has been constructed, we regard it as a semicontinuous quantity that can guide the construction process itself.

The key observation is that Saito’s criterion admits a natural semicontinuous relaxation with a clean geometric interpretation. Given an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
of
n
lines with candidate exponents
(d_{1},d_{2})
, we parameterize the spaces of logarithmic derivations of degrees
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
via the null spaces of the associated
derivation matrices
(whose rows encode the divisibility conditions – i.e.
\alpha_{i}\mid\theta(\alpha_{i})
for a certain derivation
\theta
and for each line
V(\alpha_{i})
), and express the Saito determinant as a bilinear map

T:\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{k_{2}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N},

where
k_{1},k_{2}
are the dimensions of the spaces of logarithmic derivations of degrees
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
and
N=\binom{n+2}{2}
is the dimension of the space of degree
n
polynomials. The defining polynomial
Q(\mathcal{A})
determines a direction in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and Saito’s criterion asks whether this direction lies in the image of
T
. We define a functional
\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{A})
(Definition
1
) that measures the
squared sine of the angle
between the image of
T
and the direction of
Q(\mathcal{A})
in coefficient space:

\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{A})=1-\sup_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\neq 0,\,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\neq 0}\cos^{2}\!\angle\!\bigl(T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}),\;Q(\mathcal{A})\bigr).

This functional is nonnegative, equals zero if and only if
\mathcal{A}
is free with the given exponents, and varies semicontinuously as the arrangement is modified (Propositions
1
and
2
). In contrast with the traditional formulation of Saito’s criterion – which yields a binary answer – or
discrete invariants such as the Bourbaki degree introduced
in
22
, the functional
\mathfrak{S}
gives freeness a
metric character: it measures not just
whether
an arrangement
is free, but
how far
it is from being free, in terms of a
concrete semicontinuous angular distance in the space of homogeneous polynomials.

Building on this relaxation, we develop a computational framework that autonomously explores the space of line arrangements in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
and searches for configurations satisfying the freeness condition. We design a reinforcement learning system (§§
3
and
4
) that sequentially constructs arrangements by selecting lines from a finite candidate pool, in the spirit of addition–deletion constructions. At each step, the agent receives feedback derived from the algebraic and combinatorial structure of the current configuration – including the second Betti number
b_{2}(\mathcal{A})
, the multiplicity vector of the intersection lattice, and the semicontinuous Saito functional
\mathfrak{S}
– thereby steering the search toward arrangements that are more likely to be free. An adaptive curriculum over arrangement sizes and exponent types ensures that the search covers a broad range of combinatorial regimes. Promising candidates produced by the learning process are then subjected to exact verification using Saito’s criterion over
\mathbb{Q}
.

To the best of our knowledge, this constitutes the first application of machine learning to the construction of free line arrangements. In recent years, machine learning techniques have been employed as exploratory tools in several areas of pure mathematics, particularly in settings involving large combinatorial or geometric search spaces

;
19

20
;

From the algebraic-geometric point of view, the relevance of this approach is twofold. First, it provides a systematic mechanism for generating explicit examples of free line arrangements in a setting where no general classification is currently available. Second, the functional
\mathfrak{S}
itself may be of independent interest: it assigns to each arrangement a semicontinuous invariant measuring proximity to freeness, and its behavior across families of arrangements could shed light on structural questions such as Terao’s conjecture – for instance, by comparing the values of
\mathfrak{S}
across different realizations of a fixed intersection lattice.

2
Line arrangements in
\mathbb{P}^{2}

In this section we recall the basic notions on line arrangements, logarithmic derivations, and freeness that will be used throughout the paper. Standard references include
31
;
15
;
44
.

Let
S=\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]
be the homogeneous coordinate ring of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. A
projective line arrangement
in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
is a finite collection

\mathcal{A}=\{L_{1},\dots,L_{n}\}

of
n
distinct projective lines. For each
i
one chooses a linear form
\alpha_{i}\in S_{1}
such that
L_{i}=V(\alpha_{i})
, and one associates to
\mathcal{A}
the reduced
defining polynomial
1
1

1

When no confusion can arise, we write
Q
in place of
Q(\mathcal{A})
.

Q(\mathcal{A})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}.

Thus
\mathcal{A}
may be identified with the reduced divisor
V(Q(\mathcal{A}))\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
. It is often convenient to work in the ambient vector space
\mathbb{C}^{3}
, where
\mathcal{A}
corresponds to a central hyperplane arrangement defined by the same polynomial
Q(\mathcal{A})
. We pass freely between these two viewpoints.

A basic combinatorial invariant of
\mathcal{A}
is its
intersection lattice

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})
, consisting of all nonempty intersections of subsets of lines in
\mathcal{A}
, partially ordered by reverse inclusion. For line arrangements in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, this lattice encodes the line–point incidence structure: which intersection points lie on which lines. If a point
p\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
lies on exactly
m
lines of
\mathcal{A}
, then
p
is called an
m
-fold point
, and its multiplicity is

m_{p}=\#\{L\in\mathcal{A}:p\in L\}.

We write
t_{m}(\mathcal{A})
for the number of
m
-fold points. These numbers satisfy the double-counting identity

\sum_{m\geq 2}\binom{m}{2}\,t_{m}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{p}\binom{m_{p}}{2}=\binom{n}{2},

(1)

since every pair of distinct lines meets in exactly one point.

2.1
Characteristic polynomial and Betti numbers

Associated with the lattice
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})
is the
Möbius function

\mu:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathbb{Z}
, defined recursively by
\mu(\hat{0})=1
and

\mu(X)=-\sum_{Y<X}\mu(Y)

for every
X\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})
with
X\neq\hat{0}
, where
\hat{0}
denotes the ambient space. An explicit calculation gives
\mu(L_{i})=-1
for each line
L_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
, and
\mu(p)=m_{p}-1
for each intersection point
p
.

The
characteristic polynomial
of
\mathcal{A}
is

\chi(\mathcal{A},t)=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})}\mu(X)\,t^{\dim X}.

For a line arrangement of
n
lines in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
(equivalently, a central arrangement of rank
3
in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
), this takes the form

\chi(\mathcal{A},t)=t^{3}-n\,t^{2}+b_{2}(\mathcal{A})\,t-\bigl(b_{2}(\mathcal{A})-n+1\bigr),

(2)

where we introduce the
second Betti number

b_{2}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{p}(m_{p}-1)=\sum_{m\geq 2}(m-1)\,t_{m}(\mathcal{A}).

(3)

One verifies that
\chi(\mathcal{A},1)=0
, and factoring out
(t-1)
gives

\chi(\mathcal{A},t)=(t-1)\!\left(t^{2}-(n-1)\,t+b_{2}(\mathcal{A})-n+1\right).

(4)

The
Poincaré polynomial

\pi(\mathcal{A},t)=(-t)^{3}\,\chi(\mathcal{A},-1/t)
encodes the
Betti numbers of the complement

M(\mathcal{A})=\mathbb{P}^{2}\setminus\bigcup_{L\in\mathcal{A}}L
;
more precisely, writing
b_{i}(\mathcal{A})=\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}H^{i}(M(\mathcal{A});\mathbb{Q})

for the
i
-th Betti number of the topological space
M(\mathcal{A})
,
one has

\pi(\mathcal{A},t)=\sum_{i\geq 0}b_{i}(\mathcal{A})\,t^{i}.

2.2
Logarithmic derivations

Let

{\mathrm{Der}}_{\mathbb{C}}(S)=S\,\partial_{x}\oplus S\,\partial_{y}\oplus S\,\partial_{z}

be the
S
-module of
\mathbb{C}
-derivations of
S
. A homogeneous derivation
\theta=f\,\partial_{x}+g\,\partial_{y}+h\,\partial_{z}
has polynomial degree
d
if
f,g,h\in S_{d}
. The module of
logarithmic derivations
of
\mathcal{A}
is

D(\mathcal{A})=\bigl\{\theta\in{\mathrm{Der}}_{\mathbb{C}}(S):\theta(\alpha_{i})\in S\,\alpha_{i}\text{ for all }i=1,\dots,n\bigr\}.

Equivalently,
D(\mathcal{A})=\{\theta\in{\mathrm{Der}}_{\mathbb{C}}(S):\theta(Q)\in S\,Q\}
. Its elements are the polynomial vector fields tangent to every line of the arrangement.

A distinguished element is the
Euler derivation

\theta_{E}=x\,\partial_{x}+y\,\partial_{y}+z\,\partial_{z},

which belongs to
D(\mathcal{A})
since
\theta_{E}(Q)=n\,Q
by Euler’s formula. For a central arrangement in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
, the module
D(\mathcal{A})
is a graded reflexive
S
-module of rank
3
.

Logarithmic derivations may also be interpreted as syzygies among the partial derivatives of
Q
. Writing
J_{Q}=(Q_{x},Q_{y},Q_{z})\subset S
for the
Jacobian ideal
, a derivation
\theta=f\,\partial_{x}+g\,\partial_{y}+h\,\partial_{z}
belongs to
D(\mathcal{A})
if and only if
fQ_{x}+gQ_{y}+hQ_{z}\in(Q)
. It is easy to see that

D(\mathcal{A})\cong S\,\theta_{E}\oplus\mathrm{syz}(J_{Q}).

Thus freeness, defined below, amounts to finding sufficiently many low-degree polynomial relations among
Q_{x},Q_{y},Q_{z}
.

2.3
Free arrangements and Saito’s criterion

An arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is called
free
if the
S
-module
D(\mathcal{A})
is free. For a free line arrangement in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, one has an isomorphism of graded
S
-modules

D(\mathcal{A})\cong S(-1)\oplus S(-d_{1})\oplus S(-d_{2})

for some positive integers
d_{1}\leq d_{2}
, where the summand
S(-1)
corresponds to the Euler derivation. The pair
(d_{1},d_{2})
is called the set of
exponents
of the arrangement.

A fundamental tool for detecting freeness is the following criterion due to Saito.

Theorem 1

(Saito’s criterion
35
)
.

Let
\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in D(\mathcal{A})
be homogeneous derivations, and write
\theta_{i}=f_{i1}\,\partial_{x}+f_{i2}\,\partial_{y}+f_{i3}\,\partial_{z}
. Form the coefficient matrix

M(\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\theta_{2}):=\begin{pmatrix}f_{01}&f_{02}&f_{03}\\
f_{11}&f_{12}&f_{13}\\
f_{21}&f_{22}&f_{23}\end{pmatrix}.

Then
\mathcal{A}
is free with basis
\{\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\}
if and only if

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{det}\nolimits M(\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=c\,Q

for some
c\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
.

For line arrangements in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, one takes
\theta_{0}=\theta_{E}
. Then
\mathcal{A}
is free with exponents
(d_{1},d_{2})
if and only if there exist homogeneous derivations
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in D(\mathcal{A})
of degrees
d_{1},d_{2}
such that

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{det}\nolimits M(\theta_{E},\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=c\,Q.

(5)

This determinant condition transforms freeness into an explicit polynomial identity, making it particularly suited to both symbolic verification and the semicontinuous relaxation developed in §
4
.

2.4
Terao’s factorization and numerical constraints

A major consequence of freeness is the following factorization result.

Theorem 2

(Terao’s factorization theorem
41
)
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a free arrangement with exponents
(1,d_{1},d_{2})
, then

\chi(\mathcal{A},t)=(t-1)(t-d_{1})(t-d_{2}).

Comparing with (
4
), one obtains the necessary conditions

d_{1}+d_{2}=n-1,\qquad d_{1}\,d_{2}=b_{2}(\mathcal{A})-(n-1).

(6)

Thus
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
are the roots of the quadratic equation
t^{2}-(n-1)\,t+(b_{2}-n+1)=0
, and for integer exponents to exist the discriminant

\Delta(\mathcal{A})=(n-1)^{2}-4\bigl(b_{2}(\mathcal{A})-n+1\bigr)

(7)

must be a non-negative perfect square. This provides an inexpensive arithmetic test: any arrangement for which
\Delta(\mathcal{A})
is negative or not a perfect square cannot be free.

When the discriminant condition is satisfied, we refer to the resulting pair
(d_{1},d_{2})
as the
candidate exponents
of
\mathcal{A}
. Note that the existence of candidate exponents is necessary but far from sufficient for freeness; the full Saito criterion (
5
) must still be verified.

Remark 1
.

Since every singular point of a line arrangement is an ordinary multiple point, the total Tjurina number is
\tau(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{p}(m_{p}-1)^{2}
. The double-counting identity (
1
) gives
\displaystyle{\sum_{p}}m_{p}(m_{p}-1)=n(n-1)
, from which one deduces

\tau(\mathcal{A})=n(n-1)-b_{2}(\mathcal{A}).

Substituting the second relation in (
6
), this may equivalently be written as

\tau(\mathcal{A})=(n-1)^{2}-d_{1}\,d_{2}

whenever candidate exponents exist. This expresses the Tjurina number in terms of the candidate exponents and will be useful for interpreting the combinatorial features in the learning framework.

2.5
Relevance for the computational framework

The constructions recalled in this section provide the two complementary perspectives on freeness that underlie the computational framework developed in §
4
.

On the algebraic side, Saito’s criterion (Theorem
1
) encodes freeness as a polynomial identity involving the coefficients of logarithmic derivations. This identity admits a natural semicontinuous relaxation: rather than requiring exact equality in (
5
), one can measure the distance between the left-hand side and the target polynomial
Q(\mathcal{A})
, obtaining a functional that vanishes precisely on free arrangements.

On the combinatorial side, the intersection lattice provides numerical invariants – the multiplicity vector
(t_{m})_{m\geq 2}
, the second Betti number
b_{2}(\mathcal{A})
, and the discriminant
\Delta(\mathcal{A})
– that are substantially cheaper to compute and that impose necessary conditions for freeness. These quantities serve as auxiliary features guiding the search process before the full algebraic criterion is evaluated.

The reinforcement learning framework combines these two ingredients into a single reward signal used to explore the space of line arrangements in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
.

3
Background on machine learning

In this section we give a concise overview of the machine learning concepts used in this paper, with emphasis on reinforcement learning. Our goal is to fix terminology and explain how the relevant paradigm applies to our setting. For in depth treatments of machine learning, see
28
;
16
;
34
;
39
.

Broadly speaking, machine learning is concerned with algorithms that improve their performance on a task by leveraging data. One typically works with a family of parametrized functions
f_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
and seeks to
learn

\theta
so that
f_{\theta}
captures a pattern present in the data. From a mathematical perspective, this amounts to an optimization problem: one specifies a class of
models

f_{\theta}
, a
loss function

\mathcal{L}(\theta)
measuring performance, and an algorithm for minimizing (or maximizing) that objective over the parameter space.

The nature of the available data determines the learning paradigm. In
supervised learning
, one is given input–output pairs
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}
and trains a model to predict
y
from
x
; this requires a dataset of labeled examples. In
unsupervised learning
, one is given only inputs
\{x_{i}\}
and seeks to discover latent structure (clustering, dimensionality reduction, etc.) without prescribed labels.

Neither paradigm is well suited to the problem of constructing free line arrangements. Supervised learning would require a large dataset of arrangements pre-labeled as free or non-free, which is precisely what we aim to produce. Unsupervised methods can reveal structure in a given dataset, but do not provide a mechanism for generating new examples that satisfy a target property. Instead, the problem is naturally sequential – one builds an arrangement line by line – and the goal is to guide the construction process toward a rare, desirable outcome. This is the setting of
reinforcement learning
.

3.1
Reinforcement learning

Reinforcement learning (RL) studies how an
agent
learns to make sequential decisions by interacting with an
environment
and receiving scalar
rewards
. The mathematical framework is that of a
Markov
decision process
(MDP), specified by a state space
\mathcal{S}
, an action
space
\mathscr{A}
, a transition function
P(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},a_{t})
, and a
reward function
r(s_{t},a_{t})\in\mathbb{R}
. At each step
t
, the agent
observes a state
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
, selects an action
a_{t}\in\mathscr{A}

according to a
policy

\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s)
parameterized by
\theta
,
transitions to
s_{t+1}
, and receives a reward
r_{t}=r(s_{t},a_{t})
. The objective is
to maximize the
expected discounted return

J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\gamma^{t}\,r_{t}\right],

(8)

where
\gamma\in(0,1]
is a discount factor controlling the trade-off between immediate and future rewards, and
T
is the episode length.

A key quantity is the
value function

V^{\pi}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[\sum_{k=0}^{T-t}\gamma^{k}\,r_{t+k}\;\middle|\;s_{t}=s\right],

which estimates the expected return from state
s
under policy
\pi
. Closely related is the
advantage function

A^{\pi}(s,a)=Q^{\pi}(s,a)-V^{\pi}(s),

where
Q^{\pi}(s,a)
is the expected return from taking action
a
in state
s
and following
\pi
thereafter. The advantage measures how much better an action is compared to the average behavior of the current policy, and serves as a variance-reduced signal for updating
\theta
.

Policy gradient methods

A standard approach to maximizing (
8
) is the
policy gradient
method, which updates
\theta
in the direction
\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)
.
This gradient has a clear interpretation: actions with positive advantage (better than average) are reinforced, while those with negative advantage are suppressed. In practice, the advantage is estimated from sampled trajectories rather than computed exactly. A refinement used in this work is
Proximal Policy Optimization
(PPO)
38
, which constrains each update to a trust region around the current policy.

Actor–critic architecture

In an
actor–critic
setup
24
– also used in this work – two functions are learned simultaneously: the
actor

\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s)
, which outputs a distribution over actions, and the
critic

V_{\phi}(s)
, which estimates the value function. The critic’s estimates are used to compute advantages for the policy gradient, while the actor’s trajectories provide data for improving the critic. This decoupled design allows each component to be optimized with its own objective and often leads to more stable training than pure policy gradient methods.

4
The Saito functional and the search framework

In this section we describe the reinforcement learning framework designed to construct free line arrangements in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. The central idea is to translate algebraic conditions for freeness into a semicontinuous numerical signal that can guide a sequential search through the space of arrangements. The main ingredient is a nonnegative functional, constructed from Saito’s criterion, that measures how far a given arrangement is from admitting logarithmic derivations of the expected degrees. This functional is efficiently computable, and vanishes precisely on free arrangements.

4.1
Sequential construction of arrangements

We formulate the construction of a line arrangement as a finite-horizon Markov decision process. An
episode
consists of building an arrangement of
n
lines one line at a time. At each step
t=1,\dots,n
, the agent observes the current partial arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{t}=\{L_{1},\dots,L_{t}\}
and selects the next line
L_{t+1}
from a finite set of candidates. The episode terminates when
n
lines have been placed, and the agent receives rewards at each step reflecting the algebraic and combinatorial quality of
\mathcal{A}_{t}
.

The candidate lines are drawn from a finite pool
\mathcal{P}
of projectively distinct lines with integer coefficients in a fixed range. Specifically, for a coordinate bound
R>0
, we take

\mathcal{P}=\bigl\{[a:b:c]\in\mathbb{P}^{2}:a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z},\;\max(|a|,|b|,|c|)\leq R\bigr\}/{\sim},

where
\sim
identifies proportional triples, and each class is represented by a canonical form. The agent’s action at step
t
is the choice of an index
i\in\{1,\dots,|\mathcal{P}|\}
selecting a line from
\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{t}
.

This setup mirrors addition-type constructions in arrangement theory: one builds an arrangement incrementally, and at each stage the algebraic properties of the current configuration inform the choice of the next line. The key difference is that, rather than relying on human insight or exhaustive enumeration, the search is guided by a learned policy trained to maximize a reward signal derived from the algebraic and combinatorial structure of the arrangement.

4.2
A semicontinuous relaxation of Saito’s criterion

We now describe the construction of the loss function that lies at the heart of the framework. The challenge is that freeness is a discrete algebraic property: either the Saito determinant condition (Theorem
1
) holds or it does not. Our goal is to replace this binary test with a semicontinuous functional that measures the distance to freeness in polynomial coefficient space.

Throughout this subsection, we fix an arrangement
\mathcal{A}=\{L_{1},\dots,L_{n}\}
with defining linear forms
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in S_{1}
and defining polynomial
Q=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\in S_{n}
. We assume that the combinatorial data of
\mathcal{A}
yield candidate exponents
(d_{1},d_{2})
satisfying the necessary conditions of §
2.4
; if no such exponents exist, the arrangement cannot be free and the functional is not evaluated.

4.2.1
Derivation matrices

By Saito’s criterion,
\mathcal{A}
is free with exponents
(d_{1},d_{2})
if and only if there exist homogeneous derivations
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in D(\mathcal{A})
of degrees
d_{1},d_{2}
such that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{det}\nolimits M(\theta_{E},\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=c\,Q
for some
c\neq 0
. We begin by parametrizing the space of logarithmic derivations of a given degree.

A homogeneous derivation of degree
d
has the form

\theta=f\,\partial_{x}+g\,\partial_{y}+h\,\partial_{z},\qquad f,g,h\in S_{d}.

Writing each component in the monomial basis of
S_{d}
, the derivation
\theta
is specified by a coefficient vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N_{d}}
, where
N_{d}=\binom{d+2}{2}
is the dimension of
S_{d}
.

The condition
\theta\in D(\mathcal{A})
requires
\alpha_{i}\mid\theta(\alpha_{i})
for each
i=1,\dots,n
. Since
\alpha_{i}=a_{i}x+b_{i}y+c_{i}z
is linear,
\theta(\alpha_{i})=f\,a_{i}+g\,b_{i}+h\,c_{i}
is a
homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
, and the divisibility condition imposes
linear constraints on
\mathbf{v}
, as follows. For each line
\alpha_{i}
, let

\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{w}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
be a basis for

\ker(\alpha_{i})
, so that every point of
V(\alpha_{i})
has the form

s\,\mathbf{u}_{i}+t\,\mathbf{w}_{i}
with
[s:t]\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
.
Substituting into
\theta(\alpha_{i})
we have:

\theta(\alpha_{i})(s\,\mathbf{u}_{i}+t\,\mathbf{w}_{i})=\sum_{p=0}^{d}c_{p}\,s^{p}\,t^{d-p},

whose coefficients
c_{0},\dots,c_{d}
depend linearly on
\mathbf{v}
. The
divisibility condition
\alpha_{i}\mid\theta(\alpha_{i})
holds if and only if
this binary form vanishes identically, i.e.,
c_{p}=0
for all
0\leq p\leq d
. Each equation
c_{p}=0
is linear in
\mathbf{v}
and defines one row of
the
derivation matrix

M_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n(d+1)\times 3N_{d}}.

The
n
lines contribute
d+1
constraints each, for a total of
n(d+1)
rows.
A vector
\mathbf{v}\in\ker M_{d}
corresponds to a logarithmic derivation

\theta\in D(\mathcal{A})_{d}
of degree
d
.

Remark 2
.

For computational efficiency, we work over
\mathbb{R}
(
float64
arithmetic) rather than exact rational or symbolic computation. Since the defining linear forms have integer coefficients, all entries of
M_{d}
are integers, and the null space computation is numerically stable.

4.2.2
Null space parameterization

We compute full orthonormal bases
V_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N_{d_{1}}\times k_{1}}
and
V_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N_{d_{2}}\times k_{2}}
for the null spaces of
M_{d_{1}}
and
M_{d_{2}}
, respectively, via the singular value decomposition. Here
k_{1}=\dim\ker M_{d_{1}}
and
k_{2}=\dim\ker M_{d_{2}}
are the dimensions of the spaces of logarithmic derivations of degrees
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
. These null spaces can be high-dimensional: for large
n
and moderate exponents, it is not uncommon for
k_{1}
or
k_{2}
to exceed
50
.

Any logarithmic derivation of degree
d_{j}
can now be written as
\theta=V_{j}\,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}
for some parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{j}}
. The columns of
V_{j}
form an orthonormal basis, so
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}
serves as a coordinate vector in the space of degree
d_{j}
logarithmic derivations. This parameterization replaces the high-dimensional ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{3N_{d_{j}}}
with the much smaller parameter space
\mathbb{R}^{k_{j}}
.

4.2.3
The bilinear determinant map

We now express the Saito determinant
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{det}\nolimits M(\theta_{E},\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
as a bilinear function of the null-space parameters. Write
\theta_{1}=(f_{1},g_{1},h_{1})
and
\theta_{2}=(f_{2},g_{2},h_{2})
for the components of the two non-Euler derivations. Expanding the
3\times 3
determinant along the Euler row gives

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{det}\nolimits M(\theta_{E},\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=x\,(g_{1}h_{2}-g_{2}h_{1})-y\,(f_{1}h_{2}-f_{2}h_{1})+z\,(f_{1}g_{2}-f_{2}g_{1}).

(9)

Each cross-term is a bilinear expression in the coefficients of
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
: it is the difference of two products of a degree
d_{1}
polynomial and a degree
d_{2}
polynomial, hence a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d_{1}+d_{2}=n-1
. Multiplication by
x
,
y
, or
z
then raises the degree to
n
, matching the degree of
Q
.

To make this algebraic structure explicit, we precompute the polynomial multiplication maps. Let
\{m_{\beta}\}_{\beta}
denote the monomial basis of
S_{n}
, so that
N_{\mathrm{out}}=\binom{n+2}{2}
is the number of degree
n
monomials. For each pair of monomials
m_{\alpha}\in S_{d_{1}}
and
m_{\gamma}\in S_{d_{2}}
, the product
m_{\alpha}\cdot m_{\gamma}
is a monomial of degree
n-1
, and multiplication by
x
,
y
, or
z
yields a monomial of degree
n
whose index in the basis of
S_{n}
is recorded in a lookup table. Using these tables, one assembles a tensor

T\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{out}}\times k_{1}\times k_{2}}

(10)

such that the coefficient vector of the Saito determinant (
9
), expressed in the monomial basis of
S_{n}
, is given by the bilinear contraction

T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{out}}},

(11)

where
T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})_{\beta}=\sum_{i,j}T_{\beta ij}\,(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1})_{i}\,(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})_{j}
for each output monomial index
\beta
.

The tensor
T
encodes the full algebraic content of the Saito determinant in the null-space coordinates. Once precomputed, evaluating (
11
) for any pair
(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})
reduces to matrix–vector operations, avoiding all symbolic polynomial manipulation.

4.2.4
The Saito functional

We are now in a position to define the loss function. Let
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{out}}}
denote the coefficient vector of the defining polynomial
Q
in the monomial basis of
S_{n}
. By Saito’s criterion, the arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is free with exponents
(d_{1},d_{2})
if and only if there exist
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}}
,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{2}}
, and
c\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
such that

T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})=c\,\mathbf{q}.

(12)

This is a system of
N_{\mathrm{out}}
polynomial equations in
k_{1}+k_{2}+1
unknowns. Rather than solving it exactly, we relax (
12
) into a semicontinuous minimization problem.

Definition 1
.

The
Saito functional
of an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
with candidate
exponents
(d_{1},d_{2})
is

\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{A})=1-\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}}\setminus\{0\}\\
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{2}}\setminus\{0\}\end{subarray}}\frac{\langle T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}),\,\mathbf{q}\rangle^{2}}{\|T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})\|^{2}\;\|\mathbf{q}\|^{2}},

(13)

with the convention that the supremand is
0
whenever

T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})=0
.

In other words,
\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{A})
is the infimum of

\sin^{2}\!\angle(T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}),\mathbf{q})

over all nonzero parameter pairs for which the Saito determinant does not
vanish. The functional admits a transparent geometric interpretation. The
coefficient vector
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{out}}}
determines a
direction in the space of degree-
n
polynomials, and the bilinear map
T

traces out a set of vectors – the achievable Saito determinants – as the
parameters
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}
vary. The functional

\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{A})
measures the squared sine of the smallest angle
between any vector in this image and the direction of
\mathbf{q}
: it
equals
0
when some achievable determinant is proportional to
Q
(i.e., the
arrangement is free), and it approaches
1
when every achievable determinant is
nearly orthogonal to
\mathbf{q}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{out}}}
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a line arrangement with candidate exponents
(d_{1},d_{2})
.
Then:

(i)

\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{A})\geq 0
;

(ii)

\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{A})=0
if and only if

\mathcal{A}
is free with exponents
(d_{1},d_{2})
;

(iii)

\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{A})\leq 1
.

Proof.

Write
\phi(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})=\angle\bigl(T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}),\,\mathbf{q}\bigr)
for the angle between the Saito determinant and the target
polynomial, so that
\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{A})=\inf\sin^{2}\!\phi
over all
nonzero parameter pairs with

T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})\neq 0
.

Items (i) and (iii) are immediate:
\sin^{2}\!\phi\in[0,1]
for every
angle
\phi
, so the infimum lies in
[0,1]
. For item (ii),
\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{A})=0
holds if and only if there exist
nonzero
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}
such that

T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})
is a nonzero scalar multiple
of
\mathbf{q}
, i.e.,

T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})=c\,\mathbf{q}
for some

c\neq 0
. Setting
\theta_{1}=V_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}
and

\theta_{2}=V_{2}\,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}
, this is precisely Saito’s criterion
(Theorem
1
) applied with
\theta_{E}
as the first basis
element.
∎

We now establish that the Saito functional varies semicontinuously with the defining data of a arrangement.

Proposition 2

(Upper semicontinuity)
.

Fix
n\geq 3
and candidate exponents
(d_{1},d_{2})
. Let

\mathcal{U}\subset(\mathbb{P}^{2})^{n}
denote the locally closed subset parameterizing ordered
n
-tuples of distinct lines with
b_{2}=(n-1)+d_{1}d_{2}
and constant null space dimensions
k_{j}=\dim\ker M_{d_{j}}
for
j=1,2
. Then

\mathfrak{S}:\mathcal{U}\to[0,1],\quad\quad\mathcal{A}\mapsto\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{A})

is upper semicontinuous.

Proof.

We write
T_{\mathcal{A}}
and
\mathbf{q}_{\mathcal{A}}
to emphasize the dependence on the arrangement. As shown in
§§
4.2.1
–
4.2.3
, both
T_{\mathcal{A}}
and
\mathbf{q}_{\mathcal{A}}
depend continuously (polynomially) on
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{U}
.

Let
K=S^{k_{1}-1}\times S^{k_{2}-1}
(the product of unitary spheres in
\mathbb{R}^{k_{j}}
) and define

g:\mathcal{U}\times K\to[0,1]
by

g(\mathcal{A},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})=\cos^{2}\!\angle(T_{\mathcal{A}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}),\,\mathbf{q}_{\mathcal{A}})
when

T_{\mathcal{A}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})\neq 0
, and
g=0
otherwise. Since

g
is continuous where
T_{\mathcal{A}}\neq 0
and

g\geq 0
everywhere,
g
is lower semicontinuous on

\mathcal{U}\times K
. As
K
is compact, the supremum

\mathcal{A}\mapsto\sup_{K}g(\mathcal{A},\cdot)
is lower
semicontinuous, and therefore
\mathfrak{S}=1-\sup_{K}g
is upper semicontinuous.
∎

The introduction of
\mathfrak{S}
also allows one to reformulate classical questions about freeness in analytic terms. Recall that for a fixed intersection lattice
\mathcal{L}
, the
realization space

\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{L})\subset(\mathbb{P}^{2})^{n}
is the locally closed subset parameterizing arrangements whose intersection lattice is isomorphic to
\mathcal{L}
. Terao’s conjecture predicts that freeness is constant on
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{L})
. In particular, Terao’s conjecture admits the following restatement.

Conjecture 1

(Terao’s conjecture, functional formulation)
.

For every intersection lattice
\mathcal{L}
of a line arrangement in

\mathbb{P}^{2}
, the zero set

\mathfrak{S}^{-1}(0)\cap\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{L})
is either empty or
equal to
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{L})
.

4.2.5
Optimization by alternating least squares

The functional (
13
) is not jointly convex in
(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2},c)
, due to the bilinear coupling in
T
. However, it admits an efficient approximate minimization by
alternating least squares
(ALS), exploiting the fact that fixing either
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}
or
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}
renders the problem linear.

ALS iteration.
Fix an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
with candidate exponents
(d_{1},d_{2})
, and suppose the tensor
T
and target vector
\mathbf{q}
have been precomputed.

1.

Initialization.
Choose
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{2}}
at random (e.g., from a standard normal distribution).

2.

Step 1: solve for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}
.
With

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}^{(\ell)}
fixed, the map

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\mapsto T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})

is linear. Write

T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})=A_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},

where
A_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{out}}\times k_{1}}
is obtained by
contracting
T
with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}
. The subproblem

\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\,c}\|A_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}-c\,\mathbf{q}\|^{2}

is a homogeneous least squares problem in the augmented variable

\mathbf{w}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},c)\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}+1}
: one seeks
the unit-norm vector minimizing
\|[A_{1}\mid{-}\mathbf{q}]\,\mathbf{w}\|^{2}
,
whose solution is the right singular vector of
[A_{1}\mid{-}\mathbf{q}]

corresponding to its smallest singular value. Since
\mathbf{w}
is determined
only up to scale, we normalize
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}^{(\ell+1)}
to unit
length after each solve.

3.

Step 2: solve for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}
.
With
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}^{(\ell+1)}
fixed, the map
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\mapsto T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})
is again linear. Write
T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})=A_{2}\,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}
and solve the analogous least squares problem for
(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2},c)
.

4.

Iterate.
Repeat Steps 1 and 2 for
\ell=0,1,\dots,L-1
.

5.

Evaluate.
After the final iteration, compute the loss

\mathfrak{S}=1-\frac{\langle T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}),\,\mathbf{q}\rangle^{2}}{\|T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})\|^{2}\,\|\mathbf{q}\|^{2}}.

Since the ALS procedure may converge to a local minimum, we run
R
independent restarts with random initializations and retain the solution achieving the smallest loss. In practice,
L=10
iterations and
R=3
restarts suffice: the loss stabilizes within a few iterations and additional restarts rarely improve the result.

Remark 3
.

Each ALS iteration requires a singular value decomposition of a matrix of size
N_{\mathrm{out}}\times(k_{j}+1)
, where
N_{\mathrm{out}}=\binom{n+2}{2}
. The cost depends primarily on the null space dimensions
k_{1},k_{2}
, which in turn depend on the exponent type: balanced exponents (e.g.,
(9,10)
for
n=20
) yield moderate null spaces, while extreme exponents (e.g.,

(1,n{-}2)
) can produce null spaces of dimension
500
or more. In our implementation, the full evaluation of
\mathfrak{S}
– including construction of the derivation matrices, null space extraction, tensor assembly, and ALS with
L=10
iterations and
R=3
restarts – takes approximately
4
ms for
n=6
,
60
–
100
ms for
n=15
, and
400
–
700
ms for
n=20
on a single CPU core, depending on the exponent type. While not negligible for large
n
, these times remain practical: in a typical training episode of
n=15
lines, the total time spent evaluating
\mathfrak{S}
is under one second.

Remark 4
.

For small
n
(in practice,
n\leq 12
), it is also feasible to verify Saito’s criterion
exactly
using symbolic computation over
\mathbb{Q}
. In the training procedure, we use the functional
\mathfrak{S}
as the primary signal for all
n
, and supplement it with an exact symbolic check at the end of each episode when
n
is small enough. For larger
n
, the symbolic verification is performed only on the most promising candidates identified during exploration, as a post-hoc certification step.

4.3
Combinatorial score

Before evaluating the Saito functional – which requires null space computations and the ALS procedure (see Remark
3
) – one can perform an inexpensive arithmetic check using only the intersection data of the arrangement. Recall from §
2.4
that candidate exponents
(d_{1},d_{2})
exist only if the discriminant

\Delta(\mathcal{A})=(n-1)^{2}-4\bigl(b_{2}(\mathcal{A})-n+1\bigr)

is a non-negative perfect square.

We define a
combinatorial score

\sigma_{\mathrm{comb}}:\{\text{arrangements}\}\to[-1,1]
as follows:

\sigma_{\mathrm{comb}}(\mathcal{A})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\Delta(\mathcal{A})\geq 0\text{ is a perfect square,}\\[4.0pt]
\displaystyle 1-\frac{2\,\delta(\mathcal{A})}{\delta_{\max}}&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
\delta(\mathcal{A})
measures the distance from
\Delta(\mathcal{A})
to the nearest non-negative perfect square and
\delta_{\max}
is a normalizing constant. This score is computable in
O(n^{2})
time from the intersection lattice alone, requires no linear algebra, and serves as an early filter: arrangements with
\sigma_{\mathrm{comb}}
far from
1
cannot be free and need not be evaluated further.

The precise interpolation distinguishes three regimes:
b_{2}<n-1

(no valid product
d_{1}d_{2}
),
\Delta<0
(no real roots), and

\Delta\geq 0
but not a perfect square – each with its own
smooth interpolation toward
-1
; we omit the explicit formulas
for brevity.

4.4
Algebraic score

The combinatorial score and the Saito functional are combined into a
single
algebraic score

\sigma_{\mathrm{alg}}:\{\text{arrangements}\}\to[-1,1]

that provides a unified measure of proximity to freeness.
The score is defined in two tiers:

•

Tier 1
(discriminant proximity, range
[-1,0)
):
when no candidate exponents exist,
\sigma_{\mathrm{alg}}

measures how far the discriminant
\Delta(\mathcal{A})
is from
being a non-negative perfect square, interpolating from
-1

(far from any valid exponent structure) to values approaching

0
(discriminant nearly a perfect square).

•

Tier 2
(Saito proximity, range
[0,1]
):
when candidate exponents
(d_{1},d_{2})
exist,

\sigma_{\mathrm{alg}}(\mathcal{A})=1-\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{A})
,
so that
\sigma_{\mathrm{alg}}=1
if and only if the arrangement
is free.

When the curriculum specifies target exponents
(d_{1},d_{2})

(see §
4.7
), Tier 1 is modified to measure the
normalized distance from the current
b_{2}(\mathcal{A})
to the
target value
b_{2}^{*}=(n-1)+d_{1}d_{2}
, scaled by
b_{2}^{*}
itself
rather than by the maximum possible
b_{2}
. This rescaling ensures
that arrangements targeting large exponent products receive sharper
gradient signal as
b_{2}
approaches its target.

Thus
\sigma_{\mathrm{alg}}(\mathcal{A})=1
if and only if
\mathcal{A}
is free (with the caveat that this equivalence relies on the ALS procedure finding the global minimum), and
\sigma_{\mathrm{alg}}
interpolates smoothly from
-1
(far from any valid exponent structure) through
0
(valid exponents exist but the Saito condition fails badly) to
1
(free). This score constitutes the primary reward signal in the reinforcement learning framework.

4.5
Reward composition

The reward returned to the agent at each step is a weighted sum of the algebraic score with several auxiliary signals designed to shape the search. Let
\mathcal{A}_{t}
denote the partial arrangement at step
t
. The per-step reward is

\displaystyle r_{t}\;=

\displaystyle w_{\mathrm{comb}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{comb}}(\mathcal{A}_{t})

(14)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle w_{\mathrm{alg}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{alg}}(\mathcal{A}_{t})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle w_{\mathrm{feas}}\,\mathbf{1}[\text{candidate exponents exist}]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle w_{b_{2}}\,\sigma_{b_{2}}(\mathcal{A}_{t})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle w_{\mathrm{int}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{int}}(\mathcal{A}_{t})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle w_{\mathrm{pen}}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{pen}}(\mathcal{A}_{t})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle w_{\mathrm{mult}}\,\Delta m_{\geq 3}(\mathcal{A}_{t}),

where:

•

\sigma_{b_{2}}(\mathcal{A}_{t})
is a
trajectory bonus
rewarding steps that move
b_{2}(\mathcal{A}_{t})
toward the target value
b_{2}^{*}=(n-1)+d_{1}d_{2}
required by the candidate exponents;

•

\sigma_{\mathrm{int}}(\mathcal{A}_{t})
is an
interestingness score
favoring arrangements with rich singularity structure (multiple triple and higher-order points), which are more likely to be free;

•

\sigma_{\mathrm{pen}}(\mathcal{A}_{t})
is a
pencil penalty
that discourages near-pencil configurations, in which most lines pass through a single point (such arrangements are either trivially free or uninteresting).

•

\Delta m_{\geq 3}(\mathcal{A}_{t})
is the increase in the
number of points of multiplicity
\geq 3
compared to the previous
step, rewarding the creation of higher-order singularities.

At the terminal step, an additional bonus
w_{\mathrm{free}}\cdot\mathbf{1}[\mathcal{A}_{n}\text{ is free}]
is awarded when the completed arrangement is verified to be free. For large
n
, where exact verification is deferred, this terminal bonus is replaced by a graded reward based on the algebraic score: a substantial bonus is awarded when
\sigma_{\mathrm{alg}}(\mathcal{A}_{n})
exceeds a high threshold (e.g.,
0.95
), with a smaller bonus for moderately high scores.

The weights
w_{\mathrm{comb}},w_{\mathrm{alg}},\dots
are hyperparameters. Their relative magnitudes reflect a hierarchy of signals: the algebraic score and terminal freeness bonus dominate, while the combinatorial and shaping terms provide gradient signal during the early stages of training when the agent has not yet learned to produce arrangements close to being free.

4.6
Model architecture

The policy
\pi_{\theta}
and value function
V_{\phi}
are parameterized by a Transformer-based actor–critic network
42
;
43
;
25
;
6
. The architecture is designed to handle the variable-length, permutation-sensitive structure of line arrangements.

The input to the network at step
t
consists of two components: the current partial arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{t}
and the set of candidate lines available for selection. Each line
[a:b:c]
is embedded into
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{model}}}
by a small feedforward network. A scalar summary token, encoding
17
global features of
\mathcal{A}_{t}
(including the normalized
b_{2}
, discriminant, multiplicity statistics, algebraic score, and candidate exponents), is projected into the same embedding space and prepended to the sequence of line embeddings. This sequence is processed by a Transformer encoder
42
, which captures interactions among the selected lines through self-attention.

To produce action logits, each candidate line queries the encoded arrangement context via a cross-attention mechanism, yielding a scalar score per candidate. These scores are passed through a softmax to obtain the policy distribution
\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
. The critic head extracts the value estimate from the scalar summary token via a feedforward network.

4.7
Training

The network is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
38
. To ensure that the agent encounters a diverse range of combinatorial regimes, we employ an
adaptive curriculum
over arrangement sizes and exponent types. Rather than training on a fixed
n
, each episode samples a triple
(n,d_{1},d_{2})
from a distribution that is updated during training based on the agent’s success rate: under-explored triples and triples with difficult exponent types (in the sense that
d_{1}d_{2}
is large, corresponding to arrangements far from the pencil case) receive higher sampling weight. This prevents the agent from converging to a policy that only finds easy configurations, such as those with small exponents of the form
(1,n-2)
.

Training proceeds in rollouts of a fixed number of environment steps. At each rollout, the agent collects trajectories from parallel environments, computes advantages using Generalized Advantage Estimation
37
, and updates the policy and value networks. Periodic greedy evaluations monitor progress and log any newly discovered free arrangements.

5
Conclusion and future perspectives

In this paper we have introduced a computational framework for constructing free line arrangements in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, based on a semicontinuous relaxation of Saito’s criterion for freeness. The central construction is the Saito functional
\mathfrak{S}(\mathcal{A})
, a nonnegative quantity that vanishes precisely on free arrangements and that measures, in polynomial coefficient space, the angular distance between the best achievable Saito determinant and the defining polynomial of the arrangement. This functional is semicontinuous, scale-invariant, and efficiently computable via alternating least squares over the null spaces of the derivation matrices, with evaluation times on the order of milliseconds even for arrangements of
20
lines.

Building on this semicontinuous signal, we designed a reinforcement learning system that constructs line arrangements sequentially, guided at each step by algebraic and combinatorial feedback derived from the current configuration. The reward signal combines the Saito functional with combinatorial invariants of the intersection lattice, and an adaptive curriculum over arrangement sizes and exponent types ensures that the search covers a broad range of combinatorial regimes. Promising candidates produced by the learned policy are then certified exactly using Saito’s criterion over
\mathbb{Q}
. We believe this work opens several directions for further investigation.

Toward larger arrangements

The Saito loss scales favorably with
n
, but the candidate pool
\mathcal{P}
grows rapidly with the coordinate bound
R
, and the null space dimensions
k_{1},k_{2}
increase with the exponent degrees. Developing more efficient candidate generation strategies – for instance, by exploiting the intersection structure of the current arrangement to propose geometrically motivated lines – could extend the reach of the method to
n\geq 20
.

Exploring Terao’s conjecture

A natural application of the framework is the systematic generation of examples relevant to Terao’s conjecture. By training on a fixed combinatorial type (i.e., a fixed intersection lattice) and varying the metric realization, one could search for pairs of arrangements with the same lattice but different freeness properties – the existence of such a pair would disprove the conjecture. Conversely, if the method consistently finds that freeness is preserved across realizations of a given lattice, this would provide computational evidence in favor of the conjecture. We note that the Saito functional is well suited to this task, as it provides a semicontinuous measure of “how free” each realization is, rather than a binary answer (see Conjecture
1
).

Higher-dimensional arrangements.

The algebraic construction underlying the Saito functional – parameterizing logarithmic derivations via null spaces, expressing the Saito determinant as a multilinear map, and minimizing the angular distance to the defining polynomial – extends in principle to hyperplane arrangements in
\mathbb{P}^{r}
for
r\geq 3
. In this setting, Saito’s criterion involves a
(r{+}1)\times(r{+}1)
determinant that is multilinear in
r
non-Euler derivations, and the resulting tensor
T
has
r
parameter indices rather than two. The ALS procedure generalizes naturally to this multilinear case (cycling over the
r
parameter vectors), though the computational cost and the dimension of the search space grow significantly. The combinatorial score also admits a natural
generalization: for a free arrangement of
n
hyperplanes in
\mathbb{P}^{r}
with exponents
(d_{1},\dots,d_{r})
, Terao’s factorization
theorem gives
\chi(\mathcal{A},t)=\prod_{i=0}^{r}(t-d_{i})
with
d_{0}=1
, and the coefficients of
\chi
are determined by the intersection lattice via the Möbius function. The combinatorial test
thus becomes: does the reduced characteristic polynomial
\chi(\mathcal{A},t)/(t-1)
, which is a degree
r
polynomial with
lattice-determined coefficients, factor completely over
\mathbb{Z}
into
linear factors with nonnegative roots summing
n-1
? For
r=2
this reduces to the discriminant test of §
2.4
; for
r\geq 3
, it remains a finite computation but no longer reduces to a single arithmetic condition. Investigating whether the reinforcement learning approach remains effective in higher dimensions is an interesting open question.

Beyond freeness.

The general strategy of translating a discrete algebraic property into a semicontinuous functional and using it to guide a search may be applicable to other structural properties of arrangements. A natural invariant for distinguishing classes of line arrangements is the
Bourbaki degree
introduced in
22
, which measures the complexity of the module of logarithmic derivations: free arrangements have Bourbaki degree
0
, nearly-free arrangements
12
have Bourbaki degree
1
, and so on. This suggests a natural question:

Question 1
.

Does the Bourbaki degree admit a relaxation analogous to the Saito functional? More precisely, can one define a family of nonnegative functionals
\mathfrak{S}_{k}
, parameterized by
k\geq 0
, such that
\mathfrak{S}_{k}(\mathcal{A})=0
if and only if

\mathcal{A}
has Bourbaki degree at most
k
?

Such a functional would allow the search framework to target specific classes of arrangements beyond the free case. The nearly-free case (
k=1
) is particularly natural in the present context: by results of Dimca and Sticlaru
10
, free and nearly-free arrangements are closely related through addition–deletion of lines: adding or removing a single line from a free arrangement often produces a nearly-free one, and vice versa. Since our reinforcement learning agent builds arrangements line by line, nearly-free configurations are likely to arise as intermediate steps during episodes that target free arrangements. Extending the reward signal to recognize and collect these configurations would require only a modest modification of the framework and could provide a systematic source of examples of nearly-free arrangements.

More broadly, one could define analogous functionals for
plus-one generated
arrangements
4
or divisional freeness
3
, or adapt the approach to search for arrangements satisfying prescribed conditions on their characteristic polynomials, Hilbert functions of Jacobian ideals, or other algebraic invariants.

Theoretical questions.

The Saito functional
\mathfrak{S}
is defined as an infimum over a bilinear optimization problem and computed approximately by ALS. A natural question is whether
\mathfrak{S}
admits a closed-form expression or useful lower bounds in terms of standard invariants of the arrangement. Understanding the landscape of
\mathfrak{S}
– for instance, whether local minima of the ALS procedure can be far from the global minimum, or whether the functional varies semicontinuously with the coefficients of the defining linear forms – would be of independent interest and could inform the design of more efficient optimization strategies.
