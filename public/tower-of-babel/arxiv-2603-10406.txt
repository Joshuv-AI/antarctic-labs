Aldous property for full-flag Johnson graphs
Gary Greaves Haoran Zhu

Abstract
We show that the full-flag Johnson graph has spectral gap equal to that of its Schreier quotient arising from the point-stabiliser equitable partition. Our results confirm two conjectures posed by Huang, Huang, and Cioabă, which imply an Aldous-type spectral-gap phenomenon for full-flag Johnson graphs.



Keywords:
Spectral gap, Johnson graph, Schreier graph, symmetric group, Aldous’ conjecture.

Mathematics Subject Classification:
05C50, 05C25, 20B30, 15A18, 60K35

1
Introduction

The second-largest eigenvalue of a regular graph is widely regarded as a key indicator of its global connectivity and expansion properties
[
3
,
4
,
18
,
28
]
.
In particular, relative to the degree, a smaller second-largest eigenvalue typically reflects stronger connectivity, faster mixing of random walks, and better expansion.

The notion of the
Aldous property
arises naturally at the interface of algebra, graph theory, and probability.
A Cayley graph
\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n},\Sigma)
on the symmetric group
S_{n}
with generating set
\Sigma
is said to have the
Aldous property
if its second-largest eigenvalue coincides with that of the
Schreier graph

\operatorname{Sch}(S_{n},[n],\Sigma)
, that is, the graph with vertex set
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
and an arc from
i
to
j
for each
\sigma\in\Sigma
satisfying
\sigma(i)=j
.

Aldous conjectured
[
1
, Open Problem 14.29]
that
\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n},\Sigma)
has the Aldous property when
\Sigma
is a set of transpositions.
This conjecture stimulated nearly two decades of research, including partial results for random walks on finite groups
[
20
]
, Coxeter graphs
[
8
]
, multipartite graphs
[
12
]
, and certain weighted graphs
[
17
]
, before its full resolution by Caputo, Liggett, and Richthammer
[
10
]
.

The spectral theory of Cayley graphs can be distinguished by normality.
The Cayley graph
\operatorname{Cay}(G,\Sigma)
is said to be
normal
if
\Sigma
is closed under conjugation in
G
, that is, if
\Sigma
is a union of conjugacy classes.
Normal Cayley graphs admit a particularly tractable spectral theory: their eigenvalues can be expressed explicitly in terms of the irreducible characters of the underlying group
[
16
,
30
]
.
In contrast, the spectral analysis of non-normal Cayley graphs is considerably more intricate.
The absence of conjugacy invariance prevents a direct application of character theory, and comparatively few general results are currently available.

Variations and generalisations of Aldous’ conjecture continue to attract attention
[
2
,
5
,
11
,
13
,
21
,
23
,
24
,
26
,
27
,
29
]
.
Various families of Cayley graphs
\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n},\Sigma)
have been shown to possess the Aldous property for suitably structured generating sets
\Sigma
.
In the case of normal Cayley graphs, we refer the reader to
[
24
,
29
]
.
In the case of non-normal Cayley graphs, the Aldous property has been established when
\Sigma
consists of reversals
[
13
]
, prefix-reversals
[
11
]
, and certain subsets of cycles
[
27
]
.
As we show below (see Section
2.1
), in this paper, we are concerned with the (non-normal) case where
\Sigma
consists of
(n-2)
-reducible permutations.

A
full flag
of
[n]
is a chain
U=(U_{1},\dots,U_{n})
of subsets satisfying
|U_{i}|=i
and
U_{i}\subset U_{i+1}
. For integers
n
and
k
with
0<k<n
, the
Johnson graph

J(n,k)
is the graph whose vertices are the
k
-subsets of
[n]
, where two vertices
U
and
V
are adjacent whenever
|U\cap V|=k-1
.

In 2016, Dai
[
14
]
introduced the
full-flag Johnson graph

{{F\!\!J}}(n,k)
, for
0\leq k<n
. Its vertex set consists of all full flags of
[n]
, and two full flags

U=(U_{1},\dots,U_{n})\quad\text{and}\quad V=(V_{1},\dots,V_{n})

are adjacent if and only if
\bigl|\{\,i\in[n]:U_{i}\neq V_{i}\,\}\bigr|=k
.
Equivalently,
U
and
V
, viewed as collections of subsets of
[n]
, are adjacent if and only if they have exactly
n-k
subsets in common; that is,
|U\cap V|=n-k
.

Dai
[
14
]
observed that the permutahedron of order
n
coincides with
{{F\!\!J}}(n,1)
, thereby placing
{{F\!\!J}}(n,k)
as a natural generalisation of permutahedra. In
[
15
]
, Dai calculated all eigenvalues of the quotient graph of
{{F\!\!J}}(n,1)
with respect to a certain equitable partition, and conjectured that
{{F\!\!J}}(n,1)
and its quotient graph share the same second-largest eigenvalue.

Huang, Huang, and Cioabă
[
24
]
observed that Dai’s conjecture is a special case of Aldous’ conjecture and initiated an analogous study of
{{F\!\!J}}(n,2)
and implicitly conjectured that
{{F\!\!J}}(n,2)
has the Aldous property
[
25
, Conjecture 6.9]
.
Our main theorem resolves this conjecture.

Theorem 1.1
.

For each
n\geq 4
, the full-flag Johnson graph
{{F\!\!J}}(n,2)
has the Aldous property.

The statement of Theorem
1.1
implies that
{{F\!\!J}}(n,2)
is a Cayley graph on the symmetric group
S_{n}
, a fact already established by Dai
[
14
]
.
However,
{{F\!\!J}}(n,2)
is a non-normal Cayley graph, and hence character-theoretic methods are not directly applicable for deriving expressions for its eigenvalues
[
27
]
.

Our proof of Theorem
1.1
follows an approach suggested by Huang, Huang, and Cioabă, which we outline in Section
2
. To implement this strategy, we establish two conjectures of Huang, Huang, and Cioabă
[
24
, Conjectures 23 and 24]
concerning recursive inequalities for the second-largest eigenvalue of quotient matrices arising from the point-stabiliser partition of the vertex set. The proof of these inequalities is primarily inductive and relies on several linear-algebraic arguments, viewed naturally from the perspective of Laplacian operators.

The paper is organised as follows.
In Section
2
, we present the full-flag Johnson graph
{{F\!\!J}}(n,2)
as a Cayley graph whose generating set consists of
(n-2)
-reducible permutations.
We then reduce the proof of Theorem
1.1
to establishing certain recursive inequalities (see Theorem
2.4
).
In Section
3
, we prove Theorem
2.4
.

2
Cayley graphs and equitable partitions

2.1
Full-flag Johnson graph as a Cayley graph

Let
G
be a finite group and let
\Sigma\subseteq G
satisfying
1_{G}\notin\Sigma
and
g^{-1}\in\Sigma
for each
g\in\Sigma
.
The
Cayley graph

\Gamma=\mathrm{Cay}(G,\Sigma)
is the undirected graph whose vertex set is
G
and two vertices
g,h\in G
are adjacent whenever
h=\sigma g
for some
\sigma\in\Sigma
.
The graph
\Gamma
is clearly
|\Sigma|
-regular.
Furthermore,
\Gamma
is connected if and only if
\Sigma
generates
G
.

A permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
is called
m
–reducible
if there exists a partition

[n]=I_{1}\cup\cdots\cup I_{m}
into contiguous intervals with
\sigma(I_{j})=I_{j}
for all
j
.
Further, we call
\sigma

maximally
m
-reducible
if
m
is the largest possible integer such that
\sigma
is
m
-reducible.
Define

\mathcal{R}_{n}(k):=\{\sigma\in S_{n}:\sigma\ \text{is maximally }(n-k)\text{–reducible}\}.

Dai
[
14
, Theorem 1]
showed that
{{F\!\!J}}(n,k)\cong\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n},\,\mathcal{R}_{n}(k))
.
The approach we take towards a proof of Theorem
1.1
requires us to consider the subgraph of
\operatorname{Cay}\left(S_{n},\mathcal{R}_{n}(2)\right)
generated by those
(n-2)
–reducible permutations that move the symbol
1
, explicitly,

\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\prime}(2):=\{(1,2,3),(1,3,2),(1,3),(1,2)(3,4),(1,2)(4,5),\ldots,(1,2)(n-1,n)\}.

In fact, in addition to
\operatorname{Cay}\left(S_{n},\mathcal{R}_{n}(2)\right)
, we will also show that
\operatorname{Cay}\left(S_{n},\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2)\right)
has the Aldous property (see Lemma
2.6
).

2.2
Schreier graphs and quotient matrices

Let
\Gamma=(V(\Gamma),E(\Gamma))
be a simple undirected graph on
n
vertices, and denote its adjacency matrix by
A_{\Gamma}
.
A partition
\Pi=\{V_{1},V_{2},\dots,V_{m}\}
of the vertex set
V(\Gamma)
is called an
equitable partition
if, for every pair
i,j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, each vertex in
V_{i}
has the same number
q_{ij}
of neighbours in
V_{j}
.
The matrix
Q_{\Gamma}(\Pi)=(q_{ij})_{m\times m}
is called the
quotient matrix
of
\Gamma
with respect to
\Pi
.

Lemma 2.1

(Godsil and Royle
[
22
]
, Theorem 9.3.3)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph and suppose that
\Pi
is an equitable partition of
\Gamma
.
Then
\det(xI-Q_{\Gamma}(\Pi))
divides
\det(xI-A_{\Gamma})
.

For
k\in[n]
, denote by
\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(k)
the stabiliser subgroup of
S_{n}
that fixes the element
k
.
Define
\Pi_{n}
to be the set of left cosets of
\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(1)
.
Then each part of
\Pi_{n}
is a left coset of the point-stabiliser of
S_{n}
, that is,
\Pi_{n}:=\{V_{1},\dots,V_{n}\}
where
V_{i}=(1i)\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(1)
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

{{F\!\!J}}(4,2)

.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
24
, Lemma 5]
)
.

Let
G
be a finite group.
Then the set of left cosets of any subgroup
H\leq G
gives an equitable partition of
\operatorname{Cay}(G,\Sigma)
.

The Schreier graph
\operatorname{Sch}(S_{n},[n],\Sigma)
is isomorphic to the graph with vertex set
\Pi_{n}=\{V_{1},V_{2},\dots,V_{n}\}
where there is an arc from
V_{i}
to
V_{j}
for each
\sigma\in\Sigma
and
y\in V_{j}
satisfying
\sigma(y)=x
for some fixed
x\in V_{i}
.

It follows from Lemma
2.2
that

\Pi_{n}
is an equitable partition of both the Cayley graphs
\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n},\mathcal{R}_{n}(2))
and
\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n},\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2))
.
Denote the corresponding quotient matrices by
\mathsf{Q}_{n}
and
\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}_{n}
, respectively, i.e,

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}_{n}

\displaystyle:=Q_{\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n},\mathcal{R}_{n}(2))}(\Pi_{n})

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}_{n}

\displaystyle:=Q_{\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n},\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2))}(\Pi_{n}).

It is straightforward to verify that the matrices
\mathsf{Q}_{n}
and
\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}_{n}
are adjacency matrices of the Schreier graphs
\operatorname{Sch}(S_{n},[n],\mathcal{R}_{n}(2))
and
\operatorname{Sch}(S_{n},[n],\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2))
, respectively.

In
[
24
, Section 5]
the corresponding quotient matrices
\mathsf{Q}_{n}
and
\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime}
are given as

\mathsf{Q}_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{n^{2}-n-6}{2}&n-2&2&0&\cdots&0&0\\
n-2&\frac{n^{2}-3n-2}{2}&n-2&2&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
2&n-2&\frac{n^{2}-3n-6}{2}&n-2&\ddots&0&0\\
0&2&n-2&\ddots&\ddots&2&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\frac{n^{2}-3n-6}{2}&n-2&2\\
0&\ldots&0&2&n-2&\frac{n^{2}-3n-2}{2}&n-2\\
0&\ldots&0&0&2&n-2&\frac{n^{2}-n-6}{2}\end{bmatrix}

and

\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}0&n-2&2&0&\cdots&0&0\\
n-2&1&1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
2&1&n-4&1&\ddots&0&0\\
0&0&1&n-2&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&1&0\\
0&0&\ddots&\ddots&1&n-2&1\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0&1&n-1\end{bmatrix}.

We will apply the following remark in Section
3
.

Remark 2.3
.

Using the Gerschgorin theorem and the Perron-Frobenius theorem, it follows that both the matrices
\frac{n^{2}+n-6}{2}I-\mathsf{Q}_{n}
and
nI-\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime}
are positive semidefinite with rank
n-1
.

2.3
Overview of the proof

In this section, we outline and follow the proof strategy proposed by Huang, Huang, and Cioabă
[
24
]
.
Let
M
be an
n\times n
real symmetric matrix.
We write the eigenvalues of
M
in non-increasing order as
\lambda_{1}(M)\geqslant\lambda_{2}(M)\geqslant\dots\geqslant\lambda_{n}(M)
.
To ease notation, for each
i\in[n]
, for a graph
\Gamma
, we define
\lambda_{i}(\Gamma):=\lambda_{i}(A_{\Gamma})
.

Using the previous two subsections, in order to prove Theorem
1.1
, it suffices to show that

\lambda_{2}(\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n},\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2)))=\lambda_{2}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime})\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\lambda_{2}(\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n},\mathcal{R}_{n}(2)))=\lambda_{2}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}).

We now record the following key theorem, which provides recursive inequalities for the second-largest eigenvalue of the quotient matrices
\mathsf{Q}_{n}
and
\mathsf{Q}^{\prime}_{n}
.
We defer its proof until Section
3
.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
24
, Inequality 21 and Inequality 22]
)
.

Let
n\geq 5
. Then

(a)

\lambda_{2}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime})\;>\;\lambda_{2}(\mathsf{Q}_{n-1}^{\prime})+1;

(b)

\lambda_{2}(\mathsf{Q}_{n})\ >\ \lambda_{2}(\mathsf{Q}_{n-1})+\lambda_{2}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime})
.

Huang, Huang, and Cioabă
[
24
]
proposed to use the following lemma, known as the ‘graph covering method’, which bounds eigenvalues of a graph that are not eigenvalues of a quotient matrix of a certain equitable partition.

Given a square matrix
M
, we denote by
\Lambda(M)
the set of its eigenvalues.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
24
, Theorem 7]
)
.

Let
\Gamma=\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n},\Sigma)
.
If
\lambda\in\Lambda(A_{\Gamma})\setminus\Lambda(Q_{\Gamma}(\Pi_{n}))
then,
for each
k\in[n]
,

\lambda\;\leq\;\lambda_{2}\bigl(\operatorname{Cay}(\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(k),\,\Sigma\cap\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(k))\bigr)\;+\;\lambda_{2}\bigl(\operatorname{Cay}\bigl(S_{n},\,\Sigma\setminus(\Sigma\cap\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(k))\bigr)\bigr).

Using Lemma
2.5
together with Theorem
2.4
, we can show that
\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n},\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2))
has the Aldous property.

Lemma 2.6

(
[
24
, Conjecture 24]
)
.

Let
n\geq 4
.
Then

\lambda_{2}(\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n},\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2)))=\lambda_{2}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime}).

Proof.

Let
\Gamma_{n}=\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n},\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2))
.
We decompose the generating set
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2)
according to whether or not a generator
fixes
n
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2)

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2)\cap\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(n))\cup(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2)\setminus\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(n)).

Let
\lambda\in\Lambda(\Gamma_{n})\setminus\Lambda(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime})
.
Apply Lemma
2.5
with

\Sigma=\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2)
and
k=n
to obtain

\lambda\ \leq\ \lambda_{2}\bigl(\operatorname{Cay}\left(\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(n),\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2)\cap\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(n)\right)\bigr)+\lambda_{2}\bigl(\operatorname{Cay}\left(S_{n},\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2)\setminus\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(n)\right)\bigr).

(2.1)

Observe that
\operatorname{Cay}\left(\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(n),\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2)\cap\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(n)\right)=\operatorname{Cay}\left(S_{n-1},\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n-1}(2)\right)=\Gamma_{n-1}
and
\operatorname{Cay}\left(S_{n},\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2)\setminus\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(n)\right)=\frac{n!}{2}K_{2}
.
Hence, (
2.1
) becomes

\lambda\ \leq\ \lambda_{2}\bigl(\Gamma_{n-1}\bigr)+1.

(2.2)

Now we assume that
\lambda_{2}\bigl(\Gamma_{n-1}\bigr)=\lambda_{2}\bigl(\mathsf{Q}_{n-1}^{\prime}\bigr)
.
After applying Theorem
2.4
(a), (
2.2
) becomes

\lambda\ <\ \lambda_{2}\bigl(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime}\bigr).

(2.3)

Combining with Lemma
2.1
, we can deduce
\lambda_{2}(\Gamma_{n})=\lambda_{2}\bigl(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime}\bigr)
by induction, checking the base case
n=4
directly.
∎

Similarly, armed with Lemma
2.6
together with Lemma
2.5
and Theorem
2.4
, we can now state and prove the following lemma, which is equivalent to Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 2.7

(
[
24
, Conjecture 23]
)
.

Let
n\geq 4
.
Then

\lambda_{2}(\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n},\mathcal{R}_{n}(2)))=\lambda_{2}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}).

Proof.

Let
\Gamma_{n}=\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n},\mathcal{R}_{n}(2))
.
We decompose the generating set
\mathcal{R}_{n}(2)
according to whether or not a generator
fixes
1
:

\mathcal{R}_{n}(2)=\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2)\cup\bigl(\mathcal{R}_{n}(2)\setminus\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2)\bigr)=\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2)\cup\bigl(\mathcal{R}_{n}(2)\cap\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(1)\bigr),

Let
\lambda\in\Lambda(\Gamma_{n})\setminus\Lambda(\mathsf{Q}_{n})
.
Apply Lemma
2.5
with
\Sigma=\mathcal{R}_{n}(2)
, and
k=1
,
we obtain

\lambda\ \leq\ \lambda_{2}\bigl(\operatorname{Cay}(\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(1),\mathcal{R}_{n}(2)\cap\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(1))\bigr)+\lambda_{2}\bigl(\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n},\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2))\bigr).

(2.4)

Since
\operatorname{Cay}(\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(1),\mathcal{R}_{n}(2)\cap\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(1))=\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n-1},\mathcal{R}_{n-1}(2))
, (
2.4
) becomes

\lambda\ \leq\ \lambda_{2}\bigl(\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n-1},\mathcal{R}_{n-1}(2))\bigr)+\lambda_{2}\bigl(\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n},\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{n}(2))\bigr).

(2.5)

Assume for some
n\geq 5
, the induction hypothesis

\lambda_{2}\bigl(\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n-1},\mathcal{R}_{n-1}(2))\bigr)=\lambda_{2}\bigl(\mathsf{Q}_{n-1}\bigr).

Together with Lemma
2.6
, (
2.5
) becomes

\lambda\ \leq\ \lambda_{2}\bigl(\mathsf{Q}_{n-1}\bigr)+\lambda_{2}\bigl(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime}\bigr).

Now apply Theorem

(b) to obtain
\lambda<\lambda_{2}\bigl(\mathsf{Q}_{n}\bigr)
.

Now Lemma
2.1
yields
\lambda_{2}\bigl(\operatorname{Cay}(S_{n},\mathcal{R}_{n}(2))\bigr)=\lambda_{2}\bigl(\mathsf{Q}_{n}\bigr)
by induction, verifying the base case
n=4
directly.
∎

3
Two eigenvalue inequalities

In this section, we prove Theorem
2.4
, thus completing the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
We treat each of the two inequalities of Theorem
2.4
separately.
For notational convenience, we denote the eigenvalues of an
n\times n
real symmetric matrix
M
in non-decreasing order as
\mu_{1}(M)\leqslant\mu_{2}(M)\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\mu_{n}(M)
.

Define
\mathbf{1}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to be the all-ones vector, that is, each entry of
\mathbf{1}_{n}
is equal to
1
.
Furthermore, we define the Laplacian operator
\mathfrak{L}(M)
by
\mathfrak{L}(M):=\operatorname{diag}(M\mathbf{1}_{n})-M
.
We note here that
\mathfrak{L}(M)\mathbf{1}_{n}=\mathbf{0}_{n}
, the zero vector.

3.1
The first inequality

In this section, we prove Theorem
2.4
(a), whose statement is equivalent to that of Proposition
3.3
, below.
Note that

\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime})=\begin{bmatrix}n&2-n&-2&0&\cdots&0&0\\
2-n&n-1&-1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
-2&-1&4&-1&\ddots&0&0\\
0&0&-1&2&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&-1&0\\
0&0&\ddots&\ddots&-1&2&-1\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0&-1&1\end{bmatrix}.

We begin with a rough bound on the second-smallest eigenvalue of
\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime})
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
n\geq 6
.
Then

\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime}))\leqslant 2-2\cos\Bigl(\dfrac{2\pi}{n-3}\Bigr).

In particular, for
n\geq 9
one has
\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime}))\leqslant 1
.

Proof.

By deleting the rows and columns of
\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime})
indexed by elements of
\{1,2,3,n\}
, one obtains
2I-A_{P_{n-4}}
, where
P_{n-4}
is the path graph on
n-4
vertices.

Using eigenvalue interlacing
[
19
, Corollary 1]
and
[
9
, Section 1.4.4]
, yields

\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime}))\leq\mu_{2}(2I-A_{P_{n-4}})\ =\ 2-2\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{n-3}\right).\qed

Next, we obtain an upper bound for the difference of the first two entries of an
\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime}))
-eigenvector of
\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime})
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
n\geq 9
and
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}
be a
\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime}))
-eigenvector of
\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime})
.
Then

|v_{2}-v_{1}|\ \leq\ \frac{2\mu_{2}\!\left(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime})\right)\left|\cos\left(\tfrac{(2n-5)\theta}{2}\right)\right|}{n\cos(\tfrac{\theta}{2})}\,|v_{n}|,

for some
\theta\in(0,\pi/3]
.

Proof.

Write
\mu=\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime}))
.
Taking the first two rows of the eigenvalue equation
\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime})\mathbf{v}=\mu\,\mathbf{v}
gives

\begin{cases}(n-\mu)\,v_{1}+(2-n)\,v_{2}&=2\,v_{3},\\[2.0pt]
(2-n)\,v_{1}+(n-1-\mu)\,v_{2}&=v_{3}.\end{cases}

(3.1)

This is a
2\times 2
linear system in
(v_{1},v_{2})
with coefficient matrix

M=\begin{bmatrix}n-\mu&2-n\\
2-n&n-1-\mu\end{bmatrix}.

Its determinant is

\det M=(n-\mu)(n-1-\mu)-(n-2)^{2},

which is nonzero by Lemma
3.1
. Hence, the system (
3.1
) has a unique solution.

Solving (
3.1
) yields

v_{1}=\frac{2(n-1-\mu)-(2-n)}{(n-\mu)(n-1-\mu)-(n-2)^{2}}\,v_{3}=\frac{3n-4-2\mu}{(n-\mu)(n-1-\mu)-(n-2)^{2}}\,v_{3},

v_{2}=\frac{(n-\mu)-2(2-n)}{(n-\mu)(n-1-\mu)-(n-2)^{2}}\,v_{3}=\frac{3n-4-\mu}{(n-\mu)(n-1-\mu)-(n-2)^{2}}\,v_{3}.

Therefore,

v_{2}-v_{1}=\frac{\mu}{(n-\mu)(n-1-\mu)-(n-2)^{2}}\,v_{3}.

For
n\geq 9
, using Lemma
3.1
, one finds that

(n-\mu)(n-1-\mu)-(n-2)^{2}\;>\;\frac{n}{2}.

Whence,

|v_{2}-v_{1}|=\frac{\mu}{(n-\mu)(n-1-\mu)-(n-2)^{2}}\,|v_{3}|\;\leq\;\frac{2}{n}\,\mu\,|v_{3}|.

(3.2)

From the eigenvalue equation
\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime})\mathbf{v}=\mu\,\mathbf{v}
, the rows with indices

i=4,\dots,n-1
give

v_{i+1}=(2-\mu)v_{i}-v_{i-1}.

(3.3)

The last row yields the boundary condition

v_{n-1}=(1-\mu)v_{n}.

(3.4)

Since
n\geq 9
, Lemma
3.1
yields the inequality
0<\mu\leq 1
.
Hence, the characteristic equation
r^{2}-(2-\mu)r+1=0
of (
3.3
) has
roots
\exp(\pm\sqrt{-1}\theta)
for some
\theta\in(0,\pi/3]
that satisfies
2\cos\theta=2-\mu
.
Furthermore, for each
i\geq 3
, we can write

v_{i}=c_{1}\cos((n-i)\theta)+c_{2}\sin((n-i)\theta),

with
c_{1}=v_{n}
and
c_{1}\cos(\theta)+c_{2}\sin(\theta)=(1-\mu)v_{n}
from (
3.4
).
Therefore,

v_{3}=\cos((n-3)\theta)v_{n}+(\cos\theta-1)\frac{\sin((n-3)\theta)}{\sin\theta}v_{n}.

(3.5)

Using standard trigonometric identities, (
3.5
) can be expressed as

v_{3}=\frac{\cos\bigl(\tfrac{(2n-5)\theta}{2}\bigr)}{\cos(\tfrac{\theta}{2})}v_{n}.

Combining with (
3.2
) completes the proof. ∎

Now we are ready to state and prove the main result of this subsection, which is equivalent to Theorem
2.4
(a).

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
n\geqslant 4
. Then

\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n+1}^{\prime}))<\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime})).

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}
be an eigenvector of
\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime})

corresponding to the second-smallest eigenvalue
\mu=\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime}))
.
Let
\mathbf{x}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n},v_{n})^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}-\frac{v_{n}}{n+1}\,\mathbf{1}_{n+1}
.
Since
\mathbf{v}
is orthogonal to
\mathbf{1}_{n}
, we also have
\mathbf{x}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\mathbf{1}_{n+1}=0
.
Hence, using Remark
2.3
, we deduce
\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n+1}^{\prime}))\leqslant\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n+1}^{\prime})\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\mathbf{x}}
.
We can express
\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n+1}^{\prime})
as

\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n+1}^{\prime})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime})&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}+(\mathbf{e}_{1}-\mathbf{e}_{2})(\mathbf{e}_{1}-\mathbf{e}_{2})^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}+(\mathbf{e}_{n}-\mathbf{e}_{n+1})(\mathbf{e}_{n}-\mathbf{e}_{n+1})^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}},

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
denotes the standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
whose
i
th entry is
1
and all other entries equal
0
.
It is straightforward to check that

\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n+1}^{\prime})\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\mathbf{x}}=\frac{\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime}))\,\mathbf{v}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\mathbf{v}+(v_{1}-v_{2})^{2}}{\mathbf{v}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\mathbf{v}+\dfrac{n}{n+1}\,v_{n}^{2}},

It now suffices to prove

(v_{1}-v_{2})^{2}\;\leq\;\frac{n}{n+1}\,\mu\,v_{n}^{2}.

(3.6)

Assume that
n\geq 9
.
By Lemma
3.2
we have

(v_{1}-v_{2})^{2}\ \leq\ \frac{4}{n^{2}}\,\mu^{2}\,\frac{\cos^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{2n-5}{2}\theta\bigr)}{\cos^{2}(\tfrac{\theta}{2})}\,v_{n}^{2}.

By Lemma
3.1
, we have
0\leq\mu\leq 1
.
Thus
0<\theta\leq\pi/3
and hence

(v_{1}-v_{2})^{2}\ \leq\ \frac{4}{n^{2}}\,\mu^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{(\sqrt{3}/2)^{2}}\,v_{n}^{2}=\frac{16}{3n^{2}}\,\mu^{2}\,v_{n}^{2},

which is less than
\frac{n}{n+1}\,\mu\,v_{n}^{2}
when
n\geq 9
.

The remaining cases
4\leq n\leq 8
can be verified directly by computer.
∎

3.2
The second inequality

In this section, we prove Theorem
2.4
(b).
Note that

\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n})=\begin{bmatrix}n&2-n&-2&0&\cdots&0&0\\
2-n&2n-2&2-n&-2&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
-2&2-n&2n&2-n&\ddots&0&0\\
0&-2&2-n&\ddots&\ddots&-2&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&2n&2-n&-2\\
0&\ldots&0&-2&2-n&2n-2&2-n\\
0&\ldots&0&0&-2&2-n&n\end{bmatrix}.

We begin, as in the previous section, with a rough bound on the second-smallest eigenvalue of
\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n})
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
n\geq 4
. Then

\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}))\ \leq\ \frac{12\,(n-2)(n+7)}{n(n^{2}-1)}.

(3.7)

In particular,
\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}))\leq 4
for all
n\geq 5
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be defined by

x_{i}:=i-\frac{n+1}{2},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

Then the vector
\mathbf{x}
is orthogonal to
\mathbf{1}_{n}
and hence

\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}))\ \leq\ \frac{\mathbf{x}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n})\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\mathbf{x}}.

Using the pentadiagonal structure of
\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n})
one checks that, for any
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathbf{y}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n})\mathbf{y}=(n-2)\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(y_{i+1}-y_{i})^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}(y_{i+2}-y_{i})^{2}.

For the specific choice of
\mathbf{x}
above, we have

x_{i+1}-x_{i}=1
and
x_{i+2}-x_{i}=2
, whence,

\mathbf{x}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n})\mathbf{x}=(n-2)(n-1)+2\cdot 4(n-2)=(n-2)(n+7).

It follows that

\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}))\leq\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n})\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\mathbf{x}}=\frac{12\,(n-2)(n+7)}{n(n^{2}-1)},

as required.
∎

Let
A
be a real symmetric matrix.
Denote by
A[i,j]
the
(i,j)
-entry of
A
.
We call
A
a

Robinson matrix

if

\begin{cases}A[i,j]\leq A[i,k]\qquad\text{whenever }j<k<i,\\
A[i,j]\geq A[i,k]\qquad\text{whenever }i<j<k.\end{cases}

Equivalently, for every row/column, the off-diagonal entries of
A
are nonincreasing as the distance to the diagonal increases.

The matrix
A
is called
irreducible
if it cannot be decomposed into a nontrivial direct sum of matrices.
We call a vector
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

non-increasing
if
x_{i}\leq x_{i+1}
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
.
An eigenvector for the second-smallest eigenvalue of
\mathfrak{L}(A)
is called a
Fiedler vector
.

Lemma 3.5

(
[
7
, Theorem 3.2 and Theorem 4.6]
)
.

Let
A
be a
Robinson matrix.
Then

1.

\mathfrak{L}(A)
has a non-increasing Fiedler vector.

2.

If
A
is irreducible with
\min_{i,j}A[i,j]=0
, then
\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(A))>0
and
\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(A))
is simple.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the
exchange matrix

J_{n}
is the permutation
matrix of order
n
with ones on the anti-diagonal and zeros elsewhere.
The exchange matrix satisfies
J_{n}^{2}=I
and
J^{-1}_{n}=J_{n}
.
A real matrix
A
of order
n
is called
centrosymmetric
if
AJ_{n}=J_{n}A
and a vector
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
is called

skew-symmetric
if

J_{n}\mathbf{x}=-\mathbf{x}
(that is,
x_{i}=-x_{n+1-i}
for all
i
).

Lemma 3.6

(
[
6
, Theorem 2(i)]
)
.

Let
A
be a centrosymmetric matrix of order
n
.
Then, for each eigenspace of
A
admits a basis consisting
entirely of vectors
\mathbf{x}
satisfying
J_{n}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}
or
J_{n}\mathbf{x}=-\mathbf{x}
.

Clearly,
\mathsf{Q}_{n}
is an irreducible, symmetric Robinson matrix.
Furthermore,
\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n})
is centrosymmetric for
n\geq 4
.
Thus, applying Lemma
3.5
and Lemma
3.6
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
n\geq 4
.
Then
\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n})
has a Fiedler vector that is non-increasing and skew-symmetric and
\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}))
is a simple eigenvalue.

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section, from which the proof of Theorem
2.4
(b) follows.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
n\geqslant 4
. Then

\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n+1}))<\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n})).

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}
be a unit eigenvector of
\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n})
corresponding to the second-smallest eigenvalue
\mu=\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}))
.
Let
\mathbf{x}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n},v_{n})^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}-\frac{v_{n}}{n+1}\,\mathbf{1}_{n+1}
.
Since
\mathbf{v}
is orthogonal to
\mathbf{1}_{n}
, we also have
\mathbf{x}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\mathbf{1}_{n+1}=0
.
Hence, using Remark
2.3
, we deduce
\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n+1}))\leqslant\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n+1})\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\mathbf{x}}
.
We can express
\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n+1})
as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n})&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{i+1})(\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{i+1})^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}

\displaystyle\quad+(n-1)(\mathbf{e}_{n}-\mathbf{e}_{n+1})(\mathbf{e}_{n}-\mathbf{e}_{n+1})^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}+2(\mathbf{e}_{n-1}-\mathbf{e}_{n+1})(\mathbf{e}_{n-1}-\mathbf{e}_{n+1})^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}},

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
denotes the standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
whose
i
th entry is
1
and all other entries equal
0
.
It is straightforward to check that

\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n+1})\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\mathbf{x}}=\frac{\mu+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(v_{i}-v_{i+1})^{2}+2(v_{n-1}-v_{n})^{2}}{1+\dfrac{n}{n+1}\,v_{n}^{2}},

whence,

\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n+1}))\leq\frac{\mu+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(v_{i}-v_{i+1})^{2}+2(v_{n-1}-v_{n})^{2}}{1+\dfrac{n}{n+1}\,v_{n}^{2}}.

For
n\geq 15
, it therefore remains to prove

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(v_{i}-v_{i+1})^{2}+2(v_{n-1}-v_{n})^{2}\;<\;\frac{n}{n+1}\,\mu\,v_{n}^{2}.

(3.8)

Set
d_{i}:=v_{i+1}-v_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
.
From the pentadiagonal form of
\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n})
it follows (as in the proof of Lemma
3.4
) that

\mu=\mathbf{v}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n})\mathbf{v}=(n-2)\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}d_{i}^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}(d_{i}+d_{i+1})^{2}.

Expanding the second sum yields

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=(n-2)\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}d_{i}^{2}+2\left(2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}d_{i}^{2}-(d_{1}^{2}+d_{n-1}^{2})+2\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}d_{i}d_{i+1}\right)

\displaystyle=(n+2)\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}d_{i}^{2}-2(d_{1}^{2}+d_{n-1}^{2})+4\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}d_{i}d_{i+1}.

By Corollary
3.7
, the Fiedler vector
\mathbf{v}
can be chosen to be
non-increasing and skew-symmetric.
Therefore
d_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
, and hence

\mu\ \geq\ (n+2)\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}d_{i}^{2}-2(d_{1}^{2}+d_{n-1}^{2}).

(3.9)

Skew-symmetry gives
v_{1}=-v_{n}
and
v_{2}=-v_{n-1}
, so

d_{1}=v_{2}-v_{1}=-v_{n-1}+v_{n}=v_{n}-v_{n-1}=d_{n-1},

and hence
d_{1}^{2}=d_{n-1}^{2}=(v_{n}-v_{n-1})^{2}
.
Substituting into (
3.9
) yields

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(v_{i+1}-v_{i})^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}d_{i}^{2}\ \leq\ \frac{\mu+4(v_{n}-v_{n-1})^{2}}{n+2}.

We rewrite this inequality as

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(v_{i}-v_{i+1})^{2}+2(v_{n-1}-v_{n})^{2}\leq\frac{\mu}{n+2}+2\!\left(1+\frac{2}{n+2}\right)\!(v_{n-1}-v_{n})^{2}.

(3.10)

From the last row of the eigenvalue equation
\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n})\mathbf{v}=\mu\mathbf{v}
we obtain

(n-2)(v_{n}-v_{n-1})+2(v_{n}-v_{n-2})=\mu v_{n}.

Since
\mathbf{v}
is non-increasing (i.e.,
v_{n-2}\leq v_{n}
) we obtain
(n-2)(v_{n}-v_{n-1})\;\leq\;\mu v_{n}
.

Substituting this into (
3.10
) yields

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(v_{i}-v_{i+1})^{2}+2(v_{n-1}-v_{n})^{2}\;\leq\;\frac{\mu}{n+2}+\frac{2(n+4)\mu^{2}}{(n+2)(n-2)^{2}}\,v_{n}^{2},

Comparing the right-hand side of the above to that of (
3.8
), it thus suffices to show

v_{n}^{2}\left(\frac{n(n+2)}{n+1}-\frac{2(n+4)\mu}{(n-2)^{2}}\right)>1.

Since
\mathbf{v}
is a non-increasing unit vector,
v_{n}^{2}\geq 1/n
, moreover,

v_{n}^{2}\left(\frac{n(n+2)}{n+1}-\frac{2(n+4)\mu}{(n-2)^{2}}\right)\geq\frac{(n+2)}{n+1}-\frac{2(n+4)\mu}{n(n-2)^{2}}.

By Lemma
3.4
we have
\mu\leq 4
for all
n\geq 5
.
Hence,

\frac{(n+2)}{n+1}-\frac{2(n+4)\mu}{n(n-2)^{2}}\geq\frac{(n+2)}{n+1}-\frac{8(n+4)}{n(n-2)^{2}},

which is more than
1
for
n\geq 15
.

For the remaining cases
4\leq n\leq 14
, one can directly verify the strict inequality

\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n+1}))<\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}))
using a computer.
∎

Finally, we can complete the proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Proof of Theorem
2.4
(b).

First, observe that

\lambda_{2}(\mathsf{Q}_{n})\;=\;\frac{n^{2}+n-6}{2}-\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n})),

and

\lambda_{2}(\mathsf{Q}_{n-1})\;=\;\frac{n^{2}-n-6}{2}-\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n-1})).

Clearly, the Schreier graph with adjacency matrix
\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime}
is
n
-regular and connected.
Hence,

\lambda_{1}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime})=n\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{2}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime})<n.

By Proposition
3.8
, we have

\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n-1}))-\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}))>0
and thus

\lambda_{2}(\mathsf{Q}_{n})-\bigl(\lambda_{2}(\mathsf{Q}_{n-1})+\lambda_{2}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime})\bigr)\;=\;\bigl(\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n-1}))-\mu_{2}(\mathfrak{L}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}))\bigr)+\bigl(n-\lambda_{2}(\mathsf{Q}_{n}^{\prime})\bigr)\;>\;0,

as required.
∎
