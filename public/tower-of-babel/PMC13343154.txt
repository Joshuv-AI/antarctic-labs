Development of a rapid recombinase polymerase amplification assay for avian polyomaviruses
Authors: Jiaqian Rao, Qian Wang, Mingji Li, Runlin Shao, Ruiheng Liu, Zhanxin Wang, Yang Liu, Jiajia Liu, Yuanjia Liu, Qingmei Xie, Xinheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Budgerigar fledgling disease, caused by avian polyomavirus (APV), is an immunosuppressive condition in budgerigars that typically manifests as abdominal swelling, absence of down feathers on the dorsal and ventral regions, subdermal bleeding in nestlings, and high mortality. A new APV detection relied on real-time quantitative recombinase polymerase amplification (real-time RPA) targeting the large T antigen gene using specific probe and primers. Detection can be accomplished within 25 minutes at 41°C, with this detection threshold of 2.56 × 102 copies/μL. The real-time RPA assay specific detected APV and did not show cross-reactivity with avian infectious bronchitis virus (IBV), psittacine beak and feather disease virus (PBFDV), newcastle disease virus (NDV) and H9N2 avian influenza virus(H9N2). Intra-assay repeatability testing showed that the coefficients of variations (CVs) were all below 16%, while inter-assay repeatability testing showed that the CVs were all below 12%. In 63 parrot cases suspected of APV-associated mortality, tissue specimens were examined using real-time RPA alongside qPCR assays. Both detection methods showed identical positive rates, indicating complete agreement in positive/negative classification (κ coefficient = 1). Taken together, a real-time RPA method for APV screening was established in this study.

Introduction

Owing to its wide host spectrum, budgerigar fledgling disease virus (BFDV) is commonly referred to as avian polyomavirus (APV) and contributes to the development of budgerigar fledgling disease (BFD), a highly pathogenic infectious condition in psittacine birds (Khatoon et al., 2024). APV is a polyomavirus that infects non-mammalian hosts and is classified as a distinct avian polyomavirus subgenus within Polyomavirus. APV possesses an icosahedral capsid and lacks an envelope, enclosing a circular double-stranded DNA genome with functionally segregated into early and late transcriptional regions (Kim et al., 2022). Encoded within the early region of the genome, the small and large T antigens function as non-structural proteins involved in viral replication and regulation (Hu et al., 2021), while the structural proteins VP1–VP4 and the truncated form VP4-delta are encoded by the late genomic region (Li et al., 2009). As a highly conserved multifunctional protein, the large T antigen serves multiple critical functions: regulating viral replication and transcription, facilitating virion assembly, and inducing cellular transformation (Ogawa et al., 2005; Li et al., 2009)

Psittacine birds across all age categories are considered susceptible to APV infection. The disease represents a major cause of immunosuppression in psittacine birds (Hu et al., 2021). Infected nestlings can show severe clinical signs, including abdominal swelling, feathers growing slowly, crop stasis, severe hepatitis, pericardial effusion, ascites and sudden death, with mortality reaching complete levels (Bernier et al., 1981; Davis et al., 1981; Kim et al., 2022). In contrast, infected adult parrots are frequently asymptomatic or with mild symptoms, such as feather abnormalities and beak irregularities, and although these adult parrots show no clinical signs, these birds may be the source of infection as evidenced by the detection of viral nucleic acids in their blood and faeces (Phalen et al., 1993). Furthermore, it has been shown that infected hosts can shed virus for up to six months (Ko et al., 2024). Infections with APV have been observed in multiple psittacine bird species across nearly all continents, with reports from numerous countries, including China (Hu et al., 2021), Japan (Ogawa et al., 2006), Korea (Kim et al., 2022), Turkey (Adiguzel et al., 2020), Australia (Hulbert et al., 2015), USA (Davis et al., 1981), Germany (Rahaus and Wolff, 2005), Italy (Bert et al., 2005), Czech Republic (Valastanova et al., 2021) and Chile (Gonzalez-Hein et al., 2019). Studies have shown that APV can infect not only parrots, but also other birds that have been reported including: pigeons (Li et al., 2019), hawks (Johne and Muller, 1998) and chickens (Stoll et al., 1993). APV can be transmitted both horizontally through feathers, epithelial cells and faeces (Hulbert et al., 2015), and vertically through infected birds to embryos (Phalen et al., 1991; Harkins et al., 2014). Therefore, the implementation of strengthened biosecurity practices, together with prompt isolation of infected parrots, is essential for limiting disease transmission.

Parrots hold a prominent position in the pet industry due to their aesthetic appeal, advanced cognitive abilities, and exceptional capacity for vocal imitation, including human speech (Olah et al., 2016). And because of APV’s wide range, various ways of transmission and significant impact on parrot health, timely testing and long-term monitoring of APV is particularly important. Currently the common methods for detecting APV are real-time quantitative PCR(qPCR) (Ma et al., 2019), ELISA (Khan et al., 2000) and PCR (Phalen et al., 1991), as these methods have extremely high sensitivity and specificity and can satisfy the performance criteria of the majority of laboratory diagnostic assays. However, these approaches rely on bulky or expensive equipments, are time-consuming, and require inspectors to possess advanced professional training. First described by Piepenburg (Piepenburg et al., 2006), recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) is an innovative isothermal nucleic acid amplification approach, through which proteins responsible for DNA replication, recombination, and repair work in a coordinated manner. The technology consists of two main enzymes, the recombinase T4 UvsX and DNA polymerase I from Bacillus subtilis. This approach is performed under isothermal conditions at 37-43°C, which reduces the time-consuming thermal cycling step and greatly shortens the detection time, allowing the detection to be completed in less than 30 minutes.

Some studies have combined RPA with a variety of other methods, including lateral flow strip–based assays for visual detection, a method that combines RPA with clusters of regularly spaced short palindromic repeats of sequence-associated proteins, and real-time quantification of RPA. Among them, real-time quantitative RPA (real-time RPA), analogous to real-time quantitative PCR, has probe method and dye method. The dye method has the disadvantage of being prone to false positivity (Lobato and O'Sullivan, 2018; Tan et al., 2022) while the detection method developed by combining exo probe and RPA demonstrates many advantages. It not only features constant temperature and rapid detection, but also enables real-time monitoring, facilitating nucleic acid quantification, and at the same time reduces the occurrence of false positives caused by primer dimers. The detection results can be presented by portable and rechargeable fluorescence detectors, which are particularly suitable for situations where expensive instruments are lacking and rapid on-site detection is required. Several studies have incorporated fluorescent labeling into portable lab-in-a-suitcase diagnostic platforms, thereby enabling on-site testing in field settings (Abd El Wahed et al., 2015; Mondal et al., 2016). High analytical sensitivity and specificity, together with a short assay duration and capacity to operate at low-temperature and constant-temperature conditions, underpin the widespread RPA amplification in microbial pathogen detection (Tan et al., 2022).

An RPA-based assay targeting the large T antigen gene of avian polyomavirus (APV) was established in the present study. The method demonstrated excellent performance in terms of sensitivity, specificity, and repeatability, as well as in clinical application. It achieved a detection rate comparable to that of qPCR (Ma et al., 2019) in clinical testing and can serve as an alternative to qPCR.

Method and materials

Viruses and clinical samples

Abnormal feathers were collected from parrots suspected of APV-associated mortality (n = 63) at a parrot breeding base in Guangdong Province and were used for subsequent experiments in this study. Animal tissues were sufficient chopped with bioclean scissors and then added to PBS solution. Tissue samples were processed for total DNA extraction using the FastPure Viral DNA/RNA Mini Kit (Vazyme, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The nucleic acids of Avian Infectious Bronchitis Virus (IBV), psittacine beak and feather disease virus (PBFDV), Newcastle Disease Virus (NDV) and H9N2 subtypes of Avian Influenza Viruses (H9N2) were stored in our laboratory at −80°C for subsequent analyses.

Development of primers and probe for real-time RPA

The APV genome sequences were obtained from NCBI and homology analysis was performed by MEGA 12. The results showed that the large T antigen gene exhibited a high degree of sequence conservation. Construction of standard plasmids

APV-positive nucleic acid was preserved in our laboratory. PCR amplification using primers specific to the large T gene (forward, 5′-CGAACAGAACCATGAACTGA-3′; reverse, 5′-CACAACGCCTCACTATTTCT-3′) yielded a 448-bp product, the sequence of which corresponded to the APV reference strain (GenBank sequence number No. AF241168). The pMD-19T vector (TaKaRa) was used to ligate purified amplicons, which were then introduced into E. coli DH5α competent cells via transformation. For sequence verification, the recombinant plasmid was sent to Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). After confirming the sequence, we quantified the plasmid concentration using the appropriate formula and calculated the plasmid copy number accordingly. The plasmid concentration corresponded to an estimated copy number of approximately 2.56 × 1010 copies/μL.

The real-time RPA system was established

The protocol was established in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions for the TwistAmp® RT exo kit (TwistDx Inc., Cambridge, UK). The real-time RPA reaction mixture was prepared with the following components: 29.5 µL of rehydration buffer, one tube of lyophilized RPA reaction pellet, 1.2 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 0.6 µL of probe (10 µM), 13 µL of nuclease-free water, 2.5 µL of magnesium acetate, and 2 µL of RNA template. Reactions were maintained at 37-43°C for 21 min in a real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument (TianLong, China), and fluorescence was monitored every 30 seconds. At the conclusion of the reaction, samples were defined as positive when the CT value was ≤38 accompanied by a clear amplification curve. In contrast, negative results were assigned to samples showing a Ct value exceeded 38 and lacking a distinct amplification curve.

Selection of the best primer pair

Combine the 3 forward primers with the 3 reverse primers in pairs (a total of 9 different primer pairs) for the RPA reaction, in order to select the primer pair with the best amplification effect.

Optimal reaction temperature of RPA

In order to determine the optimal reaction temperature, the RPA reaction was carried out at 37, 39, 41, and 43 °C respectively. Each temperature was tested three times.

Sensitivity

To assess the sensitivity, we prepared a series of tenfold dilutions of the standard plasmid in ddH2O corresponding to concentrations of 101, 102, 103, 104 105, 106, and 107copies/μL as positive control, alongside a ddH2O as negative control. Each sample was tested three times.

Specificity

Nucleic acids from the standard plasmid and ddH₂O were included as controls, respectively, representing positive and negative groups, while samples from IBV, H9N2, PBFDV, and NDV were incorporated to examine cross-reactivity. Each sample was tested three times.

Repeatability

Inter‑assay repeatability and intra‑assay repeatability were evaluated by testing standard plasmids at concentrations of 10⁴ to 10² copies/µL, incorporating ddH₂O as the negative control. Five independent replicates were performed for intra‑assay repeatability. The intra‑assay repeatability experiment was conducted three times to evaluate the inter-assay repeatability.

Clinical testing

Sixty-three heart and feather samples from parrots suspected of APV infection in our laboratory were employed to evaluate the clinical applicability of the real-time RPA approach. To validate the performance of the established real-time RPA assay, the same specimens were analyzed using both qPCR (Ma et al., 2019) and real-time RPA, and agreement between the two methods was assessed.

Discussion

The presence of APV transmission on almost every continents indicates that it poses a significant challenge to both wild and captive parrot populations, is a worldwide problem, and poses a significant problem for the pet parrot industry. However, increased worldwide demand for captive avian species has facilitated the growth of international trade networks, thereby substantially contributing to the spread of APV between captive and free-ranging avian populations (Olah et al., 2016). Therefore, the control and prevention of APV is critical. Infected young birds usually show severe clinical symptoms or even die suddenly, which seriously affects their health (Bernier et al., 1981; Davis et al., 1981; Kim et al., 2022). Adult parrots often show persistent subclinical symptoms after being infected with APV. They may transmit APV to their chicks and mates through horizontal transmission such as feces and feather shedding. Within the environment, the virus may also act as a reservoir for contamination (Phalen et al., 1993; Ko et al., 2024).

Although vaccine studies have demonstrated that inactivated APV vaccines exhibit protective efficacy, there has been a lack of follow-up observations on immunized parrots to examine sustained vaccine-induced immunity (Wang et al., 2022). Furthermore, the production of inactivated vaccine, which is mainly prepared by culturing primary chicken embryo fibroblasts, is labor-intensive and costly, with substantial technical demands (Ritchie et al., 1998; Wang et al., 2022). Parrots are also susceptible to stress and exhibit high levels of aggression, which makes subcutaneous injections difficult. Unfortunately, APV exhibits a broad host range and diverse transmission routes. Notably, the mortality rate of infected nestlings can reach up to 100%, while adult parrot, though non-fatal after infection, may remain persistent virus shedders and serve as reservoirs of infection. Therefore, timely diagnosis of whether a parrot is or has been infected with APV is essential. Existing approaches for APV detection include qPCR (Ma et al., 2019), ELISA (Khan et al., 2000) and PCR (Phalen et al., 1991), which have been widely used in laboratories at present. However, these methods involve intricate procedures, rely on costly equipment, and necessitate operation by highly trained laboratory personnel. Consequently, establishing a rapid and timely method for APV detection is essential.

The amplification mechanism of RPA relies on a recombination-based process derived from homologous recombination, which is essential for DNA metabolism (Piepenburg et al., 2006). RPA eliminates repetitive heating and cooling steps, and amplification can be performed at a single temperature, which not only shortens the amplification time, but also reduces the need for expensive equipment. The high amplification efficiency, short detection time and ability to function at low and constant temperatures make RPA ideal for clinical diagnostics, epidemic management, food safety testing, and field-based real-time monitoring (Cherkaoui et al., 2021; Mota et al., 2022; Tan et al., 2022). At present, a large number of studies have been combined RPA with various assay reading systems to enhance the simplicity and efficiency of using the RPA amplification system, while also making the observation of the assay results more accurate, rapid and easy to observe (Crannell et al., 2016; Li et al., 2018; Kissenkotter et al., 2020). By integrating RPA with real-time fluorescence, detection is achievable continuously while minimizing crossover risk compared with RPA-LFS. The use of an exo probe further reduces false positives arising from primer dimer formation, compared to conventional fluorescence dyes (Tan et al., 2022). The real-time RPA method has strong sensitivity coupled with fast detection efficiency, completing the assay in less than 30 minutes, and can be performed by hand-held laboratories or laboratory instruments, adapting it to a wide range of assay environments. Therefore, real-time RPA with RPA combined with exo probe is a good choice for the development of new APV assays.

In the present work, we effectively a rapid and user-friendly real-time RPA assay was established as a diagnostic tool for APV. Conserved sequences within the large T gene were used to guide primer-probe design. Sixteen primer pairs were tested to determine the most effective combination for assay performance. Among these, the F1/R1 primer pair demonstrated the strongest gene amplification signal, indicating its suitability for the assay. Accordingly, selection of the F1/R1 primer pair was based on its optimal performance in real-time RPA. Only APV nucleic acids were successfully amplified, whereas PBFDV and other avian-susceptible viruses (H9N2, NDV, and IBV) yielded no detectable amplification, confirming the high specificity of the assay. Sensitivity analysis showed that the platform reliably detected 10 copies/μL, indicating suitability for quantitative detection applications. However, several limitations are associated with RPA, such as complex primer and probe design, limited discrimination between amplification products and primer dimers in dye-based assays, and the difficulty of controlling its rapid amplification speed. In view of the significant advantages and some limitations of RPA, it is possible to combine RPA with various result reading systems in order to exploit the advantages and overcome the limitations. As this method becomes increasingly mature and is combined with various result reading systems, many problems are likely to be improved and it will become as widely used as PCR technology (Lobato and O'Sullivan, 2018; Mota et al., 2022).

Although certain limitations exist in RPA, these are expected to be addressed as the technique continues to mature. Overall, this real-time RPA approach for APV detection was successfully established, yielding results within 30 minutes. The assay demonstrates excellent sensitivity, specificity, and ease of use, providing a solid foundation for the efficient screening of APV.
