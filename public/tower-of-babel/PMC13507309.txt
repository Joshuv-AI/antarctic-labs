Risk factors associated with gestational diabetes mellitus in Ghana: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Joyce Evelyn Ansah, Daniel Apaak, Thomas Hormenu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is increasing globally, and in Africa. It has become a public health phenomenon with associated risk factors contributing to adverse maternal and birth outcomes, thus increasing economic burden on the health care system. The purpose of the study was to investigate the incidence of GDM and the risk factors that contribute to its occurrence among pregnant women visiting antenatal clinics (ANC) in some selected hospitals in Ghana. A prospective cohort design was used. The study sampled 1,577 pregnant women using convenience sampling. Data from the respondents were gathered using an adapted questionnaire and were analysed using binary logistic regression, frequencies, and percentages. The results revealed 7.2% incidence of GDM among pregnant women who attended ANC in the Central Region of Ghana. Maternal age 41 years and above, odds ratio [AOR] = 34.659, 95% Cl [2.263, 530.913], p < 0.001), a family history of diabetes mellitus (DM) (AOR = 3.366, Cl [1.778, 6.373], p < 0.001), and GDM history (AOR = 89.565, Cl [44.971, 178.381], p < 0.001) were risk variables that predicted GDM. Socio-demographic variables such as maternal age ≥ 41 years (AOR = 14.558, 95% CI [1.537, 137.876], p < 0.001), and high-income categories [> GHS 600 and GHS 400–600] with (AOR = 5.323, 95% CI [2.373, 11.944], p < 0.01) and (AOR = 2.590, 95% CI [1.071, 6.263], p < 0.05), significantly predicted GDM. GDM incidence among pregnant women in the Central Region of Ghana is high, with increasing maternal age, family history of DM, and a history of GDM as risk factors. The Ghana Health Service (GHS) should intensify pre and post conception education on the risk factors and make resources available for better assessment to identify risk factors and implement measures to prevent complications.Keywords: Prevalence, Risk factors, GDM, Pregnant women, Maternal, Birth, Outcomes, GhanaSubject terms: Diseases, Endocrinology, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Diabetes is “a group of metabolic diseases characterised by hyperglycaemia resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both”1. As the fifth most leading cause of death worldwide, and one of the fastest-growing global health emergencies in the 21st century2, diabetes mellitus (DM) is responsible for approximately 3 million deaths annually, representing 1.7–5.2% of all deaths worldwide3. Globally, an estimated 537 and 589 million persons between the ages of 20 and 79 were living with diabetes as of 2021, and 2024, respectively2,3, with Africa having 24.6 million adults living with diabetes in 2024, with a projected increase to 59.5 million (142%) in 20502. Besides, it’s been proven that three out of four diabetics reside in low and middle-income countries, which account for 87% of diabetes-related mortality2,3. This has led to a rise in gestational diabetes mellitus prevalence worldwide revealing a global prevalence of 16.3% (20–49 years), 13% in Africa, and 5.3% in Ghana3. Additionally, varying prevalence of 6.46, 9.3%, 9.0%, and 10.8% has been revealed in the Greater Accra and the Volta regions of Ghana, respectively4–6. Experts are not only concerned with the increasing prevalence but are equally worried about the modifiable risk factors contributing to the increasing prevalence, as well as the adverse maternal and perinatal outcomes7. The increasing prevalence of diabetes mellitus in the general population, especially among women may increase the risk of GDM among pregnant women. GDM, as one of the forms of diabetes which affects approximately one in five pregnancies and poses a serious threat to public health because of its rising prevalence and its implications on pregnant women and the health of the children in Africa and Ghana2–4. GDM is hyperglycaemia that is first detected during pregnancy and usually diagnosed in the second and third trimesters of pregnancy8.

Several risk factors for GDM have been identified in literature by researchers. A few of these include advanced age, obesity and overweight, a history of gestational diabetes, excessive weight gain during pregnancy, polycystic ovarian syndrome, a family history of diabetes, continuous smoking, and a history of stillbirths or congenital abnormalities in newborns2,3,9–14. A systematic review and meta-analysis study conducted in Ghana by Asamoah-Boaheng et al.5 revealed that physical inactivity, mother’s age ≥ 40, and a family history of diabetes were substantially related with GDM. Obese women at Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) have a greater incidence of GDM, according to Oppong et al.6. According to Bawah et al.15, there is a correlation between GDM, rising BMI and age, in a retrospective case-control study conducted in Ghana. Boadu et al.16 identified histories of oral contraceptive use, pre-eclampsia, and soda intake as independent predictors of GDM. Despite diverse risk factors identified across various studies, common risk factors require close monitoring and attention. GDM clients need dedicated clinics for proper assessment and identification of specific predisposing factors.

Drawing from the above, it may be difficult to achieve the targets of the SDG 3 by the year 2030 if the incidence of GDM increase. Also, reducing the global maternal mortality ratio to less than 70 per 100, 000 live births, and preventing deaths of newborns and children under 5 years of age may be highly anticipated if specific risk factors are identified and dealt with to prevent unfavourable outcomes. Again, a reduction in neonatal and under five mortalities to as low as 12 and 25 per 1,000 live births may be possible with a very low incidence of GDM. Even though studies conducted globally, in sub-Saharan Africa, and other countries have found different prevalence and risk factors contributing to GDM12,17–19, risk factors influencing the development of GDM in individual countries are different5. Furthermore, Guariguata et al.20 asserts that the development of GDM is dependent on the group being studied and the method of diagnosis used as different population groups have different risk profiles for GDM21. Knowing the risk factors contributing to GDM in Ghana and providing pre-conception counselling and a planned pregnancy approach to women in their childbearing age will go a long way to influence the health and well-being of the mother and the newborn. The purpose of this research was to ascertain the incidence of GDM, the factors that contribute to its occurrence in Ghana, and make recommendations to strengthen practice, maternal health policy formulation and future studies.

Methods

The study applied a quantitative prospective cohort study design and sampled 1,577 pregnant women receiving antenatal care (ANC) in 12 hospitals (including one teaching, one university) in the Central Region of Ghana between December 2022 and August 2023. Pregnant women (gestational age 8–20 week) of first antenatal booking, and from ages 14 to 48 without GDM and overt diabetes11,22 were followed at the ANCs for GDM development during care. Pregnant women who were critically ill at the beginning of the study, and are on medications that might impact glucose metabolism (steroids, β-adrenergic agonists, anti-psychotic drugs) were excluded from the study11. The single cohort approach was followed and those who developed GDM had the outcome of interest while those without GDM served as the non-cases (internal controls). Pregnancy was determined with an ultrasound result indicating a fetus in utero. Participants were conveniently sampled from the ANC for three months (December 2022, January and February 2023). A structured questionnaire adapted from Agbozo4 and Muche et al.11 on the socio-demographic factors, risk factors and maternal and birth outcomes was pretested and used for the study. Respondents’ socio-demographic characteristics, obstetric history and risk for developing GDM (family history of DM, previous history of GDM, history of preterm delivery, history of large baby size (≥ 4 kg), history of stillbirth, hypertension in pregnancy, history of abortion or miscarriage, were obtained from respondents using the adapted questionnaire. However, the respondents Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated by the researchers’ using data on their weights and heights obtained from their ANC folders. The anthropometric measurements such weights and heights were obtained from the participants at the facilities’s ANC after their consent. The BMI was calculated by dividing the weights in kilograms by the heights in metres squared. Since the respondents’ heights were recorded in centimetres, they were converted to metres by dividing by 100, and the figure obtained squared. This was then rounded to two decimals and used with the weight to calculate the BMI. The questionnaire was translated into the Fanti language for those who could not read and written in the English language to ensure clear communication with respondents since the local dialect is Fanti, and back to English. The World Health Organisation’s 2013 criteria for diagnosing GDM as used by the facilities were followed (Fasting plasma glucose, 5.1–6.9 mmol/L (92–125 mg/dl; following a 75 g oral glucose load an hour, the range is ≥ 10.0 mmol/L (180 mg/dl); and following a 2-hour post-load, the range is 8.5–11.0 mmol/L (153–199 mg/dl)23. The urine glucose/protein, fasting blood glucose, and oral glucose tolerance test results were also obtained from the clients at the antenatal clinics. Facility based study was conducted, and the final sample size was 1,577. During the process of follow-up, 510 respondents were lost to follow-up and 56 of the respondents had miscarriage during the antepartum period. Additionally, some women did not show interest in the study and their opinion was respected. However, the sample reported could be deemed enough for generalization of the findings to the region. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Ghana Health Service (GHS-ERC: 017/09/2022). The data collection of this study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was completely voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time. Both verbal and written informed consents were obtained from participants before their acceptance into the study. Participants who gave their consent completed the questionnaire with the support of field assistants. Data was statistically processed into Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25.0. Frequencies and percentages were used to analyse the incidence of GDM, and univariate and multivariate logistic regression were used to determine the risk factors of GDM in the region. Probability (p) value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Multivariate binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to ascertain the risk factors for GDM. Listwise deletion was done in this analysis. Table 3 revealed that age predicted GDM such that those who were aged 26–30 years [AOR = 19.210, p < 0.001], 31–35 years [AOR = 16.200, p < 0.001], 36–40 years [AOR = 19.322, p < 0.001], and those 41 years and older [AOR = 34.659, p < 0.001] were at greater risk of developing GDM when all other variables were controlled for in the multivariate analysis. Similarly, those with a history of GDM [AOR = 89.565, p < 0.001], and a family history of GDM [AOR = 3.366, p < 0.001], had an increased risk of developing GDM during pregnancy. However, the number of previous pregnancies or births, history of preterm delivery, overweight babies (≥ 4 kg), stillbirth, hypertension during pregnancy, and BMI > 30 kg/m2 did not significantly predict GDM (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The study sought to identify the risk factors contributing to the development of GDM in the Central region of Ghana. The findings indicated that pregnant women in Ghana’s Central region had 7.2% incidence of GDM. This indicates that the likelihood of developing GDM is higher in about 7 out of every 100 of 1577 pregnant women in Ghana. This outcome also implies that there is a possibility of a sudden increase in the adverse maternal outcomes associated with GDM, thus; pre-eclampsia, premature rapture of membranes (PROM), cesarean section (CS), antepartum haemorrhage (APH), postpartum haemorrhage (PPH), and genital trauma, among others. This calls for more resource allocation to the health facilities in the management of these conditions, hence increasing the economic burden on health. Even though the percentage is not high compared to the percentages found in literature by Grunnet et al.24 and Larebo et al.10, it is likely to escalate if attention is not paid to the risk factors contributing to the 7% incidence. The incidence is not too different from the 9% prevalence found in the systematic review study conducted in Sub-Saharan African (SSA) by Natamba et al.25. The variation could be because the systematic review study pooled prevalence from several African countries. Similarly, Asamoah-Boaheng et al.5 discovered a prevalence of 6.46% among adults in their systematic review study. This finding nearly confirms the incidence found in the current study. There is a probability that the comparable characteristics of Ghanaian pregnant women accounts for this prevalence and incidence similarity. More so, a cross-sectional study carried out at Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital to ascertain the prevalence of GDM and risk factors associated with it in 399 expectant mothers found a prevalence of 9.3%6. This could be because both studies were conducted in Ghana, and respondents might have similar characteristics. Both studies also used a similar structured questionnaire as the data collection instrument to solicit responses from respondents, and respondents were pregnant women who were followed for nine months. Furthermore, the findings align well with a cross-sectional study conducted in Kumasi, Ghana, by Boadu et al.16, where the prevalence of GDM was 8.5%. The study was facility-based and used fasting blood glucose greater or equal to 5.1mmol/l to diagnose GDM as with the current study. Conversely, a cross-sectional investigation on 612 pregnant women in Southern Tanzania reported a 4.3% prevalence of GDM among rural women attending prenatal clinics, along with related variables26. The prevalence might result from the fact that the study’s participants were rural woman. Some rural women cultivate the food stuff themselves, and they are likely to make food choices based on what is available for use, which may be healthy choices. Besides, other factors such as educational background, meal timing, and pregnancy-related foods contribute to the development of GDM and the rural women may not be directly linked to these factors. Again, given that the study was cross-sectional and that data was gathered in a short period (September to October 2017), it is possible that fewer pregnant women were included than expected, which would account for the 4.3% prevalence. As the incidence of 7.2% may not be considered too high in the current study, the effect of GDM on the mother and the baby is enormous. More so, the possibility of GDM increasing in future pregnancies is high. This, however, comes with health, economic, and social implications on the nation at large, considering the short and long-term adverse maternal and birth outcomes. Even though GDM cannot be eradicated, it is imperative to diagnose it as early as possible and implement measures to curb maternal and fetal complications.

Besides, maternal age, a history of previous GDM, and a family history of DM are the three main risk factors for developing GDM in Ghana’s Central Region. The study found that age increases the risk of GDM development during pregnancy. This means that older pregnant women are at a higher risk to develop GDM, especially those aged 26 years and above. This implies that the frequency of GDM in the population will rise as more women give birth to children later in age in both urban and rural settings.

The findings of the study are consistent with several other studies12,25,27. Even though the World Health Organization (WHO) has identified several risk factors linked to GDM13, some people may be more susceptible to the GDM due to particular risk factors. Different studies14,18,28 have found different risk factors for GDM, which may vary depending on the population investigated or other relevant factors. It is commonly known that the risk profiles for GDM vary throughout population groups21.

According to studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa by Natamba et al., and Mwanri et al.25,27, advanced maternal age (> 30 years) was found to be a significant predictor of GDM. The findings of the current study are consistent with the results of the studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa25,27.

The findings corroborate the results of the systematic review and meta-analysis studies carried out in Ghana by Asamoah-Boaheng et al., and Oppong et al.5,6, which revealed a significant association between age > 40 years and DM. The results of the two Ghanaian studies5,6 showed that older age was a major risk factor for DM and GDM. Respondents might have similar characteristics in these studies in comparison with the current study.

The study’s findings also align with a retrospective cohort study on 3,956 urban Chinese antenatal patients which identified advanced maternal age (31 years and above), high pre-pregnancy BMI, a family history of diabetes and elevated fasting plasma glucose in the first trimester as predictors of GDM28. This agreement may be due to similar study designs, statistical analysis tools, and data collection methods used in both studies. However, the Chinese study had a larger sample size and also obtained existing data from the facilities.

Additionally, the current study’s findings concur with a cross-sectional study in Cameroon on 200 pregnant women, which identified advanced maternal age (≥ 30 years) as a risk factor for GDM alongside obesity, history of unexplained stillbirth, and macrosomia18. Both studies sampled respondents within the age range of 14–45 years and were conducted in Africa, resulting in similar characteristics among respondents. However, the current study was prospective, while the Cameroonian study was cross-sectional. Additionally, the current study had a larger sample size, increasing its statistical power.

It is relevant to note that the findings of the current study are different from that of a study conducted in Thailand, where the development of GDM was not predicted by maternal age29. The variation in the research site and sample size could contribute to the cause of this disagreement. The findings also differ from the study conducted by Grunnet et al.24 in rural Tanzania where no significant correlation was found between maternal age and GDM. This may be explained by the Tanzanian study’s population-based design, which sampled pregnant women between the ages of 18 and 40, as opposed to the current study’s 14–48 age range. However, different statistical analysis tools were employed in the current study and Grunnet et al.’s study. The results of the present investigation similarly challenge that of Oppong et al.6, who found no significant correlation between maternal age and GDM. Although multiple logistic regression analysis and questionnaires were employed in both researches, Oppong’s study was cross-sectional and had a smaller sample size.

The study found a previous history of GDM to predict the occurrence of GDM in subsequent pregnancies. Pregnant women with a previous history of GDM were 90 times more likely to develop GDM in subsequent pregnancies. This implies that as new cases add to the old cases, the number of GDM cases will increase. Thus, increasing the risk of complications such as macrosomia, birth trauma, cesarean delivery, premature birth, pre-eclampsia, neonatal hypoglycaemia, and type 2 diabetes in later life30,31.

Consistent with the findings, Muche et al.12 found that a history of GDM constituted a substantial risk factor in their systematic review and meta-analysis study. This concordance may be explained by the fact that the systematic review and meta-analysis study reviewed studies in sub-Saharan African countries, and the current study was also in Ghana, an African country. Muche et al. concentrated on GDM while evaluating the publications. Besides, the designs and sampling techniques were different. Similarly, the current study findings corroborate with the findings of the study by Muche et al. at Northwest Ethiopia22. Although the study by Muche et al. was a cross-sectional study, multivariate analysis was done as with the current study. The findings are in line with another systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Mwanri et al.27, which showed that despite differences in design and sampling techniques, a history of GDM was a reliable predictor of GDM in subsequent pregnancies. The results of this study support that of Natamba et al.25, who reported that women with a history of GDM had a higher risk of developing GDM. Since these studies were carried out in several African countries, the outcomes were dependable.

The findings are consistent with a previous study in Enugu by Onyenekwe et al., which revealed that a history of GDM was a major risk factor for the development of GDM, as were fetal macrosomia, rising maternal age, obesity, miscarriages, and stillbirths32. This consistency may be due to the geographic similarities, as both studies were conducted in African countries and used questionnaires for data collection. Another study also found that pregnant women with a history of GDM had a 14-fold increased risk of developing GDM in their subsequent pregnancies12. The outcomes of the present investigation align with these outcomes.

Pakistia et al. found no correlation between GDM development and a previous history of GDM. The findings of the current study were significantly different from theirs33. Possible explanation for this disparity includes variations in sample sizes and methods. The present investigation was based on diagnostic tests performed in different facilities, while Pakistia et al. employed OGTT results and glucose administration. Additionally, the findings of the current study disagreed with those of Bianch et al.34, who did not discover a correlation between prior GDM and GDM.

A family history of diabetes mellitus (DM) increases a client’s risk of developing GDM by three times, according to the findings of this study. The findings align with the systematic review and meta-analysis studies carried out in some African countries by Asamoah-Boaheng et al., Muche et al. and Natamba at al.5,12,25. This is most likely because the responses shared a lot of similarities. The current researchers’ 3.36 odds family history of DM for acquiring GDM are comparable to the 3.46 odds obtained in the Ghanaian study by Asamoah-Boaheng et al.5. This research provides more evidence in favour of a Chinese retrospective study conducted by Guo et al.28, which found a family history of DM to predict GDM development. Guo et al.’s28 work had a bigger sample size than the current study, even though they used similar designs and logistic regression analysis. These findings also corroborate a 2015 study by Mwanri et al.27 that was done in sub-Saharan Africa and revealed that family history of DM was a predictor of GDM.

However, the findings contradict that of Egbe et al.18, where a family history of DM did not predict GDM. The disagreement could be due to Egbe’s cross-sectional study design and smaller sample size which could not detect small relationships. The results also differ from the studies conducted in Southern Ethiopia, where a family history of DM was not linked to GDM10,34. The disparities in study design, location, and sample size could be the plausible reasons for these variances.

The current study also found that income had a significant impact on GDM in Ghana. Pregnant women earning more than 600 Ghana cedis had a higher risk of GDM than those earning less than or equal to 399 Ghana cedis. This suggests that there is a higher likelihood of GDM among affluent women. The relationship between high income and GDM development is complex and influenced by various factors. While high income may not directly contribute to GDM, it is expected that higher income might be associated with a lower risk of GDM due to better access to healthcare, healthier lifestyle choices, nutritious food, reduced stress levels, and resources for weight management. However, individual circumstances and other factors also play a role. Besides, higher income can also lead to the overconsumption of processed foods, sugary snacks, and high-calorie meals by pregnant women when they crave for them. Some pregnant women tend to eat excessively without considering the nutritional content or timing of their meals. More so, overindulging in sedentary lifestyles, and unhealthy meals can result in weight gain and insulin resistance, which raises the risk of GDM.

Conclusions

GDM incidence among pregnant women in Ghana’s Central region is high. Maternal age, history of GDM and a family history of DM predicted GDM development. Care givers need to pay attention to the risk factors for GDM through effective assessment and close monitoring right from pre-pregnancy, pregnancy, delivery, post-natal, and beyond. Resources will need to be allocated for the care of pregnant women especially for early detection of risks for early intervention to lessen the effects on the mother and the baby.

Data was collected from the hospitals; hence, it is mandatory that GHS granted ethical to allow permission to conduct the study in the hospitals. Informed consent was obtained from pregnant women, however, those below the legal age for consent were two, hence, parental and or guardian consent was obtained from their parent before participation. Data collection began after ethical clearance was obtained. Permission and clearance letters were sent to the hospitals involved in the study and permission was sought from the medical directors/superintendents. Participants involved in the study were met during their ANC clinic days. The purpose of the study was explained to them. Participant were made aware that their participation was completely voluntary and that they might withdraw at any time. They were also provided with the participants information sheets to abreast them with information on the nature of the study. Written and verbal informed consents were obtained from participants prior to the start of the study.
