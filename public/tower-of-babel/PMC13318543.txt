Global landscape of liver disease burden in 1990–2021
Authors: Mengwei Zhang, Lijie Ma, Weiqiu Jin, Qiang Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryLiver diseases remain a major global contributor to disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). Using Global Burden of Disease 2021 data, we applied a three-tier framework linking total liver diseases to acute hepatitis, cirrhosis, liver cancer, and their main etiologies. In 2021, liver diseases caused 309.72 million new cases, 1.98 million deaths, and 63.53 million DALYs worldwide. Although global age-standardized rates declined, death and DALY rates increased in High-income North America and Australasia, with steatosis- and alcohol-related liver diseases accounting for a growing share of the burden. Independent datasets, including the World Health Organization and Global Cancer Observatory sources, supported these regional patterns. Disease burden showed marked age, sex, and etiological disparities, with metabolic factors rising rapidly. Although overall mortality is projected to decline, liver cancer due to steatohepatitis and hepatoblastoma is projected to rise. These findings highlight persistent regional, demographic, and etiological disparities and the need for targeted public health action.

Introduction

The liver is a vital metabolic organ with essential roles in detoxification, synthesis, and digestion.1 However, it is susceptible to a range of progressive diseases, including hepatitis, cirrhosis, and liver cancer,2,3 which contribute substantially to global morbidity and mortality. Although these conditions are often studied as separate diseases, they represent interconnected components within the liver disease spectrum and share overlapping risk factors and epidemiological determinants.4,5 In recent decades, the etiological landscape of liver diseases has shifted. Metabolism-related causes such as metabolic-dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) are replacing viral hepatitis as dominant liver disease drivers, especially in high-income regions with rising obesity and type 2 diabetes.4,6 Therefore, an integrated framework encompassing hepatitis, cirrhosis, and liver cancer is important to capture their evolving burden and underlying determinants.

As one of the few sources with detailed estimates of incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for all liver diseases, the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study provides an integrated database enabling up-to-date assessment of liver disease burdens and their trends.7 Many previous studies have focused on individual liver diseases or specific etiologies, and few have comprehensively examined the burden of liver diseases across the full disease spectrum. In addition, long-term projections of liver disease burden under the context of ongoing epidemiological transition remain limited. Using data from GBD 2021, this study aimed to comprehensively assess the global burden of liver diseases from 1990 to 2021, focusing on total liver diseases and three major categories—acute hepatitis, cirrhosis, and liver cancer—together with their underlying etiologies and attributable risk factors (Figure 1). Additionally, the burden in 2022–2046 was projected to reveal the epidemiological shifts of liver diseases. Results

Global burden and trends of liver diseases

In 2021, global liver disease incident cases increased by 2.03% compared with that in 1990 (Table S1). Deaths rose by 39.44%, and DALYs increased by 18.35%. Acute hepatitis accounted for most incident cases in 2021, while cirrhosis and liver cancer dominated deaths and DALYs. Despite rising case numbers, age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardized death rate (ASDR), and age-standardized DALY (ASDALY) rates, all declined between 1990 and 2021 (Figure 2). Joinpoint regression analysis revealed non-uniform trends across liver disease types and etiologies (Figures 2 and S1). While most age-standardized rates (ASRs) for acute hepatitis, cirrhosis, and liver cancer declined, ASIR for cirrhosis and liver cancer increased between 1990 and 2021. For acute hepatitis, ASRs decreased for hepatitis A, B, C, and E. Among cirrhosis, only MASLD including cirrhosis saw a continuous increase in ASIR, while ASDR remained nearly stable. Conversely, other causes (including chronic hepatitis B, chronic hepatitis C, alcohol, and other causes) showed a consistent decline in both ASIR and ASDR. Metabolic-dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) and alcohol were the top two causes of liver cancer with the fastest increases in both ASIRs and ASDRs (Figures S2 and S3).

Regional burden and trends of liver diseases

In 1990, the highest ASRs for liver disease were in Western Sub-Saharan Africa. However, in 2021, the highest ASDR was observed in Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa, while the highest ASIR and ASDALY were in Central Sub-Saharan Africa. From 1990 to 2021, global ASRs for liver diseases generally decreased, with trends varying with the Socio-demographic Index (SDI). While most regions followed the global pattern, some locations, such as Central Asia, Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Southern Sub-Saharan Africa, experienced initial increases in ASDRs and ASDALY rates before declining. In contrast, High-income North America and Australasia saw overall increases in ASDRs and ASDALY rates over the study period (Figure 3). Regions such as Central Sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, and High-income Asia Pacific, despite declining trends, continue to have a higher burden of liver disease than expected for their level of SDI. ASDRs for acute hepatitis generally decreased as the SDI improved (Figures S4 and S5). Although most regions showed continuous declines in ASDRs for cirrhosis, High-income North America experienced increasing ASDRs for cirrhosis, primarily associated with cirrhosis due to alcohol, MASLD including cirrhosis, and cirrhosis due to other causes. For liver cancers, low-SDI regions, such as Western and Central Sub-Saharan Africa, showed declining ASDRs with SDI improvements, while middle-SDI regions remained stable. With the exception of High-income Asia Pacific, most high-SDI regions showed increasing liver cancer ASDRs, mainly accounted for liver cancer due to alcohol use, MASH, and other causes. These patterns were supported by independent international data sources. The World Health Organization (WHO) Mortality Database records showed increasing ASDRs for both cirrhosis and liver cancer in most high-income countries, such as the United States of America and Australia (Figure S6). Consistently, data from the Global Cancer Observatory indicated rising liver cancer ASIRs and ASDRs in these countries over recent decades (Figure S7). Notably, both WHO Mortality Database and Global Cancer Observatory data suggested a recent decline in liver cancer ASDRs in the United States of America and Australia during the past 2 to 3 years, which does not contradict the long-term increasing trends observed over the past three decades. Data for Western and Central Sub-Saharan Africa were not available in the WHO Mortality Database or the Global Cancer Observatory. Nevertheless, WHO Global Health Observatory indicators showed persistently high and increasing levels of alcohol consumption and obesity prevalence in high-income countries, such as the United States of America and Australia (Figure S8), which were consistent with the observed increases in alcohol- and metabolism-related liver cancer mortality.

Proportion of different liver diseases

Figures 4 and S9 showed the global and regional shares of new cases, deaths, and DALYs for acute hepatitis, cirrhosis, and liver cancer in 1990 and 2021, respectively. While acute hepatitis remained the most common form of liver disease, its share of cases decreased from 87.8% in 1990 to 81.0% in 2021. Cirrhosis accounted for the majority of liver-related deaths and DALYs, with a stable proportion of deaths (71.9% in both years) and an increase in the proportion of DALYs (67.6% in 1990 to 73.1% in 2021). Regionally, the highest proportion of deaths from acute hepatitis shifted from South Asia in 1990 (23.4%) to Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa in 2021 (9.3%). The highest proportions of cirrhosis deaths were in Southern Latin America in 1990 (96.0%) and Caribbean in 2021 (89.8%). The highest proportion of liver cancer deaths was observed in High-income Asia Pacific in both 1990 (44.2%) and 2021 (58.9%). Figure S10 further breaks down the proportions of deaths in 2021 by sex. In 2021, the highest proportion of acute hepatitis deaths occurred in Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa (10.5%) for males and in South Asia (13.3%) for females. For cirrhosis, Andean and Central Latin America had the highest proportion of deaths in males (91.9%) and Eastern Europe had the highest for females (89.4%). Figure S11 analyzed death proportions attributed to specific causes of acute hepatitis, cirrhosis, and liver cancer in 1990 and 2021. For acute hepatitis, acute hepatitis A was the leading cause of death in 1990, whereas acute hepatitis B became the predominant cause in 2021, with the proportion increasing from 32.3% to 50.1%, alongside a marked decline in hepatitis-A-related deaths (59.3%–37.4%). Additionally, increases were also exhibited in deaths due to acute hepatitis C (6.3%–7.6%) and acute hepatitis E (2.1%–4.8%). For cirrhosis, the proportion of deaths attributable to MASLD increased from 4.4% to 6.8%, alcohol use from 21.8% to 24.9%, and hepatitis C from 25.2% to 25.8%. In contrast, the contribution of hepatitis B declined from 35.2% to 30.3%, as did deaths due to other causes (13.4%–12.2%). For liver cancer, the proportion of deaths increased for alcohol-related causes (16.0%–19.1%), hepatitis C (26.8%–30.3%), and MASH (6.1%–8.5%), while it decreased for hepatitis B (44.6%–37.4%), other causes (4.5%–4.3%), and hepatoblastoma (2.0%–0.5%). In summary, the shares of hepatitis B were increased in acute liver diseases but decreased in both cirrhosis and liver cancer, and the shares of metabolic (MASLD/MASH) and behavioral (alcohol) factors increased fastest in both cirrhosis and liver cancer. These patterns are concordant with WHO-reported global reductions in hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) prevalence among children under 5 years, together with rising trends in obesity prevalence since the 1990s and per capita alcohol consumption observed since the early 2010s (Figure S12), reflecting the long-term effects of expanded hepatitis B vaccination programs and shifts in metabolic and behavioral risk profiles.

National and territorial burdens and trends of liver diseases

At the national and territorial level, the highest liver disease ASIRs in 1990 were in Afghanistan, Mauritania, and Nigeria (Figure S13 and Table S2). In 2021, Somalia had the highest ASIR, followed by Mauritania and Zimbabwe (Figure 5). All countries/territories with top 10 ASIRs in 2021 showed decreases in ASIRs since 1990. Most high-ASIR countries/territories were located in Sub-Saharan Africa or North Africa and Middle East. For ASDR, Egypt had the highest rate in both 1990 and 2021, followed by Mongolia and Somalia in 2021. Among the countries/territories with top 10 ASDRs in 2021, Turkmenistan was the only one with an increased ASDR since 1990. A similar pattern was observed for ASDALY rates. Egypt had the highest rate in 1990, while Mongolia ranked the highest in 2021, followed by Somalia and Egypt. Somalia also had the highest ASDR and ASDALY rate, while Afghanistan ranked second for both ASDR and ASDALY rate. For cirrhosis, the highest ASIRs in 2021 were in Egypt, Saudi Arabia, and Qatar (Table S4), with Egypt also having the highest ASDR. In the GBD 2021 estimates, these countries ranked 12th, 22nd, and 20th, respectively; countries ranked higher than these in the GBD analysis were not available in the WHO Mortality Database, reflecting differences in reporting coverage and data availability. Consistent with the GBD estimates, WHO Mortality Database records also identified Mongolia as the country with the highest liver cancer ASDR (Table S7).

Burden of liver diseases by age and sex

Figures 6 and S15 illustrated the age- and sex-specific patterns of liver disease. Liver disease was most common in children under 5 years. Deaths and DALYs for liver disease peaked in children under 5 years and older adults. In 1990, the highest number of deaths occurred at 65–69 years for females and 60–64 years for males, with DALYs peaking at 55–59 years for females and 50–54 years for males. By 2021, both sexes exhibited peak deaths at 65–69 years and DALYs at 55–59 years. However, among the age groups with top three deaths and DALYs, delayed peaks were also found in females. In terms of the specific causes, acute hepatitis held the leading part of liver disease incident cases in all age groups for both sexes in 1990 and 2021, except among females aged 60–74 years in 2021, where cirrhosis exceeded acute hepatitis. Acute hepatitis was also the leading cause of deaths and DALYs in younger age groups (<5 years), whereas cirrhosis predominated in older groups. Cause-specific death patterns also varied by age and sex (Figure S16). In younger individuals, deaths were mainly due to other causes of cirrhosis (<25 years) or due to hepatoblastoma in liver cancer (<10 years). With increasing age, cirrhosis and liver cancer due to hepatitis B virus (HBV)/hepatitis C virus (HCV) infections and alcohol use became the main causes. In 1990, acute hepatitis A was the leading cause of deaths for most age groups in both sexes, which, however, was overtaken by hepatitis B in 2021 in most age groups.

Projections of future burdens and trends for liver diseases until 2046

Using NORDPRED models, the liver disease ASDR was projected to decrease over the next 25 years (from 23.20 in 2021 to 18.43 in 2046 per 100,000 population) (Figure 8). Similarly, the ASDRs for acute hepatitis, cirrhosis, and liver cancer were projected to fall to 0.48, 13.57, and 4.62 per 100,000 population in 2046, respectively. Consistent declines were also projected for the ASDRs of acute hepatitis and cirrhosis across different causes (Figures 8B and 8C). However, the ASDR for liver cancer due to MASH was projected to increase slightly from 0.67 in 2021 to 0.69 in 2029 per 100,000 population, before decreasing to 0.64 per 100,000 population by 2046. In contrast, hepatoblastoma ASDR was projected to rise gradually from 0.18 to 0.21 per 100,000 population. The ASIRs for total liver disease, acute hepatitis, cirrhosis, and liver cancer were also projected to decrease, although increases were observed in the projections for some specific causes like MASLDs including cirrhosis and hepatoblastoma (Figure S19). Discussion

Liver diseases are characterized by an etiological intersection of infectious agents, metabolic disorders, and human lifestyles, with an inherent interrelated natural history, progressing from acute hepatitis to cirrhosis and ultimately malignancies. Using data from the GBD 2021 study, this secondary analysis examined the epidemiological patterns and projected trends of liver diseases within a three-level hierarchical framework, covering total liver diseases, major disease categories (acute hepatitis, cirrhosis, and liver cancer), and their specific etiologies.

From 1990 to 2021, global number of newly diagnosed liver disease cases, deaths, and DALYs increased, while ASRs generally declined. This decline in ASRs may partly reflect improvements in public health efforts, such as improved hygiene and healthcare access, hepatitis B vaccination programs, and better management of viral hepatitis.8,9 However, rising ASIRs or ASDRs for MASLDs including cirrhosis, liver cancer due to MASH, and liver cancer due to alcohol suggest evolving epidemiological patterns, with lifestyle-related factors contributing increasingly to the liver disease burden. These patterns underscore the need for targeted interventions, including lifestyle modification, screening, and awareness promotion programs in high-risk populations, such as individuals with obesity or alcohol abuse.

Regionally, ASDRs and ASDALY rates rose continuously in High-income North America and Australasia, largely associated with lifestyle-related factors (MASLD/MASH and alcohol). These patterns likely reflect broader social, economic, and policy contexts shaping risk exposure rather than individual behavioral changes alone. In many high-income settings, long-term dietary transitions toward energy-dense ultra-processed foods and increasingly sedentary lifestyles have contributed to rising prevalence of obesity and type 2 diabetes, key metabolic drivers of liver disease.10,11,12 These metabolic risks may be further exacerbated among socioeconomically disadvantaged populations and migrant groups, who often face limited access to healthy food environments and preventive health resources.13 In parallel, alcohol-related burden may be shaped by policy and market dynamics. In North America, increasing binge drinking—particularly among females—has been associated with reduced alcohol costs, aggressive female-targeted marketing, and co-use of substances such as cannabis.14,15 Australia has maintained high levels of alcohol consumption since the post-World War II era.16 Although these regions possess advanced healthcare systems, preventive strategies targeting early metabolic risk and hazardous alcohol consumption have often lagged behind curative services.17,18 Together, these findings suggest that the rising burden of metabolic and alcohol-related liver diseases in high-income regions may reflect the combined influence of commercial determinants of health, lifestyle transitions, and policy environments that have not adequately addressed evolving risk profiles. Microsimulation modeling studies further suggest that policy combinations simultaneously targeting alcohol consumption and obesity—such as minimum unit pricing for alcohol and taxation of sugar-sweetened beverages—may represent more feasible and effective strategies for addressing multiple liver disease risk factors and reducing future disease burden.18

Central Sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, and High-income Asia Pacific, despite showing reductions in liver disease burden as SDI rises, have higher-than-expected rates relative to their development level. This may be related to historically large burdens of liver disease and the delayed impact of control measures. For instance, High-income Asia Pacific has experienced periods of high HBV prevalence, with progression from HBV infection to cirrhosis or liver cancer potentially taking decades.19 From 1990 to 2019, HBsAg seroprevalence among High-income Asia Pacific was the highest among all high-income regions.19 Meanwhile, significant progress in HBV control has narrowed the gap in HBsAg seroprevalence among children under 5 years between High-income Asia Pacific and other high-income regions.19

At the national and territorial level, ASIRs have decreased in most countries/territories since 1990, and countries/territories with high ASIRs were concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa and North Africa and Middle East, with Somalia, Mauritania, and Nigeria having the highest ASIRs in 2021. Egypt and Mongolia consistently had the highest ASDRs and ASDALY rates for liver disease, respectively, which may be related to long-standing HBV and HCV burdens.20,21 In Mongolia, past HBV and HCV transmission events, such as syringe reuse before 1995 and transfusion-related infections before mandatory blood screening in 1997, significantly contributed to the current liver cancer burden.20,22 These past transmission events highlight the lag in liver cancer control, as reductions in mortality and morbidity can take decades after prevention interventions. Turkmenistan stands out as the only country among the top 10 for ASDR and ASDALY rates in 2021 that has increased since 1990. This trend may be related to differences in healthcare investment and liver disease prevention efforts.23

The observed peaks in liver disease cases, deaths, and DALYs in different age groups suggest distinct epidemiological patterns. In children younger than 5 years, the high number of cases is mainly associated with acute hepatitis infections, particularly hepatitis A and B. Hepatitis A, which is often transmitted through the fecal-oral route, is more prevalent in low- and lower-middle-income countries with poor hygiene.24 The WHO recommends universal vaccination in countries with moderate hepatitis A prevalence.24 Other preventive measures include improvement of hygiene, rapid detection of outbreaks, and prompt implementation of outbreak control measures. HBV can be transmitted from mother to child, and around 4.5 million mothers with HBV infection give birth annually,25 making it a major cause of liver disease in children younger than 5 years. Effective interventions to reduce mother-to-child transmission include maternal antiviral therapy, neonatal immunoglobulin administration, and universal vaccination at birth.25 Infectious diseases may also exert broader long-term health consequences beyond their acute manifestations.26 The increase in cases among older adolescents (15–19 years) compared with younger adolescents (10–14 years) is largely associated with the increases in cirrhosis, mainly related to the rise of chronic hepatitis B including cirrhosis and the emergence of cirrhosis due to alcohol and MASLD including cirrhosis. Early interventions, such as alcohol education and lifestyle modification, targeting younger populations can help prevent progression to chronic liver disease later in life.27,28 In older populations, the peaks (50–69 years) in deaths and DALYs likely correspond to the cumulative burden of chronic liver diseases such as cirrhosis and liver cancer, associated with long-term HBV/HCV infection, alcohol use, and MASLD.

Sex differences significantly influence liver disease burden. In 2021, DALYs for males were more than double those for females. Although ASIR declined faster in males, the decline in ASDR and ASDALY rate was more pronounced in females. The delayed peak in liver disease burden among females may reflect lower incidence or slower progression in younger females. Behavioral factors likely contribute to these differences, as females are generally less likely to engage in problem drinking.29 Nevertheless, increasing alcohol consumption among females in some regions is concerning, particularly because comparable levels of alcohol exposure may be associated with greater liver-related risks in females.30 Because the GBD framework does not allow direct assessment of biological mechanisms, these interpretations should be considered descriptive. Further studies integrating epidemiological, clinical, and mechanistic data are needed to clarify the drivers of sex differences in liver disease burden.

Alcohol use remains the leading risk factor for liver disease mortality worldwide. However, the increasing contribution of high BMI and high fasting plasma glucose is concerning. In North Africa and Middle East, high BMI was the top risk factor for females in 2021, in accordance with studies showing a dramatic increase in obesity.31,32,33 Drug use was the leading risk factor for females in East Asia and Oceania in 2021, highlighting the need for targeted interventions to address substance abuse. Meanwhile, alcohol use remained the predominant risk factor for males globally, highlighting the importance of continued efforts to reduce alcohol consumption through public health initiatives, taxation policies, and improved access to addiction treatment.34,35 However, risk attribution in the GBD framework is model-based, and the estimated attributable fractions are subject to uncertainty, which should be taken into account when interpreting these patterns.

Liver disease ASDR is projected to decline in 2022–2046. Despite this overall trend, ASDR for liver cancer due to MASH is projected to increase from around 2029, which may reflect the growing relative contribution of metabolic disorders compared with viral etiologies. Additionally, the projected increase in hepatoblastoma, a rare pediatric liver cancer, suggests the need for further investigation into the determinants of childhood liver cancer.36,37 Further research is needed to understand the risk factors of hepatoblastoma and to develop prevention and early detection strategies. These projections should be interpreted with caution. The NORDPRED model extrapolates recent trends while applying predefined attenuation parameters and therefore assumes partial continuation of observed patterns. It does not explicitly incorporate potential future policy changes, therapeutic advances, or shifts in risk factor prevalence that could substantially alter disease trajectories. Accordingly, the projected estimates should be considered conditional trend-based extrapolations rather than definitive forecasts. Future studies could extend these analyses using scenario-based projection frameworks that incorporate dynamic risk-factor modeling, policy-sensitive parameters, and external validation or comparisons with alternative projection approaches.

Although several studies have examined the global burden of specific liver diseases using GBD data, most have focused on individual conditions in isolation,38,39,40 limiting the ability to capture the full spectrum and internal structure of liver disease burden. In this study, liver diseases were analyzed within a unified three-level hierarchical framework integrating acute hepatitis, cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases, and liver cancer. This framework enables a comprehensive assessment of the total liver disease burden and its internal composition (i.e., the relative contribution of different liver disease categories) and facilitates comparisons across etiologies, regions, age groups, and sexes. First, by treating liver diseases as a unified disease category, this study evaluates their overall burden within an integrated analytical framework, providing a population-level perspective that cannot be obtained from analyses focusing on individual diseases alone. Second, comparative analyses across different liver disease subtypes further highlight their interrelationships, differences, and temporal dynamics. This approach helps reveal both shared and divergent epidemiological patterns across the liver disease spectrum. For example, although ASRs generally declined over time, the relative contribution of metabolic and alcohol-related liver diseases increased, suggesting evolving epidemiological patterns in liver disease burden. Although the analysis relies on publicly available GBD estimates, its contribution lies in synthesizing these data within an integrated structural perspective, allowing changes in the internal composition of liver disease burden to be evaluated across populations rather than within individual diseases alone. More broadly, this integrative perspective may be extended to other groups of pathophysiologically related conditions to better characterize interactions within disease spectra. Lifestyle-related determinants, such as physical inactivity, obesity, and alcohol consumption, often influence multiple health outcomes simultaneously. For instance, increasing physical activity has been associated with improvements in metabolic health and reductions in mental health complications.12,41,42 Understanding these shared determinants may support coordinated prevention strategies that address multiple non-communicable diseases across organ systems.

Importantly, key findings from our GBD-based analysis were supported by independent international datasets. Rising mortality from cirrhosis and liver cancer in high-income regions, particularly in the United States of America and Australia, was consistently observed in the WHO Mortality Database and Global Cancer Observatory. In addition, the increasing contribution of metabolic and behavioral risk factors aligns with WHO Global Health Observatory reports of rising obesity prevalence and persistently high alcohol consumption, alongside declining hepatitis B surface antigen prevalence among children following vaccination scale-up. Cross-database comparisons should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously because of differences in population coverage, ICD coding practices, and completeness of death registration. These methodological differences may influence absolute estimates of mortality and disease burden. However, the consistent directional patterns observed across independent data sources suggest that such discrepancies are more likely to affect the magnitude rather than the overall trajectory of liver disease trends.

It should be noted that this study is based on GBD 2021 estimates derived from statistical modeling that integrates multiple heterogeneous data sources rather than relying exclusively on directly observed data. Consequently, the precision of estimation may vary across regions, particularly in data-sparse settings (such as low-income countries and developing regions) where modeling assumptions play a vital role. These uncertainties may influence the magnitude of the estimated burden and should be considered when interpreting the results, although the overall direction of the observed epidemiological trends appears broadly consistent. In addition, the integration of acute hepatitis, cirrhosis, and liver cancer in this study represents a conceptual and analytical framework based on hierarchical burden patterns within the GBD framework rather than a causal reconstruction of individual disease progression pathways. Although these conditions are biologically related, the present analysis reflects population-level structural relationships across disease categories and should not be interpreted as evidence of deterministic clinical trajectories.

In conclusion, the global burden of liver disease remains considerable, with increases in new cases, deaths, and DALYs, despite an overall decline in age-standardized burdens in 1990–2021. While infection-related liver diseases continue to contribute significantly to the overall burden, the growing impact of MASLD/MASH and alcohol-related liver disease—particularly in high-SDI regions—suggests an ongoing epidemiological shift toward metabolic and behavioral risk factors. Marked regional and demographic disparities further highlight the need for targeted prevention and control strategies tailored to local healthcare capacity, socioeconomic context, and disease prevalence. However, given that the present analysis relies on modeled GBD estimates derived from heterogeneous data sources, the findings should be interpreted as indicative of broad epidemiological patterns rather than precise measurements of disease burden.

Limitations of the study

This study systematically assessed the liver disease burdens with the full GBD three-level hierarchy, yet some limitations must be noted. First, although the analysis covers the main liver diseases, conditions such as autoimmune hepatitis, primary biliary cholangitis, and primary sclerosing cholangitis were not fully investigated with separate cause of death items. Second, the GBD 2021 estimates are largely model-based, particularly for countries and territories with sparse or incomplete primary data.7 Although the GBD framework applies standardized statistical modeling and uncertainty quantification to improve cross-regional comparability, the reliability of estimates may vary depending on the availability and quality of underlying data sources. In low-SDI regions with limited registry sites, disease burden estimates rely more heavily on predictive covariate-based modeling, which may introduce systematic bias. For example, infection-related etiologies may be underestimated where surveillance data are incomplete, whereas estimates in high-income regions are likely to be more precise due to better data coverage. These heterogeneities should therefore be considered when interpreting cross-regional comparisons, particularly for locations with limited primary data inputs. Third, the slow and latent progression of MASLD/MASH, as well as other chronic liver diseases, might lead to their underreported burden metrics.32,33 Finally, the absence of detailed information on disease stage limits more granular analyses. Despite these limitations, the present analysis provides a comprehensive structural overview of global liver disease burden.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study utilized publicly available data from the GBD 2021 study. As a secondary analysis of de-identified data, this study did not require additional ethical approval or informed consent.

Method details

Data source

The GBD 2021 study provides systematic estimates of diseases, injuries, and risk factors globally in 1990-2021, enabling comprehensive assessment of disease burden.7 The ASRs and absolute numbers of the incidence, mortality, and DALYs attributable to liver diseases, disaggregated by sexes, age-groups, locations, and risk factors, were retrieved from the GHDx 2021 (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/). To contextualize GBD-based estimates, we further examined data from independent international sources, including the WHO Mortality Database (https://platform.who.int/mortality), Global Health Observatory (https://data.who.int/indicators), and Global Cancer Observatory (https://gco.iarc.fr/overtime/en).

GBD region and SDI

There were 21 regions and seven super-regions, divided by GBD collaborators mainly according to the sociodemographic levels and geographical locations, involved in this study (detailed in Table S9).7 The SDIs, considering the fertility, education, and income levels of a certain location,7 serve as composite indicators with a scale from 0 to 1 designed to reflect the social and economic conditions that impact health outcomes in different locations, where an SDI value approaching 1 represents the high level of development.

Selection of liver diseases and risk factors

This study analyzed liver disease using a three-level hierarchical framework based on the GBD cause classification. The first level represented total liver diseases. The second level included three major liver disease categories: acute hepatitis, cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases (referred to as cirrhosis in this study), and liver cancer. The third level further classified each category by specific etiologies defined in the GBD cause hierarchy.

We selected the most detailed risk factors attributable to ASDR in GBD 2021, and identified five risk factors related to liver diseases: alcohol use, drug use, smoking, high BMI, and high fasting plasma glucose. These risk factors are defined as “attribute or exposure causally associated with increased incidence or prevalence of cause”.

Burden metrics

The burden of liver diseases was assessed using incidence, deaths, and DALYs. DALYs were used to quantify the overall burden of disease. DALYs are defined as the sum of years of life lost (YLLs) due to premature mortality and years lived with disability (YLDs).7 YLLs were calculated by multiplying deaths by the standard life expectancy at the age of death. YLDs were estimated using age-, sex-, location-, and year-specific prevalence of disease sequelae and their corresponding disability weights derived within the GBD modelling framework. To enable comparisons across populations with different age structures, ASIR, ASDR, and ASDALY rate were used. These ASRs were calculated using the GBD standard population and are reported per 100,000 population. GBD estimates incorporate uncertainty from multiple data sources and modeling processes. To ensure internal consistency and comparability across locations and time, epidemiological parameters are estimated using standardized modeling approaches, including DisMod-MR 2.1, spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression (ST-GPR), and the Cause of Death Ensemble model (CODEm).7 In regions with missing or sparse primary data, these approaches synthesize available information by borrowing strength across time, geographical locations, and relevant covariates to produce complete and comparable estimates. Uncertainty in estimates is quantified using 500 draws, with 95% uncertainty intervals defined by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the simulated distributions.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Joinpoint regression analysis was used to identify the transition points (calendar years, namely the Joinpoints) in the temporal trends of liver diseases epidemiology, with significance tests performed using the Monte Carlo permutation method. The longitudinal data were therefore divided into several segments by Joinpoints, and average annual percentage changes (AAPCs), as a description of overall trends, were calculated by the weighted sum of annual percentage changes (APCs) in each segment.43

The NORDPRED model was used to predict future trends in liver disease ASRs. With an assumption of Poisson distribution for rates, NORDPRED applies an age–period–cohort modelling framework to extrapolate recent trends based on the latest 10-year data. To avoid unrealistic long-term extrapolation, the projected linear trend is progressively attenuated by 25%, 50%, and 75% in successive prediction periods. Consistent with the standard implementation of the Nordpred approach, the projections represent trend-based point estimates and should be interpreted as indicative of potential future patterns rather than precise forecasts.44,45

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (R 4.4.1), with visualizations created in R software (R 4.4.1) and GraphPad Prism 9.0.0.121.
