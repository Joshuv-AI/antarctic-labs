Natural Policy Gradient as Doubly Smoothed Policy Iteration: A Bellman-Operator Framework
Phalguni Nanda, Zaiwei Chen Edwardson School of Industrial Engineering, Purdue University

Abstract
In this work, we show that natural policy gradient, a core algorithm in reinforcement learning, admits an exact formulation as a smoothed and averaged form of policy iteration. Specifically, we introduce doubly smoothed policy iteration (DSPI), a Bellman-operator framework in which each policy is obtained by applying a regularized greedy step to a weighted average of past Q Q -functions. DSPI includes policy iteration, dual-averaged policy iteration, natural policy gradient, and more general policy dual averaging methods as special cases. Using only monotonicity and contraction of smoothed Bellman operators, we prove distribution-free global geometric convergence of DSPI. Consequently, standard natural policy gradient and policy dual averaging achieve an iteration complexity of 𝒪 ⁡ ( ( 1 − γ ) − 1 ​ log ⁡ ( ( 1 − γ ) − 1 ​ ϵ − 1 ) ) \mathcal{O}((1-\gamma)^{-1}\log((1-\gamma)^{-1}\epsilon^{-1})) for computing an ϵ \epsilon -optimal policy, without modifying the MDP, adding regularization beyond the mirror map inherent in the update, or using adaptive, trajectory-dependent stepsizes. For the unregularized greedy case, corresponding to dual-averaged policy iteration, we also prove finite termination. The same Bellman-operator framework further extends to discounted MDPs with linear function approximation and stochastic shortest path problems.

In the last decade, reinforcement learning (RL)
[

In the modern era of sequential decision making, it was recognized that an MDP can be formulated as a continuous optimization problem over the policy space. From this perspective, the policy gradient (PG) method was introduced
[
41
]
. Building on this viewpoint and further exploiting the geometry of the policy space, the natural policy gradient (NPG) method, along with more general approaches such as policy mirror ascent (PMA) and policy dual averaging (PDA), was developed

,

. Over time, practical variants of these gradient-based methods, such as trust region policy optimization
[

]
and proximal policy optimization
[

, have become dominant approaches in large-scale applications
[

,

The empirical success of NPG and its variants has motivated extensive theoretical work on their convergence properties. Existing approaches for studying NPG can be broadly categorized into two groups. One prominent line of work adopts a gradient-based optimization viewpoint, leveraging tools such as the policy gradient theorem, the performance difference lemma, the Polyak Łojasiewicz condition
[
41
,
1
]
, and techniques from continuous optimization
[
26
,
44
]
. A second line of work views NPG as an approximation of PI. Indeed, the original NPG paper
[
24
, Theorem 2]
observed that, as the stepsize approaches infinity, NPG recovers PI. Following this perspective, NPG has been analyzed as an approximation of PI by leveraging properties of the Bellman operator and separately bounding the approximation error
[
15
,
8
]
.

Despite substantial progress, existing analyses still leave open a conceptual gap. The defining structural property of discounted MDPs is the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm contraction of the Bellman operator, which underlies the design and analysis of classical methods such as VI and PI. This property is not naturally visible when NPG is analyzed as a continuous optimization method over the policy space. Consequently, many optimization-based analyses either do not establish global geometric convergence
[
1
]
or achieve it by introducing additional regularization into the MDP or the algorithm to induce curvature
[
13
,
26
,
38
]
. In contrast, approaches that view NPG as an approximation of PI
[
10
,
15
]
exploit Bellman operator properties and obtain distribution-free geometric convergence without additional regularization, but require stepsizes chosen adaptively based on the algorithm trajectory to control the approximation error. See Section
1.1
for further details.

More fundamentally, a unified perspective connecting classical dynamic programming methods based on Bellman iteration, such as PI, with modern gradient-based methods, such as NPG and PDA, remains lacking at both the algorithmic and analytical levels. In this paper, we address this gap by introducing
doubly smoothed policy iteration
(DSPI), a unified framework that includes PI, dual-averaged PI, NPG, and PDA as special cases. This perspective enables a unified analysis that yields strong guarantees for a broad class of algorithms. We summarize our contributions below.

•

Doubly Smoothed Policy Iteration.
We introduce DSPI, in which the policy is updated via a regularized greedy step with respect to a weighted average of past
Q

Crucially, for NPG and PDA, interpreting these gradient-based methods as smoothed and averaged variants of PI allows the updates to be expressed via the smoothed Bellman operator, thereby enabling a principled analysis based on its properties.

•

2  Background

Consider an infinite horizon discounted MDP defined by the tuple
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,\mathcal{R},\gamma)
, where
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
denote the finite state and action spaces, respectively,
\{p(\cdot\mid s,a)\}_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}
denotes the transition kernel,
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
is the reward function, and
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor. Throughout, we denote
n=|\mathcal{S}|
and
m=|\mathcal{A}|
.

Given a policy
\pi:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
, where
\Delta(\mathcal{A})
denotes the set of probability distributions supported on
\mathcal{A}
, the
Q
-function
Q^{\pi}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\mathcal{R}(S_{k},A_{k})\,\middle|\,S_{0}=s,A_{0}=a\right],\quad\forall\,(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A},

where the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\ \cdot\ ]
is taken with respect to the randomness in both the action selection
A_{k}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid S_{k})
and the state transitions
S_{k+1}\sim p(\cdot\mid S_{k},A_{k})
. With
Q^{\pi}
defined above, the value function
V^{\pi}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as
V^{\pi}(s)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)Q^{\pi}(s,a)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Since the MDP has finitely many states and actions,
Q^{\pi}
and
V^{\pi}
can be equivalently viewed as vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{mn}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, respectively.

A policy
\pi^{*}
is
optimal
if
V^{*}:=V^{\pi^{*}}\geq V^{\pi}
for all
\pi
, or equivalently,
Q^{*}:=Q^{\pi^{*}}\geq Q^{\pi}
for all
\pi
. Throughout this paper, inequalities between vectors are understood componentwise. For notational simplicity, for any vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}
(e.g., representing a policy or a
Q
-function), we denote by
x(s)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
the vector whose
a
-th entry is
x(s,a)
.

2.1  The Bellman Equation and Policy Iteration

The key to solving an MDP lies in the Bellman equation. We next present the Bellman equations for policy evaluation and policy optimization. To facilitate the connection between PI and NPG developed later in this paper, we express these equations in terms of
Q
-functions.

Given a policy
\pi
, its
Q
-function
Q^{\pi}
is the unique solution to the Bellman equation
Q=\mathcal{H}^{\pi}(Q)
, where
\mathcal{H}^{\pi}:\mathbb{R}^{mn}\to\mathbb{R}^{mn}
is the Bellman operator associated with policy
\pi
, defined as

\displaystyle[\mathcal{H}^{\pi}(Q)](s,a)=\mathcal{R}(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a^{\prime}\mid s^{\prime})Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}),\quad\forall\,(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}.

Similarly, the optimal
Q
-function
Q^{*}
is the unique solution to the Bellman optimality equation
Q=\mathcal{H}(Q)
, where
\mathcal{H}:\mathbb{R}^{mn}\to\mathbb{R}^{mn}
is the Bellman optimality operator defined as

\displaystyle[\mathcal{H}(Q)](s,a)=\mathcal{R}(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}),\quad\forall\,(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}.

Moreover, once
Q^{*}
is obtained, an optimal policy
\pi^{*}
can be computed by choosing actions greedily with respect to
Q^{*}
. The operators
\mathcal{H}^{\pi}
and
\mathcal{H}
enjoy several useful properties. In particular, both are contraction mappings with respect to the
\ell_{\infty}
norm with contraction factor
\gamma
, and are monotone in the sense that
F(Q_{1})\leq F(Q_{2})
whenever
Q_{1}\leq Q_{2}
, where
F
denotes either
\mathcal{H}^{\pi}
or
\mathcal{H}

[
32
,
7
]
. One should not confuse the definition of monotonicity here with the notion of monotonicity in variational inequalities
[
33
]
. These two properties enable the design and analysis of several classical algorithms, such as VI and PI.

When expressed in terms of
Q
-functions, the PI algorithm can be compactly written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k+1}}(Q^{\pi_{k}})=\mathcal{H}(Q^{\pi_{k}}).

(2.1)

Note that (
2.1
) does not define a unique update. In particular,
\pi_{k+1}
can be any policy such that, when its associated Bellman operator
\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k+1}}
is applied to the previous
Q
-function
Q^{\pi_{k}}
, the result coincides with applying the Bellman optimality operator to
Q^{\pi_{k}}
. In view of the definition of the Bellman operators, one concrete way to implement the PI update in (
2.1
) is

\displaystyle\pi_{k+1}(s)\in\argmax_{\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})}\{\mu^{\top}Q^{\pi_{k}}(s)\},\quad\forall\,s\in\mathcal{S},

(2.2)

where, according to our notation,
\pi_{k+1}(s)
(respectively,
Q^{\pi_{k}}(s)
) denotes the
m
-dimensional vector whose
a
-th entry is
\pi_{k+1}(a\mid s)
(respectively,
Q^{\pi_{k}}(s,a)
).

The global geometric convergence of PI is well established in the literature
[
32
]
. The analysis proceeds in two main steps: (1) using the monotonicity of the Bellman operators to show that the policies are monotonically improving, i.e.,
Q^{\pi_{k}}\leq Q^{\pi_{k+1}}
; (2) using the contraction property of the Bellman operators to show that the convergence error
\|Q^{*}-Q^{\pi_{k}}\|_{\infty}
admits a contractive recursion. For completeness, we provide an analysis of PI in Appendix
B.1
.

3  Doubly Smoothed Policy Iteration

To present DSPI, we begin by introducing the concept of smoothed Bellman operators. Let
\nu:\Delta(\mathcal{A})\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded, non-negative, and concave function; examples include the Shannon entropy, the Tsallis entropy, the shifted negative squared norm, and the zero function. Given a scalar
\eta\geq 0
, let the smoothed Bellman optimality operator
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}:\mathbb{R}^{mn}\to\mathbb{R}^{mn}
be defined as

\displaystyle[\mathcal{H}_{\eta}(Q)](s,a)=\mathcal{R}(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\max_{\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})}(\mu^{\top}Q(s^{\prime})+\eta\nu(\mu)),\quad\forall\,(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}.

Given a policy
\pi
and a scalar
\eta\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}^{\pi}:\mathbb{R}^{mn}\to\mathbb{R}^{mn}
be defined as

\displaystyle[\mathcal{H}_{\eta}^{\pi}(Q)](s,a)=\mathcal{R}(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)(\pi(s^{\prime})^{\top}Q(s^{\prime})+\eta\nu(\pi(s^{\prime}))),\quad\forall\,(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}.

Note that
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}
(respectively,
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}^{\pi}
) reduces to
\mathcal{H}
(respectively,
\mathcal{H}^{\pi}
) when
\eta=0
. Moreover, the smoothed Bellman operators are also contraction mappings and are monotonic (cf. Appendix
B.2
).

With the smoothed Bellman operators in place, DSPI is presented in Algorithm

Proposition
3.1

This perspective allows us to build on Bellman’s foundational insights, such as the contraction and monotonicity of the Bellman operator, to establish strong convergence guarantees for NPG via the analysis of DSPI.

Policy Dual Averaging.
Since NPG is a special case of PDA when the divergence-generating function is chosen as the negative entropy

Dual-Averaged Policy Iteration.
DSPI is more general than PDA, as it allows choosing
\nu(\cdot)\equiv 0
. In this case, Algorithm
2

, where the averaging is performed in the policy space, whereas in dual-averaged PI the averaging is performed in the
Q

4  Analysis

This section presents our main theoretical results. We begin with the convergence rates of DSPI in Section
4.1

4.1  Convergence Rates of Doubly Smoothed Policy Iteration

The following theorem presents the asymptotic convergence and convergence rates of the DSPI algorithm. The proof of Theorem

.

Consider
\{\pi_{k}\}
generated by Algorithm

1.

When

When choosing

4.2  Convergence Rates of Natural Policy Gradient and Policy Dual Averaging

As Proposition

shows NPG as a special case of the DSPI algorithm,

allows us to establish the geometric convergence of NPG below.

Theorem 4.3
.

Consider
\{\pi_{k}\}

\alpha_{k}=\beta\alpha_{0}/(1-\beta)^{k}

Remark 4.4
.

The geometrically increasing stepsize sequence

,

,

. Notably, Proposition

together with the decaying smoothing parameter

The proof of Theorem

and Theorem
4.1
, and is given in Appendix

Corollary 4.5

For any
\epsilon>0

and

for all

in Algorithm

\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{k}}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
provided that

.

Theorem
4.3
and Corollary
4.5
show that NPG enjoys global geometric convergence, measured by the optimality gap of the last iterate
\pi_{k}
. Moreover, the convergence bounds have several important features, which we discuss below.

Distribution-Free Convergence Rates.
In the existing literature on NPG, convergence rates typically depend on the initial distribution
\rho
, either explicitly or implicitly through a distribution mismatch coefficient
[
31
,
38
,
44
,
48
,
9
,
8
,
29
,
12
]
. In contrast, our convergence bound is expressed in terms of the
\ell_{\infty}
norm of the value-function gap and is free of such dependencies.

No Additional Regularization.
While our iteration complexity matches the state of the art, our result is obtained without modifying the MDP or the algorithm. Several existing analyses establishing geometric convergence for NPG either regularize the MDP
[
17
,
13
,
1
,
26
,
50
,
12
]
or introduce an additional strongly convex regularizer into the algorithm
[
26
,
30
,
27
]
. In contrast, we do not rely on any such modifications; the only regularization arises from the entropy term inherent in the standard NPG formulation. Our DSPI viewpoint, together with its Bellman-operator formulation (cf. Line 4 of Algorithm
2
), allows us to directly exploit the contraction property of the Bellman operator, thereby eliminating the need for additional curvature-inducing regularization.

A Simple Prespecified Stepsize Schedule.
Our results show that NPG converges at a geometric rate with a simple stepsize schedule
\alpha_{k}=\beta\alpha_{0}/(1-\beta)^{k}
, where
\beta\in(0,1)
is arbitrary. In contrast, existing results that view NPG as an approximation of PI
[
8
,
15
]
require adaptive stepsizes that depend on the value of
\pi_{k}(a_{k}^{*}\mid s)
, where
a_{k}^{*}\in\argmax_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q^{\pi_{k}}(s,a)
. Similarly, analyses that study NPG from an optimization viewpoint require stepsizes that depend on the Bellman error (also known as the advantage gap) of the initial policy
[
23
, Theorems 3.4 and 3.5]
.

Policy Dual Averaging.
Similarly, by choosing
\nu(\mu)=C-\omega(\mu)
,
where
\omega
is a bounded divergence-generating function and

C:=\max_{\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})}\omega(\mu)
, Algorithm
2

captures PDA.
Theorem
4.1
therefore implies global geometric convergence for PDA. The results and proofs are identical to those of Theorem
4.3
and are therefore omitted.

4.3  Finite Termination of Dual-Averaged Policy Iteration

When setting
\nu(\cdot)\equiv 0
in Algorithm
2
, the DSPI algorithm reduces to the dual-averaged PI, which updates the policies greedily with respect to the averaged
Q
-functions. As a special case of DSPI, the dual-averaged PI also enjoys geometric convergence (cf. Theorem
4.1
with
\nu_{\max}\equiv 0
). Moreover, we show that the algorithm terminates in a finite number of iterations with an optimal policy. This is presented in our next result.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\{\pi_{k}\}
be generated by Algorithm
2
with
\nu(\cdot)\equiv 0
and a deterministic
\pi_{0}
, where Line 4 selects a deterministic greedy policy according to a fixed tie-breaking rule. When
\beta_{0}=1
and
\beta_{k}\equiv\beta\in(0,1)
for all
k\geq 1
, the algorithm terminates after at most

n(m-1)\left\lceil\beta^{-1}(1-\gamma)^{-1}\log\left(2(1-\gamma)^{-1}\right)\right\rceil

iterations with an optimal policy, where
\lceil x\rceil
denotes the smallest integer greater than or equal to
x
.

Remark 4.7
.

When
\gamma
is treated as fixed, this yields a strongly polynomial bound in the numbers of states and actions, in the standard sense used for discounted MDP policy-iteration analyses
[
45
,
34
]
. More generally, the bound is polynomial in
n
,
m
, and the effective horizon
(1-\gamma)^{-1}
.

The proof of Theorem
4.6
is given in Appendix
B.5
. Following
[
34
]
, the key idea is to show that dual-averaged PI eliminates at least one suboptimal action at some state every
k^{*}
iterations, where
k^{*}=\lceil\beta^{-1}(1-\gamma)^{-1}\log(2(1-\gamma)^{-1})\rceil

n(m-1)
suboptimal actions, dual-averaged PI finds an optimal policy in at most
n(m-1)k^{*}
iterations.

4.4  Extensions

Although we have focused on discounted MDPs with a tabular representation, the generality of our framework allows both the algorithm and its analysis to extend naturally to other settings. We discuss these extensions below.

Natural Policy Gradient with Linear Function Approximation.
To overcome the curse of dimensionality, algorithms often incorporate function approximation (e.g., approximate dynamic programming). In Appendix
C
, we show that NPG with linear function approximation also admits a DSPI formulation, and we establish distribution-free global geometric convergence up to a function approximation error.

Natural Policy Gradient for Stochastic Shortest Path Problems.
Beyond the discounted setting, our framework also extends to stochastic shortest path problems, a special case of undiscounted MDPs. Classical results
[
7
]
show that, under mild assumptions, the Bellman operator is monotone and contractive with respect to a weighted
\ell_{\infty}
norm. Leveraging the DSPI formulation, we show that NPG for stochastic shortest path problems enjoys distribution-free global geometric convergence. See Appendix
D
for more details.

5  Proof of Theorem
4.1

Our proof proceeds in two steps: we first establish monotonic improvement of the
Q
-functions, and then derive and solve a contractive recursion for the convergence error.

Lemma 5.1
.

Algorithm
2
satisfies
Q^{\pi_{k}}\leq Q^{\pi_{k+1}}
for all
k\geq 0
.

Remark 5.2
.

By our convention,
Q^{\pi_{k}}\leq Q^{\pi_{k+1}}
means that
Q^{\pi_{k}}(s,a)\leq Q^{\pi_{k+1}}(s,a)
for all
(s,a)
. This vector-version monotonic improvement lemma is stronger than those in the literature on NPG, where monotonicity is established only for the scalar objective
V^{\pi_{k}}(\rho)

[
1
]
. This stronger form is important for leveraging the contraction property of the Bellman operator in the next step to derive a contractive recursion for the convergence error.

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

It suffices to show that
Q^{\pi_{k}}\leq\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k+1}}(Q^{\pi_{k}})
for all
k\geq 0
. Once this is established, repeatedly applying
\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k+1}}
to both sides and using its monotonicity yields
Q^{\pi_{k}}\leq[\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k+1}}]^{n}(Q^{\pi_{k}})
for all
n\geq 0
. Taking
n\to\infty
gives
Q^{\pi_{k}}\leq Q^{\pi_{k+1}}
.

For simplicity of notation, define
f:\mathbb{R}^{mn}\to\mathbb{R}^{mn}
by

[f(\pi)](s,a)=\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\nu(\pi(s^{\prime}))
for all
(s,a)
. Then, for any policy
\pi
and
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}
, we have
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}^{\pi}(Q)=\mathcal{H}^{\pi}(Q)+\eta f(\pi)
.
We now show that
Q^{\pi_{k}}\leq\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k+1}}(Q^{\pi_{k}})
for the two cases
k=0
and
k\geq 1
.

•

For
k=0
, since
\beta_{0}=1
, we have
\bar{Q}_{1}=Q^{\pi_{0}}
. It follows that
Q^{\pi_{0}}=\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{0}}(\bar{Q}_{1})=\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{0}}^{\pi_{0}}(\bar{Q}_{1})-\eta_{0}f(\pi_{0})
.
Since
\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{0}}^{\pi_{0}}(\bar{Q}_{1})\leq\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{0}}(\bar{Q}_{1})=\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{0}}^{\pi_{1}}(\bar{Q}_{1})
and

f(\pi_{1})\leq f(\pi_{0})
, where the latter follows from initializing

\pi_{0}(s)\in\argmax_{\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})}\nu(\mu)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
, we further obtain

\displaystyle Q^{\pi_{0}}\leq\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{0}}^{\pi_{1}}(\bar{Q}_{1})-\eta_{0}f(\pi_{1})=\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{1}}(\bar{Q}_{1})=\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{1}}(Q^{\pi_{0}}).

•

For
k\geq 1
, since
Q^{\pi_{k}}=\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k}}(Q^{\pi_{k}})
and
Q^{\pi_{k}}=(\bar{Q}_{k+1}-(1-\beta_{k})\bar{Q}_{k})/\beta_{k}
(cf. Line 3 of Algorithm
2
), we have

\displaystyle Q^{\pi_{k}}=\,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{k-1}}^{\pi_{k}}\!\left(\frac{\bar{Q}_{k+1}-(1-\beta_{k})\bar{Q}_{k}}{\beta_{k}}\right)-\eta_{k-1}f(\pi_{k})

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{k-1}}^{\pi_{k}}(\bar{Q}_{k+1})-\frac{(1-\beta_{k})}{\beta_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{k-1}}^{\pi_{k}}(\bar{Q}_{k})-\eta_{k-1}f(\pi_{k})

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{k}}^{\pi_{k}}(\bar{Q}_{k+1})-\frac{(1-\beta_{k})}{\beta_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{k-1}}^{\pi_{k}}(\bar{Q}_{k})+\left(\frac{(1-\beta_{k})\eta_{k-1}-\eta_{k}}{\beta_{k}}\right)f(\pi_{k})

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{k}}^{\pi_{k}}(\bar{Q}_{k+1})-\frac{(1-\beta_{k})}{\beta_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{k-1}}^{\pi_{k}}(\bar{Q}_{k}),

where the second equality follows from
\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{k-1}}^{\pi_{k}}
being an affine operator, and the last equality follows from
\eta_{k}=(1-\beta_{k})\eta_{k-1}
.

To proceed, observe that by Line 4 of Algorithm
2
, we have

\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{k}}^{\pi_{k}}(\bar{Q}_{k+1})\leq\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{k}}^{\pi_{k+1}}(\bar{Q}_{k+1})

and

\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{k-1}}^{\pi_{k}}(\bar{Q}_{k})\geq\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{k-1}}^{\pi_{k+1}}(\bar{Q}_{k})
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle Q^{\pi_{k}}\leq\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{k}}^{\pi_{k+1}}(\bar{Q}_{k+1})-\frac{(1-\beta_{k})}{\beta_{k}}\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{k-1}}^{\pi_{k+1}}(\bar{Q}_{k})

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k+1}}\!\left(\frac{\bar{Q}_{k+1}-(1-\beta_{k})\bar{Q}_{k}}{\beta_{k}}\right)+\left(\frac{\eta_{k}-(1-\beta_{k})\eta_{k-1}}{\beta_{k}}\right)f(\pi_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k+1}}(Q^{\pi_{k}}),

where the last equality follows from Algorithm
2
, Line 3, and
\eta_{k}=(1-\beta_{k})\eta_{k-1}
.

∎

With Lemma
5.1
in hand, we next derive a contractive recursion for
\bar{Q}_{k}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Algorithm
2
satisfies

\displaystyle\|Q^{*}-\bar{Q}_{k+1}\|_{\infty}\leq(1-(1-\gamma)\beta_{k})\|Q^{*}-\bar{Q}_{k}\|_{\infty}+\gamma\beta_{k}\eta_{k-1}\nu_{\max},\quad\forall\,k\geq 1.

Proof of Lemma
5.3
.

An immediate implication of Lemma
5.1
is that
\bar{Q}_{k}\leq Q^{\pi_{k}}
for all
k\geq 0
, since
\bar{Q}_{k}
is a weighted average of a monotonically increasing sequence.

Let
\mathbf{1}
denote the all-ones vector.
Using Algorithm
2
, Line 3, we obtain for all
k\geq 1
that

\displaystyle Q^{*}-\bar{Q}_{k+1}=\,

\displaystyle(1-\beta_{k})(Q^{*}-\bar{Q}_{k})+\beta_{k}(Q^{*}-Q^{\pi_{k}})

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle(1-\beta_{k})(Q^{*}-\bar{Q}_{k})+\beta_{k}(\mathcal{H}(Q^{*})-\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k}}(Q^{\pi_{k}}))

(5.1a)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle(1-\beta_{k})(Q^{*}-\bar{Q}_{k})+\beta_{k}(\mathcal{H}(Q^{*})-\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k}}(\bar{Q}_{k}))

(5.1b)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle(1-\beta_{k})(Q^{*}-\bar{Q}_{k})+\beta_{k}(\mathcal{H}(Q^{*})-\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{k-1}}^{\pi_{k}}(\bar{Q}_{k}))+\beta_{k}\eta_{k-1}f(\pi_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle(1-\beta_{k})(Q^{*}-\bar{Q}_{k})+\beta_{k}(\mathcal{H}(Q^{*})-\mathcal{H}(\bar{Q}_{k}))+\beta_{k}\eta_{k-1}f(\pi_{k})

(5.1c)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle(1-\beta_{k})\|Q^{*}-\bar{Q}_{k}\|_{\infty}\mathbf{1}+\beta_{k}\|\mathcal{H}(Q^{*})-\mathcal{H}(\bar{Q}_{k})\|_{\infty}\mathbf{1}+\gamma\beta_{k}\eta_{k-1}\nu_{\max}\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\bigl(1-(1-\gamma)\beta_{k}\bigr)\|Q^{*}-\bar{Q}_{k}\|_{\infty}\mathbf{1}+\gamma\beta_{k}\eta_{k-1}\nu_{\max}\mathbf{1},

(5.1d)

where (
5.1a
) follows from the Bellman equations, (
5.1b
) follows from
\bar{Q}_{k}\leq Q^{\pi_{k}}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k}}
being a monotonic operator, (
5.1c
) follows from
\mathcal{H}(\bar{Q}_{k})\leq\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{k-1}}(\bar{Q}_{k})=\mathcal{H}_{\eta_{k-1}}^{\pi_{k}}(\bar{Q}_{k})
, and (
5.1d
) follows from
\mathcal{H}
being a contraction mapping with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
.
Since the vector
Q^{*}-\bar{Q}_{k+1}
has non-negative entries, the desired inequality follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Repeatedly applying Lemma
5.3
, we obtain a bound on
\|Q^{*}-\bar{Q}_{k}\|_{\infty}
. The final step of the proof is to translate this bound into one on
\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{k}}\|_{\infty}
, and then solve the resulting recursion. This step involves only algebraic manipulations, and we defer the details to Appendix
B.4
. The proof is complete after this step.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

In view of the proof of Theorem
4.1
, although DSPI captures gradient-based policy optimization algorithms (e.g., NPG and PDA), its analysis differs fundamentally from approaches based on optimization techniques. It relies directly on Bellman’s core principles: the monotonicity of the Bellman operator yields monotonic improvement of the policies (cf. Lemma
5.1
), and its contraction property induces a contractive recursion for the convergence error (cf. Lemma
5.3
).

6  Conclusion

We introduce DSPI, an algorithmic framework that bridges classical dynamic programming algorithms, such as PI and dual-averaged PI, with modern policy optimization methods, such as NPG and PDA. This perspective enables a unified analysis based on monotonicity and contraction properties of the Bellman operator, yielding distribution-free global geometric convergence for NPG and PDA, and establishing finite termination for dual-averaged PI. The framework also extends to discounted MDPs with linear function approximation and to stochastic shortest path problems.

Looking ahead, our current analysis assumes access to the exact
Q
-function
Q^{\pi}
for each policy iterate, which isolates the policy-improvement dynamics. Extending DSPI to the model-free RL setting, where
Q^{\pi}
must be estimated from sampled trajectories (e.g., via temporal-difference learning or Monte Carlo rollouts
[
40
]
), is therefore a natural next step. While finite-time guarantees for such policy evaluation methods are well understood
[
39
,
10
,
16
]
, combining them with our framework to obtain sharp sample complexity results for model-free RL remains nontrivial. In particular, determining whether these variants can achieve minimax-optimal dependence on all problem parameters, namely
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2}(1-\gamma)^{-3}mn)

[
18
]
, is, to the best of our knowledge, still broadly open in the existing analysis of actor-critic methods, and is an important direction for future work.

B.1  The Geometric Convergence of Policy Iteration

Theorem B.1
.

PI (
2.1
) satisfies
\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{k}}\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma^{k}\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{0}}\|_{\infty}
for all
k\geq 0
.

Proof of Theorem
B.1
.

Our proof follows the two-step approach outlined in the last paragraph of Section
2.1
.

•

Step 1: Monotonic Improvement:
Using the Bellman equation, we have

Q^{\pi_{k}}=\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k}}(Q^{\pi_{k}})\leq\mathcal{H}(Q^{\pi_{k}})=\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k+1}}(Q^{\pi_{k}})
,
where the last equality follows from the update rule (
2.1
). Repeatedly applying
\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k+1}}
to both sides of

Q^{\pi_{k}}\leq\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k+1}}(Q^{\pi_{k}})
and using its monotonicity yields

Q^{\pi_{k}}\leq[\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k+1}}]^{n}(Q^{\pi_{k}})
for all
n\geq 0
.
Letting
n\to\infty
gives

Q^{\pi_{k}}\leq Q^{\pi_{k+1}}
.

•

Step 2: A Contractive Recursion:
Since
Q^{\pi_{k}}\leq Q^{\pi_{k+1}}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k+1}}
is monotonic, we have

\displaystyle Q^{*}-Q^{\pi_{k+1}}=\mathcal{H}(Q^{*})-\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k+1}}(Q^{\pi_{k+1}})\leq\mathcal{H}(Q^{*})-\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k+1}}(Q^{\pi_{k}})=\mathcal{H}(Q^{*})-\mathcal{H}(Q^{\pi_{k}}).

Since both sides are non-negative vectors, we obtain

\displaystyle\|Q^{*}-Q^{\pi_{k+1}}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\mathcal{H}(Q^{*})-\mathcal{H}(Q^{\pi_{k}})\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma\|Q^{*}-Q^{\pi_{k}}\|_{\infty},\quad\forall\,k\geq 0.

Solving this recursion yields
\|Q^{*}-Q^{\pi_{k}}\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma^{k}\|Q^{*}-Q^{\pi_{0}}\|_{\infty}
for all
k\geq 0
.

To translate the
Q
-function gap
\|Q^{\pi_{k}}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}
to the value function gap
\|V^{\pi_{k}}-V^{*}\|_{\infty}
, observe that on the one hand, we have

\displaystyle V^{*}(s)-V^{\pi_{k}}(s)=\,

\displaystyle\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q^{*}(s,a)-\pi_{k}(s)^{\top}Q^{\pi_{k}}(s)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q^{*}(s,a)-\pi_{k}(s)^{\top}Q^{\pi_{k-1}}(s)

(
Q^{\pi_{k-1}}\leq Q^{\pi_{k}}
for all
k\geq 1
)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q^{*}(s,a)-\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q^{\pi_{k-1}}(s,a)

(update rule (
2.1
))

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\|Q^{*}-Q^{\pi_{k-1}}\|_{\infty}.

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle Q^{*}(s,a)-Q^{\pi_{0}}(s,a)=\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\bigl(V^{*}(s^{\prime})-V^{\pi_{0}}(s^{\prime})\bigr)\leq\gamma\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{0}}\|_{\infty}.

Combining the above two inequalities with the convergence rate of
\|Q^{*}-Q^{\pi_{k}}\|_{\infty}
established in Step 2 yields
\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{k}}\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma^{k}\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{0}}\|_{\infty}
for all
k\geq 0
.
∎

B.2  Properties of the Smoothed Bellman Operators

Lemma B.2
.

For any policy
\pi
and
\eta\geq 0
, let
F
denote either
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}
or
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}^{\pi}
. Then, for any
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}
, we have

1.

\|F(Q_{1})-F(Q_{2})\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma\|Q_{1}-Q_{2}\|_{\infty}

2.

F(Q_{1})\leq F(Q_{2})
whenever
Q_{1}\leq Q_{2}
.

Proof of Lemma
B.2
.

We prove the result only for the smoothed Bellman optimality operator
\mathcal{H}_{\eta}

\mathcal{H}_{\eta}^{\pi}
follows by a similar argument.

1.

For any
(s,a)
, we have

\displaystyle\left|[\mathcal{H}_{\eta}(Q_{1})](s,a)-[\mathcal{H}_{\eta}(Q_{2})](s,a)\right|

\displaystyle=\,

Appendix D
Natural Policy Gradient for Stochastic Shortest Path Problems

Consider an infinite-horizon undiscounted MDP
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S}_{0},\mathcal{A},p,\mathcal{R})
, where
\mathcal{S}_{0}
is a finite state space that includes a terminal state,
\mathcal{A}
is a finite action space,
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{S}_{0}\times\mathcal{A}\to[-1,0]
is the reward function, and
p
is the transition kernel. We denote the terminal state by
s_{\text{tm}}
and define
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{0}\setminus\{s_{\text{tm}}\}
. Once the system reaches
s_{\text{tm}}
, it remains there and receives no further rewards, i.e.,
p(s_{\text{tm}}\mid s_{\text{tm}},a)=1
and
\mathcal{R}(s_{\text{tm}},a)=0
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
. As in the discounted setting, we denote
n=|\mathcal{S}|
and
m=|\mathcal{A}|
.

Given a stationary policy
\pi:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
, the expected total reward starting from a state
s\in\mathcal{S}
is defined as

\displaystyle V^{\pi}(s)=\limsup_{K\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{K}\mathcal{R}(S_{k},A_{k})\;\middle|\;S_{0}=s\right],

(D.1)

where
A_{k}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid S_{k})
and
S_{k+1}\sim p(\cdot\mid S_{k},A_{k})
.
A stationary policy
\pi^{*}
is said to be optimal if
V^{*}(s)\coloneqq V^{\pi^{*}}(s)\geq V^{\pi}(s)
for all stationary policies
\pi
.

To ensure termination under the optimal policy, the notion of a proper policy is commonly adopted in the stochastic shortest path (SSP) literature
[
46
,
47
,
6
,
14
]
. The formal definition of a proper policy is stated below.

Definition D.1
.

A policy
\pi
is said to be proper if, for any initial state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, there is a positive probability of reaching the terminal state within at most
n
stages, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}\{S_{n}\neq s_{\text{tm}}\mid S_{0}=s\}<1
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
.

The notion of a proper policy was standardized in the SSP literature following
[
6
]
, where it was used to relax the prevailing assumption that the reward function be either nonnegative or nonpositive. In particular,
[
6
]
required that there exists at least one proper policy, and for every improper policy
\pi
, there exists a state
s\in\mathcal{S}
such that
V^{\pi}(s)=-\infty
, and established asymptotic convergence of VI and PI. Under the same assumption, subsequent works have also established asymptotic convergence of Q-learning
[
42
,
46
]
and of variants of PI and Q-learning
[
47
]
. However, this assumption may not be sufficient for establishing convergence rates, which typically relies on a contraction property of the Bellman optimality operator
[
7
,
47
]
. In SSP setting, the Bellman optimality operator is only non-expansive with respect to the
\ell_{\infty}
norm. Nonetheless, it becomes a contraction with respect to a weighted
\ell_{\infty}
norm (cf. Lemma
D.10
) when all stationary policies are proper–a standard condition in the literature
[
6
,
7
,
47
]
when establishing contraction properties of the Bellman operator. We therefore adopt the same assumption, formally stated below.

Assumption D.2
.

All stationary policies are proper.

For a proper policy, since the MDP terminates in finite time, the limit in (
D.1
) exists.
Under Assumption
D.2
, it has been established in
[
6
]
that
there exists an optimal proper policy
\pi^{*}
for which
V^{*}(s)
is finite for every
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
Furthermore,
V^{*}
satisfies the Bellman equation

\displaystyle V^{*}(s)=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left\{\mathcal{R}(s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}}p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\,V^{*}(s^{\prime})\right\}.

For a proper policy
\pi
, the state-action value function
Q^{\pi}
is analogously defined as

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}(s,a)=\lim_{K\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{K}\mathcal{R}(S_{k},A_{k})\;\middle|\;S_{0}=s,A_{0}=a\right].

Under Assumption
D.2
, there exists a unique optimal state-action value function
Q^{*}
, which is related to the optimal value function via
V^{*}(s)=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q^{*}(s,a)
and uniquely solves the Bellman equation
Q=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(Q)

[
6
]
, where
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}:\mathbb{R}^{mn}\to\mathbb{R}^{mn}
is the Bellman optimality operator defined as

\displaystyle[\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(Q)](s,a)=\,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}),\quad\forall\,(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}.

Similarly, for a proper stationary policy
\pi
,
Q^{\pi}
uniquely solves the Bellman equation
Q=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi}(Q)
, where
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi}:\mathbb{R}^{mn}\to\mathbb{R}^{mn}
is the Bellman operator associated with
\pi
defined as

\displaystyle[\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi}(Q)](s,a)=\,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a^{\prime}\mid s^{\prime})Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}),\quad\forall\,(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}.

Observe that the sums in the above expressions are over
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{0}\setminus\{s_{\text{tm}}\}
. Further, due to absence of a discount factor, the Bellman optimality operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
and the Bellman operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi}
for arbitrary stationary policy
\pi
are only non-expansive with respect to the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm. Nonetheless, both
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi}
are monotonic: for
Q_{1}\leq Q_{2}
, we have
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(Q_{1})\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(Q_{2})
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi}(Q_{1})\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi}(Q_{2})
.

D.1  Natural Policy Gradient for Stochastic Shortest Path Problems

In comparison to the discounted setting, policy gradient methods in the SSP setting are relatively underexplored. Policy optimization algorithms for SSP problems have been analyzed by
[
14
]
in terms of regret bounds. Concerning last-iterate convergence, policy gradient methods for undiscounted total reward MDPs were studied by
[
28
]
only very recently. The NPG update for the SSP problem admits a similar formulation as that for the discounted problem
[
28
]
; we present it in Algorithm
6
.

Input:

\pi_{0}
as the uniform policy and
\theta_{0}=\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}
.

for

do

\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}+\alpha_{k}Q^{\pi_{k}}

4:

\pi_{k+1}(s)=\argmax_{\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})}\left\{\mu^{\top}\theta_{k+1}(s)+h(\mu)\right\}
for any

D.2  Reformulation as Doubly Smoothed Policy Iteration

The development of the DSPI algorithm for SSP problems follows the same roadmap as that for the discounted problems. To that end, we first introduce the smoothed versions of the Bellman operators for the SSP setting. Given a non-negative, bounded, and concave function
\nu:\Delta(\mathcal{A})\to\mathbb{R}
and
\eta\geq 0
, the smoothed Bellman optimality operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\eta}:\mathbb{R}^{mn}\to\mathbb{R}^{mn}
is defined as

\displaystyle[\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\eta}(Q)](s,a)=\,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\max_{\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})}\left\{\mu^{\top}Q(s^{\prime})+\eta\nu(\mu)\right\},\quad\forall\,(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}.

Similarly, given policy
\pi
, the smoothed Bellman operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi}_{\eta}:\mathbb{R}^{mn}\to\mathbb{R}^{mn}
associated with
\pi
is defined as

\displaystyle[\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi}_{\eta}(Q)](s,a)=\,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\left\{\pi(s^{\prime})^{\top}Q(s^{\prime})+\eta\nu(\pi(s^{\prime}))\right\},\quad\forall\,(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}.

Similar to the discounted setting, the smoothed Bellman operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi}_{\eta}
is related to
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi}
as
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi}_{\eta}(Q)=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi}(Q)+\eta g(\pi)
, where the operator
g:\mathbb{R}^{mn}\to\mathbb{R}^{mn}
is defined as

\displaystyle[g(\pi)](s,a)=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\;\nu(\pi(s^{\prime}))

for all
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
. Moreover, the operators
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\eta}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi}_{\eta}
for arbitrary stationary policy
\pi
and for
\eta\geq 0
are non-expansive with respect to the
\ell_{\infty}
norm and are monotonic. These properties can be established using arguments similar to those in Appendix
B.2
and hence are omitted.

With the smoothed versions of the Bellman operators defined, we can now formulate the DSPI algorithm for the SSP problem, which differs from that in the discounted setting only with respect to the Bellman operators. We state the algorithm below.

Algorithm 7

Doubly Smoothed Policy Iteration for Stochastic Shortest Path Problems

1:

Input:
Initialize
\pi_{0}(s)\in\argmax_{\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})}\nu(\mu)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
\bar{Q}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
.

2:

for

k=0,1,2,3,\cdots

do

3:

Maintain a running average of past
Q
-functions:
\bar{Q}_{k+1}=(1-\beta_{k})\bar{Q}_{k}+\beta_{k}Q^{\pi_{k}}
,
where
\beta_{k}\in(0,1]
is the stepsize.

4:

Choose
\pi_{k+1}
such that
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\eta_{k}}^{\pi_{k+1}}(\bar{Q}_{k+1})=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\eta_{k}}(\bar{Q}_{k+1})
,
where
\eta_{k}=\tau\prod_{j=1}^{k}(1-\beta_{j})
with
\tau\geq 0
being a tunable parameter.

5:

end

for

Observe that the policy update is now expressed in terms of the SSP analogue of the smoothed Bellman operators. A concrete way to implement the update step remains the same as that in the discounted setting:

\displaystyle\pi_{k+1}(s)\in\argmax_{\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})}\Big\{\mu^{\top}\bar{Q}_{k+1}(s)+\tau\prod_{j=1}^{k}(1-\beta_{j})\nu(\mu)\Big\},\quad\forall\,s\in\mathcal{S}.

(D.2)

However, as discussed in Section
3
, the Bellman operator viewpoint facilitates a simplified analysis and leads to improved convergence rates, leveraging the properties of the Bellman operators.

The DSPI algorithm includes the NPG algorithm as a special case, as stated below.

Proposition D.3
.

In Algorithm
7
, when choosing
\beta_{k}=\alpha_{k}/\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}
,
\tau=1/\alpha_{0}
, and
\nu(\cdot)=h(\cdot)
, the sequence of policies generated by Algorithm
7
and Algorithm
6
are identical.

Since the NPG and DSPI updates for the SSP problems remain the same as those for the discounted setting, the proof of the above result remains the same as that of Proposition
3.1
, and hence we omit it here.

D.3  Finite-Time Analysis of DSPI for Stochastic Shortest Path Problems

Following is the main result showing convergence of Algorithm
7
. For simplicity, we state it only for using a constant stepsize.

Theorem D.4
.

Consider the sequence
\{\pi_{k}\}
generated by Algorithm
7
. Let the stepsizes be chosen as
\beta_{0}=1
and
\beta_{k}\equiv\beta\in(0,1]
for all
k\geq 1
. Then, under Assumption
D.2
, there exists a
\kappa\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{k}}\|_{\infty}\leq(1-(1-\kappa)\beta)^{k-1}\left[\frac{\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{0}}\|_{\infty}+2\tau\nu_{\max}}{1-\kappa}\right],\quad\forall\,k\geq 1.

The above result shows that Algorithm
7
achieves global geometric convergence. The convergence rate is similar to that in discounted tabular setting, except for the factor
1/(1-\kappa)
which arises due to the Bellman optimality operator not being a contraction mapping with respect to the
\ell_{\infty}
norm. Moreover, it leads to the following iteration complexity.

Corollary D.5
.

Let
\beta_{0}=1
,
\beta_{k}=1/2
for
k\geq 1
, and
\tau=1/\nu_{\max}
in Algorithm
7
. Then for any
\epsilon>0
, to achieve
\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{k}}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
, the iteration complexity is
\mathcal{O}((1-\kappa)^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1}(1-\kappa)^{-1})
.

D.4  Finite-Time Analysis of NPG for Stochastic Shortest Path Problems

As a special case of DSPI, NPG also enjoys global geometric convergence, as stated in the following theorem.

Theorem D.6
.

Consider
\{\pi_{k}\}
generated by Algorithm
6
.
When choosing
\alpha_{0}=\log(m)
and
\alpha_{k}=\beta\alpha_{0}/(1-\beta)^{k}
for all
k\geq 1
and for any
\beta\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{k}}\|_{\infty}\leq(1-(1-\kappa)\beta)^{k-1}\left[\frac{\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{0}}\|_{\infty}+2}{1-\kappa}\right],\quad\forall\,k\geq 1.

The proof of Theorem
D.6
follows from combining Proposition
D.3
and Theorem
D.4
. To apply Proposition
D.3
, we first verify that
\{\alpha_{k}\}
as chosen in Theorem
D.6
corresponds to
\{\beta_{k}\}
as chosen in Theorem
D.4
. We then substitute the specific choices
\nu(\cdot)=h(\cdot)
and
\tau=1/\log(m)
. These steps follow the arguments in Appendix
B.3
and hence are omitted. We now state the iteration complexity of NPG.

Corollary D.7
.

Let
\alpha_{0}=\log(m)
and
\alpha_{k}=\log(m)\cdot 2^{k-1}
for all
k\geq 1
in Algorithm
6
. Then for any
\epsilon>0
, to achieve
\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{k}}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
the iteration complexity is
\mathcal{O}((1-\kappa)^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1}(1-\kappa)^{-1})
.

As discussed, the analysis of NPG for SSP problems has received relatively limited attention compared to the discounted setting. To the best of our knowledge, the only existing analysis of NPG in the SSP setting is due to
[
28
]
, where it is studied as part of a broader framework of policy gradient methods for undiscounted total-reward MDPs. For NPG,
[
28
]
establish an
\mathcal{O}(1/k)
convergence rate with a constant stepsize and a geometric convergence rate with adaptive stepsizes. Their analysis is based on the mirror descent framework of
[
44
]
, resulting in convergence rates that depend on a distribution mismatch coefficient; moreover, the adaptive stepsizes depend on the optimal policy. In our setting, Theorem
D.6
establishes distribution-free geometric convergence using a simple geometrically increasing stepsize. As mentioned earlier, policy gradient methods for SSP problems have also been studied by
[
14
]
, but in an episodic setting and from a regret perspective; therefore, their results are not directly comparable to ours.

D.5  Proof of Theorem
D.4

The proof follows the same line of argument as before, establishing monotonic policy improvement and a contractive recursion. The following lemma formalizes the monotonicity of the DSPI policy updates and is proved in Appendix
D.6.1
.

Lemma D.8
.

Under Assumption
D.2
, the policies
\pi_{k}
generated by Algorithm
7
satisfy
Q^{\pi_{k+1}}\geq Q^{\pi_{k}}
for all
k\geq 0
.

Remark D.9
.

Although we are assuming that all stationary policies are proper, monotonic policy improvement can be established under the assumption that (1)
\pi_{0}
is proper, and (2) for every improper policy
\pi
, there exists
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
such that
Q^{\pi}(s,a)=-\infty
. We present the proof in Appendix
D.7.4
. Such an assumption is similar to the assumptions formulated in
[
6
]
, where the authors require (1) the existence of at least one proper policy, and (2) that every improper policy must lead to
Q^{\pi}(s,a)=-\infty
for some
(s,a)
. However, as discussed, existing analyses require all policies being proper in order to establish convergence rate. The condition that
\pi_{0}
be proper is in view of the initialization in Algorithm
7
.

Following our approach thus far, the next step is to establish a contractive recursion, which relies on the contraction property of the Bellman optimality operator. However, unlike the discounted setting, the Bellman optimality operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
in the SSP setting is not a
\ell_{\infty}
norm contraction in general; rather, it is a contraction with respect to a weighted
\ell_{\infty}
norm
[
7
]
. We formally state this property in the following lemma.

Lemma D.10

(Proposition 2.2 of
[
7
]
)
.

Under Assumption
D.2
, there exists
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}
such that
\xi>\mathbf{1}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
is a
\kappa
-contraction with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\xi}
, where

\displaystyle\kappa=\max_{1\leq i\leq mn}\frac{\xi(i)-1}{\xi(i)}\in(0,1)\quad\text{and}\quad\|Q\|_{\xi}\coloneqq\max_{1\leq i\leq mn}\frac{|Q(i)|}{\xi(i)},\;\quad\forall\,Q\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}.

The following result shows the equivalence between the weighted
\ell_{\infty}
norm
\|\cdot\|_{\xi}
and the unweighted
\ell_{\infty}
norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
, which we prove in Appendix
D.7
.

Corollary D.11
.

It holds for any
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}
that
(1-\kappa)\|Q\|_{\infty}\leq\|Q\|_{\xi}\leq\|Q\|_{\infty}
.

For the remaining part of the proof, we denote
\kappa^{\prime}=\min\left\{1,\frac{\kappa}{1-\kappa}\right\}
. Now, we present below the contractive recursion with respect to the weighted
\ell_{\infty}
norm
\|\cdot\|_{\xi}
.

Lemma D.12
.

Under Assumption
D.2
, Algorithm
7
satisfies

\displaystyle\|Q^{\ast}-\bar{Q}_{k+1}\|_{\xi}\leq(1-(1-\kappa)\beta_{k})\|Q^{*}-\bar{Q}_{k}\|_{\xi}+\kappa^{\prime}\beta_{k}\eta_{k-1}\nu_{\max},\quad\forall\,k\geq 1.

Recursively applying the inequality in Lemma
D.12
gives

\displaystyle\|Q^{\ast}-\bar{Q}_{k+1}\|_{\xi}\leq\prod_{j=1}^{k}(1-(1-\kappa)\beta_{j})\|Q^{\ast}-\bar{Q}_{1}\|_{\xi}+\kappa^{\prime}\nu_{\max}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}\eta_{i-1}\prod_{j=i+1}^{k}(1-(1-\kappa)\beta_{j}).

(D.3)

To translate the above bound to one in terms of the value function, we apply the following lemma which is proved in Appendix
D.7.3
.

Lemma D.13
.

Let
\tilde{\xi}(s)=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\xi(s,a)
. Then, under Assumption
D.2
, Algorithm
7
satisfies

1.

\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{k}}\|_{\tilde{\xi}}\leq\|Q^{*}-\bar{Q}_{k}\|_{\infty}+\eta_{k-1}\nu_{\max}
for all
k\geq 1
;

2.

\|Q^{*}-\bar{Q}_{1}\|_{\infty}\leq\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{0}}\|_{\infty}
.

For
k\geq 1
, combining Lemma
D.13
with the bound in (
D.3
) yields

\displaystyle\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{k}}\|_{\tilde{\xi}}\leq\;

\displaystyle\underbrace{\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(1-(1-\kappa)\beta_{j})\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{0}}\|_{\infty}}_{\eqqcolon T_{1}}

\displaystyle\;+\underbrace{\kappa^{\prime}\nu_{\max}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\beta_{i}\eta_{i-1}\prod_{j=i+1}^{k-1}(1-(1-\kappa)\beta_{j})}_{\eqqcolon T_{2}}+\underbrace{\eta_{k-1}\nu_{\max}}_{\eqqcolon T_{3}}.

(D.4)

Recall that
\eta_{k-1}\coloneqq\tau\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(1-\beta_{j})
. Thus, when choosing
\beta_{k}\equiv\beta

\displaystyle T_{1}=(1-(1-\kappa)\beta)^{k-1}\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{0}}\|_{\infty}\quad\text{and}\quad T_{3}=\tau(1-\beta)^{k-1}\nu_{\max}.

The term
T_{2}
can be simplified as follows

\displaystyle\kappa^{\prime}\tau\nu_{\max}\beta\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(1-\beta)^{i-1}(1-(1-\kappa)\beta)^{k-i-1}

\displaystyle\;=\kappa^{\prime}\tau\nu_{\max}\beta(1-(1-\kappa)\beta)^{k-2}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{(1-\beta)^{i-1}}{(1-(1-\kappa)\beta)^{i-1}}

\displaystyle\;=\kappa^{\prime}\tau\nu_{\max}\beta(1-(1-\kappa)\beta)^{k-2}\frac{1-\left(\frac{(1-\beta)}{(1-(1-\kappa)\beta)}\right)^{k-1}}{1-\frac{(1-\beta)}{(1-(1-\kappa)\beta)}}

\displaystyle\;=\kappa^{\prime}\tau\nu_{\max}\beta\frac{\left[(1-(1-\kappa)\beta)^{k-1}-(1-\beta)^{k-1}\right]}{(1-\beta+\beta\kappa)-(1-\beta)}

\displaystyle\;=\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa}\tau\nu_{\max}\left[(1-(1-\kappa)\beta)^{k-1}-(1-\beta)^{k-1}\right].

Now, observe that
\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa}=\min\left\{1,\frac{\kappa}{1-\kappa}\right\}\frac{1}{\kappa}=\min\left\{\frac{1}{\kappa},\frac{1}{1-\kappa}\right\}\in(1,2)
for

.
Consequently, the bound on the value function gap simplifies as

\displaystyle\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{k}}\|_{\tilde{\xi}}\leq\;

\displaystyle(1-(1-\kappa)\beta)^{k-1}\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{0}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\;+\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa}\tau\nu_{\max}\left[(1-(1-\kappa)\beta)^{k-1}-(1-\beta)^{k-1}\right]+\tau\nu_{\max}(1-\beta)^{k-1}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle(1-(1-\kappa)\beta)^{k-1}\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{0}}\|_{\infty}+\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa}\tau\nu_{\max}(1-(1-\kappa)\beta)^{k-1}

\displaystyle\;+\nu_{\max}\left(1-\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa}\right)(1-\beta)^{k-1}

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle(1-(1-\kappa)\beta)^{k-1}\left[\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{0}}\|_{\infty}+2\tau\nu_{\max}\right].

where the last inequality is due to
\kappa^{\prime}/\kappa\in(1,2)
. Finally, by the equivalence of norms

and

\displaystyle\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{k}}\|_{\infty}\leq(1-(1-\kappa)\beta)^{k-1}\left[\frac{\|V^{*}-V^{\pi_{0}}\|_{\infty}+2\tau\nu_{\max}}{1-\kappa}\right].

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k}}_{\eta_{k}}(\bar{Q}_{k+1})\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\eta_{k}}(\bar{Q}_{k+1})=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k+1}}_{\eta_{k}}(\bar{Q}_{k+1});

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k}}_{\eta_{k-1}}(\bar{Q}_{k})=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\eta_{k-1}}(\bar{Q}_{k})\geq\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k+1}}_{\eta_{k-1}}(\bar{Q}_{k}).

Applying these in the bound on

yields

\displaystyle Q^{\pi_{k}}\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k+1}}_{\eta_{k}}(\bar{Q}_{k+1})-\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}-1\right)\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k+1}}_{\eta_{k-1}}(\bar{Q}_{k})

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k+1}}(\bar{Q}_{k+1})-\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}-1\right)\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k+1}}(\bar{Q}_{k})+\left(\frac{\eta_{k}-(1-\beta_{k})\eta_{k-1}}{\beta_{k}}\right)g(\pi_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k+1}}(\bar{Q}_{k+1})-\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}-1\right)\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k+1}}(\bar{Q}_{k})

(
\eta_{k}=(1-\beta_{k})\eta_{k-1}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k+1}}(Q^{\pi_{k}}),

where the last equality is due to
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k+1}}
being affine and Line 3 of Algorithm
7
.

Observe that since

For any

As argued in the proof of Lemma
5.3

\bar{Q}_{k}

\bar{Q}_{k}\leq Q^{\pi_{k}}
for all

\displaystyle Q^{\ast}-Q^{\pi_{k}}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(Q^{*})-\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k}}(Q^{\pi_{k}})

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(Q^{*})-\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k}}(\bar{Q}_{k})

being monotonic)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(Q^{*})-\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k}}_{\eta_{k-1}}(\bar{Q}_{k})+\eta_{k-1}g(\pi_{k})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(Q^{*})-\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\eta_{k-1}}(\bar{Q}_{k})+\eta_{k-1}g(\pi_{k})

(Algorithm

(
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(Q)\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\eta}

D.2

\pi

D.7.2

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\xi(s,a)}\left|Q^{\ast}(s,a)-Q^{\pi_{k}}(s,a)\right|

D.7.2  Bound on
g

By definition of the operator
g
, we have for every
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
that

\displaystyle[g(\pi)](s,a)=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\nu(\pi(s^{\prime})),\quad s\in\mathcal{S},\;a\in\mathcal{A}.

which satisfies
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi}_{\eta}(Q)=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi}(Q)+\eta g(\pi)
for any
\eta\geq 0
and any policy
\pi
.
Now, by definition of
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
, we see that

\displaystyle[\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbf{0})](s,a)=\mathcal{R}(s,a)\text{ and }[\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbf{1})](s,a)=\mathcal{R}(s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a).

According to Lemma
D.10
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
is a contraction mapping with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\xi}
and we thus have

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbf{1})-\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbf{0})\|_{\xi}=\max_{s,a}\frac{\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)}{\xi(s,a)}\leq\kappa\|\mathbf{1}\|_{\xi}\leq\kappa\|\mathbf{1}\|_{\infty}=\kappa.

Since
1/\xi(s,a)\geq 1/[\max_{s,a}\xi(s,a)]
for every
(s,a)
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\max_{s,a}\xi(s,a)}\max_{s,a}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{s,a}\frac{\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)}{\xi(s,a)}\leq\kappa

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\max_{s,a}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa\max_{s,a}\xi(s,a)\leq\frac{\kappa}{1-\kappa}.

Note that
\kappa/(1-\kappa)>1
only if
\kappa>1/2
. Since
p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\in[0,1)
,
\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\leq 1
,
and we get

\displaystyle[g(\pi)](s,a)=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\nu(\pi(s^{\prime}))\leq\nu_{\max}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\leq\nu_{\max}\min\left\{1,\frac{\kappa}{1-\kappa}\right\}=\nu_{\max}\kappa^{\prime}.

for every
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, and hence
g(\pi)\leq\kappa^{\prime}\nu_{\max}\mathbf{1}
.

D.7.4  Monotonic Improvement Under A Weaker Assumption

We first recall the assumptions from Remark
D.9
.

Assumption D.14
.

1.

The policy
\pi_{0}
is proper.

2.

For every improper policy
\pi
, there exists
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
such that
Q^{\pi}(s,a)=-\infty
.

Under this assumption, we first establish the following result, whose proof follows arguments similar to those of
[
6
, Proposition 2.4]
.

Lemma D.15
.

Let Assumption
D.14
hold. Let
\pi
be a proper policy and
\pi^{\prime}
be any policy such that
Q^{\pi}\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi^{\prime}}(Q^{\pi})
. Then
\pi^{\prime}
is also a proper policy.

Proof.

We prove by contradiction. Suppose
\pi^{\prime}
is not proper.
First, observe that by monotonicity of
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi^{\prime}}
,
Q^{\pi}\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi^{\prime}}(Q^{\pi})\leq[\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi^{\prime}}]^{2}(Q^{\pi})\leq\cdots\leq[\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi^{\prime}}]^{n}(Q^{\pi})
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Now, for any
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi^{\prime}}(Q)=\mathcal{R}+\mathcal{P}^{\pi^{\prime}}Q
,
where
\mathcal{P}^{\pi^{\prime}}
is a
mn\times mn
matrix with
\mathcal{P}^{\pi^{\prime}}((s,a),(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}))=p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\pi^{\prime}(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})
for
(s,a),(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
.
Consequently,
[\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi^{\prime}}]^{n}
can be written as

\displaystyle[\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi^{\prime}}]^{n}(Q)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi^{\prime}}[\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi^{\prime}}]^{n-1}(Q)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}+\mathcal{P}^{\pi^{\prime}}[\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi^{\prime}}]^{n-1}(Q)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}+\mathcal{P}^{\pi^{\prime}}[\mathcal{R}+\mathcal{P}^{\pi^{\prime}}[\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi^{\prime}}]^{n-2}(Q)]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}+\mathcal{P}^{\pi^{\prime}}\mathcal{R}+(\mathcal{P}^{\pi^{\prime}})^{2}[\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi^{\prime}}]^{n-2}(Q).

Continuing in the above way thus leads to

\displaystyle[\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi^{\prime}}]^{n}(Q)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(\mathcal{P}^{\pi^{\prime}})^{i}\mathcal{R}+(\mathcal{P}^{\pi^{\prime}})^{n}Q.

Also notice that for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
,
(\mathcal{P}^{\pi^{\prime}})^{i}((s,a),(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}))
is the probability of going from
(s,a)
to
(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})
in
i
-transitions. Thus, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}[(\mathcal{P}^{\pi^{\prime}})^{i}\mathcal{R}](s,a)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{R}(S_{i},A_{i})\mid S_{0}=s,A_{0}=a].

Since,
\pi^{\prime}
is improper, by Assumption
D.14
(2), there exists
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
such that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}[(\mathcal{P}^{\pi^{\prime}})^{i}\mathcal{R}](s,a)=-\infty,

which implies
\limsup_{n}[(\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi^{\prime}})^{n}(Q)](s,a)=-\infty
. Therefore, we have
Q^{\pi}(s,a)=-\infty
, which is a contradiction given
\pi
is proper. Hence,
\pi^{\prime}
cannot be improper.
∎

With the above lemma in hand, it is now enough to show that
\pi_{k}
’s are proper and
Q^{\pi_{k}}\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k+1}}(Q^{\pi_{k}})
for every
k\geq 0
. Indeed, this implies that
\pi_{k+1}
is proper by Lemma
D.15
, and
Q^{\pi_{k+1}}
will be the unique solution to the Bellman equation
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k+1}}(Q)=Q
. Following the same arguments as before, we can apply the monotonicity of
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k+1}}
to get
Q^{\pi_{k}}\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k+1}}(Q^{\pi_{k}})\leq[\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k+1}}]^{2}(Q^{\pi_{k}})\leq\ldots\leq[\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k+1}}]^{n}(Q^{\pi_{k}})
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
Q^{\pi_{k+1}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}[\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{k+1}}]^{n}(Q^{\pi_{k}})
, we will obtain
Q^{\pi_{k}}\leq Q^{\pi_{k+1}}
.

To prove the claim, we proceed as follows. Starting from
\pi_{0}
, the arguments in Appendix
D.6.1
(1) imply that
Q^{\pi_{0}}\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\pi_{1}}(Q^{\pi_{0}})
. Since
\pi_{0}
is proper, it follows from Lemma
D.15
that
\pi_{1}
is also proper. Next, the arguments in Appendix
D.6.1
(2) ensure that

and hence Lemma
D.15
implies that
\pi_{2}
is proper. Continuing inductively, applying the arguments in Appendix
D.6.1
(2) and Lemma
D.15
, we can conclude that
\pi_{k}
’s are proper and
Q^{\pi_{k}}\leq\mathcal{H}^{\pi_{k+1}}(Q^{\pi_{k}})
for every
k\geq 0
.
