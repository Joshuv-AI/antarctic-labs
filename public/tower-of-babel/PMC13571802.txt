Prenatal Spectrum of COL2A1‐Related Spondyloepiphyseal Dysplasia Congenita: A Review and Two Case Reports
Authors: López‐Rodríguez Larissa, León‐Madero Luis Felipe, Jiménez‐Chaidez Salvador, Gallardo‐Gaona Juan Manuel, Rodríguez‐Sibaja María Jose, Castañeda‐de‐la‐Fuente Angelica, Del Castillo‐Reyes Katia Alejandra, Yañez‐Felix Ana Lucia, Morales‐Dominguez Valeria, Borboa‐Olivares Héctor Jesús, Sevilla‐Montoya Rosalba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo review the published literature on prenatal findings of COL2A1‐related SEDC, summarizing reported imaging and molecular variants, and to describe two additional prenatal cases evaluated at a tertiary referral center.MethodA narrative review with a systematic search strategy was conducted to analyze prenatal imaging findings, gestational age at detection, and molecular diagnoses in cases of COL2A1‐related SEDC. Clinical and molecular description of two additional fetal cases with postnatal molecular confirmation were also included.ResultsGestational age at initial detection ranged from 11 to 28 weeks. Five reported cases occurred in a familial setting, while two were initially considered sporadic. In several cases, complementary imaging modalities were required to better characterize vertebral and ossification defects. A clear genotype–phenotype correlation could not be reliably established; no specific molecular pattern reliably predicted the severity of prenatal imaging findings or perinatal outcomes.ConclusionOur findings highlight the nonspecific nature of prenatal manifestations in COL2A1‐related SEDC, which despite being largely consistent across cases do not allow for a specific diagnosis without molecular confirmation or a known family history. These challenges underscore the need for a comprehensive diagnostic strategy that integrates detailed imaging, molecular genetic testing, and close collaboration within a multidisciplinary team.

Introduction

COL2A1 gene encodes for the type II collagen, specifically producing the alpha‐1 chain of procollagen type II, a major component of collagen expressed in chondrocytes and vitreous humor [1, 2]. Type II collagen is essential for endochondral bone formation and growth, especially in the cartilage growth plate [2]. Variants in the COL2A1 gene cause a wide variety of rare autosomal‐dominant conditions known as type II collagenopathies [3]. According to the Nosology and Classification of Genetic Skeletal Disorders, Type 2 collagenopathies encompass a wide phenotypic spectrum, ranging from lethal perinatal conditions such as achondrogenesis type II (ACG2; OMIM #200610) to non‐lethal entities including Stickler syndrome type 1 (OMIM #108300) [4].

Fetal bone dysplasias are suspected during routine ultrasound when limb shortening is identified; however, complementary evaluation with additional imaging techniques, such as low‐dose fetal computed tomography or, where available, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), may provide valuable supplementary information when ultrasound findings remain inconclusive [5]. During prenatal evaluation, the timing of the shortening is important as some cases present early, whereas others have a later onset [6]. Assessing bone structure for bowing, fracturing and bone mineralization is essential. After complete and thorough evaluation of the long bones, systematic examination of the remainder of the fetal anatomy may reveal additional diagnostic clues. These include assessment of the hands and feet for polydactyly, brachydactyly, or positional anomalies; evaluation of skull mineralization, shape and size; facial profile for micrognathia or midface hypoplasia; thoracic dimensions and rib length for prediction of pulmonary hypoplasia; vertebral echogenicity and ossification; and the presence of polyhydramnios, all of which contribute to narrowing the differential diagnosis of skeletal dysplasia [7].

According to clinical severity, they can be divided into three main groups: lethal, severe non‐lethal and intermediate/mild non‐lethal forms [8]. Molecular genetic confirmation of diagnosis can substantially improve prediction and prognostic assessment and is particularly valuable for parental counseling in cases where imaging findings alone are insufficient to establish a specific diagnosis; clinical geneticist involvement in prenatal evaluation is therefore strongly encouraged.

The association between spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia congenita (SEDC) and the COL2A1 gene was established in the late 1980s, when Anderson and colleagues confirmed linkage to chromosome 12q [9]. Shortly thereafter, the first pathogenic variants in COL2A1 associated with SEDC were identified between 1989 and 1993 [10, 11, 12]. Early insights into the prenatal manifestations were provided by Kirk [13], who described antenatal sonographic features although molecular confirmation was not available. The first reported prenatal molecular diagnosis of a COL2A1‐related SEDC was achieved by Tiller in 1995 [14], a milestone in the integration of fetal imaging and molecular genetics for the prenatal diagnosis of type II collagenopathies.

Given the wide genotypic and phenotypic spectrum and the limited number of systematically described prenatal cases, the prenatal phenotype of COL2A1‐related SEDC remains incompletely characterized. The aim of this study was to review the published literature on prenatal presentations of COL2A1‐related SEDC, summarizing reported imaging and molecular findings, and to report two original cases with distinct prenatal phenotypes and postnatally confirmed COL2A1 variants, thereby expanding the prenatal phenotypic spectrum and highlighting the diagnostic challenges and clinical implications for prenatal counseling and perinatal management.

Methods

Narrative Review

A narrative review with a systematic search strategy was conducted to summarize prenatal imaging findings, detection timing, and molecular diagnoses in COL2A1‐related SEDC, aiming to improve prenatal recognition, support genotype–phenotype correlations, and inform prenatal counseling and perinatal management. PubMed and Scopus were searched without date restrictions for articles published in English or Spanish. The PubMed search included the terms “spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia congenita” or “SEDC” or “type II collagenopathy” combined with “prenatal,” “antenatal,” “fetal,” “fetus,” or “in utero.” In Scopus, equivalent terms were applied to the TITLE‐ABS‐KEY fields. Studies were independently selected by at least two reviewers, with disagreements resolved by consensus. The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251270910).

Studies were included if they reported fetuses or pregnancies with prenatal suspicion of skeletal dysplasia and molecular confirmation of pathogenic or likely pathogenic COL2A1 variants, consistent with SEDC or related type II collagenopathies. Reports with postnatal‐only diagnoses lacking prenatal imaging, without molecular confirmation, or involving genes other than COL2A1 were excluded. Methodological quality was assessed using a modified version of the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Series and Case Reports [15].

Demographic information, prenatal imaging findings, gestational age at first detection, molecular genetic results, and perinatal outcomes were extracted using a standardized data extraction form. Clinical and imaging variables were coded as categorical. Data extraction was performed by one reviewer and independently verified by a second reviewer, with disagreements resolved by consensus.

Data synthesis was descriptive. Extracted variables from the literature review were summarized using absolute numbers and frequencies, and findings were qualitatively assessed to identify recurrent prenatal features and assess their diagnostic specificity in the context of COL2A1‐related SEDC. Subsequently, prenatal findings from the two original cases were qualitatively compared with the features identified in the review to highlight similarities, differences, and potential extensions of the prenatal phenotypic spectrum associated with COL2A1‐related SEDC. No formal statistical comparisons were performed.

Case Reports

Two fetuses with suspected skeletal dysplasia were evaluated prenatally by ultrasound at a tertiary referral center. Prenatal imaging findings, perinatal data, postnatal clinical examinations, and molecular genetic testing results were retrospectively reviewed. Both cases had postnatal molecular confirmation of COL2A1 variants and were included to complement the literature review and expand the prenatal phenotypic spectrum associated with COL2A1‐related SEDC.

Ethical Considerations

The narrative review with a systematic search strategy included previously published anonymized data and was therefore exempt from institutional ethics committee approval. The two additional cases were retrospectively reviewed using existing medical records only and did not involve direct patient contact or prospective data collection. In accordance with institutional policies, formal ethics committee approval was not required. At the time of admission, patients sign a general informed consent authorizing the use of anonymized clinical information for educational and research purposes, ensuring preservation of privacy and confidentiality.

Results

Narrative Review

A total of 30 records were identified. After removal of duplicates, 20 unique records were screened for eligibility. Fourteen studies were excluded because of the absence of a SEDC diagnosis, lack of molecular confirmation, insufficient prenatal imaging data, or inadequate description of COL2A1 variants. Six studies were included in the literature review. The study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1). No study was excluded based on methodological quality (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2).

The main prenatal, perinatal, and postnatal characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1. The six included studies comprised a total of seven fetuses with molecularly confirmed COL2A1‐related SEDC. Gestational age at first prenatal detection ranged from 11 to 28 weeks of gestation, with a median gestational age at detection in the mid–second trimester. Five cases occurred in a familial context, while two were initially considered sporadic. Notably, in one case reported by Barat‐Houari et al. [22], the proband carried a homozygous COL2A1 variant (p.Pro458Leu), while both parents were heterozygous carriers, the father presenting with a mild phenotype including severe myopia and skeletal complaints, and the mother being clinically unaffected. This biallelic inheritance pattern is consistent with the rare autosomal recessive form of COL2A1‐related disease, now documented in at least two independent reports [22, 23], and mirrors the dual inheritance behavior described for other genes such as LZTR1 in Noonan syndrome [24, 25, 26]. This finding has important implications for genetic counseling, as it expands the inheritance paradigm of COL2A1‐related disorders and highlights the need for careful molecular and clinical evaluation of both parents when a severe or atypical presentation is identified.

Prenatal molecular diagnosis was achieved in three cases, whereas postnatal diagnosis was achieved in the remaining cases. Prenatal genetic testing was performed by chorionic villus sampling at 12 weeks of gestation in one case, performed in the context of a known familial COL2A1 variant with an increased nuchal translucency of 4 mm noted on ultrasound, and by amniocentesis at 19+2 and 19+3 weeks of gestation in the remaining two. Sanger sequencing was used for molecular analysis in all studies, and all six identified variants were missense.

Prenatal ultrasound consistently demonstrated limb shortening in all cases, representing the most common prenatal feature. Thoracic findings were uncommon and documented in only one fetus. Vertebral abnormalities and altered bone ossification were inconsistently identified prenatally, despite being more frequently described after birth. Complementary imaging using low‐dose fetal computed tomography (CT) was performed selectively in a minority of cases and contributed to the identification of vertebral and ossification abnormalities not evident on ultrasound alone; the rationale, radiation considerations, and recommended indications for this modality are discussed below.

In most cases, a working prenatal suspicion of SEDC was documented by the attending clinicians based on a known family history of COL2A1‐related disease, the combination of imaging findings including limb shortening, vertebral abnormalities, and/or delayed ossification of the pubic or ischial bones; however, a definitive confirmed diagnosis was not universally established before birth, with formal diagnosis achieved postnatally through skeletal survey and molecular confirmation in the remaining cases.

Pregnancy outcomes varied: two pregnancies resulted in termination, both in the context of a positive family history and following prenatal molecular confirmation of the familial COL2A1 variant, and five in live births. We report two additional prenatally evaluated cases that further expand the phenotypic spectrum and illustrate current diagnostic challenges.

Case Reports

Case 1 involved a female fetus referred for genetic evaluation due to suspected skeletal dysplasia, with no relevant family history. Prenatal ultrasound at 30+2 weeks of gestation demonstrated marked shortening of the long bones, reduced thoracic circumference index (0.65), abnormal femur/foot length ratio (0.72), mild enlargement of the cisterna magna (> 12 mm), and decreased fetal movements Figure 1A–F. Delivery occurred by preterm cesarean section at 36+5 weeks of gestation. At birth, the neonate presented respiratory compromise, requiring two cycles of chest compression for bradycardia.

(B) Two‐dimensional ultrasound measurement of fetal thoracic circumference at 30 + 2 weeks, showing a reduced thoracic circumference index (0.65), consistent with a short, small chest. Postnatal examination revealed short stature with rhizomelic–mesomelic limb shortening, narrow thorax, short neck, and craniofacial features (depressed nasal bridge and retrognathia). Limitations of elbow extension, bilateral fifth finger clinodactyly, and modified transverse palmar creases were noted. Skeletal survey showed delayed ossification of the pubic bones, fifth sacral vertebra and patellar ossification center Figure 1A–F. Targeted next‐generation sequencing identified a heterozygous stop‐loss variant in COL2A1 (NM_001844.5:c.4462T > G, p.(Ter1488GluextTer34)), reclassified as likely pathogenic according to ACMG/AMP criteria and supporting a diagnosis of SEDC.

Case 2 was a female infant born to non‐consanguineous parents following an uncomplicated pregnancy. Prenatal ultrasound at 24+3 weeks of gestation demonstrated symmetrical rhizomelic shortening of all extremities, with preserved thoracic dimensions and no additional structural anomalies Figure 2A‐E. Fetal movements were mildly reduced, and no invasive prenatal testing was performed. The infant was delivered at term by cesarean section.

Postnatally, relative macrocephaly, short stature, dysmorphic craniofacial features, and rhizomelic limb shortening and brachydactyly were noted. Skeletal survey demonstrated incomplete ossification of the vertebral bodies Figure 2A‐E. Targeted next‐generation sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense variant in COL2A1 (NM_001844.5:c.2752G > T, p.(Gly918Cys)), classified as pathogenic according to ACMG/AMP criteria, supporting the diagnosis of SEDC. No additional pathogenic variants were identified in either case.

Discussion

In this study, we combined a narrative review with a systematic search strategy of prenatally evaluated cases of COL2A1‐related SEDC with two additional cases assessed in a tertiary referral center (Table 2). Our findings highlight the nonspecific nature of prenatal presentation, timing of detection, and diagnostic pathways associated with this condition, though the limited number of reported cases precludes definitive conclusions regarding the full extent of phenotypic heterogeneity. This work expands the prenatal phenotypic spectrum of COL2A1‐related SEDC and underscores the challenges of achieving an accurate prenatal diagnosis, with important implications for prenatal counseling and management.

Prenatal and Postnatal Phenotypic Spectrum of COL2A1‐Related SEDC

The gestational age at which a prenatal diagnosis was established ranged from 12 to 28 weeks, with a mean of approximately 22 weeks across reviewed cases; however, this figure reflects when fetuses were scanned rather than when sonographic features first become detectable. Notably, long bone shortening was evident as early as 16 weeks of gestation in cases scanned early due to a known family history, consistent with the earliest reported gestational age for femur length reduction in COL2A1‐related SEDC [13, 27]. These observations suggest that earlier detection is achievable when surveillance is initiated promptly in at‐risk pregnancies with a known familial variant. In contrast, one of the present cases was first detected in the third trimester, reflecting delayed referral or limited access to specialized care rather than true late phenotypic onset.

The prenatal phenotype expected with COL2A1‐related SEDC includes limb shortening, thoracic findings, vertebral abnormalities, and delayed ossification of the pubic and/or ischial bones [5]. However, limb shortening (femoral length below the 5th percentile) was the only consistent prenatal feature. Thoracic findings were uncommon, reported in only one case in the literature and observed in one of our cases. Vertebral abnormalities and altered bone ossification, described in up to three of seven literature cases, were detected prenatally mainly when ultrasound was complemented by fetal CT, which may explain why these findings were not recognized prenatally in our patients and were only confirmed postnatally.

These prenatal sonographic findings of COL2A1‐related SEDC are nonspecific and overlap considerably with other skeletal dysplasias. According to the Nosology and Classification of Genetic Skeletal Disorders [4], COL2A1‐related SEDC belongs to Group 2 (Type 2 collagen disorders, NOS 02–0040), a group that itself encompasses a wide phenotypic continuum from lethal achondrogenesis type II (NOS 02–0010) to milder entities such as Stickler syndrome type 1 (NOS 02–0090). Prenatally, the most clinically relevant differential diagnoses include other conditions within this same group, particularly achondrogenesis type II and hypochondrogenesis, which present earlier and more severely, with markedly reduced bone ossification and a lethal course, as well as Kniest dysplasia (NOS 02–0060) and spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia COL2A1‐related (NOS 02–0050), which may present with overlapping limb shortening and vertebral changes. Beyond Group 2, conditions such as FGFR3‐related achondroplasia (NOS 01–0040) and thanatophoric dysplasia (NOS 01‐0010/0020) must be considered in the context of rhizomelic shortening, though the latter typically presents with more pronounced femoral bowing and platyspondyly. In non‐familial cases where the COL2A1 variant is unknown, this phenotypic overlap underscores the critical role of molecular genetic testing, ideally prenatal exome or targeted panel sequencing, to achieve a specific diagnosis and guide appropriate counseling.

Reduced fetal movements were observed in both of our cases but had not been previously reported. Additionally, mildly enlarged cisterna magna was noted in one case, although this finding was not confirmed postnatally, further illustrating the nonspecific and diagnostically challenging nature of prenatal findings in COL2A1‐related SEDC.

Prenatal impression of SEDC was reported in most published cases (6/7), whereas a specific prenatal diagnosis was not achieved in our patients. This may reflect the familial nature of the reported cases, with known parental diagnosis, and the use of fetal CT scan, which enabled detection of vertebral and ossification abnormalities not always evident on ultrasound alone. In contrast, the isolated long bone shortening observed in our cases allowed classification as a non‐lethal skeletal dysplasia but precluded precise prenatal diagnosis, highlighting the extreme phenotypic heterogeneity of these conditions [28]. Ultrasound diagnostic accuracy for skeletal dysplasias ranges from 35% to 68%, while CT provides better assessment of spinal and pelvic morphology and detection of bone synostosis [20, 28].

The use of fetal CT is controversial because of ionizing radiation, with known deterministic effects occurring at doses above approximately 100 mGy, including growth restriction, cognitive impairment, and increased lifetime cancer risk. Reported fetal doses for abdominal/pelvic CT range from 2 to 12 mGy, reaching up to 47 mGy in maternal polytrauma [29]. Although fetal MRI represents a radiation‐free alternative and advanced sequences have shown utility in postnatal assessment of vertebral ossification [18], technical limitations often restrict prenatal bone evaluation [5]. Consequently, current evidence supports the selective use of low‐dose fetal CT as the most effective complementary modality for detailed skeletal evaluation in specific clinical scenarios: when ultrasound findings are inconclusive, when vertebral abnormalities or ossification defects are suspected but not adequately characterized, or when a more precise prenatal diagnosis is expected to directly influence clinical management, including decisions regarding delivery planning, neonatal resuscitation preparedness, parental counseling regarding prognosis, and, in familial cases with known variants, informed decision‐making regarding pregnancy continuation [28].

Regarding the molecular diagnostic approach, it is important to distinguish between the methodology used in the previously published cases included in this review and that employed in our own case reports. Although prenatal exome sequencing has been shown to increase diagnostic yield in isolated skeletal dysplasias, reaching up to 53% [30], all cases identified through the literature search were identified by targeted Sanger sequencing of a known familial COL2A1 variant, reflecting a context in which the pathogenic variant had already been identified in an affected family member prior to the index pregnancy. This targeted approach, while accurate and efficient in familial settings, does not exclude alternative diagnoses and is not applicable in de novo or sporadic presentations. In contrast, both cases reported here were diagnosed using targeted next‐generation sequencing panels covering genes associated with skeletal dysplasias. This broader approach allowed systematic exclusion of other potential diagnoses within the skeletal dysplasia spectrum before establishing the final diagnosis of COL2A1‐related SEDC. As NGS‐based testing becomes increasingly accessible in prenatal settings, its use should be considered particularly in cases without a known familial variant, where a specific molecular diagnosis cannot be inferred from family history alone.

Regarding pregnancy outcomes, two of the three cases with prenatal molecular confirmation resulted in pregnancy termination, both with a positive family history, highlighting the importance of accurate prenatal diagnosis for informed decision‐making. Targeted prenatal testing has also allowed the exclusion of the familial COL2A1 variant in suspected cases [31, 32], including in preimplantation settings [33].

In almost all live‐born cases, postnatal imaging enabled clinical diagnosis, followed by molecular confirmation. Neonatal respiratory compromise was rare, reported in one published case and observed in one of our patients. Given that the prenatal ultrasound phenotype of COL2A1‐related SEDC may be limited to isolated limb shortening, particularly in non‐familial cases, additional imaging modalities are often required for accurate phenotypic characterization. In this setting, after appropriate parental counseling, low‐dose fetal CT could be considered when severe skeletal dysplasia is suspected, ultrasound findings remain inconclusive, or results are expected to influence counseling, management and decision making.

Molecular Findings and Genotype–Phenotype Considerations

COL2A1‐related SEDC represents only one point within a broad allelic continuum of type II collagenopathies. Based on a comprehensive analysis of 663 probands with COL2A1 variants, Zhang et al. [34] classified 21 COL2A1‐related disorders into five categories according to phenotypic characteristics, ranging from lethal perinatal conditions to mild adult‐onset disorders. At the severe end of this spectrum, achondrogenesis type II (ACG2; OMIM #200610) and hypochondrogenesis represent lethal prenatal presentations caused by pathogenic variants in the same gene, predominantly dominant‐negative glycine substitutions within the triple‐helical domain that severely disrupt collagen II assembly, accounting for 49 of 54 reported ACG2 cases in that series. Prenatally, ACG2 presents with extreme micromelia, absent or severely reduced vertebral ossification, and a lethal course, features that are qualitatively distinct from those of COL2A1‐related SEDC, which is associated with milder limb shortening, variable vertebral changes, and a non‐lethal outcome in most cases.

Importantly, the same COL2A1 variant may occasionally result in different phenotypes across and even within families, as illustrated by the p.Arg275Cys variant, which has been reported in both Czech dysplasia, Spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia and early‐onset osteoarthritis [16, 34], further underscoring the absence of robust genotype–phenotype correlations in this group of disorders. This phenotypic divergence despite shared molecular etiology reinforces the necessity of molecular confirmation to distinguish COL2A1‐related SEDC from its lethal allelic counterparts when imaging findings alone are insufficient, particularly in the prenatal setting.

To date, HGMD (Professional 2024.4) reports 859 COL2A1 variants associated with collagenopathies, including at least 145 linked to SEDC. Most variants cluster in the triple‐helical domain, mainly glycine substitutions (74%), with fewer in the C‐propeptide region [35]. Of the eight variants analyzed, five were located in the triple‐helical domain, four involving glycine substitutions, and three affected the C‐propeptide region, consistent with previous reports (Figure 3). Few mutational hotspots have been proposed [34], including p.(Gly504Ser) variant identified in one reviewed case [21].

Attempts to establish genotype–phenotype correlations in COL2A1‐related disorders have been hindered by marked geographic bias in published cohorts. For instance, the large‐scale analysis by Zhang et al. [34] included only one patient from Chile, representing 0.2% of the total cohort and serving as the sole representative from Latin America. In this context, the inclusion of two additional cases from Mexico contributes modestly to enhancing geographic representation. Overall, robust genotype–phenotype correlations remain scarce.

The broad phenotypic spectrum and clinical overlap among COL2A1‐related disorders are well recognized, even among affected members within the same family [34, 36]. Among the few correlations proposed, variants affecting the N‐propeptide region have been associated with milder phenotypes, particularly Stickler syndrome type I, whereas variants in the C‐propeptide domain have been linked to more severe and frequently lethal phenotypes, reported in up to 87% of cases. Missense variants represent the most common variant type (39.5%), followed by frameshift (23.8%) and nonsense variants (16.7%) [34].

The novel stop‐loss variant identified in the first case, c.4462T > G p.(Ter1488GluextTer34), initially classified as a variant of uncertain significance, was reclassified as likely pathogenic based on ACMG/AMP criteria PM4, PM5, PM2_Supporting, and PP2. This variant lies within the C‐propeptide domain, the most conserved region of fibrillar collagen chains, which plays a critical role in intracellular procollagen assembly and regulation of extracellular collagen fibril formation [37, 38]. A variant affecting the same codon, NM_001844.5:c.4462T > C, p.(Ter1488GlnextTer34), has previously been reported in two related individuals with SEDC [17].

Xu et al. [17] suggested that alterations in this region may predispose to ophthalmologic involvement, as both of their patients developed early‐onset myopia. Although ophthalmologic evaluation in our patient was normal, this feature is age‐dependent, and periodic follow‐up assessments are planned, including evaluations at 6 months, 1 year, and annually thereafter, as recommended in prior reviews [19].

An additional proposed correlation involving the C‐propeptide region relates to increased phenotypic severity. Notably, the only two neonates with reported respiratory distress, one from the literature and one from the present study, harbored variants within this domain. In the literature case, respiratory compromise may have been influenced by extreme prematurity as a twin pregnancy was delivered at 25 weeks of gestation [39], whereas no clear contributing factor was identified in our patient. Nevertheless, respiratory complications have been reported in approximately 26% of individuals with SEDC overall [19].

Another proposed genotype–phenotype association suggests that C‐propeptide variants may result in atypical SEDC presentations lacking delayed pubic ossification. While absence of delayed pubic ossification was documented in two literature cases [35, 39], our patient exhibited delayed pubic ossification, indicating that this correlation is not consistent across cases.

The variant identified in our second case, c.2752G > T, p.(Gly918Cys), is a glycine substitution, the most common variant type (∼73%) [19]. It is predicted to affect the triple‐helical domain and has been previously reported in a non‐lethal type II collagen disorder [40]. Although up to 15%–50% of exonic single‐nucleotide variants may be pathogenic through splicing alterations [41, 42, 43]. SpliceAI [44] analysis showed no predicted impact on splicing (donor site score 0.00; acceptor site score 0.01).

Hearing loss is a recognized extraskeletal manifestation of COL2A1‐related SEDC, reported in approximately 37% of affected individuals and attributed to the role of type II collagen in the structural integrity of the inner ear [19]. Its frequency and severity appear to vary across variant types, with inconsistent reports even among C‐propeptide mutations [22, 35], further illustrating the absence of robust genotype–phenotype correlations for this feature. Neither of the patients reported here had documented hearing loss at the time of evaluation; however, given its age‐dependent nature, periodic audiological assessment is planned as part of their long‐term follow‐up. Hearing evaluation should therefore be considered an essential component of postnatal surveillance in all individuals with confirmed COL2A1‐related SEDC, regardless of the variant identified.

In summary, a clear genotype–phenotype correlation could not be established. Variants affecting different regions of COL2A1 were associated with overlapping prenatal and postnatal phenotypes, and no specific molecular pattern reliably predicted the severity of prenatal imaging findings or perinatal outcomes. These findings are consistent with previous reports emphasizing substantial intra‐ and interfamilial variability in COL2A1‐related skeletal dysplasias.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

The principal strength of this study lies in the integration of a narrative review with a systematic search strategy focused specifically on prenatally detected COL2A1‐related SEDC with a detailed description of two additional cases evaluated in a contemporary prenatal setting. By combining previously published evidence with newly reported cases, this work provides a consolidated overview of prenatal imaging findings, molecular results, and perinatal outcomes, contributing to a more comprehensive characterization of this rare and diagnostically challenging condition. This approach is particularly relevant given that most prior literature reviews of COL2A1‐related disorders have predominantly focused on postnatal cohorts with mean ages exceeding 10 years [19, 34]. The narrative review was conducted independently by three reviewers, with disagreements resolved by consensus, thereby minimizing the risk of interpretation bias.

An additional strength is the standardized assessment of methodological quality using a modified JBI critical appraisal checklist for case reports and case series [15], supporting the transparency and reproducibility of the review. Overall, the methodological quality of the included studies was high. All studies adequately fulfilled the assessed items, with the exception of clear reporting of demographic characteristics, as incomplete descriptions of geographic origin or ancestry were frequently observed. Consistent with previous reviews [19, 34], Latin American populations remain underrepresented in the literature; therefore, the inclusion of two cases from Mexico modestly improves the geographic diversity of reported cohorts and contributes to a more geographically representative characterization of COL2A1‐related phenotypes. Furthermore, the parallel evaluation of prenatal ultrasound findings, postnatal imaging, and molecular data in the newly reported cases enabled direct comparison with published reports and facilitated clinically relevant observations regarding diagnostic pathways and the limitations of prenatal ultrasound alone.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the narrative review was constrained by the small number of eligible studies, reflecting the rarity of the condition. Considering that at least 145 variants have been associated with SEDC, the cases included in this review represent approximately 5.5% of the total reported molecular spectrum. In this context, some fetuses with a prenatal phenotype consistent with SEDC were excluded because of the absence of prenatal or postnatal molecular confirmation. Moreover, all included studies consisted of isolated familial case reports or small case series, inherently limiting quantitative analyses and precluding robust genotype–phenotype correlations.

Due to marked variants' heterogeneity, the number of patients harboring the same variant or mutation type was small, and many clinical manifestations were age‐dependent. As previously noted, this substantially limits the ability to establish statistically meaningful genotype–phenotype correlations and warrants cautious interpretation of such associations [19]. In addition, prenatal imaging protocols and access to complementary modalities, particularly low‐dose fetal CT, varied across studies and centers, influencing the detection of vertebral abnormalities and ossification defects. Despite these limitations, the consistent observation that isolated limb shortening represents the most reliable prenatal finding underscores the need for adjunct imaging and molecular approaches when a specific diagnosis is pursued.

Overall, this study highlights both the potential and the current limitations of prenatal diagnosis in COL2A1‐related SEDC and supports a multidisciplinary approach integrating advanced imaging techniques and molecular testing to improve prenatal characterization, parental counseling, and perinatal management.

Conclusion

While the postnatal phenotype of COL2A1‐related SEDC is well‐defined, its prenatal manifestations remain incompletely characterized and are often nonspecific, limiting the reliability of a purely clinical diagnosis during gestation. In addition, the phenotypic overlap among more than 500 recognized skeletal dysplasias further complicates accurate prenatal diagnosis based solely on ultrasound findings. These challenges underscore the need for a comprehensive diagnostic strategy that integrates detailed imaging, molecular genetic testing, and close collaboration within a multidisciplinary team, to improve diagnostic accuracy, counseling, and perinatal management.
