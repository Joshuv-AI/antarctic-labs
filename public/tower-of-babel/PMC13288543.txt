Evaluating factors associated with pharmaceutical price variation, dispersion and higher-tier pricing in Malaysia's public segmented procurement system: exploring market competition
Authors: Farahwahida Mohd Kasim, Ernieda Hatah, Lokhman Hakim Osman, Adliah Mhd Ali, Zaheer-Ud-Din Babar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEffective procurement is crucial for securing affordable medicines, yet limited evidence exists on pricing influences in Malaysia’s dynamic pharmaceutical market. This study analysed factors associated with purchased price variation, bidder price dispersion, and higher-tier drug prices within the Ministry of Health’s (MOH) segmented procurement system, focusing on market competition dynamics.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on eight high-expenditure therapeutic subgroups (2017–2021) using data from 19 MOH facilities across five regions. Price variation was calculated as the ratio of observed to minimum brand price, and bidder dispersion as the ratio of highest to lowest quoted price. Higher-tier prices exceeded the median price for a given brand. The Kruskal–Wallis H test was applied to examine distributional differences in purchased drug prices and bidder-quoted prices across categorical variables, while logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with higher-tier pricing (p < 0.05). Factors analysed included geographical zones, facility types, procurement values, therapeutic subgroups, purchased product types, dosage forms and the number of competing agents and products.ResultsAmong 97 drugs from 4,702 purchase records involving 28 suppliers, East Malaysia showed significantly higher price variation, and all regions had greater odds of higher-tier pricing than the Central region (p < 0.05). Lower procurement values were linked to greater price variation and higher-tier pricing (p < 0.05). Imported generics exhibited higher price variation (p < 0.001) but lower prices than innovators and local generics. Procurements with ≥3 competing agents had lower price variation (p < 0.008) but greater bidder dispersion (p < 0.001), while up to five agents increased the likelihood of higher-tier pricing (p < 0.05) compared to 7–12 agents.ConclusionFindings highlight that both market and non-price competition influence procurement efficiency and price stability.

Background

Public spending on health expenditures has been reported to increase in the past 20 years (WHO, 2021). For instance, health expenditure in high-income countries as a percentage of gross domestic product (GDP) is reported to be grew from 7.1% to 9.0% between 2000 and 2019 (WHO, 2021). While Malaysia, an upper middle-income country, has seen a notable increase in total health expenditure from RM8.6 billion in 1997 to RM84.2 billion in 2023 (MNHA 2021, 2024). The country operates a dual-tiered healthcare system, comprising public and private sectors. Within the public sector, the Ministry of Health Malaysia's (MOH) accounted for RM36.2 billion in 2023, representing 1.91% of GDP and 86% of the total public health expenditure (MNHA 2024). Of this, approximately 9.1% (RM3.3 billion) of the expenditure was allocated to pharmaceutical spending (Pharmaceutical Services Programme, 2024).

The public sector follows a structured procurement framework that includes pooled procurement (through open or negotiated tenders) and segmented facility-level procurement. In the pooled system, tendering is managed at the headquarters level, and individual facilities purchase only from the contracts awarded centrally. In contrast, the segmented system allows public hospitals, clinics, and institutions to procure selected medicines independently, enabling multiple competing agents to submit price quotations. This includes the direct purchase of drugs, where each facility is allowed to procure a single drug, strictly adhering to the MOH Medicines Formulary (MOHMF) list, valued at under RM50,000 per year. In these cases, a minimum of three (3) suppliers must be invited to submit quotations, ensuring competitive pricing. For a drug valued between RM50,000 and RM500,000 per year per facility, a specific process known as ‘Lampiran Q' is applied, which requires the invitation of at least five (5) quotations to allow more competitors to participate in higher-value procurement. An exception to this is the national hospital, which serves as a tertiary referral centre and is allowed a higher limit for Lampiran Q, with a threshold of up to RM1 million (Pharmacy Practice & Development Division, 2020). This creates a heterogeneous market, where competition intensity varies by facility, product type, and number of suppliers, influencing both the range of bids received and final purchase prices.

Although Malaysia's public procurement system prioritizes pooled purchasing strategies to enhance efficiency and cost savings, accounting for 77.8% of total pharmaceutical expenditure in 2023 (Pharmaceutical Services Programme, 2024), pharmaceutical prices were still reported to be two to three times higher than the International Reference Price, with no significant difference in procurement efficiency between the two methods (Hamzah et al., 2020). Research by Wirtz et al. (2009) and Waning et al. (2009) suggests that higher purchase volumes do not always guarantee lower prices. More recently, Mohd Kasim et al. (2024) noted that while pooled procurement in Malaysia's public healthcare system holds potential for long-term cost reduction, achieving substantial savings may be delayed. In contrast, segmented procurement has expanded markedly, reaching RM734 million in 2023 or 22.2% of total pharmaceutical spending, representing a twofold increase within just three years (2021–2023) (Pharmaceutical Services Programme, 2024).

Understanding the factors driving price variability in segmented procurement is critical for addressing inefficiencies, ensuring fair and equitable pricing, and supporting sustainable procurement strategies. High drug prices increase government healthcare spending (Srivastava & McGuire, 2014), and in Asia, medicine markups can reach 50%, compared to 4–25% in Western countries (Lee et al., 2021). A systematic review of pharmaceutical price determinants also highlights a gap in national-level analyses, with only six studies examining within-country pricing dynamics (Janssen Daalen et al., 2021). Most of these studies were conducted in contexts with different procurement systems and market conditions, limiting their applicability to countries like Malaysia, where public procurement involves both pooled and segmented facility-level purchasing. Evidence tailored to local procurement contexts is needed to understand pricing behaviour more accurately (Lee et al., 2021).

In Malaysia, research on public procurement has mostly been descriptive, focusing on product types, facility types, and geographical factors (Babar ZUD et al., 2007; Pharmaceutical Services Programme, 2018, 2020b; Wong et al., 2019). However, critical variables such as procurement values, therapeutic subgroups, the number of competing agents, and the diversity of competing products remain underexplored. This study, therefore, aims to fill this gap by analysing the determinants of pharmaceutical price variability, with a focus on market competition in segmented procurement practices. Specifically, it evaluates (i) the factors associated with variations in purchased drug prices, (ii) the factors related to the dispersion of prices quoted by bidders, and (iii) the factors associated with higher-tier drug prices within Malaysia's public healthcare segmented procurement system. The findings will help guide strategies to improve cost efficiency, ensure fair competition, and strengthen public healthcare procurement system. While grounded in the Malaysian context, the findings offer lessons for other publicly funded healthcare systems with similar procurement models, demonstrating how competition, procurement design, and product diversity can shape pricing efficiency and sustainable access to essential medicines.

Methods

Study design

This study is a retrospective observational analysis of database records on the segmented procurement of pharmaceutical products within Malaysia's public healthcare system. The ethical approval was obtained from the Medical Research and Ethics Committee, MOH (NMMR ID: 22-01094-9CC (IIR)) and from the Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia Research Ethics Committee (UKM PPI/111/8/JEP-2022-753).

Data sources and selection

The data selection was conducted through purposive sampling of segmented pharmaceutical procurement data between 2017 and 2021, guided by the MOHMF list. Initially, the medicines formulary list was categorized and sorted according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) system which is a standardized classification system that compares the use of drugs on levels two to five (WHO Collaborating Centre for Drug Statistics Methodology 2023). As of 31 December 2021, at the ATC level two, the formulary included a diverse range of 1640 drugs spanning 87 therapeutic categories. Therapeutic subgroups that the top ten expenditure and usage in the 2016′s public sector, which includes 16 therapeutic subgroups and 658 drugs were further considered for selections (Pharmaceutical Services Programme, 2020a).

Further selection only includes eight therapeutic subgroups with 420 drugs in the list namely drugs for drugs for diabetes (A10), antithrombotics (B01), agents acting on the renin-angiotensin system (C09), lipid modifiers (C10), antibiotics for systemic use (J01), antineoplastic agents (L01), immunosuppressants (L04). From this, using narrow approach the final analysis only includes drugs with an annual expenditure of RM4 million or more and at the ATC level five reduce the list to 159 targeted drugs. The selection of RM4 million as the lower expenditure threshold was guided by an analysis of the third quartile (Q3) expenditure distribution for pharmaceutical products at ATC Level 5 over the five-year period (2017–2021). Across the study years, Q3 values ranged from RM3.80 million to RM4.74 million, with a median Q3 expenditure of approximately RM4.19 million. To ensure consistency across years and to capture high-expenditure products with substantial budgetary impact, RM4 million was selected as a practical and standardised threshold representing the upper expenditure bracket.

Procurement data for the 159 drugs were gathered from 19 MOH facilities across Malaysia, comprising hospitals, health clinics, and central stores. These facilities were chosen based on their high expenditure on medicines within the MOH. Selection criteria also included the type of facility and their geographical distribution across five zones which are North, Central, South, and East Coast in Peninsular Malaysia, and East Malaysia. In Peninsular Malaysia, each zone included two hospitals and two health clinics while in East Malaysia, the selected facilities comprised one hospital and two central stores. The central stores in East Malaysia serve a critical role as procurement and distribution centres, supporting the health clinics in the region. Data collection

Segmented procurement data were obtained from the Pharmacy Information System (PhIS) and the eProcurement System (eP). PhIS provided procurement details, including active ingredient, strength, dosage form, pack size, brand name, purchase price, quantity, purchase date, local purchase order number, and supplier. Additional information such as bidders’ quotations for direct purchases and local contract details (contract price, period, and reference number) was retrieved from eP using the purchase order number. Contract reference numbers were used to obtain Lampiran Q documents containing bidders’ offer details. All prices were recorded in Malaysian Ringgit and systematically organized by active ingredient, strength, dosage form, pack size, year, and brand for brand-specific analysis.

Data analyses

This study assessed three complementary dimensions of pharmaceutical pricing in public segmented procurement. Price variation measured differences in final procurement prices for the same brand across facilities within a year. Bidder price dispersion captured the range between the highest and lowest supplier bids for the same drug. Higher-tier pricing identified procurement prices above the annual median for each brand as a relative benchmark. Competing-agent groups were defined based on observed supplier participation, reflecting natural distributions in the data while ensuring adequate observations for statistical analysis.

As the data were non-normally distributed, the Kruskal–Wallis H test (IBM SPSS Version 25) was used, with mean ranks comparing group differences and p < 0.05 indicating significance. When significant, Dunn’s post hoc test (1964) with Bonferroni correction was applied to identify specific group differences.

Purchase prices above 100 were classified as higher-tier prices, while those at or below 100 were categorized as lower-tier pricing. The likelihood of higher-tier prices was analysed using multiple logistic regression to estimate the strength and direction of influencing factors and predict probabilities across different predictor levels. Prior to modelling, data were screened for missing values, outliers, and influential points using Cook’s Distance and leverage values. Points with Cook’s Distance < 1 indicated no major influence (Hair et al., 2019). Leverage values < 0.2 were considered safe, 0.2–<0.5 risky, and ≥0.5 dangerous (Huber, 1981). Misclassified cases and minor outliers were retained as they did not substantially affect model performance.

The final model included only significant predictors (p < 0.05) after removing non-significant variables using the backward stepwise LR method. Model explanatory power was assessed using Nagelkerke R², while overall fit was evaluated with the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, where a non-significant result (p > 0.05) indicated good model fit. Classification accuracy and ROC curve analysis were used to assess predictive performance; an AUC>0.7 denoted acceptable discrimination. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals were reported to describe the strength and direction of associations, with significant predictors (p < 0.05) identified as factors associated with the likelihood of higher-tier prices.

Results

Data analysis was conducted on 4,702 purchase records (4,251 direct purchases and 451 local contracts) for 97 drugs and 81 brands. A total of 98.91% (4,651) of the purchase orders were sourced from 28 agents, with the majority (n = 3,775, 80.28%) managed by agents in the Central Region. In contrast, 90.76% (n = 609) of the purchase orders in East Malaysia were supplied by agents within the same region.

Factors associated with price variation in purchased drugs

Within the same brands, significant price variations were found for different geographical regions, facility types, procurement values, therapeutic subgroups, purchased product’s types, pharmaceutical dosage forms, number of competing local agents and numbers of competing agents (p < 0.05) (Supplemental Material 1(a)).

Post hoc analysis showed significant regional differences in price variation. The South region had higher variation than the North (p = 0.04), while East Malaysia consistently exhibited the greatest variation compared to all other regions (all p < 0.001). The South also had the highest number of purchase orders, indicating more frequent procurement activity. Across facility types, central stores in East Malaysia showed significantly higher price variation than hospitals and health clinics (both p < 0.001), with no difference between hospitals and clinics.

The analysis showed a significant association between procurement value and price variation, where higher procurement values were linked to lower price variation, likely due to the stabilizing effect of larger purchase quantities. Direct purchases (≤RM10,000) had significantly higher price variation than Lampiran Q purchases (p < 0.001) and purchases valued at RM30,000–50,000 (p = 0.03). Significant differences were also observed across therapeutic subgroups. C10 and L04 exhibited lower price variation than N05, J01, A10, C09 and B01 (p < 0.005) while N05 showed the highest variation compared to these subgroups and L01 (p < 0.001). Overall, C10 and L04 were associated with reduced price variability, whereas N05 demonstrated the greatest variability.

Price variation differed significantly across product types and dosage forms. Imported generics showed the highest variation compared to innovators, reference biologics, and local generics (all p < 0.001). Among dosage forms, oral formulations (including modified release/ extended-release/ prolonged-released/ disintegrating tablet) exhibited greater variation than parenteral forms (intravenous & subcutaneous; p = 0.016, subcutaneous; p < 0.001, intravenous; p = 0.010) and oral (p = 0.003), while oral combination forms varied more than subcutaneous forms (p = 0.026). These findings highlight higher price variability in imported generics and certain oral dosage forms.

Price variation decreased as the number of competing agents increased. Purchases with three or more competing agents showed significantly lower price variation than those with only one or two agents (all p < 0.01). Similarly, for competing products, items with three to five competitors had significantly lower price variation than those with two competitors (p = 0.037). These findings highlight the strong influence of competition in reducing price variation.

Factors associated with bidder price dispersion

The characteristics of bidder price dispersion by study variables are presented in Supplemental Material 1(b). All variables including number of competing agents, number of competing products, and product types showed significant differences in price dispersion (p < 0.05). The analysis showed significant differences in quoted price dispersion based on the level of competition. Price dispersion was lowest when only two competing agents were present and increased as the number of competing agents rose. Similarly, three competing agents showed lower dispersion than four to six agents (p < 0.001), and four agents had lower dispersion than six agents (p = 0.040). For competing products, dispersion was lowest with a single competing product and significantly higher when two or three to five products competed (p < 0.001). Overall, greater competition was associated with wider bidder price dispersion.

Nine price dispersion patterns were identified. Price dispersion differed significantly across product types: innovators showed the lowest dispersion, while imported and local generics exhibited substantially higher dispersion (p < 0.001). These findings suggest that innovator prices remain relatively stable and maintain premium levels, whereas generics tend to adjust their prices more in response to competition.

Of the eight variables examined, six were included in the final model (p < 0.05): geographical zone, procurement value, therapeutic subgroup, dosage form, number of competing agents, and number of competing products. Type of facility and purchased product were non-significant (p > 0.05). In the final model, four factors were significantly associated with higher-tier prices (excluding dosage form and number of competing products). MOH facilities in East Malaysia and the North, South, and East Coast regions had 18.12-, 2.07-, 1.71-, and 1.33-times higher odds of purchasing at higher-tier prices compared with the Central region (p < 0.05). Lower procurement values were also associated with higher-tier pricing, showing increased odds compared with Lampiran Q procurements (>RM50,000 to < RM1 million) (p < 0.05). Several therapeutic subgroups were more likely to be purchased at higher-tier prices: antineoplastic agents (OR = 2.67), antidiabetics (OR = 1.77), antithrombotics (OR = 1.77), and systemic antibacterials (OR = 1.55), compared with lipid-modifying agents. Competition showed a clear inverse relationship with higher-tier pricing: facilities with five competing agents had 1.71-times higher odds of higher-tier prices, while those with only one competing agent had 5.07-times higher odds, relative to facilities with seven to twelve competing agents.

Discussion

Using five years of public pharmaceutical procurement data (2017–2021), this study examined drug purchase price variation, bidders’ quoted price dispersion, and higher-tier procurement prices within Malaysia’s segmented public procurement system. The results show substantial price variation in purchased prices and notable price dispersion during the bidding process. Several factors significantly associated with higher-tier pricing, including geographical zone, procurement value, therapeutic subgroup, and the number of competing agents.

Regional and facility-level differences were evident, contrasting earlier findings by the Pharmaceutical Services Programme (2018, 2020b), which reported no significant geographical price differences. East Malaysia consistently showed greater price variation and a higher likelihood of higher-tier pricing compared to the Central region likely due to weaker distribution channels, higher handling charges, and less efficient inland transportation (Mhd. Ruslan et al., 2019). The practice of prioritizing local agents which may reduce the number of suppliers and weaken competitive bidding, also warrants further evaluation. In contrast, the Central region benefited from a larger pool of suppliers, stronger infrastructure, and more competitive market conditions, contributing to lower and more stable prices. Price variation also differed by facility type. Hospitals and health clinics showed similar distributions, whereas central stores in East Malaysia had markedly higher variation. These disparities highlight the influence of logistics and market access on procurement efficiency. Similar patterns have been observed internationally; for example, Chilean public agencies exhibited substantial price differences for identical products, with larger, more complex buyers achieving greater cost efficiency (Allende et al., 2024).

Lower procurement values are associated with higher price variation and higher-tier pricing, whereas higher procurement volumes often benefit from economies of scale as reported internationally (Helble & Aizawa, 2017; Z. Li et al., 2021), attracting more suppliers, increasing competition, and lowering per-unit prices. For example, Helble and Aizawa (2017) reported that a 10% increase in insulin procurement volume reduced unit prices by 0.47%, illustrating the cost-saving potential of larger purchases. Similarly, leveraging operational efficiencies can reduce prices without compromising profits (Nagle & Müller, 2018). However, this effect is not universal; for instance, high purchase volumes did not consistently lower antiretroviral prices globally (Waning et al., 2009). Public procurement frameworks often require a minimum number of invited suppliers to promote competition and efficient pricing. However, small-volume and frequent purchases can fragment procurement, weaken bargaining power, and increase price variation. Consolidating procurement volumes across facilities or over longer purchasing cycles can improve market attractiveness, strengthen competition, reduce price variation, and yield more favourable purchasing outcomes.

The study revealed that therapeutic subgroups associated with price variation and higher-tier pricing in Malaysia’s public procurement system. Lipid-modifying agents and immunosuppressants showed relatively stable prices, whereas psycholeptics displayed higher variability; however, psycholeptic prices largely remained within the lower-tier range, indicating that greater variation does not necessarily result in higher-tier pricing. In contrast, antibacterials, antidiabetics, and antithrombotic agents consistently exhibited higher price variation and were more frequently procured at higher-tier prices compared to lipid-modifying agents. Interestingly, antineoplastic agents were more likely to be purchased at higher-tier prices despite minimal price variation, reflecting prices closely clustered around the median. These findings align with international evidence. Iacocca and Vallen (2021) reported that cancer drugs, blood-modifying agents, CNS drugs, and anti-infectives tend to have higher brand prices than other categories. Cross-country comparisons, such as cardiovascular drugs being more expensive but consistent in Qatar than Lebanon, highlight the influence of local market conditions (Abdel Rida et al., 2019). In Malaysia, the use of originator drugs and low competition in the private sector contribute to higher prices in specific classes (Nur Sufiza & Farida, 2018), while discounts and rebates minimal for oncology medicines but more substantial for cardiovascular drugs, also affect pricing patterns (Vogler et al., 2013).

Price variation is particularly pronounced across different product types. Imported generics exhibited significantly higher price variation compared to innovators, reference biologics, and local generics. This contrasts with previous local studies reporting minimal variation for originator and generic products (Pharmaceutical Services Programme, 2018, 2020b; Wong et al., 2019). Among bidders, innovators showed less price dispersion than generics, consistent with findings from the United States where generics, particularly for neuropsychiatric medications, display substantial variability (Bernstein et al., 2019). When comparing the same drug across product types, most innovators and local generics were priced higher than imported generics, with few exceptions. Imported generics are prone to price volatility due to lower profit margins and currency fluctuations, while local generics face similar pressures from imported raw materials and limited production scale. Government policies prioritizing local products may further reduce competitive pressure from imported alternatives, contributing to observed pricing patterns.

In terms of dosage forms, oral products such as modified-release, extended-release, prolonged-release, and disintegrating tablets show greater price variations compared to parenteral forms (intravenous and subcutaneous) and standard oral formulations. However, these variations do not result in higher-tier pricing compared to the reference, as demonstrated by the modelling analysis. Cuomo et al. (2017) reported no significant difference in prices between injectable and oral cancer medications, as both were generally comparable. In contrast, Mamiya and Igarashi (2021) observed that injectable medications are more susceptible to price increases than oral medications. Son (2020) also highlighted that injectable forms encounter less price competition than orally administered medications. Although the studies did not provide specific reasons, several factors may explain why injectables are more susceptible to price increases. According to Frank et al. (2021), fewer market entries and a high rate of exits for small-market injectable products contribute to price instability and limited price reductions.

An increased number of competing suppliers in the procurement process reduces price variation and the likelihood of higher-tier pricing. The number of competing agents is a more direct measure for market competition than the number of products, as a single product may be supplied by multiple agents. In such settings, increased agent-level competition may lead to greater bid price dispersion, reflecting diverse bidding strategies. Specifically, having more than six competing agents is associated with more stable pricing, as wider bid dispersion allows buyers to select lower-priced options, potentially reducing price variation and the probability of higher-tier pricing. However, pricing strategies may still be influenced by product owners or principals, which can constrain agents’ pricing flexibility and limit the extent of price competition. These findings align with previous studies showing that greater market competition forces prices closer to minimum levels (Hyman & Silver, 2019; D. G. Li et al., 2018; Son, 2020). Evidence also indicates that supply-side factors, such as the number of suppliers, have a stronger influence on price variation than demand-side factors (Allende et al., 2024). Patent expirations increase market entry by an average of 2.4 sellers, reducing prices by 28% over four years, with each new entrant contributing an 11.7% price decrease (Allende et al., 2024). These findings underscore the importance of fostering supplier diversity and competition to enhance procurement efficiency and promote equitable access to medicines.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. Unlike earlier national reports (Pharmaceutical Services Programme, 2018, 2020b; Wong et al., 2019), which relied on short-term median price comparisons, this analysis used five years of procurement data and rank-based methods, enabling assessment of regional price variation and longer-term market dynamics. Comparisons with previous studies should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously, as earlier analyses focused mainly on centralized pooled procurement, which may mask variability inherent in segmented systems. Differences in study design, analytical methods, and procurement scope may therefore partly explain the divergent findings on regional price variation.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the study does not explicitly adjust for time-related price changes, such as inflation or strategic pricing behaviours; however, these effects are inherently reflected in the prices analysed, and some outliers may be attributed to these dynamics. Second, although the year-based analysis captures temporal trends relevant to the MOH’s annual budgeting and fiscal planning, a more granular monthly or quarterly analysis could provide deeper insights into short-term price fluctuations. Third, findings such as low variation, high dispersion, or low tiering do not necessarily equate to affordability, which remains beyond the scope of this analysis. Results describe procurement-level pricing and competition, but cannot be directly linked to access, utilisation, or financial burden. Future studies incorporating affordability measures and patient-level data would provide a more comprehensive assessment of procurement impacts. Fourth, the logistic regression model had relatively low sensitivity and was intended for explanatory analysis to identify factors associated with higher-tier pricing, defined relative to the current median price, rather than for predicting individual prices. Multilevel modelling could offer additional insights into facility- or region-level effects and should be considered in future studies. Fifth, potential endogeneity such as unobserved supplier strategies and procurement-specific constraints cannot be fully ruled out. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Lastly, actual procurement practices may vary across and within facilities despite standardized treasury guidelines, and such variations may have influenced some observed patterns. Future work incorporating qualitative insights could help explain these practice-driven discrepancies more comprehensively.

Conclusion

This study shows that price variation in Malaysia’s MOH segmented procurement system is associated with factors such as geographical zones, facility types, procurement values, therapeutic subgroups, product characteristics, and the level of competition among agents and products. Price dispersion among bidders further highlights the role of both intra- and inter-brand competition. Factors such as geographical zones, procurement values, therapeutic subgroups and the number of competing agents were key contributors to higher-tier pricing. These findings underscore how market and non-price dynamics shape medicine pricing within segmented procurement. Enhancing transparency, coordination, and supplier engagement may reduce unnecessary variability and improve equity in public purchasing. Future research should compare pricing outcomes between pooled and segmented procurement to identify the most cost-effective approach across different therapeutic areas.
