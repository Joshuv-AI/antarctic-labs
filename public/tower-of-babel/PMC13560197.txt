Antigen-specific adaptive immunity in Parkinson’s disease: peripheral priming, brain-border reactivation and parenchymal injury
Authors: Kehao Wang, Ke Han, Yanqing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdaptive immune signals in Parkinson’s disease (PD) now extend beyond descriptive neuroinflammation. Human genetic studies have implicated HLA variation, peripheral assays have detected α-synuclein- and PINK1-reactive T cells, neuropathology has identified early nigral CD8 + T-cell accumulation, and PD-relevant models have linked α-synuclein or mitochondrial antigen presentation to defined T-cell programs. This Review first places these findings in the modifying context of aging and then examines two antigen-specific routes across peripheral T-cell priming, central nervous system (CNS)-border reactivation or retention, and parenchymal effector injury. The α-synuclein/HLA-II/CD4 + T-cell route has the clearest support from human T-cell reactivity and border-associated macrophage (BAM)-dependent antigen presentation. The mitochondrial antigen/MHC-I/CD8 + T-cell route is strongest in PINK1–Parkin dysfunction and inflammatory settings, including CD8 + T-cell-mediated killing of Pink1 − / − dopaminergic neurons after mitochondrial antigen presentation. Trafficking mechanisms drawn from experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), multiple sclerosis (MS), viral encephalitis and cerebral malaria provide useful candidate pathways for PD studies, especially for post-vascular retention and glia-limitans passage. We also consider viral antigens as potential contributors to resident-memory-like CD8 + T-cell pools in the brain and discuss why PD models should be selected according to antigen axis, T-cell subset and immune context.Keywords: Parkinson’s disease, Adaptive immunity, α-synuclein, PINK1, Parkin, Brain borders, Border-associated macrophages, T cells

Introduction: from neuroinflammation to a compartmentalized immune circuit

Parkinson’s disease (PD) has traditionally been described through α-synuclein aggregation, dysfunction of vulnerable dopaminergic neurons and progressive failure of nigrostriatal circuitry [1]. This neuron-centered model remains essential. However, it does not resolve whether immune signals in PD are active participants in disease progression. The immune literature now adds another layer: adaptive immune responses with identifiable antigen specificity, anatomical localization and disease-stage dependence. Accordingly, the broad term neuroinflammation is no longer sufficiently discriminating. It collapses processes that may occur in different compartments and at different times, including antigen release, peripheral priming, vascular entry, local reactivation, cytokine-mediated injury and cytotoxic recognition of stressed neurons. A mechanistic review therefore has to separate these steps before asking how they interact.

Several lines of evidence now support this compartmental model. HLA-linked susceptibility implicates antigen presentation in PD risk. T cells from patients with PD recognized defined α-synuclein epitopes, and responses to α-synuclein and PINK1 were detected in prodromal disease [2, 3]. Human post-mortem studies showed early CD8 + T cell accumulation in the substantia nigra [4], and recent spatial and single-cell analyses identified clonally expanded, resident-memory-like T cells in the PD nigral niche [5]. Mechanistic work further showed that PINK1 and Parkin restrained mitochondrial antigen presentation on MHC class I [6], and that border-associated macrophages (BAMs) could act as decisive antigen-presenting cells for α-synuclein-driven CD4 + T-cell responses [7]. These findings do not yet define a single linear pathway. Instead, they anchor different parts of an adaptive immune circuit, T-cell specificity, brain-border activation and local effector activity.

This Review therefore uses a compartmental, antigen-specific adaptive immune perspective to organize the field. In this schema, antigen exposure and T-cell priming outside the brain may be followed by entry into brain-border compartments, local reactivation or retention, and effector activity within vulnerable parenchymal niches. The model is useful only if each step is assigned a clear evidentiary status: direct human observation, PD-relevant mechanism, or candidate mechanism inferred from broader CNS immunology (Fig. 1).

Converging evidence for adaptive immunity in PD

The current case for adaptive immunity in PD rests on convergence across genetics, antigen-specific immunology and neuropathology. At the genetic level, fine-mapping and meta-analytic studies of the HLA locus have continued to implicate antigen presentation in PD susceptibility, with a recurrent signal around HLA class II (HLA-II) variation [8, 9]. These findings place antigen presentation within the genetic aspect of PD, but they do not define the relevant peptide landscape.

At the antigen-recognition level, Sulzer and colleagues showed that T cells from patients with PD recognized α-synuclein-derived peptides, with prominent reactivity near Y39 and serine 129 [2]. Subsequent studies extended this observation into earlier disease states. α-synuclein-specific T-cell responses were enriched in preclinical and early PD [10], PINK1 emerged as an additional T-cell target, and responses to PINK1 and α-synuclein were elevated in prodromal PD [3, 11], including REM sleep behaviour disorder, hyposmia and genetically enriched cohorts.

At the anatomical level, CD8 + T-cell infiltration of the substantia nigra could precede overt synucleinopathy and dopaminergic neuronal loss, while later infiltration paralleled progressive pathology [4]. Spatial transcriptomic, single-nucleus and T-cell receptor sequencing studies add molecular resolution by identifying clonally expanded T cells with resident-memory-like features in the PD substantia nigra [5]. Together, these findings support an antigen-experienced adaptive immune component in defined PD contexts. The remaining task is to determine which components are causal, which are amplifying and which are therapeutically tractable in specific endophenotypes.

Aging as a modifying context for adaptive immunity in PD

Aging is an important context for interpreting antigen-specific adaptive immunity in PD. Adaptive immune responses do not merely accompany brain aging; they can actively shape it. During aging, T cells accumulated in neurogenic niches, white matter and other CNS or border-associated regions, where they were linked to altered neural stem-cell activity, interferon-responsive oligodendrocyte and microglial states, axon degeneration, and cognitive or motor decline [12–14]. Systemic aging also remodels the T-cell compartment through reduced naïve-cell supply, altered differentiation, impaired survival and loss of durable T-cell memory [15].

This aging-related immune and tissue context helps explain PD-associated T-cell responses is not isolated events. α-Synuclein-, PINK1- and mitochondrial antigen-reactive T cells may arise or persist in a host environment already shaped by altered immune surveillance, increased glial reactivity, barrier changes and reduced repair capacity [2, 10, 11]. This does not imply that age-related adaptive immune remodeling is sufficient to cause PD. Rather, aging may lower the threshold for disease-related antigen recognition, T-cell retention or local reactivation, whereas PD-relevant antigens provide disease-specific content. How general immunosenescence and brain aging couple to α-synuclein- or mitochondrial antigen-driven neurodegeneration remains an important unresolved question.

Antigenic drivers: α-synuclein, mitochondrial cargo and context

Before discussing PD-specific antigens, it is useful to define the two T-cell profiles. CD4 + T cells are primarily activated by extracellular or endolysosomal peptides presented on MHC class II molecules by antigen-presenting cells (APCs), and they typically mediate helper or inflammatory responses, including Th1 and Th17 [16, 17]. By contrast, CD8 + T cells recognize intracellularly derived peptides presented on MHC class I molecules. They can acquire cytotoxic functions, including IFN-γ/TNF production and perforin-granzyme-mediated killing [18, 19]. This distinction is directly relevant to PD. α-synuclein-derived peptides are most strongly linked to HLA-II-restricted CD4 + T-cell helper or inflammatory responses, whereas mitochondrial cargo generated under PINK1–PRKN dysfunction or inflammatory stress has been linked to MHC-I-restricted CD8 + T-cell cytotoxicity.

Two antigenic routes currently have the strongest mechanistic support. The α-synuclein/HLA-II/CD4 + T-cell route is anchored in human T-cell recognition, Lewy pathology and HLA-II genetics (Fig. 2A). Human studies implicated regions spanning Y39 and the C-terminal phosphorylated serine 129 (pS129)-containing sequence, and recent work suggested that pS129-containing peptides could promote Th17-skewed responses that tracked with motor severity in earlier disease [2, 20]. Sulzer et al. identified α-syn32–46 within the Y39 region as a major HLA-II-associated T-cell epitope. In vitro HLA-binding assays showed that this peptide bound with high affinity to HLA-DR molecules containing DRB1*15:01 and DRB5*01:01. The relevant interaction was therefore peptide–HLA-II binding and subsequent presentation to CD4 + T cells, not direct binding of α-synuclein to the HLA-DRB1*15:01 DNA sequence [2]. In humanized mice, immunization with this epitope induced intestinal inflammation, enteric neuronal injury and a tissue-resident-like mucosal CD4 + T-cell program [21]. Together, these findings supported α-synuclein as an HLA-II-linked antigenic driver of helper/inflammatory T-cell responses.

The mitochondrial antigen/MHC-I/CD8 + T-cell route is mechanistically distinct. Mitochondrial antigen presentation (MitAP) can involve mitochondrial-derived vesicles (MDVs) (Fig. 2B). Under mitochondrial stress, damaged mitochondria can generate MDVs that carry selected mitochondrial cargo, including oxidized mitochondrial proteins with potential damage-associated molecular pattern activity [22]. Under normal mitochondrial quality-control conditions, PINK1 and Parkin participate in an MDV-mediated trafficking pathway that helps deliver damaged mitochondrial components to the late endosome/lysosome for degradation [23, 24]. In the MitAP context, PINK1 and Parkin also restrain MDV-dependent mitochondrial antigen presentation. When PINK1–Parkin-dependent restraint is absent, inflammatory stimuli can promote processing of mitochondrial proteins and loading of mitochondrial peptides onto MHC class I molecules. The cell-surface event is therefore display of mitochondrial peptide–MHC-I complexes, not transport of intact MDVs to the plasma membrane [6]. In Pink1 −/− mice, intestinal infection with Gram-negative bacteria elicited mitochondria-specific CD8 + T cells in the periphery and brain, dopaminergic terminal pathology and L-DOPA-responsive motor impairment [25]. These data place mitochondrial cargo at the centre of a cytotoxic antigenic route, especially in PINK1–PRKN biology and inflammatory settings.

The two routes are best treated as context-dependent endophenotypes. Lewy pathology-high, class II-weighted disease may preferentially engage α-synuclein-reactive helper responses. PINK1–PRKN-deficient or mitochondrially stressed disease may weight MitAP and MHC-I-linked cytotoxicity more heavily. Later disease may become immunologically broader as ongoing injury expands the pool of α-synuclein species, mitochondrial cargo and danger signals.

Brain-border checkpoints for antigen-experienced T cells

Peripheral antigen experience creates an anatomical question: where can circulating T cells re-encounter CNS antigens and acquire CNS-specific effector features? This question is particularly relevant to PD. α-synuclein- and PINK1-reactive T cells have been detectable outside the brain, whereas T-cell accumulation and clonal expansion are observed within the substantia nigra. This pattern implies an intervening brain-border step that remains incompletely defined. The dura, leptomeninges, perivascular spaces and choroid plexus are active neuroimmune interfaces. They can sample cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)-derived material while remaining accessible to circulating immune cells [26, 27]. These regions therefore provide plausible sites for antigen re-encounter before deep parenchymal involvement.

BAMs are non-parenchymal CNS-resident macrophages located in the meninges, choroid plexus and perivascular spaces [28–30]. Their border localization distinguishes them from parenchymal microglia, which occupy brain tissue and survey local neuronal and synaptic environments. BAMs can be divided by anatomical structure into perivascular, leptomeningeal or dural, and choroid plexus-associated subsets. Single-cell studies showed that these border macrophages had tissue-shaped transcriptional identities [31, 32]. Morphologically, homeostatic microglia are highly ramified and continuously survey the parenchyma. By contrast, BAM subsets can appear elongated along vessels, bipolar in dural compartments or variably ramified at the choroid plexus [33–36]. Functionally, microglia are specialized for parenchymal surveillance, synaptic and circuit homeostasis, local phagocytosis and injury responses. BAMs are positioned to regulate CNS-border immunity, antigen presentation, perivascular or CSF dynamics, vascular permeability and immune-cell entry [37–40]. Because marker overlap is substantial, experimental distinction between BAMs and microglia should not rely on a single marker. Microglia are commonly enriched for TMEM119, P2RY12, SALL1 and, in mice, Siglec-H [41–45]. BAMs are commonly enriched for CD206/MRC1 and often express CD163 or LYVE1. However, CD163 and LYVE1 are not BAM-specific [46–50]. Iba1 and CX3CR1 should be treated as pan-myeloid or overlapping microglia/BAM markers [45, 51]. Operationally, BAM or CNS-associated macrophage-like populations are best defined as CD45 + CD11b + Iba1 +/CD68 + CD206/MRC1 + cells located in the meninges, choroid plexus or perivascular spaces. These cells should lack, or express low levels of, microglia-enriched markers such as TMEM119, P2RY12 and SALL1. Conversely, parenchymal TMEM119 + P2RY12 + SALL1 + CD206/MRC1 − cells support a microglial identity.

No universal gold-standard method exists for isolating BAM-specific responses in vivo. The strongest inference comes from combining anatomical localization, multi-marker validation, functional depletion and genetic targeting. Intracerebroventricular or cisterna magna delivery of clodronate liposomes has been used to deplete perivascular and meningeal macrophages. However, depletion efficiency and specificity depended on route, dose and timing, and required spatial verification [52]. Cre-based strategies added resolution, although standard Cx3cr1-CreER or pan-myeloid approaches did not cleanly separate microglia from BAMs. Tmem119-CreERT2 preferentially targeted microglia, whereas binary or intersectional Cre strategies biased targeting toward parenchymal microglia or vasculature-associated macrophage subsets, respectively [7, 51]. In human tissue, where depletion, Cre systems and fate mapping are unavailable, BAM studies relied mainly on marker-based enrichment, spatial localization and single-cell or spatial profiling. For example, human CNS-border macrophages were enriched as CD45 + CD206/MRC1 + Lin − cells and expressed MRC1/CD206, CD163, LYVE1 and F13A1, whereas microglia were enriched for P2RY12 and TMEM119 [53–55].

In an α-synuclein overexpression model, BAMs expanded, acquired inflammatory features, localized near T cells in human PD tissue and supported the full CD4 + T-cell-driven neuroinflammatory response. Schonhoff et al. used Tmem119-CreERT2 to distinguish microglial MHC class II antigen presentation from presentation by other CX3CR1 + or clodronate-sensitive cells. Selective disruption of microglial MHC class II did not reduce Ly6Chi monocyte, CD4 + T-cell or CD8 + T-cell infiltration. By contrast, clodronate-mediated BAM depletion reduced monocyte infiltration, CD4 + T-cell infiltration and myeloid activation [7]. These findings placed BAMs in a strong position within the α-synuclein/MHC class II/CD4 + T-cell route.

The mitochondrial antigen/MHC class I/CD8 + T-cell route rests on a different and less BAM-specific evidence base. This distinction matters for PD because this route already has PD-relevant support at the levels of PINK1-Parkin dysfunction, MitAP and dopaminergic neuronal MHC class I expression. However, the border APCs that regulate CD8 + T-cell retention or reactivation remain undefined. PINK1-Parkin dysfunction permitted mitochondrial antigen presentation, and inflammatory stress induced MHC class I expression in dopaminergic neurons [6, 56]. In other CNS inflammatory settings, perivascular macrophages helped localize antigen-specific CD8 + T cells near brain vessels, and CNS-resident myeloid APCs used MHC class I to promote CD8 + T-cell brain infiltration [37, 57]. Direct BAM-dependent presentation of mitochondrial peptides to CD8 + T cells in PD has not been shown. Thus, BAMs are directly supported in the α-synuclein/MHC class II/CD4 + T-cell pathway, but remain candidate regulators in the mitochondrial antigen/MHC class I/CD8 + T-cell pathway (Fig. 3).

T-cell trafficking through vascular and glial borders

The least resolved part of this model is how T cells move from blood to border compartments and then into the parenchyma. In experimental PD, the clearest vascular evidence concerned endothelial ICAM1-LFA1 interactions, which contributed to T-cell recruitment to the CNS [58]. By contrast, the molecular control of transendothelial migration, border retention and glia-limitans passage has been mapped in greater detail in EAE, MS, viral encephalitis and cerebral malaria than in PD. In this Review, pathways involving VLA-4-VCAM-1 [59, 60], CCL2 [61], matrix metalloproteinase-2 and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP2 and MMP9) [62], junctional adhesion molecule (JAM)-family members [63, 64] and choroid plexus entry [65, 66] are treated as candidate mechanisms for PD unless tested directly in α-synuclein-specific or MitAP-specific settings.

After endothelial adhesion, border APCs may provide chemokine-rich and contact-dependent structure for T-cell retention and re-stimulation. This step is relevant to PD because α-synuclein-driven models implicated BAMs in local antigen presentation and CD4 + T-cell-dependent neuroinflammation. However, the coupling between vascular adhesion, border retention and parenchymal access remains unclear. In EAE and MS, perivascular or leptomeningeal APCs produced CCL5, CXCL9 and CXCL10. Antigen recognition together with CCR5 and CXCR3 signaling reinforced T-cell adhesiveness and residence at the border [67]. In PD, BAM necessity was shown most clearly for α-synuclein-driven CD4 + inflammation, whereas the detailed molecular route from vascular adhesion to parenchymal access remained underdefined [7].

The glia limitans adds a further level of selectivity. Direct evidence in PD remains limited, but this barrier step is central to the framework. T cells detected in PD-relevant nigral or border-associated niches must cross, or signal across, glial and CSF-facing interfaces before contributing to parenchymal neuronal injury. In EAE and viral encephalitis, astrocytic CCL2, MMP2/MMP9 activity and JAM-family interactions regulated leukocyte progression into the parenchyma [61–63]. JAM-B was also linked to CD8 + T-cell trapping or passage at CNS border zones [64]. The choroid plexus provided a parallel blood-to-CSF route for activated lymphocytes [65, 66].

Effector programs linking antigen recognition to neuronal injury

A compartmental immune framework becomes relevant to PD pathogenesis only if T-cell programs can damage vulnerable neural circuits. The CD4 + route has substantial support. In α-synuclein overexpression models, deletion of TCRβ or CD4 dampened myeloid MHC class II responses and reduced neurodegeneration [68]. Human epitope studies suggested that α-synuclein-specific helper responses could acquire pathogenic features. pS129-containing peptides preferentially supported Th17 biology, and α-synuclein functioned as both an antigen and an adjuvant-like stimulus for helper polarization [20]. In human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-based co-cultures, IL-17-producing T cells killed patient-derived midbrain neurons, whereas blockade of IL-17 or its receptor rescued the phenotype [69].

The CD8 + route raises a more direct cytotoxic question. Human pathology and spatial studies showed early nigral CD8 + T-cell accumulation, neuronal contacts and clonal expansion with resident-memory-like features in PD [4, 5]. A key proof of principle came from Pink1 −/− systems. Under inflammatory induction, mixed neuronal preparations from Pink1 −/− mice stimulated CD8 + T-cell hybridomas specific for 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase (OGDH), a mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid-cycle enzyme [25]. This finding indicated that OGDH-derived mitochondrial peptides could be processed and presented through an MHC class I-dependent pathway in this experimental context. Cell-sorting experiments further identified the glutamate-aspartate transporter-negative neuronal fraction as the effective antigen-presenting population. GLAST is also known as EAAT1/SLC1A3. The GLAST-positive glial fraction did not show the same activity [25]. In cytotoxicity assays, OGDH peptide-specific CD8 + T cells selectively killed Pink1 −/− tyrosine hydroxylase-positive (TH +) dopaminergic neurons. Wild-type dopaminergic neurons and non-dopaminergic neurons were much less affected. Direct peptide loading onto MHC class I served as a positive control for peptide-driven killing [25]. These results were important because they connected three otherwise separable events: PINK1-dependent mitochondrial antigen presentation, neuronal MHC class I display of a defined mitochondrial peptide and antigen-specific CD8 + T-cell-mediated dopaminergic neuronal injury. They therefore provided mechanistic proof of principle for a mitochondrial antigen/MHC class I/CD8 + T-cell cytotoxic route in a PD-relevant, PINK1-deficient inflammatory setting. However, they did not establish OGDH as a dominant endogenous CD8 + T-cell antigen in idiopathic human PD. Nor did they define where cytotoxic licensing occurred along the blood-border-parenchyma route.

A later phase of immune amplification remains more hypothetical. Neuronal stress and glial activation may increase the availability of α-synuclein species, mitochondrial cargo and danger signals in interstitial fluid, CSF and lymphatic drainage routes. Such material could support epitope spreading and repeated border reactivation. In PD, this idea remains a hypothesis generated from the coexistence of α-synuclein pathology, mitochondrial stress, glial activation and antigen-experienced T-cell signatures. It is not yet a reconstructed causal sequence. Human PD has not been traced end to end from primary antigen recognition to chronic, self-amplifying immune injury.

Peripheral priming to parenchymal injury across PD endophenotypes

The compartmental, antigen-specific adaptive immune model organizes PD-associated immune events into four linked phases: prodromal priming, early CNS-border engagement, parenchymal execution and late amplification. In a prodromal phase, mucosal or peripheral inflammatory stress may expose α-synuclein and mitochondrial antigens to the adaptive immune system in permissive HLA contexts [21]. Support for this phase came from prodromal T-cell reactivity to α-synuclein and PINK1, enteric α-synuclein epitope models and Pink1 −/− infection models in which inflammatory stress drove MitAP-linked CD8 + T-cell priming [3, 10, 25].

During early CNS engagement, antigen-experienced T cells may encounter brain-border niches enriched in APCs, CSF-derived material and retention cues. The α-synuclein/MHC class II/CD4 + T-cell route is best defined at this step, with BAMs acting as local checkpoints for antigen presentation and inflammatory amplification. The mitochondrial antigen/MHC class I/CD8 + T-cell route may converge on similar border compartments, but appears to rely more heavily on peripheral APCs and stressed neurons for antigen display.

During parenchymal execution, helper and cytotoxic programs may injure vulnerable circuits through distinct mechanisms. Helper responses can sustain myeloid activation and cytokine-mediated toxicity. CD8 + T cells can target antigen-bearing neurons when the appropriate antigen-presentation context is present. In a later amplification phase, ongoing tissue injury may broaden antigen availability and stabilize inflammatory circuits. These phases are not expected to occur identically in every patient. PINK1–PRKN disease, α-synuclein-high sporadic disease and immune-high prodromal PD may occupy different positions within the same conceptual map.

Discussion

The next phase of the field requires experiments that assign antigens, APCs, clonotypes and timing to specific compartments. Naturally presented peptides should be identified by immunopeptidomics across peripheral APCs, BAMs and other border APCs, deep cervical lymph nodes and stressed neuronal preparations. This would test whether α-synuclein-derived, mitochondrial-derived or other peptides are displayed in the compartments proposed by the model. Paired T-cell receptor sequencing across blood, CSF, dura, cervical lymph nodes and substantia nigra could determine whether antigen-experienced clones are shared across compartments and where tissue-resident features emerge. Border compartments also need to be perturbed with anatomical precision. Global manipulation of CNS myeloid antigen presentation is too coarse, because dural, leptomeningeal, perivascular and choroid plexus macrophage populations may have distinct functions. Finally, time should be built into experimental design. Prodromal, early and established PD should not be collapsed into a single immune category.

A related uncertainty is whether resident-memory-like CD8 + T cells in PD brain are driven only by α-synuclein or mitochondrial self-antigens. Previous infectious antigens may also shape this pool. Smolders et al. showed that tissue-resident-memory-like CD8 + T cells populated the human brain across neurological diseases, including PD, but their antigen specificity remained unresolved [70]. Viral infection models further showed that CD8 + T cells could persist in brain or related neural tissues after viral replication had subsided, and that local antigen presentation, PD-1/PD-L1 signaling and antigen-stimulation strength influenced brain CD8 + tissue-resident memory (TRM) formation or function [71–75]. This possibility is relevant to PD but should be interpreted cautiously. A prior viral infection could leave a brain TRM pool that is later reactivated in a degenerating CNS, amplifying inflammation without initiating PD. Viral antigens could also intersect with PD antigens through molecular mimicry or cross-reactivity; for example, homologous HSV1 and α-synuclein peptides stimulated T-cell responses in PD [76]. Direct evidence that virus-induced brain CD8 + TRM cells initiate PD is lacking.

Model choice should therefore be fit for purpose [77]. No single model is preferred for antigen-specific adaptive immunity in PD. AAV models expressing wild-type human α-synuclein in immunocompetent hosts are well suited for testing whether a T-cell subset is necessary for α-synuclein-driven neurodegeneration. In this setting, CD4 + T-cell deficiency, but not CD8 + T-cell deficiency, protected tyrosine hydroxylase-positive dopaminergic neurons [68]. By contrast, RAG1 −/− bone-marrow-reconstitution models tested whether defined T-cell compartments were sufficient to mediate pathology [78]. In AAV-A53T α-synuclein models, this approach showed pathogenic potential for both CD4 + and CD8 + T cells and revealed responses to peptides near A53T and S129 [79]. Mitochondrial antigen-specific CD8 + T-cell questions are better matched to PINK1/Parkin-MitAP systems or Pink1 −/− infection models [6, 25]. α-Synuclein preformed fibril models remain valuable for aggregation and propagation, but antigen-specific adaptive immune claims require additional readouts, including peptide restimulation, T-cell receptor sequencing, MHC-I/MHC-II immunopeptidomics or peptide-MHC multimer analysis [80]. Thus, the guiding principle should be fit-for-purpose model selection rather than a universal hierarchy of PD models.

Conclusions

PD immunobiology is now moving from descriptive neuroinflammation toward antigen-specific and compartment-specific mechanisms. Peripheral priming, CNS-border reactivation or retention and parenchymal effector injury are linked processes, but their relative importance will probably differ across genotype, disease stage and tissue context. This view has practical implications. Peripheral tolerance or antigen-specific modulation may be most useful before tissue-adapted inflammatory programs are entrenched at brain borders or within the substantia nigra. Broad late anti-inflammatory treatment may miss earlier windows in which antigen presentation, border reactivation or selected chemokine and cytokine programs are the dominant bottlenecks. A therapeutically useful immune framework for PD will therefore need to identify the relevant antigen, APC, clonotype, compartment and disease stage for each targetable endophenotype (Tables 1 and 2).
