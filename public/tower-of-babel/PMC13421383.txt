Engineering adsorption selectivity in MFI zeolites through suppression of silanol-driven water competition for efficient acetone capture
Authors: Diógenes Honorato Piva, Sabrina Grigoletto, Fatim-ezzahra Merfouk, Kavosh Karami, Iago Maye, Sajjad Ghojavand, Mohammad Wahiduzzaman, Veronique Van Speybroeck, Joeri Denayer, Matthias Thommes, Guillaume Maurin, Svetlana Mintova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Efficient physisorption of acetone for indoor air purification critically depends on selecting a hydrophobic sorbent with relatively small pores, which not only provides optimal confinement for acetone molecules but also minimizes competitive adsorption of water. Here, we first systematically assessed, by density functional theory calculations, the adsorption behaviors of both acetone and water in pure silica and a series of metal-modified MFI type zeolites. These simulations reveal that in the pure silica MFI, acetone and water compete for adsorption at the silanol groups, and the incorporation of molybdenum effectively reduces the presence of these hydrophilic silanol groups. Consequently, metal incorporation reduces the H2O affinity of the zeolite framework favoring a selective adsorption of acetone over water via a coordination of its carbonyl oxygen atoms to the metal sites. In situ FTIR measurements confirmed that Mo-containing MFI largely devoid of surface silanols promotes interactions with the Mo species and extra-physisorption within the zeolite channels. Dynamic breakthrough experiments demonstrated enhanced acetone capture by Mo-MFI compared with Si-MFI in the presence of water. This joint experimental–computational study demonstrates that Mo-containing, silanol-annealed MFI zeolite is a highly efficient microporous adsorbent for the challenging selective adsorption of acetone under real conditions, i.e. in the presence of water.

Introduction

In recent years, the rapid development of industry has led to a nearly 9% annual increase in the emission of volatile organic compounds (VOCs), raising significant concerns about their impact on human health.1 VOCs affect both indoor and outdoor air quality, and prolonged exposure can cause severe damage to the respiratory, nervous, and hematopoietic systems, as well as critical diseases such as cancer and cardiovascular disorders.2,3 Common VOCs include methanol, ethanol, isopropanol, benzene, toluene, and acetone. In particular, exposure to acetone vapor at a low concentration can trigger symptoms including headaches, fatigue, and vomiting, posing a serious risk to the nervous system and internal organ function.3 Consequently, in some countries such as Sweden, the exposure limit of acetone is 250 ppm for a working day (8 h) and 500 ppm for a short-time exposure (15 min). Therefore, the development of efficient systems to control and reduce the emissions of VOCs in general and acetone in particular in daily life is an essential challenge for society.

Currently, adsorption is regarded as one of the mainstream technologies for VOC removal due to its low energy consumption, low cost, simple operation, and lack of secondary pollution.4 Various sorbents, such as activated carbons,5 zeolites,6 and metal–organic frameworks (MOFs)7 have been utilized for VOC adsorption. Among these, MFI-type zeolites have been widely studied for the adsorption of VOCs based on their selective adsorption due to their specific pore size, shape and different degrees of hydrophobicity.8 An MFI-type structure is characterized by a porous network formed by the interconnection of straight circular channels (5.4 × 5.6 Å) with sinusoidal and elliptical channels (5.1 × 5.4 Å). MFI-type zeolites show significantly lower adsorption and desorption efficiencies for larger VOC molecules such as xylenes, compared to smaller molecules like benzene (5.3 Å) and acetone (4.6 Å) for which confinement effects play a key role.8 An ideal zeolitic adsorbent for selective VOC removal should have high VOC affinity and large adsorption uptake capacity combined with easy regeneration, alongside a hydrophobic pore environment. It is well known that humidity has a vital effect on the performance and adsorption capacity of zeolites, once water molecules compete with VOCs for the adsorption sites.9 Therefore, the development of adsorbents with the ability to simultaneously adsorb a variety of VOCs while exhibiting hydrophobicity is generally considered as a key challenge in VOC removal. Our previous study has found that the metals in the VIB group (Mo and W) introduced into an MFI-type structure effectively prevent the formation of silanol groups, enhancing the hydrophobicity of the zeolite framework.10,11 Consequently, the healing of silanols by metals incorporation should represent a step forward in applications where hydrophobic and/or highly stable zeolites are required, such as for selective VOC adsorption in the presence of humidity.

In this work, density functional theory (DFT) calculations first assessed the potential of the pure silica and silanol-containing MFI zeolites to effectively adsorb acetone over water. The introduction of silanol groups was shown to enhance the water affinity of the zeolite framework, leading to a decrease in the interaction energy difference between acetone and water and hence an expected drop in the selective acetone adsorption performance of the zeolite as compared to the pure silica MFI. To circumvent this drawback, we further explored the healing of these silanol groups by systematically incorporating single metal-tetravalent metal ion (TiIV, ZrIV, HfIV, SnIV, WIV and MoIV) substituted Si sites. These DFT calculations identified ZrIV, HfIV, SnIV or MoIV as the most attractive metals enabling the highest interaction energy difference between acetone and water. These computational findings guided the synthesis of the silanol-containing MFI and molybdenum-containing MFI and their adsorption behaviours of acetone were investigated. In situ FTIR spectroscopy was employed to examine adsorption of acetone vapor and effects of subsequent high-temperature degassing (at 25–100 °C). Static adsorption/desorption experiments revealed that molybdenum-containing MFI can adsorb acetone as strongly as Si-MFI implying very similar acetone adsorption enthalpies derived from chromatography experiments. The dynamic adsorption behaviour of acetone was finally investigated by the continuous flow adsorption method. For dynamic adsorption, the competitive adsorption of acetone with water was explored under dry (RH = 0%) and moisture (RH = 40% and 60%) conditions showing that Mo-MFI is a selective adsorbent for acetone even in the presence of humidity. The results offer valuable insights for the development of high-performance adsorptive materials for VOC removal, attracting broader interest within the field of environmental chemistry.

Experimental

Synthesis

The synthesis of pure silica MFI (Si-MFI) and molybdenum-containing MFI (Mo-MFI) was performed following our previously published procedures.11 For the preparation of the Si-MFI zeolite sample, double-distilled water and tetra-n-propylammonium hydroxide (TPAOH, 20 wt% in water solution, Alfa Aesar) were initially mixed under stirring for 30 min, followed by dropwise addition of tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, 98%, Aldrich). The molar ratio of the reagents in the precursor suspension was 1SiO2 : 0.28TPAOH : 40H2O. The mixture was stirred for 1 h and then aged for 18 h at room temperature on an orbital shaker. After ageing the mixture was transferred to a pre-heated oven at 180 °C and subjected to hydrothermal treatment for 6 h. The product was separated by centrifugation and washed with double-deionized water until the pH of the supernatant was 7–8. The recovered solid product was oven-dried overnight at 60 °C and then calcined at 550 °C for 5 h to remove the organic template. For the preparation of Mo-MFI, 1 g of Si-MFI zeolite was mixed with 20 mL of Na2MoO4·2H2O solution (3 wt%) and stirred for 1 h. The mixture was placed in closed polypropylene bottles and transferred to an oven at 90 °C under static conditions for a period of 7 days. The hydrothermally treated product was washed extensively with double-deionized water, dried overnight at 60 °C and then calcined at 550 °C for 5 h. After calcination, the sample was ion exchanged with 0.2 M NH4Cl solution for 1 h at room temperature. The solid was recovered by centrifugation, with double distilled water, dried overnight at 60 °C and calcined at 550 °C for 5 h.

Characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded using a XRDynamic 500 diffractometer (Anton Paar) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54059 Å), operating at 40 kV and 40 mA. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) images were obtained using a JEOL JSM-IT800 Schottky field emission scanning electron microscope in high-vacuum mode (10−4 Pa) using a low accelerating voltage of 0.8 keV and a current of 10 nA. Scanning TEM (STEM) images were obtained with the same microscope operating at 30 kV, equipped with a Gatan Rio 1816 camera and a JEOL SDD EDS detector. Chemical composition was determined by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) using an Agilent 7900 ICP-MS. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) were performed using a NETZSCH STA 449F3 system in air in the temperature range of 35–600 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C min−1. In situ Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed using a self-supported pellet (1 cm2, av. 20 mg, hydraulic press at 1 T cm−2). The transmission spectra were recorded with a Nexus 670 Thermo Nicolet spectrometer. The cell was connected to a high vacuum line of 10−6 torr; the sample was kept under high vacuum overnight at 400 °C before the recording of the spectrum at 25 °C. Activated samples were first brought into contact with increasing doses of acetone vapor at room temperature (the adsorption run), and then evacuated and the temperature was increased up to 100 °C (the desorption run), in order to test the desorption spectral response of adsorbed species. At any step of the adsorption/desorption of acetone, in situ IR spectra were recorded at 4 cm−1 resolution (128 scans). Spectral intensities were usually normalized to the sample weight. In situ FTIR spectra using pyridine (Py-FTIR) were recorded by using the above system. Self-supported pellets of zeolites were activated at 450 °C for 3 h under vacuum (10−6 Torr) and then cooled to room temperature. Prior to measurements, successive small doses of pyridine were adsorbed at room temperature, followed by purging under vacuum for 20 min at 150 °C to remove the physisorbed pyridine. High resolution argon sorption measurements were performed at 87.3 K using a manometric sorption analyser equipped with 1, 10 and 1000 Torr pressure transducers (Autosorb 6100 from Anton Paar) over a wide range of relative pressures (from P/P0 = 10−6 to 1). Prior to the adsorption experiments, the MFI samples were outgassed overnight at 200 °C. Pore size/volume analysis was performed by applying a dedicated NLDFT (non-local density-functional theory) method to the argon adsorption data, assuming an oxidic surface and a cylindrical pore geometry.

Adsorption/desorption measurements

Acetone adsorption/desorption cycles were performed at 25 °C over a relative pressure range of approximately 10−4 up to 1, using a dedicated manometric vapour sorption device (VSTAR™ Quantatec, Anton Paar, Boyton Beach, Fl) where, apart from the temperature-controlled sample cell environments, the whole system was kept at 70 °C to avoid adsorption on any cold spots. After each first adsorption/desorption cycle, a second measurement cycle was repeated consecutively without prior reactivation of the sample.

Gas-phase pulse chromatography

Acetone adsorption in the Henry's law region was examined using an in-house-developed inverse gas chromatography (IGC) setup,12 constructed around an Agilent 7820A gas chromatograph. Acetone was detected by a flame ionization detector (FID). The adsorbent was packed into a stainless-steel column (10 cm length, 0.216 cm inner diameter) containing zeolite pellets to minimize pressure drop. The pellets were obtained by compressing the adsorbent powder using a mechanical press, followed by gentle crushing and sieving to achieve particle sizes of 400–600 µm. Prior to analysis, the column was activated under helium flow at 350 °C for 16 h. A 0.01 µL pulse of acetone was introduced via an automated injector, with helium as the carrier gas (30 N mL min−1). System dead time was determined using a zero-volume connector in the absence of a column, and this correction was applied to the first moment of the chromatographic peaks. Each measurement was repeated three times, and the average values were used for data evaluation. The Henry's law constant (KH) was derived from the first statistical moment of the chromatograms, and the corresponding adsorption enthalpy was obtained from the temperature dependence of KH using the van't Hoff relation.

Breakthrough experiments

Breakthrough experiments were performed under dry and humid conditions to investigate the influence of water vapour on acetone adsorption at low concentrations.13 Two helium streams were directed through temperature-controlled bubblers containing acetone and water, respectively, to generate the desired vapour compositions. By adjusting the bubbler temperatures, the partial pressures of acetone and water were precisely regulated. The two vapour streams were subsequently combined and, if necessary, further diluted with helium using a third mass-flow controller to achieve the target concentrations. The resulting gas mixture was either passed through a fixed-bed column packed with the adsorbent pellets and maintained at a constant temperature, or directed to bypass the column for baseline measurements. The effluent stream was periodically sampled and analysed by using a gas chromatograph (HP 6890) equipped with a Stabilwax® capillary column (0.25 mm i.d., Restek) and a thermal conductivity detector (TCD), using helium as the carrier gas. After each breakthrough experiment, the column was regenerated by purging with helium while heating from ambient temperature to 250 °C at 1 °C min−1, followed by a 3 h hold at the final temperature. All external lines were maintained at elevated temperature to prevent condensation of vapours within the system.

Computational methods: MFI structure models

The pure silica MFI structure was obtained from the IZA database. Following previous findings, silanol groups were introduced near the T11 Si site, located at the intersection of the straight and sinusoidal channels (Fig. 1a).14,15 Two distinct silanol-containing models were considered: one featuring vicinal silanol groups, and the other a silanol nest consisting of four adjacent silanol groups (a silanol tetrad) as illustrated in Fig. 1b. Metal-doped MFI models were then constructed by substituting a single metal atom at the T11 Si site with the most commonly reported tetravalent cations (TiIV, ZrIV, HfIV, SnIV, MoIV, and WIV) in synthesized metal-containing zeolites.16 For molybdenum, complementary structure models were generated to represent its diverse oxidation states. In addition to the Si-for-MoIV substitution, models incorporating MoVI = O and MoVI(OH)2 configurations were thus constructed to account for possible hexavalent Mo species.

DFT calculations

All MFI structure models were geometry optimized at the periodic DFT level (see Tables S1 and S2 for the optimized cell dimensions). These DFT calculations were carried out using the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP)17–19 version 6.4.1. The MFI models were fully relaxed, encompassing atomic position and unit cell parameter changes, using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange–correlation functional.20 Grimme's D3 method incorporating the Becke–Johnson damping function was applied to account for long-range dispersion interactions between atoms.21 Core electrons were described using PAW pseudopotentials,22 while valence electrons were treated with a plane-wave basis set, using a kinetic energy cut off of 600 eV. Due to the relatively large unit cell size, the sampling of the Brillouin zone was restricted to the Γ point. This level of theory has been successfully applied to explore metal-substituted zeolites and their adsorption properties.23,24 The convergence criteria were set to 10−6 eV for electronic energy and 0.02 eV Å−1 for forces. For the metal-doped models, spin-polarized (collinear) calculations were performed, showing that the cations TiIV, ZrIV, HfIV, SnIV, and MoVI have a spin multiplicity of 1 while WIV and MoIV exhibit a spin multiplicity of 3. The interaction energies (Eint) for acetone and water in the different MFI structures were further calculated using the following equation: Eint = Ezeolite+molecule − (Ezeolite + Emolecule). Here Ezeolite+molecule is the total energy of the geometry-optimized MFI structure loaded with one guest molecule (either acetone or water), while Ezeolite and Emolecule are the energies of the corresponding DFT-optimized empty MFI-structure and single guest molecule respectively.

In the case of the molybdenum-containing MFI models, additional simulations were carried out to explore acetone and water mobility once acetone is adsorbed in its preferential adsorption site. Ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations were thus performed using the CP2K software25,26 in the NPT ensemble at 300 K, employing the CSVR (canonical sampling through velocity rescaling) thermostat with a time step of 0.5 fs. The PBE exchange–correlation functional was equally used along with Grimme's D3 dispersion correction with Becke–Johnson damping. A triple-ζ basis set including valence and polarization functions was applied for all atoms, combined with a plane-wave cutoff of 600 Ry.27 GTH pseudopotentials were used to smooth the electron density of core electrons.28,29 Each AIMD simulation was first equilibrated for 5 ps, followed by a production run of 25 ps. For these AIMD simulations, we used the optimized geometries of acetone coordinated to MoIV-MFI and MoVIO-MFI as starting configurations (MoIV-MFI@acetone or MoVIO-MFI@acetone) and added a water molecule approaching either through the sinusoidal or the straight channel (Fig. 1a) with an initial Owater–Mo distance ranging from 2.5 to 3.0 Å.

Results and discussion

Impact of silanol groups on MFI acetone and water sorption affinity

We initiated our study by evaluating the adsorption affinities of acetone and water for the MFI zeolite, first considering its idealized pure silica form, and subsequently exploring the influence of silanol groups. DFT calculations first computed the interaction energies Eint for both guest molecules to qualitatively assess how humidity might compromise the selective adsorption of acetone within this series of microporous materials. In the pure silica MFI framework, acetone preferentially adsorbs near the T11 site, with a corresponding Eint of −62.7 kJ mol−1. This interaction stems from the molecular confinement of acetone (kinetic diameter ≈ 4.6 Å) within the straight, 10-membered ring channels of MFI (5.3 × 5.6 Å), enabling efficient packing and maximizing van der Waals interactions (closest contact between the oxygen atom of acetone and the framework silicon with a characteristic distance of 3.35 Å). In contrast, water adsorption at the same site is considerably less favourable, with an associated Eint of −16.1 kJ mol−1 in line with the hydrophobic nature of the pure silica MFI (Fig. 1a). This marked difference in affinity (46.6 kJ mol−1) highlights the inherent advantage of the pure silica MFI for potential selective acetone capture under humid conditions, as this material is expected to minimize the competition from water.

However, the presence of silanol groups is a common feature of real zeolite materials.30 These defects introduce localized polar sites capable of forming strong hydrogen bonds with polar adsorbates, thereby potentially enhancing the affinity of both acetone and water. Both silanol containing MFI structure models (vicinal silanol groups and silanol nest) lead to strong interactions with acetone (−92.5 kJ mol−1 and −105.3 kJ mol−1, respectively) associated with the formation of strong hydrogen bonds between the oxygen carbonyl atom of acetone and the hydrogen atom of the silanol group (with associated separating distances of 1.5–1.6 Å; see Fig. 1b). The same trend is observed for water with the formation of multiple hydrogen bonds with the silanol groups and associated interaction energy increasing to −57.6 kJ mol−1 and −80.7 kJ mol−1 (Fig. 1b) for the two silanol-containing MFI structures, respectively. Consequently, the affinity difference between acetone and water (|ΔEint (acetone–water)|) decreases from 46.6 kJ mol−1 in the pure silica MFI to 34.9 kJ mol−1 and 24.6 kJ mol−1 in the presence of vicinal silanol groups and silanol nests, respectively (Fig. 1c in grey and green). This emphasizes that the introduction of silanol defects is predicted to decrease the selectivity for acetone under humid conditions although the silanol containing MFI material is expected to maintain a certain ability for the selective adsorption of acetone over water.

Impact of metal-doping on MFI sorption affinity to acetone and water

A viable strategy for minimizing silanol groups in zeolites involves incorporating metals into the zeolite framework, with metal-substituted zeolites demonstrating a reduced occurrence of such silanol groups.14,30,31 In this context, we investigated the MFI structures with single metal-substituted Si sites with representative tetravalent cations (TiIV, ZrIV, HfIV, SnIV, WIV and MoIV). Substituting one Si atom by one metal introduces framework sites bearing vacant d orbitals, which can serve as coordination centers for guest molecules. In the present case, these sites enable an acetone molecule to coordinate the metal site via its carbonyl oxygen. Upon acetone adsorption, the initially four-coordinated metal centers undergo a modulation of their local geometry to a trigonal bipyramidal arrangement, as illustrated in Fig. 1c for WIV-MFI. The resulting metal–Oacetone distances range from 1.97 to 2.33 Å, depending on the nature of the metal site (see Table S3). Introducing a metal into the framework consistently boosts Eint compared to pure silica MFI, regardless of the metal type (Table S3). This reflects how metal substitution strengthens the interaction between acetone and the framework, with Eint ranging from −83.4 kJ mol−1 (TiIV-MFI) to −156.8 kJ mol−1 (WIV-MFI). Water molecules can also coordinate to the metal sites as illustrated in Fig. 1c for WIV-MFI, with typical metal–Owater distances ranging from 2.26 to 2.34 Å across the metal-substituted MFI models (Table S4). Mirroring acetone behaviour, water affinity increases markedly upon metal coordination, with Eint values spanning from −51.9 kJ mol−1 in TiIV-MFI to −87.7 kJ mol−1 in WIV-MFI. Despite this enhancement, acetone continues to demonstrate distinctly higher interaction energies compared to water (Table S4), indicating that in competition with water, acetone adsorption is expected to remain thermodynamically preferred. Metal incorporation effectively leads to |ΔEint (acetone–water)| values of at least 40 kJ mol−1 across all the MIV-MFI structures, with the notable exception of TiIV-MFI (Fig. 1c). WIV-MFI stands out with the highest |ΔEint(acetone–water)|; however, its interaction energy for acetone exceeds −150 kJ mol−1 suggesting that regenerating this material is expected to demand significant energy input. Among the other MIV-MFI structures, with ZrIV, HfIV, SnIV or MoIV, more moderate acetone interaction energies were calculated, effectively restoring the |ΔEint (acetone–water)| difference to values comparable to those found in pure silica MFI. Among these systems, MoIV-MFI is identified as the most promising candidate owing to its relatively easier synthesis compared to the other three metal-containing MFI zeolites.11

Acetone, water and binary sorption behaviours of Mo-MFI

Both acetone and water coordinate to the MoIV center in a bipyramidal geometry, as observed for other metals. Acetone, however, binds more strongly, featuring a shorter Mo–O bond distance compared to water (2.09 Å vs. 2.26 Å, Fig. 1a) and a notably higher interaction energy, with a |ΔEint (acetone–water)| of 44.2 kJ mol−1. Since molybdenum can exist in tetra- and hexavalent states, we equally explored acetone and water affinity of Mo-MFI considering MoVI = O and MoVI(OH)2 chemical environments according to previously investigated models.14,32 In the five-coordinated model (MoVI = O-MFI) acetone and water coordinations lead to a pseudo-octahedral geometry, with metal–Oacetone and Mo–Owater distances of 2.23 Å and 2.31 Å, respectively, as shown in Fig. 2b. The notably high acetone affinity is maintained, remaining on the same order of magnitude as in MoIV-MFI. The |ΔEint (acetone–water)| of 43.1 kJ mol−1 as high as in a pure silica MFI (46.6 kJ mol−1) and indeed higher than the value obtained for silanol-containing MFI (34.9 and 24.6 kJ mol−1 for vicinal silanol and silanol nest, respectively), which suggests that acetone adsorption is highly favourable over water regardless of the oxidation states of molybdenum. In the MoVI(OH)2-MFI, Mo is six-coordinated in an octahedral geometry and this full-coordination prevents any direct interactions between the metal and acetone or water molecules. Although acetone still shows relatively high affinity for this structure, this is mainly due to confinement effects, with an Mo–Oacetone distance of 3.97 Å (Table S3) and, with an associated Eint value of around −53 kJ mol−1 slightly lower as compared to that of the pure silica MFI-framework (−62.7 kJ mol−1; see Fig. 1a). The resulting |ΔEint,(acetone–water)| of 28.9 kJ mol−1 remains however relatively high to maintain a preferential adsorption of acetone over water.

Finally, AIMD simulations revealed that once the acetone molecule coordinates to the Mo site, regardless of the metal oxidation state, in either MoIV-MFI@acetone or MoVI = O-MFI@acetone, its mobility becomes limited to the vicinity of the metal center, even in the presence of water. This restricted dynamics is consistent with the high interaction energies predicted for acetone adsorption. Acetone remains preferentially bound near the Mo site, as evidenced by the highly localized positions sampled along the AIMD trajectories (black scatters), whereas water molecules (blue scatters) tend to diffuse away from the metal center (Fig. 2c, top). This behaviour is assigned to the strong acetone–metal interactions as shown by the high-intensity of the RDF peaks for the Mo–Oacetone atom pairs localized at about 1.95 Å (MoIV-MFI) and 2.35 Å (MoVI = O-MFI) that remain largely predominant in the presence of water (see the comparison with the intensity of the RDF peak of the Mo–Owater atom pairs in Fig. 2c-bottom). These AIMD calculations confirm the high potential of both MoIV-MFI@acetone and MoVI = O-MFI to strongly adsorb acetone even if the zeolite is exposed to the presence of water.

Synthesis and characterisation of MFI and Mo-MFI zeolites

Following the computational findings, silanol-containing MFI (Si-MFI) and molybdenum-containing MFI (Mo-MFI) were then prepared. The incorporation of Mo into zeolite framework samples was achieved through a post-synthesis hydrothermal treatment. The successful incorporation of Mo into the zeolite framework, along with its homogeneous distribution, has been previously reported.33 Fig. S1a shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of zeolites. Both samples display the characteristic diffraction peaks of the MFI type framework structure. Mo-MFI zeolite shows a phase transition from orthorhombic Pnma to monoclinic P21/c suggesting the incorporation of Mo atoms into the MFI zeolite framework.10 The successful incorporation of Mo into the zeolite framework resulted in the generation of pure acid Lewis sites (Fig. S1b). Mo in oxidation state VI was found to be more stable in Mo-containing species incorporated in the MFI framework, and based on chemical analysis by ICP (1.3 wt% of Mo), two silicon centers are substituted by MoVI = O per unit cell. Argon (87 K) sorption measurements demonstrated that both samples show typical type Ia isotherms according to the IUPAC (Fig. S2).34 By applying a dedicated NLDFT method for argon 87 K adsorption in cylindrical pores assuming an oxidic surface, an identical micropore size distribution centred at around 0.52 nm was found for both samples. The cumulative pore volume plots reveal that both samples exhibit micropore volumes of 0.19–0.20 cm3 g−1. A step in the argon sorption data, associated with the typical MFI crystallographic monoclinic to orthorhombic (m-o) phase transitions,35,36 can be observed for both samples at around P/P0 = 0.0008–0.002. The size and morphology of the samples was analysed by SEM. Fig. 3a and b show that both Si-MFI and Mo-MFI samples exhibit hexagonal prism crystals with a b-axis thickness of approximately 100 nm and an average length of 200 nm. These results demonstrate that the hydrothermal post-synthesis treatment does not alter the original morphology and size of the crystals. STEM-EDS mapping images reveal the homogeneous distribution of Mo atoms in the Mo-MFI zeolite (Fig. 3c). The most significant difference between the two samples is the reduction of silanol groups in the Mo-MFI, as confirmed by FTIR spectroscopy study (Fig. 3d). The Si-MFI sample exhibits intense bands in the 3750–3735 cm−1 region, attributed to both isolated external and internal silanol groups. Additionally, broad bands in the range of 3650–3500 cm−1 and 3580–3300 cm−1 corresponding to Si–OH groups perturbed by medium and strong hydrogen bonding, respectively, are observed. In contrast, these bands disappear completely in the Mo-MFI sample, while the bands assigned to isolated silanol groups are significantly reduced. Based on the semi-quantitative analysis of normalized FTIR spectra we calculated a reduction of 86% of the isolated silanol groups and 98% reduction of the strong H-bonded silanol groups for Mo-MFI zeolite (Fig. 3e). Healing of silanol defects through the hydrothermal post-synthesis treatment with Mo was previously confirmed by means of 1H NMR spectroscopy.11

Acetone adsorption on Si-MFI and Mo-MFI zeolites

The cycle experiments of acetone adsorption/desorption for Mo-MFI and Si-MFI samples are shown in Fig. 4a and b, respectively. For both materials two consecutive adsorption/desorption cycles have been measured i.e., after the last desorption point was taken in the first cycle, the pressure was reduced down to the ultralow vacuum regime, and a subsequent second adsorption/desorption cycle was then performed (details are given in the SI). This allows the differentiation of strong (irreversible) and weak (reversible) adsorption. In the first cycle both strong (irreversible) and weak (reversible) adsorption occur, while in the second cycle, only weak adsorption occurs, i.e. molecules which could be desorbed in the first cycle, can be re-adsorbed. Hence, the second cycle reflects only weak (reversible) adsorption, while the first cycle provides information on both strongly and weakly adsorbed molecules. For Mo-MFI (Fig. 4a), the good agreement of both isotherms in the micropore filling region (relative pressure below ca. p/p0 = 0.1) indicates that acetone could be reversibly adsorbed in the micropores during the first adsorption cycle, i.e., the micropore volume essentially remains fully accessible for the second adsorption/desorption cycle. This is further confirmed by the consistent micropore volumes measured for acetone at 25 °C (0.18 cm3 g−1) and argon at −186 °C (0.19 cm3 g−1). In contrast, the Si-MFI sample (Fig. 4b) exhibits a pronounced decrease in acetone uptake in the micropore region after the first cycle, indicating significant reduction in accessible micropore volume due to irreversible adsorption of acetone in the micropores. This is in line with findings from the FTIR study (Fig. 3d) where Mo-MFI exhibits a significant reduction in the bands associated with isolated silanol and silanol nests (Fig. 3e). Thus the adsorption branches of the first cycles reflect both weak and strong adsorption, whereas the adsorption branches of the second cycles reflect only weak/reversible adsorption. By subtracting the 2nd cycle (weak) adsorption isotherms from the 1st cycle (strong + weak) adsorption isotherms, one can quantitatively determine the amount of strong acetone adsorption as a function of relative pressure. The Si-MFI shows a significant amount of strong acetone adsorption occurring in the zeolite micropores (Fig. 4b) and at the external surface (ca. 21 m2 g−1 as determined by N2 adsorption analysis (−196 °C) using the t-method; see the SI), whereas for Mo-MFI (see Fig. 4a), strong acetone adsorption occurs only on the external surface, affecting the slope of the “strong + weak” adsorption isotherms at the relative pressures above ca. p/p0 = 0.15 (outside the micropore filling region). These results suggest that the silanol groups preserved in the Mo-MFI sample are mainly situated on the external particle surface. The experimental results are reproducible; two-cycle sorption experiments after reactivating the samples yielding the same results were performed.

Sorption mechanism of acetone on Si-MFI and Mo-MFI zeolites

To elucidate the adsorption mechanism of acetone in Si-MFI and Mo-MFI zeolites, in situ FTIR experiments were conducted at room temperature (25 °C), as shown in Fig. 5. When acetone was adsorbed on Si-MFI (Fig. 5a), two peaks at 1708 cm−1 and 1694 cm−1 appear and become more pronounced with increasing acetone dosage. These peaks correspond to the ν(CO) vibrations of acetone molecules hydrogen-bonded to isolated silanols and silanol nests, respectively.37,38 This observation is consistent with the DFT-predicted preferential adsorption of acetone at silanol-containing sites in the MFI framework. Notably, the band at 1694 cm−1 appears first at low acetone doses, suggesting that acetone preferentially adsorbs onto hydrogen bonds present in the silanol nests and germinal silanol before interacting with isolated silanol species. Absorbance differential spectra in the ν(OH) spectral range show a significant decrease in the intensity of the ν(OH) absorption band of silanol groups following acetone adsorption (Fig. 5a), consistent with the fact that these silanol groups are engaged in hydrogen bonding with acetone. The spectroscopic signature of this interaction (Si(OH⋯CH3COCH3) is a broad band centred at ca. 3450 cm−1. At relatively high acetone pressures exceeding those at which the first gas-phase spectral features appear, a third adsorption band emerges as a shoulder centered at around ∼1720 cm−1, which is assigned to physisorbed acetone species, most probably within the straight 10-membered ring channels. For acetone adsorption on Mo-MFI zeolite (Fig. 5b), the characteristic adsorption peaks at 1708 cm−1 and 1694 cm−1 are relatively weak consistent with a reduction of Si–OH groups such as isolated silanols and the silanol nest in Mo-MFI (Fig. 3d and e). In contrast, the band at 1720 cm−1 associated with physisorbed species shows high intensity. The FTIR spectrum of Mo-MFI zeolite shows a new band at around 1684 cm−1 likely due to the coordination of acetone to MoVI withdrawing electron density from the carbonyl O, weakening the CO bond and thus lowering the ν(CO) frequency (red shift).39Fig. 5c presents the evolution of absorption band area of physisorbed acetone and acetone hydrogen-bonded to silanol groups as a function of acetone doses. First, we observed for sample Si-MFI that the integrated band area of hydrogen-bonded acetone increases with the acetone dose before reaching a steady-state at about 5.5 µmol. At this point, the band corresponding to physisorbed acetone appears and increases with further acetone doses. This result suggests that this extra-adsorption of acetone starts only after saturation of isolated silanol groups by hydrogen-bonded acetone molecules. For sample Mo-MFI, we observed that the formation of both bands associated with acetone molecules hydrogen-bonded to isolated silanols and silanol nests and physisorbed acetone species, and the band at 1684 cm−1 assigned to the coordination of acetone to MoVI appears at a concentration of 0.35 µmol acetone. While the bands of acetone coordinated to MoVI immediately reach a steady-state, the band assigned to the acetone hydrogen-bonded to isolated silanols and silanol nests increases gradually until reaching a plateau at a concentration of 4.7 µmol acetone. In contrast, the band area of the physisorbed species increases linearly and does not reach saturation under the experimental conditions.

Fig. 6 presents the temperature desorption profile of acetone that varies significantly for the two MFI samples. For the Si-MFI sample, complete desorption occurs at 100 °C, whereas in the Mo-MFI sample, at 50 °C, all the confined acetone molecules within the straight channel and most of the hydrogen-bonded acetone molecules are released from silanol groups, as manifested by the restored 3740 cm−1 band. At this temperature, only the band at ∼1684 cm−1 assigned to adsorption of acetone to MoVI in Mo-MFI still remains and its complete desorption is achieved at 75 °C. This observation suggests that the acetone interaction in Mo-MFI is slightly weaker than that corresponding to the hydrogen-bonded acetone present in the Si-MFI zeolite sample. These results are consistent with the acetone adsorption/desorption experiments discussed above (Fig. 4). The Henry constants for acetone adsorption on Si-MFI and Mo-MFI are comparable (Table 1), indicating similar adsorption energetics. Consistently, the adsorption enthalpies derived from the temperature dependence of KH are also similar for both samples, suggesting that, at zero loading, acetone adsorption is dominated by interactions with the MFI framework and in particular with silanol groups, independent of how many silanol groups are present (Mo-MFI still exhibits silanol groups, although as shown in Fig. 3d and e and discussed before, far less than in Si-MFI). Although FTIR spectra reveal a band at 1684 cm−1 assigned to acetone coordinated to MoIV species and the adsorption affinity is DFT-predicted to be higher for MoIV-incorporated MFI vs. silanol-MFI, these sites are present in low concentration and do not significantly affect the Henry constant and adsorption enthalpy values. The competitive water and acetone adsorption were studied by in situ FTIR experiments at 25 °C after pre-treating the sample with 1 Torr H2O for 30 min at different acetone doses (Fig. S5). Interestingly, the FTIR ν(CO) band at around ∼1684 cm−1 assigned to adsorption of acetone to the MoIV center in Mo-MFI is not observed after the sample was pre-treated with 1 Torr of water. This result agrees with the aforementioned simulation, which demonstrated that water molecules can coordinate to the MoIV center and prevent any direct interactions between the metal and acetone thereby reducing the adsorption capacity for acetone. Despite this, the hydrophobic surface created by Mo introduction into the MFI framework (Fig. S6) will be more suitable for acetone adsorption than water. These results are consistent with those obtained by DFT calculations, where preferential selectivity for acetone even under humid conditions with sample Mo-MFI was observed.

The dynamic adsorption behaviour of Si-MFI and MOF-MFI samples for acetone was finally evaluated under dry (RH = 0%) and humidity (RH = 40 and 60%) conditions. As shown in Fig. 7, the breakthrough curves of acetone are similar in shape under RH = 0% conditions, and Si-MFI shows a shorter breakthrough time compared to Mo-MFI (8 min vs. 6.1 min, respectively). The shorter breakthrough time suggests that adsorption is governed predominantly by weak physisorption, leading to rapid saturation of the zeolite bed. Under wet conditions (RH = 40% and RH = 60%), Si-MFI zeolite shows a shorter breakthrough time than that under dry conditions (8 min vs. 6.4 min, respectively), indicating that the presence of water vapor has a tiny negative effect on the adsorption of acetone. In contrast, the breakthrough time for Mo-MFI at RH = 40 and 60% is very close to those under dry conditions, indicating that this sample is barely affected by water vapor. Under humid conditions, the acetone adsorption capacity for Si-MFI decreases slightly, while a slight increase in acetone adsorption capacity is observed for Mo-MFI (Table 1). In addition, we revealed that Mo-MFI shows negligible water uptake regardless of RH conditions (Table 1), while Si-MFI shows values of 0.017 and 0.026 g g−1 at 40% and 60% RH, respectively. The enhanced hydrophobicity of the Mo-MFI samples was further confirmed by TGA (Fig. S6). Moreover, the elution of acetone was accompanied by a remarkable roll-up phenomenon of H2O, meaning that the H2O molecules that have already been adsorbed can be largely displaced by the acetone, indicative of the excellent competition ability of acetone over H2O on the zeolites. The earlier H2O breakthrough observed for Mo-MFI (∼1.8 and 1 min at RH = 40 and RH = 60, respectively) compared to Si-MFI (∼5.6 min) reflects the higher hydrophobicity and lower water adsorption capacity of the Mo-containing zeolite. Despite this early onset, the Mo-MFI breakthrough curve is significantly broader, indicating a heterogeneous distribution of adsorption sites where a small fraction of strongly interacting sites retains water adsorbed for longer times. In contrast, water adsorption in Si-MFI occurs on more homogeneous silanol-related sites, leading to a delayed but sharp breakthrough. This observation strongly supports that Mo-MFI zeolite is a good selective adsorbent to acetone under wet conditions, confirming the DFT predictions presented above.

Conclusions

In summary, we have combined DFT calculations, acetone adsorption, breakthrough experiments, and in situ FTIR analysis to elucidate the role of silanol defects and metal incorporation in the competitive adsorption of acetone and water in MFI zeolites. DFT calculations reveal that pure silica MFI intrinsically favours acetone adsorption over water, while the presence of silanol defects significantly enhances the affinity of both molecules through hydrogen bonding, thereby reducing acetone – water selectivity under humid conditions. Metal–ion Si substitution emerges as an effective strategy to reduce this drawback. Among the investigated tetravalent metals, Mo-containing MFI offers the best compromise between enhanced acetone affinity, preserved selectivity over water, and practical regenerability. Post-synthesis hydrothermal incorporation of Mo leads to partial framework recrystallization and a substantial reduction of silanol defects, as evidenced by FTIR spectroscopy. Consequently, Mo-MFI exhibits a markedly different adsorption behaviour compared to silanol-rich Si-MFI. While both materials show comparable acetone adsorption affinity in the Henry regime, strong irreversible acetone adsorption within micropores is suppressed in Mo-MFI and confined mainly to external surfaces. In situ FTIR experiments further reveal the emergence of a band at 1684 cm−1 assigned to acetone coordination to MoVI species, confirming the presence of specific metal – acetone interactions. Dynamic breakthrough experiments under dry and humid conditions demonstrate that acetone adsorption capacity and breakthrough time decrease in the presence of water vapour for Si-MFI, while Mo-MFI shows stable acetone uptake at different RH values and exhibits negligible water adsorption. These results confirm that Mo incorporation effectively suppresses water interference while preserving efficient and regenerable acetone adsorption. This designed-zeolite offers a promising solution for the capture of acetone from humid indoor air, a long-standing challenge in air purification.
