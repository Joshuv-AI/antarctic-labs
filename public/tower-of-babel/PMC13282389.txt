Race/ethnicity and burden of atrial fibrillation in adults with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation
Authors: Alan Go, Kristi Reynolds, Jingrong Yang, Nigel Gupta, Elisha Garcia, Teresa N Harrison, Taylor Liu, Matthew Solomon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConflicting data exist about whether race/ethnicity affects risk of atrial fibrillation (AF) but less is known about how race/ethnicity is associated with AF burden in paroxysmal AF. We identified members of Kaiser Permanente Northern and Southern California healthcare systems who underwent 14-day continuous ambulatory electrocardiographic monitoring (ZIO® XT Patch, iRhythm Technologies, Inc.) between October 2011-October 2016 and had paroxysmal AF during monitoring. Self-reported race/ethnicity, other demographics, and stroke risk factors were obtained from electronic health records. Multivariable models evaluated the association of race/ethnicity with log-transformed AF burden (i.e., percentage of analyzable time in AF). Among 1069 adults with paroxysmal AF confirmed on continuous electrocardiographic monitoring, mean age was 69.1 years, with 45% women, 4% Black, 13% Asian/Pacific Islander, and 11% Hispanic. Median AF burden was 4% (IQR:1–13%) and varied by race/ethnicity: White (3.6%), Black (1.4%), Asian/Pacific Islander (5.7%), Hispanic (2.5%). In multivariable analyses, compared to White patients, Black patients had lower AF burden (adjusted relative estimate: -58%, 95%CI:-78% to -21%), while Asian/Pacific Islander patients had higher AF burden (adjusted relative estimate: 57%, 95%CI:7% to 130%). These findings have implications about the utility of extended continuous electrocardiographic monitoring for better understanding AF and for personalizing management across diverse populations.Subject terms: Cardiology, Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is one of the most potent risk factors for ischemic stroke1, as well as a major risk factor for development of dementia2, heart failure3 and all-cause death4. AF is estimated to have affected 3 to 6 million Americans in 20105,6 and is projected to affect >12 million in the U.S. by 20307. Data from the 2019 Global Burden of Disease assessment estimates that the number of individuals with AF or atrial flutter was approximately 60 million globally8, and the international burden of AF is expected to increase substantially given the growing population of older persons nationally and internationally.

Racial and ethnic variation in the development of AF has been of particular interest, not only for understanding which populations are at differential risk and may potentially benefit from targeted surveillance but also for helping to delineate possible explanatory pathways as a basis for developing interventions9. There have been conflicting observations about the association of race and ethnicity with the incidence and prevalence of AF that has primarily focused on differences between White and Black adults. For example, in 2775 participants from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA), using a combination of hospital discharge diagnosis and self-reported information between 2000 and 2012, the combined reported incidence of AF was higher in White participants (10.69 per 1000 person-years) than in Black participants (6.39 per 1000 person-years, P < 0.001) despite Black participants having higher body mass index and higher prevalences of hypertension and smoking; of note, AF incidence was also lower in Chinese participants (6.43 per 1000 person-years, P = 0.003) and Hispanic participants (4.79 per 1000 person-years, P < 0.001)10. In a more recent study using administrative diagnosis data from 458,785 (438,333 White, 7244 Black, 9143 South Asian, 1376 Chinese, and 2689 multiracial) adults in the UK Biobank, compared with White participants, the rate of incident AF was lower in Black (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 0.60, 95% CI: 0.49–0.74), South Asian (aHR 0.59, 95% CI: 0.49–0.72), and Chinese (aHR 0.31, 95% CI: 0.12–0.83) participants after accounting for variation in clinical cardiovascular risk factors11. However, based on results from studies using extended continuous electrocardiographic monitoring, previously reported variation in AF incidence may partly reflect differential detection of AF using 12-lead electrocardiograms or reliance on clinical recognition across racial and ethnic groups12. Importantly, even less is known about the association of race and ethnicity with AF burden (defined here as the proportion of time spent in AF) among adults with paroxysmal AF, yet this could have important clinical implications as a higher AF burden increases the risk of ischemic stroke and systemic thromboembolism that appears fully independent of the presence of other stroke risk factors13, even if the topic is still an object of investigation and assessment for its clinical implications14.

Given the expanded capability of extended cardiac monitoring options in routine clinical care and the potential clinical implications, we examined the association of race and ethnicity with the burden of AF in a U.S.-based real world, community-based cohort of adults who had confirmed paroxysmal AF on non-invasive, extended continuous electrocardiographic monitoring.

Results

Cohort assembly and baseline characteristics

Between October 2011 and October 2016, we initially identified 15,779 adults who underwent long-term continuous ambulatory electrocardiographic monitoring (Fig. 1). After applying the exclusion criteria, we identified 1069 eligible adult patients with confirmed paroxysmal AF during the up to 14 days of extended continuous ambulatory electrocardiographic monitoring, with overall mean age of 69 years, 45% women, along with 4% Black, 13% Asian/Pacific Islander, 10% Hispanic, and 2% other race/ethnicity. Compared to White patients, other racial/ethnic groups had higher representation of women; higher prevalences of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and reduced kidney function; higher CHA2DS2-VASc score; and were more likely to receive ACE inhibitor or ARB therapy at entry (Table 1). In addition, compared to White patients, patients identifying as Asian or Pacific Islander and Hispanic had a lower body mass index, while Black patients had higher diastolic blood pressures and Hispanic patients had lower diastolic blood pressures. While there were differences across racial/ethnic groups in the primary reason why the monitor was prescribed, there was no significant difference (P = 0.08) when only considering the three most common listed indications (i.e., known or suspected AF/atrial flutter, palpitations, syncope or pre-syncope) (Table 1).

Race/ethnicity and burden of AF

Overall, the median analyzable wear time was 13 (IQR: 11 to 14) days with no significant differences across racial ethnic groups. The overall median AF burden was 4% (IQR:1% to 13%), with variation by race/ethnicity: White (3.6%, IQR:0.9% to 11.7%), Black (1.4%, IQR:0.2% to 6.5%) (P = 0.02), Asian/Pacific Islander (5.7%, IQR:1.4% to 19.2%) (P = 0.03), Hispanic (2.5%, IQR:0.5% to 10.3%) (P = 0.18), and other race/ethnicity (5.3%, IQR:2.5% to 17.9%) (P = 0.08). The overall median total time in AF during the monitoring period was 9.77 (IQR: 2.37–28.68) hours, with variation by race and ethnicity: White (9.96 [IQR: 2.38–27.97] hours), Black (4.72 [IQR: 0.51–16.58] hours), Asian/Pacific Islander (12.88 [IQR: 4.28–46.63] hours), Hispanic (7.01, [IQR: 1.22–21.37] hours), and other race/ethnicity (13.00 [IQR: 6.18–57.22] hours).

Using multivariable linear regression with adjustment for age, sex, prior ischemic stroke, heart failure, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, systolic blood pressure, body mass index, eGFR, proteinuria, and receipt of targeted cardiovascular medications, we found that Black race was associated with a 59% lower log-transformed burden of AF (adjusted relative estimate -58%, 95%CI:-78% to -21%) while Asian or Pacific Islander race/ethnicity was associated with a higher burden of AF (adjusted relative estimate 57%, 95% CI: 7% to 130%) (Fig. 2). In contrast, compared to White patients, where there were no significant adjusted associations between Hispanic ethnicity or other race/ethnicity with burden of AF (Fig. 2).

In a sensitivity analysis using the outcome of log-transformed duration (in minutes) of the longest continuous episode of AF, we found that the median duration was 5.0 (IQR: 3.8 to 6.3) in Whites, 4.6 (IQR: 2.0 to 6.1) in Blacks, 5.3 (IQR: 4.1 to 6.6) in Asian or Pacific Islanders, 4.9 (IQR: 3.6 to 5.9) in Hispanics, and 5.1 (IQR: 3.8 to 7.0) in other racial/ethnic groups. After adjustment for potential confounders, compared to White patients, there were similar associations as observed in our main analyses: Black patients (adjusted relative estimate 0.53, 95% CI:0.28 to 0.99), Asian or Pacific Islander patients (1.51, 95% CI: 1.03 to 2.21), Hispanic patients (0.78, 95% CI: 0.51 to 1.18) and other race/ethnicity (1.64, 95% CI: 0.71 to 3.75).

Discussion

Among adults with confirmed paroxysmal AF, we found that the cumulative burden of AF during extended (up to 14-day) continuous electrocardiographic monitoring was significantly lower in Black patients and higher in Asian or Pacific Islander patients compared with White patients, even after adjustment for stroke risk factors and other potential confounders. Results were similar when examining the longest episode of continuous AF during the monitoring period.

Our study leveraged advanced non-invasive electrocardiographic monitoring technology within an ethnically diverse population to expand our understanding of the role of race and ethnicity on burden of AF. Our findings extend beyond prior studies that have primarily focused on racial/ethnic variation in the prevalence or incidence of AF using patient self-reported administrative diagnostic codes, 12-lead electrocardiography, or implanted devices in selected populations15–33. However, these approaches have significant limitations that are susceptible to differential ascertainment, limited generalizability, and have contributed to conflicting reports about race/ethnicity and AF epidemiology in various studies. Using a combination of administrative diagnosis data, 12-lead electrocardiogram information, and extended continuous monitoring using Zio® XT among 1556 participants (43% White, 25% Black, 21% Hispanic and 14% Chinese) with mean age 76 years and 51% women in MESA, the prevalence of clinically detected AF was highest in White (11.3%) followed by Chinese (9.9%), Hispanic (7.8%) and Black (6.6%) participants, but the frequency of monitor-detected AF was not significantly different (7.1%, 5.2%, 6.9%, and 6.4%, respectively)12. These results suggested that differential detection of AF may play an important role in observed racial/ethnic variation in clinically detected AF in the U.S. Similarly, in a clinical population of U.S. adults who received dual chamber or cardiac resynchronization therapy implantable cardioverter-defibrillator, or pacemaker implanted between January 1, 2007, and June 1, 2019, compared with White patients, there was no significant difference in the incidence of device-detected AF in Black patients (adjusted HR 1.02, 95% CI: 0.98–1.06) after accounting for differences in demographic and clinical characteristics34. In contrast, in the U.S.-based Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) prospective cohort study, results from 48-hour ambulatory Holter monitoring found a higher prevalence of AF in White (5%) compared with Black (2.7%) participants20. However, none of these studies addressed whether burden of AF varied by race/ethnicity among those with paroxysmal AF.

Our study focused on patients who had confirmed paroxysmal AF on extended (i.e., up to 14 days) continuous, beat-to-beat monitoring using validated algorithms to address the question about whether race/ethnicity influences the objective burden of AF. The finding that Black patients had a lower burden of AF compared to white patients despite having a higher prevalence of risk factors for AF and cardiovascular disease, and Asian or Pacific Islander patients having higher burden of AF despite a lower prevalence of risk factors, is intriguing. In general, few studies have examined factors linked to higher vs. lower burden of AF among adults with paroxysmal AF. Among 404 participants in the Netherlands with paroxysmal AF enrolled in the Reappraisal of Atrial Fibrillation: Interaction between Hypercoagulability, Electrical Remodelling and Vascular Destabilisation in the Progression of AF (RACE V) study who had 1 year of follow-up with continuous rhythm monitoring using either a dual-chamber pacemaker or an implanted loop recorder, higher N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) level was independently associated with higher AF burden (defined as the proportion of time in AF over 1 year) (incidence rate ratio 1.75, 95% CI: 1.75–2.06)35. In a pooled analysis of 10,016 adults with a cardiac implanted electronic device (CIED) capable of characterizing daily burden of AF, participants with higher daily AF burden were more likely to be older and male31. However, neither of these studies addressed the potential association of race or ethnicity with measures of AF burden.

Underlying reasons for racial and ethnic differences in AF burden among adults with paroxysmal AF remain unclear. Some have suggested that variation in left atrial parameters (e.g., left atrial size or volume) may contribute to these observations, but conflicting data exist. For example, smaller left atrial diameter but no difference in atrial volume were observed in White compared with Black participants in the Heart and Soul Study33. Similar findings were observed in the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) Study which found smaller adjusted left atrial diameter but no difference in adjusted left atrial volume in White vs. Black participants despite having higher prevalences of cardiovascular risk factors36. However, smaller left atrial volume was observed in Chinese and higher left atrial volume in Hispanic compared with White participants in MESA37. In addition, in a small study from the UK of adults undergoing cardiac MRI, South Asian adults had a smaller left atrial volume compared with White adults, even after accounting for differences in cardiovascular risk factors24. Variation in the risk and severity of atrial fibrosis and underlying atrial cardiomyopathy may also be an alternative explanation, although very limited information exists across racial/ethnic groups38,39. Another potential contributor may include variation in the frequency of genetic variants that are linked to AF development40–42, but there are no studies examining genetic variants and AF burden by racial and ethnic group. In addition, systematic differences in use of specific preventive strategies or interventions by race or ethnicity could potentially contribute to differences in AF burden but there was no evidence of this in our study.

Our study was strengthened by evaluating adults with paroxysmal AF based on up to 14 days of continuous electrocardiographic monitoring which also facilitated accurate assessment of the burden of AF during the same period. This approach also avoids potential ascertainment bias due to potentially different surveillance methods (e.g., 24 h vs. 48 h vs. 14 days) or reporting across racial/ethnic groups, as well as the limitations of less accurate methods for determining AF burden (e.g., 12-lead electrocardiography, intermittent electrocardiographic sampling from various devices, or patient self-report). We were also able to account for differences in a wide range of potential confounders based on comprehensive electronic health record data.

Our study also had limitations. We used data from clinically driven testing and observed modest differences in the distribution of the listed primary indication for monitoring across racial/ethnic groups but there was no significant difference in the frequency of the three most common indications (i.e., AF/atrial flutter, palpitations, syncope/pre-syncope) and we are unaware that monitoring indication would influence burden of AF in patients with paroxysmal AF. However, this was not a screened cohort, so we cannot rule out selection bias in which patients received extended electrocardiographic monitoring or at what point in their AF clinical course across different racial and ethnic groups, along with potential unmeasured confounders of differential AF burden. In addition, the smaller size of the subgroup of patients who were self-reported Black race reduced the precision of estimates of AF burden. We also did not have detailed information on subgroups of Asian or Pacific Islanders in our cohort to examine if AF burden varied among disaggregated Asian, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander populations. We were unable to examine potential genetic, pathophysiological, or other pathways that explain the lower burden of AF in Black patients and higher burden of AF in Asian or Pacific Islander patients. We recognize that self-reported race and ethnicity are primarily sociocultural constructs rather than having a primary genetic or biological interpretation, and we only had limited information on various social determinants of health. Classification of paroxysmal AF was based only on the observed AF burden during electrocardiographic monitoring and did not include any pre-monitoring documentation about the presence or pattern of clinically recognized AF. We also note that the clinical implications of the observed magnitude of variation in AF burden—both based on percentage of monitored time and absolute observed time in AF—across racial/ethnic groups is unclear with regards to risks of stroke, other systemic thromboembolism, heart failure, cognitive impairment and mortality. Finally, our study sample was derived from large integrated health care delivery systems in California, so our results may not be fully generalizable to other geographic areas or practice settings in the U.S. or in other countries.

In conclusion, within a diverse, community-based U.S. population of adults with confirmed paroxysmal AF on non-invasive, continuous electrocardiographic monitoring, Black race was independently associated with a lower burden of AF while Asian or Pacific Islander race/ethnicity was associated with a higher burden of AF. As non-invasive cardiac monitoring options expand into routine care globally, future research is needed to delineate potential explanatory mechanisms for differential AF burden that may provide insights into developing more effective and personalized preventive and therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Source population

The source population included adult members of Kaiser Permanente Northern California and Southern California, two integrated healthcare delivery systems currently providing comprehensive medical care to >9.2 million persons in California USA. The collective health plan membership is highly representative of the statewide population with regards to age, sex, race/ethnicity and socioeconomic status43,44. Their collective membership also includes a wide range of commercial, individual, Medicare and Medicaid insured individuals, which further increases the generalizability of its insured population.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved under a single Institutional Review Board (IRB) by the Kaiser Permanente Northern California IRB, with the Kaiser Permanente Southern California IRB ceding reliance (approval number: 1276565). A waiver of informed consent was granted due to the retrospective study design, which involved analysis of existing data without direct participant interaction.

Identification of paroxysmal AF

As previously described13, the Kaiser Permanente Real-world Heart Monitoring StrategY Evaluation, Treatment Patterns and Health Metrics in Atrial Fibrillation (RHYTHM) Study first identified all adult members who underwent up to 14 days of continuous ambulatory electrocardiographic monitoring using Zio® XT (iRhythm Technologies, Inc., San Francisco, California) between October 1, 2011 and October 31, 2016. Zio® XT is a lightweight, lead-wire free, single patient use monitor applied to the left chest that records and stores up to 14 days of continuous, beat-to-beat electrocardiographic data. After mailing the monitor back to iRhythm Technologies, Inc., the electrocardiographic recording was processed for rhythm detection using an FDA-cleared artificial intelligence algorithm with demonstrated performance comparable to board-certified cardiologists45. Algorithm findings then underwent review by a qualified cardiac technician in creation of a report describing rhythm findings.

The goal of the present analysis was to examine the relationship between race and ethnicity with burden of AF in the setting of paroxysmal AF. We therefore identified the subgroup of patients found to have confirmed paroxysmal AF, which was defined as having >0% and <100% overall AF burden, on Zio® XT patch electrocardiographic monitoring, and any potential information about pre-monitoring presence or pattern of AF was not included in this classification approach.

From a previously described cohort of adults aged 18 years or older at the time of monitoring with at least 12 months of prior continuous health plan membership and pharmacy benefits, we identified eligible Zio® XT devices. During initial cohort assembly, individuals with discontinued membership prior to device placement, death during the up to 14-day monitoring period, prior organ transplant, prior chronic hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis, or no analyzable Zio® XT wear time were excluded, with deaths ascertained from electronic health records, Social Security Administration vital status files46, and California state death certificate files47. We then applied additional exclusions for the present analysis, including no detected AF, persistent AF, and absence of documented outpatient systolic blood pressure, body mass index, or non-emergency department measured estimated glomerular filtration rate in the year prior to monitoring.

Race and ethnicity categorization

Race and ethnicity categorization was based on self-reported information found in health system electronic health records. Patients were classified as non-Hispanic White, Black, Asian or Pacific Islander, Hispanic, other or unknown.

Burden of AF

The primary outcome of burden was defined as the percentage of analyzable wear time during the up to 14-day electrocardiographic monitoring period spent in AF13. For any episode during monitoring to be labeled as AF (which included atrial flutter), the minimum episode duration was ≥30 s. We also provide the median time in AF during the monitoring period along with interquartile (IQR, 25th and 75th) range. The extended continuous electrocardiographic monitor used in the study was independently validated for AF burden assessment, demonstrating exceptionally high correlation with atrial pacing lead measurements (R2 > 0.99)48.

Covariates

Patient age and sex were identified from health plan databases. We also identified stroke risk factors in the setting of AF from electronic health records using previously described methods49. These included prior ischemic stroke, heart failure, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and reduced kidney function. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was based on the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration estimating equation50 using outpatient serum creatinine results, as well as proteinuria ascertained from urine dipstick results of ≥1 + 51. The most recent outpatient, non-emergency department systolic blood pressure measurement (mmHg) and body mass index (kg/m2) were obtained from electronic health records within one year before the start of monitoring. We also calculated each patient’s predicted risk of thromboembolism using the ATRIA stroke risk score (range 0 to 15)49 and CHA2DS2-VASc (range 0 to 9) score52.

Analytic approach

All analyses were conducted using SAS software, version 9.4 (Cary, N.C.). Baseline characteristics were compared across racial/ethnic groups using ANOVA for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables.

Given the observed non-normal distribution of AF burden, we used a natural log transformation of AF burden and then conducted multivariable linear regression analysis of race/ethnicity and AF burden with adjustment for age, sex, prior ischemic stroke, chronic heart failure, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, systolic blood pressure, body mass index, eGFR, proteinuria, and receipt of cardiovascular medications (i.e., β-blockers, calcium channel blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme [ACE] inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers [ARB], aldosterone receptor antagonists, and statins). In a sensitivity analysis, we performed multivariable linear regression examining race/ethnicity and natural log-transformed duration of the longest continuous episode of AF during monitoring, with adjustment for the same set of covariates as in the main analysis.
