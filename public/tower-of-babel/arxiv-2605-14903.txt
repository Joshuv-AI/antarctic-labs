Twins and Co-Twins in Circulant Graphs
Sally Cockburn, Ryhory Hatavets, Will Swartz

Abstract
Circulant graphs are a widely studied family of graphs whose members possess varying amounts of symmetry. Although considerable progress has been made in finding the automorphism groups of circulant graphs under certain restrictions, a complete classification is elusive. In general, the structure of the automorphism group of a graph with twins can be simplified by separating the effect of automorphisms that permute mutually twin vertices and those that operate on the twin quotient graph. Further simplification can be achieved in twin-free, vertex-transitive graphs that have co-twins, which we define to be vertices whose neighborhoods are complementary. In this paper, we demonstrate how the these simplifications can be used provide insight into the automorphism groups and symmetry parameters of vertex-transitive graphs in general and circulant graphs in particular.



1
Introduction

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}\setminus\{0\}
that is
inverse-closed
in the sense that
A=-A
. The circulant graph
C_{n}(A)
has vertex set
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, with
u,v\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
being adjacent iff
u-v\in A
. The elements of
A
are called the generators of
C_{n}(A)
.
Each vertex in
C_{n}(A)
has degree
|A|
; equivalently
C_{n}(A)
is a
|A|
-valent (regular) graph.
With their vertices drawn evenly spaced around a circle, circulant graphs certainly have what we would intuitively call a symmetric appearance; see the three examples in Figure
1
.
More technically, the symmetries of a graph
G=(V,E)
are the bijections of
V
that preserve adjacency and non-adjacency. Symmetries are also called graph automorphisms, and they form a group
\operatorname{Aut}(G)
under composition. The structure of
\operatorname{Aut}(G)
provides different ways of measuring of just how symmetric
G
is.

Any circulant graph
C_{n}(A)
is vertex-transitive, because for any
u,v\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
,
\alpha(w)=w-u+v
is a graph automorphism satisfying
\alpha(u)=v
. However, not all circulant graphs are edge-transitive.
Note that
\tau_{-1}(a)=-a
is automorphism of
C_{n}(A)
, which implies that a circulant graph is edge-transitive iff it is arc-transitive. The classification of all arc-transitive circulant graphs was found independently by Kovacs
[
19
]
and Li
[
21
]
. In
[
24
]
, Potoc̆nik and Wilson computed the implications of this classification for 4-valent circulant graphs;
3
-valent arc-transitive circulant graphs are given in
[
8
]
.

Another way to characterize the symmetry of a graph
G
is to quantify how easy it is to block nontrivial automorphisms. For example,
we could add the requirement that graph automorphisms fix each vertex in a set
D\subset V
; if the only automorphism that does so is the identity, then
D
is called a determining (or fixing) set.
The size of a smallest determining set,
\operatorname{Det}(G)
, is called the determining number of
G
.
As another example, we could assign any one of
d
colors to each vertex of
G
and add the requirement that graph automorphisms must preserve (setwise) the color classes. If the only automorphism to do so is the identity, then the coloring is called
d
-distinguishing. The distinguishing number of a graph,
\operatorname{Dist}(G)
, is the minimum number of colors required in a distinguishing coloring. In
[
7
]
, Cockburn and Loeb give the determining and distinguishing number of all
3
-valent and
4
-valent circulant graphs by first determining their automorphism groups.

However, finding the automorphism group of circulant graphs in general is difficult. In 2007, Morris
[
22
]
provided an excellent survey of partial results, including when
C_{n}(A)
is arc-transitive, or when
n
is prime, a prime power, or square-free, and/or the elements of
A
are all divisors of
n
. More recent results cover circulant graphs whose adjacency matrix has all integer eigenvalues or all rational eigenvalues; see
[
2
]
and
[
18
]
respectively.

In this paper, we extend an approach used in
[
7
]
to investigate the automorphism group. Vertices
are nonadajcent (respectively adjacent) twins if they have the same open (respectively closed) neighborhood.
In vertex-transitive graphs, the presence of twin vertices completely decides the determining number and reduces the task of computing the distinguishing number to computing that of its twin quotient graph.
In addition,
we can separate the effect of automorphisms that permute mutually twin vertices from those that those permute twin classes.
In a few cases, iteratively looking at twin vertices in quotient graphs completely determines the automorphism group. In all cases, we can reduce the problem of finding automorphism groups of vertex-transitive graphs to finding automorphism groups of those that are twin-free.

Vertex-transitive graphs that are twin-free can still have co-twin vertices, defined in
[
7
]
to be vertices whose neighborhoods are complementary rather than equal.
In such graphs, distinct sets of co-twin vertices cannot be independently permuted, and so their presence imposes strong constraints on the automorphism group.
If a vertex-transitive, twin-free graph with nonadjacent co-twins is also triangle-free, it must be a crown graph. If it has triangles, then its automorphism group, determining number and distinguishing number are related to those of the subgraph induced by the open neighborhood of any vertex.

The organization of the paper is as follows.
Section
2
reviews some basic facts about circulant graphs.
In Section
3
, we show that the automorphism group of a vertex-transitive graph with twins is the semidirect product of the subgroup generated by twin transpositions and the automorphism group of the twin quotient graph.
We provide an algebraic characterization of which circulant graphs have twins in Section
4
and apply this to describe all connected, 5-valent and 6-valent circulant graphs.
In Section
5
, we formally introduce co-twins and provide results on graphs that are vertex-transitive, twin-free and have co-twins.

2
Circulant Graphs

Throughout this paper,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
A
is an inverse-closed subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}\setminus\{0\}
. The corresponding circulant graph
C_{n}(A)
has vertex set
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
with
u,v\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
adjacent iff
u-v\in A
. It is immediate that the neighborhood of
0
is
N(0)=A
; more generally,
N(u)=u+A
for all
u\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. Two extreme cases are
C_{n}(\mathbb{Z}_{n}\setminus\{0\})=K_{n}
and
C_{n}(\emptyset)=N_{n}
; at the intersection of these two, there is the trivial circulant graph
C_{1}(\emptyset)=K_{1}.
For
n=2m
, then
C_{n}(A)=K_{mm}
iff
A=\{1,3,5,\dots,n-1\}
.

The complement of
C_{n}(A)
is
C_{n}(\overline{A})
, where
\overline{A}
is the complement of
A
in
\mathbb{Z}_{n}\setminus\{0\}
.
As shown in
[
6
]
,
C_{n}(A)
is connected iff
\gcd(n,A)=1
; more generally,
C_{n}(A)
has
\gcd(n,A)
components. From
[
12
]
, a connected circulant graph
C_{n}(A)
is bipartite iff
n
is even and every
a\in A
is odd.

A graph automorphism is a permutation of the vertex set that respects adjacency and nonadjacency.
For all
w\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
,

\alpha_{w}:\mathbb{Z}_{n}\to\mathbb{Z}_{n}
given by
\alpha_{w}(v)=w+v
is an automorphism of
C_{n}(A)
.
For fixed
u,v\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
,
\alpha_{u-v}
is the unique translation satisfying
\alpha(v)=u
; thus
C_{n}(A)
is vertex-transitive.
The only translation that fixes any vertex is
\alpha_{0}
, the identity map.
Thus
\operatorname{Aut}(C_{n}(A))
contains
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
as a cyclic subgroup that acts regularly on the vertices.

For any unit
t\in U(n)
, it is easy to show that
C_{n}(A)\cong C_{n}(t\cdot A)
. In
[
1
]
, Ádám conjectured that any isomorphism between two circulant graphs must be of this form, but this was found to be false in general. Considerable work has been done to find restrictions under which Ádám’s conjecture holds; see
[
23
]
.

If
u\in U(n)
satisfies
u\cdot A=A
, then
\beta_{u}:\mathbb{Z}_{n}\to\mathbb{Z}_{n}
given by
\beta_{u}(v)=uv
is an automorphism of
C_{n}(A)
. We denote the set of all such automorphisms
\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(A)
; it is easy to check that is it a subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}(C_{n}(A))
. For any
v\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
,
\beta_{-1}\alpha_{1}(v)=-1-v
and

\alpha_{1}\beta_{-1}(v)=1-v
,
so
\operatorname{Aut}(C_{n}(A))
is nonabelian for all
2<n\in\mathbb{N}
. As a special case of a result proved by Godsil in
[
9
]
, if
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is a normal subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}(C_{n}(A))
, then
\operatorname{Aut}(C_{n}(A))=\mathbb{Z}_{n}\rtimes\operatorname{Stab}_{n}(A)
.

3
Twin Vertices

Definition
.

The
open neighborhood
of a vertex
u
in the graph
G=(V,E)
is
N(u)=\{v\in V:uv\in E\}
and the
closed neighborhood
of
u
is
N[u]=N(u)\cup\{u\}
. Distinct vertices
u,v
are

nonadjacent twins
iff
N(u)=N(v)

and
adjacent twins
iff
N[u]=N[v]
.

Lemma 1
.

(a)

A vertex cannot have both a nonadjacent and an adjacent twin.

(b)

Two vertices in a graph
G
are nonadjacent twins in
G
iff they are adjacent twins in the complement
\overline{G}
.

Proof.

For (a), assume
v
has both nonadjacent twin
u
and adjacent twin
w
.
Then
w\in N(v)=N(u)
and so
u\in N[w]\setminus N[v]=\emptyset
, a contradiction. Statement (b) follows from

\overline{N_{G}(v)}=V\setminus N(v)=N_{\overline{G}}(v)\cup\{v\}=N_{\overline{G}}[v]
.
∎

In a vertex-transitive graph, either each vertex has a nonadjacent twin, each vertex has an adjacent twin, or no vertex has either a nonadjacent or an adjacent twin.
In the final case, we say that
G
is
twin-free
. Figure
1
gives examples of each of these three options within the family of circulant graphs. In the first two examples, twin vertices have the same color.

Figure 1:
C_{9}(\pm 1,\pm 2,\pm 4)
(with nonadjacent twins),
C_{8}(\pm 1,\pm 3,4)
(with adjacent twins) and
C_{8}(\pm 1,\pm 2,4)
(twin-free).

Using terminology from
[
3
]
, a
minimum twin cover

T
of a graph
G
is a subset of vertices that contains all but one vertex from each set of mutually nonadjacent twin vertices. The following is a corollary of Theorem 19 in
[
3
]
.

Proposition 2
.

If every vertex of
G
has at least one nonadjacent twin, then a minimum twin cover of
G
is a minimum size determining set for
G
.

Note that if every vertex of
G
has an adjacent twin, then every vertex of
\overline{G}
has a nonadjacent twin by Lemma
1
. Any set of mutually adjacent twin vertices in
G
will be a set of mutually nonadjacent twin vertices in
\overline{G}
, so a minimum twin cover in
\overline{G}
will also be a minimum adjacent twin cover in
G
. A minimum determining set for
\overline{G}
will also be a minimum determining set of
G
.

Corollary 3
.

Let
G
be a vertex-transitive graph of order
n
such that every vertex is in a set of
t>1
mutually (nonadjacent or adjacent) twin vertices. Then
\operatorname{Det}(G)=n(1-\frac{1}{t})
.

3.1
Twin Quotient Graph

Define
u\sim v
iff
N(u)=N(v)
in a graph
G
. This is clearly an equivalence relation on
V(G)
; intuitively, the corresponding quotient graph
\widetilde{G}
is obtained by collapsing the vertices in each equivalence class to a single vertex. More precisely.
the vertices of
\widetilde{G}
are the equivalence classes

[u]=\{v\in V(G)\mid u\sim v\}
, with
[u]
and
[z]
adjacent in
\widetilde{G}
iff there exist
v\in[u]
,
w\in[z]
such that
v
and
w
are adjacent in
G
.
By definition of
\sim
, all vertices in an equivalence class have the same neighbors, so in this case, distinct vertices
u
and
z
are adjacent in
G
iff
[u]
and
[z]
are adjacent in
\widetilde{G}
.
We refer to
\widetilde{G}
as the
nonadjacent twin quotient graph

and
[u]
as the
nonadjacent twin class
of
u
.
It can be verified that
\widetilde{G}
has no nonadjacent twins; see
[
3
]
. However it is possible that
\widetilde{G}
has adjacent twins; for example, if
G=K_{2,3}
then
\widetilde{G}=K_{2}
.

Similarly, we can define
u\wedge v
iff

u
and
v
are adjacent twin vertices in
G
, and create a corresponding
adjacent twin quotient graph
, which we denote by
\widehat{G}
. In this case, distinct vertices
u
and
z
are adjacent in
G
iff either
[u]=[z]
or
[u]
and
[z]
are adjacent in
\widehat{G}
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
G
be a vertex-transitive graph.
Then the adjacent twin quotient graph of the complement
\overline{G}
is the complement of the nonadjacent twin quotient graph of
G
; equivalently,

\widehat{\overline{G}}=\overline{\widetilde{G}}.

Proof.

If
G
has no nonadjacent twins, then by Lemma
1
,
\overline{G}
has no adjacent twins, so

\widehat{\overline{G}}=\overline{G}=\overline{\widetilde{G}}.

If every vertex in
G
has nonadjacent twins, then by Lemma
1
,
u
and
v
are nonadjacent twins in
G
iff they are adjacent twins in
\overline{G}
.
This implies that
V(\widetilde{G})=V(\widehat{\overline{G}})
. Hence we can use
[u]
to denote both the equivalence of
u
in
\widetilde{G}
and the equivalence class of
u
in
\widehat{\overline{G}}
.

Next we must show that distinct
[u]
and
[z]
are adjacent in
\widehat{\overline{G}}
iff they are not adjacent in
\widetilde{G}
.
Equivalently, we can show that distinct
[u]
and
[z]
are adjacent in
\widetilde{G}
iff they are not adjacent in
\widehat{\overline{G}}
.
By definition,
[u],[z]
are adjacent in
\widetilde{G}
iff
u,z
are adjacent in
G
iff
u,z
are not adjacent in
\overline{G}
iff
[u],[z]
are not adjacent in
\widehat{\overline{G}}
.
∎

Since
\widetilde{G}
has no nonadjacent twins, by Lemma
1
,
\overline{\widetilde{G}}=\widehat{\overline{G}}
has no adjacent twins. However,
\widetilde{G}
can have adjacent twins, so
\overline{\widetilde{G}}
can have nonadjacent twins.

[

Theorem 5
.

Let
G
be a graph in which every vertex is in a set of

be the corresponding nonadjacent twin quotient graph. If
\operatorname{Dist}(\widetilde{G})=\widetilde{d}

, where

Since
\operatorname{Dist}(G)=\operatorname{Dist}(\overline{G})

.

Example 2
.

If we apply Theorem
5
to the previous example, then starting with the obvious fact that
\operatorname{Dist}(K_{1})=1
, we get
\operatorname{Dist}(K_{2})=2
,
\operatorname{Dist}(C_{4})=3

Our next goal is to show how that automorphism group of a vertex-transitive graph with twins can be expressed in terms of automorphisms that permute twins and automorphisms that permute the twin quotient graph. If
u
and
v
are distinct nonadjacent or adjacent twins in a graph
G
, then the function
\tau_{u,v}:V(G)\to V(G)
that interchanges
u
and
v
and leaves all other vertices fixed is a graph automorphism, which we call a
twin transposition
.

Observation
.

Twin transpositions play an important role in recent work on graph stability.
The direct product
G\times K_{2}
has vertex set
V(G)\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, with
(a,x)
adjacent to
(b,y)
iff
ab\in E(G)
and
x\neq y
. Clearly
\operatorname{Aut}(G)\times\operatorname{Aut}(K_{2})
is a subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}(G\times K_{2})
;
G
is called
stable
iff
\operatorname{Aut}(G)\times\operatorname{Aut}(K_{2})=\operatorname{Aut}(G\times K_{2})
and otherwise
G
is
unstable
.
If
u
and
v
are either nonadajcent or adjacent twins in
G
, then
(u,i)
and
(v,i)
are nonadjacent twins in
G\times K_{2}
, for
i\in\{0,1\}
.
If
G
has nonadjacent twins
u,v
, then the permutation
\sigma
on
V(G\times K_{2})
defined by

\sigma\cdot(a,x)=\begin{cases}(\tau_{u,v}(a),0),\quad&x=0,\\
(a,1),&x=1\end{cases}

satisfies
\sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}(G\times K_{2})\setminus\operatorname{Aut}(G)\times\operatorname{Aut}(K_{2})
.
It is similarly easy to see that if
G
is disconnected or bipartite with a nontrivial automorphism, then
G
is unstable.
An unstable graph that connected, not bipartite and without nonadjacent twins is called nontrivially unstable. Recent work by Hujdurović, Mitorović, Kovaács, Jiang, Wilson and Zhang (see
[
15
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
14
]
[
17
]
,
[
25
]
) has focused on determining sufficient conditions for a circulant graph to be unstable.

Let
T
be the subgroup generated by all twin transpositions in
\operatorname{Aut}(G)
. Then

T
is a direct product of symmetric groups, one corresponding to each nontrivial twin class.
Because graph automorphisms must take twin vertices to twins vertices, any
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
induces

\widetilde{\alpha}\in\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{G})

given by

\widetilde{\alpha}([u])=[\alpha(u)]

for all
u\in V(G)
. Let
\lambda:\operatorname{Aut}(G)\to\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{G})
be the group homomorphism defined by
\lambda(\alpha)=\widetilde{\alpha}
. It is clear that
\ker(\lambda)=T
.

Proposition 6
.

Let
G
be a vertex-transitive graph with (nonadjacent or adjacent) twins. Then

\lambda
is is surjective and
\operatorname{Aut}(G)\cong T\rtimes\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{G})
.

Proof.

If
t
is the size of each twin class,
we can arbitrarily label the elements of a twin class
u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{t}
.
For a given
\widetilde{\sigma}\in\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{G})
, define a permutation
\sigma
on
V(G)
by
\sigma(u_{i})=v_{i}
iff
\widetilde{\sigma}([u_{i}])=[v_{i}]
. To show that
\sigma
is an automorphism, let
x_{j},y_{k}\in V(G)
. By definition of the twin quotient graph,

\displaystyle x_{j}y_{k}\in E(G)

\displaystyle\iff[x_{j}][y_{k}]\in E(\widetilde{G})

\displaystyle\iff\widetilde{\sigma}([x_{j}])\widetilde{\sigma}([y_{k}])\in E(\widetilde{G})\iff\sigma(x_{j})\sigma(y_{k})\in E(G).

Let
H=\{\gamma\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)\mid\gamma(u_{i})=v_{i}\}
; that is,
H
is the set of automorphisms that respect the arbitrarily chosen subscripts. Then
H
clearly contains the identity and is closed under composition and inverses. Thus
H
is a subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}(G)
such that the restriction of
\lambda
to
H
is an isomorphism
\lambda:H\to\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{G})
.
Note that
H
is not a normal subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}(G)
; for example, if
\sigma(u_{i})=v_{i}
, then
\sigma\circ\tau_{u_{1},u_{2}}(u_{1})=v_{2}
but
\tau_{u_{1},u_{2}}\circ\sigma(u_{1})=v_{1}
. Thus
\operatorname{Aut}(G)
is the semidirect product of
T
and
\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{G})
, not the direct product.
∎

Example 3
.

Given that Proposition
6
applies to vertex-transitive graphs with either nonadjacent or adjacent twins, we get

We close this section by noting that the property of being arc-transitive is preserved when passing to the twin quotient graph.

Corollary 7
.

Let
G
be a vertex-transitive graph with nonadjacent twins. Then
G
is arc-transitive iff
\widetilde{G}
is arc-transitive.

Proof.

Let
u,v,x
and
y
be vertices in
G
.
First assume that
G
is arc-transitive and that
[u][v]
and
[x][y]
are edges in
\widetilde{G}
. Then
uv
and
xy
are edges in
G
. By assumption, there exists
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
such that
\alpha(u)=x
and
\alpha(v)=y
. Then

\alpha^{*}([u])=[\alpha(u)]=[x]
and
\alpha^{*}([v])=[\alpha(u)]=[x]
.

Next, assume that
\widetilde{G}
is arc-transitive and assume that
uv
and
xy
are edges in
G
. Then
[u][v]
and
[x][y]
are edges in
\widetilde{G}
. By assumption, there exist
\widetilde{\tau}\in\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{G})
such that
\widetilde{\tau}([u])=[x]
and
\widetilde{\tau}([v])=[y]
.
As in the proof of Proposition
6
, we can arbitrarily select a representative
w_{1}\in V(G)
for each twin class and label the elements of the class
w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{t}
, where
t
is the number of vertices in every twin class.
We define
\tau\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
by
\tau(w_{i})=z_{i}
iff
\widetilde{\tau}([w])=[z]
.
In particular,
\tau(u_{i})=x_{j}
and
\tau(v_{k})=y_{\ell}
for some
i,j,k,\ell\in\{1,\dots,t\}
. We can compose
\tau
with appropriate twin transpositions to find an automorphism of
G
that takes
u
to
x
and
v
to
y
.
∎

4
Twins in Circulant Graphs

In this section, we provide an algebraic characterization of when
C_{n}(A)
has nonadjacent twins in terms of the coset structure of the additive group
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
modulo its proper cyclic subgroups. In particular, we use some of the algebra of the action of groups on sets (see
[
10
]
).

The automorphism group of a graph acts on the vertex set of the graph, so
\operatorname{Aut}(C_{n}(A))
acts on
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.
The dihedral group
D_{2n}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{n}\rtimes\{\pm 1\}
is always a subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}(C_{n}(A))
, where the action of
(w,t)\in D_{2n}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{n}\rtimes\{\pm 1\}
on
v\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is given by
(w,t)\cdot v=w+tv
.
Since
A
is inverse-closed,
u-v\in A
iff
(w+tu)-(w+tv)\in A
,
so
(w,t)\in\operatorname{Aut}(C_{n}(A)).

Geometrically, if the vertices of
C_{n}(A)
are arranged in clockwise order in a circle, then
(w,1)
is simply clockwise rotation by
w
while
(w,-1)
is a flip across the diameter through vertex
0
, followed by clockwise rotation by
w
.

If group
\Gamma
acts on set
X
, then
the
stabilizer
of
Y\subseteq X
is the subgroup
\operatorname{Stab}(Y)=\{\gamma\in\Gamma\mid\gamma\cdot Y=Y\}
.
The next lemma characterizes the intersection of the stabilizer of

in
\operatorname{Aut}(C_{n}(A))
with the dihedral subgroup of

Lemma 8
.

In
\operatorname{Aut}(C_{n}(A))
,

\operatorname{Stab}(A)\cap D_{2n}=\langle w\rangle\rtimes\{\pm 1\}
, for some
w\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.
Moreover, the nonadjacent twin class of
0
is
\langle w\rangle
.

Proof.

Any subgroup of
D_{2n}
is either cyclic or itself a dihedral group. Because
A
is inverse-closed,
(0,-1)\in\operatorname{Stab}(A)\cap D_{2n}
, so

\operatorname{Stab}(A)\cap D_{2n}=\langle w\rangle\rtimes\{\pm 1\}
, for some
w\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.
By definition,
0\neq u\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is a nonadjacent twin of
0
iff
N(0)=N(u)
, or equivalently
A=u+A
.
This holds iff
(u,1)\in\operatorname{Stab}(A)\cap D_{2n}
, which in turn holds iff
u\in\langle w\rangle
.
∎

An extreme case of Lemma
8
occurs if

. Then
\operatorname{Stab}(A)\cap D_{2n}=\{0,\pm 1\}

has no nonadjacent twins.
Another extreme case occurs when

is a unit in

. In this case,
\operatorname{Stab}(A)\cap D_{2n}=D_{2n}
and every nonzero vertex is a nonadjacent twin of

is an empty graph (equivalently,

).

Next, we want to detect when a nonempty circulant graph has nonadjacent twins.
We recall more terminology and facts concerning a group
\Gamma
acting on a set
X
(see
[
10
]
).
The
orbit
of an element
x\in X
is
\Gamma_{x}=\{\gamma\cdot x\mid\gamma\in\Gamma\}
; the orbits under the action of
\Gamma
partition the set
X
.
A subset
W\subseteq X
is
\Gamma
-
invariant
iff
\Gamma\cdot W=W
; equivalently, the stabilizer of
W
is the whole group, or
\operatorname{Stab}(W)=\Gamma
. Any
\Gamma
-invariant subset must be a (disjoint) union of orbits.

Proposition 9
.

A nonempty circulant graph
C_{n}(A)
has nonadjacent twins iff there exists
0\neq w\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}\setminus U(n)
such that
A
is a
union of nontrivial cosets of
\langle w\rangle
.
In this case, the nonadjacent twin classes are the cosets of
\langle w\rangle
where
w
is the element of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
of maximum order satisfying the preceding statement.

Proof.

If
C_{n}(A)
is nonempty and has nonadjacent twin vertices, then
by Lemma
8
,

\operatorname{Stab}(A)\cap D_{2n}=\langle w\rangle\rtimes\{\pm 1\}
for some
w\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
that is neither
0
nor a unit. To simplify notation, let
T=\operatorname{Stab}(A)\cap D_{2n}
; we consider the action of
T

on
V(C_{n}(A))=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.

Let
t\in T
, so
t=(kw,1)
or
(kw,-1)
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The action of
t
on
u\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is
t\cdot u=(kw,\pm 1)\cdot u=kw\pm u
,
so the orbit of
u\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
under the action of
T
is
T_{u}=(u+\langle w\rangle)\cup(-u+\langle w\rangle)
. Thus each orbit consists of either one or two cosets of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}/\langle w\rangle
, depending on whether
u=-u
in
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.
In particular, each orbit is inverse-closed.
Since
T\subseteq\operatorname{Stab}(A)
,
A
is
T
-invariant and so

A
is a union of orbits of
T
.
Finally, since each element of
A
is adjacent to
0
, no element of
A
can be a nonadjacent twin of
0
. Thus
A\cap\langle w\rangle=\emptyset
and so
A
must contain only nontrivial cosets of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}/\langle w\rangle
.

Conversely, suppose there exists
0\neq w\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}\setminus U(n)
such that
A
is
a union of nontrivial cosets of
\langle w\rangle
.
By the assumption that
A
contains only nontrivial cosets,
A\cap\langle w\rangle=N(0)\cap\langle w\rangle=\emptyset
, so the elements of
\langle w\rangle
are not adjacent to
0
. For any
kw\in\langle w\rangle
,

N(kw)=N(0)+kw=A+kw=A,

since addition by an element of
\langle w\rangle
preserves each coset of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}/\langle w\rangle
. Hence each element of
\langle w\rangle\setminus\{0\}
is a nonadjacent twin vertex of
0
. An element
w
of maximum order satisfying this condition makes
\langle w\rangle
a maximal set of mutually nonadjacent twins containing
0
, which by definition is the nonadjacent twin class of
0
.
∎

The result below shows that the nonadjacent twin quotient graph of a circulant graph is also a circulant graph. For clarity, in the statement and proof of this result, the elements of

are denoted by

Proposition 14
.

Let
C_{n}(A)
be a circulant graph with nonadjacent twin classes of size
0<d<n
.
Let
m=n/d
.
Then its nonadjacent twin quotient graph
is
C_{m}(A^{\prime})
, where
A^{\prime}=\{[a]_{m}:[a]_{n}\in A\}
. Moreover,
C_{m}(A^{\prime})
does not have nonadjacent twins.

Proof.

By Proposition
9
, each nonadjacent twin class is a coset of
\langle[m]_{n}\rangle
.
It is straightforward to verify that the canonical projection
\pi:\mathbb{Z}_{n}\to\mathbb{Z}_{m}
is a graph homomorphism from
C_{n}(A)
to its nonadjacent twin quotient graph.
If the quotient graph has nonadjacent twins, then by Proposition
9
,
A^{\prime}=\pi(A)
is a union of nontrivial cosets of the cyclic subgroup some element of
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
of order
0<d^{\prime}<m
.
We can use
m^{\prime}=m/d^{\prime}
as a generator of this subgroup.
Note that
\langle[m^{\prime}]_{n}\rangle
is a subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
containing
\langle[m]_{n}\rangle
. Thus
A
is a union of nontrivial cosets of
\langle[m^{\prime}]_{n}\rangle

and the nonadjacent twin classes

have size

, a contradiction.
∎

Co-Twins

As shown in Section
3.1
, by alternatively passing to nonadjacent and adjacent twin quotient graphs as necessary, any vertex-transitive graph
G
has a twin-free graph
G_{0}
as a homomorphic image.
By Theorem
5
, the problem of finding
\operatorname{Dist}(G)
can be reduced to finding
\operatorname{Dist}(G_{0})
.
More generally, Proposition
6
can be used to express
\operatorname{Aut}(G)
as a semidirect product of permutation groups and
\operatorname{Aut}(G_{0})
.
The task of finding the distinguishing number and automorphism group of vertex-transitive, twin-free graphs can in some cases be further simplified by investigating a similar vertex relationship.

Definition
.

Distinct vertices
u
and
v
in graph
G=(V,E)
are

nonadjacent co-twins
iff
N[u]=\overline{N[v]}
(equivalently,
N[u]\sqcup N[v]=V
),
and
adjacent co-twins
iff
N(u)=\overline{N(v)}
(equivalently,
N(u)\sqcup N(v)=V
).

.

The hypercube
Q_{3}
and isosahedral graph are vertex-transitive, twin-free with nonadjacent co-twins and the envelope graph
\overline{C_{6}}
is vertex-transitive, twin-free with adjacent co-twins. See Figures
3
and
4
, in which co-twins share the same color.

Lemma 15
.

(a)

A vertex cannot have both a nonadjacent and an adjacent co-twin.

(b)

Two vertices in a graph
G
are nonadjacent co-twins in
G
iff they are adjacent co-twins in the complement
\overline{G}
.

Proof.

Suppose
v\in V
has nonadjacent co-twin
u
and adjacent co-twin
w
.
Then
N[u]\sqcup N[v]=V=N(w)\sqcup N(v)
.
Because
w
is adjacent to
v
,
w\in N(v)\subset N[v]
.
Because
u
is not adjacent to
v
,
u\notin N(v)
and so
u\in N(w)
. Since
u
is adjacent to
w
,
w
is adjacent to
u
and so
w\in N[u]
. This is a contradiction as
N[u]
and
N[v]
are disjoint.

For (b), from the proof of Lemma
1
,
\overline{N_{G}(v)}=N_{\overline{G}}[v]
. By complementarity, this implies both

\overline{N_{\overline{G}}(u)}=N_{G}[u]
and
\overline{N_{G}[v]}=N_{\overline{G}}(v)
.
Thus
N_{G}[u]=\overline{N_{G}[v]}
iff
N_{\overline{G}}=\overline{N_{\overline{G}}(u)}(v)
.
∎

Lemma
15
shows that in some ways, co-twins are similar to twins. An important difference is that being (nonadjacent or adjacent) co-twins is not an equivalence relation because it is neither reflexive nor transitive. However, there is an analog of the twin class of a vertex.

Lemma 16
.

If
G
has no adjacent (resp. nonadjacent) twins, then any vertex in
G
has at most one nonadjacent (resp. adjacent) co-twin.

Proof.

If
u
and
w
are both nonadjacent co-twins of
v
, then
N[u]=\overline{N[v]}=N[w]
. By definition,
u
and
w
are adjacent twins in
G
. Similarly, if
u
and
w
are both adjacent co-twins of
v
, then
N(v)=\overline{N(u)}=N(w)
and so
v
and
w
are nonadjacent twins.
∎

If
G
is vertex-transitive, twin-free, with nonadjacent co-twins, then
\overline{G}
is vertex-transitive, twin-free with adjacent co-twins. Moreover

and
\overline{G}
have the same automorphism group and symmetry parameters. To simplify terminology, henceforth we focus only on nonadjacent co-twins, calling them simply co-twins.

.

Let

\overline{K_{k}\times K_{2}}\cong C_{2k}(A^{\prime})

A^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{2k}\setminus\{0\}
. Crown graphs are known to be Hamiltonian
[
20
]
; in order for
C_{2k}(A^{\prime})
to be Hamiltonian, we can assume WLOG that
1\in A^{\prime}
. Every vertex in
\overline{K_{k}\times K_{2}}
has degree
k-1
which is odd, implying that
|A^{\prime}|
must be odd. Since
A^{\prime}
is inverse-closed,
k\in A^{\prime}
. Thus
C_{2k}(A^{\prime})
contains the odd cycle
(0,1,2,\dots,k,0)
. However,
\overline{K_{k}\times K_{2}}
is bipartite, a contradiction.
∎

5.1
Co-Twin Quotient Graph

Throughout this subsection, we assume
G
is a graph of order
n=2k
that is vertex-transitive, twin-free, with co-twins. This implies
k\geq 3
. Although the relation of being
co-twins is not an equivalence relation, we can still form a co-twin quotient graph by collapsing each
co-twin pair
\{u,v\}
into a single vertex denoted by
[u,v]
and adding an edge between collapsed pairs
[u,v]
and
[x,y]
iff a vertex in
\{u,v\}
is adjacent in
G
to a vertex in
\{x,y\}
.

Lemma 21
.

If
\{u,v\}
and
\{x,y\}
are distinct pairs of
co-twins, then the induced subgraph
G[u,v,x,y]
is
K_{2}\cup K_{2}
.

Proof.

Since
x\in V(G)=N[u]\sqcup N[v]
, we can assume without loss of generality that
x\in N[u]
. Because these are distinct pairs,
x\in N(u)
. Since
N[x]
and
N[y]
are disjoint,
y
cannot also be adjacent to
u
and so
y\in N(v)
.
∎

Figure 6:
Subgraph induced by co-twin pairs
\{u,v\}
and
\{x,y\}
.

By this lemma, the co-twin quotient graph is simply
K_{k}
.
Thus the function
G\to K_{k}
that collapses each
co-twin pair into a single vertex is by definition a surjective graph homomorphism.

As with twin vertices, this leads to a relationship between the automorphism groups of the original graph and the quotient graph.
Any
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
must take
co-twins to
co-twins, so
\alpha
induces a permutation
\alpha^{*}
of the collapsed vertices, given by

\alpha^{*}([u,v])=[\alpha(u),\alpha(v)]
. Since any permutation of the vertices of a complete graph is a graph automorphism, this defines a group homomorphism
\kappa:\operatorname{Aut}(G)\to\operatorname{Aut}(K_{k})=S_{k}
.

From subsection
3.1
, a function that transposes a single pair of (adjacent or nonadjacent) twins and leaves all other vertices fixed is a graph automorphism.
The subgroup
T
generated by
all twin transpositions consists of automorphisms in which sets of mutually twin vertices are permuted independently. This subgroup is the kernel of the projection of automorphisms of
G
to automorphisms of the twin quotient graph
\widetilde{G}
.
The next result shows that
co-twin pairs cannot be transposed independently.

Lemma 22
.

Let
\beta:V(G)\to V(G)
be the function that transposes the vertices of every
co-twin pair. Then the kernel of
\kappa:\operatorname{Aut}(G)\to S_{k}
is
\langle\beta\rangle
.

Proof.

It is easy to verify that
\beta\in\operatorname{Aut}(G).
If
\alpha\in\ker(\kappa)
, then for each
co-twin pair
\{u,v\}
, either
\alpha
fixes both
u
and
v
or
\alpha
transposes
u
and
v
. In the latter case,
\alpha(N[u])=N[v]
and
\alpha(N[v])=N[u]
. Since
N[u]
and
N[v]
are disjoint,
\alpha
cannot fix any vertex. Hence if
\alpha
transposes vertices in one
co-twin pair, it must transpose the vertices of every co-twin pair and so
\alpha=\beta
.
∎

Proposition 23
.

Let
G
be a graph of order
2k
that is vertex-transitive and twin-free with co-twins. Then
\kappa:\operatorname{Aut}(G)\to S_{k}
is surjective iff
G
is triangle-free.

Proof.

First assume
G
is triangle-free. Let
\sigma\in S_{k}
; we must define
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
such that
\kappa(\alpha)=\alpha^{*}=\sigma
.
Let
[u,v]
be a collapsed co-twin pair.
If
\sigma
fixes
[u,v]
, then let
\alpha(u)=u
and
\alpha(v)=v
.
If
\sigma([u,v])=[x,y]
, where
[x,y]
is a different collapsed co-twin pair, then by Lemma
21
, the induced subgraph
G[u,v,x,y]
is
K_{2}\cup K_{2}
.
Without loss of generality,
assume
u,x
are adjacent and
v,y
are adjacent, as in Figure
6
. In this case, let
\alpha(u)=y
and
\alpha(v)=x
; that is, if
\sigma
moves
[u,v]
to
[x,y]
,
\alpha
takes each vertex in
\{u,v\}
to the nonadjacent vertex in
\{x,y\}
.

Certainly
\alpha
is a bijection on
V(G)
.
To show
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
, let
u,w\in V(G)
.
If
u
and
w
are adjacent, then they cannot be (nonadjacent) co-twins. Let
[u,v]
and
[w,z]
be the distinct co-twin pairs containing these vertices.
By Lemma
21
,
v
and
z
are adjacent.

Case 1.
If
\sigma
fixes both
[u,v]
and
[w,z]
, then
\alpha
fixes both
u
and
w
and thus
\alpha(u)
and
\alpha(w)
are adjacent.

Case 2.
Assume
\sigma([u,v])=[x,y]
but
\sigma
fixes
[w,z]
. Assuming
u
is adjacent to
x
and

is adjacent to
y
, by definition
\alpha(u)=y
and
\alpha(w)=w
.
Since
G
is triangle-free,
w
cannot be adjacent to
x
, and so by Lemma
21
,
w=\alpha(w)
is adjacent to
y=\alpha(u)
.

Case 3.
Assume
\sigma([u,v])=[x,y]
and
\sigma([w,z])=[r,s]
, where
u
is adjacent to
x
and
w
is adjacent to
r
.
Because
\sigma
is injective,
[x,y]\neq[r,s]
.
Then by definition
\alpha(u)=y
and
\alpha(w)=s
.
As in the previous case, since
G
is triangle-free and
u
is adjacent to
w
,
w
is adjacent to
y
.
Since
w
,
y
and
r
cannot induce a triangle in
G
,

y=\alpha(u)
is adjacent to
s=\alpha(w)
.

Since
\alpha
is an adjacency-preserving bijection on the vertices of a finite graph,
\alpha
is an automorphism.
Clearly,
\kappa(\alpha)=\sigma
and so
\kappa
is surjective.

Next assume that
h,j,\ell\in V(G)
induce a triangle in
G
. Let
i,k,m
be their respective co-twins; by Lemma
21
, they also induce a triangle in
G
. Let
\tau\in S_{k}
be the permutation that interchanges collapsed
[h,i]
and
[j,k]
but leaves all other vertices fixed. Assume
\kappa(\gamma)=\tau
for some
\gamma\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
.

By composing
\gamma
with
\beta
if necessary, we can assume that
\gamma
fixes both
\ell
and
m
. Since
\gamma
respects adjacency,
\gamma(h)=j
and
\gamma(i)=k
. Since
G
has no adjacent twins, there is some vertex
p
that is adjacent to
h
but not to
j
. Let
q
be the co-twin of
p
.

Since
\tau
fixes
[p,q]
,
\gamma
either fixes or interchanges
p
and
q
. Since
p
and
h
are adjacent in
G
,
\gamma(p)
and
\gamma(h)=j
must be adjacent, so
\gamma(p)=q.
By Lemma
21
,
\{\ell,m\}
and
\{p,q\}
induce
K_{2}\cup K_{2}
. Thus
\gamma
cannot fix the vertices in the first co-twin pair and interchange them in the second.
∎

Corollary 24
.

Let
G
be a graph of order
2k
that is vertex-transitive, twin-free, with co-twins. If
G
is triangle-free, then
\operatorname{Aut}(G)=S_{k}\times S_{2}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
22
, Proposition
23
and the First Isomorphism Theorem,
\operatorname{Aut}(G)/\langle\beta\rangle\cong S_{k}
. Since
\langle\beta\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
,
|\operatorname{Aut}(G)|=2\cdot|S_{k}|=2(k!)
.

Let
H
be the set of automorphisms of
G
used in the proof of Proposition
23
. More precisely,
\alpha\in H
iff whenever
\alpha
fixes co-twin pair
\{u,v\}
set-wise,
\alpha
fixes
\{u,v\}
point-wise, and whenever
\alpha(\{u,v\})=\{x,y\}
for some
\{x,y\}\neq\{u,v\}
, then
\alpha
takes each vertex in
\{u,v\}
to the nonadjacent vertex in
\{x,y\}
.

Clearly
H
contains the identity and is closed under inverses. Let
\alpha,\beta\in H
. To verify that
\beta\alpha\in H
, it suffices to consider the case

\alpha(\{u,v\})=\{x,y\}
and
\beta(\{x,y\})=\{w,z\}
for
\{x,y\}\neq\{u,v\}
and
\{w,z\}\neq\{x,y\}
.
Assuming
u
is adjacent to
x
and
x
is adjacent to
w
,
\alpha(u)=y
and
\beta(y)=w.

Because
G
is triangle-free,
u
is not adjacent
w
and so
\beta\alpha
takes each vertex in
\{u,v\}
to the nonadjacent vertex in
\{w,x\}
.
Thus
H
is a subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}(G)
and
H\cap\langle b\rangle
is trivial. Moreover, the proof of Proposition
23
establishes a one-to-one correspondence between the elements of
S_{k}
and
H
, meaning
|H|=|S_{k}|=k!
. Hence
H
is a subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}(G)
of index
2
and so is normal. As a kernel,
\langle b\rangle
is also normal. Hence
\operatorname{Aut}(G)
is the direct product of these two subgroups.
∎

Proposition 25
.

A vertex-transitive, twin-free graph with co-twins is triangle-free iff it is a crown graph
\overline{K_{k}\times K_{2}}
for some

.

Proof.

As noted earlier,

,

has even order

. All vertex-transitive graphs of orders

and

. Let

be a co-twin pair and let

.
Because

is triangle-free, no two vertices in

are adjacent. Since

, no

is adjacent to

. Thus each

must have

neighbors in

. If we let

denote the co-twin of

We do not have a result as definitive as Corollary

Proposition 27
.

Let
G
be a graph of order
n=2k
that is vertex-transitive, twin-free, with co-twins.
Let
u\in V(G)
.
Let
u\in V(G)
; let
H_{u}
be the subgraph of
G
induced by
N(u)
and
\operatorname{Stab}(u)
be the stabilizer of
u
in
\operatorname{Aut}(G)
. Then

1.

\operatorname{Stab}(u)\cong\operatorname{Aut}(H_{u})
,

2.

|\operatorname{Aut}(G)|=|\operatorname{Aut}(H_{u})|\cdot n
,

3.

\operatorname{Det}(G)=1+\operatorname{Det}(H_{u})
,

4.

\operatorname{Dist}(G)\leq\operatorname{Dist}(H_{u})
.

Proof.

All parts of the theorem clearly hold if
\operatorname{Aut}(H_{u})
is trivial, so we assume
|\operatorname{Aut}(H_{u})|>1
.
Let
\pi:\operatorname{Stab}(u)\to\operatorname{Aut}(H_{u})
be the canonical restriction map, which is clearly a group homomorphism. If
\alpha\in\ker(\pi)
, then
\alpha
fixes every vertex in
N[u]
. Because graph automorphisms preserve co-twin pairs,
\alpha
must also fix the co-twin of every vertex in
N[u]
. By Lemma
21
, no co-twin pair can be entirely inside
N[u]
. Thus
\alpha
is the identity automorphism.

To show that
\pi
is surjective, let
\gamma\in\operatorname{Aut}(H_{u})
. We want to extend
\gamma
to a bijection
\sigma
on
V(G)
. We start by setting
\sigma(x)=\gamma(x)
for all
x\in N(u)
.
If
v
is the co-twin of
u
, then we set
\sigma(u)=u
and
\sigma(v)=v
.
By Lemma
21
, the co-twin of each
w\in N[v]
is a vertex in
N[u]
, call it
z
. We let
\sigma(w)
be the co-twin of
\gamma(z)
.
In other words, the action of
\sigma
on the vertices of
N[v]
‘mirrors’ the action of
\gamma
on the vertices of
N[u]
. It is straightforward to verify that
\sigma\in\operatorname{Stab}(u)\leq\operatorname{Aut}(G)
and that
\pi(\sigma)=\gamma
.

Since
G
is vertex-transitive, the orbit of
u
is
V(G)
. The second statement of the proposition follows from the orbit-stabilizer theorem (see
[
10
]
).

Because
G
is nontrivial and vertex-transitive,
\operatorname{Det}(G)>1.
If
u
is fixed, then to eliminate any nontrivial automorphism that fixes
u
, we must also fix vertices in a determining set of
H_{u}
.

Finally, we can construct a distinguishing coloring of
G
by first using a palette of
\operatorname{Dist}(H_{u})
colors to create a distinguishing color on
H_{u}
. Next, color the co-twin of each
v\in N(u)
the same color as
v
. Finally, color
u
and its co-twin with two different colors from the palette used for
H_{u}
. Let
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
be an automorphism that respects this coloring. Because
\alpha
preserve co-twin pairs and only one co-twin pair has been assigned different colors,
\alpha(u)=u
.
∎

Future Work

The results in this paper can be used to address the notoriously difficult problem of finding automorphism groups of circulant graphs by tracking the effects of the presence twins and co-twins. We have demonstrated that in some special cases, our results completely determine the automorphism group. There is ample scope for future work in finding the automorphsim groups of circulant graphs which are twin-free and co-twin-free.
