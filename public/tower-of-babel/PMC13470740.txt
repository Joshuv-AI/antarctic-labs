Wind-Aware Methane Sensor Architecture at a Gas Plant: CFD Analysis of Tree- and Infrastructure-Controlled Plume Pathways
Authors: Mohan Sivagnanam, Ranjani Kannaiyan, Ian D. Gates
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Methane emissions from oil and gas facilities are increasingly monitored using fixed-point sensor networks and continuous monitoring systems, but the effectiveness of these systems depends strongly on sensor location. At the facility scale, plume transport is distorted by buildings, tanks, pipe racks, and surrounding vegetation, so locations that are informative under one wind regime can become ineffective or biased under another. In this study, steady Reynolds-averaged computational fluid dynamics was used to simulate methane dispersion from representative tank and flare-stack release scenarios at a gas plant in west-central Alberta while resolving the combined effects of plant infrastructure and tree canopies. The model domain was 320 m × 320 m × 120 m, and 27 mean-wind cases were examined across three wind directions, three wind speeds (3.6, 9, and 18 km/h), and three ambient temperatures (−15, 5, and 25 °C). The simulations show that wind direction and wind speed are the dominant controls on monitoring design. Temperature enters the present model primarily through density, thereby providing a density-sensitivity check rather than a full atmospheric-stability analysis. Low winds produced broad, wake-sensitive, near-ground plumes, whereas high winds produced 50–70% narrower advective corridors and, in some cases, lifted plume segments above portions of the canopy. Rotation of the wind from south–southwest to south or northeast shifted the informative monitoring sector from one side of the plant to another. The results identify five monitoring rolessource-proximal, dominant-sector downwind, sheltered-wake/canopy-edge, elevated, and background sensorsrather than a single universal layout. The simulations further show that sheltered zones within the plant can locally elevate methane concentrations in the range of 0.5–2 ppm by reducing dilution, potentially biasing release-rate inference if these locations are used without an advective plume and background reference. Overall, the study supports a wind-aware, role-based sensor architecture for gas plants surrounded by vegetation-dense areas.

Introduction

Methane (CH4) is both a potent greenhouse gas and the principal component of natural gas. For oil and gas facilities, this creates a dual-monitoring problem: emissions must be detected quickly for safety and operational response, but they must also be credibly quantified for emissions management and regulatory reporting. In practice, these two tasks are tightly coupled. A continuous monitoring system does not observe the emission rate directly; it observes concentration time series at a limited set of sensor locations, and the release rate must be inferred from those observations by using a transport model. As a result, the uncertainty in inferred methane release rate depends not only on sensor precision and inversion method but also on whether the sensor network actually samples informative plume pathways under the wind and flow regimes experienced at the site.

Recent methane monitoring studies increasingly treat this as a network-design problem rather than a sensor-hardware problem alone. Klise et al. posed site-scale methane monitoring as an optimization problem over leak scenarios and candidate sensor locations. Zi et al. extended that idea by using a distributionally robust formulation to hedge against uncertainty in meteorological conditions, while Jia et al. optimized the locations of point-in-space continuous monitoring systems at oil and gas sites using site-specific emission scenarios and wind data. Patel and Zenker further showed that methane-sensing networks designed with an explicit probabilistic framework outperform randomized layouts in terms of detection and quantification performance. At the inversion stage, Daniels et al. demonstrated that concentration and wind time series from fixed sensors can be used to detect, localize, and quantify single-source methane emissions; they addressed event-duration estimation. Ball et al. evaluated multisource quantification using fixed-point continuous monitoring systems. At the same time, Day et al. showed that point-sensor networks can struggle with short, controlled releases, underscoring that analytic sophistication cannot fully compensate for poor siting.

Related work in process safety and fenceline monitoring reaches a similar conclusion. Gas detector placement in refineries, storage facilities, and natural gas process units has been optimized using scenario probability, detector reliability, CFD-derived hazard fields, and combinations of point and open-path detectors. − These studies are important because they treat placement and network size as design variables rather than afterthoughts; however, their objective functions usually emphasize detection coverage, detection time, or safety compliance. Much less attention is given to a methane-monitoring objective that is central for emissions management: selecting the minimum number and placement of sensors needed to keep the uncertainty in the inferred methane release rate within an acceptable bound.

Methods developed for other gases are therefore highly relevant, but they cannot be transferred to methane monitoring without redefinition of the objective function. Safety-oriented networks for toxic or flammable gases often reward rapid exceedance detection, whereas methane continuous monitoring systems must also support source localization and emissions rate inference at ppm-level enhancements above a variable background. Methane is buoyant relative to air, has source rates that can span several orders of magnitude, and is often evaluated under emission management or regulatory quantification objectives rather than only alarm threshold objectives. In the present study, the optimization literature is used to motivate wind-aware candidate locations, but the CFD analysis is interpreted as a role-based placement framework rather than as a solved formal optimization problem.

A broader inverse-modeling literature provides the conceptual tools for that objective. Information-based and Bayesian optimal design methods place receptors to maximize expected information gain or minimize posterior uncertainty about unknown source parameters. − , For methane specifically, near-field inversion studies using stationary, mobile, and path-integrated observations have shown that source localization and rate estimation are feasible but strongly conditioned by measurement geometry and meteorological representation. ,, In this framing, the sensor count is not chosen heuristically. Instead, the number of sensors and their locations are selected jointly with respect to an uncertainty metric such as posterior variance, credible interval width, expected information gain, or probability of correct source discrimination.

Recent CFD and adjoint studies in complex urban settings demonstrate how such ideas can be operationalized when wind direction and flow complexity are critical. Jia and Kikumoto used the entropy of adjoint concentration fields to optimize sensor configurations for stochastic source-term estimation. Liu and Li extended sensor configuration design to multiple wind directions in complex urban neighborhoods, while Liu and Li examined how the source-term estimation performance varies with the number of sensors. Gkirmpas et al. likewise showed that the quality of source-term estimation can be sensitive not only to the number of sensors but also to the specific subset of sensors selected. Collectively, these studies indicate that sensor placement should be conditioned on the joint distribution of wind direction, wind speed, and plausible source locations and that robust performance requires weighting sensor utility across scenarios rather than optimizing for a single nominal plume.

For gas plants, the placement problem is further complicated by the fact that the transport field departs strongly from the homogeneous flow assumptions underlying simple Gaussian plume models. Buildings, tanks, pipe racks, and vegetation generate wakes, recirculation zones, acceleration corridors, and canopy sheltering that can displace concentration maxima away from simple downwind lines. − This matters directly for monitoring because a sensor that is informative under one wind regime may become nearly blind under another, and a sensor placed in a persistent wake or canopy-sheltered region may observe concentrations that are disproportionately influenced by local accumulation rather than representative downwind transport. Under such conditions, simplified inversion models can map the same concentration time series to biased emission rates if the siting problem is not treated together with the flow-physics problem. ,

Despite this progress, relatively few studies combine methane-specific monitoring objectives, facility-resolving CFD, and site-specific vegetation to answer a practical question for plant monitoring: which sensor roles and candidate zones are needed so that emission detection and release rate estimation remain informative across the dominant wind directions and wind-speed regimes of the site? This gap is especially important for tree-bordered gas plants in west-central Alberta, where vegetation may either channel or trap plumes depending on the wind direction and plume height. The present study addresses this need by reinterpreting CFD simulations of methane dispersion around a representative gas plant from a monitoring-design standpoint. The objective is not to claim a unique mathematical optimum but to demonstrate that wind direction and wind speed determine which sensor locations are informative and which monitoring roles are required to avoid blind sectors.

Materials and Methods

Computational Domain and Geometry

A three-dimensional steady RANS CFD model was developed to evaluate atmospheric methane dispersion at a representative gas plant in western-central Alberta, Canada. The computational domain included the plant and immediate surrounding vegetation. The horizontal domain measured 320 m × 320 m, and the domain height was 120 m above ground level. These dimensions were selected to capture flow development around plant obstacles while keeping boundary effects away from the main region of interest and interpreted monitoring zones. The largest modeled obstacles and tree canopies occupy only a fraction of the 120 m domain height, and the interpreted plume visualizations remained below the top symmetry boundary. The horizontal domain was selected to keep the plant and tree belt wakes away from the inlet and outlet during the mean wind comparisons.

The plant layout, shown in Figure , included seven gas tanks in the northern portion of the site, a flare stack near the southwest corner, and the main access road to the north. The southwest corner was kept relatively open to preserve the aerodynamic asymmetry seen in the site layout. Vegetation was represented using tree volumes distributed from satellite interpretation, with representative heights of 6, 12, and 14 m. A continuous tree belt approximately 32 m wide was also included around the plant perimeter to capture sheltering, wake formation, and canopy-edge interactions that can influence methane detectability.

Mesh Generation

Because the gas plant contains interconnected structures and irregular vegetation volumes, an unstructured tetrahedral mesh, as shown in Figure , was first generated to conform to the geometry with limited manual intervention. Local mesh refinement was applied near building edges, tank surfaces, flare stacks, porous zones, and methane release locations, where strong velocity and concentration gradients were expected. Inflation layers were added at solid surfaces to support the wall function treatment of the near-wall flow.

Mesh used in the gas plant geometry: (a) full domain, (b) section view, and (c) polyhedral mesh.

The tetrahedral mesh was then converted to a polyhedral mesh using ANSYS Fluent. Polyhedral control volumes can improve gradient reconstruction and reduce numerical diffusion for a given geometric fidelity, often providing comparable accuracy with fewer cells than a purely tetrahedral mesh. This conversion improved numerical robustness and convergence while preserving the geometric detail required for facility-scale plume simulations.

Governing Equations and Physical Models

The simulations solved the steady Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes equations for conservation of mass and momentum, together with transport equations for energy and chemical species. Gravity was enabled to allow methane–air density differences to influence the flow. Methane was treated as a nonreacting species within an air–methane mixture using the Fluent Species Transport model. Molecular diffusion and turbulence-driven mixing were accounted for by using an eddy-diffusivity formulation. The turbulent Schmidt number was set to 0.7, which is a common value in RANS gas-dispersion simulations. Because the RANS plume spread is sensitive to this parameter, the results are interpreted primarily in terms of relative plume pathways and sensor roles rather than as validated absolute concentrations.

Turbulence was modeled by using the renormalization group (RNG) k–ε model, which was developed to improve performance in strained and separated flows relative to the standard k–ε closure. , This choice is well-suited to the strong shear, curvature, and recirculation expected around process equipment, tanks, and vegetation. Near-wall treatment employed standard wall functions, and the mesh design followed established guidance for atmospheric boundary-layer CFD in the built environment. ,

Porous media momentum sinks were used to represent both vegetation and dense arrays of unresolved small-diameter piping. Tree canopies were represented as porous volumes rather than as explicit trunks and leaves; satellite-derived canopy footprints were assigned representative heights of 6, 12, and 14 m, and a 32 m perimeter belt was included to represent the surrounding tree line. For vegetation, the porous approach efficiently captures bulk drag, sheltering, and wake generation and is widely used in CFD studies of canopy-flow interactions. ,, For dense piping regions, porous resistance provides a practical way to reproduce aggregate pressure losses and momentum deficits without explicitly meshing every element. This implementation represents bulk momentum loss but does not include separate canopy-generated turbulence source terms; the implication of that assumption is discussed in the limitations section. Boundary Conditions and Methane Sources

All cases were simulated as steady-state cases to isolate the influence of mean wind forcing on plume behavior rather than transient plume meandering. The upwind face of the domain was prescribed as a velocity inlet with a uniform mean velocity corresponding to the selected 10 m reference wind speed, and the downwind face was prescribed as a pressure outlet. Wind direction was implemented by rotating the plant/terrain geometry relative to a fixed inlet–outlet pair, so the inflow remained normal to the inlet and the unused lateral faces could be treated as symmetry boundaries. The top boundary was also treated as a symmetry boundary to approximate an unconfined atmosphere. The ground and all plant structures were modeled as no-slip walls. Walls were treated as adiabatic in the energy equation.

The inlet mixture was methane-free air. Inlet turbulence quantities were prescribed consistently across cases using the same turbulence intensity and length-scale approach so that changes in plume behavior could be attributed to the scenario variables. Pressure–velocity coupling used the SIMPLE algorithm. Second-order upwind discretization was applied to momentum, energy, and species transport, while first-order upwind discretization was retained for the turbulence equations to improve robustness in the complex geometry. A case was accepted only when all convergence criteria were met: scaled residuals below 1 × 10–6, stable methane mass-fraction monitors at designated receptor locations, and acceptable global mass-balance closure.

Two simultaneous continuous noncombusting methane release scenarios were modeled as mass-flow inlets of pure methane: (i) a low-rate leak from Tank #1 with a mass flow rate of 1 × 10–5 kg/s (0.864 kg/day), located 11 m above ground and approximately 16 m from the plant center, and (ii) a larger unlit venting scenario from the flare stack with a mass flow rate of 0.003 kg/s. The flare-stack case therefore represents a cold methane venting or incomplete-combustion diagnostic scenario, not a normally combusting flare plume. Methane was injected at the ambient temperature specified for each case so that the simulation matrix isolated wind, geometry, and density effects rather than combustion-driven buoyant rise. The tank and flare releases were interpreted as representative low-elevation and elevated source-height classes for monitoring design.

Meteorological Scenarios

Historical 2024 meteorological data were obtained from the Alberta Climate Information Service (ACIS) to define the representative boundary conditions. The wind rose diagram, displayed in Figure , shows that the prevailing winds were primarily from the south–southwest, which motivated the inclusion of that sector in the scenario set. Three wind directions were modeled: south–southwest (SSW), south (S), and northeast (NE). The NE case represents an important opposing wind sector in the wind rose, whereas the S case was included as a neighboring southerly sector that drives the plume more directly into the northern tree belt and therefore tests the sensitivity to tree-line alignment. The SW sector was not added in order to keep the simulation matrix focused on the three representative directions.

Wind rose diagram generated from 2024 Alberta Climate Information Service data.

The 2024 wind speeds and temperatures for the area near Edson, obtained from ACIS, are shown in Figure and were used as representative west-central Alberta meteorological inputs for the plant. Wind speed and ambient temperature were each represented at three levels. The wind speeds were 3.6, 9, and 18 km/h, representing low, medium, and high 10 m reference speeds. The ambient temperatures modeled were −15, 5, and 25 °C. Combining the three wind speeds, three temperatures, and three wind directions yielded 27 cases in total. Temperature enters the present model through fluid density and methane-air buoyancy only: the inlet temperature was spatially uniform, and the model did not impose a resolved atmospheric stability profile, inversion, or surface heat-flux boundary condition. The temperature dimension is therefore interpreted as a density-sensitivity study rather than as a full stability analysis.

No site-specific controlled-release or tracer measurements were available for this plant, so the present work does not claim full field validation. As an order-of-magnitude benchmark, a neutral, unobstructed Gaussian plume estimate for the 0.003 kg/s flare-stack venting case at 9 km/h (2.5 m/s) gives centerline methane mass fractions on the order of 10–7 to 10–6 over receptor distances of tens of meters for representative plume widths. This is the same order as the advective plume levels used for visualization, while the Gaussian estimate is not expected to reproduce sheltered accumulation because it excludes buildings, pipe racks, trees, and recirculation. Consequently, the conclusions emphasize relative plume pathways and sensor roles rather than validated absolute concentration values.

Framework for Interpreting Sensor Placement from CFD

For each simulated case, the methane concentration field was examined in plan view, vertical sections, and isometric renderings, together with the local velocity field, streamlines, and zones of recirculation. The interpretation was conducted separately for the low-elevation tank release and the elevated flare stack venting release because the two source heights pose fundamentally different monitoring challenges. The flare-stack-venting release provides a useful elevated reference case: once the plume rises above most of the plant roughness layer and the top of the adjacent canopy, its trajectory is governed more by the ambient wind than by local sheltering. By contrast, the tank release is emitted within the roughness sublayer formed by buildings, tanks, pipe racks, roads, and trees, and is therefore much more sensitive to local aerodynamic distortion.

Candidate monitoring locations were evaluated using four practical criteria: repeated plume interception across cases, robustness to changes in wind speed and wind direction, ability to distinguish source-driven enhancement from plant-induced recirculation or background methane, and usefulness for emission-rate estimation rather than leak detection alone. Visualization thresholds were related to practical detectability; for example, a methane mass fraction of 1 × 10–6 corresponds to approximately 1.8 ppmv in air, which is within the concentration range relevant to many continuous methane-monitoring systems. This distinction is important because a location highly sensitive to methane is not necessarily a good one from which to infer source strength. Some points are strong detectors because they sit in sheltered accumulation zones, whereas other points are more informative for quantification because they sample the more-advective portion of the plume.

This interpretation led to five recurring sensor roles: (i) a source-proximal sentinel sensor near likely leak equipment; (ii) a sector-aligned downwind perimeter sensor located in the dominant advective pathway; (iii) a near-ground canopy-edge or wake sensor in a sheltered recirculation zone; (iv) an elevated or above-canopy sensor that can intercept lifted plume segments; and (v) a background or crosswind reference sensor. For the present site, these roles correspond to source-proximal locations near the tank and flare-associated equipment, downwind locations along the dominant SSW transport corridor near the northern road/tree line, wake-sensitive locations at canopy edges or internal sheltered zones, elevated locations able to intercept lifted plume segments, and crosswind/upwind background locations. The discussion below uses these roles to show why wind-aware placement is more defensible than uniform spacing or purely source-proximal siting for this type of gas plant.

Results and Discussion

Wind Speed Controls the Dominant Monitoring Regime

Under SSW inflow, the tank plume was transported through the plant’s asymmetric southern approach and then interacted with the northern tree line and adjacent road corridor. At 3.6 km/h, the plume remained broad and near the process area, with weak advection allowing wakes behind tanks, buildings, and pipe-rack regions to retain methane within the plant. At 9 and 18 km/h, the plume became narrower but also more sensitive to canopy-edge steering; part of the tank plume was deflected eastward along the northern tree line and appeared to be channeled by the road corridor. Temperature affected the degree of vertical dilution, but wind speed remained the primary control. The elevated flare plume remained comparatively coherent and less sensitive to local roughness than the tank plume, which highlights how strongly low-elevation releases interact with the coupled plant-and-tree environment.

Under NE inflow, the downwind sector rotated to the opposite side of the plant, and the influence of internal plant aerodynamics became especially evident. At 9 and 18 km/h, localized, detached regions of elevated methane appeared within the process area, reflecting recirculation pockets generated by tanks, buildings, and piping arrays. These zones are not separate sources; rather, they are poorly ventilated cavities in which methane accumulates because exchange with the main advecting plume is weak. A sensor placed in one of these pockets would record concentrations higher than those in the bulk plume and, if interpreted with an overly simplistic dispersion model, could imply an exaggerated methane-release rate. At 3.6 km/h, the NE plume broadened and remained closer to the plant, but as the wind speed increased, the main plume narrowed while internal hot spots persisted in sheltered zones. The NE cases therefore show that informative monitoring locations are not restricted to the geometric downwind edge of the site; some of the strongest signals can occur inside the plant, where the built environment creates persistent retention zones.

Under the south inflow, the plume encountered the northern tree belt more directly than in the SSW cases, providing the clearest example of tree-induced redirection. At 18 km/h, part of the tank plume was turned westward along the treeline, demonstrating that the canopy can act as a flow-deflecting barrier at the plant scale rather than merely as a roughness element. At 3.6 and 9 km/h, the plume remained more broadly distributed near the site, but sheltered areas still formed on the leeward side of trees and structures. As the temperature increased, buoyant rise became somewhat more apparent, reducing the strength of near-ground tree interaction, yet the dominant feature remained the redirection of low-elevation methane by the combined plant-tree geometry. The south wind cases, therefore, illustrate that vegetation placement relative to the incoming wind can determine whether methane is carried through the main downwind corridor or retained and redirected along the plant perimeter.

Considering all three wind directions together, the 3.6 km/h cases define the low-advection regime. The tank plume stayed within or just above the roughness sublayer surrounding the process area, spread laterally through the near-field, and was strongly influenced by local recirculation and stagnation zones. In this regime, a single downwind fence-line sensor is least reliable because the plume can meander through multiple sheltered areas, remain close to the source, and produce elevated concentrations inside the plant. Monitoring therefore benefits from source-proximal and sheltered near-ground sensors that can detect methane retained within the built- and tree-environment.

At 9 km/h, the cases entered a transitional regime. The plume followed a recognizable downwind direction, but wake structures behind process equipment and canopy-edge deflection were still strong enough to divide the downwind footprint into preferred subsectors. This is the regime in which the internal airflow structures of the plant become especially important for network design: the main advective plume can be sampled by a perimeter sensor, while a second sensor is needed to capture methane retained in wake-affected zones near the process area. Without this paired coverage, a monitoring network may either miss part of the plume or overinterpret a sheltered hotspot.

At 18 km/h, the cases entered the strong-advection regime. The plume became narrow and elongated, and the most reliable interception opportunities shifted toward sector-aligned positions downwind and, in some cases, to elevated locations that can sample plume segments rising over structures or above parts of the canopy. Even in this regime, however, the simulations show that trees and plant structures do not become irrelevant: they can still redirect portions of the low-elevation plume, especially when the incoming wind is aligned with a tree belt, and they can maintain localized accumulation zones within the plant. The practical message is that higher wind speeds simplify the dominant transport pathway but do not eliminate the plant-scale flow complexity. Across all three wind directions, ambient temperature produced secondary density-related changes in plume rise and dilution, whereas wind speed determined the monitoring regime and wind direction determined where that regime was expressed around the site.

Wind Direction Shifts the Informative Monitoring Sector

Building on the direction-specific plume patterns described in Section , wind direction changed not only the orientation of the plume but also which sectors of the gas plant became informative for monitoring. This occurred because different inflow directions activated different combinations of building wakes, pipe-rack sheltering, road-corridor channeling, and tree-belt deflection. Under south–southwest inflow, the plume encountered the plant asymmetrically and was steered along the northern portion of the site, where the road corridor and adjacent treeline altered the local transport path. Under due-south inflow, the plume impinged more directly on the vegetation barrier and on the southern face of the facility, which produced stronger lee-side sheltering and lateral bending along the tree edge. Under northeast inflow, the downwind sector shifted to the opposite side of the plant, and the plume interacted with a different set of wakes and internal flow cavities before leaving the site.

The practical implication is that the informative monitoring sector migrates around the plant as the wind rotates. A location that repeatedly intercepts the plume under one wind sector may become a background location under another, or it may sample a sheltered cavity rather than the main advecting plume. Wind direction also changes which recirculation zones are aerodynamically connected to the source, so the same concentration hot spot does not have the same meaning across sectors. Fixed sensor placement should therefore be derived from the full wind rose and the sector-specific concentration fields rather than from geometric symmetry or a single prevailing-wind case. If the monitoring objective requires robust coverage under both dominant and secondary wind sectors, then either additional fixed sensors or a wind-responsive mobile component will be needed.

Trees Matter Because They Reshape Detectability, Not Only Plume Size

Beyond the overall plume direction, the simulations show that coupled plant-and-tree aerodynamics regulate local methane accumulation, as illustrated in Figure . Tanks, buildings, pipe racks, and narrow passages between structures generate separated recirculation zones, stagnation pockets, and shear layers. The perimeter trees interact with these facility-induced structures by reducing inflow momentum, increasing sheltering, and partially blocking the plume exit from the process area. The result is a heterogeneous airflow field in which methane can be temporarily trapped, re-entrained, or redirected through low-velocity corridors before it reaches the plant boundary. Streamlines in Figure are selected trajectories in the mean velocity field and should not be interpreted as unattenuated flow through solid trees; the tree volumes are porous momentum sinks that decelerate the mean flow and promote canopy edge sheltering.

Velocity streamlines and methane plume evolution in the presence of trees for south wind, 3.6 km/h wind speed, and 15 °C air temperature. The blue isosurface corresponds to a methane mass fraction of 1 × 10–6, or approximately 1.8 ppmv in air; detached blue regions are interpreted as recirculated plume segments, not additional methane sources.

This behavior is evident in localized high-concentration pockets that appear within the process area in several simulated cases. These isolated regions are not evidence of multiple leaks; rather, they reflect how the gas-plant geometry fragments the plume and feeds parts of it into poorly ventilated zones between or behind equipment. The appearance of methane near the tree line in Figure arises from plume material that has been transported, deflected, and re-entrained by the coupled plant-canopy flow field. Once methane enters these isolated zones, the local ventilation rate decreases, and the residence time increases. Methane can therefore accumulate inside the plant even when the nominal site wind speed is high enough to advect the main plume away from the source. The tree belt can intensify this effect by weakening the through-flow near the plant edge and by generating canopy-edge wakes that feed back into the process area.

This distinction is critical for interpreting the sensor data. A sensor located inside one of these sheltered internal flow structures may register sustained or repeatedly elevated methane concentrations because it is sampling a poorly ventilated accumulation zone rather than the main-advecting plume. Such a location is valuable for leak detection because it is highly sensitive to methane. However, if the same concentration record is used in isolation to infer the emission rate, the result can be biased high. Simplified inversions typically attribute elevated concentrations to larger source terms, but within a recirculation zone, concentrations can also be elevated because dilution is suppressed and methane is repeatedly re-entrained. In other words, the sensor signal reflects both the source strength and the local trapping efficiency. Without accounting for the local airflow structure, inversion can overestimate the release rate.

The simulations further show that trees affect the tank and flare releases differently. The elevated flare plume is comparatively insensitive to internal recirculation once it rises above the roughness layer; therefore, concentrations measured along its advective path are more representative of bulk transport. The tank plume, by contrast, remains embedded in the obstacle layer for a substantial distance and is much more sensitive to sheltering, wake capture, and canopy-edge deflection. Low-elevation sources are therefore more vulnerable to biased quantification if the monitoring network relies too heavily on sheltered or near-source sensors. Wake or canopy-edge sensors should be interpreted as detection-oriented nodes and paired with sensors that sample the more advective portion of the plume.

Taken together, the CFD results support a role-based monitoring network rather than either a uniform perimeter layout or a purely source-proximal layout, as shown in Table . For this site, a defensible baseline network includes a source-proximal sentinel near likely leak equipment to capture low-wind and near-field retention events; a sector-aligned downwind perimeter sensor in the dominant advective corridor under the prevailing SSW sector; a sheltered canopy-edge or wake sensor where repeated accumulation occurs, particularly near treeline or internal-equipment wakes; an elevated or above-canopy sensor to intercept lifted plume segments and elevated releases; and a background or crosswind reference sensor. These roles are complementary because they sample different plume regimes rather than duplicating one another.

Sensor Roles Implied by the CFD Results

The key issue is not only the detection coverage but also robustness in quantification. The source-proximal and sheltered sensors provide high sensitivity to the presence of a leak, especially under low-wind or strong-wake conditions. The sector-aligned and elevated sensors are more informative for inferring source strength because they sample plume segments that are governed more by bulk advection than by local trapping. The background sensor is necessary so that measured methane enhancements can be interpreted relative to the site background rather than as absolute methane values. Using these roles together should reduce the risk that a single accumulation-prone sensor drives the emission estimate.

The number of sensors required depends on the wind sectors and plume regimes that the monitoring objectives must cover. Because south–southwest winds dominate the site’s wind rose, the first additional fixed sensors beyond the core network should be assigned to the south–southwest transport corridor and to the associated sheltered subsector within the plant. Coverage for the south and northeast sectors can then be added according to risk tolerance, desired all-weather coverage, and budget. Sensor count is therefore not a universal constant for gas plants of this type. It is a site-specific consequence of the wind rose, source heights, obstacle layout, and degree to which the monitoring system must support both leak detection and reliable release rate estimation.

From a practical standpoint, the present results also argue against using concentration data from a single sensor type for inversion. Near-source or sheltered sensors may provide early warning but should not be treated as representative of the entire plume. Conversely, a perimeter-only network can miss low-wind retention and internal accumulation within the plant. A wind-aware network that combines source-proximal, sheltered, sector-aligned, elevated, and background roles is better suited to both tasks: detecting leaks rapidly and constraining methane release rates without systematically inflating the inferred source strength.

The placement hierarchy derived from the existing CFD matrix is, therefore, as follows: first, assign one background/crosswind sensor to define the local methane enhancement. Second, place one source-proximal sentinel in the tank- and flare-associated equipment cluster to capture low-wind retention. Third, place a dominant-sector downwind sensor along the SSW transport corridor near the northern road/treeline sector. Fourth, add a wake or canopy-edge sensor in the sheltered subsector repeatedly identified in low- and moderate-wind cases. Fifth, an elevated or above-canopy sensor where the high-wind cases show plume lifting. Additional fixed sensors should be added only for secondary wind sectors that the monitoring objective must cover, especially the south and northeast winds.

Limitations

The study has several limitations that should be kept in mind when interpreting the monitoring implications. The simulations were steady RANS calculations and therefore represent mean plume behavior rather than transient concentration fluctuations, gusts, or plume meandering at sensor time scales. The temperature cases were imposed as uniform ambient conditions and do not represent a resolved atmospheric-stability spectrum, winter inversion structure, or surface-heat-flux condition. The discussion of sensor placement is therefore strongest for mean wind-regime effects and should not be overinterpreted as a full transient-detectability or atmospheric-stability analysis.

In addition, we use the example model here to identify sensor roles and candidate monitoring sectors rather than solving a full numerical placement optimization directly on the site geometry. The work should therefore be read as a CFD-informed sensor-architecture study, not as a claim that a unique optimal layout has already been computed for this facility. A future study should combine transient wind forcing, explicit sensor-detection thresholds, controlled-release validation, and cost-constrained optimization in order to convert the present role-based recommendations into a fully optimized site design.

Other modeling simplifications also affect absolute concentrations. The inlet is a uniform mean wind rather than a fully developed logarithmic atmospheric-boundary-layer profile with an empty-domain horizontal-homogeneity demonstration; canopy zones are represented as momentum sinks without additional canopy-turbulence source terms; porous resistance and unresolved pipe-rack losses represent bulk drag rather than detailed geometry, and the flare-stack case represents noncombusting methane venting rather than a hot, combusting flare. These assumptions are appropriate for comparing mean wind-regime pathways within the existing simulation matrix but should be revisited before using the model for regulatory quantification or final hardware siting.

Conclusions

This study used 27 steady RANS CFD cases to examine how a 320 m × 320 m × 120 m gas-plant domain, a 32 m perimeter tree belt, and two representative methane-source height classes shape candidate sensor locations. The main result is not a unique optimized layout but a wind-aware sensor architecture: buildings, process equipment, and surrounding trees do not simply change the plume shape; they move the informative monitoring zones around the site as wind direction and wind speed change.

The wind speed determines the dominant monitoring regime. At 3.6 km/h, methane remained closer to the source and inside wake/canopy-edge sheltering zones, supporting source-proximal and near-ground wake sensors. At 9 km/h, a transitional plume formed in which an advective sensor and a wake-sensitive sensor were both needed. At 18 km/h, the plume was more coherent, and downwind-sector or elevated sensors became more informative. The ambient temperature was a secondary density effect in this neutral, uniform-temperature formulation and should not be interpreted as a stability study.

The wind direction determines where these roles should be placed. Rotating the wind from SSW to the south or northeast moved the main monitoring sector around the plant and changed which recirculation zones were aerodynamically connected to the sources. The resulting baseline architecture contains five roles: source proximal, dominant sector downwind, canopy edge/wake, elevated, and background. For the present site, a prevailing-wind-focused network should prioritize the SSW transport corridor and its associated sheltered subsector, while expanded coverage of south and northeast sectors would be added according to risk tolerance and budget. These conclusions are specific to the mean-wind CFD matrix and should be coupled with transient validation and formal optimization before a final regulatory sensor layout is selected.
