Abstract
. We prove a discreteness result for the possible orders of harmonic maps from surfaces to Euclidean buildings; in particular for a building of type W W the order is of the form m k \frac{m}{k} where k k divides | W | |W| . This generalizes, in the case where the domain has dimension 2 2 , the “order gap” of Gromov and Schoen. This result follows by directly analyzing the behavior of homogeneous maps into Euclidean buildings, and then studying a related spherical billiards problem.



1.
Introduction

In this note, we demonstrate discreteness of the spectrum for the order of a harmonic map from a Riemann surface to a Euclidean building. In particular, we characterize the behavior of homogeneous harmonic maps in this setting. More than simply a curiosity, discreteness of the order spectrum has played a crucial role in regularity and rigidity results for harmonic maps in singular (and smooth) settings.

As a first step in resolving the
p
-adic superrigidity problem for rank 1 symmetric spaces of non-compact type, Gromov and Schoen
[
11
]
prove an order gap. For a harmonic map into a locally finite building
X
, they demonstrate that there exists an
\epsilon_{X}>0
so that every point in the domain either has order 1 or order at least
1+\epsilon_{X}
. This gap leads to improved regularity for order 1 points which, coupled with an induction on dimension argument, allows them to show the singular set has codimension
2
; with this level of control, they prove an integral Bochner type formula to establish rigidity. Sun
[
19
]
proves the order gap and regularity for harmonic maps into
\mathbb{R}
-trees. A key step in the proof of the holomorphic rigidity conjecture for Teichmüller space, settled by Daskalopoulos and Mese in
[
7
]
, is also to establish an order gap. More recently, in collaboration with Mese, the authors prove the full non-Archimedean superrigidity
[
3
]
by first establishing an order gap when
X
is non-locally finite.

Characterization of tangent maps also feed into regularity, uniqueness and uniformization results. Using quantitative stratification techniques, the second author shows
[
10
]
the singular set of a harmonic map into a locally finite building is
(n-2)
-rectifiable. Extending his work, the authors
[
2
]
define the singular stratum for such maps and prove
k
-rectifiability for the
k
-th stratum. Kuwert
[
15
]
determines the discrete order spectrum for harmonic maps from Riemann surfaces into non-positively curved cones by completely characterizing homogeneous harmonic maps in this setting. He then uses this characterization to study minimizers in a degree 1 homotopy class between closed surfaces with the target having isolated cone points–minimizers are unique and are precisely the Teichmüller map when the target metric is the Teichmüller metric. The first author and Mese
[
5
]
use the order spectrum and the structure of homogenous maps from
[
15
]
to define a winding number for harmonic maps into CAT(1) surfaces and ultimately prove a uniformization theorem for CAT(1) spheres.

What we refer to as the
order
first appears in the literature in Almgren’s study of
Q
-valued harmonic maps as the
frequency function
(cf.
[
1
]
).
In the case of
Q
-valued harmonic maps from surfaces, De Lellis and Spadaro
[
9
]
classify the possible orders of harmonic maps–possible orders are of the form
\frac{a}{Q^{*}}
where
Q^{*}\leq Q
; in particular the possible orders form a discrete set for any fixed
Q
. This discreteness leads to explicit fast decay for the order at scale
r
, which in turn can be used to prove uniqueness for tangent maps. As an application, they show that the singular set of such maps is discrete, an improvement over the bound on the Hausdorff dimension (which is known for all dimensions)
[
1
,
9
]
. For domains of dimension
n\geq 2
, it is known that the singular set is
(n-2)
-rectifiable by
[
8
]
; the key montone quantity in this study is, again, the order.

Mese has conjectured that the set of possible orders for harmonic maps from surfaces into Euclidean buildings is discrete; the present note shows that this conjecture holds. Precisely, we have the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
S
be a domain in a Riemann surface, and let
X
be a (not necessarily locally finite) Euclidean building of type
W
. If
u:S\to X
is a harmonic map, and
x_{0}\in S
, then the order
\text{Ord}^{u}(x_{0})=\frac{m}{k}
for
m,k\in\mathbb{N}
where
k
divides
|W|
. In particular, the possible orders lie in a discrete set determined solely by
W
.

If
X
is a rank
1
Euclidean building (a tree or an
\mathbb{R}
-tree), then in fact
\text{Ord}_{u}(x_{0})=\frac{m}{2}
for
2\leq m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Observe that in the setting where
X
is a locally finite tree, the result here is already well-known from work on the optimal partition problem (cf.
[
17
]
and the references therein).

The structure of this paper is as follows:

(1)

In section
2

.

(2)

In section

has order

for

, or (2)

takes

to a curve at constant distance from

.

(3)

In section

(4)

In collaboration with Mese, the authors are extending the ideas of this work to show discreteness for the order when

is a pluriharmonic map and
\widetilde{M}
is Kähler.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
CAT(0) spaces

A CAT(0) space
(X,d)
is a geodesic space of non-positive curvature, where curvature is defined through triangle comparison with Euclidean triangles. Particular examples of CAT(0) spaces include Euclidean buildings (the principal focus of this note) as well as Hadamard manifolds. We refer to
[
6
]
for a complete introduction to these spaces. For our purposes, it will suffice to know that harmonic maps into CAT(0) spaces satisfy certain regularity properties, detailed in
2.3
.

2.2.
Euclidean & Spherical Buildings

In this paper, we use Kleiner and Leeb’s notion of Euclidean buildings and hence refer the reader to
[
12
]
for the details. The equivalence of this notion with the one developed by Tits
[
20
]
was established by A. Parreau
[
18
]
, so there is no loss in this approach (which is more metric in flavor than Tits’).

Let
\mathbb{E}^{N}
be an
N
-dimensional affine space and
\partial_{Tits}\mathbb{E}^{N}\simeq\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
be its Tits boundary. Denote by
\rho:\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb{E}^{N})\to\mathrm{Isom}(\partial_{Tits}\mathbb{E}^{N})
the canonical homomorphism which assigns to each affine isometry its rotational part. An affine Weyl group
W_{\mathrm{aff}}
is a subgroup of
\mbox{Isom}(\mathbb{E}^{N})
generated by reflections with finite
reflection group

W:=\rho(W_{\mathrm{aff}})\subset\mbox{Isom}(\partial_{Tits}\mathbb{E}^{N})
. The pair
(\mathbb{E}^{N},W_{\mathrm{aff}})
is then called a
Euclidean Coxeter complex
. A
wall
is a hyperplane of
\mathbb{E}^{N}
which is the fixed point set of a reflection in
W_{\mathrm{aff}}
.

A
Euclidean building

(X,d)
of type
W
is a CAT(0) space endowed with a collection of isometries
\{\iota_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to X\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
which satisfy certain axioms (cf.
[
12
, Section 4.1.2]
for the precise list). We refer to the integer
N
as the
dimension
of
X
.

As a consequence of
[
12
, Corollary 4.6.2]
, the atlas
\mathcal{A}
satisfies the following two properties:

(1)

Every geodesic segment, ray, and line is contained in an image of an isometric embedding of the collection (cf.
[
12
, EB3]
), and

(2)

Two isometric embeddings
\iota_{1}
,
\iota_{2}
of the collection are compatible in the sense that
\iota_{1}^{-1}\circ\iota_{2}
is a restriction of an isometry in
W_{\mathrm{aff}}
(cf.
[
12
, EB4]
)

and
\mathcal{A}
is the maximal collection satisfying the above two properties. We call
\iota\in\mathcal{A}
a
chart
and its image
A:=\iota(\mathbb{R}^{N})
an
apartment
.

Given geodesics
c_{1},c_{2}
emanating from a common point
p\in X
, for each
t>0
consider the triangle
\Delta pc_{1}(t)c_{2}(t)
and its comparison triangle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with vertices labeled by
\Delta PC_{1}(t)C_{2}(t)
. Let
\widetilde{\angle}_{P}(C_{1}(t),C_{2}(t))
denote the angle at
P
. As this quantity is known to be monotone in
t
, we define
\angle_{p}(c_{1},c_{2}):=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\widetilde{\angle}_{P}(C_{1}(t),C_{2}(t))
(cf
[
12
, Section 2.1.3]
).

Definition 2.1
.

Two geodesics
c_{1},c_{2}
emanating from a common point
p\in X
are said to be equivalent if
\angle_{p}(c_{1},c_{2})=0
. A
geodesic germ
at
p
is an equivalence class of geodesics emanating from
p
. The space of geodesic germs at
p
along with the distance function defined by
\angle_{p}
is a complete metric space by
[
12
, Lemma 4.2.2]
and defines the
space of directions

\Sigma_{p}X
.

Definition 2.2
.

For
p\in X
, the
tangent cone

(T_{p}X,d_{p})
is a metric cone over
\Sigma_{p}X
. Denote the vertex of
T_{p}X
by
\mathsf{O}
. Any element of
T_{p}X\backslash\mathsf{O}
can be written as

([\gamma],t)

where
[\gamma]
is a geodesic germ at
p
and
t\in(0,\infty)
.

Remark 2.3
.

By
[
12
, Section 4.2.2]
, for a Euclidean building
X
of type
W
, and
p\in X
:

(1)

\Sigma_{p}X
is a spherical building modelled on
(\mathbb{S}^{N-1},W)
.

(2)

\Sigma_{p}X
consists of copies of
\Delta_{\text{mod}}:=\mathbb{S}^{N-1}/W
with disjoint interiors, and there is a canonical map
\theta_{\Sigma}:\Sigma_{p}X\to\Delta_{\text{mod}}
which is an isometry on each copy of
\Delta_{\text{mod}}
in
\Sigma_{p}X
.

2.3.
Harmonic Maps

We will not provide a full exposition of the theory of harmonic maps into CAT(0) spaces and instead refer the interested reader to
[
11
,
13
,
14
]
for further details. It will suffice for our purposes to know that for
\Omega
a Riemannian domain and
(X,d)
a CAT(0) space, the function space
W^{1,2}(\Omega,X)
is sensibly defined. For
u\in W^{1,2}(\Omega,X)
, there exists an energy density function
|\nabla u|^{2}
and a trace map which permits us to speak meaningfully of the values of
u
on
\partial\Omega
. Harmonic maps in our context will mean
minimizing
harmonic maps; maps which minimize energy when compared to competitors with the same boundary values.

Definition 2.4
.

For a map
u\in W^{1,2}(B_{r}(x),X)
the
energy of
u
on
B_{r}(x)
is

E_{u}(x,r):=\int_{B_{r}(x)}|\nabla u(y)|^{2}dy.

We say that a map
u\in W^{1,2}(B_{r}(x),X)
is
harmonic
(or energy-minimizing) if for every
v
so that
u=v
on
\partial B_{r}(x)
,

E_{u}(x,r)\leq E_{v}(x,r).

A map
u\in W^{1,2}(\Omega,X)
is
harmonic
if for every
x\in\Omega
there exists an
r>0
such that
u|B_{r}(x)
is harmonic by the previous definition.

We will sometimes omit the subscript and write
E(x,r)
when the map under discussion is clear.

Regularity of harmonic maps into Euclidean buildings is established, and we will mention results which will be of import in this note. Let
u:\Omega\to X
be harmonic, where
X
is a Euclidean building. Given
x\in\Omega
, if there exists
r>0
such that
u|B_{r}(x)
maps into a single apartment
A
of
X
, then
u|B_{r}(x)
is simply a map into a Euclidean space. We thus say that such a point is
regular
and call the set
\mathcal{R}(u)
of such points the
regular set
(of
u
). If
x\notin\mathcal{R}(u)
, we say that
x
is a
singular point
and write
x\in\mathcal{S}(u)
. When
X
is a Euclidean building (locally finite or not), we have the following bound on the size of the singular set.

Theorem 2.5
.

[
[
11
, Theorem 6.4]
and
[
3
, Theorem 1.1]
]
If
u:\Omega\to X
is a harmonic map from a Riemannian domain to a Euclidean building, then the singular set
\mathcal{S}(u)
has Hausdorff codimension
2
. In particular, if
\dim(\Omega)=2
,
\mathcal{S}(u)
has Hausdorff dimension
0
.

When
X
is locally finite, the authors go further and establish in
[
2
]
that the singular set admits a natural stratification into rectifiable strata.

2.4.
Order function

The order
\text{Ord}^{u}(x_{0})
of a harmonic map
u
is analogous to the degree of the first nonvanishing term of the Taylor expansion of
u(x)-u(x_{0})
. However, unlike in the smooth setting,
\text{Ord}^{u}(x_{0})
does not need to be integer valued at singular points of
u
.

Definition 2.6
.

For a map
u:\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\to X
, where
X
is a Euclidean building, let

\displaystyle I(x,r)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\partial B_{r}(x)}d^{2}(u(y),u(x))dy,

\displaystyle\text{Ord}(x,r)

\displaystyle:=\frac{rE(x,r)}{I(x,r)}.

In
[
11
]
the authors show that for locally finite targets, there is a

is a monotonically increasing function of

, bounded from below by
1
. This monotonicity extends easily to the non-locally finite setting and thus the limit as

of
\text{Ord}(x,r)
is hence well-defined for each
x\in\Omega
.

Definition 2.7
.

The
order function

\text{Ord}:\Omega\to[1,\infty)
is given by

\text{Ord}(x):=\lim_{r\to 0}\text{Ord}(x,r).

For harmonic maps into locally finite buildings,
[
11
]
establish an order gap.

2.5.
Homogeneity and tangent maps

Gromov and Schoen
[
11
]
develop a notion of tangent maps for harmonic maps into some CAT(0) spaces, including locally finite Euclidean buildings. The situation for more general CAT(0) space targets is developed in
[
13
,
14
]
, and a particular extension focused on non-locally finite Euclidean buildings can be found in
[
3
]
. The notion of tangent maps has also been extended to CAT(1) targets in
[
4
]
. Tangent maps in this setting are homogeneous but, in contrast to the smooth setting, are non-constant even at smooth points. In particular, at points of order
1
, the tangent map is a non-constant affine map.

Let
u:B_{\sigma}(x)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(X,d)
be a harmonic map into a Euclidean building
X
and change coordinates in the domain so that
x=0
. Then for each
\lambda>0
define the map

(2.1)

u_{\lambda}:B_{\sigma/\lambda}(0)\to(X,d_{\lambda})

by
u_{\lambda}(y):=u(\lambda y)
, where
d_{\lambda}=(\lambda^{1-n}I(0,\lambda))^{-1/2}d
.

The scalings on domain and target are chosen so that for all
\lambda>0
the functions
u_{\lambda}
have uniform energy bounds on
B_{1}
. In the locally finite setting, the regularity theory of
[
11
]
implies that a subsequence converges uniformly to a non-constant, harmonic map
u_{*}:B_{1}\to T_{u(x)}X
. In the non-locally finite setting,
[
3
]
shows how to use ultralimits to obtain a map
u_{*}
into an ultralimit building
X_{*}
.

Definition 2.9
.

Any subsequential limit map
u_{*}:B_{1}\to T_{u(x)}X
in the locally finite setting or any ultralimit map
u_{*}:B_{1}\to X_{*}
in the non-locally finite setting realized by the rescaling process above is called a
tangent map to
u
at
x
.

Remark 2.10
.

Two crucial properties of this rescaling process should be noted. First, the order remains unchanged. That is

(2.2)

\text{Ord}^{u}(x)=\text{Ord}^{u_{*}}(0).

Second, if
X
is of type
W
, then
T_{u(x)}X,X_{*}
are both of type
W
(cf.
[
12
,
3
]
).

Even in the non-locally finite setting, we shall see that for maps from surfaces, these ultralimits map (at least locally) into a metric cone over a spherical building; cf. Lemma
2.12
. We shall need the following definition to establish this.

Definition 2.11
.

A harmonic map
u:B_{1}(0)\to X
is
intrinsically homogeneous of order
\alpha
if for all
x\in B_{1}(0)
,
\lambda\in(0,1)
,

(1)

d(u(\lambda x),u(0))=\lambda^{\alpha}d(u(x),u(0)),

(2)

The image of
\lambda\mapsto u(\lambda x)
is a geodesic segment from
u(0)
.

By
[
3

and
[

, any tangent map

arising from our rescaling process is necessarily intrinsically homogeneous, even if the target is non-locally finite.

Now we prove that every intrinsically homogeneous map from a Euclidean disk into a Euclidean building maps into a cone over the space of directions, at least locally. This will justify our subsequent focus on

Lemma 2.12
.

If
u:B_{2}(0)\to X
is an intrinsically homogeneous harmonic map from the unit disc

In particular, there is a ball
B_{\rho}(0)

is isometric to a subset of a finite simplicial complex in
T_{u(0)}X
.

Proof.

Since

. Nevertheless, we claim that for each
p\in\partial B_{1}(0)=\mathbb{S}^{1}

I_{p}\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{1}

\mathcal{A}_{p}=\{A_{1,p},\dots,A_{k_{p},p}\}

is a regular point this is of course immediate, with

If

\lambda\in(1,2)

Lemma 2.14

Suppose that

\alpha=\text{Ord}^{u}(x_{0})

, where
B_{1}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

In light of this, to prove Theorem

Theorem 2.15
.

If
u:B_{1}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\to X
is a nonconstant homogeneous harmonic map of order
\alpha
, and

is a conical Euclidean building of type
W
, then
\alpha=\frac{m}{k}
for
m,k\in\mathbb{N}
where
k
divides
|W|
. If
X
is dimension
1
(a tree or
\mathbb{R}
-tree), then in fact
\alpha=\frac{m}{2}
for some natural number
m\geq 2
.

3.
First steps towards discreteness

In this section, we establish an important dichotomy for homogeneous harmonic maps from surfaces.

Theorem 3.1
.

For a nonconstant homogeneous harmonic map
u:B_{2}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\to X
of order
\alpha
, where
X
is a conical Euclidean building, one of the following holds:

(1)

either
\alpha=\text{Ord}(0)=\frac{m}{2}
for some
2\leq m\in\mathbb{N}
, or

(2)

d_{X}(u(\phi),u(0))
is constant for
\phi\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

Proof.

By homogeneity, it will suffice to understand the behavior of
u|\partial B_{1}(0)
(or indeed any circle enclosing the origin). Further, homogeneity and Theorem
2.5
imply that
\mathcal{S}(u)\subset\{0\}
.
Thus, for each
x\in\partial B_{1}(0)
, there is a ball
B_{\rho(x)}(x)
on which
u
is regular, that is, so that
u(B_{\rho(x)}(x))\subseteq A
for some apartment
A
of
X
. Because
X
is conical, we may choose to fix coordinates on
all
apartments of
X
so that
0\in A
is the cone point
0_{X}
in
X
. Working in such coordinates, and regarding
w=u|B_{\rho(x)}(x):B_{\rho(x)}(x)\to A
as a regular harmonic map,
w
satisfies the harmonic map equation in polar coordinates:

0=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}w}{\partial\theta^{2}}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial w}{\partial r}+\frac{\partial^{2}w}{\partial r^{2}}.

Writing
w(r,\theta)=r^{\alpha}g(\theta)
and setting
r=1
, we obtain a differential equation for
g
,

(3.1)

0=\frac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial\theta^{2}}(\theta)+\alpha^{2}g(\theta)

so that
g(\theta)={\bf v}_{1}\cos(\alpha\theta)+{\bf v}_{2}\sin(\alpha\theta)
for some vectors
{\bf v}_{1},{\bf v}_{2}\in A
, where
a priori
this representation
g
is valid only on a neighborhood of the point
x=(1,\theta_{x})
, possibly depending on the choice of the point
x\in\partial B_{1}(0)
and the apartment
A
. To analyze the original map
u
more closely, we want to determine which features of
g(\theta)
actually depend on these choices.

The quantity
q(\theta):=d^{2}(u(e^{i\theta}),u(0))
is a well-defined function on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
without reference to
A
or
x
, but we can also locally express it in terms of
g
, as
q(\theta)=\|g(\theta)\|^{2}
. Hence for each
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, we may locally compute
q^{\prime}(\theta)
using
g
as follows

q^{\prime}(\theta)=2g(\theta)\cdot g^{\prime}(\theta)=\alpha(\|{\bf v}_{2}\|^{2}-\|{\bf v}_{1}\|^{2})\sin(2\alpha\theta)-2\alpha({\bf v}_{1}\cdot{\bf v}_{2})\cos(2\alpha\theta).

This representation reveals that
q(\theta)
is differentiable (and indeed smooth) at each
x\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Moreover, if
A_{1},A_{2}
are two apartments so that for an interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
u(I)\subset A_{1}\cap A_{2}
, we may compare the representations of
u
in these apartments. Suppose that for all
\theta\in I
,

u(\theta)={\bf v}_{1}\cos(\alpha\theta)+{\bf v}_{2}\sin(\alpha\theta)={\bf w}_{1}\cos(\alpha\theta)+{\bf w}_{2}\sin(\alpha\theta)

where
{\bf v}_{1},{\bf v}_{2}\in A_{1}
and
{\bf w}_{1},{\bf w}_{2}\in A_{2}
. Computing
q^{\prime}(\theta)
for
\theta\in I
, and comparing coefficients on
\cos(2\alpha\theta)
and
\sin(2\alpha\theta)
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\|{\bf v}_{2}\|^{2}-\|{\bf v}_{1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|{\bf w}_{2}\|^{2}-\|{\bf w}_{1}\|^{2},

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{1}\cdot{\bf v}_{2}

\displaystyle={\bf w}_{1}\cdot{\bf w}_{2}.

That is, that the quantities
\|{\bf v}_{2}\|-\|{\bf v}_{1}\|
and
{\bf v}_{1}\cdot{\bf v}_{2}
are independent of the particular representation
g
and thus are independent of our original choice of
x
.

Thus, the representation

q^{\prime}(\theta)=\alpha(\|{\bf v}_{2}\|^{2}-\|{\bf v}_{1}\|^{2})\sin(2\alpha\theta)-2\alpha({\bf v}_{1}\cdot{\bf v}_{2})\cos(2\alpha\theta)

is valid for all
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Now, since
q
is a smooth function on all of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, unless
\|{\bf v}_{2}\|^{2}-\|{\bf v}_{1}\|^{2}={\bf v}_{1}\cdot{\bf v}_{2}=0
, we conclude immediately that
2\alpha
is an integer. On the other hand, if
\|{\bf v}_{2}\|^{2}-\|{\bf v}_{1}\|^{2}={\bf v}_{1}\cdot{\bf v}_{2}=0
, we directly see that
q^{\prime}(\theta)=0
for all
\theta
, so that
q(\theta)
is constant.
∎

In case (1) of
3.1
,
u
already satisfies the desired discreteness result for the order, so we turn to case (2). To close out this section, we show that when case (2) holds, the
order
of a homogeneous map
u
is closely related to the
length
of
u(\mathbb{S}^{1})
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose that
u:B_{1}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\to X
is a homogeneous harmonic map of order
\alpha
, where
X
is a conical Euclidean building, and
u(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is a curve at constant distance
L
from
u(0)
. Then
\ell(u(\mathbb{S}^{1}))=2\pi\alpha L
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that for any point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, there is an interval
I=[a,b]\subset\mathbb{S}^{1}
containing
x_{0}
so that
u(I)
is an interval of length
\ell(I)\alpha L
.

To see this, recall that any such
x_{0}
is a regular point of
u
, so there is a neighborhood
V
of
x_{0}
and an apartment
A
so that
u
has the representation

u(r,\theta)=r^{\alpha}\sin(\alpha\theta)\bf{v}_{1}+r^{\alpha}\cos(\alpha\theta)\bf{v}_{2}

where
\bf{v}_{1}
and
\bf{v}_{2}
are orthogonal vectors of length
L
in
A
. If
I\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{1}\cap V
is an interval of length
\ell(I)
containing
x_{0}
, direct computation of the arc length reveals that
u(I)
is a curve of length
\ell(I)\alpha L
.
∎

4.
Spherical Billiards

In this section, we connect the problem of finding the length of
u(\mathbb{S}^{1})
to a spherical billiards problem in
\Delta_{\text{mod}}
(cf. Remark
2.3
). First, we show that case (2) of the previous section’s dichotomy implies that
u(\mathbb{S}^{1})\subseteq\Sigma_{u(0)}X
.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
u:B_{2}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\to X
is a homogeneous harmonic map so that
u(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is at a constant distance to
u(0)
, there exist
\rho,\beta>0
so that
\beta u(\rho\mathbb{S}^{1})
maps isometrically into
\Sigma_{u(0)}X
, the space of directions at
u(0)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.12
, or by local finiteness, there exists a
\rho>0
so that
u(B_{\rho}(0))
is isometric to a subset of
T_{u(0)}X
. Thus by homogeneity and the fact that
u(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is at constant distance to
u(0)
,
d(u(0),u(\rho/2,\theta))=\lambda
for some constant
\lambda>0
. Thus
u(\rho/2,\theta)=(\lambda,[\gamma_{\theta}])
for germs
[\gamma_{\theta}]\in\Sigma_{u(0)}X
and we may consider
\frac{1}{\lambda}u|\partial B_{\rho}(0)
as a map into
\Sigma_{u(x_{0})}X
.
∎

The previous lemma implies that if
u
is a homogeneous harmonic map with
d(u(\mathbb{S}^{1}),u(0))
a constant, then after possibly rescaling the domain and target, we may regard
u|\mathbb{S}^{1}
as a map into
\Sigma_{u(0)}X
. We now consider properties of this map.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\rho,\beta>0
be as in Lemma
4.1
.
For
\gamma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\Sigma_{u(x_{0})}X
such that

(4.1)

\gamma(\theta):=\beta u(\rho,\theta),

the following hold:

(1)

\gamma
is a local geodesic with constant speed
\alpha
.

(2)

\ell(\gamma(\mathbb{S}^{1}))=2\pi\alpha
.

Proof.

Given
x\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, there exists
\delta>0
and an apartment
A_{x}
of
X
such that
u(B_{\delta}(x))\subset A_{x}
. By homogeneity,
\beta u(B_{\rho}(0))\cap A_{x}
lies in a plane containing
0_{X}
, so
\beta u(\rho\mathbb{S}^{1})\cap A_{x}
lies in a great circle of
\Sigma_{u(0)}A_{x}\simeq\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
. Thus,
\gamma|(B_{\delta}(x)\cap\mathbb{S}^{1})
maps into
\Sigma_{u(0)}X
as a geodesic. Since this is true for all
x\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, the map
\gamma
is a local geodesic. Moreover, in local coordinates within each apartment we may orthonormal vectors
\bf{v}_{1},\bf{v}_{2}
, such that in a neighborhood of
x\in\mathbb{S}^{1}

\gamma(\theta)=\beta\rho^{\alpha}(\cos(\alpha\theta){\bf v}_{1}+\sin(\alpha\theta){\bf v}_{2}).

Noting that
\beta\rho^{\alpha}=1
by construction, we see that
\gamma
has constant speed
\alpha.
Clearly, the length of its image must be
2\pi\alpha
.
∎

Presume
X
is a building of type
W
. Let
\theta_{\Sigma}:\Sigma_{u(0)}X\to\mathbb{S}^{N-1}/W=:\Delta_{\text{mod}}
, denote the canonical anisotropy map, analogous to the natural map
\overline{\theta}:\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\to\Delta_{\text{mod}}
which quotients out by the action of
W
.
Consider the behavior of the map

\overline{\gamma}:=\theta_{\Sigma}\circ\gamma\circ t:[0,2\pi\alpha]\to\Delta_{\text{mod}},

where
t:[0,2\pi\alpha]\to[0,2\pi]
such that
t(\theta):=\theta/\alpha
. While in the interior of
\Delta_{\text{mod}}
,
\overline{\gamma}
traces out a geodesic of unit speed, and when it is incident to a wall in a transverse direction, it reflects off with angle of incidence equal to angle of return. Notice also that
\overline{\gamma}(0)=\overline{\gamma}(2\pi\alpha)
so we may consider
\overline{\gamma}:\mathbb{R}\to\Delta_{\text{mod}}
as the obvious periodic map.

We now consider a wider class of maps with the same domain, target, and incidence behavior.

Definition 4.3
.

We say that
c:\mathbb{R}\to\Delta_{\text{mod}}
is a
closed billiards path of period
\lambda>0
in
\Delta_{\text{mod}}
if:

(1)

For each
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
c
is a “unit-speed local geodesic” at
x
in the sense that there is an interval
I\ni x
and a unit speed geodesic
\widetilde{c}:I\to\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
so that
c|I=\overline{\theta}\circ\widetilde{c}
, and

(2)

c
is
\lambda
-periodic in the sense that

c(x)=c(x+\lambda)

and there does not exist
\overline{\lambda}\in(0,\lambda)
such that
c(x)=c(x+\overline{\lambda})
.

Remark 4.4
.

Condition (1) can also be phrased (slightly less generally) as the condition that
\gamma
moves along geodesics on the interior of
\Delta_{\text{mod}}
, and reflects off of the walls when it meets them.

Remark 4.5
.

A path satisfying only condition (1) of this definition may reasonably be called a
billiards path in
\Delta_{\text{mod}}
, but in fact every billiards path in
\Delta_{\text{mod}}
is closed; the distinction is relevant in more irregular spherical domains.

Because the extension of
\overline{\gamma}
is a closed billiards path which is
\frac{2\pi\alpha}{m}
-periodic for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, to classify the possible orders of harmonic maps into
(X,W)
, it suffices to classify the possible values of
\lambda
.

Lemma 4.6
.

If
c
is a closed billiards path of period
\lambda
, then there exist
j,k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\lambda=\frac{2\pi j}{k}
where
k
divides
|W|
.

Proof.

Covering the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
by
|W|
copies of
\Delta_{\text{mod}}
, we lift
c
to
(\mathbb{S}^{N-1},W)
. Formally this amounts to finding a geodesic
\overline{c}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
such that
c:=\overline{\theta}\circ\overline{c}
. Note that while many such lifts exist, prescribing
\overline{c}(0)
completely determines the lifted map.

Choose one such lift
\overline{c}
and note that because
c(0)=c(\lambda)
, there is some
w\in W
so that
\overline{c}(\lambda)=w\cdot\overline{c}(0)
. Let
\widetilde{c}(s):=\overline{c}(s+\lambda)
. Then
\widetilde{c},w\cdot\overline{c}
are both lifts of
c
and
\widetilde{c}(0)=w\cdot\overline{c}(0)
. It follows that
\widetilde{c}=w\cdot\overline{c}
and thus
w\cdot\overline{c}([0,\lambda])=\overline{c}([\lambda,2\lambda])
.

Moreover,
\overline{c}(2\lambda)=w\cdot\overline{c}(\lambda)
. Let
k
be the order of
w
. Then, repeating this process
k
times we find that
\overline{c}(k\lambda)=w^{k}\cdot\overline{c}(0)=\overline{c}(0)
. That is,
\overline{c}:[0,k\lambda]\to\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
is a closed geodesic of unit speed (with multiplicity
j
). Therefore,
k\lambda=2\pi j
where
k
divides
|W|
.
∎

We now prove Theorem
2.15
, completing the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
2.15
.

Let
u:B_{1}(0)\to X
be a nonconstant homogeneous harmonic map of order
\alpha
. By Theorem
3.1
one of the following holds:

(1)

\alpha=\frac{m}{2}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
m\geq 2
. Because
W
is a reflection group,
2
divides
|W|
, so in this case we are done.

(2)

There is a constant
c
so that for any
\phi\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
d(u(0),u(\phi))=c
.

We now consider only case (2). Assume first that
N=1
. Since the image of
u
lies in a finite cone, the image is isometric to a subset of a
k
-pod, with cone point
u(0)
. Hence, if
d(u(0),u(\phi))
is constant for
\phi\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
u(\mathbb{S}^{1})=\{p\}
for some
p\in X
. Consider
x\mapsto d^{2}(u(x),u(0))
, which vanishes on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. By
[
11
, Proposition 2.2]
the map is subharmonic and thus vanishes on
B_{1}(0)
. In particular,
u
is a constant function, contrary to our assumption.

Now suppose
N\geq 2
. By Lemma
4.2
and the discussion following,
\lambda=\frac{2\pi\alpha}{m}
. And by Lemma
4.6
,
\lambda=\frac{2\pi j}{k}
where
k
divides
|W|
. It follows that
\alpha=\frac{mj}{k}
where
k
divides
|W|
.
∎

5.
Remarks

In extending this proof to domain dimensions higher than
2
, several difficulties arise. We may still study homogeneous maps to resolve our problem, but these maps become more complicated. First, the analogous result to
3.1
will be more involved in this context, because knowing the “radial” behavior of a map
u
will no longer uniquely determine the “tangential” behavior within each apartment. Instead, for a homogeneous map
u:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to X
of order
\alpha
, we learn that
u|\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
is an eigenfunction of the spherical Laplacian with eigenvalue
-\alpha(\alpha+m-2)
in neighborhoods of regular points. Moreover, there are technical aspects to be overcome—in domain dimension
\geq 3
, it is possible for singular points of a homogeneous map
u
to exist on the boundary of the domain.

To close out this note, we discuss the case where
X
is a tree, and offer a question related to studying the possible orders of harmonic maps into trees.

In the case where
u:\Omega\to X
is a homogeneous map into a tree, by
[
19
]
,
u
maps into a
k
-pod for some
k
even in the non-locally finite setting. Consider, as we have done here, the map
v:=u|\mathbb{S}^{m-1}:\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\to X
. For each of the (open) legs of the
k
-pod,
L_{1},\dots,L_{k}
, the preimage
S_{i}:=v^{-1}(L_{i})
is an open subset of
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
, and
v(\partial S_{i})=0_{X}
for each
i
, where
0_{X}
denotes the “vertex” of the
k
-pod, the common boundary of all of the legs. Within each
S_{i}
,
v
is an eigenfunction of the spherical Laplacian with eigenvalue
-\alpha(\alpha+m-2)
. We hence arrive at the following question.

Question 5.1
.

For a fixed
m
, for which values of
\alpha\geq 1
do there exist finitely many open domains
S_{1},\dots,S_{k}
in
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
so that the following hold?

(1)

\mathbb{S}^{m-1}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\overline{S_{i}}
,

(2)

-\alpha(\alpha+m-2)
is a Dirichlet eigenvalue of the spherical Laplacian in each
S_{i}
.

In the case
m=2
, a direct analysis of this question leads to an analogous result to Theorem
3.1
—the only such
\alpha
are
\frac{m}{2}
, for natural numbers
m\geq 2
. We hence conjecture that the possible orders of harmonic maps into trees are precisely the
\alpha
answering the previous Question. This leaves open the question of how to approach more general Euclidean buildings of dimension
>1
.
