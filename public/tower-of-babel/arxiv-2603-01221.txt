Epistemic Gain, Aleatoric Cost: Uncertainty Decomposition in Multi-Agent Debate for Math Reasoning
Dan Qiao, Binbin Chen, Fengyu Cai, Jianlong Chen, Wenhao Li, Fuxin Jiang, Zuzhi Chen, Hongyuan Zha, Tieying Zhang ∗, Baoxiang Wang ∗

Abstract
Multi-Agent Debate (MAD) has shown promise in leveraging collective intelligence to improve reasoning and reduce hallucinations, yet it remains unclear how information exchange shapes the underlying ability. Empirically, MAD exhibits paradoxical phenomena, such as accuracy improvement accompanied by substantial increase in token entropy, and remarkable divergence between homogeneous and heterogeneous model combinations. In this paper, we propose a Bayesian uncertainty analysis framework for MAD, which decomposes total predictive uncertainty into epistemic uncertainty reducible by debate context and aleatoric uncertainty induced by internal model noise. Across multiple model configurations, we find that effective debate hinges on achieving high epistemic gain under controlled aleatoric cost. Building on this insight, we design an uncertainty-guided multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) algorithm that explicitly optimizes aleatoric noise reduction and epistemic information utilization. Experiments show that our training significantly improves post-debate accuracy and stability, and enhances individual reasoning beyond single-agent RL, providing a unified Bayesian uncertainty perspective for understanding and improving MAD.

Introduction

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable general-purpose capabilities, yet these models remain susceptible to hallucinations and brittle reasoning in complex scenarios such as math problems. Inspired by human argumentation, Multi-Agent Debate (MAD) has emerged as a prominent inference-time paradigm to achieve more reliable responses by integrating perspectives from multiple source LLMs
(
11
)
. Specifically, MAD constructs input for each round by orchestrating task roles, prompt strategies, or communication topologies across multiple LLMs, and aggregates the final responses through major voting or the LLM-as-a-Judge approach. Previous studies have shown that iterative debate can elicit a "wisdom of crowds" effect
(
40
)
, which induces higher-level reasoning abilities such as verification and self-correction.

However, the underlying mechanism driving these performance gains remains largely opaque. While aggregated outcomes show improvement, the validity of the iterative debate process is increasingly being scrutinized. In
8
, authors removes the major voting process and finds that the mean accuracy of homogeneous agents debate often stagnates or even degrades across debate rounds, which implies that the effectiveness of MAD mainly stems from the aggregation process. Furthermore,
60
have highlighted a concerning frequency of correct answers flipping to incorrect ones during debate, suggesting that LLMs may be driven more by social conformity or sycophantic behavior
(
48
)
than by logical deduction.
69
have also pointed out that the inherent sycophancy of LLMs can hinder the development of positive disagreements into positive consensus during debates, which makes it impossible to effectively utilize constructive disagreements to improve reasoning. These findings indicate that the debate process may not be effective in genuine knowledge discovery and opinion utilization, presenting a critical bottleneck for the field
(
56
)
.

In this paper, to understand how information exchange in MAD fundamentally shapes the LLM reasoning ability, we focus on uncertainty quantification
(
26
)
of model’s predictive responses under different debate turns and input contexts with math reasoning problems. As shown in Fig.
1
, the accuracy rapidly increases and saturates as the debate progresses, accompanied by a significant increase in token entropy, which indicates remarkable changes in model uncertainty. Specifically, we employ epistemic uncertainty to quantify the robustness of an agent’s solution concepts beliefs about problems, and aleatoric uncertainty to capture the inherent decoding noise of the reasoning paths. In particular, we remove the final aggregation stage of MAD such as majority voting
(
11
)
or weighted voting
(
7
)
to focus purely on the belief evolution of individual agents. This framework allows us to move beyond simple accuracy metrics and rigorously characterize the dynamics of information flow—particularly within heterogeneous agent setups—by analyzing the drift of probability distributions and entropy shifts throughout the debate trajectory.

Empirical results across homogeneous and heterogeneous MAD settings have demonstrated that the effectiveness of a debate process is often influenced by two factors: high epistemic uncertainty in the initial state and effective control of aleatoric uncertainty during the debate. The former represents the potential for cognitive conflict and knowledge exchange gain, while the latter reflects the cost of debate in utilizing external knowledge and reasoning stability. This implies that superficial interventions on frozen black-box models are insufficient to fundamentally regulate these internal uncertainty dynamics. To address this challenge, we present a multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) method to explicitly optimize external knowledge utilization from heterogeneous models and reduce the internal reasoning uncertainty. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose a Bayesian uncertainty analysis framework for MAD, which rigorously decomposes total predictive uncertainty into epistemic uncertainty and aleatoric uncertainty, providing a theoretical perspective for understanding the debate dynamics.

•

Through extensive evaluation of homogeneous and heterogeneous MAD, we reveal that the efficacy of current inference-time MAD is constrained by the uncertainty trade-off between epistemic gain and uncontrolled aleatoric costs.

•

We develope an uncertainty-guided MARL algorithm to train models using reward signals to learn efficient debate and information utilization. Experimental results demonstrate that our method not only significantly improves post-debate accuracy but also outperforms the single-agent RL baseline model in independent reasoning. This suggests that the model has learned to robustly handle uncertainty in reasoning and improve the fundamental reasoning ability even without the aggregation gain of majority voting.

2.1
Multi Agent Debate

Following the most standard MAD settings
(
11
;
45
)
, we formulate MAD as a
T
-steps iterative generation process among
N
agents, where the agents are parameterized by LLM parameters
\{\pi_{\theta_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. For a given problem
x\sim\mathcal{D}
sampled from the task dataset, there exists a ground truth answer as
y^{*}
. For math reasoning tasks, the reasoning process can be diverse, but the answer is usually numerically unique and can often be precisely verified through string matching against ground truth answer

1

With a slight abuse of notation, we use
y
to represent both the full responses and the extracted final answers throughout the paper.
. At each round
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, agent

y_{i,t}
conditioned on the original problem
x

c_{i,t}
as
y_{i,t}\sim\pi_{\theta_{i}}(\cdot\mid x,c_{i,t})
, where the context includes the reference solutions from other agents and additional debate instructions. In the first round, the models usually answers the original question
x

c_{i,0}

y_{i,t}
with
L

i

\pi_{\theta_{i}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,c_{i,t})=\prod_{l=1}^{L}\pi_{\theta_{i}}(y_{l}\mid x,c_{i,t},y_{<l}).

The response usually contains intermediate reasoning steps before generating the final answer, which may be enclosed with special symbols
<think>
in the reasoning models such as OpenAI o1 and DeepSeek-R1
(
42
;
16
)
. Although there are various information schemes for MAD prompts
(
29
;
37
;
7
)
, we define a uniform context mapping function
\Phi
that constructs the input context for each agent based on the global history of peer responses from the previous rounds as

c_{i,t}=\Phi_{i}(\{Y_{k}\}_{k=1}^{t-1},\mathcal{G},\mathcal{P}),

where
Y_{k}=\{y_{1,k},\dots,y_{N,k}\}
represent all agents’ responses in debate turn
k
, communication topology
\mathcal{G}
determines the range of peer agents responses that agent
i
can access (e.g., fully connected graphs and directed graphs). Agent role prompts
\mathcal{P}
determines how each agent utilizes and processes peer agents responses, such as concatenation, introducing additional LLM for summarization, and specific role instructions.

At the final turn

\hat{y}=\Psi(Y_{T})
will be distilled from all agents responses
Y_{T}
by an aggregation function
\Psi

)
, Elo
(
75
)
, and LLM-as-a-Judge
(
77
)
.

In this work, we focus on analyzing the basic reasoning capabilities of the agent, rather than optimizing the MAD workflow to improve the final accuracy. Therefore, we adopt the most general MAD setting with a fully-connected communication graph
\mathcal{G}
containing the responses
Y_{t-1}
of all agents from the previous round
t-1
, without specialized role-playing or information summarization. Furthermore, we remove the final aggregation step
\Psi
during our experiments to directly capture the evolution of each agents’ individual response
y_{i,T}
. This allows us to isolate the impact of information exchange on intrinsic reasoning capability.

2.2
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has emerged as a powerful paradigm, which leverages rule-based verifiable binary sparse reward signals to stimulate the high-order complex reasoning capabilities of LLMs by RL training. Recent advances builds upon Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
47
)
, a specialized RL algorithm for reasoning tasks that eliminates the need for training a critic model. In the standard single-agent setting, for each query
x
, the LLM policy
\pi_{\theta}
samples a group of
G
responses
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{G}\}
. For each response
y_{i}
, a reward function
R(y_{i},y^{*})
evaluates the final answer and provide the binary rewards, which is
R=1
if the answer correct and
R=0
otherwise.

Then the GRPO objective is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\Bigl[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\frac{1}{\lvert y_{i}\rvert}\sum_{j=1}^{\lvert y_{i}\rvert}\Bigl(\min\Bigl(r_{i,j}(\theta)A_{i,j},

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\operatorname{clip}(r_{i,j},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_{i,j}\Bigr)-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\Bigr)\Bigr],

(1)

where
r_{i,j}=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,j}|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_{i,j}|x)}
represents the token-level ratio,
A_{i,j}=\frac{R_{i}-\text{mean}(\{R\}_{i=1}^{G})}{\text{std}(\{R\}_{i=1}^{G})}
represents the group-normalized advantage, and
\beta
controls the KL divergence penalty. This critic-free method can effectively stabilize the training of LLM inference and is widely used in math, code, and agentic training tasks
(
72
;
59
;
21
)
. In our work, we extend vanilla GRPO into multi agent scenarios by treating the debate process as a learnable multi-agent multi-step decision process and optimizing
\pi_{\theta_{i}}
with dense rewards to maximize the team accuracy as shown in Section
4.1
.

3
Uncertainty Mechanism in MAD

In this section, we analyze the dynamic behaviors of multi-agent debate (MAD) through an uncertainty decomposition lens. In Section
3.1
, we formalize multi-turn debate as an iterative Bayesian update process, where agents exchange information as noisy evidence to shape latent beliefs about math reasoning and refined responses. In Section
3.2
, we decompose the system-level uncertainty and identify an epistemic-gain versus aleatoric-cost tradeoff, supported by extensive experiments under both homogeneous and heterogeneous debate settings. Furthermore, in Section
3.3
, we investigate how changing context
c_{i,t}
affects the uncertainty of individual agents, and provide theoretical analysis that heterogeneous debates can yield larger epistemic gains than homogeneous debates.

3.1
Iterative Bayesian Belief Update of MAD

Motivated by recent research about LLMs as implicit Bayesian inference process
(
20
;
62
;
8
)
, we formalize agent
i
’s response generation as a hierarchical sampling process to isolate the latent beliefs
\varphi_{i}
from the observed text responses
y_{i,t}
. Latent belief variable
\varphi_{i}
represents the agent’s
implicit
belief about the given problem
x
over the potential math problems solution space given the debate context
c_{i,t}
. This can reflect the agent’s current knowledge-based understanding of the problem, such as mathematical theorems, analytical methods, and problem-solving logic.
The observed text response
y_{i,t}
is then obtained by autoregressive sampling in the vocabulary space by the LLM
\pi_{\theta_{i}}
, which can be considered as being driven by the latent belief
\varphi_{i}
as
y_{i,t}\sim p(y\mid\varphi_{i})
. Since the LLM parameters
\theta_{i}
is frozen in MAD and can be omitted in the notations, the posterior predictive distribution over responses is obtained by marginalizing out the latent belief as

p(y_{i,t}\mid x,c_{i,t})=\int p(y_{i,t}\mid\varphi_{i})\,p(\varphi_{i}\mid x,c_{i,t})\,d\varphi_{i}.

Therefore, the process of updating responses through multiple rounds of debate in MAD can be understood as an iterative Bayesian belief update process. By adding additional reference solutions to change the context
c_{i,t}
between rounds, the agent’s latent belief
p(\varphi_{i}\mid x,c_{i,t})
is implicitly updated, which in turn changes the posterior prediction distribution
p(y_{i,t}\mid x,c_{i,t})
and may change the final answer. For example, compared with the first turn initial responses
y_{i,0}
, the updated belief at turn
t
can be modeled as the Bayesian update over the latent variable as

p(\varphi_{i}\mid x,c_{i,t})\ \propto\ p(c_{i,t}\mid\varphi_{i},x)\;p(\varphi_{i}\mid x),

(2)

where
p(c_{i,t}\mid\varphi_{i},x)
represents the implicit likelihood of the peer agents’ reference solutions given the agent’s latent belief state under context
c_{i,t}
. Intuitively, the effective debate contexts can shift the agent’s posterior belief towards the ground truth and thus improve the predictive accuracy with better posterior
p(y_{i,t}\mid x,c_{i,t})
.

Unlike multiple-choice questions with limited options, math reasoning problems may often have an infinite space of answers. Considering that the ground-truth answer is usually unique up to an equivalence relation, we can simplify the belief space with binary correctness event for agent
i
at turn
t
as
h_{i,t}:=\mathbf{1}[y_{i,t}\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}]\in\{0,1\}
, where
\mathcal{Y}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
denotes the set of responses equivalent to the ground-truth answer.
Accordingly, the predictive belief of correctness is

p(h_{i,t}=1\mid x,c_{i,t})=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}}p(y_{i,t}=y\mid x,c_{i,t}).

Then we have the following lemma regarding the correctness of agent
i
’s responses at turn
t
as

Lemma 3.1

(Noisy Debate Accuracy Update)
.

Given an input question
x
, assuming that the initial context is empty and debate context
c_{t}
is valid as
p(c_{t}\mid x)>0
, the log-odds of the binary hypothesis
h\in\{0,1\}
after debate can be written as

\log\frac{p(h=1\mid x,c_{t})}{p(h=0\mid x,c_{t})}=\log\frac{p(h=1\mid x)}{p(h=0\mid x)}+\log\frac{p(c_{t}\mid h=1,x)}{p(c_{t}\mid h=0,x)}.

The derivation of Lemma
3.1
is based on the MAD Bayeisan belief update equation (
2
), whereas the more detailed proof can be found in Appendix
A.1
.

It is noted that the additive term
\log\frac{p(c_{t}\mid h=1,x)}{p(c_{t}\mid h=0,x)}
is the
log Bayes factor
induced by the debate context
c_{t}
. If it is positive, it means that the debate context provides effective evidence to improve the response correctness and may induce a flip from wrong to correct (
W\to C
). Conversely, the debate context
c_{t}
is more likely to be induced by incorrect beliefs and will deviate the initial belief away from ground truth, thus leading negative flip from correct to wrong (
C\to W
). This provides a theoretical perspective on the stagnation of accuracy and the deterioration of a large number of correct answers observed in many existing MAD works
(
8
;
60
;
69
)
. Surprisingly, this means that MAD may not guarantee the full utilization of correct cues in the debate contexts: even if the solutions provided by peers contain useful information, internal beliefs can still be misled and generate incorrect responses.

3.2
System-Level Uncertainty Trade-off in MAD

Quantifying the uncertainty of large models is crucial for understanding the reliability of the responses. However, due to the large scale of LLM parameters and high-dimensional generation space, traditional uncertainty estimation methods based on deep Bayes networks or dropout inference
(
26
;
28
)
are impractical to LLM-based reasoning process. Moreover, the implicit latent belief state
\varphi_{i}
is also computational intractable for large LLMs
(
1
;
20
)
. To address the above limitations, we consider quantifying and decomposing the uncertainty of the MAD system based on the answer-level metrics using the predictive distribution of multiple agents.

The core intuition is that the MAD system can be viewed as a natural ensemble of multiple predictive experts. Each agent in MAD provides distinct prediction
y_{i}
about the answer under the same question
x
and debate contexts
c
, where the homogeneous models can be distinct by different seeds and the heterogeneous models are pre-trained independently. More specifically, instead of assuming these responses as exact posterior samples from a joint distribution, we adopt an ensemble-of-experts proxy by mixing the answer-level distributions of each agent, where the system’s collective predictive distribution is thus approximated by a mixture model as

p_{\text{sys}}(y_{t}=Y\mid x,C_{t}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p(y_{i,t}=Y\mid x,c_{i,t}),

This ensemble proxy also aligns with Bayesian belief update under the mild assumption that each agent’s distribution
p_{i}(y)
can be viewed as an expert corresponding to implicit sampling, such that the cross-expert average approximates the Bayesian model average. Therefore, the mixture distribution
p_{\text{sys}}
can be interpreted as an approximate posterior prediction distribution. Then we can quantify the total uncertainty (TU) as the entropy of the collective predictive distribution
\mathbb{H}[p_{\text{sys}}(y^{*}\mid x,C_{t})]
. By definition of generalized Jensen-Shannon divergence, we can decompose the TU of MAD into epistemic uncertainty (EU) and aleatoric uncertainty (AU) as follows.

Proposition 3.2

(System-level Uncertainty Decomposition)
.

Let
\{p_{i,t}(y)\}_{i=1}^{N}
be agents’ answer-level predictive distributions at turn
t
, and

p_{\text{sys}}(y)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i,t}(y)
be the system mixture distribution. We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{TU}(t)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathrm{JSD}(p_{1,t},\ldots,p_{N,t})}_{\mathrm{Sys\mbox{-}EU}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(p_{i,t})}_{\mathrm{Sys\mbox{-}AU}},

(3)

where the system epistemic uncertainty (Sys-EU) is defined as
\mathrm{Sys\mbox{-}EU}(t):=\mathrm{JSD}(p_{1,t},\ldots,p_{N,t})=H(p_{sys}(y))-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(p_{i,t})
, and the system aleatoric uncertainty (Sys-AU) is defined as
\mathrm{Sys\mbox{-}AU}(t):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(p_{i,t})
.

This enables a simple and scalable decomposition of system-level uncertainty, where the Sys-EU represents the reducible cognitive uncertainty between agents responses, while the Sys-AU represents the entropy within the agents induced by decoding randomnesses and computational instability
(
14
;
34
)
. It is noted that in math reasoning tasks with deterministic answers, the questions and data often lack randomness, which is the classic source of accidental uncertainty. In this paper, we mainly define Sys-AU as the randomness of the LLM decoding process, including the generation randomness introduced by long additional contexts and potentially out-of-distribution prompts and accidental sampling errors in numerical computation.

To validate this theoretical decomposition, we evaluate both homogeneous and heterogeneous MAD configurations on a subset of MATH
(
17
)
dataset with
N=2
agents,
T=5
debate rounds, and
G=16
rollouts for every prompt. More experiments details refer to Appendix
C.5
. In Figure
6
, we present representative debate combinations under homogeneous and heterogeneous settings. Across all settings, Sys-EU consistently decreases with debate turns, which indicates that MAD acts as a consensus-seeking process regardless of the correctness. In contrast, performance differences are primarily explained by the trade-off between both uncertainties. Successful debates can keep Sys-AU low or decreasing, while failure cases usually exhibit large and explosive growing Sys-AU that overwhelms the benefit of disagreement reduction. In addition, heterogeneous MAD with one common participant typically start with higher initial Sys-EU than homogeneous pairs, which reveals that the larger initial cognitive gap can bring greater potential cognitive gains. However, these benefits can only be realized if utilization costs Sys-AU are controlled.

3.3
Epistemic Advantage of Heterogeneous MAD

In the previous section
3.2
, the system-level uncertainty analysis have summarized that debate accuracy is jointly affected by epistemic knowledge gain and internal aleatoric cost, yet insufficient to explain how the specific agent response changes. Therefore, we analyze the uncertainty differences of a single agent with homogeneous peers and heterogeneous peers. Similar to Proposition
3.2
, we quantify the uncertainty of the agent’s prediction with answer distributions. By applying the law of total entropy
(
26
)
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}[p_{i}(y_{i,t}\mid x,c_{i,t})]=-\sum_{y_{i,t}}p(y_{i,t}\mid x,c_{i,t})\log p(y_{i,t}\mid x,c_{i,t})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\varphi_{i}\sim p(\varphi_{i}\mid x,c_{i,t})}[\mathbb{H}(p(y_{i,t}\mid\varphi_{i},x,c_{i,t})]}_{\text{Aleatoric Uncertainty}}+\underbrace{I(y_{i,t};\varphi_{i}\mid x,c_{i,t})}_{\text{Epistemic Uncertainty}}.

The epistemic term represents the mutual information between the prediction and the latent belief, which can be rewritten as the expected Kullback-Leibler divergence between the conditional and marginal distributions as

I(y_{i,t};\varphi_{i})=\mathbb{E}_{\varphi_{i}}\left[D_{KL}\left(p(y_{i,t}\mid x,\varphi_{i})\parallel p(y_{i,t}\mid x,c_{i,t})\right)\right].

In the context of debate, high EU values indicate that the current context
c_{i,t}
can significantly influence reasoning and deviate the output prediction from the model’s original internal beliefs, such as by introducing low-probability solution methods or correcting high-probability illusion errors. The ideal goal of debate is to provide key information in
c_{i,t}
to guide the model’s beliefs toward a distribution that has a high probability of producing the ground truth answer.

Therefore, a successful debate step should reduce EU by incorporating new evidence, which can be defined as the Epistemic Gain provided by the additional peer message
m
as
G_{\text{epi}}(m):=I(\varphi;m\mid x,c)
.

Theorem 3.3

(Heterogeneous evidence yields larger epistemic gain)
.

Given an input question
x
and a current context
c
, considering that
m_{\text{homo}}
is the peer message from a homogeneous model and
m_{\text{hetero}}
is the peer message from a heterogeneous model.
Assume the
heterogeneous novelty condition
:

I(\varphi;m_{\text{hetero}}\mid x,c,m_{\text{homo}})\geq I(\varphi;m^{\prime}_{\text{homo}}\mid x,c,m_{\text{homo}}),

where
m^{\prime}_{\text{homo}}
is another independent homogeneous sample.
Then the heterogeneous message provides strictly larger epistemic gain:

G_{\text{epi}}(m_{\text{hetero}})\geq G_{\text{epi}}(m_{\text{homo}}).

Theorem
3.3
provides a Bayesian uncertainty perspective to explain why heterogeneous debates are generally superior to homogeneous debates. Intuitively, heterogeneous partners can provide complementary evidence beyond the inference scope of homogeneous models, offering a greater potential to alter the distribution of underlying beliefs. For homogeneous models, even if sampled responses differ with different prompts or seeds, they often stem from similar latent belief variables, which reinforce existing beliefs after debate and manifesting as a tend for consensus rather than correctness
(
60
)
. This theoretical insight aligns with empirical observations in section
3.2
: heterogeneous debate typically can produce more diverse answers and achieve higher accuracies through effective debate processes, while homogeneous debate often cannot exceed the upper limit of single agent reasoning
(
73
)
.

4
Uncertainty-Guided MARL for MAD

Based on the analysis in Section
3
, the debate process in MAD is primarily influenced by the knowledge gains from irreducible epistemic uncertainty and the cost of unstable answers due to aleatoric uncertainty. Therefore, we model the MAD process as a multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) problem to fundamentally alter the distribution of internal beliefs to achieve effective debate capabilities. Our method, named
Uncertainty-Guided Multi-Agent Debate
(UMAD) Reinforcement Learning, optimizes the agent’s policy using verifiable rewards of responses, enabling it to simultaneously calibrate internal confidence to handle aleatoric uncertainty and leverage epistemic uncertainty to effectively influence other agents.

4.1
MAD as a Dec-POMDP

Motivated by recent works on multi-LLMs cooperative learning
(
45
;
32
;
54
)
, we formulate the multi-agent debate reinforcement learning as a decentralized partially observable Markov decision process (Dec-POMDP)
(
41
)
defined by the tuple
\langle\mathcal{N},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,R,\Omega,\mathcal{O},\gamma\rangle
.

Specifically, at each round
t
, each agent
i\in{1,\dots,N}
observes the debate context
o_{i,t}=x\oplus c_{i,t}
including the query
x
and debate contexts as the local observation of the overall debate history states
\mathcal{S}
. The response sequences of LLM
i
is the agent
i
’s action at step
t
, which contains intermediate reasoning steps and final answers. To induce collaborative improvement, we employ a dense team reward
R_{t}=\sum_{i}r(a_{i,t},y^{*})
, where the individual reward can be verified easily at every step. Considering the complexity and high computational requirements of training LLMs, we adopt an independent learning paradigm as the algorithm backbone, where each agent maximizes the expected joint return. However, standard independent learning often suffers from non-stationarity and coarse credit assignment in MAD. We address these using a two-pronged uncertainty-guided mechanism.

4.2
Uncertainty-Guided Optimization Mechanisms

Based on the uncertainty trade-off identified in Section
3
, we introduce two regulation mechanisms that encourage epistemic gains and reduce aleatoric costs during policy optimization.

Aleatoric Uncertainty-Aware Advantage.
Agents in debate often exhibit miscalibrated confidence, manifesting as "stubborn hallucinations" or "hesitant reasoning." To regulate aleatoric noise, we employ a confidence-aware advantage shaping mechanism. Motivated by
(
61
)
, we utilize the token-level negative log-probability
\hat{\mathcal{U}}i
as a proxy for generation uncertainty to modulate the standard advantage
A{i,t}
:

A^{\text{au}}_{i,t}=W(\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{i})\cdot A_{i,t},

(4)

where
\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{i}=-\frac{1}{|y_{i,t}|}\sum_{j=1}^{|y_{i,t}|}\log\pi_{\theta_{i}}(y_{i,t,j}|x,c_{i,t},y_{i,t,<j})
represents the token mean negative log-probs. The weight function is defined as:

W(\bar{\mathcal{U}}i)=\exp(-\alpha_{au}\cdot\bar{\mathcal{U}}i)

where
\bar{\mathcal{U}}_{i}=\frac{\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{i}-\mu_{\mathcal{G}}}{\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}+\epsilon}
represents the group-standardized uncertainty,
\alpha_{au}
is a hyperparameter. This mechanism is designed to amplify the reward for high-confidence correct responses while intensifying the penalty for high-confidence incorrect ones. Conversely, it suppresses the advantage of responses with high relative uncertainty (
\bar{\mathcal{U}}_{i}>0
), effectively anchoring the model’s reasoning against aleatoric noise. This explicitly incentivizes the model to suppress entropy when correct, effectively anchoring the correct reasoning paths against context noise.

Epistemic Influence Intrinsic Reward.
To maximize the epistemic gain
G_{\text{epi}}
, effective debate requires agents to not only be correct but also persuasive. The core intuition is that the same context can have different effects on LLMs with different belief likelihoods. Therefore, in addition to correctness rewards, we need to incentive samples that can be more effectively utilized by others. We introduce an intrinsic reward
r^{\text{eu}}_{i,t}
that quantifies the positive information gain provided to peers:

r^{\text{eu}}_{i,t}=\frac{\eta}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\Delta R_{\text{peer}}(j,t+1),

(5)

where
\Delta R_{\text{peer}}
denotes the average correctness improvements of multiple rollout trajectories in GRPO with agent

Multi Agent Debate.
Prior works in MAD

in reasoning tasks. Most of these works focus on workflow design for specific problems

(

12

(

However, the efficacy of these inference-time interventions is increasingly challenged due to the susceptibility of LLMs to sycophancy and long contexts drift

(
60

Unlike existing methods that treat the model as a frozen black box and rely on textual confidence representations, we propose a method for numerically quantifying uncertainty in the MAD process. Furthermore, unlike existing methods that focus on adjusting rules in the prompt and aggregation stages, we train MAD using correctness reward signals, fundamentally reshaping the agent’s belief updates and debating behavior, thereby enabling robust reasoning under uncertainty.

Several concurrent studies have explored the diversity and uncertainty of MAD from various perspectives.
78
attribute the failures of MAD to low initial diversity and uncalibrated confidence, proposing a textual-confidence-modulated protocol to mitigate sycophancy.

67

While these studies collectively highlight the critical roles of diversity and uncertainty, they primarily rely on inference-time textual heuristics or output-level observations. In contrast, our work formalizes the debate process as an uncertainty-tradeoff-driven Bayesian belief update process and employs MARL to fundamentally reshape agents’ debating behaviors and reasoning strategies. These collective findings provide strong empirical and theoretical support for our conclusions regarding the necessity of modeling internal uncertainty to unlock the full potential of multi-agent reasoning.

Uncertainty in LLM.
Uncertainty quantification is pivotal for assessing the trustworthiness of LLMs
(
19
)
. Traditional approaches utilizing token-level probabilities often fail to capture semantic meaning, leading to the development of semantic-level metrics
(
25
;
55
)
. By clustering semantically equivalent responses, methods like semantic entropy provide a more robust estimation of model confidence against linguistic variations
(
25
)
. More recently, researchers have adopted a Bayesian perspective to decompose predictive uncertainty into aleatoric uncertainty, arising from inherent data ambiguity, and epistemic uncertainty, reflecting the model’s lack of knowledge
(
38
;
18
;
15
)
. This decomposition has been extensively explored to understand the sources of prediction error such as general Q&A tasks
(
1

and in-context learning
(
20

34

(
22
;
53

. Other works have further leveraged multi-agent perturbations
(
3
)
or fine-tuning
(
14
)
to better elicit and calibrate these signals. Despite these advantages, existing uncertainty quantification research in LLMs mainly focuses on single agent tasks and textualized quantification in multi-agent tasks. To our best knowledge, this work is the first to utilize internal model indicators to formalize the uncertainty mechanism of MAD and uses the uncertainty-driven MARL algorithm to improve MAD’s resistance to uncertainty interference.

Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning in LLMs.

With the advantage of LLM-based multi-agent systems, research has transitioned from heuristic-based workflow design toward optimizable learning paradigms. Initial explorations often focused on interaction-driven data generation or specific task-oriented roles. For instance, SPIRAL
(
35
)
demonstrated that reasoning capabilities could emerge from self-play in zero-sum games, though its primary focus remained on action-based tasks rather than pure mathematical reasoning. To enhance diversity, Multi-Agent Finetuning (MAFT)
(
51
)
utilized multi-agent interactions to source diverse reasoning chains, focusing primarily on supervised finetuning (SFT) rather than explicit reinforcement learning. ReMA
(
54
)
introduced a hierarchical architecture by alternating between a "Meta-thinking Agent" and a "Reasoning Agent" to decouple strategic oversight from execution.

More recent studies have adopted MARL perspective to address the reasoning ability within multi-agent debate framework. MAPoRL
(
45
)
was a pioneering effort that formalized the debate process as a MARL problem, employing an IPPO-style
(
10
)
training approach to reward persuasive and corrective behaviors. To ensure stable policy updates, MARFT
(
32
)
proposed LaMAS framework to ensure the monotonic policy improvement property motivated by HAPPO
(
24
)
, specifically tailored for LLM-based agents. Algorithmic innovations have also focused on credit assignment and group dynamics. MAGRPO
(
36
)
introduced a centralized team reward style MARL method based on MAPPO
(
71
)
, while AT-GRPO
(
76
)
implemented agent and turn-aware grouping to handle the non-stationarity of multi-turn dialogues. Further research has explored consensus-based alignment in MACA
(
46
)
and meta-policy deliberation in MPDF
(
66
)
. To facilitate these intensive training requirements, frameworks like MARTI
(
74
)
have provided scalable infrastructure for asynchronous multi-agent rollouts.

However, most of these studies are limited to homogeneous model settings and primarily rely on explicit role-prompting to pre-allocate task roles or induce thinking diversity. In contrast, our research focuses on the uncertainty decomposition mechanism during the debate process. By utilizing epistemic intrinsic rewards to drive effective heterogeneous knowledge exchange and aleatoric gain to encourage effective exploration, we leverage MARL to fundamentally improve both the reasoning capabilities and the overall effectiveness of multi-agent debates.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we address the inherent instability and inefficiency of Multi-Agent Debate, particularly in heterogeneous settings, through a Bayesian uncertainty lens. We identify that performance degradation stems from uncontrolled aleatoric noise and the failure to effectively utilize epistemic disagreement. To resolve this, we propose an uncertainty-guided MARL framework that actively regulates internal reasoning confidence and incentivizes constructive information exchange. Our approach enables robust performance gains and prevents belief collapse in long-turn debates, transforming MAD from a fragile inference-time heuristic into a stable, learnable mechanism for reliable reasoning.

8
Limitations and Future Works

Our UMAD framework relies on multi-round iterative interactions between multiple agents. While we demonstrate that training over short debate turns (T=2) can generalize to longer debate turns (T=5), the inference cost is approximately linear with
N\cdot T
. Future work could explore intervening in beliefs from a reasoning perspective to strike a balance between accuracy and efficiency. Besides, we employ an expert ensemble approach to approximate the Bayesian posterior distribution and use negative log likelihood (NLL) as a proxy for aleatoric uncertainty. While this approach is computationally efficient, token-level NLL are not always perfectly correlated with semantic uncertainty, especially for poorly calibrated models. Further research into more rigorous uncertainty quantification methods, such as semantic clustering of generated paths or variational inference, could improve the theoretical framework analysis.

Impact Statement

This work aims to enhance the reliability of Large Language Models by grounding multi-agent debate in a rigorous Bayesian framework. By mitigating confident hallucinations, our method (UMAD) supports the deployment of trusted AI in critical fields like mathematics and science. However, we acknowledge that explicitly optimizing agents for "persuasiveness" carries potential risks; if applied to subjective or sensitive domains, this mechanism could theoretically be misused to generate convincing but manipulative content. Future research should ensure such capabilities remain strictly aligned with factual verification. Additionally, we address the environmental footprint of multi-agent systems by demonstrating that efficient, short-horizon training generalizes effectively to long-context inference.

A.1
Proof of Lemma
3.1
(Noisy-evidence Log-odds Update)

Lemma Statement.

Assume
p(h\mid x,c\oplus m)=p(h\mid x,c,m)
and
p(m\mid x,c)>0
. Then:

\log\frac{p(h=1\mid x,c\oplus m)}{p(h=0\mid x,c\oplus m)}=\log\frac{p(h=1\mid x,c)}{p(h=0\mid x,c)}+\log\frac{p(m\mid h=1,x,c)}{p(m\mid h=0,x,c)}.

Proof.

Recall Bayes’ theorem:

p(h\mid x,c,m)=\frac{p(m\mid h,x,c)\cdot p(h\mid x,c)}{p(m\mid x,c)}.

(6)

Writing this for
h=1
and
h=0
separately:

\displaystyle p(h=1\mid x,c,m)

\displaystyle=\frac{p(m\mid h=1,x,c)\cdot p(h=1\mid x,c)}{p(m\mid x,c)},

(7)

\displaystyle p(h=0\mid x,c,m)

\displaystyle=\frac{p(m\mid h=0,x,c)\cdot p(h=0\mid x,c)}{p(m\mid x,c)}.

(8)

Dividing the first equation by the second cancels out the marginal likelihood
p(m\mid x,c)
:

\frac{p(h=1\mid x,c,m)}{p(h=0\mid x,c,m)}=\frac{p(h=1\mid x,c)}{p(h=0\mid x,c)}\cdot\frac{p(m\mid h=1,x,c)}{p(m\mid h=0,x,c)}.

(9)

Taking the natural logarithm on both sides yields the additive log-odds update:

\log\frac{p(h=1\mid x,c\oplus m)}{p(h=0\mid x,c\oplus m)}=\log\frac{p(h=1\mid x,c)}{p(h=0\mid x,c)}+\log\frac{p(m\mid h=1,x,c)}{p(m\mid h=0,x,c)}.

(10)

This concludes the proof.
∎

A.2
Proof of Proposition
3.2
(System-level Decomposition)

Proposition Statement.

Let
\mathrm{TU}(t):=H(\bar{p}_{t})
. Then
\mathrm{TU}(t)=\mathrm{Sys\mbox{-}EU}(t)+\mathrm{Sys\mbox{-}Cost}(t)
, where
\mathrm{Sys\mbox{-}EU}(t)=\mathrm{JSD}(p_{1,t},\dots,p_{N,t})
and
\mathrm{Sys\mbox{-}Cost}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}H(p_{i,t})
.

Proof.

This follows directly from the definition of the Generalized Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD).
For a set of distributions
\{p_{1},\dots,p_{N}\}
and weights
\pi_{1}=\dots=\pi_{N}=\frac{1}{N}
, the JSD is defined as:

\mathrm{JSD}(p_{1},\dots,p_{N}):=H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}p_{i}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}H(p_{i}).

(11)

Substituting our notation
\bar{p}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}p_{i,t}
:

\mathrm{Sys\mbox{-}EU}(t)=H(\bar{p}_{t})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(p_{i,t}).

(12)

Rearranging the terms:

H(\bar{p}_{t})=\mathrm{Sys\mbox{-}EU}(t)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(p_{i,t}).

(13)

By definition,
\mathrm{TU}(t)=H(\bar{p}_{t})
and
\mathrm{Sys\mbox{-}Cost}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}H(p_{i,t})
, which proves the identity.
∎

A.3
Proof of Theorem
3.3
(Heterogeneous Epistemic Gain)

Theorem Statement.

If
I(\varphi;m_{\text{hetero}}\mid x,c,m_{\text{homo}})\geq I(\varphi;m^{\prime}_{\text{homo}}\mid x,c,m_{\text{homo}})
, then
G_{\text{epi}}(m_{\text{hetero}})\geq G_{\text{epi}}(m_{\text{homo}})
.

Proof.

We analyze the epistemic gain
G_{\text{epi}}(m):=I(\varphi;m\mid x,c)
. We consider the scenario where the agent has already internally generated (or has access to) a homogeneous-type response
m_{\text{homo}}
.
Using the chain rule of mutual information, the joint information provided by two messages
m_{1},m_{2}
is:

I(\varphi;m_{1},m_{2}\mid x,c)=I(\varphi;m_{1}\mid x,c)+I(\varphi;m_{2}\mid x,c,m_{1}).

(14)

Case 1: Homogeneous Pair (
A,A^{\prime}
).
Let
m_{1}=m_{\text{homo}}
and
m_{2}=m^{\prime}_{\text{homo}}
(an independent sample from the same distribution).

I_{\text{total}}^{\text{homo}}=I(\varphi;m_{\text{homo}}\mid x,c)+I(\varphi;m^{\prime}_{\text{homo}}\mid x,c,m_{\text{homo}}).

(15)

Case 2: Heterogeneous Pair (
A,B
).
Let
m_{1}=m_{\text{homo}}
and
m_{2}=m_{\text{hetero}}
.

I_{\text{total}}^{\text{hetero}}=I(\varphi;m_{\text{homo}}\mid x,c)+I(\varphi;m_{\text{hetero}}\mid x,c,m_{\text{homo}}).

(16)

Subtracting the two equations:

I_{\text{total}}^{\text{hetero}}-I_{\text{total}}^{\text{homo}}=I(\varphi;m_{\text{hetero}}\mid\dots)-I(\varphi;m^{\prime}_{\text{homo}}\mid\dots).

(17)

Under the
heterogeneous novelty condition
assumed in the theorem (that the heterogeneous message provides more conditional information given the homogeneous baseline), the RHS is non-negative.
Thus, the total information gain (and consequently the reduction in epistemic uncertainty) is greater in the heterogeneous setting.
∎

B.1
Uncertainty Quantification of Multi Agent Debate

In the context of multi-agent debate, standard step-level branching leads to a combinatorial explosion of interaction paths—a computational bottleneck highlighted by
(
13
)
. To mitigate this, we adopt a ’Trajectory-level Pairwise Debate Rollouts’ strategy that maintains
K
parallel debate histories as shown in Fig.
4
. Specifically, at the initial turn (
t=0
), each participating agent generates
K=16
independent rollouts. These responses are then synchronized by index to form
K
distinct conversation threads (i.e., the
k
-th response of Agent A is paired with the
k
-th response of Agent B). For all subsequent turns (
t>0
), agents generate a single rollout
K=1
for each of these established trajectories. This mechanism ensures that each of the
K
trajectories evolves linearly with consistent context, enabling efficient GRPO without the exponential overhead of recursive branching.

Then we utilize these
K
parallel debate rollouts to approximate the implicit belief distribution by aggregating the frequency of final answers. To ensure robustness, we extract the answer key from each rollout and merge semantically equivalent terms. Crucially, to enable valid divergence calculations across agents who may sample disjoint answer sets, we define the common support set
\mathcal{Y}_{union}
as the union of all unique answers generated by the entire team at turn
t
. Each agent
i

\hat{p}{i,t}(y)

\mathcal{Y}_{union}

\bar{p}_{t}(y)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{p}{i,t}(y)

Total Uncertainty
is defined as the entropy of the team mean
H(\bar{p}_{t})
. This is further decomposed into

, and

presents the Uncertainty-Guided Multi-Agent Debate (UMAD) training framework, which extends GRPO to optimize collaborative reasoning. The procedure involves sampling a group of joint debate trajectories to estimate advantage baselines, incorporating two key regulatory mechanisms: an Epistemic Influence Intrinsic Reward that credits agents for positively shaping peer performance in subsequent turns, and an Aleatoric Uncertainty-Aware Advantage that modulates gradient updates based on token-level confidence. By integrating these components, the algorithm optimizes the policy to minimize internal reasoning noise while maximizing the effective information gain provided to the multi-agent system.

Analysis of Debate Trajectories.
As illustrated in Fig.
6
, our empirical findings reveal that the outcome of a debate is governed by a delicate trade-off between epistemic resolution and aleatoric control:

•

Universal Consensus (Epistemic Decay):
A universal trend across all Successful, Neutral, and Failure trajectories is the monotonic decrease of Epistemic Uncertainty (EU). This confirms that MAD fundamentally functions as a consensus-seeking process regardless of ground-truth correctness, continuously pruning conflicting hypotheses.

•

The "Aleatoric Barrier" to Success:
The divergence in final accuracy is primarily dictated by Aleatoric Uncertainty (AU).

–

Success Mode:
Agents maintain low or decreasing AU, effectively anchoring valid reasoning against noise.

–

Failure Mode:
Often observed in weaker models (e.g., Llama series), AU escalates uncontrollably. This indicates that the "contextual cost"—such as hallucination reinforcement—overwhelms the benefits of information exchange.

–

Neutral Mode:
Represents an equilibrium where epistemic gains are exactly offset by high aleatoric noise, leading to stagnant accuracy.

•

The Heterogeneous Advantage:
Heterogeneous pairs (e.g., Qwen-3B vs. Qwen-4B) exhibit significantly higher initial EU than homogeneous ones. This elevated "cognitive gap" offers greater potential for improvement. Optimal performance is achieved when this high epistemic potential is realized under the stability constraints provided by the stronger model, maximizing effective information gain.

Broader Impact

This work advances the understanding of multi-agent interactions in Large Language Models (LLMs) by providing a rigorous Bayesian framework for uncertainty quantification. By shifting the focus from superficial prompt engineering to the fundamental regulation of internal belief dynamics, our Uncertainty-Guided MARL (UMAD) approach paves the way for more reliable and self-correcting reasoning systems. This has broader implications for deploying LLMs in high-stakes domains (e.g., scientific research, legal analysis) where hallucinations are notoriously risky and "wisdom of crowds" mechanisms are essential for verification.
