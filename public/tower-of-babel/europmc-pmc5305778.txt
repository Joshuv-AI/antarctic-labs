Some Aspects of a Modern "Religio Medici."
Madam President and Ladies,?It is an honour I deeply
appreciate to be with you to-night. When your Secretary's
letter reached me, my first and second thoughts were that I
must refuse so responsible a task, for I felt in no sense fitted
to give you the words of wisdom and guidance and help you
would justly expect from the speaker at your opening meeting.
But your President and Vice-President are very persuasive
people, and when they wrote that my old students wanted
me, it was their use of the possessive pronoun that altered
my decision.
When I was with you as a teacher, I never dared to think
of you as my students; it was as a fellow-student I worked
among you. So, now, my only excuse for addressing you
to-night is that you have chosen me, and I come not as a
teacher, not as one who has reached those heights to which
he would fain point you, but as a fellow-traveller, who has
travelled a long time, yet very slowly, one who has known
something of the inspiration and satisfaction and joy of life,
something of its difficulties and disappointments, something
also of the failure and defeat that enter into each one's
experience.
I am, then, a fellow-worker, who has worked up to, and is
still struggling through varied experiences, and, if I under-
stand my duty and my commission to-night aright, I am
expected to give you some facts and thoughts gathered from
the experience of a working life. This is a very difficult yet

It was my privilege, recently, to live for several weeks in
a house where two of the inmates were members of an amateur
orchestral society. Every morning they practised for an hour,
and after the practice, one of the players, a kindly old doctor,
used to come up to my room and, taking compassion on my
ignorance of music?both of its technique and its interpreta-

out "something else" from that prosaic thing cat-gut. That
" something else" the old doctor called harmonics: they are
the sounds that come from some mysterious place and take
a lodging in another mysterious place?the human soul. It
is about this "something else" I desire to speak to you?the
strange and arresting and mysterious notes that are in the
commonplace material of life, when the right finger touches it.
I. The Spirit of Leisure.
It is a natural law that a large proportion of our time
cannot be spent on our hardest work, and under the name
leisure I comprise all time not given to our professional work.

The use of our leisure hours is more important, in some
ways, than that of our working hours, because it affects the
character more.
In a recent address to the Classical Association, Professor
Gilbert Murray points out how the greater part of life is
> rigidly confined to the round of things that happen from hour
to hour. Man is imprisoned, he says, in the external present,
and what he calls a man's religion is to a great extent the
thing that offers him a secret and permanent means of escape
from that prison. This thought of " escape " conveys the idea
uppermost in my mind in speaking of the spirit of leisure.
Of all avenues of escape it can be said as Channing, in his
Essay on Milton, said of poetry, "they carry the mind
beyond and above the beaten, dusty, weary walks of ordinary
life, lift it into a purer element, and breathe into it more
profound and generous emotion."
Therefore, let us retain in our leisure hours everything
that will help us to understand the significance of life, its
mystery, and its beauty. For this purpose I would urge you
to keep in constant touch with three realms?not only make
excursions into them for the refreshment and enlargement of
your mental life, but let them become part of the very fabric
of your being. These three realms are?the realm of Fairyland,
the realm of Art, and the realm of Nature.
i. The Realm of Fairyland.
I have said that by entering this realm, which is only a
part of that great realm?the Land of Wonder?you can
" escape" from the dusty, weary walks of life. May I urge
you never to grow up, in this sense: if your work keeps you
in the beaten track, be rebels as often as you can, and climb
over the fence into the "Broad Walk" and join the children
there. Some of them with hoops are running to the " Round
Pond," where, you remember, "you can't be good all the time,
however much you try." Or, if you cannot go so far, chase
with the children those childish visions, with which their
interpreters, the creators of Peter Pan, have peopled the
gardens for us.
The world to which we are thus translated is a world
without end or beginning, in which we lived as children
without knowing it; the difference now is that we do know
it. When Wordsworth wrote that "heaven lies about us in

A few weeks ago I was standing by the Peter Pan statue
in Kensington Gardens, watching four almost ragged little
children from the district round Covent Garden. They were
all lovingly stroking the rabbits and the squirrels and putting
their arms around the fairy's wings. I asked them if they
knew the story of the statue, and the eldest, a boy of eight,
eagerly answered, "Yes, sir, teacher took us." I then asked,
"Would you like to be Peter Pan and never grow up?"
And again the eldest answered?" No, sir, I want to be a
man." The answer was perhaps a child's instinctive longing
for more intense life, for if we go back in thought to our
own childhood, we will find that the unconscious element
that was common to all the ideas and pictures in that land
we wandered in, was the longing for something unattainable.
So, if we would take time to enter " Dreamland," we should
find an escape from the haunting sense of our incapacity to
meet the needs of our real world.
Now, in "Peter Pan," Barrie has crystallised, as it were,
the essential qualities of childhood. Peter Pan stands for
that wonderful world somewhere round the corner, a world
in which dreams come true. If we become convinced of the
existence of that country just round the corner, we shall feel
that the ever fuller discovery of it is the only thing worth
while. We may call it, if we will, a land of dreams out of
which we may achieve reality: we may call it more truly the
land of reality from which we may actualise in our lives any-
thing we will, but we cannot become normal?physically,
mentally, or spiritually?until we have discovered it. If, then,
we would recover the lost vision of our childhood, we must
take time to remain under the spell which the fairies weave,
and learn the sure way to keep the fairies with us.

of calling my old students, may remember that I have urged

beauty and faith.
2. The Realm of Art.
It is the tendency of each of the various professions to call
into play a limited number of mental activities, and the best
cure for such professional narrowness is the capacity of escape.
The realm of Art is so extensive and so inclusive and so much
the very texture of life that it opens wide doors for this escape.
A recent writer has defined Art as " the instinctive effort of

it is, perhaps, the most easily accessible. Thought and feeling
are the ruling powers of the world, and literature or word

thought and felt.
The Poet Laureate defines Art as " the expression of ideas
in some sensuous material" ; " these ideas, in taking material
forms of beauty, make a direct appeal to the emotions through
the senses." Thus the material or medium of sculpture is stone
or marble : the medium of painting is colours : of music is sound :
and the medium of verse is words. As a man of Letters, he
glories in the power of words to express ideas, and shows how
words which appear so simple as mere names of objects, are
each one names of various and indefinite ideas or conceptions
of things. Now these ideas, of which words are the symbols,
are called concepts, and a recent writer compares a concept in
the mind to a precious stone, say a diamond. The first vague
idea conveyed by the word?for example, to a child's mind?
will be like the rough diamond as it comes from the pit; and
its growth, to the change which comes over the diamond under
the hand of the expert gem cutter, who transforms it into a
brilliant jewel with many structural facets, which reflect and
refract all the light that falls on them. What infinite interest
and variety there is in the necessities through which thought
passes in finding expression in words! It is one of the
happiest and readiest means of escape for all of us as we try
to trace the thought either in poetry or prose. Every word,
also, has a history, and very often two or three syllables will
contain "a hidden meaning preserved in a casket, the inner
beauty of which is revealed only to those who seek the truth."
Such words as " physics," " sincere," " religion," " genius," and
many others, illustrate the help such study affords to the
acquisition of an exact sense of the beauty and significance
of words.

spiritual inheritance, for it tells of those simple, universal, actual

evening to reading a page, dreaming over every scene, and
brooding over every suggestive word, and noting the effect

within your souls the appreciation for the great things that
really matter.

3. The Realm of Nature.
Of this, the most beautiful and perfect, means of " escape,"
I need say little. It is a realm in which we must live alone, for
110 spirit can guide another spirit into her manifold mysteries
and unceasing wonders. The voices that speak in sea and sky,
in river and forest, in the majesty of the mountains and the
dreamland of the plains, in flower and grass, are alike within
the dominion of the soul.
You have come back to work from solitary hours spent on
grassy slopes, on beds of heather or moss; these hours, I trust,
have been to you a portal into the Unseen, where Nature, the
"dear old Nurse," has taken you by the hand and drawn you
to the world of dreams where you were thinking not at all, but
living and feeling intensely. And now, as you begin work
again, you are more receptive, and every sense is quieted and
attuned to the great harmony surrounding us.
Most of us will remember days in our childhood spent in
the woods, when the ground was carpeted with the starry
wood-anemone, or drifts of bluebells ; how, on our way home,
the flowers, held in hot hands or drooping over bare arms, were
tossed away without a thought, and left in the dust of the lane.
They were sacrificed on the child altar, and who shall say no
good came of the sacrifice, for there we made our first acquaint-
ance with pain, if not with tears. But as we grew up and
roamed alone, and the voices of Nature spoke to us, we learned
something of the inner meaning and mystery of all that
surrounds us ; we came to recognise beauty where we had seen
only colour, to realise life where we had seen only form. And
to-day the mystery remains, but there are lights on our path
when we take time to turn from the dusty track, and these had
their origin in those green forest paths of our childhood.

4. Other Realms.
There are many other realms which our spirits may enter
and find " escape," but of these I have time barely to mention
three. One is the realm of rest or of sleep. I do not mean
rest or sleep at night time, but during the day. I would urge
you to rest on your return in the late afternoon from your long
days work. Before beginning the evening's work, or taking
the evening meal, lie down on a couch or on your bed, and
relax all that binds your bodies and your spirits, and, if possible,
escape for half an hour. It will not be time wasted, and I am
sure it will make all the difference in your evening's work;
it will help to keep your minds and bodies in good temper,
and make you more fit instruments for the work which must
be done.
Another realm is that of amusement. We miss our way in
life unless we know the difference between work and play.
What is really important is to understand that we cannot live
by work alone, and that amusement is one of the fundamental
needs of our nature. Outdoor amusements, especially those
which give suitable exercise, afford not only the needed escape
from work, but escape from the loneliness and introspection and
self-criticism often incidental to student days. Something of
the philosophy of amusement we may learn, if we would recall
the impulses of our childhood.
The last realm to which I direct your attention is one which
includes the friendships, the intercourse, and the associations
of life. Some of you have known what a wonderful thing
friendship is, and the deeper you get into life the more you feel
how true a gift is a real friend. In a busy life, remember the
maxim : " Keep your friendships in repair." Our Principal, in
his inaugural address, spoke of Universities as " sanctuaries of

II. The Spirit of Work.
I have spoken of the spirit of our leisure, and urged that
we should enter, as a means of releasing the over-bent bow,
those realms which offer to our bodies and spirits " escape"
from the stress and strain of our days. I have tried to point
out that leisure should be so used as to render us fit instruments
for our work. What, then, must be the spirit of our work?
Most of us, before we choose our profession, take some
definite view of our own lives, of our relation to the lives of
others, and of the objects which seem to us worthy of account.
The dream life of childhood must precede the conscious life
of intellectual maturity, but there must be no fundamental
differences between the ideals which moved you in your
childhood and those which must maintain you in the difficulties
of your womanhood. I think we need feel no shame in
recalling the' dreams of our childhood, and holding fast to them,
despite the struggle which so often ends in defeat. As we
pass from childhood's aspirations, our ambitions become more
definite, and they are, Poincare writes, "due, in the main, to
an unconscious appeal to our emotions." He says that the
members of our profession have a great advantage over many
other workers in that the science and art of medicine combine
the search for the beautiful with the achievement of the useful.
I think we scarcely realise how fortunate we are in the dignity
inherent in our work.

I have been asked by some of you to re-tell a story which
I gave to the class of Morbid Anatomy in March of last year,
and again to pass on to you the lesson I learnt from an old
shepherd. If we learn from a wise interpreter the quality and
worth of any aspect of life, it is that we may hand on what we
have gained. And because of this I would like to hand on to
you this message, for it contains the idea that has been upper-
most in my mind in thinking of the spirit of our work.
I was spending a few weeks on the Cairngorms, and one
evening, in the deepening twilight, I came upon an old shepherd
sitting on the hillside with his dog. I saw that every now and
then he looked intently through a telescope at the hills opposite
and then talked to his dog, who very evidently shared his
master's anxiety. I sat down beside them, and asked the old
man what he was looking for. " Some lost sheep," he told me.
I offered, if his dog would obey a stranger, to take the dog and
round them off, but the old man said that all he wanted to
know was where they were. So I took the telescope and after
a little time located the lost sheep, and described them to him.
As I handed back the telescope, the old man, with a very quiet
courtesy, turned to me and asked, " And what might be your
vocation, sir ? " I told him, and then he said, " But you know
something about sheep?" "Yes," I said, "I was a shepherd
in New Zealand." And then very gently, but with evident
reproach in his tone, the old man asked me, " And what made
you give up such a vocation ? "
That summer, before I left Edinburgh, I had picked up a little
Latin book, in which a writer in the Middle Ages had summed up
a man's life in three words?in the mediaeval Latin of the time
? Vocatzo, Tentatio, Oratio. I had also, during the previous

2. Tentatio.
To the mediaeval Latin writer I have quoted, the word
Tentatio signified more than temptation ; it included, in a
word, all that disciplines us. Whatever we think of discipline
as a word, it is omnipresent as a fact, and it is the rule under
which we all pass our lives.
Is, then, discipline so necessary to life? We see the
beauty of the light effects in a picture, enhanced by the dark-
ness of the shadows. Is discipline as necessary to happiness
as shadow is to light ? I think there can be, for us at least,
only one answer. To meet this te7itatio in other lives, we
must know both happiness and discipline as elements in our
training. Without them, our ears will never be attuned to
hear the undertones of that delicate musical instrument, the
human body and soul of our patient.
But discipline perplexes the mind. The " Ancient Sage"
voices this age-long perplexity in the lines?

As you go in and out among your poorer patients, will
