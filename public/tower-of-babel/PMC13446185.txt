Spontaneous resolution of syringomyelia associated with an unruptured intracranial aneurysm: A case report
Authors: Adam Benomar, Houyam Tibar, Mohammed Jiddane, Ali Benomar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report a rare association of an unruptured basilar artery aneurysm with cervicothoracic syringomyelia that spontaneously regressed without surgical intervention. A 56-year-old female presented with chronic unilateral neuropathic pain as well as a thoracic sensory level involving pain and temperature sensation. Initial spinal MRI revealed extensive syringomyelia associated with Chiari type I malformation. A basilar artery aneurysm was documented and showed enlargement over time. Follow-up MRIs demonstrated complete spontaneous regression of the syrinx. Axial imaging revealed a distinct parenchymal fissure within the spinal cord, which may provide a plausible structural explanation for cerebrospinal fluid drainage and subsequent syrinx resolution. This case underscores the role of spinal cord parenchymal fissuring in spontaneous syrinx regression and highlights the importance of detailed imaging for elucidating pathophysiological mechanisms in syringomyelia associated with vascular anomalies.

Introduction

The relationship between syringomyelia and aneurysm is a rare entity [1]. Similarly, although uncommon, the spontaneous regression of a syringomyelic cavity, has been described in specific situations, generally linked to changes in cerebrospinal fluid dynamics [2]. We are reporting a rare association between an unruptured basilar artery aneurysm and syringomyelia, with spontaneous regression without surgical intervention. This observation raises questions about the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms and highlights the need for a better understanding of the interactions between vascular abnormalities and cerebrospinal fluid alterations.

Medical observation

The patient, Mrs. LL, is a 56-year-old female, married and has 1 child. Her personal medical history includes hypertension. The patient delivered a child by cesarean section on May 13, 2009. The patient had no clinical symptoms suggesting an aneurysmal rupture that could indicate subarachnoid hemorrhage. No history or clinical evidence of prior meningitis was documented. Symptoms began at the age of 20 and was characterized by progressive burning pain along the ulnar distribution of the left arm, the left breast, and the left scapular region, without pain in the other limbs. The symptoms occurred in an afebrile state with preserved general condition, without headaches or sphincter dysfunction. Our first Neurological examination in March 2025 revealed that the patient was conscious and oriented to time, place, and person, with normal gait and no motor deficit.

Sensory examination showed hypoesthesia in the left lower limb, with a band-like sensory disturbance involving pain and temperature sensation, extending from the upper thoracic region to the xiphoid level. Deep sensation was preserved. Pyramidal signs were present in the lower limbs. The remainder of the neurological examination was unremarkable. Physical examination revealed a 33° thoracic scoliosis, as well as atrophy of the left thigh, with a 5 cm reduction in circumference compared to the right side. Spinal MRI carried out in 2011 showed a syringomyelic cavity extending from C1 to T11 (Fig. 1), hyperintense on T2-weighted images, with significant involvement of the cervical and upper thoracic regions. There was a marked enlargement of the spinal cord, associated with a visible mass effect. The cavity appeared regular, with no signs of rupture or wall irregularities. No tumor or infectious etiology was detected. The patient was treated with pregabalin at a daily dose of 600 mg for syrinx-related neuropathic pain, which led to marked clinical improvement and substantial pain relief.

Discussion

We describe an exceptional case of an unruptured basilar artery aneurysm associated with spontaneous regression of a syringomyelic cavity.

Spontaneous resolution of syringomyelia

Mitchell D. Kilgore reported 1 case and a review of 39 cases of spontaneous resolution of syringomyelic cavities, including 4 cases associated with pregnancy. In 75% of the cases, an Arnold-Chiari type I malformation was identified. Complete regression of the syrinx was observed in 33% of patients [2].

The association between syringomyelia and cerebral aneurysm

In 2024, Jain et al. reported a case of aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage complicated by syringomyelia. They also provided a summary table listing 19 previously reported cases. These 19 cases were distributed as follows: 16 involved the vertebrobasilar territory, and only 3 cases were located in the carotid territory. Among the 3 carotid cases, there was 1 aneurysm of the middle cerebral artery and 2 of the anterior cerebral artery. Among the 16 vertebrobasilar cases, 8 involved the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA), 5 the vertebral artery (VA), 1 both the VA and PICA, 1 the superior cerebellar artery (SCA), and only 1 case involved the basilar artery [1].

To the best of our knowledge, in the literature, we are reporting the second case of an association between a basilar artery aneurysm and syringomyelia, following the first case described by Ishizaka in 2012. [3]. As far as we know, no previous cases have reported the diagnosis of syringomyelia associated with an aneurysm in the absence of any clinical or imaging findings suggestive of aneurysmal hemorrhage (Figs. 2, 4, 5 and 6).

There was no history of meningitis that could explain the development of syringomyelia [1]. A direct causal relationship cannot be established. In this context, the Chiari type I malformation represents a more plausible mechanism for syrinx formation through impaired cerebrospinal fluid flow at the craniocervical junction. The basilar artery aneurysm may therefore represent an incidental association, although a shared or indirect mechanism cannot be completely excluded.

Hypothesised mechanisms

Aneurysm-induced resolution of syringomyelia

In the absence of bleeding, it is unlikely that an aneurysm significantly alters cerebrospinal fluid dynamics. In this case, the aneurysm is not believed to have contributed to the resolution of the syringomyelia, as there was no evidence of prior hemorrhage on imaging or clinical evaluation.

Fissuring of the spinal cord parenchyma

The hypothesis of spontaneous syrinx resolution was proposed by Santoro et al. [4], who suggested that several mechanisms may be involved, including fissuring of the spinal cord parenchyma.

In our patient, axial imaging revealed a plausible radiological explanation for the spontaneous regression of the syrinx, suggesting a fissure of the spinal cord parenchyma. On axial T2-weighted imaging, focal linear parenchymal fissures were identified on both the left and right sides, extending from the syringomyelic cavity toward the surrounding subarachnoid space along the expected course of the posterior roots (Fig. 7). We hypothesize that these fissures may have acted as spontaneous drainage pathways, allowing communication between the syrinx cavity and the surrounding subarachnoid CSF space. Such a mechanism could have reduced the intramedullary pressure within the cavity and contributed to the progressive collapse and spontaneous resolution of the syringomyelia, despite persistent tonsillar descent. Pregnancy may also be considered as a possible contributing factor, as reported in the literature. Four cases of syrinx regression following pregnancy have been described. In 2 cases, the mode of delivery was not specified; 1 case involved cesarean section, and the other vaginal delivery [2].

In our patient, syringomyelia was documented on MRI in 2003, followed by pregnancy and delivery in 2009, with the first radiological evidence of syrinx regression observed on follow-up MRI in 2018. This chronology raises the possibility that pregnancy-related physiological changes may have contributed to the subsequent regression of a pre-existing syringomyelia. Increased intra-abdominal pressure, hemodynamic modifications, venous congestion, and transient alterations in cerebrospinal fluid dynamics during pregnancy and childbirth may theoretically have favored progressive weakening of the syrinx wall and the development of a parenchymal fissure. Such a fissure could have allowed communication between the syrinx cavity and the subarachnoid space, facilitating spontaneous drainage and progressive collapse of the syrinx. However, this interpretation remains hypothetical, and the delayed interval between delivery and the documented regression prevents any definite causal relationship from being established.

Chiari I–related changes in cerebrospinal fluid dynamics

Even in the absence of significant morphological change in tonsillar ectopia on follow-up MRI, subtle variations in CSF flow at the craniocervical junction may still be relevant. In Chiari I malformation, syrinx formation appears to be influenced more by impairment of CSF circulation at the foramen magnum than by the absolute degree of tonsillar descent [5]. Moreover, quantitative MRI studies suggest that cerebellar herniation alone has limited prognostic value and should not be overemphasized when assessing the progression of syringomyelia [6]. Therefore, an alternative mechanism involving functional changes in CSF dynamics cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case highlights the importance of individualized management in patients with syringomyelia. Current consensus recommendations do not support surgery based solely on the radiological presence of a syrinx, particularly in the absence of progressive neurological deficits, major functional impairment, disabling drug-resistant pain, or marked spinal cord compression on MRI [7]. In clinically stable patients who remain autonomous and maintain a normal daily life, conservative management with close clinical and radiological follow-up may therefore represent an appropriate strategy.
