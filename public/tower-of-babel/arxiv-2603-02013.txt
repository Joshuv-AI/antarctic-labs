Revisiting Second-Order Linear Differential Equations over Hardy Fields
Matthias Aschenbrenner, Lou van den Dries, Joris van der Hoeven

Abstract
. We review second-order homogeneous linear differential equations with coefficient functions whose germs lie in a Hardy field (and hence are strongly non-oscillating). We prove a conjecture of Boshernitzan (1982): the oscillating solutions to such an equation are given by amplitude and phase functions with germs in a bigger Hardy field, and hence oscillate in a very regular way. We give sharp conditions for the uniqueness of such germs, study their asymptotic behavior, and use this to obtain information about the zeros and critical points of oscillating solutions.



Introduction

Second-order ordinary linear differential equations are ubiquitous
in applications of mathematics. For instance, most “special functions” of mathematical physics
satisfy such equations, often with rational function coefficients; see
[
9
, p. 1]
for an explanation. Examples encountered below are the Airy, Bessel, and Coulomb wave functions.
In this paper we study the asymptotic behavior of solutions to second-order homogeneous linear
differential equations whose coefficient functions are
non-oscillating at

in a
very strong sense.

Let

\ast

∗

The maps

In this paper we restrict

This concept formalizes the 19th century idea (du Bois-Reymond, Borel, Lindelöf) of “functions of regular growth”.
Many naturally occurring non-oscillating differentially algebraic functions —for example, any univariate rational function with real coefficients, and
\operatorname{e}^{x}

\log x

This is also the case for certain differentially transcendental functions: the Riemann
\zeta
-function, Euler’s
\Gamma
-function
[
43

even some functions ultimately growing faster than each iterate of

If

A

Any hardian germ

A basic Hardy field is the field

O-minimal expansions of the real field are a natural source of Hardy fields

Next set

2.
Asymptotic Differential Algebra

Here we introduce
various objects studied in [ADH] and recall a few basic facts about them proved there.
We also include a short proof of a theorem of Kolchin (Corollary
2.4
) and
some technical results (Lemmas
2.10
and
2.11
) needed later.

Valued fields

A (Krull) valuation on a field
K
is a surjective map
v\colon K^{\times}\to\Gamma
onto an ordered abelian
group
\Gamma
(additively written) satisfying the usual laws, and extended to

v\colon K\to\Gamma_{\infty}:=\Gamma\cup\{\infty\}
by
v(0)=\infty
,
where the ordering on
\Gamma
is extended to a total ordering
on
\Gamma_{\infty}
by
\gamma<\infty
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
.
Given such a valuation
v
on
K
,
\mathcal{O}=\{a\in K:va\geqslant 0\}
is a valuation ring of
K
;
conversely, every valuation ring
\mathcal{O}
on a field
K
gives rise to a valuation

v\colon K^{\times}\to\Gamma
on
K

such that
\mathcal{O}=\{a\in K:va\geqslant 0\}
. (See [ADH, 3.1].)
A
valued field

K
is a field
together with a valuation ring
\mathcal{O}
of that field.
Let
K
be a valued field, let
\mathcal{O}
be its valuation ring with
v\colon K^{\times}\to\Gamma
the corresponding valuation. Then
\mathcal{O}
is a local ring with maximal ideal
\smallo:=\{a\in K:va>0\}
. From now on
K
(and any valued field that gets menstioned) has
equicharacteristic zero
, that is, the
residue field
\operatorname{res}(K):=\mathcal{O}/\smallo
of
K
has characteristic zero.
With
a
,
b
ranging over
K
, set

\displaystyle a\asymp b

\displaystyle\ :\Leftrightarrow\ va=vb,

\displaystyle a\preccurlyeq b

\displaystyle\ :\Leftrightarrow\ va\geqslant vb,

\displaystyle a\prec b

\displaystyle\ :\Leftrightarrow\ va>vb,

\displaystyle a\succcurlyeq b

\displaystyle\ :\Leftrightarrow\ b\preccurlyeq a,

\displaystyle a\succ b

\displaystyle\ :\Leftrightarrow\ b\prec a,

\displaystyle a\sim b

\displaystyle\ :\Leftrightarrow\ a-b\prec a.

Thus
\mathcal{O}=\{a:a\preccurlyeq 1\}
and
\smallo=\{a:a\prec 1\}
.
If
a\sim b
, then
a,b\neq 0
and
a\asymp b
; moreover,
\sim
is an equivalence relation on
K^{\times}
.

We have the residue morphism
a\mapsto\overline{a}:=a+\smallo\colon\mathcal{O}\to\operatorname{res}(K)
, which we extend to the ring morphism
P\mapsto\overline{P}\colon\mathcal{O}[Y]\to\operatorname{res}(K)[Y]
with
Y\mapsto Y
.
The valued field
K
is called
henselian
if for each
P\in\mathcal{O}[Y]
and
u\in\mathcal{O}
such that
\overline{P}(\overline{u})=0
,
\big(\frac{\partial\overline{P}}{\partial Y}\big)(\overline{u})\neq 0
, there exists
y\in u+\smallo
with
P(y)=0
. (See [ADH, 3.3].)

Let now
L
be a valued field extension of
K
; then
the relations
\asymp
,
\preccurlyeq
, … on
L
displayed above restrict to the corresponding relations on
K
, and
we identify in the usual way the value group
{\Gamma_{K}}
of
K
with an ordered subgroup of the value group
\Gamma_{L}
of
L

and
\operatorname{res}(K)
with a subfield of
\operatorname{res}(L)
.
Such a valued field extension is called
immediate
if
{\Gamma_{K}=\Gamma_{L}}
and
\operatorname{res}(K)=\operatorname{res}(L)
.
If
L
is algebraic over
K
, then the group
\Gamma_{L}/\Gamma_{K}
is torsion and the field extension
\operatorname{res}(L)\supseteq\operatorname{res}(K)
is algebraic.

Valued differential fields

Let
K
be
a
valued differential field
, that is, a valued field (of equicharacteristic zero by our earlier convention) together with a derivation on the underlying field.
We say that the derivation
\der
of
K
is
small
if
\der\smallo\subseteq\smallo
; then
{\der\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{O}}
[ADH, 4.4.2], hence
\der
induces a derivation
on the residue field
\operatorname{res}(K)
of
K
making the residue morphism
{\mathcal{O}\to\operatorname{res}(K)}
a differential ring morphism.
We also say that
K
is
monotone
if
f^{\prime}\preccurlyeq f
for all
f\prec 1
in
K
. If
K
is monotone, then it has small derivation;
the converse holds whenever
\Gamma
is archimedean [ADH, 6.1.2].
If
K
has small derivation, then every valued differential field extension
of
K
that is algebraic over
K
also has small derivation [ADH, 6.2.1]; likewise with “is monotone” in place of “has small derivation”[ADH, 6.3.10].

Liouvillian zeros of linear differential operators

Let
K
be a differential field.
A
Liouville extension
of
K

is a differential field extension
E
of
K
such that
C_{E}
is algebraic over
C
and for each
t\in E
there are
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\in L
such that
t\in K(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
and for
i=1,\dots,n
:

(1)

t_{i}
is algebraic over
K(t_{1},\dots,t_{i-1})
, or

(2)

t_{i}^{\prime}\in K(t_{1},\dots,t_{i-1})
, or

(3)

t_{i}^{\prime}\in t_{i}K(t_{1},\dots,t_{i-1})
.

See [ADH, p. 462] for basic properties of Liouville extensions, such as the following:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
M|L
and
L|K
be differential field extensions. If
M|K
is a Liouville extension, then so is
M|L
. If
M|L
and
L|K
are Liouville extensions, then so is
M|K
.

In the proof of Lemma
4.28
we shall use
a theorem of Kolchin (Corollary
2.4
) which gives information about linear differential operators
A\in K[\der]^{\neq}

having
zeros
y\neq 0
in Liouville extensions of
K
.
We include a variant of Rosenlicht’s short proof
of a more general result, since it illustrates the effectiveness of asymptotic differential-algebraic reasoning. For this we let

m\geqslant 1
,
P\in K\{Y\}
,
\deg P<m
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
L
be a differential field extension of
K
and
t\in L
,
t^{\prime}\in K+tK
,
and suppose
L
is algebraic over
K(t)
.
If
y^{m}=P(y)
for some
y\in L
, then
z^{m}=P(z)
for some
z
in a differential field extension of
K

which is algebraic over
K
.

Proof.

We may assume
t
is transcendental over
K
.
View
K(t)
as a subfield of the Laurent series field
F=K(\!(t^{-1})\!)
. We equip
F
with the valuation ring
\mathcal{O}_{F}=K[[t^{-1}]]
and the continuous derivation extending that of
K(t)
, cf. [ADH, p. 226]. Then the valued differential field
F
is monotone.
Hence the valued differential subfield
K(t)
of
F
is also monotone. We equip
L
with a valuation ring
\mathcal{O}_{L}
lying over
\mathcal{O}_{F}\cap K(t)
; then
L
is monotone, since it is algebraic over
K(t)
.
We
identify
K
with its image under the residue morphism
a\mapsto\overline{a}\colon\mathcal{O}_{L}\to{{\boldsymbol{k}}}_{L}:=\operatorname{res}(L)
. Then
K
is a differential subfield of the differential residue field
{{\boldsymbol{k}}}_{L}
of
L
,
and
{{\boldsymbol{k}}}_{L}
is algebraic over
K
.
Let now
y\in L
with
y^{m}=P(y)
, and towards a contradiction suppose
y\succ 1
.
Then
y^{\dagger}\preccurlyeq 1
, thus
y^{(n)}=y\,R_{n}(y^{\dagger})\preccurlyeq y
for all
n
, with
R_{n}=\operatorname{Ri}(Y^{(n)})
as in (
1.1
), and hence
y^{m}=P(y)\preccurlyeq y^{d}
where
{d=\deg P<m}
, a contradiction. Thus
y\preccurlyeq 1
, and
z:=\overline{y}\in{{\boldsymbol{k}}}_{L}
has the required property.
∎

Proposition 2.3

(Rosenlicht
[
41
, p. 371]
)
.

Suppose
y^{m}=P(y)
for some
y
in a Liouville extension of
K
. Then
z^{m}=P(z)
for some
z
in a differential field extension of
K
which is algebraic over
K
.

Proof.

A
Liouville sequence
over
K
is by definition a sequence
(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
of elements of a differential field extension
E
of
K
such that for
i=1,\dots,n
:

(1)

t_{i}
is algebraic over
K(t_{1},\dots,t_{i-1})
, or

(2)

t_{i}^{\prime}\in K(t_{1},\dots,t_{i-1})
, or

(3)

t_{i}^{\prime}\in t_{i}K(t_{1},\dots,t_{i-1})
.

Note that then
K_{i}:=K(t_{1},\dots,t_{i})
is a differential subfield of
E
for
i=1,\dots,n
.
By induction on
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
we now show: if
(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
is a Liouville sequence over
K

with
\operatorname{trdeg}\!\big(K(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})|K\big)=d
and

for
some

, then the conclusion of the proposition holds. This is obvious for

, so let
(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
be a Liouville sequence over
K

with
\operatorname{trdeg}\!\big(K(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})|K\big)=d\geqslant 1
and
y^{m}=P(y)
for
some
y\in K(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
.
Take
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
maximal such that
t_{i}
is transcendental over
K_{i-1}=K(t_{1},\dots,t_{i-1})
.
Then
t_{i}^{\prime}\in K_{i-1}+t_{i}K_{i-1}
, and
K(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
is algebraic over
K(t_{1},\dots,t_{i})
. Applying Lemma
2.2

to
K_{i-1}
,
t_{i}
in the role of
K,t
yields a
z
in an algebraic differential field extension of
K_{i-1}
with

. Now apply the inductive hypothesis to the Liouville sequence
(t_{1},\dots,t_{i-1},z)
over

.
∎

Asymptotic fields

An

asymptotic field
is a valued differential field
K
such that for all
f,g\prec 1
in
K^{\times}
:
{f\prec g}\Longleftrightarrow{f^{\prime}\prec g^{\prime}}
. This condition may be thought of as a valuation-theoretic
formulation of l’Hôpital’s Rule; it implies various other compatibilities between dominance and derivation, see [ADH, 9.1.3, 9.1.4]:

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
K
be an asymptotic field, and
f,g\in K
. Then

\displaystyle\text{ if }0\neq f,g\not\asymp 1,\text{ then }

\displaystyle f\preccurlyeq g\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f^{\prime}\preccurlyeq g^{\prime},

\displaystyle\text{ if }f\preccurlyeq g\not\asymp 1,\text{ then }

\displaystyle f\sim g\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f^{\prime}\sim g^{\prime}.

Compositional conjugates of asymptotic fields remain asymptotic.
In the rest of this subsection,
K
is an asymptotic field with constant field
C
. Then
C\subseteq\mathcal{O}
, and
if
\mathcal{O}=C+\smallo
(equivalently, for each
f\asymp 1
in
K
there is
c\in C
with
f\sim c
), then
K
is called
differential-valued
(
\operatorname{d}
-valued
for short). If
K
is
\operatorname{d}
-valued, then the residue morphism

\mathcal{O}\to\operatorname{res}(K)

maps the constant field
C
isomorphically onto
\operatorname{res}(K)
.
Not every asymptotic field extends to a
\operatorname{d}
-valued field; those that do are called
pre-
\operatorname{d}
-valued.
By
[
2
, Theorem 4.4]
,

(2.1)

\text{$K$ is pre-$\operatorname{d}$-valued}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{$f^{\prime}\prec g^{\dagger}$ whenever $f\preccurlyeq 1\prec g$ in $K$.}

The
asymptotic couple
of
K
is the pair
(\Gamma,\psi)

where
\psi\colon\Gamma^{\neq}\to\Gamma
is given by

\psi(vg)\ =\ v(g^{\dagger})\ \text{ for $g\in K^{\times}$ with $vg\neq 0$}.

We put
\Psi:=\psi(\Gamma^{\neq})=\big\{v(g^{\dagger}):g\in K^{\times}\setminus\mathcal{O}^{\times}\big\}
.
If we want to stress the dependence on
K
, we write
(\Gamma_{K},\psi_{K})
and
\Psi_{K}
instead of
(\Gamma,\psi)
and
\Psi
, respectively.
If
L
is an asymptotic field extension of
K
, then
(\Gamma_{L},\psi_{L})
extends the asymptotic couple
(\Gamma,\psi)
,
with
\Psi_{L}=\Psi
if
L\supseteq K
is algebraic.
Note:
K
is monotone iff
\Psi\subseteq\Gamma^{\geqslant}
.

Example
.

Let
C
be a field of characteristic zero and
x
an indeterminate. Then
{K=C(x)}
with derivation
\der=\frac{d}{dx}
and valuation
v\colon K^{\times}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
given by
v(C^{\times})=\{0\}
and
v(x)=-1

is
\operatorname{d}
-valued with constant field
C
,
and
\Psi=\{1\}
, so
K
is monotone.
(Asymptotic fields we consider later are typically not monotone.)

An
asymptotic couple
(without mentioning any asymptotic field) is a pair
(\Gamma,\psi)
consisting
of an ordered abelian group
\Gamma
and a map
\psi\colon\Gamma^{\neq}\to\Gamma
subject to some axioms
obeyed by the asymptotic couples of asymptotic fields; see [ADH, 6.5].
Let
(\Gamma,\psi)
be an asymptotic couple.
We
extend
\psi\colon\Gamma^{\neq}\to\Gamma
to a map
{\Gamma_{\infty}\to\Gamma_{\infty}}

by
\psi(0):=\psi(\infty):=\infty
.
If
(\Gamma,\psi)
is understood from the context and
\gamma\in\Gamma
we write
\gamma^{\dagger}
and
\gamma^{\prime}
instead of
\psi(\gamma)
and
{\gamma+\psi(\gamma)}
, respectively. We also set
\Psi:=\psi(\Gamma^{\neq})
; then

\Psi<(\Gamma^{>})^{\prime}
.
The following “contractive” property of
\psi
is from [ADH, 6.5.4(i)]:

Lemma 2.6
.

For all
\alpha,\beta<(\Gamma^{>})^{\prime}
in
\Gamma
we have

\psi(\alpha-\beta)>\min\{\alpha,\beta\}
.

Call
\gamma\in\Gamma
a
gap
in
(\Gamma,\psi)

if
\Psi<\gamma<(\Gamma^{>})^{\prime}
. (There is at most one such
\gamma
. For this and other facts about gaps, see [ADH, p. 388].)
We say that
(\Gamma,\psi)
is
grounded
if
\Psi
has a largest element,
and
(\Gamma,\psi)
has
asymptotic integration
if
(\Gamma^{\neq})^{\prime}=\Gamma
.
An asymptotic field is said to have a gap if its asymptotic couple does; likewise with “grounded” or “asymptotic integration” in place of “has a gap”. Any
\operatorname{d}
-valued field closed under integration is closed under asymptotic integration.
A gap in an asymptotic field
K
remains a gap in an algebraic asymptotic field extension of
K
.

An
H
-asymptotic couple
is
an asymptotic couple
(\Gamma,\psi)
such that for all
{\gamma,\delta\in\Gamma^{>}}
we have:

\gamma\leqslant\delta\Rightarrow\gamma^{\dagger}\geqslant\delta^{\dagger}
.
Every
H
-asymptotic couple either
has a gap, or is grounded, or has asymptotic integration [ADH, 9.2.16].
An asymptotic field is
H
-asymptotic
(or
of
H
-type
), if its asymptotic couple is
H
-asymptotic.

Dominant part

Let
K
be a valued differential field with small derivation, and set
{{\boldsymbol{k}}}:=\operatorname{res}(K)
.
We extend the residue morphism
\mathcal{O}\to{{\boldsymbol{k}}}

to the differential ring morphism
P\mapsto\overline{P}\colon\mathcal{O}\{Y\}\to{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\{Y\}
with
Y\mapsto Y
.
Let
P
,
Q
range over
K\{Y\}^{\neq}
.
We extend the valuation
v\colon K^{\times}\to\Gamma
, first to
K\{Y\}^{\neq}
by

v(P)\ :=\ \min\{va:\text{$a\in K^{\times}$ is a nonzero coefficient of $P$},

and then (uniquely) to a valuation
v\colon K\langle Y\rangle^{\times}\to\Gamma
.
For each
P
we
pick
\mathfrak{d}_{P}\in K^{\times}
such that
P\asymp\mathfrak{d}_{P}
, with
\mathfrak{d}_{P}=\mathfrak{d}_{Q}
if

P\sim Q
. Then

\mathfrak{d}_{P}^{-1}P\in\mathcal{O}\{Y\}
,
and we define the

dominant part
of
P
by
D_{P}:=\overline{\mathfrak{d}_{P}^{-1}P}\in{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\{Y\}^{\neq}
and the

dominant degree
of
P
by

\operatorname{ddeg}P:=\deg D_{P}
.
(Another choice of
\mathfrak{d}_{P}
multiplies
D_{P}
by an element of
{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\times}
, so
\operatorname{ddeg}P
does not depend on this choice.)

The map
v_{A}

With
K
as in the previous subsection, let
A=\sum_{i}a_{i}\der^{i}\in K[\der]^{\neq}
(all
a_{i}\in K
). In [ADH, 5.6] we defined

v(A):=\min_{i}v(a_{i})\in\Gamma
.
For
y\in K^{\times}
, the quantity
v(Ay)
only depends on
vy
, not on
y
, and we can thus define
v_{A}(\gamma):=v(Ay)
for
\gamma:=vy
.
The map
v_{A}\colon\Gamma\to\Gamma
is strictly increasing by [ADH, 4.5.1]. Much less obvious is that

v_{A}
is surjective; see [ADH, 6.0.1]. We give a proof for the easy case that is used later:

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
A\in K[\der]^{\neq}
have order
1
and
b\in K^{\times}
. Then there is a
y\in K^{\times}
such that
v(Ay)=vb
.

Proof.

We may assume
A=\der-s
where
s\in K
.
Then for
y\in K^{\times}
we have

Ay=y\big(\der-(s-y^{\dagger})\big)
. Put
a:=s-b^{\dagger}
.
If
a\preccurlyeq 1

Newtonianity

Let
K
be an ungrounded
H
-asymptotic field with

{\Gamma\neq\{0\}}
.
An element
\phi
of
K
is said to be
active in
K
if
v\phi\in\Psi^{\downarrow}
; in that case the derivation
\phi^{-1}\der
of the compositional conjugate
K^{\phi}
is small.
In this subsection
\phi
ranges over the active elements of
K
.
A property
S(\phi)
of (active) elements

is said to hold
eventually
if there is an active
\phi_{0}
in
K
such that
S(\phi)
holds for all
\phi\preccurlyeq\phi_{0}
; cf. [ADH, p. 479].
Given a differential polynomial

, its compositional conjugate

tends to become more transparent eventually, in particular,

\operatorname{ddeg}P^{\phi}
is eventually constant, as shown in [ADH, 11.1]. The eventual value of
\operatorname{ddeg}P^{\phi}
is
called the

of

, denoted by
\operatorname{ndeg}P
.
For

we say that

is

-newtonian
if every

of order
\leqslant r
with
\operatorname{ndeg}P=1
has a zero in
\mathcal{O}
,
and
r
-linearly newtonian
if every
P\in K\{Y\}
of order
\leqslant r
with
\deg P=\operatorname{ndeg}P=1
has a zero in
\mathcal{O}
.
We call
K

newtonian
if it is
r
-newtonian for all

, and likewise we define “linearly newtonian”.
By [ADH, 14.2.5], if

is

-linearly newtonian and has asymptotic integration, then

is
\operatorname{d}
-valued.
By [ADH, 14.2.2], if
K
is
r
-linearly newtonian, then

is
r
-linearly surjective. We include a proof for

:

Lemma 2.8
.

Suppose
K
is
1
-linearly newtonian. Then
K
is
1
-linearly surjective
(
and thus closed under integration
)
.

Proof.

Let
A\in K[\der]^{\neq}
have order
1
and
b\in K^{\times}
; to show:
b\in A(K)
.
Replace
A
,
K
by
A^{\phi}
,
K^{\phi}
for some
\phi
with
v\phi\in\Psi
to arrange
0\in\Psi
(so
K
has small derivation).
Lemma
2.7
yields
g\in K^{\times}
with
{v(Ag)=b}
. Replace
A
,
b
by
b^{-1}Ag
,
1
, to arrange
v(A)=0\leqslant vb
. Then
A=a_{1}\der+a_{0}
where

with
a_{0}\asymp 1
or
a_{1}\asymp 1
.
Consider first the case

, and set

For
\phi\prec 1
in

. Hence

has a zero

,
that is,

. In the remaining case we have
a_{0}\prec 1
and
a_{1}\asymp 1
. Now

gives

in
K
with

.
Then
A_{\ltimes e}=a_{1}\der+(a_{0}+a_{1}e^{\dagger})

, which gives a reduction to the previous case.
∎

Addition of sinusoids

Recall that for

\displaystyle\cos(\phi+\theta)\

\displaystyle=\ \cos(\phi)\cos(\theta)-\sin(\phi)\sin(\theta),

\displaystyle\cos(\phi-\theta)\

\displaystyle=\ \cos(\phi)\cos(\theta)+\sin(\phi)\sin(\theta).

Recall also the bijection

and

Corollary 4.10

is real closed and closed under integration, and let

On the interval

Thus
\arccos\!\big(g/\sqrt{g^{2}+h^{2}}\big)\in H

Proposition 7.4

Suppose
