Smart contact lens–trained digital twin for device-free personalized uric acid prediction
Authors: Hayoung Song, Yeon-Mi Hong, Dayeon Kim, Hunkyu Seo, Wonjung Park, Joonho Paek, Dongwook Lee, Sung Kweon Cho, Sung Soo Ahn, Jayoung Kim, Jang-Ung Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tears contain valuable biomarkers and offer potential for noninvasive disease monitoring. However, the lack of correlation analysis between serum uric acid (SUA) and tear uric acid (TUA) has limited their clinical application in personalized medicine. Here, we present a wireless smart contact lens capable of real-time, noninvasive monitoring of TUA as an alternative to blood-based UA testing. We validated the device through correlation analysis in rabbits and human participants, including individuals with hyperuricemia and gout. Daily-life monitoring enabled personalized characterization of TUA fluctuations in response to food intake and physical activity, together with individualized lag time profiling. A strong linear relationship allowed development of a regression model to derive estimated SUA from TUA. Building on these temporal profiles, lifestyle-informed digital twins were constructed to predict daily uric acid dynamics without continuous lens wear. This digital twin–enabled, device-free framework provides a practical route toward unobtrusive and personalized metabolic health monitoring.

INTRODUCTION

Uric acid (UA) is the final metabolic product of purine degradation, generated both exogenously through dietary purines and endogenously via cellular metabolism (1). UA homeostasis is governed by the balance between its production and excretion via renal and intestinal pathways. The disruption of this balance can lead to hyperuricemia, defined as a serum UA (SUA) concentration exceeding 417 μM (2). The excessive UA can crystallize and accumulate in joints, causing severe pain and inflammation, a disease known as gout. Gout is also associated with systemic comorbidities such as diabetes and cardiovascular disease, emphasizing the need for sustained UA level management (3). Frequent monitoring of UA level is essential for guiding urate-lowering therapies (ULTs) and dietary control (4). However, patients with gout often exhibit poor treatment adherence, necessitating reliable and personalized tools for longitudinal UA tracking. Now, SUA measurement via blood sampling remains the gold standard for diagnosis and management, but its invasiveness and inconvenience hinder its use for daily or continuous monitoring (5). To overcome this limitation, various research on wearable electronics has been conducted to enable continuous physiological monitoring (6–23). UA has been investigated in multiple noninvasive biofluids—including tears, saliva, and sweat—as alternative matrices for surrogate assessment of systemic UA levels (24–29). Among these biofluids, tears have emerged as a particularly attractive matrix for metabolic monitoring. Tear fluid is continuously and passively secreted under basal conditions, enabling stable sampling without active user intervention. In contrast, saliva composition can be influenced by food intake and hydration status, while sweat-based sensing typically requires external stimulation such as physical activity or elevated temperature to induce sufficient sweat production. Tear composition reflects systemic metabolites through diffusion from ocular tissues and the surrounding vasculature. From a practical perspective, the ocular surface offers an interface for seamless integration with contact lens platforms, which are already widely adopted for daily vision correction and can support long-duration, unobtrusive wear. These features position tears as a compelling biofluid for comfortable, longitudinal monitoring of metabolic biomarkers. Despite these advantages, prior studies on tear UA (TUA) analysis have relied on ex vivo tear collection, followed by laboratory-based assays, and real-time in vivo sensing of UA directly from the ocular surface has not yet been demonstrated (29).

Here, we present a wearable tear-based UA monitoring system implemented in a smart contact lens (SCL) form factor. This device enables wireless, real-time detection of TUA under diverse physiological and lifestyle conditions (e.g., gout state, ULT administration, and dietary intake), providing a dynamic UA profile without the need for tear collection or laboratory-based analysis (Fig. 1A). Using the resulting dataset, we comprehensively investigated the correlation between SUA and TUA in rabbits and human participants, including individuals with hyperuricemia and gout. We then developed regression models incorporating individualized lag time correction to enhance predictive accuracy.

Building upon these relationships, we further established a lifestyle-informed, predictive digital twin framework that simulates individual UA dynamics using tear-derived physiological signatures. In its current implementation, this framework functions as a personalized model trained on prior sensor data, enabling the accurate prediction of daily UA fluctuations without continuous lens wear. This approach represents an initial layer of a digital twin paradigm and can be extended toward continuously updated, bidirectional digital twins through future integration of physiological feedback. This integrated framework offers a noninvasive and unobtrusive approach for long-term UA management and supports a previously unidentified paradigm for comfortable, personalized gout management.

RESULTS

Wireless SCL UA biosensor for TUA detection

Tears, being less affected by external contamination and containing various biomarkers, are emerging as a promising biofluid for UA monitoring. On the basis of plasma leakage through the blood-tear barrier, various biomarkers show a positive correlation in their blood-tear dynamics (fig. S1) (30–35). Several reports have clarified the correlation between SUA and TUA, with elevated TUA levels in hyperuricemic patients, suggesting tears as an alternative biofluid for noninvasive SUA monitoring (26, 30, 36–38). However, no research has yet guaranteed the accuracy of using TUA instead of SUA for health care applications, as traditional tear collection methods (Schirmer’s strips, cellulose sponges, and capillary tubes) have substantial drawbacks (table S1) (29, 39–42). These methods can irritate the conjunctiva, induce reflex tears, and provide only momentary measurements, making it difficult to establish a reliable correlation with SUA levels, and are not suitable for continuous monitoring, which is essential for the effective management of gout.

To overcome these limitations, SCLs have been developed as a promising wearable form factor for noninvasive biomedical devices by allowing comfort and continuous contact with tears (43–46). The embedded biosensor enables reliable and continuous tracking of biochemical dynamics in tears. Various tear biomarkers (cholesterol, glucose, and lactate) have been monitored with SCL biosensors, supporting the management of chronic diseases such as diabetes and hyperlipidemia (47–49).

Our SCL UA biosensor measures TUA and wirelessly transmits data to a smartphone via Near Field Communication (NFC) technology without a power supply. The SCL UA biosensor comprises a UA biosensor, a serpentine antenna, and an NFC chip embedded within a silicone elastomer using commercial soft contact lens materials (Fig. 1A and fig. S2). Details of the fabrication procedure are in Materials and Methods and the Supplementary Materials. The UA biosensor uses an electrochemical system with a uricase modification for selective, continuous, and repetitive TUA detection. The enzymatic reaction of UA generates allantoin and hydrogen peroxide, and the resulting hydrogen peroxide is electrochemically reduced at the Prussian blue catalyst. For quantitative sensing, a detection potential of −0.2 V (versus Ag/AgCl) was selected, at which the reduction current of hydrogen peroxide exhibits a stable and concentration-dependent response to UA (Fig. 1, B and C, and fig. S3) (25, 27, 50). The biosensor showed high sensitivity (1% current change per 8.6 μM), a signal-to-noise ratio of 43.31 at 100 μM, and a detection limit of 0.98 μM. Figure 1 (D and E) displays a calibration curve for 0 to 200 μM UA (20 μM increments), covering the physiological UA range in human tears (25 to 150 μM) (29, 51). The biosensor exhibited a sensitivity of 3.70 μA·μM−1·cm−2, which is comparable to or higher than those reported for UA sensors in other biofluids (table S2).

The sensing performance was retained over 7 days and across pH ranges of human tears (6.5 to 7.6) (52). Figure 1 (F and G) demonstrates the robust performance of the UA biosensor across physiological temperatures (20° to 40°C) and its high selectivity for UA over other major tear constituents. In addition, to evaluate the stability of the biosensor configuration, continuous measurements were performed at 100 μM UA for 3 min, during which the current level remained stable without noticeable fluctuation, confirming reliable sensing performance. This result indicates that the sensing signal is maintained without substantial potential drift under the operating conditions. Furthermore, repeated measurements of 100 μM UA conducted at 1-min intervals demonstrated stable sensing performance for at least 3 hours at 35°C. These results indicate that cumulative polarization or potential instability does not affect analytical accuracy even under long-term wearable operation conditions (fig. S4).

For the wireless operation, the NFC chip and the antenna were integrated with the biosensor, enabling battery-free function. The NFC chip applies a constant −0.2 V to the biosensor via the digital-to-analog converter (DAC), while the current-to-digital converter (I2D) reads the current response (fig. S5). Data are transmitted to a smartphone through the antenna and visualized on a smartphone application. Communication occurs every second, sending average values from 10-s measurements. Real-time measurement with a 100 μM UA solution, tested on a mannequin’s eye, confirmed successful communication with the smartphone (fig. S6).

In vivo correlation analysis of SUA and TUA with rabbit models

For in-depth correlation analysis between SUA and TUA, we conducted an in vivo demonstration with rabbit models (Fig. 2A). The study was designed to monitor UA fluctuations by injecting different dosages of hyperuricemia-inducing agents (HIAs) and ULT. As illustrated in Fig. 2B, the rabbits were divided into three groups, each comprising three rabbits: group 1 (UA spike model), group 2 (hyperuricemia model), and group 3 (hyperuricemia with ULT model). To induce hyperuricemia in the rabbit models, we administered oxonic acid potassium salt (OAPS; 100 mg kg−1) and hypoxanthine (HX; 500 mg kg−1) (53). OAPS acts as a uricase inhibitor, thereby preventing the breakdown of UA and leading to its accumulation (54). In addition, HX is metabolized by xanthine oxidase into xanthine, which is then further converted into UA, contributing to elevated UA levels in the rabbits (55). These HIAs effectively elevated the UA levels in the rabbits, inducing hyperuricemia. For the ULT, rasburicase was used with a dosage of 0.2 mg kg−1. Rasburicase is a uricase recombinant that catalyzes the conversion of UA to allantoin, substantially reducing UA levels (56). The rabbits fasted for 12 hours before wearing the SCL. SUA and TUA levels were measured every 10 min and every minute, respectively, for 5 hours (movie S1).

Accurate estimation of SUA from TUA requires a strong correlation between the two, but conventional tear collection methods (e.g., Schirmer’s strips and capillary tubes) often induce reflex tears, disrupting this correlation. To ensure reliable TUA monitoring, it is essential to measure basal tears without reflex interference. Despite the SCL’s low-irritation design using commercial soft contact lens materials, external factors such as the insertion process may still cause reflex tearing. To address this, we evaluated the effects of reflex tears by simultaneously measuring tear volume (TV) and TUA using the SCL biosensor (fig. S7). Both TUA and TV rose quickly and returned to baseline shortly, while the SUA levels remained stable throughout the experiment. This transient spike is attributed to UA leakage into the interstitial space or epithelial cells, which can distort the SUA-TUA correlation (32, 57, 58). In human participants, TUA and TV returned to baseline within 5.0 and 4.5 min, respectively. Similarly, in rabbits, recovery to basal levels occurred within ~4.5 min for TUA and 4.2 min for TV (fig. S8). Based on these observations, all TUA measurements were conducted at least 10 min after SCL insertion to ensure basal tear conditions and improve measurement accuracy.

Physiological TUA levels for rabbits are not well established from previous research. To address this gap, we first confirmed the healthy status of fasting rabbits by measuring SUA levels, which were 43 ± 4 μM (n = 9), within the reported normal range of 50.4 ± 6.0 μM (59). We then quantified TUA levels in the same rabbits using the SCL biosensor, obtaining a value of 27 ± 2 μM (fig. S9; n = 9). For comparison, tear samples collected using a microcapillary tube showed 37 ± 2 μM (n = 9), which closely matched the value obtained with the gold standard enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) method (37 ± 2 μM, n = 9). To further validate the reliability of UA quantification in the tear matrix, spike-and-recovery experiments were performed using the ELISA assay. The recovery values ranged from 92 to 104%, confirming that the tear matrix does not substantially interfere with UA quantification (table S3). The higher UA concentrations measured in collected tears compared to SCL-based measurements further suggest that conventional collection methods induce reflex tearing, thereby elevating measured TUA levels.

As a control group, healthy rabbits in a fasting state were monitored continuously for 5 hours without any food intake (fig. S10). SUA and TUA levels remained stable (~32 and 23 μM, respectively) and were in the normal UA range. In group 1, a single administration of HIAs was administered to provoke a transient increase in UA levels (Fig. 2C). Both UA levels showed a similar response to the control groups at the beginning of the experiment. Then, the UA levels gradually increased after administration, reaching a maximum level at around 2 hours, and returned to baseline gradually (Fig. 2D). This phenomenon is attributed to the presence of uricase in rabbits, not in humans. Uricase breaks down UA into allantoin, indicating that the injected HIA dosage was insufficient to sustain the elevated UA levels (60). All three rabbits exhibited similar UA dynamics in both serum and tears, although the rate and magnitude varied due to individual metabolism despite administering the same dosage per body weight (fig. S11) (61). TUA level reflects the SUA level with a certain time delay, called “lag time,” that represents the time needed for blood-TUA diffusion (62–64). To quantitatively analyze the correlation degree between SUA and TUA levels, the Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated for each rabbit by varying the lag time value. The personalized lag time was defined as the lag time that resulted in the highest correlation coefficient between SUA and TUA levels for each individual (Fig. 2E and fig. S12).

To establish a sustained hyperuricemia model in group 2, HIAs were administered three times at 1-hour intervals. All three of group 2 rabbits maintained abnormally elevated SUA and TUA levels throughout the measurement, presenting a hyperuricemic state, contrasting with group 1 (Fig. 2F and fig. S13). Group 2 also showed a high Pearson correlation coefficient between SUA and TUA levels, confirming that the strong correlation was maintained even in a hyperuricemic state (fig. S14). To evaluate the therapeutic response in hyperuricemic rabbits, Group 3 received an intravenous injection of rasburicase 1 hour after the third HIA administration to lower elevated UA levels. The trend of UA variations was the same as group 2 until the treatment was applied and then showed a sharp decrease in SUA levels by half within 10 min after the rasburicase injection (Fig. 2G and fig. S15). TUA levels followed a similar trend, showing a rapid and marked reduction in response to rasburicase. Both SUA and TUA levels reached normal levels 1 hour after the injection and remained stable. These results demonstrate that the UA-lowering effect of rasburicase is promptly and reliably reflected in TUA, consistent with observations in other groups, with a high Pearson correlation coefficient (fig. S16). Figure 2 (H and I) compares the dynamic changes in TUA and SUA levels at 1-hour intervals, demonstrating that TUA reliably reflects SUA dynamics across physiological concentrations. This supports the potential of tears as a noninvasive alternative for UA monitoring in the diagnosis and management of hyperuricemia and related diseases such as gout. Each rabbit exhibited a distinct personalized lag time that yielded the highest Pearson correlation coefficient, ranging from 10 to 15 min (Fig. 2J). The TUA/SUA partitioning ratio was 0.2930, indicating a strong positive correlation between the two parameters (Fig. 2K). On the basis of this relationship, we defined an estimated SUA (ESUA) value for rabbits, described by the following linear regression equationTUA(μM)=0.2930×ESUArabbit(μM)+28.5895

This regression model enables reliable estimation of SUA values from TUA and provides the mathematical foundation for the ESUA. The ESUA values closely approximated the measured SUA concentrations, validating the model’s predictive capability (Fig. 2L). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis for SUA thresholds of 100 and 200 μM yielded area under the curve (AUC) values above 0.9 (Fig. 2M), confirming the reliability of the SCL UA biosensor for hyperuricemia/gout diagnosis and potential treatment monitoring. An AUC value of 0.9 or higher is generally considered indicative of excellent diagnostic performance (65).

After the rabbit UA monitoring study, histological analyses of rabbit corneas were performed to assess biocompatibility. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of full-thickness corneal sections revealed no structural abnormalities across the corneal layers. In addition, higher-magnification analysis of the corneal epithelium, the region in direct contact with the SCL, using cytokeratin 3 (CK3) immunostaining and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) nuclear staining showed no observable morphological alterations, indicating that SCL wear did not induce adverse effects on corneal cells (Fig. 2N). Furthermore, cytotoxicity testing on human retinal pigment epithelial cells showed a cell viability of ~99%, with no significant difference compared to the control group, confirming the biocompatibility of the SCL (fig. S17). In contrast, H&E staining of kidney tissues from rabbits subjected to repeated HIA administrations showed pathological features such as tubulointerstitial atrophy and glomerulosclerosis, demonstrating effective induction of hyperuricemia and renal injury (fig. S18) (66).

In depth correlation analysis of SUA and TUA levels with human participants

A pilot study with 20 participants (10 healthy individuals and 10 patients with gout) was conducted to monitor UA fluctuation in response to food intake and to analyze the correlation between SUA and TUA using the developed SCL UA biosensors (Fig. 3A, figs. S19 to S21, and movie S2). Participants were grouped on the basis of their gout diagnosis and treatment status: group 1 (healthy participants), group 2 (patients with gout without ULT), and group 3 (patients with gout receiving ULT with febuxostat) (Fig. 3B). Participants fasted for more than 12 hours to establish baseline measurements uninfluenced by recent food intake. They then consumed a standardized meal designed to elevate UA levels, consisting of 100 g of chicken breast (containing 22 g of protein) and 355 ml of cola (containing 65 g of sugar). Fructose in cola is metabolized in the liver, promoting UA generation, while protein in chicken breast contributes to UA production via purine metabolism (67–69). Febuxostat, a xanthine oxidase inhibitor, reduces UA synthesis and was used for ULT (70).

Initial 3-hour measurements in fasting healthy participants showed stable SUA and TUA (fig. S22). Across all 10 subjects, SUA and TUA levels remained below the known normal thresholds (417 and 119 μM, respectively), serving as a control baseline (71). After food intake, group 1 showed gradual increases in both SUA and TUA, followed by decreases returning to baseline levels (Fig. 3, C and D, and fig. S23). This pattern reflects typical postprandial UA metabolism, including transient spikes followed by natural breakdown and excretion. Personalized lag times between SUA and TUA were calculated as in the rabbit model (Fig. 3E and fig. S24). In group 2, SUA and TUA were already elevated above normal thresholds in the fasting state. Following the standardized meal, both levels rose further and later decreased but still remained abnormally high after considering lag time (Fig. 3F and figs. S25 and S26). This consistent trend across group 2 patients indicated chronic and uncontrolled hyperuricemia. Group 3, treated with febuxostat, maintained SUA and TUA levels within the normal range before and after the meal, indicating effective UA control during both fasting and postprandial conditions (Fig. 3G and figs. S27 and S28). TUA and SUA showed strong positive correlations in all groups (fig. S26).

Consistent UA fluctuation trends were observed across all participants (Fig. 3, H and I, and fig. S29). A strong positive correlation was confirmed with a TUA-to-SUA ratio of 0.4489, indicating the potential for SUA estimation from TUA (Fig. 3J). On the basis of this, the ESUA was derived using the following regression.TUA(μM)=0.4489×ESUAhuman(μM)−67.2792

The ratio between the measured SUA and ESUA was 0.9999, demonstrating near-perfect agreement across all participants (Fig. 3K). In addition, group-specific ESUA regression models were developed to reflect physiological variability, which could inform the development of more refined personalized prediction frameworks (fig. S30). These models provide insight into intergroup differences in prediction performance and may serve as a foundation for developing more refined, personalized ESUA estimation frameworks in broader populations. ROC analysis for hyperuricemia diagnosis (threshold: 417 μM) yielded high accuracy (AUC = 0.9648; Fig. 3L), supporting the diagnostic utility of the SCL UA biosensor.

Further analysis included metrics such as TUA variation, rate of increase, and AUC extracted from UA plots (fig. S31). Group 3 exhibited significantly lower TUA variations and a slower rate of increase compared to groups 1 and 2, indicating reduced postprandial UA fluctuations and effective control by febuxostat. Conversely, group 2 displayed the highest AUC, reflecting persistent hyperuricemia throughout the study. Lag time analysis revealed an average of 15.6 min in healthy participants (n = 10) and 10.2 min in patients with gout (n = 10), suggesting an approximate 5-min reduction in lag time among the patient group (Fig. 3M). This shorter lag is attributed to inflammatory responses triggered by elevated UA, which reduces the expression of intracellular tight junction proteins. This disruption impairs barrier function, widens intercellular gaps, and may accelerate UA diffusion from blood to tears (Fig. 3N) (72–75). Thus, lag time could serve as a biomarker distinguishing patients with gout from healthy individuals. Conjunctival impression cytology (CIC) supported this, showing disrupted conjunctival cell membranes and higher CIC grades in patients with gout compared to healthy controls, indicating compromised cell integrity (Fig. 3O and figs. S32 and S33).

Digital biomarker–based differentiation of gout status using TUA dynamics

We developed digital biomarker models to classify gout status and evaluate treatment adherence using data collected from 20 participants (Fig. 4A). The initial models were trained using measured TUA and SUA levels. To improve classification performance, we subsequently incorporated features derived from the TUA time series, including intraindividual variation, AUC, rate of increase, and temporal lag time between TUA and SUA. The model performance was evaluated using a subject-level grouped nested cross-validation framework to avoid data leakage between training and evaluation sets.

To assess diagnostic utility, digital biomarker classifiers were trained to distinguish group 1 (healthy) from group 2 (patients with gout) (Fig. 4B). When using either TUA or SUA as individual input features, the model achieved classification accuracies of 84.21 and 80.70% for TUA and 93.16 and 100% for SUA, respectively (Fig. 4, C and D). Combining both TUA and SUA inputs improved classification accuracy to 94.74 and 94.74% (Fig. 4E). Feature importance analysis revealed comparable contributions from TUA and SUA, supporting the utility of TUA as a reliable biomarker for distinguishing individuals with elevated UA levels due to gout (Fig. 4F).

We next evaluated treatment adherence by classifying group 2 (untreated patients with gout) and group 3 (patients undergoing ULT) (Fig. 4G). Classification using either TUA or SUA as a single input feature yielded accuracies of 91.23 and 100% for TUA and 100 and 99.25% for SUA, respectively (Fig. 4, H and I). When combined, the classification accuracy reached 100% for both groups (Fig. 4J), with feature importance scores of 0.6 for SUA and 0.4 for TUA (Fig. 4K), indicating that both contributed meaningfully to model performance. These findings demonstrate the potential of TUA for evaluating treatment adherence and optimizing therapeutic strategies.

Last, we trained a multiclass classifier to differentiate all three groups (healthy, untreated gout, and treated gout) (Fig. 4L). Using only TUA and SUA levels, classification accuracies were 63.68, 94.74, and 42.11%, with lower accuracy observed between groups 1 and 3 due to overlapping UA levels (Fig. 4, M and N). Incorporating additional TUA-derived temporal features (e.g., lag time, variation, and AUC) substantially improved classification performance for the three-group task (Fig. 4O). The model achieved classification accuracies of 100, 94.74, and 100% for groups 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Feature importance analysis indicated that AUC contributed most strongly to model performance, followed by SUA level and lag time, with additional contributions from TUA level and variation (Fig. 4P). These findings suggest that these dynamic features could serve as potential digital biomarkers for gout diagnosis and management, particularly in distinguishing healthy individuals from patients under treatment.

Lifestyle-informed digital twin for device-free prediction of daily UA dynamics

To evaluate the feasibility of predicting TUA levels without the need for continuous device wear, we developed a lifestyle-informed, predictive digital twin model trained on TUA data obtained from the SCL (Fig. 5A). Six representative daily scenarios were defined across three major activity states, including exercising (jogging and walking), eating (high-purine diet and low-purine diet), and relaxing (sitting and lying down). Each scenario was performed under controlled conditions, repeated 10 times for 2-hour sessions (Fig. 5, B to G, and fig. S34). The physiological data collected during these sessions were used to train the digital twin model. Subsequently, each participant followed a structured daily routine composed of combinations of these scenarios. To capture real-time behavioral context, participants wore Ray-Ban Meta Wayfarer glasses that continuously recorded their activities (movie S3 and fig. S35). The use of wearable artificial intelligence (AI) glasses enabled accurate recognition of contextual behaviors, enhancing the model’s ability to associate lifestyle patterns with biochemical changes, thereby improving the accuracy and personalization of TUA prediction.

To assess the reliability of context-aware UA prediction, we compared digital twin–based TUA predictions with actual TUA measurements across various dual activity combinations. The models exhibited strong agreement with measured values, with a mean squared error (MSE) below 2 μM (Fig. 5H). On the basis of these findings, full-day TUA fluctuation patterns were inferred for each subject from 8 a.m. to midnight (Fig. 5I and fig. S36). Each participant was assigned a personalized digital twin model, resulting in highly individualized predictions. The correlation between predicted and measured TUA values was high, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.9958 across all participants (Fig. 5J).

These findings highlight the potential of our digital twin–based framework for accurate, device-free prediction of TUA and SUA based on lifestyle patterns. Although only representative scenarios were tested in this study, the selection was guided by relevance and prevalence in daily life. Comprehensive coverage of all possible lifestyle combinations would require data from a substantially larger participant pool and long-term real-world monitoring, which extends beyond the practical scope of this demonstration. Nonetheless, the results strongly support the viability of this approach and provide a robust foundation for future expansion to broader population groups and a wider range of behavioral contexts.

DISCUSSION

Tear-based monitoring offers substantial potential as a noninvasive strategy for UA tracking, eliminating the discomfort, infection risk, and inconvenience associated with blood sampling. Despite this promise, several unresolved challenges have hindered its practical translation, particularly in the context of hyperuricemia and gout management. These challenges include: (i) a lack of comprehensive evidence validating the dynamic relationship between TUA and SUA, (ii) insufficient consideration of individual variability and disease-specific physiological factors affecting TUA-SUA correlation, and (iii) the practical constraint of requiring continuous SCL wear for extended periods. These limitations are especially problematic for patients with gout, whose effective care demands precise, real-time UA monitoring to prevent and manage acute flares.

To overcome these barriers, this study presents a device-free UA prediction framework using digital twin models enabled by initial SCL-based tear monitoring. Our study presents the first demonstration of a wireless, battery-free SCL platform capable of continuous TUA monitoring under real-world conditions. By integrating this data into AI models, we establish a previously unidentified paradigm for noninvasive, personalized UA estimation.

Through in vivo experiments in both rabbits and human participants, including individuals with and without gout, we confirmed a strong correlation between TUA and SUA levels. Notably, we identified individual lag times between tear and SUA changes and found that patients with gout consistently exhibited shorter lag times than healthy subjects. This observation suggests that gout-related disruption of the blood-tear barrier may facilitate faster diffusion of UA from blood into tears, providing physiological insight and a potential disease-specific biomarker. Building on these findings, we developed an ESUA regression model, enabling accurate SUA prediction from real-time TUA inputs.

Beyond regression, we demonstrated the utility of TUA-derived dynamic features—including intraindividual variation, AUC, rate of increase, and lag time—in improving machine learning (ML)–based classification performance. These features substantially enhanced the identification of gout presence and treatment adherence, supporting their relevance as meaningful digital biomarkers. Among them, lag time showed particular promise as a quantitative index of disease severity and progression. However, an important limitation of the classification analysis is the relatively small sample size of untreated patients with gout (group 2, n = 3). This imbalance warrants caution in interpretation, as it may reduce the statistical power of intergroup comparisons and limit the robustness and generalizability of the ML-based classification performance. Accordingly, the classification results should be considered preliminary. Future studies involving larger and more balanced cohorts will be necessary to validate these findings and are expected to further strengthen the predictive capability of the proposed model.

We further extended this framework to a device-free, scenario-informed digital twin framework by integrating lifestyle context such as dietary intake and physical activity. This approach successfully predicted daily UA fluctuations without requiring continuous SCL use, reducing a key barrier to long-term adherence. By balancing sensing fidelity with user comfort, it enables unobtrusive and personalized metabolic health management. Furthermore, this integrated architecture provides a scalable foundation for future integration of real-time updating and adaptive physiological feedback, offering a noninvasive and user-friendly strategy for long-term UA management and supporting a previously unidentified paradigm for personalized gout care.

In summary, our work provides a fully integrated platform that combines SCL-based biosensing, in vivo physiological correlation analysis, and digital twin–based device-free prediction of SUA. While demonstrated using UA as a model biomarker, the framework is readily adaptable to a wide range of tear-accessible analytes, providing a generalizable strategy for noninvasive, personalized disease monitoring and offering potential for expansion to broader metabolic biomarkers.

Fabrication of SCL UA biosensor

The UA biosensor is an electrochemical biosensor based on a two-electrode system. As in previous studies, Cr/Au electrodes were used for the working electrode (WE), reference electrode (RE), and contact pads of the UA biosensor (fig. S37A). A parylene passivation layer was applied to cover the connection lines between the electrodes and contact pads (47, 49). Following ultraviolet ozone treatment for 15 min, the WE and RE areas were screen-printed with PB and Ag/AgCl ink using a manual squeegee (WE: 0.785 mm2, RE: 3.448 mm2). The electrodes were then annealed at 110°C for 15 min. A 10 μl of PEO solution [PEO/methanol/deionized (DI) water = 1:5:25 weight % (wt %)] was drop-casted onto the electrodes and dried overnight at 70°C. This PEO layer served as a protection layer to avoid damage in the following lens integration process.

An anisotropic conductive film (ACF) was prepared by mixing SIS, toluene, quintone, and EGaIn in a weight ratio of 1:9:0.3:11 in a 10-ml vial for robust stretchable interconnect UA biosensor electrodes and NFC chip on the soft contact lens. The mixture was sonicated at 40% power (130 W) for 1 hour to break down the EGaIn into particles (76). The resulting mixture was spin-coated onto release paper at 1500 rpm for 40 s, forming a 15-μm-thick film. After cutting the ACF film, the ACF was applied to the contact pads of the UA biosensor to connect with the NFC chip (200-μm thick after polishing; fig. S37B). The serpentine antenna was designed with a width of 55 μm, spacing of 35 μm, and 10 turns using a high-frequency structure simulator (HFSS; Ansys). The serpentine antenna was fabricated with PI/Cu layers of 12/12 μm thickness. The serpentine antenna was positioned at the edge of the SCL, ensuring that wearing the SCL did not obstruct the participants’ vision. Subsequently, a 10-μm-thick parylene layer was deposited using chemical vapor deposition (NRPC-500, Nuri-tech) for passivating serpentine antenna. To expose the contact pads on the antenna, the parylene layer was removed by scraping with a needle. The integrated device was further embedded into a mold to form a contact lens filled with silicone elastomer, which was cured using UV exposure (10 mW cm−2, 14 min) at a pressure of 313 kPa.

Before hydrogel formation, the SCL surface was activated by oxygen plasma treatment using a reactive ion etching (RIE) system (O2, 40 standard cubic centimeters per minute (sccm), 200 mtorr, 100 W, 60 s). The photoinitiator solution consisted of 10% benzophenone in ethanol, while the prepolymer solution was prepared by mixing 200 μl of 10% α-ketobutyric acid solution with 9.8 ml of 2 M acrylamide aqueous solution. The plasma-treated SCL was immersed in the photoinitiator solution for 1 min, rinsed with ethanol, and then transferred to the prepolymer solution. UV exposure (8 mW cm−2, 20 min) was applied to initiate photopolymerization and form a crosslinked hydrogel coating on the SCL surface. To remove residual reagents, the coated SCL underwent sequential washing and UV treatments. The lens was immersed in DI water and irradiated with UV light for 10 min, followed by ethanol rinsing. Additional 5- and 3-min UV exposures were applied with intermediate rinsing steps. The lens was then sonicated in DI water for 20 s and stirred in DI water at 30°C and 300 rpm for 1 hour to remove unreacted monomers and stabilize the hydrogel layer.

After carefully removing the silicone elastomer covering the electrode regions of the UA biosensor to allow direct contact with tears, the PEO layer was dissolved by immersing the SCL in 80°C DI water for 3 hours. This process ensures the complete removal of the PEO layer, fully exposing the electrodes for subsequent uricase modification. The uricase containing mixture was prepared by mixing 2.0 μl of uricase solution (4.5 mg ml−1), 1 μl of 1 wt % BSA solution, 0.5 μl of nuclease-free water, and 1 μl of 1 wt % chitosan solution (in 0.1 M acetic acid). Then, 1 μl of the mixture was drop-casted on the WE and dried for 12 hours under controlled conditions (4°C with 10% humidity). Subsequently, 0.5 μl of a 0.00315% glutaraldehyde solution was applied on WE to crosslink the uricase enzyme with BSA and chitosan. The modified electrodes were dried for an additional 12 hours under controlled conditions (4°C with 10% humidity).

Characterization of the SCL UA biosensor performance

To confirm the sensing capability of the UA biosensor in tear-like environments, an artificial tear solution was prepared by mixing NaCl (5.5 g liter−1) and KCl (1.5 g liter−1) in diluted saline. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and chronoamperometry (CA) tests were performed using a potentiostat (PMC-CHS08A, Princeton Applied Research) controlled by the Versa Studio program. For both tests, 50 μl of the artificial tear solution, with UA concentrations ranging from 0 to 200 μM in 20 μM increments, was applied onto the electrodes of the UA biosensor. A 2-min incubation time was allowed after each concentration change to ensure proper interaction before measurement. CV tests were performed with the applied voltage swept between +0.4 and −0.3 V at a scan rate of 50 mV s−1 for 10 cycles, determining a reduction potential of −0.2 V (versus Ag/AgCl). CA measurements were conducted for 1 min at each measurement, and the calibration curve was calculated by averaging the current values during the last 20 s when the current stabilized. On the basis of these data, the sensitivity and limit of detection were calculated (Supplementary Materials). A selectivity test was conducted to evaluate the response of the UA biosensor in tear environment by spiking the solution with 100 μM UA, 0.5 mM glucose, 0.3 mM cholesterol, and 0.4 mM ascorbic acid to measure current changes. These concentrations were selected on the basis of the known concentrations of these substances in human tears (30, 77, 78). To examine the pH stability of the biosensor, the artificial tear solution’s pH was adjusted from 6.5 to 7.5 by gradually adding 0.1 M KCl while monitoring with a pH meter (Orion STAR A2116, Thermo Fisher Scientific), simulating the normal pH range of human tears. Furthermore, to assess the long-term operational stability of the UA biosensor, a stability test was performed by storing the biosensor at 4°C and measuring its response over a 7-day period.

To wirelessly monitor UA levels using the SCL UA biosensor, an NFC-based system was employed using the antenna to transmit and receive power (79). When the SCL is tagged with a smartphone, the DAC on the embedded NFC chip applies a −0.2 V (versus Ag/AgCl) to the WE of the UA biosensor, generating a UA sensing current response. This current is digitized by the I2D, which processes the signal according to precalibrated values stored in the NFC chip. The digitized signal is subsequently transmitted to a custom-designed smartphone application. After tagging, the application initiates a 10-s stabilization period before collecting the response of the UA concentration. To validate the functionality of this real-time wireless communication system before applying the SCL to human subjects, a mannequin test was conducted to simulate UA detection. The SCL was placed on the eye of a mannequin, and 20 μl of a 100 μM UA solution was carefully applied to the mannequin’s eye surface. The SCL was then tagged with a smartphone at a distance of 5 mm.

Characterization of the serpentine antenna

The antenna—with 10 turns, a line width of 55 μm, and a spacing of 35 μm—exhibited minimal resistance changes after 1000 stretching cycles at a rate of 3 mm s−1, confirming its effectiveness as a stretchable antenna (fig. S38). The resonance frequency, measured both before and after molding in connection with an NFC chip (50 pF), remained constant at 13.5 MHz, which is ideal for seamless wireless transmission with smartphones. The reflection coefficients (S11) were simulated using HFSS (Ansys) (fig. S39, A and B) and measured as −13.87 dB before molding and −13.40 dB afterward, demonstrating a consistent performance (fig. S39C). In addition, fig. S39D shows the changes in resonance frequency, while the system was immersed in artificial tears. The frequency remained stable with negligible variation over 14 days, indicating that communication with the smartphone would continue to be effective even after this period.

Rabbit animal study

All in-vivo tests were conducted according to the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Yonsei University and Yonsei University Health System (IACUC-A-202307-1699-01 and 2023-0198). A total of 12 male New Zealand white rabbits (Samtako Korea) that were 16 weeks old and weighed 2.5 to 3.0 kg were used for this study after 7 days of acclimatization. Each experiment was performed using biological replicates, with individual animals treated as independent units. All data collected from the experimental animals were analyzed without any exclusion. Animals were randomly allocated to experimental groups to minimize selection bias.

To distinguish between reflex tear and basal tear concentrations, the rabbits were anesthetized with a mixture of ketamine (35 mg kg−1) and xylazine (10 mg kg−1). After a 10-min stabilization period with the SCL applied, reflex tears were induced by gently applying a 1.4-gf von Frey filament to the conjunctiva, ensuring minimal bending (less than 5%) (47). Reflex tears were induced three times at 10-min intervals. TV was measured every minute using an SMTube, allowing for the observation of volumetric changes. TUA and TV were measured every minute, and SUA was measured every 10 min.

For additional reflex tear analysis, tear samples were collected using a microcapillary tube. The collected tear fluid was centrifuged to remove visible crystalline precipitates, and only the supernatant was used for subsequent analysis. For electrochemical measurements using the SCL sensor, 50 μl of collected tear fluid was deposited onto the biosensor surface.

For independent quantification of UA concentration, tear samples were analyzed using a commercial colorimetric UA ELISA assay kit. Each tear sample (10 μl) was added to a 96-well plate in duplicate, followed by 40 μl of buffer to obtain a final sample volume of 50 μl per well. A reaction mixture containing UA assay buffer, probe, and enzyme mix was then added to each well. The plate was incubated at 37°C for 30 min under light-protected conditions, after which the absorbance was measured using a microplate reader at 570 nm.

Furthermore, to investigate the correlation between UA levels in serum and tears, the rabbits were divided into three experimental groups, with three rabbits in each group. Group 1 consisted of rabbits with UA spike, induced by a single dose of HIAs. Group 2 included rabbits with sustained hyperuricemia, established by administering HIAs three times at 1-hour intervals. Group 3 received the same induction as group 2, followed by rasburicase treatment 1 hour after the final induction. After a 12-hour fasting period, rabbits were weighed, and HIAs were administered: HX orally (500 mg kg−1) in a 7-ml solution of 3% starch and OAPS (100 mg kg−1) in a mixed solvent of 1.5 ml of lanolin-liquid paraffin (3:2 volume ratio) injected subcutaneously into the back of the neck using a 17-gauge needle. Blood was collected every 10 min for UA quantification and TUA levels were measured every minute during a 5-hour period. Blood was collected from the marginal ear vein, transferred to a vacutainer serum separator tube (BD Vacutainer), and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min to separate the serum. SUA levels were quantified using a Roche Cobas C702 automatic biochemical analyzer. TUA levels were measured at 1-min intervals by tagging with a smartphone (Galaxy 6 plus edge, Samsung), after a 10-min stabilization period with the SCL applied.

For the histological analysis, the rabbits were euthanized with CO2 gas, and the kidney and cornea were excised. The excised tissues were fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4°C and then washed with PBS several times. The washed tissues were dehydrated in a graded series of ethanol, 45 min per step, and subsequently cleared with xylene (two times for 30 min each). The cleared tissues were embedded in paraffin to create blocks. The paraffin blocks were sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm using a microtome. The slides were immersed in xylene twice for 10 min to remove paraffin and then rehydrated by immersing in 100, 95, and 70% ethanol, and in distilled water, for 5 min each, sequentially. Following deparaffinization and rehydration, sections were stained with an H&E stain kit according to the protocol provided by Vector (Brussels, Belgium). The images of the section were obtained under a microscope (Olympus BX53M). For immunohistochemical staining, the sections were stained with mouse anti-CK3 overnight at 4°C and incubated with FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse antibody for 1 hour at room temperature. The nuclei were observed with a fluorescence microscope equipped with an antifade mounting medium with DAPI.

Human participants study

The protocol for the human participant study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Yonsei University (7001988-202403-HR-2164-02). Participants (male, age: 20 to 65) were recruited from Yonsei University and Yongin Severance Hospital through advertisements. All participants provided written informed consent before participation in the study and were informed that they could freely withdraw from the study at any time. The authors affirm that human research participants provided written informed consent for the publication of the images in figs. S2, S19, S20, S21, and S35 and movie S2.

To assess the difference between reflex tear and basal tear concentration in humans, the SCL was applied. TV was quantified using a SMTube through brief contact with the lower eyelid meniscus for 5-s intervals every minute, while TUA levels were simultaneously measured every minute using a smartphone. During the experiment, the SCL was replaced every 3 hours or earlier if any degradation in sensor performance was observed. SUA levels were measured every minute by a commercial UA meter (Benecheck Plus meter). A stabilized state was defined as a condition in which the volume of tears differed by less than 10% from the initial state. Reflex tears were induced by gently applying a 0.16-gf von Frey filament to the conjunctiva, ensuring minimal bending (less than 5%). This procedure was repeated three times at 10-min intervals. SUA levels were measured at 10-min intervals during this period.

Before human study, participants were screened for ocular conditions, including dry eye disease and other tear-related disorders. Among the 20 participants, 5 individuals reported symptoms consistent with dry eye. To evaluate the correlation between SUA and TUA levels, participants were divided into three groups. Group 1 consisted of 10 healthy participants, group 2 included 3 patients with gout who were not taking any gout medication, and group 3 comprised 7 patients with gout who were undergoing treatment with febuxostat (40 to 80 mg). The study began after all participants had fasted for at least 12 hours to ensure baseline measurements were not influenced by recent dietary intake. The SCL was applied to each participant’s eye for UA measurement, with a 10-min stabilization time. TUA levels were measured every minute using a smartphone placed near the eye. Participants also pricked their fingertips every 10 min to collect 15 μl of blood for UA measurement using a commercial UA meter. All participants in each group consumed a standardized meal as part of the study protocol, consisting of 100 g of chicken breast (containing 22 g of protein) and 355 ml of cola (containing 65 g of sugar) to induce a UA elevation. Before and 10 min after food intake, SUA levels were measured every 10 min for 3 hours, while TUA levels were continuously monitored every minute using the SCL.

To compare the conjunctival cells between patients with gout and healthy participants, CIC samples were collected. Cellulose acetate filter papers with a 0.2-μm pore size were cut into a triangular shape measuring 4 mm by 5 mm by 5 mm. These prepared filter papers were carefully pressed onto the inferior temporal bulbar conjunctiva, positioned 2 to 3 mm from the limbus, for 5 to 10 s to collect conjunctival samples. The collected samples were subsequently immersed in a solution comprising glacial acetic acid, 37% formaldehyde, and 70% ethanol in a 1:1:20 volume ratio. Last, the samples were stained with H&E for histological analysis.

To train the ML prediction model, TUA data were collected from five healthy participants under six predefined behavioral scenarios: jogging, walking, sitting, lying down, consuming a high-purine diet, and consuming a low-purine diet. Each scenario was repeated 10 times per participant, with measurements acquired every 5 min over a 120-min period. For prediction, the SCL is worn once to establish an individualized baseline TUA level. Participant behavior during data collection was continuously monitored using Ray-Ban Meta Wayfarer smart glasses to ensure adherence to assigned tasks and enable precise time stamp alignment. For the dietary scenario, the purine content of each meal was estimated on the basis of the measured weight of raw ingredients, following established nutritional databases (80, 81). Meals containing ≥133 mg of purines per serving were classified as high-purine (table S2). For physical activity scenarios, jogging was conducted for 30 min while maintaining the heart rate within zones 2 and 3 of each participant’s target heart rate range (82). Walking was performed for 30 min at zone 1 intensity. Sitting and lying down were maintained as resting conditions for the full 120-min monitoring period.

ML analysis

All training models were built using Python (v.3.12) based on the TUA data collected from 10 healthy participants and 10 patients with gout. To prevent potential data leakage and improve model generalization, model evaluation was performed using a subject-level grouped cross-validation framework, in which subject IDs were used as grouping variables to ensure that samples originating from the same participant were not simultaneously included in both training and evaluation folds. Hyperparameter optimization was performed using nested cross-validation, where model parameters were tuned within inner cross-validation folds using a predefined parameter grid, while final model performance was evaluated on independent outer folds. This approach provides a more reliable estimate of model generalization performance and mitigates the risk of overfitting, particularly for datasets with limited sample sizes.

The ML model was developed with two primary objectives: (i) classification between patients with gout and healthy participants and (ii) device-free UA prediction. Among various ML models evaluated, the extreme gradient boosting model outperformed other approaches, including linear and radial basis function kernel support vector machines, logistic regression, and random forest (fig. S40).

Statistical analysis

The error bars in all figures indicate the means ± SEM. The sample sizes (n) and statistical methods were specified in each figure legend. Statistical differences between two groups were analyzed using the unpaired Student’s t test. Significance levels were denoted as NS (P ≥ 0.05), * (P < 0.05), ** (P < 0.01), and *** (P < 0.001). The ROC curve was plotted using Python’s scikit-learn library, and the area under the ROC curve was computed to quantify the test’s ability to discriminate between positive and negative cases.
