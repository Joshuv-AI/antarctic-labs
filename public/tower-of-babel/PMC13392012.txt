Study on structural design and stress simulation of carbon fiber composite Type IV hydrogen storage cylinder
Authors: Jin Li, Chunjiang Zhao, Jian Gao, Hui Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A carbon fiber-wound Type IV hydrogen storage cylinder structure was designed. The winding layers were optimized based on grid theory, with particular attention to the thickness of the dome winding layer. The burst pressure of the hydrogen storage cylinder was predicted using a progressive damage model based on the Hassin damage theory. Additionally, the effects of the length-to-diameter ratio, ellipsoid ratio, polar ratio, and the metal boss support radius on the stress distribution of the hydrogen storage cylinder were investigated. The results show that the finite element analysis predicts a burst pressure of 162.76 MPa, which exceeds the design value of 157.5 MPa, indicating that the adopted layering method satisfies the design requirements. Increasing the ellipsoid ratio reduces the fiber stress in the cylinder. Under working pressure, the length-to-diameter ratio and polar ratio are negatively correlated with the stress in the winding layers. Furthermore, increasing the radius of the metal boss flange reduces the liner stress of the cylinder under working pressure. However, as the flange radius increases, the stress on its upper surface forms an upward-opening parabola, and the central region does not fully utilize its structural potential. These findings provide valuable insights for the practical engineering application of carbon fiber-wound Type IV hydrogen storage cylinders.

Introduction

Hydrogen energy, with its high calorific value, abundant reserves, and non-polluting nature after use, is gradually becoming a key focus of development for many countries1–3. One of the main applications of hydrogen energy is in new energy vehicles that utilize hydrogen as fuel. However, on-board hydrogen storage technology remains a critical factor limiting the development of hydrogen fuel cell vehicles4,5. High-pressure hydrogen storage cylinders are particularly crucial, as their working pressure directly affects the driving range of fuel cell vehicles. By increasing the hydrogen pressure, a longer driving range can be achieved. Currently, the vessels most commonly used to store hydrogen gas are composite pressure vessels with metallic liners (type III pressure vessels). To manufacture lightweight, high-pressure hydrogen storage cylinders and accelerate the commercialization of hydrogen in the automotive industry, Type IV high-pressure hydrogen storage cylinders have garnered widespread attention. These cylinders feature a liner composed of a metal head and a polymer liner6,7. The structural design and safety reliability of these cylinders are of paramount importance, particularly as they are also used in defense and aerospace applications.

Due to the complexity introduced by the layering of fiber winding, many design variables, including the geometry of the liner and the layering design, have a significant impact on the mechanical performance of the container. Considering the high standard working pressure of 70 MPa, the design of the cylinder needs to meet the highest safety standards. Therefore, studying the mechanical response of hydrogen storage cylinders is a complex task. In recent years, extensive research has been conducted on Type IV pressure cylinders.

Wang8 established a new method for predicting the shell thickness of composite cylinders, discovering that the dome thickness obtained from cubic spline functions closely matches actual measured thicknesses. Leh et al.9 developed a progressive model for predicting the burst pressure of Type IV hydrogen composite pressure vessels and proposed a multi-sequence dome layering prediction method that considers variations in winding angle and thickness. Hu et al.10 designed twelve different layering schemes for a 70 MPa Type IV hydrogen storage container, investigating the impact of different layering sequences on its ultimate strength. Their research indicated that hole-expanding winding can effectively reduce the fiber thickness at polar locations. Hu et al.11 also used finite element analysis to study the influence of angle and thickness in the reinforced dome section on the stress distribution across the dome cross-section. Furthermore, they researched weight reduction of the carbon fiber composite layers based on a dome reinforcement model. Nebe et al.12 examined the effects of layering sequences on the laminate quality, structural deformation, and ultimate burst pressure of composite pressure vessels. Zu et al.13 developed an optimization model for head partition reinforcement based on a multi-island genetic algorithm, targeting a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varphi$$\end{document}150 mm non-equal polar hole composite wound shell. By analyzing the stress distribution of the shell’s dome, they optimized the number and angle of reinforcement layers, obtaining optimal reinforcement angles and layer counts for different partitions. These results were validated through partition reinforcement experiments.

Sharma14 used ANSYS to analyze the effects of six different dome shapes—equal slope, hemisphere, paraboloid, ellipsoid with a height of 180 mm, and ellipsoids with heights of 120 mm and 75 mm—on burst pressure, and failure characteristics. It was inferred that ellipsoid (II) is the optimal dome profile for better vessel performance. Zhang15 utilized ABAQUS and Fe-safe software to numerically simulate the fatigue life of CHSV using the finite element method. The study examined the effects of ellipsoid ratio, maximum boundary cross-sectional thickness, and layering sequence on fatigue life. Zheng et al.16 compared the elastic-plastic behavior and failure modes of ellipsoidal and equivalent spherical heads, finding that the buckling resistance of the ellipsoidal head is greater than that of the equivalent spherical head. Zheng et al.17 further investigated the plastic failure pressure of steel ellipsoidal heads under internal pressure. By considering material hardening and geometric nonlinearity, they used the finite element method to calculate the plastic failure pressure of the ellipsoidal heads under internal pressure, and studied the impact of geometric and material parameters on this pressure. The results indicated that engineering tensile strength and thickness-to-diameter ratio significantly increase the plastic failure pressure. Park18 designed a pressure vessel with a dome shape adopting an equal-ellipsoidal curve for the plastic liner. Using finite element calculations, they determined the composite material thickness for the cylinder and dome sections that meet structural safety requirements and optimized the shape of the aluminum boss using the response surface method.

Kumar, G. C. et al.19 employed both finite element and analytical approaches to investigate the influence of composite thickness on the burst pressure of COPVs. The results revealed that COPVs with varying thicknesses of the metal liner and composite shell exhibited notable differences in deformation behavior at burst pressure. These findings indicate that the thickness of the constituent shells plays a critical role in determining the overall stiffness of COPVs.

CHO20 compared the stress distribution patterns of three different dome structures in hydrogen storage cylinders. Burst tests revealed differences in the burst locations among the three structures. Ramirez et al.21 proposed a continuous damage model capable of reproducing all modes of composite material damage. They compared the application of deterministic and probabilistic methods in predicting burst pressure. The results showed that the deterministic model underestimated the burst pressure, whereas the probabilistic model appeared to be more accurate. Hamza Yousaf et al.22 analyzed the structural behavior of pressure vessels using the finite element method and compared the theoretical failure results obtained by the Hashin damage criterion with the analysis results, verifying the damage response of composite cylinders. Zhang Qian et al.23 evaluated the safety of storage tanks using the maximum strain criterion, stress criterion, and a progressive damage model based on the Tsai-Wu criterion. Jebeli et al.24 used a bilinear cohesive law to simulate the effect of temperature changes during curing on the debonding of composite shells from polymer liners and bosses. They wrote a USDFLD subroutine to implement progressive failure analysis using the Puck failure criterion and a sudden material degradation model.

Farukoğlu, Ö. C. et al.25 investigated the effect of voids on internally pressurized tangentially fiber-reinforced tubes with both open and closed ends. Using two different failure criteria, Tsai–Wu and Hoffman, they determined the ultimate internal pressure values for tubes containing various void volume fractions.

Farukoğlu, Ö. C. et al.26 analyzed the elastic limit stresses and failure of a rotating variable-thickness disk made of fiber-reinforced material using the Tsai–Wu criterion. The results showed that under free–free boundary conditions, the deviation in angular velocities obtained from the lower bound (LB) and upper bound (UB) approaches was 28.75%.

The paper designs a 70 MPa carbon fiber-wound hydrogen storage cylinder structure, using grid theory as a foundation to optimize the design of the carbon fiber winding layers and the accumulation of dome thickness. A progressive damage model based on the Hashin damage theory is employed to predict the burst pressure of the hydrogen storage cylinder. The performance characteristics of Type IV cylinders under working pressure were analyzed. In addition, the effects of the liner length-to-diameter ratio, ellipsoid ratio, and polar ratio on the stress distribution within the cylinder were investigated, with particular emphasis on examining how variations in the radius of the metal boss flange influence the mechanical performance of the cylinder.

Dome design

For carbon fiber-wrapped hydrogen storage cylinders, commonly used dome designs include spherical dome, equilibrium tension dome, and ellipsoid dome28. Equilibrium tension dome require that the fiber stress in the winding layer on the dome is uniform throughout. However, due to complex manufacturing and winding processes, this type of dome is generally not adopted. Ellipsoidal dome are widely used due to their uniform stress distribution, good process performance, and efficient use of storage space.

Ellipsoidal ratio (the ratio of the major axis to the minor axis) value is a crucial parameter influencing the stress and deformation of ellipsoidal dome. The apex of the dome and the connection end where the dome meets the cylinder are critical parts of the ellipsoidal dome structure, providing essential analysis value and foundational basis for structural strength and deformation considerations.

Optimization of wrapping layer thickness of the boss

The cubic spline function algorithm utilized by Wang9 can accurately predict the thickness distribution of the fiber winding layer within two bandwidth ranges. Although the method allows for reducing layer thickness within a certain range using different parameters, thickness accumulation still occurs around the polar openings. To smooth out the thickness distribution on the dome, a helical layer with multiple winding angles is employed in production19. Therefore, in this study, a variable polar radius were applied to reduce thickness accumulation and fiber stacking on the dome part.

Finite element model

Due to the rotational symmetry of the hydrogen storage cylinder structure, a axisymmetric finite element model was employed. The model utilized cubic solid elements (C3D8R) to model the liner and metal boss, while continuous shell elements were used for the composite layers. The upper end of the model was fixed, and the lower end was left free to restrict axial displacement. Internal pressure was applied on the inner surface of the liner. The mesh size was 0.2 mm, resulting in a total of 751,994 elements in the model. Binding constraints were applied between the metal boss, plastic cylinder, and composite layers. Progressive damage model

The progressive damage calculation method10,24 employs the Hashin failure criteria to assess the failure of fibers and matrix, combined with the Quads criteria to evaluate interlaminar cohesive elements. The calculation process involves stress computation, failure assessment, and material degradation. Stress calculations are used in conjunction with the Hashin failure criteria to assess damage to each element. Upon damage occurrence, the stiffness of the corresponding elements is reduced according to the degradation rules based on the Camonho material degradation model30. Specific degradation criteria are detailed in Table 4. When the cumulative stiffness reduction of the entire model reaches a certain level, structural failure occurs. The magnitude of burst pressure is determined by the load corresponding to structural failure. The flowchart of the progressive damage calculation process is illustrated in Fig. 4.

Results and discussion

To assess the safety of the cylinder, an analysis based on the progressive damage model was conducted. Figures 5 and 6 depict the damage status of fibers and matrix. The finite element predicted burst pressure of the winding model is 162.76 MPa, exceeding the design value of 157.5 MPa. From the progressive damage calculation results, it is observed that structural failure in the fiber-wound pressure vessel occurs in the cylinder section, primarily due to extensive tensile fracture damage in the hoop winding fiber layers of the cylinder section. Moreover, upon structural failure of the pressure vessel, significant matrix damage occurs in the helical winding layers, accompanied by substantial delamination between the fiber-wound layers.

To investigate the material behaviors of the vessel, stress analyses were conducted, as depicted in Figs. 7 and 8. Figure 7 illustrates the Mises stress distribution on the boss and the liner. It can be observed that at an internal pressure of 70 MPa, the maximum stress on the metal boss is 291.6 MPa, which does not exceed the yield strength of materials. The high stress regions on the boss are primarily concentrated on the upper surface, particularly at the interface where the boss contacts the carbon fibers.

The maximum stress on the liner is 51.09 MPa, located at the dome section, which does not exceed the yield strength of materials. The stress levels at the ends of the plastic liner near the boss are higher compared to the stress in the middle of the cylinder body. The high stress regions are situated at the interface between the plastic liner and the boss.

Figure 8 shows the stress contour map of the fiber layer under operating pressure. It can be observed from Fig. 8 that the maximum stress on the fibers is 1433 MPa, occurring in the innermost layer, which is below the fiber’s tensile strength of 2150 MPa.

Figure 9 shows the stress distribution along the fiber direction for the first three layers of the hoop and helical winding layers under operating pressure. Figure 9a depicts the stress in the first three layers of the hoop winding under working pressure. The stress distribution curve exhibits a concave parabolic shape with higher values at both ends and lower values in the middle, consistent with findings in literature18. Specifically, the maximum stress occurs in the first layer at 1443.6 MPa, decreasing sequentially in the second and third layers. Figure 9b shows the stress in the first three layers of the helical winding under working pressure. The maximum principal stress in the helical layer appears at the transition between the cylinder and the end dome, reaching a peak of 1288.2 MPa, followed by intermediate positions in the dome section. The stress in the cylinder section is relatively smooth, whereas the dome section exhibits significant fluctuations due to variable angle winding. Similar to the hoop winding, the maximum stress is observed in the first layer of the helical winding, decreasing in subsequent layers.

Based on this analysis, it can be concluded that the model designed using the layering approach in this study meets the design requirements.

Impact of the radius of the boss flange on the stress of the fiber

Fig. 13Influence of metal boss flange radius on the fiber stress of cylinders.

Influence of metal boss flange radius on the fiber stress of cylinders.

Fixed electrode polar radius, we investigated the effect of varying metal boss support radii from 30 to 70 mm on cylinder stress, as shown in Fig. 13. Figure 13 illustrates the variation of maximum principal stress in the winding layer with flange radius under different boss support radius conditions. As the flange radius increases, there is a clear linear decrease in maximum principal stress in the winding layer. Specifically, at a flange radius of 30 mm, the hoop stress peaks at 1472 MPa, and the helical layer stress peaks at 1340 MPa. As the flange radius gradually increases to 70 mm, the maximum hoop stress decreases to 1250 MPa, and the maximum helical layer stress decreases to 1130 MPa. This demonstrates that different boss support radii significantly influence the stress distribution in the cylinder.

This phenomenon primarily arises because as the flange radius increases, the contact area between the flange and carbon fiber also increases. A larger contact area means the flange can share more load with the winding layer31, effectively reducing stress concentration in both the winding and layers. Therefore, choosing an appropriate boss support radius is crucial for optimizing cylinder design and enhancing its mechanical performance.

Stress analysis of extracted nodes along the support arc surface

Stresses extracted along the upper surface of the metal boss flange are illustrated in Fig. 15. At a flange radius of 30 mm, the minimum stress on the upper surface is 289.2 MPa, reaching a maximum of 300.24 MPa, indicating partial yielding on the surface. As the flange radius increases, the stresses on the upper surface gradually decrease, demonstrating that increasing the flange radius effectively disperses stress, enabling the flange upper surface to better withstand the load.

When the flange radius exceeds 60 mm, the stress curve on the upper surface exhibits a parabolic shape, with a significant decrease in stress in the middle section. This suggests that the middle section of the flange upper surface does not fully utilize its structural capacity, indicating potential design optimization opportunities. Particularly, at a flange radius of 70 mm, the minimum stress on the upper surface decreases further to 228.82 MPa, further confirming uneven stress distribution in the middle section and underutilization of its load-bearing capacity.

It is quite intriguing that at a flange radius of 50 mm, the stress distribution across the upper surface appears nearly uniform, resembling a series of parallel lines with evenly distributed stress values at each point. This indicates that at this radius, every point on the upper surface can fully utilize its structural capacity. Such stress distribution suggests that a flange radius of 50 mm is a relatively ideal design choice, capable of ensuring structural safety while maximizing the mechanical performance of the material.

Detailed analysis of the data in Fig. 15 reveals that selecting an appropriate flange radius is crucial for optimizing the mechanical performance of the metal boss flange. A smaller flange radius may withstand higher stress but risks partial yielding on the surface, while an excessively large flange radius could lead to uneven stress distribution in the middle section, failing to fully utilize the structural capacity and potentially resulting in material waste and increased weight of the cylinder. Therefore, in practical design, it is essential to consider the impact of flange radius on the stress distribution across the upper surface and choose the optimal flange radius to ensure both structural safety and efficiency.

Conclusions

A finite element model of a 70 MPa gas cylinder was established based on mesh theory. Employing a variable polar radii technique, the winding layer was optimized. Using progressive damage theory, the predicted burst pressure was 162.76 MPa, exceeding the designed burst pressure. Concurrently, an analysis of stress distribution in Type IV hydrogen storage cylinders was conducted.The cylinder structure performs optimally when the ellipsoid ratio is 1.414. Increasing the length-to-diameter ratio reduces maximum hoop winding stress in the winding layer. As the polar ratio increases, stress in the fiber winding layer decreases under working pressure, with greater fluctuations occurring in the transition section. The maximum stress transfers from the dome section to the cylinder body.Increasing the radius of the metal boss flange reduces the stress on the liner under working pressure. Additionally, enlarging the contact area between the metal boss and carbon fiber allows the flange to bear more load, thereby reducing the amount of composite carbon fiber used. To fully optimize structural performance, a flange radius of 50 mm is optimal for the cylinder structure.
