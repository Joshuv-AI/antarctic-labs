Carcass composition and meat quality of geese from conservation flocks depending on genotype and sex☆
Authors: Marcin Wegner, Dariusz Kokoszyński, Michał Gesek, Dorota Cygan-Szczegielniak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of the present study was to evaluate carcass composition, physicochemical properties, color, texture, and microstructure of the breast muscles of three regional goose breeds: Kartuska, Suwalska, and Rypińska, taking into account the effects of sex and the genotype × sex interaction. A total of 42 goose carcasses were analyzed, including 7 females and 7 males from each genotype. Genotype had a significant effect (P < 0.05) on the proportion of breast muscles, leg muscles, and skin with subcutaneous fat. The higher percentage of breast muscles was observed in males of the Kartuska and Suwalska breeds, while Kartuska females also exhibited a greater share of this trait compared with Suwalska and Rypińska females. Kartuska males were characterized by the highest proportion of leg muscles, and both males and females of this genotype had the lowest percentage of skin with subcutaneous fat. Genotype did not affect carcass weight or the proportion of abdominal fat, wings, neck, or carcass remains. Males had higher carcass weight and a lower proportion of skin with subcutaneous fat compared with females. Genotype significantly affected (P < 0.05) the chemical composition of breast and leg muscles. Kartuska geese were characterized by higher protein and lower fat content in the breast muscles, whereas Suwalska geese showed the highest protein and lowest fat content in the leg muscles. The highest water content in both breast and leg muscles was mainly observed in Suwalska geese. Females were characterized by higher fat content in the breast muscles and lower protein content in the leg muscles compared with males. Genotype also influenced the pH of breast and leg muscles and cooking loss in leg muscles. Kartuska geese exhibited higher pH values, whereas greater cooking loss was observed in Suwalska males and in Rypińska females. Neither genotype nor sex significantly affected electrical conductivity or most color parameters with the exception of lightness (L*) of the breast musclesa and redness (a*) values in the leg muscles. Texture analysis revealed a significant effect of genotype on springiness, with breast muscles of Kartuska geese being more elastic. Regardless of genotype, males were characterized by higher hardness, springiness, chewiness, and gumminess compared with females. Microstructural analysis of the breast muscles demonstrated a significant effect of genotype and sex on most structural parameters. The largest muscle fiber cross-sectional area and horizontal diameter were observed in Kartuska females and Suwalska males. Rypińska geese were characterized by the smallest fiber diameter. In conclusion, both genotype and sex significantly differentiated carcass traits and meat quality of the analyzed regional goose breeds, with clear interactions between these factors, particularly in terms of chemical composition and muscle microstructure.Keywords: Carcass composition, Geese, Meat quality

Introduction

Goose and turkey meat production represents a niche but important segment of the global poultry market. Global production of goose meat has steadily increased over the past decades. According to FAOSTAT data compiled by Dumlu (2024), world goose meat production reached approximately 4.42 million tonnes in 2022, representing the highest level recorded to date. China is by far the leading producer, accounting for over 97% of total production, while other countries, such as Egypt and Madagascar, contribute relatively small shares to global output (Dumlu, 2024; FAO, 2023). These trends highlight the dominance of China in goose meat production and the growing global importance of this sector. In Europe, goose meat production has a longer tradition, although on a much smaller scale compared to Asia. In recent years, the European market for duck and goose meat has shown a declining trend in consumption, with differences between countries. For example, France remains one of the largest consumers of this type of poultry in the region, although consumption has decreased over the last decade. Poland plays an important role in European goose meat production. According to data from the National Agricultural Support Centre (KOWR, 2025), the goose population in Poland at the end of December 2024 was approximately 1.3 million birds, and domestic goose meat production in 2024 is estimated at around 22,000 tons. A significant portion of this production is exported, mainly to Germany, which absorbs about two-thirds of Polish goose meat exports. Goose meat production in Poland is primarily based on two-line crosses of the White Kołuda® geese, which account for over 90% of total production (Wegner et al., 2025). The remaining share comes from the slaughter of commercial crossbreeds of foreign origin, such as White Goslings G35, as well as from native breeds and varieties and breeding flocks of lines W11 and W33, maintained for reproductive purposes (Buzała et al., 2014).

Poland maintains fourteen goose breeds included in the genetic resources conservation program. The Waterfowl Genetic Resources Station in Dworzyska, part of the Kołuda Wielka Experimental Department of the Institute of Animal Production – National Research Institute in Balice, conducts conservation breeding of selected goose breeds. The center maintains four foreign-origin breeds (Kuban, Roman, Slovak White, and Landes) and eight native breeds, including northern varieties (Pomorska, Kartuska, Rypińska, and Suwalska) and southern varieties (Kielecka, Lubelska, Podkarpacka, and Garbonosa). Polish northern geese (Kartuska, Rypińska, Suwalska, Pomorska) come from the northern and northeastern regions of Poland. Breeders, who for many years selected birds for breeding that were quite heavy, characterised by good musculature but also a tendency to fat deposition, contributed to the development of northern varieties of geese. Compared to the Kielecka, Lubelska, Podkarpacka and Garbonosa geese, which are considered southern varieties, northern geese have significantly higher body mass and dimensions (Calik et al., 2023). Populations of Polish goose genetic resources were established primarily in the 1970s and 1980s (Haraf, 2014; Kokoszyński et al., 2022; Wegner et al., 2025).

In accordance with the Convention on Biological Diversity adopted by member states at the United Nations Conference on Environment and Development in Rio de Janeiro in June 1992, the preservation of farm animal genetic resources, including geese, is considered essential due to their ecological and genetic significance, as well as social, economic, scientific, educational, cultural, recreational, and aesthetic values (Mazanowski, 2012; Hrnčár et al., 2021).

Recently, there has been a clear increase in interest in goose meat consumption in Poland, valued by consumers for its characteristic taste and intense aroma, which distinguish it from other types of poultry meat (Geldenhuys et al., 2015; Cui et al., 2015). Another factor contributing to its popularity is its high nutritional value. Studies indicate that cooked goose meat contains higher amounts of protein and minerals compared to broiler chicken meat, as well as a higher fat content, which contributes significantly to its juiciness and sensory qualities (Nowicka and Przybylski, 2018; Haraf et al., 2023). Goose meat also contains less water, which increases the concentration of nutrients. Additionally, goose fat has a favourable fatty acid profile, with a relatively high proportion of unsaturated fatty acids, enhancing its nutritional and health value (Wołoszyn et al., 2020; Kokoszyński et al., 2022). Meat from goose breast muscles is characterized by a beneficial lipid profile, high in unsaturated fatty acids and relatively low in cholesterol (Haraf et al., 2023). Furthermore, it has a desirable n-6 to n-3 fatty acid ratio, considered important for human nutrition (Uhlířová et al., 2018). Goose meat is also a valuable source of fat-soluble and B-group vitamins, including A, B1, B2, B3, and B6, highlighting its high nutritional value (Kokoszyński et al., 2022). In terms of microstructural composition, goose breast muscles are highly similar to those of chickens and quails. The pectoralis major muscle in geese is dominated by red fibers, accounting for approximately 73–83% of total fibers, while white fibers constitute 17–27% (Kokoszyński et al., 2022). A distinctive feature of goose meat is also its finer muscle fiber structure; the diameter of both white and red fibers in the breast muscle is smaller than in broiler chickens, which contributes to favorable textural properties, including greater meat tenderness.

Geese are typically slaughtered at 8–12 weeks of age, or fattened with oats up to 16 weeks (so-called oat-fed geese) (Buzała et al., 2014, Kokoszyński et al., 2022). Carcasses from geese after reproductive use, i.e., after four to five laying seasons, are usually considered of limited slaughter value, and the meat obtained is regarded as less attractive in terms of culinary and nutritional qualities. Numerous reports indicate that goose carcasses, their parts, and offal are used for the production of various traditional regional products such as cured meat, pâtés, and sausages (Damaziak et al., 2016; Nowicka and Przybylski, 2018; Kokoszyński et al., 2022). Cured goose products are also popular in the Czech Republic, Hungary, and Lithuania (Damaziak et al., 2016). The relatively small carcass weight of native geese has led to their exclusion from mass production; nevertheless, these breeds are ideally suited for producing many traditional regional products (Puchajda-Skowrońska et al., 2006; Kokoszyński et al., 2022). Meat from native goose breeds is characterized by higher protein content and lower fat content, increasing its nutritional value. Additionally, muscle fibers are thinner, and the meat is more tender compared to commercially reared geese, making these products particularly valued by consumers (Uhlířová et al., 2018).

Currently, there are no reports in the available literature on the carcass composition and meat quality of Rypińska, Suwalska, and Kartuska geese breeds after four reproductive seasons. Therefore, the results of the present study may provide valuable information for dietitians, consumers, and goose meat producers.

It was hypothesized that both genotype and sex would significantly affect carcass composition, physicochemical traits, texture, and microstructure of goose meat, and that interactions between these factors would occur for selected meat quality parameters.

The aim of this study was to compare carcass weight, carcass composition, and meat quality (physicochemical traits, texture and microstructural properties) of native northern goose breeds (Rypińska, Suwalska, and Kartuska) after four reproductive seasons.

Materials and methods

Research material

The study was conducted on a total of 42 goose carcasses, comprising seven females and seven males from each of three goose genotypes: Rypińska, Suwalska, and Kartuska, after a four-year laying period. The birds were purchased from the National Research Institute of Animal Production, Waterfowl Genetic Resources Station in Dworzyska. According to information provided by the breeder, all three genotypes were kept in a single windowless hall, in littered pens. The birds were kept in a closed building during the fourth reproductive season for 7 months before being slaughtered for research. During the laying period, the birds were fed commercial diets containing 14.75% crude protein (CP), 12.2 MJ of metabolizable energy (ME), 0.25% methionine (Met), 4.8% fiber, 2.49% calcium (Ca), and 0.60% lysine (Lys). Depending on the season, the temperature and humidity in the building ranged from 6 to 20°C and 65–75%, respectively, and the birds were provided with 10 hours of artificial light (Lightech Eko 10 W, 840 lm). After the laying period, the birds were kept outdoors. Carcasses and viscera were collected after humane slaughter (which was preceded by a 12-hour fasting period) and transported to the University laboratory, where they were chilled for 18 hours at 4°C in a refrigerated cabinet (Hendi, Gądki, Poland).

Carcass analysis

After cooling, the carcasses were weighed individually on an electronic weighing scale WLC 6/12/F1/R (Radwag, Radom, Poland) with an accuracy of 0.1 g, and dissection was performed using the simplified method described by Ziołecki and Doruchowski (1989). The following were extracted from each carcass during dissection: neck without skin, wings, skin with subcutaneous fat, breast muscles, leg muscles and carcass remains (trunk with a certain amount of skeletal muscles - intercostal, dorsal, suprascapular, and other and leg bones with a very small amount of the remaining thigh and drumstick muscles). The extracted carcass parts were weighed on the above mentioned scales.

Physicochemical analysis

After 24 h postmortem, acidity reading (pH24) and electrical conductivity measurement (EC24) of the superficial pectoral muscle and thigh muscle were taken. A CX-701 pH meter with a knife electrode (Elmetron, Zabrze, Poland) was used to measure the acidity reaction, which was calibrated before measurements in pH 7.0 and 4.0 buffers. The measurement result was read from a liquid crystal display with an accuracy of 0.01. A LF-Star CPU conductivity probe (Ingenieurbüro R. Matthäus, Nobitz, Germany) was used to measure the electrical conductivity of the flesh (EC24). The electrode was placed at a 90-degree angle along the muscle fibers of the pectoralis major muscle and shin muscle. The measurement was performed with an accuracy of 0.1 mS/cm. The method described by Walczak (1959) was used to determine the thermal leakage of pectoral and leg muscles. Muscle samples, each weighing 20 ± 2 g, were wrapped in gauze and placed in a water bath for 10 minutes with a water temperature of 85°C, and then cooled for 30 minutes in a refrigerated cabinet at 2°C after removal. After cooling, the samples were weighed using an electronic balance WLC6/12/F1/R (Radwag, Radom, Poland). The percentage weight loss of the sample was calculated based on the difference in the weight of the sample before and after heat treatment.

Color analysis

Subsequently, the pectoral and leg muscles were evaluated for color using the CIE L*a*b* color system, where L* represents lightness, a* redness, and b* yellowness. MINOLTA's CR 400 colorimeter (Konica Minolta, Chiyodaku, Japan) was used for the analysis.

Analysis of basic chemical composition

Breast and thigh muscle samples, without separating tendons and intramuscular fat (approximately 90 g each), were subjected to laboratory analysis to determine protein, collagen, fat, and water content, in accordance with the PN-A-82109:2010 standard. The analyses were performed using a FoodScan apparatus (FOSS, Warsaw, Poland) equipped with a Near InfraRed Transmission (NIT) spectrometer calibrated with an artificial neural network (ANN).

Texture analysis

Textural attributes (hardness, springiness, cohesiveness, gumminess, chewiness, cutting work, and Warner–Bratzler shear force) were assessed in 42 thermally processed samples of the pectoralis major muscle obtained from three breeds of spent geese. Prior to measurements, the samples were cooked to an internal temperature of 70.2°C and subsequently cooled to approximately 12°C. The internal temperature was monitored using a probe thermometer inserted into the geometric centre of each muscle sample. Texture analysis was carried out using a TA.XT plus texture analyzer (Stable Micro Systems, Godalming, UK). Both Texture Profile Analysis (TPA) and Warner–Bratzler (WB) procedures were applied. For the TPA measurement, a cylindrical probe with a diameter of 0.62 cm compressed each sample twice to 80% of its original height (16 mm), with the compression performed parallel to the muscle fibre direction. The crosshead speed was set at 50 mm/min and a 50 N load cell was used. Textural parameters were derived from the force–deformation curve. Hardness corresponded to the peak force recorded during the first compression cycle. Cohesiveness was calculated as the ratio of the area under the second compression curve to that of the first. Springiness was determined based on the distance recovered between compressions. Gumminess was expressed as the product of hardness and cohesiveness, whereas chewiness was calculated as hardness × cohesiveness × springiness, according to the approach described by Bourne (1982). For the Warner–Bratzler test, samples measuring 22 mm in height and 10 mm in width were prepared with fibres oriented parallel to the blade direction. The crosshead speed during shearing was 50 mm/min. Maximum shear force and the work required to cut through the sample were recorded following the methodology of Bourne (1982).

Structure analysis

For histological evaluation, samples of the pectoralis major muscle were collected from the central portion of 42 geese. The tissue specimens were fixed in Sannomiya solution (formaldehyde, ethanol, and glacial acetic acid in distilled water; approx. 10–15% formaldehyde, 70–80% ethanol, 5–10% acetic acid), followed by dehydration through a graded series of alcohol and benzene. Subsequently, the samples were embedded in paraffin. Paraffin blocks were sectioned into 10 μm-thick slices using a microtome, and the sections were mounted onto glass slides. The preparations were stained with hematoxylin and eosin according to the procedure described by Bruck (1975) and permanently mounted in Canada balsam. Morphometric measurements were performed using the MultiScanBase v. 13 image analysis system (Computer Scanning System Ltd.). The evaluated parameters included muscle fiber cross-sectional area, fiber perimeter, horizontal (H) and vertical (V) fiber diameter, as well as the thickness of the perimysium and endomysium.

Statistical analysis

The numerical data collected in this study were characterized by generally accepted methods of statistical analysis. For each trait studied, arithmetic means of both factors studied (genotype and gender) and standard error of the mean (SEM) for all three goose genotypes were calculated. Using the Shapiro-Wilk test, the empirical correspondence of the trait distribution to a normal distribution was assessed. Therefore, a parametric test in the form of a two-factor analysis of variance was used to determine the effect of genotype and gender on the trait under study. The following linear model was used: Yijk = µ + ai + bj + (a × b)ij + eijk

Where Yijk is the trait value, µ is the overall trait mean, ai is the effect of the i th genotype, bj is the effect of the j-th sex, (a × b) ij is the genotype x sex interaction effect, eijk is the random error, and k is the observation number within the group.

SAS software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) version 9.4. Significances of differences at P < 0.05 were verified between genotypes and between males and females using the Tukey post hoc test (SAS Institute, 2014).

Results

Carcass characteristics

The genotype of the geese (Table 1) had a significant effect (P < 0.05) on the proportion of breast muscles, leg muscles, and skin with subcutaneous fat (P < 0.001 – 0.043). The higher percentage of breast muscles in males was observed in Kartuska and Suwalska geese, while Kartuska females also showed a higher proportion of this trait compared with Suwalska and Rypińska females. The highest proportion of leg muscles in the carcass was found in Kartuska males (15.61%) compared with Rypińska and Suwalska males (13.52% and 13.68%, respectively). Both males and females of the Kartuska breed had the lowest proportion of skin with subcutaneous fat. Genotype did not affect other analyzed carcass traits, including carcass weight, abdominal fat content, wings, neck, and carcass remains (P = 0.155 – 0.692). Sex also influenced carcass weight and the proportion of skin with subcutaneous fat in all analyzed goose breeds (P < 0.001 and 0.014, respectively). Males were characterized by higher carcass weight but a lower proportion of skin with subcutaneous fat compared with females. Sex had a significant effect on the proportion of breast muscles and abdominal fat in Kartuska and Suwalska geese (P = 0.018 and 0.022, respectively). For other analyzed traits (leg muscles, wings, neck, and carcass remains), sex had no significant effect regardless of breed (P = 0.075–0.971). A significant genotype × sex interaction was observed for abdominal fat content (P = 0.040).

Physicochemical properties

In all analyzed goose breeds, a significant effect (P < 0.05) was observed on the protein, water, fat, and collagen content in both breast and leg muscles (Table 2). The higher water content in the breast muscles was found in males and females of the Suwalska breed and in females of the Rypińska breed (P < 0.001). Water content in leg muscles was highest in both males and females of the Suwalska breed (P = 0.002). The highest protein content and lowest fat content in breast muscles were observed in males and females of the Kartuska breed (P < 0.001 and 0.002, respectively). In leg muscles, the highest protein and lowest fat content were found in males and females of the Suwalska breed (P < 0.001). The highest collagen content in breast muscles was observed in Kartuska males (P = 0.001), while in leg muscles, it was highest in Rypińska males and females and in Suwalska males (P = 0.003). Suwalska males also had higher water content in leg muscles (P = 0.021). In contrast, females of all analyzed breeds (Suwalska, Rypińska, and Kartuska) exhibited higher fat content in breast muscles and lower protein content in leg muscles compared with males (P < 0.001 and 0.005, respectively). Leg muscles of Rypińska and Suwalska males had lower fat content (P = 0.014). Additionally, higher collagen content in both breast and leg muscles was observed in Kartuska and Suwalska males compared with females (P < 0.001). A significant genotype × sex interaction was found for water, fat, protein, and collagen content in breast muscles, as well as for protein content in leg muscles (P = 0.002 – 0.019).

Muscle color

Analysis of the color parameters (L*, a*, and b*) of breast and leg muscles (Table 4) showed no significant effect of genotype (P = 0.060 – 0.447). However, breast muscles of Rypińska and Suwalska females exhibited higher lightness (L*) values (P = 0.019). In contrast, leg muscles of males from the Rypińska, Kartuska, and Suwalska breeds showed higher redness (a*) values (P = 0.017). Sex had no significant effect on the remaining analyzed color traits (P = 0.082–0.978). No significant genotype × sex interactions were observed for L*, a*, or b* values in breast or leg muscles, except for the b* value of breast muscles (P = 0.016).

Texture

Texture analysis of the breast muscle showed (Table 5) a significant effect (P < 0.05) of genotype on springiness. Breast muscles of both male and female Kartuska geese were more elastic (higher values) (P = 0.019). For the remaining traits (WB shear force, cutting work, hardness, cohesiveness, chewiness, and gumminess), genotype had no significant effect (P = 0.067–0.904). Breast muscles of males from the Kartuska, Rypińska, and Suwalska breeds exhibited higher values of hardness, springiness, chewiness, and gumminess compared with females (P = 0.002 – 0.005). A significant genotype × sex interaction was also observed for WB shear force and cutting work (P = 0.018 and 0.049, respectively).

Structure

Analysis of the breast muscle structure showed (Table 6) a significant effect (P < 0.05) of goose genotype on all traits except fiber diameter V (P = 0.419). The largest cross-sectional area of muscle fibers was observed in Kartuska females and Suwalska males (P = 0.002). The higher fiber diameter H was found in Kartuska and Suwalska females, as well as Suwalska males (P < 0.001). Kartuska females and Suwalska males had the largest fiber circumference and the thickest perimysium, while the smallest fiber circumference was observed in Rypińska males and females (P = 0.010). Analysis of endomysium thickness revealed the highest values in Rypińska and Suwalska males and Suwalska females (P = 0.003). Sex significantly affected all analyzed traits except endomysium thickness (P = 0.656). Rypińska, Suwalska, and Kartuska females had smaller fiber cross-sectional area, fiber circumference, and fiber diameter V compared with males (P < 0.001 – 0.001). Additionally, Rypińska and Suwalska females exhibited smaller fiber diameter H compared with males (P = 0.003). Suwalska males had thicker perimysium than females (P < 0.001). A genotype × sex interaction was observed for all analyzed structural traits except fiber diameter H (P = 0.149).

Discussion

The impact of genetic factors on poultry carcass traits has been widely described in the literature, both for native geese and commercial breeds (Boz et al., 2017; Kokoszyński et al., 2022). The results obtained in the present study confirm a significant effect of genotype and sex on carcass traits, chemical composition, physicochemical properties, texture, and microstructure of the breast muscle in regional geese. Kartuska geese were characterized by the highest proportion of breast muscles and relatively low subcutaneous fat content, which aligns with the observations of Kokoszyński et al. (2022), who reported that some local goose populations exhibit a more favorable muscle-to-fat ratio compared with other native breeds. Interbreed differences in breast muscle proportion may result from divergent selection directions and genetic variability, as also emphasized by Okruszek et al. (2013). Warzocha et al. (2019) indicates a strong diversification of the genotype of geese covered by the genetic resources conservation program kept in Poland, including differences in the genetic structure of the studied Kartuska, Rypińska and Suwalska geese. The observed effect of sex on carcass weight, with males being heavier, is consistent with the findings of Uhlířová et al. (2019) and Lewko et al. (2017), who reported pronounced sexual dimorphism in productive and carcass traits of geese. Females, on the other hand, exhibited higher subcutaneous fat content, which may be associated with hormonal differences and distinct lipid metabolism. In a study conducted on chickens, Hao et al. (2025) found differential expression of genes related to steroid biosynthesis and energy metabolism affecting the quality of the breast and leg muscles depending on sex.

The significant effect of genotype on protein, fat, and water content in breast and leg muscles supports previous reports indicating notable differences among breeds in meat chemical composition (Wężyk et al., 2003; Haraf, 2014). Higher protein and lower fat content in the breast muscles of Kartuska geese may indicate a more favorable production potential for meat of higher dietary value. Greater fat content in the muscles of females, also observed in this study, has been reported in other poultry breeds (Kasperek et al., 2023; Wegner et al., 2024, Wegner et al., 2026; Chaiwang et al., 2026) and may result from sex-related differences in lipid metabolism. Differences in muscle pH among breeds reflect varying rates of post-mortem metabolism. In this study, a differential effect of sex was found depending on genotype for water and protein content in breast muscles. Furthermore, depending on genotype, different effects of sex were observed for fat and collagen content. This was likely related to differences in genetic background, different selection pressures on meat traits between the compared goose varieties, and the different effects of sex hormones on the chemical composition of meat in male and female geese (Table 3).

Higher pH values observed in Kartuska geese may suggest slower post-mortem glycolysis, which promotes higher water-holding capacity. During the conversion of muscle to meat, pH decreases due to anaerobic glycogen breakdown to lactic acid; the rate and extent of this change affect meat characteristics such as color, texture, and water-holding capacity. Higher ultimate pH typically corresponds to greater water retention and reduced drip loss, favorably affecting carcass quality (Wegner et al., 2024). Research on post-mortem changes indicates that differences in pH decline rates, related to genetic metabolic traits of muscles, are crucial for final physicochemical meat properties, including water-holding capacity and color (Leishman et al., 2022). The lack of significant sex effects on most physicochemical traits in this study is consistent with previous findings, indicating that genetic and metabolic factors have a stronger influence on technological meat quality than the sex of the birds (e.g., effects of ultimate pH and its decline rate on WHC and meat color). Leishman et al. (2022), in studies on turkey meat, showed that the rate and extent of post-mortem pH changes are strongly associated with the genetic line of the birds, while sex had a smaller effect. The absence of genotype effects on breast and leg muscle color parameters L*, a*, and b* in this study suggests that differences among regional populations do not significantly determine meat color. Similar conclusions were reported by Kokoszyński et al. (2022) in analyses of regional goose populations. The observed effect of sex on a* in leg muscles may be related to differences in muscle fiber type and myoglobin content.

The significant effect of genotype on breast muscle springiness observed in this study reflects differences in muscle fiber structure among breeds, indicating varied fiber profiles and mechanical properties of meat. Textural parameters such as hardness, chewiness, and gumminess are closely associated with muscle microstructure, particularly fiber diameter, arrangement, and density, as well as the distribution of collagen fibers in connective tissue. Increased collagen content and changes in perimysium structure significantly affect meat hardness. Kokoszyński et al. (2022) found no effect of genotype on breast muscle texture in regional geese (Landes, Slovak White, and Kuban), and Uhlířová et al. (2018) also reported no genotype effect on WB shear force, which was confirmed in the present study. However, Uhlířová et al. (2018) reported a decline in breast meat tenderness with bird age. In our study, males exhibited higher hardness, chewiness, and gumminess than females, while no sex effect was observed on WB shear force, consistent with Uhlířová et al. (2018). Research on muscle structure also indicates that sex can influence muscle fiber development and connective tissue: males often have larger fibers and more developed perimysium, reflected in higher hardness and chewiness in instrumental analyses (Zhao et al., 2024). In practice, larger fiber cross-sections in males may result from differential expression of genes regulating muscle hypertrophy and hormonal differences affecting collagen synthesis (Zhao et al., 2024). Increasingly, studies highlight that genotype × sex interactions are important in shaping meat traits, as sex effects often depend on the genetic background; in some lines, male muscle fibers grow faster and reach larger sizes than females, while in other genotypes such differences are less pronounced or absent (Wegner et al., 2023). This complexity indicates that simultaneous analysis of muscle microstructure and texture provides more comprehensive information on meat quality and production potential.

Conclusions

The results of this study confirm that genotype significantly influences selected carcass traits (particularly the proportion of breast and leg muscles), chemical composition of meat, and selected textural and microstructural properties, whereas sex mainly affects carcass weight, fat deposition, and some texture- and structure-related traits. However, carcass weight and several carcass components (including abdominal fat, wings, neck, and carcass remains) were not significantly affected by genotype. The findings are generally consistent with previous literature and indicate the breeding potential of the studied populations, particularly Kartuska geese, for the production of meat with favorable quality characteristics.
