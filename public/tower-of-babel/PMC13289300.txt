Towards proactive, integrated care across boundaries: panel management next level
Authors: Anna Wilhelmina de Boer, Mattijs Everard Numans, Hedwig Miirjam Maria Vos, Josefien De Bruin, Janet Margriet Kist, Rimke Cathelijne Vos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA promising integrated care approach is Panel Management, first described by Kaiser Permanente as a set of tools and processes for population-oriented care that are applied systematically at the level of a primary care panel. We aimed to develop an advanced panel management approach towards an iterative process across settings and boundaries. We define Panel Management Next Level as a structured set of tools and processes—based on medical and social needs—that are applied systematically at the level of a patient panel sharing a similar risk on adverse health events, with healthcare professionals directing proactive care for their empanelled patients. There are four iterative steps: (1) empanelment of (patient) groups that share a similar risk; (2) allocation of the appropriate intervention to each panel based on medical and social needs; (3) surveillance of care gaps and (4) effect and process evaluation. In conclusion, Panel Management Next Level is a proactive, integrated care approach which can be embedded in and across different settings. Challenges are multimorbidity, integrated information technology (IT) systems and alternative payment models. Panel Management Next Level has the potential to reach the quadruple aim of improved patient experience, improved population health and improved healthcare professional experience while reducing the costs of care.

Introduction

In healthcare, we face several challenges at a population level. There is a growing number of people with a chronic condition starting at an earlier age, multimorbidity has increased and the population is ageing rapidly.1 Our society is becoming more complex with a cultural and ethnically diverse population.2 Moreover, the workload in healthcare is high and there is an increasing shortage of healthcare professionals.3 There is an urgent need for reforming healthcare so that all involved stakeholders can be enabled to deliver high-quality people-centred care while keeping costs under control. One of the paths towards a sustainable healthcare system is offering more proactive and integrated care, thus increasing plannable elective care interventions at the cost of unplanned ad hoc care, potentially saving time for rewarding care needs in the future and improving the span of control. The aim of integrating care is to better coordinate care around people’s needs and across organisational boundaries.4 ‘Fully integrated’ care is characterised by integrated teams working in an organisation with a single set of governance and accountability rules and common budgets and incentives. For people with multimorbidity and/or complex needs, this implies a cooperating network of care providers.

A promising approach for integrated care in the clinical healthcare setting is structured Panel Management. This was first described by Kaiser Permanente, one of the largest non-profit integrated healthcare systems in the USA. They described Panel Management as “a set of tools and processes for population care that are applied systematically at the level of a primary care panel, with primary care physicians directing proactive care for their empanelled patients”.5 A panel stands for a part of the population comprised of people sharing a similar risk.

A first step towards panel management has previously shown to improve health outcomes by working systematically and proactively.6 A hypertension quality improvement evaluation trial, in which a proactive algorithm and an interdisciplinary treatment protocol was implemented for feedback to general practitioners, resulted in an increased number of patients with controlled blood pressure from more than 20% to more than 40%. Also, in other studies, panel management has been shown to be an effective way to improve chronic disease management.713 However, patient-reported experience measures, experienced given care and cost effectiveness were not measured yet.

This first definition focuses especially at the domain of primary care practice. However, true integrated care crosses boundaries of primary care, secondary care and the social domain. Advanced integration of care in panel management programmes can lead to better coordination and collaboration and can overcome fragmentation of care. In addition, the panel management approach described by Kaiser Permanente is a linear approach, while iterative evaluation and improvement cycles on the quadruple aim (improved patient experience, improved population health and improved healthcare professional experience while decreasing the costs of care) are needed for a sustainable programme. There is guidance needed for policy makers and professionals how to design a panel management programme, as we face an increase number of complex patients and high burden on healthcare staff and costs.

Therefore, we aimed to develop an innovative structured and proactive panel management approach towards an iterative process and to broaden the focus to empanelled integrated care on the patient level, primary care, secondary care and the social domain: Panel Management Next Level. Hereto, we adapted Kaiser Permanentes’ definition of panel management. In addition, we described the process of our approach to provide guidance for policy makers and professionals to implement a population-based approach in a clinical healthcare setting.

Description

We propose to broaden Kaiser Permanentes’ definition, by widening the focus to integrated care on the patient—and community level, and including not only primary care, but also secondary care and the social and citizens domain in the process. Besides, we propose to evaluate on the quadruple aim (improved patient experience and outcomes, improved population health and improved healthcare professional experience while decreasing the costs of care), considering the challenges we face in healthcare. We define Panel Management Next Level as a set of tools and processes for appropriate population-oriented care—based on medical and social needs—that are applied systematically at the level of a patient panel sharing a similar risk on adverse health events, with healthcare professionals directing proactive care for their empanelled patients. This way, every level in (health) care will be able to apply panel management techniques and may be enabled to function as the director of panel management programmes. In addition, we propose to make the evaluation and improvement cycles more prominently visible for the stakeholders and stated this more prominently in the process description.

The process of our Panel Management Next Level approach includes four iterative steps: (1) empanelment of (patient) groups that share a similar risk on adverse health events, based on a risk stratification algorithm including preferably medical and social determinants of health; (2) allocation of the appropriate intervention to each panel of patients based on medical and social needs; (3) periodic surveillance of care gaps (individuals having missed the intervention, gaps in care in groups with for example different social determinants of health) for each panel related to their risk; and (4) effect and process evaluation on the quadruple aim.

In the following sections, we provide a more detailed description of these four steps and illustrate their application using The Heart for Women in The Hague project as an example. This project was established to facilitate the early detection of an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and/or type 2 diabetes (T2D) among women with a history of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy and/or gestational diabetes.14 15 This project is presented as an illustrative example of how Panel Management Next Level can be implemented in practice, rather than as an evaluation of predictive accuracy or intervention effectiveness. A more detailed description of the Heart for Women in The Hague project is provided in online supplemental box 1.

Step 1 – Empanelment

The first step of Panel Management Next Level is called empanelment (figure 1), which means the identification of people sharing a similar risk on adverse health events, and allocating them to an administrative subgroup. This involves: (1) the definition of the outcome you want to achieve or prevent and (2) determinants included in your algorithm for risk stratification.

The first step of Panel Management Next Level: empanelment.

First, define the outcome you want to achieve together with the stakeholders. The outcome can either be health related or disease related. A health-related outcome to aim for could, for example, be maintaining independence in the elderly or stimulating a healthy lifestyle. Disease-related outcomes that can be aimed for are, for example, the prevention of events like chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) exacerbation, the development or progression of disease, to postpone mortality, or a reduction of unwanted unplanned emergency or secondary care visits.

Then determine the risk factors (both medical and social determinants of health known from literature and expert opinion) and use risk stratification to define the panels. For example, to prevent hospital admissions in the elderly, a frailty score can be used to empanel the population at risk. This score may include symptoms, diseases, laboratory results, psychological determinants, lifestyle, medication, socioeconomic and social support measures, to be found in—and reused from routine electronic medical record (EMR) registries or other sources.

Empanelment helps healthcare professionals to get insight in their population as the starting point for delivering proactive care. However, a risk stratification algorithm is not flawless. Therefore, after the empanelment of the population, the expertise of the healthcare professional is needed to evaluate whether an individual patient is accurately stratified into one of the panels.

The Heart for Women in The Hague project

An algorithm was developed to identify women 18–70 years with a history of a hypertensive disorder during pregnancy or gestational diabetes using the EMR. The algorithm was developed and implemented in collaboration with Zorg op Orde and in co-creation with general practitioners and practice nurses. In total, 269 women were eligible for screening.

Further formal analyses are planned to examine and test which medical and social determinants are related to an increased or decreased risk for cardiometabolic diseases. With this information, formal empanelment in three risk groups can be applied in daily practice.

Step 2 – Choosing the appropriate intervention

The second step of Panel Management Next Level is choosing or designing the appropriate intervention for each risk panel (figure 2), aiming to improve patient experience, improve population health and improve healthcare professional experience while decreasing the costs of care (the quadruple aim). Instead of one general intervention for the entire population, interventions are developed that address the medical and social needs and wishes of the specified panel at risk and can be prioritised according to the estimated risk level of individuals. Many interventions are available, but the question is whether the interventions are effective in real life setting and are they effective when applied to your specific panel. A theoretical framework is used to review existing interventions and will guide which interventions are the most appropriate for the aim and panel. Three elements of an appropriate intervention should be considered when designing an intervention: (1) the intervention should be evidence-based effective, (2) the implementation strategy is well matched to the characteristics of the intervention (functions that cannot be changed), characteristics of the adaptor (the form of your intervention which can be changed) and the context (collaboration, cultural and operational change), and (3) sustainability should be ensured (eg, finance/reimbursement).

The second step of Panel Management Next Level: choosing the appropriate intervention.

It is important to co-create the intervention with all involved stakeholders. For healthcare professionals, an intervention should fit in their work processes and in the end decrease workload. Patient involvement can be used to co-create a successful intervention which fits to their specific medical and social needs.

Before implementing a new intervention, insight is needed in factors affecting implementation. An implementation determinants framework can be used to identify barriers and facilitators of implementation, followed by the selection of appropriate implementation strategies. Three commonly used frameworks are the Consolidated Framework for Implementation (CFIR) with a focus on socioecological perspective, the Theoretical Domain Framework (TDF) with a focus on behaviour change, and the Measurement for Determinants of Innovations with a focus on the characteristics of the innovation.1618

In the Netherlands, panel management is used to organise the annual influenza vaccination campaign. Patients considered at high risk of influenza complications or death caused by influenza are offered an influenza vaccination. This is an evidence-based intervention, locally organised by general practitioners and funded from public health funding regulations. This way, care waste (providing a vaccination for those not needing it) is avoided for medium and low risk patients and there is an extra contact moment created between patient and practice potentially improving health awareness.

The Heart for Women in The Hague project

During the first screening visit, women completed a questionnaire on family history of CVD and lifestyle behaviour. In addition, blood pressure, heart rate, Body Mass Index, blood glucose, lipid profile, kidney function and albumin-to-creatinine ratio were assessed. Based on these results, the general practitioner or practice nurse provided information on cardiometabolic risk factors and, when necessary, initiated preventive lifestyle interventions and/or medication

Follow-up frequency was tailored to screening outcomes and patient preferences, in line with current guidelines. Once the determinants related to an increased or decreased risk are examined, the advice on follow-up frequency will be panel specific.

Step 3 – Surveillance of care gaps

The third step is the surveillance of care gaps (figure 3), which means identification of those having missed the appropriate intervention at the chosen time. Applying Panel Management Next Level requires to proactively periodically review the clinical registry, to identify care gaps and to reach out to these patients to address those care gaps. Both outreach (screening invitations for those at risk) and in-reach (eg, appointment reminders and case finding) approaches can be used to reach the patients. Reaching out for patients requires a cultural and organisational change in healthcare practice, as well as proper reimbursement.

The third step of Panel Management Next Level: surveillance of care gaps.

A recent study investigated approaches to enhance the participation of marginalised women in cervical cancer screening programmes.19 These women often fail to receive the invitation letters and they experience barriers to access healthcare. A direct proactive approach was most effective to close this care gap.

The Heart for Women in The Hague project

The project combined both in-reach and outreach strategies. In-reach by proactively identifying women at risk and inviting them for screening. Outreach by prospectively informing women who experienced a hypertensive disorder during pregnancy or gestational diabetes and advising them to contact their general practice. Besides, a waiting room information film was developed to increase awareness and inform women who possibly missed by the algorithm.

Step 4 – Evaluation

Finally, we define the phase of evaluation (figure 4), we want to determine and measure if the panel management programme indeed leads to the improved outcome you want to achieve or reduction of adverse events. Both an effect evaluation and a process evaluation are performed. An effect evaluation is performed to measure the effect of a panel management approach based on the quadruple aim. The effect is evaluated per panel and within the entire population. There are many indicators and data sources which may be used for each part of the quadruple aim.20 For example, (1) population health: disease prevalence/incidence, mortality; (2) patient experience: Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs), satisfaction scores; (3) costs: cost-effectiveness ratios, avoidable spending; (4) provider well-being: workload measures, professional satisfaction surveys.

The fourth step of Panel Management Next Level: evaluation.

The process evaluation is performed to fully understand both the outcomes of the effect evaluation and get insight in implementation outcomes using Proctor et al.; acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, fidelity, implementation cost, penetration (reach) and sustainability.21 A process evaluation framework such as RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation and Maintenance) can be used to guide the process evaluation taking both effectiveness and implementation into account.22 The outcome of every evaluation may generate input for the next panel management cycle.

For example, within the PROSPERA intervention, a proactive algorithm was developed, based on (inter)national guidelines to empanel patients according to cardiometabolic care complexity. Next, the appropriate intervention will be chosen during the consultation supported by a risk tool (U-prevent) in which the patients’ preference, capacity and care complexity are taken into account. During a feasibility study, both effect measures (switching from risk panel) as well as implementation outcomes were studied both quantitative (EMR) and qualitative (focus groups, using the theory domain framework) to improve the programme.23

The Heart for Women in The Hague project

Semi-structured interviews were taken with both healthcare providers and women invited for screening using the consolidated framework for implementation research in combination with the rainbow model of integrated care. Process evaluation included the experiences of both healthcare providers and women in the target population. Of the 269 eligible women for screening, 78 women (29%) could not be reached or were not interested in the screening. Of all 181 screened women, 109 women (60%) had at least one modifiable cardiovascular risk factor. The healthcare professionals and women were generally positive about the project. Barriers included the absence of an easy method to recontact the women after 1 year, limited awareness among women about their elevated cardiovascular risk, unfamiliarity of new healthcare professionals with the project, and current lack of evidence demonstrating that screening effectively prevents disease. Input was used to improve the programme. More analyses are needed to examine the (cost-)effectiveness of this project in delaying or preventing disease development.

Discussion

Panel Management Next Level is a proactive, integrated care approach which guides the implementation of population health management in a clinical healthcare setting. There are four iterative steps: (1) empanelment, (2) choosing the appropriate intervention, (3) surveillance of care gaps and (4) evaluation. The result is a targeted risk-based approach instead of one size fits all approach, that fits into a community-oriented integrated care approach organised by healthcare centres and that can be prioritised according to individual risks, aiming to improve patient experience, improve population health while decreasing the costs of care, improve health professionals’ satisfaction and improving the ‘span of control’, or the community targeted outreach by primary and secondary care.

Although we have established structured care programmes for chronic disease management, known as integrated care programmes, effectively in many countries among which the Netherlands, the concept of Panel Management Next Level could serve as a model for advancing these integrated care programmes: a more proactive, systematic and data-driven risk-based approach to chronic disease care. Panel Management Next Level can relatively easily be embedded in and across different healthcare systems once the principles of the process are recognised and have led to a way of working that can be accepted. However, successfully translating this concept into practice requires careful attention to social determinants of health, contextual adaptation, implementation support and sustainability.

Moreover, when implementing a proactive innovation in clinical practice, The Population Health Management Maturity Index can help to assess how developed an organisation is across six key domains: accountable regional organisation, cross-domain business model, integrated data-infrastructure, co-designing workforce and community, population health data analytics, emergent implementation strategy.24 These six domains can be used as prerequisites to reflect on the readiness of organisational and system-level conditions for successful implementation of proactive innovations in a region. However, the scope of the current paper is on the operational core of the framework: the application of proactive, data-driven panel management within clinical practice.

Applying Panel Management Next Level requires a cultural and operational change: the change from reactive care to proactive care. A large difference compared with the current reactive healthcare practice that is based on passive case-finding. Most Kaiser Permanente physicians reported that Panel Management is ‘the right thing to do’ for patients.5 Practice nurses are already well-trained to deliver protocolised and people-centred care. Therefore, they often have a central role in the Panel Management approach as panel managers. For physicians, this will reduce the workload, improve their span of control in the population and give room to focus on patients with complex needs, while care waste (too much care) is reduced for patients who are well controlled and well capable of self-monitoring and self-management.

One of the challenges is the coordination of care for patients with multimorbidity. When Panel Management is broadly adopted within the current system with care fragmentation and without the necessary culture change, a patient with multimorbidity may appear in different panels with corresponding intervention and control schemes. To avoid this, clear prioritisation and coordination strategies are required to prevent fragmented or duplicative care. For example, within the Utrecht Periodic Risk Identification and Monitoring (U-PRIM)/Utrecht Primary care Proactive Frailty Intervention Trial (U-PROFIT) programme, routine primary care data were used at the practice population level to identify frail older adults at increased risk of functional decline and hospitalisation, after which integrated geriatric assessment and multidisciplinary care planning were initiated.25 26 Such an approach illustrates how patients can be stratified based on overall care complexity rather than on single diseases alone. Besides, in the empanelment step (step 1), risk factors such as the achievement of treatment goals or care complexity can be used to stratify and empanel the population. The Adjusted Clinical Groups (ACG) system is one approach that can support the assessment of multimorbidity and its impact on population health.27 In the design of the appropriate intervention (step 2), treatment plans should emphasise comprehensive care, addressing the physical, psychological and social dimensions of health, with a focus on the person as a whole rather than on single diseases. To prevent fragmented care, an integrated, multidisciplinary team of healthcare professionals should collaborate to deliver this comprehensive care.28 The optimal configuration of these strategies depends on the specific (care) setting and context. Within local settings, the Medical Research Council framework for complex interventions can be used to assess acceptability, identify implementation determinants and guide further refinement.29

Another challenge is integrating healthcare data from a variety of (health) care providers and self-collected data. There are, for example, technical challenges, organisational barriers, privacy issues and the importance of a shared language. In the coming years, there will be an increase in technological developments and the linkage of social, economic and healthcare data will be available. This boost of big data and advanced methods to analyse them will make Panel Management Next Level, especially empanelment, more appropriate and more relevant for healthcare providers. In this development, there should be awareness that artificial intelligence is often not neutral and therefore not fair.30 It may reproduce racism, sexism and other inequities. Recent literature discusses ways to improve the fairness of the data and the algorithms.31

An important challenge is the application of the insights of big data to the care of an individual patient. For example, risk scores calculated in linked data currently cannot be transferred back to the healthcare professional at an individual patient level due to privacy regulations for working with linked data in Statistics Netherlands. In our illustrative example, we therefore used social economic position of the neighbourhood instead of individual data. Even though this could lead to reduced precision of the risk stratification, it helps health professionals to get insight into their population. The expertise of healthcare professionals is needed to determine what appropriate care would be for their population and the individual patient. Another limitation of the illustrative example is the absence of formal performance indicators of the algorithm as it was developed and implemented within routine clinical practice, rather than as part of an effectiveness study. Whenever possible, the use of validated algorithms is recommended, when applying the panel management approach.

Furthermore, funding is a barrier to consider in the design of an integrated care programme. In the current system, care is usually financed and organised in silos with a focus on the providers of care instead of the patients’ needs. There are different alternative payment models developed to finance integrated care, for example bundled payment, pay for performance and shared saving contracts.32

Conclusion

So altogether, Panel Management Next Level is a proactive, integrated care approach which can be applied in and across different (care) settings. It has the potential to reach the quadruple aim of improved patient experience, improved population health and improved healthcare professional experience while decreasing the costs of care. When challenges on data integration and data sharing are addressed, organisational changes are made to avoid fragmentation of care and appropriate payment models are implemented, experiences of given care by the professionals involved can improve.

Although it will take time before all these challenges can be addressed, there are already possibilities to work more proactively as shown by our illustrative example for women at risk for developing cardiometabolic diseases (online supplemental box 1).
