Abstract
Objective: to propose a neuropsychiatric hypothesis in which Korsakoff syndrome is not restricted to an amnestic
disorder but involves a disruption of the integration of present experience, affective valuation, and autobiographical
narrative. Methodology: a narrative and integrative review was conducted, combining classical European psychiatric
descriptions with recent neurobiological evidence on thiamine deficiency, Wernicke encephalopathy, the Papez circuit,
confabulation, and autobiographical memory. Results: the synthesis suggests that diencephalic–limbic lesions may
impair the assignment of emotional relevance to new experiences, hindering their incorporation into personal history
and favoring confabulatory reconstructions based on older autobiographical models. On this basis, a three-level clinical
gradation—mild, moderate, and severe—is proposed, together with the heuristic concept of a “hedonistic autonomous”
state, which should be regarded as a hypothesis rather than an established diagnosis. Conclusion: Korsakoff syndrome
may be understood as a disorder of experience integration, with implications for early psychiatric recognition and the
preservation of occupational and social roles. The proposed framework is conceptual and requires prospective,
neuropsychological, and neuroanatomical validation.
Keywords: Korsakoff Syndrome; Wernicke Encephalopathy; Limbic system; Episodic memory; Memory disorders.

1. Introduction
Korsakoff Syndrome is classically defined as an amnestic syndrome caused by thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency, most
commonly associated with chronic alcohol use disorder, though it also occurs in the context of bariatric surgery, malnutrition,
hyperemesis gravidarum, and other conditions that impair thiamine absorption or utilization (Alonso-Fernández, 1976, Arts,
Walvoort & Kessels, 2017). The etiological diversity of thiamine deficiency reflects the syndrome’s complex relationship with
both nutritional and behavioral factors, making early recognition particularly important for preventing progression (Popa et al.,
2021). The syndrome typically emerges as the chronic sequela of Wernicke’s encephalopathy, developing in approximately 80%
of untreated cases (Arts et al., 2017). Its core clinical features include severe anterograde amnesia, variable retrograde amnesia,
confabulation, and relative preservation of other intellectual functions (Kopelman, 2023).
Despite this well-established clinical description, several authors in the tradition of European psychiatry have questioned
whether the fundamental deficit of the syndrome corresponds solely to the inability to form new memories, or whether it
encompasses a much broader alteration in the way the individual experiences and integrates the present. Among these authors,
Alonso Fernández, in his foundational work on contemporary psychiatry (Alonso-Fernández, 1976), discussed whether the
essential impairment of the syndrome might involve the disruption of meaningful experiences rather than memory storage per
se. This distinction carries profound clinical and neuropsychological implications, particularly for the psychiatric management
of patients at earlier stages of the disease.
The objective is to propose a neuropsychiatric hypothesis in which Korsakoff syndrome is not restricted to an amnestic
disorder but involves a disruption of the integration of present experience, affective valuation, and autobiographical narrative.
We integrate current neurobiological literature with a comprehensive neuropsychiatric hypothesis. We propose that the core
deficit in Korsakoff Syndrome lies in the disruption of affective valuation, preventing the integration of present events into the
autobiographical narrative. We further propose a clinical grading system for Korsakoff Syndrome to assist psychiatrists in early
diagnosis, emphasizing that the disorder exists on a clinical spectrum rather than as an all-or-nothing phenomenon. The
psychiatric perspective is particularly relevant because, as has been noted in the literature, the syndrome is frequently
misdiagnosed as primary frontotemporal dementia or mania, and by the time a neurologist evaluates the patient, the condition
has often reached an irreversible stage (Batarfi, 2025).

3. Results and Discussion
The Central Problem: Memory or Experience?
If Korsakoff Syndrome were exclusively a disease of anterograde memory, one would expect primarily the inability to register
new facts, rapid forgetting, and the repetition of the same questions. However, the clinical presentation frequently encompasses
much broader manifestations. Commonly observed are a reduction in existential concern, a decrease in anticipatory anxiety, a
loss of critical judgment, emotional indifference, impoverishment of initiative, alterations in social behavior, perseveration of
self- destructive habits, and spontaneous confabulations (Bielecka, 2025; El Haj, Questel, & Moustafa, 2025). These behavioral
manifestations extend far beyond the classical amnestic presentation, suggesting that the syndrome affects not merely memory
storage but the fundamental organization of personality and existential engagement (El Haj et al., 2021). These alterations suggest
an impairment in the very way the individual experiences the present. It does not appear to be only the memory of the event that
is compromised, but rather the affective importance attributed to the event. This observation aligns with recent research
demonstrating that patients with Korsakoff Syndrome show preserved emotional intensity when recalling highly significant
public events, suggesting that the emotional processing system is not entirely destroyed, but rather disconnected from the
mechanisms of autobiographical integration (Herrmann et al., 2022).
The Hypothesis of Meaningful Experiences
We propose that Korsakoff Syndrome can be understood as a disorder of meaningful experiences. Every human
experience possesses, simultaneously, perceptive, emotional, autobiographical, motivational, and amnestic components. These
components are normally integrated into a single conscious episode. When an individual witnesses a relevant event, they do not
merely register it as information; they attribute emotional meaning, biographical priority, and adaptive value to that experience.
Such integration allows the episode to influence future decisions.
In Korsakoff Syndrome, this integration appears to break down. The present fails to acquire sufficient psychological
relevance to reorganize behavior. Consequently, the individual remains trapped in old patterns of functioning, unable to
adequately update their own history. The cortex does not function merely as a memory repository, but as an organizer of
significant life experiences. New experiences are continuously compared with equally significant previous experiences, forming
a dynamic network of associations that allows one to understand the present in light of the past and to project possibilities for
the future.
Confabulation: More Than Filling Gaps
Instead of simply forgetting, the patient seems to continuously reconstruct a reality based on older autobiographical
models. Thus, the past replaces the present. The previously consolidated history occupies the space left by new experiences that
have lost their capacity for integration. In some patients, this phenomenon may contribute to a situation in which the past
continuously invades the present, producing false autobiographical reconstructions and a relative inability to recognize the
rupture produced by the disease.

Neuroanatomical Aspects and the Papez Circuit
The classical neuropathological studies, notably the foundational monograph Le Syndrome de Korsakoff by Delay and
Brion (Delay et al., 1969), remain the cornerstone reference for understanding the neuropathology of Korsakoff Syndrome.
Published in 1969, this seminal work established the fundamental understanding of the diencephalic lesions characteristic of the
syndrome and continues to be cited as essential literature in contemporary neuropsychiatry. Delay and Brion demonstrated
predominant lesions in structures of the Papez circuit, particularly the mammillary bodies, the mammillothalamic tract, the
anterior thalamic nuclei, the medial thalamic regions, and the hippocampal connections. This circuit constitutes one of the main
pathways responsible for the integration of episodic memory, emotion, and temporal orientation.
More than five decades after Delay and Brion’s foundational work, recent neuroimaging studies continue to corroborate
and extend these classical findings. Symmetric structural abnormalities, including atrophy and microhemorrhagic lesions, have
been documented in the mammillary bodies, thalamus, and hippocampal formation (Li et al., 2025). Among the affected thalamic
structures, the mediodorsal nucleus appears particularly relevant to the progression of cognitive impairment. Morphometric and
quantitative analyses indicate that neuronal depletion in this region may reach approximately 48% during Wernicke’s
encephalopathy and increase to nearly 64% in established Korsakoff Syndrome. This progressive neuronal loss has been
associated with greater impairment of memory and other cognitive functions (Li et al., 2025). Diffusion tensor imaging has
provided evidence of distinct thalamic connectivity patterns relevant to the neuropathology of Korsakoff Syndrome. The anterior
thalamic nuclei exhibit preferential structural connections with the hippocampal formation, and these pathways are markedly
diminished in individuals with Korsakoff Syndrome. In contrast, the mediodorsal thalamic nuclei are more closely associated
with frontal regions involved in executive processing, with alterations in these connections also being observed in alcohol-related
brain dysfunction (Boere et al., 2024).
From a functional perspective, the hippocampus is essential for establishing and consolidating newly acquired
memories. Information associated with these experiences is conveyed through the mammillary bodies and mammillothalamic
tract before reaching the anterior thalamic nuclei. These nuclei, in turn, contribute to the integration of memory-related
information within the cingulate cortex and broader cortical systems involved in autobiographical representation and the temporal
organization of conscious experience. Consequently, the cognitive impairment observed in Korsakoff Syndrome may be
understood not simply as a failure to retain stored information, but as a disruption of an interconnected neural system through
which ongoing experiences are incorporated into an individual's autobiographical history.
An Integrative Hypothesis: The Loss of Affective Valuation
We propose the following pathophysiological hypothesis. Individuals continuously experience multiple stimuli from
their environment, which activate perceptual, emotional, motivational, and autobiographical systems in parallel. In normal
conditions, sensory information initially reaches the thalamus, a structure responsible for significant integration and modulation
of environmental input. From there, this information is forwarded to limbic structures, particularly those involved in attributing
biological, emotional, and homeostatic relevance to these stimuli.
From this perspective, affective valuation precedes autobiographical consolidation of experience. Before an experience
is incorporated into cortical memory networks, it must acquire significance for the organism. It must represent gain, loss, threat,
pleasure, suffering, biological need, or existential meaning. Only after this attribution of relevance does the experience integrate
cortical circuits responsible for autobiography and continuous construction of personal identity.
This mechanism can be conceptualized as a “thalamic spotlight” that favors certain representations over others, directing
attentional resources toward emotionally significant experiences. Lesions in the thalamo-mammillo-hippocampal circuit impede

Korsakoff Syndrome, this mechanism breaks down. Lesions of the diencephalic-limbic circuits prevent new experiences from
receiving sufficient emotional valuation for incorporation into autobiographical organization. Consequently, the individual
ceases building history. The problem is not merely forgetting recent events; rather, it is the inability to transform present
experience into personal narrative. Events reach consciousness but fail to acquire the affective weight necessary to modify the
subject’s internal narrative. Thus, experience ceases to produce psychological transformation.
The “Hedonistic Autonomous” State and Loss of Existential Horizon
This hypothesis clarifies the progressive loss of critical judgment observed in many patients. When experiences do not
receive sufficient emotional significance, they cease to generate concern. Without concern, the need to review decisions, correct
behaviors, and anticipate consequences disappears. Concern is one of the primary drivers of continuous personality
reorganization. In the absence of this mechanism, indifference toward one’s own existence emerges.
Patients demonstrate reduced anticipatory anxiety, decreased self-criticism, impoverished personal responsibility, and
apparent unconcern when facing objectively serious situations. Notably, these patients often display a striking cheerfulness and
apparent contentment, leading to the paradoxical observation that the patient suffers less than those around them. This is not
happiness in the psychological sense, but rather the absence of the existential concern that normally accompanies human
consciousness.
Without a past to anchor them and without a future to worry about, the individual enters what we term a “hedonistic
autonomous” state, living solely in the immediate present and driven by basic impulses, such as the compulsive search for
alcohol, regardless of social or personal costs. From an evolutionary perspective, this state resembles the behavioral organization
of non-human animals, which operate primarily through limbic valuation without the cortical layer that provides temporal
continuity and existential meaning. The individual becomes what might be described as an “autonomous minimum,” capable of
responding to immediate stimuli but devoid of the autobiographical narrative that defines human agency.
The loss of existential horizon is perhaps the most devastating aspect of the syndrome. The patient loses not only
memory but the capacity to project themselves into the future, to anticipate consequences, to maintain occupational roles, and to
sustain social relationships. This disruption of the temporal dimension of consciousness—the inability to connect past, present,
and future into a coherent narrative—represents a fundamental loss of what constitutes human existence. This state differs
fundamentally from psychopathy, which involves intentional manipulation and intact future planning, and from mania, which
involves accelerated thinking and reduced sleep need. Rather, it is the behavioral consequence of lost existential horizon, a
condition in which the individual has been stripped of the temporal and narrative structures that organize human consciousness.
Clinical Gradations of Korsakoff Syndrome
Korsakoff Syndrome exists on a spectrum of severity rather than as an all-or-nothing phenomenon, which is crucial for
early psychiatric intervention. Recognition of partial forms is particularly important because these patients are frequently
misdiagnosed, and when diagnosis is finally established, the condition may have already progressed to an irreversible stage
(Batarfi, 2025). The psychiatric recognition of partial Korsakoff is essential precisely because intervention at earlier stages may
preserve the patient’s existential horizon and prevent the complete loss of autobiographical continuity.
We propose a classification into three clinical grades. In the mild stage, the patient retains significant autobiographical
continuity but demonstrates loss of critical judgment, inappropriate cheerfulness, and subtle disorientation. These patients often
continue working and maintaining social relationships, though with diminished judgment and initiative. In the moderate stage,

Confabulations may be mistaken for psychotic symptoms, particularly delusions or hallucinations. However, confabulations in
Korsakoff Syndrome are not systematized delusions arising from primary psychotic processes. Rather, they are autobiographical
reconstructions drawing on older, consolidated memories to fill gaps left by the inability to form new memories (Bielecka, 2025).
Patients do not believe in an alternative reality; rather, they attempt to maintain narrative coherence in the face of profound
memory system disruption.
Frontal behavioral symptoms, including impulsivity, disinhibition, and poor judgment, may lead to misdiagnosis of
primary frontotemporal dementia. However, in Korsakoff Syndrome, frontal dysfunction is secondary to diencephalic-limbic
lesions, not primary autonomous state accelerates, further reducing chances of meaningful recovery. This observation suggests
that psychiatric intervention in partial Korsakoff Syndrome should prioritize not merely symptom management but the
preservation of the patient’s connection to meaningful activity and social role.
Therapeutic Implications and the Role of Existential Engagement
The proposed framework has significant implications for psychiatric management of Korsakoff Syndrome. Traditional
approaches emphasize thiamine replacement and symptomatic management, yet the neuropsychiatric perspective suggests that
preservation of existential engagement may be equally important. Patients in the mild to moderate stages of Korsakoff Syndrome
retain sufficient autobiographical capacity to benefit from interventions that maintain their connection to work, social
relationships, and meaningful activity.
The principle of laboralidade—maintaining occupational engagement—appears to function as a protective factor against
progression to complete hedonistic autonomy. Patients who continue working, even with reduced capacity, maintain a connection
to their autobiographical past through occupational identity and to a projected future through ongoing responsibilities. This
temporal continuity, though diminished, may slow the rate of existential deterioration. Research on apathy and long-term care in
Korsakoff patients underscores the critical importance of maintaining occupational and social engagement (Papez, 1937). The

as mania, psychopathy, or primary frontotemporal dementia represents a critical missed opportunity for intervention.
Psychiatrists who recognize the specific pattern of affective valuation disruption coupled with preserved intellectual function
can identify these patients earlier and implement strategies to preserve their existential horizon.

4. Conclusion
Korsakoff Syndrome should be understood less as an isolated memory disorder and more as a disease of experience
integration. The amnestic deficit is only the most evident manifestation of a deeper alteration: the disruption of the process by
which present experiences acquire personal meaning. Lesions of the Papez circuit prevent emotional valuation of the present,
disconnecting individuals from their autobiographical past and projected future. This perspective bridges neuropsychology and
psychiatric phenomenology, offering new understanding of why these patients not only forget but cease to modify their existence
based on experience.
The proposed hypothesis, while consistent with current neurobiological evidence, requires empirical testing through
prospective studies examining the relationship between diencephalic-limbic disruption severity and loss of affective valuation.
The clinical grading system proposed here may serve as a starting point for developing standardized assessment tools for early
detection of partial Korsakoff Syndrome. Early psychiatric recognition, particularly of mild and moderate forms, is essential for
intervening before complete loss of the patient’s existential horizon. Psychiatrists who understand the syndrome’s
pathophysiology are uniquely positioned to make a difference, precisely because they encounter patients at a stage when
intervention remains possible.
