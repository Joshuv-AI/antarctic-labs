Gestational Glucose Intolerance and the Risk of Hypertensive Disorders of Pregnancy
Authors: Jacqueline Maya, Kaitlyn E James, Carolin C M Schulte, Sarah Hsu, Tanayott Thaweethai, Deepti Pant, Lydia Shook, Michael C Honigberg, Marie‐France Hivert, Camille E Powe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe evaluated associations between untreated mild hyperglycemia and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy.MethodsIn this retrospective study at a US academic medical center practicing universal 2‐step gestational diabetes (GD) testing, we defined normal glucose tolerance as a normal screening 50‐g, 1‐hour glucose load test (<140 mg/dL). If the glucose loading test was abnormal, a diagnostic 100‐g, 3‐hour oral glucose tolerance test was performed. We defined GD as ≥2 abnormal oral glucose tolerance test values (Carpenter–Coustan criteria) and gestational glucose intolerance (GGI, untreated subclinical hyperglycemia) as 0 (GGI‐0) or 1 (GGI‐1) abnormal oral glucose tolerance test value. We divided GD into treated and untreated (given use of older diagnostic criteria in the study period). We examined associations between GGI or GD and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (primary outcome) or its subtypes (gestational hypertension, preeclampsia) using generalized estimating equations with confounder adjustment.ResultsAmong 41 484 pregnancies, 84% had normal glucose tolerance, 12% had GGI (GGI‐0: 9.0%, GGI‐1: 3.0%) and 3.5% had GD. There was greater risk of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy in GGI compared with normal glucose tolerance (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.24 [95% CI, 1.11–1.38]). This was attributable to increased risk in GGI‐1 (aOR, 1.53 [95% CI, 1.27–1.85]), similar to untreated GD (aOR, 1.76 [95% CI, 1.37–2.26]). The risk of gestational hypertension was increased in GGI‐1 (aOR, 1.66 [95% CI, 1.30–2.12]) and untreated GD (aOR, 1.89 [95% CI, 1.36–2.63]) but not in treated GD (aOR, 0.84 [95% CI, 0.58–1.23). The risk of preeclampsia was similarly increased in GGI‐1, treated GD, and untreated GD (aOR, 1.46–1.75).ConclusionsMild untreated hyperglycemia in pregnancy is associated with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy; hyperglycemia treatment is associated with reduced gestational hypertension risk.Keywords: epidemiology, gestational diabetes, gestational hypertension, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, preeclampsiaSubject Categories: Pregnancy, Epidemiology, Risk Factors

Our findings suggest it is possible that treating subclinical hyperglycemia in pregnancies may modify the risk of hypertensive disorders in pregnancy

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) are associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes that affect both the fetus and the birthing individual. HDP increase the risk of fetal growth restriction and small for gestational age birth weight and are associated with increased risk of preterm delivery, cesarean delivery, and postnatal complications. 1 Further, HDP are associated with adverse long term cardiovascular health in the birthing individual as well as the offspring. 1 , 2 , 3 Gestational diabetes (GD) is an independent risk factor for HDP 4 , 5 and treatment of GD reduces the risk of developing HDP. 6 , 7

In the universal 2‐step approach for screening and diagnosis of GD commonly used in the United States, all pregnant patients undergo a nonfasting 50‐g glucose loading test (GLT) at 24 to 28 weeks of gestation. 6 , 8 If the blood glucose after 1 hour is elevated (≥130–140 mg/dL, depending on local practice) a 100‐g 3‐hour diagnostic oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) is recommended. According to the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, those with ≥2 or more abnormal values on OGTT are diagnosed and treated as GD. 6 Pregnant individuals with 0 or 1 abnormal values on OGTT, referred to in this study as gestational glucose intolerance (GGI), are not usually treated, because the benefits of treatment are unknown.

We and others have shown that individuals with GGI have increased risk of perinatal complications (cesarean delivery, severe perineal lacerations, clavicular fracture, and shoulder dystocia) 9 and higher odds of developing type 2 diabetes later in life. 10 , 11 In a meta‐analysis, Roeckner et al. found increased odds of HDP when comparing those with GGI and 1 abnormal diagnostic OGTT value to either those with a normal OGTT or those with normal glucose tolerance. 12 However, this and other previous studies have grouped gestational hypertension and preeclampsia together and were therefore unable to examine if associations differed between these HDP subtypes.

We tested for an association between GGI and HDP (both gestational hypertension and preeclampsia, together and individually) in a large retrospective cohort study.

METHODS

The Massachusetts General Hospital Maternal Health Cohort contains prenatal data on 56 107 pregnancies from 39 252 individuals who delivered at Massachusetts General Hospital between October 1998 and March 2016. The use of electronic health records for this study was approved and the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board. The data are accessible with institutional review board approval and a data use agreement. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Singleton pregnancies with a first prenatal visit before 20 weeks of gestation were included. We excluded pregnancies with incomplete GD screening (patients without GLT data, patients whose GLT was performed before 22 weeks of gestation, patients with missing or incomplete OGTT data, possibly due to inability to tolerate the OGTT, declining the OGTT, or having a GLT result ≥200 mg/dL) 13 and individuals with preexisting diabetes who do not undergo GD screening during pregnancy. 13 Further, we excluded any individual with evidence of preexisting or chronic hypertension (hypertension medication prescribed before 20 weeks of gestation, evidence of chronic hypertension in electronic medical record, or elevated blood pressures (systolic BP ≥140 or diastolic BP ≥90) before 20 weeks of gestation) and missing information to allow for categorization of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (eg, first prenatal visit after 20 weeks of gestation).

We used a 1‐hour 50‐g GLT to divide pregnancies into normal glucose tolerance (GLT <140 mg/dL) and abnormal glucose screening (GLT ≥140 mg/dL). The latter group underwent a 3‐hour diagnostic OGTT. We ascertained GGI in pregnancies with an abnormal GLT and 0 or 1 abnormal values on OGTT. We further divided the GGI group into GGI with 0 abnormal OGTT values (GGI‐0) and GGI with 1 abnormal OGTT value (GGI‐1), according to Carpenter–Coustan criteria. 14 GD was defined as ≥2 of 4 abnormal values on OGTT. 6 We extracted glucose values from the OGTT to classify each pregnancy using Carpenter–Coustan criteria, which are currently used more frequently in the United States. 14 National Diabetes Data Group (NDDG) criteria were used clinically at the time of the pregnancy to diagnose and treat individuals during the study period. Carpenter–Coustan criteria use lower glucose cutoffs; as such, more individuals will meet Carpenter–Coustan criteria for GD compared with NDDG thresholds. Therefore, there is a group of individuals retroactively classified as Carpenter–Coustan GD in our study who did not meet NDDG criteria and were therefore not diagnosed or treated for GD during their pregnancy.

HDP were ascertained using electronic health record information. In pregnancies normotensive before 20 weeks of gestation, HDP were defined as either (1) 2 elevated BP measurements (systolic BP ≥140 or diastolic BP ≥90) >4 hours apart after 20 weeks of gestation and up to 1 week postpartum; (2) HDP noted as an indication for cesarean delivery, indication for induction, or complication of labor in the delivery record, or (3) 1 elevated BP after 20 weeks of gestation with evidence of preeclampsia (Table S1), preeclampsia was defined as ≥1 elevated BP after 20 weeks of gestation or HDP notation in the delivery record, plus either meeting quantitative laboratory criteria for preeclampsia (Table S1) or having an International Classification of Diseases (ICD) code for eclampsia (624.6) at the delivery encounter. Gestational hypertension was defined as HDP without features of preeclampsia. The HDP definition underwent blinded validation via chart review by maternal‐fetal medicine specialists and had an accuracy >90%.

We described pregnancy characteristics by glucose exposure category. We calculated the frequency of primary (HDP) and secondary (gestational hypertension and preeclampsia) outcomes by glucose exposure category. Primary analyses compared the rate of HDP in GGI to that in normal glucose tolerance. Secondary analyses compared the rate of HDP between normal glucose tolerance and (1) GGI‐0, (2) GGI‐1, (3) GD as a whole, (4) undiagnosed and thus untreated Carpenter–Coustan GD, and (5) diagnosed and treated NDDG GD. Furthermore, we investigated the association between glucose tolerance subgroups and HDP subtypes (gestational hypertension and preeclampsia).

Statistical Analysis

We conducted several sensitivity analyses. To better assess the impact of specific treatment modalities in the treated‐GD group, we further divided pregnancies who met criteria for NDDG GD criteria, and thus were diagnosed and treated during the study period into 2 subgroups (analysis conducted after 2007 when medication was available): (1) individuals who met NDDG criteria for GD and received lifestyle interventions without pharmacotherapy (GDA1) and (2) individuals who met NDDG criteria for GD and received pharmacotherapeutic management in addition to lifestyle intervention (GDA2). For sensitivity analyses on the HDP outcome, a more stringent definition for pregnancies with no evidence of HDP was used. In this sensitivity analysis, pregnancies that did not meet described HDP criteria but had a single elevated BP after 20 weeks of gestation were removed from the group without HDP and excluded from analysis. Another sensitivity analysis restricted HDP cases to those diagnosed after 28 weeks of gestation to account for temporal associations between GD (screening conducted at 24–28 weeks of gestation) and the outcome. For diagnostic timing of HDP, we used the first elevated BP (systolic BP≥140 mm Hg, diastolic BP≥90 mm Hg) as a proxy for the onset of hypertension for those pregnancies with HDP who had available outpatient BPs. Only those HDP cases with first elevated BP after 28 weeks of gestation were included in this analysis.

For each analysis, we fit logistic regression models using generalized estimating equations to account for clustering by individual (as some individuals contributed >1 pregnancy). We adjusted for potential confounders including maternal age, first‐trimester body mass index, parity, insurance status, marital status, and race or ethnicity.

To account for missing covariate data (described in Table S2), we performed multiple imputation and generated 25 data sets that contained all covariates and the outcome of the logistic regression model. 15 We used a previously described spline‐based interpolation/extrapolation procedure to standardize prenatal weight to 12 weeks of gestation and subtracted the derived value from weight at delivery to calculate gestational weight gain. 16 In cases of missing weight at delivery, we used the same extrapolation procedure to calculate the weight at delivery.

Analysis was conducted in R version 4.3.1 (package information presented in Data S1). 17 A 2‐sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Our final analytic cohort consisted of 41 484 pregnancies in 30 342 people (Figure 1). Of these pregnancies, 35 048 (84.5%) had normal glucose tolerance, 4968 (12%) had GGI, and 1468 (3.5%) had GD (Figure 2). GGI was subdivided into pregnancies with a normal OGTT (GGI‐0; N=3724 [9.0%]) and those with 1 abnormal OGTT value (GGI‐1; N=1244 [3.0%]). In the GD exposure category, N=580 had undiagnosed (untreated) Carpenter–Coustan GD and N=888 had diagnosed (treated) NDDG GD. In the overall cohort, N=2855 (6.9%) pregnancies were complicated by HDP, including N=1465 (3.5% of the total cohort, 51.3% of the HDP pregnancies) with gestational hypertension, and N=1390 (3.4% of the total cohort, 48.7% of the HDP pregnancies) with preeclampsia. Primary and Secondary Outcomes by Pregnancy Glucose Tolerance Category

Adjusted models assessing the risk of HDP in different glucose tolerance exposure categories compared with normal glucose tolerance showed an increased odds of HDP in GGI pregnancies (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.24 [95% CI, 1.11–1.38]; P<0.001) and pregnancies with GD (aOR, 1.42 [95% CI, 1.19–1.70]; P<0.001) compared with normal glucose tolerance (adjusted and unadjusted model output in Table S4). When assessing GGI‐0 and GGI‐1 separately, the odds of HDP were no different from normal glucose tolerance in the GGI‐0 subtype, whereas the GGI‐1 subtype demonstrated an increased risk of HDP (aOR, 1.53 [95% CI, 1.27–1.85]; P<0.001) (Figure 3A). When GD was subtyped into untreated GD and treated GD, we did not observe a significant difference in HDP risk for treated GD cases compared with normal glucose tolerance (aOR, 1.21 [95% CI, 0.95–1.53]; P=0.12), whereas untreated GD had increased odds of HDP (aOR, 1.76 [95% CI, 1.37–2.26]; P<0.001) (Figure 3A).

When assessing gestational hypertension and preeclampsia separately, GGI pregnancies had an increased risk of both conditions compared with normal glucose tolerance (Table S3). There was no increased risk of either condition in GGI‐0 compared with normal glucose tolerance after adjustment (P>0.15). In contrast, GGI‐1 was associated with an increased risk of both gestational hypertension (aOR, 1.66 [95% CI, 1.30–2.12]; Pv0.001) and preeclampsia (aOR, 1.46 [95% CI, 1.11–1.90]; P=0.006) compared with normal glucose tolerance (Figure 3B). GD demonstrated no difference in gestational hypertension risk when compared with normal glucose tolerance (aOR, 1.25 [95% CI, 0.97–1.61], P=0.08). However, when GD was subtyped into untreated versus treated, untreated GD was associated with increased odds of gestational hypertension (aOR, 1.89 [95% CI, 1.36–2.63], P<0.001), whereas treated GD was not associated with an increased risk of gestational hypertension compared with normal glucose tolerance (aOR, 0.84 [95% CI, 0.58, 1.23], P=0.40). In contrast, GD as a whole (aOR, 1.67 [95% CI, 1.33–2.11], P<0.001) and subtyped into untreated GD (aOR, 1.75 [95% CI, 1.24–2.47], P=0.002) and treated GD (aOR, 1.63 [95% CI, 1.22–2.18], P<0.001) demonstrated increased odds of preeclampsia compared with normal glucose tolerance (Figure 3C).

The sensitivity analysis conducted on NDDG GD treatment modality showed no differential impact of treatment type on association with HDP, consistent with our primary findings (Table S4). Sensitivity analyses using a stricter definition for normotensive pregnancies (excluding individuals with any documentation of elevated BP measurements that did not meet criteria for HDP) and including only HDP cases diagnosed after 28 weeks of gestation supported our primary and secondary analyses (Tables S5 through S7).

DISCUSSION

In this large retrospective cohort study, we found an increased risk of HDP in individuals with GGI‐1 (1 abnormal value on diagnostic OGTT) compared with pregnancies without hyperglycemia. The magnitude of the effect observed in GGI‐1 on the risk of HDP was similar to that observed in untreated GD. When stratifying HDP into gestational hypertension alone and preeclampsia, the risk of gestational hypertension was increased to a similar degree in both GGI‐1 and untreated GD, and the risk in treated GD was similar or lower to that in normal glucose tolerance. This leads us to hypothesize that glucose lowering treatment in GGI‐1 may be a strategy to prevent gestational hypertension, though this would need to be tested in future interventional studies. In contrast, the risk of preeclampsia was elevated to a similar degree in GGI‐1, untreated GD, and treated GD, suggesting that although hyperglycemia is a risk factor for preeclampsia, it may not be as modifiable by glucose lowering treatment. These findings have implications for both obstetric risk assessment and the understanding of mechanisms underlying HDP.

Congruent with our study, the Hyperglycemia and Adverse Pregnancy Outcomes study demonstrated that the risk of preeclampsia increased with increasing blood glucose levels on a 75‐g, 2 hour OGTT. 18 A systematic review and meta‐analysis of 25 observational studies from Roeckner et al., which in total was of similar sample size to the present study, found increased odds of HDP when comparing individuals with GGI‐1 to those with either GGI‐0 or normal glucose. 12 Although this study did not differentiate between gestational hypertension and preeclampsia, nor assess treated versus untreated GD, our results had similar effect sizes for the risk of HDP in the GGI‐1 group. We were able to differentiate treated vs. untreated GD, and thus, we were able to further demonstrate that that risk reduction in HDP with GD treatment was mostly attributable to a reduction in gestational hypertension, as the rates of preeclampsia were similarly elevated in GGI‐1 and both treated and untreated GD. This contrasts somewhat with the findings of the 2009 randomized trial of treatment for mild GD conducted by Landon et al., which found a statistically significant reduction in the rates of preeclampsia in women who received treatment for mild GD. 19 The lack of association between treatment and preeclampsia risk reduction in our study may be due to greater GD severity represented in the treated NDDG GD group compared with the untreated Carpenter–Coustan group, masking potential treatment effects. We note that our study was observational, and prospective randomized evaluation is required to test the causal effect of GGI‐1 treatment for prevention of gestational hypertension. Larger, multicenter studies more adequately powered for additional confounders could also further investigate the impact of various covariates, including race, ethnicity, body mass index, socioeconomic status, and age, on these findings.

The condition of gestational hypertension is often grouped with the more clinically severe phenotype of preeclampsia under the umbrella of HDP. American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommends close monitoring of those pregnancies with gestational hypertension mostly to the risk of progression to preeclampsia 1 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 Though it is true that gestational hypertension can progress to preeclampsia, we found a difference in the relationship of between hyperglycemia in pregnancy and these HDP subtypes. Our findings suggest there may be differing underlying pathogenesis of these 2 HDP subtypes. Congruent with our findings, Caruso et al. found that only individuals with gestational hypertension, and not those with preeclampsia, had a metabolic profile (decreased insulin sensitivity and higher triglyceride concentrations) that is characteristically found in individuals with insulin resistance. 26 Additionally, in a genome wide association study of preeclampsia and gestational hypertension across multinational biobanks, maternal variants associated with gestational hypertension that were not associated with preeclampsia were identified, and the phenotype of gestational hypertension was more strongly genetically correlated with chronic hypertension compared with preeclampsia. 27 Further research is necessary to understand more about the pathophysiology of gestational hypertension being more tightly linked to maternal cardiometabolic risk as compared with the abnormal placentation biologic pathway that may characterize preeclampsia. 1 , 25

Our study has both strengths and limitations. First, this large cohort provides a unique opportunity to observe the strength of associations for different subcategories of hyperglycemia compared with normal glucose tolerance, most notably GGI, a currently untreated dysglycemic state. The application of less stringent criteria for GD during the study period allowed us to investigate HDP and related outcomes in individuals with untreated GD who by current clinical standards would be diagnosed and treated. Second, we included a tertiary center and affiliated health centers that represent a range of racial and ethnic groups as well as different socioeconomic backgrounds. Third, the careful validation of the HDP variable likely provides internal validity superior to studies that use only ICD codes or other administrative data to ascertain outcomes. 13 However, our study also had limitations. As in other retrospective electronic health record‐based studies, data collection is limited by the quality and quantity of information gathered during clinical encounters. Unmeasured confounders may be present beyond those considered in this analysis. In addition, the potential impact of low‐dose aspirin was not assessed in this study. Current guidelines recommending low‐dose aspirin as prophylaxis for preeclampsia in high‐risk individuals were established in 2018. However, this recommendation was not present at the time the data were collected for the cohort in this study (1998–2016) and thus aspirin use was not considered in our analyses.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, our findings show increased risk of HDP among GGI pregnancies, which are not currently treated for hyperglycemia in the United States. The risk of HDP in GGI‐1 affected pregnancies was similar in magnitude to the risk observed in undiagnosed (and therefore untreated) GD. It is possible that treating hyperglycemia in GGI‐1 pregnancies may decrease the risk of HDP in this group, though this will need to be examined in future interventional studies. Our findings suggest that hyperglycemia may affect the risk of gestational hypertension and preeclampsia differently. This may reflect differing underlying mechanisms for these disorders and warrants further investigation. Prior observational studies within our cohort and others 9 , 16 have shown an association between untreated GGI‐1 and adverse maternal and perinatal outcomes. The present findings further support this association. A future randomized controlled trial that compares outcomes in treated versus untreated GGI‐1 could provide causal evidence to support treating these pregnancies with glucose‐lowering therapy.
