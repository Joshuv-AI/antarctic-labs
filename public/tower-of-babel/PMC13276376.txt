Influence of cooling rates on the fracture properties of asphalt concrete subjected to freeze-thaw cycles
Authors: Sayyed Ali Siyadati, Mansour Fakhri, M. R. M. Aliha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Low‑temperature cracking is one of the main causes of asphalt pavement damage in cold regions. While many parameters influencing its low-temperature performance are well-studied, the specific effect of the cooling rate during freeze-thaw cycles (FTCs) on the fracture performance of asphalt mixtures remains largely unexplored. In this study, the effect of cooling rate on the fracture toughness (FT), fracture energy (FE), and cracking‑resistance index (CRI) of asphalt concrete is investigated. For this purpose, dry and saturated semi-circular bend (SCB) samples were subjected to seven FTCs at different cooling rates (0.1, 0.2, 0.5 °C/min, and the shock‑freeze state) and temperatures (− 5 °C, − 15 °C, and − 20 °C), after which they were fractured under a fixed mixed‑mode I/II loading conditions. The results of this study show that increasing the cooling rate reduces all three parameters: FE, FT, and CRI. For instance, applying FTCs with a cooling rate of 0.1 °C/min was significantly less damaging, resulting in a 16% and 20% reduction in FT and FE, respectively, compared to 23% and 48% for the shock‑freeze state. Furthermore, increasing the cooling rate reduces both the displacement and the peak load at the time of fracture. This degradation is attributed to insufficient time for viscoelastic stress relaxation at rapid cooling rates, which promotes a brittle response characterized by higher stiffness and a greater tendency toward elastic behavior. Other factors also contribute to the reduction in mechanical performance, such as the increased thermal gradient between the core and surface, rapid freezing of surface water compared to core water, and volumetric expansion of trapped water within the core. These phenomena collectively increase thermal stresses and accelerate the formation of microcracks. Finally, the influence of temperature in FTCs was characterized: decreasing the temperature reduced the displacement at failure, whereas the peak load first increased down to − 15 °C before decreasing at lower temperatures.

Introduction

Cracks in asphalt pavement can lead to gradual damage to the pavement, and if not properly maintained, they increase maintenance costs for road organizations, reduce user satisfaction, and may even lead to casualties. These cracks start as microcracks, and as they grow and spread, visible cracks appear. Some of these can cause structural damage to the pavement. Two main reasons that cause cracks are environmental conditions and loading factors. Environmental factors, such as daily temperature fluctuations and temperature difference between day and night, rainfall, and snow, induce thermal stresses. Simultaneously, loading factors, including traffic volume and vehicle type, apply direct mechanical stress to the pavement.

In cold regions, the combination of rain or snow with subzero temperatures significantly increases the formation of microcracks in pavement. This is particularly true when the pavement is saturated or nearly saturated from precipitation. As temperatures fall below freezing, the volumetric expansion of trapped water within the pavement’s voids creates internal stress1. This process is the fundamental mechanism of frost damage, and it has recently been validated through numerical simulations using the Discrete Element Method2. The repeated freezing and thawing cycles gradually reduce the pavement's resistance to failure. Consequently, pavements in different geographical locations with varying climatic conditions may experience different cooling rates, leading to varying levels of damage.

In previous studies, the effect of temperature on the fracture resistance of asphalt mixtures was evaluated. These studies can be classified into several general categories. The first group investigated the fracture resistance of asphalt samples at subzero temperatures. Within this group, diverse parameters have been considered; for example, some studies examined aggregate types, while others focused on the effect of bitumen types on the mechanical properties of asphalt mixtures. Furthermore, various fracture modes were analyzed, and other articles explored the effect of sample geometry and size effect. Additional parameters, such as loading rate, different compaction method, gradation of aggregate, air void, notch length, thickness of samples, fracture test temperature and additives have also been investigated.

The influence of asphalt components on the fracture properties of asphalt mixtures has been extensively studied. Aliha et al.3 investigated the effect of two different aggregates (limestone and siliceous aggregates) on the FT of asphalt mixtures. Granite and limestone aggregates were studied by Kim and El Hussein4. Similarly, Li et al.5 investigated the FE of asphalt mixtures prepared with granite and limestone. In a research conducted by Eghbali et al.6, the effect of different nominal maximum aggregate sizes (NMAS) on FT was examined. Furthermore, the effect of fine and coarse aggregates on the fracture resistance of asphalt mixtures was studied by Aliha et al.3. The impact of different gradation on the aggregate skeleton studied by shi et al.7. The effect of different binders (PG 52-28 and PG 64-22) was also evaluated by Aliha et al.3. Additionally, other researchers have studied the fusion characteristics of aged and virgin asphalt8 and the impact of different mixing conditions on the degree of blending between them9. The impact of different percentages of air voids (3%, 5%, and 7%) on FT was investigated by Aliha et al.3,10. In addition, Zhang et al.11 investigated the effect of steel slag on the interface interaction between aggregate and asphalt in mixtures where steel slag was used to replace limestone.

The effect of sample geometry on FT was investigated in the article by Ziari et al.12. In their research, three types of geometry (symmetric specimen, classical-modified SCB specimen, and symmetric ENDB specimen) were studied. Contrary to the results of their study, which found that ENDB samples exhibited the highest FT, Fuan et al.13 reported that SCB samples had higher fracture resistance. Song et al. observed that increasing sample diameter from 76 to 150 mm led to an increase in FT by 7.6% to 25.2% across different mixtures and notch-radius ratios14,15. Additionally, Pirmohammad et al.16 obtained similar results and observed that FT rises as the sample diameter increases; moreover, this phenomenon is more significant at low temperatures. However, some researchers found no significant differences in FT among samples with different diameters17. In a research by Karimi et al.18 the effect of different notch lengths (25 mm, 32 mm, and 38 mm) on FT and FE was examined. The effect of different sample thicknesses on FT was evaluated by Eghbali et al.19.

In studies conducted by various researchers, different pure and mixed modes (I/II) have been used to determine the fracture resistance of samples. In most cases, the weakest mode has been identified as the mixed mode I/II with higher fraction of mode I10,20–22. In pure and mixed mode I and III, Haghighatpour and Aliha observed that the lowest FT occurred under pure mode III23. Fuan et al.13 reported a direct relationship between loading rate (1 to 6 mm/min) and FT for both SCB and ENDB samples. Motamedi et al.24 also reported similar results, observing an increase in FT in modes I and III with increasing loading rate. Haghighatpour and Aliha25 evaluated the effect of the compaction method and observed that samples compacted using the Marshall method had higher FT than samples produced using the gyratory method. This can be explained by greater number of normal strokes applied to the asphalt mixture. Additionally, other researchers have investigated the impact of different compaction degrees on the deformation characteristics of mixtures26 and the change in sample voids during the compaction process27.

The effect of fracture test temperature on the mechanical properties of asphalt mixtures has been evaluated in recent years. In these studies, it was observed that as the temperature decreases to -15 °C, the FT of the asphalt mixture increases and then decreases4,10,20,21,28,29. Furthermore, researchers have evaluated the effect of a wide variety of additives on the fracture resistance of asphalt mixtures. Materials such as hospital waste pyrolysis carbon black (HWPCB)12, Nano-SiO230, glass fiber and rejuvenator31 and hydrocarbon and carbon black32 have been studied.

The second group investigated the effect of freeze-thaw cycles (FTCs) on the mechanical properties of asphalt samples. Within this group, some studies focused on the number of FTCs and its impact on the fracture resistance of asphalt mixtures, while others examined the method of applying FTCs including the duration of exposure to freezing and the minimum subzero temperatures.

In several studies, the effect of the number of FTCs was investigated. Ren et al.33 observed that FT decreased with an increasing in the number of FTCs, and a maximum of 20 cycles was used. Haghighatpour and Aliha subjected ENDB samples to a maximum of 10 FTCs and with an increasing in the number of FTCs, the FT and FE of the samples decreased25. Fakhri et al.21,34 subjected SCB samples to a maximum of 11 FTCs, and reported that FT plateaued after 7 cycles. Haghighatpour and Aliha23, after applying 9 FTCs, observed a reduction in the FT of the specimens under both pure and mixed modes (I/III) loading conditions. As is clear, a wide range of 9 to 20 FTCs has been used, which indicates the lack of a specific standard.

In various studies, different time durations and temperatures have been considered for applying FTCs. For example, in the study conducted by Goh et al.35, the specimens were exposed to FTCs for 12 h at − 17.8°C, followed by 12 h in a water bath at room temperature. In the research conducted by Sanfilippo et al.36, the specimens were kept for 16 h at − 10°C and then 8 h at 20°C. In some studies, the specimens were subjected to 12 h at − 25°C and 12 h at 25°C23,25,37. In the study by Zhang et al.38, the specimens underwent 2.5 h at − 18°C and 1.5 h at 5°C. Feng et al.39 placed the specimens at − 22°C for 8 h, followed by 4 h at 60°C. Similarly, Benamara et al.40 kept asphalt samples for 6 h at − 5°C and then 6 h at 20°C. therefore, a wide range of subzero temperatures (− 5 to − 25°C) has been used in different studies in applying FTCs. A third group of studies examined the fracture resistance of asphalt mixtures using the AASHTO T-283 standard. The use of the T-283 standard, with respect to the defined freeze-thaw damage cycles, causes severe damage to samples and leads to significant strength loss12,18,30–32,41–45. A comparison between freeze-thaw damage applied using the T-283 standard and the application of FTCs under more realistic conditions was conducted by Fakhri et al.22.

The lack of a specific standard has led researchers to use different methods to apply FTCs to asphalt mixtures, contrary to the AASHTO T-283 Standard, which defines specific and standardized freeze-thaw damage cycles procedures. In addition, some important parameters such as the cooling rate have not been considered in freeze-thaw cycles, and no standard protocol has been developed for it, a point that has not been investigated in previous studies. In different regions, and in winter, air temperature decreases at different rates. In other words, the cooling rate can vary widely depending on location. This phenomenon, especially when it coincides with pavement saturation due to rain or snow, can cause microcracks in the pavement and reduce its fracture resistance. Therefore, it is important to study the effect of cooling rates on the fracture resistance of saturated asphalt mixtures.

In previous studies, the effect of cooling rates was examined using the Thermal Stress Restrained Specimen Test (TSRST) method. In this test, the samples are confined on both sides, subjected to temperature reduction at different cooling rates, and their internal stress is measured until failure46–54. In some researches, a cooling rate of 10 °C/hr has been used in accordance with the test procedure described by Jung and Vinson55–58. The problem with these studies is that, due to the sample confinement, it is not possible to evaluate the decrease in fracture strength caused by microcracks resulting from repeated FTCs at different temperatures and cooling rates. Additionally, sample saturation was not considered in previous studies, which limits their ability to represent real pavement conditions. This is crucial because the expansion of ice crystals formed in the asphalt mixture has a significant effect on the fracture strength of asphalt mixtures.

As a result, this study examines the fracture properties of asphalt mixtures under different cooling rates. The samples are first saturated and then subjected to FTCs at varying cooling rates. They are placed at temperatures of − 5, − 15, and − 20 °C, and seven FTCs are applied. Finally, after complete drying, the samples are fractured in a combined mode with a higher tensile component (Me = 0.8) at a temperature of − 5 °C.

Experimental

Materials specifications and sample preparation

Limestone aggregate was used to make asphalt samples. The specifications of the aggregates are presented in Table 2. Bitumen with a penetration grade of 60–70 was also used, and its specifications are also presented in Table 3. Figure 1 shows the gradation of the aggregates. This gradation follows the Iranian Pavement Roads Standard (code 234) and is based on gradation number 4. After determining the optimum bitumen percentage (5.5%) and the optimum air void percentage of (4%), the final samples were made using the gyratory compaction method. The specifications of the gyratory compaction device are presented in Table 4. Finally, after cutting the cylindrical samples, SCB specimens were produced. The thickness of the SCB specimens is 3 cm, and the notch thickness is 1 mm. Other specifications of the SCB specimens are shown in Fig. 2. The reason for using SCB samples is their simplicity of preparation and their ability to appropriately simulate real pavement compaction. However, other types of samples such as disc-shape compact tension (DCT)59, Brazilian disc samples (BD)60, single-edge notched bend beam (SENB)61, and edge-notched disc bend (ENDB)24,62 have also been used by other researchers. Furthermore, the selection of the SCB test is directly aligned with the primary objective of this study: to quantify the degradation in fracture properties after the samples has been subjected to damage from FTCs in a saturated state. The SCB test is exceptionally well-suited for this purpose, as it measures the residual fracture resistance of a pre-conditioned specimen. Freeze-thaw cycles procedure

As mentioned in the introduction, there is no standardized protocol for applying FTCs. Consequently, researchers have used different durations and temperatures to apply the FTCs. In this study, the samples were first saturated for 15 min using a vacuum pump and then placed in a water bath at 10 °C for 3 days to ensure full saturation. Afterward, the samples were vacuumed and transferred to a freezer at a temperature of 10°C for 8 h. The freezer's temperature was then lowered at different cooling rates (0.1, 0.2, and 0.5 °C/min) to reach the target temperatures of − 5°C, − 15°C, and − 20°C. This range of rates was deliberately chosen to conduct a parametric study covering a wide spectrum of thermal severities. The selection was intended to represent the diversity of potential conditions found in various cold regions, such as those across Iran, which experience everything from slow, overnight temperature drops to rapid thermal changes. The objective was to provide a comprehensive dataset that allows other researchers to interpolate and apply these findings to their specific local climatic conditions using meteorological data, thereby enhancing the broader applicability of the results. The time required to reach these temperatures ranged from 30 min (temperature reduction from + 10 to − 5°C at a rate of 0.5 °C/min) to 5 h (temperature reduction from + 10 to − 20°C at a rate of 0.1 °C/min). A total of seven FTCs were applied to the samples. The reason for selecting 7 FTCs was that most of the resistance drop occurs by the 7th cycle, with little noticeable change afterward21. The loading capacity of the machine was 20 KN and a loading rate of 0.3 mm/min was applied to fracture the samples. In this study, changing the support distance was used to create mixed-mode loading, and a combined mode with a higher tensile component (Me = 0.8) was used. Results and discussion

Figure 5 shows the changes in the FT of SCB samples after applying 7 FTCs at three different temperatures of − 5 ℃, − 15 ℃, and − 20 ℃. Saturated samples are marked in blue and dry samples in red. The highest values of FT occurred at a rate of 0.1 ℃/min, and with an increasing cooling rate, the FT decreased and reached its lowest value in the shock-freeze state. Thus, at different temperatures and at rates of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, and shock-freeze in the saturated state, an average decrease of 16%, 19%, 21%, and 23% was observed, and in the dry state, a decrease of 8%, 12%, 13%, and 16% was recorded compared to the base samples. The reason for the higher FT at lower rates can be attributed to two general factors: the mechanical properties of the asphalt mixture and the behavior of ice crystals formed in the samples during the repetition of FTCs.Fig. 5Fracture toughness values of saturated and dry samples at different cooling rates (a) − 5°C, (b) − 15°C, and (c) − 20°C.

Fracture toughness values of saturated and dry samples at different cooling rates (a) − 5°C, (b) − 15°C, and (c) − 20°C.

Regarding the mechanical properties of asphalt mixtures, two reasons are important. First, a rapid cooling rates lead to the development of a thermal gradient between the surface and the core of the sample, which results in the surface contracting faster than the core, thereby subjecting the sample to tensile stresses. Repeated FTCs intensify the formation of thermal stresses in the surface areas and drive these stresses closer to the maximum tensile strength of asphalt concrete, ultimately resulting in an increasing in formation of microcracks within the asphalt mixture.

Meanwhile, slow cooling rates result in the development of a similar thermal gradient between the core and the surface of the samples, and given sufficient time, a uniform contraction occurs in both regions. In other words, the differential thermal contraction between the core and the surface of the sample is reduced, thereby leading to a smaller decrease in FT due to the reduced formation of microcracks caused by lower tensile stresses.

Although the thermal gradient between the surface and the core of the sample was not directly measured using thermocouples in the present study, previous studies have confirmed that this temperature differential significantly contributes to the development of thermal stresses. Recently, Sun et al.65 demonstrated the critical role of transient heat transfer in the TSRST, highlighting a distinct temperature lag between the specimen’s surface and its average volume, which can significantly alter the calculated failure temperature. Similarly, Steiner and Hofko66 reported a distinct difference between the chamber air cooling rate and the actual specimen core cooling rate, indicating that the core temperature of the specimen is not solely a function of the chamber temperature but is also influenced by external conditions such as air velocity, humidity, and radiation. Furthermore, Tabatabaee et al.67 showed that thermal stress is a function of the core temperature, and the cooling rate directly influences the stress build-up behavior even after the core reaches a stable temperature. It should also be noted that embedding temperature sensors directly inside the specimen during tensile testing can induce stress concentration and compromise structural integrity; consequently, some researchers have opted to use a dummy specimen for temperature monitoring68,69.

Studies conducted using the TSRST method report similar results. Luo et al.70 exposed samples to five different cooling rates and observed that faster cooling rates result in higher thermal stress in asphalt samples. Alavi and Hajj54 also found that the stiffness modulus of the samples increased with increasing cooling rate. Wang et al.47 selected a cooling rate of 2 °C/h based on studies in cold regions of the United States, investigated the number of microcracks in asphalt pavements, and observed that the number of microcracks increases from 5621 at a cooling rate of 0.1 °C/step to 10,778 at 0.3 °C/step. Li et al.71 indicated that samples subjected to a rate of 10 °C/h reached fatigue failure in 35% less time than those subjected to 2 °C/h. Marasteanu et al.56 found that subjecting samples to slower cooling rates (2°C/h) results in a lower fracture temperature compared to rapid cooling rates (10°C/h). Wang et al.72 also indicated that exposing samples to cooling rates of 5°C/h and 10°C/h leads to average break temperatures of − 30.87°C and − 27.34°C, respectively. Pszczola et al.46, Shen and Kirkner49, and Tabatabaee and Bahia53 also reported similar results, confirming that with increasing cooling rate, thermal stress invariably increases.

The second reason related to the mechanical properties of asphalt mixtures is their viscoelastic behavior, which is particularly effective in resisting thermal stresses. Due to this behavior and under slow cooling rates, a portion of the stresses generated in the asphalt mixture can be dissipated through stress relaxation, thereby preventing the internal stresses of the sample from exceeding the maximum tensile strength of the asphalt concrete. Conversely, at rapid cooling rates, the asphalt specimens do not have sufficient time to accommodate thermal strains, which consequently leads to brittle behavior. In summary, since cracking occurs when the thermal stress exceeds the failure strength73, at high rates, the viscoelastic behavior of the asphalt mixture cannot play an effective role in mitigating damage within the asphalt concrete and conditions for crack initiation in the asphalt mixture become more favorable.

This principle is Supported by findings from various testing methodologies. For instance, studies using the TSRST method confirm that at the beginning of the stress-temperature curve, the relaxation zone is formed due to the viscoelastic behavior of the asphalt mixture74 and rapid cooling rates, compared to slow ones, cause the slope of stress–temperature curve to reach a constant value sooner and the sample behavior to become elastic, resulting in a smaller relaxation zone46,70. In addition, the importance of stress relaxation is not limited to thermal cracking. Recent studies also show a strong link between a material’s ability to relax stress and its fatigue resistance at intermediate temperatures75. This suggests it is a key indicator of performance against various types of cracking.

There are other factors that can affect the viscoelastic behavior of asphalt mixtures. For example, Pszczola et al.76 examined the stiffness modules of different samples and concluded that the source and modification of bitumen can have a significant effect on the mechanical properties of asphalt mixtures at low temperatures. Rahbar-Rastegar et al.77 investigated the effect of aging on the viscoelastic behavior of asphalt mixtures and found that mechanical properties including fracture energy, flexibility index, and fracture strain tolerance will decrease. Zhang et al.78 reported that aging in asphalt mixtures will induces changes in Linear Viscoelastic (LVE) properties, which result in increased stiffness and decreased relaxation capability. Apeagyei et al.79 observed that the thermal cracking performance of asphalt concrete pavements is significantly dependent on the cooling rate, with lower rates providing more opportunity for relaxation.

As shown in Fig. 5, saturated samples exhibit lower FT than dry samples. This reduction is attributed to the freezing of water in the air voids of the samples, the expansion of ice crystals, and the formation of microcracks within the asphalt mixture. Repeated freeze-thaw cycles cause water to move along the bitumen-aggregate interface, thereby reducing bitumen adhesion21,22,34,35,39 and connecting adjacent voids through water movement36. These combined effects lead to a greater drop in the FT of saturated samples compared with dry ones. Moreover, studies utilizing X-ray Computed Tomography (CT) have investigated these damage mechanisms by tracking the evolution of air void parameters, such as their number, diameter, and interconnection, and the formation of microcracks, which facilitates the growth of larger ice crystals under repeated freeze-thaw cycles36,80,81. Similar destructive effects of FTCs have been extensively studied in concrete. For example, research by Pilehvar et al.82 shows that the compressive strength of concrete samples decreases after 28 FTCs. Furthermore, Sanak et al.83 investigated the combined impact of FTCs and salt erosion and found that increasing the number of FTCs results in a decrease in compressive strength and causes the relative dynamic elastic modulus to initially increase before decreasing rapidly.

Different cooling rates also influence the mechanisms of ice crystal formation and growth. Under rapid cooling, the water within the surface voids freezes quickly due to the sudden temperature drop, while the central part has not yet reached the freezing point since heat transfer toward the core is relatively slow in asphalt. This slow heat transfer results from the asphalt’s low thermal conductivity84. It should be noted that while the thermal conductivity of the saturated samples used in this study is expected to be higher than that of dry asphalt, it remains sufficiently low to cause significant thermal gradients under rapid cooling conditions. As a result, the surface of samples contracts faster than the core, generating tensile stresses in the sample and accelerating the formation of microcracks. The inherently slow heat transfer in asphalt mixtures was highlighted by Mamlouk et al.85, who calculated that it could take between 4.6 and 8 h for a sample to cool from room temperature to 0 °C.

Moreover, rapid cooling prevents water within surface voids from moving, as they freeze almost immediately in their original positions. This phenomenon, known as hydraulic pressure generation in porous media, occurs because water does not have sufficient time to be expelled from the pores during rapid cooling86. Consequently, water in the core have limited mobility because of surface freezing and spatial confinement. This confinement means that as the trapped water freezes, its inherent 9% volumetric expansion is resisted by the surrounding solid matrix, generating immense tensile stresses87,88. In contrast, during slow cooling, the longer cooling time allows for more gradual heat transfer into the interior. This process enables water both to be physically expelled from the outer pores and to migrate toward the freezing front within the material ; a mechanism driven by cryosuction89. This migration facilitates the formation of more regular, less constrained ice crystals inside the voids90. As a result, well-formed ice crystals exert less stress on the pore walls, leading to fewer microcracks than under rapid cooling. The degree of pore saturation is another critical factor influencing the magnitude of hydraulic pressure91. According to Powers’s theory, FTCs are destructive if the concrete samples are saturated beyond 91.7%92. However, other studies suggest that saturation levels between 70 and 80% can also be damaging93.

In Fig. 6, an increase in fracture toughness is observed from − 5 to − 15°C, followed by a decrease at − 20°C. This trend can be attributed to the viscoelastic properties of the bitumen binder. As the temperature decreases to − 15°C, the binder transitions towards a more elastic state, resulting in an increase in stiffness that enhances the mixture’s resistance to crack initiation and thereby increases its FT. However, this trend reverses below − 15°C. This turning point at − 15°C is strongly correlated with the binder’s glass transition temperature (Tg), which is the critical temperature below which the binder shifts from viscoelastic to brittle behavior. Research by Khattak et al.94 has reported the Tg for neat bitumen to be approximately − 15°C. It is noteworthy that modified binders typically exhibit lower Tg values, as observed by Champion et al.95. Once the temperature drops below the binder’s Tg, its embrittlement becomes the primary driver of failure. In this brittle state, the binder can no longer accommodate the thermal stresses caused by its greater contraction compared to the aggregates, leading to the formation of microcracks once these stresses exceed the binder’s tensile strength4,5,21,96,97. Additionally, this mismatch in contraction can cause debonding at the binder-aggregate interface, weakening the adhesive bond96–98. Furthermore, these cracks allow for greater water migration to the binder-aggregate interface, making the mixture more susceptible to damage during FTCs36. As a result, a combination of factors including the binder’s brittle behavior, microcracks within the binder, reduced adhesion at the binder-aggregate interface, and the increased potential of water penetration into the binder-aggregate interface, cause the observed decrease in FT at temperatures below − 15°C.Fig. 6Fracture toughness values of samples at different temperatures and cooling rates, (a) Saturated, (b) Dry.

It is important to note the amount of decrease in Keff at different temperatures under the isolated effect of sample saturation. At − 5°C, the average decrease in Keff across different cooling rates is 0.062 MPa.m0.5, and at − 15°C remains almost unchanged at 0.06. As the temperature decreases, ice crystals tend to increase in volume. At the same time, the stiffness of the asphalt mixture increases due to the tendency of bitumen toward elastic behavior. Therefore, increasing the stiffness of the mixture resists the volume expansion of ice crystals and prevents the further formation of microcracks in the sample and a further decrease in Keff. The validity of this claim is confirmed when, with a further decrease in temperature to − 20°C, the average decrease in Keff decrease across different cooling rates is 0.072 MPa.m0.5. In other words, the greater contraction of bitumen compared to aggregates causes microcracks to form at the binder-aggregate interface, and the movement of water toward the interface leads to a greater reduction in Keff.

At slow cooling rates, the fracture path follows a different mechanism. In regions of high stress concentration, such as zone II (near the initial crack tip) and zone IV (adjacent to the loading point), if an aggregate is present, the crack tends to propagate directly through it. In contrast, the behavior in the central region (zone III) is distinct, and existing aggregates in the direct path lead to a tortuous path. The viscoelastic behavior of the binder results in lower stiffness of the binder and mastic in contrast to the aggregate. Therefore, if there are aggregates on the straight path, the crack is diverted to follow the path with less stiffness. Additionally, the viscoelastic behavior of the binder leads to stress relaxation. Therefore, fewer microcracks are created in the sample because of lower stresses and less damage in the asphalt samples, making it possible for the crack to propagate in tortuous path rather than the straight path. In this zone, dark parts are dominant and trans-aggregate fracture is minimal. This tortuous path requires greater energy for propagation, resulting in higher observed fracture energy.

In some cases, even at slow cooling rates, the geometry (i.e., position and size) of an aggregate can compel the failure path to pass through the aggregate. This phenomenon was observed in sample 3. This occurs when bypassing the aggregate requires a failure path so tortuous that it requires more energy overall compared to fracturing the aggregate itself. As a result, the failure path in this sample was asymmetrical across its thickness. In part of the sample's thickness, the crack propagated through the aggregate, creating a path of low tortuosity. Conversely, in the remaining thickness of the sample, the crack passed through the mastic, resulting in a highly tortuous path, as indicated by the arrow in the figure.

As previously explained, a slow cooling rate allows for more marked viscoelastic behavior in the asphalt mixture. Given sufficient time, it leads to more uniform contraction of both the surface and core of samples and therefore generates less thermal stress in the sample. This causes the sample to stiffen over a longer time scale, resulting in a slower increase in the initial slope of the curve. As a result, the asphaltic sample exhibits greater displacement within the initial elastic region. This region is marked in red in the figure.

Although the TSRST method is mechanically different, the lower initial stiffness at slow cooling rates happens for the same viscoelastic reasons that cause stress relaxation46,70,74. As the cooling rate increases, the slope of the stress-temperature curve approaches a constant value more quickly and the behavior of the sample becomes predominantly elastic.

Furthermore, the damage induced by higher thermal stresses in samples subjected to rapid cooling rates leads to a lower peak load compared to that at slow rates. Consequently, the FE, which is represented by the area under the force–displacement curve, decreases as the cooling rate increases.

Finally, it is important to note the limitations of this study, as laboratory results may not fully capture real-world pavement performance. Factors such as traffic loading, asphalt mixture aging, and the presence of de-icing salts were not considered. However, the findings highlight a critical mechanism: the rate of cooling is a crucial damage factor, and rapid cooling rates (characteristic of sudden thermal shocks) were found to be significantly more damaging than gradual freezing. This suggests that pavements in regions prone to abrupt temperature drops are at a higher risk of accelerated damage. Future research should aim to integrate these variables to develop more comprehensive models for pavement durability in cold climates.

Conclusion

Low‑temperature cracking in cold regions is one of the main causes of failure in asphalt pavements. In this study, the effect of the cooling rate on the fracture toughness, fracture energy, and cracking‑resistance index of asphalt mixtures is investigated. For this purpose, saturated and dry asphalt samples were subjected to seven FTCs at different temperatures (− 5 °C, − 15 °C, and − 20 °C) with different cooling rates (0.1 °C/min, 0.2 °C/min, 0.5 °C/min, and shock‑freeze state) and fractured under mixed mode I/II with higher fraction of mode I (Me = 0.8). These findings have direct practical implications for pavement design and material selection in cold regions. Specifically, in areas prone to rapid thermal shocks, asphalt mixtures should be designed to maximize fracture toughness to prevent brittle failure, whereas in regions with slower freezing cycles, improving the binder’s ductility and adhesion is more critical.
