Large language models in oncology: promise, pitfalls, and the path to real-world adoption
Authors: S. McInerney, P.S. Hall, K. Cresswell
Published: 2026

Background

Oncology services across the UK and Europe are under significant pressure. Cancer diagnoses are projected to increase by 21% by 2040.1 With a system already at breaking point,2 the need for tools to support safe, evidence-based decision making has never been greater.

Large language models (LLMs), artificial intelligence (AI) systems trained on vast quantities of text and encodable with clinical knowledge,3 may help address this by automating time-consuming tasks such as clinical information retrieval, supporting clinical decision making, and generating clinical summaries.

LLMs are becoming popular among patients and clinicians very quickly. Where our patients would have once said “I turned to Dr Google”, they now say “I asked ChatGPT.” The interest and excitement in this technology have been palpable, reflected in both the commercial funding LLMs are attracting and the volume of academic publications, with a recent systematic review identifying 4609 peer-reviewed studies on LLMs in clinical medicine published in under 4 years.4

LLMs undoubtedly hold promise for health care, but we are yet to see the impact on productivity that technology enthusiasts predicted.5 The challenge is not one of technological performance alone, but whether health care systems already under strain can assess, validate, implement, and sustain this technology. There is a pressing need to narrow the gap between technological promise and real-world adoption that has compromised past digital health investments.

Promise of LLMS in oncology settings

Across medicine, LLMs have been proposed to advance the quintuple aim of health care improvement.6 Oncology provides a particularly relevant use case for this technology, given the volume of unstructured clinical data, the complexity of treatment decision making, and the multispecialist nature of care pathways.7,8 In this editorial, we focus on two use cases that illustrate both the scale of the problem they could solve, and the challenges of real-world implementation and adoption: clinical trial matching and ambient scribes. Fewer than 5% of adult patients with cancer participate in clinical trials,9 not because eligible trials do not exist, but because cross-referencing a patient's clinical history against complex eligibility criteria is a task most teams cannot do at scale. Oncology pathways are complex, but formulaic. Clinicians must weigh genomic profiles, treatment history, comorbidities, and performance status before every consultation. Matching these against complex trial eligibility criteria is exactly the kind of multivariable reasoning across structured and unstructured data that LLMs are suited to. In controlled evaluations, TrialGPT, an LLM-powered framework for patient-trial matching, achieved 87.3% accuracy on criteria matching tasks, approaching expert-level performance.10

The administrative burden on oncology teams is equally pressing. Physicians spend an estimated 2 hours on documentation for every hour of direct patient care,11 with time taken directly from an already strained capacity. Ambient scribes promise to return that time to clinicians. Reducing documentation burden could mean more time with patients, lower burnout, and more efficient use of resources, directly aligning with the quintuple aim of improved patient experience, clinician well-being, and system efficiency.6 These are not marginal efficiency gains but represent a genuine opportunity to do more with a workforce already running beyond capacity. These examples illustrate the potential of LLMs to address both cognitive and administrative burdens in oncology, but translating this promise into real-world clinical practice remains a significant challenge.

What the evidence actually shows

A 2025 scoping review of LLM-based data extraction in oncology found a broadly positive clinical extraction performance across 24 studies, but concluded that further evaluation beyond training datasets was needed before real-world integration could be assessed.12 A critical distinction underlies much of this evidence: model-level accuracy (how well an LLM performs on a defined task) is not the same as system-level performance in practice. The latter encompasses downstream effects on workflow and organisational processes, clinician–patient interaction, and patient safety, that model benchmark evaluations cannot capture.

Ambient scribes are among the first LLM implementations to enter routine clinical practice, driven by the appeal of reducing documentation burden.

More rigorous evaluations, however have told a more complex story, with unintended consequences including increased reliance on AI-generated summaries, subtle errors or omissions that may go unnoticed and clinicians adapting how they speak during consultations to satisfy the AI rather than the patient in front of them.13, 14, 15 These findings carry implications not only for workflow efficiency but for the integrity of the long-term clinical record, the consequences of which remain poorly understood.

In trial matching, the promising results of TrialGPT, which demonstrated near expert-level matching of trial criteria and reduced screening time by over 40%, relied entirely on synthetic patient data.10 Prospective evaluation against real-world electronic health record data has revealed a complexity that synthetic datasets simply cannot anticipate. A prospective evaluation of four trial-matching tools in a real-world molecular tumour board found that even the best-performing system correctly identified appropriate clinical trials only around half of the time, highlighting the gap between synthetic performance and real-world data.16

Real-world evidence that exposes the limitations and opportunities of these tools is needed to facilitate the link between preclinical performance and the performance of the systems in real-world contexts.

From implementation gap to real-world deployment

Real-world oncology data is fragmented, inconsistent, and difficult to access at scale.

Effective LLM development requires genuine collaboration between clinicians, informaticians, data scientists, and governance specialists, a combination that remains rare in practice.

More commonly, technically capable groups work without sufficient clinical input, optimising for benchmark performance on tasks that do not reflect clinical need. The risk is not building the wrong model; it is building the right model for the wrong problem. The potential benefit that these tools may bring may come with unforeseen limitations and unintended consequences that cannot be anticipated in advance and only emerge in use (see Table 1).

Watson for Oncology is an important example: it was developed with significant resources but ultimately discontinued in clinical settings after struggling with complex unstructured clinical data and failing to integrate into real-world clinical workflows.17

These tensions are not new to digital health. Many technological innovations in health care fail to scale due to inflated expectations, unintended consequences, and difficulties embedding within socio-organisational environments of use.18 Despite this, LLMs are already entering clinical workflows without peer-reviewed evidence of efficacy. ESMO has responded directly to this governance gap, and ELCAP19 provides a framework for safe LLM use in oncology, emphasising human oversight, privacy protection, and explicit warnings against unsupervised or unvalidated deployment.

Yet governance frameworks alone20,21 cannot resolve the fundamental regulatory paradox that follows. Generating the evidence required for regulatory approval requires deployment at a clinical scale, which in turn requires regulatory approval. This regulatory tension is already visible in how LLMs are entering clinical practice—through organisations and through user-driven informal adoption. One option to manage the cost and confidentiality risks of commercial platforms is to host open-source models within hospital infrastructure. These can be fine-tuned to local data and, with sustained investment, build the institutional expertise needed to support them. But doing so still requires rigorous validation, audit trails, and ongoing evaluation and governance. At the other end of the spectrum, clinicians are already using general-purpose commercial LLMs informally and outside formal organisational governance requirements. Some uses may be limited to non-identifiable clinical queries, but others may involve drafting letters or summarising patient notes. This shadow use raises immediate concerns around patient confidentiality and accountability, and occurs entirely outside formal procurement, approval, or regulatory oversight. Even once that approval is achieved, the obligation does not end there. The most significant risks from these technologies are likely to emerge only once they are embedded in routine practice. Postmarket surveillance cannot be a bureaucratic afterthought; it needs to be in place to identify harms that predeployment evaluation could not have anticipated.22,23

Conclusion

There is clear potential for LLMs in oncology, and the evidence base is still at an early stage of development with limited data in relation to medium and long-term consequences of adoption. However, the gap between preclinical synthetic data and real-world deployment and adoption is increasing, with many systems being deployed without adequate evidence, often targeting clinicians and patients directly.

There is an urgent need to develop evaluation methods that go beyond task accuracy, and these must evolve alongside the tools themselves.

Safety-critical omissions (instances where clinically important information is missed or misrepresented) must be a central concern, given the direct patient harm that may follow, particularly where overreliance on AI-generated outputs means errors go undetected: as clinicians become reliant on these outputs, the capacity for independent verification may erode, making omissions harder to detect and correct.18 Equity of performance across patient subgroups is equally important; if tools perform less well for underrepresented populations, they risk compounding existing health inequalities rather than addressing them. Understanding how tools disrupt existing workflows and shape downstream clinical decisions (effects that may be invisible to immediate accuracy metrics but which only emerge, and matter most, in routine use over the short, medium, and long term).

The key question is not simply whether an LLM works, but whether it improves care safely, reliably, and equitably under real clinical conditions.

The challenge is a familiar one in health care innovation: how do you invest in a technology with so much promise when the real-world evidence to justify that investment does not yet exist?

It may be a gamble worth taking—if we build the foundations first, and resist the temptation to deploy the tools before those foundations are in place.
