Validity, reliability and clinical utility of the Glittre ADL test in patients with atrial fibrillation
Authors: Onurkan Alkan, Buse Ozcan Kahraman, Aylin Tanriverdi Eyolcu, Resit Yigit Yilancioglu, Oguzhan Ekrem Turan, Emin Evren Ozcan, Sema Savci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundActivities of daily living (ADL) are frequently impaired in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF). Although the 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT) is widely used for functional assessment, it primarily evaluates walking endurance and may not fully reflect complex ADL involving upper limb activities and postural changes. This study investigated the validity, reliability and clinical utility of the Glittre ADL test in AF.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 39 AF patients and 39 matched healthy controls. All participants completed the Glittre ADL test. Concurrent validity was assessed using the 6MWT. Construct validity was evaluated using peripheral muscle strength, the five-times sit-to-stand test (5STS), Short Form-36, Atrial Fibrillation Impact Questionnaire and Milliken ADL Scale. Test–retest reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) by repeating the test after 1 hour.Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted to assess the ability of the Glittre ADL test to discriminate individuals with increased symptom burden based on 6MWT performance; the optimal cut-off was determined using the Youden index.ResultsAF patients completed the Glittre ADL test significantly slower than healthy controls (198.98 s vs 180.09 s, p<0.05). Test–retest reliability was excellent (ICC=0.914; 95% CI 0.834 to 0.955). Test duration showed a strong negative correlation with 6MWT distance (r=−0.87, p<0.001) and significant correlations with 5STS performance, peripheral muscle strength and physical quality-of-life components (p<0.05). ROC analysis indicated high discriminative ability area under the curve=0.967; 95% CI 0.922 to 1.000), with an optimal cut-off of 196.52 s (sensitivity 100%, specificity 82.6%).ConclusionsThe Glittre ADL test is valid, reliable and clinically useful for assessing complex ADLs in AF patients. The defined cut-off helps clinicians identify functional impairment.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia in adults, and its prevalence increases significantly with age.1 Beyond its cardiac nature, AF is considered a systemic syndrome that affects the individual’s functional capacity, physical performance and activities of daily living (ADL).2 3 Patients with AF frequently suffer from exercise intolerance, fatigue and dyspnoea, which lead to a reduction in lower and upper limb muscle strength and overall physical performance.2–4 In particular, studies have reported decreased handgrip strength, impaired knee extensor performance and sarcopenia in AF patients, indicating that both lower and upper limb muscle groups are systematically affected.5–8 Furthermore, physiological factors such as reduced cardiac output during AF can further impair daily physical performance and the ability to perform complex tasks.9

The 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT) is the most widely used tool and correlates with symptom severity.10–12 However, it predominantly measures walking endurance and remains limited in reflecting multidimensional ADLs involving upper limb tasks and postural changes.10 11 Similarly, Sit-to-Stand (STS) tests are valid for assessing lower limb strength but are static assessments that do not capture the dynamic and multicomponent nature of real-life activities.13

Since AF leads to systemic muscle weakness affecting both upper and lower limbs, and daily life requires complex movements, a more comprehensive assessment tool is needed. The Glittre ADL test is defined as a versatile physical performance assessment tool that mimics real-life situations. Unlike isolated walking or strength tests, it includes activities requiring both upper and lower extremity use, such as sitting-standing, walking, stair climbing and carrying objects.14 15 While its validity and reliability have been established in chronic cardiopulmonary conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD),16 idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis,17 interstitial lung disease,18 asthma19 and heart failure,20 its psychometric properties in patients with AF have not yet been investigated.

To the best of our knowledge, no study has yet investigated the validity and reliability of the Glittre ADL test in patients with AF. Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the validity and reliability of the Glittre ADL test in patients with AF. The secondary aim was to determine its clinical utility by calculating a discriminative cut-off value to identify functional impairment compared with healthy controls.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was designed as a cross-sectional study. The study was conducted between June 2023 and July 2025. Clinical evaluations and patient recruitment were carried out at the Dokuz Eylul University Faculty of Medicine, Department of Cardiology, Arrhythmia Outpatient Clinic, while physical performance tests and data collection were performed at the Faculty of Physical Therapy and Rehabilitation.

Participants

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study population consisted of patients admitted to the arrhythmia outpatient clinic. The AF group included patients with a diagnosis of persistent or permanent AF, a controlled resting heart rate of 110 bpm or less, aged 40 years or older and the ability to walk independently. Patients were excluded from the AF group if they had heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, chronic respiratory disease, a history of coronary artery bypass grafting or valve surgery within the previous 6 months, a recent acute myocardial infarction, a pacemaker, severe symptoms classified as European Heart Rhythm Association (EHRA) Score IV or were unable to provide written informed consent. The control group consisted of age-matched and sex-matched healthy volunteers, including hospital staff and patient relatives, with no known cardiac, respiratory, neurological or orthopaedic disorders.

Sample size estimation

Power analysis was conducted using G*Power software (V.3.1.9.2) based on both the primary and secondary aims of the study. For the primary aim of assessing test–retest reliability, sample size was calculated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC)-specific formula proposed by Walter et al.21 Assuming a null ICC (ρ₀=0.75) and an expected ICC (ρ₁=0.90) based on reliability benchmarks in comparable functional tests, with two repeated measurements (test–retest design), 80% power and a two-sided alpha level of 0.05, a minimum of 29 participants was required. For the secondary aim of between-group comparison, sample size estimation was informed by the study of Hena et al22 using the reported time duration difference in healthy individuals (effect size=0.760) with a power of 95% and an alpha level of 0.05, resulting in a requirement of 39 participants per group. To meet the higher sample size requirement of the secondary aim, a total of 78 participants, comprising 39 patients with AF and 39 healthy controls, were included in the study.

Procedures

Demographic and clinical data were obtained from patient files and interviews. The AF group underwent a test–retest protocol in which the Glittre ADL test was administered twice on the same day with a 1-hour rest interval to assess reliability, while muscle strength assessments, questionnaires, and the 6MWT were performed once. The control group consisted of healthy individuals who completed a single-session assessment protocol.

The Glittre ADL test was administered first in all participants as the primary outcome measure, ensuring consistency across both groups. The remaining assessments (6MWT, muscle strength, five-times STS (5STS) and questionnaires) were administered subsequently. Although a formal randomisation protocol for test order was not implemented, the order of secondary assessments varied across participants based on practical considerations at the time of assessment, which inherently introduces a degree of natural counterbalancing. Additionally, to minimise cumulative fatigue, sufficient rest intervals were provided between tests, and non-exertional assessments were interspersed with physical performance tests to allow partial recovery.

Outcome measures

Glittre ADL test

The Glittre ADL test was conducted using a standardised circuit involving a chair, a backpack and shelving tasks.14 15 Participants carried a weighted backpack (2.5 kg for women, 5 kg for men). The circuit required participants to: (1) stand up from a chair, (2) walk along a flat course, (3) climb up and down a staircase (17 cm height×27 cm width steps) and (4) move three 1 kg bottles between shelves adjusted to shoulder and waist height.

This circuit was repeated for five laps as quickly as possible. The total time taken to complete the test was recorded in seconds. Perceived dyspnoea and fatigue were evaluated using the Modified Borg Scale (0–10), and vital signs (heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation (SpO2)) were monitored before and after the test. The predicted values for healthy individuals were calculated using the reference equations (equation 1 was used as it covers body mass index (BMI) >35 kg/m²).23 24

Functional capacity (6MWT)

Functional capacity was assessed using the 6MWT in a 30 m corridor. Participants were instructed to walk as fast as possible. Perceived dyspnoea and fatigue were evaluated using the Modified Borg Scale (0–10), and vital signs (heart rate, blood pressure, SpO2) were monitored before and after the tests. The total distance walked was recorded. Predicted values were calculated using the Enright and Sherrill equations.10 11

Muscle strength assessments

Peripheral muscle strength was evaluated using a digital handheld dynamometer (Lafayette Instrument Company, USA) to measure the isometric strength of the quadriceps femoris, biceps brachii, shoulder abductors and shoulder flexors.25 Three trials were performed for each muscle group with a 30 s rest, and the highest value (peak force) was recorded. Handgrip strength was measured using a Jamar hydraulic dynamometer in the standard seated position. Three measurements were taken with a 1-minute rest interval, and the average value was recorded.4 Functional lower limb strength was evaluated using the 5STS test. Participants stood up and sat down five times as fast as possible with arms crossed. The total time was recorded.13

Questionnaires

Quality of life and functional status were assessed using validated questionnaires. Disease-specific quality of life was evaluated with the Atrial Fibrillation Impact Questionnaire (AFImpact), an 18-item disease-specific scale evaluating vitality, emotional distress and sleep. Higher scores indicate worse quality of life.26 27 General quality of life was measured using the RAND-36 (Short Form (SF)-36), a generic scale assessing 8 domains of health. Higher scores indicate better quality of life.28–30 Upper extremity function in daily activities was assessed using the Milliken ADL Scale (MAS), which consists of 47 items evaluating both ‘ability’ and ‘necessity’.31 32

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS V.27.0. The normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and histograms. Continuous variables were expressed as mean±SD or median (IQR). Differences between AF and healthy groups were analysed using the Independent Samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Test–retest reliability was assessed using the ICC. ICC values were interpreted as: 0.00–0.25 (none), 0.26–0.49 (low), 0.50–0.69 (moderate), 0.70–0.89 (high) and 0.90–1.00 (excellent).33 Concurrent validity was evaluated using Spearman’s correlation analysis between Glittre ADL test time and 6MWT distance. Construct validity was tested by correlating Glittre time with muscle strength, 5STS and questionnaire scores. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the discriminative ability of the Glittre ADL test to identify functional impairment, defined as a 6MWT distance of <450 m,12 and sensitivity, specificity and area under the curve (AUC) were calculated. AUC values were interpreted as: 0.5 (random), 0.6–0.7 (poor), 0.7–0.8 (fair), 0.8–0.9 (good) and >0.9 (excellent).33 A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in this study.

ADL, activities of daily living.

The primary outcome, the Glittre ADL test time, was significantly prolonged in the AF group (median 198.98 s, IQR: 178.0–235.7) compared with the control group (median 180.09 s, IQR: 160.7–185.7) (p<0.001). AF patients required 113.2% of the predicted time to complete the test, whereas controls required only 99.3% (p<0.001).

Consistent with this impairment, the AF group walked a significantly shorter distance in the 6MWT (459.93±110.48 m vs 520.15±70.43 m, p=0.006) and demonstrated slower performance in the 5STS test (median 13.01 s vs 10.36 s, p<0.001).

Regarding peripheral muscle strength, patients with AF showed significantly lower isometric strength values for both dominant and non-dominant knee extensors (p<0.001) and elbow flexors (p=0.005). Although shoulder flexor and abductor strength values were lower in the AF group, the difference reached statistical significance only on the non-dominant side (p<0.05). No significant difference was observed in handgrip strength between the groups (p=0.730).

Metabolic cost and physiological responses

Regarding metabolic cost, there were no significant differences in total energy expenditure or average energy expenditure during the Glittre ADL test between the AF and control groups (p>0.05), despite the longer duration of the test in the AF group (table 2). Detailed comparisons of physiological responses between the AF and control groups are presented in online supplemental tables S1 and S2. While both groups demonstrated significant intragroup increases in cardiovascular and symptomatic parameters following the functional tests, the physiological burden was more pronounced in the AF cohort. Specifically, following the Glittre ADL test, patients with AF exhibited significantly higher post-test dyspnoea and fatigue scores compared with healthy controls, with a significantly greater magnitude of change (Δ post–pre) in fatigue. Similarly, during the 6MWT, the AF group experienced significantly greater increases in perceived dyspnoea and fatigue, whereas the control group exhibited a significantly greater increase in heart rate.

When examining changes in physiological parameters before and after both the Glittre ADL test and the 6MWT, post-test systolic blood pressure was significantly higher following the Glittre ADL test compared with the 6MWT (p=0.015), whereas pretest diastolic blood pressure differed significantly between the two tests (p=0.095). Reliability of the Glittre ADL test

The test–retest reliability of the Glittre ADL test was evaluated in the AF group. The results demonstrated excellent reliability, with an ICC of 0.914 (95% CI 0.834 to 0.955, p<0.001). A strong positive correlation was found between the first and second test trials (r=0.894, p<0.001).

Although reliability was assessed using ICC based on continuous measurements, non-parametric statistics were used to compare test trials due to the non-normal distribution of test times. A systematic bias (learning effect) was observed, as the median time for the second test (189.76 s (173.26–214.62)) was significantly shorter than the first test (198.98 s (178.00–235.71)) (p<0.001). This reduction indicates increased familiarity with the test circuit and improved task efficiency on repeated administration.

Concurrent validity

Glittre ADL test time showed a strong negative correlation with 6MWT distance (r=−0.865, p<0.001) and a strong positive correlation with 5STS duration (r=0.706, p<0.001), indicating that longer Glittre times reflect lower functional capacity.

Construct validity–muscle strength

Moderate-to-strong negative correlations were observed between Glittre time and upper limb muscle strength, including elbow flexors (r=−0.670, p<0.001) and handgrip strength (r=−0.584, p<0.001). Knee extensor strength showed a moderate correlation (r=−0.380, p=0.016).

Construct validity–questionnaires

Glittre ADL test time was significantly correlated with the Physical Functioning (r=−0.601, p<0.001) and Physical Health Summary (r=−0.509, p<0.001) domains of the SF-36. Regarding disease-specific quality of life, longer Glittre times were associated with worse Sleep (r=0.829, p=0.036) and Vitality (r=0.361, p=0.024) scores on the AFImpact questionnaire. Additionally, a significant correlation was observed with the total score of the MAS (r=−0.325, p=0.044).

Collectively, these findings support the multidimensional construct validity of the Glittre ADL test, reflecting functional exercise capacity, muscle strength and patient-perceived health status.

Discriminative ability (ROC analysis)

The Glittre ADL test demonstrated excellent discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.967 (95% CI 0.922 to 1.000, p<0.001). The Youden index identified an optimal cut-off value of 196.52 s. At this threshold, the test showed 100% sensitivity and 82.6% specificity for detecting functional impairment in patients with AF.

Discussion

The present study aimed to evaluate the validity and reliability of the Glittre ADL test in individuals with AF and to examine its associations with functional capacity, muscle strength, daily living activities and quality of life. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the applicability of the Glittre ADL test specifically in an AF population. The main findings demonstrate that the Glittre ADL test is a highly reliable and valid tool in patients with AF and may reflect multidimensional functional limitations relevant to daily life performance.

The primary finding of this study is that patients with AF exhibited significantly reduced functional capacity compared with age-matched and sex-matched healthy controls, as evidenced by prolonged Glittre ADL test durations. This suggests a possible association between the physiological burden of AF and tangible limitations in the performance of complex ADL.2 3 Our findings are consistent with previous studies reporting compromised physical performance and exercise intolerance in the AF population using traditional measures such as the 6MWT.12 Moreover, the functional impairment observed in our cohort mirrors patterns reported in other chronic cardiopulmonary conditions, including heart failure and COPD, where the Glittre ADL test has demonstrated that disease-related physiological limitations are associated with impaired daily tasks.16 20

In the current cohort, the Glittre ADL test time showed a strong negative correlation with the 6MWT distance and a significant positive correlation with the 5STS duration. These findings indicate that prolonged Glittre ADL test performance is associated with reduced functional capacity and impaired lower-extremity function. Similar associations between Glittre performance and 6MWT have been reported in COPD, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, interstitial lung disease, asthma and heart failure populations.16–20 24 However, the magnitude of correlation observed in AF patients (r=−0.865) was numerically higher than those reported in asthma (r=−0.61)18 and interstitial lung disease (r=−0.70)19 populations. While this observation may suggest greater responsiveness of the Glittre ADL test in capturing functional limitations in AF, direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously given methodological differences across studies and the absence of head-to-head comparative evidence.

The strong associations between Glittre ADL test time and upper-extremity muscle strength, particularly elbow flexors and handgrip strength, highlight the importance of upper-limb function in daily activities of patients with AF. Unlike traditional walking-based tests, the Glittre protocol incorporates tasks requiring arm elevation, carrying loads and coordinated upper-extremity movements. Previous studies in heart failure and post-COVID populations have similarly emphasised the contribution of upper-extremity strength to Glittre performance.34 35 Our findings extend this evidence to AF, underscoring that functional limitation in this population may not be restricted to aerobic capacity but also involves peripheral muscle dysfunction.

Lower-extremity muscle strength, particularly knee extensor strength, also demonstrated a significant association with Glittre ADL performance. Prolonged STS duration and reduced quadriceps strength observed in AF patients are consistent with prior studies linking AF to sarcopenia, reduced mobility and impaired physical performance.5 8 36 These findings suggest that haemodynamic alterations, chronic inflammation and physical inactivity commonly observed in AF may contribute to generalised muscle weakness, thereby affecting daily functional tasks.

Quality of life measures further supported the clinical relevance of the Glittre ADL test. Significant negative correlations were observed between Glittre ADL test time and SF-36 physical functioning and physical health summary scores, indicating that poorer objective performance is associated with lower perceived physical health. Similar relationships between Glittre performance and SF-36 physical domains have been reported across various chronic disease populations.3738 In contrast, correlations with mental and social domains were weaker, suggesting that the Glittre ADL test may more strongly capture physical rather than psychosocial aspects of health status.

Disease-specific quality of life assessment using the AFImpact questionnaire revealed significant positive correlations between the Glittre ADL test time and vitality and sleep domains. These findings indicate that patients who require a longer time to complete daily activities may also report greater fatigue and sleep disturbances. Previous studies have shown that poor sleep quality and reduced vitality are common in AF and are associated with increased symptom burden and arrhythmia recurrence.39 40 The lack of association with emotional distress may reflect the relatively low symptom severity in our cohort, as most participants were classified as EHRA I–II.2

Regarding the physiological impact of the assessments, our findings suggest that the Glittre ADL test imposes at least comparable, and in some parameters greater, physiological demands than the 6MWT in patients with AF. Specifically, the higher post-test systolic blood pressure observed following the Glittre ADL test likely reflects the greater haemodynamic challenge of performing complex, multilimb tasks compared with steady-state walking. This highlights that daily living activities may evoke more pronounced physiological stress in AF patients than previously captured by traditional walking tests.

Daily living activity limitations assessed by the MAS were moderately correlated with the Glittre ADL test time, particularly in personal hygiene, other activities and total score domains. This suggests that objective performance-based limitations align with patient-reported difficulties in complex daily tasks. While the MAS has not previously been applied in AF populations, similar associations between Glittre performance and daily activity questionnaires have been reported in pulmonary disease cohorts.16 41 These findings support the complementary use of performance-based and self-reported measures in comprehensive functional assessment.

The Glittre ADL test demonstrated excellent test–retest reliability in AF patients, with ICC and Cronbach’s alpha values exceeding 0.90. These reliability indices are comparable to those reported in COPD, asthma, interstitial lung disease and heart failure populations, confirming the consistency and reproducibility of the test in a cardiac rhythm disorder population. Additionally, ROC analysis identified a cut-off value of 196.52 s with high sensitivity and specificity for distinguishing AF patients from healthy controls, further supporting its discriminative validity.

However, despite the excellent ICC, a statistically significant reduction in completion time was observed between the first (212.64±47.02 s) and second (204.47±49.83 s) administrations (p<0.001), indicating the presence of a learning effect. This systematic improvement likely reflects familiarisation with the test circuit and task sequence rather than a true change in functional capacity. A similar learning effect has been previously reported in COPD patients, where the second Glittre ADL test administration was consistently faster, and a familiarisation trial was recommended prior to formal test measurement.16 Based on these findings, incorporating a practice trial before formal test administration may be advisable in AF patients, particularly when the test is used for longitudinal monitoring or treatment response evaluation. When only a single administration is feasible in clinical settings, clinicians should be aware that completion time may be slightly underestimated relative to a familiarised performance, and the second trial may more accurately reflect true functional capacity.

From a clinical perspective, the Glittre ADL test offers several advantages in the assessment of patients with AF. Unlike single-task tests such as the 6MWT, it integrates multiple daily activities and challenges both upper and lower extremities, balance and coordination. This multidimensional structure may provide a more realistic evaluation of functional limitations encountered in everyday life. Given the heterogeneous symptom profile of AF and its impact on physical performance, the Glittre ADL test may serve as a valuable tool for baseline assessment, rehabilitation planning and monitoring of functional outcomes. In real-world clinical settings, the Glittre ADL test can be administered by a trained physiotherapist within approximately 10–15 min using minimal, low-cost equipment without the need for a dedicated laboratory setting. Incorporation of this test into cardiology follow-up visits may facilitate early identification of functional decline and timely referral to cardiac rehabilitation, ultimately supporting more proactive patient management in clinical practice.

This study has several limitations. The relatively small sample size limits the generalisability of the findings. The younger age of the participants preserved physical reserves, and the homogeneous distribution of AF subtypes may have reduced the ability to detect differences, particularly in grip strength and functional capacity measures. The single-centre, cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences and limits applicability to broader clinical populations. The order of test administration was not formally randomised; although the Glittre ADL test was consistently performed first and the sequence of remaining assessments varied across participants due to practical considerations, this variability was not systematically controlled, which may have introduced order effects on secondary outcomes. In addition, the fluctuating nature of AF symptoms may not be fully captured by single-time assessments. The lack of normalisation of energy expenditure to test duration during the Glittre ADL test constrains data interpretation, and the use of a fixed backpack load may not adequately reflect interindividual differences in physical capacity. The proposed cut-off value of 196.52 s should be considered a preliminary result, as it was derived from a small, single-centre sample; external validation in larger, independent and more heterogeneous cohorts is warranted before this threshold can be recommended for routine clinical use. Therefore, future studies should employ individualised loading strategies, incorporate multiday test–retest protocols and adopt larger, multicentre designs to confirm and extend these findings.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrates that the Glittre ADL test is a valid, reliable and clinically meaningful tool for assessing complex ADL in individuals with AF. Its strong associations with functional capacity, muscle strength and patient-reported outcomes highlight the multidimensional nature of functional limitation in this population. Overall, the Glittre ADL test may serve as a practical and comprehensive assessment tool to guide individualised rehabilitation strategies and inform clinical decision-making in patients with AF.
