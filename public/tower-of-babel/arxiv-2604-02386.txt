Abstract

Introduction

The enumeration of representations of an even integer as a sum of
two positive integers subject to coprimality conditions is a
classical problem in additive number theory

[
3
,
5
]
.
While asymptotic estimates for related counting functions follow
from sieve methods and Möbius inversion

[
2
,
4
]
,
obtaining fully explicit exact formulas is a more delicate task,
as it requires precise control of residue constraints and ordering
conditions.

In this paper we study the counting function

g(2n,p)=\#\bigl\{(h,k)\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}:h+k=2n,\;h\leq k,\;\gcd(h,6p)=\gcd(k,6p)=1\bigr\},

(1)

where
p\geq 5
is a fixed prime. This function counts representations
of
2n
as a sum
h+k
of two positive integers with
h\leq k
,
both avoiding the primes
2
,
3
, and
p
.

The function
g(2n,p)
can be evaluated via inclusion–exclusion
over the prime divisors of
6p
, yielding a piecewise expression
periodic modulo
6p
, as follows from standard divisor-sum arguments.
However, such formulas typically involve alternating sums over divisor
sets and do not directly reveal the affine structure of the counting
function. Our main result, Theorem
4.13
, provides an
alternative approach: explicit closed formulas expressed in terms of
the canonical remainder operator

\delta_{k}(x):=x-k\left\lfloor\frac{x}{k}\right\rfloor,

elementary step functions, and the minimal solutions
a(p)
and
b(p)

of the congruences

6x\equiv-1\pmod{p},\qquad 6x\equiv-5\pmod{p}.

These residue parameters determine the excluded congruence classes
modulo
p
in a direct and explicit manner.

A key technical component is the explicit resolution of affine residue
equations of the form
\delta_{k}(a_{0}x+b)=c
, which reduce to
equations of the form
\delta_{k}(a_{0}x)=b_{0}
via additive compatibility.
This reduction is applied in Section
4
to determine the
precise residue exclusions governing admissible coprime decompositions.

For fixed
p
, after precomputing
a(p)
and
b(p)
in
O(\log p)

operations using the Euclidean algorithm, each evaluation of
g(2n,p)

requires only
O(1)
arithmetic operations. This represents a substantial
improvement over direct enumeration, which requires
O(n)
operations.

The resulting formulas reveal that
g(2n,p)
is piecewise affine along
arithmetic progressions of
n
, with structure determined by residue
classes modulo
3
and
p
. This affine structure is not transparent
from inclusion–exclusion formulas or asymptotic estimates, and emerges
naturally from the residue-based formulation developed here.

Theorem
4.13
is validated computationally against a brute-force
oracle for all even integers
2n\leq 10^{5}
and primes

p\in\{5,7,11,13,17,19,23\}
, with perfect agreement observed in
all cases.

The paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
fixes notation and collects standard
properties of the remainder operator
\delta_{k}
.
Section
3
derives an explicit formula for the minimal
solution of
\delta_{k}(a_{0}x)=b_{0}
via the Euclidean algorithm.
Section
4
applies these tools to obtain
Theorem
4.13
.
Section
5
presents the computational validation.

2
Preliminaries

For integers
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
, we denote by
\gcd(a,b)
their greatest
common divisor, and say that
a
and
b
are
coprime
if
\gcd(a,b)=1
.
We write
a\mid b
when
a
divides
b
, and
a\nmid b
otherwise. For
integer intervals, we use the notation

[a,b]_{\mathbb{Z}}:=\{\,n\in\mathbb{Z}:a\leq n\leq b\,\}.

2.1
Remainder operator

For
k\geq 2
, define the map

\delta_{k}:\mathbb{Z}\to[0,k-1]_{\mathbb{Z}},\qquad\delta_{k}(a):=a-k\left\lfloor\frac{a}{k}\right\rfloor.

Thus
\delta_{k}(a)
is the canonical representative of the residue class of
a

modulo
k
, and satisfies

\delta_{k}(a)\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\},\qquad\delta_{k}(a)=0\iff k\mid a.

The operator
\delta_{k}
coincides with the canonical projection

\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
followed by the choice of the standard
representative. In particular, for all
a,b,r,n\in\mathbb{Z}
, the following
identities hold:

\displaystyle\delta_{k}(a+kn)

\displaystyle=\delta_{k}(a),

(2)

\displaystyle\delta_{k}(a+b)

\displaystyle=\delta_{k}\!\left(\delta_{k}(a)+\delta_{k}(b)\right),

(3)

\displaystyle\delta_{k}(ra)

\displaystyle=\delta_{k}\!\left(r\,\delta_{k}(a)\right).

(4)

These identities express compatibility with addition and multiplication
after projection to canonical representatives; they follow directly from
the definition of
\delta_{k}
and standard properties of the floor function
(see, e.g.,
[
3
]
). For example,

\delta_{k}(ab+c)=\delta_{k}\!\left(a\,\delta_{k}(b)+\delta_{k}(c)\right).

We also record the idempotence property.

Lemma 2.1
.

For all
a\in\mathbb{Z}
and
k\geq 2
,

\delta_{k}(\delta_{k}(a))=\delta_{k}(a).

Proof.

Since
\delta_{k}(a)\in[0,k-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
, we have

\left\lfloor\frac{\delta_{k}(a)}{k}\right\rfloor=0,

and the definition immediately gives the result.
∎

The following auxiliary lemmas will be used throughout the paper.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
k\geq 2
and
a\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\delta_{k}(a)\neq 0
. Then

\delta_{k}(-a)=k-\delta_{k}(a).

Proof.

By (
3
),

\delta_{k}\!\left(\delta_{k}(a)+\delta_{k}(-a)\right)=\delta_{k}(a+(-a))=\delta_{k}(0)=0,

so
k\mid\bigl(\delta_{k}(a)+\delta_{k}(-a)\bigr)
.
Since both
\delta_{k}(a)
and
\delta_{k}(-a)
lie in
\{1,\dots,k-1\}
,
their sum belongs to
\{2,\dots,2k-2\}
, and the only multiple of
k

in this interval is
k
itself. Therefore
\delta_{k}(-a)=k-\delta_{k}(a)
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
k\geq 2
and
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then

\delta_{k}(a)=\delta_{k}(b)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\delta_{k}(a-b)=0.

Proof.

If
\delta_{k}(a)=\delta_{k}(b)
, then by (
3
),

\delta_{k}(a-b)=\delta_{k}\!\left(\delta_{k}(a)-\delta_{k}(b)\right)=\delta_{k}(0)=0.

Conversely, if
\delta_{k}(a-b)=0
, then

\delta_{k}\!\left(\delta_{k}(a)-\delta_{k}(b)\right)=0.

Since

\delta_{k}(a)-\delta_{k}(b)\in[-(k-1),k-1]_{\mathbb{Z}},

and the only multiple of
k
in this interval is
0
, it follows that

\delta_{k}(a)=\delta_{k}(b).

∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
k\geq 2
and
r\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\gcd(r,k)=1
. Then for any

a\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\delta_{k}(ar)=\delta_{k}(r)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\delta_{k}(a)=1.

Proof.

If
\delta_{k}(a)=1
, then (
4
) gives

\delta_{k}(ar)=\delta_{k}(r).

Conversely, if
\delta_{k}(ar)=\delta_{k}(r)
, then by
Lemma
2.3
,

\delta_{k}(r(1-a))=0.

Since
\gcd(r,k)=1
, multiplication by
r
is invertible modulo
k
, and
therefore
\delta_{k}(1-a)=0
, which implies
\delta_{k}(a)=1
.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
k,r\geq 2
and
a\in\mathbb{Z}
. If
r\mid k
, then

\delta_{r}\bigl(\delta_{k}(a)\bigr)=\delta_{r}(a).

Proof.

From the definition,

\delta_{k}(a)=a-k\left\lfloor\frac{a}{k}\right\rfloor.

Since
r\mid k
, the second term is divisible by
r
, and applying

\delta_{r}
gives

\delta_{r}(\delta_{k}(a))=\delta_{r}(a).

∎

2.2
Minimal solutions of linear remainder equations

We study integer solutions of equations of the form

\delta_{k}(ax+b)=c,

(5)

where
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
,
c\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
, and
k\geq 2
.

By definition of
\delta_{k}
, equation (
5
) holds if and only if

k\mid(ax+b-c).

This formulation allows us to study remainder equations entirely within
\mathbb{Z}
,
without introducing residue classes as separate algebraic objects.

Lemma 2.6

(Existence and periodicity)
.

Let
k\geq 2
,
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
,
c\in\{0,\dots,k-1\}
, and let

r:=\gcd(a,k).

Then equation (
5
) admits an integer solution if and only if

r\mid(c-b).

In that case, the set of all solutions is an arithmetic progression

x=x_{0}+t\,\frac{k}{r},\qquad t\in\mathbb{Z},

for some particular solution
x_{0}
.

Proof.

Equation (
5
) is equivalent to

ax\equiv c-b\pmod{k},

which in turn is equivalent to the linear Diophantine equation

ax-kt=c-b
for some integer
t
.
It is a standard result that this equation has an integer solution if and only
if
\gcd(a,k)\mid(c-b)
(see, for example,
[
3
]
).
When solutions exist, if
x_{0}
is one solution, then

a\!\left(x_{0}+\frac{k}{r}\right)=ax_{0}+\frac{ak}{r}\equiv ax_{0}\pmod{k},

since
ak/r
is divisible by
k
. Thus all solutions form an arithmetic
progression with common difference
k/r
.
∎

Definition 2.7

(Minimal solution)
.

Assume that equation (
5
) admits at least one integer solution.
The
minimal solution
is defined as

x_{0}:=\min\bigl\{x\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}:\delta_{k}(ax+b)=c\bigr\}.

Lemma 2.8

(Solutions in
\{0,\dots,k-1\}
)
.

Let
r=\gcd(a,k)
. If equation (
5
) admits an integer
solution, then it admits exactly
r
solutions in
\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
,
forming an arithmetic progression with common difference
k/r
.
In particular, the minimal solution exists and is unique.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.6
, all integer solutions have the form

x=x_{0}+t\,\frac{k}{r},\qquad t\in\mathbb{Z}.

The representatives in
\{0,\dots,k-1\}
correspond to those values of
t

for which
0\leq x_{0}+t\,k/r\leq k-1
.
Since consecutive representatives differ by
k/r
, and

r\cdot(k/r)=k
, exactly
r
distinct values lie in
\{0,\dots,k-1\}
.
These are

\delta_{k}(x_{0}),\;\delta_{k}\!\left(x_{0}+\tfrac{k}{r}\right),\;\dots,\;\delta_{k}\!\left(x_{0}+(r-1)\tfrac{k}{r}\right),

and they are distinct because any two differ by a nonzero multiple of
k/r

less than
k
.
Hence the minimal solution, being the smallest among these
r
values,
exists and is unique.
∎

Lemma 2.9

(Uniqueness under coprimality)
.

Let
k\geq 2
,
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
,
c\in\{0,\dots,k-1\}
, and suppose

\gcd(a,k)=1
. Then equation (
5
) has exactly one solution
in
\{0,\dots,k-1\}
, which coincides with the minimal solution.

Proof.

Since
\gcd(a,k)=1
, Lemma
2.8
gives exactly one
solution in
\{0,\dots,k-1\}
. Alternatively, since multiplication by
a

is invertible modulo
k
, the congruence
ax\equiv c-b\pmod{k}
has a
unique solution modulo
k
, whose canonical representative is the minimal
solution.
∎

3
The Euclidean Algorithm and Minimal Solutions

Lemma
2.9
guarantees that, under the coprimality
condition
\gcd(a_{0},k)=1
, the equation
\delta_{k}(a_{0}x)=b_{0}
has
a unique solution in
\{0,\dots,k-1\}
. In this section we derive
an explicit formula for that solution via the Euclidean algorithm,
expressed using the remainder operator
\delta_{k}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
k\geq 2
and
a_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
with
2\leq a_{0}<k
. Define

a^{(0)}=k,\quad a^{(1)}=a_{0},\quad a^{(n)}=\delta_{a^{(n-1)}}\!\bigl(a^{(n-2)}\bigr),\qquad n\geq 2.

Then:

1.

The sequence satisfies

0\leq a^{(n)}<a^{(n-1)}\quad\text{whenever }a^{(n-1)}\neq 0.

2.

There exists a unique index
r\geq 1
such that

a^{(r)}=\gcd(a_{0},k),\quad a^{(r+1)}=0.

3.

The algorithm terminates in
O(\log\min\{k,a_{0}\})
steps.

Proof.

Item 1.
By definition of
\delta
, for all
n\geq 2
,

0\leq\delta_{a^{(n-1)}}(a^{(n-2)})<a^{(n-1)}.

Thus the sequence is strictly decreasing until reaching
0
, and must
terminate. Uniqueness of
r
follows from this strict decrease.

Item 2.

Let
a^{(r)}
be the last nonzero term, so
a^{(r+1)}=\delta_{a^{(r)}}(a^{(r-1)})=0
, which gives
a^{(r)}\mid a^{(r-1)}
.

We claim
a^{(r)}
divides all terms
a^{(0)},\dots,a^{(r-1)}
.
We proceed by downward induction. The base cases are

a^{(r)}\mid a^{(r)}
and
a^{(r)}\mid a^{(r-1)}
.
Suppose
a^{(r)}\mid a^{(n)}
and
a^{(r)}\mid a^{(n-1)}
for some

r\geq n\geq 2
. Since
a^{(n)}=\delta_{a^{(n-1)}}(a^{(n-2)})
and

a^{(r)}\mid a^{(n-1)}
, Lemma
2.5
gives

\delta_{a^{(r)}}\!\left(a^{(n-2)}\right)=\delta_{a^{(r)}}\!\left(\delta_{a^{(n-1)}}(a^{(n-2)})\right)=\delta_{a^{(r)}}\!\left(a^{(n)}\right)=0,

so
a^{(r)}\mid a^{(n-2)}
. By induction,
a^{(r)}
divides all terms,
hence
a^{(r)}\mid k
and
a^{(r)}\mid a_{0}
,
so
a^{(r)}\leq\gcd(a_{0},k)
.

Conversely, let
d=\gcd(a_{0},k)
. Then
d\mid a^{(0)}=k
and

d\mid a^{(1)}=a_{0}
. Suppose
d\mid a^{(n-2)}
and
d\mid a^{(n-1)}

for some
n\geq 2
. Since
d\mid a^{(n-1)}
, Lemma
2.5

gives

\delta_{d}\!\left(a^{(n)}\right)=\delta_{d}\!\left(\delta_{a^{(n-1)}}(a^{(n-2)})\right)=\delta_{d}\!\left(a^{(n-2)}\right)=0,

so
d\mid a^{(n)}
for all
n
by induction. In particular

d\mid a^{(r)}
, so
d\leq a^{(r)}
.

Combining both inequalities,
a^{(r)}=\gcd(a_{0},k)
.

Item 3.

The complexity bound follows from the standard analysis of the Euclidean
algorithm
[
1
]
.
∎

3.1
Explicit formula for the minimal solution

The following result provides an explicit closed formula for the minimal solution,
obtained by expressing the extended Euclidean algorithm entirely in terms of the
remainder operator
\delta_{k}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let

k\geq 2,\quad\gcd(a_{0},k)=1,\quad b_{0}\in\{0,\dots,k-1\}.

Define sequences

a^{(0)}=k,\quad a^{(1)}=a_{0},\quad a^{(n)}=\delta_{a^{(n-1)}}(a^{(n-2)}),

and

b^{(1)}=b_{0},\quad b^{(n)}=a^{(n-1)}-\delta_{a^{(n-1)}}(b^{(n-1)}).

Let
r
be the unique index with
a^{(r)}=1
(cf. Lemma
3.1
).
Then the unique solution in
\{0,\dots,k-1\}
of

\delta_{k}(a_{0}x)=b_{0}

is

x=\delta_{k}\!\left(k\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\frac{b^{(j+1)}}{a^{(j)}a^{(j+1)}}\right).

Proof.

If
b_{0}=0
, then
x=0
is the unique solution in
\{0,\dots,k-1\}
,
and the formula gives
\delta_{k}(0)=0
, consistently.
Henceforth assume
b_{0}\geq 1
.

Since
\gcd(a_{0},k)=1
, by Lemma
2.9
the equation
admits a unique solution in
\{0,\dots,k-1\}
.

Step 1: Construction of the auxiliary system.

Set
x_{1}:=x
and write the equation as

\delta_{a^{(0)}}(a^{(1)}x_{1})=b^{(1)}.

By definition of
\delta_{a^{(0)}}
, there exists an integer
x_{2}
such
that

a^{(1)}x_{1}=a^{(0)}x_{2}+b^{(1)}.

We claim that for each
n=2,\dots,r
there exists an integer
x_{n}

satisfying

a^{(n-1)}x_{n-1}=a^{(n-2)}x_{n}+b^{(n-1)}.

We proceed by induction on
n
.

Base case

n=2
: this is
(R_{2})
already established.

Inductive step
: assume
(R_{n})
holds for some
2\leq n<r
,
so

a^{(n-1)}x_{n-1}=a^{(n-2)}x_{n}+b^{(n-1)}.

Since
a^{(n-2)}x_{n}+b^{(n-1)}
is divisible by
a^{(n-1)}
,
applying
\delta_{a^{(n-1)}}
to both sides and using additive
compatibility gives

0=\delta_{a^{(n-1)}}\!\left(a^{(n-2)}x_{n}+b^{(n-1)}\right)=\delta_{a^{(n-1)}}\!\left(\delta_{a^{(n-1)}}(a^{(n-2)})\,x_{n}+\delta_{a^{(n-1)}}(b^{(n-1)})\right).

Setting
a^{(n)}:=\delta_{a^{(n-1)}}(a^{(n-2)})
, this becomes

\delta_{a^{(n-1)}}(a^{(n)}x_{n})=a^{(n-1)}-\delta_{a^{(n-1)}}(b^{(n-1)})=b^{(n)}.

By definition of
\delta_{a^{(n-1)}}
, there exists an integer
x_{n+1}

such that

a^{(n)}x_{n}=a^{(n-1)}x_{n+1}+b^{(n)},

which is
(R_{n+1})
. This completes the induction.

Step 2: Base case of back-substitution.

At step
n=r
, relation
(R_{r})
reads

a^{(r-1)}x_{r-1}=a^{(r-2)}x_{r}+b^{(r-1)}.

Since
a^{(r)}=\delta_{a^{(r-1)}}(a^{(r-2)})=1
, the equation

\delta_{a^{(r-1)}}(a^{(r)}x_{r-1})=b^{(r)}
reduces to

\delta_{a^{(r-1)}}(x_{r-1})=b^{(r)}
,
so we may set

x_{r}:=b^{(r)}=a^{(r-1)}-\delta_{a^{(r-1)}}(b^{(r-1)})\in\{0,\dots,a^{(r-1)}-1\}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

Step 3: Back-substitution, integrality, and the closed formula.

We prove by downward induction on
\ell=r,r-1,\dots,1
that

x_{\ell}=\sum_{j=\ell-1}^{r-1}b^{(j+1)}\prod_{i=\ell-1}^{j-1}\frac{a^{(i)}}{a^{(i+1)}}.

Base case

\ell=r
: the sum reduces to the single term

j=r-1
, giving

x_{r}=b^{(r)}\cdot\prod_{i=r-1}^{r-2}(\cdots)=b^{(r)}\cdot 1=b^{(r)},

where the empty product (lower index exceeds upper index) equals
1
.
This agrees with Step 2.

Inductive step
: assume
(F_{\ell})
holds for some

2\leq\ell\leq r
.
Rearranging
(R_{\ell})
,

x_{\ell-1}=\frac{a^{(\ell-2)}x_{\ell}+b^{(\ell-1)}}{a^{(\ell-1)}}.

Substituting
(F_{\ell})
,

\displaystyle x_{\ell-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{(\ell-2)}}{a^{(\ell-1)}}\sum_{j=\ell-1}^{r-1}b^{(j+1)}\prod_{i=\ell-1}^{j-1}\frac{a^{(i)}}{a^{(i+1)}}+\frac{b^{(\ell-1)}}{a^{(\ell-1)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=\ell-1}^{r-1}b^{(j+1)}\prod_{i=\ell-2}^{j-1}\frac{a^{(i)}}{a^{(i+1)}}+b^{(\ell-1)}\prod_{i=\ell-2}^{\ell-3}\frac{a^{(i)}}{a^{(i+1)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=\ell-2}^{r-1}b^{(j+1)}\prod_{i=\ell-2}^{j-1}\frac{a^{(i)}}{a^{(i+1)}},

which is
(F_{\ell-1})
. This completes the induction.

Setting
\ell=1
in
(F_{1})
and noting that
a^{(0)}=k
, we obtain

x_{1}=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}b^{(j+1)}\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}\frac{a^{(i)}}{a^{(i+1)}}.

The product telescopes:

\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}\frac{a^{(i)}}{a^{(i+1)}}=\frac{a^{(0)}}{a^{(j)}}=\frac{k}{a^{(j)}},\qquad j\geq 1,

and for
j=0
the empty product equals
1=k/a^{(0)}=k/k
,
so the formula
k/a^{(j)}
holds uniformly for all
0\leq j\leq r-1
.
Therefore

x_{1}=k\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\frac{b^{(j+1)}}{a^{(j)}a^{(j+1)}}\;\in\;\mathbb{Z},

and by construction
\delta_{k}(a_{0}x_{1})=b_{0}
.

Step 4: Minimality.

By Lemma
2.9
there is exactly one solution in

\{0,\dots,k-1\}
, so

x=\delta_{k}(x_{1})=\delta_{k}\!\left(k\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\frac{b^{(j+1)}}{a^{(j)}a^{(j+1)}}\right)

is that unique solution, which coincides with the minimal solution.
∎

Example 3.3
.

We find the unique solution
x\in\{0,\dots,12\}
of

\delta_{13}(5x)=4.

Here
k=13
,
a_{0}=5
,
b_{0}=4
, and
\gcd(5,13)=1
.

Sequences
\{a^{(n)}\}
and
\{b^{(n)}\}
.

\begin{array}[]{c|ccccc}n&0&1&2&3&4\\
\hline\cr a^{(n)}&13&5&\delta_{5}(13)=3&\delta_{3}(5)=2&\delta_{2}(3)=1\end{array}

Thus
r=4
. The sequence
\{b^{(n)}\}
is computed as

b^{(n)}=a^{(n-1)}-\delta_{a^{(n-1)}}(b^{(n-1)})
:

\begin{array}[]{c|cccc}n&1&2&3&4\\
\hline\cr b^{(n)}&4&5-\delta_{5}(4)=1&3-\delta_{3}(1)=2&2-\delta_{2}(2)=2\end{array}

Closed formula.
The terms of the sum are

The case of a general affine equation

Lemma 4.2

4.1
Counting pairs with sum constraints

Throughout this subsection,
p\geq 2
is fixed and

s,t,r\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
.
We define the indicator functions

H(x)=\begin{cases}1&x\geq 0,\\
0&x<0,\end{cases}\qquad\mathbf{1}(x)=\begin{cases}1&x=0,\\
0&x\neq 0.\end{cases}

(7)

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
a,r\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
.
The number of pairs

(s,t)\in(\{0,\dots,p-1\}\setminus\{a\})^{2}

with
s+t=r
and
s\leq t
is

\kappa(r,a)=\left\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\right\rfloor+1-H(r-a).

Proof.

Every solution has the form
(s,r-s)
with
0\leq s\leq r-s
,
i.e.
0\leq s\leq\lfloor r/2\rfloor
, giving

\lfloor r/2\rfloor+1
pairs in total.

We now count how many of these contain
a
in at least one component.
If
a>r
, then neither
s=a
nor
t=r-s=a
is possible for
any admissible
s\in\{0,\dots,\lfloor r/2\rfloor\}
, so no pair is
excluded; this is encoded by
H(r-a)=0
.

If
a\leq r
, then
s=a
gives the pair
(a,r-a)
, which is
admissible since
a\leq r-a\iff a\leq r/2
requires checking:
if
a\leq\lfloor r/2\rfloor
then
(a,r-a)
satisfies
s\leq t

and is the unique excluded pair;
if
a>\lfloor r/2\rfloor
(and
a\leq r
) then
s=r-a
gives
the pair
(r-a,a)
, again the unique excluded pair.
In both subcases exactly one pair is excluded; this is encoded by

H(r-a)=1
.

Therefore
\kappa(r,a)=\lfloor r/2\rfloor+1-H(r-a)
.
∎

We define the diagonal indicator

\tau(r,a)=(1-\delta_{2}(r))\,\mathbf{1}(2a-r),

which equals
1
precisely when
r
is even and
r=2a
,
i.e. when the diagonal pair
(a,a)
would be present.
Define the vector

\bm{\nu}(r,a)=\bigl(\kappa(r,a)-\tau(r,a),\;\tau(r,a)\bigr).

Its first component counts strictly ordered pairs
(s,t)
with

s<t
, and its second flags the presence of the diagonal pair.

Since
r\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
, we have
p+r\in\{p,\dots,2p-1\}
.
Hence the constraints
t=p+r-s\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
and
s\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}

force
s
to range over a contiguous interval.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
a,r\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
.
The number of pairs

(s,t)\in(\{0,\dots,p-1\}\setminus\{a\})^{2}

with
s+t=p+r
and
s\leq t
is

\bar{\kappa}(r,a,p)=\left\lfloor\frac{p-r}{2}\right\rfloor-H(a-r-1).

Proof.

From
t=p+r-s
and
t\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
, we need

p+r-s\leq p-1
, i.e.
s\geq r+1
.
Combined with
s\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
, the admissible values of
s

are
s\in\{r+1,\dots,p-1\}
.
The condition
s\leq t=p+r-s
gives
s\leq(p+r)/2
, so

s\in\left\{r+1,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{p+r}{2}\right\rfloor\right\},

which contains

\left\lfloor\frac{p+r}{2}\right\rfloor-r=\left\lfloor\frac{p-r}{2}\right\rfloor

integers.

We now exclude pairs containing
a
.
If
a\leq r
, then
a<r+1
, so
a
does not appear as
s
in
any admissible pair, and
t=p+r-a>p-1
so
a
cannot appear
as
t
either; thus
H(a-r-1)=0
and no pair is excluded.

If
a\geq r+1
, then
s=a
gives the admissible pair

(a,p+r-a)
with
t=p+r-a\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
,
which must be excluded; this is encoded by
H(a-r-1)=1
.

Therefore
\bar{\kappa}(r,a,p)=\lfloor(p-r)/2\rfloor-H(a-r-1)
.
∎

Analogously to
\bm{\nu}
, define the vector

\bar{\bm{\nu}}(r,a,p)=\bigl(\bar{\kappa}(r,a,p)-\tau(p-r,\,a-r),\;\tau(p-r,\,a-r)\bigr),

which decomposes the count into strictly ordered and diagonal
contributions.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
a,b,r\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
.
The number of pairs

(s,t)\in(\{0,\dots,p-1\}\setminus\{a\})\times(\{0,\dots,p-1\}\setminus\{b\})

with
s+t=r
is

\lambda(r,a,b)=r+1-H(r-a)-H(r-b)+\mathbf{1}(a+b-r).

Proof.

Every solution of
s+t=r
with
s,t\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
has the
form
(s,r-s)
with
s\in\{0,\dots,r\}
, giving
r+1
pairs in
total.

Define

A=\{(s,t):s+t=r,\;s=a\},\quad B=\{(s,t):s+t=r,\;t=b\}.

Then the desired count is

(r+1)-|A\cup B|=(r+1)-|A|-|B|+|A\cap B|
.

Counting
|A|
.

The pair
(a,r-a)
belongs to
A
if and only if

r-a\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
, i.e.
a\leq r
.
Hence
|A|=H(r-a)
.

Counting
|B|
.

The pair
(r-b,b)
belongs to
B
if and only if

r-b\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
, i.e.
b\leq r
.
Hence
|B|=H(r-b)
.

Counting
|A\cap B|
.

A pair belongs to both
A
and
B
if and only if
s=a
and
t=b
,
i.e.
a+b=r
. Hence

|A\cap B|=\mathbf{1}(a+b-r)
.

Substituting gives

\lambda(r,a,b)=r+1-H(r-a)-H(r-b)+\mathbf{1}(a+b-r).\qed

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
a,b,r\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}
.
The number of pairs

(s,t)\in(\{0,\dots,p-1\}\setminus\{a\})\times(\{0,\dots,p-1\}\setminus\{b\})

with

\bar{\lambda}(r,a,b,p)=p-r-1-H(a-r-1)-H(b-r-1)+\mathbf{1}(a+b-p-r).

From

and

, we need

,

, giving
p-r-1
pairs in total.

Define

The desired count is
(p-r-1)-|A\cup B|=(p-r-1)-|A|-|B|+|A\cap B|

Counting
|A|
.

The pair
(a,p+r-a)

(so that

lies in

Counting

.

Similarly,

.
Hence
|B|=H(b-r-1)

Both conditions hold simultaneously iff

and

i.e.

.
Hence

Substituting gives

4.2
Counting coprime pairs

Every positive integer coprime with
6
belongs to exactly one of

U:=\{6k+1:k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\},\qquad V:=\{6k+5:k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\}.

(8)

Since
U\equiv 1\pmod{6}
and
V\equiv 5\pmod{6}
, we have:
if
u_{1},u_{2}\in U
, then
u_{1}+u_{2}\equiv 2\pmod{6}
; if
v_{1},v_{2}\in V
,
then
v_{1}+v_{2}\equiv 4\pmod{6}
; and if
u\in U
,
v\in V
, then

u+v\equiv 0\pmod{6}
.
Therefore, when
2n
is written as a sum of two integers coprime with
6
,
the residue
\delta_{3}(n)
determines the type of decomposition:

•

if
\delta_{3}(n)=0
, one summand lies in
U
and the other in
V
,

•

if
\delta_{3}(n)=1
, both summands lie in
U
,

•

if
\delta_{3}(n)=2
, both summands lie in
V
.

Define the selector function
h:\{0,1,2\}\to\mathbb{Z}
by

h(0)=3,\qquad h(1)=1,\qquad h(2)=5.

This function encodes the constant term arising in the three possible
coprime decompositions. It admits the polynomial representation

h(x)=3x^{2}-5x+3,

which allows a unified expression for the auxiliary quantity

m(n):=\frac{n-h(\delta_{3}(n))}{3},\qquad\omega(n,p):=\frac{m(n)-\delta_{p}(m(n))}{p}=\left\lfloor\frac{m(n)}{p}\right\rfloor.

(9)

Lemma 4.10
.

For all integers
n
, the quantity
m(n)
is an integer.

Proof.

We verify that
h(j)\equiv j\pmod{3}
for
j\in\{0,1,2\}
:

h(0)=3\equiv 0
,
h(1)=1\equiv 1
,
h(2)=5\equiv 2\pmod{3}
.
Since
n\equiv\delta_{3}(n)\pmod{3}
, it follows that

3\mid(n-h(\delta_{3}(n)))
, so
m(n)\in\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

We define the counting vectors

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}(n,p)

\displaystyle:=\bigl(\omega(n,p)+1,\;\lfloor\omega(n,p)/2\rfloor+1\bigr),

(10)

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{\eta}}(n,p)

\displaystyle:=\bigl(\omega(n,p),\;\lfloor(\omega(n,p)-1)/2\rfloor+1\bigr),

(11)

and recall the residue vectors
\bm{\nu}(r,a)
and

\overline{\bm{\nu}}(r,a,p)
defined in
Section
4.1
.
We define the scalar product

(x_{1},x_{2})\cdot(y_{1},y_{2}):=x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}
.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
2n
be a positive even integer with

Proof.

We treat
\delta_{3}(n)=1
; the case

is analogous
with

replaced by

Every admissible decomposition with

.

Theorem 4.13
.

Let
p\geq 5
be prime.
For every positive even integer
2n
,

g(2n,p)=\begin{cases}Q(n,p)&\text{if }\delta_{3}(n)\in\{1,2\},\\
Q_{0}(n,p)&\text{if }\delta_{3}(n)=0,\end{cases}

where
Q(n,p)
and
Q_{0}(n,p)
are given by
(

) and (
13
).

Proof.

The cases
\delta_{3}(n)\in\{0,1,2\}
are exhaustive and mutually
exclusive. For
\delta_{3}(n)\in\{1,2\}
, the result is
Lemma
4.11
. For
\delta_{3}(n)=0
, it is
Lemma
4.12
. Both lemmas apply to all
positive even integers
2n
and primes
p\geq 5
.
∎

6
Conclusions

We derived explicit closed-form formulas for the counting function

g(2n,p)=\#\bigl\{(h,k)\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}:h+k=2n,\;h\leq k,\;\gcd(h,6p)=\gcd(k,6p)=1\bigr\},

expressed in terms of elementary remainder operations and step
functions. For fixed
p
, after precomputing the parameters

a(p)
and
b(p)
in
O(\log p)
operations via the Euclidean
algorithm, each evaluation of
g(2n,p)
requires
O(1)

operations, in contrast to the
O(n)
brute-force approach.
The formulas were validated against a brute-force oracle for
all
2n\leq 10^{5}
and
p\in\{5,7,11,13,17,19,23\}
,
with perfect agreement in all cases.

The formulas make explicit a piecewise affine structure of

g(2n,p)
along arithmetic progressions of
n
, governed by
residue classes modulo
3
and
p
.
This structure is directly encoded in the minimal solutions

a(p)
and
b(p)
of the congruences

6x\equiv-1\pmod{p}
and
6x\equiv-5\pmod{p}
,
which act as the key arithmetic parameters of the problem.

The approach extends naturally to exclusion sets of the form

\{2,3,p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\}
for any finite collection of primes,
at the cost of additional case analysis governed by residues
modulo
6p_{1}\cdots p_{k}
.
We leave this generalization for future work.
