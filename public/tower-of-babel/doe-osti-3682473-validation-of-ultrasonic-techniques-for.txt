 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report presents the development and validation of an ultrasonic nondestructive evaluation technique
for measuring tensile stress in reinforcing steel (rebar), with emphasis on temperature effects and
application to rebar embedded in concrete. The approach is based on the acoustoelastic effect, in which
ultrasonic wave velocity changes with applied stress. Building on previous work with bare rebar, this
study involved investigating the temperature dependence of ultrasonic velocity at different stress levels
and evaluated the stress–velocity relationship for #8, #9, and #10 rebars embedded in concrete under
different loading conditions.

Temperature effects were first investigated using bare #8 rebar. Environmental chamber tests at
temperature change rates of 2°C–8°C/h showed a consistent linear relationship between temperature and
ultrasonic velocity, with a sensitivity of approximately −15.7 × 10−5/°C. Additional heating and cooling
tests were performed while the rebar was maintained at stress levels ranging from 38% to 91% of the
yield stress. Under rapid heating and cooling, the temperature–velocity relationships exhibited fusiform-
shaped hysteresis loops. While the slopes measured at relatively low temperature change rates showed no
clear dependence on stress, the curvature of the heating and cooling relationships generally decreased
with increasing stress, indicating a potential interaction between stress and transient temperature effects
that warrants further investigation.

The methodology was then evaluated using six concrete-embedded rebar specimens representing #8, #9,
and #10 rebars. Two specimens of each size were subjected to repeated loading and unloading cycles to
90% of the yield stress at loading rates of 75 and 100 lb/s. The ultrasonic velocity generally decreased
linearly with increasing tensile stress and recovered during unloading. Most tests exhibited similar
velocity–stress sensitivities of approximately −2.4 × 10−5/ksi for relative velocity change and
−0.14 (m/s)/ksi for absolute velocity change. These values are comparable to those previously obtained
from bare rebar, demonstrating that the stress-dependent ultrasonic response remains measurable after the
rebar is embedded in concrete. Some specimen-to-specimen variability and increased scatter were
observed, indicating the need for further investigation of factors such as sensor coupling, concrete–rebar
interaction, and test conditions.

Finally, a #8 rebar specimen embedded in concrete was monitored for approximately 44 h under natural
ambient heating and cooling. The measured temperature sensitivity was approximately −15.5 × 10−5/°C,
closely matching the environmental chamber results. This agreement suggests that laboratory-derived
temperature–velocity relationships can potentially be used to compensate for ambient temperature effects
in field measurements.

Overall, the results demonstrate the feasibility of ultrasonic acoustoelastic measurements for evaluating
stress in rebar embedded in concrete while highlighting temperature as an important factor for
quantitative stress estimation. The findings provide a technical basis for developing temperature-
compensated ultrasonic stress measurements and advancing the technique toward field deployment for
condition assessment and long-term monitoring of reinforced concrete structures in nuclear facilities.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Accurate characterization of residual and applied stresses within structural components is critical to
ensuring the safety, reliability, and long-term performance of engineered systems. Residual stresses
generated during manufacturing, fabrication, or service can significantly affect fatigue life, crack
initiation, crack propagation, and structural integrity. In reinforced concrete structures, which are widely
used in civil infrastructure and nuclear facilities, embedded steel reinforcement (i.e., rebar) is the primary
load-bearing component under tensile loading. The structural performance and service life of these
systems are strongly influenced by the stress state of the embedded reinforcement, yet these stresses
cannot be directly measured once the rebar is encased in concrete. Consequently, the development of
reliable, nondestructive methods for quantifying internal stresses of rebar is of increasing importance,
particularly for aging infrastructure, seismic applications, and safety-critical facilities such as concrete
structures in nuclear power plants.

Traditional stress measurement approaches, including strain gauges, extensometers, hole-drilling, and
destructive sampling, provide only localized measurements and often require direct access to the
structural component or permanent modification of the material. Such techniques are generally unsuitable
for in-service reinforced concrete structures, where destructive inspection is undesirable or impractical.
As nuclear power plants pursue long-term operation and utilities seek to extend the service life of critical
infrastructure while maintaining structural safety, demand is increasing for accurate, noninvasive stress-
monitoring technologies that can be deployed in the field.

This report presents the development and validation of an ultrasonic nondestructive evaluation (NDE)
methodology for assessing stress in steel reinforcement using the acoustoelastic effect, whereby changes
in ultrasonic wave velocity are correlated with the material stress state, considering the temperature effect.
Compared with conventional stress measurement techniques, ultrasonic methods provide a noninvasive
means of evaluating rebar stresses with high sensitivity, enabling inspection of embedded reinforcement
without causing major damage to the rebar and surrounding concrete. However, access to the rebar
surfaces is required for installing ultrasonic sensors. In addition to their portability, relatively low cost,
and suitability for field deployment, ultrasonic techniques are well suited for structural health monitoring
of reinforced concrete infrastructure for long-term purposes. The capability to quantify stress in
embedded steel reinforcement can support condition assessment, life-extension decisions, and integrity
management of safety-critical structures, particularly in aging civil infrastructure and nuclear power
plants.

Previous studies have investigated several NDE techniques for residual stress measurement, including
ultrasonic acoustoelastic methods, neutron and X-ray diffraction, and magnetic approaches for
ferromagnetic materials. Although diffraction techniques provide high measurement accuracy, they
require specialized facilities and are not practical for field deployment. Magnetic methods are portable but
are limited by surface accessibility, calibration requirements, and reduced applicability to embedded
reinforcement. Consequently, ultrasonic techniques have emerged as a promising alternative because they
provide a practical balance of sensitivity, portability, and suitability for in situ applications.

Building on this foundation, previous work developed and experimentally validated an ultrasonic stress
measurement methodology based on the acoustoelastic effect for rebar. Controlled tensile loading
experiments were performed on #8 rebar; ultrasonic wave propagation, applied load, and temperature
were monitored during these experiments. High-resolution signal processing using coda wave
interferometry enabled the detection of small stress-induced velocity changes despite environmental and
thermal influences. The experimental results demonstrated a strong, repeatable linear relationship
between ultrasonic wave velocity and applied stress during loading and unloading cycles while
highlighting the influence of thermoelastic effects and stress-history-dependent behavior.

 1

Building upon the previous findings, this work involved investigating the influence of temperature on
ultrasonic wave velocity under different stress levels in bare rebar. Although earlier studies established a
strong and repeatable linear relationship between ultrasonic velocity and applied stress through the
acoustoelastic effect, field deployment introduces additional environmental factors that can affect
measurement accuracy. In particular, ambient temperature variations alter ultrasonic wave velocity
through thermoelastic effects, potentially masking or biasing stress-induced velocity changes. Therefore,
quantifying the temperature dependence of ultrasonic velocity across a range of stress levels is essential
for developing reliable temperature compensation strategies and improving the accuracy of in situ stress
measurements.

This work also extended the investigation to reinforcing bars embedded in concrete to evaluate the
feasibility of applying the technique under realistic structural conditions. Specifically, the study examined
the ultrasonic response of embedded reinforcement at different stress levels and assessed the practicality
of installing ultrasonic sensors through small-diameter access holes drilled in the concrete cover. The
results provide insight into signal transmission, sensor coupling, and measurement reliability, helping
establish the viability of ultrasonic acoustoelastic stress monitoring for field deployment in reinforced
concrete structures.

In summary, this report presents a systematic investigation of ultrasonic techniques for stress
measurement in reinforcing steel, with particular emphasis on the effects of temperature and concrete
embedment. Building on previous work demonstrating the acoustoelastic relationship between ultrasonic
velocity and stress in bare rebar, this study evaluated the temperature dependence of ultrasonic velocity at
different stress levels and extended the methodology to #8, #9, and #10 rebars embedded in concrete. The
effects of rebar size, loading rate, and ambient temperature variation were also investigated. The results
provide a technical basis for developing temperature compensation strategies and evaluating the
feasibility of ultrasonic stress measurement for future field applications in reinforced concrete structures.

The remainder of this report is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the acoustoelastic effect, which
forms the theoretical basis for the ultrasonic approach. Section 3 describes the test specimens,
experimental setup, and signal processing algorithm used in this study. Section 4 discusses the results of
temperature effect tests and tensile tests performed on different rebar specimens, including bare rebar and
rebar embedded in concrete specimens. Finally, the conclusions section presents key takeaways, future
research directions, and considerations for field deployment.

 2. THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

2.1 ACOUSTOELASTIC EFFECT

The acoustoelastic effect refers to the phenomenon in which the velocity of ultrasonic waves propagating
through a material varies as a function of applied stress or strain. This behavior arises due to the nonlinear
elastic properties of solids, where the stress state alters the stiffness of the material and, consequently, the
acoustic wave speed. In the linear elastic range, the relationship between stress and ultrasonic velocity is
typically approximated as linear, particularly for longitudinal and shear wave modes. The foundational
theory was first described by Hughes and Kelly in the 1950s [1] and has since been applied extensively in
NDE, particularly for residual stress and in situ stress measurements in metals, concrete, and composites.

In practice, the acoustoelastic effect is influenced by several factors, including material anisotropy,
temperature, microstructural state, and strain rate. Temperature in particular has a direct effect on
ultrasonic wave velocity due to thermal expansion and softening of the elastic modulus. Therefore,
accurate stress measurement using the acoustoelastic effect often requires compensation for temperature
fluctuations. Additionally, the strain rate can influence the stress state during dynamic loading although
its effect is generally minimal in quasi-static tests. By leveraging the acoustoelastic effect, ultrasonic
techniques can provide a noninvasive and repeatable means to monitor stress evolution in structural
materials, making it a valuable tool for applications such as rebar stress evaluation in nuclear concrete
structures.

2.2 GUIDED WAVE DISPERSION IN REBAR EMBEDDED IN CONCRETE

Unlike bulk ultrasonic waves that propagate through an unbounded medium, ultrasonic waves traveling in
a rebar are constrained by the cylindrical geometry and propagate as guided waves. The traction-free
boundary of the rebar causes multiple reflections and mode coupling, resulting in families of longitudinal,
torsional, and flexural guided-wave modes whose propagation characteristics depend on the product of
frequency and diameter product. When a longitudinal contact transducer is mounted on the side surface of
the rebar, the excitation is dominated by the axisymmetric longitudinal guided mode, which carries most
of the transmitted energy along the bar axis. The propagation velocity of this mode is governed by the
elastic properties and density of the steel and is sensitive to changes in axial stress via the acoustoelastic
effect.

In the present study, a longitudinal contact transducer was mounted on the side surface of the rebar to
generate and receive ultrasonic guided waves. Although several guided-wave modes can theoretically be
excited in a cylindrical waveguide, the excitation is dominated by the fundamental axisymmetric
longitudinal mode, denoted as L(0, 1). This mode is preferentially generated because the transducer
applies a predominantly normal compressive load to the rebar surface, producing symmetric axial motion.
The L(0, 1) mode propagates with relatively low dispersion over short propagation distances and carries
most of the transmitted energy, making it well suited for accurate time-of-flight measurements.
Furthermore, its propagation velocity is directly influenced by changes in the elastic constants induced by
axial stress through the acoustoelastic effect.

When the rebar is embedded in the concrete, energy will leak from the rebar surface into the surrounding
concrete. The resulted attenuation is much higher than the ultrasonic wave propagating in bare rebar.
Figure 2 shows the dispersion curves of ultrasonic wave propagating in a 25.4 mm rebar embedded in
concrete from model L(0, 1) to L(0, 12). The optimal frequency of exciting the L(0, 1) is around 2 MHz
because the attenuation around this frequency is the lowest according to the attenuation dispersion curve
in Figure 2c. However, multiple modes may exist in the received signal due to the broadband of the
transmitting transducer. More details about the dispersion curve analysis can be found elsewhere [2].

 3. TEST SPECIMENS AND EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

3.1 TEST SPECIMENS

The bare rebar specimens consist of #8 bars with a 1 in. diameter and a yield strength of 60 ksi. Each bar
is 16 in. long. Ultrasonic transducers were mounted at the midpoint of the rebar, spaced 3 in. apart.

The concrete mixture consisted of Type I-L Portland cement, fine aggregate, coarse aggregate, water, and
Sika® ViscoCrete® 2100 high-range water-reducing admixture (SP) (Table 1). The constituent materials
were mixed in a 3 ft³ concrete mixer for approximately 10 min to obtain a uniform mixture.

Nine reinforced concrete specimens were cast in 6 in. × 12 in. cylindrical molds. Three reinforcing bar
sizes—#8, #9, and #10—were evaluated, and three replicate specimens were produced for each size. Each
reinforcing bar was 30 in. long. A central opening was created at the base of each mold to allow
placement of the bar, which was positioned concentrically along the longitudinal axis and secured so that
approximately 9 in. extended beyond each end of the specimen.

For each bar size, two of the three specimens included a simulated core-access opening. These openings
were formed using 2 in. diameter PVC pipes inserted laterally through the mold wall and placed in direct
contact with the reinforcing bar (example shown in Figure 3). The remaining specimen in each group was
cast without openings and will be cored and tested at a later stage.

The prepared molds were filled completely with fresh concrete. During placement, the concrete was
consolidated by rodding to reduce entrapped air and ensure uniform compaction around the reinforcing
bars and PVC inserts. After casting, each specimen was sealed in a plastic bag and stored outdoors under

 6

elevated-temperature conditions to accelerate curing. Approximately 1 L of water was added to each bag
daily to maintain humidity and limit moisture loss throughout the curing period.

Before testing, the PVC inserts were removed to expose the simulated core-access openings extending
from the exterior surface of the specimen to the centrally embedded reinforcing bar. Any residual
concrete covering the bar surface within the opening was carefully cleared. The exposed portion of the
bar at the intended transducer location was then polished with a rotary tool to create a clean, smooth, and
consistent contact surface. The compressive strength of two specimens was evaluated in accordance with
ASTM C39 using a loading rate of 1,000 lbf/s. The first specimen reached a compressive strength of
9,362.6 psi, the second reached 9,605.9 psi, and both exhibited Type-5 failure modes.

3.2 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP FOR TENSILE TEST AND ULTRASONIC MONITORING

3.2.1 Tensile Test for Bare Rebar

The bare rebar used for tensile testing was 16 in. long and 1 in. in diameter. Before tensile testing, two
ultrasonic transducers were bonded to the polished rebar surface using epoxy. The transducers were
positioned 3 in. apart at the center of the bar, and a type-T thermocouple was installed midway between
them to monitor temperature. As shown in Figure 4, the tensile grips engaged 3.75 in. at each end of the
rebar. As shown in Figure 5, a heating pad was wrapped around the central portion of the bar, including
the transducers, to elevate the temperature during loading. Throughout the tensile test, load data were
recorded simultaneously with ultrasonic signals and temperature measurements.

3.2.2 Tensile Test for Rebar Embedded in Concrete

Six reinforced concrete specimens with embedded rebar were cast in the structural laboratory at the
University of Tennessee, Knoxville. The rebar sizes ranged from #8 to #10; two specimens were prepared
for each size. Each specimen consisted of a 6 in. diameter concrete cylinder with a length of 12 in., as
shown in Figure 6. Two 1 in. diameter openings were intentionally formed to allow installation of the
ultrasonic transducers. Before installation of the transducers, the rebar surface at each opening was
exposed and polished. The transducers were then bonded to the rebar using epoxy, as illustrated in Figure

3.2.3 Ultrasonic Monitoring Setup

Ultrasonic signals were acquired using a customized ultrasonic measurement system, as illustrated in
Figure 9. A 5 MHz ultrasonic transducer (2.25 MHz transducers for rebar in concrete) was used as the
transmitter and was excited by a pulser–receiver unit (DPR300, BYK-Gardner, Pittsford, New York). The
ultrasonic waves propagated through the rebar and were captured by a receiving transducer. The received
signals were amplified by the pulser–receiver and digitized using an oscilloscope (Picoscope 5444D
MSO, Pico Technology, Cambridgeshire, UK) at a sampling rate of 20 MS/s. To improve the signal-to-
noise ratio, each waveform was averaged over 100 acquisitions. Simultaneously, the temperature of the
rebar was monitored using a type-T thermocouple connected to a temperature logger (NI-9211, National
Instruments). Both ultrasonic and temperature data were recorded every 5 s throughout the tensile test.
Data acquisition was fully automated and controlled via a custom LabVIEW program.

temperature change. The stretching technique was first proposed by Lobkis and Weaver [3]; the method
allows processing the full waveform by applying a stretching factor ε to the perturbed signal φ′. Using the
stretching technique, the cross-correlation coefficient CC is given by Eq. (3):

4.1 BARE REBAR TEST WITH HEATING AND COOLING

Before the rebar tension and temperature test, a sperate test focusing on the temperature rate effect was
conducted. Rebar samples were placed in an environment chamber (Controls 10-D1429/A) experiencing
temperature variations between 20°C and 50°C while ultrasonic signals were collected at different
temperatures, as shown in Figure 13. The test was repeated at different temperature rates of 2°C/h, 4°C/h,
6°C/h, and 8°C/h. The correlations between ultrasonic velocity and temperature are plotted in Figure 14.
These correlations all exhibit high linearity, with similar slopes around 15.7 × 10−5/°C. This result
indicates that the temperature rate does not affect the temperature–velocity correlation in the range of
2°C/h to 8°C/h. For a small-dimension specimen like rebar, even when the rate goes to 15°C/h, the
temperature–velocity correlation should still have the same slope. However, when the rate is much higher,
such as 50°C/h, the correlation may exhibit nonlinearity.

As shown in Table 2, four bare rebar samples underwent tensile testing. The maximum tensile stress
ranges from 38% to 91% of the yield stress, with maximum loading from 18 kips to 43 kips. All the rebar
samples were #8 with 1 in. diameter and 60 ksi of yield stress. Figure 15 shows the histories of the
loading, temperature, and corresponding wave velocity for Test 2 in Table 2. However, all the tests
followed the same test procedure, albeit with different maximum load/stress. First, the rebar was loaded to
the desired maximum load and unloaded to zero using the same rate. The first cycle was used to remove
the cast stress of the rebar. Then, the rebar was loaded to the same maximum load and held on this level
(start to 27 min in the figure). A heating pad covering the whole ultrasonic sensing area was installed
around the rebar.

The rebar was heated to 38°C by the heating pad with a constant voltage of 7.6 V and current of 2.2 A.
After reaching 38°C, the heating pad was turned off for natural cooling to 26°C. The heating and cooling
cycle was repeated once more while the stress was held at the maximum level. After that, the rebar was
unloaded to zero. The three stages of the testing are illustrated in Figure 15. As shown in the figure, only
Stage 1 imparted a stress effect on the ultrasonic velocity. The temperature variation in this stage was
caused by the thermoelastic effect. In Stage 2, the velocity was affected by the temperature effect only at
a specific stress level. In Stage 3, the velocity was affected by the combined effects of the natural cooling
and unloading. The present work focuses on the results from Stages 1 and 2.

4.2.1 #8 Rebar Embedded in Concrete

The stress, temperature, and ultrasonic wave velocity change histories are shown in Figure 22. During the
first loading cycle, a sudden decrease in ultrasonic wave velocity was observed at approximately 153 s, as

Overall, the ultrasonic wave velocity exhibited an inverse and approximately symmetric trend with
applied stress, decreasing during loading and recovering during unloading. At approximately 2,800 s,
when the applied stress approached the maximum target stress, the ultrasonic velocity became noticeably
noisy before and after the peak stress. Similar behavior was observed in several subsequent tests;
however, the cause of this increased variability is not yet fully understood and requires further
investigation. The temperature of the rebar showed minor decreasing and increasing in the loading and
unloading processes which was caused by the thermoelastic effect.

absolute ultrasonic velocity with respect to stress ranged from 0.19 to 0.23 (m/s)/ksi. The stress–velocity

 5. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK

Conclusions
This work investigated the feasibility of using ultrasonic wave velocity measurements to quantify tensile
stress in rebar while accounting for temperature effects and the influence of concrete embedment. The
study extended previous work on bare rebar by systematically evaluating the temperature dependence of
ultrasonic velocity at different stress levels and validating the stress–velocity relationship using #8, #9,
and #10 rebars embedded in concrete. Overall, the results demonstrate that ultrasonic velocity remains
sensitive to applied tensile stress after the rebar is embedded in concrete, and they support the feasibility
of using the acoustoelastic effect for in situ stress assessment of reinforced concrete structures.

The temperature tests on bare #8 rebar showed that ultrasonic velocity decreased approximately linearly
with increasing temperature when the temperature change rate was relatively low. Environmental
chamber tests conducted at rates of 2°C–8°C/h produced similar temperature–velocity sensitivities,
indicating that the temperature rate had little influence within this range. However, when the rebar was
subjected to much faster heating and cooling while held at different stress levels, the temperature–velocity
correlations exhibited fusiform-shaped hysteresis loops rather than simple linear relationships. The slopes
near the ends of the heating and cooling processes, when the temperature rates were lower, did not exhibit
a clear dependence on applied stress. By contrast, the overall shape and curvature of the hysteresis loops
changed with stress level. In particular, the magnitude of the curvature generally decreased as the applied
stress increased, resulting in flatter temperature–velocity correlation loops at higher stress levels. These
observations suggest a coupled influence of stress and transient thermal conditions on ultrasonic velocity
when temperature changes rapidly. Additional experiments are needed to confirm this relationship and
determine its physical mechanism.

The tensile tests of rebars embedded in concrete further demonstrated a repeatable inverse relationship
between ultrasonic velocity and applied tensile stress. Six specimens covering three rebar sizes (#8, #9,
and #10) and two loading rates (75 and 100 lb/s) were tested to 90% of the nominal yield stress. Although
some variability was observed among individual specimens, most tests produced similar velocity–stress
sensitivities of approximately −2.4 × 10−5/ksi for relative velocity change and −0.14 (m/s)/ksi for absolute
velocity change. These values are also close to the approximately −2.5 × 10−5 to −2.6 × 10−5/ksi
sensitivity obtained from bare rebar in the previous work, suggesting that concrete embedment does not
fundamentally alter the stress sensitivity of the ultrasonic response. Minor differences between bare rebar
and rebar embedded in concrete could be caused by the transducers used: 5 MHz for bare rebar and 2.25
MHz for the rebar in concrete. The consistency of the slopes among repeated loading and unloading
cycles also demonstrates the repeatability of the measurement approach. However, differences were
observed for individual specimens, particularly the higher sensitivity of the #8 specimen tested at 75 lb/s
and the lower sensitivity of the #9 specimen tested at 100 lb/s. These variations indicate that specimen-to-
specimen variability, sensor coupling, concrete–rebar interaction, and other experimental factors should
be further investigated before a universal calibration relationship is established.

The ambient temperature test provides additional evidence supporting practical temperature
compensation. A #8 rebar embedded in concrete was exposed to natural environmental conditions for
approximately 44 h and was subjected to two heating and cooling cycles. Despite the 2.5 in. concrete
cover, the rebar experienced measurable temperature variations, and the ultrasonic velocity closely
followed these changes. A temperature sensitivity of approximately −15.5 × 10−5/°C was obtained for
most heating and cooling periods; this value was close to that measured independently in the
environmental chamber. This agreement is important for field implementation because it suggests that
temperature calibration obtained under controlled laboratory conditions may be applicable to embedded
rebar subjected to natural environmental temperature variations. Therefore, stress-induced and

 32

temperature-induced velocity changes could potentially be separated using an experimentally established
temperature compensation relationship.

Future Work

A machine learning model based on physics-informed polynomial regression was developed to predict
rebar stress using ultrasonic velocity, temperature, and temperature change rate as model inputs. The
model was trained using data from the bare rebar heating and cooling tests at 38% and 91% of the yield
stress, together with data from the embedded #9 rebar tests, and was evaluated using held-out data.
However, the prediction performance was not satisfactory, and the results were therefore not included in
the main results section. The primary limitation was the insufficient amount and coverage of the training
data, particularly the lack of heating and cooling measurements at different stress levels. Additional
experiments covering different combinations of stress and temperature conditions are needed to develop
and validate a more reliable stress prediction model.

Several other challenges remain before the technique can be applied to field measurements. Some tests
exhibited increased scatter or outlier ultrasonic velocity measurements, particularly at certain stress
ranges and near peak loading. In addition, the first loading cycle occasionally differed from subsequent
cycles, indicating possible effects associated with initial stress redistribution, concrete cracking, rebar–
concrete interaction, or stabilization of the sensor coupling. These effects require additional investigation.
Furthermore, a larger number of specimens should be tested to quantify the variability associated with
rebar size, loading rate, concrete properties, sensor installation, and stress history. The combined
influence of stress and rapid temperature variation should be investigated further, particularly the
observed change in the curvature of the temperature–velocity hysteresis loops with increasing stress.

Overall, the results of this study provide an important step toward field deployment of ultrasonic NDE for
rebar stress measurement in reinforced concrete structures. The comparable acoustoelastic sensitivities
measured for bare and concrete-embedded rebars demonstrate that the ultrasonic stress measurement
approach can be extended to realistic reinforced concrete configurations. Furthermore, the agreement
between laboratory chamber and ambient temperature measurements provides a practical basis for
temperature compensation. Future work should focus on validating the technique with a larger specimen
population, improving sensor installation and long-term coupling, developing robust temperature
compensation algorithms, and evaluating the approach on representative reinforced concrete structures
under realistic field conditions. These developments will support the eventual application of ultrasonic
stress measurements for condition assessment and long-term monitoring of safety-critical reinforced
concrete structures, including those in nuclear power plants.
