Comparison of safety and long-term outcomes of retrograde chronic total occlusion percutaneous coronary intervention in patients with vs. without diabetes mellitus: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Dunliang Ma, Qiheng Wan, Song Wen, Zehan Huang, Kaize Wu, Feihuang Han, Jiquan Xiao, Feng Wang, Yuqing Huang, Bin Zhang, Jun Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe safety profile and long-term outcomes of retrograde percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for chronic total occlusion (CTO) in patients with diabetes mellitus (DM) remain inadequately characterized. We evaluated procedural and extended follow-up results in this cohort.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study enrolled adults undergoing retrograde CTO-PCI; individuals with incomplete data, malignancy, or end-stage renal disease were excluded. Primary and secondary outcomes were all-cause mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), respectively. Intergroup comparisons used standard univariate tests. Cumulative hazard functions were estimated via the Nelson-Aalen method, and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were performed to adjust for clinical confounders and identify variables associated with MACE.ResultsIn total, 836 patients met the criteria. Success rates for collateral channel (CC) tracking (92.0% in the DM group vs. 95.0% in the non-DM group, P=0.08), retrograde PCI success (87.4% in the DM group vs. 91.3% in the non-DM group, P=0.07), and final recanalization (89.4% in the DM group vs. 92.0% in the non-DM group, P=0.20) were statistically similar. In-hospital complication rates were minimal and comparable (all P>0.05). Follow-up data were obtained for 767 patients (91.7%) over a median of 1,041 days. Patients with DM experienced a significantly higher risk of MACE (19.2% in the DM group vs. 13.9% in the non-DM group, P=0.046), primarily driven by increased all-cause mortality (9.3% in the DM group vs. 4.5% in the non-DM group, P=0.007) and cardiac death (7.4% in the DM group vs. 2.2% in the non-DM group, P<0.001). After multivariable adjustment for clinical confounders, DM remained a strong independent predictor of higher long-term all-cause and cardiac mortality.ConclusionsRetrograde PCI is technically safe and effective in CTO patients with DM. However, this cohort carries a significantly higher long-term risk of mortality and MACE than non-diabetic counterparts, necessitating intensified post-procedural risk-factor management.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Among 836 retrograde chronic total occlusion (CTO)-percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) patients, technical success and in-hospital complications were identical in those with or without diabetes.

• After 1,041-day median follow-up, diabetics had higher major adverse cardiovascular events [19.2% in the diabetes mellitus (DM) group vs. 13.9% in the non-DM group, P=0.046] driven by doubled all-cause [9.3% in the DM group vs. 4.5 % in the non-DM group, adjusted hazard ratio (HR) =2.346] and tripled cardiac death (7.4 % vs. 2.2 %, adjusted HR =3.721).

• Risk amplification was confined to diabetics aged >60 years, male, hypertensive, chronic kidney disease, or left ventricular ejection fraction <40%.

What is known and what is new?

• Diabetes worsens long-term prognosis after CTO-PCI, but prior data are antegrade-centric and collateral-impairment theory remains untested for retrograde approach.

• This is the first real-world evidence showing that retrograde CTO-PCI is technically safe and equally successful in diabetics despite poorer collaterals, yet long-term mortality excess is substantial and predictable by simple baseline profile.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Diabetes should not preclude retrograde CTO-PCI, but diabetic patients require intensified lifelong risk-factor control, including optimal glycemic, lipid, and blood pressure management, as well as heart failure therapy. Early heart-team review for multivessel disease and tailored long-term surveillance are essential to mitigate the elevated mortality risk and improve clinical outcomes in this high-risk population.

Introduction

It is established that diabetes mellitus (DM) is highly prevalent among patients with coronary artery disease (CAD) and constitutes a significant risk factor for developing chronic total occlusion (CTO) (1-3). Indeed, DM is present in 27% to 45% of individuals undergoing CTO revascularization (3-5). Although earlier studies have typically reported similar procedural success for percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) across diabetic and non-diabetic populations, findings concerning perioperative adverse events and extended major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) continue to show considerable heterogeneity (6-9). Notably, the predominant revascularization strategy in these studies was the antegrade approach, with limited utilization of the retrograde technique, potentially introducing selection bias. Furthermore, substantial evidence links DM to impaired collateral circulation development in CTO patients (10-12), a critical factor known to influence both the success and complication profile of retrograde CTO-PCI (13-18). Despite this pathophysiological link and the procedural significance of collaterals, the specific outcomes of retrograde CTO-PCI in patients with DM remain unexplored in real-world practice. Consequently, the present investigation seeks to assess both procedural and long-term outcomes with extended clinical follow-up period following retrograde recanalization of CTO among diabetic subjects. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://cdt.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/cdt-2026-1-0060/rc).

Methods

Study population

We conducted a single-center, retrospective cohort study of all consecutive patients who underwent CTO-PCI at Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital between January 2011 and April 2023. Indications for CTO revascularization were based on the presence of drug-refractory angina and/or objective evidence of ischemia or viability in the territory supplied by the CTO. Ischemia was assessed primarily via non-invasive stress testing (including stress echocardiography, myocardial perfusion imaging, or cardiac magnetic resonance), while viability in territories with severe dysfunction was evaluated using cardiac magnetic resonance, dobutamine stress echocardiography, or single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT). Fractional flow reserve (FFR) was used in selected cases where non-invasive data were equivocal.

Eligibility criteria were age ≥18 years, angiographic confirmation of at least one coronary CTO, and written consent for data collection. Baseline characteristics, procedural variables, and long-term outcomes were compared between those with and without DM. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital [No. GDREC2017196H(R1)], and written informed consent was taken from all patients.

Procedures and definitions

Technical details of the retrograde CTO-PCI have been reported elsewhere (19). Both diagnostic angiography and interventional procedures were carried out via transfemoral or transradial approaches. All retrograde CTO-PCI procedures were performed by members of a single dedicated interventional team. All operators were high-volume CTO-PCI physicians, each having performed more than 300 CTO-PCI procedures in total and at least 50 CTO-PCI procedures annually as the primary operator. Operator selection and procedural strategy were based on lesion characteristics and clinical judgment rather than the subsequent study grouping. CTO was defined as complete interruption of antegrade coronary flow observed angiographically, with an anticipated persistence of ≥3 months (20). Estimation of occlusion age relied on symptomatic history, documented prior myocardial infarction (MI), and previous angiographic records. Both lesion complexity and procedural difficulty were quantified using the Multicenter CTO Registry of Japan (J-CTO) score (21). Collateral connections were categorized as previously established (22). DM was diagnosed if any of the following were present: elevated fasting glucose (≥7.0 mmol/L), 2-hour post-load glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L during oral glucose tolerance testing, hemoglobin A1c ≥6.5%, casual glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L accompanied by typical hyperglycemic manifestations, or ongoing treatment with antidiabetic medications or insulin (23). Successful collateral channel (CC) tracking required retrograde guidewire passage through CCs to the distal CTO cap (24). Technical achievement was characterized by residual stenosis <30% with thrombolysis in MI (TIMI) grade 3 flow following retrograde recanalization (18). Perioperative complications encompassed in-hospital mortality, non-fatal MI, coronary perforation, tamponade, stroke, or urgent cardiac surgery.

Follow-up

Follow-up data were collected through hospital readmissions, telephone interviews with referring physicians, and clinic appointments. The primary endpoint comprised all-cause mortality. MACE served as the secondary outcome, encompassing a composite of all-cause mortality, cardiac mortality, non-fatal MI, target vessel revascularization (TVR), and stroke throughout the follow-up period. Cardiovascular death was defined as death due to cardiovascular causes, including acute MI, sudden cardiac death, heart failure, stroke, cardiovascular procedures, cardiovascular hemorrhage, and other cardiovascular etiologies (25). MI was defined according to the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI) definition (26). TVR was defined as clinically driven repeat revascularization [either repeat PCI or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG)] of the initially treated target vessel. Physicians further gathered information on MACE events via scrutiny of original medical records.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median interquartile range (IQR) as appropriate; categorical variables as counts and percentages. Distribution normality was evaluated using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For continuous data, comparisons were made with either the independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test as appropriate. Categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test. Cumulative hazard functions were estimated using the Nelson-Aalen method, and between-group differences were compared using the log-rank test. The Cox proportional hazards model was performed to identify variables associated with MACE in CTO patients with and without DM at follow-up. Given the retrospective design, the sample size was determined by the number of consecutive eligible patients during the study period. Subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, hypertension (HTN), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) were performed; interaction terms were included in Cox models to test effect modification. Analyses were performed in R 4.3.1; two-sided P<0.05 denoted statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 898 patients were initially screened for this study. After exclusion of 50 patients with missing core data, 1 patient with concomitant malignancy, 3 patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and 8 patients with end-stage renal disease, the final analytic cohort consisted of 836 patients. Follow-up [median 1,041 days (IQR, 531–1,511 days)] was completed in 767 (91.7%) patients (Figure 1). Among 836 patients undergoing retrograde CTO-PCI, 398 (47.6%) had DM. Compared to non-diabetic patients, those with DM were older and had higher heart rates (all P<0.05). The DM cohort exhibited significantly elevated white blood cell (WBC) count, triglycerides (TG), N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), troponin T (TNT), fasting blood glucose (FBG), C-reactive protein (CRP), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), fibrinogen (Fib), and triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index; whereas reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] (all P<0.05). Additionally, diabetic patients had lower LVEF but larger left ventricular end-systolic dimension (LVESD) (all P<0.05). Notably, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor (SGLT2i) use was more prevalent in the DM group (17.3% vs. 1.8%, P<0.001) (Table 1).

Procedural outcomes

The right coronary artery (RCA) represented the predominant CTO lesion location, with the left anterior descending (LAD) and left circumflex (LCX) being the next most common sites. Multi-vessel disease was more prevalent among diabetic patients (89.4% vs. 83.8%, P=0.02). The success rate of CC tracking (92.0% vs. 95.0%, P=0.08), retrograde PCI (87.4% vs. 91.3%, P=0.07), and recanalization success (89.4% vs. 92.0%, P=0.20) were similar. Notably, CC0 was more frequently used in patients with DM (26.6% vs. 19.4%, P=0.03). Overall, 148 perioperative adverse events were documented, encompassing 112 coronary injuries (13.4%), 29 instances of tamponade (3.5%), 19 patients necessitating pericardial drainage (2.3%), and 2 urgent surgical interventions (0.2%), and 5 cases (0.6%) of in-hospital death. Moreover, no significant intergroup differences were observed for the incidence of coronary injury (13.8% vs. 13.0%, P=0.73), cardiac tamponade (3.8% vs. 3.2%, P=0.65), pericardiocentesis (2.3% vs. 2.3%, P=0.98), emergency surgery (0.6% vs. 0.5%, P=0.18), and death in hospital (0.5% vs. 0.7%, P=0.73) between diabetic and non-diabetic patients (Table 2).

Follow-up outcomes

Follow-up data were obtained for 767 individuals (91.7%) over a median of 1,041 days (IQR, 531–1,511 days). MACE occurred in 126 (16.4%) patients, comprising 52 (6.2%) all-cause deaths, 36 (4.3%) cardiac deaths, 6 (0.7%) non-fatal MI, 11 (1.3%) strokes, and 62 (7.4%) TVRs. Diabetic patients exhibited a significantly higher MACE risk (19.2% vs. 13.9%, P=0.046), primarily driven by increased all-cause mortality (9.3% vs. 4.5%, P=0.007) and cardiac death (7.4% vs. 2.2%, P<0.001) (Table 3, Figure 2). No significant intergroup differences were observed in nonfatal MI, stroke, or TVR incidence (all P>0.05).

Further adjustment for history of MI, PCI, stroke, and smoking (model 2) yielded largely consistent results, with the associations for MACE (HR =1.548; 95% CI: 1.078–2.221; P=0.02), all-cause death (HR =2.151; 95% CI: 1.200–3.854; P=0.01), and cardiac death (HR =3.585; 95% CI: 1.628–7.899; P=0.002) remaining significant.

In the most extensively adjusted model (model 3), which included laboratory parameters, cardiac function, and concomitant medications, the association between the exposure and mortality outcomes persisted and strengthened. Specifically, the HRs for all-cause death and cardiac death increased to 2.346 (95% CI: 1.190–4.625; P=0.01) and 3.721 (95% CI: 1.539–8.999; P=0.004), respectively. In contrast, the association with MACE was attenuated and became statistically non-significant (HR =1.332; 95% CI: 0.880–2.017; P=0.18). No significant associations were found for stroke or TVR in any of the models. Subgroup analysis was conducted to find out the coexisting factors which may contribute to the outcomes. Our findings indicate that among patients undergoing CTO-PCI, those with DM who present with advanced age (>60 years), male sex, a history of HTN, CKD, and LVEF <40% exhibit significantly higher rates of MACE, all-cause mortality, and cardiac mortality compared to non-diabetic patients, with statistical significance (Tables S1-S5).

Discussion

As we know, this is the first study to specifically evaluate retrograde CTO-PCI outcomes in diabetic patients, with four principal observations: first, among 398 diabetic patients undergoing retrograde CTO-PCI, CC tracking was successful in 92.0% of CTO patients with diabetes, success rate of retrograde CTO-PCI achieved 87.4%, and revascularization achieved a success rate of 89.4%. These data indicate that, despite the recognized impairment of collateral formation in DM, the retrograde approach remains technically effective in this population. Second, in-hospital complications were infrequent and did not differ between diabetic and non-diabetic patients, underscoring that retrograde technique is a safe strategy for CTO patients with diabetes. Third, over a median follow-up of 1,041 days (IQR, 531–1,511 days), diabetic patients experienced significantly higher MACE rates, driven predominantly by increased mortality. Fourth, DM patients with advanced age (>60 years), male sex, a history of HTN, CKD, and LVEF <40% are subjects to worse long-term outcomes, especially in all-cause death and cardiac death after retrograde CTO-PCI. Prior studies have linked DM with collateral circulation dysplasia, yet the clinical relevance of this association during retrograde CTO-PCI has been underexplored (10-12). In a cohort of 502 patients with ≥95% coronary artery stenosis, DM independently predicted poor collateral development (10), and a subsequent study of 643 CTO patients confirmed DM as a determinant of poor CC [odds ratio (OR) =2.36; 95% CI: 1.53–3.62; P<0.001] (12). These findings raised theoretical concerns that inadequate CC might compromise retrograde PCI success or amplify procedural risk.

Prior studies suggested similar technical success and complication rates between diabetic and non-diabetic CTO-PCI patients (2,27). However, these studies were limited by small diabetic cohorts and infrequent retrograde technique utilization. Notably, our analysis also confirms comparable success rates (CC tracking, retrograde technique, revascularization) and safety profiles between diabetic and non-diabetic patients. These findings suggested that DM should not be a disincentive to attempting retrograde PCI, even though CTO patients with DM are often associated with suboptimal collateralization.

Existing data on long-term outcomes in diabetic CTO patients remain inconsistent. Some studies reported comparable mortality or MACE rates, whereas others, including ours, demonstrate significantly higher MACE and mortality risk in CTO patients with DM (27-30). Discrepancies may arise from variations in sample size, follow-up duration, and temporal improvements in techniques and adjunctive pharmacotherapy. The heightened risk observed in diabetic CTO patients likely reflects their greater comorbidity burden, evidenced in our cohort by advanced age, elevated CRP, reduced LVEF, and higher multi-vessel disease prevalence.

Successful CTO-PCI has consistently been associated with improved long-term survival in diabetic populations (31-33). Consistent with previous studies, our study showed that failure of PCI for CTO was associated with higher risk of MACE in CTO patients with DM. However, a study included 845 patients without DM and 702 patients with DM from the Samsung Medical Center CTO registry revealed that there was no significant difference between the medical therapy (MT) group and PCI group in rate of cardiac death (adjusted HR =1.09; 95% CI: 0.62–1.90; P=0.77) among the CTO patients with DM (3). Prospective, randomized studies are needed to directly compare the clinical benefits between PCI and MT among CTO patients with DM. Additionally, it is critical to interpret our findings within the context of current guidelines, which recommend CABG as the preferred revascularization strategy for diabetic patients with multivessel disease, including CTO, due to its proven survival benefit. Our study demonstrates that retrograde CTO-PCI is a technically feasible and safe option with acceptable long-term outcomes in a real-world cohort. However, these results should not be extrapolated to suggest equivalence with surgical revascularization. The choice of revascularization modality should be made by a heart team, considering patient anatomy, comorbidities, and preferences.

PCI for CTO in elderly patients remains a subject of considerable controversy. According to a large-scale multicenter retrospective study investigating within the PROGRESS-CTO registry, CTO lesions with longer duration of occlusion were associated with advanced age and DM. These older lesions demonstrated more complex anatomical characteristics (J-CTO score: 2.58±1.29 vs. 2.44±1.28 vs. 2.74±1.25, P<0.001), corresponding to lower procedural success rates (84.5% vs. 84.8% vs. 77.2%, P<0.001) and increased utilization of retrograde CTO-PCI and antegrade dissection re-entry (ADR) strategies. No significant differences in MACE were observed between the study groups (2.74% vs. 1.62% vs. 2.32%, P=0.38) (34). However, another retrospective investigation has yielded divergent conclusions, demonstrating that the risk of MACE escalated in parallel with advancing age even after adjustment for baseline risk disparities [group 2 vs. group 1: adjusted OR (aOR) =1.7; 95% CI: 1.3–2.3; P<0.001; group 3 vs. group 1: aOR =2.6; 95% CI: 1.9–3.6; P<0.001] (35). The discrepancy between the two studies is likely attributable to differences in study populations; regrettably, neither investigation evaluated long-term MACE after CTO-PCI in elderly patients-a gap that the present study specifically addresses. In the present cohort, diabetic patients aged ≥60 years undergoing retrograde CTO-PCI exhibited a markedly higher long-term risk of all-cause and, in particular, cardiac mortality, underscoring the need for intensive, multidimensional post-operative management (optimal glycaemic, lipid, heart-failure and renal-risk modulation). Among diabetic patients <60 years, survival did not differ significantly from their non-diabetic counterparts; however, the incidence of MACE remained elevated, indicating that the principal burden is ischemic rather than fatal. This subset would therefore derive greater benefit from aggressive secondary prevention strategies [antiplatelet therapy, high-intensity statins, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI)/angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) and cardiac rehabilitation].

Prior investigations have shown that women undergoing complex PCI experience a pronounced excess in 30-day MACE and cardiac mortality relative to men, yet this sex-related divergence wanes beyond 1 year. Extended follow-up exceeding 6 months has failed to demonstrate any sustained difference in all-cause survival between sexes (36,37). None of these reports, however, have explored the distinct context of diabetic patients treated with retrograde CTO-PCI. In the current study, diabetic individuals—irrespective of gender—displayed significantly higher rates of MACE and cardiac death than their non-diabetic counterparts; moreover, within the diabetic cohort alone, no statistically significant differences in MACE, all-cause mortality, or cardiac mortality were observed between men and women. These data underscore that sex should not be invoked as an independent modifier of prognosis when counselling diabetic patients undergoing retrograde CTO-PCI. Similar conclusions apply to patients with concomitant DM and HTN. In our cohort, the presence of HTN did not further amplify the already elevated risks of MACE, all-cause mortality, or cardiac mortality attributable to DM among individuals undergoing retrograde CTO-PCI.

By fueling systemic inflammation, hypercoagulability (38), homocysteinaemia (39), and endothelial dysfunction (40), CKD has been established as a potent determinant of late adverse events after CTO-PCI. The hazard is further magnified in patients on dialysis: pooled analyses have documented a five-fold increase in mortality for CKD vs. non-CKD cohorts at a median follow-up of 30 months (OR =5.01; 95% CI: 4.04–6.22; P<0.001) (41). Consistent with these data, we observed that CKD amplifies the long-term risk of MACE, all-cause death, and cardiac death among diabetic patients undergoing retrograde CTO-PCI, whereas no significant excess was identified in diabetic patients without CKD.

Whether CTO-PCI can meaningfully improve post-procedural LVEF and whether revascularization of chronically occluded vessels in patients with already reduced systolic function translates into durable clinical benefit remain contentious. A depressed LVEF is widely perceived to amplify the risk of procedural complications—including coronary perforation, acute kidney injury, and early mortality—yet contemporary series report equivalent procedural success rates and comparable fluoroscopy or total procedure times among patients with preserved (≥50%), mid-range (40–49%), or reduced (≤40%) LVEF (42). Retrospective observations have consistently documented improvements in global and regional wall motion, and modest increments in LVEF, following successful recanalization; however, these findings have not been validated in prospective, randomized trials. In the present cohort, diabetic patients with impaired systolic function (LVEF <40%) exhibited a markedly higher incidence of MACE, all-cause mortality, and cardiac mortality than their diabetic counterparts with LVEF ≥40% and, indeed, than all non-diabetic subgroups. Conversely, no significant differences in any of these endpoints were observed between diabetic patients with LVEF ≥40% and non-diabetic controls. Whether intensified guideline-directed MT for heart failure initiated immediately after CTO-PCI can attenuate this excess risk in patients with reduced LVEF warrants dedicated investigation. Because this was a retrospective cohort study including all consecutive eligible patients during the study period, no prospective sample size calculation was performed. Therefore, subgroup analyses were considered exploratory and should be interpreted with caution. Analyses were performed in R 4.3.1, and two-sided P<0.05 denoted statistical significance.

To the best of our understanding, this investigation represents the initial evaluation of both immediate technical outcomes and long-term clinical follow-up following retrograde recanalization among diabetic CTO subjects. Nevertheless, certain constraints should be acknowledged. First, procedures were performed at high-volume center by experienced operators. Success and complication rates may differ in less specialized settings, particularly for complex diabetic lesions. Second, as a single-arm study of patients selected for PCI, we could not compare outcomes against guideline-directed MT or CABG. Further studies are needed to compare the outcomes of treatment with MT, PCI, and bypass surgery in CTO patients with and without DM. Third, owing to the observational design, residual and unmeasured confounders may persist in our findings. A randomized controlled trial remains essential to conclusively address potential disparities in procedural success, adverse event rates, and clinical outcomes between diabetic and non-diabetic cohorts. Additionally, detailed data on catecholamine or vasoactive agent use were not systematically collected. Perioperative cardiogenic shock was not specifically documented in this retrospective study. Finally, as a retrospective analysis, our study lacks systematic data on patient-centered outcomes such as post-procedural angina status, quality of life, and exercise tolerance, which are primary goals of CTO revascularization. Furthermore, we did not routinely perform follow-up imaging to assess for LVEF recovery; therefore, we cannot definitively attribute the observed prognostic benefit to an improvement in myocardial function, a point that warrants investigation in future prospective studies.

In conclusion, our study confirms the technical feasibility and safety of retrograde CTO-PCI in patients with diabetes. The higher long-term mortality and MACE in this population underscore the need for careful patient selection and optimized post-procedural management. Ultimately, the clinical value of CTO-PCI is maximized when the procedure is performed for appropriately selected lesions supplying demonstrable ischemic or viable myocardium, with the goal of achieving measurable improvements in symptoms, quality of life, and, where applicable, ventricular function. Future prospective studies incorporating these patient-centered endpoints are warranted to further define the role of CTO-PCI in this high-risk population.

Conclusions

Retrograde PCI provides a safe and effective revascularization strategy for CTO patients with DM. However, despite comparable procedural outcomes, diabetic CTO patients face significantly higher long-term risks of MACE, all-cause mortality, and cardiac mortality compared to non-diabetic counterparts. Consequently, intensified long-term surveillance and risk factor management are warranted in this population. Furthermore, the heightened risk observed in diabetic CTO patients likely reflects their greater comorbidity burden, evidenced in our cohort by advanced age, elevated CRP, reduced LVEF, and higher multi-vessel disease prevalence.
