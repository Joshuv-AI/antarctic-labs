Storage stability of yolk pigmentation, lipid oxidative stability, and physicochemical quality in lutein-enriched eggs under refrigerated and ambient conditions
Authors: Yan Pan, Shan Gao, Yu Fu, Uchechukwu Edna Obianwuna, Kai Qiu, Jing Wang, Haijun Zhang, Guanghai Qi, Shugeng Wu, Jianmin Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dietary lutein enrichment is widely used to improve yolk pigmentation and nutritional value, yet its contribution to egg quality stability during storage remains insufficiently defined. This study evaluated the effects of lutein enrichment on egg interior quality, yolk pigmentation, lutein retention, cooked-yolk texture, volatile profile, fatty acid composition, and lipid oxidation during refrigerated and ambient storage. Eggs were obtained from Jingfen No. 6 laying hens fed either a basal diet or the basal diet supplemented with marigold-derived lutein to provide 120 mg/kg supplemental active lutein. Control eggs (CE) and lutein-enriched eggs (LE) were stored at 4°C or 25°C for up to 30 d. Before storage, yolk lutein content was approximately threefold higher in LE than in CE (1452.53 vs. 480.13 μg/100 g edible portion; P < 0.001), confirming effective dietary deposition. Storage temperature and duration were the dominant determinants of conventional freshness deterioration, including increased weight loss, albumen thinning, Haugh unit decline, yolk index reduction, and yolk and albumen pH elevation (P < 0.05), whereas egg type exerted only minor and inconsistent effects on these indices. In contrast, LE maintained a persistent pigmentation advantage during storage, with higher yolk color score and greater a* and b* values than CE at both temperatures (P < 0.05), although lutein content was reduced after 30 d of storage, particularly at 25°C. Cooked-yolk texture was affected mainly by storage conditions, with limited egg-type effects. Electronic nose analysis showed progressive increases in W1W and W2W sensor responses during storage, particularly at 25°C, whereas this increase was attenuated in LE from the middle storage period onward. After 30 d at 25°C, LE had a higher relative proportion of total polyunsaturated fatty acid than CE (9.165 vs. 7.046 g/100 g; P < 0.01) and showed lower malondialdehyde-equivalent values across storage conditions (P < 0.001). These findings indicate that dietary lutein enrichment does not substantially delay conventional freshness loss but improves yolk pigmentation and lipid oxidative stability, particularly under ambient storage.

Introduction

Eggs are a nutrient-dense food matrix and an effective vehicle for delivering fat-soluble bioactive compounds through dietary manipulation of laying hens. Accordingly, value-added eggs enriched with carotenoids, vitamins, or n-3 fatty acids have received increasing attention. Among these compounds, lutein is of particular interest because it contributes directly to yolk pigmentation and, together with zeaxanthin, represents a major xanthophyll carotenoid in commercial egg yolks. The lipid-rich yolk matrix also makes eggs a bioavailable dietary source of these carotenoids (Handelman et al., 1999; Chung et al., 2004; Schlatterer and Breithaupt, 2006). Because yolk color strongly influences consumer perception and market value, natural carotenoid sources, particularly marigold-derived xanthophylls, have been widely used in layer diets. Previous studies have shown that supplementation with lutein, marigold flower meal, marigold flower extract, or other plant-derived carotenoid sources increases yolk color score, CIE a* and b* values, and yolk lutein concentration, generally without major effects on laying performance or conventional fresh egg quality traits (Leeson and Caston, 2004; Lokaewmanee et al., 2011; Jang et al., 2014; Skřivan et al., 2015, 2016; Grčević et al., 2019a; Pitargue et al., 2019; Shi et al., 2023; Dansou et al., 2024).

The commercial value of lutein-enriched eggs depends not only on carotenoid deposition at oviposition, but also on the stability of this enriched phenotype during storage. Shell eggs undergo progressive physical and biochemical deterioration after laying, mainly because water and carbon dioxide are lost through the shell pores. The resulting increase in albumen pH weakens the albumen gel structure, reduces thick albumen height, and lowers Haugh unit and yolk index. These changes are governed primarily by storage temperature and duration, with room-temperature storage accelerating the decline in albumen height, Haugh unit, specific gravity, and related freshness traits, whereas refrigeration slows but does not prevent quality loss during extended storage (Scott and Silversides, 2000; Samli et al., 2005; Jones and Musgrove, 2005). More recent shelf-life modeling has further confirmed that egg weight, albumen height, Haugh unit, and albumen pH are temperature- and time-sensitive indicators of egg freshness (Quan et al., 2021). These studies establish storage conditions as major determinants of conventional egg freshness, but whether the yolk pigmentation and lipid-related quality of lutein-enriched eggs remain stable during storage is still unclear.

Yolk quality during storage is also influenced by lipid-related deterioration. Egg yolk contains a complex lipid fraction rich in triacylglycerols, phospholipids, cholesterol, and unsaturated fatty acids. During storage, particularly under ambient temperature, yolk lipids may undergo hydrolysis and oxidation, leading to changes in fatty acid composition and accumulation of oxidation products such as malondialdehyde. Wang et al. (2017) reported that physicochemical deterioration, lipolysis, and lipid oxidation in stored eggs were more pronounced at 22°C than at 4°C. These lipid-related changes may also contribute to shifts in volatile compounds associated with odor development and consumer acceptance. Volatile aldehydes, alcohols, acids, esters, and sulfur-containing compounds have been detected during egg storage, and electronic nose systems have been used to discriminate egg freshness and predict storage-related quality changes (Adamiec et al., 2002; Yimenu et al., 2017). Evidence from polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA)-enriched eggs further indicates that dietary antioxidants can improve unsaturated fatty acid retention and reduce lipid oxidation during storage or cooking (Ren et al., 2013; Shahid et al., 2022). These findings suggest that lutein enrichment may affect both pigment retention and yolk lipid stability during storage; however, whether such effects occur in intact shell eggs, especially under ambient conditions, remains unclear.

Most previous studies on lutein-enriched eggs have focused on production performance, yolk pigmentation, carotenoid deposition, or fresh egg quality. Less attention has been paid to whether the pigmentation advantage of lutein-enriched eggs persists during storage and whether lutein enrichment provides storage benefits beyond yolk color. This distinction is important because conventional freshness deterioration is driven largely by shell-mediated gas exchange and albumen structural changes, whereas lutein is deposited mainly in the yolk lipid fraction.

Therefore, the present study compared conventional eggs and lutein-enriched eggs produced from Jingfen No. 6 laying hens fed a basal diet or the basal diet supplemented with marigold-derived lutein to provide 120 mg/kg supplemental active lutein. Eggs were stored at 4°C or 25°C for up to 30 d. Interior quality, yolk and albumen pH, yolk pigmentation, lutein retention, cooked-yolk texture, volatile responses, fatty acid composition, and malondialdehyde (MDA) accumulation were evaluated. This study was designed to determine how refrigerated and ambient storage affect both conventional freshness indices and yolk-specific quality traits in lutein-enriched eggs, and to assess whether dietary lutein enrichment confers storage-related advantages in pigmentation, lipid stability, and volatile profile beyond its established role in yolk coloration.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All experimental procedures involving laying hens were approved by the management of the Animal Care and Use Committee of Institute of Feed Research, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (approval No. AEC—CAAS-20250611).

Experimental design and egg collection

Eggs used in this study were obtained from a 12-wk feeding trial in which 180 23-wk-old Jingfen No. 6 commercial laying hens (laying rate ≥ 90%) were randomly allotted to two dietary treatments, with six replicates of 15 hens per treatment. Hens were housed in three-tier battery cages measuring 40 cm in length × 40 cm in width × 35 cm in height, with three hens per cage, providing approximately 533 cm2 of cage floor area per hen. Each replicate comprised five adjacent cages. The dietary treatments consisted of a basal diet without supplemental lutein and the same basal diet supplemented with 6 kg/t of a natural marigold-derived lutein product containing 20 g/kg active lutein (batch No. 25040901; Guangzhou Leader Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China), thereby providing 120 mg/kg supplemental active lutein. According to the manufacturer’s specification, the product was prepared from yellow carotenoids extracted from marigold flowers through saponification and stabilization treatment. Separate quantitative values for zeaxanthin and other minor carotenoids were not available. The composition and nutrient levels of the basal diet are presented in Supplementary Table S1. At the end of the feeding trial, eggs with intact, visibly defect-free shells were collected and classified as control eggs (CE) or lutein-enriched eggs (LE) according to dietary treatment, and were used as the starting material for the subsequent storage trial.

Storage trial

Eggs collected from each dietary replicate were randomly allocated to storage at either 4 ± 1°C or 25 ± 1°C and, within each temperature, to the designated sampling days. The identity of the six dietary replicates within each egg type was retained throughout the storage trial. Eggs were stored with the air cell oriented upward for up to 30 d, with sampling conducted on d 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30.

At each applicable temperature and sampling day, five eggs from each dietary replicate were used for egg-quality, yolk-color, colorimetric, and pH measurements. The yolks from these same five eggs were subsequently pooled within the dietary replicate and freeze-dried for MDA, lutein, and fatty acid analyses at the designated sampling days. Each biochemical assay was performed in duplicate, and the two analytical determinations were averaged. Three additional eggs per dietary replicate were used for texture analysis, and yolks from two additional eggs per dietary replicate were pooled for electronic-nose analysis at the applicable sampling days. For all measurements, the dietary replicate was used as the experimental unit (n = 6 per egg type).

Egg quality measurements

Egg weight loss during storage was determined gravimetrically using an electronic balance (precision 0.01 g) and calculated as: weight loss (%) = [(initial egg weight − weight at sampling) / initial egg weight] × 100. Albumen height, Haugh unit, and yolk color score were determined using the Egg AnalyzerTM (ORKA Food Technology Ltd., Ramat HaSharon, Israel). For yolk index determination, eggs were broken onto a flat surface and yolk height and diameter were measured using an electronic digital caliper; yolk index was calculated as yolk height (mm) / yolk diameter (mm). Instrumental yolk color was further assessed using a colorimeter (NH300, Shenzhen 3nh Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) to obtain CIE L* (lightness), a* (redness), and b* (yellowness) values, measured directly on the surface of the intact yolk after breakout.

Yolk and albumen pH

Yolk and albumen were separated into individual beakers; the yolk membrane was punctured, and yolk and albumen were each homogenized separately using a magnetic stirrer. The pH of each fraction was measured using a calibrated pH meter (PHS-3C, Shanghai INESA Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

MDA-equivalent TBA-reactive substances

Pooled yolk samples obtained from the five eggs used for egg-quality measurements on d 0, 10, 20, and 30 were stored at −20°C and subsequently freeze-dried. TBA-reactive substances were measured using a commercial assay kit based on the thiobarbituric acid method (ADS-W-YH002-48, Jiangsu Aidi Biological Technology Co., Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, freeze-dried yolk powder was homogenized with the extraction buffer and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was mixed with the working solution and incubated at 90 to 95°C for 30 min. After cooling on ice, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 25°C, and 200 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate. Absorbance was measured at 532 and 600 nm, and the absorbance difference was calculated as ΔA = A532 − A600. No standard curve was used; values were calculated using the MDA molar extinction coefficient specified in the kit protocol and expressed as MDA equivalents (nmol/g dry yolk). Each sample was analyzed in duplicate, and the mean value was used for statistical analysis.

Yolk lutein content

Yolk lutein concentration was determined before storage and after 30 d of storage at 4°C and 25°C by high-performance liquid chromatography with diode-array detection (HPLC-DAD) according to the method of Schlatterer and Breithaupt (2006) with minor modifications. Yolk samples were homogenized and protected from light throughout extraction. Approximately 1.0 g of homogenized yolk was extracted with 10 mL of acetone containing 0.05% butylated hydroxytoluene. The mixture was vortexed for 1 min and centrifuged at 4,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The extraction was repeated until the residue was nearly colorless. The combined extracts were evaporated to dryness under nitrogen and reconstituted in 1.0 mL of mobile phase A. The resulting solution was filtered through a 0.22-μm organic-phase membrane before HPLC analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed using an Agilent 1260 Infinity II HPLC system equipped with a diode-array detector and a YMC C30 reversed-phase column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). Mobile phase A consisted of methanol/water (95:5, v/v), and mobile phase B consisted of methyl tert‑butyl ether/methanol/water (90:7:3, v/v/v). The gradient program was as follows: 0–5 min, 0% B; 5–25 min, a linear increase from 0 to 50% B; 25–35 min, a linear increase from 50 to 100% B; 35–45 min, 100% B; and 45–50 min, return to 0% B for column re-equilibration. The flow rate was 1.0 mL/min, the column temperature was maintained at 25°C, the injection volume was 20 μL, and the total run time was 50 min. Lutein was detected at 450 nm and eluted at approximately 18.5 min. Identification was based on comparison of the retention time with that of an authentic lutein standard (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; purity ≥95%). Quantification was performed using an external calibration curve over the range of 0.1–10.0 μg/mL, with a coefficient of determination greater than 0.999. Lutein content in the edible egg portion was calculated from yolk lutein concentration, yolk weight, and edible egg weight, and expressed as μg/100 g edible egg portion. Lutein retention rate after 30 d of storage was calculated separately for each dietary replicate as follows: lutein retention rate (%) = lutein content after storage / initial lutein content of the corresponding replicate × 100.

Fatty acid composition

Yolk samples collected at d 0 and 30 were stored at −20°C and freeze-dried before fatty acid analysis. Fatty acid composition was determined according to the method of Wang et al. (2000) with minor modifications. Briefly, approximately 0.5 g of freeze-dried yolk powder was subjected to direct transesterification using n-hexane and methanol–acetyl chloride. After methylation, the mixture was centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 5 min, and the upper organic phase containing fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) was collected for analysis.

FAMEs were analyzed using a gas chromatography–mass spectrometry system (GC-MS; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a capillary column suitable for FAME separation. The injection volume was 1 μL, helium was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, and the split ratio was 10:1. The oven temperature program was as follows: 120°C for 1 min, increased to 175°C at 10°C/min and held for 10 min, increased to 210°C at 3°C/min and held for 6 min, and finally increased to 230°C at 2°C/min and held for 6 min. The ion source and interface temperatures were 220°C and 280°C, respectively, with a solvent delay of 3 min and a scan range of m/z 50 to 500. Individual fatty acids were identified by comparing their retention times and mass spectra with those of authentic FAME standards. Fatty acid composition was expressed as g/100 g total identified fatty acids.

Texture profile analysis (TPA) of cooked yolk

At d 5, 10, 20, and 30, three additional eggs from each dietary replicate were hard-boiled for 15 min, cooled to room temperature, and the intact yolk was separated and used for texture analysis using a texture analyzer (TMS-PRO, Food Technology Corporation, Sterling, VA, USA) fitted with a P/25 N cylindrical probe. Samples were subjected to a 2-cycle compression test at 40% compression depth, with a pre-test speed, test speed, and post-test speed of 0.5 mm/s and a trigger force of 0.05 N. Hardness, cohesiveness, springiness, gumminess, and chewiness were derived from the resulting force-time curves. The measurements from the three eggs were averaged to generate one replicate-level value.

Electronic nose analysis of cooked yolk

Cooked yolk volatile profiles were assessed using an electronic nose (PEN3, Airsense Analytics GmbH, Schwerin, Germany) equipped with 10 metal oxide semiconductor sensors, the sensitivity characteristics and reference volatiles of which are listed in Supplementary Table S2. Eggs were hard-boiled for 15 min, cooled to room temperature, and the yolks were separated and homogenized; yolks from 2 eggs were pooled to constitute one sample. Approximately 6 g of homogenized sample was placed into a 15-mL headspace vial, sealed, and equilibrated at room temperature for 30 min prior to measurement, with 2 analytical replicates performed per sample. Instrument parameters were set as follows: flush time, 180 s; zero-point trim time, 10 s; sample preparation time, 5 s; measurement time, 150 s; carrier gas flow rate, 300 mL/min; and injection flow rate, 300 mL/min. Sensor response values at 140 s, when readings had stabilized, were extracted for subsequent analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The replicate served as the experimental unit, and results are presented as mean ± SEM. Egg interior quality traits, yolk color score, colorimetric values, pH, and textural parameters were analyzed by 3-way ANOVA with egg type (ET), storage temperature (T), and storage duration (D) as fixed factors, including all main effects and 2- and 3-way interactions. Complete results of the 3-way ANOVA are provided in Supplementary Table S4. For presentation in the main text, Table 2, Table 3 report the effects of ET, T, and ET×T evaluated separately at each storage day, whereas Table 4 presents the overall ET and ET×T effects for textural parameters across the storage period. For Haugh unit, albumen pH, yolk color score, and colorimetric values (L*, a*, and b*), ET and ET × D effects were further evaluated across all storage conditions (All) and separately within each storage temperature, as reported in Fig. 1. Results and discussion

Initial egg lutein content

Yolk lutein content was approximately threefold higher in LE eggs than in CE eggs before storage (P < 0.001; Table 1), confirming effective deposition of dietary lutein into the yolk. This response agrees with previous studies showing efficient transfer of dietary lutein or marigold-derived xanthophylls into egg yolk, resulting in higher yolk lutein concentration and enhanced pigmentation (Jang et al., 2014; Skřivan et al., 2016; Wen et al., 2021; Dansou et al., 2024). Egg interior quality during storage

Weight loss rate, yolk index, albumen height, and Haugh unit were all strongly affected by storage duration and temperature, with eggs stored at 25°C exhibiting markedly greater weight loss and faster declines in albumen height and Haugh unit than those stored at 4°C (P < 0.001 for the temperature effect in nearly all cases; Table 2). These changes are consistent with the well-established deterioration process of stored eggs, whereby the progressive loss of water and carbon dioxide through shell pores increases albumen pH and promotes thinning of the thick albumen, resulting in reduced albumen height and Haugh unit (Nasri et al., 2020). Similar temperature-dependent deterioration has also been described by predictive models of egg freshness and shelf life, in which egg weight, albumen height, Haugh unit, and albumen pH were identified as storage-sensitive indicators (Quan et al., 2021). In contrast to these strong temperature- and time-driven effects, the influence of egg type on interior quality parameters was comparatively limited and inconsistent across storage days. Lutein-enriched eggs exhibited a lower weight loss rate than CE eggs at 4°C on d 5 and 10 (P < 0.05), a higher yolk index at 25°C on d 10 and 25 (P < 0.05), and a higher Haugh unit at 25°C on d 10 (P < 0.05). However, no egg-type differences were detected for albumen height, yolk pH, or albumen pH at any storage day or temperature, and no overall egg type × temperature interaction was observed for these parameters across the storage period (P > 0.05; Supplementary Table S4).

This overall pattern suggests that lutein enrichment exerted only minor effects on the physical structural attributes of egg interior quality. Similar observations have been reported in lutein- or marigold-supplemented eggs, in which dietary carotenoid enrichment improved yolk pigmentation but had limited effects on albumen height, Haugh unit, or other conventional freshness indicators during storage (Chen et al., 2025; Grčević et al., 2019b). Likewise, the absence of consistent effects on yolk and albumen pH indicates that the pH changes observed during storage were primarily driven by physicochemical processes associated with CO2 loss rather than by yolk lutein concentration. Collectively, these results indicate that deterioration of internal egg quality was governed predominantly by storage temperature and duration, whereas lutein enrichment had little influence on the major pathways underlying egg freshness decline.

Yolk color and lutein retention during storage

Yolk color score was approximately 2-fold higher in LE than CE eggs throughout the 30-d storage period at both temperatures (P < 0.001 at each storage day; Table 3). The overall egg-type effect was also significant across the storage period (Fig. 1C; Supplementary Table S4). Thus, the pigmentation advantage conferred by dietary lutein remained stable and was not compromised by storage duration or temperature. This pattern was corroborated by instrumental colorimetric measurements. Yolk a* value was markedly higher in LE than CE eggs at all storage days and both temperatures (P < 0.001; Table 3), and, notably, this egg-type effect was further modulated by storage temperature when assessed across the entire storage period (ET×T, P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S4), with the simple effects analysis in Fig. 1E confirming that the egg-type effect on a* value was significant within both the 4°C and 25°C conditions, while being numerically larger under 25°C storage (e.g., on d 30, the CE–LE difference was 6.55 units at 25°C versus 5.97 units at 4°C). Yolk b* value followed a similar pattern, being consistently higher in LE than CE eggs at all storage days (P < 0.001 to P < 0.01; Table 3), with the simple effects analysis in Fig. 1F likewise confirming a significant egg-type effect within each temperature condition. These results are consistent with the role of lutein as a predominant xanthophyll pigment in egg yolk, where its deposition directly contributes to increased redness and yellowness, as reported in both fresh and processed eggs from hens fed lutein or plant carotenoid sources (Schlatterer and Breithaupt, 2006; Skřivan et al., 2015; Wen et al., 2021; Kljak et al., 2021).

Interestingly, the behavior of yolk L* differed from that of a* and b* (Fig. 1D). Yolk L* value was lower in LE than CE eggs during the early-to-mid storage period (d 5–15; P < 0.05 to P < 0.01), indicating a darker yolk appearance in lutein-enriched eggs. However, this difference gradually diminished and was no longer significant after d 20. Because a* and b* values remained consistently elevated in LE eggs throughout storage, the convergence in L* is unlikely to reflect a disproportionate loss of carotenoid pigments. A more plausible explanation is that storage-induced changes in vitelline membrane integrity and yolk physical properties alter water redistribution and light-scattering behavior independently of pigment concentration. Extended storage has been shown to reduce vitelline membrane elasticity, and high-temperature storage induces proteomic alterations in vitelline membrane proteins related to structure and mechanical properties (Jones and Musgrove, 2005; Zhou et al., 2020). Therefore, yolk lightness appears to be more sensitive to physical changes associated with storage, whereas a* and b* more directly reflect carotenoid-derived pigmentation.

The colorimetric results should also be considered together with the changes in yolk lutein concentration during storage (Fig. 2). As expected, lutein content remained substantially higher in LE than CE eggs after 30 d of storage at both temperatures. Nevertheless, lutein concentrations were reduced after 30 d, particularly at 25°C. Consistently, storage temperature significantly affected lutein retention rate (P = 0.009), whereas the effects of egg type and the ET × T interaction were not significant (P = 0.218 and P = 0.231, respectively), indicating that proportional lutein loss was mainly temperature dependent. This decline is consistent with the known susceptibility of natural yolk xanthophylls to storage-related degradation. Wenzel et al. (2011) reported substantial losses of all-E-lutein and all-E-zeaxanthin during prolonged storage of freeze-dried egg yolk, although that processed yolk system differs from the intact shell eggs used in the present study. Despite this reduction, the visual and instrumental color advantages of LE eggs were largely maintained. This observation suggests that moderate losses of yolk lutein do not necessarily translate into proportional changes in yolk color. Similar findings have been reported previously, where reductions in yolk carotenoid concentration during storage were accompanied by relatively minor changes in color score because pigment concentrations remained above the threshold required to sustain visual pigmentation (Grčević et al., 2019b; Kljak et al., 2021). Taken together, these results demonstrate that dietary lutein supplementation not only enhanced initial yolk pigmentation but also maintained this advantage throughout storage. Although some degradation of lutein occurred, particularly under ambient conditions, the remaining lutein concentration was sufficient to preserve the characteristic redness and yellowness of LE yolks, indicating good color stability of lutein-enriched eggs during commercial storage.

Textural profile analysis (TPA) of cooked yolk

Egg type exerted a significant main effect on yolk hardness and chewiness across the storage period (P = 0.011 and P = 0.008, respectively; Table 4), whereas cohesiveness, springiness, and gumminess did not differ between CE and LE eggs (P > 0.05). No significant ET × T interaction was detected for any texture parameter. The textural properties of cooked yolk are determined primarily by heat-induced structural transitions of yolk lipoproteins rather than by a single nutrient component. Egg yolk is a complex lipoprotein-rich system composed mainly of plasma low-density lipoproteins (LDL), granule high-density lipoproteins (HDL), phosvitin, and livetins, and its gelation behavior depends on protein denaturation, lipoprotein aggregation, lipid-protein interactions, and water redistribution during heating (Anton, 2013; Yuan et al., 2025). Electronic nose analysis of cooked yolk

The radar charts (Fig. 3A–D) provided an overview of sensor response profiles across all 10 metal oxide semiconductor sensors at each storage day. Most sensor responses remained broadly similar among the four groups (CE-4°C, LE-4°C, CE-25°C, and LE-25°C) throughout storage. Some variation was also observed in the responses of W5S and W6S at individual storage days; however, these patterns were less consistent across the storage period. In contrast, W1W and W2W showed the clearest and most persistent storage-related and egg-type-related separation, particularly at 25°C, and were therefore selected for further analysis (Fig. 3E, F). Both W1W and W2W responses increased progressively during storage, with substantially greater increases at 25°C than at 4°C. These changes indicate shifts in the volatile-response profile of cooked yolk and are consistent with previous studies showing that egg volatile profiles are affected by storage duration and temperature (Adamiec et al., 2002; Yimenu et al., 2017). Elevated storage temperatures accelerate physicochemical deterioration and promote the formation of volatile compounds associated with flavor changes in eggs (Wang et al., 2017).

Notably, differences between CE and LE eggs were relatively small under refrigerated storage but became increasingly evident under 25°C conditions, where LE eggs consistently exhibited lower W1W and W2W responses during the middle and late stages of storage. This pattern suggests that lutein enrichment had little influence on volatile profiles under low oxidative stress but became increasingly effective in preserving flavor stability as storage conditions became more challenging. Sulfur-containing volatiles are among the major contributors to characteristic egg aroma and off-flavor development, and their accumulation is closely associated with storage-related deterioration processes (Li et al., 2025). Overall, these results indicate that lutein enrichment was associated with lower responses of these two sensors under ambient storage, providing complementary evidence of differences in the volatile-response profiles of CE and LE eggs.

Fatty acid composition and lipid oxidative stability

Before storage, the relative fatty acid composition was generally comparable between CE and LE eggs (Supplementary Table S3). After 30 d of storage, the overall content of saturated fatty acids (ΣSFA) and monounsaturated fatty acids (ΣMUFA) did not differ between CE and LE eggs at either temperature (P > 0.05; Table 5), whereas storage temperature exerted a significant main effect on several individual SFA and MUFA species, including C15:0, C16:1, and C18:1n9c (P < 0.05), and on ΣMUFA overall (P = 0.002). These changes were mainly temperature-dependent and were not accompanied by a significant ET×T interaction, indicating that lutein enrichment had limited influence on the SFA and MUFA fractions after storage. Such temperature-related shifts in yolk lipid composition may reflect the combined effects of lipid hydrolysis and oxidation during egg storage. Lutein is incorporated into a lipid-rich yolk matrix and its deposition can be affected by dietary lipid characteristics. For example, dietary fat source has been shown to influence yolk lutein, zeaxanthin, and total carotenoid contents, and marigold extract supplementation has been reported to increase yolk lutein and In contrast, the relative PUFA composition showed a more distinct egg-type response. At 25°C, LE eggs had a higher relative ΣPUFA proportion than CE eggs (9.165 vs. 7.046 g/100 g; P < 0.01), whereas this difference was not significant at 4°C. Similar patterns were observed for linoleic acid (C18:2n6c) and α-linolenic acid (C18:3n3), both of which accounted for higher relative proportions in LE than CE eggs after 30 d at 25°C (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01, respectively). Notably, the relative ΣPUFA proportion in CE eggs was lower at 25°C than at 4°C, whereas that in LE eggs was comparable between the two temperatures. This pattern suggests that lutein enrichment did not simply increase yolk PUFA content uniformly, but rather helped preserve PUFA under the more oxidatively challenging ambient storage condition. Because PUFA contain multiple double bonds, they are particularly susceptible to peroxidation during storage, and PUFA-enriched eggs generally require sufficient antioxidant protection to maintain lipid stability (Fraeye et al., 2012; Ren et al., 2013; Liang et al., 2020). Recent evidence further showed that lutein supplementation in n-3-enriched eggs reduced yolk MDA accumulation and helped maintain α-linolenic acid during storage (Shahid et al., 2022). Therefore, the higher PUFA retention in LE eggs at 25°C provides compositional evidence that lutein improved yolk lipid stability under elevated oxidative pressure.

The changes in MDA-equivalent TBA-reactive products (Fig. 4) should be considered together with the concurrent loss of yolk lutein. MDA-equivalent values increased progressively with storage duration under both temperature conditions, with LE eggs maintaining lower values than CE eggs across the storage period (P < 0.001 for the main effect of egg type). In parallel, yolk lutein concentration declined after 30 d, particularly at 25°C, whereas LE eggs retained substantially higher lutein concentrations than CE eggs. Thus, the storage condition associated with greater lutein loss was also associated with greater accumulation of lipid oxidation products, while the higher residual lutein concentration in LE eggs coincided with lower MDA-equivalent values. Dietary supplementation with lutein, marigold-derived carotenoids, or other carotenoid-rich sources has similarly been reported to reduce yolk lipid oxidation during storage (Skřivan et al., 2016; An et al., 2019; Grčević et al., 2019b; Kljak et al., 2021; Shahid et al., 2022). This relationship is biologically plausible because the conjugated polyene chain of lutein enables it to quench reactive species and suppress free-radical propagation, thereby limiting the oxidation of unsaturated lipids and the subsequent formation of secondary TBA-reactive products (Krinsky and Johnson, 2005; Fiedor and Burda, 2014). Reaction with peroxyl radicals may, in turn, oxidize or cleave the lutein molecule, contributing to the loss of measurable intact lutein during storage (Lakshminarayana et al., 2013). The concurrent decline in lutein concentration and increase in MDA-equivalent values may therefore reflect increasing oxidative pressure together with partial utilization of lutein within the yolk lipid matrix. The higher residual lutein concentration and lower MDA-equivalent values in LE eggs support a functional association between lutein retention and yolk oxidative stability, although lipid oxidation is also influenced by fatty acid composition and other endogenous antioxidant components.

These biochemical changes also provide a basis for the electronic nose results. Lipid oxidation products, including aldehydes, ketones, and other reactive carbonyl compounds, are important precursors or contributors to volatile compounds formed during egg storage and heating. Adamiec et al. (2002) showed that egg volatile profiles change during storage, and recent multi-omics evidence further indicates that fatty acids, amino acids, and their degradation products contribute substantially to characteristic volatile profiles in poultry eggs (Li et al., 2025). In the present study, the lower W1W and W2W responses in LE eggs under 25°C storage paralleled the lower MDA accumulation and better PUFA preservation under the same condition. The parallel changes in relative fatty acid composition, TBA-reactive lipid oxidation products, and electronic-nose responses were consistent with improved yolk oxidative stability in LE eggs.

Taken together, these results indicate that the main benefit of dietary lutein during storage was not the preservation of conventional freshness traits, but the maintenance of yolk pigmentation and oxidative stability. Storage at 25°C promoted PUFA loss, MDA accumulation, and sulfur-related volatile signals in CE eggs, whereas LE eggs showed better PUFA retention, lower lipid oxidation, and weaker off-odor-related sensor responses. This integrated response suggests that lutein-enriched eggs may have improved storage stability through a yolk-centered antioxidant mechanism, in which deposited lutein protects unsaturated lipids from peroxidation and consequently limits the formation of lipid oxidation products and related volatile compounds.

Conclusion

In conclusion, dietary supplementation with marigold-derived lutein to provide 120 mg/kg active lutein effectively increased yolk lutein deposition and produced a persistent pigmentation advantage during 30 d of storage. Lutein enrichment had limited effects on conventional interior quality traits, which were governed mainly by storage temperature and duration. However, under ambient storage conditions, lutein-enriched eggs showed a higher relative PUFA proportion, lower MDA accumulation, and weaker sulfur-related volatile signals, indicating improved yolk lipid and flavor stability. These findings indicate that the major storage benefit of lutein-enriched eggs lies in preserving yolk pigmentation and oxidative stability rather than delaying general freshness deterioration.
