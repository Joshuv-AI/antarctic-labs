Ultrasensitive Visual Detection of Chitin Degradation via Surface Pitting on a Nanofibrous N‑Acetylchitosan Hydrogel
Authors: Mikiko Tsudome, Mikako Tachioka, Atsushi Baba, Masayuki Miyazaki, Akihiko Nakamura, Kazunori Nagaki, Nemuri Todaka, Yasunori Kurosawa, Shigeru Deguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microbial enzymes play a central role in biomass valorization, and their discovery and characterization are increasingly driven by data-driven molecular approaches. However, for enzymes acting on water-insoluble polymeric substrates, enzymatic reactions are inherently confined to solid–liquid interfaces, and interfacial properties critically govern reaction efficiency and detectability. Here, we report an interfacial sensing strategy that enables ultrasensitive visual detection of microbial chitinolytic activity by exploiting a nanofibrous N-acetylchitosan hydrogel. The hydrogel consists of an entangled nanofiber network that provides a markedly increased surface area, thereby facilitating interfacial enzymatic reactions. When chitinolytic microorganisms are grown on the hydrogel surface, extracellular chitinases locally disrupt the nanofibrous network, producing nanoscale depressions that evolve into optically visible pits upon incubation. This approach enables visual detection of chitin degradation at the nanogram scale, with profilometric quantification down to ∼1 ng and naked-eye recognition at several tens of nanograms. By explicitly linking interfacial structure, surface morphology evolution, and enzymatic reaction kinetics, this work introduces a materials-based perspective that complements data-driven molecular approaches for the discovery and evaluation of microbial enzymes acting on insoluble polymeric substrates.

Introduction

Microorganisms are ubiquitous on Earth. A single gram of soil typically contains up to 1010 bacterial cells, while nominal cell counts exceeding 105 cells are found in a single milliliter of surface seawater. Harnessing the biotechnological potential of these microorganisms is crucial for developing biobased solutions to sustainability challenges.

Culturing on nutrient-containing hydrogels, commonly known as solidified media, is a standard method for studying microorganisms in environmental samples. The most widely used hydrogel matrix is agar, a linear, nonionic polysaccharide derived from seaweed, composed of d-galactose and 3,6-anhydro-l-galactose. Agar is chosen for its microbiological inertness and resistance to biodegradation by most bacterial species. When an environmental sample is spread and incubated on the surface of a solidified medium, microbial cells absorb the nutrients and replicate, eventually forming macroscopic dome-shaped colonies on the gel surface. The interaction between the gel surface and the cell surface restricts the diffusion and spontaneous movement of the microbial cells during growth. Thus, when the environmental sample is sufficiently diluted, each microbial cell forms an isolated colony, facilitating easy separation to obtain pure cultures. In functional screening, only colonies of microorganisms with the desired function are selected from numerous colonies (typically several hundred) on the gel surface. To ensure an efficient selection process, the ability to visually detect the target microbial function is highly desirable.

This limitation becomes particularly critical when the microbial function of interest involve enzymatic reactions acting on water-insoluble polymeric substrates. Enzymatic degradation of water-insoluble polymeric substrates such as cellulose and chitin is a central process in biomass conversion, as enzymes such as cellulases play a key role in transforming renewable biomass feedstocks into fuels, chemicals, and materials. − However, degradation of such substrates is inherently restricted to reactions occurring at the solid–liquid interface. Because enzymes can access only the substrate surface, the overall reaction rate is strongly governed by the available surface area, as well as by materials properties such as crystallinity. − As a result, enzymatic hydrolysis of these recalcitrant polymers is intrinsically slow, and weak enzymatic activities are often difficult to evaluate using conventional assay formats based on bulk materials.

Recent enzyme discovery efforts are increasingly driven by sequence-based metagenomics, large-scale genomic databases, and AI-assisted structure prediction. − However, this interfacial rate limitation represents a fundamental challenge in studying and exploiting microbial enzymes that act on water-insoluble polymeric substrates. In such systems, substrate morphology and accessible surface area critically influence reaction kinetics. Therefore, in addition to molecular-level approaches, materials-based strategies that modulate interfacial accessibility offer a complementary route for detecting and evaluating enzymatic activity. Increasing the accessible surface area of the substrate therefore offers a direct, materials-based strategy to accelerate enzymatic reactions and to render otherwise undetectable enzymatic activities observable.

By exploiting a nanofibrous architecture, ultrasensitive visual detection of microbial cellulose degradation has been demonstrated using hydrogels composed of entangled crystalline nanofibers. , Noncellulolytic microorganisms form colonies when cultured on nutrient-containing cellulose gel, as cellulose is microbiologically inert to them, much like agar is to most microorganisms. − However, when cellulolytic microorganisms are cultured, cellulases released extracellularly by the microorganisms hydrolyze the cellulose nanofibers. This disrupts the three-dimensional nanofiber network, resulting in a visible pit on the gel surface. The pit size increases with incubation time, eventually becoming visible to the naked eye. Thus, pit formation serves as a visible indicator for isolating cellulolytic microorganisms in environmental samples. Functional screening using this method, termed surface-pitting observation technology (SPOT), has yielded novel cellulolytic microorganisms with unique cellulase properties from the deep sea, demonstrating the utility of the assay. ,,

Unlike conventional enzyme assay that quantifies soluble hydrolysis products released into the bulk solution, SPOT enables ultrasensitive assay by quantifying the loss in substrate volume upon enzymatic hydrolysis using nanofibrous substrates. SPOT-based visual sensing holds potential for application in the microbial degradation of other water-insoluble polymeric substrates, such as chitin. , Chitin is a linear, nonionic polysaccharide composed of N-acetylglucosamine units linked by β-1,4 bonds. Its hydrolysis products, particularly chitooligosaccharides, exhibit diverse biological activities, including antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antitumor effects. , Enzymatic hydrolysis is considered the most effective method for producing chitooligosaccharides. As our understanding of chitin-degrading enzymes still requires significant advancement, , the discovery of new enzymes from environmental microorganisms and the investigation of their properties are essential for unlocking the full potential of these enzymes in utilizing this recalcitrant biomass.

Building on the materials strategy established for cellulose, we extend the surface-pitting principle to chitin by employing hydrogels composed of entangled nanofibrous N-acetylchitosan as a solid substrate. This approach enables ultrasensitive visual detection of microbial chitin degradation at the nanogram scale through pit formation on the gel surface.

Experimental Section

Materials

Chitosan (product name: Chitosan 10) was purchased from Wako Pure Chemical Corporation. Its viscosity-average molecular weight (M v) has been reported to be 2.1 × 105, corresponding to an estimated degree of polymerization on the order of 103. Chitin from crab shell was obtained from Nacalai Tesque (Kyoto, Japan). Acetic anhydride was obtained from Aldrich. Bacterial Chitinase from Corynebacterium sp. OZ-21 (Yatalase) was obtained from Takara Bio Inc. (Otsu, Japan). Artificial seawater (Marine Art SF-1) was obtained from Senju Pharmaceutical (Osaka, Japan). Other chemicals were commercially available and were used as received.

Colloidal chitin, an aqueous dispersion of amorphous chitin, was prepared from crab-shell chitin according to a standard procedure. Briefly, chitin was dispersed in concentrated hydrochloric acid at 4 °C and the mixture was stirred at 4 °C overnight. To the mixture, cold ethanol was added, and a precipitate was collected by spinning the mixture at 8,500 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min. The precipitate was separated and washed repeatedly with water until the conductivity of the supernatant became 5 μS/m.

Preparation of N-Acetylchitosan Hydrogels

The hydrogel was prepared by N-acetylation of chitosan in a mixture of water, acetic acid, and methanol. In a typical procedure, chitosan (1.5 g) was added to 54 mL of water and stirred for 1 h to dissolve, then 6 mL of acetic acid was added and stirred for another hour. Next, 240 mL of methanol was added, and the mixed solution was stirred overnight. To the resulting solution, 1.8 mL of acetic anhydride was added and mixed quickly, then 60 mL aliquots were dispensed into glass Petri dishes (inner diameter: 154 mm). Since the gelation started immediately after adding acetic anhydride, this dispensing process had to be done quickly. The solution was allowed to stand overnight at room temperature for the N-acetylation reaction to proceed. The entire solution solidified and formed a highly transparent gel. The transparent gel was removed from the dish and washed with copious water until the conductivity of the wash water was less than 5 μS cm–1.

In this study, glass Petri dishes were used as molds to prepare hydrogels in the form of disks with a diameter of approximately 9 cm, comparable to the typical size of agar media used for microbial culture. The lid and body of the Petri dish were sealed with adhesive tape to prevent the evaporation of methanol during the reaction. The inner surface of the body of the Petri dish was made hydrophobic by treating it with a 2% solution of dimethyldichlorosilane in octamethylcyclooctasilane (PlusOne Repel-Silane ES, GE Healthcare, Chalfont St. Giles, UK) according to the protocol supplied by the manufacturer. The surface was freshly coated each time.

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)

In-liquid AFM was employed to examine the surface morphology of as-prepared N-acetylchitosan hydrogels. A hydrogel sample was placed in a stainless-steel dish, which was then mounted on the microscope stage. A droplet of distilled water was added onto the gel surface, and in-liquid imaging was carried out by immersing the cantilever into the water droplet. Imaging was performed in the quantitative imaging (QI) mode at room temperature using a silicon nitride cantilever (PeakForce-HIRS-F-A; spring constant ≈ 0.35 N m–1, Bruker Nano GmbH, Berlin, Germany) on a JPK NanoWizard 4XP system (Bruker). The images were acquired with a scan size of 2.5 μm × 2.5 μm, a scan rate of 0.13–0.2 Hz, and a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels. Imaging was conducted in an aqueous environment on as-prepared hydrogels with a Z-length of 0.1 μm, a set point force of 200 pN, and a total acquisition time of approximately 13 min. Images were processed only for line leveling, median filtering, and low-pass filtering using the JPK software.

Cryo-Scanning Electron Microscopy (Cryo-SEM) and Environmental Scanning Electron Microscopy (ESEM)

Nanostructures of N-acetylchitosan hydrogels were examined by cryo-SEM and environmental-SEM (ESEM). The hydrogel was prepared using the same procedure described above, but with a chitosan concentration of 5% in an acetic acid/methanol solution. Liquefied ethane was prepared by condensing gaseous ethane gas (99.5% purity, GL Sciences, Tokyo, Japan) in an aluminum cup that was cooled with liquid nitrogen. Bovine serum albumin (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) was dissolved in distilled water at a concentration of approximately 5% and was used as a cryoprotectant. The hydrogel was fixed to the sample stage by applying a coating of the bovine albumin solution, and flash-frozen by plunging it into liquid ethane. A few μm from the top surface of the frozen sample was fractured using the knife equipped in the cryo unit (MP-Z08181T, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) and then inserted into the field-emission (FE)-SEM apparatus (JSM-7100F, JEOL). The sample stage temperature was maintained at −80 °C, and secondary electron images of the fractured hydrogel surface were obtained at an accelerating voltage of 1.0 kV. No conductive coating was applied to the sample.

The hydrogel for ESEM observation was prepared with a chitosan concentration of 2% in an acetic acid/methanol solution. The hydrogel was mounted on a sample stage and frozen in liquid nitrogen. ESEM imaging was conducted on a Quanta 3D field-emission gun (FEG) (FEI Company, Hillsboro, OR, USA) at 5 kV. The sample stage temperature was maintained at −20.0 °C. The pressure inside the observation chamber was kept at 200 Pa.

Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) Surface Area

Hydrogels of N-acetylchitosan were subjected to solvent exchange with tert-butyl alcohol, followed by lyophilization. The sample was further treated in a vacuum at 120 °C overnight to remove all the adsorbed species. Nitrogen adsorption measurements were performed at 77 K on a Tristar II 3020 (Micromeritics Instrument, Norcross, GA, USA). The BET analysis was performed for relative vapor pressure between 0.05 and 0.35.

Enzymatic Hydrolysis

Hydrogel of N-acetylchitosan was crushed into fine particles and sieved (nominal aperture, 500 μm). The fine particles were dispersed at a concentration of 0.05 wt % in 20 mM acetate buffer (pH 6.0), and enzymatic hydrolysis was initiated by adding Yatalase at a concentration of 0.8% at 37 °C. An aliquot was withdrawn at appropriate intervals and boiled for 5 min to denature the enzyme to terminate the reaction. Unreacted N-acetylchitosan was separated by spinning the sample at 15,000 rpm and 4 °C for 5 min. The supernatant was carefully separated and the concentration of N-acetylglucosamine was determined by 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) assay. The concentration was determined from the absorbance at 535 nm according to a calibration curve established by using solutions containing known amounts of N-acetylglucosamine. Separate experiments were also performed using as-received chitin and colloidal chitin as substrates.

Preparation of Solidified Media Using Hydrogels of N-Acetylchitosan

Freshly prepared N-acetylchitosan hydrogels contain pure water. To use the hydrogel as a solid support for microbial culture, the water was replaced with a suitable nutrient fluid according to the previously reported method for the nanofibrous cellulose hydrogel. Briefly, an equal volume of double-strength medium was placed on top of the hydrogel, which was sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 20 min. The nutrient components were allowed to diffuse into the hydrogel by gentle agitation on a shaker. Upon completion of the solvent exchange (4 h, Figure S1), the supernatant was discarded and the hydrogels were stored in a refrigerator until use.

Microbial Culture

Gram-negative Escherichia coli W3110 and Gram-positive Bacillus subtilis 168 were preincubated in a Luria–Bertani (LB) broth at 37 °C. Eukaryotic Saccharomyces cerevisiae YPH499 was preincubated in a Yeast Peptone Dextrose (YPD) broth at 30 °C. The culture was diluted with a physiological saline. The diluted culture was inoculated on N-acetylchitosan hydrogel plates containing the same medium and incubated at 37 °C (E. coli and B. subtilis) or 30 °C (S. cerevisiae) for 2 days, after which the number of colonies formed on the plates was enumerated. Control experiments were done by using conventional agar plates containing the same media.

A known marine chitinolytic microorganism, Saccharophagus degradans 2-40T (American type culture collection (ATCC) 43961), was preincubated in artificial seawater supplemented with (NH4)2SO4 at 30 °C. The culture was diluted with artificial seawater. The diluted culture was inoculated on N-acetylchitosan hydrogel plates containing the same medium and incubated at 30 °C. Images of the plates were obtained by scanning the plates with a flat-bed scanner (GT-F600, Seiko Epson Corporation, Suwa, Japan).

Optical Profilometry

Pits on the surface of N-acetylchitosan hydrogels were quantified using a confocal laser scanning microscope, VK-9700 Generation II (Keyence, Osaka, Japan). The imaging was performed with a field of view of 1,024 × 768 pixels using a 150× objective (N.A. 0.95, field of view: 93.2 μm × 69.9 μm) as follows. The surface was laterally scanned using a laser (λ = 408 nm), and the intensity of reflected light was collected. Through repeated scanning in the depth direction, a collection of images with corresponding focal positions was obtained, enabling the reconstruction of a 3D height map of the surface. Detailed description of the measurement can be found elsewhere. ,

Merge and noise canceling of profilometric images were processed by software included with the VK-9700 Generation II. The pit volume determination and visualization were performed by MountainsSPIP (Digital Surf, Besançon, France). Heights from 0 to −0.5 μm from the surface were excluded for the volume calculation to minimize roughness artifacts.

Results and Discussion

Preparation of N-Acetylchitosan Hydrogel

SPOT requires a hydrogel composed of entangled nanofibers as a substrate in order to amplify interfacial enzymatic degradation into detectable topographical changes. , However, native chitin cannot be directly processed into such a hydrogel under mild conditions owing to its highly crystalline and insoluble nature. Thus, we employed chitosan as a processable precursor and induced in situ N-acetylation to obtain a nanofibrous substrate for SPOT-based Chitinase assay.

The hydrogel was prepared following the method reported by Hirano et al. The procedure involved the N-acetylation of chitosan in a mixture of acetic acid and methanol. As the reaction progressed, N-acetylchitosan chains formed an entangled nanofiber network, and the entire solution solidified to form a highly transparent gel (Figure ).

Schematic illustration of the formation of a nanofibrous N-acetylchitosan hydrogel via N-acetylation.

The gel formation was accompanied by a substantial reduction in volume; the volume of the resulting hydrogel was approximately 35% of that of the initial reaction mixture. To prevent tearing during shrinkage, the glass surface of the Petri dish was hydrophobically coated prior to use. Without this coating, the gel adhered to the glass surface and was easily damaged as it contracted during gelation. With the coating, the gel slid freely on the glass, effectively preventing tearing.

The synthesis was highly reproducible. In an experiment where 32 gels were prepared using Petri dishes with an inner diameter of 15.4 cm, the final disk-shaped gels had a diameter of 8.25 ± 0.12 cm and a typical thickness of approximately 1.7 mm.

Nanofibrous Structure of N-Acetylchitosan Hydrogel

As reported previously, the N-acetylchitosan hydrogel exhibits a compressive modulus ranging from 40 to 630 kPa, depending on the initial chitosan content. These values demonstrate that the hydrogel possesses sufficient mechanical integrity to withstand routine handling and microbial inoculation. Wide angle X-ray diffraction analysis demonstrated that N-acetylchitosan exhibits diffraction peaks characteristic of α-chitin, the same crystalline form as native chitin.

The nanoscale morphology of the N-acetylchitosan hydrogel was examined by in-liquid AFM, cryo-SEM, and ESEM. In-liquid AFM imaging revealed surface morphologies characteristic of soft, porous polysaccharide hydrogels (Figure a). Cryo-SEM observations confirmed the presence of a three-dimensional porous network (Figure b) composed of intertwined nanofibers, tens of nanometers in diameter (Figure c). Similar nanofibrous network structures with comparable dimensions have been observed in lyophilized hydrogels of agar and cellulose. The nanofibrous structure was also confirmed by the BET surface area (540 m2 g–1). The value is significantly higher than that previously reported for nanofibrous cellulose hydrogels (∼200 m2 g–1), , suggesting that N-acetylchitosan forms finer nanofibers than cellulose.

Structure of the N-acetylchitosan hydrogel. (a) In-liquid AFM image and (b, c) cryo-SEM images showing the fine structure of the N-acetylchitosan hydrogel. Porous, three-dimensional networks composed of nanofibers several tens of nanometers in diameter are clearly visible. The scale bars represent 1 μm and 100 nm for (b) and (c), respectively. (d) ESEM image showing a microporous structure composed of thin membrane walls. The scale bar represents 10 μm.

Hirano et al. previously reported a microporous structure consisting of thin membrane walls (<1.5 μm thick) and proposed that the gel is a polyphasic system retaining small water droplets within its pores. We obtained ESEM images showing a similar microporous morphology (Figure d). Unlike the cryo-SEM samples, which were flash-frozen in liquid ethane and subsequently lyophilized, the ESEM samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen without solvent exchange. This observation indicates that the nanofibrous network of N-acetylchitosan is easily disrupted upon drying during sample preparation. The significantly lower BET surface area (∼20 m2 g–1) measured for hydrogels lyophilized without solvent exchange further supports the structural fragility of the nanofibrous network upon drying.

Enzymatic Hydrolysis Behavior of N-Acetylchitosan Hydrogel

Enzymatic hydrolysis of water-insoluble substrates such as cellulose and chitin occurs primarily at the substrate surface. Therefore, the overall hydrolyzability of such substrates depends not only on intrinsic material properties, such as crystallinity, but also on the available surface area. − For example, when nanofibrous cellulose and cellulose powder were subjected to enzymatic hydrolysis using a mixture of fungal cellulases from Trichoderma viride, the nanofibrous cellulose produced 3.5 times more glucose after 30 min of reaction.

A similar enhancement in hydrolysis was observed for chitin (Figure ). Hydrolysis of the nanofibrous N-acetylchitosan hydrogel by a bacterial Chitinase (Yatalase) was significantly faster than that observed for chitin powder. Moreover, it proceeded even faster than the hydrolysis of colloidal chitin, an amorphous form of chitin commonly used as a substrate for studying Chitinase activity because of its high accessibility. The concentration of N-acetylglucosamine released from nanofibrous N-acetylchitosan (0.57 mg mL–1) after 180 min of reaction was 10.5 times greater than that from crystalline chitin (0.05 mg mL–1) and 2.1 times greater than that from colloidal chitin (0.27 mg mL–1). As the N-acetylchitosan nanofibers have the same crystalline form as native chitin, these results confirm the critical role of surface area in accelerating enzymatic reactions on water-insoluble polymeric substrates.

Comparison of the enzymatic hydrolyzability of three chitinous substrates; chitin powder (black), colloidal chitin (green), and the N-acetylchitosan hydrogel (red). The N-acetylchitosan hydrogel exhibited the highest hydrolyzability, presumably owing to its large specific surface area provided by nanofibrillation.

Given its crystalline yet nanofibrous structure with a large specific surface area, the N-acetylchitosan hydrogel serves as an excellent model substrate for studying chitin hydrolysis, exhibiting rapid enzymatic degradation despite its crystallinity. This unusual combination of crystallinity and degradability is attributed to the high density of accessible surface sites provided by nanofibrillation and the efficient diffusion of enzymes within the porous network.

Compatibility of N-Acetylchitosan Hydrogel with Microbial Growth

The suitability of the N-acetylchitosan hydrogel as a solid support for microbial culture was evaluated using three representative model microorganisms: the Gram-negative bacterium E. coli W3110, the Gram-positive bacterium B. subtilis 168, and the eukaryotic yeast S. cerevisiae YPH499. The hydrogels used for microbial culture were prepared by replacing the water within the hydrogel pores with the appropriate culture media (LB broth for E. coli and B. subtilis, YPD broth for S. cerevisiae) according to a previously reported procedure.

No appreciable differences were observed in either the appearance or the number of colonies formed by the three microorganisms on the N-acetylchitosan hydrogel surface compared with those on the agar hydrogel surface (Figure , Table ). These results demonstrate that the N-acetylchitosan hydrogel can serve as a solid support for microbial culture in the same manner as agar, which serves as the gold standard for microbial cultivation. The comparable colony numbers also indicate that the amino groups of chitosan were almost completely N-acetylated, since residual unreacted amino groups would otherwise inhibit microbial growth owing to the intrinsic antimicrobial activity.

Ultrasensitive Visual Detection of Microbial Chitinolytic Activity via Surface Pitting

The N-acetylchitosan hydrogel remained microbiologically inert toward the three model microorganisms described above, as they lack the ability to degrade chitin. Consequently, microbial cells proliferated on the gel surface, forming dome-shaped colonies. In contrast, the hydrogel became microbiologically active in the presence of chitinolytic microorganisms. SPOT utilizes the enzymatic degradation of a polymer hydrogel as a direct visual indicator for microbial activity, enabling ultrasensitive detection of polymer-degrading functions. ,

The SPOT-based visual sensing of microbial chitinolytic activity was demonstrated by culturing a known chitin-degrading bacterium, S. degradans, on the surface of the N-acetylchitosan hydrogel. S. degradans is known to degrade complex polysaccharides, including cellulose and chitin. Its cellulolytic activity was previously detected through pit formation on the nanofibrous cellulose hydrogel. After incubation for 16 h, shallow pits were detected by optical profilometry (Figure a), and the pit size increased progressively with incubation time (Figure b–d). The pits were typically 14.5–79.8 μm in diameter and 1.1–47.9 μm in depth (Figure e). The bottom of each pit was covered with a dense mat of microbial cells (Figure f), suggesting that Chitinase secreted by the microorganism hydrolyzed the N-acetylchitosan nanofibers, thereby disrupting their three-dimensional network (Figure ) and resulting in pit formation.

(a–d) Optical profilometric images showing the time-dependent growth of a pit formed on the surface of N-acetylchitosan hydrogel by Saccharophagus degradans after incubation for (a) 16 h, (b) 24 h, (c) 30 h, and (d) 48 h. (e) Cross-sectional profiles corresponding to (a–d) showing the time-dependent deepening of the pit. (f) SEM image of a pit formed by culturing S. degradans on a nanofibrous N-acetylchitosan hydrogel after incubation at 30 °C for 38 h. A mat of bacterial cells (indicated by an arrowhead) likely covered the bottom surface of the pit but detached during specimen preparation.

Despite its apparent simplicity, SPOT can detect microbial chitinolytic activity with exceptionally high sensitivity. The estimated mass of hydrolyzed N-acetylchitosan required to form the pit shown in Figure d, calculated by multiplying the pit volume by the fiber density (0.033 g cm–3), was 1.1 ng (Figure a). The rate of degradation increased markedly after 48 h of incubation, presumably as S. degradans entered the logarithmic growth phase. After 64 h, small pits became visible to the naked eye on the gel surface, corresponding to an estimated hydrolyzed mass of only 6.2 ng of N-acetylchitosan (Figure a). After 4 days, pits became clearly visible to the naked eye (Figure b). Although their sizes showed considerable variation reflecting differences in microbial growth rates, pits with diameters of a few hundred micrometerscorresponding to the hydrolysis of several tens of nanograms of polymerwere readily observable.

(b) Appearance of an N-acetylchitosan hydrogel after culturing S. degradans at 30 °C for 4 days, showing macroscopic pits formed by chitinolytic activity. (c) Formation of clearing zones around colonies after culturing S. degradans on an agar plate containing dispersed colloidal (amorphous) chitin at 30 °C for 4 days. (d) Appearance of colonies (indicated by arrowheads) formed after culturing S. degradans on an agar plate containing dispersed crystalline chitin powder at 30 °C for 4 days, where no detectable chitin degradation is observed. Conventional detection of microbial chitinolytic activity typically relies on halo assays, in which microorganisms are cultured on agar plates containing dispersed chitin substrates and clear zones around colonies are interpreted as evidence of chitin degradation. In the present study, chitinolytic activity of S. degradans was observed when the bacterium was cultured on agar containing dispersed colloidal (amorphous) chitin (Figure c); however, the extent of degradation could not be quantified. Moreover, no detectable degradation was observed when S. degradans was cultured on agar containing dispersed crystalline chitin (Figure d).

These results demonstrate the ultrasensitive visual detectability of bacterial chitinolytic activity, enabling the recognition of enzymatic degradation involving only nanogram-scale mass loss. The high transparency of the N-acetylchitosan hydrogel further facilitates visual observation, in contrast to the opaque cellulose hydrogel used previously.

The exceptional sensitivity of SPOT-based detection arises from the large specific surface area and porous nanofibrous structure of the N-acetylchitosan hydrogel. Unlike water-soluble substrates, microbial chitinases can only access the surface of water-insoluble N-acetylchitosan, and the extensive surface area of nanofibers enhances enzymatic hydrolysis. − Moreover, the low fiber density of N-acetylchitosan in the hydrogel (0.033 g cm–3), compared with that of monolithic chitin (1.425 g cm–3), amplifies the apparent morphological changes, producing easily visible pits even when only a small mass of substrate is degraded. Thus, the SPOT platform translates minute enzymatic events into measurable topographical signals, achieving nanogram-level detection without specialized instrumentation.

It should be noted that pit formation may arise from either complete hydrolysis of nanofibers into soluble products or partial hydrolysis that generates water-soluble or dispersible fragments, both of which disrupt the nanofibrillar network. However, because the detectable mass loss occurs at the nanogram scale and water-soluble hydrolysis products readily diffuse into the surrounding hydrated network, detailed chemical analysis of intermediate substrate states is experimentally challenging under the present assay conditions.

Conclusion

We developed an ultrasensitive visual sensing platform based on SPOT that enables direct and quantitative detection of microbial chitinolytic activity using a nanofibrous N-acetylchitosan hydrogel. This platform allowed visual detection of chitin degradation, with profilometric quantification down to ∼1 ng and naked-eye recognition at several tens of nanograms. The hydrogel exhibited high enzymatic degradability due to its nanofibrous structure with a large specific surface area while retaining its crystallinity. When chitinolytic microorganisms were cultured on the gel surface, local enzymatic hydrolysis of the nanofibers produced nanoscale depressions that expanded into visible pits, allowing nanogram-level detection of hydrolytic activity without labeling or complex instrumentation. SPOT has proven highly effective for screening complex environmental samples, enabling the discovery of taxonomically novel cellulolytic bacteria from deep-sea environments. ,,, In this study’s context, it has also revealed Vibrio species producing unique chitinases containing two carbohydrate-binding domains.

Harnessing microbial enzymatic degradation of water-insoluble polymeric substrates is essential for addressing global sustainability challenges. Microbial cellulases and chitinases are increasingly recognized as key catalysts for biobased chemical production, while microbial enzymes capable of degrading synthetic polymers are gaining importance in the recycling and bioremediation of plastic waste. ,−

Our previous study demonstrated that SPOT provides a label-free, high-sensitivity method for detecting microbial degradation of cellulose. , By extending the SPOT strategy to chitin through the design of a crystalline yet nanofibrillated N-acetylchitosan hydrogel that simultaneously preserves structural order and enhances enzymatic accessibility, this work advances SPOT from a cellulose-specific assay to a more general approach for elucidating microbial functions involving insoluble polymeric substrates. More broadly, this study demonstrates how controlling the nanoscale structure of polymer substrates can transform localized interfacial enzymatic reactions into highly sensitive visual signals. Furthermore, SPOT has contributed to the conceptual development of deep-sea-inspired chemistry by linking microbial functions under extreme conditions to new approaches in sustainable chemistry and materials science.
