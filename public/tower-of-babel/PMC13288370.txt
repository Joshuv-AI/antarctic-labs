Perceptions of Cancer Risks Among Indigenous Women in Bolivia: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Eda G. Christensen, Tracy Onega, Logan Reeves, Kian Robison, Trey Johnson, Jeff Rose, Akiko Kamimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsCancer is responsible for nearly 20% of all deaths in Bolivia. This study explored sociocultural factors, risk perceptions, and healthcare‐seeking behaviors associated with cancer among Aymara women in a high‐altitude community in rural Bolivia.MethodsIn May 2025, two focus groups were conducted in Spanish with 20 women aged 18 and older in Sajama, a remote Andean town. Participants were recruited through purposive sampling with support from local collaborators. Discussions were guided by a semi‐structured interview schedule, audio‐recorded, transcribed, and translated into English. Thematic analysis was conducted using independent coding and integration of findings.ResultsCancer was described as a deeply feared and often silent illness, typically recognized only once symptoms had progressed and perceived as largely incurable. Five key themes emerged: perceptions of cancer risk; barriers to care—including geographic isolation, financial challenges, and mistrust in the healthcare system; the parallel use of traditional and biomedical medicine; lifestyle‐related cancer risks; and environmental stressors contributing to concerns about cancer.ConclusionThese findings emphasize the importance of culturally grounded education and community‐driven strategies to improve cancer awareness, early detection, and access to care among indigenous populations in rural regions.

Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of death worldwide, responsible for nearly 10 million deaths in 2020—approximately one in six globally [1]. Although many cancers are curable when detected early and treated effectively, cancer mortality reflects persistent disparities driven by geographic location, healthcare access, and socioeconomic inequality [2]. These inequities are especially pronounced among indigenous populations, who often face compounded barriers to screening, diagnosis, and treatment due to historical marginalization, cultural exclusion, economic disadvantage, and systemic discrimination [3].

Across countries such as Australia, Canada, New Zealand, and the United States, Indigenous communities experience significantly higher cancer mortality compared to non‐Indigenous populations [4]. Factors contributing to this disparity include low screening uptake, delayed diagnosis, cultural incongruence with health systems, and widespread structural discrimination [5]. Cancer risk awareness among indigenous women is shaped by unique conceptions of illness that often emphasize spiritual imbalance, stigma, and fatalism [6, 7]. These perceptions affect when and how symptoms are recognized and addressed, often diverging from biomedical expectations and underscoring the need for culturally grounded understandings of cancer care [8, 9]. Furthermore, the recognition of lifestyle risk factors for cancer tends to be especially low in low‐ and middle‐income countries [10].

Indigenous people experience higher cancer risks globally [11]. Bolivia stands out in Latin America for its high indigenous population, with around 41 percent of people identifying as indigenous [12]. Cancer is responsible for nearly 20% of all deaths in Bolivia, with the most common cancers being cervical, prostate, breast, gallbladder, and lung [13]. Notably, in 2019, Bolivia reported the world's second‐highest age‐standardized incidence of stomach cancer—34.0 cases per 100,000—ranking behind Mongolia and ahead of China [14]. Among Bolivian women, cervical cancer remains both the most diagnosed and the leading cause of cancer‐related mortality, with the highest incidence and second‐highest mortality rate in the region [5, 15]. Bolivia's cancer screening rates remain among the lowest in Latin America: for example, fewer than one‐third of women aged 15–49 have ever been screened for cervical cancer, with coverage falling below 20% in the poorest communities [15].

The context of this study matters beyond its local relevance. Bolivia experienced colonization [16] like many other countries in America, Asia and Africa. Indigenous people in Bolivia are assured autonomy of their nations within the Bolivian states and have rights over the land [17]. Their pluralistic position within the country could influence their perceptions of health and illness. Among Bolivian indigenous people, herbal medicine is an important part of their traditional cultures [18]. They tend to perceive the river system in the mountain where they live as their circulatory system in their body [18]. Their cultural traditions, including those related to health and illness are transmitted orally, not in written form [18]. While Bolivia has a universal health system, there is a shortage of resources [19]. The historical, social, and health‐system context in Bolivia is applicable to many other countries.

Despite Bolivia's sizable indigenous population (5.6 million) [20] and a growing cancer burden, little is known about how indigenous women in high‐altitude rural communities in Bolivia perceive cancer, assess cancer risks, and navigate care systems for cancer. This study contributes to addressing that gap by centering indigenous women's voices and exploring the sociocultural dynamics that shape cancer awareness in Bolivia. This study also aims to add new conceptual insights on perceptions of cancer risks among indigenous women in Bolivia. In doing so, it builds on calls to prioritize indigenous perspectives in designing culturally appropriate, community‐based prevention strategies [21, 22, 23].

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) for human subjects. Qualitative approaches are vital to developing grounded, patient‐centered responses that reflect local knowledge and address structural inequities in care [22, 23]. This qualitative, descriptive study used focus groups to examine cancer risk awareness among Aymara women in Sajama, Bolivia. Data collection took place in May 2025 during the Andean winter in the town of Sajama, located within Sajama National Park in the Oruro Department, approximately a 4‐h drive from La Paz, the nation's capital. Home to roughly 200 residents, primarily of Aymara ethnicity, Sajama is a high‐altitude community (4200–6540 meters above sea level) sustained largely by agriculture (livestock farming) and tourism.

Participants and Recruitment

Eligibility criteria included individuals aged 18 years or older who spoke Spanish. While Aymara is the primary language in the region, all residents are bilingual in Spanish. A purposive sampling strategy was used to recruit participants [24], with help from professional Bolivian guides who are long‐time collaborators with the local community. Prior to the research team's arrival, the Bolivian travel guides introduced the study to community members. Interested participants were informed about the study logistics through word‐of‐mouth. Preferred compensation was determined through community consultation and consisted of staple food items—rice (11 kg), sugar (11 kg), vegetable oil (5 L), and juice (2 L)—provided to each participant following their participation. Since this project was conducted in an economically vulnerable community, the types and quantity of compensation were carefully determined based on the preferences and needs among potential participants to be non‐coercive.

Data Collection

Informed consent was obtained from each participant using a consent cover letter stating that they gave consent by participating in a focus group. Two focus groups were held in the dining room of a local hostel where the research team stayed for 10 days. A bilingual interpreter— a native Spanish speaker residing in the United States—provided real‐time translation for the facilitator, a native English speaker with little Spanish proficiency. Focus groups were audio‐recorded. One researcher who presented at the focus groups took detailed field notes. A semi‐structured focus group guide was developed to explore cancer risk awareness and related beliefs among participants.

Theoretical Framework and Focus Group Guide

The context of this study extends beyond its local relevance, as risk, as described by Ulrich Beck [25], whether understood as a theoretical concept or as a health‐related phenomenon, constitutes a condition that transcends spatial and temporal boundaries and operates on a global scale. The guide was adapted from validated survey instruments, including the Health Information National Trends Survey (HINTS), the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), and the Complementary and Alternative Medicine (CAM) survey [26, 27, 28, 29, 30]. Drawing on relevant scales, the guide included sixteen open‐ended questions designed to elicit discussion on a range of topics: personal and community cancer experiences (e.g., “Have any of you, any of your family members, neighbors, or friends ever had cancer?”), perceived causes and symptoms of cancer (e.g. “In your opinion, what are the causes of cancer?”), knowledge of cancer prevention and screening (e.g., “What can prevent cancer?” and “Have you heard of human papilloma virus (HPV), HPV vaccine, and/or cervical cancer?”), and access to healthcare (e.g., “How accessible is health care in your community?”). The guide also addressed trusted sources of health information (e.g., “From whom/where do you get the information about health and/or cancer?”), environmental concerns (e.g., “In relation to your health, do you worry about the quality of your drinking water or anything else in your environment?”), and included culturally grounded questions to better understand community values and healthcare priorities (e.g., “What do you think are the three most important aspects of your community?” and “What are three things that you think could improve health in your community?”). The full focus group guide is presented in Appendix 1.

Data Analysis

Audio recordings were transcribed and translated into English by a bilingual research assistant (native Spanish speaker). Data analysis was based on thematic analysis [31] using the structured focus group guide. Two research assistants independently coded the transcripts and organized quotes into preliminary themes. A third assistant, who also served as a focus group facilitator, integrated the analyses. Because this study used a structured focus group guide which was already organized by themes, there were no notable disagreements in the process of coding and data analysis in the research team. To gather external feedback, a summary of findings was shared with a local guide from La Paz, who assisted with field logistics, was present during data collection, and has long‐standing ties to the Sajama community. While not directly part of the community, his familiarity with the setting provided helpful contextual insights for credibility.

Trustworthiness

Data credibility was strengthened through cross‐checking of transcripts, triangulation across six team members (three researchers and three assistants) [32], and adherence to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist.

Perceptions of Cancer

Participants generally expressed negative views about cancer, often describing it with fear, uncertainty, and sadness. It was commonly perceived as a silent, progressively worsening illness—likened to a plant “rooting” within the body before becoming evident. Many believed that by the time symptoms appeared, the disease was already terminal. Cancer was strongly associated with rapid weight loss, exhaustion, intense pain, and cognitive decline in its advanced stages. As one participant described, “in the last moments, they don't even recognize their family anymore. Yes. That is, they no longer know what they are saying, what they are thinking, it's like that, it's very sad,” illustrating how cancer is seen as not only physically devastating for the patient but also emotionally distressing for family members.

A particularly salient distinction made by participants concerned the perceived difference between benign and malignant growths, which strongly influenced care‐seeking decisions. Although benign tumors are not cancer, participants often referred to them as “benign cancer” and understood them as treatable. One woman explained, “When you talk about cancer, you think you're going to die right away… that's the concept, I think. But in some cases, yes, it can be cured; some are malignant, some are benign.” This understanding was endorsed by another participant, who shared that her daughter “healed up” after surgery on her foot because the growth—which she referred to as “cancer”—was “benign.” Malignant tumors, by contrast, were consistently viewed as incurable and fatal, with participants stating, “If it's malignant, it's like there's no solution,” and “When it's malignant, there's no cure.”

This apparent conflation of tumors and cancerous growths reflects limited access to biomedical information and underscores the need for clear, community‐informed education about abnormal growths, cancer types, diagnosis, and treatment options. The concept of timing also emerged as critical to cancer prevention, early detection, and recovery, based on the recognition that the disease often progresses silently and rapidly. As one participant observed, “And when we are already at our last gasp, we just react, we want to do everything possible, but it's already late,” highlighting the regret frequently expressed around delayed care‐seeking.

Health Beliefs and Knowledge Around Cancer

Traditional and natural medicine were widely practiced and deeply trusted within the community. Many participants expressed a strong spiritual and material connection to their community, viewing nature as a source of both healing and identity. “We live with livestock, tourism, crafts,” one woman said, “and since our parents, it has always been like that.” Others emphasized the healing power of native plants, spring water, and “pure air,” reflecting a worldview in which health is inseparable from Mother Earth. As one woman affirmed, “We are Sajameñas at heart.” Participants described the use of various medicinal plants—such as chachacoma, pupusa, and chucapaco—to treat common ailments, including coughs, colds, and stomach pain. Kalanchoe was specifically believed to be a beneficial remedy for cancer. One participant whose cancer “hadn't rooted in other places”, recounted that following surgery, she declined chemotherapy and relied exclusively on plant‐based treatments based on her beliefs: “Every day one leaf [of kalanchoe] is eaten. That did me a lot of good. After that, I took aloe vera, I took noni, then soursop, a lot of lemon, and other things. Every day, for at least about 7 months, I did that treatment with herbs only, and that helped me get better.”

At the same time, biomedical knowledge also played a role in shaping participants' understanding of cancer. Formal health information was primarily obtained from the local health center, which occasionally offered educational workshops on topics such as Pap smear campaigns and breast cancer awareness. Some participants also sought cancer‐related information through online sources. While there was general awareness of human papillomavirus (HPV) as a precursor to uterine cancer, discussions revealed that HPV vaccination efforts remained focused exclusively on girls. Across both focus groups, participants expressed a strong interest in attending additional seminars and workshops on cancer detection, prevention, and general awareness. As one participant noted, “We would like, let's say, that we could have perhaps, as I said, more in‐depth talks, to make us more aware,” reflecting a broader desire to integrate biomedical knowledge with existing community health practices.

Accessing and Trusting Cancer Care

Access to cancer care was widely identified as a significant challenge, primarily due to the community's geographic isolation and limited financial resources. Participants frequently described the only local health facility as insufficient and unequipped to handle serious illnesses such as cancer. As one participant noted, “We have a health center, but it's not first‐level, it's third‐level, you could say.” Financial constraints were a major barrier to seeking timely medical attention, with the high costs of surgery and diagnostic tests frequently cited. “We don't have a lot of money, so we can't go. And the hospital, don't think it'll ask for a little, it'll ask for a lot of money for surgery, for exams,” explained another participant. Participants also described barriers to care stemming from distance, cost, and caregiving responsibilities. With no specialized cancer treatment nearby, travel to the city was burdensome and often impossible. “Going to the city is very far, it hurts more… here you can just use [natural] medicine,” one woman said, underscoring reliance on local remedies in the absence of accessible formal care. Financial hardship compounded these barriers; one woman recounted how her father “preferred to die” rather than become a burden, reflecting the normalization of fatalism when treatment is out of reach. Family obligations also shaped health behavior. “There's more work… more with the wawas,” a participant explained. Amid these competing demands, women often placed their health as a secondary concern.

In addition to structural barriers, participants expressed deep mistrust toward the medical system and its providers. Concerns centered on perceived medical negligence, underqualified personnel, and a lack of transparency in care. Chemotherapy was particularly viewed with fear and skepticism—often described as harmful, “burning” the patient, or worsening the disease. Reflecting this sentiment, one participant recounted the words of a community member who had died of cancer: “She used to say, ‘don't get chemotherapy because it's bad,’ that's what she told us before she passed away.”

Lifestyle Factors

Participants frequently linked cancer risk and health more broadly to lifestyle conditions. Changes in diet were a major concern. Many described a shift from traditional, natural foods to chemical‐laden products as a key factor in rising cancer rates. “Before, everything was natural,” one participant explained, “but now everything is chemicals… because of that, cancer can come over time.” In contrast, subsistence practices such as cooking with firewood and preserving meat as charque were seen as healthier and more sustainable. These practices, along with daily exposure to mountain air and seasonal work, were described as promoting well‐being. “Here it's calmer, open air, there are mountains… everything is good for us, and we are used to that.”

Perceived Environmental Stressors

Participants identified environmental conditions as key contributors to cancer risk and broader health challenges. Women expressed concerns about increasing ultraviolet (UV) exposure and extreme weather, linking strong sunlight and wind to skin and stomach illnesses, particularly in children. Climate change—with its unpredictable weather patterns and prolonged winters—was also described as disrupting traditional agriculture and threatening local food security. One participant lamented, “Vegetables don't produce like before,” while others noted volcanic soil damage limiting cultivation. Despite these stressors, women demonstrated resilience and innovation, maintaining a strong connection to their environment. As one explained, “We can make some greenhouses with plastic roofs, then inside there we can do it [cultivate]. It grows,” highlighting how environmental adaptation is integral to sustaining health and livelihoods.

Water quality and availability were central concerns for participants, though these concerns were understood in distinct ways. While women regarded the spring water as inherently safe due to its natural origin, they also recognized that human and perceived environmental factors could compromise its quality. For example, frustration emerged over institutional inaction in the face of possible contamination—particularly fecal contamination. The health center had reported an increase in parasitic infections among elementary school children and suggested a potential link to water quality. “We have requested from entities in Oruro that we depend on, right? Here at the health center—and well, no, they haven't given us instructions to do studies on the water,” one participant shared. “Maybe we could also request that our drinking water be analyzed, because maybe it has heavy minerals,” commented another, immediately after. Concerns about decreasing water availability were often attributed to environmental change. “With climate change, it seems it started—the river can go, get lost,” one woman observed. Another added, “We need, let's say, for it [the spring] to be maintained. That's our concern. To have a little more water, because it has been decreasing…to maintain, or look for another place, let's say, maybe opening another place underground, right? So that's our concern”—both comments reflecting uncertainty about the long‐term reliability of natural sources. Despite these challenges, participants drew on place‐based knowledge—an understanding rooted in lived experience and deep ties to the environment—to interpret and respond to water issues. This perspective was reflected in their efforts to safeguard community health and the land's integrity by protecting their primary water source and seeking alternative sources.

Discussion

This study described Aymara women's perceptions about cancer risks in Sajama town. The key themes that emerged from the focus groups relate to perceptions of cancer, health beliefs, access to cancer treatment, lifestyle factors, and environmental issues. Even as attitudes toward cancer screening become more favorable, traditional beliefs continue to shape perceptions of disease causation and appropriate care pathways [21]. These cultural frameworks intersect with persistent structural barriers, compounding the challenges Aymara women face in accessing cancer care. A growing body of qualitative research underscores how indigenous women's engagement with cancer services is influenced by structural inequities, communication barriers, and a lack of cultural alignment within healthcare systems [33, 34, 35, 36]. Additional deterrents to screening and treatment include the cost of services, long travel distances, experiences of discrimination, and fear of diagnosis. Conversely, factors that support engagement with cancer care include the persistence or severity of symptoms, family history of illness, and exposure to culturally competent outreach and health education [6, 37, 38, 39, 40].

Health beliefs in Aymara communities are shaped by holistic frameworks rooted in reciprocity and spiritual balance. The results of this study also indicated that due to the limited access to care, traditional approaches to illness are sustained. Aymara medicine emphasizes the interconnectedness of body, spirit, and nature, and healing practices are often led by yatiris (spiritual healers) and chifleras (herbalists) [9]. The term yatiri means “wise one,” denoting a person who guides individuals in restoring harmony to both body and spirit [41]. Illness is commonly understood as a rupture in spiritual or social relationships, rather than a physiological malfunction alone. As a result, biomedical care is often sought only when symptoms become severe or unmanageable [42]. Cultural stigma, fear, and fatalistic beliefs—echoed across indigenous populations globally—contribute to the underutilization of cancer screening and treatment [43].

Cancer screening and treatment gaps are even more acute in rural, highland areas such as Sajama town, where environmental challenges intersect with social vulnerability derived from poverty, discrimination, and geographical isolation [8, 44]. The long distance to a health care facility that provides advanced cancer treatment and financial barriers are primary causes of the gap. Further issues that undermine trust and discourage follow‐up care are low confidence in nurses' ability to conduct screenings and delays in receiving test results [6, 15, 45]. To prevent delayed care, community health workers or traditional healers may lead culturally sensitive interventions. Future research should examine patient‐provider communications in rural Bolivia.

Alongside traditional epistemologies, shifting lifestyles in highland Aymara communities are reshaping patterns of health risk and illness management. Increased exposure to transgenic crops and animals, processed foods, and sedentary behaviors—driven by changes in agricultural practices, a growing influx of tourists relative to the small local population, and expanded transportation networks—is contributing to a rise in non‐communicable diseases, including cancers linked to metabolic risk factors [46, 47]. This dynamic, in contrast to patterns observed in many other indigenous populations, has enabled Aymara communities to sustain a relatively stable health profile [4, 41].

Perceived environmental issues are concerns in the highlands. Located in Bolivia's Andean region above 4000 m, Sajama National Park is home to Aymara communities living in ecologically fragile and geographically isolated terrain [48]. Water sources often contain high concentrations of arsenic, lithium, and boron, which are associated with increased cancer risk [49, 50]. Moreover, recent research suggests that high‐altitude living may heighten the odds of mortality from certain cancers, including cervical, breast, liver, and hematopoietic malignancies [51]. Further studies are necessary to examine the association between environmental issues and health risks.

The results wee overall consistent with the theoretical framework from “Risk Society” [31]. Perceived trust and credibility take key roles for how Aymara people believe risks related to cancer. Socially binding definition of truth about cancer risks can be more dominant among the population than scientific knowledge about cancer. This explains cultural beliefs and structural barriers which Aymara people experience. This study added new insights on high‐altitude ecology, tourism‐driven lifestyle changes and perceptions of benign vs malignant tumors that could advance the theoretical framework's applicability to the Andean indigenous populations and their perceptions of cancer risks. More detailed theoretical and conceptional analysis on cultural beliefs and structural barriers among Andean indigenous people should be conducted in future studies.

Study Limitations

This study is limited by its small sample size; however, given that the town's population is approximately 200, the inclusion of 20 participants represents a substantial proportion and provides valuable insight into local perspectives. While the original study design aimed to include both women and men, all participants were women. Future research should aim for greater gender inclusivity to capture a broader range of experiences. Additionally, due to the small size of the community, it was not feasible to recruit participants who were unfamiliar with one another. While existing social ties may have fostered openness and trust in some cases, they may also have limited the expression of dissenting views or the disclosure of sensitive experiences. The interpreter and translator are native Spanish speaker—one from Honduras and the other from Mexico—who live in the U.S. They do not speak Aymara. The team did not have access Aymara speakers who were fluent in English and were available to help this project. This might have caused potential loss of meaning in metaphors. However, there were no notable communication gaps between the participants and the interpreter in Spanish. Furthermore, recruitment of participants through guides might have influenced participation (e.g., social desirability, trust, gatekeeping). Despite these limitations, the study's qualitative design provides rich, context‐specific insights into indigenous women's understandings of cancer risk, care‐seeking behaviors, and health practices in rural Bolivia. To our knowledge, this is one of the first qualitative studies to explore cancer risk awareness among Aymara women in a high‐altitude Bolivian community, helping to identify critical knowledge gaps and opportunities for culturally responsive public health interventions.

Conclusion

This study examined how indigenous Aymara women in the high‐altitude town of Sajama perceive cancer risk and navigate treatment options. While healthcare scarcity in rural, high‐altitude Bolivia remains a deeply rooted structural issue, many of the concerns raised in this study could be addressed through culturally responsive, community‐based education initiatives that equip Aymara women with the knowledge and tools to take an active role in their health. Future research should focus on health promotion strategies that fit cultural and traditional perspectives in their community and evaluate the effectiveness of the strategies.
