GC-MS Unraveling, Elicitation of Tropane Alkaloids, and Antimicrobial and Antivirulence Activities of Callus and Hairy Root Cultures of Endangered Hyoscyamus albus L.
Authors: Asmaa M. Arafa, Esraa A. Mohamed, Rehab H. Abdallah, Ahmed M. Mustafa, Omer I. Fantoukh, Fatma Al-zahraa A. Yehia, Eman A. M. Ahmed, Samih I. El-Dahmy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hyoscyamus albus L. is an endangered plant that is rich in tropane alkaloids (TAs), especially scopolamine and hyoscyamine, of valuable pharmaceutical interest. Owing to the complex and challenging chemical synthesis of TAs, developing new approaches to their production is necessary to meet the substantial pharmaceutical demand. Therefore, strategies employing biotechnology and nanotechnology are urgently needed to conserve the plant and enhance TA production. This is the first detailed study of Egyptian H. albus L. and on the elicitation of zinc oxide (Z) and zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZN) in callus and hairy root (HR) cultures of this species. The alkaloidal extracts of air-dried aerial parts, roots, calli, and hairy roots of H. albus L. were subjected to GC-MS to investigate their contents. Additionally, the effects of abiotic elicitors at different concentrations on the growth and atropine content of cultures of Egyptian H. albus L. were studied. Moreover, the antimicrobial and antivirulence activities of alkaloidal extracts of Egyptian H. albus L. were assessed against various microorganisms. GC/MS revealed the induction of tropinone in the in vitro cultures. Hairy roots (HRs) of Egyptian H. albus L. produced higher amounts of scopolamine and aposcopolamine than did the parent plant. ZnO (Z) is the most effective for atropine accumulation in callus cultures of H. albus L. Additionally, the most effective ones for productivity in hairy root (HR) tissue and media are ZnO nanoparticles (ZNs) and ZnO (Z), respectively. Alkaloid extracts of Egyptian H. albus L. exhibited promising antimicrobial and antivirulence activities. These findings shed light on the use of biotechnological tools and nanotechnology for the sustainable and improved production of pharmaceutically relevant phytocompounds.

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) stated that most of the global population (80%), especially in developing countries, relies on herbal medicine for their health care. This is due to their fewer side effects, being more effective, and being cheaper than the synthetic drugs. , As a result, the international herbal market estimates its trade value to surpass USD3 billion and reach USD5 trillion by 2050. , Plant-derived natural products enclose a variety of sophisticated chemical compounds that were reported to exhibit a wide range of biological effects, including potent activity against resistant bacteria and fungi. In particular, secondary metabolites (SMs) such as alkaloids, terpenoids, flavonoids, and peptides have been used for centuries as an excellent source of drugs and for therapeutic purposes. Tropane alkaloids (TAs) are valuable natural products of the solanaceous plants. Due to their complex chemical synthesis, the commercial production of TAs is expensive and challenging. As a result, their availability for pharmaceutical needs depends on the isolation of TAs from plants, despite their relatively low yields. This creates an urgent need to employ biotechnological techniques to enhance the concentration of TAs in plants and to provide an alternative source of TAs. − Hyoscyamus albus L., belonging to the Solanaceae family, contains scopolamine and hyoscyamine as major TAs. They are anticholinergic drugs that are widely used in medicine to treat asthma, Parkinson’s disease, motion sickness, pesticide poisoning, and cough. Scopolamine is the most important one of the TAs because its global demand is about 10 times higher than that of atropine and (−)-hyoscyamine combined. Therefore, there is a growing interest in increasing its plant content.

The IUCN Red List records indicate that 723 of 5411 assessed medicinal plants (MPs) are under threat of extinction. Overexploitation, climate change, habitat fragmentation, population growth, land clearance, and deforestation are the risk factors contributing to the decline of natural habitats for MPs. H. albus L. is identified as an endangered MP due to its restricted distribution, predominance of annual forms, susceptibility to drought stress, scattered growth communities, and low capacity for emigration. In vitro cultures, such as callus, organ, and hairy root (HR), are promising alternative tools to conserve endangered MPs and produce tropane alkaloids (TAs). ,, They are more effective tools than the cultivation of plants in vivo in producing SMs under a controlled environment, independent of soil compositions or climatic factors. In addition, geographical and genetic variation among plants cultivated in vivo leads to inconsistent TA yields. A similar or higher production of SMs by HR cultures transformed by Agrobacterium rhizogenes than those found in parent roots is documented. This advantage, together with their lateral branching, rapid hormone-independent growth, and genetic stability, has shed light in the last decades on utilizing HR cultures to produce SMs. ,

Despite the growing application of nanoelicitors in plant tissue culture, their elicitation mechanisms are likely complex, insufficiently elucidated, and could involve different mediators of signaling pathways and their cross-talk. These events are based on various factors such as morphology, amount, size, and exposure time of nanoparticles (NPs), in combination with explant type, plant species, and growth conditions. − The NPs of zinc oxide (ZnO) are known to function as abiotic stressors. Their function is to decrease oxidative stress by enhancing the integrity of the membrane, stimulating cell division, generating ROS (reactive oxygen species) produced by stress, moderating concentrations of carbohydrates, protein metabolism, osmoregulation, amino acids, and photosynthetic pigments. This ROS-mediated signaling has been reported to enhance the expression and activity of enzymes involved in TA biosynthetic pathways. − Moreover, zinc (Zn) is an essential micronutrient for plants, as it is involved in numerous key cellular functions, including metabolic and physiological processes, enzyme activation, and ion homeostasis. ,, The application of NPs of zinc oxide (ZnO), iron oxide (Fe3O4), silicon dioxide (SiO2), and chitosan to the HRs of H. reticulatus resulted in enhancing the TA production. ,,, In addition, NPs of silver (Ag) and SiO2 were used for the elicitation of TAs in HRs of H. muticus and H. pusillus, respectively.

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has been declared a global health threat, as it resulted in five million deaths in 2019, constituting 9% of all global deaths. Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa were identified as the leading causes of AMR-associated deaths (73%). These pathogens have a high capability to adapt and survive in either the community or in health care settings. Clinical reliance on existing antimicrobials has led to the emergence of multidrug-resistant bacterial strains. Moreover, limited discovery of new antibiotic classes and cross-resistance between old and new antibiotics within the same class have jeopardized the overcoming of AMR. Aligned with the WHO global action plan, efforts to discover and develop nonantibiotic antimicrobial therapies were intensified.

Alkaloids are well known to be effective therapeutics against multidrug-resistant bacteria, fungi, and viruses, and they have also been shown to inhibit biofilm formation and microbial virulence. , Previous studies on the alkaloidal extracts of H. albus have demonstrated notable inhibitory activity against several pathogenic microorganisms, such as P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, Bacillus subtilis, E. coli, and Salmonella typhi. , However, evidence regarding their potential activity on microbial biofilms and virulence remains scarce. Accordingly, the current study aims to investigate the antimicrobial, antibiofilm, and antivirulence activities of H. albus alkaloids to provide deeper insight into their therapeutic potential.

To date, there have been no reports on the in vitro propagation, induction of hairy roots (HRs), and elicitation of TAs of Egyptian H. albus L. Hence, this study focuses on in vitro regeneration and HR induction of the Egyptian plant and performs a comparison between the TAs contents of wild plants and in vitro tissues for the first time. In addition, it intends to investigate the effect of abiotic elicitors on the production of TAs in the callus and HR cultures of Egyptian H. albus L. Furthermore, alkaloid extracts of Egyptian H. albus L. were tested for their inhibitory activity against bacterial and fungal growth as well as against bacterial virulence.

Materials and Methods

Plant Material

H. albus L. (family Solanaceae) (accession number HA-SO10, Herbarium of the Department of Pharmacognosy, Faculty of Pharmacy, Zagazig University, Egypt) was collected in February 2022 from El Dabaa Road (Kilo 198), Matrouh Governorate, Egypt. It was identified by Dr. Wafaa M. Amer, Professor of Flora and Taxonomy, Department of Botany and Microbiology, Faculty of Science, Cairo University, Egypt.

In Vitro Germination of Seeds

First, seeds of H. albus L. were surface-sterilized with 70% ethanol (El-Nasr Pharmaceutical Chemicals, Egypt) for 1 min, followed by immersion in commercial 5% hypochlorite solution (Clorox) for 10 min with shaking. Finally, seeds were washed three times with sterile distilled water under a laminar cabinet. The sterilized seeds were germinated on a solid autoclaved Murashige and Skoog (M&S) medium (Duchefa, Germany) containing 3% sucrose (Adwic, A.R.E.), 0.8% agar (Bioworld, USA), and a pH of 5.8 was adjusted. Jars were incubated at 25 ± 2 °C under a light/dark photoperiod of 16/8 h. A germination percentage of H. albus L. seeds was evaluated from the equation reported by Jabbour and Alzahrani.

Callus Culture

Aseptically dissected seedlings (2–4 weeks old, 1 cm) were used as explants for in vitro callus induction. Explants were inoculated on autoclaved M&S media containing 3% sucrose and 0.8% agar. Additionally, media (pH of 5.8) was supplemented with different phytohormones (Sigma Chemical Co, USA) combinations as M&S I: 1 mg/L naphthalene acetic acid (NAA) + 0.1 mg/L benzyl amino purine (BAP); M&S II: 0.4 mg/L NAA + 4 mg/L BAP; M&S III: 1 mg/L NAA + 1 mg/L kinetin (Kin); M&S IV: 0.1 mg/L 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2, 4-D) + 1 mg/L BAP; M&S V: 0.5 mg/L thidiazuron (TDZ) + 1 mg/L 2, 4-D + 0.1 mg/L BAP and M&S VI: 2 mg/L 2, 4-D, (n = 3 cultures/medium). All cultures were subcultured every 3 weeks and incubated at 25 ± 2 °C under a light/dark photoperiod of 16/8 h. The callus induction percentage was estimated using a previously reported equation. The growth curve was plotted for either fresh weight (FW) or dry weight (DW) of calli over time. Also, growth parameters were calculated for the media with a high callus induction percentage. Growth index (GI), relative growth rate (RGR), specific growth rate (μ), and doubling time (dt) were assessed as described previously.

In Vitro Propagation of H. albus L.

Regeneration of H. albus L. via organogenesis or somatic embryogenesis was studied for calli produced on M&S media from I to III. Calli were checked under light microscopy to monitor different stages of embryoids. Conditions for subculturing and incubation were the same as those of callus culture. Acclimatization of H. albus L. plantlets was performed by transplanting them into perforated pots containing sterilized peat moss: vermiculite: sand (1:1:1). They were covered with transparent plastic bags and maintained in a culture room (25 ± 2 °C and a light/dark photoperiod of 16/8 h) and watered every 2 days. After 2 weeks, the gradual removal of transparent bags was done.

Hairy Root (HR) Culture

Bacterial Growth Condition

A. rhizogenes strain (LBA 9402) was kindly provided by Dr. Hussein S. Taha, Professor of Plant Biotechnology at the Biotechnology Institute, National Research Centre (NRC), Dokki, Cairo, Egypt. A single clone of A. rhizogenes was cultured on solid LB medium containing kanamycin and rifampicin (50 and 10 mg/L, respectively) at 28 °C in the dark. One clone of A. rhizogenes was subcultured under the same conditions in a liquid LB medium containing kanamycin and rifampicin (each 10 mg/L) on a shaker at 120 rpm until an optical density (OD) at 600 nm of 0.6. The bacterial cell pellets were collected by centrifugation (4000 rpm, 10 °C) for 10 min and resuspended in hormone-free (HF) 1/2 M&S medium (pH 5.8) containing 3% sucrose. ,

Transformation and Induction of HR Culture

Either pricked leaves or leaf segments (1.5–2 cm) of 4-week-old seedlings were used as explants for the induction of HR. These explants were infected by soaking in A. rhizogenes culture for 5 min. Then, the excess bacteria were removed by drying the explants on a sterile filter paper. In addition, explants were co-cultivated on an HF 1/2 M&S medium containing 3% sucrose and 0.8% agar. After 24 h, explants were transferred to fresh medium containing 500 mg/L cefotaxime to control bacterial growth. Negative control was the nontransformed roots. HR was induced and subcultured every 10 days on fresh medium containing a gradual decrease in cefotaxime concentration from 250 to 50 mg/L. Following this, HR was transferred to flasks (250 mL) containing 35 mL HF 1/2 M&S medium (without cefotaxime) containing 3% sucrose and subcultured every 10 days. A pH of 5.8 was adjusted for all media, and these flasks were incubated at 25 °C on a shaker (120 rpm) in darkness. After 4 weeks, fresh and dry weights of HR were recorded at different time intervals to calculate growth parameters. , Also, HR induction could be evaluated from the equation of % HR transformation frequency as described previously.

Confirmation of Transformation of HR Culture

Genomic DNA was isolated from either negative control or transgenic HR (for identification of Ri-T DNA) using the Tissue DNA Preparation-Column Kit. Also, the Fast-n-Easy Plasmid Mini-Prep Kit was used for the extraction of plasmid DNA from A. rhizogenes (LBA 9402). The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was used to confirm the presence of the rol A gene of A. rhizogenes in the transgenic HR. Primer sequences were rol A F-5′-ATGGAACTAGCCGGAATAAA-3′ and rol A R-5′-TTAACCCCGTAGGTCTGAAT-3′. Each PCR reaction (25 μL) consisted of 40 ng of DNA, 10 pm/μL primer, 200 mM dNTP, 1 U of Taq DNA polymerase, 1× PCR buffer, and 1.5 mM MgCl2. All kits and PCR components were from Jena Bioscience, GmbH, Germany. Amplification of DNA was performed in a thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems ProFlex 3x, USA) using the following sequence: Initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min, annealing at 49 °C (rol A primers) for 1.5 min, and extension at 72 °C for 2 min, followed by final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The amplification products were visualized along with 100 bp DNA ladder (H3 RTU, GeneDireX, Inc., Las Vegas, USA) on 1.5% w/v agarose gel (S. D. Fine-Chem Ltd., India) stained with 0.5 μg/mL ethidium bromide (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and observed under UV transilluminator.

Elicitor Application in Callus and HR Cultures

Abiotic elicitors, zinc oxide (ZnO) (Z) (SDFCL, Mumbai, India) and ZnO nanoparticles (ZN) (ORCHID Fine Chemicals Products for Science Research Nanometals, Biotechnology, Cairo, Egypt) with an average size of 30 ± 5 nm of the white and spherical NPs with a specific surface area of ∼20 m2/g. ORCHID Fine Chemicals Products characterized ZN by TEM (JEOL JEM-2100 high-resolution TEM at 200 kV) and XDR (XPERT-PRO Powder Diffractometer system, with 2θ (20–80°), with Minimum step size 2θ: 0.001, and at wavelength (Kα) = 1.54614°) analyses (Figure S1). Elicitors were used at different concentrations: 50 (Z1, ZN1), 100 (Z2, ZN2), and 200 (Z3, ZN3) mg/L, respectively, and added separately to the M&S medium (pH of 5.8) containing 3% sucrose and 0.8% agar before autoclaving. As previously reported, , elicitors’ treatment was initiated after 21 days of culturing (n = 3 cultures/medium). All cultured jars were incubated at 25 ± 2 °C under a light/dark photoperiod of 16/8 h. The FW, DW, and atropine content of the elicited tissues were estimated compared with the control (C), which contained sterile double-distilled water instead of the elicitor. Once elicitors were applied, callus cultures were collected at 5-day intervals, starting on day 5, until reaching 25 days of culturing. On the other hand, HR cultures were harvested on day 21 after elicitation.

Alkaloids Extraction and Analysis

The alkaloids were extracted according to the reported method. Briefly, powdered air-dried aerial parts, roots, calli, and hairy roots of H. albus L. (20 g for each) were homogenized separately in 400 mL of 0.1 N HCl (EL-Nasr Chemical Co., Cairo, Egypt) in a Sonorex Super ultrasonic bath (RK 103 H, Bandelin, Germany) at 35 kHz and 140 W for 1 h. The collected acidic solution was filtered and then washed with methylene chloride (SDFCL, Mumbai, India). The aqueous layer was rendered alkaline to reach pH = 9–10 with 33% ammonia (EL-Nasr Chemical Co., Cairo, Egypt) and extracted four times with an equal volume of methylene chloride. The organic layers were collected, dried over anhyd. Na2SO4 (EL-Nasr Chemical Co., Cairo, Egypt), concentrated and stored in tightly sealed dark containers in the freezer until subsequent analysis. The extracted alkaloids were analyzed by HPLC (Waters 2695 Separations Module, Singapore) and GC-MS (Shimadzu GCMS-QP2010 (Kyoto, Japan)) , according to the published methods, and their conditions are listed in Table S1.

Screening for Antimicrobial Activity

Antimicrobial activity of the alkaloidal extracts of H. albus L. (aerial parts, M&S I callus, and hairy roots) was evaluated against standard strains including S. aureus ATCC 6538, E. coli ATCC 10536, and Candida albicans ATCC 10251, in addition to clinical isolates of Serratia marcescens, Proteus mirabilis, P. aeruginosa, and Enterococcus faecalis via well diffusion assay. Bacterial isolates were obtained from the culture collection of the Microbiology Department, Faculty of Pharmacy, Zagazig University. The midexponential phase of bacterial and yeast cultures was uniformly spread over Muller-Hinton (MH) and Sabouraud dextrose agar plates, respectively, using a sterile cotton swab. Solidified agar was punctured to create a 5 mm well. Stock solutions of aerial parts, M&S I callus, and hairy roots were prepared in DMSO at concentrations of 520, 350, and 750 mg/mL, respectively. The corresponding working solutions were adjusted to 39,000, 56,800, and 24,970 μg/mL, respectively. Subsequently, 100 μL of each alkaloidal extract (equivalent to 3900 μg for aerial parts, 5680 μg for M&S I callus, and 2497 μg for hairy roots) was aseptically added to the wells of the inoculated plates. After incubation, zones of inhibition were measured in mm. Gentamicin (EPICO Pharma, Egypt) and tioconazole (Global Napi Pharmaceuticals, Egypt) were used as positive controls for antibacterial and antifungal activities, respectively. Atropine was used to evaluate the potential role, which was predominantly found in H. albus L. extracts.

Cell viability of P. aeruginosa and C. albicans was assessed in the presence of varying concentrations of aerial parts, M&S I callus, and hairy roots, and atropine was evaluated via the viable count technique. Additionally, inoculated Mueller–Hinton and Sabouraud dextrose broths containing DMSO at the same concentration present in the working solutions were included as vehicle control to exclude any inhibitory effect attributable to DMSO. Briefly, microbial cells (106 CFU/mL) were incubated overnight in the presence of the tested compounds. Subsequently, aliquots of 100 μL were serially diluted and plated on MH agar for P. aeruginosa and Sabouraud dextrose agar for C. albicans, respectively. After plate incubation, viable colonies were counted and compared to the control untreated cells.

Screening for Antivirulence Activity

Biofilm Inhibition Assay

The biofilm complex structure encountered mainly in P. aeruginosa and C. albicans plays a significant role in their pathogenesis and antibiotic resistance. Crystal violet staining assay was performed to assess the effect of H. albus L. extracts on the biofilm mass of P. aeruginosa and C. albicans. Briefly, overnight cultures of P. aeruginosa and C. albicans were adjusted to 1 × 106 CFU/mL in MH and Sabouraud dextrose broth, respectively. Aliquots of 100 μL were transferred to the wells of 96-well sterile microtiter plates supplemented with H. albus L. extracts. Aerial parts, M&S I callus, hairy roots, and atropine were used with subinhibitory concentrations of 2600, 1665, 3787, and 6000 μg/mL, respectively. After incubation, the planktonic cells were aspirated, and the wells were washed thrice to remove nonattached cells. The formed biofilms were fixed with absolute methanol for 20 min, followed by crystal violet staining for 15 min. The excess dye was washed out, and the plates were left to air-dry. Crystal violet dye was solubilized with 33% glacial acetic acid, and the absorbances were measured at 570 nm. The percentage of biofilm inhibition was calculated.

Pyocyanin Inhibition Assay

The pyocyanin pigment interferes with host tissue redox balance and mediates P. aeruginosa evasion of host defenses. Subinhibitory concentrations of H. albus L. extracts were investigated for their effect on pyocyanin production as described previously. Briefly, overnight cultures of P. aeruginosa were diluted in MH broth. The cultures were then incubated for 48 h in the presence of H. albus L. extracts and atropine. After centrifugation, the pyocyanin pigment in the supernatants was measured spectrophotometrically at 691 nm and compared to the control. The pyocyanin inhibition percentage was calculated.

Swarming Motility Inhibition Assay

Bacterial motility is a key fitness trait that enables bacterial sensing and movement toward host sites with more favorable conditions. The effect of H. albus L. extracts on swarming motility was assessed as described previously. Briefly, two microliters of overnight culture of P. aeruginosa were spotted onto swarming MH agar plates containing subinhibitory concentrations of H. albus L. extracts. The plates were incubated in an upright position. The diameter of the swarming zones was measured and compared to the control.

Protease Inhibition Assay

Hydrolytic enzymes, including proteases, enable bacterial tissue invasion and dissemination. The influence of H. albus L. extracts on P. aeruginosa protease production was investigated using skim milk agar. Overnight culture of P.aeruginosa was diluted and incubated in the presence of subinhibitory concentrations of H. albus L. extracts. The bacterial supernatant was aseptically transferred into wells of skim milk agar. The plates were incubated in an upright position, and the zone of clearance around the wells was measured and compared with the control.

In the virulence assay experiments, inoculated Mueller–Hinton and Sabouraud dextrose broths containing DMSO at the same concentration as that present in the working solutions were included as vehicle control to exclude any inhibitory effect attributable to DMSO.

Statistical Analysis

All values were presented as means ± SD. GraphPad Prism Software (version 9, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Normality of the data was assumed by box plot graphical inspection and confirmed by the Shapiro-Wilk test. One-way ANOVA and two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test were used for comparisons. Statistical significance was tested at p < 0.05, while antimicrobial and antivirulence experiments were evaluated by the same program using one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test.

In Vitro Germination of Seeds

After 8 days, sterilized H. albus L. seeds were germinated with a 100% germination on a solid HF M&S medium (Figure ). On the contrary, presoaking of Algerian seeds in H2O2 (2%, for 10 min) was necessary to give 75% germination within 2 days. On the other hand, seed germination of H. reticulatus was performed after 5 days on a solid HF M&S medium supplemented with myo-inositol (0.1 g/L).

Callus Culture

After 5 weeks, calli were induced when culturing explants on M&S media from I to VI at 25 ± 2 °C under a light/dark photoperiod of 16/8 h. Table S2 shows that M&S media from I to III had the highest callus induction percentages; therefore, they were selected for analysis of their growth curves at 5-day intervals over 7 weeks. While growth dynamics were calculated at the end of the experiment as depicted in Table S3. As shown in Figure S2, M&S I had the highest FW and DW on day 35 of cultivation, which were 6.433 ± 0.126 and 0.192 ± 0.005 g, respectively. Also, this medium had a higher GI (5.43 ± 0.13), RGR (0.155 ± 0.004), μ (0.053 ± 0.0003 g/day), and lower dt (13.08 ± 0.08 days) (Table S3); consequently, M&S I is the best medium for growth. These results are in line with the report that NAA and BAP together caused the highest callus induction %, FW, and DW for H. muticus.

In Vitro Propagation of H. albus L.

The M&S I medium showed no signs of organogenesis or somatic embryogenesis, while nonembryogenic calli on M&S II formed shoots after 5 weeks (Figure A). Afterward, rooting was observed to form a complete plantlet on the same media after another 2 weeks (Figure B). This is consistent with reports on the organogenesis of H. muticus under the combination of NAA and BAP. On the other hand, 8–12-week-old M&S III callus (Figure C) displayed various stages of embryoids (Figure D–F) that were tracked under a microscope. Next, another one-week, mature embryoids of H. albus L. were germinated on the same media. Also, 2–4 weeks later, differentiated plantlets were developed with simultaneous formation of shoots and roots on M&S III (Figure G,H). Consistent with the literature, the combination of NAA and Kin was successful for the embryogenesis of H. niger. Six-week-old rooted plantlets were washed and transplanted into perforated pots containing sterilized peat moss: vermiculite: sand (1:1:1). As a result, 3% of plants (Figure I) survived for 4 weeks. Similarly, the high mortality of acclimatized plants was previously reported due to biotic (microbial communities in soil) and abiotic (high light intensity, temperature, and low humidity) stresses. ,

Hairy Root (HR) Culture

Transformation and Induction of HR Culture

In this study, HR appeared at the cut sites of leaf explants (except for the negative control) of H. albus L. within 10 days after transformation (Figure ). HR was fast-growing and highly branching with a transformation frequency of 72.85% with A. rhizogenes strain LBA 9402. Compared to the previous report, HR of Indian H. albus was induced with the same strain after 14 days with a 35% transformation frequency. The growth curve and dynamics of 4-week-old HR of H. albus L. cultured on HF 1/2 MS liquid medium at 25 °C on a shaker (120 rpm) in darkness were evaluated (n = 3). Figure S3 shows that after 35 days, the highest FW and DW were 6.11 ± 0.453 and 0.183 ± 0.0136 g, respectively. This finding was similar to that documented for H. reticulatus HR. Also, GI (5.11 ± 0.45), RGR (0.146 ± 0.013), μ (0.05 ± 0.002 g/day), and dt (13.54 ± 0.62 days) of HR of H. albus L. were calculated from the growth curve.

Quantification of Atropine in Callus Cultures by HPLC

HPLC analysis was used to quantify the atropine content of H. albus L. callus cultures, expressed as μg/g DW. Figure S4A,B displays the response signal of a standard atropine that appeared at 3.775 min and its calibration curve, respectively. The results showed that M&S I callus gave the highest production of atropine, 320 ± 14.142 μg/g DW, followed by M&S II and III calli with a yield of 313 ± 32.527 and 312 ± 32.527 μg/g DW, respectively. Consequently, M&S I callus was selected to perform the elicitation procedures.

Unraveling Alkaloids of H. albus L. by GC-MS

A total of seven alkaloids were identified by GC-MS analysis in the aerial parts, roots, M&S I callus, and hairy roots (HRs) of H. albus L. (Figures , , and S5–S8 and Table ). The identified compounds are classified as tropane alkaloids (TAs) except for hygrine, which is a pyrrolidine alkaloid. Table revealed the presence of three similar TAs in all samples of H. albus L. Atropine (41.03, 82.46, 64.99, and 34.15%) and scopolamine (19.4, 10.45, 7.74, and 10.81%) are the predominant alkaloids detected in the aerial parts, roots, M&S I callus, and HRs of H. albus L., respectively. Similarly, the main components of flowers, fruits, leaves, stems, and roots of H. albus L. from Egypt were atropine (56.75, 49.44, 57.88, 70.05, and 74.6%) and scopolamine (35.61, 38.77, 33.94, 24.50, and 22.6%), respectively. On the other hand, flowers, seeds, leaves, stems, and roots of Moroccan plants were documented to be rich in atropine (66.3, 80.4, 70.2, 77.8, and 63.8%) and scopolamine (16.5, 6.4, 16.6, 9.1, and 4.2%), respectively. Furthermore, the predominance of atropine and scopolamine in the alkaloids of Egyptian H. muticus, H. desertorum, and H. boveanus was previously reported. Roots are the biosynthetic and storage sites for TAs, which explains why their highest content is detected in H. albus L. roots. Compared with the parent plant organs, callus culture produces low levels of TAs (atropine and scopolamine), consistent with the literature. This was attributed to the fact that cell differentiation is crucial for the biosynthesis of TAs. ,− In addition, the auxin added to the culture media can inhibit the enzyme activity that is necessary for TAs biosynthesis. , The present study indicated that callus culture is effective in yielding more atropine than the aerial parts. Additionally, M&S I callus and HRs of H. albus L. produced significant amounts of apoatropine (9.23 and 10.58%, respectively) compared with those found in the aerial parts and roots. This agrees with those reported about the higher production of atropine in H. aureus callus than in the parent plant. Type and concentration of phytohormones (auxin and cytokinin) are responsible for the differences in the TAs contents between callus culture and the parent plant. Phytohormones stimulate the production of ornithine, which is the starting point of the TAs biosynthesis. Furthermore, they may have an indirect influence on PMT (putrescine N-methyl transferase) and H6H (hyoscyamine 6β-hydroxylase), which are responsible for the formation of atropine and the conversion of atropine to scopolamine, respectively. The GC-MS technique detected that 6-hydroxyatropine is found in the aerial parts and roots in concentrations of 0.38 and 0.71%, respectively. Also, aposcopolamine (2.18%) and hygrine (0.57%) are the distinctive alkaloids found in the aerial parts and roots, respectively. According to the literature, apoatropine, 6-hydroxyatropine, and aposcopolamine were reported in the aerial parts and roots, while hygrine was detected only in the roots. In contrast to the present study, El-Shazly et al. reported the presence of 24 additional alkaloids, including norhygrine, littorine, and tropinone in H. albus L. Moreover, HRs of H. albus L. induced the production of unique alkaloids: tropinone (2.26%) and aposcopolamine (3.6%). Similarly, atropine, scopolamine, apoatropine, tropinone, and aposcopolamine were produced in the HRs of a German H. albus L. transformed by the A. rhizogenes strain R 1601. Also, HRs of H. albus L. succeeded in producing higher concentrations of scopolamine and aposcopolamine than the parent plant. This finding accords with the higher yield of scopolamine by HR cultures of H. muticus, H. reticulatus, and Datura candida hybrid. Meanwhile, this contrasts with the low content of scopolamine produced by the HRs of a German H. albus L.

Effect of Elicitor Application on Cell Growth

On the basis of the highest callus induction and fastest growth, M&S I callus was selected as the best callus medium for elicitation of growth. M&S I callus and HR cultures of H. albus L. were treated with ZnO (Z) and ZnO nanoparticles (ZN) at different concentrations: 50 (Z1, ZN1), 100 (Z2, ZN2), and 200 (Z3, ZN3) mg/L, respectively. In elicited callus cultures, Z3 and ZN2 exhibited the highest biomass (FW and DW) on day 25 of treatment, with values 1.06- and 1.8-fold higher than the control, respectively (Figure A,B). On the other hand, the control for HR cultures showed the highest FW and DW as compared to the elicited cultures (Figure C,D). Additionally, ZN showed a small decrease in growth compared to the control. Otherwise, there was a remarkable decrease in both FW and DW for Z-treated HR cultures of H. albus L. by increasing the ZnO concentration. This result accords with the effect of NPs of zinc oxide on the HR biomass of H. reticulatus.

Effect of Elicitor Application on Atropine Content

Based on the highest atropine production, M&S I callus was selected as the optimal callus medium for subsequent elicitation and biological testing. The atropine content of M&S I callus and HR cultures was estimated after their elicitation with Z and ZN at 50 (Z1, ZN1), 100 (Z2, ZN2), and 200 (Z3, ZN3) mg/L, respectively (Figure ). The Z1 and Z2-treated callus cultures of H. albus L. showed a noticeable increase in atropine content until day 15, then decreased gradually. Moreover, Z2 yielded the highest atropine content (1024.900 ± 317.7879 μg/g DW) after 15 days of treatment, which was 3.04-fold higher than that of the control (336.515 ± 68.55399 μg/g DW). Also, ZN1 (555.955 ± 111.49 μg/g DW) was the highest one among the other NPs in the production of atropine, 1.65-fold more than the control (336.515 ± 68.55 μg/g DW), after 15 days of callus culture treatment (Figures A and S9). On the other hand, Z2 and ZN1 showed the highest content of atropine (8508.09 ± 144.57 and 12610 ± 2645.64 μg/g DW, respectively) in HR tissue of H. albus L. with 6-fold and 8.9-fold more than the control (1416.76 ± 445.955 μg/g DW), respectively (Figures B and S10). According to the literature, ZnO nanoparticles (100 mg/L) were the most effective for TAs production in the HRs of H. reticulatus, and the result was due to the highest expression of the H6H gene. Additionally, the media of Z2 and ZN1 elicited HR cultures that accumulated the highest amounts of atropine (2.239 ± 0.599 and 0.74 ± 0.37 μg/mL, respectively), with 5.18-fold and 1.687-fold higher than the control (0.432 ± 0.093 μg/mL), respectively (Figures C and S10). This is the first report on the use of ZnO (Z) as an elicitor in Solanaceous plants. Furthermore, the elicitation of H. albus L. with ZnO nanoparticles (ZN) and the comparison of the effects of ZnO and its nanoparticles have not yet been studied.

Antibacterial and Antifungal Activities of the Alkaloidal Extracts of H. albus L.

Alkaloids have previously been assigned as antimicrobial agents for their capability to disrupt cell membranes, suppress enzyme activity, and interfere with protein, DNA, and RNA production. To assess the antibacterial and antifungal activities of H. albus L. alkaloidal extracts, a well diffusion assay was performed. The results demonstrated moderate antibacterial activity of all tested extracts. The aerial parts extract exhibited the highest inhibitory zones measuring 16.1 ± 1.8, 17.6 ± 2.3, and 15.1 ± 1 mm against S. aureus, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa, respectively. Positive control gentamicin was used to assess antibacterial activity. Additionally, aerial parts of H. albus L. showed inhibitory activity against C. albicans, with an inhibition zone measuring 16.7 ± 1.4 mm compared to 21.1 ± 2.2 mm of the positive control tioconazole (Figure A). Similarly, Kebaili et al. reported the antibacterial activity of H. albus L. extracts with superior activity to the alkaloidal extracts. Thus, tropane alkaloids offer a new horizon for the development of therapeutic compounds aimed at combating pathogenic infection.

Antivirulence Activity of H. albus L. Extracts

Biofilm-forming pathogens are the leading cause of infectious diseases. Biofilm-embedded bacterial cells are protected from antibiotics and immune cells, which are more challenging to treat. The ability of H. albus L. alkaloidal extracts to interfere with P. aeruginosa and C. albicans biofilm formation was investigated. The highest biofilm inhibition percentage against P. aeruginosa biofilm was observed in M&S I callus extract (85 ± 3.7%), followed by the extracts of aerial parts (49.5 ± 18.9%) and hairy roots (44.76 ± 16%). Moreover, all H. albus L. alkaloidal extracts remarkably disrupted C. albicans biofilm formation (90 ± 7.3, 90 ± 5.2, and 86.5 ± 6.8%, for hairy roots, M&S I callus, and aerial parts, respectively). However, atropine did not exhibit any biofilm inhibitory activity against either P. aeruginosa or C. albicans biofilm, which excludes its role in the biofilm inhibitory activity of H. albus L. extracts (Figure A). Consistent with the current study, numerous plant alkaloids have been reported to exhibit potent biofilm inhibitory activity.

To establish its pathogenesis, P. aeruginosa utilizes an array of virulence factors, including pyocyanin, protease, siderophores, motility, and toxins. An antivirulence approach has been proposed as an alternative strategy to overcome bacterial antibiotic resistance. Pyocyanin is a potent bioactive pigment that induces strong oxidative stress in host cells. The amount of pyocyanin produced by P. aeruginosa in the presence of H. albus L. extracts was quantified. Pyocyanin was dramatically lost to approximately 88.25, 86.75, and 85.25% by hairy roots, M&S I callus, and aerial parts, respectively, with no superior activity for any of the extracts (Figure B). Atropine showed negligible pyocyanin inhibitory activity (9.5 ± 4.2%). Interestingly, there are no previous reports on the antipyocyanin activity of the alkaloidal extract of H. albus L. Some pyrrolidine and piperidine alkaloids have been reported to suppress pyocyanin production. , Next, swarming motility, a specialized bacterial movement, was investigated. Swarming zones were significantly inhibited in the presence of the aerial parts extract, showing a reduction of the swarming zone diameter to 1/6th of the control (Figure C). Regarding the effect of H. albus L. extracts on protease production, supernatant of P. aeruginosa culture was added to wells of skim milk agar plates, and the zone of clearance was measured. The results showed no significant difference in the diameter of the clearance zone either in the absence or presence of H. albus L. extracts, indicating that H. albus L. extracts and atropine have no effect on protease production (Figure D). Regarding the vehicle control group, in which DMSO was used at the same concentration as in the working solutions, no inhibitory activity was observed on any of the assessed virulence factors. It is noteworthy that there were no previous reports on the antivirulence activity of the alkaloidal extracts of H. albus L.

Conclusions

This research presents an efficient protocol for the in vitro propagation of H. albus L. For the first time, callus and hairy root cultures of H. albus L. have been established and may serve as alternative biofactories to produce tropane alkaloids. Furthermore, zinc oxide (Z) and zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZNPs) are promising and efficient methods for increasing the production of these compounds. Future studies could focus on the genetic and metabolic engineering of H. albus hairy roots, optimization of elicitation strategies using elicitors either individually or through the investigation of their synergistic effects, as well as scale-up production in bioreactor systems to achieve sustainable and industrial-level production of tropane alkaloids. Ultimately, the alkaloidal extracts of H. albus L. exhibited remarkable inhibitory effects on biofilm formation, pyocyanin production, and swarming motility. As a result, H. albus L. can act as a natural candidate for the development of antimicrobial and antivirulence agents. A limitation of this study is that the antivirulence and antibiofilm activities were evaluated using a crude extract, which precludes identification of the specific active constituents and their underlying mechanisms of action. Nevertheless, assessment of the extract yielded promising inhibitory effects, suggesting a beneficial activity of its components. Further studies will focus on individual testing of extract components that would provide more precise insights into the potency, contribution, and mechanistic roles of each constituent.
