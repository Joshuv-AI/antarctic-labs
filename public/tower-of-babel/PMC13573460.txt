How glycemic variability trajectories impact clinical outcomes in critically ill patients with severe pneumonia: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Xiang-yu Zhang, Ying-qian Zhu, Yuan-qiu Guo, Yi-yang Wang, Jing-chao Luo, Huan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile glycemic variability (GV) affects outcomes in critically ill patients, its temporal patterns and prognostic implications in severe pneumonia remain unclear. This study aimed to identify distinct GV trajectory patterns and their associations with clinical outcomes.MethodsThis prospective cohort study enrolled 315 patients with severe pneumonia admitted to our intensive care unit (ICU) at Taicang Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine, from January 2021 to December 2024. Using group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM), we analyzed the coefficient of variation (CV) trajectories during the first 5 ICU days. We compared clinical characteristics between trajectory groups, conducted exploratory landmark survival analysis, and assessed the performance of clinical parameters for identifying trajectory classification.ResultsGBTM identified two distinct GV trajectories: Dynamic Variant (DV, 12.1%) characterized by initially high variability that gradually decreased but remained persistently elevated, and Stable Control (SC, 87.9%) with consistently low variability. While early outcomes were comparable, after day 14, the DV group demonstrated significantly higher mortality (70.3% vs. 60.0%, p = 0.034). DV patients had higher procalcitonin (PCT, 2.35 vs. 0.76 ng/ml, p = 0.030) and glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c, 7.1% vs. 6.3%, p = 0.002) levels on ICU admission, despite similar disease severity scores and other clinical parameters. Combined prediction using PCT and HbA1c showed good performance in identifying trajectories (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve: 0.87, 95% Confidence Interval: 0.80–0.94).ConclusionDistinct GV trajectories in severe pneumonia patients are associated with differential subsequent mortality risks, with admission PCT and HbA1c levels serving as predictors of trajectory group classification.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-04044-y.

Introduction

Severe pneumonia remains a leading critical illness in intensive care units (ICUs), associated with high global morbidity and mortality rates and presenting significant therapeutic challenges [1, 2]. In patients with severe pneumonia, glycemic homeostasis disruption is very common, attributed to multiple factors including advanced age, multiple comorbidities (particularly diabetes), stress response, insulin resistance, and therapeutic interventions such as corticosteroids or parenteral nutrition [3, 4]. Previous studies have demonstrated that abnormal glycemia, whether hyperglycemia or hypoglycemia, significantly correlates with unfavorable outcomes in severe pneumonia [5, 6]. While traditional research has primarily focused on single time-point or mean glucose levels, glycemic variability (GV) has emerged as a potentially more crucial indicator [7], serving both as a marker of disease severity and a mediator of adverse outcomes through pathophysiological mechanisms including oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and dysregulated inflammatory and immune responses [3, 8].

The recovery from severe pneumonia typically involves transitions in both metabolic and nutritional patterns, during which glucose levels fluctuate in response to disease progression, nutritional support, and therapeutic interventions [9, 10]. While early GV (within 48 h of ICU admission) has been linked to increased 28-day mortality and prolonged ICU stay [11], such single time-point assessment may inadequately capture the dynamic glucose alterations throughout the disease course. Recent evidence suggests that time in targeted glucose range independently correlates with 28-day mortality in ICU patients [12], indicating that dynamic GV trajectory patterns may offer greater clinical utility than single time-point measurements. We hypothesize that distinct GV trajectories exist in severe pneumonia patients with differential prognostic implications. Through this prospective cohort study, we aim to characterize five-day ICU GV trajectories and their clinical impacts, providing insights into optimizing glycemic management strategies for this population.

Methods

Populations

This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Number: 2020-006) and conducted in a 20-bed general ICU of Taicang Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine, located in eastern China. During the study period (January 2021-December 2024), adult patients with severe pneumonia [13] were prospectively enrolled after written informed consent was obtained from the patients or their legal representatives (if the patients were unable to make independent decisions). Exclusion criteria were: age under 18 years, length of ICU stay less than 5 days, pregnancy, chronic pancreatitis or history of pancreatic surgery, confirmed hematological or solid malignancy, severe hepatobiliary disease or liver dysfunction, Cushing’s syndrome, and recent exposure to high-dose glucocorticoid therapy.

Data collection

Blood glucose levels were measured at least four times daily, combining arterial and capillary measurements. Additional measurements were performed based on clinical necessity, particularly for patients receiving insulin therapy. All therapeutic decisions, including anti-infection strategies, supportive care, nutritional support, and glycemic control protocols, were determined by the attending physicians and nursing team according to current clinical practice guidelines. We prospectively recorded demographic data, comorbidities, clinical parameters, laboratory findings (including procalcitonin [PCT], high-sensitivity C-reactive protein [hs-CRP], and glycated hemoglobin A1c [HbA1c], etc.), therapeutic interventions, and patient outcomes. Disease severity was assessed using the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II score and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score upon ICU admission.

Definitions

GV was characterized using three complementary metrics [14]: the Coefficient of Variation (CV), calculated as the standard deviation divided by the mean glucose level and expressed as a percentage; Mean Blood Glucose (MBG), defined as the arithmetic mean of all daily glucose measurements; and Largest Amplitude of Glycemic Excursions (LAGE), representing the difference between the highest and lowest glucose values within each 24-hour period. The primary outcome was defined as hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included length of mechanical ventilation (LOMV), length of ICU stay (LOICU), length of hospital stay (LOHS), and the requirement for tracheostomy or continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT).

Group-based trajectory modeling

Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM), an advanced application of finite mixture modeling [7], was implemented to characterize CV trajectories during the initial 5-day ICU stay. This method enables identification of distinct patient subgroups exhibiting similar longitudinal patterns in glycemic CV. Prior to analysis, CV measurements were standardized to normalize the distribution. Generally, 120 to 150 participants support stable GBTM for 2 to 3 distinct trajectory groups with high entropy, with each subgroup having a minimum of 25 to 30 members to prevent overfitting. Model selection utilized the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), where lower values denoted superior model fit. Forward classification determined the optimal trajectory count and polynomial order (linear, quadratic, or cubic) for CV modeling. To ensure clinical relevance, groups were constrained to comprise at least 5% of the study population, with posterior probability thresholds of ≥ 0.70 establishing group membership. The temporal evolution of CV patterns was visualized through standardized trajectory curves.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented, based on their distribution, as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range], while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Inter-group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables, and chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Violin plots were generated to visualize daily inter-group differences in three GV metrics. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method, with landmark analysis at day 14 to evaluate early (≤ 14 days) and late (> 14 days) survival differences. Survival curve differences were assessed using the log-rank test, with survival rates and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) calculated for both time periods. For trajectory prediction, variables showing significant differences on day 1 were selected as candidates. When two or more candidates were identified, both logistic regression and Support Vector Machine (SVM) models were developed for integrated prediction. The predictive performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, including area under the ROC curve (AUROC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) [15]. DeLong’s test was used to compare AUROCs between models. Optimal cutoff points were determined using Youden’s index. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patients

During the study period, 315 patients with severe pneumonia were enrolled (Fig. 1). The characteristics of overall patients are presented in Table 1. The cohort comprised 213 (67.6%) males with a median age of 75 (IQR: 67–85) years, and 127 (40.3%) patients had pre-existing diabetes at baseline, of whom 52 (40.9%) were receiving long-term insulin therapy prior to ICU admission. Upon ICU admission, 267 (84.8%) patients presented with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), 116 (36.8%) met the diagnostic criteria for acute respiratory distress syndrome, 110 (34.9%) required invasive mechanical ventilation, and 69 (21.9%) developed septic shock. The median APACHE II and SOFA scores at admission were 22 (IQR: 18–26) and 5 (IQR: 4–7), respectively. Initial laboratory findings revealed PCT levels of 0.81 ng/mL (IQR: 0.33–3.41), hs-CRP of 70 mg/L (IQR: 20–138), HbA1c of 6.4% (IQR: 5.6–7.5), and initial glucose levels of 9.8 mmol/L (IQR: 7.4–13.3). Enteral nutrition (EN) was initiated at a median of 1 (IQR: 0–2) days after admission, and 218 (69.2%) patients received parenteral lipid emulsion support during ICU hospitalization. The median lengths of ICU and hospital stay were 10 (IQR: 7–18) and 15 (IQR: 9–25) days, respectively, with an in-hospital mortality rate of 34.6% (109 patients).

Identification of GV trajectories

GBTM identified two distinct glycemic CV trajectories: a Stable Control (SC) trajectory (n = 277, 87.9%) characterized by consistently low CV levels, and a Dynamic Variant (DV) trajectory (n = 38, 12.1%) showing initially high CV that gradually decreased but remained elevated compared to the SC group (Table 1 and Fig. 2). Beyond CV, two other glycemic variability parameters, MBG and LAGE, demonstrated similar inter-group differences consistently throughout the 5-day observation period (Fig. 3 and Table S1). Compared to the SC group, patients with the DV trajectory showed no significant differences in demographics, underlying comorbidities, disease severity scores, or most laboratory parameters, including inflammatory biomarkers (Table 1).However, the DV group exhibited significantly higher levels of PCT (2.35 [IQR: 0.51–4.50] vs. 0.76 [IQR: 0.31–3.17] ng/ml, p = 0.030) and HbA1c (7.1 [IQR: 6.3–8.2] vs. 6.3 [IQR: 5.5–7.4] %, p = 0.002), and experienced delayed initiation of EN (2 [IQR: 1–3] vs. 1 [IQR: 0–2] days, p < 0.001) (Table 1). Subgroup analysis stratified by diabetes status revealed that inter-group differences in GV metrics were more pronounced and sustained in non-diabetic patients compared to diabetic patients (Figure S1).

GV trajectories and clinical outcomes

No significant differences were observed between the two trajectory groups in terms of LOMV, LOICU, LOHS, tracheostomy and CRRT rates (Table 1). Hospital mortality was 17 (44.7%) in the DV group and 92 (33.2%) in the SC group, with no significant difference between groups (p = 0.203). However, survival curve analysis revealed more nuanced temporal patterns (Fig. 4). Landmark analysis on day 14 demonstrated distinct survival characteristics: during the first 14 days after ICU admission, in this exploratory analysis, mortality rates were comparable between the DV and SC groups (14.9% [95% CI: 2.9%-77.8%] vs. 32.5% [95% CI: 22.4%-47.2%], p = 0.957). Nevertheless, among patients who survived and remained in hospita beyond day 14, the DV group exhibited significantly higher mortality compared to the SC group (60.0% [95% CI: 37.9%-94.9%] vs. 70.3% [95% CI: 59.4%-83.3%], p = 0.034). Comparison of baseline characteristics between patients experiencing early (≤ 14 days) and late (> 14 days) mortality revealed no significant differences in key demographic, comorbidity, or disease severity variables (Table S2).

Prediction for GV trajectories

Given the significant differences in PCT and HbA1c levels between trajectory groups on ICU day 1, four predictive models were developed to identify DV trajectory membership. The ROC curves (Figure S2) demonstrated varying predictive performances: (1) PCT alone yielded an AUROC of 0.61 (95% CI: 0.53–0.69); (2) HbA1c alone showed an AUROC of 0.66 (95% CI: 0.57–0.74); and (3) the combination of PCT and HbA1c using logistic regression achieved an AUROC of 0.64 (95% CI: 0.55–0.73). Pairwise comparisons revealed no significant differences in discriminative ability among these three approaches (all p > 0.05). However, (4) the SVM model incorporating both PCT and HbA1c significantly outperformed all other approaches with an AUROC of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.80–0.94), showing significantly higher AUROC compared to PCT alone (difference = 0.26, p < 0.001), HbA1c alone (difference = 0.22, p < 0.001), and the logistic model (difference = 0.23, p < 0.001). This SVM model demonstrated robust diagnostic characteristics with sensitivity of 74% (95% CI: 60–88), specificity of 94% (95% CI: 91–97), PPV of 62% (95% CI: 48–76), and NPV of 96% (95% CI: 94–99) (Table S3).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this represents the first prospective investigation of GV trajectory patterns and their prognostic implications in severe pneumonia patients. Through GBTM, we identified two distinct GV trajectories, revealed their associations with hospital outcomes, and found that admission PCT and HbA1c levels may facilitate early identification of patients in the high-risk trajectory group.

Studies have extensively explored the relationship between dysglycemia and outcomes in pneumonia. Large prospective studies have shown that hyperglycemia is common in community-acquired pneumonia patients upon admission, regardless of prior diabetes history [9], while hypoglycemia can also occur, particularly in diabetic patients, with both conditions associated with poor outcomes [16]. During hospitalization, glycemic fluctuations, influenced by primary illness, systemic inflammation, metabolic derangements, therapeutic interventions (glucocorticoids, catecholamines, nutritional support), stress response, and comorbidities [17–19], may be more clinically relevant than mean glucose levels. In diabetic patients with CAP or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, a retrospective study found that while strict glycemic control might delay recovery, GV significantly prolonged hospitalization, suggesting that glucose fluctuations better reflect energy homeostasis than absolute levels [4]. This was further validated by a Korean study demonstrating early GV as an independent predictor of 28-day mortality and extended ICU stay [11].

In this study, we extended the observation window to 5 days to cover the metabolic transition from hyperacute to acute phase in critically ill patients. Using GBTM, we identified two clinically meaningful GV trajectories: approximately seven-eighths of patients belonged to the SC trajectory group, maintaining relatively low GV levels, while the remaining one-eighth exhibited a DV trajectory, characterized by extremely high initial GV that, although declining, remained consistently elevated compared to the SC group. The absence of significant differences in initial ICU glucose levels between groups (differing from part of previous reports [10], possibly attributed not only to our cohort’s older age and higher diabetes prevalence but also to the limitation of single-point measurements in capturing underlying metabolic regulatory capacity) contrasted with significant differences in day-one mean glucose exposure and amplitude. While overall hospital mortality showed no significant difference, survival curves diverged significantly after 14 days of ICU stay, suggesting that the impact of GV, reflecting underlying energy metabolic dysregulation, requires time to manifest on clinical outcomes. However, the smaller proportion of patients in the high GV trajectory and diminishing sample sizes over time, these survival findings represent exploratory observations requiring cautious interpretation.

Although patients in the DV group represented a minority, they exhibited distinct clinical characteristics warranting more proactive metabolic management, making early trajectory identification at ICU admission of practical clinical value. Two admission-day indicators were specifically associated with trajectory classification: PCT and HbA1c. Notably, conventional inflammatory markers—white blood cell, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and hs-CRP—showed no significant inter-group differences, whereas PCT was selectively elevated in the DV group, suggesting that PCT may capture a dimension of systemic inflammatory–metabolic stress beyond general inflammatory burden. Prior evidence supports this: PCT is correlated with insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome [20], and remains positively associated with mean blood glucose and GV in critically ill patients even after adjusting for disease severity and therapeutic confounders [21]. HbA1c, in turn, reflects pre-admission glycemic regulatory capacity, with higher values indicating greater vulnerability to glucose dysregulation under the metabolic demands of critical illness [22]. Together, these two indicators—reflecting acute inflammatory–metabolic stress (PCT) and chronic glycemic reserve (HbA1c)—exhibit satisfactory discriminative ability for trajectory classification in the SVM model. When available on admission, PCT and HbA1c levels may enable early identification of patients at risk for the DV trajectory, facilitating proactive metabolic management. However, as the model was derived from the complete study dataset without external validation, these findings should be regarded as exploratory, and multicenter validation is required before clinical application.

Additionally, understanding therapeutic interventions to reduce GV is crucial. A small-scale study identified four major factors associated with increased GV: glucocorticoid administration (activating gluconeogenesis and insulin resistance) and epinephrine therapy (promoting hepatic glycogenolysis), exogenous insulin use (increasing hypoglycemia risk), and imbalanced nutritional support (energy excess and glucose overload) [23]. Our study also demonstrated that patients in the DV trajectory group experienced delayed initiation of EN. Potential strategies to mitigate adverse GV trajectories may include: implementation of precision insulin protocols incorporating continuous glucose monitoring and artificial intelligence algorithms; optimization of glucocorticoid administration while avoiding bolus dosing; judicious use of epinephrine; refined nutritional support strategies incorporating formula composition and circadian-aligned delivery; metabolic homeostasis monitoring, particularly in diabetic patients; and exploration of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists or dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors [23–25]. These interventions warrant further investigation, especially in severe pneumonia patients, to establish their efficacy in GV management and outcome improvement.

Our study had several limitations. First, as a single-center prospective study, despite our relatively large sample size, the generalizability of our findings requires validation in different healthcare settings and patient populations. Second, our requirement for a minimum 5-day ICU stay to observe complete GV trajectories excluded patients with shorter stays, including both early-discharge mild cases and critically severe cases who died within 5 days, potentially introducing selection bias that limits generalizability to these patient populations. Third, although we extended the observation window to 5 days and captured detailed glucose measurements, the use of intermittent point-of-care testing rather than continuous glucose monitoring might have underestimated the true extent of GV. Fourth, while we identified PCT and HbA1c as predictive markers, our model did not incorporate other potential metabolic indicators such as stress hormones, inflammatory cytokines, or detailed insulin resistance parameters, which might have provided additional insights into the underlying mechanisms. Fifth, despite our prospective design and comprehensive data collection, some confounding factors such as pre-ICU glycemic management strategies, detailed nutritional intake patterns, and medication timing could not be fully controlled. Finally, our analysis of interventional strategies was observational in nature, and the proposed therapeutic approaches need to be validated through dedicated interventional trials.

Conclusion

In severe pneumonia patients, we identified two distinct GV trajectories: a Dynamic Variant pattern with persistently elevated variability and a Stable Control pattern with consistently low variability, with the former associated with higher mortality risk beyond day 14. Admission PCT and HbA1c levels may help identify patients with high-risk GV patterns, though these findings require validation in larger prospective studies.
