Multivariate majorization of continuous statistical experiments
Erkka Haapasalo

Abstract
. We derive sufficient and almost necessary conditions for large sample and catalytic majorization between finite statistical experiments over standard Borel sample spaces. This work generalizes previous results, on one hand, in the bivariate case and, on the other hand, in the multivariate discrete (or, rather, finite) case, i.e., matrix majorization. We derive multivariate generalizations of the bivariate Rényi relative entropies and show that inequalities involving these multivariate Rényi divergences characterize large-sample and catalytic majorization of finite statistical experiments. As our methods are real-algebraic in nature, this work demonstrates that large deviation techniques are not the only option available to derive conditions for large sample majorization even in the case of more general sample spaces of the experiments. We also show that all general multivariate divergences, i.e., multivariate extensive and monotone maps of finite statistical experiments, can be expressed through barycentres over the set of multivariate Rényi divergences. We also show that we may characterize the optimal conversion rate of a statistical experiment into another using the multivariate Rényi divergences.

Introduction

Statistical experiments
are basic notions in statistics and information theory. They are collections of information from a phenomenon, e.g., weather, physical experiment, or a medical study. Information on a sample space
(X,\mathcal{A})
, a measurable space, comes in the form of probability measures
p:\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
, so statistical experiments are collections
P=\{p^{\vartheta}\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}
of probability measures on some sample space and some fixed collection
\Theta
of settings. It is natural to say that a statistical experiment
P=\{p^{\vartheta}\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}
is more informative than another statistical experiment
Q=\{q^{\vartheta}\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}
if there is a stochastic operator
T
(modeled as a Markov kernel) such that
Tp^{\vartheta}=q^{\vartheta}
for all
\vartheta\in\Theta

[
3
,
4
,
25
]
. Note that the experiment
P
contains at least the information of
Q
as we may produce tha data of
Q
out of

using a single data processing operation. In this situation, we say that
P

majorizes

Q
and denote
P\succeq Q
.

Having many copies of the data of the statistical experiment or an auxiliary or ‘catalytic’ experiment in our disposal makes majorization easier to characterize. To this end, given statistical experiments
P=\{p^{\vartheta}\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}
and
Q=\{q^{\vartheta}\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}
, we denote

P\boxtimes Q:=\big\{p^{\vartheta}\otimes q^{\vartheta}\big\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}

where
p\otimes q
is the product measure of measures
p
and
q
(defined on the product
\sigma
-algebra); we use this notation instead of the more standard

for internal cohesion throughout this work. We say that
P
majorizes
Q

in large samples
if
P^{\boxtimes n}\succeq Q^{\boxtimes n}
whenever
n\in\mathbb{N}
is large enough. Furthermore, we say that
P

catalytically majorizes

Q
if there exists a catalytic experiment

such that

. Large-sample majorization implies catalytic majorization
[
8
]
but the converse is not true
[
10
]
. The exact difference between these two modes of majorization is still quite unknown in general though.

In this work we focus on

[

]
. This means that the fixed set

of settings is finite. In the special case
\Theta=\{1,2\}
, majorization is also called

. Relative majorization and conditions for large-sample relative majorization were exhaustively studied by Mu

in
[
19
]
. The methods employed therein are based on large deviation techniques. However, these techniques, although useful in the relative case, do not readily lend themselves for the study of

where

with finite probability distributions

\big(p^{(k)}(x)\big)_{k,x}
which we multiply with a stochastic matrix modeling a finite Markov kernel

. We must point out however, that we have to place a certain boundedness condition on the finite statistical experiments

that we study: all the measures

We find that

majorizes

in large samples (as well as catalytically) if
D(P)>D(Q)
for
multivariate Rényi divergences

generalizing the bivariate Rényi relative entropies

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\max_{1\leq\ell\leq d}\alpha_{\ell}-1}\log{\mathbb{E}_{p^{(d)}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{dp^{(1)}}{dp^{(d)}}}}\right)^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{dp^{(d-1)}}{dp^{(d)}}}}\right)^{\alpha_{d-1}}}}\right]}

We also address the problem of characterizing the optimal rate of transforming a finite statistical experiment

We apply a

We demonstrate that these maps give rise to the multivariate Rényi divergences discussed above. We are thus able to derive sufficient (and almost necessary) conditions for large-sample and catalytic multivariate majorization in the form of inequalities between the values of these divergences on the two finite statistical experiments we are studying. Background

We use the following basic notations throughout this work:

•

•

\mathbb{R}_{>0}
: the set of all strictly positive real numbers.

•

\mathbb{R}_{+}
: The set of non-negative real numbers seen as a preordered semiring (see the definition of this notion later) with the natural addition, multiplication, and (total) order

.

•

above but with the opposite order

\Leftrightarrow

.
\mathbb{R}_{+}

.

•

.

2.1.
Preordered semirings and a Vergleichsstellensatz

Preordered semirings are essentially otherwise like commutative rings with the exception that the elements of a (preordered) semiring typically do not have additive inverses and there is a preorder which respects the algebraic structure. To formalize this, a preordered semiring is an ordered tuple
(S,0,1,+,\cdot,\trianglerighteq)
where
S\neq\emptyset
,
(S,0,+)
and
(S,1,\cdot)
are commutative semigroups where the multiplication distributes over the addition, and
\trianglerighteq
is a preorder (a reflexive and transitive binary relation) on
S
such that

x\trianglerighteq y\ \Rightarrow\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}x+a\trianglerighteq y+a,\\
xa\trianglerighteq ya,\end{array}}}\right.\quad\forall a\in S.

For a preordered semiring
S
and
x,y\in S
, we denote
x\sim y
whenever there are
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in S
such that

x\trianglerighteq z_{1}\trianglelefteq z_{2}\trianglerighteq\cdots\trianglelefteq z_{n}\trianglerighteq y.

We say that a preordered semiring
S
is of
polynomial growth
when there is
u\in S
such that

x\trianglerighteq y\ \Rightarrow\ \exists k\in\mathbb{N}:\ yu^{k}\trianglerighteq x.

Such an element
u
is called a
power universal
. A preordered semiring
S
is
zerosumfree
if

x+y=0\ \Rightarrow\ x=0=y.

We say that a preordered semiring
S
is a
preordered semidomain
if

xy=0\ \Rightarrow\ x=0\ {\rm or}\ y=0

and

0\trianglerighteq x\trianglerighteq 0\ \Rightarrow\ x=0.

Given two preordered semirings
(S,0_{S},1_{S},+,\cdot,\trianglerighteq_{S})
and
(T,0_{T},1_{T},+,\cdot,\trianglerighteq_{T})
, we say that a map
\Phi:S\to T
is a
monotone homomorphism
if

•

\Phi(0_{S})=0_{T}
,
\Phi(1_{S})=1_{T}
,

•

\Phi(x+y)=\Phi(x)+\Phi(y)
for all
x,y\in S
,

•

\Phi(xy)=\Phi(x)\Phi(y)
for all
x,y\in S
, and

•

x\trianglerighteq_{S}y

\Rightarrow

\Phi(x)\trianglerighteq_{T}\Phi(y)
.

We say that a monotone homomorphism
\Phi:S\to T
is
degenerate
if
x\trianglerighteq y
implies
\Phi(x)=\Phi(y)
. Otherwise
\Phi
is
nondegenerate
. We are primarily interested in monotone homomorphisms
\Phi:S\to\mathbb{K}
where
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}\}
. Given a monotone homomorphism
\Phi:S\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we say that a map
\Delta:S\to\mathbb{R}
is a
monotone derivation at
\Phi
if
x\trianglerighteq y
implies
\Delta(x)\geq\Delta(y)
,
\Delta(x+y)=\Delta(x)+\Delta(y)
for all
x,y\in S
, and

\Delta(xy)=\Delta(x)\Phi(y)+\Phi(x)\Delta(y)\qquad{\rm(Leibniz\ rule)}

for all
x,y\in S
. We are only interested in monotone derivations at degenerate homomorphisms
\Phi
in which case we may view
\Phi
as a monotone homomorphism of
S
to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
or, equivalently,
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}
.

Definition 1
.

We say that a preordered semiring
S
is
of degeneracy
d
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
if there is a surjective homomorphism
\|\cdot\|:S\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}\cup\{(0,\ldots,0)\}
with trivial kernel such that

a\trianglerighteq b\ \Rightarrow\ \|a\|=\|b\|,\quad\|a\|=\|b\|\ \Rightarrow\ a\sim b.

In this situation, we denote the component homomorphisms of
\|\cdot\|
by
\|\cdot\|_{(k)}
for
k=1,\ldots,d
.

Let us briefly note that, if
S
is of degeneracy
d
, we have
x\sim y
if and only if
\|x\|=\|y\|
. Naturally,
\|x\|=\|y\|

\Rightarrow

x\sim y
. For the converse, assume that
x\sim y
and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in S
are such that
x\trianglerighteq z_{1}\trianglelefteq z_{2}\trianglerighteq\cdots\trianglelefteq z_{n}\trianglerighteq y
. The first inequality in the chain implies
\|x\|=\|z_{1}\|
, the second implies
\|z_{1}\|=\|z_{2}\|
, and so on until
\|z_{n-1}\|=\|z_{n}\|
and
\|z_{n}\|=\|y\|
. Thus,

\|x\|=\|z_{1}\|=\|z_{2}\|=\cdots=\|z_{n-1}\|=\|z_{n}\|=\|y\|.

Definition 2
.

Let
S
be a preordered semiring of polynomial growth and of degeneracy
d
where we fix a power universal
u
and the vector-valued homomorphism
\|\cdot\|
of Definition
2
. We denote the set of all nondegenerate monotone homomorphisms
\Phi:S\to\mathbb{K}
with
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}\}
by
\Sigma(S,\mathbb{K})
and the set of all the monotone derivations
\Delta:S\to\mathbb{R}
at
\|\cdot\|_{(k)}
(
k=1,\ldots,d
) with
\Delta(u)=1
by
\mathfrak{D}^{k}(S)
. For
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}\}
, we define

\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathbb{K}):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{\log{\Phi(\cdot)}}{\log{\Phi(u)}}\,\middle|\,\Phi\in\Sigma(S,\mathbb{K})}}\right\}

as a set of maps
S\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}
. For
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, we define

\mathfrak{D}_{k}(S):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{S\setminus\{0\}\ni x\mapsto\frac{\Delta(x)}{\|x\|_{(k)}}\,\middle|\,\Delta\in\mathfrak{D}^{k}(S)}}\right\}.

Finally, we define the
test spectrum
through

\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(S):=\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathbb{R}_{+})\cup\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op})\cup\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+})\cup\mathfrak{D}(S,\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op})\cup\mathfrak{D}_{1}(S)\cup\cdots\cup\mathfrak{D}_{d}(S).

In
[
11
]
, the test spectrum was defined as the union of the sets
\Sigma(S,\mathbb{K})
with
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}\}
and
\mathfrak{D}^{k}(S)
for
k=1,\ldots,d
. However, it is useful for our future discussion to define the test spectrum as the set
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(S)
defined in Definition
2
. The following central result (
Vergleichsstellensatz
) for this work is Theorem 8.6 in
[
11
]
:

Theorem 3
.

Let
S
be a zerosumfree preordered semidomain of polynomial growth and of degeneracy
d
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
. We fix a power universal
u\in S
and the vector-valued homomorphism
\|\cdot\|
of Definition
1
to define the test spectrum
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(S)
. Suppose that
x,y\in S\setminus\{0\}
are such that
\|x\|=\|y\|
. If

(1)

\Delta(x)>\Delta(y)\qquad\forall\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(S),

then

(a)

x^{n}u^{k}\trianglerighteq y^{n}u^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
when
n\in\mathbb{N}
is sufficiently large and

(b)

xz\trianglerighteq yz
for some
z\in S\setminus\{0\}
which can be chosen according to

z=u^{k}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}x^{\ell}y^{n-\ell}

for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
and

sufficiently large.

If
x
is a power universal, we may omit the appearance of
u^{k}
in items (a) and (b) above. Conversely, if condition (a) or (b) above holds, then the inequalities in (
1
) hold non-strictly.

Before going on, let us note that any power universal
u\in S
has
\|u\|=(1,\ldots,1)
as long as
\|\cdot\|
satisfies the conditions of Definition
1
. This is naturally equivalent with
u\sim 1
. To see this, assume that

are such that

, so that there is

such that

. Since

, we have

and, since

2.2.
Sample spaces and stochastic maps

We are concentrating on standard Borel measurable spaces in this work. Recall that a measurable space
(X,\mathcal{A})
(i.e., a non-empty set
X
with a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
of subsets of
X
) is standard Borel if it is
\sigma
-isomorphic with
\big(E,\mathcal{B}(E)\big)
where
E
is a Borel measurable subset of a Polish space
P
(i.e., a separable completely metrizable topological space) and
\mathcal{B}(E)
is the restriction of the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(P)
onto
E
;
\mathcal{B}(E):=\{E\cap B\,|\,B\in\mathcal{B}(P)\}
. Most of our work is completely applicable to more general measurable spaces. However, stochastic maps between standard Borel spaces have a particularly nice form as they are discribed by Markov kernels which we subsequently discuss. Another reason is a bit more subtle: We subsequently have to discuss a proper class of tuples of finite positive measures over
all
standard Borel measurable spaces. However, we shall build a semiring out of this class by identifying
\sigma
-isomorphic tuples with each other. As any standard Borel measurable space is
\sigma
-isomorphic with a subspace of
\big(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\big)
, the semiring is a proper set.

In the sequel, we need to discuss certain sample spaces composed of two component sample spaces in order to define the binary operations (sum and multiplication) that we need in order to apply Theorem
3
. Let
(X,\mathcal{A})
and
(Y,\mathcal{B})
be standard Borel measurable spaces. We denote by
\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}
the coarsest
\sigma
-algebra on
X\times Y
which contains the product sets
A\times B
,
A\in\mathcal{A}
,
B\in\mathcal{B}
. Given measures
\mu:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\nu:\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}
, we denote their product measure by
\mu\otimes\nu
. We also denote by
X\sqcup Y
the disjoint union of
X
and
Y
which we equip with the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{A}\oplus\mathcal{B}
which is the coarsest
\sigma
-algebra on
X\sqcup Y
containing the disjoint unions
A\sqcup B
,
A\in\mathcal{A}
and
B\in\mathcal{B}
. Given measures
\mu:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\nu:\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define the measure
\mu\oplus\nu:\mathcal{A}\oplus\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}
through
(\mu\oplus\nu)(C)=\mu(C\cap X)+\nu(C\cap Y)
for all
C\in\mathcal{A}\oplus\mathcal{B}
. Naturally, also
(X\times Y,\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B})
and
(X\sqcup Y,\mathcal{A}\oplus\mathcal{B})
are standard Borel measurable spaces.

Let
(X,\mathcal{A})
and
(Y,\mathcal{B})
be standard Borel measurable spaces. We say that
T(\cdot|\cdot):\mathcal{B}\times X\to[0,1]
is a
Markov kernel
if
T(\cdot|x):\mathcal{B}\to[0,1]
is a probability measure for all
x\in X
and
T(B|\cdot):X\to[0,1]
is
\mathcal{A}
-measurable for all
B\in\mathcal{B}
. Given a finite measure
\mu:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
, we may define a new finite measure
T\mu:\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}
,
(T\mu)(B)=\int_{X}T(B|x)\,d\mu(x)
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}
. Clearly

3.
The majorization semiring

From nor on, we fix

. Given a standard Borel measurable space

-essentially bounded away from zero by

-tuple (over
(X,\mathcal{A})
)

for

or

-tuples over

When

For

if there exists a Markov kernel

such that

,

. We also define

and

\displaystyle P\boxplus Q

\displaystyle=\big(p^{(1)}\oplus q^{(1)},\ldots,p^{(d)}\oplus q^{(d)}\big),

\displaystyle P\boxtimes Q

\displaystyle=\big(p^{(1)}\otimes q^{(1)},\ldots,p^{(d)}\otimes q^{(d)}\big).

We denote

if there is a standard Borel measurable space
(Z,\mathcal{C})
, a regular

-tuple

over

and

for

. We lift the binary operators and relation

,
\boxtimes
, and

into

denoted respectively by

,

,

. Given the trivial measurable space

\approx
-equivalence class of the element

(where

is the zero measure on

-equivalence class of the element

In the sequel we view functions on

Next we show that the majorization semiring

(3)

\displaystyle V_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\big(v_{\lambda}^{(1)},\ldots,v^{(d)}_{\lambda}\big)\in V_{\big(\{1,\ldots,d\},2^{\{1,\ldots,d\}}\big)},

\displaystyle v_{\lambda}^{(k)}(\ell)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\lambda\delta_{k,\ell}}{d+\lambda},\qquad k,\ell=1,\ldots,d,

Lemma 4
.

Let

\lambda\geq(d-1)\frac{b}{a}+\frac{1}{a}-d.

Proof.

Define

(6)

\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}s^{\lambda}_{\ell,m}v^{(k)}_{\lambda}(\ell)=\delta_{k,m},\quad k,\ell=1,\ldots,d.

If
\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}s^{\lambda}_{k,\ell}\,dp^{(\ell)}/dp^{(d)}\geq 0
for

which are immediately seen to be also probability measures. Thus we have a Markov kernel

\displaystyle T_{\lambda}v_{\lambda}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}T_{\lambda}(\cdot|\ell)v^{(k)}_{\lambda}(\ell)=\sum_{\ell,m=1}^{d}s^{\lambda}_{\ell,m}v^{(k)}_{\lambda}(\ell)p^{(m)}=p^{(k)}

where we have used (
6

as long as

are positive measures. For this, let us evaluate

\displaystyle\frac{dT_{\lambda}(\cdot|k)}{dp^{(d)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}s^{\lambda}_{k,\ell}\frac{dp^{(\ell)}}{dp^{(d)}}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((d+\lambda)\frac{dp^{(k)}}{dp^{(d)}}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\frac{dp^{(\ell)}}{dp^{(d)}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\lambda}\big((d+\lambda)a-(d-1)b-1\big).

We immediately see that the final expression is non-negative whenever (

∎

We notice that the above proof can be refined slightly to obtain the following: Let

3.1.
The monotone homomorphisms and derivations of the majorization semiring

For

is a finite partition, we may easily show similarly as above that

\sup_{\lambda>2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Phi_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}^{\lambda}}(P|_{\mathcal{P}})}{p^{(d)}(X)}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\lambda-1}}=\max_{i\in Y_{\mathcal{P}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{p^{(1)}(A_{i})}{p^{(d)}(A_{i})}}}\right)^{\beta_{1}}\cdots\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{p^{(d-1)}(A_{i})}{p^{(d)}(A_{i})}}}\right)^{\beta_{d-1}}.

Clearly
P\succeq P|_{\mathcal{P}}
for any finite partition
\mathcal{P}
, so that
\Phi^{\prime}(P)\geq\Phi^{\prime}(P|_{\mathcal{P}})
for any
\Phi^{\prime}\in\Sigma(S^{d},\mathbb{R}_{+})
. However, perusing the proof of Proposition
6
, we notice that, for any
\Phi^{\prime}\in\Sigma(S^{d},\mathbb{R}_{+})
, there exists a sequence
(\mathcal{P}_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
of finite partitions such that
\Phi^{\prime}(P)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Phi^{\prime}(P|_{\mathcal{P}_{n}})
; e.g., the tuples
P_{n}
of the proof of Proposition
6
provide such a sequence. Thus,
\Phi^{\prime}(P)=\sup_{\mathcal{P}}\Phi^{\prime}(P|_{\mathcal{P}})
for all
\Phi^{\prime}\in\Sigma(S^{d},\mathbb{R}_{+})
where the supremum runs over all finite partitions. Thus, using our observation above,

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lambda>2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Phi_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}^{\lambda}}(P)}{p^{(d)}(X)}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\lambda-1}}=\sup_{\lambda>2}\sup_{\mathcal{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Phi_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}^{\lambda}}(P|_{\mathcal{P}})}{p^{(d)}(X)}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\lambda-1}}=\sup_{\mathcal{P}}\sup_{\lambda>2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Phi_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}^{\lambda}}(P|_{\mathcal{P}})}{p^{(d)}(X)}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\lambda-1}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mathcal{P}}\max_{i\in Y_{\mathcal{P}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{p^{(1)}(A_{i})}{p^{(d)}(A_{i})}}}\right)^{\beta_{1}}\cdots\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{p^{(d-1)}(A_{i})}{p^{(d)}(A_{i})}}}\right)^{\beta_{d-1}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{p^{(1)}(A)}{p^{(d)}(A)}}}\right)^{\beta_{1}}\cdots\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{p^{(d-1)}(A)}{p^{(d)}(A)}}}\right)^{\beta_{d-1}}.

Thus, we have
\Phi(P)\geq G
. We next show that
\Phi(P)\leq G
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
(sufficiently large), we find a finite partition
\mathcal{P}_{n}=\{A_{n,1},\ldots A_{n,m_{n}}\}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
and numbers
a^{(\ell)}_{n,i}
,
b^{(\ell)}_{n,i}
,
b^{(\ell)}_{n,i}\geq a^{(\ell)}_{n,i}
,
b^{(\ell)}_{n,i}-a^{(\ell)}_{n,i}\leq 1/n
, such that, for all
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,m_{n}\}
,

\frac{dp^{(\ell)}}{dp^{(d)}}(x)\in\big[a^{(\ell)}_{n,i},b^{(\ell)}_{n,i}\big]\quad\mathrm{for}\ P\mathrm{-a.a.}\ x\in A_{n,i}.

Let us denote
P_{n,i}=\big(p^{(1)}_{n,i},\ldots,p^{(d)}_{n,i}\big)\in V_{(A_{n,i},\mathcal{A}|_{A_{n,i}})}
,
p^{(\ell)}_{n,i}(B)=p^{(\ell)}(A_{n,i})^{-1}p^{(\ell)}(B)
for any
\mathcal{A}
-measurable
B\subseteq A_{n,i}
. With similar analysis as in the proof of Lemma
4
, we notice that, whenever

\lambda\geq\frac{b^{(\ell)}}{a^{(\ell)}_{n,i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{m=1}^{d-1}\frac{b^{(m)}}{a^{(m)}_{n,i}}+1}}\right)-d\quad\forall\ell\in\{1,\ldots,d\},

we have
V_{\lambda}\succeq P_{n,i}
. When
R
is such that
P
satisfies (
2
), we notice that
\lambda_{n}\geq 0
that satisfies the above condition for all
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,m_{n}\}
is given by

\lambda_{n}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{R}{n}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(d-1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{R}{n}}}\right)+1}}\right]-d\to 0\ \mathrm{as}\ n\to\infty.

We now have, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

P=\bigboxplus_{i=1}^{m_{n}}\big(p^{(1)}(A_{n,i}),\ldots,p^{(d)}(A_{n,i})\big)\boxtimes P_{n,i}\preceq\bigboxplus_{i=1}^{m_{n}}\big(p^{(1)}(A_{n,i}),\ldots,p^{(d)}(A_{n,i})\big)\boxtimes V_{\lambda_{n}}.

Using the monotonicity of
\Phi
, the easily verifiable fact that
\Phi(V_{\lambda})=\lambda+1
, and (
19
), we have

\displaystyle\Phi(P)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq m_{n}}\Phi\big(p^{(1)}(A_{n,i}),\ldots,p^{(d)}(A_{n,i})\big)\cdot\Phi(V_{\lambda_{n}})

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{n}+1)\max_{1\leq i\leq m_{n}}\underbrace{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{p^{(1)}(A_{n,i})}{p^{(d)}(A_{n,i})}}}\right)^{\beta_{1}}\cdots\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{p^{(d-1)}(A_{n,i})}{p^{(d)}(A_{n,i})}}}\right)^{\beta_{d-1}}}_{\leq G}\leq(\lambda_{n}+1)G.

Since this evaluation holds for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lambda_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, we have
\Phi(P)\leq G
. Thus, we have
\Phi(P)=G
.

We still need to show that any map
\Phi
having the form of the claim is actually monotone. However, we have essentially already proven that, whenever
P\in V_{(X,\mathcal{A})}
is non-zero and
\lambda\mapsto\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}^{\lambda}
is the path appearing earlier in this proof, we have

\Phi(P)=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Phi_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}^{\lambda}}(P)}{p^{(d)}(X)}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\lambda-1}}.

As the quantities appearing in the limit are monotone, so is
\Phi
as the pointwise limit.

Let us now prove the final claim. We pick
\Phi\in\Sigma(S^{d},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op})
. We first show that, whenever
a>0
,
\Phi(a,\ldots,a)=1
; this also holds in the
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}
-case, but that already follows from our earlier work
[
9
,
26
]
. We first show this for a rational
a>0
. When
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we immediately have
\Phi(n,\ldots,n)=\Phi(1,\ldots,1)=1
just using the tropical additivity of
\Phi
. Moreover, whenever
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\Phi(1,\ldots,1)=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((m,\ldots,m)\boxtimes\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{m},\ldots,\frac{1}{m}}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi(m,\ldots,m)\cdot\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{m},\ldots,\frac{1}{m}}}\right)=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{m},\ldots,\frac{1}{m}}}\right).

Now, when
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{m},\ldots\frac{n}{m}}}\right)=\Phi(n,\ldots,n)\cdot\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{m},\ldots,\frac{1}{m}}}\right)=1.

Thus,
\Phi(a)=1
for any rational
a>0
. We next pick any
a>0
and some rational
p,q>0
,
p<a<q
. We immediately see

(a,\ldots,a)\succeq(a-p,\ldots,a-p)\boxplus(p,\ldots,p)\succeq(a,\ldots,a),

so that
\Phi(a,\ldots,a)=\max\{\Phi(a-p,\ldots,a-p),\Phi(p,\ldots,p)\}\geq\Phi(p,\ldots,p)=1
. On the other hand,

(q,\ldots,q)\succeq(q-a,\ldots,q-a)\boxplus(a,\ldots,a)\succeq(q,\ldots,q),

so that
1=\Phi(q,\ldots,q)=\max\{\Phi(q-a,\ldots,q-a),\Phi(a,\ldots,a)\}\geq\Phi(a,\ldots,a)
. Putting these observations together, we have
\Phi(a,\ldots,a)=1
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{A})
be standard Borel and consider
t\in(0,1)
,
U=\big(u^{(1)},\ldots,u^{(d)}\big)\in V_{(X,\mathcal{A})}
, and
V=\big(v^{(1)},\ldots,v^{(d)}\big)\in V_{(X,\mathcal{A})}
. We denote

tU+(1-t)V:=\big(tu^{(1)}+(1-t)v^{(1)},\ldots,tu^{(d)}+(1-t)v^{(d)}\big)\in V_{(X,\mathcal{A})}.

It is obvious that
tU+(1-t)V\preceq tU\boxplus(1-t)V
. Thus, using the observation above together with the monotonicity of
\Phi
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi\big(tU+(1-t)V\big)

\displaystyle\geq\Phi\big(tU\boxplus(1-t)V\big)

\displaystyle=\max\{\Phi(U)\cdot\Phi(t,\ldots,t),\Phi(V)\cdot\Phi(1-t,\ldots,1-t)\}

\displaystyle=\max\{\Phi(U),\Phi(V)\}.

This already looks pretty bad for
\Phi
, but let us be thorough.

Let us fix
P=\big(p^{(1)},\ldots,p^{(d)}\big)\in V_{(X,\mathcal{A})}
for some standard Borel
(X,\mathcal{A})
. Let us assume that
R>0
is such that
P
satisfies (
2
). Let us denote
P_{0}:=\big(p^{(d)},\ldots,p^{(d)}\big)
. Whenever
0<t<\min\{1,1/R\}
, we may define
U=\big(u^{(1)},\ldots,u^{(d)}\big)\in V_{(X,\mathcal{A})}
where
u^{(\ell)}=(1-t)^{-1}\big(p^{(\ell)}-tp^{(d)}\big)
and
V=\big(v^{(1)},\ldots,v^{(d)}\big)\in V_{(X,\mathcal{A})}
where
v^{(\ell)}=(1-t)^{-1}\big(p^{(d)}-tp^{(\ell)}\big)
. We thus have
P=tP_{0}+(1-t)U
and
P_{0}=tP+(1-t)V
, so that

\displaystyle\Phi(P)

\displaystyle=\Phi\big(tP_{0}+(1-t)U)\geq\max\{\Phi(P_{0}),\Phi(U)\}\geq\Phi(P_{0})

\displaystyle=\Phi\big(tP+(1-t)V\big)\geq\max\{\Phi(P),\Phi(V)\}\geq\Phi(P).

Hence, we have
\Phi(P)=\Phi(P_{0})=1
since
P_{0}\succeq\big(p^{(d)}(X),\ldots,p^{(d)}(X)\big)\succeq P_{0}
and
\Phi\big(p^{(d)}(X),\ldots,p^{(d)}(X)\big)=1
. Thus,
\Phi(P)=1
whenever
P\neq 0
and
\Phi(0)=0
, i.e.,
\Phi
is the trivial tropical homomorphism which is degenerate. This proves the final claim.
∎

For any
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\beta$}}}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\beta_{1}+\cdots+\beta_{d}=0
and
\beta_{k}=1
for one
k
, let us define the function
\Phi^{\mathbb{T}}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\beta$}}}}:S^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
through

(20)

\Phi^{\mathbb{T}}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\beta$}}}}(P)=\underset{x\in X}{P{\rm-ess\,sup}}\,\prod_{\ell:\,\ell\neq k}\frac{dp^{(\ell)}}{dp^{(k)}}(x)^{\beta_{\ell}}

for any
P=\big(p^{(1)},\ldots,p^{(d)}\big)\in V_{(X,\mathcal{A})}
on any standard Borel
(X,\mathcal{A})
. According to Proposition
7
, we have

\displaystyle\Sigma(S^{d},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+})

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{k=1}^{d}\{\Phi^{\mathbb{T}}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\beta$}}}}\,|\,\beta_{1}+\cdots+\beta_{d}=0,\ \beta_{k}=1,\ \beta_{\ell}\leq 0\ \mathrm{whenever}\ \ell\neq k\},

\displaystyle\Sigma(S^{d},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}\}

\displaystyle=\emptyset.

4.
Multivariate Rényi divergences and their applications in majorization

Let us denote by
\mathcal{P}^{d}
the section of
S^{d}
of those
P\in S^{d}
such that
\|P\|=(1,\ldots,1)
, i.e.,
\mathcal{P}^{d}
is the set of regular finite statistical experiments. This is naturally the set of actual information-theoretic interest.

Definition 8
.

We say that a map
D:\mathcal{P}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
(multivariate) divergence
if

(i)

P\succeq Q

\Rightarrow

D(P)\geq D(Q)
(monotonicity) and

(ii)

D(P\boxtimes Q)=D(P)+D(Q)
for all
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}^{d}
(extensivity).

We denote the set of divergences
D:\mathcal{P}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
by
\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{P}^{d})
.

Let us investigate a divergence
D:\mathcal{P}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. Extensivity of
D
immediately gives us
D(1,\ldots,1)=0
. Since, for any probability measure
p
,
(p,\ldots,p)\succeq(1,\ldots,1)\succeq(p,\ldots,p)
, we also have
D(p,\ldots,p)=0
due to monotonicity. Since
P\succeq(1,\ldots,1)
for all
P\in\mathcal{P}^{d}
, we have
D(P)\geq D(1,\ldots,1)=0
. Thus, in fact,
D:\mathcal{P}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

The consistent way of defining divergences on
\mathcal{P}^{d}
using the monotone homomorphisms and derivations is using a fixed power universal. Let us consider the fixed power universal
U:=V_{1}
where
V_{\lambda}
,
\lambda>0
, are are as in (
3
). Recall that, due to Proposition
5
, these are power universals. Recalling that
\Sigma(S^{d},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op})
is empty, we define the sets
\mathfrak{D}(S^{d},\mathbb{K})
,
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}\}
,

(21)

\mathfrak{D}(S^{d},\mathbb{K}):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathcal{P}^{d}\ni P\mapsto\frac{\log{\Phi(P)}}{\log{\Phi(U)}}\,\middle|\,\Phi\in\Sigma(S^{d},\mathbb{K})}}\right\}

of divergences. The sets of derivations
\mathfrak{D}^{k}(S^{d})
when restricted on
\mathcal{P}^{d}
are already divergences. For
\Phi_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}\in\Sigma(S^{d},\mathbb{R}_{+})\cup\Sigma(S^{d},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op})
,
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})
, we have

\Phi_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}(U)=\frac{1}{d+1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}2^{\alpha_{\ell}}.

As a function of
\textstyle\alpha
this is somewhat cumbersome and there does not seem to be other power universals in
\mathcal{P}^{d}
that would result into anything prettier. This is why we replace the above normalization with the simpler
\max_{1\leq k\leq d}\alpha_{k}-1
which as a function of
\textstyle\alpha
shares the important properties with the function
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}\mapsto\log{\Phi_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}(U)}
. This prompts the following definition.

.

Let us denote by
A_{+}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
the set of those
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})
such that
0\leq\alpha_{\ell}<1
for
\ell=1,\ldots,d
and
\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}=1
. We also denote, for any
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, by
A_{k}
the set of those
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})
such that
\alpha_{k}>1
,
\alpha_{\ell}\leq 0
whenever
\ell\neq k
, and
\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}=1
. We also define
A_{-}:=A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{d}
. For any
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})\in A_{+}\cup A_{-}

temperate multivariate Rényi divergence

D_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}:\mathcal{P}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

\displaystyle D_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}(P)

(X,\mathcal{A})
.

\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\beta$}}}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

.

We denote the set of all of the above temperate and tropical divergences

\Delta^{(k)}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\gamma$}}}}
of (

) depicted in the case

of those points whose coordinates sum up to 1. The temperate divergences are associated with the areas

Let us fix
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\beta$}}}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d})\in B_{k}
and define the path

\mathbb{R}_{+}\ni\lambda\mapsto\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}^{\lambda}=\big((\lambda-1)\beta_{1},\ldots,(\lambda-1)\beta_{k-1},\lambda,(\lambda-1)\beta_{k+1},\ldots,(\lambda-1)\beta_{d}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We immediately see that, when
0\leq\lambda<1

\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}^{\lambda}\in A_{+}

\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}^{\lambda}\in A_{k}

\beta_{\ell}

\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}^{\lambda}

\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}:=\frac{\beta_{\rm min}}{\beta_{\rm min}-1}.

Using Jensen’s inequality, it is easy to see that

\lambda_{0}\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2},\ \lambda_{1}\neq 1\neq\lambda_{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad D_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}^{\lambda_{1}}}(P)\leq D_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}^{\lambda_{2}}}(P).

Moreover, it is easy to verify that, for any

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 1}D_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}^{\lambda}}(P)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\ell:\,\ell\neq k}\beta_{\ell}D_{\rm KL}\big(p^{(k)}\big\|p^{(\ell)}\big)=\Delta^{(k)}_{-\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\beta$}}}}(P),

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}D_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}^{\lambda}}(P)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lambda>1}D_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}^{\lambda}}(P)=D^{\mathbb{T}}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\beta$}}}}(P).

This shows that we obtain all the derivations and all the tropical divergences as pointwise limits of the temperate divergences.

4.1.
Sufficient conditions for large-sample and catalytic majorization

With the slight deviation of using a different normalization from the one used in Subsection
2.1
, the temperate and tropical multivariate Rényi divergences of Definition
9
and mixtures of the binary Kullback-Leibler divergences form the test spectrum
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(S^{d})
of the majorization semiring
S^{d}
which we may identify with the set
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(\mathcal{P}^{d})
of Definition
9
. A straightforward application of Theorem
3
gives us the following result on large-sample and catalytic majorization generalizing the bivariate (
d=2
) result of
[
19
]
and the multivariate results on finite sample spaces of
[
9
]
. Note that the experiments
P
and
Q
of the following theorem are assumed to be elements of the semiring
S^{d}
, so condition (
2
) is assumed to holds for both with some
R>0
. Furthermore, conditions in (
26
) together with the fact that
D_{\rm KL}(p\|q)>0

\Rightarrow

p\neq q
imply that
P=\big(p^{(1)},\ldots,p^{(d)}\big)
is such that
p^{(k)}\neq p^{(\ell)}
whenever
k\neq\ell
so that, according to Proposition
5
,
P
is a power universal.

Theorem 10
.

Let
P=\big(p^{(1)},\ldots,p^{(d)}\big)\in\mathcal{P}^{d}
and
Q=\big(q^{(1)},\ldots,q^{(d)}\big)\in\mathcal{P}^{d}
. If

(24)

\displaystyle D_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}(P)

\displaystyle>D_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}(Q),\hskip 62.0pt\forall\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}\in A_{+}\cup A_{-},

(25)

\displaystyle D^{\mathbb{T}}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\beta$}}}}(P)

\displaystyle>D^{\mathbb{T}}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\beta$}}}}(Q),\hskip 62.0pt\forall\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\beta$}}}\in B,

(26)

\displaystyle D_{\rm KL}\big(p^{(k)}\big\|p^{(\ell)}\big)

\displaystyle>D_{\rm KL}\big(q^{(k)}\big\|q^{(\ell)}\big),\qquad\forall k,\ell\in\{1,\ldots,d\},\ k\neq\ell,

then
P^{\boxtimes n}\succeq Q^{\boxtimes n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large and there also exists
R\in\mathcal{P}^{d}
such that
P\boxtimes R\succeq Q\boxtimes R
. In the final part, we may always choose the catalyst
R=\big(r^{(1)},\ldots,r^{(d)}\big)
according to

r^{(k)}=\frac{1}{n+1}\bigoplus_{m=0}^{n}\big(p^{(k)}\big)^{\otimes(n-m)}\big(q^{(k)}\big)^{\otimes m},\qquad k=1,\ldots,d,

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large. On the other hand, if
P^{\boxtimes n}\succeq Q^{\boxtimes n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
or
P\boxtimes R\succeq Q\boxtimes R
for some
R\in\mathcal{P}^{d}
, then the inequalities in (
24
), (
25
), and (
26
) hold non-strictly.

4.2.
Barycentric representations of multivariate divergences

We next characterize all multivariate divergences
D\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{P}^{d})
following the proof of Theorem 2 of
[
19
]
. The cornerstone of the proof is the following simple lemma:

Lemma 11
.

The implication

\forall\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(\mathcal{P}^{d}):\ \Delta(P)\geq\Delta(Q)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad D(P)\geq D(Q)

holds for any multivariate divergence
D\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{P}^{d})
.

Proof.

Let us fix
D\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{P}^{d})
. Let us first assume that
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}^{d}
are such that
\Delta(P)>\Delta(Q)
for all
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(\mathcal{P}^{d})
. Using Theorem
3
and fixing a power universal
U\in\mathcal{P}^{d}
(see Proposition
5
), we find
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
P^{\boxtimes n}\boxtimes U^{\boxtimes k}\succeq Q^{\boxtimes n}\boxtimes U^{\boxtimes k}
. Thus, we have

nD(P)+kD(U)=D\big(P^{\boxtimes n}\boxtimes U^{\boxtimes k}\big)\geq D\big(Q^{\boxtimes n}\boxtimes U^{\boxtimes k}\big)=nD(Q)+kD(U),

so that
D(P)\geq D(Q)
. Let us next assume that
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}^{d}
are such that
\Delta(P)\geq\Delta(Q)
for all
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(\mathcal{P}^{d})
. We make these inequalities strict by using powers of
P
and
Q
and the power universal
U
: for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

\Delta\big(P^{\boxtimes n}\boxtimes U\big)=n\Delta(P)+\underbrace{\Delta(U)}_{>0}>n\Delta(P)\geq n\Delta(Q)=\Delta\big(Q^{\boxtimes n}\big)

for all
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(\mathcal{P}^{d})
. Thus, according to the first part of this proof, we have

nD(P)+D(U)=D\big(P^{\boxtimes n}\boxtimes U\big)\geq D\big(Q^{\boxtimes n}\big)=nD(Q)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e.,
D(P)+D(U)/n\geq D(Q)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus,
D(P)\geq D(Q)
.
∎

Using the above lemma in the same way as in the proof of Theorem 2 of
[
19
]
(see the proof of Theorem 7 of
[
13
]
for details), we may show that any
D\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{P}^{d})
can be expressed as a barycentre of a finite positive measure on the compactification of the set
A_{+}\cup A_{-}
. This compactification is to be viewed as
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(\mathcal{P}^{d})
which is a compact Hausdorff space in the topology of pointwise convergence
[
11
]
. This means that, instead of a single point included at the limits
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}\to e_{k}
(a natural basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
), we have the full
(d-1)
-dimensional probability simplex corresponding to the derivations
\Delta^{(k)}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\gamma$}}}}
where
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\gamma$}}}=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{d})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
,
\sum_{\ell:\,\ell\neq k}\gamma_{\ell}=1
. Naturally, these simplexes are spanned by the
d-1
extreme points giving rise to (possible) point measure contributions from the pairwise Kullback-Leibler relative entropies. The pointwise limits at the infinity coincide with the tropical divergences
D^{\mathbb{T}}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\beta$}}}}
,
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\beta$}}}\in B
. In this setting, using Lemma
11
, we may define a positive linear functional
H
on the subspace of the Banach space of continuous functions on the compact Hausdorff space
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(\mathcal{P}^{d})
spanned by the evaluation functions
{\rm ev}_{P}
at a statistical experiment
P
,
{\rm ev}_{P}(\Delta)=\Delta(P)
for all
\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(\mathcal{P}^{d})
, through
H({\rm ev}_{P})=D(P)
. Using a theorem due to Kantorovich
[
16
]
related to the Hahn-Banach theorem, we may extend
H
into a positive linear functional
I
defined on the whole space of continuous functions on
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(\mathcal{P}^{d})
. According to the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani theorem, there is a(n inner and outer regular) finite measure
\mu:\mathcal{B}\big(\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(\mathcal{P}^{d})\big)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
(where the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}\big(\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(\mathcal{P}^{d})\big)
is defined w.r.t. the topology of pointwise convergence) such that
I(f)=\int f\,d\mu
. This means that we arrive at the following:

Theorem 12
.

We shall denote by
\mathcal{B}(C)
the Borel (or Lebesgue)
\sigma
-algebra of any measurable subset
C
of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose that
D\in\mathfrak{D}(S^{d})
is a multivariate divergence. There are finite measures
\mu:\mathcal{B}(A_{+}\cup A_{-})\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\nu:\mathcal{B}(B)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\gamma_{k,\ell}\geq 0
,
k,\ell=1,\ldots,d
such that

D(P)=\int_{A_{+}\cup A_{-}}D_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}(P)\,d\mu(\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}})+\int_{B}D^{\mathbb{T}}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\beta$}}}}(P)\,d\nu(\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\beta$}}})+\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{d}\gamma_{k,\ell}D_{\rm KL}\big(p^{(k)}\big\|p^{(\ell)}\big)

for all
P=\big(p^{(1)},\ldots,p^{(d)}\big)\in\mathcal{P}^{d}
.

Remark 13
.

Let us highlight that this result has also been previously independently proven in
[
1
]
, therein as Theorem 5.1. This goes to show that same results can be reached with greatly different methods. The biggest difference between the approaches taken in this paper and in
[
1
]
is that, in this paper, Theorem
12
is a consequence of Theorem
10
proven using real-algebraic techniques and the results of
[
13
]
whereas, in
[
1
]
, the methodology similar with
[

]
is used, but the preceding proof techniques are different

.

4.3.
Optimal achievable rates

We now derive the form of optimal transformation rate between finite statistical experiments. However, we start in the more general setting of Subsection
2.1
. We let
S
be a zerosumfree preordered semidomain of polynomial growth and degeneracy
d
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Fixing a power universal
u\in S
, we define the test spectrum
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(S)
. Let us consider
x,y\in S
,
x\sim y
, where
x
is a power universal, so that
x\sim 1
. Thus, we have
1\sim x\sim y
, i.e.,
\|x\|=\|y\|=(1,\ldots,1)
. We say that
r\geq 0
is
(x,y)
-achievable
if there is a sequence
(m_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
of natural numbers such that
r\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}m_{n}/n
and
x^{n}\trianglerighteq y^{m_{n}}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large. The
optimal achievable rate of transformation

x\to y
is given by

r(x\to y):=\sup\{r\geq 0\,|\,r\ \mathrm{is}\ (x,y)\mathrm{-achievable}\}.

The following result is Corollary 29 of
[
26
]
.

Corollary 14
.

Let
x,y\in S
,
x\sim y
, where
x
is a power universal. We have

(27)

r(x\to y)=\min_{\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(S)}\frac{\Delta(x)}{\Delta(y)}.

Let us now concentrate on multivariate majorization. Given
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}^{d}
, the optimal rate
r(P\to Q)
corresponds to the optimal ratio of transforming copies of
P
into copies of
Q
with a single stochastic map, i.e., a Markov kernel. Recalling that the total set
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(\mathcal{P}^{d})
(which corresponds to the test spectrum
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(S^{d})
) of multivariate Rényi divergences is the closure (w.r.t. the topology of pointwise convergence) of the set of
D_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}
with
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}\in A_{+}\cup A_{-}
, we immediately obtain the following result as a consequence of Corollary
14
.

Corollary 15
.

Suppose that
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}^{d}
and that the probability measures
p^{(k)}
appearing in
P
are pairwise distinct. The optimal transformation rate of copies of
P
into copies of
Q
is given by

r(P\to Q)=\min_{\Delta\in\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(\mathcal{P}^{d})}\frac{\Delta(P)}{\Delta(Q)}=\inf_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}\in A_{+}\cup A_{-}}\frac{D_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}(P)}{D_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}(Q)}.

5.
Conclusions

We have derived sufficient and almost necessary conditions for large-sample and catalytic majorization of finite statistical experiments using real-algebraic methods, highlighting the power of the theory of asymptotic spectra also in the non-discrete setting of the current work. The multivariate Rényi divergences characterizing large-sample majorization also determine all the general multivariate divergences through barycentres. Thus, we have generalized the work done in the continuous bivariate case
[
19
]
and the results on multivariate matrix majorization
[
9
]
.

In this work, we have concentrated on the case where all the probability measures within the same statistical experiment are mutually dominating, i.e., belong to the same measure class. However, it is also possible to study more general support conditions in the same way as in
[
26
]
. Let us highlight two special cases of support conditions within the same statistical experiment
P=\big(p^{(1)},\ldots,p^{(d)}\big)
over some standard Borel measurable space
(X,\mathcal{A})
:

•

For all
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,
p^{(k)}=p^{(k)}_{\parallel}+p^{(k)}_{\perp}
where the two measures are orthogonal and
p^{(k)}_{\parallel}\ll p^{(\ell)}_{\parallel}
for all
k,\ell\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. This case corresponds to the minimal restrictions semiring studied in
[
26
]
.

•

For all
k\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
,
p^{(k)}\ll p^{(d)}
. This case corresponds to the dominating column semiring of
[
26
]
. In this case, it seems likely that we have to require

\underset{x\in X}{p^{(d)}\mathrm{-ess\,sup}}\,\frac{dp^{(k)}}{dp^{(d)}}(x)<\infty,\qquad k=1,\ldots,d-1,

taken into account the results of
[
26
]
and the methods that we have used in this work.

In
[
26
]
, the power universals were exhaustively characterized in the above two cases (and were conjectured in a variety of other cases). We anticipate that similar characterizations are possible also in the continuous case and they should lead us to proper continuous generalizations of the characterization of large-sample matrix majorization with varying support conditions.

Our main result, Theorem
10
requires the boundedness condition of (
2
) to hold both for the input experiment
P
and the output experiment
Q
. Since this condition is quite restrictive, it would be desirable to have a less strict condition. Let us point out that the condition of (
2
) for
P
simply means that the tropical divergences
D^{\mathbb{T}}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\beta$}}}}(P)
are well defined for
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\beta$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\beta$}}}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d})\in B
such that
\beta_{k}=1
,
\beta_{\ell}=-1
, and
\beta_{m}=0
otherwise. This immediately means that all the tropical divergences are well defined. In other words, the condition in (
2
) just means that the tropical divergences are well defined and we need this property to state the conditions (
25
) among the sufficient conditions for large-sample and catalytic majorization in Theorem
10
. To lift the boundedness condition in (
2
), it thus seems that we have to discuss a form of majorization where the tropical conditions do not play a role. In vector majorization, we know that the counterpart of the temperate conditions are enough to characterize catalytic majorization
[
17
]
whereas the counterparts of tropical conditions are required when studying large-sample majorization
[
9
, Corollary 46]
. Thus, it might be possible to drop the condition (
2
) or at least replace it with something less stringent if we only want to characterize catalytic multivariate majorization. Another possibility is studying asymptotic majorization in stead. Asymptotic large-sample majorization of a statistical experiment
P=\big(p^{(1)},\ldots,p^{(d)}\big)
over
Q=\big(q^{(1)},\ldots,q^{(d)}\big)
means that, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a statistical experiment
Q_{\varepsilon}=\big(q^{(1)}_{\varepsilon},\ldots,q^{(d)}_{\varepsilon}\big)
such that
\big\|q^{(k)}-q^{(k)}_{\varepsilon}\big\|_{\rm TV}<\varepsilon
for
k=1,\ldots,d
(i.e.,
Q_{\varepsilon}
is
\varepsilon
-close to
Q
in total variation distance) such that
P
majorizes
Q_{\varepsilon}
in large samples, i.e.,
P^{\boxtimes n}\succeq Q^{\boxtimes n}_{\varepsilon}
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
large enough. As the tropical divergences are pointwise limits of the temperate quantities, it seems likely that some form of asymptotic majorization only requires non-strict versions of the conditions (
24
), suggesting that something weaker than (
2
) is required. However, we leave this for future investigation.
