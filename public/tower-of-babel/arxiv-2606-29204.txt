Light anti-nuclei in pp collisions at the LHC: production by coalescence and interaction of anti-nucleons
N. Ciavarelli \from ins:x \from ins:y \ETC, F. Ercolessi \from ins:x \from ins:y, F. Noferini \from ins:x, F. Bellini \from ins:x \from ins:y

Abstract
A unified afterburner framework is presented to describe nucleon–nucleon final-state interactions and light-(anti)nuclei production via coalescence in high-energy measured in pp collisions at the LHC collisions. The model reproduces qualitatively light-(anti)nuclei spectra without fine-tuning of the model parameters, as well as correlation observables, and can be extended to beyond proton–proton collisions.

1
Introduction

The production mechanism of light nuclei and antinuclei in high-energy collisions
is still not fully understood and is relevant for characterizing nuclear matter
under extreme conditions
[
1
]
as well as for interpreting potential
dark-matter signals in indirect searches
[
2
]
.

One of the proposed mechanisms for the production of light-(anti)nuclei in high-energy interactions is
coalescence
[
3
,
4
]
, which occurs when nucleons that are
close in phase space at freeze-out bind together to form a nucleus.

Nucleons produced in high-energy collisions interact in the final
state: the formation of a bound state can be described by the coalescence process; if they do not bind,
spatial information about the emission source is preserved and can be accessed
via femtoscopic techniques using two-particle correlation functions.

2
Coalescence and Correlation Functions

2.1
Coalescence

The probability of two (or more) nucleus to coalesce depends on the momentum (or pair average transversal momentum
k_{T}
, the size of the nucleons source and the emission size of the nucleus
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.
Experimental observations from ALICE
[
6
]
show that increasing the
source size, and thus the average nucleon separation, leads to a decrease in the
coalescence probability, reflecting the reduced phase-space density in an
extended emission region.

2.2
Correlation function

Final state interactions and the size of the particle-emitting source can be probed
using femtoscopic techniques derived from Hanbury–Brown–Twiss (HBT) interferometry
[
4
]
through the two-particle correlation function

C(k^{*})
, which is experimentally defined as
C(k^{*})=\xi\,\frac{S(k^{*})}{B(k^{*})}\,
,
where
k^{*}=|\vec{p}_{1}-\vec{p}_{2}|/2
is the relative momentum of the particle pair
in its rest frame.

S(k^{*})
is the signal distribution, obtained by pairing particles from the same
event and containing the physical correlations.

B(k^{*})
is the background distribution, obtained by pairing particles from different
events and providing an uncorrelated reference sample.
The factor
\xi
is the normalization factor.

3
Afterburner model

In order to model nucleon–nucleon interactions (e.g. strong and Coulomb potentials) and coalescence at the same time, an afterburner has
been developed. It is a post-processing simulation module applied after the event
generation stage, operating on the final-state particles, doing coalescence event by event, and used in cascade with
generators such as PYTHIA
[
7
]
.
Two theoretical formalisms are implemented: one based on wave functions and one on
Wigner functions.
At present, the Wigner formalism is primarily used to validate the wave-function
approach.
The framework extends a previously developed Wigner-based afterburner
[
8
]
,
which successfully reproduces experimental data from the ALICE experiment.

3.1
Wave-function formalism

The wave-function formalism is modeled using an interaction potential within a
non-relativistic approximation.
The interaction is based on the nucleon–nucleon potential obtained by equating
the source energy to the final energy at infinity, corresponding to a vanishing
potential:

\left\langle\psi_{\text{source}}\middle|K+V\middle|\psi_{\text{source}}\right\rangle=\frac{k^{*2}}{2m}\;(v\ll c)

where
K
is the kinetic energy and
V
the interaction potential.
The strong interaction is modeled by a square-well potential adjusted to the
deuteron radius,

V_{\text{strong}}(r)=-V_{0}\,\Theta(R_{D}-r)
,
with
V_{0}=17.4~\text{MeV}
and
R_{D}=3.2~\text{fm}
.
The Coulomb interaction is given by
V_{\text{Coul}}(r)=\frac{1.44~\text{MeV\,fm}}{r}
.
The source is described by a Gaussian distribution multiplied by a plane wave,

\psi_{\text{source}}(\vec{r})=A\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{8R_{0}^{2}}\right)\,\exp\!\left(i\,\frac{\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r}}{\hbar}\right)
, where
A
is the amplitude and
R_{0}
the source radius.
The coalescence probability is obtained from the projection of the source wave
function onto the deuteron ground state,

P_{\text{coal}}=\left|\left\langle\psi_{\text{source}}\middle|\psi_{\text{deuteron}}\right\rangle\right|^{2}.

3.2
Wigner formalism validation

The validation of the model is based on the Wigner formalism, in which the source is described by the Wigner function

W(\vec{r},\vec{p})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\hbar)^{3}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{4R_{0}^{2}}\right)\,\exp\!\left[-4R_{0}^{2}\left(\frac{\vec{k}+\vec{p}}{\hbar}\right)^{2}\right]
, which is Gaussian both in coordinate space and in momentum space, with relative widths that are inversely proportional,
\sigma_{r}=\hbar/\sigma_{k}
.
The deuteron ground state Wigner function,
W_{\text{deuteron}}(\vec{r},\vec{p})
, is obtained from the numerical integration of the corresponding wave function.
The coalescence probability is then given by

P(k^{*})=\int W_{\text{deuteron}}(\vec{r},\vec{p})\,W_{\text{source}}(\vec{r},\vec{p};\,k^{*})\,d\vec{r}\,d\vec{p}

4

The afterburner shows consistency between the wave function and Wigner function formalisms, with the wave function approach chosen because it is computationally more efficient, approximately 33 times faster.

5
Conclusions

A unified afterburner framework has been developed to model nucleon–nucleon
final-state interactions and light-(anti)nuclei production in high-energy
collisions.
The model treats coalescence and correlation effects within a common approach and
has been implemented using both wave-function and Wigner formalisms, whose
equivalence has been validated.

First results show that the framework reproduces qualitatively the measured
light-(anti)nuclei spectra and captures the effect of bound-state formation on
two-particle correlation functions without fine-tuning of the model parameters.
The afterburner is readily applicable to collision systems beyond
proton–proton interactions, providing a versatile tool for the study of nucleon
correlations and light-(anti)nuclei production in different regimes.

The model has been proven to be able to be applied for systems other than pp collisions, i.e ZEUS at HERA and the EIC, to explore nucleon correlations and (anti)nuclei production in different regimes.
