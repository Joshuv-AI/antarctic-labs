Engineering smart polymeric lipid nanoparticles for breast cancer: AI-guided formulation design, biological barriers, and translational constraints
Authors: Tianzhao Du, Ye Yuan, Ruihan Shen, Yan Wang, Cui Jiang, Jie Wu, Zhichao Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer is a biologically heterogeneous disease in which tumor-intrinsic diversity and the tumor immune microenvironment jointly shape therapeutic resistance and variable clinical outcomes. Although nanomedicine has improved the safety and pharmacokinetic profiles of several anticancer agents, clinically approved nanocarriers have produced limited efficacy gains, partly because of heterogeneous tumor accumulation, restricted penetration, and empirical formulation design. Polymeric lipid nanoparticles (PLNs), also known as lipid–polymer hybrid nanoparticles, provide a tunable core–shell platform that combines the structural stability of polymeric systems with the biomimetic and functional versatility of lipid-based carriers. These properties enable controlled drug loading, adjustable release kinetics, and surface engineering for targeting or immune modulation. Artificial intelligence (AI) may support PLN development by organizing complex formulation variables and prioritizing experimentally testable designs rather than replacing mechanistic nanobiology. Machine learning, graph-based models, generative approaches, and predictive pharmacokinetic frameworks can help connect biological barriers, including receptor heterogeneity, stromal restriction, immune contexture, and delivery variability, with modifiable formulation parameters such as particle size, lipid–polymer composition, ligand density, and release behavior. Microfluidic manufacturing may further improve reproducibility by translating computationally prioritized formulations into controlled physical nanoparticles. This review summarizes the structural rationale and functional advantages of PLNs in breast cancer, evaluates barrier-oriented PLN design strategies, and examines the role of AI in formulation optimization, biological fate prediction, drug-release modeling, and translational workflow design. We also discuss current limitations, including data scarcity, limited PLN-specific validation, clinical delivery heterogeneity, and regulatory challenges. Overall, AI-guided PLN development should be viewed as a biology-informed and manufacturing-aware framework for improving formulation prioritization and reproducibility, rather than as an immediate clinical solution.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Polymeric lipid nanoparticles, Lipid–polymer hybrid nanoparticles, Breast cancer, Tumor microenvironment, Artificial intelligence, Machine learning

Introduction

Breast cancer is a heterogeneous and dynamic disease shaped by both tumor-intrinsic diversity and the tumor microenvironment. Although traditional classification based on estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and HER2 status remains clinically important, molecular profiling has revealed additional functional subtypes, including HER2-low disease and diverse forms of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), highlighting the limitations of conventional taxonomies [1–3]. Interactions among cancer cells, immune populations, stromal components, extracellular matrix structures, and vascular abnormalities further influence tumor progression, immune evasion, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance, particularly in aggressive subtypes such as TNBC [4, 5].

Nanomedicine has been developed to improve the delivery and safety of anticancer therapies. Formulations such as liposomal doxorubicin and albumin-bound paclitaxel demonstrate improved pharmacokinetics and reduced toxicity [6]. However, their impact on overall efficacy has been limited. Much of nanomedicine design has relied on the enhanced permeability and retention effect, yet this phenomenon is highly variable in human tumors and does not consistently translate from preclinical models [7]. In addition, barriers within the tumor microenvironment, including dense extracellular matrix, abnormal vasculature, and high interstitial pressure, restrict nanoparticle penetration [8, 9]. Consequently, only a very small fraction of administered nanoparticles reaches the tumor, limiting therapeutic benefit.

Despite advances in nanotechnology, the development of effective nanotherapeutics remains constrained by trial-and-error approaches. Nanoparticle design is often guided by iterative experimental optimization, which is time-consuming and poorly suited to address the complexity of human tumors. The lack of standardized reporting and reproducibility further limits progress and prevents the development of predictive design principles [10]. In addition, many systems that perform well in preclinical models fail clinically due to the inability of these models to capture tumor heterogeneity and microenvironmental variability [6, 7]. Thus, the central challenge is not simply to create more complex nanocarriers, but to determine which formulation features are most relevant to specific biological barriers in breast cancer.

Polymeric lipid nanoparticles (PLNs), also referred to as lipid–polymer hybrid nanoparticles, offer a useful platform for addressing this challenge because their structure contains multiple tunable design variables. The polymeric core can influence drug loading, stability, degradation, and release behavior, whereas the lipid shell can affect biocompatibility, circulation, surface functionalization, and cellular interaction [11]. These features make PLNs especially relevant for breast cancer, where delivery is shaped by receptor heterogeneity, stromal restriction, vascular abnormality, immune contexture, metastatic site, and treatment-induced biological change [12]. However, this same tunability also creates a multidimensional formulation space that is difficult to optimize through empirical experimentation alone [13].

Artificial intelligence (AI) may help organize this design space by linking tumor-level biological information and formulation-level parameters to experimentally testable nanoparticle designs. In this context, AI should not be viewed as a stand-alone solution or a substitute for mechanistic biological research [14]. Rather, its potential value lies in prioritizing formulation variables, identifying non-obvious structure–function relationships, predicting biological fate, and reducing the number of trial-and-error experiments required for PLN development [15]. Similarly, microfluidic manufacturing is relevant not because it makes immediate point-of-care production clinically ready, but because it offers a controlled and reproducible method for translating computationally proposed formulations into physical nanoparticles that can be tested under defined quality constraints [16].

This review focuses on how breast-cancer-specific biological barriers can inform the rational design of polymeric lipid nanoparticles. Rather than treating cancer biology, nanomedicine, AI, and manufacturing as separate themes, the manuscript links defined biological constraints, including tumor penetration, receptor-mediated uptake, immune interactions, and intracellular release, to tunable PLN features such as core composition, lipid shell chemistry, surface functionalization, particle size, and release kinetics. AI is discussed as an enabling tool for prioritizing and optimizing these design choices, while microfluidic manufacturing and regulatory considerations are framed as practical requirements for clinical translation.

Polymeric-lipid hybrid nanoparticles (PLNs): the structural foundation

Polymeric lipid nanoparticles (PLNs), also referred to as lipid–polymer hybrid nanoparticles (LPHNPs), provide a structurally versatile platform for addressing the biological barriers that limit nanomedicine efficacy in breast cancer [17]. Rather than functioning simply as hybrid carriers that combine polymeric and lipid-based systems, PLNs are best understood as modular core–shell constructs in which each structural component can be matched to a specific delivery problem [18]. The polymeric core can regulate drug loading, stability, degradation, and release kinetics, whereas the lipid shell can influence circulation, biocompatibility, immune recognition, surface functionalization, and cellular uptake [19, 20].

This architecture may help PLNs overcome several clinically relevant barriers in breast cancer, including stromal and extracellular matrix restrictions, intratumoral receptor heterogeneity, immune-mediated clearance, intracellular drug-release inefficiency, and challenges associated with metastatic-site delivery [19, 21]. However, the same flexibility also creates a multidimensional formulation space, making rational prioritization of design variables essential rather than optional [22].

Architectural rationale and functional advantages

The unique architectural design of PLNs arises from their core–shell configuration, which synergistically combines the structural robustness of polymeric nanoparticles with the biocompatibility and functional versatility of lipid layers. In this system, the polymeric core acts as the primary drug reservoir, enabling high encapsulation efficiency (EE%) and controlled release, while the lipid shell provides a biomimetic surface that enhances stability, cellular interaction, and targeting capability [17]. This dual composition addresses key limitations of conventional nanocarriers, such as premature drug leakage, poor stability, and suboptimal tumor accumulation.

The polymeric core serves as a controlled-release matrix by regulating drug diffusion and retention through its physicochemical properties. Studies have shown that polymer cores can achieve encapsulation efficiencies ranging from 72.8% to 94.5%, depending on polymer type and drug characteristics [23, 24]. For instance, sunitinib-loaded PLNs with a chitosan-based core achieved an EE% of 83.03% and released 84% of the drug over 48 h, following Fickian diffusion kinetics [25]. Similarly, Belinostat-loaded hybrid nanoparticles exhibited 94.5% EE with particle sizes around 166 nm, resulting in a 20-fold increase in tumor accumulation compared with the free-drug or control formulation in a specific mouse tumor model [24]. These findings highlight the core’s capacity to maintain drug stability, protect therapeutic agents from premature degradation, and enable sustained release aligned with tumor pharmacodynamics. Importantly, these quantitative improvements should be interpreted in the context of the comparator formulation and experimental model. Reported advantages of PLNs, including lower IC50 values, prolonged circulation, increased tumor accumulation, and enhanced tumor growth inhibition, are highly dependent on factors such as drug payload, targeting strategy, receptor expression, tumor model, dosing conditions, and assay methodology. Therefore, fold-change metrics reported across studies should be interpreted as model-specific preclinical observations rather than universal indicators of therapeutic superiority.

Encapsulating the polymeric core, the lipid shell contributes essential biomimetic properties that improve systemic circulation and facilitate targeted uptake. Lipid components such as phospholipids and PEGylated lipids mimic cellular membranes, reducing immune clearance and enabling functionalization with targeting ligands. Surface modification strategies have shown substantial enhancement in tumor-specific internalization; for example, folic acid-decorated PLNs achieved 9–11-fold lower IC50 values than the comparator formulation in folate receptor-positive breast cancer cells, while hyaluronic acid-functionalized nanoparticles enhanced CD44-mediated uptake [26, 27]. The lipid shell thus not only improves biocompatibility but also serves as a modular interface for personalized targeting.

The synergy of polymeric core and lipid shell has been associated with improved pharmacokinetic and biological performance in individual preclinical studies, although the magnitude of benefit varies according to the comparator and model system. Reported examples include 6.5-fold increases in peak plasma concentration and 5.6-fold increases in area under the curve compared with control formulations, as well as up to 20-fold higher tumor accumulation in specific xenograft models [24, 28]. pH-sensitive formulations have shown approximately 1.6–1.7-fold higher drug release under acidic conditions, while co-loaded systems such as paclitaxel–curcumin PLNs have achieved > 90% encapsulation efficiency with enhanced cytotoxicity compared with free-drug controls [29]. Similarly, several animal studies have reported tumor growth inhibition, including reductions exceeding 60% in selected models [30, 31].

Overcoming biological barriers & immune evasion

A major limitation in breast cancer nanotherapy is the presence of multiple biological barriers that restrict nanoparticle accumulation, tumor penetration, and therapeutic efficacy. PLNs have emerged as a rationally engineered platform capable of navigating systemic circulation, evading the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), modulating immune interactions, and penetrating the dense tumor stroma characteristic of breast tumors. The hybrid architecture, composed of a polymeric core and lipid shell, integrates the structural stability of polymeric nanoparticles with the membrane-mimicking and stealth properties of liposomes, enabling prolonged circulation, controlled drug release, and enhanced tumor-specific accumulation [17, 26].

Following intravenous administration, nanoparticles face rapid clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system, particularly macrophages in the liver and spleen. Lipid–polymer nanoparticles overcome this challenge through PEGylation, optimized lipid composition, and nanoscale size control, which collectively prolong systemic circulation and reduce off-target biodistribution. Several studies demonstrate substantial improvements in pharmacokinetics and tumor accumulation. For instance, Belinostat-loaded lipid–polymer nanoparticles exhibited a 20-fold increase in tumor uptake within 4 h, suggesting improved tumor retention and altered systemic disposition in xenograft model [24]. Similarly, gemcitabine-loaded LPHNPs achieved a 4.2-fold increase in half-life compared with the commercial drug formulation, indicating prolonged systemic circulation and improved stability [32]. Chitosan-coated paclitaxel–curcumin LPHNPs further enhanced pharmacokinetic performance, showing 3.8-fold and 6.6-fold increases in AUC for curcumin and paclitaxel, respectively, along with 2.2-fold and 1.3-fold increases in half-life, demonstrating improved bioavailability and sustained drug exposure [29].

These pharmacokinetic enhancements are largely attributed to the hybrid structure, which reduces protein adsorption and macrophage-mediated clearance while maintaining sufficient colloidal stability in circulation. For example, doxorubicin-loaded LPHNPs demonstrated nanoscale stability, sustained Fickian diffusion-controlled release, enhanced cellular internalization, and greater antiproliferative activity compared with free doxorubicin formulations [33]. Similar improvements in prolonged circulation and antitumor performance were also observed in gemcitabine-loaded systems [32].

Beyond passive evasion, PLNs actively modulate immune responses within the tumor microenvironment, which is critical for overcoming immune suppression in aggressive breast cancer subtypes such as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Multifunctional lipid–polymer nanoparticles can enhance immunogenic cell death, regulate macrophage polarization, and stimulate cytotoxic T-cell responses. The iRGD-DOX-oHA-PLN system demonstrated a strong immunomodulatory effect by increasing CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, granzyme B expression, and pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IFN-γ) while simultaneously reducing IL-6 levels, regulatory T cells, and tumor-associated macrophages, resulting in effective tumor suppression and reduced pulmonary metastasis in a syngeneic TNBC model [34]. This dual role, enhancing immune activation while suppressing immunosuppressive components, illustrates the capacity of PLNs to overcome immune evasion mechanisms.

Similarly, antibody-functionalized lipid–polymer nanoparticles targeting EGFR and TROP2 introduced a multicheckpoint targeting strategy for siRNA delivery, reducing off-target effects and enhancing tumor selectivity, thereby minimizing immune-related clearance and improving therapeutic safety [35]. Optimization of lipid and polymer composition in siRNA-loaded PLNs also significantly improved cellular internalization and silencing efficiency, providing a rational framework for overcoming immune and intracellular delivery barriers [36]. Together, these strategies demonstrate that PLNs can function not only as drug carriers but also as immune-modulating platforms capable of reshaping the tumor microenvironment.

Breast tumors are characterized by dense extracellular matrices, abnormal vasculature, and elevated interstitial fluid pressure, which significantly limit nanoparticle penetration. Targeting ligands such as integrin-binding peptides, aptamers, and hyaluronic acid (HA) have been widely incorporated into PLNs to enhance tumor penetration and cellular uptake. iRGD-modified lipid–polymer nanoparticles significantly improved tumor targeting and cellular accumulation through integrin-mediated penetration and prolonged circulation, resulting in enhanced tumor growth inhibition in breast tumor-bearing mice [37]. Similarly, α(v)β[3] integrin aptamer-functionalized LPHNPs demonstrated markedly improved therapeutic efficacy, reducing cell viability to 5.9 ± 0.7% and 7.8 ± 0.6% in 4T1 and MDA-MB-231 cells, respectively, compared with 17–24% in non-targeted and free drug formulations, while increasing apoptosis to ~31% compared with ~21% and ~13% in controls [38].

CD44-targeted HA-decorated LPNPs further enhanced receptor-mediated endocytosis and intracellular drug delivery, improving anticancer activity in breast cancer cells [27]. In addition, psoralen-loaded PLNs combined with paclitaxel significantly reduced tumor growth and lung metastasis while suppressing the IRAK1/NF-κB/FAK signaling pathway, indicating effective tumor microenvironment modulation and stromal penetration [30]. These findings highlight the importance of ligand-mediated targeting in overcoming physical tumor barriers and improving intratumoral drug distribution.

Efficient intracellular delivery remains another critical barrier, particularly for nucleic acids and combination therapies. Lipid–polymer nanoparticles provide protection against degradation and enable controlled intracellular release through pH-sensitive and stimuli-responsive mechanisms. Cationic lipid–polymer nanoparticles achieved 95 ± 4% siRNA EE% and significantly downregulated IGF-1R expression in breast cancer cells, demonstrating efficient gene delivery and intracellular stability [39]. Microfluidically synthesized LiPoNs achieved 124 nm particle size, PDI < 0.2, and 60% EE%, successfully suppressing miR-21 expression and reducing tumor cell migration, highlighting their potential for RNA-based theranostics [40].

Stimuli-responsive systems further enhance intracellular delivery by exploiting tumor-specific conditions. pH-sensitive docetaxel LPHNPs showed improved cellular uptake and reduced IC50 values with lower drug distribution in vital organs [41], while pH-sensitive PLGA-TPGS hybrid nanoparticles enhanced doxorubicin accumulation and antitumor activity through controlled release [42]. Moreover, hybrid nanoparticles designed for cell-distinct drug release in cancer stem-like cells enabled differential release of all-trans retinoic acid and doxorubicin, effectively inhibiting tumor growth and metastasis in TNBC models [43]. Emodin-loaded PLNs also demonstrated 122.7 nm particle size and > 60% tumor growth inhibition, confirming enhanced apoptosis and therapeutic efficacy [23] (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Barrier-oriented PLN design strategies in breast cancer

PLN development in breast cancer is best understood as a set of formulation strategies designed to address specific biological barriers rather than as a uniform progression toward universally effective nanocarriers. Early lipid–polymer hybrid systems primarily aimed to improve drug loading, colloidal stability, circulation time, and controlled release. Although these features often improved cytotoxicity, pharmacokinetics, or tumor accumulation in preclinical models, they did not by themselves resolve the major translational barriers that limit nanomedicine efficacy in patients, including heterogeneous tumor deposition, stromal restriction, receptor variability, immune clearance, and incomplete intracellular release. Therefore, this section does not present preclinical PLN studies as clinically validated solutions. Instead, it organizes them according to the biological problem they attempt to solve: receptor-mediated uptake, tumor and stromal penetration, immune modulation, intracellular trafficking, and stimuli-responsive release. This barrier-oriented organization provides the rationale for why AI-guided design may be useful: AI is not proposed to replace mechanistic nanobiology, but to prioritize which tunable PLN variables are most relevant to a defined biological constraint.

Receptor-mediated targeting and tumor penetration

Receptor-mediated targeting is one of the most widely explored strategies for improving PLN–tumor cell interactions, but its value depends on whether the selected receptor represents a biologically relevant and sufficiently expressed barrier in the intended tumor context. In breast cancer, receptors such as folate receptor, CD44, integrins, HER2, EGFR, and TROP2 have been used to guide ligand-functionalized PLNs toward tumor cells or tumor-associated compartments [35, 44]. These approaches are therefore discussed here not as plug-and-play targeting solutions, but as examples of how surface chemistry can be matched to specific receptor landscapes and penetration barriers. Among the most extensively studied targets is the folate receptor (FR), exploited through folic acid (FA) conjugation. FA-functionalized PLNs (~143 nm; ~93% EE%) demonstrated marked therapeutic enhancement, achieving a 9–11-fold reduction in IC₅₀ alongside increased apoptosis, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and reduced migratory capacity in MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells [26]. Similarly, FA-conjugated magnetic hybrid nanoparticles (~ controlled release with pH/redox sensitivity) exhibited significantly enhanced cellular uptake and apoptosis via folate receptor-mediated endocytosis combined with dual-triggered intracellular release mechanisms [45]. Another folate-based system incorporating cholesterol grafting further confirmed the benefit of dual uptake pathways, showing enhanced internalization, sustained release over 7 days, and modulation of apoptotic markers including Bcl-2/BAX and Ki-67 in MDA-MB-231 cells [46]. These studies consistently demonstrate that folate targeting not only improves binding specificity but also amplifies downstream apoptotic signaling.

Importantly, early FA-based LPHNP systems already demonstrated significant tumor inhibition improvements compared to non-targeted controls (e.g., ~ 65% vs. 48% tumor suppression), establishing folate targeting as one of the earliest validated receptor-mediated strategies in this platform [47].

Targeting of CD44 receptors via HA and related ligands has also shown strong therapeutic impact. CD44-targeted lipid–polysaccharide nanoparticles (~30 nm) achieved a 2–3-fold reduction in IC₅₀ compared with free doxorubicin, driven by enhanced uptake and CD44-mediated internalization combined with pH/redox-responsive release behavior [48]. Similarly, HA-functionalized LPHNPs and PEGylated hybrid systems significantly increased cellular uptake and anticancer efficacy in MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 models [27]. An alternative CD44-targeting strategy using chondroitin sulfate (~172 nm; ZP ~ − 26 mV) increased uptake by 1.45-fold, reduced IC₅₀ to 3.5 µM, and induced apoptosis through Wnt/β-catenin inhibition and TRAIL sensitization [49]. These findings highlight CD44 as a robust and versatile receptor for ligand-mediated breast cancer targeting, with multiple ligand chemistries converging on similar internalization pathways.

Integrin-targeted systems further extend the scope of active targeting. iRGD-modified LPHNPs demonstrated enhanced tumor penetration and accumulation, significantly increasing apoptosis and tumor inhibition in 4T1 models through integrin-mediated homing and deep tissue penetration [37]. In a more advanced design, αvβ3 integrin aptamer-functionalized PLNs co-delivering paclitaxel and tamoxifen reduced cell viability to approximately 6–8% and increased apoptosis to ~31% in TNBC models, confirming the advantage of dual-drug and receptor-specific targeting synergy [38]. Similarly, antibody-based strategies targeting EGFR and TROP2 receptors enabled selective siRNA delivery with reduced off-target effects, highlighting the potential of antibody-functionalized PLNs for precision oncology applications [35]. HER2 targeting via trastuzumab-conjugated PLNs further validated receptor-specific uptake and enhanced cytotoxicity in HER2-positive BT474 cells [50].

Beyond classical receptor systems, low-density lipoprotein receptor–associated protein (LRP)/megalin-mediated targeting via apolipoprotein E (ApoE)-decorated lipid–polymer nanoparticles has demonstrated enhanced uptake in drug-resistant MCF-7 subpopulations, achieving approximately threefold increases in intracellular siRNA delivery and restoring chemosensitivity [51]. In addition, claudin-3 (CLDN3)-targeted antibody-functionalized lipid–polymer nanocarriers have enabled selective binding and photothermal ablation in T47D breast cancer models, highlighting a dual diagnostic–therapeutic (theranostic) strategy that integrates receptor specificity with external energy-triggered tumor destruction [52].

Recent antibody-engineered PLN systems further demonstrate “multi-checkpoint” targeting capability, where simultaneous EGFR and TROP2 silencing improves selectivity and reduces off-target gene suppression, reinforcing the shift toward combinatorial receptor targeting strategies [35].

Additional receptor systems broaden the targeting landscape. Glycan-based targeting strategies using fucose ligands improved bioavailability by 8–10-fold and enhanced tumor suppression through lectin receptor-mediated uptake [53], while fructose-conjugated PLNs exploited GLUT transporters to induce a high apoptosis index (0.89) by leveraging cancer metabolic reprogramming [54]. Sialic acid-targeting via 4-carboxy phenyl boronic acid further enhanced ROS production and reduced migration across multiple breast cancer models [55]. At a subcellular level, mitochondrial targeting using triphenylphosphonium (TPP)-modified PLNs successfully overcame doxorubicin resistance by inducing organelle-specific apoptosis [56], while nuclear targeting via AS1411 aptamer-mediated CRISPR delivery achieved gene knockout and proliferation suppression [57]. Notably, AS1411-based nuclear-targeting systems extend PLN functionality beyond cytoplasmic delivery, enabling gene editing approaches such as CRISPR/Cas-mediated knockouts with significantly improved intracellular trafficking efficiency [57].

Despite these advances, active targeting should not be viewed as a simple plug-and-play modification. The rational design of targeting moieties, including optimized aptamers, antibody fragments, peptides, or chemically engineered lipid ligands, requires careful consideration of both receptor affinity and formulation-level effects. Minor chemical changes in a ligand or lipid anchor can substantially alter the apparent pKₐ, surface charge, colloidal stability, protein corona composition, and endosomal trafficking of the resulting nanoparticle. Similarly, ligand density, orientation, and linker chemistry can influence not only receptor binding but also circulation behavior, aggregation tendency, and off-target uptake. Therefore, successful active targeting requires co-optimization of ligand chemistry, lipid–polymer composition, and manufacturing parameters rather than simple surface decoration alone.

Stimuli-responsive release and barrier-adaptive PLN design

Whereas receptor-mediated targeting is primarily intended to enhance cellular recognition and uptake, stimuli-responsive PLN design addresses the distinct pharmacological challenge of balancing systemic stability with efficient intracellular cargo release. Ideally, PLNs should maintain structural integrity during circulation, thereby minimizing premature drug leakage and off-target exposure, while enabling controlled payload release upon exposure to tumor-associated or intracellular stimuli including acidic pH, elevated glutathione concentrations, enzyme-rich microenvironments, or externally applied external triggers.

The acidic tumor microenvironment (pH ~6.5) and endosomal compartments (pH ~5.0) represent the most widely exploited triggers. pH-sensitive PLGA–TPGS hybrid nanoparticles co-loaded with doxorubicin and therapeutic agents demonstrated enhanced intracellular uptake and significant tumor reduction in vivo through acid-triggered drug release [42]. Similarly, pH-responsive LPHNPs loaded with docetaxel achieved improved pharmacokinetics, tumor-selective release, and increased survival in both MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 models [41]. Dual pH/redox-responsive systems further enhanced intracellular delivery; for instance, CD44-targeted nanoparticles (~30 nm) combined environmental sensitivity with receptor-mediated uptake, achieving superior cytotoxicity compared to free drug formulations [48]. FA-functionalized magnetic hybrid nanoparticles also utilized combined pH and glutathione (GSH)-triggered release mechanisms, enhancing intracellular drug liberation under tumor-relevant redox conditions [45].

Externally controlled delivery systems provide an additional layer of precision. Magnetic field-guided nanoparticles incorporating Fe₃O₄ enabled external targeting, resulting in reduced tumor marker expression and low systemic toxicity in vivo [58]. Similarly, multifunctional magnetic hybrid nanoparticles demonstrated controlled navigation and tumor localization, supporting the feasibility of physically guided drug delivery strategies.

Beyond these approaches, transport and barrier-specific targeting has emerged as an important direction, where PEGylated lipid–polymer nanocarriers functionalized with pluronic F127 significantly enhanced tumor penetration and reduced metastatic burden through improved mucus permeation and inhibition of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), demonstrating a delivery-oriented (non-receptor) targeting strategy [59]. Similarly, oral mucoadhesive chitosan-based lipid–polymer hybrid nanoparticles have been shown to enhance gastrointestinal residence time and systemic bioavailability of therapeutic agents while maintaining cytotoxic efficacy against MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells, supporting the feasibility of non-invasive administration routes for PLNs [60]. Furthermore, brain metastasis–targeting lipid–polymer nanocarriers capable of crossing the blood–brain barrier (BBB) via surface-engineered transport pathways have achieved up to 5.5-fold increases in circulation time and approximately fivefold enhancement in drug exposure, enabling significant tumor delay in metastatic TNBC models [61] (Fig. 2).

Taken together, these studies indicate that smart PLN development should not be viewed as a single technological category, but rather as a set of design strategies tailored to distinct biological barriers. Receptor-targeted systems are primarily designed to improve cellular recognition and uptake, stimuli-responsive systems to enable controlled payload release, immune-modulating systems to reshape suppressive tumor microenvironments, and transport-oriented systems to overcome stromal, mucosal, or blood–brain barrier restrictions. This barrier-to-design framework also clarifies where AI may contribute most directly, not as a substitute for experimental validation, but as a tool for prioritizing formulation variables most likely to improve performance under defined biological constraints.

AI-guided nanoinformatics for barrier-informed PLN design

In the context of this review, AI is not presented as an independent therapeutic solution, but as a computational strategy for linking biological barriers to modifiable PLN design variables. The barrier-oriented framework outlined above creates a set of practical design questions: which particle size and surface chemistry may improve stromal penetration; which ligand or ligand density may improve receptor-mediated uptake; which lipid–polymer composition may stabilize circulation while preserving intracellular release; and which formulation features may reduce immune clearance or toxicity. AI-guided nanoinformatics is relevant because these variables are highly interdependent and difficult to optimize through one-factor-at-a-time experimentation. Therefore, the following sections discuss AI methods according to their role in formulation optimization, molecular interaction modeling, biological fate prediction, and release-kinetic design.

Machine learning for formulation optimization and design prioritization

The formulation of PLNs involves multiple coupled variables, including polymer identity, lipid composition, lipid–polymer ratio, solvent conditions, mixing parameters, particle size, PDI, zeta potential, encapsulation efficiency, and release kinetics [62, 63]. In breast cancer, these variables are not only physicochemical descriptors; they influence biological performance by shaping circulation time, tumor penetration, cellular uptake, intracellular release, and toxicity. Conventional formulation development has often relied on iterative trial-and-error experimentation, which is poorly suited to this multidimensional design space. ML can support PLN development by identifying relationships between formulation inputs and critical quality attributes, thereby helping prioritize experimentally testable formulations rather than exhaustively screening all possible combinations.

Classical ML algorithms, including Random Forest, Support Vector Machines (SVM), and Gaussian Processes, have demonstrated strong performance in predicting critical formulation characteristics. For instance, Gaussian Process models were successfully applied to 32 poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) nanoparticle formulations, achieving low normalized root-mean-squared errors for encapsulation efficiency (NRMSE) (0.187) and cytotoxicity predictions (0.206–0.296), highlighting the model’s robustness in capturing nonlinear relationships between material properties and functional outcomes [64]. Similarly, liposome formulations have been predicted with high fidelity using ML, with particle size, PDI, zeta potential, and encapsulation efficiency accurately estimated and key formulation features ranked according to their influence on performance, enabling rational material selection [65]. Decision-tree-based algorithms have also been successfully applied to solid lipid nanoparticle formulations, where Random Forest models predicted particle size within the 263–498 nm range with minimal mean absolute error (0.028), confirming that classical supervised learning methods can reliably model formulation variables such as mixing time and lipid composition to optimize physicochemical properties [66]. More advanced regression approaches further strengthen this capability, as Gaussian Process Regression and Kernel Ridge Regression models achieved R² values of 0.9427 for particle size and 0.9841 for zeta potential in PLGA nanoparticle synthesis, with mean absolute percentage errors below 4%, demonstrating high precision in predicting formulation outcomes based on polymer concentration and anti-solvent parameters [67].

ML-driven optimization is not limited to nanoparticles. Microsphere formulations of small-molecule drugs were accelerated using ensemble ML approaches across 286 datasets, achieving R² values of 0.880 at 37 °C and 0.958 at 45 °C, indicating excellent predictive power for in vitro release profiles. Importantly, feature importance analyses provided actionable insights into the contributions of formulation components, bridging predictive modeling with experimental design. Multicomponent solid forms, such as co-crystals and co-amorphous systems, also benefit from ML-guided co-former selection, reducing the number of laboratory trials required for successful formulation [68]. Moreover, generative artificial intelligence approaches, such as LipidGAN-based hybrid algorithms, have enabled the creation of large synthetic lipid datasets exceeding 17,800 compositions, allowing systematic exploration of formulation space and identification of exosome-mimetic lipid nanoparticles with high cellular uptake (91–95%) and minimal toxicity (> 90% cell viability), illustrating the potential of generative ML to design next-generation lipid and lipid–polymer nanocarriers [69].

Advanced ML techniques, including ensemble methods, Bayesian optimization, and deep learning, have further improved formulation prediction and optimization. For example, mRNA-lipid nanoparticle vaccines were optimized using XGBoost/Bayesian methods and self-validated ensemble models, predicting particle size (94–96 nm), PDI, zeta potential, and encapsulation efficiency with > 97% accuracy, closely matching experimental results. This demonstrates the ability of ML not only to accelerate formulation design but also to provide actionable predictions for process control [70]. Lipid-based formulations, particularly for poorly soluble drugs, have been optimized using solubility-predictive ML models, such as Sol_ME, achieving correlation coefficients of 0.998 and reducing formulation volume by 75%, exemplifying practical translation to dosage form development [71]. However, these reported metrics should be interpreted as evidence of model feasibility within defined experimental spaces rather than proof of broad translational generalizability. For PLN development, the key question is not whether ML can fit existing formulation datasets, but whether it can prospectively prioritize formulations that remain robust across different biological models, manufacturing conditions, and tumor contexts.

Comparatively, while traditional trial-and-error methods remain limited by human intuition and experimental throughput, ML models can explore large parameter spaces, identify non-obvious relationships, and prioritize material and process variables. However, challenges remain, including model interpretability, generalizability, and integration with molecular and physics-based modeling approaches to ensure mechanistic understanding and clinical relevance [72, 73].

Although artificial intelligence has shown substantial promise in nanomedicine formulation design, an important limitation is that most current ML frameworks have been developed using datasets derived from conventional liposomes, solid lipid nanoparticles, or ionizable LNPs, particularly those associated with mRNA delivery systems, rather than true core–shell PLNs. Consequently, direct extrapolation of these models to PLNs should be approached cautiously. In hybrid PLNs, formulation behavior is influenced not only by lipid composition but also by complex polymer–lipid interfacial interactions, compartmentalized drug partitioning, polymer-dependent degradation kinetics, and core–shell thermodynamics, which differ substantially from those governing conventional LNP systems. Although isolated studies have explored computational or ANN-assisted optimization of PLNs, these investigations have generally relied on relatively small datasets and limited formulation spaces [74]. Similarly, computational studies involving lipid–polymer hybrid systems have primarily focused on mechanistic or physicochemical modeling rather than large-scale predictive AI frameworks [75]. These limitations highlight a major unmet need in the field: the development of curated PLN-specific datasets incorporating polymer identity, lipid composition, fabrication parameters, physicochemical properties, release behavior, and biological outcomes. In the near term, transfer-learning approaches may provide a practical strategy for adapting existing LNP-trained models to hybrid PLN systems; however, rigorous external validation using true PLN datasets will be essential before such models can be considered reliable for translational nanomedicine design.

The convergence of ML with molecular dynamics simulations and high-throughput experimental data allows for a more comprehensive understanding of formulation behavior at both macroscopic and molecular scales, exemplified in studies combining ML prediction with coarse-grained simulations to elucidate drug distribution within liposomes [65].

Advanced machine learning architectures for structure–function modeling and de novo design

Beyond classical machine-learning models, advanced deep-learning architectures, including graph neural networks and generative models, may help capture higher-order structure–function relationships that are difficult to represent using conventional regression approaches. For PLNs, these approaches are most relevant when formulation behavior depends on nonlinear interactions among polymer structure, lipid chemistry, drug properties, ligand presentation, and biological context. Their value therefore lies not simply in improving prediction accuracy, but in supporting more systematic exploration of design spaces that are too large for empirical screening alone [76, 77]. These advanced AI approaches integrate imaging data, molecular interactions, and physicochemical parameters to optimize nanoparticle targeting, drug delivery efficiency, and therapeutic response in heterogeneous tumor environments.

Deep learning for intelligent nanocarrier optimization

Deep learning has become a central tool for extracting complex structure–function relationships that govern nanoparticle behavior in breast cancer. Neural network-based models have demonstrated high predictive performance in nanoparticle internalization, with artificial neural network frameworks achieving strong correlation coefficients (Q² ≈ 0.90) in predicting cellular uptake in TNBC, thereby establishing a quantitative relationship between nanoparticle physicochemical properties and tumor cell interaction and providing a robust foundation for PLN formulation optimization [78]. Similarly, convolutional neural networks such as VGG16, ResNet50, and Inception V3 have enabled automated classification of nanoparticle uptake from confocal imaging data, significantly accelerating formulation screening and supporting AI-guided selection of optimal nanocarriers for targeted therapy [79].

Beyond uptake prediction, deep learning has improved nanoparticle visualization and therapeutic monitoring. U-Net-based models enable automated intracellular tracking of plasmonic nanoparticles, facilitating real-time assessment of biodistribution and delivery efficiency, while advanced nano-CT deep learning approaches allow high-resolution three-dimensional quantification of nanoparticle localization at the single-cell level [80, 81]. Imaging-driven AI has also been applied to contrast nanoparticle systems to predict signal retention and safety profiles, supporting imaging-guided therapy and clinical translation of intelligent nanocarriers [82]. In therapeutic monitoring, CNN-based plasmonic nanoparticle analysis has achieved approximately 92% accuracy in predicting drug response and chemoresistance patterns, demonstrating strong potential for AI-assisted clinical decision support in breast cancer treatment [83]. Single-cell AI profiling further enhances this capability by enabling precise characterization of nanoparticle drug uptake heterogeneity, supporting precision therapy design in breast cancer [84]. Despite these advances, these reported performance metrics should be interpreted with caution. Many AI models in nanoinformatics and formulation science are still developed using relatively small, homogeneous, or internally generated datasets, such as limited formulation libraries, single-platform imaging datasets, or narrowly defined nanoparticle chemistries. Under these conditions, high R², Q², or classification accuracy may reflect strong performance within a constrained experimental space rather than robust generalizability across laboratories, nanoparticle classes, biological models, or manufacturing conditions. This concern is particularly relevant for PLN design, where changes in polymer identity, lipid composition, fabrication method, serum environment, tumor subtype, or immune context may shift the model outside its training distribution. Therefore, future AI-guided PLN studies should prioritize independent external validation, out-of-distribution testing, standardized benchmark datasets, and transparent reporting of data partitioning, model tuning, and validation procedures. Until more standardized validation frameworks are established, many currently reported predictive metrics should be interpreted primarily as proof-of-concept evidence rather than definitive indicators of clinical or translational readiness.

Graph neural networks for molecular interaction modeling

Graph neural networks, as a specialized class of deep-learning models, represents a critical advancement in modeling the molecular interactions that determine PLN performance. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) enable the representation of polymers, lipids, drugs, and biological targets as interconnected molecular networks, allowing accurate prediction of binding affinity, transport mechanisms, and structural stability within complex biological systems [85]. For PLNs, graph-based models are conceptually useful because the platform is defined by interacting molecular components rather than a single active pharmaceutical ingredient: polymer chains, lipid shells, drug molecules, targeting ligands, and proteins at the nano–bio interface collectively determine performance.

Recent developments in molecular modeling highlight the potential of AI-driven interaction prediction in nanomedicine. Machine learning integrated with molecular dynamics simulations has been used to predict receptor–ligand interactions in liposomal systems, enabling stable targeting and improving drug delivery precision in breast cancer [86]. Predictive modeling of amphiphilic peptide-based nanocarriers has further demonstrated accurate estimation of drug loading capacity and toxicity through physicochemical interaction analysis, providing a computational basis for rational polymer–lipid interface design [87]. Moreover, AI-guided protein and ligand engineering enables programmable nanocarrier interactions and optimized transcytosis targeting, highlighting the future potential of GNN-driven PLN surface engineering and molecular-level customization [88]. These advances illustrate how graph-based AI frameworks can simulate polymer–lipid–drug interactions prior to experimental validation, significantly reducing development time and enabling precision-tailored PLN architectures.

Generative AI for De Novo PLN Design

Generative AI, often implemented through deep-learning architectures such as GANs, VAEs, transformers, or diffusion-based models, is conceptually distinct from predictive modeling. Whereas predictive models estimate predefined outcomes from existing inputs, generative models are designed to create, sample, or propose new candidate structures, formulations, or design spaces that can subsequently be evaluated by predictive models or experimental validation. In nanomedicine, this distinction is important because generative AI should be interpreted primarily as a design-space exploration tool rather than as direct evidence of therapeutic performance.

Early applications of generative adversarial networks demonstrated the ability to predict intratumoral nanoparticle distribution at the pixel level, enabling accurate modeling of nanoparticle penetration and supporting rational tumor-targeting strategies [89]. Recent applications such as LipidGAN illustrate this concept by generating and exploring large numbers of candidate lipid compositions with desirable physicochemical and biological profiles, rather than simply predicting a single optimal formulation. Although these approaches have mainly been developed for lipid nanoparticles rather than true core–shell PLNs, they provide a conceptual blueprint for future PLN discovery, where the generative design space could be expanded to include polymer identity, lipid–polymer ratio, core–shell assembly conditions, surface functionalization, release kinetics, and manufacturability [69].

Recent progress also indicates that the field is moving beyond conventional generative models toward foundation model-guided and autonomous laboratory platforms. The AGILE platform combined deep learning with combinatorial chemistry to accelerate ionizable lipid discovery for mRNA delivery, enabling in silico screening, synthesis, and evaluation of lipid candidates while revealing cell-type-specific preferences in LNP performance [90]. More recently, LUMI-lab integrated a transformer-based foundation model with active learning and robotic experimentation to create a self-driving workflow for lipid discovery. This platform autonomously synthesized and screened more than 1,700 LNPs and identified new ionizable lipid features, including brominated lipid tails, that improved mRNA delivery and in vivo gene editing efficiency [91]. Although these platforms have primarily been developed for ionizable LNPs rather than true core–shell PLNs, they provide a highly relevant blueprint for future PLN discovery. In principle, similar foundation model-driven workflows could be adapted to hybrid polymer–lipid architectures by expanding the design space to include polymer identity, lipid–polymer ratios, core–shell assembly conditions, microfluidic parameters, release kinetics, and biological performance. This transition from model-assisted prediction to closed-loop autonomous experimentation may represent a key step toward data-efficient discovery of next-generation smart PLNs.

Generative and multi-modal AI approaches are also being applied to immunotherapy and gene delivery systems, enabling personalized nanomedicine strategies through biomarker prediction, aptamer-guided targeting, and siRNA nanoparticle optimization [92, 93]. AI-assisted materials design and biomimetic nanoparticle modeling further reinforce the potential of generative frameworks to create adaptive and data-driven nanocarriers for breast cancer precision therapy [94, 95].

Toward autonomous intelligent PLN platforms

The convergence of machine learning, deep-learning architectures, graph-based models, and generative design frameworks is driving the development of autonomous PLN platforms capable of predictive design, adaptive targeting, and imaging-informed therapeutic monitoring. AI-assisted nanotheranostics and diagnostic systems have already demonstrated improved accuracy in breast cancer detection and treatment optimization, supporting the clinical translation of intelligent nanocarriers [96–99]. In parallel, predictive modeling of chemotherapy resistance, research trends, and drug release kinetics highlights the growing importance of AI-driven precision nanomedicine and reinforces the role of PLNs in future breast cancer therapy [100–102].

However, the development of truly autonomous PLN platforms remains technically challenging. Such systems require integration of heterogeneous data streams, including formulation parameters, physicochemical characterization, imaging, omics, pharmacokinetic profiles, and biological-response datasets, which are often generated using incompatible experimental standards and data formats [103, 104]. In addition, interoperability between predictive machine-learning models, molecular simulations, PBPK frameworks, generative design systems, and automated manufacturing platforms remains limited [103]. Closed-loop autonomous workflows also require robust validation pipelines capable of continuously comparing computational predictions with experimental outcomes, while accounting for batch variability, biological uncertainty, and out-of-distribution behavior [104]. Importantly, autonomous systems must address explainability, traceability, and regulatory auditability, particularly if AI-generated formulations are intended for translational or clinical applications [105].

In practical terms, an autonomous PLN platform would likely operate as an iterative closed-loop workflow rather than a fully unsupervised system. Patient-derived or experimentally generated biological data could first be integrated into AI-based predictive frameworks to prioritize candidate PLN formulations. These computationally prioritized formulations could then be synthesized using programmable microfluidic platforms and subsequently evaluated through physicochemical characterization, in vitro assays, biodistribution analysis, and toxicity testing. Experimental feedback would then be reintegrated into the computational pipeline through active-learning or reinforcement-learning strategies, enabling progressive refinement of formulation hypotheses over multiple design cycles [104, 106]. Similar closed-loop and self-driving laboratory concepts are increasingly being explored in nanomaterials discovery, AI-assisted microfluidics, and digital-twin-oriented nanomedicine workflows [103, 105].

Predictive modeling of biological fate

Accurate prediction of the biological fate and biodistribution of PLNs remains a critical challenge in cancer nanomedicine, as inefficient tumor delivery and complex whole-body pharmacokinetics continue to limit clinical translation; consequently, physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling integrated with machine learning has emerged as a powerful in silico strategy to quantitatively simulate nanoparticle distribution across tissues and optimize delivery efficiency before experimental validation. An artificial intelligence-assisted PBPK framework integrating a machine learning-based quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) model with deep neural networks demonstrated strong predictive performance for nanoparticle tumor delivery, achieving determination coefficients of R² = 0.83 for delivery efficiency at 24 h, R² = 0.56 at 168 h, and R² = 0.82 for maximum delivery efficiency, with RMSE values of 3.01, 2.27, and 3.51, respectively, and showing strong agreement with experimental pharmacokinetic data (R² ≥ 0.70 in 133 of 288 datasets), highlighting the feasibility of predicting nanoparticle biodistribution directly from physicochemical properties without relying on extensive animal experiments [107]. Complementary machine learning models trained on the Nano-Tumor Database further demonstrated that deep neural networks outperform conventional regression and ensemble models in predicting tissue distribution, achieving R² values of 0.79 in spleen, 0.87 in lung, 0.83 in kidney, and 0.41 in tumor, while identifying nanoparticle core material as a dominant determinant of delivery efficiency and enabling high-throughput screening through a web-based predictive dashboard [108]. Multiscale PBPK modeling has been further advanced through physics-informed and mechanistic frameworks, where a multiphysics compartmental model incorporating vascular hydrodynamics, immune system interactions, and nanoparticle surface chemistry successfully reproduced experimental biodistribution patterns and demonstrated that vascular branching significantly enhances organ uptake, while neural network-assisted ODE solvers improved computational efficiency in solving stiff pharmacokinetic systems [109]. Similarly, a DeepONet-enabled multiscale framework combining a Fokker–Planck equation for nanoparticle margination in the red-blood-cell-free layer with PBPK modeling enabled prediction of microvascular concentration profiles based on hematocrit and vessel radius, providing a physiologically grounded approach to simulate organ-level biodistribution and circulation dynamics [110]. Immune-aware pharmacokinetic modeling has also been introduced to address the critical role of tumor-associated macrophages in nanoparticle retention, with a six-compartment PBPK model demonstrating that immune cell-mediated sequestration in the tumor microenvironment significantly influences nanoparticle accumulation and should be considered a central determinant of biodistribution rather than traditional passive targeting assumptions [111]. Mechanistic PBPK models applied to gold nanoparticle drug delivery systems further showed improved pharmacokinetic performance, including increased liver partition coefficient from 0.27 to 0.55 and mean residence time from 1.28 to 5.67 h, with predicted concentrations remaining within 0.5–2-fold error of in vivo data, supporting the reliability of PBPK-based extrapolation across tissues and administration scenarios [112]. Advanced therapeutic PBPK modeling has also been extended to mRNA-based cancer therapies, where a hierarchical multiscale PBPK framework successfully predicted whole-body distribution of recombinant and mRNA-encoded bispecific T-cell engagers and enabled in silico optimization of dose and administration schedules, reducing development time and supporting personalized treatment design [113]. Experimental validation of in silico biodistribution predictions is further supported by MRI-trackable magnetoliposomes that mimic clinically approved liposomal formulations, demonstrating comparable tumor deposition (1.5 × 10⁸ ± 1.8 × 10⁷ vs. 1.2 × 10⁸ ± 6.3 × 10⁷ fluorescence intensity) and prolonged circulation with tumor accumulation up to 24 h and imaging capability up to 1 week, highlighting the potential of predictive modeling combined with imaging surrogates for patient stratification and clinical translation of nanoparticle delivery systems [114]. These machine learning-enhanced PBPK approaches establish a comprehensive and multiscale quantitative foundation for predicting nanoparticle biodistribution by integrating physicochemical properties, vascular transport dynamics, immune interactions, and therapeutic kinetics. Importantly, this convergence not only accelerates the rational optimization of PLNs but also lays the groundwork for the development of unified, patient-specific predictive frameworks that can reduce reliance on animal studies and enable precision-guided nanomedicine in breast cancer and other solid tumors.

The formation of the protein corona (PC) at the nano–bio interface is a key determinant of PLN biodistribution and therapeutic performance, as nanoparticles rapidly adsorb biomolecules in biological fluids, creating a new biological identity that governs immune recognition, targeting efficiency, and pharmacokinetics. Recent evidence shows that disease-associated metabolic conditions can significantly reshape corona composition; for instance, triglyceride-rich environments increase lipoprotein adsorption while reducing immunoglobulin and complement proteins, leading to enhanced macrophage activation and greater liver and spleen accumulation of nanoparticles in hypertriglyceridemic models compared with normal physiological conditions [115]. Beyond metabolic modulation, personalized protein corona strategies have demonstrated clinical potential, where disease-specific protein–aptamer coronas enabled highly accurate multiplex cancer diagnosis with AUC values of 99.50% (ovarian cancer, n = 121) and 96.54% (NSCLC, n = 107). Advances in proteomics and artificial intelligence further highlight the predictive power of corona profiling, with machine learning analysis identifying 887 corona proteins and 22 key biomarkers associated with metastatic prostate cancer and atherosclerosis [116], while proteomic transfer learning integrating 956 serum and 4730 urine proteins achieved diagnostic AUC of 0.996 and staging AUC of 0.914 for bladder cancer. Mechanistically, the biomolecular corona regulates targeting, absorption, distribution, metabolism, and clearance of lipid nanoparticles, and its systematic characterization is increasingly recognized as essential for optimizing gene delivery and therapeutic precision [117]. High-resolution analytical approaches such as single-nanoparticle fluorescence quantification confirm that corona formation is highly heterogeneous and dynamic, with protein exchange occurring between vascular and extravascular environments, including strong collagen binding that can replace albumin on nanoparticle surfaces [118, 119]. Overall, these findings establish the protein corona as a dynamic and AI-predictable regulator of the nano–bio interface, underscoring the importance of integrating metabolic context, proteomic profiling, and machine learning to design corona-aware PLNs with improved targeting and translational potential in cancer nanomedicine.

Predicting toxicity and immunogenicity is a critical component of PLN design, as nanomaterials can induce cytotoxicity, inflammation, genotoxicity, and organ-specific adverse effects that often limit clinical translation; consequently, artificial intelligence-driven nanotoxicology and mechanistic safety modeling have emerged as essential tools for early-stage risk assessment and IC50 prediction. Recent machine learning frameworks integrating PBPK modeling enable accurate prediction of both in vitro and in vivo nanoparticle toxicity and organ-specific exposure, providing explainable structure–toxicity relationships and allowing rational design of safer nanoparticles while reducing reliance on extensive animal testing [120]. Similarly, surface chemistry-informed machine learning models have mapped the toxicity landscape of superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles, demonstrating that coating type, charge, and functional groups strongly influence cytotoxic responses, with positively charged chitosan-derivative coatings showing higher predicted toxicity, thereby guiding safer nanoparticle formulation strategies [121]. Large-scale cytotoxicity modeling using datasets comprising 2,060 instances from 56 studies and 23 physicochemical and biological features identified drug concentration, nanoparticle composition, and cell type as the most important determinants of IC50 and cytotoxic outcomes, with random forest models achieving strong predictive performance and association rule mining revealing high-risk conditions linked to toxic responses [122]. Experimental and in silico evidence further supports the need for integrated toxicity assessment, as cysteamine-modified silver nanoparticles showed dose-dependent hematological toxicity in vivo, with 300 mg/kg inducing toxic responses and lower doses (25–50 mg/kg) reducing RBC counts, highlighting the importance of predictive safety modeling before clinical application [123]. Mechanistic studies also demonstrate that nanoparticle toxicity is closely linked to oxidative stress and inflammation, where carbon black nanoparticles induce DNA damage and inflammatory responses correlated with surface area and dose, emphasizing the role of physicochemical parameters in genotoxicity prediction [124]. Advanced high-content screening and 3D multicellular tumor spheroid models further enhance toxicity prediction by enabling single-cell and subcellular resolution analysis of nanoparticle-induced membrane, mitochondrial, and lysosomal damage, revealing heterogeneous cellular responses that cannot be captured in conventional 2D cultures [125, 126]. Integration of multi-omics and machine learning has also enabled identification of protective agents against nanotoxicity, with transcriptomics–proteomics–metabolomics analysis revealing 121 genes, 45 proteins, and 17 metabolites involved in silica-coated magnetic nanoparticle toxicity and identifying glutathione and citrate as protective compounds that reduce reactive oxygen species and neurotoxicity in vivo [127]. Moreover, machine learning models predicting cancer stem cell induction by polystyrene nanoparticles demonstrate that nanotoxicity can extend beyond cytotoxicity to tumor aggressiveness, as exposure increased CSC markers in breast and colon cancer cells and enabled predictive modeling of tumor progression dynamics [128]. These advances demonstrate that AI-driven nanotoxicology, IC50 prediction models, PBPK-informed safety frameworks, and multi-omics toxicity profiling provide a comprehensive strategy for predicting immunogenicity and adverse effects of PLNs, enabling the rational design of safer nanomedicines with optimized therapeutic windows and improved translational potential in cancer therapy.

Deep learning in drug release kinetics

Deep learning is increasingly being integrated into nanomedicine to model and predict drug release kinetics, shifting formulation development from empirical trial-and-error toward data-driven time-series prediction of release profiles. Computational frameworks combining artificial intelligence with molecular simulations enable mechanistic understanding of how nanoparticle composition, surface charge, lipid–polymer interactions, and ligand functionalization influence controlled drug release, thereby allowing rational optimization of sustained versus burst release behavior in lipid-based nanocarriers and polymeric–lipid hybrid nanoparticles. These approaches bridge atomic-scale molecular dynamics insights with macroscopic release kinetics, enabling prediction of release stability, diffusion behavior, and carrier degradation patterns before experimental validation, which significantly reduces formulation screening time and improves reproducibility in controlled-release systems [129].

Quantitative machine learning models demonstrate the practical feasibility of predictive release kinetics, where curated datasets from 115 studies and 190 experimental datapoints were used to forecast cumulative drug release from nanoparticles using Random Forest and XGBoost algorithms, achieving strong predictive performance and identifying key determinants of release such as polymer composition, particle size, and encapsulation parameters, while excluding low-impact variables like release medium temperature through feature importance analysis. The superiority of Random Forest over XGBoost across multiple time points highlights the importance of algorithm selection and dataset refinement in achieving reliable kinetic predictions, providing a clear framework for applying similar models to polymeric–lipid nanoparticles where release dynamics are governed by lipid diffusion, polymer degradation, and drug–matrix interactions [130].

From a conceptual standpoint, deep learning contributes to three core aspects of release kinetics modeling in PLNs: prediction of time-dependent release curves, identification of formulation–release relationships, and optimization of sustained-release architectures. AI-driven nanomedicine platforms increasingly integrate QSAR-based modeling, high-throughput screening, and predictive analytics to optimize controlled-release formulations and personalize dosing strategies, although challenges remain in handling heterogeneous datasets, ensuring model interpretability, and capturing complex in vivo release dynamics. Current models are largely trained on limited or heterogeneous experimental datasets and often lack standardized kinetic parameters, which restricts their generalizability across nanoparticle platforms and slows clinical translation [131].

Recent advances in intelligent nanocarriers further emphasize adaptive and controllable drug release as a central objective of AI-driven nanomedicine, where biodegradable and biocompatible nanomaterials are optimized to achieve precise release rates, reduced toxicity, and real-time therapeutic monitoring. Deep learning-assisted optimization enables balancing of therapeutic efficacy and release stability, supporting the development of next-generation nanocarriers capable of controlled degradation and programmable drug release, which is particularly relevant for polymeric–lipid nanoparticles designed for sustained chemotherapeutic delivery in breast cancer [132]. Similarly, AI-guided nanocarrier design workflows demonstrate how computational modeling can engineer sustained-release core–shell structures with optimized drug loading and controlled release characteristics, reinforcing the role of AI in linking formulation parameters directly to kinetic behavior rather than downstream therapeutic outcomes [133].

Despite these advances, deep learning in drug release kinetics remains constrained by limited high-quality PLN-specific datasets, insufficient time-series modeling frameworks, and the lack of standardized evaluation metrics across studies. Most available models focus on general nanocarriers or polymeric systems rather than polymeric–lipid hybrids, creating a translational gap between computational predictions and clinically relevant PLNs. Addressing this limitation requires PLN-focused kinetic datasets, integration of sequential deep learning architectures for time-dependent release prediction, and tighter coupling of molecular simulation outputs with experimental release curves to create robust predictive pipelines for controlled drug delivery. Deep learning-driven kinetic modeling represents a critical step toward fully programmable PLNs, where drug release can be predicted, tuned, and personalized at the formulation design stage, directly supporting the development of precision nanomedicine (Fig. 3). Overall, AI is most defensible in PLN development when it is framed as a prioritization and integration tool rather than as a replacement for mechanistic experimentation. Its contribution is strongest where multiple variables must be optimized simultaneously: formulation composition, manufacturing parameters, biological barrier profiles, release kinetics, biodistribution, and safety. In this framework, AI does not independently solve the clinical translation problem of nanomedicine. Rather, it can help generate more biologically informed hypotheses, reduce inefficient formulation screening, identify failure-prone designs earlier, and support prospective testing of PLN candidates under defined experimental and regulatory constraints.

Why AI remains relevant despite limited clinical translation of nanomedicine

The limited clinical translation of cancer nanomedicine does not mean that formulation optimization is irrelevant. Rather, it shows that many nanocarriers have been developed without sufficiently predictive links between physicochemical design, biological fate, and patient-level delivery heterogeneity. Although nanomedicines can improve pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, and toxicity profiles, their efficacy is often constrained by inefficient tumor accumulation, off-target organ uptake, heterogeneous EPR effects, poor tumor penetration, and incomplete understanding of in vivo behavior [134–136]. Therefore, the translation gap should not be interpreted as evidence that nanocarrier design is unimportant, but as evidence that formulation design must be more tightly connected to biological barriers and clinically relevant delivery constraints.

These limitations are especially relevant to PLNs because their hybrid architecture creates a multidimensional formulation space. Polymer identity, lipid composition, lipid–polymer ratio, drug–carrier compatibility, particle size, surface charge, PEGylation, ligand density, drug loading, and release kinetics can simultaneously influence colloidal stability, protein adsorption, macrophage uptake, tumor penetration, intracellular release, biodistribution, and toxicity [137–140]. Therefore, small formulation changes may produce multiple downstream biological effects, making one-factor-at-a-time optimization inefficient.

In this setting, AI is best understood as a decision-support and hypothesis-prioritization tool. Machine learning, PBPK modeling, QSAR/QSPR approaches, and related computational methods can help connect nanoparticle properties with biodistribution, tumor delivery, pharmacokinetics, nano–bio interactions, and toxicity [107, 141]. These models do not replace mechanistic biology or experimental validation, but they may help reduce inefficient screening, identify failure-prone designs earlier, and prioritize formulation variables that are most relevant to defined biological barriers.

For PLN development, a more realistic translational workflow should begin with a specific delivery problem, such as stromal restriction, receptor heterogeneity, immune clearance, poor intracellular release, or metastatic-site delivery. Mechanistic and imaging-based studies can define the barrier, computational models can prioritize modifiable PLN variables, and controlled experimental systems can validate candidate formulations. Human-relevant platforms, including tumor organoids, multiscale imaging, intravital microscopy, and theranostic tracking, may strengthen this feedback loop by providing more predictive readouts of tumor penetration, biodistribution, and treatment response [142–144].

Thus, AI-guided PLN optimization should not be presented as a solution that has already overcome the historical limitations of cancer nanomedicine. Its current value is more limited but still important: organizing complex formulation spaces, supporting biologically informed design hypotheses, and improving the efficiency and reproducibility of preclinical formulation development. The evidence remains predominantly preclinical, computational, and proof-of-concept, and prospective validation will be essential before AI-guided PLNs can be considered clinically reliable.

The convergence: Ai-guided, patient-specific PLNs

Translating tumor biology into patient-specific PLN design

Multi-omics profiling is increasingly used in breast cancer precision oncology to refine diagnosis, prognosis, molecular subtype classification, therapeutic selection, and longitudinal disease monitoring [145]. Its relevance to PLNs, however, lies in a more specific translational function: converting tumor-level biological information into modifiable delivery parameters. In contrast to small-molecule therapy, where molecular profiling often supports selection of a pathway-directed drug, PLN development must also address whether a carrier can accumulate in tumor tissue, penetrate the tumor parenchyma, enter the appropriate cellular compartment, avoid premature immune clearance, and release its cargo under the intended biological conditions. Therefore, multi-omics is not presented as a generic personalization tool, but as a source of biological constraints that can guide formulation decisions such as particle size, lipid–polymer composition, surface functionalization, ligand selection, release kinetics, and intracellular delivery strategy.

This distinction is central to precision nanomedicine because the therapeutic performance of nanocarriers depends on sequential biological barriers rather than target expression alone. Many cancer nanomedicines fail to achieve optimal therapeutic indices because of poor tumor accumulation, inefficient cellular internalization, limited tissue penetration, or inaccurate subcellular localization. Fan et al. emphasized that effective nanomedicine design should consider hierarchical targeting at the tumor tissue, cellular, and organelle levels rather than relying on a single targeting mechanism [146]. Accordingly, breast cancer profiling becomes relevant to PLN design when molecular and microenvironmental features are mapped onto delivery-relevant variables. For example, receptor expression may guide ligand selection, extracellular matrix and stromal features may influence particle size and surface chemistry, immune contexture may shape stealth or immunomodulatory design, and resistance-associated pathways may guide combination loading or sequential release.

Receptor expression provides the most direct example of how tumor profiling can inform PLN design. Breast tumors show heterogeneous expression of therapeutic and delivery-relevant receptors, including HER2, EGFR, TROP2, CD44, folate receptor, integrins, and other surface markers. In small-molecule therapy, receptor or pathway information may primarily determine whether a molecular inhibitor is appropriate. In ligand-functionalized PLNs, however, the same information must be translated into surface engineering decisions, including the choice of ligand, ligand density, linker chemistry, orientation, surface charge, and colloidal stability. For example, folate-modified lipid–polymer hybrid nanoparticles improved paclitaxel delivery and tumor inhibition compared with non-targeted nanoparticles, demonstrating how receptor expression can be converted into a ligand-guided delivery strategy [35]. Similarly, antibody-directed lipid–polymer hybrid nanoparticles targeting EGFR and TROP2 have been developed for siRNA delivery, illustrating how multi-receptor expression patterns may be used to reduce dependence on a single antigen and improve target selectivity (Das et al., 2025).

Stromal architecture and extracellular matrix composition represent a second class of biological inputs that are particularly relevant to nanoparticle design. Dense matrix, abnormal vasculature, and elevated interstitial fluid pressure can restrict nanoparticle penetration, meaning that formulation variables such as particle size, surface hydrophilicity, charge, PEGylation, degradability, and size-transformable behavior may determine therapeutic performance. Zhang et al. reviewed how tumor penetration is strongly influenced by nanoparticle size, shape, and surface charge, and argued that poor penetration is a major contributor to the limited efficacy of nanomedicines in solid tumors [147]. Consistent with this principle, matrix metalloproteinase-sensitive size-shrinkable nanoparticles were designed to reduce particle size within the tumor microenvironment, improving deep tumor penetration and pH-triggered doxorubicin release [148]. More recently, tumor progression has been shown to alter the relative importance of particle size and ligand-mediated multivalent interactions, indicating that active targeting nanoparticle design may need to be adapted to tumor stage and microenvironmental structure [149].

The tumor immune microenvironment also provides design-relevant information for PLNs. Immune-cold tumors, macrophage-rich tumors, or tumors with immunosuppressive stromal programs may require different delivery strategies than immune-inflamed tumors. In this setting, TIME profiling may inform whether a PLN should prioritize immune evasion, macrophage targeting, immunogenic cell death, immune checkpoint modulation, or combination chemoimmunotherapy. For example, iRGD-DOX-oHA-PLNs were designed to combine integrin-mediated tumor penetration with CD44 engagement and immunomodulatory effects in triple-negative breast cancer, leading to increased CD8-positive T-cell infiltration, enhanced pro-inflammatory cytokine signaling, and reduced immunosuppressive mediators [34]. Similarly, nanomedicine-based immunotherapy strategies in breast cancer increasingly aim to remodel immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments rather than merely deliver cytotoxic drugs [150]. These examples support the view that immune profiling can shape formulation-level decisions related to targeting, cargo selection, and immune interaction.

Resistance-associated tumor features can also be translated into PLN design variables. Genomic, transcriptomic, and liquid biopsy–derived data may identify drug-resistance pathways, evolving clonal populations, or treatment-induced adaptive states. In conventional precision oncology, such information may be used to change the therapeutic agent. For PLNs, it may also inform the delivery architecture, including co-loaded cargo, RNA-based payloads, release sequence, subcellular targeting, and adaptive reformulation. For example, lipid–polymer hybrid nanoparticles have been engineered for cell-distinct release of all-trans retinoic acid and doxorubicin to overcome cancer stem-like cell–associated chemoresistance in triple-negative breast cancer [62]. Liquid biopsy may further support longitudinal monitoring of tumor evolution and residual disease, which is relevant to future adaptive nanomedicine strategies because changing tumor biology may require not only a change in drug choice but also a change in delivery design [151, 152].

Artificial intelligence provides the computational layer that can connect these biological constraints to experimentally testable formulation choices. AI is not proposed here as a substitute for mechanistic biological research, nor is multi-omics assumed to be essential for every AI-guided nanomedicine application. Rather, AI is positioned as a tool for integrating heterogeneous tumor and formulation data, identifying structure–function relationships, and prioritizing nanoparticle designs for experimental validation. Adir et al. described how AI can support precision cancer nanomedicine by analyzing patient-specific disease profiles and optimizing nanomaterial properties according to predicted interactions with drugs, biological fluids, the immune system, vasculature, and cell membranes [153]. More recent reviews similarly emphasize that machine learning can assist nanoparticle design by modeling synthesis parameters, nano–bio interactions, protein corona formation, biodistribution, tumor penetration, and cellular internalization [154, 155]. Thus, the conceptual link among tumor profiling, AI, and PLNs is not that each field independently advances precision medicine, but that tumor biology defines the delivery problem, AI helps prioritize candidate formulations, and PLNs provide a tunable material platform through which those predictions can be tested.

Accordingly, patient-specific PLN design can be framed as a two-step translational process. First, tumor profiling identifies delivery-relevant biological constraints, including receptor heterogeneity, stromal restriction, immune contexture, metastatic niche, vascular permeability, and resistance mechanisms. Second, AI-assisted formulation models prioritize modifiable PLN variables, including polymer core composition, lipid shell chemistry, particle size, surface ligand strategy, release kinetics, and manufacturing parameters. This framing directly addresses the conceptual distinction between small-molecule precision therapy and nanomedicine: multi-omics may guide both, but in PLNs its added value is the translation of tumor biology into delivery architecture rather than drug selection alone.

Digital twin concepts for integrating biological and formulation data

AI and digital twin technologies are emerging as powerful enablers for the design, optimization, and manufacturing of advanced nanomedicine platforms [156]. In the context of lipid and polymeric nanoparticles, AI-driven predictive models, including ML algorithms and generative adversarial networks (GANs), have demonstrated the capacity to optimize key formulation parameters such as lipid composition, encapsulation efficiency, and tumor targeting [157]. For example, in RNA-based lipid nanoparticles developed for cancer vaccines, AI-assisted approaches have significantly accelerated formulation optimization, while digital twin models have been applied to streamline downstream processes such as lyophilization, reducing development timelines from years to months [157]. In parallel, digital twin simulations of microfluidic systems enable precise control over nanoparticle size, dispersity, and reproducibility by directly linking process parameters to critical quality attributes [158]. Moreover, Quality by Digital Design (QbDD) frameworks integrate mechanistic understanding with data-driven modeling to systematically map the relationships between process variables and nanoparticle performance [159]. End-to-end digital twin platforms further support modular simulation of continuous nanoparticle production, allowing sensitivity analyses, process optimization, and workflow adaptation without extensive experimental burden [160].

However, despite these advances, the application of AI and digital twin strategies to PLNs, particularly in the context of breast cancer, remains largely unexplored. The hybrid structural nature of PLNs, combining lipid and polymeric components, creates a multidimensional and highly interdependent design space that cannot be efficiently addressed through conventional trial-and-error approaches [161]. In addition, the pronounced heterogeneity of breast tumors, including variations in receptor expression, molecular subtype, and TIME, further complicates the identification of optimal nanoparticle configurations for individual patients [162].

In this framework, AI is better conceptualized as an integrative decision-support layer rather than as an autonomous designer. Its role is to connect patient- or model-derived biological constraints with formulation variables that can be experimentally tested, refined, or rejected. By combining formulation parameters with patient-specific biological data, such as genomic, proteomic, and imaging-derived features, AI-driven models can predict optimal nanoparticle characteristics, including size, lipid-to-polymer ratio, surface functionalization, and targeting ligand selection [163]. Such integrative modeling has the potential to transform PLN development from an empirical process into a predictive and data-driven workflow.

Building on this concept, a digital-twin-inspired framework can be used to organize the iterative development of PLNs for breast cancer, linking biological inputs, formulation parameters, microfluidic fabrication, and experimental feedback. In this framework, patient-specific inputs, including tumor receptor profiles, molecular signatures, and TIME characteristics, are incorporated into predictive models to generate optimized nanoparticle formulations tailored to individual disease profiles. These formulations can then be synthesized and experimentally evaluated, with the resulting biological and therapeutic outcomes fed back into the model to iteratively refine predictions. This closed-loop system, analogous to a digital twin, enables continuous learning and adaptation, bridging the gap between computational design and clinical application.

Despite its considerable promise, several challenges remain. The predictive accuracy of AI models is often constrained by the availability, quality, and diversity of training datasets, while current digital twin implementations may not fully capture the dynamic complexity of tumor biology or treatment response. Furthermore, the integration of multi-omics data and TIME-specific features into nanoparticle design pipelines is still in its early stages, limiting the realization of truly personalized nanomedicine [164].

Importantly, fully integrated digital-twin-guided PLN systems that combine patient-specific biological profiling, AI-driven formulation prediction, automated microfluidic manufacturing, and adaptive therapeutic optimization have not yet been clinically demonstrated. Current evidence primarily supports individual components of this framework in isolated preclinical or proof-of-concept settings rather than fully closed-loop translational systems [165]. Accordingly, the framework presented in this review should be interpreted primarily as a long-term conceptual and translational roadmap rather than a near-term clinically established platform.

Microfluidic manufacturing for reproducible translation of computationally prioritized PLN designs

One of the critical bottlenecks in translating AI-driven nanomedicine design into clinical reality lies in the disconnect between in silico optimization and physical fabrication. While computational models can prioritize candidate PLN compositions and processing conditions, conventional bulk synthesis methods lack the precision and reproducibility required to faithfully implement these formulations. In this context, microfluidic technologies emerge as a pivotal enabling platform that can bridge this translational gap by converting digital design outputs into physically consistent nanocarriers in a controlled and scalable manner [166].

Microfluidic systems offer a fundamentally different paradigm compared to traditional batch synthesis by leveraging laminar flow and diffusion-dominated mixing at the microscale. This enables precise control over nanoparticle physicochemical properties, including size, PDI, morphology, and encapsulation efficiency, which are otherwise highly variable in bulk methods due to uncontrolled mixing kinetics [167]. Importantly, such fine-tuned control directly aligns with the needs of AI-guided formulation, where small variations in parameters can significantly influence biological performance.

Recent advances demonstrate that microfluidic hydrodynamic focusing (HF) can be systematically engineered to tailor nanoparticle attributes through modulation of flow rate, inlet configuration, and channel geometry. Computational and experimental analyses reveal that parameters such as inlet angle, channel cross-section, and total flow rate critically influence mixing profiles and nanoparticle formation dynamics, ultimately dictating size uniformity and encapsulation efficiency [168]. Notably, optimized configurations can achieve smaller and more homogeneous nanoparticles with enhanced cellular uptake, reinforcing the importance of integrating design-aware manufacturing strategies.

Beyond lipid-based systems, microfluidics has also proven highly effective in the synthesis of polymeric and hybrid lipid–polymer nanoparticles. Controlled nanoprecipitation within microchannels enables tunable particle size and drug loading by adjusting flow conditions and polymer composition, overcoming long-standing limitations in polymeric nanoparticle reproducibility [169]. For hybrid systems, microfluidic platforms enable the synchronized self-assembly of lipid shells and polymeric cores, producing structurally well-defined core–shell architectures with improved stability and therapeutic performance [170, 171]. These capabilities are particularly relevant for PLNs, where multi-component complexity demands highly controlled fabrication environments.

Another key advantage of microfluidics is its compatibility with continuous and scalable manufacturing. Unlike batch processes, microfluidic platforms support continuous production with high reproducibility, reduced processing time, and improved cost-efficiency [172]. Parallelization strategies further enable industrial-scale throughput without compromising nanoparticle quality, as demonstrated in large-scale lipid nanoparticle production systems [173]. This scalability is essential for translating personalized nanomedicine approaches from bench to bedside.

Importantly, microfluidics also provides a natural interface for integration with artificial intelligence. Emerging frameworks suggest that AI models can predict optimal formulation parameters, which are then directly executed by programmable microfluidic chips, enabling closed-loop, automated nanoparticle synthesis [174]. In the near term, this integration is more realistically positioned as a platform for reproducible preclinical and early translational testing rather than immediate point-of-care production.

Furthermore, microfluidic platforms enable the fabrication of advanced nanostructures, including core–shell and exosome-mimicking nanoparticles with controlled surface functionalization, expanding the design space for precision oncology applications [175, 176]. These systems allow fine control over biological identity and targeting capabilities, which are critical for improving therapeutic efficacy in complex diseases such as breast cancer.

Despite these advantages, challenges remain, including device standardization, integration with clinical workflows, and regulatory considerations for on-demand manufacturing. Nevertheless, the convergence of microfluidics with AI-driven design represents a transformative step toward fully automated, reproducible, and personalized nanomedicine production. In this framework, microfluidics should not be viewed merely as a fabrication tool, but rather as the manufacturing backbone of AI-guided nanomedicine, enabling the seamless translation of computational insights into clinically actionable nanotherapeutics (Fig. 4).

Clinical translation: realities, trials, and hurdles

Current clinical landscape & AI’s role

The clinical translation of nanomedicine in breast cancer reflects a pattern of incremental therapeutic refinement rather than transformative efficacy, with most nanocarrier platforms functioning as reformulations of established cytotoxic agents. Across multiple clinical settings, pegylated liposomal doxorubicin (PLD) and related lipid-based systems consistently demonstrate improved tolerability and pharmacokinetic stability, yet fail to deliver meaningful survival advantages. In early-stage breast cancer and adjuvant treatment settings, randomized and phase II studies have reported non-inferior disease-free and overall survival compared with conventional regimens, positioning PLD primarily as a safety-driven alternative rather than a superior therapeutic modality [177–179]. In advanced disease, available trials generally show modest or non-superior efficacy, often without clear survival improvement.

In the metastatic and heavily pretreated setting, liposomal formulations exhibit modest and often transient activity. Trials evaluating liposomal irinotecan in metastatic TNBC (mTNBC) and brain metastases demonstrate objective response rates in the range of 22–30%, yet with limited progression-free survival and poor durability of response [180–182]. These findings highlight a critical biological barrier: inadequate and heterogeneous tumor penetration, particularly across the blood–brain barrier (BBB). Similarly, PLD in late-line settings yields only marginal benefit, further emphasizing that pharmacokinetic enhancement alone is insufficient to overcome resistant tumor biology [183]. These results suggest that delivery efficiency and intratumoral distribution appear to be major bottlenecks, alongside tumor biology, payload sensitivity, patient selection, and treatment setting. In contrast, early-phase combination studies such as nab-paclitaxel plus non-pegylated liposomal doxorubicin have shown high preliminary response rates in first-line HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer, but these findings remain hypothesis-generating because they were derived from very small dose-finding cohorts without comparator arms [184].

Combination strategies incorporating nanomedicines with immunotherapy or targeted agents have yielded mixed and largely underwhelming results. Trials combining PLD with immune checkpoint inhibitors demonstrate only minor improvements in progression-free survival, often accompanied by increased toxicity and without meaningful clinical gain [185, 186]. These outcomes reflect a lack of patient selection based on TIME characteristics, leading to ineffective immunotherapy synergy. In contrast, biologically guided approaches show greater promise; for example, the combination of PLD with HER2-targeted therapy in the neoadjuvant setting achieves high pathological complete response rates (~80%), underscoring the importance of subtype-specific therapeutic alignment [187]. Conversely, the failure of EGFR-directed liposomes to meet clinical endpoints suggests that receptor expression alone may be insufficient to ensure clinical benefit; however, clinical failure may also reflect factors such as patient selection, dosing, tumor penetration, pharmacokinetics, payload activity, and trial design [188].

Polymeric nanoparticle systems, including paclitaxel-loaded micelles (NK105), further reinforce the central limitation of current nanomedicine paradigms. Despite favorable toxicity profiles, large phase II/III trials consistently demonstrate non-inferior but not superior efficacy compared to standard formulations, with no improvement in progression-free or overall survival [189, 190]. These findings suggest that altering drug formulation without fundamentally addressing tumor biology or delivery heterogeneity is unlikely to yield substantial clinical benefit. Similarly, other liposomal agents, such as mitoxantrone liposomes and liposomal eribulin, demonstrate modest response improvements but are constrained by short progression-free intervals and unresolved pharmacokinetic–efficacy disconnects [191, 192].

A recurring and underappreciated theme across these trials is the profound heterogeneity of nanoparticle delivery at the tumor level. Imaging-based studies using theranostic liposomes reveal up to 35-fold variability in tumor deposition among patients, providing direct clinical evidence that nanomedicine efficacy is fundamentally limited by inconsistent intratumoral distribution [193]. This variability likely underlies the failure of otherwise promising agents such as MM-302, where may partly explain the modest clinical activity of MM-302 [194]. Furthermore, negative trials combining nanomedicines with agents such as metformin illustrate the consequences of empiric, non–biology-driven design, reinforcing the need for mechanistically informed therapeutic strategies [195].

Within this landscape of modest efficacy and high biological complexity, AI emerges as a critical enabler of next-generation nanomedicine. A central opportunity lies in patient stratification using multi-omics and tumor microenvironment profiling, particularly in heterogeneous subtypes such as TNBC, where extreme resistance patterns have led to complete lack of pathological response in some trials [196]. AI-driven models integrating genomic, transcriptomic, and immune features can identify patients most likely to benefit from specific nanocarrier systems, thereby transforming trial design from population-based to precision-guided approaches.

Beyond patient selection, AI has the potential to address the fundamental challenge of delivery heterogeneity. Predictive modeling of nanoparticle pharmacokinetics and tumor penetration, including BBB permeability in brain metastases, could enable pre-treatment identification of patients with favorable delivery profiles [180, 182]. Similarly, AI-guided optimization of dosing, scheduling, and combination strategies may bridge the persistent gap between pharmacokinetics and therapeutic efficacy observed in multiple studies [192, 197]. Importantly, integration of imaging data with computational modeling offers a pathway toward real-time, patient-specific prediction of nanoparticle distribution, enabling adaptive treatment strategies based on actual tumor uptake.

AI can facilitate rational design of combination therapies by modeling complex biological interactions within the tumor microenvironment. The limited success of immunotherapy combinations underscores the necessity of TIME-aware approaches, where AI can identify immunologically responsive niches and guide synergistic pairing of nanomedicines with immune modulators [185]. Likewise, multi-omics-driven targeting strategies may overcome the limitations of single-receptor approaches by capturing the multidimensional nature of tumor biology [188] (Table 2).

Clinical translation of nanomedicine in breast cancer and AI intervention opportunities

Data scarcity, model generalizability, and validation challenges

A fundamental yet often underrecognized barrier in the integration of AI into nanomedicine is the scarcity, fragmentation, and poor standardization of high-quality datasets. While AI has demonstrated remarkable capability in modeling nanoparticle behavior and optimizing formulation design, its performance is inherently constrained by the quality and representativeness of the underlying data. In nanomedicine, the available datasets are typically small, heterogeneous, and experimentally inconsistent, limiting model generalizability and predictive reliability [155].

Beyond data scarcity, several broader challenges limit the translational reliability of AI-guided nanomedicine. Models trained on one nanoparticle class, biological system, assay condition, or tumor context may not generalize to other nanocarrier platforms or disease settings. This is particularly relevant for PLNs, where polymer–lipid interactions, core–shell architecture, release behavior, and nano–bio identity may differ from conventional liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, or ionizable lipid nanoparticles. In addition, many reported models lack independent external validation or prospective experimental confirmation, and available datasets may be affected by publication bias, incomplete metadata, and inconsistent reporting of failed formulations. Limited model interpretability also makes it difficult to extract mechanistically meaningful design principles or justify model-guided formulation decisions. These limitations have direct regulatory implications, because clinical translation will require evaluation not only of the nanoparticle product, but also of the algorithmic model, training data, validation strategy, and manufacturing workflow [15, 155, 198].

One of the core issues lies in the lack of standardized reporting across nanoparticle studies. Critical formulation parameters, including particle size, PDI, zeta potential, encapsulation efficiency, and release kinetics, are often measured under varying experimental conditions and reported using inconsistent methodologies. As a result, datasets generated across different studies are not directly comparable, preventing meaningful aggregation into large-scale training datasets. This lack of harmonization significantly impairs the development of robust AI models and contributes to poor reproducibility in nanomedicine research [15, 198].

Compounding this challenge is the issue of publication bias, which systematically skews the available data landscape. In nanomedicine, as in many biomedical fields, studies reporting positive therapeutic outcomes are disproportionately represented in the literature, while negative or inconclusive results remain unpublished. This “file drawer problem” leads to an artificially inflated perception of nanoparticle efficacy. When AI models are trained on such biased datasets, they may learn distorted structure–function relationships and generate overly optimistic predictions that do not generalize to real-world settings. This problem is better understood as dataset bias and limited external validity rather than overfitting alone [199].

Data heterogeneity further exacerbates these limitations. Nanoparticle performance depends on a complex interplay of physicochemical properties (size, charge, composition), biological context (tumor subtype, microenvironment, immune status), and experimental conditions (in vitro vs. in vivo models) [200]. However, many datasets lack comprehensive metadata capturing these variables. The absence of standardized ontologies and structured data frameworks prevents integration of multi-modal data, including imaging, omics, and pharmacokinetic profiles, thereby limiting the ability of AI to model the full complexity of nano–bio interactions [201].

Importantly, these data limitations have direct implications for clinical translation. AI models trained on incomplete or biased datasets may fail when applied to heterogeneous patient populations, contributing to the translational gap observed in nanomedicine. As highlighted in recent analyses, data scarcity, bias, and lack of standardization are among the primary factors limiting confidence in AI-driven nanotherapeutic design [201].

Addressing this bottleneck requires a paradigm shift toward data-centric nanomedicine. Key priorities include the development of standardized reporting frameworks (e.g., minimum information guidelines for nanomaterial characterization), establishment of open-access nanoparticle databases, and integration of high-dimensional datasets across studies. In addition, emerging approaches such as federated learning offer a promising solution by enabling collaborative model training across institutions while preserving data privacy. Ultimately, overcoming the “garbage in, garbage out” problem is not merely a technical challenge, but a foundational requirement for realizing the full potential of AI-driven precision nanomedicine. Ultimately, overcoming the “garbage in, garbage out” problem requires not only larger datasets, but also standardized reporting, unbiased data capture, independent validation, interpretable modeling, and regulatory pathways capable of evaluating AI-guided nanomedicine workflows.

Regulatory bottlenecks

The clinical translation of AI-guided PLNs raises regulatory questions that are not fully addressed by conventional product-based approval pathways. Traditional regulatory paradigms, as implemented by agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA), are designed to evaluate static, well-defined drug products with fixed composition, manufacturing processes, and predictable pharmacokinetics. In contrast, AI-driven nanomedicine introduces a dynamic paradigm in which formulations may vary across patients, evolve over time, and be continuously optimized based on incoming data [202].

A central challenge lies in the definition of the therapeutic product itself. In conventional drug development, a formulation is considered a single, standardized entity. However, in AI-guided PLN systems, each patient-specific formulation, defined by unique combinations of size, composition, surface functionalization, and targeting ligands, may effectively constitute a distinct therapeutic. This raises a critical regulatory question; should each personalized nanoparticle formulation be evaluated as a new drug? Current regulatory frameworks are not equipped to address this level of variability, creating significant uncertainty for clinical approval pathways [203].

These challenges are further compounded by the integration of AI into the therapeutic pipeline. When AI models are used to design nanoparticle formulations, optimize dosing, or predict patient response, they effectively become part of the therapeutic system. This places them within the scope of Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) regulation [204]. However, existing regulatory approaches struggle to accommodate adaptive AI systems that evolve over time. As noted by the FDA, traditional regulatory paradigms “were not designed for adaptive artificial intelligence and machine learning technologies,” particularly those that continuously update based on real-world data. This creates a fundamental tension between the static nature of regulatory approval and the dynamic nature of AI-driven systems [205].

Manufacturing represents an additional regulatory bottleneck. The emergence of microfluidic and continuous manufacturing platforms enables on-demand production of personalized nanoparticles with high precision and reproducibility. However, regulatory frameworks have historically been based on batch manufacturing processes with clearly defined quality control parameters. The transition to continuous, AI-integrated manufacturing systems challenges existing concepts of batch release, quality assurance, and process validation. Ensuring consistent quality across dynamically generated formulations requires new regulatory approaches, such as real-time release testing and adaptive quality control frameworks.

Moreover, the complexity of nanomedicine itself introduces additional regulatory challenges. Issues such as scalability, reproducibility, characterization, and safety assessment remain unresolved, particularly for hybrid systems like PLNs. Regulatory agencies must evaluate not only the therapeutic payload but also the nanocarrier’s physicochemical properties, biodistribution, immunogenicity, and long-term safety, all of which may vary across formulations and patient populations [206]. The absence of standardized characterization methods and predictive models further complicates this process.

At a broader level, the regulatory landscape for nanomedicine remains fragmented and underdeveloped. If future PLN systems move toward highly individualized formulations, regulators would need to determine whether such products should be evaluated as discrete formulations or as members of a predefined design space. In the near term, however, AI-guided PLNs are more realistically developed as families of controlled formulations rather than unrestricted n-of-1 products. This gap is particularly evident in the context of personalized nanomedicine, where existing regulations fail to capture the dynamic, data-driven nature of next-generation therapeutic systems [207].

To overcome these barriers, a shift toward adaptive and integrated regulatory frameworks is required. Concepts such as Quality by Design (QbD) and its extension, Quality by Digital Design (QbDD), offer promising approaches by linking formulation parameters, manufacturing processes, and clinical performance within a unified framework. Similarly, regulatory pathways must evolve to accommodate AI-enabled systems through lifecycle-based evaluation, continuous monitoring, and predefined change control plans [208].

In this context, regulatory development in this area is more likely to involve incremental adaptation of existing quality-by-design, continuous manufacturing, and software lifecycle frameworks rather than a complete replacement of current approval paradigms. Without such transformation, the full potential of AI-guided, patient-specific nanomedicine will remain constrained not by technological limitations, but by the inability of existing regulatory frameworks to keep pace with innovation.

Future roadmap and conclusion

Short-term milestones: standardization, data infrastructure, and privacy-preserving collaboration

The next phase of progress in AI-guided polymeric–lipid nanomedicine will depend less on conceptual innovation and more on the establishment of a reliable translational infrastructure. Among the most urgent priorities is the development and adoption of standardized reporting frameworks for nanoparticle characterization, biological testing, and computational modeling. At present, the field remains hindered by inconsistent reporting of critical variables such as particle size, polydispersity index, zeta potential, encapsulation efficiency, release kinetics, serum stability, corona composition, cell model conditions, and in vivo biodistribution metrics. Without harmonized descriptors and minimum metadata requirements, even technically sophisticated AI models remain constrained by fragmented and non-comparable datasets. Therefore, implementation of structured reporting standards, including frameworks analogous to Minimum Information for Nanomaterial Characterization (MINC), should be considered an immediate priority. Such standards would not only improve reproducibility, but also enable the generation of interoperable datasets suitable for machine learning, PBPK modeling, and digital twin development.

Equally important is the creation of shared, high-quality, multi-institutional data ecosystems that integrate formulation parameters with biological, imaging, omics, pharmacokinetic, and clinical outcome data. However, because patient-specific nanomedicine inherently relies on sensitive genomic and clinical information, centralized data pooling is often impractical and ethically complex. In this context, federated learning represents a highly promising short-term solution. By enabling AI models to be trained across distributed institutional datasets without transferring raw patient data, federated learning can preserve privacy while still supporting the development of robust, globally informed predictive models. For breast cancer in particular, where molecular subtype, TIME composition, prior treatment exposure, and metastatic pattern all influence therapeutic response, federated infrastructures could allow models to learn from diverse patient populations without compromising confidentiality. In practical terms, the short-term roadmap should therefore focus on three interconnected milestones: standardized data generation, interoperable database development, and privacy-preserving collaborative AI training. Without these foundations, the field risks producing increasingly sophisticated algorithms that remain disconnected from clinical reality.

Long-term perspective: controlled adaptive manufacturing and clinically testable PLN design

Beyond standardization and data integration lies a more transformative vision: controlled adaptive nanomedicine development rather than unrestricted decentralization of manufacturing. The ultimate goal is not merely to design better nanoparticles in silico, but to create a closed-loop clinical ecosystem in which patient-specific biological information can directly drive the on-demand production of personalized nanotherapeutics. In this long-term model, the oncology clinic evolves from a site of drug administration into an active node of therapeutic design and fabrication. Multi-omics profiling, imaging, and liquid biopsy data would feed into AI-driven digital twin platforms that predict the optimal nanoparticle architecture for a given patient, including cargo selection, lipid-to-polymer ratio, surface ligand composition, release kinetics, and dosing strategy. However, these outputs should not be interpreted as immediate instructions for generating a unique nanoparticle for every patient. Instead, they are more realistically viewed as a means of matching biologically defined delivery barriers to validated families of PLN formulations.

A fully decentralized “nano-foundry” model remains a long-term and highly speculative vision rather than a near-term translational pathway. Although breast cancer biology is temporally dynamic and often changes under therapeutic pressure, there is no inherent feature of nanomedicine that makes fully individualized n-of-1 therapies easier to manufacture or regulate than other personalized therapeutic approaches. In fact, individualized PLNs may create additional complexity because both the therapeutic payload and the carrier’s physicochemical properties, biodistribution, immunogenicity, release behavior, and manufacturing reproducibility must be controlled. A more feasible long-term direction is the development of adaptive PLN platforms that can be selected or modified within predefined formulation boundaries in response to evolving resistance patterns, receptor expression, or tumor immune microenvironment features.

Although point-of-care and decentralized microfluidic manufacturing platforms represent an attractive future direction for personalized nanomedicine, several major translational and regulatory barriers remain unresolved. Even if AI-guided systems successfully generate optimized PLN formulations in real time, clinical implementation still requires stringent release testing, including sterility assessment, endotoxin determination, batch consistency evaluation, and comprehensive physicochemical characterization. Many of these quality-control procedures currently require hours to days, and in some cases days to weeks, depending on assay type and regulatory requirements. Therefore, they cannot yet be fully integrated into rapid bedside manufacturing workflows. Consequently, the feasibility of real-time personalized PLN production will likely depend on the development of accelerated and parallelized quality-control strategies, including inline process analytical technologies, automated microfluidic sensing systems, rapid sterility assays, and validated digital monitoring frameworks. In parallel, regulatory agencies are increasingly exploring adaptive manufacturing paradigms, distributed production models, and continuous manufacturing frameworks for advanced therapeutics, which may eventually support decentralized nanomedicine production under tightly controlled GMP-compatible conditions. Nevertheless, substantial standardization, validation, and regulatory harmonization will still be required before autonomous point-of-care PLN manufacturing can become clinically practical.

Achieving this vision will require major advances in automation, regulatory science, process validation, and clinical systems integration. AI models must become more interpretable, more mechanistically grounded, and more capable of incorporating longitudinal patient data. Microfluidic systems must become more robust, modular, and clinically deployable. Regulatory frameworks must evolve to oversee not only the nanoparticle product, but also the algorithmic and manufacturing systems that generate it. Therefore, the long-term future of AI-guided PLNs is unlikely to be defined by unrestricted production of individualized nanocarriers at the point of care. Rather, it is more plausibly defined by controlled adaptive platforms, predefined formulation families, and validated design spaces that allow biologically informed PLN selection while preserving manufacturing reproducibility and regulatory feasibility.

Concluding remarks

Breast cancer remains a paradigmatic example of biological complexity, shaped by interpatient heterogeneity, intratumoral evolution, metastatic plasticity, and dynamic interactions within the tumor immune microenvironment. This complexity is precisely why conventional therapeutic models, including both standard chemotherapy and first-generation nanomedicine, have delivered only partial and often transient gains. PLNs offer a structurally versatile and biologically powerful platform, but their full potential cannot be realized through empirical design alone. At the same time, artificial intelligence provides unprecedented capacity to model nonlinear systems, integrate multi-modal patient data, and predict formulation behavior, yet it cannot affect clinical outcomes unless its predictions are translated into reproducible physical products. Microfluidics closes this final gap by enabling precise, scalable, and programmable fabrication of smart nanocarriers.

Taken together, the convergence of AI, microfluidics, and smart PLNs defines a new therapeutic paradigm for breast cancer, one that moves beyond reformulating old drugs and toward truly adaptive, patient-specific nanomedicine. This convergence should not be viewed as a futuristic luxury or a speculative technological ambition. It is a necessary clinical evolution. The persistent failure of one-size-fits-all treatment strategies, the limited efficacy of current nanocarriers, and the multidimensional heterogeneity of breast cancer all demand a new framework capable of matching therapeutic design to biological reality. In this sense, the future of breast cancer nanomedicine will not be determined by whether these disciplines can converge, but by how rapidly and rigorously that convergence can be translated into clinical practice. Only through such an integrated, data-driven, and manufacturing-aware approach can nanomedicine move from incremental improvement to genuine therapeutic transformation.
