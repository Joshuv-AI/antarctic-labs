Association of ACE I/D polymorphism with blood pressure response to valsartan-hydrochlorothiazide combination therapy in hypertensive patients from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan
Authors: Aftab Ullah, Abdur Rahim, Asif Jan, Muhammad Khan, Junaid Iqbal, Mequanente Dagnaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundInterindividual variability in antihypertensive response may be partly explained by genetic variation. The angiotensin-converting enzyme insertion/deletion polymorphism (ACE I/D; rs1799752) has been associated with variable response to renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system-targeting therapies, but evidence for valsartan-hydrochlorothiazide (Val-HCTZ) combination therapy remains limited, particularly in South Asian populations.MethodsIn this multicenter prospective cohort study, 645 hypertensive patients from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan, receiving fixed-dose Val-HCTZ were enrolled; 627 were included in the final analysis after excluding 18 patients with poor adherence (<80%) followed for 3 months. ACE I/D genotyping was performed by polymerase chain reaction with confirmatory testing. Introduction

Hypertension remains a paramount global public health challenge, constituting a primary modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease, stroke, and premature mortality [1]. Current estimates indicate that over 1.3 billion adults worldwide are affected, with a disproportionate burden falling disproportionately on low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [2]. In Pakistan, the prevalence is alarmingly high, estimated at 18–33% among adults, yet control rates are critically low, falling below 3% [3,4]. While effective antihypertensive drugs are widely available, a significant impediment to optimal management is the substantial interindividual variability in therapeutic response. A considerable proportion of patients, ranging from 30–50%, fail to achieve target blood pressure (BP) control on their initial prescribed regimen, leading to a cycle of trial-and-error dosing, polypharmacy, poor adherence, and increased cardiovascular risk [5,6].

This variability is increasingly attributed to genetic factors that influence drug metabolism, transport, and targets. Pharmacogenetics, the study of how genetic variation affects drug response, offers a promising paradigm to move from empirical to personalized hypertension management [7]. By identifying genetic markers predictive of efficacy or adverse effects, therapy can be tailored at the outset, improving first-step success rates. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified hundreds of loci associated with BP regulation and drug response, though their translation into clinical practice has been gradual [8]. Among the most investigated systems is the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), a critical pathway in BP homeostasis and the target of several drug classes.

The angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) gene harbors a widely studied insertion/deletion (I/D) polymorphism, rs1799752, within intron 16, defined by the presence of the insertion (I) allele or absence/deletion (D) allele of a 287-bp Alu repeat sequence [9]. This polymorphism is an important determinant of circulating and tissue ACE activity, with the D allele generally associated with higher enzymatic activity than the I allele [10]. Consequently, ACE I/D variation may influence angiotensin II generation and downstream renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) activity. It has also been linked to the risk of essential hypertension, cardiovascular remodeling, and variable response to RAAS-targeting therapies [11,12].

Previous studies examining ACE I/D in relation to antihypertensive monotherapy have produced mixed findings rather than a uniformly consistent pattern. Some reports suggest that the D allele or DD genotype may be associated with greater response to angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), potentially because individuals with higher baseline RAAS activity derive greater benefit from pharmacologic blockade of this pathway [13,14]. However, results have varied across populations, study designs, drug classes, and outcome definitions. Likewise, evidence regarding thiazide response has been inconsistent, although some reports suggest relatively better response among I-allele carriers [15]. Valsartan, an ARB, and hydrochlorothiazide (HCTZ), a thiazide diuretic, are widely used in fixed-dose combination therapy because of their complementary effects on vasoconstriction and volume control [16]. Because ACEIs and ARBs act at different points within the RAAS pathway, genotype effects may not necessarily be identical across these drug classes.

Despite available data on monotherapy, a critical gap remains in understanding the pharmacogenetic relevance of ACE I/D in fixed-dose combination therapy, which is increasingly recommended as first-line treatment in hypertension [17]. The simultaneous use of an ARB and a thiazide diuretic creates a more complex pharmacodynamic profile than monotherapy, and the response to combination therapy cannot be inferred from prior single-agent studies alone. Pharmacogenetic data from South Asian populations, including Pakistan, are also limited despite the high burden of hypertension and potentially distinct allele frequencies in the region [18]. Furthermore, no prior study has specifically examined the association of ACE I/D genotype with response to valsartan-hydrochlorothiazide combination therapy in a Pakistani cohort, even in a South Asian population. Therefore, whether ACE I/D genotype is associated with response to valsartan-hydrochlorothiazide therapy in Pakistani patients remains insufficiently characterized.

This study aimed to evaluate whether ACE I/D genotype is associated with variation in blood pressure response to fixed-dose valsartan-hydrochlorothiazide therapy in hypertensive patients from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan.

Methodology

Study design and setting

A multicenter prospective cohort study was conducted from 1 August 2025 to 31 December 2025 across five tertiary and secondary care hospitals in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan, including Saidu Teaching Hospital (STH) in Swat, District Headquarters Hospitals (DHQ) in Charsadda and Nowshera, Hayatabad Medical Complex (HMC) in Peshawar, and Nasirullah Khan Babar Memorial Hospital (NKBMH) in Peshawar. All laboratory analyses were performed at the Pharmacogenomics Laboratory, Department of Pharmacy, University of Peshawar.

Study participants and sample size

A total of 645 adult hypertensive patients (aged >30 years) prescribed a fixed-dose valsartan/hydrochlorothiazide (Val-HCTZ) combination were enrolled. The sample size was calculated using OpenEpi software (Version 3.01) based on an anticipated genotype frequency of 0.5, a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and a 10% attrition rate. After excluding 18 patients (2.8%) with medication adherence <80%, the final analysis included 627 patients.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients were included if they had a diagnosis of essential hypertension (systolic BP ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic BP ≥90 mmHg) and were prescribed Val-HCTZ as part of their treatment regimen. Exclusion criteria included secondary hypertension, severe renal or hepatic impairment, heart failure, pregnancy, and refusal to participate.

Data collection and clinical assessment

At baseline, demographic and clinical data were collected using a standardized questionnaire. Blood pressure was measured in the seated position using a calibrated sphygmomanometer according to American Heart Association recommendations.

Participants were followed weekly for three months. Medication adherence was assessed at each visit through patient self-report and review of prescribed medication quantities; patients reporting adherence <80% were excluded from the final analysis to minimize misclassification bias. The primary outcome was change in systolic and diastolic blood pressure from baseline to three months across ACE I/D genotypes. A secondary outcome was achievement of target blood pressure, defined as systolic BP <140 mmHg and diastolic BP <90 mmHg after three months of therapy. Patients meeting this threshold were classified as responders, whereas those who did not were classified as non-responders.

Blood collection and DNA extraction

Venous blood (2–3 mL) was collected from each participant in EDTA-coated tubes and stored at −20°C until processing. Genomic DNA was extracted using the Thermo Scientific DNA Extraction Kit (K0721) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA concentration and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically (NanoDrop™) with A260/A280 ratios between 1.8–2.0 considered acceptable.

Genotyping of ACE I/D polymorphism

The insertion allele (I) produced a 490-bp fragment, whereas the deletion allele (D) produced a 190-bp fragment. Genotypes were identified as II (490 bp), ID (490 and 190 bp), and DD (190 bp) (Figure S1). Throughout the manuscript, I denotes the insertion allele, and D denotes the deletion allele for ACE rs1799752.

The primer sequences were:

Forward: 5’-GCCCTGCAGGTGTCTGCAGCATGT-3’.

Reverse: 5’-GGATGGCTCTCCCCGCCTTGTCTC-3’.

Each 25 µL reaction contained 12.5 µL DreamTaq Green Master Mix, 1 µL each primer, 9.5 µL nuclease-free water, and 1 µL template DNA. PCR conditions were: initial denaturation at 96°C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, 58°C for 30 s, 72°C for 1 min; and final extension at 72°C for 5 min.

Gel electrophoresis

PCR products were resolved on a 2% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide and visualized under ultraviolet illumination (Figure S1). To avoid mistyping of DD homozygotes, a confirmatory PCR using insertion-specific primers was performed for all samples initially classified as DD.Forward: 5’-TGGGACCACAGCGCCCGCCACTAC-3’Reverse: 5’-TCGCCAGCCCTCCCATGCCCATAA-3’A 337-bp product indicated the presence of an I allele, reclassifying the sample as ID, as shown in Figure S2).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1. Descriptive statistics were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) was assessed using the chi-square test. The primary analysis evaluated the association between ACE I/D genotype and change in systolic and diastolic blood pressure using linear regression. Multivariable models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, baseline systolic and diastolic blood pressure, study center, and duration of hypertension. These covariates were selected a priori based on their potential to confound the genotype-response association. Secondary analysis evaluated the association between genotype and achievement of target blood pressure using binary logistic regression, with II as the reference genotype, adjusting for the same covariates. Formal genetic model testing was performed to compare additive, dominant, recessive, codominant, and overdominant/heterozygote-disadvantage models using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). A global genotype test was also conducted. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Because this study evaluated a single prespecified polymorphism, nominal p values are reported. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 645 hypertensive patients were enrolled in this multicenter study. After excluding 18 patients (2.8%) with medication adherence <80%, the final analysis included 627 patients. Complete data were available for all variables included in the primary analysis. No missing data were present for genotype, baseline BP, or follow-up BP measurements. Among participants, 397 (63.3%) were classified as responders, achieving target blood pressure (<140/90 mmHg) after 3 months, while 230 (36.7%) were non-responders. Covariate data were complete for age, sex, and BMI. Duration of hypertension was missing for 4 participants (0.7%) and was handled by multiple imputation. Association between ACE I/D genotypes and blood pressure response

The association between ACE I/D genotype and antihypertensive response is shown in Table 3. Genotype-stratified analyses demonstrated a graded pattern of therapeutic response, with DD carriers showing the most favorable response, II carriers an intermediate response, and ID carriers the least favorable response (DD > II > ID), as shown in Figure 2. In the secondary analysis of target blood pressure attainment, response rates were 75.8% (157/207) for II, 40.8% (115/282) for ID, and 90.6% (125/138) for DD. Compared with the II reference genotype, the ID genotype was significantly associated with poorer treatment response after adjustment for age, sex, body mass index, baseline SBP, study center, and duration of hypertension (adjusted OR = 5.12, 95% CI: 1.92–15.74, p = 0.0028). In contrast, although DD carriers showed the most favorable observed response profile, the adjusted comparison versus II was not statistically significant (adjusted OR = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.04–1.35, p = 0.112). Genetic model testing and sensitivity analyses

Formal genetic model testing revealed that the overdominant/heterozygote-disadvantage model (ID vs DD+II) provided the best fit for the data (AIC = 218.4), compared with additive (AIC = 230.5), dominant (AIC = 235.2), recessive (AIC = 238.7), and codominant (AIC = 220.1) models. A global genotype test was significant (χ2 = 12.8, df = 2, p = 0.002), supporting a genotype-specific effect rather than a simple allele-dose relationship (Supplementary Table S3).

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings. Among participants with documented adherence ≥ 90% (n = 487, 77.7%), the association between ID genotype and poorer response remained significant (adjusted OR = 5.41, 95% CI: 1.98–16.23, p = 0.002) (Supplementary Table S4). Restricted to participants receiving the same formulation (valsartan 160 mg/HCTZ 25 mg; n = 398, 63.5%), the findings were consistent (adjusted OR for ID vs II = 5.28, 95% CI: 1.88–16.41, p = 0.003) (Supplementary Table S5). When using continuous BP reduction as the outcome and adjusting for baseline BP, the ID-associated poorer response remained significant (β = −10.8, 95% CI: −16.2 to −5.4, p = 0.004) (Supplementary Table S6). After excluding 12 patients with protocol deviations (dose changes during follow-up, n = 8; major intercurrent illness, n = 4), the findings were unchanged (Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

This multicenter prospective cohort study evaluated the association between ACE I/D genotype and blood pressure response to valsartan-hydrochlorothiazide combination therapy in hypertensive patients from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan. The principal finding was a genotype-stratified pattern of response, with DD carriers showing the most favorable observed response, II carriers showing an intermediate response, and ID carriers showing the least favorable response (DD > II > ID). The strongest statistical signal in adjusted analysis was the poorer response observed among ID carriers. These findings suggest that ACE I/D may function as a pharmacogenetic marker of treatment response; however, given the observational design, causal inference is not warranted. The mechanistic interpretation of these findings is inferential, as this study did not measure plasma renin activity, ACE activity, angiotensin II, aldosterone, or other RAAS-related biomarkers. The proposed biological explanations should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating.

The genotype distribution in our cohort was in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, and the allele frequencies (I = 0.445, D = 0.555) were broadly consistent with prior South Asian reports [9,18]. The observed genotype-response pattern is biologically plausible in light of the known relationship between ACE I/D and RAAS activity, although prior pharmacogenetic studies have not yielded a universally consistent pattern across different antihypertensive drug classes [10–15]. In our cohort, the clearest adjusted association was the poorer response observed among ID carriers relative to II. This may reflect a non-linear pharmacogenetic effect rather than a simple dominant or recessive inheritance pattern. Because valsartan and hydrochlorothiazide act through different but interacting physiological pathways, combination-therapy response may not mirror prior monotherapy studies exactly.

DD carriers showed the most favorable observed response profile, with more than 90% achieving target blood pressure. However, the adjusted comparison versus II was not statistically significant, so this pattern should be interpreted as descriptive rather than confirmatory. One possible explanation is that individuals with greater baseline RAAS activity may derive greater benefit from ARB-based therapy, but this hypothesis requires further validation. These findings also highlight the complexity of fixed-dose combination therapy, where the pharmacogenetic effects relevant to valsartan may differ from those relevant to hydrochlorothiazide.

Our finding that ID carriers showed the poorest response contrasts with some prior reports that suggested D allele carriers respond better to ACE inhibitors [13,14]. However, it is consistent with studies showing that heterozygous individuals may exhibit more complex RAAS feedback regulation, potentially leading to unpredictable responses to RAAS blockade [10]. A systematic review by Scharplatz et al. noted considerable heterogeneity in ACE I/D pharmacogenetic associations across studies, which our results further substantiate in the context of combination therapy [10]. The graded response pattern (DD > II > ID) observed in our cohort suggests a non-allele-dose effect, highlighting the need for more nuanced genotype-stratified analyses in future pharmacogenetic studies.

The effect of ACE I/D may plausibly differ between ACE inhibitors and ARBs because these drug classes interrupt the RAAS pathway at different points. ACE inhibitors reduce conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II and may also influence bradykinin metabolism, whereas ARBs selectively block angiotensin II signaling at the AT1 receptor. Accordingly, the relationship between ACE genotype, underlying RAAS activity, and therapeutic response may not be identical for ACE inhibitors and ARBs. This mechanistic distinction may partly explain why ACE I/D associations have varied across prior studies and drug classes.

Our results add to the heterogeneous literature on ACE I/D and antihypertensive response. Rather than supporting a simple allele-dose effect, our data suggest a genotype-specific response pattern in which ID carriers had the least favorable outcome. If replicated, this may indicate that some patients with the ID genotype respond less favorably to fixed-dose valsartan-hydrochlorothiazide than patients with II or DD genotypes. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously until validated in larger, multi-center, and ideally genotype-stratified interventional studies.

The clinical implications of these findings are considerable, especially in resource-limited settings like Pakistan with high hypertension prevalence and low control rates [3,4]. A simple, cost-effective PCR-based genotyping test could identify nearly 45% of patients in our cohort with the ID genotype who are at high risk of failing a commonly prescribed first-line combination. Avoiding initial therapeutic failure could improve patient confidence, adherence, and time to control, while reducing clinic visits and costs associated with trial-and-error prescribing. However, the economic feasibility and cost-effectiveness of routine genotyping would require formal evaluation in the local context. These findings do not yet justify clinical implementation of ACE I/D genotyping to guide Val-HCTZ therapy. Rather, they support further research, including independent replication, biochemical validation, and genotype-stratified interventional trials.

This study has several limitations. First, although the sample size was substantial, the cohort was derived from a single region of Pakistan, which may limit generalizability to other ethnic groups or geographic regions. Second, while fixed-dose valsartan-hydrochlorothiazide therapy was evaluated, further work is needed to determine whether genotype-response relationships differ by dose strength or treatment escalation strategy. Third, we did not measure plasma renin activity, ACE activity, or angiotensin II levels to provide biochemical confirmation of the proposed RAAS-based mechanism; therefore, the mechanistic interpretation remains inferential and should be considered hypothesis-generating. Fourth, medication adherence was assessed by self-report rather than by pill counts, refill data, or biochemical confirmation, and this may have introduced misclassification. Fifth, because the study evaluated one prespecified polymorphism, nominal p values were reported; nevertheless, conservative consideration of multiple testing remains important in broader pharmacogenetic research. Sixth, we did not account for potential gene-drug interactions with other antihypertensive agents that patients may have been taking concomitantly, as only patients on Val-HCTZ monotherapy were included. Finally, the follow-up period was limited to three months, so longer-term treatment response and cardiovascular outcomes could not be assessed.

Future research should focus on validating these findings in larger, multi-ethnic prospective cohorts and in randomized controlled trials testing a genotype-guided strategy versus standard care. Investigating polygenic risk scores that incorporate ACE I/D with other relevant variants (AGTR1, NEDD4L, ADD1) could improve predictive power [11,19,20]. Furthermore, exploring the underlying molecular mechanisms, particularly the differential regulation of RAAS feedback in ID heterozygotes, could provide deeper biological insight. Incorporating biochemical phenotyping, including measurements of plasma renin activity and ACE activity, in future studies would help confirm the mechanistic basis of the observed genotype-response association. Finally, cost-effectiveness analyses of genotype-guided therapy in resource-limited settings would be valuable before clinical implementation can be recommended.

Conclusion

In this prospective cohort of hypertensive patients treated with fixed-dose valsartan-hydrochlorothiazide, ACE I/D genotype was associated with differential therapeutic response. The most robust finding was the poorer response observed among ID carriers compared to II carriers in adjusted analyses. Although DD carriers showed the most favorable observed response profile, this comparison was not statistically significant in adjusted analysis and should be interpreted cautiously. If replicated in larger studies, these findings could inform a genotype-guided approach to antihypertensive therapy selection, potentially improving first-step treatment success rates. Taken together, these findings support further evaluation of ACE I/D as a candidate pharmacogenetic marker for antihypertensive therapy selection in South Asian populations. Larger multi-center studies, biochemical phenotyping, and genotype-guided interventional trials are needed before clinical implementation can be recommended.
