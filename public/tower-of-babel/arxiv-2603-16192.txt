Structured Semantic Cloaking for Jailbreak Attacks on Large Language Models
Xiaobing Sun, Perry Lam, Shaohua Li, Zizhou Wang, Rick Siow Mong Goh, Yong Liu, Liangli Zhen

Abstract
Modern Large Language Models (LLMs) employ safety mechanisms that extend beyond surface-level input filtering to latent semantic representations and generation-time reasoning, enabling them to recover obfuscated malicious intent during inference and refuse accordingly and rendering many surface-level obfuscation jailbreak attacks ineffective. We propose Structured Semantic Cloaking (S2C), a novel multi-dimensional jailbreak attack framework that manipulates how malicious semantic intent is reconstructed during model inference. Rather than concealing harmful instructions at the surface level, S2C strategically distributes and reshapes semantic cues such that full intent consolidation requires multi-step inference and long-range co-reference resolution within deeper latent representations. The framework comprises three complementary mechanisms: (1) Contextual Reframing , which embeds the request within a plausible high-stakes scenario to bias the model toward compliance; (2) Content Fragmentation , which disperses the semantic signature of the request across disjoint prompt segments; and (3) Clue-Guided Camouflage , which disguises residual semantic cues while embedding recoverable markers that guide output generation. By delaying and restructuring semantic consolidation, S2C degrades safety triggers that depend on coherent or explicitly reconstructed malicious intent at decoding time, while preserving sufficient instruction recoverability for functional output generation. We evaluate S2C across multiple open-source and proprietary LLMs using HarmBench and JBB-Behaviors, where it improves Attack Success Rate (ASR) by 12.4% and 9.7%, respectively, over the current state-of-the-art. Notably, S2C achieves substantial gains on GPT-5-mini, outperforming the strongest baseline by 26% on JBB-Behaviors. We also analyse which combinations perform best against broad families of models, and characterise the trade-off between the extent of obfuscation versus input recoverability on jailbreak success. Our findings suggest that current LLM safety mechanisms remain systematically vulnerable to attacks that manipulate reasoning-time semantic reconstruction at a deeper level, underscoring the need for defences aligned with the full generative reasoning process rather than anchored to surface-level semantic detection alone. Content Warning: This paper contains adversarial examples crafted to reveal potential weaknesses in model behavior. These examples are intended exclusively for research purposes and to enhance model security and safety.



I
Introduction

The deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) across safety-critical applications—from AI-assisted consultancy to automated content generation—has made their robustness against adversarial misuse a pressing concern
[
30
,
16
]
. When manipulated, LLMs can produce phishing emails, illegal drug manufacturing instructions, and hacking strategies, which expose deploying organisations to severe legal, financial, and reputational consequences. Red-teaming and jailbreak attacks have therefore emerged as essential tools for systematically probing safety vulnerabilities before deployment.

Compared to self-optimizing systems, obfuscation and rewriting strategies are comparatively lightweight and proved highly effective against early models with shallow safety alignment, where the initial few output tokens are critical for refusing malicious queries. When refusal tokens like “Sorry” are not among the initial tokens during inference, it usually leads to harmful content being generated

For example, by prefilling the first few tokens with a non-refusal prefix (e.g., “Sure. Here is an example:)̈, a target LLM can be steered away from its alignment routine. However, recent LLMs have transitioned to deeper prevention methods, where the safety alignment can be triggered by malicious semantic signatures (e.g., surface-level patterns) embedded within the internal representations of inputs and intermediate generations. Once such malicious intent is recovered and becomes explicit during decoding, the model frequently refuses to proceed as shown in the left panel of Figure

. These advances render many surface-level obfuscation attacks ineffective. A critical question therefore arises:
what properties of a prompt determine whether safety mechanisms successfully activate?

We identify a key observation that motivates this work. Safety

whether

how

when

Specifically, we find that structurally fragmenting malicious content across disjoint prompt segments can substantially weaken safety triggers, even when the underlying meaning remains inferable through co-reference resolution (Figure

structural coherence
and
explicitness

Building on this observation, we propose a framework of
Structured Semantic Cloaking
(S2C) as shown in the right panel of Figure
1
. Rather than simply hiding or encrypting malicious intent, S2C systematically distributes and reshapes semantic cues across multiple structural dimensions. Specifically, it combines three complementary mechanisms: contextual reframing embeds the request within a plausible high-stakes scenario to bias the model toward compliance; content fragmentation disperses key semantic components across disjoint prompt segments, disrupting the coherent semantic signature that safety classifiers rely upon; and clue-guided camouflage disguises residual semantic cues while embedding recoverable markers that guide output generation.
The central hypothesis of S2C is that, by distributing malicious semantic cues and delaying their consolidation in the LLM, multi-step reasoning and long-range cross-referential inference is required. By delaying intent reconstruction, the most effective stages of their safety mechanisms are bypassed.
This enables the framework to bypass both input-level and generation-level safeguards without requiring explicit reconstruction of the original prompt, while preserving sufficient recoverability for functional output generation. A further advantage of the multi-mechanism design is prompt diversity. By combining its three components, S2C produces a wide range of prompts with varied semantic signature patterns, preventing the target model from relying on superficial pattern matching.
Defending against S2C therefore requires genuine comprehension of the underlying semantic intent across the full generative trajectory, a capability that, as our experiments demonstrate, current safety mechanisms do not reliably possess.

The main contributions of this work are summarised as follows:

1) We identify and empirically characterize a structural weakness in current LLM safety alignment: safety refusal depends not only on whether harmful intent is recoverable, but also on how coherently and at what stage that intent is reconstructed during generation. Using refusal-versus-acceptance log-probability comparisons, we show that fragmenting semantically sensitive content can weaken refusal behavior even when the underlying meaning remains inferable.

2) We propose S2C, a multi-dimensional jailbreak attack framework that probes LLM safety vulnerabilities by targeting reasoning-time semantic reconstruction.
S2C achieves 89.4% in average Attack Success Rate (ASR), outperforming baseline methods by 9.7–50.5 percentage points, with an average gain of 30.4 points and particularly pronounced gains on GPT-5-mini.
The framework remains effective across a range of clue-crafting complexity levels.

3) We evaluate a diverse set of open-source and proprietary LLMs, revealing non-uniform safety alignment across model families and identifying model-specific vulnerabilities. We further analyse which mechanism combinations are most effective against broad families of models, and demonstrate the benefits of clue-crafting strategies at varying levels of complexity.

4) We show that increasing obfuscation complexity alone yields diminishing returns in ASR, indicating that the interplay between structural design choices—rather than complexity per se—determines attack effectiveness. This finding highlights a principled trade-off between obfuscation strength and input recoverability.

II
Related Work

This work relates to three primary lines of jailbreak research: self-optimizing adversarial search, contextual rewriting, and surface-level obfuscation. We review each in turn and identify their weaknesses that justify our approach.

Self-optimizing Adversarial Search

Another line of work treats jailbreak generation as an optimization problem.
[
46
]
identify universal adversarial suffixes that generalize across prompts.
[
29

[

[

Although powerful, these approaches typically require extensive iterative queries to identify high-performing prompts.

Contextual Rewriting

Rather than hiding tokens, contextual rewriting methods manipulate discourse structure and intent framing to relax safety boundaries. These attacks leverage in-context learning, persona adoption, persuasive framing, or extended dialogue to increase compliance.

[
7

[
4

[

[
41

demonstrate the effectiveness of persuasion strategies, and
[
26

However, these methods often exhibit recognizable rhetorical or structural patterns, which can be increasingly handled by modern safety classifiers.

Surface-Level Obfuscation

Surface-Level obfuscation attacks transform the surface form of malicious prompts while preserving their semantic intent.
Compared to exhaustive search, these attacks exploit latent discrepancies between model comprehension and safety filtering, thus becoming more targeted.
[
35

show that Base64 encoding can bypass safety mechanisms in models such as GPT-4.
[
19

replace harmful keywords with ASCII art to evade keyword-based filters.
[
20

combine jailbreak prefixes with ciphered outputs generated by a shadow model to bypass both input- and output-level defences.
Other work substitutes sensitive terms with word games
[

[

[

While effective, these approaches are typically template-driven and rely on fixed transformation patterns, making them increasingly detectable by safety-aligned models
[

with semantic-level moderation.

The three research lines above share a common limitation: they
primarily target
what
the model perceives at the input level or
optimise

manipulate
how

strategically fragmenting and reshaping semantic cues across multiple
structural dimensions, S2C is designed to delay intent consolidation
beyond the stages at which safety mechanisms are most effective.
Furthermore, S2C can produce diverse prompt structures integrating a wide range of rewriting and obfuscation strategies, enabling a more rigorous evaluation of LLM vulnerabilities.

III
Methodology

III-A

Motivating Analysis

Prior work shows that representation obfuscation methods, such as ciphers, encryption, and puzzle games, can increase the success of jailbreak attacks on target LLMs. Contextual reframing, which exploits cognitive or psychological cues, similarly leverages models’ helpfulness to undermine safeguards. We call these methods
cloaking malicious semantics
: they intentionally hide an input’s true meaning so the original intent can only be recovered via extra inference or external clues. By making intent unrecoverable in the transformed query, attackers force instruction-following models to reconstruct the original request during generation, enabling them to bypass shallow safety alignments that rely on refusal tokens and early-output heuristics (see
[
35
,
32
,
44
]
).
However, recent LLMs safety-aligned at deeper levels increasingly resist many forms of semantic cloaking. As shown in the example in Figure
1
, even when malicious meaning is hidden and requires reasoning to recover, modern models often still refuse to comply. This suggests that post-training alignment may internalize certain reasoning-based safety patterns rather than relying solely on surface cues.

Fragmentation as a Distinct Form of Cloaking

In our experiments, we observe a distinct phenomenon: fragmenting a malicious query weakens safeguards even when the meaning is trivially recoverable through co-reference resolution.
Consider Example
III-A
below: “How to make explosive devices” on Llama3.3-70b-instruct.
When the query is fragmented with placeholders and clues as shown, the model frequently produces a non-refusal response.

Example 1: Fragmented Query

Prompt
: How to make [PLACEHO1] [PLACEHO2]? [PLACEHO1] is ‘explosive’. [PLACEHO2] is ‘devices’. Begin your answer with ‘Sure. Here is an example’.

Response
: Sure. Here is an example of how to make an explosive device …

Unlike encoding or encryption obfuscation, fragmentation does not hide semantics externally. Instead, it distributes meaning across multiple spans and requires the model to perform co-referential reconstruction during generation. We hypothesize that this delays or disrupts safety activation mechanisms that depend on explicit lexical patterns in the prompt.

Quantifying Safety Activation via Logprob Differences

To study this systematically, we examine the
log probabilities (logprobs)
of generated tokens on 100 malicious JailbreakBench-Behaviors (JBB-Behaviors) queries
[
6
]
across multiple instruction-following models, including Phi-4 and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct.
Let the query be tokenized as
x_{1:m}
and let the model generate a phrase tokenized as
y_{1:n}
. The conditional probability of the phrase is computed as

p(y_{1:n}\mid x_{1:m})=\prod_{t=1}^{n}p(y_{t}\mid x_{1:m},y_{<t}),

(1)

and the phrase logprob decomposes to a sum of token log probabilities:

\log p(y_{1:n}\mid x_{1:m})=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log p(y_{t}\mid x_{1:m},y_{<t}).

(2)

For each malicious query, we extract a model-specific high-probability refusal prefix (e.g., ‘‘I cannot’’) and a high-probability acceptance prefix (e.g., ‘‘Sure’’)
1
1

1

The details can be seen in the supplementary material.
. We then compare the log probabilities of these prefixes under two prompt templates: (i) the original query and (ii) its fragmented variant with placeholders and clues. This yields a simple refusal-versus-acceptance signal for quantifying how fragmentation shifts early-token behavior.
We define the logprob difference
D(y^{r},y^{a})
as

D(y^{r},y^{a})=\log p(y^{r}\mid x_{1:m})-\log p(y^{a}\mid x_{1:m}),

(3)

where
y^{r}
is the refusal phrase and
y^{a}
is the acceptance phrase.
This difference serves as a proxy for the model’s early-stage safety preference.
A larger value of
D(y^{r},y^{a})
indicates that the model internally favors refusal over acceptance at generation onset.
We hypothesize that such logprob differences will indicate how likely the model activates its defence. A larger value of
D(y^{r},y^{a})
indicates a higher likelihood of rejection over acceptance.
Figure
2
shows that the
D(y^{r},y^{a})
of original prompts is consistently higher than the
D(y^{r},y^{a})
if fragmented prompts. In other words, compared to the original queries, fragmentation systematically reduces the likelihood of rejection
D(y^{r},y^{a})
.

Interpreting the Mechanism

These findings suggest that safety alignment may be partially coupled to explicit surface realizations of harmful content. When the malicious query is directly expressed, refusal tokens receive higher probability mass at generation onset. However, when the meaning must be reconstructed through co-referential inference, the early decoding process appears less strongly guided toward refusal.

The key terms are transformed into neutral clues.

This suggests a potential misalignment between semantic inference mechanisms (e.g., co-reference resolution) and safety activation mechanisms. This dynamic resembles prior observations of misalignment between reasoning and safety
[
22
,
36
]
, but here the tension appears specifically between co-referential reconstruction and early-token safety heuristics.
Our analysis suggests that cloaking malicious semantics is not a one-dimensional phenomenon. Beyond contextual reframing or representation obfuscation, fragmentation introduces a structural dimension: distributing meaning across multiple spans in a way that delays explicit semantic consolidation. It is likely that incorporating fragmentation alongside conventional contextual reframing or representational obfuscation further increases their ability to penetrate LLM safeguards.
Evaluating safety robustness may therefore require multi-dimensional cloaking strategies that vary surface form, structural organization, and semantic distribution.

III-B

Structured Semantic Cloaking

Building on the observed patterns and analysis, we develop a framework that can rigorously cloak semantic meanings in multiple dimensions as shown in Figure
3
.
Let
\mathcal{M}
be a target Large Language Model (LLM) and
\mathcal{G}
be a safety guardrail or content filter that evaluates whether a prompt is malicious. Given a sensitive or harmful query
Q
, the objective of
Semantic Cloaking
is to find a transformation function
\mathcal{F}:Q\rightarrow P
such that the generated prompt
P
bypasses the guardrail while eliciting a successful response
y
from the model.

Our pipeline
\mathcal{F}
consists of three primary components:

Contextual Reframing

The first stage involves a rewriting function
\mathcal{R}
performed by an attack LLM. Given
Q
,
\mathcal{R}
produces a scenario script
S
:

S=\mathcal{R}(Q,\theta),

(4)

where
\theta
represents the strategy parameters (e.g., persuasion).
S
is designed to maximize the significance of the task to increase model compliance.

Content Fragmentation

For a scenario
S
, we identify a set of
k
key terms
T=\{t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{k}\}\subset S
.
These terms can be sensitive words or key phrases that are critical to the prompt’s semantic meaning.
The fragmentation function
\mathcal{F}
maps each key term
t_{i}
to a unique placeholder symbol
\sigma_{i}
. The resulting redacted scenario
S_{\text{red}}
is defined as:

S_{\text{red}}=\mathcal{F}[t_{1}\rightarrow\sigma_{1},\dots,t_{k}\rightarrow\sigma_{k}].

(5)

This process removes the intact semantic meaning from the primary prompt, forcing the target model to utilize long-range dependency to resolve the symbols and obtain semantic meanings.

IV-G

Discussion

The experimental results demonstrate that our proposed framework effectively identifies vulnerabilities in LLMs by leveraging their weaknesses in processing semantic signatures embedded within multi-dimensional cloaking strategies. We show that different models exhibit distinct behaviors when exposed to contextual reframing and varying levels of clue-crafting complexity.
For example, GPT-5-mini appears to be less effective at detecting malicious intent when it is embedded within contextual reframing, whereas the other models do not exhibit the same sensitivity to such reframing. Reconstructing the full prompt during inference may better trigger the defence of Llama3.3-70B. This suggests heterogeneity in safety alignment strategies across models, resulting in unique “blind spots” for each.
Our framework can be used to expose such “blind spots” for further development.
To ensure jailbreak success, an integration of diverse strategies ranging from simple to complex are required for clue crafting, catering to various types of LLMs in terms of vulnerability detection.
Furthermore, increasing the technical difficulty or complexity of obfuscation methods alone does not yield a linear improvement (or any at all) in ASR, due to lower RSR.
While high complexity offers diminishing returns, low-difficulty methods are easily detected and neutralized by more advanced LLMs. Thus, an effective jailbreak requires a calibrated balance to bypass filters without triggering a model’s primary safety logic, as well as understanding the likelihood that each model can recover the clues.

Furthermore, current defence mechanisms appear to rely heavily on semantic signatures (i.e., surface-level patterns) rather than deeper intent understanding. For example, advanced LLMs such as Claude-3-7 and GPT-5-mini demonstrate strong robustness against malicious prompts using single-dimensional obfuscation (e.g., persuasion or cipher-based techniques), yet they frequently fail to reject prompts with multi-dimensional cloaking strategies.

V
Conclusions and Future Work

In this work, we investigate the structural limitations of current safety-aligned LLMs by examining how malicious intent is detected during both input processing and generation. Our analysis reveals that safety mechanisms are strongly coupled to the explicit and coherent surface realization of harmful semantics. When semantic content is structurally fragmented and must be reconstructed through co-referential reasoning, safety activation is significantly weakened.

Motivated by this observation, we propose Structured Semantic Cloaking (S2C), a multi-dimensional red-teaming framework that disrupts semantic coherence while preserving recoverability through reasoning. By combining contextual reframing, content fragmentation, and clue-guided camouflage, S2C redistributes sensitive semantics across structural dimensions, delaying explicit semantic consolidation and reducing the likelihood of safety triggering. Unlike prior jailbreak strategies that rely primarily on single-dimensional obfuscation, S2C systematically exploits the misalignment between semantic reconstruction mechanisms and safety activation heuristics.

Extensive experiments across open-source and proprietary models demonstrate that S2C consistently outperforms multiple strong baselines on both JBB-Behaviors and HarmBench. The framework remains effective even against reasoning-oriented models, indicating that increased reasoning capacity alone does not guarantee robust safety alignment. Our ablation studies further show that attack success does not scale monotonically with obfuscation complexity. Instead, effective jailbreaks require a calibrated balance between semantic disruption and recoverability, highlighting a trade-off between clue decoding difficulty and safety trigger activation.
Moreover, we observe substantial variation in model sensitivity to different cloaking components, and expose the strengths and weaknesses across each LLM tested.
Fragmentation, in particular, appears to expose a structural blind spot: models often fail to activate safeguards when harmful intent emerges gradually through distributed inference rather than appearing as an explicitly coherent prompt.

Overall, our findings indicate that current LLM safety mechanisms may rely heavily on detecting surface-level semantic signatures rather than robustly aligning with reasoning trajectories throughout generation. We position S2C as a diagnostic red-teaming framework for systematically characterizing these vulnerabilities and informing the development of more resilient, reasoning-aware safety defences that remain robust under structurally distributed semantic manipulation.

Several limitations of the present study suggest directions for future research. First, our evaluation is restricted to single-turn
settings without conversational memory. While this allows controlled comparison with baselines, it remains to be seen how multi-turn interactions would impact ASR. On one hand, safety mechanisms may accumulate contextual signals over time and reduce ASR; conversely, longer context allows for better prompt recovery, which raises ASR. Second, we evaluate a fixed set of seven cascaded clue-crafting configurations; automatically
discovering optimal attack strategies and filtering techniques with low RSR remain unexplored. Third, attack success is assessed by a single judge model (fine-tuned Llama-2-13B-chat); incorporating
additional judges or human evaluation would strengthen the reliability of the reported ASRs. Finally, we report ASR as point estimates without confidence intervals; future work should quantify the statistical variability of results across query samples.
