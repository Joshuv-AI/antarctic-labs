Returning Home From Shift: Boundary Maintenance and Infection Control Practices Among Nurses in the Pre‐Vaccination Phase of COVID‐19
Authors: Eileen Willis, Sam Fien, Ashlyn Sahay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo examine how nurses maintained and reconfigured boundaries between work and home in order to protect themselves and their families during the pre‐vaccination phase of the COVID‐19 pandemic.DesignA qualitative descriptive study using a cross‐sectional online survey with open‐ended questions.MethodA self‐designed online survey was conducted between October 2020 and February 2021 with a sample of nurses. Qualitative data from open‐ended survey responses were analysed using thematic analysis then deductively mapped onto the theories of boundary maintenance, spillover and crossover.ResultsA total of 69 nurses participated in this study, drawn from hospital, aged care, general practice and community settings, with ages ranging from 18 to 74 years. Five themes were identified: stress and work intensification; confusion arising from rapidly changing guidelines; resource constraints; anger and resentment towards colleagues able to work remotely; and deliberate strategies to protect family members from infection. The final two themes highlight how nurses actively constructed new physical, emotional and symbolic boundaries between work and home in response to the perceived risks of viral transmission.ConclusionPrior to vaccination, nurses created their own infection control rituals at home to shield their families, revealing how professional knowledge spilled into domestic life, becoming an extension to nurses' work. These critical boundary practices often remained unseen but are vital to frontline nursing labour work during an infection‐control crisis. Understanding and recognising these early responses provides valuable insight for future pandemic preparedness, workforce support and infection prevention policy.Patient or Public ContributionNo patient or public contribution.Keywords: boundary maintenance, COVID‐19, infection control, nurses, shift work, support

Introduction

The COVID‐19 pandemic fundamentally disrupted the organisation of work, care and everyday life across the globe. Unlike workers who transitioned to remote or hybrid working arrangements, frontline nurses remained physically present in workplaces characterised by uncertainty, heightened risk and rapidly evolving infection control protocols (Chaudhary et al. 2024; Trougakos et al. 2020; Smallwood et al. 2021) with concerns about viral transmission extending into domestic and family spaces (Souadka et al. 2020).

This paper presents a qualitative analysis of nurses' experiences during the pre‐vaccination phase of COVID‐19, focusing on how nurses managed the transition from work to home and the strategies they employed to prevent potential transmission of COVID‐19 to family members and loved ones. Drawing on theories of boundary maintenance, spillover and crossover (Ashforth et al. 2000; Rodríguez‐Muñoz et al. 2014; Sahay and Wei 2021), the study examines how nurses reworked everyday routines, domestic spaces and interpersonal relationships in response to perceived infection risk.

While later phases of the pandemic have been characterised by vaccination, improved access to personal protective equipment (PPE) and clearer clinical guidance, the pre‐vaccination period was marked by ambiguity, fear and reliance on individual judgement (Shah et al. 2020; Quigley et al. 2021). Revisiting this period is timely. As health systems increasingly focus on pandemic preparedness, workforce sustainability and burnout prevention, understanding how nurses coped during conditions of profound uncertainty provides critical lessons for policy, leadership and organisational support (Smallwood et al. 2021).

Background

The first case of COVID‐19 was reported in late 2019 in Wuhan, China; by March the World Health Organization (WHO) had declared it a pandemic (Lupton and Willis 2021). Australia detected its first case on 25 January 2020 in Victoria. By mid‐2020, containment strategies including border restrictions, community lockdowns and financial assistance packages were implemented nationally (Storen and Corrigan 2020). While many workers transitioned to home‐based arrangements, healthcare professionals continued direct patient care, necessitating strategies to strengthen the boundary between work and home.

Early Research on Patient Facing Nurses

One of the first major studies on the risk to healthcare workers and their families was conducted by Shah et al. in the National Health Service (NHS) in Scotland. Published in September 2020, the study was conducted between March and June of that year, drawing on data from over 150,000 healthcare workers, primarily nurses and doctors, with 57.3% of them engaged in direct patient care. Over 200,000 household members were associated with these healthcare professionals. Shah's study found that healthcare workers and members of their household made up one‐sixth of all hospital admissions, giving them a two‐ to threefold increased risk to COVID‐19 (Shah et al. 2020). Internationally, the International Council of Nurses estimated that 600 nurses had died and over 450,000 were infected by June 2020 (Quigley et al. 2021; Tang et al. 2020), with most infected professionals working in non‐infectious wards.

In Australia, Quigley et al. (2021) estimated that approximately 536 healthcare workers contracted the virus prior to July 2020, and a further 3500 following an outbreak in Victoria in late July. Nurses comprised 6.03% of all cases and were 2.69 times more likely to become infected, with infection rates varying significantly across jurisdictions, reflecting differential outbreak exposures and access to infection control resources (Quigley et al. 2021).

Nurses alongside other healthcare workers faced prolonged shift work, multiple deaths per shift and significant resource strain, particularly where both partners worked in healthcare (Smallwood et al. 2021). In some countries, frontline workers lost rental accommodation or were shunned by neighbours due to fear of contamination (Souadka et al. 2020). The Australian COVID‐19 Frontline Healthcare Workers Survey found mild‐to‐severe anxiety, burnout and depression at 58%, 70% and 57% respectively, with nurses, women and younger workers (aged 25–34) most at risk (Smallwood et al. 2021). Creating a protective boundary around family and friends was a key concern of this cohort.

Boundaries, Spillover and Crossover of Roles

Boundary maintenance theory encompasses three perspectives: the clear delineation of professional and family roles, where employer demands should not encroach upon the home (Pocock 2005); the management of multiple role transitions requiring behavioural containment (Ashforth et al. 2000); and challenges to rigid boundaries through spillover and crossover (Sahay and Wei 2021).

For example, boundary work acknowledges that workers move between roles several times during their day or working week. A nurse may take on the roles of manager of a clinic, transfer to that of a parent or from supervisor of a team to a subordinate within the executive structure of the hospital. Each of these role transitions requires considerable energy and thinking given they may occur several times throughout the day. Managing the boundaries between work, family and other social spaces occur regularly for most individuals. What is required is an understanding of the expectations governing these behavioural boundaries, with the capacity for containment (Ashforth et al. 2000). Individuals attempt to achieve this with minimal disruption to themselves, their colleagues or family roles and expectations.

Clear demarcations between roles practiced at work and those at home are difficult to maintain. Much of what we develop as parents or managers spills or crosses over into other life domains. The notion of spillover and crossover between boundaries is not an unfamiliar concept (Golparvar et al. 2014; Rodríguez‐Muñoz et al. 2014; Westman 2001). Most people are familiar with bringing home worries from work, or taking family concerns into the workplace. The walls between family and work are permeable (Ashforth et al. 2000). Sahay and Wei (2021) distinguish spillover and crossover from one another in the following way: a spillover is the way in which the practices and associated emotional factors occur for the person across a variety of roles they inhabit. For example, in an ethnographic study by Willis (2009) a nurse reports that she obsessively checked supermarket food used‐by‐dates aware that this practice was developed out of a concern for medication expiry dates (Willis 2009). The practice spilled over into ordinary everyday life as the habit became ingrained beyond the role of nurse. Crossover occurs when these practices and emotional responses are thrust upon others. Crossover also occurs when the tensions from either work or home impacts on family or work colleagues. An example would be when personal troubles at home result in a nurse coming late for a shift, being sharp with colleagues or snapping at a patient. Spillover can be said to be at the individual level, while crossover manifests through interactions.

The risks of spillover and crossover to roles and the breaching of boundaries are more likely to occur during times of stress whether these be personal, social or political. The COVID‐19 pandemic is such an event given its protraction, uncertainty, the intense media focus and the fear of contagion. Our interest is to provide spillover and crossover accounts of nurses' attempts to create a protective infectious control boundary around their family during the first 2 years of the pandemic, prior to the roll out of vaccinations.

Methods

A survey was administered between October 2020 and February 2021 using the Qualtrics platform. In total, 126 surveys were returned, of which 69 were completed by registered nurses and form the analytical basis of this paper. Ethical approval was granted by the Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC 22584).

All responses were anonymous and data were treated as confidential throughout.

Participants

Participants were recruited using purposive sampling through healthcare professional organisations and targeted social media platforms, including profession‐specific workforce group pages. Invitations were distributed via email. Eligibility criteria required participants to be aged 18 years or older and employed as a healthcare professional at the time of the survey.

Survey

The survey comprised 24 items with seven open‐ended questions. For the purposes of this paper, only the demographic details and analysis of the qualitative open‐ended questions are presented. The demographic variables included gender, age, country of practice/state, marital status, number of people in household, primary area of work (hospital, aged care, community) and if they have engaged in the care of a positive COVID‐19 presentation.

The seven open‐ended questions (see Appendix S1) sought participants' perspectives and views on the support they required, the strategies they employed to protect their families, how they supported patients during the pandemic, and whether or not they had witnessed incidents that put themselves, patients or other staff at risk of infection. Participants were also asked about future support needs and how infection control practices and policies could have been improved. Collective responses totalled 9946 words across 642 sentences.

Data Analysis

The responses of the 69 nurses were retrieved and analysed manually by author EW using thematic analysis as outlined by Braun and Clarke (2006). Thematic codes were colour coded within a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet to facilitate systematic organisation and comparison of the data. The coded dataset was independently reviewed by all authors, and consensus was reached through discussion where discrepancies arose. Initial analysis proceeded inductively: an iterative reading of the data revealed a prominent theme centred on nurses' infection control practices at the work‐home boundary. This finding prompted engagement with sociological literature on boundary maintenance, spillover and crossover (Ashforth et al. 2000; Pocock 2005, 2008; Rodríguez‐Muñoz et al. 2014; Sahay and Wei 2021; Westman 2001), following which responses were re‐read deductively to assess congruence with these frameworks. This approach resonates with template analysis whereby researchers may start out with a theoretical hunch and read the data for confirmatory evidence (Brooks et al. 2015; Waring and Wainwright 2008).

Results

Demographic Details

A total of 69 nurses with an age range of 18–74 years participated in the survey. The majority were female (91.3%), and over half were married (56.5%), with a further 14.5% partnered and 8.7% in a de facto relationship. Most participants lived in households of two to four people (2: 33.3%; 3: 18.8%; 4: 24.6%). Of the 69 nurses, 51 (73.9%) were registered nurses, with 40 (58.0%) working in a hospital setting; others worked in residential aged care, general practice or community settings. Forty‐five nurses (65.2%) reported 10 or more years of experience in their occupation. Whilst many nurses had not engaged in the care of a positive COVID‐19 case during this time (59.4%), the majority reported concern about bringing COVID‐19 into their own home and compromising the health of their family and loved ones.

Thematic Analysis

We identified five major themes from the qualitative findings. These were, feelings of stress and overwork; feelings of confusion; resource constraints; anger at those colleagues who could work at home; and strategies for protecting the family. The first three themes situate COVID‐19 as exacerbating what is part and parcel of normal everyday nurse experience (Sahay and Wei 2021), while the remaining two themes: anger at colleagues working off site and strategies for protecting family capture the notion of boundaries, spillover and crossover.

Feelings of Stress, Overwork and Confusion

The first two themes identified reflect the intensification of everyday practice for nurses. Stress and overwork were experienced by several respondents as one registered (RN) caring for COVID‐19 patients noted:

Increased staff and resources. Increased education on mindfulness and how to deal with stress/burnout. An assessment of mental health to see if we were fit for work with regards to anxiety/burnout and stress. (Hospital RN, 25–34 years)

Participants indicated their confusion of constant changes to shifts, guidelines and protocols led to ward tensions, due to a lack of regular communication strategies from hospital and state medical offices. This, in turn, led to tensions when communicating with patients and their families. The quote below highlights some of these issues raised by a nurse manager:

Better communication/instructions. As often the first point of call for the public, there was a lack of communication between hospital, community services, GPs, pathology regarding basic logistic questions—e.g., opening hours, testing requirements/referrals. We would often have to google search information for patients, and it was often a source of irritation for the public that we didn't know instantly. There was frequently anger towards admin staff when visiting restrictions were in place or when visitors were directed to the ward (because they were in a palliative bed as per standing instructions) only to be chastised by nursing staff because the patient was not palliative and the ward structure had changed so that palliative beds were not used for palliative patients but for ‘Red Zone’ patients awaiting swab results. (Hospital nurse manager, 35–44 years)

While another respondent noted that planning at the local level still had to be done, despite high level advice:

Better more frequent communication, initially it was very muddled and I just went with my gut protecting myself and the patients as you would with any potential infectious event, only more so. (RN in General Practice, 45–54 years)

Resource Constraints

The third theme identified resource constraints linked to the speed of the pandemic, and the capacity of healthcare systems to put the necessary resources in place in time. There was the issue of responding to a crisis, as well as working within a healthcare system that was already under strain from decades of efficiency and productivity measures (van Gool and Pearson 2014; Willis et al. 2017). The theme hooks into the nation‐wide confusion over guidelines, given the different regulations across the various jurisdictions, shortages of PPE which compromised staff health, and long shifts and staff shortages as outlined by an RN engaged in aged care in the community:

Overall, our organisation provided good support, however as a national company they did at times forget that COVID‐19 impacts were different across states and at times, applied a one‐rule fits all. Eventually, feedback was taken onboard and different rules were made for different states. It would have been good to have less paperwork/governance around this time, given the unique situations we were facing. In terms of PPE, the organisation was fantastic in doing its absolute best to resource us with PPE. The issue however there was just no stocks. Therefore, at times, staff did not have enough PPE but that was not the organisation's fault as they were doing their best to get supplies. As soon as supplies came in, these were distributed as best as possible. (Aged Care RN, 35–44 years)

While another registered nurse listed a range of equipment shortages

A little more recognition that I was doing the right thing. Responsible for ensuring we had enough hygiene, cleaning and other equipment‐ cleaning cloths, hand gel, video conference equipment, etc. (Hospital RN, 45–54 years)

Themes Related to COVID‐19 and the Boundaries Between Work and Home

Two themes captured the feelings and actions of nursing staff as they negotiated the boundaries between work and home; anger at colleagues working off‐site given their own fear and exhaustion, and strategies for protecting family from the infection through establishing improved boundaries between work and home. As already noted, frontline healthcare workers could not work at home, although it is clear that senior managers in some health facilities did so. This created tensions for frontline staff in terms of emotional support, as a RN working in radiography noted:

A chance to talk to managers but they stayed off site … so no other nursing supports on site to discuss all the new guidelines with. (Hospital RN, 55–64 years)

These feelings of isolation were exacerbated by the increased intensity of the work, and the fatigue, frustration and exhaustion experienced by nurses coupled with what they saw as sub‐optimal care:

I always had to strongly advocate to put interventions in place to keep patient appointments or proactively manage infection risk. It was quite exhausting, and 1‐ 2 weeks later our interventions became the norm. It would have been nice to be supported in the1st instance. (Outpatient RN, 15–25 years)

When asked how the situation could have been improved, one nurse in a residential aged care noted:

Allow more of those who could work from home to do so, allow more local or limited home visits ie., Allied health, allow more telehealth consults, spread nurses across departments to enhance their skills during such a time. (Aged care Nursing Assistant, 25–34 years)

The second theme was the most extensive and cohesive, with several examples of spillover behaviour. Nurses outlined the strategies put in place to ensure they did not take infection into their home and contaminate their family. In the majority of cases these nurses established a physical barrier within their home, along with a set of actions that signalled they were moving from one site to the next or breaching a boundary. These nurses felt it was important not to take the stress they experienced at work home as one RN suggested when reflecting on how she might reduce crossover between the two sites (work and home) and how she might be supported. Interestingly, this nurse identifies crossover issues coming from both work and home:

To be listened to when stating how difficult the job was doing the mental and physical work which was far greater than other years. To have someone to talk to to download the day [debrief] and not take the stressors home with me. For my siblings to understand how difficult it was to care for our mother who is 89 and insists on living alone and her demands for things to be done for her that were not safe for me to do. (Hospital/Aged care RN, 64–75 years)

Several nurses outlined the steps they instigated as they finished their shift and moved from the hospital to their home demonstrating the spillover of skills learnt as a nurse. For most, this meant establishing an area within the house that acted as a threshold between their own possible contamination and the family. They included practices such as doing a separate wash for their uniform, constant cleaning, changing their clothes in the garage, not greeting or hugging family members until they had showered, sleeping separately from spouse or getting them to do all the shopping, living in another part of the house or in one case, living on the other side of the border. As reported by a number of nurses:

Moved rooms ie., other end of the house, different schedules to others ie. Dinner at a much later time, more regular cleaning. (Nursing Assistant 35–44 years, Aged Care)

State government policy meant that other than my husband I did not see family, staying away was protection enough. (Hospital RN, 45–54 years)

No hugs till after shower, clothes washed separately to rest of family's washing. (Hospital RN 35–44 years)

Disinfecting my car, clothes & shoes. Not accepting too many new clients. Stripping in my garage then going straight into the shower. (Hospital EN, 35–44)

Others intensified their hand hygiene at work before crossing the threshold to home:

Serious hand hygiene before leaving and when returning, changing clothes and shoes before entering home, immediate shower when returning home, put my clothes and shoes in the closest room to the front door under a cupboard as opposed to usual place when returning home, used disposable containers/materials for meals. (Hospital RN, 24–35)

Conversely, a young RN who felt confident of the infection control practices in her workplace, was more concerned at contracting COVID‐19 in her local shopping centre:

I avoided using any common rooms. But I wasn't worried about bringing it home from work. We had the PPE and we used it accordingly. We did not wear the same PPE all shift it would change after every single encounter. I was more concerned about contracting the virus whist grocery shopping than at work. (Hospital RN, 24–35)

One of the major outcomes of COVID‐19 has been the cessation of celebration rituals and events. This has occurred partly to reduce the coming together of large crowds, but also because it is difficult to celebrate events via Skype, or Zoom, or to organise refreshments in such situations. However, as one RN reported when asked what she had done to improve the working conditions:

Allowing staff to speak about how they are feeling, reminding staff about employee assistance scheme, communicate with the team, ensure celebrations are continued in a covid safe way‐ we celebrated international nurses day‐ all food was individually wrapped, team T‐shirts, etc. (Hospital RN, 45–54 years)

Discussion

This study examined how nurses in Australia negotiated the boundary between work and home during the early, pre‐vaccination phase of the COVID‐19 pandemic. The findings highlight that boundary maintenance became an intensified and expanded form of nursing labour, enacted under conditions of uncertainty, resource scarcity and heightened fear of contagion. Although most participants were not directly caring for patients diagnosed with COVID‐19, they nonetheless experienced substantial work intensification, confusion arising from rapidly changing policies and anxiety about transmitting infection to family members. These conditions shaped both emotional experiences and practical responses, revealing complex patterns of spillover and crossover between professional and domestic domains.

Consistent with existing literature, nurses reported elevated stress, fatigue and frustration associated with workload intensification and policy ambiguity during the early pandemic period (Smallwood et al. 2021; Vagni et al. 2020). Rapidly evolving guidelines, inconsistencies across jurisdictions and limited access to PPE contributed to uncertainty at the point of care. Importantly, these stressors were not confined to the workplace but crossed into home life, as nurses struggled to disengage from professional vigilance and anxiety after shifts. This crossover of emotional strain is well documented in boundary theory, particularly during periods of crisis when role demands become intensified and less predictable (Ashforth et al. 2000; Westman 2001).

A distinctive finding of this study is the extent to which nurses actively reconstructed boundaries between work and home through deliberate infection control practices. Unlike much of the work and family‐focused literature, which emphasises the erosion or collapse of boundaries during crises, our findings demonstrate a paradoxical strengthening of boundaries at the physical threshold of the home. Nurses described ritualised practices changing clothes in garages, immediate showering, separating laundry, disinfecting personal items, avoiding physical contact with family members, and isolating within the home that functioned symbolically and practically to contain perceived risk. These practices illustrate spillover in its most literal sense: professional infection control knowledge and routines were transferred into domestic space.

This pattern complicates claims that the pandemic universally increased permeability between work and home (Sahay and Wei 2021). Rather than simple boundary erosion, nurses enacted what might be understood as selective permeability; that is, while emotional strain and vigilance crossed domains, the home was simultaneously reconfigured as a controlled extension of the clinical environment. Infection prevention rituals became institutionalised within households, transforming everyday domestic practices into quasi‐clinical routines. In this sense, boundary work did not disappear but was intensified, reworked and spatially relocated.

The findings also reveal important organisational dynamics of power and inequality shaping boundary maintenance. Several nurses expressed resentment and anger towards colleagues and managers who were able to work remotely, while frontline staff remained physically present and exposed. Similar tensions have been reported internationally, where remote managerial work was perceived as a withdrawal of visible leadership and emotional support (Donato et al. 2021; Souadka et al. 2020). In this study, perceived managerial absence exacerbated feelings of isolation and reduced opportunities for informal debriefing, increasing the likelihood that stress crossed over into home life. These findings highlight that boundary maintenance is not solely an individual responsibility but is shaped by organisational structures and leadership practices (Pocock 2008; Sahay and Wei 2021).

Notably, fear of infecting family members emerged as a dominant concern, even though actual infection rates among participants were low. This discrepancy highlights the role of perceived risk, media coverage and uncertainty in shaping behaviour (Lupton and Willis 2021). The moral responsibility nurses felt towards protecting loved ones intensified self‐surveillance and precautionary practices, adding an invisible layer of labour to already demanding roles. Boundary maintenance thus became not only a personal coping strategy but also a moral practice grounded in professional identity and care ethics.

Together, these findings contribute to sociological and nursing scholarship by foregrounding boundary work as a critical yet unrecognised component of frontline nursing during public health crises or emergencies. They demonstrate how spillover and crossover are not merely psychological processes but are enacted through embodied practices, spatial reconfigurations and domestic routines (Ashforth et al. 2000; Sahay and Wei 2021). Recognising this expanded scope of nursing labour has implications for workforce support, organisational leadership and pandemic preparedness planning.

Future responses to infectious disease outbreaks must acknowledge that infection control and risk management extends beyond clinical settings and into nurses' homes and personal lives. Supporting nurses requires not only adequate PPE and clear communication, but also organisational recognition of the emotional, moral and domestic burdens that accompany frontline care. Making this invisible labour visible is essential for developing more sustainable and equitable health system responses in future crises.

Limitation

A limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size (n = 69), which may limit the representativeness of the findings. As a preliminary study conducted during an acute phase of the pandemic, participation may have been constrained by frontline nurses' competing clinical and family responsibilities, contributing to potential self‐selection bias among those most attuned to pandemic impacts. Recruitment via email and selected social media platforms may also have limited participation to regular users of these channels. In addition, qualitative insights were drawn from open‐ended survey responses, which were often brief and did not allow for follow‐up or clarification.

Conclusion

This study captures a critical moment in the COVID‐19 pandemic when nurses navigated uncertainty without the protective assurance of vaccination. Their experiences reveal how boundary maintenance became an essential, though unrecognised, component of nursing work. As health systems prepare for future pandemics, these findings highlight the need to recognise and support the full scope of nurses' labour both inside and beyond the clinical setting.
