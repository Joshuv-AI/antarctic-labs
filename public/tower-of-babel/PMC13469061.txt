Expanding 68Ga radiolabeling capacity: off-label use of PSMA-11 single vial cold kits through aliquoting and dual generator utilization
Authors: Olivier Perrin, Sarah Benkhoris, Johanne Vanney, Laurie Barthelemi, Vincent Boudousq, Emmanuel Deshayes, Claire Guisset, Ian Soulairol, Léa Rubira, Cyril Fersing, Daniele Alizé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProstate cancer is a highly prevalent malignancy, and PET imaging targeting prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) has become essential for assessing recurrence, metastatic spread and eligibility for radioligand therapy, with a 38% increase in PSMA PET activity reported in France between 2022 and 2023. [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 prepared from single-vial cold kits such as Locametz® is now widely used, but kit cost, generator activity decay, and preparation procedures restricted to the marketing authorization dossier can limit the number of patients treated per day. This multicenter study investigated, under GMP conditions, an off-label strategy combining aliquoting of Locametz® vials with single or dual elutions from GalliaPharm® and GalliAd®Ge/68Ga generators and compared manual versus automated eluate fractionation. Four main approaches were evaluated: single or double elution for a full kit vial and single or double elution for two aliquots prepared 4–6 h apart, with full quality control according to the European Pharmacopoeia and the Locametz® summary of product characteristics (SmPC), as well as pharmaco-economic and dosimetric evaluations.ResultsRadiolabeling was effective under all conditions that remained within the kit’s buffer capacity. Single-generator labeling of complete vials or aliquots reliably yielded radiochemical purity (RCP) > 98% and stable complexes for 4 h. Aliquoting allowed for two separate preparations from a single Locametz® kit without compromising RCP. Elutions with dual generators proved efficient when the overall HCl 0.1 M volume was < 2.4 mL, resulting in activities reaching approximately 1.7 GBq with RCP ≥ 98%. High acidity or uneven distribution of aliquots caused pH variations and decreased RCP, causing a restricted number of failures (7%). Radiochemical yields achieved approximately 89% in refined standard procedures. Automated eluate fractionation greatly decreased radiation exposure to hands in comparison to manual handling. Pharmaco-economic evaluation showed significant cost savings per patient, with routine daily capacity ranging from 8 to 16 patients per kit, depending on generator age.ConclusionsAliquoting Locametz® vial and optimizing generator use significantly increase [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 production capacity. These strategies expand patient throughput and reduce costs of PSMA imaging. They provide a practical solution for centers facing rising PSMA PET demand and limited access to 18F-PSMA tracers and can be potentially applied to other 68Ga cold-kit radiopharmaceuticals.Keywords: Gallium-68, PET, PSMA, Prostate cancer, Radiopharmaceuticals, Radiosynthesis, Double elution, Cold kit, Radiolabeling optimization, Dual generators

Background

Prostate cancer is the fourth leading cause of cancer worldwide and was the second most frequently diagnosed malignancy among men in 2022, according to the Global Cancer Observatory (Absolute numbers 2022). Positron emission tomography (PET) targeting the prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) has become the gold standard in nuclear medicine for assessing prostate cancer recurrence and metastasis, as well as for pre-therapeutic planning with radioligand therapies such as [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-617 or [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T (Lenzo et al. 2018, Eder et al. 2014, Fendler et al. 2017, Garje et al. 2025). In France, the clinical demand for PSMA PET imaging increased by 38% between 2022 and 2023, underscoring its rapid integration into routine practice (Janneau and Couturier 2023).

Since its first use in human in 2011 (Afshar-Oromieh et al. 2013), [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 PET imaging has become an established procedure in nuclear medicine, supported by the development of cold kit formulations (Kurash et al. 2020, Calderoni et al. 2020) such as Locametz® (Novartis), Isoprotrace® (Isotopia Molecular Imaging LTD) and Illuccix® (Telix), which simplify the radiolabeling process (Gelardi et al. 2023). Locametz® received approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in December 2020, followed by Illuccix® in December 2021 and in March 2025 by Gozellix®. In Europe, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) approved Locametz® in October 2022, Illuccix® has been granted marketing authorization through a European decentralized procedure in January 2025 (EMA LOCAMETZ 2022, ILLUCCIX 2021), and via national procedure only for Isoprotrace® (ISOPROTRACE 2024). These cold kits differ in formulation and key parameters such as the number of vials, buffer composition and concentration, amount of vector molecule, and total reaction volume (Table 1). By eliminating the need for pre-purification of the 68Ga eluate, terminal purification of the radiolabeling product, and final sterilization, these cold kit formulations substantially reduce preparation time and simplify quality control procedures (Meisenheimer et al. 2020, Meisenheimer et al. 2021).

Although automated “in-house” radiolabeling of investigational radiopharmaceuticals using synthesis modules requires the compilation of an investigational medicinal product dossier (IMPD), it can offer significantly lower costs per preparation once the initial investment is amortized, especially owing to the minimal amounts of precursor required (Yüksel and Uğur 2022). However, regulatory requirements for radiopharmaceuticals vary across countries. In some countries like France, once a radiopharmaceutical receives marketing authorization, “in-house” preparation is no longer permitted. Instead, production should adhere to the specifications outlined in the marketing authorization, including the approved indications and preparation method (Decristoforo et al. 2017, Nataf et al. 2023). This regulatory constraint, coupled with the high costs of cold kits and 68Ga generators and suitable equipment brings a challenge in meeting the growing demand for PSMA PET imaging, especially in publicly funded institutions operating under constrained and finite resource allocations.

Concurrently, advancements in digital PET/CT technology have enabled significant reductions in administered activity while preserving high image quality. Recent PET systems, such as OMNI digital PET and whole-body PET, allow activity levels as low as 1.1 MBq/kg, thereby improving both cost-effectiveness and patient safety (Liu et al. 2024). Fluorine-18 offers superior physical properties and a longer half-life than 68Ga (Sánchez-Crespo et al. 2004). Its lower positron energy results in a shorter positron range in tissue, improving spatial resolution and producing sharper PET images. In addition, the centralized production of 18F-labeled radiotracers introduces logistical constraints, including delivery delays due to production or quality control failures, variability in available activity at the time of administration, transportation distance from cyclotron facilities, restricted production days, and potential disruptions related to road traffic. These factors may affect scheduling flexibility and tracer availability. While FDA and EMA approvals of 18F-labeled PSMA tracers (e.g., [18F]piflufolastat) have broadened diagnostic options, 68Ga remains the most convenient alternative, despite occasional generator shortages. A key strategy to enhance radiolabeling efficiency involves the use of higher 68Ga activity, which can be achieved through multiple generator elutions (Breeman et al. 2005, Brandwijk et al. 2024). Also, the use of high-activity generators (up to 3.7 GBq) is authorized in the United States (Wang et al. 2024), and cyclotron-based production methods can generate substantially greater quantities of 68Ga (Lepareur 2022, Rodnick et al. 2020, Thisgaard et al. 2021, Becker et al. 2021). Furthermore, the European Pharmacopoeia distinguishes between generator-produced and cyclotron-produced [68Ga]gallium chloride in two separate monographs (EuropeanPharmacopeia Monograph 2020, European Pharmacopeia Monograph 2023).

In Europe, several GMP-certified 68Ge/68Ga generators (good manufacturing practice) are approved and commercially available, including GalliaPharm® (Eckert & Ziegler, Berlin, Germany) and GalliAd® (IRE ELiT, Fleurus, Belgium). These generators use a titanium oxide (TiO2)-based matrix and elute 68Ga with 0.1 M HCl, varying in elution volume. The pH of the reaction medium in which the eluate is used is critical for radiolabeling efficiency, necessitating precise adjustment of the HCl volume.

To address increasing clinical demand and economic constraints, we sought to explore strategies for optimizing the 68Ga radiolabeling of PSMA-11 (gozetotide) cold kits using single or dual generator elutions under good manufacturing practice conditions. The European Pharmacopoeia places responsibility on the final user to ensure appropriate handling and quality of the preparation, thus allowing some procedural flexibility in radiolabeling (Meisenheimer et al. 2021, Nelson et al. 2022).

In this context, building on the work reported by Van Brandwijk et al. in 2024 (Brandwijk et al. 2024), we evaluated an approach involving the division of single-vial cold kits into two aliquots, combined with the use of one or two GMP-grade 68Ge/68Ga generators for labeling. However, this strategy introduced specific challenges, such as an increased reaction volume and more complex pH buffering due to the combined eluates. Quality controls procedures were performed in accordance with the specifications described in the summary of product characteristics (SmPC) of the cold kit used, including pH and radiochemical purity (RCP) measurement. Additional analyses, such as radionuclide purity and sterility testing, were performed to ensure a more comprehensive quality assessment of the radiopharmaceutical drug on three different batches.

We adapted multiple methodologies to optimize cold kit preparation using various generator configurations, employing either manual or automated processes. Automation offers the dual advantage of improving reproducibility and reducing radiation exposure for operators (Brandwijk et al. 2024). Our objective was to enhance access to 68Ga radiopharmaceuticals by aliquoting cold kit while maintaining high-quality and safety standards for clinical application. This investigation ultimately enabled the identification of the most suitable generator configurations.

Methods

General information

To date, the literature on [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 radiolabeling has primarily focused on large-scale production involving pooled eluates, typically derived from two generators. In this study, we expanded on existing approaches by not only employing eluate pooling from two generators but also implementing aliquoting of the cold kit. To assess the impact of these different methodologies, we evaluated several radiolabeling strategies:

A single elution from either model of generator for labeling a single Locametz® vial (SmPC conditions).

A single elution from either model of generator for labeling a single Locametz® vial (SmPC conditions).

A double elution from either model of generator for labeling a single Locametz® vial, using either the same or different generators from various manufacturers.

Equipment for synthesis

Hospital A Radiopharmaceutical preparations were carried out in an ISO 5 high-energy hot cell (NMC-50 mm68Ga, Tema Sinergie, Faenza, Italy). The facility was equipped with a calibrated ionization chamber (CRC®-25 PET, Capintec, USA). Generators elution was performed using the GAIA® V2 module (Elysia-Raytest, Germany) controlled via the dedicated software (GAIA Control, Elysia-Raytest, Germany). Initially, one GalliAd® 1.85 GBq generator (IRE ELiT, Fleurus, Belgium) and one GalliaPharm® 1.85 GBq generator (Eckert & Ziegler AG, Berlin, Germany) were used. In a later phase, two GalliaPharm® 1.85 GBq generators were employed. Elutions were performed sequentially (starting with the generator of lower activity) into a single bulk syringe for subsequent use.

Hospital B Radiopharmaceuticals were prepared in an ISO 5 shielded cell with a laminar airflow (MEDI 9000 Research 4R, LemerPax, La Chapelle-sur-Erdre, France) equipped with a calibrated ionization chamber (Capintec 55t PET dose calibrator). Syntheses involved one or two GalliAd® 1.85 GBq generators (IRE ELiT, Fleurus, Belgium), each sequentially eluted with 1.1 mL of 0.1 M HCl directly into cold kit vials. Generators elution was performed using a GAIA® V2 module to generate vacuum within the kit vial.

Equipment for PET/CT imaging

Hospital A In Hospital A, two PET/CT (OMNI®, General Electric) systems were used.

Hospital B In Hospital B, one conventional PET/CT system (Discovery®, General Electric) was used.

Aliquot preparation

In both hospitals, lyophilized PSMA-11 kits (Locametz®, Novartis, France) were reconstituted with 1 mL of water for injection (WFI, B. Braun, Germany) and gently mixed using a Vortex-Genie® (Scientific Industries Elmis) prior to being divided into two 0.5 mL aliquots. The first aliquot was transferred with a low dead volume 1 mL syringe (Injekt®-F 1 mL, B. Braun, Germany) with a coated needle (Sterican® 23G 0.60 × 25 mm, B. Braun, Germany) in a sterile vial (TC-ELU-5®, Curium). The second aliquot remained aseptically stored in its origin Locametz® vial at 2–8 °C for 4 to 6 h before use in a subsequent radiolabeling later the same day. All medical devices recommended by the suppliers, including syringes, needles, and air venting filters, were used to minimize the risk of metal contamination. In Hospital A, a precision balance was used to measure the mass of each aliquot, ensuring accurate repartition. The weights of both the Locametz® and TC-ELU-5® vials were recorded before and after reconstitution. This allowed precise calculation of the proportion of PSMA-11 and buffer retained in each aliquot from the original Locametz® vial in each aliquot.

Conventional preparation process

The preparation of [68Ga]Ga-PSMA began with the elution of the generator. Once eluted, the 68Ga eluate was carefully transferred to the Locametz® or the aliquot vial, depending on the selected radiolabeling strategy. A needle connected to a 0.2 μm sterile air venting filter was used, as outlined in the SmPC, in order to maintain atmospheric pressure during the 68GaCl3 transfer for Galliapharm®. For GalliAd®, the Locametz® vial or the aliquot vial was connected through the vent needle with 0.2 μm sterile air venting filter to a vacuum vial (25 mL minimum volume) by means of a sterile needle (size 21G-23G) or to a peristaltic pump to start the elution. The vial was gently mixed by repeated inversions to ensure complete homogenization of the reagents, followed by an incubation period of 5 min at room temperature to allow radiolabeling. After incubation, the activity of the preparation was measured using a dose calibrator. The final step involved dilution of the radiolabeled solution with saline, reaching the appropriate volume and concentration for clinical administration.

Locametz® buffer capacity

The maximum volume of hydrochloric acid (HCl 0.1 M) that could be added without lowering the pH below 3.2 (SmPC pH Lower limit) was determined based on the buffering capacity of the sodium acetate in the Locametz® formulation (i.e. 573.2 µmol per kit). The calculation was performed using the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, considering the acetic acid/acetate buffer pair (pKa = 4.76). This allowed estimation of the amount of HCl required to shift the acetate/acetic acid ratio to the targeted pH. Based on this approach, the maximum allowable volume of HCl 0.1 M was determined to be 5.58 mL per kit to maintain the pH at or above 3.2.

Preparation under alternative conditions

For each method, three independent preparations of [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 were performed, except for those already described in the SmPC.

Elution processing

Hospital A Elution of the GalliaPharm® generators for PSMA-11 aliquots radiolabeling required processing via fractionated elution. Initially performed manually, fractionation was subsequently automated on the GAIA® system using a sterile fluidic cassette kit (ABX or HUAYI) to optimize operator radiation safety. Fractionation was performed to increase the volume activity of 68Ga, allowing for a higher concentration of radioactivity in a smaller volume while minimizing the amount of 0.1 M HCl added to each aliquot. Automated fractionation was particularly important when handling high 68Ga activity to minimize extremity exposure. Method readjustments were necessary throughout the lifetime of the generators. To optimize the process, an elution profile study of the 68Ge/68Ga generator was conducted to determine the volume at which approximately 70% of the available 68Ga activity was eluted. This involved manual fractionation and activity measurement of successive 0.5 mL fractions of the eluate every 15 s in separate vials. Additionally, it was crucial to ensure that the cumulative, fractionated elutions from both generators did not lead to an increase in breakthrough levels of 68Ge. In the case of double elution of GalliaPharm®, a protocol was developed in which fractionated elutions were first pooled to form a bulk solution. The total volume was visually verified in a syringe before transfer into the aliquot vial. If the bulk volume exceeded 2.4 mL, the surplus was discarded to prevent synthesis failure due to excessive acidity, and radiolabeling was carried out manually, ensuring optimal radiation protection through the use of syringe shields.

Hospital B 68Ga eluates were used without elution processing, after elution of either one or two GalliAd® generators.

At both sites, to ensure process reproducibility and consistent radiolabeling performance, generators were eluted daily from Monday to Friday.

Quality controls

Quality controls were conducted on the 3 test batches for each method, in accordance with the specifications outlined in the European Pharmacopoeia monograph of [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 and the SmPC of Locametz®.

Appearance

Organoleptic characteristics were assessed by visual inspection, to confirm that the final solution was clear, colorless and free of particulate matter.

pH evaluation

The expected pH range of the radiolabeling products, as defined by the SmPC of Locametz®, was between 3.2 and 6.5.

Hospital A: pH was assessed using pH-Fix indicator strips 0.0–6.0 (ref. 92115, Macherey-Nagel) and 4.5–10.0 (ref. 92120, Macherey-Nagel).

Hospital A: pH was assessed using pH-Fix indicator strips 0.0–6.0 (ref. 92115, Macherey-Nagel) and 4.5–10.0 (ref. 92120, Macherey-Nagel).

Hospital B: pH was measured using 2-zone Rota pH indicator strips (range 1–11, VWR, PA, USA) and MQuant® pH indicator strips (range 2.5–4.5, Merck, NJ, USA).

Hospital B: pH was measured using 2-zone Rota pH indicator strips (range 1–11, VWR, PA, USA) and MQuant® pH indicator strips (range 2.5–4.5, Merck, NJ, USA).

Radioactivity measurement and molar activity calculation

In both hospitals, final radioactivity was measured using a calibrated ionization chamber. The total volume of each preparation was visually assessed. Molar activity, expressed in MBq/nmol, was calculated by dividing the measured radioactivity of the preparation by the theoretical amount of PSMA-11 contained in the vial.

Radiochemical purity and identity

Thin-layer chromatography (TLC)

For RCP determination by TLC, a 4 µL sample of [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 was applied on iTLC-SG strips (Agilent, Folsom, USA), which were developed using a mobile phase consisting of aqueous ammonium acetate 1 M and methanol (1:1, v/v, pH 7) (VWR). The strips were then analyzed using a miniGITA Star radio-TLC scanner (Elysia-Raytest, Germany), and radioactivity distribution on plates was assessed with TLC Control v2.30 and GINA Star TLC™ v6.0 software. The retention factor (Rf) values were defined as:

68Ga non-complexed species: Rf = 0–0.2, expected: < 3%.

68Ga non-complexed species: Rf = 0–0.2, expected: < 3%.

[68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11: Rf = 0.8–1, expected: > 97%.

[68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11: Rf = 0.8–1, expected: > 97%.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

RCP and radiochemical identity were assessed via HPLC in accordance with Ph. Eur. 2.2.29 and monograph 04/2021:3044. The HPLC-grade mobile phases consisted of 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) in Milli-Q® water (Millipore, Thermo Scientific) (A) and 0.1% TFA in acetonitrile (B) (VWR) at a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min. Milli-Q® water was used for column rinsing and blank injections before analysis. The HPLC operated in gradient mode as follows: 0–5 min 5% B; 5–10 min 5%‒40% B; 10–11 min 40‒5% B; 11–16 min 5% B. Radiochemical identity was confirmed by comparing the retention time of [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11to the reference standard specified in the European pharmacopeia expected at 7 min 50 s ± 10%. A minimum RCP value of 95% was expected in HPLC.

Hospital A radio-HPLC analyses were performed on an Agilent 1260 Infinity system equipped with a diode array detector and a GABI radio detector (Elysia-Raytest). Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Phenomenex Luna® C18 column (150 × 3 mm, 100 Å, ref. 00 F-4251-Y0), with data acquisition and system control managed using GINA X software.

Hospital B radio-HPLC analyses were performed using a Nexera X3 apparatus (Shimadzu, Japan). The system included a solvent degasser (DGU-405), a solvent pump (LC40D), an autosampler (SIL-40) set at a 20 µL injection volume, a column oven (CTO-40 S) set at 30 °C, a UV detector (SPD-40, 190–700 nm) set at 254 and 280 nm, and a radioactivity detector (GABI Nova with a mid-energy probe and 2 × 5 µL flow cell). The stationary phase consisted of a 3.0 × 150 mm C18 ACE® Equivalence™ column with a 100 Å pore size and 3 μm particle size. The system was controlled and data were acquired using GINA X software.

Radionuclide identity and radionuclide purity

Half-life determination was performed at multiple time points using a Capintec® CRC25 dose calibrator to confirm radionuclide identity. Additionally, γ-spectrometric analysis was conducted with an AMG Hidex counter to detect peaks at 511, 1022, and 1077 keV, characteristic of 68Ga. 68Ge breakthrough was assessed more than 48 h after elution or preparation, using a 10-minute γ-count on the AMG Hidex counter with a full energy window (15‒1500 keV). 68Ge amounts were expected to remain below 0.001% in eluates and in each aliquot of [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11.

Sterility and endotoxin testing

Sterility testing followed Ph. Eur. 2.6.1 and monograph 0125, using direct inoculation into aerobic and anaerobic media. All testing was conducted in an ISO 5 laminar flow hood, shielded for radiation protection. Media included trypticase soy broth (TSB-T, ref. 42100, bioMérieux) for aerobic bacteria and fungi, and thioglycolate broth with resazurin (THIO-T, ref. 42074, bioMérieux) for anaerobic bacteria. Incubation was performed over 14 days: THIO-T tubes were incubated at 32 ° C in a Memmert oven (UNE200), whereas TSB-T tubes were maintained at room temperature (20–25 °C). Sterility was assessed visually by monitoring for turbidity in the growth medium.

Endotoxin levels were measured using the Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) test (Ph. Eur. 2.6.14) on a Charles River Endosafe® Nexgen-PTS system. The acceptance threshold was < 175 IU/mL.

Assessment of radiochemical yield (RCY) and radiocomplex stability

Three batches were produced for each preparation process to validate the radiolabeling protocol, ensuring all quality parameters met predefined acceptance criteria. Stability of the radiolabeling product was evaluated over a period of four hours following the end of synthesis (EoS, H0). RCP was evaluated at H1, H2, H3, and H4 by both TLC and HPLC. For each time point, the RCP of [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 was required to remain above 97%, as per SmPC and Ph. Eur. specifications for TLC, and 95% for HPLC.

Radiochemical yield was determined by calculating the percentage of [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 activity measured at EoS in relation to the total 68Ga activity available at the time of a blank elution (measured 24 h prior). This approach enabled standardized comparison of synthesis efficiency across batches and preparation methods.

Pharmaco-economic study

Cold kit vials fractionation is expected to significantly optimize production costs in radiopharmacy. Thus, a pharmaco-economic analysis was conducted over a one-year period to quantify the financial benefits of the kit fractionation approach. The primary objective was to evaluate the long-term economic impact of vial fractionation on patient management and scheduling efficiency in prostate cancer imaging. For that purpose, the impact of optimization methods on [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 production costs was investigated.

In Hospital A, the maximum number of patients per synthesis was estimated based on a fixed dose of 100 MBq per patient (average weight of 90 kg) and 20-minute intervals between injections. The number of patients per vial can be calculated based on available activity and subsequent estimation of cost per patient. This analysis considered all associated production costs, including generator expenses, radiopharmacist time, synthesis module tubing set, PSMA-11 cold kit vials, and quality control supplies. The costs associated with patient doses preparation (cassettes and cartridges for automated doses filling and dispensing, or manual syringes) were not included. The cost per patient was calculated by dividing the total cost of a synthesis by the number of patient doses prepared per complete Locametz® vial. Finally, a cost comparison was made between [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 and [18F]piflufolastat (an alternative radiotracer for PSMA PET imaging) to assess the economic competitiveness of 68Ga-based imaging, particularly in resource-constrained settings. This comparative analysis accounted for tracer production logistics, infrastructure requirements, and cost-per-patient over a standardized clinical workflow.

In Hospital B, the pharmaco-economic study was conducted by comparing conventional use of Locametz® with the aliquoting of cold kits combined with radiolabeling using a single generator eluate, which was implemented as a routine practice. A structural difference between the two centers is that Hospital B operates a single PET/CT system, which inherently limits daily patient throughput compared with Hospital A.

Dosimetric analysis of automated and manual fractionation techniques

To ensure optimal radiation protection for operators, radiation exposure was assessed both at the extremities and at the whole-body level. A comparative study was conducted at Hospital A to evaluate the dosimetric impact of manual versus automated (synthesis module-assisted) preparation techniques for [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11. Exposure measurements were performed using an electronic dosimeter (ED3 APVL) capable of real-time monitoring of the individual dose equivalent and dose rate. The dosimeter featured probes calibrated in Hp (0.07) for beta/gamma radiation and Hp (3) for gamma radiation of the eye lens.

The operator wore the dosimeters during six consecutive radiolabeling sessions. Radiation shielding included a 50 mm lead-equivalent 68Ga hot cell with a 50 mm lead-equivalent glass window, providing appropriate radiation protection. Additional radiation monitoring involved an electronic dosimeter (ED3) worn on the dominant hand, and for whole body exposure an active dosimeter (Dosilab® electronic personal dosimeter) on the chest of the operator. The automated fractionation method, which minimized manual handling of radioactive material, was expected to significantly reduce extremity doses and contribute to better compliance with radiation protection standards, especially in the context of high-activity generator elution and fractionation procedures.

Radiolabeling outcomes for each synthesis strategy

Simple elution of one generator for a single Locametz® vial

Using a reference preparation protocol which fully aligned with the SmPC recommendations, radiolabeling was performed successfully at both study sites. All quality controls met the expected specifications, and the RCP values reached > 99%, either in iTLC or HPLC. The pH values were 5‒6, ensuring that the process was both effective and compliant.

Double elution from two generators for a single Locametz® vial

Hospital A When attempting radiolabeling with two complete elutions from GalliaPharm® generators (totaling approximately 10 mL of 0.1 M HCl), the process did not yield satisfactory results. This failure is attributable to the limited buffering capacity of the Locametz® vial, which is optimized for a maximum elution volume of ~ 5 mL. The formulation could not adequately compensate for the excess acidity, leading to suboptimal labeling efficiency and a RCP of 9.45% (single assay).

In contrast, a modified approach combining automated elutions from a GalliAd® generator and a GalliaPharm® generator was tested, resulting in a total volume of 5.3 ± 0.2 mL. Under these adjusted conditions, the radiolabeling process was successful over the 3 assays, with mean RCP values of 99.81% ± 0.25% (iTLC) and 99.74% ± 0.25% (HPLC). All quality control parameters remained within the defined acceptance criteria, demonstrating the feasibility and robustness of this dual-generator protocol when carefully managed.

Hospital B During the preparation of three test batches, the successive and direct elution of two GalliAd® generators (total eluate volume = 2.2 mL) into a full Locametz® vial enabled the rapid and straightforward production of a radiolabeled compound with excellent RCP, as confirmed by both TLC (99.99% ± 0.01%) and HPLC (98.00% ± 0.20%). The pH of the reaction medium was consistently 5. Given that the native Locametz® cold kit is compatible with up to 5 mL of 0.1 M HCl when using a single GalliaPharm® generator, the introduction of only 2.2 mL of 0.1 M HCl from the combined elution of two GalliAd® generators remained within the buffering capacity of the single-vial kit.

Simple elution of one generator for aliquoted Locametz®

Hospital A For the six validation batches (3 vials of Locametz® aliquoted) prepared using a single generator elution in aliquoted kit vials, the RCP reached 99.77 ± 0.19% by HPLC and 99.85 ± 0.09% by iTLC and radiocomplex stability was > 98%.

These results demonstrated excellent labeling efficiency and compliance with specifications. This method was further validated during routine production, with 20 aliquoted Locametz® preparations (corresponding to 40 radiolabeled vials), showing a mean RCP of 99.42 ± 0.66% (iTLC) and a mean pH of 4.03 ± 0.27, confirming the robustness and reproducibility of the process. The RCY for the three test batches was 76.80 ± 5.44%, and after process optimization, routine RCY improved to 89.55 ± 5.49%.

Hospital B The division of a single Locametz® vial into two aliquots, each radiolabeled with an individual eluate from a GalliAd® generator at an interval of 4 to 6 h, resulted in the production of three batches, each comprising two preparations with high radiochemical purity (99.59% ± 0.35% by TLC and 98.75% ± 0.31% by HPLC). Notably, the mean RCP of the first radiolabeling reactions (99.45% ± 0.55% by TLC and 98.99% ± 0.27% by HPLC) did not differ from that of the subsequently labeled aliquots (99.72% ± 0.06% by TLC and 98.52% ± 0.22% by HPLC).

Double elution from two generators for aliquoted Locametz®

Hospital A In the three validation batches using dual generator elution in aliquoted kit vials, the RCP reached 99.93 ± 0.06% by HPLC and 99.74 ± 0.27% by iTLC, confirming again excellent labeling efficiency. In addition to these three test batches, 9 Locametz® preparations were performed in a routine work setting, showing a mean RCP of 98.80 ± 1.16% in TLC, a mean pH of 4.65 ± 0.63, and a final elution volume of 2.15 ± 0.19 mL.

The RCY for the three validation batches was 70.46 ± 2.64%, and 66.31 ± 6.78% in routine production, indicating slightly lower yields compared to the single-generator method, likely due to higher fractionated dual elution complexity.

Hospital B Although more straightforward than the protocol involving two GalliaPharm® generator eluates for the radiolabeling of aliquoted kits, the use of two GalliAd® generator eluates for aliquot-based radiolabeling yielded radiopharmaceuticals with quite variable RCP (95.84% ± 4.94% by TLC and 93.72% ± 4.39% by HPLC, n = 3 × 2 aliquots). Despite a mean pH of 3.9, which falls within the optimal range for efficient 68Ga radiolabeling (Breeman et al. 2005), significant amounts of free gallium were occasionally observed in the preparations. This was most likely attributable to the lower amounts of vector molecule available for effective complexation of the radiometal.

Pharmaco-economic study

The results of our pharmaco-economic study are summarized in Table 3. The implementation of an aliquot-based fractionation strategy using a single Locametz® cold kit generated savings of approximately 80 € per patient, reducing the radiopharmaceutical cost from 382.8 € to 299.8 € (representing a 21.7% cost reduction). In a nuclear medicine unit equipped with two PET/CT systems (Hospital A), this optimized approach allowed the management of up to 18 patients per day, compared with 8 patients using the reference SmPC full-vial preparation method. To reach this maximum capacity (100 MBq administered every 20 min), an initial activity of approximately 1700 MBq was required at the start of the production day. This activity level was achieved by combining elutions from two 68Ge/68Ga generators: one recently installed (< 4 weeks) and one older (> 33 weeks).

Compared with the SmPC method (single full vial, single generator < 4 weeks), the fractionation strategy reduced the total cost per patient from 382.8 € to 293.4 €, corresponding to a 23.4% cost reduction. Even when using a single generator (< 4 weeks), the optimized preparation strategy remained economically favorable (299.8 € per patient; -21.7% vs. reference). In contrast, the cost per patient for the 18F-labeled equivalent ([18F]DCFPyL) reached 410 €, representing a 7.1% increase compared with the SmPC reference and a higher cost than fractionation-based strategies.

While, in theory, it is possible to combine two generators throughout their overlapping life cycles (i.e., using a newly delivered generator in conjunction with an older one until its expiration), this strategy is not always cost-effective. When the activity gain between a single-generator elution and a dual-generator elution is less than 100 MBq (approximately one patient dose), pooling provides little practical benefit. Furthermore, combining two GalliaPharm® generators introduces technical complexities, particularly regarding elution volume and acidity, requiring meticulous process adjustment and validation, which may outweigh the marginal gain in recovered activity. For instance, replacing a generator at week 34 allows for its continued use alongside the new generator until approximately week 57, offering a 23-week overlap. Replacing at week 26 would extends this period by an additional 11 weeks. These observations suggest that a 6-month replacement interval offers an optimal balance between cost efficiency and clinical throughput, especially in high-demand nuclear medicine departments. Importantly, the present analysis shows that the main factor of cost efficiency is kit fractionation rather than generator pooling. By dividing a single vial into two aliquots, the number of patients treated per vial increases, thereby spreading fixed preparation costs across more administrations. This economic advantage is maintained even when using a single aged generator (> 33 weeks), as the aliquot-based strategy enables a number of patients per vial comparable to that achieved with the SmPC full-vial method using a recently installed generator.

In Hospital B, considering the initial cost of the generator and its amortization over its useful lifetime based on the number of elutions, the cost per elution was estimated at 475 €. The cost of the kit was 1500 €, while the minor consumables required for radiolabeling (tubing, needles, adapters) were considered negligible. In both scenarios, three patient doses were prepared per batch throughout the generator’s lifetime. Without aliquoting, the cost per patient dose was 658 €; with aliquoting, it decreased to 408 €, representing a 38% cost reduction. Although the absolute cost per patient remained higher than that observed in Hospital A, the aliquoting strategy still generated economic savings. This difference is mainly due to structural factors, including lower patient throughput due to the presence of a single PET/CT system of a different generation, resulting in higher dosages (2 MBq/kg), which translates into higher average activities administered per patient. These factors limit the dilution of fixed preparation costs; nevertheless, kit aliquoting maintained a clear economic advantage.

Whole-body exposure remained negligible (0 µSv) for both methods, confirming effective shielding for whole body radiation. Despite slightly higher activity handled during manual synthesis (627 ± 5.57 MBq vs. 526.3 ± 13.05 MBq), the reduction in extremity doses with automation is important, highlighting the significant radioprotection benefit provided by automated systems.

Discussion

Gallium-68 radiolabeling offers significant advantages, facilitating the clinical implementation of innovative PET imaging agents, including ligands for somatostatin receptors, PSMA, fibroblast activation protein (FAP) or CXCR4. Initially, the development of such experimental radiopharmaceuticals relied on complex radiolabeling protocols, often involving manual synthesis or time-consuming automated setups. These methods required extended preparation times for cassette installation and synthesis, thereby limiting routine clinical use. To facilitate broader clinical application, cold kit-based radiolabeling has emerged as a simpler and faster alternative (Lepareur 2022). This strategy reduces preparation time by combining the vector molecule to radiolabel, a buffer agent and any other ingredients required for the reaction into one or two vials, enabling single-step labeling. With PSMA-11, this procedure is particularly efficient and only involves room temperature incubation, due to the favorable complexation kinetics of its acyclic chelating moiety (Golan et al. 2020). Regulatory approvals of these kits (e.g., Locametz®) allowed widespread clinical use (Lenzo et al.2018).

However, a major limitation of cold kits remains the restricted patient throughput per vial. To address this, Van Brandwijk et al. demonstrated that combining two GalliAd® generators within an automated synthesis process increased the available activity beyond the SmPC limit (1369 MBq), achieving up to 2035 MBq when using one generator aged 3 weeks and another aged 28 weeks (Brandwijk et al. 2024). Building upon this, our study optimized multiple protocols, including generator combinations and vial aliquoting methods, to maximize the number of patients managed per synthesis, regardless of the age or type of generator.

From a chemical standpoint, Ga(III) is a hard Lewis acid and forms stable complexes preferentially with oxygen or nitrogen-containing ligands (Velikyan 2011). Controlling the pH of the reaction medium is critical, as Ga(OH)3 precipitates readily within the pH 3–7 range. In our study, even when reconstituting the Locametz® vial with 1 mL of WFI, no significant colloid formation was observed, and RCP remained comparable to standard preparations. This robustness can be attributed to the presence of concentrated acetate buffer (573.2 µmol; 212 µmol/mL) in the formulation.

PSMA-11, which bears a linear HBED chelating moiety, forms stable complexes with Ga3+ at room temperature. However, complexation may induce conformational rearrangements and stereoisomers formation, which can be minimized by heating (Tsionou et al. 2017). Despite this, the Locametz® SmPC protocol does not require heating or sterile filtration of the 68GaCl3 eluate, simplifying and shortening the preparation time. In our procedure, the total synthesis time, including both generator elution and radiolabeling, averaged 8 min. This duration is notably shorter than those reported in earlier studies, which typically include an additional sterile filtration step of the eluate (Brandwijk et al. 2024, Wang et al. 2024). Even with the fractionation of GalliaPharm® generator, the process was only extended by 3 min, primarily due to manual weighing steps. Moreover, the use of a cassette-based system with the GalliaPharm® generator significantly improved process reproducibility and reduced operator exposure, although it added approximately 10 min for setup, prior to the radiolabeling phase.

The Locametz® cold kit contains a fixed amount of acetate buffer that requires strict control of the 0.1 M HCl elution volume to maintain optimal pH and molarity during radiolabeling. Based on theoretical calculations, the maximum elutable volume that maintains pH ≥ 3.2 was estimated at 5.58 mL per vial. This value was experimentally validated using GalliaPharm® and GalliAd® generators in combination, where 5.3 ± 0.2 mL of 0.1 M HCl produced radiolabeled compounds that met all SmPC quality control criteria.

Experiments using 0.1 M HCl volumes exceeding 2.4 mL per aliquot resulted in pH levels below 3.2, leading to suboptimal radiolabeling conditions. Indeed, during aliquoting of the Locametz® vial, part of the buffer and PSMA-11 is lost within the syringe and needle during transfer. Gentisic acid, present in minimal concentrations (6.49 µmol), had negligible impact on acidification. Consequently, a 2.4 mL HCl limit per aliquot was established for automated elution of GalliaPharm® generators.

Fractionated elution proved critical for achieving high activity concentrations and consistent RCY. With a single GalliaPharm® generator, optimized fractionation led to a stable RCY of ~ 89%. However, combining fractions from two GalliaPharm® generators required a more complex protocol, resulting in lower reproducibility and a mean RCY of ~ 66%. This was attributed to challenges in controlling HCl amounts during dual fractionation, with regard to buffer capacity. Conversely, GalliAd® generators, with their inherently low elution volumes, allowed for direct use without elution fractionation, improving ease of use and radiolabeling robustness.

Although cold kits are typically designed for manual radiolabeling, fractionated elution required the use of synthesis modules to ensure precise volume handling and reproducibility (Durieux et al. 2023). These automated procedures also significantly improved radiation safety, reducing extremity dose exposure by a factor of 5.8 compared to manual procedures. This finding is consistent with the results of Van Brandwijk et al. (Brandwijk et al. 2024) and Kleynhans et al. (Kleynhans et al. 2020)., who similarly reported lower operator exposure following the transition to automated synthesis. Manual fractionation remains a feasible alternative in settings with limited access to automation.

In Hospital A, in order to preserve radiolabeling performance, rigorous double verification procedures were implemented. These included confirming the generator’s inlet and outlet connections prior to synthesis to prevent reversal errors. Elution volumes were pre-verified the day before use, and each aliquot was weighed post-transfer to confirm the correct distribution of the PSMA-11 peptide and acetate buffer. While a semi-automated approach using a syringe pump on the HCl inlet could theoretically reduce both extremity dose and consumable costs, it posed practical challenges. These included the need to place the syringe pump outside the Class A shielding and difficulties controlling parallel or sequential elution of the two generators. Additionally, manual switching of flow paths inside the shielded area would still be required. Other synthesis modules, such as the miniAIO® (Trasis, Belgium), equipped with dual syringe pumps, offer promising solutions for simultaneous generator elution and process standardization.

The 68Ge breakthrough in 68Ga eluates consistently remained below 0.001%, even after 11 months of generator use and under dual-elution conditions. However, when two generators were used simultaneously, a cumulative increase in 68Ge activity was observed, and with the generator lifespan now extended to 18 months (Sudbrock et al. 2014), this effect could also be observed over time. Additionally, breakthrough levels tend to increase when elution intervals exceed 48 h, emphasizing the importance of daily elution to minimize 68Ge accumulation, as previously reported (Lin et al. 2012).

RCP and stability over time of [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 complied with the specifications of the European Pharmacopoeia across all three validation batches for each method tested, except in Hospital B, as RCP reached only 93% with 2 generators and 2 aliquots of one Locametz® vial. Routine quality control, including radionuclide identification, TLC, sterility, and endotoxin testing, confirmed the overall preparation reliability and regulatory compliance (Gillings et al. 2021, Todde et al. 2017). The molar activity remained high (> 18.64 MBq/nmol) (Luurtsema et al. 2021), the excess amount of PSMA (26.4 nmol) and the low amounts of 68Ga3+ (remaining well below 20 pmol per elution) ensuring adequate chelation.

Nevertheless, synthesis activity levels reached up to 1750 MBq, confirming that Locametz® cold kits can accommodate such levels without compromising radiochemical integrity. All procedures adhered to Good Preparation Practices and complied with relevant sterility and quality standards.

To minimize metallic contamination, type 1 glass TC-ELU-5® vials were used, with careful positioning of the air vent needle to prevent interaction with the radiolabeled solution. Despite using non-coated stoppers, no negative impact on product quality was observed, likely due to the short contact time between the acidic eluate and stopper surface, consistent with previous findings (Wang et al. 2024).

A total of 74 radiopharmaceutical syntheses were completed at Hospital A, corresponding to 37 Locametz® vials and an average of 11.47 patients per cold kit. At Hospital B, a lower throughput (6 patients per kit) was observed due to the use of a single PET/CT camera and one 68Ge/68Ga generator. Importantly, no significant differences in quality control parameters or clinical imaging outcomes were noted between the standard and optimized preparation methods.

From a pharmaco-economic standpoint, the optimized preparation protocol reduced costs by 21.7% in Hospital A, compared to the standard Locametz® SmPC procedure. Implementing a dual-generator strategy combining one generator aged < 4 weeks with another aged > 33 weeks achieved up to 23% cost savings. Further savings could theoretically be achieved through in-house cold kit fractionation under GMP conditions. However, current French regulations prohibit in-house fractionation when the manufacturer holds a marketing authorization, leading to a cost discrepancy with reports such as Kleynhans et al., who reported €10 per synthesis (Kleynhans et al. 2020). Nevertheless, the proposed protocol remains a cost-effective alternative for high-demand and budget-constrained centers, especially during generator or kit shortages. This approach is increasingly relevant given the recent extension of GalliaPharm® shelf life to 18 months, provided that ⁶⁸Ge breakthrough remains ≤ 0.001%.

The vial-splitting approach described in this study is not included in the SmPC. The same goes for the use of a high ⁶⁸Ga activity involving two or more generators and fractionated elution(Nelson et al. 2022, Rodnick et al. 2022). Such an approach should therefore be considered an off-label practice, inspired by fractionated cold kits radiolabeled with 99mTc (Baker 1999, Penglis and Tsopelas 2000, Thokchom et al. 2022, Decristoforo and Riccabona 1996, Dyck et al. 1996). Nevertheless, in the context of hospital radiopharmacy, such procedures may be implemented under institutional medical and pharmaceutical responsibility, provided that appropriate traceability, pharmaceutical validation, and risk assessment are ensured. In the present work, all preparations and administrations were fully documented in batch records and patient files. Moreover, several reports suggest that minor deviations from RCP acceptance limits are unlikely to significantly affect clinical safety (Larenkov and Maruk AYa, Kodina 2018, Vis et al. 2015, Vanney et al. 2026).

While this study provides valuable insights into the cost-effectiveness of the proposed protocol in high-demand centers, several limitations should be considered. The study was conducted at only two centers, which limits the generalizability of the results to other institutions with different resources and clinical practices. The analysis focused solely on the preparation costs, excluding other factors such as infrastructure and equipment maintenance, which could influence the overall economic impact. However, this study presents a practical solution for centers already producing gallium-based preparations, with the goal of optimizing production capacity. By aliquoting cold kits, available resources could be used more efficiently, helping centers accommodate high demand while avoiding substantial additional costs.

Conclusions

Given the growing clinical demand for PSMA PET imaging, the fractionation of cold kits and optimized use of 68Ge/68Ga generators represent robust, cost-effective strategies to enhance radiopharmaceutical production efficiency. These approaches enable centers to meet high patient throughput requirements while mitigating costs and ensuring continuity of care, particularly important as the broader implementation of 18F-labeled PSMA radiotracers remains limited by accessibility and pricing constraints.

The proposed protocols could be readily adapted to other vector molecules formulated in lyophilized cold kits, thereby expanding their applicability to other radiotracers beyond PSMA-11. Moreover, further studies evaluating the stability of reconstituted aliquots beyond 5 h would be beneficial for centers with lower daily patient volumes, allowing for broader adoption without compromising quality or safety. Nonetheless, rigorous generator management is crucial for consistent performance, including regular monitoring of elution profiles and daily elution to limit 68Ge breakthrough.

Overall, this work provides practical insights for optimizing costs in nuclear medicine, especially regarding the current economic positioning of fluorinated PSMA agents versus gallium-based preparations in resource-constrained settings.
