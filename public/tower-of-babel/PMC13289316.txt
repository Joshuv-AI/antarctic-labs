Role of thromboelastography in risk stratifying patients with traumatic brain injury on prehospital antiplatelet agents
Authors: Tracy Okonya, Snigdha Gulati, Emily A Reeson, John K Mailolo, Clay A Rahaman, Nicole V Warrington, Nicholas G Matthees, Trevor M Low, Kristina M Kupanoff, Jordan A Weinberg, James N Bogert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe Brain Injury Guidelines (BIG) offer valuable criteria for identifying trauma patients requiring observation without immediate intervention. One variable evaluated in BIG is prehospital aspirin (ASA) or clopidogrel use. These patients are classified as BIG3 requiring repeat head CT and neurosurgery consultation. This study aims to investigate the rate of hemorrhage progression and neurosurgery intervention (NSI) in patients fulfilling all BIG1 criteria except prehospital ASA or clopidogrel use and how thromboelastography (TEG) with platelet mapping (PM) could be used to identify patients whose platelets remain uninhibited by prehospital antiplatelet therapy, suggesting safe BIG 1 management.MethodsRetrospective review of data at a Level I trauma center between 2017 and 2022 focused on patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI) who reported prehospital ASA or clopidogrel use. Patients were categorized BIG 1, 2, or 3 based on initial CT, Glasgow Coma Scale admission, intoxication, skull fracture type, and presence/size of intracranial hemorrhages. Impact of antiplatelet agents on platelet function was analyzed using TEG with PM.ResultsWe identified 236 blunt TBIs on prehospital ASA or clopidogrel. BIG scores, excluding antiplatelet medications, resulted in BIG1 (24.6%), BIG2 (19.5%), and BIG3 (55.9%) classifications. 32% of patients on prehospital clopidogrel showed ADP inhibition greater than 60%, whereas 61% of those on prehospital ASA had arachidonic acid inhibition greater than 60%. Of the 58 cases fulfilling BIG1 criteria in all parameters except prehospital ASA or clopidogrel use, all but one patient demonstrated a stable repeat head CT, and no NSI was required.DiscussionPrehospital ASA or clopidogrel use did not impact hemorrhage progression or need for NSI in patients with blunt TBI otherwise meeting BIG1 criteria. TEG with PM provided valuable insights into platelet inhibition. Findings indicate the benefits of tailoring management strategies for trauma patients on antiplatelet therapy to optimize resource allocation and improve care.Level of evidenceIV therapeutic/care management.

Introduction

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, traumatic brain injury (TBI) accounted for approximately 214 000 hospitalizations and 69 000 deaths in the USA in 2020.1 In addition, TBI imposes a large economic burden with an estimated healthcare cost of US$327 billion in the USA in 2019.2 To ease the economic burden of TBI, the Brain Injury Guidelines (BIG), originally published in 2014, were developed to improve resource utilization.3 BIG offers a standardized algorithm for the classification and management of patients with mild TBI based on history, physical examination, and radiographic findings. Some of the key metrics include size and location of hemorrhage, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), intoxication, presence of skull fracture, and use of prehospital antiplatelets. These metrics are used to categorize patients with TBI into three BIG categories that correspond with a therapeutic plan. BIG1 does not indicate hospitalization, repeat head CT, or neurosurgery consultation. BIG2 indicates hospitalization but no repeat head CT or neurosurgery consult. BIG3 indicates hospitalization, repeat head CT, and neurosurgery consultation. The full guidelines are outlined in table 1.

BIG was later modified to clarify specific aspects of the original guidelines that were vague, thereby improving patient safety and the generalizability of BIG. The modified BIG (mBIG) also includes the addition of direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) medication use and reclassification of all epidural hemorrhages as class 3 (mBIG3), which were eight times more likely to progress on CT and place patients at greater risk.4 Overall, the BIG and mBIG criteria are effective, validated measurements that can improve TBI-associated resource utilization and reduce healthcare costs. One limitation of these studies however, is the broad categorization of any anticoagulant/antiplatelet use as class 3 (BIG3 or mBIG3) regardless of other presenting variables.

From 2011 to 2012, one-third of US adults 40 years and older reported taking preventive aspirin (ASA) and/or other antiplatelet medications.5 However, research on the specific management of patients with mild TBI taking antiplatelet medication is limited. Thromboelastography (TEG) is a diagnostic modality that uses whole blood to quantitatively assess properties of clot formation. TEG with platelet mapping (TEG-PM) can be used to assess the inhibitory effect of antiplatelet agents including ASA and clopidogrel.6 The use of TEG to analyze platelet function and identify platelet inhibition percentage may be one means to improve the efficiency and cost reduction of BIG criteria.

TEG is a whole blood hemostatic assay that has gained popularity in the evaluation of trauma patients in recent years.7 However, TEG alone lacks the ability to detect receptor-specific inhibition of platelet function.8 TEG-PM was introduced to address this weakness. TEG-PM measures platelet aggregation (maximum amplitude—MA) from arachidonic acid (AA) or ADP stimulation of the platelets and compares this to platelet aggregation observed with thrombin stimulation from the activated coagulation cascade. Results are reported as % inhibition of a specific activation pathway’s (ADP or AA) ability to cause platelet aggregation. For example, ADP % inhibition is defined as 1− ((ADP MA—Fibrin MA)/(Thrombin MA—Fibrin MA)). ADP % inhibition is used to measure the impact P2Y12 inhibitors have on platelet function. AA % inhibition is used to measure the impact ASA has on platelet function.

The purpose of this study was to investigate the rate of hemorrhage progression and need for neurosurgery intervention (NSI) in patients fulfilling all mBIG1 criteria except prehospital ASA or clopidogrel use. Additionally, we explored the potential of TEG-PM to identify patients whose platelets remain uninhibited by prehospital antiplatelet therapy, suggesting these patients could be managed safely as mBIG1 patients. We hypothesized that patients on prehospital antiplatelet agents without significant platelet inhibition identified on TEG-PM would have no greater risk of hemorrhage expansion than mBIG1 patients not taking prehospital antiplatelet agents.

Methodology

Our center is a 500+ bed level 1 trauma center including a large neurosurgery center with a 64-bed neurointensive care unit. TEG-PM is obtained on arrival to our trauma room for all patients meeting our criteria for highest level trauma activation and for all patients with suspicion of a TBI regardless of activation level. Our trauma registry was queried for patients admitted between December 2017 and March 2022 with head and neck body part region injury severity scores ≥2 who reported taking prehospital ASA or clopidogrel and underwent a non-contrast CT of the head. Patients who expired in the trauma bay or patients without a confirmed head injury of subdural hematoma (SDH), subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), intraparenchymal hemorrhage (IPH), epidural hematoma (EDH), or intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) were excluded. Patient demographics, injury characteristics, treatments, CT, TEG-PM results, imaging, and outcomes were collected. Patients were categorized as mBIG 1, 2, or 3 based on the scoring criteria (table 1) without consideration of the prehospital clopidogrel or ASA criteria. Patients who were classified as a mBIG3 solely due to prehospital clopidogrel/ASA were reclassified as a mBIG1 if they met all other mBIG1 criteria aside from prehospital antiplatelet use. The original BIG criteria were later modified.4 For EDH and in areas where there was ambiguity on the original BIG criteria, we followed the mBIG criteria (table 2). We used the mBIG definition for SAH and SDH, as the original BIG did not clearly define these hemorrhages.

For analysis purposes, age was categorized into <65 years, 65–74 years, and ≥75 years and GCS score at 3–8, 9–12, and 13–15. These classifications for age were applied because of worse TBI-related outcomes in patients ≥65 and again ≥75 years of age.9 GCS classifications are standard classifications representing mild, moderate, and severe TBI.10 46% of TEGs were completed using version 6s and 54% using version 5000. AA and ADP percent inhibition were stratified at 30% and 60% based on previous literature suggesting increased risk of bleeding.1113

TEG results, hemorrhage expansion, and neurosurgical intervention were documented via chart review. All patients with intracranial hemorrhage received a follow-up CT of the head within 6 hours regardless of BIG designation. Hemorrhage was considered to be expanded if the final neuroradiologist read specified expansion. Evolution, and redistribution of hemorrhage on the final neuroradiologist read were not considered expansion. Neurosurgical intervention was defined as any neurosurgical procedure. This included craniotomy, craniectomy, intracranial pressure monitor placement, external ventricular drain placement, or middle meningeal artery embolization at any time point during hospitalization.

In an effort to avoid information bias, our team reviewed multiple records together to ensure chart review was done in a consistent manner. AA inhibition was measured for patients taking prehospital ASA and ADP inhibition was measured for patients taking prehospital clopidogrel. Percent inhibition was categorized into <30%, 30–59%, 60%+. Counts with percentages were used to describe patient demographics, injury characteristics, inhibition and BIG status. χ² analyses were done to assess for the association between BIG and inhibition. IBM SPSS V.27 was used for analysis. This project was approved by our Institutional Review Board.

Results

All patients

236 patients met study inclusion criteria. 64% of the sample was male and 76.3% White (table 3). Patient age ranged from 17 to 103 years with a median age of 76 years (70 -83). The mechanism of injury was a fall for 197 (83.5%) patients followed by 22 (9.3%) patients in motor vehicle accidents 233 (98.7%) were admitted to the intensive care unit. Admission GCS score was less than 13 for 8.1% of the sample. All patients reported taking prehospital antiplatelets: ASA 65.7%, clopidogrel 13.6%, both ASA and clopidogrel 20.8%, and as a result no patient in our study would be classified as mBIG1. 157 (66.5%) patients had a bleed in a single area, 93 SDH, 59 SAH, 3 IVH, and 2 IPH. Discussion

The findings of this study suggest that the prehospital use of antiplatelet agents, specifically ASA and clopidogrel, does not significantly impact hemorrhage progression or the necessity for NSI in patients with TBI who meet all mBIG1 criteria except for the use of prehospital antiplatelet agents. These findings are particularly relevant considering the increasing prevalence of antiplatelet use among older adults, with approximately one-third of adults ≥40 years old in the USA reporting use of preventive ASA and/or antiplatelet therapy.5 With the rapidly increasing older population in the USA, there is a growing need to optimize trauma management and resource utilization for patients taking antiplatelet medications.

In both the original BIG study and mBIG, there was no hemorrhage progression or need for NSI in BIG1 patients.3 4 Similarly in our study, among patients on prehospital antiplatelet agents but otherwise fulfilling mBIG1 criteria, only one patient showed hemorrhage progression on repeat head CT scans, and none of the patients in this subgroup required NSI. This reinforces the potential safety of managing patients with TBI on antiplatelet therapy without the need for aggressive interventions typically indicated by mBIG3 classification, especially if the patient does not have significant platelet inhibition. The one patient with hemorrhage expansion had a measurable platelet inhibition that was >60% on TEG-PM. This is consistent with previous literature which has shown that platelet inhibition >60% is associated with a higher mortality rate in patients with TBI.14 However, to our knowledge, no previous data have shown an association between TEG-PM measured platelet inhibition and hemorrhage expansion, so the significance of AA inhibition >60% in our one patient with hemorrhage expansion is unknown.

In addition, a recent study by Billings et al assessing the risk of clinical progression of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) in patients taking antiplatelet agents who would otherwise have been classified as mBIG 1 or 2 found that the use of antiplatelet medications did not have an increased risk of clinical worsening on examination, require NSI, or have increased risk of mortality.15 The results of the Billings et al study contributed to the exclusion of ASA from mBIG. Our results along with previous literature indicating that there is no significant increase in risk of ICH expansion in patients taking antiplatelets may support the idea of excluding both clopidogrel in addition to ASA from mBIG scoring, without the need to take TEG-PM results into account for mBIG 1 patients.16

Results on outcomes in patients taking preinjury clopidogrel and/or ASA are inconsistent.17 Several studies have reported increased risk of negative outcomes in patients with TBI taking antiplatelet agents. Mathieu et al reported a meta-analysis that associated dual antiplatelet therapy with increased risk of ICH progression.18 Scotti et al reported similar findings in an elderly cohort.16 Whereas, Harland et al reported no increased risk of hemorrhage expansion in patients taking antiplatelet agents.19 However, none of these studies stratified patients based on hemorrhage size and type like done with mBIG scoring. When hemorrhage phenotype is scored using mBIG criteria, the risk of hemorrhage expansion or need for neurosurgical intervention appears vanishingly small (Billings 2020).15 Our data seem to corroborate the low risk of hemorrhage progression in patients on antiplatelet agents who otherwise would be scored as mBIG 1.

The variation in outcomes in patients with TBI taking preinjury antiplatelet agents may be contributed to by the idea that patients taking antiplatelet medications may have varying degrees of inhibition. In recent years, the idea of antiplatelet resistance or non-responders has been thoroughly discussed in the scientific community. Some studies define the normal response to clopidogrel as ≥40% inhibition of ADP induced platelet aggregation.20 21 Whereas ASA resistance has been defined as ≥50% AA induced aggregation on TEG.22 Nearly a quarter of patients in our study showed platelet inhibition less than 30% through their AA pathway, and nearly 40% of patients had platelet inhibition less than 30%, indicating that many patients on antiplatelet medication may have suboptimal platelet inhibition. The definition of resistance depends on the test used, and medication non-compliance may also play a role. However, this data suggests there is limited value of TEG-PM in providing insight into appropriate risk stratification of patients with TBI meeting imaging criteria for BIG 1. This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design and the relatively small sample size, which is further limited when examining BIG classifications for patients who were administered TEGs. These sample limitations combined with the fact that our findings are specific to a single Level I trauma center, may limit the generalizability of the results. Future prospective studies with larger, more diverse populations are needed to validate these findings and refine the criteria for managing patients with TBI on antiplatelet therapy. ASA dosing is another weakness of this study. We do not know the ASA dose of the single patient in our study who showed hemorrhage progression. Regardless of the dosing, this patient’s platelet activity was impacted by the ASA dosing showing AA inhibition of >60% on TEG-PM. Previous studies do not separate ASA by dosing strategies such as 81 mg versus 325 mg daily so the impact of dosing remains unknown.1519 Further research, specifically using TEG-PM or other diagnostic tools looking at the degree of platelet inhibition is required to draw stronger conclusions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study suggests that prehospital ASA or clopidogrel use does not significantly increase the risk of hemorrhage progression or the need for NSI in patients with TBI otherwise meeting mBIG1 criteria. TEG-PM is unlikely to be a valuable tool for assessing platelet inhibition and helping tailor management strategies for these patients.
