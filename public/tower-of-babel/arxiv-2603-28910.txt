Input-to-State Stability of Gradient Flows in Distributional Space
Guillem Pascual, Sonia Martínez

Abstract
This paper proposes a new notion of distributional Input-to-State Stability (dISS) for dynamic systems evolving in probability spaces over a domain. Unlike other norm-based ISS concepts, we rely on the Wasserstein metric, which captures more precisely the effects of the disturbances on atomic and non-atomic measures. We show how dISS unifies both ISS and Noise to State Stability (NSS) over compact domains for particle dynamics, while extending the classical notions to sets of probability distributions. We then apply the dISS framework to study the robustness of various Wasserstein gradient flows with respect to perturbations. In particular, we establish dISS for gradient flows defined by a class of l l -smooth functionals subject to bounded disturbances, such as those induced by entropy in optimal transport. Further, we study the dISS robustness of the large-scale algorithms when using Kernel and sample-based approximations. This results into a characterization of the error incurred when using a finite number of agents, which can guide the selection of the swarm size to achieve a mean-field objective with prescribed accuracy and stability guarantees.



I
Introduction

Recent advances in artificial intelligence have renewed the interest
in the practical deployment of very large robotic swarms. Made of low-cost simple robots, swarms are highly adaptive while
exhibiting remarkable resilience. Yet, their coordinated control remains complex, as is affected by
multiple
communication and sensor failures, enviromental events, and
uncertainty. Thus, analyzing the impact of the effect of disturbances
becomes paramount to ensuring swarm safety and graceful stable
degradation. Particularly motivated by this, we seek to develop and
analyze a bounded input-disturbance-to-state stability notion that is
applicable to general probability measures. This question is also relevant in stochastic control and machine
learning applications, such as generative learning, which rely on
algorithms that evolve in spaces of probabilities.

Introduced in
[
25
]
for deterministic dynamical systems in
finite-dimensional vector spaces, Input-to-State Stability (ISS) has
been extended to multiple domains. For stochastic
systems, NSS
[
9
,
6
]
, and its
p
-th moment generalization
(
p
thNSS)
[
20
]
, alongside
p
-th moment ISS
[
16
]

serve as a probabilistic counterpart of ISS for
continuous SDEs; while probabilistic ISS
(pISS)
[
7
]
applies to discrete-time systems. ISS
has also been extended to the infinite-dimensional
realm
[
8
]
, with applications to PDE
control
[
17
]
and large-scale swarm
control
[
33
,
32
]
. In this case, the
distribution of agents is viewed as an element of an
L_{p}
space. A
drawback of this approach is that it does not capture the geometry of
measures (
L^{p}
just computes “vertical distances” while particles
move in the domain), and fails to assess the representation of finite
swarms by discrete measures.

In recent years, the theory of Optimal Transport (OT) has been a
fast-growing subject in applications that involve analysis in
probability spaces. Originally posed as an optimization problem, under
certain conditions
[
27
,
21
]
OT provides a geometric
characterization of a probability space as a Riemannian manifold. A
class of functional optimization problems over this space leads to the
definition of Wasserstein gradient flows
[
1
]
, which are
linked to classical continuity
equations. This geometric interpretation of OT has been used for (mean-field)
control in different scenarios, such as stochastic linear system
steering
[
4
]
, Model Predictive
Control
[
10
]
, coverage control
[
18
]
, and safe
swarm control via Banach Barrier
functions
[
14
]
.

In addition, the OT geometry allows for the generalization of notions
of convexity, gradient dominance, and smoothness to functionals over
probability spaces, which are fundamental in optimization. Thus,
these tools are widely used in Machine Learning, for instance to
steer samples of a certain distribution to those of a
target
[
30
]
. In this field, the analysis of algorithm
perturbations is done by computing convergence
ratios
[
19
]
or asymptotic
bounds
[
29
]
. Instead, here we aim to provide a
control-theoretic, stability perspective as in
[
26
]
, for
finite-dimensional gradient flows, by accounting for the Wasserstein
geometry.

Statement of Contributions:
In this manuscript, we introduce
the notion of distributional Input-to-State Stability (dISS) for
continuous-time gradient Wasserstein flows, establish associated
Lyapunov characterizations, and prove its applicability for a class of

l
-smooth functionals. We show how dISS unifies and strengthens the
notions of ISS for deterministic systems, and of NSS for stochastic
systems. Then, we demonstrate its applicability in the context of swarm
control, specifically for swarms subject to entropic disturbances, and
approximated dynamics based on agent-density approximations (via KDEs
and discrete-measures). This results into a characterization of the
mean-field objective error that is incurred when using a finite number
of agents. Finally, we validate our results through simulations.

IV-B

Relationship of dISS with microscopic ISS and NSS

Here, we establish the relationship of dISS with single-agent notions
of ISS for deterministic systems
[
25
]
and NSS for stochastic
systems
[
6
]
. In
d
, (N-)ISS characterizes
the stability towards a certain set
M\subset\real^{d}
.
Thus, in order to bridge this gap, we will limit dISS to target distributions that are supported on that
set,

\mathfrak{P}*=\mathcal{P}_{M}\equiv\mathcal{P}_{2}(M)\coloneqq\{\rho\;|\;\text{supp}(\rho)\subset M\}.

This target set reduces dISS to an ISS notion on the expected value of
the state. To see this, we use the Monge
formulation (
1
) of the distance to the set
\mathcal{P}_{M}
:

W_{2}^{2}(\rho,\mathcal{P}_{M})=\inf_{T(x)\in M}\int_{\Omega}|x-T(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho.

For this problem, the optimal transport map exists and
is given by
T(x)=\arg\min_{y\in M}\|x-y\|^{2}
, which yields,

\small W_{2}^{2}(\rho,\mathcal{P}_{M})=\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{dist}^{2}(x,M)\mathrm{d}\rho=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}[\operatorname{dist}^{2}(x,M)].

(9)

When the state is a Dirac delta,
\rho=\delta_{x}
, the Wasserstein
distance to the set
\mathcal{P}_{M}
reduces to

W_{2}(\delta_{x},\mathcal{P}_{M})=\operatorname{dist}(x,M)
, allowing us to draw a direct
connection between dISS and classical ISS for deterministic systems.

Proposition IV.5

Given a set
\Omega\subset\real^{d}
and a compact

M\subset\Omega
, the microscopic
dynamics (
4

M

if the density flow in (

respect to
\mathcal{P}_{M}
. Furthermore, the converse also holds whenever the

\beta\in\mathcal{KL}
function is linear in the domain variable.

Proof:

(
\text{dISS}\implies\text{ISS}
):
Take
as initial measure a Dirac delta centered
at the initial particle condition,
\rho_{0}=\delta_{x_{0}}
. The
evolution of this distribution is
\rho_{t}=\delta_{x_{t}}
, where

x_{t}
is the particle flow. The dISS condition guarantees:

W_{2}(\delta_{x_{t}},\mathcal{P}_{M})\leq\beta(W_{2}(\delta_{x_{0}},\mathcal{P}_{M}),t)+\gamma(\|u\|_{c}).

Using the identity
W_{2}(\delta_{x},\mathcal{P}_{M})=\operatorname{dist}(x,M)

we recover the particle ISS formulation:

\operatorname{dist}(x_{t},M)\leq\beta(\operatorname{dist}(x_{0},M),t)+\gamma(\|u\|_{c}).

(
\text{ISS}\implies\text{dISS}
):
The converse relies on
the definition of the Wasserstein distance to the set
\mathcal{P}_{M}
. Using
the change of variables formula (push-forward) for the flow map of
the particle dynamic system,
\Phi_{t}(x)

W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{t},\mathcal{P}_{M})=\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{dist}^{2}(x,M)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}=\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{dist}^{2}(\Phi_{t}(x),M)\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}.

Since
\operatorname{dist}^{2}(\cdot,M)
is a size function,
the ISS property implies the existence

and
\gamma\in\mathcal{K}
such that:

\operatorname{dist}^{2}(\Phi_{t}(x),M)\leq\beta(\operatorname{dist}^{2}(x,M),t)+\gamma(\|u\|_{c}).

Plugging this bound directly into the integral:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{dist}^{2}(\Phi_{t}(x),M)\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}\leq\int_{\Omega}\Big(

\displaystyle\beta(\operatorname{dist}^{2}(x,M),t)

\displaystyle+\gamma(\|u\|_{c})\Big)\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}.

We now utilize the linearity of

\int_{\Omega}\beta(\operatorname{dist}^{2}(x,M),t)\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}\leq L(t)\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{dist}^{2}(x,M)\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}.

(10)

Connecting back with the Wasserstein distance identity

W_{2}^{2}(\rho_{0},\mathcal{P}_{M})=\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{dist}^{2}(x,M)\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}
, we
obtain the desired dISS bound:

W_{2}(\rho_{t},\mathcal{P}_{M})\leq\beta_{d}(W_{2}(\rho_{0},\mathcal{P}_{M}),t)+\gamma_{d}(\|u\|_{c}).

where we defined

\beta_{d}(r,t)=r\sqrt{L(t)}
, and

\gamma_{d}=\sqrt{\gamma}
.
∎

Consider a differentiable functional

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=-\nabla\cdot(\rho_{t}(-\nabla\frac{\delta G}{\delta\rho}|_{\rho_{0}}))=\nabla\cdot(\rho_{t}\nabla\phi).

(11)

Let

\partial_{t}\widehat{\rho}_{t}=\nabla\cdot(\widehat{\rho}_{t}(\nabla\phi+\zeta_{u_{t}}(\widehat{\rho}_{t}))),

(12)

is a time-varying bounded input and

G

Proposition V.1

Suppose that the functional
G
has a compact set of minimizers

\mathfrak{P}^{*}
, and that it satisfies quadratic growth, gradient
dominance, and local
l
-smoothness (Section
II
). Let

\zeta_{u}(\rho)

\|\zeta_{u}(\rho)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathrm{d}\rho)}\leq\gamma(\|u\|)
,
where
\gamma\in\mathcal{K}_{\infty}

12
) is dISS.

Proof:

Let
G
, the functional in (
11
), be the dISS
Lyapunov functional candidate. The positivity bounds are satisfied
due to quadratic growth and local
l
-smoothness, which together
guarantee:

\frac{\lambda}{2}W_{2}^{2}(\rho,\mathfrak{P}^{*})\leq G(\rho)-G^{*}\leq\alpha_{l}(W_{2}^{2}(\rho,\mathfrak{P}^{*})),

where
\alpha_{l}
is a
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
function that bounds the
growth of the local

\dot{G_{u}}=-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi\cdot(\nabla\phi+\zeta_{u}(\rho))\mathrm{d}\rho=-\int_{\Omega}(|\nabla\phi|^{2}+\nabla\phi\cdot\zeta_{u}(\rho))\mathrm{d}\rho,

Applying Young’s inequality to the second term:

\dot{G_{u}}\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi|^{2}d\rho+\frac{1}{2}\int|\zeta_{u}(\rho)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho.

(13)

Now, from gradient dominance and quadratic growth inequalities to

-\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi|^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho\leq-2\lambda(G(\rho)-G^{*})\leq-\lambda^{2}W_{2}^{2}(\rho,\mathfrak{P}^{*}).

Substituting in expression (

), and using the

\displaystyle\dot{G}_{u}(\rho)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}W_{2}^{2}(\rho,\mathfrak{P}^{*})+\frac{1}{2}\gamma(\|u\|).

∎

V-B

Sample Approximations

The implementation of mean field algorithms can not be done exactly as
the number of agents is finite. Instead, agents employ density
approximations, which can also be understood as a type of perturbation
to the original flow. Here, we establish dISS properties of the flows
associated with Kernel Density Estimators (KDEs) and particle
approximations.

Approximations given by KDEs

The KDE approximation of a density for
N
agents is defined as

\rho^{h,N}(x;z)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{h}(x-x_{i})
, where
h

is the bandwidth of the kernels, and
z\in\Omega^{N}
represents the
agent locations,
z=(x_{i})
, with
x_{i}
the position of agent
i
.
We assume that
\rho^{h,N}
is the actual state of the system. Let

G
be a functional on
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega)
, and denote
G^{h,N}(z)\coloneqq G(\rho^{h,N}(x;z))
.

To minimize
G(\rho)
, a reasonable agent control input is:

\dot{x}_{i}=-\partial_{x_{i}}G^{h,N}(z):=-\frac{1}{N}(\nabla\phi\ast K_{h})(x_{i}),

(16)

where
\ast
is a shorthand notation which reduces to standard
convolution for integral functionals,
and
\phi
the first variation of
G
at
\rho^{h,N}
, see
[
18
]

for details.
Under (
16
), the KDE evolves as:

\partial_{t}\rho=\nabla\cdot(\rho\nabla\phi^{h,N}),

(17)

where
\nabla\phi^{h,N}
can be obtained by expressing

\partial_{t}\rho^{h,N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla_{x_{i}}K_{h}(x-x_{i})(-\dot{x}_{i})

as a continuity equation, yielding:

\nabla\phi^{h,N}(x)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\partial_{x_{j}}G^{h,N}(z)K_{h}(x-x_{j})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}K_{h}(x-x_{j})}.

(18)

At points where
\rho^{h,N}(x)\to 0
, this expression is understood
in the limit sense. Notice how equation (
18
) takes the
form of a Kernel regression estimator of the convoluted ideal velocity
field
[
28
]
. We aim to characterize the dISS properties
of (
17
), with respect to the ideal trajectory
in (
11
).

Lemma V.5
(KDE-based Continuity Equation is dISS)

Consider the Wasserstein gradient flow of a functional

G
(
11
), and its
counterpart (
17
) using a KDE approximation.
Assume that the kernel
K_{h}
satisfies the following properties:

A1)

h=cN^{-\frac{1}{d+2}}
, guaranteeing proper convergence of

\rho^{h,N}
to
\rho
in the mean-field limit, and
minimizing the squared error of the estimator (
18
).

A2) It has finite first and second-order moments
\int_{\Omega}K_{h}(z)\|z\|\mathrm{d}z\triangleq h\mu_{1}(K_{h})
,

\int_{\Omega}K_{h}(z)\|z\|^{2}\mathrm{d}z\triangleq h^{2}\mu_{2}(K_{h})
.

Suppose
G
satisfies the conditions of
Proposition
V.1
, and assume that its first variation

\nabla\phi
is Lipschitz continuous with constant

.
Then (

Proof:

To show dISS, we bound the
L_{2}(\rho^{h,N})
norm of the
perturbation
\zeta_{h,N}=\nabla\phi^{h,N}-\nabla\phi
. Defining
the convex combination coefficients

w_{i}(x)=\frac{K_{h}(x-x_{i})}{N\rho^{h,N}(x)}
, we write:

\zeta_{h,N}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}(x)e_{i}(x),

where
e_{i}(x)=(\nabla\phi\ast K_{h})(x_{i})-\nabla\phi(x)
. Then,
applying Jensen’s inequality,

(\sum_{i}w_{i}e_{i})^{2}\leq\sum_{i}w_{i}e_{i}^{2}
, and integrating
with respect to
\rho^{h,N}
, yields:

\|\zeta_{h,N}\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\rho^{h,N})}\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\Omega}K_{h}(x-x_{i})|e_{i}|^{2}\mathrm{d}x.

We bound the integrand by adding and subtracting
\nabla\phi(x_{i})

to the term
e_{i}(x)
and applying

\|a+b\|^{2}\leq 2(\|a\|^{2}+\|b\|^{2})
. Because
\nabla\phi
is

L

second moment (A2):

\int_{\Omega}K_{h}(x-x_{i})|\nabla\phi(x_{i})-\nabla\phi(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\leq L^{2}h^{2}\mu_{2}(K_{h}).

The second term can be directly bounded by the Kernel’s first moment
(A2), giving:

\displaystyle|(\nabla\phi\ast K_{h})(x_{i})

\displaystyle-\nabla\phi(x_{i})|^{2}\int_{\Omega}K_{h}(x-x_{i})\mathrm{d}x\leq(Lh\mu_{1}(K_{h}))^{2}.

\displaystyle|(\nabla\phi\ast K_{h})(x_{i})

\displaystyle-\nabla\phi(x_{i})|

\displaystyle=|\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(x_{i}))K_{h}(x-x_{i})\mathrm{d}x|

\displaystyle\leq L\int_{\Omega}|x-x_{i}|K_{h}(x-x_{i})\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=Lh\mu_{1}(K_{h})

Combining these two bounds yields:

\|\zeta_{h,N}\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\rho^{h,N})}\leq 2L^{2}(\mu_{2}(K_{h})+\mu_{1}^{2}(K_{h}))h^{2}.

The final bound in terms of
N
follows immediately from the
coupling
h=cN^{-\frac{1}{d+2}}
in (A1).

\rho^{*}(\mathcal{W}_{i})=\frac{1}{N}\quad\forall i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.
The SD-OT particle flow is defined through the following input:

\dot{x}_{i}=-\frac{N}{2}\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{2}^{2}(\rho^{N},\rho^{*})=-(x_{i}-N\int_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}x\mathrm{d}\rho^{*}),

(20)

we define the centroid of the Voronoi-Laguerre cell as

c_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}\coloneq N\int_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}x\mathrm{d}\rho^{*}
. Note
here that even though the cells depend on the agent location, its
effect on the derivative vanishes, as noted
in
[
5
]
. The stability of this particle flow is
characterized via the following lemma.

Lemma V.6

Consider an empirical distribution
\rho^{N}
evolving under the
SD-OT flow in (
20
).
Assume that the support of the target distribution
is bounded. Then, the flow is dISS with respect to the target
\rho^{*}
,
and the ultimate dISS
bound is proportional to
N^{-2/d}
for sufficiently large
N
.

Proof:

We define our Lyapunov function as the SD-OT functional.
Taking the derivative along the continuous-time trajectories
\dot{x}_{i}
yields:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}W_{2}^{2}(\rho^{N},\rho^{*})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{2}^{2}(\rho^{N},\rho^{*})\dot{x}_{i}=

(21)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle x_{i}-c_{\mathcal{W}_{i}},\dot{x}_{i}\rangle=-\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|x_{i}-c_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}|^{2}.

Expression (
21
) is related to the SD-OT functional in (
19
),
which can be rewritten by adding and subtracting
the centroid
c_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}
inside the norm and squaring the sum:

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\rho^{N},\rho^{*})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(|x_{i}-c_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}|^{2}\int_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}\mathrm{d}\rho^{*}

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}|c_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}-x|^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho^{*}+2\left\langle x_{i}-c_{\mathcal{W}_{i}},\int_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}(c_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}-x)\mathrm{d}\rho^{*}\right\rangle).

Since
c_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}
is the centroid of
\mathcal{W}_{i}
the
last term vanishes. Noting that the mass of each optimal transport cell is uniform (
\int_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}\mathrm{d}\rho^{*}=1/N
),
the SD-OT functional decomposes perfectly into:

W_{2}^{2}(\rho^{N},\rho^{*})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|x_{i}-c_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}|c_{\mathcal{W}_{i}}-x|^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho^{*}.

We identify the second term as the intrinsic error
u(t)
, which is bounded because the domain of
\rho^{*}
is bounded.
Then, we can write the Lyapunov decay (
21
) in terms of the SD-OT functional and the error.

\frac{d}{dt}W_{2}^{2}(\rho^{N},\rho^{*})=-2W_{2}^{2}(\rho^{N},\rho^{*})+2u(t).

The ultimate dISS bound can be obtained as:

\limsup_{t\to\infty}W_{2}^{2}(\rho^{N},\rho^{*})\leq\frac{1}{2}\limsup_{t\to\infty}(2u(t))\leq CN^{-2/d}.

Where we used that for
N
particles optimally placed in
\rho^{*}
the error term is bounded by

C_{d}D^{2}N^{-2/d}
, where
D=\text{diam}(\text{supp}(\rho^{*}))
and
C_{d}

is a constant depending on the dimension
[
31
]
, and that for sufficiently large
N
, the
algorithm assymtoticaly approaches the global minima.
∎

VII
Conclusions

This paper introduces a framework to analyse disturbances in
large-scale multi-agent systems, via the notion of distributional
ISS. With this, we are able to study the robustness of Wasserstein
gradient flows of certain relevant functionals, such as the
Wasserstein distance to a target distribution. Moreover, we
illustrated the notion with different examples motivated by swarm
control, such as entropic disturbances and sample approximations, and
we provided simulation results confirming the bounds. Current work
includes the expansion of the scope of this work to other discrete
algorithms and dynamic systems in probability spaces.

\min_{x\in\Omega}V(x)=V^{*}=V(y)

and the following holds:

P1)
The function
V-V^{*}

is a proper size function for
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
.

P2)
The gradient magnitude
|\nabla V|
is a
size function for
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
. Equivalently, there
exists a class
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
function
\alpha_{2}
such that

\alpha_{2}(V(x)-V^{*})\leq|\nabla V(x)|^{2}

x\in\Omega
.

P3)
The gradient
\nabla V
is locally
Lipschitz continuous.
\bullet

Note that the minimizers of the functional
\mathcal{V}(\mu)
are the set
of measures with support on the minimizers of

\mathcal{P}_{M}
.
We then define

\mathcal{V}^{*}=V^{*}
.

Quadratic growth:
Since
V
is a proper loss function,

is a size function with respect to
M
. From the
properties of size functions,
V
admits a lower bound in terms of
the distance to
M
. We
have
V(x)-V^{*}\geq\alpha_{1}(\operatorname{dist}(x,M)^{2})
, where

\mu

\displaystyle\int(V(x)-V^{*})\mathrm{d}\mu(x)
