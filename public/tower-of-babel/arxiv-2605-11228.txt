Quantum Algorithm for Identifying Hidden Graphs: Spectral Theory and Numerical Evidence
Paweł Wocjan

Abstract
We give a quantum algorithm for a novel type of black-box problem: identifying a hidden d d -regular base graph G G on n n vertices from oracle access to an obfuscated version of it, rather than traversing it. From G G we build the spired graph G spire G_{\mathrm{spire}} in three steps: each vertex is lifted into an exponentially large cluster, with the clusters of adjacent vertices joined by a random bipartite graph; each cluster is then crowned with a balanced spire; finally, all vertices are randomly relabelled to obfuscate the structure. Specializing to G = K 2 G=K_{2} recovers the welded-trees graph. Our quantum algorithm is conceptually simple: a continuous-time quantum walk on G spire G_{\mathrm{spire}} , followed by a single Hadamard test at a classically precomputed evolution time t ∗ t^{*} ; the algorithm returns the candidate whose predicted amplitude is closest to the measurement. Its design and analysis rest on a rigorous spectral theory: from the apex of any spire, the walk is automatically confined to a polynomial-dimensional invariant subspace on which it evolves under the adjacency matrix of a much simpler towered graph G tower G_{\mathrm{tower}} ; that matrix block-diagonalizes into n n independent tridiagonal systems of size n n , each solved in closed form by a Chebyshev secular equation. Efficient numerics enabled by this decomposition diagonalize G tower G_{\mathrm{tower}} for any d-regular base graph G G , supplying t ∗ t^{*} and the predicted return amplitudes that the algorithm needs. Specifically, on the prism graphs Y m Y_{m} versus the Möbius ladders M m M_{m} (each on n = 2 ​ m n=2m vertices), the numerical study supports a precise conjecture that O ~ ​ ( n 2 / log ⁡ n ) \widetilde{O}(n^{2}/\log n) measurements at evolution time of order m 2 m^{2} suffice to distinguish the two families; we have tested 4 ≤ m ≤ 5121 4\leq m\leq 5121 ( n n up to 10242 10242 ). By analogy with the welded-trees lower bounds, we further conjecture that any classical algorithm requires queries exponential in n n . Together these conjectures point to an exponential quantum speedup for the identification of an obfuscated base graph.

Introduction

We introduce a novel type of problem:
identifying
which graph is hidden, not merely traversing it. The algorithm is given an obfuscating oracle for a spired graph
G_{\mathrm{spire}}
built from a hidden
d
-regular base graph
G
on
n

G_{1},\ldots,G_{r}
, and must identify

. This requires extracting global spectral information about the hidden graph, qualitatively different from the vertex-to-vertex navigation that has been the focus of earlier black-box quantum-walk separations.

From a
d
-regular base graph
G
on
n

The quantum algorithm is conceptually simple: a single-time Hadamard test estimating the return amplitude of a quantum walk at a classically precomputed optimal time

in closed form via a Chebyshev secular equation, yielding

For our quantum evaluation we work on the prism graphs

Our results open a new direction within a recent line of exponential black-box separations between quantum and classical computation: identification, not traversal. The welded trees problem of Childs et al.
[

]

For the classical-hardness conjecture we further specialize: we set

Construction of the spired graph

The construction takes three inputs: a simple, connected,
d
-regular graph
G=(V,E)
with
n=|V|
vertices and adjacency matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
; a

,
L\geq 1
, the height of the spire over each base-graph vertex; and a
distinguished vertex

, the unique vertex of
G
at which the algorithm is given an entry point. The construction also depends on a positive integer
c\geq 1
, the
thickening parameter
, and we set
D=cd
. We write
\Sigma=\{1,2,\ldots,D\}
for an alphabet of size
D
. Throughout the paper,

denotes the distinguished vertex of
G
, while
v
and

are reserved for arbitrary base-graph vertices; we write edges as
\{v,w\}\in E
.

The construction proceeds in three steps:

•

the
lifted graph

, a thickening of
G
;

•

the
spired graph

G_{\mathrm{spire}}
, obtained from

by crowning each lifted cluster with a

•

the
obfuscating oracle

, which presents

to any algorithm only through random labels.

2.1
The lifted graph

For each
v\in V
, write
S_{v}^{(L)}=\{(v,x):x\in\Sigma^{L}\}
for the
cluster
of
D^{L}
length-
L
strings indexed at
v
. The lifted graph replaces each vertex of
G
with its cluster, and each edge with a random
c
-regular bipartite graph between the corresponding clusters.

Definition 2.1

(Random
c
-regular bipartite graph
C_{e}
)
.

For an edge
e=\{v,w\}\in E
, let
\Omega_{e}
be the set of all
c
-regular bipartite graphs on
S_{w}^{(L)}\uplus S_{v}^{(L)}
. Sample
C_{e}
uniformly at random from
\Omega_{e}
; this contributes
cD^{L}
edges per original edge of
G
. We write
\mathbf{C}=(C_{e})_{e\in E}
for the full family of sampled graphs.

Definition 2.2

(Lifted graph
G_{\mathrm{lift}}
)
.

The
lifted graph

G_{\mathrm{lift}}=(V_{\mathrm{lift}},E_{\mathrm{lift}})
has vertex set
V_{\mathrm{lift}}=\biguplus_{v\in V}S_{v}^{(L)}
and edge set
E_{\mathrm{lift}}=\biguplus_{e\in E}E(C_{e})
. It is
D
-regular.

The cluster
S_{v}^{(L)}
should be thought of as a
thickened
version of vertex
v
: the
d
original neighbours of
v
in
G
contribute
d
random
c
-regular bipartite graphs attached at the cluster, each giving every vertex in
S_{v}^{(L)}
exactly
c
neighbours in the partner cluster, for a total of
D=cd
neighbours per cluster vertex.

The natural projection
\pi:V_{\mathrm{lift}}\to V
, mapping every vertex
(v,x)\in S_{v}^{(L)}
to
v
, is a graph homomorphism. At
c=1
,
C_{e}
is a uniformly random perfect matching
S_{v}^{(L)}\leftrightarrow S_{w}^{(L)}
and
\pi
becomes a local isomorphism, exhibiting
G_{\mathrm{lift}}
as a
d^{L}
-fold random covering of
G
in the sense of algebraic graph theory
[
4
]
. For
c\geq 2
,
\pi
fails to be a local isomorphism (each cluster vertex has
c
neighbours in
S_{w}^{(L)}
while
v
has only one neighbour

), and

is more accurately described as a
thickening
of
G
in which each base-graph edge
e
is replaced by the
c
-regular bipartite graph
C_{e}
on
2D^{L}
vertices.

2.2
Adding the spires

To obtain the spired graph
G_{\mathrm{spire}}
, we crown each cluster
S_{v}^{(L)}
of
G_{\mathrm{lift}}
with a spire defined using the prefix structure of strings over
\Sigma
.

Definition 2.3

(Spire
G_{v}
)
.

The spire
G_{v}=(S_{v},E_{v})
at vertex
v
is the graph whose vertex set is the disjoint union of
L+1
level sets,

S_{v}\;=\;\biguplus_{\ell=0}^{L}S_{v}^{(\ell)},\qquad S_{v}^{(\ell)}\;=\;\{(v,s):s\in\Sigma^{\ell}\},

where
\Sigma^{0}=\{\epsilon\}
consists of the empty string. The level
\ell=0
is a singleton,
S_{v}^{(0)}=\{(v,\epsilon)\}
, and we call
a_{v}=(v,\epsilon)
the
apex
of the spire. The level
\ell=L
coincides with the cluster
S_{v}^{(L)}
already present in
G_{\mathrm{lift}}
, and we call this set the
foundation
of the spire. The edge set
E_{v}
is given by the
prefix property
: for each level
\ell=0,\ldots,L-1
, each vertex
(v,s)\in S_{v}^{(\ell)}
is connected to the
D
vertices
(v,s\cdot\alpha)\in S_{v}^{(\ell+1)}
(
\alpha\in\Sigma
).

As a graph,
G_{v}
is a perfectly balanced
D
-ary tree of depth
L
, but
inverted
: the apex
a_{v}
is the unique root and protrudes outward as the only addressable entry point, while the foundation
S_{v}^{(L)}
is the set of leaves and forms the base on which the spire stands.

Definition 2.4

(Spired graph
G_{\mathrm{spire}}
)
.

The
spired graph

G_{\mathrm{spire}}
is obtained by crowning each cluster in
G_{\mathrm{lift}}
with its corresponding spire:

G_{\mathrm{spire}}\;=\;\biggl(\biguplus_{v\in V}S_{v},\;\biguplus_{v\in V}E_{v}\;\uplus\;E_{\mathrm{lift}}\biggr).

The spire acts as a tool that hides the foundation
S_{v}^{(L)}
behind its single apex, addressable from outside as the only entry point of the spire. Internal vertices of the spire each have degree
D+1
inside the spire alone, while the apex has degree
D
since it has no parent; foundation vertices have degree
D+1
in
G_{\mathrm{spire}}
, contributed by one parent edge upward into the spire and the
D
edges from the
d
alternating cycles incident at
S_{v}^{(L)}
.

The height
L
controls the size of the spired graph:

\displaystyle|V_{\mathrm{spire}}|=n\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}D^{\ell}=n\,\frac{D^{L+1}-1}{D-1},

exponential in
L
.

The construction is illustrated in Figures
1
and
2
. Figure
2
draws the random cycle only between the bottom and right spires, since including all random cycles would make the figure too crowded. We use
L
to denote the length of the attached paths in the towered-graph figure.

2.3
The obfuscating oracle

The spired graph
G_{\mathrm{spire}}
is not given explicitly. Instead, the algorithm receives an obfuscating oracle
\mathcal{O}_{G}
that exposes
G_{\mathrm{spire}}
only through random labels.

Definition 2.5

(Obfuscating oracle
\mathcal{O}_{G}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\{0,1\}^{a}
be a set of
a
-bit labels, with
a=2\lceil\log_{2}|V_{\mathrm{spire}}|\rceil=O(L\log D+\log n)
, so that the probability that a uniformly random
a
-bit string falls inside the image
\iota(V_{\mathrm{spire}})
is at most
|V_{\mathrm{spire}}|/2^{a}\leq 1/|V_{\mathrm{spire}}|
, exponentially small in
L
. Sample an injective labelling
\iota:V_{\mathrm{spire}}\to\mathcal{X}
uniformly at random, independently of
\mathbf{C}
. Given a label
x=\iota(w)\in\iota(V_{\mathrm{spire}})
, the oracle returns the
set

\mathcal{O}_{G}(x)\;=\;\bigl\{\iota(y):y\in N_{G_{\mathrm{spire}}}(w)\bigr\}

of labels of neighbours of
w
. On any label outside
\iota(V_{\mathrm{spire}})
the oracle returns
\bot
. The algorithm receives the seed label
\iota(a_{u})
of the apex of the spire over the distinguished vertex as input.

Three points deserve emphasis. First, the labelling
\iota
is injective, so distinct vertices have distinct labels and the response
\mathcal{O}_{G}(x)
is genuinely a set rather than a multiset. Second, the oracle exposes no further positional or type information beyond the obvious degree distinction at the apices: every vertex has degree
D+1
except the
n
apices, which have degree
D
. The algorithm cannot tell from a label whether the corresponding vertex is an internal spire vertex or a foundation vertex, nor which spire or which foundation a vertex belongs to. Third, the structural information about
G
is doubly hidden, by the random sampling of
\mathbf{C}
and by the uniformly random labelling
\iota
: in particular,
\iota
destroys the prefix structure that would otherwise identify the level
\ell
of a spire vertex from its
\Sigma
-string coordinates.

Because the labelling
\iota
randomises away every distinguishing feature of
V_{\mathrm{spire}}
except the apex degree, the only structural information available to the algorithm is the value of
L
(and the apex count
n
). The height
L
thus functions as the natural security parameter of the construction: any classical algorithm that recovers
G
from
\mathcal{O}_{G}
is conjectured to require a number of queries exponential in
L
, with a base that grows with the thickening
c
(Section
11
).

In the security analysis (this section and Section
11
), we specialize to
c=2
and require each
C_{e}
to be a uniformly random
alternating Hamiltonian cycle
on
S_{v}^{(L)}\uplus S_{w}^{(L)}
, a strict subset of the
2
-regular bipartite graphs (which generically split into a disjoint union of even cycles). At
G=K_{2}
,
d=1
, the spired graph then specializes exactly to the welded-trees graph of Childs et al.
[
1
]
: two binary trees joined at their leaves by an alternating Hamiltonian cycle.

This conservative choice inherits the welded-trees obfuscation mechanism (random alternating cycles plus random labelling), whose classical opacity Childs et al. and Fenner–Zhang
[
2
]
established for the traversal version of the problem; the formal comparison and the resulting classical-hardness conjecture are taken up in Section
11
.

In the quantum setting, the oracle is implemented as a unitary that maps
|x\rangle|0\rangle\mapsto|x\rangle|\mathcal{O}_{G}(x)\rangle
in superposition. This enables simulation of the spired-graph Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{spire}}=\frac{1}{c}A_{\mathrm{spire}}
as a sparse Hamiltonian (maximum degree
D{+}1
), using standard sparse Hamiltonian simulation techniques
[
6
]
. The simulation cost per unit time is polylogarithmic in the precision and polynomial in the degree and the norm.

3
The effective Krylov Hamiltonian

The spired graph
G_{\mathrm{spire}}
has exponentially many vertices, making direct analysis intractable. However, the quantum walk started from an apex evolves within a
polynomially-dimensional invariant subspace
, whose structure we now describe.

3.1
Level states and the Krylov subspace

Define the Hamiltonian of the spired graph as
H_{\mathrm{spire}}=\frac{1}{c}A_{\mathrm{spire}}
. Throughout this section we work with the starting state
|a_{u}\rangle=|(u,\epsilon)\rangle
, where
u\in V
is the distinguished vertex of Section
2
and
a_{u}
is the apex of the spire
G_{u}
.

Definition 3.1

(Level states)
.

For each
v\in V
and
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,L\}
, the
level state
is the uniform superposition over all vertices at level
\ell
of the spire:

|S_{v}^{(\ell)}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{D^{\ell}}}\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{\ell}}|(v,x)\rangle.

In particular,
|S_{v}^{(0)}\rangle=|(v,\epsilon)\rangle
is the apex.

The level states span an
n(L{+}1)
-dimensional subspace of the exponentially large Hilbert space. The crucial observation is that this subspace is invariant under
H_{\mathrm{spire}}
.

Definition 3.2

(Krylov subspace)
.

The
Krylov subspace
of the starting state
|(u,\epsilon)\rangle
is

\mathcal{K}=\mathrm{span}\{A_{\mathrm{spire}}^{j}|(u,\epsilon)\rangle:j=0,1,2,\ldots\}.

3.2
Action of the Hamiltonian on level states

The adjacency matrix of
G_{\mathrm{spire}}
decomposes as
A_{\mathrm{spire}}=A_{\mathrm{lift}}+\sum_{v\in V}A_{v}
, where
A_{\mathrm{lift}}
is the adjacency matrix of the lifted graph and
A_{v}
is the adjacency matrix of the spire
G_{v}
. Due to the regularity of the lifted graph and the perfect balance of the spires, the level states transform in a particularly simple way.

Lemma 3.3

(Action on level states)
.

For all
v\in V
and
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,L\}
:

\displaystyle A_{\mathrm{lift}}|S_{v}^{(L)}\rangle

\displaystyle=c\sum_{\{v,w\}\in E}|S_{w}^{(L)}\rangle,

\displaystyle A_{\mathrm{lift}}|S_{v}^{(\ell)}\rangle

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{for }\ell<L,

(1)

\displaystyle A_{v}|S_{v}^{(\ell)}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{D}\bigl(|S_{v}^{(\ell-1)}\rangle+|S_{v}^{(\ell+1)}\rangle\bigr),

\displaystyle A_{v}|S_{w}^{(\ell)}\rangle

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{for }v\neq w,

(2)

where we set
|S_{v}^{(-1)}\rangle=|S_{v}^{(L+1)}\rangle=0
.

Proof.

For the lifted graph: each vertex in
S_{v}^{(L)}
has exactly
c
neighbours in
S_{w}^{(L)}
for each edge
\{v,w\}\in E
(from the random
c
-regular bipartite graph
C_{e}
between
S_{v}^{(L)}
and
S_{w}^{(L)}
). The uniform superposition
|S_{v}^{(L)}\rangle
therefore maps to
c\sum_{w}|S_{w}^{(L)}\rangle
, where the factor
c
arises from the
c
-regularity of
C_{e}
. For
\ell<L
, the vertices in
S_{v}^{(\ell)}
belong to the tree interior and have no lifted-graph edges, giving zero.

For the tree adjacency: a vertex
(v,x)\in S_{v}^{(\ell)}
with
0<\ell<L
has one parent in
S_{v}^{(\ell-1)}
and
D
children in
S_{v}^{(\ell+1)}
. The normalization factors in the level states produce: the parent contributes
D^{-(\ell-1)/2}
(from
|S_{v}^{(\ell-1)}\rangle
) with a factor
D^{\ell/2}
(from dualizing
\langle S_{v}^{(\ell)}|
), giving
\sqrt{D}
; the children similarly contribute
\sqrt{D}
. The boundary cases
\ell=0
(no parent) and
\ell=L
(no children) give the convention
|S_{v}^{(-1)}\rangle=|S_{v}^{(L+1)}\rangle=0
.
∎

3.3
The towered graph

The structural data of the restriction (which level states couple to which, with what weights) collects naturally into a small graph that we call the towered graph.

Definition 3.4

(Towered graph
G_{\mathrm{tower}}
)
.

The
towered graph

G_{\mathrm{tower}}
has:

•

Vertex set
V_{\mathrm{tower}}=\{(v,\ell):v\in V,\,\ell=0,\ldots,L\}
.

•

Top:

(v,0)
, the top of the tower at
v
.

•

Bottom:

(v,L)
, identified with the base-graph vertex
v
.

•

Tower edges
\{(v,\ell),(v,\ell{+}1)\}
with weight
\gamma=\sqrt{D}/c=\sqrt{d/c}
for
\ell=0,\ldots,L{-}1
.

•

Base-graph edges
\{(v,L),(w,L)\}
with weight
1
for
\{v,w\}\in E
.

At
c=2
,
\gamma=\sqrt{d/2}
.

Write
A_{\mathrm{tower}}
for the (weighted) adjacency matrix of
G_{\mathrm{tower}}
.

Theorem 3.5

(Restriction to the Krylov subspace)
.

The Krylov subspace
\mathcal{K}
is spanned by the level states
\{|S_{v}^{(\ell)}\rangle:v\in V,\,\ell=0,\ldots,L\}
and has dimension
n(L{+}1)
. The restriction of
H_{\mathrm{spire}}=\frac{1}{c}A_{\mathrm{spire}}
to
\mathcal{K}
(the
effective Krylov Hamiltonian

H_{\mathrm{spire}}|_{\mathcal{K}}
) equals the towered-graph adjacency matrix:

H_{\mathrm{spire}}|_{\mathcal{K}}\;=\;A_{\mathrm{tower}},

where the identification of
\mathcal{K}
with
\mathbb{C}^{V_{\mathrm{tower}}}
is via
|S_{v}^{(\ell)}\rangle\leftrightarrow|(v,\ell)\rangle
.

Proof.

The level state
|S_{u}^{(0)}\rangle=|(u,\epsilon)\rangle
is the starting state, so it belongs to
\mathcal{K}
. By Lemma
3.3
, applying
A_{\mathrm{spire}}
to any level state produces a linear combination of level states, so
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathrm{span}\{|S_{v}^{(\ell)}\rangle\}
. Since
|(u,\epsilon)\rangle\in\mathcal{K}
and the level states at all levels and vertices can be reached by iterated application (the tower edges connect adjacent levels, and the lifted graph connects different vertices at level
L
), the Krylov subspace equals the full span.

For the effective Krylov Hamiltonian: using
H_{\mathrm{spire}}=\frac{1}{c}A_{\mathrm{spire}}=\frac{1}{c}(A_{\mathrm{lift}}+\sum_{v}A_{v})
and Lemma
3.3
, the matrix elements in the level-state basis are:

\displaystyle\langle S_{v}^{(\ell)}|H_{\mathrm{spire}}|S_{v}^{(\ell\pm 1)}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{D}}{c}=\gamma,

\displaystyle\langle S_{v}^{(L)}|H_{\mathrm{spire}}|S_{w}^{(L)}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{c}\cdot[\{v,w\}\in E]=[\{v,w\}\in E],

and all other matrix elements vanish. The tower-edge weight may be rewritten as
\gamma=\sqrt{D}/c=\sqrt{cd}/c=\sqrt{d/c}
. These are exactly the matrix entries of
A_{\mathrm{tower}}
in the basis
\{|(v,\ell)\rangle\}
.
∎

4
Base graph families: prism and Möbius ladder

We study two families of
3
-regular vertex-transitive graphs on
n=2m
vertices,
m\geq 3
. Both families share most of their edge set; they differ in only four edges, which we call the
closing rail edges
. This shared structure makes them an unusually transparent test case: any distinguishability between the two (spectral, algorithmic, or query-complexity-theoretic) is fully attributable to the choice of closing edges.

4.1
Pair coordinates and shared structure

Both graphs
Y_{m}
(the prism) and
M_{m}
(the Möbius ladder) have the common vertex set

V\;=\;\{(k,b):0\leq k\leq m-1,\;b\in\{0,1\}\},\qquad|V|\;=\;2m.

We call
\{(k,0)\}_{0\leq k\leq m-1}
the
outer rail
and
\{(k,1)\}_{0\leq k\leq m-1}
the
inner rail
. The two graphs share the following
common edges
:

•

rungs
:
\{(k,0),(k,1)\}
for
0\leq k\leq m-1
;

•

open rail edges
:
\{(k,b),(k{+}1,b)\}
for
0\leq k\leq m-2
and
b\in\{0,1\}
.

This common edge set forms a
2{\times}m
ladder graph: two parallel paths of
m
vertices each, connected at every position by a rung. Each vertex on the outer or inner rail has at most three incident common edges; each of the four
rail endpoints

(0,b),(m{-}1,b)
(
b\in\{0,1\}
) has only two common edges and is therefore one rail edge short of degree
3
.

4.2
The prism and the Möbius ladder

The two graphs differ only in how the rail endpoints are joined to close the ladder into a closed band:

\displaystyle E(Y_{m})\setminus E(M_{m})

\displaystyle=\bigl\{\{(m{-}1,0),(0,0)\},\ \{(m{-}1,1),(0,1)\}\bigr\}

\displaystyle\text{(parallel)},

\displaystyle E(M_{m})\setminus E(Y_{m})

\displaystyle=\bigl\{\{(m{-}1,0),(0,1)\},\ \{(m{-}1,1),(0,0)\}\bigr\}

\displaystyle\text{(twisted)}.

We call the four edges in
E(Y_{m})\,\triangle\,E(M_{m})
the
closing rail edges
, and the four-vertex set
\{(0,b),(m{-}1,b):b\in\{0,1\}\}
together with these four edges the
differing
4
-cycle
, informally
the twist
. In drawings (Figures
4
and
5
) we place the twist on the right.

Eigenvalues.

The prism
Y_{m}=C_{m}\square K_{2}
is the Cartesian product of an
m
-cycle and an edge, so its eigenvalues are

\mu_{k}^{\pm}\;=\;2\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi k}{m}\right)\pm 1,\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,m-1.

(3)

The Möbius ladder
M_{m}
is isomorphic to the cycle
C_{2m}
with
m
additional “rung” edges joining vertex
i
to vertex
i{+}m
for
i=0,\ldots,m{-}1
, with eigenvalues

\mu_{k}\;=\;2\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi k}{2m}\right)+(-1)^{k},\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,2m-1.

(4)

Both formulas follow from the standard spectral theory of vertex-transitive graphs.

Unified form.

The two formulas can be brought into a common form that exposes which eigenvalues coincide between
Y_{m}
and
M_{m}
and which differ. Writing
\theta_{j}:=2\pi j/m
for the
m
cycle-of-length-
m
angles, the Möbius formula (
4
) splits by parity of
k
(
k=2j
even,
k=2j+1
odd,
j=0,\ldots,m{-}1
) into two branches of
m
eigenvalues each, matching the
\pm 1
branches of the prism formula (
3
).

Proposition 4.1

(Unified spectral form)
.

For
j=0,1,\ldots,m-1
, the eigenvalues of
Y_{m}
and
M_{m}
are

\displaystyle Y_{m}{:}\quad

\displaystyle\mu_{j}^{+}=2\cos\theta_{j}+1,\qquad\mu_{j}^{-}=2\cos\theta_{j}-1,

\displaystyle M_{m}{:}\quad

\displaystyle\mu_{j}^{+}=2\cos\theta_{j}+1,\qquad\mu_{j}^{-}=2\cos(\theta_{j}+\pi/m)-1.

The
+1
branches coincide between the two graphs; the
-1
branches differ by the half-step phase shift
\theta_{j}\mapsto\theta_{j}+\pi/m
in the cosine argument.

Proof.

For
Y_{m}
, the Cartesian product
Y_{m}=C_{m}\square K_{2}
has eigenvalues that are sums of cycle and edge eigenvalues,
2\cos\theta_{j}\pm 1
. For
M_{m}
, the Cayley graph of
\mathbb{Z}_{2m}
with generators
\{\pm 1,m\}
has eigenvalues
\chi_{k}(1)+\chi_{k}(-1)+\chi_{k}(m)=2\cos(\pi k/m)+(-1)^{k}
at character
\chi_{k}(j)=e^{\pi ikj/m}
,
k=0,\ldots,2m{-}1
. Splitting by parity,
k=2j
gives
\mu^{+}_{j}=2\cos\theta_{j}+1
, and
k=2j+1
gives
\mu^{-}_{j}=2\cos(\pi(2j+1)/m)-1=2\cos(\theta_{j}+\pi/m)-1
.
∎

4.3
Bipartiteness

Define the parity colouring
c:V\to\{0,1\}
by

c(k,b)\;=\;(k+b)\bmod 2.

Proposition 4.3

(Alternating bipartiteness)
.

Y_{m}
is bipartite if and only if
m
is even, and
M_{m}
is bipartite if and only if
m
is odd. In both bipartite cases, a
2
-colouring witness is the parity colouring
c
.

Proof.

The colouring
c
assigns differing parities to the two endpoints of every rung and every open rail edge. The closing rail edges contribute as follows.

•

In
Y_{m}
, the parallel closing edge
\{(m{-}1,b),(0,b)\}
has parity difference
(m-1+b)-b=m-1
; this is odd iff
m
is even, so
c
is a proper
2
-colouring iff
m
is even.

•

In
M_{m}
, the twisted closing edge
\{(m{-}1,b),(0,1{-}b)\}
has parity difference
(m-1+b)-(1-b)=m-2+2b\equiv m\pmod{2}
; this is odd iff
m
is odd.

Conversely, in each failing case the graph contains an explicit odd cycle. For
Y_{m}
with
m
odd, the outer rail
(0,0)\to(1,0)\to\cdots\to(m{-}1,0)\to(0,0)
is an odd
m
-cycle. For
M_{m}
with
m
even, the closed walk
(0,0)\to(1,0)\to\cdots\to(m{-}1,0)\to(0,1)\to(0,0)
(using
m{-}1
open rail edges, one twisted closing edge, and one rung) is an odd cycle of length
m+1
.
∎

Corollary 4.4

(Bipartite member toggles between families)
.

For every integer
m\geq 3
, exactly one of the graphs
Y_{m},M_{m}
is bipartite:
Y_{m}
when
m
is even,
M_{m}
when
m
is odd. In particular, the bipartite member of the pair
\{Y_{m},M_{m}\}
alternates between the two families as
m
increments by one.

Proof.

Immediate from Proposition
4.3
, since every integer is either even or odd but not both.
∎

5
Spectral theory of the towered graph

We now develop a complete spectral decomposition of the towered graph
G_{\mathrm{tower}}
. The key insight is that
A_{\mathrm{tower}}
admits a tensor-product decomposition that reduces the
n^{2}
-dimensional eigenvalue problem to
n

n
.

5.1
Tensor product structure

We identify the vertex
(v,\ell)
of
G_{\mathrm{tower}}
with the tensor product state
|v\rangle\otimes|\ell\rangle
, so the Hilbert space is
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{L+1}
, where
L=n-1
.

Definition 5.1

(Path matrix)
.

The weighted path matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}^{(L+1)\times(L+1)}
is

P=\gamma\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\bigl(|\ell\rangle\langle\ell{+}1|+|\ell{+}1\rangle\langle\ell|\bigr).

Proposition 5.2

(Tensor decomposition)
.

The adjacency matrix
A_{\mathrm{tower}}
satisfies

A_{\mathrm{tower}}=A\otimes|L\rangle\langle L|+I_{n}\otimes P,

(5)

where
|L\rangle\langle L|\in\mathbb{R}^{(L+1)\times(L+1)}
is the projection onto the bottom
\ell=L
.

Proof.

We verify that (
5
) reproduces the correct matrix elements. For a state
|v,\ell\rangle=|v\rangle\otimes|\ell\rangle
:

Case
1\leq\ell\leq L{-}1
(interior of the path):
The only neighbors are
(v,\ell\pm 1)
, both connected by weight
\gamma
. We compute:

(A\otimes|L\rangle\langle L|)|v,\ell\rangle=A|v\rangle\otimes\langle L|\ell\rangle|L\rangle=0
since
\ell\neq L
. And
(I\otimes P)|v,\ell\rangle=|v\rangle\otimes(\gamma|\ell{-}1\rangle+\gamma|\ell{+}1\rangle)
. Combined:
A_{\mathrm{tower}}|v,\ell\rangle=\gamma|v,\ell{-}1\rangle+\gamma|v,\ell{+}1\rangle
.
\checkmark

Case
\ell=0
(top):
The only neighbor is
(v,1)
. We get
(A\otimes|L\rangle\langle L|)|v,0\rangle=0
and
(I\otimes P)|v,0\rangle=\gamma|v,1\rangle
.
\checkmark

Case
\ell=L
(bottom):
The neighbors are
(v,L{-}1)
(weight
\gamma
) and all
(w,L)
for
\{v,w\}\in E
(weight

). We get
(A\otimes|L\rangle\langle L|)|v,L\rangle=A|v\rangle\otimes|L\rangle=\sum_{w}A_{vw}|w,L\rangle
and
(I\otimes P)|v,L\rangle=\gamma|v,L{-}1\rangle
.
\checkmark

∎

5.2
Block diagonalization

The tensor structure enables an exact block diagonalization.

Lemma 5.3

(Commutativity)
.

[A_{\mathrm{tower}},\;A\otimes I_{L+1}]=0
.

Proof.

We compute each term separately:

\displaystyle[A\otimes|L\rangle\langle L|,\;A\otimes I]

\displaystyle=(A^{2}-A^{2})\otimes|L\rangle\langle L|=0,

\displaystyle[I\otimes P,\;A\otimes I]

\displaystyle=(A-A)\otimes P=0.

Both commutators vanish because
A
commutes with itself and
I
commutes with everything.
∎

(Block diagonalization)
.

+1

Y_{m}
and
M_{m}
); the red columns of
M_{7}

\pi/m=\pi/7

Y_{7}

M_{7}

\mu=-3

, reflecting the bipartiteness of
M_{7}

\pm 2\gamma=\pm\sqrt{6}
; eigenvalues outside this band come from the analytic continuation of the secular equation to the hyperbolic branch (Section
6.2
).

6
The secular equation

We now determine the eigenvalues of
H^{(\mu)}
explicitly, using the connection to Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind.

6.1
Recurrence relations and Chebyshev polynomials

The eigenvalue equation
H^{(\mu)}|\phi\rangle=\lambda|\phi\rangle
yields three conditions on the amplitudes
\phi_{\ell}
. At the top we have the
top condition

\lambda\phi_{0}=\gamma\,\phi_{1};

(8)

in the interior we have the
bulk recurrence

\lambda\phi_{\ell}=\gamma\,\phi_{\ell-1}+\gamma\,\phi_{\ell+1},\qquad 1\leq\ell\leq L-1;

(9)

and at the bottom we have the
bottom condition

\lambda\phi_{L}=\gamma\,\phi_{L-1}+\mu\,\phi_{L}.

(10)

Setting
x=\lambda/(2\gamma)
, the bulk recurrence becomes
2x\,\phi_{\ell}=\phi_{\ell-1}+\phi_{\ell+1}
, the defining recurrence for the Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind:
U_{0}(x)=1
,
U_{1}(x)=2x
,
U_{\ell+1}(x)=2x\,U_{\ell}(x)-U_{\ell-1}(x)
.

Lemma 6.1

(Path amplitudes)
.

The sequence
\phi_{\ell}=U_{\ell}(x)
satisfies both the top condition (
8
) and the bulk recurrence (
9
), with
\phi_{0}=U_{0}(x)=1
at the top for all
\lambda
.

Proof.

For the top condition,
\phi_{0}=U_{0}(x)=1
and
\phi_{1}=U_{1}(x)=2x=\lambda/\gamma
, so
\gamma\,\phi_{1}=\lambda=\lambda\,\phi_{0}
, verifying (
8
). For the bulk recurrence, the Chebyshev defining identity
U_{\ell+1}(x)=2x\,U_{\ell}(x)-U_{\ell-1}(x)
, with
x=\lambda/(2\gamma)
, becomes
\gamma\,U_{\ell+1}(x)=\lambda\,U_{\ell}(x)-\gamma\,U_{\ell-1}(x)
, i.e.,
\lambda\,\phi_{\ell}=\gamma\,\phi_{\ell-1}+\gamma\,\phi_{\ell+1}
, which is (
9
).
∎

Theorem 6.2

(Secular equation)
.

The sequence
\phi_{\ell}=U_{\ell}(x)
satisfies the bottom condition (
10
) if and only if
\lambda
is a root of

\boxed{\;\gamma\,U_{L+1}(x)=\mu\,U_{L}(x),\qquad x=\frac{\lambda}{2\gamma}.\;}

(11)

Proof.

By Lemma
6.1
, the sequence
\phi_{\ell}=U_{\ell}(x)
satisfies the top condition (
8
) and the bulk recurrence (
9
) for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, so it suffices to determine for which
\lambda
this sequence additionally satisfies the bottom condition (
10
). Substituting
\phi_{\ell}=U_{\ell}(x)
into (
10
) gives

\lambda\,U_{L}(x)\;=\;\gamma\,U_{L-1}(x)+\mu\,U_{L}(x),

which rearranges to
(\lambda-\mu)\,U_{L}(x)=\gamma\,U_{L-1}(x)
. The Chebyshev defining recurrence at index
L
, namely
U_{L+1}(x)=2x\,U_{L}(x)-U_{L-1}(x)
, yields
U_{L-1}(x)=2x\,U_{L}(x)-U_{L+1}(x)
. Substituting and using
2\gamma x=\lambda
,

(\lambda-\mu)\,U_{L}(x)\;=\;\gamma\bigl(2x\,U_{L}(x)-U_{L+1}(x)\bigr)\;=\;\lambda\,U_{L}(x)-\gamma\,U_{L+1}(x).

Cancelling
\lambda\,U_{L}(x)
from both sides yields
\gamma\,U_{L+1}(x)=\mu\,U_{L}(x)
, which is the secular equation (
11
). Each step is reversible, so the bottom condition holds if and only if
\lambda
is a root of the secular equation.
∎

Proposition 6.3

(Root count and simplicity)
.

For any
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, the secular equation has exactly
L{+}1
distinct real roots. This follows from the classical result
[
5
]
that a symmetric tridiagonal matrix with all nonzero off-diagonal entries has simple eigenvalues.

6.2
In-band and out-of-band eigenvalues

The spectral band of the infinite weighted path is
[-2\gamma,2\gamma]
, corresponding to
|x|<1
. For in-band eigenvalues, the trigonometric parametrization
x=\cos\theta
with
\theta\in(0,\pi)
gives
U_{\ell}(\cos\theta)=\sin((\ell{+}1)\theta)/\sin\theta
, the standing waves along the path. The secular equation becomes

\gamma\,\sin\bigl((L{+}2)\theta\bigr)=\mu\,\sin\bigl((L{+}1)\theta\bigr),

(12)

which is crucial for the efficient eigenvalue computation described in Section
9
.

Out-of-band eigenvalues (
|x|>1
) correspond to modes exponentially localized near the bottom, with top weights that decay exponentially in
L
. These contribute negligibly to the quantum walk dynamics and can be safely excluded from the computation.

7
Top weights and the return amplitude

Having determined the eigenvalues of
H^{(\mu)}
via the secular equation, we now compute their spectral weights at the top of each tower and assemble the return amplitude that drives the quantum walk.

7.1
The top weight formula

The spectral weight of an eigenvalue at the top is the squared overlap of the corresponding normalised eigenstate with the top basis vector
|v,0\rangle
.

Proposition 7.1

(Top weight)
.

The spectral weight of eigenvalue
\lambda_{i,k}
at the top
(v,0)
is

\boxed{\;W_{i,k}(v)=\frac{p_{i}(v)}{\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}U_{\ell}(x_{i,k})^{2}},\;}

(13)

where
x_{i,k}=\lambda_{i,k}/(2\gamma)
and
p_{i}(v)=\sum_{s=1}^{m_{i}}|\langle v|\alpha_{i,s}\rangle|^{2}
. For vertex-transitive
G
:
p_{i}(v)=m_{i}/n
for all
v
.

Proof.

The normalized eigenstate is
|\hat{\Psi}_{i,k,s}\rangle=|\alpha_{i,s}\rangle\otimes|\phi_{i,k}\rangle/\|\phi_{i,k}\|
. Since
\phi_{i,k,0}=U_{0}(x_{i,k})=1
, the squared overlap with the top
|v,0\rangle
is
|\langle v|\alpha_{i,s}\rangle|^{2}/\|\phi_{i,k}\|^{2}
. Summing over degenerate states gives (
13
).
∎

The denominator admits a closed form that is essential for efficient computation:

\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}U_{\ell}(\cos\theta)^{2}=\frac{(2L{+}3)\sin\theta-\sin\bigl((2L{+}3)\theta\bigr)}{4\sin^{3}\theta}.

(14)

This follows from the identity
U_{\ell}(\cos\theta)=\sin((\ell{+}1)\theta)/\sin\theta
and the summation formula
\sum_{k=1}^{M}\sin^{2}(k\theta)=M/2-\sin((2M{+}1)\theta)/(4\sin\theta)+1/4
. The closed form evaluates in
O(1)
per eigenvalue, avoiding the
O(L)
cost of the Chebyshev recurrence.

7.2
The return amplitude

Summing the weighted eigenvalue contributions over all
n(L{+}1)=n^{2}
eigenpairs gives the return amplitude as a sum of complex exponentials.

Proposition 7.2

(Return amplitude)
.

For a vertex-transitive base graph, the return amplitude at the top is

f_{G}(t)=\langle v,0|\exp(-iA_{\mathrm{tower}}t)|v,0\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{k=1}^{L+1}W_{i,k}\,e^{-i\lambda_{i,k}t},

(15)

with
W_{i,k}=m_{i}/(n\|\phi_{i,k}\|^{2})
. All weights satisfy
W_{i,k}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i,k}W_{i,k}=1
.

8
The quantum algorithm

8.1
Problem formulation

Input:
An apex label
x_{0}=\iota((u,\epsilon))
and black-box oracle
\mathcal{O}_{G}
for the spired graph
G_{\mathrm{spire}}
.

Promise:
The hidden base graph
G
is one of
r
candidates
G_{1},\ldots,G_{r}
, all
d
-regular on
n
vertices.

Goal:
Identify
G
with probability
\geq 1-\delta
.

We assume perfect Hamiltonian simulation: given black-box access via the oracle
\mathcal{O}_{G}
, the quantum computer can implement
\exp(-iH_{\mathrm{spire}}t)
exactly for any time
t
. This is a standard idealization: decisions based on a close approximation
\widetilde{U}\approx U
remain close to those based on
U
by standard operator-norm arguments, and we suppress this analysis to focus on the structural features of the problem.

8.2
The cross-graph spectral test

Given
r
candidate base graphs
G_{1},\ldots,G_{r}
, the algorithm estimates the return amplitude
f(t)=\langle x_{0}|\exp(-iH_{\mathrm{spire}}t)|x_{0}\rangle
at a classically precomputed optimal time
t^{*}
. The classical precomputation evaluates
f_{G_{1}},\ldots,f_{G_{r}}
on a fine grid using the spectral pipeline of Section
9
, and chooses

t^{*}\;=\;\arg\max_{t\in[0,T_{\max}]}\,\min_{j\neq k}|f_{G_{j}}(t)-f_{G_{k}}(t)|.

(16)

Quantum estimation via the Hadamard test.

Let
U=\exp(-iH_{\mathrm{spire}}t^{*})
. A standard Hadamard test (Figure

) suffices: by Theorem
3.5
, the algorithm’s dynamics on the Krylov subspace of
|x_{0}\rangle
coincides with evolution under the towered-graph adjacency matrix
A_{\mathrm{tower}}
, so the single controlled-
U
at the precomputed time
t^{*}
already encodes the spectral signature of the hidden base graph. Repeating
N_{\mathrm{rep}}
times in each branch yields an estimator
\widetilde{f}\in\mathbb{C}
with
\widetilde{f}\approx f(t^{*})
. Structurally, this echoes the quantum-feature paradigm of Havlíček et al.
[
9
]
: the quantum subroutine estimates a single complex number that would be classically expensive to compute, and the subsequent decision is purely classical.

Remark 8.1

(Pair-tournament alternative)
.

For
r>2
, an alternative to the single global
t^{*}
is a pairwise tournament that runs a Hadamard test at each pair’s optimal
t^{*}_{j,k}=\arg\max_{t}|f_{G_{j}}(t)-f_{G_{k}}(t)|
and outputs the candidate that wins all
r-1
of its contests. This trades
\binom{r}{2}
Hadamard-test instances for the freedom to use each pair’s own optimal time, and can be more efficient when no single
t^{*}
distinguishes all pairs simultaneously well. We do not pursue this trade-off here.

8.3
Measurement budget

Definition 8.2

(Peak distinguishability)
.

The peak distinguishability of
r
candidate graphs over the time horizon
[0,T_{\max}]
is

\mathrm{Dis}(m)=\max_{t\in[0,T_{\max}]}\min_{j\neq k}|f_{G_{j}}(t)-f_{G_{k}}(t)|
.

Theorem 8.3

(Measurement budget)
.

For the cross-graph spectral test of Section
8.2
, success probability
\geq 1-\delta
is achieved by repeating the Hadamard test

N_{\mathrm{rep}}\;=\;\left\lceil\frac{2z^{2}}{\mathrm{Dis}(m)^{2}}\right\rceil

(17)

times in each of the real and imaginary branches, where
z=\Phi^{-1}(1-\delta/(r-1))
.

Proof.

The Hadamard test of Section
8.2
returns a complex estimator
\widetilde{f}
whose real and imaginary parts are independent unbiased estimators of
\mathrm{Re}\,f(t^{*})
and
\mathrm{Im}\,f(t^{*})
, each with Gaussian noise of standard deviation at most
1/\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{rep}}}
. Under the true hypothesis
j^{*}\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
, the true value lies at
f_{G_{j^{*}}}(t^{*})
, separated from each other candidate prediction
f_{G_{k}}(t^{*})
(
k\neq j^{*}
) by at least
\mathrm{Dis}(m)
in
\mathbb{C}
(by definition of
t^{*}
). The decision rule misclassifies only if
\widetilde{f}
falls closer to some wrong prediction
f_{G_{k}}(t^{*})
than to
f_{G_{j^{*}}}(t^{*})
, which for that
k
requires a noise displacement of magnitude
\geq\mathrm{Dis}(m)/2
. By the Gaussian tail bound, each such pairwise misclassification event has probability
\leq 2\Phi(-\mathrm{Dis}(m)\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{rep}}}/2)
; a union bound over the
r-1
wrong candidates gives a total of
\leq 2(r-1)\Phi(-\mathrm{Dis}(m)\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{rep}}}/2)
, and setting this below
\delta
gives (
17
).
∎

8.4
The Parseval sum and choice of time horizon

The difference signal
\Delta f(t)=f_{G_{A}}(t)-f_{G_{B}}(t)=\sum_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}e^{-i\lambda t}
is a sum of
\Theta(n^{2})
complex exponentials, where
c_{\lambda}=W_{\lambda}^{(G_{A})}-W_{\lambda}^{(G_{B})}
is the difference of top weights at eigenvalue
\lambda
.

Definition 8.4

(Parseval sum)
.

The
Parseval sum
of two base graphs
G_{A},G_{B}
is the time-averaged squared distinguishability:

\mathrm{Par}^{\Delta}\;=\;\mathrm{Par}^{\Delta}_{A,B}\;=\;\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}|\Delta f(t)|^{2}\,dt.

(18)

Proposition 8.5

(Parseval identity)
.

\mathrm{Par}^{\Delta}\;=\;\sum_{\lambda}|c_{\lambda}|^{2},

(19)

where
c_{\lambda}=W_{\lambda}^{(G_{A})}-W_{\lambda}^{(G_{B})}
is the weight difference at eigenvalue
\lambda
(with
c_{\lambda}=W_{\lambda}^{(G_{A})}
if
\lambda\notin\sigma(H_{G_{B}})
, and similarly with the sign reversed).

Proof.

Expand
|\Delta f(t)|^{2}=\sum_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}c_{\lambda}\bar{c}_{\lambda^{\prime}}e^{-i(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})t}
. The time average over
[0,T]
gives
|c_{\lambda}|^{2}
for the diagonal terms (
\lambda=\lambda^{\prime}
) and
O(1/(T|\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|))
for off-diagonal terms, which vanish as
T\to\infty
.
∎

Proposition 8.6

(Peak vs. Parseval bound)
.

For any time horizon
T_{\max}
large enough that the Cesàro average is close to its limit,

\mathrm{Dis}(m)^{2}\;=\;\max_{t\in[0,T_{\max}]}|\Delta f(t)|^{2}\;\geq\;\mathrm{Par}^{\Delta}\,(1-o(1)).

(20)

Proof.

The time average of
|\Delta f(t)|^{2}
converges to
\mathrm{Par}^{\Delta}
, and the time average cannot exceed the maximum.
∎

Remark 8.7

(Multi-candidate generalization)
.

For
r>2
candidates, the Parseval analysis above applies to each pair
(G_{j},G_{k})
separately, with the per-pair Parseval
\mathrm{Par}^{\Delta}_{j,k}
providing the natural per-pair scale; sharp bounds on the global max-min
\mathrm{Dis}(m)
of Definition
8.2
are left for future work.

The ratio
\mathrm{Dis}(m)^{2}/\mathrm{Par}^{\Delta}
quantifies the
constructive-interference advantage
: by how much the single-time algorithm (measuring at the optimal time
t^{*}
) outperforms a strategy based on the time-averaged signal.

Resolving the constructive interference requires a time horizon long enough to separate the relevant frequency differences. The eigenvalue spacing within a single channel (fixed
\mu
) is
\Theta(1/n)
, while the finest inter-channel spacings can be
\Theta(1/n^{2})
, suggesting the
quadratic
time horizon

T_{\max}=c\cdot m^{2}

(21)

with a moderate constant
c\geq 1
. Numerical experiments confirm
c=1
is both necessary and sufficient:
\mathrm{Dis}(m)
drops by roughly a factor of four at
c=0.5
(e.g., from
0.249
to
0.068
at
m=8
, in line with the
\Omega(n^{2})
Fourier-uncertainty bound) and saturates to numerical precision for any
c\geq 1
. We adopt
T_{\max}=m^{2}
throughout.

Remark 8.8

(Classical precomputation cost)
.

The classical precomputation of
t^{*}
requires evaluating the return amplitude at
O(m^{2})
time points, at cost
O(n^{2})
per evaluation (or
O(n^{2}\log n)
total using the nonuniform fast Fourier transform (NUFFT)), for a total of
O(n^{4})
operations with the brute-force method or
O(n^{2}\log^{2}n)
with the NUFFT. Since this is a one-time computation performed before any quantum queries, it does not affect the quantum complexity. The search over
t
is also trivially parallelizable across time intervals.

9
Efficient classical computation

The spectral decomposition of Sections
5
–
6
transforms the problem of evaluating the return amplitude from an exponentially large matrix exponential into a structured computation that can be performed in polynomial time. We describe two complementary implementations: the
direct method
, which constructs and diagonalizes the full towered graph Hamiltonian without invoking any spectral theory, and the
SERF method
(secular equation root-finding), which exploits the full theoretical machinery to scale to large
m
. Agreement between the two at small
m
validates the entire theoretical chain.

9.1
The computational pipeline

The evaluation of
f_{G}(t)
at
T
time points proceeds in two stages:

Stage 1: Spectral data.
For each of the
n
base-graph eigenvalues
\mu_{j}
, find the
n
eigenvalues
\lambda_{j,k}
of the path Hamiltonian
H^{(\mu_{j})}
and compute the top weights
W_{j,k}
. The total spectral data consists of
n^{2}
eigenvalue–weight pairs
\{(\lambda_{j,k},W_{j,k})\}
.

Stage 2: Signal evaluation.
Evaluate
f(t)=\sum_{j,k}W_{j,k}\,e^{-i\lambda_{j,k}t}
at all
T
time points.

We have implemented two principal methods, described below.

9.2
The direct method: full towered graph diagonalization

The direct method is the
ground-truth
implementation that performs no spectral analysis: it just constructs the
n^{2}\times n^{2}
towered-graph adjacency matrix
A_{\mathrm{tower}}=A\otimes|L\rangle\langle L|+I_{n}\otimes P
(the tensor-product form of Proposition
5.2
) and diagonalizes it with a single call to
numpy.linalg.eigh
. The top weight for eigenstate
|\psi_{k}\rangle
is simply

W_{k}=|\langle v{=}0,\,\ell{=}0|\psi_{k}\rangle|^{2},

the squared magnitude of the first component of the
k
-th eigenvector. No channel decomposition, no Chebyshev formulas, no secular equation; just one matrix diagonalization. This makes the direct method the most trustworthy validation tool: any error in the spectral theory of Sections
5
–
6
would show up as a discrepancy between the direct and SERF results.

The limitation is computational: the matrix has dimension
n(L{+}1)=n^{2}
, so the cost is
O(n^{6})
and memory is
O(n^{4})
. In practice, the direct method is feasible for
m\leq 32
(matrix dimension
4096
,
\sim 30
s) and borderline at
m=64
(dimension
16384
).

9.3
The SERF method: secular equation root-finding

The production method exploits the full theoretical machinery. For Stage 1, it bypasses matrix diagonalization entirely by solving the secular equation in
\theta
-space. Setting
x=\cos\theta
with
\theta\in(0,\pi)
, the secular equation (
11
) becomes
G(\theta)=\gamma\sin((L{+}2)\theta)-\mu\sin((L{+}1)\theta)=0
.

The
L{+}1
in-band roots are bracketed by Cauchy interlacing: the
(L{+}1)\times(L{+}1)
path Hamiltonian
H^{(\mu)}
has a leading
L\times L
principal submatrix whose eigenvalues are
2\gamma\cos(k\pi/(L{+}1))
for
k=1,\ldots,L
. By the interlacing theorem, each interval
[k\pi/(L{+}1),\,(k{+}1)\pi/(L{+}1)]
contains at most one eigenvalue. We set up an
(n)\times(L{+}1)
array of bracket pairs and perform
vectorized bisection
: all
n(L{+}1)
brackets are bisected simultaneously, with 53 bisection steps achieving full double precision. Top weights are computed via the closed-form norm sum (
14
) in
\theta
-space. The total cost of Stage 1 is
O(n^{2})
with a small constant.

For Stage 2, we observe that
f(t)=\sum_{k}W_{k}\,e^{-i\lambda_{k}t}
has the form of a
type-3 nonuniform discrete Fourier transform
. We use the FINUFFT library
[
7
,
8
]
(Flatiron Institute Nonuniform Fast Fourier Transform), which computes such sums in
O((N+T)\log(N+T)\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon))
operations, reducing Stage 2 from
O(n^{2}T)
to
O(n^{2}\log n)
.

Remark 9.1

(Memory-efficient chunking for large
m
)
.

For
m=4096
(
n=8192
), the spectral data consists of
\sim 134
million active eigenvalue–weight pairs per graph pair, and the coarse time grid has
\sim 33.5
million points. The SERF method addresses this by
chunking
both the time points (during the coarse scan,
\sim 4\times 10^{6}
per block) and the eigenvalues (during refinement,
\sim 30\times 10^{6}
per block). Since
f(t)=\sum_{k}W_{k}e^{-i\lambda_{k}t}
is a linear sum, the eigenvalue blocks can be computed independently and accumulated. This reduces peak memory from
\sim 6
GB to
\sim 1.5
GB per NUFFT call, enabling execution on free-tier cloud platforms.

Remark 9.2

(Why FINUFFT)
.

We chose FINUFFT because it is (i) optimized for double precision with guaranteed error bounds, (ii) available as a compiled C++ library with a Python interface (
pip install finufft
), and (iii) widely used in computational physics and signal processing. The NUFFT acceleration is critical for large
m
: at
m=2048
, the brute-force signal evaluation would require
\sim 10^{14}
operations; the NUFFT reduces this to
\sim 10^{8}
.

9.4
Peak finding with refinement

To locate
\mathrm{Dis}(m)=\max_{t}|\Delta f(t)|
accurately despite the large time horizon
T_{\max}=m^{2}
, we employ a two-stage strategy:

Coarse scan.
Evaluate
|\Delta f(t)|
on a uniform grid with spacing
\delta t=0.5
over
[0,T_{\max}]
, yielding
O(m^{2})
grid points. The NUFFT makes this affordable even for
m=4096
(
\sim 33.5\times 10^{6}
grid points). We identify the top
3
–
5
candidate peaks.

Refinement.
Around each candidate peak, we evaluate
|\Delta f(t)|
on a fine grid with spacing
\delta t=0.01
over a window of width
\pm 10
, then
\pm 5
. This resolves the peak to 4–5 significant digits beyond the coarse-scan estimate. We verified by convergence testing (varying
\delta t
from 0.5 down to 0.001) that
\delta t=0.01
is more than sufficient: the relative change in
\mathrm{Dis}(m)
between
\delta t=0.01
and
\delta t=0.001
is below
10^{-6}
for all
m
.

is due to out-of-band eigenvalues whose top weights decay exponentially with

m=32
the agreement is at machine precision.

All computations in this paper, including the largest instance
m=5121
(
n=10242
,
{\sim}210
million eigenvalue–weight pairs), complete in under 20 minutes on a consumer desktop with a 6-core Intel Core i5 CPU and 32 GB of RAM.

\mathrm{Dis}(m)\;\geq\;\sqrt{\mathrm{Par}^{\Delta}}\;=\;\Omega(1/n).

The peak follows a closed-form approximation.

\mathrm{Dis}(m)

to within

across

with

R^{2}\approx 0.91
on the odd points (each fit on

\approx 1.3\%
, and the parity oscillation is fluctuation around a common trend rather than a difference between two scaling laws. We also tried the alternative one-parameter laws
C\sqrt{\log_{2}m}
,
C(\log_{2}m)^{3/2}

in the range

The intuition is constructive interference: the difference signal
\Delta f(t)
is a sum of
\Theta(n^{2})
complex exponentials with amplitudes
\Theta(1/n^{2})
; at the optimal time

,
\Theta(n^{2}/\log n)
of the spectral phases align constructively, producing a peak that exceeds the root-mean-square level

by a factor of

. Figure

\sqrt{1.4\log_{2}32}\approx 2.65
. The logarithmic growth reflects the increasing difficulty of global phase alignment as the number of components grows.

Combining the empirical scalings
\mathrm{Par}^{\Delta}=(3-o(1))/n^{2}
and
\mathrm{Dis}(m)^{2}/\mathrm{Par}^{\Delta}=\Theta(\log_{2}m)
with the measurement budget of Theorem
8.3
gives the sample size

N_{\mathrm{rep}}\;=\;\frac{2z^{2}}{\mathrm{Dis}(m)^{2}}\;\approx\;\frac{z^{2}n^{2}}{2\log_{2}n}\;=\;\widetilde{O}\!\left(\frac{n^{2}}{\log n}\right).

(23)

Each shot evolves the Hamiltonian for time
T_{\max}=m^{2}=\Theta(n^{2})
on a
(D{+}1)
-sparse oracle, at quantum cost
\widetilde{O}(n^{2})
per shot by sparse Hamiltonian simulation
[
6
]
. The total quantum cost is therefore
N_{\mathrm{rep}}\cdot\widetilde{O}(n^{2})=\widetilde{O}(n^{4})
. We record this as our central conjecture of efficiency.

Conjecture 10.1

(Quantum efficiency for prism vs. Möbius)
.

For the prism-vs-Möbius identification problem, the cross-graph spectral test of Section
8.2
achieves success probability
\geq 1-\delta
in
\widetilde{O}(n^{2}/\log n)
oracle queries and
\widetilde{O}(n^{4})
quantum gates.

The conjecture is supported by
80

m=4
to
m=5121
(so
n
up to
10242
), with cross-validation between the direct method (theory-free ground truth) and the SERF method on the overlap range
m\leq 33
. Among the candidate one-parameter laws of the form
C(\log m)^{p}
,
0<p\leq 2
, the data favour

but do not formally exclude
\sqrt{\log m}
,
(\log m)^{3/2}
, or
\log m\cdot\log\log m

11
Conjecture of classical hardness

The quantum algorithm of Sections
8
–
10
identifies a hidden
d
-regular base graph using
O(n^{2}/\log n)
queries to the obfuscating oracle. We now turn to the classical side. The construction of
G_{\mathrm{spire}}
in Section
2
was designed precisely so that the base graph
G
is hidden from any classical algorithm with only oracle access: spires of height
L
separate the apex of the distinguished spire from the lifted-graph layer where the structure of
G
lives, and the random alternating cycles
C_{e}
together with the random labelling
\iota
destroy all positional information. We conjecture that this hiding is exponentially effective.

This section is organized around the cleanest concrete instance of the question (distinguishing
\mathcal{O}_{Y_{m}}
from
\mathcal{O}_{M_{m}}
for
m
odd) rather than around the most general statement. Specialising to a single concrete pair sharpens the conjecture: the only difference between
Y_{m}
and
M_{m}
is the parallel-vs-twisted choice of two closing rail edges, hidden inside an exponentially large spired graph, accessed only through the obfuscating oracle.

11.1
The distinguishing problem

Fix two
d
-regular base graphs
G_{A},G_{B}
on the same vertex set
V
with the same distinguished vertex
u\in V
, and the security parameter
L\geq 1
of Section
2.3
. Nature samples a bit
b\in\{A,B\}
uniformly at random, builds the spired graph for
G_{b}
with fresh independent random cycles
\mathbf{C}
and labelling
\iota
, and exposes the resulting obfuscating oracle
\mathcal{O}_{G_{b}}
. A classical algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, given the seed label
\iota(a_{u})
, makes at most
t
adaptive oracle queries and outputs a guess
\hat{b}\in\{A,B\}
. Its
advantage
is

\operatorname{Adv}(\mathcal{A})\;:=\;\Pr[\hat{b}=b]-\tfrac{1}{2}.

We are interested in how
\operatorname{Adv}(\mathcal{A})
scales with the query budget
t
, the security parameter
L
, and the graph sizes.

11.2
The conjecture

Conjecture 11.1

(Classical exponential lower bound)
.

Let
m
be odd,
m\geq 5
, and set

G_{A}\;:=\;Y_{m},\qquad G_{B}\;:=\;M_{m},\qquad u\;:=\;\bigl((m{-}1)/2,\,0\bigr),

in the pair coordinates of Section
4.1
, with security parameter
L\geq 1
arbitrary. There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that every classical
t
-query algorithm
\mathcal{A}
with access to the obfuscating oracle
\mathcal{O}_{G_{b}}
satisfies

\operatorname{Adv}(\mathcal{A})\;\leq\;C\cdot\frac{t^{2}}{D^{L}}.

In particular, achieving constant advantage requires

t\;=\;\Omega\bigl(D^{L/2}\bigr)\;=\;\Omega\bigl(6^{L/2}\bigr),

exponential in the security parameter
L
.

Remark 11.2

(Why
m
odd and
u
symmetric)
.

By Proposition
4.7
, with
m
odd the distinguished vertex
u=((m{-}1)/2,0)
sits at equal graph distance
(m{-}1)/2
from the two outer-rail closing-edge endpoints
(0,0)
and
(m{-}1,0)
, and at this maximal distance the two “sides” of the graph (clockwise and counter-clockwise from
u
) are interchangeable. This is the worst case for any classical adversary: the parallel-vs-twisted distinction lives entirely in the differing
4
-cycle, which is sandwiched between two indistinguishable halves of the rail. For
m
even this symmetric placement is unavailable (Proposition
4.7
, last clause): the closest closing-edge endpoint sits one step closer than the farther one, breaking the left-right symmetry. Conjecture
11.1
is stated for the
m
-odd case where the symmetric placement is genuinely available; the quantum algorithm of Section
8
, by contrast, is insensitive to the parity of
m
and applies at every
m\geq 4
. We expect the same exponential classical lower bound to hold for
m
even, with a proof that controls the
O(1)
rail asymmetry of Proposition
4.7
.

Remark 11.3

(Comparison to the welded-trees problem)
.

The welded-trees problem of Childs et al.
[
1
]
is structurally close to our setting at
G=K_{2}
: it shares the same
obfuscation mechanism
(random alternating cycles plus random labelling) and is classically hard via an exponential traversal lower bound. It is not literally a special case of our identification problem (at
G=K_{2}
there is only a single base graph, so nothing to identify); Conjecture
11.1
extrapolates the welded-trees hardness from traversal to identification.

Remark 11.4

(Generalisation beyond
Y_{m}
vs
M_{m}
)
.

We expect that exponential quantum-classical separations of the type stated by Conjecture
11.1
extend to many other pairs of
d
-regular base graphs, plausibly including pairs differing only in a small set of local edges with the distinguished vertex placed far from them. Identifying the precise structural conditions on a graph pair that enable both the quantum efficiency and the conjectured classical hardness is an open question for future work. Conjecture
11.1
is stated only for the prism-vs-Möbius pair because here the shared common-edge structure (Section
4.2
) makes the geometric setup and the symmetric placement of
u
unambiguous.

12
Conclusions and open problems

Conjectures
10.1
and
11.1
together would yield an exponential quantum–classical separation for hidden-base-graph identification in the black-box model. We close with several open directions stemming from this work.

Other graph families.

Our numerics focus on prism vs. Möbius ladder graphs (
3
-regular,
n=2m
). Both are vertex-transitive, which simplifies the analysis by ensuring that the return amplitude
f_{G}(t)
is the same for every starting vertex. More generally, the spectral decomposition and the SERF method apply without modification to any pair of
d
-regular base graphs (only the adjacency matrix
A
changes), so one could study arbitrary vertex-transitive graphs. Natural extensions include higher-degree regular graphs (
d\geq 4
), where
D=2d
changes the path weight
\gamma=\sqrt{D}/2
, and graphs with richer spectral structure. Ramanujan graphs, which are optimal expanders in the sense that all non-trivial eigenvalues satisfy
|\mu|\leq 2\sqrt{d-1}
, are a particularly interesting family: their tight spectral concentration may produce different scaling behavior for
\mathrm{Dis}(m)
and
\mathrm{Par}^{\Delta}
compared to prisms and ladders, which have eigenvalues spread across the full interval
[-d,d]
.

Alternative expansion structures.

In our construction, the spires (balanced
D
-ary trees of height
L
) play a dual role. On the quantum side, a walker starting at the single apex
a_{v}
coherently spreads its amplitude down the spire to all
D^{L}
copies of
v
at the foundation, so the effective dynamics reduce to the polynomial-dimensional towered graph
G_{\mathrm{tower}}
. On the classical side, the spires confuse any local algorithm: a classical walker cannot distinguish between retreating toward the apex, moving through the lifted edges of the base graph, or entering a spire attached to a neighboring vertex. The natural question is whether structures other than balanced spires can serve this dual purpose. Any replacement must (i) funnel the quantum amplitude from a single entrance vertex into an equal superposition over all copies of a base-graph vertex, producing a low-dimensional invariant subspace, while (ii) remaining locally indistinguishable from the rest of the graph to a classical observer. Understanding which alternatives preserve (or break) the quantum speedup is an important structural question.

Identification vs. traversal and the exit-finding problem.

Our work differs from the welded-trees problem
[
1
]
and its generalizations
[
10
,
11
]
in a fundamental way: those results solve
traversal
problems (reaching a designated exit vertex), whereas our algorithm solves an
identification
problem (determining which base graph is hidden). A natural problem that bridges the two is
exit-finding
: given oracle access to
G_{\mathrm{spire}}
and the seed apex label, find the remaining apices (the apices corresponding to base-graph vertices other than the seed). For different base graphs, the number and arrangement of apices differ, and the difficulty of exit-finding may depend on the spectral properties of the hidden graph.

Beyond black-box separations.

Like the welded-trees result of Childs et al.
[
1
]
and the subsequent hierarchical-graph and pathfinding extensions
[
10
,
11
]
, our work is a
black-box separation
(Conjectures
10.1
and
11.1
are relative to the obfuscating oracle). The open challenge in quantum algorithms is to translate such oracle separations into concrete computational advantages, as the hidden subgroup framework does for the discrete logarithm problem (where Shor’s algorithm achieves an exponential speedup for a problem of direct cryptographic relevance). It remains an important goal to find a natural computational problem whose mathematical structure contains an exponentially large graph with hidden global symmetry of the kind described above. Detecting that symmetry would translate the oracle separation into a concrete computational advantage.
