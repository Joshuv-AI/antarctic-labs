NOP2-mediated m5C methylation impairs mitophagy and aggravates acute lung injury by targeting PINK1
Authors: Xiaoyuan Chen, Rui Zhao, Hong Zhu, Yang Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis-induced acute lung injury (ALI) involves complex pathological mechanisms. 5-methylcytosine (m5C) RNA modification, catalyzed by methyltransferases like NOP2, plays a crucial role in regulating inflammation and cellular processes. However, the role of NOP2 and its potential regulation of m5C modification in sepsis-induced ALI remains unclear. An in vitro ALI model was established by treating human pulmonary epithelial A549 cells with lipopolysaccharide (LPS). Inflammatory cytokine levels (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) were measured by ELISA. Apoptosis was assessed by flow cytometry. Mitophagy was evaluated via immunofluorescence staining for mitochondrial Parkin and western blot analysis of Parkin, LC3-II, COX IV, and p62. The m5C modification of PINK1 mRNA was analyzed by m5C-RIP-PCR. The specific m5C site was identified using bioinformatics and validated by dual-luciferase reporter assays. LPS treatment significantly upregulated NOP2 expression in A549 cells. Knockdown of NOP2 attenuated LPS-induced inflammation, apoptosis, and promoted mitophagy, as evidenced by increased Parkin translocation, elevated LC3-II levels, and decreased p62 and COX IV levels. Mechanistically, NOP2 knockdown reduced m5C modification on PINK1 mRNA, particularly at site 197, thereby enhancing PINK1 mRNA stability and increasing its expression. Furthermore, knockdown of PINK1 reversed the protective effects of NOP2 knockdown on inflammation, apoptosis, and mitophagy in LPS-treated A549 cells. NOP2 is upregulated in LPS-induced ALI models. Its knockdown alleviates cellular injury by reducing the m5C modification of PINK1 mRNA, which enhances PINK1 expression and promotes mitophagy. The NOP2/m5C/PINK1 axis represents a novel regulatory pathway in sepsis-induced ALI, suggesting potential therapeutic targets for its treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56994-5.Keywords: Sepsis, Acute lung injury, NOP2, PINK1Subject terms: Cell biology, Diseases, Molecular biology

Introduction

Sepsis, a systemic inflammatory response syndrome triggered by infection, frequently leads to multiple organ dysfunction, particularly acute lung injury (ALI) and its more severe form, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)1. As a major indirect cause of ALI, sepsis involves complex and multifactorial pathological mechanisms. Studies indicate that the development of ALI significantly impacts the prognosis of septic patients2,3. In recent years, research on sepsis-associated ALI has increased, revealing numerous potential interventional targets. For instance, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have shown promise in mitigating sepsis-induced ALI, primarily by modulating inflammatory responses and oxidative stress4. Therefore, elucidating the specific molecular mechanisms underlying sepsis-induced ALI is crucial for improving clinical outcomes.

5-Methylcytosine (m5C) is a widespread RNA modification, primarily catalyzed by RNA methyltransferases, such as members of the NOP2/Sun (NSUN) family, which add a methyl group to the fifth carbon of cytosine residues5. This process not only alters the chemical properties of RNA but also significantly impacts RNA stability, translation efficiency, and interactions with other molecules6,7. Nucleolar Protein 2 (NOP2), an RNA methyltransferase, is primarily responsible for catalyzing m5C formation. Research demonstrates that NOP2 interacts with various RNA species, regulating their stability and translation, thereby influencing cellular functions such as proliferation and migration8,9. Recent studies have found that elevated m5C levels are associated with enhanced injury and inflammation in pulmonary epithelial cells, potentially through m5C-mediated regulation of genes involved in cell proliferation and inflammation10. Furthermore, m5C modification is closely linked to metabolic reprogramming, influencing cellular metabolic states by modulating the stability of specific transcripts, thereby playing a role in the pathogenesis and progression of lung injury11. Inhibiting the expression of m5C methyltransferases has been shown to alleviate the progression of sepsis-induced ALI12. However, the alteration of NOP2 expression in ALI and the role of its mediated m5C modifications in this context remain unclear.

Mitophagy, a selective autophagy process for removing damaged mitochondria, has recently emerged as a mechanism closely associated with the development and progression of lung injury13. PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1) is a key regulator of mitophagy. The primary role of PINK1 is to monitor mitochondrial health and, upon detecting dysfunction, recruit the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin to facilitate the clearance of damaged mitochondria, thereby maintaining cellular metabolic homeostasis and functional integrity14. Studies suggest that PINK1-mediated mitophagy plays a significant role in various lung injury-related diseases. For example, in ALI models, PINK1 expression correlates with mitophagic activity, and PINK1 deficiency leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and cell death15,16. Additionally, during the progression of gestational diabetes mellitus, PINK1 expression is regulated by m5C modification, resulting in disrupted mitophagy17. Nevertheless, whether PINK1 is similarly regulated by m5C modification in ALI remains unknown.

Therefore, this study aims to establish an in vitro cellular model of ALI to investigate the regulatory mechanism of NOP2-mediated m5C modification on PINK1 expression. We seek to elucidate the role of the NOP2/PINK1 signaling axis in regulating inflammatory responses and mitophagy during ALI progression. This work is expected to provide novel insights for future clinical management of sepsis-induced acute lung injury.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

A549 cells were purchased from Procell (Wuhan, China). A549 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM; Gibco, New York, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin. All cells were cultured in an incubator with 5% CO2 and 37 °C.

For ALI model establishment, A549 cells were treated with LPS (1 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) for 6, 12, 24 and 48 h, respectively18.

For NOP2 and PINK1 knockdown, A549 cells were grouped and transfected with plasmids carrying short hairpin RNA targeting NOP2 or PINK1 (shNOP2 or shPINK1) and their negative controls (sh-NC). The cell transfection was carried out following the protocols of lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA). The knockdown efficiency were verified by RT-qPCR and collected for next experiments.

CCK-8 assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo, Japan). A549 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well and subjected to the indicated treatments. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Bio-Tek, USA). Cell viability was calculated as the percentage relative to the control group (set as 100%).

LDH release determination

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release was measured using a commercially available LDH cytotoxicity assay kit (Beyotime, China). After the indicated treatments, the culture supernatant was collected from each well. Following the manufacturer’s protocol, 120 µL of the supernatant was transferred to a new 96-well plate, mixed with 60 µL of LDH working solution, and incubated at room temperature for 30 min in the dark. The absorbance at 490 nm was recorded using a microplate reader. LDH release (%) was calculated as (experimental release – spontaneous release) / (maximum release – spontaneous release) × 100%.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) determination

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; Beyotime, China). After treatment, A549 cells were harvested, washed twice with pre-warmed PBS, and incubated with DCFH-DA (10 µM) in serum-free DMEM at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. The cells were then washed three times with PBS to remove extracellular probe. Fluorescence intensity was immediately analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD FACSCelesta) with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and emission at 525 nm. A total of 10,000 events were collected per sample, and the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was used to quantify ROS levels. Data were processed using FlowJo software.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) determination

MMP (ΔΨm) was assessed using the JC-1 probe (Beyotime, China). Following the indicated treatments, A549 cells were harvested, washed once with PBS, and resuspended in 0.5 mL of cell culture medium. Then, 0.5 mL of JC-1 staining working solution was added and mixed gently. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark, then centrifuged (600 × g, 4 °C, 5 min), washed twice with JC-1 staining buffer, and finally resuspended in 0.5 mL of staining buffer. Fluorescence was measured using a flow cytometer (BD FACSCelesta) with excitation at 488 nm. JC-1 monomers (depolarized mitochondria) emit green fluorescence (FL1 channel, ~ 529 nm), whereas JC-1 aggregates (polarized mitochondria) emit red fluorescence (FL2 channel, ~ 590 nm). MMP was expressed as the ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity (red/green ratio), which reflects the mitochondrial membrane potential level. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from A549 cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of the RNA were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. One microgram of total RNA from each sample was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using a PrimeScript RT reagent kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara Bio, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR was subsequently performed on a QuantStudio Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (Takara Bio). The reaction mixtures were incubated at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. The relative expression level of NOP2 and PINK1 was normalized to the endogenous control β-actin (ACTB) and calculated using the comparative 2–ΔΔCt method. The sequences of the primers used were as follows:

NOP2 forward: 5′-AAGGGTGCCGAGACAGAACT-3′

NOP2 reverse: 5′-GAGCACGACTAGACAGCCTC-3′

PINK1 forward: 5′-CAAGAGAGGTCCCAAGCAAC-3′

PINK1 reverse: 5′-GGCAGCACATCAGGGTAGTC-3′

ACTB forward: 5′-ACGGCATCGTCACCAACTG-3′

ACTB reverse: 5′-CAAACATGATCTGGGTCATCTTCTC-3′

Western blot

A549 cells were harvested and lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. The protein concentration of the lysates was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, Nantong, China). Equal amounts of protein (20 µg per lane) were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-Parkin (1:1000; ab77924, Abcam, USA), anti-LC3 (1:2000; ab192890, Abcam), anti-COX IV (1:1000; ab16056, Abcam), anti-p62 (1:1500; ab207305, Abcam), anti-PINK1 (1:1000; ab186303, Abcam) and ATCB (1:5000; ab6276, Abcam). After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with corresponding secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, Beyotime) substrate and imaged with a chemiluminescence imaging system. Protein levels were normalized to β-actin as a loading control.

ELISA

The interleukin 1β (IL-1β; SP10180), IL-6 (SP10234), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α; SP10205) in the cells were detected by ELISA kits from Wuhan Saipei Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China).

Apoptosis assay by flow cytometry

Cell apoptosis was detected using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, A549 cells were harvested after the respective treatments, washed twice with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in 1× binding buffer. The cell suspension was then incubated with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) in the dark for 15 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the apoptotic rates were immediately analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD FACSCelesta). The data were processed with FlowJo software, and the cells were categorized as follows: viable cells (Annexin V−/PI−), early apoptotic cells (Annexin V+/PI−), late apoptotic cells (Annexin V+/PI+), and necrotic cells (Annexin V−/PI+). The gating strategy was as follows: cellular debris and doublets were first excluded using forward scatter (FSC) vs. side scatter (SSC) and FSC-A vs. FSC-H plots, respectively. Apoptotic cells were then distinguished based on Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) staining: viable cells (Annexin V−/PI−, lower left quadrant), early apoptotic cells (Annexin V+/PI−, lower right quadrant), late apoptotic cells (Annexin V+/PI+, upper right quadrant), and necrotic cells (Annexin V−/PI+, upper left quadrant).

Immunofluorescence staining

A549 cells were incubated with MitoTracker Deep Red FM (200 nM; Invitrogen) in pre-warmed culture medium at 37 ℃ for 30 min to label mitochondria. Subsequently, the cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After blocking with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with a primary antibody against Parkin (1:200; ab77924, Abcam, USA). The cells were then washed and incubated with an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (1:500; Invitrogen, A11034) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 µg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min. The coverslips were mounted onto glass slides using ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant. Images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 880) and processed with ImageJ software.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

To investigate the interaction between NOP2 and PINK1 mRNA, RIP was performed using a Magna RIP™ RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Millipore, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A549 cells were harvested and lysed in complete RIP lysis buffer. The cell lysate was incubated with magnetic beads conjugated with 5 µg of anti-NOP2 antibody (Abcam, USA) or normal mouse IgG (Millipore, USA) as a negative control, and rotated overnight at 4 °C. After extensive washing with RIP wash buffer, the RNA-protein complexes were digested with proteinase K, and the co-precipitated RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. Purified RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and the presence of PINK1 mRNA was detected by RT-qPCR using the specific primers for PINK1 described above. The enrichment of PINK1 mRNA was calculated as the percentage of input RNA recovered, and IgG served as a control for nonspecific binding.

M5C-RIP-PCR

The m5C modification on PINK1 mRNA was assessed using the Magna MeRIP m5C Kit (Millipore, USA) with adaptations for m5C immunoprecipitation. Briefly, total RNA was isolated from A549 cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and subjected to fragmentation using RNA fragmentation buffer to generate fragments of approximately 100–200 nucleotides. A small portion of the fragmented RNA was saved as the “Input” control. The remaining RNA was incubated with 2 µg of anti-m5C antibody (Millipore) or normal mouse IgG (Millipore) as a negative control, conjugated to magnetic beads, for 2 h at 4 ℃. The bead-antibody-RNA complexes were extensively washed, and the co-precipitated RNA was then eluted and purified. The enrichment of PINK1 mRNA in both the input and m5C-immunoprecipitated samples was quantified by RT-qPCR using SYBR Green chemistry. The relative m5C enrichment of PINK1 was calculated by normalizing the amount of PINK1 in the immunoprecipitated sample to that in the corresponding input sample.

Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay.

To investigate the regulatory impact on specific sites within the PINK1 3’UTR, DNA fragments encompassing the wild-type or mutant PINK1 3’UTR (sites of interest: site 1: 196, site 2: 197, site 3: 724) were synthesized and cloned downstream of the firefly luciferase gene in the pmirGLO Dual-Luciferase miRNA Target Expression Vector (Promega, USA). A549 cells were co-transfected with the constructed reporter plasmids (wild-type or mutant) and either a shNC or an shNOP2 using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen). Forty-eight hours post-transfection, the cells were lysed, and firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured sequentially using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) on a GloMax Discover Microplate Reader (Promega). The firefly luciferase activity for each sample was normalized to the corresponding Renilla luciferase activity to control for transfection efficiency. The relative luciferase activity was then calculated by comparing the normalized value of the NOP2-knockdown group to that of the control group.

RNA stability determination

A549 cells were transfected with either a shNC or an shNOP2. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, transcription was halted by the addition of actinomycin D (5 µg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) to the culture medium. Total RNA was isolated from the cells at predetermined time points (0, 4, 8, 12 h) post-actinomycin D treatment using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Subsequently, the relative abundance of PINK1 mRNA at each time point was quantified by RT-qPCR.

Bioinformatic analysis

Genes associated with NOP2 were obtained from GeneMANIA database (https://genemania.org/). The m5C modification sites of PINK1 were obtained from RNAm5Cfinder databas (http://www.rnanut.net/rnam5cfinder/).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates (i.e., separate cell culture passages and independent transfections or treatments). For each biological replicate, technical triplicates were included in all quantitative assays (including RT-qPCR, ELISA, flow cytometry, dual-luciferase reporter assays, and m5C-RIP-qPCR), and the average value of the technical replicates was used for subsequent analysis. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) calculated from the biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0). Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using the two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

NOP2 was upregulated in LPS treated A549 cells.

In the LPS treated A549 cells, both mRNA levels (Fig. 1A) and protein levels (Fig. 1B) of NOP2 was significantly increased in a time dependent manner. In addition, after 24 h of LPS treatment, the expressions of NOP2 was the highest in the A549 cells. Furthermore, LPS treatment significantly decreased the cell viability (Fig. 1C) and increased the LDH release (Fig. 1D).Similarly, the inhibition of cell viability and promotion of LDH release were most effective at 24 h and 48 h, and there was no significant difference in cell viability and LDH release levels between 24 h and 48 h. Therefore, we selected a 24-hour condition for the subsequent functional experiments.

NOP2 knockdown inhibited the inflammation and apoptosis of LPS treated A549 cells

In the LPS treated A549 cells, shNOP2 transfection decreased the NOP2 expression levels (Fig. 2A and B). LPS treatment significantly increased the IL-1β (Fig. 2C), IL-6 (Fig. 2D), TNF-α (Fig. 2E) contents and apoptosis rate (Fig. 2F) in A549 cells. NOP2 knockdown significantly decreased the IL-1β (Fig. 2C), IL-6 (Fig. 2D), TNF-α (Fig. 2E) contents and apoptosis rate (Fig. 2F) in LPS treated A549 cells. In addition, NOP2 knockdown also decreased the MMP (Fig. 2G abnd 2 H) and ROS (Fig. 2I abnd 2 J). Furthermore, NOP2 knockdown significantly decreased the p-p65/p65, cleaved caspase-3, Bax protein levels and increased the Bcl-2 protein levels in the LPS treated A549 cells (Fig. 2K). In addition, the LPS treated A549 cells were also transfected with another shNOP2 to avoid off-target effects. The results also showed that NOP2 knockdown inhibited IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α contents and the apoptosis rate of LPS treated A549 cells (Supplementary Fig. 1) .

NOP2 knockdown promoted mitophagy in the LPS treated A549 cells

Immunofluorescence staining showed that LPS treatment significantly decreased the Parkin expressions in the mitochondrion of A549 cells, while NOP2 knockdown significantly increased it (Fig. 3A). In addition, in the LPS treated A549 cells, the protein levels of Parkin and LC3 II were decreased, COX IV and p62 were increased. NOP2 knockdown significantly increased Parkin and LC3 II protein levels, and decreased COX IV and p62 protein levels (Fig. 3B).

NOP2 knockdown increased the PINK1 levels through decreasing the m5C modification

Through the GeneMANIA database, we identified multiple genes associated with NOP2, including the PINK1 gene, which is closely associated with the occurrence of mitophagy (Fig. 4A). After NOP2 knockdown, the mRNA levels of PINK1 were increased (Fig. 4B). RIP assay confirmed that NOP2 could be directly combined with PINK1 (Fig. 4C). In addition, NOP2 knockdown decreased the m5C modification levels of PINK1 (Fig. 4D). Then, through the RNAm5Cfinder database, we obtained three m5C modification sites (site 1: 196, site 2: 197, site 3: 724) of PINK1 (Fig. 4E). We subsequently introduced mutations at three sites (site 1: 196, site 2: 197, site 3: 724) within the PINK1 RNA sequence, constructing both wild-type (wt) and mutant (mut) PINK1 variants. Dual-luciferase assays revealed that NOP2 knockdown did not significantly affect the luciferase activity of PINK1 constructs containing site 1 or site 3, but markedly enhanced the luciferase activity of the construct containing site 2. This enhancing effect was abolished when site 2 was mutated (Fig. 4F-H). These findings suggest that NOP2 likely mediates m5C modification of PINK1 at site 2. Besides, we further found that NOP2 knockdown enhanced the mRNA stability (Fig. 4I) and protein expressions (Fig. 4J) of PINK1.

PINK1 knockdown reversed the role of NOP2 knockdown in LPS treated A549 cells

Finally, we conducted rescue experiments to further demonstrate whether NOP2 affected A549 cell damage and mitophagy by regulating PINK1 expressions. After shPINK1 transfection, the PINK1 levels were significantly decreased in LPS treated A549 cells (Fig. 5A and B). In the LPS treated and shNOP2 transfected A549 cells, PINK1 knockdown increased the IL-1β (Fig. 5C), IL-6 (Fig. 5D), TNF-α (Fig. 5E) contents, apoptosis rate (Fig. 5F), and decreased the Parkin levels in mitochondrion (Fig. 5G and H). Additionally, PINK1 knockdown decreased the Parkin and LC3 II protein levels and increased the COX IV and p62 levels (Fig. 5I). Besides, PINK1 knockdown increased the MMP (Fig. 5J) and ROS (Fig. 5K and L) levels in the LPS treated and shNOP2 transfected A549 cells. Western blot results showed that PINK1 knockdown increased the p-p65/p65, cleaved-caspase-3, Bax protein levels and decreased the Bcl-2 protein levels (Fig. 5M).

Discussion

The inflammatory response has been demonstrated to be a critical mechanism in sepsis-induced lung injury, ultimately leading to the apoptosis of pulmonary epithelial cells19. Recent studies have revealed that m5C modification plays a significant regulatory role in inflammation. Evidence suggests that m5C can influence the expression of various inflammatory mediators, thereby modulating the body’s inflammatory response. For instance, Nsun2, an m5C methyltransferase, was found to be closely associated with the expression of inflammatory mediators in an asthma model. Knockdown of Nsun2 reduced the stability of Hnrnpk mRNA, consequently suppressing the expression of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α10. Furthermore, in sepsis-induced ALI, m5C modification is implicated in regulating the inflammatory response by affecting the stability of TFRC mRNA11. These findings indicate that m5C modification not only contributes to mRNA stability but also directly influences the expression of inflammatory mediators, thereby participating in regulating the intensity and duration of the inflammatory response. NOP2, a primary m5C methyltransferase, has recently been implicated in processes such as tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion9. Studies show that NOP2 is upregulated in various cancers (e.g., colorectal cancer, lung adenocarcinoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma), and its expression level is closely correlated with patient prognosis20,21. Analysis of GEO datasets (GSE57065, GSE65682, and GSE95233) by Zhang et al.22 revealed significantly elevated NOP2 expression in septic patients, suggesting its potential as a biomarker for sepsis diagnosis. However, the role of NOP2 in sepsis-induced ALI remains unclear. In this study, we established an in vitro ALI model and found that NOP2 expression was significantly increased during LPS-induced injury in A549 cells. Knockdown of NOP2 attenuated the LPS-induced inflammatory response and cell apoptosis. This suggests that beyond being a diagnostic marker for sepsis, NOP2 may actively promote the development of sepsis-induced ALI. Targeting NOP2 expression could thus represent a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating ALI progression.

Mitophagy, the selective autophagic clearance of damaged or dysfunctional mitochondria, is essential for maintaining mitochondrial quality and overall cellular health. In ALI, mitophagy serves as a crucial mechanism for eliminating damaged mitochondria, helping to maintain cellular energy homeostasis and reduce oxidative stress, thereby modulating the inflammatory response23. Enhanced mitophagy has been shown to inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation, consequently reducing inflammation levels in ALI24. Similarly, Liu et al.25 demonstrated that overexpression of TFEB promoted mitophagy and alleviated LPS-induced lung injury and inflammation. Our study found that knockdown of NOP2 increased the expression of Parkin in mitochondria and promoted the protein level of LC3II, while reducing the expression of p62 and COX IV. Parkin, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, acts as a key amplifier of the mitophagy signal by phosphorylating ubiquitin and mitochondrial outer membrane proteins, leading to extensive ubiquitination of these proteins26. LC3II, an autophagosome marker protein, is formed by lipidation of LC3I and embeds into the autophagosomal membrane; it interacts with p62 via its LIR domain, guiding ubiquitinated mitochondria into autophagosomes. p62, an autophagy receptor, uses its UBA domain to recognize ubiquitinated mitochondrial outer membrane proteins and its LIR domain to bind LC3II, forming a ‘bridge’ that connects damaged mitochondria to autophagosomes. While p62 knockdown does not affect Parkin recruitment, it impedes the clearance of damaged mitochondria27. COX IV, a core subunit of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV, serves as a marker for mitochondrial mass; its degradation and decreased expression indicate active mitophagy28. These results suggest that the inhibitory effects of NOP2 knockdown on LPS-induced inflammation and apoptosis in A549 cells may be mediated through the induction of mitophagy. Promoting mitophagy may therefore represent an effective therapeutic approach for alleviating sepsis-induced ALI.

Subsequently, bioinformatics analysis in this study revealed a high correlation between NOP2 and PINK1, and identified multiple potential m5C modification sites within the PINK1 RNA sequence. PINK1, a key regulator of mitophagy, primarily functions to monitor mitochondrial health. Upon detecting mitochondrial dysfunction, it recruits the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin to facilitate the clearance of damaged mitochondria, thereby maintaining cellular metabolic homeostasis and functional integrity29. Recent research indicates that impaired PINK1-mediated mitophagy is a significant factor in lung injury. For example, in a mouse model of LPS-induced ALI, PINK1 expression was significantly decreased, accompanied by mitochondrial dysfunction and increased apoptosis30. Conversely, PINK1 upregulation is considered an adaptive cellular response to oxidative stress and inflammation, aimed at clearing damaged mitochondria via mitophagy to alleviate cellular injury. Furthermore, studies have found that PINK1 deficiency suppresses mitophagy and exacerbates LPS-induced lung injury, underscoring the protective importance of PINK1 in lung tissue31. Our mechanistic investigations demonstrated that NOP2 knockdown reduced the m5C modification level of PINK1 RNA, consequently enhancing PINK1 expression and mRNA stability. Dual-luciferase reporter assays further suggested that the m5C modification of PINK1 by NOP2 likely occurs specifically at site 197. Finally, functional experiments revealed that knockdown of PINK1 reversed the suppressive effects of NOP2 knockdown on inflammatory response and apoptosis, as well as its promotive effect on mitophagy, in LPS-treated A549 cells. These results indicate that NOP2 knockdown likely alleviates LPS-induced inflammation and apoptosis in A549 cells by inhibiting m5C modification of PINK1, thereby upregulating its expression and initiating mitophagy.

We acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, regarding the m5C site, we used dual-luciferase assays for functional validation but lacked direct chemical evidence (e.g., bisulfite sequencing). Second, we did not perform autophagic flux assays using bafilomycin A1/chloroquine, which would strengthen the mitophagy conclusion. Third, we did not employ animal models or primary cells. Future studies using in vivo models, bisulfite sequencing and autophagic flux assays are warranted to further validate the NOP2/m5C/PINK1 axis and its therapeutic potential in ALI.

In summary, this study identified consistently elevated NOP2 expression in an in vitro ALI model. Knockdown of NOP2 alleviated LPS-induced inflammatory response and apoptosis in A549 cells. Mechanistic analyses demonstrated that NOP2 knockdown mediates its protective effects by reducing m5C modification of PINK1 RNA, which enhances PINK1 expression and promotes mitophagy, ultimately mitigating cellular injury in A549 cells.
