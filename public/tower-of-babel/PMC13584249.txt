Barriers and facilitators to implement innovations in Canadian long-term care facilities: a longitudinal study of organizational readiness for knowledge translation
Authors: Marie-Soleil Hardy, Randa Attieh, Maxime Sasseville, Maude Laberge, Vincent Couture, Machelle Wilchesky, André Côté, Clémence Dallaire, Marie-Pierre Gagnon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic negatively affected residents of long-term care facilities (LTCFs). This situation highlighted the necessity to translate evidence-based policies and practices into this setting. However, implementing innovations represents a challenge for LTCFs. This study aims to identify the facilitators and barriers to the implementation of innovations in Canadian LTCFs.MethodsWe conducted a longitudinal correlational study involving different LTCFs participants and the use of the Organizational Readiness for Knowledge Translation (OR4KT) measurement tool before and after the implementation of an innovation. Only data collected at T1 were considered in the analysis due to the low response rate obtained at T2. A mixed-model effect with a random effect for LTCFs was used to account for within organization dependency on responses.ResultsIn total, 256 respondents from 55 (60.4%) LTCFs participated in the study of which 107 participants were high and middle-level managers, and 149 were non-managerial staff. Variations in OR4KT scores were explained by some organizational factors. The province of location of the LTCF emerged as a significant factor for five subscales of the OR4KT as well as for the total score. The presence of a medical director and belonging to a chain (an organization with multiple LTCFs) seemed to be associated with higher OR4KT scores.ConclusionSuccessful LTCF innovation implementation may rely on the geographical administration of healthcare (e.g., the province), the presence of a medical director and the organizational contextual factors between the different actors of LTCFs and the health system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s43058-026-00984-1.Keywords: Population aging, Long-term care facilities, Organizational readiness, Implementation, Innovation

• This original work provides a common set of facilitators and barriers to implementing of evidence-based practices in LTCFs in Canada.

• The facilitators and barriers serve as a guide for successful implementation of promising practices in a challenging setting.

• The OR4KT tool used to identify LTCHs that are most likely prepared organizations to integrate emerging practices can also be used to identify other contexts that are likely to successfully implement practices.

Introduction

An increasing number of older people with physical or cognitive impairment, or both, are living in long-term care facilities (LTCFs) and absolutely require care and support from staff [1]. Long-term care facilities include long-term care homes (LTCH) and retirement homes (RH) (also referred to as nursing homes or congregate living settings for older adults) in Canada [2]. Poorly adapted physical environments, the lack of training, the important turnover of healthcare team members, and long-standing operational deficiencies in this sector have contributed to suboptimal care and service outcomes [3]. These factors have accelerated the initiation of numerous administrative, clinical and operational initiatives to improve the safety and quality of life of these older adults, their loved ones, caregivers and health professionals through the implementation of evidence-based practices. Implementing evidence-based policies and practices, however, has been a challenge for the long-term care sector, which was already lacking structure before the COVID-19 pandemic [4]. Indeed, translating evidence-based health services practices into a variety of clinical, organizational, and policy contexts represents a significant challenge in applied health services [5]. Barriers to translating promising innovations and policies within the LTCF context include varying levels of non-regulated staff, lack of staff knowledge about the setting, high turnover rates, understaffing, low or no financial resources for improving care, variable management models and a lack of support for managers and leaders [3].

To implement promising practices in long-term care settings, the Canadian Foundation for Healthcare Improvement and the Canadian Patient Safety Institute, now Health Care Excellence Canada (HEC), joined forces with the Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR) and financial partners (Health Research British Columbia, New Brunswick Health Research Foundation, Saskatchewan Health Research Foundation, Center for Aging + Brain Health Innovation). They funded 22 Canadian Implementation Science Teams (ISTs) and their LTCF partners to lead projects on implementing one or more of the following six promising practices: preparedness, prevention, people in the workforce, pandemic response and surge capacity, planning for COVID-19 and non-COVID-19 care and the presence of family [4]. Additionally, all funded ISTs were invited to participate in the Common Measurement Framework (CMF) project to collectively understand the facilitators and barriers to successful implementation as well as the impacts of their interventions. The CMF project was managed by a funded team composed of 11 researchers, experts in implementation science and long-term care.

This present study aimed to identify facilitators and barriers to the successful implementation of evidence-based practices in LTCFs in Canada by assessing their level of readiness to implement evidence-based practices.

Methods

Study design

A longitudinal survey-based correlational design was conducted between January 2021 and June 2023. The reporting of this study was guided by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [6].

Population recruitment and selection

In total, our CMF team invited 22 ISTs from 18 Canadian academic institutions to participate in the project. The 22 IS teams, comprising 377 members, partnered with a total of 91 LTCFs in 10 Canadian provinces (none of the ISTs involved LTCFs in the three Canadian territories). Each IST was affiliated with a variable number of LTCFs, ranging from 2 to 15. The funders divided the 22 ISTs into two cohorts: cohort 1 including 14 teams and cohort 2 including eight teams (Fig. 1). The CIHR introduced the CMF team to the two cohorts in January and March 2021, respectively, to explain the aim of the project, the research process and the tools used.

Recruitment of Canadian Implementation Science Teams (ISTs) and their partner LTCFs

In each LTCF, managers (senior and middle managers) and non-managerial staff (health professionals, caregivers, housekeeping and technical support staff) were recruited to participate in the survey. Each IST assigned a key person to select two to ten participants from each LTCF for a total of 182 to 910 participants, depending on the size of the LTCFs and their number of staff. Each IST contacted its partner LTCFs and planned the respondent’s participation and selection process.

Data collection

Our CMF team collected the data at one time point between April 2021 and January 2023, at the start of implementation (T1) of the promising practice. Although a second wave of data collection was planned after implementation (T2) to assess changes over time, low response rates prevented its inclusion in the current analyses. Therefore, all analyses presented here are based on cross-sectional data.

Our CMF team used a hybrid data collection method to facilitate the collection process by the key person(s) involved in the implementation. Questionnaires in English and French were completed on paper or electronically. In addition to the 59 items of the Organizational Readiness for Knowledge Translation (OR4KT) tool, the questionnaire included four sociodemographic variables for the respondents (job title, years of seniority, gender and age group). Additionally, senior managers provided LTCF descriptive characteristics (facility type, public/private, province, region, number of beds, staff and COVID-19 vaccination programs).

Our CMF team used the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) platform hosted on secure servers at Université Laval for electronic data entry and collection. For the electronic version, the CFM team sent an automatic reminder every 2–3 weeks to participants who agreed to participate but did not complete all surveys. When ISTs chose to answer in paper form, they emailed the scanned paper questionnaires back to the CMF team. The CMF team removed all identifying information, and a research assistant manually entered all paper questionnaires into the REDCap database, and a second research assistant validated it. All data were digitally stored and backed up on a secure internal server protected by an intrusion detection system and firewall.

Measurement tool

The CMF team used the Organizational Readiness for Knowledge Translation (OR4KT) tool developed by Gagnon et al. [7] to assess the level of organizational readiness in healthcare settings regarding the implementation of new practices or interventions using the perception of their employees [7]. This tool, validated in French, English and Spanish as well as in various healthcare and service contexts, has 59 items and six dimensions, which are the organizational climate for change, organizational contextual factors, change content, leadership and participation, organizational support, and motivation [8, 9]. Building upon established theories of change (e.g. PARiHS, PCM, TCU-PCM, ARCC, RIM, etc.), OR4KT considers contextual factors such as environment, clientele and service organization as well as other factors, including individual, interpersonal and political, which can also influence the success of the implementation. Prior validation of the tool demonstrated strong internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.79 to 0.92 across its six dimensions and 0.95 for the overall score [9].

Data analysis

The CMF team conducted a descriptive analysis based on the characteristics of LTCFs collected at T1. OR4KT scores (total and subscale scores) by manager and non-managerial staff were compared. Given low response rates obtained at T2, our analyses were restricted to the data collected at T1. A mixed-effects model with a random effect for LTCFs was used to account for the clustering of responses within organizations. Both unadjusted and adjusted models are presented. Organizational factors considered for adjustment were organization type (public / private), province, geographic area coverage, presence of a medical director, belonging to a chain, and the number of beds. All analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 with a bilateral alpha threshold of 0.05. The questionnaires with missing data were excluded from data analysis.

Results

A total of 256 respondents emanating from 55 (60.4%) LTCFs located in 10 Canadian provinces participated in the study. Of this number, 46 LTCFs responded with 107 high and middle-level managers and 42 LTCFs responded with 148 non-managerial staff in T1 (Fig. 2).

Flow chart of LTCFs and participants (time 1)

Thirty-two public (58.2%) and twenty-three (41.8%) private organizations participated in T1. Of these 55 organizations, 16 (29.1%) are in Ontario and 7 (12.7%) in Quebec. A total of 31 (56.4%) organizations are in large population centers, i.e., more than 100,000 inhabitants while 24 (43.6%) in small or medium population centers (< 100,000 inhabitants).

The results show that 22 of the 55 participating LTCFs at T1 (40.0%) belonged to a chain (an organization with multiple LTCFs) (Table 1).

In addition, results show that a significant difference was observed for the OR4KT total score and organizational support subscale between managers and staff, but this difference was no longer statistically significant when the models were adjusted for organizational factors (Table 2). Direct comparison of scores within each LTCF was initially challenging due to small numbers of respondents. To address this potential bias, we conducted an analysis at the organizational level by summarizing manager and staff scores for each LTCF. Among the 46 LTCFs with manager responses and the 42 with staff responses, only 33 had both respondent types, allowing for within-facility comparison. This reduced the impact of differing response rates since each LTCF was compared to itself. Given the limited sample size, a non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied, and the results closely mirrored the adjusted model findings, showing no statistically significant differences overall. Nonetheless, a noteworthy difference was observed for “Leadership and participation” dimension, where managers scored higher than staff–an expected difference, but it was not statistically significant. Results for OR4KTscores for the 6 subscales are presented in Supplementary file.

The results presented in Table 3 show that the province was a significant factor that emerged for five subscales of the OR4KT as well as for the total score. Moreover, the results show that the presence of a medical director and belonging to a chain were positively associated with the OR4KT score, although it was only significant in the motivation subscale.

Discussion

This study aimed to identify facilitating factors and barriers to the successful implementation of evidence-based practices in LTCFs in Canada. Our results show that the variations in the organizational readiness scores were explained by some organizational factors of the LTCFs and not by respondent managerial status. Unlike managers, non-response rate was higher among staff for some dimensions such as organizational contextual factors, leadership and participation, Organizational support and Motivation. This suggests that the items of the OR4KT questionnaire should be adapted according to the respondent managerial status.

Previous reviews have examined factors influencing the implementation of practices in LTCFs and found similar findings to those identified in our study. Organizational culture and required resources were the most considered aspects of organizational contextual factors and were recognized as influencing the innovation implementation in LTC homes [10]. Similarly, Bunn et al. [10] showed the impact of positive cultural attributes on the implementation of innovation. They found a better uptake of innovation when an intervention was acceptable to healthcare professionals, residents, and staff; when it fitted into existing LTCF routines; and when there were opportunities for ongoing consultation with staff [10]. Fakha et al. [11] found that context and characteristics of LTCFs play an integral role in implementing innovations in these organizations. In addition, our study showed that readiness to implement an intervention varied between provinces in which the LTCF is located. This could be explained by the organizational structure of LTCFs, which not only varies between provinces but also within them. These structural differences include governance models such as government-operated, private, public, or municipal–which can influence staffing levels, nurse roles and the professional profile of certain staff [12]. Nursing care in Canadian LTCFs is typically provided by a mix of registered nurses (RNs), licensed practical nurses (LPNs) or registered practical nurses (RPNs), and in some cases, nurse practitioners (NPs). However, the dominant staffing model now relies heavily on unregulated staff, including care aides, personal support workers, orderlies or nurse assistants [3]. The presence and ratio of these professionals vary by province, shaped by regulatory requirements and resource availability [3]. These structural and staffing variations likely influence the capacity of LTCFs to implement new practices.

Our research also showed that the presence of a medical director and belonging to a chain were significantly and positively associated with the OR4KT score and specifically in the motivation subscale. Similarly, Bunn et al. [10] highlighted the importance of leadership from care home managers and/or senior direct-care staff and from staff acting as clinical champions or persuasive leaders. Like the results reported by Åvik Persson et al. [13], managers highlighted that leadership is of great importance for preparation when changes need to be made within an organization. It is necessary that leadership offers the conditions for evidence-based practice implementation [13]. Furthermore, Mills et al. [14] highlighted that continued staff motivation by champions and opportunity for participation in implementation was key facilitators to successful implementation of an intervention in nursing homes. Indeed, the findings of this study are in line with the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) regarding how organizational context, in conjunction with other factors including leadership, organizational support and motivation, can influence the implementation [15]. Furthermore, structural factors such as belonging to a chain or geographic location are difficult for organizations to change, yet they influence resource availability, access to expertise, and capacity for innovation [16, 17]. Chain-affiliated LTCFs often benefit from centralized decision-making and shared resources, which facilitate implementation. Independent LTCFs could emulate these advantages by forming collaborative networks or partnerships to share best practices and implementation support.

Moreover, our review showed that items in the organizational support and motivation subscales of the OR4KT were rarely completed by staff, unlike managers in LTCFs. This could be explained by social desirability which may have prevented respondents from giving their opinion and they simply did not respond or did not have the knowledge on the subject. In addition, nurses and nursing assistants, according to Zhang et al. [18] and Xiao et al. [19], encounter challenges such as pressure, multitasking, limited autonomy, lack of colleague support, workplace conflict, and resource constraints, which could impact their perception of organizational support in LTCFs. According to Liu et al. [20], staff who perceive their organization as supportive and meet their social and emotional needs, including appreciation and recognition, are more likely to be engaged in their work and participate more actively in the implementation process. Therefore, increasing support to staff in the work environment through regular training and development, as well as providing positive feedback and promoting an open, supportive workplace, can lead to increased motivation among staff and enhance their sense of belonging and organizational commitment [20]. The low response rate to some dimensions of the OR4KT among employees compared to managers would suggest that the OR4KT instrument should be adapted according to the type of respondent.

Limitations

The results of this study are of great importance for policymakers and LTCFs as they present evidence pertaining to the facilitators and barriers to implementing evidence-based practices. However, certain limits need to be highlighted. First, the low response rate of the LTCFs who participated in the study which could be explained by the difficult context of LTCFs in times of pandemic and the shortage of human resources affected the representativeness of the sample as well as the generalizability of the results. Also, the recruitment of participants from each LTCF was carried out by a local implementation champion, which may have resulted in selection bias. Second, completing the OR4KT questionnaire at two measurement time points also contributed to the withdrawal of some LTCFs from the study, which hampered the analysis of score differences between the two-time points by province and intervention. Third, our analysis didn’t control for variation in the types of promising practices implemented across participating LTCFs. These differences may have influenced the observed outcomes, as certain practices could require different levels of organizational capacity and resources. Moreover, differences in response rates between managers and staff pose a potential bias in the analyses. A final limitation to highlight is that within the framework of this study, and given the nature of the data collected, we were not able to consider multilevel influences on the preparedness of LTCFs in our analyses, as initially planned [2].

Conclusion

LTCFs are facing significant challenges in ensuring quality care for their residents. These require constant improvements involving the implementation of innovations and new policies. Our findings suggest that the organizational contextual factors play an important role in facilitating the implementation of practices in LTCFs. Results also show that the organizational structure of the LTCFs from one province to another, the presence of a medical director and belonging to a chain, seemed to have a positive impact on the readiness to implement interventions.
