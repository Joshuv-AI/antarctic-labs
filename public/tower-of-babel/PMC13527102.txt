Robust artificial intelligence frameworks for lung cancer subtyping and malignancy detection on thoracic CT
Authors: Mustafa Tahsin Yilmaz, Enes Algul, Ishak Pacal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate characterization of thoracic malignancies on computed tomography (CT) remains challenging because histological subtype differentiation and nodule malignancy assessment require different visual cues, ranging from localized structural abnormalities to broader contextual patterns. This study presents a controlled comparative evaluation of convolutional neural network (CNN) and Vision Transformer (ViT) architectures for two CT-based thoracic oncology tasks: four-class histological subtyping and three-class lung cancer classification. A total of 30 contemporary architectures, including 15 CNN-based and 15 ViT-based models, were evaluated under a unified experimental setting using two publicly available datasets. The Chest CT-Scan Images dataset was used for Adenocarcinoma, Large cell carcinoma, Squamous cell carcinoma, and normal classification, while the IQ-OTH/NCCD dataset was used for normal, benign, and malignant classification. Model performance was assessed using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, AUC, parameter count, confusion matrices, and Grad-CAM-based visual interpretation. For histological subtyping, DeiT3-Base achieved the strongest performance among the evaluated models, with an F1-score of 0.9732, followed closely by high-performing CNN models such as InceptionNeXt-Base. For lung cancer classification, EfficientNetV2-Small, Swin-Base, and MViTv2-Base achieved the highest F1-score of 0.9816. These findings indicate that attention-based architectures may be advantageous for subtype discrimination requiring broader contextual modeling, whereas efficient CNNs and hierarchical transformers remain highly competitive for localized structural abnormalities. Introduction

Cancer persists as one of the most formidable challenges in global healthcare and remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide. Lung cancer, in particular, represents the most prevalent and lethal malignancy, accounting for approximately 1.8 million deaths annually, as reported by the World Health Organization1. The prognosis for patients is heavily dependent on the stage at diagnosis; while advanced-stage disease often has limited therapeutic options, early and accurate diagnosis can significantly enhance treatment efficacy and survival rates. Medical imaging, particularly computed tomography (CT), plays a central role in the screening, detection, and radiological characterization of thoracic malignancies. Although definitive assignment of lung cancer subtype requires pathological examination, CT images may contain macroscopic imaging cues associated with tumor morphology, lesion distribution, margin characteristics, density variation, and surrounding parenchymal changes. These radiological patterns can support computational models designed to predict histologically defined lung cancer subtypes and to distinguish normal, benign, and malignant cases. However, CT interpretation remains labor-intensive and subject to inter-observer variability, especially when early lesions, subtle margins, or overlapping radiological appearances are present. These challenges motivate the development of reproducible and interpretable deep learning approaches for CT-based thoracic cancer classification.

The rapid development of deep learning has substantially advanced medical image analysis by enabling automated feature learning from complex imaging data. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have been widely used because they can extract hierarchical and spatially localized representations from high-dimensional images. Architectures such as ResNet2, DenseNet3, and EfficientNet4 have shown strong performance in tasks ranging from pulmonary nodule detection to cancer image classification. For instance, Ardila et al.5 developed a CNN-based model for lung cancer screening using low-dose CT scans and reported an AUC of 0.94. These findings demonstrate the value of CNN-based models as strong baselines for automated thoracic image interpretation. Nevertheless, convolutional operations are inherently local, which may limit their ability to model broader spatial dependencies in CT images when subtype-related patterns are subtle or distributed across multiple regions. Similarly, Esteva et al.6 applied CNNs to classify skin lesions, demonstrating dermatologist-level accuracy, which underscores the generalizability and potential of CNN architectures across various medical imaging domains.

Despite their remarkable success, CNNs possess inherent limitations, primarily due to the local nature of convolutional operations. This inductive bias restricts their ability to model long-range dependencies and capture global context, which are critical for interpreting complex medical images where pathological indicators may span multiple regions or exhibit subtle inter-regional relationships. To address these constraints, Vision Transformers (ViTs), introduced by Dosovitskiy et al.7, have emerged as a paradigm shift in computer vision. By leveraging self-attention mechanisms, ViTs are capable of modeling global dependencies across the entire image, offering a complementary approach to the local feature extraction of CNNs. ViTs have shown significant promise in medical imaging; for example, Chen et al.8 demonstrated that ViT-based models could outperform traditional CNNs in classifying retinal diseases by effectively modeling global image context. Furthermore, hierarchical variants like the Swin Transformer9 have introduced shifted window attention mechanisms to balance local and global processing, achieving state-of-the-art performance in tasks such as brain tumor segmentation10. Additionally, data-efficient variants like DeiT11 and hybrid architectures like MobileViT12 have been developed to enhance computational efficiency, making these powerful models viable for resource-constrained clinical environments.

Recent studies have explored CNN and ViT models for thoracic oncology, yet the literature remains fragmented across diagnostic tasks, datasets, and architectural families. In CT-based histological subtype prediction, prior work has investigated deep learning models for separating pathology-defined lung cancer categories such as Adenocarcinoma and Squamous cell carcinoma. Other studies have used histopathology slide images, which provide microscopic tissue information but differ fundamentally from CT-based non-invasive imaging. In malignancy detection, 3D CNN-based approaches have been applied to pulmonary nodule assessment and prognosis, with reported AUC values ranging from 0.86 to 0.93. However, many previous studies evaluate a single model family or a limited set of architectures, making it difficult to determine whether convolutional locality, hierarchical attention, or global self-attention is more suitable for different CT-based thoracic cancer classification tasks13–15.

Challenges, gaps, and contributions

Despite the remarkable strides in computer-aided diagnosis for thoracic oncology, the current landscape is characterized by significant fragmentation and methodological impediments. Predominantly, existing literature treats diagnostic tasks in isolation, focusing exclusively on either pulmonary nodule malignancy detection or histological subtyping, thereby restricting a comprehensive understanding of architectural performance across multifaceted clinical workflows. Furthermore, a substantial portion of state-of-the-art methodologies is optimized strictly for curated, homogenous datasets; consequently, their robustness when applied to heterogeneous imaging sources, which are increasingly prevalent in multi-center studies and open-access repositories, remains insufficiently validated. This issue is compounded by the “black-box” nature of many deep learning models, where a scarcity of rigorous visual explainability mechanisms provides an interpretability deficit that hinders clinician trust. Finally, the prohibitive computational complexity of emerging Vision Transformers often precludes their deployment in resource-constrained environments, highlighting a critical need for a rigorous comparative evaluation of high-capacity models against lightweight, efficient architectures suitable for edge computing.

In response to these multifaceted challenges, this study establishes a robust, unified deep learning framework designed to benchmark a diverse spectrum of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers (ViTs) against heterogeneous imaging constraints. By integrating rigorous performance metrics with granular explainability analyses, we aim to bridge the gap between theoretical model complexity and practical clinical utility. Related work

The application of deep learning to medical imaging, particularly for chest and lung cancer classification, has seen significant advancements in recent years, driven by the development of CNNs and, more recently, ViTs. This section provides a comprehensive review of prior work in cancer classification using these architectures, focusing on their application to chest and lung cancer detection, their strengths and limitations, and the gaps addressed by our unified framework. We categorize the literature into CNN-based approaches, ViT-based approaches, and hybrid or multi-modal methods, highlighting key studies and their contributions to the field.

CNN-based approaches for cancer classification

Convolutional Neural Networks have been the cornerstone of medical image analysis due to their ability to extract spatially localized features through convolutional operations, making them well-suited for tasks like cancer detection and classification. Early work by LeCun et al.16 established CNNs as effective for image recognition, and their adaptation to medical imaging has yielded significant results. For lung cancer, Ardila et al.5 developed a deep learning model based on a 3D CNN for lung cancer screening using low-dose CT scans, achieving an AUC of 0.94 on the National Lung Screening Trial dataset. Their model focused on detecting pulmonary nodules and predicting malignancy risk, demonstrating performance comparable to or better than radiologists. However, the model’s reliance on 3D inputs increased computational complexity, limiting its applicability in resource-constrained settings.

For CT-based lung cancer subtype prediction, CNNs have been investigated as non-invasive tools for estimating pathology-defined tumor categories such as Adenocarcinoma and Squamous cell carcinoma. Coudray et al.13 used deep learning for non-small cell lung cancer subtype discrimination from histopathology images, achieving strong performance in distinguishing Adenocarcinoma from Squamous cell carcinoma. This distinction is important because histology slides contain microscopic tissue information, whereas CT captures macroscopic radiological morphology. Other studies have applied CNN-based models to CT-derived lung cancer classification, showing that local texture, shape, density, margin characteristics, and lesion-related imaging patterns may contribute to subtype or malignancy prediction. Nevertheless, many CNN-based studies remain limited by single-task design, restricted model comparison, or insufficient analysis of whether model attention corresponds to radiologically plausible regions17.

EfficientNet4 introduced a compound scaling approach that optimizes model depth, width, and resolution, leading to improved performance with reduced computational cost. Hosny et al.14 adapted EfficientNet for lung cancer prognosis using CT scans, achieving an AUC of 0.86 for survival prediction. Their work demonstrated the efficiency of scaled architectures but was limited to prognostic rather than diagnostic tasks. More recent CNN architectures, such as ConvNeXt18, incorporate transformer-inspired features like large receptive fields, achieving competitive performance in medical imaging. For instance, Riquelme et al.15 used a 3D CNN for lung nodule detection and classification, but their model struggled with small or low-contrast nodules, a common challenge in lung cancer datasets.

Despite their success, CNN-based approaches often struggle with modeling long-range dependencies, as their convolutional kernels are inherently local. This limitation can hinder performance in tasks requiring global context, such as identifying subtle inter-regional relationships in CT images of chest or lung cancer. Additionally, many CNN studies focus on single-modal datasets or specific cancer types, limiting their generalizability across diverse imaging modalities and histopathological classes.

Vision transformer-based approaches

The introduction of Vision Transformers (ViTs) by Dosovitskiy et al.7 marked a paradigm shift in image classification, leveraging self-attention mechanisms to model global dependencies across image patches. Unlike CNNs, ViTs treat images as sequences of patches, enabling the capture of long-range relationships, which is particularly valuable for medical imaging tasks where lesions may span multiple regions. Chen et al.8 applied ViTs to retinal disease classification, achieving superior performance compared to CNNs by modeling global image context, with an AUC of 0.95 for multi-class disease detection. Their work highlighted ViTs’ ability to handle complex medical images but required large-scale pre-training on datasets like ImageNet to achieve optimal results.

In lung cancer imaging, ViT-based architectures have been explored for detection and classification because self-attention can capture longer-range spatial dependencies than conventional convolutional operations. Hierarchical variants such as Swin Transformer balance local and global feature modeling through window-based attention, making them suitable for high-resolution medical images. For CT-based histological subtype prediction, transformer models may be useful when class separation depends on distributed radiological patterns rather than a single localized lesion feature. However, transformer-based architectures often require careful fine-tuning, sufficient training data, and stable optimization. Their performance may also be affected by public-release image formats, preprocessing differences, and limited acquisition metadata. These constraints highlight the need for controlled comparisons between ViTs and CNNs under the same experimental protocol9,10,19.

Data-efficient ViT variants, such as DeiT11, have addressed the high data requirements of traditional ViTs by incorporating distillation techniques, improving performance on smaller datasets. Mehta & Rastegari12 introduced MobileViT, a lightweight hybrid convolutional-transformer model, which achieved competitive performance with significantly fewer parameters, making it suitable for clinical deployment on resource-constrained devices. For instance, Roy et al.18 applied MobileViT to lung cancer classification, highlighting its potential for real-time applications. However, ViT-based models often require extensive computational resources, and their performance can degrade on non-DICOM formats like JPG/PNG, which are common in public datasets but introduce compression artifacts. BeiT20, a self-supervised ViT variant, has shown promise in general image classification but struggled in medical imaging tasks without extensive fine-tuning.

Hybrid and multi-modal approaches

Recent studies have explored hybrid and multi-modal approaches to combine the strengths of CNNs and ViTs, addressing their individual limitations. He et al.21 proposed a hybrid CNN-ViT model for breast cancer detection in ultrasound images, integrating local feature extraction from CNNs with global context modeling from ViTs. Their approach outperformed standalone CNNs and ViTs, demonstrating the complementary nature of these architectures. Similarly, Hosny et al.22 developed a multi-modal framework combining CNNs and ViTs for lung cancer classification, incorporating both CT images and clinical data. Their work highlighted the potential of multi-modal integration to improve robustness, particularly for heterogeneous datasets.

Multi-modal approaches have also incorporated non-imaging data, such as genomic or clinical information, to enhance diagnostic accuracy. For instance, Mobadersany et al.23 integrated histopathological images with genomic data using a CNN-based model, achieving improved survival prediction for lung cancer patients. However, their approach was limited to histological images and required complex data preprocessing. More recently, Hosny et al.22 proposed a multi-modal ViT framework for lung cancer diagnosis, combining CT scans with electronic health records. While promising, these studies often face challenges in data availability, as multi-modal datasets are scarce and require sophisticated integration techniques.

Methodology

This section outlines the methodology employed to develop and evaluate a unified deep learning framework for classifying chest and lung cancers from CT images. The framework integrates Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) and Vision Transformer (ViT) architectures to leverage their complementary strengths in local and global feature extraction. We describe the datasets, preprocessing steps, model architectures, training protocols, evaluation metrics, and visualization techniques used to ensure robust performance and clinical interpretability. The methodology is designed to address challenges such as non-DICOM image formats, limited dataset sizes, and the need for high recall in clinical applications.

Datasets

Chest dataset

The Chest CT-Scan Images dataset comprises approximately 1,000 CT images categorized into four diagnostic classes: Adenocarcinoma, Large cell carcinoma, Squamous cell carcinoma, and normal cases. In this study, these labels were used for CT-based prediction of histologically defined lung cancer subtypes and normal controls. It should be noted that the model inputs are radiological CT images rather than microscopic histology slides; therefore, the task aims to learn imaging patterns associated with pathology-defined subtype labels rather than direct histopathological structures. The dataset is organized into predefined training, validation, and test folders, and this original split structure was preserved during experimentation. Because the images are publicly available in JPG/PNG format rather than in the original acquisition format, detailed acquisition parameters, reconstruction metadata, and full radiometric information are not accessible24.

Lung cancer dataset

The lung cancer dataset, sourced from the Iraq-Oncology Teaching Hospital/National Center for Cancer Diseases (IQ-OTH/NCCD), was collected over three months in fall 2019 at specialist hospitals in Iraq. It includes 1,190 CT scan slices from 110 cases, grouped into three classes: normal (55 cases), benign (15 cases), and malignant (40 cases). The CT scans were originally collected in DICOM format using a Siemens SOMATOM scanner with a protocol of 120 kV, 1 mm slice thickness, window width of 350–1200 HU, and window center of 50–600 HU, performed with breath hold at full inspiration. All images were de-identified to protect patient privacy, and written consent was waived by the institutional review board, which approved the study. Each case comprises 80–200 slices, capturing various angles and sides of the human chest. The dataset’s diversity in patient demographics (gender, age, educational attainment, area of residence, and occupation, including employees of Iraqi ministries, farmers, and others from Baghdad, Wasit, Diyala, Salahuddin, and Babylon) enhances its representativeness. For compatibility with our framework, DICOM images were converted to PNG format, preserving essential radiological features while aligning with the chest dataset’s format.

Model architectures

The comparative framework evaluates a diverse set of CNNs and ViT architectures to examine how different model families behave in CT-based thoracic cancer classification. CNNs are well suited to capturing spatially localized image features through convolutional operations, which makes them effective for identifying radiological structures such as pulmonary nodules, lesion margins, density variations, and other morphology-related patterns16. In contrast, ViT-based architectures use self-attention mechanisms to model longer-range spatial dependencies, allowing them to capture broader contextual relationships that may be relevant when subtype-related imaging patterns are subtle or spatially distributed7. The selected models were therefore used to compare local feature extraction, hierarchical representation learning, and global contextual modeling under a unified experimental protocol.

The architecture set was constructed to provide a balanced benchmark across model families, computational scales, and design principles. The selection criteria included: (1) architectural diversity, covering residual, dense, compound-scaled, inception-based, depthwise separable, hierarchical attention, self-attention, and hybrid convolution-transformer designs; (2) a broad parameter range from lightweight models to high-capacity backbones, enabling analysis of accuracy-efficiency trade-offs; (3) prior relevance or frequent use in medical image analysis; and (4) availability of reproducible PyTorch or timm-based implementations. VGG13 was included as a classical CNN baseline because it preserves the core sequential convolutional structure of the VGG family while using fewer layers than VGG16. It was not intended to represent a modern state-of-the-art model, but rather to provide a reference point for assessing the performance gains of more recent CNN and ViT architectures.

For the ViT family, ViT-Base with 16 × 16 and 32 × 32 patch sizes was evaluated to examine the effect of patch granularity under the same backbone configuration. This within-backbone comparison was complemented by structurally distinct transformer variants, including Swin-Base, DeiT3-Base, MViTv2-Base, MobileViT, MobileViTv2, PiT, Tiny-ViT, BeiT, MaxViT, FastViT, RepViT, and GcViT. This design allowed both patch-level sensitivity and broader transformer architecture differences to be assessed within the same comparative setting. The following subsections describe the general mathematical formulations of CNNs and ViTs, followed by the specific architectures evaluated in this study.

MViTv2-Base33, with 50.71 M parameters, was selected to assess multi-scale attention, which enables the model to integrate local and broader contextual representations at different spatial resolutions.

Lightweight and hybrid transformer variants were also included to evaluate the accuracy-efficiency trade-off. MobileViT-base12 and MobileViTv2-10034, with 4.94 M and 4.39 M parameters, respectively, combine convolutional operations with transformer blocks to retain local feature sensitivity while reducing computational cost. PiT-Base35, with 72.74 M parameters, applies pooling-based token reduction to improve efficiency during transformer processing. Tiny-ViT-11 m36, with 10.55 M parameters, represents a compact transformer design developed for efficient inference. These models and their variants have been widely investigated in medical image classification, segmentation, lesion detection, and computer-aided diagnosis studies, supporting their relevance as representative transformer baselines for thoracic CT analysis40–46.

Results and discussion

This section reports the quantitative performance of the evaluated CNN and ViT architectures and interprets their behavior across the two CT-based thoracic oncology tasks. All results were obtained on held-out test subsets that were not used during model training or hyperparameter selection. The analysis focuses not only on predictive performance, but also on class-wise behavior, model complexity, and visual interpretability.

Experimental design

All experiments were conducted under a unified training protocol to ensure a fair comparison between CNN- and ViT-based architectures. The computational environment consisted of a high-performance workstation equipped with four NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPUs, each with 24 GB of memory, 64 GB RAM, and a 16-core AMD EPYC processor. The implementation was carried out using Python 3.13, PyTorch 2.7, CUDA 12.8, and torchvision 0.15.

Each model was trained for a maximum of 50 epochs with a batch size of 32. Experimental results

The experiments were designed to compare CNN and ViT-based architectures across two CT-based thoracic oncology classification tasks with different diagnostic objectives. The first task used the Chest CT-Scan Images dataset for four-class histological subtyping, including Adenocarcinoma, Large cell carcinoma, Squamous cell carcinoma, and normal cases. The second task used the IQ-OTH/NCCD lung cancer dataset for three-class classification of normal, benign, and malignant cases. A broad set of CNN architectures, including ResNet50, DenseNet169, EfficientNetV2-Small, ConvNeXt-Base, and InceptionNeXt-Base, was evaluated alongside ViT-based models such as ViT-Base-Patch16, Swin-Base, DeiT3-Base, MViTv2-Base, and MobileViT. This experimental design enabled a controlled assessment of how convolutional feature extraction, hierarchical representation learning, and attention-based contextual modeling perform under distinct CT classification settings. Discussion

Task-specific performance of CNN and ViT architectures

CNN and ViT architectures are better interpreted as task-dependent model families rather than as approaches with a single dominant winner. Their behavior was strongly task-dependent. Histological subtyping and malignancy detection rely on different visual cues in CT images. Subtype classification requires discrimination of subtle inter-class patterns, whereas malignancy detection is more closely related to localized structural abnormalities such as nodule morphology, density variation, and margin irregularity. This distinction helps explain why different architectures performed best across the two diagnostic settings.

For the Chest CT-Scan Images dataset, DeiT3-Base achieved the highest performance among all evaluated models, with an F1-score of 0.9732. This result suggests that attention-based contextual modeling can be advantageous when subtype-related differences are subtle and spatially distributed. InceptionNeXt-Base also achieved strong performance among CNNs, with an F1-score of 0.9692. The small gap between these models is important because it shows that transformer-based designs do not automatically replace modern CNNs. Instead, their benefit appears most evident when broader spatial context contributes to class separation.

For the IQ-OTH/NCCD lung cancer dataset, the strongest results were obtained by EfficientNetV2-Small, Swin-Base, and MViTv2-Base, each reaching an F1-score of 0.9816. This pattern suggests that malignancy detection in this dataset can be captured effectively by both efficient convolutional representations and hierarchical attention mechanisms. EfficientNetV2-Small is particularly relevant because it achieved top-tier performance with a moderate parameter count, making it a strong candidate for accuracy-efficiency trade-off analysis. Swin-Base and MViTv2-Base also performed well, likely because their hierarchical designs preserve local structure while incorporating wider contextual information.

These results clarify the main contribution of the study. The work does not claim superiority of a newly proposed architecture. Its contribution lies in a controlled benchmark of 30 contemporary CNN and ViT models across two clinically distinct CT-based thoracic oncology tasks. This design allows the influence of architectural inductive bias to be examined under comparable experimental conditions. CNNs remained highly competitive for localized structural patterns, while ViT-based models showed particular strength when broader contextual modeling was beneficial. The evidence therefore supports a complementary interpretation of CNNs and ViTs rather than a universal preference for one model family.

Interpretability, reliability, and clinical implications

The confusion matrices and Grad-CAM visualizations provided additional context for interpreting the numerical results. For histological subtyping, DeiT3-Base produced high class-wise discrimination across Adenocarcinoma, Large cell carcinoma, Squamous cell carcinoma, and normal images, with errors mainly occurring in visually challenging cases where subtype boundaries may be less distinct. For lung cancer classification, the leading models showed strong separation among normal, benign, and malignant classes, while the remaining errors were mostly associated with subtle or low-contrast findings. These patterns are consistent with the known difficulty of separating benign abnormalities from early malignant or normal-appearing structures in CT images.

The Grad-CAM maps supported the quantitative findings by showing that the high-performing models generally focused on lesion-containing or anatomically relevant regions rather than unrelated image areas. This is important because high classification performance alone does not confirm that a model is relying on clinically meaningful evidence. At the same time, Grad-CAM should be interpreted as a qualitative interpretability tool, not as proof of clinical reasoning. Expert reader assessment and prospective validation are still required to determine whether these attention patterns are sufficiently reliable for decision-support use.

Several methodological choices were used to reduce optimistic bias. For the IQ-OTH/NCCD dataset, the split was performed at the patient level so that all slices from the same patient remained within a single subset. This is essential for CT-based classification because adjacent slices from one patient can contain highly correlated anatomical information. Training-time augmentation, early stopping, and learning-rate scheduling were also used to reduce overfitting. Nevertheless, the relatively small test sets limit the statistical certainty of model ranking, especially when several architectures achieve closely matched performance.

The findings are therefore best interpreted as controlled benchmark evidence rather than clinical validation. The datasets were retrospective and publicly available, and the Chest CT-Scan Images dataset was available only in released JPG/PNG format. Although the IQ-OTH/NCCD images originated from DICOM data, conversion to a windowed PNG representation may reduce radiometric depth and remove scanner-specific metadata. These constraints may affect subtle texture information and limit generalization to native clinical DICOM workflows. Future studies should validate these architectures on larger multi-center DICOM-based cohorts with preserved acquisition metadata, external test sets, confidence intervals, calibration analysis, and reader-study evaluation. Such work is necessary before these models can be considered as candidates for clinical decision-support systems.

Limitations and future work

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the experiments were conducted using retrospective, publicly available datasets. Although these datasets provide a useful basis for controlled benchmarking, their scale remains limited, particularly for evaluating 30 high-capacity CNN and ViT architectures. The relatively small test sets increase uncertainty in model ranking, especially when the performance differences between top models are narrow. To reduce optimistic bias, the IQ-OTH/NCCD dataset was divided at the patient level, training-time augmentation was applied only to the training subset, and early stopping with learning rate scheduling was used during optimization. Nevertheless, these safeguards cannot replace validation on larger and more diverse external cohorts.

Second, the use of publicly released imaging data introduces source-related constraints. The Chest CT-Scan Images dataset was available only in JPG/PNG format, whereas the IQ-OTH/NCCD dataset required harmonization into the same input pipeline. This design allowed a consistent comparison across architectures, but it may also limit access to acquisition parameters, reconstruction details, scanner-specific metadata, and full radiometric information. Such factors can influence subtle texture and contrast patterns that may be relevant for differentiating benign, malignant, and histological subtype-related features. Therefore, the reported results should be interpreted as benchmark evidence under public-dataset conditions rather than as proof of readiness for direct clinical deployment.

Future work should prioritize larger multi-center cohorts with standardized acquisition documentation, preserved imaging metadata, and harmonized preprocessing protocols. External validation should include confidence intervals, calibration analysis, subgroup evaluation, and testing across different scanner settings and patient populations. Reader-study evaluation will also be necessary to determine whether model outputs improve diagnostic consistency, interpretation time, or decision confidence when used alongside radiologists. Methodologically, future studies may examine uncertainty-aware prediction, domain-specific pretraining, carefully controlled CNN-ViT fusion, and computational optimization for resource-limited environments. These steps would provide stronger evidence for whether the evaluated architectures can progress from retrospective benchmarking toward clinically useful decision-support candidates.

Conclusion

This study provides a controlled comparative benchmark of 30 CNN and ViT architectures for two CT-based thoracic oncology tasks: CT-based prediction of histologically defined lung cancer subtypes and malignancy-oriented lung cancer classification. DeiT3-Base achieved the strongest performance for subtype prediction, with an F1-score of 0.9732, whereas EfficientNetV2-Small, Swin-Base, and MViTv2-Base achieved the highest performance for normal, benign, and malignant lung CT classification, each reaching an F1-score of 0.9816. These findings indicate that architectural performance is task-dependent. Attention-based models were particularly effective when subtype prediction required broader contextual modeling, while efficient CNNs and hierarchical transformers remained highly competitive for localized structural abnormalities, including nodule-related morphology, density variation, and margin characteristics. Grad-CAM visualizations further supported the quantitative results by showing that high-performing models generally focused on lesion-related or anatomically plausible thoracic regions rather than unrelated background areas. The findings therefore suggest that CNNs and ViTs should be viewed as complementary model families for CT-based thoracic cancer analysis rather than as competing approaches with a single universally superior architecture. Nevertheless, the results remain controlled benchmark evidence because the study relied on retrospective public datasets, limited test-set sizes, heterogeneous image formats, and no external clinical validation. Future investigations using larger multi-center imaging cohorts, preserved acquisition and reconstruction metadata, standardized preprocessing, calibration analysis, and reader-study evaluation are required before these models can be considered reliable candidates for clinical decision-support workflows.
