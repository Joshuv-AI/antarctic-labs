Delusional parasitosis with shared psychotic features secondary to vitamin B12 deficiency and mild cognitive impairment: a case report
Authors: Chiyoko Bando, Naomichi Okamoto, Enkhmurun Chibaatar, Satoru Ide, Reiji Yoshimura, Atsuko Ikenouchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVisual illusions are misperceptions of real external stimuli and, when persistent, may indicate underlying neurological or metabolic disorders. Delusional parasitosis is characterized by a fixed belief of infestation without medical evidence and may occur secondary to medical conditions. However, the relationship between visual illusion-related perceptual abnormalities and delusion formation, particularly in reversible metabolic states such as vitamin B12 deficiency, remains unclear.Case presentationAn 80-year-old man presented with a persistent belief that mites infested his body and environment. His delusion was preceded by misinterpretation of real stimuli, including lint and skin debris, consistent with visual illusions. Cognitive assessment indicated mild cognitive impairment. His wife gradually came to share his delusional beliefs. Laboratory findings revealed marked vitamin B12 deficiency. Brain perfusion imaging demonstrated hypoperfusion in the medial occipital and parietal association cortices. Treatment with low-dose quetiapine and mecobalamin resulted in rapid resolution of visual illusions and subsequent disappearance of delusional parasitosis. Quetiapine was discontinued without relapse, and follow-up imaging showed improvement in cerebral perfusion. The wife’s shared beliefs resolved following clinical improvement and separation.ConclusionsThis case suggests that visual illusions due to reversible metabolic disturbances may precipitate delusional parasitosis in cognitively vulnerable individuals and highlights the importance of evaluating all suspected patients and closely associated individuals.

Introduction

A visual illusion is a perceptual phenomenon in which a veridical external stimulus is misperceived and interpreted as having a form that differs from its actual physical properties. In contrast to visual hallucinations, illusions are characterized by the presence of a real sensory input underlying perception [1]. Although visual illusions may occur in healthy individuals, they are typically transient and self-limiting. Their persistence and clinical salience can serve as important indicators of underlying pathology, including neurodegenerative, structural, or metabolic disorders [2].

Delusional parasitosis is a psychiatric condition characterized by a fixed false belief of being infested with parasites, despite the absence of medical evidence [3]. Clinically, it can be diagnosed into a primary form, in which delusions occur in isolation, and a secondary form, in association with underlying psychiatric or medical conditions, including schizophrenia, depression, hypothyroidism, and intracranial lesions [4]. Visual illusions involving the misperception of minor visual stimuli, such as lint or skin debris, as insects or foreign bodies may serve as precipitating factors for delusional parasitosis when such perceptual disturbances are subsequently elaborated into a delusional interpretation.

Shared psychotic disorder refers to a phenomenon in which delusional beliefs are transmitted from an affected individual to a closely related person, typically in the context of prolonged cohabitation and social isolation [5]. In older adults, the acceptance and internalization of such delusional beliefs may be facilitated by vulnerabilities in cognitive function and judgment; however, the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms remain insufficiently understood, and reported cases are limited [6].

Visual Illusions in older adults have been widely recognized as early manifestations of neurodegenerative disorders such as dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), but they may also arise from reversible metabolic conditions such as vitamin deficiency. However, the processes through which visual illusions associated with vitamin deficiency contribute to the development of delusional beliefs, as well as their impact on visual processing networks and predictive processing systems, remain poorly characterized. Here, we report a case of vitamin B12 deficiency and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) in which visual illusions of mites precipitated secondary delusional parasitosis, with partial sharing of the delusional beliefs by the patient’s spouse.

Case presentation

An 80-year-old man was referred to the psychiatric department of our hospital with persistent complaints of numerous mites in his room and burrowing into his skin. The patient had no history of developmental abnormalities. After graduating from high school, he served in the government service and worked as a taxi driver until retirement. He married at approximately 30 years of age and had two children. His medical history included cataract surgery. He had no history of gastrectomy. The patient maintained adequate oral intake, had no dietary restrictions or preferences, and was not vegetarian. There was no personal or family history of psychiatric disorders. He was characterized by a premorbidly anxious personality. His current medications included pravastatin (5 mg), famotidine (10 mg), fexofenadine (60 mg), and bisoprolol fumarate (2.5 mg), all of which had been prescribed for 3 years prior to presentation.

Four months before admission, the patient began misidentifying lint and small debris on his bed and floor as mites because they appeared to be moving. He mistook black specks in his sputum for mites and gradually developed a fixed belief that the mites were invading his mouth and skin. He frequently sprayed insecticides in his room and collected lint and skin flakes using adhesive tape, presenting these as evidence of mites (Fig. 1A). He showed no diurnal fluctuations in consciousness. Ophthalmological evaluation revealed normal visual acuity and visual fields, and dermatological examination showed no evidence of parasites on the skin. In this case, the perceptual abnormalities were triggered by actual external visual stimuli.

The patient lived with his wife, who was in her seventies. Although their home was not markedly unsanitary, housekeeping and organization were suboptimal, and dust accumulation was noted on elevated surfaces, including lighting fixtures. He had difficulty discarding unnecessary items and tended to accumulate clutter. His sons lived at a distance and were unable to provide regular support due to occupational commitments. The couple’s relationship was described as good, characterized by mutual support and consideration.

Initially, the patient’s wife was skeptical of his beliefs; however, over time, she began to partially share them. She agreed with his complaints that “ticks might be attached to his skin” and “they might migrate to his brain,” and began assisting him in checking his body and surroundings for insects. However, her level of conviction was weaker than that of the patient. She considered both the possibility of parasitic infestation and that her husband might have dementia, indicating that she retained partial insight.

Two days after initiation of quetiapine at a dose of 12.5 mg in an outpatient setting, the patient was admitted for further evaluation and management. Physical examination revealed that he was emotionally reactive and appropriate. No muscle rigidity, tremors, constipation, or autonomic dysfunction were observed. There were no diurnal fluctuations in consciousness or cognitive function, and no evidence of parkinsonism or rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder. Cognitive assessment demonstrated a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score of 27 (orientation: −2; delayed recall: −1) and a Montreal Cognitive Assessment–Japanese version (MoCA-J) score of 24 (visuospatial/executive function: −2; attention: −3; language: −1), consistent with MCI. Regarding instrumental activities of daily living, the patient required mild assistance with certain tasks, including medication and financial management; however, he remained largely independent in basic activities of daily living.

Laboratory investigations revealed no endocrine abnormalities in the thyroid or adrenal glands. Serum vitamin B12 levels were markedly reduced to 116 pg/mL (reference range: 200–900 pg/mL), whereas other vitamin levels were within normal limits (vitamin B1: 35 ng/mL; vitamin B2: 2.8 ng/mL; folate: 10.9 ng/mL). No evidence of anemia was observed (hemoglobin: 12.5 g/dL; mean corpuscular volume: 92.8 μm³). Given the possibility of drug-induced vitamin deficiency, famotidine was discontinued, and mecobalamin was initiated at a dose of 1500 µg/day. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed mild cerebral atrophy in the parietal and occipital lobes, without focal lesions, vascular abnormalities, or significant atrophy of the hippocampus or parahippocampal gyrus (Fig. 1B). Dopamine transporter scintigraphy using 123I-ioflupane showed no reduction in uptake in the bilateral striatum (Fig. 1C). Similarly, 123I-meta-iodobenzylguanidine scintigraphy showed no decrease in uptake in either the early or delayed phases (Fig. 1D). Brain perfusion single-photon emission computed tomography using technetium-99 m ethyl cysteinate dimer demonstrated mildly reduced uptake in the parietal association cortex and markedly reduced uptake in the medial occipital cortex, whereas perfusion in the medial temporal lobe, lateral occipital cortex, and cingulate gyrus was preserved (Fig. 1E). The patient’s visual illusions began to improve around the 4th day after initiation of quetiapine (12.5 mg). Ten days after starting mecobalamin at 1500 µg/day, his vitamin B12 level increased to 283 pg/mL; quetiapine was discontinued on the same day, and no recurrence of visual illusions was observed. With the resolution of the visual illusions, the patient’s delusions of parasitic infestation also resolved seven days later.

Following hospitalization, separation from the patient, and improvement in his symptoms, the wife’s delusional beliefs also resolved. Although she had no history of dementia or psychiatric disorders, her MMSE score was 25 (calculation: −4; delayed recall: −1), and her MoCA-J score was 21 (visuospatial/executive function: −2; attention: −3; language: −2; delayed recall: −2), suggesting possible MCI similar to that of the patient.

At the 2-month follow-up, the patient was treated with mecobalamin alone without antipsychotics, and no recurrence of visual illusions or delusions was observed. Although there were no significant changes in MMSE (28 points; orientation: −1; comprehension: −1) or MoCA-J (22 points; visuospatial/executive function: −2; language: −1; delayed recall: −5), brain perfusion imaging demonstrated improvement in the previously observed reduction in uptake in the parietal association cortex and medial occipital cortex (Fig. 1F). Discussion

In this case, secondary delusional parasitosis was precipitated by visual illusions arising from a reversible metabolic disturbance. The delusional content was partially shared with the patient’s spouse, providing clinically and pathophysiologically important insights.

Visual hallucinations and illusions are typically observed in neurodegenerative disorders such as DLB and posterior cortical atrophy; however, in the present case, these conditions were considered unlikely based on the clinical features, neuroimaging findings, and reversible course of symptoms. In addition, the absence of impaired consciousness and diurnal fluctuations in symptoms argues against delirium. Vitamin B12 deficiency is relatively common in older adults with an estimated prevalence of > 20% [8] and is often asymptomatic, warranting caution when inferring causal relationships. Nevertheless, considering the overall clinical course and laboratory findings, vitamin B12 deficiency was important factor in this case. Given that cognitive vulnerability associated with MCI can trigger a wide range of psychiatric symptoms [9], this case may represent MCI-related neuropsychiatric symptoms (i.e., visual illusion and secondary delusional parasitosis) arising from vitamin B12 deficiency.

Although the diagnostic threshold may vary depending on guidelines and laboratory reference ranges, vitamin B12 deficiency is defined as a serum level < 200 pg/mL [10]. Histamine H2 receptor antagonists and proton pump inhibitors may impair the release of protein-bound vitamin B12 by reducing gastric acid secretion, potentially leading to decreased absorption with long-term use [11]. The association between vitamin B12 deficiency and psychiatric symptoms is often overlooked in clinical practice, as such symptoms are not necessarily accompanied by megaloblastic anemia or peripheral neuropathy [12]. A previous systematic review identified 50 cases of hallucinations associated with vitamin B12 deficiency, with visual hallucinations being the most common; some cases involved higher-order perceptual disturbances, including complex, composite, or panoramic experiences [12]. Although delusional parasitosis associated with vitamin B12 deficiency has been reported [13], to the best of our knowledge, this is the first report describing the development of secondary delusional parasitosis preceded by persistent visual illusions in the context of vitamin B12 deficiency and MCI.

Vitamin B12 functions as a cofactor for methionine synthase in the central nervous system and is essential for maintaining myelin lipids and neuronal membrane integrity. Its deficiency leads to elevated levels of homocysteine and methylmalonic acid, which are associated with increased oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and abnormalities in fatty acid metabolism, ultimately resulting in neurotoxicity [14]. These molecular alterations may contribute to white matter microstructural damage and reduced axonal conduction efficiency, thereby disrupting the functional integration of brain networks. However, further research is required to determine the precise role of vitamin B12 in brain function.

The occipital lobe–parietal association cortex is a key network involved not only in the integration of visual information and spatial cognition but also in precision weighting during the integration of sensory input and prior expectations [15, 16]. In the present case, perceptual input derived from real stimuli, namely, lint and skin debris, was preserved. This differs from visual agnosia, in which object recognition itself is impaired. Thus, the patient’s experience is better understood within the framework of predictive processing. Specifically, when occipital dysfunction reduces the precision of visual input, potentially disrupting the balance between top-down predictions and bottom-up sensory signal processing. The resulting increase in prediction errors may cause ambiguous but real stimuli to be assigned excessive or aberrant salience [17, 18]. Although the phenomenon in our patient can be broadly conceptualized as a visual illusion, it may also share features with pareidolia, a phenomenon wherein ambiguous sensory stimuli are perceived as meaningful forms based on prior expectations and higher-order cognitive processing. In this context, the patient may have misidentified visible small particles as mites because they appeared to be moving.

Patients with vitamin B12 deficiency exhibit alterations in cerebral blood flow compared with healthy controls, with partial recovery observed approximately 6 weeks after treatment [19]. These findings support the notion that neurological dysfunction associated with vitamin B12 deficiency may be at least partially reversible. The posttreatment improvement in cerebral blood flow in our patient is consistent with previous reports [19]. Given that delusional parasitosis is generally resistant to treatment [4], the early resolution of the secondary delusional symptoms in our patient may have been due to visual function improvement.

Furthermore, the delusional content in our patient was partially shared by the patient’s spouse, presenting features consistent with shared psychotic phenomena. In delusional parasitosis, sharing of delusional beliefs among close relatives or cohabiting individuals has been reported in a notable proportion of cases, accounting for 14.4% (65 of 449 cases) [4]. Although the patient’s wife had no clear history of psychiatric illness, she exhibited mild forgetfulness and executive dysfunction, suggestive of MCI. Vulnerability in cognitive function and judgment may facilitate the acceptance and maintenance of delusional beliefs, thereby enabling the partial sharing [6]. Therefore, when managing delusional parasitosis in older adults, it is essential to adopt a comprehensive approach that extends beyond the patient and includes cohabiting family members and caregivers, with careful attention to their mental health, cognitive function, and psychological burden.

Conclusions

This case underscores the importance of considering not only neurodegenerative disorders but also reversible medical conditions, such as vitamin B12 deficiency, in the differential diagnosis of visual illusions in older adults. Furthermore, in managing delusional parasitosis in elderly patients, attention should be directed not only to the patient but also to the mental state of closely involved caregivers and cohabiting individuals.
