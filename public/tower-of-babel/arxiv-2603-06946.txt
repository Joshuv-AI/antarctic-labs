Joint MDPs and Reinforcement Learning in Coupled-Dynamics Environments
Ege C. Kaya, Mahsa Ghasemi, Abolfazl Hashemi

Abstract
Many distributional quantities in reinforcement learning are intrinsically joint across actions, including distributions of gaps and probabilities of superiority. However, the classical Markov decision process (MDP) formalism specifies only marginal laws and leaves the joint law of counterfactual one-step outcomes across multiple possible actions at a state unspecified. We study coupled-dynamics environments with a multi-action generative interface which can sample counterfactual one-step outcomes for multiple actions under shared exogenous randomness. We propose joint MDPs (JMDPs) as a formalism for such environments by augmenting an MDP with a multi-action sample transition model which specifies a coupling of one-step counterfactual outcomes, while preserving standard MDP interaction as marginal observations. We adopt and formalize a one-step coupling regime where dependence across actions is confined to immediate counterfactual outcomes at the queried state. In this regime, we derive Bellman operators for n n th-order return moments, providing dynamic programming and incremental algorithms with convergence guarantees. Keywords: reinforcement learning, reinforcement learning theory, Markov decision processes, distributional reinforcement learning, policy evaluation, dynamic programming, stochastic approximation, counterfactual modeling.

Introduction

Distributional reinforcement learning (DRL) studies the discounted return random variable (RV)
Z^{\pi}(s,a)
belonging to a policy
\pi
, and seeks to represent or estimate its distributional properties beyond its expectation. Formally,

\begin{gathered}Z^{\pi}(s,a)=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R_{t},\;S_{0}=s,A_{0}=a,\;A_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid S_{t})\\
R_{t}\sim P_{R}(\cdot\mid S_{t},A_{t}),\;S_{t+1}\sim P_{S}(\cdot\mid S_{t},A_{t}).\end{gathered}

(1)

DRL methods customarily learn per-action marginal return laws at state
s
, i.e.,
\{\mathrm{Law}(Z^{\pi}(s,\allowbreak a))\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}

[
3
,
4
]
. However, a range of distributional quantities that are of interest in decision-making
[
2
,
24
]
are not functions of per-action marginals alone. For actions
a,\tilde{a}\in\mathcal{A}
, examples include:

1.

the
gap
RV
G^{\pi}(s;a,\tilde{a}):=Z^{\pi}(s,a)-Z^{\pi}(s,\tilde{a})
and its distribution
\mathrm{Law}(G^{\pi}(s;a,\tilde{a}))
,

2.

any tail functional of
G^{\pi}(s;a,\tilde{a})
, such as the quantile
q_{\alpha}(G^{\pi}(s;a,\tilde{a}))
or the
\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(G^{\pi}(s;a,\tilde{a}))
,

3.

the

\mathbb{P}(Z^{\pi}(s,a)>Z^{\pi}(s,\tilde{a}))
.

These quantities depend on the joint law of
(Z^{\pi}(s,a)),\allowbreak Z^{\pi}(s,\tilde{a}))
and thus require knowledge of coupling structure.

A limitation of the Markov decision process (MDP) formalism.
MDPs specify the marginal distributions of the reward and successor state under each action. However, they make no specification about the joint distribution of
counterfactual, one-step outcomes across multiple actions at a state
. Therefore, joint objects such as
\mathrm{Law}(Z^{\pi}(s,a)-Z^{\pi}(s,\tilde{a}))
are not well-defined without adopting additional coupling conventions
[
32
]
.

Coupled-dynamics environments.
The preceding limitation is substantive in environments where counterfactual one-step outcomes under multiple actions are operationally meaningful and sampleable. A canonical setting is scenario-based simulation, where an exogenous disturbance
U
is realized at each decision point and one-step outcomes are functions of
(s,a,U)
. Evaluating multiple candidate actions under the same realized disturbance is standard in simulation optimization
[
15
,
26
]
and yields a multi-action generative interface.

Contribution and scope.

•

We propose
joint MDPs
(JMDPs) as a formalism for coupled-dynamics environments. A JMDP augments an MDP with a multi-action sample transition model that specifies a coupling of one-step counterfactual outcomes.

•

We focus exclusively on
policy evaluation
: For a fixed policy
\pi
, we derive moment Bellman operators up to arbitrary finite order and provide both dynamic programming (DP) and incremental estimation algorithms with convergence guarantees, along with computable Bellman residual certificates
[
25
]
.

•

One-step coupling regime.
We adopt a one-step coupling regime throughout the work, where the dependence across actions is confined to the immediate counterfactual outcomes at the queried state. The future stochasticity is assumed to be independent across counterfactual branches conditional on successor states. This regime is aligned with multi-action one-step interface and avoids the exponential blow-up associated with fully-coupled counterfactual trees.

MDP formalism and generative interfaces.
In the standard MDP formalism, the environment is specified by a per-action reward law and a per-action transition kernel

,

,

,

,

]
. These methods learn per-state action

(Z^{\pi}(s,a))_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
. A complementary line studies multivariate return distributions induced by vector-valued rewards and cumulants, including Bellman-GAN
[

]
, MD3QN
[

, and the recent foundations and convergent DP/TD methods of

Moment-based evaluation, risk sensitivity, and gap/advantage distributions.
Return-moment policy evaluation has a long history
[
27
]
, including DP characterizations of variance and modern TD-style estimators for variance and related quantities
[

,

,

. Coherent risk measures such as CVaR have likewise been incorporated via risk-aware Bellman operators
[

]
. These works within a single action’s return law. By contrast, our target quantities are intrinsically joint across actions and require mixed moments encoding cross-action dependence. This distinction is especially visible for gap and advantage distributions
[

Z^{\pi}(s,a)-Z^{\pi}(s,\tilde{a})
depends on an unspecified coupling, motivating canonical-coupling axioms
[

. Our approach instead models environments in which the coupling is part of the environment itself. Under our one-step coupling regime, gap and advantage RVs are well-defined by construction, and their moments are computable from learned mixed moments.

Counterfactual modeling perspectives.
The multi-action generative interface admits a counterfactual interpretation: At a state, the environment reveals multiple potential one-step outcomes under shared exogenous randomness. This connects to potential-outcome and structural causal model (SCM) perspectives in RL
[
17
,
1
]
. We do not address identifiability or learning such an SCM from data, rather, assuming simulator access to counterfactual one-step queries, we ask what formalism and policy-evaluation theory are appropriate. Existing causal and counterfactual approaches motivate the use of cross-action structure, but do not formalize or estimate the joint counterfactual return laws studied here.

Overall, prior DRL and risk-sensitive RL methods either model marginal per-action return laws or multivariate returns arising from vector-valued rewards along a realized trajectory. Our work isolates a different axis of jointness: across actions at a fixed state, induced by coupled-environment randomness. We propose joint MDPs as a formalism and algorithmic basis for policy evaluation of both marginal and joint return moments, enabling principled treatment of intrinsically joint distributional quantities.

Preliminaries

Definition 3.1

(MDP)
.

An MDP is a quintuple
(\mathcal{S},\allowbreak\mathcal{A},\allowbreak P_{R},\allowbreak P_{S},\allowbreak\gamma)
, where
\mathcal{S}
is a finite state space,
\mathcal{A}
is a finite action space with
N=\left\lvert\mathcal{A}\right\rvert
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor,
P_{R}(\cdot\mid s,a)
is a reward kernel over
[0,1]
and
P_{S}(\cdot\mid s,a)
is a transition kernel over
\mathcal{S}
.

A policy is a Markov kernel
\pi(\cdot\mid s)\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})
. For measurable spaces
(\Omega_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i})
, let
\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{i})
denote the set of probability measures over
\Omega_{i}
. An
m
-variate distribution
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{1}\times\ldots\Omega_{m})
is a coupling of its marginals
\{\nu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, where
\nu_{i}
is the
i
th coordinate marginal. We write
[m]
for
\{1,\ldots,m\}
. To conclude the section, let us recall a standard convention, used throughout RL, for describing the interaction with an environment via a generative model.

Definition 3.2

(Sample transition model (STM)
[
4
]
)
.

Let
\xi\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
and
\pi
be a policy. The STM is the joint distribution of
(S,A,R,S^{\prime})
generated by

\begin{gathered}S\sim\xi,\quad A\mid S\sim\pi(\cdot\mid S),\\
R\mid S,A\sim P_{R}(\cdot\mid S,A),\quad S^{\prime}\mid S,A\sim P_{S}(\cdot\mid S,A).\end{gathered}

(2)

4
Coupled-Dynamics Environments and Joint MDPs

Many distributional quantities in RL are intrinsically joint across actions at a fixed state, including gaps and advantages, probabilities of superiority, and tail functionals of such quantities. Formalizing such quantities requires a joint law for
counterfactual
one-step outcomes that would occur under different actions taken from the same state, under the same realization of exogenous randomness. The MDP formalism does not account for this, since it only specifies marginal one-step laws for each action and leaves the joint counterfactual structure unspecified. Consequently, environments that agree on all one-step marginals when modeled using an MDP may still disagree on joint-action quantities.

We now present a multi-action variant of the STM, the
m
-joint STM (
m
-JSTM). Informally, an
m
-JSTM allows the learner to query
m
distinct actions at the same state, and obtain
m
coupled counterfactual one-step outcomes.

Definition 4.1

(
m
-JSTM)
.

For an integer
m\geq 1
, an
m
-JSTM is a kernel

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{J}_{m}(\cdot\mid s,a_{1:m})\in\mathcal{P}\big(([0,1]\times\mathcal{S})^{m}\big),\;s\in\mathcal{S},a_{1:m}\in\mathcal{A}^{m}\text{ distinct},\end{gathered}

(3)

such that each coordinate marginal matches the classical one-step marginals. Concretely, if
((R_{i},S_{i}^{\prime}))_{i=1}^{m}\sim\mathcal{J}_{m}(\cdot\mid s,a_{1:m})
, then for each coordinate
i\in[m]
,

\begin{gathered}(R_{i},S^{\prime}_{i})\sim\big(P_{R}(\cdot\mid s,a_{i}),P_{S}(\cdot\mid s,a_{i})\big).\end{gathered}

(4)

Specifically, when
m=N
and
a_{1:N}
is a fixed ordering of
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{J}_{m}(\cdot\mid s,a_{1:N})
specifies a joint coupling across all action at
s
. The additional expressiveness provided by an
m
-JSTM is the
dependence
it specifies across counterfactual outcomes for different actions. When this dependence is nontrivial, the MDP formalism omits information that is directly relevant for joint distributional questions. This motivates treating the coupling as part of the model.

Definition 4.2

(Coupled-dynamics environment)
.

An environment with marginal kernels
(P_{R},P_{S})
is a
coupled-dynamics
environment if it admits an
m
-JSTM
\mathcal{J}_{m}
for some
m\geq 2
such that for some query
(s,a_{1:m})
,
\mathcal{J}_{m}(\cdot\mid s,a_{1:m})
is
not
the product coupling of its marginals. Equivalently, the environment contains joint one-step counterfactual structure that is not determined by
(P_{R},P_{S})
alone, and is therefore lost under a purely marginal MDP description.

The following examples illustrate the issue: Identical MDP marginals can conceal different joint structures.

Example 1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{1,2\}
and fix
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Let
U\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1/2)
, and let the counterfactual rewards be
R^{(1)}=U
,
R^{(2)}=1-U
. Each action’s marginal reward law is
\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1/2)
, while their joint law is perfectly anti-correlated.
\mathbb{P}(R^{(1)}>R^{(2)})=1/2
and
\mathbb{E}[R^{(1)}R^{(2)}]=0
, neither fact determined by the marginals alone.

Example 2
.

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{0,1\}
,
\mathcal{A}=\{1,2\}
, and fix
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Let
U\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1/2)
, and the counterfactual next states be

(2.1)

S^{\prime(1)}=S^{\prime(2)}=U
. Each action’s marginal transition law is uniform on
\mathcal{S}
, while
S^{\prime(1)}=S^{\prime(2)}
.

(2.2)

S^{\prime(1)}=U
and
S^{\prime(2)}=1-U
. Each action’s marginal transition law is uniform on
\mathcal{S}
, while
S^{\prime(1)}=1-S^{\prime(2)}
.

We now formalize a joint extension of the MDP model, appropriate for modeling coupled-dynamics environments.

Definition 4.3

(JMDP)
.

A
JMDP
is a quadruple
(\mathcal{S},\allowbreak\mathcal{A},\allowbreak\gamma,\allowbreak\mathcal{J})
, where
\mathcal{S}
is a finite state space,
\mathcal{A}
is a finite action space with
N=\left\lvert\mathcal{A}\right\rvert
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor, and
\mathcal{J}(\cdot\mid s)\in\mathcal{P}(([0,1]\times\mathcal{S})^{N})
is a Markov kernel over counterfactual one-step outcome tables
((R^{(a)},S^{\prime(a)}))_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
.

At time
t
, conditional on
S_{t}=s
, the environment samples a one-step outcome table
((R^{(a)},\allowbreak S^{\prime(a)}))_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\allowbreak\sim\mathcal{J}(\cdot\mid s)
. The agent selects action
A_{t}
to execute, the realized transition is given by the executed action:
(R_{t},S_{t+1})=(R_{t}^{(A_{t})},S_{t+1}^{(A_{t})}).
The remaining coordinates of the sampled outcome table are counterfactual and do not affect the realized trajectory.

Remark 4.4
.

Every JMDP induces a marginal MDP description of the environment when we take coordinate marginals of the kernel
\mathcal{J}
. To see this, consider the collection of
1
-JSTMs
\mathcal{J}_{1}(\cdot\mid s,a)
for each
(s,a)
such that

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{J}_{1}(\cdot\mid s,a):=\mathrm{Law}\big((R^{(a)},S^{\prime(a)})\big),\;\text{where }\big((R^{(b)},S^{\prime(b)})\big)_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\sim\mathcal{J}(\cdot\mid s).\end{gathered}

(5)

Then, the reward and transition marginals
P_{R}(\cdot\mid s,a)
and
P_{S}(\cdot\mid s,a)
of
\mathcal{J}_{1}
will constitute the reward and transition kernels of a classical MDP description of the environment.

In a similar vein, fixing
m\geq 2
, for any state
s
and any
m
-tuple of distinct actions
a_{1:m}
, we can induce an
m
-JSTM
\mathcal{J}_{m}
as the joint law of the
m
queried coordinates:

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{J}_{m}(\cdot\mid s,a_{1:m}):=\mathrm{Law}\big((R^{(a)},S^{\prime(a)})_{a\in a_{1:m}}\big),\;\text{where }\big((R^{(b)},S^{\prime(b)})\big)_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\sim\mathcal{J}(\cdot\mid s).\end{gathered}

(6)

To summarize, a coupled-dynamics environment is one where the marginal description of an MDP formalism does not determine the joint law of counterfactual one-step outcomes across actions. JMDPs model the missing structure via a kernel
\mathcal{J}(\cdot\mid s)
over counterfactual outcome tables, from which the standard MDP interaction model STM and the multi-action
m
-JSTM interface are obtained by marginalization. The JMDP formalism retains the dependence information discarded by an MDP description of the environment, essential for joint distributional questions.

The JMDP definition specifies how a counterfactual one-step outcome table is sampled at each visited state, and how a single executed action generates the realized transition. Before developing policy evaluation algorithms for joint quantities, it remains to formalize what we have referred to as the
one-step coupling
regime up to now, i.e., how the counterfactual dependence encoded in the outcome table relates across time. We focus on the case where cross-action dependence is confined to the
immediate
counterfactual outcomes at the queried state and all subsequent randomness is refreshed in the next step. This choice matches the multi-action sampling interfaces of interest and avoids the exponential blow-up of counterfactual trajectory trees.

Assumption 4.5

(Exogenous noise representation
[
8
,
20
]
)
.

We assume that there exist an i.i.d. exogenous sequence
(U_{t})_{t\geq 0}
over a measurable space
\mathcal{U}
, and measurable maps

\begin{gathered}g:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{U}\to[0,1],\quad h:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{S},\end{gathered}

(7)

such that at any time
t
and state
s
, the counterfactual one-step outcomes for any action
a\in\mathcal{A}
are related by

\begin{gathered}R^{(a)}_{t}=g(s,a,U_{t}),\quad S^{(a)}_{t+1}=h(s,a,U_{t}).\end{gathered}

(8)

Thus, the full outcome table sampled by the JMDP kernel can alternatively be expressed as

\big((R_{t}^{(a)},S^{(a)}_{t+1})\big)_{a\in\mathcal{A}}=\big((g(S_{t},a,U_{t}),h(S_{t},a,U_{t}))\big)_{a\in\mathcal{A}}.

(9)

Crucially, the preceding assumption means that after the executed transition to
S_{t+1}
, subsequent one-step outcome tables are generated using fresh exogenous variables
U_{t+1},U_{t+2},\ldots,
independent of
U_{t}
. The dependence across different actions is entirely mediated by the current table draw, and does not persist through future shared noise.

We now define the main object of the rest of our inquiry.

Definition 4.6

(Joint return vector)
.

Let
\pi
be a stationary policy. For any state
s
, we define the
joint return vector
belonging to
\pi
as
Z^{\pi}(s):=\big(Z^{\pi}(s,a)\big)_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
.

Under the one-step coupling regime, the components of
Z^{\pi}(s)
are coupled through
U_{0}
, while their continuations evolve independently, conditional on their successor states.

5
Joint Iterative Policy Evaluation

We now develop DP and incremental policy evaluation schemes for JMDPs. Throughout this section, we fix a policy
\pi
, and the goal is to estimate joint moments of
Z^{\pi}(s)
, including mixed moments. We start with the case of moments up to
2
nd order, displaying an approachable and interpretable instance of the general case of
n
th-order moments. Proofs are deferred to Appendix
A
.

Let
\mathcal{X}:=\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
denote the state-action space. For each
(s,a)\in\mathcal{X}
, we define the first moments

\mu^{\pi}(s,a):=\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{\pi}(s,a)\right],

(10)

and for each
(s,a,\tilde{s},\tilde{a})\in\mathcal{X}^{2}
, the second moments

\Sigma^{\pi}(s,a,\tilde{s},\tilde{a}):=\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{\pi}(s,a)\,Z^{\pi}(\tilde{s},\tilde{a})\right].

(11)

The algorithms defined below will operate on candidate moment collections
M=(M_{\mu},M_{\Sigma})
, where

\begin{gathered}M_{\mu}\in\{f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\},\quad M_{\Sigma}\in\{f(s,a,\tilde{s},\tilde{a}):\mathcal{X}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}\}.\end{gathered}

(12)

Thus,
M_{\mu}(s,a)
will be a candidate value for
\mu^{\pi}(s,a)
, and
M_{\Sigma}(s,a,\tilde{s},\tilde{a})
for
\Sigma^{\pi}(s,a,\tilde{s},\tilde{a})
. The introduced algorithms will map any such dimensionally consistent moment collection
M
to the true collection of moments
M^{\pi}_{2}:=(\mu^{\pi},\Sigma^{\pi})
.

We now define the
2
nd-order joint Bellman operator.

Definition 5.1

(
2
nd-order joint Bellman operator)
.

The
2
nd-order joint Bellman operator
T^{\pi}_{2}
maps a moment collection
M
to
T^{\pi}_{2}M
coordinatewise through the equations

\begin{gathered}(T^{\pi}_{2}M)_{\mu}(s,a):=\mathbb{E}\big[R^{(a)}+\gamma M_{\mu}\big(S^{\prime(a)},A^{\prime(a)}\big)\mid S=s\big],\end{gathered}

(13)

for each
(s,a)
. Furthermore, for each
(s,a,\tilde{s},\tilde{a})
,

\begin{gathered}(T^{\pi}_{2}M)_{\Sigma}(s,a,\tilde{s},\tilde{a}):=\mathbb{E}\big[R^{(a)}\tilde{R}^{(\tilde{a})}+\gamma R^{(a)}M_{\mu}\big(\tilde{S}^{\prime(\tilde{a})},\tilde{A}^{\prime(\tilde{a})}\big)+\gamma\tilde{R}^{(\tilde{a})}M_{\mu}\big(S^{\prime(a)},A^{\prime(a)}\big)\\
+\gamma^{2}M_{\Sigma}\big(S^{\prime(a)},A^{\prime(a)},\tilde{S}^{\prime(\tilde{a})},\tilde{A}^{\prime(\tilde{a})}\big)\mid(S,\tilde{S})=(s,\tilde{s})\big].\end{gathered}

(14)

It is instructional to consider the joint laws with respect to which we take the expectation in these equations. In (
13
), this is the joint law of
(R^{(a)},S^{\prime(a)},A^{\prime(a)})
, where

(R^{(a)},S^{\prime(a)})\sim J_{1}(\cdot\mid s,a),\quad A^{\prime(a)}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid S^{\prime(a)}),

(15)

where
\mathcal{J}_{1}
is a
1
-JSTM induced from the JMDP kernel
\mathcal{J}
.

This is also the case in the “diagonal” scenario of (
14
) (
s=\tilde{s}
and
a=\tilde{a}
), where the expression simplifies to

\begin{gathered}(T^{\pi}_{2}M)_{\Sigma}(s,a,s,a)=\mathbb{E}\big[\big(R^{(a)}\big)^{2}+2\gamma R^{(a)}M_{\mu}\big(S^{\prime(a)},A^{\prime(a)}\big)+\gamma^{2}M_{\Sigma}\big(S^{\prime(a)},A^{\prime(a)},S^{\prime(a)},A^{\prime(a)}\big)\mid S=s\big],\end{gathered}

(16)

When
a\neq\tilde{a}
, the joint law is of
(R^{(a)},\allowbreak S^{\prime(a)},\allowbreak A^{\prime(a)},\allowbreak\tilde{R}^{(\tilde{a})},\allowbreak\tilde{S}^{\prime(\tilde{a})},\tilde{A}^{\prime(\tilde{a})})
. When we have
s\neq\tilde{s}
,

\begin{gathered}(R^{(a)},S^{\prime(a)})\sim\mathcal{J}_{1}(\cdot\mid s,a),\quad(\tilde{R}^{(\tilde{a})},\tilde{S}^{\prime(\tilde{a})})\sim\mathcal{J}_{1}(\cdot\mid\tilde{s},\tilde{a}),\quad A^{\prime(a)}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid S^{\prime(a)}),\quad\tilde{A}^{\prime(\tilde{a})}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid\tilde{S}^{\prime(\tilde{a})}).\end{gathered}

(17)

\mathcal{J}_{1}
is a
1
-JSTM induced from
\mathcal{J}
, the draws are independent.

Crucially, in the “same-state” case where
s=\tilde{s}
, we have

\begin{gathered}\big(R^{(a)},S^{\prime(a)},\tilde{R}^{(\tilde{a})},\tilde{S}^{\prime(\tilde{a})}\big)\sim\mathcal{J}_{2}\big(\cdot\mid s,(a,\tilde{a})\big),\quad A^{\prime(a)}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid S^{\prime(a)}),\quad\tilde{A}^{\prime(\tilde{a})}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid\tilde{S}^{\prime(\tilde{a})}),\end{gathered}

(18)

where
\mathcal{J}_{2}
is a
2
-JSTM induced from
\mathcal{J}
.

Note that, by construction,
M^{\pi}_{2}
is a fixed point of
T^{\pi}_{2}
, since

\begin{gathered}\mu^{\pi}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}\big[R^{(a)}+\gamma Z^{\pi}\big(S^{\prime(a)},A^{\prime(a)}\big)\mid S=s\big],\\
\Sigma^{\pi}(s,a,\tilde{s},\tilde{a})=\mathbb{E}\big[\big(R^{(a)}+\gamma Z^{\pi}\big(S^{\prime(a)},A^{\prime(a)}\big)\big)\cdot\big(\tilde{R}^{(\tilde{a})}+\gamma Z^{\pi}\big(\tilde{S}^{\prime(\tilde{a})},\tilde{A}^{\prime(\tilde{a})}\big)\big)\mid(S,\tilde{S})=(s,\tilde{s})\big].\end{gathered}

(19)

We now prove that
T^{\pi}_{2}
is a contraction mapping.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\lambda:=2/(1-\gamma)
. For any moment collection
M
, define the norm

\left\lVert M\right\rVert_{\lambda}:=\max\left\{\left\lVert M_{\mu}\right\rVert_{\infty},\frac{1}{\lambda}\left\lVert M_{\Sigma}\right\rVert_{\infty}\right\}.

(20)

Then,
T^{\pi}_{2}
is a
\gamma
-contraction in
\left\lVert\,\cdot\,\right\rVert_{\lambda}
.

We now consider the iterative process expressed by

M_{k+1}=T^{\pi}_{2}M_{k}.

(21)

Theorem 5.3
.

T^{\pi}_{2}
admits a unique fixed point
M^{\pi}_{2}
. Moreover, for any initialization
M_{0}
, the iteration (
21
) has geometric convergence with rate
\gamma
in
\left\lVert\,\cdot\,\right\rVert_{\lambda}
:

\left\lVert M_{k}-M^{\pi}_{2}\right\rVert_{\lambda}\leq\gamma^{k}\left\lVert M_{0}-M^{\pi}_{2}\right\rVert_{\lambda}.

(22)

Furthermore, for any moment collection
M
, define the
2
nd-order Bellman residual as
\left\lVert M-T^{\pi}_{2}M\right\rVert_{\lambda}
.
Then,

\left\lVert M-M^{\pi}_{2}\right\rVert_{\lambda}\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\left\lVert M-T^{\pi}_{2}M\right\rVert_{\lambda}.

(23)

This enables the interpretation of (
21
) as an algorithm with a computable stopping criterion for
\epsilon
accuracy in
\left\lVert\,\cdot\,\right\rVert_{\lambda}
, which we call
2
nd-order joint iterative policy evaluation (JIPE-
2
).

5.1
JIPE-2 with Function Approximation

In high-dimensional or continuous state spaces, exact tabular JIPE-
2
becomes impractical, so it is preferable to move to a function-approximation setting in which the moments are restricted to a parameterized
\hat{M}(\theta)
, with a PSD-structured parameterization for
M_{\Sigma}
to preserve valid
2
nd moment geometry. The resulting objective is a projected fixed point equation of the form
\hat{M}(\theta^{*})=\Pi T^{\pi}_{2}\hat{M}(\theta^{*})
. This yields a projected JIPE-
2
iteration with a standard projection for the mean term and a PSD-constrained projection for the
2
nd-moment term. Under standard regularity conditions, Appendix
B
shows that the projected operator is contractive in a suitable weighted norm, giving existence and uniqueness of the projected fixed point, convergence of the iteration, and a standard approximation-error bound.

5.2
Incremental JIPE-2

We next give an incremental variant of JIPE-
2
, suitable when
\mathcal{J}
is accessed only through samples from its induced
1
- and
2
-JSTMs. The method is an instance of stochastic approximation for the operator
T^{\pi}_{2}
. Let us define an index set to simplify indexing a moment collection
M=(M_{\mu},M_{\Sigma})
:

\mathcal{I}_{2}:=\mathcal{X}\times\{\mu\}\cup\mathcal{X}^{2}\times\{\Sigma\}.

(24)

For
i=((s,a),\mu)
, we write
M(i)=M_{\mu}(s,a)
, and for
i=((s,a),(\tilde{s},\tilde{a}),\Sigma)
, we write
M(i)=M_{\Sigma}(s,a,\tilde{s},\tilde{a})

We define the random one-sample backup

(i)

If

\hat{T}^{\pi}_{2}(M,i):=R^{(a)}+\gamma M_{\mu}\big(S^{\prime(a)},A^{\prime(a)}\big).

(25)

(ii)

If

(R^{(a)},S^{\prime(a)})\sim\mathcal{J}_{1}(\cdot\mid s,a)

A^{\prime(a)}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid S^{\prime(a)})
. We set

\begin{gathered}\hat{T}^{\pi}_{2}(M,i):=\big(R^{(a)}\big)^{2}+2\gamma R^{(a)}M_{\mu}\big(S^{\prime(a)},A^{\prime(a)}\big)+\gamma^{2}M_{\Sigma}\big(S^{\prime(a)},A^{\prime(a)},S^{\prime(a)},A^{\prime(a)}\big).\end{gathered}

(26)

(iii)

If
i=((s,a),(\tilde{s},\tilde{a}),\Sigma)

(27)

For

M_{k+1}(i)=\begin{cases}(1-\alpha_{k}(i))M_{k}(i)+\alpha_{k}(i)\hat{T}^{\pi}_{2}(M_{k},i),&i=I_{k},\\
M_{k}(i),&i\neq I_{k},\end{cases}

[

Then, the incremental JIPE-

\left\lVert\,\cdot\,\right\rVert_{\lambda}

\left\lVert M_{k}-M^{\pi}_{2}\right\rVert_{\lambda}\to 0\;\;\text{almost surely as}\;k\to\infty.

5.3
Generalization to Higher Moments

It is logically straightforward to extend the construction to the case of
n
th-order moments, with heavier notation. For each
k\in[n]
, we consider a
k
th-order moment collection

M^{(k)}:=\mathcal{X}^{k}\to\mathbb{R},\quad M^{(k)}(x_{1:k})=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}Z^{\pi}(x_{i})\right].

(31)

The collection of all moments of orders
1
through
n
is
M:=(M^{(1)},\ldots,M^{(n)}).

Let
k\leq n
and
x_{1:k}\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
. Let
\{G_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
be the partition of
[k]
into groups that share the same current state, i.e.,
i,j\in G_{l}
for some
l
if and only if
s_{i}=s_{j}
. For each group
G_{l}
with common state
s^{(l)}
and action subtuple
a_{G_{l}}:=(a_{i})_{i\in G_{l}}
, let

\big((R_{i},S^{\prime}_{i})\big)_{i\in G_{l}}\sim\mathcal{J}_{\left\lvert G_{l}\right\rvert}(\cdot\mid s^{(l)},a_{G_{l}})

(32)

under the induced
\left\lvert G_{l}\right\rvert
-JSTM. Assume these groupwise draws are independent across
l
. Conditioned on
(S^{\prime}_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
, let actions be drawn independently as
A^{\prime}_{i}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid S^{\prime}_{i})
for
i\in[k]
. We write
X^{\prime}_{I}:=(X^{\prime}_{i})_{i\in I}
for any index set
I\subseteq[k]
. This construction is the direct
k

2
nd-order scenario. Within a state, the relevant coordinates share one coupled draw via induced JSTMs, whereas across distinct states, the one-step randomness is independent under the one-step coupling regime.

We define the operator
T^{\pi}_{n}
through the system of equations

\begin{gathered}(T^{\pi}_{n}M)_{k}(x_{1:k}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{I\subseteq[k]}\gamma^{\left\lvert I\right\rvert}\big(\prod_{i\notin I}R_{i}\big)M_{\left\lvert I\right\rvert}(X^{\prime}_{I})\mid x_{1:k}\right],\end{gathered}

(33)

for each
k\in[n]
and each
x_{1:k}\in\mathcal{X}^{k}
. We also define

\left\lVert M\right\rVert_{\lambda}:=\max_{k\in[n]}\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}\left\lVert M^{(k)}\right\rVert_{\infty},

(34)

for
\lambda_{k}:=(2/(1-\gamma))^{k-1}
. The operator
T^{\pi}_{n}
is a
\gamma
-contraction with respect to
\left\lVert\,\cdot\,\right\rVert_{\lambda}
, and admits as unique fixed point the true collection of moments up to
n
th order,
M^{\pi}_{n}
. The JIPE-
n
algorithm and its incremental variant can be defined analogously to the previous section, and admit similar results to those of Theorems
5.3
and
5.4
.

5.4
Connection to the Gap RV

We now return to the discussion of the gap RV
G^{\pi}(s;a,\tilde{a})
. Quantities that only involve the first moments require no joint modeling, for instance,
\mathbb{E}[G^{\pi}(s;a,\tilde{a})]=\mu^{\pi}(s,a)-\mu^{\pi}(s,\tilde{a})
is already determined by the marginal dynamics. However, joint structure becomes relevant when we consider comparative or risk-sensitive questions about gaps. JMDPs specify a concrete coupling of counterfactual outcomes for
(a,\tilde{a})
at
s
, inducing a well-defined joint return law. The moment recursions of the previous section provide mixed moments
\mathbb{E}[Z^{\pi}(s,a)^{i}Z^{\pi}(s,\tilde{a})^{j}]
, which determine the moments of
G^{\pi}(s;a,\tilde{a})
and enable moment-based bounds or approximations for comparative risk criteria. For example,
2
nd-order mixed moments yield the variance of the gap:

\begin{gathered}\mathrm{var}\big(G^{\pi}(s;a,\tilde{a})\big)=\Sigma^{\pi}(s,a,s,a)-\Sigma^{\pi}(s,\tilde{a},s,\tilde{a})-2\Sigma^{\pi}(s,a,s,\tilde{a})-\big(\mu^{\pi}(s,a)-\mu^{\pi}(s,\tilde{a})\big)^{2},\end{gathered}

(35)

where all moments are computable through JIPE-
2
.

Similarly, if
\mu_{G}:=\mathbb{E}[G^{\pi}(s;a,\tilde{a})>\allowbreak 0]
, by the Chebyshev inequality
[
9
]

\mathbb{P}(G^{\pi}(s;a,\tilde{a})\leq\allowbreak 0)\leq\allowbreak\sigma^{2}_{G}/(\sigma^{2}_{G}+\mu^{2}_{G})
, where
\sigma^{2}_{G}\allowbreak:=\mathrm{var}(G^{\pi}(s;a,\tilde{a}))
.

7
Conclusion

We introduced JMDPs for coupled-dynamics environments that explicitly model the joint law of counterfactual outcomes across actions. Under a one-step coupling regime, we derived Bellman operators for return moments and obtained convergence guarantees for DP algorithms and their incremental variants, along with a projected function-approximation formulation. This makes intrinsically joint quantities such as gap moments and inferiority-probability bounds computable from multi-action simulator access. Experiments in coupled tabular and ALE environments show that learned mixed moments recover meaningful cross-action dependence absent from marginal MDP descriptions. A natural next step is control: extending joint moment evaluation to policy improvement under joint distributional objectives in JMDPs.

JIPE-2 with Function Approximation

The exact DP algorithm, namely JIPE-
2

\mathcal{X}

\hat{M}(\theta)=(\hat{M}_{\mu}(\theta_{\mu}),\hat{M}_{\Sigma}(\theta_{\Sigma}))

\theta=(\theta_{\mu},\theta_{\Sigma})

Specifically, we adopt a linear function approximation architecture. Assuming access to a set of basis functions, formulated as feature maps

B.3
Necessity of Assumption
B.3

While versions of Assumption
B.3
are standard in approximate dynamic programming and off-policy RL
[

P
.
the specific utilization in the context of an on-policy evaluation scheme studied here emerges
uniquely from the proposed JMDP formalism.
In classical on-policy policy evaluation, the sampling distribution perfectly aligns with the stationary distribution of the transition kernel. In our setting, while the marginal sampling distribution
\nu
is the invariant measure of the marginal transition kernel
P^{\pi}
, the product distribution
\nu\otimes\nu
we were forced to use for the weighted projection
\Pi_{\Sigma}
is fundamentally not the invariant measure of the coupled joint transition kernel
P_{2}
.

Intuitively, Assumption
B.3
restricts the degree to which the shared exogenous noise in the coupled-dynamics environment can couple counterfactual trajectories. The product distribution
\nu\otimes\nu
establishes a
theoretical
baseline where the two queried state-action pairs
(s,a)
and
(\tilde{s},\tilde{a})
and their subsequent transitions evolve with
strict statistical independence
. The transition kernel
P_{2}
however dictates how these variables
actually evolve
given that they originated from the exact same state under the exact same exogenous noise realization. The coefficient
c_{\rho}
dictates that this coupled one-step transition must not concentrate the probability mass of the successor joint state-action pairs into a narrow region of the product space that possesses low probability mass under the independent product measure
\nu\otimes\nu
. If the environment’s coupling deterministically forces the two counterfactual trajectories to transition to a highly specific joint configuration that is exceedingly rare under independent sampling, the underlying density ratio diverges. In such pathological cases,
c_{\rho}
becomes prohibitively large, and
\gamma^{2}
is rendered insufficient to suppress the amplification of the function approximation error, ultimately causing the iterative projection sequence to diverge.

To concretize our discussion, consider the environment defined by a finite state space
\mathcal{S}
of cardinality
M
and a finite action space
\mathcal{A}
of cardinality
2
, denoted as
\mathcal{A}=\{a,\tilde{a}\}
. Let the target policy
\pi
be the uniform distribution over
\mathcal{A}
for all states. The transition mechanism operates by drawing an exogenous noise variable from a specified distribution to determine the successor states. Consequently, the invariant state-action distribution
\nu
is uniform over
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, yielding
\nu(x)=\frac{1}{2M}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and the product measure evaluates to
(\nu\otimes\nu)(x,y)=\frac{1}{4M^{2}}
for all
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}^{2}
.

Now, let us consider two cases:

Example 1: Statistically independent counterfactual transitions.

Suppose the exogenous noise variable is a vector
U=(U_{a},U_{\tilde{a}})
, where each component is drawn independently from a uniform distribution over
\mathcal{S}
. The deterministic transition map is defined such that
g_{S}(s,a,U)=U_{a}
and
g_{S}(s,\tilde{a},U)=U_{\tilde{a}}
. Under this construction, the counterfactual trajectories evolve with strict statistical independence. The joint transition kernel
P_{2}
factors exactly as the tensor product of the marginal transition kernels, i.e.
P_{2}=P_{1}\otimes P_{1}
. As shown in Lemma
B.6

P_{1}
is non-expansive in
L_{2}(\nu)
; the tensor product
P_{1}\otimes P_{1}
preserves this non-expansiveness in
L_{2}(\nu\otimes\nu)
, i.e., for any function
f\in L_{2}(\nu\otimes\nu)
, the inequality
\|P_{2}f\|_{\nu\otimes\nu}\leq\|f\|_{\nu\otimes\nu}
holds. Therefore, this independent construction achieves its theoretical minimum of
c_{\rho}=1
. As the standard discount factor satisfies
\gamma\in(0,1)
, we have
\gamma^{2}\sqrt{c_{\rho}}=\gamma^{2}<1
.

Example 2: Perfectly coupled counterfactual transitions.

Suppose instead that the exogenous noise variable
U
is a scalar drawn from a uniform distribution over
\mathcal{S}
. The transition map forces perfect coupling by defining
g_{S}(s,a,U)=U
and
g_{S}(s,\tilde{a},U)=U
. Regardless of the action executed, the successor states for any two counterfactual branches are deterministically identical, such that
S^{\prime(a)}=S^{\prime(\tilde{a})}=U
almost surely. Consider the indicator function defined over the product space
f(x,y)=\mathbf{1}_{\{s_{x}=s_{y}\}}
. The squared norm of this function under the independent product measure evaluates to

\|f\|_{\nu\otimes\nu}^{2}=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{1}{4M^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{\{s_{x}=s_{y}\}}.

(83)

Because there are
M
matching state pairs and
4
action combinations for each, the summation yields
\|f\|_{\nu\otimes\nu}^{2}=\frac{4M}{4M^{2}}=\frac{1}{M}
, which implies
\|f\|_{\nu\otimes\nu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}
. Conversely, when evaluating the operator norm, the joint transition dictates that the successor state coordinates are identical with probability
1
. Consequently,
(P_{2}f)(x,y)=\mathbb{E}[f(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\mid X=x,Y=y\,]=1
everywhere on
\mathcal{X}^{2}
. That is,
\|P_{2}f\|_{\nu\otimes\nu}=\|1\|_{\nu\otimes\nu}=1
. The ratio of these norms establishes

\sqrt{c_{\rho}}\geq\|P_{2}\|_{\text{op}}=\sup_{g\neq 0}\frac{\|P_{2}g\|_{\nu\otimes\nu}}{\|g\|_{\nu\otimes\nu}}\geq\frac{\|P_{2}f\|_{\nu\otimes\nu}}{\|f\|_{\nu\otimes\nu}}=\sqrt{M},

(84)

If the state space is sufficiently large such that
M>\gamma^{-4}
, the scalar
c_{\rho}
becomes prohibitively large, yielding
\gamma^{2}\sqrt{c_{\rho}}>1
. That is, the transition mapping concentrates the entire probability mass onto the highly specific diagonal subspace
\{s_{x}=s_{y}\}
, representing an event that is rare under the theoretical baseline product distribution
\nu\otimes\nu
.
