Evolution of hemoglobinopathy diagnosis over 30 years in southern Spain: impact of migration and laboratory screening strategies
Authors: María-Angustias Molina-Arrebola, Alicia Sánchez-Crespo, Ana-María Alarcón-Gallo, Carmen Porrino-Herrera, Lucía Martínez-Carreras, Carlos González-Oller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHemoglobinopathies are a heterogeneous group of inherited disorders whose prevalence and phenotypic spectrum are influenced by population dynamics and diagnostic strategies. Longitudinal data from nonendemic European regions remain scarce.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective longitudinal analysis of hemoglobinopathies diagnosed at a regional hospital in southern Spain over a 30-year period (1996-2025). Diagnoses were classified as thalassemia syndromes or structural hemoglobin variants and analyzed according to the native or migrant origin of the patient. Temporal trends were assessed in relation to demographic changes and evolving laboratory methods.ResultsA total of 5340 hemoglobinopathies were identified: 1716 (32.1%) thalassemias and 3624 (67.9%) structural variants. Migrant patients accounted for 83.8% of diagnoses. Thalassemias predominated among native patients (88.1%), whereas structural variants, particularly hemoglobin S, predominated in migrant patients (78.6%). Annual diagnoses increased more than 5-fold during the study period, particularly after systematic high-performance liquid chromatography screening was introduced in 2008, with cases among migrant patients showing a strong linear trend (R2 = 0.86).DiscussionThis 30-year experience demonstrates a major shift in the epidemiology and diagnosis of hemoglobinopathies in southern Spain. Migration and advances in laboratory diagnostics have increased the detection of structural variants and complex genotypes, highlighting the need for continuously updated diagnostic algorithms in increasingly diverse populations.

Introduction

Hemoglobin (Hb) is a tetrameric protein composed of 2 pairs of globin chains, each associated with a heme group that enables reversible binding of oxygen. In adults, Hb consists predominantly of HbA (α2β2), with minor fractions of HbA2 (α2δ2) and fetal Hb (HbF; α2γ2).

Hemoglobinopathies are a heterogeneous group of inherited disorders caused by alterations in the structure or synthesis of Hb. They are broadly classified into structural variants and thalassemias, and their clinical expression ranges from asymptomatic carrier states to severe disease. These conditions represent one of the most common monogenic disorders worldwide, and they are increasingly encountered in nonendemic regions of Europe.1

In recent decades, migration has substantially modified the epidemiology of hemoglobinopathies across Europe, leading to increased prevalence, greater genetic diversity, and more complex diagnostic scenarios.2 In parallel, advances in laboratory techniques, including high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), capillary electrophoresis, and molecular methods, have substantially improved the detection and characterization of Hb variants.3

Neonatal screening for sickle cell disease (SCD) and other hemoglobinopathies using HPLC was implemented throughout the Andalusia region of Spain in 2018, in line with European recommendations.4 Early results confirmed a non-negligible birth prevalence of SCD and carrier states in the region.5

Major organizational changes were introduced in 2022, including the establishment of a specialized outpatient clinic for red blood cell disorders, enabling structured follow-up, patient education, and genetic counseling. The clinic joined national and regional working groups and registries, contributing to epidemiologic surveillance and harmonization of diagnostic practices.6,7 Laboratory-based reports were progressively replaced by comprehensive interpretative reports integrated into electronic health records, incorporating laboratory findings, reported origin, diagnostic conclusions, and management recommendations.3

Hospital Universitario Poniente in Almería, southern Spain, serves a reference population of approximately 280 000 inhabitants. In recent decades, Almería has undergone a marked demographic transformation, with foreign-born residents accounting for approximately 23% of the population in 2025 and 28% to 32% of patients within the hospital catchment area, exceeding one-third of the inhabitants in some municipalities (Figure 1). Migrants to this region originate predominantly from sub-Saharan Africa and the Maghreb (including Morocco, Senegal, Mali, and Ghana), although increasing heterogeneity has been observed in recent years, with more than 150 nationalities represented across the province.

In our most recent data (2024-2025), hemoglobinopathies were identified in patients from 40 countries. In addition, approximately 12% of affected individuals were second-generation patients born in Spain, reflecting the progressive consolidation of these disorders in the local population.2

This study aimed to analyze the evolution of hemoglobinopathy diagnoses in a single European laboratory over a 30-year period, focusing on the impact of migration and diagnostic advances on case detection, distribution, and complexity.

Methods

Hemoglobin disorders diagnosed between 1996 and 2025 were identified through several pathways: referral for suspected hemoglobinopathy, incidental detection of Hb variants during routine HbA1c testing, evaluation of hematologic indices suggestive of thalassemia, investigation of anemia, and genetic counseling requests. Since 2008, systematic screening using HPLC has been performed in migrant patients undergoing hematologic evaluation, including those referred from the Tropical Medicine Unit.8 Although primary care physicians do not have direct access to HPLC, routine HbA1c testing in migrant patients frequently enables the incidental detection of Hb variants. Chromatograms were systematically reviewed for abnormal peaks or analyzer flags suggestive of Hb variants, and samples with suspected abnormalities underwent the complete hemoglobinopathy diagnostic workup described in the sections that follow. Red blood cells were analyzed using automated hematology analyzers with laser-based light-scatter technology (ADVIA 2120; Siemens Diagnostics) or, since 2015, Beckman-Coulter’s proprietary multiparametric VCS-based analyzers with laser light scatter (DxH 800 and DxH 900). Peripheral blood films were stained using the May-Grünwald-Giemsa method, and red blood cell morphology was examined in detail.

Hemoglobin analysis was performed using HPLC systems (Hi-AUTO A1c 8140-8160; Menarini) from 1996 to 2015 and subsequently using Tosoh-based systems (G8 and G11; Horiba Medical). These platforms allow reliable quantification and detection of common Hb variants (S, C, D, and E). In accordance with standard recommendations, all suspected Hb variants were confirmed using a second analytical method. Presumed HbS was confirmed using a modified sodium metabisulfite solubility test.8,9 Other Hb variants were confirmed by capillary electrophoresis (Sebia). Molecular characterization by sequencing of the HBB, HBA1, and HBA2 genes was performed when phenotypic methods were insufficient to establish a definitive diagnosis, in patients with suspected rare or complex Hb variants, or when confirmation was required for genetic counseling or prenatal diagnosis. Both capillary electrophoresis and molecular studies were performed in a reference laboratory following standardized protocols.

In cases of microcytosis, iron metabolism was assessed to exclude iron deficiency. Heterozygous β-thalassemia was diagnosed when HbA2 levels exceeded 3.5%, including cases with concomitant iron deficiency when clinical and hematologic findings were strongly suggestive.10,11

Carriers of β-thalassemia may also show a mild increase in HbF (1%-5%, occasionally higher).12 Microcytosis associated with an isolated HbF level above 5% is considered suggestive of δβ-thalassemia, whereas elevated HbF levels in the presence of normocytosis raise the possibility of hereditary persistence of fetal hemoglobin (HPFH).8,13 In cases suggestive of δβ-thalassemia, molecular confirmation testing was performed using multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) with the SALSA MLPA Probemix P102 HBB kit (MRC Holland, Amsterdam, The Netherlands), allowing the detection of deletions involving the HBB and HBD genes. In select cases, additional molecular studies were performed when clinically relevant.

Molecular analysis of α-thalassemia was performed using targeted polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays for the common ‒α³·7 and ‒α4·2 deletions. Cases with persistent clinical suspicion despite negative PCR results underwent further characterization using an α-globin reverse-hybridization assay (StripAssay; ViennaLab Diagnostics, Vienna, Austria); MLPA with the SALSA MLPA Probemix P140 HBA kit (MRC Holland); or, when required, sequencing of the HBA1 and HBA2 genes, according to the diagnostic strategy available during each study period. Microcytosis with normal HbA2 and HbF levels, in the absence of iron deficiency, was considered compatible with α-thalassemia trait based on established hematologic criteria. As many of these patients were identified through automated complete blood cell count screening rather than targeted clinical requests, molecular confirmation testing was not routinely performed but was instead reserved for patients referred for genetic counseling or prenatal assessment, following a specific clinical request, or when the hematologic phenotype was atypical or inconclusive. This approach was particularly appropriate in our setting, where α-thalassemia is highly prevalent among individuals of sub-Saharan African origin, making universal molecular confirmation neither clinically indicated nor routinely justified. Cases that required molecular confirmation were investigated using the strategy described above. Cases of microcytosis that remained unexplained after exclusion of iron deficiency and in the absence of definitive phenotypic or molecular findings were classified as unidentified microcytosis. The proportion of molecularly confirmed cases increased over time in parallel with the progressive implementation of advanced molecular diagnostic techniques.

Throughout the study period, the diagnostic algorithm evolved in parallel with the availability of new laboratory techniques. Although hematologic indices and HPLC provided the basis for initial screening and phenotypic characterization, capillary electrophoresis improved the identification of structural Hb variants. Molecular methods were reserved for cases that required definitive genotypic confirmation, characterization of complex hemoglobinopathies or α-globin defects, and genetic counseling. This stepwise approach combined the strengths of phenotypic and molecular techniques while avoiding unnecessary molecular investigations.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Poniente Hospital Trust, Hospital Universitario Poniente, El Ejido, Almería, Spain (approval No. PI_26_03). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Owing to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized clinical data, the Ethics Committee waived the requirement for written informed consent.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Categorical variables were expressed as absolute numbers and percentages. Comparisons between native and migrant populations were performed using the χ2 or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Temporal trends were assessed using the χ2 test for trends. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows, version 25.0, software (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY).

Results

Between 1996 and 2025, 5340 hemoglobinopathies were identified in the hospital’s catchment area. Of these, 863 (16.2%) were diagnosed in native Spanish patients and 4477 (83.8%) in migrant patients. A progressive increase in the number of diagnoses was observed over time, particularly among migrant patients, showing a strong linear trend (R2 = 0.86), whereas the trend in native patients was considerably weaker (R2 = 0.10; P < .001) (Figure 2). When stratified by time period, the increase was particularly evident after 2008, coinciding with the implementation of systematic chromatographic screening.

Evolution of red blood cell disorder diagnoses (1996-2025), showing stratification by native and migrant populations. Annual hemoglobinopathy diagnoses increased markedly over the study period, driven mainly by a progressive rise in cases among migrant patients, while native cases remained relatively stable.

Overall, thalassemias accounted for 1716 (32.1%) diagnoses, whereas variant hemoglobins represented 3624 (67.9%) cases, with clear differences between native and migrant populations. Among the native patients, thalassemias predominated (88.1%), whereas variant hemoglobins accounted for 11.9% of cases. In contrast, variant hemoglobins were predominant among migrant patients (78.6%), with thalassemia accounting for 21.4% of diagnoses. The differences were statistically significant (P < .001). A progressive increase in the number and diversity of hemoglobinopathies was observed over the study period, particularly after the implementation of systematic chromatographic screening and confirmatory techniques.

To further characterize the migrant population, the geographic origin of patients diagnosed in the most recent period (2024-2025) was analyzed. The geographic distribution of patients showed a marked predominance of African (77.2%) origin, followed by European (12.9%) and Latin American (8.7%) origins, with only a minor contribution from Asian countries (0.6%) (Table 1).

Thalassemias

A total of 1716 thalassemia diagnoses were recorded during the study period (Table 2). In native patients, heterozygous β-thalassemia was the most frequent diagnosis (467 [54.1%] cases), followed by heterozygous α-thalassemia due to the ‒α³·7 deletion (126 [14.6%] cases), δβ-thalassemia (61 [7.1%] cases), and homozygous α-thalassemia due to the ‒α³·7 deletion (41 [4.8%] cases). Rare but clinically relevant findings included heterozygous α0-thalassemia and nondeletional α-thalassemia variants. Despite the improved diagnostic resolution, 43 patients (4.98%) were classified as having unidentified microcytosis.

The thalassemia spectrum was broader in migrant patients. Variant hemoglobins

A total of 3624 structural hemoglobinopathies were diagnosed during the study period (Table 3), predominantly among patients of migrant origin. The most frequent variant was heterozygous HbAS, identified in 2291 (42.9%) patients. Only 33 (3.8%) cases occurred in native patients, whereas 2258 (50.4%) cases were diagnosed in migrant patients. Co-inheritance of HbAS with α-thalassemia, HPFH, or unidentified microcytosis was common.

Heterozygous HbAC was the second-most frequent structural variant (858 [16.1%] cases), again mainly among migrant patients (835 [18.7%] cases). Heterozygous HbAD was less frequent overall (101 [1.9%] cases) but proportionally more common in native patients.

Clinically significant sickle cell disorders were identified exclusively in the migrant population. Fifty cases of sickle cell anemia (HbSS; 1.1%) were diagnosed, frequently associated with modifying factors such as α-thalassemia deletions or HPFH. The compound heterozygous states included HbSC, HbS/β-thalassemia, and HbS/O-Arab.

A wide range of rare structural variants was detected, mainly in migrant patients. In 27 (0.5%) patients, the Hb variant could not be definitively characterized and was classified as unidentified.

Select illustrative cases

The following cases were selected to illustrate specific diagnostic challenges, rare molecular mechanisms, or analytical pitfalls encountered during the study period. All were diagnosed during the final years of the study, when demographic changes and progressively expanded laboratory and molecular testing resulted in a broader and more complex spectrum of hemoglobinopathies.

Case 1: HbT-Saudi

Hemoglobin T-Saudi was identified in a Moroccan patient with heterozygous HbAS and non–iron-deficient microcytic hypochromic anemia. Molecular screening excluded α-globin gene deletions or triplications. Sequencing identified the *HBA2 (NM_000517.6): c.94A > G variant (HbT-Saudi, polyA1), which affects the polyadenylation signal and reduces α2-globin expression.14

Case 2: HbJ-Baltimore

Hemoglobin J-Baltimore was detected in a native Spanish patient with normal hematologic indices. High-performance liquid chromatography revealed an indeterminate Hb variant interfering with HbA1c quantification. Capillary electrophoresis demonstrated a fast-migrating fraction (51.4%). Sequencing identified the heterozygous *HBB (NM_000518.5): c.50G > A (p.Gly17Asp) variant (HbJ-Baltimore), which is clinically silent but interferes with HbA1c measurement.15

Case 3: Hb Hofu

Hemoglobin Hofu was identified in a Moroccan woman with microcytosis but without anemia. High-performance liquid chromatography showed borderline HbA2 levels (3.9%). Capillary electrophoresis revealed a fast-migrating fraction (24%) and an HbA2 level of 3.3%. Sequencing confirmed heterozygous HBB c.380T > A (p.Val127Glu) (Hb Hofu-II) with co-inherited ‒α³·7 α-thalassemia. The elevated HbA2 level by HPLC was considered artifactual due to co-elution.16

Case 4: α0Mococa-thalassemia

α0Mococa-thalassemia was identified in a native Spanish woman with non–iron-deficient microcytic anemia and normal HbA2 and HbF levels. Initial molecular testing excluded common α-thalassemia and β-thalassemia defects. Multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification revealed an approximately 17-kilobase pair deletion within the α-globin gene cluster involving HBA2, HBA1, HBAP1, HBM, and HBZP1, consistent with the ‒MOCOCA deletion and heterozygous α0-thalassemia.17

Case 5: Hb Groene Hart (Hb Bernalda)

Hemoglobin Groene Hart was identified in a native Spanish woman with microcytosis and hypochromia without anemia or iron deficiency. Her HbA2 and HbF levels were normal. Molecular screening excluded α-globin deletions or triplications. Sequencing identified the pathogenic *HBA1 (NM_000558.5): c.358C > T (p.Pro120Ser) variant, consistent with Hb Groene Hart.18

Case 6. Novel HBA1 variant

A previously undescribed HBA1 variant was identified in a Moroccan woman with non–iron-deficient microcytic anemia. High-performance liquid chromatography showed an indeterminate Hb variant, and capillary electrophoresis revealed a fast-migrating fraction (17.0%). Sequencing identified a heterozygous HBA1 (NM_000558.5): c.349_357dup (p.Glu117_Thr119dup) duplication not previously reported in the literature or Hb variant databases, consistent with a novel α-globin variant.

Discussion

This study provides a 30-year overview of hemoglobinopathy diagnoses in a nonendemic European setting, demonstrating a marked increase in case numbers, molecular heterogeneity, and diagnostic complexity. The progressive predominance of patients of migrant origin, now accounting for more than 80% of diagnoses, has fundamentally reshaped the local epidemiology. Compared with our previous Acta Haematologica report,19 the number of confirmed cases has increased more than 5-fold, reflecting both demographic changes and improved diagnostic capacity. These trends reflect successive changes both in population structure and in diagnostic practices, with recent years characterized by migration-driven increases and improved detection.

Our findings are consistent with data from other European settings, where migration has emerged as the main driver of hemoglobinopathy epidemiology, increasing both case numbers and genetic diversity. In our cohort, native patients were predominantly diagnosed with heterozygous β-thalassemia, whereas migrant populations showed a broader and more complex spectrum of diagnoses, including homozygous and compound heterozygous states. The absence of severe β-thalassemia among native individuals likely reflects population genetics—particularly lower carrier frequency and consanguinity—rather than underdiagnosis, in contrast to high-prevalence regions such as the Middle East, North Africa, and South Asia.20 Despite overall population growth, diagnoses among native individuals remained stable, likely reflecting demographic changes and the growing predominance of migrant populations.

Diagnostic advances have also played a key role. Initial investigations relied mainly on hematologic indices and HPLC, whereas capillary electrophoresis, targeted PCR, MLPA, and DNA sequencing were progressively incorporated according to clinical indications, improving the detection of Hb variants, including asymptomatic carriers and rare forms. Increasing diagnostic complexity poses challenges, however, particularly in cases with co-inherited variants that modify hematologic indices and interfere with HbA2 and HbF interpretation. Analytical limitations such as co-elution in HPLC and interference with HbA1c measurement remain relevant and require confirmatory testing with complementary methods. Therefore, part of the observed increase should be interpreted in light of improved diagnostic sensitivity rather than a true increase in disease incidence.

These changes have important clinical implications. The growing proportion of second-generation individuals born in Spain highlights a shift toward an established local disease burden, with increasing demands on primary care for early recognition and referral. Neonatal screening for SCD, introduced in 2018, is modifying referral pathways by enabling early diagnosis and structured follow-up, in contrast to the previous predominance of incidental detection during anemia workup or HbA1c testing. Integrated care pathways involving laboratory diagnostics, outpatient clinics, obstetrics, and pediatrics are essential to address this increasing complexity.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective, single-center design may limit generalizability, and molecular confirmation was not available in all cases, particularly in earlier years of the study. In addition, changes in diagnostic methods over time may have influenced case detection and classification; therefore, part of the observed increase likely reflects improved detection rather than a true rise in incidence. Nevertheless, the large cohort and long study period provide a robust representation of real-world diagnostic practice and its evolution.

Overall, hemoglobinopathies are no longer rare conditions in nonendemic settings but now represent an increasingly complex and relevant component of routine clinical practice. This shift illustrates a transition from an imported condition to an established component of the local disease burden. Migration-driven diversity, together with evolving diagnostic strategies, underscores the need for coordinated clinical and laboratory approaches. Future multicenter and prospective studies will be essential to further define epidemiologic patterns and optimize treatment in these populations.

This study showed how migration, technological advances, and organizational changes have made hemoglobinopathies a central component of routine hematologic practice in nonendemic regions. The growing proportion of second-generation patients born in Spain reflects a shift from an imported condition to an established local disease burden, posing new challenges for primary care. Ongoing adaptation of diagnostic algorithms and closer integration between laboratory and clinical services will be essential to address this increasing complexity.
