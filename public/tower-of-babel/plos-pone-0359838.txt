Knowledge, attitudes, and prevention practices regarding human papillomavirus among health and social science female students in Northwest Ethiopia: A comparative cross-sectional study

Birhanemaskal Malkamu, Abiyu Ayalew, Addisu Melake, Alemie Fentie, Ayenew Assefa, Ayenew Berhan, Birhanu Getie, Birhanu Malede

ABSTRACT
Background: University students are vital for Human Papilloma Virus (HPV) prevention, yet comparative data between academic disciplines remain limited. This study compared HPV knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) among female health and Social Science students at Debre Tabor University. Objective: To compare HPV knowledge, attitudes, and prevention practices (KAP) among female Health and Social Science students and identify sociodemographic predictors of these outcomes. Methods: Design: Institutional-Based, comparative cross-sectional study. Setting: Debre Tabor University, Northwest Ethiopia, conducted from June to July 2025. Main outcome measures: HPV knowledge (good vs. poor), attitudes (good vs. poor), and vaccination practices (good vs. poor). Multivariable logistic regression identified independent predictors of poor outcomes. Results: Health science students (HSS) reported higher awareness (83.6% vs. 56.7%) and more positive attitudes (57.3% vs. 37.6%) than Social Science students (SSS). However, both groups exhibited poor knowledge: 61.2% of HSS and 80.9% of SSS scored <50% on knowledge questions, and critically low vaccination rates (HSS: 18.2%; SSS: 15.2%). For HSS, lack of prior awareness was the strongest predictor of poor practice (AOR = 7.61, 95% CI = 1.65–35.12). For SSS, second-year students had 3.39 times higher odds of poor practice compared to seniors (AOR = 3.39; 95% CI: 1.04–10.99). SSS predominantly relied on social media (45.0%), while HSS used academic sources (67.5%). Conclusion: A profound knowledge-practice gap exists regardless of the field of study. High reliance on social media among SSS and low practice among mid-level students suggest that university-based interventions could potentially contribute to national efforts, though achieving population-level targets requires multi-sectoral strategies..

Introduction Human Papilloma virus (HPV) is a highly Prevalent viral infection that poses a significant public health threat worldwide, primarily due to its strong association with cervical cancer and other anogenital and oropharyngeal cancers. Globally, cervical cancer remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality among women, particularly in developing countries where the burden of disease is highest [ 1 , 2 ]. In Ethiopia, while national immunization programs have expanded, the burden of cervical cancer remains high, necessitating a deeper look at the ‘last mile’ of prevention-the transition from awareness to clinical practice among highly educated cohorts. The World Health Organization (WHO) has identified HPV as the leading cause of this preventable cancer, and despite the availability of safe and effective HPV vaccines, the global burden remains substantial, highlighting the need for improved prevention strategies [ 3 , 4 ]. Prevention of HPV- related diseases relies on a multifaceted approach that includes comprehensive health education, vaccination, and regular screening. Research has consistently shown that a high level of knowledge and a positive attitude toward HPV and its vaccination are critical precursors to vaccination uptake [ 5 , 6 ]. However, significant disparities exist in awareness and prevention practices, often influenced by socio-demographic factors such as age, residence, marital status, and educational background [ 7 , 8 ]. The academic setting, particularly among young women, is a crucial environment as it offers a unique opportunity to provide directed education and promote preventative behaviors [ 9 ]. This study was guided by a comparative empirical framework, focusing on measurable differences in KAP between academic disciplines without imposing a specific behavioral theory. In Ethiopia, a developing country with a high burden of cervical cancer, the implementation of HPV prevention programs faces unique challenges. Recent regional studies have documented general HPV awareness among Ethiopian students [ 10 – 12 ]; however, these findings often treat the student population as a monolithic group. This oversight is significant because it fails to account for the divergent information sources and professional expectations between students in health-related versus non-health-related disciplines. Understanding these factors is essential for designing effective, evidence-based public health interventions. This study aimed to address this critical evidence gap by providing a, comparative analysis of HPV knowledge, attitudes, and practices among female students at Debre Tabor University. By specifically contrasting Health Science Students (HSS)-who have curriculum-based exposure-with Social Science Students (SSS), who rely predominantly on informal information sources. We hypothesized that Health Science students would demonstrate higher knowledge and more favorable attitudes due to curriculum‑based exposure, but that vaccination uptake would remain low in both groups due to structural barriers. This research identifies the specific points where knowledge fails to translate into practice. Our findings provide the evidence-based rationale needed to transition from generalized public health messaging to discipline-specific institutional strategies, providing baseline data that may inform institutional components of broader national strategies Materials and methods Study design, area, and period This institutional-based, comparative cross-sectional study was conducted at Debre Tabor University (DTU) from June to July 2025. DTU is located in Debre Tabor, the capital city of the South Gondar zone in Amhara regional state, approximately 667 kilometers southwest of Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. The university, established in 2008, comprises five colleges, one faculty, and two schools. The study specifically targeted students from two of these colleges: the College of Health Sciences and the College of Social Sciences and Humanities. This setting was selected to allow for a direct comparison of HPV knowledge, attitudes, and practices between students with different academic backgrounds. This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. Patient and public involvement (PPI) Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research. Study population and sampling The source population for this study included all female students registered at Debre Tabor University in 2025, while the study population specifically consisted of eligible female students from the College of Health Sciences (HSS) and the College of Social Sciences and Humanities (SSS). A non-probability quota-based convenience sampling approach was employed due to logistical constraints, including the absence of a complete, real-time sampling frame and the restricted data collection window from June to July 2025. Initially, the two colleges were purposively selected to enable a comparative analysis between health-focused and non-health-focused curricula. The required sample size was determined using a single population proportion formula: n = Zα/2 2 p*(1-p)/d 2 . A 95% confidence level (Za/2 = 1.96), a 5% margin of error (d = 0.05), and a proportion (p) of 72.7%, representing the prevalence of adequate HPV awareness in a similar university population from a previous study at the University of Gondar and Bahr Dar University, were used [ 13 ]. The initial calculated sample size was 305. To account for a 10% non-response rate, an additional 30 students were included, bringing the total sample size to 335 female health science students and 335 Social Science students, for a total of 670 participants. A 100% response rate was achieved as all distributed questionnaires were completed and returned during the supervised data collection sessions Data collection A structured, self-administered questionnaire was used to collect data. The tool was adapted from similar studies and modified after an extensive literature review [ 13 , 14 ]. The questionnaire was designed to capture attitude, knowledge, and practice framed as measuring perceived susceptibility, perceived benefit and actual practice. Its content validity was ensured through an expert review process involving faculty members from the College of Health Sciences. The questionnaire was initially prepared in English, translated into the local language (Amharic), and then back-translated into English by a different translator to ensure consistency. The questionnaire consisted of three main sections: Socio-demographic data: Age, marital status, religion, residence, academic year, and field of study. HPV Awareness: Questions to assess if students had heard about HPV and their sources of information. Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices (KAP): Specific questions to measure participants’ knowledge, attitudes, and prevention practices regarding HPV and its vaccination. A total of 670 eligible female students (335 from the College of Health Sciences and 335 from the College of Social Sciences and Humanities) were included in the study, representing 100% of the calculated target sample size. To ensure the study reached its planned statistical power, recruitment and data collection continued within each academic cohort until the required quota of 335 completed and valid questionnaires per group was achieved. Operational definitions Awareness: In this study, awareness is defined as having previously heard of HPV (measured as a binary ‘Yes/No’ response). Knowledge: Participants were categorized as having ‘good knowledge’ if they correctly answered greater than or equal to 50% of the knowledge-related questions. Scores less than 50% were categorized as ‘poor knowledge’. The 50% cutoff was chosen based on modified Bloom’s criteria, which categorizes scores below 50% as poor, and is consistent with prior Ethiopian HPV studies, such as Mekonnen et al 2024 [ 13 ] Attitude: Participants’ attitudes were measured using a composite score. Scores were classified as poor attitude for less than 50%, and good attitude equal to or more than 50% of the total attitude score. Practice: Good practice was defined as having received at least one dose of the HPV vaccine. Participants who had received no doses at all were categorized as having poor practice.“ Data quality control To ensure data quality, the questionnaire was pre-tested on 10% of the sample (67) in a different population of engineering students, a population with similar demographics but a different academic background, to ensure clarity and reliability before the main data collection began. The pre-test data were not included in the final analysis. Based on this pre-test, necessary adjustments were made to the tool to improve clarity and validity. To assess the internal consistency of the questionnaire, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated for the knowledge and attitude domains separately for Health Science and Social Science student cohorts. The knowledge domain (14 items) demonstrated excellent internal consistency in both groups (Health Science: α = 0.930; Social Science: α = 0.930). The attitude domain (5 items) showed acceptable reliability in both groups (Health Science: α = 0.762; Social Science: α = 0.762). Corrected item-total correlations for all items exceeded 0.30, confirming that each item contributed meaningfully to its respective scale. These results indicate that the questionnaire reliably measured the intended constructs across both student populations. During the main data collection phase, rigorous quality control was maintained through two principal measures. To assess the consistency of data abstraction and recording, inter-rater reliability (IRR) checks were conducted on the data abstraction process, by two separate, trained data collectors on 5% of the collected questionnaires. The agreement was measured using the Kappa statistic, which yielded a result of kappa = 0.65, indicating substantial agreement. Data Entry Accuracy: All collected data were subjected to double data entry by two independent data clerks. The entries were subsequently compared using validation procedures in SPSS to identify and correct any inconsistencies, ensured an error rate of less than 1%. Furthermore, the principal investigator reviewed all completed questionnaires daily for completeness and consistency. Study participants were clearly informed about the study’s purpose to foster honest responses. In addition, STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines were followed throughout the study. Ethical approval and consent to participate Ethical approval and clearance for the study were obtained from the Research and Community Service Corporate Directorate Director, College of Health Science, Debre Tabor University, protocol number 017/2024, at July 20, 2024. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was secured from each respondent after a thorough explanation of the study’s purpose and their rights, including the option to withdraw at any time. All data obtained were kept confidential and private, with no personal identifiers recorded. The privacy and rights of the participants were respected throughout the study. Data processing and analysis All collected data were checked for completeness, coded, and entered into SPSS statistical software version 26.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize socio-demographic characteristics, awareness, and KAP levels. For inferential analysis, both bi-variable and multivariable logistic regression models were used to identify factors associated with poor HPV knowledge, attitude, and practice. Variables with a < 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were considered candidates for the multivariable logistic regression model to control for potential confounders. A backward stepwise elimination method was used to identify independent predictors of knowledge, attitude, and practice. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), and the results confirmed the absence of significant collinearity (Max VIF = 1.836). The goodness-of-fit of the multivariable models was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, with a p-value = 0.34 ( > 0.05) indicating an adequate fit. In The final multivariable model, an Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) with a 95% Confidence Interval (CI) was used to measure the strength of association, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Discussion Our study revealed a significant disparity in basic HPV awareness: 83.6% of Health Science students (HSS) had heard of HPV, compared to only 56.7% of Social Science Students (SSS). These findings contrast with the study by Yohannes et al. (2023) among adolescent school girls in Ambo Town, which reported an awareness level of 47.3%. The higher awareness in our cohorts, particularly among Health Science Students, likely reflects the cumulative effect of academic exposure and age. However, the relatively lower awareness among our Social Science Students (56.7%) suggests that the awareness gains seen in earlier school years, as documented by Yohannes et al., are not being sufficiently sustained or expanded for Social Science students upon entering higher education [ 11 ]. However, despite this difference in awareness, the significant knowledge- practice gap in our study reveals a key challenge for HPV prevention. Consistent with prior research in developing nations, a substantial portion of our study population demonstrated a poor level of HPV knowledge [ 14 , 15 ]. This deficit was particularly pronounced among Social Science students, where over 80% were categorized as having poor knowledge. While health science students exhibited a higher proportion of good knowledge, a majority (61.2%) still fell into the poor knowledge category. This finding is significant as it challenges the assumption that students in health-related fields are inherently well-informed on public health issues like HPV. Significant disparities in primary information sources likely drive the observed knowledge gap, with health science students (HSS) primarily utilizing academic curriculum (67.5%) while Social Science students (SSS) predominantly rely on social media (45.0%). Despite these differences, a critical knowledge-practice gap persists across both cohorts; for example, 57.3% of HSS demonstrated positive attitudes toward HPV, yet vaccination remains exceptionally low at 18.2%. Similarly, SSS students exhibited low positive attitudes (37.6%) and comparably low practice (15.2%), suggesting that substantial non-cognitive barriers-such as cost, access, and logistical hurdles-prevent favorable sentiments from translating into preventive action. [ 16 – 18 ]. Furthermore, while Yohannes et al. (2023) identified that positive attitudes toward vaccination were high among younger adolescents (72.8%), our study found a significant decay in these sentiments among Social Science University Students (37.6%) [ 11 ].This cross- sectional finding suggests that positivity may differ by academic year, but longitudinal data are needed to confirm a true decline.. This highlights that university-based interventions cannot assume that awareness built during primary or secondary schooling is sufficient to drive vaccination practice in young adulthood. Our comparative analysis, identified distinct predictors of HPV knowledge and prevention practice for each student group, emphasizing the need for educational strategies. Our finding that HPV knowledge is low among university students aligns with similar studies conducted at Addis Ababa University and Jimma University [ 19 , 20 ] Predictors for poor KAP varied significantly by discipline; for health science students (HSS), a lack of prior HPV awareness was the strongest determinant of both poor knowledge (AOR = 6.96, 95% CI: 1.65–35.12: p < 0.001) and poor practice (AOR = 7.61, 95% CI:1.65–35.12: p = 0.01), confirming that basic awareness is foundational yet currently insufficient in depth. Conversely, Social Science students (SSS) displayed a paradoxical trend where being in the 2nd academic year (AOR = 0.12,95%CI: 0.05–0.31: p < 0.001) and the 20–23 age group (AOR = 0.25,95% CI: 0.07–0.93: p = 0.04) were protective against poor knowledge compared to seniors. This suggests that while initial, potentially social-media-driven exposure provides early awareness, this knowledge is not reinforced as students’ progress through a non-health-related curriculum, creating an observed gap in later academic years. Unlike Getaneh et al. (2021), who identified that being in a senior year of study was a positive predictor for screening knowledge [ 12 ], our comparative analysis found a more nuanced trend. In our Social Science cohort, seniority did not significantly improve vaccination practice, whereas it did for Health Science students. This may indicate that the ‘academic maturation’ effect noted in the Gondar study may be limited to students in health-related fields. For students in the Social Sciences, the observed gap in later academic years persists throughout their university tenure, highlighting a missed opportunity for institutional health promotion that the Getaneh study did not explore. Among Social Science students (SSS), residence was the sole significant predictor of HPV prevention practices. Students from urban areas were significantly less likely to report poor practice (AOR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.29–0.99, P = 0.047) compared to their rural counterparts. This suggests that urban residence may afford better access to information or vaccination services, whereas rural students face geographic or structural barriers to care. While academic year was a significant predictor of knowledge, it did not statistically influence practice in this cohort ( P > 0.05). This “knowledge-practice gap” emphasizes that increasing academic awareness alone is insufficient for non-health tracks; targeted interventions must address the practical barriers faced by rural students. Furthermore, while this study focused on academic]discipline, the Ethiopian context necessitates exploring unmeasured cultural and religious influences on sexual health. Expanding future research to include male students remains critical for achieving community-wide immunity and meeting the WHO “90-70-90” targets [ 21 ]. While Yohannes et al. (2023) noted that teachers and health workers were primary information sources for younger girls, our results show a shift in the information landscape for university students. For our Social Science cohort, social media (45.0%) has overtaken institutional sources. This evolution in information-seeking behavior between adolescence and university age is a critical finding of our study, identifying social media reliance was associated with lower knowledge scores; this finding generates the hypothesis that social media content may lack sufficient clinical detail. We did not directly assess misinformation exposure; this interpretation is speculative and requires confirmation. Our findings regarding the disparity in attitudes between HSS (55.4% positive) and SSS (37.6% positive) build upon the regional evidence provided by Getaneh et al. (2021). In their study at the nearby University of Gondar, they reported that only 13.7% of students had ever undergone cervical cancer screening, despite a majority having heard of the disease. While Getaneh et al. highlighted a massive gap in screening utilization among a general undergraduate population, our study identifies that a similar, discipline-specific gap exists for primary prevention (vaccination). Specifically, we demonstrate that even when students are in an environment with high screening awareness (like Northwest Ethiopia), those in Social Science tracks remain significantly more hesitant toward vaccination than their health science peers. This suggests that regional awareness of screening does not ‘spill over’ into vaccine acceptance without discipline-specific curriculum intervention. Our finding of 18.2% vaccination uptake is strikingly similar to a 2022 study in Bahir Dar City, which reported a 16% uptake among female preparatory students (Likneh et al., 2022) [ 22 ]. This suggests that as female students transition from primary education to higher levels in the Amhara region, vaccination coverage remains consistently low. The significantly higher uptake (66.5%) reported in North Shoa (Kassa et al., 2021) was observed among primary school girls, who are the primary target of Ethiopia’s school-based National Expanded Program on Immunization [ 23 ]. In contrast, our study population consists of university students who likely missed these initial school-based campaigns and face greater barriers to accessing the vaccine as young adults. Our findings of low vaccination rates among university students mirror trends observed across other Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs). For instance, studies in Nigeria have noted uptake rates ranging from 1.5% to 20.4% among university students, with vaccine unavailability (66.7%) and lack of awareness (63.2%) identified as primary barriers [ 24 ]. In India, a study among female nursing and paramedical students reported only 7.7% vaccination coverage, with high cost (64.9%) and lack of information (25.4%) as major obstacles [ 25 ]. Similarly, in Malaysia, 40% of university students had not received the vaccine and had no scheduled appointment, highlighting that university students often fall into a ‘catch-up’ gap where the absence of campus-based vaccination clinics hinders preventive action [ 26 ]. This highlights a universal challenge: the transition to higher education often results in a loss of momentum for series completion or initial uptake if not supported by institutional health services. Ethiopian national data from 2025 reports a 30.8% HPV vaccination uptake among school girls, while a meta-analysis estimated regional acceptance at 41.46% (PMA Ethiopia, 2025; BMC Public Health, 2023) [ 27 , 28 ]. Our lower reported practice (15.2%–18.2%) suggests that actual uptake in the university setting is lagging behind national expectations, indicating a critical need for university-based catch-up programs. The low vaccination uptake, despite positive attitudes, highlights a critical challenge for achieving the WHO’s Global Strategy to Accelerate the Elimination of cervical cancer [ 29 ]. This global strategy aims to achieve a “90-70-90” target by 2030, which includes vaccinating 90% of girls by age 15 [ 30 , 31 ]. While Ethiopia has demonstrated a strong commitment to this goal by incorporating the HPV vaccine into its national immunization program, recent coverage rates remain far below this target, with administrative coverage for the two-dose schedule at 74% in 2022 and with the country having since shifted to a single dose schedule [ 32 , 33 ]. The low vaccination rate in our study is not merely a result of poor individual knowledge, but a reflection of systemic policy barriers. Ethiopia’s HPV vaccine rollout primarily targets 9–14-year-olds; university students are not currently a priority group for routine national campaigns, resulting in low coverage in this age cohort. Therefore, for Ethiopia to approach its ‘90-70-90’ targets, national strategies must be complemented by localized, data-driven interventions at the institutional level. The discrepancy between the positive attitudes observed in our study and the low vaccination rates is a major finding, mirroring a well-documented challenge in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [ 34 , 35 ]. This gap suggests that factors beyond simple awareness are at play. In the Ethiopian context, these barriers include logistical and financial hurdles such as inconsistent vaccine supply, inadequate cold chain infrastructure, and the high cost of vaccination, which can be prohibitive even when the vaccine is available [ 36 ]. Vaccine hesitancy, fueled by misinformation and social stigma surrounding sexual health, also remains a significant barrier to uptake in many communities [ 37 , 38 ]. Our findings therefore suggest that university-based interventions could serve as a valuable platform for achieving national and global goals by addressing both the attitudinal and logistical barriers. Implementing targeted educational campaigns in these settings could not only raise awareness but also directly address misinformation and hesitancy. Furthermore, integrating vaccination services directly within the university health system could improve accessibility and overcome logistical challenges, thereby converting positive attitudes into tangible prevention practices. This approach aligns with the National Cancer Control Plan of Ethiopia (2025–2029), which prioritizes increasing access to HPV vaccination as a key strategy for reducing the national cancer burden [ 39 ]. Study limitations and strengths This study employed a comparative cross-sectional design, which provides a valuable snapshot of the current situation but cannot establish causal relationships between the variables. Additionally, the self-reported nature of our data is also subject to recall and social desirability bias. Furthermore, our operational definition of ‘good practice’ was receiving at least one dose of the HPV vaccine; this was a pragmatic choice, but it does not differentiate between partial and full vaccination according to the national schedule at the time of vaccination. Nevertheless, a key strength of this research is its comparative nature, which provides a detailed, nuanced understanding of the differences in KAP and associated factors between two distinct student populations, a critical step for developing effective, evidence-based interventions. While this study achieved its target sample size with a 100% response rate, the use of non-probability convenience sampling is a limitation that may impact internal validity. This approach introduces a potential for selection bias, as students who were present in class during the data collection window were the ones recruited, potentially differing from those who were absent. To ensure the target was met, recruitment continued within each department until the pre-specified quota of 335 students per college was achieved. This high level of participation ensures that the captured data represents the majority of students active during the study period, providing a solid foundation for this comparative analysis. Our study did not measure psychological constructs such as perceived susceptibility or barriers to vaccination, which may mediate the knowledge-practice gap observed. Future research should incorporate validated Health Belief Model instruments. In addition, prevention practices were measured solely by vaccination uptake; screening behaviors (e.g., Pap smears) and vaccination intentions were not assessed. Consequently, we cannot determine whether current attitudes will predict future screening or vaccine uptake. Furthermore, some of the measures of association in the multivariable models exhibited wide confidence intervals, particularly concerning vaccine practice and awareness. These results suggest a limited degree of statistical precision for certain sub-categories. While the associations remain statistically significant, the instability of these specific estimates should be considered when generalizing these findings to broader populations. Our cross-sectional design was unable to differentiate between students who were ineligible for vaccination, those who were hesitant, and those who lacked access. A future study using qualitative methods or a more detailed survey could provide critical insights into the specific reasons for low vaccination rates. Future longitudinal studies should incorporate cervical cancer screening behaviors and vaccination intentions to provide a more holistic assessment of HPV prevention strategies among university students. Conclusion and implications Based on our observational findings, we recommend university-wide HPV initiatives at Debre Tabor University to address pervasive knowledge deficits, particularly among Social Science students (80.9% poor knowledge). For health science cohorts, interventions should focus on curriculum-driven strategies to bridge the attitude-practice gap, while Social Science disciplines require tiered, peer-led campaigns and mandatory foundational public health modules to counter lower knowledge levels observed in later academic years. Institutional strategies could complement national efforts, though cross-sectional KAP studies cannot directly measure progress toward elimination targets [ 40 ]. Therefore, future research must utilize qualitative methods to investigate the socio-cultural drivers of vaccine hesitancy. Such data-driven, discipline-specific approaches are vital for converting awareness into tangible preventive actions and reducing the national cervical cancer burden in Ethiopia.
