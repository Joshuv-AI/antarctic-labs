Risk factors for tigecycline-associated drug-induced liver injury in critically Ill patients: a 2020–2023 retrospective study from a Chinese hospital
Authors: Yonghui Wang, Siying Zhang, Miaozhe Shen, Ke Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTigecycline is extensively used in Chinese intensive care unit (ICU) for multidrug-resistant infections, yet the profile of its associated drug-induced liver injury (DILI) requires further investigation. Therefore, this study was designed to explore the incidence, identify independent predictors, and characterize the dose-response relationship and timing of tigecycline-associated DILI in critically ill patients.MethodsThis single-center, retrospective cohort study enrolled all critically ill patients (n = 309) who received intravenous tigecycline between 2020 and 2023. DILI was diagnosed according to established international biochemical criteria (n = 71). We employed Pearson correlation analysis and multivariable logistic regression to assess relationships between clinical factors and DILI development. The dose-response relationship between tigecycline exposure and DILI risk was characterized using ROC analysis and generalized additive models (GAM). Time-to-onset of DILI was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier analysis to estimate cumulative incidence and median event time.ResultsThe incidence of tigecycline-associated DILI was 23.0%. Multivariable analysis identified total duration of tigecycline therapy (OR = 2.01 per week, 95% CI: 1.42–2.92) and cumulative tigecycline dose (OR = 1.51 per gram, 95% CI: 1.19–1.94) as significant independent risk factors. The dose-response relationship was found to be non-linear, with a GAM revealing an initial increase in risk that attenuated at higher cumulative doses (P < 0.05). The cumulative dose of tigecycline was associated with DILI (AUC = 0.647). The median time to DILI onset among affected patients was 4 days; the Kaplan‑Meier estimate of median DILI‑free survival in the full cohort was 18 days.ConclusionsIn patients who develop DILI, the median onset time of 4 days underscores the importance of early hepatic monitoring; the 18‑day Kaplan‑Meier estimate further supports extended vigilance throughout prolonged therapy. Cumulative dose and treatment duration should be managed to mitigate the risk of hepatotoxicity.Clinical trial number Not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13780-y.

Introduction

Tigecycline, a broad-spectrum glycylcycline antibiotic, represents a critical last-line therapeutic option against severe infections caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, vancomycin-intermediate, Klebsiella pneumoniae, extended-spectrum β-lactamase–producing Escherichia coli [1]. Its expanded clinical utility, particularly in intensive care units (ICUs), is driven by persistently high in vitro susceptibility rates reported by global and national surveillance networks [2, 3]. Consequently, international treatment guidelines endorse tigecycline-based regimens for complex abdominal, skin, and soft tissue infections, as well as off-label for severe respiratory infections when MDR pathogens are suspected [4–6].

The most frequently documented adverse events are gastrointestinal disturbances; however, hepatotoxicity remains a significant safety concern [7, 8]. Initial clinical trials identified a low incidence (2%) of asymptomatic liver enzyme elevation associated with tigecycline, prompting its inclusion in prescribing warnings [9]. However, post-marketing studies employing more rigorous assessments suggest a higher burden of clinically relevant liver injury. A recent real-world analysis using the RUCAM causality tool confirmed DILI in 5.7% of patients [10]. Strikingly, other cohort studies have reported hepatotoxicity incidence rates as high as 28.9% in specific clinical settings, indicating that the risk may be substantially greater than initially recognized [8]. Post-marketing data have further substantiated these concerns, with case series describing instances of clinically significant DILI, including cholestatic hepatitis and acute liver injury [11, 12]. Initial registration trials reported treatment durations typically ranging from 7 to 14 days under strict patient selection criteria [13]. In contrast, a real-world ICU study observed a median treatment duration of 9 days (range 1–78 days) [14]. The longer treatment durations and broader patient profiles encountered in real-world practice may lead to an underestimation of DILI risk in clinical trial settings.

In real-world clinical practice, especially within ICUs, tigecycline is frequently administered in off-label regimens. This includes use for infections off-label prescribing (e.g., ventilator-associated pneumonia, bacteremia) and at doses exceeding the standard regimen, aiming to overcome pharmacokinetic limitations and improve efficacy in critically ill patients with augmented renal clearance or severe sepsis [15, 16]. This widespread off-label and high-dose use potentially alters the drug’s risk-benefit profile, necessitating robust post-marketing safety evaluations [17].

Despite its growing importance, the epidemiology of tigecycline-associated DILI remains inadequately characterized. Existing evidence is predominantly derived from isolated case reports or retrospective analyses with small sample sizes, often lacking standardized causality assessment using tools like the Roussel Uclaf Causality Assessment Method (RUCAM) [18, 19]. Major knowledge gaps persist regarding the precise incidence in critically ill cohorts, the quantitative dose-response relationship, the timing of onset during prolonged therapy, and the identification of independent, modifiable risk factors. Therefore, this single-center, retrospective cohort study aimed to: (1) determine the incidence of tigecycline-associated DILI in a contemporary ICU population using stringent biochemical criteria and RUCAM; (2) identify independent clinical and treatment-related predictors; (3) characterize the dose-response relationship between cumulative exposure and DILI risk; and (4) define the time-to-onset profile to guide evidence-based monitoring strategies.

Material and methods

Study design and patient selection

A single-center, retrospective cohort study was conducted. The study population comprised adult inpatients treated in the intensive care unit (ICU) of hospital between January 2020 and December 2023. Patients were excluded from the analysis based on the following pre-defined criteria: Incomplete medical records, specifically the absence of baseline laboratory data prior to the initiation of tigecycline therapy; Tigecycline treatment duration of less than 72 h; Pre-existing chronic liver diseases known to cause liver injury, including viral hepatitis (HAV, HBV, HCV, HEV), cirrhosis, or autoimmune hepatitis (AIH), documented before tigecycline administration; A hospital length of stay exceeding 365 days.

Data collection

Data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical records and clinical databases. The primary objective of data collection was to identify patients who developed suspected DILI following tigecycline treatment. For all eligible patients, a comprehensive dataset was assembled, encompassing the following domains:

Demographics: age (years), sex (men, women), year of admission (2020, 2021, 2022, 2023), type of health insurance (out-of-pocket, with health insurance), smoking history (never smoker, current smoker, former smoker), alcohol use (never drinker, current drinker, former drinker), number of comorbidities (none, one comorbidity, two or more comorbidities), history of liver disease (yes, no; including cholecystitis, cholelithiasis, fatty liver, etc.).

Clinical course and management: concomitant drug burden (≤ 1, 2, ≥ 3 drugs), fluconazole use (yes, no), and cefoperazone sulbactam use (yes, no), pulmonary infection (yes, no), severity of illness score (APACHE II score at ICU admission), duration of mechanical ventilation (weeks), length of hospital stay (weeks), length of ICU Stay (weeks), use of hepatoprotective drugs (yes, no; The most commonly used agents included glutathione, magnesium isoglycyrrhizinate, ademetionine, and tiopronin.), management after DILI onset (drug withdrawal, dose reduction, continued use), clinical outcome (cured/improved/discharged, transfer/ineffective/death), liver injury outcome (cured/improved, ineffective/treatment abandonment), 30-day all-cause mortality (deceased, discharged/transferred).

Tigecycline exposure and DILI assessment: tigecycline loading dose (g), tigecycline maintenance dose (g), total duration of tigecycline therapy (weeks), total cumulative tigecycline dose (g), time to DILI onset from tigecycline initiation (days), cumulative tigecycline dose at DILI onset (mg), RUCAM score (The Roussel Uclaf Causality Assessment Method was applied to each suspected case to quantitatively assess the causality of tigecycline-associated DILI).

Laboratory parameters: laboratory values were collected at three key timepoints: baseline (within 24 h prior to the first tigecycline dose), peak (the most abnormal value during or after tigecycline therapy), and final (the last available value during hospitalization). Identification and assessment of suspected tigecycline-associated DILI

Suspected cases of tigecycline-associated drug-induced liver injury (DILI) were identified based on the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Drug-Induced Liver Injury (2023) [20], with the following biochemical criteria: ALT or AST ≥ 5 times the upper limit of normal (ULN). ALT ≥ 3 × ULN concomitant with TBIL ≥ 2 × ULN and ALP ≥ 2 × ULN, particularly when accompanied by an elevated GGT and after excluding bone disease as a cause of elevated ALP. For patients with pre-existing liver test abnormalities prior to tigecycline initiation, the baseline values obtained before treatment were used as their individual reference ULN for assessing the magnitude of change. In addition to cases identified by the above guideline criteria, patients with a clinical diagnosis of DILI documented by the treating physician were also initially considered as suspected cases. To attribute the liver injury to tigecycline, alternative causes were excluded—including acute hepatic injury of viral or autoimmune origin and competing hepatotoxic drugs—and causality was assessed using the RUCAM scale. Only cases with a RUCAM score ≥ 6 (probable or highly probable) were classified as tigecycline-associated DILI.

Of the 309 patients in the final cohort, 187 met the biochemical criteria for suspected DILI and underwent RUCAM causality assessment. After excluding 3 unlikely and 113 possible cases, 71 cases with a RUCAM score ≥ 6 (64 probable and 7 highly probable) were classified as tigecycline-associated DILI. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1), with a two-sided p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and compared using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when > 20% of cells had expected counts < 5. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range based on normality assessment by Anderson-Darling test, with group comparisons using independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test accordingly. The analytical approach encompassed multiple methodologies. First, baseline characteristics between patients with and without tigecycline-associated drug-induced liver injury (DILI) were compared. Second, Pearson correlation analysis with pairwise deletion for missing values examined relationships between continuous variables and DILI occurrence. Third, univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to explore associations between variables and DILI status. The adjusted models controlled for sex, age, health insurance type, year of admission, smoking history, and alcohol use. Multicollinearity among covariates in the multivariable models was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with a threshold of 5 used to identify problematic collinearity. For dose-response assessment, cumulative tigecycline exposure was analyzed through dose stratification into quartiles with Cochran-Armitage trend test, logistic regression models adjusted for age, sex, liver disease history, and illness severity, generalized additive models with thin-plate regression splines for nonlinear relationships, and receiver operating characteristic curve analysis for predictive performance. Finally, time-to-event analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method. For the overall cohort (N = 309), survival time was defined from tigecycline initiation to DILI onset, and patients who did not develop DILI were censored at treatment end. To characterize the timing of DILI among affected patients, a separate Kaplan-Meier analysis was conducted for the subgroup of patients who developed DILI (n = 71). Median times to DILI onset were estimated from both Kaplan-Meier curves. Data management utilized the tidyverse packages, while statistical analyses employed base R with specialized packages including nortest for normality testing, survival and survminer for time-to-event analysis, and mgcv for generalized additive modeling.

As illustrated in Figures S1-S6, laboratory parameter analysis demonstrated distinct patterns between groups. Significant differences between the DILI and non‑DILI groups were observed in ALT and AST at both baseline and peak, peak IBIL, peak ALB, and final BUN. Median ALT at baseline was 35 U/L in the non‑DILI group versus 52 U/L in the DILI group (P = 0.017); at peak, 97 versus 181 U/L (P < 0.001). Median AST at baseline was 36 versus 43 U/L (P = 0.030); at peak, 95 versus 162 U/L (P < 0.001). Peak IBIL was 12.2 versus 17.8 μmol/L (P = 0.039), peak ALB was 33.6 versus 35.1 g/L (P < 0.001), and final BUN was 9.33 versus 6.70 mmol/L (P = 0.003). No significant differences were found in ALP, GGT, TBIL, DBIL, TP, PT, APTT, INR, CREA, CRP, or PCT at any time point.

Figure 2 illustrates the significant correlations between clinical parameters and tigecycline-associated DILI status by Pearson correlation analysis, while Figure S7 presents the comprehensive correlation analysis of all variables examined by Pearson correlation analysis. The analysis revealed distinct patterns of association: RUCAM score, ALT (Peak), AST (Peak), tigecycline duration, total tigecycline dose, hospital stay, ALT (Baseline), IBIL (Peak), and TP (Baseline) demonstrated significant positive correlations with DILI development. Conversely, BUN (Final), CREA (Final), time to DILI onset, and CREA (Peak) exhibited significant negative correlations with DILI status (all p < 0.05).

Figure 4 shows the estimated dose-response relationship of cumulative tigecycline dose on DILI risk. GAM analysis revealed a non-linear association, with risk rising steeply at lower cumulative doses and attenuating thereafter (P = 0.006). Discussion

This retrospective cohort study, encompassing 309 critically ill patients, provides a detailed quantification of tigecycline-associated DILI risk in a real-world ICU setting. The key findings—an incidence of 23.0%, cumulative dose and treatment duration showed the strongest independent associations with DILI, a non-linear dose-response relationship, a median onset time of 4 days among affected patients, and a Kaplan‑Meier estimated DILI‑free survival of 18 days—collectively offer a nuanced profile of this significant adverse event, with direct implications for clinical stewardship.

The observed incidence of 23.0% in our ICU cohort is substantially higher than the 2–5% rates reported in pivotal registration trials [9]. It also exceeds the 5.7% incidence found in a large, general-hospital retrospective study that employed the RUCAM [10]. Notably, another ICU-based study by Jiang et al. that used a comparable definition of hepatotoxicity reported an incidence of 33.8% in 148 ICU patients, which is even higher than the 23.0% observed in our study [21]. This suggests that the higher incidence relative to registration trials and general hospital studies is attributable to the ICU population rather than to the diagnostic criteria used. This discrepancy starkly illustrates the heightened vulnerability intrinsic to the critically ill population. Our finding aligns more closely with studies specifically investigating high-dose or prolonged tigecycline courses in severe infections [21], and another ICU-based study reporting a comparable incidence [8], underscoring that both patient acuity and treatment intensity are pivotal drivers of risk. The ICU environment presents a “perfect storm” of predisposing factors: systemic inflammatory response syndrome, capillary leak altering drug volume of distribution [22, 23], polypharmacy with potential drug-drug interactions [24], and frequent organ hypoperfusion. These elements collectively compromise hepatic resilience and increase susceptibility to drug-induced injury. Additionally, review of concomitant medication records showed that commonly used ICU agents were administered at comparable frequencies in both groups, and no concomitant drug had a temporal association with liver injury stronger than that of tigecycline. These findings, together with the conservative RUCAM threshold of ≥ 6, support the attribution of the observed liver injuries to tigecycline rather than to alternative etiologies. In the multivariate analysis, cumulative dose and treatment duration showed the strongest independent associations with DILI. Among the three variables related to concomitant medication exposure, concomitant drug burden (≥ 3 vs. ≤ 1 drugs) showed a significant crude association with DILI (OR 2.24, 95% CI 1.13–4.44, P = 0.021), whereas fluconazole and cefoperazone-sulbactam use did not. This suggests that a higher polypharmacy burden may contribute to hepatic vulnerability in ICU patients.

These findings strongly support a pharmacokinetic-driven model of toxicity, where sustained hepatic exposure is central, a concept corroborated by studies linking elevated tigecycline exposure (AUC0−24 h) to liver enzyme elevation [25]. The observed non-linear dose-response relationship—characterized by a steep initial rise in hepatotoxicity risk that plateaus at higher cumulative doses—is a critical and novel finding. This pattern may be best explained by a saturation phenomenon in key hepatic pathways. Specifically, saturation of bile acid efflux transporters (e.g., BSEP/ABCB11), a well-established mechanism for drug-induced cholestasis, could account for the diminishing incremental risk at higher drug loads [26]. This saturation‑based mechanism may be particularly relevant in the ICU, where high‑dose tigecycline regimens are frequently used for severe infections. Such dosing strategies likely achieve intrahepatic drug concentrations that saturate bile acid efflux transporters more rapidly and more completely than standard doses, thereby increasing the risk of early and severe hepatotoxicity. The ROC‑derived cutoff of 1.33 g for cumulative dose may serve as a practical threshold for clinicians to reassess the risk–benefit balance of continuing tigecycline therapy. However, the moderate specificity (41.2%) suggests that this cutoff is better suited for screening than for definitive diagnosis. The unique pharmacokinetics of tigecycline in critically ill patients, characterized by a large and variable volume of distribution and altered clearance, may further predispose it to such non-linear dynamics [27].

The dose- and duration-dependent pattern observed in our study, likely mediated by saturation of bile acid efflux transporters such as BSEP, aligns more closely with intrinsic (direct) hepatotoxicity than with idiosyncratic reactions in the established DILI classification framework [28]. The non-linear dose-response relationship we identified—a steep initial rise in risk that plateaus at higher cumulative doses—is characteristic of such saturable, predictable toxicological processes. This interpretation is further supported by evidence that pharmacological interference with BSEP function constitutes a susceptibility factor for human liver injury [26]. In contrast, idiosyncratic DILI is typically dose-independent and unpredictable, encompassing both immunoallergic and metabolic subtypes. While the metabolic subtype could theoretically share some features with our findings, the clear dependence on cumulative dose and treatment duration in our cohort argues against a classical idiosyncratic mechanism. We acknowledge that in a real-world ICU setting, this distinction may not be absolute—a direct, dose-related toxic insult to hepatocytes may be modulated by host factors that also contribute to idiosyncratic susceptibility. However, the dominant pattern we report is consistent with the pharmacological properties of tigecycline and the known contribution of BSEP inhibition to drug-induced hepatotoxicity [29].

Concurrently, this risk plateau likely reflects increased clinical vigilance in the high-risk ICU setting. Early biochemical signals in patients receiving high cumulative doses often prompt preemptive interventions such as dose adjustment or initiation of hepatoprotectants, thereby mitigating further injury and shaping the observed dose-response curve. This is supported by emerging evidence suggesting that in vulnerable populations like those with liver failure, standard-dose regimens may offer a better safety-efficacy balance compared to high-dose regimens, which have been associated with increased mortality risk [30].

Among the 71 patients who developed DILI, the median time from tigecycline initiation to liver injury was 4 days (IQR, 2–6; Table 1). This early onset, consistent with the median latency of 4.5–8.0 days reported in previous studies, indicates that hepatic function should be monitored closely during the first week of therapy, particularly in critically ill patients receiving prolonged treatment courses. Previous studies of tigecycline‑associated hepatotoxicity have reported onset times consistent with our descriptive finding. Jiang et al. reported a median time to hepatotoxicity of 8.0 days in 148 ICU patients [21], and Shi et al. found a median latency of 4.5 days (IQR, 2.0–7.4) for hepatocellular injury in a large cohort of 973 inpatients [31]. Yu et al. identified treatment length as an independent risk factor for tigecycline DILI in a multicenter study of 397 patients [10]. The descriptive median of 4 days (IQR, 2–6) among our DILI cases falls within this reported range, indicating that the time from drug initiation to liver injury in patients who develop DILI is broadly similar across studies. In the full cohort, the Kaplan‑Meier analysis estimated a median DILI‑free survival of 18 days. This serves as supplementary evidence that the cumulative risk of hepatotoxicity persists beyond the initial weeks. This prolonged interval, which notably exceeds the typical 1-week window often considered for many drugs, likely reflects the cumulative nature of the injury mechanism during extended treatment courses for refractory ICU infections [28]. Consequently, this delayed onset mandates that liver function monitoring be sustained throughout the entire treatment course and potentially beyond, challenging common practices that may taper vigilance after the initial weeks. The association between DILI and prolonged hospitalization observed in our cohort underscores the significant clinical and economic burden, a finding corroborated by studies linking antimicrobial-associated hepatotoxicity to increased healthcare resource utilization [32].

Our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single‑center design may limit generalizability and introduces the potential for unmeasured confounding. The ICU environment is inherently complex, with dynamic changes in hemodynamics, fluid balance, and organ function that may influence liver enzyme levels independently of drug exposure. Although we adjusted for key baseline variables including age, sex, and illness severity (APACHE II score), residual confounding from unmeasured factors—such as transient hypotensive episodes, fluid resuscitation volumes, concomitant use of vasopressors, and varying degrees of systemic inflammation—cannot be excluded. Second, the single‑center design reflects the clinical practices and patient demographics of a single institution, which may differ from other ICUs in terms of tigecycline prescribing patterns, monitoring frequency, and hepatoprotective co‑interventions. Third, the diagnosis of DILI, while based on stringent international criteria and RUCAM, lacks histopathological confirmation. Fourth, the common use of hepatoprotective agents, though a reflection of real‑world practice, may have modified the biochemical expression and course of DILI. The agents used—primarily glutathione, magnesium isoglycyrrhizinate, ademetionine, and tiopronin—are generally considered to have no intrinsic hepatotoxicity, and their administration in this cohort was therapeutic. While their use is best interpreted as a clinical consequence of hepatic concern rather than a confounding cause of DILI, a modulating effect on the biochemical expression of liver injury cannot be excluded. Fifth, body weight data were not systematically available, precluding weight‑adjusted dose analysis. Because tigecycline is administered at fixed doses regardless of body weight, this limitation is partially mitigated. Sixth, abdominal imaging was not systematically performed to formally exclude biliary obstruction, and continuous hemodynamic data were not uniformly available to categorically rule out transient ischemic hepatic injury. The RUCAM scale, which assigns negative scores when biliary obstruction is supported by imaging, was applied to all suspected cases, partially mitigating the impact of incomplete imaging data. Seventh, although a systematic review of concomitant medication timelines was performed, and three variables reflecting concomitant medication exposure were evaluated as independent factors, residual pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic interactions cannot be fully excluded. Formal interaction analysis was not conducted due to the limited number of DILI events. Finally, some variables assessed in the regression models (e.g., peak transaminase levels, clinical outcomes, etc.) were measured after the onset of DILI; their associations with DILI status may therefore reflect disease progression rather than etiological risk.

Conclusion

This real-world cohort study confirms a significant burden of tigecycline-associated DILI among critically ill ICU patients, with an incidence of 23.0%. Both cumulative dose and prolonged therapy duration were identified as significant, independent, and modifiable risk factors. The median time to DILI onset among affected patients was 4 days, emphasizing the importance of early hepatic monitoring during the first week of therapy. The 18‑day Kaplan‑Meier estimate from the full cohort provides supplementary evidence supporting extended vigilance throughout prolonged therapy. These findings underscore the necessity for vigilant, extended hepatic function surveillance and strict stewardship of tigecycline dosing and duration in this vulnerable population.
