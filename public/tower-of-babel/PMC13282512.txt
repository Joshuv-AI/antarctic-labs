Reply of the authors: Commentary on “Frozen embryo transfer outcomes in patients with prior cesarean delivery: a focus on endometrial lining parameters”
Authors: Emily A. Clarke, Jing Wu, Hannah Milad, Kerry Flannagan, Jiarui Wang, Joseph Lee, Luis Hoyos, Meike Uhler, Michael Homer, Jenna Friedenthal, Alan B. Copperman, Phillip Romanski, Mary Morris
Published: 2026

To the Editor

We thank Dr. Jaiswal for the thoughtful reflection on our article, “Frozen embryo transfer outcomes in patients with prior cesarean delivery: a focus on endometrial lining parameters” (1). As noted in the commentary, the study demonstrated that patients with a prior cesarean delivery had similar endometrial thickness during frozen embryo transfer (FET) cycles compared with those with a prior vaginal delivery, yet experienced a modest reduction in live birth following FET. This reduction was observed despite adequate (>10 mm) endometrial thicknesses in both groups, and remained evident when the analysis was restricted to single euploid embryo transfers. As Dr. Jaiswal highlights, these findings suggest that among patients with a prior cesarean delivery, endometrial thickness alone may not fully reflect implantation potential, and additional post-cesarean uterine factors independent of endometrial lining may contribute.

Several mechanisms may underlie the observed reduction in live birth, including alterations in the endometrial microbiome, changes in uterine contractility, and cesarean scar niche formation with intracavitary fluid accumulation. One limitation of the study is that detailed phenotyping of cesarean scar morphology, including the presence of a niche or isthmocele, was not available. Further, as Dr. Jaiswal notes, information regarding surgical techniques and peripartum contexts was unavailable, resulting in a heterogenous prior-cesarean cohort. In agreement with Dr. Jaiswal, future prospective studies should focus on characterizing the post-cesarean uterus at structural, functional, and molecular levels, including markers of endometrial receptivity, to better understand the observed reduction in live birth. Importantly, the present study was not designed to directly evaluate endometrial receptivity; rather, endometrial thickness was selected as the primary outcome because of its widespread use as a well-established marker of reproductive potential in FET cycles (2).

Finally, it is important to recognize that the difference in live birth observed in the study was modest (49.5% vs. 53.0%, P<.01; adjusted relative risk, 0.94 [95% confidence interval, 0.91–0.98]), and miscarriage rates were similar between groups. Thus, while the study supports that prior cesarean delivery may alter subsequent reproductive potential after FET, the magnitude of the association is quite small and should be interpreted in the context of overall favorable assisted reproductive technology success rates.

We are grateful to Dr. Jaiswal for commending our research and for framing its results around the key question of why live birth may be reduced after cesarean delivery despite adequate endometrial thickness. This observation suggests that uterine factors beyond endometrial thickness, the most readily available measure for uterine implantation potential, may contribute and warrant further investigation.
