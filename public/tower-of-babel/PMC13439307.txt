How should we manage incidental gastric subepithelial lesions?
Authors: Sung Eun Kim
Published: 2026

In South Korea, gastric cancer screening through esophagogastroduodenoscopy is performed every two years for adults aged 40 years and older. An increase in the number of esophagogastroduodenoscopies has led to an increase in the detection of gastric subepithelial lesions (SELs) during these procedures. Most clinicians actively treat gastric SELs presenting with symptoms such as bleeding or abdominal pain. However, a recent domestic survey revealed that clinicians have different approaches to treating asymptomatic gastric SELs.1

To address this confusion, guidelines for gastric SELs were recently published in South Korea.2 According to the guidelines, endoscopic follow-up alone may be considered for non-malignant gastric SELs measuring less than 1 cm.2 Furthermore, for gastric SELs measuring 1–2 cm that are initially detected via endoscopy, if the diagnosis remains unclear after endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS), a follow-up endoscopy is conducted after 6 months. If there is no change in size, further examination is scheduled 12 months later.2 The recommendations in the guidelines cannot be applied uniformly to all gastric SELs. However, the availability of domestic guidelines is beneficial for clinicians treating patients with these lesions. In addition to these guidelines, understanding the prevalence and natural history of incidental gastric SELs is important for clinicians.

In this regard, the study by Heo et al.3 is highly practical, as it offers clinicians important information on incidental gastric SELs. The authors identified 30,754 participants who underwent esophagogastroduodenoscopy screening between January 2013 and December 2016 and reported a prevalence of 1.9% (n=599) gastric SELs. This result is nearly identical to that of a domestic multicenter study that analyzed data from 65,233 participants who underwent esophagogastroduodenoscopy screening and reported a prevalence of 1.6% (n=1,044) for gastric SELs.4

In addition, Heo et al.3 identified patients who underwent follow-up endoscopy at intervals of 6 months or longer. Among 262 patients who underwent follow-up endoscopy, 8.4% (n=22) showed significant changes in size (an increase in size of >25% and an increase in the longest diameter of >5 mm). Among the patients included in this study, the median diameter of the initial gastric SELs was 7 mm (interquartile range [IQR], 5–10 mm) in the stable SEL group and 10 mm (IQR, 6–14 mm) in the progressive SEL group. This indicates a slightly higher rate of size increase than that reported by previous studies on gastric SELs measuring 1 cm or less; these studies reported growth rates of 4%–6.4%.5,6

In terms of applying the recent Korean guidelines for gastric SELs, a limitation of this study is that the percentages of patients who underwent EUS and the follow-up intervals for those who underwent follow-up endoscopy are unknown. Additionally, for the progressive SEL group, it would be more beneficial for clinicians to determine the extent of size increase on the basis of the follow-up intervals as well as the specific size (in cm) or clinical signs that prompted the decision for resection. Nevertheless, I believe that such studies will help accumulate domestic data on gastric SELs and will be important when updating the domestic guidelines for these lesions in the future.
