Development and validation of a hypertension risk prediction model using electronic health records data from general practices in Hampshire and Isle of Wight integrated care system
Authors: Jurgita Gammall, Daria Ruffini, Somil Parmar, Nikolaos Mastellos, Laura Beegan, Kathryn Griffiths, Chris Gibbons, Richard Betteridge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertension is a major risk factor for heart, stroke and kidney disease. Identifying patients at risk of developing hypertension early and applying preventative measures can reduce the disease burden and improve outcomes. This study aimed to develop and validate a hypertension risk prediction model utilising data from electronic health records in Hampshire and Isle of Wight Integrated Care System.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study using data from the Oracle Health Data Intelligence platform. We used an observation period of all history up to 31st July 2017 and a prediction period in the following five years, up to 31st July 2022. We included all adult patients registered with a general practitioner without existing hypertension and other cardiovascular disease. We considered a total of 54 predictors and used feature selection based on a combination of random forest feature importance and feature significance in backwards stepwise logistic regression. The outcome was a new development of hypertension within the 5-year prediction period. We evaluated three types of predictive models, logistic regression, decision tree and random forest. The hypertension prediction model was further redeveloped by applying the same methodology to a different geographic population of patients registered to practices within Lewisham and Greenwich.ResultsWe included 569,405 patients, and of those 58,833 (10.3%) developed hypertension within five years. A total of 39 predictive factors were included following feature selection. The logistic regression model slightly outperformed the other two models and achieved a ROC-AUC of 0.82 within both the training and testing cohort. The model achieved a sensitivity of 75.82% and specificity of 73.67% within the training cohort. Additional model on new geographic population achieved a ROC-AUC of 0.82 within the training cohort and 0.83 within the testing cohort.ConclusionsThe model was developed using a substantially larger number of patients compared with existing models and demonstrated good performance. It is used to identify persons at high risk of developing hypertension and support the provision of prophylactic interventions with model outputs presented as a percentage risk score within population health management tools. Further research could include an assessment of the impact of this model in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-05634-x.

Background

Hypertension is a medical condition in which the blood pressure in the arteries is persistently higher than the recommended level and a key risk factor for heart disease, stroke, and kidney disease globally [1]. In England, it is estimated that over 26% of the adult population have hypertension, with prevalence in general practice (GP) clinics ranging between 18.8% and 31% depending on the age structure and health status of the population with noticeable inequalities across regions [2].

Hypertension is one of the most preventable and treatable health conditions, however, more than half of people with high blood pressure in England are not diagnosed or receiving treatment [3, 4]. Identifying patients with hypertension and initiating blood pressure lowering treatment can significantly improve patient outcomes, with studies showing that a 10 mmHg reduction of systolic blood pressure reduces the risk of major cardiovascular disease events by 20% (coronary heart disease by 17%, stroke by 27%, heart failure by 28%) and death by 13% [5].

Population health management is becoming increasingly important as integrated care systems (ICS) are established across England to enable local health and care partners to deliver services that are tailored to their local population needs in line with the NHS Long Term Plan [6]. Health systems require a system-to-person view of their population to identify patient groups at risk of developing long-term conditions, such as hypertension, and intervene early to help prevent the onset of those conditions. Population health management approaches involving case finding and risk stratification can help identify people with or at high-risk of hypertension at an early stage and help ensure that they receive a diagnosis, lifestyle modification advice and treatment as appropriate to prevent or manage their condition [7].

Risk prediction models have been developed using traditional regression-based or modern machine learning approaches to predict the risk of hypertension in the general population to help with the identification, screening, and management of patients with or at risk of hypertension [8, 9]. A recent systematic review of hypertension risk prediction models found that both approaches produce similar results in terms of model performance [10]. However, only a few models were externally validated, and bias and applicability were noted as major concerns in many studies.

This study aims to develop and validate a risk prediction model for new onset hypertension using clinical and demographic data from electronic health records (EHR) in the Hampshire and Isle of Wight (HIOW) ICS in England. The development of this predictive risk model was initiated for direct patient care to support hypertension prevention services in the local population. Although several options were discussed, it was agreed that training a model bespoke to this population would be most appropriate for the intended use case and to accelerate adoption by clinicians at the ICS. This included the model design decisions and feature definitions developed in partnership with HIOW ICS. We had considered validating existing models, but were unable to find a model that had an exact feature match to our data and use case, therefore the decision was made to develop a new model. Our model was developed primarily as an operational risk stratification tool customised to the local population, further work would be needed to achieve universal generalisability for other regions and populations.

Methods

Study design and data sources

We retrospectively analysed historical GP EHR data from Oracle Health Data Intelligence platform in HIOW ICS. The predictive model was set up by splitting the data into two periods: observation and prediction. An observation period was used to assess historical information about the patient, while the prediction period was used to assess the hypertension outcome. The observation period included all historic data up to 31st July 2017, while the prediction period included 5 years of data from 1st August 2017 to 31st July 2022. This baseline was selected based on the most recent available dataset available at the time of model training. A prediction period of 5 years was selected after review of similar models in the literature [11] as well as to consider the impact on primary care interventions and diagnoses due to the Covid-19 pandemic. The output of the model was a percentage risk score for new onset hypertension in the next 5 years. In addition to the risk score, risks were segmented into risk groups (Very High Risk, High Risk, Moderate Risk and Low Risk) based on risk percentiles in the observed population (see Additional File 1 for further details on the risk ranges risk and group definitions).

Patient cohort

Our patient cohort included people who were 18 years old or older at the end of the observation period and were residents and had a GP registration in HIOW. We excluded deceased patients with date of death before the start of the prediction period. Patients who died during prediction period of 5 years were not excluded from the training data. Their hypertension outcome was therefore assessed in the period from the prediction period start date to the date of death. We also excluded patients with an invalid date of birth (age at the end of the observation period > 130 years). Patients who had unresolved hypertension or any of the following cardiovascular diseases at the end of observation period were also excluded from the model: heart failure, myocardial infarction, ischaemic stroke, transient ischaemic attack, coronary artery disease, peripheral artery disease, abdominal aortic aneurism. Patients with these cardiovascular diseases were excluded from the model because they had a pre-existing condition that is linked to hypertension which would be already known to the healthcare professionals and managed accordingly. For example, heart failure can be caused by hypertension, and blood pressure management is part of the treatment for this condition, therefore it would not be appropriate to predict a risk of developing hypertension for these patients. Equally, patients with conditions known to cause hypertension would not benefit from having a risk score calculated in the same way that a healthier individual would as they should already be under surveillance for their known cardiovascular disease. Similar exclusions were applied in other studies [11–15].

Hypertension definition

According to the estimates published by the UK Health Security Agency and Office for Health Improvement and Disparities (previously known as Public Health England) [16], more than 40% of patients with hypertension are undiagnosed in England. Due to this reason, we extended the hypertension definition from using only a hypertension diagnosis to also including blood pressure measurements and antihypertensive medication data. This was in line with the hypertension definition reported in other studies that developed hypertension risk predictive models [17–19].

An individual was considered to have hypertension if they met at least one of the following criteria: (1) hypertension diagnosis code recorded at any point in patient’s health record, unless a hypertension resolved diagnosis code was recorded after the most recent hypertension diagnosis code; (2) at least one antihypertensive medication record in the last five years and at least one high blood pressure reading (systolic ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic ≥ 90 mmHg or both) recorded in the last five years (unless a hypertension resolved diagnosis code was recorded after the most recent antihypertensive medication start date); (3) at least three high blood pressure (systolic ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic ≥ 90 mmHg or both) readings recorded over the last five years on three different dates (unless a hypertension resolved code was recorded after the most recent date of a high blood pressure reading). The aforementioned definition of hypertension was used in two ways in the predictive model development: as an exclusion criterion, to exclude patients who already have hypertension, and as the hypertension outcome, to determine which patients developed hypertension in the prediction period of 5 years. A patient was considered to have hypertension outcome if they developed hypertension in the period of 5 years, regardless of whether it was resolved after.

Predictive factors

In total, 54 predictive factors were considered for the predictive model, including demographic, behavioural, medication, condition and clinical (biochemical results and observations) factors. The list of predictive factors was informed by previous literature [11, 19–21] and input from a clinical user focus group from HIOW ICS. The predictive factor list was also compared with the factors included in QRISK3 [22] risk prediction algorithm to estimate future risk of cardiovascular disease. The decision to keep the predictive feature list limited to an evidence based or clinically informed feature set, as opposed to a broader list, whereby the machine learning algorithm might select more unexpected features, was made to limit the features to a more intuitive list. It was thought that end users would be more receptive to a model influenced by features that made clearer clinical sense. It was important that the model was simple and model predictions were interpretable to encourage usage. Different lookback periods were applied for hypertension predictive factors. Patients’ full history of data was used for the condition and behavioural factors. For clinical measurements/biomarkers, the last 5 years of historical data was used, assuming that older readings might be outdated and not reflective of a patient’s status. Applying a shorter lookback period for clinical measurements would have decreased data availability, because these measurements might not be taken regularly unless there is a need for investigation. For medication factors, three different lookback periods were tested (5 years, 12 months, 6 months) to determine the most appropriate lookback period to account for short-term and long-term medication effects. For demographic factors, the current or most recent status was used. Additional File 2 provides an overview of all predictive factors considered in the initial data analysis, including their lookback periods.

Demographic features such as age, sex and ethnicity were considered in the initial feature set for the model since these features had been used by multiple predictive studies in the literature [9]. It is documented that socially deprived individuals and those from certain ethnic minorities may have higher cardiovascular risk [1]. Therefore, we rationalised that it would be beneficial to account for these factors in the model, provided bias assessment results were satisfactory. Additionally, it was the preference of potential end users of the model that demographic features were a consideration in the model. We acknowledge that inclusion of ethnicity as a feature in the predictive model brings a risk of further propagating biases associated with under diagnosis and under treatment of minority groups. We mitigated this risk by evaluating model predictive performance and calibration in each ethnic group, as well as each age and sex group.

Missing data and outliers

Data missingness was assessed by calculating prevalence numbers for all predictive features. Numeric variables which had more than 80% data missing (less than 20% of patients had data available for that variable) were excluded from the model. The following variables were excluded as the missing data exceeded this threshold: waist circumference, activated partial thromboplastin time, fibrinogen, gamma glutamyl transferase, LDL cholesterol, uric acid, urinary albumin, glucose, triglycerides.

For the remaining clinical numeric variables, missing values were replaced by the population median. For the BMI numeric variable, the population median BMI was used to replace missing values in situations where BMI group was unknown. If the BMI group was known, while a numeric BMI value was missing (e.g., it was recorded that a patient is overweight, but no BMI value was provided), the population median within that BMI group (underweight, normal, overweight, obese, severely obese) was used to replace missing values. The population median was used to replace the missing data as opposed to other imputation methods due to an assumption that an individual does not have data for a particular clinical measurement, because there was no reason to conduct an assessment. In other words, we assumed that a patient who did not have any readings for certain clinical variables is likely to be within the normal range of that variable [23]. We also rationalised that since we may be allowing end users of the risk prediction tool access to the feature values, it would be more straightforward for users to conceptualise median value imputation as opposed to more complex statistical techniques such as multiple imputation.

Some machine learning techniques are sensitive to the presence of outliers in the training data, therefore outlier values were cleaned for all clinical numeric variables. After patient cohort and predictive features were derived on the training data, population percentiles were calculated for each numeric feature (this procedure was conducted before replacing missing data with medians to not distort percentile values). Any numeric values that were above 99.9% percentile value of the population were capped at the respective 99.9% value. Similarly, any numeric values that were below 0.1% percentile value of the population were capped at the respective 0.1% value. The BMI value was considered to be invalid if it was under 10 or over 90. Numeric values that were used to cap numeric features are provided in Additional File 11 in Supplementary Materials.

Model development

The training dataset was randomly split into two subsets: train (80%) and test (20%). The train subset was used for feature selection, training the models, and tuning the model parameters, while the test subset was used for evaluating model performance. Although the final predictive model was based on logistic regression, the use of data-driven feature selection and hyperparameter tuning may increase the risk of overfitting despite the large sample size. For feature selection process, hyperparameter tuning was constrained through regularisation parameters to reduce model complexity. While logistic regression is generally less prone to overfitting compared with more flexible machine learning algorithms, selecting predictors based on the same dataset used for model estimation can inflate apparent performance. To minimise this risk, we used 10-fold cross-validation during model development and evaluated model performance on a held-out test set. These steps help ensure that model coefficients and performance estimates are not overly dependent on the specific training data.

The predictive model was developed using Caret (Classification and Regression Training) package in R programming language. Three types of machine learning algorithms were trained on the data and compared: logistic regression, decision tree C5.0 and random forest. Automatic hyperparameter tuning was conducted for all types of algorithms using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Area Under the Curve (AUC) as a performance metric. Model performance using the AUC metric was similar for all three machine learning methods with logistic regression (AUC: 0.825) slightly outperforming the other two methods (random forest AUC: 0.822, decision tree AUC: 0.817). More details provided in Additional File 3. Considering the model performance results, logistic regression was selected as a preferred method for the final model. Logistic regression was a popular method used in multiple previous studies [12, 17, 20] for predicting hypertension. It also enables easier interpretation and explainability compared to other machine learning methods, which is very important for predictive model adoption in clinical practice [24]. It also had implementation advantages within the Oracle Health Data Intelligence Platform. The final logistic regression model was trained using a generalized linear model (GLM) method with binomial family and logit link function. Two multivariable methods were used for feature selection: random forest feature importance and logistic regression backward stepwise elimination. The features were determined as not important and dropped from the model if they were dropped by logistic regression backward stepwise elimination and were not among the top 40 most important features in random forest. The results from the feature selection analysis suggested that medication use measured over a longer period (5 years) is a stronger predictor than medication use over a shorter period of time (6–12 months) across all medication types. Therefore, all medications with a time period of 5 years were kept in the model. 39 features were included in the final predictive model (see Results section).

Model evaluation and risk groups

Validity of the final model was assessed using ROC curves, which were used to estimate the AUC statistic. A confusion matrix was used to assess the sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value of the model. However, these metrics do not add valuable information on model performance unless informed by a clinically relevant cut-off and therefore were only calculated for illustrative purposes. We calculated these metrics using a threshold of 10%, which reflects the average hypertension incidence in the outcome period. In clinical practice application, the selected cut-off would likely be much higher, leading to a more manageable number of patients for preventative care programme. If a higher cut-off is selected, the values in the confusion matrix would change (e.g. positive predictive value would increase). Generally, the intended use of the model is to rank patients by their predicted risk and group them into multiple risk groups instead of classifying result as binary (i.e. positive vs. negative). Model calibration analysis was performed comparing predicted probabilities with the observed outcome. Calibration analysis was deemed more important than the confusion matrix results since the outputs of the model would be presented as a risk score as opposed to a binary classification of hypertension. Variable importance in the logistic regression model was measured by Z-value, which is a test statistic that is calculated as a ratio between absolute coefficient estimate and its standard error. All model evaluation metrics were compared for two data cohorts: training and testing. Hypertension risk groups were defined using risk segmentation methodology according to the Kaiser pyramid [25] as shown in Additional File 4.

Risk groups were defined using observed risk percentiles from the most recent available data (March 2022). The most recent available data was used to define risk groups to ensure that risk groups are up to date and reflect current risk distribution in the population to be used in practice for direct patient care. The risk groups were tested in calibration analysis. Additional File 1 provides details on risk group definitions and the percentage of the population that goes into each group.

Model redevelopment on new population

After the live deployment of the model within HIOW’s chronic disease management tool, Oracle’s hypertension risk model was localised to general practices in Lewisham and Greenwich (L&G). The model was developed on a training cohort of 310,414 patients using GP data but also data from a secondary care acute source (Lewisham and Greenwich NHS Trust). A similar development process was followed, and a list of comparisons were made against the HIOW population including the hypertension incidence, demographic breakdowns, categorical factors, numeric data availability, numeric factor percentiles, numeric factor outcomes, model performance metrics, calibration analysis and effect direction of model coefficients.

Whilst the machine learning algorithm and initial feature set for consideration were the same, feature selection techniques applied on the new dataset produced a final feature set and coefficients tailored to L&G. Care was taken in introducing the acute data to mitigate against predictive factor values being skewed by acute admissions. Specifically, for most clinical risk factors, it was agreed that any GP data would still be prioritised as they would be closer to a ‘baseline’ value and therefore more reflective of long-term hypertension risk, and an acute reading would be considered in the absence of any GP result. The use of acute data, where GP data does not exist, was thought to add the benefit of characterising patients who develop hypertension but are not seen in routine care. Equally, following clinical input it was agreed that capturing some aspect of clinical risk factor volatility during acute admissions could be useful for predictions, so two features, heart rate standard deviation and creatinine standard deviation, were added leading to a total of 56 potential predictive factors.

For the cohort exclusion and hypertension outcome definitions, blood pressure readings across all data sources were considered with equal importance. Other risk factors, including demographics, behaviours and conditions, considered all data sources. To assess the impact of adding acute data, the cross-validated AUC for a GP data only model was compared to one that contains all sources. The remaining methodology was vastly similar to that of the model in HIOW, although the predictive model was developed using the scikit-learn package in Python programming language.

Results

Patient cohort

There were 761,456 adults in the patient cohort at the end of observation period before exclusions were applied. 25.2% patients were excluded from the patient cohort due to presence of hypertension or other cardiovascular diseases (Table 1). The final patient cohort consisted of 569,405 patients.

Hypertension outcome

Out of 569,405 patients who did not have hypertension at the start of the prediction period, 36,042 patients consequently developed hypertension in the next 5 years. 7.4% of patients had three or more high blood pressure readings, 6.3% of patients had at least one antihypertensive medication record and at least one high blood pressure reading and 3.5% of patients had hypertension diagnosis recorded during prediction period. The overall hypertension incidence rate over 5 years was 10.3% (Table 1).

Demographics

Demographic characteristics and hypertension outcome numbers in the overall patient cohort, as well as split to the train and test cohorts, are provided in Table 2. The youngest age group, between 18 and 39 years old, constituted almost a half of the patient cohort, and 51% of the patient cohort were female. There was a small number of patients with unknown sex who were grouped together with male patients. The number of patients with unknown sex was too small to maintain as a separate category for modelling purposes (< 0.01% of patients). These patients were not excluded from the model to be able to calculate risk predictions for such patients once the model is implemented in clinical practice. Female patients had higher hypertension incidence rate than male patients, implying increased detection within routine care and/or higher risk of hypertension; therefore, sex variable was defined as a binary flag showing whether a patient is female. There was no significant statistical association between sex and the risk of hypertension found in the modelling part of the study after adjustment for other factors, and therefore, sex was not included in the final model, which helps to mitigate a sex bias risk. As part of the bias assessment, the AUC was assessed for both sexes. No significant sex bias was detected in the model with performance in both groups comparable to the whole population. Hypertension incidence over five years increased with age and reached 29.5% in the oldest age group of 65+ year olds. Hypertension incidence rate was higher for females at 10.9% versus 9.8% for non-female patients. A large proportion of the patient cohort lived in the least deprived areas. However, hypertension incidence rates were comparable across Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) quintiles. The white ethnic category constituted 76.0% of patients in the cohort, while 17.8% of patients had an unknown ethnicity. The highest hypertension incidence rate was observed for patients in the white ethnic category.

Predictive factor analysis

The features with the highest prevalence (> 20%) were observed for alcohol – unknown, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medications, antidepressants, and family history of cardiovascular diseases. The lowest prevalence numbers (< 1%) were observed for hypertension resolved, pre-eclampsia, sodium – low, atrial fibrillation, and severe mental illness. It is worth noting that pre-eclampsia can only affect a small patient cohort (pregnant women). Patients with hypertension were excluded from the patient cohort, therefore only a very small number of patients with a history of hypertension are included (if they had a hypertension resolved code recorded as the most recent hypertension-related record). Factor prevalence for all categorical predictive factors included in the final model is shown in Additional File 5.

Blood pressure and BMI were the factors with the best data availability (> 50% of patients in the cohort), while total cholesterol to HDL ratio and heart rate were the factors with the worst data availability (< 30% of patients in the cohort). Note that systolic blood pressure standard deviation had lower data availability compared to systolic blood pressure, because its calculation required at least two readings of systolic blood pressure. Data availability, estimated as percentage of patients in patient cohort who had data available for the factor, for all numeric predictive factors included in the final model is shown in Additional File 6.

Figure 1 compares the percentage of patients who developed the hypertension outcome for each categorical predictive factor broken down by whether patients had that factor or not. For example, 30% of patients with diabetes developed hypertension, while only 10% of patients without diabetes developed hypertension during the 5-year prediction period. All numbers are reported as percentage of the whole population for consistency (including contraceptives). All categorical factors, except unknown smoking status, contraceptives – used in the last 5 years, unknown alcohol status and current smoker status, had a higher percentage of patients who developed hypertension when patients had the factor, compared to patients without the factor. For all factors, the percentage of patients who did not have the factor and developed hypertension were around 10%, which is comparable to the average incidence rate in the whole patient cohort (10.3%). Factors that showed the highest percentage of patients who developed hypertension in the 5-year period were hypertension resolved, atrial fibrillation and diabetes. However, it is worth noting that these factors had very low prevalence rates in the patient cohort.

The percentage of patients who developed hypertension increased with higher BMI values and ranged from 4% in the underweight category to 25% in the severely obese category (see Additional File 7). Additional File 8 shows the hypertension incidence rates over the five-year prediction period by predictive factor percentiles for all other numeric predictive factors included in the final model. The strongest association between numeric predictive factor and hypertension outcome was observed for blood pressure-related factors (systolic blood pressure and mean arterial pressure), but all numeric factors showed a somewhat upward trend. Model performance

Model performance metrics calculated at a probability threshold of 10% are provided in Additional File 9. Our predictive model achieved AUC score of 0.82 (Additional File 10), which shows excellent discrimination [26] between high risk and low risk patients. This level performance was in line or above the performance of other similar predictive models published in literature [11, 19, 20, 27].

Confusion matrix results on the training cohort, at a probability threshold of 10%, included sensitivity (75.82%), specificity (73.67%), accuracy (73.89%), positive predictive value (24.91%), and negative predictive value (96.36%) of the model. The testing data showed similar results (more details are provided in Additional File 9). It is worth noting that in this hypertension risk stratification model use case, we were not looking to classify patients into two groups of positives and negatives. Instead, we aimed to rank patients by their predicted risk (probability) to identify high-risk individuals. Hence, calibration was an important performance assessment tool. Model calibration analysis is presented in Figs. 2 and 3, which show a comparison between model predicted hypertension risks (probabilities) and observed percentage of patients who developed hypertension during the 5-year prediction period. The calibration analysis shows that the model is well calibrated, i.e., the observed percentage of patients who developed hypertension increases when predicted hypertension risks increase. The graph also shows that majority of the population (blue area) have low risk of developing hypertension. For example, Fig. 2 demonstrates that the model predicted that about 48% of patients have hypertension risk lower than 5%, and only 2% of those patients actually developed hypertension. There were 21% of patients in the second lowest group where the model predicted a 5-9.9% risk of developing hypertension. In that group, only 7% of patients actually developed hypertension, which is in line with model prediction. Figure 2 also shows calibration analysis using four risk groups (Very High Risk, High Risk, Moderate Risk and Low Risk). The results were similar to those described above, i.e., the observed percentage of patients who developed hypertension were consistently higher when going from low to high-risk groups.

Model calibration analysis by risk range and group

Figure 3 shows model calibration analysis using a LOESS curve. The LOESS curve results suggest that the model tends to over-predict at very high predicted risk levels. This applies to a very small proportion of patients (only 1.7% of patient cohort have predicted risk of 50% and above) and all these patients would be classified as High or Very High Risk. As LOESS curve shows, even when the mean predicted probabilities are higher than observed proportions, the observed proportions are above 50%, which still justifies classification to High and Very High Risk groups. Considering the model intended use is to identify patients who are at high risk of developing hypertension, the classification of these patients to a High Risk or Very High Risk group is more important than the absolute predicted probabilities.

Bias assessment

To assess for model bias, we calculated the ROC-AUC for different demographic groups. As well as a comparison against the overall AUC, we used the four fifths rule to determine if a disadvantaged group was less than 80% of an advantaged group. All demographic groups pass this test. The greatest difference was seen between the under 50 years age group and the 50 + years age group. Most of the cohort falls into the younger age group and it is acknowledged that we have some class imbalance in different age groups. Age is by far the most important risk factor in the model, and it is known that hypertension incidence increases significantly with age. When the strongest predictor – variation of age – is removed from the model (i.e. when we only look at patients who are 50 years or older), we expect for the discrimination ability of the model to drop. However, an AUC score of 74% is still considered a good discrimination [26]. A difference was also noted between the Asian ethnicity group and the Mixed ethnicity group. However, all differences were considered acceptable for real-world use (Table 4).

Model redevelopment on new population

In the first iteration of the model developed for HIOW, all GP data were provided by EMIS (Egton Medical Information Systems). Once additional practices were onboarded to the Oracle Health Data Intelligence Platform, we were able to test the model on GP data from the other main GP IT system provider in England, TPP (The Phoenix Partnership) in order re-train the model on both data sources. This allowed us to assess model performance even further and provide an updated model to HIOW that serves a greater population. The model achieved a ROC-AUC of 0.819 within the training cohort and 0.817 within the testing cohort. Additional data sources, such as acute data from hospital trusts, were also thought to bring additional value to the model and this was explored in a second population; Lewisham and Greenwich. In the Lewisham and Greenwich model, 310,414 patients were included; of those, 29,339 (9.45%) developed new hypertension within the prediction period. The process of feature selection limited the final model to 24 (compared to 34 in the HIOW population) predictive factors. Comparison of a separate GP data only model to a model additionally including the secondary care source demonstrated a slight increase in the corresponding cross-validated mean AUC from 0.82 to 0.84. In this comparison, the cohort size and hypertension outcomes were identical (based on GP data only), with the only difference being the data source(s) used to capture predictive factors. Additionally, the inclusion of secondary care data is beneficial as it allows more accurate capturing of cohort exclusions, hypertension outcomes and predictive factors. The final model, using a logistic regression method, achieved a ROC-AUC of 0.82 within the training cohort (cross-validated mean) and 0.83 within the testing cohort.

Discussion

Oracle’s hypertension risk model differs to existing prediction models in terms of performance, cohort size, and its real-world application. It uses a relatively simple machine learning algorithm, logistic regression, to deliver cutting edge performance results whilst maintaining interpretability of the model predictions, an important factor when considering end-user update and confidence in the model.

A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis into the prediction of hypertension using traditional regression and machine learning models [28] showed overall pooled C-statistic of 0.75 [0.73–0.77]. The model in this study was therefore competitive in its performance with a C-statistic of 0.82.

In addition, the number of participants in this study was substantially larger compared to existing hypertension risk models, with the highest included in the systematic review being 308,711 versus 761,456 (569,405 after exclusions applied) participants in this study. In fact, the total number of participants across 42 machine learning-based models was 1,211,093 which is comparable to our total population size which makes our model uniquely large in size.

Few existing models have been validated on multiple populations and their applicability in clinical practice were a concern in many studies. Localising the Oracle’s hypertension risk model to Lewisham and Greenwich, an inner-city population located within London, evidenced that the model methodology is applicable across different populations. The model was also shown to perform well using additional data sources (acute hospital data) and alternative GP data systems (TPP). One benefit of working with population health teams from different populations was the outputs of the clinical collaboration received from physicians within these teams working in conjunction with clinically experienced employees within Oracle, resulting in enhancements to the hypertension risk model with each localisation to a new population.

Another key difference in this model is its real-world application. The model is surfaced in a tool accessible to general practitioners in the HIOW ICS. The tool surfaces the risk as an absolute risk percentage score and provides end users with the functionality to prioritise patients based on their risk as well as additional risk factors of cardiovascular disease, demographic factors, and wider determinants of health.

Discussion with stakeholders within the ICS showed that HIOW recognises the importance of the prevention and management of cardiovascular conditions. Through the implementation of the hypertension risk model, HIOW aim to be able to identify persons within HIOW that are at risk of developing hypertension. Furthermore, the hypertension risk model is seen as supportive of the Core20Plus5 approach to the reduction of health inequalities, including the ability to carry out hypertension case finding - achieved through combining the hypertension risk model outputs alongside primary care data. The model will allow HIOW to create cohorts of patients that are at risk of developing hypertension but also within the highest areas of deprivation.

More specifically, by running the risk model against the HIOW population, HIOW can identify those individuals who have no diagnosis but are likely to develop hypertension. The model can therefore be used as a mechanism for delivering health promotion advice. Once these individuals are identified, next steps include the sending out of reminders to those eligible individuals and asking them to get their health check completed. This process demonstrates a way in which systems can develop their analytical capacity and build in a population health management approach into their standard ways of working.

HIOW ICS see themselves using the hypertension risk model in conjunction with supplementary information from primary care to apply a population health approach to support PCNs to implement changes in the area of hypertension across HIOW. The risk model offers a way to cut through the array of information and flag where PCNs may want to start looking at patients and targeting care. This is seen as key to identifying areas across the system which have the greatest number of high-risk patients.

The adoption of the hypertension risk model will continue across HIOW, alongside the roll out of the Oracle Health Data Intelligence. Benefits monitoring work is in development and, once the model has been deployed for a sufficient length of time, will demonstrate impact. A technical model monitoring process has been deployed in order to continuously assess model performance and identify data drift or potential biases as population characteristics change over time. If a significant change in the model’s performance is identified, a process for re-training and re-evaluating the model on more up-to-date data has been established. Continuous model monitoring also includes an assessment of model performance and calibration across different demographic groups, to mitigate the risk of exacerbating social inequities through use of the model.

Lewisham and Greenwich serve a different, more urban, population base with less than 10% of people registered to a GP being over 65 years old and broad ethnic diversity. They are implementing the hypertension tool as part of a larger piece of work on cardiovascular disease, recognising the inequity in both identification and outcome across their population. The aim is for the hypertension risk tool to be used within primary care to provide a holistic review of CVD risk and tailored education. Similar to HIOW, the tool may also be used to direct patients eligible for health checks. As hypertension is one of the leading factors of poor health it forms the initial focus area for action in Southeast London ICS’s Vital 5 programme. In parallel to finding the people with undiagnosed hypertension and improving control of those already on treatment for the condition, healthcare practitioners within the ICS are keen to identify those at risk of developing hypertension so that interventions can be implemented to prevent or delay the onset. This work will form part of the wider programme of work through integrated neighbourhood teams within Lewisham and Greenwich.

There are multiple intended users of the hypertension risk model starting with the GP teams who would hold and control the patient level data, using the information to allocate specific preventative interventions such as education support, dietician input, smoking cessation and weight loss services to patients. Additionally, social prescribing link workers could support patients in modifying their lifestyle behaviours by engaging with high-risk patient groups with a specific intervention focusing on cardiovascular risk reduction in the GP setting. Community organisations could participate, with agreement from the data controller, by using the information provided by the tool to tailor services in terms of culture, language, age and ethnicity in the community setting. Public health teams could identify individual patients to engage in interventions around the Vital 5 initiative and use aggregated data to aid in heat mapping location of proactive events around cardiovascular health. Furthermore, the population health strategy team within Lewisham and Greenwich could liaise with clinical teams to strategise how the tool is used in coordination with other work and use the tool to identify cohorts to match potential interventions.

In the first phase of live deployment the risk scores are surfaced in a casefinding view of a population health tool where the end user is able to see individual patient risk scores, risk groups and rank patients by their risk score (e.g. highest to lowest). Oracle recognise the benefit in both model transparency and interpretability of model predictions, and therefore, are looking to surface several visualisations to this effect in the front-end tool where the risk scores are shown at patient level, as a percentage. Visualisations will display information on the model both as a whole and a patient level. At a model level, the visualisation is intended to display feature importance, and at a whole population level, a Sankey diagram [29] dynamically represents how patients move between risk groups over the previous year or five years. At a patient level, the tool aims to trend the risk score as well as key variables in the model, such as historic blood pressure results and body mass index. Also at patient level, the tool aims to present SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) [30, 31] values which would enable end users of the tool to answer the question of why a particular patient is at a particular risk of developing hypertension and see the individual contribution of each variable in the model. Machine learning used in medicine is often criticised as being ‘black box’, and a key intention of the tool is to enable interpretability of model predictions and promote practitioner confidence in ML techniques. Building end-user confidence in the model is theorised to be important for clinician engagement and, in the end, patient outcomes.

Limitations

We considered several limitations in this study. Our hypertension risk model was trained on data from two distinct geographies in England; however, additional localisations of the model would provide further assurances on the robustness of the model. To improve model performance for elderly patients, we could review the initial set of features and add features that are more relevant in older age groups e.g. frailty scores and additional long term conditions. Although the same statistical methodology and the same initial set of features were used to create the models in multiple geographies, the final models had small differences and therefore should be taken into account when comparing model performance. Data-driven feature selection and hyperparameter tuning techniques increase the risk of overfitting the model to the training data despite the large sample size. Although we used logistic regression algorithm for the final model, which is less prone for overfitting, and we evaluated the model with 10-fold cross validation, further analyses on external datasets could help ensure that model coefficients and performance estimates are not dependent on the specific training data. Using methods such as random forest importance and backward stepwise logistic regression for feature selection maximises model interpretability by reducing the number of features, however could lead to a less stable feature set. In the cases where the main goal is model prediction with an automated and stable feature set without focusing on model interpretability, methods such as LASSO regression could be considered alternatively.

Another limitation we identified was data availability for clinical factors. Certain clinical factors, such as glucose, had very low availability in EHR data and had to be excluded from the analysis. The assumption that no recorded measurement implies a normal result is considered acceptable for routinely collected electronic health records. We acknowledge that single value imputation underestimates variability of the data and could bias risk estimates for variables tied to clinical utilisation as well as affecting model calibration. Multiple imputation methods would be preferable in future work. Also, some factors that were identified in the previous literature are not generally well recorded in GP data, such as occupation, education level, marital status, and couldn’t be included in the model. A further limitation of data availability relates to the lack of data that exist for underserved populations such as the homeless population, meaning that these patients, and therefore their risk profiles, are not able to be captured by the model. Additional supplementary data sources such as community and mental health data could further enhance the model, although GP data is broadly considered to be the richest source of patient information in England.

Finally, another limitation of electronic health record datasets that are collected through routine care is that patients enter the dataset at different stages of health trajectory without having a consistent starting point across patients. By defining a fixed observation window applied uniformly across all individuals, we ensure temporal consistency in model training and capturing outcomes. The model intends to estimate the 5-year risk of hypertension from a fixed calendar point, rather than an individualised clinical event. We acknowledge the model does not directly model time to event or disease onset, but the goal is to estimate relatively longer-term risk to support population-level risk stratification and primary prevention planning. A further limitation relates to the potential for misclassification and degree of leakage inherent in defining hypertension status from EHRs, as the outcome definition includes a combination of diagnosis, medication and blood pressure records. Whilst this helps address known underdiagnosis in primary care, it may be influenced by differential healthcare utilisation and documentation practices.

Future research

As part of the next steps for this model, Oracle are looking to conduct further research into assessing model bias. Oracle also aim to add the model visualisations previously discussed into the risk stratification tool. The model will likely be deployed by Oracle in other geographic areas where the validity of the model will be assessed. Following a period of usage, an impact study will be conducted to assess the benefit of end users having access to near real-time information within the risk stratification tool to accurately identify, screen, and manage cohorts at risk of developing hypertension. Oracle will also be looking to assess the impact on patient outcomes and service costs to support planning and service redesign. Following validation, a study in real-life clinical settings will be conducted to assess the impact of having access to near real-time information at the system and person level and risk stratification tools on the ability of end users to accurately identify, screen, and manage cohorts at-risk of developing hypertension, as well as on patient outcomes and service costs to support planning and service redesign.

Conclusions

Hypertension is a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease. Preventative measures can be more effective if patients at high risk of developing hypertension are targeted for intervention early. This study used electronic health records data from 569,405 patients within Hampshire and the Isle of Wight, to develop and validate a model aimed at predicting the risk of developing hypertension in the next five years. A logistic regression model with 39 predictive features achieved a ROC-AUC of 0.82 within the training and testing cohort. Model redevelopment on a second geographic population, Lewisham and Greenwich, achieved similar results. Our model was developed using a much larger volume of patients compared with existing models in the literature and demonstrated better performance compared with previous regression and machine learning models. Another key difference is that our model has already been surfaced within tools developed and deployed by Oracle in HIOW and L&G. Population health teams in those regions can use our model to identify cohorts at risk of developing hypertension and match potential interventions. Future research may assess the uptake and impact of using the model in clinical practice.
