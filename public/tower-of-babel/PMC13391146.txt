Medial Antebrachial Cutaneous Neuroma Following Punch Biopsy: An Iatrogenic Nerve Injury Case Report
Authors: Mohamed Abdelsattar Ali, Salim Al Lahham, Bara Shraim, Aldana Shahbik, Sara Al Harami, Ahmed Al Qahtani, Rami A.M. Mesk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:The medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve is a sensory nerve that provides sensation to the forearm and elbow. Neuropathy of this nerve can result from various causes, including iatrogenic injury. We report a case of medial antebrachial cutaneous neuroma following a skin punch biopsy and discuss the surgical management and outcome. Although such complications are uncommon, this case emphasizes the importance of anatomical awareness and postoperative evaluation when patients present with altered sensation.

The medial antebrachial cutaneous (MABC) nerve is a purely sensory branch of the brachial plexus that supplies the medial and anterior skin of the forearm and elbow. It originates from the medial cord and carries fibers from the C8 and T1 nerve roots. After descending along the ulnar side of the biceps brachii, it pierces the deep fascia in the mid-arm and divides into anterior and posterior branches proximal to the medial epicondyle.1

MABC neuropathy can arise from various causes, including trauma, repetitive strain, compression, nerve entrapment, or systemic conditions such as diabetes, autoimmune disorders, and infection. Iatrogenic causes have also been reported, most notably following venipuncture, corticosteroid injection, cubital tunnel decompression, and elbow arthroscopy.2,3

Although skin punch biopsy is a standard and generally low-risk dermatologic procedure, it carries a potential but uncommon risk of superficial nerve injury, particularly in anatomically vulnerable regions.4

Symptoms of MABC neuropathy include pain, numbness, tingling, or a burning sensation along its distribution. Moreover, the diagnosis of this condition can be confirmed by electromyography or nerve conduction studies. Conservative management, including rest, physical therapy, and analgesics, is usually effective, but in some cases, depending on the severity and cause, surgical intervention such as nerve exploration and neurolysis may be warranted.

We present a case of MABC neuroma that developed after a skin punch biopsy performed for the diagnosis of lichen planus, an uncommon and likely underrecognized mechanism of iatrogenic injury. This report highlights the need for clinical awareness of potential sensory nerve injury during common outpatient procedures.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 41-year-old man presented to our clinic with a several-month history of decreased sensation along the medial aspect of his left forearm. He had previously undergone a 4-mm skin punch biopsy by a dermatologist to evaluate a suspected lichen planus lesion located on the medial forearm.

On examination, there was localized hypesthesia to light touch and pain within the distribution of the MABC nerve. Review of the original dermatopathology from the punch biopsy did not demonstrate nerve tissue. A clinical diagnosis of iatrogenic nerve injury was suspected based on symptom location and temporal relation to the biopsy procedure (Fig. 1). Surgical exploration revealed a thickened segment of the MABC nerve consistent with neuroma formation (Fig. 2). The neuroma was excised, resulting in a 2-cm nerve gap, which was reconstructed using a vein conduit harvested from a superficial forearm vein and used without intraluminal muscle packing (Figs. 3, 4).

Histopathologic analysis demonstrated unencapsulated spindle cell proliferation arranged in fascicles, confirming a diagnosis of traumatic neuroma. At 4-month follow-up, the patient reported subjective improvement in sensation with no recurrence of pain or paresthesia. Objective sensory testing showed a static 2-point discrimination of 5 mm in the affected region, compared with 5 mm on the contralateral side. Temperature and pinprick sensations were preserved, and no signs of recurrent neuroma were present.

DISCUSSION

The MABC nerve exhibits a wide range of anatomical variations, with branching patterns that may differ unilaterally.5 This variability has important clinical implications, particularly for procedures involving the medial forearm.

Although the MABC nerve is purely sensory and does not contribute to motor function, its injury can lead to a range of symptoms depending on the mechanism and severity. Although some patients may experience numbness alone, others may develop painful neuromas if the nerve is partially transected or scarred.

Due to its superficial course near the cubital fossa and its proximity to commonly accessed veins, the MABC nerve is vulnerable to both traumatic and iatrogenic injury. Previous reports have described injuries following venipuncture, steroid injection, and elbow arthroscopy.2,3 Although an older dermatologic report described a case of cutaneous nerve extrusion following punch biopsy,6 it did not involve the MABC nerve nor document neuroma formation. To the best of our knowledge, no published cases specifically report MABC neuroma as a complication of punch biopsy.6

Punch biopsy is a commonly used dermatologic procedure due to its simplicity and diagnostic yield, particularly for diagnosing inflammatory skin disorders due to its ability to obtain full-thickness skin samples. It involves applying downward pressure while rotating a cylindrical blade into the skin. The most commonly reported complications of skin punch biopsy are bleeding, infection, and surrounding skin damage.7 Nevertheless, in regions where superficial sensory nerves are present, it may pose a risk of nerve trauma. The risk is heightened when deeper penetration is required or when anatomical landmarks are overlooked.

In our case, the injury appears to have resulted from direct trauma during the punch biopsy due to its proximity to the nerve site, potentially compounded by rotational pressure as the instrument advanced. An additional contributor could be needle trauma during local anesthetic infiltration if the injection traversed the nerve. This emphasizes the need for clinicians to consider nerve anatomy, particularly when sampling skin overlying major neurovascular bundles.

Surgical options for symptomatic cutaneous nerve injury and neuroma include direct repair when feasible, autologous nerve grafting, nerve conduits (such as autologous vein or muscle-in-vein), and neuroma-targeted techniques such as burying the proximal stump in muscle or bone.8 Contemporary reviews emphasize tailoring the technique to gap length and nerve caliber; for short sensory defects, conduit reconstruction is favored because it avoids donor-nerve morbidity and provides a protected channel for axonal regeneration.8–10 Muscle-in-vein and vein conduits, in particular, have shown favorable outcomes for small sensory nerve gaps with reliable pain improvement.9 In this case, a reversed autologous vein conduit was selected to match the small caliber of the MABC nerve while avoiding additional sensory deficit, providing a favorable outcome, with restoration of sensation and no signs of neuroma recurrence at 4-month follow-up, consistent with published clinical experience.9,10

In summary, MABC nerve injury is an underrecognized potential complication of skin punch biopsy. Although not commonly reported, such injuries may be more prevalent than documented, especially if symptoms are misattributed or remain uninvestigated. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion when patients report persistent sensory symptoms after superficial skin procedures.
