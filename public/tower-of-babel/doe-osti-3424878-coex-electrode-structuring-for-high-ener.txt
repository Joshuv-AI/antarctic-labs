Executive Summary:

 This project was a collaboration between SRI International (SRI) and Oak Ridge National
 Laboratory (ORNL) focused on advancing SRI’s CoEx technology for printing of battery
 electrodes, specifically for improving fast charging performance. Our proposed approach was
 predicted to deliver a 20% increase in energy density by enabling thick electrodes to operate
 with nearly the same performance as thin electrodes under fast charging conditions. As a drop-
 in replacement for the incumbent technology, slot die coating, it should be possible to upgrade
 coating lines to CoEx technology with minimal disruption to the rest of the line. In addition, the
 reduced solvent used in the CoEx process would deliver 15% cost savings and enable either
 100% greater dryer throughput or 50% energy savings as compared to the state-of-the-art slot-
 die approach, reducing capital costs as U.S. manufacturing ramps up to meet expected demand
 for batteries for electric vehicles (EVs). These features align with the DOE-AMMTO goals of
 reducing industrial energy intensity, as total fabrication energy / kWh capacity will decrease.

 Prior work funded by the Vehicle Technologies Office (VTO) served as proof-of-concept for
 CoEx printing, with the scope limited to demonstrating that CoEx structuring in the cathode
 would improve discharge performance. In that work, a thick cathode was paired with an anode
 produced through conventional means (i.e.it had no structuring), and the baseline comparison
 was a cell with conventionally produced cathode and anode of similar loading, along with a
 lower loaded cell that was intended to represent cells of typical loading. The result of that work
 showed that the CoEx cells had higher energy density than the thin baseline cells, due to the
 thicker electrode layers, and had much higher power density than the thick baseline cells at
 higher discharge rates, because the CoEx cathode had higher performance than the conventional
 cathode. In this project, we proposed to extend the CoEx technology to structure both the anode
 and cathode of a lithium ion battery, resulting in at least a 20% increase in energy density under
 fast charging conditions, while reducing costs by 15% and cutting energy usage during
 production. Feedback from commercialization outreach after the VTO project led us to
 emphasize use of a more advanced cathode (NMC622 vs NMC522), to choose an aqueous
 formulation for the anode slurry, and to switch from our pneumatic pressure driven fluid delivery
 to a volumetric metering system that would be more compatible with long coating lengths.

 The division of project tasks reflected the strengths and capabilities of the two organizations.
 SRI has developed the CoEx technology, and has the facilities and expertise for doing process
 and slurry development. ORNL has the Battery Manufacturing Facility, a DOE-funded open-
 access battery manufacturing research and development center, which contains a dry room and
 a prototype coating line that we were able to convert for use with a CoEx printhead.
 Unfortunately, this dry room facility was unoperational for a large portion of the funded period
 of this project, which did affect the final outcome. Work at SRI focused on electrochemical
 modeling to identify target electrode geometries for improved performance, slurry and print
 process development for creating those geometries in both anode and cathode electrodes, and
 pump development for creating a CoEx-compatible metering system that is conducible to
 continuous coating. This equipment would be transferred to ORNL for a series of coating
 sessions that would produce structured cathode and anode electrodes. Independently, ORNL
 would produce conventional cathode and anode electrodes for baseline batteries using the same
 active materials as a comparison. Finally, ORNL would perform electrochemical testing to
 evaluate the batteries and determine the relative performance between CoEx-structured and
 baseline batteries.

 The results of this program are unfortunately inconclusive. Due to technical issues, we were not
 able to generate the full number of electrodes needed for full electrochemical testing, as will be
 further explained below. We continue to seek commercialization partners for this technology,
 and this project funding will help us achieve this.

Introduction

 CoEx is a printing technology that enables high speed production of patterned thick films. The
 CoEx printhead takes two fluids as input, and interdigitates them before deposition, producing
 a finely patterned film consisting of alternating stripes of the two fluids (Figure 1). We have
 applied this technology to battery manufacturing, specifically in creating electrodes with
 structured porosity that improves performance. One limitation in increasing the charge or
 discharge capability in lithium ion batteries is how quickly ions can move from one electrode

 to the other. Thicker electrodes, while being energy-dense, present larger path lengths that
 inhibit charging. Another major factor is how tortuous the path is through the particle
 network that comprises the electrode. Tight packing with low porosity means that ions have to
 take a long tortuous path through the electrodes. One potential solution is to intelligently
 structure the porosity within the electrodes, creating more optimized paths for ion flow. CoEx
 is able to create those structures.

 The primary benefit of this technology is that it will improve the ionic conductivity of cathode
 and anode electrodes. We have designed the CoEx printhead to be a drop-in replacement for
 slot dies (the most common coating method) on a conventional coating line, and the method of
 creating slurries for CoEx is very similar to that used for conventional coating. In other words,
 battery manufacturers can retrofit their factories without having to change the entire line.
 Manufacturers can choose to use this technology in different ways. They can make thicker
 electrodes, which results in greater energy density on the cell- and pack-level, due to a
 reduction in inactive components. They can also simply improve the performance of their
 electrodes at the same thickness. CoEx is capable of printing higher viscosity fluids than the
 standard slot die coater, and as such less solvent is needed to achieve a printable slurry. This
 translates to lower energy costs, as there is significant cost associated with collecting and

 condensing vapor from the dryers, especially when using n-methyl pyrrolidone (NMP), the
 most common solvent used in cathode slurries.

Background

 The incumbent battery electrode manufacturing process uses a slot die to coat current
 collectors with a thin slurry of active material, binder, and conductive additives in a
 carrier solvent. While improvements to this relatively simple process have driven
 dramatic decreases in costs over the past ten years, the basic process remains unchanged.
 One approach to increase the energy density and decrease the specific cost is to move to
 thicker electrodes, thus reducing parasitic components (e.g., current collectors and

separators). However, commercial electrodes are already manufactured at their maximum
thickness, beyond which they will be limited by the ionic transport properties of the
electrode itself. SRI’s CoEx printing technology enables the use of thick electrodes by
producing electrodes with structured ionic conductivity within the same manufacturing
paradigm. The CoEx printhead takes two fluids as input, and interdigitates them before
deposition, producing a finely patterned film consisting of alternating stripes of the two
fluids (Figure 1). By carefully controlling the composition of these two inks, we create a
coating that dries to form fine grooves, which get filled by electrolyte during the cell
assembly process. These regions of pure electrolyte have enhanced ionic conductivity
compared to the rest of the porous cathode, resulting in overall improvements in
performance at high discharge rates. CoEx structuring of a thick cathode demonstrated a
30% increase in energy density at high discharge rates (2D, or full discharge in 30
minutes), in a prior VTO-funded project.

SRI’s development of CoEx printing for lithium ion EV batteries builds on a solid base of
experience in applying CoEx to industrial processes. The initial application for CoEx was
in solar cell manufacturing, where it was used to deposit silver metallization onto
crystalline silicon solar cells at a pitch and fineness that was not possible with
conventional screen printing. CoEx was integrated into a pilot production tool with
SolarWorld Innovations, where multi-channel CoEx printheads printed viscous silver
gridline pastes at full production speeds and high uptime, demonstrating both cost
reduction and efficiency improvement.1 SRI has adapted the CoEx process for lithium ion
(Li+) battery printing, working with LiCoO2 (LCO), LiNi1/3Mn1/3Co1/3O2 (NMC111), and
LiNi0.5Mn0.3Co0.2O2 (NMC532) materials to demonstrate high-performance test cells. SRI
has over 20 issued or pending patents on CoEx printing technology and a long history
working with government and commercial partners to successfully transition new
technologies to market. Our partner Oak Ridge National Laboratories (ORNL) operates
the Battery Manufacturing Facility (BMF), the country’s largest open-access battery
manufacturing research and development center. The BMF houses battery manufacturing
tools, such as web coaters, pouch cell assembly equipment, and cell testers. The
combination of printing experience and innovation and manufacturing know-how make
up a team with the ideal innovation, expertise, and facilities needed to succeed in
delivering the proposed project goals. Using funding from the Vehicle Technologies
Office, we most recently have used CoEx to fabricate NMC532 cathode with structured
porosity. These cathodes were paired with conventional (homogeneous) anode electrodes
and assembled into 16 – 18 Ah pouch cells. Compared to baseline cells composed of
homogenous cathodes and anodes, CoEx cells showed 7-11% improvement in energy
density at 1D discharge, and 30% improvement at 2D discharge.

Our projected benefits include
• Increase in Useable Energy Density: By incorporating structured porosity into the
 electrode, we improve energy density under stressed conditions, such as during fast
 charging. Our prior work comparing CoEx and un-structured cells in ~ 16 Ah pouch
 cells showed 30% greater energy density at 2D discharge rates (here, we will use the
 suffixes “D” and “C” to distinguish between discharging and charging rates,
 respectively). By structuring the anode, which is crucial to obtaining fast charge
 performance, we expect similar improvements under fast charging conditions.

 • Increased Cell Lifetime: Thick electrodes are desired for their greater energy
 density, but their limitations are most acutely felt during fast charging and
 discharging, where the cell experiences the most stress. It is in these instances that the
 electrode will have the greatest underutilization and the greatest chance of electrode
 degradation.2 CoEx structuring provides pathways for these ions, increasing cell
 utilization and limiting battery degradation.
 • Reduction in Solvent Usage: CoEx inks are engineered to be higher in solids loading
 and viscosity than the typical inks used in slot die coating, in order to limit mixing
 and turbulence within the printhead. By careful optimization of the printhead design,
 as well as our expertise in the rheological design of highly loaded colloidal
 suspensions, we are able to print high quality electrodes while maintaining
 manufacturing performance and reliability. This increase in solids loading comes with
 a concomitant decrease in solvent loading. Compared with the example formulations
 used in the BatPAC Battery Manufacturing Cost Estimation tool developed by
 Argonne National Laboratories3, CoEx inks use 40 – 50% less solvent.
 • Reduction in Energy for Solvent Drying: The coating and drying process accounts
 for nearly 20% of the cost and over 45% of the energy consumption involved in
 battery production.4 Thus, one effect of the reduced solvent for CoEx inks is a
 reduction in the embedded energy of the processed electrode. After coating, the
 carrier solvent (typically N-methyl pyrrolidone (NMP) for the cathode and either
 NMP or water for the cathode) must be evaporated. NMP has a boiling point of
 202°C, significantly higher than water, and the drying process uses a considerable
 amount of energy. Drying is usually achieved by running the coating through a large
 horizontal dryer. NMP is considered a hazardous material and environmental
 regulations require that the evaporated solvent be recovered, which is an additional
 energy cost. CoEx inks, with their reduced solvent loading, will result in less solvent
 to evaporate and recover and thus 40 – 50% less energy associated with these dryers.
 Tt takes approximately 6 kWh of energy to dry and recover the solvent from the
 coatings used in a 67 Ah NMC622 cell, meaning that production of 1 GWh of battery
 capacity involves the expenditure of 25 GWh of energy in drying and solvent
 recovery alone. Transition to CoEx inks and printhead would result in savings of 10 –
 15 GWh of energy per 1 GWh battery capacity produced.
 • Reduction in Production Cost: The biggest limitation on coating speed is not due to
 limitations of the web feeding equipment or the slot die itself, but on the maximum
 solvent load of the dryer.5 In this case, increasing the coating speed means increasing
 the length of the dryer section, which may require prohibitive increases in capital
 expenditures and increases the overall size of the factory. CoEx has been designed to
 be a drop-in replacement for slot die coating, meaning that the vast majority of the
 balance of the coating line can remain the same, and capital utilization and space
 efficiency both go up. Moving to CoEx structuring means that the same coating line
 can operate at significantly higher speeds, generating more battery capacity for the
 same factory footprint. For coating lines in which throughput is limited by the length
 of the dryer, upgrading to CoEx printing would enable higher print speeds. Using
 BatPAC for analyzing the cost reduction, this change results in a 5 – 15% reduction in
 costs on a $/kWh basis.

Results and Discussion

The results for this project will be discussed in task-order.

Task 1: CoEx Ink Development (M1-M4)

Task Summary: In this task, we will identify the raw materials needed for this project
with input from the national labs for guidance on which active electrode materials to use,
procure the materials and create inks that are compatible with CoEx printing. None of
the raw materials will be sourced from China. Coatings for each electrode will comprise
two different fluids that are deposited simultaneously, so two inks will be made for both
the anode and the cathode, totaling four ink formulations. In order to achieve good co-
flow within the printhead, the inks must be matched rheologically. The composition must
also be tuned to achieve the desired structure when dried.

Subtask 1.1: Select Active Materials (M1)

Subtask summary: At the beginning of the project, we will select and purchase anode and
cathode materials for use in this project. None of the raw materials will be sourced from
China. Anode materials will be based on graphite. Cathode materials will be based on
Lithium Nickel Manganese Cobalt Oxide (NMC), likely NMC622 or NMC811
(LiNi0.6Mn0.2Co0.2O2 or LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2, respectively). The exact materials chosen
will depend on material availability at the time of the project start, as well as particle
size distribution and performance specifications.

Before ordering materials, we consulted with some subject matter experts that were
recommended to me by AMMTO. Ozge Kahvecioglu at Argonne National Laboratory
(ANL) recommended that I choose a low-Co chemistry, and also suggested we consider
spinel materials, since high-Mg chemistries are of growing interest. However, the spinel
materials are high voltage materials, and since ORNL does not have as much experience
with the compatible electrolytes, we decided to proceed with a conventional NMC622
material from BASF. This material is produced in the United States. For anode
materials, we were trying to decide between standard graphite or a graphite silicon
material. After speaking to Wenquan Lu at ANL and Anthony Burrell at NREL, we
decided to proceed with a standard graphite material. We attempted to source this
product from the United States, reaching out to Superior Graphite, Novonix, and Urbix,
as well as various other global suppliers. In the end, we were only able to source graphite
from Targray, purchasing a product that is produced in India.

Subtask 1.2: Develop Cathode Inks (M1-M2)

Subtask Summary: Cathode materials will be used to create inks suitable for CoEx
printing.

Since materials were only ordered at the start of the project, there was some time before
they arrived. In the interim, we made slurries with surrogate materials, i.e. other similar
active materials we had on hand. This work allowed us to quickly get up to speed when
the new material arrived. The CoEx printing process requires two inks, one as the
majority fluid which forms the bulk of the electrode, and one as the minority fluid that

experiences the drying shrinkage to form the grooved valley regions. The majority and
minority fluids that we developed have NMC622 loadings of 95.5 and 83 wt%,
respectively, on a dry basis. If these inks are printed at the desired volumetric ratio of 4:1,
we expect the films to be approximately 94.5% active material on a dry weight basis.
These inks were measured to have viscosities of 23 and 13 Pa-s at 10 s-1 shear rate.

Subtask 1.3: Develop Anode Inks (M1-M4)

Subtask summary: Anode materials will be used to create inks suitable for CoEx printing.

The lead time for anode material was significantly longer than the cathode material, so it
wasn’t until Q3 that we received our first large batch of graphite powder and could begin
ink development. The CoEx printing process requires two inks, one as the majority fluid
which forms the bulk of the electrode, and one as the minority fluid that experiences the
drying shrinkage to form the grooved valley regions. The majority and minority fluids
that we developed have graphite loadings of 94 and 90.8 wt%, respectively, on a dry
basis. If these inks are printed at the desired volumetric ratio of 4:1, we expect the films
to be approximately 93.6% active material on a dry weight basis. The viscosities of the
majority and minority inks at 10 s-1 shear rate are approximately 33 and 11 Pa-s,
respectively.

Task 2: Electrochemical Modeling (M1-M3)

Task Summary: A macro-homogeneous porous electrode model will be developed to
estimate the performance of thick graphite-NMC CoEx cells, with improvement in active
material utilization at charge and discharge rates in the range C/2-2C. The relationship
between geometry and performance at extreme fast charging conditions (4.5-6C) will be
investigated. The macro- homogeneous model can be complemented by a mesoscale
analysis if its predictive capability decays at high rate and local microstructural effects
become dominant. Although electrolyte channels and increased porosity improve active
material utilization in thick electrodes, the electrochemical model will be used to explore
trade-offs between volumetric and gravimetric energy density for electric vehicle (EV)
applications. Several configurations, including anode and cathode thickness and the
depth and spacing of electrolyte channels, will be analyzed while also considering
manufacturability constraints imposed by the CoEx method. The electrochemical model
(combined with a theoretical prediction of the average properties for a pouch cell) will
be used to efficiently explore the design space and investigate several combinations of
geometry and material properties.

During the course of our prior VTO-funded battery project, we created 1 Ah baseline
cells in addition to the desired CoEx cells. Our initial modeling work sought to replicate
that data. Figure 3 shows the experimental data for baseline cells at a loading of 25

mg/cm2, as well as curves predicted through modeling. While the initial model was
qualitatively the same shape as the experimental curve, quantitatively it underperformed
the experimental model. This was fixed by adjusting the maximum and electrode state of
charge within the model.

Figure 3. Specific capacity vs. discharge rate for 1 Ah baseline cells with an active
material loading (NMC532) of 25 mg/cm2. The experimental data is outlined in blue,
along with two modeling curves in orange and gray. The model was adjusted, mainly by
changing parameters correlating lithium concentration to the battery depth of discharge.

After the model was validated, we continued to study baseline cathode and anode
compositions. Our initial research thrust was to comprehensively study the effect of
anode and cathode porosity on charge and discharge performance. To start, we chose a
cathode with loading of 25 mg/cm2, with an anode loading designed to match (1.15 N:P
ratio). The cathode composition was a 96/2/2 ratio of active material / carbon black /
PVDF on a dry weight basis, while the anode composition was 94/2/2/2 in terms of
graphite / carbon black / CMC / SBR on a dry weight basis. The thickness of the cathode
is determined by the porosity, ranging from 20 to 40% porosity (73 to 97.5 microns).
Similarly, the thickness of the matching anode was varied from 78.4 to 176.4 microns by
adjusting the porosity between 10 and 60%. The goal of covering this range of porosities
is to determine the limiting porosities below which performance is affected. Figure 4
shows this data for discharge performance. In this plot, we show specific capacity vs.
anode porosity for three different cathode porosities (20, 30, and 40%) at two different
discharge rates (1D and 4D discharge). For the 1D discharge, we can see that the curves
for 30 and 40% cathode porosity are nearly identical, indicating that above 30% porosity,
there is no performance benefit to further increasing viscosity. Similarly, there is not
much difference in specific capacity when the anode porosity is increased above 30%.
At 4D discharge, which is a much more aggressive situation, this maximum porosity
value is 40%.

cathode porosity in baseline electrodes

Figure 5 shows the data of Figure 4 depicted in terms of gravimetric and volumetric
energy density. The energy density peaks at about 30% for the 1D discharge and 40% for

the 4D discharge, consistent with our analysis of the specific capacity curves. Increasing
porosity to 30% improves the energy density at 1D, but further increases in porosity only
serve to reduce energy density by increasing weight in the form of excess electrolyte, or

Figure 14. Example setup for CoEx Structure Modeling

Figure 14 is an example setup for CoEx structure modeling. In this image, the orange
regions are the anode, the dark blue regions are the cathode, and the light blue regions
indicate where the electrolyte is the predominant species. One key change for this
modelling effort compared to previous studies is that here our geometries are not “square
wave” structures with completely vertical walls, but rather the modeled geometries are
closer to those actually observed in printed structures.

One key variation that we wanted to measure is how much the alignment between the
anode and cathode structures affects the electrochemical performance.

Figure 15. Specific capacity of CoEx batteries under charge and discharge conditions,
comparing completely aligned and completely misaligned structures.

Figure 15 shows modeling data for a CoEx battery, with a CoEx cathode loading of 30
mg/cm2 and a matching CoEx anode. The data compares the specific capacity under fast
charge/discharge (4C/4D) for two alignment conditions. In both electrodes, the structure
ratio is 4:1 (ridge:valley, FWHM). For the aligned case, the valleys are opposite to one
another across the separator, representing the fastest route for ion conduction. In the
misaligned case, the anode is offset by one-half the pitch (225 µm), such that the valleys
are the farthest from each other. Here, we show that the difference in specific capacity
between these two alignments is minimal, less than 2%, over a wide range of anode
porosities.

Figure 16. Effect of differing ridge:valley ratios between the cathode and anode on
specific capacity for a 4C charging rate. The cathode active material loading is 30
mg/cm2

We also studied the effect of having different structure ratios for the anode and cathode
electrodes. Figure 16 shows the specific capacity of CoEx batteries for nine
combinations of anode and cathode structure geometries. The three levels chosen for the
structure ratios are 2:1, 5:1, and 7:1. Considering the 450 µm pitch, this reflects FWHM
widths for the valley regions of 150 µm, 75 µm, and 56 µm respectively. All of these
electrodes are at the same loading, 30 mg/cm2 NMC loading on the cathode side, and as
such the high structure ratio electrodes are thinner than the low structure ratio samples
due to their reduced open space regions. For example, CoEx cathode/anode thicknesses
are 106.5 µm and 127.8 µm, respectively for the 7:1 structure ratios and 116.3 µm and
140.0 µm for the 2:1 structure ratios.

The results indicate the highest specific capacity is reached with a 7:1 structure ratio on
the anode and a 2:1 structure ratio on the cathode, and the lowest capacity is the vice-
versa situation. The difference between these structure combination is about a 25%
difference in specific capacity. It is not clear at this moment why there is such a large
difference between these two examples, and why it isn’t always beneficial to have a high
ridge:valley ratio. It should be noted that this reflects the performance at the specific
capacity level and not the energy density level.

Subtask 3.1 : Specify and Acquire Pump(s) (M1-M5)

Subtask Summary: The high viscosity of CoEx inks and the requirement for low flow
variability during printing operation means that pump specifications will be more
stringent compared to the case of pumping a single fluid through a slot die head. Pumps
will be selected based on their flow rate uniformity, maximum pressure, cost, and size.

In order to determine what kind of pumps we should purchase, we first determined the
range of volumetric flow rates that would be required under standard printing. In our
planned web-coater sessions at ORNL, we will likely be coating at 2-3 m/min coating
speed with a film that is 180 mm wide and ~ 200 µm thick, which leads to a specification
of 70 – 100 mL slurry flow rate per minute. The CoEx printhead has 2 input fluids,
however, and as such requires two pumps. Furthermore, the flow of the two fluids is
quite asymmetric, with the majority fluid flow rate being 3 – 10 times greater than the
minority fluid flow rate. In the case of a 1:10 flow rate ratio, the minority fluid pump
could have flow rates as low as 6 mL/min. We considered gear pumps as well as
progressive cavity pumps, and felt that the progressive cavity pumps would be the least
likely to damage the slurry particles and most likely to provide even flow. We ordered
progressive cavity pumps from Fluid Research, which are assembled in the US. We have
ordered one pump designed for relatively low flow rates (0.56 mL / revolution), and one
designed for relatively higher flow rates (3 mL / revolution).

Subtask 3.2: Integrate Pump (M4-M8)

Subtask Summary: After the specified pump(s) are received, we will integrate the pumps
into the CoEx printing process and verify that CoEx structuring can be achieved.

We received the pumps and verified that they are operational. Before we began to test
these pumps with battery inks containing active material, we started working with a
surrogate fluid of reasonable viscosity to test variability. For our purposes, we used a
suspension of ethyl cellulose binder dissolved in diethylene glycol butyl ether (Butyl
Carbitol). Butyl carbitol is a non-toxic, high boiling point organic solvent that would
allow us to continuously cycle fluid through the pump system without concern about
solvent loss over time. In order to confirm that we are achieving a < 20% deviation in
flow rate, we first needed to determine the best way to determine the flow rate. Ideally,
we would use a gear-type volumetric flow gauge, in which the motion of the fluid turns
the cogs of a gear. However, we could not find a reasonably priced gauge that could
operate at the low flow rates that we desire, in the range of 10 – 50 mL/min, with the
degree of sensitivity we require. We decided to start by measuring the mass of fluid
dispensed over time by the smaller of the two pumps acquired. Not only does the smaller
pump require less fluid to operate, but any measurement technique that could reliability
measure the variance in the flow of the minority fluid would likely work for the majority
fluid as well. The pump output was connected to a flexible metal hose and capped with a

fine syringe tip, in order to provide a comparable pressure drop for the fluid. The fluid
exiting the syringe tip was captured into a beaker placed onto a scale, and the change in
mass was recorded with time. Figure 18 shows representative data for this test, showing
(a) the cumulative mass dispensed over time and (b) the mass flow rate over time as the
pump was cycled on and off. This test was intended to determine how long it takes for the
system to reach “steady state”.

Figure 18. Data from initial testing of progressive cavity pumps. (a) Mass dispensed as a
function of time, and (b) mass flow rate over time

There were two takeaways from this experiment. For one, we saw a considerable amount
of time needed to reach steady state when the pump is turned on as well as off. It is
likely this is due to entrapped air within the system, and we are working on ways to
reduce the amount of air within the system. The second thing we learned is that this mass
measurement method is not fast enough to capture useful data. The balance we have is
capable of only 5-6 Hz measurements, and given the amount of noise within the signal,
that is not enough of a data rate in order to achieve reliable results.

For the second test, we switched over to pressure measurement as an indicator of
variability. A piezoelectric pressure sensor is capable of over 1000 Hz measurement, so
is better able to resolve rapid changes in pressure and to have enough data to average and
reduce noise.

Figure 19. Normalized pressure vs. time for three different pump rpms and flow rates.

Figure 19 shows an example of this data. Three different pump rpms were chosen to
cover a range of possible flow rates. When the CoEx printer is installed on the Frontier
roll coater at ORNL, we intend to run the coater at speeds of approximately 3 m/min.
The rates chosen here reflect possible minority fluid flow rates for a 180 mm wide, 200
µm thick coating with flow ratios of 2.5:1 to 10:1 (majority:minority). In general,
running at a low flow ratio (i.e. higher minority flow rate) will result in wider grooves
and reduced volumetric energy density. It is our desire to run at as high a flow ratio as is
possible without creating coating defects.

One observation we had was that there is a pronounced ripple in the pressure curves once
they reach their setpoint. The frequency of this ripple appears to correspond to the rpm
of the motor, indicating that this is not a random variation but reflects either mechanical
variability in the pump speed or asymmetry in the rotor/stator pairing. We attempted two
different fixes for this issue of pressure variation. First, we switched to a different stator,
hoping that this stator would have lower asymmetry. While the variation was reduced, it
was not eliminated. Second, we added a pressure dampener to the system. This
dampener is charged with air, which is quite compressible, and this air reservoir absorbs
changes in pressure and results in overall reduced variation, at the expense of slower
response time. For our use case of an extended print session, we don’t anticipate that a
slower response time will cause issues.

time with pressure dampener installed.

reduces variation from roughly 5-10% down below 2%.

In early March 2024, SRI personnel visited the Battery Manufacturing Facility for the
first printing attempts. In advance of this trip, a substantial amount of the equipment
(pumps, pump controls, fluidic assemblies, etc.) was sent beforehand. Just before the
trip, a quantity of ink was made at SRI and shipped to ORNL. We also planned on
having ink made at ORNL to use as a backup. The first working day at the facility was
spent reviewing safety protocols, and assembling and setting up the pump and fluidics.
There was some delay, as the pump control boxes initially did not meet ORNL’s
electrical requirements, and the grounding connections had to be rewired. On our third
day we were finally able to load the pumps with ink and begin anode printing. Anode
printing continued on the fourth day of our visit, after which we disassembled and
cleaned the printheads to prep for cathode printing on the fifth day.

Figure 27. Representative image of the printing setup

Figure 27 shows our printing setup. The two pumps and their control boxes are situated
on a rolling cart, for ease of use. This allows them to be moved out of the way when
necessary, and keeps the pumps together and upright. The vessels covered in aluminum
foil are the ink reservoirs for the two pumps. The system is set up so that the fluid path is
vertical. This allows the ink in the reservoirs to feed into the pump assembly via gravity,
and it reduces the chance that air will be trapped in the fittings during priming, since it
exits the pump vertically and can push any residual air out. The two pumps are different
sizes, reflecting the fact that we run our two fluids at a volumetric asymmetry, with
substantially more flow in the majority fluid, such that we require the pumps to have
different “sweet spots” for flow. The pumps are connected to the printhead via high-
pressure flexible hosing (in this image, only the majority fluid pump is connected to the
printhead). The length of flexible hose is longer than we have typically used when
printing at SRI, the consequence of which is that we had a larger amount of “dead
volume” within the fluidic system, which results in greater compliance and a longer time
required to reach steady state flow.

Before performing a printing run, we had to first calibrate the pumps. This was done by
running the pumps at three different RPM’s for a fixed length of time (generally 10 –
30s), and collecting the extruded fluid. This ink was weighed, and based on our estimate
for ink density this was used to calculate the flow rate of ink per RPM. For instance, for
our anode ink the smaller pump dispensed at about 0.031 mL/min/RPM, and the large
pump dispensed at 0.17 mL/min/RPM. These values were used to set a target
majority:minority fluid flow rate via control over the pump RPM.

Over the course of two days, we performed seven anode prints. ORNL staff operated the
web coater, while SRI personnel were responsible for the operation of the pumps and the
printhead. These prints typically involved a 60 – 90 s dispense at an average web speed
of 5 ft/minute, which was a comfortable speed for the ORNL staff and allowed for
sufficient heater residence time to evaporate all of the solvent. Pump RPM’s were
chosen to achieve a target majority:minority volumetric flow rate of 3:1, and overall
loading of ~ 15 mg/cm2 graphite (appropriate loading to match a target 25 mg/cm2 NMC
loading). After our initial print, punches were taken of the coating film in order to assess
loading. Adjustments to the loading were made by slight changes in the coating speed,
with an increase in speed resulting in reduced loading and vice-versa. Prints 1 – 5 were
single side coatings, while Prints 6 and 7 were performed such that Print 7 was on the
opposing foil side of Print 6. The web coater setup at ORNL is not capable of
simultaneous deposition onto both sides of the foil, so Side 1 must first be coated, dried,
then cut from the foil roll and spliced back into the input roller, flipped to allow for Side
2 to be printed.

After our first visit, the exhaust system in the dry room at ORNL malfunctioned, and it
was over six months before it was fixed and we were able to continue working. In
November 2024, we visited ORNL twice (Nov. 5-8 and 19-21) for more print sessions.
Unlike our first visit in March of 2024, where we printed both anodes and cathodes
within the same visit, for the two November visits we focused on the anode and the
cathode for the first and second visit, respectively. Our visits proceeded similarly to our
initial visit – the first day was spent assembling the printhead and the pump system,
which was following by priming and calibrating the pumps and then finally performing
coating tests.

Anode coatings started at 2 feet per minute (fpm) line speed, as this was slow enough that
the coatings would dry fully by the time they exited the serpentine dryer. There are two
main ways that we can adjust the loading; we can change the coating line speed and we
can change the pump flow settings. In general, we would change the pump settings to
achieve an acceptable ridge:valley ratio, and then fine tune the loading by changing the
line speed. The line speed can only be changed in increments of 0.1 fpm at maximum,
nominally 5% of the baseline speed. We printed multiple short runs of 60 s, i.e. 2 ft or ~
60 cm while we dialed in the settings, and then two longer coatings of 2.7 m and 5 m
long double sided coating. A similar process was followed for cathode films, which
culminated in a 24 ft double sided print.

There are three main issues that would make a coating unusable for battery assembly.
One, the loading may be off. We are nominally targeting an NMC loading of 25 mg/cm2,
and then aiming for anode loading that is capacity matched with an N:P ratio of 1.1 –
1.15. We sample our coatings by taking small punches, and use that data to adjust print
settings for the subsequent print. Eventually, we will be matching electrodes individually
by mass. Second, the structuring can be off. This can be due to incorrect flow rates of
the majority and minority fluids, such that our “grooves” are too wide, for example.
Alternately, the printhead could clog, which manifests as abnormally wide ridges or
valleys if the intervening flow is cut off. And finally, we could have poor adhesion
issues. This would eventually result in high contact resistance and lower battery
performance, and increases the risk of flaking during processing that would lead to bare
electrode spots. The main issue we had with our anode inks was with adhesion.

Our final visit to ORNL occurred during the first week of February 2025. Our focus for
this trip was on the anode coatings, specifically attempting to manufacture them with
better adhesion than was seen during the first visit. During that visit, we could only
adjust coating parameters, since slurries were made at SRI and shipped to ORNL. The
main levers we tried to adjust were coating speed, coating temperature, and foil
treatment. For the foil treatment, we used the corona treater to plasma clean the copper
foil before coating, in case that trace organics were inhibiting the adhesion of the anode.
For this visit, we adjusted the formulation as well. The majority slurry comprises most
of the volume of the deposited film, so it was the only fluid that was changed. The
majority fluid has a solids composition that is nominally 94 wt% graphite, and then 2wt%
each of carbon black conductive additive, CMC binder, and SBR (94/2/2/2). The SBR

serves as the adhesion promoter, so the most obvious change would be to increase the
SBR proportion of the formulation. Preliminary tests were performed at SRI using a
blade coater to see if adhesion was improved, and it appeared that better results were seen
with a 93/2/2/3 formulation, i.e. increasing the SBR content by 1% and reducing the
graphite content to compensate. Further tests at 4 and 6 wt% SBR showed slightly better
adhesion than the 3%, suggesting that there were limited returns for increasing the binder
more. Our desire was to limit the extent of the formulation modification, considering that
we did not have time or resources to do extensive print testing of a new formulation at
SRI.

At ORNL, the coater is optimized to reduce the total footprint needed for the line; after
passing by the slot die the foil passed through a spiraling heater section before reaching
the takeup roll (see Figure 31)

 Figure 31. Interior of ORNL’s coater, revealing the orientation of the heating section

In this image, the foil is entering from the top left before spiraling clockwise through 9
heating zones and reaching the takeup zone. Due to the close proximity of the differing
heating zones, there is a fair amount of cross-talk, and it is difficult to get large
temperature differences between adjacent heating zones. For the most part, zones 3-8 are

set to the same temperature. The first test prints varied the temperature of the zones to
see what effect it could have on the adhesion. Heating too quickly will cause adhesion
issues, as the vertical shrinkage differential will cause destructive strain. Heating too
slowly means that the films are not fully dried by the time they exit the heater. We
started our print session with a maximum temperature of 100°F, followed by tests at
140°F, and then 140°F with twice the web speed (4 ft/min vs. 2 ft/min). Adhesion was
evaluated qualitatively during based on the edges of the punched samples that are used to
measure loading. None of these process adjustments helped with the adhesion.

There were two attempts to adjust slurry formulation on-site at ORNL. Both of the anode
slurries were prepared and tested at SRI before shipping to ORNL. However, when it
came time to load the slurries into the pump feeds, the majority slurry was much thicker
(higher in viscosity) than intended, with poor flowability. The pumps are gravity fed, so
we were concerned that they would become fluid starved. We decided to transfer the
slurries to the ORNL planetary mixer, add solvent (water), and mix overnight before
coating the next morning. Unfortunately, the hydraulics on the mixer failed at some point
during the evening, causing the mix can to drop and exposing the slurries to the dry room
atmosphere for multiple hours. The following morning, we arrived to find the slurry
largely dried. We did the best we could to recover the formulation by removing large
chunks of the dried fluid and remix the fluid. It is possible, however, that this event
irrevocably ruined this batch of slurry.

After our initial coatings showed no improvement in adhesion despite the increased
binder content, we decided to attempt one more formulation change. In this case, more
SBR binder was added to the formulation to increase the ratio of components closer to
91/2/2/5 (graphite / carbon black / CMC / SBR). Prints with this ink showed rapid
clogging of the printhead, and we believe that the multiple reformulation attempts and the
concurrent multiple exposures to the atmosphere led to aggregate formation in the slurry
that caused clogging. This was the last print we were able to perform on this project.

Milestone 5.2.1: At least five 2 Ah or larger CoEx cells created, with anode and cathode

Although we were not able to fabricate anode coatings that had good adhesion, we
decided to assemble cells with the coatings that we did have, even if they were not ideal
coatings. Normally, a pouch cell would be created with double-sided cathodes, with
double-sided anodes between each set of cathodes and single sided anodes at the top and
bottom of the electrode assembly stack. However, for our case we had a good amount of
quality single sided cathode coating and very little high-quality single sided anode. For
this reason, we used double-sided anodes throughout the assembly and a combination of
double-sided and single-sided cathodes to create the cells. Five cells were created, with
an expected capacity of 2.2 – 2.3 Ah per cell, based on the listed 177 mAh/g capacity
given by the cathode powder manufacturer.

 1. Key Achievements

 Many of the final objectives of this project were not achieved, in part due to technical issues
 outside of our control. However, some of the intermediate objectives of this project were achieved.
 We structured program activities and tasks to address areas of deficiency we experienced when
 doing commercialization outreach following the prior VTO-funded project. In that project, we
 demonstrated an increase in energy density in CoEx-structured cathodes relative to unstructured
 baseline cathodes. Our main concern in creating an appropriate baseline was to make sure that the
 active material loading was equivalent between the CoEx electrodes and the baseline electrodes.
 When discussing these results with potential partners, however, a common item of feedback was
 that the porosity of our cathode and the amount of binder was much higher than would typically be
 acceptable, and that the absolute magnitude of our energy densities were not very high. When we
 visited a potential customer (GM) for a printing demonstration on their test coater line, the fact that
 we used a pneumatically actuated cartridge system for our slurry delivery was a point of concern.
 Thus, for this project, a point of emphasis was on creating battery electrodes that had porosity,
 composition, and capacity values closer to what industry expected, and to integrate a continuous
 pumping system that was closer to the slurry metering solutions found on a commercial coating
 line. We developed CoEx slurry formulations that were significantly higher in active material

 content (96+ wt% vs 90 wt% previously) and lower in solvent content, and printed structures with
 lower envelope porosity (35% vs 45+%). We also demonstrated that progressive cavity pumps can
 deliver an appropriate flow rate of slurry at an appropriate pressure for successful operation of the
 CoEx printhead.

2. Benefits Assessment

 It is difficult to make accurate estimates of benefits for this project, since we were unable to
 electrochemically evaluate our structured electrodes relative to baseline electrodes.

3. Lessons Learned

 There are some risks that we were unable to mitigate in this project, and some unanticipated risks
 that emerged. One of the requirements of this project was to not use component materials that were
 produced in China, which significantly reduced the available options for active and inactive
 materials. This was particularly evident on the anode side, where we may have purchased materials
 that were sub-optimal for our fast-charging experiments. Developing both an NMP-based
 formulation (for cathode slurries) and an aqueous-based formulation (for anode slurries)
 complicated our process development, and we found that our experience with the NMP/PVDF
 cathode slurries did not translate to the aqueous/CMC/SBR anode slurries. In order to be efficient
 in our use of resources, we performed most of our development in the SRI facilities, with the
 assumption that we would be able to replicate those results at the ORNL facilities. In this way, we
 would limit the number of trips required and the concordant expenses. This assumption was not
 true, especially in regards to the adhesion of the anode films to the copper current collectors. When
 printing at SRI, we did not have adhesion issues, while at ORNL, the adhesion of the anode was
 quite poor. This may be due to the dry room atmosphere, or the drying conditions (box oven vs.
 multi-zone belt dryer).
