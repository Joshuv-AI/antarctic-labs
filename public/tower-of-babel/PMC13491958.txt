Nanoparticle-based strategies targeting disulfidptosis, anoikis, and PANoptosis for enhanced cancer therapy
Authors: Jiaquan Mao, Adel Habibul, Yongke Bai, Sheng Xin, Jun Zhang, Sen Fu, Wen Song, Xiaodong Song, Kun Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The evasion of regulated cell death (RCD) is a distinct hallmark of cancer that drives tumor progression, metastatic dissemination, and therapeutic resistance. Recent discoveries of non-canonical cell death pathways, namely disulfidptosis, anoikis, and PANoptosis, have provided promising new directions for overcoming resistance to conventional therapies. Disulfidptosis is characterized by intracellular disulfide stress and actin cytoskeleton collapse under glucose starvation; anoikis serves as an extracellular matrix detachment-induced barrier to metastasis; and PANoptosis acts as a highly regulated inflammatory cascade integrating pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis, exhibiting heightened potential for immune activation. However, the clinical utility of small-molecule inducers for these RCD pathways is currently hindered by off-target toxicity, poor pharmacokinetics, and the potential for compensatory resistance. To address these limitations, advanced nanomedicine platforms have been developed to enable tumor-targeted delivery and controlled release of therapeutic agents, while also synergizing with multiple treatment modalities to remodel the tumor microenvironment. Herein, this review summarizes the molecular mechanisms of these three RCD pathways and provides an in-depth analysis of nanotherapeutic strategies designed to exploit them. Furthermore, we explore the synergistic approaches that combine nano-platforms with conventional therapies, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, phototherapy, and sonodynamic therapy, to enhance antitumor efficacy and remodel the tumor immune microenvironment. Finally, critical challenges are discussed, including the specificity of tumor targeting, management of inflammatory responses, and the development of personalized therapeutic approaches. The review concludes by highlighting the clinical prospects of using nanotechnology to precisely regulate non-canonical cell death pathways for effective tumor eradication and metastasis suppression.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Cell death is functionally categorized into accidental cell death (ACD) and regulated cell death (RCD) [1, 2]. ACD is an uncontrolled process triggered by unforeseen physical or chemical insults that exceed the cell’s adaptive capacity [3]. It occurs without the involvement of dedicated internal signaling and execution machinery [3]. In contrast, RCD involves specific signal transduction pathways and defined effector mechanisms. RCD can be activated under both physiological conditions and pathological stress, and when occurring under physiological conditions, it is termed programmed cell death [2]. Recent research has identified numerous non-apoptotic forms of RCD that play evolutionarily conserved roles in human development and pathophysiological responses. Based on distinct molecular characteristics, RCD can be subdivided into several subroutines, including apoptosis, autophagic cell death, necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, cuproptosis, parthanatos, oxytosis, NETosis, disulfidptosis, lysozincrosis, and anoikis [3–5]. Historically, these subroutines were considered to have independent activation and execution pathways. However, substantial evidence now shows that, under specific conditions, cells engage in molecular crosstalk, interconnecting multiple RCD subroutines [6–8]. PANoptosis, a recently identified pathway, exemplifies this complex interaction. It exhibits core features of pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis, but cannot be fully inhibited by blocking effectors specific to any single one of these pathways [8, 9].

Dysregulation of RCD and associated inflammatory responses are fundamental hallmarks of cancer in the tumor microenvironment (TME) [8, 10]. Current non-invasive cancer therapies aim to selectively induce cancer cell death while minimizing damage to healthy tissues [11]. Tumor cells exploit this complexity by evolving mechanisms to evade specific RCD pathways, thereby promoting cancer progression and metastasis [10]. Conversely, distinct forms of RCD can remodel the TME by releasing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), influencing the host response to anticancer therapies [12–14]. Thus, elucidating the crosstalk between different RCD pathways holds significant promise. Targeting this crosstalk may circumvent tumor cell resistance to specific RCD modalities, offering a promising avenue for future cancer therapies.

Cancer cells with high SLC7A11 (a key component of the system xc− cystine/glutamate transporter) expression exhibit increased cystine uptake. However, under glucose starvation, sustained cystine influx leads to NADPH depletion, causing excessive accumulation of intracellular cystine and disulfide bonds. This disulfide stress triggers disulfidptosis in cancer cells [15]. SLC7A11 is a transmembrane protein responsible for cystine uptake and glutamate efflux, promoting intracellular glutathione (GSH) biosynthesis [16]. Paradoxically, high SLC7A11 expression activates glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), a central regulator of ferroptosis, suppressing lipid peroxide accumulation on the cell membrane and protecting tumor cells from ferroptosis [17]. Additionally, highly active SLC7A11 inhibits apoptosis and is associated with the activation of pro-oncogenic pathways [16]. Consequently, targeting disulfidptosis holds therapeutic potential for treating SLC7A11-overexpressing malignancies resistant to ferroptosis- and apoptosis-based therapies.

Resistance to anoikis is essential for the metastasis, colonization, and proliferation of malignant tumor cells [18]. Cancer cells must acquire anoikis resistance (AR) to survive in the circulatory system. The activation or inhibition of key signaling molecules and pathways, including integrin/Src/FAK [19], PI3K/AKT/NF-κB/TrkB [20], CaMKK2/AMPK [21], caspases [22, 23], MAPK [24], PKC [25], ERK [22], Wnt/β-catenin [26], TGF-β/Smad [27], and Hippo signaling, may promote AR [28, 29], potentially mediating tumor cell metastasis and enhancing survival. Promising therapeutic strategies for reversing AR include natural products such as Oroxylin A [30] and Curcumin [31] as well as molecular inhibitors like epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors [32, 33] and crizotinib [34]. Additionally, monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) [35, 36] nanomedicines, and gene-editing therapies [37–39] represent advanced approaches that exert their therapeutic effects by targeting key signaling pathways involved in AR.

PANoptosis, first defined by Kanneganti, derives its name from its core features: pyroptosis (P), apoptosis (A), and necroptosis (N). The activation of PANoptosis and the assembly of the PANoptosome, a cytoplasmic protein complex, are implicated in the pathophysiology and progression of cancer [40–42]. Additionally, PANoptosis plays a critical role in innate immune cells that surveil and eliminate intracellular pathogens [40]. Molecular clustering studies reveal that differential expression of PANoptosis-related genes is associated with the tumor immune microenvironment, biological functions, clinicopathological and immunological characteristics, prognostic features, and sensitivity to immunotherapy and chemotherapy [43–45]. When one or more RCD pathways are inhibited, PANoptosis may activate alternative cell death defense mechanisms, exerting antitumor effects. Therefore, distinguishing PANoptosis from singular RCD modalities is essential for developing novel cancer diagnostics, targeted therapies, and prognostic biomarkers.

Conventional chemotherapy and immunotherapy often lack specificity, lead to multidrug resistance (MDR), and cause significant side effects. Over the past few decades, the application of nanomaterials in medicine has focused on enhancing drug specificity, improving biocompatibility, and leveraging the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect to promote tumor accumulation [46]. To date, various nanomaterials have been developed for cancer therapy, including lipid-based nanoparticles, polymeric nanoparticles, inorganic nanoparticles, organic-inorganic hybrid nanocomposites, carbon-based nanomaterials, and natural product-derived nanoparticles [47, 48]. These nanomaterial platforms can serve as carriers that may facilitate the targeted delivery and uniform distribution of therapeutic agents, such as nucleic acid aptamers, chemotherapeutic drugs, immunomodulators, and physical therapeutic agents, within tumor tissues [49]. Nanomaterial-based therapeutic strategies, including chemotherapy, targeted therapy, photodynamic therapy (PDT), photothermal therapy (PTT), chemodynamic therapy (CDT), and sonodynamic therapy (SDT), offer innovative cancer treatment approaches. By enabling precise targeting of distinct cell death mechanisms (e.g., pyroptosis, ferroptosis, apoptosis, and immunogenic cell death [ICD]), these strategies provide effective, low-toxicity alternatives [50] addressing the shortcomings of traditional treatments.

In recent decades, numerous novel forms of non-apoptotic RCD have been discovered. This review elucidates the current understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying three RCD modalities: disulfidptosis, anoikis, and PANoptosis. Furthermore, this review systematically summarizes and analyzes research advances in applying these RCD pathways to tumor therapy, with a particular emphasis on nanotechnology-mediated strategies targeting disulfidptosis, anoikis, and PANoptosis. Finally, current challenges and future perspectives for the clinical translation of RCD-targeting nanotherapeutics are outlined (Fig. 1).

Disulfidptosis, anoikis, and PANoptosis in cancer therapy

Overview of disulfidptosis, anoikis, and PANoptosis

Disulfidptosis is a metabolism-associated RCD pathway, first identified as a process distinct from ferroptosis, apoptosis, necroptosis, and autophagy [15]. Its hallmark is the collapse of the actin cytoskeleton, triggered by disulfide stress. This stress originates from the aberrant accumulation of cystine in cells with high expression of SLC7A11 under glucose starvation conditions [15, 51–54]. The resulting disulfide stress promotes abnormal disulfide bond formation within actin and between actin-cytoskeletal proteins, leading to cytoskeletal disintegration [15, 55, 56]. A genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 screen identified the Rac1/WAVE regulatory complex (WRC)/Arp2/3 signaling axis as a critical regulator of this process [15]. Rac1, a Rho GTPase overexpressed in many tumors [57, 58], initiates actin remodeling by binding to the WRC [59–62]. This activation recruits and activates the Arp2/3 complex, which serves as a central nucleator of actin branching [60, 63–65]. The coordinated action of this pathway promotes intense actin polymerization at the cell cortex [65–69]. This dynamic remodeling of the actin network is proposed to promote the accumulation of disulfide bonds in cytoskeletal proteins, thus directly driving the cytoskeletal collapse that characterizes disulfidptosis (Fig. 2) [56, 62].

Anoikis, a subtype of apoptosis crucial for maintaining tissue architecture by eliminating displaced cells, was first defined by Frisch et al. after observing death in epithelial and endothelial cells following detachment from the extracellular matrix (ECM) [70]. Its execution involves canonical apoptotic pathways: detachment-induced signals activate either the intrinsic (mitochondrial) or extrinsic (death receptor) pathways, both culminating in caspase activation, DNA fragmentation, and membrane blebbing [71–74]. For metastatic dissemination, tumor cells must evade this process by developing AR, which enables anchorage-independent growth (AIG) [18, 71, 75]. Key regulators of anoikis include integrin-mediated signaling. Integrins, heterodimeric transmembrane receptors, transduce signals between the ECM and the cell interior [74, 76–79]. Upon loss of cell-ECM adhesion, integrins undergo bidirectional activation mediated by inside-out or outside-in signaling [78]. Activated integrins undergo conformational changes in their extracellular domains, cluster within the plasma membrane, and recruit intracellular proteins to form focal adhesion complexes [77, 78]. FAK is a key component of these complexes [78]. Subsequently, autophosphorylation of FAK at tyrosine residue 397 (pFAK-Y397) initiates downstream signaling cascades, including activation of Src family kinases, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, and MAPK/ERK pathways [72, 78, 80, 81]. Consequently, the loss of these survival signals triggers apoptotic cascades via intrinsic or extrinsic pathways, thereby executing anoikis and preventing aberrant cell colonization (Fig. 3) [72].

In contrast to traditional mixed cell death, which constitutes a mere spatiotemporal superposition of distinct death signaling pathways devoid of direct molecular crosstalk, PANoptosis is a highly coordinated inflammatory programmed cell death mediated by a multiprotein complex termed the PANoptosome [41, 82]. Its defining features encompass mechanistic integration and functional redundancy within the signaling network. Upon the recognition of specific danger signals by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) [83–85], upstream sensors such as ZBP1 and AIM2 recruit and physically associate with adaptor proteins and effector molecules, including RIPK1/3, FADD, and various caspases, to assemble the supramolecular PANoptosome scaffold [86–88]. Functioning as a central hub for death signal processing [89], this complex concurrently drives pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis via direct protein-protein interactions, thereby conferring cellular resistance to the inhibition of any single pathway [86]. Given this intricate network of interlocking mechanisms, a tripartite validation framework has been established to define PANoptosis. First, the key biochemical executioners of all three cell death pathways must be concurrently activated within the same spatiotemporal context [41, 89–91]. Second, direct physical interactions among core molecules and the structural assembly of the PANoptosome should be confirmed using techniques such as co-immunoprecipitation [83, 85, 92–99]. Finally, in functional intervention assays, inhibitors targeting a single pathway typically fail to completely reverse cell death; only the combined use of multi-pathway inhibitors or the direct knockout/knockdown of upstream core genes responsible for complex assembly can effectively rescue cell survival (Fig. 4; Table 1) [85, 100–103].

Specific applications in cancer therapy

Disulfidptosis in cancer therapy

Previous studies have demonstrated that SLC7A11 is crucial for the cystine/glutamate antiporter system, which provides the necessary components for GSH synthesis [16]. By maintaining GSH levels, SLC7A11 supports the activity of GPX4, an enzyme that scavenges lipid peroxides [104]. This makes the SLC7A11/GPX4 axis essential for redox balance and the prevention of ferroptosis. Therefore, when cancers overexpressing SLC7A11 become resistant to therapies targeting ferroptosis or apoptosis [16, 105], exploring their vulnerability to disulfidptosis presents a promising therapeutic strategy.

Liu et al. identified SLC7A11 and NADPH depletion as key factors in disulfidptosis [15]. Glucose starvation is typically achieved by blocking glucose uptake or increasing glucose consumption. The glucose transporter (GLUT) and sodium-glucose cotransporter (SGLT) transporter families are the main glucose importers in mammals, and their overexpression is linked to cancer growth and progression [106]. Inhibiting GLUT or SGLT reduces glucose uptake. Key regulators like glucose oxidase (GOx) and AMPK control glucose metabolism through aerobic oxidation and glycolysis [107]. In vitro studies show that tumors with high SLC7A11 expression are more sensitive to GLUT inhibitors [15]. These inhibitors block glucose uptake in renal cell carcinoma (RCC) cells, depleting glucose and NADPH, and triggering disulfidptosis. In vivo studies also showed that GLUT inhibitors reduce the growth of RCC with high SLC7A11 expression, suggesting disulfidptosis as a potential cancer treatment [15]. However, most research on disulfidptosis through glucose depletion focuses on nanomaterial development.

In addition to directly targeting glucose uptake, several studies have shown that disulfidptosis can also be triggered by indirectly regulating key factors in glucose metabolism. Bioinformatics analyses have found that high expression of lncRNA TMEM105 is linked to poor prognosis in pancreatic cancer and plays a role in regulating disulfidptosis [108]. It has been observed that under glucose starvation, knocking down TMEM105 increases pancreatic cancer cell mortality alongside noticeable F-actin contraction, a phenotype that can be reversed by the disulfidptosis inhibitor dithiothreitol (DTT) [15, 108]. This confirms that TMEM105 negatively regulates disulfidptosis. Therefore, targeting the TMEM105/β-catenin/c-MYC/GLUT1 axis to modulate disulfidptosis holds significant therapeutic promise for pancreatic cancer [108, 109]. In another study, Wan et al. found that STAT3 activates LDHB, which suppresses G6PD activity, leading to NADPH depletion and disulfide bond accumulation, causing actin network collapse. This suggests that LDHB could be a promising target for integrating disulfidptosis into cancer immunotherapy regimens [110]. Additionally, bioinformatics analyses identified ZIC5 as highly expressed in lung adenocarcinoma and associated with poor prognosis. Silencing ZIC5 in lung adenocarcinoma cells disrupted the cellular redox balance, increasing the NADP+/NADPH ratio and depleting GSH, which induced abnormal disulfide crosslinking in the cytoskeleton and triggered disulfidptosis [111]. Similarly, FGA was shown to interact with the SLC7A11 protein, enhancing the disulfidptosis process and inhibiting lung adenocarcinoma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [112], providing new potential targets for lung adenocarcinoma immunotherapy.

Multiple bioinformatics analyses support the potential of disulfidptosis for tumor treatment and clinical prognosis. Zhao et al. developed a disulfidptosis-related gene signature (DRGS) and tested it across six cancer cell line datasets. They found that high DRGS scores were associated with lower glucose uptake in tumors and increased sensitivity to disulfidptosis, which also correlated with better patient outcomes. In lab tests, melanoma cells with high DRGS scores were more susceptible to disulfidptosis under glucose starvation. Importantly, triggering disulfidptosis enhanced the effectiveness of anti-cancer drugs such as etoposide, methotrexate, and chloromethylketone [113]. Zhang et al. analyzed TCGA and GEO databases, identifying 15 genes linked to disulfidptosis. High expression of these genes was associated with poor prognosis and drug resistance in liver cancer. In sorafenib-resistant hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells, MYH9 expression was elevated. Inhibiting MYH9 made the cells more sensitive to sorafenib, causing F-actin contraction and inducing disulfidptosis. These findings suggest that targeting MYH9 could overcome drug resistance by triggering disulfidptosis [114].

Anoikis in cancer therapy

During tumor metastasis, cancer cells detach from the primary site and spread to distant organs by gaining the ability to survive without attachment to their native ECM or during prolonged adhesion to non-physiological substrates [115]. This process, known as AR, is crucial for metastatic spread. As such, targeting and inhibiting AR offers a promising therapeutic approach to prevent cancer progression and metastasis.

Activation of the integrin/Src/FAK pathway enables cancer cells to resist anoikis, allowing them to survive in circulation and promoting metastasis [116]. This pathway thus presents a promising therapeutic target for preventing cancer progression. LRRC15, a membrane protein overexpressed in various solid tumors, binds to fibronectin (FN) in the ECM and aids in the recruitment of integrin β1 [117–119]. Ray et al. demonstrated that the antibody-drug conjugate ABBV-085, which targets LRRC15, effectively inhibits integrin β1/FAK pathway activation, significantly reducing metastatic potential and AR in ovarian cancer [119]. Notably, integrin β1-mediated EGFR activation is critical for anchorage-dependent cell survival [120], highlighting the complex interplay between EGFR, integrins, and anoikis regulation. Studies involving co-localization and co-immunoprecipitation show that integrin-α2β1 physically interacts with EGFR in anchorage-independent tumor cells, promoting pro-survival signaling [121]. Moreover, Zou et al. reported that muscone inhibits EGFR activity, downregulating integrin β1/FAK signaling. This suppression reduces TOP2A expression and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in temozolomide-resistant glioblastoma cells, triggering mitochondrial-dependent anoikis and restoring temozolomide sensitivity [122]. Natural compounds also demonstrate potential to inhibit AR. For example, Jinfukang enhances anoikis sensitivity in circulating tumor cells (CTCs) by suppressing integrin/Src and MEK/ERK signaling in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [19]. Additionally, numerous FAK-targeting drugs are currently under development, with twelve FAK-targeting antitumor agents having entered clinical or preclinical evaluation worldwide as of 2024 [123]. These findings highlight the critical role of integrin-related pathways in AR mechanisms, suggesting that targeting these pathways could provide novel strategies to combat cancer metastasis.

Metabolic reprogramming is a key feature of cancer cells, supporting increased proliferation, migration, and invasion by providing energy and biosynthetic resources while enabling adaptation to the TME [124]. Disruptions in the metabolism of essential biomolecules, such as proteins, lipids, and nucleotides, can interfere with signaling dependent on anchorage and potentially influence pathways related to anoikis [75]. Targeting metabolic reprogramming to counteract AR presents a complex but promising therapeutic strategy. CD98 heavy chain (CD98hc/SLC3A2), a transmembrane glycoprotein receptor associated with integrins, plays a crucial role in regulating oxidative stress and amino acid metabolism [125]. Nalla et al. identified empagliflozin as a tumor suppressor and miR-128-3p mimetic. Empagliflozin degrades SP1 mRNA, downregulates CD98hc activity, elevates intracellular ROS and lipid peroxides in breast cancer cells, and consequently sensitizes cells to anoikis [126]. Additionally, enhanced mitochondrial metabolic activity in tumor cells promotes anabolism, biomass production, and ATP generation [127], which increases invasiveness, metastatic potential, and AR capacity. Shikonin, a compound known for its anti-cancer properties, activates GSK-3β, leading to the phosphorylation of PGC-1α at Thr295 in cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF)-induced triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells. Phosphorylated PGC-1α is subsequently targeted for degradation by the NEDD4 E3 ligase, resulting in downregulation of PGC-1α/ERRα signaling, suppression of mitochondrial biogenesis, and impairment of mitochondrial function and metabolic activity, ultimately reducing AR [128]. Carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A (CPT1A), a key enzyme in fatty acid oxidation (FAO), is involved in the transport of long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondrial matrix for β-oxidation [129]. Increased CPT1A activity, regulated by the transcription factor ETV4 and the ubiquitin ligase RNF2, maintains NADPH and GSH homeostasis via FAO, promoting AIG and AR in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma cells [130]. Dysregulation of post-translational modifications, particularly arginine methylation, is also implicated in cancer progression [131–133]. For example, methylenetetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase 1 undergoes symmetric dimethylation at arginine 173 when bound to protein arginine methyltransferase 5. This modification enhances NADPH metabolic flux, facilitating AR and promoting tumor metastasis [134].

Furthermore, biomechanical properties of the TME, including ECM stiffness, fluid shear stress (FSS), and circulatory dynamics, exhibit profound reciprocal regulation with anoikis. ECM stiffness heterogeneity serves not only as a crucial physical hallmark of the TME but also as a primary driver of AR in tumor cells. Research has identified the tumor suppressor DAPK1 as a core sensor of matrix stiffness. Within the rigid environment of healthy tissue, DAPK1 is inhibited by Src or EGFR-mediated tyrosine phosphorylation. Conversely, within the soft environment of the circulatory system, DAPK1 dissociates from adhesion complexes and undergoes activation via PTPN12-mediated dephosphorylation, thereby inducing anoikis [135]. Consequently, targeting the DAPK1/PTPN12 axis to restore the stiffness-sensing capability of tumor cells represents a potential strategy to counteract AR. During hematogenous dissemination, FSS, which is a central physical feature of the circulatory microenvironment, plays an increasingly recognized role in regulating tumor cell AR. For instance, Li et al. demonstrated that low shear stress directly induces the overexpression of the mechanosensitive protein Caveolin-1 in breast cancer cells, which subsequently exerts dual inhibitory effects on apoptotic pathways. This mechanical force-driven signal remodeling significantly enhances the circulatory survival of CTCs. This suggests that interventions targeting Caveolin 1 and its downstream shear stress response pathways could serve as a viable therapeutic strategy to eliminate CTCs and impede metastasis [136]. Within the context of circulatory dynamics, tumor cells detached from the matrix often undergo anoikis triggered by a surge in ROS. Evidence indicates that solid tumors can hijack factors such as IKZF1, NFE2, BTG2, and IRF8 by initiating an adherent to suspension transition (AST) program, thereby reprogramming their anchorage dependence. AST factors suppress the YAP/TEAD axis to drive spontaneous cell detachment while simultaneously upregulating HBA1/2 ectopically to construct an ROS buffering system, thereby conferring AR. Precisely intervening in the specifically activated AST program within CTCs, such as using lenalidomide to induce the degradation of these factors, provides a novel antimetastatic strategy that operates independently of the primary tumor to block hematogenous dissemination [137].

PANoptosis in cancer therapy

PANoptosis is a recently discovered form of RCD characterized by the simultaneous engagement of apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis, typically leading to lytic cell death and membrane rupture [138]. Regulated by the PANoptosome complex, it plays a key role in the innate immune defense system [139].

Bioinformatics analyses show that abnormal expression of PANoptosis-related genes is strongly linked to survival outcomes across 21 cancer types [140]. Additionally, PANoptosis scores, derived from algorithms, are significantly associated with TME composition, immune cell infiltration, and responses to immunotherapy [140]. Multiple studies also suggest that PANoptosis plays a role in immune escape and drug resistance during cancer progression [40, 138, 141]. Therefore, targeting the molecular regulators of the PANoptosis pathway is crucial for improving tumor immunotherapy and identifying new therapeutic targets.

Several studies have identified key factors in the upstream and downstream pathways related to PANoptosis. Lin et al. showed that NFS1 knockdown causes cell death in colorectal cancer (CRC) cells, and this effect was only partially reversed by inhibitors targeting apoptosis, pyroptosis, or necroptosis [142]. They also found that NFS1 deficiency, combined with oxaliplatin, increases oxidative stress, triggering PANoptosis [142]. This effect may be due to oxaliplatin-induced oxidative stress, which enhances phosphorylation at NFS1 S293, helping maintain iron-sulfur cluster balance [142]. Interferon regulatory factor 1 (IRF1) is a transcription factor activated by pathogen signals to trigger type I interferon (IFN) responses [143]. It also regulates PANoptosis. Karki et al. found that AOM/DSS-treated and ApcMin/+Irf1−/− mice had higher CRC susceptibility and reduced activity of key executioners like gasdermin D (GSDMD; pyroptosis), cleaved caspase-3/7 (apoptosis), and MLKL (necroptosis) [144]. This shows that IRF1 specifically induces PANoptosis-mediated antitumor effects, separate from its role in inflammation [97, 145, 146]. Targeting IRF1 or its downstream effectors to trigger PANoptosis could be a promising therapy for inflammation, infections, and cancer. Adenosine deaminase acting on RNA 1 (ADAR1) is another key upstream regulator of PANoptosis. ADAR1 binds to ZBP1 through its Zα2 domain, reducing the ZBP1-RIPK3 interaction and inhibiting PANoptosis activation [147]. On the other hand, ADAR1 deficiency or treatment with IFN and nuclear export inhibitors strongly activates ZBP1-dependent PANoptosis, inhibiting tumor growth [147]. This suppression likely results from ADAR1’s RNA-editing activity, which helps double-stranded RNA evade detection by PRRs [148, 149]. Loss of SAMHD1, an innate immune suppressor [150], causes DNA damage and cytosolic double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) buildup, which activates the DNA damage adaptor STING [151, 152]. Increased STING protein (via cGAS-independent pathways) leads to the formation of the caspase-8/RIPK3/ASC PANoptosome complex in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) cells, driving MLKL phosphorylation, caspase-3 cleavage, and GSDME cleavage to induce PANoptosis [153]. Additionally, Cai et al. showed that STING agonists enhance the effectiveness of programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors in DLBCL by activating PANoptosis [153].

Several studies have identified drugs that can induce PANoptosis through different pathways. Kanglexin (KLX), a novel anthraquinone derivative, directly binds to ZBP1, causing conformational changes that reduce its interaction with the E3 ubiquitin ligase RNF180. This inhibits ZBP1 degradation and stabilizes the protein [154]. Additionally, KLX upregulates HOXD10, a key transcriptional activator of ZBP1, boosting ZBP1 gene expression. This combined effect, encompassing transcriptional activation and post-translational stabilization, increases ZBP1 levels, which promotes PANoptosis in HCC cells [154]. The deubiquitinating enzyme inhibitor Spautin-1 promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of the key metabolic enzyme ACLY by targeting USP13, leading to severe mitochondrial damage and triggering PANoptosis in B lymphoma cells [155]. In pancreatic cancer, the PKMYT1-specific inhibitor RP-6306 induces PANoptosis by disrupting the G2/M checkpoint, causing DNA damage and mitotic catastrophe [156]. Similarly, Yang et al. found that mebendazole targets and inhibits tubulin α1A, causing acute myeloid leukemia cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase, which activates ZBP1-mediated PANoptosis and reduces tumor cell invasion [157]. These studies offer a strong foundation for developing therapeutic strategies that induce PANoptosis.

Nanoparticles targeting disulfidptosis, anoikis, and PANoptosis for cancer therapy

Nanoparticles targeting disulfidptosis for cancer therapy

Disulfidptosis is a novel form of RCD driven by intracellular redox imbalance. This process has garnered significant attention because of its central mechanism, which involves the irreversible collapse of the actin cytoskeleton in cancer cells triggered by disulfide stress. Although researchers have developed various therapeutic strategies to trigger disulfidptosis in tumor cells, these conventional interventions face notable limitations [158, 159]. Primarily, untargeted pharmacological agents lack spatial specificity. While these drugs disrupt the energy supply of the tumor, they frequently provoke severe systemic toxicity. Furthermore, inducing disulfidptosis relies heavily on the dual conditions of aberrant disulfide accumulation and glucose depletion. Conventional monotherapy fails to establish this required microenvironment consistently across space and time within the tumor. To overcome these challenges, multifunctional nanomaterials have emerged as enabling platforms for targeted disulfidptosis. This emergence is due to their unique physicochemical properties, customizable surface chemistry, and behaviors that respond to stimuli within the TME. Specifically engineered nanoparticle delivery systems can precisely target key metabolic nodes, including glucose and GSH, within tumors. This precision enables the concurrent loading and spatially controlled release of therapeutics directed at multiple targets. Consequently, nanomedicine represents an indispensable core strategy for triggering disulfidptosis in vivo.

Cancer cells adapt to rapid proliferation and metastasis by establishing a unique microenvironment characterized by metabolic dysregulation, altered redox balance, and immunosuppression [160]. Exploiting this vulnerability, a major frontier in nanodesign involves engineering ‘active mechanistic participants’ that transcend mere drug delivery to act as direct catalytic hubs. Building upon the aberrant redox status of tumor cells, therapies utilizing enzyme catalysis to modulate oxidative stress have shown great promise in inducing or potentiating disulfidptosis. For instance, Jin and colleagues developed a nanoplatform based on copper termed CGBH NNs concurrently loaded with GOx and buthionine sulfoximine [161]. These nanoparticles exhibit three distinct activities that mimic enzymes. Specifically, they mimic GPX, peroxidase (POD), and GOx, which synergistically amplify oxidative stress through an enzymatic cascade. Compared with untargeted pharmacological agents, CGBH NNs possess functions integrated across space and time. This integration enables the sustained disruption of cellular homeostasis through cascading enzymatic reactions. This cascade precisely depletes NADPH, induces the aberrant intracellular accumulation of cystine, and directly drives the formation of disulfide crosslinks within the actin cytoskeleton. Moreover, the antitumor efficacy of CGBH NNs was markedly attenuated upon the addition of tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP), confirming their capacity to effectively trigger disulfidptosis. The mechanism by which these nanoparticles trigger disulfidptosis is fundamentally distinct from conventional apoptosis mediated by ROS, thereby providing an innovative technological strategy for antitumor immunotherapy (Fig. 5). Similarly, Zhang et al. developed a BMP-Au nanoplatform via biomineralization mediated by bovine serum albumin. Its tripartite functional integration achieves the spatial and temporal synchronization of metabolic intervention, chemical cascade reactions, and immune activation [162]. This system harnesses the catalytic cascade of Au NPs and Mn2+ to profoundly deplete NADPH, eliciting disulfidptosis characterized by the collapse of the F-actin cytoskeleton. This precisely regulated form of cell death at the nanoscale, coupled with the enhancement of the cGAS/STING pathway mediated by Mn2+, generates a robust ICD effect. This effectively remodels the tumor immune microenvironment and promotes the conversion of immunologically cold tumors to active ones. In another approach, Yang et al. developed a Prussian blue nanomotor system powered by oxygen termed GOx@HPB [163]. This system leverages the tandem catalytic cascade between GOx and HPB to generate an oxygen flow. This endows the nanoparticles with autonomous propulsion within dense tumor tissue and effectively overcomes the diffusion barriers encountered by conventional passive delivery systems. This active delivery strategy optimizes the distribution of therapeutics across space and time within tumors, synergistically induces glucose starvation and oxidative stress, disrupts the intracellular NADPH supply chain, and efficiently triggers cell death cascades mediated by disulfides, thereby significantly enhancing cancer therapeutic efficacy.

Additionally, a hybrid nanoplatform (GOx/CuSAE) composed of GOx and a single-atom copper nanoenzyme induces aberrant intracellular disulfide crosslinking. This directly triggers disulfidptosis by depleting existing NADPH and simultaneously inhibiting its regeneration. Importantly, this cell death induced by disulfide stress could be alleviated by reducing agents such as DTT, β-mercaptoethanol, and TCEP [164]. Moreover, Liu and researchers demonstrated that nanoactivators (CGH NAs) based on Cu₂O initiate autonomously amplifying enzymatic cascades. These cascades induce disulfidptosis while simultaneously impairing mitochondrial function, thereby further compromising the metabolic defenses of tumor cells and producing potent synergistic antitumor effects [165]. Beyond enzymatic cascade strategies, increasing attention has been directed toward the direct delivery of oxidants into tumor cells, aiming to disrupt intracellular disulfide homeostasis through actively amplified redox imbalance. For example, nitroimidazole-grafted poly (sodium lipoate) (NI@PSL) nanoparticles degrade into dihydrolipoic acid in the high-GSH environment of tumor cells. This compound forms abnormal disulfide bonds with the cytoskeletal proteins’ thiol groups. Additionally, the nitroimidazole is reduced to aminoimidazole under hypoxic conditions, consuming NADPH and disrupting the intracellular redox balance. This prevents the repair of disulfide bonds, causing cytoskeletal collapse, triggering disulfidptosis, and significantly inhibiting tumor cell migration and invasion [166]. These studies underscore the potential of nanotechnology to precisely target and intervene in the abnormal redox homeostasis of tumors, enhancing treatment efficacy and precision.

The SLC7A11/GSH/GPX4 axis is a crucial pathway for maintaining redox balance in tumor cells and has been identified as a potential therapeutic target for inducing both disulfidptosis and ferroptosis. However, targeting this axis presents a fundamental paradox. While SLC7A11-mediated cystine transport promotes cystine accumulation and triggers disulfidptosis, the increased intracellular cystine also serves as a substrate for GSH synthesis. Elevated GSH levels, in turn, sustain GPX4 activity, which inhibits ferroptosis and undermines therapeutic effectiveness. To resolve this issue, FeOOH nanoshuttles loaded with gold nanodots and Fe-apigenin (Ap) complexes (FeOOH@Fe-Ap@Au NSs) were developed [167]. Ap blocks glucose uptake by inhibiting GLUT1, while the gold nanodots mimic GOx to increase glucose consumption. This combination depletes NADPH, preventing cystine from being converted to cysteine, which leads to cystine buildup and triggers disulfidptosis (Fig. 6). At the same time, low cysteine levels reduce GSH production, decreasing GPX4 activity and causing ferroptosis. Thus, this nanoplatform triggers both disulfidptosis and ferroptosis by depleting NADPH through glucose starvation. Similarly, Su et al. developed the BZTFH nanoplatform, which is designed to establish a metabolic negative feedback loop between ferroptosis and disulfidptosis [168]. These multifunctional BZTFH nanoparticles are capable of gradually releasing Fe²⁺, H₂O₂, and Zn²⁺ within the acidic TME. Specifically, the Fe²⁺ triggers the Fenton reaction, generating ROS that induce ferroptosis. In response, tumor cells may upregulate SLC7A11 to enhance their reductive capacity and resist ferroptosis. In such cases, the BZTFH system counteracts this adaptation by releasing Bay876, which inhibits glucose uptake and depletes NADPH, ultimately leading to disulfidptosis. Taking a step further, Zhou et al. addressed the metabolic heterogeneity within tumors by developing a system termed M@GOx/Fe-HMON [169]. This system moves beyond the conventional approach of indiscriminately inducing a single pathway of cell death, instead adapting dynamically to spatial gradients in glucose concentration within the TME. The platform is constructed using hollow mesoporous organosilica nanoparticles (HMON) loaded with GOx and an Fe2+/Fe3+ redox pair, and it is further coated with HCC cell membranes to enhance tumor targeting and immune evasion. Leveraging the heterogeneous distribution of glucose in HCC, the system initiates cascade reactions involving GOx and POD in regions rich in glucose, depleting GSH and generating substantial ROS to trigger ferroptosis. In regions deficient in glucose, however, the platform exacerbates glucose deprivation, further depleting NADPH and blocking cystine reduction. This action leads to the abnormal accumulation of disulfide bonds and ultimately triggers disulfidptosis via cytoskeletal collapse. Such an adaptive strategy significantly broadens the therapeutic window of interventions targeting metabolism. In integrated diagnosis and treatment, the GOx-IA@HMON@IO nanosystem was developed. This system, with excellent biocompatibility and therapeutic effects, offers multi-mechanism treatment for TNBC, combining ferroptosis, immune suppression reversal, and disulfidptosis, all guided by T₁-weighted MRI, showing great potential for diagnostic and therapeutic applications [170]. These approaches combine two cell death pathways into a single intelligent nanozyme system, demonstrating its potential as a dynamic metabolic adaptor for targeted cancer therapy.

While the aforementioned ‘active mechanistic participants’ show considerable promise for inducing disulfidptosis via their intrinsic physicochemical properties, tumor heterogeneity often severely impedes the consistent realization of their intrinsic therapeutic activity. To overcome this limitation, recent design strategies have shifted from developing intrinsically active materials toward constructing highly efficient delivery platforms. Precisely modulating the pharmacokinetics of nanocarriers can significantly enhance drug bioavailability at the tumor site while concurrently reducing nonspecific toxicity [171–173]. Addressing these challenges, Li et al. developed transformable human serum albumin nanocapsules, termed HGV, co-loaded with GOx and the ASCT2 inhibitor V9302 [174]. This approach exploits the compensatory upregulation of albumin uptake by tumor cells under metabolic stress, thereby facilitating efficient intracellular drug delivery and enabling a dual metabolic blockade of glucose and glutamine. Consequently, this process indirectly disrupts cellular redox homeostasis, leading to the efficient induction of disulfidptosis. Additionally, Huo et al. designed an acid-stable adhesive hydrogel (AG@G) for co-delivering allicin and GOx, which induces both disulfidptosis and ferroptosis for a synergistic anti-cancer effect [175]. Similarly, nanocarriers play an indispensable role in synergizing epigenetic interventions with metabolic therapies [176–179]. To overcome the limitations of the FTO m6A demethylase inhibitor meclofenamic acid (MA), specifically its poor bioavailability and inadequate tumor accumulation that prevent the maintenance of effective in vivo concentrations, Tian et al. developed spherical nucleic acids loaded with MA and functionalized with a PD-L1 targeting aptamer (SNAMA-apt) [180]. This delivery strategy endowed the system with active targeting capabilities and an optimized pharmacokinetic profile, while also exploiting the responsive cleavage of disulfide bonds within the nanocarrier backbone to deplete endogenous GSH. Overall, nanodelivery platforms exhibit significant advantages in achieving precise stoichiometric codelivery and suppressing unintended catalytic toxicity. These features make them highly suitable for integrating established small molecule inhibitors to reverse specific genetic or metabolic resistance. Conversely, active mechanistic participants, particularly nanozymes based on transition metals, possess unique potential for mediating localized and continuous cascade signal amplification. Natural enzymes are highly susceptible to denaturation within the complex tumor microenvironment, and this inherent structural vulnerability frequently restricts the practical therapeutic efficacy of polymeric nanoparticles loaded with glucose oxidase. By comparison, inorganic nanozymes provide robust catalytic stability. This durability allows them to continuously deplete reduced NADPH and GSH while concurrently avoiding the risk of premature drug leakage. Nevertheless, when evaluated against fully biodegradable polymeric or proteinaceous carriers, these active metallic platforms are subject to significantly more rigorous challenges regarding their long-term clearance kinetics in vivo and overall systemic biosafety.

Although the aforementioned nanomedicine platforms based on the disulfidptosis mechanism show promising potential for overcoming tumor drug resistance, therapeutic strategies targeting this emerging paradigm of cell death still face multidimensional challenges. First, the triggering of disulfidptosis is highly dependent on the cellular phenotype. This mechanism relies heavily on the high surface expression of SLC7A11 on tumor cells and the concurrent deprivation of glucose metabolism. However, significant heterogeneity exists in metabolic phenotypes and oxidative stress baselines across different tumor types, as well as within different evolutionary stages of the same tumor. Consequently, accurately screening patient subpopulations that are highly sensitive to disulfidptosis within the complex TME represents the primary translational barrier for this therapy at present. More challengingly, highly complex intratumoral heterogeneity introduces a significant risk of primary resistance against the complete eradication of tumor cells by nanomedicines. The aberrant vascular architecture within solid tumors creates spatial gradients in the distribution of glucose and oxygen. This architectural anomaly dictates that different subpopulations of cells within the same tumor face distinct metabolic pressures and exhibit heterogeneous expression levels of SLC7A11. Such spatial heterogeneity in the microenvironment and cellular phenotype makes it difficult for nanoplatforms utilizing global intervention strategies to achieve a uniform induction of disulfidptosis. Following the effective elimination of sensitive cell clones, resistant subclones residing in regions rich in glucose or those possessing an intrinsically low basal expression of SLC7A11 can survive and rapidly enrich, ultimately driving tumor relapse and therapeutic resistance. Second, existing nanoplatform designs often focus on global metabolic deprivation, and such nonspecific interference may induce unintended toxicity in normal tissues that are highly dependent on glucose. The systemic safety profile of these intervention strategies, therefore, warrants prolonged evaluation. Finally, the metabolic plasticity of tumors and the lack of in vivo monitoring tools complicate the clinical application of therapies targeting disulfidptosis. Under prolonged disulfide stress, tumor cells may evolve compensatory reduction mechanisms independent of NADPH, leading to acquired resistance. Concurrently, the current absence of molecular probes capable of specifically monitoring actin crosslinking or disulfide stress in real time makes it difficult to accurately assess the true in vivo efficacy of these nanomedicines.

Nanoparticles targeting anoikis for cancer therapy

Anoikis, a specialized form of RCD triggered by ECM detachment, serves as a critical biological barrier against tumor metastasis. However, aggressive malignant cells frequently acquire AR, enabling their survival in the systemic circulation and subsequent colonization of distant organs [181]. Because AR is a complex, network-driven process intricately linked to metabolic reprogramming and robust oxidative stress resistance, conventional single-target interventions often fail due to the activation of compensatory survival pathways. To overcome these challenges, recent advancements in nanomedicine have focused on engineering multifunctional nanoparticles to effectively re-sensitize tumor cells to anoikis. By precisely delivering pro-anoikis agents directly to CTCs or pre-metastatic niches, these sophisticated nanoplatforms can simultaneously disrupt multiple aberrant survival signals and synergistically inhibit metastatic initiation. Consequently, the application of nanotechnology transcends mere formulation improvement. It represents an indispensable strategy for remodeling anoikis sensitivity and dismantling the cancer metastatic cascade.

The progression of AR relies heavily on aberrant interactions between tumor cells and the ECM. A primary mechanism driving AR is the activation of a signaling axis that includes FN, integrin, and FAK [182–188]. To address this critical barrier, Wang et al. developed an ECM deprivation system (EDS) using self-assembling peptides that target FN, shifting the therapeutic paradigm from competitive inhibition to biophysical entrapment [189]. In this context, the nanosystem acts as an active mechanistic participant. EDS specifically recognizes FN, which is overexpressed in tumor tissues, and undergoes an FN-induced in situ transformation from nanoparticles into nanofiber networks with a β sheet conformation. The resulting encapsulating barrier prolongs matrix retention and physically disrupts integrin-mediated mechanotransduction, which blocks downstream FAK signaling and effectively reverses AR. Simultaneously, by alleviating cell adhesion-mediated drug resistance, EDS restores doxorubicin chemosensitivity in resistant cell lines. This dual strategy of physical deprivation and chemical cytotoxicity synergistically suppresses tumor growth and metastasis (Fig. 7). In a related approach highlighting nanomaterials as active effectors, Covarrubias et al. developed bio-reduced arsenic nanoparticles (AsNPs) capable of degrading collagen matrices within the ECM, forcibly detaching tumor cells from their physical anchorage. These nanoparticles further promote apoptosis by downregulating survival-promoting signaling pathways, such as Akt and ERK, while upregulating tumor suppressors like PTEN and p53. This coordinated modulation ultimately activates both intrinsic and extrinsic apoptotic pathways, enabling a seamless transition from matrix disruption to programmed cell death [190]. Addressing both the paucity of immunocompetent macrophages in the TME and the pathological dependence of tumor cells on matrix support [191–195], Zhao et al. engineered artificial nanomacrophages based on a metal-organic (ZIF-8) framework to overcome the limitations of conventional cell-based therapies [37, 196–198]. This active participant system exploits the acidic nature of the TME to trigger the responsive degradation of the ZIF-8 framework, releasing Zn²⁺ that function as artificial cytokines. These ions significantly downregulate the expression of key adhesion proteins, namely E-cadherin and integrin β1, severing both the physical scaffolding and survival signaling between tumor cells and the ECM. The consequent loss of adhesive homeostasis initiates an apoptotic cascade, culminating in an irreversible process that induces immunogenic anoikis. This mechanism effectively reverses the immunosuppressive microenvironment and overcomes tumor immune tolerance (Fig. 8).

While alterations in the interaction between tumor cells and the ECM represent the external defense against anoikis, the homeostatic remodeling of intracellular survival signaling circuits constitutes the core engine driving malignant progression. Currently, multiple lines of research utilize nanotechnology to delve into the intracellular milieu. In these approaches, nanoparticles primarily serve as sophisticated delivery carriers rather than active mechanistic participants, precisely modulating survival circuits to reverse AR at its source. In TNBC, PTPN14 has been identified as a key regulator that induces anoikis [199]. Intratumoral delivery of PTPN14 mRNA using lipid nanoparticles dephosphorylates BCAR3, which subsequently inhibits downstream PI3K/AKT and ERK survival cascades. This function-restoring strategy circumvents the acquired resistance typically associated with traditional kinase inhibitors while significantly curtailing both primary tumor expansion and metastatic relapse. In the context of bone metastatic prostate cancer, Dong and colleagues focused on the aberrantly upregulated structural protein TUBB3, engineering bone-targeting ionizable lipid nanoparticles for TUBB3 siRNA delivery (BT-LNPs) [200]. Silencing this gene disrupts the integrin/FAK/Src axis alongside its connection to downstream survival-promoting pathways, effectively blocking the colonization and progression of tumor cells within the bone microenvironment. Furthermore, nanoscale interventions targeting the transcription factor ZNF304 represent a strategy that intercepts survival signals at their genetic origin. By preventing ZNF304 from enhancing the transcription of the integrin β1 promoter, ovarian cancer cells are resensitized to anoikis [201]. This paradigm shift from obstructing extracellular anchorage to reprogramming intracellular signals utilizes nanocarriers to intervene at core genetic targets, offering a profound therapeutic perspective for reversing tumor metastasis.

Other strategies employ complex multifunctional biomimetic nanoplatforms to restore anoikis sensitivity through the induction of redox imbalance [75, 202]. Liu et al. developed a biomimetic nanoplatform targeting AR by encapsulating copper(II)-incorporated dendritic mesoporous silica nanocarriers (DMSN-Cu@GOx/PEG) within thermosensitive Pluronic F127 hydrogel (designated H@DMSN-Cu@GOx/PEG) [203]. In this design, the nanostructure functions as a highly active mechanistic participant. This system leverages a cascade catalytic reaction to generate explosive oxidative stress, which directly induces mitochondrial dysfunction to trigger apoptosis while alleviating the hypoxic TME through enzyme-mimetic activities. By downregulating HIF-1α, it suppresses the epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT), thereby attenuating the AR of bladder cancer cells at the molecular level. This dual strategy combining physical obstruction with biochemical sensitization offers a promising approach for post-surgical antirecurrence therapy. However, further investigation remains necessary to elucidate the distribution kinetics of redox intermediates under complex physiological conditions and the precise regulatory mechanisms underlying anoikis pathway modulation (Table 3).

Despite the considerable potential of nanotechnology-based interventions in reversing AR in tumor cells, their clinical translation remains constrained by several challenges. First, targeted intervention in dynamic CTCs is inherently difficult. Most current nanodelivery systems rely on the EPR effect in solid tumors, rendering them ineffective at capturing and targeting rare, fast-moving CTCs under complex hemodynamic conditions. Second, AR operates fundamentally as a multinodal and dynamically evolving network. Nanotherapeutic agents targeting a single pathway are prone to trigger compensatory survival mechanisms within the TME, leading to progressive attenuation of anoikis-inducing efficacy. Third, existing nanostrategies face issues of targeting heterogeneity and potential off-target toxicity. Although several cutting-edge studies aim to induce anoikis by targeting specific ECM components such as FN, these molecules also play essential roles in maintaining normal tissue adhesion and facilitating wound healing. Without precise discrimination between tumor-associated and physiological FN, nanosystems may elicit off-target effects, warranting comprehensive evaluation of their long-term systemic toxicity. Finally, certain nanoplatforms physically block interactions between tumor cells and the ECM through steric hindrance. Such nonselective physical blockade may inadvertently impede the infiltration of effector immune cells into tumor tissues, thereby undermining antitumor immune responses, an adverse outcome that merits careful consideration. To overcome these obstacles, a comparative evaluation of the impact of distinct nanoplatform designs on anoikis is essential. Nanoparticles functioning as biological carriers, such as lipid nanoparticles, offer the distinct advantage of modulating specific intracellular signaling hubs with high molecular targeting precision. However, these systems universally encounter therapeutic bottlenecks primarily caused by endosomal entrapment and rapid systemic clearance. In contrast, nanoplatforms operating as active mechanistic participants, including self-assembling peptides and catalytic frameworks, actively remodel the ECM microenvironment and induce a profound physical matrix deprivation effect. Although these active materials can directly trigger the anoikis cascade independently of complex genetic regulatory networks, their clinical translation remains constrained by the potential risk of off-target tissue adhesion disruption and the toxicity concerns associated with long-term material retention. Ultimately, integrating the targeting precision of carrier vehicles with the robust microenvironmental modulation capabilities of active materials to engineer hybrid systems with dual mechanisms may define the new frontier in anoikis based metastatic therapies.

Nanoparticles targeting PANoptosis for cancer therapy

PANoptosis, a highly coordinated inflammatory RCD pathway, holds significant promise for overcoming tumor drug resistance and subsequently driving ICD. However, early induction strategies relying on cytokine combinations or small-molecule drugs frequently lack tumor specificity, leading to uncontrolled systemic adverse effects. Conversely, nanoscale delivery systems offer distinct advantages by operating in two fundamentally different roles: as passive structural carriers and as active mechanistic participants. Primarily, when acting as nanocarriers, these systems can localize the potent PANoptotic process to the tumor site through the EPR effect, active targeting modifications, and TME responsive release mechanisms. This carrier role broadens the therapeutic window and ensures systemic safety. Furthermore, the assembly and activation of the PANoptosome depend heavily on complex spatiotemporal crosstalk among diverse molecular pathways, making it challenging for conventional single agent administration to achieve synchronized multiple target modulation. Leveraging their superior concurrent delivery capacity, nanoplatforms acting as passive carriers enable the spatiotemporally synchronized release of various inducers within target cells. Beyond simple delivery, certain nanomaterials utilize their intrinsic physicochemical properties to directly catalyze or engage in biological cascades, thereby acting as active mechanistic participants. Therefore, the application of nanotechnology represents an indispensable core strategy for safely and controllably inducing PANoptosis in situ within tumors through both payload delivery and active biological engagement.

Intracellular organelle communication remains critical for maintaining cellular homeostasis, rendering it a potent target for therapeutic intervention [204]. The disruption of these communication networks can trigger a lethal cascade. Highlighting the role of nanomaterials as active mechanistic participants, Qi and colleagues developed an interception platform targeting the infrastructure of tumor organelle communication, utilizing multi-hydroxylated fullerenols (MFs) in combination with an mTOR inhibitor to dismantle the survival foundation of cancer cells. Rather than acting solely as a carrier, this platform achieves the physical disruption of the lysosomal membrane by precisely modulating the hydroxyl density on the MFs themselves. This intrinsic material reactivity overcomes the limitations of uncontrollable trajectories associated with conventional ROS mediated diffuse damage, thereby inducing cascade damage to the mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) driven by the leakage of Ca2+ and cathepsins. By integrating the prior activation of autophagic flux via mTOR inhibition with a terminal degradation blockade mediated by lysosomal damage, this system effectively severs autonomous repair pathways, resulting in the substantial accumulation of ineffective autolysosomes. This systematic collapse, based on the axis connecting the lysosome-ER-mitochondria, specifically induces PANoptosis in cancer cells by driving the concurrent activation of the executioner proteins GSDMD, Caspase-3, and MLKL. Through pharmacological antagonism using BAPTA and CA-074 alongside genetic validation via mTOR knockout, the study elucidated the functional dependencies of this cascade response. Consequently, this provides a novel strategy for highly efficient and precise antitumor therapy through the dismantling of subcellular communication networks using nanomaterials as active physical disruptors (Fig. 9) [205].

Recent studies have revealed that PANoptosis induced by oncolytic viruses (OVs) plays a critical role in remodeling the TME [206]. Wang et al. constructed a synergistic logic framework that integrates signal initiation and effector amplification by combining the innate immune activation triggered by the oncolytic herpes simplex virus (oHSV) with the regulatory effect of Fusobacterium nucleatum-derived outer membrane vesicles (Fn-OMVs) on effector protein stability [207]. In this context, these biological nanoparticles function dually as delivery vectors and active pathway participants. The oHSV promotes the accumulation of endogenous Z-RNA by upregulating the expression of interferon-stimulated genes, thereby activating ZBP1-driven PANoptosis. Concurrently, the Fn-OMVs inhibit the ubiquitination-mediated degradation of the execution proteins GSDMD, GSDME, and MLKL, establishing a positive feedback loop at the protein level. This coordinated mechanism effectively converts immunologically quiescent tumors into inflamed environments and significantly enhances the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. CRISPR/Cas9 based validation demonstrated that the knockout of the sensor ZBP1 abolishes the antitumor effect, whereas the deletion of individual executioners such as GSDMD, GSDME, or MLKL results only in partial inhibition. This logic of sensor absolute dependency and executor path redundancy confirms the intrinsic nature of PANoptosis as a multiple pathway integrated cell death modality.

Essential metal ions are key regulators of cell fate, and their targeted dysregulation offers a promising therapeutic strategy [208]. Although individual ions such as Cu²⁺ or Zn²⁺ typically induce a singular form of cell death, their combination can promote the assembly of the AIM2-PANoptosome. Hou et al. developed hyaluronic acid-functionalized Zn-CuO₂ nanoparticles (HZCO NPs) that perfectly illustrate the transition from passive carriers to active mechanistic participants [209]. These nanoparticles exploit the high chelation affinity of Cu²⁺ for metallothioneins. By competitively displacing endogenous Zn²⁺ and concurrently releasing exogenous Zn²⁺, these nanoparticles induce catastrophic zinc overload in tumor cells. This severe ionic dysregulation cascades into oxidative stress, mitochondrial and ER stress, and the release of dsDNA. This release subsequently activates the cGAS/STING alongside the AIM2 pathways, ultimately driving AIM2-PANoptosome assembly and PANoptosis. The functional dependency of this process was confirmed through AIM2 gene silencing and pharmacological blockade, establishing that the observed effect is not merely a secondary outcome of a single pathway. This study demonstrates that metal ion nanocarriers can trigger multiple pathway coordinated cell death via extreme ionic homeostasis disruption, thereby effectively overcoming tumor resistance to single pathway therapeutic interventions (Fig. 10; Table 4).

Despite the considerable advantages of nanomaterial-mediated PANoptosis induction in remodeling the TME and overcoming therapeutic resistance, its clinical translation remains hindered by biosafety concerns and systemic toxicity arising from the associated potent inflammatory response. As a highly proinflammatory form of PCD, the activation of PANoptosis is inevitably accompanied by the explosive release of cellular contents, DAMPs, and a cascade of proinflammatory cytokines. Without stringent spatiotemporal targeting, the extratumoral accumulation of nanodelivery systems can readily trigger systemic inflammatory response syndrome or even a fatal cytokine storm. Furthermore, certain nanocarriers with slow biodegradation kinetics may persist in vivo and synergistically amplify the immunological cascade induced by PANoptosis, resulting in compounded toxicological effects that exacerbate the metabolic burden on the liver and kidneys. To optimize future translational designs, it is essential to compare the efficacy and safety profiles of different nanoplatforms based on their fundamental roles. Purely carrier based nanoplatforms, such as inert polymeric micelles or liposomes delivering small molecules, excel in precisely controlling pharmacokinetics and minimizing off target toxicity. However, their therapeutic efficacy heavily depends on successful intracellular payload release and the inherent sensitivity of the tumor cells. Conversely, nanoplatforms functioning as active mechanistic participants, including the aforementioned MFs and HZCO NPs, demonstrate superior potency. They directly dismantle subcellular organelles or trigger extreme ionic storms to forcefully overcome inherent drug resistance mechanisms. While these active platforms achieve more robust PANoptosis induction, their intrinsic physicochemical reactivity carries a distinctively higher risk of unpredictable long term metabolic consequences and broader off target tissue damage. Lastly, substantial heterogeneity in tumor genetic backgrounds and cellular phenotypes, particularly the variable expression levels of core PANoptosome sensors, poses a fundamental biological barrier that must be addressed before either of these nanotherapeutic strategies can achieve precise and individualized clinical intervention.

Nanoparticles targeting disulfidptosis, anoikis, and PANoptosis for combination therapy

Combination Chemotherapy

Despite decades of clinical use, conventional chemotherapy remains hindered by nonspecific distribution, severe systemic toxicity, poor solubility, and the emergence of MDR. These limitations significantly compromise therapeutic efficacy, failing to adequately suppress tumor growth, metastasis, and recurrence. Consequently, integrating nanomedicine with chemotherapy has emerged as a promising strategy to overcome these barriers and enhance treatment outcomes [50, 210].

One critical challenge in metastatic cancer is the intrinsic resistance to chemotherapeutics observed in CTCs. For instance, metastatic NSCLC frequently exhibits resistance to paclitaxel. To address this, Pramual et al. developed paclitaxel-loaded PLGA-lipid hybrid nanoparticles specifically designed to target suspended lung cancer cells. This nanocarrier improved biocompatibility and enhanced intracellular drug accumulation, effectively overcoming AR and restoring sensitivity to paclitaxel [211]. Beyond overcoming resistance, nanocarriers facilitate the co-delivery of agents to induce distinct cell death pathways. Song et al. engineered a biocompatible carrier using hydrophilic sodium alginate and hydrophobic cholesterol to co-deliver metformin and doxorubicin to melanoma tumors. The study demonstrated that this dual-delivery system exerted potent antitumor effects by triggering PANoptosis, highlighting the potential of nanomedicine to activate complex cell death mechanisms [212]. While the aforementioned strategies rely largely on passive targeting via the EPR effect, functionalizing nanocarriers with ligands enables active targeting of metastatic populations. Han et al. engineered a multifunctional, pH-responsive nanotheranostic system (Cis@MFPPC) that co-delivers cisplatin and a peptide mimic of the IL-8 binding domain to target osteosarcoma [213]. Notably, as explicitly corroborated by the in vivo bioluminescence and fluorescence microscopy imaging in Fig. 11, this peptide mimic effectively and competitively binds to the CXCR1 receptors on CTCs. By antagonizing the IL-8/CXCR1/Akt signaling axis, this targeted blockade actively dismantles AR and significantly suppresses distant metastasis. Additionally, the system utilized polyacrylic acid for pH-triggered cisplatin release within the acidic TME and incorporated magnetic Fe3O4 and Cy7 for dual-modal MRI and fluorescence imaging, enabling simultaneous therapeutic intervention and in vivo tracking (Fig. 11).

Combination radiotherapy

Radiotherapy remains a fundamental modality in oncology [214]. However, its therapeutic index is frequently compromised by tumor hypoxia, poor X-ray absorption, and an immunosuppressive TME. To overcome these resistance mechanisms, recent research has focused on nanoparticle-based platforms that not only sensitize tumors to radiation but also activate complex cell death pathways [215].

Within the framework of combination therapy, nanomaterial-based strategies targeting PANoptosis hold promise for overcoming the inherent resistance associated with conventional treatments by remodeling the immunosuppressive TME. Zhang and colleagues developed a covalent organic framework responsive to dual stimuli incorporating radiosensitive diselenide bonds for the precision delivery of the small molecule drug CBL0137 (PorSe-CuPt@CBL) [216]. As illustrated in Fig. 12, this system reverses hypoxia-induced radioresistance, enhances radiodynamic effects, and enables controlled, on-demand drug release specifically within tumor sites. Distinct from conventional DNA damage mechanisms, this nanoplatform effectively blocks the repair of damaged DNA into the canonical B-DNA conformation, thereby promoting the abundant formation of highly immunogenic Z-DNA. This process lays the groundwork for activating key intracellular sensors. Moreover, genetic experiments confirm that the antitumor effects of radiotherapy mediated by this nanoplatform are highly dependent on the ZBP1-driven PANoptosis pathway. The resulting cascade, from the induction of PANoptosis to the remodeling of the immunosuppressive TME, establishes a positive biological feedback loop that fundamentally overcomes tumor resistance to radiotherapy and significantly enhances the overall efficacy of combination therapy (Fig. 12). In a distinct metabolic strategy, Zhen et al. utilized a mitochondria-targeting metal-organic framework (Th-Ir-DBB/Dig) to deplete intracellular cholesterol and glucose [217]. This metabolic interference facilitates disulfidptosis while simultaneously downregulating immune checkpoints, thereby amplifying the antitumor immune response in conjunction with radiotherapy.

Combination Immunotherapy

Although ICB has revolutionized the landscape of cancer treatment as a vital complement to traditional therapies, its clinical efficacy remains restricted in many cases. The intense immunosuppressive characteristics of the TME and the lack of effective tumor antigen presentation hinder the conversion of most solid tumors from an immunologically “cold” to a “hot” phenotype, thereby limiting overall response rates. To address this challenge, the combination of nanomaterial-induced ICD with ICB therapy offers a promising strategy to overcome tumor immune tolerance by targeting multiple tumor-associated pathways to exert synergistic effects.

Emerging evidence suggests that inducing disulfidptosis can effectively remodel the metabolic and immune landscapes of tumors. As discussed regarding the SPCP/CCP@Bay nanosystem [218], metabolic modulation can have profound immunological consequences. Wang et al. demonstrated that the disulfidptosis induced by this system not only remodels the metabolic environment but also downregulates PD-L1 expression. Concurrently, the induction of ER stress and the release of DAMPs facilitate dendritic cell maturation and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration. This effectively reverses the immunosuppressive microenvironment, enabling the combination of SPCP/CCP@Bay and αPD-1 antibody to elicit systemic immune memory and inhibit distant metastasis.

Yun et al. engineered an ultra-pH-sensitive nanoplatform, Elesclomol-Cu@PDPA (PEC NPs), designed to therapeutically couple cuproptosis with PANoptosis for combination therapies. As evidenced by the morphological analysis in Fig. 13A, CT26 colon cancer cells treated with PEC NPs exhibited pronounced membrane bubbling and giant blebbing, which are quintessential hallmarks of pyroptosis. This phenotypic transition was further validated by the molecular data in Fig. 13B, where western blot analysis revealed a significant increase in the expression of PANoptosis-related key executive proteins. When integrated with αPD-1, the PEC NPs robustly amplified the secretion of critical pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-1β. This strategy achieved a tumor inhibition rate of 96.0% and achieved complete tumor regression in 40% of mice in a colon cancer model. Such results underscore the clinical potential of utilizing nanoparticle-mediated PANoptosis to dismantle the immunosuppressive barriers of the tumor niche, providing a highly efficient template for next-generation combination immunotherapy (Fig. 13) [219].

Combination phototherapy

Phototherapy harnesses light of specific wavelengths to generate therapeutic effects within target tissues by converting optical energy into thermal energy or biochemical reactions [220]. Additionally, optical energy can be converted into acoustic signals for diagnostic purposes (photoacoustic imaging, PAI). While PTT, PAI, and PDT have emerged as valuable approaches for cancer diagnosis and treatment, they are often hindered by limited tumor accumulation and inherent resistance mechanisms. Consequently, recent research has focused on utilizing functionalized nanocarriers to overcome these barriers and specifically trigger alternative cell death pathways, including disulfidptosis, anoikis, and PANoptosis.

The clinical utility of photosensitizers like Chlorin e6 (Ce6) is often restricted by the dense ECM and the hypoxic nature of the TME. To address this, Mohanty et al. engineered a hybrid lipid-coated prodrug nanomodulator, Pro@FLNC, which leverages the TME’s unique chemistry to induce disulfidptosis [221]. Upon entering the tumor, high GSH concentrations cleave the disulfide bonds within the nanocarrier, releasing Ce6 and ursolic acid (UA). This reaction depletes intracellular GSH, which serves as the primary antioxidant defense of the tumor, while UA could ameliorate hypoxia to potentially enhance Ce6-mediated ROS generation. The resulting severe redox imbalance forces an upregulation of SLC7A11 expression, triggering disulfidptosis and subsequently activating immunogenic pyroptosis. Other strategies focus on overcoming metastasis via anoikis. Zhang et al. developed dual-responsive RCC/LND NPs to co-deliver Ce6 and lonidamine (LND) directly to mitochondria [222]. By inhibiting mitochondrial respiration and disrupting ECM components, this system suppresses HIF-1α and AR factors. The combination of oxidative stress and metabolic disruption activates both intrinsic and extrinsic apoptotic pathways associated with anoikis. In a related study, Zhang et al. utilized porphyrin-18-nanoconfined liposomes (P18⊂L) to mechanically compromise the ECM [223]. By degrading collagen components, P18⊂L reduces the adhesion capabilities of paclitaxel-resistant cells, sensitizing them to both anoikis- and PDT-induced apoptosis. Furthermore, Ma et al. demonstrated that integrating PDT with caspase activation can trigger PANoptosis [224]. Their iRGD-conjugated liposome (CJP-TiN) facilitates the targeted delivery of Ce6 and Jolkinolide B (JB). Upon irradiation, the ROS generated by Ce6 synergizes with JB-mediated caspase-8 activation to induce PANoptosis, eliciting a robust systemic immune response.

Photothermal therapy (PTT) employs photothermal transduction agents (PTAs) to induce localized hyperthermia; however, its therapeutic efficacy is frequently hindered by cellular thermoresistance, such as heat shock protein overexpression, and suboptimal photothermal conversion efficiency (PCE). To circumvent these barriers, Zhu et al. engineered Pd2Sn@GOx-SP nanorods, an innovative intermetallic platform integrating robust photothermal properties with multienzyme-mimetic activities [225]. Upon near-infrared irradiation, these nanorods drive cascade biocatalytic reactions that concurrently trigger a massive ROS storm and severe glucose depletion. This dual metabolic and thermal stress synchronously triggers pyroptosis and disulfidptosis. Crucially, the inherent optical and X-ray attenuation properties of the Pd2Sn core enable advanced theranostic applications. As demonstrated in the Fig. 14K and L, intravenous and intratumoral injections of Pd2Sn@GOx-SP in tumor-bearing mice produced highly distinct photoacoustic and CT signals, respectively, validating their robust capacity as contrast agents for precise, image-guided synergistic therapy (Fig. 14). To address the limitations of conventional PTT, Wang et al. engineered mesoporous silica nanospheres with a cerium oxide (CeO2) core, serving as a dual catalase and POD mimic [38]. By coating this catalytic core with the conjugated polymer PCPDTBT to form MSN-Ce@SP/PEG, the authors achieved a synergistic enhancement of enzymatic activity and photothermal conversion upon laser irradiation. This dual-modal stress effectively disrupts the EMT process, thereby inhibiting AR in tumor cells. Distinct from morphological optimization strategies, Lin et al. employed a “hydrophobic core densification” approach to develop MPF@IR780 nanoparticles [226]. This design converts the aggregation-caused quenching effect of the encapsulated photosensitizer (IR780) into enhanced heat generation, significantly boosting the PCE from 8% to 23%. Upon irradiation, MPF@IR780 triggers PANoptosis and ferroptosis, achieving potent antitumor efficacy through the synergy of PTT and CDT. To further overcome physical barriers in the TME, Zhang et al. constructed Janus-type HGPP nanomotors powered by dual chemical (H2S) and photothermal propulsion [227]. This active transport mechanism significantly improves autonomous motion and deep tumor penetration. Concurrently, the release of H2S and GOx activity disrupts metabolic symbiosis and depletes glucose, driving the tumor cells into disulfidptosis. Finally, hybrid nanomaterials are increasingly engineered to leverage the high contrast of optical imaging with the deep tissue penetration of ultrasound for PAI-guided theranostics [220, 228]. For instance, Lee et al. developed CEACAM6-targeted nanoparticles loaded with indocyanine green (ICG; I@C6-NPs) to serve as PAI contrast agents [229]. By specifically accumulating in tumor tissues, I@C6-NPs enhance photoacoustic signals, enabling the high-sensitivity detection of early-stage metastases and AR cell populations.

Combination ultrasound therapy

SDT is an emerging non-invasive modality that exploits the deep tissue penetration of ultrasound (US) to treat deep-seated tumors [230, 231]. SDT is thought to depend on the synergistic interaction between US and sonosensitizers, which encompass materials from nanoparticles to organic compounds, to convert acoustic energy into ROS, inducing tumor cell death [232]. Despite its potential, clinical translation is currently hindered by the limited efficacy of sonosensitizers, tissue hypoxia, and the immunosuppressive nature of the TME [233]. To overcome these barriers, recent research has focused on engineering multifunctional nanoplatforms that combine SDT with metabolic or immune modulation to trigger novel cell death pathways. A primary strategy involves leveraging metabolic vulnerabilities to induce disulfidptosis. Xu et al. developed the CYBC nanoplatform, which employs an analogous mechanism of delivering exogenous cystine and inhibiting glucose uptake to metabolically reprogram tumor cells. Their study demonstrated that the actin cytoskeleton collapse characteristic of disulfidptosis facilitates the release of DAMPs. This process triggers ICD, promoting dendritic cell maturation, M1 macrophage polarization, and cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, effectively reconstructing the TME metabolic landscape [234]. Additionally, Chen et al. utilized a phase-change strategy with ICG@C3F8-KL nanobubbles [235]. Upon US irradiation, these bubbles rupture to release the encapsulated Glut1 inhibitor, synergizing with ROS generated by ICG to trigger disulfidptosis. Beyond metabolic disruption, recent advancements have utilized SDT to induce PANoptosis. He et al. designed a bismuth-based ternary heterojunction, Bi@Bi2O3-Pt-PEG (BBOP), which integrates Z-scheme heterojunctions and localized surface plasmon resonance to enhance sonocatalytic activity [236]. By combining the catalase-like activity of platinum nanoparticles with the GSH-depleting capacity of Bi³⁺ ions, BBOP amplifies ROS generation to trigger PANoptosis and inhibit metastasis. However, the inclusion of heavy metals in heterojunctions raises potential biosafety concerns [237, 238]. Addressing this, Zhong et al. developed a nanocatalyst based on hafnium oxide (HfO₂), a material with established clinical safety [239]. Through transition metal doping, they optimized the sonopiezoelectric and multi-enzyme mimetic activities of HfO₂, enabling efficient ROS generation and PANoptosis induction without the toxicity associated with traditional heavy metals. Furthermore, a sophisticated ultrasound-responsive nanoplatform based on nano- and genetically engineered extracellular vesicles (PLPE) has been developed to initiate PANoptosis, which subsequently triggers ICD and sustains the cancer-immunity cycle [240]. Upon US stimulation, PLPE drives PANoptosis through a synergistic triple catalysis mechanism comprising biological catalysis via loaded GSDMD protein to induce pyroptosis, chemical catalysis via sonosensitizer (PpIX)-mediated ROS generation to trigger apoptosis, and physical catalysis via phase-change perfluorohexane cavitation to initiate necroptosis. The successful execution of this synchronized RCD is visibly corroborated by confocal laser scanning microscopy and bright-field imaging. This robust induction of PANoptosis facilitates the massive release of DAMPs, which effectively reactivates the cancer-immunity cycle to overcome the immunosuppressive TME (Fig. 15).

Conclusions and perspectives

The discovery of new RCD pathways, including disulfidptosis, anoikis, and PANoptosis, may present promising opportunities for cancer treatment, particularly when combined with the flexible delivery capabilities of nanotechnology. Although nanoparticle-based approaches have shown significant promise in preclinical models for selectively triggering these cell death pathways, several key challenges remain before these strategies can be translated into clinical use. These challenges involve ensuring biological specificity, overcoming physiological barriers, and addressing immunological safety, all of which require interdisciplinary advancements in materials science, molecular biology, and immunology.

Regarding disulfidptosis, current nanoparticle-based interventions face several unresolved experimental challenges that may hinder further clinical translation. The first potential hurdle is achieving precise tumor targeting in the context of heterogeneous SLC7A11 expression. Currently, conventional single-ligand nanocarriers often struggle to completely eradicate tumors, as intra-tumoral spatial variability may allow SLC7A11-low subpopulations to escape therapy. Instead of relying on static targeting, a promising avenue for prospective studies involves exploring dynamic, adaptive recognition systems tailored to the TME. Researchers could explore multistage transformable nanoparticles. For instance, nanocarriers equipped with TME-responsive cleavable outer shells could be designed to expose secondary multi-ligand targeting systems. This approach would potentially facilitate the selective internalization of disulfide stress-inducing payloads by the specific SLC7A11-overexpressing cell populations, thereby mitigating heterogeneous therapeutic resistance. A second critical unresolved issue relates to the difficulty of maintaining a localized and sustained glucose depletion within the complex in vivo TME. Although recent pioneering studies have successfully constructed nanosystems co-delivering GOx and GLUT inhibitors to induce disulfidptosis, achieving long-term metabolic blockade in heavily vascularized tumors without premature drug leakage or off-target effects remains experimentally challenging. Rapid in vivo clearance and compensatory metabolic pathways can still compromise efficacy. Future research could consider focusing on spatiotemporally controlled metabolic disruption rather than simple co-delivery. It could be advantageous to integrate these GOx/GLUT-inhibitor systems into stimuli-responsive local hydrogel implants or employ biomimetic cell-membrane coatings. Such designs are anticipated to prolong tumor retention and offer a more controlled exhaustion of intracellular glucose, precisely triggering the NADP+/NADPH imbalance necessary for aberrant F-actin cross-linking, while minimizing systemic toxicity. Finally, the experimental validation of disulfidptosis is often confounded by its extensive crosstalk with other ROS-driven cell death pathways. Many existing ROS-generating nanomedicines simultaneously trigger apoptosis, ferroptosis, and disulfidptosis, making it experimentally challenging to isolate and confirm the specific contribution of disulfidptosis in vivo. Future experimental frameworks would likely benefit from decoupling these interconnected pathways to establish more definitive structure-activity relationships for nanomaterials. As a pathway-specific resolution, future studies might focus on developing theranostic nanoprobes integrated with specific built-in reporters that fluoresce exclusively in response to aberrant disulfide bond accumulation or F-actin collapse. Additionally, utilizing these advanced nanocarriers to co-deliver specific pathway inhibitors or CRISPR/Cas9 systems targeting actin-regulatory proteins could serve as a robust experimental control. This would help researchers rule out concurrent cell death mechanisms and confirm the targeted pathway activation.

Although targeting anoikis presents a promising anti-metastatic strategy, current experimental nanotechnology still faces several unresolved challenges. Primarily, capturing CTCs and micrometastases remains a profound hurdle. Conventional nanoparticles often suffer from rapid reticuloendothelial system clearance and frequently lack the spatiotemporal precision needed to intercept these rare, highly dynamic cells before they form protective microthrombi or adhere to new metastatic niches. Furthermore, within solid tumors, the dense desmoplastic stroma not only physically impedes nanoparticle penetration but inherently provides biochemical cues that confer intrinsic AR. To effectively intercept CTCs, future studies could explore biomimetic nanosystems to prolong circulation and co-navigate with CTCs in the blood compartment. To overcome the TME barrier and sensitize cells to anoikis, next-generation nanoparticles could be designed to actively disrupt cell-ECM attachments. Strategies utilizing size-tunable particles or the co-delivery of matrix-degrading enzymes may help to physically detach cancer cells from their protective niches while simultaneously delivering anoikis-inducing agents.

While the capacity of PANoptosis to trigger ICD offers a promising avenue for reversing tumor immunosuppression, current basic research often underestimates the severe safety liabilities associated with its highly inflammatory nature. The robust release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and DAMPs can precipitate lethal systemic toxicities, including cytokine release syndrome, autoimmune reactions, and multi-organ dysfunction. Therefore, striking a delicate balance between potent localized immune activation and systemic safety remains a paramount challenge for clinical translation. To address these critical gaps, it is recommended that future experimental designs incorporate comprehensive toxicological and immunological profiling using immunocompetent or humanized in vivo models to map long-term systemic immune dynamics. This approach, ideally coupled with advanced imaging modalities to track real-time PANoptosome assembly and post-treatment cytokine cascades, could provide crucial insights to guide the development of pathway-specific nanotechnological solutions. To mitigate systemic toxicity, nanoparticles should be engineered with stringent spatiotemporal control. For instance, multi-stimuli-responsive systems triggered by tumor hallmarks or exogenous stimuli could restrict the activation of upstream sensors and the core complex strictly to the TME. Furthermore, utilizing active targeting strategies are advised to ensure precise intracellular delivery, thereby minimizing collateral PANoptosis and related cell death pathways in healthy tissues.

Beyond the specific challenges associated with each pathway, broader translational issues also need to be addressed. Tumor heterogeneity calls for a shift toward personalized nanomedicine, where nanoparticle design is customized to individual patient profiles, including SLC7A11 expression, adhesion signaling, and inflammatory status. Additionally, scalable synthesis, batch consistency, long-term stability, and biocompatibility are essential for clinical application. The integration of multi-omics data with advanced nanoparticle platforms could allow real-time adaptation to changing tumor characteristics.

In conclusion, while nanoparticles present a promising approach for leveraging disulfidptosis, anoikis, and PANoptosis in cancer therapy, their success depends on overcoming significant biological and technological challenges. Future research should prioritize the development of multifunctional, adaptive nanosystems that combine targeting, monitoring, and therapeutic capabilities. With continued innovation, nanomaterial-driven induction of these novel cell death pathways could revolutionize precision oncology.
