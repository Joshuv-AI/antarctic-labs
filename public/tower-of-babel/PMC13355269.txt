Staged transurethral surgery for complete stone clearance in a rare giant infected ureteral stone: a case report
Authors: Xinguang Wang, Nian Zhang, Xin Liu, Xinxin Zhang, Lei Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGiant ureteral calculi are uncommon but pose significant management challenges. When associated with infection, they heighten the risk of urosepsis and complicate surgical planning, as conventional single-stage procedures may be hazardous. Optimal treatment for this rare combination remains undefined. We present a case of a giant infection-related ureteral stone successfully managed through a planned staged endourological strategy, achieving complete clearance and preservation of renal function. This report highlights a tailored approach providing a practical staging algorithm based on stone size (9 cm), infection status (Escherichia coli infection), and anatomical factors (stricture).Case DescriptionA 75-year-old female presented with left lower abdominal pain and was found to have a giant left ureteral calculus (maximum diameter of approximately 9 cm) causing hydronephrosis, with urine culture positive for Gram-negative bacilli. Given the large stone burden and active infection, a staged ureteroscopic strategy was employed to achieve complete clearance while minimizing septic risk. Three sequential procedures were performed at 3-week intervals: initial laser lithotripsy and stenting to establish drainage, followed by a time-limited second-stage for definitive stone extraction, and a final flexible ureterorenoscopy to clear residual fragments. At one-year follow-up, the patient was stone-free with preserved renal function.ConclusionsThis case demonstrates that a staged ureteroscopic strategy is safe and effective for rare, infectious giant ureteral calculi. It achieves initial infection control and renal preservation, followed by complete stone clearance while minimizing the risk of urosepsis. This approach offers a strategic solution for this complex clinical scenario.

Introduction

Urolithiasis represents a pervasive global health concern, with ureteral stones constituting a significant source of morbidity due to their propensity for obstruction, pain, and infection (1). Contemporary endourological techniques, including ureteroscopy (URS) and percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL), have greatly advanced management, offering high success rates for most calculi. However, a distinct and formidable clinical challenge arises in the rare instance of a giant ureteral stone (2,3). Here, the surgeon faces a perilous intersection of two critical imperatives: the need for definitive, complete stone clearance to prevent recurrence and preserve renal function, and the overriding obligation to avoid iatrogenic, life-threatening urosepsis—a known catastrophic complication of prolonged manipulation in an infected, obstructed system (4).

This inherent conflict exposes a significant gap in urological guidelines, which lack a standardized approach for this rare but high-stakes scenario. A single-stage, aggressive pursuit of clearance, while technically conceivable, may constitute an unacceptable gamble with patient safety (5,6). It necessitates a strategic pivot towards a management philosophy that prioritizes physiological stabilization and risk mitigation as the foundation for ultimate therapeutic success.

We present a paradigmatic case of a giant, obstructive stone with clear infectious etiology. This report details the rationale and execution of a planned, multi-stage surgical strategy. Our approach was fundamentally guided by the principle of controlled decompression and sepsis prophylaxis, followed by definitive stone eradication. This case underscores that in the face of complex infectious stone disease, procedural staging is not a concession but a deliberate and necessary recalibration of priorities to safely achieve the dual endpoints of renal salvage and stone-free status (7). To further clarify the specific clinical contribution of this case: (I) it offers a concrete decision framework with operative thresholds (stone burden 9 cm, active infection, and coexisting distal stricture) that can guide surgeons in selecting patients for a staged strategy; (II) it provides a detailed analysis of a post-operative systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) event, showing that even when systemic inflammation occurs in a staged setting, progression to septic shock can be avoided, thus reinforcing the argument for risk mitigation; and (III) it directly compares the staged ureteroscopic approach with PCNL and open surgery, explaining why the former is safer and more tailored for giant distal ureteral stones with active infection. These practical insights extend beyond a mere descriptive report and offer generalizable guidance for similarly complex cases. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://tau.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tau-2026-0360/rc).

Case presentation

A 75-year-old female was referred to our department in September 2024 for management of a giant, obstructive calculus in the left distal ureter. Pelvic non-contrast computed tomography (CT) imaging revealed a stone with a maximum diameter of approximately 9 cm, extending along approximately one-third of the ureteral length and complicated by a distal ureteral stricture adjacent to the calculus (Figure 1A-1C). Associated findings included significant hydronephrosis and ipsilateral renal atrophy. Her past medical history was significant for gastric ulcer and prior extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy for a left renal stone 16 years earlier. Preoperative evaluation revealed a polymicrobial Gram-negative urinary tract infection. Following multidisciplinary consultation, a staged endoscopic strategy was adopted to mitigate sepsis risk.

Preprocedural pelvic non-contrast CT imaging. Coronal reformats delineate the complete course of the obstructing left ureteral stone: (A) distal, (B) mid, and (C) proximal segments. CT, computed tomography.

First stage: preoperative laboratory evaluation demonstrated nitrituria and urine culture yielding mixed Gram-negative bacilli. Definitive antibiotic therapy with a broad-spectrum penicillin/β- lactamase inhibitor combination was administered. Radiographic assessment (KUB) prior to intervention identified a substantial calculus localized to the distal left ureter (Figure 2A). Initial ureteroscopic laser lithotripsy was performed with the primary goal of decompression. A double-J stent was placed, and partial stone clearance was achieved. The perioperative course was uneventful.

Comparative KUB imaging before and after the procedure. (A) Preoperative radiograph revealing a large ureteral stone occupying approximately one-third of the total length of the left ureter. (B) The initial postoperative radiographic assessment (KUB) revealed loosened stone fragments. A double-J ureteral stent was placed adjunctively to facilitate effusion drainage from the left kidney. (C) The second postoperative KUB image reveals successful removal of the majority of fragments, with small residual stones retained in the renal pelvis and proximal left ureter. (D) Final KUB image confirming successful extraction with no residual stones evident. KUB, kidneys, ureters, and bladder.

Second stage: the preoperative KUB demonstrates loosened stone fragments with a persistent high burden following the initial procedure (Figure 2B). Three weeks later, a second URS achieved substantial clearance (Figure 2C). Postoperatively, the patient developed SIRS with a marked inflammatory response (peak procalcitonin 48.6 µg/L), requiring intensive antibiotic therapy. She remained hemodynamically stable throughout and complete stone clearance was achieved during the reoperation performed four weeks later (Figure 2D). The characteristic surface appearance of an infectious stone and the fragments retrieved following surgery are shown (Figure 3).

Representative intraoperative and postoperative images of the lithotripsy procedure. (A) Ureteroscopic view of the stone surface. (B) Fragments retrieved during the second surgery.

Third stage: analysis of retrieved fragments confirmed infection stone composition (struvite and carbonate apatite), which was confirmed by Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Figure 4). In contrast to the two prior procedures using rigid URS, the present surgery was performed using a single-use flexible ureteroscope (7.5 Fr). After targeted antibiotic prophylaxis for a pre-operative Escherichia coli infection, a final combined rigid and flexible URS was performed four weeks later with strict intrarenal pressure control, achieving complete clearance.

At one-year follow-up, the patient remained stone-free with stable renal function and negative urine culture results.

Discussion

The management of ureteral calculi has been revolutionized by advancements in endourology, with URS and shockwave lithotripsy (SWL) establishing high success rates for most stones (8,9). However, the presence of active infection introduces a critical therapeutic inflection point. Infected hydronephrosis mandates urgent decompression, typically via stent or nephrostomy, to prevent urosepsis prior to any definitive stone intervention (10,11). This paradigm becomes further nuanced in the context of genuine infection stones, primarily struvite and carbonate apatite, which form in urine colonized by urease-producing organisms (12). These stones act as persistent bioreservoirs, rendering antibiotic therapy alone insufficient and making complete surgical removal the cornerstone of treatment to prevent recurrence and chronic renal damage (13), as was imperative in our case where stone analysis confirmed the infectious etiology.

Based on our experience with this case, we propose a practical decision framework for considering staged ureteroscopic management in patients presenting with a giant infected ureteral stone. The following factors favor a staged approach over single-session treatment: (I) stone diameter exceeding 5 cm, which inevitably prolongs operative time and increases the risk of mucosal injury and bacterial translocation; (II) presence of active urinary tract infection with positive cultures or elevated inflammatory markers (e.g., procalcitonin >0.5 µg/L); (III) severe hydronephrosis with thin renal parenchyma, indicating high intrarenal pressure vulnerability (14); and (IV) a coexisting distal ureteral stricture that impedes complete stone clearance in one session. In contrast to PCNL, which is preferred for large renal stones or proximal ureteral stones, a giant distal ureteral stone with a tight distal stricture makes percutaneous access technically challenging and carries a higher risk of bleeding and renal parenchymal injury. Open ureterolithotomy, while definitive, risks prolonged operative time, increased wound infection rates, and delayed recovery in an already infected field. Thus, a planned staged ureteroscopic strategy-prioritizing decompression and infection control in the first stage-provides a safer and more tailored alternative for this specific subset of complex cases.

The confluence of a giant stone burden and active infection, as presented here, amplifies the surgical dilemma exponentially. The principal challenges are twofold: technical and septic. Technically, a large stone within a potentially friable, inflamed ureter complicates access, prolongs operative time, and increases the risk of perforation (15). From an infectious standpoint, high-pressure irrigation and manipulation can induce bacteremia and endotoxin release, precipitating life-threatening sepsis (16). Our patient’s postoperative SIRS response following the second-stage procedure, marked by a profound procalcitonin elevation, starkly illustrates this inherent risk, even with preoperative antibiotics. This complication underscores why a single-session, aggressive pursuit of complete clearance is often contraindicated. Importantly, despite the development of SIRS, the patient remained hemodynamically stable and did not progress to septic shock—a common catastrophic sequela of prolonged manipulation in an infected system (17). The ability to contain the inflammatory response within a manageable spectrum was largely attributable to the staged strategy, which limited the operative time and bacterial load exposure in each session. Thus, rather than undermining the value of staging, the SIRS episode in this case actually reinforces the rationale for a stepwise approach, as it demonstrates that even when systemic inflammation occurs, it remains non-fatal and allows for successful completion of stone clearance in a subsequent stage. Our adopted staged strategy—prioritizing drainage and source control in the first stage was designed to transform an emergent septic risk into an elective procedure, thereby mitigating this central peril.

Given the high recurrence rate of infection stones, postoperative management extends beyond achieving a stone-free status (18). Eradication of the nidus must be coupled with long-term strategies to prevent re-colonization. This involves a tailored, culture-guided antibiotic course post-operatively to sterilize the urinary tract, as evidenced by our patient’s negative follow-up urine culture. For patients with recurrent struvite stones, metabolic evaluation is warranted to identify and address any predisposing factors (19,20). Furthermore, vigilant long-term surveillance with imaging and urine studies is essential. The 1-year stone-free status and sterile urine in our patient validate the efficacy of the combined surgical and medical approach, highlighting that definitive clearance, when safely achieved, forms the foundation for successful long-term prevention.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this case epitomizes a high-stakes urological scenario where standard protocols are inadequate. It reaffirms that in managing giant infected ureteral stones, physiological safety must supersede technical ambition. A deliberate, staged endoscopic approach, emphasizing initial decompression and infection control, provides a rational framework to safely navigate toward the ultimate goal of complete stone eradication. This strategy, followed by meticulous postoperative surveillance and antimicrobial management, represents a prudent and effective pathway to preserve renal function and prevent disease recurrence in these complex cases.
