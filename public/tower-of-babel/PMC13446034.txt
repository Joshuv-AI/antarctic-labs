MRI findings consistent with sacrococcygeal osteomyelitis temporally associated with ultrasound-guided caudal epidural steroid injection: A case report
Authors: Murat Erten, Ozge Onurmen-Zeyrek, Ridvan Isik, Turan Dogan, Kemal Nas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCaudal epidural steroid injection (CESI) is a widely used minimally invasive procedure for the management of chronic low back pain and radicular symptoms and is generally considered safe. However, rare but serious infectious complications such as epidural abscess, cellulitis, and osteomyelitis may occur. We report a case of MRI-consistent sacrococcygeal osteomyelitis temporally associated with ultrasound-guided CESI in a 65-year-old female patient with a history of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, coronary artery disease, and prior L4–S1 posterior spinal stabilization surgery. The patient underwent CESI due to persistent low back and bilateral leg pain despite conservative treatment. Although she initially experienced significant symptomatic relief, she developed malaise and a burning sensation at the injection site on the third post-procedural day. Laboratory investigations revealed elevated acute-phase reactants, and the patient was subsequently hospitalized for further evaluation and management. Contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated findings were suggestive of sacrococcygeal osteomyelitis involving S4–S5 and Co1 levels, accompanied by surrounding soft tissue infection (cellulitis). Following infectious diseases consultation, empirical intravenous antibiotic therapy was initiated, resulting in marked clinical and laboratory improvement. This case highlights sacrococcygeal osteomyelitis as an exceptionally rare but serious complication of CESI and emphasizes the role of risk factors such as diabetes mellitus and prior spinal instrumentation surgery in the development of post-procedural infection, as well as the importance of early recognition of new or worsening pain after the procedure.Keywords: Caudal epidural steroid injection, Sacrococcygeal osteomyelitis, Epidural injection complications, Spinal infection

Introduction

Caudal epidural steroid injection (CESI) is a widely preferred and effective minimally invasive method in the non-surgical management of chronic low back pain etiologies, such as degenerative disc disease, spinal stenosis, and radiculopathy. This procedure aims to reduce inflammation and achieve pain control by injecting corticosteroids and local anesthetics into the epidural space through the sacral hiatus [1].

Spinal infections (epidural abscess, discitis, or osteomyelitis), which are among the most serious complications of epidural injections, have a very low incidence and are reported in approximately one out of every 10,000 injections [2]. Vertebral osteomyelitis is an inflammatory process involving the medullary cavity and cortex of the bone tissue, frequently developing via hematogenous spread or direct invasion from adjacent tissues. Systemic factors, particularly uncontrolled diabetes, chronic renal failure, and immunosuppression, increase the risk of infection [3].

Although cases of lumbar osteomyelitis following CESI exist in the literature, the development of osteomyelitis directly affecting the sacrococcygeal vertebrae is an extremely rare clinical entity [4]. The anatomical location of the coccygeal region and its proximity to the injection site may carry a risk of direct spread in the event of a breach in sterility or local tissue trauma. In cases where an early diagnosis cannot be established, the process may become chronic, leading to serious morbidities that require surgical intervention and long-term intravenous antibiotic therapy [5].

By presenting this case report, we aim to discuss a rare occurrence of sacrococcygeal osteomyelitis that developed after a caudal epidural steroid injection for low back pain, as well as its clinical management, in light of the current literature.

Case Report

A 65-year-old female with a history of hypertension, insulin-independent diabetes mellitus, and coronary artery disease (status post coronary stenting) presented with chronic low back pain, bilateral leg pain, neurogenic claudication, and neuropathic symptoms. She had undergone L4–S1 posterior spinal stabilization surgery 7 years prior due to lumbar spinal stenosis. Her medical treatment included gabapentin (600 mg three times daily) and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.

On physical examination, no motor or sensory deficits were detected; however, inspection of the lumbar region revealed a postoperative scar, and lumbar flexion and extension range of motion were limited and painful, whereas right and left lateral flexion range of motion was preserved but painful.

Given that the patient did not benefit from other conservative treatment modalities, including physical therapy modalities, exercise, and medical treatment, and considering her persistent symptoms in the context of spondylosis and postoperative changes, an ultrasound-guided caudal epidural steroid injection was performed under sterile conditions in the procedure room. After obtaining verbal and written informed consent, the patient was placed in the prone position, and a pillow was positioned under the pelvis to optimize sacral flexion and improve the visualization and accessibility of the sacral hiatus. The sacrococcygeal region was prepared and disinfected three times with povidone-iodine solution, followed by sterile draping. The procedure was performed in a dedicated injection room under ultrasound guidance (HM70 EVO, Samsung Healthcare, Seoul, South Korea) by a qualified pain medicine physician (ME). A sterile probe cover and sterile single-use ultrasound gel were used throughout the procedure, and all medications were prepared from single-dose vials under meticulous aseptic conditions. Using a linear ultrasound probe (10–12 MHz), the sacral hiatus was first identified in the short-axis view, and then the probe was rotated to obtain a long-axis view to better delineate the sacral canal and guide needle trajectory. Under real-time ultrasound guidance, a 22-gauge spinal needle was advanced into the sacral hiatus with a caudo-cranial manner and a careful visualization. After negative aspiration for blood and cerebrospinal fluid was confirmed on two separate checks, ensuring correct epidural placement, the injectate was administered.

The injectate consisted of 3 ml of 0.5% (15 mg) bupivacaine and 1 ml (3 mg) betamethasone acetate, diluted to a total volume of 10 mL. The patient tolerated the procedure well, experienced complete pain relief shortly thereafter, and was discharged without any immediate complications.

At 48 hours post-procedure, the patient remained asymptomatic. However, on day 3, she developed malaise and a burning sensation at the injection site. She subsequently presented to the emergency department on three consecutive days because of persistent pain. On each occasion, she received symptomatic treatment consisting of intravenous crystalloid fluids and analgesics and was discharged after transient symptom relief. As her symptoms failed to resolve and progressively worsened, she was referred back to our pain clinic for reevaluation on day 10. Laboratory investigations revealed elevated inflammatory markers: CRP 29 mg/L, ESR 40 mm/h. As part of the diagnostic evaluation for a suspected infectious complication, blood cultures were obtained prior to the initiation of antimicrobial therapy; however, no bacterial growth was detected. Due to the severity of pain and elevated inflammatory markers, including increased C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), the patient was hospitalized for further treatment and monitoring.

Contrast-enhanced lumbar and sacrococcygeal MRI, interpreted by a radiology specialist, demonstrated hyperintense signals on T2-weighted and STIR sequences in the presacral region and subcutaneous fatty tissues, along with fluid collections and marked contrast enhancement (see Fig. 1). These findings were consistent with osteomyelitis involving the S4–S5 and Co1 levels, accompanied by soft tissue infection (cellulitis). Subsequently, a biopsy was attempted by the interventional radiology team; however, the obtained specimen was insufficient for pathological evaluation. Following consultation with the infectious diseases department, empirical intravenous ampicillin-sulbactam therapy was initiated. By day 14 of treatment, the patient demonstrated significant clinical improvement, accompanied by a decline in inflammatory markers (CRP: 10 mg/L, ESR 35 mm/h). Following completion of 21 days of intravenous antibiotic therapy, she was discharged on oral antibiotics to complete a total of 28 days of antimicrobial therapy, with outpatient follow-up arranged.

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for both the procedure and publication of this case.

Discussion

This case report presents sacrococcygeal osteomyelitis, an exceedingly unusual clinical entity that developed temporally associated with an ultrasound-guided caudal epidural steroid injection, and aims to raise awareness of this condition.

Image-guided epidural steroid injection (ESI) is widely regarded as a safe and effective procedure in clinical practice. In a retrospective study including 4265 patients, McGrath et al. reported an overall complication rate of 2.4% [2], with the most common complication being a transient increase in pain at the injection site. In contrast, major complications such as infections requiring hospitalization and advanced care are exceedingly rare, with reported infection rates ranging from 0.001% to 0.1% [6]. Although cases of epidural abscess following ESI have been described in the literature, vertebral osteomyelitis remains extremely rare, with only a limited number of reported cases [7,8].

Vertebral osteomyelitis is a serious condition that may lead to catastrophic outcomes if not diagnosed and treated promptly. Our aim is to emphasize that such infectious complications may occur even in the early postoperative period, and that careful evaluation of patient-related risk factors is essential. In a review of reported cases of discitis following epidural procedures, symptom onset typically occurred within approximately one week after the intervention. In contrast, our patient developed symptoms as early as the third day after the procedure [9].We consider that the development of this complication may be associated with the patient's underlying risk factors.

Notably, long-standing diabetes mellitus and a history of prior lumbar surgery with instrumentation may have predisposed the patient to infection. Yamaguchi et al. described a case of epidural abscess following epidural block in a patient with both diabetes mellitus and an underlying immunosuppressive condition [9]. Simopoulos et al. reported a case of vertebral osteomyelitis after lumbar epidural steroid injection (LESI) in a 77-year-old man with insulin-dependent type 2 diabetes mellitus and advanced coronary artery disease [10]. Yue and Tan described a 73-year-old woman with non–insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus who developed vertebral osteomyelitis due to Pseudomonas after a caudal epidural steroid injection [11]. Furthermore, a meta-analysis of 915 patients with epidural abscess identified diabetes mellitus as the most frequently reported risk factor [12]. Nevertheless, our case presents a distinct feature compared to the existing literature, as it involves not only osteomyelitis but also concomitant soft tissue involvement. Additionally, the patient's history of prior lumbar surgery with instrumentation may have served as a predisposing factor.

The development of infection in such cases is generally explained by three principal mechanisms: direct inoculation, hematogenous spread, and contamination originating from the local skin flora. Following caudal epidural steroid injection, direct inoculation is considered the most likely pathogenic mechanism, particularly in situations where aseptic conditions are inadequate or optimal skin antisepsis cannot be achieved. Nevertheless, several studies have demonstrated that the patient's endogenous skin flora represents the most common source of infectious complications occurring after epidural steroid injections [6]. The most frequently isolated pathogens include Staphylococcus aureus, followed by coagulase-negative staphylococci and, less commonly, Streptococcus species. Alternatively, infection may develop via hematogenous spread. In this scenario, bacterial seeding of the sacral and vertebral structures may occur through the vertebral venous plexus (Batson's plexus) following bacteremia. This mechanism is more likely in patients with diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression, or a history of concurrent systemic infection.In addition, gram-negative organisms originating from gastrointestinal or genitourinary sources (e.g., Escherichia coli, Klebsiella spp.) have been reported as causative agents of osteomyelitis, particularly in elderly patients with multiple comorbidities [13]. In rare cases, anaerobic and polymicrobial infections have also been described. In the large series by Reihsaus et al., spinal epidural infections were most commonly associated with Staphylococcus aureus, and invasive spinal procedures were identified as an important risk factor [12]. Since we were unable to detect any organisms in the microbiological culture and a tissue biopsy could not be performed, a definitive causal relationship cannot be established, yet the temporal association may suggest a procedure-related infection in this case.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of sacrococcygeal osteomyelitis temporally associated with an ultrasound-guided caudal epidural steroid injection. Our case suggests that such a surgical background may represent an important risk factor for the development of infectious complications following caudal epidural steroid injection. Furthermore, an additional strength of the present report is that the procedure was performed under ultrasound guidance, whereas the majority of previously reported post-procedural infectious complications have occurred after fluoroscopy-guided interventions. This distinction demonstrates that serious infectious complications may occur even when ultrasound-guided procedures are performed under meticulous aseptic conditions, underscoring the importance of strict aseptic technique and careful clinical follow-up regardless of the imaging modality employed. A key limitation of this case report is the failure to obtain microbiological cultures despite repeated attempts; consequently, the causative pathogen could not be identified. This limitation precluded definitive microbiological characterization of the infection, the administration of targeted culture-directed antimicrobial therapy, and a more precise comparison with previously reported cases. Identification of the causative organism would have enabled a more comprehensive interpretation of the present case within the context of the existing literature.

Conclusion

This case highlights MRI findings suggestive of sacrococcygeal osteomyelitis temporally associated with ultrasound-guided caudal epidural steroid injection. While a definitive diagnosis could not be established because microbiological confirmation was unavailable, clinicians should consider this rare but potentially serious infectious complication in patients presenting with new or worsening pain following the procedure, especially those with diabetes mellitus or previous spinal surgery.
