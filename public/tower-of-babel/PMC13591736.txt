Inferior ST-segment elevation as a diagnostic pitfall in proximal LAD occlusion: insights from a wrapped left anterior descending artery——a case report
Authors: Yu-Sha Zhang, Jin-Qian Cao, Ya-Xin Liao, Qing-Yun Zheng, Zhi-Hua Bian, Jia He, Li Shen, Qing-Yi Zhu, Ming-Qi Ouyang, Jian-Jun Tang, Ya-Qin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElectrocardiographic localization of the infarct-related artery in ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction is generally reliable; however, coronary anatomical variants and dynamic ischemic processes may result in misleading initial ECG patterns.Case presentationWe describe a clinical scenario in which isolated inferior ST-segment elevation was the initial ECG manifestation of acute myocardial infarction, while coronary angiography revealed complete occlusion of the proximal left anterior descending artery. Subsequent angiography demonstrated a wrapped left anterior descending artery extending beyond the apex and supplying the inferior wall. Serial electrocardiograms showed a sequential evolution, with resolution of inferior ST-segment elevation after reperfusion followed by the emergence of anterior changes, rather than simultaneous multiterritory involvement.ConclusionAwareness of this dynamic ECG phenomenon is essential to avoid diagnostic delay in high-risk presentations.

Introduction

In acute myocardial infarction, electrocardiographic findings usually allow reliable identification of the infarct-related artery [1, 2]. Inferior ST-segment elevation is classically associated with occlusion of the right coronary artery (RCA) or the left circumflex artery (LCX). However, coronary anatomical variants, particularly a wrap-around left anterior descending artery (LAD) supplying the inferior wall, may produce atypical and potentially misleading ECG presentations [3, 4].

Recognition of this diagnostic pitfall is clinically important, as proximal LAD occlusion is associated with a large ischemic burden and a high risk of hemodynamic compromise [5]. In this case, we described how inferior ST-segment elevation obscured extensive anterior wall ischemia caused by a wrap-around LAD, underscoring the importance of interpreting ECG findings as a dynamic rather than static process. The significance of this case does not lie simply in reconfirming the known association between a wrap-around LAD and inferior ST-segment elevation. Rather, it illustrates a dynamic pattern of ECG evolution: isolated inferior ST-segment elevation initially obscured proximal LAD occlusion, whereas anterior ST-T changes became apparent after reperfusion. This case also highlights the importance of interpreting ECG localization in the context of clinical severity, particularly when the degree of hemodynamic instability appears disproportionate to the infarct territory suggested by the initial ECG.

Clinical Observation

A 46-year-old man with a history of heavy smoking presented with acute chest pain lasting 5 h. The admission ECG demonstrated isolated ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, and aVF, without significant anterior changes (Fig. 1). Cardiac troponin I was initially within the normal range.

Given the clinical presentation and ECG findings, inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction was suspected. After administration of loading doses of aspirin and clopidogrel, the patient was scheduled to undergo coronary angiography. Before the procedure, the patient suddenly developed cardiac arrest. Immediate cardiopulmonary resuscitation, endotracheal intubation, and vasoactive support were initiated. Ventricular fibrillation occurred during resuscitation and was treated with two defibrillation shocks and antiarrhythmic therapy. Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved after approximately 40 min, and the patient subsequently became hemodynamically stable. Emergency coronary angiography showed complete occlusion of the proximal LAD, with TIMI 0 flow before intervention. The RCA and LCX showed no significant stenosis, and both had TIMI 3 flow (Fig. 2). Emergency percutaneous coronary intervention was then performed with intra-aortic balloon pump support. Thrombus aspiration was carried out first, followed by implantation of a drug-eluting stent. Post-procedural coronary angiography showed that the LAD was wrap-around in course, with the distal LAD extending beyond the apex and continuing in a PDA-like course (Fig. 3). Serial ECGs showed gradual resolution of ST-segment elevation in the inferior leads, followed by evolving ST-segment changes in the anterior leads (Fig. 4). This pattern was more consistent with sequential ischemic evolution than with simultaneous infarction in multiple coronary territories. After standard medical treatment, the patient remained hemodynamically stable and fully conscious. He was discharged two weeks later with no obvious neurological deficits and had largely regained independence in activities of daily living.

Discussion

This case highlights an important pitfall in ECG-based localization of acute myocardial infarction. Inferior ST-segment elevation is typically attributed to occlusion of the right coronary artery or the left circumflex artery [6]. However, in the presence of a wrap-around LAD that supplies the inferior myocardium, even proximal LAD occlusion may initially manifest with ischemic changes confined to the inferior leads. A wrap-around LAD is generally defined as an LAD that extends beyond the apex and continues along the diaphragmatic surface of the left ventricle, thereby supplying part of the inferior myocardium. Angiographically, this anatomical variant can be identified using the right anterior oblique caudal projection, where the LAD is seen to extend beyond the apex and supply at least approximately one-quarter of the inferior left ventricular wall [7, 8]. In our patient, angiography clearly showed that the distal LAD passed beyond the apex and continued along the diaphragmatic surface toward the inferior wall, consistent with the anatomical features of a wrap-around LAD.

In this case, the initial ECG did not suggest simultaneous involvement of both the anterior and inferior territories. Instead, the serial ECGs showed a clear temporal evolution: after reperfusion, the inferior ST-segment elevation gradually resolved, and anterior changes became more evident thereafter. From the perspective of electrocardiographic vector analysis, ST-segment deviation reflects the surface projection of injury currents generated by ischemic myocardium. Therefore, the final ECG pattern is determined not only by the culprit artery, but also by the spatial distribution, transmural extent, and temporal evolution of ischemic injury. Previous studies on the ST-segment injury vector have shown that the direction of the ST vector is closely related to the anatomical location of myocardial ischemia, suggesting that vector-based interpretation may be useful when conventional lead-based localization is misleading [9]. In the anatomical setting of a wrap-around LAD, acute LAD occlusion may involve the anterior wall, the apex, and part of the inferior myocardium supplied by the distal LAD. Thus, isolated inferior ST-segment elevation in this context can be interpreted as the surface ECG manifestation of apical-inferior ischemic involvement, rather than definitive evidence of right coronary artery or left circumflex artery occlusion.

This interpretation is also supported by previous studies of anterior myocardial infarction accompanied by inferior ST-segment elevation. Tamura et al. reported that inferior ST-segment elevation during acute anterior myocardial infarction was associated with inferior ischemic involvement and wrap-around LAD anatomy [10]. Sasaki et al. further showed that ST-segment changes in the inferior leads during anterior myocardial infarction were related to both LAD length and the site of occlusion [11]. More recently, Bozbeyoğlu et al. found that although inferior ST-segment elevation was more frequent in patients with a wrap-around LAD, the association was not strong, and ECG alone could not reliably identify this anatomical variant [12]. These findings suggest that inferior ST-segment elevation during LAD occlusion cannot be explained by coronary anatomy alone. Rather, it likely reflects the combined influence of LAD course, occlusion site, ischemic extent, and the resultant ST-segment injury vector.

In addition, proximal LAD occlusion does not always present with typical anterior ST-segment elevation in the early phase. The de Winter pattern and other atypical ECG patterns of occlusion myocardial infarction indicate that, before overt anterior ST-segment elevation becomes apparent, acute LAD occlusion may present with ST-segment depression, hyperacute T waves, subtle anterior ST-T changes, or dynamic ECG evolution [2, 13, 14]. In the present case, the initial ECG showed ST-segment elevation only in leads II, III, and aVF, whereas coronary angiography confirmed complete proximal LAD occlusion, with no significant stenosis in the RCA or LCX. After reperfusion, the inferior ST-segment elevation gradually resolved, followed by the emergence of anterior ST-T changes. This temporal sequence was more consistent with dynamic ECG evolution during ischemia and reperfusion than with a fixed ECG–coronary anatomy relationship, and did not support simultaneous multivessel infarction. From a differential diagnostic perspective, simultaneous ST-segment elevation in the inferior leads and V3–V6 should not be attributed automatically to a single inferior-wall STEMI. The main considerations include wrap-around LAD occlusion with apical–inferior involvement, concomitant multivessel coronary occlusion or coronary spasm, and non-ischemic STEMI mimics such as acute pericarditis or early repolarization. In the present case, the absence of significant RCA or LCX stenosis and the sequential ECG evolution after reperfusion favored proximal wrap-around LAD occlusion rather than simultaneous multiterritory infarction. However, because neither vectorcardiography nor body-surface potential mapping was performed, this mechanism should be regarded as a plausible interpretation based on ECG vector principles, coronary anatomy, and serial ECG changes, rather than direct electrophysiological evidence.

In general, inferior wall infarction due to RCA occlusion typically exhibits ST-segment elevation in lead III > lead II, accompanied by ST-segment depression in lead aVL > lead I. In contrast, inferior infarction resulting from LCX occlusion is often characterized by ST-segment elevation in lead II ≥ lead III. In our case, the most pronounced ST-segment elevation occurred in lead aVF, suggesting a possible LAD occlusion. However, aVF predominance alone is not a reliable independent localizing sign. Clinically, proximal LAD occlusion carries a larger ischemic burden and a higher risk of malignant arrhythmias and hemodynamic compromise [15]. Misinterpretation of inferior ST-segment elevation in this context may delay the initiation of appropriate reperfusion therapy. Therefore, this case also highlights an important issue in clinical decision-making. Although the admission ECG suggested an inferior STEMI pattern, the subsequent ventricular fibrillation, cardiac arrest, and prolonged resuscitation were disproportionate to an apparently limited inferior infarct territory. This mismatch between ECG localization and clinical severity should be regarded as a warning sign. ECG-based culprit-vessel localization is useful, but it should not override high-risk clinical features. Malignant ventricular arrhythmias, cardiac arrest, shock, pulmonary edema, or marked hemodynamic instability should raise suspicion for a larger ischemic burden, including possible left main coronary artery, proximal LAD, or RCA occlusion, even when ST-segment elevation is confined to the inferior leads.

In this case, we cannot determine whether earlier recognition of proximal LAD occlusion would have prevented cardiac arrest or changed the outcome. However, earlier suspicion of LAD involvement might have prompted more urgent angiography, preparation for high-risk PCI, and timely consideration of hemodynamic support.

The key teaching message is that hemodynamic instability disproportionate to the apparent ECG territory should raise suspicion for proximal LAD occlusion, even when the initial ECG suggests inferior STEMI. Therefore, recognition of coronary anatomical variants and an understanding of the dynamic evolution of ECG changes may help reduce diagnostic delay in high-risk presentations of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction and improve risk stratification.

Conclusion

Inferior ST-segment elevation may transiently mask proximal LAD occlusion in patients with a wrapped left anterior descending artery. Interpreting ECG findings as a dynamic process, rather than a static representation of infarct territory, is essential to avoid underestimation of ischemic risk.
