Glioma microenvironment: Cellular crosstalk, immunosuppression, and novel therapeutic perspectives
Authors: Yanjia Hu, Jian Liang, Jianguo Xu, Linjie Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioma, the most aggressive primary brain malignancy, arises from glial cells within the central nervous system. Despite aggressive treatment, the prognosis remains poor, with all patients expected to experience recurrence. An important component of glioma growth is the interaction with the other cells in the brain environs. This complex ecosystem, termed the tumor microenvironment (TME), includes recruited immune cells and the resident cells in the central nervous system. Glioma cells recruit myeloid cells and reprogram their phenotype to fuel tumor growth. Tumor cells and reprogrammed myeloid cells further suppress adaptive immune responses. Additionally, glioma co-opts normal brain cells—including endothelial cells, neurons, and astrocytes—to create a microenvironment favoring tumor progression. Following this phenotypic shift, normal cells synergize with tumor cells to drive proliferation, invasion, immunosuppression, and angiogenesis. This review integrates recent advances in understanding the multidimensional interactions within the glioma TME, including insights gained from single-cell and spatial transcriptomics approaches that reveal its heterogeneous architecture and functional niches. By elucidating the underlying cellular and molecular mechanisms, we highlight how targeting the TME offers potential therapeutic opportunities to overcome treatment resistance and improve patient outcomes.

Introduction

Glioma remains associated with a dismal prognosis despite the multimodal standard-of-care therapy involving maximal safe resection, radiotherapy, and temozolomide chemotherapy.[1] Treatment resistance occurs in the majority of patients with glioma.[2] This resistance is now understood to stem not only from the intrinsic heterogeneity of glioma cells but also from the highly complex and dynamic tumor microenvironment (TME).[3]

The glioma TME is a masterfully orchestrated society of cells, comprising a vast heterogeneity of stromal and immune components that can account for 30–50% of the total tumor bulk.[4] In this milieu, malignant cells hijack and reprogram the normal residents of the brain and recruit peripheral immune cells.[5,6] Myeloid cells, primarily the resident microglia of the brain and recruited macrophages, are not passive bystanders but are “educated” to become the very pillars of immunosuppression, constructing a barrier that effectively disarms the adaptive immune forces of the body.[7] This creates a paradoxical state of inflammation without immunity, whereby lymphoid cells, if they manage to infiltrate the tumor fortress, are often rendered exhausted and dysfunctional.[8] In addition to immune regulation, glioma progression is equally driven by its interaction with non-immune components of the milieu of the central nervous system (CNS). Neurons have recently emerged as central mediators of glioma progression, forming bona fide excitatory synapses with tumor cells through the release of neurotransmitters such as glutamate.[9–11] This synaptic integration not only provides proliferative signals to the tumor but also embeds them within functional neural circuits, creating a feed-forward loop of activity-dependent growth. Concurrently, glial cells are integrated to establish a supportive stroma. Reactive astrocytes enhance tumor invasion through gap–junction communication and secretion of proinvasive factors while sculpting an immunosuppressive niche.[12,13] Cells of the oligodendrocyte lineage, particularly oligodendrocyte precursor cells, are manipulated to support tumor angiogenesis and immune evasion.[14] Additionally, glioma-induced vascular remodeling generates a leaky, disorganized network that fuels metabolism and restricts drug delivery.[15] Collectively, these cellular components are the active architects of a microenvironment that fosters tumor maintenance, treatment resistance, and recurrence.

This complex interplay positions the glioma TME as both a central orchestrator of treatment failure and a source of unprecedented therapeutic opportunities. The old paradigm of exclusively targeting cancer cells is giving way to a more nuanced strategy: to cut the wires of communication with stromal cells and reprogram the immune landscape from suppressive to attacking. This review summarizes recent advances in decoding the multilayered glioma microenvironment, with particular emphasis on tumor–stromal crosstalk and myeloid plasticity, providing a conceptual framework for next-generation therapeutic strategies.

Complex Components and Organization of Glioma TME

The cellular components of glioma TME are composed of malignant tumor cells and nonmalignant cells, including recruited peripheral immune cells and resident cells in the CNS [Figure 1]. The recruited immune cells include monocytes/macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), dendritic cells (DCs), neutrophils, and adaptive immune cells (mainly T and B cells), while the resident cellular components involve neurons, astrocytes, microglia, endothelial, and pericytes.[5,16] In glioma TME, major cell populations included astrocytes and macrophages, whereas lymphocytes were relatively infrequent.[4] Glioma TME was traditionally thought to be poorly organized and randomly distributed with different cells and cellular states. However, the applications of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomic technologies allow for the interrogation of spatially resolved transcriptional programs and cellular interactions in segregated tumor niches, revealing that glioma TME is not uniformly disorganized but well organized in some regions.[17] Hypoxia is the central organizer of glioma TME.[17–19] It creates distinct niches that foster genomic instability, metabolic adaptation, and the evolution of therapy-resistant cells, driving the formation of well-organized regions within glioma TME across patients and scales.[17] Therefore, a systematic understanding of glioma TME is critical. It shifts our perspective from viewing the TME as a chaotic collection of cells to recognizing it as a coordinated system that sustains tumor progression and therapy resistance.

Myeloid cells: Pillars of immunosuppression

The glioma immune landscape is primarily composed of myeloid cells, which form the foundation of an immunosuppressive barrier. The myeloid compartment is highly heterogeneous and primarily consists of distinct populations of brain-resident microglia, infiltrating tumor-associated monocytes/macrophages, neutrophils, MDSCs, and DCs.[20] Myeloid cells are recruited by the tumor and educated within the microenvironment to support tumor growth, foster invasion, and directly suppress adaptive immune responses.

Microglia, monocytes, and macrophages

The myeloid compartment of the glioma microenvironment consists mainly of brain-resident microglia, and peripherally derived monocytes and macrophages (tumor-associated microglia and macrophages, TAMs).[21] Microglia originate from yolk sac progenitors that populate the CNS during early embryogenesis and are maintained by self-renewal throughout adulthood.[22] In the initial stages of glioma development, myeloid cells comprise resident microglia. With tumor progression and disruption of the associated blood–brain barrier (BBB), a substantial influx of circulating monocytes differentiates into macrophages [Figure 2A, B].[23] Tumor-derived cytokines and chemokines orchestrate this recruitment process. The C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2)/ C-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2) axis is the principal pathway through which tumor-secreted CCL2 attracts CCR2-expressing monocytes to the tumor bed,[24,25] whereas the colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF1)/colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R) axis governs the survival and expansion of both microglia and macrophages.[26]

The activation states of TAMs are traditionally described as a binary system of either tumor-suppressive (M1) or tumor-supportive (M2) phenotypes.[27] This classification is largely based on in vitro responses to specific cytokines (e.g., interferon-gamma [IFN-γ] vs. interleukin 4 [IL-4]).[28] However, the in vivo TME exposes TAMs to a complex and dynamic mixture of signals, leading to phenotypes not simply predicted by the rigid M1/M2 paradigm [Figure 2B].[29] Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses have revealed profound heterogeneity of these cells. Therefore, the activation states of TAM should be defined more specifically and precisely to determine their functions. Monocyte-derived cluster of differentiation 169-positive (CD169+) macrophages exhibit antitumor immunity activity.[30] This subgroup of macrophages not only facilitates the phagocytosis of apoptotic glioma cells but also promotes the accumulation of T and natural killer (NK) cells. Sialic acid-binding immunoglobulin-like lectin 9-positive (SIGLEC9+) TAMs are enriched in patients with immune checkpoint blocker (ICB)-nonresponsive glioblastoma (GBM), and targeting this population alleviates immunosuppression and potentiates ICB therapy.[31] Furthermore, spatial transcriptomics study showed that hypoxic macrophages in the perinecrotic niche destabilize endothelial adherens junctions by activating adrenomedullin paracrine signaling, thereby stimulating the hyperpermeable neovasculature that hampers drug delivery in glioma xenografts.[18]

Beyond immune suppression, TAMs actively remodel the tissue architecture to facilitate glioma growth and invasion. They cooperate with tumor cells to degrade the extracellular matrix (ECM) through pro-matrix metalloproteases 2 (MMP2) activation.[32,33] TAMs also contribute to tumor angiogenesis by accumulating around blood vessels and secreting proangiogenic factors, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), epidermal growth factor (EGF), and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CXCL2), which stimulate the formation of a dysfunctional vascular network.[34–36] In addition, TAMs have been reported to support glioma growth by providing energy to cells. Glioma cells exploit de novo creatine synthesis in cluster of differentiation 163-positive (CD163+) myeloid cells to fuel tumor cell growth and survival within the hypoxic TME.[37] Additionally, TAMs in the tumor core promote glioma growth and invasion through secreting extracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP).[38]

Neutrophils

Gliomas actively recruit peripheral neutrophils through chemokines, such as interleukin 8 (IL-8), tumor necrosis factor (TNF), and CCL2 [Figure 2C].[39,40] A high frequency of tumor-infiltrating neutrophils is strongly correlated with poor clinical outcomes. Neutrophils are educated by a network of soluble inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α and ceruloplasmin, to possess immunosuppressive and proangiogenic capacities.[41] Cluster of differentiation 71-positive (CD71+) neutrophils, characterized by increased glycolytic metabolism, suppress T-cell activity via arginase-1 (ARG1) upregulation.[42] Additionally, neutrophils can induce ferroptosis in glioma cells, which triggers necrosis and creates a self-amplifying loop between inflammation and tumor aggressiveness.[43] Notably, a dendritic-like “hybrid” neutrophil subset originating from skull bone marrow has recently been identified that can prime T cell cytotoxicity and memory responses, thereby restraining tumor growth.[44] These findings highlight the functional heterogeneity of neutrophils in gliomas, suggesting that their anatomical origins and polarization states may determine their pro- or anti-tumor effects, which should be expanded in future investigations.

MDSCs

MDSCs are a heterogeneous population of immature myeloid cells that expand into the cancer microenvironment. MDSCs potently suppress T cell function primarily by expressing enzymes such as ARG1 and inducible nitric oxide synthase, which deplete essential nutrients such as L-arginine and generate reactive nitrogen species, leading to T cell dysfunction [Figure 2D].[45,46] In glioma, monocytic MDSCs (mMDSCs) predominate over granulocytic (gMDSCs) and display elevated expression of integrin β1 and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4), driving immunosuppression and tumor progression.[47] Intriguingly, there appears to be a sexual dimorphism in the distribution of mMDSCs and gMDSCs in patients with glioma: mMDSCs are enriched in male tumors, whereas gMDSCs are elevated in the blood of female patients.[48] This discovery points to distinct, sex-specific treatment strategies: antiproliferative agents against mMDSCs for males and IL-1β blockade against gMDSCs for females. In addition, a new subset termed early MDSCs was identified within the glioma TME, which exhibits robust signatures of active anabolism and catabolism, as well as responses to hypoxia and cellular stress.[19] Early MDSCs are recruited by chemokines secreted from stem-like glioma cells, thereby fostering a suppressive immune microenvironment.

DCs

DCs are central antigen-presenting cells (APCs) that initiate and orchestrate adaptive antitumor immunity.[49] Two major subsets are recognized: type 1 conventional DCs (cDC1s), which cross-present antigens to cluster of differentiation 8+ (CD8+) T cells and are essential for combating tumor responses;[50,51] and type 2 conventional DCs (cDC2s), which primarily activate cluster of differentiation 4+ (CD4+) T helper cells.[52,53] In glioma, however, DCs exhibit marked heterogeneity and functional impairment.[20,54] Tumor-derived soluble factors, including VEGF, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-10 (IL-10), directly inhibit the maturation of DCs and their antigen-presenting ability [Figure 2E].[55–57] Similarly, aberrantly activated signal transducer activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling in tumor cells inhibits the maturation of DCs, thereby impairing the antigen presentation process.[58,59] Moreover, other myeloid cells within the microenvironment reinforce this dysfunction. For example, MDSCs release truncated lipids that impair the DC cross-priming capacity.[60] Collectively, these findings underscore the concerted roles of neutrophils, MDSCs, and DCs in establishing the immunosuppressive landscape of gliomas and their potential as targets for immune-restorative therapies.

Lymphoid lineage: Suppressed antitumor force

In contrast to the abundant myeloid compartment, adaptive immune cells are not only scarce within the glioma TME but are also frequently dysfunctional.[4] This suppressed state arises not from a lack of immune recognition, but rather from a complex network of active inhibitory mechanisms orchestrated by tumor cells and other cellular components within the glioma TME.

T cells

Glioma generally carries a lower tumor mutational burden compared with melanoma or lung cancer, typically ranging from 1 to 10 mutations per megabase. Recurrent driver mutations such as epidermal growth factor receptor variant III (EGFRvIII), isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 R132H (IDH1 R132H), and phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) alterations, along with patient-specific somatic mutations, can give rise to immunogenic peptides presented by major histocompatibility complex molecules.[61,62] After processing, these neoantigens are captured by DCs and other APCs within the tumor or peritumoral space. These APCs then migrate via the meningeal lymphatic vessels or cerebrospinal fluid pathways to the deep cervical lymph nodes, which are the primary site for peripheral immune priming.[63] In these lymphoid organs, naïve T cells are activated by tumor-derived antigens and differentiate into effector CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell subsets.

Activated T cells enter the CNS parenchyma by well-defined mechanisms. One key pathway involves C-C motif chemokine receptor 6 (CCR6)-mediated migration across the choroid plexus epithelium by T helper 17 (Th17) cells.[64] Another major route is the inflammation triggered by glioma invasion, and secreted factors can directly induce microvascular alterations. These changes facilitate the recruitment of T cells and innate immune cells through both the native microvessels of the brain and new tumor-associated vessels formed in response to proangiogenic factors.[65]

When T cells enter the glioma microenvironment, they encounter a complex array of immune-regulatory mechanisms. Transforming growth factor beta 2 (TGF-β2), derived from tumor cells or other immune cells, suppresses interleukin-2 (IL-2)-dependent T cell survival [Figure 2F].[66,67] Furthermore, TGF-β is capable of attenuating the ability of cytotoxic T cells to express crucial effector molecules, such as granzyme B and IFN-γ.[68] Glioma cells also induce T cell exhaustion by co-opting myeloid cells to release IL-10 [Figure 2F].[69] Another challenge is the metabolically hostile glioma microenvironment. First, glioma cells predominantly rely on aerobic glycolysis and compete fiercely with T cells for glucose, causing local nutrient deprivation.[70] Second, elevated levels of enzymes such as indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO1) catabolize essential amino acids such as tryptophan into kynurenines, which are directly toxic to T cells and promote Treg differentiation.[71,72] Third, the accumulation of lactate and reactive oxygen species contributes to T cell hypo-responsiveness.[73–75] These multifaceted mechanisms induce deep functional and metabolic impairments that cause T cell exhaustion or dysfunction.[76]

One hallmark of T cell dysfunction/exhaustion is the coexpression of multiple coinhibitory receptors including programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 3 (TIM-3), lymphocyte activation gene 3 (LAG-3), and T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif domains (TIGIT).[77] These coinhibitory receptors may induce activation of the T cell receptor without sufficient costimulation signaling, resulting in the binding of nuclear factors of activated T cells to promote the exhaustion of activated CD8+ T cells.[78–80] Tregs can also induce T-cell exhaustion by downregulating costimulatory molecules on APCs.[81] In contrast, glioma cells and APCs within the TME express ligands of coinhibitory receptors that antagonize essential receptor engagement required for normal function and restrict the antitumor activity of CD8+ T cells.[82]

B cells and plasma cells

The functional roles of tumor-infiltrating B cells and plasma cells (PCs), and consequently the prognostic and predictive significance of immunoglobulin repertoires, are highly dependent on the specific tumor type and stage.[83,84] In the glioma TME, the number of infiltrating B cells is relatively lower than that of other immune cells.[35,85] The roles of B and PCs in gliomas remain unclear. Recently, PCs have been reported to be aberrantly enriched in the GBM-infiltrating B-lineage population, and are characterized by low somatic hypermutation and a correlation with unfavorable prognosis.[86] Mechanistically, PC-derived immunoglobulin G promotes glioma stem cell proliferation through activation of the Fc gamma receptor IIA (FcγRIIA)/protein kinase B (AKT)/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway. The functional landscape of B cells and PCs in glioma is underexplored, and further research is warranted to determine whether some subsets of these cells exert antitumor roles.

Glioma nonimmune microenvironment

The progression of glioma is not solely determined by the recruited immune cells, but is also influenced by the unique resident cells in brain microenvironment.[87] The CNS–TME interaction plays a fundamental role in regulating tumor proliferation, invasion, and therapeutic resistance.

Neurons drive glioma progression

Glioma maintains a deep and active interplay with neurons. Glioma cells can modulate neuronal network hyperexcitability and even integrate functionally into synaptic circuits [Figure 2G].[88,89] A key mechanism of neuron–glioma crosstalk is the formation of direct functional synapses between neurons and glioma cells. scRNA-seq analyses have revealed that glioma cells highly express neuron-specific and neurodevelopmental genes, supporting their capacity for synaptic integration.[90] Ultrastructural studies have confirmed that these neuron–glioma synapses possess presynaptic terminals with vesicles and postsynaptic densities enriched with glutamate receptors, closely resembling normal neuronal synapses.[91]

Neuronal activity, which is fundamental for information processing in the brain, also plays a critical role in modulating glioma progression. Neuronal activity influences gliomas through both brain-wide circuits and local interactions. These effects are mediated by paracrine factors such as neuroligin-3 (NLGN3), brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF1), and semaphorin 4F (SEMA4F), which regulate glioma cell proliferation and invasion.[9,92–95] For instance, the postsynaptic scaffold protein NLGN3 is crucial for synaptic transmission and behavior and is cleaved and released upon activation, where it potently stimulates glioma growth.[92,93] In addition to direct neuronal signaling, glioma cells actively reshape their microenvironment. They secrete exosomes that directly influence key tumorigenic processes, including angiogenesis, invasion, migration, and immunosuppression within the TME, which in turn promotes further tumor expansion.[96,97]

Glioma progression is also directly modulated by classical neurotransmitters, as glioma cells express their corresponding receptors.[98] Glutamate, a key excitatory neurotransmitter, promotes tumor growth by activating postsynaptic ionotropic receptors-α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptors (AMPARs) and N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors (NMDARs), leading to membrane depolarization, and activation of metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluRs) via G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) signaling.[99,100] Conversely, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter, unexpectedly promotes glioma progression.[10] Although GABA activation of GABAA (ionotropic) and GABAB (metabotropic) receptors typically cause hyperpolarization in neurons, it also depolarizes glioma membranes due to high intracellular chloride concentrations resulting from upregulated Na+–K+–Cl– cotransporter 1 (NKCC1) expression. Blocking NKCC1 with bumetanide attenuates diffuse midline gliomas.[10] Glioma cells actively shape their microenvironment by releasing glutamate, which causes neuronal hyperexcitability and excitotoxicity, and GABA, which induces neuronal network hyperactivity and supports tumor growth.[10,98] However, the specific mechanisms through which GABA facilitates glioma growth are not fully understood.

Astrocyte-mediated glioma progression

Astrocytes constitute up to 50% of all cells in CNS and play a fundamental physiological role.[101] These include supporting neuronal activity patterns, maintaining homeostasis by scavenging neurotransmitters, facilitating synaptic communication, regulating neuronal metabolism, and maintaining the BBB.[102] Under pathological conditions such as ischemia, trauma, or inflammation, astrocytes become “reactive”.[103–105] This reactive state is complex and context-dependent. Some reactive astrocytes exhibit anti-inflammatory functions by enhancing antigen presentation and complement activation, whereas others contribute to protective scar formation by secreting thrombospondins and neurotrophic factors that support neuronal survival.[106]

Interestingly, reactive astrocytes are also recognized as critical enhancers of glioma growth and aggressiveness. For instance, experimental depletion of reactive astrocytes has been shown to attenuate tumor progression.[12,107] Glioma cells, along with microglia and hypoxic conditions within the TME, can activate surrounding astrocytes. Glioma infiltration is driven by alterations in the cellular membrane potential, particularly in nonsynaptic-dependent potassium currents.[91] Reactive astrocytes buffer this excess of extracellular potassium, thereby synchronizing with glioma cells to maintain ionic homeostasis and promoting tumor progression.[91] Reactive astrocytes form direct cell-to-cell contacts with glioma cells via gap junctions (GJs) that are primarily composed of connexin-43 [Figure 2H]. In vitro studies have shown that disrupting connexin-43 impairs glioma cell migration.[108,109] The cytoskeletal protein growth-associated protein 43 (GAP43), which is highly expressed in reactive astrocytes, is typically associated with axonal growth cones.[110] Astrocytes facilitate the formation of protrusions that help to establish GJs and physically guide glioma cell migration. Considering their crucial role in maintaining the BBB, reactive astrocytes significantly modulate immune cell infiltration and activity in the glioma TME.[91] They can contribute to an immunosuppressive environment by altering the expression of cell adhesion molecules and secreting factors that inhibit or reprogram the function of antitumor immune cells, such as T lymphocytes.[91,111] Glioma-derived interleukin-11 (IL-11) drives a STAT3-dependent TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) expression program in astrocytes that suppresses anti-tumor immunity by inducing T cell apoptosis [Figure 2H].[112] A bidirectional annexin A1 (ANXA1)–formyl peptide receptor 1 (FPR1) communication axis between astrocytes and GBM cells suppresses anti-tumor immunity by inhibiting immunogenic necroptosis and astrocyte inflammatory signaling [Figure 2H].[113] Additionally, reactive astrocytes can remodel the TME in glioma via metabolites, or alternate angiogenesis influenced the progression of glioma.[99]

Gatekeeper: Endothelial cells (ECs)

ECs and mural cells, particularly pericytes, are essential for maintaining the structural and functional integrity of the BBB. ECs, normally sealed by tight and adherent junctions to form a protective barrier, are involved in supporting tumor growth by secreting proangiogenic factors, such as VEGF, fibroblast growth factor (FGF), and angiopoietins [Figure 2I].[114–116] However, glioma-associated vessels become structurally and functionally abnormal, exhibiting pathological features such as larger diameters, disorganized basement membranes, and increased permeability due to defective pericyte coverage and aberrant ECs. This leakage promotes inflammation and immune cell infiltration, paradoxically impeding effective drug delivery. Beyond blood ECs, recent evidence highlights a critical role for lymphatic ECs (LECs) in glioma progression.[117] In gliomas, LECs promote glioma stem cell proliferation and self-renewal via the C-C motif chemokine ligand 21 (CCL21)/C-C motif chemokine receptor 7 (CCR7) signaling axis, which stabilizes 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase 1 (HMGCS1) and drives cholesterol biosynthesis to fuel tumor growth [Figure 2I]. However, the intricate interactions between glioma cells and LECs remain largely unexplored.

Crosstalk between glioma cells and the tumor vasculature, including ECs and pericytes, is fundamental for understanding glioma progression and therapeutic resistance. A key mechanism is vascular co-option, in which glioma cells migrate and spread along existing blood vessels to promote invasion.[118] This behavior relies on direct contact between the glioma cells and ECs, facilitated by adhesion molecules such as integrins. Simultaneously, glioma cells drive angiogenesis by secreting soluble factors such as VEGF, SDF-1, and TGF-β, along with matrix metalloproteases, to stimulate new vessel formation and further accelerate tumor growth.[119,120] VEGF and angiopoietin-Tie pathways are spatially enriched in regions adjacent to necrosis and peritumoral areas.[121] Given that tumor vessels fuel glioma progression, antiangiogenic therapies have been investigated as promising strategies. However, their clinical success has been limited, underscoring the complexity of the tumor-vascular microenvironment.

Therapeutic Strategies Targeting the TME

The complex and dynamic nature of the glioma microenvironment necessitates a fundamental shift in our therapeutic strategy from directly targeting tumor cells to actively modulating the TME [Figure 2]. Researchers are now designing refined strategies to dismantle the immunosuppressive network in the glioma TME, with the ultimate goal of unleashing or enhancing antitumor immunity [Table 1]. Interactions between glioma cells and other nonimmune components should also be considered. Given the complex cellular interactions and signaling pathways within the glioma TME, multifaceted treatment strategies must be adopted to overcome the redundant mechanisms that sustain treatment resistance and promote durable antitumor activity.

Depletion of TAMs

TAMs depletion is a critical strategy for modulating the immunosuppressive microenvironment in gliomas.[122] CSF1R inhibition is a promising therapeutic approach.[22] In preclinical models, small-molecule inhibitors, such as PLX3397 (pexidartinib), have demonstrated substantial efficacy.[26,123,124] However, clinical experience with CSF1R inhibitors has uncovered treatment resistance, which constrains their utility as monotherapy. It is likely that CSF1R blockade triggers compensatory upregulation of IGF1 in surviving TAMs, which in turn activates phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) signaling in tumor cells and drives resistance.[125] This insight has stimulated the development of combination therapies that concurrently target both CSF1R and IGF1R or PI3K pathways. In addition, the fibrotic response to anti-CSF1R therapy potentiates glioma recurrence.[126] Fibrotic areas encapsulate surviving glioma cells to promote dormancy and evade immune surveillance. Mechanistically, the fibrotic treatment response was mediated by perivascular-derived fibroblast-like cells via activation by TGF-β signaling and neuroinflammation. Thus, inhibition of treatment-associated fibrosis may improve the efficacy of anti-CSF-1R therapy. The CCL2–CCR2 axis has emerged as another attractive target, given its critical role in recruiting monocytes/macrophages.[127] CCR2 antagonists can markedly reduce the infiltration of monocyte-derived macrophages/MDSCs into the tumor bed, thereby altering the composition of the myeloid compartment and alleviating immunosuppressive pressure.[24,25,128]

Reprogramming TAMs

In addition to depletion strategies, researchers are pursuing therapeutic approaches to reprogram TAMs. Targeting the cluster of differentiation 47 (CD47)/signal regulatory protein α (SIRP α) axis represents an innovative strategy.[129–131] Blocking antibodies against CD47 or its receptor signal regulatory protein α (SIRPα) disrupt the “don’t eat me” signal exploited by tumor cells to evade phagocytosis.[132,133] Early phase clinical trials evaluating CD47 blockers in tumors have revealed their potential in combination with existing immunotherapies.[134–137] Toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists such as CpG oligodeoxynucleotides (Toll-like receptor 9 agonists) can stimulate TAMs to produce proinflammatory cytokines while enhance their antigen-presenting capacity.[138,139] The cluster of differentiation 40 (CD40) agonists constitute another promising avenue, and agonistic antibodies targeting this pathway have demonstrated the ability to activate APCs, thereby promoting a proinflammatory shift in the TME and improving T-cell priming efficiency.[140–142] Particularly noteworthy are approaches targeting metabolic reprogramming of TAMs through inhibition of enzymes such as PI3Kγ.[143–146] This strategy not only reverses the immunosuppressive polarization of macrophages and enhances CD8+ T cell-mediated tumor killing but also creates synergy ICB therapy.[147] Although a growing body of evidence identifies triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) as a target in glioma TAMs, its functional role remains controversial, with studies yielding conflicting conclusions. TREM2 loss of function reshapes the TME and potentiates the efficacy of anti-PD-1 treatment.[148,149] However, other studies reported that TREM2 inhibits gliomas by enhancing the phagocytosis of tumor cells and promoting immune responses by facilitating major histocompatibility complex II-associated CD4+ T-cell responses.[150,151] TREM2 overexpression impedes GBM progression and synergizes with anti-PD-1 therapy by enhancing the binding of CNS-enriched sphingolipids to TREM2 on myeloid cells.[152] Therefore, the role of TREM2 in TAMs requires further investigation. In addition to these reprogramming strategies, engineering chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) to endow macrophages with antitumor capacities has emerged as a powerful immunotherapeutic approach.[153] CAR-macrophages (CAR-Ms) enhance tumor-targeted phagocytosis, secrete proinflammatory cytokines, and present tumor antigens to activate cytotoxic T lymphocyte responses, thereby bridging innate and adaptive immunity.[154] These multifaceted properties position CAR-Ms as potent agents against therapy-refractory solid malignancies.

Targeting neutrophils

With regard to tumor-associated neutrophils, interventions have focused on disrupting their protumor functions through C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CXCR2) inhibition and targeting neutrophil extracellular trap formation via protein arginine deiminase 4 (PAD4) inhibition or S100 calcium-binding protein A8/A9 (S100A8/A9) blockade.[155–157] These approaches have demonstrated significant potential in preclinical models, not only in reducing tumor progression but also in improving vascular function and enhancing chemotherapy efficacy. Particularly promising are strategies that exploit neutrophil trafficking mechanisms for targeted drug delivery, while simultaneously inhibiting their protumorigenic functions.

Targeting MDSCs

MDSCs remain a formidable barrier to effective immunotherapy, and several pharmacological strategies have emerged to counter their immunosuppressive functions. Acetylsalicylic acid delays glioma development by inhibiting prostaglandin E2 (PGE2)-induced recruitment of gMDSCs in the GBM microenvironment.[158] Brain parenchyma-derived cluster of differentiation 200 (CD200) recruits MDSCs via CD200 receptor (CD200R) expressed on myeloid cells.[159,160] Targeting CD200R inhibits MDSC expansion, slows tumor growth, and significantly enhances survival.[161] Notch signaling is highly activated within the glioma TME and is positively associated with the expansion of MDSCs.[162] Likewise, the pharmacological blockade of Notch signaling can rescue the oncolytic herpes simplex virus (oHSV)-induced immunosuppressive TME and activate a CD8-dependent antitumor memory response. CCR5 is found in diverse subsets of immune cells, and the coinhibition of CCR2 and CCR5 improves the survival and synergizes robustly with anti-PD-1 therapy.[163] In addition to reducing MDSCs infiltration, strategies aimed at reshaping immunosuppressive function also show treatment potential. In GBM models, mMDSCs express higher levels of DPP-4 than gMDSCs, and targeting DPP-4 in mMDSCs inhibits phosphorylated extracellular signal-regulated kinase (pERK) signaling to extend survival in preclinical GBM models.[47]

Targeting DCs

Over the past few decades, DC vaccines have gained increasing attention for use in glioma therapy. Several clinical trials have shown the feasibility, safety, and bioactivity of DC vaccines.[164–167] However, the highly immunosuppressive microenvironment of gliomas results in the limited efficacy of DC vaccines, prompting the redefinition of their design and integration with combination therapies. In a murine glioma model, the deletion of Tregs by anti-CD25 treatment strongly enhanced the efficacy of DC vaccination.[168] The results of a randomized phase II trial showed that TLR agonists in combination with DC vaccination enhanced the systemic immune response and improved patient prognosis.[169] Preconditioning the DC vaccine site with a potent recall antigen can significantly improve lymph node homing and the efficacy of tumor antigen-specific DCs.[170] This pretreatment enhances DC migration and improves the survival of patients with glioma. Simultaneously, the combination with sarcosine promotes the trafficking of DCs and improves the efficacy of antitumor DC vaccines.[171]

Overcoming T-cell exhaustion

Revitalizing functionally impaired T cells within glioma TME is challenging. Antibody-mediated ICB targeting PD-1, programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1), and cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4) has fundamentally reshaped cancer treatment paradigms; however, its translation into gliomas has been met with limited clinical success. The phase III CheckMate-143 trial demonstrated no overall survival benefit with nivolumab (anti-PD-1) over bevacizumab in recurrent glioma, underscoring the distinct biological barriers to this malignancy.[172] In response, investigations have expanded toward novel immune checkpoints, such as Y-box binding protein 1 (YBX1), Y-box binding protein 3 (YBX3), TIM-3, LAG-3, and TIGIT, which are frequently upregulated in glioma-infiltrating lymphocytes and appear to deliver nonredundant inhibitory signals.[173–176] In most cases, additional interventions are needed to sensitize cells to immune checkpoint therapy. For instance, blocking IL-8 or inhibiting its receptor (CXCR1/2) can potentiate the efficacy of ICB in gliomas.[177] Targeting actin-related protein 2/3 complex subunit 1B (ARPC1B) overcomes ICB resistance in GBM by reversing protumorigenic macrophage polarization.[178] Concurrently, biomarker-guided enrollment strategies aim to identify patients most likely to respond to checkpoint inhibition. This approach specifically targets individuals exhibiting pre-existing T-cell immunity or hypermutated tumors due to mismatch repair deficiency, as they stand to acquire more meaningful clinical benefits.[179]

CAR-T-cell therapy

Adoptive cell therapy constitutes a complementary and rapidly advancing strategy for redirecting T-cell activity against gliomas. CAR-T cells engineered to recognize surface antigens such as interleukin-13 receptor α-2 (IL-13Rα2)[180,181], EGFRvIII,[182] and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)[183] have elicited striking objective responses in early-stage trials. However, several key obstacles, such as antigenic heterogeneity, insufficient trafficking across the BBB, potent local immunosuppression, and poor T-cell persistence, continue to hinder progress. To address these limitations, researchers are developing a suite of complementary strategies across the different stages of therapy. Using dual-targeting CAR constructs or T cells engineered to secrete bispecific engagers can broaden antigen recognition and help prevent escape.[184,185] Administration routes may center on direct locoregional routes, such as infusion into the brain ventricles or surgical cavity, with the goal of fully circumventing the BBB.[180] To counteract local suppression, “armored” CAR-T constructs are being developed to coexpress protective elements (e.g., dominant-negative TGF-β receptors) or immunostimulatory cytokines such as IL-12.[186,187] Furthermore, enhancing T-cell longevity involves optimizing manufacturing to enrich for memory-like subsets, developing “off-the-shelf” allogeneic products to reduce delays, and combining CAR T-cells with vaccine boosters or lymphodepleting preconditioning to improve engraftment and sustained activity.[188]

Modulating the metabolic microenvironment

In gliomas, tumors adopt several metabolic strategies, including the metabolism of glucose, tryptophan, arginine, and adenosine, to support tumor growth and foster an immune suppressive microenvironment.[3,189,190] Targeting these metabolic nodes may hold promising treatment potential. Glioma cells exhibit enhanced glycolysis, which limits the availability and utilization of glucose by other cells in the TME, compromising antitumor immune response.[191] Therefore, reversing this metabolic program could improve immune cell access to glucose to stimulate the antitumor response. Hexokinase 2 (HK2) is an essential rate-limiting enzyme in glycolysis that converts glucose into glucose-6-phosphate. HK2 is highly expressed in glioma cells and is associated with poor clinical outcomes.[192] A preclinical model showed that HK2 depletion suppresses the proliferation and angiogenesis of glioma cells. HK2 also activates NF-κB and subsequently upregulate PD-L1 expression.[193] Targeting HK2 promotes CD8+ T-cell activation and infiltration into tumor tissues, thereby reducing brain tumor growth. Tryptophan metabolism is another strategy for targeting the immunometabolic interface in gliomas.[194] Targeting the indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 pathway can restore tryptophan homeostasis and alleviate the associated immunosuppressive effects.[71,195,196] Arginine deprivation also presents a treatment strategy in gliomas. Arginine deprivation unleashes a dual attack against gliomas: it metabolically primes tumor cells for radiation-induced killing via peroxynitrite while simultaneously converting the immunosuppressive microenvironment to an antitumor state by reprogramming glioma-associated macrophages.[197] Adenosine contributes to immune suppression by regulating the regulatory and effector T cells. Strategy targeting adenosine metabolism focuses on disrupting the cluster of differentiation 39 (CD39)/cluster of differentiation 73 (CD73)-A2a adenosine receptor (A2aR) axis.[198,199]

Vascular normalization and improving drug delivery

Despite improved progression-free survival, the limited overall survival benefit conferred by the anti-VEGF antibody bevacizumab highlights the limitations of inducing profound hypoxia through excessive vessel pruning.[200–203] This insight catalyzed a paradigm shift toward vascular normalization.[204] The resulting improvement in perfusion, reduction in hypoxia, and enhanced immune cell infiltration create a microenvironment that is more conducive to combination therapies. Concurrent blockade of VEGF and angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2), key drivers of vascular abnormalities, has demonstrated more robust vascular normalization, reduced infiltration of tumor-associated macrophages, and improved chemotherapeutic delivery in preclinical models.[205,206] In addition, activation of the Ang-2 receptor, tunica interna EC kinase 2 (Tie2), can normalize the vasculature at both the tumor periphery and center, similar to the effects of vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2) blockade.[207–209] Across various tumor models, simultaneous tunica interna EC kinase 2 activation and Ang-2 inhibition form a powerful therapeutic strategy to elicit a favorable TME and enhance the delivery of chemotherapeutic agents into tumors.[210]

Targeting neuronal–glioma interactions

An emerging field in cancer neuroscience suggests that glioma progression may be critically influenced by glioma interactions with the nervous system. This insight unveils novel avenues for therapeutic intervention, focusing on disrupting these neural–tumor connections. One of the most promising strategies involves targeting the neuron–glioma synaptic connections. Preclinical studies have indicated that inhibition of AMPA receptor may help suppress synaptic activity-driven proliferation and invasion at the tumor margin.[9,11,91] Another important approach is to interfere with paracrine mitogenic signaling (e.g., NLGN3) within the TME.[92,93,211] A logical direction would be to combine these novel interventions with standard therapies. Integrating agents, such as AMPA receptor antagonists or NLGN3-neutralizing antibodies, with temozolomide chemotherapy or radiotherapy, may offer a synergistic effect, targeting both the intrinsic resilience of the tumor and its reliance on neural support. Strategies for reactive astrocytes have focused on disrupting their supportive interactions with tumor cells through connexin 43 hemichannel inhibitors, such as tonabersat, which have shown potential in preclinical models.[212] Additionally, Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) pathway inhibitors, including ruxolitinib, can be explored to block astrocyte-mediated immunosuppression and tumor invasion.[107]

Future Perspectives

The glioma microenvironment constitutes both the primary obstacle and potential treatment targets for therapeutic innovation. Effective intervention requires moving beyond a singular focus on neoplastic cells to engage the entire suppressive network where immunosuppressive myeloid populations, compromised adaptive immunity, pathological vasculature, and integrated neural components collectively sustain tumor progression and therapeutic resistance. Future success depends on strategically engineered combination therapies that concurrently address multiple ecosystem components. Promising directions include using vascular normalization to improve drug access, reprogramming myeloid cells to alleviate immunosuppression, and disrupting neuron–glioma circuits to inhibit growth, while harnessing activated DCs to reestablish effective antitumor immunity. The integration of single-cell technologies, spatial multiomics, and advanced delivery platforms is instrumental in the development of these sophisticated approaches. Fundamentally, reconceptualizing glioma as a dynamic ecosystem rather than merely cellular aggregates provides a transformative framework for therapeutic development. By targeting the critical interdependencies among microenvironmental elements and adapting our strategies to the evolving tumor landscape, we can overcome historical therapeutic barriers and achieve meaningful clinical breakthroughs in overcoming devastating disease.
