The application of internal ring closure in laparoscopic transabdominal preperitoneal (TAPP) repair for large indirect inguinal hernias: a retrospective study of 162 cases
Authors: Muyang Liu, Xiaojun Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePatients with primary unilateral indirect inguinal hernias and large internal inguinal rings have a higher incidence of seroma formation following laparoscopic transabdominal preperitoneal (TAPP) hernia repair. This study aims to investigate the clinical efficacy of suturing and closing the internal inguinal ring during the TAPP procedure to reduce the postoperative seroma rate.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical data of 162 patients with primary unilateral indirect inguinal hernias and an internal ring defect diameter of ≥ 3.0 cm, who were treated at Kunshan First People’s Hospital from December 2023 to November 2024. All patients underwent the TAPP procedure with hernia sac transection. Based on whether the internal inguinal ring was sutured closed intraoperatively, patients were divided into two groups: an experimental group (internal ring closure, n = 81) and a control group (no internal ring closure, n = 81).The study compared the incidence of postoperative seromas, perioperative indicators (operative time, incision size, blood loss, length of hospital stay, etc.), and other related complications (postoperative pain, sensory abnormalities, infection, urinary retention, testicular edema, etc.) between the two groups, as well as short-term recurrence rates.ResultsThere were no statistically significant differences in the baseline characteristics between the two groups, and all surgeries were completed successfully. The incidence of seroma in the experimental group at 7 days and 1 month postoperatively was significantly lower than in the control group [8.64% (7/81) vs. 28.40% (23/81), P = 0.002; and 2.47% (2/81) vs. 13.58% (11/81), P = 0.018, respectively]. Regarding severity (Morales-Conde classification), the experimental group recorded 5 Grade I and 2 Grade II cases, with zero Grade III events; whereas the control group observed 12 Grade I, 8 Grade II, and 3 Grade III events. There were no significant differences in seroma incidence between the two groups at 3 months and 6 months postoperatively (P > 0.05). The operative time for the experimental group [(63.67 ± 9.26) min] was slightly longer than that of the control group [(60.21 ± 10.23) min] (t = 2.255, P = 0.025). There were no statistically significant differences in other perioperative indicators, other related complications, or short-term recurrence rates between the two groups (P > 0.05).ConclusionFor primary unilateral indirect inguinal hernias with an internal ring defect diameter of ≥ 3.0 cm, suturing and closing the internal ring defect during the TAPP procedure was associated with a lower rate of seroma formation.Keywords: Laparoscopic transabdominal preperitoneal hernia repair, Internal ring closure, Indirect inguinal hernia, Seroma

Introduction

Indirect inguinal hernia is one of the most common surgical diseases, accounting for approximately 90% of all external abdominal hernias [1]. Transabdominal preperitoneal (TAPP) repair is a widely adopted approach. Despite the advantages of laparoscopy, postoperative seroma remains a frequent concern, particularly in patients with large internal ring defects (EHS L3). While overall seroma rates after conventional TAPP are often around 10% [2], reports in large-defect or scrotal hernias are substantially higher, reaching up to 64% [3]. A seroma is a collection of sterile serous fluid that accumulates in tissue spaces following surgery, trauma, or inflammatory reactions, primarily composed of lymphatic fluid and serum components. Although most seromas are asymptomatic, Morales-Conde grade III–IV seromas can delay postoperative recovery, and some cases require percutaneous aspiration [4].

Current clinical preventive measures against postoperative seroma mainly include modifying the management of the hernia sac, improving mesh materials, and placing postoperative drains, but none have been conclusively proven effective. In reviewing a substantial body of literature, we noted that in the treatment of direct inguinal hernias, the direct hernia defect is typically sutured closed. Relevant clinical studies have already demonstrated that closing the direct hernia defect can effectively reduce the postoperative seroma rate in direct inguinal hernias [5]. However, there are no corresponding guideline recommendations on whether the internal ring defect should be sutured closed during the treatment of indirect inguinal hernias. Furthermore, several studies have pointed out that whether the hernia sac is transected has a significant impact on seroma formation. Some researchers believe that complete dissection of the hernia sac leads to a higher incidence of seroma compared to transection [6], while other studies have proposed the opposite conclusion [7–9]. A few researchers have conducted related studies on patients with primary unilateral indirect inguinal hernias with a hernia sac diameter of ≥ 3 cm, finding that laparoscopic closure of the internal ring can effectively reduce the incidence of postoperative seroma and is suitable for type III indirect hernias or scrotal hernias. However, these studies had limitations such as the absence of a control group, different surgical techniques, short follow-up periods, and varied hernia sac management methods, leading to a certain degree of heterogeneity in the results. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective analysis of 162 patients with primary unilateral indirect inguinal hernias who underwent TAPP surgery at Kunshan First People’s Hospital from December 2023 to November 2024, to investigate the clinical efficacy of laparoscopic internal ring closure combined with hernia sac transection in the treatment of indirect inguinal hernias.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

A single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted at Kunshan First People’s Hospital, enrolling 162 adult patients with indirect inguinal hernias who underwent transabdominal preperitoneal (TAPP) inguinal hernia repair between December 2023 and November 2024. Patients were divided into two groups based on intraoperative surgeon assessment (non-randomized grouping): the closure group underwent internal ring closure combined with hernial sac excision to the level of the ring margin, while the non-closure group underwent hernial sac excision without internal ring closure. Closure was considered when extensive defects, thinning of the transversum abdominis membrane, and a large potential dead space were observed; The non-closure approach was selected if closure risked increased spermatic cord compression, excessive suture tension, or nerve traction. All procedures were performed by the same primary surgeon leading an experienced TAPP team. To minimize surgeon-driven allocation bias, predefined multivariate adjustments were implemented (see Statistical Methods). Inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in relevant sections. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The patient enrollment and allocation process is illustrated in Fig. 1. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (Approval No.: 2022-04-001-K01). To ensure patient confidentiality, all personal identifiers were removed from the dataset prior to analysis. Data was fully anonymized, and individual patients cannot be identified from the reported results. The requirement for written informed consent was waived because this study was a retrospective analysis using de-identified data, in accordance with the policy of the institutional ethics committee. Individuals were guaranteed the right to opt out.

Sample size

This study is a single-center retrospective study, and the sample size was primarily limited by the number of available cases during the study period. To enhance methodological transparency, we provide a theoretical sample size reference: in a two-independent-sample comparison framework, with a two-sided α = 0.05 and a power of 90%, and referencing previous literature, we set the postoperative seroma incidence in the control group at 30% and in the experimental group at 7%. The calculated required sample size is at least 58 cases per group. Considering that some medical records might be incomplete or not fully meet the inclusion criteria during the screening process, we allowed for an approximately 20% buffer, thus requiring at least 69 cases per group. In this study, 81 cases were included in each group, providing a sufficient sample size.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion Criteria: (1) Met the diagnostic criteria for indirect inguinal hernia according to the “International HerniaSurge guidelines for groin hernia management (updated version)“ [10]; (2) Age ≥ 18 years; (3) Intraoperative probe measurement of the internal ring defect diameter ≥ 3.0 cm (i.e., EHS L3); (4) No major organ dysfunction, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification(ASA) I-III, and preoperative assessment indicating tolerance for general anesthesia.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Recurrent hernia, direct hernia, bilateral inguinal hernia, incarcerated hernia, or strangulated hernia; (2) Presence of clear surgical contraindications; (3) History of surgery in the inguinal region accompanied by severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction, coagulation disorders, or other diseases, ASA ≥ IV, or inability to tolerate general anesthesia.

Surgical method

All patients underwent general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation. The surgical position was supine with a head-down tilt. Standard skin disinfection, draping, and preparation were performed.

Control group

A 10 mm incision was made at the superior edge of the umbilicus, and a 10 mm trocar was inserted. A pneumoperitoneum was established using a gas insufflator, maintaining an intra-abdominal pressure of 12 mmHg. A 5 mm trocar was inserted at the left midclavicular line below the umbilicus, and a 10 mm trocar was inserted at the right midclavicular line at the level of the umbilicus. Under laparoscopic guidance, the abdominal cavity was explored to confirm the absence of occult or combined hernias, and the diameter of the internal ring defect and the contents of the indirect inguinal hernia were assessed. The omentum was then reduced. The peritoneum was incised horizontally 2 cm above the neck of the hernia sac, extending medially from the anterior superior iliac spine. The preperitoneal space was dissected sharply, extending inferiorly to the Cooper’s ligament, medially to the midline, and laterally to the anterior superior iliac spine. The hernia sac was bluntly dissected and then transected approximately 3 cm distal to its neck, and the proximal sac was dissected (in male patients, the sac was completely separated from the spermatic cord, and the spermatic cord was fully parietalized; In female patients, the round ligament was preferentially preserved: after sac transection and internal ring closure, a short window was created lateral to the mesh to allow the ligament to pass anterior to the mesh; if a small mesh slit was required, its edges were sutured to avoid a keyhole defect. Division with double ligation of the round ligament at the level of the internal ring was reserved for situations in which preservation compromised mesh coverage or created undue tension. The femoral canal and Cooper’s ligament were routinely inspected to exclude an occult femoral hernia). A Bard 3DMAX mesh was fully flattened and placed in the preperitoneal space, covering the entire myopectineal orifice. The mesh was adequately fixed using biologic adhesive. After checking for active bleeding in the surgical field, the peritoneum was closed with a continuous 3 − 0 absorbable suture. The instruments, laparoscope, and trocars were sequentially withdrawn, the insufflator was turned off, and the skin incisions were sutured. Experimental group

In addition to the procedures performed on the control group, the internal ring was closed under laparoscopic guidance using the “five-stitch method.” A 3 − 0 absorbable barbed suture was used to complete the first three stitches: a reverse suturing technique was employed, with the needle entering from the outside to the inside, suturing the iliopubic tract and the conjoint tendon in a forward direction. The needle entry was perpendicular to the iliopubic tract, with three continuous stitches placed 8–10 mm apart. The fourth stitch was taken through the superficial fibers of the iliopubic tract (in male patients, passing superficial to the spermatic cord to prevent compression), and the needle was then passed through the transversalis fascia behind the inferior epigastric vessels, entering the retropubic space (space of Retzius) medial to the vessels. The fifth stitch was placed immediately adjacent to the exit point of the previous stitch, re-puncturing the transversalis fascia and taking a small bite of the conjoint tendon, with the needle exiting and returning to the lateral side of the inferior epigastric vessels. Finally, the suture was tightened to approximate the iliopubic tract, conjoint tendon, and transversalis fascia, completing the closure of the internal ring (as shown in Fig. 3). Observational content

The primary outcome was the incidence of clinical seroma, classified using the Morales-Conde system (grades 0–IV). Grade 0 denotes no clinical seroma, including asymptomatic collections detectable only by imaging; grade I, a clinical seroma lasting < 1 month; grade II, a clinical seroma persisting > 1 month; grade III, a seroma with minor related complications (symptomatic and potentially requiring intervention); and grade IV, a seroma with complications requiring treatment. Grades I–IV were considered clinical (symptomatic) seromas. Routine imaging was not performed in asymptomatic patients; therefore, grade-0 seromas may have been underestimated and were not counted toward the primary outcome, although incidental radiologic findings at nonstudy visits were recorded descriptively. At each follow-up, standardized physical examination and symptom inquiry were conducted. Ultrasonography was performed only when signs or symptoms were present, and seroma diagnosis required concordant clinical findings plus ultrasonographic evidence of a fluid collection, with aspiration confirmation when indicated. This approach prioritizes clinically meaningful seromas but may underestimate the true incidence of asymptomatic cases.

Secondary outcomes included perioperative parameters (operative time, incision length, blood loss, and length of hospital stay), other postoperative complications (pain, sensory disturbance, infection, urinary retention, and testicular edema), and the short-term recurrence rate. Postoperative pain was assessed using a 10-point visual analog scale (VAS) on postoperative day 1 (higher scores indicate greater pain). Chronic postoperative pain is defined as groin or surgical site pain lasting ≥ 3 months, presenting as bothersome or moderate to severe intensity, and impacting daily activities. Postoperative infection was defined as any surgical-site infection, including superficial (e.g., incisional or sinus-tract infection) and deep infections (e.g., mesh infection). Sensory disturbance after TAPP encompassed numbness, paresthesia, hypoesthesia, or persistent neuropathic pain attributable to intraoperative nerve traction, compression, or injury. Because the TAPP mesh covers the entire myopectineal orifice, any ipsilateral groin hernia detected during follow-up was considered a recurrence, including both true recurrences (same type and location) and pseudo-recurrences (missed or new hernias).

Follow-up and missing data

The prespecified follow-up assessments were scheduled for postoperative day 7 and at 1, 3, and 6 months. Missing values for the primary outcome were not imputed; instead, two sensitivity analyses were conducted. For covariates with ≤ 10% missingness, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was applied. The 6-month visit was used only for secondary endpoints; when missing, we reported complete-case analyses and performed inverse-probability weighting (IPW) sensitivity analyses.

Statistical methods

SPSS 27.0 statistical software was used for analysis. If baseline data were normally distributed, the results were presented as mean ± standard deviation; if skewed, the results were presented as median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were summarized as proportions. In univariate analyses, continuous variables were compared using the independent-samples t test or the Mann–Whitney U test, and categorical variables using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. To further analyze risk factors for seroma, binary logistic regression was performed. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Notably, Grade III seroma occurred exclusively in the non-closure group (3/81 vs. 0/81), while no Grade III-IV events were observed in the closure group (Table 3).

Discussion

Primary unilateral inguinal hernia is a common surgical condition, and Transabdominal Preperitoneal (TAPP) repair is currently one of the mainstream surgical approaches for its treatment [11]. For patients with a large internal ring defect (EHS L3), postoperative seroma is a major factor affecting prognosis [2]. Seromas located within the inguinal canal are classified as overt seromas, which often cause patient anxiety. Seromas located outside the inguinal canal are classified as occult seromas; these are easily overlooked by patients due to the lack of obvious clinical manifestations but can be detected via ultrasound examination. Previous studies have indicated that seroma formation is associated with chronic diseases, BMI, hernia sac management techniques, hernial ring defects, surgical trauma, patch materials, and fixation methods [12–14].

A previous randomized controlled trial demonstrated that suturing closed direct hernia defects can effectively reduce the incidence of postoperative seroma in patients with direct inguinal hernias without increasing the risks of postoperative infection, pain, or recurrence [5]. Additionally, Parviz K. Amid pointed out that in the Lichtenstein open hernia repair, tightening the internal ring or transecting the hernia sac can confine exudate to the preperitoneal space [15]. The stronger absorption capacity of the preperitoneal space allows for the rapid absorption of covert seromas, thereby reducing the incidence of postoperative seroma. This suggests that defect closure is another potential method for preventing postoperative seroma formation. However, for EHS L3 hernias with large internal ring defects, if the hernia sac is transected flush with the internal ring and the ring is sutured closed during surgery, the distal sac becomes a blind pouch that no longer communicates with the peritoneal cavity or the preperitoneal space. This predisposes to fluid accumulation. Therefore, we transect the hernia sac approximately 3 cm distal to the sac neck. Theoretically, this allows exudate to be widely absorbed along the loose tissue plane rather than accumulating in a blind pouch, effectively reducing the occurrence of seroma. Based on this, we proposed a hypothesis: in EHS L3 patients, implementing the “five-stitch method” to suture closed the internal ring concomitant with standardized distal hernia sac transection can further reduce the incidence of clinical seroma compared to traditional TAPP.

We conducted a retrospective analysis of 162 patients with primary unilateral indirect inguinal hernias and an internal ring defect diameter of ≥ 3.0 cm. The experimental group underwent internal ring closure using the five-stitch method, while the control group received traditional TAPP surgery. Both groups had the hernia sac transected, used the same type of mesh fixed with biological glue, and did not have drains placed. No patient in either group experienced conversion to open surgery, major intraoperative bleeding, or other unexpected events. The results of this study show that for EHS L3 hernias with an internal ring defect diameter of ≥ 3.0 cm, using the “five-stitch method” to close the internal ring during TAPP surgery can effectively reduce the incidence of postoperative seroma (P < 0.05). The potential mechanisms include: (1) Suturing the internal ring physically blocks the passage of exudate into the inguinal canal and scrotum. The loose tissue of the inguinal canal and scrotum has a significant reservoir effect and weaker absorption capacity, making clinically visible and symptomatic “overt seromas” more likely. If the exudate is confined to the preperitoneal space, which has a richer blood supply and lymphatic drainage and is more diffuse, the local clearance efficiency can be improved, causing it to manifest mostly as asymptomatic “covert seromas,” thereby significantly reducing the incidence of clinical seromas and the need for subsequent intervention. (2) For large internal ring defects, simply covering with a mesh is a bridging repair, and the mesh can easily bulge into the defect, forming a potential dead space. If we close the internal ring defect, the mesh becomes a reinforcing layer, reducing the potential space and theoretically decreasing the formation of postoperative seromas.

The addition of the “five-stitch method” to traditional TAPP resulted in a slightly longer operative time for the experimental group compared to the control group; however, the average extension was controlled within 10 min, having no substantial impact on anesthesia safety or recovery quality. The experimental group used monofilament barbed sutures to close the internal ring, leading to a slightly higher total surgical cost than the control group, but the difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). The lack of significant difference in hospital stay between the two groups suggests that the modified technique did not prolong hospitalization. EHS L3 hernias are more common in middle-aged and elderly patients with a longer disease course, and postoperative complications are relatively more frequent, primarily urinary retention and postoperative infection; these were effectively controlled after standardized symptomatic treatment. Additionally, three patients in the control group experienced seromas lasting longer than three months, all of whom had undergone aspiration therapy. Therefore, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups regarding postoperative length of stay and the incidence of other related complications. Patient overall satisfaction with the surgical outcome was high, indicating significant clinical application value.

Our results are highly consistent with those reported by Zhu and Binyu et al. [5, 16], confirming that suture closure of the internal ring defect significantly reduces the incidence of seroma in the early postoperative period (7 days and 1 month). Furthermore, utilizing the Morales-Conde classification, this study demonstrates that seromas in the experimental group were not only less frequent but also predominantly mild (Grade I/II), representing a significant improvement over the severe (Grade III) cases observed in the control group. Regarding surgical efficiency, although both this study and Binyu. observed that defect closure prolongs operative time, the average extension in our study was only approximately 3.5 min—significantly shorter than the 9 min reported by Binyu. This indicates that the time cost of this maneuver is minimal when performed by proficient surgeons. In terms of postoperative pain, our data corroborate the view of Zhu. that defect closure does not increase postoperative pain; however, we did not observe the “pain-alleviating” effect reported by Binyu, thereby confirming the safety of this technique without imposing additional patient distress. Notably, in contrast to Zhu, who observed significant differences persisting at 3 months, we found no statistically significant difference in seroma incidence between the two groups at 3 and 6 months postoperatively. This suggests that the core clinical value of this technique lies in significantly improving early postoperative quality of life and reducing the need for early aspiration intervention.

One of the barriers preventing the widespread clinical adoption of internal ring closure is postoperative pain. A previous prospective study indicated that the incidence and severity of postoperative pain are significantly positively correlated with the occurrence and volume of seroma [17]. A possible reason is that accumulated seroma fluid generates local tension, compressing surrounding tissues and nerve endings, thereby inducing or aggravating pain. It may also be related to nerve entrapment during suturing. During surgery, we are concerned about nerve and vascular injury. The anatomy of the internal ring region is complex, containing important nerves such as the iliohypogastric nerve, ilioinguinal nerve, genitofemoral nerve, and lateral femoral cutaneous nerve, as well as critical structures like the spermatic cord, inferior epigastric vessels, and iliac vessels. While the spermatic cord and vessels are easily distinguishable and injury can be avoided with careful suturing, the course of nerves in the internal ring region is more complex. We previously believed that the genitofemoral nerve and lateral femoral cutaneous nerve both emerge from below the iliopubic tract to the body surface. However, according to research by Reinpold et al. [18], the traversal range of the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve and the femoral branch of the genitofemoral nerve in the inguinal region varies greatly, reaching up to approximately 1–2 cm above the iliopubic tract. Furthermore, it is common for the femoral branch to emerge above, below, or through the iliopubic tract. Therefore, compared to the traditional Triangle of Pain, the true Triangle of Pain should be larger, and the area above the iliopubic tract is not an absolutely safe zone. The iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves are two other important nerves that run between the internal oblique and transversus abdominis muscles. Since TAPP is performed in the preperitoneal space, these two nerves are typically not visible during normal TAPP procedures. However, if the needle penetrates too deeply during internal ring suturing, it is possible to catch the iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves located superficial to the internal ring, causing acute or chronic postoperative pain. Therefore, cautious manipulation is required when suturing the internal ring. In the lower part of the suture plane, the tissue to be sutured is the iliopubic tract. As it is thin and superficial, small and shallow bites should be taken without crossing into the iliopsoas muscle to avoid muscle injury causing postoperative pain or limited mobility. In the upper part of the suture plane lies the conjoint tendon. Since the inguinal canal, containing the iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves, runs immediately below it, deep suturing can easily damage nerves, leading to postoperative pain or cutaneous paresthesia. Thus, the principle of “better shallow than deep” should also be followed during suturing, aiming only to achieve internal ring closure. When suturing near the medial side of the internal ring, because the iliopubic tract is deep and has indistinct boundaries making it difficult to suture, the conjoint tendon should be gently pulled medially towards the transversalis fascia during surgery. One or two U-shaped stitches are placed behind the inferior epigastric vessels to closely approximate the conjoint tendon and transversalis fascia, achieving tension-free closure. Simultaneously, the position and tension of the spermatic cord must be continuously observed while tightening the suture to prevent the suture from compressing the spermatic cord and causing compromised blood supply. Additionally, we perform suturing perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the internal ring defect, i.e., parallel to the direction of nerve travel. Theoretically, this maximizes the reduction of the likelihood of the suture needle directly piercing or cutting the nerve, thereby lowering the incidence of postoperative pain and sensory dysfunction. We chose monofilament barbed sutures intraoperatively, utilizing their advantages of knotless self-locking, uniform tension, and mid-term strength retention to achieve rapid continuous closure under laparoscopy, minimization of dead space, and stable approximation of cut edges, while providing support during the critical healing period to reduce the risk of seroma and adhesions. In this study, there was no significant difference in postoperative pain severity between the two groups, demonstrating good safety regarding nerve protection. However, according to the latest HerniaSurge guidelines, the ideal assessment period for chronic pain should be at least 12 months. Therefore, we will continue follow-up to draw more rigorous conclusions.

In terms of long-term outcomes, although the recurrence rate after TAPP surgery has been reduced to a very low level [19], exploring the role of internal ring closure in further preventing hernia recurrence is still of theoretical value. According to Laplace’s law, for a given mesh size, patients with large defects have a doubled probability of postoperative mesh displacement and protrusion compared to those with small defects. This is more likely to lead to hernia sac protrusion and recurrence when intra-abdominal pressure increases [20]. After closing the internal ring, the defect is smaller, which reduces the difficulty of mesh coverage and helps in proper mesh fixation, potentially preventing postoperative recurrence to some extent. In the short-term follow-up of this study, no difference in recurrence was observed between the experimental and control groups. Given the overall low recurrence rate after TAPP, the limited follow-up period, and the fact that this study did not fully control for potential recurrence-related factors (such as mesh type, fixation method, surgeon experience, etc.), we cannot infer the impact of internal ring closure on recurrence risk based on these data. Relevant conclusions still need to be verified by prospective studies with larger samples and long-term follow-up.

The “five-stitch method” is a technique for closing the internal ring. Mechanistically, the added maneuvers primarily involve superficial suturing and tissue approximation around the internal ring. Theoretically, once mastered, it imposes no significant cumulative burden on operative time or pain. However, mild time extension or a technical adaptation process may occur during the early learning phase. According to departmental training records, we believe that for surgeons already proficient in laparoscopic suturing, the “five-stitch method” typically requires an adaptation period of 8–12 cases, trends toward stability by the 12th–20th case, and enters a plateau phase around the 15th–25th case. Once proficient, the impact of the added steps on total operative time can generally be controlled within 5–10 min. As this study was completed by the same surgical team, internal consistency was strong, making outcome estimates more precise. However, this may still underestimate the early burden for beginners or low-volume centers, suggesting that future research should adopt multi-center, multi-surgeon designs with stratification by experience to verify external validity.

Conclusion

For patients with primary unilateral indirect inguinal hernias presenting with an internal ring defect diameter ≥ 3.0 cm, internal ring closure combined with hernia sac transection significantly reduces the incidence of seroma formation following transabdominal preperitoneal (TAPP) repair. During the formulation of the surgical strategy, concerns were raised that tension at the internal ring suture site might cause traction on surrounding nerves and tissues—potentially exacerbating postoperative pain—or result in compression of the spermatic cord and vas deferens. However, our results demonstrated no significant differences in postoperative pain scores between the groups, and no cases of testicular edema were observed; consequently, the safety of this technique has been preliminarily validated. Nevertheless, this study is subject to the inherent limitations of a single-center retrospective design. First, it is susceptible to selection bias, and baseline patient characteristics may exhibit non-random imbalances. Second, the procedures were primarily performed by a fixed surgical team; while the consistency of surgeon experience minimized operative variability, it suggests that the findings may be influenced by specific operative habits, limiting the direct generalizability of the results to surgical teams with varying levels of experience. Third, retrospective data collection relied on historical medical records, where postoperative follow-up details for some patients may have been incompletely recorded. This study employed a “complete case analysis” approach for missing data, including only samples with complete records. Although this minimized subjective imputation errors, it may have further reduced the sample size and introduced additional selection bias. Furthermore, this study did not analyze the applicability of this technique to European Hernia Society (EHS) L1 or L2 hernias, nor did it explore potential differences in efficacy across different EHS classifications. Additionally, the impact of the internal ring suturing technique on postoperative quality of life and cost-effectiveness was not evaluated. These shortcomings warrant addressal in future research. Based on the current findings, for patients with primary unilateral indirect inguinal hernias and an internal ring defect diameter ≥ 3.0 cm, we recommend intraoperative suture closure of the internal ring to reduce postoperative seroma formation, thereby improving patient prognosis. However, given the limitations of this study, these conclusions necessitate further validation through multi-center, large-sample, prospective randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to more scientifically establish the clinical value of our findings.
