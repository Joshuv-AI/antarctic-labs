Performance of four large language models in addressing family-centered questions on morbidity and outcomes in patients supported by extracorporeal membrane oxygenation
Authors: Wouter Vankrunkelsven, Leen Vercaemst, Dieter F Dauwe
Published: 2026

Main text

Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO) can provide life-saving temporary respiratory and/or cardiac support in the critically ill patient. The use of ECMO has increased exponentially with a peak of respiratory ECMO during the COVID-19 pandemic [1]. The growing use of ECMO is likely to be accompanied by an increasing demand from patients and their families for accessible and accurate medical information regarding indications, risks and expected outcomes. Patients and families often gather medical information from publicly available media, the internet and more recently ‘Large Language Models’ (LLMs). Fernando et al. previously demonstrated that media coverage of ECMO disproportionately emphasizes survival and favorable outcomes, diverging from data of large cohort studies and randomized trials [2]. Inspired by their findings, we investigated the overall performance of four commonly used LLMs in answering patient- and family-centered questions on morbidity and outcomes in adult ECMO-patients. We were particularly interested to investigate whether LLMs also provide an overly optimistic view of ECMO-outcomes.

We formulated four questions likely to be asked by family members. Google Trends identified ECMO as the principal search term, with limited use of the terms VenoArterial-ECMO (VA-ECMO) and VenoVenous-ECMO (VV-ECMO), reflecting limited awareness of this distinction among most family members. Candidate questions were subsequently compiled from three sources: the top 20 Google frequently asked questions related to ECMO, the top 25 ECMO-related search terms identified through Google Trends, and the most frequently asked questions by family on ECMO, generated by four LLMs (ChatGPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3, Gemini-2.0 Flash, and Claude-3.7 Sonnet). All authors reached consensus on four questions judged to best reflect typical family concerns (Table-1). These questions were presented to the previously mentioned LLMs between May 4–11, 2025 using free versions on default settings. All questions were queried once per LLM within the same ‘chat’-session. We compared the responses from LLMs to recent ELSO-registry data (2020–2024) [1] and review articles [3–5]. In addition, six ECMO-experts scored the responses using a seven-domain framework assessing content quality (accuracy, completeness, balance, relevance) and expert-perceived communicative quality (readability, empathy, appropriateness), each rated on a 5-point Likert scale. All LLMs acknowledged the high-risk nature of ECMO and independently differentiated between VV-ECMO, VA-ECMO and ECPR. While survival rates and ECMO-duration generally aligned with ELSO-registry data, the LLMs reported shorter VV-ECMO support. Interestingly, this was more representative for current VV-ECMO support durations and might give families a more representative picture. ELSO-registry’s mean duration might be inflated by inclusion of the COVID-19 pandemic within the sampled timeframe, a period marked by a surge in respiratory ECMO-cases with prolonged support [1]. All LLMs identified the most frequent complications, although some discrepancies in complication rates were observed when compared to the ELSO-registry. These differences likely reflect that LLMs and ELSO sample different datasets and definitions, and that neither is straightforwardly the truth that families should be told. Since no ELSO-registry comparator exist for quality-of-life, we referred to recent reviews [3–5]. All LLMs except for DeepSeek mentioned impairments related to the Post-Intensive Care Syndrome in physical health, mental health and cognitive domains, with reported incidence rates aligned with current literature.

Detailed assessment by six ECMO-experts of content and expert-perceived communicative quality is available in the Supplement. Overall performance of the different LLMs across the seven domains was significantly different (p < 0.001). Gemini achieved the highest average scores in both content and expert-perceived communicative quality, while DeepSeek consistently ranked lowest. Important however is the proportion of questions that yielded a satisfactory response (4/5 ‘agree’ or 5/5 ‘strongly agree’). Gemini answered 95% of the answers related to content quality satisfactory, ChatGPT 80%, Claude 71% and DeepSeek 61% (p < 0.001). Concerning expert-perceived communicative quality, 81% of Geminis answers were deemed satisfactory, followed by Claude (68%), ChatGPT (63%) and DeepSeek (58%) (p = 0.027). Mean percentage of agreement among the six ECMO-experts was 74.2±1.3% with an overall Kappa of 0.35±0.02, indicating fair inter-rater agreement. Inter-rater agreement was higher for communicative quality than for content quality (Supplement). All LLMs provided an explicit disclaimer to consult healthcare providers for individualized information.

A primary limitation is that all four questions were queried sequentially within a single chat session, which may artificially inflate internal coherence and disclaimer behavior. This approach was chosen to reflect typical user interactions involving follow-up questions within one conversation. A post-hoc sensitivity analysis using an independent ‘fresh’ chat session for each question, yielded comparable clinical content and disclaimer patterns. Additionally, because we evaluated free-tier versions over a one-week period in May 2025, our results capture a single snapshot within a rapidly evolving field. Since these LLMs will have been superseded by the time of publication, our findings reflect LLM-capabilities within the evaluated timeframe rather than stable model rankings.

In summary, the evaluated LLMs provided responses that are directionally consistent with available evidence, providing more balanced views than media portrayals. Both content quality and expert-perceived communicative quality varied across LLMs. A significant number of responses were scored unsatisfactory by the ECMO-experts. LLMs demonstrated lower performance on completeness and expert-perceived empathy. Clinicians should remain cautious and provide patients and families with individualized, evidence-based information. LLMs can be used as adjuncts in the information sharing process, but not as substitutes.
