Spatial access to inpatient mental health services in Kerala, India: a Geospatial Information System-based travel-time isochrone analysis
Authors: Deepak Thomas Varughese, Johvana Elizabeth Thomas, Jaico Katadiyil Paulose
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to estimate the spatial accessibility of inpatient mental health services in Kerala, India. We also aimed to calculate bed-to-population ratios for these services for each district in the state.DesignWe used a Geospatial Information System (GIS)-based travel time isochrone analysis to estimate potential spatial accessibility.SettingKerala, India.Data sources and methodsA list of all mental health facilities licensed to admit patients was obtained from data published by the Kerala State Mental Health Authority in 2025. The facilities were geocoded and mapped. Population data were also obtained using satellite-based estimates from 2020 at a resolution of 100 m. Information on Road Networks was obtained using OpenStreetMap through the OpenRouteService plugin in 2025.Outcome measuresWe calculated the proportion of people who could access facilities within 15 min, 30 min and 45 min. Bed-to-population ratios varied substantially between districts.Keywords: India, EPIDEMIOLOGY, PUBLIC HEALTH, Geographical mapping, Geographic Information System

Introduction

Mental disorders constitute 12%–15% of the total global burden of disease. Nearly one in six people live with a disability due to mental illness, and there has been no decrease in this burden over the last decade.1 In India, mental disorders affect nearly 150 million people; however, a vast treatment gap persists, with less than 20% of the population having access to adequate mental healthcare, representing one of the largest unmet needs globally.2 India has an estimated 0.75 psychiatrists per 100 000 population and an estimated 1.43 hospital beds available for mental health patients per 100 000 population.3

Kerala is a state of southern India. It leads the country with the highest life expectancy at birth and the lowest multidimensional poverty.4 However, the state has a ‘suicide-risk’ prevalence of 2.23%, which is nearly twice the national estimate in the National Mental Health Survey. The lifetime prevalence of any mental morbidity among adults in Kerala was estimated to be 14%.5 The prevalence of serious mental illness among older adults is estimated to be 1%. This is particularly significant as Kerala has the highest proportion of elderly people in India (16.5%), and this proportion is predicted to increase to 20.9% by 2031.6 Although access to health services is relatively better in Kerala, studies have pointed to inequities among disadvantaged communities, including Indigenous communities, persons with disabilities and migrants.7,9 Older adults with serious mental illness tend to belong to households with lower socioeconomic status.6

Another factor that has been poorly studied in terms of equity and access to health services is geographical location. Most existing health system studies in India tend to estimate access using metrics, such as doctor-to-population ratios or bed-to-population ratios. However, with modern Geospatial Information System (GIS) technology, the role of geographical location in access can now be explored further. Negaro et al showed that in the USA, rural residents face significantly longer driving times to mental healthcare, which corresponds to higher suicide rates.10 Cao et al found that only around a third of the population in Nepal had access to a tertiary public health facility within 30 min.11 Although these techniques exist, no geospatial analyses have been published on mental health services in Kerala.

Spatial accessibility can be broadly divided into potential spatial accessibility and revealed spatial accessibility.12 Potential spatial accessibility refers to the probable usage of a service by a needy population in space and time, which is commonly examined based on the evaluation of an existing system of service delivery. Revealed spatial accessibility refers to the actual use of a service, which may be reflected by the frequency of visits to services or individuals’ satisfaction level of using the services.13

In this study, we used GIS to analyse potential spatial accessibility of inpatient mental health services in Kerala using network analysis with travel time isochrones. We estimated the proportion of the population that could access mental health services in the 15-minute, 30-minute and 45-minute brackets. We also calculated traditional facilities for population measurements using current satellite-based population estimates and updated mental health facility data.

Methods

Study design

An isochrone map shows all areas reachable within a specified travel time using a chosen mode of transportation considering real world factors such as road traffic conditions. A description of the analysis has been given below. The analysis was done in R (V.4.5.1) and QGIS (V.3.40.9), both of which are free and open-source tools. This allows the technique to be reproduced without expensive licences. The results were reported using the International Initiative on Spatial Lifecourse Epidemiology Reporting Standards.14

Study area

This study was conducted in the southern Indian state of Kerala. Kerala is situated between the Arabian Sea to the west and the Western Ghats to the east, lying roughly between latitudes 8°18’N and 12°48’N and longitudes 74°52’E and 77°22’E. It has Tamil Nadu to the east and Karnataka to the north. It covers an area of 38 852 km2. The altitude of Kerala ranges from sea level to 2695 m above sea level. The state has a tropical monsoon climate with relatively high humidity and rainfall levels. Figure 1 shows a map of the study area.

Data sources

Mental health provider data

The National Mental Health Act of 2017 mandated the establishment of a mental health authority in every state. All mental health facilities in the state are required to register with the State Mental Health Authority. The Kerala State Mental Health Authority publishes details of all registered institutions. These data are available as a PDF file and contain variables, including the name of the institution, address, date of registration, number of available beds and facility category. All facilities were categorised as follows: (A) Psychiatric hospitals; (B) General hospitals with psychiatric units; (C) Rehabilitation centres; (D) De-addiction centres; (E) Psychiatric units with rehabilitation centres and (F) Psychiatric units with de-addiction centres. Only centres with a psychiatric unit were analysed for this study, as centres without a psychiatric unit usually do not provide access to a psychiatrist.

An updated list of establishments registered with the Kerala State Mental Health Authority was downloaded from the official website (www.ksmha.org) in March 2025. The information was entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. The address for each centre was geocoded manually to latitude-longitude coordinates using a free-to-use Google Maps tool. This was saved as a csv file. The csv file was then converted into a shapefile using the sf package in R. The data were projected using the geographical coordinate reference system WGS84 EPSG 4326. The newly generated points were then verified by reverse geocoding using the facility location on Google Maps.

A shapefile comprising the name of the facility, category of the facility, number of beds and coordinates was prepared for further analysis.

Population data

We obtained a 100 m × 100 m grid of population data for India from WorldPop.Org.15 16 The data set was based on 2020 estimates. Unconstrained estimates were used for the analysis. The data set was downloaded as a GeoTiff file. The projection was the geographical coordinate system, WGS84 EPSG 4326. This provided data on the number of people available per grid cell. This study included individuals of all age groups.

The zonal statistics tool in QGIS was used to obtain the population for each panchayat and taluk using shapefiles that contained the administrative boundaries for the panchayat and taluk, respectively.

Geographical boundary data

The smallest administrative unit in Kerala is the Ward. Wards make up local self-government institutions, also referred to as ‘Panchayats’. Panchayats are further clubbed to make up subdistrict units called ‘taluks’. Taluks are then clubbed to form districts, which constitute the state.

Panchayat shapefiles were obtained from the Socioeconomic High-resolution Rural-Urban Geographic Platform for India database maintained at https://www.devdatalab.org/shrug17 18 in 2025. Shapefiles were also obtained at district and state levels.

Outcome variables

Psychiatric inpatient beds per 100 000 population

Information on the population of each district was computed using the zonal statistics tool. The total number of beds in each district was computed from the mental health provider data set. The proportion between the number of beds and the population was calculated.

Proportions of the population living within 15-minute, 30-minute and 45-minute travel of inpatient mental health services

Mental health facilities in the state were plotted on a map using the coordinate reference system WGS84 EPSG 4326 projection. We then generated isochrones for 15 min, 30 min and 45 min of travel time by car from each hospital. This was performed using an application programming interface from the open-source routing engine available at openrouteservice.org. These services are based on the road networks available in OpenStreetMap and were accessed in 2025; the model has been described elsewhere.19 After the isochrones were generated, they were exported to their own layers. The Dissolve tool was then used to prevent overlapping between the isochrones and generate a layer that could represent the areas that could be reached within 15 min, 30 min and 45 min of travel. The Difference tool was then used to remove areas of overlap and to divide the isochrones into four mutually exclusive areas: those that could be reached within 15 min, those that could be reached between 15 min and 30 min, those that could be reached between 30 min and 45 min, and those that required more than 45 min. Each layer was saved as a vector file. The zonal statistics tool was used to count the number of people in each zone. This was performed by extracting the sum of the population in each zone from the population raster file based on the vector file of the relevant zones.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Psychiatric inpatient beds per 100 000 population

According to the Kerala State Mental Health Authority data, there are 155 psychiatric units with inpatient facilities. This amounts to 7083 beds. This figure does not include registered mental health facilities designated as rehabilitation and deaddiction centres that do not have a psychiatric unit. Population estimates were obtained from satellite data, as described above. The overall population of Kerala is 33 570 218. Ernakulam was the most populous district, whereas Wayanad was the least populous.

Overall, the state has 21 inpatient psychiatric beds for every 100 000 people. The distribution of beds is presented in table 1. There was a wide variance in the inpatient bed-to-population ratio, ranging from 0.75 beds per 100 000 population in Kasargod to 68 beds per 100 000 population in Idukki. Of these, six districts were below the state figure of 21, with three of these districts (Kasargod, Kannur and Malappuram) having fewer than 10 beds per 100 000 population. Four districts (Idukki, Ernakulam, Pathanamthitta and Thiruvananthapuram) have more than 30 beds per 100 000 population. A map showing all psychiatric units in Kerala is presented in figure 2. The map also shows the distribution of population in Kerala.

Discussion

In this study, we mapped all psychiatric units that provide inpatient services in Kerala. We measured access to psychiatric inpatient services, both in terms of beds per 100 000 population as well as through driving time isochrones, using OpenStreetMap.

Various approaches have been used to assess potential spatial access to healthcare. They vary in complexity. The most basic models include population-to-provider ratios and nearest neighbour analyses. Travel time analyses account for road networks. Gravity-based models such as a two-step floating catchment area and the enhanced two-step floating catchment area additionally also account for supply factors such as number of doctors or number of beds.12 20 21 Recent literature has described the three-step floating catchment area method.22 23 Others such as Raeesi et al have used composite indexes along with econometric analyses such as the Gini and Lorenz coefficient for an even more robust analysis.24

We used two approaches in this study. We calculated beds per 100 000 population as well as a travel time isochrone analysis. Travel time isochrone analysis is largely supply insensitive. However, we chose this design because of the paucity of data regarding the number of doctors per facility. We chose to keep the model simple and limited to travel time. Service capacity was evaluated separately using a population-to-bed ratio to provide a pragmatic overview of the resource landscape.

However, there are challenges in estimating facility-to-population ratios in India. The last population census was conducted in 2011. However, in this study, we used satellite-based estimates for the population, which provides a robust estimate of the current population. The population estimates used for this analysis were for 2020 but were the best available estimates to our knowledge.

All psychiatric units offering inpatient services require registration, which ensures that this information is largely complete. This has helped us present a fairly reliable estimate of the facility-to-population ratio. We also obtained information on the bed strength of each facility, which allowed us to calculate bed-to-population ratios. While we were unable to independently verify the number of beds or the presence of a psychiatrist at the centre, the State Mental Health Authority verified these at the time of registration.

Kerala has 21 psychiatric inpatient beds per 100 000 population. Of these, Kasargod district has the lowest with 0.7 beds per 100 000 population. However, one must keep in mind that Kasargod is the northernmost district of Kerala and borders the Dakshina Kannada district in Karnataka. Dakshina Kannada district is an educational hub with multiple teaching hospitals. Kasargod is well connected to Dakshina Kannada by road, which may provide access to mental health services. Idukki had the highest ratio, with almost 60 beds per 100 000 population. However, Idukki has only eight facilities, with 450 of the 756 beds located in a single facility. In contrast, Ernakulam has 1098 beds divided among 34 facilities. While both approaches may be beneficial, it is interesting to illustrate how the spread of beds across the district may affect accessibility.

The required number of psychiatric beds per population is highly debatable as it has serious resource implications. Mundt et al used the Delphi method to develop an expert consensus on the minimum and optimal number of psychiatric inpatient beds.25 54% of this panel were from low-income and middle-income countries. Forty countries from six WHO regions were included in this study. The panel recommended that 60 psychiatric beds per 100 000 population were considered optimal and 30 beds were the minimum. 25–30 beds were considered mild shortages, 15–25 beds were considered moderate shortages and less than 15 beds were considered severe shortages. Based on this classification, one district was classified as optimal, three districts met the minimum standards but were not optimal, two districts had mild shortages, four districts had moderate shortages and four had severe shortages.

We also used driving time isochrone analysis to assess access.26 A total of 67.95% of the population has spatial access to inpatient psychiatric services within an estimated 15 min by road. This figure rose to almost 96.85% of the population with access to roads within 45 min. Driving-time isochrone analysis is a better technique than simply calculating the distance. This is because drive-time isochrones account for actual road networks and are not merely areal ‘as the crow flies’ distance. The technique we used assessed roadways based on the road network available in OpenStreetMap, which is an open-source mapping service. The model assumes fixed speeds for certain road types; for example, highways may have a faster assumed speed, and roads labelled as dirt roads will have lower assigned speeds. While this is certainly an improved estimate from calculating distance alone, it may not consider other factors, such as traffic congestion or broken roads, and access to personal vehicles, which often affect travel times in the low- and middle-income country context. However, this technique is still useful for estimating travel time.

In this study, we combined several data sets. Facility data, accurate as of 2025, were obtained from the State Mental Health Authority, and road network data were sourced from OpenStreetMap via the OpenRouteService tool using 2025 maps, while population data relied on 2020 estimates from WorldPop.org. Although a 5-year gap exists between the population estimates and the 2025 data sets, we believe that this temporal mismatch will not meaningfully affect our findings on spatial accessibility. Local population shifts over this period are minimal at the geographical scale of our analysis, as demographic patterns typically exhibit high stability over 5-year intervals. However, we acknowledge this temporal mismatch as a limitation of the study.

Notably, this study only aimed to assess potential spatial accessibility. Potential spatial accessibility refers to the theoretical or possible ease of access to healthcare. It is based on spatial factors such as the proximity of healthcare providers to the population, travel times estimated from geographical data, road networks and transportation infrastructure. It represents the opportunity or possibility that people have to access healthcare services, regardless of whether they actually use them. This can be very different from ‘realized access’ which depends on various spatial and aspatial factors. Other factors include approachability (extent to which the service is known and engaged with), affordability and appropriateness (whether services provide care that the population needs).27 This study aimed to assess only spatial factors. To effectively guide policy planning and prioritisation, it is essential to combine these factors with geographical accessibility to identify existing gaps.

The spatial model for the border districts of Kasargod may be underestimated. This is due to the edge effect as the people of Kasargod may be able to seek care across the border from Karnataka. Future studies could incorporate dynamic catchment area mapping to address this problem.28 However, it is important to note that health is largely a state subject in India and hence understanding access within Kerala is of value to policymakers.

This study facilitates the rapid identification of mental health facilities on a map by policymakers and researchers while also providing detailed information on the level of access available in each district. This approach assists in prioritising services based on actual needs rather than assumptions.

Our study offers a pragmatic initial estimation for spatial access. There is scope for more nuanced techniques in future. Using newer gravity-based models such as three-stage floating catchment area techniques or composite indexes can help account for supply and competition. In this study we used gridded WorldPop Data. However, there is an increasing scope for geospatial machine learning algorithms to increase the accuracy of the model in future studies.29 These approaches can be used in future studies.

We intend to incorporate these analyses into future research. Additionally, we plan to integrate aspatial elements, such as stigma and cost, into the model in the future, which could represent a promising direction for further research.

Conclusion

Kerala has an average of 21 beds for mental health per 100 000 population. This varies between 0.75 beds per 100 000 in Kasargod to 68 beds per 100 000 population in Idukki. A total of 67.95% of the population has spatial access to inpatient psychiatric services within an estimated 15 min by road. This figure rose to almost 96.85% of the population with access to roads within 45 min.
