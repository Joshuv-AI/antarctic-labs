Association between weekend catch-up sleep and stroke among US adults: A cross-sectional analysis of NHANES
Authors: Yue Fei, Xinhong Li, Xinlong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the association between weekend catch-up sleep (WCS) and stroke among US adults. Data from the 2017 to 2020 and 2021 to 2023 cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey were analyzed, including 11,134 participants aged ≥20 years. Sleep duration and stroke status were obtained from self-reported questionnaires. WCS was calculated as the difference between weekend and weekday sleep duration. Participants were categorized into 4 groups: ≤0 hours, 0 to <2 hours, 2 to <3 hours, and ≥3 hours. Multivariable logistic regression models, subgroup analyses, interaction tests, and restricted cubic spline analyses were performed to evaluate the association between WCS and stroke. In the fully adjusted model, participants with WCS > 0 hours had a lower likelihood of stroke compared with those with WCS ≤ 0 hours (adjusted odds ratio = 0.78, 95% confidence interval: 0.62–0.97, P = .0268). Among the categorized groups, only WCS of 2 to <3 hours was significantly associated with a lower likelihood of stroke (odds ratio = 0.60, 95% confidence interval: 0.39–0.94, P = .0251). Subgroup analyses showed that this association remained significant among women, individuals aged 36–64 years, and those with a history of alcohol consumption. A significant interaction was observed for age (P for interaction = .0385). WCS was associated with a lower likelihood of stroke among US adults, particularly when WCS duration was between 2 and 3 hours.

1. Introduction

Stroke, also known as a cerebrovascular accident, is an acute neurological event caused by the sudden interruption of cerebral blood flow, leading to neuronal injury and functional impairment.[1] It remains one of the leading causes of death and long-term disability worldwide, posing a substantial public health burden.[2] According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019, stroke accounted for over 12 million new cases, 6.5 million deaths, and more than 143 million disability-adjusted life years globally.[3] In the United States, approximately 800,000 individuals experience a new or recurrent stroke each year, with notable disparities across demographic and socioeconomic groups.[4] Although traditional risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and smoking are well established, growing evidence suggests that sleep-related behaviors may also play an important role in cerebrovascular health.[5,6]

In modern societies, chronic sleep deprivation has become increasingly prevalent, with more than one-third of adults reporting insufficient sleep during weekdays.[7] As a compensatory behavior, many individuals engage in weekend catch-up sleep (WCS), defined as extended sleep duration on non-workdays compared with weekdays.[8] Unlike habitual sleep duration, WCS reflects a dynamic pattern of sleep compensation and circadian variability. This pattern may have specific implications for cerebrovascular health. On one hand, moderate WCS may partially counteract the adverse physiological effects of sleep debt by reducing systemic inflammation, improving endothelial function, and restoring autonomic balance and metabolic homeostasis – pathways closely linked to stroke development.[9–11] On the other hand, excessive or irregular catch-up sleep may indicate circadian misalignment and sleep fragmentation, which have been associated with adverse vascular outcomes, including increased arterial stiffness, impaired glucose metabolism, and elevated blood pressure variability.[12,13] Together, these findings suggest that the relationship between WCS and cerebrovascular health may be complex and potentially nonlinear.

However, the relationship between WCS and stroke has not been well established, and existing evidence remains limited. Most prior studies have focused on overall sleep duration or sleep quality, with relatively little attention to compensatory sleep behaviors such as WCS. Therefore, whether WCS is associated with stroke risk and the potential shape of this association remain unclear.

Given these gaps in the literature, further investigation is warranted. Therefore, this study utilized data from the 2017 to 2023 cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to examine the association between WCS and the prevalence of stroke among US adults. We hypothesized that different durations of WCS may be associated with stroke prevalence, and we further explored potential effect modification across demographic and behavioral subgroups.

2. Methods

2.1. Study population

The NHANES, conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics, is an ongoing nationwide program designed to evaluate the health and nutritional status of the civilian, noninstitutionalized US population. It employs a complex, multistage, stratified probability sampling design and is conducted in biennial cycles to ensure nationally representative estimates. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study protocols were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board. Additional details are available at: https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/Default.aspx.

From the selected NHANES cycles, a total of 27,493 individuals were initially identified. Participants were excluded sequentially if they lacked sleep duration data (n = 9065), had missing information on stroke status (n = 1682), or were pregnant or had missing key covariates (n = 5612). After applying these criteria, 11,134 participants remained and were included in the final analysis. The participant selection process is presented in Figure 1.

Flowchart of participant selection. The NHANES 2017 to 2020 and 2021 to 2023 cycles initially included 27,493 participants. Following the application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, 11,134 participants were ultimately retained for the final analysis. NHANES = National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey.

2.2. Definition of stroke

Stroke status was ascertained using self-reported data from the Medical Condition Questionnaire. Participants were classified as having a history of stroke if they responded “yes” to the question, “Has a doctor or other health care professional ever told you that you had a stroke?” Those who answered “no” were categorized as not having a history of stroke.

2.3. Evaluation of WCS

WCS was assessed using data from the Sleep Disorders Questionnaire in the 2017 to 2020 and 2021 to 2023 NHANES cycles. Participants reported their typical sleep duration on weekdays or workdays (variable SLD012) and on weekends or non-workdays (variable SLD013). In this study, WCS was defined as a longer sleep duration on weekends compared with weekdays.[14,15]

2.4. Identification of covariates

Covariates were selected based on prior literature and potential clinical relevance to both sleep patterns and stroke risk. The following variables were included: age, sex, race/ethnicity (Mexican American, Other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and Other), marital status (living with a partner vs living alone), education level (less than high school, high school, and above high school), and poverty income ratio (PIR). In addition, health-related factors were incorporated, including body mass index (BMI), alcohol consumption, sedentary behavior, smoking status, diabetes, and hypertension.

PIR was divided into 3 categories: <1.3, 1.3 to 3.5, and >3.5.

BMI was classified as <25, 25 to 30, and ≥30.

Diabetes was determined by an glycated hemoglobin level of 6.5% or higher, a self-reported diagnosis, use of antidiabetic medications, or fasting plasma glucose ≥7 mmol/L. Hypertension was defined as an average systolic blood pressure of at least 140 mm Hg, a mean diastolic blood pressure of at least 90 mm Hg, or a self-reported physician diagnosis.

Alcohol consumption was ascertained through 2 survey questions: ALQ121 inquired about the frequency of alcohol use in the past 12 months, while ALQ131 addressed the typical number of drinks consumed on drinking days during the same period. Individuals reporting at least 12 alcoholic beverages per year were considered drinkers.

Smoking status was assigned to those with a history of consuming at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime.

Sedentary behavior was evaluated using responses to the NHANES physical activity questionnaire, which captured average daily sedentary time (minutes per day) involving activities such as sitting, watching TV, or computer use.

2.5. Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses accounted for the complex sampling design of NHANES by applying appropriate sample weights, strata (SDMVSTRA), and primary sampling units (SDMVPSU), thereby ensuring nationally representative estimates and valid variance calculations. Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations, whereas categorical variables are expressed as proportions.

To evaluate the association between WCS and stroke, 3 logistic regression models were constructed in a stepwise manner. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 adjusted for demographic characteristics, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, education level, and marital status. Model 3 further incorporated clinical and socioeconomic variables, including PIR, BMI, alcohol consumption, smoking status, hypertension, and diabetes. Restricted cubic splines were applied to address potential nonlinearity. Stratified multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed according to sex, age group, alcohol consumption, hypertension, and diabetes to explore possible effect modifications. Subgroup analyses were additionally conducted to evaluate the relationship between WCS and stroke, as well as clinical indicators. Two sensitivity analyses were performed to test the robustness of the results. In addition, multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with VIF values <5 indicating no evidence of significant multicollinearity.

Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value <.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.3 (https://www.R-project.org; The R Foundation) and Empower software (www.empowerstats.com; X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston).

3. Results

3.1. Characteristics of study participants

Table 1 shows the characteristics of adults with and without WCS. The study included 11,134 participants, with 6158 in the non-WCS group and 4976 in the WCS group. The WCS group was significantly younger, with 28.88% aged ≤35 years compared with 17.02% in the non-WCS group, and a smaller proportion aged ≥65 years (13.87% vs 39.38%, P < .0001). There were no significant differences in gender distribution (P = .1003). Significant racial/ethnic differences were observed (P < .0001), with the WCS group having higher proportions of Mexican American and other Hispanic participants and a lower proportion of non-Hispanic White participants. The WCS group was also more likely to have higher education and income levels (both P < .0001), and a greater percentage were married or living with a partner (63.58% vs 60.69%, P < .0001). Clinically, participants with WCS had lower rates of hypertension (26.59% vs 37.44%), diabetes (8.95% vs 13.63%), stroke (1.96% vs 4.80%), and smoking (37.50% vs 45.01%; all P < .0001). Alcohol intake was slightly more common in the WCS group (P = .0033). No significant difference was found in sedentary activity (P = .2100). Notably, the WCS group reported shorter weekday sleep duration (7.16 ± 1.31 hours) but longer weekend sleep (8.90 ± 1.40 hours) compared with non-WCS participants (both P < .0001). 3.2. Association between WCS and stroke risk

The association between WCS and stroke risk was evaluated using 3 distinct models, each adjusting for a range of potential confounders. As shown in Table 2, a significant inverse relationship was observed between WCS and the risk of stroke across all models. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), individuals with any WCS (>0 hours) had lower odds of stroke compared with those without WCS (odds ratio [OR] = 0.78, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.62–0.97, P = .0268). Subgroup analysis by WCS duration revealed a similar pattern, with participants obtaining 2 to <3 hours of WCS exhibiting a reduced stroke risk relative to those with ≤0 hours (OR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.39–0.94, P = .0251). All covariates included in the fully adjusted model had VIFs below 5 (maximum VIF = 3.65), indicating no evidence of significant multicollinearity.

3.3. Nonlinear relationships

The analysis revealed a nonlinear association between WCS duration and stroke. As illustrated in Figure 2, both insufficient and excessive sleep compensation were associated with a higher prevalence of stroke, whereas moderate levels of catch-up sleep were associated with a lower prevalence.

3.4. Subgroup analyses

As shown in Figure 3, a stronger inverse association was observed among female participants (OR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.50–0.91, adjusted P = .0132) and individuals with a history of alcohol consumption (OR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.56–0.94, P = .0196). A significant interaction by age was detected (P for interaction = .0385), with the association being more pronounced among individuals aged 36–64 years (OR = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.34–0.74, P = .0013).

3.5. Sensitivity analysis

The results of the sensitivity analyses, including the exclusion of participants with extreme sleep durations and a parsimonious model with established stroke covariates, further confirmed the stable and robust association between WCS and stroke risk (Tables S1 and S2, Supplemental Digital Content 1).

4. Discussion

This large, nationally representative cross-sectional study is among the first to evaluate the association between WCS and stroke prevalence in US adults using NHANES data from 2017 to 2023. Our findings suggest that moderate WCS, particularly between 2 and 3 hours, is associated with a lower likelihood of stroke, with the strongest associations observed in women, individuals aged 36–64 years, and those with a history of alcohol consumption.

Previous research has highlighted that insufficient sleep leads to hypertension, atherosclerosis, increased inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation, all of which are recognized risk factors for stroke.[16–18] Prior work has established that aberrant sleep duration – either excessively short or prolonged – is associated with increased cardiovascular morbidity, including stroke.[5,19–21] However, most existing studies have focused on average sleep duration or quality rather than compensatory sleep patterns such as WCS. Our results add to the emerging literature suggesting that sleep recovery on weekends may be related to a reduction in the adverse effects of accumulated weekday sleep debt.[22,23] Recent studies have further examined compensatory sleep behaviors and cardiometabolic outcomes. For example, Åkerstedt et al reported that extended sleep on non-working days may attenuate mortality risk associated with short weekday sleep,[24] while Nishiyama et al demonstrated associations between irregular sleep patterns and adverse cardiometabolic profiles.[25] These findings support the notion that WCS may have complex and potentially beneficial associations depending on its duration and context. Biological plausibility for this relationship includes evidence that intermittent sleep extension can improve metabolic balance, reduce systemic inflammation, and ameliorate vascular endothelial dysfunction.[26–28]

The use of restricted cubic spline modeling in our analysis revealed a nonlinear relationship between WCS duration and stroke risk, indicating that both extreme deficiency and excess in WCS are associated with elevated risk. This “U-shaped” pattern is consistent with prior research on total sleep duration and stroke mortality,[29,30] suggesting a potential threshold beyond which the association may differ, with moderate sleep compensation rather than large deviations from habitual sleep.

Subgroup analyses indicated that the inverse association was particularly significant among women, middle-aged individuals (36–64 years), and participants with a history of alcohol intake. Sex-specific differences in sleep physiology, endocrine function, and vascular risk may help explain the stronger association seen in women.[31] Similarly, middle-aged adults may be more vulnerable to the consequences of sleep debt due to higher occupational and social stress. Moderate WCS may represent a behavioral pattern associated with lower stroke prevalence in this group, potentially reflecting compensation for chronic partial sleep restriction.[24,32] Among individuals with a history of alcohol use, moderate catch-up sleep may be particularly relevant due to known sleep fragmentation and circadian disruption related to alcohol.[33]

While these results provide novel insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, participants with missing data were excluded; although the proportion of missingness was relatively low, this complete-case approach may introduce selection bias if the data were not missing completely at random. In addition, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded, as unmeasured or imprecisely measured factors (e.g., sleep apnea, sleep quality, depression, and other lifestyle factors) may have influenced the observed associations. Second, the cross-sectional nature of NHANES precludes causal inference and raises the possibility of reverse causality; additionally, the use of ORs may overestimate the strength of associations, and thus effect sizes should be interpreted with caution. Third, sleep duration and stroke status were self-reported, which may be subject to recall bias or misclassification. Finally, the generalizability of these findings beyond the United States may be limited.

5. Conclusion

Moderate WCS is associated with a lower prevalence of stroke in US adults, particularly among women and middle-aged individuals. These findings highlight a potential link between balanced sleep compensation and cerebrovascular health, although causal relationships cannot be inferred due to the cross-sectional design. Clinicians may consider incorporating sleep patterns into stroke risk assessments, while public health efforts could promote awareness of healthy sleep behaviors. Further longitudinal and mechanistic studies are needed to clarify causality and better define the role of WCS in stroke risk.
