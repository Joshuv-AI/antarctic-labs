ABSTRACT
The Arctic, with nearly 1,700 billion metric tons of carbon stored in its soils, is experiencing
significant warming trends that raise concerns about the potential release of greenhouse gases,
particularly methane. This report investigates the conversion of carbon stored in Arctic soils
and permafrost into methane and its subsequent atmospheric release over the coming decades.
Recent data indicate a rise in global methane concentrations, highlighting the importance of under-
standing the mechanisms of methane generation under anaerobic conditions prevalent in thawing
permafrost. Our study employs a multi-faceted approach that integrates fieldwork, benchtop exper-
iments, and numerical simulations to explore the subsurface hydrological-thermal-biogeochemical
systems influencing methane emissions. We focus on the relationship between microbial activity
in decomposing organic matter under oxygen-limited conditions and the transport of methane out
of these saturated wetland soils. Key hydrological parameters, including permeability, porosity,
and thermal conductivity, were measured on soil samples from Fairbanks, Alaska, and used to pa-
rameterize PFLOTRAN, a coupled hydrological-thermal-reactive transport simulator. Our model
enables prediction of the evolution of the active layer and its impact on methane production and
release. Our findings underscore the critical need for a mechanistic understanding of methane dy-
namics in permafrost environments to improve predictions of methane emissions in Earth systems
models.

1. INTRODUCTION

Nearly 1,700 billion metric tons (1,700 Pg) of carbon are stored in Arctic soils [87]. Recent warming
trends in the Arctic have raised concerns about the decomposition of newly-thawed organic matter,
which may become available for microbial activity. This could lead to increased releases of methane
and carbon dioxide into the atmosphere, potentially triggering a feedback loop between warming
and carbon release. While microbial respiration of organic matter into carbon dioxide has been
well studied and incorporated into global climate models, our mechanistic understanding of the
anaerobic processes that govern methane generation and its subsequent release into the atmosphere is
still developing. Methane is particularly concerning in permafrost environments, where microbial
activity thrives in the anaerobic conditions created by permafrost thaw and groundwater flow.
Despite its shorter residence time in the atmosphere, methane is a potent greenhouse gas with a
Global Warming Potential of almost 30 times that of carbon dioxide over 100 years [73]. This
highlights the critical need to understand how methane is generated and transported through the
soil and into the atmosphere.

This report addresses the scientific question of how much carbon stored in permafrost soils will be
converted into methane and subsequently released into the atmosphere over the next several decades.
Recent estimates of global mean methane concentrations indicate a rise in emissions, reaching 1,934
ppb in 2023 – an increase of 11 ppb from the previous year. This rise is accompanied by a relative
shift in 𝛿13 C𝐶𝐻4 values, suggesting an increase in microbial emissions [146]. Approximately 40%
of atmospheric methane originates from natural sources [127], with an estimated 35 Tg CH4 yr−1
emitted from wetlands [117]. The Arctic is particularly susceptible to wetland expansion due to
thermokarst formation [30] and topographically driven groundwater flow, which can further expand
wetlands in Arctic lowlands [52]. Developing a mechanistic understanding of methane generation
and transport in permafrost soils is crucial for accurately predicting the methane source term in
Earth systems models, which has been an ongoing challenge [33].

This problem is addressed through a multi-faceted approach that integrates fieldwork, benchtop
experiments, and numerical simulations. Our conceptual model assumes the following:

 • Arctic soils contain sufficient carbon to not be a limiting factor in methane releases.

 • Methanogens exist in all soils and just require soil temperatures above freezing and anaerobic
 (water saturated) conditions to become active.

 • Methane generation can be simulated as a one-way reaction converting dissolved organic
 carbon into dissolved methane.

 • Evapotranspiration is the main driver of methane out of saturated subsurface soils.

 13

Our focus is on the subsurface hydrological-thermal-biogeochemical system and its effects on
methane release into the atmosphere. Methane production and consumption in soil systems
are biologically mediated processes governed by redox conditions, substrate availability, and
microbial community composition. In Arctic wetlands and thawing permafrost, anaerobic mi-
crobes—primarily methanogens—decompose organic matter into methane under oxygen-limited
conditions, which occur in saturated soils.
Key hydrological parameters that govern the thermal-hydrological behavior of the subsurface in-
clude permeability, porosity, and thermal conductivity of permafrost and unfrozen soils, initial soil
temperature profiles with depth, and time varying boundary conditions such as surface tempera-
tures. Methane production and consumption in soil systems are simulated as biologically mediated
processes governed by redox conditions, substrate availability, and microbial community composi-
tion. Intrinsic and relative permeability as well as porosity measurements were conducted on soil
samples collected near Smith Lake, Fairbanks, Alaska by researchers at the University of Texas
(UT), Austin. In addition, incubation experiments were performed on these soil samples to measure
how much methane and carbon dioxide could be produced under carefully controlled anaerobic
conditions as a function of temperature [115]. Additional field measurements of soil temperatures
with depth were collected at several locations in Alaska by researchers at the University of Alaska,
Fairbanks (UAF).
All these data were used to parameterize and calibrate PFLOTRAN, a coupled hydrological-
thermal-reactive transport model. Borehole temperature data over time were used to calibrate a
numerical model of heat and fluid transfer in the subsurface to better understand the evolution and
extent of the active layer over the year, and to project its behavior into the future. A reactive transport
model dependent on hydrological, thermal, and geochemical conditions was developed and coupled
to the thermal-hydrological process model to estimate methane emissions from present-day to the
end of the 21st century.

1.1. Background

Three interrelated aspects of permafrost systems influence methane emissions: microbial reactions,
biologic and hydrogeologic environments and transport mechanisms. Microbial gas generation and
transport to the atmosphere are both dependant on and controlled by the biologic and hydrogeologic
environment. This work largely focuses on terrestrial water saturated wetland environments, where
the highest methane emissions are typically found. Below we will review review existing regional
studies of Arctic greenhouse gas emissions, present the key biologic and hydrogeologic land classes
used in this study, review subsurface transport mechanisms and finally present the conceptual model
for this study.

1.1.1. Regional studies of greenhouse gas emissions in the Arctic

Regional studies of greenhouse gas emissions can broadly be categorized into bottom-up and
top-down studies [116]. Top-down studies typically consist of inverse modeling of observations
of atmospheric concentrations to estimate regional methane emissions, while bottom-up studies

 14

estimate emissions using process-based modeling [65]. Top-down estimates of natural methane
emissions from the high northern-latitude regions of North America include between 16.6 and
17.9 Tg yr−1 from 2005-2010 (>50° N) [132], 31 ± 15 Tg yr−1 (>30° N) [151], and 16 Tg yr−1
[13]. Wetland methane emissions from the Arctic and boreal regions using top-down estimation
methods include 19 ± 13 Tg yr−1 [132] and 44.80 ± 19 Tg yr−1 in 2012 [109].
Bottom-up studies use a synthesis of measurements applied to an estimate of the spatial extent of
that environment. For example, Treat et al. [133] synthesized growing season and nongrowing
season methane flux measurements to develop a data-constrained process-based model that esti-
mated wetland methane emissions (>40° N) to be 37 ± 7 Tg yr−1 . Upscaling of eddy covariance
observations has been used along with maps of wetlands, and carbon and nitrogen cycling process
models to estimate annual wetland emissions (>45° N) ranging from 24.7 to 37.6 Tg yr−1 [106].
Though top-down and bottom-up estimates are of the same order of magnitude, since these studies
use different latitudes and partitioning to define the area of interest, direct comparison between
methods can be challenging.

1.1.2. Microbial reactions

Microorganisms convert organic carbon in soils to both carbon dioxide and methane under the
right conditions. Organic carbon can be processed and cycled through several microbial path-
ways with implications for both the compositions and rates of production. The pathway that
predominates is controlled by the availability of terminal electron acceptors (TEAs). Oxygen is
the most energetically favorable TEA, followed by iron and manganese in anaerobic conditions,
while methanogenesis occurs when higher-energy TEAs are depleted [82]. At colder temperatures,
homoacetogens outcompete hydrogenotrophic methanogens for hydrogen, despite being less ther-
modynamically favorable [20, 63, 62, 64, 153]. Homoacetogenesis occurs in a variety of ecosystems
under thermodynamically unfavorable conditions, but the exact mechanisms which allow this are
uncertain [20, 45, 153].
When permafrost thaws, especially in scenarios of abrupt thaw, soil columns subside and can result
in anoxic, water-saturated conditions [123, 136]. When these conditions are prolonged, higher-
energy TEAs will be depleted, leading to methanogenic reactions [16, 71, 135, 141]. Dry soils,
in contrast, experience aerobic microbial reactions and fluctuating soil moisture conditions create
periods of oxic and anoxic conditions. This allows for TEAs such as iron and manganese to be
oxidized during the oxic period, which can then be reduced during the anoxic period [61, 82, 135].
Thus, this replenishment of oxidized, higher-energy TEAs inhibits methanogenesis.
Factors such as temperature, pH, organic carbon availability, and microbial community composition
also influence carbon cycling. For example, Holm et al. [47] found that even under anoxic
conditions, permafrost soils with initially undetectable levels of methanogens did not produce
methane even after seven years of incubation. Uniquely, permafrost ecosystems may have low
microbial diversity due to dispersal limitations, which may limit carbon cycling in isolated systems
[11, 89]. Incubation studies of mineral permafrost soils tend to exhibit lower rates of CO2 and CH4
production, likely due to carbon existing in more stable inorganic forms (e.g., carbonates, graphite,
etc.) (as compared to organic rich soils), and longer lag times to methane production [118, 135].

Mineral permafrost soils are estimated to cover approximtely 49% of land area in the permafrost
zone, yet they are not as well studied compared to organic-rich soils, active layers, and methane
hotspots [48, 131, 135].

1.1.4. Subsurface transport

Transport, or the mechanism by which fluids and dissolved components move through the soil, is a
key control on the fate of greenhouse gases. Within the soil, three main mechanisms of transport
are considered: diffusion, advection, and evapotranspiration.
Diffusion is the process by which dissolved components are transported in proportion to their
concentration gradient [32]. The concentrations of dissolved methane and carbon dioxide depend
on the rate of organic decomposition. At a high rate of generation, the concentration of methane
in the deeper, anaerobic soils will increase above background concentration quickly, increasing
the concentration gradient and resulting in a higher diffusive flux. However, diffusion is generally
much slower transport process compared with advection and evaportranspiration.
Advection is the process by which bulk fluid motion transports dissolved components down a
pressure gradient in the subsurface. Pressure gradients in the subsurface can arise from a number
of different sources, including topographic gradients (gravity-driven flow), surface inputs from

 17

rainfall or snow melt, through density differences, or through ebullition (when a gas phase forms
from solution and rises to the surface).
Transpiration is the movement of water and dissolved gases from the soil to the atmosphere via plant
metabolism. Plants can transport methane from regions below the water table where methanogene-
sis occurs, through their root systems and into the atmosphere. This transport mechanism bypasses
the aerobic zone, where methane oxidation would otherwise occur. Plant-mediated flux accounts
for a significant fraction of the total methane flux, ranging from at least 37% of the total methane
flux to as much as 75% [119].
Evapotranspiration is the sum of the soil evaporation, vegetation transpiration, evaporation from
open water bodies, as well as the sublimation of frozen water [154]. Because the net water balance
from the subsurface is related to how quickly moisture near the soil surface evaporates, as well as
how water and methane is transported through plants, it is an important parameter for modeling the
hydrological system.

1.2. Conceptual model

The active layer is the upper region of the soil column underlain by permafrost that experiences
seasonal freeze and thaw cycles [108]. With increasing average (mean annual and/or summer)
surface temperatures, the active layer deepens [67], thawing previously-frozen organic matter and
microbial communities. The active layer and the permafrost below contain significant stores of
organic carbon. The upper three meters of soil in permafrost regions are estimated to contain
1035 ± 150 Pg of carbon [48, 124]. Whether this carbon can be converted into methane and how
much is released into the atmosphere is the main question driving this work.
Our conceptual model relies entirely on microbial methane generation within the first several meters
of soil below the surface. The annual release of methane from the soil during the summer relies
on methane generation and transport to the atmosphere during the warmer months of the year.
Support for this hypothesis has been found at a number of wetland sites, where methane fluxes
spanning several orders of magnitude (1 − 100 mg m−2 d−1 ) have been found to correlate well with
centimeter-degrees, or the product of active layer depth and mean soil temperature to the base of
the active layer [93, 149]. In a warming climate scenario, the active layer grows deeper and thawed
conditions extend longer, resulting in greater potential for methane releases.
Due to the methane source being entirely contained within the upper three meters of soil in this
conceptual model, laboratory reaction rates are a good approximation of methanogenesis rates in
the soil column. A synthesis of incubation studies of Arctic soils [135] revealed that the greatest
methane production rates are found in the upper 50 cm of soil, and the maximum production rates
per gram of organic soils are five times as high as those of mineral soils. This lends credence to the
hypothesis that much of the production of methane occurs in the near-surface regions of the active
layer, and the authors suggest that the decomposition of organic matter in newly-thawed permafrost
may not be as large of a contributor to methane emissions than active layer methanogenesis.
Microbially-mediated reactions depend on the microbial communities as well as the substrates
present. The methane that is transported out of the soil column and into the atmosphere is produced

 18

by the anaerobic decomposition of dissolved organic carbon by microbes. Microbially-mediated
reactions depend largely on the substrates available and the energy gained by the microbes from
facilitating the reaction. When oxygen and dissolved organic carbon are present, aerobic respiration
will be the dominant reaction, but when oxygen is not available, anaerobic methanogenesis becomes
the highest energy-producing reaction, and therefore proceeds. Similarly, when both oxygen and
methane are present, methane is quickly oxidized. Reaction rates of methane generation and
oxidation increase with increasing temperature. We assume that drier soils are insignificant source
of methane production and therefore these areas are not included in the model.
Due to the necessity of anaerobic conditions, methanogenesis typically occurs in inundated wet-
lands, where the high soil moisture reduces oxygen availability. Geologic studies of the extent of
wetland environments over the Holocene [134] indicate that there is a positive correlation between
the extent of fens and average annual air temperature. Our conceptual model includes this effect
assuming that the rate of this wetland expansion can be derived from observations of wetland
contraction during the recent cooling period starting around 1,000 years before present and ending
at the start of the industrial revolution.
While methane can be generated in a number of different ways, the approach used in this report
follows the conceptual model that methane is generated in the active layer, and transported out of
the soil by evapotranspiration or fluid movement. This conceptual model was chosen because it
is a relatively consistent source of methane, that is well correlated with the thermal-hydrological
state of the soil. Modeling methane generated in deep taliks requires many more location-based
assumptions that are challenging to address in a large-scale model, including the talik age and depth,
groundwater flow patterns, as well as understanding heterogeneities in the soil that can provide fast
pathways to the surface. Due to the short transport pathway required for methane transport from
its generation point in the active layer to the surface, fewer assumptions are required for modeling
these fluxes. By modeling the methane generated in the active layer and ignoring possible deeper
sources, our estimates may underestimate total emissions and certainly do not include methane
hotspots, which have been observed.

2. CH4PACE MODELING APPROACH

The approach developed in this project incorporates data from several sources to generate spatial
estimates of future methane flux in Alaska (Fig. 2-1). The figure shows experimental data on the
left, the software used in the center, and the field data on the right. Data flows within the individual
simulations are shown in the top half of the figure, and the data flowing into the spatial simulations
are on the bottom half.

The two codebases used to generate methane flux maps are PFLOTRAN and the CH4PACE
Framework, displayed as green boxes. Experimental and field data are used in generating inputs
to PFLOTRAN, and calibrating the models that are then fed into the CH4PACE Framework. Data
enters PFLOTRAN through the thermal-hydrological (HYDRATE) or the biogeochemical (GIRT)
process models.

The freezing curve is a representation of the relationship between temperature and the phase
transition (liquid-solid) of pore water. This is different for each soil type, and determines the
subcooling needed to reach a corresponding ice saturation. The permeability of the soil defines
how easily fluid can move through the subsurface. Relative permeability of the gas and water
phases are used in determining the flow of each phase, and its behavior is impacted by the presence
of ice. Data from microbial incubations are used in the bio-geochemistry process model to set
reaction rates.

Field data are used to calibrate the 1D PFLOTRAN models that are used in the CH4PACE Frame-
work. Temperature data from borehole sites are used to both validate the implementation of the TH
process models in PFLOTRAN as well as to calibrate petrophysical input parameters (e.g., thermal
conductivity). Flux chamber and flux tower measurements are used to calibrate and validate the
biogeochemistry model and its inputs. Reaction rates and flow conditions transporting generated
methane to the surface are calibrated to the fluxes measured by flux towers and chambers at selected
locations.

The CH4PACE Framework generates PFLOTRAN input decks, setting the material properties,
initial and boundary conditions based on the wetland type. Temperature data is used as a surface
boundary condition, using historical as well as projected temperatures. After the input decks are
generated, the framework runs the set of input deck simulations using PFLOTRAN, and outputs
a plot of methane fluxes from the subsurface. Additional data including average methane flux by
wetland type, average temperature, and cumulative methane flux are calculated as a post-processing
step.

2.1. CH4PACE Framework

The Boreal-Arctic Wetland and Lake Dataset (BAWLD-CH4) [65] provides a comprehensive dataset
of CH4 emissions from high-latitude wetland ecosystems within 0.5° × 0.5° grid cells. Methane
emissions measurements associated with each land class are also reported. These data, along
with meteorological datasets, are used within the CH4PACE Framework to generate PFLOTRAN
subsurface models, which simulate the subsurface thermal-hydrological system and microbial
production of methane.
The CH4PACE framework developed for this study is illustrated by Fig. 2-2 and outlined here:
  1. The BAWLD dataset provides land area fractions for each wetland class per 0.5° × 0.5° grid
 cell, and is used to discretize the area of interest (this study: Alaska).
  2. One-dimensional PFLOTRAN models are constructed based on the wetland class percentages
 derived from the BAWLD dataset. The CH4PACE Framework allows users to define a
 longitudinal range or specify a list of BAWLD cells for simulation.
  3. Each grid cell is defined by a series of generic PFLOTRAN models that simulate the wetland
 type present in that grid cell. The models are defined by an input deck that specifies model
 and material parameters, biogeochemical conditions, and initial and boundary conditions,
 which are elaborated upon in subsequent sections. The spatial domain of each 1D model is
 defined as 1 m × 1 m × 5 m, with uniform discretization in the z-direction of 0.1 m (50 cells).
  4. Meteorological (water balance, temperature) data is used to define the top surface boundary
 conditions for the PFLOTRAN models. In this study, the CH4PACE framework retrieves the
 nearest temperature data from a series of RCP-based climate scenarios, and creates monthly
 boundary conditions for the years 2006-2100.
  5. The PFLOTRAN simulations are launched, simulating the period of time defined by the user.
  6. Post-processing of the simulations produces visualizations of spatial methane release rates,
 release rates by wetland class, and cumulative estimates of methane release.

While we assume that the peat-like upper soil layer contains a significantly higher amount of total
carbon than the mineral soil layer, we set the initial concentration of dissolved organic carbon to a

the greater limiting factor for the production of methane is the total unfrozen quantity of water
containing dissolved organic matter. During the winter months, when the soil column is mostly
frozen, little dissolved organic carbon is available for the reaction to move forward. As the frozen

pore water thaws, the dissolved organic carbon becomes available for the methanogenesis reaction
to proceed.

3.1.1. Soil property inputs

In all three models, the initial concentration of aqueous methane was set to 3 × 10−20 mol/L. This
ensured that the dynamics of methane transport arose from methane generated mechanistically in
the simulation rather than from the effects of the initial conditions. The methane generation rate was
then tuned by changing the value of 𝛼 in the methanogenesis portion of the DAMM-GHG model. We
used a value of 𝛼 = 9.9 × 1017 𝜇mol/L-hr, activation energy of 100 kJ/mol, and inhibition constant
of 3 𝜇mol/L, corresponding to a methane generation rate of 1.656 × 10−10 kg/m3 -s. Although this
𝛼 value is considerably larger than the value presented in the original DAMM-GHG model [126],
the resulting reaction rate is similar to reported rates of methanogenesis (1.656 × 10−10 kg/m3 -s)
presented by [79] for marine gas hydrates. The activation energy and half saturation constant values
are similar to those used in the DAMM-GHG model [126].
The sediment column of the fen and tundra wetland was assumed to be composed of two soil
types with different porosity values. The upper sediment was an organic peat-like soil, underlain
by a mineral soil. Quinton et al. [112] found that in Arctic tundra, the active porosity of peat
could approach above 80% near the surface, although at depth there could be an abrupt decrease
in porosity. Boelter et al. [8] found similar characteristics of peat found in bogs in northern
Minnesota. In water retention experiments on peat from Quebec, Schlotzhauer et al. [120] noted
the porosity to be around 90%. Given these studies, we chose a value of 80% for the peat-like soil
in our sample. To account for changing porosity with ice formation, the pore compressibility was
set to 10−5 Pa−1 . This is comparable to pore compressibility values of peat found by Price et al.
[111] (8 × 10−6 – 3.7 × 10−5 Pa−1 ) and Johari et al. [51] (8.87 × 10−7 – 1.6594 × 10−5 Pa−1 ).
In the mineral soil layer, porosity was set to to 30% and pore compressibility to 10−8 Pa−1 , based
on data from Quinton et al. [112]. This is consistent with the sharp decrease in porosity noted by
Quinton et al. [112] in tundra peat. In our fen model, the top 70 cm of soil was assumed to be
the high porosity soil noted above while the remaining 4.3 m of the sediment column was modeled
as the lower porosity soil (Figure 3-1a.). In the tundra wetland model, the top 1.5 m of soil was
assumed to be the high porosity soil and the remainder 3.5 m of the sediment column was modeled
as the lower porosity soil (Figure 3-1b.). Finally, in the marsh example, an additional layer was
added to the top of the domain to simulate standing water within the marsh ecosystem: the top 20
cm of the soil was modeled with a porosity of 94% and pore compressibility of 1 × 10−5 Pa−1 .
Below this, 3.8 m of sediment was assumed to be high porosity soil (80% porosity) and the deepest
1 m of soil was modeled as low porosity soil (Figure 3-1c.).
The relative permeability function has slightly different parameters from the capillary pressure-
saturation function to aid simulation convergence. The van Genuchten 𝑚 parameter for the relative
permeability function is based on the experimental results in Appendix B. The residual saturation
is set to 0 in the relative permeability function to prevent simulation failure due to ice formation.
Allowing fluid to flow during freeze and thaw prevents extreme pressurization of the pore space
due to the density difference between ice and water.

3.1.2. Initial and boundary conditions

The temperature was initialized to -2.4 °C throughout the column for the tundra wetland simulations,
and 0.25 °C for the fen and marsh. Tundra wetlands typically have near-surface permafrost so
initializing the domain to a below-freezing temperature and using a below-freezing boundary
condition forces freezing at the deepest part of the model. Fen and marsh, on the other hand, are not
expected to have permafrost beneath them. Temperature was assigned as a time-variable Dirichlet
boundary condition to the top face. A mass flux boundary condition for the water component
was assigned to the top face to simulate evapotranspiration. Evapotranspiration was approximated
using an average of the evapotranspiration rates described in [29]. The bottom boundary was set to
a Dirichlet pressure and temperature condition, both equal to the initial conditions.

and include this effect in the model explicitly as these counter effects may cancel each other out
and since we are focused on wetlands, these areas are typically located in locally near horizontal
areas.

3.2. Temperature projections for Alaska (present - 2100)

The temperature top boundary was set as a time-dependent boundary condition to model seasonal
temperature variations throughout Alaska. We used temperature projections to the year 2100
AD to predict how methane emissions in Alaska would develop over time under different climate
scenarios.

Time-variable temperature boundary conditions were generated by Scenarios Network for Alaska
and Arctic Planning (SNAP) [140] through a process that compiled a number of global climate
models based on three different Representative Concentration Pathway (RCP) scenarios. The
downscaling process used by SNAP is summarized briefly here. The first step involved evaluating
a subset of 21 global climate models from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project, version
5 (CMIP5), based on their ability to reproduce the historical climate of Alaska, using root-mean-
square error metrics for surface air temperature, precipitation, and sea level air pressure. Following
this evaluation, the selected models underwent statistical downscaling using the delta method,
which calculates the change (delta) in temperature from a historical reference period (1961-1990)
to future time slices (here, 2010-2100). This method includes a bias correction, assuming that
the model’s bias in the historical period remains consistent in the future, thereby improving the
accuracy of the downscaled temperature projections.

 34

We selected three different climate scenarios for the temperature boundary condition, reflecting
a range of Representative Concentration Pathways (RCP) (Figure 3-4). RCP 4.5 represents an
intermediate emissions scenario, where anthropogenic carbon emissions reach their peak around
2040, and decline in the following years. RCP 6 represents an emissions scenario where anthro-
pogenic carbon emissions peak around 2080, while RCP 8.5 represents an emissions scenario
where emissions climb through 2100. In each of these scenarios, the average yearly temperature of
Alaska increases over time. The RCP 8.5 scenario projects the largest increase while the differences
between the RCP 6 and RCP 4.5 are not as large. (Figure 3-4). These climate models represent
a one-way coupling; the methane emissions modeled in this study do not influence the climate
models or temperature projections.

4. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

4.1. Mapping Arctic flux

We focus here on emissions from three saturated wetland environments: fens, marshes, and tundra
wetlands. The distributions of these wetlands across Alaska are displayed in Fig. 4-1. We include
these primarily because they are the major regions of methane production from saturated environ-
ments. Saturated wetlands, or wetlands with a water table near or above the surface, experience
little impact on methane emissions from fluctuating water table depth and the introduction of oxy-
gen into the system. Furthermore, we assume that saturated wetland environments have similar
transport mechanisms of methane through the subsurface and into the atmosphere.

4.2. Methane emissions projections for Alaska (present-2100)

To estimate natural methane emissions from Alaska through the end of the current century we
employ a two step process.
 • Estimate methane emissions assuming current wetland distribution. These estimates only
 consider the effects of continued warming based on the scenarios presented in the previous
 section.
 • Scale emissions to reflect expected expansion of the extent of wetlands as temperatures rise.
 The scaling factor is temperature-dependent and is thus different for each RCP scenario.
Each of these steps is explained in the sections below.

4.2.1. Methane emission estimates without wetland expansion

wetland simulation was run using cell specific temperature boundary conditions for each of the three

and the area fraction of each of these wetland environments in the cell. Combining the results from

4.2.3. Methane emissions estimates including wetland expansion

As the climate warms, permafrost thaws and the subsequent groundwater and surface water flow
is likely to expand the extent of saturated wetland environments. Permafrost thaw will drain
water to lower topographic regions, creating saturated environments similar to fens and marshes.
From the beginning of the Holocene, to roughly 1 ka, the areal extent of fens increased by an
estimated 1.5 × 106 km2 [134]. Other wetland environments, including marshes, swamps, and
bogs, experienced similar, though less rapid, growth over the same time period. As permafrost
formed, the growth of these wetlands slowed and their extent reduced.

Based on historical estimates of wetland growth, we model wetland expansion in fens. First, we
approximate the rate of fen area reduction (area / time) from about 1000 years before present to
the industrial revolution (∼1850) during which temperatures decreased [134]. A linear regression
over this time period returned a slope of -0.6391 ×103 km2 yr−1 with an R2 of 0.9735 (Fig. 4-8,
upper dashed line). We assume that this process is symmetric, meaning that a similar increase
in temperature would result in the same amount of fen area expansion. Using a temperature
reconstruction over the past 2000 years [81], we estimate the rate of change in Arctic temperature
(Δ°C / time) from 1000 – 1850 AD. A linear regression over this time period returned a rate of
0.0013 Δ°C yr−1 with an R2 of 0.373 (Fig. 4-8) (Fig. 4-8, lower dashed line). By dividing the rate
of fen expansion by the rate of temperature change, we approximate the rate of fen expansion per
temperature change degree Celsius 491.6 × 103 km2 / Δ°C. We scale this value by the present-day
fen area, 767.7 × 103 km2 to get a fractional change in area over change in temperature, which is
then applied to the areal extent of fens in our area of interest and RCP scenario.

5. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK

We estimate that with projected temperature increases in Alaska a significant amount of current
permafrost will thaw, exposing greater amounts of organic carbon in the soil to anaerobic condi-
tions where methane-producing microbes will thrive. Additionally, water released from thawing
permafrost will flow and collect near the surface, expanding the area of current wetlands and
significantly increasing the areal extent of anaerobic conditions in the shallow subsurface and re-
sulting methane emissions. The expansion of saturated wetlands will likely influence total methane
emissions more than the rise in temperature from existing wetlands.
While we are quite confident in these general results, there are considerable uncertainties that could
change the magnitude of these estimates. For example, we still do not understand the detailed
process and dynamics of how communities of methanogens function other than at a very high level.
Microbial decomposition of organic matter includes other geochemical reactions, is dependent on
pH and other geochemical components, and includes an incredible complexity that is challenging
to incorporate, especially with the limited data available.
Improved wetland estimations of methane emissions would incorporate the influence of lateral
groundwater flow on the transport of dissolved gases and organic carbon. We attempted to find
a mechanistic relationship between groundwater flow from thawing permafrost within watersheds
and methane flux, but were unable to determine any correlation. This was partially due to the lack
of reliable groundwater data associated with flux measurements. Incorporation of other wetland
environments, as well as their expansion and contraction, would improve our understanding of
methane emissions over time. Furthermore, incorporation of methane emission hot spots from
taliks, as well as understanding the mechanisms of talik formation, would further improve spatial
estimates of methane emissions.

APPENDIX B. Permeability Investigations

This section is a draft manuscript of Mills et al., The effective permeability of permafrost,
submitted to Geophysical Research Letters.
We measured the gas permeability of two permafrost soils at a range of ice saturations and developed
a relative gas (𝑘 𝑟𝑔 ) and water (𝑘 𝑟𝑤 ) permeability model for frozen sediments using van Genuchten-
Mualem equations. Our frozen sediment 𝑘 𝑟𝑔 model falls along the same curve as 𝑘 𝑟𝑔 data from
literature for frozen and unfrozen sediments and our frozen sediment 𝑘 𝑟𝑤 model is in agreement
with unfrozen 𝑘 𝑟𝑤 data from literature. These results indicate that frozen and unfrozen 𝑘 𝑟𝑔 and
𝑘 𝑟𝑤 forms for a given sediment are the same, ice is an intermediate phase in frozen sediments, and
an approximation for frozen sediment 𝑘 𝑟𝑤 and 𝑘 𝑟𝑔 can be obtained from simpler, unfrozen flow
experiments. We provide a path for researchers to model the flow of carbon in the gas and water
phases across the 0 °C isotherm with a simple form that can be incorporated into large-scale climate
simulations.

B.1. Introduction

Arctic and high-latitude air and ground temperatures are rising and thawing permafrost [6, 107,
113, 129]. This thaw exposes soil organic carbon to microbial decomposition which produces CO2
and CH4 and allows subsurface carbon to be transported in the water or gas phase towards surface
waters and the atmosphere, thereby acting as a positive climate feedback [28, 60, 87, 124]. To
better constrain the mobility of carbon species in stable and thawing permafrost sediments, it is
imperative to quantify how gas and water move through these systems. The transport properties of
permafrost are fundamentally dependent on the respective connectivity of the vapor, water, and ice
phases and their saturations: the fraction of the pore space occupied by a particular phase. In fact, at
a range of subzero temperatures, vapor, ice, and liquid water phases can all be present in permafrost
sediments (e.g., [26]). This is because the relative partitioning of water and ice in sediment pores
is dependent on temperature and pore size, where the temperature required to freeze pore water
decreases for decreasing pore sizes [2, 43, 101]. Effective permeability (𝑘 𝑒 𝑓 𝑓 ), the permeability
of one phase in the presence of other phases, is commonly characterized by scaling the intrinsic
permeability (𝑘 𝑖 ) by the relative permeability (𝑘 𝑟 ):

                                              𝑘 𝑒 𝑓 𝑓 = 𝑘𝑟 𝑘𝑖 ,                                     (B.1)

where 𝑘 𝑟 varies from zero to one. For a given sediment, the relative permeability of the gas
(𝑘 𝑟𝑔 ) and water (𝑘 𝑟𝑤 ) phases are constrained as a function of the respective saturations of gas (𝑆 𝑔 )
and water (𝑆 𝑤 ) (e.g., [7, 12, 139]). Moreover, in frozen sediments, as the saturation of ice (𝑆𝑖 )

 73

increases, the connectivity and saturation of gas and water decreases, which also decreases 𝑘 𝑟𝑔 and
𝑘 𝑟𝑤 , respectively.
Previous researchers have primarily focused on calculating 𝑘 𝑟𝑤 for frozen two-phase (ice-water)
systems using modeling techniques [49, 54, 72, 77, 102, 144] due to the difficulty of experimentally
flowing water through frozen sediments (e.g., [14, 145]). Other researchers have experimentally
measured 𝑘 𝑟𝑔 in frozen three-phase (gas-ice-water) systems as a function of 𝑆𝑖 [18, 59, 125, 143];
however, these experiments only used clean sands. There is little known about the behavior of 𝑘 𝑟𝑔 in
natural permafrost sediments which contain organic matter and finer-grained sediments—sediments
in which microorganisms may produce greenhouse gases.
Here, we performed gas flow experiments using an organic-rich (yedoma) permafrost sediment
obtained near Fairbanks, Alaska and a clean sand for comparison. We developed a method to
prepare a series of specimens with a range of ice saturations for these two sediment samples and
measured their effective permeabilities. The relative permeability behavior of these samples was
used to develop a 𝑘 𝑟𝑔 and 𝑘 𝑟𝑤 model for frozen sediments. Comparison of this model to a range of
experimentally measured 𝑘 𝑟𝑔 and 𝑘 𝑟𝑤 data from literature for frozen (three-phase; gas-ice-water)
and unfrozen (two-phase; gas-water) sediments showed excellent agreement. From this, we infer
that gas is the least-wetting phase, ice is an intermediate phase, and water is the wetting phase in
frozen sediments. Thus, the effective permeability to gas or water for a frozen sediment can be
calculated from much simpler two-phase (gas-water) experiments. We provide a final functional
form to describe the effective permeability to gas and water in permafrost. This functional form
can be integrated into reactive transport models (e.g., PFLOTRAN; [42, 77]) to estimate the
composition and magnitude of greenhouse gasses released as permafrost thaws.

B.2. Materials and Methods

Materials We acquired a natural, Pleistocene-aged yedoma sample from Vault Creek Tunnel (VCT)
near Fairbanks, Alaska (Fig. B-1a). The material was collected from three depths within the tunnel
(0.2 kg from 5 m depth, 1.5 kg from 14 m depth, and 0.3 kg of from 21 m depth), sieved to remove
large organic debris, and homogenized into one distinct sediment that we refer to as the VCT
sediment. The VCT and the sediments therein are described by [83] and [44]. We compared the
VCT material to an industrially provided silica sand from Sigma Aldrich (50-70 mesh) composed
of pure quartz with no organic material present.
Material characterization methods We characterized the size distributions of the two materials
with a laser particle size analyzer; we also analyzed the VCT size distributions by the hydrometer
method, which is better suited for fine-grained sediments. The VCT sediment was analyzed for
mineralogy (bulk and <2 𝜇m fraction) using X-ray diffraction, total organic carbon (TOC) using an
elemental analyzer, and grain density using helium porosimetry.
Synthetic permafrost preparation We created homogeneous frozen soil with specific ice satu-
rations using a modified undercompaction technique [37, 69]. This method allows us to develop
replicable specimens. We fit a latex membrane (inside) and aluminum mold (outside) around a
brass pedestal which had -7 °C ethylene glycol (EG) cycled through it from a connected chiller.

B.4. Discussion

Water has been observed to wet mineral surfaces in frozen (two-phase; ice-water) and unfrozen
(two-phase; gas-water) sediments [2, 7, 43, 101]. This is because the polar nature of water is
attracted to mineral surfaces through surface and adsorptive forces [25, 50]. In unfrozen sediments
(gas-water), gas has been shown to be the least-wetting phase [7]. For the case of frozen three-
phase (gas-ice-water) systems, Miller [84, 85], and more recently Kurylyk and Watanabe [66],
used rudimentary interfacial tension assumptions to depict water as the wetting phase, ice as an
intermediate phase, and gas as the least wetting phase.

[75, 92]. Interestingly, it is remarkable how similar the relative permeability behavior is for a range
of sediment types (Fig. B-3c).
We interpret, by integration of our results and previous results (Fig. B-3c), that in permafrost
systems, gas is the least-wetting phase (occupies the largest pores), ice is the intermediate phase
(occupies the intermediate pores), and water is the wetting phase (occupies the smallest pores). In
this case, the form of a respective 𝑘 𝑟𝑔 or 𝑘 𝑟𝑤 curve for a given sediment will be the same under
frozen (gas-ice-water) or unfrozen (gas-water) conditions. Thus, 𝑘 𝑟𝑔 and 𝑘 𝑟𝑤 curves for a frozen
sediment can be obtained by performing unfrozen gas and water fractional flow experiments. This
is advantageous because the three-phase frozen experiments are extremely challenging.
Fig. B-4 shows 𝑆 𝑔 , 𝑆𝑖 , 𝑆 𝑤 , 𝑘 𝑟𝑔 , and 𝑘 𝑟𝑤 as a function of temperature for the VCT sediment calculated
using the characteristic freezing curve (Eq. B.5 and Fig. B-2) and applications of Equations 6-10.
At temperatures above 0 °C, 𝑆 𝑔 is 0.3 and 𝑆 𝑤 is 0.7 with gas in the large pores and water in the
small pores (saturations arbitrarily chosen) (Figures 4a and 4b). As the temperature drops below
0 °C, gas remains in the largest pores and an ice phase develops in the intermediate pores (largest
water filled pores) (Figures 4a and 4b). Ice then fills the intermediate pores down to the pore
size defined by the freezing curve (Fig. B-2c) for a given temperature, with water filling the pores
smaller than this point (Figures 4a and 4b). The expansion of ice causes 𝑆 𝑔 to drop slightly and
𝑆 𝑤 to drop drastically at lower temperatures (Fig. B-4a). Using the 𝑆 𝑔 and 𝑆 𝑤 values at a range of
temperatures (Fig. B-4a) and Equations 7-10 (fitting parameters outlined above), we calculated 𝑘 𝑟𝑔
and 𝑘 𝑟𝑤 across the 0 °C isotherm (Fig. B-4c). The expansion of ice has little effect on 𝑘 𝑟𝑔 due to a
small change in 𝑆 𝑔 but reduced 𝑘 𝑟𝑤 by three orders of magnitude by -2 °C due to a large change in
𝑆 𝑤 (Figures 4a and 4c). This approach can serve as a foundation for multiphase multicomponent
reaction transport models that describe the flow of water and greenhouse gasses (GHGs) in stable
and thawing permafrost.
Our approach is similar to the efforts of previous workers to describe relative permeability in
three-phase permafrost systems. For example, the three-phase (gas-ice-water) MarsFlo [102]
model assigns the ice phase to the largest water-filled pores (intermediate pores) and utilizes a van
Genuchten-Mualem form from unfrozen sediments to predict the water relative permeability, in
accordance with our results. However, it is thought that the high mobility of gases causes 𝑘 𝑟𝑔 to
be less important than 𝑘 𝑟𝑤 in multiphase multicomponent flow models, which has led to the use
of a simple linear 𝑆 𝑔 =𝑘 𝑟𝑔 relationship in models [102] or the omission of 𝑘 𝑟𝑔 predictions in other
models (e.g., [54, 77, 122, 130]).
Future air and ground temperature increases in the arctic and high-altitudes are projected to result
in permafrost degradation [31, 129] as well as a general drying of soils due to increased hydraulic
connectivity [102, 147, 148]. Near the water table ice and water saturations can be quite high and, in
this region, the relative permeability to gas is low. We infer that in this critical region, there will also
be considerable microbial reactions affecting GHG concentration and mobility. The modifications
to gas and water relative permeability driven by saturation changes during drying and increasing
temperatures will directly affect the transport of dissolved organic carbon in water and GHGs such
as CO2 and CH4 (e.g., [19, 94]). For these reasons, a focus on the relative permeability of both
water and gas in permafrost systems is important. Our findings provide a simple path forward
in which gas and water transport can be modeled across the 0 °C isotherm and incorporated into
large-scale climate simulations.

C.1. Materials and Methods

Two coring sites were selected: Smith Lake #1 (SL1) and Smith Lake #4 (SL4) (Fig. C-1). These
sites are located in the discontinuous permafrost zone and boreal region of Alaska. SL1 soils are
classified as Chatanika mucky silt loam and generally have a shallow Oi horizon (0 - 10 cm) of
slightly decomposed plant material followed by a ∼5 cm thick A horizon of mucky silt loam [137].
Below these horizons are additional silt to silt loam horizons [137]. SL4 soils are classified as Bolio
peat which generally have very thick Oi, Oe, and Of horizons of peat and mucky peat (0 to ∼183
cm) followed by silt horizons [137]. The parent material is the Holocene Ready Bullion Formation,
which is characterized by homogeneous unconsolidated eolian silt that has been reworked and
transported (Pewe et al., 1976). The Ready Bullion Formation (∼0.9 - 9 m thick) unconformably
overlies the Pleistocene Goldstream Formation which also consists of reworked eolian silt (Pewe et
al., 1976).

SL4 is located in a depression, is very poorly drained, frequently ponds, and has negligible runoff
[137]. SL1 is located on a hillslope, is poorly drained, frequently ponds, and has very high runoff
[137]. This results in SL4 being water saturated during summer while SL1 experiences fluctuating
water saturation (Fig. C-1).

Over the last 15 years, permafrost at SL1 has gradually warmed and a supra-permafrost talik formed
in 2018 (Fig. C-1). The talik is located between 40 cm and 85 cm depth and remains near 0.2 °C
during winter. Since 2015, active layer temperatures at SL4 have increased but the active layer
thickness has not changed (Fig. C-1). During the winter, SL4 shows periods of slight warming and
cooling, but no distinct trend or potential talik development (Fig. C-1).

Soil cores were collected in March of 2023 when soil was frozen to the surface. A Talon Drill
System was used to recover soil cores to a depth of three meters at both sites. Cores were 2.7 cm
in diameter and were recovered in ∼0.5 m - 1 m increments. Cores were immediately transferred

C.2. Incubation design

Incubation experiments were designed to test the effects of temperature, thaw status (active layer
vs talik vs permafrost), organic carbon concentration, and water content on carbon gas production.
Three different types of incubations were performed.
First, a set of untreated incubations were made to reflect in-situ conditions. These incubations
cover the SL4 active layer (SL4-AL1; SL4-AL2), SL1 talik (SL1-TAL) and permafrost at both sites
(SL1-PFB; SL1-PFC; SL4-PFA; SL4-PFB; SL4-PFC). Incubations were prepared by adding 5-6 g
of frozen material to an autoclaved, 25 mL glass vial. Material was cut from the frozen core in a
6 °C cold room using a sterilized handsaw and small hand soil corer. Samples were disturbed as
little as possible during the cutting and transferring process. Vials were sealed with butyl rubber
stoppers and aluminum crimped rings. From each depth, 9 untreated incubations were prepared and
then separated into 3 groups of 3 for incubation at 2 °C, 6 °C, and 25 °C. SL4-AL2 and SL4-PFA
were only incubated at 25 °C (still in triplicate).
Once sealed, the headspaces of all incubation vials were flushed with ultra-high purity (UHP)
nitrogen for 20-25 minutes at a flow rate of ∼40 mL/min. The vial headspace was then flushed for
an additional 10-15 minutes at the same flow rate. Vials were flushed in a 25 °C room. Immediately
after flushing, vials were covered with aluminum foil and kept frozen. Once all vials were flushed,
they were placed in the dark at their designated incubation temperature. Incubations remained at
their designated temperature for a 9 day “pre-incubation” period. This pre-incubation period is
designed to return samples to near-site conditions and limit any influence of storage or sampling
[46, 150]. After the pre-incubation period, vial headspaces were re-flushed with ultra-pure nitrogen
for 20-25 minutes at a flow rate of ∼40 mL/min. No water was sparged during the second flush.
Once re-flushed, vials were returned to their incubation temperature, marking the start of the
incubation period. Untreated incubations were incubated for 162 days.

C.3. CO2 and CH4 in untreated incubations

Fig. C-2 illustrates the µmoles respired CO2 -C per gram dry weight vs time for the untreated
incubations. A striking feature is the low to negative CO2 production rate for the 2 °C and 6 °C
samples (blue and purple lines (Fig. C-2). In contrast, CO2 production rate in the 25 °C samples
generally increased significantly with time (pink line, Fig. C-2).
Active layer incubations at SL4 (AL1 and AL2), at all temperatures, had measurable methane con-
centrations on incubation day 1 while SL4-PFA (incubated at 25 °C only) had measurable methane

at all temperatures, rates of methane production were higher than rates of CO2 production. How-
ever, many incubations did not produce a measurable amount of methane during the incubation
duration (zero production rate).

consider the relationship, if any, between organic carbon content and carbon gas production. In

C.4. Discussion

Methane production is largely absent in the incubated samples. It is possible that methanogens
are simply not present in these soil layers or have a ‘lag’ period longer than the duration of
this incubation. Other permafrost incubation studies have observed a lag to methane production
ranging from several days to upwards of 5 years [47, 135]. Additionally, red and black/brown
staining (likely oxidized iron and manganese) was observed at several points along cores from both
sites. Manganese and iron reduction is more thermodynamically favorable than methanogenesis
which could result in iron/manganese reducing microbial communities outcompeting methanogens
[82].
Other incubation studies have similarly observed that active layers and permafrost layers of uplands
and hillslopes as well as permafrost of floodplains often have low, if any, methane production and
often require long lag times [71, 135].
Alternatively, active layers of floodplains are often high methane producers with limited lag times
[71, 135]. Therefore, it is not surprising that methane production was observed in the SL4 AL

Further, SL4-PFB untreated incubations were inundated and SL4-PFC incubations were muddy in

We find that there may be varying factors influencing the initiation of methane production, but once

APPENDIX E. Snow Surface Model

A snow surface model was developed to account for how the ground surface temperature and water
saturation boundary conditions are affected by snowpack. This simple snow model modulates the
soil surface temperature when snow is present, serving to insulate the ground from air temperature
fluctuations and supply meltwater to the underlying soil. During periods of no snowpack, the model
is not applied and soil surface temperature is assumed to be equal to air temperature.
There are several processes occurring in the snowpack that can affect conditions at the ground
surface such as snowfall, solar radiation, wind, surface melt, runoff, albedo, and vegetation cover.
For simplification only surface melt, snowfall, and surface temperature were included as inputs.
The simulation models snow compaction and heat transfer and solves for the density structure and
temperature structure of the snowpack. Snow compaction was simulated based on the weight of
the overlying snow and ice and snow layer temperature as:
