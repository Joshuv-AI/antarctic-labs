Monoclonal Antibodies Targeting Bacterial Infections: A Broad Review of the Field
Authors: Matthew J. Slarve, Christine Tkaczyk, Michael P. Doyle, Timothy J. Break, Alexander B. Smith, Galit Alter, Brian Luna, Brad Spellberg, Antonio DiGiandomenico
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ever-increasing trend of antibiotic resistance necessitates new therapeutic strategies for fighting infection. Standing on decades of extensive research and development, monoclonal antibodies have great promise for treating antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections. In this comprehensive review of the field, we summarize the rapidly emerging field of monoclonal therapeutics that offer alternative approaches to target bacterial pathogens that pose critical concern to human health. Organizing the findings by species and by molecular target, we discuss antibodies that have demonstrated therapeutic potential as well as those that did not provide efficacy, highlighting new insights for the design and discovery of highly effective therapeutics for the future. Furthermore, we discuss the latest advances in molecular biology that have revolutionized the implementation, efficacy, and utility of monoclonal antibodies as therapeutics, and that continues to drive what will be an exciting era for antibacterial monoclonal antibodies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40259-026-00787-9.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is becoming an increasingly serious threat to human health and well-being. In 2019 alone, it led to 5 million additional deaths worldwide and was responsible for hospital costs estimated at US$693 billion [1, 2]. The rising rate of AMR has been, in part, fueled by the excessive use of antimicrobials in healthcare and agriculture. Ironically, this not only creates a greater need for new drugs and strategies but also hinders their development [3, 4]. Small-molecule drugs exhibit strong selective pressure that results in escape mutations, causing AMR and eliminating the utility of the drug [5]. This problem cannot be remediated by simply developing novel antibiotics, as there is no biochemical pathway that a new drug can attack that bacteria cannot readily evolve to defend [6]. Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) offer an alternative approach that kills bacteria indirectly through multiple host-mediated protective mechanisms (e.g., serum bactericidal activity and opsonophagocytic activity) [7, 8]. Though escape mutations can also occur in response to mAb treatment (a well-documented phenomenon for viruses), the selective pressures differ from that of small molecules, making therapeutic mAbs a valuable approach for reducing the onset of resistance [9, 10]. Decades of extensive research and development of anti-bacterial mAbs have yielded numerous drug candidates for the prevention and treatment of infections caused by a multitude of pathogenic species. Furthermore, advancements in the field of molecular engineering have resulted in novel antibody formats, structures, and delivery methods that have expanded the utility of these molecules beyond that of natively occurring mAbs and moving the field of mAb therapy forward at an incredible pace.

Despite these advancements—and the approval of numerous mAbs for other indications—few anti-bacterial mAbs have been approved for clinical use, which is limited to obiltoxaximab (Anthim) and raxibacumab (Abthrax) for treatment of Bacillus anthracis toxin exposure, and bezlotoxumab (Zinplava®) for Clostridioides difficile infection of the gastrointestinal tract [11–13]. In this broad review, we summarize much of the story of anti-bacterial mAb discovery and development, focusing on key bacterial pathogens, and the numerous mAbs developed to target their specific epitopes (a concise listing is available in Table 1 of the Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM]). The successes and failures of each mAb discussed here have played a role in the emergence and expansion of this field. By leveraging the immune system to clear the pathogen, mAbs have the potential to protect patients by reducing disease rates, preserving antibiotics, safeguarding the microbiome and improving overall health outcomes and quality of life, particularly for those patients who are immunocompromised or medically vulnerable.

Clostridioides difficile

Clostridioides difficile, a Gram-positive anaerobic gastro-intestinal bacteria, is transmitted via the fecal-oral route in the form of spores [14]. Dysbiosis of the intestinal flora, often caused by antimicrobial exposure, facilitates spore germination to a toxin-producing vegetative form that results in gastrointestinal diseases [15]. When secreted in the lumen of the colon, both Toxin A (TcdA) and Toxin B (TcdB) are considered major virulence factors in C. difficile pathogenesis. TcdA and TcdB are glucosyltransferases, exerting their virulence by glycosylating proteins of the Rho family GTPases in the cytosol of host cells. Each toxin contains four functional domains: an N-terminal glucosyltransferase domain (GTD), an auto-protease domain, a delivery domain with receptor binding, pore formation and cargo translocations functions, and a domain formed by combined repetitive oligopeptide sequences (CROPs). Both toxins intoxicate host cells through a multi-step process as follows: (1) binding to their specific receptor; (2) endocytosis; (3) pore formation in acidified endosomes, and translocation of the GTD and the auto-protease domain across the endosomal membrane; and (4) autoprocessing, and release of GTD in the cytosol, and 5-glucosylation of host GTPases (Fig. 1) [14]. This results in loss of epithelial barrier function in the colon by disrupting tight junctions and increasing membrane permeability causing mild-to-severe diarrhea, colitis and in more serious cases, toxic megacolon, perforation, sepsis, and death [16]. Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) cause an estimated 462,100 cases annually in the USA, with 12,800 deaths and $1 billion in health care cost each year [17]. Although antibiotic exposure can cause CDI, the first line of treatment for CDI is antibiotic therapy including broad-spectrum metronidazole, vancomycin, or more recently, narrow-spectrum fidaxomicin [18]. While providing immediate relief, antibiotic treatment prolongs the state of dysbiosis of the gut microbiota, which leaves the patient at risk for CDI recurrence (rCDI) [19]. This CDI paradox has driven exploration for alternative therapies such as fecal microbiota transplant and biotherapies against TcdA and TcdB [20]. Early attempts of immunological control used oral therapy with hyperimmune bovine colostrum, which showed therapeutic potential in preclinical and clinical testing by reducing disease severity and frequency of rCDI, but was not developed further because of complex manufacturability [21, 22]. C. difficile: Anti-Toxin mAbs

Medarex was the first to report anti-toxin mAbs, which were later licensed by Merck [23]. Two fully human IgG1κ mAbs, anti-TcdA (CDA1/actoxumab) and TcdB (CDB1/bezlotoxumab) were selected for in vitro potency, and efficacy in a lethal intoxication hamster model [24]. The mAb combination was further tested for prevention and adjunctive treatment in mouse and hamster CDI models [25, 26]. Bezlotoxumab recognizes two distinct sites on the TcdB CROPs domain and exerts neutralizing activity by preventing TcdB binding to host cell surface receptor CSPG4 [27]. A similar mechanism of action was later described for actoxumab [28]. Two global phase III studies (MODIFY I and MODIFY II) for prevention of rCDI showed a similar rate of recurrence between groups receiving bezlotoxumab or actoxumab/bezlotoxumab combined [13]. Bezlotoxumab led to a significant reduction in rCDI as compared with placebo (17% vs 28% in MODIFY I; and 16% vs 26% in MODIFY II) while the actoxumab-group showed a similar rate of rCDI when compared to placebo (26% vs 28% MODIFY I) [13]. Bezlotoxumab (Zinplava®) was approved as a single intravenous infusion of 10 mg/kg in the USA and European Union for prevention of rCDI in individuals at high risk for CDI and receiving antibacterial treatment [23]. The key role of TcdB in CDI pathogenesis was subsequently confirmed in a gnotobiotic piglet CDI model [29]. Collectively, these results suggested that TcdB is a clinically validated target and neutralizing TcdB with a mAb would be sufficient to provide a clinical benefit in preventing rCDI. Unfortunately, Zinplava® was discontinued in January 2025 with no explanation provided by the manufacturer [30].

AstraZeneca is developing another anti-TcdB mAb, having licensed two anti-toxin mAbs from Progenics (both IgG1κ) [31]. The anti-TcdB mAb, AZD5148, was engineered with a YTE substitution, a triple-mutation M252Y/S254T/T256E in the Fc region that extends the mAb’s half-life in human sera [32, 33]. AZD5148 (formerly PA41) binds the GTD of TcdB and prevents the delivery of TcdB enzymatic cargo into the host cell cytosol [34]. TcdB binds at least three classes of receptors including CSPG4, Nectin-3, and PVRL3 [35–37]. The two most recently discovered receptors are known to bind TcdB outside of the CROPs domain. Therefore, it is possible that TcdB could escape neutralization by Zinplava® [14]. In contrast, the strategy employed by AZD5148, focused on binding the GTD, is independent of the receptor expressed on the target cell surface and may offer broader neutralizing activity. A recent study on 9134 global isolates showed that the AZD5148 epitope was highly conserved (99.58%) when compared to Zinplava® (82.47%). AZD5148 also exhibited broad neutralization in vitro against eight recent circulating TcdB ribotypes [33]. In addition, AZD5148 conferred protection in a CDI gnotobiotic piglet model, and in a mouse CDI model against strains with different TcdB ribotypes [38, 39]. AZD5148 is currently being evaluated for efficacy and safety for the prevention of recurrence of CDI in individuals 18 years of age and above in the PRISM study (NCT07285213) [40, 41].

Though toxin-neutralizing mAb treatment has shown value for patients with C. difficile infections, these treatments do not eliminate C. difficile spores. Therefore (prior to its removal from the market) the Infectious Diseases Society of America recommended ZINPLAVA as an adjunct treatment alongside vancomycin or fidaxomicin, not a primary therapeutic [42]. Future mAb approaches could include spore-targeting mAbs alongside toxin-neutralizing mAbs to promote decolonization as a primary therapeutic.

Staphylococcus aureus

Staphylococcus aureus is a Gram-positive bacterium found on the skin or in the nostrils of 20–30% of people [43]. It is a major cause of hospital-acquired infections and accounted for about one million deaths worldwide in 2019 [44]. Extensive use of broad-spectrum antibiotics to treat hospitalized patients during the coronavirus disease pandemic has exacerbated this crisis and resulted in a 13% increase of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) infections [45]. Upon access to the body through a breach in the skin or an indwelling device, S. aureus causes diseases that range from mild skin and soft-tissue infections to life-threatening sepsis, endocarditis, osteomyelitis, and pneumonia [43]. While most bacteria exert pathogenesis through few exotoxins or surface antigens, S. aureus relies on a large collection of secreted and surface-expressed virulence factors to penetrate through the tissues, promote adhesion to any surface, and impair any aspect of the host immune response (Fig. 2) [46–48]. To complicate the situation, virulence gene expression is modulated temporally and spatially during infection, and many virulence factors are often functionally redundant [49, 50]. S. aureus: mAbs Against Pore-Forming Toxins

Staphylococcus aureus possesses a family of six cytolytic pore forming toxins comprising alpha toxin (Hla) and bi-component leukocidins (LukED, LukSF, HlgAB, HlgCB, LukAB) [47]. Hla demonstrates extensive cell tropism through its interaction with the receptor ADAM10 [51]. It contributes to disease pathogenesis by lysing immune cells, erythrocytes, and platelets, initiating a pronounced proinflammatory response, and promoting bacterial translocation across tissues and organs [47]. Several human anti-Hla mAbs have been characterized. The human IgG1 mAb LC10, generated from Velocimmune® mice, provided protection in prophylaxis [52–55] or in adjunctive therapy in mouse, ferret, and rabbit models for pneumonia and dermonecrosis [56, 57]. LC10 was further engineered with a YTE substitution for half-life extension. The resulting molecule, suvratoxumab (also known as MEDI4893), showed a terminal half-life of 81–112 days in healthy volunteers [58], and a 31.8% relative reduction in S. aureus ventilator-associated pneumonia in comparison to placebo recipients in the phase II SAATELLITE study [59]. The mAb LTM14, identified by ScFv phage display but converted into IgG (precise isotype not stated), binds a different epitope than LC10 and showed protection in preclinical mouse models of bacteremia, dermonecrosis, and pneumonia [60]. Tosatoxumab (AR-301, human IgG1λ) was tested in a randomized phase I/II study for pharmacokinetics and efficacy in adjunctive therapy with antibiotics for severe S. aureus pneumonia in intensive care unit patients. Out of 48 enrolled patients, six individuals developed pneumonia attributable to MRSA. The mAb showed a half-life of 25 days. Although the study was underpowered for efficacy, a post hoc analysis of ventilator-associated S. aureus pneumonia showed that tosatoxumab had shortened the ventilation duration and provided faster microbiologic eradication than placebo control [61]. A phase III study on 240 subjects was initiated in 2019 but no results have been released [62]. ASN1, a mAb cross-neutralizing Hla, LukED, LukSF-PV, HlgAB, and HlgBC, was generated by IgG yeast display, and showed efficacy in pneumonia and bacteremia mouse models [63]. ASN100, a combination comprising ASN1 and anti-LukAB mAb ASN2 provided protection in a rabbit pneumonia model [64]. ASN100 demonstrated acceptable safety and tolerability with a 3-week half-life in healthy volunteers but was stopped due to futility in a phase II study [65, 66].

S. aureus: mAbs Against Other Secreted Proteins

Staphylococcal complement inhibitor, and chemotaxis inhibitory protein of S. aureus facilitate immune-evasion by interfering with components of the complement activation cascade [67, 68]. The human IgG (precise isotype unclear) mAb 6D4 inhibits Staphylococcal complement inhibitor activity in vitro in a C3b deposition assay on S. aureus [69]. Several mAbs were generated that block the activity of chemotaxis inhibitory protein of S. aureus against C5a or fMLP in vitro [67]. S. aureus superantigens (SAgs) possess potent immuno-stimulating activities on both T cells and B cells [70]. Among the SAg family, Staphylococcal enterotoxin B (SEB) is one of the most important toxin threats in bioterrorism and could be used as an air-, food-, or water-borne toxin [70]. The binding sites for SEB on T cells and MHC class II presenting cells are distinct, and mAbs have been generated to block each epitope [71, 72]. Humanized IgG2 anti-SEB mAbs protected mice from lethal SEB intoxication in a S. aureus-induced sepsis model either alone or in adjunctive therapy with vancomycin [73]. Last, several potent neutralizing IgG or scFv mAbs have shown efficacy in preclinical models against toxic shock syndrome toxin-1, the cause of menstrual toxic shock syndrome [74–76].

S. aureus: mAbs Against Surface Proteins

Staphylococcus aureus possesses a large collection of cell wall-anchored proteins that are differently expressed among strains. The MSCRAMM (molecular surface component recognizing adhesive matrix molecules) represent the largest class, and are involved in tissue colonization, adhesion to the extracellular matrix and proteins in blood, and biofilm formation [46, 77] Clumping factor A (ClfA), found in all isolates, binds fibrinogen to promote both agglutination in blood and attachment to devices or tissues—two features linked to bloodstream infections [46, 78]. The mouse mAb Aurexis and its humanized IgG1κ version tefibazumab demonstrated protection in different animal models in combination with antibiotics [79]. Tefibazumab safety and pharmacokinetics were further tested in a phase II, randomized, double-blinded, multi-center clinical trial. Sixty patients with S. aureus bacteremia and treated with standard of care antibiotics received tefibazumab (20-mg/kg single infusion) or placebo. Tefibazumab was well tolerated with a plasma half-life of 18 days. A composite clinical endpoint occurred in six patients (four placebo and two tefibazumab patients) and included five deaths (four placebo and one tefibazumab patient). Progression in sepsis severity occurred only in four placebo patients. Although not statistically different, the difference in composite clinical endpoint between both groups may suggest a potential benefit for tefibazumab in adjunctive therapy to prevent sepsis progression [80]. However, no further clinical trials with greater statistical power were conducted by Inhibitex. Further investigation suggested that the epitope was not broadly conserved [81]. AZD7745, another human IgG1 anti-ClfA mAb binds to a different but well-conserved epitope [82], mediates opsonophagocytic killing (OPK) activity in vitro (an assay measuring mAb ability to mediate bacteria killing via complement-dependent phagocytosis), and provides protection against multiple clinical isolates in a lethal mouse bacteremia model [83]. AZD7745 was engineered with the N3Y substitution for half-life extension in humans [84]. AZD7760, a multi-mechanistic mAb combination comprising the Hla neutralizing mAb suvratoxumab and AZD7745, is currently being evaluated in a phase I/IIa study for safety and pharmacokinetics in healthy adults and adults with end-stage renal diseases receiving hemodialysis with a central-venous catheter [85]. Additionally, several anti-ClfA mAbs targeting different epitopes than AZD7745 have been described recently and could be promising candidates for future development [86]. Two IgG1κ mAbs have also been developed against ClfB, effectively inhibiting its interaction with cytokeratin 10 in vitro and significantly decreasing S. aureus nasal colonization in murine models [87]. Mouse anti-collagen binding MSCRAMM (CNA) mAbs (IgG1κ, IgG2a-κ, and IgG2b-κ) showed in vitro inhibition of collagen binding to S. aureus, along with blocking C1q binding to CNA [88]. The S. aureus surface protein A is another MSCRAMM antigen exploited as a potential target. The human IgG3 anti-Sas mAb, 2H7, was reported to mediate OPK and improved survival in mouse sepsis and peritoneal infection models [89].

Staphylococcus aureus protein A (SpA) is ubiquitous on S. aureus isolates. It is composed of five repeated sequences of IgG binding domains and impairs both IgG effector function and binding activity. Staphylococcus aureus protein A utilizes distinct binding sites for the Fcγ domain of human antibodies of IgG1/2/4 isotypes, and for the human Fab regions with a VH3 rearrangement of any antibody isotypes [90–92]. Additionally, SpA association with VH3-type B-cell receptors triggers B-cell superantigen activity [93]. The anti-SpA murine IgG2a mAb 3F6 neutralizes SpA binding to the Fcγ domain, decreasing B-cell activation and protecting mice in a neonatal sepsis model [94]. An optimized and humanized IgG1 version of 3F6 inhibited human IgG binding to SpA, and showed better stability and complement mediated-phagocytosis (OPK) in human umbilical cord blood [95–97]. Recently, a human anti-SpA mAb, 514G3, showed high opsonic activity in vitro. This IgG3 subclass mAb differed from the others owing to its lack of Fc binding to SpA [89]. A potential drawback of this subclass, however, is the characteristically shorter half-life, which may limit utility. Although a phase I study was initiated, no data have been reported [98].

Lipoteichoic acid is a another major cell wall component that inhibits OPK activity of S. aureus in vitro, and induces a cytokine cascade through TLR2 activation [99]. The anti-lipoteichoic acid mouse/human chimeric IgG1 pagibaximab promotes staphylococcal phagocytosis in vitro and protection in mouse and in neonatal rat sepsis models. The mAb was well tolerated and showed a dose-dependent linear pharmacokinetic half-life in a phase I/II, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in very low birth weight neonates [100]. However, in a follow-up phase III study, the use of pagibaximab compared to placebo did not demonstrate a significant reduction in late-onset sepsis in preterm [101]. Recently, Beudeker et al. suggested that the failure of pagibaximab to confer protection in very low birth weight neonates may be a consequence of the insufficient activation of the preterm neonatal complement system [102].

S. aureus: mAbs Against Nutrient and Iron Uptake

The host has developed nutritional immunity to sequester nutrient metals essential to bacterial growth and virulence [103]. Staphylococcus aureus expresses surface-exposed lipoproteins involved in nutrient uptake [104]. The iron-regulated surface determinant (Isd) system scavenges heme-iron from the host and facilitates bacterial survival in iron-limited environments [46]. Anti-IsdA and anti-IsdH mAbs provide protection in a murine sepsis model [105].

Several anti-IsdB mAbs with different mechanisms of action have been described. A mouse anti-IsdB IgG2b mAb with OPK activity demonstrated protection against lethal bacteremia in mice as well as in an indwelling catheter mouse model [106]. A human IgG1 anti-IsdB mAb conferred protection in a mouse sepsis model despite lacking OPK activity—enhanced clearance in the liver combined with increased interferon-γ production by T cells may contribute to this protective effect [107]. Despite the success of these mAbs in preclinical studies, particular attention should be given to safety analyses. In 2007, Merck developed an anti-S. aureus vaccine targeting IsdB, which passed initial safety evaluations with healthy patients in phase I trials, but was terminated in phase II, suggesting harm in patients who were interleukin-2 deficient [108, 109, 109]. It is possible that mAbs may offer a more controlled approach to targeting this essential receptor family and may have a better safety profile, but given this history, exceptional care must be taken.

Recently, a camelid nanobody cross-reactive for the heme-binding pocket of both IsdH and IsdB was described, demonstrating reduced in vitro growth of three pathogenic MRSA strains [110]. Several anti-manganese transport protein C mAbs induced neutrophil respiratory burst activity in vitro, reduced bacterial burden in murine bacteremia, and provided protection in an infant rat model [111].

Pseudomonas aeruginosa

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a Gram-negative bacillus that acts as an opportunistic pathogen, and has long been a target for new drug development given its profile of drug resistance and significant virulence [112]. Anti-pseudomonal mAb development has been a turbulent process for decades, with promising drug candidates showing both successes and failures in clinical trials [113–117].

P. aeruginosa: mAbs Targeting PcrV

The needle-tip protein of the type III secretion system, PcrV, has all the characteristics of an ideal mAb target: it is a highly conserved surface protein that is accessible to antibody binding and is critical for the virulence of the pathogen such that a mAb inhibiting its mechanism can protect against the infection even if no Fc-dependent activity occurs [118–121]. Hence, it is unsurprising that PcrV has proven a desirable and effective target for novel mAbs. In 2002, a research group at the Medical College of Wisconsin developed a murine mAb that binds the C-terminus of PcrV, blocking the translocation of cytotoxins into host cells, and protecting mice in a sepsis model of infection [113]. This mAb became the basis of KB001, a humanized PEGylated Fab antibody that retained the efficacy of its predecessor and advanced into clinical trials. KB001 demonstrated encouraging safety and tolerability in phase I studies [114, 115]. In a phase II study, KB001 reduced airway inflammation markers in patients with cystic fibrosis, but did not reduce the time to need for antibiotic therapy, which the authors attributed to lower levels of PcrV proteins than reported in prior examinations of P. aeruginosa clinical isolates [122, 123].

Numerous other anti-PcrV mAbs have been developed using various methods. Two separate research groups produced and then humanized anti-PcrV mAbs from murine hybridomas, both of which had similar injection-blocking functionality [121, 124]. An additional two groups isolated plasma B cells from patients who were convalescent or chronically infected with P. aeruginosa, and discovered fully human mAbs that block the toxin-injecting function of PcrV [125, 126]. Interestingly, one group published a fully optimized protocol for isolating anti-PcrV antibody-producing plasma cells from patients in order to generate mAbs, including screening methods to ensure that mAbs that successfully inhibit toxin translocation are selected for [127].

P. aeruginosa: mAbs Targeting Psl Exopolysaccharide

Additionally, broadly reactive mAbs to the serotype independent Psl exopolysaccharide have been described [128]. This group employed a target-agnostic screening strategy to identify protective antibodies against P. aeruginosa, rather than targeting known virulence factors. The investigators constructed and screened human single-chain variable fragment phage display libraries, derived from both healthy donors and convalescent patients, against whole bacterial cells. Using this unbiased approach in combination with early functional screening for OPK killing activity, researchers identified mAbs targeting Psl exopolysaccharide—a previously unknown protective antigen linked to P. aeruginosa host cell attachment, biofilm formation, and colonization [128].

The most potent antibody identified, Cam003, demonstrated remarkable breadth and efficacy against phenotypically and geographically diverse P. aeruginosa strains [128]. It recognized Psl from 96% of isolates derived from acute infections and 73% of strains derived from chronic infection (e.g., patients with cystic fibrosis). Importantly, anti-Psl mAbs maintained binding to bacteria recovered from infected animals, indicating stable in vivo expression following acute infection. Mechanistically, Cam003 mediated concentration-dependent OPK activity and blocked P. aeruginosa attachment to cultured epithelial cells, with an Fc-mutant version showing reduced efficacy in vivo, confirming that Fc receptor-dependent mechanisms are important for protective activity. In preclinical models, Cam003 provided dose-dependent prophylactic protection against lethal pneumonia, thermal injury, and corneal infection, reduced bacterial burden in multiple organs, and demonstrated activity against highly virulent cytotoxic strains. These findings established Psl as an accessible, serotype-independent, high-density protective antigen and validates phenotypic whole-cell screening as a powerful strategy for antibody discovery against bacterial pathogens [128].

P. aeruginosa: Anti-Psl and Anti-PcrV Bispecific mAb, Gremubamab

Gremubamab (also known as MEDI3902) is a first-in-class, bivalent, bispecific human IgG1κ mAb engineered to simultaneously target both Psl exopolysaccharide and PcrV [129]. Approximately 99% of clinical isolates screened express one or both targets, making this dual-targeting approach broadly applicable against P. aeruginosa [130, 131]. The unique molecular configuration positions the anti-Psl scFv in the upper hinge region of the anti-PcrV antibody scaffold, providing optimal spacing between binding sites to allow simultaneous engagement of both surface targets.

Gremubamab demonstrated superior efficacy compared with either a parental antibody alone or their combination, achieving enhanced protection through maintenance of each distinct, yet complementary mechanisms of action in a single molecule [129]. In vitro, it exhibits enhanced anti-cytotoxic activity at low concentrations similar to its PcrV binding parent, potent opsonophagocytic killing, and improved inhibition of bacterial attachment to epithelial cells compared with the parental mAbs. Mechanistically, the anti-Psl component is thought to bind to the abundantly expressed surface exopolysaccharide, creating a high local antibody concentration around bacteria that enhances neutrophil recognition, complement activation, and Fc receptor engagement. Upon phagocytosis, the anti-PcrV component inhibits T3S function within the phagosome, allowing for more efficient acidification and intracellular bacterial killing [132]. In preclinical models in both mice and rabbits, MEDI3902 provided dose-dependent protection against multidrug-resistant strains in acute pneumonia, ventilator-associated associated pneumonia, thermal injury, keratitis, and bacteremia models, significantly reducing bacterial burden and inflammatory cytokines while demonstrating synergy with multiple antibiotic classes [129, 133–137]. A phase I study in healthy adults confirmed the drug was well tolerated by participants with any adverse events considered mild or manageable, linear pharmacokinetics maintaining therapeutic levels for 21–29 days after a single infusion, dose-dependent functional activity, and minimal anti-drug antibody responses [138]. Although a phase II study (EVADE) in mechanically ventilated, P. aeruginosa-colonized intensive care unit patients did not meet its primary endpoint of reducing nosocomial pneumonia incidence in the overall population, post-hoc analyses suggested a potential benefit in patient subgroups with lower baseline inflammatory markers (procalcitonin ≤ 0.55 μg/L or absolute neutrophil count ≤ 8.17 × 103/μL), indicating that patient selection and disease severity may be critical factors for therapeutic success [139].

More recently, gremubamab was evaluated in patients with bronchiectasis chronically colonized with P. aeruginosa. Chronic lung infection with P. aeruginosa in this patient population is associated with increased exacerbations, lung function decline, and premature death [140]. In this clinical trial (GREAT-2), 37 patients were randomly assigned to receive either gremubamab at two different doses (500 mg or 1500 mg) or placebo through intravenous infusion every 4 weeks for 12 weeks. The results showed that the 500-mg dose significantly reduced bacterial levels in the lungs compared with placebo, while both doses led to meaningful improvements in patients’ quality of life as measured by respiratory health questionnaires. Additionally, the higher 1500-mg dose significantly reduced the frequency of exacerbations [141]. These findings establish gremubamab as a promising multi-functional immunotherapeutic candidate that addresses the complex challenge of targeting P. aeruginosa in multiple disease settings.

P. aeruginosa: mAbs Targeting Lipopolysaccharide

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is a critical virulence factor for Gram-negative bacteria given its role in inflammation and sepsis, and neutralization of LPS via mAb therapy is a strong potential mechanism for protection [142]. A human IgM mAb directed against P. aeruginosa serotype O11 LPS was initially shown to reduce inflammation and bacterial load in a mouse model of pneumonia, and support the innate immune response by recruiting neutrophils to the site of infection [143]. This mAb, dubbed panobacumab, was investigated clinically by Aridis Pharmaceuticals, and was found to be well tolerated in healthy volunteers with no adverse events reported [144]. In a phase II study, panobacumab was tested as an adjunctive treatment for nosocomial pneumonia caused by P. aeruginosa and demonstrated a statistically shorter time to clinical resolution compared with placebo [145]. Another research group developed a cocktail of LPS-binding IgG2b mAbs with specificity for serotype O5 LPS, which reduced inflammation and airway colony-forming units (CFUs) in a mouse model of pneumonia [146]. These mAbs show therapeutic promise, but the significant diversity of LPS serotypes in P. aeruginosa poses a challenge for breadth of activity of all LPS-targeting mAbs [147].

P. aeruginosa: mAbs Targeting Other Structures

Aridis Pharmaceuticals developed AR-105, a human IgG1 mAb targeting alginate, an exopolysaccharide that forms a thick extracellular layer surrounding P. aeruginosa cells and contributes to biofilm formation [148]. Though this mAb showed promise in early preclinical models as a possible first-line adjunctive treatment, and satisfactory safety and tolerability in phase I studies, it did not reduce the time to clinical cure in infected patients, and the project was abandoned [149–151]. Another group developed a mAb that targets the PilQ-PilA disulfide (QA) region of type IV pilus, which contributes to twitch motility in P. aeruginosa. The anti-QA mAb showed adjunctive efficacy when given in combination with antibiotics [152]. The Barbier research group at the University of West Virginia developed two murine IgG2b mAbs against P. aeruginosa, the first targeting the siderophore FpvA, which slightly reduced CFUs in lung and nasal lavage samples of intranasally infected mice, but the in vivo work was limited to CFU assays, and no survival study was conducted [153]. This group’s second mAb was developed targeting conserved regions of FliC, which is the primary protein subunit of flagella, and is structurally similar across several pathogenic species. This mAb showed protective efficacy in vivo against P. aeruginosa in murine sepsis and pneumonia survival experiments, and in vitro binding across several pathogenic species of concern [154]. Another group developed an IgG2a mAb targeting the outer membrane protein OprF, which is involved in host cell adhesion and biofilm formation. While treatment with this mAb yielded a modest reduction in in vitro biofilm formation, it provided no protection in a murine model of acute pneumonia [155].

Acinetobacter baumannii

Acinetobacter baumannii is an extensively drug-resistant, Gram-negative bacillus that causes 23,000 annual deaths in the USA, and has been declared by the World Health Organization to be of critical concern for the development of novel therapies [156, 157]. Given its propensity for developing sweeping resistance against all known classes of antibiotics, mAb-based therapy has become a common goal for A. baumannii researchers [158]. The main mechanism of pathogenesis for A. baumannii involves immune concealment followed by activation of TLR4 by LPS leading to sepsis, which has been extensively reviewed elsewhere [159]. As such, there are few highly conserved, critical virulence factors for A. baumannii that are logical mAb targets, complicating the approach for mAb development.

A. baumannii: mAbs Targeting Outer Membrane Proteins

Researchers have focused on outer membrane proteins as potential target antigens because they are highly conserved among different A. baumannii isolates and are known to contribute to the bacterium’s virulence and drug resistance [160, 161]. Prominently, outer membrane protein A (OmpA) has shown some promise as a potential vaccine candidate in murine models, making OmpA appear to be an ideal target for mAb therapy [162]. In what may be the most important publication within the scope of A. baumannii-targeting mAbs, the Russo research group at the University at Buffalo, using five independently generated mAbs, demonstrated that OmpA was only accessible to mAb binding in non-encapsulated mutant strains, and not in clinical isolates that have intact capsules. They further demonstrated that genetically ablating the capsule restored the activity of anti-OmpA mAbs, cementing the capsule as a major barrier for OmpA (and likely other outer membrane proteins) targeted therapeutics—a finding that others have noted in similarly encapsulated bacteria but could also be attributable to the mAb quality or the targeted epitope [163, 164]. Given that the capsule is thought to be the primary virulence factor of A. baumannii and has tremendous structural variability, mAbs against outer membrane proteins—or any other surface-bound target that is deeply buried in the capsule polysaccharides—need to be thoroughly screened to ensure that they can reach their epitope, and mediate their antimicrobial function. It is exceptionally important to evaluate these mAbs against diverse clinically important isolates because of the broad heterogeneity in capsule structure and function [165]. Regardless, outer membrane proteins remain a tantalizing epitope that several researchers have attempted to target. One such research team developed an anti-OmpA mAb that had considerable binding and opsonophagocytic activity against non-encapsulated strains in vitro, but no in vivo data were included [166, 167]. A separate research group created mouse mAbs targeting OmpA (also called Omp38 in their studies). These did not confer protection when designed as murine IgG1 antibodies. In contrast, when the mAbs were expressed in a murine IgG2a format, four out of ten showed protection in a lethal pneumonia model and reduced lung, kidney, and liver burden in a bloodstream infection model. This protection was improved when the mAbs were delivered as cocktails targeting different parts of OmpA. It is important to note, however, that survival was only tracked for 72 h, possibly too short a period to assess long-term treatment outcomes in their lung infection model, considering the extensive tissue damage observed in lung histopathology [168].

A. baumannii: mAbs Targeting Capsule

Given that the capsule of A. baumannii might shield critical antigens from mAb targeting, and serves as a major virulence factor, several investigators shifted focus towards developing mAbs that target the capsule itself [165]. This process is complicated by the extensive diversity of capsular carbohydrate structures within the species [169]. The Russo laboratory that initially exposed the challenges of targeting OmpA was also the first to develop anti-capsular mAbs, showing that an IgM mAb that targeted the capsule carbohydrates of A. baumannii strains expressing the K1 capsule type was able to improve bacterial clearance in a rat model of soft-tissue infection [170]. However, their next attempt at an anti-capsular IgG3 mAb targeting the K2 capsule type demonstrated that treatment with this mAb worsened survival outcomes in a mouse pneumonia model, despite encouraging OPK activity in vitro. They suggested that capsule shedding in vivo produced carbohydrate decoys, which bound mAb and formed immune complexes that hindered phagocytic activity, leading to treatment failure [171].

The Spellberg laboratory at the University of Southern California used a target-agnostic approach by immunizing mice with live bacteria to develop several mAbs. These mAbs demonstrated highly potent protective efficacy in mouse models of bloodstream and pulmonary infection, and it was determined ex post facto that these mAbs targeted capsular polysaccharide [172–175]. Their initial mAb, a humanized IgG1, demonstrated not only the ability to enhance opsonophagocytosis but also to alleviate sepsis, exhibit synergistic effectiveness with colistin—the current standard antibiotic for carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii (CRAB)—and maintain efficacy even when any two (but not all three) of complement, macrophages, or neutrophils were depleted. Additionally, it displayed broad binding activity across a varied range of clinical isolates [172, 173]. Their second mAb, another humanized IgG1, had similar therapeutic activity but was shown to have non-overlapping binding to the first mAb, presumably reacting with a different capsule structure [174]. Both of these mAbs were tested for their ability to trigger escape mutation by serial passaging mAb-sensitive A. baumannii strains with mAb. No resistance was detected, supporting the hypothesis that mAbs are less likely to promote resistance development under the same conditions as small-molecule antibiotics [172, 174]. These two mAbs were later used to produce a humanized bispecific mAb, which retained the efficacy of both prior antibodies and was shown to target capsule types K3, K9, and K22 [176, 177]. A third anti-capsular humanized IgG1 mAb was produced that targeted A. baumannii strains with K2, K6, K23, K42, K58, and K81 capsule types, and P. aeruginosa strains with O7 and O9 type O-antigens, likely targeting the sugar pseudaminic acid, which is prominent in each of these oligosaccharides. This mAb showed broad in vitro binding and potent in vivo activity in bloodstream and soft-tissue models of A. baumannii infection, but no efficacy against P. aeruginosa [175, 178]. Differences in the in vivo outcome between this mAb, and the earlier K2 targeting mAb developed by the Russo lab group may be due to the targeted epitope, or the activity of the infecting strains that either mAb was tested against. Additionally, the Spellberg group used their anti-pseudaminic acid mAb to develop and validate a mAb susceptibility assay—conceptually similar to a minimal inhibitory concentration assay for antibiotic susceptibility—which may be useful for determining whether clinical isolates are susceptible or resistant to therapeutic mAbs. However, this method has not been applied to other mAbs or other bacterial species [175].

Additional anti-capsular mAbs raised by other groups include K9 targeting murine IgG mAbs that proved efficacious against pulmonary infection and demonstrated opsonophagocytosis in vitro, another murine IgG1 mAb that targets pseudaminic acid and shows efficacy in a bloodstream infection survival model, and a human IgG mAb that targets capsule type K49 and reduced tissue dissemination in a bloodstream infection model [179–181]. Interestingly, the Reece laboratory that developed a K49 binding mAb also developed human IgG mAbs binding LPS oligosaccharide type OCL1, and the membrane-bound carbapenemase OXA-23, and found that neither of these mAbs had therapeutic potential, possibly because these structures could be shielded by the capsule [181].

Klebsiella pneumoniae

Klebsiella pneumoniae is a pervasive, highly drug-resistant, Gram-negative pathogen that causes opportunistic bacteremia, pneumonia, and urinary tract infections, and colonizes indwelling devices [182]. Additionally, strains of hypervirulent K. pneumoniae have emerged that are able to infect immunocompetent individuals [183]. Similar to A. baumannii, K. pneumoniae relies heavily on capsule-based immune evasion followed by LPS-TLR4 activation for its mechanism of virulence, with few prominent virulence factors that can readily be targeted by mAbs [184]. Therefore, like the research into anti-A. baumannii mAbs, much of the development of mAbs against K. pneumoniae has been done using target-agnostic approaches, revealing capsular carbohydrate, LPS O-antigen, fimbriae, and siderophores as the most attractive targets (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Klebsiella pneumoniae virulence factors and antibody targets. K. pneumoniae: mAbs Targeting LPS O-Antigen

The importance of LPS as a virulence factor for Gram-negative bacteria, surface exposure of its oligosaccharide ‘O-antigen’ component, and relative lack of diversity of O-antigen in K. pneumoniae make O-antigen a feasible target for mAb development [185]. Early work in pursuit of mAbs against O-antigen made two critical observations: (1) that mAbs directed against the O-antigen of LPS were able to penetrate the capsule and (2) that the predominance of specific O-serotypes in clinically relevant isolates could enable a single mAb to broadly neutralize most isolates of K. pneumoniae [186, 187]. Some efforts to harness this potential showed that the capsule could block some mAbs from binding to the O-antigen. This outcome may be attributed to specific capsules being better at obstructing access to the O-antigen, which may be further influenced by the bacterial growth stage and culture conditions, and is likely dependent on the particular mAb and epitope involved [164, 188]. An IgG1κ mAb produced by Arsanis Biosciences demonstrated that O-antigen targeting can be efficacious without penetrating the capsule, as the binding and neutralization of LPS was able to protect rabbits in a bacteremia model of infection, and endotoxin-sensitized mice against directly injected LPS in a sepsis model. They further demonstrated that although the mAb had complement depositing and opsonophagocytic properties in vitro, these mechanisms of protection were secondary, and could be dispensed without losing efficacy in vivo, suggesting that neutralization of LPS/TLR4 activity was sufficient for protection in their models [189]. Arsanis Biosciences also recently developed mAbs that target multiple sub-serotypes of O3 LPS, providing broad coverage to their O-antigen targeting mAbs, though no in vivo data have yet been shown [190].

Significant efforts towards developing mAbs against the O-antigen have been made by AstraZeneca. They developed two antibodies against O1 type LPS—one that had opsonizing capability but no ability to neutralize LPS/TLR4 activity, and another that neutralized LPS, but did not opsonize. With these two mAbs they demonstrated via in vivo modeling that although both had efficacy in therapeutic models with wild-type mice, only the opsonizing mAb promoted survival in prophylactic models, or in mice with TLR4 deficiencies, indicating that some LPS-driven TLR4 activity is beneficial for protection [191]. This opsonizing mAb (KPE33) was advanced to further testing, but demonstrated no activity against another common LPS serotype, O2 [192]. They then expanded their mAb discovery efforts via phage display, using a unique target-agnostic screening method that enriched for binders against surface antigens using whole-cells from capsule-deficient K. pneumoniae, followed by a second round of screening against the same strain but with the capsule intact, selecting for capsule penetration. This approach yielded a humanized IgG1 mAb called B39, which binds an epitope common to O1 and O2 LPS types, greatly improving the binding breadth of this mAb over its predecessor. B39 also demonstrated robust efficacy in in vivo models against encapsulated wild type O1 and O2 strains of K. pneumoniae [193].

K. pneumoniae: mAbs Targeting Surface Proteins

Continuing with target-agnostic mAb discovery, AstraZeneca discovered MrkA, the main subunit of type 3 fimbriae that contributes to biofilm formation and adhesion to both biotic and abiotic surfaces, to be a valid target that is not excluded by capsule and independent of the O-serotype. Monoclonal antibodies raised against MrkA were efficacious in vivo in a pneumonia model of infection [194]. Two additional target-agnostic mAb discovery campaigns have resulted in human IgG MrkA antibodies that have in vivo activity and extensive strain binding [195, 196]. Another research group recently discovered humanized IgG mAbs that cross-react between Escherichia coli and K. pneumoniae FimH antigens, which function as mannose-binding adhesins on the tip of type 1 fimbriae. FimH is essential for uropathogenic strains of E. coli and K. pneumoniae to colonize and infect the urothelium and kidney. Monoclonal antibody inhibition of FimH effectively protected against urinary tract infections (UTI) by reducing bladder and kidney CFUs in a mouse model of infection with both pathogens [197].

K. pneumoniae: mAbs Targeting Capsule

The Fries laboratory at Stony Brook University has pursued anti-capsular mAbs, first targeting the K1 capsule that is most associated with hypervirulent K. pneumoniae. The two mAbs (IgG1 and IgG3) developed in this initial work were able to reduce CFUs and improve survival in both intratracheal and intraperitoneal infection models, though the two mAbs working in conjunction outperformed either of them alone [198]. In their next publication, the same laboratory group developed two IgG3 mAbs that target the capsule of clade 2 ST258 K. pneumoniae, which represents a broad genetic group that has unique capsular carbohydrates [199]. The resultant mAbs were able to promote opsonophagocytosis, neutrophil-mediated killing, reactive oxygen species release, NETosis, complement deposition, and decreased lung CFUs and inflammation in a murine pneumonia model, though no protection from lethal infection was included [200]. Seeking to improve breadth of activity, the Fries group immunized mice with capsule carbohydrates representing the three most common capsule assembly loci that occur in clinical isolates and developed an IgG2b mAb with cross-clade activity. This mAb was capable of serum-dependent killing that translated into significant CFUs and inflammatory cytokine reductions of infections caused by diverse isolates in an intratracheal pneumonia model, though again protection studies were not reported [201]. Another research group recently discovered a mAb targeting capsule type K64, which is common in highly virulent K. pneumoniae, and has emerged worldwide as a prominent lineage. The K64-targeting mAb directs serum-dependent bactericidal activity, opsonophagocytic uptake, and prophylactic efficacy in a bacteremia model of infection [188]. Targeting the K. pneumoniae capsular polysaccharide with mAbs remains challenging from a clinical perspective because of the high degree of antigenic diversity, with more than 140 capsule types genetically predicted [202].

Mycobacterium tuberculosis

Mycobacterium tuberculosis is an obligate human pathogen, and the cause of tuberculosis—one of the most prevalent and lethal infectious diseases for humankind. Mycobacterium tuberculosis caused more than 10 million new infections in 2021 and 2023, with more than 10% mortality [203, 204]. This excessive lethality is owed in part to this organism's extensive resistance to many classes of antibiotics, the long treatment courses required to clear the pathogen, and its ability to evade the host’s immune response. Therefore, the development of novel therapeutics for M. tuberculosis is critical, and mAbs are emerging as a strategy of interest [203–205].

The Changing Dogma Surrounding Antibodies for M. tuberculosis

For decades, antibodies were dismissed in tuberculosis because people with more severe disease had higher antibody levels—suggesting these antibodies were just a response to more bacteria, not a cause of protection [206]. This observation reinforced a T-cell-centric paradigm and the prevailing dogma that antibodies are ineffective because M. tuberculosis resides intracellularly within macrophages and within shielded granulomas for life. However, it is clear that antibodies provide protection against other intracellular pathogens, including Salmonella and others, through Fc-mediated mechanisms—including opsonophagocytosis, complement activation, and immune-cell recruitment—rather than direct neutralization [207, 208]. Over the past two decades, systems-level analyses have shown that antibody quality, including their ability to engage immune cells and drive functional responses, rather than the quantity of these responses, determines whether an individual controls infection (protection) or progresses to active disease. Functional profiling showed that individuals with latent infection or resistance exhibit antibodies with enhanced Fc-receptor binding, glycosylation, and effector function compared to those with active tuberculosis [209].

More recent systems and translational studies have strengthened this paradigm shift. In nonhuman primates, intravenous BCG vaccination—capable of inducing near-sterilizing immunity—identified antigen-specific antibodies, particularly IgM, among the strongest correlates of protection and rapid bacterial clearance in the lung [210, 211]. Together, these findings support a model in which antibodies penetrate granulomas, recruit local immune cells, and contribute to bacterial control, analogous with the penetration of checkpoint inhibitors into tumors where they interact with diverse immune cells and mobilize responses within tumors.

M. tuberculosis: mAbs Targeting Surface Antigens

Mechanistic studies have provided direct evidence that both polysaccharide and protein surface targeting antibodies can contribute to protection. Work on the heparin-binding hemagglutinin adhesin demonstrated that antibodies targeting this surface protein can limit bacterial dissemination in vivo [212, 213]. The heparin-binding hemagglutinin adhesin antibody, 4057 D2, led to reduced lethality in the mouse challenge model, and reduced dissemination of M. tuberculosis from the lungs to the spleen [214]. Similarly, an alpha-crystalline-specific IgA also conferred protection by limiting M. tuberculosis growth in the lung [215]. Parallel studies identified antibodies to mycobacterial glycans, including lipoarabinomannan, as functional mediators of protection including SMITB, which robustly protected animals against a lethal M. tuberculosis challenge [216]. These lipoarabinomannan-specific antibodies were further demonstrated to drive antimicrobial control via opsonophagocytosis [217, 218].

mAbs to a Variety of Targets can Limit M. tuberculosis in an Fc-Dependent Manner

To define whether antibodies solely targeting the surface alone were sufficient to drive M. tuberculosis control, a cross-comparative study tested two dozen M. tuberculosis-specific antibodies sourced from global laboratories [219]. The study highlighted that antibodies to an array of secreted toxins, surface proteins, and polysaccharides were able to control M. tuberculosis in vivo. Moreover, deeper analysis of LAM-specific antibodies, not all LAM-specific antibodies were able to drive antimicrobial control, highlighting the importance of the targeted epitope. Strikingly, some LAM-specific antibodies drove enhanced bacterial replication, whereas others, like A194, drove enhanced bacterial control. Further in vivo analyses using both tissue-specific immune cell analysis and single-cell transcriptomics highlighted that protective M. tuberculosis-specific antibodies functional via both canonical opsonophagocytic mechanisms, but also novel innate immune functions and in some instances required the adaptive immune activity [219–222]. Collectively, M. tuberculosis-specific antibodies mediated protection in an Fc-dependent manner, requiring cross-communication with immune cells within the lung to mediate their antimicrobial functions. Together, these studies challenge the notion that intracellular localization precludes antibody efficacy, instead demonstrating that antibodies act by engaging infected phagocytes and reshaping antimicrobial responses both via the recruitment of canonical and non-canonical opsonophagocytic mechanisms, paving a path for future M. tuberculosis-specific monoclonal therapeutic discovery to complement and potentially accelerate antibiotic therapies.

Biofilms

The growth of microbial biofilms in wounds and on epithelial surfaces represents a significant hazard to human health, as they protect resident microbes from antimicrobial drugs and immune activity [223–225], and enable chronic or recurrent infections by housing persister cells [226]. Prevention of biofilm formation and disruption of existing biofilms have therefore become attractive strategies for novel therapeutics, and are enabled by the reliance of many biofilm-forming bacteria on common structures [227].

Biofilms: mAbs Targeting DNABII

Perhaps the most prominent biofilm structure that is shared by diverse bacterial pathogens is the DNABII family of proteins, which are ubiquitous across eubacteria as structural support for intracellular DNA [227]. In some species, DNABII proteins also migrate outside of the cell to coordinate the extracellular DNA that is a major component of biofilms [228]. Antibody targeting of extracellular DNABII has been observed to cause DNABII to dissociate from the extracellular DNA, thereby shifting the equilibrium of the protein out of the biofilm and causing structural failure and collapse [228].

The Bakaletz research group at Ohio State University found that polyclonal rabbit sera directed against DNABII were able to disrupt bacterial biofilms, rendering the resident bacteria susceptible to antibiotic therapy [229]. This group then moved forward to develop a cocktail of mAbs directed at the DNA binding tips of DNABII proteins that were able to collapse the extracellular DNA matrix, releasing resident bacteria into the planktonic state in in vitro assays. Critically, this effect was observed across four relevant pathogenic species (Moraxella catarrhalis, Burkholderia cenocepacia, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Staphylococcus aureus) in in vitro assays. Furthermore, treatment with these anti-DNABII mAbs improved infection outcomes in rabbit models of otitis media caused by non-typable Haemophilus influenzae, and murine models of pneumonia caused by P. aeruginosa when coupled with tobramycin treatment [230]. Over a series of subsequent publications, this group has since demonstrated that antigen-binding antibody fragments (Fabs) directed at the DNA binding tips had comparable efficacy to full antibodies. Furthermore, this group used peptides derived from the DNA binding tips to immunize animals, successfully preventing otitis media biofilm infections in a chinchilla model. They also demonstrated efficacy against Acinetobacter baumannii and Streptococcus pneumoniae, and have shown that their antibodies disrupt biofilms formed by two genera of bacteria together [231–236]. This technology is being clinically developed by Clarametyx Biosciences as the humanized mAb CMTX-101, which at the time of writing is being assessed for safety and efficacy in patients with cystic fibrosis in a phase Ib/IIa study [237–239].

Another company, Trellis Biosciences, has adopted a similar strategy in developing a native human IgG1 mAb known as TRL1068 that reacts with DNABII’s DNA binding tips. Early experiments with TRL1068 demonstrated its ability to disrupt biofilms formed by MRSA and P. aeruginosa, and to potentiate daptomycin therapy in a mouse model of implant infection, reducing the CFU burden of implant-bound bacteria by nearly 100-fold more than daptomycin alone [240]. Focusing on the present clinical problem of biofilm-contaminated implants and medical devices, this group later demonstrated that TRL1068 potentiates vancomycin treatment against S. aureus and imipenem treatment against A. baumannii in rodent models of catheterization and orthopedic implantation [241, 242]. Trellis has also carried out a phase I clinical study with TRL1068, wherein patients with chronic and drug-resistant periprosthetic joint infections were dosed alongside perioperative antibiotics ahead of surgical removal of the infected implant and current standard of care procedures. Treatment with TRL1068 eliminated the contamination of explanted prostheses for 3 of 11 patients and prevented a relapse of infection following implantation of new prostheses within the 169-day study for all treated patients, while one in four patients given placebo had a relapse of P. aeruginosa infection. Furthermore, no adverse events were associated with TRL1068 treatment [243]. Following this promising proof of concept, a phase II study has been initiated and is recruiting at the time of this writing [244].

Biofilms: mAbs Targeting Other Structures

Another structural element of bacterial biofilms that is exploitable as a mAb target is poly-N-acetyl glucosamine (PNAG). Acetylated and deacetylated forms of PNAG make up an adhesin that is structurally critical in biofilms formed by S. aureus, S. epidermidis, A. baumannii, B. pertussis, E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and Y. pestis [245]. Two human IgG1 mAbs specific to acetylated or deacetylated PNAG were developed and employed in a murine model of S. aureus infection, and demonstrated to synergistically rescue 90% of mice, showing potential for PNAG targeting as a therapeutic strategy [245]. However, robust surveys of these mAbs’ activity against all the above pathogens have not been reported.

An additional strategy that has been implemented with success is development of mAbs against Curli—a heteropolymeric amyloid fibril that serves as a scaffold and promoter of adhesion in biofilms formed by the Enterobacteriaceae family [246]. Monoclonal antibody targeting of the polymerizing subunits of Curli inhibits the formation of the amyloid. This in turn abrogated the construction of Salmonella typhimurium biofilms, damaged existing S. typhimurium biofilms, synergized with ampicillin to eliminate the biofilm CFU burden, and promoted phagocytosis by macrophages in in vitro assays. Using a murine model of implantation with pre-colonized catheters, it was demonstrated that anti-Curli mAbs promoted a phagocytic response [246]. This targeting strategy may have promise against Enterobacteriaceae, but further infection model screening has not yet been carried out.

Another research group developed several human IgG1 mAbs that bind biofilms formed by S. aureus, which have broad chemical and structural diversity between clinical isolates, with variable inclusion of PNAG being a major determinant of biofilm phenotype. Furthermore, they demonstrated that a single antibody could target both planktonic cells and biofilm structures, and though no direct in vivo efficacy was demonstrated, the group posits that these mAbs could serve as a basis for therapeutic antibody-drug conjugates [247].

Single-Domain Antibodies

While traditional IgG formats have proven to be effective against several bacterial targets covered in this review, novel antibody formats may overcome some of their limitations. Camelid single-domain antibodies, which possess a single antigen-binding region known as a VHH (Variable Heavy domain of a Heavy chain only antibody), are produced by a unique arm of the camelid B-cell compartment and completely lack light chains and CH1 domains. VHHs, analogous to Fab fragments, are 12–15 kDa and can mediate antigen binding as stand-alone molecules (Fig. 4). Their small size, exceptional stability, and ability to access cryptic conserved epitopes have made these molecules attractive for targeting viral glycoproteins, as is evidenced by the growing body of literature highlighting their anti-viral activity [248, 249]. Additionally, because VHHs do not require a light chain partner, they can serve as ideal building blocks for multi-specifics, leading to better coverage and potency where required [250]. Though the lack of an Fc domain on VHH limits effector function, this can be corrected by fusing the molecule with a human Fc [250]. The most obvious place to deploy anti-bacterial VHHs is against protein targets such as secreted toxins. While VHHs against toxins such as Shiga toxin from E. coli and lethal factor/edema factor from B. anthracis (and many others) have been described, S. aureus and C. difficile are two toxin-producing pathogens for which VHHs have been best characterized [251, 252]. Jangra and colleagues discovered VHHs targeting the S. aureus beta-hemolysin from an Indian desert camel, one of which was found to have toxin-neutralizing activity [253]. For C. difficile, several groups have discovered anti-toxin VHHs by immunizing llamas with a portion of the TcdB C-terminal repeats of polysaccharide-binding (CROPs) domain, or alpacas with TcdA or TcdB holotoxins carrying different point mutations on the GTD [254–256]. Kordus and colleagues have reported multiple VHHs with TcdA- and TcdB-binding activity. TcdB-targeting VHHs, specifically, cover epitopes on GTD, DDRB, and CROPs domains, with those targeting the GTD and CROPs domains having toxin neutralization activity. The authors highlight the diagnostic potential of these molecules, using them to develop potent ELISA to quantify TcdA and TcdB in vitro and in cecal or fecal contents of infected mice—opening new avenues for toxin detection tools in clinical samples [256]. However, they may also possess therapeutic potential should they be incorporated into a multi-specific antibody [255, 256]. Hussack et al. focused on VHHs targeting the CROPs domain for therapeutic applications [257]. As stand-alone VHHs, none of the 29 candidates highlighted had toxin neutralization activity. When either linked by a flexible linker or appended to a human Fc, however, these molecules were able to mediate TcdB neutralization. VNA2, a VHH from Schmidt and colleagues, capitalizes on the distinct properties of VHHs against the C. difficile toxins [258]. This VNA (VHH-based neutralizing agent) is a tetramer of four VHHs; two targeting TcdA, and two targeting TcdB. VNA2 potently neutralizes both toxins and demonstrated protection in mouse, hamster, and gnotobiotic piglet challenge models when administered as a protein or by adenoviral gene therapy [258]. This example highlights the promise and ease of VHH concatenation to achieve the breadth and potency required of a potential therapeutic. While TcdA-specific VHHs have been characterized [259], it is unlikely that these would progress to the clinic because of disease enhancement [29]. Another fascinating approach involves probiotic platforms using an engineered Lactobacillus strain expressing two TcdB VHHs and a Saccharomyces boulardi strain expressing a TcdA/TcdB tetra-specific VHH. Each showed promising results in preclinical models [260, 261].

In addition to secreted toxins, surface-associated protein antigens, and even carbohydrates, have been the subject of successful VHH discovery efforts [262, 263]. Valenciano-Bellido and colleagues isolated VHHs against S. aureus hemoglobin receptors IsdH and IsdB by alpaca immunization and phage display selections. A single VHH, termed VHH6, was found to bind to both the NEAT2 domain of IsdB and the NEAT3 domain of IsdH, and was able to inhibit bacterial growth of multiple S. aureus strains [110]. Mei and colleagues isolated 18 VHHs from a synthetic yeast display library with a range of binding affinities to S. aureus ClfA [264]. One of these VHHs, CNb1, was able to potently block ClfA binding to fibrinogen, the mechanism by which S. aureus attaches to host cells. An anti-ClfA VHH may be an attractive candidate to pair with an anti-alpha toxin VHH, as this target pair is the subject of a phase I clinical study being run by AstraZeneca [265]. Finally, while previous efforts to target lipid A with mAbs were unsuccessful, the Davies group reported on a VHH that is able to bind lipid A on a native bacterium and promote membrane instability, and ultimately bacterial killing [266]. While additional engineering of this molecule was required to achieve anti-bacterial activity, this is an elegant example of how the small size of single-domain antibodies can be advantageous when targeting sites that would be otherwise inaccessible to traditional IgG modalities on the surface of a bacterium.

Antibody-Antibiotic Conjugates

Although antibiotics remain the standard of care for most bacterial infections, there are many pitfalls with systemic antibiotic administration, including (1) increased selective pressure for AMR strains, (2) gut dysbiosis, which can lead to increased incidence of microbiome imbalance and C. difficile infections, and (3) non-specific or off-target toxicity. To circumvent these issues, a novel approach was employed to create antibody-antibiotic conjugates (AACs), which take advantage of linker chemistry similarly employed by many antibody-drug conjugates that have been or are currently being developed, especially for the delivery of toxic warheads in cancer (Fig. 5). In this way, an antibiotic can be chemically attached to free cysteine or lysine residues of a mAb and only released in the lysosome of cells that take them up, usually through the action of cathepsin B or other lysosomal proteases [267]. This could be a very powerful tool if the AAC is targeting the cell surface of a bacteria, as the AAC would be internalized with the bacteria and provide targeted delivery of the antibiotic inside the cell. Mechanism of action for antibody-antibiotic conjugates (AACs). An AAC is administered in vivo and opsonizes specific circulating or tissue-infiltrating bacteria based on the monoclonal antibody target. Macrophages and other phagocytic cells internalize the opsonized bacteria through Fc-mediated phagocytosis. As the phagosome fuses with the lysosome to form the phagolysosome, the pH drops, which enhances catalytic activity of proteases present in the vesicle. These proteases cleave the linker that attaches the antibiotic to the monoclonal antibody and release antibiotic from the AAC. The antibiotics are then free to bind to phagocytosed bacteria and exert their function to induce bacterial death and lysis

Although patents were filed for variations on antibody-antibiotic conjugates as early as 1989, the first example of an AAC in the literature was a S. aureus mAb called THIOMAB, which targeted cell wall teichoic acids, linked to a novel rifampicin-class antibiotic (dmDNA31 or rifalogue) via a protease-cleavable linker [268, 269]. The IgG1 AAC outperformed vancomycin, THIOMAB alone, and the AAC with a non-cleavable linker in clearance of intracellular reservoirs of S. aureus within macrophages, endothelial cells, and epithelial cells. Furthermore, when administered as treatment in a mouse model of S. aureus bacteremia, the AAC provided greater protection than systemic administration of vancomycin, rifalogue, and a non-cleavable form of the AAC [268]. In a phase I study, the AAC, dubbed DSTA4637S, exhibited a pharmacokinetic profile similar to a traditional mAb in healthy adults. Furthermore, there was limited systemic release of the antibiotic (dmDNA31) and no ADA. The safety profile was also favorable, with limited adverse events compared with the placebo group [270]. However, when DSTA4637S was taken to a phase Ib study in S. aureus bacteremia, 40% of the AAC-treated patients withdrew from the study because of infusion-related reactions. There was also lower exposure (maximum concentration and area under the concentration–time curve), compared with the prior phase I study in healthy adults, highlighting the importance of establishing these metrics in the target patient population [271]. Similarly, this antibody conjugate exhibited a shorter half-life in infected mice than total antibody, with clearance rates about four times greater [272]. This increased clearance rate was also observed in naïve mice, rats, and cynomolgus monkey models, with clearance rates of the AAC 2–3 faster than antibody in naïve animals [273]. This may indicate that for prolonged benefit from an AAC, it may need to be administered more frequently than a traditional antibody.

In another study, an AAC with vancomycin conjugated to a human mAb targeting S. aureus protein A (M0662) was tested for efficacy against prosthetic joint infections caused by S. aureus. In contrast to the THIOMAB conjugate, this AAC was only able to effectively clear S. aureus in vitro in several cell lines when used in combination with free vancomycin [274]. It was found that the AAC was only effective in clearing intracellular S. aureus reservoirs, while vancomycin was needed in combination to clear both intracellular and extracellular bacteria. Furthermore, in a prosthetic joint infection model in mice, although vancomycin administration was able to reduce bacterial load, the AAC/vancomycin combination therapy provided greater protection than the antibiotic alone [274]. However, the fact that the AAC must be administered in conjunction with antibiotics limits its usefulness, as the adverse effects of systemic antibiotic administration would not be circumvented.

Beyond S. aureus, an IgG1 AAC was also developed against Pseudomonas aeruginosa LPS O-antigen (mAb 26F8) by conjugation to G2637 (an arylomycin analog) [275]. In this study, the investigators were able to establish intracellular concentrations of G2637 in macrophages as high as 16 µM 2 h after phagocytosis, which was an approximate increase of 26-fold in the intracellular environment, based on starting conjugated antibiotics. This increased local concentration of G2637 was able to enhance the killing ability of macrophages against P. aeruginosa in vitro. Unfortunately, there was no therapeutic benefit of this AAC in an in vivo model of acute P. aeruginosa pneumonia in mice [275], highlighting again the challenges of designing an AAC that is active both in vitro and in vivo.

Although ESKAPE pathogens have been the major focus on AAC development to date, other pathogens have been targeted. In one case, an antimicrobial peptide was conjugated to a mAb targeting a drug efflux pump on Neisseria gonorrhoeae [276]. This antibody-drug conjugate had a linker that was susceptible to an IgA protease that is secreted by N. gonorrhoeae to more specifically target the pathogen. This ADC was shown to reduce toxicity to human cells, compared with the antimicrobial peptide, and kills MDR N. gonorrhoeae in vitro when cleaved from the mAb [276]. Although further work would be needed to show the utility of these approaches, these studies highlight the versatility in which this technology could be deployed.

Overall, there are several factors that must be considered when designing an AAC. The most important factor is the antibiotic itself, as the antibiotic must be amenable to attachment to an antibody, retain activity (or gain activity) in the highly acidic environment of the phagolysosome, be retained within the host cell long enough to have its effect, and be robust enough to kill AMR strains of the target bacteria. Second, the antibody directs the response; therefore, the mAb target must have sufficient surface coverage to allow for internalization of antibiotic in quantities that exceed the MIC threshold needed to kill bacteria and provide broad coverage across many strains of bacteria within a given species. Last, the linker is likely to play a role in the amount of protection an AAC may provide. There are several variations on linkers that have been used for AACs in the past but thought should be put into the linker design in an effort to improve pharmacokinetics, alter hydrophobicity, or change other qualities that may be needed for a given target. In conclusion, although progress has been made with AACs and similar molecules, there is still much to be learned before one is able to move from the clinic to a fully approved drug.

In Vivo Expressed Biologics

Therapeutic mAbs have the advantage of being highly specific, safe, and provide immediate protection upon delivery even in immuno-compromised patients, a population at high risk for any bacterial infections. However, the manufacture of clinical-grade mAbs from large-scale bioreactors is time intensive and costly owing to challenges associated with expression, aggregation, purification, and post-translational modifications [277]. These obstacles can be eliminated by in vivo expressed biologic platforms that use the body as its own bioreactor upon delivery of nucleic acid-encoded mAb sequences. Gene therapy was historically developed using DNA sequences packaged in a viral vector such as adeno-associated virus (AAV) [278]. More recently, non-viral vector technologies have been explored such as synthetic DNA plasmid delivered by electroporation or messenger RNA (mRNA) formulated with lipid nanoparticles (mRNA/LNP) [277, 279]. Each platform requires its own delivery system and presents its own challenges and advantages. However, despite hundreds of preclinical studies, only few nucleic acid-encoded mAbs have been tested in the clinic.

AAV-Based mAb Delivery

Adeno-associated virus is a small 25-nm non-enveloped virus that can infect dividing and quiescent cells. Adeno-associated viruses are considered non-pathogenic and have not been associated with human diseases. Hence, they can be safely utilized as vectors for transgene delivery [280]. There are several serotypes of AAV and each displays various tissue tropisms making them attractive for selective organ delivery [281]. Adeno-associated virus can enter target cells, transfer its genome to the nucleus and maintain long-term expression [282]. There are currently four gene replacement therapies clinically approved for treatment of hemophilia, macular degeneration, or neurological disorders [283–286]. However, AAVs are only able to encapsulate DNA sequences < 3.3 kb long, limiting the format of mAb they can deliver. Numerous preclinical studies demonstrated the feasibility of the AAV platform to deliver protective doses of IgG mAbs, reaching a peak of expression in sera after 14–21 days and sustaining activity for several months against viral targets such as SIV, Ebola, and HIV [287–289]. Anti-bacterial toxin mAbs delivered via AAV provide protection against anthrax, and C. difficile toxin challenge disease models [290, 291]. Interestingly, the AAV platform promotes long-term expression and protective activity of mAbs in other formats such as the anti-P. aeruginosa bispecific mAb MEDI3902 or an anti-neurotoxin VHH-Fc antibody [292, 293]. Although AAV-encoding mAbs induced circulating levels >100 mg/mL and demonstrated protection in HIV-preclinical models, the platform demonstrated immunogenicity issues and did not progress beyond phase I clinical studies [294]. Some anti-drug antibody responses associated with some serotypes have been described and resulted in accelerated clearance in cynomolgus monkeys [295]. Most humans exhibit antibody titers against some AAV serotypes, which may induce anti-drug antibodies and immunogenicity after redosing, causing accelerated clearance and potential life-threatening toxicity [296–298]. Together, these data suggest that the AAV viral vector delivery may not be the fastest or safest platform to deliver DNA-encoded mAbs.

DNA Plasmid-Based mAb Delivery

In the early 1990s, the injection of a “naked” plasmid DNA in skeletal muscle resulted in luciferase expression in mice, introducing a potential easy method for gene delivery [299]. However, naked DNA vaccines resulted in weak cellular and antibody responses in clinical studies, suggesting that in contrast to viral vectors, plasmid DNA would necessitate an efficiently designed delivery system to achieve efficacy [300]. Over the years, several strategies were evaluated to enhance plasmid expression, and the “adaptive” in vivo electroporation was first described in the early 2000s [301]. Hyaluronidase, an enzyme breaking down hyaluronic acid, facilitates DNA progression through the extra-cellular matrix and enhances plasmid uptake, either when co-delivered with the DNA or before DNA administration via electroporation [302]. Unlike the AAV vector platform, non-viral DNA is a cold-chain independent system that facilitates the delivery of large DNA sequences, does not elicit viral vector immunity (thus permitting repeat administration), and offers both cost-effectiveness and scalability [278, 279].

DNA-based mAb (DmAb) delivery has demonstrated efficacy across multiple preclinical models, enabling serum expression of therapeutic mAb levels, either as single agents or dual IgG combinations, against targets including severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), influenza A and B, and P. aeruginosa [303–305]. The Weiner laboratory achieved a significant milestone by establishing the clinical feasibility of the DmAb platform in a phase I trial [306]. In this study, healthy volunteers received up to four intramuscular administrations of a plasmid encoding two anti-SARS-CoV-2 mAbs, delivered via CELLECTRA electroporation. Monoclonal antibodies were detectable in sera from all participants, reaching peak concentrations of 1.61 µg/mL with sustained expression over 72 weeks and demonstrating neutralizing activity in pseudovirus assays [307]. Despite its promise, the DmAb platform has notable limitations. First, the peak expression typically occurs 14–21 days post-delivery, similar to AAV-based platforms, which may be too late for effective treatment of acute bacterial infections where rapid therapeutic intervention is critical. Second, DmAb delivery requires an electroporation device, necessitating specialized medical staff training, which could potentially limit widespread deployment in clinical settings.

mRNA-Based mAb Delivery

Unlike DNA-based approaches, mRNA drives protein expression by directly engaging the intracellular translation machinery within the cytoplasm, thereby circumventing the need for nuclear entry and eliminating the potential risk of insertional mutagenesis [300]. For in vivo IgG mAb expression, co-delivery of mRNA/LNP formulations encoding both heavy and light chains is required (Fig. 6) [277]. Following endosomal escape, translated heavy and light chains undergo cytoplasmic assembly, with the fully formed IgG subsequently secreted into circulation. Preclinical models demonstrate that intravenous mRNA/LNP administration at doses that range from 0.1 to 3 mg/kg produces peak mAb expression within approximately 24 h [277]. This rapid expression kinetics makes the platform particularly advantageous for passive immunization with anti-infective mAbs when immediate protection is essential. Through mRNA optimization strategies that extend the half-life, protein expression can be sustained for several days; as a result, mRNA-encoded mAbs may demonstrate superior pharmacokinetic profiles compared with their recombinant protein counterparts [308]. Synthetic mRNA is produced in vitro from DNA plasmids by transcription using T7 or similar RNA polymerases. The mature mRNA comprises five key cis-acting elements: a 5′ cap, a 5′ untranslated region, an open reading frame, a 3′ untranslated region, and a poly(A) tail. Each element can be engineered to enhance expression using several distinct processes [309]. The cap and poly(A) tail influence translation efficiency and stability, while the 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions are thought to regulate translation and the mRNA half-life. The open reading frame is often enriched for GC content and incorporates modified nucleosides such as N1-methylpseudouridine to dampen innate immune sensing. Further gains can be achieved with machine learning-guided algorithms through codon optimization and sequence designs that improve mRNA stability [310]. Similar data-driven selection strategies have been applied to untranslated regions, mined from human tissue libraries to improve expression [311, 312]. Early preclinical studies following intravenous administration of mRNA/LNP formulations reported functional IgG expression with protective serum levels up to 170 μg/mL against HIV, influenza, SARS-CoV-2, and Chikungunya virus [313–317]. Functional expression of mRNA-encoded IgG combinations can be challenging because of heavy and light chain mispairing after a single intravenous administration. In vivo expression of scFv-Fc targeting S. aureus, IgA targeting P. aeruginosa, and VHH targeting botulinum neurotoxin or C. difficile TcdA/TcdB have also been reported [314, 318–320]. The mRNA platform is particularly attractive for VHH because their smaller size enables a greater mRNA payload per LNP, potentially increasing expression relative to IgG. As previously stated, the absence of an Fc domain reduces serum half-life and effector functions [250]. This shortcoming has been addressed by codelivery of a two-component system, comprising a tagged VHH and an anti-tag effector antibody. This approach prolonged exposure and expanded the therapeutic window of a tetra-specific anti-C. difficile VHH in mice and piglets [319].

Following these promising preclinical data, an mRNA-encoded mAb has been evaluated in a single phase I clinical study [321]. The mRNA-1944, encoding a neutralizing anti-Chikungunya IgG, was delivered in a single intravenous infusion to 38 healthy volunteers at doses up to 0.6 mg/kg. The study showed dose-dependent expression, with serum levels >1 μg/mL at 24 h and persisting for up to 16 weeks [322]. However, intravenous delivery is not convenient for broad deployment because of infusion time, healthcare cost, and potential adverse events. Intramuscular administration would be more practical but has not yet been shown to achieve therapeutic serum concentrations, likely owing to the limited number of transfectable cells at the injection site. Future success will require mRNA sequence optimization, formulations targeting specific organs or cell types, or evaluation of alternative platforms such as self-amplifying RNA or circular RNA. One considerable challenge that mRNA/LNP technology has faced historically is that the delicate structure of mRNA requires cold-chain storage and distribution. Recent work has demonstrated that mAb-encoding mRNA/LNP formulations can be lyophilized and distributed as a dry powder. Under optimized formulation conditions, mAb encoding mRNA/LNPs were able to be stored at room temperature for a full month before degradation was observed in particle size and encapsulation efficiency, mRNA amount, or in vivo efficacy [323]. This finding may greatly increase the practicality and affordability of mAbs delivered as mRNA/LNP.

Additional Emerging Technologies

Fc-Engineering

Fc-engineering has become a critical part of monoclonal therapeutic design, with both Fc-“silencing” and half-life-extension strategies now fundamental to modern antibody development. In settings where Fc effector activity could create liability rather than benefit, developers have often attenuated Fc function either by selecting low-effector isotypes or by introducing silencing mutations: in oncology, this logic is exemplified by the widespread use of IgG4 backbones for programmed cell death protein-1 antibodies such as pembrolizumab (Merck), nivolumab (Bristol Myers Squibb), cemiplimab (Regeneron/Sanofi), and dostarlimab (GSK/Tesaro), typically with hinge-stabilizing S228P, whereas the cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 blockade illustrates how isotype choice can be tuned differently, with ipilimumab from Bristol Myers Squibb as IgG1 and tremelimumab from AstraZeneca as IgG2 [324]. In infectious diseases, Fc attenuation has more commonly been achieved by point mutation than by wholesale IgG1-to-IgG4 conversion; for example, AstraZeneca’s anti-SARS-CoV-2 combination tixagevimab-cilgavimab (AZD7442) incorporated TM substitutions to reduce Fcγ receptor and C1q engagement while pairing these with YTE to prolong persistence, a design principle also used in other prophylactic antiviral antibodies [325]. Half-life extension itself is now a widely exploited Fc-engineering objective because stronger pH-dependent engagement of the neonatal Fc receptor enhances recycling and serum durability: AstraZeneca’s YTE platform (M252Y/S254T/T256E) underpins nirsevimab and was first clinically validated with motavizumab-YTE; YTE was also used in AZD7442, while the LS platform (M428L/N434S), developed and advanced by Xencor and widely adopted across the field, is found in viral antibodies such as sotrovimab and VRC01LS [326].

Beyond the neonatal Fc receptor, point mutations that tune Fcγ receptor and complement engagement are increasingly important because they can enhance opsonophagocytosis, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, and, where desired, complement-dependent killing; a prominent coronavirus disease-era example is VIR-7832 from Vir Biotechnology/GlaxoSmithKline, which added the GAALIE Fc motif to increase activating Fcγ receptor engagement and promote more potent effector-cell recruitment and CD8 T-cell priming relative to a neutralization-only design [327]. However, many additional Fc-point mutations and Fc-glycoengineering opportunities remain that can be leveraged to enhanced functionality against various targets, including bacteria [328, 329].

Fc engineering may have great promise for mAbs against organisms such as S. aureus or streptococci. As discussed above, SpA produced by S. aureus binds and inactivates antibodies as a key mechanism of immune evasion. Attempts have been made to modify the Fc of mAbs to prevent their inactivation by S. aureus, though this has produced complications with a reduced half-life [97]. Groups C and G streptococci employ the same strategy with their surface antigen, protein G [330]. In principle, Fc modifications could be made to give anti-streptococcus mAbs an edge in avoiding capture, but this has not been investigated at the time of writing.

Looking ahead, the field is moving well beyond half-life engineering alone. As the protective mechanisms required for bacterial control become clearer, Fc domains should be rationally optimized for the precise biology needed at different sites of infection, including phagocyte recruitment, intracellular killing, mucosal activity, and complement engagement, using improved screening platforms and increasingly predictive structure-guided Fc design.

Computational Discovery and Design

An incredible tool for streamlining and improving the process of antibody discovery is computational design. Driven by the rapidly diverging variants of SARS-CoV-2 that quickly mutated to escape antibody targeting, the field of in silico protein development accelerated to incorporate calculated modifications to mAb structures that could account for the changing targets [331]. Though this process requires an informational investment far greater than more traditional techniques, in silico computational methods have the potential to optimize the binding domains of existing antibodies for precise binding to protein targets with well-characterized molecular structures [331]. Recent advancements in this field have added the ability to produce high quality de novo mAb candidates of various formats (IgG, ScFv, VHH) entirely in silico, without referencing a known binding antibody [332]. Interestingly, ScFv and VHH targeting TcdB of C. difficile have been developed. The binding and structural data were encouraging, though no efficacy data were published [332]. The technical triumph of this paper is exciting; however, this being the only current entry of de novo antibody design into the anti-microbial antibody pipeline shows the infancy of the field. TcdB, as previously discussed, is a well-characterized protein that has been extensively validated as a therapeutic target for mAb development, making it a good choice for testing novel mAb development methodologies. Future de novo computational mAb design campaigns may have potential for addressing outstanding issues in the field, such as binding elusive epitopes on high-value antigens.

Though the field of de novo computational antibody discovery and design is fascinating, particularly in an era of emerging artificial intelligence in life sciences, the full nuance is well beyond the scope for this review. We would encourage interested readers to investigate further with these additional reviews [333, 334].

Conclusions

The development of mAbs targeting bacterial pathogens represents a promising yet complex frontier in combating AMR. Decades of research have yielded valuable insights into both successful and unsuccessful therapeutic approaches across multiple critical pathogens. While only a handful of anti-bacterial mAbs have achieved regulatory approval, the field has demonstrated that carefully vetted targets can provide clinical benefit. The successes of anti-toxin mAbs, particularly those targeting conserved virulence factors such as TcdB, alongside the promising development of multi-specific approaches, such as gremubamab targeting key P. aeruginosa virulence factors, validate the therapeutic potential of mAb approaches. However, challenges remain, including the structural diversity of bacterial surface antigens, the differential regulation of virulence factor expression across different tissue types, the shielding of target epitopes, and the need for broad strain coverage across heterogeneous clinical isolates.

Looking forward, advances in antibody engineering, including half-life extension technologies, rational Fab and Fc design, multi-specific formats and novel delivery methods are expanding the utility of anti-bacterial mAbs beyond what naturally occurring antibodies can achieve. The lessons learned from both clinical successes and failures have refined target selection criteria, emphasizing the importance of epitope accessibility, conservation across strains, and functional relevance to pathogenesis. As the field continues to evolve with innovative screening methodologies, such as target-agnostic approaches and patient-derived antibody discovery, and the introduction of VHH multi-specific mAb technology, combined with rational engineering strategies, mAbs are poised to play an increasingly important role in the therapeutic arsenal against life-threatening bacterial infections in the era of rising AMR.
