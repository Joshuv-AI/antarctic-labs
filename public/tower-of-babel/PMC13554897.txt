Double-Snare Retrieval and Mapping-Guided Reimplantation of a Dislodged Atrial Leadless Pacemaker

History of Presentation

A 64-year-old man with tachycardia-bradycardia syndrome and syncope underwent dual-chamber leadless pacemaker (LP) implantation (Aveir DR; Abbott). At the initial atrial LP implantation, the device demonstrated favorable electrical parameters, including a capture threshold of 1.25 V at 0.4 ms, a P-wave amplitude of 3.6 mV, and an impedance of 330 Ω, with a confirmed current of injury (COI) observed, consistent with adequate initial device fixation (Supplemental Figure 1A).

However, the capture threshold progressively increased during follow-up, resulting in premature battery depletion approximately 2.5 years postimplant. Consequently, a second procedure was performed for device replacement. During reimplantation, identifying an optimal atrial substrate proved technically challenging despite extensive mapping, and the new atrial LP was ultimately deployed at a site with suboptimal electrical parameters: a high capture threshold of 4.25 V at 0.4 ms, a P-wave amplitude of 1.0 mV, an impedance of 300 Ω, and a small COI (Supplemental Figure 1B). One week later, follow-up imaging revealed dislodgement of the atrial LP, with inversion into the right ventricle.

Past Medical History

The patient had paroxysmal atrial fibrillation treated with amiodarone and end-stage renal disease on chronic hemodialysis for more than 30 years. In the setting of the prolonged uremic state, uremic atrial myopathy with atrial fibrosis and impaired myocardial excitability was suspected, potentially contributing to initial fixation instability and elevated pacing thresholds.

Differential Diagnosis

The causes for elevated atrial capture thresholds included 1) device dislodgement or microdislodgement1; 2) early postimplant inflammatory changes related to helix fixation2; 3) thrombus or tissue debris at the device-tissue interface3; 4) atrial myocardial fibrosis or scarring, including uremic atrial myopathy3; and 5) amiodarone effects.

Investigations

Chest radiography demonstrated atrial LP dislodgement with inversion into the right ventricle (Figure 1A). A 12-lead electrocardiogram showed a ventricular-paced rhythm with a total loss of atrial capture (Figure 1B).

Management

Retrieval of the dislodged atrial LP

Initial retrieval using a catheter with a triloop snare (Aveir retrieval catheter; Abbott) failed to achieve stable fixation helix capture because of tight embedding and unfavorable orientation. Docking button engagement was achieved; however, the LP slipped within the protective sheath during withdrawal, raising concern for loss of control or re-embolization. A second single-loop snare was introduced to stabilize the device, allowing the triloop snare to securely re-engage the docking button for safe extraction (Figures 2A to 2H, Video 1).

Mapping-guided atrial LP deimplantation

To minimize repeated device manipulation and optimize fixation in a diseased atrium, right atrial 3-dimensional (3D) electroanatomic mapping (EAM) was performed before reimplantation. A site near the base of the right atrial appendage with favorable voltage and pacing characteristics was identified. The mapping catheter was left at the target site as a spatial reference. The atrial LP (Aveir AR) demonstrated robust atrial electrograms and COI before fixation and was successfully screwed into position (Figures 3A and 3B, Supplemental Figure 1C). At implantation, the capture threshold was 2.25 V at 0.6 ms, with a P-wave amplitude of 1.2 mV and an impedance of 330 Ω.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Follow-up chest radiography confirmed stable atrial LP position (Figure 3C). At 4 months after reimplantation, device interrogation demonstrated excellent electrical parameters: P-wave amplitude of 3.5 mV, capture threshold of 0.5 V at 0.4 ms, and impedance of 360 Ω.

Discussion

Despite the overall safety of leadless pacemaker systems, atrial LP dislodgement remains a clinically significant complication and occurs more frequently than ventricular LP dislodgement. Current data suggest that ventricular LP dislodgement rates are approximately 0.88%,5 whereas atrial LP show higher rates, around 1.7%,6 underscoring the need for effective retrieval and prevention strategies. Because of the risk of device embolization, snare-based retrieval is essential, and the device's location and orientation can influence the technical approach and procedural difficulty. In this case, fixation helix capture proved unreliable when the device was tightly embedded, underscoring the importance of docking button engagement. Slippage within the protective sheath during withdrawal highlighted the need for careful control and slow manipulation; stabilization with a second snare provided enhanced procedural safety and control. Our case illustrates that a double-snare technique can enhance secure docking button engagement during retrieval of complex atrial LP dislodgements.

Beyond retrieval, optimization of the atrial substrate for reimplantation is essential. Although the initial implantation demonstrated acceptable electrical parameters, the progressive increase in capture threshold suggests evolving impairment at the device-tissue interface, likely reflecting underlying atrial substrate abnormalities. In diseased atrial myocardium, such as uremic atrial myopathy, impaired myocardial integrity and reduced tissue excitability may compromise effective helix engagement and long-term device stability.4 At the time of reimplantation, difficulty in identifying an appropriate atrial substrate despite multiple mapping attempts may reflect advanced atrial myopathy. Repeated device-based mapping or positioning attempts may further disrupt the tissue-device interface or introduce debris at the helix-myocardial interface, thereby compromising fixation efficacy. Deployment at a site with a high capture threshold suggests suboptimal tissue engagement and may have contributed to subsequent device instability and early dislodgement.

In this context, preimplant 3D EAM may facilitate identification of viable atrial myocardium, enabling optimal site selection and effective fixation at the first attempt, thereby reducing the risk of device instability. In our case, the use of a mapping catheter as a spatial reference resulted in durable electrical performance, with a capture threshold of 0.5 V at 0.4 ms at the 4-month follow-up despite the presence of underlying atrial myopathy. These findings suggest that suboptimal substrate selection, rather than initial fixation failure alone, may play a key role in atrial LP instability.

Conclusions

Double-snare retrieval is a safe and effective strategy for complex atrial LP dislodgement. In patients with diseased atrial substrate, preimplant 3D EAM may reduce fixation failure and optimize atrial LP sensing and pacing outcomes.
