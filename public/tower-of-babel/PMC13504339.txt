Allele frequency trajectories across age groups reveal ongoing natural selection shaping disease susceptibility
Authors: Jing-Lian Chen, Mei-Ling Kang, Cheng-Jui Lin, Yun-Hua Lo, Yann-Jang Chen, Hsiao-Hui Lee, Valis Tanapima, Chao-Kuang Lin, I-Hui Lee, Yoko Satta, Wen-Ya Ko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUnderstanding how selection shapes disease risk remains challenging. Variants influencing complex traits, including common diseases, can also impact fitness and thus be constrained by purifying selection. Consequently, genetic variance underlying disease susceptibility may be attributed to low-frequency, population-specific variants. We analyzed 509,817 genome-wide variants from 72,635 Han Taiwanese individuals to identify loci showing age-dependent allele frequency shifts that signal ongoing selection. After adjusting for potential age-related population structure, we detected 168 variants deviating from neutrality, with most showing declining frequencies in younger generations, consistent with purifying selection on deleterious alleles influencing disease risk. These variants were enriched for rare alleles (≤0.1%) and disease-associated variants. At BRCA1, we identified 16 rare pathogenic variants in strong linkage disequilibrium undergoing purifying selection that coexist with a positively selected haplotype, revealing temporally fluctuating selection; comparable patterns at BRCA2 and MLH1 suggest recurrent selective trade-offs in DNA repair genes. Phenome-wide association analysis across 30 hematologic and cardiometabolic traits linked a subset of candidates to increased erythrocyte volume and reduced hemoglobin concentration, suggesting subclinical physiological effects. These results demonstrate ongoing natural selection on disease-relevant variation, particularly affecting hematologic traits in the Han Taiwanese population, and highlight opportunities to refine precision-medicine risk models.Keywords: ongoing natural selection, age-dependent allele frequency change, disease susceptibility, BRCA1, erythrocyte volume, ATG9A, FADS2, Taiwan Biobank, purifying selection, population genomics

Introduction

Irrespective of the relative contributions of positive or negative selection in maintaining or removing disease-associated variants, the connection between evolutionary selection and disease susceptibility is well-documented and critically important.1 While positive selection favors beneficial genetic variants, potentially increasing their prevalence in populations, certain variants under positive selection might also predispose individuals to particular diseases, often due to trade-offs or pleiotropy.2,3 Conversely, purifying selection (negative selection) reduces the frequencies of harmful variants. Both processes shape the genomic landscape, influencing disease susceptibility. As environments and lifestyles have dramatically shifted, genetic variants that were once favored or constrained by natural selection may now be under different selection pressures, potentially leading to unexpected consequences in modern contexts.2,4 Variants under selection often pinpoint biologically important genes and genomic loci critical to health, making them prime candidates for identifying disease-associated genetic factors.2,5

A wide range of statistical approaches has been established to detect genomic signatures of positive selection across different evolutionary timescales.6 These methods capture different aspects of selection footprints (e.g., skewed allele frequency spectrum, distortions in genealogical patterns, extended haplotypes, or elevated locus-specific genetic differentiation between populations) by comparing observed patterns to expectations under a neutral model or genome-wide empirical null distributions (reviewed in Gao5). While the footprints of natural selection typically reflect historical selective pressure, Mostafavi and colleagues7 leveraged available large-scale genotyped and imputed data and developed a method that directly tracks changes in allele frequency across different human age groups, thereby detecting ongoing selection on viability. Applying this approach to UK Biobank participants, they examined polygenic score distributions across ages and successfully discovered a number of significant associations between parental age at death and sets of trait-associated alleles. These included variants associated with increased risk of high cholesterol, elevated body mass index, coronary artery disease, and asthma, which showed genetic signatures consistent with negative viability selection, as well as variants linked to later puberty timing and younger age at first birth, which exhibited signatures of positive viability selection. Their findings underscored the widespread action of natural selection on genetic variants influencing a broad array of disease-related complex traits in contemporary human populations. In addition, Zeng and colleagues8 demonstrated widespread evidence of negative natural selection acting on genetic variants associated with 23 of 28 complex traits tested, including those related to reproduction, cardiovascular health, and anthropometric measures, by analyzing the inverse relationship between SNP effect-size variance and minor-allele frequency (MAF).

However, in the single-variant analysis of Mostafavi and colleagues’ study, only a small number of common variants showed a notable decline in frequency among individuals over 65 years old at the APOE ε4 allele associated with late-onset Alzheimer disease (AD2 [MIM: 104310]). The authors speculated that many pathogenic variants, including those with late-onset effects, may be kept at low frequencies due to ongoing purifying selection. This interpretation aligns with theoretical predictions by Eyre-Walker,9 who examined multiple population genetic models under the assumption that variants influencing complex traits also affect fitness. The study demonstrated that unless selection is extremely weak, the majority of genetic variance in a complex trait is expected to be contributed by low-frequency variants. Under models of moderate-to-strong negative selection, most trait-associated variants contributing to disease susceptibility are predicted to remain in the population at allele frequencies below 1%.

In this study, we applied the method introduced by Mostafavi and colleagues7 to detect age-dependent shifts in allele frequency across 509,817 genotyped variants from 72,635 Han Taiwanese individuals enrolled in the Taiwan Biobank (TWB), spanning from age 24 to 70. Motivated by both empirical and theoretical evidence emphasizing the importance of low-frequency variants in the genetic architecture of disease, we adopted a lower-frequency threshold of 0.05% (corresponding to >72 copies of a given allele in our samples) and restricted our analysis to directly genotyped variants to ensure reliable SNP calling. This approach identified 168 candidate variants showing significant deviations from the null model, with 151 (90%) of them exhibiting population allele frequencies below 0.1%. We then conducted linkage disequilibrium (LD) analyses to identify putative causal variants or genes in proximity to these candidates. To evaluate their functional relevance and potential contributions to disease susceptibility, we performed phenome-wide association analyses across 30 hematological or cardiometabolic traits and annotated these variants using the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) and data from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project.

Material and methods

The whole-genome genotyping/sequencing data from the TWB

The whole-genome genotyping data, along with 30 anthropometric, hematologic, and cardiometabolic measurements, were obtained from the TWB (https://www.twbiobank.org.tw/) for 88,339 Taiwanese citizens who self-identified as Han. All individuals were genotyped using the customized Axiom Genome-wide TWB 2.0 array plate, which covers 752,921 variants. Each participant also completed a medical history survey. Additionally, the study included a TWB dataset of 1,487 individuals with available whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data encompassing 41,611,044 variants.10 Ethical approval for this research was obtained from the institutional review board (IRB) of National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University (IRB protocol number: NYCU113024AE). All participant data were de-identified prior to analysis. All analyses were conducted in accordance with TWB’s ethical and data-use policies, as well as all applicable institutional and regulatory guidelines.

Quality controls for the whole-genome genotyping data

PLINK v.1.911 was used to conduct a series of quality control (QC) steps, following the guidelines by Anderson et al.,12 to exclude problematic samples and variants. Individuals with a genotype missing rate exceeding 3% or a heterozygosity rate deviating by more than 3 standard deviations from the population mean were excluded. Genetic relatedness was assessed by calculating pairwise identical-by-descent (IBD) values for all individual pairs in the dataset. One individual from each pair with IBD > 0.185 was removed, resulting in a final set of 75,060 individuals after QC. Variants were filtered by removing those with a genotyping missing rate exceeding 3%, a MAF below 0.05%, or a significant deviation from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) (p < 10−50). Variants on the X chromosome were included in the analyses, whereas variants on the Y chromosome were excluded. After QC filtering, 509,817 variants remained for downstream analyses.

To minimize age-related bias due to varying recruitment periods, individuals recruited before 2013 were excluded, yielding a final sample size of 72,635 participants. For subsequent analyses, the ages recorded in 2013 for these participants were used.

Principal-component analysis

Principal-component analysis (PCA) was conducted using PLINK v.1.9 to control for population stratification in logistic regression models and to verify the ancestry of individuals who self-identified as Han. A subset of variants was selected for PCA based on the following criteria: MAF > 0.01 and HWE p > 10−6. Variants with strong LD were also excluded to ensure independence based on the pairwise estimates of r2 > 0.3 using a sliding window approach (window size: 50 variants, step size: 5 variants). Additionally, variants reported in the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) database13 as associated with phenotypes or diseases were excluded to ensure that PCA primarily captured the population stratification driven by demographic history rather than disease-associated variants. The first ten principal components (PCs) were included as covariates in the regression models to control for population substructure across different age groups.

Statistical model to detect allele frequency changes across generations

We applied the logistic regression model developed by Mostafavi and colleagues7 to identify variants exhibiting allele frequency differences across age groups. The model incorporates four explanatory variables: (1) individual ancestry, (2) genotyping batch, (3) sex, and (4) age group. Age, ranging from 24 to 70 years, was treated as a categorical variable and divided into eight bins at 5-year intervals, except for the first group (ages 24–30) and the last group (ages 61–70), where the latter combined individuals aged 66–70 due to a small sample size (Figure S1). For each variant, a likelihood ratio test (LRT) was performed to compare the null model, which included PCs, genotyping batches, and sex as covariates, with the alternative model, which additionally incorporated age as a variable. This test assessed whether the alternative model provided a significantly better fit than the null model. The age-by-sex effect was further tested against the alternative model by adding the predictor J × L. The Manhattan and quantile-quantile (QQ) plots were plotted using custom Python code and the R package gaston,14 respectively.

Simulations for evaluating the power of the statistical model

To evaluate the statistical power of the logistic regression model to detect allele frequency changes across age groups, we performed simulations modeling allele frequency shifts under varying strengths of purifying selection. The synthetic population was generated to reflect the sample size and ancestry structure of the empirical dataset, following the approach of Mostafavi et al.7 In each simulation, genotypes for individuals within a given age group were randomly assigned using a binomial distribution, with the success probability set equal to the group’s specified allele frequency. Initial allele frequencies were defined in the oldest age group and systematically reduced in younger groups by predetermined fractions of the initial frequency. The two logistic regression models described above were then applied, and p values were obtained via a LRT. Each selection scenario was simulated 1,000 times, and statistical power was estimated as the proportion of simulations yielding significant results. The significance threshold was set to match our empirical analysis (p = 1.6 × 10−5).

Annotation of candidate variants and identification of causal variants through LD analyses, VEP, and GTEx

Annotations for the identified variants of interest were obtained from the Single Nucleotide Polymorphism Database (dbSNP)15 and processed using the Ensembl VEP16 to determine their functional impact. All genomic coordinates and variant annotations were based on the GRCh38/hg38 human reference genome assembly. The integrated annotations in VEP included CADD (Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion), EVE (evolutionary model of variant effect),17 Downstream (which predicts the effects of frameshift variants on protein sequences), MaxEntScan (which predicts the splicing efficiency of exon-intron boundaries),18 NearestGene (which identifies the nearest genes to non-genic variants), DisGeNET (a database that integrates information on gene-disease associations),19 and PHENOTYPES (which retrieves overlapping phenotype information). Furthermore, we queried the GTEx project,20 an expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) database, to investigate the association between the selected variants and differences in gene expression across specific tissues. The distribution of candidate variants across chromosomes was visualized using PhenoGram, an online tool developed by the Ritchie Lab, enabling a clear graphical representation of their genomic location.21

To further identify potential causal variants adjacent to the candidate variants identified in the logistic regression analyses, LD analyses were performed for all candidate variants existing in the WGS dataset with 1,487 individuals. The r2 values between the core variant and adjacent variants within an 800-kb window centered on the candidate variants were calculated and visualized, alongside CADD scores,22 a genomic map generated with the Gviz package,23 and variant consequences when r2 > 0.5. The visualization of pairwise r2 for variants of interest was plotted using the R package LDheatmap.24

Phenome-wide association analyses of candidate variants

For each candidate variant, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted using PLINK v.1.9 on a cohort of 74,854 individuals across 30 physiological complex traits, which were broadly categorized into six disease-related categories, i.e., hematology, liver function, cardiovascular diseases, renal functions, diabetes mellitus, and bone mineral density (Table S1). All phenotypes were normalized using rank-based inverse-normal transformation. Population stratifications were carefully controlled by including PC1–10, genotyping batch, age, sex, and BMI as covariates. For hepatic measurements, the presence of hepatitis B and C virus antibodies was also considered. Detecting signatures of adaptive natural selection in regions adjacent to candidate variants

We performed iSAFE25 (integrated selection of allele favored by evolution) analysis on 800-kb windows centered on candidate variants within the WGS dataset comprising 1,487 individuals. In cases where a candidate variant was absent from the WGS data, the position of the variant was designated as the center of the analysis window. The resulting iSAFE scores were visualized, and the pairwise LD values (r2) between the lead iSAFE variants and the surrounding variants were calculated. For loci exhibiting evidence of positive selection, a haplotype was defined as the set of variants showing strong LD (r2 > 0.6) with the lead iSAFE variant also present in the genotyping dataset. For loci exhibiting evidence of purifying selection, candidate haplotypes were defined by a cluster of linked candidate variants (r2 > 0.6) exhibiting concordant age-dependent allele frequency changes.

In addition, we calculated the singleton density score (SDS)26 for all variants in 139 non-overlapped windows encompassing the 168 candidate variants using the WGS dataset. FDR correction was applied across all analyzed variants. Genomic windows containing variants with SDS reaching FDR ≤ 0.05 were considered evidence of recent positive selection.

Random sampling analyses for enrichment testing

Permutation-based random sampling analyses were performed to determine whether the observed enrichment of trait associations and clinically relevant variants among candidate loci exceeded random expectation. In each analysis, a number of variants equal to the number of candidate variants identified by the logistic regression analyses was randomly sampled without replacement, and the procedure was repeated 100 times. For the PheWAS control analysis, variants were drawn from 496,757 autosomal genotyped variants and tested for association with mean corpuscular volume (MCV) and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC) using the same linear regression framework applied to the candidate variants. To assess enrichment of pathogenic variants, random sets were generated either from all 509,817 analyzed variants or from variants matched to the allele frequency distribution of the candidate variants. The number of significant trait associations or ClinVar pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants was then compared with the observed results.

Results

Identifying variants under ongoing selection through detecting intergenerational allele frequency differences

We applied a logistic regression model to detect allele frequency differences across eight age groups (ranging from 24 to 70 years) for 509,817 genotyped variants in 72,635 Taiwanese Han individuals. For each variant, an LRT was conducted to assess whether including age group as an additional explanatory variable significantly improved model fit compared to the null model, with both models accounting for population substructure through PC1–10 (Figure S2), as well as sex and batch effects as covariates. Figure 1A presents the Manhattan plot of the LRT p values for all analyzed variants. The genomic inflation factor (λ = 1.035) indicates minimal overall inflation, as also reflected in the corresponding QQ plot. Although deviation from the null expectation is observed in the extreme tail, the vast majority (99.68%) of test statistics closely follow the null distribution (Figure S3A), supporting appropriate calibration for most variants. While some extreme values may include false positives, such tail deviations are expected when a subset of variants exhibits genuine age-dependent allele frequency changes.

To further evaluate whether the observed extreme tail deviations reflect non-null signals rather than model misspecification or unaccounted confounding, we restricted the analysis to variants with a MAF ≥ 0.01, which are less sensitive to fine-scale population stratification. Under this condition, the QQ plot closely follows the null expectation, with 99.997% of the variants aligned with the expected distribution (Figure S3B), indicating that the statistical framework and covariate adjustment do not systematically generate inflated test statistics. Note that this additional analysis assesses baseline model calibration rather than directly validating signals from low-frequency variants. Taken together with PC-based control of age-related population structure (Figure S4), these results argue against widespread inflation or systematic confounding as the primary explanation for the observed tail deviations.

After correction for multiple testing, 168 variants were identified as the candidates targeted by ongoing selection, achieving genome-wide significance at an FDR < 0.05 (p < 1.6 × 10−5). A synonymous variant in ATG9A (MIM: 612204) had the lowest p value (2.7 × 10−15), followed by a deletion in MSH6 ([MIM: 600678]; p = 1.6 × 10−11) that leads to susceptibility of Lynch syndrome (LYNCH5 [MIM: 614350]; Figure 1A). Clusters of candidate variants were found in breast-ovarian cancer-causing genes BRCA1 (MIM: 113705) and BRCA2 (MIM: 600185). All candidate variants exhibited distinct allele frequency changes across age groups, deviating from the expected trajectories under the null model (see Figure S5 for all candidate variants). The majority of candidate variants (159 of 168) exhibited an overall decreasing allele frequency in younger generations, as illustrated in Figure 1B, while eight showed a general increasing trend (Figure 1C), and only one variant displayed a fluctuating pattern across generations (Figure 1D). Overall, 51% (86) of the candidate variants have been previously reported to be associated with various diseases and/or functional effects. The 168 candidates, along with their locations, molecular consequences, and related traits/diseases, are summarized on a chromosome map (Figure 2; also see Table S2 for detailed information on all candidate variants identified). We further evaluated an extended model that includes a “sex × age” interaction term to assess whether allele frequency trajectories across age groups differ by sex. However, inclusion of the age-by-sex interaction term did not identify any variants showing significant interaction effects, as the distribution of p values closely followed the null expectation (Figure S6).

Among all candidate variants, 90% (151) had allele frequencies ≤ 0.1% in the population, whereas variants at this frequency level account for only 1% of markers on the SNP array (Figure S7), indicating a strong enrichment of rare variants among the detected signals. Among these 151 rare variants, the magnitude of average allele frequency change across age bins ranged from 11.5% to 15.46%, with only one variant showing a slightly smaller change (9.25%; also see Table S2). To evaluate the statistical power to detect signals in such low-frequency variants, we conducted power simulations modeling ongoing purifying selection based on the observed sample sizes across age groups and individual genetic variances, following the framework described in Mostafavi et al.7 Multiple simulation runs were performed under varying initial frequencies and different selection intensities acting against deleterious variants across generations. As shown in Figure S8, detection power increases with both the magnitude of allele frequency decline and the initial allele frequency. While power is highest for common variants, the method retains appreciable power even for very rare variants (initial frequency ≈ 0.001). For declines of 10%–15%, the power ranges from ∼10% to ∼90%, increasing rapidly with the magnitude of allele frequency change (Figure S8B). Notably, the observed allele frequency changes among our candidate variants fall within this simulated range, indicating that the detected signals occur under conditions where the method retains meaningful power to identify age-dependent allele frequency shifts.

Genetic signatures of adaptive natural selection on the DNA repair genes: BRCA1, BRCA2, and MLH1

A notable pattern in the Manhattan plot of the LRT results is the absence of variant clusters with elevated −log(p) values surrounding the candidate variants. This pattern can largely be attributed to the fact that most candidate variants segregate at low frequencies, with 154 of 168 variants having allele frequencies below 1%. As a result, adjacent variants are generally not in strong LD with these low-frequency candidate variants. One exception is the candidate locus located in BRCA1, where 17 significant variants were detected. Among them, 16 exhibit strong LD, except for a stop-gained variant, rs80357377, despite their allele frequency being as low as 0.0006 (see Figure 3A and Table 1 for the 17 BRCA1 candidates). All the variants in the haplotype are predicted to have significant functional consequences, including 14 frameshift variants, one nonsense variant, and one missense variant. Remarkably, 15 are labeled as pathogenic in ClinVar. The accumulation of such a high number of potentially deleterious variants on a single haplotype over time appears unusual in an evolutionary context without invoking adaptive natural selection.27 In addition, another haplotype consisting of high-frequency variants (∼0.37) was found in the same region (Figure 3A). Indeed, previous studies have shown evidence of positive selection acting on both BRCA1 and BRCA2 in the ancestral lineage of humans and chimpanzees.28,29 However, whether these two genes were undergoing adaptive evolution in contemporary human populations remains unexplored. To investigate this, we conducted an iSAFE analysis, a method specifically designed to detect signatures of positive selection,25 on an 800-kb region encompassing the BRCA1 gene using the WGS dataset of 1,487 individuals. Our analysis revealed strong evidence of recent positive selection, indicated by elevated iSAFE scores (>0.35) within a 400-kb window that includes BRCA1 and nearby genes. The selection signatures correspond to the alternative haplotype consisting of high-frequency variants (∼0.37), suggesting this haplotype might have been under adaptive selection (Figure 3B). Notably, most variants in this haplotype are intronic or intergenic, which have no effect on protein sequence. The only five variants affecting protein sequences are reported as benign (four missense and one splice donor variant in BRCA1 and long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) NBR2 [MIM: 618708], respectively) (Table S3). The low-frequency candidate haplotype carrying the 16 protein-altering variants, however, is absent from our WGS dataset due to its rarity.

PheWASs of the 168 candidate variants

To investigate the functional effects of the identified variants, we conducted multiple linear regression analyses across 30 hematological and cardiometabolic complex traits related to cardiovascular, renal, hepatic, hematologic diseases, diabetes, and bone marrow density in 74,854 individuals. After correcting for multiple testing across variants and traits, 160 of 168 variants demonstrated significant associations with at least one trait (Figure S11). The PheWAS result is detailed in Table S4. The majority of significant associations observed were with MCV and MCHC. Notably, 150 variants were associated with both MCV and MCHC, all exhibiting a consistent pattern—positively associated with MCV and negatively associated with MCHC—except for rs768318915 (a nonsense variant in TTC1 [MIM: 601963]), which showed the opposite directions of effect for both traits (Figure 4A). In fact, rs768318915 was the only variant in this group to show an increase in allele frequency in younger generations, whereas all others exhibited a decrease (Figure S5B).

To rule out the possibility that the overrepresentation of significant associations with MCV and MCHC resulted from systematic ascertainment bias, we performed a control analysis by randomly sampling 168 variants from a set of 496,757 autosomal genotyped variants, repeating 100 times. Linear regressions between genotype and MCV/MCHC were conducted for each set, and the number of variants that met the significance threshold was recorded. In stark contrast to the observed PheWAS results, the randomly sampled variants yielded fewer than seven significant associations with either trait per iteration (Figure S12) and fewer than one with both MCV and MCHC, effectively ruling out ascertainment bias as a confounding factor.

The most significant variant identified in the LRT analysis (p = 2.7 × 10−15), the synonymous variant rs55842930 in ATG9A, was positively associated with hematocrit (HCT), MCV, albumin, and uric acid and negatively associated with MCHC, bone density, and HbA1c (Figure 4B). Moreover, the intronic variant rs2072114 in fatty acid desaturase 2 (FADS2 [MIM: 606149]), which exhibits a fluctuating pattern in allele frequency change with age (Figure 1D), was significantly associated with 15 traits spanning six major disease-related categories. These include hematopoietic traits (platelets, red blood cells [RBCs], hemoglobin, HCT, and MCHC), cardiovascular traits (triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), total cholesterol, and heart rate), renal function markers (microalbumin and uric acid), diabetes (fasting glucose), liver function (total bilirubin), and bone health (bone density) (Figure 4C). The breadth of these associations points to pleiotropic effects of FADS2, most likely reflecting its central role in long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acid (LC-PUFA) metabolism.32

Finally, we observed an inverse relationship between effect size and allele frequency (p) among the 159 significant variants that showed a decreasing frequency in young generations. This relationship was quantified by fitting the function f(p)=cp(1−p), described by Park et al.,31 where cˆ = 0.01. The model explained a modest proportion of the variance (R2 = 0.19) and was statistically significant (p < 10−5, significantly differing from c = 0; Figure 4D). This pattern aligns with expectations under purifying selection, which predicts that variants with large effect sizes are more likely to be deleterious and subject to stronger negative selection, resulting in lower population frequencies. In contrast, variants with smaller effect sizes experience weaker selection, allowing them to persist at higher frequencies within the population.9,33

Functional annotations of candidate variants using Ensembl VEP and GTEx

All candidate variants were annotated by the information retrieved from dbSNP,15 Ensembl VEP,16 and the GTEx project.20 The result is detailed in Table S5. Annotation of the candidate variants revealed that 86 of 168 variants (51.2%) had previously been associated with disease risks or physiological traits (Figure 5A). Specifically, 39 variants are linked to cancer, and most of the remaining pathogenic variants are reported to be responsible for rare genetic diseases that could affect an individual during infancy or very young age (summarized in Table S6), including inherited rare multisystem disorders (6), neuromuscular diseases (4), and neurodevelopmental diseases (3) (see Figure 5A). We also conducted a control analysis to examine potential ascertainment bias due to the predetermined variant set on the genotyping array. Among 100 random sets of 168 variants drawn from the full set of 509,817 analyzed variants, we observed an average of only 0.31 variants (range: 0–3) classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in ClinVar34 (Figure S13A), substantially fewer than the 71 such variants (in ClinVar) among our 168 candidate variants. Since the candidates were highly skewed toward rare variants (≤0.1%) compared to the allele frequency distribution of all analyzed variants in the full dataset (Figure S7), we also generated 100 random sets of 168 variants matched to the frequency distribution of the observed candidates. The frequency-matched sets yielded an average of 16.4 pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants per replicate (range: 7–25), which remains markedly lower than the 71 such variants observed among our candidate variants (Figure S13B).

In particular, rs34401996 (chr2:185890515 [GRCh38/hg38]) is one of the few variants that exhibited a general increasing trend in allele frequency among younger generations (Figure 1C). This intergenic variant is in strong LD with several variants located within fibrous sheath interacting protein 2 (FSIP2 [MIM: 615796]), which encodes a structural protein essential for the sperm fibrous sheath and plays a key role in spermatogenesis and sperm motility.35 It is also linked to antisense lncRNAs FSIP2-AS1 and FSIP2-AS2 (Figure 6A). rs34401996 is associated with variation in gene expression, serving as an eQTL for FSIP2 in the pituitary, colon, and lung and for FSIP2-AS2 in multiple tissues, including the brain, adipose tissue, and testis. In fact, most variants within the same LD block also act as eQTLs for these transcripts (Table S7). Several variants within the FSIP2 LD block are potential causal candidates, including the missense variant rs7565527 and the intronic variant rs35191782, showing a moderately high CADD score of 9.5 (both with r2 = 0.93). Discussion

We applied the method developed by Mostafavi et al.7 to examine age-associated changes in allele frequency by analyzing 509,817 whole-genome genotyping variants in a cohort of 72,635 Han Taiwanese individuals. This approach allowed us to characterize the genetic landscape shaped by ongoing selective pressures in the contemporary population. Although some degree of age-related population stratification was detected, it was effectively captured through PCA (Figure S4A). Specifically, the null model incorporated individual ancestry by including PC1–10, which adequately accounted for population stratification when detecting allele frequency trends across age groups. For instance, variants with the highest loadings on PC1, which explained 17.7% of the total variance, showed predicted allele frequency trajectories under the null model that closely matched their empirical age-stratified patterns. Despite apparent differences across age groups, these variants yielded non-significant p values (Figure S4B), indicating that the observed frequency changes in our significant variants are not attributable to age-dependent population substructure. For PC11–20, which were not included in the null model, we did not observe significant differences in mean PC values across age groups (Figure S4A), suggesting limited additional age-related structure beyond that captured by the top PCs.

Compared to the allele frequency spectrum of all genotyped variants on the array, the significant candidate variants we identified were strongly skewed toward rare alleles, with 90% exhibiting frequencies ≤ 0.1% (Figure S7). This marked deviation from the background distribution supports the robustness of our findings and argues against the possibility that the observed signals are spurious results stemming from model misspecification. If the majority of these candidates were false positives, their frequency distribution would be expected to more closely mirror the background, or even be biased toward more common alleles, given that our simulations show higher sensitivity for detecting frequency shifts in common variants across age groups (Figure S8).

Among the 168 candidate variants identified as targets of positive or negative selection, approximately half (86) have been previously reported to be associated with various diseases (71 based only on ClinVar), including cancers and multiple early-onset genetic disorders (Figure 5). Given that the Axiom Genome-Wide TWB 2.0 array analyzed in this study encompasses a considerable number of variants with established disease or clinical relevance, we performed control analyses by randomly sampling 168 variants from the TWB 2.0 array with 100 replicates. These were drawn either from the full background distribution or from a rare-allele-enriched subset designed to resemble the allele frequency spectrum of the 168 candidates. On average, only 0.31 and 16 variants per replicate were found to be pathogenic for known disease or clinical features, with observed ranges of 0–3 and 7–25 variants, respectively (Figure S13). These results indicate a significant enrichment of disease-associated or clinically relevant variants among the selection-targeted candidates, suggesting that our identified candidate variants under ongoing natural selection significantly contribute to contemporary disease susceptibility.

Genetic variation landscape shaped by ongoing purifying selection

Mostafavi et al.7 analyzed both genotyped and imputed single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions or deletions (indels) with allele frequencies ≥ 1% in the GERA cohort. They identified only a small number of common variants in APOE that showed a decline in allele frequency among individuals older than 65 years, a pattern consistent with ongoing negative selection against variants that reduce late-life viability. In this study, we did not identify any significant variants in our cohort that exhibited a similar pattern of decreasing allele frequency among older individuals. This discrepancy is likely due to the age range of our dataset, which includes individuals up to 70 years old, whereas the GERA cohort analyzed by Mostafavi et al.7 included participants aged over 84. Consequently, our study lacked sufficient statistical power to detect allele frequency drops in the most senior age groups. Instead, we identified a substantial number of candidate variants (n = 159) that show a decreasing allele frequency trend toward younger age cohorts. Notably, the majority of these variants (151) are rare, with population allele frequencies below 0.1% (Figure S7). Although this pattern was not observed in the study of Mostafavi et al. (likely because variants with frequency ≤ 1% were excluded), it is consistent with expectations under purifying selection, which act to reduce the frequencies of deleterious alleles over successive generations. Our PheWAS analyses revealed an inverse relationship between allele frequencies and effect sizes among these candidate variants (Figure 4D), further supporting the action of purifying selection on these candidates.8,9

In genome-wide analyses, spurious associations can arise when ancestry proportions differ systematically between comparison groups because allele frequencies may vary across subpopulations. For example, Leslie et al.37 identified fine-scale genetic structure associated with geographic regions in the UK population, and Hu et al.38 further showed that such structure, driven by rare variants, may not be fully captured by PCA. In our study, however, genome-wide logistic regressions compare allele frequencies across age bins rather than between case-control cohorts. Under this design, population stratification would generate spurious signals only if ancestry proportions differed systematically between age groups. To evaluate this possibility, we examined whether individuals in different age bins were sampled disproportionately from specific geographic regions. Overall, we did not observe any substantial geographic sampling bias across age groups, and the proportions of sampling locations are broadly consistent among age bins. Minor variation was observed for Tainan and Taipei: the proportion from Tainan ranges from 11.6% to 6.5% across age groups 1 to 8, whereas Taipei shows a modest increasing trend from 5.3% to 8.4% (Figure S16). To further assess the potential impact of these differences, we repeated the logistic regression analyses for the 168 candidate variants after excluding individuals sampled from these two cities and re-examined their allele frequency trajectories across age groups. The resulting trajectories show patterns highly consistent with those observed in the full dataset, and the LRT results remain highly significant for all variants (Figure S5). In addition, Lo et al.39 reported no detectable fine-scale genetic structure among Han Taiwanese individuals using fineSTRUCTURE40 beyond patterns already captured by ADMIXTURE analysis. While residual population substructure cannot be completely excluded, the absence of geographic sampling bias across age groups and the lack of detectable fine-scale structure in previous analyses suggest that population stratification is unlikely to be the primary driver of the observed signals.

Among the 159 candidate variants exhibiting decreasing allele frequencies toward younger age cohorts, 86 have previously reported disease relevance. Of these, 39 are associated with cancer (including 17 BRCA1 variants), while most of the remaining variants are associated with a wide array of rare genetic diseases that substantially affect survival into adulthood (see Figure 5A and Table S6). Indeed, previous studies have consistently reported that genes associated with cancer risk and Mendelian diseases are under purifying selection.41,42,43 Moreover, several BRCA1 pathogenic variants were previously inferred to be under strong negative selection by a machine-learning model.44

We also implemented the methods described in Mostafavi et al.7 and conducted multiple simulation runs to assess the statistical power of our analytical approach. These simulations replicated the trajectories of allele frequency changes observed across age groups by systematically varying parameters such as selection intensity and frequency levels (Figure S8). Our results confirmed that our approach maintains sufficient statistical power to detect variants under strong purifying selection, particularly when allele frequency changes exceed 10% between age groups. With additional forthcoming data from the TWB, we expect further enhancement in statistical power. This improvement will enable the detection of variants under weaker purifying selection, which may also significantly contribute to disease susceptibility.8,45

Linking functional and clinical impact to selection-targeted loci

To investigate the functional consequences of candidate variants, we performed a PheWAS across 30 hematological and cardiometabolic traits and retrieved annotations from the Ensemble VEP and GTEx database. A summary of these results is provided in Table S5. We highlight some of our significant findings below.

ATG9A rs55842930

Among all identified candidates, the synonymous variant ATG9A rs55842930 exhibited the most statistically significant p value. This SNV showed a reduction in allele frequency among younger cohorts (Figure S5), which corresponded with the signature of purifying selection. It regulates RESP18 (MIM: 612721) expression in multiple brain regions and also affects ATG9A expression in the colon, breast, spleen, and skin. Both genes influenced by rs55842930 have been implicated in neuronal disorders. ATG9A has been associated with hereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP [MIM: 182600]),46,47 while RESP18 has been linked to Parkinson disease (PD [MIM: 168600]) in a mouse model.48 Studies across various model organisms and cell lines have demonstrated that ATG9A is critical for diverse biological functions, affecting survival, longevity, reproduction, motor control, immune response, and neurodegeneration.49,50,51,52,53,54,55 Our PheWAS further revealed that rs55842930 is significantly associated with multiple traits, including HbA1c (a diabetes marker), uric acid levels (indicative of renal function), bone marrow density, and several hematologic parameters (HCT, MCV, and MCHC) (Figure 4B). Imagawa et al.56 reported that ATG9A-deficient mice display smaller skeletal structures and increased bone porosity. Furthermore, ATG9A, which is ubiquitously expressed, is an essential protein involved in degradative autophagy by playing a key role in autophagosome membrane expansion.57,58 Overall, the functional analyses combined with previous studies underscore the crucial roles of ATG9A and highlight potential targets for purifying selection.

FADS2 rs207214

Another intriguing variant is rs207214, an intronic variant in FADS2. While signatures of adaptive natural selection have been reported in the FADS gene clusters across various human populations,59,60,61,62 the evolutionary dynamics driving the temporal frequency shifts observed in the contemporary Han Taiwanese population appear to be more complex. The predicted allele frequency trajectory of rs207214 under the null model showed an age-associated increase in the younger generation, suggesting that the observed fluctuations in allele frequency across age groups may reflect a combination of natural selection and age-related population substructure. rs207214 exhibits strong pleiotropic effects at both functional and clinical levels. Our PheWAS analyses associated this SNV with 15 traits across various physiological domains, including cholesterol, HDL, and triglyceride levels; RBC- and hemoglobin-related parameters; fasting glucose; bone marrow density; and many others (Figure 4C). Additionally, several variants in strong LD (r2 > 0.9) with rs207214, such as rs2727270, rs2524299, and rs2072113, have been linked to fatty acid desaturation activities.36,63,64 Moreover, in weaker LD, rs207214 is associated with a haplotype spanning across several genes, including FADS1, FADS2, FEN1, TMEM258 (MIM: 617615), and MYRF (Figure 6B), where many variants have been implicated in associations with cardiovascular diseases, cholesterol levels, cancers, and other traits. In particular, the effect of rs174547 in FADS1 (r2 = 0.57 with rs2072114) on lipid-related measurements has been extensively studied.36,64,65,66 Additionally, a 3′ UTR variant in FEN1, rs4246215 (r2 = 0.57), has been associated with colorectal cancer (CRC [MIM: 114500]), lung cancer (LNCR [MIM: 211980]), and several other cancers,67,68,69 while an intronic variant in TMEM258, rs102275 (r2 = 0.56), has been linked to Crohn disease (IBD1 [MIM: 266600])70 and HDL cholesterol levels.64,71,72

Positive selection on FSIP variants and functional implication

Using the WGS data from 1,487 individuals, we found that the intergenic candidate variant rs34401996 is in strong LD (r2 > 0.9) with variants in FSIP2 (Figure 6A), a gene that plays a key role in spermatogenesis and sperm motility.35,73 Additionally, FSIP2 variants act as eQTLs for various genes and lncRNA across multiple tissues, notably in the brain, testis, and pituitary (see Table S7). Prior research has identified genes related to spermatogenesis, sperm function, and motility as targets of positive selection in human-chimpanzee divergence and in recent human populations.74,75 Consistent with this, our result showed a temporal increase in the allele frequency of rs34401996 (Figure 1C), supporting the hypothesis of ongoing positive selection potentially linked to male reproductive fitness. Furthermore, SDS analysis revealed clear signatures of positive selection in the genomic region adjacent to FSIP2, including rs34401996 (Figure S14).

Adaptive natural selection acting on the DNA repair genes: BRCA1, BRCA2, and MLH1

A notable finding in our study is the identification of 16 BRCA1 variants co-occurring on a single haplotype, which exhibits strong LD (r2 ranging from 0.67 to 0.90), despite their well-established pathogenic effects (Table 1). Although this haplotype is currently present at a very low frequency (0.0006) in the population and appears to be under purifying selection, a haplotype under negative selection would be unlikely to persist long enough to accumulate such a high number of deleterious variants.27 One plausible explanation is that this haplotype may have conferred a selective advantage in the past. Consistent with this hypothesis, we identified a distinct BRCA1 haplotype carrying variants with a frequency as high as 0.37 that exhibits signatures of positive selection (Figure 3B). Similar patterns were observed in two other DNA repair genes, where one low-frequency haplotype is under apparent negative selection, while a separate haplotype shows evidence of positive selection (Figures S9 and S10). These observations suggest that balancing or fluctuating selection may have acted on these loci, potentially driven by host-pathogen interactions. This is consistent with the hypothesis proposed by Lou et al.,29 who identified positive selection in BRCA1 and BRCA2 along the human-chimpanzee lineages based on the elevated ratios of nonsynonymous to synonymous substitutions. Several previous studies have also shown that numerous viruses target host DNA repair machinery and cell cycle regulators.76,77 Collectively, our findings provide compelling evidence that adaptive natural selection, operating through shifting selection schemes, has shaped the genetic architecture of DNA repair genes and continues to affect modern human disease susceptibility.

Identified genetic variants influence erythrocyte volumes

To our surprise, the phenome-wide association analyses revealed a large number of candidate variants (149) exhibiting a significant positive correlation with MCV and a negative correlation with MCHC (Figure 4A). This correlation pattern indicates that carriers of these MCV-increasing variants tend to have larger RBCs, which in turn results in a MCHC per cell. To ensure that this striking pattern was not simply due to ascertainment bias in the genotyped data, we performed a control analysis by randomly sampling 168 autosomal variants and tested the correlations with MCV and MCHC independently. In all 100 replicates, fewer than 7 significant associations were observed for each trait, with average values of 1.64 and 1.29 for MCV and MCHC, respectively. Less than one significant result (0.18) was detected when considering both traits simultaneously (see Figure S12). This stark contrast with our empirical findings underscores the robustness of our results. One exception is rs768318915 (a nonsense variant in TTC1), which exhibited the opposite pattern—a negative correlation with MCV and a positive correlation with MCHC. Moreover, the allele frequency trajectory of rs768318915 across age groups exhibited an increase in younger individuals (Figure S5), in contrast to the trend observed in the 149 candidate variants (decreases in younger individuals). This suggests that rs768318915 may be under positive selection, while the other 149 variants are currently under purifying selection.

One plausible explanation for the strong associations between numerous candidate variants/genes and MCV and MCHC is that these hematological traits directly reflect the outcomes of cell proliferation and differentiation, processes regulated by a multitude of genes.78 During erythropoiesis, cell volume undergoes distinct changes as the cell matures. Proerythroblasts begin as relatively large cells (∼120–140 fL) and progressively decrease in volume as they differentiate. This reduction results from cytoplasmic condensation and the loss of organelles. Ultimately, enucleation produces reticulocytes, which mature into smaller, biconcave erythrocytes optimized for gas exchange (∼86–98 fL).79,80,81 Disruption in this tightly regulated process can plausibly lead to macrocytosis, the formation of abnormally enlarged RBCs.

We further hypothesize that historical selective pressure from malaria may have favored genetic variants that influence erythrocyte volumes. Malaria was endemic in Taiwan until ∼60 years ago,82 suggesting that certain deleterious alleles affecting RBC traits may have conferred a survival advantage under malarial pressure.83 These variants may still segregate in the population today because insufficient time has passed for purifying selection to completely eliminate them. Indeed, the relatively high prevalence of thalassemia—driven by both α-thalassemia (MIM: 604131) and β-thalassemia (MIM: 613985) with a combined carrier frequency exceeding 6%, as well as G6PD deficiency (MIM: 300908; ∼3% in male infants)—in Taiwan supports the persistence of malaria-associated alleles.84,85 It is therefore plausible that some of the 149 variants identified in our study were historically maintained by positive selection due to malarial resistance but are now subject to purifying selection in the absence of malarial pressure.

A recent genome-wide association study (GWAS) based on the UK biobank cohort of 408,112 individuals identified 755 variants associated with MCV and 257 with MCHC.30 None of our candidate variants overlapped with their findings, and only five variant-linked genes (SLC2A1 [MIM: 138140], RARB [MIM: 180220], VPS13B [MIM: 607817], CDKAL1 [MIM: 611259], and BRIP1 [MIM: 605882]) were shared with their study. This discrepancy may reflect the rarity and thus population-specific nature of the variants detected in our dataset. Interestingly, most variants reported by Vuckovic et al.30 showed negative associations with MCV and MCHC—leading to smaller RBC sizes and lower MCH concentrations—whereas our results indicated the opposite pattern (see Figure 4A). Taken together, these contrasting signals suggest that recent adaptive selection may have shaped genetic variation and influenced disease susceptibilities related to erythrocyte traits in the Han Taiwanese population, assuming that the variants reported by Vuckovic et al. may instead be constrained by purifying selection.83

Conclusion

Overall, our study demonstrates the feasibility of detecting ongoing selection by directly measuring allele frequency differences between age groups in a relatively large dataset. Identifying genetic variants targeted by adaptive or negative natural selection in contemporary populations not only reveals selective pressures shaping human genomes but also illuminates their impact on disease susceptibility. As biobank and precision medicine efforts expand worldwide, identifying these variants will offer critical insight to support the development of personalized medical interventions based on individual genetic profiles.
