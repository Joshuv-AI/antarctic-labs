Corrosion behavior of selective laser melted NiTi shape memory alloy
Authors: Anurag Srivastava, Adnan Khan, Vasanth C. Shunmugasamy, Chaudhry Ali Usman, Alaa Elwany, Ibrahim Karaman, Bilal Mansoor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The present work explores corrosion response of Selective Laser Melting (SLM) prepared NiTi shape memory alloys with an aim to study processing-induced defects and surface-specific microstructural features inherent to SLM, influence on alloy corrosion response. The study systematically investigated corrosion behavior within the defect-minimized region of established SLM printability maps by comparing representative low- and high-power/scan speed conditions (P-80: 80 W, 330 mm s⁻¹; P-200: 200 W, 1080 mm s⁻¹) and by varying hatch spacing (64 μm and 80 μm) under constant laser power and scan speed. Corrosion behavior analysis was carried out on four distinct surfaces following a 72-h immersion in Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS): the internal and external surface of P-80 and P-200 specimens sectioned parallel to build direction (BD), and top and bottom surface of h-64 and h-80 specimens sectioned perpendicular to BD. Unlike prior studies that compared SLM NiTi against wrought material or varied parameters broadly, this work systematically isolates surface-specific defect populations within the defect-minimized region of an established printability map at matched volumetric energy density. The parallel to BD, P-80 specimens, showed the highest porosity of 5.4% and a corrosion current density of 140 nA/cm², with localized pits reaching up to 110 μm in depth, initiating primarily at porosity defects thereby disrupting the passive film formation. In contrast, perpendicular to BD specimens, particularly h-80 with larger hatch spacings, showed enhanced corrosion resistance on a single-specimen basis, attributed to a more effective passive film formation. Surfaces with higher defect density developed effective thicker but defect-rich passive films with poor protective performance, whereas Ti-enriched, low-defect surfaces formed effective thinner yet compact and highly resistive films. These results indicate that within the defect-minimized region of the printability map, surface-specific defect populations and surface chemistry are the dominant factors governing corrosion response, rather than nominal process parameters. The findings imply that build-orientation selection and surface-specific post-processing—beyond bulk-density optimization—are required for corrosion-critical SLM NiTi components. Direct passive-film characterization, replicate testing, and Ni-ion-release quantification are identified as essential follow-on work.

Introduction

NiTi are a class of shape memory alloys (SMAs) used in manufacturing various biomedical devices such as bone screws, cardiac stents, orthodontic wires due to their biocompatibility and good corrosion resistance1–4. In this regard, Additive manufacturing (AM) processes for NiTi alloys offer unique advantage such as preparing near net shape parts with minimal post processing steps5–9. One of the most utilized AM process namely, selective laser melting (SLM) utilizes a high-power laser to selectively melt and fuse fine metal powder bed to fabricate custom biomedical devices7,10. Most research on AM of NiTi has focused on process parameter optimization, developing structure-property relationships to understand shape memory effect, super elasticity and thermal properties4,7,11–13. NiTi alloy has potential for a wide range of applications in biomedical sector, with a key concern being possible Ni ions leaching into human body14,15. The presence of high chloride ions concentration in physiological environments can lead to localized corrosion of medical devices, releasing toxic corrosion byproducts and compromising part integrity16. Nickel is known to have carcinogenic and toxic effects, causing allergic reactions at concentrations of 300–500 ppb in the human body17,18. Halwani et al. have stated that repetitive motion in biomedical devices can cause localized breakdown of the surface passive film, leading to dispersion of heavy metal ions like Ni and Ti into surrounding tissues, which can be detrimental over time19.

Despite efforts to fabricate high density specimens, defects like pores, lack of fusion micro-cracks remain a challenge in SLM20. In this context, material printability maps are critical tools in additive manufacturing, particularly for NiTi alloys. These maps define optimal process parameters to achieve high-quality prints with minimal defects and consequently acceptable mechanical property for SLM NiTi alloys7,21,22. Even within optimal ranges, significant variations in corrosion behavior can occur due to microstructural differences and surface defects23–28. Understanding these variations in the presence of unavoidable processing defects is crucial for optimizing the corrosion resistance and understanding mechanism of localized damage of NiTi alloys, especially for biomedical applications where material integrity and life are paramount19,29. Corrosion response of NiTi prepared using various AM methods has been studied in literature. Study on corrosion response of NiTi fabricated by the Laser Engineered Net Shaping in physiological conditions9 observed that the specimens prepared with highest laser power showed highest corrosion potential and lowest corrosion current density (Power = 400 W, Ecorr = -200 mV vs. SCE, Icorr = 0.4 µA/cm2) making them nobler specimens that were prepared with lower laser power parameters (Power = 200 W, Ecorr = -275 mV vs. SCE, Icorr = 0.6 µA/cm2). The reason for improved corrosion resistance at higher laser power was attributed to formation of large grain sizes (P-200, Grain size = 10 μm; P-400, Grain size = 15 μm)9. In the case for SLM prepared NiTi, corrosion response has been studied with respect to various factors including scan strategy, surface orientation relative to the build direction, and the impact of phase distribution on localized corrosion. The effect of chess board scan strategy on the corrosion response of SLM NiTi was performed to elucidate the role of chess board size used during AM, which affects surface microstructure characteristics and consequently, corrosion rates30. The NiTi specimen prepared with a 5 mm × 5 mm chessboard pattern exhibited the best corrosion resistance (Ecorr = 0.101 V vs. SCE) and lowest corrosion rates (Icorr = 1.670 × 10− 5 A.cm− 2)30. This was due to the highest relative density (99.7%) and lowest surface roughness (9.95 μm) among the manufactured specimens. Hence, the specimen with fewer surface defects demonstrated that variation in chessboard pattern size greatly influences heat accumulation and surface characteristics, which play significant roles in the corrosion response of AM NiTi30.

Study on directional influence on corrosion response of SLM NiTi10, showing surfaces perpendicular to the build direction (transverse surface) exhibited better corrosion properties than surface parallel to the build direction (longitudinal surface)10. Transverse surfaces exhibited a chess board pattern of grains, while columnar grain structures were observed on longitudinal surfaces7,10,31. The difference in grain sizes and morphology between the transverse and longitudinal surface led to a lower corrosion current density and the formation of a more compact and resistant passive film on transverse surface10. The influence of NiTi phases on the localized corrosion mechanism of SLM NiTi was reported in32. The study reported that SLM of NiTi (45:55 wt% (Ti: Ni)) resulted in a microstructure with both austenite and martensitic phases. Corrosion analysis showed that pitting initiated at the phase boundary owing to galvanic coupling formation at the interface, leading to a non-uniform protective film32. Consequently, deeper pits were observed on specimens with mixed phases compared to those with a pure austenite phase32. This finding is consistent with33, which reported that the grain boundaries, where austenite and martensite phases are present, influenced localized corrosion. Similarly, phase interfaces have been identified as preferential sites for localized corrosion, implying that a high concentration of the austenite phase would result in fewer potential sites for localized corrosion32,33.

In summary, selecting process parameters in additive manufacturing is crucial for determining the microstructural characteristics of NiTi alloys, including minimizing surface defects and controlling variations in grain structure and phase distribution, as they significantly impact corrosion response. While prior studies have examined the corrosion behaviour of AM NiTi alloys through broad parameter variation, comparisons with wrought materials, and assessments of microstructural features such as grain structure and phase distribution, systematic investigation of corrosion sensitivity within defect-minimized processing windows remains limited. Moreover, the influence of surface orientation relative to the build direction, exposing fundamentally different defect morphologies and microstructural characteristics to corrosive environments has not been comprehensively explored in the context of localized corrosion mechanisms.

The present study addresses this by investigating how variations in process parameters - specifically laser power, scan speed, and hatch spacing, selected from within the defect-minimized region of printability map - affect the corrosion response across different surfaces of SLM-prepared NiTi shape memory alloy and correlating these findings with defects and microstructural features.

Materials and methods

Preparation of SLM NiTi specimens

In the present study, 4 cubes with dimensions of 10 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm were printed with Ni rich NiTi powder (50.8% at% Ni, 49.2% at% Ti) obtained from AP&C. The powder exhibited spherical morphology (Fig. 1a) with a size range of 10–60 μm and a median (D50) of 22.3 μm (Fig. 1b). The specimens were prepared using 3D Systems ProX DMP 200 L-PBF selective laser melting (SLM) system, as shown in Fig. 1c. The varied SLM process parameters include laser power, scan speed and hatch spacing, with a constant layer thickness of 40 μm. The nomenclature for different specimens studied in present work and parameters for printing them are listed in Table 1. The laser power, speed, and hatch spacing were selected based on established process printability maps, focusing on the defect-minimized region7. Two different process parameter combinations for laser power and scan speed were chosen, representing specifically lower (P-80: 80 W, 330 mm.s− 1) and higher (P-200: 200 W, 1080 mm.s− 1) power and scan speeds at constant hatch spacing, representing the lower and upper end of the defect-minimized region as highlighted in the printability map (see Fig. 1d)7. In addition to these, two different hatch spacings: 64 and 80 μm (at 160 W laser power and 830 mm.s− 1 scan speed, representing intermediate process parameters) were utilized to study effect of hatch spacing on the corrosion response. The hatch spacing of 64 μm was selected to keep the volumetric energy density (~ 79 J/mm³) closely matched to the P-80 condition (80 J/mm³), thereby decoupling the effect of hatch spacing from total energy input (Table 1).

Relative density and microstructure characterization

Relative density of the SLM prepared specimen were measured using Archimedes principle, following ASTM B962-17 standard9,34. The density comparison for the different specimens was performed by analyzing surface porosity using optical microscopy (OM, Zeiss AxioVert 40 MAT fitted with ERc5s camera). Specimen porosity area fraction was determined using optical microscopic images captured at 5× magnification on at least three images for each specimen.

Specimens were chemically etched with hydrofluoric acid, nitric acid and distilled water with a ratio of 1:3:10 for 15 s7,35, to observe melt pool geometry, boundary and presence of defects. Grain size analysis was carried out by analysing Electron Backscatter Diffraction (EBSD) pattern on the SLM printed specimen. EBSD patterns were obtained using field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM, ThermoFisher Scientific Apreo S) fitted with an EBSD analyzer, using 25 kV accelerating voltage, 13 nA probe current, step size of 0.1 μm and confidence index of 0.6. The qualitative local composition of SLM prepared NiTi alloy in as-printed condition was characterized using energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). The NiTi powder utilized and SLM prepared specimens were studied using a TA Instruments Q2000 differential scanning calorimeter (DSC) to measure the transformation temperatures mainly – austenite finish (Af) temperature. DSC was carried out by running at a heating/cooling rate of 10 °C/min for 2 thermal cycles from − 150 °C to 150 °C. Phase analysis of the specimens was also conducted using Rigaku Ultima IV X-ray diffractometer. Cu-Kα radiation was utilized with an operating voltage of 40 kV and a current of 30 mA. The 2θ angle was varied from 10° to 80° at a scan rate of 1°/min. All the SLM prepared specimens were metallurgically prepared and polished up to 0.05 μm alumina suspension before analysis.

Electrochemical characterization

All the corrosion studies were performed in Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) at 37 ± 1 °C temperature and at constant pH of 7.4 ± 0.1, to mimic physiological environment. HBSS was selected as a controlled physiological inorganic electrolyte that captures chloride-driven pitting susceptibility while excluding the confounding effects of proteins and amino acids; this approach is consistent with prior NiTi corrosion studies36–38 and isolates the role of processing-induced defects on passive-film behavior. Dynamic, protein-containing, and longer-term exposure are identified as essential follow-on work. The experiments were performed in a closed Gamry Multiport™ corrosion jacketed cell using a Gamry Reference 3000™ Potentiostat/Galvanostat/ZRA with a three electrode setup39. The solution pH was controlled using N-2-hydroxyethylpiperazine-N’-2-ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) buffer system (pH adjusted to 7.4 with NaOH). The corrosion setup comprised of working electrode as SLM prepared specimen, counter electrode as graphite rod, and reference electrode as a bridge tube holding Ag/AgCl. The active volume of corrosive medium was maintained at 350 ml, and working electrode exposed surface was 0.5 cm2.

All specimens underwent sequential OCP and EIS measurements at 1 h, 13 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h, with potentiodynamic polarization performed only after the final 72 h measurement. The OCP values recorded at different immersion times are obtained as the final value of each OCP experiment. Electrical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) test was carried out after OCP measurement at the different time intervals by frequency sweeping from 104 Hz to 10− 2 Hz at 10 mV40. The corrosion potential and corrosion current density was determined by recording potentiodynamic polarization (PD) curves starting from free potential and scanning the electrodes from cathodic to anodic potential, i.e., from − 2.0 volts to 1.5 volts at a scan rate of 0.5 mV/s after 72 h of immersion. Electrochemical measurements were performed on individual specimens for each processing condition due to the destructive nature of cyclic potentiodynamic and Mott–Schottky testing and practical resource constraints. Trends and rankings among conditions are therefore the appropriate basis for inference; the absolute magnitudes of iCorr donor density, and effective film thickness reported in Tables 3, 4 and 5 should be interpreted with this caveat.

Mott-Schottky tests were carried out post 24 h of immersion to study the film characteristics to assess the defect-driven electronic quality and semiconductor characteristics of the passive film, providing insight beyond film thickness40. The specimens were scanned at a frequency of 1 kHz from 0.0 V to 1 V (vs. Ag/AgCl.), with an signal amplitude of 5.0 mV and a potential step of 0.1 V40. Corroded specimens were cleaned with a solution mixture of 1 ml H2SO4 and 9 ml distilled water to remove the corrosion products as per ASTM G1-03 standard40. The localized corrosion phenomena of the corroded specimen surface were examined using SEM and confocal microscopy (Leica DCM8 3D Surface Metrology). SEM analysis of a chosen porosity surface defect was performed, before and after immersion in the corrosion media, along with profilometry to visualize localized corrosion phenomena such as, widening and increase in defect depth of existing surface micro defects.

Results

Relative density, surface porosity and microstructure characterization

All the SLM prepared specimens showed relative density > 91%, as presented in Table 1. Notably, the P-80 specimen, fabricated using lower power and scan speed combination, exhibited the lowest density and pronounced porosity on both its internal and external surfaces. Surface porosity measurements were used to characterize and quantify defects exposed to the corrosive environment. Porosities on the internal and external surfaces of the specimens are shown in Fig. 2 a-d. The observed inhomogeneities were quantified using ImageJ on at least three OM images for each case and the results are presented in Table 2. The external surfaces showed higher porosity compared to the internal surfaces, a trend consistent across both P-80 and P-200 specimen types (Table 2). The higher porosities in P-80 specimens can be linked to significant lack of fusion and crack defects, that can be attributed to inadequate heat input for metal powder fusion41–43. In contrast, the P-200 specimens exhibited porosity potentially due to gas entrapment within the molten powder20.

The specimens prepared with varied hatch spacings, namely h-64 and h-80, showed similar relative density (Table 1). Analysis of the area fraction of porosity showed that smaller hatch spacing h-64 specimen exhibited reduced porosity, compared to h-80 specimen (Fig. 2 e-h). In addition, higher porosity levels were observed on the bottom surfaces across both h-64 and h-80 specimen (Table 2). Reducing hatch spacing enhances the overlap of laser tracks, thereby improving interlayer bonding and reducing porosity43–46. However, this does not indicate that a direct correlation between hatch spacing and surface porosity exists, as there are other factors such as the linear energy density and interaction between melt pools. Saghaian et al. observed increasing porosity with increasing hatch spacing from 40 to 160 μm, prepared at constant laser power of 50 W and 125 mm/s scan speed. However, the study reported that the linear energy density (ratio of laser power to scan speed) along with hatch spacing, had a strong influence on the porosity of SLM NiTi46. Feng et al. also reported that the amount of porosity does not change monotonously with hatch spacing, buts it is confluence of factors such as energy density, hatch spacing and melting/remelting of successive tracks that determines porosity47.

Etched microstructures of SLM fabricated NiTi observed along parallel and perpendicular to build direction (BD), using optical microscopy is presented in Figs. 3 and 4, respectively. The OM images show different inhomogeneities such as lack of fusion defects, micro- voids and cracks at the melt pool boundaries, in both parallel and perpendicular specimen planes. Grain orientation maps obtained through EBSD for specimens prepared with different hatch spacings (h-64 and h-80), top and bottom surfaces along with grain size distribution, are presented in Fig. 5. It is evident that the hatch spacing significantly impacts the grain structure and size distribution of the top and bottom surfaces of these specimens. The top surfaces of both h-64 and h-80 exhibit coarser grains, with h-64 showing slightly finer grains due to increased overlap of laser passes, resulting in better fusion and a marginally finer grain structure compared to h-80. In contrast, the bottom surfaces display finer grains, attributed to faster cooling rates near the build plate48–50. Reduced hatch spacing generally results in better fusion, enhanced heat distribution and melting uniformity, and increased cooling rates, contributing to finer grain structures and enhanced overall microstructural homogeneity48–53. While grain morphology differences between P-80 and P-200 conditions are expected based on thermal history differences, detailed grain size analysis for these conditions was not performed in the present study.

DSC results for the utilized NiTi powder and SLM prepared specimens are presented in Fig. 6. Based on DSC results it could be observed that the specimens would be in austenite phase at the tested temperature (37 °C) utilized in present study. Analysis of phase composition of the SLM NiTi using X-ray diffraction analysis showed predominantly B2 austenite phase (ICDD PDF 65–917), evidenced by characteristic peaks, as presented in Fig. 6. The XRD spectra highlights the influence of Ni-rich alloy composition on phase stabilization, particularly under rapid cooling conditions (approximately 106 to 107 K/s) encountered during SLM54. It has been reported that secondary precipitates such as Ni2Ti, Ni4Ti3 or NiTi3, have been observed in XRD spectra of SML NiTi54. In the present work, secondary precipitates are not observed, and their absence highlights the critical role of cooling rates in phase formation during the SLM process54. DSC and XRD analyses showed that bulk SLM NiTi was predominantly in the B2 austenite phase at the corrosion test temperature (37 °C). The likelihood of a continuous bulk austenite/martensite galvanic couple is therefore low under these test conditions. However, local stress-induced or surface-deformation-induced martensite at machined or corroded sub-surface regions cannot be excluded from bulk XRD and DSC alone.

Comparison of icorr of SLM prepared NiTi to traditionally manufactured NiTi alloy exposed in similar physiological media (HBSS) showed that SLM prepared specimens exhibit significantly lower icorr, indicating slower rate16,27,57,58. For instance, Stergioudi et al. reported a value of 700 nA/cm2 for commercial NiTi alloy exposed in HBSS, which is almost ~ 5 times the corrosion current density observed for P-80-E specimen from present study57. Similarly, it has been observed that the corrosion current density of conventionally manufactured NiTi alloy was approximately 2000 nA/cm2, ~ 14 times greater than that of the P-80-E specimen13. Comparison of the present study results with literature on wrought NiTi, highlights the enhanced corrosion resistance of SLM prepared NiTi alloys over their traditionally manufactured counterparts. While these comparisons suggest that SLM-prepared NiTi can achieve corrosion performance comparable to or better than conventionally manufactured material, direct quantitative comparison should be approached with caution. Differences in surface finish, microstructure, texture, residual stress state, and minor compositional variations beyond porosity alone may significantly influence corrosion behavior. Accordingly, the key implication of the present results is that defect-optimized SLM processing can produce NiTi alloys with acceptable corrosion resistance for biomedical applications, rather than demonstrating inherent superiority over wrought material.

The influence of hatch spacing on corrosion response of SLM NiTi specimens was examined using OCP values and EIS spectra. The OCP measurements indicated spontaneous passivation, with a balance between oxide film formation and dissolution. Both the specimens showed similar EIS spectra shape, i.e., incomplete spectra and continuous drifting towards an increasing value at low-frequency range, indicative of enhanced resistance and highly capacitive nature of the surface film with increasing immersion duration. The EIS spectra obtained for top surface were modelled using a series combination of capacitance (Cp) and resister (Rp). Discussion

SLM prepared NiTi can help in preparing custom biomedical devices, however their long-term performance in physiological environments is strongly governed by localized corrosion behavior. In this present study, NiTi cubes are prepared using SLM process based on good regions of the process parameter printability maps, where the microstructure is minimized for defects. Although the microstructure is minimized for defects in the good area of printability map, defects (pores, lack of fusion and micro cracks) could be present. It has been well established in literature that these defects have been sites for localized corrosion initiation, in SLM prepared specimens25,67–69. The effect of such defects on SLM prepared NiTi alloy overall corrosion response and its susceptibility to localized corrosion, needs to be fully understood for application as biomedical devices. In the present work, from the good area of printability maps, process parameters with varying laser power and scan speed are chosen at the lower and higher end of spectrum (P-80 and P-200). In the intermediate range, the effect of hatch spacing is studied at constant laser power and scan speed. All the surface studied in the present work showed > 91% relative density (Table 1) and < 5.5% surface porosity (Table 2). The present results demonstrate that variations in defect density and morphology influence corrosion response, underscoring defect-driven mechanisms as the dominant factor governing corrosion susceptibility in SLM-prepared NiTi alloys, rather than process parameter selection alone.

The electrochemical response of SLM NiTi specimens, influenced by process parameter combinations of laser power/scan speed and surface location, revealed key insights into corrosion behavior. The P-200 specimens exhibited superior corrosion resistance compared to P-80 specimens. Internal surfaces generally showed better corrosion resistance than external surfaces. The higher positive OCP for the P-80 external surface suggested a thicker but less protective passive layer. In contrast, impedance measurements indicated that the P-200 external surface developed a more protective film over time. The P-80 surface’s higher icorr and lower pitting potential suggest its passive layer is less effective due to higher porosity. SEM and profilometry analyses further confirmed that localized corrosion initiates at processing-induced defects, where passive film disruption promotes pit growth and accelerated material degradation.

Corrosion analysis of specimens prepared with different hatch spacings showed that bottom surface, i.e., near the build plate (Fig. 8c, d) exhibited localized corrosion in comparison to top surface. This can be explained based on the surface inhomogeneities observed between the top and bottom surface of SLM prepared specimens. For both h-64 and h-80 specimens, the bottom surface showed higher surface porosities than top surface (Table 2). SLM prepared NiTi microstructure has chess board pattern grains along the plane perpendicular (ND-TD plane) to the build direction and columnar grains along the build direction (BD-TD plane)7,10. The bottom and top surfaces of SLM prepared specimens undergo varying cooling rates during processing, resulting in distinctive microstructures70. Study on analysis of thermal cooling rate during Laser Metal Deposition of Ni based super alloy showed that higher cooling rate resulted in smaller grain size48. In the present work, top surface showed larger grain size distribution compared to bottom surface, observed from the grain size distribution obtained from EBSD maps, as presented in Fig. 5e, f. The bottom surface adjacent to the base plate experiences much higher cooling rate49, resulting in smaller grain sizes (Fig. 5) but also leading to increased defects (Table 2)20. The localized corrosion occurring in the bottom surface due to defects, results in repeated breakdown of the passive film, is reflected in the OCP variation with respect to immersion time, where noticeable drops were observed (Fig. 10b).

Based on OCP and EIS results from the study, it could be observed that h-80 specimen with higher hatch spacing, produced a compact and well adherent passive surface film, while h-64 specimen with lower hatch spacing showed a thicker but higher conductive surface film (Tables 4 and 5). Studies in literature have shown that surface film formed on NiTi alloys, typically exhibits n-type semiconductor character64,65,69. During corrosion of conventional NiTi in the presence of aggressive Cl− ions, corrosion starts at the defect sites and results in breakage of native surface film along with Ni-Ti bonds, resulting in release of Ni ions into the corrosion media16. Due to the Ni-Ti bond breakage and subsequent release of Ni ions, remaining Ti readily reacts with oxygen to form titanium oxide. It has been reported that heat of formation of TiO2 is -956 kJ/mol while that of NiO is -241 kJ/mol16, suggesting TiO2 as the surface protective barrier for NiTi alloy. The oxide layer formation on the specimen surface leaves the presence of excess electrons, which would cause the film to have a n-type semiconductor behaviour as observed in the M-S plots (Fig. 11). The nature of the formed TiO2 film governs the corrosion response of NiTi alloy16.

To study the localized composition of SLM prepared NiTi specimens with varying hatch spacing, EDS line-scan analyses were conducted on as-printed specimens, and the elemental variation are presented in Fig. 12. From the EDS line scan analysis, it could be observed that h-80 specimen shows higher content of element Ti for both top and bottom surfaces. During SLM of NiTi, it has been previously reported that evaporation of Ni occurs, altering the composition and thereby the functional properties of near equiatomic NiTi64,71. The higher Ti content observed on h-80 top and bottom surfaces by EDS is consistent with preferential Ni evaporation under those processing conditions and could plausibly promote more rapid formation of a compact TiO2 rich passive layer. Nevertheless, the observed compositional differences alone may not fully account for the enhanced corrosion resistance of the h-80 condition. Additional contributing factors may include variations in residual stress arising from differing thermal histories during SLM, grain boundary character distributions influencing passive film stability, and differences in defect morphology. These factors may act synergistically with Ti enrichment to promote the formation of a more compact and protective passive film in the h-80 condition.

Collectively, the electrochemical results reveal that corrosion resistance is dictated not solely by effective passive film thickness, but by the balance between film thickness and compactness. High-porosity surfaces such as P-80-E and h-64-B develop comparatively thicker passive layers, as reflected by higher capacitance values, yet these films exhibit elevated donor densities indicative of defect-rich structures that provide limited protection. In contrast, surfaces with lower defect densities and Ti enrichment, particularly the h-80 condition, form thinner but significantly more compact and highly resistive passive films. This thick-but-defective versus thin-but-compact distinction reconciles the seemingly contradictory trends observed in OCP, EIS, and Mott–Schottky analyses across all processing conditions.

Given the strong influence of defect distribution and surface orientation on corrosion behavior, build strategies should be carefully planned such that surfaces intended for prolonged exposure to physiological environments correspond to top-facing or internal orientations where defect density is minimized. Moreover, post-processing approaches and surface qualification protocols may require surface-specific optimization rather than uniform treatment across all printed faces. The present study intentionally focused on isolating the influence of processing-induced defects, surface orientation, and passive film characteristics on the corrosion behavior of SLM-fabricated NiTi within the defect-minimized region of established printability maps. The 72-hour immersion period provided a controlled accelerated assessment of early-stage passive film development and localized corrosion initiation.

The electrochemical measurements, impedance-derived effective film thickness estimates, and Mott–Schottky analysis collectively indicate the formation of predominantly TiO₂-based passive films with varying defect densities across different SLM conditions. A direct chemical and structural characterization of the passive layers was not performed in the present study. Accordingly, the inferred film compositions and protective mechanisms are based on established passivation behavior of NiTi alloys in chloride-containing physiological environments reported in the literature. Direct characterization of passive film chemistry and structure would provide further mechanistic resolution.

Limitations of present work

It should also be noted that present study results are based of static corrosion tests in carried HBSS for 72 h and further analysis is warranted to study dynamic, tribocorrosion, protein and amino acid containing, and long-term Ni ion release conditions and quantification for SLM prepared NiTi parts to be utilized in biomedical service. Ni ion release into solution was not quantified, which limits direct translation of corrosion-resistance trends into biocompatibility predictions. The present study results are also inferred from corrosion characterization of a single specimen due to the destructive nature of cyclic potentiodynamic and Mott–Schottky measurements. Passive-film composition and structure were inferred from electrochemical and EDS data; no direct surface-analytical characterization (XPS, AES, TEM) was performed, and the TiO₂-rich passive-film mechanism is therefore hypothesized. The phase identification is based on bulk DSC and XRD analysis and it may not capture local stress-induced martensite. These identified items will be carried out as a follow-on work of present research.

Conclusion

The present study evaluated corrosion response of SLM prepared NiTi SMA to elucidate the effects of processing-induced defects and surface-specific microstructural features inherent to SLM on corrosion response. Based on study results, the following conclusions can be drawn:

The present study indicated that corrosion behaviour of SLM-fabricated NiTi within defect-minimized processing windows is significantly influenced by surface-specific defect populations. Variations in porosity and defect morphology significantly influenced passive film stability and localized corrosion susceptibility. Surface orientation relative to the build direction played a critical role in corrosion performance, with top and internal surfaces consistently exhibiting superior resistance compared to bottom and external surfaces, respectively, due to differences in defects and microstructural features.The top surface exhibited a stable passive layer formation in Open Circuit Potential, while the bottom surface exhibited repeated dissolution and repassivation. This was also further supported by the lower donor density for the top surface compared to the bottom surface (h-64-T had lower ND than h-64-B). Comparing the internal and external surfaces of SLM prepared NiTi specimens (P-80 and P-200), it was observed that internal surfaces exhibited better corrosion response. Internal surfaces showed substantially lower corrosion current densities (~ 85% and ~ 55% lower for P-80 and P-200, respectively, on a single-specimen basis) and substantially lower donor densities (~ 60% and ~ 50% lower). These indicators suggest improved corrosion resistance because a more stable open circuit potential and lower donor density are commonly associated with a more protective and stable passive oxide film that reduces charge transfer.Hatch spacing affected corrosion behavior in a surface-specific way. The h-80 top surface formed a thinner but more compact passive film (higher impedance, lower donor density) than its h-64 counterpart, despite reduced laser-track overlap. EDS line scans suggested surface Ti enrichment under h-80 conditions, potentially due to preferential Ni evaporation; in the absence of direct passive-film chemical characterization (e.g., XPS or AES), this mechanism remains hypothesized. On bottom surfaces, the trend reversed, with h-80-B exhibiting higher i_corr than h-64-B — indicating that larger hatch spacing is not globally advantageous and that surface orientation must be considered alongside hatch spacing. Overall, these findings highlight that optimization of SLM NiTi for corrosion-critical biomedical applications cannot rely solely on printability maps or bulk density metrics. Instead, surface-specific defect distributions and passive film quality must be explicitly considered in process design, post-processing strategies, and component qualification.

Comparing the internal and external surfaces of SLM prepared NiTi specimens (P-80 and P-200), it was observed that internal surfaces exhibited better corrosion response. Internal surfaces showed substantially lower corrosion current densities (~ 85% and ~ 55% lower for P-80 and P-200, respectively, on a single-specimen basis) and substantially lower donor densities (~ 60% and ~ 50% lower). These indicators suggest improved corrosion resistance because a more stable open circuit potential and lower donor density are commonly associated with a more protective and stable passive oxide film that reduces charge transfer.

Hatch spacing affected corrosion behavior in a surface-specific way. The h-80 top surface formed a thinner but more compact passive film (higher impedance, lower donor density) than its h-64 counterpart, despite reduced laser-track overlap. EDS line scans suggested surface Ti enrichment under h-80 conditions, potentially due to preferential Ni evaporation; in the absence of direct passive-film chemical characterization (e.g., XPS or AES), this mechanism remains hypothesized. On bottom surfaces, the trend reversed, with h-80-B exhibiting higher i_corr than h-64-B — indicating that larger hatch spacing is not globally advantageous and that surface orientation must be considered alongside hatch spacing. Overall, these findings highlight that optimization of SLM NiTi for corrosion-critical biomedical applications cannot rely solely on printability maps or bulk density metrics. Instead, surface-specific defect distributions and passive film quality must be explicitly considered in process design, post-processing strategies, and component qualification.
