Quantum batteries and time dilation
Esteban Martínez Vargas

Abstract
Is spacetime fundamental or can it be derived through quantum interactions? We propose here a way to describe time dilation solely from quantum mechanics. First we start by observing that any operational notion of time must imply some sort of regular motion and, crucially, some sort of memory. Thus the clock model we use here is a simple example of a quantum memory: a charging battery. We describe here the charging of such batteries with quantum open dynamics. The expected value of these batteries grow linearly in time like φ ​ t \varphi t . The open dynamics is dependent on an auxiliar state σ \sigma . Therefore, with a different auxiliar state we have a different φ \varphi . We can describe time dilation and thus a metric this way. We exemplify with a black hole metric.

Therefore it

We ourselves to special relativity, then a myriad of effects have been predicted

However, the dynamical aspect of spacetime, the
The fact that it can change its configuration has escaped description within

Thus, retrieving the full phenomenology of general relativity within quantum

The concept of spacetime has been introduced in quantum mechanics in several
ways. Firstly, it was introduced through the Klein-Gordon equation, which
considers a momentum operator and a mass term

]

being considered a non-physical equation. Another way is to
consider the Dirac equation, which contains only positive-energy particles.
There is another standard way of introducing spacetime in quantum mechanics.
The procedure implies the quantization of a state so that a field is considered.
Then, a symmetry with respect to the Poincaré group is imposed. Observe then
that the usual procedure to include spacetime within quantum mechanics is by
imposing certain symmetry on wavefunctions.

These approaches ask for the existence of spacetime into a
theory that is not necessarily compatible with it.
In contrast, there have been
more recent approaches that try to build the phenomenology of spacetime

the Poincaré invariance implies. Such an approach doesn’t presuppose the

II
CPU maps

The continuous evolution of an open quantum system can be described
with a master equation
[
7
,
26
]
. This global evolution, which assumes
openness to an environment, is
a generalization of Schrödinger’s equation, normally called the Lindblad equation or the Markovian quantum master equation. This Lindblad
equation fulfills the physical requirement that the evolution of a quantum state has to be
completely positive and trace preserving, in other words, it has to be described with a quantum channel
[
23
]
.
Notice that this point of view is centered on
the quantum state, which is the object that evolves in time. There exists a dual picture
of the physics involved. Such a picture assumes that the observables evolve in time,
which is known as Heisenberg’s
[
13
]
. A correspondent open quantum system can be obtained in this
picture, which is known as the quantum Langevin equation or adjoint quantum master equation

[
26
,
7
]
. Here, the main point to notice is
that the evolution is given by the dual of a quantum channel.
Notice that applying the channel
\Phi
to a state
\rho
would produce, with
respect the observable
A
the following

{\rm tr}\,[\Phi[\rho]A]={\rm tr}\,[\rho\Phi^{\dagger}[A]],

(2)

where
\Phi^{\dagger}
denotes the dual transformation for
\Phi
. We notice that the
numerical value is the same, the change in time is transformed from the state
to the observable. We notice that the adjoint transformation
\Phi^{\dagger}
has to be
unital
[
13
]
, as

{\rm tr}\,[\Phi[\rho]\mathds{I}]={\rm tr}\,[\rho\Phi^{\dagger}[\mathds{I}]]=1.

(3)

As
\Phi
is trace preserving then

\Phi^{\dagger}[\mathds{I}]=\mathds{I}.

(4)

Therefore
\Phi^{\dagger}
is unital. Also, as
(\Phi\otimes\mathds{I})^{\dagger}=\Phi^{\dagger}\otimes\mathds{I}
then

\Phi^{\dagger}
is completely positive
[
13
]
.

III
\Phi^{\dagger}

\Phi^{\dagger}

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\,_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}[Z_{A}(\mathds{I}\otimes\mathds{I})]

\displaystyle=\mathds{I},

\displaystyle Z_{A}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

(5)

The first condition asks for the transformation to be unital and the second asks
to be completely positive
[
23
]
. If we want
Z_{A}
to have
the observable
A
as a fixed point then

\Phi^{\dagger}[A]={\rm tr}\,_{\mathcal{H}2}[Z_{A}(\mathds{I}\otimes A^{\intercal})]=A.

(6)

Given
A
we can obtain a
Z_{A}
that fulfills the fixed point condition (
6
)
as well as the completely positive and unital conditions (
5

Given

Z_{A}\equiv A\otimes\frac{\left|{v}\rangle\!\langle{v}\right|^{\intercal}}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}+\frac{\left(\mathds{I}-\frac{A}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\right)}{\left(\frac{N}{{\rm tr}\,A}-\frac{1}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\right)}\otimes\left(\frac{\mathds{I}}{{\rm tr}\,A}-\frac{\left|{v}\rangle\!\langle{v}\right|^{\intercal}}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\right)

(7)

We can easily check that
Z_{A}
fulfills conditions of unitality (
5
) and fixed point (
6
).
The positivity condition is a bit more delicate and thus treated as a theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Z_{A}\geq 0
if and only if

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\geq\mathds{I}\left(\frac{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle-{\rm tr}\,A}{N-1}\right),

\displaystyle\mathds{I}\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq A.

(8)

Where
N=\text{dim}\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

\Rightarrow
We multiply
Z_{A}
given by Eq. (
7
) by
\mathds{I}\otimes\left|{v}\rangle\!\langle{v}\right|^{\intercal}

and get the trace in the second Hilbert space (
\mathcal{H}2
). This yields the inequality

\frac{A}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}+\left(\mathds{I}-\frac{A}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\right)\frac{\frac{1}{{\rm tr}\,A}-\frac{1}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle\penalty\ }}{\frac{N}{{\rm tr}\,A}-\frac{1}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle\penalty\ }}\geq 0.

(9)

After some algebra, we reach

A\geq\mathds{I}\left(\frac{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle-{\rm tr}\,A}{N-1}\right).

(10)

Then, we multiply
Z_{A}
given by Eq. (
7
) by
\mathds{I}\otimes\left|{v^{\perp}}\rangle\!\langle{v^{\perp}}\right|
where

\left|{v^{\perp}}\right\rangle
is a vector such that
\langle{v^{\perp}}|{v}\rangle=0
. We also get the trace in

\mathcal{H}2
to get

\frac{\mathds{I}-\frac{A}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle\penalty\ }}{\frac{N}{{\rm tr}\,A}-\frac{1}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle\penalty\ }}\frac{1}{{\rm tr}\,A}\geq 0,

(11)

therefore it is immediate to see

\mathds{I}\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle\geq A.

(12)

\Leftarrow
Taking equation (
9

\otimes\left|{v}\rangle\!\langle{v}\right|^{\intercal}

\frac{A}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\otimes\left|{v}\rangle\!\langle{v}\right|^{\intercal}+\left(\mathds{I}-\frac{A}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\right)\otimes\left|{v}\rangle\!\langle{v}\right|^{\intercal}\frac{\frac{1}{{\rm tr}\,A}-\frac{1}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle\penalty\ }}{\frac{N}{{\rm tr}\,A}-\frac{1}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle\penalty\ }}\geq 0.

Multiplying Eq. (

\otimes\mathds{I}{/v}

Summing Eqs. (

\displaystyle+\left(\mathds{1}-\frac{A}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\right)\frac{\left\langle{v}\right|B\left|{v}\right\rangle}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\frac{\cancel{\left(\frac{{\rm tr}\,A}{{\rm tr}\,A}-\frac{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle\penalty\ }\right)}}{\left(\frac{N}{{\rm tr}\,A}-\frac{1}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\right)}

(18)

V
Kraus operators

Remember that a quantum channel (CPTP map) can be written in terms of Kraus operators

[
23
]
as

\Phi[\rho]=\sum_{i}B_{i}\rho B_{i}^{\dagger},

(25)

for suitable operators
B_{i}
which fulfill

\sum_{i}B_{i}^{\dagger}B_{i}=\mathds{I}.

(26)

Condition (
26
) is necessary for trace preservation.
Then, from equation (
2
) we observe
that

{\rm tr}\,[\Phi[\rho]A]={\rm tr}\,[\sum_{i}B_{i}\rho B_{i}^{\dagger}A]={\rm tr}\,[\rho\sum_{i}B_{i}^{\dagger}AB_{i}]={\rm tr}\,[\rho\Phi^{\dagger}[A]],

(27)

therefore,

\Phi^{\dagger}[A]=\sum_{i}B_{i}^{\dagger}AB_{i}.

(28)

Now, remember that we are considering maps which are unital, therefore
we do not ask the fulfillment of condition (
26
) but an
analog condition

\Phi^{\dagger}[\mathds{I}]=\sum_{i}B_{i}^{\dagger}B_{i}=\mathds{I}.

(29)

We should stress that there is not a completeness condition in this
case as
\Phi^{\dagger}
is not necessarily a CPTP map.
Observe that our CPU map is written in terms of
a Choi matrix in equation (
7
). Using proposition 2.20 of
[
23
]

we can relate between the Choi representation of linear maps and its Kraus
representation. For this purpose we write a diagonalization of the observable
A

as

A=\sum_{i}a_{i}\left|{a_{i}}\rangle\!\langle{a_{i}}\right|.

(30)

We also complete the identity with a suitable orthonormal basis with respect to

\left|{v}\rangle\!\langle{v}\right|
as

\mathds{I}=\sum_{j}\left|{v_{j}}\rangle\!\langle{v_{j}}\right|.

(31)

Let us define
\left|{v_{r}}\rangle\!\langle{v_{r}}\right|=\left|{v}\rangle\!\langle{v}\right|^{\intercal}
. Using these decompositions
and following the correspondence of Choi and Kraus representations we
define the following operators

\displaystyle B_{i}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{a_{i}}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\penalty\ }\left|{a_{i}}\rangle\!\langle{v}\right|,

\displaystyle C_{ij}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left(\frac{1-\frac{a_{i}}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\penalty\ }{\frac{N}{{\rm tr}\,A}-\frac{1}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\penalty\ }\right)\left(\frac{1}{{\rm tr}\,A}-\frac{\delta_{jr}}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\right)}\left|{a_{i}}\rangle\!\langle{v_{j}}\right|.

(32)

We can check that the completeness relation (
26
) is fulfilled

\sum_{i}B_{i}^{\dagger}\mathds{I}B_{i}=\sum_{i}\frac{a_{i}}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\left|{a_{i}}\rangle\!\langle{a_{i}}\right|.

(33)

Analogously we have

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}C_{ij}^{\dagger}\mathds{I}C_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\left(\frac{1-\frac{a_{i}}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\penalty\ }{\frac{N}{{\rm tr}\,A}-\frac{1}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\penalty\ }\right)\left(\frac{1}{{\rm tr}\,A}-\frac{\delta_{jr}}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\right)\left|{a_{i}}\rangle\!\langle{a_{i}}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left(\frac{1-\frac{a_{i}}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\penalty\ }{\frac{N}{{\rm tr}\,A}-\frac{1}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\penalty\ }\right)\left|{a_{i}}\rangle\!\langle{a_{i}}\right|\left(\frac{N}{{\rm tr}\,A}-\frac{1}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{I}-\sum_{i}\frac{a_{i}}{\left\langle{v}\right|A\left|{v}\right\rangle}\left|{a_{i}}\rangle\!\langle{a_{i}}\right|.

(34)

Therefore, we obtain the unitality condition

\Phi^{\dagger}[\mathds{I}]=\sum_{i}B_{i}^{\dagger}B_{i}+\sum_{ij}C_{ij}^{\dagger}C_{ij}=\mathds{I}.

(35)

VI
Example: Quantum batteries

Let us consider a specific model of a quantum system that interacts with an
environment. As mentioned before, the most natural system for us to focus on is a
quantum battery modeled by a harmonic oscillator. The environment is modelled by
another harmonic oscillator, and we will have an interaction term, following
[
3
]
,

H=\omega_{a}a^{\dagger}a+\omega_{b}b^{\dagger}b+\varepsilon(e^{i\omega_{L}t}a+e^{-i\omega_{L}t}a^{\dagger})+\chi(a^{\dagger}b+b^{\dagger}a)=H_{0}+H_{I}.

(36)

Where we define

H_{I}\equiv\chi(a^{\dagger}b+b^{\dagger}a).

(37)

We define a state transformation

\tilde{\rho}\equiv e^{iH_{0}t}\rho(t)e^{-iH_{0}t}.

(38)

A Von Neumann equation, which describes the general evolution of a quantum system
is given in the interaction picture as

\frac{d\tilde{\rho}}{dt}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\tilde{H},\tilde{\rho}].

(39)

Notice however that
H_{I}
defined in (
37
) only considers a transmission
of particles between the main system and the environment, which occur in a one-by-one manner.
This means that if the system loses a particle, the environment wins one and vice versa.
This leaves us with the very natural generalization for the interaction Hamiltonian

H_{I}^{G}\equiv\sum{n,r}\chi_{n,r}({a^{\dagger}}^{n}b^{n}+{b^{\dagger}}^{r}a^{r})

(40)

VII
Simulation of black holes

We can apply these mathematical insights to build an interaction with an external
field such that time dilation appears. Observe that a particle in
a Schwarzschild metric would present a time dilation of the form

\Delta T=\left(\frac{32M^{3}e^{-\frac{r}{2M}}\penalty\ }{r}\right)\Delta t,

(52)

this is done with the Kruskal extension coordinates
[
22
]
. Here

r
is the distance to the center of the coordinates and
M
the mass of the
black hole.

In order to recover a metric as in equation (
52
)
then the state
\sigma
must depend on
r
and
M
i.e.
\sigma(r,M)
.
However, there is a significant departure from the Schwarzchild metric
because the quantity
\varphi(\sigma)
defined in (
48
) is
bounded: it depends on the bases
\left|{j}\right\rangle
and
\left|{n}\right\rangle
. The more aligned
the bases are, the higher the value of
\varphi
. The maximum is obtained
for
\langle{j}|{n}\rangle=\delta_{j,n}
, thus
\varphi_{\text{max}}=\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}j
.
If the bases are orthogonal, then
\varphi=0
. Therefore, there is no
singularity at the origin as it would imply an unbounded parameter
\varphi
.

The behavior of the states
\sigma
with respect to
r
asks for a
physical interpretation. Observe that the smaller
r
, the more aligned
the bases
\left|{j}\right\rangle
and
\left|{n}\right\rangle
. A possible interpretation is that
as
r
gets smaller, the field “saturates” the interaction. This would mean
that there are more states
\sigma
the smaller the
r
.

The metric in this case would be for an observable
X
as

\langle X\rangle=\left(\frac{32M^{3}e^{-\frac{r+r_{0}}{2M}}\penalty\ }{r+r_{0}}\right)t,

(53)

for some finite value
r_{0}>0
.

VIII
Discussion

The experimentally observed phenomenon of time dilation has fundamental
implications in our understanding of the world. The usual approach to
describe this phenomenon is by using the classical concept of spacetime.
However, this concept has its limitations when one tries to expand one’s
understanding to quantum systems. To measure time, it is always necessary
to have some kind of memory. The simplest kind of quantum memory
is a charged battery. Therefore, we study a
quantum battery that charges as it interacts with its environment.
We show that there exist completely positive unital maps that imply
a linear growth of an observable. We apply these insights and generalize
previous descriptions of the charging of a quantum battery
that is open to its environment. The result is that the charging speed
depends on the state of the environment
\sigma
. Changing this
state leaves us with a simulation of a metric. We expose how this
simulation would look for a metric with central spatial symmetry.
Notably, this description does not present a singularity.

There are questions regarding the physical interpretation of a metric
built here. Specifically, what would mean that the state is more saturated
as
r\rightarrow 0
? This is a relevant question to make a consistent description
of a quantum description of a black hole metric, and a matter for future research.
Another interesting perspective of future research would be to
engineer a metric that confines particles spatially.
