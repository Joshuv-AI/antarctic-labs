This Technical Report covers the period of June 1, 1994 to August 31, 2023, during which time
the PI’s Laboratory for Nuclear Science (LNS) received the support of the Nuclear Physics
Program of the Office of Science (OS) of the US Department of Energy, (USDOE) grant Number
DE-FG02-94ER40870, for a total of approximately $4.2M. During these twenty-nine years the PI
enjoyed the very supportive and much appreciated professional help of the Nuclear Physics (NP)
program managers, starting in 1994 with Dr. Dennis Kovar, then Dr. Eugene Henry, till 2023 with
Dr. Sharon Stephenson. For example, the FY04 support of $121,000 per annum steadily increased
to reach $215,000 in FY22, when Dr. Paul Sorensen increased the funding by 7.5% over the
previous cycle. The grant was however terminated the following year, although it was increased
by 7.5% the year before.

Nevertheless, Dr. Sharon Stephenson made the much-appreciated effort to continue the research
with a no cost extension and a supplementary funding of $30,000. She did so at her discretion as
a program manager, despite the recommendation of the review panel to terminate the grant.

The PI discussed with Dr. Stephenson while they met at the FRIB, what he considered to be a very
troubling indeed faulty review that led to the grant termination.

I consider the circumstances of the termination of the grant troubling enough to warrant feedback
as I do here. I further place this preamble at the top of my Technical Review with all due respect
and with utmost sincerity, to initiate a discussion perhaps corrections and instruction to the panel.
I consider this feedback a must.

2. Abstract

The Laboratory for Nuclear Science (LNS) at the Avery Point campus of the University of Connecticut
(UConn) is engaged in low energy Nuclear Physics (NP) research in Nuclear Astrophysics (NA) and
Nuclear Structure (NS) of light nuclei. In NA we aim to measure key reactions that were known with poor
accuracy which directly affected our understanding of stellar evolution and the Standard Model of the Sun
(SMS). In both these measurements we developed new methods to measure these reactions by measuring
the time reversed process utilizing virtual and real gamma particles (photons).

The cross section of the fusion of 7Be + p to form 8B in the 7Be(p,g) reaction that occurs in the core of the
sun was known in 2000 with the poor accuracy of +20% -18%. It led John Bahcall at that time to declare
“the measurement of the 7Be(p,g) reaction the most important measurement of nuclear physics”. Our
research at the RIKEN lab in Japan (1992-1996) and the GSI lab in Germany (1996-2000) together with
the research at the University of Washington in Seattle and at the Weizmann Institute of the Sciences,
reduced the uncertainty of the formation of solar 8B to ±3%.

In traditional NA measurements one measures at higher energies (where the cross sections are large enough
to be measured) the same fusion process that occurs in stars. The measured cross sections are extrapolated
to stellar conditions at much lower energy. We developed the method of the Coulomb Dissociation (CD) of
8
  B in the field of virtual photons (g*) emanating from 208Pb. As such we measured the time reverse process
of 8B + g* → 7Be + p. Our result for the CD of 8B preceded by three years the traditional measurements
carried out at Seattle and Weizmann. All three results agreed which allowed us to confirm the high accuracy
of ±3%. And in the same time we confirmed our very new method utilizing CD and time reversed
measurement to measure nuclear processes.

The cross section of the fusion of 12C with helium (alpha-particle) to form 16O in the 12C(a,g) reaction during
stellar helium burning is still today not known with sufficient accuracy. The measurement of the 12C(a,g)
reaction was pronounced by Fred Hoyle in 1954 as an essential input for our understanding of the formation
(indeed destruction) of carbon, and Willie Fowler in his Nobel paper of 1984 declared this measurement of
“paramount importance” to NA, as it is still today.

We developed a new method to measure the cross section of the 12C(a,g)16O reaction by measuring the
photodissociation of 16O in gamma-beams, with the 16O target material contained in the CO2 gas of a Time
Projection Cahmber (TPC) detector, hence active target TPC (AT-TPC). In this effort our lab already in
2004 developed and constructed one of the first AT-TPC that are now commonly used in nuclear physics
research. This study has been developed further for measurements using of the Warsaw TPC by the US-
Poland-UK-ELINP collaboration at the High Intensity gamma Source (HIgS) of the Triangular Universities
Nuclear Lab (TUNL) at Duke University.

In NS we are testing the new Algebraic Cluster Model (ACM) of Bijker and Iachello as well as the Cluster
Shell Model (CSM) of Del Rocca, Bijker and Iachello. We studied the NS of 12C that lead to the discovery
of the D3h symmetry in 12C predicted by the ACM and the suggestion of a yet to be formulated hole and
particle-hole states of the CSM in 7Be and 8Be.

3 Detector R&D

The LNS houses a Detector Development Lab where an optical readout TPC (O-TPC) was
constructed and delivered to the TUNL in 2006. A mini-TPC, optical avalanche counter (OPAC2)
was constructed in our lab in 2002 and used at the LNS and later at TUNL for measuring detector

3.1 The UConn O-TPC Detector

The PI developed, constructed, tested and calibrated an Optical Readout Time Projection Chamber
(O-TPC) detector at the LNS at UConn during 2004-2006. It was among the first detectors used
as an active target (AT-TPC) that are now commonly used in nuclear physics. The pioneering work
of the design of the O-TPC was carried out during 2000-2004 in collaboration with Professor Amos
Breskin of the Weizmann Institute in Isarel, and Volker Dangendorf at the PTB in Braunschweig,
Germany.

3.2 The UConn OPAC2

A mini TPC (OPAC2) was first constructed at the LNS in 2002 [7] to study oxygen containing
gasses by Dr. Leonid Weissman, a UConn Assistant Professor in Residence. UConn graduate
student, Swindell [8], worked at the TUNL in collaboration with Dr. Calvin Howell, on measuring
fast timing signal of nitrogen gas using the UConn mini-TPC (OPAC2). In Fig. 3 we show the
setup of the LNS OPAC2 detector operating in pure nitrogen that was used at the TUNL, including
the 241Am source, silicon trigger, grids, quartz window and PMT. We measured the prompt light
emission of nitrogen (378 nm), followed by the slow drift signal recorded 2.5 µs later (purple). A
measurement of the fast timing signal (~nsec) from the gas contained in the TPC is essential for
using TPCs in time domain measurements. So far no such measurements could be carried out due
to the slow (~µsec) drift signal. For example it will allow TOF measurements of neutrons by
measuring the prompt light emission of the charged particles emitted in neutron induced reactions.

4. Nuclear Astrophysics

Nuclear Astrophysics and Stellar Evolution have become sufficiently accurate to allow the use of
stars for measurements in fundamental physics. In particular the SSM allow us to study neutrino
properties. Supernova explosions, such 1987A, allow us to study the properties of nuclear matter
and the existence of postulated exotic nuclear matter in the center of neutron stars. We concentrate
in our research on such measurements using stars as laboratory for fundamental physics.

12

The final fate of SN1987A, black hole or neutron star, is of current interest. If indeed a BH was
left by SN1987A, as predicted by Brown et al. [22], it will indicate a soft equation of state of

of the 20 solar masses progenitor Sanduleak −69 202 (Sk -69 202) Blue Supergiant. Recent
observation of blue light from Sanduleak [23], while not definitive may allow us to conclude a
neutron star, hence place a lower limit on the C/O ratio.

5. Nuclear Structure

Light nuclei are well known to exhibit alpha-clustering. In 2002 Bijker and Iachello applied group
theoretical methods used in molecular physics, to develop the Algebraic Cluster Model (ACM) of
light nuclei [30]. Their application of geometrical symmetries led them to predict D3h symmetry
that was observed for the first time in 12C, by the UConn-Birmingham collaboration [31].

5.1 The ACM: The Structure of 12C

The presence of 12C in the CO2 gas presents a formidable background from the photodissociation
of 12C in the 12C(g,3a) reaction. The total energy deposited in the TPC in the photodissociation of
12
 C is only 112 keV different than for the photodissociation of 16O. It cannot be properly separated
using the TPC energy resolution with FWHM ≈ 75 keV. This necessitated the elaborate
algorithms to remove this background that was developed, improved and used by four graduate
students [2,3,4,6] and published in the Phys. Rev. Lett. [32] and Nature [26,28] and submitted to
(Nature) Communications Physics [29].

In the same time it turned out that this background allows us to study the nuclear structure of 12C.
Zimmerman [2] used the data of the 12C(g,3a) reaction to provide unambiguous evidence for the
much sought for second 2+ of 12C [32].

5.2 Conjectured Hole States of the CSM: The Structure of 7,8Be

The PI examined 7Be and 19F in an attempt to consider neutron and proton hole states, respectively,
on the Cluster Shell Model (CSM) Della Rocca, Bijker and Iachello [34]. This led the PI to further
consider particle-hole states in 8Be. Such states, if they exist are predicted to be deform, hence
support a rotational band structure in 8Be. It was proposed by the PI that a few such bands are
already observed [35] as shown in Fig. 11. A search for “the missing states” was conducted using
HIE of the ISOLDE collaboration at CERN employing the d(7Be,p) reaction and ISOLDE
Solenoidal Spectrometer (ISS). [36] This search turned out to be not conclusive due to the required
beam energy beyond 11.0 MeV/u that cannot be provided by the HIE.
