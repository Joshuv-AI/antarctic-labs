The Gibbs phenomenon for the Krawtchouk polynomials
John Cullinan, Elisabeth Young

Abstract
. We study the Fourier approximation ℱ N \mathcal{F}_{N} of the sign function by the Krawtchouk polynomials. We give numerical evidence that the Gibbs phenomenon of the approximation differs from the classical Gibbs constant; this is in contrast to other families of orthogonal polynomials. We also show that the steepness ℱ N ′ ​ ( 0 ) \mathcal{F}_{N}^{\prime}(0) of the approximation is bounded by explicitly proving lim N → ∞ ℱ N ′ ​ ( 0 ) = log ⁡ 4 \lim_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\prime}(0)=\log 4 . This is also in contrast to approximations by classical orthogonal polynomials, where the steepness has been shown to be unbounded as the degree increases.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation

Let
I\subseteq\operatorname{\mathbf{R}}
be a real interval and
\{p_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
a set of continuous functions on
I
that are orthogonal with respect to an inner product
\langle~,~\rangle
. If
f:I\to\operatorname{\mathbf{R}}
is a real-valued function, then the
N
th Fourier approximation
\mathcal{F}_{N}
of
f
is defined pointwise on
I
by

\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\operatorname{Proj}_{p_{n}}(f)\,p_{n}(x),

where
\operatorname{Proj}
denotes orthogonal projection using
\langle~,~\rangle
. The classical Gibbs phenomenon occurs in the approximation of discontinuous functions by trigonometric Fourier series. We immediately consider a special case that is illustrative of this project. Let
a
be a positive real number and let
I=[-a,a]
. Let
f=\mathsf{sgn}:I\to\operatorname{\mathbf{R}}
be the sign function.

Example 1
.

If
a=\pi
and
p_{n}(x)=\sin(nx)
, then

\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)=\frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{1}{2n+1}\sin((2n+1)x).

Example 2
.

If
a=1
and
T_{n}
is the
n
th Chebyshev polynomial, then by
[
4
, Equation (3.10)]
,

\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)=\frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2n-1}T_{2n-1}(x).

Similarly, as shown in
[
4
, Equation (6.14)]
, if
p_{n}
is the
n
th Legendre polynomial then

\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(2-\frac{1}{2n}\right)p_{2n-2}(0)p_{2n-1}(x).

In the first example it is a standard exercise to show that as
N\to\infty
and
x\to 0^{+}
,
\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)
“overshoots” the value 1 by a constant
\gamma
, given explicitly by

\gamma=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,{\rm d}t\approx 1.179.

One can ask whether or not there exists a similar Gibbs constant for the series of Example
2
. In fact, the articles
[
1
]
,
[
3
]
, and
[
7
]
show that if we approximate
\mathsf{sgn}
(or a suitable variant if
I=\operatorname{\mathbf{R}}
) by the Chebyshev, Gegenbauer, Hermite, Jacobi, Laguerre, or Legendre polynomials, then there is a Gibbs constant associated to each of these series and it is exactly
\gamma
. In each of the cases listed above, including the trigonometric case, it is also the case that the
steepness

\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\prime}(0)
of the approximation at 0 is unbounded as
N\to\infty
(in
[
7
, §3.2]
the author explicitly shows how the steepness varies with the parameter in the one-parameter family of Gegenbauer polynomials).

A common feature of each of the aforementioned Fourier series is that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{N}(x)
can be summed by the Christoffel-Darboux identity
[
11
, Thm. 3.2.2]
. For example if
H_{n}(x)
denotes the
n
th Hermite polynomial (defined on the real line
\operatorname{\mathbf{R}}
), then

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2N+1}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(\frac{1}{2n+1}\right)\,\frac{H_{2n}(0)H_{2n+1}(x)}{\|H_{2n}\|^{2}}.

Using the derivative rule
H_{n}^{\prime}(x)=2nH_{n-1}(x)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2N+1}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{H_{2n}(0)H_{2n}(x)}{\|H_{2n}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{N}}{2^{2N}N!\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{H_{2N+1}(x)}{x},

where we have simplified the sum using the Christoffel-Darboux formula along with the known values of
H_{2n}(0)
; see
[
1
, §3]
for a full derivation. This last expression is useful from two points of view. First, using known estimates on the zeroes of the Hermite polynomials
[
11
, (6.31.20), (6.31.23)]
, one can approximate the smallest critical point
\theta_{N}
of
H_{2N+1}(x)
as

\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2N+1}}<\theta_{N}<\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2N+1}}\underbrace{\left\{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left[1-\left(\frac{2\pi}{2N+1}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}\right\}^{-1/2}}_{\to 1^{+}\text{ as }N\to\infty}.

Second, by the fundamental theorem of calculus, we can write

\mathcal{F}_{N}(\theta_{N})=\frac{(-1)^{N}}{2^{2N}N!\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\theta_{N}}\frac{H_{2N+1}(x)}{x}\,{\rm d}x.

It can then be shown, using standard approximations to the Hermite polynomials, that as
N\to\infty
this value approaches
\gamma
.

This raises the question of whether this Gibbs constant is “universal” in all continuous approximations of
\mathsf{sgn}
. In this paper we show that the Krawtchouk polynomials exhibit a Gibbs phenomenon, but the actual constant reflecting the overshoot is different. We also show that the steepness of the approximation is bounded as
N\to\infty
.

1.2.
Krawtchouk Polynomials

Fix a positive integer
N
and let
p
and
q
be positive real numbers with
p+q=1
. On the interval
[0,N]
define the set of
Krawtchouk polynomials

\{K_{n}^{(p)}\}_{n=0}^{N}
by

K_{n}^{(p)}(x;N)=\sum_{v=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-v}\binom{N-x}{n-v}\binom{x}{v}p^{n-v}q^{v}.

Define the weighting function
w_{N}(x)=\binom{N}{x}p^{x}q^{N-x}
. Then the
K_{n}^{(p)}(x;N)
are orthogonal on
[0,N]
with respect to the discrete inner-product

\langle f,g\rangle=\sum_{y=0}^{N}f(y)g(y)w_{N}(y).

We now immediately specialize to case that we will study in this paper.

From now on we take
N
to be even and set
p=1/2
; see Section
2
for the case of
p\neq 1/2
. We consider the shifted polynomials

(3)

\displaystyle k_{n}(x;N):=K_{n}^{(1/2)}(x+N/2;N)=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{v=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-v}\binom{N/2-x}{n-v}\binom{N/2+x}{v}

on the interval
I_{N}=[-N/2,N/2]
with respect to the shifted inner product. We study the Fourier expansion of
\mathsf{sgn}
by the
k_{n}(x;N)
.

A common feature of the classical orthogonal polynomials is that they satisfy a Sturm-Liouville differential equation (see
[
11
, §8.61]
), whereas the Krawtchouk polynomials – which are orthogonal on a discrete set – do not. It is precisely this differential equation which allows one to write the Fourier approximation in a form whose derivative can be summed by the Christoffel-Darboux formula. The discrete nature of the Krawtchouk polynomials suggests that our methods will be based on combinatorics, not calculus. Indeed, we will derive, by way of the Super Catalan Numbers, that

\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\frac{k_{n}(0;N)k_{n+1}(x;N)}{\|k_{n}(-;N)\|^{2}},

which is is very similar to the form of the approximation for the classical polynomials. See Section
5
for further discussion of Fourier expansions in other bases.

1.3.
Main Result

Recall that the steepness of the Fourier approximation to
\mathsf{sgn}
is
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\prime}(0)
and that for the classical orthogonal polynomials, the steepness is unbounded as
N\to\infty
. Our main result is that this is not the case for the Krawtchouk polynomials.

Theorem 4
.

With all notation as above, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\prime}(0)=\log 4.

while
\log 4\approx 1.3862
.

2.
Polynomial Interpolation and Independence of
p

The function
\mathsf{sgn}:[-N/2,N/2]\to\operatorname{\mathbf{R}}
equips us with a set
\mathcal{P}_{N}
of points in the plane:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{N}

\displaystyle=\{(-N/2,-1),\dots,(-1,-1),(0,0),(1,1),\dots,(N/2,1)\}

(5)

\displaystyle=\{(i-N/2,\mathsf{sgn}(i-N/2))\}_{i=0}^{N}.

Fix
p\in(0,1)
. The goal of this section is to prove that
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(p)}(x)
is independent of
p
. We will do this by showing that
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(p)}(x)
is the (unique) interpolating polynomial of degree
N-1
on the
N+1
points of
\mathcal{P}_{N}
. Hence our convention to restrict our study to
p=1/2
.

Proposition 6
.

With all notation as above,
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(p)}(x)
is a polynomial of degree
N-1
that interpolates the points of
\mathcal{P}_{N}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathsf{sgn}
is an odd function, the Fourier coefficients of the even-degree
k_{n}^{(p)}(x)
are all zero. Since
N
is even, it follows that the maximal degree Krawtchouk polynomials appearing in
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(p)}(x)
is
N-1
. Therefore, the degree of
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(p)}(x)
is
N-1
. Since
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(p)}(x)
is an odd function we are reduced to showing that
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(p)}(m)=1
for
m=1,\dots,N/2
.

Fix
m\in\{1,\dots,N/2\}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(p)}(m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{\langle\mathsf{sgn},k^{(p)}_{n}(-;N)\rangle}{\|k^{(p)}_{n}(-;N)\|^{2}}k^{(p)}_{n}(m;N)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(\sum_{y=-N/2}^{N/2}\mathsf{sgn}(y)k_{n}^{(p)}(y;N)w_{N}(y+N/2)\right)\frac{k_{n}^{(p)}(m;N)}{\|k^{(p)}_{n}(-;N)\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y=-N/2}^{N/2}\mathsf{sgn}(y)w_{N}(y+N/2)\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{k_{n}^{(p)}(y;N)k_{n}^{(p)}(m;N)}{\|k^{(p)}_{n}(-;N)\|^{2}}.

Write

K_{N}(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{k_{n}^{(p)}(y;N)k_{n}^{(p)}(x;N)}{\|k^{(p)}_{n}(-;N)\|^{2}}.

Then
K_{N}(x,y)
is the Christoffel-Darboux kernel of the (shifted) Krawtchouck polynomials. By
[
11
, Thm. 3.1.4]
, we have

K_{N}(x,y)w_{N}(y+N/2)=\delta_{xy}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(p)}(m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y=-N/2}^{N/2}\mathsf{sgn}(y)w_{N}(y+N/2)K_{N}(m,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y=-N/2}^{N/2}\mathsf{sgn}(y)\delta_{my}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{sgn}(m)=1.

∎

As discussed above, Proposition
6
immediately implies that
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(p)}(x)
is independent of
p
because
\deg\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(p)}<|\mathcal{P}_{N}|
. We will therefore set
p=1/2
for the remainder of the paper and suppress the superscript “
(1/2)
” notation. To conclude this section we give an alternate form of
\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)
. While this form is simpler to state, it is less useful for the purposes of Fourier analysis. However, it will further highlight the deep connections to combinatorics exhibited by the Krawtchouk polynomials.

Let
I_{N}(x)
be the Lagrange form of the interpolating polynomial of the points of
\mathcal{P}_{N}
. Then

I_{N}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\mathsf{sgn}(i-N/2)\ell_{i}(x),

where

\ell_{i}(x)=\prod_{j=0,j\neq i}^{N}\frac{x-(j-N/2)}{i-j}.

Proposition 7
.

With all notation as above, we have

I_{N}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\mathsf{sgn}(i-N/2)\binom{N/2+x}{i}\binom{N/2-x}{N-i}.

Proof.

Since
I_{N}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\mathsf{sgn}(N/2-i)\ell_{i}(x)
, it remains to determine
\ell_{i}(x)
. From the definition:

\displaystyle\ell_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=0,j\neq i}^{N}\frac{x-(j-N/2)}{i-j}

\displaystyle=\frac{(x+N/2)(x+N/2-1)\cdots(x+N/2-i+1)(x+N/2-i-1)\cdots(x-N/2)}{i!(N-i)!(-1)^{N-i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{N/2+x}{i}i!\binom{N/2-x}{N-i}(N-i)!(-1)^{N-i}}{i!(N-i)!(-1)^{N-i}}

\displaystyle=\binom{N/2+x}{i}\binom{N/2-x}{N-i}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 8
.

Numerical experimentation suggests that the leading coefficient of
{I}_{N}(x)
(and, therefore,
\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)
) is given by

\frac{(-1)^{(N-2)/2}}{(N-1)!}C\left(\frac{N-2}{2}\right),

where
C(n)
is the
n
th Catalan number. See Appendix
A
for further connections to the Catalan numbers.

3.
The Approximation

Recall from the Introduction that the polynomials
\{k_{n}(x;N)\}_{n=0}^{N}
are orthogonal on
[-N/2,N/2]
with respect to the shifted discrete inner-product

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{y=-N/2}^{N/2}f(y)g(y)w_{N}(y+N/2)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{y=-N/2}^{N/2}f(y)g(y)\binom{N}{y+N/2}.

Taking
f=\mathsf{sgn}
, we have

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{\langle\mathsf{sgn}(x),k_{n}(x;N)\rangle}{\|k_{n}(x;N)\|^{2}}k_{n}(x;N).

A direct application of the inner-product shows that

(10)

\displaystyle\|k_{n}(x;N)\|^{2}=4^{-n}\binom{N}{n}.

Since
\mathsf{sgn}
is odd,
w_{N}
is even, and
k_{n}
is an odd/even function in odd/even degree, it follows that

(11)

\displaystyle\langle\mathsf{sgn}(x),k_{n}(x;N)\rangle=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{2^{N}}\sum_{y=1}^{N/2}k_{n}(y;N)\binom{N}{N/2+y}&\text{ if $n$ is odd, and}\\
0&\text{ if $n$ is even.}\end{cases}

The remainder of this section is dedicated to determining an explicit expression for
\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)
that mirrors the Fourier approximations of the sign function by other families of orthogonal polynomials.

Proposition 12
.

With all notation as above, we have

\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)=\frac{1}{2^{N-1}}\binom{N}{N/2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\frac{k_{n}(0;N)k_{n+1}(x;N)}{\|k_{n}(-;N)\|^{2}}.

Proof.

Applying Equation (
11
) to Equation (
9
) allows us to write

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)=\frac{1}{2^{N-1}}\sum_{n=0\atop n\text{ odd}}^{N}\frac{1}{\|k_{n}(-;N)\|^{2}}\sum_{y=1}^{N/2}k_{n}(y;N)\binom{N}{N/2+y}k_{n}(x;N).

Now consider the product
k_{n}(y;N)\binom{N}{N/2+y}
:

(14)

\displaystyle k_{n}(y;N)\binom{N}{N/2+y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{v=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-v}\binom{N/2-y}{n-v}\binom{N/2+y}{v}\binom{N}{N/2+y}.

Elementary manipulation of binomial coefficients allows us to write:

\displaystyle\binom{N/2+y}{v}\binom{N}{N/2+y}

\displaystyle=\binom{N}{v}\binom{N-v}{N/2-y},

\displaystyle\binom{N/2-y}{n-v}\binom{N-v}{N/2-y}

\displaystyle=\binom{N-v}{n-v}\binom{N-n}{N/2+y-v},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\binom{N-v}{n-v}\binom{N}{v}

\displaystyle=\binom{N}{n}\binom{n}{v}.

Altogether, this implies

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{N-1}}\sum_{n=0\atop n\text{ odd}}^{N}\frac{\binom{N}{n}}{(-2)^{n}\|k_{n}(-;N)\|^{2}}\sum_{y=1}^{N/2}\sum_{v=0}^{n}(-1)^{v}\binom{n}{v}\binom{N-n}{N/2+y-v}k_{n}(x;N),

which simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{N-1}}\sum_{n=0\atop n\text{ odd}}^{N}(-2)^{n}\sum_{y=1}^{N/2}\sum_{v=0}^{n}(-1)^{v}\binom{n}{v}\binom{N-n}{N/2+y-v}k_{n}(x;N),

using Equation (
10
). Interchanging the sums and reindexing, we rewrite
\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{N-1}}\sum_{n=0\atop n\text{ odd}}^{N}(-2)^{n}\left(\sum_{v=0}^{n}(-1)^{v}\binom{n}{v}\sum_{s=N/2+1-v}^{N-v}\binom{N-n}{s}\right)k_{n}(x;N).

Recall that the Super Catalan numbers
S(n,m)
are given by

S(n,m)=\frac{(2n)!(2m)!}{n!m!(m+n)!},

and define
T(n,m)=\frac{2n+1}{m+n+1}S(n,m)
. We point the reader to Appendix
A
where we prove as part of a more general result that

(15)

\displaystyle\sum_{v=0}^{n}\sum_{s=N/2+1-v}^{N-v}(-1)^{v}\binom{n}{v}\binom{N-n}{s}=(-1)^{\frac{n+1}{2}}T\left(\frac{N-n-1}{2},\frac{n-1}{2}\right).

Applying Equation (
15
) to our expression for
\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)
yields

\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)=\frac{1}{2^{N-1}}\sum_{n=0\atop n\text{ odd}}^{N}(-2)^{n}(-1)^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\frac{(N-n)!(n-1)!}{\left(\frac{N-n-1}{2}\right)!\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)!}k_{n}(x;N).

Finally, using the fact that

k_{m}(0;N)=\begin{cases}\binom{N/2}{m/2}\frac{(-1)^{m/2}}{2^{m}}&\text{ if $m$ is even, and}\\
0&\text{ if $m$ is odd},\end{cases}

together with Equation
10
, allows us to reindex and simplify
\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)
as

\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)=\frac{1}{2^{N-1}}\binom{N}{N/2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\frac{k_{n}(0;N)k_{n+1}(x;N)}{\|k_{n}(-;N)\|^{2}},

as claimed.
∎

4.
Steepness

We now prove the main result of the paper, that the steepness of
\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)
approaches
\log 4
as
N\to\infty
. As described in the Introduction, this is different from the classical orthogonal polynomials and trigonometric functions.

Theorem 16
.

The steepness of
\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)
approaches
\log(4)
as
N\to\infty
.

We start by deriving an expression for
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\prime}(0)
. By the linearity of the derivative, this will follow immediately once we have an expression for
k_{m}^{\prime}(0;N)
, for positive integers
m
and
N
with
N
even.

Lemma 17
.

Let
m,N\geq 1
with
N
even and
m\leq N
. Then

k_{m}^{\prime}(0;N)=\frac{1}{2^{m-1}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{(m-1)/2}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}\binom{N/2}{\ell}}{(m-2\ell)}

if
m
is odd, and 0 if
m
is even.

Proof.

Since
k_{m}^{\prime}(0;N)
is the linear coefficient of
k_{m}(x;N)
, we require an expression for the linear term of Equation (
3
). The generating function of
2^{m}k_{m}(x;N)
is

G(t;N)=(1-t)^{A}(1+t)^{B},

where
A=N/2-x
and
B=N/2+x

[
11
, (2.82.4)]
. Then we may write

\displaystyle G(t;N)

\displaystyle=(1-t^{2})^{N/2}\left(\frac{1+t}{1-t}\right)^{x}

\displaystyle=(1-t^{2})^{N/2}\exp\left(x\log\left(\frac{1+t}{1-t}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=(1-t^{2})^{N/2}\left(1+x\log\left(\frac{1+t}{1-t}\right)+O(x^{2})\right).

Therefore, the linear coefficient of
k_{m}(x;N)
is the coefficient of
t^{m}
in
(1-t^{2})\log\left(\frac{1+t}{1-t}\right)
, scaled by
2^{-m}
. We multiply the power series representations

(1-t^{2})\log\left(\frac{1+t}{1-t}\right)=\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}\binom{N/2}{\ell}t^{2\ell}\right)\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{2\ell+1}}{2\ell+1}\right),

and extract the coefficient of
t^{m}
by convolution; we find it to be

2\sum_{\ell=0}^{(m-1)/2}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}\binom{N/2}{\ell}}{(m-2\ell)},

if
m
is odd and
0
is
m
is even. Scaling by
2^{-m}
completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 18
.

Let
c(n;N)=(-1)^{n/2}\binom{N/2}{n/2}/\binom{N}{n}
if
n
is even and 0 if
n
is odd. Then the steepness of
\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)
is given by

\frac{1}{2^{N-1}}\binom{N}{N/2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}c(n;N)\sum_{\ell=0}^{n/2}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}\binom{N/2}{\ell}}{(n+1-2\ell)}.

Proof.

Recall that

(19)

\displaystyle k_{n}(0;N)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{(-1)^{n/2}}{2^{n}}\binom{N/2}{n/2}&\text{ if $n$ is even}\\
0&\text{ if $n$ is odd, and}\end{cases}

(20)

\displaystyle\|k_{n}(-;N)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4^{n}}\binom{N}{n}.

By Proposition
12
, we have

\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)=\frac{1}{2^{N-1}}\binom{N}{N/2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\frac{k_{n}(0;N)k_{n+1}(x;N)}{\|k_{n}(-;N)\|^{2}},

hence

\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\prime}(0)=\frac{1}{2^{N-1}}\binom{N}{N/2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\frac{k_{n}(0;N)k_{n+1}^{\prime}(0;N)}{\|k_{n}(-;N)\|^{2}}.

Theorem 24
.

For
M\geq 1

and

Before beginning the proof we set some convenient notation. For integers
m
and
M
with
0\leq m\leq M-1
, we define

(25)

\displaystyle C(m,M)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\frac{\binom{M}{m}}{\binom{2M}{2m}}

(26)

\displaystyle D(m,M)

\displaystyle=C(m,M)\left(\frac{2M-2m+1}{2M+2}\right).

By applying well known properties of binomial coefficients, one verifies that

(27)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2M+1}{2M+2}\right)C(m,M+1)

\displaystyle=D(m,M),\text{ and}

(28)

\displaystyle D(m,M)-D(m+1,M)

\displaystyle=C(m,M).

We also define

(29)

\displaystyle X(m,M)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}\binom{M}{\ell}}{2m+1-2\ell}

and verify by Pascal’s identity
\left(\binom{a}{b}=\binom{a-1}{b-1}+\binom{a-1}{b}\right)
that

(30)

\displaystyle X(m,M+1)=X(m,M)-X(m-1,M).

We now prove an auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 31
.

With all notation as above, we have

X(M,M+1)=(-1)^{M+1}\left(1-\frac{2^{2M+1}}{\binom{2M+1}{M+1}}\right).

Proof.

We have

X(M,M+1)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{M}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}\binom{M+1}{\ell}}{2M+1-2\ell}.

Write

\frac{1}{2M+1-2\ell}=\int_{0}^{1}x^{2M-2\ell}\,{\rm d}x,

so that

\sum_{\ell=0}^{M}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}\binom{M+1}{\ell}}{2M+1-2\ell}=\int_{0}^{1}x^{2M}\sum_{\ell=0}^{M}\binom{M+1}{\ell}\left(-x^{-2}\right)^{\ell}\,{\rm d}x.

By the Binomial Theorem

\sum_{\ell=0}^{M+1}\binom{M+1}{\ell}\left(-x^{-2}\right)^{\ell}=(1-x^{-2})^{M+1},

hence

\sum_{\ell=0}^{M}\binom{M+1}{\ell}\left(-x^{-2}\right)^{\ell}=(1-x^{-2})^{M+1}-(-x^{-2})^{M+1}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{M}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}\binom{M+1}{\ell}}{2M+1-2\ell}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}x^{2M}\left((1-x^{-2})^{M+1}-(-x^{-2})^{M+1}\right)\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=(-1)^{M+1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-x^{2})^{M+1}-1}{x^{2}}\,{\rm d}x.

Integration-by-parts gives

(-1)^{M+1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-x^{2})^{M+1}-1}{x^{2}}\,{\rm d}x=(-1)^{M+1}\left(1-2(M+1)\int_{0}^{1}(1-x^{2})^{M}\,{\rm d}x\right).

The latter is a Wallis integral with value

\int_{0}^{1}(1-x^{2})^{M}\,{\rm d}x=\frac{2^{2M}}{(2M+1)\binom{2M}{M}}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{M}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}\binom{M+1}{\ell}}{2M+1-2\ell}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{M+1}\left(1-2(M+1)\frac{2^{2M}}{(2M+1)\binom{2M}{M}}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{M+1}\left(1-\frac{2^{2M+1}}{\binom{2M+1}{M+1}}\right),

as claimed.
∎

With this notation in place we may write
S(M)
more succinctly as

(32)

\displaystyle S(M)=\frac{1}{2^{2M-1}}\binom{2M}{M}\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}C(m,M)X(m,M).

Applying Equation (

\displaystyle S(M+1)=\frac{1}{2^{2M-1}}\binom{2M}{M}\sum_{m=0}^{M}D(m,M)X(m,M+1).

Next, write

Applying Lemma

5.
The Gibbs Phenomenon and Connection to Classical Families

Classical orthogonal polynomials typically satisfy a derivative rule (inherited from the Sturm-Liouville differential equation) wherein the derivative of a degree
n
member is related to degree
n-1
member. For example, the Jacobi, Generalized Laguerre, and Hermite polynomials satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha+\beta+n+1}{2}P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1)}(x),

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)

\displaystyle=-L_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1)}(x),\text{ and}

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}H_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=2nH_{n-1}(x),

respectively. The Gegenbauer and Legendre polynomials, both of which are subfamilies of the Jacobi polynomials, satisfy similar relations. It is precisely these derivative formulas that allow one to express
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\prime}(x)
as a Christoffel-Darboux sum (see Section
1
for the Hermite,
[
7
, Prop. 1]
for the Gegenbauer, and
[
1
, §4]
for the Generalized Laguerre). This allows one to express the critical points of
\mathcal{F}_{N}(x)
as the zeroes of a single classical orthogonal polynomial, which have been extensively studied for their asymptotic properties. By contrast, the Krawtchouck polynomials satisfy the difference equation

k_{n}(x+1;N)-k_{n}(x;N)=k_{n-1}(x+1/2;N-1).

Since
k^{\prime}_{n+1}(x;N)
is not a constant multiple of
k_{n}(x;N)
, it is not possible to directly apply the Christoffel-Darboux formula to

\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{2^{N-1}}\binom{N}{N/2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\frac{k_{n}(0;N)k_{n+1}^{\prime}(x;N)}{\|k_{n}(-;N)\|^{2}}.

In conclusion we mention a deep relationship between the Krawtchouk and Hermite polynomials, known to Krawtchouk
[
8
]
and explained in
[
9
]
. With all notation as in Section
1
, it is shown in
[
9
, Equation (4)]
that for fixed
n
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\frac{2}{Npq}\right)^{n/2}n!K_{n}^{(p)}(\sqrt{2Npq}x+Np;N)=H_{n}(x),

the
n
th Hermite polynomial. However, the approximations for fixed
N
only hold for
n=O(N^{1/3})
and
x=O(n^{1/2})
. Therefore, it is not feasible to use the known Gibbs constant for the Hermite polynomials to deduce an exact value for the Krawtchouk.

Recall from
[
5

that the
Super Catalan Numbers

S(n,m)
are defined as

S(p,q)=\frac{(2p)!(2q)!}{p!q!(p+q)!}.

We further define

T(p,q)=\frac{(2p+1)!(2q)!}{p!q!(p+q+1)!}=\frac{2p+1}{p+q+1}S(p,q).

We use this appendix to prove a lemma involving the Super Catalan Numbers that would have detracted from the main flow of the proof of Proposition
12

Lemma 40
.

Let
N
be even and
n
be odd with
n\leq N
. Then

\sum_{v=0}^{n}\sum_{s=N/2+1-v}^{N-v}(-1)^{v}\binom{n}{v}\binom{N-n}{s}=(-1)^{\frac{n+1}{2}}T\left(\frac{N-n-1}{2},\frac{n-1}{2}\right).

In order to prove Lemma
40
we first prove the following identity for
T(p,q)
.

Proposition 41
.

With all notation as above, we have

(-1)^{q+1}T(p,q)=\sum_{v=0}^{2q+1}\sum_{s=p+q+2-v}^{2p+2q+2-v}(-1)^{v}\binom{2q+1}{v}\binom{2p+1}{s}.

Proof.

Consider the sum

(42)

\displaystyle\sum_{v=0}^{2q+1}\sum_{s=p+q+2-v}^{2p+2q+2-v}(-1)^{v}\binom{2q+1}{v}\binom{2p+1}{s}

and set
n=2q+1
,
M=2p+1
,
K=p+q+2
, and
N=2p+2q+2
. Then the sum in (
42
) can be written as

(43)

\displaystyle\sum_{v=0}^{n}(-1)^{v}\binom{n}{v}\sum_{s=K-v}^{N-v}\binom{M}{s}.

Let
f(v)=\sum_{s=K-v}^{N-v}\binom{M}{s}
so that

(44)

\displaystyle\sum_{v=0}^{n}(-1)^{v}\binom{n}{v}\sum_{s=K-v}^{N-v}\binom{M}{s}=\sum_{v=0}^{n}(-1)^{v}\binom{n}{v}f(v).

Recall that if
h:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbf{Z}
is a function, then the
forward difference

\Delta f:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbf{Z}
is defined as

\Delta h(z)=h(z+1)-h(z).

Iterating (using the linearity of
\Delta
), it is straightforward to check that

\Delta^{(n)}f(0)=\sum_{v=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-v}\binom{n}{v}f(v),

whence

(45)

\displaystyle\sum_{v=0}^{n}(-1)^{v}\binom{n}{v}f(v)=(-1)^{n}\Delta^{(n)}f(0).

We compute directly that

\Delta f(v)=\binom{M}{k-v-1}-\binom{M}{N-v}.

Then

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}\Delta^{(n)}f(0)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\Delta^{(n-1)}\Delta f(0)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\sum_{v=0}^{n-1-v}\binom{n-1}{v}\Delta f(v)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\sum_{v=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{n-1-v}\binom{n-1}{v}\left[\binom{M}{K-v-1}-\binom{M}{N-v}\right].

Substitute back in for
n
,
M
,
K
, and
N
:

\displaystyle(-1)^{2q+1}\sum_{v=0}^{2q}(-1)^{2q-v}\binom{2q}{v}\left[\binom{2p+1}{p+q+1-v}-\binom{2p+1}{2p+2q+2-v}\right].

Simplifying, and noting that
\binom{2p+1}{2p+2q+2-v}=0
in the range
v\in[0,2q]
, allows us to rewrite the sum in (
42
) as

(46)

\displaystyle-\sum_{v=0}^{2q}(-1)^{v}\binom{2q}{v}\binom{2p+1}{p+q+1-v}.

We have reduced the problem to proving that

(-1)^{q}T(p,q)=\sum_{v=0}^{2q}(-1)^{v}\binom{2q}{v}\binom{2p+1}{p+q+1-v}.

By the work of
[
6
, §3]
, the sum on the right is interpreted as the coefficient of
x^{p+q+1}
in the product
(1-x)^{2q}(1+x)^{2p+1}
, which is shown in
loc. cit.
to be

(-1)^{q}\frac{\binom{2p+1}{p}\binom{2q}{q}}{\binom{p+q+1}{q}},

which is precisely
(-1)^{q}T(p,q)
.
∎

It is now a simple matter to complete the proof of Lemma
