Abstract
. The stability of non-isolated equilibria to quasilinear parabolic problems of the form u ′ = A ⁡ ( u ) ​ u + f ⁡ ( u ) u^{\prime}=A(u)u+f(u) is established in interpolation spaces (and thus extending previous results relying on maximal regularity). The approach allows full flexibility in choosing the interpolation methods and requires only low regularity assumptions on the semilinear part f f . Applications to concrete problems are presented, including the capillarity-driven Hele–Shaw problem and the fractional mean curvature flow.

1.
Introduction and Main Results

In this paper we study stability properties of equilibrium solutions to quasilinear parabolic problems of the form

(1.1)

u^{\prime}=A(u)u+f(u),\quad t>0,\qquad u(0)=u^{0},

assuming that
A(u)
is an unbounded operator on a Banach space
E_{0}
with domain
E_{1}
which generates an analytic semigroup on
E_{0}
.

A well-established and powerful tool for proving
exponential stability or instability of (isolated) stationary solutions is the principle of linearized stability, where
these properties are inferred from the sign of the real parts of the spectral values of the corresponding linearized operator at the equilibrium.
Owing to its significance in applications, such abstract results have been developed in the parabolic setting within a variety of frameworks and by means of different techniques, see
[
12
,
14
,
19
,
26
,
27
,
36
,
41
,
42
,
30
,
29
]
for an incomplete list of related works.

In certain applications, however, the set of equilibrium solutions is not discrete but instead forms a finite-dimensional smooth manifold. In some cases, conserved quantities (flow invariants) single out a unique equilibrium, which can then be identified as the omega-limit set of a given initial value by applying the principle of linearized stability to an appropriate restriction of the problem.

A standard—though technically involved—approach to analyzing the stability of stationary solutions in situations where flow invariants cannot be fully exploited is based on center manifold theory.
This theory is well established in the analysis of nonlinear parabolic evolution problems, see, e.g.,
[
6
,
27
,
21
,
12
,
4
,
24
,
23
,
34
,
44
,
10
]
.

More recently, working within continuous, Hölder, or
L_{p}
-maximal regularity frameworks, the principle of linearized stability has been substantially extended in
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
.
These works treat the situation in which the set of stationary solutions forms, in a neighborhood of an equilibrium
u_{*}
,
a finite-dimensional
{\rm C}^{1}
-manifold whose dimension coincides with that of the kernel of the linearized operator at
u_{*}
.
The resulting generalized principle of linearized stability yields stability of
u_{*}
and exponential convergence of solutions starting sufficiently close to
u_{*}

toward a (possibly different) stationary solution near
u_{*}
, provided that
0
is a semi-simple eigenvalue of the linearized operator and that the remainder of the spectrum lies in the open left half-plane.
This generalized principle is particularly valuable in applications, as it provides sharp convergence results under minimal regularity and spectral assumptions,
and has been successfully applied to a wide range of nonlinear models, including quasilinear parabolic equations, free-boundary problems, fluid dynamics, phase transition models, and geometric evolution equations.

In this research we establish a similar result as in
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
without assuming a maximal property.
Instead, we work in general interpolation spaces with full flexibility with respect to the choice of interpolation methods.
A key ingredient of our analysis is the concept of evolution operators, which is used to reformulate the evolution problem (
1.1
) as a fixed-point problem for
u
in suitable interpolation spaces between
E_{0}
and
E_{1}
.
In particular, our first main result, see Theorem
1.3
below, provides linearized stability of equilibria for quasilinear problems within the well-established parabolic theory of
[
1
]
. We thus also extend the results of

[
7
]
, where the semilinear case was considered (see Remark
1.4
).
In addition, we extend this theory in Theorem
1.6
to the case of inclusions

\mathrm{dom}(f)\subsetneq\mathrm{dom}(A)
for the domains of the function
u\mapsto f(u)
and the quasilinear term
u\mapsto A(u)
for which the well-posedness and stability theory developed in
[
28
,
29
]
is formulated in phase spaces lying between
\mathrm{dom}(f)
and
\mathrm{dom}(A)
.
Hence, this lowers the regularity assumption on
f
and includes a limiting critical case which, in some applications, is a function space that is scaling invariant for the underlying evolution equation.

The relevance of our abstract results for applications is illustrated in Example
4.1
-Example
4.3
in the context of the Hele-Shaw problem with surface tension, the fractional mean curvature flow (for periodic graphs), and a quasilinear evolution problem in critical scaling-invariant spaces.

Throughout this paper we assume that
E_{0}
and
E_{1}
are Banach spaces over
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
with continuous and dense embedding
E_{1}\hookrightarrow E_{0}
. For each
\theta\in(0,1)
we fix an arbitrary admissible interpolation functor
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\theta}
of
exponent
\theta
(see
[
2
, I. Section 2.11]
) and define
{E_{\theta}:=(E_{0},E_{1})_{\theta}}
as the corresponding interpolation space with norm
\|\cdot\|_{\theta}
.

Linearized Stability of Non-Isolated Equilibria

We begin with the case of equal domains
\mathrm{dom}(f)=\mathrm{dom}(A)
. To this end, we fix exponents

(1.2a)

0<\gamma\leq\beta<\alpha<1

and assume, for some open subset
O_{\beta}
of
E_{\beta}
, that

(1.2b)

(A,f)\in{\rm C}^{1}\big(O_{\beta},\mathcal{H}(E_{1},E_{0})\times E_{\gamma}\big),

where
\mathcal{H}(E_{1},E_{0})
denotes the open subset of
\mathcal{L}(E_{1},E_{0})
consisting of generators of strongly continuous analytic semigroups on
E_{0}
.

We first recall from
[
1
]
(see also
[
30
, Theorem 1.1]
) that the quasilinear Cauchy problem (
1.1
) is locally well-posed in
O_{\alpha}
, where

O_{\theta}:=O_{\beta}\cap E_{\theta}
for
\theta\in(\beta,1]
:

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume (
1.2
). Then, for each
u^{0}\in O_{\alpha}
, the Cauchy problem (
1.1
) possesses a unique maximal classical solution

(1.3)

u=u(\cdot;u^{0})\in{\rm C}^{1}\big((0,t^{+}),E_{0}\big)\cap{\rm C}\big((0,t^{+}),E_{1}\big)\cap{\rm C}^{\alpha-\theta}\big([0,t^{+}),E_{\theta}\big)

for every
0\leq\theta\leq\alpha
, where
t^{+}=t^{+}(u^{0})\in(0,\infty]
is the maximal existence time.
Moreover, if the orbit
u([0,t^{+}(u^{0}));u^{0})
is relatively compact in
O_{\alpha}
, then
t^{+}(u^{0})=\infty
.

We build upon this well-posedness result to investigate stability properties of equilibria that form a finite-dimensional manifold. Our approach is inspired by
[
38
]
and follows the strategy developed therein.
To state the precise result, we need some preparation and notation.

Denote the set of equilibria of (
1.1
) by
\mathcal{E}
, that is,

\mathcal{E}:=\{u\in O_{1}\,:\,A(u)u+f(u)=0\}.

We study the asymptotic behavior of solutions
u(\cdot;u^{0})
with initial data
u^{0}\in O_{\alpha}

in a neighborhood of a fixed equilibrium
u_{*}\in\mathcal{E}
on which we impose suitable assumptions specified below.
We assume that there exist a neighborhood
U
of
0
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, a function
{\Psi\in\mathrm{C}^{2}(U,E_{1})}
, and a constant
r>0
such that

(1.4)

\Psi(U)=\mathcal{E}\cap\mathbb{B}_{E_{1}}(u_{*},r),\qquad\Psi(0)=u_{*},\qquad\mathrm{rank}(\partial\Psi(0))=m.

Assumption (
1.4
) guarantees that, in a neighborhood of
u_{*}
, the set of equilibria
\mathcal{E}
forms an
m
-dimensional
\mathrm{C}^{2}
-manifold.

Introducing
v:=u-u_{*}
, we linearize problem (
1.1
) at
u_{*}
to obtain the form

(1.5)

v^{\prime}=A_{*}(v)v+g(v),\quad t>0,\qquad v(0)=u^{0}-u_{*},

where, given
v\in O_{\beta}^{*}:=O_{\beta}-u_{*}
, we defined

A_{*}(v)w:=A(u_{*}+v)w+(\partial A(u_{*})[w])u_{*}+\partial f(u_{*})w,\qquad w\in E_{1},

and

g(v):=f(u_{*}+v)-f(u_{*})-\partial f(u_{*})v+\big(A(u_{*}+v)-A(u_{*})-(\partial A(u_{*})v)\big)u_{*}.

It follows from (
1.2b
) that

(A_{*},g)\in{\rm C}^{1}\big(O_{\beta}^{*},\mathcal{L}(E_{1},E_{0})\times E_{0}\big)

with

(1.6)

g(0)=0,\qquad\partial g(0)=0.

Moreover, setting
\psi(\xi):=\Psi(\xi)-u_{*}
for
\xi\in U
, the first condition of (
1.4
) entails that

(1.7)

A_{*}(\psi(\xi))\psi(\xi)+g(\psi(\xi))=0,\qquad\xi\in U.

Differentiating this identity at
\xi=0
and using that
\psi(0)=0
and
\partial g(0)=0
readily implies that
A_{*}(0)\partial\psi(0)=0
.
That is,
T_{u_{*}}\mathcal{E}\subset\mathrm{ker}(A_{*}(0))
, with
T_{u_{*}}\mathcal{E}=\mathrm{rg}(\partial\Psi(0))
denoting the tangent space of
\mathcal{E}
at
u_{*}
, and

A_{*}(0)=A(u_{*})+(\partial A(u_{*})[\cdot])u_{*}+\partial f(u_{*}).

In fact, we shall further assume that

(1.8a)

T_{u_{*}}\mathcal{E}=\mathrm{ker}(A_{*}(0)),

that
0
is a semi-simple eigenvalue of
A_{*}(0)
, i.e.,

(1.8b)

\text{$\mathrm{rg}(A_{*}(0))$ is closed and}\ E_{0}=\mathrm{ker}(A_{*}(0))\oplus\mathrm{rg}(A_{*}(0)),

and that

(1.8c)

\sigma(A_{*}(0))\setminus\{0\}\subset[\mathrm{Re}\,z<0].

In particular,
0
is an isolated spectral point of
\sigma(A_{*}(0))
which yields the disjoint union

\sigma(A_{*}(0))=\{0\}\cup\sigma_{s}(A_{*}(0)),

-\omega_{0}:=\sup\{\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda\,:\,\lambda\in\sigma_{s}(A_{*}(0))\}<0.

We point out that assumptions (
1.4
) and (
1.8
) express the fact that the equilibrium solution
u_{*}
is
normally stable

[
37
,
38
,
39
]
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{L}(E_{0})
be the spectral projection onto
\mathrm{ker}(A_{*}(0))
corresponding to the spectral set
\{0\}
of
A_{*}(0)
, see e.g.
[
27
, Appendix A]
, and denote by

Q:=1-P
the associated projection of
E_{0}
onto
\mathrm{rg}(A_{*}(0))
.
Then
\mathrm{rg}(P)=\mathrm{ker}(A_{*}(0))\subset E_{1}
and
{\mathrm{rg}(Q)=\mathrm{rg}(A_{*}(0))}
.
Moreover, setting
E_{\theta}^{0}:=P(E_{\theta})
and
E_{\theta}^{s}:=Q(E_{\theta})
, we obtain the topological sum decompositions

(1.9a)

E_{\theta}=E_{\theta}^{0}\oplus E_{\theta}^{s},\qquad\theta\in[0,1],

and, since
\mathrm{rg}(P)=\mathrm{ker}(A_{*}(0))=E_{1}^{0}
, we have in fact that

(1.9b)

E_{\theta}^{0}=\mathrm{ker}(A_{*}(0))=E_{1}^{0},\qquad\theta\in[0,1].

The decomposition (
1.9a
) reduces
A_{*}(0)
into

(1.9c)

A_{*}(0)=0\oplus A_{*}^{s}(0)

and, in view of
[
27
, Appendix A]
, the stable part
A_{*}^{s}(0)
satisfies

(1.9d)

A_{*}^{s}(0)\in\mathcal{H}(E_{1}^{s},E_{0}^{s})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma(A_{*}^{s}(0))=\sigma_{s}(A_{*}(0))\subset[\mathrm{Re}\,z\leq-\omega_{0}].

Finally, in order to state our stability result we require a further, technical assumption: There are
\zeta\in[0,1)
and
Banach spaces
F
and
G
such that

(1.10a)

\qquad E_{\zeta}\hookrightarrow G,\qquad P\in\mathcal{L}(F,P(F)),\qquad P(F)\hookrightarrow E_{1}^{0},

and

(1.10b)

\|A(w)-A(u_{*})\|_{\mathcal{L}(G,F)}\leq L\|w-u_{*}\|_{E_{\beta}},\qquad w\in O_{\beta}.

Clearly, we may actually assume without loss of generality that
\alpha<\zeta<1
.

Remark 1.2
.

Assumption (
1.10
) constitutes an essential ingredient of our analysis as it compensates for the lack of maximal regularity properties of the quasilinear part. In fact, it allows us to handle the semilinear term
g
in the reformulation (
1.5
) of (
1.1
), which is only
E_{0}
-valued.
We emphasize, however, that this assumption imposes no significant restriction in many applications and is easily checked, see Example
4.1
-Example
4.3
, and in particular Proposition
4.2
.
In fact, in applications the operator
P\in\mathcal{L}(F,P(F))
is usually an extension of the projection
P\in\mathcal{L}(E_{0})
and
P(F)=E_{1}^{0}
.

We also note that, since
E_{1}^{0}
is finite-dimensional, all norms on
E_{1}^{0}
are equivalent,
and we equip
E_{1}^{0}
with
\|\cdot\|_{1}
.

We shall prove that the normally stable equilibrium
u_{*}
for (
1.1
) is stable.
Moreover, we shall show that for any initial value
u^{0}
sufficiently close to
u_{*}
in
E_{\alpha}

the corresponding maximal solution
u(\cdot;u^{0})
is globally defined and converges exponentially fast to an equilibrium
\hat{u}_{*}\in\mathcal{E}
.
The asymptotic limit
\hat{u}_{*}\in\mathcal{E}
is uniquely determined by the initial value
u^{0}
, although it may vary with
u^{0}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume (
1.2
) and, for the equilibrium solution
u_{*}\in\mathcal{E}
, assume (
1.4
), (
1.8
), and (
1.10
). Then
u_{*}
is stable in
E_{\alpha}
. Moreover, for any given
\omega\in(0,\omega_{0})
there exist constants
\delta>0
and
{K\geq 1}
such that for each
u^{0}\in\mathbb{B}_{E_{\alpha}}(u_{*},\delta)
, the maximal solution
u(\cdot;u^{0})
to (
1.1
) is globally defined, and there exists a unique
\hat{u}_{*}\in\mathcal{E}
such that

(1.11)

\|u(t;u^{0})-\hat{u}_{*}\|_{E_{\alpha}}\leq Ke^{-\omega t}\|u^{0}-u_{*}\|_{E_{\alpha}},\qquad t\geq 0.

The proof of Theorem
1.3
is given in Section
2
, and we refer to Example
4.1
and Example
4.2
for two applications of this result.

Of course, Theorem
1.3
also applies to the semilinear case where
A(u)=A
is independent of
u
. In this case, however, less restrictive assumptions are needed as observed in
[
7
]
:

Remark 1.4
.

(i)
In (
1.4
) we assume, for simplicity, that the set of equilibria forms an
m
-dimensional
{\rm C}^{2}
-manifold in a neighborhood of
u_{*}
.
However, it suffices that this set is a
{\rm C}^{2-}
-manifold, or even a
{\rm C}^{1+\delta}
-manifold for some
\delta\in(0,1)
.
In the semilinear case, a
{\rm C}^{1}
-manifold is actually sufficient (see Footnote
1
).

(ii)
In the semilinear case, condition (
1.2a
) may be replaced by the slightly more general requirement

0\leq\gamma<\alpha<1,

i.e. the choice
\gamma=0
is admissible, see
[
32
, Theorem 1.2]
and the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

(iii)
Assumption (
1.10
) is automatically satisfied in the semilinear case.

Linearized Stability of Non-Isolated Equilibria in Time-Weighed Spaces

In some applications, the semilinear term
u\mapsto f(u)
in (
1.1
) requires higher regularity of
u
than the quasilinear part
u\mapsto A(u)
.
In such situations, Theorem
1.3
no longer provides optimal results.
Using the well-posedness theory developed in
[
32
,
28
]
, we now extend Theorem
1.3
to this setting, following an approach analogous to that in
[
29
, Theorem 1.3]
.

With interpolation spaces
E_{\theta}
,
\theta\in[0,1]
, as defined above, we fix

(1.12a)

0<\gamma<\beta<\xi<1,\qquad q\geq 1,

and impose on the quasilinear part in (
1.1
) the same assumptions as before, namely

(1.12b)

A\in{\rm C}^{1}\big(O_{\beta},\mathcal{H}(E_{1},E_{0})\big).

The main difference to the previous setting concerns the semilinear part satisfying

(1.12c)

f\in{\rm C}^{1}(O_{\xi},E_{\gamma})

and thus possibly requiring more regularity as
\xi>\beta
.
Moreover, we additionally assume for each
R>0
that there exists a constant
C(R)>0
such that

(1.12d)

\displaystyle\|f(u)-f(v)\|_{E_{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\leq C(R)\big[1+\|u\|_{\xi}^{q-1}+\|v\|_{\xi}^{q-1}\big]\big[\big(1+\|u\|_{\xi}+\|v\|_{\xi}\big)\|u-v\|_{\beta}+\|u-v\|_{\xi}\big]

for
u,v\in O_{\xi}\cap\bar{\mathbb{B}}_{E_{\beta}}(0,R)
.
The quantitative estimate (
1.12d
), involving the parameter
q\geq 1
that measures
the growth of
f
with respect to
E_{\xi}
-terms,
leads naturally to the use of time-weighted spaces, see (
3.1
) below, when studying the well-posedness
of (
1.1
). In
[
32
,
28
]
this approach is developed under slightly milder assumptions than stated in (
1.12
),
whereas
[
40
,
42
,
25
,
22
]
address the problem in suitable maximal regularity frameworks.
The advantage of employing time weights for the well-posedness of (
1.1
) lies in the use of phase spaces
E_{\alpha}
with parameters

\alpha\in(\beta,\xi)
for which the semilinearity
f
need not be defined.
To be more precise, we define the critical value
\alpha_{\rm crit}<\xi
by

(1.12e)

\alpha_{\rm crit}:=\frac{q\xi-1-\gamma}{q-1}\quad\text{if }q>1,\qquad\alpha_{\rm crit}:=-\infty\quad\text{if }q=1,

and consider initial data
u^{0}\in O_{\alpha}
with

(1.12f)

\alpha_{\rm crit}\leq\alpha\in(\beta,\xi).

In the critical case
\alpha=\alpha_{\rm crit}\in(\beta,\xi)
,
the well-posedness theory developed in
[
28
]
requires the following additional assumption:
there exists an interpolation functor
\{\cdot,\cdot\}_{\alpha/\xi}

of exponent
\alpha/\xi
, and for each
\eta\in\{\alpha,\beta,\xi\}

there exists an interpolation functor
\{\cdot,\cdot\}_{\gamma/\eta}

of exponent
\gamma/\eta
, such that

(1.13)

E_{\alpha}\doteq\{E_{0},E_{\xi}\}_{\alpha/\xi},\qquad E_{\gamma}\doteq\{E_{0},E_{\eta}\}_{\gamma/\eta},\quad\eta\in\{\alpha,\beta,\xi\}.

Property (
1.13
) is not really restrictive in applications as it is automatically satisfied if, for each
\theta\in\{\gamma,\beta,\alpha,\xi\}
, the interpolation functor
{(\cdot,\cdot)_{\theta}}
is either the complex interpolation functor
[\cdot,\cdot]_{\theta}
, or the continuous interpolation functor
{(\cdot,\cdot)_{\theta,\infty}^{0}}
,
or the real interpolation functor
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\theta}=(\cdot,\cdot)_{\theta,p}
with parameter
p\in[1,\infty]
(see e.g.
[
2
, I.Remarks 2.11.2 (b)]
).

The local well-posedness of (
1.1
) in
O_{\alpha}
under hypotheses (
1.12
) and (
1.13
)
was established in
[
28
,
32
]
and is summarized in
[
29
, Theorem 1.2]
.
We recall this result:

Theorem 1.5
.

Assume (
1.12
)-(
1.13
). Then, for each
u^{0}\in O_{\alpha}
, the Cauchy problem (
1.1
) possesses a unique maximal classical solution
u=u(\cdot;u^{0})
such that

(1.14a)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\in{\rm C}^{1}\big((0,t^{+}),E_{0}\big)\cap{\rm C}\big((0,t^{+}),E_{1}\big)\cap{\rm C}\big([0,t^{+}),O_{\alpha}\big)\cap{\rm C}^{\alpha-\beta}\big([0,t^{+}),E_{\beta}\big)

and

(1.14b)

\lim_{t\to 0}t^{\xi-\alpha}\|u(t)\|_{E_{\xi}}=0,

where
t^{+}=t^{+}(u^{0})\in(0,\infty]
denotes the maximal existence time.

We can now state the stability result for normally stable equilibria within this framework:

Theorem 1.6
.

Assume (
1.12
)-(
1.13
) and, for the equilibrium
{u_{*}\in\mathcal{E}}
, assume (
1.4
), (
1.8
), and (
1.10
).
Then
u_{*}
is stable in
E_{\alpha}
. Moreover, for any given
\omega\in(0,\omega_{0})
there exist constants
\delta>0
and
{K\geq 1}
such that for each
u^{0}\in\mathbb{B}_{E_{\alpha}}(u_{*},\delta)
, the maximal solution
u(\cdot;u^{0})
to (
1.1
) is globally defined, and there exists a unique
\hat{u}_{*}\in\mathcal{E}
such that

(1.15)

\|u(t;u^{0})-\hat{u}_{*}\|_{E_{\alpha}}+t^{\xi-\alpha}\|u(t;u^{0})-\hat{u}_{*}\|_{E_{\xi}}\leq Ke^{-\omega t}\|u^{0}-u_{*}\|_{E_{\alpha}},\qquad t\geq 0.

The proof of this result is given in Section
3
and an application in Example
4.3
.
We note that assumption (
1.13
) is not needed in the non-critical case
\alpha_{\rm crit}<\alpha\in(\beta,\xi)
:

Remark 1.7
.

In the noncritical case
\alpha_{\rm crit}<\alpha\in(\beta,\xi)
one may in fact omit assumption (
1.13
) in Theorem
1.6
.
The claim still holds (with a similar proof) if estimate (
1.15
) is replaced by

\|u(t;u^{0})-\hat{u}_{*}\|_{E_{\alpha}}+t^{\mu}\|u(t;u^{0})-\hat{u}_{*}\|_{E_{\xi}}\leq Me^{-\omega t}\|u^{0}\|_{E_{\alpha}},\qquad t>0,

for some fixed (but arbitrary)
\mu>\xi-\alpha
.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

In this section we provide the proof of Theorem
1.3
.
We use the notation introduced earlier and adapt the proof of
[
38
, Theorem 2.1]
to our setting.
Moreover, we use the fact that

A\in\mathcal{H}(F_{1},F_{0})
for some densely embedded Banach spaces
F_{1}\hookrightarrow F_{0}
if and only if there exist constants
\kappa\geq 1
and
\omega>0
such that
A\in\mathcal{H}(F_{1},F_{0};\kappa,\omega)
, meaning that

\omega-A\in\mathcal{L}is(F_{1},F_{0}):=\{T\in\mathcal{L}(F_{1},F_{0})\,:\,\text{$T$ is bijective}\}

and

\|(\lambda-A)x\|_{F_{0}}\geq|\lambda|\,\|x\|_{F_{0}}+\|x\|_{F_{1}},\qquad x\in F_{1},\ {\rm Re}\ \lambda\geq\omega.

We refer to
[
2
]
for details.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

We divide the lengthy proof into four steps.

(i)
We first represent the manifold
\mathcal{E}
in a neighborhood of
u_{*}
as a translated graph.
To this end, define
h:U\to E_{1}^{0}
by

h(\xi):=P\psi(\xi)
for
\xi\in U
and note that

\partial h(0)=P\partial\psi(0)\in\mathcal{L}is(\mathbb{R}^{m},E_{1}^{0})

due to (
1.4
), (
1.8
), and (
1.9b
). The inverse function theorem then ensures that the mapping
h
is a
{\rm C}^{2}
-diffeomorphism in a neighborhood of
0
and hence invertible.
Thus, there exists
r_{0}>0
such that

h^{-1}\in{\rm C}^{2}\big(\mathbb{B}_{E_{1}^{0}}(0,r_{0}),U\big),\qquad h^{-1}(0)=0.

Set
\Phi(x):=\psi\big(h^{-1}(x)\big)
for
x\in\mathbb{B}_{E_{1}^{0}}(0,r_{0})
. Then
\Phi\in{\rm C}^{2}\big(\mathbb{B}_{E_{1}^{0}}(0,r_{0}),E_{1}\big)
satisfies
\Phi(0)=0
and, recalling (
1.2
),

\big\{\Phi(x)+u_{*}\,:\,x\in\mathbb{B}_{E_{1}^{0}}(0,r_{0})\}=\mathcal{E}\cap W

for some neighborhood
W
of
u_{*}
in
O_{1}
. Since

In this section, we illustrate how Theorem

Moreover, in Example

4.1.
Example (Fractional Mean Curvature Flow)

The evolution of a family of periodic graphs
{\{\Sigma(u(t)):t\geq 0\}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
driven by the nonlocal fractional mean curvature of order
\sigma\in(0,1)
, is governed by

(4.1)

V(t)=-H_{\sigma}(\Sigma(u(t))),\quad t>0,\qquad\Sigma(0)=\Sigma_{0},

where
V(t)
denotes the normal velocity of
\Sigma(u(t))
and

\displaystyle H_{\sigma}(\Sigma(u))(x,u(x)):=-\frac{2}{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u(x)-u(x-y)-y\cdot\nabla u(x-y)}{\big[|y|^{2}+(u(x)-u(x-y))^{2}\big]^{\tfrac{n+1+\sigma}{2}}}\,{\rm d}y,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

is the fractional mean curvature of order
\sigma\in(0,1)
of the graph
{\Sigma(u)=\{(x,u(x)):x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}}
. This geometric concept has been introduced quite recently in
[
5
]
.
In the periodic graph setting, problem (
4.1
) has been formulated in
[
31
]
as a quasilinear evolution problem

(4.2)

u^{\prime}=A(u)u,\quad t>0,\qquad u(0)=u^{0},

where

A(u)v(x):=\frac{2}{\sigma}(1+|\nabla u|^{2})^{1/2}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{v(x)-v(x-y)-y\cdot\nabla v(x-y)}{\big[|y|^{2}+(u(x)-u(x-y))^{2}\big]^{\tfrac{n+1+\sigma}{2}}}\,{\rm d}y,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

A suitable analytic framework for (
4.2
) is that of little Hölder spaces
{\rm h}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
,
s\geq 0
, defined as the closure of

{\rm C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
in the classical Hölder spaces
{\rm C}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
.
Here,
{\rm C}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
denotes the Banach space of functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which are
2\pi
-periodic in each variable and possess
(s-[s])
-Hölder continuous derivatives of order

[s]:=\max\{k\in\mathbb{N}:k\leq s\}
.
Moreover,
{\rm C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
is the intersection of all
{\rm C}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
with
s\geq 0
.

As shown in
[
31
]
via a scaling argument, the space
{\rm h}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n})={\rm C}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
can be identified, as an
invariant space for (
4.2
). Additionally, it is proven in
[
31

that problem (
4.2
) is locally well-posed in the subcritical spaces
{\rm h}^{1+\vartheta}(\mathbb{T}^{n})

with
\vartheta\in(0,1)
arbitrarily close to

. Also in a graph setting, but allowing for
functions with linear growth that possess gradients in
{\rm C}^{\vartheta}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\vartheta>\sigma
, the local well-posedness has been recently established in
[
3
]
.

Concerning stability properties of equilibria to (
4.2
), we note that the stationary solutions
are exactly the constant functions
[
3
,
31

, which form the one-dimensional linear manifold

\mathcal{E}=\mathbb{R}.

We point out that it is not at all clear whether there are flow invariants for (

We therefore rely on Theorem

\sigma(A_{*}(0))\setminus\{0\}=\big\{-\omega_{0}|k|^{1+\sigma}\,:\,k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\big\}\subset(-\infty,-\omega_{0}],

hence also (
1.8
) is satisfied.

Finally, to verify assumption (
1.10
), we note that the projection corresponding to the spectral set
\{0\}
of
A_{*}(0)
is given by

Pu:=\langle u\rangle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}u\,{\rm d}x,\qquad u\in E_{0}.

Moreover, in view of (
4.7
) and (
4.9
), we further have

A\in{\rm C}^{\infty}\big({\rm h}^{1+\vartheta^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{n}),\mathcal{H}({\rm h}^{1+\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{n}),{\rm h}^{\eta^{\prime}-\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{n}))\big).

Hence, setting
F:=\mathrm{h}^{\eta^{\prime}-\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
and
G=E_{\zeta}:=\mathrm{h}^{1+\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
, and choosing

O_{\beta}
to be a sufficiently small neighborhood of
0
in
E_{\beta}={\rm h}^{1+\vartheta^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
, this property shows that (
1.10
) holds (with
P\in\mathcal{L}(F)
as defined above).

Consequently, we may apply Theorem
1.3
to the evolution problem (
4.2
) and conclude that
0
(hence also every other constant solution) is a stable equilibrium of (
4.2
).
Moreover, given any
\omega\in(0,\omega_{0})
, there exist constants
\delta>0
and
K\geq 1
such that, for each
{u^{0}\in\mathbb{B}_{E_{\alpha}}(0,\delta)}
, the maximal solution
u(\cdot;u^{0})
to (
4.2
) is globally defined and there exists
\hat{u}_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\|u(t;u^{0})-\hat{u}_{*}\|_{E_{\alpha}}\leq Ke^{-\omega t}\|u^{0}\|_{E_{\alpha}},\qquad t\geq 0.

Estimate (
4.6
) is now a straightforward consequence of this estimate and of property (
4.8
).
∎

The exponential convergence (
4.6
) has also been shown in
[
31
, Theorem 1.3]
by a direct proof based on the principle of linearized stability from
[
30
, Theorem 1.3]
. However, the proof given herein is considerably less technical.
For a related convergence of bounded global solutions in

{\rm C}^{1+\vartheta}(\mathbb{T}^{n})
(with

) to flat graphs via an energy approach, we refer to

.

As we have seen in the above proof, the verification of (
1.10
) was rather simple due to the given freedom for the choice of the parameters in (
4.7
). That is, it was a consequence of the fact that the quasilinear part has a generation property in a scale of interpolation spaces. It is worthwhile to point out that this is a special case of the following general observation, where (
4.10
) reflects the situation of (
4.7
):

Proposition 4.2
.

Consider a densely and compactly injected Banach couple
\mathsf{F}_{1}\hookrightarrow\mathsf{F}_{0}
and for arbitrary admissible interpolation functors

(\cdot,\cdot)_{\theta}
with
\theta\in[0,1]
set
\mathsf{F}_{\theta}:=(\mathsf{F}_{0},\mathsf{F}_{1})_{\theta}
. Fix

(4.10a)

0\leq\theta_{0}<\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<\theta_{3}<\theta_{4}<\theta_{5}\leq 1,

let
\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{2}}}
be open in
\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{2}}
, and set
\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{3}}}:=\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{2}}}\cap\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{3}}
.
Assume that

(4.10b)

\mathsf{A}\in{\rm C}^{1}\big(\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{3}}},\mathcal{H}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{5}},\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{1}})\big)\cap{\rm C}^{1}\big(\mathsf{O}_{{\mathsf{F}}_{\theta_{2}}},\mathcal{H}({\mathsf{F}}_{\theta_{4}},{\mathsf{F}}_{\theta_{0}})\big)

and

(4.11)

\mathsf{f}\in{\rm C}^{1}\big(\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{3}}},\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{1}}\big).

Let
u_{*}\in\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{5}}\cap\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{2}}}
, set

(4.12)

\mathsf{A}_{*}:=\mathsf{A}(u_{*})+(\partial\mathsf{A}(u_{*})[\cdot])u_{*}+\partial\mathsf{f}(u_{*})\in\mathcal{H}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{5}},\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{1}})\cap\mathcal{H}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{4}},\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{0}}),

and assume that
0
is a semi-simple eigenvalue of
\mathsf{A}_{*}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{5}},\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{1}})
. Then
0
is also a semi-simple eigenvalue of
\mathsf{A}_{*}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{4}},\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{0}})
.

If

\mathsf{P}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{0}})\cap\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{1}})
denotes the spectral projection onto
\mathrm{ker}(A_{*})
corresponding to the spectral set
\{0\}
, then
(
1.10
) is valid for
(E_{0},E_{1})=(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{1}},\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{5}})
and
(F,G)=(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{0}},\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{4}})
.

Proof.

Let us first observe that

\mathsf{A}(u_{*})\in\mathcal{H}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{5}},\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{1}})\cap\mathcal{H}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{4}},\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{0}}),\qquad(\partial\mathsf{A}(u_{*})[\cdot])u_{*}+\partial\mathsf{f}(u_{*})\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{3}},\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{1}}),

so that the almost reiteration property
[
2
, I.Remarks 2.11.2 (a)]
together with (
4.10a
) and the perturbation result
[
2
, I.Theorem 1.3.1 (ii)]
imply (
4.12
).

Next, since
0
is a semi-simple eigenvalue of
\mathsf{A}_{*}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{5}},\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{1}})
, it follows from
[
27
, Proposition A.2.2]
that 0 is a simple pole of
\lambda\mapsto\big(\lambda-\mathsf{A}_{*}\big)^{-1}
(as a mapping into
\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{1}})
), or equivalently, that

(4.13)

0=\mathsf{A}_{*}\mathsf{P}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{1}})

according to
[
27
, Proposition A.2.1]
. Noticing that
\mathsf{A}_{*}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{5}},\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{1}})
and
\mathsf{A}_{*}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{4}},\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{0}})
both have compact resolvents, their resolvent sets coincide as they consist only of eigenvalues. Hence 0 is also an isolated eigenvalue of
\mathsf{A}_{*}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{4}},\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{0}})
and it follows that

\mathsf{P}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\big(\lambda-\mathsf{A}_{*}\big)^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\in\mathcal{L}\big(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{0}}\big)\cap\mathcal{L}\big(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{1}}\big),

where
\Gamma
is a curve in the resolvent set around 0, see
[
27
, Appendix A]
.
As
\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{1}}
is dense in
\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{0}}
, we infer from (
4.13
) that
0=\mathsf{A}_{*}\mathsf{P}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{0}})
and therefore 0 is a semi-simple eigenvalue of
\mathsf{A}_{*}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{4}},\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{0}})
due to
[
27
, Proposition A.2.2]
.

Also note from
[
27
, Proposition A.2.2]
that

\mathsf{P}\big(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{0}}\big)=\mathrm{ker}\Big(\mathsf{A}_{*}\big|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{4}},\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{0}})}\Big),\qquad\mathsf{P}\big(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{1}}\big)=\mathrm{ker}\Big(\mathsf{A}_{*}\big|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{5}},\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{1}})}\Big).

To identify these kernels, let
x\in\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{4}}
satisfy
\mathsf{A}_{*}x=0
.
Then
(\omega-\mathsf{A}_{*})x=\omega x\in\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{1}}
for any
\omega\in\Gamma
, which implies
x\in\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{5}}
.
Therefore, the kernels coincide, hence
\mathsf{P}\big(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{0}}\big)=\mathsf{P}\big(\mathsf{F}_{\theta_{1}}\big)
.
This proves (
1.10a
), while (
1.10b
) is a straightforward consequence of (
4.10b
).
The proof of Proposition
4.2
is thus complete.
∎

.

4.2.
Example (Hele-Shaw Problem Driven by Capillarity)

The capillarity-driven Hele-Shaw problem
[
20
]
models the two-dimensional motion of an incompressible fluid blob confined
to a narrow channel between two parallel plates.
Let
\Omega(t)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the bounded domain occupied by the fluid at time
t\geq 0
and let
\Gamma(t):=\partial\Omega(t)
be its boundary.
The evolution is then governed by the system

(4.14a)

\left.\begin{array}[]{rcllll}\Delta u(t)&=&0&\quad\text{in $\Omega(t)$,}\\
u(t)&=&\kappa_{\Gamma(t)}&\quad\text{on $\Gamma(t)$,}\\
V(t)&=&-\partial_{{\rm n}_{\Gamma(t)}}u(t)&\quad\text{on $\Gamma(t)$}\end{array}\right\}

for
t>0
.
Here,
u(t)
denotes the fluid pressure.
The velocity field is assumed to obey Darcy’s law
[
13
]
and has been eliminated from the system using the incompressibility property.
Furthermore,
V(t)
denotes the normal velocity of
\Gamma(t)
,
{\rm n}_{\Gamma(t)}
its outer unit normal, and
\kappa_{\Gamma(t)}
its curvature.
In (
4.14a
), all material parameters are normalized to
1
.
Additionally, we impose the initial condition

(4.14b)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Omega(0)&=&\Omega_{0}.\end{array}

An important feature of the flow (
4.14
) is the fact that it preserves both the area and the center of mass of the fluid domain, that is,

(4.15)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}|\Omega(t)|

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Gamma(t)}\partial_{{\rm n}_{\Gamma(t)}}u(t)\,{\rm d}\Gamma=0,

(4.16)

\displaystyle-\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega(t)}z\,{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma(t)}z\partial_{{\rm n}_{\Gamma(t)}}u(t)\,{\rm d}\Gamma=\int_{\Gamma(t)}u(t){\rm n}_{\Gamma(t)}\,{\rm d}\Gamma=\int_{\Gamma(t)}\kappa_{\Gamma(t)}{\rm n}_{\Gamma(t)}\,{\rm d}\Gamma=0,

where
z=(x,y)
is the variable in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
; see e.g.
[
35
,
33
]
.
By a maximum–principle argument, one may identify the stationary solutions to (
4.14
)
as a three–dimensional smooth manifold
\mathcal{E}
consisting exclusively of circles (with varying radii and centers).

A broad range of analytical techniques has been developed to study the stability of circular equilibria for (
4.14
) as well as in related geometric free–boundary problems.
For the two-phase Hele–Shaw (Muskat) setting,
[
9
]
established convergence to a circle for small analytic perturbations by employing complex–analytic methods together with energy estimates.
Based on a center–manifold approach, stability results were obtained in Hölder-type settings:

[
16
]
treated perturbations of spheres in the little Hölder spaces
{\rm h}^{2+\alpha}(\Sigma)
in the context of the Mullins–Sekerka problem (where
\Sigma
is a sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
),
while
[
15
]
used this approach in a non-Newtonian Hele–Shaw flow in the regularity class
{\rm h}^{4+\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
.
In the framework of the Mullins–Sekerka problem,
[
38
,
37
]
used a generalized principle of linearized stability based on
L_{p}
–maximal regularity to prove
exponential stability for perturbations in
W^{4-4/p}_{p}(\Sigma)\hookrightarrow{\rm C}^{2}(\Sigma)
,
p>n+2
.
The stability of Muskat bubbles in (critical) Wiener spaces on the torus
\mathbb{T}
was addressed in
[
18
,
17
]
using potential theory and energy methods.

We now show that the stability of equilibria for (
4.14
) can be deduced from Theorem
1.3
in the scale of Bessel potential spaces
H^{r}(\mathbb{\mathbb{T}})
over the torus
\mathbb{T}

in a framework of (almost) optimal regularity.
Specifically, by exploiting scaling invariance, one identifies
r=3/2
as the critical exponent for (
4.14
) within the scale of Bessel potential spaces
[
33
]
.

To set the stage, we introduce

\mathcal{V}_{r}:=\{\rho\in H^{r}(\mathbb{T}):\rho>0\},\qquad r\in(3/2,2],

and define, for a given function
\rho\in\mathcal{V}_{r}
, the periodic curve

\Gamma_{\rho}:=\{\rho(\tau)\,e^{i\tau}:\tau\in\mathbb{R}\}

enclosing a bounded star–shaped domain
\Omega_{\rho}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Assuming that
\Omega(t)=\Omega_{\rho(t)}
for
{t>0}
and using potential theory and tools from harmonic analysis, the evolution problem (
4.14
) is reformulated in
[
33
]
as a quasilinear problem for
\rho
of the form

(4.17)

\rho^{\prime}=A(\rho)\rho,\quad t>0,\qquad\rho(0)=\rho^{0},

where

(4.18)

A\in{\rm C}^{\infty}\bigl(\mathcal{V}_{r},\,\mathcal{L}(H^{r+1}(\mathbb{T}),\,H^{r-2}(\mathbb{T}))\bigr),\qquad r\in(3/2,2].

Focusing on the stability of the circle centered at the origin of radius
1
, that is, the stationary solution
\rho_{*}=1
(a similar argument yields exponential stability for any circle of radius
r>0
centered at
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
), it is natural – in view of the invariants
(
4.15
)–(
4.16
)
– to consider the asymptotic behavior of solutions satisfying the identities

(4.19)

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(\rho^{2}-1)\,{\rm d}s=0,\qquad\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\rho^{3}\cos s\,{\rm d}s=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\rho^{3}\sin s\,{\rm d}s=0,

which simply express that the fluid blob has area
\pi
(the first identity) and that its center of mass is at
(0,0)
(the second and third identities).
Applying Theorem
1.3
we establish the following result:

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
3/2<\bar{r}<r<2
. Then problem (
4.17
) has for each
\rho^{0}\in\mathcal{V}_{r}

a unique maximal solution
\rho=\rho(\cdot;\rho^{0})
such that

(4.20)

\rho\in{\rm C}\big([0,t^{+}),\mathcal{V}_{r}\big)\cap{\rm C}\big((0,t^{+}),H^{\bar{r}+1}(\mathbb{T})\big)\cap{\rm C}^{1}\big((0,t^{+}),H^{\bar{r}-2}(\mathbb{T})\big)

and, for some
\eta\in(0,(r-\bar{r})/3]
,

(4.21)

\rho\in{\rm C}^{\eta}\big([0,t^{+}),H^{\bar{r}}(\mathbb{T})\big),

where
t^{+}=t^{+}(\rho^{0})>0
is the maximal existence time of the solution.

Moreover,
\rho_{*}=1
is a stable equilibrium in
H^{r}(\mathbb{T})
, and, for any given
\omega\in(0,6)
, there are constants
\delta>0
and
{K\geq 1}
such that for each
\rho^{0}\in\mathbb{B}_{H^{r}}(1,\delta)

satisfying (
4.19
), the maximal solution
\rho(\cdot;\rho^{0})
to (
4.17
) is globally defined and

(4.22)

\|\rho(t;\rho^{0})-1\|_{H^{{r}}}\leq Ke^{-\omega t}\|\rho^{0}-1\|_{H^{{r}}},\qquad t\geq 0.

Proof.

The well-posedness of (
4.17
) is established in

[
33
, Theorem 1.1]
relying on (
4.18
) and abstract quasilinear parabolic theory (see Theorem
1.1
). We omit details here.

As for the stability statement, we set
E_{0}:=H^{\bar{r}-2}(\mathbb{T})
and
E_{1}:=H^{\bar{r}+1}(\mathbb{T})
and choose

0<\beta:=\frac{2}{3}<\alpha:=\frac{2+r-\bar{r}}{3}<\zeta:=\frac{3+r^{\prime}-\bar{r}}{3}<1\qquad\text{with}\qquad 3/2<r^{\prime}<\bar{r}<r<2.

We recall the interpolation property

(4.23)

\displaystyle[H^{r_{0}}(\mathbb{\mathbb{T}}),H^{r_{1}}(\mathbb{\mathbb{T}})]_{\theta}=H^{(1-\theta)r_{0}+\theta r_{1}}(\mathbb{\mathbb{T}}),\qquad\theta\in(0,1),\ \,-\infty\leq r_{0}\leq r_{1}<\infty,

where
[\cdot,\cdot]_{\theta}
refers to the complex interpolation functor of exponent
\theta\in(0,1)
.
Setting
E_{\theta}:=[E_{0},E_{1}]_{\theta}
for
\theta\in[0,1]
, we infer from (
4.23
) that

E_{\zeta}=H^{1+r^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T})\hookrightarrow E_{\alpha}=H^{r}(\mathbb{T})\hookrightarrow E_{\beta}=H^{\bar{r}}(\mathbb{T}),

and (
4.18
) ensures that
\Phi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}\big(O_{\beta},\mathcal{L}(E_{1},\,E_{0})\big)
with
O_{\beta}:=\mathcal{V}_{\bar{r}}
.
Therefore, we have verified assumption (
1.2
).

Concerning the set of equilibria close to the unit circle, we follow
[
16
]
and solve the equation

|\rho e^{i\tau}-(a,b)|^{2}=r^{2}

for
\rho\in(0,\infty)
. This yields that, for
(a,b,r)
in a sufficiently small neighborhood

U
of
0
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the circle with radius
1+r
and center
(a,b)
coincides with the curve
\Gamma_{\rho}
for
\rho
given by

\rho=a\cos+b\sin+\sqrt{(a\cos+b\sin)^{2}+(1+r)^{2}-a^{2}-b^{2}}=:\Psi(a,b,r)\in E_{1}.

Then
\Psi\in{\rm C}^{\infty}(U,E_{1})
satisfies
\Psi(0)=1
and

(4.24)

\mathrm{rg}(\partial\Psi(0))=\mathrm{span}\{1,\,\sin,\,\cos\},

which shows that (
1.4
) holds.

To verify (
1.8
), we note from
[
33
, Section 5]
that

A_{*}(0)=A(1)=H\circ\Big(\frac{{\rm d}^{3}}{{\rm d}\tau^{3}}+\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\tau}\Big),

where
H
denotes the periodic Hilbert transform.
In particular,
A_{*}(0)\in\mathcal{L}(E_{1},E_{0})
is the Fourier multiplier with symbol
m(k):=|k|(1-|k|^{2})
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. That is,

A_{*}(0)\rho=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}m(k)a_{k}e^{ik\tau},\qquad\rho=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{k}e^{ik\tau}\in E_{1}.

Hence,

\sigma(A_{*}(0))=\{|k|(1-|k|^{2})\,:\,k\in\mathbb{Z}\}

consists solely of isolated eigenvalues.
Together with (
4.24
), this verifies (
1.8
). We also note that
-\omega_{0}=\sup\{|k|(1-|k|^{2})\,:\,|k|\geq 2\}=-6
.

Finally, regarding the last assumption (
1.10
) of Theorem
1.3
, we note that the projection
P
corresponding to the spectral set
\{0\}
of
A_{*}(0)
is given by

P\rho=\sum_{|k|\leq 1}a_{k}e^{ik\tau},\qquad\rho=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{k}e^{ik\tau}\in E_{1}.

Therefore, setting
G:=E_{\zeta}=H^{1+r^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T})
and
F=H^{r^{\prime}-2}(\mathbb{T})
, we may infer from
(
4.18
) (with
r
replaced by
r^{\prime}
), after possibly making
O_{\beta}
smaller,
that (
1.10
) is fulfilled.

We are thus in a position to apply Theorem
1.3
to (
4.17
) and establish the stability of the equilibrium
\rho_{*}=1
and
the desired estimate (
4.22
) by using (
4.19
).
∎

The convergence (
4.22
) has also been shown in
[
33
, Theorem 1.4]
by applying an abstract stability result
[
33
, Theorem A.1]
and exploiting the flow invariants
(

)–(

)
. However, the proof given herein is considerably shorter and less technical.
We point out that, in this example as well, assumption (
1.10
) alternatively follows from Proposition
4.2
in view of (
4.18
).

4.3.
Example (A Quasilinear Problem with Scaling Invariance)

In this example we shall apply Theorem
1.6
to the quasilinear evolution equation
[
42
,
43
]

(4.25a)

\partial_{t}u={\rm div}(a(u)\nabla u)+|\nabla u|^{\kappa}\qquad\text{in }\Omega,\ \,t>0,

subject to homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions

(4.25b)

\partial_{\nu}u=0\qquad\text{on }\partial\Omega,\ \,t>0,

and initial condition

(4.25c)

u(0)=u^{0}\qquad\text{in $\Omega$,}

where
{\kappa>3}
,
a\in{\rm C}^{3-}(\mathbb{R})
is a strictly positive function, and
u^{0}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a given function on a smooth and bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n\geq 1
.
For
p\in(1,\infty)
we set

H_{p,N}^{2\theta}(\Omega):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\{u\in H_{p}^{2\theta}(\Omega)\,:\,\partial_{\nu}u=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\},&1+\frac{1}{p}<2\theta\leq 2,\\[3.0pt]
H_{p}^{2\theta}(\Omega),&-1+\frac{1}{p}<2\theta<1+\frac{1}{p},\end{array}\right.

and note, taking
a
to be constant and using scaling invariance arguments, that the space
H^{s_{c}}_{p,N}(\Omega)
with

(4.26)

s_{c}:=\frac{n}{p}+\frac{\kappa-2}{\kappa-1}

is a critical space for (
4.25
), see
[
42
,
28
]
. Local well-posedness of (
4.25
) in critical scaling invariant spaces has been addressed in
[
42
, Example 2]
and subsequently in
[
28
, Example 4]
.
Moreover, the exponential stability of the zero solution to (
4.25
) with (
4.25b
) replaced by a homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition (in this case is
0
the only constant solution to (
4.25a
))
has been established in the corresponding critical Bessel potential space of exponent (
4.26
) in
[
29
, Example 3]
.
For homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions, all constants are equilibrium solutions to (
4.25
), which makes the stability analysis more intricate.
The following theorem, which is new to the best of our knowledge, establishes the stability of the constant solutions to (
4.25
) in the critical space identified by (
4.26
).
For simplicity, in Theorem
4.4
we consider the asymptotic behavior of solutions that are initially close to
u_{*}=0
in this phase space (though
0
may be replaced by any other constant).

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\kappa>3
,
p\in(2n,(\kappa-1)n)
with
p\neq(n-1)(\kappa-1)
, and
\tau\in(0,1)
such that

\frac{1}{2}<2\tau<1-\frac{n}{p}.

Further set

0<\bar{s}:=2\tau+\frac{n}{p}<s_{c}<s:=1+\frac{n(\kappa-1)}{p\kappa}<2-2\tau

and

\mu:=\frac{1}{2(\kappa-1)}-\frac{n}{2p\kappa}\in(0,1),\qquad\vartheta:=\frac{\kappa-2}{2(\kappa-1)}-\tau\in(0,1).

Then, given any
u^{0}\in H^{s_{c}}_{p,N}(\Omega)
, there exists a unique maximal solution
u=u(\cdot;u^{0})
to (
4.25
) such that

(4.27a)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\in{\rm C}\big((0,t^{+}),H^{2-2\tau}_{p,N}(\Omega)\big)\cap{\rm C}^{1}\big((0,t^{+}),H^{-2\tau}_{p}(\Omega)\big)\cap{\rm C}\big([0,t^{+}),H^{s_{c}}_{p,N}(\Omega)\big)

\displaystyle\cap{\rm C}^{\vartheta}\big([0,t^{+}),H^{\bar{s}}_{p}(\Omega)\big)

and

(4.27b)

\lim_{t\to 0}t^{\mu}\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}_{p}}=0,

where
t^{+}:=t^{+}(u^{0})\in(0,\infty]
is the maximal existence time of the solution.

Moreover,
u_{*}=0
is a stable equilibrium in
H^{s_{c}}_{p,N}(\Omega)
, and there exists
\omega_{0}>0
and for each
\omega\in(0,\omega_{0})
there exist constants
\delta>0
and
K\geq 1
such that for all
