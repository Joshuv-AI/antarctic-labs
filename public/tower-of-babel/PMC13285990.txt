A case report of acute right ventricular failure in a patient undergoing transoesophageal echocardiogram for evaluation of tricuspid regurgitation
Authors: Mustafa Mohammed, Andrew Chou, Sachin Parikh, Alexandra DePorre, James McCord
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTransoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is generally considered a low-risk procedure. However, in patients with severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) complicated by right ventricular (RV) dysfunction and pulmonary hypertension (PH), standard procedural sedation can precipitate life-threatening haemodynamic collapse.Case summaryAn 83-year-old woman with end-stage renal disease, severe PH (95 mmHg), and severe TR underwent a TEE. Shortly after induction with propofol and probe insertion, the patient developed profound systemic hypotension (56/30 mmHg). Real-time TEE imaging revealed acute, severe RV dilatation and a precipitous decline in systolic function. The procedure was immediately aborted, and the patient was stabilized with intravenous phenylephrine and ephedrine. She returned to her haemodynamic baseline within 20 min of procedure termination and probe removal.DiscussionThis case highlights the ‘triple hit’ effect that places patients with RV failure at high risk during sedation: propofol-induced systemic vasodilation, the vagal response to probe insertion, and hypercapnia-induced increases in pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR). For this high-risk phenotype, a ‘slow and low’ anaesthetic titration, meticulous PVR management, and early consideration of RV-protective vasopressors are essential to prevent catastrophic RV-pulmonary uncoupling.

Introduction

In recent years, right ventricular (RV) dysfunction has gained increasing attention, particularly with the advent of tricuspid valve (TV) transcatheter interventions.1 Due to the complex structure of the RV and the variable factors affecting its function, managing and anticipating acute changes can be challenging.2 Moreover, while the impact of RV dysfunction and pulmonary hypertension (PH) on haemodynamics during the peri-sedation period is known, the risk may be even higher with valvular diseases.1 To highlight this concern, we present a case of acute severe RV dysfunction in the setting of severe PH during a transoesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) for evaluation of severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR).

Case presentation

An 83-year-old woman presents for TEE as part of an evaluation for TR. She has a relevant past medical history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, end-stage renal disease (ESRD) on haemodialysis with last dialysis session 1-day prior, obstructive sleep apnoea, peripheral artery disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and hypertension. The patient underwent a transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) earlier on the same day that showed ejection fraction (EF) of 63%, normal LV, moderately enlarged RV with moderately reduced RV systolic function, bowing of intra-atrial septum from left to right, severely dilated left atrium, pulmonary artery systolic pressure 95 mmHg, severe mitral regurgitation (MR), and severe TR (see Supplementary material online, Video S1 and Figure 1).

TTE showing apical four-chamber view with moderate RV dilation.

The patient presented to the echocardiography lab and was prepared for the procedure. She was given viscous lidocaine gargle as a topical anaesthetic prior to sedation. Sedation was administered by the anaesthesia team, with an initial bolus of propofol 70 mg followed by infusion up to 75 mcg/kg/min. During the procedure, the patient was placed on supplemental oxygen with a simple mask, and end-tidal CO2 was monitored. The TEE probe was advanced without difficulty, and images were obtained. The patient became hypotensive shortly after beginning the procedure; her blood pressure went from initial 107/69 down to 56/30 within 15 min of starting the procedure. Her respiratory status remained stable without reported desaturations. Subsequently, she was treated with phenylephrine and ephedrine, respectively. TEE evaluation was notable for severely dilated RV with severely reduced RV systolic function (see Supplementary material online, Videos S2–S3, Figures 2 and 3 showing RV function at +6 and +15 min, respectively). The procedure was aborted early, and the patient had quick recovery of blood pressure with treatment. The patient was monitored until she recovered fully from anaesthesia and was sent back in stable condition to her inpatient unit (Table 1 shows the timeline of events).

Discussion

With the advent of new transcatheter interventions for valvular diseases, patients previously deemed high risk for cardiac surgery are increasingly undergoing TEE. While TEE is generally considered a low-risk procedure with a major complication rate of <0.2%,3 standard sedation protocols pose a disproportionate risk to patients with significant TR, PH, and RV dysfunction. In this population, the safety profile of sedation is altered by unique haemodynamics. Similar to patients with significant MR, preserved EF may over-represent the effective cardiac output (CO). Consequently, circulation time is prolonged, leading to a delayed onset of medication effect. If providers are not cognizant of this delay, there is a danger of ‘stacking’ doses (administering additional anaesthetic in response to persistent levels of consciousness before the initial bolus has taken effect) leading to unintentional overdose and haemodynamic collapse.

Furthermore, the margin for error regarding ventilation is minimal in patients with RV dysfunction. Hypoxia and hypercapnia are poorly tolerated because both conditions directly increase pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR). Hypoxia triggers hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, an adaptive mechanism that diverts blood from poorly ventilated lung regions but significantly increases RV afterload when global. Similarly, hypercapnia induces respiratory acidosis, which acts as a potent pulmonary vasoconstrictor.4 In a patient with a failing RV, acute increases in PVR can uncouple the RV from the pulmonary circulation, precipitating acute right heart failure and cardiac arrest.

The risk of RV failure during sedation is well-documented, yet often underappreciated. A retrospective observational study from our own institution by Chou et al. reported two cases of RV failure during TEE specifically in patients undergoing evaluation for TR, one of which required inotropic support.1 This mirrors findings in broader literature; Kersey et al. reported a case where propofol administration in a patient with mild PH and moderate RV dysfunction led to pulseless electrical activity arrest and ventricular fibrillation. In that case, rescue TEE revealed minimal contractility and severe dilation, indistinguishable from the presentation in our case.5

These adverse events highlight the critical need for pre-procedural risk stratification using TTE. The importance of baseline RV function is underscored by the design of major clinical trials. For example, the TRISCEND trial excluded patients with severe baseline RV failure and consequently reported no poor outcomes regarding RV function post-implant.6 These exclusion criteria implicitly acknowledge that patients with severe RV dysfunction represent a distinct, high-risk phenotype. Therefore, applying standard sedation practices to a population that would have been excluded from safety trials is fraught with hazard.

In our case, we believe the patient’s baseline RV dysfunction, combined with PH and ESRD with volume overload, created a fragile substrate. This was likely exacerbated by the direct vasodilatory effects of propofol, the vagal response to the TEE probe, and potential transient hypoventilation. This ‘triple hit’ led to a spiral of increased PVR and decreased contractility, resulting in acute RV failure.

Conclusion

Managing anaesthesia for TEE in patients with TV disease requires a paradigm shift from routine sedation to a focused, high-risk cardiac strategy. Based on this case, we propose three key considerations for this population.

First, comprehensive pre-procedural haemodynamic profiling is essential. Baseline RV function must be scrutinized via TTE prior to the procedure. In patients with known PH or severe RV dysfunction, invasive haemodynamic monitoring (i.e. arterial line or pulmonary artery catheter) should be considered prior to induction to allow for immediate recognition of haemodynamic shifts. Availability and preparation of vasopressors and inotropes must be considered along with their unique vasoactive effects. In the case of existing PH, pure alpha blockade with phenylephrine may further increase PVR and worsen RV function. Vasopressin may be a more appropriate choice to increase SVR while maintaining PVR. In this case, a drop in blood pressure may not be from SVR alone but a decrease in CO due to worsening of RV function, making an inotrope like epinephrine an appropriate first line choice to maintain CO during the procedure.

Second, sedation strategies must be adapted for slow circulation times. The standard titration of propofol is hazardous in this demographic due to prolonged onset time and vasodilatory effects. A ‘slow and low’ approach, utilizing alternate agents with more stable haemodynamic profiles (such as etomidate or ketamine), or a lighter plane of sedation, is advisable. In high-risk cases, the involvement of a cardiac anaesthesiologist is crucial to manage the delicate balance of RV preload and afterload.

Third, intra-procedural vigilance must focus on PVR management. Any degree of hypoxia, hypercapnia, or systemic hypotension must be treated aggressively to prevent the vicious cycle of increased PVR and RV spiral. By recognizing TEE as a high-risk intervention in this specific population, multidisciplinary teams can better optimize patients and prevent catastrophic haemodynamic collapse.
