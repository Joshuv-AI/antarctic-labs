 Executive Summary
Groundwater in the Lost Lake Aquifer Zone (LLAZ) in the Southern Sector of the M-Area
Hazardous Waste management Facility (HWMF) is contaminated with chlorinated ethenes,
including trichloroethylene (TCE) and tetrachloroethylene (PCE). Treatment of contaminated
groundwater with humic acid is being evaluated as a potential corrective action for these volatile
organic compounds (VOCs) in the LLAZ in Southern Sector (SRNS, 2019). Pilot scale injections
of Huma-K brand humic acid for groundwater treatment were previously performed in M-Area
between 2017 and 2020 (Amidon, 2023). Groundwater was extracted from the Lost Lake Aquifer
Zone (LLAZ), then a solution of humate was mixed with recovered groundwater and subsequently
re-injected into the same aquifer unit. Operations of the historical humate pilot testing were
discontinued in 2020 due to COVID-19 restrictions, low injection rates, and recirculation fluid
spillage. No further field scale or laboratory research to investigate humate amended groundwater
injection was performed at the SRS until 2024. At the request of Area Completion Projects (ACP),
the Savannah River National Laboratory (SRNL) performed supplementary testing to help
determine the viability of further injection of humate as a remedial option for VOCs within the LLAZ
in the Southern Sector of the M-Area HWMF.
During the previous humate injection pilot scale testing, multiple factors were observed by
operations personnel that may have impacted the injection rates and overall performance of
injection. These factors include the precipitation of humate from solution during injection, the
buildup of excessive amounts of foam during humate amendment mixing and injection, and the
potential clogging of wells screens within the surrounding aquifer formation during humate
injection. Four testing procedures were performed to investigate humate injectants in simulated
pilot scale testing procedures. These studies were designed to support a more successful
transition of this approach to future field deployment.
 1. Humate Injectant Saturation: Using samples collected from the remaining stock of dry
 humate flake (Huma-K) purchased to support the 2017 – 2020 pilot scale testing, the
 maximum amount of humate that could be fully, and reliably, dissolved into solution was
 determined. The addition of humate to solution was performed until complete saturation
 was achieved; i.e. humate, or other solids, no longer dissolved completely in solution.
 Results from this testing were used to bound the high end of Huma-K concentrations used
 for subsequent testing.
 2. Injectant Steady State Mixture and Filtration: Laboratory experiments were conducted
 to evaluate the performance of humate solutions at different concentrations over time,
 prepared using varied mixing techniques and filtration schemes. An alternative humate
 (BorreGro HA-1) was tested alongside Huma-K in these tests, as well as in subsequent
 pH testing and soil column experiments. BorreGro was purchased as a pre-mixed high
 concentration liquid solution and diluted for testing. Results from these steady state
 mixture and filtration experiments specifically show that humate, once in solution, is
 relatively stable under storage conditions with minimal precipitation of solids. The mixing
 and filtration procedures produced little variation in the stability and consistency of
 solutions. Filtration was found to be most valuable to ensure the removal of any
 undissolved humate particulate following initial mixing or multi-day storage.
 3. Injectant pH Manipulation: Precipitation of humate as a result of changing pH conditions
 in the subsurface was identified as a hypothesized hinderance to injection viability.
 Simulated injection fluid was generated with both Huma-K and BorreGro at humate

 v

 concentrations approximately matching those in the 2019 Underground Injection Permit
 (292 ug/L) using M-Area groundwater (SRNS, 2019). The controlled decrease of sample
 stock pH was then performed to determine where potential precipitation would occur and
 if the native pH conditions of the LLAZ would induce the precipitation of humate from
 solution during injection. Significant humate precipitation was found to occur at far lower
 pH values than the known pH conditions of the LLAZ.
 4. Soil Column Study: Following testing performed to investigate mixing technique, stability,
 filtration, and the pH conditions under which precipitation of humate could occur, soil
 columns were used to simulate injection of humate amended groundwater into the LLAZ
 at a laboratory scale. Variables tested using soil columns included the impact of injectant
 concentration and composition on injection rates, the sorption of humate to LLAZ soil
 particles, and the concentrations of PCE and TCE within injectant during injection.
To match field conditions as closely as possible, all testing was performed using M-Area
groundwater collected from the LLAZ aquifer. In addition, soil column experimentation was
conducted using sediment taken from historical coring soils retrieved from within the LLAZ at
locations proximal to the historical humate pilot testing site.
Data collected from testing show that solutions created using BorreGro liquid humate are more
consistent and stable during simulated injection procedures. In most experiments conducted,
appreciable advantages to future pilot scale injection testing were noted for solutions that included
BorreGro liquid humate, such as the lack of precipitation during storage and greater humate
concentrations able to pass through LLAZ sediments in soil column experiments. In other tests
conducted, Huma-K and BorreGro performed similarly without clear advantages once mixed into
solution. Humate solutions were found to reduce the hydraulic conductivity of LLAZ sediments by
an average of 49.4 % in all soil column experimentation, indicating there may be some difficulty
spreading injectant through the aquifer system. Overall, injection of humate appears viable and
BorreGro liquid humate demonstrated advantages over Huma-K for the success of proposed pilot
scale testing.

1.0 Introduction

Historical operations performed in A/M-Area of the Savannah River Site (SRS) included the
production of fuel and target assemblies for SRS production reactors. These operations are
known to have released chlorinated volatile organic compounds (cVOCs) to the subsurface,
resulting in soil and groundwater contamination. Multiple aquifer units beneath A/M-Area were
contaminated as a result. Corrective action to remediate these VOCs is ongoing under a
Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) Permit Renewal.
The primary portion of the dissolved phase cVOC plume that exists within the Southern Sector of
A/M-Area resides within the Lost Lake Aquifer Zone (LLAZ) of the Steed Pond Aquifer. The LLAZ
is characterized by silty sand composition and a prominent downward gradient across the vertical
extent of the aquifer (Jackson et al., 1996). The LLAZ is underlain by the regional Crouch Branch
Confining Unit which consists of interbedded clay and sand units. Southward migration of cVOC
contaminants within the LLAZ are predominant in the lower half of the aquifer. Pump and treat
systems, hydraulic control, airlift recirculation, and other in situ methodologies are some of the
historical corrective action technologies employed to remediate the LLAZ.
Research conducted in 2014 and 2015 by the Savannah River National Laboratory (SRNL)
investigated the potential for a more passive, low-cost approach to promote the natural depletion
and attenuation of cVOCs by aerobic degradation of humic and fulvic acid (dissolved organic
carbon). Initial microcosm testing with this amendment proved promising, as concentrations of
VOCs in groundwater were reduced during lab experiments (Jackson et al., 2014). With the
concurrence of South Carolina Department of Environmental Services (SCDES), a field scale test
to inject humate into the Southern Sector of the M-Area HWMF subsurface using former airlift
recirculation well (ARW) SSR001 was approved. The objective of this test was to increase the
sorption capacity in the aquifer, stimulate microbial growth, and promote aerobic co-metabolism
to enhance aerobic degradation of cVOCs in the groundwater of the LLAZ. Field scale testing of
humate injection began in 2017 (Amidon, 2023) preceded by well redevelopment and localized
aquifer testing conducted in 2016 (Amidon et al., 2016).
The humate injection field (pilot) scale test was designed to utilize existing ARWs. An approximate
total of 26,500 pounds of humate was planned to be mixed with groundwater recovered from the
lower well screen of SSR001, re-injected using the upper well screen of SSR-001, and
recirculated over a 16-month period using ARW SSR001 and its associated infrastructure as the
test site (Jackson et al., 2016). The humate injectant was intended to act as an alternative carbon
source within the LLAZ to promote the growth of microbial communities that degrade chlorinated
solvents through cometabolic processes. The targeted injection concentration of 100 milligrams
per liter (mg/L) of fractional organic matter in aquifer groundwater would sustain this process.
However, injection efforts were hindered by multiple factors during the pilot scale testing, including
foaming of humate injectant during recirculation, damage to the SSR001 well screen during its
original installation, and precipitation of humate injectant. As a corrective measure, a new injection
well (SSM040CC) was installed, and two other existing wells (SSR1B and SSM037B) were also
targeted to be employed for future use. However, due to recirculation fluid spillage (F-listed
groundwater) and the onset of COVID-19 restrictions, these wells were never used (Amidon,
2023). All work was halted in late 2020.

 1

No further field scale testing with humate in M-Area has been conducted following the cessation
of field activities in 2020. Documentation and data gathered during the field scale testing was
assessed by ACP personnel and summarized in an interoffice memorandum and technical review
dated June 13, 2023 (Amidon, 2023). The 2013 Permit Renewal Application for M-Area and Met
Lab HWMF also summarizes the findings of previous pilot testing that assessed humate as a
potential alternative for targeting the source plume in M-Area HWMF’s Southern Sector but
acknowledges that further investigation is needed to augment findings from previous lab studies
and pilot scale field deployment (SRNS, 2020). A revised strategy for humate application was
presented to SCDES for approval in 2019, but testing was halted before this new configuration
could be employed.
At the request of ACP, SRNL performed laboratory-based approaches to address findings and
comments of the 2023 technical review. This testing was designed to ultimately support a
determination if humate is a viable method for the remediation of dissolved solvent plumes by
injection and a suitable alternative for corrective action within the Southern Sector of the M-Area
HWMF (SRNS, 2020).

2.0 Humate Amendment Injection Background

Humate amendments have previously been tested in soil column studies by SRNL and its
university partners using F-Area soils to determine optimal conditions for its application under
known groundwater conditions but targeting alternative contaminants of concern (Lagos et. al.,
2016; Lagos et al., 2014). Humate amendments are also used for copper detoxification of H-Area
surface waters (Looney, 2009). Pilot-scale field tests for optimization of this technology have also
been conducted at SRS, focusing on contaminant stabilization in F-Area specifically (Millings et
al., 2013). Later studies built upon this earlier work to confirm the ability of humate amendments
to induce bacterial biomass growth necessary for the bioremediation of chlorinated solvent
contamination (Ross et. al., 2016). Results from these and other previous studies were used in
planning and execution of a humate injection pilot test at SRS’ Southern Sector of the M-Area
and Met Lab HWMF. The goal of injection testing was to assess an aerobic co-metabolic
degradation pathway for the remediation of PCE and TCE. However, complications injecting
humate into the subsurface during the pilot-scale field test in Southern Sector highlighted areas
needing further investigation.

Overall slow injection rates and the production of large amounts of foam during mixing and
injection were noted as challenges to the progress of historical M-Area pilot scale testing. Down-
well camera surveys show that injection well screens became clogged with precipitated humate
during the injection event and likely contributed to reduced injection performance. An anti-foaming
agent, Xiameter® Antifoam Emulsion, was introduced in subsequent batches of injectant during
pilot scale testing to combat foam generation.

Supplementary laboratory testing was performed in 2024 and 2025 to evaluate the injection
viability of humate and determine whether continued pilot scale events were recommended. Two
commercially available products containing humate were selected for this testing: 1) Huma-K
procured as dry flakes from Land and Sea Organics, and 2) BorreGro HA-1 liquid humate

 2

produced by Borregaard Biorefineries. Precipitation of humate from solution during injection and
potential injection rates were the focus of 2024 and 2025 experimental testing.

3.0 Humate Injectant Batch Testing

All laboratory testing utilized native LLAZ groundwater to create solutions of simulated,
recirculated injection fluid. Concentrations of humate were varied in multiple testing schemes to
study the effects this may have on injectate storage, mixing procedure practices, and injection
viability for potential future pilot scale and remediation events. Injectant testing results were
analyzed to determine potential causes and solutions to observed challenges during the historical
humate injection pilot scale testing conducted from 2017 to 2020.

Laboratory testing was conducted to closely simulate proposed pilot scale testing and the
conditions seen in the field. Historical and proposed future humate injection system design utilizes
a recirculation method to create and inject amended groundwater. This design uses groundwater
extracted from a designated recovery well, addition of humate amendment to this recovered
groundwater, and then subsequently reinjects the humate-amended groundwater into a second
well. Separate stages of this process were targeted with individual experimentation to investigate
the behavior of injectant and possible humate precipitation at each step.

3.1 Injectant Saturation Testing Methods

Saturation testing experimentation was performed to determine the maximum amount of Huma-
K that could be reliably dissolved into solution for injection. Future humate injection pilot testing
may be conducted with higher concentrations to reduce total injection volumes and achieve the
goal of in-situ humate of 100 mg/L in less time. This test was performed to determine what
injectant concentrations are feasible using dry Huma-K, as well as to define the upper bound of
Huma-K concentrations possible for further experimentation.
Experimental procedures for saturation testing were as follows:
 1. Simulated injection stocks were prepared using groundwater retrieved from well MSB-40.
 Using MSB-40 groundwater as a matrix, humate, the nutrient additive (Miracle-Gro Start
 Planting & Transplant Starting Solution 4-12-4), and anti-foaming agent (Xiameter® AFE
 1510 Antifoam Emulsion) were mixed per January 2019 UIC permit application at the
 following concentrations:
 I.  Approximately 292.39 mg/L Huma-K
 II.  0.23 mg/L Miracle-Gro Start Planting & Transplant Starting Solution 4-12-4
 III.  0.05 mg/L Xiameter® Antifoam Emulsion
 2. While under constant agitation, small aliquots of dry Huma-K flake were added at 3 minute
 intervals to the simulated UIC injectant until incomplete mixing or precipitation was noted.
 3. When precipitation or incomplete mixing was noted, end solutions were passed through a
 100 micron filter to remove any undissolved humate particulate.
 4. Final measurements were measured using ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy at an
 absorbance of 254 nm wavelength to determine the humate concentration in solution.

3.1.1 Injectant Saturation Testing Results

Precipitation during injectant saturation testing was noted at an approximate humate
concentration of 550 mg/L. During saturation testing, small, visible particles of undissolved solids
were first observed at this concentration. These precipitates served as an indicator that additional
Huma-K cannot be fully dissolved in solution. Once these particles were observed, saturation
trials were ended. A photograph of particulate observed in saturation testing is presented in Figure
1.
Huma-K is an unrefined dry material created by a leaching process where alkaline treatments are
used to extract the humic components of Leonardite (a low-grade form of coal). As a result of this
process, impurities exist within the final product. It is possible that the solids seen in saturation
testing are a result of these impurities dropping out of solution and not humate precipitating.
Regardless, the presence of solid particulate in solution is an important concern for its use in
subsurface injection. In high volumes, these particles could likely lead to pore space and well
screen clogging with eventual injection rate reduction.
BorreGro HA-1 humate was not included in injectant saturation testing because it was precured
as a premixed liquid. The obtained stock of BorreGro humate was supplied in a, concentrated (>
20% = > 200,000 mg/L), pre-made liquid solution.

3.2 Injectant Steady State Testing Methods

Steady state testing was performed to investigate the impacts of mixing time, storage, and
filtration methodology on humate injectant stock solutions of varying concentrations and humate

 4

types. The long-term stability of humate injectants provides information for its practical usage
during field scale pilot testing, such as storability and injectant stock mixing requirements. These
factors inform the procedures and necessary equipment needed to optimize injection
performance. The identification of humate settling or precipitation depending on mixing times,
storability, and filtration also provides information on possible best practices for injection to
minimize screen zone and pore volume clogging during future pilot scale events. Testing was
performed to identify noticeably different humate precipitation or settling (if any) according to the
following key variables:

 • Humate concentration: Old stocks of Huma-K, newly procured Huma-K stocks, and
 BorreGro HA-1 solutions of differing concentrations were tested. ‘Old’ Huma-K is material
 that had been stored in the SRS Core Facility since initial pilot scale testing (procured for
 the initial pilot scale injection), while the ‘new’ Huma-K was procured for this testing. Both
 new and old Huma-K were evaluated in this study to ensure storage of the dry flake did
 not impact behavior of the material.
 • Mixing length: Injectant stock solutions were mixed for four different lengths of time.
 • Filtration methodology: Three injectant stock solutions underwent filtration following
 different mixing lengths and one solution was left unfiltered.
All injection stock solutions were prepared with native groundwater retrieved from monitoring well
MSB-40 to match field scale testing as closely as possible. All tests were performed in duplicate,
utilizing identical mixing, filtration, and inspection procedures.
Experimental procedures for steady state testing were as follows:
 1. Injectate stock solutions were prepared in reference to the January 2019 UIC Permit
 Application using the identified maximum saturation concentration. New Huma-K and old
 Huma-K stock solutions were prepared to maximum saturation (550 mg/L), UIC (292
 mg/L), 75% of UIC (219 mg/L), and 50% of UIC (149 mg/L) concentrations of humate.
 BorreGro HA-1 was prepared to the maximum strength concentration only. Each solution
 also received the corresponding percentage of Miracle-Gro and Xiameter™ antifoam
 additive.
 I. Initial humate concentration measurements were collected using a UV-vis
 spectrometer set to 254 nm wavelength and initial pH measurements were also
 made for later comparison.
 II. Each stock solution was separated into eight separate aliquots (four treatment
 conditions each in duplicate) for further testing.
 2. Mixing and filtration procedures were then applied to three of the four stock solutions in
 duplicate (6 of the 8 aliquots) for each humate concentration and each humate type, with
 one pair left unfiltered and mixed only to initial dissolution. Humate precipitation and
 solution pH was monitored periodically throughout testing to determine any appreciable
 differences between sample aliquots.
 I. One duplicate pair was allowed to settle under steady state conditions for the
 entirety of this testing. These samples were designated ‘Non-Filtered Steady State’
 (NFSS).
 II. A second duplicate pair was filtered through a 100-micron filter and allowed to
 settle under stead state conditions for the remainder of this testing. These samples
 were designated ‘Filtered Stead State’ (FSS).

 5

 III.A third duplicate pair was mixed under constant agitation for 24 hours, then filtered
 through a 100-micron filter and allowed to settle under steady state conditions for
 the remainder of testing. These samples were designated ‘Agitated-Filtered
 Steady State’ (AFSS).
 IV. A fourth duplicate pair was mixed under constant agitation for 1 week, then filtered
 through a 100-micron filter and allowed to settle under steady state conditions for
 the remainder of testing. These samples were designated ‘Long Agitated-Filtered
 Steady State’ (LAFSS).
 3. Following four weeks, humate solution pH and humate concentrations were measured to
 determine any quantitative differences were exhibited. Visual inspection for precipitation
 or other characteristics was performed weekly throughout testing.

3.2.1 Injectant Steady State Testing Results

Steady state testing results were assessed to discern if appreciable differences in mixing time,
method, filtration, humate concentration, and humate type impacted the viability for further
injection testing. Differences between treatments were not seen to impact the stability of humate
in solution when stored for prolonged periods of time. Further, there was no discernable pattern
between solution starting concentration and the ability of humate to stay in solution. Following
four weeks of testing, humate concentrations were found to have decreased by an average 7.5
% in solutions prepared with existing Huma-K stocks at a concentration of approximately 500
mg/L and an average of 20.7 % in solutions prepared at approximately 200 mg/L. Humate
concentration decreases of all other solutions ranged between these values without an identifiable
pattern. Measurements of pH changes over the four week period, likewise, show no apparent
pattern with increases and decreases distributed across all experimental results. Steady state
testing results are presented in Table 1. Inspection of any visible precipitate was performed, and
nearly all samples were found to have at least some precipitate detectable by eyesight by the end
of the four week period. Precipitation was found to be moderate, resulting in little impact to the
concentration of humate remaining within the liquid samples (UV-Vis used for humate
quantification). Notably, no precipitate was seen in experiments conducted using BorreGro liquid
humate, further supporting its use over that of Huma-K. A photograph that is representative of
samples that exhibited precipitation is presented in Figure 2.

3.2.2 Supplementary Filtration Testing

Filtration during steady state testing produced no apparent variation in results or measured
precipitation between samples. Filtration of humate solutions with the 100-micron filters utilized
would remove larger, visible particulate and prevented entry into the injection stream where it
could clog pore space within the aquifer. Further filtration was performed to determine if prepared

from simple filtration, therefore the small positive variations in humate content are likely a result

3.3 Effect of pH Testing Methods

The possibility of pore volume and well screen clogging due to the precipitation of humate from
solution is a potential primary cause of the low injection volumes and rates observed during the
historical humate pilot scale testing. Humic acids are considered completely soluble at elevated
pH values, and insoluble at extreme low pH values, but these values can vary based on source
and composition. Experimentation was performed to evaluate the pH value at which precipitation
occurred for available Huma-K stocks and BorreGro HA-1 in solution with LLAZ groundwater. The
determination of this breakpoint for precipitation informs the viability for humate amendment
during future injection events. Should humate precipitate appreciable amounts at intermediate pH
values close to native LLAZ conditions (pH of approximately 5), screen and pore volume clogging
would be a likely barrier to successful subsequent field scale injection events.
Experimental procedures for effect of pH testing were as follows:
 1. Injectant stock solutions were prepared according to the concentrations of the January
 2019 UIC Permit Application. New Huma-K, old Huma-K, and BorreGro humate products
 were prepared to 292 mg/L concentrations with 0.23 mg/L Miracle-Gro Start Planting &
 Transplant and 0.05 mg/L Xiameter™ Antifoam Emulsion (AFE)-1510. Native M-area
 LLAZ groundwater was used as the matrix fluid for this testing.
 2. Aliquots of each humate solution were equally divided into three sets of duplicate
 containers for pH testing.
 3. Initial pH measurements were conducted to collect baseline sample information.
 4. Following starting measurements, 6 M hydrochloric acid (HCl) was added to each sample
 until the desired pH was reached. pH measurements and visual inspection for precipitation
 were recorded continuously during this process.
 5. Once the desired pH of each sample was reached, additions of HCl were ceased and
 each sample was visually inspected for any sign of precipitation.
 6. Each sample was then loosely covered and left overnight, without mixing. Samples were
 then re-inspected for precipitation the following day.

As prepared, all humate injectant samples exhibited pH values greater than those of groundwater
within the LLAZ (approximately 5 pH). In all cases, injectant prepared with Huma-K and BorreGro
were measured to have pH values between 6.5 and 8.5. It can be concluded that injectant pH will
be higher than that of the groundwater within the LLAZ and subsurface conditions are not
expected to increase beyond the maximum injectant pH after introduction. Therefore, this pH was
used to bound ‘effect of pH’ testing parameters. Testing was limited to include pH lowering only
to reflect the expected conditions of future humate injection pilot scale testing.

During pH testing, humate was mixed in larger batches and then those batches were divided to
make duplicate samples. For example, all BorreGro samples and duplicates were mixed at the
same time, within the same container, before being split for testing to ensure that all duplicate
samples were homogenous in content. Following sample splitting, small sequential additions of 6
N HCl were used to lower the pH of each sample to the specified pH range of 2, 3, or 5. During
this procedure, notation of acid volumes were recorded to study the homogeneity (or lack thereof)
in replicated tests. Assessment of averaged percent differences in acid volumes needed to reach
a targeted pH value provide some information on the consistency of formulations within a humate
injectant mixture, independent of the concentration of humate within a sample or the targeted pH
value for each trial. On average, acid volumes needed to reduce the pH of samples were higher
for those prepared with Huma-K than samples prepared with BorreGro liquid humate. Trials with
Huma-K show a great deal of variability in acid volume required. Significant differences in acid
volumes needed to achieve the same pH value when adjusting both new and old Huma-K
solutions suggests there may be a high amount of variability in product composition. This same
variability was not seen in the trials using BorreGro, however. The averaged percent differences
among replicates for acid volume addition for BorreGro trials was only 2.7 %, while that for existing
“old” Huma-K stock trials was 7.4 %, and that for newly acquired Huma-K was 11.5 %. These
results support that BorreGro liquid humate is more homogenous and there is less variability in
the composition of the product. Huma-K is a mixture of humic and fulvic acids, and this
composition may vary from sample to sample as a result of the extraction and manufacturing
process. The extraction process used to generate Huma-K results in the introduction of impurities
in the product that contribute to this heterogeneity. BorreGro appears to be more consistently
manufactured, encouraging its use in field scale injection work.
After pH adjustment to the desired value, samples were left overnight (without any agitation) and
observed the next day to identify if further precipitation would occur. Next day inspections mirrored
results of the previous day, confirming precipitation of humic acids from Huma-K solutions only.

3.4 Humate Injectant Batch Testing Experimentation Discussion

Huma-K saturation experiments determined maximum concentrations of this product that can be
put into solution. Laboratory testing was able to reliably achieve a humate concentration of
approximately 550 mg/L prior to any noticed incomplete mixing or potential precipitation. The
target, in-situ, concentration of humate for future pilot scale injection testing within M-Area of the
SRS is 100 mg/L (SRNS, 2019). Increasing concentrations of injection solution beyond what is
recommended in the UIC permit can help reduce injection volumes needed to successfully create
a subsurface treatment zone. Should Huma-K products be selected for further injection field scale
testing, increasing concentrations up to 500 mg/L can reduce the needed injection volumes to
accomplish the establishment of a subsurface treatment zone. Subsequent soil column testing
was completed, in part, to confirm that there were no physical limitations created by injecting a
higher concentration solution.
Steady state testing results provide data that may be used to identify best practices for future
humate injections. Qualitative observations made during experimentation are also used to inform
procedure and equipment usage recommendations. These observations are as follows:
 • Precipitation was observed in fifteen of the twenty experiments conducted using dry
 Huma-K after full dissolution at the beginning of testing. Precipitates only formed following
 the cessation of agitation, and only within solutions made using Huma-K. It can be
 concluded that humate injectants formed from dry materials, put into solution by the end
 user, may be susceptible to the reformation of solid phase humate particulate or other
 impurities when not under constant agitation. If Huma-K is the chosen humate product for
 further injection, constant mixing is advisable for any injectant to be stored for later
 injection.
 • Filtration during all testing of newly made humate solutions did not result in any substantial
 change in humate concentrations, supporting that all humate is colloidal or fully dissolved
 in solution. There were also no changes in pH observed as a result of filtration. Precipitates
 were not found to accumulate on 100-micron filters immediately following preparation of
 humate solutions. These filters would, however, capture precipitated particulate or un-
 dissolved humate left from initial mixing. If the use of a solid humate is pursued, filter usage
 during field scale injection using Huma-K is recommended to prevent unmixed or potential
 precipitants from entering and injection well to mitigate screen or pore space clogging.
 • BorreGro liquid humate was not found to form any precipitates when mixed to the 2019
 UIC Permit concentration or within more concentrated solutions. These characteristics
 seen during testing indicate that the humic acids within BorreGro liquid humate are more
 stable in solution than user-mixed Huma-K.
 • No significant difference in solution stability was seen that can be attributed to differences
 in mixing procedure, filtration procedure, or mix time length. Qualitatively, precipitation
 amounts were noted to be consistent. Quantitatively, humate measurements show that
 humate concentrations decreased by 7.59 % up to 20.70 % as a result of precipitation,
 with an overall average of 14.8 % loss and standard deviation of 4.6 %.
 • Batch testing results support the viability of using increased concentrations of Huma-K or
 BorreGro within injectant solutions. Results suggest that injection viability would not be
 hindered because of increasing solution concentration. Soil column data were used to
 confirm this conclusion.

 13

Testing focused on precipitation as a result of changing pH shows that BorreGro performed more
consistently than Huma-K. No precipitation or separation was noticed in samples containing
BorreGro during ‘effect of pH’ experiments. In addition, the needed volumes of acid to manipulate
samples with BorreGro liquid humate were the most consistent across duplicated samples and
overall trials. The results observed during pH testing led to the following conclusions:
 • The testing data indicates liquid BorreGro may be more practically used in field scale
 humate injection events than Huma-K flakes (used during initial pilot scale study). The
 consistency of pH manipulation is some evidence that BorreGro is more consistently
 manufactured and/or mixed into solution during laboratory testing. To ensure adequate
 mixing and mitigate unwanted impacts from incomplete dissolution, incorporation of
 constant agitation or mixing into the feed system should be considered if use of the solid
 Huma-K is pursued. Regulation of humate content in injectant will be a key factor in future
 injections to meet subsurface target humate concentrations while optimizing injection
 performance.
 • No separation or precipitation of humic acids was noticed during pH trails conducted with
 BorreGro liquid humate solutions, in contrast to those conducted with Huma-K products.
 This testing indicates that BorreGro liquid humate is more stable over a wider pH range
 than Huma-K. Precipitation of Huma-K from solution was seen to occur at pH values at or
 below 2, which are not expected to be encountered at any point in the solution preparation
 or injection process of a field scale pilot test. Although there is no expectation for injection
 fluids or groundwater within the LLAZ aquifer to reach a pH value near 2, the stability of
 BorreGro in solution with LLAZ groundwater was seen to be greater than that of Huma-K;
 thus, supporting use of this alternative humic acid product in future pilot scale injections.

4.0 Injectant Soil Column Testing

Any future injection work performed with humate solutions would involve large volumes of
injectant passing through LLAZ soils over time spans ranging from days to months. Injectant
would need to pass through aquifer sediments proximal to the injection well and create a treatment
zone around the injection site for an injection campaign to be considered successful. Clogging of
near-injection well sediments with humate could result in pilot scale injection challenges and may
have contributed to the difficulties experienced during the previous injection campaign.
To further investigate the viability of humate injection within the LLAZ, and to assess the impact
of increasing solution concentration beyond that pursued in the current UIC permit, soil column
testing was performed using multiple humate solutions at different concentrations. The
concentrations used for testing were informed by the results of saturation batch testing. Soils
collected during historical drilling events from the LLAZ were used during this testing to simulate
aquifer conditions expected to be encountered during future injection pilot testing. Hydrogeologic
characteristics, such as pore volume and hydraulic conductivity, were measured to evaluate the
impact of humate injectant on these variables during a simulated injection event. The change in
hydraulic conductivity, compared to the volume of injectant passing through LLAZ soils, was the
primary metric assessed during soil column experiments. Any change to the ease with which
fluids flow through soils resulting from injection of humate provides valuable information to the
viability of field scale pilot testing.

 14

4.1 Soil Column Preparation Methods

All soil column testing was completed using native LLAZ soils collected during historical soil boring
events at locations proximal to the proposed future humate injection pilot scale testing site. All
soils subsampled for use in this testing have been stored in the SRS Core Facility since collection.
Soil samples were collected from borings taken at well locations RWM-016, SSM-13, and SSM-
14 at depths between 140 feet (ft) and 160 ft, the approximate depth of the proposed future
humate injection. These locations are within approximately 1000 ft of the proposed future humate
injection site of SSR001. Each soil column test was completed using identical glass soil columns,
peristaltic pumps, tubing, and connections for consistency of results. All soil column tests were
performed in duplicate.
Twenty two total soil columns were prepared and used for testing. Nine we used for three pore
volume testing, ten were used for single pore volume testing, and two were used for initial
experimentation design testing. Following either single or three pore volume tests, the column
was repacked with new soils. No soil columns were used for more than one test.
Soil column testing was completed as follows:
 1. LLAZ soils were lightly disaggregated by hand using a mortar and pestle and then sieved
 to a size of 2 millimeters (mm) to remove any large particles.
 2. Four identical clear glass soil columns were cleaned and prepared for testing. Each
 column was capped on either end with permeable filter stones embedded within each end
 cap.
 3. Soil columns were layered with approximately 2 centimeters (cm) of filter sand (Alfa Aesar,
 0.1 - 0.3 mm), followed by approximately 10 cm of LLAZ soils, and then a second layer of
 2 cm of filter sand. All columns were wet packed using deionized water while following
      U.S. EPA Method 1314 (EPA, 2017).
 4. Soil columns were fully saturated and allowed to equilibrate overnight prior to any further
 testing to ensure the release of any trapped gasses before the start of testing.
 5. Each packed soil column underwent initial constant head permeability testing to derive
 hydraulic conductivity values following overnight equilibration. These hydraulic
 conductivity values were compared among columns to ensure consistent packing, as well
 as compared to field conductivity measurements for M-Area soils to ensure the columns
 were representative of field conditions (Dixon, 2019).
 I. Constant head testing was conducted by gravity feed of water through the packed
 column. Constant head pressure was applied through an infinity pool vessel
 located above the column intake.
 II. Columns were secured to a metal rack and water inflow was introduced to each
 from the top of the column. Flow was allowed to stabilize for five minutes prior to
 outflow volume measurements.
 III. After flow stabilization, outflow volume measurements were collected, and a flow
 rate was determined (mL/sec). This value was then used to calculate hydraulic
 conductivity with the Darcy’s Law equation (Equation 1).

4.3 Single Pore Volume Testing Methods

The recirculation of humate injectant from point of introduction, at an injection well, to the point of
extraction at a recovery well some distance away was used in the previous pilot study injection
scheme (SRNL, 2024). This approach is also proposed for future injection testing. The slow
injection rates documented during historical injection of humate may have been caused by pore
space clogging within the LLAZ sediments. The reduction of pore space in any sediment will
reduce its hydraulic conductivity, which was measurable during single pore volume testing. To
determine the potential impact of humate injection using different humate products and
concentrations, single pore volume testing was conducted to evaluate hydraulic conductivity
before the introduction of humate injectants and following initial contact of LLAZ soils with a
humate injectant.
Experimental procedures for single pore volume testing were as follows:
 1. Injectant stock solutions were prepared for testing using existing Huma-K, new Huma-K,
 and BorreGro liquid humate. Injectant solutions were prepared in two concentrations for
 single pore volume testing:
 I. Injectant solutions in concentrations approximately equal to the 2019 UIC permit
 application of 292 mg/L humate.

 18

 II. Injectant solutions in concentrations of approximately 550 mg/L humate.
 Injectant additives of Miracle-Gro Start Planting & Transplant Starting Solution 4-12-4 and
 Xiameter Antifoam Emulsion (AFE)-1510 were included in injectant solutions at the
 amounts detailed in the 2019 UIC Permit of 0.23 mg/L and 0.05 mg/L respectively. All
 injectant solutions were prepared using a matrix fluid of native LLAZ groundwater.
 2. Following initial hydraulic conductivity measurements, soil columns were positioned and
 connected to a peristaltic pump to regulate a 2 milliliters per minute (mL/min) flow rate of

 3. One pore volume, estimated to be approximately 180 mL from bromide tracer testing, was

 4. Following one pore volume of injectant, a second constant head test was performed to
 measure any change in hydraulic conductivity of each column.

Hydraulic conductivity measurements were performed using identical soil column experimental

wells during proposed field scale testing, should be considered when determining the true impact
of hydraulic conductivity reductions during humate introduction to LLAZ soils and the viability of
future injection planning.
4.4 Three Pore Volume Soil Column Testing Methods

Three pore volume testing was conducted to investigate the effect of differing humate injection
stocks on the sorption of humate to LLAZ sediment. During any proposed further injection events
into the LLAZ, the primary goal is to achieve the desired concentration of humate of 100 mg/L
(SRNS, 2019) within the subsurface treatment zone. Higher injectant concentrations would
directly correlate to reduced injection time, material, and cost for the overall injection event. Three
pore volume tests utilized more concentrated humate solutions to discern some possible
advantages or disadvantages in increasing injectant humate concentrations.
To collect the necessary data, soil columns were completed in duplicate with varied humate
concentrations passing through LLAZ soils contained in each soil column. Three pore volumes of
injectant were passed through each soil column as continuous humate concentration
measurements and volumes were recorded. Humate concentrations at each pore volume, as
compared to stock influent concentrations, allowed for the comparison of humate reaching the
effluent of each column and a percent recovery of humate to be calculated. This recovery
percentage can be used to determine an estimated injection efficiency related to humate
concentration within the injectant.
Experimental procedures for three pore volume testing we as follows:
 1. Injectant stock solutions were prepared for testing using existing Huma-K stocks, new
 Huma-K samples, and BorreGro liquid humate. Injectant solutions were prepared in two
 concentrations for three pore volume testing:
 I. Stock injectant solutions in concentrations approximately equal to the 2019 UIC
 permit application of 292 mg/L humate.
 II. Stock injectant solutions in concentrations of approximately 550 mg/L humate.

 21

 Injectant additives of Miracle-Gro Start Planting & Transplant Starting Solution 4-12-4 and
 Xiameter Antifoam Emulsion (AFE)-1510 were also included in injectant solutions at the

 0.094 mg/L for higher concentration tests, respectively. All injectant solutions were
 prepared using a matrix fluid of LLAZ groundwater. Humate measurements of these stock
 solutions were collected for later comparison to pore volume effluent samples.
 2. Following initial saturation and hydraulic conductivity measurements, soil columns were

 A single pore volume of 180 mL was used based on previous bromide tracer testing,
 resulting in a three pore volume total of 540 mL for each soil column.
 4. Effluent monitoring measurements of humate concentration were taken at each pore
 volume using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer set to a wavelength of 254 nm (Lagos, 2016).

4.4.1 Three Pore Volume Testing Results

Data collected from three pore volume testing provided information useful for assessing further
humate injection viability. Humate concentrations of the initial stock solutions used as soil column
influent were compared to concentrations of humate within effluent samples after moving through
the column at each pore volume during the three pore volume testing. Using this data, differences
between influent and effluent humate concentrations were calculated to determine the percent
change in humate at each pore volume (i.e., how much humate has sorbed to the soil or been
deposited in the column as a function of pore volume). These percentages provide evidence that
certain formulations of humate injectant may perform better during prolonged injection and
recirculation within the LLAZ.
Three pore volume soil column testing was performed for humate injectants using stocks of
existing Huma-K, newly procured samples of Huma-K, and liquid humate BorreGro at humate
concentrations approximate to the 2019 UIC Permit of 292 mg/L. Three pore volume testing was
also conducted using elevated concentrations of humate at approximately 550 mg/L using newly
acquired samples of Huma-K and liquid BorreGro. Testing results show that for each pore volume,
progressively more humate reached the effluent collection point for each soil column, for all
experiments performed. BorreGro liquid humate solution, mixed in the elevated concentration of
approximately 550 mg/L showed the highest percentage of humate within effluent after the second
and third pore volumes at an averaged 48% and 87.2% respectively. BorreGro liquid humate,
mixed at the 2019 UIC Permit concentration of approximately 292 mg/L showed the second
highest concentrations of humate at the second and third pore volumes at 10.1% and 65.8%
respectively. The third highest effluent humate concentrations were collected from mixtures of
elevated humate concentrations of approximately 550 mg/L using newly acquired Huma-K
samples at 6.1% and 61.0% for the second and third pore volumes respectively. Three pore
volume testing data is summarized in Table 5. Photographs of soil columns used in testing are
presented in Figure 5.

4.5 Soil Column Testing Discussion

Proposed future pilot scale testing would involve the extraction of groundwater from the LLAZ
from a designated recovery well, the addition of humate amendment to the extracted groundwater,
and the subsequent injection of the amended groundwater into a nearby injection well. Continuous
operation of this system will recirculate amended groundwater to create a humate treatment zone
within the LLAZ. Single pore volume and three pore volume soil columns were designed to
primarily investigate the viability for sustained humate injection within LLAZ sediments. Testing
replicated anticipated field conditions by using soils obtained from the same aquifer depth
intervals, native groundwater obtained from LLAZ wells within M-Area, and identical humate
supplies proposed for injection use. These soil column trials were separated into single pore
volume and three pore volume testing to investigate the hydrogeologic impact of humate
amendment at the initiation of injection and the advantages or disadvantages of differing humate
amendments for continuous injection.
Single pore volume testing results show that the hydraulic conductivity of LLAZ soils surrounding
the injection site may decrease by an average of 49.4% following a single pore volume of humate
injectant. Results of single pore volume testing conducted with elevated humate concentrations
of 550 mg/L also demonstrated the same trend: decreasing the hydraulic conductivity of the soils
by an averaged 50.7%. When considered together, these data suggest several conclusions:

 • There is no significant advantage or disadvantage to Huma-K or BorreGro liquid humate
 when attempting to preserve the hydraulic conductivity of LLAZ soils during injection.
 • There is no significant disadvantage to increasing humate concentrations to
 approximately 550 mg/L in injectant solution.
 • Humate injection at concentrations of 292 mg/L to 550 mg/L will likely decrease the
 hydraulic conductivity of LLAZ soils by approximately 50% after injection of one pore
 volume of solution.
 • Increasing humate concentrations to 550 mg/L would help reduce the total injection
 volume needed to reach project goals without significant disadvantages in potential pore
 space clogging when compared to UIC concentration humate injectants.
 • Filtration testing shows that humate solutions do not contain high amounts of suspended
 particulate humic acids and that humate content is colloidal or smaller in size. Therefore,
 the reductions in hydraulic conductivity are more likely the result of humate sorption to
 soil grains within the columns creating subsequent pore volume clogging rather than
 larger particle sizes blocking the pore spaces of soils immediately following contact with
 injection fluid.
The primary goal of injection remediation is to create a widespread treatment zone within a
contaminant plume using the least number of injection wells, materials, and time. Efficient
methods for achieving this goal typically involve tailoring injectant solution formulas to reduce
injection volume while increasing the capacity of an injectant to be spread through the aquifer
unit. Three pore volume soil column experiments were designed to investigate if increased
injection efficiency could be achieved by altering the concentration and formula of humate
injectant for proposed field scale testing within the LLAZ at the SRS. While single pore volume
soil column testing evaluated the immediate impact on the hydrogeological property of hydraulic
conductivity during humate injection, three pore volume testing evaluated the ability for more
sustained humate amendment deployment by measuring the humate breakthrough, per pore

 25

volume, of injectant passing through LLAZ soils. Data collected during these tests show a clear
difference in recovered humate percentage between Huma-K products and BorreGro liquid
humate. Of the humate amendment concentrations tested, BorreGro liquid humate solutions
reached higher percentages in soil column effluent fluid after three pore volumes for each
concentration of humate injectant tested (an averaged 26 % higher). Conclusions drawn from
three pore volume testing include:
 • Injectants formulated with BorreGro liquid humate can more readily be spread into the
 wider LLAZ system than those made from solutions prepared from dry Huma-K.
 • Increasing the concentration of humate to at least 550 mg/L would increase the amount
 of amendment spread into aquifer sediments while also reducing the needed injectant
 volume to reach the targeted, in-situ humate concentration.

5.0 Conclusions

Humate injectant testing was divided into two parts: 1) batch testing to assess solution stability
and 2) soil column experiments to simulate injection of the humate solutions. Humate batch
testing was focused on variables important to the preparation, storage, and combination of
humate amendments with LLAZ groundwater. Soil column testing was aimed at the interaction of
simulated injectants when in contact with LLAZ sediments, and its effect on hydraulic conductivity
within the aquifer. These experiments were designed to provide information about humate
injection that have previously not been examined. In doing so, useful data has been obtained to
help inform decision makers on the viability of humate injection within the M-Area LLAZ for
treatment of cVOCs. The data collected during these experiments can be used in planning for
humate injection with different products, concentrations, and equipment/system design.
Humate batch testing simulated humate amended groundwater under different conditions at
varying levels of concentration. Saturation testing was performed to investigate if higher amounts
of humate could be mixed into solution so that overall injection volumes may be reduced.
 • Key conclusions from humate batch testing are: Humate concentrations in solution, using
 either Huma-K or BorreGro products, can be increased from the current UIC concentration
 without precipitation. The highest Huma-K concentration able to be fully dissolved into
 solution with native M-Area groundwater without oversaturation or precipitation was
 approximately 550 mg/L. Notably, BorreGro liquid humate can be provided at even higher
 concentrations than what can be reliably created with Huma-K in standard laboratory
 mixing procedures (up to 20% in solution).
 • Tests conducted by the manipulation of the pH within simulated humate injectant stocks
 show that there is little evidence that in-situ LLAZ conditions cause short term precipitation
 of humate from solution. Only very low pH conditions, not possible during injection into the
 LLAZ, induced immediate precipitation in Huma-K solutions. BorreGro solutions were not
 found to precipitate. Once in solution, testing conducted by manipulating the pH of humate
 amended groundwater showed that Huma-K will start to precipitate at a pH of
 approximately 2, while BorreGro solutions will not immediately precipitate at any pH
 tested.
 • Steady state testing showed that a reduction in measurable humate within solution will
 take place as humate solutions are stored for several weeks at an average decrease of

 26

 14.8 %. Steady state testing also shows that some solid precipitates will form in Huma-K
 solutions during this period, regardless of previous mixing time or filtration.
 • Steady state testing shows that long-term (multi week) storage of humate solutions
 (without agitation) will precipitate over time. Huma-K solutions will form solid precipitates
 within storage vessels if left unmixed. Filtration showed no benefit for sufficiently mixed
 solutions but would filter larger particulate should solutions be improperly mixed or
 precipitates form through some other means. Therefore, filtration is recommended to be
 included in a treatment system design, particularly if use of a solid humic acid (like Huma-
 K) is pursued over a liquid formulation like BorreGro.
Humate soil column experiments investigated the initial contact of humate solutions with LLAZ
sediments and the changes in hydrogeological properties of the LLAZ during injection of different
concentrations of injectant. Three pore volume soil column tests provide data pertaining to
injection viability over longer periods of time and the potential for continued pore volume clogging
using different humate products and concentrations, while single pore volume testing focused
more on immediate physical impacts to humate flow through the sediments. Single pore volume
testing data shows that on average, humate injectants will reduce the hydraulic conductivity of
LLAZ sediments by 49.4 %. These data sets indicate a reduction in conductivity of this magnitude
is probable when using UIC Permit concentrations of humate at 292 mg/L or elevated
concentrations at 550 mg/L. It is possible that the consistency of hydraulic conductivity decreases
is a function of available pore space or the sorption kinetics of the humic acid products to the
sediments. It is unclear from the testing performed which rational may be more likely. In the
context of delivering humate injectant to a wider aquifer zone, with the least volume, soil column
testing does show that higher concentrations (of 550 mg/L humate content) would be more
efficient.
Key Conclusions from humate batch testing are:
 • Humate injectants at concentrations of 292 mg/L to 550 mg/L will reduce the hydraulic
 conductivity of LLAZ sediments by approximately 50 % following a single pore volume of
 injection. This was seen in all testing, conducted using both Huma-K products and
 BorreGro liquid humate.
 • Effluent concentrations of humate as compared to influent stock concentrations show that
 following three pore volumes of injectant, BorreGro liquid humate solutions were able to
 pass appreciably more humate through each soil column. BorreGro injectant solutions
 were able to penetrate soil columns at higher concentrations than Huma-K products
 following three pore volumes of simulated injection. Results were consistent during these
 tests across pore volume and replicates.
Humate solution batch and soil column testing results do show that, overall, BorreGro solutions
are able to remain in solution and penetrate LLAZ soils more readily than those prepared with
Huma-K. This phenomenon shows BorreGro is more suitable for injection but may also indicate
a possible inhibitor to the use of this product. The end goal of humate injection into the LLAZ is to
create an immobile treatment zone for passive remediation of cVOCs. This is achieved by the
sorption of humate to soil particles following the injection into the LLAZ. The apparent stability of
BorreGro in injectant fluid and testing results indicate that this sorption process may be slower or
less efficient than Huma-K. Soil column testing shows that significant portions of the humate
content are remaining in pore spaces, however further experimentation can be done to quantify
sorption between these humate products to ensure that BorreGro would remain within a treatment

 27

zone and not mobilize with natural groundwater flow. Follow-on (FY26) scope will incorporate
additional testing to help address these unknowns.

6.0 Recommendations, Path Forward or Future Work

Several conclusions can be made related to the concentrations of injectant, the products that can
be used for further injection, and the practical handling of injectants during any future injection
events using humate amended groundwater. Recommendations for future injection events based
on testing results are as follows:
 1. Humate concentrations should be increased in future injection events for the LLAZ above
 the previously used 292 mg/L concentrations. Testing results show little difference in
 potential precipitation, little difference in impact to hydraulic conductivity of sediments, and
 advantage to humate spread through the LLAZ at concentrations up to 550 mg/L. Higher
 humate concentrations within injectants can directly correlate to smaller injection volumes
 needed to reach an in-situ targeted concentration of 100 mg/L, as they diffuse throughout
 a recirculated treatment zone. Lessened injection volumes would in turn reduce man hours
 and injection event duration to reach success of future injection events.
 2. BorreGro liquid humate solutions were shown to be more consistent in composition, more
 stable, and can be provided in much higher concentrations in solution than Huma-K.
 Though hydraulic conductivity measurements collected during soil column testing do not
 show a clear advantage of one product over the other for preventing pore space clogging,
 results do show that BorreGro is more easily passed through LLAZ soils than Huma-K.
 SRNL recommends the use of BorreGro in future field scale injection(s), pending positive
 microcosm and sorption testing results in FY26.
 3. Incorporation of a filtering component into a pilot-scale system could be used if long term
 storage of the humate solution is foreseen.
 4. Laboratory-scale sorption tests should be completed using Huma-K and BorreGro to
 quantify sorption efficiency using LLAZ soils and groundwater (planned for FY26). Data
 from such a study would provide useful information to ensure the stability of BorreGro in
 solution would not create a mobile treatment zone.
