Acquiring Knowledge About Adolescents’ Activities: Muslim Mothers and Their Daughters and Sons
Authors: Christopher Daddis, Aaron Metzger, Ayah Said
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionDrawing on social cognitive domain theory, this study investigated how Muslim mothers in the United States acquired knowledge regarding their adolescent children's activities. Despite the importance of parental monitoring in adolescent development, research remains limited regarding how cultural and religious contexts shape these processes in understudied populations like Muslim Americans.MethodsThe sample included 99 Muslim mothers (M age = 41.26) of middle and high school students (M age = 13.87). Mothers reported on their knowledge acquisition across six distinct domains of their children's activities: conventional, conventionally religious, moral, prudential, personal, and multifaceted. They also indicated the degree to which they employed maternal solicitation, voluntary child disclosure, and explicit parental rules to acquire information.ResultsMaternal solicitation was associated with knowledge aggregated across domains. Associations, however, varied by domain; for instance, knowledge of conventionally religious activities relied solely on voluntary disclosure, whereas moral issues were associated with both solicitation and rules, and multifaceted issues were uniquely associated with explicit rules. Significant Gender X Source interactions emerged: for conventional and multifaceted issues, solicitation was the primary knowledge source for sons, while voluntary disclosure was the primary source for daughters regarding conventional issues. Additionally, mothers reported high levels of religious belief, which was positively associated with maternal knowledge; however, domain‐ and gender‐specific patterns remained significant after accounting for religiosity.ConclusionsThe findings underscore the domain‐specific and gendered nature of parental monitoring within Muslim American families. By demonstrating that information‐sharing processes are sensitive to both the type of activity and the child's gender, this study contributes to a more nuanced developmental understanding of how cultural and religious contexts influence adolescent‐parent dynamics.

Muslim Mothers’ Knowledge of About Adolescents’ Activities: Monitoring Muslim Sons and Daughters in the United States

The present study examined how Muslim mothers living in the United States acquire knowledge about their adolescent children's activities and whereabouts when they are not at home. Specifically, we investigated the associations between different sources of information and maternal knowledge, as well as whether these associations varied by child gender and type of activity. To these ends, Muslim mothers rated the extent of their knowledge and their right to know about their sons’ and daughters’ activities and behaviors that were divided into conceptually distinct groups using the social cognitive domain theory (Rote and Smetana 2015; Smetana 2013; Turiel 2006). Mothers were also asked to rate the degree to which they employ various sources to obtain knowledge about their children's lives.

Parental Monitoring

Normative adolescent development is characterized by an increased emphasis on behavioral autonomy (Baudat et al. 2022), accompanied by teens spending more time with peers and less time with their parents (Kerr et al. 2010). As direct supervision becomes less practical, parents must adjust how they gather information about their children's daily experiences (Dishion and McMahon 1998). One result is that parents increasingly depend on adolescents’ own reports to obtain such knowledge (Kerr et al. 2010; Padilla‐Walker and Son 2019). Because autonomy development involves establishing firmer boundaries around the personal domain (authors; Smetana 2011), adolescents may be reluctant to voluntarily disclose information. Accordingly, another response is that parents increasingly employ tracking behaviors like direct solicitation of information or employment of explicit rules that regulate teen behavior (Darling and Tilton‐Weaver 2019). Much of what is known about parental tracking and child disclosure, however, comes from studies of Western, majority‐culture youth in Europe and North America (Ahmad et al. 2015). Therefore, the present study examined how Muslim mothers living in the United States acquire information about their children's activities.

For Muslim parents living in the United States, obtaining information about adolescents’ behavior outside the home may be particularly challenging. As they spend more time away from parental supervision, Muslim teens are challenged to navigate the tension between the autonomy valued in American culture and the communal, family‐oriented norms emphasized in many Muslim households (Aroian et al. 2016). Whereas typical American parents support increasing adolescent independence and individual decision‐making, Muslim parents tend to prioritize family needs and collective obligations over personal preferences (Beitin and Aprahamian 2014). Because adolescent behavior is often viewed as a reflection of the entire family, violations of cultural or religious expectations, particularly those related to parental authority, may carry significant social and moral implications (Carolan et al. 2000; Hodge 2005; Peteet 2005). These contrasting expectations may contribute to discrepancies between what parents believe they have the right to know and what adolescents consider private.

Parental Knowledge

Recent research on adolescent‐parent relationships has focused on the sources through which parents develop knowledge about their adolescents’ daily lives; a shift that was prompted by Stattin and Kerr's (Kerr and Stattin 2003; Stattin and Kerr 2000) distinction between monitoring practices and the development of parental knowledge. Parental knowledge, defined as parents’ awareness of youths’ activities, whereabouts, and peer relationships (Crouter and Head 2002), has been consistently linked to positive adolescent adjustment (Abar et al. 2017; Blocklin et al. 2011; Laird et al. 2008). Because adolescents actively regulate what they share, maternal knowledge is shaped by both adolescents’ disclosure decisions and parents’ efforts to obtain information (Tilton‐Weaver 2014; Marshall et al. 2005).

Work in this area has examined three primary sources of parental knowledge: adolescents’ voluntary disclosure, parental solicitation of information, and behavioral control through rules that limit unsupervised activities (Stattin and Kerr 2000). Importantly, survey measures of these constructs assess behavioral frequency rather than emotional tone or relational context; in the present study, they should therefore be interpreted as indices of how often these processes occur rather than how they are experienced within the parent–adolescent relationship. A robust literature has demonstrated that voluntary disclosure is the strongest predictor of parental knowledge, surpassing both solicitation and behavioral control (Salari and Thorell 2015; Stattin and Kerr 2000; Wang et al. 2013), and disclosure is reliably associated with lower misconduct (Crouter et al. 2005; Deković et al. 2004; Marshall et al. 2005). With age, adolescents increasingly regulate parental access to information through selective disclosure, secrecy, and partial communication (Tilton‐Weaver 2014), altering the balance between parental monitoring efforts and adolescents’ growing autonomy (Keijsers and Poulin 2013). Nonetheless, adolescents disclose more when relationships are marked by warmth, responsiveness, and appropriate behavioral control (Fletcher et al. 2004; Soenens et al. 2006; Waizenhofer et al. 2004).

Interestingly, however, disclosure is not always the sole or strongest predictor of parental knowledge. Waizenhofer et al. (2004) reported that solicitation was more strongly linked to parental knowledge than disclosure, and both can contribute to knowledge across diverse family structures (Padilla‐Walker et al. 2011). Maternal solicitation and rule‐setting have also been linked to greater consistency between mothers’ and teens’ reports (Laird and LaFleur 2016), and solicitation may become a primary source of knowledge when adolescents have less supervision or challenge parental authority (Laird et al. 2010). Accordingly, the links between parental efforts and knowledge likely depend on broader cultural norms and expectations (Laird and Zeringue 2019). Therefore, the present study directly examined associations between maternal knowledge and three sources: child voluntary disclosure, maternal solicitation, and explicit rules.

Domain Differences in Information Management and Parental Knowledge

Within the Social Cognitive Domain Theory (SCDT; Smetana et al. 2009; Turiel 1983, 2002) framework, the growth of adolescent autonomy is often tracked through shifts in beliefs regarding the personal domain, which is a domain of activity viewed as inherently private and beyond the scope of legitimate external regulation (Nucci 1994). Research indicates that, with age, adolescents expand the boundaries of what they consider to be personal, frequently leading to disagreements with parents who may instead categorize those same activities as matters of social convention or safety (Smetana 2025).

Crucially, SCDT suggests that adolescent resistance to parental oversight is not a sign of global defiance but is instead highly domain‐specific (Smetana 2025). While in general, there exists a shared understanding between parents and youth regarding the legitimacy of parental authority over moral issues (centered on harm and welfare) and conventional issues (centered on social norms), adolescents increasingly claim jurisdiction over matters they perceive as personal. Research has consistently demonstrated that the extent to which adolescents disclose information is related to differences in their social judgments of the issues. For instance, Smetana et al. (2006) found that adolescents’ conceptions of the legitimacy of parental authority determined their willingness to share information; when adolescents believed parents have legitimate authority over an issue, they reported a greater obligation to disclose.

SCDT researchers argue that these assessments of authority and obligation to disclose differ significantly by domain. In studies of ethnically diverse youth, researchers have differentiated among prudential issues (issues involving health and safety), personal issues (issues pertaining to individual choice), and multifaceted‐peer issues (overlapping personal and prudential concerns). Adolescents generally feel more obligated to disclose regarding prudential issues and least obligated regarding personal issues, with multifaceted concerns falling in between. This pattern is mirrored in parental perspectives; mothers report wanting to know significantly more about prudential activities than multifaceted or personal activities (Smetana and Rote 2015).

Domain‐specific variations also emerge in the strategies parents choose to employ when attempting to acquire information. In a sample of Muslim families living in the U.S., Daddis (2026) found that mothers were more likely to use active tracking methods like solicitation and rules on prudential and religious issues than on personal and multifaceted issues. In addition, explicit rules were used more often to monitor prudential issues than other types of issues. These findings indicate that the pathways to parental knowledge are highly sensitive to the specific type of activity being monitored.

Acquiring Information in Muslim Families

Islamic religious principles generally emphasize traditional values including integrity, security, obedience, interdependence, and family honor (Triandis 1995). Within families, these values translate into expectations of conformity, respect, and loyalty to parents (Peteet 2005). At the same time, Muslim families are far from uniform; substantial variability in parenting practices has been documented. Although Islamic religious principles may serve as a common foundation, Muslim families represent a wide range of national, cultural, and ethnic backgrounds. For example, families with roots in the Middle East may endorse different cultural norms and parenting practices than those originating from Southeast Asia, such as Indonesia. As a result, shared religious values may be expressed in diverse ways across family contexts. Importantly, heterogeneity exists not only across regions but also within them. For instance, Smetana and Ahmad (2018) documented considerable variability in how Palestinian, Iraqi, and Syrian refugee youth living in Jordan characterized their parents’ characteristic styles, highlighting differences in reported use of authoritative, authoritarian, dismissive, permissive, and punitive approaches despite shared regional context.

Muslim mothers were the focus in the current study because they typically assume primary responsibility for daily childrearing and monitoring (Al‐Mateen and Afzal 2004; Beitin and Aprahamian 2014; Carolan et al. 2000; Kulwicki 2016). They are often held accountable for children's behavior and may experience heightened stress when adolescents violate expectations (Kayyali 2005). Moreover, mothers are central to teaching cultural and religious norms, including expectations surrounding modesty, gender segregation, and adherence to ritual practices.

A growing body of research indicates that Muslim adolescents are strongly encouraged to share information about their lives with parents (Joseph 2005). Specific concerns about privacy within close‐knit communities, however, may inhibit disclosure. Ahmad et al. (2015) found that Muslim adolescents from Palestinian families living in Jordan hesitated to disclose information for fear it would be spread among family networks. Similarly, Azaiza (2005) reported that Muslim 12th graders living in the Middle East tended not to share personal issues or secrets with parents, particularly in domains involving romantic relationships. These patterns underscore the importance of examining multiple sources of parental knowledge beyond voluntary child disclosure, including parental solicitation and behavioral control through the use of explicit rules.

Gender Differences in Information Sharing

Gender also shapes how parents acquire information about adolescent activities. In broader Western samples, girls typically disclose more and respond more openly to parental inquiry, making disclosure a stronger correlate of parental knowledge for daughters (Smetana et al. 2006; Stattin and Kerr 2000), whereas boys are more likely to limit disclosure and rely on strategic information management, increasing the importance of parental solicitation for sons (Frijns et al. 2010; Keijsers et al. 2010). These differences vary across types of information (Smetana et al. 2006) and are strongly shaped by cultural norms rather than universal developmental processes (Laird and Zeringue 2019).

Within Muslim American families, gender distinctions often are even more pronounced. Boys typically receive greater freedom, whereas girls tend to be more closely monitored, are expected to stay closer to home, and are more likely to be restricted from mixed‐gender or unsupervised activities (Aroian et al. 2014; Al‐Mateen and Afzal 2004; Kayyali 2005). Similarly, Azaiza (2005) found that Muslim female adolescents in Israel experienced greater monitoring than males, particularly as they matured physically, with intensified efforts to preserve family honor and close tracking of daughters’ whereabouts.

These gendered patterns also shape adolescents’ engagement with the broader social context. In Dearborn, Michigan, differential treatment contributed to boys assimilating more into American peer culture, while girls experienced stronger anti‐assimilation pressures through intensive parental oversight (Ajrouch 1999). More recent research suggests that daughters are socialized as custodians of religious identity whereas sons are granted greater flexibility, with Arab American women reporting higher ethnic centrality and religious engagement than men (Rahal et al. 2022) and girls’ activities more tightly constrained around the home and religious preservation while boys are encouraged to pursue advancement in broader social contexts (Giuliani et al. 2017). Within a contemporary climate of heightened Islamophobia, Muslim parents also engage in “religious racial socialization” involving intensified monitoring, particularly for daughters (Karam 2025).

Disclosure patterns also differ. Azaiza (2005) reported that Muslim daughters shared less with their parents about personal concerns than did sons. In contrast, Ahmad et al. (2015) found that Palestinian girls perceived higher levels of maternal control and solicitation yet reported greater disclosure than boys, with parent‐driven processes uniquely associated with higher maternal knowledge, suggesting that adolescents may interpret these behaviors as care rather than intrusion. Together, these findings indicate that the pathways to parental knowledge are distinctly gendered within Muslim families.

Present Study

The present study examined how Muslim mothers living in the United States acquired information about their adolescents’ activities and whereabouts. Specifically, we examined associations between three sources (i.e., child disclosure, parental solicitation, and use of explicit rules) and mothers’ self‐reports of their knowledge. Adolescent behaviors were categorized into moral, conventional, prudential, personal, and multifaceted domains (Smetana and Rote 2015); because religion plays a central role in many Muslim families, a conventionally religious domain (e.g., modesty expectations, attending religious services) was also included.

Several hypotheses were generated. Because solicitation becomes especially important when adolescents limit disclosure (Laird et al. 2010; Waizenhofer et al. 2004), we hypothesized that maternal solicitation would be positively associated with knowledge across all domains. Because disclosure is generally the strongest predictor of knowledge for activities centered on adolescents’ own behavior (Crouter et al. 2005; Salari and Thorell 2015; Stattin and Kerr 2000), we expected disclosure to be associated with knowledge for personal and prudential issues. Because explicit rules often regulate activities perceived as risky (Darling and Tilton‐Weaver 2019) and Muslim parents may view moral, prudential, and multifaceted issues as carrying social, religious, or safety consequences (Aroian et al. 2016; Peteet 2005), we hypothesized that rule‐setting would be associated with greater knowledge in these domains. Finally, given gendered patterns of disclosure (Frijns et al. 2010; Smetana et al. 2006) that may be amplified in Muslim families (Aroian et al. 2014; Kayyali 2005), we expected adolescent gender to moderate these pathways, with solicitation playing a stronger role for sons and disclosure for daughters.

Within SCDT, judgments about the legitimacy of parental authority are shaped by broader moral and cultural belief systems (Smetana 2013; Turiel 2006), and mothers who strongly endorse Islamic beliefs may view certain behaviors as more morally or socially consequential (Smetana et al. 2006; Rote and Smetana 2015). Accordingly, we included a measure of Islamic religious beliefs to ensure that observed associations were not attributable to variation in religiosity.

Method

Participants

The sample included n = 99 Muslim mothers ranging in age from 28 to 60 years old (M = 41.26, SD = 5.87), who were recruited from a large suburban Islamic cultural center near a large Midwestern city of the United States. The center serves Muslim families with multi‐cultural and multi‐ethnic backgrounds including Arab and non‐Arab Muslim families. The average age of all adolescents was 13.87 years (SD = 2.62). Fifty mothers reported on a male child and 49 reported on a female child. Fifty‐five percent of the mothers were White (i.e., of European, Middle Eastern, or North African descent), 32% were Asian, 2% were Black, 1% American Indian/Alaskan Native, and 9% did not list their ethnicity. The sample was relatively well educated, with 71% of the sample graduating from 4‐year college and 28% of the sample holding a graduate degree.

Measures

Islamic Religious Beliefs

Participants completed the belief subscale of the Psychological Measure of Islamic Religiousness (PMIR; Abu Raiya et al. 2008), which is an assessment of religiosity that is specific to Islam as a world religion. The two subscales in the measure assessed the dimensions of Practices and Beliefs. The Islamic beliefs subscale was used in the present study and was assessed with a 10‐item scale that asked participants to indicate the degree to which they agreed with basic ethical guidelines including acts that are encouraged (e.g., being humble, respecting parents, etc.) and acts that are discouraged (e.g., eating pork, drinking alcohol, etc.) among Muslims. Participants rated each item on a 5‐point scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) to statements such as, “Islam is the major reason why I am a tolerant person” or “Islam is the major reason why I do not engage in gossip.” Higher scores reflected greater Islamic belief. Internal consistency among belief items was excellent (α = 0.93).

Adolescent Activity and Behaviors Stimulus Items

The same 18 stimulus items, equally divided among moral, personal, conventional, religious conventional, and prudential domains, were used to assess mothers’ self‐reports of child disclosure, parental solicitation, parental rules, and maternal knowledge. Issues were chosen to fit the conceptual definitions of each domain, and many were based on previous research (Nucci 1994; Smetana and Asquith 1994; Daddis and Randolph 2010). The moral issues included teasing or making fun of a classmate, telling a lie, and hitting or kicking a sibling when angry. Conventional issues were using appropriate table manners and politeness, swearing or cursing, and when to do household chores. The religious conventional issues were observing prayer expectations, following religious dress code or modesty rules, and attending religious services. Personal issues included spending own money, listening to favorite music, and spending time with best friends. Prudential issues included eating junk food, smoking cigarettes, and getting bad grades. Finally, multifaceted issues (containing aspects of both personal and conventional or prudential domains), included having a boyfriend or girlfriend, using social media like Facebook, Instagram, or Snapchat, and getting a part‐time job. Each of the three items within each domain were combined to provide six separate mean scores by domain for each variable.

Parental Solicitation

With the parental solicitation scale, participants rated the degree to which they solicit information from their child about each of the 18 stimulus items on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = never ask, 3 = sometimes ask, 5 = always ask). Mean scores were created for each domain of knowledge with higher scores indicating more parental solicitation. Internal consistency ranged from fair to good (α = 0.73, 0.67, 0.78, 0.67, and 0.66 for moral, conventional, religious‐conventional, personal, and prudential domains, respectively). It is important to note that this measure (as well as the rules and disclosure measures) captures the frequency with which mothers ask about adolescents’ activities but does not differentiate the emotional tone (e.g., supportive vs. controlling) or conversational context of these interactions.

Parental Control Through Rules

Participants were asked to indicate the degree to which they maintained and enforced rules about each of the 18 stimulus activities on a 3‐point Likert scale (1 = no rules or restrictions, 2 = some rules and restrictions, 3 = many or very explicit rules and restrictions). Mean scores were created by domain with higher scores indicating more parental solicitation. Internal consistency ranged from fair to good (α = 0.59, 0.64, 0.67, 0.66, 0.76 for moral, conventional, religious‐conventional, personal, and prudential domains, respectively).

Child Voluntary Disclosure

Adapted from Smetana et al. (2006), the disclosure questions asked participants to indicate the extent to which their child voluntarily discloses information about each of the 18 stimulus items to his or her mother. For each item, participants specifically were asked to indicate the “extent to which child usually tell or are willing to tell you, without you asking.” Participants rated each item on a 5‐point scale ranging from 1(never tell) to 5 (always tell) with higher scores indicating increased disclosure. Internal consistency ranged from good to very good (α = 0.85, 0.74, 0.79, 0.70, and 0.80 for moral, conventional, religious‐conventional, personal, and prudential domains, respectively).

Maternal Knowledge

On a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = I know nothing, 3 = I know something, 5 = I know everything), participants indicated degree to which they know about their child's activates in each of the 18 stimulus behaviors. Mean scores were created by domain with higher scores indicating more parental knowledge. Internal consistency ranged from fair to good (α = 0.70, 0.63, 0.71, 0.60, and 0.72 for moral, conventional, religious‐conventional, personal, and prudential domains, respectively).

Procedure

Recruitment took place at an Islamic Cultural Center in a large Midwestern city using flyers and brief verbal announcements. Project staff read a recruitment script to community members after Friday Prayers for five consecutive weekends and to parents during Saturday and Sunday weekend‐school drop‐off and pick‐up.

Recruitment materials invited “Muslim mothers who have a child in middle or high school” to participate. Interested mothers were read the same verbal consent script and encouraged to ask questions before providing consent. After consenting, participants received a card with project staff contact information and the university's Office of Responsible Research Practices. Participants could choose to complete the survey on site or at home. Those completing it on site used a project laptop at the Center; those preferring to participate from home received a flyer with a QR code linking to the online survey and could complete it on a phone, tablet, or computer. Each participant was assigned a unique identification number and reminded that participation was voluntary and that they could skip any questions. Upon survey completion, each participant received a $10 Amazon gift card.

Results

Plan of Analysis

Descriptive analyses are presented first including means, standard deviations, and correlations among relevant study measures. Then, to examine the associations between sources of information management and maternal knowledge, a series of hierarchical multiple regression analyses were conducted. Maternal knowledge served as the dependent variable, and three sources of information management (i.e., maternal solicitation, child voluntary disclosure, and explicit rules) were included as predictors. In each regression model, predictors were entered sequentially in three steps. Adolescent gender was entered in the first step to control for potential gender differences. In the second step, the three sources of information (maternal solicitation, child voluntary disclosure, and explicit rules) were entered simultaneously to assess their unique contributions to maternal knowledge. In the third step, interaction terms between gender and each source of information (Gender X Source) were entered to examine whether associations between sources of information and maternal knowledge differed by adolescent gender. Both correlations and regressions were run using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 31).

To evaluate both general and domain‐specific patterns, one regression model examined maternal knowledge aggregated across all domains. In addition, six separate regression models were conducted to examine domain‐specific maternal knowledge (i.e., religious, moral, conventional, personal, prudential, and multifaceted domains), with corresponding domain‐specific predictors entered at each step. To mitigate Type I error across the six regressions, p‐values were adjusted using the Holm‐Bonferroni sequential step‐down procedure (Holm 1979). This method controls the familywise error rate while maximizing statistical power. When significant interaction effects were identified, simple slope analyses were conducted following procedures outlined by Aiken and West (1991) to further probe the nature of moderation by adolescent gender. Continuous variables were mean centered prior to examining the significance of the simple slopes. Final standardized beta coefficients are reported.

The regressions also demonstrated that associations between sources of information and Muslim mothers’ knowledge varied by domain and revealed interesting and meaningful patterns. First, child disclosure was positively associated with mothers’ knowledge of personal, conventional, and conventionally religious activities, β's = 0.28, 0.32, 0.32; p's = 0.007, 0.002, 0.002, respectively. In contrast, knowledge of moral activities was associated with both active maternal solicitation, β = 0.44, p < 0.001, and the presence of explicit rules β = 0.25, p = 007. Knowledge of prudential activities was associated with maternal solicitation, β = 0.26, p = 0.01 and adolescents’ voluntary disclosure, β = 0.28, p = 0.008. Finally, knowledge of multifaceted issues was only associated with the presence of explicit rules, β = 0.31, p = 0.002, Consistent with prior analyses, no significant main effects for adolescent gender emerged.

First, active solicitation of information about conventional activities like swearing, cursing, and when to do household chores was associated with increased maternal knowledge for sons, but not for daughters. Specifically, post hoc interpretations (Aiken and West 1991) of the slopes predicting maternal knowledge of conventional issues indicated that the simple slope of soliciting information from sons was statistically significant, t(49) = 3.40, p = 0.001. In contrast, voluntary disclosure of information about these same issues was associated with increased maternal knowledge for daughters, t(48) = 3.17, p = 0.002, but not for sons. Finally, when considering multifaceted issues like getting a part‐time job, having a boyfriend or girlfriend, or social media use, maternal solicitation was positively associated with maternal knowledge for sons, t(49) = 4.66, p < 0.001, but not for daughters.

Discussion

While developmental researchers increasingly advocate for the study of diverse samples (Baumann et al. 2015; García Coll 2020), conceptions of “normative” family functioning continue to be grounded largely in middle‐class, Euro‐American experiences (Harkness and Super 2020; Lansford 2021). Despite long‐standing arguments that ethnic groups should be examined on their own terms rather than through a comparative lens (Phinney and Landin 1998), this remains the exception rather than the rule. Furthermore, adolescent‐parent relationships are deeply embedded in cultural contexts that, for many, include significant religious influences (French et al. 2013). To address this gap, the present study examined parental authority beliefs and information sources within a specific sample of Muslim mothers in the U.S. While the foundational work of Stattin and Kerr (2000) prompted extensive research into the interplay between parental knowledge and authority, these processes remain largely unexplored within the context of Muslim families. Broadly, the present findings show both continuity with and divergence from the broader parental monitoring literature. Consistent with prior research, maternal knowledge was associated with active information‐seeking behaviors such as solicitation, supporting the view that parents play an important role in shaping their awareness of adolescents’ lives (e.g., Stattin and Kerr 2000; Laird et al. 2010). At the same time, the domain‐specific and gender‐differentiated patterns observed in this study suggest that these processes are not universal but are instead shaped by culturally meaningful expectations regarding adolescents’ behavior and parental authority.

An important contextual feature of this sample is the uniformly high level of Islamic religious belief. On average, mothers reported very strong endorsement of religious beliefs, indicating that religion represents a salient and organizing component of family life for these mothers. Thus, the present findings should be interpreted within a context in which religious values are not peripheral but instead constitute a shared foundation for parenting goals and expectations regarding adolescents’ behavior.

As hypothesized, the results of the current study demonstrated that maternal knowledge of adolescent activities aggregated across domains was positively associated with maternal solicitation. While a robust body of literature on Western, Euro‐American samples often identifies adolescent voluntary disclosure as a primary correlate of parental knowledge, our findings align with research suggesting that disclosure is not the sole correlate to parental knowledge. Specifically, this study echoes the work of Waizenhofer et al. (2004), who found that solicitation can be uniquely important and, in some cases, more strongly linked to knowledge than disclosure. The importance of solicitation in this sample may reflect the cultural context of Muslim families in the United States. As Muslim adolescents navigate the “dissonant acculturation” (Telzer 2010) between American values of individual autonomy and heritage values of family interdependence, mothers may employ more active tracking behaviors to maintain cultural and religious continuity.

Beyond these behavioral processes, Islamic religious belief was positively associated with mothers’ reports of knowledge, such that mothers who more strongly endorsed religious beliefs reported greater awareness of their adolescents’ activities, even when accounting for disclosure, solicitation, and rule‐setting. This pattern is consistent with prior research indicating that religiosity is closely tied to parenting practices and goals, including heightened emphasis on moral guidance, parental involvement, and responsibility for children's behavior (Rich et al. 2024), and that more religious families tend to report higher levels of parental monitoring and oversight of adolescents’ activities (Quinn and Lewin 2019). One possible interpretation is that religiosity reflects a broader parenting orientation in which attentiveness to children's behavior is prioritized as part of moral and value socialization processes (Flor and Knapp 2001). At the same time, because maternal knowledge was based on self‐report, it is also possible that more religious mothers perceive themselves as more knowledgeable due to stronger beliefs about parental obligation and meaning making within the family (King and Boyatzis 2015). These interpretations are not mutually exclusive and should be examined in future multi‐informant research.

Domain‐Specific Sources of Maternal Knowledge

Consistent with SCDT, the present findings indicate that the processes through which parents acquire knowledge vary systematically across domains. In line with this framework, the results showed that for Muslim mothers in the United States, the pathways to acquiring knowledge about their adolescents’ daily lives are highly dependent on the behavioral domain in question. This pattern aligns with Social Cognitive Domain Theory (Turiel 1983, 2002), which suggests that parents and adolescents distinguish between different types of behavior and that these distinctions shape the boundaries of parental authority (Smetana 2025).

Maternal knowledge of conventionally religious activities (e.g., following modesty rules and attending religious services) was uniquely associated with adolescents’ voluntary disclosure. This suggests that in the realm of religious practice, mothers may rely more on their children's internalized values and spontaneous transparency rather than active questioning or the imposition of rules. Given that Islamic traditions often emphasize integrity and obedience (Triandis 1995), adolescents in these families may feel a particular moral or communal obligation to share information about their religious adherence, making voluntary disclosure the primary mechanism for maternal awareness in this domain.

For activities within the personal and conventional domains, ranging from listening to music to table manners and household chores, maternal knowledge was also associated with adolescent voluntary disclosure. This pattern is consistent with broader developmental literature suggesting that adolescents are more likely to share information about routine, low‐risk, and readily able aspects of their daily lives. Because these activities often occur within the home or in the presence of parents, mothers may have less need to actively track them through solicitation or rules. Instead, adolescents may disclose such information more spontaneously, as these behaviors do not typically require concealment or negotiation over autonomy.

In contrast to domains in which maternal knowledge was primarily associated with adolescents’ voluntary disclosure, moral and multifaceted issues were characterized by a greater reliance on active parental tracking strategies. Maternal knowledge of moral activities was associated with both active maternal solicitation and the use of explicit rules, whereas knowledge of multifaceted activities was solely associated with rules. The pattern observed for moral issues likely reflects the strong consensus between parents and adolescents regarding the legitimacy of parental authority in this domain (Smetana 2011). Because moral behaviors involve clear concerns about harm to others and violations of widely shared ethical standards, mothers may both actively inquire about these behaviors and establish explicit rules to regulate them, resulting in a dual reliance on solicitation and rule‐setting.

By contrast, multifaceted activities were uniquely associated with the use of explicit rules, suggesting a more structured and directive form of regulation. These activities often combine elements of personal choice with concerns about safety and social consequences, which tend to elicit increased parental oversight (Smetana and Villalobos 2009). In many Muslim families, behaviors such as romantic involvement, social media use, or employment may be viewed not only as developmental milestones but also as potential risks to individual well‐being and family reputation. As a result, mothers may rely more heavily on clearly defined expectations and restrictions to manage these complex situations, rather than on conversational strategies such as solicitation or reliance on adolescent disclosure.

Finally, maternal knowledge of prudential issues was associated with a combination of active maternal solicitation and adolescent voluntary disclosure. This pattern suggests that a shared concern for safety and wellbeing contributes to maternal awareness in domains involving health, safety, and well‐being. Prior research indicates that solicitation becomes especially important when parents perceive behaviors as risky (Laird et al. 2010), while disclosure remains a central pathway through which parents gain insight into adolescents’ lives (Tilton‐Weaver 2014). From a social cognitive domain perspective, prudential issues fall within the legitimate scope of parental authority due to their implications for safety and welfare (Smetana and Rote 2015). As a result, adolescents may feel some obligation to disclose, even as mothers actively seek information. Together, this dual pathway reflects a shared recognition of the importance of these issues.

By identifying these distinct domain‐specific patterns, the present findings highlight that Muslim mothers’ monitoring is a nuanced process that shifts dynamically based on the perceived cultural significance and nature of adolescents’ activities. Differentiating behaviors by domain revealed relationships that would have been obscured by the aggregate approach, underscoring that parental knowledge processes are situational and heterogeneous rather than monolithic. More broadly, these results contribute to the parental monitoring literature by demonstrating that links between monitoring strategies and maternal knowledge are not universal but instead vary as a function of the activity being monitored. At the same time, this study advances understanding of Muslim families in the United States by moving beyond simplified characterizations of “strict” parenting. Instead, the findings point to a sophisticated and flexible parenting model in which monitoring strategies are selectively deployed to balance the preservation of religious and cultural values with adolescents’ developing autonomy in a Western context.

Importantly, these domain‐specific patterns remained evident even after accounting for mothers’ level of religious belief. This suggests that, although religiosity provides a meaningful contextual backdrop for parenting, it does not fully explain how mothers acquire knowledge about their adolescents’ lives. Rather, the organization of behavior into distinct domains and the corresponding expectations regarding parental authority appear to guide information‐sharing processes in ways that extend beyond global levels of religious commitment. Taken together, the results indicate that parental knowledge processes among Muslim mothers reflect both general developmental patterns and culturally specific adaptations to the social and religious contexts in which families are embedded.

Adolescent Gender and Sources of Maternal Knowledge

The analyses revealed significant interactions between adolescent gender and the source of information, suggesting that Muslim mothers in the United States employ distinct strategies to acquire knowledge based on whether they are dealing with their sons or daughters. These gender differences emerged in the form of interaction effects rather than overall differences in levels of knowledge. Specifically, maternal solicitation of information regarding conventional activities (e.g., swearing, cursing, completing household chores, etc.) was associated with increased maternal knowledge for sons, but not for daughters. Conversely, voluntary disclosure regarding these same conventional issues was significantly associated with maternal knowledge for daughters, but not for sons. These findings suggest that while mothers may need to actively prompt sons for information about daily norms and expectations, daughters appear to be more spontaneously transparent in these domains.

A similar pattern emerged for multifaceted issues, such as having a part‐time job, social media use, or romantic relationships. For these complex behaviors, parental solicitation was positively associated with maternal knowledge for sons, whereas it was not a significant correlate for daughters. This difference likely reflects the distinct cultural expectations and pressures placed on Islamic sons and daughters, particularly regarding mixed‐gender activities. Research indicates that Muslim girls often have significantly less latitude regarding mixed‐gender interactions and dating. Because these gendered expectations are often explicit and defined early in development, mothers may feel less of a need for daily active solicitation with daughters, as the boundaries for their behavior are already firmly established.

Interestingly, when activities involved potential harm—either to others (i.e., moral domain) or to the self (i.e., prudential domain)—mothers did not seem to differentiate based on child gender. Knowledge of both sons’ and daughters’ moral behavior was similarly linked to maternal solicitation and the use of explicit rules. For prudential issues, such as smoking or academic performance, maternal knowledge was also consistently associated with solicitation. However, unlike moral issues, knowledge in the prudential domain was more closely related to changes in disclosure. Whether a conversation was initiated by the mother's questioning or the child's spontaneous sharing, effective communication in these “risk” domains was the primary driver of maternal awareness.

Ultimately, these findings underscore that parental knowledge processes among Muslim mothers are situational and heterogeneous rather than monolithic. By identifying several distinct pathways of knowledge for sons and daughters across different activities, this study demonstrates that Muslim mothers are not treating all situations the same. Instead, they approach each domain with differing strategies, balancing cultural preservation, safety concerns, and the unique gendered social pressures their children face in a Western context. Notably, these gendered patterns emerged within a sample that was highly religious, further underscoring that variation in information‐sharing processes reflects more than religiosity alone and instead involves domain‐ and gender‐specific expectations.

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

A primary strength of this research is its specific contribution to the literature using a vastly understudied population, not only within the field of parental monitoring but also in broader developmental psychology. Much psychological research samples predominantly White, Christian families, which often results in findings from these specific dynamics being treated as the “ideal” or universal standard. Consequently, differences that emerge in other populations are frequently viewed as deviations from the norm rather than unique, valid processes. In the present study, Muslim families were investigated as their own unit of analysis rather than being compared to Euro‐American benchmarks, allowing for a culturally contextual understanding of normative parenting. To fully understand the diversity of family life, psychological science must move toward this more cohesive and inclusive description of family dynamics.

Additionally, by grounding this research in Social Cognitive Domain Theory, we were able to identify nuanced, domain‐specific associations that an aggregate approach would have obscured. For instance, our finding that mothers rely solely on voluntary disclosure for conventionally religious activities but employ a mix of solicitation and rule‐setting for moral activities, highlights the situational nature of American Muslim maternal monitoring. Our inclusion of gender as a moderating factor also revealed that mothers use different information‐seeking strategies for sons versus daughters. These include actively soliciting information about conventional issues from sons while relying on disclosure from daughters; this further illustrates that these processes are heterogeneous rather than monolithic.

Despite these contributions, several limitations should be noted. First, all data were based on maternal self‐report, and prior research indicates that parents may overestimate their knowledge relative to adolescents’ reports (Laird and De Los Reyes 2013); future research should incorporate multi‐informant designs to provide a more comprehensive perspective. Second, variation in maternal education may be associated with differences in cultural values, including the adoption of more individualistic orientations, which was not directly examined in the present study. A third limitation concerns the lack of contextual specificity in some measures, which may have introduced ambiguity in how mothers interpreted the prompts. for example, behaviors classified as moral (e.g., teasing, lying) and the assessment of “rules” (which could reflect prohibition, obligation, or regulation) may have been understood differently across respondents, potentially contributing to lower reliability scores and variability in responses; as modeled in other SCDT studies, future research should incorporate more contextually detailed or scenario‐based measures. Finally, the relatively small sample size (N = 99) limits generalizability, underscoring the need for larger and more diverse samples to examine how factors like generational status (first vs. second or third generation) and geographic density of the Muslim community influence these monitoring strategies. Such research would clarify whether the observed gender differences in monitoring activities are stable across different cultural backgrounds within the faith.
