When do trajectories matter? Identifiability analysis for stochastic transport phenomena
Matthew J. Simpson, Michael J. Plank

Abstract
Stochastic models of diffusion are routinely used to study dispersal of populations, including populations of animals, plants, seeds and cells. Advances in imaging and field measurement technologies mean that data are often collected across a range of scales, including count data collected across a series of fixed sampling regions to characterize population-level dispersal, as well as individual trajectory data to examine at the motion of individuals within a diffusive population. In this work we consider a lattice-based random walk model and examine the extent to which model parameters can be determined by collecting count data and/or trajectory data. Our analysis combines agent-based stochastic simulations, mean-field partial differential equation approximations, likelihood-based estimation, identifiability analysis, and model-based prediction. These combined tools reveal that working with count data alone can sometimes lead to challenges involving structural non-identifiability that can be alleviated by collecting trajectory data. Furthermore, these tools allow us to explore how different experimental designs impact inferential precision by comparing how different trajectory data collection protocols affects practical identifiability. Open source implementations of all algorithms used in this work are available on GitHub .

Introduction

Random walk models are often used to understand and interpret a range of physical, chemical and biological phenomena. Random walks provide a natural microscopic description of stochastic transport, uncertainty accumulation, and emergence of continuum-scale behaviour. In mathematical finance, random walk models underpin the classical idea that asset prices evolve through unpredictable increments
[
35
]
, and first-passage formulations of random walk models are especially useful for understanding barrier crossing phenomena in a financial context, such as bankruptcy
[
50
]
. In mathematical biology, random walk models are used to describe cell migration, chemotaxis, and dispersal, providing a bridge between individual-level cellular movement mechanisms and population-level outcomes
[
39
,
4
,
14
,
46
]
. A similar framework is used in mathematical ecology to model animal movement and foraging behaviour
[
40
,
24
]
, as well as animal-mediated seed dispersal
[
74
,
37
]
. Random walk models are also used to understand chemical reaction phenomena, providing a clear link between well-mixed stochastic reactions and additional stochasticity introduced when diffusive transport is incorporated along with modelling stochastic reactions
[
22
,
27
]
.

As measurement technologies advance, especially in ecology and cell biology, it is natural for us to seek to understand the extent to which different types of data can be used to characterize random walk processes. This characterization could involve determining model parameters so that solutions of the relevant mathematical model give a plausible explanation of observational data. This process of parameter estimation can be interpreted as providing a means of turning empirical measurements into mechanistic understanding. One of the most fundamental measurements that can be made is to simply count numbers of individuals within a population undergoing some form of stochastic transport process. For example, Figure
1
depicts a schematic snapshot showing the spatial distribution of individuals within a population. In this snapshot it is visually obvious that the number density decreases with
x
. One way to quantify this trend is to place three equally-sized, non-overlapping rectangular regions on the population. Count data can track how numbers of individuals per region changes over time which provides a simple way to characterize the stochastic transport phenomena driving the motion of these individuals
[
55
,
68
]
. The same concept is often used in ecological settings, where field surveys are performed by placing a coarse grid across an area of interest and counting numbers of individuals per grid cell. These grid cells are sometimes called
quadrats
in the ecology literature
[
52
,
66
]
. This procedure provides a quantitative picture about the relative numbers of individuals in different spatial regions. In addition to counting individuals, there is also great interest in tagging some subset of individuals within a population undergoing some form of stochastic transport process and studying properties of the trajectories of those tagged individuals. This notion is illustrated schematically in Figure
1
where we have
tagged
a small number of individuals by colouring them red. Collecting trajectory data would involve tracking the locations of these labelled individuals. In animal ecology it is possible to physically tag individuals within a population
[
45
]
, while in cell biology it is possible to use fluorescent labels to track locations of individuals within a population
[
18
,
19
]
. Despite the growing interest in collecting trajectory data, relatively little is known about data quality and data quantity requirements to infer mechanisms from that trajectory data. One potential reason for this is that trajectory data are known to be highly variable
[
6

.

Trajectory data are often reported in the cell biology literature where experimentalists make observations about the motion of individually-tagged cells within a bulk population. Such measurements can be made in classical two-dimensional (2D) culture systems where cells migrate on a plastic dish
[
9
,
69
,
64

65

, or similar measurements can be made in a three-dimensional (3D) setting, for example by tagging individual cells within 3D tissues and characterizing the resulting trajectories
[
18
,
19
]
. Cell migration in the context of biofilm formation can also involve the interpretation of individually-labelled trajectories
[
5
,

]
. In movement ecology, individual animals can be tracked using GPS, telemetry and radar, while the resulting data are interpreted using random walk models to understand dispersal and spatial spread
[

,

,

]
. This is especially clear in insect dispersal studies, where radar has been used to reconstruct butterfly flight paths in the field
[

, and in collective animal systems such as bird flocks, where 3D trajectory data reveal diffusion-like motion of individuals within the group
[
12

. Together, these systems provide a broad range of biological applications in which one can compare the information content of individual trajectories with population-level observations for estimating transport parameters and testing diffusion-based descriptions.

In this work we are concerned with parameter estimation for trajectory data, and in particular we are interested in the concept of
identifiability

[
29
,
72
,
26
,
54
]
. Identifiability plays a central role in parameter estimation, since it characterizes whether unknown parameters can be determined uniquely from measured outputs. This issue is critical because non-identifiability undermines estimation, interpretation and prediction, yet sometimes this important point can be overlooked. A key standard distinction that we will make use of is made between
structural identifiability
, which is a property of the mathematical model under idealised infinite, noise free data, and
practical identifiability
, which is a joint property of a mathematical model combined with a set of data. Put simply, structural identifiability asks whether unique parameter estimates are possible in principle, whereas practical identifiability asks whether parameter estimates are possible in practice. This work deals with both structural and practical identifiability
[
29
,
72
,
26
,
54
]
.

Standard inference methods for random walk models often rely on simulation-based methods, such as approximate Bayesian computation, which introduce challenges relating to computational requirements and understanding the sufficiency of various summary statistics
[
7
,
32
,
60
]
. Although the use of surrogate deterministic models for inference using count data is relatively well developed
[
7
,
56
]
, comparable techniques for trajectory data are less developed. We address this point by considering a canonical lattice based random walk model describing biased motion. The random walk model involves a carrying capacity parameter that specifies the maximum occupancy of each lattice site. This feature gives us a flexible modeling framework that interpolates between different modeling frameworks without needing to treat those frameworks separately. Using well-controlled simulation data, mean-field partial differential equation (PDE) descriptions of the random walk model
[
14
,
46
]
, and likelihood-based inference and identifiability analysis
[
44
,
60
,
54
]
, we explore the extent to which trajectory data complements more standard count data. Specifically we show that some fundamental problems, such as characterising transport parameters using count data associated with an unbiased motility mechanism leads to a challenge involving structural non-identifiability
[
54
,
29
]
. In contrast, charaterizing transport parameters using trajectory data for biased motility can lead to challenges in practical non-identifiability
[
54
,
29
]
. As we will demonstrate, one way of dealing with non-identifiability is to work with combined data involving pooling count and trajectory data. A key feature of our approach is that all steps in the parameter estimation, parameter identifiability and model-based prediction workflow are performed using coarse-grained PDE descriptions which means that our workflow is computationally tractable, and avoids relying on a working with repeated stochastic simulations.

A major theme of this work is that the transport characteristics of diffusive populations are often measured by counting individuals, giving rise to
count
data, as illustrated in Figure
1
. A standard approach to model this data is to divide these counts by some unknown maximum carrying-capacity count,
\kappa
. This intermediate step converts counts into a non-dimensional quantity,
\rho=N/\kappa
, so that
\rho=1
corresponds to the maximum packing density. Phenomenological continuum models, such as exponential growth, logistic growth, and ecological competition models, are typically formulated in terms of
\rho
without necessarily asking whether
\kappa
can be estimated from the data
[
39
,
62
,
20
,
31
]
. Certainly, in the case of the snapshot shown in Figure
1
, the value of
\kappa
is not obvious from recording the count data. In the current work, we take a more fundamental and practical approach by working directly with count data. After formulating our discrete models in terms of counts, we explore the extent to which trajectory data can assist in estimating
\kappa
and other relevant parameters. As we will illustrate, a surprising outcome is that count data can be insensitive to
\kappa
, whereas trajectory data allows us to estimate
\kappa
reasonably precisely.

2
Mathematical Models

We now outline both the random walk model and briefly derive coarse-grained PDE descriptions of that model. Stochastic simulation data will be compared with solutions of the PDE descriptions to provide confidence in the accuracy of the new PDE models.

2.1
Discrete model

We consider a discrete time random walk model on a 2D square lattice with spacing
\Delta
with time steps of duration
\tau
. Each site can be occupied by at most
\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
agents, meaning that the parameter
\kappa
is a carrying capacity which we treat as a constant, unknown parameter in the model. The approach of introducing a discrete carrying capacity per site is often made in practice
[
15
,
16
,
67
]
, and as we will later explore, introducing the carrying capacity allows us to relate our random walk model to some well known limiting cases. All simulations are non-dimensional in the sense that we set
\Delta=\tau=1
, noting that outcomes of these simulations can be re-dimensioned to match any particular choice of length and time scales by re-scaling
\Delta
and
\tau
appropriately
[
60
]
. We perform simulations on rectangular domain of height
H
and width
W=2L
, so that
0\leq y\leq H
and
-L\leq x\leq L
. Each site is indexed in the usual way
(i,j)
, and is associated with a coordinate in the Cartesian plane
(x,y)
so that
y=(j-1)\Delta
for
j=1,2,3,\ldots J
, and
x=-L+(i-1)\Delta
for
i=1,2,3,\dots I
.

Agent motility is simulated using a random sequential update method
[
13
]
so that during each time step of duration
\tau
, all agents are given an opportunity to move with probability
M\in[0,1]
. A motile agent at
(x,y)
steps to
(x,y\pm\Delta)
with probability
(1\pm\rho_{y})/4
, or to
(x\pm\Delta,y)
with probability
(1\pm\rho_{x})/4
, where
\left|\rho_{x}\right|\leq 1
and
\left|\rho_{y}\right|\leq 1
. Here
\rho_{x}
and
\rho_{y}
are constant bias parameters that control the degree of motility bias, noting that setting
\rho_{x}=\rho_{y}=0
models unbiased motility. The success of potential motility events depends upon the occupancy status of the target site. Let
N_{i,j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
denote the number of agents located at site
(i,j)
. Potential motility events that would place an agent at site
(i,j)
are accepted with probability
(1-N_{i,j}/\kappa)
, and aborted otherwise. This mechanism prevents lattice site from having
N_{i,j}>\kappa
, provided that the initial occupancy of all sites does not exceed
\kappa
.

The discrete framework that we work with is reasonably flexible. Other than specifying the initial configurations of agents, we only need to specify four parameters
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\textrm{d}}=(M,\kappa,\rho_{x},\rho_{y})^{\top}
. Certain choices of
\kappa
mean that the discrete model simplifies to some well-known classes of random walk models. Setting
\kappa=1
means that the random walk model simplifies to an
exclusion process
which is a kind of random walk that is often implemented for applications where crowding effects are important
[
2
,
8
,
10
,
17
,
34
,
38
,
42
,
71
]
. In contrast, setting
\kappa
to be sufficiently large means that sites can be occupied by a many agents, and the random walk model simplifies to a biased Brownian motion in the limit
\kappa\to\infty

[
23
,
28
,
47
,
53
,
63
]
. For intermediate values of
\kappa
the discrete model interpolates between these two well-known classes of random walk models.

Typical simulation data are presented in Figure
2
, showing outcomes for unbiased (
\rho_{x}=\rho_{y}=0
) and biased
(\rho_{x}>0,\rho_{y}=0)
motility. This simulation is conducted on a lattice with
I=200
and
J=50
, with no flux boundary conditions imposed along all boundaries. Simulations are initialized by placing 10 agents per site for all sites with
|x|\leq 10
, and leaving all remaining lattice sites vacant, as shown in Figure
2
(a)–(b). This choice of initial condition and boundary condition mean that macroscopic gradients in the vertical
y
-direction vanish
[
10
,
30
,
60
]
for
t\geq 0
. The system evolves so that macroscopic gradients and population-level transport occurs in the horizontal,
x
-direction. This geometric simplification is both mathematically convenient and often adopted in cell biology experiments
[
10
,
59
]
. Simulation results in Figure
2
(c)-(d) show discrete occupancies after
600
time steps, where it is clear that the unbiased simulation leads to symmetric spreading about
x=0
whereas the biased simulation leads to an asymmetric distribution of agents, as expected.

2.2
Surrogate continuum model: Count data

Instead of relying on repeated stochastic simulations, the random walk model is related to a PDE whose solution approximately describes the evolution of the mean behaviour of the random walk model
[
14
,
46
]
. To proceed to the continuum limit description we denote the average occupancy of site
(i,j)
at time
t
by
\langle N_{i,j}\rangle\in[0,\kappa]
, and note that this average is constructed by considering a sufficiently large number of identically prepared realizations of the discrete model. With this framework we propose an approximate discrete conservation statement describing the expected change in occupancy in site
(i,j)
during a time step of duration
\tau
,

\displaystyle\delta\langle N_{i,j}(t)\rangle\ =\dfrac{M}{4}\left[\langle N_{i-1,j}(t)\rangle\left(1+\rho_{x}\right)+\langle N_{i+1,j}(t)\rangle\left(1-\rho_{x}\right)\right]\left[1-\dfrac{\langle N_{i,j}(t)\rangle}{\kappa}\right]

\displaystyle+\dfrac{M}{4}\left[\langle N_{i,j-1}(t)\rangle\left(1+\rho_{y}\right)+\langle N_{i,j+1}(t)\rangle\left(1-\rho_{y}\right)\right]\left[1-\dfrac{\langle N_{i,j}(t)\rangle}{\kappa}\right]

\displaystyle-\dfrac{M}{4}\langle N_{i,j}(t)\rangle\left(1+\rho_{x}\right)\left[1-\dfrac{\langle N_{i+1,j}(t)\rangle}{\kappa}\right]-\dfrac{M}{4}\langle N_{i,j}(t)\rangle\left(1-\rho_{x}\right)\left[1-\dfrac{\langle N_{i-1,j}(t)\rangle}{\kappa}\right]

\displaystyle-\dfrac{M}{4}\langle N_{i,j}(t)\rangle\left(1+\rho_{y}\right)\left[1-\dfrac{\langle N_{i,j+1}(t)\rangle}{\kappa}\right]-\dfrac{M}{4}\langle N_{i,j}(t)\rangle\left(1-\rho_{y}\right)\left[1-\dfrac{\langle N_{i,j-1}(t)\rangle}{\kappa}\right].

(1)

Positive terms on the right of Equation (
1
) describe events that would place agents onto site
(i,j)
, while negative terms on the right describe events that remove agents from site
(i,j)
. For example, the first term on the right right of Equation (
1
) describes the movement of agents from site
(i-1,j)
to site
(i,j)
under the action of a bias in the positive
x
-direction,
\rho_{x}
. This term is proportional to: (i) the probability that site
(i-i,j)
is occupied
\langle N_{i-1,j}\rangle/\kappa
; (ii)
M
, the probability that an individual agent will attempt to move during the time step of duration
\tau
; (iii) the probability that the movement will be in the positive
x
-direction
(1+\rho_{x})/4
; and (iv) the probability that the occupancy status of the target site will accept the potential movement event,
(1-\langle N_{i,j}\rangle/\kappa)
. Taking the product of these various probabilities and treating the resulting term as being proportional to a net transition probability implies that the occupancy status of lattice sites are independent. While this approximation is clearly questionable for any single event, it has been long established that this standard mean-field approximation turns out to be accurate under a wide range of conditions
[
3
,
21
]
. We will provide evidence to support this observation later.

To proceed to the continuum limit we divide both sides of Equation (
1
) by
\tau
and identify the discrete variable
\langle N_{i,j}\rangle
with the continuous variable
N(x,y,t)
. We then expand all terms in Equation (
1
) in truncated Taylor series about the point
(x,y)
, neglecting terms of order
\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{3})
. The resulting PDE for
N(x,y,t)
can be written as

\frac{\partial N}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathcal{\mathbf{J}}=0,

(2)

where
\mathbf{J}=\left(\mathcal{J}_{x},\mathcal{J}_{y}\right)
is the flux, whose components are given by

\mathcal{J}_{x}=-D\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x}+v_{x}N\left(1-\dfrac{N}{K}\right),\quad\mathcal{J}_{y}=-D\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial y}+v_{y}N\left(1-\dfrac{N}{K}\right),

where the carrying capacity
K
, diffusivity
D
, and components of the drift velocity
\mathbf{v}=(v_{x},v_{y})
, are given by

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Delta\to 0\\
\tau\to 0\end{subarray}}\left(\kappa\right),\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\ D=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Delta\to 0\\
\tau\to 0\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{M\Delta^{2}}{4\tau}\right),

(3)

\displaystyle v_{x}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Delta\to 0\\
\tau\to 0\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{M\rho_{x}\Delta}{2\tau}\right),\quad v_{y}=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Delta\to 0\\
\tau\to 0\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{M\rho_{y}\Delta}{2\tau}\right).

(4)

To obtain a well-defined continuum limit we require the ratio
\Delta^{2}/\tau
to remain
\mathcal{O}(1)
in the limit
\Delta\to 0
and
\tau\to 0
. When the motility is biased we require
\rho_{x}=\mathcal{O}(\Delta)
and
\rho_{y}=\mathcal{O}(\Delta)
to keep
v_{x}=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
v_{y}=\mathcal{O}(1)
as
\Delta\to 0
and
\tau\to 0

[
14
,
46
]
. In practice this means that the continuum limit is valid for sufficiently small motility bias, however in practice we find that the solution of the PDE maintains reasonable accuracy even when the motility is maximally biased with
\rho_{x}=\pm 1
and/or
\rho_{y}=\pm 1
. Some interpretation is required when we relate the discrete and continuum carrying capacity parameters since
\kappa
is a positive integer while
K
is a continuous parameter, meaning that the interpretation of the statement
\displaystyle{K=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Delta\to 0\\
\tau\to 0\end{subarray}}\left(\kappa\right)}
is nuanced.

The continuum-limit description for
N(x,y,t)
is given by Equation (
2
). This general model is valid for
K>0
, and simplifies to some well-known cases for limiting values
K
. For
K=1
the continuum limit description simplifies to Equation (
2
) with

\mathcal{J}_{x}=-D\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x}+v_{x}N\left(1-N\right),\quad\mathcal{J}_{y}=-D\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial y}+v_{y}N\left(1-N\right),

which is the well-known continuum limit description for exclusion process random walk models where lattice sites can be occupied by no more than one agent
[
46
]
. In the limit
K\to\infty
, the continuum limit description simplifies to Equation (
2
) with

\mathcal{J}_{x}=-D\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x}+v_{x}N,\quad\mathcal{J}_{y}=-D\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial y}+v_{y}N,

which is the well-known continuum limit description for a biased Brownian random walk model where the occupancy of lattice sites is unlimited
[
14
]
.

2.3
Surrogate continuum model: Trajectory data

We now describe how to obtain a macroscopic model describing the motion of individual agents within the population by supposing that one of the individual agents in the population is
tagged
at time
t=0
and its location tracked through time
[
48
]
. Let
P(x,y,t)
denote the PDF for the agent’s location at time
t
. To begin, let
P_{i,j}(t)
denote the probability that the tagged agent is located at lattice site
(i,j)
at time
t
. As previously, let
\langle N_{i,j}(t)\rangle
denote the expected number of agents at site
(i,j)
. With this framework we may write a discrete conservation equation for the expected change in
\langle P_{i,j}(t)\rangle
during a time step of duration
\tau
,

\displaystyle\delta\langle P_{i,j}(t)\rangle\ =\dfrac{M}{4}\left[\langle P_{i-1,j}(t)\rangle\left(1+\rho_{x}\right)+\langle P_{i+1,j}(t)\rangle\left(1-\rho_{x}\right)\right]\left[1-\dfrac{\langle N_{i,j}(t)\rangle}{\kappa}\right]

\displaystyle+\dfrac{M}{4}\left[\langle P_{i,j-1}(t)\rangle\left(1+\rho_{y}\right)+\langle P_{i,j+1}(t)\rangle\left(1-\rho_{y}\right)\right]\left[1-\dfrac{\langle N_{i,j}(t)\rangle}{\kappa}\right]

\displaystyle-\dfrac{M}{4}\langle P_{i,j}(t)\rangle\left(1+\rho_{x}\right)\left[1-\dfrac{\langle N_{i+1,j}(t)\rangle}{\kappa}\right]-\dfrac{M}{4}\langle P_{i,j}(t)\rangle\left(1-\rho_{x}\right)\left[1-\dfrac{\langle N_{i-1,j}(t)\rangle}{\kappa}\right]

\displaystyle-\dfrac{M}{4}\langle P_{i,j}(t)\rangle\left(1+\rho_{y}\right)\left[1-\dfrac{\langle N_{i,j+1}(t)\rangle}{\kappa}\right]-\dfrac{M}{4}\langle P_{i,j}(t)\rangle\left(1-\rho_{y}\right)\left[1-\dfrac{\langle N_{i,j-1}(t)\rangle}{\kappa}\right]

(5)

Like the derivation of the PDE for
N(x,y,t)
, Equation (
5
) invokes a standard mean-field approximation by assuming the occupancy status of adjacent lattice sites are independent
[
3
,
21
]
. To proceed we divide both sides of Equation (
5
) by
\tau
, and identify discrete quantities
\langle P_{i,j}(t)\rangle
and
\langle N_{i,j}(t)\rangle
with smooth functions
P(x,y,t)
and
N(x,y,t)
, respectively. Expanding terms of the form
\langle N_{i\pm 1,j}(t)\rangle
,
\langle N_{i,j\pm 1}(t)\rangle
,
\langle P_{i\pm 1,j}(t)\rangle
and
\langle P_{i,j\pm 1}(t)\rangle
in truncated Taylor series, and taking the limit as
\Delta\to 0
and
\tau\to 0
simultaneously with the ratio
\Delta^{2}/\tau

\mathcal{O}(1)
, we obtain

\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathcal{\mathbf{J}}=0,

(6)

where the components of the flux
\mathbf{J}=\left(\mathcal{J}_{x},\mathcal{J}_{y}\right)
are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{x}

\displaystyle=-D\left(1-\dfrac{N}{K}\right)\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial x}-D\dfrac{P}{K}\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x}+v_{x}P\left(1-\dfrac{N}{K}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{y}

\displaystyle=-D\left(1-\dfrac{N}{K}\right)\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}-D\dfrac{P}{K}\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial y}+v_{y}P\left(1-\dfrac{N}{K}\right).

The continuum-limit description for
P(x,y,t)
is given by Equation (
6
), and is valid for
K>0
. As for Equation (
2
), this general model simplifies for special values of
K
. With
K=1
, the continuum limit PDE simplifies to Equation (
6
) with

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{x}

\displaystyle=-D\left(1-N\right)\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial x}-DP\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x}+v_{x}P\left(1-N\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{y}

\displaystyle=-D\left(1-N\right)\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}-DP\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial y}+v_{y}P\left(1-N\right).

which recovers the recently-derived result for an exclusion process
[
48
]
. In contrast, as
K\to\infty
the continuum limit description simplifies to Equation (
6
) with

\mathcal{J}_{x}=-D\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial x}+v_{x}P,\quad\mathcal{J}_{y}=-D\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}+v_{y}P.

which means that the evolution equation for
P(x,y,t)
is the same as the evolution equation for
N(x,y,t)
when there is no crowding effects.

The new PDE model for
P(x,y,t)
models the evolution of the PDF of a subset of tagged agents within the bulk population. This is conceptually similar to previous PDE descriptions that track different interacting subpopulations within the same bulk population
[
56
]
, but there are key differences. For example, previous PDE descriptions of various interacting subpopulations do not model trajectory data
[
56
]
, and the dependent variables in these multispecies models have very different meanings. The dependent variable for PDE models in terms of count data models the evolution of
N(x,y,t)\in[0,K]
since the model is formulated so that that there is a maximum carrying capacity allowed in the discrete model. In contrast, PDE models for the PDF of tagged agents models the evolution of
P(x,y,t)\in[0,\infty)
such that
\displaystyle{\int_{-L}^{L}\int_{0}^{H}P(x,y,t)\,\textrm{d}y\,\textrm{d}x=1}
, since
P(x,y,t)
is an unbounded probability density function.

2.4
Simplified surrogate continuum models in one-dimension

As in Figure
2
, in this work we consider simulations where individuals are free to move in any direction on the lattice, however the initial condition and boundary conditions mean that macroscopic gradients in the vertical direction vanish. Under these conditions the PDE models for
N(x,y,t)
and
P(x,y,t)
models simplify to

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(-D\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x}+v_{x}N\left[1-\dfrac{N}{K}\right]\right),

(7)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(-D\left[1-\dfrac{N}{K}\right]\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial x}-D\dfrac{P}{K}\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x}+v_{x}P\left[1-\dfrac{N}{K}\right]\right),

(8)

for
N(x,t)
and
P(x,t)

and

(9)

3
Inference and Identifiability

Given our efficient PDE-based surrogates for interpreting discrete count and trajectory data, we now wish to understand the extent to which parameters
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(D,v,K)^{\top}
can be reliably estimated from different types of data. We will consider a likelihood-based approach for parameter estimation, identifiability analysis and model-based prediction
[
29
,
54
,
11
]
. In particular, our focus will be to compare the performance of count data and/or trajectory data.

For count data, suppose we have counts of agents per column
\mathcal{N}_{i}(t)
at a single inspection time
t=T
, such as the data in Figure
3
(c)–(d). Given a solution of our PDE for
N(x,t)
, obtained with parameters
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, we compute the quantity
u(x,t)=N(x,t)/K\in[0,1]
. We can interpret
u(x,t)
as the average occupancy of lattice sites in the column located at
x
, relative to their maximum occupancy
K
, noting that
K
is an unknown parameter to be estimated. In this setting it is natural to assume that observed column counts are drawn from a binomial distribution so that for the
i
th column we have
[
59
]
,

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}_{i}(t)\;|\;\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\binom{KH}{\mathcal{N}_{i}(t)}u(x_{i},t)^{\mathcal{N}_{i}(t)}\left(1-u(x_{i},t)\right)^{KH-\mathcal{N}_{i}(t)},

(10)

which has support on
\mathcal{N}_{i}(t)\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,HK\}
, thereby accounting for the fact that counts of agents per column are always non-negative and bounded above by
KH
. This distribution is associated with the loglikelihood function

\ell_{i}^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathcal{N}_{i}(t))=\log\binom{KH}{\mathcal{N}_{i}(t)}+\mathcal{N}_{i}(t)\log\left(u(x_{i},t)\right)+\left(KH-\mathcal{N}_{i}(t)\right)\log\left(1-u(x_{i},t)\right),

(11)

where the superscript ‘c’ indicates that this loglikelihood function arises from
count
data. This kind of loglikelihood function for count data has been used previously for parameter estimation for data from lattice-based random walk models
[
60
]
and for modelling experimental data
[
59
]
where the carrying capacity parameter was treated as a known quantity that was pre-estimated. Under these circumstances, the binomial coefficient term did not need to be included in the calculation of the loglikelihood since it acts like a constant factor that is independent of
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. In this work we are interested in estimating
K
so we need to explicitly retain the binomial coefficient in our calculations.

If we collect count data along all columns of the lattice we have
\mathcal{N}_{i}(t)
for
i=1,2,3,\ldots,I
. Treating these column counts as independent, we have a loglikelihood of the following form

\ell^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))=\sum_{i=1}^{I}\ell_{i}^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathcal{N}_{i}(t)),

(12)

where
\mathbf{N}(t)
indicates the set of count data,
\mathbf{N}(t)=\left(\mathcal{N}_{1}(t),\mathcal{N}_{2}(t),\ldots,\mathcal{N}_{I}(t)\right)^{\top}
. Given the count-based loglikelihood function
\ell^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))
we may now perform likelihood-based estimation and identifiability analysis to understand the extent to which counts can be used to recover macroscopic parameters. For computational purposes we evaluate
\ell^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))
using the log gamma function
[
1
]
which brings two advantages: (i) the log gamma function avoids underflow/overflow in floating point arithmetic that can arise when computing factorials of large numbers; and (ii) the log gamma function means that we can evaluate the loglikelihood for non-integer values of the carrying capacity parameter, which is useful since Equation (
7
) treats
K
as a continuous variable.

In addition to working with count data, we also collect trajectory data with a view to performing likelihood-based estimation and identifiability analysis. Supposing we tag
S
agents at
t=0
, just like in Figure
2
(e)–(h), where each tagged agent is initially located in the
i
th column of the lattice. A simulation is performed until the inspection time
t=T
, and the horizontal coordinate of the location of each tagged agent
x_{s}
is recorded for
s=1,2,3,\ldots,S
. With our numerical solution of
P(x,t)
we have a trajectory-based loglikelihood for the location of the
s
th agent

\ell_{s}^{\textrm{\,t}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;x_{s}(t))=\log\left(P(x_{s},t)\right),

(13)

where
P(x,t)
is the solution to Equation (
6
) and the superscript ‘t’ indicates that this loglikelihood function arises from
trajectory
data. For a collection of independent trajectories we have

\ell^{\textrm{\,t}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{x}(t))=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\ell_{s}^{\textrm{\,t}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;x_{s}(t)),

(14)

where
\mathbf{x}(t)
indicates the set of trajectories,
\mathbf{x}(t)=\left(x_{1}(t),x_{2}(t),\ldots,x_{S}(t)\right)^{\top}
.

For the purposes of estimation and identifiability analysis we will use both
\ell^{\textrm{\,t}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{x}(t))
and
\ell^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))
in the same way. In the general case where movement could be biased, we will aim to estimate three parameters,
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(D,v,K)^{\top}
. In the special case where movement is unbiased and
\rho_{x}=v=0
, we will estimate just two parameters,
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(D,K)^{\top}
. We now describe our workflow for estimation and identifiability analysis in terms of
\ell^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))
for three parameters
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(D,v,K)^{\top}
, and note that the computational workflow applies in the same way when we work with
\ell^{\textrm{\,t}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{x}(t))
. Given a set of observation data, we evaluate
\ell^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))
across a broad region of parameter space that contains the true parameter values. For example, if we consider the simulation data in Figure
2
(d) where
M=1
,
\rho=0.1
and
\kappa=10
, the true PDE parameters are
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(D,v,K)^{\top}=(0.25,0.05,10)^{\top}
. Under these circumstances we can conservatively choose to explore the region
0.20\leq D\leq 0.30
,
0.04\leq v\leq 0.06
and
8\leq K\leq 12
. Since we work with a simple three-dimensional parameter space we explore different parameter combinations by taking a uniform
25\times 25\times 25
regular discretization of the parameter space and evaluate
\ell^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))
at each of the
25^{3}
parameter combinations defined by the uniform discretization. We use indices
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\gamma
to denote the uniform parameter discretization, so that each point in the discretised three-dimensional parameter space is given by
(D_{\alpha},v_{\beta},K_{\gamma})^{\top}
for
\alpha,\beta,\gamma=1,2,3,\ldots,25
. The maximum value of
\ell^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))
over the
25^{3}
mesh points provides an approximation of the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE)

\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left[\ell^{\mathrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\mathbf{N}(t))\right].

(15)

Given the MLE, the normalised loglikelihood is defined as

\bar{\ell}^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))=\ell^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))-\ell^{\textrm{\,c}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t)),

(16)

so that
\bar{\ell}^{\textrm{\,c}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))=0
.

To assess the identifiability of each parameter we visualize and quantify the shape of the loglikelihood function in one of two ways. First, for problems involving just two unknown parameters, such as
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(D,K)^{\top}
when we consider unbiased motility, we simply plot
\bar{\ell}^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))
as a heat map or contour map to provide a simple visualization of how peaked the loglikelihood function in the region nearby the MLE,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

[
54
]
. Going beyond visualization, we invoke Wilks’ theorem
[
73
,
51
]
to identify a threshold value of the loglikelihood associated with the 95% parameter confidence set,
\bar{\ell}^{*}=-\Delta_{p,q}/2
where
p
is the quantile of the
\chi^{2}
distribution and
q
is the number of degrees of freedom. With two unknown parameters, we have
\bar{\ell}^{*}=-\Delta_{0.95,2}/2=-2.996
. Superimposing this threshold contour on the loglikelihood function allows us to visualize the extent of the 95% confidence set of parameters for which
\bar{\ell}\geq\bar{\ell}^{*}

[
54
]
. When parameters are well-identified by the data we obtain a loglikelihood function with a unique MLE and a relatively constrained confidence set where
\bar{\ell}\geq\bar{\ell}^{*}
. In contrast, poorly identified and non-identifiable problems are characterised by a poorly defined MLE that is potentially non-unique, and a relatively broad 95% parameter confidence set, and/or strong correlation between estimates of different parameters
[
58
]
. We will show examples of both cases as we proceed.

For problems that involve three parameters, e.g.
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(D,v,K)^{\top}
when we consider biased motility, we are no longer able to simply visualize
\bar{\ell}^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))
as a two-dimensional heat map or contour plot. In these cases we construct a series of profile likelihood functions by partitioning
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
into interest parameters
\boldsymbol{\psi}
, and nuisance parameters
\boldsymbol{\omega}
, so that
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\omega})^{\top}

[
44
,
29
,
54
]
. For a particular set of data the profile loglikelihood for the interest parameter(s)
\boldsymbol{\psi}
is

\bar{\ell}^{\textrm{\,c}}_{\textrm{p}}\left(\boldsymbol{\psi}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t)\right)=\max_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\;|\;\boldsymbol{\psi}}\;\left[\bar{\ell}^{\textrm{\,c}}(\psi,\omega\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))\right],

(17)

which defines an implicit function
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\psi})
that gives optimal values of
\boldsymbol{\omega}
for each value of
\boldsymbol{\psi}
. For problems involving three parameters, e.g.
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(D,v,K)^{\top}
, we construct and visualize three bivariate profile loglikelihood functions by setting: (i)
\boldsymbol{\psi}=(D,v)^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}=K
; (ii)
\boldsymbol{\psi}=(D,K)^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}=v
; and (iii)
\boldsymbol{\psi}=(v,K)^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}=D
. Superimposing the same threshold
\bar{\ell}^{*}=-\Delta_{0.95,2}/2=-2.996
reveals the pairwise parameter confidence sets, and the shape of the confidence region provides additional information about the extent to which pairs of parameters are correlated. The three bivariate profile loglikelihood functions also provide important qualitative and quantitative information about parameter identifiability by defining and visualising the extent of the 95% confidence set. In this work we focus on using the profile likelihood to assess practical identifiability since this approach is widely known to provide reliable insight
[
72
,
26
,
36
]
. While many implementations of profile likelihood methods tend to focus on univariate profile likelihood functions where
\boldsymbol{\psi}
is a scalar. Here we focus on bivariate profile likelihood functions since these functions, just like univariate profile likelihood functions, can be visualised very easily. Unlike univariate profile likelihoods, bivariate profile likelihoods provide information about correlation, or lack thereof, between pairs of parameters.

Evaluating
\bar{\ell}^{\textrm{\,c}}_{\textrm{p}}\left(\boldsymbol{\psi}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t)\right)
is straightforward because we have already evaluated
\bar{\ell}^{\textrm{\,c}}_{\textrm{p}}\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t)\right)
on a
25^{3}
uniform discretization of
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. To evaluate the bivariate profile loglikelihood for
\boldsymbol{\psi}=(D,v)^{\top}
we consider each pair of
(D_{\alpha},v_{\beta})
values on the discretization of
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and compute the index
\gamma
that maximises
\bar{\ell}^{\textrm{\,c}}_{\textrm{p}}\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t)\right)
on the uniform discretization of
(D_{\alpha},v_{\beta},K_{\gamma})
for fixed values of
D_{\alpha}
and
v_{\beta}
. This reduces the problem to a 1D search along a single fibre of the three-dimensional array. While it is possible to use numerical optimization to maximize these functions
[
54
]
, our approach of taking a uniform discretization of
\bar{\ell}^{\textrm{\,c}}_{\textrm{p}}\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t)\right)
and maximizing by searching along 1D fibres in the 3D array brings three advantages: (i) the method is computationally straightforward, (ii) it avoids the need for establishing an initial estimate required for iterative numerical optimization algorithms, and (iii) this approach is known to perform well for problems involving poorly-identified parameters where the optimal value is not well-defined and iterative solvers can fail to converge
[
61
,
58
]
. Note that all discussion about the profile loglikelihood here is written in terms of the loglikelihood for count data,
\bar{\ell}^{\textrm{\,c}}_{\textrm{p}}\left(\boldsymbol{\psi}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t)\right)
. The same procedure can be used to compute the profile loglikelihood for trajectory data
\bar{\ell}^{\textrm{\,t}}_{\textrm{p}}\left(\boldsymbol{\psi}\;|\;\mathbf{x}(t)\right)
.

Results and Discussion

4.1
Unbiased motion: When do trajectories matter?

We begin by considering the count data in Figure
3
(c) for unbiased migration and explore the extent to which this count data identifies
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(D,K)^{\top}
. Before proceeding it is instructive to note that Equation (
7
) is independent of
K
which means that the solution of the mean-field model for
N(x,t)
cannot be used to estimate
K
. This observation indicates that this PDE model is structurally non-identifiable
[
29
,
54
]
. In this work we take a likelihood-based approach which means that we combine the solution of the PDE model with the noise model to form the loglikelihood
\ell^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))
which does depend upon
K
through the binomial noise model. We evaluate
\ell^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))
on a uniform
25\times 25
discretization of the
(D,K)
parameter space which gives an approximate MLE of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(0.250,9.167)^{\top}
, which is relatively close to the true value. Noting that this point estimate does not provide any insight into the uncertainty in our estimates we plot
\bar{\ell}^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))
as a heat map in Figure
4
(a), superimposed with the 95% threshold to define the 95% confidence set of parameters inside that contour. Here we see the shape of the confidence set indicates that
D
is well-identified by the count data, however
K
is not since the loglikelihood function is relatively flat in the direction of
K
. This result is consistent with our observation that Equation (
7
) is independent of
K
, and the relatively weak dependence on
K
arises through the binomial term in the loglikelihood function. This example emphasises an important point that while
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is relatively close to the expected value,
\ell^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))
is sufficiently insensitive to
K
that there is a broad range of
K
values that equally match the data, suggesting that
K
is not-identifiable with count data.

To give a complete picture here it is possible to combine both the count data and the trajectory data to explore the extent to which considering the total data affects our ability to estimate
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(D,K)^{\top}
. Invoking a standard assumption that the observed count data and trajectory data are conditionally independent given model parameters, the combined likelihood function is the product of the count likelihood and the trajectory likelihood. Hence we have the following loglikelihood function

4.3
Experimental design: How do trajectories matter?

Results in Figures
4
–
5

|x|\leq 10

4.4
Likelihood-based prediction

Given a normalized loglikelihood function and parameter confidence sets, we can explore and quantify how variability in the parameter confidence set translates into a prediction variability by constructing a prediction interval
[
70
,
60
,
61
]
. To demonstrate the construction of various prediction intervals we will focus on results in Figure
2
(e) and (g) for unbiased motility where all individuals in the column at
x=10
are tagged. As discussed earlier with reference to Figure
4
, working with count data alone means that
D
is well identified by the data but
K
is not. In contrast, working with trajectory data alone as in Figure
4
(b), or combining count and trajectory data as in Figure
4
(c) means that both
D
and
K
are identifiable. While typically we would restrict our attention to making likelihood-based predictions for identifiable problems where the parameter confidence set is a relatively constrained, here we generate likelihood-based predictions for all three normalised loglikelihood functions in Figure
4
(a)–(c).

The loglikelihood in Figure
4
(a) is associated with count data. Accordingly, we will use this loglikelihood function to generate a prediction interval for count data. We generate
S=500
samples of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
within the 95% confidence region, where
\bar{\ell}^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))\geq\bar{\ell}^{*}
. These samples are generated using a simple rejection algorithm where parameters are uniformly randomly sampled within a user-defined region containing
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}
. With these
S
parameter samples we then solve Equations (
7
)–(
8
) to give
S
continuous solutions,
HN_{s}(x,t)
for
s=1,2,3\ldots,S
. Each curve represents the expected number of agents per column at location
x
and time
t
for the particular choice of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
s within the 95% confidence set. In addition to predicting the mean number of agents, we can use the noise model to provide a description of the variability about the mean. At each spatial location
x
, we compute an interval
[HN_{s}^{-}(x,t),HN_{s}^{+}(x,t)]
, where the lower and upper values correspond to the 0.025 and 0.975 quantiles of the binomial noise model with mean
HN(x,t)
, respectively. With this information, instead of having
S
solution curves describing the mean of the noise model, we instead have
S
solution intervals that provide a simple measure of the data variability about the mean. Taking the union of intervals
[HN_{s}^{-}(x_{i},t),HN_{s}^{+}(x_{i},t)]
over
s=1,2,3,\ldots,S
at each spatial location
x_{i}
on a uniform discretization of the domain,
-100\leq x\leq 100
constructed with 401 mesh points, gives the green shaded prediction interval in Figure
7
(a).

Results in Figure
7
(a) superimpose the noisy data and the MLE solution on the prediction interval. This visualization confirms that the MLE solution captures the key trends and features of the noisy data but without characterizing any variability, whereas the prediction interval encloses the stochastic data reasonably well. Specifically, the prediction interval encloses 397 out of 401 data points, or 99% of the data in this realization. In this case it is prudent to note that we have formed this prediction interval from the region in Figure
4
(a) where the bounds of the 95% parameter confidence set are not determined by the curvature of the loglikelihood function alone. Here,
K
is not identifiable from count data alone since there are many values of
K
that match the data equally well so we identify set of
S
points that jointly satisfy
\bar{\ell}^{\textrm{\,c}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\;|\;\mathbf{N}(t))\geq\bar{\ell}^{*}
and
5\leq K\leq 15
because of the truncated region of parameter space that we have chosen to examine. This example provides a cautionary example of the potential to over-interpret model-based predictions for non-identifiable problems since the prediction intervals appear to be reasonable when compared with both the mean trend in the data and when compared with the noise in the data, however these intervals have not been constructed using a well-defined confidence set, and care needs to be exercised when interpreting such prediction intervals
[
58
]
. A more usual approach would be to restrict our attention to model-based predictions for a identifiable problems, such as working with trajectory data the loglikelihood function in Figure

(b).

For the purposes of the results in Figure

Another approach would be to implement Laplace’s approximation and draw samples from an associated multivariate Gaussian distribution

To make likelihood-based predictions of the trajectory data we obtain
S=500
samples of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
using the same rejection method as before. For each value of
\boldsymbol{\theta}

such that
\Phi(x^{-})=0.025
and
\Phi(x^{+})=0.975

P_{s}(x,t)
. This approach gives the prediction interval shown in Figure

Overall this exercise illustrates how to take a loglikelihood function that well-identifies the parameters and to understand how parameter variability within the parameter confidence set translates into variability in terms of the measurable quantities. As we explained, care needs to be taken when interpreting the prediction intervals depending on the identifiability of the parameters in question. While previous work has generated prediction intervals for count and density data, similar to Figure

5
Conclusions and Future Work

In this work we use a combination of stochastic simulations, mean-field PDE approximations and likelihood-based tools to explore the value of individual trajectory data for parameter inference and identifiability analysis. Our work on this problem is motivated by the observation that experimental cell biologists and field ecologists place a very high value on tagging individual cells and animals within larger populations, and making measurements of the trajectories of those tagged individuals. Instead of working with noisy experimental data, we focus our analysis on a reasonably intuitive, lattice-based random walk model of population dispersal. The simulation model involves just three parameters that describe the carrying capacity of lattice sites
\kappa>0
, the motility probability per time step for isolated agents
M>0
, and the drift parameter
\rho_{x}\in[-1,1]
. To provide a more rigorous mathematical description of simulation data, we derive a PDE-based continuum description involving three related parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(K,D,v_{x})^{\top}
.

The derivation of the continuum PDEs for the expected number of agents per site
N
and the probability density function of tagged agent location
P
reveal some interesting features. For example, the PDE for
N
is independent of
K
when motility is unbiased with
\rho_{x}=0
. This means that
K
is structurally non-identifiable from count data, whereas the PDE for
P
involves both
K
and
D
meaning that collecting trajectory data can resolve this non-identifiability
[
29
]
. This mathematical observation provides a remarkable illustration of the value in collecting trajectory data since more standard count data is insensitive to
K
, whereas the motion of tagged trajectories is sensitive to
K
. For biased motility where
\rho_{x}\neq 0
, the PDE models for both
N
and
P
depend upon all three parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(K,D,v_{x})^{\top}
. This indicates that if we have access to sufficient data for biased motility, all three parameters can be estimated using either count data or studying the motion of tagged individuals.

The question of practical identifiability can not be determined by examining the structure of the mean-field PDE models alone. Here we use simulation data to explore the extent to which parameter estimation is enhanced by collecting both count and trajectory data. For count data we develop a novel binomial loglikelihood function. This approach differs from previous problems where
K
has been pre-estimated and the binomial coefficient in the loglikelihood function is not explicitly evaluated since it acts like a normalisation constant
[
59
,
60
,
33
]
. Here we have a different situation where
K
is unknown and so we must explicitly compute the binomial coefficient. For trajectory data we use the new PDE model for
P
, which we use here for the first time in an inference and identifiability analysis context. For biased motility, collecting expensive count data leads to reasonable parameter estimates together with reasonably narrow parameter confidence set. In contrast, for unbiased motility, count data enables
D
to be estimated quite well, whereas
K
is not well-constrained by the curvature of the loglikelihood function. We also demonstrate how to combine count data and trajectory data to give a combined loglikelihood function that performs well in terms of inference, leading to accurate point estimates of the parameters with a reasonably well-constrained parameter confidence set.

One of the motivations for this work is that field ecologists and experimental cell biologists often collect trajectory data alongside or instead of count data. The amount of time and effort that goes into creating trajectory data means that there is scope for building mathematical models and developing a quantitative and objective workflow to examine how different types of tagging impacts how well parameters can be identified. Through a series of simple computations we compare inferential accuracy and precision by tagging different arrangements of agents in the same simulation and comparing the inferential precision by comparing the size and shape of the resulting parameter confidence set. For example, comparing the inferential accuracy and prevision between tagging individuals at the edge of the population compared to tarring individuals well-behind the edge of the population shows that the uncertainty in our parameter estimates is highly sensitive to this kind of experimental design question. This relationship between experimental design and parameter identifiability is important because spending time and money to perform an experiment that leads to poor identifiability may not be a good use of resources. Therefore, developing tools that operate at the interface of stochastic simulations, surrogate continuum PDE models, likelihood based estimation and identifiability analysis are very insightful to explore the implications of different experimental design choices.

There are many ways that the tools presented in this work can be extend. For instance, here we focus on a particular initial condition and boundary conditions in the 2D random walk model which means that the surrogate PDE models simplify to one-dimensional PDEs. This simplification is often relevant to experimental studies
[
9
,
69
,
19
,
17
]
, however the tools we develop here can also be deployed for different choices of initial conditions where the macroscopic quantities
N
and
P
depend upon both the horizontal and vertical location in the Cartesian plane
[
56
]
. In this case the same likelihood-based estimation, identifiability analysis, and likelihood-based prediction can be undertaken with count data collected by placing a coarser mesh across the simulation domain and solving the full two-dimensional PDEs, Equations (
2
) and (
6
)
[
56
]
. The rest of the inference-identifiability-prediction workflow remains unchanged.

More substantial generalizations of this work could involve applying different methods to coarse-grain the stochastic simulations to avoid the mean-field assumption
[
3
,
21
]
or working with lattice-free simulation frameworks
[
47
,
46
]
. In this first instance we have taken the most fundamental approach of working with a lattice-based simulation framework along with standard mean-field based PDE descriptions to keep the exposition as clear as possible. Our continuum-discrete comparisons in Figure
3
provide confidence that the mean-field PDE descriptions provide reasonably accurate approximations of the random walk model. Our open-access software on GitHub can be used to explore how the continuum-discrete comparison performs under a range of parameter values and our computational explorations (not shown) indicate that the accuracy of the continuum PDEs persists under a wide range of parameter values.

Another avenue for further consideration is to note that here we have taken a very simple view of tagging an individual: we simply record both the first and last position of that agent and use the PDE description of
P
to evaluate the associated loglikelihood. This approach can be refined by recording several intermediate locations along the trajectory path. For example, if we take a single trajectory and divide it into two equal durations we can use the PDE description of
P
to evaluate a loglikelihood for both durations. While this approach is conceptually straightforward, it requires multiple PDE solves per trajectory whereas the current approach does not.
