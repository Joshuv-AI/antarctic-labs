A social determination framework for mental health
Authors: Franco Mascayano, María Teresa Muñoz-Quezada, Alfonso Urzúa, María Soledad Burrone, Rodrigo Casanueva, Sebastián Rojas-Navarro, Pamela Grandón, Sandra Saldivia, Cristian Montenegro

Background

Although several authors have called for more socially embedded approaches to mental health research, the dominant paradigms in the field continue to emphasize individual-level risk factors and biomedical approaches, often neglecting the broader historical, social, and structural context. Global North scholars have critiqued this “black box” paradigm and called for multi-level, ecosocial perspectives [1, 2]. For example, Susser and Susser envision an “eco-epidemiology” for mental health that integrates levels from molecular to societal dimensions [1]. Similarly, Nancy Krieger’s ecosocial theory centers power, politics, race, and history, emphasizing that sociopolitical models analyze disease in relation to capitalism, inequality, and rights [2].

However, in Latin America—particularly through the paradigm of social determination of health—these critiques have been more fully articulated and developed. By “social determination,” we refer to a relational and historically situated process through which health and mental distress are produced and reproduced within configurations of power. Rather than viewing social factors as external influences acting upon otherwise autonomous individuals, this perspective understands mental health as constituted through dynamic interactions among political economy, institutions, social relations, and embodied experience [3]. Instead of mechanical causation, social determination refers to structured yet contested processes through which power relations shape the production and reproduction of mental health over time. This emphasis on contestation recognizes that social arrangements are historically changeable and that collective action can modify the conditions that generate suffering [3–5]. In contrast, the conventional “social determinants” approach often fragments structural causes into isolated risk factors and risks depoliticizing the origins of mental distress [4].

A Latin American social determination framework builds on this critique by foregrounding how historical and structural processes—such as colonialism, class exploitation, and violence—shape health across generations [5]. These concerns resonate with recent calls to decolonize global mental health [6], politicize its determinants, and move beyond fragmented categorizations of the ‘social’ that obscure overlapping realities [7]. Within this perspective, mental health is not treated as bounded individual pathology but as embedded in broader historical, economic, and political systems.

Jaime Breilh, a central figure in Latin American critical epidemiology, argues that health and illness are not merely shaped by “determinants” but arise from complex, dialectical social processes and historical structures [3]. He challenges reductionist and a-historical approaches and advances a paradigm that centers power, collective agency, and the transformation of unjust systems. In this view, research and policy must be oriented toward social transformation: health is inseparable from struggles for justice, and knowledge must be linked to collective action aimed at dismantling the conditions that create vulnerability.

When we refer to “dialectical social processes,” we denote the dynamic interactions between structure and agency—such as domination and resistance or accumulation and dispossession—as articulated within Latin American critical epidemiology and collective health traditions [3]. Dialectical does not imply simple opposition but the mutual constitution of levels and forces: macroeconomic arrangements shape everyday life, while collective practices and struggles may in turn reshape institutional configurations [3, 5]. Mental health outcomes therefore emerge from reciprocal and often contradictory processes rather than from additive or linear models of causation. Unlike multilevel or “complex causal pathway” models that treat levels as analytically separable and primarily additive, a dialectical perspective emphasizes historicity, structured power relations, and the possibility that collective action may transform institutional arrangements and policy trajectories [3, 5].

This debate is not merely academic. The framing of causality shapes policy priorities, funding flows, and the relative power of actors in global health. As Montenegro et al. [8] and Beresford and Rose [9] argue, global mental health must confront epistemic injustice, colonial legacies, and the global diffusion of Western psychiatric models that often marginalize local perspectives and reinforce individualizing frames. Addressing these limitations is essential for advancing global mental health equity. A rights-based perspective is also central to this effort. The 2020 report of the UN Special Rapporteur on the Right to Mental Health documented the iatrogenic effects of biomedical-dominant approaches and called for a fundamental reorientation toward power redistribution, meaningful participation, and the protection of human dignity [10]. This perspective underscores that mental health equity is not solely a technical or epidemiological challenge but a matter of social justice and rights accountability.

In this article, we aim to (i) highlight the limitations of mainstream “social determinants” approaches, (ii) re-center Jaime Breilh’s paradigm of Social Determination of Health within Latin American social medicine and collective health traditions, and (iii) outline how this framework can guide research, policy, and collective action. We first develop a critique of reductionist frameworks, then present the conceptual foundations of a social determination approach to mental health, illustrate its application through cases of discrimination, employment, and environmental injustice, and conclude with implications for transformative mental health research and practice.

Main body

Limitations of the ‘social determinants’ approach

Research on social determinants of mental health has steadily expanded, evidenced by the 2018 Lancet Commission on Global Mental Health [11] and the inclusion of mental health and equity goals in the UN 2030 Sustainable Development Agenda [12]. These developments have helped shift attention from individual symptoms to the broader social conditions that shape health. However, important limitations persist. Much mental health scholarship underutilizes regional and local knowledge about how colonial histories, extractive economies, and neoliberal reforms drive mental health problems [8]. As a result, even as interventions on social determinants expand, they frequently prioritize behavior change or service access over more transformative efforts to alter the structures producing vulnerability [13]. For instance, Howell et al. [14] have noted that global mental health advocacy often focuses on clinical interventions and outcomes, and does not confront issues such as poverty and inequality and the required structural redress. At the same time, it would be inaccurate to characterize the entire SDH tradition as inattentive to structural power. The WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health, for example, explicitly called for tackling the inequitable distribution of power, money, and resources as a core recommendation [15]. These formulations represent an important articulation of structural and political concerns within the SDH framework. Our critique, therefore, is not that the SDH tradition has been uniformly silent on power and structural inequities, but that in its dominant translation into research priorities and policy practice, the emphasis has tended toward risk-factor identification, service expansion, and behavior change rather than toward the structural transformation and collective praxis that the social determination tradition treats as constitutive of analysis itself.

As Krieger argues, critical scholarship in the Global North has engaged with structural and political dimensions of health. Krieger’s ecosocial theory and related approaches—including the social production of disease and political economy perspectives—posit that socially structured exposures, ranging from macroeconomics to psychosocial stress, drive population patterns of health [2]. Moreover, Waitzkin et al. [16] note that such paradigms differ from mainstream public health in their dialectical view of health–illness and emphasis on praxis (combining theory with political action). Similarly, Horwitz and Grob’s historical analysis of psychiatric epidemiology [17] highlights how conceptualizations of mental disorder have evolved within broader institutional and political contexts, while Flynn’s work [18] conceptualizes global capitalism as a societal determinant shaping population health inequalities. Despite these insights, these perspectives have been underrecognized in Anglophone literature—a gap that Yasmin Yamada (drawing on liberation theology) urged to fill, calling these frameworks a source of relevant analysis, models of resistance, and exemplars of social medicine applicable worldwide [19]. These contributions demonstrate that structural, political, and historical critiques are not exclusive to Latin American traditions. Our contribution does not claim novelty in identifying structural forces per se; rather, it foregrounds a Latin American lineage in which collective praxis and structural transformation are treated as constitutive elements of analysis rather than as downstream policy implications.

The social determination of mental health framework

We propose a social determination framework for mental health rooted primarily in Latin American social medicine and collective health traditions. This framework draws conceptually from Breilh’s work on structural causality, historical processes, and collective praxis, while also engaging convergent insights from ecosocial and eco-epidemiologic traditions that emphasize embodiment, multilevel dynamics, and the role of history in shaping health inequalities. However, these traditions are not treated as defining components of the framework itself. Rather, the proposed model is grounded in the Latin American critical epidemiology tradition, within which structural transformation and collective praxis are constitutive elements of analysis rather than downstream implications.

Central to this framework is the notion of dialectical social processes, distinct from complex causal pathways in that they do not merely describe interactions between variables, but the mutually constitutive relationship between historical structures and collective agency. In this view, social conditions both shape and are transformed through ongoing, historically situated tensions and contestation [3]. Its key features include:

Multi-level causation: Mental health emerges within nested and interacting social systems. Drawing on Susser’s eco-epidemiologic paradigm and Krieger’s ecosocial theory [1, 2], this framework integrates macro-level forces (political economy, racism, coloniality), meso-level contexts (community relations, labor conditions, violence), and processes operating at the individual level. Here, embodiment refers to the material incorporation of structured social and environmental conditions into biological, psychological, and affective functioning across the life course, consistent with ecosocial theory [2]. In this sense, power relations become expressed in patterned stress regulation and related pathways, contributing to durable inequalities in mental health [2, 20].

Rather than treating social factors as static, it sees them as historical products connected to processes such as colonialism, dictatorship, and neoliberalism. This mirrors ecosocial emphases on history and accountability. It also echoes Latin American theorists who draw on historical materialism: health inequities are not accidents but the predictable result of who controls resources and policies [19]. Structural configurations and institutional arrangements: Our model distinguishes between two analytically related levels. At the macro level, historical–structural configurations—including colonialism, class structures, racialization, and the global political economy—organize the conditions under which institutions operate and populations live. At the meso level, institutional arrangements—policies, organizational structures, and regulatory frameworks governing education, housing, labor markets, and health systems—mediate and concretize these macro-level forces in everyday life. These levels are not independent: macro-level configurations shape and constrain institutional arrangements, which in turn structure the conditions in which people grow, work, and experience distress. As the Commission on Social Determinants of Health affirmed [15], health inequities arise from the circumstances in which people grow, live, work, and age, as well as the systems put in place. In practice, this means analyzing, for example, how austerity measures, discrimination, or forced displacement do not simply impact, but instead produce and reproduce conditions such as depression, anxiety, and psychosis.

While collective agency refers to the organized capacity of communities and social movements to act upon structural conditions, collective praxis—the unifying concept in our framework (Fig. 1)—denotes the integration of critical knowledge production and transformative action: the process through which collective agency becomes oriented toward structural change through reflexive engagement with the conditions that produce suffering [3, 16]. Collective praxis thus encompasses and mobilizes collective agency, linking it to critical analysis and historically grounded action. Research, therefore, is not limited to describing inequities but is conceived as part of broader processes of social change [3, 5].

A common critique of structurally oriented approaches is that they may risk overshadowing individual agency or subjective experience by emphasizing large-scale social forces. The perspective proposed here does not deny these dimensions. Rather, it situates individual experiences, meanings, and choices within historically structured contexts that shape the possibilities for action. Individuals are therefore not treated as passive recipients of structural conditions but as social actors whose lives are embedded in institutional arrangements and power relations. Within this framework, agency operates both at the level of everyday decision-making and, crucially, through collective processes in which communities and social movements organize to contest and transform the conditions that generate suffering. Recognizing these dynamics helps reconcile structural analysis with lived experience and underscores the importance of collective praxis as a pathway for expanding agency under conditions of inequality.

Contrasting social health frameworks

Recent research provides evidence for the social determinants of mental health and for advanced multilevel prevention strategies grounded in epidemiological research. For example, Kirkbride et al. [21] provide an extensive review of determinants across the life course and propose structured preventive interventions. Such contributions represent an important expansion beyond the clinical level, but they often operate within an evidentiary and preventive framework that prioritizes risk identification and measurement. By contrast, a social determination perspective centers the historical production of these determinants and situates them within broader configurations of political economy, governance, and power, emphasizing collective transformation as integral rather than after analysis.

However, it is important to stress that the perspective proposed here does not deny the symptomatic dimension of suffering nor the relevance of situated responses that seek to alleviate it. Rather, it advocates for an understanding that is attentive to the complexity of human suffering, viewing it as the product of structural social processes, such that the most profound and sustainable transformations will derive from interventions at this level [3]. In other words, focusing on the social determination of health and on the need to address structural dimensions does not exclude the importance of clinical or psychosocial interventions aimed at symptoms or trauma repair when necessary.

This distinction matters: whereas SDH-informed interventions have often prioritized pragmatic, implementable responses to health inequalities—reflecting a legitimate orientation toward actionable policy change—the social determination approach foregrounds structural transformation and collective praxis as integral to the analytical process itself. These orientations are not necessarily opposed; technocratic and service-oriented interventions can be linked to and synergize with broader structural initiatives, provided they are situated within an explicit analysis of the power relations and historical processes that generate inequities. Methodologically, recent innovations—such as Discovery-Oriented Biographical Analysis—demonstrate the need for frameworks that account for narrative, context, and social history to more fully explain the production of mental health and distress [22].

Operationalizing a social determination perspective requires research strategies capable of documenting how historically structured social processes shape mental health across levels. Historically grounded research therefore involves reconstructing policy trajectories and institutional transformations over time—such as trade liberalization, labor deregulation, environmental governance, or colonial land dispossession—and situating contemporary exposures within these broader historical dynamics. Methodologically, this often requires combining documentary and policy analysis with quantitative mental health data, while also incorporating biographical or life-course approaches that trace how structural arrangements become embodied across time. Mixed-method and participatory research designs are particularly valuable in this context, as they allow researchers to link macro-level political–economic processes with institutional practices and lived experience. Rather than focusing solely on exposure–outcome associations, historically grounded research seeks to analyze the institutional decision-making processes, power relations, and forms of collective action through which mental health inequalities are produced and contested.

Illustrative applications

To demonstrate the explanatory and practical power of our framework, we present three concise case vignettes—one each on employment, discrimination, and the environment—drawn from diverse Latin American settings. These examples reconstruct interconnected and historically structured processes linking macro-level forces (e.g., trade liberalization, colonial land dispossession, spatial apartheid), meso-level social relations (e.g., workplace exploitation, ethnic exclusion, substandard housing), and the embodied experience of distress at the individual level. Rather than depicting a linear sequence, the cases illustrate how global and national policy dynamics—often shaped by distant actors—are materialized in everyday life and contested through collective distress.

Employment

In northern Honduras’ export-processing zones, young women employed in maquila factories work under short-term contracts, intense production quotas, wage theft, and frequent verbal or sexual harassment—conditions rooted in the region’s neoliberal trade policies and weak labor protections. Surveys and labor-rights reports document high rates of anxiety, chronic stress, and depressive symptoms among these workers, who also face the strain of family separation and hazardous occupational exposures [23–25].

From a social determination perspective, these conditions cannot be understood as isolated workplace stressors. Rather, they reflect the historically structured organization of global production, in which trade liberalization, subcontracting systems, and gendered labor markets generate precarious forms of employment. Mental distress emerges as the embodiment of these broader political–economic arrangements rather than as the consequence of individual vulnerability.

At the same time, these dynamics are not unidirectional. Labor organizing, transnational advocacy networks, and legal challenges to labor abuses have sought to transform working conditions in export-processing zones. Interventions led by unions and women’s organizations—including campaigns for living wages, enforcement of safety regulations, and legal empowerment—illustrate how collective action can reshape the institutional arrangements that structure workers’ everyday lives. Mixed-methods research combining quantitative mental-health screening with participatory life-history interviews can help reconstruct how global trade regimes become embodied as workers’ mental suffering while also documenting the forms of collective agency that seek to alter these conditions.

Discrimination

Across rural Oaxaca, Zapoteco adolescents encounter persistent racism in schools, clinics, and public spaces, reflecting a long history of land dispossession, cultural marginalization, and political exclusion. Population studies report elevated depressive symptoms, suicidal ideation, and substance use among Indigenous youth, while qualitative research links these outcomes to linguistic discrimination, cultural devaluation, and chronic social exclusion [26].

A social determination framework situates these experiences within the structural violence of coloniality. Discriminatory institutional practices and the erosion of communal autonomy generate enduring psychosocial stressors that become embodied as mental distress. In this perspective, racism is not treated as an isolated risk factor but as a historically produced system of power that organizes everyday interactions, shapes institutional arrangements, and constrains life opportunities.

Historically grounded research in this context can combine population-based mental health data with ethnographic and biographical approaches to trace how structural discrimination becomes embodied across the life course. Mixed-method designs integrating survey data, participatory life-history interviews, and community-based research can help reconstruct how institutional racism in education, health systems, and public administration shapes adolescents’ experiences of belonging, identity, and psychosocial stress.

Community responses demonstrate that these dynamics are historically mutable. Indigenous organizations, youth collectives, and intercultural education initiatives have mobilized to defend language rights, strengthen cultural recognition, and challenge discriminatory institutional practices. Programs such as the UNESCO-supported Indigenous youth network in Oaxaca have produced mental-health materials in Zapoteco and promoted culturally grounded mental-health initiatives [27]. These efforts illustrate how collective agency can reshape the institutional contexts that generate psychosocial stress, linking cultural revitalization with mental-health promotion.

Environment

The coastal towns of Quintero–Puchuncaví in Chile are widely known as environmental “sacrifice zones,” shaped by decades of export-oriented industrial development and permissive environmental governance. In these territories, environmental exposure is inseparable from political economy: regulatory decisions and economic priorities have historically concentrated environmental risks in specific communities.

Similar dynamics are observed in rural areas of the Maule Region, where large-scale pig farming and agro-industrial operations have generated chronic environmental exposure under conditions of limited regulatory transparency and restricted community participation. Environmental monitoring has identified pesticide residues in soil and household water supplies, with higher concentrations in homes located near agricultural and livestock operations [28]. Quantitative analyses link these contamination patterns to elevated depressive symptoms and psychological distress [29].

Within a social determination framework, these findings are interpreted not simply as exposure–outcome associations but as expressions of territorially organized governance and uneven political power. Environmental degradation reshapes everyday life—altering routines, eroding trust in institutions, and producing persistent uncertainty about health and safety [30].

Community organizations have responded through environmental justice campaigns, legal action, and participatory research initiatives that combine environmental monitoring with community-based mental-health assessments [31–33]. These efforts demonstrate how knowledge production and collective mobilization can challenge the institutional arrangements that generate environmental harm, illustrating the dialectical relationship between structural conditions and collective action.

Conclusions

The social determination of mental health framework, grounded in Latin American social medicine and critical epidemiology, challenges the reductionism of mainstream “social determinants” approaches by demanding that we address the structural roots of mental health inequities. Rather than focusing on risk factors, it situates mental suffering within histories of colonialism, labor exploitation, and environmental injustice, showing how these macro-forces become embodied as distress and disease. The three vignettes situated in Latin America underscore that meaningful interventions must extend well beyond clinics into labor law, land rights, education policy, and environmental regulation.

Adopting this paradigm has implications not only for research and practice in Latin America but for global mental health as a whole. It supports recent international calls to decolonize and politicize global mental health, moving the field toward genuine equity and transformative action [6, 9]. Researchers should privilege participatory and historically informed methodologies that link lived distress to structural causation; funders should support transdisciplinary studies and community-led action; and policymakers should embed goals within broader struggles for social, economic, and environmental justice. Research within this framework requires systematic documentation of how structured power relations operate across macro, meso, and micro levels, as well as empirical demonstration of how these configurations are materially embodied and subject to contestation over time.

Policies, in turn, must be transparent, equitable, inclusive, and oriented toward reducing structural inequalities. Many Latin American scholars have called for upstream interventions—such as living wages, education equity, and strengthened community resources—rather than exclusively downstream clinical treatments [3, 19]. Voices of lived experience and collective movements, including post-dictatorship mental health programs in Chile or community psychiatry in Brazil, become integral to the analysis and implementation [22]. Although several Global North traditions—including ecosocial and political economy approaches—explicitly address power, history, and structural inequities [2], their translation into policy agendas and funding priorities has often emphasized measurement, risk stratification, and service expansion. In contrast, Breilh’s paradigm situates collective agency and structural transformation as constitutive elements of analysis rather than subsequent applications, insisting that research itself forms part of broader processes of social and political change [3, 5].

In short, centering social determination does not replace or dismiss the contributions of the social determinants tradition but broadens and deepens its analytical scope—shifting attention from identifying and intervening upon risk factors toward interrogating and transforming the historically constituted systems that produce vulnerability. By foregrounding collective praxis, historical analysis, and structural transformation as constitutive elements of inquiry, this framework offers complementary resources that can strengthen and politicize global mental health scholarship and action.

Only by addressing these upstream structural determinants can the field of mental health fulfill its promise to advance global population well-being and social justice.
