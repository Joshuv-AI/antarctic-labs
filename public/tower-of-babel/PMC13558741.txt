Digital image analysis coupled with artificial neural networks for simultaneous prediction of biomass and pigments in microalgal cultivation
Authors: Nima Omidi, Seyed Ali Hosseini Tafreshi, Mohammad Reza Zoghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Real-time, non-destructive monitoring of multiple physiological parameters in microalgal cultures remains a significant analytical challenge, as conventional methods are destructive, time-consuming, and unsuitable for in situ applications. This study developed a novel digital image analysis framework integrating five color spaces (RGB, HSI, HSV, L*a*b*, YCbCr) with two distinct modeling platforms—Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) and Response Surface Methodology (RSM)—for the simultaneous prediction of biomass (optical density, OD₇₅₀) and key pigments (chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and total carotenoids) in Dunaliella salina cultures subjected to combined salinity and light stress. Validation using an independent cultivation dataset demonstrated that the optimal modeling approach was dictated by the physiological nature of the target parameter: ANN models significantly outperformed RSM for non-linear, stress-induced responses, with the ANN-RGB model achieving the best carotenoid prediction (MSE: 0.507, R²: 0.918) and the ANN-L*a*b* model excelling for chlorophyll a (MSE: 0.252, R²: 0.814), whereas a simpler RSM-YCbCr model sufficed for chlorophyll b (MSE: 0.514, R²: 0.670). Novel temporal error analysis (CDF and Heatmap) further revealed the superior stability of ANN models throughout the full cultivation cycle. This low-cost, image-based AI framework offers a robust, non-invasive tool for real-time monitoring in microalgal bioprocessing and smart farming applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58819-x.

Introduction

Real-time, non-destructive monitoring of key bioprocess parameters—biomass and high-value metabolites—remains a significant analytical bottleneck in microalgal biotechnology. Conventional methods for pigment and biomass quantification rely on extraction with organic solvents followed by spectrophotometry or high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). These techniques are inherently destructive, labor-intensive, time-consuming, and unsuitable for in situ, real-time monitoring1. Microalgae are a diverse group of photosynthetic organisms with immense biotechnological potential, classified by Fritsch2 into 11 major classes and studied since Linnaeus first described them in 17533. Among these, the class Chlorophyceae, which includes the genus Dunaliella—unicellular, biflagellate green algae lacking a rigid cell wall4—is particularly important due to its capacity to synthesize valuable bioactive compounds including proteins, lipids, and pigments5. Dunaliella salina, in particular, is a commercial source of β-carotene and glycerol, with its carotenoids widely used as food colorants, provitamin A supplements, and antioxidants6,7. These pigments serve dual physiological roles as accessory light-harvesting complexes and photoprotective agents that quench reactive oxygen species8. The inability of conventional analytical techniques to provide real-time, non-invasive data underscores the urgent need for alternative monitoring strategies capable of simultaneous, cost-effective multi-parameter estimation in microalgal cultures.

Digital image processing (DIP) has emerged as a promising non-destructive analytical tool to address these challenges. DIP employs algorithms to extract quantitative color and texture information from digital images, effectively converting visual data into measurable parameters9. In plant sciences, image-based chlorophyll estimation has been successfully demonstrated in soybean and wheat using digital cameras and smartphones10–12. In phycology, pioneering studies have applied DIP for pigment monitoring: Kamath et al.13 correlated green intensity with chlorophyll content in Haematococcus pluvialis, Su et al.14 employed three-color analysis for simultaneous chlorophyll a and lipid estimation in Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, and Wood et al.15 developed a simple image-based method for chlorophyll quantification in Chlamydomonas cultures. Beyond microalgae, Xia et al.16 demonstrated DIP-based high-throughput screening of Monascus pigment-producing strains. Critically, the choice of color space fundamentally influences the information captured. The RGB space encodes color as red, green, and blue channels corresponding to human visual perception17. The HSI and HSV spaces separate chromatic information (hue and saturation) from intensity, providing robustness against illumination variations18,19. The CIELAB (L*a*b*) space is perceptually uniform and separates lightness (L*) from color-opponent dimensions (a*: green–red, b*: blue–yellow)20,21, while YCbCr separates luminance from chrominance and is widely used in image compression22. Despite these advances, existing studies have typically employed a single color space for predicting only one or two parameters, and a systematic comparison of multiple color spaces for simultaneous multi-parameter estimation has not been conducted.

To model the relationship between image-derived color features and physiological parameters, two distinct and complementary modeling paradigms are available. Response Surface Methodology (RSM) is a statistical and mathematical technique that models linear or polynomial relationships between independent and response variables, enabling factor interaction identification and process optimization through interpretable Eq.23. In contrast, Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) belong to the domain of artificial intelligence and machine learning—they are computational models inspired by biological neural networks, capable of learning complex, non-linear relationships from data without requiring a pre-defined model structure24,25. The complementary strengths of RSM (statistical transparency and interpretability) and ANN (capturing intricate non-linearity) have been productively combined in various biotechnological applications, including optimization of fermentation media25, extraction process modeling24, and recently, symbiotic culture optimization for chlorophyll accumulation26. However, a direct comparative evaluation of these two fundamentally different modeling platforms—one statistical (RSM) and one AI-based (ANN)—for image-driven pigment and biomass prediction has not been thoroughly investigated.

This study aimed to develop and validate a novel, non-destructive digital image analysis framework integrating five distinct color spaces (RGB, HSI, HSV, L*a*b*, YCbCr) with both ANN and RSM modeling platforms for the simultaneous, real-time prediction of four key culture parameters: biomass (OD₇₅₀), chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and total carotenoids in D. salina under combined salinity and light stress. These four parameters were selected because they collectively represent the most critical aspects of D. salina bioprocess monitoring: biomass accumulation (OD₇₅₀) as a measure of overall culture performance, chlorophyll a and *b* as indicators of photosynthetic apparatus status and light-harvesting adaptation, and total carotenoids as the primary high-value product whose synthesis is strongly regulated by environmental stress. Unlike previous studies that typically focused on estimating only one or two parameters—such as chlorophyll alone13,15 or chlorophyll alongside lipid content14—using a single color space and a single modeling approach, the present work provides: (i) the first comprehensive head-to-head comparison of five color spaces to identify optimal feature sets for each target parameter, (ii) a direct evaluation of statistical (RSM) versus AI-based (ANN) modeling paradigms, clarifying which approach is better suited for parameters governed by different degrees of physiological complexity, and (iii) the novel introduction of temporal error analysis using Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) plots and time-resolved heatmaps, revealing model stability across a full cultivation cycle—an aspect not addressed in prior image-based monitoring studies. Beyond microalgal biotechnology, this low-cost, smartphone-based framework offers a scalable strategy for precision agriculture and smart farming systems, enabling real-time, non-invasive oversight of photosynthetic culture performance for data-driven process optimization.

Materials and methods

Culture conditions and mediums

Dunaliella salina (UTEX LB 2538) was cultivated in a modified Johnson medium27. Under sterile conditions, 100 mL of fresh medium was dispensed into 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks. Each flask was inoculated with an equal initial cell density of 23,000 cells mL⁻¹. The cultures were incubated at 24 ± 1 °C under a cool white fluorescent light source with a 12:12 h light-dark photoperiod for 32 days.

Experimental design

A full factorial experimental design was implemented with two independent factors: salinity (at 2, 2.5, and 3 Molar) and light intensity (at 4.32, 20.25, and 216 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), resulting in nine distinct treatment combinations (Table 1). This range of salinity and light intensity treatments was selected to generate a wide spectrum of physiological states—from optimal growth to severe stress—in D. salina. This experimental strategy served a dual purpose: (i) to investigate the individual and interactive effects of these key environmental factors on biomass and pigment dynamics, and (ii) to ensure that the training dataset encompassed sufficient variability, thereby enabling the developed ANN and RSM models to demonstrate robustness and generalizability across diverse culture conditions during independent validation (Culture 2). Each treatment was performed in triplicate (n = 3). Over the 32-day culture period, 4 mL samples were collected from each replicate at ten time points for subsequent image acquisition, optical density measurement, and pigment analysis.

Spectrophotometric analysis of pigments and optical density

For pigment and optical density analysis, parallel samples were processed. For pigment extraction, 1 mL of culture was centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 5 min (Eppendorf, Germany). The pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of chilled 80% (v/v) acetone, vortexed in darkness for 5 min, and centrifuged at 13,500 rpm for 5 min to remove cell debris. The absorbance of the acetone supernatant was measured from 400 to 700 nm using a spectrophotometer (WPA Biowave II, UK) with 80% acetone as a blank. Concurrently, optical density (OD) was measured at 750 nm to monitor algal growth while avoiding interference from pigments29,30. A separate 1 mL aliquot of each culture was homogenized, and its absorbance at 750 nm was recorded using the same spectrophotometer. The OD₇₅₀ data were used to construct growth curves.

Image acquisition and preprocessing

To obtain uniform imaging samples, 2 mL of each culture was transferred to a 4.5 mL transparent cuvette and gently inverted to homogenize the suspension. Digital images were acquired using a smartphone (Huawei Nova 3e) equipped with a dual-camera system (16 MP + 2 MP ASPH lenses). All images were captured with fixed settings: aperture f/2.2, focal length 4 mm, ISO 50, shutter speed 1/30 s, and a resolution of 4608 × 3456 pixels. No flash was used, and the phone was kept horizontal. A uniform, continuous light source with an intensity of 500 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ illuminated the samples from a distance of 75 cm. The camera lens was positioned 25 cm from the cuvette. Five images were captured per sample session and saved in RGB color space as JPEG files.

From each set of five images, the one with the best focus, contrast, and uniform illumination was selected for analysis (representative images are shown in Fig. 1). Using Adobe Photoshop CC 2021, a consistent central region of interest (ROI) was cropped from each selected image to exclude edge artifacts. This preprocessing yielded a final dataset of 270 cropped images saved in their original RGB format.

Image analysis and feature extraction

All cropped images were imported into MATLAB R2021a as unsigned 8-bit integer (uint8) matrices of size M × N × 3. Color features were subsequently extracted from each image across five distinct color spaces: RGB17, HSI18, HSV19, CIELAB (L*a*b*)20,21, and YCbCr22. These spaces were chosen to capture a comprehensive set of color and luminance information. The mean value of each channel (e.g., R, G, B for RGB; L, a, b for CIELAB) was calculated per image, generating a set of three features per color space for subsequent modeling. Transformations between color spaces were performed using built-in MATLAB functions (rgb2hsv, rgb2lab, rgb2ycbcr) or standard Eq.19 for HSI conversion.

Dataset creation for modeling

Two distinct dataset structures were prepared to match the requirements of the two modeling platforms (RSM and ANN). Data from the first culture day (Day 0) were excluded from all datasets due to the lack of visible pigmentation. All datasets linked the three extracted color features from a given color space to the four corresponding measured response variables: carotenoid, chlorophyll a, and chlorophyll b concentrations, along with optical density (OD₇₅₀).

For RSM modeling

A dataset was created where each row represented one biological sample. The three input features were the mean channel values calculated per image for a specific color space. This resulted in a compact dataset with 243 rows (samples) and 7 columns (3 features + 4 responses) for each color space.

For ANN modeling

To provide the ANN with a larger and more granular dataset, each image was downsampled to a resolution of 15 × 15 pixels. The measured response values for the corresponding sample were assigned to each individual pixel using bicubic interpolation. This generated an expanded dataset for each color space with 54,675 rows (pixels) and 7 columns, where each row represented a single pixel’s color features and its associated response values.

All datasets were exported in CSV format for model development.

Model development (ANN and RSM)

Predictive models were developed independently using Response Surface Methodology (RSM) and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN).

RSM model development

The compact datasets (mean color features per sample) for each color space were imported into Design-Expert software (Version 13, Stat-Ease Inc., USA). For each of the four response variables (carotenoid, Chl a, Chl b, OD), a linear model was fitted using the three color features of the respective space as numeric factors. Model fitness was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²) and mean squared error (MSE).

ANN model development

The expanded pixel-level datasets for each color space were used in MATLAB R2021a (The MathWorks Inc., USA). A feedforward neural network with three hidden layers was designed. The Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm was employed as the training function, with mean squared error (MSE) as the performance metric. The network used a log-sigmoid transfer function in the hidden layers and a linear output function. To ensure robustness and avoid local minima, each network was trained ten times with random initial weights. For each run, the data were randomly partitioned into training (70%), validation (15%), and testing (15%) subsets. Training was conducted on a GPU (NVIDIA GeForce MX230) with an error goal of 1 × 10⁻⁶ and was set to stop after a maximum of 1000 epochs or after 6 consecutive validation failures. The network instance with the lowest MSE from the ten repetitions was selected as the final model for each color space-response pair.

Model validation and statistical analysis

Model performance was evaluated using a multi-step validation and analysis protocol.

Independent validation with a second culture

To test generalizability, a second independent culture of D. salina was established (Culture 2). This culture used the same medium and stress factors but was inoculated with half the initial cell density (11,000 cells mL⁻¹) and maintained for 26 days. Images were captured (n = 146), and color features were extracted identically to the first culture. These new feature sets served as unseen input data to the pre-trained RSM and ANN models. The mean squared error (MSE) between the model predictions and the actual measured values (OD and pigments) from Culture 2 was calculated for each model, providing a key metric of real-world predictive accuracy.

Regression analysis

For the best-performing model of each response variable (selected based on the lowest MSE on the independent test), a regression plot of predicted versus actual values was generated. The coefficient of determination (R²) from this analysis was reported.

Error distribution analysis

The cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the prediction errors was plotted for the selected models. This analysis shows the proportion of errors below a given threshold, offering a detailed view of model accuracy beyond a single aggregate metric like MSE. The error value corresponding to the 80th percentile of the CDF was noted for comparison.

Temporal performance analysis

A heatmap was constructed to visualize the MSE of the selected models for each day of Culture 2. This temporal analysis revealed the stability and reliability of the models throughout the different growth and pigment accumulation phases.

Comparative model selection

The final selection of the optimal model for predicting each response variable was based on a comprehensive evaluation of the following criteria: (1) Lowest MSE on the independent test set (Culture 2), (2) Highest R² from the regression plot, (3) Favorable error distribution (CDF plot), and (4) Consistent performance across the culture period (Heatmap).

Software and computational tools

All computational workflows, including image analysis, dataset preparation, and model development, were executed on a computer running Windows 10 Home (64-bit) equipped with an Intel Core i5-1035G1 CPU, 16 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce MX230 GPU. The following software packages were utilized: MATLAB R2021a (for image processing, feature extraction, and ANN development), Design-Expert version 13 (for RSM modeling), Adobe Photoshop CC 2021 (for image cropping), Microsoft Excel 2019 (for data organization), and GraphPad Prism version 8.4.3 (for generating graphical representations of growth and pigment data). The online tool ClustVis was used for heatmap generation31.

Statistical analysis of experimental data

Data obtained from the spectrophotometric measurements (pigment concentrations and optical density) were subjected to Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). When ANOVA indicated significant differences (p < 0.05), means were compared using Duncan’s multiple range test. All experiments were conducted with three independent biological replicates, and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. For the independent validation culture (Culture 2), all nine treatment combinations were maintained, and 146 images were acquired across the 26-day cultivation period to ensure robust model testing.

Results and discussion

Algal growth and pigment accumulation under stress conditions

Optical density (biomass)

The optical density (OD₇₅₀), serving as a proxy for biomass32,33, showed clear trends in response to salinity and light stress (Fig. 2A). As expected, the most restrictive conditions—combining high salinity (3 M) with low light intensity (4.32 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹)—resulted in the lowest final biomass. Conversely, robust growth was observed in treatments with lower to moderate salinity (2–2.5 M) paired with moderate to high light intensity (20.25–216 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹). Interestingly, cultures at 2 M salinity maintained substantial growth even under the highest light intensity (216 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), suggesting a degree of tolerance or acclimation. We interpret this pattern as reflecting a physiological trade-off in D. salina: while moderate salinity and sufficient light energy support robust photosynthetic growth, extreme conditions—particularly the combination of high salinity and low light—overwhelm cellular homeostasis, diverting energy toward stress maintenance rather than cell division. This interpretation is consistent with the known physiology of D. salina, where optimal growth typically occurs at salinities below saturation and under adequate light availability5.

Carotenoid content

Carotenoid accumulation, a key indicator of photoprotective and antioxidant response, was profoundly influenced by the applied stresses (Fig. 2B). As anticipated, high light intensity was the primary driver for carotenoid synthesis. Treatments exposed to the highest light level (216 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) consistently showed elevated carotenoid content per culture volume compared to those under low or moderate light. However, salinity modulated this response. At a given high light intensity, carotenoid yield was generally higher at lower salinities (2 M) and diminished as salinity increased to 3 M. We attribute this pattern to the dual role of carotenoids in D. salina: under high light, carotenoid synthesis is upregulated as a photoprotective mechanism, with β-carotene accumulating in chloroplast lipid droplets to quench reactive oxygen species and prevent chlorophyll photo-damage. However, our observation that carotenoid yield diminished at the highest salinity despite high light suggests that severe salt stress suppressed overall biosynthetic capacity by limiting cell division—thereby reducing net pigment accumulation per culture volume even if per-cell synthesis was induced. This interpretation aligns with the known physiology of D. salina under combined stress conditions4. We further note that the quantitative carotenoid yield observed in our study may be strain-specific, as previous reports have documented substantial variation in carotenoid productivity among different D. salina isolates34. The observed reduction in total carotenoid content at the highest salinity (3 M), despite the high-light stimulus, is likely attributable to the significant negative impact of severe salt stress on overall biomass growth (as seen in Fig. 2A). While high salinity may induce β-carotene production on a per-cell basis, the pronounced suppression of cell division and culture density likely results in a lower net accumulation of the pigment per unit volume of culture. This highlights the critical trade-off between stress-induced metabolite synthesis and biomass productivity in optimizing D. salina bioprocesses.

Chlorophyll content

The chlorophyll content, comprising both chlorophyll a (Chl a) and chlorophyll b (Chl b), exhibited distinct yet interconnected response patterns to salinity and light stress, reflecting adjustments in the photosynthetic apparatus of D. salina (Fig. 2C & D). The highest concentrations of both pigments were generally maintained under moderate light intensity (20.25 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) across salinity levels, indicating optimal conditions for photosynthetic complex stability. However, under high light stress (216 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), a significant decline in Chl a was observed, particularly at lower salinities, signaling photoinhibition and potential degradation of core reaction centers35. Concurrently, Chl b, which is primarily associated with light-harvesting antenna complexes, showed pronounced sensitivity to high salinity (3 M), with marked reductions at all light intensities. This suggests that the assembly or stability of antenna complexes is particularly vulnerable to ionic/osmotic stress36. Previous studies have shown that high irradiance induces a reduction in the chlorophyll antenna size of D. salina37. Our findings indicate that high salinity acts as an additional, potent driver for reducing light-harvesting components, likely as part of a coordinated down-regulation to limit excess energy absorption under combined stress. The overall reduction in total chlorophyll under severe stress combinations correlates with growth inhibition (Fig. 2A), representing a strategic downscaling of the photosynthetic machinery to mitigate reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. Taken together, our findings suggest a coordinated acclimation strategy in D. salina under combined stress: the photosynthetic machinery is strategically downscaled—evidenced by reductions in both Chl a and Chl b—to minimize ROS generation, while photoprotective carotenoids are simultaneously upregulated. This physiological trade-off between light capture and photoprotection represents an adaptive mechanism for managing excess excitation energy and oxidative stress under adverse conditions38.

Model development and initial performance

Given their complementary strengths, Response Surface Methodology (RSM) and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) were selected as the two primary platforms for constructing predictive models from image-derived color data. RSM is a well-established statistical approach that excels at modeling linear or polynomial relationships and clearly identifying factor interactions through interpretable mathematical Eq.23. In contrast, ANN is a powerful machine learning tool inspired by biological neural networks, renowned for its ability to learn and generalize complex, non-linear patterns without requiring a pre-defined model structure24,25. This comparative framework allows for a robust evaluation of which modeling paradigm is best suited for estimating different microalgal culture parameters from simple image features.

The principle by which image data substitute for biochemical measurements should be clarified. The color of a microalgal culture, captured as pixel intensities across color channels, directly reflects its pigment composition and biomass density. As pigments accumulate or degrade under changing conditions, spectral absorption shifts produce measurable changes in these channel values. The ANN and RSM models learn the statistical relationship between these optical signatures and spectrophotometric measurements. This is a correlative approach: rather than modeling light–culture interactions mechanistically, the models exploit the reproducible quantitative mapping between color and composition established through paired training data.

The initial performance of the RSM and ANN models, trained on the dataset from the first culture, is summarized in Table 2. For optical density (OD), both modeling approaches yielded relatively low mean squared error (MSE) values, indicating a good fit. Among these, ANN models demonstrated a slight edge, with the RGB model achieving the lowest MSE (0.00526).

A more pronounced advantage for ANN was evident in modeling pigment concentrations. For carotenoid prediction, the ANN-based RGB model substantially outperformed all RSM models, achieving an MSE of 0.96089 compared to the best RSM model (MSE: 1.72766 for L*a*b*). A similar trend was observed for chlorophyll a, where ANN models (particularly YCbCr and L*a*b*) showed superior accuracy (MSE: ~0.53–0.67) compared to RSM models (MSE: ~0.95–1.99).

The prediction of chlorophyll b presented a different scenario. Here, the performance gap between ANN and RSM models was narrower, with both platforms achieving comparable MSE values in a relatively low range (0.417 to 0.606). This suggests that the relationship between the extracted image features and chlorophyll b concentration may be more linear or less complex than for the other parameters.

We interpret this performance discrepancy as a direct consequence of the underlying physiological complexity governing each parameter. The biosynthesis and accumulation of carotenoids and Chl a are regulated by multiple interacting stress signaling pathways, producing highly non-linear responses to environmental stimuli—a complexity that ANN models are inherently better equipped to capture. In contrast, the comparatively stable relationship between Chl b content and image features suggests a more linear dependence, likely reflecting the structural role of Chl b in light-harvesting antenna complexes that respond more gradually to environmental change. This interpretation is consistent with the established understanding that RSM excels at modeling linear or polynomial relationships, whereas ANN is superior for capturing intricate, non-linear patterns characteristic of many biological processes26. Our empirical results—showing ANN’s dominance for nonlinearly-responding pigments (carotenoids, Chl a) and its comparable performance with RSM for a potentially linear-response pigment (Chl b)—directly corroborate this theoretical framework and demonstrate the importance of platform selection based on response variable behavior.

The possible physiological and optical bases for these color space preferences merit discussion. The superior performance of RGB for carotenoid prediction may reflect the direct correspondence between carotenoid absorption in the blue-green region (400–500 nm) and the R and G channels: carotenoid accumulation likely reduces blue light transmission while increasing red-orange reflectance, producing proportional changes in RGB channel intensities that the model can exploit. The L*a*b* space—which separates lightness (L*) from the green–red opponent dimension (a*)—appears particularly suited to chlorophyll a estimation, possibly because changes in this dominant photosynthetic pigment modulate the green-to-red transition captured by the a* channel, especially during stress-induced chlorosis. The more linear relationship observed for chlorophyll b might be attributable to its relatively stable stoichiometry within light-harvesting antenna complexes; unlike chlorophyll a, which undergoes selective degradation under photoinhibitory conditions, chlorophyll b content appears to change more gradually with culture conditions, potentially resulting in a simpler mapping to image-derived color features.

Validation of models with independent data

The rigorous validation of predictive models using independent datasets is crucial to assess their generalizability and robustness beyond the specific conditions used for training, thereby preventing overfitting and ensuring practical utility39. In this study, model performance was primarily evaluated using the Mean Squared Error (MSE) on an unseen dataset, providing a direct measure of prediction accuracy. Additionally, the coefficient of determination (R²) was used to evaluate the goodness of fit between predicted and actual values40.

The true predictive power of the developed models was evaluated using a completely independent dataset from a second D. salina culture (Culture 2), which was initiated with a different cell density and followed a distinct growth pattern. The performance metrics of all models on this unseen data are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

For optical density estimation, both the RSM-based L*a*b* model and the ANN-based RGB model demonstrated strong and comparable predictive accuracy on the new data, with low MSE values of 0.00546 and 0.00502, respectively. This confirms the robustness of image-based OD prediction across different culture conditions.

The results for pigment prediction revealed more distinct differences between the modeling platforms. For carotenoid content, the ANN-based RGB model significantly outperformed all RSM models, achieving the lowest MSE (0.50694) on the independent test, which was less than half the MSE of the best RSM model (1.21480 for RGB). This underscores the superior ability of the ANN to generalize its learned non-linear relationship to new data.

A similar trend was observed for chlorophyll a, where the ANN-based L*a*b* model provided the most accurate predictions (MSE: 0.25176), substantially better than the best RSM model (MSE: 0.88621 for RGB). In contrast, for chlorophyll b, the RSM-based YCbCr model slightly outperformed the ANN models, achieving the lowest MSE (0.51370) on the validation set. This independent validation solidifies the initial findings: ANN models are generally more robust for predicting parameters governed by complex physiology (carotenoids, Chl a), while a simpler RSM model can be sufficient for a parameter with a more straightforward relationship to image color (Chl b).

The regression plots presented in Fig. 3 provide a direct visual confirmation of this validation outcome. These plots compare the actual measured values from the independent test set (Culture 2) against the values predicted by the selected best-performing models (as identified in Supplementary Table S1). The tight clustering of data points along the line of perfect fit (y = x) for the ANN-RGB (carotenoids) and ANN-L*a*b* (chlorophyll a) models graphically underscores their superior accuracy and generalization capability. Conversely, the comparable scatter patterns of the RSM-YCbCr and ANN models for chlorophyll b align with their similar MSE values, visually reinforcing the conclusion that a simpler linear modeling approach can be adequate for this parameter.

Crucially, the performance hierarchy of the models observed during the initial training phase was largely conserved when tested on the independent dataset from Culture 2. For instance, the ANN RGB model consistently showed the lowest error for carotenoids in both phases, and the RSM YCbCr model remained a top performer for chlorophyll b. This consistency between the initial evaluation and the independent validation strongly confirms that the observed differences in model performance are not due to random noise or overfitting to the training data, but rather reflect genuine, inherent differences in how effectively each modeling platform captures the underlying relationships between image color features and specific algal culture parameters.

Comparative analysis of model performance using CDF and heatmap

While aggregate statistics like MSE provide a single-value performance summary, they can obscure the underlying distribution of errors. The Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) plot offers a more nuanced view by showing the proportion of predictions that fall within a given error threshold, thereby directly quantifying prediction reliability and precision. This error distribution analysis is practically more informative than single aggregate metrics, as it directly quantifies the probability of predictions falling within a specified error tolerance—an essential consideration for real-time bioprocess control where decisions depend on predefined accuracy thresholds41. This is particularly valuable for assessing the practical utility of a model, as it indicates the likelihood of encountering errors of a certain magnitude.

The CDF of prediction errors for the selected best models (Fig. 4) revealed that 80% of the errors for the ANN RGB OD model and the ANN RGB carotenoid model were below 0.1 and 1.0, respectively, indicating a high concentration of low-error predictions. The error distribution for the ANN L*a*b* chlorophyll a model was even tighter, with 80% of errors below approximately 0.5. For chlorophyll b, both the RSM YCbCr and ANN RGB models showed similar CDF profiles, with 80% of errors between 0.5 and 1.0, confirming their comparable accuracy as suggested by the MSE values.

Complementary to error distribution, understanding how model accuracy evolves over time is critical for process monitoring applications. This is particularly vital in microalgal cultivation, where the cellular composition—specifically the ratios and concentrations of pigments—dynamically shifts in response to growth phase and applied stresses. Consequently, a model trained on data from one physiological state may lose predictive power in another. The temporal stability of the models’ predictive accuracy was assessed via a heatmap of MSE values across the days of Culture 2 (Fig. 5). The ANN-based models for OD, chlorophyll a, and carotenoids demonstrated consistently low error rates throughout most of the 26-day culture period, showcasing their robustness across these dynamic physiological shifts. In contrast, the RSM models for carotenoids and chlorophyll a exhibited a clear pattern of increasing error after approximately day 14, indicating a degradation in prediction performance during later culture stages. This superior temporal stability of ANN models reflects their ability to learn non-linear physiological dynamics without being constrained by a pre-defined polynomial structure, allowing them to adapt to evolving pigment–color relationships throughout the cultivation cycle, unlike linear RSM models whose accuracy declines after day 14. Notably, the RSM YCbCr model for chlorophyll b maintained relatively stable and low error across the culture timeline, performance on par with its ANN counterpart, reinforcing its suitability for this specific parameter.

In contrast to previous studies employing image analysis for algal pigment estimation, which typically report aggregate accuracy metrics (e.g., a single R² or MSE value) for the entire dataset or culture period13,15, our study introduces a novel, time-resolved evaluation framework. By deploying CDF plots and temporal heatmaps, we move beyond a static performance score to dynamically quantify prediction reliability and monitor its evolution throughout the cultivation timeline. This approach uniquely reveals, for instance, the declining predictive stability of certain linear models (RSM for carotenoids) in later growth phases—an insight critical for practical deployment but masked by traditional evaluation methods. To our knowledge, this is the first application of such a comprehensive temporal error analysis in the context of image-based monitoring of microalgal cultures. A direct comparison with previous studies further highlights the contributions of the present work. Prior image-based pigment monitoring approaches have typically employed a single color feature or color space and a single modeling approach—ANN13, linear regression14,15, or SVM16—to estimate one or two parameters, with reported R² values ranging from 0.967 to 0.9913–15. However, none of these studies included independent validation on a separate culture or evaluated model stability over time. In contrast, the present framework simultaneously predicts four parameters—biomass (OD₇₅₀), chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and total carotenoids—across an independent validation culture, systematically compares five color spaces and two distinct modeling platforms (ANN and RSM), and introduces temporal error analysis (CDF and Heatmap) to assess model robustness throughout the full cultivation cycle.

The significance of these findings extends beyond the specific case of D. salina. The demonstration that the optimal modeling configuration is dictated by the physiological behavior of the target parameter—with ANN excelling for non-linear stress responses and RSM sufficing for more linear relationships—provides a transferable framework for image-based monitoring across diverse microalgal species and bioprocess contexts. Furthermore, the temporal error analysis (CDF and Heatmap) establishes a new paradigm for model evaluation in this field, shifting the focus from static aggregate accuracy metrics to time-resolved performance assessment, which is essential for reliable in situ deployment where culture conditions continuously evolve.

Implications for bioprocess monitoring and control

The temporal stability assessment provided by the CDF and heatmap analysis transitions the developed models from laboratory curiosities to viable tools for industrial bioprocessing. This evaluation framework allows for the informed selection of operationally robust models—such as the ANN for carotenoid monitoring—that maintain accuracy throughout a full production cycle. It enables predictive maintenance schedules for models by identifying when accuracy degrades (e.g., RSM for Chl a after day 14), signaling the need for recalibration. Ultimately, this approach facilitates the design of adaptive, reliable, and cost-effective monitoring systems, where low-cost image sensors coupled with stable algorithms can replace expensive analytical hardware, ensuring consistent product quality and economic feasibility in large-scale microalgal cultivation.

The demonstrated success of the ANN and RSM models, particularly those utilizing the RGB and L*a*b* color spaces, in estimating OD, chlorophylls, and carotenoids from simple images, establishes a powerful, non-invasive platform for real-time, data-driven culture management. While this method is especially potent for optimizing stressed cultures of D. salina for β-carotene production, its implications extend beyond specialized bioprocessing. The core innovation—translating low-cost digital images into reliable multi-parameter predictions via AI—addresses a universal challenge in precision agriculture and controlled-environment farming: the need for affordable, real-time monitoring of crop physiology. By providing simultaneous insights into biomass and pigment profiles, this image-based strategy empowers precise control over environmental stress regimes, paving the way for intensified and predictable production not only in photobioreactors but also in smart greenhouse and vertical farming systems, where visual cues are paramount for early stress detection and yield optimization.

Conclusion

This study establishes and validates a novel, non-destructive image-analysis framework for the real-time, simultaneous monitoring of biomass and pigment dynamics in Dunaliella salina cultures. The central finding demonstrates that the optimal modeling approach is dictated by the physiological behavior of the target parameter: Artificial Neural Network (ANN) models, particularly using RGB and L*a*b* color features, delivered superior accuracy and temporal stability for predicting non-linear, stress-induced responses (carotenoids and chlorophyll a), whereas a simpler Response Surface Methodology model sufficed for the more linear chlorophyll b response. Crucially, temporal error analysis (CDF and heatmap) provided unprecedented insights into model robustness over a full cultivation cycle, revealing the sustained performance of ANN models compared to classical statistical approaches. Beyond microalgal biotechnology, this low-cost, smartphone-based framework translates into a practical tool for precision agriculture and smart farming applications, enabling real-time, non-invasive oversight of photosynthetic cultures. By bridging digital imaging with AI-driven analytics, this work paves the way for data-driven optimization of controlled environment cultivation systems, contributing to more sustainable and efficient production of high-value bioactive compounds.

Future work may extend these findings in several promising directions, which is necessary to overcome certain limitations of the present study. The framework was demonstrated under controlled laboratory conditions with D. salina; extending its application to diverse microalgal species, mixed consortia, and other pigmented microorganisms such as cyanobacteria, and outdoor cultivation systems would further establish its versatility. Similarly, while standardized imaging conditions were employed herein, integrating automated calibration protocols could enhance robustness against variable ambient lighting and camera specifications. The pixel-level interpolation approach used for ANN training proved effective; exploring alternative data augmentation or super-resolution techniques may further refine prediction accuracy for heterogeneous cultures. Additionally, coupling ANN predictions with explainable AI tools could complement the mechanistic interpretability offered by RSM. Finally, evaluating model performance over extended cultivation periods and under continuous or semi-continuous operating modes represents a natural next step toward industrial deployment.
