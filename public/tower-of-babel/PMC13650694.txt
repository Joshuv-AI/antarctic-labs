Paradoxical enhancement of oxidative stress-induced DNA damage and impaired redox homeostasis by Glutaredoxin 1 overexpression in human HeLa cells
Authors: Tingyi Zhao, Takahito Moriwaki, LanYun Yan, WeiZhi Wang, Qiu-Mei Zhang-Akiyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOxidants, including reactive oxygen species (ROS), oxidize intracellular molecules, such as lipids, proteins and deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA). Cysteine thiol groups in proteins are particularly susceptible to oxidation, forming intra- and inter-protein disulfides or disulfide bonds with glutathione (GSH). Glutaredoxin 1 (GRX1), an antioxidant enzyme, reduces these disulfide bonds by oxidizing and subsequently regenerating them via GSH, GSH reductase and reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH). While antioxidants protect cells from oxidative stress, excessive amounts may be harmful. This study investigated the effects of the overexpression of GRX1 in human tetracycline-regulated expression (T-REx) HeLa cells exposed to γ-irradiation, H2O2 or heat shock. GRX1-overexpressing cells exhibited reduced survival and impaired proliferation under these conditions relative to controls. Intracellular oxidant levels, measured using 2′, 7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate, were elevated in these cells following oxidative stress. We conducted alkaline comet and micronucleus formation assays to assess DNA damage and measured γH2AX levels. The results revealed increased single-strand breaks and double-strand breaks in GRX1-overexpressing cells under oxidative stress. Additionally, in GRX1 overexpressing cells, NADPH recovery was delayed after stress, indicating impaired redox homeostasis. Moreover, the overexpression of GRX1 reduced the expression levels of the stress response factor JNK. This study highlights the complex role of GRX1 in cellular stress responses, suggesting that excessive antioxidant activity may paradoxically increase oxidative damage and impair stress adaptation.Keywords: GRX 1 (Glutaredoxin 1), oxidative stress, reactive oxygen species (ROS), DNA damage (SSBs and DSBs), antioxidant defense

INTRODUCTION

Reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as superoxide anion radical (O2−), peroxides (e.g. H2O2) and hydroxyl radical (⋅OH), are produced through normal cellular metabolism and can also be generated by various environmental factors, including radiation and chemicals [1–5]. ROS play crucial roles in redox signaling pathways and the maintenance of cellular homeostasis; however, they can also disrupt cellular functions by oxidizing DNA, lipids and proteins [6–10]. Such oxidative stress can lead to cellular damage, aging, cancer and neurodegenerative diseases [11, 12].

Oxidative modifications primarily target the thiol groups of cysteine residues in proteins, potentially altering their function by forming disulfide bonds within or between proteins or glutathione (GSH) [13–16]. These modifications can either inactivate or activate proteins. For instance, in Escherichia coli, oxidation enhances the activity of the transcription factor OxyR. Similarly, oxidative stress can modulate the function of human ataxia-telangiectasia mutated (ATM) protein, which, when oxidized, activates p53 through phosphorylation [17, 18].

Glutaredoxins (GRXs) are enzymes that reverse oxidative modifications in proteins, thereby preserving the cellular function and redox balance [19]. These enzymes are categorized as either monothiol or dithiol, based on the number of cysteine residues in their active sites, and are essential in various cellular processes, including cell cycle progression and embryogenesis. GRX1, a dithiol GRX predominantly found in the cytosol, plays a crucial role in antioxidant defense by reducing GSH, detoxifying ROS and maintaining redox homeostasis [20, 21].

NADPH is essential for the function of antioxidant systems, including the GRX and thioredoxin systems, in maintaining cellular redox balance [22]. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PDH), a key player in the pentose phosphate pathway, is crucial for regenerating NADPH and thus influences cellular responses to oxidative stress [23]. However, an imbalance in antioxidant levels, such as excessive antioxidant activity, can have detrimental effects. For example, mice overexpressing thioredoxin exhibit increased tumor formation [24]. This paradox underscores the delicate nature of antioxidant balance and the importance of regulating it to prevent adverse outcomes, such as enhanced tumorigenesis.

This study examined the effect of the overexpression of GRX1 in cells subjected to oxidative stressors, including γ-rays, hydrogen peroxide and heat shock. We investigated how altered GRX1 levels impact cellular physiology, focusing on maintaining redox balance and preserving DNA integrity. Our findings suggest that the overexpression of GRX1 may compromise the oxidative stress response, leading to increased DNA damage and disrupted redox homeostasis, ultimately affecting cell survival and proliferation. Further research is needed to fully elucidate the regulatory role of GRX1 and its implications in diseases, such as cancer, cardiovascular disorders and neurodegenerative conditions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell culture

Cells were cultured as previously described [9, 25]. Briefly, we cultured the cells in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with L-glutamine (Wako), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS (Gibco-Invitrogen), and 5 μg/ml blasticidin in an environment with 5% CO2 and 100% humidity at 37°C. We treated T-REx HeLa/myc-GRX1 cells with 0.1 μg/ml tetracycline for 24 h to induce the expression of Glutaredoxin 1 (GRX1), unless otherwise noted. T-REx HeLa cells were obtained from Invitrogen.

Generation of T-REx HeLa/myc-GRX1 cells

T-REx HeLa/myc-GRX1 cells were established using the pcDNA4/TO/myc-HisA-GRX1 vector, as previously described [9]. We constructed the pcDNA4/TO/myc-HisA-GRX1 plasmid by subcloning the human GRX1 gene into the multicloning site of pcDNA4/TO/myc-HisA using HindIII and XbaI digestion. The GRX1 gene was amplified using PCR with the following primers: forward primer, ATATATAAGCTTGCCATGGCTCAAGAGTTTGTGAACTGC; reverse primer, ATATATTCTAGACTGCAGAGCTCCAATCTGCTTTAGCC. We transfected T-REx HeLa cells with the plasmid using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) and subsequently selected them with 100 μg/ml zeocin. The expression level of myc-GRX1 was assessed using western blotting. We purchased the pcDNA4/TO/myc-HisA plasmid from Invitrogen.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed as previously described [9, 25]. Both control T-REx HeLa cells and T-REx HeLa/ GRX1 cells were treated with 0.1 μg/ml tetracycline for 24 h before γ-ray irradiation. Whole-cell extracts were used, and the protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay; ~30 μg of protein was subjected to SDS-PAGE. After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in phosphate-buffered saline containing Tween-20 (PBST) (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 8.1 mM Na2HPO4, 1.5 mM KH2PO4 and 0.1% Tween-20) and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing three times with PBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Following three washes with PBST, the protein bands were visualized using Super Signal West Pico PLUS ECL reagent (Thermo Scientific) and imaged using a LAS-3000 mini (Fuji Film) or X-ray film (Fuji Film). Signal quantification was performed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health). The following antibodies were used: anti-ACTB (Proteintech, 66009–1-Ig, at 1:5000 dilution), hFAB™ Rhodamine Anti-Actin Primary Antibody (Bio-Rad, 12004163, at 1:10000 dilution), anti-ATM (phospho-Ser1981) (Santa Cruz, sc-47739, at 1:100 dilution), anti-BCL2 (Santa Cruz, sc-7382, at 1:100 dilution), anti-GRX1 (Sigma-Aldrich, SAB4200159, at 1:1000 dilution), anti-γ-H2AX (Sigma-Aldrich, MFCD04974783, at 1:1000 dilution), anti-JNK (Santa Cruz, sc7345, at 1:500 dilution), anti-myc (Santa Cruz, sc-40, at 1:300 dilution), anti-TRX1 (santa cruz, sc-271381, at 1:100 dilution), anti-TRX2 (santa cruz, sc-133201, at 1:500 dilution), anti-TUBB (Santa Cruz, sc-9104, at 1:1000 dilution) anti-53BP1 (phospho-Ser25) (Bethyl Laboratories, A300-652A, at 1:1000 dilution), HRP-conjugated Affinipure Goat Anti-Mouse IgG(H + L) (Proteintech, SA00001–1, at 1:5000 dilution), Rabbit Anti-Mouse IgG (HRP)(abcam, ab6728, at 1:10000 diilution), Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (HRP)(abcam, ab6721, at 1:10000 dilution), goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (Santa Cruz, sc-2030, at 1:5000 dilution) and StarBright Blue 700 Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (Bio-Rad, 12004 159, at 1:10000 dilution).

Immunostaining

Immunostaining was performed as previously described [9]. We cultured 5 × 104 cells for 24 h, then the expression of GRX1 was induced with 0.1 μg/ml tetracycline for an additional 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were washed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 8.1 mM Na2HPO4 and 1.5 mM KH2PO4) for 30 min, and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 2 min. The cells were blocked with 10% goat serum in TN buffer (0.1 M Tris–HCl, pH 7.5 and 0.15 M NaCl) at room temperature for 15 min, followed by incubation with an anti-myc antibody (Santa Cruz, sc-40, at 1:300 dilution) in Tris–NaCl–Tween 20 buffer (TNT) buffer (TN buffer containing 0.05% Tween-20) at room temperature for 1 h. After three washes with PBS, the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG antibody (Santa Cruz, sc-3890, at 1:300 dilution) and 100 μM DAPI in TNT buffer at room temperature for 1 h. Fluorescence was observed using an LX70 fluorescence microscope (Olympus). Fluorescence was then observed using an LX70 fluorescence microscope (Olympus).

Survival assay

Survival assays were conducted as previously described [9, 25]. Initially, cells were cultured for 24 h, then subjected to GRX1 induction with 0.1 μg/ml tetracycline for an additional 24 h. Following induction, the cells were either irradiated with γ-rays at a rate of 0.67 Gy/min or treated with H2O2 or methyl methanesulfonate (MMS) at specified doses for 25 min. After colony formation for 2 weeks of culture, the cells were fixed and stained with Giemsa. Colonies consisting of more than 50 cells were counted to determine the survival fractions.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation assays were conducted as previously described [25]. Initially, cells were cultured for 24 h, followed by induction of GRX1 with 0.1 μg/ml tetracycline for an additional 24 h. Post-induction, the cells were either irradiated with γ-rays at 5 Gy, subjected to heat shock at 44°C for 30 min, or treated with 150 μM H2O2 for 25 min. Subsequently, 0.42 × 105 cells from the treated groups and 0.30 × 105 cells from the untreated groups were seeded in 35-mm culture dishes. Cells were harvested, reseeded and counted at the specified time points using a hemocytometer and trypan blue staining.

Measurement of the total ROS level

Total ROS levels were measured as described in previous studies [9, 25]. Cells were cultured for 24 h and then GRX1 was induced with 0.1 μg/ml tetracycline for 24 h. Subsequently, 1 × 105 cells per 35-mm dish were either irradiated with γ-rays (5 Gy), treated with 350 μM H2O2 for 25 min, or subjected to heat shock at 44°C for 30 min. After 48 h of further culture, the cells were washed twice with PBS and then incubated with 1 ml of 50 μM 2′, 7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) in PBS at 37°C in the dark for 15 min. Following incubation, the cells were rewashed twice with PBS and analyzed using an LX70 fluorescence microscope (Olympus). Quantification was performed using the ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).

Alkaline comet assay

Cells were cultured for 24 h and then GRX1 was induced with 0.1 μg/ml tetracycline for 24 h. Post-induction, cells were treated with 350 μM H2O2 for 25 min. Cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and incubated with 70% ethanol for 5 min. After washing with PBS, cells were collected using cell scrapers, embedded in 1% low-melting agarose and spread onto microscope slides pre-coated with 1% agarose. A 100 μL aliquot of the cell suspension was immediately dropped onto a slide and kept at 4°C for 30 min. The slides were then immersed overnight at 4°C in an alkaline lysis solution (2.5 M NaCl, 100 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100 and 10% DMSO, pH adjusted to 10.0 with NaOH). Subsequently, slides were incubated for 20 min in an alkaline buffer (1 mM EDTA and 300 mM NaOH) and electrophoresed under the same buffer conditions (20 V, 300 mA, for 20 min). After washing in Tris-acetate-EDTA (TAE) buffer (40 mM Tris, 40 mM acetate and 1 mM EDTA), the slides were stained with 100 μM DAPI and analyzed using a fluorescence microscope (LX70, Olympus). The tail moment of the observed cells was calculated using the software.

Measurement of micronucleus formation

Micronucleus formation was assessed using a previously established method [10, 26]. We seeded 0.8 × 105 cells in 35-mm dishes and cultured them for 48 h. To synchronize cell cycle progression and facilitate the detection of micronucleus formation after DNA damage, thereby improving the sensitivity and consistency of the assay, the cells were then treated with 2.5 mM thymidine for 24 h, after which the thymidine was removed, and the cells were washed twice with PBS, followed by an additional 10-h culture period. Cells were subsequently treated with 1 mM hydroxyurea and incubated for 16 h. After this incubation period, the cells were cultured for another 24 h, followed by GRX1 induction using 0.1 μg/ml tetracycline for 24 h. After induction, the cells were either irradiated with γ-rays at a dose of 5 Gy or treated with 300 μM H2O2 for 30 min. After 24 h of further culture, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min, and stained with 200 μM DAPI. Micronuclei were observed using a fluorescence microscope (LX70, Olympus).

Measurement of the amount of NADPH

Cells were cultured for 24 h and then GRX1 was induced with 0.1 μg/ml tetracycline for an additional 24 h. Subsequently, cells were treated with H2O2 for 15 min. After two PBS washes, the cells were harvested using cell scrapers and resuspended in NADPH lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.5, 10% sucrose and 0.1% Triton X-100). Protein concentrations were determined using the Bradford assay. NADPH levels were quantified by measuring the OD340 of 100 μg protein extracts using a spectrophotometer.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SigmaPlot (ver. 12.0, Systat Software Inc.) or Microsoft Excel (Microsoft). Results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Differences between groups were determined using Student’s t-test, with P-values <0.05 considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Construction of T-REx HeLa cell lines overexpressing GRX1 and confirming GRX1 expression and localization involved several steps

The gene sequence encoding GRX1 was subcloned into the vector pcDNA4/TO/GRX1-myc-HisA, and this recombinant plasmid was transfected into T-REx HeLa cells. Several stable clones resistant to zeocin were isolated. We initially verified the successful overexpression of GRX1 in these cells. The expression of GRX1 was induced by adding tetracycline (0.1 μg/ml) to the transfected cells. GRX1 expression levels in these cells were assessed by western blotting with anti-myc and anti-GRX1 antibodies (Fig. 1A and Supplemental Fig. 1A). The molecular weight of GRX1, including the myc and His tags, was larger than that of endogenous GRX1. The clones corresponding to the upper band confirmed the overexpression of the GRX1 protein (Supplemental Fig. 1A). Consequently, post-tetracycline induction for 12 h, samples exhibiting bands identified as candidates for GRX1 overexpression were confirmed using an anti-Myc antibody (Fig. 1A). No significant difference was observed in the induction across different tetracycline concentrations (data not shown). The time-dependent induction level was also examined, revealing that the expression of GRX1 was induced after 12 h and persisted for at least 72 h (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, the localization of GRX1 was verified through immunostaining, which demonstrated that GRX1 was present in the cytoplasm (Fig. 1B). The confirmed cell clones were designated T-REx HeLa/GRX1.

GRX1-overexpressing cells were more sensitive to oxidative stress exposure

Previous studies have shown that GRX1 deficiency renders cells more sensitive to oxidative stress [25]. To investigate whether the overexpression of GRX1 increases resistance to ROS, we treated T-REx HeLa and T-REx HeLa/GRX1 cells with γ-rays, H2O2 and the DNA-damaging agent MMS and then performed colony formation assays. Cells overexpressing GRX1 were more sensitive than the control group when treated with H2O2 and γ-rays (Fig. 2A andB). However, there was no significant difference in survival rates in the MMS-treated groups (data not shown). These results suggest that the overexpression of GRX1 leads to decreased viability in T-REx HeLa cells following exposure to H2O2 and γ-rays.

Overexpression of GRX1 slightly inhibits cell growth under oxidative stress exposure

Subsequently, we investigated the effects of the overexpression of GRX1 on the proliferation of T-REx HeLa cells under oxidative stress conditions. As illustrated in Fig. 2C-E, the overexpression of GRX1 in T-REx HeLa cells did not affect cell growth under non-stress conditions. However, H2O2, γ-ray irradiation, or heat shock treatments inhibited cell proliferation and the GRX1-overexpressing cells exhibited slightly decreased growth efficiency in comparison to wild-type cells. These results suggest that the proliferation of T-REx HeLa cells may be inhibited by oxidative stress due to the overexpression of GRX1.

After oxidative stress exposure, the abnormal elimination of intracellular total oxidants, including ROS, in T-REx HeLa/GRX1 cells was observed

The reduced survival of GRX1-overexpressing cells following oxidative stress treatment suggests that the regulation of the intracellular redox state may be impaired in T-REx HeLa/GRX1 cells. We further assessed the level of intracellular total oxidants, including ROS, using DCFH-DA, which is readily oxidized by various intracellular oxidants, notably ROS, to produce the fluorescent molecule 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein [25, 27]. The fluorescence was monitored, and the results are shown in Fig. 3. We observed that oxidants accumulated more in GRX1-overexpressing cells (Fig. 3A and B) than in T-REx HeLa cells. Additionally, the fluorescence intensity in GRX1-overexpressing cells was significantly higher than that in wild-type cells after heat shock and H2O2 treatment (Fig. 3B). These findings support the notion that oxidative stress leads to an enhanced accumulation of intracellular oxidants, including ROS, in GRX1 overexpressing T-REx HeLa cells.

Increased levels of oxidized proteins were observed in GRX1-overexpressing T-REx HeLa cells after exposure to gamma rays

Previous studies have indicated that a deficiency in GRX1 leads to increased levels of oxidized proteins when cells are exposed to ROS [25]. This prompted us to investigate whether overexpression of GRX1 would increase or decrease cellular levels of oxidized proteins upon ROS exposure. Cells irradiated with 5-Gy gamma rays were collected post-irradiation, and the oxidized forms of proteins were detected. Initially, we conducted experiments using the Oxyblot protein oxidation detection kit. The time-course results demonstrated increased amounts of oxidized proteins in cells overexpressing GRX1. Interestingly, elevated levels of oxidized proteins were present in GRX1-overexpressing cells even in the absence of gamma-ray treatment (Supplemental Fig. 2).

Exposure to oxidative stress results in elevated levels of DNA strand breaks in GRX1-overexpressing cells

DNA damage often leads to an increase in cell mortality. The comet assay, a widely utilized technique, measures the levels of intracellular DNA damage. In our study, this assay was conducted under alkaline conditions, which allows for the detection of DNA single-strand/double-strand breaks (DSBs). Our findings revealed that, relative to the control group, GRX1-overexpressing cells exhibited an increase in DNA strand breaks following exposure to γ-ray irradiation under alkaline conditions, as illustrated in Fig. 4 A–C. Exposure to oxidative stress leads to an increase in DNA DSBs in GRX1-overexpressing cells

The alkaline comet assay results indicated that GRX1-overexpressing cells exhibited higher strand-break levels than the control group. Given that DNA DSBs typically result from oxidative stress exposure, we further examined the occurrence of intracellular DSBs. The correct segregation of chromosomes during cell division can be disrupted by DSBs, potentially leading to abnormal chromosome division and micronuclei formation. Hence, the presence of micronuclei serves as a marker for DSB occurrence in cells. Our observations revealed an increased rate of micronuclei formation in GRX1-overexpressing cells following γ-ray irradiation and H2O2 treatment, relative to that in wild-type cells (Fig. 4E–G). This increase suggests elevated levels of DSBs in GRX1-overexpressing cells.

DSBs trigger the rapid phosphorylation of the histone H2A variant, H2AX, at serine 139, forming γH2AX, a marker for DNA DSBs [28, 29]. Therefore, we examined the levels of γH2AX in T-REx HeLa control cells and T-REx HeLa GRX1-overexpressing cells by western blotting. After oxidative stress exposure, GRX1-overexpressing cells showed increased γH2AX levels relative to wild-type cells (Fig. 5A), further indicating that heightened DSBs were associated with the overexpression of GRX1 in T-REx HeLa cells.

T-REx HeLa cells overexpressing GRX1 exhibited reduced NADPH levels following H2O2 exposure

The results imply that cells overexpressing GRX1 might have a diminished capacity to scavenge intracellular oxidants, including ROS, thereby accumulating oxidative stress-induced DNA damage. Consequently, we further explored the potential causes underlying the increased intracellular oxidant levels. The introduction outlines that NADPH is critical in supporting antioxidant systems, such as thioredoxin and GRX, which help detoxify ROS and maintain cellular redox balance. Moreover, decreased NADPH levels can disrupt cellular redox balance, rendering cells more vulnerable to oxidative stress. To elucidate this, we quantified intracellular NADPH levels. Our findings revealed no notable difference between the control and GRX1-overexpressing groups under normal conditions. However, upon H2O2 treatment, NADPH levels were significantly lower in GRX1-overexpressing cells (Fig. 5B). This result suggests that the overexpression of GRX1 disrupts the NADPH balance in T-REx HeLa cells following oxidative stress exposure.

Differential expression of TRX1, TRX2, p-ATM, p-53BP1, JNK and BCL2 in GLRX1-overexpressing and T-REx HeLa cells following γ-ray irradiation

To evaluate whether the overexpression of GRX1 affects the thioredoxin system, which reduces disulfide bonds in target proteins, we examined TRX1 and TRX2 expression levels after γ-irradiation in T-REx HeLa control and GRX1-overexpressing cells. A densitometric analysis showed that γ-irradiation did not significantly increase the expression of TRX1 or TRX2 (Fig. 6A). No significant differences were observed between GRX1-overexpressing and control cells. These results suggest that the overexpression of GRX1 does not activate the thioredoxin system in response to γ-irradiation.

To investigate the impact of the overexpression of GRX1 on cellular stress response signaling pathways, we analyzed the expression levels of phosphorylated ATM (p-ATM), phosphorylated 53BP1 (p-53BP1), c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) and BCL2 by western blot analysis. ATM is a central regulator of the cell cycle that responds to DNA DSBs and oxidative damage caused by ROS, leading to its activation [30–32]. These signaling pathways play critical roles in various cellular functions; however, their regulatory interactions remain incompletely understood. The GRX system has been implicated in essential cellular processes, including DNA replication and the synthesis of deoxyribonucleotides required for DNA repair [33]. Our results showed that exposure to 5 Gy γ-irradiation did not significantly alter the levels of p-ATM or p-53BP1 in either parental T-REx HeLa cells or GRX1-overexpressing cells (Fig. 6B). JNK, a member of the MAPK family, responds to stressors such as oxidative stress, UV irradiation and cytokines. Notably, the baseline level of JNK in GRX1-overexpressing cells was lower than that in control cells even before irradiation. Consistent with this observation, JNK levels remained significantly lower in GRX1-overexpressing cells than in control cells both before and after irradiation (Fig. 6C, panel a). BCL2 is a key anti-apoptotic protein that promotes cell survival by inhibiting mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis. It plays an important role in cellular responses to stressors such as DNA damage, oxidative stress and radiation. Our results showed that BCL2 expression levels were much higher in GRX1-overexpressing cells than in control cells and that the expression was induced by gamma-irradiation (Fig. 6C andB). Together, these findings suggest that the overexpression of GRX1 alters the balance of redox-regulatory pathways by differentially modulating antioxidant responses following γ-irradiation.

DISCUSSION

Antioxidant enzymes play a crucial role in maintaining intracellular redox homeostasis. GRX1 is a thiol–disulfide oxidoreductase that reduces oxidized proteins, and GRX1 deficiency has been shown to increase cellular sensitivity to oxidative stress, ultimately leading to cell death [25]. These observations raise the important question: Does overexpression of GRX1 enhance cellular resistance to oxidative stress and protect human cells from oxidative damage?

In the present study, we established GRX1-overexpressing T-REx HeLa cell lines (Fig. 1) to examine the effects of GRX1 on cellular responses to γ-ray irradiation and H2O2 exposure. Contrary to our initial hypothesis, the overexpression of GRX1 significantly reduced cell survival and proliferation following γ-ray irradiation and H2O2 treatment compared with control cells (Fig. 2). In contrast, no significant difference was observed between GRX1-overexpressing and control cells following treatment with MMS. Notably, MMS is an alkylating agent that primarily induces DNA base damage without generating intracellular ROS, whereas γ-rays and H2O2 directly increase intracellular ROS levels. This difference may explain the selective hypersensitivity of GRX1-overexpressing cells to oxidative stress-inducing treatments.

Consistent with this interpretation, γ-ray irradiation, heat shock and H2O2 treatment markedly increased intracellular oxidant levels, including ROS, in GRX1-overexpressing cells. Unexpectedly, ROS accumulation was significantly higher in GRX1-overexpressing cells than in control cells under these stress conditions (Fig. 3). This finding contradicts the assumption that the overexpression of GRX1 suppresses the generation of ROS and enhances resistance to oxidative stress. Instead, our results indicate that the overexpression of GRX1 is associated with elevated intracellular oxidant levels under oxidative stress conditions, suggesting a disturbance in redox homeostasis rather than enhanced antioxidant capacity. Supporting this conclusion, we observed increased levels of oxidized proteins in GRX1-overexpressing cells following γ-ray irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 2), further indicating impaired redox homeostasis. Notably, GRX1-overexpressing cells exhibited higher levels of oxidized proteins even under unirradiated conditions. Although GRX1 is generally regarded as a protective oxidoreductase, excessive GRX1 expression may disturb basal redox homeostasis. One possible explanation is that enhanced deglutathionylation reduces protective reversible S-glutathionylation of proteins, thereby increasing their susceptibility to irreversible oxidative modification. In addition, persistent GRX1 overexpression may place excess demand on the GSH/NADPH-dependent reducing system, resulting in impaired maintenance of protein redox balance. Because the oxidized protein data are qualitative in nature, this interpretation should be considered cautiously.

Excess ROS can directly induce DNA single-strand breaks (SSBs) or indirectly generate oxidative base lesions, such as 8-oxoG, formamidopyrimidines (FaPy) and thymine glycol, which can lead to SSBs and DSBs during DNA repair [10, 34]. Using the alkaline comet assay, we detected that GRX1-overexpressing cells exhibited significantly increased SSBs/DSBs following H2O2 treatment (Fig. 4). This suggests that elevated ROS levels in GRX1-overexpressing cells lead to enhanced DNA damage under oxidative stress. Furthermore, micronucleus formation assays revealed increased genomic instability and DSB formation in GRX1-overexpressing cells exposed to oxidative stress [35] (Figs 4E–G). These findings were corroborated by elevated levels of γH2AX, a well-established marker of DSBs [36, 37], in GRX1-overexpressing cells (Fig. 5A).

Interestingly, previous studies have shown that GRX1 deficiency elevates intracellular oxidant levels but does not significantly affect nuclear DNA break formation [25]. The accumulation of ROS in GRX1-overexpressing cells raises questions regarding the underlying mechanism. One plausible explanation involves altered NADPH homeostasis, as NADPH is essential for maintaining cellular redox balance. We observed slightly reduced NADPH levels in GRX1-overexpressing cells under basal conditions, with a pronounced decrease following H2O2 exposure relative to control cells (Fig. 5B). Although the precise mechanism remains unclear, excessive GRX1 activity may increase NADPH consumption within the GRX system, thereby depleting the cellular NADPH pool and impairing the function of NADPH-dependent antioxidant enzymes.

Another possible mechanism underlying NADPH depletion involves impaired NADPH regeneration. NADPH is primarily regenerated from NADP+ through G6PDH activity in the pentose phosphate pathway [38]. G6PDH activity is regulated by several signaling pathways, including those mediated by ATM kinase [38]. ATM plays a critical role in DNA damage signaling and oxidative stress responses [39–41] and can be activated through the formation of disulfide-linked dimers involving cysteine residues at position 2991 [18]. Although the reductase responsible for cleaving this disulfide bond remains unidentified, overexpressed GRX1, which is predominantly localized in the cytoplasm but may translocate to the nucleus under stress conditions, could potentially interfere with ATM disulfide bond formation. Such interference could attenuate ATM activation, although direct evidence for this mechanism remains to be established. This may consequently reduce G6PDH activity and impair NADPH regeneration.

In Fig. 6, under unirradiated conditions, GRX1-overexpressing cells showed lower JNK levels and higher BCL2 levels than control cells. This pattern may reflect altered basal stress signaling caused by chronic GRX1 overexpression. Reduced JNK expression may indicate suppression or dysregulation of redox-sensitive stress response pathways, whereas elevated BCL2 may represent a compensatory pro-survival response to persistent intracellular oxidative imbalance. However, despite this apparently protective baseline profile, GRX1-overexpressing cells were more sensitive to oxidative stress and irradiation, suggesting that these compensatory changes are insufficient to preserve long-term redox homeostasis and cellular resistance.

Reduced NADPH availability may further compromise DNA repair capacity, as NADPH is required for ribonucleotide reductase (RNR) activity and the production of deoxyribonucleotides (dNTPs), which are necessary for DNA repair [42–44]. ATM-mediated regulation of G6PDH therefore contributes not only to redox homeostasis but also to nucleotide biosynthesis. Thus, GRX1-mediated perturbation of ATM signaling may result in insufficient NADPH and dNTP pools, ultimately exacerbating DNA damage accumulation [45]. A schematic summary of these proposed mechanisms is presented in Fig. 7.

Schematic model illustrating the role of GRX1 in redox regulation and oxidative stress response in cells. Upon exposure to stressors such as γ-rays, heat shock or hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), ROS are generated and oxidize protein thiol groups (protein–(SH)2) to form disulfide bonds (protein–S2). GRX1 catalyzes the reduction of these oxidized proteins back to their functional thiol form using electrons derived from NADPH via the GSH system. Under normal conditions, this GRX1-mediated redox cycle maintains protein thiol homeostasis and protects cells from oxidative damage. However, overexpression of GRX1 impedes the antioxidant function of the cellular reductase system by excessively consuming NADPH, thereby disrupting redox homeostasis and compromising the stability of the reductase system. This leads to elevated ROS levels and a decline in antioxidant defense capacity. Consequently, the accumulation of intracellular ROS induces DNA damage, resulting in impaired cell proliferation and increased cell death.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that the overexpression of GRX1 paradoxically sensitizes T-REx HeLa cells to oxidative stress rather than conferring protection. GRX1-overexpressing cells exhibit increased ROS accumulation, enhanced protein oxidation and elevated DNA damage, accompanied by reduced cell survival. These effects are likely associated with disrupted NADPH homeostasis and altered redox signaling, potentially involving impaired ATM activation. Our findings underscore that both deficiency of GRX1 and its overexpression disrupt redox balance, highlighting the importance of tight regulation of the GRX1 expression for maintaining cellular redox stability.

Although our data suggest a potential link between the overexpression of GRX1 and impaired ATM-mediated redox signaling, direct experimental validation is required. Future studies should therefore determine the ATM activation status, including disulfide-linked dimer formation and kinase activity, as well as G6PDH enzymatic activity and NADPH regeneration capacity in GRX1-overexpressing cells. In addition, rescue experiments restoring NADPH levels or modulating GRX1 expression will help establish causality. Further investigation of ATM redox signaling will be essential to clarify the mechanistic basis of GRX1-mediated redox dysregulation.
