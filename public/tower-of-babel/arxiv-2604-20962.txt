Abstract

1.2

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle\coloneq\{R(v):v\in V\},\text{ and }

We begin with trivial lower bounds on

Observation 2.1

For all choices of

and

\displaystyle\delta_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{k_{1}}{n},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{k_{2}}{n}.

In particular,
\delta_{1}

Proof.

Let
\lambda

. Then we have

\displaystyle\min_{R\in\mathcal{R}}\lambda(R)

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{1}}{n},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\min_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\lambda(B)

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{2}}{n},

and the result follows from the definitions of

.

We now aim to show that
\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}\leq 1
. Our strategy will be to randomly sample vertices

\lambda_{1}
and

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose that
\delta_{1}>\frac{1}{2}
and let
S=\supp(\lambda_{1})
. Then

is a red clique.

Proof.

Suppose for a contradiction that
xy\in G[S]
is not red. Let

, and let
\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
be given by

\lambda^{\prime}(v)=\begin{cases}\alpha(\lambda_{1}(v)-\ell),&\text{if }v\in\{x,y\};\\
\alpha\lambda_{1}(v),&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\alpha=\frac{1}{1-2\ell}
. Now taking

, we have

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}(R)-\lambda_{1}(R)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in R}(\lambda^{\prime}(v)-\lambda_{1}(v))

\displaystyle=(\alpha-1)\left(\sum_{v\in R}\lambda_{1}(v)\right)-\alpha\ell|R\cap\{x,y\}|

\displaystyle\geq(\alpha-1)\left(\sum_{v\in R}\lambda_{1}(v)\right)-\alpha\ell,

where the inequality follows from the fact that

is not a red edge and therefore cannot be contained in a red clique. Thus, by assumption,

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}(R)-\lambda_{1}(R)

\displaystyle>(\alpha-1)\cdot\frac{1}{2}-\alpha\ell

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{1-2\ell}-1\right)-\frac{\ell}{1-2\ell}=0,

and so

\min_{R\in\mathcal{R}}\lambda_{1}(R)<\min_{R\in\mathcal{R}}\lambda^{\prime}(R),

contradicting the definition of

. It follows that

∎

The above enables us to conclude the following:

Lemma 2.3
.

\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}\leq 1
.

Proof.

Let

as in

2.2
, and suppose for a contradiction that
{\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}>1}
. We may assume without loss of generality that
\delta_{1}>\frac{1}{2}
, and so it follows from

2.2

is a red clique.

Sample a vertex

\lambda_{1}
, and independently sample a vertex

\lambda_{2}
. That is, set

{\mathbb{P}[(s,t)=(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})]=\lambda_{1}(s^{\prime})\lambda_{2}(t^{\prime})}.

We consider
\mathbb{P}(t\in B(s))
, which we will show is bounded below by
\delta_{2}
, and above by

, from which the result follows.

Beginning with the lower bound, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(t\in B(s))

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in V}\lambda_{1}(s)\lambda_{2}(B(s))

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{s\in V}\lambda_{1}(s)\min_{v\in V}\lambda_{2}(B(v))

\displaystyle=\min_{v\in V}\lambda_{2}(B(v))

\displaystyle=\delta_{2},

as desired. For the upper bound, consider

. If

, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(t\in B(s)\mid t=t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq 1-\mathbb{P}(s\in R(t^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=1-\lambda_{1}(R(t^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\leq 1-\min_{R\in\mathcal{R}}\lambda_{1}(R)

\displaystyle=1-\delta_{1}.

If

, then it follows from

. In this case, take a vertex

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(t\in B(s)\mid t=t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{1}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=1-\lambda_{1}(V\setminus\{t^{\prime}\})

\displaystyle\leq 1-\lambda_{1}(R(v))

\displaystyle\leq 1-\min_{R\in\mathcal{R}}\lambda_{1}(R)

It follows that for all values of

We now apply linear optimisation to construct probability measures

\mu_{2}

Lemma 2.4
.

There exist probability measures
\mu_{1}
on
\mathcal{R}
and
\mu_{2}
on
\mathcal{B}
such that for every

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(\{X\in\mathcal{R}:v\in X\})\leq\delta_{1},\text{ and }

\displaystyle\mu_{2}(\{X\in\mathcal{B}:v\in X\})\leq\delta_{2}.

Proof.

We establish the existence of a suitable
\mu_{1}
, as the proof for
\mu_{2}
follows similarly. Consider the following linear optimisation problem.

\mathbf{P}
:

maximise

\sum_{X\in\mathcal{R}}\mu(X)

subject to

\sum_{X\in\mathcal{R}:v\in X}\mu(X)\leq\delta_{1}

\forall v

and

\mu(X)\geq 0

\forall X\in\mathcal{R}
.

We will show that if
\mu^{*}
is a maximiser for
\mathbf{P}
, then
\sum_{X\in\mathcal{R}}\mu^{*}(X)\geq 1
. It follows by scaling each
\mu^{*}(X)
term by the appropriate constant that a suitable probability measure
\mu_{1}
exists. Note that
\mathbf{P}
is a bounded linear program (since for all
X\in\mathcal{R}
,
\mu(X)
is bounded above by
\delta_{1}
) and that the feasible region of
\mathbf{P}
is non-empty. It follows that
\mathbf{P}
has an optimal solution, and so by the strong duality theorem its optimal solution is equal to the optimal solution of the dual problem

\mathbf{D}
:

minimise

\delta_{1}\sum_{v\in V}\lambda(v)

subject to

\sum_{v\in X}\lambda(v)\geq 1

\forall X\in\mathcal{R}

and

\lambda(v)\geq 0

\forall v
.

It therefore suffices to show that the optimal solution of
\mathbf{D}
is at least
1
. Now, let
\lambda^{*}
be a minimiser for
\mathbf{D}
, and let
\nu
be the probability measure on
V
defined by letting
\nu(v):=\frac{\lambda^{*}(v)}{\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V}\lambda^{*}(v^{\prime})}
for each
v\in V
. Then for each
X
in
\mathcal{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in X}\nu(v)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V}\lambda^{*}(v^{\prime})}

and so,

\displaystyle\min_{X\in\mathcal{R}}\nu(X)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V}\lambda^{*}(v^{\prime})}.

Optimising
\nu
over the collection of all possible probability measures
\mathcal{P}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V}\lambda^{*}(v^{\prime})}\leq\min_{X\in\mathcal{R}}\nu(X)\leq\delta_{1},

by the definition of
\delta_{1}
. It follows that
\delta_{1}\sum_{v\in V}\lambda^{*}(v)\geq 1
, as required. Hence, the primal problem
\mathbf{P}
satisfies
\sum_{X\in\mathcal{R}}\mu^{*}(X)\geq 1
, and we can take
\mu_{1}(X)=\frac{\mu^{*}(X)}{\sum_{Y\in\mathcal{R}}\mu^{*}(Y)}
to be a suitable probability measure on
\mathcal{R}
. Interchanging the roles of the red and blue cliques as well as
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
in this argument similarly establishes the existence of a suitable

, completing the proof.
∎

We are now able to bound

Lemma 2.5

Let
\delta_{1}^{\prime}\geq\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}^{\prime}\geq\delta_{2}

n\geq\frac{k_{1}}{\delta_{1}^{\prime}}+\frac{k_{2}}{\delta_{2}^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{\delta_{1}^{\prime}\delta_{2}^{\prime}}.

Proof.

Let
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
be probability measures on
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{B}
as described in

2.4
. For each vertex

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(v)

\displaystyle:=\mu_{1}(\{X\in\mathcal{R}:v\in X\}),\text{ and}

\displaystyle\mu_{2}(v)

\displaystyle:=\mu_{2}(\{X\in\mathcal{B}:v\in X\}).

Summing over every vertex in
V
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}\mu_{1}(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{X\in\mathcal{R}:v\in X}\mu_{1}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{R}}\mu_{1}(X)|X|

\displaystyle=k_{1}\sum_{X\in\mathcal{R}}\mu_{1}(X)

\displaystyle=k_{1},

and similarly,
\sum_{v\in V}\mu_{2}(v)=k_{2}
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}\mu_{1}(v)\mu_{2}(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{X\in\mathcal{R}:v\in X}\sum_{Y\in\mathcal{B}:v\in Y}\mu_{1}(X)\mu_{2}(Y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{R}}\sum_{Y\in\mathcal{B}}\mu_{1}(X)\mu_{2}(Y)|X\cap Y|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{X\in\mathcal{R}}\sum_{Y\in\mathcal{B}}\mu_{1}(X)\mu_{2}(Y)

\displaystyle=1,

where the inequality follows from the fact that a red clique and a blue clique intersect at at most

, let
\alpha_{i}=\frac{1}{\delta_{i}^{\prime}}
(noting that by

is positive). Then by

v\in V
, we have
\alpha_{i}\mu_{i}(v)\leq\alpha_{i}\delta_{i}\leq\alpha_{i}\delta_{i}^{\prime}=1
, and so

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in V}(1-\alpha_{1}\mu_{1}(v))(1-\alpha_{2}\mu_{2}(v))

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}(1-\alpha_{1}\mu_{1}(v)-\alpha_{2}\mu_{2}(v)+\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\mu_{1}(v)\mu_{2}(v))

\displaystyle=n-\alpha_{1}\sum_{v\in V}\mu_{1}(v)-\alpha_{2}\sum_{v\in V}\mu_{2}(v)+\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\sum_{v\in V}\mu_{1}(v)\mu_{2}(v)

\displaystyle\leq n-\alpha_{1}k_{1}-\alpha_{2}k_{2}+\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}

and so

, as required.
∎

Finally, we optimise this bound, establishing the lower bound on
n(k_{1},k_{2})
given in
Theorem

1.2
.

Lemma 2.6
.

For all
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 2
, we have

n(k_{1},k_{2})\geq\left(\sqrt{k_{1}-1}+\sqrt{k_{2}-1}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

By
2.1
, we have that
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}>0
, and by
Lemma

2.3
, we have
\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}\leq 1
. It follows that we may choose some
\delta_{1}^{\prime}
and
\delta_{2}^{\prime}
such that
\delta_{1}^{\prime}+\delta_{2}^{\prime}=1
,
\delta_{1}\leq\delta_{1}^{\prime}<1
and
\delta_{2}\leq\delta_{2}^{\prime}<1
. Then by
Lemma

2.5
, we have

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\geq\frac{k_{1}}{\delta_{1}^{\prime}}+\frac{k_{2}}{\delta_{2}^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{\delta_{1}^{\prime}\delta_{2}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{1}-1}{\delta_{1}^{\prime}}+\frac{k_{2}-1}{\delta_{2}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\sqrt{k_{1}-1})^{2}}{\delta_{1}^{\prime}}+\frac{(\sqrt{k_{2}-1})^{2}}{1-\delta_{1}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\sqrt{k_{1}-1}+\sqrt{k_{2}-1}\right)^{2}

by Sedrakyan’s inequality. It follows that
n(k_{1},k_{2})\geq(\sqrt{k_{1}-1}+\sqrt{k_{2}-1})^{2}
.
∎

To complete the proof of
Theorem

1.2
, it now suffices to construct a
(k_{1},k_{2})
-enabling graph on
n=(\sqrt{k_{1}-1}+\sqrt{k_{2}-1})^{2}
vertices whenever
(\sqrt{k_{1}-1}+\sqrt{k_{2}-1})^{2}\in\mathbb{N}
. In the case
k_{1}=k_{2}=k
, our construction gives a family of extremal examples that generalises the
P_{4}
blow-up construction of Feige and Pauzner.

Lemma 2.7
.

Suppose that

-enabling graph

Note that
2\sqrt{k_{1}-1}\cdot\sqrt{k_{2}-1}=n-(k_{1}-1)-(k_{2}-1)\in\mathbb{N}

and
\frac{k_{2}-1}{g}
are both square integers, so let

\displaystyle k_{1}

\displaystyle=1+ga^{2}

\displaystyle k_{2}

\displaystyle=1+gb^{2},

for positive integers

•

every vertex
v\in R
has exactly
gab

B
, and

•

every vertex

,

(gab,ga^{2})

Proof of
Theorem

1.3

We now consider the case where the complete graph on
n
vertices is edge-coloured with
r\geq 2
colours. Recall that
n_{r}(k)
denotes the least integer
n
such that there exists an
r
-coloured
k
-enabling graph on
n
vertices. We prove
Theorem

1.3
, that

2rk-2r(r-1)\leq n_{r}(k)\leq 2r(k-1).

Our proof strategy will be largely similar to the strategy for
Theorem

1.2
. Suppose that
G=(V,E)
is an
r
-edge-coloured complete graph on
n
vertices for some
r\geq 2
, and that
G
is
r
-coloured
k
-enabling. For each colour
i\in[r]
and for each
v\in V
, let
C_{i}(v)
be a monochromatic size-
k
clique of colour
i
containing
v
, and define
\mathcal{C}_{i}:=\{C_{i}(v):v\in V\}
. Take
\mathcal{P}
once again to be the set of probability measures on
V
, and define each
\lambda_{i}
and
\delta_{i}
analogously to
\lambda_{1}
and
\delta_{1}
in
Section

2
. That is, set

\delta_{i}\coloneq\max_{\lambda\in P}\min_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}\lambda(C),

and choose
\lambda_{i}
to be any probability measure satisfying

\min_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}\lambda_{i}(C)=\delta_{i}.

Finally, we define
\alpha_{i}\coloneq\frac{1}{\delta_{i}}
. Note that since
G
is
r
-coloured
k
-enabling, we have that for any two colours,
i
and
j
in
[r]
, the subgraph of
G
with edges coloured
i
or
j
must be
(k,k)
-enabling, and therefore all arguments from the previous section apply to this subgraph. Thus, by repeating the proof of
Lemma

2.4
for each colour
i
, we may again construct probability measures
\mu_{i}
on
\mathcal{C}_{i}
satisfying the property that

\mu_{i}(\{X\in\mathcal{C}_{i}:v\in X\})\leq\delta_{i}.

We write
\mu_{i}(v)
for
\mu_{i}(\{X\in\mathcal{C}_{i}:v\in X\})
, as in
Section

2
, and define

Y_{i}(v)\coloneq\alpha_{i}\mu_{i}(v).

Thus, for all
i\in[r]
and for all
v\in V
, we have
Y_{i}(v)\in[0,1]
. In order to produce a bound on
n
similar to that in
Lemma

2.5
, we will need the following inequality.

Lemma 3.1
.

For all real numbers
0\leq x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\leq 1
, we have

\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq m}x_{i}x_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}+1\geq 0.

Proof.

Let
S=\sum_{i<j}x_{i}x_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}+1
. Since
S
is a multilinear function of variables in
[0,1]
, it follows that the minimum value of
S
is achieved by some assignment
(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in\{0,1\}^{m}
. Let
s
be the number of variables
x_{i}
equal to
1
at this minimiser. Then
S=\binom{s}{2}-s+1
, which is non-negative at integer values of
s
, completing the proof.
∎

We will also use the following lower bound on
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

We have

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}\geq 2r.

Proof.

By applying
Lemma

2.3
with any distinct
i,j\in[r]
, we have that
\delta_{i}+\delta_{j}\leq 1
, and by
2.1
,
\delta_{i}>0
. Hence,
0<\delta_{i}<1
, and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta_{i}}+\frac{1}{\delta_{j}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\delta_{i}}+\frac{1}{1-\delta_{i}}

\displaystyle\geq 4.

Recall that
\alpha_{i}=\frac{1}{\delta_{i}}
, and hence
(r-1)\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=\sum_{i<j}(\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j})\geq 4\binom{r}{2}
. Since we have
r\geq 2
, it follows that
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}\geq 2r
, as desired.
∎

We now use
Lemma

3.1
and an adaptation of the proof of
Lemma

2.5
to prove the lower bound.

Lemma 3.3
.

For all integers
r,k\geq 2
we have
n_{r}(k)\geq 2rk-2r(r-1).

Proof.

Recall from the proof of
Lemma

2.5
that, for all
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}\mu_{i}(v)

\displaystyle=k

and

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}\mu_{i}(v)\mu_{j}(v)

\displaystyle\leq 1.

By selecting a vertex
v\in V
uniformly at random, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{k\alpha_{i}}{n},

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}Y_{j})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}}{n}.

Let
S
be a subset of
[r]
. Applying
Lemma

3.1
to the real values
\{Y_{i}\mid i\in S\}
and taking expectations, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i<j\in S}\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}Y_{j})-\sum_{i\in S}\mathbb{E}(Y_{i})+1

\displaystyle\geq 0.

Substituting in the values for
\mathbb{E}(Y_{i})
and
\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}Y_{j})
, and multiplying by
n
, gives

\displaystyle\sum_{i<j\in S}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}-\sum_{i\in S}k\alpha_{i}+n

\displaystyle\geq 0.

Let
m\coloneq|S|
and
\bar{\alpha}_{S}\coloneq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in S}\alpha_{i}
. Then we have

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\geq k\sum_{i\in S}\alpha_{i}-\sum_{i<j\in S}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}

\displaystyle=km\bar{\alpha}_{S}-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\bar{\alpha}_{S}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in S}\alpha_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq km\bar{\alpha}_{S}-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\bar{\alpha}_{S}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m\bar{\alpha}_{S}^{2},

where the final line follows from the fact that the root mean square of the real numbers
(\alpha_{i}:i\in S)
is at least their arithmetic mean
\bar{\alpha}_{S}
. Assume now that for a fixed
m
, we choose
S
to maximise
\bar{\alpha}_{S}
. Then
\bar{\alpha}_{S}\geq\bar{\alpha}
(where
\bar{\alpha}\coloneq\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}=\bar{\alpha}_{[r]}
), and so for all
0\leq m\leq r
we have

n\geq km\bar{\alpha}-\frac{m(m-1)}{2}\bar{\alpha}^{2}.

It remains to optimise this bound. Let

\displaystyle f_{m}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneq kmx-\frac{m(m-1)}{2}x^{2},

and

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle\coloneq\max_{0\leq m\leq r}f_{m}(x).

It follows that
{n\geq f(\bar{\alpha})}
. We show that
f(x)
is non-decreasing in
x
for
x>0
. Note that
f
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, and observe that

f_{m}(x)-f_{m-1}(x)=kx-(m-1)x^{2}.

If
f_{m}^{\prime}(x)<0
, then we have
km-m(m-1)x<0
, which implies that
m\neq 0
. Multiplying by
\frac{x}{m}
, this further implies that

f_{m-1}(x)>f_{m}(x)

whenever
f_{m}^{\prime}(x)<0
. Hence,
f_{m}^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
in the region where
f(x)=f_{m}(x)
. Thus,
f
is continuous and piecewise non-decreasing, and is therefore non-decreasing. By
Lemma

3.2
, we have that
\bar{\alpha}\geq 2
, so

\displaystyle f(\bar{\alpha})

\displaystyle\geq\max_{0\leq m\leq r}2km-2m(m-1)

\displaystyle\geq 2kr-2r(r-1),

and hence
n_{r}(k)\geq 2kr-2r(r-1)
, as desired.
∎

Theorem

1.3
, we now exhibit a construction for the upper bound.

Lemma 3.4
.

For all integers
r,k\geq 2
, we have
n_{r}(k)\leq 2r(k-1)
.

Proof.

Let
G
be a complete graph with vertex set
V(G)=V_{1}\sqcup V_{2}\sqcup\dots\sqcup V_{r}
, where
|V_{i}|=2(k-1)
for each
i\in[r]
. For each
i
, let the edges in
G[V_{i}]
be coloured
i
, and for each
1\leq i\neq j\leq r
let the bipartite graph
G[V_{i},V_{j}]
be coloured in such a way that each
v\in V_{i}
has exactly
k-1
neighbours in
V_{j}
of colour
i
and
k-1
neighbours of colour
j
(and vice-versa for vertices in
V_{j}
). An example in the case where
r=k=3
is given in
Figure

2

(k-1)
-regular bipartite graphs with vertex parts of size

is
r
-coloured
k
-enabling and has
2r(k-1)
vertices, completing the proof.
∎

Conclusion

While the lower bound found in

1.3

k

r
.
See
1.4

We conclude by observing that if
1.4
is correct, then the condition that
k
is sufficiently large with respect to
r
is necessary.

Lemma 4.1
.

There are infinitely many integers
k
such that when
r=k+1
, we have
n_{r}(k)<2r(k-1)
.

Proof.

Let
p
be prime, and let
k=p
,
r=p+1
. Consider the complete graph
G
on vertex set
V=\mathbb{Z}_{p}\times\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with edge-colouring
c
given by
c((x,y),(z,w))=\frac{x-z}{y-w}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\cup\{\infty\}
(where
c((x,y),(z,w))=\infty
if
y=w
and division is otherwise interpreted in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
). In the case
k=3
, this construction is demonstrated in
Figure

3
. Then
G
is
r
-coloured
k
-enabling, with the
k
-clique of colour
i
containing
(x,y)
given by

C_{i}((x,y))=\begin{cases}\{(x+in,y+n):n\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\}&\text{if }i\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\\
\{(x+n,y):n\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\}&\text{if }i=\infty.\end{cases}

Then
|V(G)|=p^{2}<2r(k-1)=2p^{2}-2
, as required.
∎
