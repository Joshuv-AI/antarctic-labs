Adaptive LQR active control of pantograph based on MDO algorithm
Authors: Hui Lichuan, Zhang Hanlin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the continuous increase in the operating speed of high-speed railways, the fluctuation of the contact force in pantograph–catenary systems becomes increasingly significant, posing higher requirements for current collection quality and operational reliability. Conventional passive control methods mainly rely on structural parameter optimization and lack online adaptability, which limits their effectiveness under complex operating conditions. Although the Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) provides good response performance and robustness, its weighting matrices are typically selected through empirical tuning, and the fixed-gain structure cannot adequately address the nonlinear and time-varying characteristics of pantograph–catenary systems. To overcome these limitations, this paper proposes an Adaptive Linear Quadratic Regulator based on the Multi-strategy Dandelion Optimization Algorithm (ALQR-MDO). First, the original Dandelion Optimization (DO) algorithm is enhanced by integrating several improvement strategies, including boundary reflection, sub-elite guidance, Gaussian–Cauchy hybrid mutation, and dynamic population adjustment, thereby improving global search capability and convergence efficiency. Second, an adaptive LQR controller based on pantograph head displacement-segmented gain scheduling is developed, in which smooth transitions of feedback gains under different operating conditions are achieved using linear interpolation. Subsequently, the weighting matrices of the ALQR controller are treated as optimization variables, and a fitness function considering both pantograph head displacement and contact force fluctuations is constructed. The optimal control parameters are obtained using the MDO algorithm. Finally, a pantograph–catenary coupled dynamic model is established and validated through simulations under different operating speeds. Additionally, control energy consumption analysis and closed-loop stability verification are conducted. Simulation results show that under 200 km/h operating conditions, the proposed ALQR-MDO method reduces the standard deviation and range of contact force as well as the standard deviation and range of pantograph head displacement by 49.72%, 41.92%, 59.69%, and 50.96%, respectively, compared with passive control. Under 300 km/h, the corresponding improvements reach 50.48%, 48.14%, 54.95%, and 48.60%. These results demonstrate that the proposed method significantly improves current collection performance while exhibiting strong adaptability to high-speed operating conditions and robust disturbance rejection capability.

Introduction

With the continuous increase in train operating speeds, the reliability of pantograph–catenary contact and the stability of current collection have become increasingly critical issues in high-speed railway systems1. The fluctuation amplitude of the contact force between the pantograph and the catenary increases significantly with train speed, which severely affects the current collection quality of the system2. Both insufficient and excessive contact force can adversely influence the stability of the pantograph–catenary system and reduce the service life of the pantograph. Therefore, maintaining the contact force within a reasonable range is a key requirement for ensuring stable and reliable current collection in high-speed railways3.

In passive pantograph control strategies, vibration suppression is typically achieved by optimizing structural parameters such as springs or pneumatic cylinders. Wilk et al.4 proposed a novel method for estimating inertial and dissipative parameters of a railway pantograph model by combining multibody dynamic simulation with experimental measurements, thereby enabling accurate parameter identification and resolving friction issues in pantograph joints. Gil et al.5 developed a nonlinear pantograph model by introducing two Z-shaped springs between the pantograph head and the frame, forming a multi-degree-of-freedom system. The model parameters were optimized using a genetic algorithm, effectively suppressing system oscillations. Although passive control methods are relatively simple to implement, they lack the ability to adapt to changing operating conditions.

Against this background, researchers have continuously explored and investigated active control strategies. In recent years, a wide range of advanced control architectures has been explored, many of which enhance structural flexibility by augmenting or extending the classical PID framework. The current state-of-the-art control strategies include the Gudermannian PID controller, softsign fractional order controller, sigmoid PID controller, and BELBIC PID controller, among others.

Reference6 proposed a Gudermannian function-based proportional-integral-derivative (G-PID) controller to enhance the transient performance of automatic voltage regulator (AVR) systems operating under highly dynamic conditions. By embedding the smooth, bounded nonlinear mapping of the Gudermannian function into the classical PID structure, the proposed controller improves adaptability to large signal variations while effectively suppressing overshoot. Reference7 proposed a novel fractional-order proportional-integral-derivative (softsign-FOPID) controller incorporating the softsign function, optimized using a fungal growth optimizer (FGO), for the stabilization and precise position control of an unstable magnetic ball suspension system. The proposed controller introduces the smooth nonlinear softsign function into the conventional FOPID structure to constrain abrupt control actions and enhance transient smoothness, while retaining the flexibility of fractional-order dynamics. Reference8 proposed a novel control method, the sigmoid-based fractional-order PID (SFOPID), aimed at improving the dynamic response and robustness of AVR systems. The parameters of the proposed SFOPID control strategy are optimized using the dandelion optimizer (DO). Simulation results demonstrate that the proposed DO-SFOPID control technique is highly successful in terms of stability and robustness. Reference9 proposed an optimal fractional-order BELBIC to improve the small-signal stability of an interconnected hybrid power system. The FOBELBIC is introduced to enhance the damping capability of automatic generation control (AGC). Simulation results clearly confirm the damping performance of the MOALO-based FOBELBIC, enhancing the small-signal stability of the hybrid power system. However, although many specialized PID variants exhibit excellent performance in single-loop stability, they typically require complex decoupling or structural modifications to match the high-dimensional state feedback employed for the pantograph-catenary system in this study.

Active control strategies, in contrast, improve the current collection quality of pantograph–catenary systems by applying external control forces through dedicated controllers. Various control approaches have been investigated, including sliding mode control, PID control, H∞ control, model predictive control (MPC), and linear quadratic regulator (LQR) control. These controllers are fundamentally based on state-space representations, which naturally accommodate the multi-input multi-output (MIMO) characteristics of the three-mass model. Walters10 applied a fuzzy control strategy to reduce fluctuations in pantograph–catenary contact force; however, the control accuracy remained limited and the controller parameters were manually tuned. Wen et al.11 proposed a pantograph active control algorithm based on Temporal Difference–Model Predictive Control (TD-MPC), in which MPC performs short-term trajectory optimization using a learned environmental dynamic model, while temporal difference reinforcement learning estimates long-term rewards to determine optimal control strategies. Chang12 established a pantograph–catenary dynamic model based on a three-mass pantograph representation and derived the equivalent excitation force generated by fluctuating wind acting on the contact wire, followed by the design of a sliding-mode active controller. Chen et al.13 designed an optimal output tracking controller based on the LQR method, demonstrating through simulations that the stability of pantograph contact force was significantly improved.

Overall, active control methods generally provide higher control accuracy but exhibit strong dependence on system models. Different control approaches have their own advantages and limitations in terms of robustness, computational efficiency, and constraint handling. Among them, LQR control combined with intelligent optimization algorithms provides an effective approach for improving control performance while maintaining relatively low online computational complexity.

Although the Riccati equation can provide the optimal state feedback gain for a given set of weighting matrices, traditional pantograph LQR active control strategies typically rely on manual experience to determine the weighting matrices, obtaining the optimal state feedback control law through repeated trial and error. This approach consumes significant time and effort, and the resulting optimal solution can only improve pantograph-catenary performance within a specific speed range, making it difficult to achieve global optimization. To overcome this limitation, intelligent optimization algorithms have been widely employed to determine optimal LQR weighting matrices. For instance, Yang14 optimized the weighting coefficients of an active suspension LQR controller using the Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA), significantly improving ride comfort and vehicle stability. Panjwani et al.15 designed an LQR controller for an inverted pendulum system and further optimized the weighting matrices using WOA and a multi-objective genetic algorithm, demonstrating superior reference tracking and robustness to parameter uncertainties. Feng et al.16 addressed the limitation of empirically selected weighting parameters in pantograph LQR control by optimizing controller parameters using an improved artificial bee colony algorithm, which significantly improved current collection quality. Mao and Luo17 combined a genetic algorithm with an LQR controller based on a three-mass pantograph model and verified through simulations that the optimized controller effectively reduced fluctuations in contact force and pantograph head displacement. Although these studies demonstrate the effectiveness of combining optimization algorithms with LQR control, most existing approaches focus on fixed-gain controllers, which cannot adequately adapt to the nonlinear and time-varying characteristics of pantograph–catenary systems under different operating speeds. Moreover, the convergence speed and susceptibility to local optima remain challenging issues for many optimization algorithms.

In recent years, several low-complexity optimization methods—such as the improved smooth function algorithm18, the norm-constrained SPSA (simultaneous perturbation stochastic approximation) algorithm19, and the safe experimental dynamics algorithm20—have attracted attention in the field of controller parameter tuning. The improved smooth function algorithm focuses on "denoising and smoothing" at the mathematical level, enhancing search stability by addressing the issue of non-differentiable objective functions. The norm-constrained SPSA algorithm, while maintaining the efficiency of high-dimensional stochastic search, ensures that controller parameters always remain within physically realizable ranges through hard constraints. The safe experimental dynamics algorithm shifts the perspective from “optimal results” to “process safety”, safeguarding system security during online tuning by employing probabilistic models or stability criteria. These three approaches target the three critical dimensions of function differentiability, parameter compliance, and experimental safety, respectively, providing a complete solution for controller tuning in complex systems that bridges mathematical computation and engineering practice. However, the optimization problem considered in this study involves high-dimensional weighting parameters. Furthermore, the evaluation of candidate solutions relies on time-domain closed-loop simulations, resulting in an objective function that is highly non-convex, multimodal, and irregular. Compared with traditional gradient-based optimization methods, the dandelion optimizer (DO)21 offers advantages such as a simple structure, few parameters, and strong global search capability. These characteristics make it suitable as the fundamental optimization framework for this study.

Pantograph–catenary coupled model

Pantograph model

Based on the pantograph–catenary electrical contact experimental platform developed by the research group (Fig. 1), systematic dynamic characteristic tests were conducted on the DSA380 pantograph.Fig. 1Pantograph–catenary electrical contact experimental platform.

Pantograph–catenary electrical contact experimental platform.

In this experimental platform, a pure copper wire with a nominal cross-sectional area of 120 mm2 is embedded in the groove along the edge of a rotating disk to simulate the contact wire in the catenary system. A metal-impregnated carbon strip is mounted on a transverse sliding platform to simulate the pantograph contact strip on the locomotive. The relative sliding motion between the wire and the strip reproduces the zigzag contact trajectory observed in real pantograph–catenary interactions.

By simulating pantograph–catenary contact processes under different operating speeds, multidimensional dynamic data—including pantograph head displacement, contact force, and frame vibration—are collected for subsequent analysis.

The pantograph, which serves as the current collection device of an electric train, consists of several key components, including the pantograph head, frame, base, and transmission mechanism22. In pantograph–catenary dynamic analysis, the contact between the pantograph and the catenary is commonly modeled as the interaction between the pantograph and a variable-stiffness spring.

In the study of pantograph dynamic characteristics, lumped-mass equivalent models are commonly employed for modeling and analysis23. Using frequency response methods, the pantograph system can be simplified into two or three equivalent concentrated masses to describe its dynamic behavior. Based on the experimental data obtained from the platform, this study identifies the equivalent mass, stiffness, and damping parameters of the pantograph system using a combination of the frequency response method and least-squares parameter identification.

Previous studies have shown that when the train speed exceeds 250 km/h, the three-mass model provides higher simulation accuracy and more accurately reflects the dynamic interactions among different pantograph components24. Therefore, the pantograph is modeled using a three-mass lumped parameter model, as shown in Fig. 2. The key parameters of the pantograph obtained from experimental data are summarized in Table 5. Catenary model

Catenary systems can be classified into several types, including simple suspension, simple catenary suspension, compound catenary suspension, and elastic catenary suspension. Among these, the elastic catenary suspension optimizes the distribution of structural elasticity by incorporating elastic droppers, making the elasticity at support points and mid-span more uniform. At speeds of 160 km/h and above, it significantly reduces contact force fluctuations and improves the dynamic current collection performance of the pantograph-catenary system. Additionally, it offers unique advantages in vibration suppression and wave propagation control in scenarios involving multiple pantographs or complex current collection. Its parametric characteristics align well with the high reliability and stability requirements of modern high-speed railways and urban rail transit power supply systems.

Furthermore, this type of catenary has been validated in engineering applications on multiple high-speed lines both domestically and internationally. Modeling based on its parameters effectively enhances the engineering applicability and accuracy of simulation results, providing a scientifically reliable analytical basis for the matching design, performance optimization, and fault prediction of pantograph-catenary systems. Therefore, this paper selects the elastic catenary suspension as the modeling type for the contact wire. Adaptive LQR controller design

Adaptive gain scheduling strategy

The conventional LQR controller generally exhibits satisfactory convergence and stability under ideal operating conditions. However, its control performance is highly dependent on the selection of the weighting matrices. In pantograph–catenary systems, the contact stiffness varies nonlinearly with contact force, and significant geometric nonlinearities may occur during large-amplitude vibrations. Consequently, a fixed weighting matrix cannot effectively accommodate the full-speed operating conditions of the system, which may lead to degraded control performance.

To address this issue, an adaptive gain scheduling strategy based on pantograph head displacement is proposed in this study. The pantograph head displacement is divided into several operating regions, and a local linear controller is designed for each region. By dynamically adjusting the weighting matrices according to the operating region, smooth switching between controllers can be achieved, thereby improving the stability and reliability of the pantograph control system under varying operating conditions.

The vertical displacement of the pantograph head directly reflects the vibration level of the system and is closely related to the fluctuation of the contact force during train operation. Therefore, the pantograph head displacement amplitude is selected as the scheduling variable in this study. Based on the vibration characteristics of the pantograph and the service standards of the catenary system, three gain scheduling points are defined.

Adaptive LQR controller design

For each scheduling point, a corresponding LQR controller is designed. In the pantograph–catenary system model, the catenary stiffness is time-varying, which results in time-varying system matrices. Improved boundary handling strategy

In the original DO algorithm, boundary handling is performed after each position update in the three evolutionary stages (rising, descending, and landing). The original boundary handling rule is defined as21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{*{20}c} {X_{new} = \left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}c} {max\left( {X_{i,j} ,lb} \right),X_{i,j} \le lb} \\ {min\left( {X_{i,j} ,ub} \right),X_{i,j} \ge ub} \\ \end{array} } \right.} \\ \end{array}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{i,j}$$\end{document} represents the position of the individual before boundary checking, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{new}$$\end{document} denotes the updated position after boundary correction, lb and ub represent the lower and upper bounds of the search space, respectively.

Based on the processing method described in the above equation, seeds that fall below the lower bound of the search space are updated to the lower boundary position, while those exceeding the upper bound are moved to the upper boundary. This approach tends to cause out-of-bounds individuals to cluster near the boundaries, which is detrimental to maintaining population diversity.

Inspired by the lens opposition-based learning strategy26, a boundary reflection mechanism is introduced in this study. When an individual exceeds the search boundary, it is reflected back into the feasible region while preserving a random displacement proportional to the distance beyond the boundary. This strategy maintains population diversity while preventing excessive concentration of individuals at the boundaries. The improved boundary handling rule is expressed as22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{*{20}c} {X_{i,j}{\prime}^{t + 1} = \left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}c} {X_{i,j}^{t + 1} - \left( {1 + r} \right)\left( {X_{i,j}^{t + 1} - ub} \right),X_{i,j}^{t + 1} > ub} \\ {X_{i,j}^{t + 1} + \left( {1 + r} \right)\left( {lb - X_{i,j}^{t + 1} } \right),X_{i,j}^{t + 1} < lb} \\ \end{array} } \right.} \\ \end{array}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{X}_{i,j}{\prime}}^{t+1}$$\end{document} denotes the new reflected position after boundary handling, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{i,j}^{t+1}$$\end{document} is the original position of the individual exceeding the boundary in the current iteration and r is a random number in (0,1].

Search strategy optimization

In the landing stage of the original DO algorithm, the search process relies solely on the current global best individual to guide the population. Although this strategy can accelerate convergence, it may also cause individuals to cluster around a single elite solution, increasing the risk of premature convergence and trapping the algorithm in local optima.

To overcome this limitation, a dual-elite cooperative guidance mechanism is introduced in this study. In the proposed strategy, both the best individual and the second-best individual in the current population are used to guide the search direction.

Compared with the original single-elite strategy, the dual-elite mechanism provides a more robust search direction and reduces the risk of misleading guidance caused by an inaccurate elite solution. Consequently, individuals are more likely to move toward promising regions in the search space, thereby improving the efficiency and stability of the optimization process.

The improved position update rule for the landing stage is defined as23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{*{20}c} {X_{i,j}^{t + 1} = mX_{elite}^{t} + \left( {1 - m} \right)X_{elite2}^{t} + Levy\left( \lambda \right) \cdot \alpha \cdot \left( {X_{elite}^{t} - X_{i,j}^{^{\prime\prime}t + 1} \cdot \frac{2t}{T}} \right)} \\ \end{array}$$\end{document}where m is a fixed coefficient set to 0.55, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{elite}^{t}$$\end{document} represents the global elite individual position in the t-th iteration, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{elite2}^{t}$$\end{document} denotes the global sub-elite individual position in the t-th iteration.

This strategy effectively balances exploration and exploitation, allowing the algorithm to maintain diversity while accelerating convergence.

Gaussian–Cauchy hybrid mutation

During the later stages of optimization, individuals in the population tend to cluster around the current best solution. If this solution is not the global optimum, the population may stagnate in a local optimum, preventing further improvement of the solution quality.

To alleviate this issue, a Gaussian–Cauchy hybrid mutation strategy is introduced to perturb elite individuals and increase population diversity. Gaussian mutation mainly enhances local search capability, as the generated perturbations are usually small and concentrated around the current solution. In contrast, Cauchy mutation produces occasional large perturbations, which helps the algorithm escape from local optima and improves global exploration ability27.

Therefore, a hybrid mutation strategy combining Gaussian mutation and Cauchy mutation with equal probability (0.5) is adopted in this study. Dynamic population adjustment strategy

During the natural dispersal process of dandelion seeds, environmental conditions such as climate changes may influence the success rate of seed propagation. Adverse weather conditions may reduce the number of seeds that can successfully spread and survive.

Inspired by this natural phenomenon, a dynamic population adjustment strategy is introduced in the landing stage of the MDO algorithm. In the early stage of the optimization process, a larger population is beneficial for global exploration, enabling the algorithm to search a wider region of the solution space. However, in the later stage of optimization, excessive population size may reduce convergence speed and increase computational cost.

Therefore, the number of individuals in the landing stage is gradually reduced as the iteration progresses, allowing the algorithm to focus more on local exploitation in the later stage. The dynamic population adjustment rule is defined as25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{*{20}c} {num_{i}{\prime} = round\left[ {\left( {1 - \frac{at}{{T_{max} }} \cdot e^{{\frac{bt}{{T_{max} }}}} } \right) \cdot num_{i} } \right]} \\ \end{array}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${num}_{i}{\prime}$$\end{document} is the population size during the landing phase at the current iteration, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${num}_{i}$$\end{document} is the population size after the previous iteration, and a and b are fixed parameters set to 0.02 and 0.3, respectively.

Convergence analysis

To provide a rigorous theoretical foundation for the proposed MDO algorithm, we analyze the population’s evolutionary behavior as a discrete-time linear time-varying (LTV) dynamical system28–30. Specifically, focusing on the landing stage where the final convergence occurs, the position update rule for a seed across each dimension can be simplified into a one-dimensional form by treating the elite positions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{elite}^{t}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{elite2}^{t}$$\end{document} as local constants. The update equation is expressed as:26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{*{20}c} {x_{c} \left( {t + 1} \right) = - S \cdot \alpha \cdot \frac{2t}{{T_{max} }} \cdot x_{c} \left( t \right) + \left[ {p + S \cdot \alpha \cdot p_{1} } \right]} \\ \end{array}$$\end{document}

This can be generalized into the standard state-space representation:27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{*{20}c} {y\left( {t + 1} \right) = A\left( t \right) \cdot y\left( t \right) + B\left( t \right)} \\ \end{array}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y(t)={x}_{c}(t)$$\end{document} represents the state variable, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A(t)=[-S\cdot \alpha \cdot \frac{2t}{{T}_{max}}]$$\end{document} is the time-varying coefficient matrix, and B(t) denotes the external input term influenced by the dual-elite guidance. According to Lyapunov stability theory for discrete systems, a sufficient condition for the asymptotic stability and convergence of the individual state sequence x(t) is that the spectral radius of the transition matrix, represented here by the eigenvalue λ (t), satisfies \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|\lambda (t)|\le 1$$\end{document} throughout the iterative process.

As illustrated in Fig. 5, the eigenvalue analysis reveals a distinct contrast between the original and improved algorithms. In the original DO, the magnitude of λ (t) frequently exceeds the stability boundary (> 1) during early iterations, leading to erratic search trajectories and potential divergence. In contrast, the MDO eigenvalues remain strictly within the unit circle |λ (t)|<1 and monotonically approach zero as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t\to {T}_{max}$$\end{document}. This mathematical framework confirms that the dual-elite cooperative guidance and adaptive step-size scaling ensure stable convergence toward the global optimum while eliminating the unstable search regions inherent in the baseline algorithm.Fig. 5Comparison of Eigenvalue Changes.

Conclusion

An Adaptive Linear Quadratic Regulator (ALQR) controller optimized via the Multi-strategy Dandelion Optimization (MDO) algorithm is proposed for pantograph active control in high-speed railway systems.

The main conclusions are as follows:A multi-strategy improved Dandelion Optimization (MDO) algorithm was developed, integrating boundary reflection, dual-elite guidance, Gaussian–Cauchy hybrid mutation, and dynamic population adjustment. Benchmark function tests indicate that MDO outperforms DO, PSO, and GA in terms of convergence speed and optimization accuracy.An adaptive LQR controller with displacement-based gain scheduling was designed to dynamically adjust control gains under different vibration conditions. This method effectively accommodates the nonlinear and time-varying characteristics of the pantograph–catenary system, outperforming conventional fixed-gain LQR controllers.Simulation results under 200 km/h and 300 km/h show that the ALQR-MDO controller significantly reduces pantograph vibration and contact force fluctuations. Quantitative improvements include: Compared with passive control, the standard deviation of contact force and pantograph head displacement are reduced by 49.72% and 59.69%, respectively, under the 200 km/h operating condition; under the 300 km/h condition, the reductions are 50.48% and 54.95%, respectively.Frequency-domain analysis confirms effective suppression of dominant vibration modes (~ 4 Hz). Control energy analysis shows that the MDO-optimized controller reduces actuator energy by 11.6% compared with DO-optimized LQR.Closed-loop eigenvalue analysis and robustness verification confirm the stability and robustness of the ALQR-MDO controller, validating its suitability for high-speed railway pantograph systems.
