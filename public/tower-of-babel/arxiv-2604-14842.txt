Simplification Ad Absurdum? Revisiting Gas Flow Modeling for Integrated Energy System Planning Thanks: This work is part of the project iKlimEt (FO999910627), which has received funding in the framework of ”Energieforschung”, a research and technology program of the Klima- und Energiefonds.
Thomas Klatzer, Yannick Werner, Sonja Wogrin

Abstract
This paper analyzes the implications of simplified pipeline gas flow models for integrated energy system planning. A case study of an integrated power–hydrogen expansion planning problem shows that simplifying pressure–flow relationships and gas dynamics can lead to expansion plans that incur substantial regret when evaluated under a more realistic dynamic gas flow model – due to suboptimal system expansion, operation, and non-supplied hydrogen. Numerical experiments show that planning under the highly simplified transport and transport-linepack models – commonly used in expansion studies – can result in regret exceeding several thousand percent and yield expansion plans that lack robustness across demand levels. Planning under steady-state conditions partially mitigates these effects, but still leaves significant cost-reduction potential untapped compared to dynamic planning due to neglected linepack flexibility. Developing efficient solution algorithms for the dynamic model is a promising direction for future research.



I
Introduction

The transition towards decarbonized energy systems increasingly emphasizes integrated planning of the power and gas sectors
[
EUEnergySystemIntegration2020
]
to support large-scale production of renewable hydrogen
[
EURFNBO2023
]
and transmission via pipelines
[
EHB2023
]
. Planning of the underlying power–gas infrastructure is commonly supported by integrated energy system optimization models (ESOMs). Within ESOMs, modeling pipeline gas flows is particularly challenging, as gas transport is governed by nonlinear and non-convex pressure–flow relationships and slow dynamics that enable short-term storage via linepack
[
Raheli2025
]
.

Against this background, pipeline expansion planning – traditionally addressed by the gas systems community – has received increasing attention in the power systems community. Due to differing objectives, however, the problem is treated differently across the two fields.
The gas systems community typically focuses on optimal network design and does not explicitly consider coupled decisions on gas production or storage, e.g.,
[
Andre2013
,
Baufume2013
,
Borraz-Sanchez2016
]
. Here, steady-state gas flow models are state-of-the-art for expansion planning, while dynamic models are used for operational control
[
Koch2015
,
Lenz2021
]
.
In contrast, the power systems community focuses on integrated expansion of generation, storage, and transmission across power and gas sectors. Gas flow modeling ranges from dynamic
[
Zeng2017
]
, to steady-state
[
Borraz-Sanchez2016a
]
, to highly simplified transport models that approximate linepack
[
Bodal2024
,
He2021
]
, or omit it entirely
[
Zhao2018
,
Neumann2023
]
. Due to its computational efficiency, the latter is commonly used in ESOMs such as PyPSA
[
Brown2018
]
and LEGO
[
Wogrin2022
]
.

Systematic comparisons of modeling approaches across different levels of gas flow representation in expansion planning remain scarce. One notable exception is
[
Reuss2019
]
, which analyzes pipeline expansion and hydrogen reconversion in a star- and tree-shaped network under a discrete arc-sizing model, comparing steady-state and transport formulations. However, the study relies on exogenous assumptions for power generation and hydrogen production capacities and does not consider dynamic gas flow representations.
Overall, a comprehensive comparison of gas flow modeling choices for expansion planning and a systematic evaluation of commonly used simplifications are missing. In this work, we address this gap by analyzing the implications of considering (or omitting) technical gas flow characteristics on the integrated expansion of renewable-powered electrolyzers and hydrogen transmission pipelines. Specifically, our contributions are twofold:

(1) We compile and harmonize a range of pipeline gas flow models within a single modeling framework, enabling straightforward comparison across different levels of technical detail – from a dynamic model that captures nonlinear pressure–flow relationships and slow gas dynamics (linepack) to a highly simplified transport model.
(2) We conduct in-depth analyses of expansion outcomes for a stylized yet realistic power-hydrogen system and illustrate the implications of planning decision obtained from simplified gas flow models across demand levels when validated under dynamic conditions.

III
, the numerical experiments and results are presented. Finally, Section
concludes the paper.

II-B

Gas Flow Modeling Prerequisites

Following standard assumptions in the literature, e.g.,
[
Koch2015
,
Lenz2021
]
, gas flow in ESOMs is modeled assuming horizontal isothermal pipelines using a system of nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs) describing conservation of momentum (
2a
) and mass (
2b
),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}+\frac{c^{2}\lambda}{2DA^{2}}\frac{f|f|}{p}=0,

(2a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}\frac{A}{c^{2}}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=0,

(2b)

where
x
and
t
denote space and time, and the unknowns are the gas flow
f
(
\text{\,}\mathrm{kg}
) and gas pressure
p
(
\text{\,}\mathrm{bar}
). The parameters
D
(
\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
) and
A
(
\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}
) represent the pipeline diameter and cylindrical cross section, while
c
deontes the speed of sound, which we define as
c=$1320\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$
​ for hydrogen. The friction factor
\lambda
captures friction-induced pressure losses in pipeline gas flow and is commonly approximated using the Nikuradse formula. Finally,
|\cdot|
denotes the absolute value.

For use in ESOMs, the PDEs are discretized in space and time using the pipeline length
L
(
\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
) and the time resolution
\Delta
as the spatial and temporal step sizes, respectively.

II-C

Dynamic Model

The resulting discretized PDEs define the dynamic model.
Let
\pi_{t,i}
denote the gas pressure (
\text{\,}\mathrm{bar}
) at node
i\in\mathcal{I}
. For compactness, the parameters
R_{a,d}=\frac{16c^{2}L_{a}\lambda_{d}}{\pi^{2}D^{5}_{a,d}}
and
H_{a,d}=\frac{8c^{2}}{\pi L_{a}D_{a,d}^{2}}
condense pipeline characteristics, where
L_{a}
,
D_{a,d}
, and
\lambda_{d}
denote the pipeline length (
\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
), diameter (
\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
), and corresponding friction factor, respectively, while
c
and
\pi
are constants. Moreover,
\underline{\Pi}_{i}
and
\overline{\Pi}_{i}
represent the minimum and maximum nodal gas pressure (
\text{\,}\mathrm{bar}
), respectively, and
\Gamma_{c}
is the compression ratio of compressor
c
.
The
dynamic model
is formulated as:

\displaystyle\big(\pi^{2}_{t,i}-\pi^{2}_{t,j}\big)=\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}R_{a,d}x_{a,d}\ f_{t,a}|f_{t,a}|\quad\forall t,a

(3a)

\displaystyle f_{t,a}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(f^{\mathrm{In}}_{t,a}+f^{\mathrm{Out}}_{t,a}\Big)\quad\forall t,a

(3b)

\displaystyle\pi_{t,i}+\pi_{t,j}=\pi_{t-1,i}+\pi_{t-1,j}

\displaystyle+\Delta\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\!H_{a,d}x_{a,d}\Big(f^{\mathrm{In}}_{t,a}-f^{\mathrm{Out}}_{t,a}\Big)\quad\forall~t\in\mathcal{T}\backslash\{1\},a

(3c)

\displaystyle\pi_{t,i}+\pi_{t,j}=\pi_{T,i}+\pi_{T,j}

\displaystyle+\Delta\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\!H_{a,d}x_{a,d}\Big(f^{\mathrm{In}}_{t,a}-f^{\mathrm{Out}}_{t,a}\Big)\quad\forall~t\in\{1\},a

(3d)

\displaystyle\pi_{t,j}=\Gamma_{c}\pi_{t,i}\quad\forall t,c

(3e)

\displaystyle\underline{\Pi}_{i}\leq\pi_{t,i}\leq\overline{\Pi}_{i}\quad\forall t,i

(3f)

In (
3
), constraint (
3a
) represents the momentum equation governing bidirectional gas flow, defined as the average of inflow and outflow in (
3b
). Constraint (
3c
) enforces mass conservation across consecutive time steps, thereby capturing a pipeline’s short-term storage capability via linepack, while (
3d
) cyclically links the final and first time step. Constraint (
3e
) describes pressure increases from compressors, and (
3f
) imposes lower and upper bounds on nodal pressures. The dynamic model constitutes a mixed-integer nonlinear program (MINLP) with time-linking constraints and is the most computationally demanding among the models considered.

II-D

Steady-state Model

The dynamic model (
3
) can be simplified by assuming stationary gas flow, i.e., inflow equals outflow. Consequently, the mass balance constraints (
3c
) and (
3d
) are omitted. In this setting, pressure variables are expressed as squared nodal gas pressure (
\text{\,}{\mathrm{bar}}^{2}
), denoted by
\pi^{\mathrm{Sqr}}_{t,i}
, with corresponding bounds
\underline{\Pi}^{\mathrm{Sqr}}
and
\overline{\Pi}^{\mathrm{Sqr}}
. The
steady-state model
is formulated as:

\displaystyle\big(\pi^{\mathrm{Sqr}}_{t,i}-\pi^{\mathrm{Sqr}}_{t,j}\big)=\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}R_{a,d}x_{a,d}\ f_{t,a}|f_{t,a}|\quad\forall t,a

(4a)

\displaystyle f^{\mathrm{In}}_{t,a}=f^{\mathrm{Out}}_{t,a}=f_{t,a}\quad\forall t,a

(4b)

\displaystyle\pi^{\mathrm{Sqr}}_{t,j}=\Gamma^{2}_{c}\pi^{\mathrm{Sqr}}_{t,i}\quad\forall t,c

(4c)

\displaystyle\underline{\Pi}^{\mathrm{Sqr}}_{i}\leq\pi^{\mathrm{Sqr}}_{t,i}\leq\overline{\Pi}^{\mathrm{Sqr}}_{i}\quad\forall t,i

(4d)

In (
4
), constraint (
4a
) represents the momentum equation governing bidirectional gas flow, with inflow equal to outflow (
4b
). Constraint (
4c
) defines the pressure increase from compressors, while (
4d
) imposes lower and upper bounds on nodal pressures. The steady-state model constitutes a MINLP.

II-E

Transport Model

The steady-state model (
4
) can be approximated as a transport model by omitting the explicit pressure–flow relationship and bounding gas flows by
\overline{F}_{a,d}=\sqrt{\frac{\overline{\Pi}_{a}}{R_{a,d}L_{a}}}
, corresponding to the maximum flow implied by the maximum pressure
\overline{\Pi}_{a}
of pipeline
a
.
The
transport model
is formulated as:

\displaystyle-\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\overline{F}_{a,d}x_{a,d}\leq f^{\mathrm{In}}_{t,a},f^{\mathrm{Out}}_{t,a}\leq\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\overline{F}_{a,d}x_{a,d}\quad\forall t,a

(5a)

\displaystyle f^{\mathrm{In}}_{t,a}=f^{\mathrm{Out}}_{t,a}\quad\forall t,a

(5b)

In (
5
), constraints (
5a
) limit bidirectional inflow and outflow, while (
5b
) enforces their equality. The transport problem constitutes a mixed-integer linear program (MILP).

II-F

Transport Model with Linepack Approximation

The transport model (
5
) can be extended to approximate linepack storage. Let
m_{t,a}
denote the gas stored in pipeline
a
at time
t
. The minimum and maximum linepack are given by
\underline{M}_{a,d}=\frac{\pi L_{a}\underline{\Pi}_{a}D_{a,d}^{2}}{4c^{2}}
and
\overline{M}_{a,d}=\frac{\pi L_{a}\overline{\Pi}_{a}D_{a,d}^{2}}{4c^{2}}
, where
\underline{\Pi}_{a}
and
\overline{\Pi}_{a}
denote the minimum and maximum pipeline pressures (
\text{\,}\mathrm{bar}
). The
transport-linepack model
is formulated as:

\displaystyle-\!\!\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\overline{F}_{a,d}x_{a,d}\leq f^{\mathrm{In}}_{t,a},f^{\mathrm{Out}}_{t,a}\leq\!\!\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\overline{F}_{a,d}x_{a,d}\quad\forall t,a

(6a)

\displaystyle m_{t,a}=m_{t-1,a}+\Delta\big(f^{\mathrm{In}}_{t,a}-f^{\mathrm{Out}}_{t,a}\big)\quad\forall~t\in\mathcal{T}\backslash\{1\},a

(6b)

\displaystyle m_{t,a}=m_{T,a}+\Delta\big(f^{\mathrm{In}}_{t,a}-f^{\mathrm{Out}}_{t,a}\big)\!\quad t\in\{1\},~\forall~a

(6c)

\displaystyle\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\!\underline{M}_{a,d}x_{a,d}\leq m_{t,a}\leq\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\!\overline{M}_{a,d}x_{a,d}\quad\forall t,a

(6d)

In (
6
), constraints (
6a
) limit the bidirectional gas inflow and outflow. Constraint (
6b
) enforces mass conservation across consecutive time steps, thereby approximating pipeline linepack, while (
6c
) cyclically links the final and first time step, and (
6d
) imposes lower and upper bounds on linepack.
The transport-linepack model constitutes a MILP with time-linking constraints.
