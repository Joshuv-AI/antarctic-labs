Diabetic retinopathy treatment cascade and care continuum in the USA: a systematic review

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a leading cause of blindness among working-age adults in the USA.1 Timely screening and treatment interventions, such as intravitreal injections (IVI) and pan-retinal photocoagulation (PRP), can prevent irreversible vision loss.2 However, engagement across the DR care continuum remains suboptimal, with persistent gaps in DR diagnosis, delays in screening and inadequate follow-up.3,5 These gaps in care contribute to vision loss or blindness that could have been prevented with earlier detection and intervention.

Prior reviews of DR care generally focus on isolated stages, such as screening adherence or treatment outcomes, without evaluating the full spectrum of care from initial diabetes diagnosis to visual impairment.6,8 Therefore, the cumulative impact of gaps at multiple stages of DR care remains unclear. Applying a cascade-of-care framework, a model widely used in chronic disease and infectious disease research, offers a systematic approach to conceptualise DR care as a sequential process.9 This framework allows identification of critical drop-offs in engagement and highlights stages where interventions are most needed to prevent vision loss.

This objective of this systematic review is to characterise the DR treatment cascade in the USA, quantify engagement at each stage and identify key gaps contributing to preventable vision loss. By framing DR care within a cascade model, we aim to provide a comprehensive understanding of where patients disengage from care, informing targeted strategies to improve outcomes across the continuum.

Methods

This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 guidelines (online supplemental table 1) and Synthesis without Meta-Analysis in Systematic Reviews 2020 guidelines (online supplemental table 2).10 11 The review protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) in September 2025 (osf.io/ptjmc).

Eligibility criteria

We included English-language studies of individuals with diabetes published between 1 January 2018 and 31 December 2025, including US-based cross-sectional, cohort and case–control studies and systematic reviews containing US-based data. We included systematic reviews only to inform estimates of DR-related blindness in the USA, where primary estimates were limited. To minimise the risk of double counting data already captured in primary studies, we restricted systematic reviews to the DR-related blindness stage of the cascade, where we used two reviews to estimate blindness and did not use them to inform other cascade stages. The 2018 publication cut-off reflects the contemporary era of DR care as anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy had been widely adopted as standard of care and screening guidelines had been updated as reflected by the 2017 American Diabetes Association (ADA) Position Statement.12 Notably, some included studies analyse data collected before the 2018 publication cut-off, with the earliest from 2005.13 While these data precede the 2017 ADA guideline update, we intended to capture studies published in the era following widespread adoption of anti-VEGF therapy without excluding studies reporting older data to ensure breadth. Exclusion criteria included: (1) study not relevant to one of the defined DR cascade stages and (2) editorial, perspective or commentary pieces.

Search strategy

We systematically searched Ovid MEDLINE and Ovid Embase from September 2025 to November 2025 with an updated search in March to April 2026 using the same eligibility criteria. To ensure comprehensiveness, we reviewed reference lists of relevant articles and opportunistically searched the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) publications.

We conceptualised the DR treatment cascade into five sequential stages to align with the clinical pathway of DR: (1) diagnosis of diabetes, (2) adherence to DR screening, (3) diagnosis of DR, (4) adherence to DR care and (5) DR-related blindness. We developed stage-specific search strategies in Ovid using database-specific controlled vocabulary for each database (Medical Subject Headings, MeSH and Emtree) and extensive free-text keywords, including synonyms, drug names, procedures and outcome terms, using Boolean operators (AND, OR) and proximity operators (adj2) to optimise retrieval. The full search strategies for each cascade stage and number of records retrieved per database are provided in online supplemental table 3. For the diagnosis of diabetes stage, we prioritised population-level prevalence estimates from the CDC identified through targeted grey literature searches and large database studies given that the purpose of this stage was to provide a starting point to contextualise the cascade within the USA.

Study selection

Two reviewers (JJF and SSA) independently screened titles and abstracts and applied the predefined exclusion criteria. We resolved discrepancies through discussion and consensus. We retrieved full texts of potentially eligible studies for independent screening by two reviewers (JJF and SSA). We excluded articles meeting the predefined exclusion criteria and resolved discrepancies through discussion to reach consensus.

Risk-of-bias assessment

Two researchers (JJF and SSA) assessed the risk-of-bias of included observational studies using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort studies and the NOS-xs, for cross-sectional studies.14 15 The NOS is a tool to assess risk-of-bias in observational studies and uses a ‘star system’ with three domains to judge study quality: (1) selection of the study group, (2) comparability of the groups and (3) ascertainment of the exposure or outcome of interest. We assessed the risk-of-bias of included systematic reviews using the tool to assess Risk of Bias in Systematic Reviews, which contains four domains through which bias can be introduced: (1) eligibility criteria, (2) study selection, (3) data collection and study appraisal and (4) synthesis and findings.16 Risk-of-bias assessments are provided in online supplemental table 4.

Data extraction and synthesis

One reviewer (JJF) extracted data with confirmation by a second reviewer (SSA). Extracted variables included study design, sample size, study population, study duration, outcomes measured and cascade stages addressed. For each stage, we captured relevant data including (1) diagnosis of diabetes: diabetes prevalence and incidence in the USA, awareness of diagnosis; (2) adherence to DR screening: rates of screening adherence; (3) diagnosis of DR: DR prevalence and incidence in the US, awareness of diagnosis; (4) adherence to DR care: rates of initial linkage to care following DR diagnosis, rates of follow-up and (5) DR-related blindness: prevalence of DR-related blindness or visual-impairment.

Due to heterogeneity in study designs, definitions of cascade stages and definitions of outcomes, we did not perform a meta-analysis. We synthesised data narratively and grouped studies by study population, settings and outcome definitions according to the conceptual DR treatment cascade framework to identify stages with the greatest loss of engagement and highlight potential targets for intervention. We did not make changes to the grouping of studies after protocol registration. We included all eligible studies contributing to each cascade stage and did not prioritise studies by design, risk of bias assessments or relevance. We identified when studies used shared national datasets (eg, National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES)) to contextualise potential overlap in study populations. We reported quantitative outcomes as minimum-maximum ranges of proportions or prevalence estimates as reported in individual studies. We did not perform statistical transformation of effect estimates and instead summarised values with ranges, medians and IQRs to accommodate heterogeneity in grouped studies. We did not formally assess certainty of evidence. Instead, findings should be interpreted in the context of study heterogeneity, study design and risk-of-bias assessments.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Summary of included studies

Our search identified 18 320 records through database searching and 2 additional records from the CDC. After removing duplicates, 14 893 records were screened, of which 14 820 were excluded. Full-text assessment of 73 articles resulted in 46 studies meeting our inclusion criteria (figure 1). Excluded studies are summarised in online supplemental table 5. We identified 27 cohort, 15 cross-sectional, 2 systematic reviews and meta-analyses and 2 records directly from the CDC. Diagnosis of diabetes

Prevalence and incidence of diabetes

In 2021, the US CDC estimated that 38.1 million US adults, or 14.7% of all US adults, had a diagnosis of diabetes.17 Between 2017 and 2023, estimates from the NHANES and Behavioural Risk Factor Surveillance System found that 11.3%–12.1% of all US adults had a diagnosis of diabetes.18 19 Prevalence is higher in rural areas with an estimated 12.9%–14.3% of US adults with diagnosed diabetes in these areas.20 21 Diabetes prevalence is higher among men, individuals with lower educational attainment and individuals identifying as Black or African American.22

The US CDC estimates that the incidence of diabetes has stayed constant for the past decade with around 1.5 million new cases per year.23 Incidence increases with older age.17

Awareness of diabetes diagnosis

The US CDC estimates that approximately 22.8% of US adults with diabetes are not aware of their diabetes diagnosis.17 Estimates from the NHANES show that 22.9%–28.4% of US adults with diabetes are not aware of their diagnosis, corresponding to an estimated 3.5%–4.5% of all US adults.19 22 24 These individuals may have delayed access to care, elevating their risk for preventable complications of diabetes.24 In summary, approximately 22.8%–28.4% (median 25.5%, IQR 22.9%–28.3%, n=4) of US adults with diabetes are not aware of their diagnosis.

Adherence to DR screening

Screening recommendations

The American Academy of Ophthalmology and the ADA recommend at least annual DR screening starting at the time of diagnosis for type 2 diabetes and 5 years after diagnosis for type 1 diabetes.25 26 Recommended screening intervals change depending on exam findings. For example, the ADA recommends annual to biennial screening if there is both adequate glycaemic control and no evidence of retinopathy on one or more annual exams.26

Screening adherence rates

Despite the recommendations and purported benefits of DR screening, adherence rates remain suboptimal. Longitudinal, population-based studies evaluating adherence report annual screening rates of 2.7%–27.7% and biennial screening rates of 15.3%–71.1%.4 27 Studies evaluating the proportion of individuals with diabetes receiving an eye exam in a given year report rates of 35.2%–69.1% (median 58.75%, IQR 40.4%–64.9%, n=6).1328,32 Similarly, studies evaluating the proportion of individuals with diabetes receiving an eye exam over a 2-year period report rates of 33.9%–78.7% (median 60.9%, IQR 40.8%–76.4%, n=4).1333,36 In summary, approximately 2.7%–69.1% (median 47.3%, IQR 31.4%–64.2%, n=8) of individuals with diabetes adhere to annual DR screening and approximately 15.5%–78.7% (median 59.4%, IQR 33.9%–74.0%, n=6) adhere biennially.

Diagnosis of DR

Prevalence and incidence of DR

Among US adults with diabetes, the prevalence of DR in 2021 is estimated as 20.6%–26.4%.1 37 Among all US adults, estimated prevalence of DR in 2022 was 0.07% due to type 1 diabetes and 5.06% due to type 2 diabetes.38 Yearly DR prevalence increased from 2007 (10.9%) to 2021 (20.8%).37 Estimated prevalence of vision-threatening DR was 3.38% in 2018 and 5.06%–6.9% in 2021, of diabetic macular oedema (DME) was 2.6% in 2018 and 4.9% in 2021, and of proliferative DR (PDR) was 3.5% in 2021.1 37 38 DR prevalence varies by geographic location with higher prevalence in the Southern USA, similar to patterns seen in diabetes.1 Of the individuals diagnosed with DR, many already have advanced DR on diagnosis. An estimated 10.5% of eyes in individuals with diabetes present with PDR at baseline, and an estimated 1.74% of individuals have PDR within 5 years of diabetes diagnosis.39 40

Since 2018, the incidence of DR has remained relatively stable at approximately 200 000 new cases per year, following a steady increase from about 1 00 000 cases in 2013.37

Awareness of DR diagnosis

Like with diabetes diagnosis, a substantial proportion of individuals with DR are unaware of their diagnosis. On the NHANES, 70%–70.1% of individuals with self-reported diabetes and photographic evidence of DR were not aware of their diagnosis.3 13 Similarly, a 10-year prospective study found that 54.9% of individuals with photographic evidence were unaware of their diagnosis of VTDR, and 88.5% of individuals with photographic evidence were unaware of their diagnosis of mild DR.41 In summary, approximately 54.9%–88.5% (median 70.1%, IQR 62.5%–79.3%, n=4) of individuals with photographic evidence of DR are unaware of their diagnosis.

Adherence to DR care

Linkage to care

Following a DR diagnosis, delays can occur in linkage to care and initiation of treatment. DR may be detected in the primary care or emergency department setting, requiring referral to ophthalmology for follow-up care and potential treatment. In the emergency department, Hinkle et al41 estimated follow-up rates of 61.9% for PDR referral, and Watane et al estimated follow-up rates of 53.0% for non-PDR (NPDR) referral.42 43 In the primary care setting, follow-up rates after DR referral range from 30.9% to 62.7% (median 52.0%, IQR 40.9%–59.1%, n=5).544,47

DR follow-up

Recommended follow-up intervals for DR depend on disease severity, ranging from annual visits for mild NPDR with no DME to monthly for DME.25 Treatment options, including anti-VEGF IVI, PRP and vitreous surgery, are integral to DR care and can significantly improve vision loss in severe disease.2

Reported rates of patients with a lapse in DR follow-up care range from 55.3% to 77.6% when defined based on provider recommendation, and 77.8% when defined as any interval greater than 6 months between visits (median 70.3%, IQR 59.2%–77.7%, n=4).48,51 Among patients receiving treatment for PDR, reported rates of loss to follow-up range from 11.7% to 61.0% (median 39.9%, IQR 18.6%–57.7%, n=4) for overall treatment,52,55 12.3%–29.4% for anti-VEGF IVI alone, 12.6%–28.0% for PRP alone52 54 and 31.3% following pars plana vitrectomy.56

DR-related blindness

Blindness and visual impairment are serious and debilitating consequences of DR. Several studies have estimated the prevalence of DR-related blindness in the USA. Chen et al estimated a prevalence of 10.61 per 100 000 for DR-related blindness in the US.57 A 2020 meta-analysis reported a prevalence of 30 per 100 000 in the high-income Global Burden of Disease (GBD) region, which includes the USA.58 DR-related blindness has increased over time; in 2010, prevalence in the USA was 5.48 per 100 000.57 Prevalence of DR-related moderate or severe visual impairment is similar, with rates of 80 per 100 000 in 2020 in the high-income GBD region.58

In summary, we estimate that in the USA, approximately 0.4% (~34 800 individuals) of individuals with DR have DR-related blindness and approximately 2.64% (~420 000 individuals) have moderate to severe visual impairment.57 59 We derived this estimate from prevalence values of DR-related blindness in the USA reported by Chen et al56, combined with DR prevalence estimates from Teo et al.57,59 We excluded estimates from Vision Loss Experts Group et al to avoid double-counting and because they apply to the broader high-income GBD region.58 Progression to visual impairment is variable and influenced by genetic, systemic and socioeconomic factors.40 DR-related visual impairment and blindness impose significant functional and social costs to individuals.60 The societal burden is significant as well, with the estimated annual cost of DR treatment at $800 million and the estimated burden of visual impairment and blindness as a whole in the USA at US$134.2 billion.61

Discussion

This systematic review reveals persistent gaps in engagement across the DR care continuum in the USA, with drop-offs occurring at every stage of the treatment cascade from diabetes diagnosis to follow-up care. By applying a cascade-of-care framework, we identify the stages where patients are most likely to disengage from care. Our analysis reveals the most pronounced declines in engagement occur among individuals unaware of their DR diagnosis and in those who are not successfully linked to or retained in DR care, suggesting these stages may disproportionately contribute to preventable visual impairment and blindness.

Approximately 54.9%–88.5% of individuals with DR are not aware of their diagnosis.3 13 41 Across these studies, it is not possible to determine whether unawareness reflects individuals who were never examined by an eye care professional or those who were examined but were either not informed of their diagnosis or did not understand that they had DR. Regardless, this discrepancy reflects substantial gaps in DR detection, care delivery and communication with patients.

Lack of awareness of a DR diagnosis is essentially equivalent to undiagnosed DR, and can lead to delayed care, thus perpetuating the risk for irreversible blindness which may have been preventable with timely treatment.3 Our group reported an association between lack of awareness of DR and both longer diabetes duration and elevated haemoglobin A1c.3 Individuals with poorly controlled, long-standing diabetes may face systemic barriers, burnout or comorbid conditions that impair engagement in both diabetes management and DR screening.62 Notably, longer diabetes duration and poor glycaemic control are independent risk factors for developing DR, suggesting that those most at risk for DR may also be most at risk for lack of awareness of their diagnosis.62 Silva et al40 reported associations between lack of awareness and not having an eye exam in the last year, further supporting that lack of engagement in screening compounds lack of DR awareness.41

Linkage to DR care following DR diagnosis is also suboptimal with only an estimated 30.9%–55.4% of individuals diagnosed with DR in the primary care setting successfully connecting with care. Adherence to DR follow-up care after initial linkage is also suboptimal, with 55.3%–77.8% of eligible individuals having a lapse in care. Numerous studies have identified barriers to DR follow-up and treatment, including younger age, systemic racism, language barriers, transportation difficulties and lack of insurance.42,4448 49 52 53 55 Additionally, some studies report an association between severe DR and increased follow-up adherence, highlighting how patients may delay engaging in care until symptoms become more noticeable.43 In contrast, other studies report that the presence of DME and greater diabetes severity predict lower adherence, suggesting that the overall burden of diabetes may outweigh patients’ ability to prioritise eye care.5 52 53 63 Prior studies describe a range of strategies intended to address these barriers, including patient navigation, telemedicine-based services, AI-assisted diagnosis tools, rural health clinics and community co-designed digital health tools.64,67

The same barriers that hinder DR follow-up adherence also hinder DR screening adherence. However, the drop in engagement is larger for DR follow-up adherence compared with DR screening adherence. This difference may reflect the greater burden of DR management, which often requires more frequent visits compared with annual or biennial screening exams. The increased time commitment, transportation challenges and coordination of care can make follow-up more difficult for patients. Furthermore, individuals requiring ongoing DR care often experience more advanced diabetes and related complications, such as kidney disease, which may further reduce their capacity to prioritise eye care.68 Although follow-up adherence faces unique challenges compared with screening, improving engagement at both stages is critical to preventing vision loss and reducing the overall burden of DR.

DR-related blindness can occur at any stage of the treatment cascade, from lack of awareness of diabetes or DR diagnosis to inadequate screening or follow-up care. For example, two studies found that patients lost to follow-up after DR treatment experienced significant declines in visual acuity, sometimes worse than 20/200, the threshold for legal blindness.66 67 Additionally, Wykoff et al39 reported that advanced stage DR at diagnosis increased the risk of sustained blindness, highlighting how delayed diagnosis, often resulting from inadequate screening adherence, can contribute to DR-related vision loss.40 Similarly, studies show that lack of awareness of diabetes or DR can also lead to blindness, as the absence of perceived need often delays the initiation of screening and treatment.3 68 When considering the entire cascade, the cumulative losses in patient engagement are significant, emphasising the need for interventions that target multiple stages of the cascade rather than just one.

DR-related blindness can occur at each stage of the care cascade through various mechanisms. We hypothesise that lack of awareness of DR diagnosis contributes disproportionately to blindness by delaying entry into care and therefore increasing the likelihood of advanced disease at initial presentation. In contrast, loss to follow-up after diagnosis may accelerate disease progression among individuals already identified as high risk, particularly those requiring ongoing treatment, thereby increasing the risk of vision loss despite prior detection. While the available literature does not permit direct comparison of the relative contribution of these stages to DR-related blindness, this cascade-of-care framework generates testable hypotheses regarding how delayed diagnosis versus loss to follow-up in care differentially influence visual outcomes. Specifically, future studies must determine whether individuals with DR who receive routine treatment have sustained preservation of vision, and whether individuals unaware of their DR diagnosis are more likely to present with advanced disease and progress to blindness. Addressing these questions will require large, longitudinal, population-based studies linking eye exam utilisation, disease severity at diagnosis and treatment adherence with long-term visual outcomes. Accordingly, the cascade-of-care model presented here is intended to be hypothesis-generating and provide a foundation for identifying interventions most likely to reduce preventable DR-related blindness rather than to assign causality of DR-related blindness to any single stage.

Study limitations

One limitation of this review is that because some included studies analyse data collected prior to 2018, estimates may not fully reflect current care patterns and may not accurately reflect engagement. Readers are therefore cautioned that not all reported estimates necessarily represent patterns of DR care in the most recent 5–7 years. Additionally, this review is limited in that it is based on estimates of prevalence values aggregated from studies that have heterogenous population characteristics and methodologies.68 Studies may differ in their definitions of screening or follow-up adherence or may be examining a specific population of interest. This was partially mitigated by aggregating results only from studies reporting similar outcomes within each cascade stage. For example, rates of linkage to initial DR care are only reported in the primary care setting. Given the heterogeneity of included studies, medians and IQRs represent only a visual and conceptual synthesis of engagement across cascade stages rather than definitive central estimates. This review is also limited in that there is a degree of overlap between the treatment cascade stages. For example, DR screening may be interchanged with DR follow-up under the umbrella of diabetic eye exams. Furthermore, there is partial overlap in data sources and study periods across some included studies. For example, several studies draw from national datasets such as NHANES (eg, Fang et al24, Gwira et al and Harding et al), which may include overlapping populations. To minimise the impact of duplication on our synthesis, we did not pool estimates and instead report ranges, medians and IQRs across studies. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as a conceptual synthesis of the literature rather than independent population estimates and overlapping data sources may reduce the variability observed in some outcomes. There is additionally potential for bias in certain stages of the cascade secondary to limited literature on the subject. For example, while stages such as DR follow-up adherence have more than 15 articles relevant to the subject, other stages such as awareness of DR diagnosis have only a few articles. Finally, findings should be interpreted in the context of the inherent methodological limitations of the included studies, as reflected in the risk of bias assessments. For example, some studies relied on self-reported diagnoses rather than examination-based measures or included populations not representative of the broader US population, such as Medicare recipients. Although heterogeneity in study design and definitions limited meta-analysis, this review synthesises the most recent US data and reframes it within a structured, reproducible cascade model. This approach provides clinicians, policymakers and health systems with a clear map of where to intervene, thereby advancing beyond previous narrative or stage-specific reviews.

Conclusions

This review highlights critical gaps in the DR care continuum, with awareness of DR diagnosis and linkage and adherence to DR follow-up emerging as the lowest points of engagement. Decreased engagement at these stages may disproportionately contribute to DR-related blindness. The cascade model provides a structured approach to organising existing evidence and identifying areas of priority for future research and focus.
