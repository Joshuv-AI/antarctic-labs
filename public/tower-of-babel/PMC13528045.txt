Response surface optimization of imazethapyr dose and application timing for weed suppression and faba bean yield
Authors: Amin Taheri-Garavand, Abdolreza Ahmadi, Omidali Akbarpour, Dimitrios Fanourakis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEffective weed management in faba bean (Vicia faba L.) requires precise adjustment of herbicide dose and application timing to achieve effective weed suppression while maintaining crop growth and yield. This study employed response surface methodology (RSM) to quantify and optimize the interactive effects of imazethapyr rate and application timing on weed biomass, morphophysiological traits, and yield of faba bean under field conditions in western Iran during a single 2024–2025 growing season.Imazethapyr (Pursuit® 10% SL) was applied at rates ranging from 0 to 1000 mL ha⁻¹ at pre-plant incorporated, pre-emergence, and post-emergence stages using a central composite design. Leaf area index, plant height, number of pods per plant, 100-seed weight, biological yield, grain yield, and weed dry weight were modeled using quadratic and cubic RSM functions. Strong nonlinear dose by timing interactions were observed for all responses. Intermediate imazethapyr rates (250–500 mL ha⁻¹) applied from pre-planting to early post-emergence (3–10 days after sowing) maximized canopy development, reproductive performance, biological yield, and grain yield while minimizing weed biomass. Higher rates (≥ 750 mL ha⁻¹) or late post-emergence applications reduced crop performance despite improved weed suppression, indicating phytotoxic effects. Model diagnostics showed high predictive accuracy, particularly for leaf area index, 100-seed weight, and weed dry weight. Multi-response desirability analysis identified a favorable management range balancing weed control and yield performance, indicating the value of RSM as a decision-support framework for precision herbicide management in faba bean.Keywords: Acetolactate synthase, Crop safety, Dose–response modeling, Phenology, Weed biomass

Introduction

Faba bean (Vicia faba L.) is one of the most important grain legumes worldwide, valued for its high protein content, its role in human and animal nutrition, and its contribution to sustainable cropping systems through biological nitrogen (N) fixation [1–4]. In Mediterranean and semi-arid regions, faba bean is increasingly promoted as a component of diversified rotations that improve soil fertility and reduce dependence on synthetic N fertilizers [5]. However, its productivity is frequently constrained by severe weed competition, particularly during early growth stages when faba bean exhibits slow canopy development and limited competitive ability [6, 7].

Weed interference during this critical period can cause substantial yield losses by reducing light interception, nutrient uptake, and water availability [8–10]. Effective weed control early in the growing season is therefore essential to ensure yield stability [10]. Chemical weed control remains a key management strategy in many faba bean production systems, particularly where labor availability for hand weeding is limited [11]. Among available herbicides, imazethapyr, an acetolactate synthase (ALS) inhibiting herbicide, is widely used because of its broad-spectrum activity against annual broadleaf and grass weeds and its systemic mode of action [12, 13]. However, the effectiveness and selectivity of herbicide applications are strongly influenced by application rate and timing [14–16]. Sub-optimal doses may lead to inadequate weed suppression, whereas excessive or poorly timed applications can induce phytotoxic effects, reduce crop growth, and ultimately decrease yield [17–19]. Beyond yield losses, improper herbicide use may also increase production costs and contribute to unnecessary chemical inputs, with potential environmental consequences [20, 21].

Traditional approaches to herbicide evaluation and optimization often rely on simplified experimental frameworks that may not adequately capture the complexity of crop–herbicide interactions, potentially resulting in less efficient management recommendations [22]. Such approaches can lead to conservative over-application or repeated treatments, increasing both economic costs and environmental load [23].

Response surface methodology (RSM) provides a flexible framework by enabling simultaneous evaluation of multiple factors and their interactions while reducing experimental complexity [23, 24]. By identifying favorable response regions rather than single-factor thresholds, RSM allows more efficient herbicide use and supports precision input management [25, 26]. RSM has been successfully applied in herbicide dose–response studies to identify management windows that balance weed suppression with crop performance and input efficiency [27, 28].

Despite this potential, limited information is available on the combined effects of imazethapyr dose and application timing on morphophysiological traits, yield components, and weed biomass in faba bean using a quantitative optimization framework. In particular, few studies have integrated crop response, weed suppression, and multi-response optimization to evaluate trade-offs between yield, crop safety, and herbicide input intensity. Therefore, the objective of this study was to apply RSM to model and evaluate the interactive effects of imazethapyr rate and application timing on weed control efficiency, crop growth, and yield of faba bean. By identifying a favorable dose–time domain, this work aims to contribute to more economically efficient and environmentally responsible herbicide management strategies, supporting sustainable faba bean production systems under semi-arid conditions.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and plant material

This study was conducted during a single 2024–2025 growing season using RSM within a central composite design (CCD) to evaluate the effects of imazethapyr (Pursuit® 10% SL, BASF Corp., Tehran, Iran) concentration and application timing on weed control in the ‘Barakat’ faba bean cultivar (developed by the Institute of Technology, Karaj, Iran).

The experiment was carried out at an experimental field in Khorramabad, western Iran (33°26 15 N, 48°15 38 E; 1100 m above sea level), representing a semi-arid faba bean production system. The climate of the region is classified as arid to semi-arid, with a long-term mean annual air temperature of 17.2 °C and an average annual precipitation of 504 mm. Under these conditions, early-season weed competition and herbicide performance are major determinants of crop productivity.

Primary soil physicochemical properties of the experimental field (0–30 cm depth) are presented in Table 1. Soil samples were collected before sowing from multiple points across the experimental area and composited by depth. Organic carbon was determined using the Walkley–Black method, available N by the Kjeldahl method, available phosphorus using the Olsen extraction, and available potassium by ammonium acetate extraction. Soil pH was measured in a 1:2.5 soil–water suspension, and electrical conductivity (EC) was determined in the saturated paste extract. As shown in Table 1, the soil was characterized by moderate fertility, neutral pH, and low salinity, conditions typical of faba bean production fields in the region and suitable for evaluating herbicide responses without confounding soil stress effects.

The faba bean cultivar ‘Barakat’ was selected due to its wide cultivation and agronomic relevance in western Iran. This cultivar is well adapted to local soil and climatic conditions and is commonly used in commercial production, making it suitable for evaluating practical herbicide management strategies. Moreover, its moderate sensitivity to ALS-inhibiting herbicides allows clear discrimination between effective and phytotoxic dose–time combinations.

The experiment was established in a field previously cropped with wheat, where a natural population of broadleaf weed species was present, ensuring realistic weed pressure. Land preparation included moldboard plowing to a depth of 20–25 cm followed by disking prior to sowing. Faba bean was planted on 1 November 2024, corresponding to the standard sowing window for the region, at a density of 15 seeds m⁻², equivalent to 150,000 seeds ha⁻¹, targeting a final stand close to this value under the observed emergence conditions (90–95% emergence). This planting density falls within the typical commercial range for faba bean (120,000–200,000 plants ha⁻¹) under semi-arid conditions and therefore reflects realistic farmer practice rather than an artificially dense or sparse experimental setup. Each experimental plot consisted of five rows, 7 m in length, with 40 cm spacing between rows. Irrigation was managed using a controlled Hydro Fix system to prevent lateral water movement between plots. Each plot was equipped with four independently controlled irrigation lines. Crop water requirements were calculated at 10-day intervals using the FAO Penman–Monteith equation implemented in NETWAT software, based on long-term regional meteorological data, ensuring uniform and precise water supply throughout the experimental period.

Experimental design and treatments

The experiment was conducted using RSM within a central composite design (CCD) to evaluate the combined effects of imazethapyr dose and application timing on weed control and faba bean performance. Imazethapyr (Pursuit®, 10% SL) was selected because it is one of the most widely used ALS-inhibiting herbicides in grain legumes, including faba bean, due to its broad-spectrum efficacy against annual broadleaf and grass weeds and its systemic activity. Its known sensitivity to application rate and timing makes it particularly suitable for quantitative optimization studies aimed at identifying thresholds between effective weed control and crop phytotoxicity.

Imazethapyr was applied at rates of 250, 500, 750, and 1000 mL ha⁻¹, covering a range from sub-recommended to supra-recommended doses. This range was chosen to encompass levels expected to result in insufficient weed suppression, favorable weed control with acceptable crop safety, and potential phytotoxic effects, thereby enabling accurate characterization of nonlinear dose–response relationships. The inclusion of both moderate and high application rates allowed assessment of crop tolerance limits and identification of favorable dose domains within the RSM framework.

Herbicide applications were conducted at three distinct timings, pre-plant incorporated (PPI), pre-emergence (PRE), and post-emergence (POST), representing the main application windows used in commercial faba bean production systems. Within the response surface methodology framework, application timing was modeled as a continuous quantitative variable expressed as days relative to sowing, while PPI, PRE, and POST were used as agronomic descriptors of the corresponding application windows. This approach enabled evaluation of nonlinear dose–time interactions across the entire temporal application range rather than restricting the analysis to discrete categorical comparisons. These timings were selected to capture differences in herbicide availability in the soil, weed emergence dynamics, and crop developmental sensitivity.

PPI applications target early-emerging weeds through soil incorporation, PRE applications aim to control weeds at germination, and POST applications coincide with active weed growth but increased crop sensitivity. In addition, a weed-free treatment, maintained throughout the growing season by hand weeding, was included as a reference control to represent maximum yield potential in the absence of weed interference. The weed-free treatment was included as a biological reference only and was not incorporated into the response surface model fitting, optimization, or desirability analysis.

The PPI treatments were applied one day before sowing, the PRE treatments were applied two days after sowing, and the POST treatments were applied at the 2–4 leaf stage of faba bean. In the weed-free control, weeds were removed weekly by hand to eliminate weed competition during the entire cropping period. The untreated control (0 mL ha⁻¹ imazethapyr) was distinct from the weed-free treatment, retained natural weed infestation throughout the growing season, and was included in analyses as part of the continuous dose–time factor space.

Herbicide applications were carried out using a Matabi electric knapsack sprayer (Goizper S. Cooperative Co., Guipúzcoa, Spain) equipped with 8002 VS flat-fan nozzles. This sprayer type was selected because it is commonly used in small- to medium-scale field research and allows precise control of application pressure and spray volume, ensuring consistent delivery across experimental plots. The sprayer was calibrated to deliver a spray volume of 300 L ha⁻¹ at a constant operating pressure of 250 kPa, a configuration widely recommended for soil-applied and early post-emergence herbicides to ensure uniform coverage of the soil surface and crop canopy while minimizing spray drift. The chosen spray volume and pressure reflect standard agronomic practice for imazethapyr application under field conditions and enhance the reproducibility and practical relevance of the treatments.

This experimental design enabled robust quantification of nonlinear dose by timing interactions and provided an appropriate framework for evaluating favorable imazethapyr application strategies that balance weed suppression with crop safety.

Experimental unit, replication, and subsampling

The experimental unit was the plot. The central composite design (CCD) comprised 13 treatment combinations (design points) derived from two factors, imazethapyr dose (D) and application timing (T), including factorial, axial, and center points. Each design point was implemented in three independent replicate plots, resulting in a total of 39 experimental plots. Plots were arranged in a randomized complete block design with three blocks, and treatment allocation within each block was randomized to minimize the effects of spatial field variability. Center points (corresponding to an intermediate imazethapyr dose of 500 mL ha⁻¹ applied at 5 days after sowing) were replicated five times to provide an estimate of pure error and to allow formal lack-of-fit testing within the RSM framework. Within each plot, crop morphophysiological and yield traits were assessed on 10 plants (subsamples) and averaged to a single plot mean prior to analysis.

Weed density and biomass were assessed using two 0.25 m² quadrats (subsamples) per plot and similarly averaged to obtain plot-level values, which were used as the observational units in all statistical analyses.

Measurement of morphophysiological traits and yield components of faba bean

Growth and morphophysiological traits of faba bean were recorded on ten randomly selected plants per plot and averaged prior to statistical analysis. The selected parameters included plant height (PH), leaf area index (LAI), number of pods per plant (NPPP), 100-seed weight (W100S), biological yield (BY), and grain yield (GY). These variables were chosen because they collectively capture canopy development, reproductive performance, total biomass accumulation, and final yield formation, thereby allowing a comprehensive evaluation of crop responses to herbicide dose and application timing. Together, they link early vegetative growth with reproductive success and yield outcomes, which is essential for assessing both crop safety and agronomic performance under herbicide treatments.

PH was measured at the flowering stage, when stem elongation was completed and height differences among treatments were fully expressed. Measurements were taken from the soil surface to the apical meristem of each selected plant using a measuring tape (± 0.1 cm), and the mean value was considered representative of each plot. LAI was determined at the fully developed canopy stage using a SunScan canopy analysis system (Delta-T Devices, Cambridge, UK), a widely accepted non-destructive method for estimating canopy light interception. Measurements were conducted by positioning the sensor at a 45° angle relative to the crop rows, and three readings per plot were taken and averaged. All LAI measurements were performed between 10:00 and 14:00 h under stable light conditions to minimize diurnal variability and ensure measurement consistency.

Yield components were assessed at physiological maturity. The NPPP was counted on the same sampled plants used for growth measurements. GY was defined as the dry weight of harvested seeds per unit area and was determined by harvesting plants from a 2 m² area in the center of each plot to avoid border effects. BY was defined as the total aboveground dry biomass, including grain and vegetative tissues, harvested from the same area and expressed on a dry matter basis.

For determination of W100S, representative grain samples were oven-dried at 70 °C to constant weight (48 h) to eliminate moisture variability. Seeds were then weighed, and W100S was calculated and expressed on a dry matter basis. The selected drying temperature and duration ensured complete moisture removal without thermal degradation of seed tissues, providing accurate and reproducible weight measurements. All measured traits were determined from ten randomly selected plants per plot, sampled within a 2 m² central area of each plot [17, 29].

Weed density and biomass

Weed density and biomass were assessed three weeks after post-emergence herbicide application (December 2024). In each plot, weeds were sampled using two permanently established quadrats (0.25 m² each). All weeds within each quadrat were clipped at ground level, identified to species, and counted to determine weed density. The harvested weed material was then oven-dried at 75 °C to constant weight (72 h), and dry biomass was expressed on an area basis (g m⁻²). Weed species were identified during sampling to confirm typical site flora. However, weed biomass was analyzed as a pooled response variable because the study focused on integrated weed suppression rather than species-specific herbicide selectivity, and individual species contributions were not modeled separately.

Model selection and overfitting control

Linear, quadratic, and cubic polynomial models were evaluated for each response using sequential model sum of squares and ANOVA diagnostics in Design-Expert®, including model significance (P), lack-of-fit testing, and goodness-of-fit criteria. To avoid over-parameterization, higher-order terms were retained only when they significantly improved model adequacy and reduced lack-of-fit, and model hierarchy was respected (i.e., lower-order terms were retained when higher-order terms were included). The final model form for each response was selected based on the combined evidence of (i) significant model terms, (ii) non-significant lack-of-fit, and (iii) improved predictive diagnostics (e.g., adjusted and predicted R² and PRESS, where applicable), rather than R² alone. Models were used strictly for interpolation within the experimental dose–time domain and were not intended for extrapolation beyond the studied ranges. For clarity, Table 3 reports performance statistics only for the final selected model form for each response variable. Intermediate linear and quadratic model results used during model screening are not shown.

Statistical analysis parameters by RSM

For all analyses, plot-level means were used as the observational units (n = number of plots), and subsamples were not treated as independent replicates. The number of observations differed slightly among response variables (e.g., n = 36–38) due to occasional missing or excluded plot-level measurements for specific traits. Occasional missing or excluded observations resulted from limited cases of inadequate plant establishment, incomplete plot-level measurements, or failure to satisfy data quality criteria for specific response variables. These cases represented only a small proportion of the dataset and did not affect the overall response surface trends. However, the experimental design and replication structure remained unchanged. A comprehensive set of statistical performance indices was used to evaluate the accuracy, robustness, and predictive performance of the response surface models. Specifically, the Pearson correlation coefficient (R), R², root mean square error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE) were calculated. These indices jointly quantify the strength of agreement between predicted and observed values and the magnitude of prediction errors. The use of multiple complementary performance metrics to evaluate empirical models and to avoid overinterpretation of single statistics follows established practice in recent agronomic and horticultural modeling studies, including data-driven and predictive frameworks [33]. Higher values of R and R² indicate stronger agreement between observed and predicted responses, whereas lower RMSE and MAE values reflect improved predictive accuracy and reduced model error. Collectively, these metrics provide a comprehensive evaluation of model performance for each response variable (Table 3).

In addition, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on standardized data using the prcomp function in R to explore multivariate relationships among treatments and response variables. The proportion of variance explained by each principal component was calculated, and biplots were generated using the factoextra package (fviz_pca_biplot) to visualize treatment clustering. Variables were displayed as vectors and observations as points with confidence ellipses, with graphical elements customized to enhance interpretability. PCA was performed on plot-level data from herbicide-treated plots only. The weed-free control was not included in the PCA analysis to maintain consistency with the continuous-factor RSM framework.

Results

Leaf area index

The response of LAI to imazethapyr dose and application timing is presented in Fig. 1. LAI responded nonlinearly to imazethapyr dose and application timing, with maximum values observed under low to intermediate doses applied during early growth stages. A clear interaction between herbicide dose and application timing was observed across the experimental range.

Three-dimensional response surface (a) and corresponding contour plot (b) illustrating the interactive effects of imazethapyr dose (mL ha⁻¹) and application timing (days relative to sowing) on leaf area index (LAI) of faba bean. The surfaces depict the nonlinear dose × timing relationship generated using response surface methodology (RSM), highlighting regions of maximum and minimum LAI within the experimental domain

High LAI values (2.89) were observed at low to moderate imazethapyr doses (0–500 mL ha⁻¹) applied at early to intermediate timings, particularly under pre-emergence applications near sowing. In contrast, LAI declined sharply at higher doses (750–1000 mL ha⁻¹) and late post-emergence applications (> 10 days after sowing), reaching a minimum value of 1.74. Both increasing herbicide dose and delayed application markedly suppressed canopy development, resulting in lower LAI values. Overall, pre-emergence application at appropriate doses resulted in the highest LAI, whereas post-emergence applications consistently reduced LAI, even at relatively low rates.

Model diagnostics demonstrated excellent agreement between observed and predicted LAI values (R² = 0.968), with minimal residual bias and low residual dispersion (Fig. 2). Residual diagnostics indicated satisfactory model performance across the experimental domain. These findings indicate that LAI was strongly influenced by both imazethapyr dose and application timing.

Plant height

The response of faba bean PH to imazethapyr dose and application timing is presented in Fig. 3. PH decreased progressively with increasing herbicide dose and delayed application timing.

Three-dimensional response surface (a) and corresponding contour plot (b) illustrating the interactive effects of imazethapyr dose (mL ha⁻¹) and application timing (days relative to sowing) on plant height (PH) of faba bean. The plots depict the nonlinear response of plant height across the dose–time domain as generated by response surface methodology (RSM)

Low doses (0–250 mL ha⁻¹) applied at early timings (PPI and PRE; −3 to 6 days) resulted in minimal growth reduction and preserved maximum PH, ranging from 80 to 82 cm. In contrast, high doses (750–1000 mL ha⁻¹), particularly when applied at late post-emergence stages (> 10–15 days after sowing), caused pronounced reductions in PH, with minimum values of 65–69 cm. Early applications combined with low herbicide doses maintained greater PH, whereas increasing dose and delayed application progressively reduced plant growth. No clear favorable dose–time combination was identified for maximizing PH.

Model diagnostics showed strong agreement between observed and predicted PH values (R = 0.944), with moderate prediction error (RMSE = 1.456 cm). Residual diagnostics indicated satisfactory model performance across the experimental range (Fig. 4).

NPPP was strongly influenced by imazethapyr dose and application timing (Fig. 5).

100-seed weight

The effects of imazethapyr dose and application timing on W100S are presented in Fig. 7. W100S was strongly influenced by both herbicide dose and application timing.

Three-dimensional response surface (a) and corresponding contour plot (b) illustrating the interactive effects of imazethapyr dose (mL ha⁻¹) and application timing (days relative to sowing) on 100-seed weight (W100S) of faba bean, as modeled using response surface methodology (RSM)

Lower W100S values (67–86 g) were observed when imazethapyr was applied at low to moderate doses (0–400 mL ha⁻¹) during the intermediate application window (1–5 days after sowing). Increasing the herbicide dose above 500 mL ha⁻¹ during this period did not improve W100S. In contrast, higher W100S values were recorded for pre-plant applications (3 days before sowing) and for later post-emergence applications (> 10–11 days after sowing), where contour values ranged from 104 to 141 g. Discussion

Dose–time interactions as primary determinants of crop–weed balance

The present study demonstrates that imazethapyr efficacy and crop safety in faba bean are governed by strong nonlinear interactions between application dose and timing rather than by either factor alone, consistent with previous reports in grain legumes [8, 34]. Response surface analysis identified specific dose–time combinations in which weed suppression was achieved while canopy development, reproductive performance, and yield were maintained. In contrast, low herbicide doses resulted in insufficient weed control, whereas high doses or delayed applications reduced crop growth and productivity, reflecting the well-known trade-off between weed suppression and crop safety [8, 34].

The high predictive performance of the fitted RSM models further supports the value of multivariate approaches for herbicide optimization compared with conventional single-factor strategies [34, 35]. The observed dose-by-timing dependency likely reflects the temporal overlap between herbicide activity, weed susceptibility, and crop developmental sensitivity. Faba bean is particularly vulnerable during early vegetative growth because of its relatively slow canopy establishment, which increases both weed competition and sensitivity to herbicide stress [35]. Consequently, application timing relative to crop phenology emerged as a major determinant of crop performance and weed control, particularly when comparing early and late post-emergence treatments.

The desirability analysis confirmed that favorable crop performance and reduced weed biomass were achieved within a relatively limited range of imazethapyr doses and application timings. These findings emphasize the importance of synchronizing herbicide application with crop developmental stage rather than relying solely on herbicide rate, in agreement with recent recommendations for integrated herbicide management in grain legumes [34].

Canopy development and vegetative growth responses

LAI and PH responded differently to imazethapyr dose–time combinations, indicating distinct sensitivities of lateral canopy expansion and axial growth [8]. LAI exhibited a clear optimum at intermediate imazethapyr doses applied near sowing, whereas PH declined progressively with increasing dose and delayed application (Figs. 1 and 3).

These contrasting responses suggest that canopy expansion is more responsive to moderate weed suppression, while stem elongation is more directly constrained by herbicide-induced stress [36, 37]. The presence of a distinct LAI optimum, but not for PH, further highlights functional differences in how vegetative traits integrate competitive release and chemical stress [38].

The reduction in LAI under high-dose or late post-emergence applications is consistent with the known mode of action of imazethapyr as an ALS inhibitor [38].

Inhibition of branched-chain amino acid biosynthesis restricts meristematic activity and leaf expansion, while secondary oxidative stress and impaired carbon assimilation may further accelerate premature senescence [36–38]. The strong predictive performance of the fitted LAI model indicates that these physiological responses were effectively captured within the RSM framework.

In contrast, PH exhibited a continuous decline across the dose–time range, reflecting the sensitivity of stem elongation processes to herbicide exposure [37, 38].

This response suggests that PH is influenced more strongly by direct herbicide stress than by competitive interactions with weeds [36]. Consequently, although PH responded consistently to increasing herbicide stress, it appeared less suitable than LAI for identifying agronomically favorable management windows. Instead, PH may serve as an early indicator of sub-lethal herbicide stress, whereas LAI provides a more integrative measure linking weed suppression, canopy function, and yield formation [37].

Reproductive traits and yield components

Reproductive performance, reflected by NPPP and W100S, was strongly influenced by the interaction between imazethapyr dose and application timing [37, 39]. NPPP reached maximum values at intermediate herbicide doses applied from pre-emergence to early post-emergence stages (Fig. 5), whereas delayed applications and higher doses markedly reduced reproductive output, consistent with previous findings in grain legumes [39, 40]. These responses suggest that pod formation integrates the combined effects of early canopy establishment, weed suppression, and avoidance of herbicide-induced stress during reproductive initiation [40]. Moderate weed suppression within favorable application windows likely improved light interception and assimilate availability without imposing excessive physiological stress, resulting in greater pod numbers [39]. In contrast, late post-emergence or high-dose applications coincided with sensitive reproductive stages and were associated with reduced NPPP, likely due to impaired floral development and increased reproductive abortion [37, 39].

W100S exhibited a more temporally restricted response pattern. The lowest seed weights were observed when herbicide applications occurred during the early post-sowing period (1–5 days after sowing), indicating that grain filling processes are particularly sensitive to transient stress during this developmental phase (Fig. 7).

Outside this period, W100S remained relatively stable even at higher herbicide doses, suggesting some compensatory capacity once reproductive sink size had been established [39, 40].

The contrasting responses of NPPP and W100S indicate partial decoupling between reproductive sink number and individual seed mass, a phenomenon reported previously in grain legumes exposed to weed competition or herbicide stress [40].

While pod number appeared sensitive to dose–time effects operating earlier in development, seed weight responded more strongly to stress occurring within a narrower phenological window [39]. These findings emphasize the importance of evaluating multiple reproductive traits simultaneously when assessing herbicide responses in faba bean [37].

Notably, the regions associated with favorable NPPP and W100S largely overlapped with the zone of maximum LAI (Fig. 1), highlighting the importance of early canopy establishment for supporting reproductive sink strength [35]. When canopy development was compromised by either insufficient weed control or excessive herbicide stress, both pod formation and seed filling were negatively affected [39]. This coordinated response helps explain the close association among vegetative performance, reproductive traits, and final yield observed in the optimization analyses.

Biological and grain yield integration

BY and GY exhibited similar but not identical response patterns across the imazethapyr dose–time range, reflecting their shared dependence on early vegetative performance but differing sensitivity to reproductive allocation processes. BY displayed a relatively broad favorable response range (Fig. 9), whereas GY showed a narrower optimum (Fig. 11), consistent with previous reports that grain yield is more strongly constrained than biomass under herbicide and weed stress [40]. This distinction suggests that total biomass accumulation is less restrictive than grain production, which additionally depends on reproductive efficiency and harvest index.

BY was strongly affected by application timing, with pronounced reductions observed when imazethapyr was applied during the early post-sowing period (Fig. 9), in agreement with previous studies in pulses [39]. Outside this sensitive period, particularly under pre-plant or later applications, BY remained relatively high despite increasing herbicide dose, suggesting greater tolerance of vegetative growth once canopy establishment had occurred [37]. In contrast, GY exhibited a more restricted favorable response range, particularly at intermediate herbicide doses applied during intermediate timings (Fig. 11). Reduced GY at low herbicide doses was associated with insufficient weed suppression and increased crop competition, whereas higher doses likely imposed herbicide stress and reduced reproductive efficiency [37, 39].

The partial decoupling between BY and GY highlights the importance of reproductive partitioning in determining agronomic performance [40]. While several dose–time combinations supported relatively high biomass production, fewer conditions resulted in maximum GY, indicating that reproductive allocation imposes additional constraints beyond vegetative growth [37, 39]. The close association between favorable GY, LAI, NPPP, and W100S responses further emphasizes the importance of early canopy establishment and reproductive stability for final yield formation [41].

Collectively, these findings demonstrate the usefulness of RSM for integrating vegetative and reproductive responses and identifying management strategies that optimize grain yield rather than biomass alone. This distinction is particularly relevant for precision herbicide management, where agronomic and economic performance depend primarily on reproductive output and crop safety [39, 40].

Weed suppression dynamics and competitive release

DWW was strongly influenced by imazethapyr dose and application timing, with the lowest weed biomass observed at intermediate herbicide rates applied during early growth stages (Fig. 13) [42, 43]. Under these conditions, weed suppression was highly effective, whereas low herbicide doses resulted in persistent weed competition regardless of timing [42, 44, 45]. DWW also increased under delayed post-emergence applications, even at higher imazethapyr doses, likely because weeds had progressed beyond their most susceptible developmental stages and canopy closure reduced spray interception and herbicide translocation [42, 43]. These results highlight the importance of synchronizing herbicide application with weed developmental stage rather than relying solely on increasing herbicide dose.

A notable feature of the DWW response was the limited additional weed suppression achieved at herbicide doses above 500 mL ha⁻¹ when applications were performed early, consistent with dose–plateau responses previously reported in imazethapyr-treated legumes [44]. Beyond this threshold, increasing herbicide dose produced only minor reductions in weed biomass, whereas crop performance continued to decline with increasing dose (Figs. 1 and 3, and 11). This asymmetry between weed suppression and crop tolerance likely explains the reduction in GY observed at high imazethapyr doses despite sustained weed control [38, 40]. While weed biomass remained low, crop growth and reproductive performance were increasingly constrained by herbicide-induced stress, consistent with previous reports of ALS-inhibitor phytotoxicity in legumes [38, 40].

Model diagnostics for DWW demonstrated strong predictive performance, indicating that weed biomass was effectively captured within the RSM framework [46] (Table 3; Fig. 14). Together with crop performance variables, DWW provided a reliable indicator for identifying management strategies that improve weed suppression while minimizing unnecessary crop stress and excessive herbicide input [40, 47].

Multivariate optimization and desirability analysis

The multivariate desirability analysis provided an integrated framework for balancing crop performance and weed suppression by simultaneously incorporating morphophysiological traits, reproductive attributes, yield components, and DWW [48].

The desirability function identified a relatively limited range of imazethapyr doses and application timings associated with favorable overall system performance (Fig. 15), consistent with previous agricultural applications of multiresponse optimization approaches [49, 50]. The overall desirability value (0.746) represents a practical compromise among multiple biological responses rather than maximization of a single trait.

The regions associated with favorable desirability values largely overlapped with those identified independently for LAI, NPPP, W100S, GY, and minimum DWW, indicating strong consistency among the fitted response surfaces. Intermediate imazethapyr doses (250–500 mL ha⁻¹) applied within 0–10 days after sowing provided the most balanced combination of crop growth, reproductive performance, and weed suppression, whereas excessively low or high herbicide doses resulted in less favorable outcomes [51, 52].

The fitted models revealed strong nonlinear interactions between herbicide dose and application timing, reflecting threshold effects and diminishing returns that are characteristic of crop–weed–herbicide systems [48–50]. These interactions would likely be difficult to identify using conventional single-factor approaches, highlighting the usefulness of RSM for evaluating complex agronomic responses.

From a practical perspective, desirability analysis provides an interpretable decision-support approach for identifying herbicide management strategies that improve agronomic performance while minimizing unnecessary crop stress and excessive chemical input [53]. Combined with the satisfactory predictive performance of the fitted models (Table 3), this framework provides a useful basis for precision herbicide management in grain legumes [51, 52].

Implications and limitations

The present findings have important implications for herbicide optimization in grain legumes, particularly in systems where crop tolerance and weed pressure overlap during sensitive phenological stages [34]. The convergence of favorable responses across morphophysiological traits, reproductive components, grain yield, and weed suppression indicates that imazethapyr performance in faba bean is governed by integrated crop–weed–herbicide interactions rather than isolated responses of individual traits [38, 41]. These findings support the concept that herbicide management should be considered a dynamic, phenology-dependent process rather than a fixed input decision.

From an agronomic perspective, the results indicate that yield penalties arise from two opposing constraints: insufficient weed suppression at low herbicide doses and herbicide-induced crop stress at excessive doses or delayed application timings [52, 54]. Intermediate dose–time combinations provided the most favorable balance between crop performance and weed control, whereas excessively low or high herbicide inputs reduced system performance and yield stability. The maximum grain yields observed under optimized conditions fall within the upper range previously reported for faba bean under favorable management and low weed interference [34, 55]. However, the fitted response surfaces should be interpreted as empirical interpolation tools within the tested factor space, and broader agronomic recommendations require additional validation across seasons and environments [16].

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the experiment was conducted during a single growing season at one location, limiting extrapolation across contrasting climatic conditions [56]. Seasonal variation in temperature, rainfall, and radiation may alter crop growth, weed emergence, and herbicide persistence, potentially shifting the favorable dose–time combinations identified in this study [34]. Second, only a single faba bean cultivar was evaluated, and varietal differences in herbicide tolerance, canopy architecture, and phenology may influence response patterns [41]. Third, the weed community was site-specific, and differences in weed species composition and competitive ability may modify treatment responses under different agroecological conditions [8].

Biomass-related traits such as BY exhibited greater variability than canopy- or weed-related responses, likely reflecting spatial heterogeneity in soil properties, microclimate, and plant competition. Although the RSM framework effectively identified favorable management regions, predictive precision could likely be improved by incorporating site-specific covariates such as soil texture, organic matter, soil moisture dynamics, and weed community structure [57]. Future multi-environment studies integrating RSM with spatially explicit datasets would improve model transferability and strengthen its application in precision herbicide management.

Accordingly, the optimized dose–timing combinations identified here should be interpreted as context-specific and require validation across seasons, locations, and cultivars before broad agronomic implementation [52, 56].

Practical recommendations and future research directions

The results of this study provide evidence-based guidance for improving imazethapyr management in faba bean production systems [43]. The combined response surface and desirability analyses indicated that intermediate imazethapyr doses (250–500 mL ha⁻¹) applied from pre-emergence to early post-emergence (0–10 days after sowing) provided the most favorable balance between crop performance and weed suppression, consistent with previous findings in grain legumes [58, 59]. Applications outside this window, particularly at delayed timings or inappropriate doses, resulted in either inadequate weed control or increased crop stress, both of which negatively affected yield stability [42]. Relative to the weed-free treatment, optimized imazethapyr applications approached but did not exceed maximum yield potential, indicating that application timing is at least as important as herbicide dose for achieving favorable agronomic outcomes [43, 52].

From an environmental and economic perspective, the absence of additional agronomic benefit at doses above 500 mL ha⁻¹ indicates diminishing returns from dose escalation and highlights opportunities to reduce herbicide input without compromising crop performance [52, 60]. Phenology-aligned applications at intermediate herbicide rates may therefore improve input-use efficiency, reduce production costs, and minimize unnecessary chemical load, supporting more sustainable weed management strategies in grain legumes [42, 60].

Future research should evaluate the stability of the identified dose–time relationships across multiple seasons, locations, soil types, and weed communities, particularly given the strong environmental influence on herbicide performance reported in legumes [43, 58]. Additional studies incorporating cultivar variability and weed functional traits could further improve the transferability and predictive capacity of the response surface models [42, 58, 59]. Integration of RSM with modern high-throughput phenotyping, image analysis, and economic optimization approaches may also enhance the development of decision-support tools for precision herbicide management [23, 61].

Because this study was conducted during a single growing season under semi-arid field conditions in western Iran, broader agronomic recommendations require further validation under contrasting environmental conditions. Particular attention should be given to potential phytotoxic responses associated with soil-applied imazethapyr treatments across variable environments.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that imazethapyr dose and application timing jointly influence crop performance and weed suppression in faba bean through strong nonlinear interactions. Across vegetative growth, reproductive development, yield formation, and weed biomass, intermediate herbicide doses applied during early growth stages provided the most favorable balance between effective weed control and crop tolerance.

These conditions were associated with improved canopy development, enhanced reproductive performance, and higher grain yield.

The response surface analysis proved useful for identifying complex dose–time response patterns and for integrating multiple agronomic responses within a unified optimization framework. The desirability analysis further highlighted the value of balancing crop performance and weed suppression through compromise management strategies rather than maximizing individual responses.

Beyond its crop-specific findings, this study supports the broader applicability of RSM-based approaches as decision-support tools for precision herbicide management.

By quantifying the agronomic consequences of different dose–time combinations, this framework may assist in improving the alignment between herbicide application and crop phenology.

Because the experiment was conducted during a single growing season under semi-arid conditions in western Iran, broader agronomic recommendations require further validation across environments, cultivars, and weed communities. Nevertheless, the observed response patterns suggest that phenology-aligned, intermediate imazethapyr applications may improve crop performance while limiting unnecessary herbicide input in faba bean production systems.
