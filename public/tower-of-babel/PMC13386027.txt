Disruption of Claudin‐2 and Trpv6 Results in Hypocalcemia, Hypercalciuria, and Altered Bone Microarchitecture in Mice
Authors: Harneet Kaur Bhullar, Amanda Lima Deluque, Debbie D O'Neil, Wanling Pan, Michael R Doschak, Henrik Dimke, R Todd Alexander
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTransepithelial calcium absorption occurs via a transcellular or paracellular pathway. The intestinal transcellular pathway relies, at least partially, on the apical calcium channel transient receptor potential vanilloid 6 (TRPV6), whereas paracellular calcium (re)absorption occurs through tight junction proteins, including claudin‐2 (CLDN2). Both CLDN2 and TRPV6 are also expressed in the kidney. CLDN2 contributes to proximal tubular calcium reabsorption, whereas the functional relevance of TRPV6 remains unclear. Cldn2 −/− knockout mice exhibit reduced intestinal calcium permeability and hypercalciuria, while maintaining normal bone mineral density (BMD), plasma calcium, and calciotropic hormone levels. Trpv6 D541A/D541A pore mutants were reported to have normal urinary and plasma calcium levels, despite reduced intestinal calcium absorption on a low calcium diet. However, we found subtle sex‐specific differences in calcium homeostasis in these mice. We hypothesized that transcellular and paracellular calcium absorption pathways compensate for one another in these models. To test this, we crossed Cldn2 −/− with Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice to generate a functional double knockout (DKO, Cldn2 −/−/Trpv6 D541A/D541A). These mice displayed hypocalcemia, elevated calcitriol, and reduced BMD. Surprisingly, despite unchanged fecal calcium excretion, renal calcium wasting was greater than in Cldn2 −/− mice. Gene expression studies revealed increased calbindin‐D9k expression in the proximal colon and calbindin‐D28k in the kidney, consistent with partial compensation by the colon and distal nephron. Together, these findings indicate that combined impairment of paracellular and transcellular calcium transport reduces intestinal and renal calcium (re)absorption, leading to hypocalcemia and increased calcitriol levels, and highlight a role for TRPV6 in distal nephron calcium handling.Keywords: bone mineral density, calcium homeostasis, claudin‐2, paracellular calcium pathway, transcellular calcium pathway, Trpv6

Introduction

Calcium, the fifth most prevalent mineral in the human body, has a myriad of functions, including propagating heart and muscle contractions, nerve conduction, cell signaling, and maintaining bone integrity [1]. Plasma calcium concentrations are tightly regulated by the coordinated actions of calciotropic hormones, including parathyroid hormone (PTH) and calcitriol (1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D), which together control dietary calcium absorption and renal reabsorption [2].

Dietary calcium absorption occurs via both transcellular and paracellular pathways [3, 4]. Paracellular calcium absorption is a passive process accounting for the majority of calcium uptake when dietary calcium is sufficient. It is mediated by tight junction proteins, called claudins (CLDNs), with claudin‐2 (CLDN2) playing a key role. Cldn2 deletion in mice results in reduced paracellular calcium permeability in the colon, yet total intestinal calcium absorption is similar to wild‐type (WT) animals, likely due to compensation by another, potentially the transcellular pathway [5, 6]. As the paracellular pathway relies on the electrochemical gradient, it can either absorb or secrete calcium, depending on the lumen‐to‐blood calcium gradient [4].

Transcellular calcium absorption predominates when a diet with low calcium content is ingested and is reliant on apical calcium channel(s). Specifically, it is mediated by the apical calcium channel transient receptor potential vanilloid 6 (TRPV6), and under some circumstances by the L‐type voltage channel, Cav1.3 [3, 7]. Inside enterocytes, calcium is bound with high affinity to a class of proteins known as calbindins, with calbindin‐D9k (calb9k, encoded by S100g) being predominantly expressed in the intestine [8, 9, 10, 11]. Importantly, calbindin expression is induced by increased transcellular calcium flux. Along with their role in ferrying calcium from the apical side of the cell to the basolateral side, calbindins also act to buffer calcium in the cytosol, thereby preventing its toxic effects at higher concentrations [12]. At the basolateral side, calcium is extruded via the plasma membrane calcium ATPase (PMCA1b) as well as the sodium‐calcium exchanger (NCX1) [9, 11, 13, 14].

Both CLDN2 and TRPV6 are also expressed in the kidney. CLDN2 is present in the proximal tubule (PT), where 60%–70% of the filtered calcium is reabsorbed via a paracellular pathway. Cldn2 −/− mice display hypercalciuria, due in part to defective PT calcium reabsorption, but maintain normal serum ionized calcium (iCa2+), bone mineral density (BMD), and normal blood PTH and calcitriol levels [6]. TRPV5 plays a significant role in calcium reabsorption in the distal convoluted tubule/connecting tubule (DCT/CNT), reabsorbing about 5%–10% of calcium from the prourine. Calcium enters the epithelial cell through TRPV5, where it is bound by calbindin‐D28K (calb28k, encoded by Calb1), which ferries calcium to the basolateral membrane. Calcium is then extruded by PMCA4 and NCX1 across the basolateral membrane [15, 16, 17]. On a low calcium diet, renal Trpv5 expression increased in Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice; however, Trpv6 expression was comparable to that of the wild‐type animals [18]. Trpv6 is known to be expressed in the kidney; its exact location and role in renal calcium handling are not fully understood. Its calcium permeability depends on an aspartate at position 541. In rodents, alterations of this residue resulted in abrogation of channel function [19]. Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice were reported to have no alteration in calcium homeostasis from WT littermates on a normal calcium diet. However, when challenged with a low calcium diet, both WT and mutant mice display normal serum calcium, bone mineral density, and urine calcium excretion, yet a reduced ability to increase intestinal calcium absorption [18].

There are known significant sex differences in calcium homeostasis, but the sex‐specific phenotype of the animals employed for these studies was not reported. We found that male Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice had hypocalcemia and increased calcitriol, whereas female knockin mice maintained normal blood calcium and calciotropic hormone levels, likely due to lower urinary calcium excretion. Next, to investigate whether paracellular calcium absorption compensates for impaired transcellular absorption, we crossed Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice with Cldn2 −/− mice to generate functional double knockout (DKO) animals. Surprisingly, DKO mice developed hypocalcemia and more severe hypercalciuria than Cldn2 −/− mice, and had elevated calcitriol levels. Furthermore, calb9k mRNA expression in the colon was 10‐fold higher in the DKO mice compared to WT littermates, consistent with compensatory upregulation of transcellular calcium absorption. This study thus addresses the integrated role of paracellular and transcellular pathways in calcium homeostasis, providing insights into their sex‐specific regulation and compensation.

Results

Trpv6 D541A /D541A Male Mice Display Hypocalcemia and Elevated Calcitriol

We first assessed the calcium phenotype of male and female Trpv6D541A/D541A mutant mice on a normal calcium diet. Female WT and mutant animals had lower body weight than male animals, reflecting a significant effect of sex (p < 0.0001, Table S1). No genotype‐dependent differences were observed in chow intake, water intake, urine volume, urine pH, or fecal mass (Table S1). Male Trpv6D541A/D541A mice displayed significantly reduced plasma iCa2+ compared to WT littermates, whereas no difference was observed in female mice. Accordingly, two‐way ANOVA found a significant effect of genotype for iCa2+ levels (Figure 1A and Table S2). Serum PTH was not significantly different between genotypes of either sex (Figure 1B and Table S2). In contrast, calcitriol levels were significantly elevated in male Trpv6D541A/D541A mice (p = 0.033), with no difference observed in females (p = 0.741, Figure 1C). A significant effect of genotype was also observed for calcitriol levels (Table S2).

Trpv6 D541A /D541A Female Mice Have Reduced Urinary Calcium Excretion

Urinary calcium excretion was unchanged in male Trpv6D541A/D541A mice, but significantly reduced in female mutant animals, both over 24 h and when normalized to creatinine (Figure 1D,E). Two‐way ANOVA revealed a significant effect of sex and genotype, as well as a significant interaction between these factors (Figure 1D,E and Table S2). Fecal calcium excretion, expressed as a percentage of dietary intake, was not significantly different between genotypes of either sex. Calcium balance and calcium bioavailability were not significantly altered, consistent with no detectable difference in whole‐body calcium handling in Trpv6D541A/D541A mice (Figure 1F–H). Additionally, urinary phosphate excretion was not significantly different between genotypes. However, a significant effect of sex was observed for phosphate excretion normalized to creatinine (Figure 1I and Table S2).

Tables S1 and S2 provide further information from the metabolic cage studies, as well as serum and urine biochemistry of the Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice. Overall, no significant genotype‐dependent changes were noted in metabolic cage parameters (Table S1). Beyond the calcium‐related parameters described above, serum chloride and AnGap were only significantly different in the male Trpv6D541A/D541A animals, with no significant changes noted in other serum values (Table S2). Urinary creatinine displayed a significant difference between sexes as well as a sex by genotype interaction (Table S2). No additional consistent genotype‐dependent differences were observed for urinary parameters.

Gene expression analyses found tissue and sex‐specific differences in Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice (Figures S1–S3). In the duodenum, female mutant mice showed reduced expression of Trpv6 and calb9k, with overall lower expression of Cldn2, Trpv6, calb9k, and PMCA1b compared to male mutant mice. In the proximal colon, male mutant mice exhibited reduced expression of Ca v 1.3 and NCX, while Trpv6 expression differed between sexes. In the kidney, female mutant mice had increased Cldn12 expression, whereas male mutant animals displayed increased Cyp27b1 expression, consistent with hormonal compensation (Figures 1C, S1–S3 and Table S3).

Cldn2 −/−/Trpv6 D541A/D541A DKO Mice Have Hypocalcemia and Elevated Calcitriol Levels

Others have found that Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice only have a significant calcium phenotype when placed on a low calcium diet, where they were unable to increase intestinal calcium absorption to the same extent as their WT littermates [18]. To determine whether paracellular calcium absorption compensates for the loss of Trpv6, we generated Cldn2 −/− /Trpv6 D541A/D541A DKO mice. Male DKO mice had reduced body weight compared to WT littermates, with significant differences between sexes and genotypes as well as a sex by genotype interaction, with female mice having lower body weight than males overall (Table S4). Chow consumption, water intake, urine volume, urine pH, and fecal output were not significantly different between genotypes, although these parameters showed an effect of sex (Table S4).

A significant reduction in iCa2+ was observed in both male and female DKO animals when compared to their WT littermates (p < 0.05, Figure 2A and Table S5). PTH levels showed a significant effect of genotype, although no significant differences were detected between genotypes within each sex (Figure 2B and Table S5). Circulating calcitriol levels were significantly increased in both sexes (p < 0.0001, Figure 2C and Table S5). No other differences in blood biochemistry were observed, except that male DKO mice had lower blood glucose in comparison to their WT littermates, with a significant sex by genotype interaction observed for this parameter (Table S5).

Cldn2 −/−/ Trpv6 D541A /D541A Mice Have Unaltered Intestinal Calcium Absorption but Increased Urinary Calcium Excretion

Urinary calcium excretion, both over 24 h and when normalized to creatinine, was significantly increased in male and female DKO mice compared to WT animals (Figure 2D,E and Table S5). Female DKO mice exhibited greater urinary calcium excretion than male DKO animals. Two‐way ANOVA identified a significant effect of sex and genotype, as well as a significant sex by genotype interaction for both parameters (Table S5).

To determine whether combined disruption of paracellular and transcellular calcium transport exacerbates renal calcium wasting, urinary calcium excretion was compared across WT, Cldn2 −/−, Trpv6 D541A/D541A , and DKO mice (Figure 2F). DKO mice of both sexes displayed the greatest urinary calcium excretion among all genotypes. In addition, the Cldn2 −/− mice exhibited greater urinary calcium excretion than Trpv6 D541A/D541A animals of both sexes (Figure 2F). Together, these findings demonstrate a worsening of urinary calcium loss with combined disruption of paracellular and transcellular calcium transport pathways in the nephron.

Calcium balance and calcium bioavailability were not significantly different between genotypes, although calcium bioavailability showed a tendency toward reduction in DKO mice (Figure 2G,H). Urinary phosphate excretion was also assessed with no consistent genotype‐dependent difference observed between groups (Figure 2I and Table S5). However, urinary phosphate excretion was significantly increased in male DKO compared to WT animals, whereas female DKO mice had only a tendency toward increased phosphate excretion. Urinary total electrolyte excretion, both over 24 h and normalized to creatinine, was also assessed and is reported in Table S5. Overall, these results demonstrate pronounced renal calcium wasting in DKO mice despite preserved calcium bioavailability.

Renal Trpv6 Expression Localizes to Distal Nephron Segments

To further investigate the renal localization of Trpv6, we examined Trpv6 expression using a reporter mouse where tdRFP was expressed after the Trpv6 promoter [20, 21]. TdRFP‐positive cells were detected in tubular segments that did not overlap with the proximal tubular marker SGLT2, indicating that Trpv6 is not expressed in the proximal tubule (Figure 3A,B). In contrast, tdRFP‐positive cells colocalized with TRPV5‐positive tubular epithelial cells (Figure 3C), supporting localization of Trpv6 to the DCT and CNT. WT control kidneys showed no tdRFP signal, confirming reporter specificity (Figure 3D–F). In conclusion, these findings support the possibility that TRPV6 may form homo‐ or functional heterotetramers with TRPV5 in the distal nephron [22].

Cldn2 −/−/ Trpv6 D541A/D541A Mice Have Increased Proximal Colon Calcium Absorption Pathway Gene Expression

Next, we measured the expression of genes mediating calcium absorption in the duodenum and proximal colon of the DKO mice. In the duodenum, male DKO mice exhibited reduced expression of Cldn2 and NCX compared to WT males (Figure S4). WT male mice also displayed higher expression of Cldn2, Ca v 1.3, calb9k, NCX, and PMCA1b compared to WT females. Two‐way ANOVA found a significant effect of sex for calb9k and PMCA1b, and a significant effect of genotype for Cldn2, Trpv6, and Ca v 1.3 (Table S3). Overall, only modest changes in duodenal calcium transport gene expression were observed in DKO mice despite the duodenum representing a major site of transcellular intestinal calcium absorption.

Cldn2 was not detected in the colon of either male or female DKO animals (Figure 4A). Trpv6 and calb9k expression were significantly increased in both male and female DKO mice compared to WT littermates (Figure 4B,D). This increase is consistent with the elevated circulating calcitriol levels observed in DKO mice, which act to increase Trpv6 expression, yet the channel is nonfunctional [23]. Further, we noted no changes in mRNA abundance of Ca v 1.3, NCX, and PMCA1b, other genes involved in calcium absorption (Figure 4C,E,F). Similarly, expression of Cldn12, another tight junction protein responsible for forming cation‐permeable pores [5], Cldn15, Cldn23, and nhe3 was not significantly altered between genotypes or sexes (Supplemental Figure S5A–D). Two‐way ANOVA additionally revealed significant sex‐dependent effects for several proximal colon calcium transport genes (Table S8). Taken together, these results suggest an increase in transcellular calcium absorption from the proximal colon, stimulating increased calb9k expression, in compensation for reduced calcium absorption from the small intestine [24].

Cldn2 −/−/ Trpv6 D541A /D541A Mice Have Altered Expression of Calcium Reabsorption Pathway Genes

To further examine compensatory mechanisms, we measured the expression of genes responsible for calcium reabsorption along the nephron. Cldn2 expression was not detectable in DKO mice of either sex (Figure 5A), and Cldn12 expression was not significantly altered (Figure 5B). Claudin‐16 (Cldn16) and −19 (Cldn19) are expressed in the thick ascending limb and are responsible for forming divalent cation‐permeable pores [17, 25, 26]. In the DKO mice, we found increased expression of Cldn16 in males, but not females (Figure 5C), whereas no genotype‐dependent differences were observed for Cldn19 expression (Figure 5D). Claudin‐14 (Cldn14) expression was significantly increased in male DKO animals only (Figure 5E).

Trpv5 and calb28k expression were significantly increased in both male and female DKO animals compared to WT littermates (Figure 5F,G). NCX expression was increased only in female DKO mice relative to WT females (Figure 5J). Similarly, Cyp27b1, the enzyme responsible for 1‐hydroxylating 25‐hydroxyvitamin D, was significantly upregulated in both male and female DKO mice (Figure 5K), whereas no significant genotype‐dependent differences were observed for PMCA1b, PMCA4, or Cyp24a1, the gene encoding the enzyme that 24‐hydroxylates 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D (Figure 5H,I,L). Several renal calcium transport genes also exhibited significant sex‐dependent expression patterns, with female mice generally expressing higher levels than males, except for Cyp24a1 (Table S8). Overall, these results are consistent with significant renal compensation in the DKO animals. However, this is insufficient to correct the hypocalcemia or even reduce urinary calcium excretion to that of the Cldn2 −/− mice. These findings support an important role for TRPV6 in DCT/CNT calcium reabsorption.

Cldn2 −/−/ Trpv6 D541A /D541A Have Altered Bone Microarchitecture

Cldn2 −/− mice do not have altered trabecular bone volume and bone mineral density (BMD) [6]. Similarly, Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice at six months of age did not display changes in bone microarchitectural morphometry or bone mineral density [27]. Given the calcium phenotype of the DKO mice, we next analyzed the bone of DKO animals by μ‐CT. Trabecular BMD was significantly reduced in male DKO but remained unchanged in females (Figure 6A). Both male and female DKO mice exhibited reduced trabecular bone (Figure 6B) and tissue volume (Figure 6B and Table S6), whereas a reduction in the bone volume fraction was observed only in males (Table S6). Trabecular thickness was decreased in both sexes and genotypes (Figure 6C), while trabecular number was reduced exclusively in male DKO mice and trabecula separation was unchanged (Table S6).

Analysis of cortical bone revealed a reduction in cortical tissue mineral density (TMD) in male DKO mice (Figure 6D), whereas females were unaffected. In addition, cortical tissue and bone volume did not differ between genotypes, while bone volume fraction was reduced only in males (Figure 6E and Table S6). Cortical thickness was greater in females than in males, independent of genotype (Figure 6F). Several trabecular and cortical bone parameters also exhibited significant effects of sex, genotype, or sex by genotype interaction (Table S7), supporting altered bone microarchitecture in DKO mice.

Discussion

Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice fed a normal calcium diet did not demonstrate a dramatic calcium phenotype, consistent with previous work [18]. However, male mice displayed mild hypocalcemia and increased plasma calcitriol levels, findings that were not previously reported [18]. Cldn2 −/− mice exhibit hypercalciuria but maintain normal blood calcium and bone density [6]. Based on these observations, we hypothesized that the paracellular pathway, in part mediated by Cldn2, compensates for the loss of transcellular calcium absorption in the Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice. To test this hypothesis, we generated Cldn2 −/−/Trpv6 D541A/D541A DKO mice. Although overall calcium bioavailability was preserved, DKO mice exhibited hypocalcemia, marked hypercalciuria, and altered bone microarchitecture relative to WT and single mutant animals. Increased calb9k expression in the proximal colon of the DKO mice suggests activation of compensatory transcellular calcium transport pathways in this intestinal segment. Similarly, increased renal expression of Trpv5 and calb28K indicates activation of distal nephron calcium‐conserving mechanisms. However, these adaptations were insufficient to normalize plasma calcium or prevent urinary calcium loss. Together, these findings support a model in which combined disruption of paracellular and transcellular calcium transport results in renal calcium wasting that cannot be fully compensated by intestinal or renal adaptive responses.

Our investigation of the Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice also revealed a sex‐specific difference in blood calcium that has not been previously reported [18]. One important distinction between our study and that of Woudenberg‐Vrenken et al. is that we analyzed animals separately by sex, whereas the sex of the animals used in their study was not clearly described [18]. In addition, we also measured ionized calcium rather than total blood calcium, which also includes calcium bound to proteins [18]. The previous work fed Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice a diet containing 1% calcium, whereas our animals received a diet containing 0.81% calcium. We observed a significant decrease in urinary calcium excretion in the Trpv6 D541A/D541A female mice, which may represent a compensatory mechanism contributing to the preservation of plasma calcium. Importantly, many of our findings remain consistent with previous work, including unaltered intestinal calcium absorption when the mice were fed a normal calcium diet.

Our work and the previous study on Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice contrast with findings from the Trpv6 KO animals, which display alopecia and growth retardation [28]. The Trpv6 D541A/D541A model relies on making the channel non‐functional rather than completely deleting it. Having the channel intact allows for interactions with scaffolding or regulatory proteins. Thus, complete deletion of Trpv6 may disrupt other potential channel‐associated functions, which might contribute to a more severe phenotype. Of note, the Trpv6 KO mouse generated by Bianco et al. also contains a partial deletion in the EphB6 gene, which may represent a confounding variable resulting in the greater calcium perturbations observed in these Trpv6 KO mice.

Cldn2 −/−/Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice displayed a significant calcium phenotype characterized by urinary calcium loss, low plasma calcium levels, elevated calcitriol levels, and altered bone microarchitecture. PTH levels also demonstrated a significant genotype effect, consistent with activation of calcium‐conserving endocrine responses to reduced plasma calcium. The persistent hormonal compensation, together with uncompensated urinary calcium loss, likely contributed to the skeletal abnormalities observed in DKO mice. Elevated circulating calcitriol levels together with increased renal Cyp27b1 expression further support activation of compensatory vitamin D signaling pathways in DKO mice. In contrast, Cyp24a1 expression was not significantly altered, suggesting sustained activation of calcitriol‐dependent responses without a parallel increase in vitamin D catabolism. Remarkably, the hypercalciuria observed in the DKO mice exceeded that observed in Cldn2 −/− mice or Trpv6 D541A/D541A animals alone [6, 18]. This occurs despite increased expression of several renal calcium reabsorption pathway genes, including Trpv5 and calb28K. These findings suggest an important contribution of TRPV6 to distal nephron calcium reabsorption, potentially through functional interactions with TRPV5. This interpretation is further supported by the localization of Trpv6‐expressing cells to TRPV5‐positive distal nephron segments in the reporter mouse model. Introducing a pore mutation such as TRPV6D541A may influence the ability to assemble into functional heteromers with TRPV5, thereby disrupting calcium reabsorption. This mutation, affecting the selectivity filter, impairs ion permeability and may potentially interfere with heterotetramer function, thereby contributing to altered calcium handling [22].

We did not observe a significant alteration in overall intestinal calcium absorption in DKO mice. Given the known roles of TRPV6 and CLDN2 in mediating calcium absorption from the intestine, these findings suggest compensation by alternative transport pathways. This interpretation is supported by a quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping study by Reyes‐Fernandez et al. [29], which identified multiple loci regulating intestinal calcium absorption and adaptive responses to low dietary calcium, none of which mapped to canonical calcium transport genes. These findings support the existence of alternative, currently uncharacterized pathways contributing to intestinal calcium transport. Interestingly, the duodenum exhibited only modest transcriptional adaptation despite representing a major site of intestinal calcium absorption. We observed dramatically increased calb9k in the colon, consistent with increased transcellular calcium absorption from this segment. In the absence of functional TRPV6, another calcium channel may mediate calcium entry across the apical membrane. The L‐type channel Cav1.3 has previously been suggested to mediate an alternative calcium absorption pathway [30, 31]. Consistent with this possibility, Cacna1d KO pups exhibit a complete loss of transcellular calcium flux across the jejunum when assessed ex vivo in Ussing chambers [7]. Similarly, perfusion of rat jejunum found a significant reduction in calcium absorption following treatment with the L‐type channel inhibitor, nifedipine [30, 32, 33]. Although we did not observe increased expression of Cav1.3 in intestinal segments from DKO mice, enhanced calcium flux through Cav1.3 channels could occur independently of transcriptional regulation. Future studies will be required to directly test whether Cav1.3 contributes to compensatory intestinal calcium absorption in DKO animals. Together, these findings highlight substantial plasticity in intestinal calcium transport along the intestinal tract.

Bone represents the principal reservoir of calcium in the body, and the skeletal phenotype observed in DKO mice likely reflects sustained endocrine activation secondary to impaired epithelial calcium transport. Persistent hypocalcemia, together with activation of calcium‐conserving endocrine pathways, including elevated calcitriol levels, is expected to stimulate osteoclastogenesis through enhanced osteoclast differentiation, thereby promoting mineral mobilization to stabilize extracellular calcium concentrations [34]. The reduction in trabecular bone parameters and cortical tissue mineral density, particularly pronounced in males, is consistent with sustained PTH‐driven bone resorption under chronic calcium stress. Although osteoblasts express claudin genes, including Cldn2, as well as tight junction–associated proteins [35], prior studies have demonstrated preserved bone microarchitecture in Cldn2 KO mice under both normal and low‐calcium diets [6]. Similarly, Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice do not exhibit major alterations in bone microarchitecture at baseline, despite subtle differences in femoral length [27]. These findings suggest that disruption of either pathway alone is insufficient to impair skeletal integrity. In contrast, the combined impairment of paracellular and transcellular calcium transport in our DKO mice appears to impose a greater systemic demand for calcium mobilization, resulting in detectable trabecular and cortical changes. Interestingly, TRPV channels have also been implicated in osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption [36], including roles in estrogen‐dependent regulation of osteoclast activity [37]. This may contribute to the sexual dimorphism observed in our model. Male DKO mice exhibited greater reductions in bone mineral density, whereas females, despite showing some trabecular alterations, maintained higher baseline bone mass. The relative preservation of skeletal architecture in females likely reflects estrogen‐mediated suppression of osteoclast activity, potentially combined with more efficient renal compensatory responses. Consistent with this possibility, female mice generally exhibited greater expression of several renal calcium transport genes compared to males.

Although altered expression of calcium transport genes strongly supports compensatory adaptations in the intestine and kidney, the precise segment‐specific transport mechanisms responsible for maintaining calcium balance in DKO mice remain to be fully defined. In particular, the potential functional interaction between TRPV6 and TRPV5, as well as the contribution of alternative intestinal calcium transport pathways, will require further physiological and electrophysiological investigation. Future studies directly examining protein expression, as well as calcium flux across individual nephron and intestinal segments ex vivo may provide additional mechanistic insight into these adaptive responses.

In summary, we identified subtle sex‐specific differences in Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice that were not previously reported, including hypocalcemia in males and reduced urinary calcium excretion in females. Cldn2 −/−/Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice exhibited incompletely compensated hypercalciuria, with both sexes displaying hypocalcemia despite activation of calcium‐conserving endocrine responses. These findings highlight the importance and complementary nature of the TRPV6‐mediated transcellular pathway and the CLDN2‐mediated paracellular pathway in systemic calcium homeostasis and support substantial plasticity in colonic transcellular calcium absorption. Finally, the severe hypercalciuria observed in DKO mice, which exceeded that observed in Cldn2 −/− animals, supports a functional role for TRPV6 in distal nephron calcium reabsorption.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Approval and Mutant Mice

All animal experiments were approved by the University of Alberta Animal Care and Use Committee (AUP00000213) and conducted in accordance with institutional and national guidelines. Cldn2 −/− mice and Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice were generated and maintained as previously described [5, 6, 38]. We generated Cldn2 −/−/Trpv6 D541A/D541A double knockout (DKO) mice by crossing Cldn2 −/− animals with Trpv6 D541A/D541A mutant mice. Both strains had been backcrossed onto the FVB/N strain, which was used as the wild type (WT) comparator. The mice were housed in a temperature‐ and light‐controlled room with ad libitum access to food and water. They were fed a standard rodent chow (Picolab Rodent Diet 20, 5053) containing 4% fat, 0.61% phosphorus (0.33% non‐phytate phosphorus), Vitamin D3 (2.3 IU/g), and 0.81% calcium. 72‐h metabolic cage studies were performed as previously described on 2–4‐month‐old mice [39, 40, 41]. Both male and female mice were used in all experiments, and data were analyzed with sex as an independent biological variable. Separate experimental cohorts were used to compare WT vs. Trpv6 D541A/D541A and WT vs. DKO mice. For urinary calcium excretion analyses, an additional Cldn2 −/− cohort was included to allow comparison across genotypes by normalizing to the wild type of each group and then combining. Other parameters were assessed within their respective experimental cohorts. Genotype was confirmed by PCR as previously described [6, 38], and Cldn2 deletion was verified by RT‐PCR on intestinal and kidney tissues. For the evaluation of Trpv6 expression in the kidney, we utilized Trpv6‐Cre expressing mice crossed with B6.Cg‐Gt(ROSA)26Sor tm1Hjf /J with cre‐inducible expression of RFP [42, 43] that was generously provided by Prof. Viet Flockerzi (University of Saarlandes).

Metabolic Cage Studies

WT and DKO animals, aged 2–4 months, were placed in metabolic cages for 72 h. Water and chow were provided ad libitum. Body weight was measured on Day 0, when the mice were placed in the metabolic cages. Body weight, urine volume, urine pH, and fecal weight were recorded every 24 h, with only third‐day samples used for analysis. After 72 h, mice were euthanized with a lethal dose of sodium pentobarbital. Blood was collected using cardiac puncture and stored in heparin‐coated tubes. Plasma was separated by spinning at 3500 rpm for 20 min at 4°C. Tissue was collected and snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored at −80°C. WT and Trpv6 D541A/D541A mice were subjected to the same protocol.

Urine, Fecal, and Serum Analysis

An iSTAT1 handheld Analyzer (Abaxis, Union City, CA, USA) with a CHEM8+ cartridge was used to measure blood electrolytes, including sodium (Na+), potassium (K+), chloride (Cl−), as well as ionized calcium (iCa2+), glucose, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), hematocrit (Hct), and hemoglobin (Hb). Urine and fecal electrolytes were measured via ion chromatography and urine creatinine by HPLC as previously described [5, 6]. These analyses were performed with Chromeleon 7 Chromatography Data System Software (Thermo Scientific). Blood collected was also used to measure total PTH (Immunotopics Mouse Intact PTH 1‐84) and calcitriol (Immunodiagnostics System Limited, UK) by ELISA following the manufacturer's instructions.

Real‐Time Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction

RNA extraction from tissue was performed as previously described [5, 7]. In brief, total RNA from frozen tissue was isolated using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). 2 μg of the isolated RNA was then made into cDNA (Thermo Fisher high‐capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit, CA, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions. All WT and DKO RNA or WT and Trpv6 mutant mice samples were combined to make standards, which were used to generate the standard curve for analysis. The cDNA, loaded in triplicate, was used to determine mRNA expression of Cldn‐2, ‐12, ‐15, ‐23, ‐19, ‐16, ‐14, Trpv6, Trpv5, S100g (encoding CalbindinD9k), Slc8a1 (encoding NCX1), Atp2b1 (encoding PMCA1b), Atp2b4 (enconding PMCA4), Slc9a3 (encoding Nhe3), Cacna1d (encoding Cav1.3), Calb1 (encoding CalbindinD28k), Cyp24α1 (encoding 24‐hydroxylase) and Cyp27β1 (encoding 1‐α‐hydroxylase) using primers and probes (Integrated DNA technologies) as previously [41, 44]. Ezrin or 18S were used as controls to determine the fold change and were not different if reported. Figure legends describe which gene samples were normalized to. A cycle threshold (cq) value greater than 35 was used as the cut‐off for a gene to be considered not expressed. Expression levels were quantified with an ABI Prism 7900 HT Sequence Detection System (Applied Biosystems). Standard curve and samples analysis were performed using Quant Studio Design and Analysis Software version 2.4.3.

Immunohistochemistry

Animals were perfusion‐fixed, and the kidneys were then postfixed for 3 h. The samples were next immersed in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) until paraffin embedding was completed using a Tissue Tec Vacuum Infiltration Processor 6 (manufactured by Sakura Finetek, Torrance, CA, USA). Kidneys were sectioned into 2 μm sections with an HM 355S Automatic Microtome (Thermo Scientific, Kalamazoo, MI, USA). Immunostaining was carried out as previously described [44]. Briefly, sections were rehydrated in Tissue‐clear (Sakura Finetek, Brøndby, Denmark) and graded ethanol. Antigen retrieval was performed by boiling sections in Tris‐EGTA buffer solution (10 mM Tris, 0.5 mM EGTA, pH = 9.0), and endogenous peroxidase activity and free aldehyde groups were blocked by incubation in a PBS solution containing 0.6% hydrogen peroxide and 50 mM ammonium chloride. The sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary anti‐mCherry antibody (SICGEN—AB0040‐200 goat 1:1000). Following washing, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) and then with a fluorescent Cy3‐coupled TSA substrate (TSA Cyanine 3, Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA). The sections were then boiled again in TEG buffer to remove bound antibodies while the TSA substrate remained attached, followed by a new round of immunolabeling with the anti‐SGLT2 (BiCELL—20802 rabbit 1:100) and anti‐TRPV5 primary antibodies (clone 10, produced in‐house [44]) and Alexa‐labeled 488 secondary antibodies.

Microcomputed Tomography (μ‐CT)

Tibia from WT and DKO animals were isolated after euthanization, fixed in neutral buffered formalin for three days, and then dehydrated in 70% ethanol, individually wrapped in paper towel strips before being stored at 4°C. The tibias were scanned directly in air‐tight centrifuge tubes, with attention given to ensure a central orientation for each tibial bone sample. X‐ray image projections were acquired using a Skyscan 1176 micro‐computed tomography imager (μ‐CT, Bruker‐Skyscan, Kontich, Belgium) at a resolution of 9 μm, voltage and current were set to 45 kV and 555 μA, respectively, with a 0.2 mm Aluminum filter used to remove low‐energy photons, a rotational step of 0.7°, and an exposure time of 750 ms. Reconstruction of the individual trabecular bone slices was performed using embedded NRecon software. Data Viewer and CT‐Analyzer software were used to reorient and quantify the trabecular and cortical percent bone volume and bone mineral density (BMD), trabecular thickness, number, and spacing, and cortical bone thickness, as previously conducted [5, 7, 45]. The trabecular bone region of interest consisted of 100 slices, starting at an offset of 20 slices beneath the proximal tibial metaphyseal growth plate. CTVol software (v.2.2.3.0) was used for 3D visualization of the bones.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 software. Outliers were identified using the ROUT method (Q = 1%) and excluded prior to analysis. Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data were subject to a two‐way ANOVA with sex and genotype as factors, followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test, unless otherwise stated. For datasets that deviated from normality, a log10 transformation was applied before analysis. Main effects and interaction terms are reported, with detailed statistical outputs provided in the Supporting Information tables. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
