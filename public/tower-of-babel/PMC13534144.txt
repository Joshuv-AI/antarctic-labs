Effectiveness of psychosocial interventions for anticipatory grief in informal caregivers for people living with dementia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Cong Fu, Marques Shek Nam Ng, Wai Tong Chien, Thi Nhi Vo, Stinne Glasdam, Patricia C Heyn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesPsychosocial interventions support informal caregivers of people with dementia, but their impact on anticipatory grief and related health outcomes remains unclear. This review examines the effectiveness of different psychosocial approaches in addressing these outcomes.Research Design and MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted following PRISMA guidelines and registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024629388). Eight databases were searched from inception to January 10, 2025. Two reviewers independently screened studies, assessed bias, and extracted data. Leave-one-out sensitivity and subgroup analyses addressed heterogeneity. Evidence quality was assessed using the GRADEpro Guideline Development Tool.ResultsEleven interventional studies were included. Psychosocial interventions (n = 6) had no significant short-term (<1 month: SMD = −0.57, 95% CI: [−1.14, 0.01], p = .05, I2 = 85%) or medium-term (1–6 months; SMD = −0.76, 95% CI: [−1.99, 0.46], p = .22, I2 = 88%, n = 3) effect on anticipatory grief but showed improvement in the long term (≥6 months; MD = −0.41, 95% CI: [−0.62, −0.20], p = .0001, I2 = 0%, n = 2). Short-term reductions in depression were also observed, with no significant effects in other outcomes. Subgroup analyses did not identify frameworks or delivery methods linked to better outcomes. The GRADEpro rated the evidence as “Low” to “Very Low.”Discussion and ImplicationsOverall, psychosocial interventions have limited short- and medium-term impact on anticipatory grief. Individual, technology-mediated cognitive-behavioral approaches may improve anticipatory grief in long term, but evidence is limited. Further research should clarify the concept of anticipatory grief and evaluate interventions in large, rigorous RCTs.Clinical Trial Registration numberCRD42024629388.Keywords: Dementia caregiving, Multiple losses, Emotional distress, Support for family member

Background

Dementia is a chronic and progressive condition that causes cognitive and noncognitive impairments, affecting memory, behavior, and the ability to perform daily activities (World Health Organization [WHO], 2023), resulting in increased disability and dependency on others (Duong et al., 2017; WHO, 2023). In 2020, 55 million people worldwide were diagnosed with dementia, and this number is estimated to triple by 2050, resulting in significant challenges to healthcare systems and societies (Nichols et al., 2022). Most people living with dementia live at home, and the care is primarily provided by informal caregivers (Engel et al., 2022). Informal caregivers are family members, extended relatives, friends, and neighbors who provide unpaid care and daily support (Li & Song, 2019). They provide emotional support, practical support, managing finances, and coordinating with healthcare providers throughout the caregiving journey (Clemmensen et al., 2021). Given the increasing number of people affected by dementia (Nichols et al., 2022), it is important to support informal caregivers of people living with dementia (Cheng & Zhang, 2020).

Due to the irreversible and prolonged nature of dementia, informal caregivers of people living with dementia often witness the progressive decline in cognitive and functional abilities while the care recipient remains alive (Lindeza et al., 2024). This prolonged caregiving journey can be emotionally taxing, as these informal caregivers not only need to manage increasing care demands but also experience multiple losses such as the loss of shared past experiences, the diminishing connection with the care recipient in the present, and uncertainty about the future (Blandin & Pepin, 2017; Conway et al., 2018). Such cumulative losses are conceptualized in recent literature as anticipatory grief, which Rando (1986) characterizes as a multidimensional experience involving losses in the past, present, and future prior to death. While some research associates experiencing high anticipatory grief and low preparedness for losses with poorer psychological and bereavement outcomes (Nielsen et al., 2016), others position it as part of a preparatory process through which informal caregivers may begin to emotionally adjust to subsequent transitions in the caregiving trajectory (Chan et al., 2013; Rando, 1986). For this review, anticipatory grief was considered as the target outcome.

Anticipatory grief may begin as early as diagnosis and continue throughout the caregiving trajectory (Andersson et al., 2019). It is a common experience among informal caregivers, although its reported prevalence depends on how it is measured or defined (Chan et al., 2013; Chaudhry et al., 2025). Anticipatory grief manifests in a wide range of emotional and cognitive responses, such as sadness, guilt, anger, social isolation, sleep disruption, appetite changes, and difficulty concentrating (Caputo, 2021; Lindeza et al., 2024). Informal caregivers experiencing anticipatory grief often report other psychosocial health problems, such as increased depression (Liew et al., 2019), caregiver burden (Pérez-González et al., 2021), psychological distress, and anxiety (Park & Galvin, 2021).

Psychosocial interventions are widely recommended and adopted to support informal caregivers for people living with dementia (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, 2018). However, anticipatory grief has received limited attention in the design of interventions for informal caregivers in dementia care (Cheng et al., 2020). Existing interventions often involve informational, cognitive, or social activities designed to promote the psychological and emotional well-being of people in need (Moniz-Cook et al., 2011). Psychosocial interventions include but are not limited to psychoeducation, psychological therapies, support groups, and counseling, which can be delivered either face-to-face or remotely (Liu & Shorey, 2021). Psychosocial interventions, such as psychoeducation, have shown to produce small to moderate improvements in informal caregivers’ knowledge of dementia and reduction of depressive symptoms (Cheng et al., 2020; Kishita et al., 2018; Teahan et al., 2020).

Systematic reviews have explored interventions addressing anticipatory grief in dementia caregiving (Byeon, 2020; Rupp et al., 2023; Sánchez-Alcón et al., 2023; Wilson et al., 2017). Two reviews rely on narrative synthesis and are therefore unable to statistically combine study results to determine overall intervention effectiveness (Rupp et al., 2023; Wilson et al., 2017). Byeon’s (2020) review assesses three studies (including one RCT) and reports no significant effects on anticipatory grief, with no other outcomes reported. Sánchez‐Alcón et al.’s (2023) review included five studies (of which two were RCTs) and reported small effects favoring the intervention group in improving anticipatory grief. However, one of the included studies contributed an effect estimate despite incomplete reporting of the mean data, and the review did not clearly describe how this estimate was derived. This may limit the transparency of the results, as it is unclear how the effect estimate was calculated, so the pooled results should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, neither review assessed intervention effects beyond the immediate post-intervention period, nor did they examine the effectiveness of interventions on other psychosocial outcomes (e.g., anxiety, caregiver burden, stress). Moreover, neither review assessed the quality of evidence using the GRADE framework, and both searched only a limited number of databases. Although Sánchez-Alcón et al. (2023) is a relatively recent review, the field of anticipatory grief in dementia caregiving is still emerging (Rupp et al., 2023), and several new RCTs have been published since that were not included in their analysis. Therefore, the existing evidence base does not provide a comprehensive understanding of the effectiveness of psychosocial interventions on anticipatory grief and other psychosocial outcomes among informal caregivers of people living with dementia. Moreover, optimal characteristics of effective psychosocial interventions addressing anticipatory grief in informal caregivers of people living with dementia remain unclear, including their delivery mode, format, and main components. Therefore, this review aimed to examine the effectiveness of various approaches to psychosocial interventions addressing anticipatory grief and related health outcomes of informal caregivers of people with dementia. Such findings may help in making recommendations for the design of intervention in future research and practice.

Method

This review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement (Page et al., 2021) and was pre-registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO).

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted in eight databases, including MEDLINE, CINAHL Complete, Embase, PsycINFO, Web of Science, Cochrane library, CNKI (Chinese Knowledge Infrastructure), and WanFang, covering all studies published from inception to January 10, 2025. The search strategies were consulted with an experienced research librarian. Search terms were developed according to the population (P), intervention (I), comparator (C), and outcome (O) (PICO) framework. Major clinical trial registries, including the ClinicalTrials.gov and the International Standard Randomized-Controlled Trial Number (ISRCTN) Registry, were searched to complement the search results. To ensure all relevant studies were included, we also conducted a citation search in Scopus and searched grey literature using the GreyMatters engine. Details of PICOs are presented in Supplementary Information 1. The key search terms and the full electronic search strategy in each database are presented in Supplementary Information 2.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) informal caregivers (aged ≥18 years) providing unpaid care to individuals living with dementia, regardless of the care recipient’s disease stage, type of dementia, living conditions (e.g., home, community, long-term care, or palliative care settings), or care recipient’s comorbid conditions; (2) informal caregivers include spouses/partners, adult children, friends, relatives (kinship or non-kinship), and neighbors, as well as those caring for multiple ill relatives and those with previous dementia caregiving experience; (3) interventional study designs, including randomized controlled trials (RCTs), pilot RCTs, cluster randomized controlled trials (cRCTs), quasi-experimental studies, and nonrandomized controlled trials; (4) used a psychosocial intervention targeting anticipatory grief; (5) included a control or comparison group, which involved no intervention, waitlist control, active control, or routine intervention/health care service; and (6) the outcomes assessed included anticipatory grief alone or together with other psychological outcomes (e.g., depression, caregiver burden, anxiety, and psychological stress). Studies were excluded if they met the following criteria: (1) informal caregivers of care recipients without a confirmed dementia diagnosis; (2) psychosocial interventions that do not provide a clear description of their important information such as content, format, duration, and theoretical framework; and (3) studies only reported outcomes related to care recipients.

Deviations from protocol

There are some deviations from the a priori protocol. The search strategy was expanded to include one additional Chinese database, two major trial registries, and an updated search period until January 2025 to capture the most current evidence. In addition, the description of the data synthesis approach and the criteria for subgroup analysis were refined to improve clarity and transparency. Full explanations of each deviation are presented in Supplementary Information 3.

Study selection and retrieval process

The relevant articles identified were imported into the Covidence (www.covidence.org) for screening (Innovation, n.d). Duplicated records were removed by Covidence. Initially, 299 articles were identified from electronic databases and trial registries search. After removing 80 duplicates, articles were screened based on their titles and abstracts. During title and abstract screening, records were assessed against the predefined eligibility criteria. Records were retained for full-text screening whenever they suggested possible relevance to grief, loss, or related psychological experiences during dementia caregiving, or when eligibility could not be determined with confidence. Records were excluded only when the title and abstract clearly indicated ineligibility, such as a clear focus on bereavement and post-death grief, leaving 58 articles for full-text assessment. Fifty-one articles were further excluded, resulting in seven articles being included in the final review. Moreover, the citation search in Scopus identified four eligible studies, resulting in a total of 11 included studies. Additional searches in ClinicalTrials.gov, the ISRCTN Registry, and GreyMatters did not lead to inclusion of more publications. The screening process was collaboratively taken by two authors, C.F. and T.N.V. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion between them. A third reviewer, W.T.C. or M.S.N.N., was available for consultation when necessary. The process of study search and selection according to the PRISMA framework is shown in Figure 1.

Data extraction

Data extraction was guided by the Cochrane Consumers and Communication Review Group’s recommendation (Ryan et al., 2016). Extracted data of included studies was organized and synthesized in a structured form from the following aspects: (1) authors, (2) publication year, (3) study location, (4) study design and setting, (5) participants, (6) interventions, (7) outcome measures, and (8) main results. Data extraction was performed independently by two authors, C.F. and T.N.V., who resolved discrepancies through discussion. The extracted data was checked for accuracy by all authors. The characteristics of all included studies are summarized in Supplementary Information 4.

Assessment of risk of bias in included studies

The risk of bias in the included RCT studies was assessed using Version 2 of the Cochrane risk of bias tool (RoB 2) (Higgins et al., 2024). This tool assesses five domains: (1) bias arising from the randomization process, (2) deviations from intended interventions, (3) missing outcome data, (4) measurement bias of the outcome, and (5) bias in the selection of reported results. The studies were categorized as low, moderate, or high risk of bias.

Quasi-experimental and nonrandomized experimental studies were assessed using ROBINS-I (Sterne et al., 2016). ROBINS-I assesses seven bias domains: (1) confounding; (2) participant selection; (3) intervention classification; (4) deviations from intended interventions; (5) missing data; (6) outcome measurement; and (7) selective reporting of results. Each study was classified as low risk, moderate risk, or high risk for each of the above categories. The visualization of risk-of-bias plots was generated using the Risk-of-bias VISualization (robvis) available online (McGuinness & Higgins, 2021).

Assessment of quality of evidence

The evidence quality was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) profiler Guideline Development Tool (Guyatt et al., 2011) via an online platform. The overall evidence quality, categorized as “High,” “Moderate,” “Low,” or “Very low.” Any disagreements among the two authors, C.F. and T.N.V., were resolved through discussion and consensus. A third reviewer, W.T.C. or M.S.N.N., was available during this process. Details are presented in Supplementary Information 5.

Data analysis

Meta-analyses were conducted using Review Manager (RevMan) 5.4. Intervention effects were categorized according to the follow-up timepoint after intervention completion as short-term (assessed immediately post-intervention or <1 month post-intervention), medium-term (assessed 1 to <6 months post-intervention), and long-term (assessed ≥ 6 months post-intervention) follow-up if sufficient data were available.

Effect sizes were estimated based on mean changes from baseline between intervention and control groups. Change scores were used to avoid potential baseline imbalances, which were calculated using both “Meanchange,” and “SDchange” (Higgins et al., 2023). The Meanchange was calculated by subtracting post-intervention means to baseline means. When baseline and post-intervention SDs were unknown, SDs of changes were calculated based on the formula: SD_change = √(SD_baseline2 + SD_post2 − 2 × Corr × SD_baseline × SD_post) (Higgins et al., 2023). The correlation coefficient (Corr) was set as 0.5 (Daly et al., 2021). A sensitivity analysis was performed with varying correlational parameters (0.2, 0.5, 0.8) to assess the differences in meta-analytic results (see Supplementary Information 6).

Data were pooled for meta-analysis when two or more studies reported on the same outcome. For continuous data measured on the same scale, mean differences (MDs) were used. When studies used different measurement tools for the same outcome, standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were applied (Higgins et al., 2023). Effect sizes of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 were interpreted as small, medium, and large, respectively (Cohen, 1992). A random-effects model was used due to anticipated heterogeneity across studies. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. I2 values were interpreted as low (I2 < 40%), moderate (40% ≤ I2 ≤ 75%), or high (I2 > 75%) (Higgins & Thompson, 2002). When studies did not report sufficient data, authors were contacted to request missing information. Publication bias was not assessed because of the limited number of studies included for each outcome (i.e., less than 10 studies) (Sterne et al., 2011). Studies not suitable for pooling due to high heterogeneity or incomplete data were narratively summarized. Statistical significance was set as p < .05.

Sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the results by excluding influential studies. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed by removing one study at a time to examine the robustness of the pooled effect estimate. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on delivery modality and framework when data from at least two studies were available for each subgroup.

Results

Study characteristics

Of 11 included studies, four studies were 2-arm randomized controlled trials (RCTs) (Jahani et al., 2022; Meichsner & Wilz, 2018; Meichsner et al., 2019; Risch et al., 2024), three studies were pilot RCTs (Brooks et al., 2022, 2024; Han et al., 2023), and four studies were quasi-randomized or nonrandomized experimental studies (Bravo-Benítez et al., 2021; MacCourt et al., 2017; Paun et al., 2015; Sanders & Sharp, 2004). Four studies compared an intervention with no intervention (Jahani et al., 2022; MacCourt et al., 2017; Meichsner & Wilz, 2018; Risch et al., 2024), the remaining studies compared the intervention group with either a waitlist group or usual service such as informative talks or telephone check-ins. The included studies were carried out in Germany (n = 3), the United States (n = 3), Australia (n = 2), Canada (n = 1), Iran (n = 1), and Spain (n = 1). The characteristics and main results of all included studies are summarized in Supplementary Information 4.

The 11 included studies involved 870 informal caregivers for people living with dementia. They were aged 18 to 89 years, with a mean age of 62.85 years. The majority of them were female (78.85%, n = 686), with 418 (48%) spouses and 324 (37%) adult children. One study did not report the informal caregivers’ relationships with care recipients (Jahani et al., 2022). The majority of the care recipients had Alzheimer’s disease and were at a moderate to severe stage of dementia.

Study interventions

One of the studies evaluated a cognitive behavioral therapy intervention with additional grief-specific components (Meichsner & Wilz, 2018), one study evaluated a technology-mediated cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) intervention that incorporated elements from Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) (Meichsner et al., 2019), two studies assessed ACT-based interventions (Han et al., 2023; Risch et al., 2024), one evaluated a compassion-based program (Jahani et al., 2022). The other interventions were informed by frameworks such as Stress Process Model of Caregiving (n = 3), Caregiver Grief Model (n = 1), Shear’s complicated grief program (n = 1), and Theory of Ambiguous loss (n = 1). The components of these interventions were: (1) providing psychoeducation about dementia, stage-specific symptoms, and the link between disease progression and grief; (2) identifying difficult caregiving situations or thoughts that heighten grief and teaching strategies to disengage from unhelpful cognitions; (3) facilitating validation and acknowledgment of grief and loss; (4) enhancing stress-management skills, including mindfulness, compassion, problem-solving, and emotional regulation; and (5) promoting behavioral activation by encouraging participation in meaningful or rewarding activities.

Seven studies delivered interventions using technology-mediated formats, such as telephone, WhatsApp, Zoom, or a secured website, with three studies delivered face-to-face intervention (Bravo-Benítez et al., 2021; Paun et al., 2015; Sanders & Sharp, 2004). One study delivered intervention through a combination of face-to-face and technology-mediated modality (MacCourt et al., 2017). Interventions were delivered individually in seven studies in groups in three studies (Bravo-Benítez et al., 2021; Paun et al., 2015; Sanders & Sharp, 2004), and one study employed both individual-based and group-based formats (MacCourt et al., 2017). All interventions were therapist-guided, including clinical psychologists (n = 4), mental health counselors (n = 2), nurses (n = 2), ACT experts (n = 1), dementia care experts (n = 1), and social workers (n = 1). The duration of intervention ranged from five to 24 weeks. All studies conducted baseline and immediate post-intervention assessments. Six studies conducted follow-up assessment, with the follow-up period ranging from one to six months. Attrition rate ranged from 10% to 21%, with four studies that did not report the overall attrition rate (Bravo-Benítez et al., 2021; Jahani et al., 2022; MacCourt et al., 2017; Sanders & Sharp, 2004).

Outcome measurements

The primary outcome, anticipatory grief, was measured in all 11 studies, using the Caregiver Grief Scale (n = 6) (Bravo-Benítez et al., 2021; Brooks et al., 2022, 2024; Meichsner & Wilz, 2018; Meichsner et al., 2019; Risch et al., 2024), and three versions of the Marwit–Meuser Caregiver Grief Inventory, including the full 50-item, 18-item, and 6-item versions (n = 5) (Han et al., 2023; Jahani et al., 2022; MacCourt et al., 2017; Paun et al., 2015; Sanders & Sharp, 2004).

Additionally, anxiety was measured in three studies using Geriatric Anxiety Inventory (n = 1) (Brooks et al., 2024), 21-item Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (n = 1) (Han et al., 2023), and Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (n = 1) (Risch et al., 2024). Caregiver burden was measured in four studies using a numeric analogue scale (n = 2) (Meichsner et al., 2019; Risch et al., 2024), Zarit Burden Interview (n = 1) (Han et al., 2023), and Caregiver Burden Interview (n = 1) (Bravo-Benítez et al., 2021). Psychological stress was evaluated in three studies using Perceived Stress Scale (n = 2) (Brooks et al., 2022, 2024), and 21-item Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (n = 1) (Han et al., 2023). Depressive symptoms were assessed in five studies using Centre for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale CES-D (n = 4) (Brooks et al., 2022, 2024; Meichsner et al., 2019; Paun et al., 2015) and 21-item Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (n = 1) (Han et al., 2023).

Risk of bias assessment

The results of methodological assessment are presented in Figure 2. All seven RCTs were rated as having low to moderate risk of bias overall, with six studies showing moderate risk at least one domain, primarily related to allocation sequence during randomization, deviations from intended intervention and outcome measurement (Brooks et al., 2022, 2024; Jahani et al., 2022; Meichsner & Wilz, 2018; Meichsner et al., 2019; Risch et al., 2024). Although all RCTs used random allocation methods (simple, block, or stratified), only two studies reported how allocation was concealed (Han et al., 2023; Jahani et al., 2022). One study was rated as “moderate” for deviations from the intended interventions, because the analysis did not report using intention-to-treat (ITT) principles (Jahani et al., 2022). For bias due to missing outcome data, most RCTs were judged at low risk. However, Jahani et al. (2022) were rated as “moderate” because inadequate reporting on attrition and missing outcome data limited assessment of missing data bias. Five RCTs were assessed as having “moderate” risk of bias in measurement of outcomes due to the use of self-reported instruments (Brooks et al., 2022, 2024; Jahani et al., 2022; Meichsner & Wilz, 2018; Meichsner et al., 2019).

Risk-of-bias assessment of included studies. (A) Randomized controlled trial (RCTs, n = 7). (B) Quasi and non-randomized controlled studies (n = 4).

Four quasi-experimental and nonrandomized, pretest-post-test studies were assessed using ROBIN-I. One study attempted to adjust for potential confounding and was therefore rated as “moderate” risk in the confounding bias domain (Paun et al., 2015), whereas the other three did not adjust for confounding and were deemed as “high risk” (Bravo-Benítez et al., 2021; MacCourt et al., 2017; Sanders & Sharp, 2004). Three studies were determined to have “high risk” due to inadequate reporting on missing data or its handling (Bravo-Benítez et al., 2021; MacCourt et al., 2017; Sanders & Sharp, 2004). Moreover, these studies did not report care-recipient characteristics, such as disease stage or severity, further limiting the assessment of baseline comparability and potential confounding (Bravo-Benítez et al., 2021; MacCourt et al., 2017; Sanders & Sharp, 2004). The outcome measurement relied on self-reported data, resulting in all studies being rated as “moderate” risk in this domain.

Effectiveness of interventions

Short-term effectiveness of intervention on anticipatory grief

All seven RCTs examined the short-term effects of psychosocial interventions (<1-month post-intervention) on anticipatory grief (Brooks et al., 2022, 2024; Han et al., 2023; Jahani et al., 2022; Meichsner & Wilz, 2018; Meichsner et al., 2019; Risch et al., 2024). However, as one study (Brooks et al., 2024) did not provide the outcome data upon request, only six RCTs were included in the meta-analysis. A random-effect meta-analysis showed that psychosocial intervention did not improve anticipatory grief compared with control groups (e.g., no intervention or waitlist groups; SMD = −0.57, 95% CI: [−1.14, 0.01], p = .05, 6 RCTs, 433 participants, Figure 3A), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 85%). Sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding one study at a time, indicating that Jahani et al. (2022) contributed most to the high heterogeneity (z-value = 6.60, p < .00001). After removing this study, the pooled results showed no statistically significant difference between the groups, and heterogeneity was eliminated (SMD = −0.20, 95% CI: [−0.40, 0.01], p = .06, 5 RCTs, 363 participants, I2 = 0%, Figure 3B).

(B) A sensitivity analysis was conducted excluding Jahani et al. (2022), showing the short-term effects on anticipatory grief in the remaining RCTs. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals are presented for each study and overall. Negative SMD values indicate improvements in anticipatory grief in the intervention group compared to the control group. IV = Inverse Variance; SMD = Standardized Mean Difference.

A subgroup analysis was conducted to examine intervention effectiveness by delivery modality and framework. Interventions delivered via online platforms showed a larger effect size (SMD = −0.99) compared to those delivered by telephone (SMD = −0.17; Figure 4A). Neither CBT-based or ACT-based intervention were effective in improving anticipatory grief (CBT: SMD = −0.16, 95% CI: [−0.41, 0.09], p = .22; 2 RCTs, 242 participants, I2 = 0%, Figure 4B; ACT: SMD = −0.35, 95% CI: [−0.92, 0.21], p = .22; 2 RCTs, 100 participants, I2 = 32%; Figure 4B). Subgroup analysis could not be performed for compassion-based and stress-process model informed intervention as only one study was available for each type (Brooks et al., 2022; Jahani et al., 2022).

Medium-term effectiveness of the intervention on anticipatory grief

Three RCTs assessed the medium-term effects (1 to <6 months post-intervention) of psychosocial interventions on anticipatory grief compared with controls (e.g., “usual care” or waitlist) (Han et al., 2023; Jahani et al., 2022; Meichsner et al., 2019). The pooled results indicated no significant difference in improving anticipatory grief between intervention and control groups (SMD = −0.76, 95% CI: [−1.99, 0.46], p = .22, 3 RCTs, 116 participants, Figure 5A), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 88%). Sensitivity analysis indicated that the study by Jahani et al. (2022) contributed significantly to the heterogeneity. However, the pooled results remained statistically nonsignificant (SMD = −0.16, 95% CI: [−0.74, 0.42], p = .60, 2 RCTs, 46 participants, I2 = 0%, Figure 5B) after removing Jahani et al.’s (2022) study.

(B) Sensitivity analysis excluding Jahani et al. (2022), showing the medium-term effects on anticipatory grief in the remaining RCTs. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals are presented for each study and overall. IV = Inverse Variance.

Long-term effectiveness of the intervention on anticipatory grief

Two RCTs evaluated the long-term effects of psychosocial interventions (≥6 months post-intervention) on anticipatory grief (Meichsner & Wilz, 2018; Risch et al., 2024). Meta-analysis showed that interventions significantly improve the level of anticipatory grief compared to the control group, with low heterogeneity (MD= −0.41, 95% CI: [−0.62, -0.20], p = .0001, 2 RCTs, 267 participants, I2 = 0%, Figure 6).

Effectiveness of interventions on other outcomes

Anxiety

A meta-analysis was conducted on two RCTs assessing anxiety in 100 participants (Han et al., 2023; Risch et al., 2024) within one-month post-intervention. The pooled results demonstrated that interventions did not statistically reduce anxiety compared to control groups (SMD = −0.24, 95% CI: [−0.63, 0.15], p = .23, I2 = 0%, Supplementary Information 7, Figure 6).

Caregiver burden

The pooled results from three RCTs (130 participants) within one month post-intervention (Han et al., 2023; Meichsner et al., 2019; Risch et al., 2024) found no significant effect of psychosocial interventions on caregiver burden compared to controls (SMD = 0.13, 95% CI: [−0.57, 0.83], p = .72, I2 = 69%; Supplementary Information 7, Figure 7A). Similarly, no significant difference was observed in reducing caregiver burden at medium-term (Han et al., 2023; Meichsner et al., 2019) (SMD = 0.35, 95% CI: [−0.24, 0.93], p = .24, I2 = 0%; Supplementary Information 7, Figure 7B).

Stress

A meta-analysis of two pilot RCTs (40 participants) within one-month post-intervention (Brooks et al., 2022; Han et al., 2023) found no significant effect of interventions on psychological stress (SMD = 0.15, 95% CI: [−0.47, 0.78], p = .63, I2 = 0%, Supplementary Information 7, Figure 8).

Depressive symptoms

The pooled results from four RCTs (151 participants) within one month post-intervention (Brooks et al., 2022; Han et al., 2023; Meichsner et al., 2019; Risch et al., 2024) showed that psychosocial interventions improved depressive symptoms compared to controls (SMD = −0.35, 95% CI: [−0.67, −0.03], p = .03, I2 = 0%; Supplementary Information 7, Figure 9A). However, no significant difference was observed in reducing depressive symptoms at 1- to 6-month follow-up (SMD = 0.28, 95% CI: [−0.36, 0.92], p = .39, I2 = 15%; Supplementary Information 7, Figure 9B).

Narrative synthesis

A meta-analysis was conducted only when two or more studies reported the same outcome and provided sufficiently comparable data for pooling. When this was not possible, findings were synthesized narratively. Individual study data were extracted, and effect sizes were calculated where possible to support interpretation (Bravo-Benítez et al., 2021; Brooks et al., 2024; MacCourt et al., 2017; Paun et al., 2015; Sanders & Sharp, 2004) (Table 1).

Three studies (Bravo-Benítez et al., 2021; Paun et al., 2015; Sanders & Sharp, 2004) implemented group-based programs combining psychoeducation about dementia and caregiving-related grief, facilitated discussion or emotional expression of loss, coping skills training to manage grief, sadness, and guilt, and social support through group interaction or shared experiences. Two of these studies evaluated structured interventions lasting 10 weeks (Bravo-Benítez et al., 2021) or 12 weeks (Paun et al., 2015), whereas Sanders and Sharp (2004) delivered a five-week program. Bravo-Benítez et al. (2021) examined informal caregivers of people living with dementia of mild-to-moderate severity and reported significant reductions in the Emotional Pain (SMD = −0.69, 95% CI: [−1.25, −0.12], p = .02) and Absolute Loss (SMD = −0.61, 95% CI: [−1.17, −0.06], p = .03) subdomains of anticipatory grief, while no significant changes were observed in the remaining subdomains. Paun et al. (2015), which included informal caregivers of individuals with late-stage dementia residing in long-term care, found no improvement in the overall anticipatory grief scores (SMD = −0.06, 95% CI: [−0.50, 0.37], p = .78). However, a significant improvement was noted in the Heartfelt Sadness and Longing subdomain (SMD = −0.50, 95% CI: [−0.94, −0.05], p = .03) (Paun et al., 2015). Diverging from these, Sanders and Sharp (2004), focusing on informal caregivers of people with mid-to-late-stage Alzheimer’s disease, reported an increase in anticipatory grief in the intervention group. Qualitative data from this study suggested that informal caregivers might have been grieving but interpreted it as stress, partly because grieving when the care recipient is still alive had not been recognized or validated as a normal response; therefore, acknowledging these feelings and developing coping strategies were important for informal caregivers.

Two other studies (Brooks et al., 2024; MacCourt et al., 2017) evaluated interventions delivered through different modalities. MacCourt et al. (2017) reported improvements in anticipatory grief, although comparisons between five delivery modalities (e.g., individual face-to-face, individual telephone, face-to-face group, telephone group, and online group) were not provided. Brooks et al. (2024) piloted a 12-week individualized videoconferencing counseling program for informal caregivers of individuals transitioning to residential care and found no significant improvement in anticipatory grief. Nevertheless, qualitative findings suggested that informal caregivers experienced the program as beneficial as it offered a neutral space to acknowledge grief and loss, receive emotional validation, and discuss coping strategies.

Across these studies, psychosocial interventions for informal caregivers of people living with dementia showed mixed short-term effects on anticipatory grief, with no consistent improvement in overall grief. However, some benefits were observed in specific subdomains, such as Heartfelt Sadness and Longing (Paun et al., 2015) and Absolute Loss (Bravo-Benítez et al., 2021). This pattern was broadly consistent with the meta-analysis, which found no significant short-term effect on overall anticipatory grief. Qualitative findings from the narrative synthesis suggested that these interventions commonly incorporated psychoeducation about dementia and grief, facilitated expression of loss, and supported coping skills development. They also appeared to provide informal caregivers with opportunities to recognize, express, and validate grief-related experiences that had previously been difficult to identify or discuss.

Certainty of evidence for included studies in meta-analysis

Publication bias was not assessed due to the small number of RCTs contributed to each analysis, which prevented the use of reliable funnel plots or statistical tests. However, publication bias could not be ruled out given the emerging nature of the field and the possibility that studies with null findings may be underrepresented in the published literature. Therefore, the certainty of evidence was downgraded by one level for suspected publication bias. The overall GRADEpro assessment of the outcomes indicated that the evidence ranged from “Low” to “Very Low” (Supplementary Information 5). Therefore, the meta-analysis results should be interpreted with caution, and more rigorous, high-quality research is needed to strengthen the conclusions.

Discussion

This was the first systematic review and meta-analysis to comprehensively evaluate various approaches to psychosocial interventions for improving anticipatory grief of informal caregivers for people living with dementia. Overall, the evidence suggests limited effectiveness of current psychosocial interventions in addressing anticipatory grief of these informal caregivers. The quality of the evidence ranged from a low to very low level of certainty because of substantial heterogeneity in the approaches to intervention and outcome measures across studies. Three out of seven identified RCTs were pilot studies; and two were small-scale trials (<90 participants in total). More than half of the included studies were published between 2019 and 2024, reflecting growing professional and research interest in anticipatory grief among dementia caregivers.

Effectiveness of interventions

This review first examined whether psychosocial interventions could improve anticipatory grief and secondary outcomes of informal caregivers of people living with dementia. The meta-analysis found no significant effects at immediate post-intervention. The narrative synthesis showed a similar pattern to the meta-analysis, with no clear effect at immediate post-intervention on overall anticipatory grief. This finding contrasts with earlier findings from Sánchez-Alcón et al. (2023), who assessed 2 RCTs and 2 nonrandomized interventional trials in meta-analysis and reported significant improvements in anticipatory grief immediately post-intervention (SMD = −0.25). One explanation might be that their inclusion of both RCTs and nonrandomized trials have led to an overestimation of intervention effectiveness. Furthermore, our meta‑analysis found no significant effects at medium-term (1-to-6-month post-intervention). A significant effect at longer-term follow-up (≥6 months post-intervention) was observed, but this finding was based on only two RCTs evaluating individually delivered, technology-mediated interventions informed by cognitive-behavioral frameworks in German-speaking populations. Therefore, this result should not be generalized to the broader range of psychosocial interventions identified in this review. Given the limited literature comparing the effectiveness of anticipatory grief interventions at medium-term or long-term follow-ups, our results were compared with those from meta-analyses of RCTs in bereaved adults (Johannsen et al., 2019). A similar pattern was also found in Johannsen et al. (2019), who also found no immediate post-intervention effects for older bereaved adults but observed significant effects at follow-up. One possible interpretation is that older individuals may experience slower or more subtle changes in their grief processing over time compared to younger individuals (Johannsen et al., 2019). Another explanation can be that other factors than the interventions also effect the grief course and the coping abilities over time. However, interventions with shorter follow-up periods may not be able to fully capture the interventions’ effectiveness.

The intervention approaches included in this review varied considerably, and thus an optimal intervention approach could not be clearly identified. However, some intervention components emerged and provided some insights. Psychoeducation about dementia was frequently included, which aligns with a previous cross-sectional study suggesting that greater dementia knowledge is associated with lower levels of anticipatory grief (Moore et al., 2020). One included study used compassion-focused intervention demonstrated stronger short-term effects compared to other intervention approaches (Jahani et al., 2022). One possible explanation for the outlying effect reported by Jahani et al. (2022) may relate to the intervention’s between-session support. Jahani et al. (2022) implemented a 5-week intervention with ongoing between-session contact, allowing participants to share comments or experiences through a therapist-provided contact number alongside weekly Cyberspace- and WhatsApp-delivered content. This continuous support may have enhanced short-term participants’ engagement and intervention effectiveness. In addition, given that feelings of shame, guilt, and self-criticism are common among informal caregivers for people living with dementia (Murfield et al., 2022), compassion-based interventions that encourage caregivers to cultivate self-compassion may help mitigate these negative emotions (Gilbert, 2010). However, as the Jahani et al. (2022) study is the only study using compassion-based intervention, reliable conclusions about its effectiveness in improving anticipatory grief cannot yet be drawn. Furthermore, some studies included in this review adopted acceptance of loss as one of the intervention strategies. This finding is consistent with previous theoretical and empirical evidence suggesting that acceptance of loss might be a core therapeutic process addressing anticipatory grief (Blandin & Pepin, 2017; Meichsner et al., 2019; Moore et al., 2023). Acceptance may support informal caregivers by acknowledging their emotional experiences and helping them process ongoing and inevitable losses (Meichsner et al., 2019; Moore et al., 2023). Across both the RCTs included in the meta-analysis and the narrative synthesis, similar intervention components were identified, suggesting that they may be relevant and acceptable for informal caregivers. However, grief expression is also shaped by cultural norms (Eisenbruch, 1984), and most included studies in the current review were conducted in Western countries. In some cultures, emotional restraint or private grieving is more valued than open expression (Zhou et al., 2023), which may influence how acceptance-based strategies could be utilized in intervention. Awareness of these cultural nuances is therefore essential when considering how anticipatory grief is conceptualized across different settings.

The studies included in the current review used a range of frameworks, with no single framework consistently proving more effective in guiding interventions for anticipatory grief. The lack of consensus in defining anticipatory grief may have contributed to the inconsistent effectiveness of interventions (Coelho & Barbosa, 2017). Several theoretical models of anticipatory grief in dementia care exist (Blandin & Pepin, 2017; Noyes et al., 2010). However, these models are neither empirically derived nor based on widely used grief theories such as the Dual Process Model (Stroebe & Schut, 1999). Nielsen et al. (2016) suggest that an application of the dual process model may be appropriate to understand anticipatory grief, but its applicability requires further investigation because most grief theories were developed for bereavement-related grief. The current study calls for future research to develop theoretical frameworks to deepen the understanding of anticipatory grief, which can facilitate better intervention programs.

In this review, all included RCTs employed individual, technology-mediated formats, while the included non-RCTs were delivered in group, face-to-face format. We were not able to determine which intervention format was more effective. However, the observed variation in delivery approaches reflects broader trends in caregiver intervention design. Technology-mediated intervention may be suited to informal caregivers of people living with dementia, who often face significant time constraints due to their caregiving responsibilities. Such formats offer flexibility in time and location, allowing caregivers to engage in interventions without disrupting their daily routines (Wasilewski et al., 2017). Some research indicating that individual-based intervention formats tend to produce larger effects than group-based formats in grief interventions among bereaved adults (Johannsen et al., 2019), those experiencing perinatal grief (Liu & Shorey, 2021), and bereaved informal caregivers (Finucane et al., 2025). However, while technology-mediated interventions are accessible, affordable, and can be used at one’s own place (Zagorscak et al., 2018), face-to-face formats may offer more direct interpersonal interactions and guidance. Future interventions might consider hybrid approaches that combine the accessibility of technology with the relational depth of face-to-face interaction to better meet the diverse needs and preferences of informal caregivers for people living with dementia.

In this meta-analysis, psychosocial interventions were effective in reducing depressive symptoms at immediate post-intervention, but they did not demonstrate statistically significant effects on caregiver burden, anxiety symptoms, and stress at immediate post-intervention. This is consistent with a meta-review of reviews, where different interventions only are shown evident in reducing depressive symptoms (Cheng & Zhang, 2020). Research suggests that depressive symptoms are more modifiable (Cheng & Zhang, 2020; Hopkinson et al., 2019), whereas caregiver burden, anxiety, and stress are more strongly influenced by ongoing caregiving demands and may be less responsive to change.

The effectiveness of anticipatory grief interventions may be influenced by informal caregiver characteristics, such as gender and type of informal caregivers (spousal vs. adult–child). In this review, female informal caregivers accounted for the majority of participants, while male caregivers represented only 18% of the total sample. Nearly half of all participants were spouses, and approximately one-third were adult children. Walter and Pinquart (2020) reviewed all types of intervention designed for informal caregivers for people with dementia and found interventions involving samples with higher proportions of female caregivers or spousal caregivers tended to show reduced effectiveness. Research indicates that women in caregiving roles are more likely to have greater time commitments and to prioritize the needs of the care recipient over their own (Sharma et al., 2016). This prioritization of caregiving responsibilities may create barriers to engaging in intervention programs, as female caregivers may perceive attending to their own emotional needs as secondary to their caregiving duties, potentially limiting the effectiveness of psychosocial intervention addressing anticipatory grief. Moreover, adult–child and spousal caregivers may experience anticipatory grief differently, which further complicates intervention responsiveness. Adult–child caregivers may face role reversal challenges and heightened feelings of grief and guilt due to loss of their own freedom, while spousal caregivers’ grief experiences are related to the loss of companionship and changes in partner relationships (Fenton et al., 2022). However, these aspects of informal caregivers’ characteristics were not reflected in the included studies.

Furthermore, anticipatory grief may intensify at certain transitional points, such as following the diagnosis of care recipients’ dementia or prior to nursing home placement (Andersson et al., 2019; Gibson et al., 2019). However, in this review, no study was conducted during the early stages of dementia caregiving, and two studies focused on transitions into institutional care. Timely screening of anticipatory grief and intervention may provide more benefits and improve long-term outcomes. A better understanding of how caregivers’ and care-recipients’ characteristics influence intervention outcomes and how interventions can be tailored to address these differences is essential for improving intervention effectiveness and should be a focus of future research.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. Construct validity represented a notable limitation in the current evidence base. Anticipatory grief was operationalized inconsistently across the included studies. In line with a recent measurement review in dementia caregivers (Dehpour & Koffman, 2023), the Marwit–Meuser Caregiver Grief Inventory (MM-CGI) and Caregiver Grief Scale were the most frequently employed instruments to assess anticipatory grief, though they differ conceptually. For example, the MM-CGI, including the MM-CGI-Short Form, comprises three dimensions, including Personal Sacrifice Burden, Heartfelt Sadness and Longing, and Worry and Felt Isolation. Personal Sacrifice Burden and Worry and Felt Isolation overlap with constructs such as caregiver burden and depression (Liew & Yap, 2020). Accordingly, total scores of MM-CGI may reflect a broader set of grief-related emotions and difficulties. In contrast, the Caregiver Grief Scale was developed to minimize overlap with other caregiving constructs while capturing grief-related dimensions such as relational loss, absolute loss, and acceptance of loss (Meichsner et al., 2016), offering a different conceptualization of anticipatory grief. Overall, these differences highlight variation in how anticipatory grief has been defined and measured across the literature. Pooled effect sizes in this review might reflect intervention effects on overlapping but nonequivalent grief-related constructs rather than a single, uniform outcome. Further qualitative research is needed to clarify how informal caregivers conceptualize anticipatory grief and to inform more precise measurement of the construct. This construct may also vary according to factors such as dementia stage and informal caregiver characteristics (e.g., the caregiver–care recipient relationship), yet none of the included studies examined whether intervention effects differed across these factors. This calls for future research using longitudinal designs to assess how such factors influenced intervention effectiveness over the caregiving trajectory.

The current review also had methodological limitations. First, the observed long-term effects should be interpreted cautiously, as they were based on two similar RCTs from Germany evaluating interventions informed by a cognitive-behavioral framework. Accordingly, these long-term findings may have limited generalizability and should be regarded as preliminary evidence. Second, three of the included RCTs in this review were pilot RCTs (Brooks et al., 2022, 2024; Han et al., 2023), and two RCTs were relatively small-scale RCT (<90 participants in total) (Meichsner et al., 2019; Risch et al., 2024). These small sample sizes may reduce statistical power and limit the validity and reliability of the findings. Also, incomplete attrition reporting across four studies limited assessment of missing data bias. Sensitivity analyses also showed that some pooled estimates were affected by the assumed correlation coefficient used to derive change-score standard deviations (Supplementary Information 6). Both individual study results and pooled estimates should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Moreover, this review also included quasi-experimental and nonrandomized studies, which may lead to selection bias and reporting bias. Moderate risks of bias were identified in the majority of included RCTs, particularly due to inadequate allocation concealment and the lack of blinding during outcome assessments. Such issues may introduce systematic errors and potentially weaken the internal validity of the studies and affect their findings. In addition, the narrative synthesis itself may have been limited by the variability in study methodologies and outcomes, which also limit the overall conclusions drawn from this review. Random-effects models were used to account for heterogeneity, but differences in intervention types and outcome measures may affect comparability and measurement validity. Relevant studies may have been missed because our search relied mainly on outcomes reported in titles or abstracts. Furthermore, our review contains some deviations from the original preregistration. To provide more detailed descriptions and maintain consistency, we updated the preregistration record. The differences between the preregistration and the manuscript, along with explanations for each change, are outlined in Supplementary Information 3. Finally, most studies were conducted in Western countries, which may limit the generalizability of findings to culturally and socioeconomically diverse populations.

Implications for research and practice

This review highlights that psychosocial interventions may have long-term benefits in addressing anticipatory grief among informal caregivers of people living with dementia. However, these results should be interpreted with caution. Future research should focus on well-designed trials with adequate follow-up periods to clarify the sustained impact of interventions and account for potential confounding factors that could affect intervention effectiveness. Moreover, psychoeducation and acceptance strategies emerged as commonly used components in the interventions assessed in this review. Despite the high variability in intervention format, content, and study design, these components show promise in addressing anticipatory grief.

Further studies are needed to identify caregivers at higher risk of experiencing intensified anticipatory grief, particularly during critical caregiving transitions, such as a dementia diagnosis or institutionalization. Targeting these caregivers with early psychosocial interventions could provide significant benefits. Future research should continue to explore and refine these approaches to determine whether they could be key to improving outcomes. Future studies could include repeated assessments during and after the intervention to better understand when changes begin, how they develop over time, and whether observed effects are related to intervention exposure or the timing of follow-up. Finally, developing a clearer conceptual framework for anticipatory grief is essential. This would support more consistent intervention design and outcome measurement and help identify which intervention components are most effective in improving anticipatory grief.

Given the impact of anticipatory grief on informal caregivers, especially in the context of dementia care, supporting informal caregivers for people living with dementia is imperative. This review suggests that both individually delivered, technology-mediated interventions and face-to-face or group-based formats may both be potentially feasible and effective. Future research should remain flexible and consider the needs of informal caregivers when designing or selecting intervention formats. Considering the demanding responsibilities that informal caregivers bear, tailoring interventions to fit caregivers’ routines and address grief-specific challenges may enhance their relevance and impact in dementia caregiving.

Conclusion

The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis revealed limited evidence for the effectiveness of psychosocial interventions in the short-term and medium-term. A possible benefit was identified in the long term (e.g., ≥6 months post-intervention follow-up), but this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as it was based on only two RCTs of individually delivered, technology-mediated interventions informed by cognitive-behavioral frameworks. Psychoeducation on dementia and acceptance of loss were adopted in different intervention approaches as potential core strategies. No framework was identified as more effective, possibly due to a lack of consensus in understanding anticipatory grief, which may also explain the limited effectiveness in immediate and medium-term intervention effects. Intervention effectiveness might also be attributed to the small sample sizes in the included studies. Future research should focus on developing the concept of anticipatory grief to inform further intervention design and enhance effectiveness. Incorporating qualitative methods may also help capture intervention effects that are not fully reflected in quantitative outcomes.
