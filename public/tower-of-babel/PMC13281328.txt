The way we do things matters: the deep integration of lived experience in the a/part of the crowd project to respond to loneliness in young adults
Authors: Candice Peart, Matthew Lewis, Jennifer Bibb, Grace Read, Kisanet Tesfu, Kushagra Rathore, Li Xuan Tan, Toby Wong, Alison Turner, Farhan Andalib, Gianna Ziero, Jemma Rule, Lidan Cao, Lyam Merritt, Nargis Rezaie, Tyesha Shelton, Cath Kaylor-Hughes, Amy Coe, Phillip Orcher Muruwori/Gumbaynggirr, Victoria J. Palmer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLoneliness in youth (15–24 years old) is increasing. The impacts over the life course are more fully recognised and require co-designed responses. A participatory designed project, a/part of the crowd, has led to a web-delivered, preventive response to loneliness with young adults 18–25 years old. As a part of this, an integrated Lived Experience Advisory Group with co-researcher roles was established to centre youth perspectives and to grow research capabilities.MethodThe co-designed response was developed from 128 short stories, poetry, artworks (hand-drawn, digital and collage), and music submitted by 18 to 25-year-olds in Australia. Ethics approval was granted for the larger project. A 12-member Lived Experience Advisory Group provided advice on design, recruiting, and promoting. They shared impressions on data and supported elements of the co-designed web space and steps for further translation. Five members of the LEAG were also appointed as co-researchers. In this paper, we outline the Lived Experience Advisory Group establishment and role, and share co-reflections on the contributions made and how to embed lived experience in the research process and practice.ResultsFive co-researchers worked alongside the interdisciplinary research team. Reflections identified the importance of a project coordinator to maintain communication and connection, and that collaborative research meetings supported working alongside as a team sharing interpretations and discussing research. The Lived Experience Advisory Group role has included advising on design elements for the co-designed response, and user testing, which was recently made publicly available.ConclusionThe advisory group and co-research roles created a mechanism to increase participation in decision processes and share roles in research activities. Co-researchers have been trained to analyse content, and frequent reflective meetings have fostered understanding of current lived realities for 18- to 25-year-olds, and grown new collaborations and activities. This has developed the capabilities of co-researchers and the wider research team in youth-centred approaches to loneliness research. Members of the LEAG and co-researchers have now adopted additional roles in other activities supporting an element of implementation into mental health research translation. This led to an example of an integrated lived-experience approach to adopt within research studies in the future.

Loneliness is impacting youth (aged between 15 and 24 years old) at increasing rates and intensity over time [1, 2]. The experience of loneliness is said to have been accelerated by societal shifts arising through the COVID-19 pandemic and precarious global political, social, environmental and economic circumstances which play out locally [1]. In a recent Australian Suicide Prevention Report Card (2024) one in four people aged 18–25 years in Australia reported experiencing suicidal distress [3]. Loneliness and social isolation were frequently reported as one of the stressors alongside the “cost of living” and “family and relationship breakdowns” [3]. Importantly, loneliness and social isolation are understood to refer to different experiences and impacts where one speaks to the internal emotional experience of a perceived or actual deficit in the quantity or quality of social interactions (loneliness) and the other to an individual’s objective absence of social contact (social isolation) [4]. While these concepts are often discussed together, they remain distinctive in how people describe and experience them.

The young adulthood ages of 18–25 cover a life period often marked by profound changes in circumstances which this life period has also been referred to as emerging adulthood (particularly in middle to high-income countries) [5]. Such changes include leaving home, beginning university or other education, starting a trade, or going into work for the first time, big relationship shifts and sometimes parenthood [6, 7]. The impact of these changes can vary from person to person, and responsibilities as a young person and young adult may be quite different when social and cultural factors are factored in. For some, life changes are destabilising and can impact feelings of belonging, connectedness and overall health and wellbeing [8]. These life shifts can remove people from family, friends and established networks and can foster feelings of being isolated [9]. For people living with ongoing mental ill-health, loneliness can increase persistent and severe mental health outcomes [10]. For First Nations young adult (a term used to respectfully refer to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people of Australia) this is confounded further by the ongoing impacts of colonisation, trauma, racism and harms caused by structural inequities and inadequately implemented wellbeing policies [11].

The rise in reported loneliness is of increased concern at all life stages, with recent analyses showing that men aged 40–50 years are more likely to report what has been termed “severe loneliness” [12]. Government and non-government reports and prevalence studies have led to a growing focus on loneliness in mental health, suicide prevention and societal wellbeing. Since loneliness is at its highest levels for 18 to 25 year olds, there is also a need to understand the contextual factors that may contribute such as life transitions, which may play an additional role in the impacts [13]. On an international level, the World Health Organisation has also called for an increased focus on social connections to counter both isolation and loneliness [14]. Meaningful responses that are driven by the people most impacted by loneliness remains an ongoing challenge though in our hyper-individualised, fragmented and disconnected world. Furthermore, it is critical that the responses to loneliness reflect cultural differences, adopt social approaches to prevention over the life course, and centre young adult and their experiences of loneliness today [15]. For example, cultural conceptions of what young adulthood and emerging adulthood mean need to be further understood, and dominant Western frameworks need to be evaluated to ensure that the diversity and equity issues that are impacting are addressed through research design, application and translation into delivery settings. Thus, embedded and integrated lived-experience research approaches are essential. While guidelines exist around this and participatory toolkits [16] describe good principles of involvement, there are gaps in how responses to loneliness are driven by and for young adults. There is a need for greater detail on the bidirectional relationship between meaningful participation in co-designing responses, and in involvement in advisory groups and roles. This includes the need to develop a better picture on how this may lead to positive experiences of wellbeing, trust and self-confidence and reduced stigma, discrimination and wider inclusion of groups that may have historically been marginalised or actively excluded [17].

The need for lived experience involvement is recognised as critical for addressing loneliness, and so too is the notion that engaging youth in research has wider impacts [16]. A systematic review found common research processes in youth involvement in mental health research has included: youth shaping the data collection instrument/s, being actively involved in data collection, involvement in the findings from the study by youth engagement in the analysis, and enhanced knowledge dissemination [18]. Despite the recognition of these findings, surprisingly few loneliness projects have invested in embedded and integrated approaches, and this is not yet standard research practice [19]. Fewer studies seem to account for the need for diversity and the equity considerations that may arise through participation and engagement. Recent guidelines point to absences in information and research on the effectiveness of youth involvement and the extent that ‘participation improves or strengthens projects on which young adult have advised’ [17]. Literature on youth participation also outlines challenges to achieving better integration. Some of these include young adult experiencing time scarcity because of competing commitments (education, work or personal), young adult experiencing marginalisation or exclusion, feeling apprehensive about engaging with institutions, and young adult feeling pressured to conform to “adult” expectations [16].

It is evident that increased attention on loneliness has been coupled with a notable rise in a discourse of risk of the future physical and mental health impacts of loneliness [20–22]. Whilst these are important aspects to address of the potential outcomes associated with loneliness, the challenge is that the risks for future mental and physical health [23] dominate over and above the social and structural factors that contribute to the situation [24]. Medicalising loneliness is concerning, as it can often place the onus on the individual, assuming they need “fixing” [25]. While there has been greater recognition of the need to incorporate models of care premised on cultural and social determinants of health [26] there is still a tendency for loneliness responses to emphasise risk with the narrative of preventing future ill-health [27]. These outcomes are important, but the framing may implicitly sideline socially and lived-experience-derived perspectives and may (either unconsciously or inadvertently) overlook the culturally bound conceptions of loneliness and life changes [11].

Despite the acknowledged need for involvement of people with lived experience and implementation of community approaches, the dominant lens around loneliness prevention is weighted toward the clinical outcomes and drivers of risk, and relational, social and community led responses lag. This dominance may compound barriers for people to talk about loneliness and address the experience as a social, and increasingly common problem [28]. This is important since studies of youth engagement in mental health research and promotion cite the need to address stigma as an ongoing issue [29]. In this way, medicalising loneliness and focusing on individual level intervention may exacerbate stigma and continue to emphasise individualised approaches to a problem that requires a collaborative, tailored and holistic approach [21]. To date, most published work exploring youth loneliness has been observational, with relatively few papers focused on program delivery or developing collective solutions to loneliness [13, 30]. An even smaller body of work has applied participatory methodologies or described meaningful lived experience involvement in the design, delivery or evaluation of approaches to addressing loneliness [31, 32].

A/part of the crowd is an Australian research project that is responding to these gaps in loneliness research in young adults and implementing integrated lived-experience approaches. This is achieved in two ways. First, by adopting a participatory experiential design to gather experiences of loneliness from youth nationally to facilitate greater potential ownership of the project [16]. Second is through an integrated Lived Experience Advisory Group (LEAG) with embedded youth co-researchers in the team [18]. Co-researchers have participated as paid research team members who are engaged in research design, data collection and analysis, and translational activities including co-authoring and providing co-reflections for this paper. Lived experience advisory members have advised and commented on researcher and co-researcher impressions and interpretations that have been shared within meetings, but do not have as frequent engagement within the research team, nor do they participate in capacity building and its activities to the same degree.

The aim of this paper is to outline the importance of participatory design approaches to understand loneliness experiences and its impacts during emerging adulthood and youth life stages to reinforce how research design is a critical mechanism in integration processes and for embedding lived experience in research on loneliness. We draw on reflections shared by the Lived Experience Advisory Group (LEAG) members inclusive of five members who held dual roles as advisors and co-researchers. This paper develops further the methodological literature on lived experience approaches to loneliness research, and the integration of lived experience within project designs. We reflect on how this led to further integration of lived experience within a National Centre dedicated to embedded lived experience research approaches and experientially driven models of care. The paper provides additional detail of the delineation between the activities of co-research and advisory roles in applied research for which there is less guidance and information currently available [33]. We explore the opportunities for further scaled approaches of integration and reflect on the impacts of embedded lived experience research models both internally for the advisory group members and externally in project outputs and importantly, for the future translational steps and stages.

Methods

The broader project

A/part of the crowd is a research project that seeks to explore and respond to the current state of loneliness for young adults (aged 18–25) in Australia. It was funded by the Medibank Better Health Research Hub in 2023 to work with young adults to co-design a model of care to respond to loneliness and to implement this across primary care and community settings. This was supported by Medibank research priorities in mental health, loneliness and primary care. It was coordinated as a national flagship research project by the ALIVE National Centre for Mental Health Research Translation, which operates an embedded lived experience research model which guided the establishment of the group [34]. The National Centre was funded for a first five-year investment as part of a special initiative in mental health established by the National Health and Medical Research Council (GNT2002047) to conduct system change and implement new models of care to improve experiences and outcomes. The broader project received ethics approval from the University of Melbourne Human Research Ethics Committee (no. 28499 and no. 33132).

Since June 2024, the broader a/part of the crowd project invited young adults (18–25 years) to submit short reflections using creative means (such as prose, drawing, collage, photography, or music) to develop a multifaceted understanding of loneliness during big life changes. The grant commenced with three research objectives: (1) to use a participatory crowdsourcing method [35, 36] to gain experiential understanding of loneliness through story sharing; (2) to co-create an interactive website to share those stories and foster dialogue about loneliness in young adult; and (3), to implement and evaluate the platform and model in primary care and community settings. Young adults were invited to contribute via a “crowdsourcing space” which was available on the ALIVE National Centre website. The crowdsourcing space supported written submissions, as well as image, audio, video and link sharing. People were also offered a second pathway to attend a co-design workshop that was promoted as story making later which was informed by the LEAG and is described in latter parts of the results section of this paper.

One hundred and twenty-eight submissions were received from people across the 18–25 age range via eleven different modalities. The submissions varied in form including prose, photos, short video, poetry and collage, or small numbers of music and audio, and text and visual contributions combined. Key themes and inflection points from all the submissions were developed to establish the design principles for the initially planned interactive website (the outcomes from submissions and the co-design of the webspace are reported on separately to this current paper). Based on ethical principles of high-quality rigour in research, a decision was made to exclude Artificial Intelligence (AI)-generated submissions as well. The LEAG agreed that this would help to ensure submissions reflected the experiences of people directly impacted.

The reflections (or, data) in this paper have been derived from multiple qualitative sources such as: notes from LEAG meetings (details of discussions and outcomes are provided in Table 1), research questions that co-researchers responded to via email, and research activities completed during LEAG meetings plus co-researcher team capacity building sessions and reflections shared during co-analysis meetings. The co-reflections were facilitated by a lived experience research officer appointed to the project who was responsible for the coordination of most LEAG meetings and the communications with the co-researchers about activities including content preparation that supported recruitment in the broader project.

Establishing the project’s Lived Experience Advisory Group

Recruitment for membership to the Lived Experience Advisory Group was conducted in early 2024 via an open call that was shared through social media channels (LinkedIn, Instagram) and local networks (emailing lists, and partner organisations) across Australia. This resulted in 98 expressions of interest. Of the 98 nearly half were incomplete leaving 58 expressions of interest which were reviewed by the research team. We aimed to establish a group that reflected mixed locations across Australia, taking into account regional versus metropolitan, Socio-Economic Indexes for Areas rank (SEIFA) [37] as well as ages, experiences of life transitions that the person suggested that their expertise could speak to, and cultural backgrounds if shared. Additional sampling included the interest applicants had in supporting loneliness research and how they outlined their expertise in relation to this.

A 12-member youth and young adult Lived Experience Advisory Group (LEAG) was formed (new members were recruited in 2025 to replenish the group) in early 2024. Members ranged from 18 to 25 years, with representation across male, female and nonbinary members. Ten members lived in States and two were from the ACT (Australian Capital Territory). All interested applicants reported having experiences of mental ill-health and varying experiences of the life changes that the project was focused on. We did not have representation from the Northern Territory, or Tasmania due to no expressions of interest, and there was no representation of Aboriginal and or Torres Strait Islander young adult on the initial advisory group. We note that there was an Aboriginal research team member on the project to inform considerations, but the project was not developed from the start to focus on First Nations loneliness and would require further co-design with Aboriginal and Torres Strait islander communities and young adult to reflect the needs, cultural distinctions about loneliness and priorities as defined by communities. A young adult Aboriginal researcher also joined the team in 2025 as part of an internship program in the National Centre and attended three LEAG meetings. The researcher has continued engagement in the project as a research support officer since the internship concluded, and contributed to developing outputs of the final stages. The LEAG’s terms of reference detailed involvement as: providing guidance and feedback about the study, reviewing written materials, providing advice on how to work well with young adult, contributing to research directions, and co-creating the planned interactive website or resources to evolve from submissions.

All members in the advisory group were invited to consider co-researcher roles. The co-researcher roles were described to have additional engagement responsibilities with the project that meant people needed to have time and an interest in the conduct of research activities. These additional activities included contributing to recruitment strategies and design of materials, sharing materials or links to partner organisations, literature reviews and syntheses, data analysis, co-presentations and being co-authors of research papers and input to the final web resource. We offered the co-research opportunity to all LEAG members following the first meeting to ensure equity in opportunity, and to ensure flexibility for people around availability and for times when people might want to step back and have less involvement. Five people expressed interest in this via email with details of previous activities or experiences they had, and all were appointed into the co-research roles.

All research activities within advisory group meetings were reimbursed in recognition of the value of expertise and time shared and, to establish equitable research relationships, inclusivity and shared power [38]. Early activities within the group established ways of working together in an online meeting space and ways to support safety within the group to ensure members felt comfortable sharing their voices and to counter issues noted in previous research on the impact of participation on wellbeing [17, 39].

We now provide further details of what we learned through the processes of integration and embedding of the LEAG and co-research roles within the project drawing together data sources from meeting notes, email exchanges and co-reflection meetings. This includes reflections on the impacts of youth involvement in the research processes and project outcomes from the perspective of the whole team and direct co-researcher reflections on the approach the project took and why it is important, their views on what they feel is needed to address loneliness in young adults and to build on impressions of the submissions so far, and any understanding of the experiences shared to date. We invited co-researchers to share opportunities for the project and approach as well.

Results

Safe working relationships and the creation of an embedded approach to loneliness research

The first LEAG meeting was an opportunity to establish shared ways of working and to understand the different perspectives within the group and included sharing the research team’s pre-established methods for the participatory crowdsourcing. To achieve a safe start, the meeting commenced with a photo-elicitation activity which entailed placing a series of pre-selected photos on a Mural board related to places, people and things and inviting new lived experience advisors to select a photo and to share how they connected the image to loneliness or a life transition as a prompt for introductions. This 'conditions for safety activity' is shown in Fig. 1 with some of the responses the method evoked. This material was drawn on and included in the first poster output from the group which was presented at a national mental health conference in 2024 (the details of which appear in Table 1 later).

The photo activity helped to grow shared understanding around how members viewed loneliness with people making references to ‘feeling different’ (Lyam), ‘moving to Australia and the cultural transitions’ (Lidan and Toby), ‘questions of what is home’ (Toby), and ‘how the pressures of housing security impact on fostering social networks’ (Jemma). As the grant was written and funded ahead of the LEAG establishment, it was important to gain agreement that the proposed initial design and methodologies put forward would work from young adult perspectives.

To this end, following the establishment of safety in the first LEAG meeting, we then discussed and reached agreement on the method for inviting young adults to share their stories about loneliness during big life changes. In later co-reflections Li, Lidan and Gianna provided the following perspectives on the creative methods and approach for understanding loneliness as follows:The project’s approach is unique in that it collects stories, poems, and artwork, offering a deeply personal and nuanced snapshot into people’s lives. Traditional focus groups can sometimes struggle to capture these complexities, but storytelling and visuals provide a more in-depth, emotional perspective. While the approach itself is layered and multifaceted, I think that reflects the complexity of loneliness - it’s not a straightforward issue with a single cause or solution (Li, LEAG Member and Co-researcher).

The advisory group mechanism was therefore critical to ensuring the project design and methods reflected what young adults might identify with. Additional LEAG activities over the course of the broader project have included further recruitment strategy development, and testing and refining the data collection and submission pathways. The testing of the recruitment and awareness-raising efforts ensured that we were meeting youth where they were at and addressing what all co-researchers found important in co-reflections ‘ensuring generational context and cultural factors that shape loneliness are understood’.

From time-to-time LEAG members produced short videos to encourage other young adult to share their stories. This was beneficial for driving engagement and sharing back what was heard in real time and it provided a public platform to deliver messages and encouraged further story sharing.

Co-research roles to foster leadership and shared team learnings

The LEAG has also created a project structure for members who were interested in co-research to hold dual roles and develop further research method and analysis expertise. This has supported and ensured the integration of lived-experience perspectives into all of the research methods and current outputs. For co-researchers, there have been opportunities to co-author outputs, for example, a conference poster presentation drew on our initial photo elicitation activity (presented above in Fig 1) and the team completed a systematic narrative review and synthesis (under review elsewhere) of the interventions that have been published on addressing loneliness in young adults and they worked collaboratively on this research paper. Some co-researchers have co-presented with the research team at research updates of the ALIVE National Centre with academic, network member and community research partner audiences. A two-way exchange of learning has been promoted, with research team members providing training in how to conduct a narrative review, and how to use the online review system (Covidence), including the data extraction steps and synthesis. There has also been support for conducting qualitative thematic and visual analysis (of the submissions) which established design principles for the website, with check-in meetings held as an opportunity to discuss questions and establish direction. In all LEAG meetings, the research team and co-researchers have worked to create a learning space so that advisory group members could hear directly from co-researchers about updates and new project directions, and the researchers present to listen. This has helped to shift power dynamics of traditional research and advisor roles through authentic youth representation which Kush reflected “goes beyond just being in the room - it’s about ensuring our voices shape decisions”. Capacity building activities have grown the leadership skills of co-researchers through the course of the project and of LEAG members. Co-reflections from Lidan support this “as a young adult, I feel validated, acknowledged, and empowered to speak of my cultural background and perspective.” ( LEAG member and Co-Researcher). The space of being with other members who shared lived experience also grows supportive capacities as members value reflecting together.

Co-researchers have gained confidence in the aims of the project and learned from the stories other young adults have shared. Members have also been offered opportunities to be involved in the larger National Centre research governance framework and have joined the overarching LEAG for the Centre. Recently one co-researcher has been appointed as a strategic lived experience co-lead to the Centre ensuring that integration continues and embedded approaches to co-research are adapted and transferred within the Centre.

Navigating challenges in integrating lived experience

Creating the relational processes and approaches has taken time, consideration and intention from the whole team of researchers across all LEAG members and co-researchers. Whilst this can be viewed as a constraint in the context of project timelines or resources and is commonly referred to in the broader literature, working relationally has supported depth of exploration of the content and research direction [16, 40]. A core principle in supporting this, has been flexibility for participation. An example of this is hosting meetings at times that suit the young adult we work with – the research team determined via a poll that out-of-hours timeslots worked for the majority of our LEAG. Therefore, an alternating out-of-hours meeting schedule was adopted, with the top two highest-voted timeslots.

All LEAG members and co-researchers have contributed in a capacity that has worked for them, and communication was reciprocal [17]. This was a core principle from the outset but did create some challenges in scheduling of research work and tasks and allowing time for completion. Regular, reciprocal engagement can be challenging on a project with varying objectives and outputs. In the first year of LEAG meetings a funder partner also engaged in the LEAG meetings but ceased attendance in the 2nd year. The partner attendance created constraints for the research team and LEAG members to be able to share their views openly. To ensure success in this project, the coordination of the LEAG by a lived experience (LE) research officer provided a regular point of contact and communication with the LEAG and co-researchers. While coordinating the practical aspects of the LEAG was essential, the LE research officer also held lived experience of the research topic, and is under 30 years of age, which helped to create familiarity and safety in exploring loneliness. What one co-researcher said made the group successful was the openness to ideas and willingness to experiment with different approaches:

What truly made the group successful was our openness to ideas and willingness to experiment with different approaches. By creating space for members to suggest new ways of working and trial innovative engagement methods, we were able to build trust, foster a sense of ownership, and ultimately increase participation and impact (Li, Co-researcher and LEAG member).

What truly made the group successful was our openness to ideas and willingness to experiment with different approaches. By creating space for members to suggest new ways of working and trial innovative engagement methods, we were able to build trust, foster a sense of ownership, and ultimately increase participation and impact (Li, Co-researcher and LEAG member).

Power imbalances are inherent in research, and we recognise that forming a group of young adults - the focus of the research, with experienced researchers, can reproduce these dynamics. At times, it was observed by the research team that there may have been hesitancy to provide critical responses from the LEAG to the project team, an established challenge in youth participatory work [16]. Co-reflections suggested that this decreased over time, particularly with the number of engagements held with LEAG members. The project team recognised that LEAG members’ readiness to share was variable. This may have been due to several factors, including trust, prior experience and uncertainty about how feedback would be received.

Some of the ways power imbalance was countered were to first, regularly encourage free discussion, opinion sharing and critique. Second, outside of LEAG meetings, the research team offered expressed pathways to provide their thoughts or feedback, so people did not have to share in a larger group forum (such as a one-on-one phone call or Zoom to the LE research officer, or via a team monitored a/part of the crowd email). We also encouraged LEAG members to provide us feedback asynchronously outside of meetings, in case they needed more time to process the discussions had. A full list of engagement principles that a/part of the crowd used in working with the LEAG is shown in Table 3.

In all these aspects it is possible that tensions were present that the research team may not have been aware of given the nature of the constructed research environment for the project.

Integrated lived experience for community translation

The LEAG and co-researchers have shaped the directions of the research and the adaptation of recruitment strategies and submission pathways throughout. This has included the team adopting the LEAG’s suggestion for co-design workshops for young adult to gather and share their story through creative participatory methods [41], using picture elicitation, story-making, and rapid ideation. The initial stories that were submitted (n = 128) as part of the broader project have now been shared in an interactive website called a/ site to belong – www.apartofthecrowd.au – which has met the second objective of the research project outlined in the methods overview and involved LEAG and co-researchers in website testing, content co-creation and promotion. The website provides a space for ongoing contributions using creative mediums, the prior 128 submissions are available within a full digital book for reading, viewing, or listening where relevant and a co-created resource called a/ kit to belong has been co-designed. Discussion

Central to this project and repeatedly emphasised by the LEAG members and the current published guidance [17] has been the importance of the voice and perspectives of young adults being authentically present throughout research design, implementation and translation. The LEAG highlighted the importance of making stories and emotions visible, in a way that numbers or quantitative approaches to this topic could not. The participatory approaches and multiple streams of engagement adopted through the project supported the collection of rich, diverse stories from the community most impacted. Other projects that have adopted similar participatory approaches, such as poetry for example as a multilevel response to addressing loneliness have found a similar connection between creative methods providing the opportunities to understand experiences more deeply [42]. The two pathways which were shaped by the LEAG for participants to share their story allowed for meaningful engagement in a way that allowed young adult to share their experiences safely; these are all outcomes that have been shaped by having an integrated approach and reflect important elements of suggested guidelines for youth involvement and participation. These extensions beyond traditional consultation centred what had meaning for participants and co-researchers and provided the space to safely present responses in a way and pace that was comfortable.

Adoption of an integrated approach and embedded co-research has supported the team to both share their knowledge of methods, and for co-researchers to develop a range of qualitative analysis skills, and to understand the varied emotions and experiences of young adult navigating loneliness during significant life changes. A key consideration is the need for peer support across participation in content analysis, since people share elements of lived experience they may require supportive conversations following engagement in work [17]. Guidelines similarly note that mentorship is a desirable aspect of youth participation but there is less evidence of this and guidance on where it works well [17]. Finding the balance between peer approaches and expertise of research methods to support this mentorship is an ongoing challenge. Mendes et al., [29] also found that youth participation in mental health promotion in Brazil required ‘adult and youth partnership, peer support training, combatting adultism and mental health stigma [29]. These challenges have similarly been encountered in the broader project, but the mechanisms of a lived experience project officer and the strength of a creative study design created the conditions to address these. In particular, co-research roles were essential for not reverting to adultism in the project analysis and co-designed responses. This issue was also established in research examining screening for social and emotional wellbeing in the Torres Strait [43]. In this example, the words used by community members to describe social and emotional wellbeing needs were worry, sadness, and stress, yet no screening tools for wellbeing include the word worry [43].

The co-researchers reflected that through the participatory design of the project and the membership of the LEAG that they platformed a diversity of experiences. This was important in the co-design of the supportive webspace that will be implemented as community-level preventive approach. Co-researcher reflections also suggest that equality between advisors and co-researchers was maintained, and contributions of advisor roles had equal value to contributors. Creative approaches expanded our ability to capture the intersectionality of loneliness with other social and emotional dimensions.

By fostering safe spaces for engagement, co-researchers have been able to freely share their perspectives and contribute to initiatives that were important to them [17]. Through the project, co-researchers have reflected in feedback and in presentations that their voice had value and impact. LEAG members and co-researchers acknowledged that there was a strong sense of ownership that came with participating in and co-leading a research project [16]. Notwithstanding this there are some limitations worth noting.

It is important to note that relational approaches to research take time and intentional planning of resources needs to be considered to foster embedded co-research processes. Similar to Arelingaiah, Navaneetham [38], we encouraged diversity in representation, the delineation of opportunities and boundaries of influence within the project and allocated resources to training, activities and communication. We did not conduct formalised training programs however and in the future, it will be important to establish if these are needed or if formalisation of peer co-research leads to co-option into dominant research frameworks and approaches. Additionally, future considerations should account for transitioning responsibilities of LEAG meetings and conversations to co-researcher roles so that the leadership and ownership of the project is fostered throughout all stages.

Through this, LEAG members described a sense of validation through reading submissions from other young adult who had navigated similar challenges which supports previous research on the impacts of youth participation in research [17]. Bringing different perspectives together provided an opportunity to view loneliness as a springboard to explore independence, growth, and self-acceptance.

Conclusion

A/part of the crowd created conditions and provided resourcing to establish a youth Lived Experience Advisory Group where members and co-researchers indicated that they have felt safe and comfortable to contribute their ideas and experiences to shape research processes from the outset. This resulted in closer alignment to the lived experience of loneliness, its intersectional nature, and supported shared meaning-making and shaped the project webspace and resources directly that are now being implemented.

The result of integrated lived experience approaches has been a longer-term relational engagement rather than a traditional transactional, one-off or time limited research engagement. Co-researchers and members are now joining different working groups within the National Centre and have been appointed into further leadership roles to build on the knowledge and expertise developed in this project. By understanding the drivers, meanings and experiences of loneliness within the Australian context as defined and shared by young adults, we can support other young adult who experience it. This means that now more than ever, greater attention to the way we do things matters for ensuring that embedded approaches can lead to greater ownership and leadership of the research agenda and its outcomes from the people most impacted. Shifting to collective and embedded lived experience approaches is part of the change needed to ensure that loneliness does not become a medicalised and individualised problem.
