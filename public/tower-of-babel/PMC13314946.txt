Phase-only synthesis of cosecant-squared patterns with reduced sidelobes in large planar arrays via physics-informed deep neural networks
Authors: Tarek Sallam, Ahmed M. Attiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper introduces a deep learning-based framework for phase-only synthesis of cosecant-squared (csc²) radiation patterns in planar antenna arrays with high efficiency and accuracy. The proposed method employs a physics-informed deep neural network (PIDNN), where the training process is guided by a loss function that enforces consistency between the desired and generated radiation patterns. By embedding physical constraints into the learning procedure, the model effectively achieves two critical objectives: suppression of sidelobe level (SLL) and minimization of ripples within the shaped beam region. To meet the target csc² profile under sidelobe constraints, only the phase excitations of the array elements are optimized, reducing the complexity of the problem. The performance of the proposed approach is evaluated against established optimization methods, including genetic algorithms (GA) and particle swarm optimization (PSO). Numerical and statistical analyses demonstrate that the PIDNN provides superior results in terms of pattern fidelity, loss function convergence, and computation time, particularly for large-scale planar arrays.

Introduction

The cosecant-squared (csc²) radiation pattern is frequently utilized in airborne and ground radar systems to achieve near-constant echo power from detected targets, ensuring that the received signal level remains largely independent of target al.titude1–5. Classical analytical approaches, such as Fourier transform6,7, Schelkunoff8,9, and Woodward–Lawson synthesis10,11, have been extensively employed to generate shaped radiation patterns based on continuous amplitude and phase distributions. In certain cases, shaped beams can also be achieved by applying phase-only compensation, eliminating the need for amplitude control12. However, these methods do not fully utilize all available degrees of freedom in the synthesis process, often resulting in suboptimal designs in terms of array size or the number of elements13. Furthermore, they provide limited capability in effectively controlling sidelobe levels14.

Global optimization techniques, including simulated annealing15, genetic algorithm (GA)16–18, particle swarm optimization (PSO)19,20, differential evolution21, and invasive weed optimization22, have also been applied to this problem. However, their computational complexity increases rapidly with the number of unknown parameters, making them computationally prohibitive for large-scale array implementations (as will be shown in Section IV-C).

In23, a discrete random phase approximation technique was introduced for shaped beam generation in two-dimensional (2-D) arrays using phase-only digital metasurfaces. Nevertheless, due to the restricted modulation capability of 2-bit phase quantization, this approach typically results in elevated sidelobe levels (SLL), as it cannot simultaneously ensure accurate beam shaping and effective sidelobe suppression. In24, a method for synthesizing mask-constrained shaped beams in 2-D arrays was presented based on convex optimization and polynomial factorization, avoiding the use of global optimization techniques. Despite this advantage, the approach suffers from significant computational burden, requiring approximately two hours to complete the synthesis process.

In recent years, neural networks (NNs) have demonstrated strong capabilities in handling highly nonlinear and nonconvex problems, particularly in cases where the input–output relationship is not explicitly known in advance. Owing to their learning capacity and fast inference, NNs have been increasingly adopted in complex electromagnetics applications to obtain accurate and computationally efficient solutions25–29. For instance, encoder–decoder architectures have been explored for shaped beampattern synthesis30,31. Nevertheless, such approaches are fundamentally data-driven, requiring a large volume of training samples to adequately learn the underlying nonlinear mapping. These datasets are typically generated through numerical or full-wave electromagnetic (EM) simulations, which becomes impractical as the dataset size grows. Consequently, assembling sufficiently comprehensive training data to span the parameter space demands substantial computational resources and time. In addition, the training process itself becomes increasingly computationally demanding due to the scale of the data, particularly when addressing large planar array configurations.

Antenna array synthesis problems are inherently nonlinear and highly multidimensional because the radiation pattern depends on the complex interaction among the excitation coefficients of all array elements. In particular, phase-only synthesis introduces additional challenges due to the strong coupling between the element phases and the resulting sidelobe behavior and beam shape. Consequently, determining the optimal excitation phases for large planar arrays becomes a difficult nonconvex optimization problem.

Deep learning techniques provide an attractive framework for addressing such problems because deep NNs are capable of approximating highly nonlinear mappings between desired radiation characteristics and array excitation parameters. Unlike traditional optimization algorithms that repeatedly search the solution space through stochastic iterations, deep NNs can efficiently learn the underlying relationship between antenna parameters and electromagnetic responses. Furthermore, by incorporating physical constraints directly into the learning process, physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) improve both solution accuracy and convergence stability while reducing computational complexity.

In this work, a physics-informed deep neural network (PIDNN) is developed to synthesize csc² radiation patterns for 2-D arrays using phase-only control. Physics-informed neural networks, originally introduced by Raissi et al.32, incorporate underlying physical principles directly into the learning process. In the proposed framework, the loss function is formulated based on the discrepancy between the target and synthesized radiation patterns, enabling the network to learn the optimal phase excitations that reproduce the desired beam shape. A key advantage of this approach is that it does not rely on pre-generated training datasets, thereby avoiding the need for extensive input–output data collection. PIDNNs have recently been explored in a range of electromagnetics applications33–37. Furthermore, their use has been extended to csc² beam synthesis for linear arrays with complex (amplitude and phase) excitation control38. However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, the application of PIDNNs to the synthesis of csc² patterns for planar arrays using phase-only excitation has not yet been reported. Phase-only array configurations offer practical benefits, particularly in terms of simpler feed network implementation compared to amplitude or complex weighting schemes. Therefore, the proposed method represents a novel contribution by enabling 2-D shaped beam synthesis using a PIDNN with phase-only synthesis.

To assess the effectiveness of the proposed PIDNN in beampattern shaping, its performance is compared against two well-established global optimization techniques, namely GA and PSO, across planar arrays of varying sizes. Three representative configurations are considered: a 16 × 16 array as a small-scale case, a 32 × 32 array representing a moderate-sized structure, and a 64 × 64 array corresponding to a large planar array. The obtained results demonstrate that the PIDNN outperforms both GA and PSO, achieving lower deviation within the csc²-shaped region and reduced SLL, particularly for larger arrays. In addition, several evaluation criteria are employed to validate the superiority of the proposed method, including loss function values, computational time, and statistical analysis using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test39. Owing to these advantages, the PIDNN-based synthesis framework offers both high efficiency and reduced computational time, making it a promising candidate for real-time array synthesis applications.

PIDNN-based csc2 pattern synthesis

From an intuitive perspective, it is desirable to approximate an inverse mapping \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}^{-1}(\cdot\:)$$\end{document} such that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\upalpha\:}={y}^{-1}\left(\mathrm{Mask}\right)$$\end{document}, enabling an efficient array synthesis process. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\upalpha\:}\:$$\end{document}denotes the vector of element phase excitations, while Mask represents the sampled version of the desired radiation pattern. Motivated by this concept, a PIDNN-based framework is proposed for phase-only synthesis of csc² beampatterns in 2-D arrays, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

The proposed PIDNN architecture comprises an input layer, multiple hidden layers, and an output layer. The network input, defined as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{Mask}=\left[\mathrm{Mask}\right({\theta\:}_{p}\left)\right],p=\mathrm{1,2},\dots\:,P,$$\end{document}

corresponds to the sampled mask values at discrete angular points \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}_{p}$$\end{document}. The network output is given by\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{\upalpha\:}=\left[{\alpha\:}_{mn}\right],m=1,\dots\:,M;{\hspace{0.25em}\hspace{0.05em}}n=1,\dots\:,N,$$\end{document}

which represents the predicted phase excitation coefficients associated with the desired mask.

The PIDNN is formulated to operate as an array synthesis tool. In antenna array design, the desired radiation pattern is typically specified in advance, whereas the corresponding excitations of the array elements remain unknown. In contrast to conventional data-driven NNs that rely on large sets of input–output training pairs to learn complex mappings, the PIDNN operates with a single unlabeled input, namely the desired radiation pattern Mask, without requiring associated excitation data.

During the training/optimization process, the network optimizes this input to generate a corresponding set of phase excitations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{\upalpha\:}$$\end{document}. By minimizing the discrepancy between the synthesized array pattern and the target mask, the PIDNN converges to a solution that satisfies the imposed pattern constraints, thereby addressing the mask-constrained synthesis problem. Accordingly, the loss function defined in (3) is constructed based on this pattern deviation.

From this viewpoint, the PIDNN effectively functions as a NN–based optimizer. It iteratively updates its internal parameters (weights and biases) to minimize the loss function, which in turn leads to the indirect optimization of the array phase excitations. Consequently, the training process does not require pre-collected datasets, but instead relies solely on a physics-informed loss formulation derived from the mismatch between the desired and achieved radiation patterns. In short, it is an online optimization process rather than a training procedure.

The adopted PIDNN architecture consists of an input layer with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P\:$$\end{document}neurons, followed by five hidden layers, each comprising 100 neurons, and an output layer containing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:MN$$\end{document} neurons corresponding to the phase excitation coefficients of all array elements. The hyperbolic tangent (tanh) function is employed as the activation function across all layers. Network parameters, including weights and biases, are optimized using the Adam algorithm40. The learning rate is initially set to 0.01 and is gradually reduced during training using a decay factor of 0.0005. Since the framework operates with a single input sample, the batch size is fixed at one.

Small planar arrays

A 16 × 16 array is considered as a representative example of a small planar configuration. The convergence behavior of the PIDNN is illustrated in Fig. 3, where the loss function Loss defined in (3) is plotted against the number of iterations. As observed, the PIDNN attains a best loss value of approximately 0.0038 dB. In addition, the convergence is notably fast, with the optimization process terminating after only 129 iterations, requiring about 3 s. In contrast, as reported in Table 1, both GA and PSO require 1000 iterations to complete, yielding final loss values of 0.0038 dB and 0.005 dB, respectively. Statistical analysis and computation time

To statistically evaluate the performance, the loss function values obtained by the PIDNN, GA, and PSO are recorded over 50 independent runs for each array configuration. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test39 is then employed to assess whether the PIDNN provides a statistically significant improvement over the other methods. According to this test, a p-value below 0.05 (corresponding to a 5% significance level) indicates a meaningful difference in performance, whereas higher values suggest no significant distinction.

The resulting p-values for comparisons between PIDNN and GA, as well as PIDNN and PSO, across the three array sizes are presented in Table 4. As observed, all p-values fall below the 0.05 threshold, providing strong evidence against the null hypothesis. This confirms that the superior performance achieved by the PIDNN, relative to GA and PSO, is statistically significant and not due to random variation.

Conclusion

This work has presented a PIDNN-based approach for phase-only synthesis of cosecant-squared radiation patterns with low SLL in planar antenna arrays, and its performance has been evaluated against GA and PSO. The proposed method has been applied to arrays of different sizes—small, medium, and large—and consistently demonstrates superior performance across all configurations. In particular, the PIDNN maintains high accuracy and robustness even as the problem dimensionality increases, whereas the performance of GA and PSO, especially PSO, deteriorates noticeably for larger arrays. Numerical results indicate that the PIDNN consistently achieves the lowest sidelobe levels among the compared methods while preserving an almost ripple-free csc²-shaped beam. In other words, the PIDNN provides the best tradeoff between sidelobe suppression and csc²-shaped beam accuracy among the compared methods Although the desired sidelobe mask was set to − 30 dB, the achieved SLL depends on the available array degrees of freedom and the phase-only excitation constraint. The obtained SLL values remain close to the target specification, particularly for the small and medium array configurations.

The quality of the obtained solutions is further validated through statistical analysis using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, confirming the significant performance advantage of the PIDNN over GA and PSO. In addition, the proposed method requires substantially lower computational time, making it a promising optimization tool for real-time array synthesis, particularly in large-scale scenarios. Furthermore, additional simulations with different shaped-beam angular regions confirm the robustness and flexibility of the proposed PIDNN framework for different csc² beam specifications. The PIDNN framework can be extended to the synthesis of other shaped radiation patterns and applied to different array configurations.
