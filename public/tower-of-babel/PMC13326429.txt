Biomechanical characteristics of osteotomy correction for kyphosis secondary to ankylosing spondylitis: a finite element study
Authors: Xu Zhu, Alimujiang Yusufu, Abudunaibi Aili, Jie Dai, Rongchang Fu, Yuan Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study comparatively analyzed the biomechanical performance of different vertebral level osteotomies in patients with severe kyphosis secondary to ankylosing spondylitis (ASK) using finite element analysis.MethodsThe hilus pulmonis-hip axis (HP-HA) method was employed to determine osteotomy angles. Posterior vertebral column resection (PVCR) was simulated at four distinct vertebral levels (T12, L1, L2, and L3), followed by pedicle screw-rod fixation combined with interbody fusion cages. Total displacement, von Mises stress distribution in the pedicle screw-rod construct, titanium cage, and cage-vertebral interface were evaluated and compared across models.ResultsFour finite element models (A–D, corresponding to T12, L1, L2, and L3 osteotomies) were successfully established and validated. Total displacement decreased progressively from model A to model D, measuring 6.88 mm, 5.44 mm, 3.90 mm, and 2.26 mm, respectively. Peak von Mises stresses in the pedicle screw-rod systems were 115.58 MPa, 139.53 MPa, 191.35 MPa, and 184.14 MPa, respectively. Model D (L3 osteotomy) demonstrated the smallest displacement and well-dispersed stress distribution, while model A (T12 osteotomy) exhibited the largest displacement despite having the lowest screw-rod stress. All stresses remained below the yield strength of the internal fixation devices.ConclusionThis finite element study demonstrates that L3 osteotomy yields superior stability compared to T12 osteotomy, though the conclusion is based on a single-case model and requires further validation through multicase studies. These findings underscore the importance of considering segment-specific biomechanical factors in surgical planning for ASK patients.Keywords: Ankylosing spondylitis, Kyphosis, Posterior vertebral column resection (PVCR), Finite element analysis, Biomechanics, Osteotomy level

Introduction

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is a chronic inflammatory disorder predominantly involving the spine and sacroiliac joints [1, 2]. It frequently results in persistent spinal lesions and pronounced sagittal deformities, causing chronic pain, compromised posture, and ultimately reduced quality of life. Surgical correction of severe kyphotic deformities in AS patients represents a challenging issue in spinal surgery. These deformities often manifest as substantial C-shaped kyphosis affecting multiple spinal segments. Although spinal osteotomies are routinely performed to rectify kyphotic deformities and improve patient outcomes, these procedures carry significant risks, including excessive intraoperative bleeding, potential internal fixation failure, and severe neurological injuries [3, 4]. Consequently, careful selection of patients for osteotomy and meticulous management of associated complications are crucial to mitigate adverse outcomes. The success of corrective procedures depends on the surgical approach, osteotomy site, segment width, and the number of segments involved, highlighting the importance of optimizing spinal reconstruction techniques to minimize surgical risks and enhance patient outcomes. Thus, spinal reconstruction strategies and risk mitigation have become prominent research topics in this field [5–7].

Surgical correction of ankylosing spondylitis kyphosis (ASK) primarily involves osteotomy, with the choice of procedure being critical. Common methods for ASK correction include Smith-Peterson osteotomy, pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO), and posterior vertebral column resection (PVCR). One study demonstrated an average kyphosis correction of 38° [8] using PVCR, which is recommended for severe kyphosis exceeding 40°, and also suitable for patients with significant coronal imbalance. Despite its potential for greater kyphosis correction, PVCR requires complete removal of the spine’s tricolumnar structures, posing risks such as vertebral shortening, displacement, neurological impairment, and necessitating anterior internal fixation after vertebral body removal [9, 10]. This complexity presents significant challenges for surgeons, particularly concerning the long-term stability of pedicle screws, connecting rods, and fusion devices. However, biomechanical analyses of PVCR for ASK correction remain notably scarce.

We propose constructing a 3D finite element model to simulate ASK osteotomy correction. PVCR was simulated at the thoracic 12 and lumbar 1–3 segments. Stress distributions on the pedicle screw-rod system under loading conditions were assessed to determine the optimal number of segments requiring screw fixation. Stresses following osteotomy gap closure were analyzed, and overall spinal displacement after fixation was quantified. This study aims to identify a safe osteotomy approach for clinical kyphosis correction, reduce complications such as spinal nerve and vascular injuries, mitigate surgical risks, and provide a reliable theoretical basis for maintaining the physiological curvature and stability of the spine post-osteotomy. The investigation focused on effectively restoring sagittal alignment and ensuring stable internal fixation in ASK patients to minimize surgical risks.

Materials and methods

Modeling objectives and software

A patient diagnosed with kyphosis secondary to AS was enrolled in the study (November 12,2024). Full-spine X-ray and CT imaging from cervical level 1 to the sacrococcygeal region revealed a kyphotic Cobb angle of 76° (T2-L3) with no other spinal or spinal cord abnormalities identified. The patient provided informed consent approved by the Ethics Committee of the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University.All enrolled patients underwent bone mineral density (BMD) testing prior to surgery to exclude osteoporosis.

The ASK model was created by reconstructing CT data using Mimics 22.0 software. Geomagic Studio 2020 was utilized to optimize and align model data and reconstruct the NURBS surface model. UG NX 8.5 software refined the anatomical relationship between implants and the spine (Fig. 1) and simulated surgical procedures. Hypermesh software was employed for mesh generation, while ANSYS Workbench 2020 R1 was used to construct the 3D finite element model and conduct mechanical analyses.

Reconstruction of the intact ASK model using CT data (A), 3D model generated by Geomagic Studio 2020 software (B)

Estimation of spinal osteotomy angle

The HP-HA technique (Fig. 2) was utilized to correct the sagittal balance of spinal-pelvic parameters, which is widely applied clinically [11]. On full spinal radiographs, the hilus of the lung (point A), the midpoint of the bilateral hip axis (point B), and the apex of the anterior aspect of the osteotomy vertebra (point C) were identified and integrated into the kyphosis model. The apex of the osteotomy vertebra served as the center of rotation, with the radius defined as the distance from the apex to the hilus. The osteotomy angle was determined by aligning the hilus line with the body’s gravity line.

Overview of the HP-HA method: A represents the lung hilus, B the hip axis, C the apex of the osteotomy vertebra (L2), and D the intersection of the arc (radius AC) with the gravity line (A). Sagittal pelvic parameters include PI and PT (B)

The pelvic incidence (PI) was measured as 38.4°, and pelvic tilt (PT) was 12.1°. The theoretical pelvic incidence (t-PI) was calculated as t-PI = PI × 0.37—7° [12]. The simulated osteotomy angles at vertebrae T12, L1, L2, and L3 were 59.2°, 47.8°, 36.9°, and 32.2°, respectively.

Osteotomy and internal fixation modeling

PVCR was chosen for osteotomy, involving removal of the posterior spinal structures (lamina, bilateral pedicles), the target vertebral body, and adjacent intervertebral discs. Pedicle screws were inserted into three segments above and below the osteotomized vertebra. A temporary fixation rod was placed on one side to prevent displacement of the osteotomy site. A titanium cage was inserted into the vertebral gap, and screws were secured on the opposite side using a second connecting bar (Fig. 3) [13].

Illustrations (A–D) showing PVCR performed at T12, L1, L2, and L3 vertebrae, respectively

PVCR was performed at thoracic 12 (T12) and lumbar vertebrae (L1–L3). Models A–D corresponded to osteotomies at T12, L1, L2, and L3, respectively (Fig. 3).

Finite element analysis

Optimized ASK osteotomy model data were imported into HyperMesh software for mesh generation. The vertebral bodies, intervertebral discs, and calcified ligaments were modeled according to previous literature (Table 1). The data were then transferred to ANSYS Workbench 2020 R1. Given extensive ossification of intervertebral discs and ligaments in ASK, their material parameters were assumed identical to cancellous bone [14]. Stresses and displacement variations at internal fixation and osteotomy surfaces were calculated and compared across the four osteotomy models. The sacrum was constrained inferiorly, and a preload was applied to the C1 vertebral surface for analysis.

Loads, boundary conditions, and measurement parameters

The sacrum was fixed to restrict movement, and displacement at the superior surface of the first thoracic vertebral body was constrained in all planes. Neuromuscular influences were neglected, as patients were under general anesthesia with muscle relaxation during surgery. Consequently, the simulation excluded muscle and gravitational influences. To replicate internal fixation stress, a uniform 400 N compressive force and 10 N·m moment were applied to all models. Material parameters such as Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio were assigned as listed.

Internal fixation stress and displacement were computed for the four osteotomy models under various loading conditions. Stress distributions were analyzed at screws and rods, the cage, and osteotomy contact surfaces to evaluate fixation failure potential. Displacements were assessed to evaluate internal fixation stability.

Model validation

To validate the feasibility of the four models, simulations were performed to assess vertebral segment angles and displacements in supine, flexion, hyperextension, lateral flexion (left and right), and rotational positions under a pure torque of 10 N·m. Results

Four finite element models for posterior osteotomy fixation were developed to analyze displacement and stress variations among the surgical configurations. Additionally, the stability of the ASK models was evaluated.

Stability of the ASK model

Figure 4 shows the displacement patterns for the ASK spinal models following osteotomy correction. Total displacements for models A–D were 6.88 mm, 5.44 mm, 3.90 mm, and 2.26 mm, respectively. The large displacement observed in model A may increase the risk of internal fixation failure and cause excessive kyphosis correction, resulting in an undesirable “looking up to the sky” posture. Conversely, the minimal displacement in model D indicates potential undercorrection, impairing daily activities. According to the HP-HA technique, the required osteotomy angles for models A to D were 59.2°, 47.8°, 38.9°, and 32.2°, respectively. Thus, selecting a lower-level osteotomy vertebra is recommended to prevent excessive spinal displacement due to overly large osteotomy angles.

Von Mises stress on pedicle screw-rod systems

Figure 5 illustrates stress distribution at the pedicle screw-vertebral interfaces in the four osteotomy correction models. Maximum Von Mises stress values were 115.58 MPa (model A), 139.53 MPa (model B), 191.35 MPa (model C), and 184.14 MPa (model D) (Table 3). All values were below the yield strength of the pedicle screw-rod system. Among these, the highest stress occurred in the L2 model, followed by L3, L1, and T12 models. Maximum rod stress appeared at the screw-rod interface adjacent to the osteotomy site, while maximum screw stress occurred at the screw tail and rod connection points. Placement of the cage between the upper and lower endplates following osteotomy resulted in a symmetrical stress distribution on both sides of the screw-rod system.

Discussion

This study aimed to compare the biomechanical performance of different osteotomy levels (T12-L3) for ankylosing spondylitis kyphosis using finite element analysis. Four models were successfully established, revealing level-specific effects on stress distribution and stability, thus achieving the research objective.Severe kyphosis associated with AS typically requires osteotomy for deformity correction. Thoracolumbar osteotomy procedures carry significant risks, which can adversely impact patient quality of life in cases of complications. Therefore, meticulous preoperative planning is crucial for optimizing orthopedic outcomes, minimizing surgical risks, and improving patient quality of life [26]. Currently, finite element mechanical analyses specific to orthopedic surgery for kyphotic ankylosis are limited [22].

Application of finite element analysis to surgeries addressing thoracolumbar kyphosis in AS patients could provide valuable objective clinical data and offer theoretical support for future research.

Available osteotomy methods include PSO, PVCR, and vertebral column decancellation (VCD). Often, multiple osteotomies are utilized to restore sagittal balance [27, 28], with selection depending on factors such as severity of the deformity, location of the posterior convex apex, and patient expectations. Larger osteotomies do not necessarily yield superior outcomes. In this study, PVCR was chosen to analyze the mechanical characteristics of internal fixation systems and vertebral bodies adjacent to the osteotomy, aiming to identify optimal osteotomy and fixation strategies. Initially introduced by MacLennan for severe scoliosis correction using combined anterior and posterior vertebral resection, PVCR was later refined by Suk et al. [9], who recommended it for correcting severe rigid scoliosis. Kim et al. [8] described six cases of AS kyphosis treated with PVCR, reporting an average correction of 38°. This suggests that PVCR effectively corrects severe kyphosis exceeding 40° and benefits patients with significant coronal imbalance. Despite its effectiveness, PVCR involves complete resection of all three spinal columns, carrying high neurological risks due to vertebral body shortening and spinal cord displacement during surgery. Anterior internal fixation is often required following resection. Lenke et al. [10] reported a 40% complication incidence, and Kim et al. [8] reported 39%. Therefore, analyzing mechanical properties of internal fixation devices and osteotomy sites postoperatively is critical to prevent fixation failure, correction loss, and spinal cord injury.

In this study, sagittal imbalance was corrected by constructing a three-dimensional finite element model of AS to simulate PVCR osteotomies at thoracic 12, lumbar 1, lumbar 2, and lumbar 3 vertebrae. Pelvic parameters (PI, PT, and bilateral hip midpoints) were integrated to predict the osteotomy angle. Four finite element osteotomy internal fixation models were established to assess relative displacement, equivalent stress, and internal fixation stability. Results indicated that as osteotomy segments approach the cranial side, relative displacement and restoration of spinal sagittal alignment increase. However, excessive postoperative displacement significantly compromises the stability of the pedicle screw-rod system, elevating the risk of internal fixation failure. Clinically, the selection of osteotomy segments typically aligns with the desired sagittal force line restoration.

In all four models, the stress on the pedicle screw-rod system was greater than that on the titanium cage and osteotomy contact surfaces, except for model B. This highlights the pedicle screw-rod system’s pivotal role in internal fixation stress distribution. Although all models exhibited stresses below the material yield threshold, studies suggest that patients with AS frequently have severe osteoporosis. Therefore, careful consideration to prevent internal fixation device failure is essential.Among the four osteotomy levels analyzed, L3 osteotomy (Model D) provided the most favorable biomechanical environment with minimal displacement and well-distributed stresses, while T12 osteotomy (Model A) achieved greater correction at the expense of increased instability. These findings highlight the importance of level-specific biomechanical considerations in surgical planning for ASK.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, the model was based on single-patient data, which cannot fully represent the variability among ASK patients. Second, ossified discs and ligaments were simplified to cancellous bone properties, neglecting the cortical-like characteristics of syndesmophytes, potentially underestimating overall stiffness. Third, clinical sagittal parameters such as SVA were not calculated. Fourth, the effect of osteoporosis on fixation strength was not considered. Future studies should include multi-patient models, sensitivity analyses, and more precise material assignments.This study simplified ossified intervertebral discs and ligaments to cancellous bone material parameters, without accounting for the cortical-like properties of syndesmophyte bridges. This assumption may underestimate overall model stiffness and affect the absolute values of stress distribution following osteotomy. Future studies should incorporate more precise material assignments based on microstructural characteristics of ossified tissues.

Clinical implications

This study demonstrates that osteotomy level selection significantly affects postoperative biomechanical stability. L3 osteotomy showed minimal displacement and favorable stress distribution, making it suitable for patients prioritizing stability. T12 osteotomy achieved greater correction but exhibited larger displacement and higher instability risk, appropriate for cases requiring substantial sagittal correction. Surgical decision-making should balance correction magnitude against construct stability based on individual patient characteristics.

Despite this limitation, the present study provides valuable proof-of-concept evidence that osteotomy level selection significantly impacts construct stability and stress distribution, laying the groundwork for more comprehensive, multi-patient analyses in the future.As a proof-of-concept study, these findings provide a biomechanical rationale for level-specific osteotomy selection in ASK, but they require validation in larger, more diverse patient cohorts and through systematic sensitivity analyses that account for variability in bone quality and deformity characteristics. Such future work will be essential to translate these preliminary insights into evidence-based clinical guidelines.

Conclusion

This finite element study demonstrates that L3 osteotomy yields superior stability compared to T12 osteotomy, though the conclusion is based on a single-case model and requires further validation through multicase studies. These findings underscore the importance of considering segment-specific biomechanical factors in surgical planning for ASK patients.
