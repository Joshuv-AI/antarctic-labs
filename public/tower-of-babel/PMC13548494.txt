Enhanced identification of key bacterial motility genes via a cross-species genomic hybrid feature machine learning approach
Authors: Peicheng Lu, Qingyi Guo, Leyu Li, Muhammad Zubair, Guomin Han, Ying Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Efficient and accurate identification of functional genes is critical to biological research, yet traditional single-species approaches are often limited by low efficiency. Previously, we established a novel method for identifying key genes using cross-species protein domain features and machine learning. However, the high multiplicity of gene members associated with specific domains creates a substantial workload for subsequent experimental validation. To address this, this study proposes an enhanced approach that integrates EggNOG-based protein sequence annotation with domain analysis. Unannotated sequences are subsequently analyzed for protein domains, generating a comprehensive “direct gene annotation plus domain” hybrid feature matrix. While the hybrid matrix model yielded comparable predictive accuracy, it significantly enhanced feature resolution: the top 50 predicted features were all known motility-related genes or domains. Furthermore, among the top 100 ranked features, 58 are confirmed to be directly related to motility based on experimental evidence. Although strict genus-level control still yielded 51 confirmed features, excessive taxonomic restriction drastically reduces the number of training genomes, which may paradoxically impair identification efficiency. These results demonstrate that the new method effectively reduces the subsequent experimental workload and enables high-throughput identification of functional genes in a single analysis. With accuracy and efficiency far exceeding those of existing single-species identification methods, it provides a highly efficient solution for mining key genes underlying other complex bacterial phenotypes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13083-1.

Introduction

Since the rediscovery of Mendelian genetics in 1900, the field of genetics has witnessed significant progress [1]. Two primary strategies, forward genetics and reverse genetics, are employed for functional gene localization. Forward genetics initiates with a specific biological phenomenon or phenotype and seeks to identify potential genes through methods such as mutagenesis, without requiring prior assumptions regarding molecular mechanisms. Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) represent a significant application of forward genetics, identifying phenotype-associated genes by analyzing genetic variation characteristics within populations [2–4]. Conversely, reverse genetics begins with a known gene and investigates its function by altering its expression or sequence through techniques like gene knockout or gene silencing, subsequently observing the resulting phenotypic changes. Although the functions of many genes have been identified, existing methods based on single species are typically complex, time-consuming, and expensive, leaving the functions of a vast number of genes unknown [5]. Therefore, the development of more efficient methods for functional gene identification is of great importance.

Rapid advancements in bioinformatics and sequencing technologies have propelled the widespread application of machine learning in biological research. Machine learning is capable of processing complex datasets with low cost and high efficiency, achieving remarkable success in areas such as disease prediction and drug development [6–8]. Decreasing sequencing costs and the accumulation of phenotypic data are unlocking the potential of machine learning in bacterial phenotype research [9]. For instance, existing studies have utilized machine learning to accurately predict bacterial anaerobic/aerobic characteristics, optimal growth temperatures, Gram stain phenotypes, and adaptation to optimal pH values [10–13]. Our research group has achieved a technological breakthrough by introducing machine learning into the field of functional gene identification, successfully establishing a method to rapidly determine key regulatory genes for bacterial rod shape using multi-species genomic information. We experimentally verified the critical roles of genes such as pal and mreB in maintaining the rod shape of Escherichia coli [14]. However, our newly established method possesses certain limitations when analyzing multi-species protein domains and screening for corresponding genes in target species. For example, the excessive number of genes or gene family members corresponding to certain important domains leads to a massive workload for subsequent experiments. Furthermore, due to the aforementioned high multiplicity of members, the number of key genes predicted by the new method may be restricted, suggesting room for further improvement.

Bacterial motility is crucial for survival and reproduction. Bacterial movement on solid surfaces, such as swarming motility, typically relies on type IV pili, involving multiple genes such as pilA and pilB [15, 16]. Additionally, several regulatory genes, including suhB [17], retS [18], moaB1 [19], and pdeL [20], participate in the regulation of bacterial motility. In-depth research on bacterial motility-related genes provides excellent validation conditions for the development of novel functional gene localization methods.

This study aims to improve existing methods by designing a more accurate and efficient approach for functional gene identification based on multi-species genomic and phenotypic data. Building upon the original domain-based analysis, this new method introduces EggNOG to annotate protein sequences prior to domain analysis [21]. Sequences that are not annotated are then analyzed using protein domains. Information from both sources is combined to form a Gene-Domain Hybrid matrix (Fig. 1) to accurately screen a large number of key regulatory genes and domain-corresponding genes affecting bacterial motility. This provides technical support for the efficient and accurate identification of key genes in other common bacterial phenotypes.

Methods

The overall experimental design and analysis workflow of this study is illustrated in Fig. 1. Initially, bacterial genome sequences and corresponding motility phenotype data were retrieved from the NCBI RefSeq and BacDive databases [22, 23], respectively. Following rigorous data filtering and redundancy removal, protein sequences were extracted. To comprehensively capture genomic features, we performed dual functional annotation using Pfam_scan and EggNOG [21, 24]. Based on these annotations, three distinct feature datasets were constructed: the Protein Domain matrix, the Gene Frequency matrix, and the proposed Gene-Domain Hybrid matrix. Subsequently, four machine learning algorithms—Decision Tree (DT), Conditional Inference Tree (CTree), Naive Bayes (NB), and Random Forest (RF)—were employed to construct predictive models [25–28]. Model performance was evaluated using Accuracy, Recall, and the Kappa coefficient [29, 30]. Finally, the RF algorithm [14], which demonstrated superior performance, was utilized to rank feature importance, thereby precisely identifying key genes associated with bacterial motility.

Data sources

Bacterial nomenclature information was downloaded from the NCBI RefSeq database. Additionally, bacterial motility information was retrieved based on the BacDive database. Files containing bacterial names with both genomic and motility information were compared, resulting in a final dataset of 3,123 species (as of December 2023). Bacterial motility was categorized as “motile” (1,328 species) and “non-motile” (1,795 species). Based on the motility data of these 3,123 bacteria, a protein domain matrix was constructed and sorted by genus and species. To mitigate sample bias and data redundancy caused by the over-representation of specific species, a deduplication process was performed, resulting in a final subset of approximately 2,000 representative samples for subsequent machine learning modeling and analysis.

To control for phylogenetic structure and minimize data leakage resulting from shared ancestry, we further deduplicated the data at the genus level. By randomly selecting one representative species from each genus, we generated a phylogenetically balanced dataset comprising 446 unique genera. Furthermore, we evaluated and visualized the taxonomic composition of the training and test sets at the phylum level using stacked bar charts to ensure that the data partition was balanced across different taxonomic groups (Figure S1).

Protein sequence annotation and domain analysis

Protein domains for each bacterial genome were analyzed using a local version of the Pfam-A database (v33.0) and pfam_scan software with default parameters. Additionally, the EggNOG 5.0 database was used for gene function annotation using default parameters.

Feature matrix construction

Three types of feature matrices were constructed: (1) Protein Domain Frequency Matrix: This matrix tallied the frequency of each protein domain type within each bacterium [10, 11, 14]. (2) Gene Frequency Matrix: This matrix recorded the occurrence frequency of different genes annotated by EggNOG in each bacterium. (3) Gene-Domain Hybrid Matrix: This matrix integrated EggNOG annotation information by first calculating gene frequencies. Excluding annotated gene sequences, unannotated partial sequences were annotated using pfam_scan, and protein domain frequencies were calculated. The frequencies from both analysis methods were merged to construct the Gene-Domain Hybrid matrix.

In the initial dataset of 2,000 samples, the total number of features for the three matrices was 9,721, 19,740, and 29,379, respectively. Subsequently, to construct the phylogenetically balanced dataset and reduce computational noise, we retained only the 446 unique genera. A strict filtering threshold was applied, where only features appearing in at least 50 bacterial genomes were retained for model construction. Following this rigorous filtering, the feature counts for the three matrices were reduced to 1,881, 3,737, and 6,313, respectively.

In this study, the raw occurrence frequencies of features were utilized directly without normalization by genome size. This approach was chosen to fully preserve the original biological variation and the natural distribution of feature counts.

Machine learning model training

Machine learning models were trained and optimized using DT, CTREE, NB, and RF algorithms. Specifically, the R packages “e1071 (v1.7-17)“ [31], “rpart (v4.1.27)“ [32], “party (v1.3-20)“ [33], “pROC (v1.19.0.1)” ,“caret (v7.0-1)”, “dplyr (v1.1.4)”, “tidyr (v1.3.1)”, “PRROC (v1.4)” and “randomForest (v4.7-1.2)“ [34] were utilized for model construction.

RF hyperparameters were set according to the method of Liu et al. [14]. During the RF model training, key hyperparameters were set to optimize model performance and analytical efficiency. We explicitly set the number of trees (ntree) to 1000. This value was chosen to balance model stability and computational efficiency: a sufficient number of trees reduces the model’s random fluctuations, ensuring the reliability of feature importance assessment, while avoiding excessive computational overhead. Additionally, we enabled feature importance evaluation (importance=TRUE) and sample proximity matrix calculation (proximity=TRUE) to rank the contribution of each feature and analyze sample relationships after model construction; however, these two parameters do not directly affect the model’s predictive performance. For other core hyperparameters, such as the number of features randomly sampled at each split (mtry) and the minimum size of terminal nodes (nodesize), we used the default values provided in the R package (i.e., mtry is the square root of the total number of features, and nodesize is 1 for classification tasks). The use of default values aimed to simplify and standardize the analytical pipeline, which not only improved computational efficiency but also ensured the comparability and reproducibility of the results across different analysis batches.

For the other machine learning algorithms evaluated in this study—including Naive Bayes and Conditional Inference Trees—we utilized the robust, practice-validated default hyperparameters provided by their respective R packages. Specifically, for the Decision Tree model, a complexity parameter (cp.) of 0.01 was set for standard pruning to effectively prevent model overfitting.

Model evaluation

Model performance was evaluated using Accuracy, Recall, Kappa coefficient [35], Precision, F1-score, ROC-AUC and PR-AUC. The Kappa coefficient measures the agreement between the model’s prediction and a random prediction, with values ranging from 0 to 1, where higher values indicate better agreement.

To systematically identify key features associated with bacterial motility, we ranked the features of the three models in descending order based on their Permutation Importance scores for the “Motile” category. Simultaneously, we calculated standard Random Forest evaluation metrics for the genus-unique dataset, including Mean Decrease Accuracy and Mean Decrease Gini. The stability of feature rankings was assessed via 10-fold cross-validation, with results reported as Mean ± SD to ensure the statistical robustness of our findings.

Additionally, to strictly evaluate the model’s generalization capability and eliminate bias introduced by a single data split, we performed 10 independent random partitions of the training and test sets on the genus-unique dataset. The overall performance metrics across these multiple iterations were aggregated and reported as Mean ± SD.

Evaluation of candidate gene identification efficacy

Referring to our group’s earlier hypothesis—specifically, constructing an interpretive model using the “importance” function of the RF algorithm—annotated genes or gene sequences corresponding to domains with relatively high contributions in the model were selected as candidate genes. These candidate genes were compared with experimentally verified known motility-related genes to evaluate the model’s efficacy.

Results

Gene identification via existing domain matrix models

Our project previously established a gene identification method based on protein domains [10]. This method uses pfam_scan to analyze protein domains, constructs a dataset of domain counts for each bacterium, and combines this with phenotypic data for machine learning-based prediction. This approach successfully identified genes determining bacterial rod shape. Building on this foundation, this study further evaluates its effectiveness in identifying bacterial flagellar motility genes.

Model construction via domain matrix

Motility records for 3,123 bacterial species were retrieved from the BacDive database. To mitigate the impact of uneven data distribution, we subdivided bacterial genera and species and randomly reduced data from genera with an excessive number of bacteria. Ultimately, 2,000 datasets were selected and divided into training and test sets at a 3:1 ratio. Model accuracy results showed that the RF demonstrated high accuracy (87.91%) and stability on the test set, along with a high Kappa coefficient (Figure S2). The RF algorithm also exhibited excellent recall rates for both motile and non-motile bacteria in the training and test sets. Therefore, the RF algorithm demonstrates robust stability and classification performance in this model.

To further control for phylogenetic structure and minimize data leakage from shared ancestry, we performed an analysis using the genus-unique dataset of 446 genera. On this phylogenetically balanced dataset, we first evaluated the Protein Domain matrix (Fig. 2A, B). The Random Forest model achieved an Accuracy of 81.25%, an AUC of 0.81, a PR-AUC of 0.85, an F1-score of 82.93%, Precision of 77.27%, and Recall of 89.47%. Despite the reduced sample size and stricter phylogenetic control, the performance remained stable. We analyzed the confusion matrices of each machine learning model on the independent test set (n = 112). The results indicated that the model correctly identified 51 motile samples and 40 non-motile samples, with 15 false positives and 6 false negatives (Figure S3).

Candidate gene screening via domain matrix

We employed the “importance” function of the RF algorithm to evaluate and rank the classification importance of protein domains. Ten-fold cross-validation (Fig. 3A) determined that the top 100 domains were critical for reducing the model’s error rate. We selected the top 100 most important protein domains (Table S1) and analyzed their corresponding genes in E. coli (Table S2). Among these, domains related to bacterial flagellar motility or chemotaxis, such as Flagellin_N, FHIPEP, ATP.synt_ab, and CheW, received particular attention. Heatmap analysis (Figure S4) revealed that 10 protein domains, including MCPsignal and HAMP, were significantly more abundant in motile bacteria. The functions of these domains are all potentially associated with bacterial flagellar motility or chemotaxis.

Enhancing model accuracy to improve gene identification

By introducing EggNOG gene annotation, we constructed a gene frequency matrix and a Gene-Domain Hybrid matrix to improve identification accuracy. We evaluated the performance of the new method using the RF algorithm.

Effectiveness of gene matrix alone

We evaluated the performance of gene matrix models with different data sizes using the RF algorithm. The results showed that as the data size decreased, the overall accuracy of the model tended to decline (Table S4). In terms of importance ranking for motility-related genes, the model using the original dataset performed best. The training and test set accuracies for the original dataset, 1/2 dataset, and 1/4 dataset models all exceeded 80%, indicating relatively accurate training and prediction results. The accuracies of the remaining four models all reached above 70%, also demonstrating a certain degree of accuracy. Regarding the Kappa coefficient, the training and test set Kappa coefficients for the original dataset, 1/2 dataset, and 1/4 dataset models were all above 0.61, representing a high degree of agreement.

The top 100 genes ranked by models of different data sizes are shown in Table S5. The supplementary table lists all known regulatory and functional genes related to bacterial flagellar motility, totaling 94 flagellar motility genes. Among them, filG, tsr_1, pilA1, pilY2, PilZ, and pilS/pilR were absent in the gene matrices of models with different sample sizes, while the remaining 87 genes were present across all models. The model with the highest accuracy ranking for bacterial flagellar motility genes was the original dataset model, wherein the top 41 genes were all closely related to flagellar motility.

On the genus-unique dataset of 446 genera, the Gene Matrix model achieved an Accuracy of 83.93%, an AUC of 0.84, a PR-AUC of 0.90, an F1-score of 84.75%, Precision of 81.97%, and Recall of 87.72% (Fig. 2C, D).

Confusion matrix analysis (Figure S5) revealed that, compared to the Protein Domain Matrix, the Gene Matrix model significantly improved the identification specificity for non-motile genera. In the independent test set, the Random Forest model correctly classified 50 motile genera and 44 non-motile genera. The number of false positives was substantially reduced to 11, while the number of false negatives remained low (7). Among the top 100 ranked genes, 45 have been confirmed by published experimental evidence to be directly related to flagellar motility or chemotaxis (Table S6).

Effectiveness of Gene-Domain Hybrid Matrix

We constructed a Gene-Domain Hybrid matrix to evaluate the performance of models with different data sizes. Results showed that the accuracy and Kappa coefficients for the original dataset, 1/2 dataset, and 1/8 dataset models were relatively high (Table S7). Among the top 100 features of the original dataset and 1/2 dataset models (Table S8), the number of known related genes and domains exceeded half, and other models also maintained a certain proportion of related features. Notably, using 2,000 bacterial genomes for gene identification, the top 50 genes or domains directly obtained from the importance ranking were all known genes or domains related to motility (Table S9). Furthermore, among the features ranked 51–100, there were 8 additional known motility-related genes or domains, further indicating a significant improvement in the accuracy of the enhanced gene identification method. Comparing the number of genes identified in the top 50 and top 100 Gene-Domain rankings reveals that the new method significantly reduces the number of candidate genes compared to the original domain-based method. This demonstrates that the improved method not only enhances accuracy but also significantly reduces the candidate gene pool Fig. 4C.

On the genus-unique dataset of 446 genera, the Gene-Domain Hybrid matrix yielded an Accuracy of 84.82%, an AUC of 0.85, a PR-AUC of 0.89, an F1-score of 85.71%, Precision of 82.26%, and Recall of 89.47% (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Bacterial motility prediction model

Bacterial motility, a ubiquitous characteristic essential for cell survival and reproduction, enables migration toward more favorable environments. Koblitz et al. also employed genomic protein domain features to accurately predict various phenotypes, including motility, achieving an AUPR and F1-score of 0.88 and 0.86, respectively [12]. They identified major protein families related to flagellar motility in 327 strains. However, analysis methods based solely on protein families yield an excessive number of candidate genes, wasting substantial experimental resources. This study aimed to accurately predict bacterial motility using genomic information. By analyzing protein domains from over 3,000 bacteria and combining this with motility data from the BacDive database, we constructed prediction models using four machine learning algorithms. Results showed that the RF algorithm performed best on the test set (accuracy 87.91%, Kappa coefficient 0.76), demonstrating good stability and predictive capability, superior to other algorithms such as CTree. Our prediction accuracy using protein domain features is fundamentally consistent with that reported by Koblitz et al. [12].

To enhance model performance, we introduced EggNOG gene annotation to construct a gene frequency matrix and a Gene-Domain Hybrid matrix, progressively reducing training data volume for model evaluation. Results indicated that the Gene-Domain Hybrid matrix model based on 2,000 bacterial genomes performed best (accuracy 88.44%, Kappa coefficient 0.77), slightly outperforming the Domain-only and Gene-only matrix models (Table S7). This model showed slight improvements in both accuracy and recall, suggesting that a more complete matrix structure and multi-dimensional information better capture complex gene patterns associated with motility.

Crucially, to strictly verify whether the high predictive performance of this study stems from genuine biological functional signals—rather than phylogenetic autocorrelation (i.e., data leakage caused by evolutionary kinship among closely related strains)—we conducted a comprehensive and in-depth evaluation of the model using the phylogenetically balanced dataset containing only 446 unique genera. The results show that the Gene-Domain Hybrid model maintains extreme robustness even under conditions of significantly reduced sample sizes. In the single data-split evaluation, the model achieved an accuracy of 84.82% and a PR-AUC of 0.89 (Table S11). To further eliminate potential bias from a single random split, we executed 10 independent random partitioning operations. Across these 10 iterations, the Gene-Domain Hybrid model exhibited considerable stability, with a mean accuracy of 83.87% ± 3.16%, a mean F1-score of 84.25 ± 2.99%, and a PR-AUC consistently at 0.865 ± 0.031 (Table S12).

Although there was a slight decrease in performance compared to the initial dataset of 2,000 samples (88.44% accuracy), the ability to maintain such excellent evaluation metrics after strictly eliminating genus-level taxonomic redundancy provides compelling evidence: our method captures highly conserved biological functional patterns rather than merely phylogenetic signatures. Furthermore, the confusion matrix analysis confirmed that the hybrid strategy optimally mitigated class imbalance, thereby significantly reducing mispredictions. Ultimately, these highly consistent results across multiple random partitions demonstrate the model’s robustness and its powerful generalization capability across diverse bacterial lineages.

As early as 2012, researchers used the NB algorithm to analyze 378 bacterial genomes, predicting oxygen requirement-related phenotypes with accuracies of 88% for anaerobes, 87% for aerobes, and 35% for facultative anaerobes [36]. Members of our project group also utilized a larger dataset of bacterial genomes and phenotypes (1,813 bacterial genomes) to classify bacterial aerobic types, achieving 90.62% accuracy [11]. A recent study utilizing sequence-based functional annotations from the RAST algorithm achieved extremely high accuracy (> 99%) for Gram stain phenotype prediction, with an average accuracy of 81.5% for respiratory types [37]. In our previous domain-based model, the accuracy for predicting bacterial shape reached 94.76%, with a Kappa coefficient of 0.93 [14]. Compared to previous studies, the hybrid model in this study exhibits greater balance in classification problems. Although the motility model’s accuracy is slightly lower than that of other trait prediction models, it retains high predictive capability.

Accuracy of the novel gene identification method

Traditional methods for identifying bacterial functional genes are time-consuming and labor-intensive, making the development of more efficient approaches crucial. Our research group previously validated the feasibility of using candidate genes derived from machine learning by overexpressing specific candidates identified through bacterial aerobic type classification in facultative E. coli [11]. Building on this foundation, we applied a similar method to identify genes associated with rod-shaped morphology. Experimental validation clearly confirmed two key genes, pal and mreB, thereby directly substantiating the viability of this novel functional gene identification approach [14]. However, a significant technical challenge remained: a single domain identified by the method could correspond to anywhere from one to dozens of gene members, resulting in a substantial workload for experimental validation.

In this study, we introduced a novel gene functional annotation strategy to improve upon the existing methodology, enabling the direct identification of a large number of key regulatory genes and domains influencing bacterial motility. This effectively enhanced both the accuracy and prediction efficiency of the method. The Gene-Domain Hybrid matrix model not only yielded a slight improvement in model accuracy but, more importantly, the top 50 features predicted by this model were all known motility-related genes or domains—representing an increase of nine compared to the result obtained from the gene matrix alone (Table S5 and Table S8). Among the top 100 ranked important genes, 58 are directly associated with motility. Furthermore, even under strict phylogenetic control using the genus-unique dataset, 51 of the top 100 ranked features (domains or genes) were confirmed by published experimental evidence to be directly associated with flagellar motility or chemotaxis (Table S10). This may be due to the fact that the overly strict genus-level restriction drastically reduced the number of genomes used for training, which could conversely be detrimental to the efficiency of functional gene identification.

The capacity to identify a substantial number of known motility-related key genes via direct annotation demonstrates that the new method more precisely recognizes genes with prior experimental validation. By filtering out these previously verified genes, the subsequent experimental workload can be significantly minimized. Furthermore, our candidate list may encompass instances of “old genes with new functions” as well as novel candidate sequences identified through the domain recognition method. Current results indicate that the improved method vastly increases the efficiency of functional identification for complex bacterial traits. It mitigates the issue of excessive validation workload caused by the “one-to-many” domain-gene correspondence inherent in the existing method. This approach enables the identification of a greater number of functional genes in a single analysis, with accuracy and efficiency significantly outperforming current single-species identification techniques. In summary, our improved method provides a highly efficient solution for identifying the majority of key genes associated with other complex phenotypes of interest.

It should be noted that the construction of the hybrid feature matrix in this study inherently relies on, and to some extent leverages, the established biological knowledge curated within functional annotation databases such as EggNOG and Pfam. Nevertheless, by integrating multidimensional genomic information, our approach effectively filters and ranks biologically plausible candidate genes, thereby enabling precise targeting and significantly mitigating the workload for downstream experimental validation.

Conclusions

By integrating direct gene annotation with protein domain features, we developed a hybrid machine learning framework that overcomes the “one-to-many” mapping bottleneck inherent in traditional domain analysis. This approach enables high-throughput, precise identification of bacterial motility genes, with feature resolution and efficiency far surpassing existing single-species methods. Ultimately, this robust strategy facilitates the rapid decoding of the genetic architecture underlying complex bacterial phenotypes across diverse lineages.
