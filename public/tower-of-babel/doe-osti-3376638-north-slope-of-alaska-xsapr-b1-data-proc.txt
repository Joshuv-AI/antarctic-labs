1.0 Introduction
 The U.S. Department of Energy’s Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) User Facility operates an
 X-band Scanning ARM Precipitation Radar (XSAPR) at the North Slope of Alaska (NSA) atmospheric
 observatory in Utqiaġvik. This radar provides year-round dual-polarization measurements of snow and ice
 in an arctic environment. Over the past few years, there has been greater focus on the NSA XSAPR, and
 recent efforts by ARM staff have resulted in quality-controlled, b-level data (Rocque et al. 2026b). This
 report outlines the processing of the second set of b-level data released for this radar from May 2025
 through April 2026 following similar methods from Rocque et al. (2026b).

 1.1 Radar Performance
 The percentage of data files available from the NSA XSAPR from May 2025 through April 2026 is
 shown in Figure 1. Several of the daily outages are related to transmitter or software faults. Towards the
 end of December 2025, the radar began experiencing issues with the elevation where it would periodically
 get stuck. The elevation encoder completely failed at the beginning of January, and despite site visits in
 February and March, the radar remained offline for the remainder of this period. The radar was operating
 as expected for about 60% of the time between May 2025 and April 2026, but performance increases to
 around 88% when only looking at operations from May through December 2025. The temperature and
 precipitation patterns were similar to last year (Rocque et al. 2026b), with temperatures above 0°C from
 mid-June through mid-September and the most frequent precipitation occurring during the late summer
 and fall.

 1.2 Outline for b1 Processing
 As for other ARM radars, a-level data from the NSA XSAPR is analyzed and cross-compared with other
 instruments to estimate reflectivity and differential reflectivity offsets. A censor mask is also developed to
 more easily identify meteorological echoes. The methods specifically used to generate b-level data from
 the NSA XSAPR have been detailed in Rocque et al. (2026b) and results are summarized below.

2.0 Corrections

 2.1 Reflectivity

 2.1.1 Ground Clutter Stability

 The time series of daily 95th percentile and daily mean total power from ground clutter targets shows the
 radar was fairly stable during this time period (Figure 2). There is a steady increase in the ground clutter
 mean throughout June and a slight shift in the 95th percentile is observed towards the end of June. A
 similar pattern of increasing mean reflectivity in June was observed last year as well. This year, the
 transmitter experienced several faults during June, and a ring of noise likely related to the transmitter was
 first observed on 25 June 2025 that may have caused the slight increase observed in the 95th percentile.
 While this does not impact the ground clutter results, it is important to note the clutter filter was turned off
 on 20 November 2025. Thus, users may see more ground and sea/ice clutter targets with stronger
 reflectivity and near-zero Doppler velocity after this date.

 Figure 2. Time series of daily 95th percentile (magenta cross) and mean (blue dot) total power from
 ground clutter targets from May 2025 through April 2026. The clutter filter was turned off on
 20 November 2025 (dashed vertical line), and the radar was offline due to elevation issues
 starting 5 January 2026.

 2.1.2 Cross-Comparison with Ka-band ARM Zenith Radar (KAZR)

 Following the methodology outlined in Rocque et al. (2026b), the NSA XSAPR reflectivity from
 hemispheric range height indicators (HSRHIs) is cross-compared with the KAZR general sensitivity (GE)

 2

mode reflectivity in non-precipitating ice clouds aloft to estimate the reflectivity offset. The KAZR
reflectivity has been analyzed in depth and it was determined that the radar was well calibrated compared
to the disdrometer during this period (Rocque et al. 2026a). A time series of the daily mean comparisons
between XSAPR and KAZR is shown in Figure 3. Two offset periods are identified, one from 1 May to
25 June 2025 and the other after 25 June. The estimated offset for the first period is 7.4 dB, which is
consistent with results from the previous b1 processing report. During June, the transmitter experienced
frequent faults (as shown in Figure 1), and on 25 June a 3-km-wide ring of noise appeared (mentioned in
the previous b1 report and below in Section 4). While the cross-comparisons with the KAZR are
performed above 4 km (beyond the 3-km ring of noise), the noise may be related to the transmitter ,which
could thus impact the reflectivity. The reflectivity offset for the second period after the end of June is
around 4.1 dB. Histograms of the XSAPR and KAZR reflectivity shown in Figure 4 also show similar
offsets of 7.2 and 4.7 dB for each period.

Average profiles of XSAPR and KAZR reflectivity are also shown for cases from each offset period. For
the 25 May 2025 case, the XSAPR is clearly about 7 dB lower than the KAZR throughout most of the
profile (Figure 5). For the 14 October 2025 case, the XSAPR reflectivity is closer to the KAZR,
especially at lower levels (Figure 6). The profiles diverge in the mid-levels where the difference is around
3-4 dB.

2.2 Differential Reflectivity (ZDR)
The ZDR offset is estimated using two methods, one for drizzle and one for dry snow
(Rocque et al. 2026b). Offsets determined from vertical scans of light rain in the summer are quite
consistent with offsets determined from low-elevation plan position indicators (PPIs) of dry snow. The
average ZDR offset during this analysis period is -1.8 dB which is similar to the offset from last year
(-1.5 dB; Figure 7).

Figure 7. Time series of daily mean ZDR offset from May 2025 through April 2026 calculated using
 the summer, drizzle VPT method (light grey period) and the winter, dry snow PPI method
 (white periods). The radar was offline starting 5 January 2026 (dark grey). The colors of the
 dots indicate the total number of points per day and standard deviations are shown as error
 bars. The mean of the daily mean ZDR offset is -1.8 dB across 88 days.

3.0 Censor Mask
 The censor mask uses velocity texture and the normalized coherent power (NCP) to distinguish signals
 from background noise. Examples of the mask applied to a PPI and an HSRHI are shown in Figures 8 and
 9, respectively. Users are directed to the Corrected Radar Moments in Antenna Coordinates (CMAC)
 Value-Added Product for a more comprehensive mask of hydrometeor and clutter classification
 (Collis et al. 2026).

4.0 Data Quality Issues
 A few data quality issues have persisted throughout this processing period. These have been documented
 in the previous b1 report, including interference-like radial spikes and a 3-km-wide ring of noise. The
 radial spikes are thought to be processing artifacts as there are no spike signatures in the signal-to-noise
 ratio (SNR) and the engineers did not find any strong interference signals at their last site visit in March
 2026. It is also suspected that the ring of noise may be internal to the radar rather than an external noise
 source. This is likely related to the transmitter, and the strong appearance of the ring at the end of June
 2025 corresponds to a shift in the estimated reflectivity offsets (Figure 3). The cause of these artifacts are
 still under investigation. Examples of these data quality issues are shown in Figure 10. Users should
 exercise caution when using all data within 3 km of the radar and reflectivity and ZDR data at low
 elevations as they may be impacted by these artifacts.

 Figure 10. a-b) PPIs at 0.0° elevation on 19 August 2025 at 2019 UTC showing a) clutter-filtered
 reflectivity (dBZ), and b) SNR (dB), highlighting the interference-like radial spikes. c-d)
 HSRHIs at 52.0° azimuth on 30 December 2025 at 09 UTC showing c) reflectivity (dBZ),
 and d) SNR (dB), highlighting the ring of noise within 3 km of the radar.
