A long first intron of PROS1 modulates protein S expression across tissues in mice and humans
Authors: Keiko Maruyama, Sheng Ye, Yuka Eura, Akihiro Tsuji, Yoshitaka Fujihara, Koichi Kokame
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Elements within PROS1 intron 1 modulate PS expression across tissues.•Rare intron 1 variants reduce reporter activity and may contribute to PS deficiency.

Introduction

Introns are widespread in eukaryotic genes. In addition to their roles in splicing,1 some introns harbor regulatory elements that influence transcription, RNA processing, nuclear export, transcript stability, or translation.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Disease-associated variants are also enriched in noncoding regions, where they can alter regulatory function and contribute to pathogenesis.8, 9, 10, 11, 12

Protein S (PS) is a vitamin K–dependent glycoprotein encoded by the PROS1 gene that acts as a cofactor for activated protein C in the regulation of activated coagulation factors V and VIII and for tissue factor pathway inhibitor in the regulation of activated coagulation factor X.13, 14, 15 In addition, PS functions as a ligand for Tyro3/Axl/Mertk receptors and regulates phagocytosis of apoptotic cells, immune and inflammatory responses, tumor cell metastasis, and angiogenesis.16, 17, 18 Hepatocytes are the major sources of circulating plasma PS,19 and PS is also expressed in several other cell types (eg, megakaryocytes and vascular endothelial cells).20, 21, 22 Platelet PS limits thrombin generation in venous but not arterial thrombi.23 PS deficiency increases the risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE), and heterozygous PROS1 pathogenic variants are associated with an increased VTE risk.24,25 Homozygous or compound heterozygous PS deficiency is a rare disorder that causes neonatal purpura fulminans. In mice, complete deficiency is embryonically lethal, whereas partial deficiency predisposes to thrombosis and vascular abnormalities.26, 27, 28

Despite these well-established clinical consequences, conventional genetic testing targeting PROS1 coding exons and exon-intron boundaries fails to identify causal variants in a substantial proportion of individuals with clinically defined PS deficiency. Large cohort studies have reported that approximately half of affected families lack detectable coding-region variants with standard testing strategies.29,30 This diagnostic gap suggests that pathogenic variation in noncoding regulatory regions of PROS1 may be underappreciated and motivates a systematic analysis of the noncoding architecture that controls PS expression.

Human PROS1 is located at 3q11.2, spans ∼100 kb, and contains an exceptionally long first intron (46 266 bp). Using the Human Genome Variation Society nomenclature, c. notation is referenced to NM_000313.4 (c.1 = A of the initiator ATG; intron 1 = c.76+1-c.76+46266), and genomic coordinates follow GRCh38.p14 (NC_000003.12). A highly homologous pseudogene, PROS2P, lies nearby and shares 96% sequence identity.31, 32, 33 Notably, PROS2P lacks the promoter and first exon.

The unusual length of intron 1, together with its conserved first-intron architecture across mammals despite limited primary-sequence conservation and its enrichment for regulatory annotations, suggests that this region may harbor key cis-acting elements that tune PROS1 transcription. Therefore, in this study, we focused on a systematic functional dissection of intron 1 as a tractable first step toward defining the noncoding regulatory architecture of PROS1 while recognizing that additional regulatory elements in other noncoding regions (eg, upstream enhancers or more distal introns) are also likely to contribute to PS regulation.

Here, we investigated whether PROS1 intron 1 sequences contribute to PS regulation. We mapped intron 1 regulatory segments with luciferase reporters, deleted most of intron 1 in HepG2 cells and mice, and examined a patient with PS deficiency who was negative by conventional coding-region sequencing. These approaches indicate that intron 1 modulates PS expression in vitro and in vivo and that rare intronic variation can alter PS expression.

Methods

Additional experimental details are provided in supplemental Methods.

Cloning of PROS1 promoter and intron 1 reporter constructs

The PROS1 promoter fragment (c.−2000_−2, relative to the translation start site) was cloned into pGL3-Basic to generate a promoter-only control vector.34 Intron 1 inserts were designed in 2 stages: an initial set of ∼2-kb tiles with ∼500-bp overlaps spanning the full intron (c.76+1_76+46266), followed by finer subtiles (∼500 bp) and shorter segments within regions of interest. Inserts were cloned downstream of the firefly luciferase open reading frame (ORF). Transversion mutations were introduced by site-directed mutagenesis, and all constructs were sequence verified. Oligonucleotides for plasmid construction are listed in supplemental Table 1.

Luciferase reporter assay in HepG2 cells

Human hepatoma HepG2 cells were transiently transfected with PROS1 firefly luciferase reporter plasmids together with a Renilla control plasmid. Luciferase activity was measured 48 hours later with a dual-luciferase assay, and firefly luminescence was normalized to Renilla. Relative luciferase activity was expressed relative to the promoter-only control (set to 1).

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated deletion of regulatory regions within PROS1 intron 1 in HepG2 cells

Guide RNAs flanking the target regions in PROS1 intron 1 were designed and cloned into a Cas9 expression vector. For engineered deletions, HepG2 cells were cotransfected with guide RNA/Cas9 plasmids and the corresponding homology-directed repair donor constructs, selected with puromycin, and screened by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The target region of established cells was verified by Sanger sequencing. Three independent clones for each genotype were isolated, expanded, and analyzed. Oligonucleotides for plasmid construction are listed in supplemental Table 2.

Generation of PROS1 intron 1–deleted mice

All animal experiments were approved by the animal care and use committees of the National Cerebral and Cardiovascular Center (approval number 25001). Pros1 intron 1–deleted mice were generated by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing of fertilized BDF1 oocytes as described previously.35 Founder and offspring genotypes were confirmed by PCR.

Preparation of mouse plasma and platelets

Whole blood was collected from anesthetized mice into 3.2% citrate. Platelet-rich plasma was prepared by low-speed centrifugation, platelets were pelleted and washed, and platelet-free plasma was obtained by additional centrifugation. Platelets were lysed in sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis sample buffer for immunoblot analysis.

Isolation of mouse hepatocytes, cardiac fibroblasts, and lung vascular endothelial cells

Hepatocytes, lung vascular endothelial cells, and cardiac fibroblasts were isolated from mice by enzymatic digestion and enrichment procedures as described previously.36, 37, 38 Briefly, hepatocytes were obtained by liver perfusion and Percoll enrichment, lung endothelial cells by collagenase digestion followed by CD31 bead enrichment and CD102-based sorting, and cardiac fibroblasts by enzymatic dissociation and adherent culture.

Digital PCR for quantification of PS mRNA

Total RNA was extracted from mouse tissues or isolated cells, reverse transcribed to complementary DNA, and analyzed by digital PCR using TaqMan assays for PS and TATA-box binding protein. Relative PS mRNA was calculated as PS/TATA-box binding protein mRNA and reflects total PS transcripts detected by the assay, without distinguishing splice isoforms.

Quantification of secreted PS in HepG2 cell culture supernatants

HepG2 cells were cultured in serum-containing medium supplemented with vitamin K1, and conditioned media were collected. Secreted human PS was quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and normalized to total cellular protein from the corresponding wells.

Quantification of mouse plasma PS antigen by ELISA

Mouse plasma PS antigen was quantified by ELISA using a monoclonal capture antibody and pooled wild-type (WT) plasma as the calibration standard.

Western blot analysis

Platelet PS was assessed by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and western blotting with an anti–mouse PS antibody. Signal intensities were quantified, and equal loading was confirmed by total protein staining.

Genetic analysis of PROS1 in PS deficiency

PROS1 was analyzed by long-range PCR followed by Oxford Nanopore long-read sequencing, as described previously.39 Amplicons covered the full PROS1 genomic region, including all exons and introns (supplemental Figure 1; supplemental Table 3). Reads were analyzed using software tools, including the newly developed TaKoHigh40 and Sniffles,41 and candidate variants were validated by PCR-direct Sanger sequencing. This study was approved by the research ethics committee of the National Cerebral and Cardiovascular Center (approval number M29-108-6) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 10. Tests are specified in the figure legends, and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Effects of PROS1 intron 1 sequences on promoter-driven luciferase activity

To examine whether intron 1 sequences influence transcription from the PROS1 promoter, we constructed a reporter in which the PROS1 promoter (c.−2000_−2) was placed upstream of the firefly luciferase ORF in pGL3-Basic, and intron 1 tiles (∼2 kb) were inserted downstream of the ORF (Figure 1A). Intron 1 was first tiled into 28 ∼2-kb units with ∼500-bp overlaps. Relative luciferase activity, defined as firefly/Renilla normalized to the promoter-only control (pGL3-PROS1[c.−2000_−2; no intron insert]), was reduced for most inserts. In contrast, 5 tiles were not reduced relative to the promoter-only control (Figure 1B, red bars): 2 in the 5′ intron 1 interval (c.76+1_76+2000 and c.76+1501_76+3500) and 3 in a distal interval centered on the 36-kb region of intron 1 (c.76+34499_76+36500, c.76+36001_76+38000, and c.76+42001_76+44000), hereafter referred to as the distal 36-kb intron 1 interval. Therefore, we subdivided these regions into ∼500-bp subtiles and retested them. Four subtiles enhanced luciferase activity relative to the promoter-only control (supplemental Figure 2, black bars): 2 in the 5′ intron 1 interval (c.76+1_76+480 and c.76+381_76+860) and 2 in the distal 36-kb intron 1 interval (c.76+35282_76+35765 and c.76+35661_76+36163). One tile, c.76+34499_76+36500, showed greater variability in this coarse screen (Figure 1); however, we did not rely on this single construct for functional assignment but instead interpreted this region in the context of the overlapping subtiles c.76+35282_76+35765 and c.76+35661_76+36163, which reproducibly showed regulatory activity in the fine-tiling assay (supplemental Figure 2).

To refine candidate activator elements, we introduced block-transversion substitutions into the positive subtiles. The c.76+381_76+490 subtile enhanced activity (Figure 2A, red bars), and this effect was reduced by block-transversion substitutions spanning c.76+431_76+437 and c.76+438_76+450 (Figure 2A, green bars). Likewise, c.76+760_76+860 enhanced activity, and the effect was counteracted by a block transversion spanning c.76+850_76+860 (Figure 2B). The c.76+35976_76+36076 subtile enhanced activity, which was reduced by block-transversions at both c.76+35985_76+36004 and c.76+36020_76+36030 (Figure 2C). These results delineate putative enhancer-like segments within the 5′ intron 1 interval (c.76+431_76+450 and c.76+850_76+860) and the distal 36-kb intron 1 interval (c.76+35976_76+36076).

We also obtained evidence for silencer-like segments. The c.76+1_76+572 subtile did not affect activity (Figure 2D, red bars), but introducing a block transversion spanning c.76+491_76+500 increased activity (Figure 2D, green bars). Although c.76+760_76+860 enhanced activity (Figure 2E, red bars), extending this subtile to c.76+760_76+950 abolished enhancement (Figure 2E, green bars), whereas block transversions spanning c.76+861_76+905 and c.76+906_76+950 did not alter activity (supplemental Figure 3A). In addition, c.76+35875_76+36076 enhanced activity (Figure 2F, red bars), and this effect was reduced by extending to c.76+35875_76+36163 (Figure 2F, green bars); a block transversion spanning c.76+36107_76+36163 slightly enhanced activity (supplemental Figure 3B). Together, these data suggest silencer-like segments within the 5′ intron 1 interval (c.76+491_76+500 and c.76+861_76+950) and the distal 36-kb intron 1 interval (c.76+36077_76+36163). Thus, intron 1 sequences both enhance and repress transcription from the PROS1 promoter in this reporter context. In the distal 36-kb region, the reporter assay data are most consistent with a composite regulatory interval rather than a single uniformly activating or repressive element.

Taken together, we refined 6 short intron 1 segments that modulate promoter activity: 4 in the 5′ intron 1 interval (c.76+431_76+450 and c.76+850_76+860, enhancer-like; c.76+491_76+500 and c.76+861_76+950, silencer-like) and 2 in the distal 36-kb intron 1 interval (c.76+35976_76+36076, enhancer-like; c.76+36077_76+36163, silencer-like; Figure 2). The nucleotide sequences are provided in supplemental Table 4.

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated intron 1 deletions alter endogenous PS mRNA levels in HepG2 cells

To test whether the regulatory regions identified in reporter assays affect endogenous expression, we generated HepG2 clones lacking either candidate regulatory elements or most of PROS1 intron 1. Sanger sequencing of the targeted PROS1 region in all edited clones showed both WT and deleted alleles, consistent with monoallelic editing of the PROS1 locus (Figure 3A). Deletion of most of intron 1 (c.76+310_76+45878del) significantly decreased PS mRNA levels (Figure 3B). Among enhancer-element deletions, the 2 edits in the 5′ intron 1 interval (c.76+399_76+451del and c.76+678_76+857del) had different effects on PS mRNA, with only c.76+399_76+451del reducing expression. Among silencer-element deletions, 2 edits in the 5′ intron 1 interval (c.76+491_76+540del and c.76+862_76+964del) did not alter PS mRNA, whereas deletion in the distal 36-kb intron 1 interval (c.76+36096_76+36149del) increased PS mRNA. We were unable to generate clones harboring a deletion spanning c.76+35976_76+36076. Together, these results indicate that sequences within intron 1 contribute to the regulation of endogenous PS mRNA levels in HepG2 cells.

We next examined whether intron 1 deletions also affected PS production at the protein level. Secreted PS was measurable in HepG2 culture supernatants under our assay conditions. Clones carrying the large intron 1 deletion (c.76+310_76+45878del) showed a marked reduction in secreted PS compared with WT clones (Figure 3C). Both enhancer-element deletions, c.76+399_76+451del and c.76+678_76+857del, also reduced PS secretion, even though only c.76+399_76+451del significantly lowered PS mRNA. Among silencer-element deletions, c.76+491_76+540del showed PS secretion comparable to WT, whereas c.76+862_76+964del and c.76+36096_76+36149del caused only minor changes. These data indicate that intron 1 sequences influence PS expression at both mRNA and protein levels in HepG2 cells.

Intron 1 deletion reduces plasma and platelet PS antigen in mice

To examine the effect of intron 1 on the regulation of PS expression in vivo, we generated intron 1–deleted mice. Intron 1 spans 46 266 bp in humans and 30 896 bp in mice. Two independent deletion strains were established (c.76+134_76+30767del and c.76+135_76+30769del; supplemental Figure 4). Homozygous intron 1–deleted mice were born at frequencies close to the Mendelian ratio and exhibited no gross anatomical abnormalities. Plasma PS antigen levels in homozygotes were significantly reduced to ∼20% to 30% of WT levels (Figure 4A). PS antigen in platelets was also reduced to ∼9% of WT levels, as assessed by quantitative analysis of western blot band intensities (Figure 4B; supplemental Figure 5). Thus, intron 1 is required to maintain PS expression in vivo. The magnitude of PS reduction is broadly consistent with levels observed in heterozygous PS deficiency in humans and Pros1-deficient mice, conditions that are associated with a marked increase in VTE risk in experimental models and in patients.24, 25, 26, 27, 28

Tissue- and cell type–dependent effects of intron 1 deletion on PS mRNA in mice

To examine whether intron 1 regulates PS expression across tissues, we quantified PS mRNA in the liver, lung, heart, and kidney in mice. PS mRNA levels were reduced to ∼10% of WT levels in the liver to ∼50% of WT levels in the lung and kidney and only slightly reduced in the heart (Figure 5A), suggesting tissue-dependent effects of intron 1 deletion.

We next measured PS mRNA in cell types that express PS: hepatocytes from the liver, vascular endothelial cells from the lung, and cardiac fibroblasts from the heart. PS mRNA in hepatocytes was reduced to ∼10% of WT levels, whereas lung vascular endothelial cells showed a more modest decrease to ∼70% of WT levels (a ∼30% reduction). No significant reduction was observed in cardiac fibroblasts (Figure 5B). Thus, the impact of intron 1 deletion on PS mRNA differs by tissue and cell type.

Identification of PROS1 intron 1 deletions in a PS-deficient patient

The patient was a Japanese man aged 62 years who, at the age of 60 years, developed high-risk pulmonary embolism and right lower-extremity proximal deep-vein thrombosis. The acute event occurred after a right leg fracture with immobilization in a cast, representing a strong transient provoking factor. On presentation, laboratory testing revealed PS activity of 44% (normal, 65%-105%) and free and total PS antigens of 30% (normal, 60%-128%) and 59% (normal, 70%-140%), respectively, consistent with type 1 PS deficiency. He was treated with anticoagulation and has remained free of recurrent thrombosis during follow-up while receiving rivaroxaban. His medical history included atrial fibrillation treated with catheter ablation at the age of 49 years, after which anticoagulation had been discontinued.

Initial PCR-direct sequencing (Sanger method) of all PROS1 coding exons and exon-intron boundaries failed to identify pathogenic variants. Long-read sequencing uncovered 2 rare heterozygous intron 1 deletions in cis: NC_000003.12:g.[93938221_93938304del; 93938219del] NM_000313.4:c.[76+35370_76+35453del; 76+35455del] (Figure 6A-C; supplemental Figure 1C). Genome coordinates follow GRCh38.p14. For brevity, this can also be expressed as c.76+35370_76+35455delinsG. These variants are registered in Single Nucleotide Polymorphism Database (dbSNP) as rs1709219428 (c.76+35370_76+35453del) and rs1367473752 (c.76+35455del), with Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) global allele frequencies of 0.0000788 and 0.0000723, respectively. These variants are also registered in the Japanese genetic variation database, ToMMo 61KJPN on jMorp, with allele frequencies of 0.00927 and 0.00928, respectively. Their very similar allele frequencies in both databases, together with the long-read phasing result in our patient, suggest that these 2 deletions may commonly occur together as a linked intron 1 deletion haplotype, although this was not directly assessed at the population level in this study. This deletion haplotype lies within the functionally active distal 36-kb intron 1 interval identified by the overlapping tiles (Figure 1B; c.76+34499_76+36500) and subtiles (supplemental Figure 2; c.76+35282_76+35765).

Functional assessment of intron 1 variants by luciferase reporter assay

We measured luciferase activity in HepG2 cells transiently transfected with PROS1 promoter constructs harboring an intron 1 fragment from the distal 36-kb intron 1 interval corresponding to the region that contains the patient’s deletions (Figure 6A). For the WT construct, this fragment was cloned with the reference intron 1 sequence; for the patient construct, we introduced the 2 intron 1 deletions c.76+35370_76+35453del and c.76+35455del in cis to mimic the patient allele. In this reporter context, the patient-derived intron 1 allele significantly reduced PROS1 promoter-driven luciferase activity compared with the WT intron 1 fragment (Figure 6D). These findings indicate that intron 1 variants alter the regulatory output of this interval in the reporter context and support the possibility that they contribute to the patient’s PS deficiency.

Discussion

Figure 7 provides an overview schematic aligning the reporter-defined regulatory segments, the HepG2 and mouse deletions, and the deletions identified in the patient, thereby integrating the functional data sets across experimental systems.

Reporter assay–defined tiles (green bars) and putative regulatory elements identified in reporter assays (cyan bars, enhancer like; magenta bars, silencer like), CRISPR/Cas9 deletions generated in HepG2 cells (blue bars), intron 1 deletions created in mice (orange bars), and intron 1 deletions identified in the patient (red bars) are shown on the corresponding intron schematics.

PS deficiency is a risk factor for VTE, and PROS1 contains an exceptionally long intron 1 (46 266 bp). This study provides, to our knowledge, the first evidence that PROS1 intron 1 sequences regulate PS expression in vitro and in vivo. Reporter assays identified intron 1 segments with enhancer- or silencer-like activity. The analysis of HepG2 clones lacking candidate regulatory segments or most of intron 1 revealed that deletion of most of intron 1 significantly decreased PS mRNA. These observations support a model in which first-intron sequences tune PROS1 transcription through multiple cis-regulatory mechanisms, potentially including effects on transcription, chromatin context, and RNA processing.

Transcription is the first step of gene expression, followed by RNA processing to yield mature mRNA. In vertebrates, long first introns are enriched for diverse functional elements, including cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG) islands, chromatin marks, and transcription factor (TF)-binding sites, supporting important regulatory roles.42,43 In addition, intron-intron interactions have been proposed to influence multiple aspects of gene expression and regulation.44 In our study, most regulatory segments were initially defined by reporter activity upon transversion mutations, whereas mRNA expression was assessed in deletion clones; differences between mutation-based mapping and deletion-based perturbation may underlie the partial discrepancies observed between reporter readouts and endogenous mRNA levels. In the plasmid-based assay, short intron 1 fragments are assayed outside of their native chromatin environment and at a fixed distance from the PROS1 promoter, whereas the endogenous locus integrates multiple positive and negative elements within a 3-dimensional chromatin context. Therefore, we interpret the tiling data primarily as a high-resolution screen that nominates candidate regulatory segments, and the CRISPR deletions as integrative perturbations that reveal the net contribution of larger intron 1 intervals in situ. The observation that 1 small deletion reduced secreted PS without a statistically significant change in steady-state PS mRNA further suggests that some intron 1 perturbations may influence PROS1 output through mechanisms not fully captured by total mRNA quantification alone, including modest transcriptional effects below our detection threshold or posttranscriptional processes affecting protein production or secretion.

Our tiling reporter assays were designed to identify intron 1 segments that can modulate PROS1 promoter activity, but they do not resolve the precise mechanism by which these elements act. Intronic enhancers and silencers frequently regulate transcription by looping to the promoter in the context of chromatin, and the geometry of a circular plasmid differs from that of the endogenous PROS1 locus. Therefore, it is possible that some elements behave differently in the reporter assay than at the native chromosomal site. Nevertheless, several observations support the relevance of the mapped regions in vivo: (1) deletion of most of intron 1 in HepG2 cells and in mice reduces PS mRNA and antigen levels, (2) smaller CRISPR deletions within intron 1 in HepG2 cells reproduce the direction of effect for a subset of the elements identified in the tiling screen, and (3) patient-specific intron 1 deletions reside within the functionally active distal 36-kb intron 1 interval identified by reporter assays and alter promoter activity when modeled in the same reporter context. These convergent data suggest that tiled intron 1 fragments indeed contain cis-acting regulatory sequences, even though the balance between promoter looping, local chromatin effects, and potential coupling to splicing remains to be fully elucidated.

In mice, intron 1 deletion lowered PS antigen in plasma (to ∼20%-30% of WT levels) and platelets (to ∼9% of WT levels; Figure 4). Consistent with PS production across tissues, PS mRNA was dramatically reduced in the liver (<10% of WT levels), the major source of circulating plasma PS but showed modest reductions in the lung and kidney (∼50% of WT levels), with minimal change in the heart (Figure 5A). The magnitude of PS mRNA reduction also differed by cell type: in hepatocytes, PS mRNA levels fell to ∼10% of WT levels; in lung vascular endothelial cells, they fell to ∼70% of WT levels; in cardiac fibroblasts, no significant change was observed (Figure 5B). These findings indicate that the contribution of intron 1 to PS expression varies by tissue and cell type. Previous studies have shown that partial Pros1 deficiency predisposes mice to thrombosis and vascular abnormalities.26, 27, 28 Although the long first intron is conserved mainly at the architectural rather than the primary-sequence level, our data support the conservation of intron 1 regulatory function across species. Prior work has shown that certain introns regulate gene expression in tissue- or development-specific manners.45, 46, 47 Consistently, TF binding information within intron 1 retrieved from ChIP-Atlas (https://chip-atlas.org/)48 suggests that TF occupancy patterns within intron 1 differ across tissues and cell types.

Finally, we identified 2 rare intron 1 deletions in a patient with congenital PS deficiency who lacked coding-region variants by conventional sequencing. These deletions cluster within a functionally active interval of intron 1 identified by our tiling reporter assays, and, when modeled in the same reporter context, the patient-derived allele significantly reduced PROS1 promoter-driven luciferase activity in HepG2 cells (Figure 6D). We attempted to generate a cell line harboring deletions of c.76+35370_76+35453 and c.76+35455 but were unsuccessful. Notably, causal variants remain undetectable by standard genetic testing in approximately half of families with PS deficiency,29,30 potentially because of variants in noncoding regions and/or regulatory elements. Our findings support the concept that noncoding intronic variants can contribute to congenital PS deficiency and support broader evaluation of PROS1 intron 1 in diagnostic genetic testing. The 2 rare intron 1 deletions identified here, together with their functional effects in reporter assays, provide proof of principle that clinically relevant noncoding variation in PROS1 may contribute to PS deficiency. Data from a single individual do not establish how often congenital PS deficiency is attributable to PROS1 intron 1 variants. Therefore, systematic long-read or targeted sequencing of PROS1 intron 1 in larger PS-deficient cohorts will be essential to define the overall contribution of intronic variants to disease. Because rs1709219428 has a minor allele frequency of ∼0.9% in the Japanese ToMMo 61KJPN reference panel, future population-based studies that link PROS1 intron 1 genotypes with adjudicated VTE outcomes will be needed to determine whether this variant, or related intronic lesions, contributes measurably to thrombosis risk and diagnostic yield.

Limitations

Reporter assays tested intron 1 fragments outside their native chromatin context and at fixed distances from the promoter on a circular plasmid, which may not fully recapitulate the 3-dimensional organization and looping interactions of the endogenous PROS1 locus. Some segments that modulated promoter activity in the tiling screen had modest or no effects when deleted at the chromosomal locus, likely reflecting context dependence, redundancy, and differences in spacing or orientation of regulatory motifs. We did not define the precise mechanisms or TFs responsible for intron 1 regulation. In addition, the edited HepG2 clones carried monoallelic deletions in a hyperdiploid background and represent a single hepatocyte-derived model; therefore, validating intron 1 regulatory logic in additional hepatocyte and nonhepatocyte systems, particularly endothelial and megakaryocytic cells, will be important. We also did not model patient-specific deletions at the endogenous PROS1 locus in HepG2 cells, perform isoform-resolved RNA sequencing, or conduct chromatin-conformation assays.

Interpretation of PROS1 variation is complicated by the highly homologous pseudogene PROS2P. Our long-range PCR and long-read sequencing strategy reduces mismapping and paralogous sequence interference within intron 1, increasing confidence in variant detection and break point assignment. This technical consideration is critical when extending genetic testing to noncoding regions proximal to PROS1.

In summary, we demonstrate that PROS1 intron 1 sequences harbor transcriptional regulatory elements and that a large intron 1 plays an important role in regulating PS expression in vitro and in vivo. These results provide a framework for interpreting noncoding PROS1 variants and for exploring tissue-specific regulatory mechanisms underlying PS deficiency.
