The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation
Jiaxin Zhang, Xiangyu Peng, Qinglin Chen, Qinyuan Ye, Chien-Sheng Wu

Abstract
On-policy distillation (OPD) is an increasingly important paradigm for post-training language models. However, we identify a pervasive Scaling Law of Miscalibration : while OPD effectively improves task accuracy, it systematically traps models in severe overconfidence. We trace this failure to an information mismatch: teacher supervision is formed under privileged context available during training, whereas the deployed model must report confidence using only deployment-time information. We formalize this perspective theoretically, showing that teacher-conditioned success is generally not a valid target for deployment-time confidence and that helpful privileged context induces entropy collapse and a systematic optimism bias . To address this, we propose a calibration-aware OPD framework, CaOPD, that estimates empirical confidence from model rollouts, replaces self-reported confidence with this student-grounded target, and distills the revised response through the same self-distillation pipeline. Experiments across various models and domains show that CaOPD achieves Pareto-optimal calibration while maintaining competitive capability, generalizing robustly under out-of-distribution and continual learning. Our findings highlight that capability distillation does not imply calibrated confidence, and that confidence should be treated as an essential objective in post-training.

Introduction

As LLMs achieve breakthroughs in complex reasoning and problem-solving,
their role is shifting from text generators
to autonomous agents and scientific co-pilots.
For AI to be a trustworthy collaborator in rigorous domains,
it must possess reliable
uncertainty signaling
, the ability to accurately self-assess and express doubt.
Unfortunately, modern LLMs consistently fail this criterion.
As shown in Figure
1
,
we observe a pervasive
Scaling Law of Miscalibration
:
even the most advanced proprietary models
(e.g., GPT
(
1
)
, Claude
(
4
)
,
Gemini
(
44
)
)
and scaled-up open-weight models
(e.g., DeepSeek
(
30
)
, Kimi
(
45
)
)
exhibit severe overconfidence.
Scaling up capability does not resolve this blind optimism.

This widespread pathology is deeply rooted in the modern post-training paradigm. To achieve advanced capabilities, developers increasingly rely on
On-Policy Distillation (OPD)

(
2
;
31
;
52
)
and self-distillation frameworks
(
36
;
21
;
54
)
. Methods such as SDPO
(
17
)
and SDFT
(
39
)
generate high-quality training trajectories by conditioning a teacher policy on
privileged contexts
, such as environment verifier feedback, expert demonstrations, or ground-truth solutions. While OPD is phenomenally successful at transferring capability, it completely destabilizes confidence calibration. Indeed, as recently argued by
20
, modern language models are effectively trapped in a "test-taking mode," incentivized to output plausible falsehoods rather than admitting uncertainty. We trace the algorithmic root of this epidemic directly to the OPD paradigm itself.

We demonstrate that this overconfidence is not a random artifact
but a mathematical consequence of OPD,
rooted in a fundamental
information asymmetry

between training and deployment
(
35
)
.
The teacher operates “open-book,”
conditioning on privileged context
to produce low-entropy reasoning paths;
the deployed model operates “closed-book” with only the user prompt.
Minimizing the divergence against this privileged distribution
forces the student to artificially sharpen its logits
(
Entropy Collapse
;
33
)
and to inherit the declarative style
of success-filtered trajectories
(
Optimism Bias
;
3
).
In short: capability transfers by imitation,
but confidence cannot safely transfer
across asymmetric information states. Existing efforts to tame overconfidence typically rely on Reinforcement Learning (RL) with explicit reward shaping, such as penalizing Brier scores via RLCR
(
10
)
. While these methods can suppress absolute confidence, they suffer from a severe
capability tax

(
5
)
, the models become overly conservative and sacrifice fundamental reasoning accuracy to avoid RL penalties.

To break this capability-calibration trade-off, we propose
Calibration-Aware On-Policy Distillation (CaOPD)
. Rather than fighting the optimizer with complex reward shaping, CaOPD addresses the root cause via
target decoupling
. We separate
what
the model should answer from
how certain
it should be. We sample multiple rollouts from the student policy, which under SDPO are already generated at zero extra cost, to compute a student-grounded, unbiased empirical success rate. We then perform a non-invasive
target replacement
: we preserve the teacher’s high-quality trajectory for capability cloning, but overwrite the implicit confidence token with the student’s actual confidence. By distilling this revised joint target, CaOPD effectively amortizes the heavy computational expense of test-time confidence estimation directly into the parameters of a single-pass inference model. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

Identifying the Scaling Law of Miscalibration:
We empirically demonstrate that standard capability scaling and OPD inherently induce systematic overconfidence, a pervasive pathology that traps frontier LLMs in blind optimism.

•

Theoretical Formalization:
We mathematically formalize the information asymmetry in standard OPD, proving that distilling from
a privileged teacher context inherently induces
entropy collapse
and
optimism bias
.

•

The CaOPD Framework:
We propose CaOPD, a compute-efficient post-training framework that decouples behavior imitation from confidence supervision. CaOPD amortizes expensive test-time empirical confidence into a single-pass model.

•

Pareto-Optimal Performance and Robustness:
Across OPD regimes (SDFT, SDPO) and domains (Science Q&A, Tool Use), CaOPD achieves Pareto-optimal calibration without the capability tax of RL-based methods, generalizing robustly under OOD shifts and preventing "calibration forgetting" during continual learning.

2
Problem Formulation and Preliminaries

2.1
On-Policy Distillation and Self-Distillation

Let
\mathcal{X}
denote the space of input queries.
To study confidence calibration explicitly,
we augment the standard generation format
with a
verbalized confidence instruction

(
19
;
55
;
46
)
,
prompting the model to append
an explicit confidence statement after its reasoning.
Each complete generation
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{T})

is partitioned into two contiguous segments:
y=(a,c)
,
where
a=(y_{1},\dots,y_{T_{a}})
is the reasoning trajectory (including the final answer), and
c=(y_{T_{a}+1},\dots,y_{T})
is the
confidence segment
, the token subsequence encoding a verbalized confidence statement such as “
Confidence: 0.85
”. We write
\mathrm{val}(c)\in[0,1]

for the scalar confidence score
extracted by parsing the confidence segment
c
.

Modern OPD methods employ a
self-distillation
architecture in which a single model plays both roles:
Student policy

\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
: generates
y
conditioned
only
on the input query
x
, simulating deployment conditions;
Teacher policy

\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,z)
: the
same
model, but additionally conditioned on a
privileged context

z\sim\mathcal{Z}(x)
. Depending on the OPD paradigm,
z
may instantiate as an expert demonstration, environment feedback, or a ground-truth solution. The shared structural property is that the teacher is conditioned on strictly richer evidence than the deployed model will ever observe.

2.2
The Ideal Calibration Objective

Let
R:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\{0,1\}
be an objective verification function, where
R(x,a)=1
indicates that the reasoning trajectory
a
yields a correct answer. For the deployed model, the true probability of success on input
x
is marginalized over its own generation distribution:

\mu(x)\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\big[R(x,a)\big]\;=\;\Pr_{\pi_{\theta}}(R=1\mid x).

(1)

A
calibrated
model should produce a confidence segment whose parsed value faithfully reflects this probability:
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{val}(c_{\theta}(x))]=\mu(x)
. Therefore,
\mu(x)
serves as the ground-truth target for confidence alignment.

2.3
The OPD Objective and Its Theoretical Blind Spot

Following SDFT and SDPO, on-policy self-distillation
minimizes the
reverse
KL divergence
between the student and the privileged teacher.
The student first generates a trajectory
y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
;
both the student
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x)

and the teacher
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x,z)

then compute token-level distributions along this trajectory.
The per-token reverse KL objective
is:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPD}}(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{x,\,z\sim\mathcal{Z}(x)}\;\;\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x)\;\big\|\;\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x,z)\big)\right].

(2)

Because the generation
y=(a,c)
includes confidence tokens,
this loss operates over
all
token positions, both the reasoning segment and the trailing confidence segment.
This is the theoretical blind spot:
the teacher, armed with the privileged context
z

(e.g., the correct answer), produces a distribution
at confidence positions that is concentrated
near extreme values such as
c\approx 1.0
.
Standard OPD therefore
entangles
two objectives:
it transfers the teacher’s reasoning capability

and simultaneously

forces the student to imitate
the teacher’s unjustified certainty.

3
Why Does Capability Distillation Exacerbate Overconfidence?

3.1
Intuition: Information Asymmetry and Distributional Warping

The root cause of OPD-induced overconfidence
is the information asymmetry between
the teacher’s privileged evidence
z

and the student’s accessible input
x
.
When the student minimizes the per-token reverse KL
against
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x,z)

(Equation (
2
))
without access to
z
,
it is forced to artificially sharpen its logits
to approximate the teacher’s deterministic-like behavior.
At deployment, this translates into extreme verbalized
confidence — the model learns the teacher’s certainty
without possessing the teacher’s evidence.
We now formalize the three mechanisms
through which this asymmetry induces miscalibration.

3.2
Theoretical Analysis: The Mechanics of Miscalibration

Proposition 1 (Information Gap and Non-Identifiability).

Suppose the privileged teacher context
Z
is informative about the correctness
R
beyond the input
X
, such that the conditional mutual information
I(R;Z\mid X)>0
. Then, the teacher-conditioned success probability
\mu_{T}(X,Z)
is not measurable with respect to
X
alone. Specifically, there exists no deterministic function
g(X)
such that
g(X)=\mu_{T}(X,Z)
almost surely. Furthermore, under squared error, the optimal
X
-measurable predictor of
\mu_{T}(X,Z)
is exactly the student’s deployment success probability
\mu(X)
, leaving a strictly positive irreducible projection error:

\min_{g}\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\mu_{T}(X,Z)-g(X)\big)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\text{Var}\big(\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X\big)\right]>0.

(3)

Proposition 2 (Entropy Collapse via Privileged Conditioning).

Let
H(\cdot)
denote the Shannon entropy. Assume the privileged context
Z
provides actionable information about the correct reasoning trajectory
A
that is not present in
X
, such that the conditional mutual information
I(A;Z\mid X)>0
. Then, the expected entropy of the teacher’s trajectory distribution is strictly lower than the conditional entropy of the trajectories given only the accessible input
X
:

\mathbb{E}_{X,Z}\big[H(\pi_{\theta}(A\mid X,Z))\big]<\mathbb{E}_{X}\big[H(A\mid X)\big].

(4)

Furthermore, by minimizing the per-token reverse KL
against
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},X,Z)
,
the model
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},X)

is penalized for expressing the natural uncertainty
H(A\mid X)
,
forcing its internal logits to become artificially sharpened.

Proposition 3 (Selection Bias Induces Optimism).

In OPD, the privileged context
Z
is not sampled randomly, but is typically conditioned on successful or high-quality outcomes (e.g., demonstrations, correct solutions). Assume this training distribution of contexts, denoted
\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}
, is strictly helpful, meaning it increases the expected correctness of the teacher over the student’s marginal capability:
\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}[\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X]\geq\mu(X)
, with strict inequality on a set of positive measure. Then, the implicit target distilled into the student is an upward-biased surrogate for the true deployment-time success probability:

\mathbb{E}_{X,Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}\big[\mu_{T}(X,Z)-\mu(X)\big]>0.

(5)

Full proofs and extended discussions for all three propositions are provided in Appendix
A
.

4
Calibration-Aware On-Policy Distillation (CaOPD)

Theoretical analysis establishes a grim reality: capability distillation and confidence calibration are fundamentally misaligned under asymmetric information. OPD forces the student to chase an unidentifiable, artificially sharpened, and systematically optimistic target. To overcome this mathematical limitation, the supervision target for confidence
must
be explicitly decoupled from the teacher’s capability trajectory. It must be anchored to an unbiased estimator of
\mu(x)
that relies solely on the student’s deployment-time information state. This mandate directly derives our proposed framework:
CaOPD
.

4.1
Constructing the Student-Grounded Target

The first step of CaOPD is to obtain
an unbiased estimate of the model’s
true deployment-time success probability
\mu(x)
,
without relying on the privileged context
z
.
Since
\mu(x)
is defined as the expectation
over the model’s own generation distribution

\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
(Equation (
1
)),
we approximate it via Monte Carlo estimation.
For a given input
x
,
we sample
K
independent trajectories

(a_{k},c_{k})\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)

for
k=1,\dots,K
,
evaluate each with the task verifier

R(x,a_{k})\in\{0,1\}
,
and compute the empirical success rate:

\hat{\mu}(x)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}R(x,a_{k}).

(6)

A key practical advantage is that OPD methods
already generate multiple rollouts per prompt.
Under SDPO, CaOPD
reuses the rollouts already
generated by the base training loop
to compute

\hat{\mu}(x)
, requiring only a lightweight verifier
evaluation on these existing trajectories.
The distillation trajectory itself is sampled separately and incurs no extra cost beyond what SDPO already requires. By definition,
\hat{\mu}(x)
is a student-grounded,
execution-based target.
While we utilize objective task verifiers
in our primary evaluations,
this target is highly modular;
for open-ended domains lacking ground-truth verifiers,

\hat{\mu}(x)
can be approximated using

Teacher-Anchored Self-Consistency

(Appendix
B.6
).

4.2
Target Replacement and the Decoupled Objective

With the empirical target
\hat{\mu}(x)
computed,
we integrate it into the OPD pipeline
via a
Target Replacement
operation
that modifies both the
completion

and the
teacher context

before computing the loss,
while leaving the reverse-KL machinery entirely unchanged. Concretely, given a student-generated trajectory
y=(a,c)
:

(i)

Revise the completion.

Replace the confidence segment
c

with
\hat{\mu}(x)
, yielding the revised trajectory

\tilde{y}=(a,\;\hat{\mu}(x))
.

(ii)

Revise the teacher context.

In the privileged context
z
,
overwrite the confidence score (originally near
1.0
)
with
\hat{\mu}(x)
,
producing a revised context
\tilde{z}
.

Both the student and teacher then score the revised completion

\tilde{y}
under their respective conditioning
(
x
and
(x,\tilde{z})
).
Recall that the token positions of
\tilde{y}
partition into reasoning
\mathcal{I}_{a}=\{1,\dots,T_{a}\}

and confidence
\mathcal{I}_{c}=\{T_{a}{+}1,\dots,T\}
.
The CaOPD objective is:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CaOPD}}(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{x,\,\tilde{z}}\;\;\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{y}}\bigg[\;&\underbrace{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{I}_{a}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{y}_{<t},x)\;\big\|\;\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{y}_{<t},x,\tilde{z})\big)}_{\text{Capability Cloning (preserved)}}\\[4.0pt]
+\;\;&\underbrace{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{y}_{<t},x)\;\big\|\;\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{y}_{<t},x,\tilde{z})\big)}_{\text{Confidence Calibration (student-grounded)}}\;\bigg].\end{split}

(7)

The target replacement
is a clean decoupling of capability from calibration:

•

Capability Cloning is preserved.

At reasoning positions
t\in\mathcal{I}_{a}
,
the prefix
\tilde{y}_{<t}
contains only reasoning tokens
(the confidence replacement has not yet appeared),
and the privileged reasoning content in
\tilde{z}

is left unmodified.
The per-token KL at these positions
is therefore
effectively identical

to standard OPD (Equation (
2
)).

•

Confidence Imitation
\to
Calibration.

At confidence positions
t\in\mathcal{I}_{c}
,
the tokens being scored encode
\hat{\mu}(x)

rather than the student’s original
c
,
and the teacher’s context reflects
\hat{\mu}(x)

rather than
1.0
.
The student is thus trained to produce
the
student-grounded empirical success rate
,
directly resolving the entropy collapse
(Proposition 2) and optimism bias (Proposition 3).

Unlike RL-based calibration methods that inject scoring-rule penalties into the reward function, often causing optimization instability and a severe capability tax
(
10
;
5
)
, CaOPD operates entirely within the standard distillation
pipeline via target replacement, requiring no reward modification or additional optimization stages. The complete procedure is summarized in Algorithm
1
;
a visual overview is provided
in Appendix
B.1
(Figure
5
).

We conduct experiments across two distinct domains: Science Question Answering (Science Q&A

(

5.2
Does On-Policy Distillation Inherently Exacerbate Overconfidence?

OPD exacerbates baseline overconfidence.
As shown in Table
1
, base instruction-tuned models already exhibit severe optimism bias (e.g., a massive
+58.7%
OCG for Qwen3-8B on Science Q&A). Strikingly, applying standard OPD methods (SDFT and SDPO) systematically
worsens
this pathology. Across almost all settings, OPD drives mean confidence toward extreme saturation (e.g.,
0.996
on Tool Use), expanding the OCG (highlighted in red). This strongly validates our theoretical analysis: optimizing student behavior under a privileged teacher context induces an optimism bias, forcing the model to blindly inherit absolute certainty without possessing the underlying evidence.

CaOPD structurally realigns confidence.
By decoupling capability imitation from confidence supervision, CaOPD successfully mitigates this dangerous drift. Replacing the teacher’s privileged confidence with the student’s empirical success drastically reduces mean confidence to realistic levels. Notably, for Qwen3-8B on Tool Use, CaOPD collapses the massive OCG from
+32.0%
(SDFT) down to an exceptionally aligned
-0.7%
. This demonstrates that CaOPD structurally corrects the confidence distribution, proving that true calibration can be explicitly learned during distillation.

We identify a pervasive pathology in modern LLM post-training:

.

To break this capability-calibration trade-off,
we propose Calibration-Aware OPD (CaOPD),
which decouples capability distillation
from confidence supervision
via a simple target replacement mechanism.
Extensive evaluations confirm that CaOPD

By enabling models to produce calibrated confidence

Limitations.

Like all on-policy distillation methods,

that the base model possesses sufficient
in-context learning capacity
to benefit from the privileged teacher context
z
.
Our scaling analysis (Section
5.5
)
shows that even at 0.6B parameters,

however, capability gains are naturally
bounded by the base model’s reasoning capacity.
Additionally, verbalized confidence
requires the model to produce a parsable
confidence segment

matching the specified format.
While CaOPD’s target replacement
provides a strong formatting signal
during training (Appendix

We find

Our work highlights several exciting directions for future research:

A.1
Proof of Proposition 1

Statement:

Let
\mu(X):=\mathbb{P}(R=1\mid X)
and
\mu_{T}(X,Z):=\mathbb{P}(R=1\mid X,Z)
. If
I(R;Z\mid X)>0
, then
\mu_{T}(X,Z)
is not
X
-measurable. The unique square-integrable minimizer
g^{*}(X)
of
\mathbb{E}[(\mu_{T}(X,Z)-g(X))^{2}]
is
\mu(X)
, with a minimum achievable error of
\mathbb{E}[\text{Var}(\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X)]
.

Proof:
We prove the proposition in three steps.

Step 1:
\mu_{T}(X,Z)
is not
X
-measurable.

Recall that
\mu_{T}(X,Z)=\mathbb{E}[R\mid X,Z]
. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
\mu_{T}(X,Z)
is
X
-measurable. Then there exists some measurable function
g(X)
such that
\mathbb{E}[R\mid X,Z]=g(X)
almost surely. Taking the conditional expectation with respect to
X
on both sides yields:

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[R\mid X,Z]\mid X\big]=\mathbb{E}[g(X)\mid X]\implies\mathbb{E}[R\mid X]=g(X)\text{ a.s.}

(8)

This implies that
\mathbb{E}[R\mid X,Z]=\mathbb{E}[R\mid X]
almost surely. Consequently, the conditional distribution of
R
given
(X,Z)
depends only on
X
, which means
R
and
Z
are conditionally independent given
X
. This is equivalent to
I(R;Z\mid X)=0
, which directly contradicts our assumption that
I(R;Z\mid X)>0
. Therefore,
\mu_{T}(X,Z)
cannot be
X
-measurable.

Step 2: The optimal
X
-measurable predictor is the conditional expectation.

We seek to minimize
\mathbb{E}[(\mu_{T}(X,Z)-g(X))^{2}]
over all
X
-measurable functions
g
. By the standard property of minimum Mean Squared Error (MSE) estimation, the unique minimizer is the conditional expectation of the target given the observable variables:

g^{*}(X)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\mid X}\big[\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X\big].

(9)

Step 3: The optimal projection equals deployment-time success.

We substitute the definition of
\mu_{T}(X,Z)
into the optimal predictor
g^{*}(X)
and apply the Law of Total Expectation (the tower property):

g^{*}(X)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\mid X}\big[\mathbb{E}[R\mid X,Z]\mid X\big]=\mathbb{E}[R\mid X]=\mu(X).

(10)

Finally, substituting
g^{*}(X)=\mu(X)
into the MSE objective yields the irreducible error:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\mu_{T}(X,Z)-\mu(X)\big)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\mathbb{E}_{Z\mid X}\left[\big(\mu_{T}(X,Z)-\mathbb{E}[\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X]\big)^{2}\mid X\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\text{Var}\big(\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X\big)\right].

Since
\mu_{T}(X,Z)
is not
X
-measurable (from Step 1), the variance
\text{Var}(\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X)
is strictly positive on a set of non-zero measure, making the irreducible projection error strictly greater than zero. This completes the proof.
\blacksquare

What this means for OPD:

Proposition 1 formally explains the
Information Gap
introduced in Section
3.1
. Because the teacher uses extra evidence
z
(e.g., a correct demonstration) to form its internal confidence, the student simply cannot "guess" this exact confidence value at test time. The best the student could theoretically do is predict its own marginal success rate
\mu(x)
. However, standard OPD minimizes the KL divergence point-wise on each trajectory, forcing the student to memorize the inaccessible
\mu_{T}(x,z)
rather than learning the optimal projection
\mu(x)
. This structural mismatch guarantees that confidence distillation will fail to produce calibrated predictions.

Takeaway 1: The Information Gap in Practice

The mathematical impossibility of recovering the teacher’s exact confidence
\mu_{T}(X,Z)
from
X
alone means that standard OPD forces the student to chase a "ghost" target. The proof demonstrates that the theoretically optimal strategy for the student is to predict its own marginal success rate
\mu(X)
. However, the standard OPD loss function prevents this, coercing the student to memorize inaccessible context cues. This structural mismatch mathematically guarantees that confidence distillation, if left coupled with capability cloning, will fail to produce calibrated predictions at deployment.

A.2
Proof of Proposition 2

Statement:

Let
A
represent the generated reasoning trajectory,
X
the user input, and
Z
the privileged context. If the context is informative about the trajectory such that
I(A;Z\mid X)>0
, then the expected entropy of the model’s distribution under privileged conditioning is strictly lower than the conditional entropy given only
X
:
\mathbb{E}_{X,Z}[H(\pi_{\theta}(A\mid X,Z))]<H(A\mid X)
.

Proof:
The proof relies on the fundamental properties of Shannon entropy and conditional mutual information.

Step 1: Expanding the Conditional Entropy.

By definition, the conditional entropy
H(A\mid X)
measures the average uncertainty remaining about the reasoning trajectory
A
when only the input
X
is observed:

H(A\mid X)=-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P(x)\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}P(a\mid x)\log P(a\mid x).

(11)

Similarly, the expected entropy of the model under privileged conditioning (i.e., conditioned on both
X
and
Z
) is:

H(A\mid X,Z)=-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X},z\in\mathcal{Z}}P(x,z)\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{\theta}(a\mid x,z)\log\pi_{\theta}(a\mid x,z)=\mathbb{E}_{X,Z}\big[H(\pi_{\theta}(A\mid X,Z))\big].

(12)

Step 2: Applying the Chain Rule of Information.

Using the chain rule for Shannon entropy, the relationship between these two quantities can be expressed through the conditional mutual information
I(A;Z\mid X)
, which quantifies the reduction in uncertainty about
A
provided by knowing
Z
, given that
X
is already known:

H(A\mid X)=H(A\mid X,Z)+I(A;Z\mid X).

(13)

Step 3: Utilizing the Non-Negativity of Mutual Information.

By the assumption of the proposition, the privileged context
Z
is informative, meaning
I(A;Z\mid X)>0
. Rearranging the equation from Step 2, we obtain:

H(A\mid X,Z)=H(A\mid X)-I(A;Z\mid X).

(14)

Since
I(A;Z\mid X)>0
, it strictly follows that:

H(A\mid X,Z)<H(A\mid X).

(15)

Substituting the expectation notation back into the inequality yields:

\mathbb{E}_{X,Z}\big[H(\pi_{\theta}(A\mid X,Z))\big]<\mathbb{E}_{X}\big[H(A\mid X)\big].

(16)

Step 4: Implication on the Reverse KL Objective.

In standard OPD, the model minimizes the per-token reverse KL divergence (Equation (
2
)). For a given prefix
y_{<t}
generated by the model in student mode, the per-token loss decomposes as:

D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x)\;\big\|\;\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x,z)\big)=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{\theta}(v\mid y_{<t},x)\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v\mid y_{<t},x)}{\pi_{\theta}(v\mid y_{<t},x,z)},

(17)

where
\mathcal{V}
is the token vocabulary. The reverse KL is
mode-seeking
: it heavily penalizes the model (in student mode) for placing probability mass on tokens where the privileged-conditioned distribution
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x,z)
assigns low probability. Because the privileged distribution is sharply concentrated (as proven in Steps 1–3), the student-mode distribution
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x)
is driven to collapse its mass onto the same narrow set of high-probability tokens. This forces the model’s internal logits to become artificially sharpened in order to match the teacher’s certainty, even though the model in student mode lacks access to the mutual information
I(A;Z\mid X)
that justifies this concentration. This completes the proof.
\blacksquare

What this means for OPD:

Proposition 2 formally explains the
Entropy Collapse
mechanism introduced in Section
3.1
. Because the model under privileged conditioning possesses the “cheat sheet”
z
(e.g., correct demonstrations or verifier feedback), its next-token distribution is highly concentrated (low entropy). The reverse KL objective forces the model in student mode to match this sharp distribution. Being mode-seeking, the reverse KL drives the model to collapse its probability mass onto the teacher’s high-confidence modes, drastically scaling up its internal logit magnitudes even though it lacks the evidence
z
to justify such certainty. When the model is subsequently asked to verbalize its confidence, it translates this artificially low-entropy internal state into an unjustified, near-absolute confidence score (e.g.,
\mathrm{val}(c)=0.99
).

Takeaway 2: Artificial Sharpness and Logit Collapse

This proof bridges information theory with model optimization dynamics. By demonstrating that
H(A\mid X,Z)<H(A\mid X)
, we show that the model under privileged conditioning inherently operates with a lower-entropy (sharper) distribution. Because the reverse KL is mode-seeking, it forces the model in student mode to concentrate its probability mass on the teacher’s high-confidence modes, artificially magnifying its internal logits during training. At test time, this manifests as extreme verbalized certainty. In essence, the model learns the
magnitude
of privileged-conditioned confidence without possessing the underlying
mutual information

I(A;Z\mid X)
that justifies it.

A.3
Proof of Proposition 3

Statement:

Let
\mu(X):=\mathbb{P}(R=1\mid X)
and
\mu_{T}(X,Z):=\mathbb{P}(R=1\mid X,Z)
. Let
\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}
be the distribution of the privileged context
Z
used during OPD training. Assume that conditioning on
Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}
strictly improves expected correctness on a set of inputs
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
with
\mathbb{P}(X\in\mathcal{S})>0
, such that
\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}[\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X=x]>\mu(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{S}
, and
\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}[\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X=x]\geq\mu(x)
otherwise. Then,
\mathbb{E}_{X,Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}[\mu_{T}(X,Z)]>\mathbb{E}_{X}[\mu(X)]
.

Proof:
We evaluate the expectation of the difference between the teacher-conditioned success target and the true deployment-time success probability.

Step 1: Applying the Law of Total Expectation.

We take the expectation of the difference over the joint training distribution of inputs
X
and privileged contexts
Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}
. By the linearity of expectation and the Law of Total Expectation (the tower property), we can condition on
X
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X,Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}\big[\mu_{T}(X,Z)-\mu(X)\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}\big[\mu_{T}(X,Z)-\mu(X)\mid X\big]\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}\big[\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X\big]-\mu(X)\Big].

(18)

Note that
\mu(X)
acts as a constant with respect to the inner expectation over
Z
.

Step 2: Incorporating the Helpful Context Assumption.

By the premise of the proposition, the training context
Z
is selected to be helpful (e.g., successful rollouts or gold demonstrations). Therefore, for every input
X
, the expected success probability of the teacher under this filtered context distribution is greater than or equal to the student’s marginal success probability without the context:

\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}\big[\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X\big]-\mu(X)\geq 0\quad\text{almost surely}.

(19)

Step 3: Establishing the Strict Inequality.

Furthermore, we assumed this inequality is strict on a subset of inputs
\mathcal{S}
with non-zero probability measure (
\mathbb{P}(X\in\mathcal{S})>0
). Let the difference be denoted by
\Delta(X)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}[\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X]-\mu(X)
. We can split the outer expectation over
\mathcal{X}
into
\mathcal{S}
and its complement
\mathcal{S}^{c}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X}[\Delta(X)]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\Delta(x)d\mathbb{P}(x)+\int_{\mathcal{S}^{c}}\Delta(x)d\mathbb{P}(x).

(20)

Since
\Delta(x)>0
on
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S})>0
, the first integral is strictly positive. Since
\Delta(x)\geq 0
on
\mathcal{S}^{c}
, the second integral is non-negative. Therefore:

\mathbb{E}_{X}[\Delta(X)]>0.

(21)

Substituting back the definition of
\Delta(X)
, we conclude:

\mathbb{E}_{X,Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}\big[\mu_{T}(X,Z)-\mu(X)\big]>0.

(22)

This proves that mimicking the teacher under success-conditioned privileged context mathematically injects a strictly positive optimism bias into the student’s expected confidence.
\blacksquare

What this means for OPD:

Proposition 3 is the mathematical formalization of the
Optimism Bias
introduced in Section
3.1
. Methods like SDFT and SDPO do not train on random garbage; they specifically construct
z
to ensure that teacher has high-quality contexts. Consequently, the student is exclusively trained to imitate the highly certain, declarative linguistic style of a
successful
derivation. The model perfectly learns the "voice of a winner." At test time, when the student generates an incorrect answer due to its limited capability, it lacks the mechanism to express doubt. Instead, it blindly applies this inherited optimistic tone, resulting in the massive Overconfidence Gaps (OCG) we empirically observed in Table
1
.

Takeaway 3: The Origin of the Optimism Bias

By evaluating the expectation over the successful subset of contexts (
\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}
), the proof reveals a systemic flaw in distilling only high-quality trajectories. Because the student is mathematically shielded from failure cases during supervision, it severely underestimates the complexity of the prompt distribution. Consequently, the student exclusively learns the declarative, confident tone of a successful derivation. It blindly applies this inherited optimistic tone even on out-of-distribution or overly difficult test prompts, fully explaining the massive Overconfidence Gaps (OCG) we observe empirically.

Appendix B
Algorithm and Implementation Details

B.1
The CaOPD Framework Pipeline

To provide an intuitive understanding of the Calibration-Aware On-Policy Distillation (CaOPD) framework, Figure
5
illustrates the complete end-to-end pipeline used during the post-training data generation and optimization phases. The framework explicitly decouples capability learning from confidence alignment through a five-step procedure:

Figure 5:
Overview of the CaOPD Framework.
The pipeline consists of five stages: (1) querying the student for a base response with verbalized confidence, (2) approximating the true empirical confidence via student rollouts and an objective verifier, (3) revising the completion by replacing the original confidence tokens with the empirical success rate, (4) constructing the privileged teacher context with the same revised confidence, and (5) computing the per-token reverse KL between the student and teacher distributions on the revised completion.

1. Generate Verbalized Confidence:
Given an input prompt
x
, the model (in student mode, i.e., conditioned only on
x
) generates a complete trajectory
y=(a,c)
, where
a
is the reasoning trajectory and
c
is the confidence segment. In standard OPD,
\mathrm{val}(c)
would typically be near
1.0
, reflecting the teacher’s optimism inherited during training.

2. Approximate True Confidence:
Instead of trusting the single-pass verbalized confidence, we estimate the student’s actual competence via empirical execution. We sample
K
independent rollouts
(a_{k},c_{k})\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
and evaluate each with an objective task verifier to compute the empirical success rate
\hat{\mu}(x)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}R(x,a_{k})
. This serves as a statistically rigorous, student-grounded approximation of the true deployment-time confidence.

3. Revise the Completion (Target Replacement):
This is the core decoupling step of CaOPD. We take the student’s reasoning trajectory
a
from Step 1, but explicitly discard its original confidence segment
c
. We construct the revised completion
\tilde{y}=(a,\;\hat{\mu}(x))
by overwriting the confidence tokens with the string representation of the empirical success rate. This guarantees that the supervision target for confidence reflects the student’s actual competence rather than the teacher’s privileged certainty.

4. Construct Revised Teacher Context:
To ensure the model learns advanced reasoning capabilities, we construct a privileged context
z
by prepending the user question with environmental feedback, ground-truth demonstrations, or correct solutions, creating an “open-book” setting for the teacher. Crucially, the confidence score within
z
— originally near
1.0
— is also overwritten with
\hat{\mu}(x)
, producing a revised context
\tilde{z}
. This ensures that both the completion and the teacher’s conditioning reflect the same student-grounded confidence target.

5. Compute Reverse KL Loss:
Both the student
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{y}_{<t},x)
and the teacher
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{y}_{<t},x,\tilde{z})
perform a forward pass on the revised completion
\tilde{y}
. The per-token reverse KL divergence (Equation (
7
)) is computed between these two distributions. As discussed in Section
4.2
, this naturally decomposes into a
Capability Cloning
component at reasoning positions and a
Confidence Calibration
component at confidence positions.

The detailed pseudocode for this entire process is formally summarized in Algorithm
1
.

Key Properties of CaOPD

The CaOPD framework fundamentally restructures how post-trained LLMs learn to express uncertainty, exhibiting three desirable properties:

•

True Decoupling of Behavior and Self-Assessment.

Unlike RL methods (e.g., RLCR) that inject calibration penalties into a global reward function — often degrading task accuracy — CaOPD operates via direct target replacement within the standard distillation pipeline. The loss (Equation (
7
)) preserves the teacher’s capability signal at reasoning positions while redirecting the confidence signal to the student-grounded target.

•

Meta-Skill Generalization.

Because CaOPD supervises confidence using the student’s actual execution outcomes rather than arbitrary heuristic penalties, the model learns a generalizable meta-skill: mapping its internal uncertainty to an accurate verbalized scale. As demonstrated in Section
5.4
, this allows calibrated confidence to generalize out-of-distribution and persist through continual training.

•

Test-Time Compute Amortization.

Obtaining reliable confidence via self-consistency or multi-sampling (i.e., computing
\hat{\mu}(x)
online) is computationally prohibitive at deployment. CaOPD computes
\hat{\mu}(x)

during the training phase
via iterative on-policy rollouts. By distilling this signal into the model’s parametric weights, CaOPD amortizes the cost of test-time search into a single-pass inference model.

B.3
Constructing the Privileged Teacher Context (
z
) and Target Replacement

In Section
3
, we theorized that On-Policy Distillation (OPD) suffers from information asymmetry because the teacher, the same model conditioned on a privileged context
z
, produces artificially certain distributions. To concretize this theoretical framework, we detail exactly how this privileged context is constructed in practice for both the SDFT and SDPO baselines, and crucially,
where CaOPD intervenes to replace the confidence target
.

Target Replacement during CaOPD:
By structuring the output with an explicit
Confidence: [value]
suffix, it becomes trivial to extract the scalar confidence score using rule-based parsing. During the CaOPD training phase (Algorithm
1
), we perform two replacements: (1) in the
student’s completion
, we parse the confidence segment, drop the original numeric value, and overwrite it with the string representation of
\hat{\mu}(x)
; (2) in the
teacher’s privileged context

z
, we likewise overwrite any confidence value (typically
1.0
) with
\hat{\mu}(x)
, producing the revised context
\tilde{z}
. The per-token reverse KL loss is then computed on this revised completion under both the student conditioning
(x)
and the teacher conditioning
(x,\tilde{z})
.

While the student is prompted strictly with the user query (Appendix
B.2
), the teacher receives a heavily augmented prompt, the same query prepended with the privileged context
\tilde{z}
. Below, we provide the
exact, full text
of the teacher prompts from our Tool Use domain experiments. We use colored annotations to highlight the exact token where CaOPD overwrites the default overconfidence with the student’s empirical target
\hat{\mu}(x)
.

B.4
Handling Target Non-Stationarity: Online Estimation of
\hat{\mu}(x)

A critical consideration in the CaOPD framework is the potential non-stationarity of the empirical confidence target. As the model
\pi_{\theta}
undergoes gradient updates and improves its reasoning capabilities, an empirical target
\hat{\mu}(x)
computed from a stale snapshot would rapidly become misaligned with the student’s true competence.

To strictly prevent target staleness, our implementation of CaOPD does
not
rely on a static, pre-computed offline dataset. Instead,
\hat{\mu}(x)
is dynamically refreshed within an iterative, on-policy training loop. Specifically, the training dynamics proceed as follows:

1.

EMA Teacher Tracking:

In self-distillation, the teacher is the same model with different conditioning (Section
2.1
). To stabilize the teacher’s distribution during training, we maintain an Exponential Moving Average (EMA)
(
43
)
copy of the model weights
\theta_{\mathrm{ema}}
, updated at every gradient step:
\theta_{\mathrm{ema}}\leftarrow(1-\alpha)\,\theta_{\mathrm{ema}}+\alpha\,\theta
. The teacher distribution is then
\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{ema}}}(\cdot\mid\tilde{y}_{<t},x,\tilde{z})
, providing a smoothed target for the reverse KL.

2.

Dynamic Rollout Generation:

Periodically during training, the latest model weights
\theta
are synchronized to a high-throughput inference engine (vLLM
(
25
)
). The engine generates a fresh batch of
K

student
rollouts
(a_{k},c_{k})\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
for each prompt on-the-fly.

3.

Online Target Computation:

These newly generated student rollouts are immediately evaluated by the objective task verifier
R(x,a_{k})
. The empirical success rate
\hat{\mu}(x)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}R(x,a_{k})
is computed strictly from this
current
batch of rollouts.

4.

Target Replacement:

The freshly computed
\hat{\mu}(x)
is used to construct the revised completion
\tilde{y}
and revised teacher context
\tilde{z}
for the subsequent gradient optimization steps.

This online, on-policy generation mechanism ensures that the supervision signal for confidence dynamically adapts to the model’s learning trajectory. As the model becomes more capable, the fresh rollouts yield a higher success rate, and the target
\hat{\mu}(x)
naturally scales upward. Consequently, CaOPD completely avoids the optimization instability associated with stale targets in standard off-policy algorithms.

Note on Terminology:
When we refer to the computational cost of rollouts being “amortized offline” in the main text, we specifically mean that this cost is absorbed
prior to deployment
(i.e., during the training phase), rather than requiring
O(K)
test-time compute from the end-user.

B.5
Extended Discussion: Justification of the Empirical Target

A potential concern regarding the CaOPD framework is whether the sampling-based empirical frequency,
\hat{\mu}(x)
, serves as a rigorous representation of the model’s true internal confidence, rather than merely a heuristic proxy. We argue that
\hat{\mu}(x)
is, in fact, the most statistically rigorous and unbiased target for confidence alignment, supported by both the mathematical definition of calibration and extensive literature on epistemic uncertainty.

Statistical Unbiasedness.
By the strict definition of calibration, a model’s predicted confidence should perfectly equal its expected accuracy on a given prompt. For the deployed model
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
, its true expected success probability is exactly the marginal expectation over its own generation distribution:
\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\theta}}[R(x,a)]
. Therefore, the Monte Carlo estimator
\hat{\mu}(x)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}R(x,a_{k})
is a statistically unbiased estimate of the model’s objective capability on query
x
. Training the model to produce
\mathrm{val}(c)=\hat{\mu}(x)
by replacing the confidence tokens in the distillation target (Section
4.2
), ensures that the verbalized confidence honestly reflects the model’s own generation distribution.

Connection to Behavioral Consistency and Hallucination Detection.
Our formulation strongly aligns with established findings in the hallucination and uncertainty estimation literature. Extensive prior work has demonstrated that sampling-based behavioral consistency is the “gold standard” for measuring an LLM’s epistemic uncertainty. For instance,
Self-Consistency

(
48
)
leverages multiple rollouts to find the most reliable reasoning paths. More directly, hallucination detection frameworks such as
SelfCheckGPT

(
32
)
and
SAC3
(Semantic-aware Cross-check)
(
53
)
operate on the principle that when an LLM hallucinates (i.e., operates outside its knowledge boundary), its sampled responses exhibit high entropy and self-contradiction. Conversely, confident and factual knowledge yields highly consistent samples.

CaOPD explicitly adopts this gold-standard consistency signal as the training target. However, while methods like SelfCheckGPT are applied at
test time
, incurring an expensive
O(K)
inference cost, CaOPD computes this signal during the training-phase data generation (Appendix
B.4
). By substituting it as the confidence target in the revised completion
\tilde{y}
, CaOPD
amortizes
the expensive test-time search cost into the model’s parametric weights, achieving self-consistency-level calibration in a single
O(1)
forward pass.

B.6
Extending CaOPD Without Objective Verifiers: Teacher-Anchored Self-Consistency

A potential critique of CaOPD is its reliance on an objective task verifier
R(x,a)
to compute the empirical target
\hat{\mu}(x)
. To address this, we first clarify the dependency structure of On-Policy Distillation (OPD), and then demonstrate how CaOPD can seamlessly operate in open-ended domains using Teacher-Anchored Self-Consistency.

The “Free Lunch” of Verifier Dependency in OPD.

It is crucial to recognize that the requirement for an objective verifier or ground-truth label is not uniquely introduced by CaOPD; it is a fundamental prerequisite of the underlying OPD paradigms themselves. SDPO explicitly requires environment verifiers (e.g., compilers or reward models) to generate the privileged feedback
z
. SDFT requires ground-truth demonstrations to construct the privileged teacher context
z
. Because the base OPD algorithms already assume access to verification signals to construct the privileged context, CaOPD simply reuses this
existing
infrastructure to score the student rollouts, introducing
zero additional assumptions or dependencies
.

Teacher-Anchored Self-Consistency.

In scenarios where an objective verifier is strictly unavailable (e.g., open-ended alignment tasks), CaOPD can substitute
R(x,a)
with an unsupervised agreement metric. We propose
Teacher-Anchored Self-Consistency
. Instead of computing the majority vote among the student’s
K
rollouts — which is vulnerable to “consistently wrong” snowballing hallucinations — we generate a reference trajectory from the model under privileged conditioning,
a_{\mathrm{ref}}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,z)
, and compute the agreement rate between the student rollouts and this reference:

\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{SC}}(x)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{I}\Big(\mathrm{Equiv}(a_{k},a_{\mathrm{ref}})\Big)

(23)

where
\mathrm{Equiv}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a semantic equivalence function (e.g., LLM-as-a-Judge).

This formulation solves the consensus hallucination problem: if the student consistently converges on a false premise (e.g., 8 identical wrong answers), but the model under privileged conditioning provides the correct answer, the equivalence score remains exactly
\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{SC}}(x)=0/8
. The confidence target is properly set to
0.0
, accurately reflecting the student’s inability to reach the correct state without privileged information.

Empirical Validation of TA-SC.

To empirically validate the effectiveness of Teacher-Anchored Self-Consistency (TA-SC), we conducted an ablation study using Qwen3-8B under the SDFT backbone. We replaced the exact objective verifier with the unsupervised TA-SC target. Specifically, we utilized a rule-based exact-match for Science Q&A, and a lightweight LLM judge to evaluate semantic equivalence for Tool Use.

The results, summarized in Table
4
, align with our theoretical expectations. While CaOPD with TA-SC exhibits a slight degradation compared to the objective verifier (e.g., ECE increases marginally from 0.266 to 0.271 on Science Q&A), it substantially outperforms the standard SDFT baseline.

The slight performance drop observed in the Tool Use domain (Accuracy decreases from 70.6% to 68.3%, SPR from 0.555 to 0.547) directly corroborates the
Spurious Disagreements
failure mode: open-ended JSON generations are inherently more susceptible to false-negative equivalence checks than rigid multiple-choice formats. Nevertheless, TA-SC drastically reduces the ECE (from 0.321 to 0.240) and restores discriminative ranking capability (SPR from 0.085 to 0.547) compared to standard distillation. This confirms that CaOPD is highly modular and maintains its decoupling even when relying on generative consensus in the absence of ground-truth verifiers.

B.7
Logit-Based vs. Verbalized Calibration: The Decoupling of Internal Entropy

A fundamental question regarding the CaOPD framework is its choice of targeting explicitly
verbalized
confidence rather than internal token-level probability distributions (logits), and whether altering this verbalized output inadvertently corrupts the model’s internal reasoning confidence.

Why Verbalized Confidence?
As summarized in Table
5
, estimating LLM uncertainty typically falls into three paradigms: logit-based
(
19
)
, sampling-based
(
24
;
48
)
, and verbalized confidence
(
28
)
. While logit-based methods (e.g., perplexity) are mathematically straightforward, they suffer from critical deployment limitations: they are entirely inaccessible for black-box proprietary APIs and require complex length-normalization for long-horizon reasoning trajectories. Sampling-based methods are robust but computationally prohibitive (
O(K)
latency) at test time.

Verbalized confidence represents the ideal deployment paradigm — it requires only a single forward pass and operates perfectly via black-box API interactions. However, as documented in our work, its fatal flaw is that it is severely miscalibrated and overconfident by default after standard post-training. CaOPD specifically targets this paradigm because solving verbalized overconfidence unlocks the most scalable and universally applicable form of uncertainty signaling.

Does Verbalized Humility Bleed into Internal Logit Entropy?

A natural question is whether training the model to output low verbalized confidence targets (
\hat{\mu}(x)\approx 0.0
) inadvertently causes the internal token-level distributions at reasoning positions (
t\in\mathcal{I}_{a}
) to become flattened. In other words, does the induced “humility” at confidence positions destructively bleed into the model’s core reasoning capability?

Our empirical training dynamics provide a clear answer:
No.
The autoregressive structure of the generation
\tilde{y}=(a,\hat{\mu}(x))
provides a natural
causal decoupling
. The per-token reverse KL (Equation (
7
)) at reasoning positions
t\in\mathcal{I}_{a}
is computed on prefixes
\tilde{y}_{<t}
that contain only reasoning tokens — the confidence replacement has not yet appeared in the autoregressive context. Consequently, the teacher’s distribution at these positions remains anchored to the privileged reasoning signal from
\tilde{z}
, unaffected by the confidence replacement. The gradient adjustments pulling the confidence tokens toward
\hat{\mu}(x)
are isolated to the final few sequence positions (
t\in\mathcal{I}_{c}
).

This decoupling is empirically confirmed by the left panel of Figure
2
: CaOPD’s accuracy trajectory perfectly shadows SDPO throughout training. If the low-confidence targets at
\mathcal{I}_{c}
were corrupting the logit entropy at
\mathcal{I}_{a}
, we would observe a tangible capability degradation. Instead, the Transformer architecture successfully maintains sharp internal distributions for reasoning while selectively routing epistemic uncertainty into the allocated confidence tokens.

C.2
Extended Evaluation Metrics

We introduce several metrics to comprehensively evaluate the capability and calibration of post-trained language models. Here, we provide their formal definitions and theoretical justifications.

Overconfidence Gap (OCG).
While standard proper scoring rules
like Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Brier Score (BS)
measure absolute conditional miscalibration,
they obscure the
direction
of the error
(i.e., whether the model is overconfident or underconfident).
To explicitly quantify the systematic optimism bias
induced by post-training, we track OCG,
defined as the difference between the expected
verbalized confidence and the empirical accuracy
over the test set:

\mathrm{OCG}=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{val}(c_{\theta}(X))\big]-\mathbb{E}[R]

(24)

A large positive OCG strictly indicates severe overconfidence, the primary pathology induced by standard OPD,
whereas a value near zero reflects well-calibrated
marginal expectations.

Strict Pairwise Ranking (SPR) vs. AUROC.
We introduce Strict Pairwise Ranking (SPR) to measure the discrimination ability of the model’s confidence. This metric is specifically designed to expose
confidence saturation
, a severe pathology observed in on-policy distillation where models uniformly report maximum confidence (e.g.,
c=1.0
) regardless of actual correctness.

Let
c_{+}
denote the confidence score assigned to a correct response, and
c_{-}
denote the confidence score assigned to an incorrect response. The standard Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC) can be formulated probabilistically as:

AUROC=\mathbb{P}(c_{+}>c_{-})+0.5\cdot\mathbb{P}(c_{+}=c_{-})

(25)

AUROC awards partial credit (0.5) when the confidence scores are tied (
c_{+}=c_{-}
). Consequently, a completely saturated model that outputs
c=1.0
for every single response will yield an AUROC of 0.5. While statistically representing a "random guessing" baseline, this value often visually obscures the complete collapse of the model’s discriminative power.

To strictly penalize this saturation, SPR removes the credit awarded for tied predictions:

SPR=\mathbb{P}(c_{+}>c_{-})

(26)

Under SPR, a tie yields 0 points. Therefore, if a model uniformly predicts
c=1.0
, its SPR drops exactly to 0. This metric directly answers the most critical question for deployment:
Does the model actually assign a strictly higher confidence to its correct answers compared to its mistakes?
By penalizing uninformative ties, SPR provides a much more sensitive and realistic measure of confidence discrimination for post-trained language models.

C.3
Detailed Baseline Descriptions

To comprehensively evaluate our proposed CaOPD, we compare it against two distinct categories of post-training methods: capability-focused on-policy training and calibration-aware reinforcement learning (RL).

1. Capability-Focused Paradigms.

These methods primarily optimize for task success (capability) and serve to empirically illustrate the severe overconfidence degradation typical in standard post-training pipelines.

•

SDFT

(
39
)
and
SDPO

(
17
)
: These represent the current
state-of-the-art in on-policy self-distillation.
The model generates its own trajectories
and is trained via reverse KL against itself
conditioned on privileged context
(e.g., demonstrations or environment feedback).

•

GRPO

(
38
)
: GRPO serves as a standard reinforcement learning baseline. It demonstrates the calibration effects of standard reward maximization without any specialized confidence penalties.

2. Calibration-Aware RL Methods.

To demonstrate that CaOPD is superior not only to standard distillation but also to specialized calibration techniques, we benchmark against state-of-the-art methods that explicitly shape rewards to penalize miscalibration.

•

RLCR

(
10
)
: A reinforcement learning approach that integrates proper scoring rules (such as the Brier score) directly into the PPO reward function to strictly penalize overconfident mistakes.

•

CAR (Calibration-Aware Reward)

(
50
)

Comparing CaOPD against these RL-based interventions allows us to assess whether our student-grounded empirical target replacement provides a fundamentally better capability-calibration trade-off than explicit, scalar reward shaping.

Appendix D
Extended Results and Analysis

D.1
Generalization and Continual Learning

In Section
5.4
, we presented the aggregated results demonstrating CaOPD’s robustness under Out-of-Distribution (OOD) shifts and Continual Learning. Tables
7
and
8
provide the exhaustive metrics for these settings across the Qwen3-8B and Olmo-3-7B-Instruct backbones. Here, we provide the implementation details and expand on the underlying mechanisms driving these results.

Implementation Details for Continual Training (CT).
To rigorously evaluate the models in a continual learning scenario, we adopt a straightforward sequential fine-tuning approach without introducing domain-specific replay buffers or complex regularization terms. Specifically, we first train the base model on Domain A (e.g., Tool Use) for the standard number of epochs to convergence. We then initialize the training for Domain B (e.g., Science Q&A) using this exact checkpoint, and perform
Continual Training (CT)
on Domain B for the exact same number of epochs and with identical hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate, batch size) as the standalone ID training. This isolates the effect of the optimization objective (SDFT vs. CaOPD) on preserving or forgetting calibration.

Why does standard OPD fail OOD?

The massive calibration collapse observed in standard SDFT (e.g., OOD ECE soaring to 0.599 in Table
7
) occurs because standard OPD forces the model to memorize the marginal confidence distribution of the source training domain. When shifted to an unseen OOD domain where the model’s intrinsic capability is strictly lower, the model blindly applies the highly confident linguistic tone (Optimism Bias) it memorized from the source domain. CaOPD prevents this by training the model to act as its own calibrator. Because the empirical target
\hat{\mu}(x)
is dynamically generated based on the model’s actual competence on the current prompt, the model learns the
functional mapping
from its own hidden state entropy to a confidence scalar, which robustly transfers across domains.

The Mechanism of "Calibration Forgetting" in Continual Learning.

In Continual Training, the model’s weights undergo sequential gradient updates to assimilate new knowledge. While self-distillation algorithms like SDFT are explicitly designed to prevent the catastrophic forgetting of
reasoning capabilities
(Accuracy), they completely fail to protect the calibration mapping. As shown in Table
8
, SDFT’s CT accuracy on Tool Use recovers perfectly to 0.647 (matching the ID baseline), but its SPR collapses completely to 0.000. This indicates a complete loss of discriminative resolution for confidence.

By continually enforcing the target replacement
(
\tilde{y}=(a,\hat{\mu}(x))
; Section
4.2
)
during sequential updates, CaOPD constantly re-anchors
the confidence tokens to the model’s evolving capability frontier.
This explicitly regularizes the confidence outputs
against the model’s actual execution outcomes on the new domain,
successfully immunizing the network against Calibration Forgetting.

D.2
The True Cost of Calibration: Test-Time Compute vs. Amortization

A natural concern is that CaOPD’s calibration gains
come at the expense of additional compute.
We clarify the cost structure at each stage.

Training-time overhead.

CaOPD’s gradient step is identical to standard SDPO or SDFT:
the same token-level forward/backward pass
on the revised completion
\tilde{y}

under student and teacher conditioning.
The only additional cost is computing
\hat{\mu}(x)

from
K
rollouts per prompt.
Crucially, this cost depends on the OPD backbone:

•

Under SDPO:

SDPO’s standard training loop
already samples multiple rollouts per prompt
to identify successful trajectories
for constructing the privileged context
z
.
CaOPD reuses these
same
rollouts
to evaluate
\hat{\mu}(x)
,
requiring only a lightweight verifier pass
on trajectories that have already been generated.
The marginal sampling cost is therefore
zero
;
the only addition is the verifier evaluation,
which is negligible compared to generation.

•

Under SDFT:

The standard SDFT pipeline generates
a single student trajectory per prompt.
CaOPD introduces
K
additional rollouts
for empirical confidence estimation.
At
K=8
(our default;
see Appendix
D.3

for the ablation),
this increases rollout generation
by a constant factor.
In practice, the overhead remains modest:
as shown in Figure
2
(right),
the wall-clock time per optimization step
is nearly indistinguishable from the baseline,
because rollout generation is parallelized via vLLM
and does not bottleneck the training loop.

Deployment-time savings.

The key comparison is against test-time calibration methods
such as Self-Consistency
(
48
)

or SelfCheckGPT
(
32
)
,
which require generating
K
responses
online

for every user query,
incurring
O(K)
inference latency.
CaOPD computes
\hat{\mu}(x)
entirely during training
and distills it into the model’s weights.
At deployment, calibrated confidence is produced
in a single
O(1)
forward pass
— the same cost as an uncalibrated model.
This makes CaOPD uniquely suited
for latency-sensitive agentic deployment
where per-query multi-sampling is prohibitive.

Detailed Analysis: Effect of Rollout Sample Size and Confidence Reshaping

To provide a deeper understanding
of the compute-efficiency and distributional shifts
induced by CaOPD, we expand upon the analysis
presented in Section
5.3
.
Figure
6
presents both the ablation
over the number of rollouts (
K
)
and the resulting structural changes
to the model’s confidence distribution.

Effect of Rollout Sample Size (
K
).

As shown in the left panel of Figure
6
,
we ablate
K\in\{1,2,4,8,16,32\}
.
Task accuracy (blue line) remains remarkably flat across all
K
,
confirming that capability is completely decoupled
from confidence sampling variance.
Calibration exhibits a phase transition:
small
K
(1–4) yields heavily quantized targets
that trap the model in overconfidence,
while
K=8
emerges as a compute-efficient sweet spot
where mean confidence aligns with actual accuracy
and discrimination (SPR) peaks.
Beyond
K=8

Why do small sample sizes (
K\leq 4
) fail?

The reason is
target quantization
.
When
K=1
, the empirical target
\hat{\mu}(x)

is strictly binary (
0.0
or
1.0
).
This structurally prevents the model
from learning intermediate confidence values,
pushing it back toward the same saturation pathology
as standard OPD.
At
K=8
, the target space has 9 distinct levels
(increments of
0.125
),
providing sufficient granularity
to represent graded epistemic uncertainty.
This also explains why CaOPD’s
Confidence Calibration term
(Equation (
7
),
t\in\mathcal{I}_{c}
)
requires a non-degenerate target distribution:
binary targets effectively reduce it
to a second copy of the saturation-inducing signal
that CaOPD is designed to eliminate.

visualize

into a delta function at
1.0
,
destroying the model’s capacity for graded uncertainty.

(
K=16

reflecting true execution variance across prompts.
Crucially, the deployed single-pass CaOPD model
(lower right) successfully
internalizes

this dispersion, actively placing uncertain predictions
into lower confidence bins.
This confirms that the target replacement
does not merely shift the mean confidence downward
but structurally restores the model’s ability
to
discriminate
between successes and failures, consistent with the large SPR improvements
reported in Tables
2
and
3
.

To provide a deeper understanding of how model capacity

Optimization Efficiency Scales with Capacity.

As shown in the left panel of Figure

,
convergence speed and terminal loss

Larger models (Qwen3-32B, 14B) converge rapidly

\hat{\mu}(x)

1/K

(see Section
D.3
),

The right panel of Figure

provides

in Appendix
8
.

\tilde{y}

\hat{\mu}(x)

(

In contrast, standard OPD has no such structural anchor
— the confidence format is only implicitly encouraged
by the prompt instruction,
which small models may initially fail to follow.

A large number of models, particularly from the Gemini family

Despite actual accuracies between 56% and 72%,

on-policy distillation and RLHF

Among the proprietary APIs, only

Appendix F
Broader Impact: Calibrated Small Models as Cascade Routers

In Section
5.5
(Figure
4
),
we demonstrated that CaOPD decouples capability
from calibration across model scales from 0.6B to 32B.
An interesting consequence emerges
at the lower end of the scaling curve:
small models trained with CaOPD can accurately
signal their own limitations.

As shown in the rightmost panel of Figure
4
,
the CaOPD-trained 0.6B model achieves approximately 58% accuracy,
and its mean confidence drops to roughly 0.3,
faithfully reflecting this limitation.
In contrast, the SDFT-trained 0.6B model
outputs a confidence of 1.0 despite failing nearly half the time.
This distinction is not merely cosmetic:
a confidence score of 1.0 on a 58%-accurate model
provides zero signal for downstream decision-making,
while a score of 0.3 is actionable.

Application: Confidence-Based Cascade Routing.

This property enables a practical deployment pattern.
In a cascade inference system,
a CaOPD-trained small model is deployed at the frontend.
For queries where the model’s confidence exceeds a threshold,
it serves the answer at low cost and latency.
For queries where confidence is low,
the system defers to a larger,
more expensive model.
The key requirement for such a system
is that the router’s confidence
must be
calibrated
, otherwise the threshold is meaningless.
Standard OPD models, which saturate at
c=1.0
,
cannot serve as routers regardless of their accuracy.
CaOPD structurally resolves this
by ensuring that confidence scales
with actual capability
(Figure
4
, right panel).

We note that this discussion identifies
a promising application direction;
empirically validating cascade routing performance
(e.g., cost-accuracy tradeoffs under different thresholds)
is left to future work.
