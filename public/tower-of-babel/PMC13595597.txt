Too many for too long: physicians dismantling mass incarceration
Authors: Michelle Suh, Justin Berk, Josiah Rich
Published: 2026

“If you are like the many people who approach us and ask, 'How do we end [mass incarceration]?', keep reading. You’ve come to the right place.” So closes the introduction to Dismantling Mass Incarceration: A Handbook for Change, a collection of abolitionist and reformist pieces edited by Premal Dharia, James Forman Jr., and Maria Hawilo. This book focuses on the United States which has the ignominious honor of being an historical and international outlier in incarcerating more of its citizenry than any other country ever, although its lessons are still relevant for all countries. The book is a helpful sampler of sorts, in the way a medical student completes their mandatory clerkships in their clinical curriculum. The expectation is not to master a specialty in a 4-week block but rather gain a rudimentary understanding of the field, and in the case of the book, a glimpse of what could be possible if we work towards a world in which a person is not judged for their worst moments. In this review, we summarize the book’s framework for dismantling mass incarceration and propose specific roles physicians can play in this effort.

The editors, all prominent former public defenders, organize the book into six parts, each focused on a key aspect of the criminal system: police, prosecutors, public defenders, judges, prisons, and post-release. Each part features interviews, articles, book excerpts, judges’ opinions, and organizing documents written by a range of guest authors, including Angela Y. Davis, Ruth Wilson Gilmore, progressive prosecutor Larry Krasner and organizer Derecka Purnell. The apparatus of mass incarceration did not appear overnight but was assembled brick by brick. Rather than identifying the magic brick-disappearing wand to wave, the book highlights different strategic bricks to tackle via a variety of perspectives, solutions, and perhaps most intriguingly, guiding questions for the reader to consider. As physicians with no legal training, we found the book to be a patient, thoughtful teacher who could explain the United States criminal legal system and paint clear throughlines of its structural racism and classist oppression, as well as its toll on the human rights and dignity of people who are incarcerated.

For example, part 1 focuses on police, the inadequacy of our current policing system, and potential alternatives. Ghettoside author Jill Leovy points out that “crime free” neighborhood “inhabitants are the inheritors of centuries-long legal, bureaucratic, and political processes that have manufactured high levels of personal safety…. They are the beneficiaries of institutional progress that has shifted the burden of conflict resolution from individuals, families, clans, or sects to a highly developed criminal justice system, rooted in democratic processes, controlled by an independent judiciary, and governed by the rule of law.” From a physician perspective, we see the harmful and widespread effects of personal, family, and neighborhood trauma, including sometimes the police response itself, on our patients. At times, we also participate and even perpetuate structural violence ourselves, whether medically “clearing” our patients for jail, disproportionately reporting families of color for child abuse and neglect, and operating within a healthcare system where insurance status affects access to care. The framework of structural violence calls on physicians to identify and address the institutional and systemic sources that perpetuate this trauma Farmer et al. 2006). The other essays highlight alternatives to policing and even successful pilot studies, such as traffic cameras to decrease the incidence of police traffic stops which lead to violence, CAHOOTS as a crisis response initiative, and the University of Chicago’s Crime Lab which showed the Choose to Change cognitive behavioral therapy programs reduced participants’ involvement in violence. Physicians can develop, implement, and disseminate evidence-based and trauma-informed interventions to address the root causes for communities suffering from structural violence.

The remaining five parts similarly balance the theoretical “brick” with practical examples of how dismantling of mass incarceration can happen. Part 2 focuses on prosecutors and the ability to “seek justice and accountability instead of convictions,” from supporting legislation limiting their power to increasing transparency of the office focusing on actions that repair neighborhoods rather than punish individuals. This is particularly needed given that in many communities, victims and perpetrators are often interlinked and may even be the same person. It also includes the striking essay by Paul Butler that questions whether progressive prosecutors can truly identify as judicial “environmentalists” rather than a “low-level polluter with a conscience.” Part 3 focuses on public defenders and the inherent tension between protecting clients versus enabling the court by providing “a veneer of impartiality and respectability”. Community organizer Raj Jayadev also proposes participatory defense, as a community-building mechanism for people facing the legal system. In our own community, physicians partnered with the local public defenders’ office to advocate for their patients, such as detailing the impact incarceration might have on a patient’s health Vanjani et al. (2020). Part 4 focuses on judges and their power to perpetuate or interrupt traditions such as bail or racist harsh sentencing practices. Part 5 focuses on prisons and a powerful collection of abolitionist perspectives that argue “reconciliation, not punishment” is a proper response to criminal acts. The example of North Dakota’s prison leadership enacting practices learned from the Norwegian approach of accountability and rehabilitation, and its benefits for staff as well as people who are incarcerated, show a different but attainable way from the United States’s relentless focus on punishment. We also acknowledge the role of physician researchers, such as Dr. Brie Williams, whose work demonstrated the powerful impact of rehabilitation interventions on not only those who are incarcerated but also the correctional officers involved Cloud et al. (2021). Physicians can also advocate for abolition by pursuing compassionate release for their medically ill patients Stanley et al. (2025) and partnering with medicolegal organizations such as Medical Justice Alliance. Finally, part 6 focuses on the aftermath, or the reality facing people upon release from incarceration. To be truly “released” after incarceration, people need meaningful opportunities for housing, education, employment, and civic engagement.

The editors write that one of the book’s unifying themes is that ordinary people can play powerful and necessary roles in creating change. As we read the book, we found numerous ways we can contribute towards dismantling mass incarceration – as physicians, and as ordinary citizens. Working in the emergency rooms and wards of safety net hospitals, and in correctional settings, we bear witness every shift to the physical and mental suffering of our patients, often as a direct result of these policies. Giving voice to the cost of the structural vulnerabilities of our incarcerated and formerly incarcerated patients can enlighten our fellow citizens, as well as the actors in the criminal system. Physicians are uniquely positioned to describe the impacts of mass incarceration; given our voices in the public square, we can use this opportunity to remove the bricks composing mass incarceration. For example, Harvard Law School lecturer and retired Judge Nancy Gertner writes that judges should receive “training about the impact of trauma and exposure to violence, poverty, and lack of access to schools, health care, employment, etc…. They should hear from scientists about the neuroscience of trauma, addiction, and adolescent neurodevelopment; from sociologists about the social and cultural contexts of men and women they are sentencing; from health professionals about the social determinants of health.”

In addition to education, addressing employment for people with criminal records is critical-not just for earning income but also feeling welcomed back into society and having a purpose. In this case, Johns Hopkins Hospital is featured for its hiring practices. Concrete steps that could be reproducible at other hospital employers include delaying background checks (based on research that employers are less likely to consider a conviction disqualifying after meeting the applicant) and limiting employment restrictions to specific jobs (as a drug conviction likely has no bearing on the accounting department).

However, the discussion is not finished yet. We want to emphasize a major source of mass incarceration – namely, the War on Drugs. The overdose crisis has led to a million deaths in the last few decades, and the public health movement has led to more support for treating opioid addiction as a disease rather than a crime. For example, research has shown drug busts by law enforcement actually increased local community overdoses Ray et al. (2023). In contrast, medications for opioid use disorder and other supportive services have successfully disentangled people from the cycle of addiction and incarceration Evans et al. (2022), Friedmann et al. (2025). However, this response stands in stark contrast to the criminalization of other drugs in the history of the United States that disproportionately affected communities of color. The structural racism leading to the racial disparities in incarceration cannot be ignored.

There are additional tensions left unresolved by the book, including the eternal struggle between reform and abolition. The authors offer a variety of viewpoints, such as the benefits for immediate, pragmatic steps to reform for people currently suffering within the system to the limits of seeking incremental change rather than pursuing true abolition. However, even since the release of this book, the United States has seen an increasingly authoritarian bent to policing and immigration enforcement, including the extrajudicial killings of its citizens in Minnesota. This raises the larger question of the limits of individual action versus structural change, and the stakes have never been higher. If physicians across all specialties, practice settings, and communities come together, we could be a powerful force raising our collective voices and pursuing strategic actions using the book’s framework for dismantling mass incarceration.

Given the dramatic changes in the current United States federal administration, the important messages of this book are more relevant than ever. Indeed, the bricks composing our monstrous apparatus of mass incarceration seem to be piling up even more in present-day. Now, more than ever, an intentional, collective strategy to dismantling the individual bricks within our sphere is critical. In the next election, do we have the option to vote for a prosecutor who supports jail diversion programs such as drug rehabilitation over sentencing? Can we establish a medicolegal partnership to advocate for our patients in court, not just clinic? Are we working towards reform and abolition or perpetuating a system of mass incarceration and inequality? Although these things require awareness and engagement beyond our sole clinical duties as physicians, the book makes a compelling case that the cost of mass incarceration is too high for the status quo to remain.
