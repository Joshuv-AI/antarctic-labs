Reflective lattices and hyperkähler manifolds
Ekaterina Amerik

Abstract




Abstract:

Using the results of Nikulin and Vinberg on the
groups of isometries generated by reflections, we construct a subvariety
called the Nikulin–Vinberg locus in the moduli space of
polarized hyperkähler manifolds. It is obtained as a finite union
of components of higher Noether–Lefschetz loci which parameterize manifolds with
certain special Néron–Severi lattices. The Nikulin–Vinberg locus
is the closure of the set of hyperkähler manifolds with Picard number
\geqslant 3

which have finite groups of birational automorphisms.
Using this construction and a refinement of an argument by Oguiso, we show that any non-trivial family
of projective deformations of a hyperkähler manifold with
b_{2}(M)\geqslant 6

has a dense set of fibers which have an infinite group of
birational automorphisms.

1.1
Lattices

An integral lattice is a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module
\Lambda\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{r}
together with a non-degenerate
symmetric bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot)\colon\Lambda\otimes\Lambda\to\mathbb{Z}
.
The corresponding quadratic form will be denoted by
q
, so that
q(v)=(v,v)

for any
v\in\Lambda
.
We will also extend
(\cdot,\cdot)
to a
\mathbb{Q}
-bilinear form on the vector
space
\Lambda\otimes\mathbb{Q}
.
The dual lattice
\Lambda^{*}=\operatorname{Hom}(\Lambda,\mathbb{Z})
is embedded into
\Lambda\otimes\mathbb{Q}
via the form

(\cdot,\cdot)
, so that

\Lambda^{*}=\{v\in\Lambda\otimes\mathbb{Q}\enskip|\enskip\forall u\in\Lambda\,\,\,(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}\},

and the quotient
\Lambda^{*}/\Lambda
is called the discriminant group, denoted
d(\Lambda)
.
The exponent of the lattice is the smallest positive
integer
e(\Lambda)
which annihilates the discriminant group.

A vector
v\in\Lambda\otimes\mathbb{Q}
with
q(v)\neq 0

(such
v
is called non-isotropic)
defines a reflection

s_{v}
, that is the following involution of
\Lambda\otimes\mathbb{Q}
:

s_{v}(u)=u-2\frac{(u,v)}{(v,v)}v.

A primitive non-isotropic element
v\in\Lambda
is called a root if
s_{v}(\Lambda)=\Lambda
. Equivalently,

v
is a root if
q(v)
divides
2(u,v)
for all
u\in\Lambda
. It is
well-known (and follows directly from the definitions) that in this case
q(v)

divides
2e(\Lambda)
, see e.g.
[
SW
, section 1]
. Any vector
v\in\Lambda

with
0<|q(v)|\leq 2
is automatically a root. Let us denote by
\mathrm{R}(\Lambda)
the set
of all roots
v\in\Lambda
with
q(v)<0
. Note our convention for the sign of the roots:
the condition
q(v)<0
is not standard and not necessary for studying lattices in general, but
for our applications only the negative roots will be relevant.

Given a lattice
\Lambda
, consider the group
\mathrm{O}(\Lambda)
of orthogonal transformations
of
\Lambda\otimes\mathbb{Q}
preserving
\Lambda
. The reflections
s_{v}
, where
v\in\mathrm{R}(\Lambda)
, generate a subgroup
\mathrm{W}(\Lambda)\subset\mathrm{O}(\Lambda)
called
the Weyl group of
\Lambda
. If the lattice
\Lambda
has no negative roots, the Weyl group is trivial.

A lattice
\Lambda
is called Lorentzian if the quadratic form
q

has signature
(1,r-1)
. Note that in the literature dedicated
to the theory of lattices another convention is often used: the Lorentzian signature is
(r-1,1)
.
Our convention is more convenient in the context of algebraic geometry.

A lattice
\Lambda
is called reflective if
W(\Lambda)

is of finite index in
O(\Lambda)
. We refer to
[
Bel
]

for an overview of the results related to the classification of reflective Lorentzian lattices.

1.2
Moduli spaces of polarized hyperkähler manifolds

By a hyperkähler (also known as irreducible holomorphically symplectic
or IHS) manifold
M
we will mean a compact simply connected complex
2n
-dimensional
manifold that admits a Riemannian metric of holonomy
\mathrm{Sp}(n)
,
see
[
Bea
]
,
[
Ma
]
and
[
Ve
]
for the
definitions and the foundations of the theory of such manifolds.

Let
\Lambda=H^{2}(M,\mathbb{Z})
be the second cohomology of a hyperkähler
manifold
M
equipped with the Beauville–Bogomolov–Fujiki (BBF) form
q
.
The latter has signature
(3,b_{2}(M)-3)
, and we will assume that it is
rescaled to be integral and primitive. All deformations of
M
that admit
Kähler metrics will also be hyperkähler manifolds with the same
\Lambda

and
q
.

From now on we fix a deformation class of hyperkähler manifolds and
denote by
M
one of the manifolds in this deformation class.
The moduli theory of the deformations of
M
is governed by the period
domains attached to
\Lambda
. Let us recall their definitions.

Given a primitive sublattice
\mathbb{L}\subset\Lambda
of signature
(1,k)
,
its orthogonal complement
\mathbb{L}^{\perp}
in
\Lambda
has signature
(2,b_{2}(M)-k)
.
Let

\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{L}}=\{x\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{L}^{\perp}\otimes\mathbb{C})\enskip|\enskip q(x)=0,\,q(x,\overline{x})>0\}.

This is a Hermitian symmetric domain whose points parametrize hyperkähler
manifolds
M
with
\mathbb{L}\subset\mathrm{NS}(M)
, where the latter is the Néron–Severi
group of
M
.
For a very general (that is, lying outside of a countable union
of proper subvarieties) point of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{L}}
we have equality
\mathbb{L}=\mathrm{NS}(M)
.
We will call
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{L}}
the period domain of
\mathbb{L}
-polarized hyperkähler manifolds.
Note that the condition on the signature of
\mathbb{L}
guarantees that all
\mathbb{L}
-polarized
hyperkähler manifolds are projective. In particular, if
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{Z}h
for some
primitive
h\in\Lambda
with
q(h)>0
, we will write
\mathcal{D}_{h}
instead of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{L}}
and call it
the period domain of
h
-polarized hyperkähler manifolds.
We will denote the orthogonal complement
h^{\bot}
in
\Lambda

by
\Lambda_{h}
.

Clearly, given two primitive sublattices
\mathbb{L}_{1}\subset\mathbb{L}_{2}
, we have

\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{L}_{2}}\subset\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{L}_{1}}
. In particular, if
h\in\mathbb{L}
, we
have
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{L}}\subset\mathcal{D}_{h}
. We call such
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{L}}
a special subvariety
of the
h
-polarized period domain
\mathcal{D}_{h}
.

The mapping class group
\Gamma=\operatorname{Diff}(M)/\operatorname{Diff}_{0}(M)

acts on the Teichmüller space parametrizing the complex structures
on
M
, see
[
Ve
]
. Let
\Gamma^{M}
denote
its subgroup preserving the connected component of the
Teichmüller space containing
M
.
Recall that the image of
\Gamma^{M}
in
\mathrm{O}(\Lambda)

under the natural action on the second cohomology is a finite index subgroup
of
\mathrm{O}(\Lambda)
called the monodromy group, denoted
\mathrm{Mon}(M)
. For
\mathbb{L}\subset\Lambda

we will denote by
\mathrm{Mon}_{\mathbb{L}}(M)\subset\mathrm{Mon}(M)
the (pointwise) stabilizer
of the sublattice
\mathbb{L}
.

The image of
\mathrm{Mon}_{\mathbb{L}}(M)
is of finite index in
\mathrm{O}(\mathbb{L}^{\perp})
.
It is well-known (see
[
BB
]
) that the quotient
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{L}}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{L}}/\mathrm{Mon}_{\mathbb{L}}(M)

is a quasi-projective variety. We will call this quotient the moduli space of

\mathbb{L}
-polarized hyperkähler manifolds. When
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{Z}h
, the moduli space of

h
-polarized hyperkähler manifolds will be denoted by

\mathcal{M}_{h}=\mathcal{D}_{h}/\mathrm{Mon}_{h}(M)
. It comes with
the collection of special subvarieties
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{L}}
corresponding to the sublattices

\mathbb{L}
containing
h
.

Recall how one can describe the (bimeromorphic) automorphism group of
M

in terms of
\mathrm{NS}(M)
. There exists a
\mathrm{Mon}(M)
-invariant collection of second
cohomology classes in
\Lambda
called MBM classes, see
[
AV2
]
. We will denote them
by
\mathrm{MBM}\subset\Lambda
. Recall from
[
AV1
]
that
\mathrm{MBM}
consists of a
finite number of
\mathrm{Mon}(M)
-orbits, in particular the BBF squares of the MBM classes are bounded.

Let
\mathbb{L}=\mathrm{NS}(M)
and
\mathrm{MBM}_{\mathbb{L}}=\mathrm{MBM}\cap\mathbb{L}
. Denote by
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{L}\otimes\mathbb{R}

the connected component of the positive cone that contains the Kähler classes. Then
for
x\in\mathrm{MBM}_{\mathbb{L}}
the hyperplanes
x^{\perp}
cut
\mathcal{C}
into a locally finite collection
of open subcones called Kähler chambers. The automorphism group of
M
is isomorphic to
the subgroup of
\mathrm{Mon}(M)
that preserves
\mathbb{L}
, the Kähler chamber of
M
and the period of
M
.

The divisorial MBM classes on
M
are elements of
\mathrm{MBM}_{\mathbb{L}}
which are stably prime exceptional (
[
Ma
]
, section 6; see also
[
AV0
]
). Let
W^{+}(\mathbb{L})
be the subgroup of
W(\Lambda)
that is
generated by reflections in divisorial MBM classes. Recall from
[
Ma
]
that we
have
W^{+}(\mathbb{L})\subset\mathrm{Mon}(M)
. Since the roots that we are considering are always
q
-negative,
the action of
W^{+}(\mathbb{L})
on
\mathbb{L}\otimes\mathbb{R}
preserves the cone
\mathcal{C}
. The fundamental
domain of
W^{+}(\mathbb{L})
-action on
\mathcal{C}
that contains a Kähler class of
M
is called the
bimeromorphic Kähler chamber of
M
. The group of bimeromorphic automorphisms of
M
is
isomorphic to the subgroup of
\mathrm{Mon}(M)
that preserves the bimeromorphic Kähler chamber and the
Hodge structure of
M
(in particular,

\mathbb{L}=\mathrm{NS}(M)
is preserved automatically). It is finite exactly when
W^{+}(\mathbb{L})
is of finite index in the orthogonal group (
[
Ma
]
, theorem 6.18; see also
[
Vi3
]
for general lattice-theoretic
treatment).

Proposition 2.1:

There exists a finite collection of lattices
\mathbb{M}_{i}

Proof:
We denote by
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{V}
the set of isomorphism classes of integral reflective Lorentzian
lattices of rank at least three that are unscaled (i.e. such that the Gram matrix
of the lattice is indivisible). It follows form
[

and

[
Vi2
, Theorems 2 and 4]
that the set
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{V}
is finite.

Recall from Section
1.2
that under our assumptions on

the orthogonal group
of the lattice

contains a finite index subgroup generated by reflections
in the roots that are also MBM classes. It follows that the lattice
\mathrm{NS}(M)
is reflective,
though possibly not unscaled. Recall also that the squares of the MBM classes are
negative and bounded, the bound being deformation-invariant. Let
-N
be the lower bound
for the squares of the MBM classes. Define

\mathcal{N}\mathcal{V}^{+}=\cup_{k=1}^{N}\{\mathbb{L}(k)\enskip|\enskip\mathbb{L}\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{V}\}
.
The latter is a finite set of isomorphism classes of lattices, and we
see that
\mathrm{NS}(M)\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{V}^{+}
.

To conclude, recall e.g. from
[

, Satz 30.2]

that for any lattice
\mathbb{L}
there exist only finitely many primitive embeddings

\mathbb{I}(d)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{L}
, up to the action of
\mathrm{O}(\mathbb{L})
.
For every
\mathbb{L}\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{V}^{+}
and for every such primitive embedding

\mathbb{I}(d)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{L}
consider its orthogonal complement

\mathbb{M}=\mathbb{I}(d)^{\perp}\subset\mathbb{L}
. Then there are, up to isomorphism,
only finitely many lattices that can occur as such orthogonal complements.
We denote these lattices
\mathbb{M}_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,m_{0}\}
.
Since
\mathrm{NS}(M)\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{V}^{+}
and
M
is
h
-polarized, with
q(h)=d
,
we see that
\mathrm{NS}(M)\cap\Lambda_{h}
is one of the orthogonal complements
constructed above.

We now define the family of subvarieties that form the Nikulin–Vinberg
locus in the moduli space. Recall from Section

that
\mathrm{Mon}_{h}\subset\mathrm{O}(\Lambda_{h})

is a finite index subgroup. Combining this fact with
[

we conclude that for each of the lattices

from

there exist finitely many (up to the action of

\mathbb{M}^{\prime}_{j}\subset\Lambda_{h}

the images of such embeddings that represent every of the

for all

from

Definition 2.2:

Let
\mathcal{X}_{i}\subset\mathcal{M}_{h}

2.2
Binary Lorentzian lattices

Given
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we will say that a lattice
\mathbb{L}
represents
n
if there exists
a non-zero
v\in\mathbb{L}

with
q(v)=n
. For a lattice
\mathbb{L}
let us denote by
\mu(\mathbb{L})
the following number

\mu(\mathbb{L})=\mathrm{max}\big(\{q(v)\enskip|\enskip v\in\mathbb{L}\}\cap\mathbb{Z}_{<0}\big),

i.e.
\mu(\mathbb{L})
is the largest negative integer represented by
\mathbb{L}
.

Given two lattices, the symbol
\oplus
will be used to denote their orthogonal direct sum.
A binary lattice is a lattice of rank two. We will consider
binary Lorentzian lattices
\mathbb{L}_{a}=(\mathbb{Z}^{2},q)
, where

q=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-a\end{pmatrix}
for some integer
a>0
.

Proposition 2.3:

Let
n>0
be a fixed integer. Then for any integer
b>0
there exist infinitely
many integers
a>0
such that
\mathbb{L}_{ab}
does not contain isotropic vectors and

\mu(\mathbb{L}_{ab})<-n
, i.e. such that the lattice
\mathbb{L}_{ab}
does
not represent
0,-1,-2,\ldots,-n
.

Proof:
For any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
the condition that
\mathbb{L}_{ab}
represents
-k
means that
there exist
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
x^{2}-aby^{2}=-k
. This implies
that
-k\equiv x^{2}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,p)
for any prime
p
dividing
a
.
For any
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
choose a prime
p_{k}
that violates the
condition
-k\equiv x^{2}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,p_{k})
for any

x
(
-k
is not a quadratic residue).
By
2.2
below there exist infinitely many such primes,
so we may also assume that
p_{k}
are distinct and greater than
b
. Then define
a=\prod_{k}p_{k}
.
By construction, the integer
ab
is not a square, hence
\mathbb{L}_{ab}
does not
represent zero, and since there are infinitely many choices of
p_{k}
for every
k
,
there are also infinitely many choices of
a
as above.

Lemma 2.4:

For any integer
k>0
there exist infinitely many primes
p
such that

-k
is not a quadratic residue modulo
p
.

Proof:
We have
k=2^{a_{0}}\prod_{i}q_{i}^{a_{i}}
where
q_{i}
are distinct odd primes,
a_{i}>0
. Then

\left(\frac{-k}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)\left(\frac{2}{p}\right)^{a_{0}}\prod_{i}\left(\frac{q_{i}}{p}\right)^{a_{i}},

(2.1)

where
\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)
denotes the Legendre symbol.
Imposing the condition
p\equiv-1\,(\mathrm{mod}\,8)
will guarantee that

\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)=-1,\quad\left(\frac{2}{p}\right)=1,

implying that the product of the first two factors in (
2.1
) is equal to
-1

for any
a_{0}
.
Moreover, assuming that
p\neq q_{i}
for all
i
, the congruence
p\equiv-1\,(\mathrm{mod}\,8)
implies that we have

\left(\frac{q_{i}}{p}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{q_{i}-1}{2}}\left(\frac{p}{q_{i}}\right),

by the quadratic reciprocity. Choosing the residues of
p
modulo
q_{i}

we can guarantee that
\left(\frac{q_{i}}{p}\right)=1
for all
q_{i}
.
Then (
2.1
) will imply that
\left(\frac{-k}{p}\right)=-1
.
By Dirichlet’s theorem on arithmetic progressions there exist infinitely many primes
p
that
satisfy the above congruences.

Remark 2.5:
If one needs to construct a binary Lorentzian anisotropic lattice

2.4
Strong density of a family of divisors

We next discuss the density properties of the divisors constructed above.

Definition 2.8:

We call a family of divisors
\{\mathcal{Y}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
in a complex analytic space
\mathcal{M}

strongly dense if the following condition holds: for any
non-constant holomorphic map
f\colon\Delta\to\mathcal{M}
, where
\Delta

is the unit disc in
\mathbb{C}
, the subset
f^{-1}(\cup_{j}\mathcal{Y}_{j})
is dense in
\Delta
.

Remark 2.9:
Strong density clearly implies that
\cup_{j}\mathcal{Y}_{j}
is dense in
\mathcal{M}

in the analytic topology, because through any point
p\in\mathcal{M}
there
pass arbitrarily small holomorphic discs, and any of those discs has
to intersect at least one of the divisors
\mathcal{Y}_{j}
. The converse is not
true. For example, consider the linear projection
\pi\colon\mathbb{C}P^{2}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}P^{1}

from a fixed point in
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
. For a countable and dense set of points
in
\mathbb{C}P^{1}
, their preimages under
\pi
form a family of lines that
is dense but not strongly dense in
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
.

Our proof of strong density of the divisors constructed in
2.3
is similar to Oguiso’s arguments from
[
Og
]

\{\mathcal{Y}_{j}\}
in a neighbourhood of a point

f(\Delta)
, and then approach this hyperplane by
\{\mathcal{Y}_{j}\}

we will need to recall the description of
\mathcal{D}_{h}
as a homogeneous space of

[
AV1
]
