Development of vignette-based evaluation with generative artificial intelligence (VEGA) for nursing students: a pilot study
Authors: Sujin Shin, Jaehwa Choi, Eunmin Hong, Miji Lee, Jinseon Go, Subin Yu, Minjae Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdvances in AI have introduced new opportunities in nursing education. Vignette-based item sets are valuable for evaluating clinical reasoning, but are time-consuming to develop. Generative AI offers a scalable approach to streamline item generation. This study aimed to develop and evaluate a vignette-based evaluation with generative artificial intelligence (VEGA), which automatically generates vignette-based item sets, defined as short clinical scenarios followed by structured assessment items.MethodsA pilot study employed a mixed-methods design to evaluate the usability and user experiences of VEGA among nursing students. VEGA, a generative AI agent for vignette-based item set generation within the Collective AI on the Foundation AI platform, was developed based on the Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, and Evaluation model and structured according to the NCSBN Clinical Judgment Measurement Model. Content validity was established through expert review and a preliminary survey prior to implementation. Quantitative data were collected through a post-usability survey administered to 12 undergraduate nursing students, and qualitative data were obtained through focus group interviews exploring their user experiences.ResultsIn the quantitative phase, VEGA demonstrated an overall usability score of 3.62 ± 1.04, with the highest domain score for information (4.25 ± 0.87). In the qualitative phase, focus group interviews identified four themes: individualized learning, enhancement of clinical reasoning, applicability in education and practice, and areas for improvement.ConclusionThe AI-based item generation tool was perceived by nursing students as a potential educational resource for supporting engagement with clinical reasoning, with further improvements needed in technical stability, feedback depth, and multimedia integration. Given the small sample size and pilot study design, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09685-8.Keywords: Artificial Intelligence, Clinical Decision-Making, Computer-Assisted Instruction, Educational Technology, Nursing Education

Introduction

Background

Information technology innovations such as big data, augmented reality, mixed reality, simulation, and artificial intelligence (AI) are revolutionizing education and healthcare, potentially heralding a new era of teaching methods [1]. Among these, ChatGPT, an AI-driven conversational large language model (LLM), has significant potential in healthcare education, research, and practice if its related concerns are actively addressed [2]. It has been extensively studied for various applications in healthcare education, notably item generation [3, 4].

A vignette, typically presented in textual or visual form as a descriptive episode that simulates real-life events, is often used in conjunction with terms such as case studies, scenarios, or stories to enrich the context and details of a situation [5]. Such item formats have recently gained prominence in licensure examinations. For example, the National Council of State Boards of Nursing (NCSBN) developed the Next Generation NCLEX (NGN) to ensure alignment with essential nursing competencies by introducing a new case-based item type composed of six items linked to a single patient presentation [6].

Although vignette-based learning is effective in promoting clinical judgment and critical thinking among nursing students, it can be time-consuming and burdensome for nursing educators [7, 8]. These demands make it difficult to create a large and diverse pool of vignettes, thereby limiting student exposure to a wide range of clinical situations. Existing approaches to automated item generation have demonstrated efficiency in producing standardized test items [9, 10]; however, they have largely focused on general knowledge assessment and have not been designed to generate clinically contextualized, multi-step vignette-based item sets aligned with nursing-specific frameworks such as the NCSBN Clinical Judgment Measurement Model (NCJMM).

Despite growing interest in the use of generative AI in healthcare education, little research has examined how generative AI can be applied to automate the development of vignette-based item sets in nursing education. Therefore, a tool capable of generating diverse, NCJMM-aligned vignette-based items on demand is needed to support scalable clinical reasoning practice. This study aims to develop and evaluate Vignette-based Evaluation with Generative Artificial Intelligence (VEGA), an AI-based tool that automatically generates structured vignette-based items aligned with the NCJMM. VEGA provides learner-response-based feedback to support competency development in nursing education.

Practical needs: the rationale for a vignette-based approach

As noted above, vignette-based learning is a valuable pedagogical approach in nursing education; however, its implementation remains constrained by practical resource limitations. Despite these educational benefits, the widespread use of vignette-based learning has been limited by the substantial time, cost, and expertise required to develop high-quality and pedagogically aligned scenarios [7, 8]. In particular, implementing case-based learning across a curriculum can be burdensome due to variability in instructors, clinical settings, teaching styles, and time constraints, making consistent and scalable implementation difficult [8]. This resource-intensive process thereby limits students’ exposure to a wide range of clinical situations.

VEGA was developed to address this practical challenge by automating the generation of vignette-based item sets using generative artificial intelligence. By enabling the on-demand creation of diverse and structured clinical scenarios, VEGA reduces the resource burden associated with manual development and supports scalable opportunities for clinical reasoning practice across a broad range of patient cases.

Theoretical framework

This study is grounded in two complementary theoretical frameworks: the NCJMM, which provided the theoretical basis for the content and structure of the tool, and the ADDIE instructional design model, which guided its developmental process [9]. The NCJMM, developed by NCSBN [6], delineates a six-step clinical judgment process: recognizing cues, analyzing cues, prioritizing hypotheses, generating solutions, taking action, and evaluating outcomes. In this study, each of the six NCJMM stages directly shaped the structure of the vignette-based items generated by VEGA: one item per stage was developed, with prompt instructions explicitly operationalized to reflect the cognitive demands of each step. This alignment ensured that the item sets were not only clinically contextualized but also pedagogically grounded in a recognized framework for nursing clinical judgment.

The ADDIE model, which consists of the phases of analysis, design, development, implementation, and evaluation, guided each phase of the tool development in a concrete way [9]. Specifically, the analysis phase informed the selection of body systems and disease conditions; the design phase determined the vignette structure and feedback format; the development phase encompassed algorithm construction and content validation; the implementation phase involved student use of the tool; and the evaluation phase incorporated both quantitative usability data and qualitative user experience interviews. Through the integration of NCJMM for content alignment and ADDIE for development methodology, this study seeks to ensure both pedagogical relevance and methodological rigor in the design and deployment of an AI-supported system for vignette-based item set generation.

Methods

Study design and aim

This pilot study aimed to evaluate the usability and user experiences of VEGA, a generative artificial intelligence-based vignette evaluation system. A convergent parallel mixed-methods design was employed, in which quantitative usability data and qualitative interview data were collected concurrently and merged at the interpretation stage to provide a comprehensive understanding of users’ experiences with the system. This study followed relevant EQUATOR reporting standards and applied the Good Reporting of A Mixed-Methods Study (GRAMMS) guidelines [10]. A completed GRAMMS checklist is provided as supplementary file 1.

Participants and setting

The inclusion criteria were as follows: third- or fourth-year undergraduate nursing students in South Korea who owned a smart device, had clinical practicum experience, and completed coursework in adult nursing. There were no exclusion criteria for this study. Based on previous studies on the use of digital healthcare applications among nursing students [11] and methodological recommendations suggesting that six to ten participants are appropriate for focus group interviews (FGI) [12], the minimum sample size was set at ten. This is further supported by usability studies of mobile apps for health care education, in which sample sizes have ranged widely and samples of fewer than ten participants have been commonly employed in pilot evaluations [13]. Twelve students were recruited, accounting for a potential 20% dropout rate.

Data collection procedures

Participants were recruited using purposive sampling, which is commonly adopted in qualitative research, to select participants who could provide rich and relevant information aligned with the study’s aims [14]. Faculty members from nursing universities assisted with recruitment by sharing study information with eligible nursing students. Students who expressed interest completed a pre-study online survey to provide demographic and background information and register for participation. After using VEGA for one week, participants completed a post-usage survey assessing usability, followed by FGIs to explore their learning experiences. The participants in the preliminary survey were a separate group of nursing students from those who took part in the main study. In the main study, the same 12 participants completed the pre-study survey, used VEGA for one week, completed the post-study usability survey, and participated in the FGIs.

VEGA development

The VEGA was developed using the ADDIE model [9]. It was built on the Collective AI on the Foundation AI (CAFA) Platform [15], a generative AI-based engineering platform designed for structured and scalable application development (Fig. 1). The VEGA platform [16] is accessible at https://ai.cafalab.com/E4AF175E-4247-497D-A9B5-283010D46F8A and requires a GPT Application Programming Interface (API) key. VEGA employed a LLM accessed via the OpenAI API (GPT-4.1-nano). Few-shot/zero-shot prompting and dynamic-system-prompt context engineering was applied at each generation stage using exemplar vignette-item pairs aligned with publicly available NCSBN materials. The CAFA platform operates through multiple structured prompts across the generation pipeline. For item generation, a single prompt incorporating explicit stage-specific instructions and exemplar items for all six NCJMM stages was used.

Stage 1. Analysis

During the analysis phase, the NCJMM was reviewed to establish a six-item structure for each vignette, with each item corresponding to one of the core stages of the model. A widely used medical-surgical nursing textbook was used to classify nine body systems (cardiovascular, respiratory, nervous, sensory, gastrointestinal, urinary, reproductive, endocrine, and musculoskeletal) and identify representative disease conditions, ensuring alignment with standard nursing curricula. These conditions were organized into structured clinical frameworks to guide vignette and item development, and distilled into system-level schemas and embedded into the generation framework to constrain the scope of clinical content and minimize the generation of non-curricular or clinically inaccurate outputs.

Stage 2. Design

During the design phase, the vignette content was constructed to simulate real-world clinical situations by incorporating patient symptoms, medical history, and diagnostic results. Each vignette was paired with six NCJMM-aligned items to support a structured and pedagogically grounded approach to item development. Immediate feedback was designed to follow each learner’s response, providing explanatory rationales in addition to indicating correctness. Few-shot prompting techniques [17, 18], based on exemplar vignettes and item formats published by the NCSBN [19], were used to generate stage-specific items aligned with the cognitive demands of each NCJMM step. Each NCJMM stage was operationalized through explicit prompt instructions that defined the expected cognitive task and constrained item generation to prevent overlap between stages, thereby ensuring functional pedagogical alignment. The pedagogical quality of the feedback was evaluated as part of the content validity process conducted by nursing experts in Stage 3.

Stage 3. Development

An algorithm was implemented using the CAFA framework to automatically generate clinical vignettes and the associated NCJMM-based items (Fig. 2). Learners first selected a body system through the interface, after which a probabilistic filtering mechanism was applied to promote content diversity. Specifically, this mechanism randomly selected a disease condition from a predefined list established during the analysis phase, and combined with the inherently probabilistic nature of LLMs, prevented repeated generation of the same conditions. The system then indexed the selected content, generated a vignette, and produced six NCJMM-aligned items, which were translated into Korean and presented to the learners. Through a chat-based interface, learners submitted responses, received immediate feedback with explanations, and could either continue with a new item set or end the session.

A preliminary survey was conducted with ten undergraduate nursing students to assess the clarity and comprehensibility of the vignettes, items, and feedback. The participants used VEGA at least three times (three item sets, totaling 18 items) before completing the survey. The survey used a 4-point Likert scale to evaluate whether the content provided by the application was easy to understand and read. The preliminary survey questionnaire is provided in supplementary file 2.

Content validity was evaluated by six experts including nursing faculty members and clinical nurses, using an eight-item survey assessing the appropriateness and clarity of the vignettes, items, and feedback. Experts rated each item on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not valid at all to 4 = highly valid). The content validity index (CVI) was calculated as the proportion of ratings of 3 (valid) or 4 (highly valid), with values ≥ 0.78 and S-CVI/Ave ≥ 0.90 considered acceptable for a panel of six experts [20].

Stage 4. Implementation

During the implementation phase, 12 third- and fourth-year undergraduate nursing students who voluntarily agreed to participate were provided access to VEGA. Before using VEGA, participants completed a brief online pre-study survey assessing demographic characteristics and prior experience with AI-based learning tools. After registration, participants received individualized access to VEGA and were instructed to use the app freely for one week. Technical support was provided upon request. Participants were asked to complete a minimum of six item sets, with optional additional use. To monitor engagement and verify fidelity, participants were required to upload screenshots of their completed item sets, along with the date of each session, to a shared Google Sheet. Stage 5. Evaluation

During the evaluation phase, a mixed-methods approach was used to assess the usability and user experience of VEGA. For the quantitative usability evaluation, participants completed a post-usage online survey after one week of app use. The survey included the Mobile Application Rating Scale (MARS) [21], using the Korean version translated by Lee [22]. Eighteen items across five domains—engagement, functionality, aesthetics, information, and subjective quality—were rated on a 5-point Likert scale. The survey questionnaire is provided in supplementary file 3.

Qualitative data were collected through FGIs to explore participants’ experiences, perceived benefits, limitations, and suggestions for improvement. Four online FGIs were conducted via a video conferencing platform to allow participants to respond in a comfortable and familiar environment, and were facilitated by one of the research team members (ML), who has completed graduate-level qualitative research coursework and has experience conducting qualitative studies. Interviews were guided by open-ended questions regarding overall expressions, memorable learning experiences, and comparisons with usual learning methods. An interview guide was developed prior to data collection and is provided as supplementary file 4. Four online FGIs were conducted, each lasting approximately 60 min. With participants’ consent, interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, anonymized, and securely managed.

Data analysis

Quantitative data collected from the pre-study demographic survey and the post-usage usability evaluation using MARS were analyzed using descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, with SPSS version 30.0. The internal consistency of the MARS in this sample was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.909).

Qualitative data obtained from the FGIs were analyzed using an inductive content analysis approach following the procedures outlined by Elo and Kyngäs [23]. The transcribed data were read repeatedly to identify meaningful units. Through open coding, categorization, and abstraction, key themes and insights were derived to capture participants’ experiences, perceptions, and suggestions related to the use of VEGA. The analysis was conducted independently by two members of the research team, and any discrepancies in coding were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Quantitative and qualitative results were merged at the interpretation stage and compared to identify convergences and divergences, allowing both data strands to complement each other in explaining the usability and learning experiences of VEGA.

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the researcher’s affiliated institution (IRB approval number: ewha-202503-0024-01). All personal data were anonymized and stored on an encrypted external device, retained for three years after study completion, and then permanently deleted. Only the researchers had access to the data. The phone numbers collected were deleted immediately after incentive delivery. Audio recordings of the interviews were transcribed using participant codes and deleted upon completion. Participants could withdraw at any time, and their data would be permanently erased. A small incentive was provided to all participants. Study participation was entirely voluntary, and students were explicitly informed that their decision to participate or withdraw would have no bearing on their academic standing. All data were collected and managed solely by the research team, and responses were kept anonymous from faculty members involved in recruitment.

Results

Preliminary evaluation

Content validity evaluation

The experts who reviewed the application had an average of approximately 12 years of clinical experience and 10 years of teaching experience. Each item was rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“not at all valid”) to 4 (“highly valid”). The CVI was calculated as the proportion of experts rating each item as either 3 (“Valid”) or 4 (“Highly valid”). Item-level CVIs (I-CVIs) ranged from 0.83 to 1.00, and the scale-level CVI (S-CVI/Ave) was 0.96, both meeting or exceeding the recommended thresholds for a panel of six experts [20], indicating acceptable content validity of the educational content provided by the app.

Although this threshold was met, several expert suggestions were incorporated to enhance the overall quality and consistency of the application. Most notably, experts recommended that diagnostic labels be excluded from clinical scenarios; accordingly, the prompt structure was revised. In addition, the response feedback format was updated to provide clearer indications of correct or incorrect answers in a more standardized manner. Concerns were also raised about awkward translations and inconsistent prompt styles, which were addressed through refinement of the app’s language and format.

Preliminary Survey

A preliminary survey was conducted with ten third- and fourth-year undergraduate nursing students to evaluate the comprehensibility of the app’s cases, items, and feedback. The participants completed at least three item sets before responding to the survey. Each aspect (case, item, and feedback) was rated on a 4-point Likert scale regarding ease of understanding and readability.

The mean age of the 10 participants was 23.0 ± 1.6 years (range 21–26). Among them, 70.0% were female, and 80.0% were in their fourth year of study. The mean scores for the preliminary survey items indicated that the app’s content was generally easy to understand and read. Specifically, the case scenarios were rated as easy to understand (3.7 ± 0.5), the items as understandable (3.5 ± 0.5), and the feedback as clear (3.6 ± 0.5). In terms of readability, the case scenarios (3.8 ± 0.4), items (3.5 ± 0.5), and feedback (3.7 ± 0.5) all received high scores. All items were scored between 3 and 4 on a 4-point Likert scale, indicating that the app’s case content, questions, and explanations were perceived as easy to comprehend and pleasant to read. Open-ended responses generally reflected positive impressions such as “easy to understand,” “helpful for studying,” and “convenient to use.” One frequently mentioned suggestion was that feedback content should be presented in bullet points rather than in long paragraphs. Based on this, the app was updated to deliver feedback in bullet-point format.

Main study results

General characteristics of participants

Twelve students participated in the main study, with ages ranging from 20 to 38 years (23.8 ± 4.8). Most were female (66.7%) and in their fourth year of nursing (66.7%). In terms of perceived academic achievement, 75.0% rated themselves as high, and 25.0% rated themselves as moderate. The majority (75.0%) had prior experience using AI-based learning tools. When asked whether such tools were helpful for learning, 41.7% answered “very helpful,” another 41.7% answered “helpful,” and 16.7% answered “not helpful”; no participant responded that the tools were “not at all helpful.”

User experiences identified through focus group interviews

Theme 1. Enables individualized learning

Participants described VEGA as a learning tool that supported individualized study through easy access, flexible content selection, and immediate feedback, enabling efficient and focused learning.

Subtheme 1. High accessibility and ease of use

VEGA was perceived as easy to use and accessible, reducing the need for additional learning materials and allowing efficient engagement with learning activities.

I found it easier to use than expected. Solving items through VEGA online meant that I did not need any additional learning materials, which made it more convenient.

Subtheme 2. Allows practice of diverse items tailored to learner’s needs

Participants highlighted the ability to select specific content areas and repeatedly practice vignette-based items as supporting targeted learning.

I really liked that I could choose specific body systems, like the gastrointestinal or cardiovascular systems, depending on what I wanted to study.

Subtheme 3. Provides immediate and detailed feedback to enhance learning

Immediate feedback was viewed as enhancing learning efficiency by clarifying key concepts.

With workbooks, the answers are often at the bottom or back, so I have to flip back and forth, which is inconvenient. However, with VEGA, the explanations appeared immediately, so I could see why the answer was correct or incorrect. This makes it much more convenient.

Theme 2. Allows indirect experience of clinical situations and supports engagement with clinical reasoning skills

Participants described VEGA as enabling indirect exposure to clinical situations through structured, vignette-based learning, which supported clinical thinking and decision-making process.

Subtheme 1. Vignette-based and stepwise items enable simulated clinical experiences

Participants perceived that stepwise, vignette-based items created a learning experience that resembled clinical practice by presenting patient situations in a logical and integrated sequence.

It felt like I was actually performing nursing tasks. I appreciated how the items followed a logical flow, which allowed me to solve them in an integrated and organic manner.

Subtheme 2. Supports development of prioritization and clinical thinking skills

Participants reported that engaging with multiple interconnected items within a single vignette helped them think critically about patient care and prioritize nursing actions, bridging theoretical knowledge and practice.

With commercial textbooks, I mostly memorized items like an item bank, but the VEGA pushed me to analyze clinical cases and make nursing decisions myself, making the learning experience more active.

Subtheme 3. Offers unlimited vignettes and vignette-based items via artificial intelligence to supplement limitations of clinical practicums

The ability to repeatedly generate diverse vignette-based items was perceived as compensating for limited opportunities in in-person clinical practice.

With an AI app like this, I can generate new vignettes over and over again, almost like a vending machine. My biggest advantage was the ability to endlessly produce vignette-based items.

Theme 3. Applicability in nursing education and clinical settings

Participants described VEGA as applicable across both educational and clinical contexts, supporting learning continuity from classroom instruction to clinical practice.

Subtheme 1. Helps bridge the gap between theory and practice for nursing students

In educational settings, VEGA was perceived as a useful tool for post-lecture review and preclinical preparation by helping students apply theoretical knowledge to practice-oriented scenarios.

It can also be used as a review tool after completing a body system in a theory class, serving as a bridge between academic knowledge and practical applications.

I believe that VEGA will be very helpful in clinical practice. If further developed, it can be integrated at the class level, where students are asked to solve a set of items related to the assigned ward before entering clinical practice.

Subtheme 2. Supports clinical adaptation for nurses in transition, including new and transferring nurses

Participants noted that VEGA could support nurses in transition, such as new graduates or nurses changing departments, by facilitating exposure to unfamiliar clinical areas and promoting practical readiness.

When working in oncology wards, nurses mainly focus on cancer-related content and may be unfamiliar with other areas. Practicing with VEGA could help them prepare for transitions to different wards or specialties.

I thought it would be particularly useful for new nurses as they needed to develop the ability to apply theoretical knowledge in practice. The VEGA offers a structure that helps them build practical awareness and supports their clinical adaptation.

Theme 4. Needs improvement in technical stability, in-depth explanations, and learning resources

Participants identified areas for improvement related to technical stability, feedback depth, personalization, and additional learning supports.

Subtheme 1. Requires resolution of technical issues

Participants reported technical instability, such as delayed loading or slow transitions between items, which disrupted learning flow.

Aside from the slight delay when transitioning between items, I still felt that technology had advanced significantly.

Subtheme 2. Needs comprehensive explanations including rationale for each option, intent of the item, and concept clarification

Participants requested more comprehensive explanations, including rationales for each option and clarification of item intent and related concepts.

I thought it would be helpful if I could also ask questions about or receive explanations for the other options, not just the incorrect one I selected.

Subtheme 3. Requires enhanced personalization features tailored to learner’s level and interests.

Participants suggested personalization features such as adjustable difficulty levels tailored to learners’ level, interests, or selected topics.

“I thought it would be helpful to have adjustable difficulty levels so that I could solve items based on how challenging I wanted them to be. It would also be beneficial if the difficulty could be set by the body system or the topic area.”

Subtheme 4. Needs additional functions and materials to support learning

Participants recommended adding learning resources and features, such as multimedia-based items, to better reflect clinical contexts.

In clinical practice, we often rely on data like CT images or listen to actual patient sounds like breath sounds. Because the app did not include such multimedia features, I felt that aspect was lacking.

Discussion

Nurses are required to make timely clinical judgments in complex care environments, underscoring the importance of educational strategies that support the development of clinical reasoning and judgment skills. This study developed VEGA, a vignette-based item set generation tool based on the NCJMM, to address this need. The development process followed the ADDIE instructional design model, which ensured a systematic and pedagogically sound approach. VEGA was designed to support nursing students in applying theoretical knowledge to simulated clinical situations. This mixed-methods study examined VEGA’s usability and the learning experiences of students who engaged with the tool, with the aim of exploring its applicability to vignette-based learning in nursing education.

In this pilot study, the overall usability of VEGA was assessed using MARS, with the highest domain scores observed for information, followed by functionality, aesthetics, subjective quality, and engagement. While the overall mean score of 3.62 out of 5 indicates moderate usability, the relatively lower scores in engagement (3.40) and subjective quality (3.44), combined with the large standard deviations, suggest considerable variability in user experience and highlight areas requiring further improvement. These results are consistent with those of a prior study by Kim et al. [24], who developed a mobile app-based self-directed learning program for nursing students and evaluated its usability using the same instrument. In Kim et al.’s [24] study, the domain ratings were similarly high for information, functionality, and aesthetics, with lower scores for engagement and subjective quality. This alignment suggests that educational apps designed for nursing students may tend to perform relatively better in delivering accurate and well-organized content and providing reliable functionality, whereas interactivity and personal appeal may require further enhancement to optimize learners’ engagement. Similarly, in the qualitative findings of this study, participants expressed satisfaction with the high-quality vignettes and items provided, while also suggesting the inclusion of more engaging elements to enhance enjoyment and motivation.

The first qualitative theme highlighted the role of VEGA in supporting individualized learning. The participants appreciated the tool’s accessibility, ease of use, and time efficiency, noting that the ability to select specific content areas according to their learning needs facilitated a more targeted and self-directed study. This aligns with learner-centered educational principles, which emphasize the importance of designing instruction around students’ needs and the relevance of learning content to their personal goals and motivation [25]. Participants in this study suggested that, to support individualized learning and comprehensive feedback further, similar educational tools could include explanations for all answer options and incorporate follow-up questioning capabilities, such as those enabled by LLMs.

The second qualitative theme emphasized the value of VEGA in enabling indirect experiences of clinical vignettes and supporting engagement with clinical reasoning processes. Participants appreciated how the stepwise vignette-based item structure simulated realistic patient care situations and supported integrated decision-making. Recent studies that employed NCJMM-based case studies provide contextual support for this interpretation; for example, Sullivan et al. [26] found that implementing interactive unfolding case-based activities aligned with the NCJMM not only promoted sustained engagement but also enhanced nursing students’ decision-making in classroom settings. Similarly, Robinson et al. [27] reported that the early introduction of NCJMM-aligned case learning improved students’ clinical reasoning abilities as reflected in their written reflections and perceived preparedness for clinical practice. These findings suggest the potential educational value of structured NCJMM-aligned tools such as VEGA, which leverage AI to automatically generate diverse and repeatable clinical vignettes, thereby providing sustained opportunities for engagement with clinical reasoning process.

The third qualitative theme underscored the applicability of VEGA in academic and clinical contexts. Participants noted that VEGA could reinforce post-lecture reviews, support preclinical preparation for nursing students, and assist transitioning nurses, such as new graduates or those changing specialties, in applying theoretical knowledge to clinical settings. These findings are consistent with the recent literature highlighting the effectiveness of case-based learning in nursing education. For example, a recent meta-analysis by Xiang et al. [28] found that case-based learning significantly improved nursing students’ critical thinking dispositions, theoretical knowledge, and practical skills, which may contribute to narrowing the theory-practice gap in nursing education. This suggests that structured vignette-based learning tools may have the potential to support the application of theoretical knowledge in clinical practice among nursing students and transitioning nurses, potentially contributing to narrowing the gap between academic preparation and professional practice.

The fourth qualitative theme highlighted areas for improvement in VEGA, particularly technical performance, content depth, and user engagement features. While some minor technical issues were noted, the participants primarily emphasized the need for more in-depth explanations and personalized features, such as adjustable item difficulty and multimedia item integration. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies. Butler et al. [29] demonstrated that elaborative feedback, including explanations of incorrect and alternative choices, promotes a better transfer of learning than simply providing correct answers. A recent narrative review [30] highlighted that adaptive testing, in which item difficulty is adjusted based on learner responses, can enhance both test reliability and learner engagement by tailoring challenge levels to individual abilities. Furthermore, a recent study on multimedia-assisted item development [31] found that integrating videos, images, and audio into assessment items enhanced clinical authenticity and strengthened reasoning skills. These insights may inform future enhancements of AI-driven learning tools to support personalized, clinically relevant nursing education.

Despite these promising findings, the use of generative AI in educational settings carries inherent risks that must be acknowledged. AI-generated content may be clinically inaccurate or inconsistent due to prompt instability or model variability, and learners who uncritically accept incorrect feedback may develop misconceptions with potential patient safety implications [32, 33]. These risks underscore the importance of ongoing expert review of AI-generated content and the need for learners to develop critical appraisal skills when engaging with AI-based educational tools.

This study had several limitations. First, the findings were based on a small and relatively homogeneous sample, which limits the generalizability of the results. Second, this study focused on learners’ perceptions and did not directly assess the educational effectiveness of VEGA through objective learning outcomes. Third, the short-term nature of the evaluation limits insight into the long-term learning effects. Fourth, because the same participants completed both the quantitative usability survey and the qualitative FGI, prior exposure to the survey items may have influenced their interview responses, potentially introducing bias into the qualitative data. Fifth, the quality of AI-generated outputs, including clinical accuracy and psychometric properties such as item difficulty and discrimination, could not be systematically verified, as VEGA continuously generates new items rather than administering a fixed item set. Sixth, as participation was voluntary, self-selection bias may have introduced a more favorable attitude toward AI-based learning tools among the sample. Additionally, the current version of VEGA requires users to provide their own GPT API key, which may pose a practical barrier to accessibility for some users. Despite these limitations, this study made meaningful contributions by introducing VEGA, an AI-powered vignette-based item set generation tool grounded in the NCJMM, and exploring its potential through both quantitative and qualitative methods. VEGA may serve as a scalable and pedagogically grounded educational tool that supports learners’ engagement with clinical judgment-related learning through individualized learning experiences and repeated opportunities for clinical reasoning practice.

Future work

This study developed VEGA, a vignette-based evaluation tool using generative AI, and explored its potential for educational applications. Based on the findings of this study, future research is proposed to validate the educational effectiveness of VEGA and enhance its comprehensiveness. First, future iterations of VEGA could embed an AI-based automated item review mechanism to assess and improve item quality prior to delivery to learners. Second, longitudinal experimental studies are required to verify educational effectiveness through outcomes such as clinical judgment, academic achievement, and licensure exam performance. Third, system refinement should reflect user feedback by enhancing technical stability, providing more detailed feedback, introducing adaptive features, and integrating multimedia resources. Fourth, future research will explore the evolution of VEGA into a Multi-Agent System (MAS) architecture. This will involve the implementation of the MACRO (Multi-Agent Context Routing and Orchestration) framework [34] to transition VEGA from a single-agent process to a distributed network of specialized agents. This architecture would assign distinct roles—such as a Narrative Architect for vignette generation, a Pedagogical Proctor for real-time learner interaction, and an Evaluator Agent utilizing the AIR (AI as Reviewer) framework [34] for high-fidelity assessment. By modularizing these functions, the system can reduce “LLM fatigue” and hallucination, ensuring that the evaluation of clinical judgment remains grounded in specific evidence while allowing for complex, multi-turn reflective feedback loops that were previously computationally prohibitive in a single-agent model.

Conclusion

This study developed VEGA, an AI-powered vignette-based item-set generation tool grounded in the NCJMM, and explored its usability and learning experiences among nursing students. The findings suggest VEGA’s potential to support engagement with clinical reasoning through flexible, learner-centered features and realistic vignette-based practices. While these insights are drawn from participants’ perceptions and experiences rather than objective learning outcomes, they provide a meaningful foundation for future investigation. Importantly, the participants’ feedback also highlighted the need for future refinement, particularly in technical reliability, feedback depth, and multimedia integration, to further enhance its educational value. Future research should evaluate the impact of these AI-driven learning tools on measurable learning gains across broader and more diverse learner populations to further validate their utility in nursing education.
