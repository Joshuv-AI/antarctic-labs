Deciphering the genetic background of an industrial 2-ketogluconic acid-producing strain Pseudomonas plecoglossicida JUIM01 using whole-genome sequencing
Authors: Lulu Li, Lei Sun, Xinyi Zan, Fengjie Cui, Mei Zhao, Wenjing Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract2-Ketogluconic acid (2KGA) is an important precursor for the food antioxidant erythorbic acid, currently produced via microbial fermentation using Pseudomonas species. To facilitate the genetic improvement of production strains, the complete genome of an industrial 2KGA producer P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 was sequenced and analyzed. The genome consists of a 5.13-Mb circular chromosome with a GC content of 63.58%, encoding 4,517 predicted proteins. Comprehensive functional annotation identified a putative global regulatory network comprising 75 core regulators, which were classified into six functionally cooperative modules, potentially governing the strain’s metabolism and environmental adaptability. We further delineated the genetic determinants hypothetically linked to efficient 2KGA synthesis, including glucose metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, and the oxidative phosphorylation system. These outputs could provide the genomic resource for elucidating high productivity and robustness, and rationally engineering the high-performance chassis cells toward robust 2KGA production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13068-026-02784-0.Keywords: 2-Ketogluconic acid (2KGA), P. plecoglossicida, Genetic background, Whole-genome sequencing, Key pathway

Introduction

2-Ketogluconic acid (2KGA) is an organic acid produced by the biological or chemical oxidation of glucose. It serves as a valuable building block for synthesizing chiral and heterocyclic compounds and finds diverse applications as a plasticizer and set retarder in cement and concrete, a builder in detergents, an additive in calcium-enriched animal feed, and a developing agent in photography [1, 2]. Currently, the most significant industrial application of 2KGA is as a precursor for the synthesis of d-isoascorbic acid (erythorbic acid) and its sodium salt, which are generally recognized as safe (GRAS) by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and widely used as food preservatives, antioxidants, and color stabilizers in the food industry [3–5]. The predominant industrial 2KGA production is fermented by the oxidative bacteria such as Pseudomonas, Gluconobacter, Klebsiella, Acetobacter, and Serratia [6–10] since microbial conversion of glucose into 2KGA is cost-effective, highly efficient with low byproduct formation, and environmental friendliness [3, 6]. Among these bacteria, Pseudomonas species are particularly prevalent in industrial fermentation due to their high product titer, yield (based on sugar conversion), volumetric productivity, and safety profile [9, 11, 12]. However, their tolerance and their production efficiency under the stresses of high temperature, acidity and high glucose concentrations still require improvement [13]. On this basis, improving the efficiency and robustness, and developing the genetic tractable chassis strains are becoming a key research focus for enhancing the competitiveness of 2KGA production.

Generally, the well-defined genomic backgrounds, high genetic stability, enhanced biosafety and ease of genetic manipulation of the chassis cells benefit the predictable improvement of inherent traits or the acquisition of entirely new functions through systematic genome engineering [14]. Compared to traditional chassis such as Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas species show some potential advantages including higher tolerance to environmental stresses (e.g., organic solvents, oxidative stress, and heavy metals), broader substrates (aromatic compounds, organic acids, and complex industrial byproducts), and highly robust metabolic networks [15]. Among Pseudomonas species, P. putida KT2440 is one of the most extensively studied and genetically well-characterized biosafe strains with a clear genetic background, a mature gene-editing toolkit and safe [16–20]. P. putida KT2440 presents remarkable metabolic plasticity for efficient synthesis of high-value compounds such as muconic acid, polyhydroxyalkanoates, rhamnolipids, terpenoids, and catechol. Additionally, it could utilize lignin derivatives, aromatic pollutants, and plastic monomers as the substrates, which has contributed it to be a versatile chassis [19, 21–23].

Pseudomonas plecoglossicida JUIM01 is an industrial 2KGA producer with over 90% of the theoretical yield and shares a high genetic similarity to P. putida KT2440 [24]. Our group previously have well investigated the growth and fermentation profiles of P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 and its response to high glucose concentration and temperature stress [25, 26]. However, lack of a complete genome of P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 hampered finding and characterizing the interesting genes and/or metabolic transcriptional regulators for improving 2KGA production. Therefore, this study aims to provide the genetic background including the key metabolic and environmental adaptability regulatory network of P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 via whole-genome sequencing and in-depth bioinformatic analysis. The outputs hopefully provide the genomic resource for constructing a chassis to improve 2KGA synthesis and a robust genomic basis to develop cell factories to produce the specific high-value chemicals.

Materials and methods

Strain cultivation and genomic DNA extraction

P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 is an industrial strain with high 2-ketogluconic acid (2KGA) production efficiency. It was previously isolated by our laboratory from soil samples collected from an industrial fermentation workshop in Shangrao, Jiangxi Province, China. The strain has been deposited at the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC) with accession number CGMCC No. 7150. For whole-genome sequencing, the strain P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 was retrieved from storage at − 80 °C and activated onto an agar slant medium containing: peptone 10 g/L, beef extract 5 g/L, 5 g/L NaCl, and agar 20 g/L (pH 7.0). After incubation at 30 °C for 24 h, cells were harvested from the slant by rinsing with sterile water. The cell suspension was collected and inoculated to 50 mL of seed medium containing glucose 20 g, corn steep powder 5 g/L, urea 2 g/L, KH₂PO₄ 2 g/L, MgSO₄·7H₂O 0.5 g/L, and CaCO₃ 5 g/L (pH 7.0), in a 500-mL flask at an inoculum size of 2% (v/v). The seed cultivation was conducted at 30 °C with a shaking speed of 265 rpm for 12 h for obtaining sufficient cell pellets for DNA extraction. The cells were harvested by centrifuging at 8,000×g for 2 min at 4 °C, washed twice with sterile water, flash-frozen, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent DNA extraction.

Genomic DNA of P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 was extracted from the frozen cells using the TIANamp Bacteria DNA Kit (TIANGEN Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Plasmid DNA was separately isolated using the SanPrep Column Plasmid Mini-Preps Kit (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). DNA purity was assessed by measuring the A₂₆₀/A₂₈₀ and A₂₆₀/A₂₃₀ ratios using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), with acceptable ratios of 1.8–2.0 and 2.0–2.2, respectively. DNA concentration was precisely quantified using a Quantus Fluorometer (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA). DNA integrity was verified by electrophoresis on a 0.6% agarose gel, requiring no degradation and no RNA contamination. A total of at least 10 μg of genomic DNA was extracted and used for subsequent sequencing.

Library construction and sequencing

Paired-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 3000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). One microgram of high-quality genomic DNA was fragmented to approximately 400 bp using a Covaris ultrasonicator (Covaris, Woburn, MA, USA), and the fragment size distribution was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. Libraries were prepared using the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit (Bioo Scientific, Austin, TX, USA) by ligating adapters A and B, removing adapter–adapter concatemers, and enriching the libraries. Enrichment was carried out with 8–12 cycles of PCR, and intermediate products were purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). Subsequently, single‑stranded DNA fragments were generated by sodium hydroxide denaturation. During bridge PCR amplification, one end of the single‑stranded DNA fragment hybridized to a complementary primer immobilized on the flow cell, while the other end randomly hybridized to a nearby second primer, forming a “bridge” structure. DNA clusters were generated through PCR amplification and then linearized to single strands. For sequencing, a modified DNA polymerase and four fluorescently labeled dNTPs were added, and one base was extended per cycle. The flow cell surface was scanned with a laser to read the first incorporated nucleotide for each template strand. The fluorophore and terminator groups were then chemically cleaved to restore the 3'‑hydroxyl group, allowing extension of the next base. By recording the fluorescence signals collected in each cycle, the sequence of each template DNA fragment was obtained. The sequencing read length was 150 bp paired‑end (2 × 150 bp), and the target sequencing coverage was ≥ 100×.

Sequencing was performed on a PacBio Sequel IIe platform (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA, USA). At least 10 μg of high‑quality genomic DNA was sheared into 8–10 kb fragments using a Covaris g‑TUBE (Covaris, Woburn, MA, USA) and purified with AMPure PB beads (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA, USA). End repair was carried out according to the PacBio standard protocol, followed by ligation of single‑stranded circular adapters to both ends of the fragments using the SMRTbell Prep Kit 3.0 (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA, USA). This ligation created a dumbbell‑shaped (“lasso”) structure, termed the SMRT Bell. Fragments longer than 10 kb were selected using the BluePippin system (Sage Science, Beverly, MA, USA) to remove short fragments and adapter dimers. For single‑molecule sequencing, the single‑stranded circular library was annealed (25 °C, 30 min) and bound to polymerases immobilized at the bottom of zero‑mode waveguides (ZMWs). When the laser was directed from the bottom, the energy was confined to an extremely small detection volume, so only fluorescence signals from the ZMW bottom were collected, while free nucleotides outside the well generated no background. Each ZMW contained a single polymerase bound to a template DNA molecule; upon addition of sequencing reagents, each base incorporation event produced a corresponding fluorescence signal that was detected in real time. A single SMRT Cell contains approximately 150,000 ZMWs, each containing a single DNA molecule being synthesized at a rate exceeding 100 bases per minute, and each incorporated base produced a pulse peak. With a high‑resolution optical detection system, real‑time monitoring was achieved. Sequencing reagents were the Sequel Sequencing Kit 2.0 (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA, USA), and each SMRT Cell was run for 15 h. The target coverage was ≥ 50×.

Sequencing data quality control and genome assembly

The raw Illumina sequencing data were stored in FASTQ format. To obtain high‑quality valid data, quality trimming was performed using fastp v0.20.0 with the following filtering criteria: a sliding window of 4 bp was used to trim bases from both ends, and when the average Phred quality score within the window fell below Q20, all bases after that window were removed; reads containing more than 10% ambiguous bases (N) were discarded; and reads shorter than 25 bp after trimming were excluded. The resulting clean reads were used for subsequent assembly. After quality control, the clean data exhibited Q20 > 98% and Q30 > 95%. The PacBio Sequel IIe platform generated HiFi reads as the primary output. HiFi reads were produced using the ccs module of the SMRT Link v8.0 software package with the following parameters: minimum number of passes ≥ 3, predicted accuracy ≥ 0.99 (i.e., Q20). Reads shorter than 500 bp were filtered out. The total number of reads, total bases, mean read length, and read N50 were also recorded.

Hybrid assembly was performed using Unicycler v0.4.8 [27] on the quality‑controlled Illumina clean data and PacBio HiFi reads. Unicycler first constructed an initial contig graph using the PacBio long reads, then refined, corrected, and closed the graph using the Illumina short reads, ultimately yielding a complete circular genome sequence (finished genome). Default assembly parameters were used. The initial assembly was polished with Pilon v1.24 [28] by mapping the Illumina short reads back to the assembled genome to correct potential insertions/deletions (indels) and single‑base errors. The polished genome sequence was taken as the final finished genome. The completeness and contamination of the assembled genome were assessed using CheckM v1.2.4, and the assembly was required to meet the criteria of completeness > 90% and contamination < 5%.

Genome prediction and annotation

Based on the polished genome assembly, a custom in-house bioinformatics pipeline was implemented to predict and annotate gene structures and functions. Since the final assembly was a bacterial finished genome (a single circular chromosome with no plasmid detected), coding sequences (CDSs) were predicted using Prodigal v2.6.3 [31] with the single-mode parameter (-p single). To ensure prediction accuracy, we also performed cross-validation using Glimmer3 [29] and GeneMarkS v4.3 [30]; no substantial discrepancies were observed. All predicted CDSs were uniformly filtered to retain only those longer than 100 bp and possessing start codons (ATG, GTG, TTG) and stop codons (TAA, TAG, TGA). Transfer RNAs (tRNAs) and ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) were identified using tRNAscan‑SE v2.0.12 [32] (bacterial mode with the -B parameter) and Barrnap v0.9 (–kingdom bac, E-value ≤ 1e‑6), respectively. Potential small RNAs (sRNAs) were predicted by searching the Rfam database (version 33.1) with Infernal v1.1.4 [33] using the cmscan program and the built-in gathering thresholds (–cut_ga), followed by filtering with an E-value cutoff of 1e‑5 and a sequence coverage ≥ 80%. Genomic islands and CRISPR-Cas systems were predicted using IslandViewer v1.2 [34] and Minced v0.2.0, respectively. IslandViewer v1.2 integrates three complementary algorithms (IslandPath-DIMOB, SIGI-HMM, and IslandPick), and the final predictions were taken as the intersection of the three methods to reduce false positives. CRISPR–Cas systems were identified using Minced v0.2.0 (based on the CRT algorithm) with default parameters (minimum number of repeats ≥ 2, repeat length 23–47 bp, spacer length 26–72 bp), and all predictions were manually verified. For functional annotation, the predicted protein sequences were used as queries against the NCBI Non‑Redundant Protein Database (NR), Swiss‑Prot, the Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) database, and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [35] using Diamond v0.8.35 (E-value ≤ 1e⁻5). Protein domain annotation was performed by searching the Pfam database (version 33.1) with HMMER3 (E-value ≤ 1e⁻5) [36]. Based on the NR BLAST result, Gene Ontology (GO) annotation was carried out using Blast2GO [37]. In addition, genes encoding carbohydrate-active enzymes and antibiotic resistance genes were specifically annotated using the Carbohydrate-Active enZymes Database (CAZy) [38] (with HMMER3, E-value ≤ 1e⁻5) and the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) [39] (with Diamond v0.8.35, E-value ≤ 1e⁻5), respectively. Finally, a circular whole-genome map of P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 was generated using CGView v2 [40] to visually summarize its genomic features and annotation results.

Integrative analysis of the core regulatory network and key metabolic pathways

An integrated analytical strategy was adopted to reconstruct the core regulatory network and key metabolic pathways of P. plecoglossicida JUIM01. First, based on the whole-genome functional annotation obtained, genes encoding known regulatory factors (e.g., two-component systems, transcription factors) and genes involved in central carbon metabolism (with emphasis on glucose and 2KGA metabolism), fatty acid metabolism, and electron transport chain complexes were identified and categorized. This initial identification was supplemented by a comprehensive literature review on the Pseudomonas genus, particularly focusing on the model strains P. putida KT2440 and P. aeruginosa PAO1.

Subsequently, a comparative genomics approach was employed. The protein sequences of the identified genes from strain JUIM01 were used as queries in BLASTP searches against the annotated proteomes of P. putida KT2440 and P. aeruginosa PAO1. High-confidence orthologs were then mapped to the well-characterized regulatory networks, metabolic pathways, and protein functions of these reference strains. This mapping provided a predicted metabolic regulatory framework, the core carbon metabolism pathways (including glucose and 2KGA metabolism), the fatty acid metabolism framework, and the genetic basis of the respiratory electron transport system in strain JUIM01. Results and discussion

General genomic features of Pseudomonas plecoglossicida JUIM01

Through hybrid sequencing and assembly based on PacBio and Illumina platforms, a high-quality, complete whole-genome sequence of P. plecoglossicida strain JUIM01 was obtained for the first time. After quality control, the Illumina paired-end sequencing generated 8,292,964 reads, with a total of 1,243,944,600 bp. The percentage of bases with a quality score Q20 (error rate < 0.01) was 98.34%, and that with Q30 (error rate < 0.001) was 95.05%, resulting in a sequencing coverage of 242×. For PacBio sequencing, 34,052 reads were obtained after quality control, with a total of 311,784,579 bp, an average read length of 9,156 bp, an N50 read length of 9,603 bp, and a coverage of 60×. The final assembly yielded a single circular chromosome of 5,126,406 bp, with a contig N50 of 5,126,406 bp and a contig L50 of 1 (Table 1). The genome completeness was estimated at 99.31%, with a contamination level of 0.75%. No plasmids were detected, indicating that all genetic information resides on a single chromosome (Table 2). The overall GC content is 63.58%, consistent with the high GC content characteristic commonly observed in the genus Pseudomonas [41, 42]. Using an integrated multi-software gene prediction pipeline, a total of 4,517 protein‑coding genes were identified, whose coding sequences collectively cover 88.69% of the genome, reflecting a dense gene arrangement. In addition, systematic annotation revealed 72 tRNA genes, 19 rRNA genes (comprising 7 5S, 6 16S, and 6 23S rRNA genes), and 91 sRNA genes, suggesting a relatively complex transcriptional regulatory potential. Bioinformatics analysis further identified 11 genomic islands and three CRISPR–Cas systems. The genomic islands may be associated with environmental adaptability or metabolic plasticity, while the CRISPR–Cas systems provide a genetic basis for immune defense.

Functional annotation of the genome

Overview of genomic functional annotation for P. plecoglossicida JUIM01

The functional annotation of the P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 genome is summarized in Table 2. When aligned against the NR, Swiss-Prot, Pfam, COG, GO, and KEGG databases, 4,503, 3,514, 3,993, 3,824, 1,870 and 2,752 predicted protein-coding genes were annotated, respectively. Among them, 104 genes encoding carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZy) and 388 genes associated with antibiotic resistance (via CARD) were identified, which might be hypothetically linked to its carbon source utilization and environmental adaptability.

COG annotation

COG database was used to functionally categorize the gene set of P. plecoglossicida JUIM01. A total of 3,824 genes were annotated accounting for 84.66% of the predicted total genes (4,517), and classified into 24 COG functional categories (Fig. 2). About 865 genes are regarded to be related to carbon metabolism, indicating that P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 has a high genetic potency to utilize various carbon sources. Among them, 457 genes belonging to COG category E, 238 genes belonging to COG category I and 192 genes belonging to COG category G are putatively involved in amino acid transport and metabolism, lipid transport and metabolism, and carbohydrate transport and metabolism, respectively. Notably, the number of genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism was significantly lower than that for amino acid metabolism (only about 42% of the latter), which suggests that P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 shares the similar preference to use amino acids, organic acids, and aromatic compounds as the carbon sources to that of Pseudomonas [43]. Additionally, 798 genes (17.67% of the predicted total) are potentially associated with the regulation of cellular processes including transcription (e.g., various transcription factors, 375 genes), signal transduction (e.g., two-component systems, 313 genes), and post-translational modification/chaperones (177 genes). These regulatory genes suggest that P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 possesses a complex and precise regulatory network to efficiently coordinate the metabolic pathways and rapid response to environmental changes. Meanwhile, 114 genes (2.52% of the predicted total) were identified to be potentially involved in defense mechanisms (category V).

GO annotation

The homology-based alignment gave the gene-ontology annotation of a total of 1,870 genes (41.4% of all predicted genes), which could be categorized to biological process, cellular component, and molecular function. Among them, the molecular function category occupies the largest number of genes (1,498 genes, corresponding to 33.2% of all genes), followed by biological process (1,056 genes, 23.4%) and cellular component (947 genes, 21.0%). Figure 3 shows the 10 specific GO terms with the highest number of genes in each category. Among the biological process categories, the GO terms with the highest number of genes are primarily associated with the fundamental processes related to genetic information transfer and protein metabolism, including “regulation of DNA-templated transcription” (GO:0006355, 61 genes), “translation” (GO:0006412, 59 genes), and “proteolysis” (GO:0006508, 50 genes). In the cellular component category, annotated genes are primarily localized to membrane systems, including “integral component of membrane” (GO:0016021, 464 genes), “plasma membrane” (GO:0005886, 222 genes), and “cytoplasm” (GO:0005737, 215 genes). In molecular function, binding activities were most prominent, including “ATP binding” (GO:0005524, 175 genes), “DNA binding” (GO:0003677, 165 genes), and “metal ion binding” (GO:0046872, 144 genes). Notably, within the biological process category, we identified a large number of genes associated with carbon metabolism, involving a total of 100 specific GO terms. These terms cover core pathways of central carbon metabolic pathways such as glycolysis (GO:0006096), gluconeogenesis (GO:0006094), the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (GO:0006099), the pentose phosphate pathway (GO:0006098), and the glyoxylate cycle (GO:0006097), as well as fatty acid biosynthesis (GO:0006633) and beta-oxidation (GO:0006635), as well as the synthesis and catabolism of various amino acids. These results are consistent with the aforementioned COG functional classification analysis, which also provides complementary evidence that the genome of P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 harbors a complex and complete genetic repertoire for carbon metabolism networks.

KEGG annotation

Using the KEGG database, a total of 2,752 genes (accounting for 60.93% of the total genes) in the genome of P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 were annotated and classified into six major functional categories (Fig. 4). Among all these categories, the “Metabolism” category contained the largest number of genes (952 genes, representing 34.59% of the annotated genes), followed by “Environmental Information Processing” (414 genes, 15.04%). Within the metabolism category, the most abundant subcategories were amino acid metabolism and carbohydrate metabolism, with 289 and 251 genes, respectively, together constituting the core of the strain’s metabolic network. Additionally, 74 genes related to lipid metabolism were annotated, covering key pathways such as fatty acid biosynthesis (ko00061; 21 genes) and degradation (ko00071; 25 genes).

Further analysis of carbohydrate metabolic pathways revealed that the genome of P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 encodes the complete set of core pathways for central carbon metabolic pathways, including glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (ko00010; 38 genes), the TCA cycle (ko00020; 30 genes), and the pentose phosphate pathway (ko00030; 33 genes). Additionally, genes involved in the degradation of various specific sugars were annotated, including starch and sucrose metabolism (ko00500, 15 genes), fructose and mannose metabolism (ko00051, 14 genes), and galactose metabolism (ko00052, 6 genes). This genetic repertoire provides the potential for the strain to utilize a diverse range of carbohydrates as carbon sources. Within the environmental information processing category, the two-component system (184 genes) in the signal transduction module accounted for nearly half (44.4%) of the genes in this category. This suggests that P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 may possess a vast environmental sensing and response regulatory network, laying a genetic foundation for its adaptation to complex and variable external environments.

CAZyme annotations

By aligning the genome of P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 with the CAZy database, a total of 104 genes encoding Carbohydrate-Active enZymes (CAZymes) were identified, accounting for approximately 2.3% of the total predicted genes (Fig. 5). These genes encompass all categories in the CAZy database, with the following quantitative distributions of Glycosyl Transferases (GTs, 32), Carbohydrate Esterases (CEs, 29), Auxiliary Activities (AAs, 20), Glycoside Hydrolases (GHs, 20), Polysaccharide Lyases (PLs, 2), and Carbohydrate-Binding Modules (CBMs, 1). The CAZyme profile reveals a significant enrichment of Glycosyl Transferases (GTs) and Carbohydrate Esterases (CEs). The abundance of GTs (accounting for 30.8% of CAZymes genes) indicates a strong capacity for the biosynthesis of endogenous polysaccharides (such as lipopolysaccharides) and glycoconjugates, which could provide a genetic basis for cell wall construction, environmental adaptation, and energy storage. Particularly notable is the high enrichment of CEs (29 in total), with the CE1 (acetylxylanase) and CE10 (aryloxidase) families (10 genes each) being the most abundant. As key enzymes involved in "deacetylation" and "aromatic ester cleavage" during the deconstruction of complex plant cell walls (particularly hemicellulose), their co-occurrence indicates that P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 possesses the genetic potential to efficiently pretreat lignocellulosic biomass, removing crucial chemical modification barriers for subsequent glycosidic bond hydrolysis. Regarding degradative enzyme systems, the strain exhibits diverse oxidative and hydrolytic capabilities. Auxiliary Activities (AAs) are dominated by AA3 family oxidoreductases (11 genes), consistent with an active aerobic metabolism that likely supports efficient carbon source conversion. Additionally, the presence of AA1 family laccases (2 genes) implies potential oxidative pathways for degrading lignin-derived aromatic compounds. Glycoside Hydrolases (GHs) demonstrate broad substrate specificity, targeting starch (multiple GH13 subtypes, GH15), cellulose/hemicellulose (GH3, GH5), and nitrogen-containing polysaccharides such as chitin/peptidoglycan (GH19, GH23), establishing a foundation for utilizing various environmental polysaccharides.

Furthermore, genes encoding β-agarase (GH50) and alginate lyase (PL7) have been annotated in this genome. These two enzymes specifically degrade agar from red algae and alginate from brown algae, respectively. This genetic repertoire expands the potential carbon source utilization range of P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 from terrestrial plant polysaccharides to marine macroalgal polysaccharides, which could support its ability to adapt to the complex and diverse ecological environments.

Antibiotic resistance gene annotation

Based on the CARD database, a systematic annotation of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in the genome of this strain was conducted, identifying a total of 388 ARGs involving 39 different classes of antibiotics. Analysis of the resistance gene profile (Fig. 6A) revealed that P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 possesses a broad potential for resistance to multiple important classes of antibiotics. Among these, the highest numbers of resistance genes were found for tetracyclines (109 ARGs), penicillins (89 ARGs), fluoroquinolones (87 ARGs), and macrolides (86 ARGs). In terms of resistance mechanisms (Fig. 6B), antibiotic efflux (204 ARGs) is the predominant resistance strategy, followed by antibiotic target modification (102 ARGs) and antibiotic inactivation (20 ARGs). Notably, some genes are associated with multiple mechanisms simultaneously (e.g., synergistic interaction between efflux and target modification), indicating the complexity of resistance functions. The large and diverse encoded repertoire of resistance genes and active efflux as the core mechanism might likely provide P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 a critical survival advantage in dynamic environments.

Predicted metabolic and environmental adaptation regulatory networks in P. plecoglossicida JUIM01

The genus Pseudomonas exhibits exceptional metabolic diversity and environmental adaptability, which are closely attributable to the sophisticated genetic regulatory networks shaped through evolution [15]. However, the architecture and components of regulatory networks in P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 remain poorly understood. Based on whole-genome sequencing data from P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 and integrated systematic bioinformatics annotation with literature mining, 75 core regulatory factors (encoded by 151 genes) were identified and a conceptual hierarchical regulatory network was constructed. The regulatory network primarily comprises six functional modules including regulation of elemental homeostasis, regulation of structural components, sensing of environmental stress and regulation of adaptive survival, regulation of global carbon metabolism, regulation of carbon catabolite repression, and a functionally uncharacterized regulatory module (Supplemental Table 1; Fig. 7A).

The putative regulatory factors (A) and the potential mechanism of carbon catabolite repression (B) in P. plecoglossicida JUIM01

The regulation of elemental homeostasis is a core physiological process by which microorganisms maintain optimal intracellular concentrations of essential and toxic elements through genetic and biochemical networks to support normal growth and metabolism. The present study found that P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 possesses a highly differentiated elemental homeostasis system, which can be specifically divided into three functional sub-modules: (1) essential transition metal balance regulation submodule including the iron regulator Fur [44], copper-responsive systems CueR/CusSR [45, 46], and the zinc regulator Zur [47, 48]. These systems maintain physiological metal ion concentrations to support electron transfer and enzyme function, while preventing oxidative toxicity or erroneous metal substitution in metalloenzymes due to metal overload. (2) Specialized detoxification regulation submodule such as ArsR for arsenic [49] and CadR for cadmium [50, 51], which respond to exogenous heavy metal stress by activating specific efflux or transformation mechanisms. (3) Osmotic pressure and signaling ion regulation submodule including the potassium regulatory system KdpD/KdpE [52] and the calcium regulatory system CarR/CarS [53], whose core functions most likely lie in maintaining cell membrane potential, osmotic balance, and precise calcium signal transduction.

The structural components regulation module governs the synthesis and assembly of physical cellular structures including cell envelope, division apparatus, and motility organelles, which determine cell morphology, integrity, division, and motility. The module in P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 operates via two principal regulatory mechanisms. First, the global transcriptional repressor MraZ coordinates peptidoglycan synthesis with the cell division cycle by negatively regulating the division and cell wall (dcw) gene cluster in a feedback loop [54, 55]. Second, a multi-level transcriptional cascade centered on FleQ involving the FleS/FleR two-component system, the sigma factor FliA, anti-sigma factor FlgM, and the regulatory protein FleN temporally controls flagellar gene expression and assembly. This network underlies bacterial motility, chemotaxis, and environmental colonization [56–59].

The environmental stress sensing and survival adaptation regulation module serves as the core mechanism for Pseudomonas bacteria to cope with complex stresses and achieve ecological competitiveness. It consists of six synergistic sub-modules, forming a complete adaptation regulatory process from sensing to behavioral output: (1) osmotic and membrane stress responses: rapidly senses acute osmotic changes via the EnvZ/OmpR system [60], and regulates the accumulation of compatible solutes (e.g., glycine betaine) through the BetI/CdhR/GbdR network [61–64] for long-term protection. Simultaneously, the PhoQ/PhoP [65, 66] and ColS/ColR [67, 68] systems detect membrane damage signals, triggering membrane lipid modification and outer membrane remodeling to maintain membrane integrity. (2) Oxidative stress response, DNA repair, and competitive behavior activation: OxyR [69, 70] and OhrR [71, 72] scavenge reactive oxygen species, while Anr [73] coordinates hypoxic adaptation. The SOS repair network mediated by LexA [74, 75], in coordination with RecX [75, 76] and NrdR [77, 78], repairs DNA damage and can convert damage signals into competitive behaviors such as bacteriocin synthesis via PrtR induction [79, 80]. (3) Nutrient limitation sensing and metabolic adaptation: the PhoR/PhoB/PhoU system [81–83] senses phosphorus limitation, while the NtrB/NtrC system [84, 85] and the sensor GlnK [86, 87] detect nitrogen limitation. These systems globally reprogram metabolism and transport systems to optimize resource allocation. (4) Central integration and advanced adaptive regulation: global regulators such as Lrp [88], CsrA [89, 90], Fis [91, 92], and QseB/QseC [93] constitute a high-level command center, integrating multidimensional environmental and metabolic signals to coordinate core physiological processes, including carbon metabolism, motility, biofilm formation, stress response, and antibiotic resistance. (5) Biofilm formation and collective behavior regulation: the GacS/GacA system [94, 95] acts as a global decision-maker, synchronously driving biofilm formation and secondary metabolism through the Gac/Rsm cascade. Its downstream KinB/AlgB system [96, 97] serves as a specific execution unit, precisely regulating matrix synthesis (e.g., alginate) and controlling the transition between planktonic and sessile lifestyles. (6) Xenobiotic recognition and efflux-detoxification: A multi-level efflux pump regulatory network is constructed, ranging from specific induction [98–100] (e.g., TtgR, NfxB, MexT) to global coordination [101, 102] (e.g., RstB/RstA, EvgS/EvgA). This network is a key genetic basis for mediating multidrug resistance.

The global carbon metabolism regulation module hierarchically governs carbon source recognition, metabolic flux distribution, and synthetic conversion. It includes five functional sub-modules: (1) carbon source recognition, transport, and primary response: the GntR/PtxS/GtrS-GltR/HexR network [103–105] and FruR [106, 107] regulate glucose and fructose metabolism, respectively; the DctB/DctD [108] and TctE/TctD [109] systems govern organic acid transport; and the CreC/CreB [110] system responds to specific metabolic states. (2) Regulation of central carbon metabolic flux distribution: PdhR [111] and PycR [112] regulate the flux of pyruvate into the TCA cycle and anaplerotic reactions, respectively, serving as core nodes for carbon flux partitioning. (3) Degradation and assimilation of aromatic compounds: This process exhibits clear cascade regulation. BenR [113, 114] initiates benzoate degradation, while PcaR [115, 116] dominates the subsequent steps, enabling complete integration of aromatic ring carbon skeletons into central metabolism. (4) Metabolic integration of amino acids as dual carbon–nitrogen sources: Catabolism of various amino acids is regulated by specific regulators, including AauS/AauR [117], PutA [118, 119], OruR [120], BkdR [121, 122] HutC [123, 124], ArgR [125], TyrR [126, 127], GcvA/GcvR [128, 129], and DecR [130, 131]. Additionally, RutR coordinates carbon and nitrogen resource redistribution at the intersection of arginine and pyrimidine synthesis [132, 133]. (5) Utilization of secondary carbon sources and support for biosynthesis: FabR [134, 135] and GlpR [136, 137] regulate fatty acid and glycerol metabolism, while AcoR [138] controls the reuse of the reserve carbon source acetoin. Notably, BirA regulates biotin synthesis, thereby directly modulating the activity of key carboxylases and forming a biochemical bridge linking catabolism with the synthesis of substances such as lipids [139, 140].

The carbon catabolite repression regulatory module is a sophisticated control unit operating at the post-transcriptional level. Its core function is to dynamically prioritize metabolic pathways based on carbon source quality, ensuring maximum energy acquisition efficiency in mixed carbon environments. In this study, we annotated conserved key components of carbon catabolite repression in the Pseudomonas genus, including the RNA chaperone Hfq, the repressor protein Crc, the two-component system CbrA/CbrB, and the sRNA CrcZ. The proposed mechanism is as follows (Fig. 7B): when preferred carbon sources are available, the Hfq/Crc complex binds to the 5'UTR region of target mRNAs, inhibiting the translation of genes involved in non-preferred carbon metabolism. When preferred carbon sources are depleted, the CbrA/CbrB system is activated, inducing high expression of CrcZ sRNA, which competitively binds to Hfq, thereby relieving translational repression. This post-transcriptional regulatory module is central to achieving prioritized utilization and dynamic switching of carbon sources [141–144].

The regulatory module of unknown function comprises two transcription regulators, CRP and SoxR, which were annotated from the genome of P. plecoglossicida JUIM01. In model organisms such as E. coli, SoxR acts as a classic sensor of superoxide stress, activating antioxidant defense genes through the SoxS cascade. However, published literature indicates that no clear SoxS homologs have been identified in Pseudomonas species, and SoxR is not a key regulator of oxidative stress adaptation. For example, in P. putida, the canonical SoxR/SoxS cascade is altered. The superoxide stress response here is primarily mediated through SoxR-independent pathways involving novel transcription factors like FinR, while the native regulatory targets of SoxR remain unknown. In P. aeruginosa, SoxR has reduced sensitivity to superoxide and has evolved to regulate virulence and quorum sensing processes [145–147]. In E. coli, CRP serves as a central global regulator of carbon catabolism, strictly responding to dynamic changes in intracellular cAMP levels and primarily activating the transcription of genes involved in the utilization of carbon sources such as lactose and galactose. However, studies show that CRP in P. putida has evolved to possess an extremely high affinity for cAMP (approximately 100 times higher than that of E. coli CRP) and can respond to cGMP to adapt to its physiological environment characterized by extremely low and stable intracellular cAMP concentrations. This CRP also has been shown to be largely uninvolved in the regulation of carbon metabolism [148–150]. Therefore, although CRP and SoxR are annotated in P. plecoglossicida JUIM01, their regulatory functions in other Pseudomonas species have significantly deviated from the classical E. coli models. Hence the specific physiological roles and regulatory mechanisms in P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 still need to be further elucidated.

These six major regulatory modules form a dynamic, sophisticated, and multi-level regulatory system through signal integration and cross-talk mediated by core global factors (such as CsrA, Lrp, and Fis). Among them, the metabolic regulatory network, encompassing element homeostasis, structural composition, carbon metabolism, and catabolite inhibition, provides the material, energy, and structural foundation for cell survival and growth. Building upon this foundation, the environmental adaptation regulatory network including the modules for environmental stress sensing, survival adaptation, and a functionally uncharacterized module enables the cell to rapidly sense and respond to the external challenges. The close relationship between two core networks would need to be proven to verify the metabolic versatility and robust environmental adaptability of P. plecoglossicida JUIM01.

The genetic basis of glucose metabolism and fatty acid metabolism in P. plecoglossicida JUIM01

The genetic background underlying the glucose and fatty acid metabolism was elucidated based on the annotation and comparative genomic analysis of P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 genome. A complete set of genes encoding the metabolic pathways for utilizing glucose and fatty acids as carbon and energy sources, with key components is summarized in Fig. 8. P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 contains typical modules for glucose metabolism characteristic of the Pseudomonas genus. These include genes encoding key enzymes involved in the initial periplasmic oxidative pathway (glucose dehydrogenase (Gcd) and gluconate dehydrogenase (Gad)), as well as key genes for the three pathways responsible for the transport and phosphorylation of glucose, gluconate, and 2-ketogluconate (e.g., glk, gntP/gnuK, kguT/kguK/kguD) [151]. For the catabolism of the central metabolite 6-phosphogluconate, genes encoding the complete Entner–Doudoroff (ED) pathway (key enzyme genes edd, eda) and the pentose phosphate (PP) pathway (key enzyme gene gnd) were identified [152]. Further annotation confirmed the high completeness of its downstream metabolic networks, encompassing: genes for the latter half of glycolysis (catalyzing the conversion of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate to pyruvate); a complete set of TCA cycle enzymes; key enzymes for the glyoxylate shunt (aceA, glcB); and core enzymes for gluconeogenesis (e.g., pcyAB, pck, and maeB) [153]. Simultaneously, P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 possesses the complete genetic repertoire for fatty acid metabolism. The genome encodes all key genes involved in the transport (fadL), activation (fadD), and β-oxidation (fadE, fadB, fadA) of long-chain fatty acids [154–156]. Additionally, genes encoding the full enzyme system required for de novo fatty acid synthesis were identified, including those for the acetyl-CoA carboxylase complex (accABCD) and multiple enzymes catalyzing the chain elongation cycle (e.g., fabH, fabD, fabG, fabA/Z, fabV, etc.) [157–159].

The predicted network of glucose and fatty acid metabolism in P. plecoglossicida JUIM01

By integrating these complete gene sets and referencing classical metabolic models of P. putida KT2440 and P. aeruginosa PAO1, an integrated glucose and fatty acid metabolic network in P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 was proposed and shown in Fig. 8. This genetic architecture underpins a highly interconnected and flexible central metabolic system. Such "full-pathway" capability likely confers a key adaptive advantage in fluctuating environments. First, periplasmic oxidation coupled with multiple parallel import pathways enables efficient glucose capture across a range of substrate concentrations. Second, the coexistence of the energy-efficient ED pathway and a complete gluconeogenesis pathway supports rapid growth under carbon abundance and allows synthesis of sugar precursors from alternative sources (e.g., fatty acids) during scarcity. Finally, the complete TCA cycle and glyoxylate branch ensure efficient energy production and facilitate flux balancing and anaplerosis around the central metabolite acetyl-CoA from diverse carbon inputs. Alignment of the extensive and complete metabolic network in P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 genome with those known metabolic plasticity and environmental adaptability of pseudomonads indicated its inherent versatility likely linked to cope with nutritional fluctuations and achieve competitive success in complex ecological niches.

Structural analysis of putative 2KGA metabolism-related gene clusters

The gene clusters associated with conversion of glucose to 2KGA were analyzed to find the putative genetic clusters. As shown in Fig. 9A, P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 possesses a conserved Pseudomonas-type regulatory network for glucose metabolism. This network consists of four core transcription factors including HexR, GntR, PtxS, and the GtrS/GltR two-component system, which coordinately regulate three pathways responsible for the transport and phosphorylation of glucose and gluconate, as well as the assimilation of 2KGA. These pathways ultimately channel 6-phosphogluconate into central metabolism primarily via the ED pathway [104]. The key regulatory relationships are as follows: (a) HexR regulates the zwf/pgl/eda and edd/glk/gltR/gtrS operons, as well as the gapA gene; (b) GntR regulates gntP and gnuK; and (c) PtxS regulates the gad operon (gndS/gndL/gndC) and the kgu operon (kguE/kguK/kguT/kguD) [103, 105, 160, 161]. The composition and chromosomal distribution of these regulatory modules are highly conserved.

The putative 2KGA metabolism gene clusters in P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 using comparative genomic and phylogenetic analysis. A Conserved regulatory network of glucose metabolism genes in the JUIM01 strain. High synteny with homologous gene clusters in Pseudomonas indicates core functional conservation in 2KGA metabolism. B The putative genomic features associated with the high-yield 2KGA phenotype in the JUIM01 strain including tandem amplification of key genes and duplication and differentiation of regulatory elements. C Phylogenetic analysis of Zwf paralogs in the JUIM01 strain

Compared to P. putida KT2440 and P. aeruginosa PAO1, P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 genome presents some typical structural features possibly associated with its high-yield 2KGA phenotype (Fig. 9B). The copies of the key gene sets including the identified carbohydrate porin gene (oprB-1, oprB-2, and oprB-3) and the glucose dehydrogenase gene (gcd-1, gcd-2, and gcd-3) are arranged in tandem, which might synergistically enhance glucose uptake and periplasmic oxidation. The key regulators showed high duplication and divergence. P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 contains the designated hexR1 and gtrS1/gltR1 in distal genomic regions homologous to hexR and gtrS/gltR located within classical operons. These homologs exhibit low sequence identity of 44% and 36%/50%, respectively, to their classical counterparts, suggesting the potential neofunctionalization or subfunctionalization. The genome also contains multiple 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase (Zwf) paralogs. The genomic locations of three zwf copies (zwf-1, zwf-2, and zwf-3) are consistent with previously reported Pseudomonas patterns [162, 163]. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that these Zwf paralogs are distributed across distinct evolutionary branches (Fig. 9C), each showing the highest sequence similarity to one of the ZwfA, ZwfB, or ZwfC paralogs from P. putida KT2440. Based on their genomic context, it could be hypothesized that these paralogs fulfill distinct physiological roles: the Zwf-1 encoded within the classical HexR regulon likely plays a dominant role in glucose catabolism, Zwf-2 encoded adjacent to the pentose phosphate pathway gene gnd may primarily participate in this pathway; and Zwf-3 encoded near hexR1 may have a more specialized function. The paralog diversity might be hypothetically involved in fine-tuning of metabolic flux and redox balance through differential cofactor preferences (e.g., NADP⁺ vs. NAD⁺) to enhance the metabolic adaptability in dynamic environments. While retaining the typical Pseudomonas regulatory network, this gene cluster exhibits tandem amplification of key genes (e.g., oprB and gcd) and functional differentiation of regulatory elements (e.g., hexR and gtrS/gltR). These putative genomic features could provide a genetic support for enhancing precursor flux, increasing regulatory complexity, and optimizing redox balance.

Genetic basis of the oxidative phosphorylation system in strain JUIM01

The genetic basis of oxidative phosphorylation system (electron transport chain) and key substrate-dehydrogenase complexes possibly involved in energy metabolism of obligate aerobe and synthesis of metabolic precursors for 2KGA were analyzed and shown in Fig. 10. Based on whole-genome sequencing and annotation, a complete set of genes encoding the oxidative phosphorylation machinery in P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 was identified. Among them, the core electron-transport chain components encoded in the genome include NADH ubiquinone oxidoreductase (Complex I), succinate dehydrogenase (Complex II), cytochrome bc₁ complex (Complex III), a complete ubiquinone biosynthesis gene cluster, cytochrome c synthase, and five terminal oxidases: cytochrome bo₃ quinol oxidase (Cyo), cyanide-insensitive oxidase (CIO), aa₃-type cytochrome c oxidase (aa₃), cbb₃-1-type cytochrome c oxidase (cbb₃-1), and cbb₃-2-type cytochrome c oxidase (cbb₃-2). Genes encoding ATP synthase were also identified. Based on previously reported electron transport models for Pseudomonas species, it could be proposed that P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 likely possesses a highly branched respiratory chain (Fig. 10). Specifically, electrons derived from substrate oxidation, e.g., from the periplasmic enzyme membrane-bound PQQ-dependent glucose dehydrogenase (PQQ-mGDH) and membrane-bound FAD-dependent gluconate dehydrogenase (FAD-mGADH) are transferred to ubiquinone. Electrons from reduced ubiquinone can then be transferred to molecular oxygen via two main routes: either directly through the Cyo or CIO oxidases, or indirectly via the cytochrome bc₁ complex and cytochrome c to the terminal oxidases (aa₃, cbb₃-1, or cbb₃-2) [164–166]. This electron transport process coupled with proton transport across the cytoplasmic membrane generates the proton motive force for ATP synthesis.

PQQ-mGDH/Gcd: membrane-bound pyrroloquinoline quinone-dependent glucose dehydrogenase; FAD-mGADH/Gad: membrane-bound flavin adenine dinucleotide-dependent gluconate dehydrogenase; MDH: malate dehydrogenase; GAPDH: glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase; PDH: pyruvate dehydrogenase; CoQ: coenzyme Q, ubiquinone; Cyo oxidase: bo₃-type quinol oxidase; CIO oxidase: cyanide-insensitive oxidase; aa₃ oxidase: aa₃-type cytochrome c oxidase; cbb₃-1 oxidase: cbb₃-1-type cytochrome c oxidase; cbb₃-2 oxidase: cbb₃-2-type cytochrome c oxidase

Among the dehydrogenases catalyzing primary substrate oxidation, the PQQ-dependent membrane-bound glucose dehydrogenase (PQQ-mGDH/Gcd) predominantly participating in the 2KGA synthesis was analyzed. The activity of PQQ-mGDH/Gcd strictly depends on the presence of the cofactor pyrroloquinoline quinone (PQQ) [167]. Genomic analysis identified a PQQ biosynthesis gene cluster composed of nine genes of pqqFABECDMHI, which usually varies among bacteria, with the pqqABCDE core unit being conserved in P. plecoglossicida JUIM01. Notably, variations in the copy number of certain genes within this cluster such as pqqF affect PQQ synthesis levels [167–169]. Beyond dehydrogenases directly involved in the oxidation of carbon sources for respiration, genome annotation revealed that P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 encodes five paralogous proteins of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), which could be classified into three categories based on their cofactor preferences and catalytic functions. NAD⁺-dependent GAPDH (GapA, EC 1.2.1.12) encoded by ACYC76_1451 and ACYC76_3616 is a key player in glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, catalyzing the reversible oxidation and phosphorylation of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate to 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate while reducing NAD⁺ to NADH [170]. NADP⁺-dependent GAPDH (GapN, EC 1.2.1.9) encoded by ACYC76_1740 and ACYC76_2291 directly catalyzes the irreversible oxidation of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate to 3-phosphoglycerate while reducing NADP⁺ to NADPH [171, 172]. Arsenate detoxification-related GAPDH (EC 1.2.1.107) encoded by ACYC76_4582 along with the adjacent ACYC76_4581-encoded protein (putatively encoding the efflux protein ArsJ) is reported to mediate a novel arsenate efflux resistance mechanism [173]. From the above analysis, P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 possesses the genetic components required for a complex branched respiratory chain and a diverse set of substrate dehydrogenases, which offer metabolic flexibility in adapting to different oxygen environments, energy metabolism, redox balance, and the redox regulation likely associated with 2KGA synthesis.

Conclusion

The present study provided the first high-quality genomic map of a 2KGA industrial producer P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 with high yield. The genome comprises a circular chromosome of 5.13 Mb harboring 4,517 predicted protein-coding genes. Functional annotation revealed a significant enrichment of genes putatively involved in carbon metabolism, transport, and regulation, underscoring the metabolic plasticity typical of pseudomonads. A conceptual hierarchical regulatory network integrating six synergistic modules encompassing 75 core regulatory factors (encoded by 151 genes) for metabolic and environmental adaptation was further deciphered. The network provided a molecular mechanism of P. plecoglossicida JUIM01 to maintain cellular homeostasis, efficiently utilize diverse carbon sources, and cope with environmental stress. Comparative genomic analysis pinpointed at least 3 genomic features potentially linked to its high 2KGA production phenotype, including tandem amplifications of key genes (oprB and gcd), putative functional differentiation or neofunctionalization of transcriptional regulators (hexR and gtrS/gltR), and functional divergence among glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase homologs (Zwf). The genome also encodes complete pathways for the ED pathway, the TCA cycle, gluconeogenesis, and fatty acid metabolism. Coupled with a highly branched electron transport chain featuring multiple terminal oxidases, these genetic traits provide a robust foundation for efficient assimilation of multiple carbon sources and energy generation under varying environmental conditions. The functional validation of these specific duplicated genes, paralogs, or regulatory systems is ongoing to provide a foundational genomic resource and testable hypotheses for rational chassis engineering toward robust 2KGA production by P. plecoglossicida JUIM01.
