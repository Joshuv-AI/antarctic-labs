Exploring infant safe sleeping practice in a multicultural setting: findings from the maternal and infant health monitoring system in Abu Dhabi
Authors: Rima Kaddoura, Basant Elsiwi, Wei Chen, Mira Mousa, Zeinab Hassan, Zeinab Hassan, Zeinab Hassan, Kawthar Salem Al Ameri, Ruba Meqbel, Razan Jamal Alramahi, Saloua El Azzabi, Haja Isatu Carew, Mervat Muad, Kawther Suleiman Abu-Sahyoun, Andrea Kaelin Agten, Kiran Jilani, Mayada Tayallah, Balqis Alawamleh, Zainab Salih, Rima Kaddoura, Wei Chen, Mira Mousa, Jessica Rappu, Rakesh Rajagopalan, Hala Ghadban, Bali Renu, Laila Lakhal, Amnish Ravindranath, Mohamed Ali Abdulla Karji Almarzooqi, Mazin Adil Mohamed Elsaid, Avashnee Rampersad, Hamda Al Suwaidi, Stuart Gietel-Basten, Dean B Everett, Yousef Al Hammadi, Omniyat Al Hajeri, Khairat Al- Habbal, Hamda Al Suwaidi, Stuart Gietel-Basten, Dean B Everett

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSafe infant sleep recommendations are well established in many high-income countries, several of which are multicultural countries. However, less is known about infant sleep practices in non-Western, highly multicultural settings, where, the majority of residents are expatriates from diverse world regions. The United Arab Emirates (UAE) represents a unique pediatric context, with more than 80% of residents being expatriates from over 200 nationalities, and limited national guidance on infant sleep.MethodsUsing cross-sectional data collected between 2023 and 2024 data from the Abu Dhabi Maternal and Infant Health Monitoring System, we determined the prevalence of five infant sleep practices: (1) back sleep position, (2) separate approved sleep surface, (3) room sharing with an adult without bed sharing, (4) room sharing with bed sharing and (5) no soft objects or loose bedding. We used multivariable logistic regression to estimate the associations between sociodemographic and behavioral factors and each of the infant sleep practices.ResultsThe least prevalent safe sleep practices were using a separate approved sleep surface (8.4%) and avoiding soft bedding (11.5%). Region of origin was associated with all sleep practices. Reported receipt of healthcare-provider advice was infrequent and was not associated with adherence to any sleep practice.ConclusionIn a multicultural, high-income setting in the Middle East, adherence to infant safe sleep recommendations is selective and is associated with cultural background. These findings highlight the need for culturally responsive pediatric guidance and suggest that US-derived safe sleep recommendations may require contextual adaptation to improve effectiveness in diverse populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09395-5.

What’s new

This is the first population-based study of infant sleep practices in a highly multicultural Middle Eastern population, where expatriates comprise more than 80% of the residents. Adherence to US-derived safe sleep recommendations, adopted by governmental entities in Abu Dhabi, is highly selective, with high use of supine positioning (87%) but very low use of separate approved sleep surfaces (8%) and avoidance of soft bedding (12%). Region of origin was more strongly associated with all sleep practices than education or income, and reported receipt of healthcare provider advice was not associated with adherence. These findings highlight the need for culturally adapted guidance in multicultural settings.

Background

Infant sleep positioning remains a major public health focus due to its association with sudden infant deaths (SIDS), and deaths of undetermined cause in infants under one year [1]. SIDS account for 21% of infant deaths globally [2], and is the third leading cause of infant mortality in the United States (U.S.).

Modifiable factors such as infant sleep practices are associated with an increased risk of SIDS [3]. In addition, other non-modifiable risk factors associated with an increased risk of SIDS include male infants, poverty, and race [4]. In 1992, the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) Taskforce on SIDS and the accompanying Back to Sleep (now Safe to Sleep) campaign provided a recommendation that infants be placed on their backs to sleep [5]. AAP has also expanded safe sleep recommendations to include a firm sleep surface (e.g., crib or bassinet), room sharing without bed sharing, and avoiding soft objects and loose bedding [6].

Globally, sleep-related infant mortality has fallen dramatically since this first public health prevention campaign [7]. Mortality rates from 1990 to 2022 have declined from 1.30 to 0.42 deaths per 1000 live births in the U.S [8, 9], and from 1.50 to 0.28 deaths per 1000 live births in the United Kingdom (U.K.) [10, 11]. According to the Global Burden of Disease of 2021 study, low Sociodemographic Index (SDI) regions have a SIDS mortality rate of 0.43 deaths per 1000 live births [12]. Country-level data from the Middle East is sparse. Based on figures reported in the only study conducted in the United Arab Emirates (UAE), the SIDS rate was estimated at approximately 0.66 deaths per 1000 live births [13], higher than the global average of 0.24 per 1000 live births [12].

Nonetheless, most research on safe sleep practices is conducted in Western contexts, showing reductions in non-supine sleep and soft bedding use, while bed sharing remains common in some populations [14–18]. Despite awareness campaigns, a large proportion of infants continue to sleep unsafely, with less than half using supine positioning or cribs/bassinets [17]. Factors influencing adherence include maternal age, education, breast feeding, ethnicity, cultural norms, and caregiver knowledge, though the association between SIDS awareness and practice is inconsistent [15, 19].

The United Arab Emirates (UAE) represents a unique context for studying infant sleep practices due to its distinctive demographic composition. Approximately 88% of UAE residents are expatriates (non-citizens), representing over 200 nationalities, from both high-income and low- and middle- income countries (LMIC), while only 12% are Emirati citizens (Highest proportion of residents globally, closely followed by Qatar and Kuwait) [20, 21]. This demographic composition differs from multicultural Western countries, where immigrant populations typically represent minorities within majority host populations. In the UAE, there is no single majority cultural group, and the pediatric population reflects this diversity. Understanding infant care practices in this context is important because families may maintain cultural practices from their countries of origin, there may be limited social pressure to conform to a dominant cultural norm, healthcare providers must serve families from extremely diverse backgrounds, and development of public health guidance must consider this diversity.

The establishment of the Maternal and Infant Health Monitoring System provides a rare population-based platform in the region to examine not only the prevalence of infant sleep practices, but also how adherence to specific recommendations might vary by region of origin, socioeconomic factors, and reported receipt of healthcare-provider advice. The objective of this study were thus to (1) determine the prevalence of infant sleep practices in the UAE, and (2) examine sociodemographic, behavioral, and healthcare-related factors of these practices in order to identify opportunities for culturally responsive paediatric sleep guidelines.

Methods

Data source

This is a cross-sectional study using data from the ongoing Abu Dhabi Maternal and Infant Health Monitoring System, a population-based surveillance system designed to capture approximately 10% of annual births in Abu Dhabi. Abu Dhabi is the capital emirate and the largest by area, accounting for approximately 35% of the UAE’s population. The system thus aims at recruiting a representative sample of the Abu Dhabi emirate. Further details on the monitoring system are available in a previous publication [22].

Mothers were recruited from nine major hospitals in Abu Dhabi which included both public and private hospitals to capture Abu Dhabi’s population diversity. Recruitment occurred by trained, bilingual (English and Arabic) interviewers during the immediate postpartum period (within 48 h of delivery) (Phase A), with a follow-up survey conducted between 3 and 6 months postpartum (Phase B). Questions about infant sleep patterns and practices were asked during Phase B. Thus, each participant was surveyed once during the postpartum period, providing a snapshot of infant sleep practices at that time point. The population was stratified based on birthweight into a low birthweight group (less than 2500 g); and a normal birthweight (2500 g or higher). A stratified sampling approach was used, with oversampling of mothers of LBW infants due to the lower prevalence. The sample selected represents a proportional cross-section of births from each selected hospital.

Eligibility criteria included: (1) singleton live birth, and (2) ability to complete the survey in English or Arabic. Mothers were excluded if their infant had major congenital anomalies requiring specialized care.

During the immediate postpartum period, the interviewers approached eligible mothers, explained the study purpose and procedures, and provided an information sheet in the mother’s preferred language (English or Arabic). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Ethical approval was obtained from Khalifa University and the Department of Health in Abu Dhabi. Consent was documented in the REDCap database.

All data were collected and stored in accordance with UAE data protection regulations and international best practices. Each participant was assigned a unique study ID number, and all data were de-identified prior to analysis.

Study population

Stage One of the monitoring system was conducted in 2023, with 691 mothers from four hospitals completing Phase A, of whom 472 completed Phase B. After analyzing feedback received on the survey instruments, questions related to sleep practices were introduced at a later stage of Phase B in 2023, resulting in responses from 272 participants. Between 1 and 4 responses (< 1.5%) were missing for individual sleep-related questions, yielding to final sample sizes ranging from 268 to 272 across analyses. The second stage of the monitoring system was conducted in 2024 and involved a total of 2174 mothers from 9 hospitals across Abu Dhabi completing Phase A, with 1267 continuing to Phase B. In Phase B, missing responses for sleep practice questions ranged from 17 to 22 (< 2%), resulting in sample sizes from 1245 to 1250. For this study, the final sample sizes included in the analyses ranged from 1513 to 1522.

Surveys

Consistent with previous literature, we examined characteristics that may be associated with sleep practices. In Phase A, the mothers were asked about the infant’s gestational age, and about their age, education, employment, and region of origin, which was classified into Emirati Nationals and each of the World Health Organization (WHO) regions.

In Phase B surveys, the interviewers asked mothers about infant sleep practices, the infant’s age, and if they were informed about any medical condition that the baby had. Mothers were also asked about their smoking status, if they were still breast feeding at the time of the survey, total monthly household income, and other variables related to having basic needs such as shelter and food hardship during the 12 months before the baby was born. We categorized household income into: low income (less than 10,000 AED/month), middle income (10,000–40,000 AED/month), and high income (40,000 AED/month or more). To capture material hardship, we constructed a Material Hardship Index based on five indicators of unmet basic needs: (1) whether mothers were worried about not having stable housing, (2) whether they were worried about food running out, (3) whether food actually ran out before they had money to buy more, (4) whether they postponed going to the doctor due to transportation or distance and (5) whether utility services had threatened to shut off. Each of these variables was transformed into a percentile rank. We took the average of the percentile ranks for all five hardship items for each participant. The resulting index was rescaled to a 0–100 scale, with higher scores indicating lower material hardship and improved socioeconomic conditions.

Outcome

We examined five binary infant sleep practices as outcomes, including four recommended by the AAP and one additional non-APP-recommended practice (bed sharing) due to its cultural relevance in the study population. The AAP-recommended practices included: (1) back sleep position, (2) separate approved sleep surface, (3) room sharing with an adult without bed sharing, and (4) no soft objects or loose bedding. The fifth outcome (5) was room sharing with bed sharing. Back sleep position was categorized as back versus side or stomach. The other sleep practices were composite measures developed following the approach in a study based on data from the 2016 U.S. Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System (PRAMS) [15]. Separate approved sleep surface was defined as a composite variable requiring that the (1) infant slept alone in their own crib or bed and (2) that caregivers did not report use of non-approved sleep surfaces (e.g., twin or larger mattress/bed, infant car seat or swing, or couch or armchair), consistent with AAP safe sleep recommendations. Room sharing without bed sharing was defined based on (1) infant sleeping alone in their own crib and (2) in the same room as an adult caregiver (e.g. parents, other adult family members, or a nanny/ domestic worker). Room sharing with bed sharing was defined using the same room-sharing variable combined with the infant not consistently sleeping alone in their own crib or bed, as determined by the crib-sleeping question. No soft bedding was defined based on maternal report about the infant usually sleeping without blankets, toys, cushions, or pillows and crib bumper pads.

In the questionnaire, the question on whether the infant slept alone in their own crib or bed referred to behaviors in the past two weeks and used response categories of “always,” “often,” “sometimes,” “rarely,” or “never.” For composite outcomes incorporating the crib-sleeping question (e.g., room sharing without bed sharing and separate approved sleep surface), responses of “always” or “often” were classified as adherence to this sleep practice, whereas “sometimes,” “rarely,” or “never” were classified as non-adherence.

Statistical analysis

We used chi-squared tests (for categorized values) or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (for continuous or ordinal values) to examine bivariate associations between each outcome and the covariates. Variables moderately associated with an outcome in the bivariate analysis (p < 0.25) were included in multivariable logistic regression models to estimate adjusted associations between each of the five sleep practicesand the selected covariates. Variables that were clinically significant from the literature were also included. Final models included: maternal age in years; education; employment; WHO origin of region; material hardship index; income; gestational age; child has medical condition; still breast feeding; and ever smoked. Missing data were minimal for most variables (< 5% for all outcome variables and most covariates). Covariates with less than 10% missing data were excluded. Sensitivity analyses comparing characteristics of participants with complete versus incomplete data showed no significant differences in key demographic variables (maternal age, education, region of origin), suggesting missing data were likely missing completely at random. Total household income was the only variable with a high percentage of missingness (20%), therefore we applied multiple imputation by chained equations for this variable, where the imputation models included maternal age, education, employment status, and WHO region of origin, as these were significantly associated with the likelihood of missing income. For each of the five outcomes, we constructed multivariable logistic regression models including all covariates specified a priori based on existing literature and the results of the bivariate analysis. We did not apply Bonferroni correction, as our analyses were based on multivariable regression models examining a set of prespecified infant sleep practices in relation to a common set of risk factors, rather than a series of independent hypothesis tests. The multivariable logistics regression models were fitted on each imputed dataset and final estimates and 95% CI were obtained by pooling results across imputations using Rubin’s Rules [23]. All analyses were conducted using R, version 4.3.3.

Results

Participant characteristics

More than three-quarters (80.1%) of infants were approximately 3 months old at the time of the Phase B call, the majority were born full term (84.9%), and most had no reported medical conditions (90.5%) (Table 1). Nearly one-third of mothers were UAE citizens (29.9%), less than half were from the Eastern Mediterranean region (43.3%), with mothers originating from the following countries: Algeria, Djibouti, Morocco, Tunisia, Egypt, Gaza Strip, West Bank, Jordan, Lebanon, Syria, Iraq, Iran, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Bahrain, Yemen, Somalia and Sudan. Over half the mothers (59.8%) were aged between 25 and 35 years and had completed an undergraduate degree (57.9%). About one-third were older than 35 years (30.6%), had only completed secondary school (31.2%), and were housewives (36.3%). During the 12 months before the baby was born, more than half the mothers had a middle household income (60.4%), while approximately one-third had experienced low to mild hardship (37.9%).

Study participants’ characteristics

Prevalence of sleep practices and receipt of sleep advice

Overall, less than three-quarters of mothers (68.6%) reported placing their infants on their back to sleep (Table 2). More than half the mothers (58.5%) noted that their infants “always or often” slept alone in their crib or bed, and almost all (97.2%) reported that their infants slept in the same room as an adult caregiver, including parents, other adult family members, or a nanny/ domestic worker. A smaller proportion (63.6%) reported that they were room sharing with their infants without bed sharing, meaning they “always or often” placed their infant to sleep in a separate crib located in the same room as an adult. In contrast, more than a quarter (33.0%) of mothers reported room sharing with bed sharing. The most common sleep surface used was a crib, bassinet, or pack n play/portable crib (74.7%), followed by a twin or larger bed (67.2%), an infant car seat or swing (52.1%). This is a concerning finding as the car seat and swing are not approved sleep surfaces and are associated with increased risk of positional asphyxiation, particularly if the infant is left unattended or if straps are not properly secured [24]. Despite the large proportion of mothers reporting that a crib or bassinet was used for their infants, which is the sleep surface recommended by AAP, only 8.4% indicated that they “always or often” used a separate crib as the only sleep surface for their infant when asked “Where does your baby usually sleep?”. Only a small proportion of mothers (11.5%) reported not using soft bedding for infant sleep. Most mothers reported the use of blankets (83.2%). Hence, while supine positioning was relatively common in our sample, adherence to other recommendations, such as the use of a separate approved sleep surface and avoidance of soft bedding, was generally low. This finding suggests a selective uptake of US-derived recommendations rather than a uniform adoption of safe sleep guidelines. It is worth noting that these guidelines are adopted by multiple governmental entities in the UAE such as the Abu Dhabi Early Childhood Authority (ECA) and the Abu Dhabi Department of Education and Knowledge (ADEK) [25, 26].

Infant sleep practices and reported receipt of healthcare provider advice

The prevalence of mothers who reported to have received advice from a healthcare worker about sleep practices was generally low. Only about one-third of mothers reported receiving advice about placing infants in a back sleep position (31.7%), with lower proportions reported for advice about placing infants to sleep in a crib, bassinet or pack n play/portable crib (22.9%), and about what items are allowed or prohibited to be placed in bed with infants (11.5%).

Bivariate analysis

Bivariate analyses showed that maternal age, education, occupation, region of origin, household income, and material hardship index were significantly associated with one or more sleep practices. However, receiving health care provider advice was not significantly associated with any infant sleep practices. None of the examined covariates were associated with the use of separate safe sleep surfaces (Supplementary Table 1).

Mothers with a graduate degree were more likely to place their infants in the supine sleep position (74.4%) and to avoid soft bedding (14.8%) compared with mothers with an undergraduate degree or secondary-level education. Similarly, mothers with a high household income and no material hardship more frequently used the supine position (80.4% and 74.2%, respectively) and practiced room sharing without bed sharing (72.2% and 66.7%, respectively) compared with those with low/ middle income or low to mild/ high material hardship. Emirati mothers demonstrated a higher prevalence of supine positioning (71.3%) and room sharing without bed sharing (75.1%). Mothers from the Americas/Europe showed the highest prevalence across several practices, including supine positioning (85.4%), room sharing without bed sharing (82.6%), and avoidance of soft bedding (27.7%). Supine positioning was also relatively common among mothers from South-East Asia and the Western Pacific region (approximately 70%). Maternal employment was associated only with higher use of the supine sleep position (73.0%), while mothers aged 25–35 years old were more likely to avoid soft bedding (13.2%).

In contrast, room sharing with bed sharing was more common among mothers from the South-East Asian (54.3%), African (40.7%) and Western Pacific regions (39.8%), as well as among mothers with an undergraduate degree (35.7%), those with low household income (41.2%), and those currently breastfeeding (35.0%).

Multivariable analysis

After adjustment, most characteristics remained significantly associated with one or more safe sleep practices, except for maternal age (Table 3). Mothers who were employed (adjusted Odds Ratio [aOR] = 1.56, 95% CI = 1.14, 2.13) or housewives (aOR = 1.43, 95% CI = 1.06, 1.94) had higher odds of placing infants in the supine position compared with unemployed mothers. Higher odds were also observed among mothers from the combined Americas/European regions relative to Emirati nationals (aOR = 3.28, 95% CI = 1.12, 9.62), whereas mothers experiencing high (aOR = 0.61, 95% CI = 0.42, 0.89) or low to mild material hardship had lower odds of supine positioning (aOR = 0.61, 95% CI = 0.42, 0.89) compared to mothers with no hardship.

Use of separate approved sleep surfaces was more likely among mothers from the Americas/European regions (aOR = 2.57, 95% CI = 1.05, 6.25), while mothers with an undergraduate degree had lower odds compared with those with secondary education (aOR = 0.59, 95% CI = 0.37, 0.92). Avoidance of soft bedding was more prevalent among mothers from the Western Pacific region (OR = 2.78, 95% CI = 1.53, 5.06).

Compared with Emirati mothers, those from the Eastern Mediterranean (aOR = 0.57, 95% CI = 0.40, 0.79), Western Pacific (aOR = 0.44, 95% CI = 0.26, 0.73), African (aOR = 0.42, 95% CI = 0.21, 0.82) and South-East Asian regions (aOR = 0.26, 95% CI = 0.16, 0.41) had lower odds of room sharing without bed sharing. Conversely, for the separate outcome of room sharing with bed sharing, mothers from the Western Pacific (aOR = 2.01, 95% CI = 1.20, 3.38) and South-East Asian regions (aOR = 3.51, 95% CI = 2.22, 5.57)had higher odds. High material hardship was associated with reduced odds of room sharing without bed sharing (aOR = 0.58, 95% CI = 0.39, 0.85) compared with mothers reporting no hardship.

Indeed, region of origin was associated with multiple sleep practices, highlighting the significant role of cultural norms and prior caregiving practices.

Discussion

This study provides the first population-based assessment of infant sleep practices in a highly multicultural Middle Eastern population, addressing a significant geographic and cultural gap in the literature. It is the first and largest study in the region to examine sleep practices across multiple nationalities residing in the same country, allowing for direct comparison of practices across cultural groups while controlling for the shared healthcare and policy environment. In this cross-sectional study, we collected information about multiple risk factors, identified in prior research, and introduced a new composite variable to capture the complexity of socioeconomic status. It is important to report that all information collected in the surveys were self-reported, which could potentially introduce social-desirability bias, likely underestimating non-adherence to recommended practices. As well, the cross-sectional design identifies associations between sociodemographic factors and sleep practices, but not temporal relationships or causal pathways.

We found that some infant sleep practices in the UAE mirror global trends, such as the adherence to supine sleep positioning. Nearly two-thirds (68.6%) of mothers reported placing their infants in the supine position, a higher prevalence than that reported in the only prior study in the UAE (n = 716) from 2007 (49.1%) [13], and comparable to estimates from studies in Saudi Arabia [27, 28] and Palestine [29]. This increase likely reflects global improvements in adherence to safe sleep positions following educational campaigns [30]. Nevertheless, side sleeping remains common in several Middle Eastern countries, such as Iraq [31], Egypt (n = 180) [32] and Turkey [33, 34], often driven by concerns about choking and to prevent occipital flattening.

Almost all mothers in this study reported room sharing with their infants (97.2%), consistent with findings from other studies in the region [13, 27, 29, 32–35]. Using a composite measure aligned with AAP recommendations, 63.6% of mothers practiced room sharing without bed sharing, comparable to that reported in a study from Saudi Arabia (65.3%) [27]. However, one-third of mothers (33.0%) reported room sharing with bed sharing, a practice common in the region and associated with cultural norms, breastfeeding practices and economic factors. Indeed, the prevalence of bed sharing in this study was similar to that reported in the UAE [13], Saudi Arabia [27, 35, 36], Turkey [33], and Iran [37], but lower than in Palestine [29] and Egypt [32], where maternal bonding beliefs and rising costs of baby cribs are key drivers. Adherence to crib use and avoidance of soft bedding was low in our sample. Only 11.5% of mothers reported avoiding all soft bedding, similar to findings from Saudi Arabia (6.9%) and Turkey (1.9%). Blankets were the most commonly used soft item, reported by over 80% of mothers (83.2%), with similar prevalences in other studies conducted in the region [13, 27, 29, 35]. Indeed, region of origin emerged as an important associated factor where mothers from the Americas/ European regions were more likely to follow multiple safe sleep recommendations. Mothers from Western Pacific and South-East Asian regions were more likely to room share with bed sharing, consistent with the prior UAE study [13] and with regional norms reported in the literature, which indicate that bed sharing is more commonly practiced in Southeast Asian, African, and Western Pacific regions. These findings suggest that infant sleep behaviors in the UAE are embedded in culturally rooted caregiving practices acquired prior to migration, rather than being shaped by current socioeconomic conditions or access to healthcare. Hence, it would be worth exploring the limitations of applying US-derived safe sleep guidance in contexts where cultural norms and caregiving environments differ.

Several maternal and sociodemographic characteristics were associated with sleep practices.

Employment was associated only with sleep position, with employed mothers and housewives more likely to place infants on their backs than unemployed mothers. Associations between employment and sleep practices have been inconsistent across the literature, with increased alignment with sleep guidelines reported among working mothers in Palestine [29], and lower adherence observed in Iran [37]. This likely reflects the heterogeneity of employment, including differences in job type, work conditions, and work environment.

Although education was linked to multiple practices in bivariate analyses, only the use of approved sleep surfaces remained significant, with lower adherence among mothers with an undergraduate education. Prior studies report inconsistent associations between education and sleep practices, with enhanced alignment with sleep guidelines observed among mothers with higher education in Saudi Arabia [27] and Palestine [29], and less alignment with sleep guidelines reported in Turkey [34].

The association between household income and material hardship was not investigated in any other study in the region. We found that mothers experiencing higher levels of material hardship were less likely to practice supine sleep positioning and room sharing without bed sharing compared to those reporting no hardship. The prevalence of room sharing with bed sharing was also higher among mothers who were breastfeeding, which is consistent with the current literature; however, this association did not remain significant in the multivariable analyses.

We did not observe any association between the receipt of advice from health care providers and any sleep practices. This is inconsistent with the findings of the U.S. PRAMS study, which reported receipt of provider advice to be associated with increased prevalence of each corresponding safe sleep practice. This disconnect raises questions about how safe sleep messages are delivered, interpreted, and culturally contextualized in healthcare settings such as those in the UAE. The lack of association between reported receipt of healthcare provider advice and sleep practices may reflect several possibilities: advice may not be effectively communicated or culturally adapted, mothers may not recall or prioritize advice that conflicts with cultural norms, or advice may be given inconsistently across providers. As well, standard counseling may be insufficient in a context where advice may not be culturally tailored or consistently reinforced. There is a need for the healthcare provider to engage with caregivers’ beliefs and culturally embedded norms when recommending best practices and guidelines.

It is important to note that there is no single, detailed guideline on safe infant sleep from WHO. The latter only provides general infant-care advice such as place babies on their back to sleep and the recommended number of hours of sleep. In the absence of a global standard, countries have developed their own safe sleep guidelines [38] such as that developed by AAP in the U.S. and the National Institute for Health Care and Excellence (NICE’s) guideline on postnatal care developed in the U.K [39]. Most countries in the Middle East region do not have formal national guidelines. If recommendations exist, they are typically adopted from international guidelines. These do not fully account for traditional norms and cultural contexts, which have a significant association with parents’ decision regarding infant sleep practices [40]. In the UAE, current recommendations on safe sleep primarily emphasize placing infants on their backs and avoiding toys and soft bedding [25, 26]. Although AAP recommend against bed sharing, it remains widely practiced in many cultures due to beliefs about physical bonding and closeness, ease of breastfeeding, or simply due to limited space and financial constraints. The high prevalence of such practices supports proposing recommendations tailored to the cultural context while accounting for safe sleep guidelines. This includes promoting safer bed sharing environments, such as avoiding soft bedding and excessive use of blankets that could lead to overheating, maintaining a safe distance between the infants and adults sharing the bed, and using firm mattresses.

In summary, these findings have relevance not only for the UAE and other Gulf countries, but also for multicultural Western settings. Counseling strategies that focus only on individual behaviors may be less effective than approaches that acknowledge cultural norms and practices particularly in regards to bed sharing, and sleep surfaces. Developing guidance that is locally adapted; such as promoting safer sleep environments when bed sharing occurs; may be more effective and feasible in that setting.

Conclusion

In a multicultural, high-income setting without national safe sleep guidelines, adherence to infant sleep recommendations is selective and associated with region of origin. While supine positioning is adopted, other practices such as adherence to an approved sleep surface and avoidance of soft bedding were uncommon. These findings showcase the limitations of applying globally derived safe sleep recommendations that are not adapted to local cultural and social contexts. In societies such as the UAE, population-level surveillance can identify gaps in adherence and highlight the specific recommendations that fail to be implemented across different cultures. Future interventions may benefit from considering cultural beliefs and practices, though longitudinal research is needed to identify modifiable factors and effective intervention strategies and to develop culturally responsive infant sleep guidelines to reduce preventable sleep-related infant deaths.
