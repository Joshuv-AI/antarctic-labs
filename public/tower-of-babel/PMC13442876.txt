Strain-coordination strategy enabling long-cycling all-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries
Authors: Jiaxu Zhang, Shengjie Xia, Pushun Lu, Suzhe Liang, Jiamin Fu, Zhimin Zhou, Wenlin Yan, Guantai Hu, Kaiyong Tuo, Jian Hong, Shutao Zhang, Ziqing Wang, Xueliang Sun, Changhong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAll-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries offer high energy density and enhanced safety. However, their practical application is hindered by high external operating pressure to mitigate mechano-chemical failures at interfaces. Here we show a strain-coordination strategy that leverages the opposite volume changes of electrodes during cycling to reduce electrode-level stress evolution and the external pressure required for stable operation. Using an FeS2 positive electrode and a prelithiated Si negative electrode as a representative system, we modulate the Li-to-Si ratio to achieve a near-zero-strain effect, where the expansion of FeS2 and the contraction of Li2Si partially counterbalance each other. This self-compensated electrode configuration mitigates mechano-electrochemical degradation under reduced pressure. As a result, the all-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries deliver a discharge capacity of 868.4 mAh g⁻1 at 15 MPa. Under 100 MPa, the cells achieve an areal capacity of 21.7 mAh cm⁻2, and cycle life 4500 cycles at 1 C (60 min) and 140,000 cycles at 15 C (4 min). Furthermore, low-pressure all-solid-state pouch cells achieve stable cycling over 500 cycles at 15 MPa. This strain-coordination strategy provides an approach for enabling stable operation of all-solid-state batteries under reduced external pressure.Subject terms: Batteries, Energy, Batteries, Batteries

Introduction

All-solid-state lithium-sulfur batteries (ASSLSBs) are considered a promising next-generation energy storage technology due to their higher theoretical energy density and enhanced safety compared to conventional lithium-ion batteries (LIBs)1,2. By replacing flammable liquid electrolytes with solid-state electrolytes (SSEs), ASSLSBs using high-capacity lithium metal negative electrodes and sulfur-based positive electrodes mitigate risks associated with polysulfide shuttle, electrolyte leakage, and thermal runaway. Despite these advantages, the widespread adoption of ASSLSBs remains hindered by several critical challenges, including interfacial instability, volume-induced mechanical degradation, and the necessity for high external pressures to maintain efficient ion transport3,4.

A major challenge of ASSLSBs is the substantial volume change during cycling, particularly at electrode interfaces. The sulfur positive electrode undergoes significant expansion (~80%) upon lithiation due to the formation of Li2S, while the lithium metal negative electrode experiences continuous consumption and volumetric contraction, leading to uneven interfaces and void formation5. Unlike liquid electrolytes, which adapt to these volumetric changes, SSEs require intimate contact between electrode components to ensure efficient lithium-ion transport. Loss of contact due to volumetric expansion and contraction results in increased interfacial resistance, electrochemical degradation, and premature battery failure6,7. To address these issues, ASSLSBs are typically operated under hundreds of megapascals to maintain mechano-chemical stability and suppress lithium dendrite formation. However, applying such high pressures is not only technically challenging but also leads to mechanical stress accumulation, phase instability, and potential mechanical failure of electrode materials over long-term cycling. Therefore, reducing the operational pressure while still maintaining long cycle life is crucial for the commercialization of ASSLSBs8.

To address this challenge, we propose a strain-coordination strategy that leverages the opposing volume changes of the negative electrode and positive electrode to achieve mechanochemically stable interfaces under low operation pressure. Specifically, a high-capacity FeS₂ positive electrode is paired with a prelithiated Si negative electrode using Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 (denoted as LPSC) as the solid electrolyte. During lithiation, FeS2 expands while the Si negative electrode contracts, and during delithiation, FeS2 contracts while Si expands. This synchronized volume compensation mitigates internal mechanical stress, preserves electrode-electrolyte interfacial contact, and enables stable operation under significantly reduced external pressures. As a result, the mechanically self-compensating ASSLSBs demonstrate a high discharge capacity of 868.4 mAh g⁻1 at 15 MPa, an areal capacity of 21.7 mAh cm⁻2, and an extended cycle life 4,500 and 140,000 cycles at 1 C and 15 C at 100 MPa, respectively. Moreover, an all-solid-state pouch cell achieves a discharge capacity of 615.8 mAh g⁻1 at 15 MPa and a cycle life of over 500 cycles at 1 C. These results highlight the effectiveness of strain coordination strategy in mitigating electrochemical-mechanical failure.

Results and Discussion

Design Principles of Self-Compensated Zero-Strain ASSLSBs

As a proof-of-concept, a high-capacity and cost-effective FeS2 positive electrode is paired with either Li or prelithiated Si negative electrodes. The FeS₂ positive electrode was composed of FeS2 active material, LPSC solid electrolyte, and conductive carbon, while the negative electrode consisted of pure Li metal or a prelithiated Si thin film. Cell fabrication details are provided in the methods section. During cycling, the large volume change of FeS₂ generates stress within the composite positive electrode, which is redistributed throughout the pellet and accumulates at the electrode/electrolyte interface due to mechanical mismatch with the dense solid electrolyte separator. By balancing the opposite volume-change behaviors of the positive electrode and negative electrode, the strain-coordination strategy reduces the overall electrode-level stress evolution and stabilizes interfacial contact under reduced external pressure, Figure S1. Typically, in Li | |FeS2 batteries, lithiation proceeds via two consecutive two-electron reactions. Pyrite FeS2 first transforms into Li2S and FeS, and then FeS reduces to Fe and Li2S. Given the molar volumes of FeS2 (24.1 cm3/mol), Li (12.9 cm3/mol), Fe (7.1 cm3/mol), and Li2S (27.8 cm3/mol), the total volume shrinks from 75.7 cm3/mol to 62.7 cm3/mol, a molar volume change (ΔV) of approximately −17.2% (Fig. 1a). This significant volume change leads to poor solid-solid contact, impairing cycling stability unless high external pressure is applied.

Stress Analysis of Electrode During Cycling

As different electrochemical systems require different optimal operating pressures9–11, we first determined the optimal operation pressure of all-solid-state Li2Si | |FeS2 batteries. After applying a fabrication pressure of 500 MPa, we measured the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) of Li2Si | |FeS2 cells under various operation pressures (Figure S2). Without external pressure, the cell exhibits a high resistance of 356 Ω (Figure S2a) due to inadequate solid-solid contact. As pressure increases to 1, 3, 5, and 10 MPa, the total resistance decreases significantly, indicating improved solid-solid contact. Between 15 and 100 MPa (Figure S2b and Figure S2c), resistance remains nearly constant, suggesting that pressure above 15 MPa ensures stable solid-solid contact for normal battery operation. Thus, 15 MPa is chosen as the optimal operating pressure for subsequent experiments.

To validate this strain-coordination strategy, we systematically investigated stress variations of different electrode configurations. As shown in Fig. 2a, Li4Ti5O12 (LTO) was used as the counter electrode due to its minimal volume change (~0.2%) during charge and discharge12. Thus, observed stress changes primarily originate from the working electrode. In the LTO | |FeS2 configuration (Fig. 2b), where FeS2 serves as the positive electrode and lithiated LTO as the negative electrode, Li ions are extracted from LTO and intercalate into FeS2 during discharge, causing a nonlinear increase in net stress. To compare stress variations across configurations, we normalized the maximum stress change by capacity. The stress change per capacity (σc) for the FeS2 positive electrode is 0.8 MPa mAh−1. It is noted that the result change with different SSEs, because SSEs may share mechanical load via local amorphization, grain-boundary sliding, and time-dependent plasticity under external pressure, thereby helping accommodate conversion-induced strains. To confirm the reliability of this result, we further measured σc of common positive electrodes, including LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (NCM) and LiCoO2 (LCO), using the zero-strain LTO negative electrode (Figure S3 and Figure S4). The σc of NCM is 0.213 MPa mAh−1, and the σc of LCO is −0.1558 MPa mAh−1. These results are consistent with the large volume change (259%) of FeS2 compared to NCM ( ~ 7%) and LCO ( ~ 4%)13,14, confirming the reliability of this measurement.

Furthermore, the σc of Li-Si alloy with a different molar ratio (1:1, 2:1 or 3:1) was evaluated. As shown in Fig. 2c and Figure S5, the σc of LiSi, Li2Si, and Li3Si are 0.71, 0.88, and 0.94 MPa mAh−1, respectively. In contrast, the σc of Li metal is 1.03 MPa mAh−1, significantly higher than that of LixSi (Fig. 2d). These results are consistent with the volume change of solution-type electrodes15. Given the similar σc values of FeS2 and Li2Si, we paired FeS2 positive electrode with a Li2Si negative electrode. Interestingly, as shown in Fig. 2e, cell stress initially decreases during discharge, suggesting Li2Si shrinkage exceeds FeS2 expansion. As discharge continues, stress increases, indicating FeS2 expansion dominates. Compared to the individual stress changes of FeS2 and Li2Si electrodes, the total stress (max stress value in Fig. 2e) variation of the Li2Si | |FeS2 cell is reduced by 80%, with a σc of 0.16 MPa mAh−1.To further confirm stress matching, we applied Lagrange interpolation. As shown in Fig. 2f, the fitted stress curves of LTO | |FeS2 (y1fit) and LTO | |Li2Si (y2fit) sum closely match the experimental Li2Si | |FeS2 results, confirming minimal overall electrode stress.

Strain Analysis of Electrode During Cycling

Comprehensive strain analysis of all-solid-state Li2Si | |FeS2 batteries was also conducted. In-situ optical microscope was employed to directly observe the volume change of electrodes. During discharge, the FeS2 positive electrode layer expands while the Li2Si negative electrode layer shrinks (Fig. 3a, b), with the opposite behavior occurring during the charge process (Fig. 3c). This confirms tiny changes in the cell’s net volume (Supplementary Movie 1, Figure S6). To further investigate volume changes during lithiation and delithiation, ex-situ X-ray computed tomography (XCT) imaging was performed on a 2 × 2 mm region at the center of the cell (Figure S7). In the pristine state, the positive electrode, SSE, and negative electrode exhibit thicknesses of approximately 56 μm, 268 μm, and 28 μm, respectively (Fig. 3d), with corresponding X-Y cross-sections shown in Figure S8. After discharge, the positive electrode expands to ~68 μm, while the negative electrode contracts to ~13 μm (Fig. 3e, Figure S9). Additionally, thickness variations were in situ recorded under constant pressure to analyze strain evolution during charge-discharge cycling. As shown in Fig. 3f, the Li2Si | |FeS2 cell first contracts during charge and then expands during discharge, aligning with the stress behavior in Fig. 2e. Notably, the total thickness change of the FeS2-based cell was only ~1.4 μm, even smaller than reported values for cells with NCM positive electrodes10. These strain-related analysis confirms that the self-compensated electrode volume change enables quasi zero-strain ASSLSBs.

Dynamic Analysis of Electrodes under Low Pressure

In-situ EIS was employed to investigate the resistance evolution of the ASSLSBs under 15 MPa. The first 1.5 cycles were analyzed considering the allotropic transformation of FeS2 from cubic to orthorhombic during the initial lithiation/delithiation process (Fig. 4a). To further interpret the in-situ EIS results, distribution of relaxation time (DRT) analysis was used to clearly distinguish the electrode and cell dynamic behaviors. The relaxation peak at 10−5 ~ 10−6 s is associated with particle contact and current collectors16. Compared to Li | |FeS2 cells, the Li2Si | |FeS2 cell exhibits a weaker peak in this region, indicating improved particle contact (Fig. 4b, c, and Figure S21, S22). Additionally, the peaks in the 10−3 ~ 10−2 s range that emerge at the end of charge/discharge are attributed to the contact loss caused by the void formation in Li metal under low pressure17. Notably, the Li2Si | |FeS2 cell exhibits fewer peaks, suggesting better interfacial contact even under low pressure. Peaks appearing in the 10⁻2 to 1 s range are linked to the Li diffusion at the negative electrode/SSE interface18,19. In the Li2Si | |FeS2 cell, weaker peaks appeared during the first discharge, indicating faster interfacial dynamics. In contrast, the Li | |FeS2 cell exhibits significant peaks, likely due to the formation of a passivation layer between Li metal and LPSC20,21. Peaks appearing in the longer relaxation times (~10 s) is generally associated with overall cell polarization. The Li2Si | |FeS2 cell shows a less pronounced peak compared with the Li | |FeS2 cell, suggesting reduced polarization during cycling. In-situ EIS analysis convincingly demonstrates that the Li2Si | |FeS2 cell with quasi-zero change exhibits significantly enhanced interfacial mechano-chemical stability and Li-ion transport kinetics.

The galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) was further performed under low pressure to investigate the mass transport dynamics in Li | |FeS2 and Li2Si | |FeS2 cells. At the initial discharge state, Li | |FeS2 and Li2Si | |FeS2 cells display the Li-ion diffusion coefficient (DLi) of 4.1 × 10−12 cm2 s−1 and 4.7 × 10−12 cm2 s−1 (Fig. 4d, e). As the discharge progresses, the Li2Si | |FeS2 cell maintains a higher DLi than the Li | |FeS2 cell, primarily due to its more mechanically and chemically stable interfacial contact, which is consistent with the DRT analysis. During the second discharge process, the reduction of (S–S) 22- persulfide units shortens diffusion pathways and induces significant structural changes, thus lowering DLi22,23. However, as layered γ-LixFeS2 forms in the subsequent discharge process, DLi increases until the full conversion to Li₂FeS2, after which DLi declines again due to the reduction of Fe2+ to Fe0 and the formation of resistive Li2S24. Notably, the Li2Si | |FeS2 cell exhibits minimal irreversible DLi loss, unlike Li | |FeS2, where losses accumulate with cycling (Fig. 4d). These findings underscore the importance of strain-matched electrode engineering in achieving stable and high-performance ASSLSBs.

Electrochemical Performance of ASSLSBs at low pressure

The electrochemical performance of Li | |FeS2 and Li2Si | |FeS2 cells was evaluated under a low operation pressure of 15 MPa at 0.1 C (Fig. 5a). The Li2Si | |FeS2 cell exhibits a high initial discharge capacity of 740.0 mAh g−1, significantly outperforming Li | |FeS2 (599.9 mAh g−1). It also maintains high capacity retention after 30 cycles, whereas Li | |FeS2 retains only 25% of its capacity after 10 cycles. The superior discharge capacity and cycling stability of Li2Si | |FeS2 are attributed to enhanced interfacial solid-solid contact and the stable Li2Si/LPSC interface. Voltage profiles further confirm these advantages. The third discharge curve of the Li2Si | |FeS2 exhibits a stable voltage decrease, indicating steady ion transport. In contrast, Li | |FeS2 shows voltage fluctuations at the end of discharge (Fig. 5b), which is caused by void formation at the Li/SSE interface. The rate performance under low pressure shows Li2Si | |FeS2 maintaining discharge capacities of 697.0, 617.3, 564.7, 528.4, and 498.9 mAh g⁻¹ at 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5 C, respectively, with a recovery capacity of 632.0 mAh g⁻¹ upon returning to 0.1 C (Fig. 5C). In situ stress measurements at different current densities (Figure S20) reveal a slightly reduced stress response at higher rates, which can be attributed to the lower delivered capacity and thus reduced lithium transport. In contrast, Li | |FeS2 shows rapid capacity fading, delivering only 127.8 mAh g⁻¹ after returning to 0.1 C.

The high-temperature operation further enhances low-pressure performance25,26. At 55 °C, Li2Si | |FeS2 delivers 868.4 mAh g−1 at 0.05 C and cycles stably for over 50 cycles at 0.1 C, with capacity increasing to 807 mAh g−1 at the 50th cycle (Fig. 5d, e). This capacity increase is attributed to LPSC reduction to Li2S by VGCF and Fe particles, enhancing the Li2S/S conversion reaction27. To demonstrate the practicality, an all-solid-state Li2Si | |FeS2 pouch cell was fabricated and tested under 15 MPa. The pouch cell successfully powers an LED and delivers 615.8 mAh g−1 at 0.05 C (Figure S10). At 1 C, the pouch cell achieves 442.3 mAh g−1 in the second cycle, corresponding to 619.2 Wh/kg (based on active material, Table S6), and maintains over 500 cycles (Fig. 5F). This low-pressure performance is competitive compared to the previously reported FeS2-based ASSLSBs (Fig. 5G). To further substantiate the effectiveness of our design, we evaluated Li2Si | |FeS2 cells under a moderate pressure of 30 MPa at 25 °C (Figure S17). The cell delivers a capacity retention of 94.3% after 50 cycles. Moreover, the assembled pouch cell operated at 30 MPa also exhibits markedly improved stability, further confirming that the Li2Si | |FeS2 configuration is mechanically and electrochemically robust under accessible pressures.

Encouraged by the excellent low-pressure performance, we further evaluated the long-term cycling stability of Li2Si | |FeS2 ASSLSBs under commonly reported high pressures (e.g., 100 MPa). Although such elevated pressures are impractical for real-world applications, they enable an assessment of the intrinsic potential of FeS₂ when interfacial stress is properly managed. These results further highlight the effectiveness of the strain-coordination strategy in achieving high-performance, long-life, and cost-effective ASSLSBs at reduced pressure levels, offering promising pathways toward scalable and sustainable energy storage technologies. After activation, the Li2Si | |FeS2 cell delivers a capacity of 411 mAh g−1 after ~4500 cycles at 1 C, corresponding to 85% capacity retention (Fig. 6a). A comparative evaluation (Fig. 6d) reveals that the system delivers a significantly prolonged cycle life, despite using commercially available micro-sized FeS2 and cost-effective Li2Si alloys. (The phase and morphology were characterized in Figures S11–S14, which are consistent with our previous reports28,29.) At high current densities, the Li2Si | |FeS2 cell maintains a capacity of 339.5 mAh g−1 ( ~ 0.54 mAh cm⁻²) after 30,000 cycles at 4 C (5.3 mA cm⁻²) and 243.6 mAh g−1 ( ~ 0.39 mAh cm⁻²) after 140,000 cycles at 15 C (19.7 mA cm⁻²) (Fig. 6a and Figure S15), demonstrating its remarkable durability for long-life applications. The rate capability test shows stable discharge capacities from 674.2 mAh g−1 at 0.1 C to 73.8 mAh g−1 at 40 C (52.58 mA/cm2), showing competitive performance compared with previously reported FeS2-based ASSLSBs (Fig. 6b, d). This superior high-rate performance is attributed to the self-compensating electrode strain, which effectively mitigates interfacial mechano-chemical failures under high current densities.

To assess commercial viability, we further tested a high-loading Li2Si | |FeS2 cell with a mass loading of 60.96 mg cm⁻2 and 50 wt% FeS2 content. The cell achieves an high areal capacity of 21.7 mAh cm⁻2 at 0.1 C over 100 cycles (Fig. 6c). This performance is competitive compared to the previously reported FeS2-based batteries, including those using solid-state or liquid electrolytes (Fig. 6f, Table S4). We further increased the FeS2 content to 55 and 60 wt% (Figure S18). Reducing the SSE fraction leads to lower capacity at 25 °C, likely due to limited ionic transport in the FeS2 composite electrode. This electrochemical performance loss can be partially mitigated at elevated temperature (Figure S19). Furthermore, a qualitative cost (intrinsic material cost) estimation highlights the economic advantages of Li2Si | |FeS2 batteries, particularly for grid-scale energy storage applications (Fig. 6e). These findings establish new performance benchmarks in the field of ASSLSBs, paving the way for their practical deployment in next-generation energy storage systems.

To further understand the long-term cycling stability of Li2Si | |FeS2 ASSLSBs under low pressure, aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-STEM) was employed. The high-resolution bright field image of FeS2 composite positive electrodes at the initial state reveals well-defined lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.31 nm, corresponding to the (111) crystal planes of pyrite FeS2 (Fig. 7a, b), indicating high crystallinity30. After 100 cycles, the dark field STEM image of discharged FeS2 positive electrode shows the formation of Fe0 and Li2S (Fig. 7c, Figure S16), which aligns with the expected reaction process (Fig. 7e)31. Atomic-resolution STEM imaging and elemental mapping further confirms the presence of pure phase Fe0 nanoparticles, showing a strong matched Fe signal and mismatched S signal (Fig. 7d). These Fe0 nanoparticles are uniformly embedded within the Li2S and LPSC matrix, facilitating a highly reversible and efficient redox process despite the insulating nature of Li₂S, thus contributing to the excellent cycling stability of the FeS2 positive electrode. Additionally, interfacial stability between the prelithiated Si negative electrode and LPSC was evaluated through reaction energy calculations (Fig. 7f), showing significantly improved interfacial stability compared to the Li metal-LPSC interface. Taken together, the high reversibility and stability of both the FeS2 positive electrode and Li2Si negative electrode contribute to the superior electrochemical performance and long-term stability of Li2Si | |FeS2 ASSLSBs under low pressure.

In this study, we present a novel strain-coordination strategy that significantly improves the performance and stability of ASSLSBs, particularly under low operational pressures. By pairing a high-capacity FeS₂ positive electrode with a prelithiated Si negative electrode, we exploit their inverse expansion-contraction mechanochemical behavior to achieve a quasi-zero strain effect during cycling. This strategy effectively mitigates internal mechanical stress, preserves electrode-electrolyte interfacial contact, and promotes stable electrochemical behavior, even under demanding operation conditions. The Li2Si | |FeS2 cell demonstrates exceptional performance, including a high initial discharge capacity, remarkable cycle life of 4,500 cycles at 1 C and 140,000 cycles at 15 C, and outstanding rate capability, maintaining 73.8 mAh g−1 at 40 C. Moreover, the high-loading Li2Si | |FeS2 cells exhibit an high areal capacity of 21.7 mAh cm⁻2 over 100 cycles. These findings underscore the effectiveness of the strain-coordination strategy in achieving high-performance, long-life, and cost-effective ASSLSBs with reduced pressure, to provide new energy storage solutions for large-scale and sustainable applications.

Methods

Preparation of the FeS2 electrode

The FeS2-based electrode consists of commercial micro-sized FeS2 (D50 = 14–18 μm, Macklin, 99.5%), Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 solid electrolytes (>6 mS/cm, GLABAT), and vapor-grown carbon fibers (VGCF, GLABAT) in a 20:40:4 or 50:42:8 (wt%) ratio. These materials were placed in a zirconia jar containing zirconia balls and milled at 300 rpm for 2 h using a planetary ball milling apparatus. Except for ball milling, all sample preparation procedures were carried out in an Ar-filled glovebox (O₂ <0.1 ppm, H₂O < 0.1 ppm).

Preparation of the LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 and LiCoO2 electrode

The composite positive electrode consisted of LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (GLABAT) and LiCoO2 powder (GLABAT), Li3InCl6 solid electrolytes (>1.5 mS/cm, D50 = 1-3 μm, GLABAT), and VGCF, mixed in a 70:26:4 (wt%) ratio for 30 min using a mortar and pestle. All the process are conducted in an Ar-filled glovebox (O2 < 0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm).

Preparation of the Li4Ti5O12 electrode

The Li4Ti5O12 electrode consists of commercial micro-sized Li4Ti5O12 (MTI HeFei), Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 solid electrolytes, and VGCF in a 50:42:8 (wt%) ratio. These components were placed in a zirconia jar filled with zirconia balls and milled at 400 rpm for 2 h using a planetary ball milling apparatus. Except for ball milling, all sample preparation procedures were carried out in an Ar-filled glovebox (O₂ <0.1 ppm, H₂O < 0.1 ppm).

Preparation of the Li7Ti5O12 electrode

The prelithiated Li4Ti5O12-based positive electrode (Li7Ti5O12) was prepared by disassembling the discharged Li4Ti5O12 | LPSC|Li-In battery, and the Li7Ti5O12 was then manually scraped off. All the process are conducted in an Ar-filled glovebox (O2 < 0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm).

Preparation of the LixSi electrode

The Si negative electrode was prepared by repeatedly rolling a mixture of Si (Macklin, 99.9%) and PTFE (GLABAT) in a 97:3 weight ratio to create a uniform film (thickness: ~30 μm) with a loading of approximately 3 mg cm⁻². The film was then punched into discs with a diameter of 10 mm. Fresh Li foil (purity: >99.9%, supplied by China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd.) was rolled and punched into discs with approximate thicknesses of 22, 45, and 65 μm (8 mm diameter), corresponding to masses of 0.6, 1.2, and 1.8 mg, respectively. The LiₓSi negative electrodes were in situ formed by brief contact between the Si films and Li foils during the cell assembly process. All the process are conducted in an Ar-filled glovebox (O2 < 0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm).

Preparation of the Li-In electrode

An In foil disk (diameter: 10 mm, thickness: 30 μm, purity: 99.999%, Wanda Scientific Research Metal Materials Co., Ltd.) and a Li foil disk (diameter: 8 mm, thickness: ~20 μm, purity: >99.9%, China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd.) were stacked and mechanically pressed together to form the Li–In alloy negative electrode during the cell assembly process. All the process are conducted in an Ar-filled glovebox (O2 < 0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm).

Cell fabrication

For the electrochemical tests, pellet cells were assembled using homemade mold cells consisting of stainless-steel clamping fixtures, a polyetheretherketone (PEEK) outer sleeve, and an alumina ceramic inner liner with a diameter of 10 mm. All assembly procedures were carried out in an Ar-filled glovebox (O₂ <0.1 ppm, H₂O < 0.1 ppm). First, ~80 mg of sulfide solid electrolyte powder was loaded into the mold cell and pressed at 125 MPa (1 ton) for 1 min to form a dense electrolyte pellet. Subsequently, the corresponding positive composite electrode material was uniformly spread onto one side of the electrolyte pellet and pressed at 125 MPa for 1 min. A Si film was then placed on the opposite side of the electrolyte pellet, followed by Li foil on top of the Si film. Finally, the multilayer pellet cell was formed by pressing at 500 MPa (4 tons) for 1 min. The LiₓSi|LPSC|FeS₂ cells were assembled using the procedure described above. For Li|LPSC|FeS₂ cells, the LiₓSi alloy layer were replaced by fresh Li foil and the final pressing pressure is 15 MPa. For Li₇Ti₅O₁₂ | LPSC|FeS₂, Li4Ti₅O₁₂ | LPSC | NCM, and Li4Ti₅O₁₂ | LPSC | LCO cells, the corresponding composite positive electrodes were pressed onto one side of the solid electrolyte pellet, followed by the negative electrode on the opposite side. For Li|LPSC|Li₄Ti₅O₁₂, Li-In|LPSC|Li₄Ti₅O₁₂ and LiₓSi|LPSC|Li₄Ti₅O₁₂ cells, the Li₄Ti₅O₁₂ composite electrode was used as the positive electrode, while Li foil, Li-In or LiₓSi served as the negative electrode. The N/P ratio of the full cell is ~1.5. Unless otherwise specified, all pellet cells were assembled using the same mold-cell configuration described above. All fabrication procedures were conducted in an Ar-filled glovebox (O₂ <0.1 ppm, H₂O < 0.1 ppm).

The pouch cell was fabricated entirely via a solvent-free process. The FeS2 positive electrode composite was prepared by mixing FeS2, LPSC, VGCF and PTFE in a weight ratio of 50:42:8:0.5 using a Hummer Acoustic Mixer (HAM100). The resulting mixture underwent repeated fiberization and roll pressing to form a freestanding film, which was then transferred onto carbon-coated aluminum foil. The negative electrode was assembled by placing a lithium foil directly onto a pre-formed silicon film, as described above. The solid electrolyte membrane was also prepared via a solvent-free protocol described earlier in the manuscript. Electrodes and electrolyte membranes were cut to size using a pneumatic punching machine. The dimensions of the negative electrode, solid electrolyte, and positive electrode sheets were 50 mm × 60 mm, 50 mm × 60 mm, and 45 mm × 56 mm, respectively. Tab welding to the current collectors was performed using ultrasonic welding. The cell was assembled in a stacked configuration and enclosed in a laminate pouch. After vacuum sealing, the assembled cell was subjected to a pressure of 300 MPa for 90 s using a BTKF high-pressure press (Bao Tou KeFa High Pressure Technology Co., Ltd). The final pouch cell had an area of 50 mm × 60 mm and a positive electrode mass of approximately 112 mg (FeS₂: 56 mg, LPSC: 47.04 mg, VGCF: 8.96 mg). The LPSC separator layer weighed about 253 mg (thickness ~90 μm), and the Li₂Si alloy negative electrode weighed approximately 60 mg. All fabrication steps were conducted in an argon-filled glovebox (H₂O < 0.1 ppm, O₂ <0.1 ppm) to prevent moisture and oxygen exposure.

At least three independent pellet cells were tested for the electrochemical performance measurement. The representative data shown in the manuscript were selected from cells exhibiting consistent electrochemical behavior. For mechanistic characterizations, operando/in situ measurements, and pouch-cell tests, one or two representative cells were assembled due to the complexity and time-consuming nature of the experiments.

Electrochemical test

Galvanostatic charge–discharge measurements were performed using a battery test system (LAND, CT2001A). Unless otherwise specified, electrochemical tests were conducted at 25 ± 1 °C under constant-current (CC) mode. For the high-loading Li₂Si|LPSC|FeS₂ cells, constant-current–constant-voltage (CCCV) charging was applied. The voltage ranges of the cells were set as follows:

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were conducted using a BioLogic VSP-300 electrochemical station, spanning a frequency range of 7 MHz to 20 mHz (10 data points for per decade of frequency), with an alternating current (AC) amplitude of 10 mV. Galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) measurement was performed at a rate of 0.1 C ( ~ 0.13 mA/cm2) for 10 min, followed by a 2-h relaxation period, with one data point recorded every 10 s.

In situ optical microscope characterization

The in-situ optical microscope experiment was carried out within an argon-filled glovebox with YUESCOPE YD650. The light source used did not cause any visible damage to the samples. Initially, a Li2Si | |FeS2 cell was assembled in a model cell with a fabrication pressure of 250 MPa, with the thicker positive electrode (~20 mg cm−2) and negative electrode (~12 mg cm−2) to enhance observability. After demolding the pellet, the pellet was transferred to cell holder, enabling a cross-sectional view of the interfaces. Galvanostatic charge-discharge cycling of the cell was performed, and interface images were captured digitally in real-time. All the above procedures were conducted in an Ar-filled glovebox (O₂ <0.1 ppm, H₂O < 0.1 ppm).

Stress measurements and thickness measurements test

Stress measurements during charge/discharge were performed using a custom-built pressure-measuring device integrated with a force sensor. The assembled cell was tested at 25 °C under an operational pressure of 15 MPa. All stress changes during the charge and discharge cycles were monitored using proprietary software. (CHUANGLI Ningbo) Thickness measurements were conducted with SPFT2000 IESTTECH, and all stress changes were also tracked using the proprietary software.

X-ray computed tomography (XCT) measurements

Micro X-ray computed tomography (XCT) was conducted using an Carl Zeiss Xradia 610 & 620 Versa system. The cycled cells were disassembled in an Ar-filled glovebox and encapsulated in plastic film to prevent exposure of the cell to air. The cells were scanned in the central region (2 × 2 mm) to enhance resolution (~4 µm).

Phase and structure characterization

The phase composition of FeS2, μm-sized Si and Li-Si alloy with a different mole ratio were determined by powder X-ray Diffraction (XRD; Rigaku). The microstructure of the samples was investigated by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM; Hitachi). The STEM images and mapping were collected using a CEOS probe corrected FEI Themis TEM (electron accelerating voltage is 300 kV). The interfacial reaction energies between the negative electrodes and LPSC were evaluated using the Materials Project database and the Interface Reactions Calculator tools.
