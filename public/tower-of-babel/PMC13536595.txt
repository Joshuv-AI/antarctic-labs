Selective detection of clinically relevant Gram-negative bacteria using a colistin-functionalized ISFET biosensor
Authors: Seyed Saman Nemati, Melika Moniriraad, Mehri Haeili, Gholamreza Dehghan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRapid and selective detection of Gram-negative bacteria is important for clinical diagnosis and for monitoring environmental and food-related samples. In this study, we developed a label-free ISFET sensor based on a p-type silicon/SiO2 platform modified with ZnO and colistin for the detection of Gram-negative bacteria. The sensor was evaluated against several Gram-negative species, including Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, Salmonella enterica serovar Enteritidis, Klebsiella oxytoca, and Klebsiella pneumoniae, with the Gram-positive species Staphylococcus aureus and Enterococcus faecium used as negative controls. The device showed measurable changes in drain–source current upon bacterial binding, and the standard curves indicated a low detection limit and a linear response within the tested concentration range. The calculated limits of detection were approximately 137 CFU/mL for E. coli, 169 CFU/mL for A. baumannii, 188 CFU/mL for S. enterica, 218 CFU/mL for K. oxytoca, 235 CFU/mL for K. pneumoniae, and 289 CFU/mL for P. aeruginosa. The sensor stability was also assessed over 14 days in the E. coli assay. In addition, real-sample experiments in commercial bottled drinking water and human urine spiked with E. coli showed good agreement with PBS measurements, with a recovery of 101.13% ± 3.08% and an RSD of 3.05% in bottled water. These results indicate that the proposed platform has potential for selective detection of Gram-negative bacteria under controlled laboratory conditions and in tested real matrices.Keywords: Gram-negative bacteria, Biosensor, ISFET, Colistin, Label-free detection

Introduction

Gram-negative bacteria are important microorganisms in the transmission of infectious diseases in humans and animals, and they exhibit inherent resistance. They can cause inflammation due to their cell wall structure, which includes an outer membrane rich in lipopolysaccharide (LPS). This compound stimulates part of the innate immune system and can lead to fever, septic shock, and severe inflammation [1]. The presence of species such as Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Salmonella enterica, and Klebsiella pneumoniae, as well-known Gram-negative pathogens, is widespread and contributes to the spread of hospital infections and antibiotic resistance. These bacteria often exhibit high pathogenicity due to their ability to form biofilms, transfer resistance genes horizontally, and adapt metabolically to diverse environments [2, 3]. In contrast, although Gram-positive bacteria can also be pathogenic, their antibiotic susceptibility varies widely. Therefore, identifying the specific type of bacterium using differential methods is essential to ensure appropriate treatment and to prevent improper antibiotic use and the development or spread of resistance [4, 5].

Given the high growth rate of bacteria, rapid, accurate, and inexpensive detection of these pathogenic bacteria is of great clinical and epidemiological importance [6]. Early identification of the causative agent of bacterial infection facilitates the selection of appropriate antibiotics and prevents the spread of resistant strains [7]. Traditional methods include microbial cultivation in different media such as MacConkey agar or LB [8, 9], biochemical tests (such as IMViC and oxidase) [10, 11], and colony morphology examination [12], and in more advanced stages, molecular methods such as PCR and 16 S rRNA sequencing [13, 14]. Despite their high accuracy, these methods are time-consuming and require specialized equipment and skilled personnel. In recent years, new technologies based on biosensors and immunoelectronics (immunosensors) have been developed, offering fast, highly sensitive results [6, 15].

One of the biochemical characteristics that distinguishes Gram-negative bacteria is the presence of an outer membrane containing LPS, which is the main target of the antibiotic colistin. Colistin (Polymyxin E) has positively charged functional groups that bind to the negatively charged phosphate groups of LPS, thereby disrupting the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria. In contrast, Gram-positive bacteria lack LPS and are therefore inherently resistant to colistin [16, 17]. This difference in bacterial cell wall structure, which enables colistin to selectively interact with Gram-negative but not Gram-positive bacteria, can serve as a valuable diagnostic indicator for measuring the electrochemical or biological response of cells to colistin [18].

Among the new technologies, ISFET (Ion-Sensitive Field-Effect Transistor)-based sensors have gained a special place due to their high sensitivity, miniaturization capability, detection speed, and simple measurement [19]. ISFET-based sensors operate on semiconductors and convert the effect of interactions with the gate surface into an electrical signal. In previous reports, field-effect transistors (FETs) have been used to detect bacteria. Thakur et al. utilized a reduced graphene oxide (rGO) FET sensor for rapid, low-cost detection of E. coli in water samples [20]. The sensor detected E. coli within a linear range of 103-109 CFU/mL in deionized water. Even at low concentration, a measurable change in the graphene channel current was observed. However, this approach relies on polyclonal antibodies against the O (somatic) and K (capsular) antigens of E. coli, which are typically produced in animals such as rabbits or goats and are associated with high cost. A graphene-based FET (GFETs) employed Magainin I and vancomycin as bioreceptors to detect Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, respectively. The sensor showed strong selectivity toward Gram-negative bacteria and effectively distinguished them from Gram-positive bacteria [21]. However, Magainin I is considerably more expensive to synthesize or purify from its natural source compared to colistin, which can be produced at low cost. In addition, Magainin-based sensors can be unstable due to peptide degradation at room temperature and may partially respond to Gram-positive bacteria, whereas colistin is more stable and interacts specifically with the LPS of Gram-negative bacteria. Mechanistically, Magainin I primarily inhibits bacterial growth, which may allow residual bacteria to persist and potentially contaminate the sensor. In contrast, colistin actively lyses bacterial cells by disrupting the outer membrane, thereby reducing the risk of sensor contamination [22].

In this study, colistin was used as a bioreceptor on an ISFET featuring a p-type Si/SiO2/ZnO semiconductor structure. After verifying the correct synthesis of the device and its performance, it was used to detect Gram-negative bacteria: E. coli, P. aeruginosa, A. baumannii, S. enterica, K. oxytoca, and K. pneumoniae, in a 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution (pH 7.0). Also, the Gram-positive bacteria, S. aureus and E. faecium, were used as a negative control. The results showed that this method is much faster than classical culture-based methods and does not require staining or fluorescent labeling. In addition, high sensitivity, in-situ detection capability, and differential detection of Gram-negatives due to colistin’s specific response are other advantages of ISFET systems for detecting Gram-negative bacteria.

Experimental

Methods and materials

Tryptone, sodium chloride (NaCl), sodium azide (NaN3), zinc nitrate (Zn(NO3)2), yeast extract, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), disodium phosphate (Na2HPO4.12H2O), potassium chloride (KCl), hexamethylenetetramine (HMTA), potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KH2PO4) hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC), and acetone were purchased from Merck Company (Germany). 3-(triethoxysilyl) propylamine (C9H23NO3Si) (APTES) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Taufkirchen, Germany). Silver glue (MECHANIC MCN DJ200 (China)) and boron-doped silicon oxide wafer (p-type silicon oxide) (Nanobazar, Iran) were used to record the results. Bottled drinking water (ZamZam Azerbaijan Co., Iran) was used as a real sample matrix. The Ag/AgCl electrode was used as the gate electrode. Ethanol (C2H5OH) was obtained from Razi Alcohol Company (Iran). Colistin sulfate was obtained from Glentham Life Science (UK). E. coli ATCC 25922, A. baumannii ATCC BAA-747, S. enterica serovar Enteritidis [23], and S. aureus ATCC 25923, as well as clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa, K. oxytoca, K. pneumoniae, and E. faecium (identified using the BD Phoenix automated microbiology system [(Becton Dickinson Diagnostic Systems, Sparks, MD, USA]) were obtained from the Microbial Collection of the University of Tabriz.

X-ray diffraction (XRD, Tongda, TD-3700, China) and field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, MIRA3 FEG–SEM, Tescan, Czech Republic) were used to characterize ZnO and SiO2. A shaker incubator from Azma Sahand Azar (Iran) was used for bacterial growth. A Kendro centrifuge (Germany) and a PG Instruments Limited spectrophotometer (United Kingdom) were also used in the preparation of bacterial samples. ISFET fabrication was carried out using photolithography, and measurements were performed with an I–V meter (both from Nanopazhohan Raga Company, Iran).

ISFET fabrication

The basic steps of ISFET fabrication were based on protocols from previous studies, with minor modifications [24, 25]. Briefly, the p-type silicon wafer was cleaned to reduce surface contamination. The cleaning operation was performed with RCA 1 solution (5:1:1 mixture of distilled water, hydrogen peroxide, and ammonia) at 80 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, the wafer surface was washed with distilled water and dried in a clean, dry environment. Then, thermal oxidation was performed at 850 °C for 90 min in a pure oxygen flow to form a uniform SiO2 layer. After the wafer was cooled in an oven, a positive photoresist layer of ShipleyS1813 was spin-coated on the surface, and the source and drain contact areas on the Si/SiO₂ structure were defined by photolithography. Next, n-type doping was performed using Filmtronics P509 solution (containing 10 wt% phosphorus) at 3000 rpm for 40 s. After coating, the wafer was first incubated at 200 °C for 15 min, then at 800 °C under nitrogen for 30 min to complete phosphorus diffusion and form n-type regions on the p-substrate. Finally, the process was performed by etching with an oxide buffer (NH4F: HF solution at a 1:6 ratio). ZnO was grown on the Si/SiO₂ surface as a charge-transfer layer. The wafer was incubated in a solution containing Zn(NO3)2 (7 mM) and HMTA (7 mM) at a 1:1 ratio at 90 °C in the dark for 12 h. After the reaction, the surface was washed, dried, and annealed at 370 °C for 20 min to improve the adhesion and crystallinity of ZnO. Then, the source and drain connections were made with conductive silver adhesive. Then, a polycarbonate (plexiglass) chamber, serving as an electrolyte cell, was mounted on the gate surface with a transparent, impermeable adhesive to provide a liquid measurement environment (Fig. 1a).

Schematic illustration of the sensor fabrication process a, and stepwise modification of the ZnO sensing surface with APTES linker, immobilization of colistin and bovine serum albumin (BSA) b

The ZnO surface was prepared as a gate region for the detection of Gram-negative bacteria. To functionalize the ZnO gate surface for colistin immobilization, a 2% (v/v) solution of APTES in anhydrous toluene was prepared and dropwise applied to the gate, followed by incubation for 30 min under ambient light. The excess solution was removed, and the wafer surface was washed first with toluene and then with ethanol to remove unbound APTES. Then, it was heated to 90 °C for 60 min to complete silane crosslinking. The PBS solution containing colistin at 200 µg/mL was incubated with 0.2 M EDC and 0.05 M NHS at room temperature for 30 min to form active ester groups (Colistin–CO–NHS). Then, 20 µL of it was dropwise applied to the ZnO surface in the gate region. After addition, the wafer was kept at room temperature for 120 min under clean conditions to stabilize colistin molecules on the APTES-functionalized ZnO surface via covalent interactions (carboxyl groups in colistin with amine groups in APTES). Finally, to block any remaining active sites on the functionalized surface, 1% (w/v) bovine serum albumin in PBS was added to the gate surface, and the surface was washed with PBS after 30 min to remove unbound BSA (Fig. 1b).

Bacterial sample preparation

In this study, eight bacterial species, including S. aureus, and E. faecium (Gram-positive; negative control), E. coli, P. aeruginosa, A. baumannii, S. enterica, K. oxytoca, and K. pneumoniae (Gram-negative) were examined. For each strain, a single colony was selected and grown overnight in Luria–Bertani medium (LB) at pH 7.0 and 37 °C under shaking conditions. After growth, 2 mL of each culture was transferred to sterile microtubes and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min to pellet the bacterial cells and remove the culture medium. The supernatant was then discarded, and the bacterial pellet was washed with 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7). Centrifugation and washing were repeated once more to completely remove the residual culture medium. From this step, a 0.1 M PBS solution containing the “master stock” of each strain was prepared and used for subsequent dilution steps.

The turbidity of the prepared bacterial suspension was visually adjusted to match the 0.5 McFarland standard, and the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was subsequently measured to be 0.08–0.09 (≈ 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL). Subsequently, a series of (approximately) 1:3 serial dilutions was prepared, to obtain 15 different bacterial concentrations (the final values of the concentrations are shown in Table 1). Assay method

During the measurement process with the p‑Si/SiO2/ZnO@Colistin biosensor, first, at a gate voltage above threshold, the changes in source-drain current as a function of source-drain voltage were investigated. At each step, experiments were carried out in triplicate, with all tests performed at room temperature in a dark environment. First, PBS pH 7.0 (0.1 M) was used as the base electrolyte, and the changes in IDS versus VDS were recorded (in all tests, the VDS step length was 50 mV). Then, different dilutions of E. coli, P. aeruginosa, A. baumannii, S. enterica, K. oxytoca, K. pneumoniae, S. aureus, and E. faecium, prepared by diluting the original bacterial stock suspension in PBS, were used to measure changes in sensor response. The sample volume required for each measurement was 100 µL, and the bacteria were incubated for 5 min to stabilize the interaction between the bacteria and the antibiotic. Time was given inside the gate chamber, and then the measurement was performed. Since the interaction between colistin and cell surface LPS is electrostatic and hydrophobic, rather than covalent, a recovery buffer with very low pH or a detergent solution was not required; the gate activity level was recovered using 0.1 M PBS. To preserve the sensor and prevent possible bacterial contamination, it was stored at 4 °C with 0.1 M PBS in the presence of sodium azide (0.05% w/v).

For evaluating the sensor performance in real matrices, a single concentration within the linear detection range was spiked, in triplicate, into commercial bottled drinking water and human urine samples (obtained from healthy volunteers after informed consent) separately. For urine samples, the urine was first centrifuged, and the supernatant was sterilized using a 0.22 μm pore-size membrane filter. The bottled drinking water was autoclaved before use. Both matrices were tested without any modifications to their composition, and only bacterial spiking was performed before measurement. For each matrix, the sensor response was defined as the difference between the current recorded in the presence of E. coli and the baseline current in the absence of bacteria, and this response was compared with the corresponding signal obtained in PBS under identical conditions. Based on these measurements, the recovery percentage and the capability of the biosensor for accurate detection of E. coli in complex real samples were determined. In addition, S. aureus was used as a negative control in both bottled water and urine to verify that no significant signal was generated in the presence of this Gram‑positive bacterium, further confirming the selectivity of the colistin‑functionalized ISFET sensor toward Gram‑negative bacteria.

The IDS vs. VDS curve was remeasured under the same conditions (constant VG), and the change in current relative to baseline was recorded as the sensor signal. These steps were repeated for all concentrations to obtain quantitative data. The lowest concentration of bacteria (CFU/mL) that elicited a sensor response in the actual assay was recorded, and a standard curve was generated using standard calculations to determine the limit of detection (LOD) and sensitivity. The IDS curve was plotted against bacterial concentration (CFU/mL), and the data were linearly fitted in the linear region; the slope of the line (S) was defined as the sensor sensitivity. In the measured I–V curves, the recorded IDS values represent the absolute drain current, and the voltage sweep was performed over a VDS range of 0 to 1.4 V. Because the current changes corresponding to variations in CFU/mL were relatively small, the µA‑scale currents obtained from the I–V curves were converted to nA values (by multiplying by 1000) for use in the calibration plots. For calibration, the IDS value at VDS=1.4 V was selected, as this region corresponds to the plateau of the I–V characteristics and provides a stable and reproducible measurement point.

The standard deviation used in the LOD calculation was obtained from five independent blank measurements and is reported in the manuscript as the blank SD. This blank SD was applied in the LOD calculation. However, for real‑sample measurements, the SD represents the standard deviation of replicate measurements.

Results and discussion

Nanostructure characterization

To examine the surface morphology and confirm the formation of the ZnO layer, SEM images were obtained (Fig. 3a and b). The images revealed a well-organized array of nanorods forming a flower-like architecture on the SiO2 surface, characterized by uniform growth and clear separations at a magnification of 1.5 μm. Cross-sectional SEM analysis further confirmed that the ZnO layer had an average thickness of approximately 10 μm on the wafer surface. This relative thickness is consistent with previous studies that reported an optimal range exceeding 1.5 μm for ZnO-ISFETs [25]. Contrary to the initial assumption that a large thickness might impede gate field transfer, this effect is absent in the present nanorod structure, as the electric field penetrates only the surface region (less than 100 nm). However, the presence of porosity and conductive paths between the nanorods allows potential transfer. As a result, the 10 μm thickness does not impair the transistor performance. On the other hand, it increases the surface-to-volume ratio, improves mechanical stability, increases the number of active sites for ionic interactions, and better transmission of surface signals, which will increase the sensitivity and repeatability of the ISFET sensor, which is an important performance advantage for this structure [26, 27].

FESEM images of the ZnO nanorods a and cross of the ZnO layer on p-type Si/SiO2 wafer b. X-ray diffraction of p-type Si/SiO2 and on p-type Si/SiO2/ZnO c

The XRD technique was used to confirm the fabrication process and formation of SiO2 and ZnO layers (Fig. 3c). The XRD pattern (range of 10° to 80°) of the SiO2-coated p-Si sample before adding ZnO showed a broad and gentle ridge between about ~ 20° and ~ 33° with a peak at 24.72° in the 2θ which is due to the amorphous nature of the SiO2 layer and confirms the complete and uniform structure of a stable dielectric layer, SiO2, on the surface of the p-type silicon wafer. After depositing ZnO on the same substrate, in addition to maintaining the amorphous ridge, several sharp peaks were observed, indicative of the hexagonal wurtzite structure of ZnO. Peaks appeared at 31.54° (100), 35.94° (101), 47.36° (102)/(112), 56.32° (110), 62.63° (103)/(201) and 68.84° (112)/(203) and a sharper peak at 34.20° (002) indicated the dominant growth along the c-axis. The high intensity of the (002) peak and the complete presence of the ZnO-specific peak set confirm the synthesis of high-quality, desirable morphology of the ZnO layer, and the underlying SiO2 substrate also maintained its stable performance; thus, the XRD results confirm the successful fabrication process [28, 29].

ISFET characterization

To verify the ISFET’s proper operation before applying the biolayer, the device’s electrical response was tested under standard physical conditions. It was measured over the voltage range VDS = 0–1.5 V and at a fixed VG = + 2 V. The experiments were performed with two reference environments, including deionized (DI) water and PBS 0.1 M. VDS was increased in steps of 50 mV50 mV (Fig. 4a). In the entire voltage range, no leakage current or nonlinear behavior in the subthreshold region was observed, indicating the proper fabrication quality, the correct bonding between the ZnO layers and the Si/SiO₂ substrate, and the stable operation of the gate circuit. On the other hand, the IDS value in the PBS environment was significantly larger than that in DI. This difference in IDS is due to the presence of more ions in PBS than in DI, leading to a stronger electrical double layer at the electrolyte-ZnO interface and increased modulation of channel conductance. The increase in IDS in the presence of an ionic solution compared to the ion-free environment indicates the inherent sensitivity of the ZnO structure to changes in surface charge and ionic species concentration. Therefore, the fabricated device is structurally well synthesized, and its field-sensing mechanism also works effectively.

Sensor performance and characterization. a Efficiency of the sensor in discrimination between deionized (DI) water and PBS as electrolyte. b IDS-VG in constant VDS (0.1 V). c, d I–V curves to verify proper immobilization of colistin: comparison of E. coli assay in the absence c and presence d of APTES as the immobilization linker

To determine the threshold voltage (Vth) of the ISFET, IDS vs. VGS measurements were performed under a constant VDS=0.1 V and a VG range of − 5 to + 5 V (Fig. 4b). In the negative voltage region up to about VG ≈ + 1.5 V, the current flowing through the channel is almost constant and negligible, indicating that the conduction path is closed below Vth. As the gate voltage (VG) increased beyond approximately + 1.5 V, the drain current (IDS) rose exponentially, indicating the onset of the conduction region and, consequently, the Vth of the transistor. Accordingly, the value of Vth was determined to be about + 1.5 V. Since in FETs, VG must be chosen larger than Vth to effectively activate the channel and ensure a field-sensitive response, a constant VG = + 2 V was used in all subsequent measurements to ensure stable operation and proper repeatability of the device under operating conditions.

To evaluate the effect of surface functionalization on the sensitivity of the ZnO-based field-effect sensor, comparative experiments were performed in the absence and presence of the APTES linker layer. To examine the role of APTES-mediated immobilization on sensor stability and reproducibility, three concentrations of E. coli (0 CFU/mL, 282 CFU/mL, and 2.54 × 103 CFU/mL) were prepared in 0.1 M PBS. IDS–VDS measurements were carried out at a constant VG = + 2 V to assess the electrical response of the device under different bacterial concentrations in two configurations: one without APTES, where colistin was physically adsorbed onto the gate surface (Fig. 4c), and one with APTES serving as the immobilization linker for colistin (Fig. 4d).

In the absence of APTES, colistin attachment to the ZnO surface was unstable, and successive washing steps led to its partial removal, resulting in a negligible sensor response to bacterial presence. This behavior indicates weak physical adsorption and unsuccessful functionalization of the surface. In contrast, when APTES was employed as a linker, IDS increased progressively with increasing bacterial concentration, confirming stable immobilization of colistin and reproducible sensor performance. These results verify the successful functionalization of colistin on the ZnO surface via APTES-mediated immobilization. The formation of a stable and bioactive sensing layer enabled reliable detection of charge variations induced by E. coli, demonstrating that the ion-sensing mechanism of the ISFET structure operates effectively upon proper surface modification.

Bacterial assay

For each concentration of E. coli, P. aeruginosa, A. baumannii, S. enterica, K. oxytoca, K. pneumoniae, S. aureus, and E. faecium, IDS vs. VDS curves were recorded. The changes in drain current with increasing cell density were analyzed to elucidate the relationship between the transistor’s electrical response and bacterial concentration.

To measure the response of the designed sensor to changes in E. coli concentration, experiments were performed over a wide range of cell densities from 0 to 1.5 × 108 CFU\mL, and IDS vs. VDS curves were recorded (Fig. 5a). The standard curve derived from the I − V graph shows an almost linear relationship between cell density and the increase in output current (R2 = 0.974) at increasing bacterial concentrations up to about 5 × 107 CFU/mL, indicating a direct and reliable response of the device to the presence of bacterial species (Fig. 5b). Also, the LOD calculated using the signal-to-noise ratio formula was approximately 137 CFU/mL. The sensitivity was calculated to be 0.0390 nA/(CFU/mL), and the standard deviation (SD) was 1.783, indicating high repeatability and low measurement error in the sensor’s response to E. coli concentration.

I – V curves and corresponding standard curves for bacterial assay: E. coli a, b, A. baumannii c, d, S. enterica e, f, P. aeruginosa g, h, K. oxytoca i, j, and K. pneumoniae k, l. The numerical data corresponding to the calibration plots for all tested bacteria are provided in Table S1

To quantify the A. baumannii concentration, experiments were performed with 15 different concentrations of bacterial cells ranging from 0 to 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL. The recorded IDS vs. VDS curves showed that, with increasing bacterial concentration, the IDS increased continuously, indicating an increase in channel conductance due to the presence of bacterial cells in the detection medium (Fig. 5c). In the quantitative analysis, the standard curve derived from the linear region (0 to 5 × 107 CFU/mL) demonstrated a strong linear correlation between drain current and bacterial concentration (R2 = 0.987) (Fig. 5d). The slope of 0.0357 nA/CFU/mL indicates the sensor’s high sensitivity to changes in cell density. Despite this high sensitivity, the sensor’s practical LOD was calculated using standard statistical relations, with a measurement SD of 2.017, corresponding to approximately 169 CFU/mL. This LOD value shows that the sensor has an acceptable efficiency for detecting low concentrations of bacteria.

The concentration of S. enterica was evaluated using fifteen different bacterial suspensions. I–V measurements demonstrated that the sensor effectively detected this bacterium. The standard plot derived from the I–V data indicated a linear relationship between the sensor response and bacterial concentration over the range of 0 to 5 × 107 CFU/mL, with R2 = 0.963, indicating a strong correlation between the electrical signal and cell density (Fig. 5e and f). Also, the sensitivity of the sensor was estimated to be 0.0343 nA/(CFU/mL), and the LOD was estimated to be 188 CFU/mL with a SD of 2.153. These results indicate high accuracy and reliable responsiveness of the sensor against S. enterica.

To quantify P. aeruginosa concentration, measurements were performed over a range of 0 to 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL. The results showed that IDS increased with increasing bacterial concentration (Fig. 5g). In the linear range of the sensor response (0 to 5 × 107 CFU/mL, a direct and reliable relationship was observed between the drain current and bacterial concentration (R2 = 0.938) (Fig. 5h). The slope of the curve was 0.023 nA/(CFU/mL), indicating the sensor’s sensitivity to changes in cell concentration. The LOD was calculated based on the SD of 2.217, yielding an estimated value of approximately 289 CFU/mL. Comparing the sensor’s sensitivity and LOD for P. aeruginosa with values obtained for other Gram-negative strains, revealed differences that are consistent with the characteristic outer-membrane properties of this bacterium. P. aeruginosa can reduce the net negative charge of its LPS by modifying lipid A with 4-amino-4-deoxy-L-arabinose, a process primarily regulated by the PmrA/PmrB two-component system. This LPS modifications weakens electrostatic interactions between colistin and the bacterial surface, resulting in reduced colistin binding and therefore, smaller gate potential change [30, 31]. As a result, the response of the colistin-based ISFET sensor against P. aeruginosa is less intense and more limited in sensitivity, with the current-concentration curve showing a gentler slope than other Gram-negative strains.

To evaluate the sensor’s performance in detecting K. oxytoca, IDS–VDS measurements were recorded over a range of bacterial concentrations from 0 to 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL. As shown in the I–V curves (Fig. 5i), the drain current increased progressively with increasing cell density, confirming that the presence of K. oxytoca enhances channel conductance in a concentration‑dependent manner. Quantitative analysis of the standard plot in the linear detection range (0 to 5 × 107 CFU/mL) demonstrated a strong correlation between the electrical response and cell concentration, with an R2 value of 0.982, indicating excellent linearity of the sensing behavior (Fig. 5j). The SD of repeated measurements was 2.724 µA, corresponding to an RSD of 4.404%, reflecting good reproducibility of the recorded signals. Based on the signal‑to‑noise relationship and calibration slope, the LOD was estimated to be approximately 218 CFU/mL, which confirms the sensor’s capability for detecting low bacterial concentrations. Taken together, these results show that the developed sensing platform provides a reliable, sensitive, and statistically robust response toward K. oxytoca across the tested concentration range.

To evaluate the detection capability of the sensor toward K. pneumoniae, IDS–VDS measurements were carried out over a bacterial concentration range from 0 to 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL. The recorded I–V characteristics showed that the drain current increased gradually with increasing bacterial concentration, indicating that the presence of K. pneumoniae leads to an increase in the channel conductance of the device. Analysis of the calibration curve in the linear detection range (0 to 5 × 107 CFU/mL) revealed a strong correlation between the electrical response of the sensor and the bacterial concentration, with an R2 value of 0.976, demonstrating reliable and consistent sensing behavior (Fig. 5k and l). The SD obtained from repeated measurements was 2.781 µA, corresponding to an RSD of 4.586%, which indicates acceptable repeatability of the sensor response. Based on the signal‑to‑noise ratio and the slope of the calibration curve, the LOD was estimated to be approximately 235 CFU/mL. These results confirm that the developed sensing platform is capable of detecting K. pneumoniae with good linearity, reproducibility, and sensitivity within the investigated concentration range.

It should be noted that all LODs reported in this study were determined using the conventional LOD calculation formula based on the slope of the standard curve and SD, which is different from the methods used in most previous studies that only reported the number of bacteria added as the LOD [20, 21, 32]. Using the conventional LOD formula, baseline fluctuations and noise during the reported assays are statistically accounted for, and the response will be reported to a very high level of confidence. Precision and repeatability are confirmed, and false positive responses are eliminated [33, 34]. However, in vitro, detectable changes in the output signal were observed in all Gram-negative bacterial strains, even at the lowest inoculation level of ≈ 31 CFU/mL. To further assess the specificity of the proposed sensing platform, two Gram‑positive bacteria (S. aureus and E. faecium) were evaluated as negative controls. Despite performing the full set of measurements used for Gram‑negative strains, including IDS–VDS characterization over a broad concentration range, the sensor exhibited no meaningful or interpretable response toward these species. For both bacteria, the recorded drain‑current changes were weak, scattered, and lacked any linear or concentration‑dependent trend (Fig. 6a–d). Statistical analysis confirmed the absence of a significant correlation between drain current and cell density, indicating that the device does not respond to these Gram‑positive species.

I – V curves and corresponding standard curves for Gram-positive bacterial assay: S. aureus a, b, and E. faecium c, d. The numerical data corresponding to the calibration plots for Gram-positive bacteria are provided in Table S2

This irregular behavior and lack of sensor response are due to the structural and chemical characteristics of the Gram-positive bacteria, S. aureus and E. faecium, which possesses a thick, multilayered peptidoglycan cell wall and lacks surface LPS [35]. As a result, its surface charge is much lower than that of Gram-negative bacteria, and the electrostatic interaction between the positive amino groups of colistin and the negative surface charges of the cell is practically non-existent. As a result, the experimental data show that the ISFET sensor with the colistin layer provides a specific and selective response for Gram-negative bacteria.

To improve the accuracy and consistency of the calibration analysis, the concentration axis was converted to log(CFU/mL), and all calibration curves were recalculated accordingly (Fig. 7). This transformation is widely applied in microbial biosensing studies, as bacterial responses often exhibit logarithmic behavior over broad dynamic concentration ranges. Following the logarithmic transformation, two distinct linear regions were observed for most Gram‑negative bacteria. The first region appeared in the lower concentration range (31–7.62 × 103 CFU/mL), with relatively small slopes (1–4 µA/log(CFU/mL)) and R2 values ranging from 0.83 to above 0.90. This regime likely reflects diffusion‑limited conditions, in which only a fraction of bacterial cells interact with the electrode surface, resulting in weaker electrochemical signals and greater variability.

Logarithmic calibration plots of the electrochemical sensor for Gram-negative bacteria over 31–1.67 × 107 CFU/mL. Two linear regimes are observed, indicating a transition in the dominant sensing mechanism across the dynamic range of the device

At higher concentrations (2.29 × 104–1.67 × 107 CFU/mL), a second linear region emerged with substantially steeper slopes, exceeding 148–171 µA/log(CFU/mL) for species such as E. coli, A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa, and K. pneumoniae. The slopes increased by more than one order of magnitude compared to the low‑concentration regime, while R2 values generally exceeded 0.90. This behavior suggests a transition to a surface‑interaction‑dominated regime, where increased bacterial coverage enhances electron‑transfer processes and produces a stronger and more stable electrochemical response. Therefore, while the sensor maintains practical linearity with respect to absolute bacterial concentration, logarithmic analysis provides clearer mechanistic insight into the transition from sparse to dense bacterial interactions across the sensor’s dynamic range.

Stability and reusability of the device

The long-term stability, repeatability, and reusability of the p-Si/SiO2/ZnO@Colistin ISFET sensor were evaluated by monitoring its sensitivity at multiple time intervals after fabrication (days 3, 7, and 14) relative to the initial measurements on day 0. The electrical responses to 16 serial dilutions of E. coli (prepared by standard methods) were recorded using the same instrument, and IDS vs. VDS curves were plotted; standard calibration plots (IDS vs. CFU/mL) were finally derived from the current changes. At day 3, the sensor maintained its robust performance, exhibiting a sensitivity of 0.0385 nA/(CFU/mL) and an R2 value of 0.927 (Fig. 8a, b). After repeated use, measurements on day 7 showed that the sensitivity remained at 0.0357 nA/(CFU/mL) with R2 of 0.928, indicating minimal signal bias and excellent device stability (Fig. 8c, d). On day 14, the sensitivity decreased to an average of 0.032 nA/(CFU/mL) (R2 =0.91), but the response was still concentration-dependent (Fig. 8e, f). Relative to the initial sensitivity on day 0, the sensor retained 98.71% of its activity on day 3, 91.53% on day 7, and 82.5% after 14 days (Table 3), indicating high operational stability and reproducibility over two weeks. This gradual attenuation is attributed to decreased bioactivity resulting from colistin functionalization or subtle surface modifications, while the device overall maintained reliable quantitative detection.

Real sample analysis

To assess the real‑sample applicability of the developed sensor, its performance for detecting E. coli at a concentration of ~ 2.29 × 104 CFU mL–1 was examined in both commercial bottled drinking water and urine, and the results were compared with those obtained in PBS. In PBS, the change in source–drain current (ΔIDS), relative to the bacteria‑free control, was measured to be 79.182 µA.

For bottled drinking water, three independent measurements yielded ΔIDS values of 81.123, 81.790, and 77.308 µA, resulting in an average response of 80.074 µA with a SD of 2.443 µA. The corresponding recovery, calculated relative to the PBS response, was 101.13% ± 3.08%. These results indicate that the dissolved ionic species and constituents present in drinking water exert negligible interference with the sensor signal, while the RSD of 3.05% reflects good reproducibility of the sensing platform in this matrix (Fig. 9a and b).

Similarly, for urine samples, the ΔIDS values obtained at the same bacterial concentration were 75.469, 80.215, and 73.977 µA. These measurements yielded a mean response of 76.554 µA with an SD of 3.257 µA. The recovery relative to PBS was calculated to be 96.68% ± 4.12%, demonstrating minimal signal attenuation in the presence of urine‑derived species. Moreover, the RSD of 4.26% confirms the acceptable repeatability and overall reliability of the sensor for E. coli detection in complex biological matrices (Fig. 9c and d).

To further evaluate the selectivity of the proposed sensing platform, additional experiments were performed using a representative Gram‑positive bacterium, S. aureus, under the same experimental conditions in both bottled drinking water and urine samples (Fig. 9e and f). This absence of response confirms that the sensor exhibits negligible sensitivity toward Gram‑positive bacteria and maintains high selectivity toward Gram‑negative. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the proposed sensing platform is capable of selectively and reliably detecting E. coli in real environmental and biological samples, highlighting its strong potential for practical applications in environmental monitoring and clinical diagnostics.

Conclusion and future outlook

In this work, an ISFET sensor based on a p-Si/SiO2/ZnO structure functionalized with colistin was developed for the label-free detection of Gram-negative bacteria. The device response was evaluated against several Gram-negative species, including Escherichia coli, Acinetobacter baumannii, Salmonella enterica, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella oxytoca, and Klebsiella pneumoniae, while Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus and Enterococcus faecium) were used as negative controls. Under the tested conditions, the sensor showed selective responses toward Gram-negative bacteria, with negligible signal changes observed for Gram-positive controls, indicating that colistin–lipopolysaccharide interactions contribute to the sensing mechanism.

For quantitative analysis, linear calibration curves were obtained for all tested Gram-negative bacteria within the investigated concentration ranges. The slopes of these calibration plots were used to define sensor sensitivity, and the limits of detection were estimated to be approximately 137 CFU/mL for E. coli, 218 CFU/mL for K. oxytoca, 235 CFU/mL for K. pneumoniae, 169 CFU/mL for A. baumannii, 188 CFU/mL for S. enterica, and 289 CFU/mL for P. aeruginosa. These results indicate lower limits of detection for E. coli, A. baumannii, K. oxytoca, K. pneumoniae, and S. enterica, consistent with higher analytical sensitivity. The response to P. aeruginosa was comparatively weaker. This behavior may be attributed to differences in bacterial surface charge properties and variations in colistin-LPS binding affinity.

The p‑Si/SiO2/ZnO@Colistin ISFET sensor demonstrated high long‑term stability, repeatability, and reusability, maintaining over 80% of its initial sensitivity after two weeks of use. The slight decrease in performance was possibly attributed to reduced bioactivity of colistin or minor surface modifications, without compromising the sensor’s quantitative accuracy. Furthermore, real‑sample evaluations in bottled drinking water and urine confirmed high recovery rates (approximately 96–101%) and negligible signal interference from matrix components, indicating strong analytical reliability under real-sample conditions. The lack of clear concentration-dependent response to S. aureus further supported the sensor’s selectivity toward Gram‑negative bacteria. Collectively, these results highlight the sensor’s robustness, precision, and specificity, making it a promising candidate for practical applications in environmental monitoring and clinical diagnostics.

The results of this study demonstrate that the ISFET sensor offers a rapid, sensitive, reliable, and cost-effective approach for detecting Gram-negative bacteria, making it a promising alternative to time-consuming methods that rely on specialized equipment, including traditional culture-based or molecular techniques. Also, the ability to clearly distinguish between Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria is an important advantage for clinical, food, and environmental applications.

Despite improvements in results and the use of a dedicated sensor with a low-cost sensing layer compared to peptides designed as antibodies, future work could explore more stable immobilization strategies, such as APTES–PEI (polyethylenimine) or ZnO/TiO2 hybrid layers, to improve long-term durability while perserving sensitivity.

In addition, a microfluidic version, Lab‑on‑Chip, could be effective in reducing sample volume, accelerating the process, and integrating sample-to-result steps. The development of multi-sensor arrays would enable simultaneous detection of multiple species or antibiotic resistance profiles. In some assays, noise was observed, which could be reduced by using better readout electronics and by providing concentrations below the device’s LOD.
