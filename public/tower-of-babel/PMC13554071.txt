Cover cropping differentially shapes soil fungal and bacterial communities in citrus orchards
Authors: Adesuwa S Erhunmwunse, Elena Karlsen-Ayala, Yaslin N Gonzalez, Gabriel Maltais-Landry, Sarah L Strauss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe role of cover crops in promoting the abundance of beneficial soil microbes is increasingly gaining attention in tree crops. However, soil fungi remain comparatively understudied despite their roles in nutrient cycling and soil organic matter turnover, functions that are directly influenced by cover crops and may, in turn, affect the cash crop. This study compared the effects of cover crop mixtures to a weedy control on soil bacterial and fungal communities in young (< 10 years) and old (> 20 years) citrus orchards—with different lengths of cover cropping—across three soil depths over 2 years. Soil fungi responded to cover crops, whereas soil bacteria showed minimal changes in both orchards. In the young orchard, non-legume cover crop mixtures altered soil fungal communities three months after planting and after 2 years, both cover crop mixtures resulted in distinct community shifts relative to the control. In the old orchard, soil depth shaped fungal communities, with the strongest cover crop effects in the topsoil and subsurface soil. In both orchards, cover crops enriched potentially plant-beneficial microbial taxa, including arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, highlighting the importance of cover crops as a management tool for driving fungal-mediated ecological processes and soil health benefits in orchard systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57565-4.Keywords: Microbial diversity, Soil depth, Cover crop duration, Fungal guilds, Soil healthSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Fruit and nut tree crops account for more than 10% of global cropland and contribute substantially to global food production1. However, conventional tree systems are typically managed as monocultures that rely heavily on synthetic inputs and intensive mechanization, which can lead to soil degradation, pest pressure, and declines in soil microbial diversity2–5. As a result, integrating cover crops has received growing attention as a strategy to improve soil health and sustain productivity in tree crop systems3,4,6.

Much of what we know about cover crop benefits comes from annual row crops, where cover crops replace an unplanted fallow and often improve soil carbon (C) and nitrogen (N) and enhance microbial diversity7–9. However, cover cropping in tree crop systems differs from annual systems. In tree crops, cover crops are grown between trees or tree rows, receive minimal tillage, and may grow year-round3. These distinct management practices might not only influence soil C and nutrient availability10,11 but also affect soil microbial communities, key drivers of soil health and ecosystem function12,13.

Soil microbes and microbial processes contribute to ecological mechanisms that drive soil health, including soil organic matter (SOM) accumulation, decomposition, nutrient cycling, plant growth promotion, and disease suppression14,15. Recent studies in tree crop systems have demonstrated that cover crops can enhance soil microbial diversity and promote potentially beneficial soil microbial taxa16–21. In citrus orchards, cover crops increased soil bacterial diversity and N-cycling populations that were linked to increases in soil C and N17,22 with stronger effects observed under a mixture containing both legumes and non-legumes compared to a mixture composed solely of non-legumes. Cover crop associated bacterial benefits can also extend into the subsoil (> 30 cm), promoting microbes that may buffer against abiotic and biotic stress and support ecosystem functions, including enhanced nutrient availability and nutrient uptake by trees23,24.

Cover crop effects on soil microbial communities are strongly influenced by orchard age, management history, environmental conditions, cover crop duration, and soil depth because these factors alter soil organic matter inputs, nutrient availability, root distribution, moisture, and microbial habitat conditions17–19,22,25. Previous studies reported increases in soil microbial diversity and activity with orchard age, likely due to greater soil moisture, total C and N, and lower soil pH19,21,25,26. In another study, continuous cover cropping for more than 4 years increased bacterial and fungal diversity in 0–20 cm compared to 20–40 and 40–60 cm27. These findings demonstrate the need for comprehensive research that considers orchard age, soil depth, and historical management to understand soil microbial responses to cover crops. This information can assist growers in selecting appropriate cover cropping practices for building soil health and sustainable tree production.

Despite growing interest in the beneficial roles of the soil microbiome in cover cropped tree systems, soil fungi remain comparatively understudied in these systems3. Fungi play important roles in nutrient cycling and SOM turnover because of their diverse enzymatic capabilities for degrading organic residues28. Evidence across a wide range of cropping systems suggests that fungal diversity and community composition respond directly to cover crops because many fungi depend on C inputs from plant litters, root exudates, and rhizodeposits. Meanwhile, bacteria respond strongly to gradual changes in soil properties induced by cover crops19,20,29,30. Although studies on fungal communities in tree crop systems have burgeoned in the last three years and showed general positive soil fungal responses to cover cropping in tree systems17,31,32, research remains limited.

Florida is the second largest citrus producing state in the U.S., with approximately 111,169 ha in 202433. Citrus in Florida are typically grown on sandy soils containing 94–98% sand and only 1–2% organic matter, creating challenges related to poor soil fertility, low ion exchange capacity, and low water retention34. Maintaining soil health under these conditions is challenging, making cover cropping a potential strategy to improve soil health and enhance beneficial microbes4,6. However, in Florida citrus systems, only about 15% of the cover crop studies focused on soil fungal communities17,22,35,36, leaving major gaps in our understanding of how fungi contribute to soil health and ecosystem functioning, especially across soil depths and over time, in orchard systems.

In this study, we determined the effects of two cover crop mixtures (legumes + non-legumes and non-legumes only) compared with a grower standard control [planted bahiagrass (Paspalum notatum) and weeds] on soil bacterial and fungal communities in two sites within a Florida commercial citrus orchard with < 10 years and > 20 years tree ages at two timepoints (three months and two years after planting cover crop) and three sampling depths (0–15, 15–30, and 30–50 cm). We hypothesized that cover cropping would alter soil bacterial and fungal communities, with responses varying across orchard age, soil depth, and cover crop duration. We further expected longer-term cover crop management to enhance microbial responses through cumulative plant residues and nutrient inputs.

Materials and methods

Experimental site, design, and management practices

The field experiment was conducted in a commercial citrus orchard in southwest Florida with the consent of the grower. Within the orchard, two sites were selected for this study with citrus trees < 10 years (referred to as Young Grove [26° 30′ 14.14″ N; 81° 24′ 17.90″ W]) and > 20 years (referred to as Old Grove [26° 30′ 31.28″ N, 81° 23′ 17.10″ W]) as described by Gonzalez et al.37. Cover crops were previously planted for three years in the Old Grove22, while the Young Grove had no cover crops but weeds and planted bahiagrass (Paspalum notatum) prior to the beginning of this study in 2021.

The soil at the experimental site is classified as Basinger series (Siliceous, hyperthermic Spodic Psammaquents) in the Young Grove and Immokalee soil series (Sandy, siliceous, hyperthermic Arenic Alaquods) in the Old Grove. Soil fertility and biochemical properties, as well as cover crop biomass, were previously measured and reported by Gonzalez et al.37,38. The chemical properties of the topsoil (0–15 cm) for the Young and Old Grove, based on 15 samples collected across treatments and blocks in each grove three months after cover cropping are presented as mean values (with standard deviations in parentheses), respectively, as follows: pHwater (6.8 ± 0.7 and 7.1 ± 0.2), SOM (17 ± 2 and 25 ± 4 g kg− 1), ammonium (1.7 ± 0.5 and 1.7 ± 0.7 mg kg− 1), nitrate (0.3 ± 0.4 and 0.6 ± 0.5 mg kg− 1), Mehlich-3 extractable P (52 ± 8 and 200 ± 64 mg kg− 1), K (32 ± 25 and 23 ± 10 mg kg− 1), Mg (65 ± 18 and 134 ± 20 mg kg− 1), and Ca (1196 ± 595 and 1460 ± 277 mg kg− 1). There were minimal changes in soil properties among treatments and blocks three months after cover cropping. Temperature and precipitation were recorded hourly using an automated weather station near the experimental field (< 6 km) (Supplementary Fig. S1) (https://fawn.ifas.ufl.edu/data). Mean annual air temperature and total rainfall were 23.1 °C and 1147 mm in 2021 and 23.3 °C and 1447 mm in 2023.

Fifteen plots were arranged in a balanced incomplete block design with five replicate plots assigned to each treatment as previously described22. Each plot (150 m × 14 m for Young Grove and 195 m x 14 m for Old Grove) was formed by two-rows of trees with rows spaced 7 m apart22. At both sites, the three treatments consisted of: (1) a grower standard control (GC); (2) a mixture of only non-legume cover crops (NL); and (3) a mixture of legume + non-legume cover crops (LG). Unlike annual systems where bare fallow soil often serves as the control, the grower standard control consisted of planted bahiagrass and naturally occurring weeds, including Megathyrsus maximus, Eleusine indica, Cyperus globulosus, Panicum spp., and Lepidium virginicum17,39.

Details on cover crop species and field maintenance can be found in Gonzalez et al.37. Briefly, cover crops were planted with a no-till drill each year in June and November from 2021 to 2023. In June, the non-legume cover crop mixtures consisted of pearl millet (Pennisetum glaucum), buckwheat (Fagopyrum esculentum), Egyptian wheat (Sorghum vulgare roxburghii), and sorghum sudangrass (Sorghum bicolor × S. bicolor var. sudanese); and legume cover crop mixtures included the same non-legumes as well as sunn hemp (Crotolaria juncea) and cowpea (Vigna unguiculata). In November, the non-legume cover crops planted were black oat (Avena strigose), ‘Florida 401’ rye (Secale cereale), and daikon radish (Raphanus sativus); and the legume cover crop mixture included the same non-legumes as well as hairy vetch (Vicia villosa) and sunn hemp. Cover crops planted in June and November were the same each year except for sorghum sudangrass which was not planted in June 2023. A month prior to summer and winter cover crop planting, cover crop treatments (and weeds) were terminated by mowing and shallow disking.

Soil sampling, DNA extraction, and sequencing

Bulk soil samples were collected in September 2021 (three months after cover crops were planted) and 2023 (two years after cover crops were planted). Soil samples were collected from the treatment row middle within each plot at 10 random sampling points using soil probes (3.2 cm diameter × 38 cm length) for 0–15 and 15–30 cm soil depths, and a sub-soil probe (2.5 cm diameter x 97 cm length) for 30–50 cm soil depth. Sampling probes were sterilized between treatments using 70% ethanol. Within each plot, soil cores collected from the 10 random points were composited by soil depth to generate one composite sample per depth for each plot. Composite samples were collected across five plots per treatment, three treatments, two sampling timepoints, and two groves resulting in 180 soil samples (5 replicate plots × 3 treatments × 3 depths × 2 sampling timepoints × 2 sites). Samples were kept on ice in the field and transported at 4 °C to the UF/IFAS Southwest Florida Research and Education Center (SWFREC) Soil Microbiology Laboratory. Soil samples for microbial analyses were stored at − 80 °C until use.

Soil DNA was extracted from 0.25 g of soil using the DNAeasy 96 PowerSoil Pro QIAcube HT Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA) according to manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA was quantified in duplicates using the QuantiFluor ONE dsDNA (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). The extracted soil DNA was sent to the Genomics and Microbiome Core Facility at Rush University (Chicago, IL, USA) for amplicon sequencing using Illumina Miseq (Illumina, Inc. San Diego, CA, USA). The ITS1 region of the fungal rRNA genes was amplified using ITS1F40 and ITS2R41 primers, and the V4–V5 region of the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using 515 F and 926R primers42.

Bioinformatics and statistical analyses

A total of 11,488,105 bacterial and 12,372,870 fungal reads were obtained from all samples with an average of 58,913 reads for 16S rRNA and 62,489 reads for ITS in each sample. Forward and reverse primers were removed using cutadapt version 4.9′ in QIIME2 version 2024.243. Trimming, quality filtering, removal of chimeric sequences, denoising, merging, and construction of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were carried out using DADA2 v.1.30.0. Bacterial and fungal ASVs were taxonomically assigned to the SILVA 138.244 and Unite version 1045 databases, respectively. Singletons, unassigned, and reads that were classified as mitochondria and chloroplast were removed. A total of 50,221 bacterial and 13,538 fungal ASVs were obtained for all samples.

The 16S rRNA and ITS reads were rarefied at 15,000 and 20,000 sequencing depth, respectively for alpha diversity. For beta diversity, ASVs with zero total counts across all samples were removed. The ASV count data were then converted to proportions and subjected to Hellinger transformation. Alpha (number of observed ASVs, Shannon, and Simpson indices) and beta diversity (Bray-Curtis dissimilarity-based principal coordinates) were carried out using the phyloseq v.1.48.0 package in R46. The effect of cover crop treatment, soil depth, sampling timepoint, and their interactions on beta diversity at each site was tested by permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA), with block treated as a random effect, using “Adonis2” in vegan R package47. Pairwise comparisons were carried out using the pairwiseAdonis package. The functional inference of the bacterial and fungal communities were predicted using PICRUSt248 and FUNGuild R version 2.0 database49, respectively. Alpha diversity indices as well as log transformed fungal guilds from FUNGuild and predicted pathways from PICRUSt2 at each site were analyzed using three-way ANOVA, with cover crop treatment, soil depth, timepoint, and their interactions considered as fixed effects and block as a random effect. In addition to graphical observations, Shapiro-Wilk’s test was used to assess the normal distribution of the residuals of alpha diversity indices, fungal guilds from FUNGuild, and predicted pathways from PICRUSt2. Levene’s tests were used to determine equality of variance. Statistical differences among levels of the fixed effects were determined using Tukey’s post hoc test in emmeans package in R.

Differential abundance was determined using the DESeq2 package in R50 to identify the bacterial and fungal taxa responsive for the trends observed in alpha and beta diversity. The Benjamini-Hochberg method was used to correct P values (≤ 0.05) for multiple testing as part of DESeq2. The relationships between microbial community composition and soil properties measured and reported by Gonzalez et al.38 including SOM, permanganate oxidizable carbon (POXC), autoclaved-citrate extractable (ACE) protein, potentially mineralizable nitrogen (PMN), 24-hour C mineralization (Cmin), total carbon (C), total nitrogen (N), ammonium (NH4+), nitrate (NO3−), pH (1:1 soil: water, pHwater), buffer pH (pHb), cation exchange capacity (CEC), moisture content, and Mehlich-3 extractable nutrients (P, K, Ca, Mg, S, Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, and B) were determined using distance-based redundancy analysis (db-RDA) with ‘capscale’ function51. Soil properties were tested for multicollinearity using a variance inflation factor (VIF). Soil organic matter, pHb, PMN, C, ACE, CEC, Ca, Mg, Mn, and Zn had VIF that were > 10 and were removed for db-RDA analysis. The significance of the constrained ordination was tested by a permutation test using 9,999 permutations. Statistical significance was considered at P ≤ 0.05.

Results

Soil fungal response to cover crops at the Young and Old Groves

Diversity, community composition, and differential abundant taxa

Cover crop treatment, sampling timepoint, and soil depth had no significant effect (P > 0.05) on fungal Shannon and Simpson diversity at both sites (Supplementary Table S1 and Fig. 1a). However, fungal richness was affected by soil depth at both sites (Supplementary Table S1). In the Young Grove, fungal richness was significantly greater in the topsoil (0–15 cm) and subsurface soil (15–30 cm) by approximately 42% and 17%, respectively, compared to the subsoil (30–50 cm). Similarly, in the Old Grove, fungal richness was significantly greater in the topsoil and subsurface soil by approximately 57% and 10%, respectively, relative to the subsoil (Supplementary Table S2).

The major fungal phyla at both sites were Ascomycota and Basidiomycota, accounting for 74% and 18.1% in the Young Grove and 80% and 9.8% in the Old Grove, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S4). At the genus level, Penicillium (7.3%), Talaromyces (6.5%), Aspergillus (5.5%), Trechispora (3.3%), and Trichoderma (3.0%) were dominant in the Young Grove, whereas Talaromyces (12.2%), Penicillium (8.7%), Aspergillus (4.4%), Ramicandelaber (2.7), and Calcarisporiella (2.5%) were dominant in the Old Grove (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Among the differentially abundant fungal genera, 10 genera were significantly enriched under NL and LG in the Young Grove after three months of cover cropping (Fig. 2a). After two years, this increased to 21 enriched fungal genera, primarily from Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Chytridiomycota, Mucoromycota, and Kickxellomycota (Supplementary Table S4 and Fig. 2b). Both NL and LG enriched the fungal genera belonging to Basidiomycota more than three times after 2 years compared to after 3 months (Fig. 2). Across soil depths in the Young Grove, NL and LG significantly influenced the differential abundance of fungal genera (Supplementary Fig. S5). In the topsoil, 12–13 differentially abundant genera were detected under both NL and LG, predominantly from Ascomycota and Basidiomycota. Trechispora, Aspergillus, and Conocybe were enriched under both NL and LG, whereas Corynascus was depleted (Supplementary Table S4 and Supplementary Fig. S5a). In the subsurface and subsoil, Rozellomycota was identified alongside Ascomycota and Basidiomycota (Supplementary Fig. S5b,c). The subsurface soil contained 10 differentially abundant genera, with Talaromyces, Chaetomium, and Bipolaris enriched under both NL and LG, while Curvularia was depleted under NL but enriched under LG (Supplementary Table S4 and Supplementary Fig. S5b). In the subsoil, 21–22 differentially abundant genera were identified; Melanconiella, Chaetomium, Aspergillus, Conocybe, and Preussia were enriched under both NL and LG, with additional enrichment of Penicillium under NL, whereas Hawksworthiomyces, Pseudopithomyces, and Nigrospora were consistently depleted by both NL and LG (Supplementary Table S4 and Supplementary Fig S5c).

Relationship of soil chemical properties and fungal communities

Soil fertility and biochemical properties explained 22 and 30% of the variation in soil fungal community composition in the Young and Old Groves, respectively (Supplementary Table S8). The Db-RDA axes 1 and 2 together explained 37% of fungal variation in the Young Grove and 58% in the Old Grove (Supplementary Fig. S7). POXC, soil pHwater, NH4+, NO3−, moisture, K, and B were significant predictors of fungal communities at both groves, with both POXC and NH4+ explaining ~ 21% of the variation in the Young Grove and POXC explaining 36% in the Old Grove. Total N, Fe, and Cu were unique predictors in the Young Grove (Supplementary Fig. S7 and Supplementary Table S8), whereas Cmin, P, and S were predictors in the Old Grove (Supplementary Table S8 and Supplementary Fig. S7). After three months in the Young Grove, fungal communities under NL and LG were associated with soil pHwater, moisture, and Fe in the subsurface and subsoil, and with the other indicators in the topsoil (Supplementary Fig. S7). After two years, fungal communities under the GC were positioned near the center of the ordination, indicating weak associations with soil indicators. In the Old Grove, fungal communities under NL and LG in the topsoil were associated with all indicators except moisture, which was associated with subsurface and subsoil communities (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Soil bacterial response to cover crops at the Young and Old Groves

Diversity, community composition, and differential abundant taxa

Cover crop treatments only impacted bacterial richness in the Young Grove, which also differed significantly between timepoints. However, this pattern was consistent across all depths, as no significant three-way interaction was observed (Supplementary Table S1 and Fig. 6). After three months of planting cover crops in the Young Grove, the bacterial richness was significantly greater in the NL treatment compared to GC and LG across all depths (Supplementary Fig. S8). However, two years after cover crops were planted in the Young Grove, cover crop treatment had no effect on bacterial richness across all depths (Supplementary Fig. S8). Bacterial diversity indices were affected by sampling timepoint in both the Young and Old Groves, with diversity generally increasing after two years of cover cropping compared to three months after cover cropping (Fig. 6).

(A) Bacterial alpha diversity, estimated using observed ASV, Shannon, and Simpson indices and (B) Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray Curtis dissimilarity on soil bacterial community composition under the three treatments (GC: grower control; NL: non-legume cover crop mixture; LG: legume + non-legume cover crop mixture) across three soil depths (0–15 cm, 15–30 cm, and 30–50 cm), and two sampling timepoints (3-months and 2-years after planting cover crops) at the Young and Old Groves. *Shows that all bacterial alpha diversity indices were significantly (P < 0.05) affected by timepoint, with greater diversity after 2 years compared to 3 months.

There was a significant cover crop effect on bacterial community composition in the Young Grove, and no effect was observed in the Old Grove (Table 1; Fig. 6b). However, pairwise comparisons with P value adjustments showed no significant differences among cover crop treatments in the Young Grove (Supplementary Table S3). The dominant bacterial taxa at the phylum level across all samples at both sites were Pseudomonadota (33.6% and 28.7% in the Young and Old Groves, respectively), Actinomycetota (19.3% and 20.8%), and Acidobacteriota (10.8% and 12.8%) (Supplementary Fig. S9). At the genus level, bacterial taxa differed between groves. The Young Grove was dominated by Acidibacter (4.4%), Gaiella (4.4%), Pedomicrobium (4.3%), and Niallia (4.3%), whereas the Old Grove was dominated by Acidothermus (8.0%), Acidibacter (7.4%), Candidatus Udaeobacter (3.0%), and Pedomicrobium (3.0%) (Supplementary Fig. S9).

Differential abundance analysis showed that both NL and LG compared to GC significantly increased the relative abundance of the bacterial genus Acidothermus and an unclassified Pseudomonadota genus after three months in the Young Grove (Supplementary Fig. S10). After two years, cover crop mixtures significantly enriched the N-cycling bacteria Nitrospira and potential plant-growth promoting bacteria Streptomyces (Supplementary Table S4 and Supplementary Fig. S10). No differentially abundant genera were detected across soil depths in the Young Grove, except in the subsoil, where NL and LG reduced Nitrospira, while NL increased Candidatus Udaeobacter and LG enriched Actinoallomurus (Supplementary Fig. S11).

In the Old Grove, both NL and LG significantly reduced the relative abundance of Tumebacillus compared to GC after three months, while NL increased Aquisphaera (Supplementary Fig. S12). No genera were significantly enriched or depleted after two years. In the topsoil, NL significantly enhanced Labrys while reducing the relative abundance of an unclassified Methylomirabilota genus, whereas LG depleted the relative abundance of an unclassified Planctomycetota genus (Supplementary Fig. S13). In the subsurface and subsoil, NL and LG promoted 1921-2 and FCPS473 (Chloroflexota) along with an unclassified GAL15 genus, known for their roles in decomposing plant substrates (Supplementary Table S4 and Supplementary Fig. S13). At a depth of 30–50 cm, LG increased Acidibacter. However, both NL and LG reduced Acidothermus, with NL further depleting Mycobacterium (Supplementary Table S4 and Supplementary Fig. S13).

Cover crop treatment, timepoint, and soil depth had no significant effect on predicted bacterial metabolic pathways involved in C, N, phosphorus, sulfur, and fatty acid and lipid metabolisms, as well as production of secondary metabolites, retrieved from PICRUSt2 at both sites (Supplementary Table S9).

Relationship of soil chemical properties and bacterial communities

Soil fertility and biochemical properties explained 30% and 34% of the variation in soil bacterial community composition in the Young and Old Groves, respectively (Supplementary Table S8). The Db-RDA axes 1 and 2 together accounted for 52% of the changes in bacterial communities in the Young Grove and 64% in the Old Grove (Supplementary Fig. S14). At both groves, POXC, pHwater, moisture, NH4+, and B significantly predicted changes in bacterial communities, with POXC accounting for 21% and 38% of the variation in the Young and Old Groves, respectively. Total N, Fe, and Cu were significant predictors in the Young Grove (Supplementary Fig. S14), while Cmin, P, K, and S were significant in the Old Grove (Supplementary Fig. S14). Subsurface and subsoil bacterial communities were associated with soil moisture at both sites and around pHwater and B in the Young Grove. Topsoil bacterial communities were associated with the remaining soil indicators (Supplementary Fig. S14).

Discussion

Cover crop effects on soil fungal communities were shaped by temporal and spatial factors

In our study, planting cover crops in citrus orchards induced significant shifts in soil fungal community composition with responses depending on the duration of cover cropping in the Young Grove and soil depth in the Old Grove. Given their strong association with plant-derived inputs, fungi are generally sensitive to changes in plant community composition16,20,29,52–57. For example, fungi depend directly on plants for C and energy through mycorrhizal and endophytic associations, or as saprophytes decomposing plant residues57. This may explain the temporal shifts in fungal community composition observed in the Young Grove following cover crop establishment. However, in the Old Grove, where cover crops had been established three years prior to this study, soil fungal community composition showed no further change over the length of time under cover cropping but cover crop effect on fungal community was shaped by soil depth. The absence of further temporal changes in the fungal community composition of the Old Grove contrasts with studies that reported continued shifts in soil fungal communities under long-term cover cropping (> 5 years)20,27,58,59. These differing results may be attributable to differences in management practices. In other studies, cover-cropped plots were typically compared with bare-soil controls and often received fertilizer applications, whereas the control treatment in our study consisted of naturally occurring vegetation and cover crop rows did not receive additional fertilization. Furthermore, soil depth may have exerted a stronger influence on fungal community composition than temporal variation, potentially masking temporal effects, as reported by Zhao et al.60.

The observed shifts in fungal community composition in the Young Grove indicate that cover crop establishment can rapidly influence soil fungal communities within a short time. Similar rapid responses of soil fungi have been documented in other systems, including pecan and tea plantations19,61. In the Young Grove, these early responses were detected under the non-legume cover crop mixture and may have been driven by new C inputs from the cover crops62,63 that differ in quantity and chemistry from those of the existing bahiagrass and weeds. The absence of a similar response in the legume-containing cover crop mixture during the same period may be related to differences in cover crop growth. Although biomass was not quantified during this period, field observations indicated that the non-legume mixture established more rapidly than the legume-containing mixture, potentially contributing to the stronger fungal community response.

Changes in fungal community composition under both cover crop mixtures after two years of cover cropping may be related to their greater biomass relative to the weedy control, as reported by Gonzalez et al.37 in a concurrent study at the same site. Cover crop biomass is known to be a key determinant of soil microbial responses to cover crops64, potentially through its effects on organic matter inputs and rhizosphere processes. However, because cover crop biomass was not measured during the early stages of establishment in this study, the extent to which biomass contributed to the temporal differences in fungal community responses cannot be determined.

Soil fungal groups differ in their C preferences and demands, and as a result, shifts in both the quality and quantity of C inputs can reshape community structure65. The greater biomass production under the cover crop mixtures compared to the weedy control37 likely increased inputs of plant litter and root exudates. These additional resources may have contributed to the higher relative abundance of Basidiomycota and Symbiotrophs, particularly AMF, observed under cover crops after two years in the Young Grove. Basidiomycota possess diverse enzymatic capabilities for degrading lignin and other complex plant compounds and often become more prominent when soils accumulate more complex organic matter inputs66. Their increased abundance may therefore reflect a greater capacity to utilize the diverse plant inputs from the different cover crop species in the mixtures used in this study. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, on the other hand, are obligate symbionts that depend on plants for C and are positively linked to changes in plant growth67. Previous studies have reported increases in the relative abundance of AMF with cover cropping such as ryegrass and hairy vetch68,69. The cover crop mixtures used in our study contained radish, a Brassicaceae species considered a non-host of AMF that may suppress mycorrhizal fungi through the production of glucosinolate-derived compounds, including isothiocyanates70. However, the inclusion of Brassicaceae species in diverse cover crop mixtures does not necessarily reduce AMF abundance as reported in other studies71,72. Cover crop mixtures have been shown to increase AMF abundance71 and both cover crop mixtures in our study contained multiple AMF host species such buckwheat73, oat71, and cowpea74, whose diverse root systems and carbon inputs may have contributed to the increased AMF abundance after two years of cover cropping.

In the Old Grove, continuous cover cropping did not alter soil fungal communities over the time period of this study, but a longer history of cover cropping was associated with vertical stratification of fungal communities across soil depths. Continuous cover cropping under minimal soil disturbance characteristic of perennial systems has been shown to promote vertical stratification of microbial communities due to stratification of soil organic carbon across soil depths75. Cover crop effects on soil fungal communities were limited to the topsoil and subsurface soil, which could be driven by greater root densities, plant litter accumulation, and soil C and nutrients76–79. In our study, POXC was a major predictor of changes in soil fungal communities. Although cover crops did not generally influence soil nutrients and C across depths in the Old Grove38, the decline in SOM and POXC with depth (Supplementary Table S10) may have reduced the potential for fungal community responses in deeper soils. Moreover, soil biochemical properties only explained about 30% of the changes in soil fungal composition in the Old Grove, indicating that other properties likely linked to older orchard age and longer use of cover crops, such as root density and litter accumulation across depths, not measured in this study may account for the unexplained variation.

At both orchards, cover crops increased the relative abundance of potentially beneficial fungi such as Aspergillus, Penicillium, and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi. Some members of these fungal groups play essential roles in plant growth promotion, organic matter decomposition, and nutrient cycling80,81. However, there was also an increase in potentially pathogenic fungi, including Fusarium and Curvularia. Because our findings were interpreted primarily based on genus-level taxonomic assignment and FUNGuild annotations, these functional interpretations should be treated with caution as species within the same genus can differ substantially in ecological functions. For example, some species within the genera Fusarium and Curvularia are well known plant pathogens, whereas others may contribute to plant growth through mechanisms such as nutrient solubilization, C and N cycling, and the secretion of plant-growth hormones82,83. Differentiating between the pathogenic and beneficial roles of these fungi was beyond the scope of this study. Nevertheless, their multiple inferred functional roles indicate complex soil microbial interactions that warrant further investigation through direct approaches such as metagenomic and metatranscriptomic to understand how they influence plant and soil health under different cover cropping systems.

Soil bacterial diversity and community responses to cover crop

In contrast to the pronounced fungal responses, cover crops had relatively weak effects on bulk soil bacterial communities. Generally, bacterial communities are not directly structured by plant–microbe associations84. Instead, bacterial community changes tend to occur indirectly, mediated by plant-driven changes to soil chemical properties9,17,55,85. For example, Castellano-Hinojosa et al.17 detected cover crop effects on soil bacterial communities only in orchards where there were increases in soil C and N. At our study sites, a concurrent study by Gonzalez et al.38 showed minimal impact of cover crop mixtures on soil biochemical properties, which explains the limited bacterial response to cover crops we observed.

After three months in the Young Grove, we observed a slight significant increase in bacterial diversity under non-legume mixture compared to the grower’s standard control and the legume + non-legume mixture. Such short-term increases in bacterial diversity are well documented in annual systems86–88 but are less common in tree crop systems, where cover crops are typically compared against weedy vegetation rather than bare soil19,20,57,89. Given the minimal changes in soil biochemical properties in our study, the greater biomass of the non-legume mixture mentioned earlier, corroborated visually and supported by patterns in the Old Grove, likely drove changes in microhabitats that influenced these soil bacteria19,90.

Despite the longer history of cover cropping in the Old Grove, bacterial diversity remained largely unchanged. This contrasts with earlier work in the same orchard, where cover crops increased N-cycling bacteria after about three years22. However, that study observed effects only in 0–10 cm, with declining effects in deeper layers. In perennial systems under warm climates, cover crop effects on soil bacteria often remain confined to the surface soil due to minimal tillage, residues not incorporated into deeper layers, and rapid decomposition75,91,92. By sampling to 15 cm, we may have diluted the observable impact of cover crop on soil bacteria75.

Even without broad shifts in overall community composition, cover crops selectively enriched specific bacterial taxa. In the Young Grove, cover crops promoted the relative abundance of Acidothermus, Nitrospira, and Streptomyces, which contain species involved in promoting plant health and nutrient cycling93–95. In the Old Grove, cover crops affected the bacterial phylum Chloroflexota, particularly in the subsurface and subsoil. The vertical distribution of soil microbial communities is often attributed to declining C availability and changing environmental conditions with soil depth77,96. Members of Chloroflexota include facultative anaerobes with diverse metabolic pathways. They are generally considered slow-growing oligotrophs adapted to the low-resource conditions typical of deeper soils18,97–99. Chloroflexota abundance has been reported to be negatively associated with soil organic C and total N and positively associated with iron (Fe) concentrations in the subsoil100,101. In our study, SOM generally declined with depth, while subsurface and subsoil bacterial communities were associated with moisture and Fe in the Old Grove. However, Gonzalez et al.38 reported no effects of cover crop on these soil properties across depths, suggesting that cover crop effect on Chloroflexota may be influenced by other cover-crop induced changes such as root-derived C input and microhabitat changes that were not measured in this study.

Conclusions

Our study showed that soil fungal responses to cover crops were shaped by time under continuous cover cropping in the Young Grove and by soil depth in the Old Grove. Soil fungal community composition in the Young Grove changed within three months of cover crop establishment, and both cover crop mixtures influenced community composition after two years. However, in the Old Grove, where cover crops had been established three years prior to this study, continuous cover cropping did not lead to additional changes in soil fungal communities. Rather, in the Old Grove, cover crop mixtures influenced fungal communities in the topsoil and subsurface soil and had no impact on the subsoil.

Short-term soil fungal response to cover crops in the Young Grove after three months, coupled with the increase in potentially beneficial microbial taxa across both orchards, highlights the importance of cover crops as a management tool for driving fungal-mediated ecological processes and soil health benefits in orchard systems. However, in our study, orchard age and cover crop history were fully confounded. Therefore, future research is needed to tease apart their individual contributions to observed microbial community patterns, especially fungal communities.
