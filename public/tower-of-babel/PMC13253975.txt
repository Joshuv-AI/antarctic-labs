A Theoretical Model for the Treatment of Androgenetic Alopecia Using the STEAP3 Protein, and the Genetic Challenges of Treating AGA
Authors: Ignacy Klemens Młodzianowski, Małgorzata Grochocka, Tadeusz Tadrowski, Marta Hałas‐Wiśniewska, Magdalena Izdebska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAndrogenetic alopecia (AGA) is a prevalent condition. The treatment methods currently available for this condition have limited potential (minoxidil) and side effects with long‐term therapy that affect the physiology of the male population (5α‐reductase inhibitors). In recent years, there has been a considerable expansion in knowledge about the etiology of AGA, while current treatment methods appear to be outdated in light of new findings.MethodsA review of the latest publications on androgenetic alopecia concerning its etiology and genetics was conducted, with the aim of examining the molecular pathways associated with mutations in the following genes: The following genes were found to be implicated: AR (WNT/β‐catenin pathway), EDA2R (WNT/β‐catenin and proapoptotic pathways), HDAC9 (Treg pathway), PAX1 and FOXA2 (development pathways). This facilitated the development of a theoretical model for the treatment of AGA using the STEAP3 protein.ResultsA theoretical model of AGA therapy has been designed using the STEAP3 protein to activate the inhibited WNT/β‐catenin pathway as a result of mutations in the AR and EDA2R genes. STEAP3 catalyses the conversion of Fe3+ to Fe2+, which in turn facilitates the phosphorylation of GSK3β, resulting in its subsequent inactivation. This process culminates in the dissolution of the complex that hinders the transfer of β‐catenin to the cell nucleus.ConclusionIt is hypothesised that STEAP3‐based therapy has the potential to become an effective treatment for AGA, acting on the underlying cause of the disease (determined by the mutations described). It should be noted that all analyses presented in the following text constitute a theoretical model of AGA treatment using STEAP3 protein and its derivatives, and are not supported by laboratory and clinical studies. Nevertheless, they serve as an introduction to further in vitro, in vivo, and clinical trials.

Introduction

Androgenetic alopecia (AGA) affects mental health. Studies suggest that affected men under 26 years of age are significantly impacted by this problem, which even determines their functionality. Both sexes with the disease experience reduced physical and mental attractiveness [1, 2]. The significant psychological impact of AGA on patients motivates them to find solutions, such as popular hair transplantation and pharmacotherapy.

The alopecia epidemic is daunting, with the androgenic form causing the vast majority of its cases. Approximately 80% of men and 50% of women experience an advanced form of AGA during their lifetime [3]. The prominence of androgenetic alopecia has led to the emergence of a diverse range of treatment options, derived from a variety of scientific fields, reflecting the multifactorial nature of the condition.

Current Treatment Methods

The drugs currently employed in the treatment of AGA, namely dutasteride, finasteride and minoxidil, as well as therapies such as LLLT (Low‐Level Laser Therapy), offer no definitive solution for the condition andand may pose certain risks in some cases.

Minoxidil is the preferred topical medication for AGA treatment for both genders. By activating hair growth via its active component, minoxidil sulphate, generated through sulphotransferase action in the hair follicle's root [5]. The drug is classified as safe, however, its effectiveness is moderate. In 40% of patients, a therapeutic outcome is achieved with 5% minoxidil [6]. 60% efficacy is observed with 15% minoxidil in patients who do not respond to 5% minoxidil [7]. The need to identify the group of patients who respond to minoxidil therapy has arisen due to a considerable number of individuals being insensitive to the drug. Diagnostic methods analyzing the follicle root for sulphotransferases have achieved 93% sensitivity and 83% specificity [5].

Additionally, oral finasteride and dutasteride act as 5α‐reductase inhibitors, ultimately reducing dihydroxytestosterone (DHT) concentrations. Sexual dysfunction, such as erectile dysfunction, ejaculation problems, decreased libido, or gynaecomastia, may occur during treatment to the extent that treatment cannot be continued due to their effects on hormones [8].

Hair treat present, therapeutic approaches are not fully effective. In combination with 5% minoxidil, low‐level laser therapy (LLLT) shows a better therapeutic effect than 5% minoxidil alone, but its effect on therapy is not significant according to studies [9].

Transplantation has become increasingly popular in recent years. It is a desirable alternative for those experiencing baldness who have not responded to traditional treatments. Although the procedure can result in scarring, this issue is being addressed. The high cost of treatment may deter some, but hair transplantation is a durable option that many patients choose to pursue [10].

AR Mutation and WNT Pathway Interference

A number of mutations in the androgen receptor (AR) gene have been identified in patients with AGA. These mutations are located within exons 1, 4 and 5, as well as in a region preceding the enhancer region of the AR gene by 737 bases. More recent studies explicitly identify the nature of these mutations as up‐regulatory relative to the AR gene. In addition, two allelic variants of the AR gene were identified: GGN—24 and ACAAAAAGCATTTAAG‐24‐ATA. It has been observed that GGN—24 is more prevalent in individuals without AGA [11, 12].

Genetic observations indicate that the androgen receptor gene is of significant diagnostic value. However, its influence on the mechanism of hair loss is also notable. The androgen receptor is activated by DHT, the active form of testosterone formed by the action of 5α‐reductase. Both excessive testosterone production and 5α‐reductase hyperactivity favour AR receptor activation. At the same time, AGA‐defined mutation of this gene promotes its overexpression. These mechanisms also impact the overexpression of glycogen synthase protein‐3β (GSK‐3β), which is a transcription factor for the activated AR receptor [12, 13].

GSK‐3β is a key protein in the complex that blocks the WNT/β‐catenin differentiation pathway. Together with Axin, APC, CK1α proteins, it forms a complex that blocks β‐catenin entry into the cell nucleus, preventing the initiation of cell differentiation (refer to Figure 1) [14, 15].

Theoretical model of scalp cell pathophysiology in molecular terms. It can be observed that an excess of AR in Xq11‐12 mutants causes an increase in GSK‐3β protein in scalp stem cells. GSK‐3β is involved in the recruitment of the β‐catenin blocking complex (AXIN, APC, CK1α and GSK3β). When β‐catenin is blocked by the complex, it cannot reach the cell nucleus, resulting in no change in the molecular pathways of cell function. Consequently, scalp stem cells fail to differentiate into hair follicle cells, leading to insufficient or no hair growth. This is consistent with the miniaturisation theory specific to AGA.

This is consistent with the hair miniaturisation model. As a result of the shortening of the growth period (anagen) in favour of a prolonged period of hair death (catagen), the hair becomes weaker and the hairline regresses. This is observed in the Hamilton‐Norwood scale in male patients. New hair growth ceases at a certain point due to a lack of differentiation of scalp stem cells into follicle cells, which are responsible for hair formation.

EDA2R and Apoptotic Mechanisms

Mutation of the ectodysplasin A2 receptor gene (EDA2R/XEDAR), as evidenced by SNP‐rs1352015, which is situated 8 kbp downstream from this gene, is a characteristic feature of AGA [16]. Studies that have defined the expression of the EDA2R in AGA have demonstrated an increase in the concentration of this protein within dermal papillae and hair follicles [17].

The function of EDA2R is crucial for hair development and its correct formation. This receptor is a member of the TNF family and induces two key pathways of cellular metabolism: Nf‐κB and JNK. Activation of the EDA2R receptor occurs when it attaches to its N‐terminus, the EDA‐A2 ligand on the outer side of the cell. This is followed by attachment to the C‐terminus on the inner side of TRAF3 and TRAF6, initiating signal transduction [18].

Signal transduction for the JNK pathway leads to the formation of c‐Jun. c‐Jun Nuclear Kinases (JNKs) stimulate apoptosis by several key mechanisms, including the phosphorylation of the H2AX protein, which inactivates it and leads to DNA fragmentation, initiating downstream apoptotic mechanisms. JNK also leads to the phosphorylation of a Bax‐inactivating complex called 14‐3‐3 proteins. Phosphorylation by JNK of certain Bax‐related 14‐3‐3 isoforms leads to the dissociation of Bax from 14‐3‐3. It has been suggested that JNK may also phosphorylate anti‐apoptotic proteins such as Bcl2, Bcl‐xl and Mcl‐1 inactivating them and stimulating apoptosis. Consequently, apoptotic products of the JNK pathway induce mitochondrial‐derived apoptosis [19].

Stimulated via TNF receptors, the Nf‐κB pathway is activated by a cascade of ubiquitination of blocking subunits. Phosphorylation of IKKβ ultimately leads to the release of the Nf‐κB dimer from IκBα. The effect of this signal transduction is to translocate p50 and p65 to the nucleus where they function as transcription factors [20]. This is consistent with mouse models that have demonstrated the essential role of a functional Nf‐κB pathway in normal development and maintenance of mouse fur hair structure. Studies have indicated that EDAR receptors are master regulators of hair follicles (HF) [21]. Both the Nf‐κB and JNK pathways induce altered gene expression. The transcription factors p50 and p65 of the Nf‐κB pathway, and the transcription factor AP‐1, which is activated by the JNK pathway via the protein c‐JUN, are both involved in the regulation of gene expression.

Researchers have identified a correlation between an excess of c‐Jun resulting from an increased EDA2R population and an AGA profile [16]. The region of these couplings targets the androgen receptor. Exogenous and endogenous c‐Jun show activation processes towards the AR, reducing its silencing and leading to activation [22]. This is consistent with the relevance of AR in AGA and its overactivation leading to inhibition of the WNT/β‐catenin pathway.

Concurrently, a correlation is observed between an increase in the EDA2R (XEDAR) population and an increase in DKK‐1 protein concentration in dermal papillae and within the hair follicle. The coupling to EDA2R of the EDA‐A2 ligand leads to an increase in DKK‐1 protein concentration within dermal papillae (DP), which affects the hair cycle [17]. DKK‐1 induces endocytosis of the WNT‐binding extracellular element (LRP5/6), thereby leading to inhibition of the WNT/β‐catenin differentiation pathway [23].

The conclusions drawn from observations of changes in DKK‐1 concentrations in dermal papillae presented by the authors cited above correlate with mouse models of the DKK‐1‐treated hair cycle [24].

Studies have demonstrated that the introduction of rhDKK‐1 (recombinant human DKK‐1) into the skin of mice induces the entry of the hair follicle cycle into a catagen state. Conversely, the use of anti‐DKK‐1 antibodies has been shown to reverse this process. These results, in conjunction with the observation of an increase in mRNA levels for DKK‐1 in late anagen, suggest that this molecule initiates the hair follicle transition to catagen [24].

The effect of DKK‐1 does not only relate to endocytosis of the LRP5/6 receptor. RhDKK‐1 leads to significant changes in the Bcl‐2 protein by inhibiting its anti‐apoptotic capacity. At the same time, it leads to an increase in the Bax protein, directing keratinocytes in the ORS (Outer Root Sheath) towards apoptosis [24].

Recent studies have demonstrated that the LRP receptor‐blocking protein DKK‐2 is involved in the pathophysiology of AGA. Furthermore, the gene for DKK‐2 is upregulated in patients with AGA (refer to Figure 2) [12].

EDA2R—molecular correlations. The ligand for the EDA2R receptor is the EDA‐A2 molecule. Activation of the EDA2R receptor translates into an increase in DKK‐1 levels contributing to endocytosis of the LRP5/6 receptor (leading to inhibition of the WNT/β‐catenin differentiation pathway), an increase in BAX protein (promoting apoptosis). The excess of c‐Jun observed in EDA2R activation leads to androgen receptor stimulation (stimulating inhibition of the WNT/β‐catenin pathway), increase in BAX protein (promoting apoptosis), increase in AP‐1 transcription factor (promoting gene expression of hair follicle development, among other things). The EDA2R‐coupled Nf‐κB pathway through transcription factors (p50 and p65) enables normal hair development.

HDAC9 and the Inflammatory Hypothesis

In recent years, research into AGA has seen a return to the concept of a pro‐inflammatory aetiology of androgenetic alopecia. While observations of inflammation within the hair follicle are not common, AGA is generally considered a non‐inflammatory condition. A recent large‐scale analysis conducted in 2022 revealed that 87.9% of a cohort of 58 patients diagnosed with AGA exhibited mild inflammation within the affected skin. Additionally, 50 patients exhibited inflammation within the miniaturised hair follicles [25].

It is crucial to acknowledge that studies on such a limited number of subjects do not permit the conclusion that inflammation is the primary pathway for AGA formation. Nevertheless, these findings compel us to consider the inflammatory pathway in AGA with greater attention. The discovery in 2011 of a correlation between androgenetic alopecia and mutation of the histone deacetylase 9 (HDAC9) gene further underscores the relevance of inflammatory pathways in AGA. The discovered SNP‐rs756853, and a 6 kb downstream SNP‐rs2249817, were identified in intron fragments of the HDAC9 gene in individuals affected by AGA [13, 26].

The histone deacetylase 9 gene is a representative of class IIa of NAD + ‐dependent histone deacetylases, which are capable of translocating between the cell nucleus and the cytoplasm. HDAC9 is involved in inhibiting pro‐inflammatory responses by reducing gene expression as a result of promoting higher levels of DNA condensation on histones [27].

It has been postulated that elevated HDAC9 expression in hair follicle keratinocytes impedes tolerogenic processes, thereby promoting the occurrence of secondary inflammatory reactions. Conversely, a study demonstrated that Treg lymphocyte knockout within hair follicles contributes to inflammation [28, 29].

The involvement of inflammatory processes and the HDAC9 gene in the pathogenesis of AGA is not well‐documented. This topic requires continued research. The post‐inflammatory theory may provide a breakthrough in the understanding of androgenetic alopecia and its treatment.

PAX1/FOXA2 and Developmental Pathways

The androgenetic alopecia‐specific SNP‐rs1160312, which has been identified on chromosome 20p11.22 between the PAX1 and FOX A2 genes, is situated at a distance of 350 kb. This SNP is a marker for mutations in the PAX1 and FOX A2 genes, specifically those associated with AGA. Unfortunately, these mutations have not been significantly described or studied. However, there is a correlation between them and a mutation in the AR gene, which is seen in Irish, Danish and British populations. In men affected by androgenetic alopecia, this correlation is observed in 14% of cases, while in women it is observed in 13.7% of cases [30].

In addition to PAX1 and FOX A2 mutations, a SNP locus, rs1998076, has been identified on chromosome 20. This locus occurs in three alleles: AA, AG and GG. AA indicates a severe variant of androgenetic alopecia, AG represents a mild form, and GG is a wild allele that characterises non‐AGA sufferers [31].

The PAX1 gene is a member of the gene family involved in fetal development, and its mutation is described as up‐regulated. In contrast, the FOX gene family is most likely involved in inflammatory regulation and is considered a down‐regulated mutation. Fetal development genes are not currently affected post factum. This also applies to the HOXB13 gene, whose mutation has also been reported in AGA [12].

Diagnostics

The significant progress in understanding the genetics and molecular basis of androgenetic alopecia outlined above brings us closer to discovering more effective treatments. However, in order to properly tailor the therapy, it is essential to correctly diagnose the problem, which cannot be based only on the doctor's naked eye opinion.

Genetic testing will make it possible to identify the correct cause of the problem. This is because the mechanism of blocking differentiation is different in the AR mutation and in EDA2R. Conversely, the hypothesis of a pro‐inflammatory aetiology of AGA, which appears to be supported by the HDAC9 mutation, necessitates a distinct approach to treatment. Long‐standing developments in dermatoscopy permit the differentiation between various forms of alopecia, including AGA and AA [32]. It is therefore plausible that, following appropriate population‐based analyses, dermatoscopy may also distinguish the visual characteristics of individual AGA mutations. Or at least the inflammatory from the non‐inflammatory subtype. For the time being, genetic testing for specific single nucleotide polymorphisms may be necessary in order to effectively diagnose patients with androgenetic alopecia.

A Theoretical Model for the Use of STEAP3 Protein in the Treatment of AGA

To effectively address androgenetic alopecia in the subtype with AR gene mutation, the differentiation pathway of scalp stem cells into hair follicle cells needs to be activated. However, the use of finasteride and dutasteride to block 5α‐reductase may be associated with clinically significant sexual dysfunction [8]. It is imperative to introduce therapies that directly target the WNT/β‐catenin pathway, which is inhibited by AR gene mutations. We are speculating that the STEAP3 protein could be used to nullify GSK‐3β overproduction. STEAP3 is a metalloreductase that converts intracellular Fe3+ ions into Fe2+, which promotes phosphorylation of GSK‐3β, leading to its inactivation and breakdown of the β‐catenin blocking complex. This ability of the STEAP3 protein was discovered during studies on hypoxia‐induced differentiation of cancer cells. Furthermore, STEAP3‐AS1 (lncRNA) has also been shown to be a transcription factor for the STEAP3 gene and also contributes to the activation of the WNT/β‐catenin pathway [15, 33].

Both STEAP3 and STEAP3‐AS1 may become promising therapeutics for the treatment of AGA, but these findings need to be confirmed in laboratory studies. The therapeutic potential of Fe2+ ions, whose mobilisation by STEAP3 directly affects GSK‐3β inactivation and stimulation of the WNT/β‐catenin pathway, cannot be excluded either (refer to Figure 3).

Theoretical model of a new therapy for androgenetic alopecia. The long non‐coding RNA STEAP3‐AS1 facilitates transcription of the STEAP3 protein, which is involved in cellular mechanisms that respond to hypoxia. This protein has potential for treating AGA. STEAP3 is a metalloreductase that converts Fe3+ to Fe2+. An increase in intracellular Fe2+concentration promotes phosphorylation of the GSK3β protein, causing it to dissociate from the β‐catenin blocking complex. As a result, the remaining elements of the blocking complex also disassociate. This releases β‐catenin, which can then enter the nucleus and activate the WNT/β‐catenin differentiation pathway.

Therapeutic Perspectives for EDA2R Mutations

The EDA2R mutation, like the AR mutation, has a limiting effect on the WNT/β‐catenin differentiation pathway. However, this inhibition is guided from the protein levels of DKK‐1 and c‐Jun.

STEAP3 is likely to counterbalance the mobilising effect of c‐Jun on AR that occurs in the mutation in question.

In order to eliminate the DKK‐1 protein and restore the membrane position of the LRP5/6 receptor, anti‐DKK‐1 antibodies may be necessary, which have effectively fulfilled their role in studies to understand the function and effect of DKK‐1 on hair follicles. However, the cost of antibody therapy may prove prohibitive. Dorsomorphin, a novel DKK‐1 inhibitor used in the treatment of breast cancer, may be the answer [34].

It can be reasonably assumed that by blocking DKK‐1 with Dorsomorphine, the pro‐apoptotic action of DKK‐1 can be inhibited. However, this deviation of the NF‐κB and JNK pathways into the apoptotic pathway requires more precise methods than blocking the EDA2R receptor.

Microneedling—Potential Route to Enhance the Effects of STEAP3 Therapy

Microneedling is a relatively novel method. Studies have indicated that scalp pricking per se is correlated with an increase in fibroblast and epidermal growth factors [35], and may affect the activation of the WNT/β‐catenin pathway through increased WNT protein expression [36]. This may represent a kind of compensatory mechanism for the negative effect of DKK‐1.

Although microneedling affects the molecular functioning of the scalp, it is primarily a method to facilitate the introduction of hair growth‐promoting drugs. The wide spectrum of needle types used in the microneedling method allows for the intensification of the point expression of the drug, thus enhancing its efficacy [37].

The use of dissolving needles for micropuncture of the scalp appears to be an effective method of drug introduction, with the potential for precise delivery. This method has been demonstrated to be efficacious in the introduction of PROTAC [37] and other drugs, including finasteride [38] and minoxidil [39]. This method can also be adapted for the insertion of STEAP3 protein, which has the potential to make it even more accessible and effective for the treatment of AGA.

The stimulatory effect of STEAP3 protein on the WNT/β‐catenin pathway and the upregulation of WNT and growth factor expression as a result of microneedling are likely to be of considerable benefit in the treatment of androgenetic alopecia.

Conclusion

The discoveries of genetics and the analyses of the molecular pathways causing AGA have shown that the current therapeutic approach is flawed. The drugs currently available are either ineffective or have a negative impact on the patient. The numerous cited researchers have prompted us to change our approach in the treatment of androgenetic alopecia. In order to obtain more favourable outcomes and to counteract the disease, it is necessary to implement genetic diagnostics to determine the subtype and to act on the molecular pathways so as not to upset the balance in other parts of the body.

At a theoretical level, STEAP3 and STEAP3‐AS1 appear to be an effective pathway to cure AGA in the AR subtype. STEAP3‐assisted dorsomorphine may also be an effective treatment for EDA2R subtype. At this stage, these are merely theoretical speculations, which appear promising on paper. However, laboratory studies and further development are necessary to verify their therapeutic efficacy. The concept of a pro‐inflammatory aetiology of AGA, which is experiencing a renaissance, opens our eyes to the broad spectrum of causes of the condition. Many times forgotten and brought back to life in history, it could be a spectacular field for future research. It is important to acknowledge that the therapeutic concepts outlined in this text are merely a theoretical model. These findings have not been substantiated by laboratory tests. The objective of this study was to propose an innovative approach to the advancement of medicine. In order to confirm these considerations, it is necessary to conduct in vitro and in vivo tests, as well as clinical trials of the proposed substances. Furthermore, we advocate for additional investigation into the correlation between inflammation, AGA, and the HDAC9 mutation. In order to explore this topic, it is necessary to determine the percentage of this pathomechanism in the AGA patient population and to validate antibiotic and molecular treatment in the form of HDAC inhibitors.

The multiplicity of mechanisms, genes and subtypes in AGA, coupled with a number of as yet undiscovered or misunderstood elements, leads us to the conclusion that the problem is much more complicated than previously thought. While the treatment of androgenetic alopecia and other types of alopecia is considered the domain of aesthetic medicine, it is important to recognise the tragic consequences of this condition. The significant psychological burden and the visible impact on the lives of young men in particular provide compelling evidence of the need to identify effective treatments. This is because AGA is no longer just an aesthetic problem but a disease that affects the whole man, encompassing both his skin and his psyche. As physicians and scientists, we have a moral obligation to identify the genetic causes of baldness and to develop solutions that can help people who are currently without effective therapeutic alternatives.
