Full Visualization of Contact Endoscopy for Basal Ganglia Hemorrhage: A Multicenter Retrospective Study Comparing With Microscopy and Neuroendoscopy
Authors: Shaofei Ni, Aojie Duan, Jiachen Liu, Yanni Zheng, Yubo Ren, Renjie Shou, Fei Wang, Zixuan Wang, Rui Huang, Li Zhang, Shan Gao, Linqiang Zhou, Qiang Zhou, Yanfei Liu, Weiguo Tan, Xigang Yan, Zhouqing Chen, Dengfeng Lu, Boyu Yang, Jing Wang, Xiaoou Sun, Zhong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Basal ganglia hemorrhage (BGH) is a severe subtype of spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage. Surgical intervention remains a critical strategy for reducing hematoma volume, alleviating mass effect, and improving clinical outcomes. This study compared fully visualized contact endoscopy with microscopy and standard neuroendoscopy for BGH treatment.METHODS:A retrospective, multicenter, observational cohort study was conducted on patients undergoing surgical evacuation for BGH (n=335) at 8 tertiary medical centers in Suzhou from January 2021 to May 2024, stratified into microscopy (n=217), neuroendoscopy (n=76), and contact endoscopy (n=42) groups. Surgical outcomes and prognostic indicators were systematically evaluated across groups, with multivariable analysis conducted to independently assess the impact of surgical methods on long-term prognosis.RESULTS:Both endoscopic groups showed significantly shorter operation times, reduced blood loss, and shorter intensive care unit stays than the microscopy group (P<0.05), with no difference between endoscopic techniques. Endoscopy was associated with lower intracranial infection rates (P<0.0167). There were no significant differences in hematoma evacuation rates, neurological scores at 7 days, or functional outcomes (both Glasgow Outcome Scale scores and Karnofsky Performance Status scores) at 3 months. Multivariable analysis identified older age, hypertension, and larger hematoma volume, but not surgical technique, as independent predictors of poor long-term outcome.CONCLUSIONS:Microscopy, neuroendoscopy, and contact endoscopy all demonstrated effectiveness for hematoma evacuation and life preservation for BGH. Endoscopic approaches may offer superior perioperative safety profiles without significant long-term outcome differences. Contact endoscopy is a safe and feasible alternative for BGH surgery.

CLINICAL PERSPECTIVE

What Is New?

•This multicenter retrospective study is the first to compare fully visualized contact endoscopy with both microscopy and standard neuroendoscopy for basal ganglia hemorrhage evacuation, demonstrating that contact endoscopy eliminates blind puncture by providing real-time visualization along the entire working channel, thereby enabling precise trajectory adjustment to avoid critical vessels and normal brain tissue.

This multicenter retrospective study is the first to compare fully visualized contact endoscopy with both microscopy and standard neuroendoscopy for basal ganglia hemorrhage evacuation, demonstrating that contact endoscopy eliminates blind puncture by providing real-time visualization along the entire working channel, thereby enabling precise trajectory adjustment to avoid critical vessels and normal brain tissue.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

•Contact endoscopy achieves comparable hematoma evacuation rates and 3‑month functional outcomes to microscopy and standard neuroendoscopy while offering significantly shorter operative time, less blood loss, and lower intracranial infection rates, establishing it as a safe, feasible, and minimally invasive alternative, particularly for centers without access to neuronavigation or surgical robots.

Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a devastating acute cerebrovascular disease with high mortality and disability.1–3 Hypertensive ICH most commonly affects the basal ganglia, accounting for ≈70% of cases, posing a major public health challenge4,5

For selected patients, particularly those with large hematomas (>30 mL), deteriorating neurological status or signs of mass effect-microsurgical hematoma evacuation may offer clinical benefit by preventing brain herniation, controlling intracranial hypertension, and reducing the impact of blood products on surrounding brain tissue.2,6,7 However, the substantial trauma involved, including scalp incisions and brain tissue retraction required for surgical exposure, along with elevated risks of complications such as intracranial infection, cannot be overlooked.8 Recent meta-analyses have confirmed that neuroendoscopic evacuation of intracerebral hematomas is a safe, effective, minimally invasive technique with satisfactory hematoma clearance and fewer complications, also showing certain advantages in improving neurological function.5,9–11 However, the percutaneous puncture procedure for establishing a drainage channel during surgery is performed blindly, carrying risks of damaging critical structures or bleeding. In addition, it demands high technical proficiency and presents a steep learning curve. The contact endoscope used in this study (Figure 1) is a novel minimally invasive endoscopic technique. By providing visualization throughout the entire puncture process, it assists surgeons in precisely locating hematomas while avoiding critical anatomic structures. Compared with conventional neuroendoscopic hematoma evacuation, contact endoscopy frees surgeons from reliance on large equipment (eg, surgical navigation systems and surgical robots), significantly shortens preoperative preparation time, enables early intervention for intracranial hematomas, and lowers the threshold for learning. Consequently, it may demonstrate advantages in avoiding damage to critical structures, reducing intraoperative bleeding, improving hematoma evacuation rates, and enhancing patient outcomes. Research on contact endoscopic surgery for basal ganglia hemorrhage (BGH) remains relatively scarce. This study compares evacuation of BGH with microscopy, neuroendoscopy, and contact endoscopy to investigate the safety and efficacy of contact endoscopic surgery for BGH, providing guidance for selecting surgical options for BGH in clinical practice.

Assembly of the contact endoscope, including the endoscope camera, visualization guide tube, transparent endoscope sleeve, sleeve core, and miniature light source camera system.

The null hypothesis of this study was that there is no significant difference in long-term functional outcomes (as measured by Glasgow Outcome Scale [GOS] and Karnofsky Performance Status [KPS] at 3 months) between microscopy, neuroendoscopy, and contact endoscopy for BGH evacuation. These outcome measures were selected because they are widely used and validated in stroke and ICH research, providing complementary assessments of global functional status and daily living capacity.12–14

Subjects and Data Collection

A retrospective analysis was conducted on data from 335 patients with BGH who underwent surgical treatment at 8 tertiary hospitals in the Suzhou region between January 2021 and May 2024. The participating hospitals included the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, the Affiliated Zhangjiagang Hospital of Soochow University, Suzhou Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine, the People’s Hospital of SND, Wuzhong District People’s Hospital, Xiangcheng District People’s Hospital, Suzhou Kowloon Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiaotong University School of Medicine, and Kunshan Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine. This multicenter retrospective study was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (No. 2025499). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, informed consent was waived. All applicable data protection regulations were followed to ensure patient confidentiality. The treatment protocol for all patients included in this study was designed in accordance with the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Cerebral Hemorrhage (2019). The selection of surgical approach is not randomized but determined collaboratively by each center’s multidisciplinary team based on the patient’s specific condition, family preferences, and surgeon preference. Patients were categorized into 3 groups based on the surgical approach: microscope group (217 cases), neuroendoscope group (76 cases), and contact endoscope group (42 cases). Data for the microscope and neuroendoscope groups were collected from January 2021 to December 2023. The contact endoscope (KeZhong, Changsha) was first utilized in the Suzhou region in June 2023; thus, data for this group were collected from June 2023 to May 2024. Inclusion criteria were (1) diagnosis of BGH confirmed by cranial computed tomography (CT), with or without ventricular rupture; (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) preoperative Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score ≥6 points; (4) hematoma volume ≥30 mL; and (5) surgery performed within 24 hours of symptom onset. Exclusion criteria were (1) secondary ICH due to vascular malformations or aneurysms; (2) established brain herniation; (3) severe coagulopathy or use of anticoagulant medications; (4) GCS score <6 points or deep coma; (5) critical condition upon admission with severe dysfunction of vital organs such as the heart, liver, or kidneys; and (6) a history of central nervous system disorders, such as traumatic brain injury, cerebral infarction, cerebrovascular diseases, or brain tumors, or those requiring external ventricular drainage for hydrocephalus before the current surgery. Patients lost to follow-up were excluded. Baseline data collected for all 3 groups included sex, age, history of hypertension, hemorrhage location, whether it had broken into the ventricles, preoperative level of consciousness impairment, GCS score, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, and hematoma volume.

Surgical Technique

The microscope group performed a traditional craniotomy, with the surgical approach determined by preoperative head CT imaging. A question mark or arc incision was made, and the skull was milled open. The size of the bone flap was determined by the surgeon. The dura mater is incised, and the hematoma is removed under the microscope by transcortical fistula or by separating the lateral fissure through the insula to the hematoma cavity. Electrocoagulation stops the hemorrhage, and the bone flap is repositioned to close the cranium. After the hematoma has been removed, if brain tissue swelling and elevation persist, the bone flap is removed to reduce intracranial pressure. Whether a drainage tube or an intracranial pressure probe is inserted during surgery is determined by the surgeon based on the intraoperative situation.

Postoperative Management

Following surgery, patients were transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) or a general ward. Postoperative head CT scans were performed within 6 hours and compared with preoperative scans to assess residual hematoma volume, hematoma clearance rate, and the presence of new hemorrhage. Neurological status, pupillary response, and vital signs were monitored. Treatment included oxygen administration, airway protection, timely suctioning, blood pressure control, hemostasis, neurotrophic agents, and prevention of complications. Postoperatively, systolic blood pressure was maintained at ≈140 mm Hg. For patients with an intracranial pressure monitor, intracranial pressure was monitored postoperatively to guide the use of dehydrating agents. Patients with impaired consciousness or difficulty clearing secretions required endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation, airway humidification, protective lung ventilation strategies, and prevention of ventilator-associated pneumonia. Early extubation was attempted as soon as possible. Tracheostomy was considered if extubation was not anticipated in the near term. Antibiotic therapy was initiated for patients with pulmonary infections, as indicated by chest CT findings, with monitoring of inflammatory markers and sputum culture to guide antibiotic selection. According to the Chinese Guidelines for Early Stroke Rehabilitation, rehabilitation therapies, such as acupuncture and hyperbaric oxygen therapy, were initiated early after stabilization.

Clinical Parameters and Definition of Complications

The following parameters were recorded for each group: the operation time, hematoma clearance rate, satisfactory hematoma clearance rate, intraoperative blood loss, degree of consciousness impairment at 7 days postoperatively, GCS score, NIHSS score, and length of stay in the ICU. Hematoma volume was calculated using 3D-Slicer software, and hemorrhage into the ventricle was not included. Hematoma clearance rate=(preoperative CT hematoma volume−postoperative follow-up CT hematoma volume)/preoperative hematoma volume×100%. Satisfactory hematoma clearance rate=number of patients with satisfactory hematoma clearance (number of patients with hematoma clearance rate ≥80%)/total number of patients×100%.

Postoperative complications included mortality, rebleeding, puncture site hemorrhage, cerebral infarction, hydrocephalus, pulmonary infection, deep vein thrombosis, gastrointestinal bleeding, epilepsy, and intracranial infection. Postoperative rebleeding is defined as a newly emerged or significantly enlarged hematoma at the surgical site or along the trajectory, confirmed by a postoperative CT scan within 72 hours after surgery. Cerebral infarction was defined as a newly developed, nonhemorrhagic hypodensity on follow-up CT or magnetic resonance imaging, located in a vascular territory distinct from the surgical hematoma cavity, and associated with corresponding neurological deterioration. Hydrocephalus is defined as ventricular enlargement observed on postoperative CT, with an Evans index >0.3. The Evans index is calculated as the ratio of the maximum width between the frontal horns of the lateral ventricles to the maximum internal transverse diameter of the skull on the same axial slice. The diagnosis of intracranial infection is made if criteria a, b, and c are all met or if criterion d alone is met: (a) persistent postoperative high fever in the patient; (b) presence of meningeal signs such as neck stiffness, Kernig sign, or Brudzinski sign; (c) significant elevation of white blood cell count in blood tests or findings in routine cerebrospinal fluid analysis including turbid cerebrospinal fluid with white blood cell count >10×109/L and a white blood cell to red blood cell ratio >500; and (d) positive bacteriologic examination of cerebrospinal fluid, with the same bacterium identified in 2 consecutive cultures. All the above complications were monitored during the postoperative hospital stay and throughout the 3-month follow-up period.

The initial identification of complications was based on diagnoses explicitly documented in the patients’ original medical records (eg, radiology reports, discharge summaries, and progress notes). To ensure consistency and accuracy across all 8 centers, we implemented a 2-step adjudication process. First, centralized review: all suspected complication events were reevaluated by our core research team. This involved a blinded review of the relevant imaging scans (CT/magnetic resonance imaging) and clinical notes against the prespecified, standardized definitions listed above. Second, consensus decision: any ambiguous cases (eg, small, asymptomatic hypodensity of uncertain significance) were discussed by a dedicated clinical event adjudication committee consisting of 3 independent senior neurosurgeons not involved in the original surgeries. A final diagnosis was made by consensus based on the predefined criteria. This procedure was designed to minimize diagnostic variability and ensure that all reported complications met our uniform study definitions.

The GOS and KPS scores were used to assess clinical outcomes at 3 months postoperatively, enabling a more comprehensive and sensitive evaluation of the impact of different surgical approaches on patients’ functional prognosis.12–14 The GOS favorable outcome rate was calculated as the number of patients with a favorable GOS score (scores of 4 and 5)/total number of patients×100%. The KPS favorable outcome rate was calculated as the number of patients with a favorable KPS score (scores of 60, 70, 80, 90, and 100)/total number of patients×100%.

Multivariable Analysis

To independently assess the impact of the surgical approach on long-term functional outcome while adjusting for potential baseline confounders, we performed 2 separate multivariable logistic regression analyses. The primary outcomes were poor functional outcome defined as a GOS score of <4 at 3 months postoperatively or a KPS score of <60 at 3 months.

Covariates for adjustment were selected a priori based on their established clinical relevance as prognostic factors in ICH, irrespective of their univariate significance. The models adjusted for surgical approach (categorical), sex (categorical), age (continuous), side of hemorrhage (categorical), intraventricular extension (categorical), history of hypertension (categorical), history of diabetes (categorical), preoperative GCS score (continuous), preoperative NIHSS score (continuous), preoperative hematoma volume (continuous), time from symptom onset to surgery (continuous), and midline shift on CT (continuous).

We excluded intraoperative variables (eg, operation time and blood loss) and postoperative complications from the primary multivariable model because these are not baseline predictors and may lie on the causal pathway between surgical approach and functional outcome, potentially introducing overadjustment bias. All selected covariates had complete data; therefore, no missing data imputation was required. The results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with their corresponding 95% CIs. Prespecified sensitivity analyses stratified by intraventricular extension and baseline GCS score (using a cutoff of 9) were performed as exploratory analyses. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2).

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 software. Categorical data are presented as n (%), and intergroup comparisons were performed using the χ2 or Fisher exact test. Metric clinical data were assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (sample size >50) or the Shapiro-Wilk test (sample size <50). Normally distributed data were analyzed using ANOVA, while nonnormally distributed data were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Ranked data were analyzed using the rank-sum test. For comparisons among the 3 groups, we first performed omnibus tests (the ANOVA/Kruskal-Wallis H test for continuous data and the χ2/Fisher exact test for categorical data) to compare all 3 groups simultaneously. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05. When the omnibus test was significant, pairwise comparisons were conducted. For metric data, the Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons. For categorical data, the adjusted significance level was set at 0.0167.

Multivariable Analysis of the Long-Term Outcomes

Due to potential imbalances in baseline characteristics among patient groups, multivariable analysis was performed to independently assess the impact of surgical approach on long-term outcomes.

To identify independent predictors of long-term functional outcomes following hematoma evacuation in the basal ganglia region, we conducted 2 separate multivariable logistic regression analyses. The models adjusted for key prognostic covariates, including surgical approach, sex, age, hemorrhage side, ventricular spread, hypertension, diabetes, preoperative level of consciousness, preoperative GCS score, preoperative NIHSS score, preoperative hematoma volume, time from symptom onset to surgery, and midline shift on CT.

In the first model, defined as poor prognosis with a GOS <4 at 3 months postoperatively, the analysis revealed the following: advanced age (OR, 1.08 [95% CI, 1.06–1.11]; P<0.001), history of hypertension (OR, 3.09 [95% CI, 1.28–8.02]; P=0.015), and larger preoperative hematoma volume (OR, 1.02 [95% CI, 1.01–1.04]; P=0.008) were identified as significant independent risk factors associated with increased risk of poor functional outcome (Figure 4A).

Discussion

Currently, several surgical options are available for treating ICH. The most common surgical procedures for removing hematomas include microsurgical, neuroendoscopic, and stereotactic puncture drainage. Each surgical approach has its own advantages and disadvantages. First, traditional microsurgical hematoma evacuation provides adequate exposure of the surgical field and often achieves satisfactory hematoma removal, but it involves prolonged craniotomy time, significant trauma, and a high incidence of complications such as intracranial infection.15 For patients with superficial hematomas, massive bleeding, or supratentorial hematomas complicated by brain herniation, craniotomy for hematoma evacuation may be considered.16,17 Microscopes achieve image magnification through the refraction of light via a combination of lenses. During deep hematoma removal surgery, insufficient light and poor microscope imaging often occur due to obstruction by surrounding brain tissue. Due to blind spots in the field of view, it is often necessary to retract normal brain tissue to obtain a clear view of the entire hematoma cavity, which carries the risk of exacerbating brain damage.17 Second, neuroendoscopic surgery requires only a 2- to 3-cm bone window, resulting in shorter surgical times. The endoscopic light source provides a clear view of deep surgical sites, facilitating thorough removal of deep hematomas and improving hematoma clearance rates. In addition, there is minimal brain tissue stretch injury during surgery, resulting in overall less trauma compared with traditional craniotomy. However, the endoscopic operative channel restricts instrument maneuverability and the surgical toolset, which may complicate the management of vigorous bleeding from robust vessels. The ability to achieve definitive hemostasis under endoscopy can be challenging compared with the bimanual microsurgical technique with sucker and bipolar forceps. The steep learning curve of this procedure and the high cost of endoscopic equipment have limited its adoption in primary care hospitals to some extent. Third, hematoma drilling and drainage often rely on CT or stereotactic guidance to precisely locate the hematomas. This procedure requires smaller bone holes and shorter surgery times, and can even be performed under local anesthesia, making it suitable for elderly patients, those with poor coagulation function, or small blood clots in deep brain regions. Preoperative imaging processing and surgical positioning are often required, as blind puncture carries a significant risk of vascular injury. In addition, prolonged tube placement time may also increase the risk of intracranial infection. Some studies have shown that compared with traditional craniotomy, neuroendoscopic surgery can reduce operative time and blood loss, shorten ICU hospital stays, improve hematoma clearance rate, and enhance patients’ good prognosis.18–22

Neuroendoscopic hematoma evacuation involves 2 main stages: establishing an endoscopic working channel and endoscopic hematoma evacuation. Previously, endoscopic hematoma evacuation under direct visualization could only be performed after successfully establishing the working channel. Methods for establishing the working channel (referred to as Endoport) include using anatomic landmarks on the body surface or, under guidance such as neuro navigation, using a brain puncture needle/endoscopic sheath to reach the hematoma cavity, followed by aspiration with a syringe to confirm whether the needle tip is within the hematoma cavity. Previous research has focused on improving puncture accuracy when establishing a working channel through the use of neuronavigation, robotic arms, ultrasound-assisted positioning, and so on. Techniques such as image fusion and 3-dimensional model–assisted positioning can be used to help accurately guide the direction and depth of the puncture to make the procedure precise. However, these methods always have a certain degree of blindness.

To address the aforementioned limitations, this study introduced a novel technique, contact endoscopy. The core concept of real-time navigation in contact endoscopy represents a paradigm distinct from conventional external neuronavigation. In external navigation, such as frameless stereotaxy, the surgeon relies on preoperative or intermittently updated intraoperative images (eg, CT/magnetic resonance imaging) projected onto a separate screen, creating a virtual map. The surgical instruments are tracked in space, and their position is displayed relative to this static or periodically updated map. This method is excellent for trajectory planning and localizing deep-seated lesions, but it involves a cognitive shift between the surgical field and the navigation screen, and its accuracy can decrease due to brain shift during surgery.

In contrast, contact endoscopy employs an internal or direct-vision navigation principle. The transparent sheath and endoscope provide a continuous, wide, and magnified view of the actual anatomy along the entire working channel from the cortical surface to the hematoma cavity. This constitutes a form of real-time anatomic navigation where blood vessels, critical tracts (like the internal capsule visualized in some cases), and hematoma margins are directly seen and avoided. This method is inherently real-time, integrates visualization and intervention seamlessly, and is immune to brain shift. It is particularly suited for procedures where the target (eg, a hematoma) is relatively large, and the primary risks are vessels and eloquent tissue along a linear path. External navigation remains indispensable for targeting small, deep-seated lesions (eg, biopsies, small tumors, and electrode implantation in deep brain stimulation) or when the surgical corridor lacks distinct visual landmarks.

Compared with traditional neuroendoscopy, the advantages of contact endoscopy include: the use of contact imaging visualization technology, which does not require external light sources to display real-time images. Unlike the concept of structural visualization under auxiliary positioning methods, such as robots, navigation, and 3-dimensional models, this study achieves full visualization throughout the surgical process, including needle insertion, retraction of brain tissue, avoidance of critical blood vessels, reaching the target point in the hematoma cavity, and establishing an endoscopic working channel. This eliminates any blind aspiration confirmation procedures, thereby minimizing accessory damage caused by needle insertion and maximizing protection of brain tissue.

To our knowledge, this is the first multicenter retrospective study on the use of contact endoscopy for the surgical treatment of ICH. The results of this study showed that the operation time and intraoperative blood loss in both the neuroendoscopy group and the contact endoscopy group were lower than those in the microscopy group. This difference may be due to the fact that (1) neuroendoscopy and contact endoscopy require shorter surgical incisions, smaller bone windows, and relatively simpler and shorter surgical procedures during craniotomy and cranial closure; (2) during neuroendoscopy and contact endoscopy, direct puncture into the hematoma cavity is performed through a transparent sheath; compared with the process of entering the hematoma cavity via separation of the lateral fissure or cortical fistulization under a microscope, this method requires less operative time and results in less bleeding; (3) neuroendoscopy and contact endoscopy are performed under direct visualization during surgery; bright and clear surgical field illumination facilitates rapid removal of hematomas, identification of bleeding sites, and swift hemostasis, thereby minimizing the impact on surrounding normal tissues; and (4) the endoscopic working channel exerts minimal tension on normal brain tissue and blood vessels, thereby reducing mechanical damage to brain tissue.22 In contrast, microscopic hematoma evacuation requires the use of a brain retractor to pull brain tissue aside to access the hematoma cavity and excessive stretching may cause brain tissue damage,23 leading to prolonged surgery time and increased bleeding. In addition, based on the above reasons, the neuroendoscopy group and the contact endoscopy group can shorten ICU hospitalization time.10

There was no statistically significant difference in the incidence of puncture site bleeding between the neuroendoscopy group and the contact endoscopy group in this study, with both groups showing low rates of 3.9% and 2.4%, respectively. The reasons for this result may be that (1) the puncture access routes selected for establishing the catheter, such as the midfrontal approach, are avascular regions, and the tip of the puncture sheath is rounded and blunt; (2) bleeding from the puncture site can be observed through the transparent puncture sheath; after the removal of the intracerebral hematoma, a technique involving simultaneous sheath withdrawal and hemostasis can be used, allowing for direct visualization during electrocoagulation or compression hemostasis; and (3) contact endoscopy enables full visualization of the working channel throughout the procedure. Vascular and normal brain tissue damage can be avoided during puncture, thereby effectively preventing bleeding along the puncture site. Compared with neuroendoscopy, this approach significantly reduces the potential risk of bleeding along the puncture site and offers advantages in minimizing vascular and brain tissue damage.

The postoperative intracranial infection rates were lower in both the neuroendoscopy group and the contact endoscopy group compared with the microscopic surgery group. The reason for this difference may be that endoscopic surgery requires a smaller bone window, shorter incisions, and fewer electrocoagulation hemostasis procedures, thereby reducing the exposed area of brain tissue and surgical duration,5 In addition, endoscopic surgery causes less disturbance to brain tissue, thereby reducing the incidence of intracranial infection. There are many factors contributing to intracranial infections following neurosurgical procedures, such as prolonged surgical duration, placement of drainage tubes during surgery, cerebrospinal fluid leaks, and the presence of diabetes, among others. Among these, prolonged surgical duration is the most significant factor.24 Therefore, endoscopic minimally invasive techniques can reduce the incidence of perioperative complications by shortening the duration of surgical intervention.25

The meta-analysis of endoscopic versus craniotomy surgery for ICH pointed out that endoscopic surgery has the advantage of a high hematoma clearance rate,5,10 and the difference in hematoma clearance rate between the 3 groups in this study was not statistically significant: 92.38% in the microscope group, 93.13% in the neuroendoscopy group, and 93.62% in the contact endoscopy group, all of which effectively evacuated the hematoma. The probable reasons affecting the clearance of hematomas could be that (1) the external light source of the microscope is inadequate for illuminating deep surgical fields, affecting hematoma removal; the internal light source of the endoscope can approach deep hematomas and provide a clear surgical field of view, thereby improving hematoma removal rates and (2) the brain-hematoma pressure gradient formed during the removal of hematomas using neuroendoscopy and contact endoscopy forces the hematoma to move automatically into the endoscopic working channel, thereby increasing the hematoma removal rate.26

There were no statistically significant differences in the incidence of postoperative complications other than intracranial infection or in GOS and KPS scores at 3 months postoperatively, among the 3 groups. However, these findings must be interpreted with caution due to important limitations inherent in the study design. The retrospective nature and the marked imbalance in group sizes (microscopy n=217, neuroendoscopy n=76, and contact endoscopy n=41) may have introduced selection bias and, more critically, severely limited statistical power. Post hoc power analyses using the 3-month GOS and KPS favorable outcome rates as primary end points showed that the power for detecting the observed differences was extremely low (≤17.1% for contact endoscopy versus microscopy and ≤8.4% for contact endoscopy versus standard endoscopy). Consequently, the finding of no significant difference in long-term functional outcomes is possibly a false negative, and we cannot definitively conclude that one procedure is superior or inferior to another. Rather, we report observed differences as hypothesis-generating. Sensitivity analyses stratified by intraventricular extension and baseline GCS yielded directionally consistent but statistically nonsignificant effect estimates, with wide CIs reflecting the limited subgroup sample sizes. This study provides essential effect size parameter estimates for future prospective randomized controlled trials with adequately powered sample sizes.

The MISTIE III trial (Minimally Invasive Surgery Plus Rt-PA for ICH Evacuation Phase III) established reducing hematoma volume to ≤15 mL as a key predictor of improved functional outcome, setting a clear efficacy goal for minimally invasive techniques.27,28 Our results align with this principle: a high proportion of patients achieved this target across all groups (neuroendoscopy: 89.47%; contact endoscopy: 95.24%; and microscopy: 92.17%). The numerically highest rate in the contact endoscopy group, supported by its full-channel visualization enabling precise trajectory alignment along the hematoma’s long axis, provides preliminary data for its potential advantage in achieving maximal evacuation.

While the recent ENRICH trial (Early Minimally Invasive Removal of Intracerebral Hemorrhage) demonstrated the benefit of early minimally invasive evacuation for lobar ICH, its BGH subgroup showed no significant advantage.29 In contrast, our study focused on BGH and found high rates of successful deep evacuation, especially in contact endoscopy groups. This suggests that surgical technique may be critical for BGH. The contact endoscopy technique, differing from the ENRICH approach, offers full-channel visualization through a transparent sheath, allowing real-time vascular avoidance and potentially providing a more intuitive pathway to deep basal ganglia hematomas. Our findings offer a positive signal that technical refinement may benefit BGH patients, addressing the clinical hesitation indicated by post-ENRICH surveys.30 Prospective randomized controlled trials directly comparing techniques for BGH are warranted.

It must be explicitly stated that as a multicenter retrospective study, this research inherently possesses several other limitations, except for the sample size. Among these, selection bias represents the most significant and unavoidable confounding factor. The choice of surgical approach is not randomized but determined collaboratively by each center’s multidisciplinary team based on the patient’s specific condition, family preferences, and the operating surgeon’s proficiency and preference for different techniques. Generally, microsurgical approaches may be preferred for critically ill patients with conditions such as brain herniation, while endoscopic surgery may be considered for less urgent cases. This nonrandomized allocation based on clinical judgment represents an inherent limitation of this retrospective study. Despite incorporating multiple indicators, including history of hypertension and diabetes, preoperative level of consciousness impairment along with GCS and NIHSS scores, time from onset to surgery, presence of intraventricular extension, preoperative hematoma volume, and midline shift distance, our baseline data still showed no significant differences in clinical severity among the 3 patient groups, which may help address this concern. Another major limitation is the differing time frames for data collection. Due to its novelty, the contact endoscopy group was enrolled later (2023–2024), whereas the microscope and standard endoscopy groups spanned an earlier period (2021–2023). This introduces potential temporal bias, as overall advances in intensive care management, anesthetic techniques, and perioperative care standards during this interval may have positively influenced outcomes in the later groups. In addition, functional outcomes were assessed only at 3 months postoperatively; longer-term follow-up beyond this timepoint was not performed, leaving potential late recovery differences undetected. Moreover, the sample size for the contact endoscopy group was relatively small, which may have limited the statistical power of this study to detect subtle differences between groups in certain outcome measures. Finally, surgeon experience and learning curves, which could significantly impact outcomes, were not quantitatively analyzed. Despite those limitations, our findings retain significant clinical value. They authentically reflect the current application and outcomes of different surgical techniques in clinical practice. Our conclusion that all 3 techniques are effective treatments, but endoscopic techniques demonstrate superior perioperative outcomes should be interpreted as an observational finding within the current clinical decision-making paradigm. It should be pointed out that the localization of hematomas in all 3 patient groups in this study was based on preoperative CT scans. Regardless of the approach used, the assistance of neuronavigation will be beneficial in ensuring the accuracy of the trajectory. Ultimately, to definitively determine which surgical approach offers superiority, future large-scale, multicenter, prospective randomized controlled trials are required to provide the highest level of evidence-based medical proof.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of a retrospective study, fully visualized contact endoscopy proves to be a feasible and effective surgical technique for the evacuation of BGHs. It demonstrates perioperative benefits over traditional microscopic craniotomy, such as reduced operative time and blood loss, and is noninferior to standard neuroendoscopy in terms of safety, efficacy, and short-to-medium-term functional outcomes. While not demonstrating superiority to established techniques, it presents itself as a valuable additional tool in the neurosurgical armamentarium for ICH. Prospective randomized trials comparing all contemporary minimally invasive techniques are warranted to definitively establish their respective roles.
