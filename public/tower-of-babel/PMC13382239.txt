A lethal form of ASCC3 disease: Severe global developmental delay, axial hypotonia, hypoplasia of corpus callosum, hypothyroidism, and micropenis
Authors: Ginat Narkis, Vadim Dolgin, Sufa Sued-Hendrickson, Juman Zahran, Bibi Kanengisser-Pines, Gil Smooha-Schechter, Lior Carmon, Zamir Shorer, Analia Michaelovsky, Ohad S Birk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryActivating Signal Cointegrator 1 Complex, Subunit 3 (ASCC3) encodes a DNA helicase that is pivotal in transcriptional regulation, DNA damage response, and ribosomal quality control. Bi-allelic ASCC3 variants have been recently associated with a wide spectrum of non-lethal neuromuscular and neurodevelopmental disorders, with no consistent brain malformations or endocrine dysfunction. We describe a severe lethal form of ASCC3 disease: eight individuals of three consanguineous Bedouin families presented with a consistent phenotype of severe global developmental delay, axial hypotonia, and hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, lethal in infancy. Shared dysmorphic features included narrow palpebral fissures, a sunken flat nasal bridge with a short nasal tip and anteverted nares, and low-set ears. Congenital hypothyroidism was evident in most affected individuals, and all males had micropenis/cryptorchidism. Linkage analysis identified a chromosome 6q16.3 locus segregating within the families consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance (maximum multipoint LOD score: 3.1). Whole-exome sequencing followed by segregation analysis identified within this locus a single disease-associated variant: ASCC3 c.3061T>A (GenBank: NM_006828.4) (p.Phe1021Ile). The phenotype, consistent across all affected individuals, significantly broadens the phenotypic spectrum of ASCC3-related disorders, with unique dysmorphism, previously unreported agenesis of the corpus callosum, micropenis/cryptorchidism, congenital hypothyroidism (possibly relevant to ASCC3’s pituitary and thyroid expression and its thyroid hormone receptor interacting protein 4 [TRIP4] interaction), and early lethality.Keywords: ASCC3, developmental delay, monogenic disease, phenotype, corpus callosum, hypothyroidism, hypotonia, micropenis, lethal

Introduction

Global developmental delay is characterized by a combination of delayed motor, cognitive, and linguistic development, as well as aberrant social interactions. The etiology is heterogeneous and is dependent on genetic and environmental factors. Due to this complexity, identifying the causes of this condition has been challenging. Recently, bi-allelic variants in Activating Signal Cointegrator 1 Complex, Subunit 3 (ASCC3) have been reported in individuals presenting with a wide array of non-lethal neurodevelopmental disorders, with no endocrine dysfunction or consistent brain malformations (MIM: 620700, intellectual developmental disorder, autosomal-recessive 81).1,2 We now present 8 individuals of three different Bedouin families sharing a homozygous ASCC3 variant, leading to global developmental delay, hypotonia, micropenis/cryptorchidism, hypothyroidism, agenesis/thinning of corpus callosum, and consistent dysmorphic features, with early lethality.

Subjects, material, and methods

Ethics statement

Soroka Medical Center Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval (#5071G) and written informed consent were obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. The study complied with the principles set out in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical phenotyping

The phenotypic assessment was conducted by senior pediatric neurologists and geneticists. All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, following protocols approved by the Soroka Medical Center IRB (approval #5071G) and the Israel National Committee for Human Genetic Studies (approval #920100319).

Genetic studies

Blood samples were collected from affected and unaffected family members. Genomic DNA was isolated from peripheral blood leukocytes using standard extraction methods. Genome-wide linkage analysis was conducted as previously described, using high-density single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) arrays.3 DNA samples from affected individuals, their parents, and available healthy family members were analyzed using the Illumina Infinium OmniExpress BeadChip, covering over 740,000 SNP loci across the genome. Homozygosity mapping was performed with HomozygosityMapper (http://www.homozygositymapper.org/), assuming an autosomal-recessive inheritance pattern and a shared ancestral mutation. Multipoint LOD scores were calculated using SUPERLINK ONLINE SNP 1.1 software,4 and genomic positions were referenced according to the GRCh37/hg19 genome assembly.

Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed using the xGen Exome Research Panel v.1.0 (IDT). Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed using the Illumina TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Library construction kit. Sequencing was conducted on the NovaSeq 6000 sequencing system (Illumina) with 150 bp paired-end reads. Read alignment and variant calling were performed using the Franklin variant interpretation platform (Genoox) with the human genome assembly hg19 (GRCh37) as the reference. Data were analyzed using the Franklin variant interpretation platform (Genoox) (https://franklin.genoox.com/clinical-db/home) and our in-house-generated Varista online variant analysis platform (https://varista.link/),5 with standard analysis parameters. Variants with high allele frequency in the general population were excluded based on standard databases, including gnomAD, and our in-house database of approximately 500 ethnically matched control subjects. Variants’ validation and segregation analyses were done via Sanger sequencing. The genetic variants were interpreted following ACMG guidelines.6

Results

Clinical data

Eight affected individuals of three consanguineous Bedouin families were studied (Figure 1, families A, B, and C). Of the 8 individuals, two did not reach birth: one was a stillborn baby (fetal death at 37 weeks) and the other a case of termination of pregnancy at 15 weeks. All six live-born affected individuals died in infancy (lethality at 40 h to 14 months). The affected infants presented with severe global developmental delay (hardly achieving any developmental milestones), axial hypotonia, and limb hypertonia. Repeated developmental assessments showed abnormal eye contact, fixation, and tracking, and four of the tested live-born individuals failed newborn hearing screening tests. All 6 live-born individuals had dysmorphic features, mostly narrow palpebral fissures and a sunken flat nasal bridge with a short nasal tip and anteverted nares. Plagiocephaly, posteriorly rotated ears, flat philtrum, and wide mouth with thin lips and a tented upper lip were reported in five. Of note, unaffected family members (parents and siblings) were examined and had none of these dysmorphic features. Brain structural abnormalities, specifically of the corpus callosum, were consistently detected: MRI demonstrated hypoplasia of the corpus callosum in all individuals, ranging from a thin corpus callosum to complete agenesis, with accompanying variable degrees of ventriculomegaly/colpocephaly in four. The absence of the cavum septum pellucidum was seen in two affected individuals, suggesting it is a possible component of the syndrome with variable expression. Pathological EEG patterns were detected in three affected individuals. Congenital hypothyroidism was demonstrated in four. All affected male individuals were diagnosed with genital abnormalities, including micropenis and cryptorchidism. No genital abnormalities were noted in affected females. Testosterone levels measured in one male (III-2, family A, age 3 months) were below detection (<0.2 ng/mL). Cortisol levels (measured in two individuals) were normal. Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) levels measured in 4 affected individuals were elevated. There were no consistent cardiac defects (patent foramen ovale in three, mitral regurgitation/tricuspid regurgitation in two, and septal defects in one affected individual). Similarly, while there were some aberrant ocular findings in 4 affected individuals, none were consistent (note nystagmus in two). A detailed phenotypic description is provided in Table S1.

Identification of an ASCC3 homozygous variant

Assuming autosomal recessive inheritance of founder mutation, linkage analysis of 21 individuals, including 4 affected individuals (family A, III-2 and III-3; family B, IV-2; and family C, IV-6; Figure 1) and 17 unaffected family members (family A, I-1, I-4, II-1, II-2, II-4, II-5, III-4, III-7, and III-8; family B, III-1, III-2, IV-3, IV-5, IV-6, and IV-7; and family C, II-1 and II-2; Figure 1) identified a single homozygous locus spanning 1.7 Mbp on chromosome 6q16.3, shared by all affected individuals (Figure 2A, rs761714–rs1610029). This homozygous haplotype segregated as expected for autosomal-recessive inheritance with a maximal multipoint LOD score of 3.1 between rs761714 and rs1610029 (Figure 2B). The LOD score calculations delineated a single narrow genomic locus as the most likely to be disease-associated. WES analysis was conducted under an autosomal-recessive inheritance model due to the consanguinity in the families. Homozygous variants were filtered based on minor-allele frequency in gnomAD and a local cohort, with common variants excluded. The detailed filtering strategy is described in the subjects, material, and methods section. This analysis revealed a single homozygous variant within the ASCC3 gene shared by three affected individuals (families A, B, and C, Figure 1): ASCC3 c.3061T>A (GenBank: NM_006828.4) (p.Phe1021Ile), chr6:101099450A>T (hg19; GRCh37; ClinVar: SUB16271977). This variant segregated with the disease in the families, with all affected individuals homozygous, all parents heterozygous, and none of the unaffected individuals homozygous for the variant (Sanger sequencing, Figure 2C). The observed variant was not reported in gnomAD or in ethnicity-matched control individuals. It is predicted to be deleterious by commonly used in silico prediction tools (Revel: 0.9 and BayesDel: 0.39). The affected residue, phenylalanine at position 1021, is highly conserved across species down to S. cerevisiae (Figure 2D). Bioinformatic scores utilized to determine the pathogenicity of a variant are given in Table S2. No additional shared homozygous variants were identified within this locus. WGS analysis in one affected individual (family C, II-2; Figure 1) did not identify any additional potentially pathogenic variants shared by all affected individuals.

Discussion

Through combined linkage analysis and next-generation sequencing, we identified a pathogenic variant in ASCC3 causing a consistent early lethal neurological syndrome in three consanguineous Bedouin families. As the variant was found in 3 seemingly unrelated Bedouin Israeli families of Arabian Peninsula ancestry, the variant is likely a founder mutation in that cohort and should be considered for population carrier screening in such cohorts.

ASCC3 encodes a DNA helicase responsible for generating single-stranded DNA as part of the DNA damage response pathway. ASCC3 is a critical component of the activating signal cointegrator 1 (ASC-1) transcriptional coactivator complex. This complex, composed of ASCC1, ASCC2, ASCC3, and thyroid hormone receptor interacting protein 4 (TRIP4), is a multifunctional regulator with pivotal roles in transcriptional coactivation, RNA processing, nuclear receptor modulation, the DNA damage response, and ribosome-associated quality control (RQC). It is thus essential in gene expression regulation, translation surveillance, proper ribosome recycling, translation initiation, and cellular homeostasis.7,8,9,10,11,12

Mutations in various components of this complex have been associated with neurological/neuromuscular disorders: TRIP4 mutations have been associated with a broad spectrum of congenital neuromuscular disorders primarily characterized by proximal muscle weakness, delayed motor development, hypotonia, and respiratory insufficiency in severe cases (MIM: 616866, spinal muscular atrophy with congenital bone fractures 1). Some individuals exhibit a spinal muscular atrophy-like phenotype with progressive muscle atrophy, joint contractures, and scoliosis, while others present sensory-motor polyneuropathy with limb weakness and sensory deficits. Additionally, cerebellar hypoplasia has been reported, leading to ataxia and developmental delays, and some affected individuals develop skeletal abnormalities, including osteoporosis and congenital fractures.13,14,15,16,17,18 Mutations in ASCC1 cause spinal muscular atrophy with congenital bone fractures 2 (SMABF2; MIM: 616867), an autosomal-recessive severe congenital neuromuscular disorder characterized by prenatal-onset hypotonia, joint contractures (arthrogryposis multiplex congenita), congenital bone fractures, and respiratory failure. Most affected infants present with reduced fetal movements, polyhydramnios, and intrauterine growth restriction, leading to perinatal respiratory distress and early mortality. In severe cases, additional findings include hydrops fetalis, cystic hygroma, and skeletal anomalies.19,20,21,22,23

We now report a consistent ASCC3 disease phenotype of severe global developmental delay, hypotonia, congenital hypothyroidism, and hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, culminating in death in infancy. Distinct shared dysmorphic features included narrow palpebral fissures and a sunken flat nasal bridge with a short nasal tip and anteverted nares, as well as low-set ears in some. The phenotype observed in this study is consistent across all affected individuals and significantly broadens the phenotypic spectrum of ASCC3-related disorders, with previously unreported agenesis of the corpus callosum, congenital hypothyroidism, unique dysmorphism, and early lethality.

Few recent reports associated bi-allelic ASCC3 variants with a wide spectrum of mostly non-consistent neuromuscular and neurological phenotypes ranging from mild developmental delay to severe intellectual disability accompanied by profound motor impairments, with hypotonia in ∼50% of the reported individuals.1 Nair et al.1 reported seven individuals (from six unrelated families) with bi-allelic variants in the ASCC3 gene and updates on the one previously reported consanguineous Iranian family with 4 affected individuals.24 All individuals had a neurologic disorder, yet the severity was extremely variable, ranging from severe developmental delay to mere muscle fatigue. Dysmorphic features were mostly non-consistent and were noted in only a few of the reported individuals: an upturned nose, low-set ears, a thin upper lip, and large central incisor front teeth were each noted in at least 2 of the affected families. Prognathism was noted in one affected family. A further recent manuscript delineated 3 unrelated Chinese individuals (ages 1–5 years) with compound heterozygous ASCC3 variants, presenting with global developmental delay, characterized by intellectual disability, motor impairment, language retardation, and hypotonia.2 There was no dysmorphism.2 A slightly thin corpus callosum was noted by MRI in one of the three.2 None of the previous reports noted hypothyroidism.

Notably, six of the eight affected individuals underwent brain imaging; hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, ranging from a thin corpus callosum to complete agenesis, was demonstrated in all six. This is in contrast with previous reports, with brain malformations reported in only one fetus (cerebral and cerebellar hypoplasia) and one infant (cerebellar lobe hypoplasia).1 Our findings in this regard are well in line with the documented expression of ASCC3 in the corpus callosum (Protein Atlas, https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000112249-ASCC3/tissue/Corpus+callosum).

Also of interest, congenital hypothyroidism, not previously reported in individuals with bi-allelic variants in the ASCC3 gene, was noted in 4 of the 6 live-born affected individuals we describe. This is well in line with the evident expression of ASCC3 in the thyroid, mostly in glandular cells (GTEX, https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000112249-ASCC3/tissue/Thyroid+gland). Notably, ASCC3 is part of the ASC-1 complex, which interacts with thyroid hormone receptors through another member of the ASC-1 complex, TRIP4. Furthermore, ASCC3’s function is linked to immune regulation, which might affect the thyroid environment. Alternatively, as ASCC3 is highly expressed in pituitary endocrine cells (Protein Atlas, https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000112249-ASCC3/single+cell), it is plausible that variants in ASCC3 cause pituitary malfunction, culminating in hypothyroidism, micropenis, and low testosterone levels. However, TSH levels were elevated, and cortisol levels were normal.

In terms of lethality, all 6 live-born affected individuals died by the age of 14 months. This is in contrast with the 13 previously reported individuals being in the first decade of life,1 and the 4 Iranian affected family members reported alive even at 37–41 years.24 It was previously suggested that there appeared to be a genotype-phenotype correlation, as the most mildly affected previously reported individual was homozygous for a rare missense variant, while the more severely affected individuals were compound heterozygotes for a missense and a presumed loss-of-function (LoF) variant.1 There were no individuals with bi-allelic presumed LoF variants in their cohort or in gnomAD, and they suggested that the bi-allelic LoF genotype may not be compatible with life.1 It is thus plausible that the p.Phe1021Ile variant we describe has a severe LoF effect. Of note, in contrast to the previously reported mostly unrelated cases compiled through GeneMatcher, our cohort is due to a founder variant with a LOD score > 3 and a consistent phenotype not found in unaffected family members, reflecting the effects of the ASCC3 variant.

The phenotype observed in our study is consistent across all affected individuals and extensively broadens the phenotypic spectrum of ASCC3-related disorders, highlighting consistent dysmorphic features, agenesis of the corpus callosum, and congenital hypothyroidism with micropenis in males as cardinal features and demonstrating consistent severity and early lethality with the reported variant. Our findings emphasize the critical role of ASCC3 in early neurodevelopment (most notably of the corpus callosum) and neuromuscular function and elucidate its significant role in endocrinology. These findings contribute to the growing understanding of ASC-1 complex dysfunction and reinforce the importance of advanced genetic screening in undiagnosed neurodevelopmental disorders. Further functional studies are required to elucidate the molecular mechanisms driving this phenotype and to establish the precise role of ASCC3 in embryonic development.
