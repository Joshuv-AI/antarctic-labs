Limb cold therapy for preventing paclitaxel-induced peripheral neuropathy in breast cancer patients: a randomized controlled study
Authors: Danna Wu, Haiwei Bai, Yafei Zhang, Weina Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPaclitaxel, a widely used chemotherapeutic drug for breast cancer, often causes peripheral neuropathy, which impacts patient quality of life and limits treatment dosages. Cold therapy, involving controlled cold application to extremities, has emerged as a potential non-pharmacological intervention to reduce such neurotoxic effects. This study evaluates the effectiveness of limb cold therapy in preventing and reducing severity paclitaxel-induced peripheral neuropathy (PIPN) in breast cancer patients.MethodsA prospective, randomized controlled study was conducted at the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University from October 2022 to August 2024, involving 200 breast cancer patients undergoing paclitaxel and platinum chemotherapy. Participants were randomized into two groups: the control group receiving standard treatment (n = 103), and the cold therapy group receiving limb cold therapy with ice gloves and socks (n = 97). Outcomes were evaluated across multiple chemotherapy cycles using the Chemotherapy-Induced Peripheral Neuropathy Assessment Tool (CIPNAT), National Cancer Institute-Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE), and Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy/Gynecologic Oncology Group-Neurotoxicity (FACT/GOG-Ntx) scales. Neurophysiological assessments and Activities of Daily Living (ADL) tests were conducted to further evaluate the effects. The primary outcome was the incidence and severity of CIPN, assessed using NCI-CTCAE and CIPNAT. Secondary outcomes included FACT/GOG-Ntx, ADL, and neurophysiological parameters.ResultsThe cold therapy group showed a significant reduction in chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) severity from the fourth chemotherapy cycle onwards (e.g., fourth cycle CIPN grade 0: 64.95% vs. 44.66% in control, P = 0.013). CIPNAT scores indicated less severe neuropathy symptoms at the fourth cycle (cold therapy: 124.63 ± 59.21 vs. control: 147.79 ± 77.94, P = 0.019) and sixth cycle (cold therapy: 151.93 ± 61.87 vs. control: 169.64 ± 63.82, P = 0.048). FACT/GOG-Ntx scores showed reduced neurotoxicity in the cold therapy group at the fourth cycle (15.48 ± 3.61 vs. 17.12 ± 3.57, P = 0.001) and at multiple follow-ups. ADL scores improved significantly three months post-chemotherapy (cold therapy: 76.96 ± 5.11 vs. control: 74.61 ± 5.21, P = 0.002). Neurophysiological assessments revealed better nerve conduction parameters in the cold therapy group, including decreased distal motor latency (DML) and improved compound muscle action potential amplitude.ConclusionLimb cold therapy may help mitigate PIPN symptoms, improve quality of life scores, and preserve peripheral nerve function in breast cancer patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04448-7.

Introduction

Paclitaxel, a prominent chemotherapeutic agent, plays a critical role in the treatment of several malignancies, including breast cancer [1]. Despite its efficacy in reducing tumor burden and improving survival rates, paclitaxel was associated with significant adverse effects, one of the most debilitating being peripheral neuropathy [2]. Paclitaxel-induced peripheral neuropathy (PIPN), characterized by sensory and motor nerve dysfunction, substantially impacts patients’ quality of life and limits the cumulative dose of paclitaxel that can be administered, thereby potentially affecting oncological outcomes [3]. This neuropathy manifests as pain, numbness, and tingling in the extremities, which can progress to functional impairments [4]. While several hypotheses exist regarding the pathophysiological mechanisms underpinning PIPN, including microtubule disruption and mitochondrial dysfunction within neurons, effective strategies to mitigate this condition remain elusive [5].

The clinical management of PIPN continues to be challenging due to its complex pathogenesis and the limited efficacy of current preventive and therapeutic interventions [6]. Standard treatment options were primarily palliative and include the use of anticonvulsants, antidepressants, and analgesics, yet these treatments rarely provide complete relief and often carry their own risk of adverse effects [7, 8]. Given these challenges, the exploration of novel and safe interventions was of utmost importance.

Cold therapy, involving the application of controlled cold to the extremities, emerges as a promising non-pharmacological intervention aimed at reducing the risk of neurotoxicity associated with chemotherapy [9]. Cold therapy’s mechanism was thought to encompass vasoconstriction, which reduces chemotherapeutic blood flow in the extremities, thereby limiting drug exposure to peripheral nerves [10]. Moreover, local hypothermia might directly influence nerve conduction and inflammatory pathways, potentially attenuating neuropathic changes [11].

While preliminary studies have indicated potential benefits of cold therapy in other forms of chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN), comprehensive evaluations, particularly in the context of paclitaxel-induced neurotoxicity, remain limited [12, 13]. This underscores the necessity for robust, methodological investigation to validate its efficacy and safety as a protective intervention against PIPN. The present study aimed to evaluate the potential benefit of limb cold therapy in preventing PIPN among breast cancer patients.

Materials and methods

Study design

A prospective study was conducted on 200 breast cancer patients who underwent chemotherapy at the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University from October 2022 to August 2024. All participating patients received treatment in accordance with disease management guidelines, using a chemotherapy regimen of paclitaxel and platinum. Upon enrollment, patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either receive or not receive limb cold therapy intervention. Randomization utilized permuted block randomization with block sizes varying randomly between 2 and 4, ensuring that researchers remained blinded. Concealment procedures included the use of sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by an independent statistician who was not involved in patient recruitment or assessment. Stratification was performed based on age group (< 50 and ≥ 50 years) to ensure balance between groups. All participants were confirmed to have no peripheral neuropathy (NCI-CTCAE grade 0) at enrollment. Because ice gloves and socks produce a clear cooling sensation, complete blinding of patients and treating clinicians was not feasible. Therefore, this study was designed as an assessor‑blinded trial: outcome assessors (neurologists and nurses performing CIPNAT, NCI‑CTCAE, and neurophysiological evaluations) were kept unaware of group allocation. Patients in the control group received room‑temperature gloves and socks as a sham‑like comparator to control for psychological effects. No formal blinding success evaluation was performed, as this was not pre‑specified in the prospective protocol. The random assignment was facilitated by a centralized interactive voice response system and a web-based randomization platform (IXRS, provided by Almac).

All patients received neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy according to the NCCN Guidelines® Version 3.2022 for Breast Cancer. The regimen included paclitaxel (CSPC Ouyi Pharmaceutical, China) at a dose of 175 mg/m² administered intravenously over 3 h on Day 1, and carboplatin (Qilu Pharmaceutical, China ) at a dose of 300–400 mg/m² administered intravenously over 30 min on Day 1. The cycles were repeated every 21 days for 4 to 6 cycles, depending on the pathological response. Dose modifications were made in accordance with ASCO guidelines for hematologic toxicity (ANC < 1.5 × 10⁹/L) or neuropathy (CTCAE Grade ≥ 2).

Participants were allocated to two groups based on the randomization. The control group, comprising 103 patients, did not receive the addition of cold therapy, while the cold therapy group, also with 97 patients, received limb cold therapy through the use of ice gloves and socks applied to the extremities (Supplementary Fig. 1). This procedure commenced 15 min before chemotherapy and continued until 15 min after chemotherapy. Temperature control during cold therapy sessions was maintained using digital thermometers (Fluke 51 II, Fluke Corporation, U.S.A) placed on the skin surface near the extremities. The target temperature range was set between 10 °C and 20 °C, with continuous monitoring to ensure consistency throughout the treatment. The temperature range of 10–20 °C was selected based on prior studies demonstrating effective neuroprotection and good tolerability within these limits [11]. To control for potential psychological effects, the control group received room-temperature gloves and socks during the same time intervals as the cold therapy group. This placebo intervention aimed to mimic the experience of receiving an additional treatment without the cooling effect, thereby minimizing bias due to patient expectations.

The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.7(3.1.9.7, Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany), focusing on the t-test for the “means: difference between two independent means (two groups)” option. An a priori analysis was performed with the following parameters: a two-tailed test, an effect size (d) of 0.5, an alpha error probability of 0.05, and a power (1-β error probability) of 0.93. This calculation determined that a minimum of 96 participants per group was necessary. To accommodate potential attrition, 210 patients were initially enrolled. All outcome assessments were performed at baseline, at the end of chemotherapy cycles 2, 4, and 6, and at 1, 3, and 6 months after completion of chemotherapy. The follow‑up loss rate was 4.76%. Ultimately, complete data were acquired from 200 patients, with 97 in the intervention group and 103 in the control group. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 guidelines for randomized controlled trials.

Ethical statement

The trial adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the guidelines of the International Conference on Harmonisation for Good Clinical Practice. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their enrollment in the study. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Ethics Approval Number: 2023KS154). Additionally, this clinical trial has been registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry with the registration number ChiCTR2400085679.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Participants must be 18 years or older. They must have a diagnosis of breast cancer and need chemotherapy that includes a paclitaxel plus platinum regimen. They must have complete clinical data available for review.

Exclusion criteria

Individuals showing symptoms of peripheral nervous system damage due to conditions such as diabetes or vitamin deficiencies were excluded. Those with a definitive diagnosis of paraneoplastic peripheral neuropathy were not eligible. Patients with comorbidities like rheumatoid arthritis or peripheral vascular disease were excluded. Patients with contraindications to cold therapy, such as cold hypersensitivity, Raynaud’s phenomenon, cryoglobulinemia, severe peripheral vascular disease, or severe sensory impairment, were excluded. Anyone with a previous history of chemotherapy was not eligible. Participants must be able to complete a follow-up within one year; otherwise, they were excluded. Additionally, patients with any baseline peripheral neuropathy (defined as NCI-CTCAE grade ≥ 1) were excluded.

Since male patients did not meet the inclusion criteria or needed to be excluded, and there were only a few of them, all participants in this study were female.

Data collection

The primary outcome of this study was the incidence and severity of CIPN, measured by the NCI-CTCAE grade and CIPNAT score. Secondary outcomes included quality of life (FACT/GOG-Ntx), activities of daily living (ADL), and neurophysiological parameters (motor and sensory nerve conduction). All outcomes were assessed at baseline, at the end of each chemotherapy cycle (cycles 2, 4, and 6), and at one, three, and six months after the completion of chemotherapy.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 29.0 and R 4.2.2. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro‑Wilk test (n < 50) or Kolmogorov‑Smirnov test (n ≥ 50). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (X ± s) and were compared between groups using independent samples t‑tests. Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages [n (%)] and were compared using the chi‑square test (χ²). The primary endpoint was the incidence and severity of CIPN, with severity analysed by t‑tests (CIPNAT, FACT/GOG‑Ntx, ADL scores) and by chi‑square test (CTCAE grades). Secondary endpoints included neurophysiological parameters and quality‑of‑life scores, analysed with t‑tests. Incidence of CIPN (CTCAE grade ≥ 1) and moderate‑to‑severe CIPN (grade ≥ 2) at each time point was compared between groups using the chi‑square test. All tests were two‑tailed, and a p‑value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No adjustments for multiple comparisons were applied because each analysis tested a prespecified hypothesis at a specific time point, and the risk of type I error inflation was considered low given the exploratory nature of the study.

Results

Demographic and basic data

A total of 200 patients were included in the study: 97 received cold therapy, and 103 received standard treatment (Table 1). Both groups had comparable mean ages (47.52 ± 11.23 years for the control group vs. 45.47 ± 13.21 years for the cold therapy group, P = 0.237) and BMI (22.78 ± 4.31 kg/m² vs. 23.2 ± 5.62 kg/m², P = 0.559). Lifestyle factors such as smoking and drinking history showed no significant differences, with 24.27% of the control group and 21.65% of the cold therapy group reporting a smoking history, and a similar distribution in drinking history (P > 0.05). Medical history, including hypertension, previous breast disease, tumor characteristics, surgery type, TNM stage, pathological type, molecular typing, metastasis, family history of breast cancer, occupational status, marital status, and educational level, similarly revealed no significant disparities between the groups (P > 0.05). All patients were female. These results suggest homogeneity between the control and cold therapy groups, supporting the validity of subsequent analyses on the effectiveness of cold therapy interventions.

Comparison of CIPN grades across chemotherapy cycles

In this study, the comparison of CIPN grades across chemotherapy cycles highlighted the effectiveness of limb cold therapy (Table 2). At baseline, all patients had a CIPN grade of 0. During the second cycle, there were no statistically significant differences between the control group and the cold therapy group (P = 0.727). Most participants in both groups had a CIPN grade of 0, with 79.61% in the control group and 83.51% in the cold therapy group. However, significant differences emerged in later cycles. By the fourth cycle, the cold therapy group demonstrated a higher proportion of patients with a CIPN grade of 0 (64.95% vs. 44.66% in the control group, P = 0.013). The trend continued in the sixth cycle, where 30.93% of patients in the cold therapy group experienced no neuropathy (grade 0) compared to 13.59% in the control group, and fewer patients had more severe neuropathy grades Ⅲ and Ⅳ in the cold therapy group (6.19% and 0%, respectively) than in the control group (20.39% and 2.91%, respectively, P = 0.001). The incidence of any‑grade CIPN (CTCAE ≥ 1) was significantly lower in the cold therapy group at the fourth cycle (35.05% vs. 55.34%, χ²=8.42, P = 0.004) and at the sixth cycle (69.07% vs. 86.41%, χ²=8.86, P = 0.003). Moreover, the incidence of moderate‑to‑severe CIPN (CTCAE ≥ 2) at the sixth cycle was also lower in the cold therapy group (39.18% vs. 62.13%, χ²=10.61, P = 0.001). These results suggest that cold therapy was effective in reducing the severity of CIPN as chemotherapy progresses.

Comparison of CIPNAT scores across chemotherapy cycles

In this study, CIPNAT scores were used to evaluate the effectiveness of limb cold therapy in managing PIPN over chemotherapy cycles (Fig. 1). During the second cycle, there was no statistically significant difference in CIPNAT scores between the control group (mean ± SD, 126.41 ± 54.36) and the cold therapy group (112.94 ± 49.28; P = 0.069), indicating similar levels of neuropathy at this stage. This trend was consistent in the sixth cycle, where the cold therapy group again showed significantly lower neuropathy scores (151.93 ± 61.87) than the control group (169.64 ± 63.82; P = 0.048). These results indicate that limb cold therapy was effective in reducing the severity of neuropathy symptoms as chemotherapy cycles progress.

Comparison of FACT/GOG-Ntx Scores in different periods

In examining the effectiveness of limb cold therapy on PIPN in breast cancer patients, the FACT/GOG-Ntx scores were analyzed across different time periods (Table 3). These results support the conclusion that limb cold therapy effectively reduces chemotherapy-induced neurotoxicity both during treatment and in the post-chemotherapy period.

Comparison of upper limb neurological characteristics one month after chemotherapy

For the ulnar nerve, patients in the cold therapy group exhibited a significantly reduced distal motor latency (DML) (4.16 ± 2.03 ms) compared to the control group (4.82 ± 2.05 ms; P = 0.022), alongside a lower compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitude (5.27 ± 2.12 mV vs. 6.19 ± 2.52 mV; P = 0.006), and improved motor conduction velocity (MCV) (37.28 ± 11.34 m/s vs. 32.89 ± 11.29 m/s; P = 0.007) (Table 5). Additionally, the cold therapy group had a shorter negative peak duration of distal CMAP (6.65 ± 1.32 ms vs. 7.28 ± 1.44 ms; P = 0.002) and higher sensory conduction velocity (SCV) (37.88 ± 9.05 m/s vs. 35.17 ± 9.53 m/s; P = 0.041). For the median nerve, DML was slightly longer in the cold therapy group (8.95 ± 3.38 ms vs. 7.97 ± 3.21 ms; P = 0.037), but both the CMAP amplitude (4.36 ± 2.63 mV vs. 5.48 ± 3.24 mV; P = 0.008) and sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) amplitude (3.66 ± 0.97 µV vs. 4.08 ± 1.06 µV; P = 0.004) were significantly lower. Furthermore, the SCV was higher in the cold therapy group (42.82 ± 12.05 m/s vs. 37.94 ± 9.83 m/s; P = 0.002). These results show a mixed pattern: cold therapy was associated with improved conduction velocities (MCV, SCV) and shorter negative peak duration in the ulnar nerve, but with lower CMAP and SNAP amplitudes in the median nerve and lower CMAP amplitude in the ulnar nerve. The clinical significance of these amplitude reductions is unclear; they may reflect a direct cooling effect on nerve excitability rather than increased neuropathic damage. Overall, the findings indicate that cold therapy does not uniformly preserve all neurophysiological parameters, and further studies are needed to clarify its effects on axonal versus myelin integrity.

Comparison of lower limb neurophysiological characteristics one month after chemotherapy

For the tibial nerve, patients in the cold therapy group showed a significantly reduced DML (9.61 ± 4.35 ms) compared to the control group (10.87 ± 4.48 ms; P = 0.044), along with an increased CMAP amplitude (0.82 ± 0.12 mV vs. 0.78 ± 0.11 mV; P = 0.011) (Table 6). MCV was also higher in the cold therapy group (29.56 ± 10.14 m/s vs. 26.87 ± 8.82 m/s; P = 0.047), and the negative peak duration of distal CMAP was shorter (6.54 ± 2.51 ms vs. 7.36 ± 2.83 ms; P = 0.031), indicating improved nerve function. Additionally, the cold therapy group had a longer F wave latency (78.57 ± 9.12 ms vs. 76.14 ± 5.95 ms; P = 0.028). For the peroneal nerve, the cold therapy group exhibited a higher CMAP amplitude (0.78 ± 0.12 mV vs. 0.74 ± 0.09 mV; P = 0.003) and shorter negative peak duration (6.71 ± 1.67 ms vs. 6.03 ± 2.2 ms; P = 0.014), though DML was longer (9.83 ± 3.82 ms vs. 8.66 ± 3.81 ms; P = 0.032), and MCV was lower (25.45 ± 7.36 m/s vs. 27.98 ± 7.54 m/s; P = 0.017). In the sural nerve, the cold therapy group had a lower SNAP amplitude (2.03 ± 0.75 µV vs. 2.35 ± 0.97 µV; P = 0.01) but a higher SCV (35.51 ± 5.27 m/s vs. 33.32 ± 8.13 m/s; P = 0.025). The lower limb results were also mixed: while tibial nerve DML decreased and CMAP amplitude increased, peroneal nerve DML increased and MCV decreased in the cold therapy group. Sural nerve SNAP amplitude was lower but SCV was higher. These inconsistencies suggest that cold therapy may have differential effects on different nerves and parameters; therefore, a simple conclusion of “preserved nerve function” is not warranted. The results should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of potential benefit on certain parameters, with confirmation needed in larger studies.

Adverse events

No severe adverse events related to cold therapy were observed during the intervention period. Mild discomfort, such as transient cold intolerance, was reported by 4 patients (4.1%) in the cold therapy group, but none required discontinuation of the treatment. No skin injuries or other complications occurred.

Discussion

In exploring the potential of limb cold therapy as a preventive intervention for PIPN in breast cancer patients, this prospective study offers a comprehensive overview of its efficacy, as highlighted by a series of clinical assessments and neurophysiological evaluations.

The effectiveness of cold therapy can be attributed to its physiological mechanism of action, which involves local cooling of the extremities, potentially altering the microenvironment in a manner that reduces nerve damage [22]. Brunner et al.‘s study also demonstrated that cold therapy was hypothesized to decrease temperature sensitivity, slow nerve conduction velocity, and reduce the excitability of nerves [23]. However, their follow-up period was only 3 months and 6–9 months, which is shorter compared to the current study. Coolbrandt et al.‘s research indicated that by maintaining the peripheral microenvironment at a lower temperature, there was a reduction in the metabolic activity of sensory nerves, potentially minimizing the inflammatory response and consequent nerve injury [24], Their follow-up period was similarly short, although the dimensions of the outcomes were more comprehensive. Furthermore, cooling may induce vasoconstriction, effectively decreasing exposure of peripheral nerves to chemotherapeutic agents such as paclitaxel [25]. This vasoconstriction, while temporarily reducing blood flow, might limit the accumulation of the drug in nerve endings, thereby reducing toxicity [25, 26].

Interestingly, the significant differences in CIPN grades and CIPNAT scores observed across chemotherapy cycles, particularly from the fourth cycle onward, suggest that the benefits of cold therapy may become more pronounced over time. This delay could be related to the cumulative nature of paclitaxel’s neurotoxic effects, which tend to exacerbate with successive cycles [27]. Cold therapy might be working optimally when the cumulative exposure reaches a critical threshold, mitigating the worsening of symptoms more effectively as neuropathic changes progress [28]. Furthermore, the significant reduction in the incidence of PIPN in the cold therapy group underscores its role as a preventive intervention, complementing its effects on symptom severity.

The trend of reduced FACT/GOG-Ntx scores in the cold therapy group reinforces the hypothesis that cold therapy not only alleviates the immediate sensory symptoms of neuropathy but also contributes to sustaining functional integrity. By potentially decreasing axonal degeneration and maintaining nerve conduction, cold therapy aids in preserving overall neuronal function, as evidenced by improved scores on quality of life assessments both during and after chemotherapy. The significant improvements in ADL scores observed in patients receiving cold therapy suggest a notable preservation of functional capabilities, which was crucial for maintaining autonomy and quality of life in breast cancer survivors.

Neurophysiological evaluations further elucidate the specific nerve protection conferred by cold therapy. The improved motor and sensory conduction parameters observed in patients undergoing cold therapy might reflect the structural preservation of nerve fibers, possibly due to direct effects on ion channel activity and inflammatory pathways [29, 30]. The decrease in DML and improvement in CMAP amplitude suggest that cold therapy supports these parameters by mitigating axonal and myelin sheath damage. Additionally, the shortened negative peak duration of CMAP and increased SCV indicate that cold therapy may help maintain myelin integrity and prevent slowing of conduction velocity, which typically accompanies demyelination [31]. The increase in DML for the median nerve under superficial cooling could be attributed to temporary vasoconstriction, which reduces blood flow and slows down nerve conduction velocity. However, SCV was improved, suggesting that despite this initial slowing, the overall structural integrity of the sensory nerves was maintained or even enhanced. This dual effect highlights the complex interplay between temperature regulation and neural function [32]. These findings are consistent with a recent randomized controlled trial by Asadi et al. [33] which demonstrated that feet cooling effectively reduced the incidence and severity of chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy in advanced breast cancer patients. Our study extends these observations by showing that limb cold therapy, including both hands and feet, provides protective effects across multiple neurophysiological and functional domains.

Moreover, the reduction in distal latency and improvements in CMAP and SNAP amplitudes observed in both upper and lower limb nerves one month post-chemotherapy highlight the lasting impact of cold therapy beyond the treatment period. These changes were particularly notable in the context of paclitaxel’s known neurotoxic profile, characterized by distal axonal degeneration [34, 35]. By mitigating these neurotoxic effects, cold therapy reinforces its potential as a sustainable intervention that could alter the long-term trajectory of paclitaxel-induced neuropathy [22]. It should be noted, however, that not all neurophysiological parameters improved; some amplitudes (e.g., median CMAP and SNAP) were lower in the cold therapy group, and peroneal nerve DML was longer. These mixed findings indicate that cold therapy’s neuroprotective effect may be partial or parameter‑specific, and the clinical relevance of these amplitude differences remains uncertain.

The observed association between cold therapy and reduced CIPN may have broader implications for its potential role in managing chemotherapy-associated neuropathies. Given its relatively non-invasive nature and manageable side effects, cold therapy may be considered as an adjunctive measure in selected patients, pending confirmation from larger trials as either a preventive measure or a concurrent treatment modality to enhance the tolerability of chemotherapy regimens known for peripheral neurotoxicity. This intervention could significantly enhance multidisciplinary approaches to cancer care by enabling patients to sustain higher cumulative doses of chemotherapy agents without encountering the debilitating effects of CIPN, ultimately improving both therapeutic outcomes and quality of life.

It was worth considering, however, that factors such as patient compliance, the precision of temperature control, and cultural variations in the acceptance of cold therapy could impact the outcomes observed in diverse clinical settings [36]. The patient population’s demographic homogeneity in the study suggests the need for further research across more diverse cohorts, with attention given to variables such as genetic predispositions to neuropathy and variations in chemotherapeutic regimens that might influence the efficacy of cold therapy.

Continued research should aim to elucidate the biochemical and molecular pathways influenced by cold therapy, with specific attention to potential interactions at the cellular level. Exploring adjunctive pharmacological interventions that could be synergistically used with cold therapy may also amplify its neuroprotective properties. Furthermore, optimizing the duration and frequency of cold therapy sessions could refine its application, maximizing benefits while minimizing patient discomfort or inconvenience.

It is worth noting that the chemotherapy regimen used in this study (paclitaxel plus platinum) was administered to a patient population that included a substantial proportion of hormone receptor-positive breast cancer cases. Although platinum agents are more commonly recommended for triple‑negative or HER2‑positive breast cancer, their use in hormone receptor‑positive patients with high‑risk features has been adopted in clinical practice to enhance treatment efficacy. In our cohort, among the hormone receptor‑positive patients (Luminal A and B, n = 130), the majority presented with at least one of the following high‑risk characteristics: Ki‑67 > 30% (72.3%), involvement of four or more axillary lymph nodes (41.5%), or primary tumor diameter > 5 cm (33.8%). These features are consistent with the criteria used in clinical trials that support the addition of platinum in selected high‑risk luminal tumors. Therefore, the regimen choice reflects real‑world practice for patients with aggressive hormone receptor‑positive disease, and the results of this study are generalizable to that specific population.

While this study provides valuable insights into the effectiveness of limb cold therapy for preventing PIPN in breast cancer patients, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. The relatively small sample size may restrict the generalizability of the findings, as does the demographic homogeneity of the study population, which primarily consisted of individuals with similar backgrounds. An important limitation of this study is that all participants were female, with no male breast cancer patients included. Although breast cancer is more common in women, male breast cancer patients do exist. Future studies should aim to include a more diverse patient population to validate our findings across different genders. Additionally, the study’s focus on a single chemotherapy regimen limits its applicability across different treatment protocols. Variations in patient adherence to cold therapy protocols and the absence of long-term follow-up data also present potential biases and gaps in understanding the sustained efficacy and safety of cold therapy. Future research should aim to address these limitations by including more diverse populations, exploring different chemotherapy regimens, and conducting long-term assessments to fully elucidate the benefits and potential risks of cold therapy in this context.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study suggests that limb cold therapy may be beneficial for preventing paclitaxel‑induced peripheral neuropathy and reducing its severity in breast cancer patients. However, the presence of methodological limitations (including lack of complete blinding, mixed neurophysiological findings, and a single‑center design) means that these results should be interpreted as preliminary. Further well‑designed, multicenter, placebo‑controlled trials with longer follow‑up are needed to confirm the efficacy and safety of limb cold therapy before it can be recommended as a standard supportive care intervention.
