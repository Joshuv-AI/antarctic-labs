Unveiling Fine-Grained Visual Traces: Evaluating Multimodal Interleaved Reasoning Chains in Multimodal STEM Tasks
Jing Jin, Hao Liu, Yan Bai, Yihang Lou, Zhenke Wang, Tianrun Yuan, Juntong Chen, Yongkang Zhu, Fanhu Zeng, Xuanyu Zhu, Yige Xu

Abstract
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have shown promising reasoning abilities, yet evaluating their performance in specialized domains remains challenging. STEM reasoning is a particularly valuable testbed because it provides highly verifiable feedback, but existing benchmarks often permit unimodal shortcuts due to modality redundancy and focus mainly on final-answer accuracy, overlooking the reasoning process itself. To address this challenge, we introduce StepSTEM : a graduate-level benchmark of 283 problems across mathematics, physics, chemistry, biology, and engineering for fine-grained evaluation of cross-modal reasoning in MLLMs. StepSTEM is constructed through a rigorous curation pipeline that enforces strict complementarity between textual and visual inputs. We further propose a general step-level evaluation framework for both text-only chain-of-thought and interleaved image-text reasoning, using dynamic programming to align predicted reasoning steps with multiple reference solutions. Experiments across a wide range of models show that current MLLMs still rely heavily on textual reasoning, with even Gemini 3.1 Pro and Claude Opus 4.6 achieving only 38.29% accuracy. These results highlight substantial headroom for genuine cross-modal STEM reasoning and position StepSTEM as a benchmark for fine-grained evaluation of multimodal reasoning. Source code is available at https://github.com/lll-hhh/STEPSTEM .

Recent advances in Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs)

In addition to understand increasingly complex visual and textual inputs, MLLMs are capable of generating intermediate reasoning trajectories in text, image, or interleaved image-text formats, and derive the final answer based on these trajectories
Su et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025a
;
Chern et al. 2025
. This emerging capability has made MLLMs a promising foundation for solving complex multimodal reasoning problems. Among existing multimodal reasoning scenarios, STEM tasks are particularly attractive because of their strong verifiability: the correctness of the final answer is often objectively checkable, which provides a natural basis for supervising and analyzing the reasoning process itself.

Despite the growing attention to multimodal STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematical) reasoning, the field still faces several important challenges. (1) First, current MLLM reasoning often exhibit a form of
modality collapse
, in which they over-rely on textual cues while under-utilizing visual evidence, leading to weak visual grounding and degraded performance on tasks that require genuine visual reasoning
Zhao et al. 2025
;
Wu et al. 2025b
;
Yao et al. 2025
. In STEM reasoning, the modality collapse becomes more significant because of the detailed textual description on STEM benchmarks. (2) Second, the generation and understanding of structured STEM visuals remain limited. Prior work shows that scientific figures, diagrams, and plots are poorly covered by existing training data, while current image-generation pipelines are largely optimized for naturalistic imagery rather than structured, logically constrained visuals with precise layouts, symbols, and labels
Li et al. 2024b
;
Wei et al. 2025
. (3) Third, current evaluation and post-training practices still emphasize final-answer correctness more than intermediate-step quality. The broader reasoning literature has shown that chain-of-thought rationales are not always faithful to the model’s actual reasoning process, while both LLM and MLLM studies suggest that step-wise supervision can better support reliable reasoning than outcome-only signals
Turpin et al. 2023
;
Lightman et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
. As a result, it remains difficult to determine whether a model truly performs grounded cross-modal reasoning or merely reaches the correct answer through superficial shortcuts.

To address these limitations, we introduce
StepSTEM
, a new multimodal STEM benchmark with fine-grained reasoning step evaluation that includes engineering, physics, mathematics, chemistry, and biology subset.
StepSTEM
contains graduate-level multimodal STEM problems that require both textual information and visual evidence to derive the correct solution, thereby discouraging unimodal shortcuts and encouraging genuine cross-modal reasoning. As illustrated in Figure
1
, during data construction, we deliberately curate problems whose solutions cannot be recovered from text alone or vision alone. More importantly, for each problem we provide one or more ground-truth solution trajectories that contain both textual intermediate steps and visual intermediate steps.

Built upon
StepSTEM
, we further introduce a
step-level
evaluation framework for multimodal reasoning. Instead of evaluating only the final answer, our framework first measures the similarity between each predicted reasoning step and the annotated ground-truth solution steps, and then aligns the predicted trajectory with one or multiple reference ground truth trajectories through dynamic programming to obtain an overall step-level reasoning score. In this way, the proposed protocol captures not only whether a model reaches the correct answer, but also whether it follows a plausible, grounded, and interpretable multimodal reasoning path.

We evaluate a broad range of representative MLLMs on
StepSTEM
. Experimental results show that
StepSTEM
remains highly challenging for existing models, revealing substantial room for improvement in genuine multimodal STEM reasoning. Moreover, the proposed step-level scores provide a much more fine-grained view of model behavior than answer-only evaluation, enabling us to identify differences in reasoning quality, modality usage, and grounding ability that are otherwise obscured by final accuracy alone.

In summary, our contributions are three-fold:

(1) A new multimodal STEM reasoning benchmark that requires cross-modality reasoning
: We introduce
StepSTEM
, consisting of 283 carefully curated problems that enforce strict complementarity to prevent unimodal shortcuts.

(2) A fine-grained evaluation framework for intermediate reasoning step evaluation
: We propose a
step-level evaluation framework
for systematically assessing intermediate multimodal reasoning steps beyond final-answer accuracy.

(3) A comprehensive empirical analysis
: We evaluate representative MLLMs on
StepSTEM
and provide empirical insights into their capabilities and limitations in cross-modal STEM reasoning. We further show that step-level scores strongly correlate with final-answer accuracy, highlighting the importance of evaluating reasoning processes in addition to final outcomes.

2.1
Multimodal Chain-of-Thought Reasoning

Traditional Chain-of-Thought (CoT) techniques have achieved remarkable success in pure text logical deduction; however, when confronted with complex scenarios highly dependent on spatial perception and geometric intuition, pure text derivations often lack cognitively meaningful physical anchors. Consequently, multimodal interleaved CoT has emerged, with the core objective of decoupling the complex problem-solving process into a series of intermediate steps where textual deduction and visual evidence are deeply interwoven.

Current explicit multimodal CoT methods primarily evolve along two technical trajectories. The first entails tool-augmented designs
Li et al. 2024a
;
Zhang et al. 2025a
;
Hu et al. 2024
;
Duan et al. 2025
;
Zhu et al. 2026
;
Li et al. 2025d
, which leverage external visual modules or code interpreters to assist reasoning; nevertheless, the interleaving process of such methods often exhibits high fragility and low flexibility. The second trajectory focuses on intrinsic visual CoT (Intrinsic VCoT) under unified model architectures. For instance, THINKMORPH
Gu et al. 2025
reveals advanced reasoning characteristics that emerge under the multimodal interleaved training paradigm, while MathCanvas
Shi et al. 2025a
constructs an intrinsic VCoT framework specifically designed for mathematical reasoning. However, existing mainstream text-to-image models are predominantly pre-trained on general open-domain scenes, such as natural landscapes, daily scenarios, and virtual characters. As a result, their training objectives are largely centered on general-purpose visual generation rather than task-oriented image generation for downstream reasoning, which limits their effectiveness in reasoning-focused applications.

2.2
Multimodal Reasoning Dataset

In parallel with the limitations of current generation models, another critical challenge lies in the scarcity of high-quality multimodal reasoning data tailored for downstream tasks. Early efforts in multimodal reasoning primarily focus on visual understanding and perception, where the core objective is to interpret visual inputs and map them into textual answers. Representative benchmarks include MMMU
Yue et al. 2024
, MathVista
Lu et al. 2023
, ScienceQA
Lu et al. 2022
, and MathVerse
Zhang et al. 2024
. While these datasets have significantly advanced the evaluation of multimodal models, their solution processes are predominantly text-centric. Unified benchmarks like Uni-MMMU
Zou et al. 2025
, RealUnify
Shi et al. 2025b
and others
Wang et al. 2026
;
Huang et al. 2026
evaluate both perception and generation but typically focus on foundational tasks. They lack the graduate-level depth and systematic protocols required to audit intermediate reasoning steps, leaving the logical coherence of cross-modal processes unverified.

Recent work has explored multimodal reasoning with visual generation, such as Zebra-CoT
Li et al. 2025a
and THINKMORPH
Gu et al. 2025
, which emphasize reasoning within the visual modality. However, existing STEM benchmarks still suffer from
modality redundancy
: models can often rely on textual shortcuts without genuinely using visual evidence. In addition, many current datasets are not sufficiently challenging to rigorously evaluate state-of-the-art models on deep, multi-step reasoning.

2.3
Fine-Grained Evaluation and Supervision of Reasoning Processes

Traditional evaluation protocols overly rely on final-answer accuracy, thereby neglecting the faithfulness and logical coherence of the reasoning path. To overcome this limitation, the academic community is accelerating the transition toward a process-oriented monitoring paradigm. Research on Process Reward Models (PRMs), such as PRM800K
Lightman et al. 2023
and DREAM-PRM
Cao et al. 2025
, compellingly demonstrates the critical role of step-level feedback in mitigating logical hallucinations. Within automated evaluation systems, the “LLM-as-a-Judge” paradigm (e.g., MT-Bench
Zheng et al. 2023
, JudgeLM
Zhu et al. 2023
, and Prometheus
Kim et al. 2024
) has been established as the mainstream approach for evaluating open-ended complex logic. Concurrently, relevant works in the multimodal domain, such as T2I-CoReBench
Li et al. 2025c
, and Science-T2I
Li et al. 2025b
, have also begun attempting to conduct fine-grained quantification of the quality of intermediate generated content. Our work builds on this trend but focuses on interleaved STEM reasoning, where both local step alignment and global trace coverage are needed to evaluate whether a model followed a valid cross-modal solution path.

Our
StepSTEM
consists of two core components: the rigorous curation of a multimodal dataset (detailed in Section
3.2
) and a fine-grained evaluation protocol: (1) For the dataset, we carefully choose 283 graduate-level multimodal STEM reasoning problems and provide one or more ground truth reference solutions. The overall dataset construction workflow is illustrated in Figure
1
, and the domain distributions of
StepSTEM
is shown in Figure

. (2) For the evaluation phase, our framework assesses both final-answer correctness and intermediate reasoning quality. We evaluate answer correctness, local and global reasoning quality, and their fusion, selecting the best score across multiple ground-truth solutions by comparing each one separately.

3.2
Data Curation

To construct a multimodal STEM benchmark tailored for process-level evaluation, we specifically curate two categories of problems characterized by substantial visual dependency: (1)
strongly dependent
problems, where reasoning cannot proceed reliably without the accompanying images, and (2)
reasoning-simplifying
problems, where visual information significantly compresses the reasoning chain and reduces semantic ambiguity. The overall curation pipeline comprises data collection, data filtering, and solution structuring. This rigorous process was conducted by eight experts with interdisciplinary backgrounds, who transformed raw multimodal data into structured reasoning chains suitable for fine-grained process evaluation. The detailed statistical distributions of the dataset are presented in Table
1
.

3.2.1
Data Collection

During the collection phase, experts gathered potential samples from public competitions, undergraduate textbooks, and other open educational resources. For each candidate problem, we recorded the problem statement, reference image, ground-truth answer, and a standard solution trajectory detailing key intermediate steps. We adopted a task-attribute-first principle to assess visual dependency: problems were retained only if their resolution strictly required the geometric structure, spatial relations, or local details of the image, or if the image explicitly shortened the reasoning path. Notably, to elevate the evaluation dimensionality, we specifically included 14 drawing tasks where the
final output is required to be an image
. This inclusion fundamentally extends the evaluation paradigm from standard “image-text understanding to text generation” to the more challenging “iage-text understanding to image generation”.

3.2.2
Data Filtering

To ensure data quality and evaluation operability, we established rigorous exclusion criteria. We discarded samples where: (1) the image is purely decorative or highly redundant with the text; (2) the problem is ambiguous or solely tests OCR capabilities; or (3) the image quality is too low to support stable annotation. Furthermore, we implemented an objective two-ended truncation filter for problem difficulty. We removed “overly simple” problems that experts universally agreed required no explicit intermediate reasoning, thereby preventing the task from degrading into basic visual recognition. Conversely, we excluded “overly difficult” problems where experts could not reach a stable consensus or formulate a verifiable step-level standard process. This dual filtering ensures that the retained samples maintain high consistency in both verifiable reasoning and moderate difficulty.

It is worth noting that the distribution of test instances varies across domains, reflecting the varying degrees of intrinsic visual dependency across scientific disciplines. To prevent unimodal shortcuts, we exclusively retained problems where the solution strictly necessitates the interpretation of spatial relations or geometric structures. Engineering and Physics naturally offer a higher density of such visually-indispensable tasks at the graduate level , whereas many candidate problems in Mathematics or Biology were excluded for being solvable via pure textual logic.

3.2.3
Solution Structuring

Following the filtering phase, the expert-verified reference solutions were unified into an interleaved image-text step structure suitable for multimodal evaluation. To enhance structural efficiency and standardization, we utilized GPT-5.4 Codex with fixed prompts for the initial step-level decomposition and modality tag assignment. It is important to note that GPT-5.4 Codex was strictly limited to formatting the expert-provided reference solutions and was not used to independently generate new answers. This automated structuralization was followed by rigorous manual review and correction to ensure each step constitutes an independent and verifiable reasoning unit.

For visual steps, experts further annotated the local visual evidence supporting the current reasoning utilizing bounding boxes (BBoxes). When a single step relied on multiple dispersed regions, multiple BBoxes were permitted. This design allows visual steps to pinpoint the specific local evidence actively participating in the reasoning process, providing fine-grained supervision for subsequent step-level image alignment evaluation.

Additionally, to construct alternative valid reasoning paths (Solution 2 and Solution 3) for samples exhibiting multi-path solution characteristics, we introduced a combined mechanism of
expert manual exploration
and
LLM-inspired generation
. Specifically, experts actively explored diverse problem-solving strategies based on their domain backgrounds. Simultaneously, we input the problems into advanced multimodal models such as Gemini 3.1 Pro and Claude Opus 4.6 to detect whether there were significantly different solution strategies from the primary reference solution. For novel solutions independently proposed by experts, as well as those generated by models that were rigorously reviewed by experts to be logically consistent, correct, and substantially different, we standardized them into additional alternative reference solutions. Therefore, these multiple solutions represent distinct and valid deductive paths rather than mere linguistic paraphrases, providing critical support for the subsequent
best-over-solutions
evaluation.

Through this comprehensive pipeline, we ultimately constructed a dataset comprising 283 problems across six domains: Engineering, Physics, Chemistry, Biology, Mathematics, and Other.

3.3
Evaluation Protocol

To rigorously assess the quality of multimodal reasoning, we propose a fine-grained evaluation framework that decouples the interleaved reasoning traces into explicit local step-level alignment and implicit global logic coverage. For textual steps, we further annotate key points to support fine-grained evaluation of semantic correctness and reasoning completeness, while the evaluation emphasizes semantic consistency with the reference solution as well as coverage of annotated key points. For visual steps, we annotate one or more important bounding boxes to specify the regions that should be attended to or referenced by the model at each stage.

To establish a unified cross-modal computation, we first define a shared notation system: let
\mathcal{G}^{T}
and
\mathcal{G}^{I}
denote the sets of ground-truth (GT) textual and visual steps, respectively; similarly, let
\mathcal{P}^{T}
and
\mathcal{P}^{I}
denote the predicted textual and visual steps. The complete protocol consists of modality-specific local alignments, large language model (LLM) based global judgements, and a final metric fusion mechanism designed to handle problem-solving diversity.

3.3.1
Textual Reasoning Steps Evaluation

For textual reasoning steps, we prioritize semantic process coverage over generic string similarity. We treat the textual reasoning trace as a strict logical chain. To compute the pairwise similarity between a GT text step
g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}^{T}
and a predicted text step
p_{j}\in\mathcal{P}^{T}
, following
Abnar and Zuidema 2020
, we utilize the attention rollout mechanism of Qwen3.5-9B
Qwen Team 2026
, which effectively captures token-level causal dependencies across deep network layers.

Let
W^{(l,h)}
denote the attention weight matrix of the
h
-th head in the
l
-th transformer layer. To prevent the dilution of attention signals in deep networks, we incorporate residual connections and compute the global attention rollout matrix
\bar{A}
by multiplying the averaged attention matrices across all layers:

\bar{A}=\prod_{l=1}^{L}\left(\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}W^{(l,h)}+I\right),

(1)

where
H
and
L
are the total number of attention heads and network layers, respectively, and
I
is the identity matrix representing the residual connection.

Instead of relying on coarse sentence embeddings, we extract fine-grained dependency weights directly from
\bar{A}
. For the
i
-th GT text step
g_{i}
and the
j
-th predicted text step
p_{j}
, the confidence-weighted similarity score
M^{T}[i,j]
is calculated as follows: for each reference token
u\in g_{i}
, we identify its maximum attention weight from the predicted tokens
v\in p_{j}
, and then average these maximum weights across all tokens in
g_{i}
.

M^{T}[i,j]=\frac{1}{|g_{i}|}\sum_{u\in g_{i}}\max_{v\in p_{j}}\bar{A}_{u,v}.

(2)

Given the computed similarity matrix
M^{T}
, we apply dynamic programming to find the optimal monotonic matching path
M^{*}
. This dynamic programming approach naturally allows for skipped intermediate steps—acknowledging that models may condense trivial derivations—while strictly forbidding crossed logic sequences that would violate causality. The textual local score
S_{\text{local}}^{\text{text}}
is defined as the average score along this optimal path.

3.3.2
Image Reasoning Steps Evaluation

For visual reasoning steps, such as intermediate geometric plots or state diagrams, we employ a Many-to-Many (M2M) alignment strategy based on deep visual features. This strategy maintains strict mathematical symmetry with the textual token alignment.

We utilize a visual encoder
\phi(\cdot)
to extract feature maps. A GT image step
g_{i}\in\mathcal{G}^{I}
is typically anchored by an expert-annotated local bounding box (BBox), and its core visual feature is represented as
\phi(g_{i})
. To search for corresponding visual evidence within an unannotated predicted image step
p_{j}\in\mathcal{P}^{I}
, we define a set of sliding-window patches
W(p_{j})
, where
w
is a single sliding-window patch. The element of the image step alignment matrix
M^{I}[i,j]
is calculated by finding the maximum cosine similarity between the GT feature and all window features of the predicted image:

M^{I}[i,j]=\max_{w\in W(p_{j})}\frac{\phi(g_{i})\cdot\phi(w)}{\|\phi(g_{i})\|\|\phi(w)\|}.

(3)

However, this naive matching approach is susceptible to a degenerate loophole where a model generates a single generic image to mistakenly satisfy all GT visual steps during the
\max
operation. To explicitly penalize predictions that fail to generate distinct visual evidence for different reasoning steps, we introduce a diversity ratio
D_{\text{img}}
. The visual local score
S_{\text{local}}^{\text{img}}
is computed by scaling the maximum coverage
C_{\text{img}}
with a diversity penalty hyperparameter
\lambda
. Ultimately, the text and image scores are combined based on their respective step proportions to form the unified local score
S_{\text{local}}
.

3.3.3
Global Logic Coverage Evaluation

While local step-level alignment captures explicit step-by-step logic, models may successfully arrive at correct deductions via valid but unstructured alternative paths. To accommodate this flexibility, we introduce an LLM-based global judge to assess whole-trace coverage.

This evaluation operates from a macro perspective. The entire raw predicted trajectory is fed into the judge alongside the GT reference points, encompassing both textual key points and visual regions. Acting as a generous evaluator, the judge determines whether each critical GT proposition is semantically entailed or mathematically represented in the candidate’s trajectory. It outputs a binary decision of 1 for covered and 0 for missing for each reference point. The global coverage score
S_{\text{global}}
is then calculated as the average of these binary decisions across all references.

3.3.4
Final Evaluation and Metric Fusion

Independent of the intermediate process evaluation, we rigorously assess the final answer correctness. If the GT final answer is textual, we employ Qwen3.5 9B as an LLM judge for the final determination. For drawing tasks where the final answer is visually required, the evaluation method is identical to the aforementioned image reasoning steps evaluation.

For process evaluation, we combine the Local and Global scores into a single fused process score
S_{\text{fused}}
.This is achieved via a weighted Root Mean Square (RMS):

S_{\text{fused}}=\sqrt{\frac{w_{s}S_{\text{local}}^{2}+w_{j}S_{\text{global}}^{2}}{w_{s}+w_{j}}},

(4)

where
w_{s}
and
w_{j}
are the weights for the local score and global score, respectively (both set to 0.5 by default).

4.2
Main Results

Table
2
summarizes the overall performance on 269 problems with textual final answers and 14 problems with image-based final answers. For clarity, we group the evaluated MLLMs into three categories according to their reasoning paradigm: unified models, interleaved generation models, and understanding-only models.

The benchmark is highly challenging.

Overall, the results indicate that our benchmark remains difficult even for the strongest contemporary MLLMs. On the 269 problems with textual final answers, the best accuracy is only 38.29%, achieved by Gemini 3.1 Pro and Claude Opus 4.6. On the 14 problems requiring image-based final answers, the highest accuracy is 50.80%, achieved by Gemini 2.5 Flash Image. Taken together, these results suggest that current MLLMs are still far from reliably solving graduate-level multimodal STEM reasoning tasks.

Vision-text-to-text models exhibit a fundamental limitation.

Understanding-only models, such as Claude Opus 4.6 and Gemini 3.1 Pro, achieve relatively strong scores on text-related process metrics, particularly on Local-T and Global-T. However, all such models obtain zero scores on Img. Acc., Local-I, and Global-I. This pattern reveals a fundamental limitation of vision-text-to-text models: while they can process multimodal inputs and produce textual reasoning traces, they cannot handle tasks that require explicit visual generation as part of the reasoning process. Therefore, evaluating only the final answer is insufficient for complex multimodal STEM reasoning, since it fails to capture whether a model can produce and leverage image-based intermediate derivations.

Unified models achieve the strongest multimodal process scores, but still struggle on final problem solving.

The unified models outperform the other categories on fused and global process metrics. GPT-5.4 + GPT-Image achieves the best Fused score (71.52) and the highest Global score (80.94), while Gemini 2.5 Flash Image attains the best Img. Acc. (50.80%) and a strong Local-I score (64.10). Nevertheless, their final-answer accuracy remains limited, especially on the text-answer subset, where GPT-5.4 + GPT-Image reaches 33.21% and Gemini 2.5 Flash Image reaches only 15.61%. These findings suggest that, although current unified models are capable of generating plausible intermediate visual structures, they still struggle to fully exploit the generated visual information to solve challenging STEM reasoning problems.

Interleaved generation alone is insufficient for effective multimodal reasoning.

Interleaved generation models obtain non-trivial scores on image-related process metrics, indicating a limited ability to produce intermediate visual content. For example, OmniGen2 achieves 59.05 on Local-I, and Bagel reaches 48.70 on Global-I. However, both fail to generate correct image-based final answers, yielding 0% accuracy on the image-answer subset. This suggests that interleaving image generation into the reasoning chain alone is insufficient. Effective multimodal STEM reasoning requires not only generating intermediate visual evidence, but also integrating it coherently into the reasoning process to arrive at correct final solutions.

4.3.1
Analysis of Step-Level Evaluation Scores

Step-level process scores are strongly associated with final-answer correctness.

As shown by the blue intervals in Figure
4
, trajectories that lead to correct final answers consistently receive higher fused process scores than those ending with incorrect answers. This pattern is further confirmed by the correctness split in Table
3
. For example, the fused score increases from 64.73 to 81.43 for Gemini 2.5 Flash, from 68.81 to 78.41 for GPT-5.4 + GPT-Image, from 47.46 to 65.84 for Bagel, and from 35.33 to 42.86 for Gemini 3.1 Pro when moving from incorrect to correct cases. The consistency of this trend across unified models, interleaved generation models, and understanding-only models suggests that the proposed process score captures meaningful variation in reasoning quality rather than incidental overlap with the reference solutions.

Higher-performing models tend to obtain higher process scores.

Beyond the correctness split, we also observe that models with stronger multimodal reasoning ability generally achieve higher absolute process scores. In Table
2
, the two unified models obtain the highest fused scores overall, indicating that stronger reasoning performance is accompanied by better intermediate trajectories. A similar trend is visible in Figure
4
, where better-performing models are generally located farther to the right on the fused-score axis. This observation provides further evidence for the validity of our step-level evaluation framework: stronger models do not merely produce more correct final answers, but also exhibit higher-quality reasoning processes.

The correct–incorrect gap is itself informative.

The magnitude of the blue gap in Figure
4
also reveals differences in model behavior. Models such as Bagel and Janus-Pro show relatively large separations between correct and incorrect cases, indicating that successful predictions are supported by substantially better reasoning trajectories. In contrast, weaker models such as Showo2 and OmniGen2 exhibit much smaller gaps, suggesting that their intermediate trajectories remain of limited quality regardless of whether the final answer happens to be correct. This result highlights an important advantage of step-level evaluation: it can distinguish between models that occasionally reach the correct answer and models that more consistently follow a plausible reasoning process.

Overall, these results demonstrate the necessity of evaluating multimodal reasoning at the step level. Final-answer accuracy only indicates whether a model reaches the correct endpoint, but does not reveal whether the model follows a coherent cross-modal solution path. In contrast, our step-level evaluation scores provide a finer-grained diagnostic signal for assessing the quality of intermediate reasoning trajectories. This is particularly important for challenging multimodal STEM problems, where success depends not only on the final prediction, but also on whether the model can sustain a logically consistent reasoning chain across textual and visual steps.

4.3.2
Analysis of Multiple Ground-Truth Solutions

For problems with multiple valid solution strategies, we compute the process score against each ground-truth solution and examine the resulting distribution. As shown by the red intervals in Figure
4
, the same model trajectory can receive substantially different scores under different references. This variation does not necessarily indicate unstable model behavior; rather, it reflects the fact that complex multimodal STEM problems often admit multiple legitimate reasoning paths. A model may therefore follow a correct and coherent solution strategy while still obtaining a low score against one particular annotated reference.

This observation highlights a limitation of single-reference process evaluation. When only one ground-truth solution is available, the metric may unfairly penalize trajectories that are valid but structurally different from the chosen annotation. To mitigate this issue, we provide multiple ground-truth solutions whenever alternative solving strategies exist. With this design, a model can obtain a high process score as long as its reasoning is close to at least one valid reference path. This improves both the stability and the fairness of the evaluation framework by reducing spurious low scores caused by annotation incompleteness.

Moreover, the gap between the best-matched and worst-matched references is itself informative. A larger gap suggests greater diversity in valid solution trajectories and indicates that multi-reference evaluation is particularly important for such problems. Overall, these results show that multiple ground-truth solutions are essential for reliable step-level evaluation of multimodal reasoning.

4.3.3
Case Study

Figure
5
presents a representative example involving a two-mass system rotating about point
A
, where the goal is to derive the angular-momentum vector in the
\mathbf{i}_{1}
-
\mathbf{j}_{1}
-
\mathbf{k}_{1}
basis. This problem admits two valid ground-truth solution trajectories with the same final answer. The first solution follows a direct vector-based derivation by explicitly computing
\mathbf{r}\times(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{r})
for each mass, while the second solution reformulates the problem through the inertia tensor and derives
\mathbf{H}
via
\mathbf{I}_{A}(\Omega\mathbf{k}_{1})
. This example highlights a key challenge of step-level trajectory evaluation: even when two reasoning chains are both correct, they may differ substantially in their intermediate structure.

Our framework correctly recognizes such alternative reasoning paths. GPT-5.4 + GPT-Image is matched to Solution 1 and obtains a matched-solution score of 0.6453, together with a perfect final-answer score. By contrast, Gemini 3.1 Pro is matched to Solution 2 with a matched-solution score of 0.7009, again producing the correct final answer. Importantly, the two models are not required to mimic the same sequence of intermediate steps. Instead, each model is rewarded because its reasoning remains semantically close to at least one valid reference trajectory. This result demonstrates that the proposed evaluation framework is able to accommodate diverse but valid solution strategies, rather than overfitting to a single annotated chain of thought.

The example also illustrates the diagnostic value of step-level evaluation when the final prediction is incorrect. Qwen3.5-122B receives a relatively high score on problem understanding of 0.7271, suggesting that it correctly identifies the task setting and the relevant physical quantities. It also shows partial alignment with Solution 2 during intermediate reasoning. However, the model ultimately produces an incorrect final expression and therefore receives a zero score on the final-answer step, resulting in a substantially lower matched-solution score of 0.3635. A closer inspection suggests that the model captures part of the desired decomposition, but fails to recover the complete
k_{1}
component in the final angular-momentum vector. Such fine-grained failure patterns would be invisible under final-answer accuracy alone.

Overall, this case study demonstrates two important properties of our benchmark. First, multi-reference annotation is necessary for fair process evaluation, since a single reference would incorrectly penalize valid reasoning paths that differ in form but not in substance. Second, step-level scoring provides a more informative diagnosis of model behavior by revealing whether an error arises from misunderstanding the problem, from a flawed intermediate derivation, or only at the final composition stage. These observations further support the need for fine-grained, multi-reference evaluation in multimodal STEM reasoning.

5
Conclusions

We introduced
StepSTEM
, a benchmark and evaluation framework for interleaved multimodal STEM reasoning. By combining expert-curated problems, step-level textual and visual annotations, and multi-solution trajectory matching,
StepSTEM
enables evaluation of not only whether a model reaches the correct final answer, but also how it reasons across modalities. Experiments show that current state-of-the-art MLLMs still exhibit a substantial gap between answer correctness and reasoning trajectories quality: to-text MLLMs cannot generate vision intermediate steps, whereas unified generative models better expose multimodal reasoning behavior but remain far from reliable. We hope
StepSTEM
will support future research on multimodal process supervision, reasoning-oriented data construction, and more faithful evaluation of cross-modal reasoning systems.
