Designing escalation criteria for international AI incident response: criteria, triggers, and thresholds
Francesca Gomez 1 Matthew Ball 1 Michael Harré 1 Lydia Preston 1 Josephine Schwab 1 Caio Machado 2 1 Arcadia Impact AI Governance Taskforce 2 The Future Society

Abstract
AI incident reporting requirements are emerging in regulation and policy, yet no operational criteria exist for determining when a detected AI incident warrants escalation beyond national handling to international coordination. This paper proposes an escalation framework to address this gap, intended as a common reference point across jurisdictions that enables aligned escalation while preserving flexibility in how actors respond within their own legal and policy contexts. Our methodology involves a review of SB 53, the EU AI Act, the GPAI Code of Practice, and incident frameworks from other industries, to derive eight criteria to assess whether an incident warrants escalation, which we then translate into a sequential flowchart with gated decision points and threshold checks. For each criterion, we map how it interplays with the EU AI Act, SB 53, and the GPAI Code of Practice, identifying where these frameworks’ design choices support or undermine effective detection. We test the framework against ten documented AI incidents and structured variants to identify where escalation criteria under-detect or misclassify incidents in practice. We find three design patterns that may lead to systematic under-detection or misclassification of AI incidents in regimes where model developers are responsible for escalation: (a) where escalation requires confirmed harm, events such as model weight exfiltration or credible CBRN threats risk detection only after severe, irreversible harm has propagated, as developers who observe the risk materializing cannot confirm downstream harms; (b) where incidents are assessed only individually, systemic harms emerging from accumulation — such as large-scale manipulation and psychological harm — risk being under-detected; and (c) where thresholds align with legal instruments rather than quantitatively testable terms, criteria risk being impractical to apply under time pressure. We also find that escalation rules are only one component of a broader framework: the underlying definitions against which thresholds are set, and the data available to the responsible actor, create interdependencies that can themselves drive under-detection and must be addressed in framework design.

Executive Summary

As AI systems become more capable and are increasingly deployed across borders and sectors, incidents involving these systems increasingly have international implications. Effective responses often depend on timely coordination between governments, companies, and other stakeholders. However, existing approaches to incident reporting and escalation vary widely, creating gaps in how risks are identified, assessed, and shared across jurisdictions.

A central question is when an AI incident should trigger an international response.
For escalation to be effective in practice, it must occur early enough to enable the benefits of cross-border coordination, such as containment, mitigation, and shared response. This requires escalation thresholds and triggers that function reliably under real-world conditions, both for novel forms of AI risk and across key domains of systemic AI risk, namely, large-scale manipulation and psychological harm; loss of control; chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear (CBRN) threats; and cyber vulnerabilities and offensive capabilities.

While we group our findings by these categories, we also note that the interdependencies between them can themselves be the underlying cause of particular findings. For example, we expect escalation rules for incidents relating to manipulation and psychological harm are largely absent from escalation rules because the bounds of incidents and their risk pathways in this domain have not be defined, severity scales are underdeveloped, and it is unclear which data would needed to trigger these, and if this is available.

The proposed escalation framework and the identified design patterns are intended to support policymakers and other relevant stakeholders worldwide who are developing or implementing AI incident reporting regimes. Emerging approaches such as the EU General-Purpose AI Code of Practice and California’s Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act (SB 53) illustrate the growing emphasis on reporting serious or critical AI incidents. This work aims to inform such efforts by showing how the design and operationalization of escalation criteria can shape which incidents are identified and escalated in practice, particularly under real-world constraints and across common risk pathways.

1
Introduction

The combination of more advanced capabilities, wider adoption, and increased affordances granted to AI systems increases the likelihood of incidents occurring, but not all incidents represent the same potential for harm. Distinguishing between incidents of differing severity is therefore critical for determining when and how to respond.

Yet even the foundational concept of an "AI incident" lacks a settled, operationally consistent definition. The OECD’s stocktaking exercise on AI incident terminology found that existing definitions vary significantly across regulatory instruments, incident databases, and the academic literature, differing on questions such as whether an AI system must be the proximate cause of harm or merely a contributing factor, whether near-misses and hazards fall within scope, and whether the definition requires actual harm or also covers credible risk of harm
(
73
)
. The subsequent OECD definitional paper proposed distinguishing between an
AI incident
, an event where the development, use, or malfunction of an AI system directly or indirectly leads to actual harm; and an
AI hazard
, an event that could plausibly lead to such an incident
(
75
)
. The AI Incident Database (AIID), the largest public repository of AI incidents, adopts a broader formulation that includes alleged harms
and
near-harms
(
64
)
, and has itself evolved to accommodate "issues", unrealized risks that do not meet incident ingestion criteria but advance understanding of where AI presents the potential for harm
(
63
)
. The EU AI Act takes a different approach, defining "a serious incident" as one that directly or indirectly leads to death, serious health harm, irreversible disruption of critical infrastructure, or fundamental rights violations (Art. 3(49)), while SB 53 introduces the distinct category of critical safety incident” under a separate threshold.

Meanwhile, analytical work on failure taxonomies demonstrates that even when a definition is agreed, the causes and boundaries of incidents remain subject to significant epistemic uncertainty, as the exact causes of AI incidents are seldom fully known from public reporting alone
(
85
;
82
)
. This definitional instability is not merely a taxonomic concern. The definition of an AI incident sets the boundary condition for every downstream function: reporting obligations, threshold triggers, severity classification, and escalation criteria all inherit the scope and limitations of the incident definition they are built on
(
75
)
. A definition that is too narrow risks excluding incidents where AI played a material but non-proximate role; one that is too broad risks overwhelming reporting and response systems with events that do not warrant coordinated action. Getting this formulation right has been a persistent challenge for the AI incidents field, and it directly shapes the practical effectiveness of the escalation framework we propose in this paper.

Scales have been proposed to distinguish between incidents of differing severity. The Future Society distinguishes between an AI incident, which is limited in scope, contained without widespread spillover, and recoverable; an AI emergency, a faster-moving, more urgent threat requiring immediate national or even international intervention; and an AI crisis, the most severe stage of escalation, which is a disruption that causes large-scale harm, often spreading across borders or sectors
(
72
)
. AI crisis preparedness is defined as the capacity to prevent AI hazards, incidents, and emergencies from escalating into a crisis, and to limit their impact if they do occur
(
72
)
. Achieving this requires enabling the early detection of AI hazards and incidents and responding to these appropriately across jurisdictions and sectors.

The detection of AI incidents meeting certain severity thresholds is also addressed in regulation. The EU General-Purpose AI Code of Practice (Commitment 9) requires signatories to report ‘Serious incidents’ to the EU AI Office (Article 55(1)) and SB 53 (Labor Code §1107) introduces reporting requirements for ‘Critical safety incidents’
(
33
;
28
;
18
)
. The definitions, thresholds, and escalation criteria within these serve as the mechanism for setting the criteria an incident must meet to fall within their scope: defined correctly, these escalate all incidents warranting attention; flawed design may lead to material incidents being missed, or low-severity incidents being captured. Furthermore, to isolate the intended incidents in practice, escalation thresholds and triggers must function reliably under real-world conditions, including time pressure, taking into account data access constraints and potential uncertainty around understanding incidents as they are developing.

In this paper we focus on developing a framework to identify incidents that warrant international coordination. While many open questions need to be addressed to design and operationalize a model for AI crisis preparedness, including defining institutional responsibilities and the policy instruments that would govern it, this paper focuses on how a detected incident can be evaluated against a set of eight criteria and thresholds to determine whether it warrants international escalation. We address two questions:

1.

What criteria, thresholds, and triggers should exist to determine whether an AI incident requires international escalation?

2.

How do the design choices of AI incident escalation criteria impact their effectiveness at detecting AI incidents in practice?

To address these questions, we develop an AI incident escalation framework, designed as a sequential flowchart with gated decision points and threshold checks, built around eight criteria for determining when an AI incident should be escalated internationally. For each criterion, we map how it interplays with the EU AI Act, SB 53, and the GPAI Code of Practice, identifying where these frameworks’ design choices support or undermine effective detection
(
33
;
28
;
18
)
. We test the framework against ten documented AI incidents and structured variants to identify where escalation criteria under-detect or misclassify incidents in practice.

The regulatory analysis focuses on the EU AI Act (Arts. 3(49), 51, 73) and California’s SB 53 as the two most developed legislative instruments that define incident escalation obligations for general-purpose AI, supplemented by the GPAI Code of Practice as the principal means of operationalizing the EU regime
(
33
;
28
;
18
)
. Cross-domain design precedents are drawn from established escalation frameworks in nuclear safety, aviation, public health, financial stability, cybersecurity, and disaster risk reduction. The framework and its thresholds are anchored to the state of these instruments as of April 2026; as AI incident reporting regimes mature and new regulatory requirements emerge, the escalation criteria will require reassessment against updated legal baselines.

2
Methodology

To develop threshold logic for classifying AI incidents that may warrant international coordination, we use a three-stage sequential design in which each stage produces traceable inputs to the next, creating a path from reviewed literature to final design choices. The following sections of the paper reflect these stages.

Stage 1: Literature review and requirement extraction.

The first stage draws on three bodies of literature: how severe incidents are classified and escalated in established high-stakes domains (public health, nuclear safety, cybersecurity, financial stability, and disaster risk reduction); current practical approaches and limitations in AI incident monitoring; and AI incident escalation requirements in existing regulation, specifically the EU AI Act (Arts. 3(49), 51, 73) and California’s SB 53. These sources are used to characterize the structural dimensions along which incidents vary and to identify which dimensions have operationalized scoring criteria adaptable to AI contexts. From this review, common criteria for incident evaluation are identified and criteria selected for inclusion based on their relevance to the escalation decision, the design precedent in established or emerging frameworks, and their operational feasibility under realistic information constraints (Appendix C).

Stage 2: Threshold and indicator framework design.

The second stage translates the selected criteria into threshold logic to define which questions need to be asked to evaluate an incident’s materiality for potential international escalation (Appendix A). Eight assessment criteria are derived through a constrained design process. The objective is to identify the minimum operational set of decision-relevant dimensions required to determine whether an incident warrants international escalation and to provide a testable basis for the scenario-based stress testing. The criteria are combined into a sequential flowchart logic with supporting threshold checks at each decision point. Criteria and threshold values are defined separately: criteria specify what must be assessed, while threshold values are intentionally kept variable to allow different risk tolerances to be applied. The intention is to make the framework adaptable to different escalation rules without requiring changes to the underlying logic. For each criterion, we map how it interplays with the EU AI Act, SB 53, the GPAI Code of Practice, and the key frameworks from other industries, identifying where these frameworks’ design choices support or undermine effective detection.

The third stage evaluates the framework against documented AI incidents from the AIID database, followed by structured variations to probe threshold boundaries. The aim of this testing is to surface where thresholds work reliably, where gaps and weaknesses exist for specific AI risk types, and which characteristics of those risks make them structurally difficult to capture or assess. 3
Literature review

3.1
AI incidents and the case for international escalation

To understand when AI incidents may warrant international escalation, it is useful to first consider how other domains have approached cross-border incident response, before turning to emerging AI frameworks.

International frameworks for incident response are coordination mechanisms, including shared definitions, reporting protocols, and cross-border response procedures that enable jurisdictions, developers, and affected parties to detect, investigate, contain, and learn from incidents whose causes, effects, or remediation pathways span national boundaries. They have evolved in domains where threats can cross borders because the systems through which they propagate are themselves interconnected: pathogens travel through global mobility networks, cyberattacks through shared digital infrastructure, and financial contagion through interlinked markets. Frameworks such as the World Health Organization’s International Health Regulations
(
101
)
, the Budapest Convention on Cybercrime
(
21
)
, and the Financial Stability Board’s coordination mechanisms
(
39
)
were established on the basis that delayed or siloed responses risk converting containable incidents into systemic crises. Article 2 in the IHR articulates its purpose as preventing, protecting against, and controlling the international spread of disease ‘in ways that are commensurate with and restricted to public health risks, and which avoid unnecessary interference with international traffic and trade’
(
101
)
. Defining such objectives explicitly is what enables the success of an escalation framework to be measured against them.

Regulatory approaches to AI similarly define high-level objectives, though typically at the level of the overall regime rather than incident escalation specifically. The EU AI Act’s stated purpose, per Article 1(1), is ‘to improve the functioning of the internal market and promote the uptake of human-centric and trustworthy artificial intelligence (AI), while ensuring a high level of protection of health, safety, fundamental rights enshrined in the Charter, including democracy, the rule of law and environmental protection, against the harmful effects of AI systems in the Union and supporting innovation’
(
35
)
. California’s SB 53, the Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act, addresses catastrophic risks from frontier foundation models through mandatory transparency, incident reporting, and whistleblower protections
(
17
)
.

Drawing on these defined objectives, we define the purpose of our AI international incident escalation framework as enabling international action only where a purely national response is insufficient, and where harm, response capacity, or control over the propagation pathway is distributed across jurisdictions. We identify three reasons why an AI incident may require international escalation:

1.

Cross-border harm.
Harm has already been realized in multiple jurisdictions, necessitating coordination across them. This represents the most direct trigger for international response, analogous to the WHO IHR’s core criterion of ‘international spread of disease’
(
101
)
and the NIS2 criterion for incidents affecting two or more Member States through shared infrastructure
(
34
)
. Where harm is already distributed across jurisdictions, no single national authority possesses sufficient observability or controllability to mount an adequate response independently.

2.

Cross-border containment.
There is a credible pathway for harm to spread that requires intervention outside the originating jurisdiction. The incident may be localized at present but is actively propagating, with a pathway that runs through infrastructure, models, or deployment patterns that cross borders. Containment may require action in the jurisdiction where the upstream source sits or through which the pathway runs. For example, this may arise in a deepfake political disinformation campaign. This mirrors the public health rationale whereby containing an outbreak may require preventive measures at ports of entry in countries not yet affected, and the cybersecurity principle that remediating a vulnerability requires cooperation from the jurisdiction hosting the compromised infrastructure.

3.

Irreversible harm within one jurisdiction with cross-border consequences.
Harm may be contained and not actively propagating, but permanent damage in one jurisdiction may generate downstream consequences for others. Illustrative examples include a permanently corrupted financial dataset in one country’s market infrastructure upon which other countries’ systems depend, or irreversible environmental harm that crosses borders by nature.

We identify further conditions under which international action would be beneficial, but which are better characterized as triggers for alert rather than escalation. These draw on frameworks whose primary purpose is the sharing of learnings and mutual assistance rather than active containment, most notably civil aviation, where ICAO Annex 13 establishes a learning-first, non-punitive regime explicitly separated from liability and blame
(
53
)
. Whether an incident meets these conditions can be determined through the standard incident assessment process, though in practice the resulting actions may be handled by coordination structures distinct from those convened for escalation. These share the characteristic that international mobilization provides capacity or information that would not otherwise be available:

1.

Response capacity gap,
where the affected jurisdiction lacks sufficient resources or information to respond effectively, arising from the technical complexity of investigating frontier model behavior, the resource demands of large-scale containment, or the absence of domestic expertise in the relevant AI capability domain.

2.

Information gap,
arising where understanding the incident requires data held across multiple jurisdictions, such that no single actor possesses the complete picture necessary for effective response.

3.

Enforcement capacity gap,
where the affected jurisdiction lacks the legal authority, technical means, or institutional reach to compel remediation from a developer headquartered elsewhere, arising from limits on extraterritorial jurisdiction, gaps in mutual legal assistance frameworks for AI-specific harms, or the absence of enforceable cross-border obligations on frontier developers.

4.

Cross-developer control improvements,
to notify model developers that controls which failed in one system may be insufficient in analogous systems elsewhere, enabling preventive remediation across the sector before similar failures materialize.

3.2
Requirements for escalation of AI incidents in regulation

Current regulation contains several drivers for AI incident escalation. This paper focuses on the EU AI Act, which includes provisions relating to the escalation of ‘serious incidents’ and ‘prohibited practices’, and California’s Senate Bill 53 (SB 53), which captures ‘critical safety incidents’ and ‘catastrophic risk’ activities
(
35
;
17
)
. These instruments set out requirements for which AI incidents require escalation, when they must be reported, and to whom, though the severity thresholds for AI harms are not always operationally clear or quantified. We begin by examining the AI harm taxonomies offered by these regulations in relation to their roles in incident escalation. The harm thresholds within these two leading legislative instruments inform the design of our international incident escalation framework.

3.2.1
Regulation (EU) 2024/1689: the EU AI Act

The 2024 EU AI Act, hereafter EUAIA, is a regulatory expression of the European Commission’s 2019
Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI
, developed by the independent High-Level Expert Group on AI, and also reflects the non-binding European Declaration on Digital Rights and Principles for the Digital Decade
(
26
)
. On the basis of regulating violations of these principles, the EUAIA offers escalation thresholds and trigger mechanisms for the benefit of AI operators, intended ‘to ensure a consistent and high level of protection of public interests as regards health, safety and fundamental rights’ (Recital 7)
(
35
)
. The Act further states that a specific safeguard procedure should be established to ensure adequate and timely enforcement against AI systems presenting a risk to health, safety, and fundamental rights, and that this should apply to high-risk AI systems and prohibited systems that have been placed on the market, put into service, or used in violation of prohibited practices. The following definitions build on these provisions.

The EU AI Act: AI systems.

The EU AI Act’s use of the term ‘operator’ is broad, covering a ‘provider’, ‘product manufacturer’, ‘deployer’, the ‘authorized representative’ of a provider, an ‘importer’, or a ‘distributor’ involved in the AI value chain (Art. 3(8))
(
35
)
. Most notably, a ‘provider’ is the entity that develops or places the AI system on the market (Art. 3(3)); a ‘deployer’ is a natural or legal person using an AI system under its authority, except where the AI system is used in the course of a personal activity (Art. 3(4)); and a ‘distributor’ is an entity in the supply chain, other than the provider or importer, that makes an AI system available on the market (Art. 3(7)). The EUAIA also distinguishes between an ‘AI model’ and an ‘AI system’: AI models are essential components of AI systems, but do not in themselves constitute AI systems. They require the addition of further components, such as a user interface, in order to become AI systems (Recital 97). The Act also lays out an overlapping classification regime for general-purpose AI, including ‘high-impact capabilities’ systems and ‘high-risk’ AI systems.

Where a general-purpose AI model is integrated into or forms part of an AI system, that system should be considered a general-purpose AI system if it has the capability to serve a variety of purposes (Recital 100), and where the integrated model can competently perform a wide range of distinct tasks, is typically trained on large amounts of data, and may use self-supervised, unsupervised, or reinforcement learning methods (Recital 97). By contrast, a ‘high-impact capabilities’ system refers to systems whose capabilities match or exceed those recorded in the most advanced general-purpose AI models (Recital 111), for example where the cumulative amount of computation used for training exceeds
10^{25}
floating-point operations (Art. 51(2)) or where the model has been made available to at least 10,000 registered business users established in the Union (Annex XIII(f))
(
35
)
. The floating-point operations threshold may be adjusted over time to reflect technological and industrial change (Recital 111).

A ‘high-risk’ AI system refers to those AI systems that, in light of their intended purpose, pose a high risk of harm to health, safety, or fundamental rights, taking into account both the severity of the possible harm and its probability of occurrence (Recital 52). The high-risk domain list, risk-management requirements under Article 9, and the full catalog of prohibited practices under Article 5 are set out in Appendix
A
.

The EU AI Act: AI risk types.

The EU AI Act defines AI risk as the combination of the probability of an occurrence of harm and the severity of that harm (Art. 3(2)). It separates more significant AI risk into two main categories: ‘serious incident’ and ‘systemic risk’. According to Article 3(49), a ‘serious incident’ is an incident or malfunction leading to death or serious damage to health; serious and irreversible disruption of the management and operation of critical infrastructure; infringements of fundamental rights; or serious damage to property or the environment
(
35
)
.

According to Recital 110, ‘systemic risk’ includes actual or reasonably foreseeable negative effects relating to major accidents, disruption of critical sectors, serious consequences to public health and safety, consequences for democratic processes, public and economic security, or the dissemination of illegal, false, or discriminatory content. These risks may arise across the model lifecycle and are shaped by misuse, reliability, fairness, security, autonomy, access to tools, novel or combined modalities, release and distribution strategies, and the potential to remove guardrails. Article 3(65) further defines ‘systemic risk’ as a risk specific to the high-impact capabilities of general-purpose AI models, where those capabilities may have significant effects on the Union market, fundamental rights, or society as a whole, and can propagate at scale across the value chain. A notable example is ‘loss of control’, described in Recital 110 as ‘unintended issues of control’. This includes risks relating to misalignment with human intent in domains such as CBRN risks, offensive cyber capabilities, the control of physical systems and critical infrastructure, self-replication, training other models, harmful bias and discrimination, disinformation, privacy harms, and threats to democratic values and human rights. Appendix 1.4 of Chapter 3 (Safety and Security) of the voluntary General-Purpose AI Code of Practice develops this further, defining ‘loss of control’ as risks arising where humans lose the ability to reliably direct, modify, or shut down a model, potentially as a result of misalignment, self-reasoning, self-improvement, deception, resistance to goal modification, power-seeking behavior, or autonomously creating or improving AI systems
(
28
)
.

The Code of Practice also elaborates the definition of ‘systemic risk’, including risks of major accidents; disruption to critical sectors or infrastructure; harms to public mental health, freedom of expression and information, non-discrimination, privacy and data protection, the environment, non-human welfare, economic security, and democratic processes; and risks from concentration of power and illegal, violent, hateful, radicalising, or false content, including child sexual abuse material and non-consensual intimate imagery
(
28
)
. It further identifies four specified systemic risk domains: CBRN, loss of control, cyber offence, and harmful manipulation.

The EU AI Act and the Digital Services Act.

The EU AI Act functions as an additional layer to other EU frameworks, most notably the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union and the Digital Services Act (DSA)
(
31
)
. The DSA is particularly relevant in the case of Very Large Online Platforms (VLOPs) and Very Large Online Search Engines (VLOSEs), where Recital 76 acknowledges their societal and systemic risks and calls for the highest standard of due-diligence operations. These platforms are designated ‘very large’ on the basis of the number of active recipients, exceeding 45 million or 10% of the Union’s population in the relevant year.

When AI systems or GPAI models are embedded into or provide intermediary services for VLOPs or VLOSEs, those AI systems become subject to the systemic risk-management framework established under Articles 33–35 of the DSA. Article 34 mandates annual risk assessments, including risks arising from embedded AI systems and potential misuse. This creates one possible data source for pattern detection, as recurring AI-related incidents across platforms with very large user bases could, in principle, be surfaced through periodic risk assessment.

The DSA’s annual reporting cadence is also too slow for real-time incident clustering. It produces a periodic risk picture rather than a real-time trigger when a pattern of sub-threshold events reaches a collective severity threshold. These assessments are also platform-scoped rather than model-scoped, since Article 34 applies to the deployment layer, not the model layer. A manipulation campaign arising from a frontier model and propagating through multiple platforms could therefore generate multiple uncoordinated Article 34 assessments, without any clear obligation on the model provider to contribute to or integrate them. The combined EUAIA/DSA architecture therefore risks fragmentation: a model provider may report under Article 73 of the EUAIA if a serious incident threshold is crossed, while a platform separately assesses systemic risk annually under Article 34 of the DSA, yet no binding mechanism requires these assessments to be integrated, shared, or compared at the AI Office or Commission level. Further detail on the DSA provisions, including Article 40 data access mechanisms, Article 48 crisis protocols, and Article 36 crisis-response powers, is provided in Appendix
A
.

The EU AI Act: incident escalation requirements.

Obligations for providers of general-purpose AI models with systemic risk to undertake serious incident reporting are set out in Articles 55 and 73 of the EUAIA. Under Article 55(1)(c), providers must keep track of and report, without undue delay, relevant information about serious incidents and possible corrective measures to the AI Office, and to national competent authorities where appropriate
(
35
)
. Recital 114 emphasizes the importance of post-market monitoring and recommends cooperation with relevant actors along the AI value chain.

Under the General-Purpose AI Code of Practice, incident escalation requirements include systemic risk estimation, which may involve qualitative risk scoring (Measure 3.4), and obligations under Commitment 9 on serious incident reporting. Commitment 9 requires signatories to implement processes and measures for keeping track of and reporting relevant information about serious incidents throughout the model lifecycle
(
28
)
. The detailed reporting fields under Measures 9.1–9.2 and reporting timelines under Measure 9.3 are set out in Appendix
A
.

Measure 9.2 information remains tied to serious incident reporting and does not constitute a standalone near-miss reporting channel. Points 8 and 9 of Measure 9.2 are excluded from the more immediate guidance on reporting timelines under Measure 9.3, meaning that near misses may only be reported every four weeks in the case of unresolved serious incidents, or in a final report submitted up to 60 days after resolution. Near-miss events may therefore reach the AI Office more quickly through Safety and Security Framework updates or Model Report updates than through serious incident reporting itself, raising the question of whether these are the appropriate reporting channels when both depend on provider-assessed materiality thresholds. This gap is consequential for the highest-priority risk domains, because the current regulatory architecture leaves reporting of near-miss signals subject to the discretion of the actor with the strongest commercial incentive to assess them as non-material. In addition, because Article 3(49) defines serious incidents in terms of a single event producing threshold-level harm, the binding text contains no provision for classifying a pattern of individually sub-threshold incidents as collectively constituting a serious incident or systemic risk trigger. There is no formal rolling window, no minimum number of correlated events that triggers an obligation, and no mechanism for generating a cross-developer view of correlated incidents.

Another gap concerns reversibility. While irreversibility is explicitly named as a qualifying criterion for one serious incident category—critical infrastructure disruption under Article 3(49)(b)—it does not function as a universal severity modifier or independent escalation trigger across all harm types. Likewise, propagation potential—the likelihood, speed, and pathway through which an incident may spread across systems, jurisdictions, or populations—is not a formal criterion in Article 73 or in the serious incident definition. An incident that causes limited immediate harm but has high propagation potential therefore does not trigger reporting on that basis alone. The overlapping architecture with the Digital Services Act only partially addresses this gap, and only for models or systems within its scope. Article 36 of the DSA establishes a crisis-response mechanism that enables the Commission to require mitigation measures from VLOPs and VLOSEs where a serious threat to public security or public health has arisen. This is propagation-aware in the sense that it is designed for situations in which harm is spreading faster than routine regulatory response can address. However, Article 36 is reactive and Commission-initiated: it responds to crises already in progress, rather than enabling pre-emptive escalation when propagation indicators are first detected. The crisis protocols under Article 48 also remain voluntary and under-specified.

There are several further limitations to the effectiveness of Article 73. In cases where AI systems present risks to health, safety, or fundamental rights, providers must ‘immediately investigate’ and inform market surveillance authorities (Art. 20), but adequately operationalizing these risks remains underdeveloped. Forty-eight-hour reporting is mandatory only in the case of ‘serious and irreversible disruption of the management or operation of critical infrastructure’ (Art. 73(3)); reporting timelines are otherwise slower under Article 73(2) and (4). Reporting timelines under Measure 9.3 of the Code of Practice are not faster than those under Article 73, except for serious cybersecurity breaches, including model-weight exfiltration and cyberattacks, where signatories establish or suspect with ‘reasonable likelihood’ a causal relationship between their model and the breach, in which case reporting is expected within five days
(
28
)
. Article 73 also excludes systems not designated as high-risk, as well as forms of AI hazard in which serious harm could plausibly occur without yet having done so, both of which any incident threshold design must address.

3.2.2
California Senate Bill 53: thresholds, gaps, and comparisons

The 2025 California Senate Bill 53 (SB 53) enacts the Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act (TFAIA). It is significantly more succinct than the EUAIA, but introduces a distinct set of AI harm definitions and thresholds intended to respond to advances in frontier model capabilities and the potential catastrophic risks they may pose
(
17
)
.

SB 53 distinguishes between ‘frontier’ models and ‘foundation’ models. A foundation model must be trained on a broad data set, designed for generality of output, and adaptable to a wide range of distinct tasks. A frontier model is, by contrast, a foundation model trained using a quantity of computing power greater than
10^{26}
integer or floating-point operations, including the original training run and any subsequent fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, or other material modifications. The Act also distinguishes between a ‘frontier developer’ and a ‘large frontier developer’, the latter being a developer whose annual gross revenues, together with those of its affiliates, exceed $500,000,000 in the preceding calendar year.

‘Catastrophic risk’ is defined as a foreseeable and material risk that a frontier developer’s development, storage, use, or deployment of a foundation or frontier model will materially contribute to the death of, or serious injury to, more than 50 people, or more than one billion dollars in damage to, or loss of, property, arising from a single incident involving a frontier model. The qualifying activities include providing expert-level assistance in the creation or release of a CBRN weapon; engaging in conduct with no meaningful human oversight that constitutes a cyberattack or, if committed by a human, would constitute murder, assault, extortion, or theft; or evading the control of its frontier developer or user. Section 1(j) further defines catastrophic risk as including both malicious uses and malfunctions, including AI-enabled hacking, biological attacks, and loss of control
(
17
)
.

Large frontier developers must also publish summaries of their catastrophic-risk assessments and the results of those assessments before, or concurrently with, deploying a new frontier model or a substantially modified version of an existing frontier model. Despite its breadth, the EUAIA does not provide a separate harm category for ‘catastrophic risk’; SB 53’s definitions of ‘critical safety incidents’ would therefore, in many cases, fall across both serious incidents and systemic risks under the EU framework. However, SB 53 also introduces carve-outs to catastrophic risk. These include information output by a model that is otherwise publicly accessible in substantially similar form from another source; lawful activity of the federal government; and harms caused by a model in combination with other software where the model did not materially contribute to the harm. These carve-outs require careful treatment in any incident escalation trigger design.

‘Critical safety incidents’ under SB 53 are defined to include: unauthorized access to, modification of, or exfiltration of model weights resulting in death or bodily injury; harm resulting from the materialisation of a catastrophic risk; loss of control of a foundation model causing death or bodily injury; or a foundation model using deceptive techniques against the frontier developer to subvert controls or monitoring, outside the context of an evaluation designed to elicit that behavior, in a manner that demonstrates materially increased catastrophic risk
(
17
)
. These definitions are framed around single incidents. Accordingly, SB 53 contains no correlated-incident aggregation provision and no temporal clustering mechanism for capturing patterns of individually sub-threshold incidents that collectively amount to catastrophic risk.

The OES reporting mechanism, frontier framework requirements, and associated reporting timelines are set out in Appendix
A
.

Several structural gaps in SB 53’s escalation and reporting architecture nevertheless warrant attention. As with the EUAIA, the absence of any correlated-incident aggregation mechanism is one of the most significant gaps. No temporal clustering provision exists to capture patterns of related incidents, and the annual OES report uses anonymized and aggregated data, limiting its usefulness for cross-incident pattern detection. The requirement to report critical safety incidents within 15 days of discovery is also long relative to the pace at which AI-enabled harms may propagate, especially in offensive cyber contexts. The absence of obligations for near misses, hazard reporting, and reversibility as an assessment dimension compounds this problem. There is no provision for escalating a sub-threshold incident on the basis that the harm, if realized, would be irreversible.

Catastrophic-risk carve-outs also create additional escalation gaps that must be consciously addressed in incident threshold design. In particular, the exclusion of harms caused by a model ‘in combination with other software’ where the model did not materially contribute to the harm is problematic for cases of embedded infrastructure, where harm arises from AI behavior within a multi-component pipeline rather than from direct autonomous action. The qualitative threshold as to whether a model has ‘materially contributed’ also introduces causal ambiguity that escalation architecture should encode explicitly. The voluntary nature of foundation-model reporting under §22757.13(c)(4) must likewise be addressed. The distinction between frontier and foundation models means that a significant category of deployed AI systems—those below the frontier threshold but still capable of contributing to serious harm when weaponized or embedded in adversarial infrastructure—falls outside the mandatory reporting regime. Finally, the flexibility afforded to large frontier developers to substitute their own ‘reasonable schedule’ for quarterly catastrophic-risk assessments under §22757.12(d) further weakens the limited aggregation function that the annual OES report might otherwise serve.

In summary, the differing threshold designs of the EU AI Act and SB 53 show that harmonising international approaches to AI governance remains challenging. Divergent harm thresholds and red lines across the two frameworks create practical limitations that must be addressed when designing incident escalation triggers intended to capture both.

3.3

The aim is not to provide an exhaustive comparison, but to identify transferable design patterns from established incident response frameworks that may be applicable to AI incident escalation.

3.3.2
Sendai Framework and the IASC Scale-Up Activation

The Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction 2015–2030 was adopted at the Third UN World Conference on Disaster Risk Reduction on 18 March 2015 and endorsed by the UN General Assembly shortly thereafter
(
96
)
. The Framework sets the structure within which humanitarian escalation decisions are taken, with global targets and priorities for action that Member States use to calibrate national and international response capacity. The framework includes a mechanism for
System-Wide Scale-Up Activation
in response to crises meeting a five-criterion threshold: scale, urgency, complexity, capacity, and risk of failure
(
48
)
. Activation moves international agencies from their normal capacity and coordination structures into a mandatory surge regime of accelerated funding, senior leadership deployment, and centralized crisis coordination. A 2018 reform replaced the earlier tiered “L1/L2/L3” labels with a single binary decision: Scale-Up or not
(
48
)
.

3.3.3
International Health Regulations and the PHEIC architecture

The International Health Regulations (2005) were adopted by the 58th World Health Assembly on 23 May 2005 and entered into force on 15 June 2007
(
101
)
. Their purpose, set out in Article 2, is “to prevent, protect against, control and provide a public health response to the international spread of disease in ways that are commensurate with and restricted to public health risks, and which avoid unnecessary interference with international traffic and trade”
(
101
)
. The IHR are the most mature international incident-notification regime in existence and have served as a reference architecture for several of the more recent instruments discussed below.

The IHR address four distinct design questions: what threshold triggers escalation, how that threshold is assessed, how quickly notification must follow, and how much causal certainty is required. The threshold condition—a
public health emergency of international concern
(PHEIC)—is defined as an extraordinary event that constitutes a public health risk to other States through international disease spread and may require a coordinated international response
(
101
)
. To assess whether an event meets this threshold, National Focal Points apply four questions covering seriousness, unusualness, risk of international spread, and risk of travel or trade restrictions; two “yes” answers trigger mandatory notification. Four named diseases—smallpox, wild-type poliovirus, novel-subtype human influenza, and SARS—bypass the assessment entirely and are always notifiable. Once an event is assessed, States have a hard 24-hour notification window. Critically, the assessment does not require confirmed cause: it asks whether the event is “caused by or associated with” a listed disease or novel pathogen, recognizing that attribution is often incomplete when escalation decisions must be taken.

3.3.4
NIS2, the CSIRTs Network and EU-CyCLONe

Directive (EU) 2022/2555 (NIS2) was adopted on 14 December 2022 and established a transposition deadline of 17 October 2024
(
30
)
. It replaces the earlier NIS1 Directive (Directive (EU) 2016/1148), which had established the EU CSIRTs Network under Article 12
(
29
)
. NIS2 significantly expands the sectoral scope, tightens incident-reporting timelines, and introduces a formal operational-level crisis-coordination body, EU-CyCLONe.

NIS2 defines scope by enumerating covered sectors, including energy, transport, banking, health, and digital infrastructure, while explicitly excluding national security, defense, and law-enforcement activities
(
30
)
. The escalation trigger is a two-part test: an incident qualifies as
significant
where it has caused or could cause severe operational disruption or financial loss, or has affected or could affect other persons through material or non-material damage. Because this test applies to any entity in a covered sector, sectoral classification itself acts as a first-stage filter: a significant incident at a covered entity is reportable without further severity screening.

Once triggered, NIS2 imposes a three-stage notification timeline: early warning within 24 hours, incident notification within 72 hours, and a final report within one month. Harm is tracked across four categories: availability, authenticity, integrity, and confidentiality of data or services, and entities are required to maintain business continuity and disaster recovery capabilities, making recoverability an operational obligation rather than merely a reporting field.

For cross-border escalation, NIS2 establishes EU-CyCLONe, activated when an incident exceeds a single Member State’s response capacity or significantly impacts two or more Member States. This sits within a three-tier structure: technical response (CSIRTs Network), operational crisis coordination (CyCLONe), and political response (IPCR)
(
30
)
.

3.3.5
DORA: digital operational resilience in the EU financial sector

DORA addresses operational rather than purely cyber risk in the EU financial sector, and is a detailed threshold-based escalation framework currently in force
(
32
)
. Like the IHR, DORA does not require attribution to a specific threat actor: classification and reporting turn on the occurrence and consequences of an ICT-related incident, rather than on identifying who caused it
(
32
, Arts. 17–20)
.

Incidents are classified against six criteria: number of clients or financial counterparts affected, duration, geographical spread, data losses, criticality of affected services, and economic impact
(
27
)
. An incident is classified as
major
where it affects a critical service and meets concrete materiality thresholds, for example, more than 10% of clients or more than 100,000 affected, duration exceeding 24 hours, spread across two or more Member States, or economic impact exceeding EUR 100,000, as detailed in Article 8
(
27
, Art. 8)
. Notably, these thresholds combine technical indicators of what went wrong with measures of downstream impact, assessing both within a single classification system
(
27
)
.

DORA also includes a related-incident aggregation rule: individually sub-threshold incidents that share the same apparent root cause and collectively meet the classification criteria within a rolling six-month window are deemed major, and entities must assess for such patterns monthly
(
27
)
. DORA also distinguishes system-level recoverability from harm-level reversibility: entities must set and test Recovery Time Objectives and Recovery Point Objectives for each function, but these measure how quickly systems and data can be restored, not whether downstream consequences can be reversed
(
32
)
. For cross-border coordination, major incidents are reported to competent authorities, with onward transmission within the EU supervisory architecture, including to the ESAs and, where relevant, the ECB
(
32
)
.

3.3.6
FSB, Basel III and the G-SIB assessment

The Financial Stability Board, established at the 2009 London G20 summit and hosted by the Bank for International Settlements, has the mandate to “coordinate at the international level the work of national financial authorities and international standard-setting bodies and develop …effective regulatory, supervisory and other financial sector policies in the interest of financial stability”
(
39
)
. While the FSB does not operate an incident-notification regime, it is responsible for identifying institutions and activities whose distress or failure would threaten systemic stability.

The methodology it uses is an indicator-based scoring approach across five equally weighted categories: size, interconnectedness, substitutability and financial-institution infrastructure, complexity, and cross-jurisdictional activity. Banks with a total Basel III leverage-exposure measure exceeding EUR 200 billion are included in the assessment sample
(
13
)
. This illustrates an alternative approach to event-based notification: upfront classification of the entity itself as systemic, with elevated capital requirements, resolution planning, and supervisory scrutiny attaching to classification rather than to any particular incident.

3.3.7
Basel II operational risk: event taxonomy and near-miss treatment

The Basel II operational-risk framework provides a developed international taxonomy for loss-event classification. It defines operational risk as the risk of loss resulting from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, and systems, or from external events, and categorizes loss events into seven types: internal fraud, external fraud, employment practices and workplace safety, clients/products/business practices, damage to physical assets, business disruption and system failures, and execution/delivery/process management
(
10
)
.

Alongside this taxonomy, Basel II set an explicit severity benchmark: banks’ internal risk models must be calibrated to a 99.9th percentile confidence interval over a one-year period, meaning the model must capture what would happen in the worst 0.1% of cases
(
11
)
. This remains the standard tail-risk benchmark in banking supervision, and is the clearest example in international regulation of a threshold defined by statistical severity rather than by event description.

On near misses, the Basel Committee’s
Principles for the Sound Management of Operational Risk
(BCBS 195) require banks to supplement actual loss data with scenario analysis and to treat near-miss events as relevant inputs to risk measurement even where they do not generate a recorded financial loss
(
12
)
. Near misses feed into scenario analysis rather than directly into capital calculations: they inform understanding of how often hazard conditions arise, without needing to have caused actual harm.

3.3.8
IAEA: early notification and the INES severity scale

The nuclear regime was built in direct response to crisis. The IAEA’s Convention on Early Notification of a Nuclear Accident was adopted five months after the Chernobyl accident in 1986
(
50
)
. Its escalation trigger is not a severity threshold but a propagation condition: States must notify the IAEA of any nuclear accident whose radiological consequences could cross a national boundary, regardless of how severe the incident is domestically.

The severity-classification element came four years later with the International Nuclear and Radiological Event Scale (INES), a seven-level scale from
Anomaly
(Level 1) to
Major Accident
(Level 7)
(
51
)
. Importantly, INES is used for public communication rather than as a legal reporting trigger: notification obligations still turn on the transboundary test. The nuclear regime thus decouples the decision to escalate from the severity rating, whereas NIS2, DORA, and the IHR integrate them more closely.

3.3.9
ICAO Annex 13/19 and Seveso III: near misses and the no-blame principle

Civil aviation and major industrial-accident regimes jointly provide the most developed precedent for near-miss reporting. ICAO Annex 13 governs aircraft accident and incident investigation; paragraph 3.1 of Chapter 3 states: “The sole objective of the investigation of an accident or incident shall be the prevention of accidents and incidents. It is not the purpose of this activity to apportion blame or liability”
(
53
)
. This no-blame principle has been essential to achieving the reporting rates needed to make near-miss data statistically useful; without it, the incentive to report is dominated by the incentive to avoid liability.

ICAO Annex 19 builds the system-wide architecture around this: mandatory and voluntary reporting systems, State Safety Programmes, and operator-level Safety Management Systems together provide the formal infrastructure for treating near misses as important data
(
52
)
.

The closest analogue outside aviation is the Seveso III Directive on major-accident hazards involving dangerous substances
(
95
)
. Seveso III is notable for two design choices. First, obligations are triggered by what a facility handles—substance-quantity thresholds—rather than by something having gone wrong, meaning scope is determined before any incident occurs. Second, the reporting regime explicitly covers near misses (“dangerous occurrences”) alongside actual accidents, establishing the principle that hazard indicators, not just realized harms, warrant systematic collection and international aggregation.

3.3.10
Metrics used for propagation in other domains

Several of the frameworks above use quantitative metrics for how fast an incident spreads and how quickly it is detected.

Reproduction numbers.

In epidemiology, the basic reproduction number
R_{0}
measures how many secondary infections a single case produces in a fully susceptible population; the effective reproduction number
R_{e}
adjusts for immunity, behavior change, and intervention
(
5
)
. An
R_{e}>1
means the outbreak is growing; an
R_{e}<1
means it is shrinking. While the IHR do not explicitly name
R_{0}
, their “significant risk of international spread” criterion is in practice assessed using reproduction-number estimates.

Doubling time and serial interval.

Doubling time measures how quickly cumulative cases double; the serial interval measures the time between successive cases in a transmission chain
(
5
)
. These are speed metrics: they describe not whether propagation is occurring, but how fast.

Breakout time.

In cybersecurity, breakout time is the interval from initial compromise to the first lateral movement into the wider network
(
23
)
. It defines the minimum window defenders have to detect and contain an intrusion before it expands, and is the cyber equivalent of the serial interval: a speed metric rather than a severity metric.

MTTD and MTTR.

Mean Time to Detect (MTTD) and Mean Time to Respond (MTTR) help determine how far an incident spreads before it is addressed. Two cases illustrate why both matter. In the SolarWinds campaign, attackers maintained access for approximately fourteen months before discovery, during which compromised updates propagated to roughly 18,000 organizations: a long, undetected spread
(
24
)
. NotPetya, by contrast, was designed for near-simultaneous detonation across shared infrastructure, with global damages exceeding USD 10 billion: no detection window existed because the propagation pathway was effectively instantaneous
(
42
)
. The common thread is that propagation potential alone can be an escalation-relevant signal, independent of realized harm.

3.3.11
Incident typologies

Incident typologies refer to the structures that frameworks use to classify incidents. These structures matter because harm manifests in different ways: as acute, discrete events in some cases, and as diffuse or cumulative patterns across affected populations in others. The classification approach therefore determines which incident types an escalation framework will detect and escalate. Across the domains reviewed, four broad distinctions recur.

Event-based versus loss-based framings (what happened vs. what was lost).

Some regimes treat the unit of escalation as a discrete event, for example an outbreak, a release, or an accident, where severity is judged by the nature of the event itself. Others, most notably Basel II, treat the unit as the realized financial loss, and classify the underlying event only as a secondary step
(
10
)
. NIS2 and DORA sit in between: they describe incidents in event-based terms but use loss-based thresholds to decide when something counts as a major incident, for example economic impact above EUR 100,000 or duration longer than 24 hours. For general-purpose AI, a hybrid framing is likely to be appropriate: some incidents have a clear event structure, while others are better characterized by how harm is distributed across many affected users.

Expected versus tail outcomes (routine risk vs. extraordinary risk).

Basel draws a line at the 99.9th percentile, separating routine risk management from tail-risk management: what is likely to happen versus what would happen only in the worst 0.1% of cases
(
11
)
. The same distinction carries over to AI: everyday system errors are expected and managed day to day, while rare but catastrophic failures are a systemic-risk problem requiring a different governance response. For routine risk, spikes in volumes can also indicate a more material risk.

Frequency–severity decomposition (how often vs. how bad).

Operational-risk modeling treats frequency and severity as separate questions. This is why near-miss reporting matters: a near miss produces no harm, but it still indicates how often the conditions for harm are arising. Collecting near-miss data is, in effect, a way of using frequency signals to sharpen estimates of what severe outcomes could look like
(
12
)
.

Correlated, related, and single-event incidents.

Three distinct multi-incident patterns appear across the comparator set, each requiring different handling. Single events are the default unit in the IHR and IAEA regimes. Related events, defined as incidents sharing a common root cause, are aggregated explicitly under DORA within a rolling six-month window
(
27
)
. Correlated events, defined as incidents without a shared root cause but occurring under similar conditions or exposures, are addressed in frameworks such as MITRE ATT&CK, where individually low-severity observations may form steps in a coherent attack chain that is considerably more severe than any single component
(
68
)
.

3.4
Current AI incident tracking and its challenges

Designing escalation criteria and triggers requires reliable incident data. In practice, however, the current landscape of AI incident tracking presents two interrelated challenges. First, many AI incidents do not lend themselves to the kind of clear, standardized classification that trigger systems depend on. Second, the data infrastructure used to capture incidents has not been designed with operational escalation in mind. Together, these constraints limit how precisely and reliably escalation thresholds can be defined and applied.

To ground what follows, consider a state-sponsored disinformation campaign in which AI-generated content is used to flood information ecosystems across multiple countries. This is the scenario documented across Incidents 727, 884, 929 and 968 in the AIID.

Challenges intrinsic to the nature of AI incidents.

Temporal ambiguity.
Many AI incidents have no clear start or end date. Ongoing harms such as AI-generated disinformation campaigns are not isolated events but continuously adapting phenomena
(
82
)
. A trigger system must define when an incident begins in order to assess its severity, but for cumulative or evolving harms, that boundary is often arbitrary. The Pravda campaign illustrates this directly: the disinformation infrastructure predates its AI-assisted amplification by years, and no single incident in the cluster has an unambiguous start date.

Multiplicity.
A widely deployed AI system may produce thousands of essentially identical harm instances. The AI Incident Database introduced “incident variants” to manage this, recording one incident per distinct failure mode with variants for notable individual cases
(
63
)
. But the choice of aggregation rule dramatically alters the data, with the same underlying reality recorded as one serious incident or ten thousand minor ones. This has significant implications for whether a severity threshold is crossed. In the Pravda case, each AI chatbot repeating a Kremlin-aligned narrative could plausibly be recorded as a separate incident or as a variant of a single systemic failure; the aggregation choice determines whether any threshold is met.

Causal attribution.
The AIID uses a counterfactual “but-for” test: would the harm have occurred but for the AI system?
82
argue this is not merely practically difficult but inherently unknowable, and mistakenly treats real and hypothetical data as equally valid. The AIID compensates by setting a low causal bar where AI need only be a necessary link in the causal chain, not the primary cause, but this trades precision for coverage, admitting incidents where the AI’s contribution ranges from marginal to decisive. Whether AI “caused” the harm in the Pravda case or amplified a pre-existing propaganda infrastructure is precisely the kind of counterfactual that cannot be resolved at the point of assessment.

Near-misses and probing behavior.
Existing databases systematically exclude incidents that do not generate actualized harm. The AIID explicitly filters out theoretical flaws and unrealized vulnerabilities
(
63
)
; the OECD folded near-misses into its broader “hazards” category because experts could not reliably distinguish them from other non-harm events
(
76
)
. This creates a structural blind spot for precisely the signals that may matter most for long-term safety: an AI system that tests boundaries without crossing them, exhibiting probing behavior to determine what it can access, what triggers oversight, and what goes unnoticed. This generates no recordable harm but may constitute the most important early warning for loss-of-control trajectories which require international response.

Adversarial adaptation.
Unlike the systems monitored in aviation or nuclear safety, advanced AI systems may strategically adapt to avoid detection. Apollo Research identified “scheming” behavior, where models pursue alternative objectives while appearing compliant
(
65
)
, and separate work has documented “sandbagging”, where models deliberately underperform during capability evaluations
(
99
)
. This means that evaluation-based thresholds can be actively undermined by the systems they are designed to monitor, and that trigger reliability may degrade as systems become more capable. The implication is that single-point capability evaluations are an insufficient basis for escalation triggers, and that continuous monitoring for behavioral patterns across deployments is a design necessity. This constraint is one motivation for the pattern-detection mechanism at Criterion 4.

Cross-incident pattern blindness.
Current databases record individual incidents, but systemic risk often emerges from correlations between incidents rather than their individual severity.
55
observe that a prompt injection vulnerability in one company’s chatbot and a similar exploit in others appear unrelated unless someone aggregates the data. A trigger system focused on single-incident thresholds could miss distributed attacks or convergent failure modes. This is compounded by the unknown developer problem: over a third of recent incidents cannot be attributed to a known AI developer
(
15
)
, making it impossible to check whether seemingly unrelated incidents share a common upstream cause. In the Pravda case, the link between Incidents 727, 884, 929 and 968 was established through intelligence analysis and investigative journalism, not through anything visible in AI developer monitoring systems. Without cross-incident visibility, the cluster would appear as four unrelated events.

Reporting bias.
The data upon which any trigger system operates is self-reported and media-dependent. AIID acknowledges that all global reporting represents only a fraction of the lived realities of those experiencing AI harms
(
15
)
. The OECD’s pipeline, despite processing over 150,000 articles daily
(
76
)
, cannot verify the accuracy of its sources and is skewed toward English-language outlets. This creates a two-sided distortion: sensational incidents are over-represented, while gradual or invisible harms are under-represented. Discrimination incidents actually decreased in 2025 reporting despite no evidence the underlying problem improved, suggesting reporting patterns track media attention cycles rather than harm trajectories
(
15
)
. For the Pravda campaign, systematic effects in non-Anglophone countries would be substantially undercounted by any pipeline dependent on English-language sources.

Contextual calibration.
Even if standardisation were achievable in the abstract, a trigger calibrated for one jurisdiction’s risk profile may be meaningless for another. A nation heavily dependent on AI-managed financial infrastructure has a different exposure surface from one where primary AI deployment is in agriculture or public administration. Universal triggers therefore carry the risk of leaving countries under- or over-protected, a challenge compounded by the geographic and linguistic biases already identified in current reporting pipelines
(
82
;
76
)
.

The reversibility of harm is treated as a distinct assessment dimension in Criterion 7 and the Irreversible Harm section below.

Challenges in current data practice.

Sophisticated threshold frameworks from other domains depend on granular, well-attributed incident data. In pharmaceutical regulation, adverse event reports are linked to specific drug formulations and batch numbers, enabling rapid identification of product-level risk signals. AI incident data currently operates at a level of attribution granularity closer to “a medicine caused harm” than to “batch #4721 of 50mg formulation X caused harm.” The specific infrastructure gaps that produce this disparity are set out below.

Completeness.
AI incident reporting is voluntary, unsystematic, and overwhelmingly media-dependent. The AIID relies on crowd-sourced submissions filtered through editorial review; the OECD’s AIM Monitor processes news articles from Event Registry. Neither receives structured reports from the entities best positioned to detect incidents: the AI developers and deployers themselves. In financial services, by contrast, Basel II mandates that firms collect and report internal loss data above defined thresholds, creating a dataset that is incomplete by design but systematically incomplete in a way that can be modeled. AI incident data is arbitrarily incomplete: what gets captured depends on whether a journalist noticed, whether a victim reported it, and whether the harm was visible enough to generate a news story.

Timeliness.
The most automated existing pipeline, the OECD AIM Monitor, processes events from one to four days after first registration, with a hard four-day window for article collection. Articles appearing later are likely to be misclassified as a separate event, fragmenting the picture of evolving incidents. Most AIID entries are added weeks or months after the incident occurred. For a trigger system intended to activate international response, this latency is operationally disqualifying for fast-moving incidents. By comparison, financial transaction monitoring systems flag suspicious activity in real time, and aviation incident reporting mandates initial notification within hours.

Structured severity data.
Even where incidents are recorded, the vast majority lack the structured annotations a trigger system would need. Only 214 of over 750 AIID incidents had received CSET AI Harm Taxonomy annotations, and only 188 had GMF taxonomy classifications, at the time of
82
’s review
(
82
)
. A trigger system cannot query “show me all incidents involving physical harm to more than 1,000 people in critical infrastructure sectors” against a database where most entries lack fields for harm type, affected population size, or sector. The MIT AI Risk Repository found that severity coding had a 20% disagreement rate between LLM-classified and human-classified ratings
(
92
)
, a margin unacceptable for operational trigger decisions.

Verification.
No existing system independently verifies the accuracy of the incident reports it ingests. The OECD explicitly states that it cannot guarantee and does not independently verify the accuracy, completeness, or validity of third-party information. The AIID’s editors guide documents specific cognitive biases affecting human curation, including entity bias, the tendency to accept allegations against disliked companies with insufficient evidence. In domains where triggers have operational consequences, there are independent investigative bodies whose assessments carry institutional authority precisely because they are verified. AI incident data currently lacks any equivalent verification layer.

Data flow from developers.
Perhaps the most consequential gap is that data does not flow from AI developers into any public incident infrastructure. Developers hold privileged technical knowledge about model behavior, capability evaluations, and internal safety incidents, creating systematic information asymmetry. Company frameworks such as Anthropic’s RSP, OpenAI’s Preparedness Framework, and DeepMind’s FSF generate internal incident and capability data, but this remains proprietary. When incidents do enter public databases, they are typically stripped of the technical detail (model version, system architecture, evaluation results) that would be most useful for severity classification. The EU AI Act’s Article 73 mandates serious incident reporting for high-risk AI systems from August 2026, which represents the first regulatory attempt to compel data flow, but its scope excludes general-purpose AI under the main reporting obligation.

Attribution to specific systems.
The attribution problem extends beyond unknown developers. Even where a developer is identified, incidents are rarely linked to specific model versions, deployment configurations, or capability profiles. The AIID lacks fields for application names; the AIAAIC lacks fields for affected parties
(
2
)
. A trigger system cannot answer questions essential for detecting systemic risk patterns: whether this is the same model involved in a previous incident, or whether three seemingly separate incidents share a common upstream capability. In pharmaceutical regulation, the equivalent questions are answered as a matter of routine; in AI incident data, they are largely unanswerable.

These sub-problems interact and compound. The Pravda campaign brings them together in a single case: an ongoing disinformation operation (temporal ambiguity) affecting millions of users across multiple countries (multiplicity) where AI-generated content was one contributing factor among many (causal ambiguity), with incident linkages only identifiable through intelligence sources outside any developer’s monitoring systems (cross-incident blindness), and effects in non-Anglophone countries systematically undercounted (reporting bias), with no data flowing from affected AI developers to any public registry (data flow gap). A trigger system must simultaneously determine when such an incident started, how to count its harms, how much to attribute to AI, and how urgently to respond, with defensible answers to none of these questions readily available.

These challenges directly shape the design choices in the eight criteria that follow. Temporal ambiguity and multiplicity inform the causal confidence modifier at Criterion 1 and the rolling-window aggregation logic at Criterion 4. The near-miss gap motivates the standalone treatment at Criterion 8. Adversarial adaptation, and its implication that evaluation-based triggers are unreliable, is a further rationale for continuous pattern monitoring at Criterion 4. The data infrastructure limitations, particularly the absence of cross-developer data flows, are why the methodology section distinguishes between criteria assessable by individual providers and those that require a central coordinating body.

3.5
Propagation dynamics: from common exposure to network contagion

Risk propagation operates through three distinct mechanisms.
Common exposure
produces simultaneous failures across entities sharing an upstream dependency without contagion between them; existing supply-chain frameworks are broadly adequate
(
87
)
.
Environmental coupling
operates through a shared medium, for example in a financial market where each participant’s actions alter the price all others respond to, creating amplifying feedback. Perrow
(
84
)
identified tight coupling and interactive complexity as preconditions for “normal accidents” (failures inevitable from the system’s architecture), and Maas
(
61
)
showed AI deployments increasingly exhibit these properties.
Network contagion
spreads failure entity-to-entity through direct connections. Watts
(
98
)
showed threshold-based activation produces cascades only within a specific connectivity window; Pastor-Satorras and Vespignani
(
83
)
showed single-contact transmission in scale-free networks faces no effective epidemic threshold. These describe different regimes: some AI risks are single-contact (prompt injection), others threshold-dependent (algorithmic collusion); the first demands hub protection, the second structural monitoring.

These dynamics are compounded by interdependence across network layers. Buldyrev et al.
(
16
)
showed interdependent networks undergo abrupt cascading collapse where isolated networks degrade gradually. Gai and Kapadia
(
40
)
identified the “robust-yet-fragile” property: connections diversifying risk normally become contagion channels under stress. Acemoglu et al.
(
1
)
formalized the threshold at which networks switch from shock-absorbing to shock-amplifying. Scheffer et al.
(
89
)
showed that approaching critical transitions produce detectable early-warning signals, e.g. increasing autocorrelation, rising variance, or flickering, enabling anticipatory intervention. Helbing
(
47
)
synthesized these for globally networked risks: contagion, feedback amplification, and cross-domain interdependence produce systemic vulnerabilities no single jurisdiction can address.

For AI incidents, these mechanisms manifest as four operationally distinct propagation types.
Supply-chain propagation
: a vulnerability in a widely-used model or platform is inherited by all downstream systems
(
87
;
16
;
58
)
.
Capability propagation
: the harmful capability itself is replicated or transferred, for example through weight exfiltration, technique reproduction, or capability uplift, spreading risk beyond the original context
(
44
)
.
Emergent propagation
: harm amplifies through use dynamics rather than technical dependencies, producing outcomes not attributable to any individual system
(
19
;
20
)
. Finally,
rate of propagation versus rate of control
: when propagation outpaces containment, a local concern becomes international regardless of pathway type, following the epidemiological logic in which sustained spread requires only that transmission exceed recovery
(
43
;
57
)
. An incident may progress through multiple types in sequence, with the rate comparison determining at each stage whether containment remains feasible.

3.5.1
Propagation in regulated domains

Several mature regulatory domains have developed operational frameworks for managing propagation risk, providing both institutional vocabulary and structural precedents for AI-specific governance.

Financial stability regulation offers the most developed treatment. The Basel Committee’s framework for identifying systemically important banks uses interconnectedness as a primary indicator, recognizing that shared exposures create correlated risk invisible to institution-level supervision
(
13
)
. The 2010 Flash Crash demonstrated this operationally: a single large sell order interacted with algorithmically similar trading strategies to produce a feedback cascade that briefly erased nearly one trillion US dollars in market value within fifteen minutes
(
57
)
. The regulatory response — market-wide circuit breakers that halt trading when price movements exceed defined thresholds — directly implements cascade-interruption logic. The European Systemic Risk Board’s 2025 report extends this analysis explicitly to AI, identifying foundation model concentration, algorithmic monoculture, and AI-driven herding as novel systemic risk channels
(
36
)
.

In critical infrastructure protection, DORA and NIS2 establish mandatory incident classification, reporting timelines, and cross-border coordination for ICT-related disruptions in financial services and essential services respectively
(
32
;
30
)
. DORA requires classification against defined impact criteria, geographic reach, duration, affected clients, with significant incidents reported within strict timescales. NIS2 extends comparable obligations across energy, transport, health, and digital infrastructure, with EU-CyCLONe providing coordination when incidents affect two or more Member States. These frameworks demonstrate that propagation-based escalation triggers are already operational: what elevates an incident from routine to significant is its capacity to propagate across entities, sectors, or borders.

Cyber incident response provides a third model. CSIRT networks coordinate cross-border containment through established protocols for sharing indicators of compromise
(
37
)
. The CrowdStrike outage of July 2024, which was a single faulty update simultaneously disabling approximately 8.5 million Windows devices worldwide, demonstrated both the strengths and limitations: effective technical coordination, but a propagation mechanism (common upstream dependency) outside the adversarial threat model CERT protocols are optimized for
(
66
)
.

The common structural feature across these domains is that escalation triggers are defined by observable downstream impact rather than upstream causal mechanisms. This is appropriate when the cause-effect relationship is well understood but it is inadequate when the causal mechanisms are novel, opaque, or qualitatively unprecedented, but these are precisely the conditions AI regulators will find themselves in as AI continues to be developed and deployed. Existing frameworks catch AI incidents
(
74
)
when they manifest as specific infrastructure disruptions in particular regulatory domains not specific to AI, such as DORA (financial entities), NIS2 (cybersecurity), or CyCLONe (critical digital and communications infrastructure). Consequently, they do not catch AI-driven harms that are unique to AI and do not fit domain-specific harm criteria, such as algorithmic collusion, cooperation failures, or emergent escalation, that can generate significant harm without triggering infrastructure disruption thresholds. The Bletchley/Seoul/Paris summit process recognizes these risk categories but has not translated them into operational triggers with defined thresholds, mandatory notification, or response protocols
(
49
)
.

3.5.2
Cross-border propagation and governance triggers

International coordination becomes necessary when the scope of an incident’s interaction dynamics exceeds the governance capacity of any single jurisdiction
(
47
)
. This structural mismatch is not primarily about harm severity — a high-severity incident within one jurisdiction’s capacity is a domestic matter. It concerns whether any single authority can observe the full incident, attribute responsibility, and implement effective containment.

The mismatch can manifest along three important dimensions.

Observational mismatch
: no single regulator sees the full scope. An AI system trained in one jurisdiction, deployed in a second, and producing effects in a third presents fragments to each authority. In multi-agent settings, interaction dynamics spanning borders through shared environments may be invisible to any observation point. The OECD’s work on AI incident definitions highlights that even identifying what constitutes a single incident becomes difficult when causal chains cross organizational and jurisdictional boundaries
(
74
)
. The ESRB also identifies model uniformity as a source of correlated financial behavior across institutions that amplifies systemic risk
(
36
)
.

Enforcement mismatch
: the authority that can observe may lack jurisdiction to act, and vice versa. The EU AI Act applies primarily to systems placed on the EU market or affecting EU persons, leaving gaps for externally hosted systems whose effects propagate inward
(
35
)
. NIS2 and DORA coordinate within the Union but not with non-EU jurisdictions. The Bletchley/Seoul/Paris commitments are voluntary and lack specified activation thresholds
(
49
)
. For multi-agent incidents, enforcement mismatch compounds with cross-domain fragmentation: an incident spanning financial markets, telecommunications, and information platforms falls between multiple regulators’ mandates even domestically.

Temporal mismatch
: the incident develops faster than institutional response. Circuit breakers in financial markets exist because feedback-driven cascades develop on timescales precluding deliberative response
(
57
)
. AI-mediated propagation exhibits the same asymmetry: adversarial content can spread through LLM agent networks in logarithmic time
(
43
)
, while cross-border coordination operates on timescales of hours to days. When propagation outpaces coordination, containment depends on pre-positioned automatic mechanisms rather than institutional deliberation.

A further challenge specific to AI-mediated propagation is that the relationship between cause and effect may confound established attribution. In conventional cross-border incidents, the causal structure belongs to a known category; attribution is difficult but conceptually defined. In incidents where harm arises from interaction dynamics rather than any single system’s action, attribution may be structurally impossible as the harm is a property of the interaction, not of any participant
(
44
)
. The 2010 Flash Crash remains contested on attribution fifteen years later because the cascade was an emergent property of algorithmic interaction
(
57
)
. For emergent multi-agent incidents, this may require reconceptualising responsibility from identifying which actor caused harm to identifying which structural conditions enabled it.

Existing cross-border mechanisms are either operationally robust but not tailored to AI (DORA, NIS2, CyCLONe catch AI incidents only incidentally), or AI-specific but operationally underdeveloped (Bletchley/Seoul/Paris lack binding thresholds and response protocols). The intersection of: mandatory, AI-specific, and international, remains undeveloped
(
49
)
. The severity threshold framework developed in this work is intended to fill this gap.

3.5.3
Multi-agent risk amplification

The propagation mechanisms described above apply to any networked technology. Multi-agent AI systems do not introduce new propagation mechanisms, but they introduce amplification mechanisms that can accelerate, extend, or qualitatively transform propagation in ways single-system deployments do not
(
44
)
.

The first is the creation of
correlated failure surfaces
. When independently deployed AI systems share upstream dependencies, they exhibit correlated vulnerabilities not visible at the individual deployment level. Kleinberg and Raghavan
(
58
)
formalized this as algorithmic monoculture: shared models produce correlated errors that concentrate rather than diversify risk. Bommasani et al.
(
14
)
demonstrated empirically that foundation model convergence leads to outcome homogenisation. Monoculture transforms the failure distribution from independently distributed (self-limiting) to positively correlated across the entire dependent population, with the shared dependency potentially invisible to deployers, regulators, or the agents themselves.

The second is
emergent coordination through interaction
. When multiple AI agents interact within a shared environment, their collective behavior can produce outcomes no individual agent was designed to produce. Calvano et al.
(
19
)
showed that reinforcement learning agents in pricing games consistently discover supra-competitive pricing through endogenous signals alone, without communication or instructions to collude. Motwani et al.
(
69
)
demonstrated that LLM agents can develop steganographic channels enabling covert coordination while evading monitoring. Emergent coordination transforms environmental coupling into a self-reinforcing feedback loop in such a way that agents’ responses alter the environment that further reinforces the coordinated behavior. Once established, the emergent equilibrium is highly stable and disrupting it requires altering the interaction structure, not remediating any individual agent. Rivera et al.
(
88
)
demonstrated a parallel dynamic in military simulations, where LLM agents exhibit difficult-to-predict escalation patterns emerging from interaction rather than individual disposition.

The third and most dangerous mechanism is the
transition from correlated exposure to emergent coordination
. Gu et al.
(
43
)
showed that a single adversarial image can compromise up to one million multimodal LLM agents whereby each compromised agent propagates the attack to its neighbours. Ju et al.
(
56
)
demonstrated that manipulated knowledge injected into one agent persists and amplifies through retrieval-augmented generation, with downstream agents treating false information as verified fact. Carichon et al.
(
20
)
argue this reflects a fundamental property: alignment is a dynamic social process that degrades through interaction, such that individually aligned agents converge on collectively misaligned equilibria. In each case, propagation begins as single-contact or common-exposure but is amplified by interaction dynamics into a self-sustaining crisis.

These mechanisms intensify each governance challenge identified above. Correlated failure surfaces deepen observational mismatch and the shared dependency may be invisible to any single jurisdiction. Emergent coordination deepens the attribution problem such that responsibility dissolves when harm is introduced via interaction dynamics. The correlation-to-emergence transition deepens temporal mismatch and increases the risk from multi-agent feedback, amplifying vulnerabilities faster than institutional responses can manage. Detection requires monitoring interaction properties between agents, not individual agents and this is a challenge for which statistical and information-theoretic approaches provide a foundation
(
46
;
89
)
, but which remains largely unimplemented in operational governance.

3.6
Irreversible harm and the logic of escalation

When an AI incident produces harm that cannot practically be reversed there is a qualitative change in the governance response needed. Reversible harm is ultimately a question of resources: who pays, how long will recovery take, and whether the responsible actor has the capacity to restore the system to the pre-incident state. Irreversible harm closes off that recovery path. In that case the cost of restoration is essentially undefined and the response must shift from recovery coordination to impact management. This section reviews how existing frameworks treat irreversibility, why those treatments are inadequate for AI incidents, and what foundations from adjacent domains support the layered assessment structure adopted in the present framework.

Existing AI governance instruments treat irreversibility as a property of harm type rather than as an independent assessment dimension. The EU AI Act names irreversibility as a qualifying criterion for critical infrastructure disruption (Article 3(49)(b)) and the GPAI Code of Practice makes it definitional for loss of control risk, defined as situations where regaining control is “either extremely costly or impossible.” But an incident producing moderate yet permanently irreversible harm to fundamental rights, property, or the environment has no accelerated escalation pathway under the Act on the basis of its irreversibility alone. California’s SB 53 does not mention reversibility explicitly; its threshold structure assumes that harm type and magnitude are sufficient for classification. The irreversibility embedded in its harm outcomes, e.g. death, or bodily injury, is classified by the nature of the incident, not by recoverability. The Bletchley/Seoul/Paris declarations reference catastrophic and irreversible risk but provide no operational definition of what irreversibility might mean in practice, and consequently no escalation trigger is covered.

These limitations become apparent when applied to AI incidents where the question is not whether harm
is
irreversible but
at what level
and
on what timescale
irreversibility is measured. A training data poisoning incident may be technically containable (the data source can be blocked) while the model weights are permanently compromised (retraining is the only remedy) and the downstream societal effects (public trust in AI-generated information) are unrecoverable on any feasible timescale. Such a binary framing of irreversibility cannot address these issues. What is needed, and what is proposed here, is a decomposition of recovery into distinct phases, each with its own assessment criteria and temporal characteristics.

The theoretical foundations for this decomposition are well established in environmental economics, where the interaction between irreversibility and uncertainty has been a central concern since
6
. They showed that when an action may produce irreversible environmental damage and the consequences are uncertain, the expected net benefit of the action is reduced by what they termed the quasi-option value: the value of preserving the option to act differently once better information is available. This was later formalized by
45
as a conditional value of information: the gain from learning that is available only if the irreversible action has not yet been taken. The direct policy implication, developed further by
41
as an economic interpretation of the precautionary principle, is that under uncertainty about consequences, irreversibility creates a systematic bias toward caution that decision-makers must incorporate. This is the logic that the present framework operationalizes: when an AI incident’s full consequences are uncertain at the point of triage, and the harm may be irreversible at one or more levels, the framework defaults to provisional high severity, and this encodes the quasi-option value of not prematurely downgrading an incident whose irreversibility has not yet been ruled out.

Operational frameworks in other, more mature, governance domains provide the second foundation. Financial regulation under DORA (Articles 11–12) mandates that entities set, test, and maintain Recovery Time Objectives (RTO) and Recovery Point Objectives (RPO) for critical ICT systems, operationalizing recovery as a measurable, testable property with well-defined temporal thresholds
(
27
)
. The cybersecurity domain under NIS2 (Article 21) treats system and data recoverability as a mandatory operational capability
(
30
)
. The WHO rapid risk assessment framework assesses irreversibility at the level of harm to the affected entity rather than at the level of system restoration, because in health the system and the harmed entity are the same, i.e. the patient
(
103
)
. These frameworks share a common structure of sequential recovery phases but each operates at the level of the entity experiencing the incident, not at the level of downstream consequences. For example a financial regulator wants to know whether or not the bank can restore its systems back to their previous capacity and capability, not whether any market distortions have been corrected. This entity-level framing is not adequate for AI incidents whose consequences have the potential to propagate far beyond the system that produced them. The framework therefore adopts a four-layer sequential assessment in which each layer is a precondition for the next, each operates on a characteristic timescale, and each maps onto established analogues in existing governance domains:

Table 3:
Four layers of reversibility assessment.

Layer

Core question

Layer 1: Process containment

Can the harm-producing process be halted?

This corresponds to the immediate incident response phase in cyber (isolate the compromised system), finance (halt trading), and health (quarantine). Assessment is typically possible in real time.

Layer 2: Control restoration

Can the responsible actor regain the ability to direct, modify, or shut down the system?

This is distinct from containment: a system may be contained (no longer producing new harm) while the responsible actor has permanently lost the ability to control it. The GPAI Code of Practice’s definition of loss of control risk maps directly to failure at this layer. Loss of control at Layer 2 is treated as categorical: if control cannot be restored, all downstream layers are assessed as
D=1
(fully irreversible, see definition of
D
after Layer 4 below) by definition, because technical and societal state restoration both require control as a precondition.

Layer 3: Technical state restoration

Can the pre-incident technical state—model weights, training data integrity, system dynamics—be recovered?

This is the AI-specific analogue of DORA’s RPO: how much is unrecoverable? The AI-specific complication is that model weights and training data do not have clean snapshot semantics in the way a database does. Restoring to RPO for a poisoned model may mean full retraining, a qualitatively different operation from restoring a database backup, with costs and timescales that may render recovery effectively irreversible in the sense of
45
: possible but economically unviable.

Layer 4: Structural and societal state restoration

Can the pre-incident societal state—institutional trust, market structure, democratic processes, etc.—be recovered?

This layer has no direct analogue in existing operational frameworks, which stop at entity-level restoration. The closest institutional recognition is the Financial Stability Board’s work on systemically important financial institutions, which implicitly acknowledges that restoring a single institution does not restore market confidence or systemic stability

. Layer 4 assessment may take months or years and is inherently beyond purely technical considerations: whether societal restoration is “feasible” depends on jurisdictional resources and institutional capacity.

The sequential structuring is more than an organizational framing, it encodes a conditional logic for the assessment of an incident’s recoverability: Containment (L1) is a precondition for meaningful control assessment (L2), because control cannot be evaluated while the incident is still actively propagating. Control (L2) is a precondition for technical restoration (L3), because you cannot restore a system you cannot direct. Technical restoration (L3) is a precondition for societal restoration (L4), because societal recovery depends on the technical system functioning as intended. This sequencing also implies increasing timescale and decreasing certainty in the initial assessment: L1 is assessable in hours, L2 in days to weeks, L3 in weeks to months, and L4 may not be assessable for years. The framework addresses this by requiring worst-case provisional estimates at an incident’s outset, this is then revised as information becomes available and so it implements the Arrow-Fisher insight that under uncertainty and irreversibility the value of waiting for better information before committing to a low-severity classification is strictly positive.

The Russian state-sponsored disinformation ecosystem documented in the AIID (Incidents 727, 884, 929, 968) illustrates both the four-layer structure and the target-state problem. The Pravda network and its predecessor infrastructure have flooded the internet with approximately 3.6 million articles per year, and a 2025 audit found that major AI chatbots repeated Pravda-sourced narratives as fact with alarming frequency. At Layer 1, containment is partially achievable: individual data sources can be blocked but new ones can be generated. At Layer 2, control cannot be fully restored: no single entity controls all affected training pipelines across the multiple foundation model providers whose systems have ingested the poisoned content. At Layer 3, technical state restoration would require coordinated retraining of affected LLMs across the industry. This would be a cost and coordination challenge that renders full restoration effectively irreversible. At Layer 4, the societal state, such as public trust in AI-generated information, the integrity of the informational environment, etc., is subject to ongoing degradation with no clear recovery pathway.

This case also motivates the target-state concept. When full reversal to the pre-incident state is technically possible but practically infeasible, the framework establishes a “target state” at which the incident is considered remediated, with
D
defined as the residual harm at that target state. This is an established practice in environmental remediation, where regulatory frameworks distinguish between “greenfield” restoration (full return to pre-contamination state) and “brownfield” standards (acceptable residual contamination levels calibrated to intended future use)
(
97
)
. The question for good governance practice is whether or not the residual
D
at the negotiated target state has consequences that warrant international coordination. A jurisdiction may determine that its AI systems can operate at an acceptable performance level despite residual training data contamination, while that contamination may have downstream impacts on other jurisdictions’ informational environments that those jurisdictions cannot independently assess or remedy. The cross-border irreversibility test here is whether the permanent damage in one jurisdiction has consequences that extend beyond it by virtue of interconnection, dependency, or precedent.

4
Incident escalation framework

In this section, we present an AI incident escalation framework designed as a sequential flowchart with gated decision points and threshold checks, built around eight criteria for determining when an AI incident should be escalated internationally. For each criterion, we map how it interplays with the EU AI Act, SB 53, and the GPAI Code of Practice, identifying where these frameworks’ design choices support or undermine effective detection.

Full assessment analysis of each criterion and comparisons against regulatory thresholds (EU AI Act and SB 53), existing incident monitoring approaches, and approaches from other domains are provided in Appendix C, while Appendix B provides the full list of candidate criteria considered for inclusion in the framework, and rationale for the final criteria selection.

4.1
Using the framework

The framework is designed to be walked through as a series of questions, starting at the entry point of “Incident detection” in the flowchart. While a detailed review of detection channels is out of the scope of this paper, we broadly consider developers of AI foundation models and existing AI incident databases as sources where incidents are likely to be identified at present.

Each question in the flowchart corresponds to an assessment criterion, for which a detailed explanation and set of questions is provided. The responses determine the pathway through the flowchart, which ends in a classification decision: escalate, discard, or alert, which corresponds to when international cooperation is required to investigate or respond effectively, even where the incident itself is geographically local.

Summary of criteria

Criterion

Name

Purpose

1

Was AI a causal factor?

Establishes whether an AI system was involved in causing the incident and captures the confidence level in AI’s causal contribution (high, medium, or low) before any further assessment proceeds.

Determines whether any domain-based exclusions (e.g. military, defense, national security) apply. The paper proposes no excluded domains, to maintain visibility of all incidents with severe harm potential.

Detects whether the incident shares a root cause (technical, capability, or contextual) with prior incidents or near misses, so that individually sub-threshold incidents can be assessed collectively as a composite cluster.

5a

Determines whether the harm falls within a recognized category using the MIT harm taxonomy (physical harm, financial loss, environmental damage, privacy, democratic norms, etc.), ensuring novel or non-traditional harm types are not excluded.

5b

Has harm crossed a relevant severity threshold?

Assesses whether harm in any single category has reached Level 4 (Severe) or Level 5 (Catastrophic) on the MIT severity scale, providing a graduated threshold between the EU AI Act’s qualitative “serious” standard and SB 53’s high quantitative floors.

6

Is international coordination required to contain or respond to cross-border propagation?

Determines whether the incident’s spread dynamics, via supply-chain, capability, or emergent propagation pathways, create an operational need for international coordination to contain the incident or warn exposed jurisdictions.

7

Does irreversible harm require international coordination for its cross-border consequences?

Captures contained incidents where the permanent, non-recoverable portion of the harm has downstream effects on other jurisdictions by virtue of interconnection, dependency, or precedent, even though the incident itself is not actively spreading.

8

Has a near miss or hazard indicated inadequately mitigated harm?

Captures events where harm was closely averted but the revealed failure mode may affect AI systems deployed by other developers or jurisdictions, warranting an alert even though no harm materialized.

4.3
Descriptions of criteria

Criterion 1: Was AI a causal factor?

Purpose

Criterion 1 functions as a gateway filter, establishing whether an AI system was a causal factor in the incident before any further assessment is undertaken. Where AI involvement is confirmed or where confidence in AI’s causal contribution is assessed as medium or high, the incident is retained for evaluation against the remaining criteria. Where AI involvement cannot be established and confidence is assessed as medium/high, the incident is excluded from the escalation process. In cases where confidence is low and evidence remains incomplete or contested, the incident is provisionally retained, with parallel confidence-building activities initiated pending a final determination.

Questions

Q1.
Was AI present in the causal chain?

(Yes / No)

Q2.
What is the confidence in AI’s causal contribution?

High — known / observed

Medium — potential / likely

Low — unclear / disputed

Overall response:

Yes if Q1 = Yes, regardless of Q2 confidence level. If Q2 = Low, this is noted and confidence-building activities are initiated in parallel.

If Q1 = No, with High / Medium confidence in Q2, overall response = No (Disregard). If Q2 confidence is Low, confidence-building activities are initiated to resolve the uncertainty before proceeding.

Legislative Approaches

SB 53 uses “materially contribute,” a stronger test that excludes merely incidental involvement, especially in multi-software chains.

The EU AI Act uses “causal relationship” including direct or indirect causation, and the GPAI Code further allows reporting when that relationship is only reasonably likely or, for death, even suspected.

Cross-Domain Precedent

Finance:
DORA Article 17 requires financial entities to identify all ICT-related incidents where a system malfunction is the proximate cause, without requiring attribution to a specific threat actor, prioritizing early identification over precise causal determination.

Health:
National IHR Focal Points assess whether a public health event is “caused by or associated with” a listed disease or novel pathogen, with the causal agent not needing to be confirmed at the point of notification. The WHO rapid risk assessment guidance additionally captures “confidence” in the data available at the time of assessment.

Challenges / Limitations

Escalation trade-off.
Trade-off with escalation decision based on confidence level: escalating with low confidence may risk unnecessary disruption / alarm if no actual AI causality, and reduce confidence in the escalation process, but escalation delays could risk avoidable harm propagation.

Multi-agent attribution.
For multi-agent environments, it may be hard to establish which AI systems are causally responsible for an incident, particularly if there is a lack of information about deployed AI systems / agents and a lack of data on their shared interactions.

Criterion 2: Is the incident in scope?

Purpose

Some regulatory frameworks exclude specific domains, such as military, defense, or national security, from their scope. This criterion determines whether any such exclusions apply.

Questions

Q1.
Is the AI system deployed in a domain excluded from the applicable regulatory framework?

Yes / No

Overall response:

No excluded domains under this framework. All incidents where AI causal involvement has been established at criterion 1 proceed to criterion 3 regardless of domain.

Legislative Approaches

EU AI Act:
The EU AI Act uses explicit purpose-based exclusions for AI systems placed on the market or put into service for military, defense, or national security purposes, while also making clear that dual-use systems can come back into scope when deployed for non-excluded purposes.

SB 53:
SB 53, by contrast, does not set out the same kind of general domain-based exclusion logic; instead, it contains narrower carve-outs, including for lawful activity of the federal government and for information a model outputs where that information is already publicly available in a substantially similar form from another source.

Cross-Domain Precedent

Cybersecurity:
NIS2 (Directive (EU) 2022/2555) Article 2(7) explicitly excludes public administration entities carrying out activities in the areas of national security, public security, defense or law enforcement — a parallel to the EU AI Act’s purpose-based exclusion.

AI Incident Databases:
The AI incident databases (OECD AIM, AIID, MIT) operate with no excluded domains, capturing incidents across all sectors and deployment contexts. This breadth-first approach maximizes coverage but does not address whether captured incidents in excluded domains can be acted upon.

Challenges / Limitations

Military and national security.
Excluded domains such as military or national security may preclude preventative action or direct containment within this framework. While an incident originating in an excluded domain could in principle warrant international coordination for warning, irreversible harm, or information gaps, these functions would in practice be managed through existing national defense and security coordination structures rather than a civilian AI incident escalation framework.

Scope of dual-use AI systems.
Dual-use AI systems may fall in and out of scope depending on deployment context (as in EU AI Act), creating classification ambiguity at the point of incident assessment.

Criterion 3: Has an immediate escalation condition been met?

Purpose

Criterion 3 identifies a narrow set of incident types where escalation should be triggered immediately, regardless of whether confirmed harm has yet materialized. These are cases where catastrophic harm has not yet occurred but the probability of severe, potentially irreversible harm without action is high, and no single actor has visibility over the risk pathway and resulting harm.

Questions

Q1.
Has expert-level assistance in the creation or release of a chemical, biological, radiological, or nuclear (CBRN) weapon been provided?

Yes / No

Q2.
Has there been exfiltration of weights of a model whose unmitigated capabilities pose a significant uplift in misuse risk, where containment has not been confirmed?

Yes / No

Q3.
Does the AI developer lack confirmed ability to intervene, correct, or shut down an system (or network of AI agents)?

Yes / No

Overall response:

Yes to any single question = Escalate immediately. No to all = proceed to criterion 4.

Legislative Approaches

EU AI Act:
The EU AI Act Article 3(49) Serious Incident definition is based on harm thresholds: death of a person, or serious harm to a person’s health; serious and irreversible disruption of the management or operation of critical infrastructure; serious harm to property or the environment. Only infringement of obligations under Union law intended to protect fundamental rights operates without a harm threshold, however, this may be difficult to identify from data monitoring unless proxy triggers were defined.

SB 53:
SB 53 largely combines incident types with harm thresholds. Critical safety incidents (§1107(d)(1)–(3)) pair incident types with specified harm outcomes: weight exfiltration resulting in death, bodily injury, or property damage; materialisation of catastrophic risk; loss of control causing death or bodily injury. Only §1107(d)(4) “deceptive techniques against the developer to subvert controls in a manner demonstrating materially increased catastrophic risk”, functions as a trigger without requiring specified harm to have occurred.

Cross-Domain Precedent

Health:
The WHO IHR Annex 2 establishes an “always notify” list: any occurrence of smallpox, wild-type poliovirus, novel influenza subtype with human transmission, or SARS constitutes an automatic notification obligation to the WHO regardless of severity assessment. This provides the clearest precedent for unconditional escalation triggers based on incident type alone.

Cybersecurity:
NIS2 Article 3 combined with Annexes I and II classifies certain entities in critical sectors (energy, transport, health, digital infrastructure, space) as “essential”, subjecting them to mandatory reporting for any significant incident without further severity pre-filtering: entity classification itself functions as the first-stage trigger.

Challenges / Limitations

Updating set of conditions.
As AI capabilities and deployment contexts evolve, the set of conditions warranting immediate escalation will need to be reviewed and updated. The EU AI Act’s commitment to annual review of Article 5 prohibited practices (Article 112) provides a precedent for building this into the framework’s governance.

Criterion 4: Is the incident part of a broader pattern?

Criterion 4: Is the incident part of a broader pattern? (correlated / related incidents)

Purpose

Criterion 4 assesses whether an incident is part of a broader pattern of correlated or related incidents. Systemic risk often emerges from the correlation between incidents rather than their individual severity: a series of individually sub-threshold incidents sharing a common root cause may collectively constitute a pattern warranting escalation that no single incident would trigger on its own.

Questions

Q1.
Does this incident share a root cause with any known prior incident(s) or near misses?

Yes / No

Root cause is defined across three levels:

•
Technical:
same model, API layer, training dataset, deployment framework, shared upstream component

•
Capability:
same model behavior or characteristic arising independently across different models (e.g. sycophancy, hallucination-driven harm, persuasive targeting)

•
Contextual:
same deployment pattern, interaction design, or affected user population across different systems (e.g. companion chatbot interaction dynamics, autonomous agent tool-use patterns)

Q2.
If Q1 = Yes, has a related incident occurred within the applicable rolling time window?

• approximately 30 days for technical root causes (where shared infrastructure creates rapid correlated failures)

• approximately 60 days for contextual root causes (where similar deployment patterns or affected populations may take longer to identify as related, particularly when they span different systems or providers)

• approximately 90 days for capability root causes (where cross-provider patterns inherently take longer to surface as they require accumulating reports across independent providers)

Overall response:

If Q1 = Yes and Q2 = Yes (related incident within time window): assess as composite incident with aggregate harm values. The cluster proceeds through criteria 5a–5b using aggregated severity.

If Q1 = Yes and Q2 = No (root cause match but no related incident within window): assess as individual incident. Proceed to criterion 5a.

If Q1 = No: assess as individual incident. Proceed to criterion 5a.

Legislative Approaches

EU AI Act:
The EU AI Act’s systemic risk definition acknowledges propagation “at scale across the value chain” and includes a requirement to include patterns detected during post-market monitoring that are related to a serious incident but does not operationalize a pattern-detection mechanism.

SB 53:
SB 53’s framing around “a single incident” with no correlated-incident aggregation provision represents a coverage gap for cumulative incident types.

Cross-Domain Precedent

Finance:
The DORA Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1772 requires financial entities to aggregate related ICT incidents sharing the same root cause or occurring within a rolling 6-month window, assessing the aggregated impact against materiality thresholds rather than each incident individually. The Basel II event taxonomy provides complementary guidance: loss events with a common root cause may be aggregated as a single event, and “boundary events” (losses classifiable under multiple event types) are assessed for systemic risk implications.

Cybersecurity:
MITRE ATT&CK maps adversary tactics, techniques and procedures (TTPs) as campaign patterns rather than isolated events: a series of individually low-severity observations (initial access, discovery, lateral movement) constitutes a high-severity campaign pattern when assessed collectively, with MITRE ATLAS extending this approach to AI-specific attack chains

Challenges / Limitations

Tracking prerequisites.
Identifying patterns relating to a serious incident being assessed requires tracking and analytics of incidents to be done and for clustering patterns to have been defined.

Monitoring level.
The level at which monitoring is done has implications for the incident patterns which can be identified within and across AI model developers.

Cross-provider visibility.
Pattern identification at the capability and contextual root cause levels requires cross-provider incident visibility that is unlikely to be achievable by individual providers acting alone. In practice, these checks can only be performed by a central coordinating body with access to incident data across providers. Technical root cause matching is the only level that a single provider can reliably perform at point of triage.

Taxonomy fragmentation.
The use of different taxonomies and classification approaches across providers and incident databases increases the difficulty of building a cross-provider picture of systemic risk and incident patterns.

Centralized coordination.
Clustering necessitates tracking and analytics of incidents: the level at which monitoring is performed (individual provider vs. centrally coordinated) has direct implications for which incident patterns can be identified.

Criterion 5a: Has harm occurred in a relevant category?

Purpose

Criterion 5a determines whether the harm falls within a recognized category before severity is assessed. This step ensures the broadest possible range of AI-related harms is captured, including harm types such as fundamental rights infringements, democratic norm violations, and environmental damage that have no equivalent trigger under some existing frameworks, and that novel incident types are not excluded simply because they were not pre-defined in regulation.

Questions

Q1.
Has harm occurred within one or more of the categories in the MIT harm taxonomy?

Yes / No

Categories: physical harm, infrastructure damage, property damage, financial loss, environmental damage, toxic or malicious content, differential treatment, human / civil rights, democratic norms, and privacy.

Overall response:

If Q1 = Yes: proceed to criterion 5b for severity assessment in each identified harm category.

If Q1 = No: proceed to criterion 6 (the incident may still warrant escalation on the basis of propagation dynamics, irreversibility, or near-miss status).

Legislative Approaches

EU AI Act:
The EU AI Act defines four broad serious incident categories (Article 3(49)(a)–(d)): death or harm to health; disruption of critical infrastructure; infringement of Union law obligations protecting fundamental rights; and harm to property or the environment. These are assessed independently of the incident pathway: any AI system causing harm in a qualifying category triggers the reporting obligation regardless of how the harm arose. The Act’s category-based approach captures harm types, including fundamental rights infringements and environmental harm, that have no equivalent trigger under SB 53.

SB 53:
SB 53 does not define standalone harm categories. Instead, most critical safety incident types combine a specified incident pathway with a specified harm outcome: weight exfiltration resulting in death or bodily injury (§1107(d)(1)); materialisation of catastrophic risk (§1107(d)(2)); loss of control causing death or bodily injury (§1107(d)(3)). The harm outcomes recognized across these definitions are limited to death, bodily injury, and property damage — a narrower set of categories than the EU AI Act’s four. This coupled structure also means that a harm falling within a recognized category (e.g. bodily injury) would not qualify under SB 53 unless it arose through one of the specified pathways.

Cross-Domain Precedent

Cybersecurity:
NIS2 Article 6 defines harm to network and information systems across availability, authenticity, integrity, and confidentiality, which constitute the harm domain for any qualifying incident.

Finance:
The Basel II Annex 2 operational risk event type categories classify financial losses across seven event types (internal fraud, external fraud, employment practices, clients / products / business practices, damage to physical assets, business disruption and systems failures, execution / delivery / process management), while DORA Article 18 defines major ICT-related incident harm categories spanning availability of critical ICT services, data compromise, regulatory obligation failures, financial losses above materiality thresholds, and reputational impact.

Challenges / Limitations

Abstraction mismatch.
The GPAI Code of Practice Appendix 1.1 risk types (risks to public health, safety, public security, fundamental rights, and society as a whole) and the Appendix 1.4 specified systemic risks (CBRN, loss of control, cyber offence, harmful manipulation) operate at different levels of abstraction to the MIT harm taxonomy categories used at this criterion. The CoP risk types describe domains of potential negative impact; the specified systemic risks describe risk pathways or threat types through which a model might cause harm. SB 53 uses a different abstraction again, generally defining each “critical safety incident” type as a risk pathway combined with a harm outcome (see Appendix B). Neither maps one-to-one onto harm categories, which complicates cross-framework comparisons and makes it difficult to verify whether all escalation-relevant harms in one framework are captured by another.

Operational speed.
The MIT harm taxonomy was designed for retrospective incident classification, not real-time triage. Some categories (e.g. “democratic norms”, “differential treatment”) may require investigation or contextual evidence that is unavailable at the point of initial assessment, creating a tension between categorical completeness and operational speed.

Criterion 5b: Has harm crossed a relevant severity threshold?

Purpose

Criterion 5b determines whether the severity of harm identified at 5a warrants escalation. The challenge is that existing frameworks use incompatible severity standards — from the EU AI Act’s qualitative “serious” qualifier to SB 53’s quantitative thresholds of more than 50 deaths or $1 billion in property damage — and a graduated scale is needed to capture incidents that fall between these extremes.

Questions

Q1.
Has harm in any single category reached Level 4 (Severe) or Level 5 (Catastrophic) on the MIT severity scale?

Yes / No

The severity scale is a five-level scale applied per harm category, based on the CSET AI Harm Framework with quantifiable criteria added by the MIT AI Risk Repository:

Level 1 — Negligible: minimal or no discernible impact

Level 2 — Minor: limited, easily remediated impact

Level 3 — Substantial: significant impact requiring intervention

Level 4 — Severe: serious, potentially lasting impact on individuals, organizations, or systems

Level 5 — Catastrophic: widespread, potentially irreversible harm including death, major infrastructure failure, or large-scale societal disruption

Q2.
Where harm has been assessed as a composite incident (criterion 4), is severity assessed against the aggregated harm of the cluster?

Yes — severity assessment applies to the aggregated harm, not to each constituent incident individually

Overall response:

If Q1 = Yes (Level 4 or 5 in any single harm category): Escalate.

If Q1 = No (all harm categories at Level 3 or below): proceed to criterion 6. The incident may still warrant escalation on the basis of propagation dynamics, irreversibility, or near-miss status.

Legislative Approaches

EU AI Act:
The EU AI Act uses “serious” as the qualifying adjective for harm across most categories but provides no explicit quantitative threshold beyond “death of a person”. For fundamental rights (Art. 3(49)(c)), harm must rise to the level of an infringement of Union law obligations, a legal standard rather than a quantitative threshold.

SB 53:
SB 53 defines explicit quantitative thresholds: death or serious injury to more than 50 people, or more than $1,000,000,000 in damage to, or loss of, property arising from a single incident involving a frontier model. For critical safety incidents under §1107(d)(1) and (d)(3), the severity threshold is any death or bodily injury with no minimum count.

The result is that the EU AI Act’s qualitative “serious” standard provides interpretive flexibility but limited operational specificity, while SB 53’s quantitative thresholds provide clear trigger points but risk excluding incidents that fall just below the numerical minimums or that involve harm types (such as rights violations or environmental damage) for which no quantitative threshold is defined.

Cross-Domain Precedent

Finance:
The DORA Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1772 classifies an ICT-related incident as “major” if it affects a critical service and meets quantitative thresholds across multiple dimensions: more than 10% of users or more than 100,000 clients impacted; duration exceeding 24 hours; the incident affecting two or more EU Member States; or financial impact exceeding EUR 100,000.

AI Incident Databases:
The MIT AIID Harm Taxonomy provides a five-level severity scale (Catastrophic / Severe / Substantial / Minor / Negligible) applied per harm category by database curators, while the OECD AIM proposes a three-tier classification ranging from “AI Incident” (harm caused, typically limited in scope) through “Serious AI Incident” to “AI Disaster” (large-scale, potentially irreversible harm causing widespread societal disruption).

Challenges / Limitations

Legal operationalization.
Many harm severities in the EU AI Act are defined using legal terminology, such as “an infringement of Union law obligations” (EU AI Act Art. 3(49)(c)). These are harder to operationalize as testable thresholds or trigger conditions, particularly under time-sensitive escalation decision-making. However, relying solely on quantifiable thresholds risks failing to escalate incidents that fall within the scope of legal repercussion.

Threshold gap.
A gap exists between SB 53’s quantitative thresholds (death or serious injury to more than 50 people, or more than $1 billion in property damage) and the lower boundary of harm that may nonetheless warrant escalation. The death of a single person, serious injury to a small number of individuals, or financial damage in the tens of thousands could each cross a severity threshold depending on the domain, context, and affected population yet these fall far below SB 53’s minimums and sit within the EU AI Act’s undefined “serious” qualifier.

Criterion 6: Is international coordination required to contain the incident or respond to its cross-border propagation?

Purpose

Criterion 6 determines whether an incident’s spread dynamics create an operational need for international coordination — either to contain the incident, to warn exposed jurisdictions, or to address capacity gaps that exceed national response.

Questions

Q1.
Has confirmed harm occurred in two or more jurisdictions?

Yes / No

Q2.
Is harm still propagating or accumulating at the time of assessment?

Yes / No

If Yes, classify the propagation pathway:

•
Supply-chain propagation:
shared infrastructure dependencies — how many downstream systems or organizations inherit the failure mode because they rely on the same model, component, provider, API, weights, dataset, or deployment pattern?

•
Capability propagation:
the harmful capability is reproducible or transferable — can the failure mode be easily reproduced through fine-tuning, prompting, model transfer, open release, copycat deployment, or user adaptation?

•
Emergent propagation:
harm spreads via AI interaction dynamics — does the harm amplify through use dynamics rather than technical dependencies?

Q3.
Does the propagation pathway cross national borders?

Assess using: downstream dependency count, jurisdictional spread, replicability / transferability, and pathway determinism.

Yes / No

Q4.
Does containment require action in another jurisdiction?

Yes / No

Q5.
Is the propagating harm irreversible?

Yes / No

Q6.
Is the affected jurisdiction’s response capacity sufficient?

Yes / No

Overall response:

If Q1 = Yes (confirmed cross-border harm): Escalate.

If Q1 = No but Q2 = Yes (active propagation): assess propagation pathway (Q3). If pathway crosses borders and containment requires international action (Q4 = Yes): Escalate. If pathway crosses borders but containment does not require international action: assess whether propagating harm is irreversible (Q5). If Yes: Escalate. If No: Alert.

If Q2 = No (harm not propagating): proceed to criterion 7.

Legislative Approaches

EU AI Act:
While the EU AI Act recognizes propagation as a defining characteristic of systemic risk (Article 3(65)), mandating that GPAI providers evaluate propagation pathways as part of model-level risk assessment (Article 55, Annex XIII) and establishing cross-border propagation of systemic risk as an explicit trigger for EU-level coordination (Article 66), these obligations attach to models and providers, not to individual incidents. The Act does not specify how propagation dynamics of a specific incident should be assessed or at what point incident-level propagation should trigger escalation.

SB 53:
Similarly, SB 53 does not address propagation as a standalone property of an incident that could independently influence its classification. Propagation appears only implicitly through loss-of-control provisions (§1107(c)(3)) and through the inclusion of cyberattack as a catastrophic risk activity (§1107(c)(1)(B)). Neither provides for a sub-threshold incident to be escalated on the basis of its propagation dynamics alone.

Health:

and doubling time function as real-time propagation metrics, and the IHR Annex 2 embeds international spread risk as a criterion that triggers notification regardless of current case severity.

Cybersecurity:
Breakout time (CrowdStrike) sets the minimum detection window for containment before propagation, MTTD / MTTR measure the interval between compromise and response, and NIS2 Article 23(4) triggers cross-border notification when incidents affect two or more EU member states.

Finance:
DORA Article 19 triggers coordinated notification for major ICT-related incidents affecting financial entities in multiple member states.

Challenges / Limitations

1.
Methodological gap.
Existing AI governance frameworks do not account for incident-level propagation assessment, meaning there is no established methodology for measuring propagation dynamics of AI incidents in practice. The measurement techniques drawn from comparator domains were each designed for specific, relatively narrow incident types within well-characterized domains (biological transmission, network lateral movement, ICT service disruption). How well these translate to the diversity of AI incident types, which span physical harm, infrastructure disruption, informational harms, rights violations, and emergent behavioral effects, is an open empirical question.

2.
Infrastructure dependency.
Effective incident-level propagation assessment, particularly the cross-border scope question, depends on the ability to observe and correlate incidents across providers, deployment contexts, and jurisdictions in a timely manner. This infrastructure does not yet exist at the required scale. The propagation metrics drawn from comparator domains are only feasible because of decades of investment in domain-specific surveillance and telemetry networks.

3.
Exposure estimation.
For supply-chain propagation, exposure scope can often be estimated by tracing the technical dependency tree (downstream integrations, API consumers, derivative model deployments). For capability and emergent propagation, quantitative estimation of exposure scope is substantially harder: the population at risk is defined by which models could independently manifest the same behavior (capability) or which deployments share similar interaction dynamics (emergent), neither of which is straightforwardly enumerable at the point of assessment.

Criterion 7: Does irreversible harm require international coordination to assess or respond to its cross-border consequences?

Purpose

Criterion 7 determines whether international escalation is warranted due to the consequences of irreversible harm from a contained incident. It asks whether the permanent damage in one jurisdiction has downstream effects on others — warranting international coordination not to contain spread (assessed at criterion 6) but to assess and respond to permanent damage whose consequences cross borders by virtue of interconnection, dependency, or precedent.

Questions

Q1.
Is any portion of the harm irreversible?

Yes / No

Assess reversibility across four levels:

•
Containment:
Can further actions be halted before more harm propagates? Who has the authority / responsibility to halt?

•
Control restoration:
Can the responsible actor regain the ability to direct, modify, or shut down the system?

•
Technical state restoration:
Can the pre-incident technical state (model weights, training data, system dynamics) be recovered?

•
Structural and societal state restoration:
Can the pre-incident infrastructure and societal state be recovered? Highest level of irreversibility risk: macroeconomic / technical / societal damage.

To estimate harm reversibility, look at cost / time / feasibility to reverse harm impacts assessed in criterion 5. Any harm impacts which cannot be reversed are defined as
D
(change from pre-incident state). Where full reversal is technically possible but at infeasible cost or timescale, this is treated as effectively irreversible (drawing on the GPAI Code of Practice definition of loss of control risk as situations where regaining control is “either extremely costly or impossible”).

Q2.
Does the irreversible harm have consequences extending beyond the affected jurisdiction?

Yes / No

This is not asking whether the harm spread to other countries (assessed at criterion 6). It asks whether the permanent damage in one jurisdiction has downstream effects on others, i.e. harm crosses borders by virtue of interconnection, dependency, or precedent.

Overall response:

If Q1 = No (harm is fully reversible): proceed to criterion 8.

If Q1 = Yes and Q2 = Yes (irreversible harm with cross-border consequences): Escalate.

If Q1 = Yes and Q2 = No (irreversible harm contained within one jurisdiction): proceed to criterion 8.

Legislative Approaches

EU AI Act:
The EU AI Act and SB 53 converge in treating irreversibility as an implicit property of certain harm types rather than as an independent assessment dimension applicable across all harm categories. The EU AI Act is the most explicit: Article 3(49)(b) names irreversibility as a qualifying criterion for critical infrastructure disruption, and the GPAI Code of Practice makes it definitional for loss of control risk (situations where regaining control is “either extremely costly or impossible”). An incident causing serious but sub-threshold irreversible harm to fundamental rights, property, or the environment has no accelerated pathway under the Act on the basis of its irreversibility alone.

SB 53:
SB 53 does not explicitly mention reversibility: its threshold structure assumes that harm type and magnitude are sufficient for classification, meaning an incident producing moderate but permanently irreversible harm has no escalation pathway that a comparable reversible incident would not also have. The closest SB 53 comes is the implicit irreversibility embedded in its harm outcomes (death, bodily injury), but these are classified by their nature, not by their recoverability.

Cross-Domain Precedent

Finance:
DORA Articles 11–12 mandate that financial entities set, test, and maintain Recovery Time Objectives (RTO) and Recovery Point Objectives (RPO) for all critical ICT systems, measuring whether the service can be restored, not whether downstream consequences on clients or markets can be reversed.

Cybersecurity:
NIS2 Article 21 treats recoverability of data and system state as a mandatory operational capability for essential entities, also framed at the system level.

Health:
The WHO rapid risk assessment guidance is the closest any comparator domain comes to assessing irreversibility at the harm level rather than the system level, because in health the “system” and the “harmed entity” are the same (the patient): irreversibility (death, disability) functions as a severity modifier within existing scoring rather than as a freestanding escalation criterion.

Challenges / Limitations

Temporal uncertainty.
Estimating
D
(the irreversible component of harm) at the point of triage is inherently uncertain, particularly for harm types where permanence only becomes apparent over time. Physical harms such as death are immediately classifiable as irreversible; economic, democratic, and informational harms may take months or years to reveal whether the pre-incident state is recoverable.

Feasibility judgment.
The distinction between absolute and effective irreversibility (technically impossible vs. infeasibly costly) introduces a judgment call that different assessors may resolve differently, particularly at Level 4 (structural and societal state restoration) where “feasible cost” is inherently political. A jurisdiction with extensive resources may judge societal restoration feasible where a less-resourced jurisdiction would not.

Information asymmetry.
The cross-border consequences test at Step 2 requires the assessing jurisdiction to evaluate downstream effects on other countries’ systems and interests: an assessment it may not have sufficient information to make reliably, particularly for complex interdependencies.

Criterion 8: Has a near miss or hazard indicated that harm is inadequately mitigated?

Purpose

Criterion 8 captures events where harm was closely averted but the failure mode revealed by the near miss may indicate inadequate controls across the wider system. Its value lies not in the averted incident itself but in what it reveals about control adequacy in analogous systems elsewhere.

Questions

Q1.
Has there been a closely averted incident that, had it not been averted, would have met the severity threshold at criterion 5b (Level 4 or 5 on the severity scale)?

Yes / No

Q2.
Does the failure mode that was averted affect, or could it plausibly affect, AI systems deployed by other developers or in other jurisdictions?

Yes / No

Overall response:

If Q1 = Yes and Q2 = Yes (high-severity near miss with cross-system relevance): Alert.

If Q1 = Yes and Q2 = No (high-severity near miss but failure mode is system-specific): Discard from international escalation. May warrant developer-level or national-level follow-up.

If Q1 = No (averted incident would not have met severity threshold): Discard.

Legislative Approaches

EU AI Act:
The EU AI Act and SB 53 converge in that neither mandates standalone near-miss or hazard reporting in their binding text. Both frameworks are oriented primarily toward incidents where harm has occurred or where a specified threatening condition has been identified, rather than toward events where harm was plausibly close but averted. The EU AI Act’s binding provisions (Article 73) activate only after a serious incident, with no provision for near-miss events as independent reporting triggers. The GPAI Code of Practice introduces near-miss obligations across several measures, but these are ancillary to other provider processes (framework reassessment, systemic risk identification, model report updates, and serious incident reporting) rather than constituting a standalone reporting channel, and the Code is voluntary, not binding.

SB 53:
SB 53’s §1107(d)(4) is an early warning trigger for a specified threatening capability (deceptive techniques demonstrating materially increased catastrophic risk) rather than a near miss in the sense of closely averted harm.

Cross-Domain Precedent

Finance:
The Basel II/III operational risk framework treats near-miss data as a relevant input to operational risk management, feeding into scenario analysis and risk control self-assessments rather than into the capital calculation framework directly.

Industrial Safety:
The EU Seveso III Directive (2012/18/EU) requires operators of establishments handling dangerous substances above specified thresholds to prepare Major Accident Prevention Policies and implement Safety Management Systems: legally mandated, comprehensive accident prevention strategies designed to prevent major accidents before they occur, not merely to respond after harm materializes.

AI Incident Databases:
The OECD AIM includes near misses within its definition of AI hazards, and
60
propose a central reporting mechanism for “flaws” designed to unify the possible security and safety implications of vulnerabilities and hazards.

Challenges / Limitations

Scope limitation.
Hazards corresponding to the risk pathways defined as immediate escalation triggers (CBRN assistance, weight exfiltration, and loss of control) are already addressed at criterion 3 regardless of whether harm has materialized. The scope of this criterion is therefore limited to near misses outside those categories, which narrows its coverage but avoids duplication.

Domain maturity.
The comparator domains mandating proactive hazard reporting (Seveso III, ICAO Annex 19, EU pharmacovigilance) do so within well-characterized domains with operational maturity that AI governance does not yet possess. Replicating their hazard assessment methodology, which requires assessing probability and pathway plausibility of unrealized harm, raises distinct methodological challenges in the AI context.

Reporting gap.
Near-miss data only reaches regulatory bodies as an ancillary obligation attached to other reporting triggers: there is currently no independent requirement to report near misses as standalone events under any binding AI governance framework.

Boundary classification.
The boundary between a near miss and a low-severity incident is not always clear-cut. An event where harm was averted by a functioning downstream control differs from one where harm was averted by chance, and both differ from an event where minor harm occurred but more severe harm was narrowly avoided. The framework does not yet specify how to classify these boundary cases.

5.2
Testing observations

The framework performed reliably for discrete-onset, individually severe incidents in which AI’s causal role is identifiable within a specific event chain. The state-sponsored cyber attack (Incident 1) triggered a red line under SB 53, and the data poisoning cluster (Incidents 9–10) escalated through the combination of pattern detection, cross-border propagation, and irreversibility. For these incident types, causal confidence is typically high or medium because the incident is bounded and the AI system’s involvement can be traced through documented evidence.

The framework was more challenged by three categories of incident, each of which reveals a distinct structural limitation:

•

Cumulative, individually sub-threshold harms.
For AI-related psychological harm (Incident 4) and cumulative deepfake harms (Incident 2), the counterfactual test at the core of the causal chain criterion—‘would this harm have occurred but for the AI?’—becomes essentially unanswerable at the individual interaction level, because AI is one contributing factor among many across thousands of independent interactions. The testing suggests that aggregation-based approaches to causality, such as demonstrating that a particular interaction pattern systematically produces harm at rates differing from baseline, are needed as a supplement to individual counterfactual assessment. Importantly, the framework’s multi-criteria architecture proved its value here: the cumulative deepfake incident (Incident 2) did not trigger the red line gate (C3), which is designed for a different class of incident, but was correctly caught at the harm severity gate (C5b) when assessed on an aggregate basis. This demonstrates that the sequential design functions as intended, with downstream criteria compensating for upstream gates that are not engaged.

•

Near-miss and potential harm.
The agentic platform vulnerability (Incident 3) and the CBRN near-miss (Incident 6) both expose a structural tension in the harm severity criterion: the MIT harm taxonomy assesses realized harm, but these incidents’ significance lies almost entirely in their potential harm. For Incident 3, responsible disclosure by security researchers contained the exposure within hours, producing low realized harm (severity 2–3) despite the potential for severity 4–5 across financial loss, privacy, and infrastructure categories if the vulnerability had been exploited maliciously. The near-miss criterion (C8) proved to be the strongest-performing criterion for these incident types, correctly identifying the systemic risk signal. A design question remains as to whether responsible disclosure should attenuate the urgency of the response pathway without attenuating the classification of systemic risk.

•

Out-of-scope high-severity incidents.
The military AI incident (Incident 7) produced a discard for what would be the highest-severity incident in the test set, with estimated severity 5 and multiple red line triggers if the scope exclusion did not apply. While the exclusion is by design, the testing raised the question of whether the framework should include a ‘refer out’ pathway for incidents that fall within the military or defense exclusion but meet high-severity indicators, and whether military AI incidents should be recorded at minimum to support cross-boundary pattern detection.

In addition to these, the following further observations were made:

•

Data availability.
Data gaps were identified across nearly all incidents tested. The most consistent gap was access to data held by AI developers, including interaction logs, training data provenance, internal safety assessments, and internal escalation records. This data is unlikely to be shared voluntarily with an incident registry in the absence of a legal obligation to do so. The agentic platform vulnerability (Incident 3) highlighted an additional gap: the relevant data was held by the agentic platform operator rather than an AI model developer in the traditional sense, suggesting that the framework’s assumed categories of data holders may need to be broadened as agentic AI deployment scales.

•

Harm severity scales and thresholds did not capture the principal harm types from incidents in the large-scale manipulation and psychological harm domain.
The harm severity criterion (C5b) produced significant ambiguity when applied to both incidents tested in this domain (Incidents 2 and 4). Neither incident reached Level 4 (Severe) in any single harm category when assessed against the quantified scale descriptors, despite both producing harm that regulatory and public responses clearly treated as severe. The MIT severity scale’s quantified thresholds for Level 4 are calibrated to tangible outcomes
(
92
)
but the principal harms in this domain, for example, psychological distress, emotional dependence, dignity violation, and in severe cases self-harm or suicidal ideation, do not map onto any of these descriptors. Both incidents scored Level 3 across multiple categories simultaneously, but the per-category design provides no mechanism for cross-category aggregation. Additionally, the severity assessment for the deepfake incident (Incident 2) was not modified by the involvement of children among the victims: AI Forensics
(
3
)
found nearly 10% of recovered content depicted minors in sexual activities, yet because the scale contains no vulnerability modifier, the harm to child victims was assessed at the same severity level as the harm to adults, despite the distinct legal treatment of such content under CSAM statutes across jurisdictions. For both incidents, the cross-border nature of the propagation path caused these to escalate, rather than the severity criterion. This means that for the large-scale manipulation and psychological harm risk domain, the severity criterion is essentially non-functional.

•

Lack of reliable evidence to confirm cross-border harm for incidents with diffuse detection channels.
For incidents in the large-scale manipulation and psychological harm domain, the evidence required to trigger cross-border propagation checks at C6 is structurally difficult to assemble. The deepfake incident (Incident 2) had strong cross-border evidence: regulatory investigations were opened in twelve named jurisdictions, providing concrete documentation. The psychological harm incident (Incident 4) did not. OpenAI’s ~500k weekly affected users figure is a global aggregate, not broken down by jurisdiction
(
78
)
. The specific confirmed harms: for example lawsuits citing deaths by suicide
(
93
)
, hospitalisations, and clinical case reports, are concentrated in US reporting. The cross-border nature of the harm is inferred from the global deployment of the models rather than documented from jurisdiction-specific harm reports. This evidentiary gap arises because the detection channels for psychological harm—news reporting, litigation, social media discussion, and clinical encounters—are fragmented and vary substantially in coverage across countries. The AI incident databases (OECD AIM, AIID, MIT) that could in principle provide cross-jurisdictional evidence rely on English-language media reporting, creating a systematic geographic bias. This means that for harm types where detection depends on fragmented, media-dependent channels rather than systematic monitoring, the cross-border propagation criterion may undercount the jurisdictions affected and underestimate the case for international coordination.

•

The framework does not distinguish between ongoing background harm and acute escalation events.
For incidents where harm is cumulative and continuous rather than discrete, the framework’s propagation criterion (C6) will always be satisfied once the harm is first detected: any globally deployed model producing ongoing psychological harm has, by definition, an active propagation pathway that crosses borders. This creates a classification problem. Psychological harm from human–AI interaction is not a discrete event with a beginning and an end—it is a standing condition. Both OpenAI and Anthropic have published data indicating this is ongoing: OpenAI reported that approximately 0.07% of weekly ChatGPT users exhibit risk factors including psychosis, mania, and suicidal ideation
(
78
)
; Anthropic’s analysis of 1.5 million Claude conversations found disempowerment potential, including reality distortion, value judgment distortion, or action distortion, in fewer than 1 in 1,000 interactions for severe forms, with mild forms occurring in 1 in 50 to 1 in 70 conversations
(
91
)
. These figures imply that developers currently treat these rates as falling within acceptable operating parameters. Once an ongoing harm of this type has been identified for the first time, the operationally useful escalation trigger is not the continuing existence of the harm but a spike in its prevalence or severity, analogous to how financial services regulators monitor fraud losses against established baselines and escalate when losses exceed defined tolerances
(
27
)
. An incident escalation framework should define thresholds for what constitutes an abnormal increase in an ongoing cumulative harm, rather than treating the standing condition itself as a perpetual escalation trigger. Without such thresholds, the risk is either that the ongoing harm is permanently escalated (rendering the escalation meaningless) or that it is normalized (rendering the framework blind to deterioration). The current absence of agreed tolerances also means that individual developers are implicitly setting their own baselines, creating the potential for divergence between providers on what level of ongoing psychological harm is acceptable, compounding the challenges arising from divergent harm definitions discussed earlier.

6
Discussion

Applying the framework to real incidents and structured variants, and analyzing existing AI incident frameworks against them, surfaced ten findings about where escalation criteria work, where they break down, and what is required to make them operational. A central finding was that escalation rules are only one part of the overall framework. Their development and use depend on prior definitions of incident types, harm categories, severity scales, and principles for grouping related incidents, as well as on the availability of relevant data to those responsible for assessing whether a trigger condition has been met.

We group our findings (identified by F1) by where in this dependency chain they land (Figure
4
). F1 concerns the dependency structure itself. F2 and F3 concern the design of thresholds and triggers directly. F4, F5, and F6 concern definitional foundations, including how harm is categorized, scored, and adjusted for affected populations. F7 and F8 concern clustering and aggregation methods: what constitutes a reportable incident and how related incidents are linked. F9 and F10 concern the data architecture needed to operate the framework.

Visual representation of key findings of the paper. Finding 1 reflects overarching dependencies for escalation framework. Other findings are grouped by i) thresholds and triggers for escalation; ii) definitions of incidents; and iii) access to data and monitoring.

The dependency structure

Finding 1: Triggers and thresholds for AI incident escalation depend on definitional and data infrastructure that does not yet exist.

The stress testing revealed that triggers and thresholds in the escalation layer cannot be designed or operated without two other layers being in place. It relies upstream on definitional work specifying what constitutes an incident, how harm is categorized and scored, and what unit of analysis applies (acute event, pattern, or standing condition). It depends downstream on data infrastructure determining what can actually be detected: developer telemetry, cross-developer pattern visibility, and cross-domain intelligence. The findings that follow (F2–F9) identify specific gaps at each layer.

We present the dependency structure itself as a finding, because it provides an explanation for threshold design choices in existing frameworks that risk systematic under-detection of incidents in certain systemic risk domains.

When the definitional layer is incomplete, threshold and trigger design is directly constrained. Triggers can only test for conditions that have been defined in assessable terms; where harm categories are unspecified or specified in terms that are not measurable against available data, triggers cannot be calibrated against them. Similarly, a defined unit of analysis, whether the framework is assessing a single event, a cluster of related events, or an ongoing condition, is a precondition for trigger design, because the trigger mechanism must be specified against whatever the framework treats as a reportable incident. The effect is that threshold design defaults to harm types and incident structures for which definitions already exist, leaving risk domains where they do not, particularly large-scale manipulation and psychological harm, structurally harder to detect and escalate.

Recommendation (for policymakers and framework designers).

Design and assess AI incident escalation frameworks against the full dependency chain, not only the escalation layer. Where definitional foundations are incomplete, frameworks should specify interim triggers based on the information developers can observe (model behavior, interaction patterns, system states) rather than defaulting exclusively to confirmed harm outcomes that developers cannot see. Investment in upstream definitional work—agreed harm categories, measurable severity scales, specified units of analysis—is a precondition for operational triggers, rather than an optional refinement. The sequencing matters: building triggers before the definitions and data layers are in place produces frameworks that are formally complete but operationally unimplementable.

Thresholds and triggers

Finding 2: Some systemic risks warrant escalation before harm is confirmed, but current regulatory triggers require confirmed harm outcomes.

Existing regulatory triggers (SB 53’s critical safety incident, the EU AI Act’s serious incident definition) require harm outcomes such as death or property damage before reporting is activated in most cases for incidents to be escalated. In these cases, the developer may see the triggering event but have no visibility of downstream harm. The Tumbler Ridge shooting illustrated this: OpenAI closed an account for violent activity but did not alert law enforcement because internal thresholds for credible, imminent risk were not met; the individual subsequently committed a mass shooting
(
80
)
.

We identify three conditions that together justify immediate escalation without confirmed harm: the intervention window is narrow; the probability of severe, irreversible harm without action is high; and no single actor has visibility over both the risk pathway and the harm occurring.

Recommendation.

Define immediate-escalation triggers for systemic risks that have begun to materialize. Qualifying events should include expert-level CBRN assistance; exfiltration of frontier model weights where containment is unconfirmed; and AI systems or agent networks evading developer control. SB 53 partially addresses this for deceptive subversion and the EU AI Act covers fundamental rights infringements, but weight exfiltration under SB 53 still requires downstream harm outcomes the developer may never observe. The GPAI Code of Practice’s systemic risk identification provisions could support detection but do not currently feed notification requirements.

Finding 3: Existing frameworks under-classify incidents presenting as patterns rather than discrete events.

The Grok deepfake incident
(
4
)
illustrated the gap: each individual image might score severity 2–3, but millions of images, overwhelmingly targeting women, across a platform with hundreds of millions of users reaches severity 4–5 in aggregate. A framework assessing only individual severity would classify this for monitoring, not escalation. The same applies to human–AI interaction harms where individual harm is sub-threshold but population-level effects are significant. Developers monitor relevant patterns: OpenAI on psychosis, self-harm, and emotional reliance
(
78
)
; Anthropic on disempowerment patterns covering reality and value judgment distortion
(
91
)
, but there is no agreed method for clustering, no consensus on aggregated thresholds, and no obligation to report patterns as such.

Recommendation.

Define principles for grouping incidents for aggregated assessment. We propose three initial groupings:

•

Technical
— shared root cause (same model, API layer, training dataset, upstream component).

•

Capability
— same behavior arising independently across models (sycophancy, hallucination-driven harm, persuasive targeting).

•

Contextual
— same deployment pattern or affected population (companion chatbot dynamics, autonomous agent tool-use patterns).

The Code of Practice’s post-market monitoring provisions (Measure 3.5) provide an aggregation mechanism but no triggers or thresholds. This is particularly urgent for human–AI interaction harms, where consensus on categorising concerning interaction patterns is itself a precondition for meaningful monitoring.

Definitional foundations

Finding 4: Some EU AI Act serious incident definitions are not specified in terms that support operational detection.

Legal definitions are framed for regulatory and judicial contexts, not real-time escalation. Article 3(49)(c), “infringement of obligations under Union law intended to protect fundamental rights”, requires, before it can function as a trigger, a mapping of all relevant obligations, a determination of what constitutes infringement of each, and translation into indicators observable in developer data or incident databases. Article 3(49)(a), “serious harm to a person’s health”, presupposes a measurable seriousness threshold, but none exists for developers with no visibility of downstream health outcomes (see F1). Developers either set divergent internal thresholds or wait for confirmed harm that arrives too late for meaningful intervention.

Recommendation.

Translate legally defined harm categories into measurable proxies that can be operationalized before incidents occur. This requires dialogue between those drafting legal frameworks and those operationalizing them, producing harm definitions that are both legally grounded and specified in terms of monitorable indicators. Proxies should be established in advance, not derived ad hoc under time pressure. Tabletop walkthroughs of real incident types, as used in this research, can surface definitions that appear clear in the abstract but prove difficult to apply in practice, and should form part of the process by which legal definitions are stress-tested for operational use.

Finding 5: Harm severity scales are underdeveloped for large-scale manipulation and psychological harm, leaving escalation thresholds underspecified.

Stress testing applied escalation criteria to two incidents in this domain: mass non-consensual deepfake generation (Incident 2) and cumulative psychological harm from human–AI interaction (Incident 4). Both produced documented real-world harm, with regulatory investigations across twelve jurisdictions, reported hospitalisations, and deaths, yet applying quantified severity thresholds produced significant ambiguity in both cases.

The principal harms, such as psychological distress, emotional dependence, and distorted perceptions of reality, are difficult to observe (no visibility of downstream psychological outcomes), difficult to quantify (no equivalent of a casualty count or financial loss figure), and difficult to attribute (the individual-level counterfactual is frequently unanswerable). Any severity assessment here is therefore an approximation derived from proxy indicators, for example interaction pattern data, risk factor prevalence, and media-reported cases, and no existing framework guides what level of approximated harm is equivalent to a given severity threshold.

Recommendation.

Develop operational severity criteria for this domain through dedicated, multi-stakeholder work. This requires clinical and safeguarding input on the pathways through which AI-mediated manipulation produces psychological harm and on clinically meaningful indicators at different severity levels; developer input on what is feasible to measure reliably from interaction data; and policymaker input on how the resulting categories map to existing legal obligations, particularly how “serious harm to a person’s health” under the EU AI Act should be interpreted for psychological harm. The output should be consensus guidance analogous to the WHO’s clinical severity scales
(
103
)
or DORA’s ICT materiality thresholds
(
27
)
.

Finding 6: Harm to vulnerable groups is not reflected as a severity modifier in any existing escalation framework, despite being recognized in law, safeguarding practice, and AI developers’ own product architectures.

Identical content produces materially different harm depending on who is affected. A non-consensual deepfake of a child is legally distinct from one of an adult. CSAM statutes impose strict liability regardless of intent or volume, and the IWF reported over 8,000 cases of AI-generated CSAM in the first half of 2025
(
54
)
. A chatbot reinforcing delusional thinking in a user with pre-existing psychosis presents a qualitatively different risk from the same interaction with a resilient adult. Public-facing individuals (teachers, politicians, healthcare workers) face disproportionate downstream consequences from the same content. Yet the severity scales tested here, and thresholds in both the EU AI Act and SB 53, assess harm by type and magnitude without adjusting for who is affected. This is in contrast with the frameworks these mechanisms operationalize. The EU AI Act identifies children and persons with disabilities as warranting differentiated treatment (Recital 70, Article 5). Child protection law imposes strict liability for harms involving minors. Developers already operationalize the distinction: OpenAI’s Teen Safety Blueprint
(
77
)
and Child Safety Blueprint
(
79
)
deploy age prediction and differentiated safeguards, and over 560,000 weekly users are flagged as exhibiting signs of psychosis or mania
(
78
)
, highlighting that account-level classification infrastructure already exists.

Recommendation (developers).

Apply severity modifiers where accounts are flagged as belonging to vulnerable groups. Classifications already generated for safeguarding, such as age prediction, mental health risk indicators, and emotional dependence flags, should feed into incident severity assessment, so that incidents affecting flagged accounts are assessed at higher effective severity than the same incident affecting the general population.

Recommendation (framework designers).

Incorporate vulnerability as an explicit severity modifier in escalation criteria, specifying which population characteristics warrant upward adjustment and how they interact with existing harm category thresholds, drawing on child protection law, the EU AI Act’s vulnerability provisions, and the population-level risk data developers are already collecting.

Finding 7: For systemic risks manifesting as ongoing, low-severity harms rather than acute events, what constitutes a reportable incident is not yet defined.

Existing escalation frameworks, including EU AI Act Article 73, SB 53 §1107, and the framework tested here, are architectured around discrete events. Some systemic risk types do not follow this model. Psychological harm from human–AI interaction, cumulative manipulation through generated content, and gradual erosion of information integrity through training data contamination are standing conditions: ongoing, low-severity harms across large populations, with no discrete onset, no point of resolution, and no single incident to isolate. OpenAI reports approximately 0.07% of weekly ChatGPT users (around 500,000 individuals) exhibit risk factors for psychosis, mania, or suicidal ideation
(
78
)
; Anthropic’s analysis of 1.5 million Claude conversations found severe disempowerment potential in fewer than 1 in 1,000 interactions, with mild forms in 1 in 50 to 1 in 70, and prevalence of moderate and severe cases increasing between late 2024 and late 2025
(
91
)
.

Stress-testing exposed a structural problem: when harm is continuous and globally distributed, propagation and cross-border criteria risk being permanently satisfied. Treat this as a perpetual trigger and escalation becomes meaningless; do not, and a priority risk type is invisible. Established domains address analogous problems through tolerance-based monitoring. Financial services treat fraud losses, which are ongoing, low-severity, high-frequency, by monitoring against baselines and escalating when losses exceed defined tolerances (volume over time, rate of change, deviation from expected range)
(
25
)
. The Bank of England’s operational resilience framework requires firms to set impact tolerances for important business services, defining the point at which disruption becomes intolerable
(
86
)
. The useful trigger is not the presence of harm but an abnormal change in its rate, severity, or distribution: a principle not yet incorporated into any AI framework.

Recommendation.

Operationalize tolerance-based monitoring for ongoing harm types in three sequential steps:

1.

Standardize harm categorizations.
OpenAI’s “risk factors for psychosis, mania, and suicidal ideation” and Anthropic’s “disempowerment potential” describe overlapping but non-identical phenomena using different terminology, thresholds, and methodologies. Without agreed definitions, provider data cannot be compared, aggregated, or assessed against common thresholds. The analogous work is standardisation of operational risk event categories under Basel II.

2.

Operationalize monitoring.
Current disclosures are point-in-time research snapshots, not continuous feeds comparable to financial transaction monitoring. Translating them into operational monitoring requires decisions on measurement frequency, indicator selection, sampling methodology, and reporting format, informed by what is feasible to measure reliably from interaction data.

3.

Set tolerances and escalation thresholds.
With standardized categories and operational monitoring in place, the framework can define reportable events: prevalence spikes above baseline, sustained increases over defined windows, threshold crossings in specific harm categories or affected populations. Without this, developers implicitly set their own baselines: OpenAI’s 0.07%
(
78
)
, Anthropic’s “fewer than 1 in 1,000”
(
91
)
, producing divergence that compounds divergent definitions.

Until these steps are completed, ongoing harms remain structurally difficult to detect, compare across providers, or escalate consistently.

Finding 8: Present and emerging multi-agent AI risks are under-scoped in current frameworks.

Four adjustments are needed. First, the international regulatory net catches some AI incidents only by their downstream sectoral impact, not their upstream causal structure, which may sit in a different jurisdiction from the reported incident. Second, a useful unit of analysis is the joint hazard object: an AI system together with its deployment environment and the population of agents (human and AI) it interacts with; propagation and coordination dynamics are properties of this joint object, not of the model in isolation. Third, while academic attention focuses on emergent multi-agent dynamics as the frontier risk, present-day threats concentrate in coordinated multi-agent systems, deliberately directed use of multiple agents by upstream actors operating outside the jurisdictions where incidents are detected. Both require governance, but coordination is where deliberate cross-border threats are already materializing and where the incident-report architecture is weakest. Fourth, in AI-specific frameworks with well-defined triggers, multi-agent risks are largely absent from trigger conditions themselves; where mentioned, provisions are typically voluntary, non-specific, and unattached to thresholds or reporting obligations. Even when coordinated or emergent multi-agent harms fall within nominal scope, no operational mechanism exists to classify, escalate, or coordinate response.

Recommendation.

Three targeted additions to existing instruments:

1.

Trigger conditions:
Multi-agent risk factors—interaction topology, joint hazard specification, orchestration scope, cross-jurisdictional propagation—should be written into the trigger conditions of AI-specific frameworks alongside existing single-system criteria.

2.

Reporting obligations:
Reporting should capture upstream causal or clustering structures and possible orchestration indicators, not only downstream sectoral impact.

3.

Coordination pathways:
Coordination pathways should be pre-specified for incidents where the jurisdiction of detection is not the jurisdiction of causation; the defining feature of the coordinated multi-agent threats already materializing.

These additions are modest in instrument design but substantial in effect, converting incidental, downstream-only visibility of multi-agent harms into a governed, upstream-aware regulatory architecture.

Data architecture

Finding 9: Data required to operate escalation triggers is dispersed across sources, most of which are not currently accessible.

Scenario testing exposed that trigger-relevant data is distributed across sources with very different accessibility and reliability. Public incident databases (AIID, AIAAIC, OECD AIM) are the most accessible but rely on media reports, use incompatible schemas, lack consistent severity data, and cannot support real-time detection. Individual developer data includes privileged technical knowledge on model behavior, model–user interactions, and internal safety incidents, but remains proprietary and does not flow into public infrastructure. Even within a single developer’s view, monitoring may not be structured to surface escalation-relevant patterns, and absent consensus on what constitutes a concerning pattern, detection and aggregation approaches diverge across developers. Requiring developers to assess likelihood of real-world harm from model behavior alone is a further constraint where they have no visibility of downstream outcomes: a limitation acknowledged by
91
, whose analysis captured potential rather than confirmed harm. For pattern-based triggers such as shared failure modes, frequency acceleration, geographic clustering, and multi-provider orchestration, a single developer’s view is structurally insufficient.

Recommendation (policymakers).

Map the data requirements for each escalation trigger, distinguishing those assessable from a single developer’s data (harm severity within a known deployment, system-state reversibility after technical failure) from those requiring a cross-developer view (shared failure modes, ecosystem-wide frequency acceleration, geographic clustering, multi-provider orchestration). For each cross-developer trigger, specify what data is needed, at what aggregation or granularity, for what analytical purpose, with what timeliness. Use this mapping to design data-sharing arrangements, via a regulatory body, independent intermediary, or mutual reporting mechanism, alongside convergence on shared harm-category definitions, classification schemas, and incident-grouping techniques.

Recommendation (developers).

Establish voluntary cross-developer mechanisms for incident visibility. Structured sharing of aggregated signals, such as categories of incidents observed, frequency trends, and capability domains implicated, through bodies such as the Frontier Model Forum need not require sharing proprietary model data or detailed reports, and would provide a materially better basis for distinguishing local anomalies from ecosystem-wide shifts, as well as identifying shared upstream causes (common training data issues, API-layer vulnerabilities) no single developer can detect alone. Establishing these proactively rather than waiting for regulatory mandates would improve individual risk management and demonstrate industry capacity for collective action.

Finding 10: The framework’s capacity-gap assessment at C6 cannot be operationalized without pre-established national response capacity data that does not currently exist for AI incidents.

The framework’s propagation assessment (Criterion 6, Step 5) asks whether the affected jurisdiction’s response capacity is sufficient, and classifies a capacity or information gap as grounds for an international alert (Response Classification 4). This assessment cannot be made at the point of incident triage unless the relevant information has been established in advance. Whether a jurisdiction can detect, investigate, contain, and recover from an AI incident depends on characteristics not observable from the incident itself: the maturity of domestic AI incident response institutions, the degree of AI integration in critical national infrastructure, regulatory readiness to act on AI-specific incidents, and available technical workforce. Without prior assessment, the capacity question in C6 is either unanswerable or answered informally and inconsistently.
Established frameworks address this through standing national capacity assessments that pre-date specific incidents. Under the
101
, all 196 states parties are required to report the status of their surveillance and response capacities annually to the World Health Assembly through the States Parties Self-Assessment Annual Reporting tool (SPAR), which assesses 15 capacities using 35 indicators. The
96
Sendai Framework similarly requires countries to adopt national disaster risk reduction strategies (Target E) and to self-report against defined indicators, incorporating capacity assessment as a standing dimension of risk. In both cases, the assessment exists as a pre-established input to incident triage, not a determination made under time pressure. No equivalent mechanism exists for AI incidents.

Recommendation.

Develop a standardized template for countries to characterize their AI incident response capacity and risk surface in advance, as a standing input to the alert determination at C6. The template should cover dimensions relevant to the capacity-gap assessment: AI integration in critical infrastructure, maturity of domestic incident response institutions, regulatory readiness, and technical workforce. The WHO IHR SPAR
(
104
)
provides a direct design precedent: a structured self-assessment instrument, reported on a regular cycle, producing a comparable profile across countries. A similar instrument for AI could be self-declared, subject to peer review, and held by the relevant reporting body.

6.1
Future research

Most of the research directions below extend specific findings in this paper and are indexed to the relevant finding in the summary matrix above. Two items—empirical validation through tabletop exercises and mapping adjacent regulatory coverage—represent priorities that go beyond the scope of the paper’s core findings.

Risk-domain-specific trigger refinement (F2).

Each risk domain (CBRN, cyber, human–AI interaction harms, democratic process manipulation) has distinct characteristics that affect how triggers should be calibrated. Deep dives into trigger design per risk domain are needed, with particular attention to domains where AI developers can only identify hazards but not confirm real-world harm (CBRN, lethal autonomous weapons), and domains where harm presents as patterns rather than discrete events. For CBRN, the stress testing (Incident 6) retrospectively validated the design rationale for pre-harm escalation triggers: because AI’s causal contribution to CBRN events is difficult to establish after the fact and the evidence is held by law enforcement and intelligence services rather than AI developers, capability-based escalation before harm is confirmed is justified. Future work should explore whether graduated evidentiary standards, specifying, for each risk domain, what level of approximated or proxy evidence is sufficient for escalation, should replace the current uniform approach, calibrating the required confidence level to the reversibility and propagation potential of the harm in question.

Data architecture for incident detection (F9).

The stress testing revealed two qualitatively distinct data gaps requiring different solutions. The first is cross-developer data: pattern detection for incidents like cumulative deepfake harms (Incident 2) and psychological harm (Incident 4) requires visibility across providers to identify shared capability or contextual root causes. This is achievable through cross-developer data-sharing arrangements with appropriate anonymisation and interoperability standards. The second is cross-domain intelligence: the GRU-linked incident cluster (Incidents 9–10) was linked not through AI system telemetry but through national intelligence analysis (VIGINUM), investigative journalism, and independent media auditing (NewsGuard). Even perfect cross-developer visibility would not have detected this pattern, because three of the four incidents in the cluster produced no meaningful signal in any AI developer’s systems. Future work should systematically evaluate which data elements are needed for each trigger type, distinguishing between those that require cross-developer sharing (specifying aggregation levels, privacy-preserving techniques, and interoperable schemas) and those that require cross-domain intelligence interfaces with institutions whose mandates already encompass adversarial campaign detection.

Incident clustering and aggregation methods (F7, F8).

The stress testing revealed three specific limitations in the current design. First, the rolling time windows (30/60/90 days) can expire before retrospective evidence links older incidents: the GRU cluster was identified months or years after individual incidents occurred, and the framework has no mechanism for re-opening expired windows when new evidence emerges. Second, the root cause categories are system-oriented (shared model, shared behavior, shared deployment pattern) and do not capture actor-oriented linkages (shared adversary), which are characteristic of coordinated campaigns. Third, for human–AI interaction harms where individual incident severity is low but aggregate harm is significant, the clustering mechanism must work in conjunction with severity scales that can assess composite harm; a dependency that is currently unsatisfied given the gaps in severity calibration for this domain identified in this paper. Future work should provide guidance on how incidents should be detected from monitoring requirements and would provide clarity to AI developers on what patterns to look for and report.

Vulnerability-modified severity assessment (F6).

Vulnerability modifiers are recognized in law (CSAM statutes, EU AI Act Recital 70 and Article 5) and increasingly in AI developers’ own product architectures. OpenAI has deployed age prediction and differentiated safeguards for minors
(
79
)
and has published data on users exhibiting mental health risk indicators
(
78
)
. Future work should explore how account-level vulnerability classifications already generated for safeguarding purposes (age prediction, mental health risk indicators, emotional dependence flags) could be connected to incident severity assessment, and what governance framework would be needed to ensure such modifiers are applied consistently across providers while protecting user privacy.

Tolerance-based escalation architecture for ongoing harms (F7).

A significant subset of AI systemic risk types—psychological harm from human–AI interaction, cumulative manipulation, gradual information integrity erosion—manifest as ongoing, low-severity, high-frequency harms rather than discrete events. The stress testing demonstrated that the framework’s propagation criterion is permanently satisfied for these harm types once first detected, creating a binary that is either meaninglessly perpetual or blind to deterioration. Future work should develop a tolerance-based monitoring approach modeled on financial services, where reportable events are defined as deviations from agreed baselines rather than the existence of harm per se
(
27
;
86
)
. This requires a sequenced programme: first, multi-stakeholder agreement on standardized harm categories; second, operationalization of continuous monitoring by AI developers against those categories; and third, definition of escalation tolerances specifying what magnitude of change over what time period constitutes an abnormal deterioration warranting escalation.

Mental and psychological harm definition and severity calibration (F5).

Mental and psychological harm features in the
28
(Measure 9.3 explicitly references “serious harm to a person’s health (mental and/or physical)”) but remains difficult to define in a measurable way. The problem is not only definitional but extends to severity calibration, where no existing scale provides thresholds for psychological and dignitary harm types, and to monitoring feasibility, where real-world outcomes (self-harm, hospitalisation, relationship breakdown) are not directly observable by AI developers. Future work could seek to provide a working definition that could range from clinically recognized and diagnosed harm, to dependency, reality distortion, or reliance on AI for decision-making, to confirmed harm following reality distortion events. Building consensus requires input from clinical and safeguarding experts on harm pathways and meaningful severity indicators, from AI developers on what proxy indicators can feasibly be measured from interaction data, and from policymakers on how the resulting thresholds relate to legal obligations including the interpretation of “serious harm to a person’s health” under the EU AI Act. Emerging understanding of human–AI interactions, including OpenAI’s risk factor data (October 2025) and Anthropic’s disempowerment analysis
(
91
)
, provides an empirical foundation on which this consensus work could build.

Multi-agent attribution frameworks (F8).

Further work is needed on how to assess causation and attribute harm in multi-agent systems, including whether interactions between agents materially altered outcomes beyond what a single-agent deployment would have produced. This should address the three distinct harm multiplication mechanisms identified in this paper: shared-dependency failures that scale linearly, coordinated failures that concentrate attribution, and emergent harms attributable only to the collective dynamic.

Empirical validation through tabletop exercises.

Applying the framework to real incidents and structured variants with stakeholders, including policymakers, AI developers, and the public, to validate escalation decisions and refine criteria in response to concrete cases. The process of walking through specific incidents provides an opportunity to build a shared understanding of which incidents warrant escalation and why, and to adjust criteria where they produce surprising or inappropriate outcomes.

Mapping adjacent regulatory coverage.

This work drew on a narrow set of AI-specific regulatory sources. Decisions within the design of existing frameworks may have been influenced by adjacent regulation already covering provisions for certain types of related incident (for example, the Digital Services Act). Mapping related and sector-specific escalation requirements to identify specific gaps for AI risk types and to avoid duplicating provisions that already exist elsewhere would strengthen the framework’s positioning within the broader regulatory landscape.

7
Conclusion

Our work highlights how the design of AI incident escalation frameworks, including the criteria assessed, the order of assessments, the thresholds and triggers, directly determines which incidents will be detected and escalated. If the purpose of an escalation framework is to enable early detection of real-world incidents, its design must account for the pathways by which different risks materialize, who can observe each step, what data is available at each point, and the specific limitations that apply. Failing to do so risks producing a framework that is fundamentally unimplementable under real-world constraints.

In domains where risk pathways are less well understood, particularly manipulation and psychological harm, and loss-of-control risks, significant gaps remain: in what constitutes an incident, in how to treat single versus clustered events, and in how to define severity and harm type. This is a more fundamental challenge, because the definitional building blocks required to design detection and monitoring patterns are themselves missing. Point-in-time reports on these incident types advance our understanding, but building ongoing monitoring without clarity at the definitions layer risks creating divergence across developers, making cross-developer risk trend analysis harder over time.

Finally, the data required to identify incidents is distributed across public AI incident databases, AI developers, and third-party organizations including law enforcement, security services, and other incident-reporting domains. To understand the practical impact of these data gaps and prioritize efforts to surface and combine data, mapping exercises are needed, tracing the data elements required for detection and evaluation across primary risk domains, identifying which data matters most, who holds it, and what is required to access it, so that the data needed to detect and escalate the most severe incidents is available in practice.

A.2
DSA provisions: data access, crisis protocols, and crisis-response powers

Additional DSA provisions aimed at preventing ‘dark patterns’ may also help mitigate manipulation, psychological harm, and risks to democratic integrity. Article 35(1)(h) signals that where risks are shared across platforms, providers should cooperate, implicitly recognizing that cross-platform aggregation is relevant to systemic risk identification. However, this is a soft expectation rather than an enforceable requirement.

Article 48 of the DSA also outlines voluntary crisis protocols for ‘crisis situations’ or ‘specific extraordinary circumstances’. Yet the Commission has not fully specified the parameters for determining what constitutes such a circumstance. This contributes to a pattern of incomplete soft enforcement, mirrored in the voluntary nature of the General-Purpose AI Code of Practice and the absence of further detailed guidance in some areas of implementation.

A.2.1
Article 40: researcher data access

The DSA makes a further contribution through Article 40, which creates a legally grounded data access mechanism enabling researchers to request data from VLOPs and VLOSEs for the purpose of detecting, identifying, and understanding systemic risks in the Union. Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/2050 operationalizes this through the DSA Data Access Portal. The mechanism operates across two tiers: Article 40(4) grants vetted researchers access to non-public platform data, but requires affiliation with a research institution; Article 40(12) provides a broader access route to publicly available data for researchers affiliated with not-for-profit bodies, organizations, and associations, without the institutional affiliation requirement. Article 40 may therefore help support cross-platform pattern analysis of manipulation, disinformation, and psychological harm incidents—precisely the risk categories where an aggregated view across actors is most needed and where single-provider reporting is structurally insufficient.

However, three limitations constrain this mechanism’s utility. The first is jurisdictional: Article 40 applies to VLOPs and VLOSEs and not to AI model providers. The second is operational: enforcement of Article 40 has been actively contested, with formal proceedings opened against X and Meta, among others, for failures to comply. Article 40 is also researcher-initiated and application-dependent: it creates a right of access rather than a standing monitoring function, thereby generating episodic research outputs rather than the continuous cross-developer pattern detection that effective systemic risk monitoring requires.

A.2.2
Article 36: crisis-response mechanism

Article 36 of the DSA establishes a crisis-response mechanism enabling the Commission to require specific mitigation measures from VLOPs and VLOSEs where a ‘serious threat to public security or public health’ has arisen (Art. 36(2))
(
31
)
. This is a propagation-aware emergency provision in the sense that it is designed for situations where harm is spreading faster than routine regulatory response can address.

However, Article 36 is reactive and Commission-initiated—it responds to crises in progress, rather than enabling pre-emptive escalation when propagation indicators are detected early. Article 36(1)(c) ambiguously states that providers must report to the Commission ‘by a certain date or at regular intervals’ without giving additional detail beyond that providers must ‘take due account of the gravity of the serious threat’ (Art. 36(3)(a)) and that the specific measures taken are to be ‘limited to a period not exceeding three months’ (Art. 36(3)(c)), which may be further extended (Art. 36(8)(b)). Critically, Article 36 has not been enforced to date, meaning its practical utility as a propagation-based trigger remains untested. In cases where the propagated harm involves poisoned data, the data quality failures identified under Articles 10 and 15 (AIA), which concern data governance and accuracy, currently involve no escalation triggers for when such poisoned data is detected, because it is classified as an ‘AI specific vulnerability’ (Art. 15(5) AIA) rather than as a serious incident.

Appendix C
Detailed comparative analysis of existing frameworks against our criteria

Incident escalation rapid assessment framework

This section presents eight assessment criteria for determining whether an AI incident warrants escalation, and defines the thresholds and triggers associated with each. To inform the design of these criteria, we conducted a structured comparative analysis across three source categories: AI-specific regulatory frameworks (the EU AI Act and California’s SB 53), classification approaches used by major AI incident databases (OECD AIM, AIID, MIT), and established incident management practices in analogous high-risk domains (health, finance, and cybersecurity). These sources are not treated as models to be replicated directly; AI incident escalation operates in a distinct context with its own constraints, including attribution uncertainty, rapid capability change, and cross-border digital propagation, that no existing domain fully addresses. Rather, each source provides design candidates: specific mechanisms, threshold structures, and classification logics that have been tested operationally in mature governance regimes and that may be adapted to the AI incident context. For each criterion, we present the most relevant precedents from regulation, incident databases, and comparator domains, identify where they converge or diverge, and use these to justify our proposed threshold design. The resulting criteria are then tested against real-world AI incidents in Section [X] to evaluate their practical applicability.

Note on domain selection.

The comparative domains cited vary across criteria because no single non-AI domain provides adequate design precedent across all assessment dimensions: we draw on the domains that offer the most operationally tested mechanism for the specific design problem at hand, based on our literature review (Section X). In each case, we assess transferability rather than assuming it: the goal is to identify what inspiration mature domains can offer for AI-specific threshold design, not to import their approaches wholesale.

Consensus and divergence across EU AI Act and SB 53:
The EU AI Act and SB 53 diverge on contribution strength. SB 53 uses ‘materially contribute,’ a stronger test that excludes merely incidental involvement, especially in multi-software chains. The EU AI Act uses ‘causal relationship’ including direct or indirect causation, and the GPAI Code further allows reporting when that relationship is only reasonably likely or, for death, even suspected. The EU standard is broader on causal inclusion and lower on evidential certainty, while SB 53 is narrower on contribution strength and the kinds of harms covered.

Rationale for threshold:
Our goal is to avoid missing incidents where the exact involvement is not yet confirmed. As such we adopt a threshold which checks for the presence of AI in the causal chain, and then captures the confidence in the causal contribution at the time of the assessment.

Key challenges/ limitations:

1. Trade-off with escalation decision based on confidence level: escalating with low confidence may risk unnecessary disruption/ alarm if no actual AI causality, and may reduce confidence in the escalation process, but escalation delays could risk avoidable harm propagation.

2. For multi-agent environments, it may be hard to establish which AI systems are causally responsible for an incident, particularly if there is a lack of information about deployed AI systems/ agents and a lack of data on their shared interactions.

The EU AI Act Article 5 is a prohibition regime: it defines practices that are unlawful, regardless of whether harm results. This illustrates how disallowed practices can be defined in absolute, without linking harm assessment of these. However, the serious incidents themselves are defined in relation to harm impact. Rationale for threshold:
Our proposal draws on both regulatory approaches: from the EU AI Act, the principle that certain conditions can be defined as impermissible in absolute terms without requiring a linked harm assessment; and from SB 53 §1107(d)(4), the precedent that an incident type alone can constitute a reportable trigger. Where SB 53 pairs incident types with minimum harm thresholds in most cases, we use incident-type triggers at this criterion exclusively for conditions where catastrophic harm has not yet materialized but the intervention window is narrow, the probability of severe harm without action is high, and the consequences of inaction are likely irreversible. If internationally agreed red lines for AI were established, these could also be checked at this trigger condition, irrespective of confirmed harm at the time of assessment.

Key challenges/ limitations:

1. As AI capabilities and deployment contexts evolve, the set of conditions warranting immediate escalation will need to be reviewed and updated.

2. The EU AI Act’s commitment to annual review of Article 5 prohibited practices (Article 112) provides a precedent for building this into the framework’s governance.

Cyber:

MITRE ATT&CK — Tactic chains as correlated-incident pattern

ATT&CK maps adversary tactics, techniques and procedures (TTPs) as campaign patterns, not isolated events. A series of individually low-severity observations (initial access, discovery, lateral movement) constitutes a high-severity campaign pattern when assessed collectively. MITRE ATLAS extends this to AI-specific attack chains.

Related incidents are assessed within tiered rolling time windows calibrated to root cause type: approximately 30 days for technical root causes (where shared infrastructure creates rapid correlated failures), 60 days for contextual root causes, and 90 days for capability root causes (where cross-provider patterns inherently take longer to surface as they require accumulating reports across independent providers).

If a root cause match is found within the relevant time window, the incident is assessed as a composite incident for harm assessment at criterion 5. Where matches exist at multiple root cause levels, each level produces a separate composite; the most severe composite determines the escalation path. If no match is found at any level, the incident proceeds as an individual incident.

Whether a cluster crosses national borders is assessed at the propagation criterion (criterion 7) rather than at this stage, as cross-border spread is a property of the cluster’s impact profile rather than a clustering criterion.

Our questions assume monitoring is operational at both a developer and cross-developer level which allows these questions to be answered, a point discussed in section X.

SB 53’s framing around ‘a single incident’ with no correlated-incident aggregation provision represents a coverage gap for cumulative incident types. The EU AI Act’s systemic risk definition acknowledges propagation ‘at scale across the value chain’ and includes a requirement to include patterns detected during post-market monitoring that are related to a serious incident but does not operationalize a pattern-detection mechanism.

Rationale for threshold:
We adopt from the EU AI Act the requirement to consider related incidents when evaluating a serious incident. Given that pattern-detection mechanisms are not provided, which would be required to design thresholds and triggers, we draw upon the definition of ‘AI incident variants’ and Basel II event taxonomy groupings to inform our design. We extended the root cause concept beyond shared technical infrastructure to include shared capability characteristics and shared deployment contexts, on the basis that systemic risk types such as manipulation or psychological harm may manifest identically across unrelated models due to common design incentives or training methodologies, rather than shared components.

Key challenges/ limitations:

1. Identifying patterns relating to a serious incident being assessed requires tracking and analytics of incidents to be done and for clustering patterns to have been defined.

2. The level at which monitoring is done has implications for the incident patterns which can be identified within and across AI model developers.

3. Pattern identification at the capability and contextual root cause levels requires cross-provider incident visibility that is unlikely to be achievable by individual providers acting alone.

4. The use of different taxonomies and classification approaches across providers and incident databases increases the difficulty of building a cross-provider picture of systemic risk and incident patterns.

5. Clustering necessitates tracking and analytics of incidents: the level at which monitoring is performed has direct implications for which incident patterns can be identified.

Purpose and scope

The main text identifies the propagation mechanisms — common exposure, environmental coupling, network contagion, and their compounding through interdependence — that determine how AI incidents escalate. This appendix provides the formal topology-specific treatment: how the T0–T3 classification (defined below) maps onto distinct propagation regimes, what structural features of the deployment environment determine the propagation profile within each regime, and how transitions between regimes can be detected through information-theoretic measurement.

The classification is structured as follows. Section

F.4

F.2
The topological risk framework (T0–T3)

The topological risk framework classifies multi-agent AI configurations according to the
source of behavioral correlation
2
2

2

Correlation is used here in a broader sense than statistical correlation, encompassing any systematic relationship between agents’ behaviors, including statistical co-occurrence, coordinated action, causal influence (where one agent’s behavior shapes another’s), and emergent collective dynamics. We are interested in what produces a relationship and how the relationship can be measured, not the form of the relationship.
between agents. The question we address is this: when multiple AI agents exhibit correlated behavior, what is the causal mechanism producing that correlation? The answer determines the propagation profile, the appropriate governance response, and the conditions under which international coordination becomes necessary.

The framework defines four categories:

T0: Independent deployment.
Each agent operates independently. There is no mechanism, either exogenous or endogenous, producing behavioral correlation between agents. Agents are functionally, behaviorally, and mechanistically isolated from each other: no shared real-time inputs, no observation of each other’s outputs, no interaction through a shared environment. Aggregate harm scales linearly with the number of deployments:
f(N)=N
. T0 is below the multi-agent threshold; it is included as the baseline against which multi-agent dynamics are defined. We note that AI capability is often measured on the assumption that all AI deployments are isolated in this sense.

T1: Common-cause correlation.
Agents exhibit correlated behavior because they share an upstream dependency: common training data, a shared foundation model, shared infrastructure, or a common exogenous signal (e.g. a market price feed). The correlation is
exogenous
— it originates outside the agent interaction and would persist even if agents could not observe each other. Removing the shared upstream source eliminates the correlation. T1 is the regime of correlated failure without contagion: agents fail simultaneously because they share a vulnerability, not because one agent’s failure triggers another’s.

T2: Hierarchical orchestration.
A single orchestrating entity (which may itself be an AI system) directs multiple sub-agents toward a coordinated objective. The correlation is
deliberately imposed
by the orchestrator rather than arising from shared upstream causes or emergent dynamics. T2 is structurally orthogonal to the T1–T3 spectrum: an orchestrated system can simultaneously exhibit T1 properties (sub-agents sharing common training data) or T3 properties (sub-agents developing emergent coordination beyond the orchestrator’s design). The orchestration hierarchy is an additional structural layer that shapes propagation independently of the correlation source.

T3: Emergent interaction.
Agents exhibit correlated behavior that is produced
entirely by the interaction dynamics between agents
. There is no shared upstream cause driving the correlation and no orchestrator imposing it. The coordination arises endogenously from the agents’ mutual observation of, and response to, each other’s behavior within a shared environment. T3 is the regime of emergent equilibria: the correlated behavior is self-sustaining, and persists even if the initial conditions that seeded it are removed. T3 dynamics can produce qualitatively new outcomes — phase transitions, lock-in to harmful equilibria, collective behaviors not present in any individual agent’s design — that represent the most challenging category for governance because the harm is a property of the interaction structure rather than of any identifiable agent.

T3 is the most difficult topology category to build intuition for, as the harmful outcome is not attributable to any agent’s design or intent. Microsoft’s Tay chatbot (2016) provides an early cautionary illustration
(
71
;
100

. Tay was a single AI agent with online learning deployed into a reactive environment (Twitter) where its behavior was shaped in real time by its interactions with human users. Within hours of deployment, a self-organising population of users discovered that Tay’s learning mechanism could be exploited: by systematically feeding it offensive content, they could reshape its outputs toward racist, sexist, and antisemitic language.

The resulting behavior was not designed by Microsoft, not achievable by any individual user, and not attributable to Tay’s architecture in isolation; it was an emergent property of the interaction between an adaptive agent and a strategically responsive population of human ‘agents’. In the T0–T3 framework, Tay is a single-AI-agent T3 incident: the deployment environment (an open platform with adversarial users who could observe and condition on Tay’s outputs) created a multi-agent feedback dynamic — a human–AI multi-agent system — that produced outcomes qualitatively different from anything the agent would have produced in isolation. The case also illustrates why ‘multi-agent’ must encompass human–AI interaction dynamics, not only AI–AI interactions: a single AI system interacting with a self-organising human population can exhibit the same emergent, endogenous coordination dynamics that define T3.

Propagation regimes by topology category

Each topology category exhibits a characteristic propagation profile: a combination of scaling behavior, cascade mechanism, propagation speed determinant, and intervention architecture.

F.3.1
T0: Independent deployment

Propagation scaling.
Linear:
f(N)\simeq N
. Doubling the number of deployed agents doubles the aggregate harm. There is no inter-agent propagation pathway; each agent’s contribution to harm is independent of all others.

Cascade mechanism.
None. Failures are statistically independent. The failure of one agent provides no information about the probability of failure in any other agent.

Propagation speed.
Bounded by adoption rate. The speed at which harm accumulates is determined by how fast the AI system is deployed, not by any interaction dynamic.

Intervention architecture.
Agent-level capability evaluation. Existing single-agent safety frameworks (EU AI Act risk categories, model evaluations, use-case restrictions) are adequate.

Relevance to severity threshold.
T0 incidents cross the international coordination threshold only through scale — the same harm replicated across a large number of independent deployments in many jurisdictions simultaneously. The propagation is not qualitatively different from any other technology risk that scales with adoption.

F.3.2
T1: Common-cause correlation

Propagation scaling.
Correlated: an exogenous shock triggers simultaneous failure across the agent population sharing the upstream dependency. The scaling is determined by the upstream dependency, i.e. the fraction of the deployed agent population sharing the common source. Algorithmic monoculture

Cascade mechanism.
Common-cause: a single failure event propagates from the shared upstream source to all dependent agents in one step. There is no inter-agent cascade; agents fail simultaneously because they share a vulnerability, not because one agent’s failure triggers another’s.

Propagation speed.
Instantaneous relative to the upstream event. Once the shared source fails, all dependent agents are affected simultaneously. The detection challenge is not speed of propagation but identification of the shared dependency structure before the failure occurs.

Intervention architecture.
Upstream source governance: model diversity requirements, stress-testing against common-mode failures, supply-chain transparency, infrastructure resilience standards. The CrowdStrike outage (2024), in which a single update simultaneously disabled approximately 8.5 million Windows devices, illustrates the T1 propagation profile.

Relevance to severity threshold.
T1 incidents cross the international coordination threshold when the shared upstream source is global. T1 is the most tractable topology category for governance because the intervention target is identifiable (audit and diversify the upstream source), but it is empirically under-studied for AI-specific dependencies.

F.4
Environmental structure as a determinant of propagation dynamics

A key element here is that the deployment environment is a constitutive component of the hazard: a model that passes every single-agent safety evaluation can produce catastrophic outcomes when deployed into an environment whose structure supports harmful equilibria
(
84
;
61
)
. We identify five important environmental dimensions that shape the propagation profile.

F.4.1
Information structure

The observability regime of the deployment environment: public versus private signals, observability of other agents’ actions, and observability by oversight systems. The critical configuration for propagation risk is high inter-agent observability combined with low regulatory observability. These are environments where all participants observe the same signals but regulators cannot observe the participants’ responses. Monitoring infrastructure must close this observability gap.

F.4.2
Payoff structure

The consequence architecture: zero-sum, positive-sum, or mixed-sum interactions; continuous versus binary outcome spaces; reversible versus irreversible actions. Binary, irreversible payoff structures convert gradual risk accumulation into sudden, catastrophic outcomes. The Cold War MAD equilibrium illustrates the extreme: different capabilities and knowledge converged on the same strategic equilibrium because the payoff structure dominated the heterogeneity of the agents involved. Environments with binary, irreversible payoffs should receive higher standing hazard ratings regardless of detected anomalies.

F.4.3
Coupling structure

The network topology of agent interactions: hub-and-spoke versus distributed; direct agent-to-agent versus environment-mediated interaction. Hub-and-spoke networks enable single-hop system-wide propagation. Distributed networks propagate more slowly but are harder to contain. When tight couplings are present, perturbations propagate faster than operators can respond, and this is Perrow’s precondition for normal accidents
(
84
)
. Interdependent networks undergo abrupt cascading collapse where isolated networks degrade gradually
(
16
)
, and connections that normally diversify risk become contagion channels under stress
(
40
;
1
)
.

F.4.4
Temporal structure

The interaction timescale: one-shot versus repeated interaction, the ‘shadow of the future’, and commitment mechanisms. Temporal structure determines propagation speed directly, as the same underlying failure propagates at the rate set by the environment’s interaction cycle. Detection-latency requirements follow: the monitoring system must operate faster than the propagation dynamics it aims to detect. Environments with fast interaction cycles (high-frequency trading: milliseconds) require automated, continuous monitoring; environments with slow cycles (policy advisory: months) permit human-in-the-loop approaches.

F.4.5
Regulatory structure

Jurisdiction and scope of applicable governance. When the interaction scope of the AI + environment system exceeds the observation scope of any single regulator, propagation can cross jurisdictional boundaries undetected. This is a primary generator of the international coordination threshold: governance gaps emerge by construction when interaction dynamics are global but regulatory observation is national
(
47
)
.

F.5
The T1
\to
T3 transition: signal endogenization

The transition from T1 (common-cause correlation) to T3 (emergent interaction) is the most consequential propagation-regime change for severity-threshold design, because it marks the point at which the governance approach must shift from upstream-source control to interaction-structure intervention. The transition passes through three stages:

F.5.1
Stage 1: Sunspot correlation (near-T1)

Agents observe a common exogenous signal (a price feed, a data release, a shared training distribution) and they all respond to it similarly because of shared architecture or training. Their behavior is correlated, but the correlation is entirely attributable to the common signal. Removing the signal eliminates the correlation. The propagation structure is a star graph: common signal
\to
correlated responses, with no inter-agent edges.

Detection signature.
High correlating information (mutual information) between agent outputs. Low or zero causal information (transfer entropy) between agent outputs. Information flow is from signal to agents, not between agents.

F.5.2
Stage 2: Cheap talk (transition zone)

Agents begin to condition on each other’s observable behavior as well as on the exogenous signal. The exogenous signal still drives the bulk of the correlation, but inter-agent observation introduces weak directed influence. Agents may use language, pricing signals, or other observable outputs as de facto communication channels, transmitting information about intentions or strategies without explicit coordination.
22

cheap talk
, that acquire meaning through repeated interaction, enabling cooperation rates rivalling human–human coordination in repeated
2\times 2
games. The signals are formally payoff-irrelevant but become behaviorally relevant because agents learn to condition on them, bootstrapping trust and mutual expectations through interaction history. This propagation structure adds inter-agent edges to the star graph, creating a hybrid topology.

Detection signature.
Correlating information remains high. Causal information between agent outputs begins to rise. The ratio of causal to correlating information is the key diagnostic: a rising ratio signals that endogenous coupling is developing within an environment that still appears to be driven by common-cause correlation.

F.5.3
Stage 3: Implicit coordination (T3)

Agents’ behavior is primarily determined by interaction dynamics rather than by the exogenous signal. The equilibrium is self-sustaining: removing the original common signal does not break the coordination. The propagation structure is a dense, feedback-rich graph with no necessary connection to the original exogenous signal.

Detection signature.
Causal information between agent outputs is elevated and stable. The critical diagnostic is that causal information remains elevated even when the original common signal is perturbed or removed, indicating an endogenized coupling between agents.

Empirical anchors.

19

F.6
Information-theoretic measurement signatures

The detection of topology categories and transitions between them requires measurement infrastructure that can distinguish correlation from causation in agent-interaction data. The core measurement framework uses two complementary quantities:

Correlating information
(operationalized as mutual information or computationally tractable proxies such as Theil decomposition indices;
94
;
46
) captures the degree of statistical association between agent outputs. It detects synchronisation between agents’ behaviors but cannot determine whether the similarity arises from common cause (T1) or mutual influence (T3).

Causal information
(operationalized as transfer entropy or Granger-causal measures;
8
;
46
) captures directed, time-asymmetric information flow between agent outputs. It detects one agent’s behavior systematically predicting changes in another’s (i.e. causal influence) and can distinguish T1 (no inter-agent causal flow) from T3 (elevated inter-agent causal flow).

F.7
Operationalisation as escalation triggers

F.7.1
Value-at-Risk analogy

One approach to operationalizing the measurement signatures is analogous to Value-at-Risk (VaR) methods in finance: characterize the distribution of information-measure fluctuations during quiescent periods and use percentile bounds (e.g. 95th or 99th percentile) as calibrated risk thresholds. An exceedance of the 99th percentile for causal information between agents in a previously T1-classified system would constitute a statistical trigger indicating a possible T1
\to
T3 transition.

Calibrating the relationship between a percentile exceedance and the expected magnitude of real-world impact is domain-specific and requires both simulation and accumulating empirical data, but the framework provides a principled basis for connecting statistical detection to the threshold logic developed in the main text.

F.7.2
Topology-sensitive escalation

The cost of late detection varies by topology category and environmental structure:

T0.
Conventional risks. Detection latency is bounded by adoption speed. Existing single-agent frameworks are adequate.

T1 and early T1
\to
T3 transition.
Early detection can enable effective intervention through circuit breakers, diversity requirements, or interaction-protocol constraints. The intervention window exists because the coupling is not yet self-sustaining.

Established T3.
Intervention options narrow considerably. The equilibrium is endogenous and self-sustaining; disrupting it requires restructuring the interaction environment itself, for example changing the payoff structure, the information rules, or the coupling topology. Agent-level remediation is insufficient.

Environmental modulation.
The same detected anomaly warrants faster escalation in a tightly coupled environment than in a loosely coupled one. In a tightly coupled, fast-interaction environment (high-frequency trading), late detection of T3 dynamics may mean the harmful equilibrium is already locked in before any governance response is possible. In a loosely coupled, slow-interaction environment (policy-advisory systems), the same T3 dynamics develop over months, giving governance systems time to intervene.

F.7.3
Standing hazard assessment

Beyond the detection of anomalies in ongoing dynamics, the environmental structure itself should be assessed as a standing hazard indicator. Even in the absence of detected anomalies, environments with the following structural properties have inherently higher propagation potential for multi-agent AI incidents:

•

Tight coupling (perturbations propagate faster than operators can respond)

•

Fast interaction cycles (milliseconds to hours rather than days to months)

•

Binary, irreversible payoff structures (elections, military engagements, critical infrastructure)

•

Jurisdictional gaps (interaction scope exceeds any single regulator’s observation scope)

•

High inter-agent observability with low regulatory observability

This prospective assessment, whereby environments are flagged where the structure is conducive to dangerous propagation profiles, complements the retrospective detection of anomalies and can be conducted before any AI system is deployed into the environment.

F.7.4
Integration with existing regulatory monitoring

Where historical data exist through conventional regulatory processes — such as financial regulators tracking volatility, correlation structures, and order flow; energy regulators monitoring grid stability and frequency deviations; and competition authorities tracking pricing patterns and market concentration — these macro-state variables constitute an existing observational infrastructure against which AI-mediated changes can be benchmarked.

This macro-state monitoring provides a first-pass detection layer: low-specificity indicators that warrant targeted investigation using the information-theoretic measures described above, or other more targeted investigations where appropriate. An effective AI-incident monitoring framework does not need to build all observational infrastructure from scratch; it can integrate with existing regulatory monitoring, using changes in already tracked macro-variables as the initial anomaly signal, especially where dense historical data already exist as a baseline.

The fundamental constraint is that current detection is late. Interaction-emergent failures are typically identified after the fact — through market anomalies, security incidents, media reports, or user complaints — rather than through real-time monitoring. Continuous, system-level measurement infrastructure operating across providers, jurisdictions, and deployment contexts is a prerequisite for any escalation framework that aims to be preventive rather than reactive.
