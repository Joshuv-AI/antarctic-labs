From wrinkled seeds to plant oil accumulation networks: the legacy of a Plant Physiology classic
Authors: Que Kong, Sitakanta Pattanaik, Fantao Kong, Liang Guo, Ling Yuan, Wei Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlant seeds accumulate triacylglycerol (TAG) as a major storage reserve that supports postgermination growth and seedling establishment. Vegetable oils are also essential for human nutrition and provide renewable feedstocks for industrial and biotechnological applications. In 1998, Focks and Benning published a landmark study in Plant Physiology describing the Arabidopsis WRINKLED1 mutants (wri1), which display a distinctive wrinkled seed phenotype and a dramatic reduction in seed oil accumulation. The conceptual importance of this discovery was not simply the identification of a low-oil mutant, but the demonstration that seed oil accumulation depends on developmental control of carbon flux from carbohydrates into fatty acid precursors. Cloning of the Arabidopsis WRI1 (AtWRI1) gene in 2004 transformed this physiological phenotype into a molecular framework by identifying WRI1 as a member of the APETALA2 (AP2) family of transcription factors that activates late glycolytic and fatty acid biosynthetic genes. Subsequent work uncovered the AW-box cis-element, upstream seed-maturation regulators, WRI1-interacting partners, post-transcriptional and post-translational modification mechanisms controlling WRI1 stability and activity, and the structural basis of WRI1-DNA recognition. These discoveries established WRI1 as a central regulatory node linking seed development, carbohydrate metabolism, and seed oil accumulation. More recent studies have broadened WRI1 biology beyond canonical seed oil biosynthesis to include non-seed oil-storing tissues, hormone and nutrient-associated processes, environmental responses, and structure-guided crop engineering. Here, we revisit the original Plant Physiology classic and trace how one mutant phenotype reshaped modern understanding of plant carbon partitioning, transcriptional regulation, and metabolic engineering

Introduction: a Plant Physiology classic

Since its founding a century ago, Plant Physiology has published numerous seminal studies that have shaped modern plant science. Among these, the landmark work by Focks and Bening, published in 1998 (Focks and Benning 1998), stands out because it connected a visible seed phenotype to a central problem in plant metabolism—how developing seeds allocate carbon into storage lipids. The study reported Arabidopsis wri1 mutants with a distinctive wrinkled seed phenotype and approximately 80% reduction in seed oil content (Focks and Benning 1998) (Fig. 1a). By combining genetic analysis with detailed metabolic and enzymatic profiling, the authors established that WRI1 functions in the seed-specific developmental regulation of glycolysis, fundamentally reframing how carbon partitioning into storage lipids is controlled (Focks and Benning 1998). The conceptual shift is the reason why this original article remains as a Plant Physiology classic and provides the foundation for this centennial review. The original wri1 mutant was important because it revealed a regulatory problem that could not be solved by listing lipid biosynthetic enzymes alone. By the 1990s, many steps of fatty acid biosynthesis and oil modification were already known, yet it remained unclear how developing seeds coordinated carbohydrate metabolism with TAG accumulation. Focks and Benning (1998) showed that the low-oil phenotype of wri1 mutants was associated with impaired conversion of sucrose and glucose into lipid precursors, while vegetative growth was largely unaffected. This finding shifted attention from individual enzymatic reactions to developmental control of carbon flux. In retrospect, the Plant Physiology 1998 paper anticipated the modern view that oil accumulation is a network-level carbon-partitioning program involving transcriptional regulation, metabolic capacity, and developmental timing.

The 1990s context and the wri1 discovery

By the mid-1990s, the major biochemical steps of fatty acid biosynthesis in developing seeds had been characterized, largely through genetic and biochemical studies in Arabidopsis and other oilseed crops (Ohlrogge and Browse 1995; Harwood 1996). Considerable progress had also been made in modifying seed oil composition through metabolic engineering (Kinney 1994; Ohlrogge 1994). Nevertheless, a fundamental question remained unresolved: how developing embryos coordinate carbohydrate metabolism, glycolytic flux, fatty acid biosynthesis, and triacylglycerol (TAG) accumulation during seed filling. This gap highlighted a missing regulatory layer above individual enzymes: the developmental control of metabolic network capacity.

It was within this context that Focks and Benning identified the wri1 mutants in Arabidopsis, providing a pivotal breakthrough in understanding seed oil accumulation (Focks and Benning 1998) (Fig. 1a). The mutant was isolated based on its characteristic wrinkled seed phenotype and exhibited a dramatic reduction (approximately 80%) in seed oil content, while vegetative growth remained largely unaffected (Focks and Benning 1998). Detailed metabolic analyses revealed that developing wri1 seeds accumulated elevated levels of soluble sugars and starch, accompanied by a strong reduction in the incorporation of sucrose and glucose into TAG. In contrast, the incorporation of downstream precursors such as pyruvate and acetate into lipids was increased. These findings demonstrated that the primary defect in wri1 was in the conversion of carbohydrates into lipid precursors, implicating a disruption in glycolytic flux during seed development (Focks and Benning 1998).

This discovery was highly novel at the time because it provided genetic evidence that carbon allocation into seed oil is developmentally programmed. Importantly, the authors anticipated 2 lines of future research that became defining themes of the field: identification of the WRI1 gene to reveal its molecular function and evaluation of WRI1 as a potential target for engineering oilseed crops (Focks and Benning 1998). The following 3 decades confirmed both predictions. WRI1 became a model for understanding how transcriptional regulation controls carbon partitioning, and it became one of the most widely used transcriptional regulators for plant oil engineering.

From metabolic phenotype to transcriptional master regulator

The next conceptual breakthrough was the conversion of the wri1 phenotype into a transcriptional regulatory framework. In 2002, the Benning and Ohlrogge laboratories performed comparative transcriptomic analysis (microarray) of wild-type (WT) and wri1 developing seeds (Ruuska et al. 2002). This study showed that many genes with decreased expression in wri1 encode key enzymes in late glycolysis and fatty acid biosynthesis (Ruuska et al. 2002), providing molecular evidence that WRI1 coordinates the metabolic entry points needed for oil accumulation. A few years later, the Benning laboratory identified the Arabidopsis WRI1 (AtWRI1) gene (Fig. 1a), and showed that it encodes an APETALA2 (AP2) transcription factor (Fig. 1b) (Cernac and Benning 2004). Subsequent studies demonstrated that numerous genes involved in late glycolysis and fatty acid biosynthesis are direct targets of WRI1 (Baud et al. 2007; Maeo et al. 2009). These findings were further strengthened by the identification of the AW-box ([CnTnG](n)7[CG]) as a conserved cis-element recognized by WRI1 (Maeo et al. 2009). Collectively, these studies established WRI1 as a transcriptional regulator that channels carbohydrate-derived carbon toward fatty acid biosynthesis, ultimately leading to its designation as a “master regulator” of plant oil biosynthesis (Chapman and Ohlrogge 2012). WRI1 also regulates genes that modulate fatty acid biosynthesis capacity, including GLB1, which encodes the PII protein, (Baud et al. 2010), BIOTIN ATTACHMENT DOMAIN-CONTAINING (BADC) genes (Liu et al. 2019), and CARBOXYLTRANSFERASE INTERACTOR (CTI) (Ye et al. 2020), indicating that WRI1 controls both biosynthetic enzymes and feedback-regulatory components of lipid metabolism.

Developmental regulators upstream of WRI1

Once WRI1 was recognized as a central regulator of seed lipid metabolism, a key question became how its expression is embedded within seed developmental programs. Genetic and molecular studies over the past 2 decades have placed WRI1 within a broader seed maturation regulatory hierarchy governed primarily by the LAFL regulators LEAFY COTYLEDON1 (LEC1), LEAFY COTYLEDON2 (LEC2), ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE3 (ABI3), and FUSCA3 (FUS3) (Fig. 2). This hierarchy explains how seed identity, maturation state, and carbon storage programs are coupled with the activation of oil biosynthesis.

Early evidence implicated LEC1, a CCAAT-binding factor, and LEC2, a B3-domain regulator, as upstream transcriptional activators of AtWRI1. Expression of AtWRI1 is elevated in the LEC1 gain-of-function mutant tnp (Casson and Lindsey 2006) and in transgenic plants overexpressing LEC1 (Mu et al. 2008), suggesting that LEC1 positively regulates AtWRI1 expression. Similarly, inducible overexpression of LEC2 activates AtWRI1, whereas AtWRI1 expression is reduced in lec2 mutants (Baud et al. 2007), supporting a role for LEC2 as another key transcriptional activator of AtWRI1. Although early studies could not determine whether LEC1 or LEC2 directly bind the AtWRI1 promoter, recent chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) experiments demonstrated that AtWRI1 is a direct target of LEC1, providing definitive molecular evidence for this regulatory relationship (Pelletier et al. 2017). The regulatory connection between LEC proteins and WRI1 appears to be evolutionarily conserved. Overexpression of maize LEC1 (ZmLEC1) increases expression of maize WRI1 (ZmWRI1) and activates a ZmWRI1 promoter-driven GUS reporter in maize cell culture (Shen et al. 2010). Likewise, transgenic soybean plants overexpressing soybean LEC2 (GmLEC2) show elevated expression of soybean WRI1 (GmWRI1) (Manan et al. 2017), indicating that LEC-mediated control of WRI1 is a conserved feature across oilseed species.

In addition to LEC1 and LEC2, the B3 domain transcription factor FUSCA3 (FUS3) functions as another upstream regulator of AtWRI1. Comparative transcriptome analyses revealed reduced AtWRI1 expression in fus3 mutants (Yamamoto et al. 2010). FUS3 had been hypothesized to have a functional redundancy with LEC2 in regulation of WRI1 expression (Yamamoto et al. 2010). Later on, new evidence from chromatin immunoprecipitation coupled with microarray analysis (ChIP-chip) verified that AtWRI1 is a direct transcriptional target of FUS3 (Wang and Perry 2013). Notably, sucrose treatment induces AtWRI1 expression (Masaki et al. 2005). Expression of some sugar-responsive genes is activated by AtWRI1, indicating the possible role of WRI1 in sugar signaling from carbon flow to oil production (Masaki et al. 2005). FUS3 does not affect the expression of LEC1/LEC2 expression, suggesting that activation of AtWRI1 by sucrose is via FUS3 instead of LEC1/LEC2 (Zhang et al. 2016). The B3 domain transcription factor ABI3 is a key component of the abscisic acid (ABA) signaling pathway, regulating essential processes such as seed maturation and dormancy (Rohde et al. 2000; Monke et al. 2012; Boulard et al. 2017; Cheng et al. 2026). Recent ChIP-chip analyses have identified AtWRI1 as a direct target of ABI3, along with multiple genes involved in fatty acid metabolism, such as FAD3 and FATTY ACID BIOSYNTHESIS 2 (FAB2) (Tian et al. 2020). Recently, another study showed that CYCLIN-DEPENDENT KINASE 8 (CDK8) is required for maximal AtWRI1 expression by promoting ABI3- and FUS3-dependent transactivation of the AtWRI1 promoter (Zhai et al. 2023).

In contrast to the positive regulation of AtWRI1 by LEC1, LEC2, FUS3, and ABI3, several Arabidopsis R2R3-MYB transcription factors (including MYB73, MYB44, and MYB89), act as repressors of AtWRI1 expression (Li et al. 2017; Yang et al. 2025; Chin et al. 2026). Transgenic plants overexpressing these MYBs exhibit reduced AtWRI1 expression levels, supporting their role as negative regulators. Functional analyses, including ChIP or electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA), further demonstrated that MYB73 and MYB89 can directly bind to the AtWRI1 promoter, indicating that AtWRI1 is a direct target of these MYB proteins (Li et al. 2017; Yang et al. 2025).

In addition to the canonical transcriptional regulators LEC1, LEC2, FUS3, and ABI3, alternative upstream regulators appear to control WRI1 expression in other plant species. In oil palm mesocarp (an oil-rich, non-seed tissue in which EgWRI1 is highly expressed), the absence of detectable expression of key regulators such as EgLEC1, EgLEC2, EgFUS3, and EgABI3 suggests that EgWRI1 expression could be controlled by alternative fruit-specific upstream regulators (Bourgis et al. 2011). Consistent with this notion, the transcription factors EgNF-YA3, EgNF-YC2, and EgABI5 have been identified to bind the EgWRI1 promoter and activate its expression (Yeap et al. 2017), highlighting the diversification of upstream regulatory networks controlling WRI1 expression across plant species.

Fine-tuning WRI1 stability, activity, and protein interactions

WRI1 activity is essential for seed oil biosynthesis and also contributes to developmental processes associated with early seedling establishment and germination (Focks and Benning 1998; Cernac and Benning 2004; Cernac et al. 2006). Because WRI1 sits at a metabolic control point, its abundance and activity must be tightly adjusted rather than constitutively maximized. Multiple layers of regulation, including ubiquitin-mediated turnover, phosphorylation, SUMOylation, intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), and protein-protein interactions, therefore fine-tune WRI1 function in response to developmental and environmental inputs (Fig. 2; Box 2).

WRI1 is not simply an on/off transcriptional activator. Its expression is controlled by seed maturation regulators, including LEC1, LEC2, FUS3, and ABI3, while its protein abundance and activity are adjusted by ubiquitination, phosphorylation, SUMOylation, intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), and interacting cofactors. Negative regulators such as BPMs, KIN10, TCP4, and bZIP52 constrain WRI1 activity, whereas 14-3-3 proteins, BBX32, PIF4/PIF5, MED15, and BLI can stabilize or enhance WRI1-dependent transcription. These layers allow developing seeds to fine-tune fatty acid biosynthesis according to developmental stage, carbon availability, and environmental conditions. A key future challenge is to determine how these mechanisms are integrated dynamically rather than studied as separate regulatory modules.

One major regulatory pathway involves ubiquitin-mediated protein turnover. The BTB/POZ-MATH (BPM) proteins, which function as adaptors of the CULLIN3 (CUL3) E3 ligase complex, physically interact with WRI1 and target it for degradation via the 26S proteasome (Chen et al. 2013). Additional regulation is mediated by the RNA-binding protein PUMILIO PROTEIN 24 (APUM24), which modulates BPM mRNA stability and thereby indirectly influences WRI1 turnover (Huang et al. 2021). In silico analyses revealed that WRI1 contains 3 IDRs, including a C-terminal IDR3 harboring both a PEST motif associated with proteolytic degradation and a nearby transactivation domain (TAD) (Ma et al. 2015). Deletion of the IDR3-PEST motif or mutation of putative phosphorylation residues within this motif enhances WRI1 protein stability and increases oil accumulation, highlighting the importance of phosphorylation-dependent regulation of WRI1 activity (Ma et al. 2015). 14-3-3 protein family members also physically interact with WRI1 (Ma et al. 2016). Co-expression of 14-3-3s with WRI1 in Nicotiana benthamiana enhances WRI1 stability and transcriptional activity, leading to elevated oil accumulation (Ma et al. 2016). Interestingly, 14-3-3 proteins and BPM1 bind overlapping regions of WRI1, suggesting that 14-3-3 proteins may competitively interfere with BPM-mediated proteasomal degradation, thereby stabilizing WRI1 (Ma et al. 2016; Kong and Ma, 2018). Another essential regulator is the SNF1-related protein kinase KIN10, which phosphorylates WRI1 and promotes its degradation (Zhai et al. 2017). Substitution of the phosphorylation sites T70 and S166 with alanine prevents KIN10-mediated phosphorylation and stabilizes the protein (Zhai et al. 2017). Conversely, the sugar signaling metabolite trehalose 6-phosphate (T6P) suppresses KIN10 activity, thereby indirectly stabilizing WRI1 and enhancing fatty acid biosynthesis (Zhai et al. 2018). In addition to phosphorylation-dependent regulation, WRI1 stability is also modulated via reversible SUMOylation. The SUMO E3 ligase SAP AND MIZ1 DOMAIN-CONTAINING LIGASE1 (SIZ1) stabilizes WRI1 through SUMO conjugation at residues K257 and K266, thereby protecting WRI1 from ubiquitination and degradation (Huang et al. 2025). This SUMO-dependent WRI1 stabilization mechanism is particularly important under high-temperature stress to sustain fatty acid biosynthesis. Recent work further demonstrated that the SUMO-specific proteases SUMO PROTEASE-RELATED TO FERTILITY 1 (SPF1) and SPF2 antagonize this process by mediating WRI1 deSUMOylation, leading to reduced WRI1 stability and decreased seed oil accumulation (Liu et al. 2026). Genetic and biochemical analyses showed that SPF1 and SPF2 physically interact with WRI1 and function upstream of WRI1-dependent oil biosynthesis (Liu et al. 2026), establishing a dynamic SUMOylation/deSUMOylation regulatory module that fine tunes seed development and seed oil accumulation.

While transcription factor complexes formed within the same protein family are common in plants, interactions between TFs from distinct families are increasingly recognized as important regulatory mechanisms that expand transcriptional specificity and responsiveness to developmental and environmental cues (Bemer et al. 2017; Tripathi et al. 2017; Sui et al. 2018; Alizadeh et al. 2021). Such cross-family interactions can modulate DNA-binding affinity, transcriptional activity, and target gene specificity, thereby fine-tuning metabolic pathways and developmental programs (Bemer et al. 2017). Emerging evidence indicates that WRI1 functions within an extensive regulatory network involving both transcriptional repressors and activators from diverse transcription factor families (Fig. 2).

Several TFs have been identified as negative regulators of WRI1 activity. A large-scale screen of an Arabidopsis transcription library identified TEOSINTE BRANCHED1/CYCLOIDEA/PROLIFERATING CELL FACTOR4 (TCP4) as a direct WRI1-interacting partner (Kong et al. 2020). Functional analyses demonstrated that TCP4 suppresses WRI1-mediated transcriptional activation, while tcp4 knockout mutants accumulate significantly higher seed oil content than WT (Kong et al. 2020). Besides, the basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factor bZIP52 physically interacts with WRI1 and represses its activity; whereas overexpression of bZIP52 reduces seed oil accumulation and bzip52 mutant exhibits increased oil content (Yang et al. 2023). Although knowledge of transcription factor-mediated repression of WRI1 remains limited, these studies identified TCP4 and bZIP52 as important negative regulators that constrain WRI1-dependent oil biosynthesis (Kong et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2023). In contrast, several transcription factors function as positive regulators of WRI1 activity. B-BOX DOMAIN PROTEIN32 (BBX32) interacts with WRI1 and enhances transcriptional activation of downstream targets, including BCCP2 and PKP-β1 (Lim et al. 2023). Consistent with this role, BBX32 overexpression increases seed oil content, whereas bbx32 mutants display reduced oil accumulation. EMSA further demonstrated that BBX32 enhances the DNA-binding affinity of WRI1 (Lim et al. 2023). More recently, PHYTOCHROME-INTERACTING FACTOR4 (PIF4) and PIF5 were identified as additional WRI1-interacting partners that promote WRI1 transcriptional activity and stimulate oil accumulation (Liao et al. 2025). Together, these findings establish BBX32 and PIF4/5 as co-transcriptional activators that enhance WRI1-dependent regulation of seed oil biosynthesis.

Beyond direct modulation of WRI1 activity, interacting partners also contribute to the assembly of higher-order transcriptional complexes. In eukaryotes, transcription factors commonly recruit the Mediator (MED) complex to facilitate transcription initiation (Taatjes 2010). In Arabidopsis, WRI1 physically interacts with the MED15 subunit, and MED15 overexpression enhances the expression of numerous fatty acid biosynthetic genes, many of which are WRI1 targets (Kim et al. 2016). Interestingly, MED15 can also activate fatty acid biosynthetic genes independently of WRI1, suggesting that different transcription factors recruit MED15 to fatty acid biosynthetic pathways (Kim et al. 2016). In contrast, the plant-specific coiled-coil protein BLISTER (BLI) appears to function specifically as a WRI1-dependent coactivator, enhancing expression of WRI1 target genes only in the presence of WRI1 (Huang et al. 2022).

Collectively, these studies reveal that WRI1 functions within a highly dynamic transcriptional regulatory network modulated by both repressors and co-activators. The interplay between negative regulators such as TCP4 and bZIP52 and positive regulators including BBX32, PIF4/5, MED15, and BLI provides additional flexibility and robustness to the control of seed oil accumulation, enabling fatty acid biosynthesis to be accurately fine tuned in response to developmental and environmental signals.

Structural insight converts WRI1 into an engineering framework

A major mechanistic breakthrough came from structural elucidation of the WRI1-DNA complex. Earlier studies have shown that WRI1 regulates fatty acid biosynthetic genes by binding to the AW-box cis-element ([CnTnG](n)7[CG]) in target promoters (Maeo et al. 2009), but the structural basis of sequence recognition remained unresolved. The high-resolution crystal structure reported in 2022 revealed that the tandem AP2 domains of WRI1 form 2 antiparallel beta-sheets that insert into adjacent major grooves of DNA, creating a DNA-binding configuration distinct from other AP2-domain containing transcription factors (Qiao et al. 2022). Structure-guided functional analyses identified residues required for AW-box recognition and linked DNA-binding affinity directly to oil accumulation in planta. Most importantly, the structure enabled rational engineering: the W74R substitution increased AW-box binding affinity and enhanced oil accumulation in transient N. benthamiana assays and stable Arabidopsis plants (Qiao et al. 2022). Introduction of the corresponding W-to-R substitution into WRI1 orthologs from Brassica napus, Camelina sativa, Glycine max, and Zea mays similarly enhanced oil accumulation, illustrating how basic structural biology can be translated into crop-relevant metabolic engineering. This work transformed WRI1 from a descriptive pivotal regulator into an engineerable molecular module.

From mechanism to crop engineering

A defining feature of WRI1 is its broad functional conservation across plant lineages and oil-accumulating organs. Since the discovery of AtWRI1 (Focks and Benning 1998; Cernac and Benning 2004), WRI1 orthologs have been characterized in diverse monocot and dicot plants, including Avena sativa (Grimberg et al. 2015; Yang et al. 2019), Brassica napus (Liu et al. 2010), Brachypodium distachyon (Yang et al. 2015), Camelina sativa (An et al. 2017), Cocos nucifera (Sun et al. 2017), Cyperus esculentus (Grimberg et al. 2015), Elaeis guineensis (Bourgis et al. 2011; Ma et al. 2013), Glycine max (Manan et al. 2017; Chen et al. 2020; Guo et al. 2020), Gossypium spp (Qu et al. 2012), Helianthus annuus (Lim et al. 2022), Jatropha curcas (Ye et al. 2018), Linum usitatissimum (Li et al. 2022), Oryza sativa (Mano et al. 2019), Paeonia ostii (Li et al. 2024), Persea americana (Kilaru et al. 2015), Ricinus communis (Tajima et al. 2013; Ji et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2019), Solanum tuberosum (Grimberg et al. 2015), Triticum aestivum (Yang et al. 2022a, 2022b), Zea mays (Shen et al. 2010; Pouvreau et al. 2011). Functional complementation studies demonstrated that AtWRI1 or WRI1 orthologs can rescue the low-oil phenotype of Arabidopsis wri1 loss-of-function mutants (Cernac and Benning 2004; Pouvreau et al. 2011; Ma et al. 2013; An et al. 2017; Yang et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2020; Li et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2022a, 2022b; Li et al. 2024). The biological lesson is not merely that many plant species contain WRI1s, but that WRI1 provides a conserved regulatory module for directing carbon into storage lipids while allowing tissue-specific diversification in seeds, oil palm mesocarp, avocado mesocarp, and nutsedge tubers (Bourgis et al. 2011; Grimberg et al. 2015; Kilaru et al. 2015).

The potent activity and conservation of WRI1 rapidly made it an attractive target for translational biotechnology (Box 3). Overexpression of AtWRI1 or WRI1 orthologs in multiple plant species consistently enhances seed oil accumulation, demonstrating that manipulation of WRI1 activity is a robust strategy for improving oil yield (Cernac and Benning 2004; Liu et al. 2010; Shen et al. 2010; An and Suh 2015; Yang et al. 2015; Ivarson et al. 2016; Sun et al. 2017; Chen et al. 2018; Ye et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2020; Guo et al. 2020; Li et al. 2022; Lim et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2022a, 2022b; Li et al. 2024; Kong et al. 2025). Additionally, WRI1 engineering illustrates an important principle: transcription factor manipulation must be coordinated with developmental timing and tissue specificity. For example, transgenic maize accumulated substantially more oil when ZmWRI1 expression was driven by an embryo-preferred OLEOSIN promoter rather than an endosperm-specific 19 KD ZEIN promoter (Shen et al. 2010) and extending AtWRI1 expression during seed maturation using the FUS3 promoter enhanced seed oil accumulation in Arabidopsis (Kanai et al. 2016). These findings underscored the importance of coordinating WRI1 expression with developmental programs controlling seed maturation and storage reserve deposition.

WRI1 became a major engineering target because it activates coordinated sets of genes required for late glycolysis and fatty acid biosynthesis. Overexpression of WRI1 or its orthologs can increase oil content in seeds and vegetative tissues, but successful engineering depends on promoter choice, tissue specificity, developmental timing, and downstream metabolic capacity. The WRI1-DNA structure added a new engineering dimension by enabling rational variants, such as W74R, with enhanced AW-box binding and increased oil accumulation. Future applications will likely combine WRI1 engineering with DGAT1, oil droplet packaging, lipase suppression, and stress-resilient regulatory variants to improve both oil yield and stability across environments.

Beyond seeds, WRI1 has emerged as a powerful tool for engineering TAG accumulation in vegetative tissues. Transient expression of AtWRI1 or WRI1 orthologs in N. benthamiana leaves efficiently stimulates TAG biosynthesis (Vanhercke et al. 2013; Grimberg et al. 2015; Ma et al. 2015; Zhai et al. 2017; Kong et al. 2020; Lim et al. 2022; Qiao et al. 2022). In addition, co-expression of WRI1 with DGAT1, which catalyzes the final step of TAG synthesis, results in synergistic increase in oil accumulation, illustrating the importance of integrating carbon flux activation with downstream lipid assembly pathways (Vanhercke et al. 2013). Enhancement of oil production was also achieved by using more stable WRI1 variants or co-production of WRI1-interacting proteins that increase WRI1 stability or using a WRI1 variant with stronger binding affinity to cis-element (Ma et al. 2015, 2016; Zhai et al. 2017; Qiao et al. 2022). To enhance oil accumulation in vegetative tissues, transgenic Arabidopsis plants were engineered to overexpress AtWRI1 while simultaneously suppressing the gene encoding small subunit of ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase (AGPase) through RNA interference (RNAi). This combinatorial strategy redirected carbon flux from starch to lipid biosynthesis, leading to a significant increase in TAG accumulation in leaves (Sanjaya et al. 2011). These findings directly translated mechanistic insights into practical metabolic engineering strategies. Engineering approaches combining WRI1 with additional metabolic modifications have generated impressive results. The “push, pull, package, and protect” engineering strategy involves simultaneously enhancing (“pushing”) de novo fatty acid biosynthesis through activation of key transcriptional regulators, directing (“pulling”) metabolic intermediates into TAG production via rate-limiting enzymes, packaging TAG into oil droplets, and protecting stored TAG from degradation through suppression of lipase activity (Vanhercke et al. 2014; Vanhercke et al. 2019). Additionally, WRI1 has also proven valuable to assist the production of unusual fatty acids with industrial applications. Overexpression of a fatty acid hydroxylase gene in Arabidopsis often reduces seed oil accumulation because of feedback inhibition on de novo fatty acid synthesis (Bates and Browse 2011; Bates et al. 2014). However, co-expression of AtWRI1 with the castor hydroxylase RcFAH12 simultaneously increased hydroxy fatty acid production and total seed oil content, demonstrating that WRI1 can overcome metabolic bottlenecks associated with the feedback inhibition by the fatty acid hydroxylase (Adhikari et al. 2016).

Although WRI1 overexpression is an effective strategy for enhancing oil accumulation, constitutive expression of WRI1 can lead to undesirable effects, including aberrant growth and cell death in some transgenic plant species (Cernac and Benning 2004; Marchive et al. 2014; Yang et al. 2015). These findings highlight the importance of precisely controlling the spatial and temporal expression of WRI1. Therefore, the careful selection of promoters appropriate for specific plant species has become a critical consideration for successful bioengineering of oilseed crops.

Future perspectives

Plants dynamically modify metabolism in response to developmental stage, nutrient status, and environmental conditions. The next phase of WRI1 research should therefore move beyond asking whether WRI1 promotes oil accumulation and instead ask how WRI1 integrates developmental, metabolic, and environmental information to produce context-dependent carbon-partitioning outcomes. Environmental conditions are known to profoundly influence both seed oil accumulation as well as fatty acid composition (Li et al. 2006; Singer et al. 2016; Balbino 2017; Karki and Bates 2018), yet the molecular mechanisms connecting these inputs to WRI1 expression, activity, target selection, and metabolic output remain incompletely understood.

The central future challenge is predictability. Manipulation of transcriptional regulators has often been more effective than engineering individual metabolic enzymes alone (Broun 2004; Grotewold 2008; Shen et al. 2010), but transcription factors can also affect multiple developmental and metabolic programs. For WRI1, predictive engineering will require a deeper understanding of how upstream regulators, chromatin state, interacting proteins, post-translational modifications, promoter architecture, and tissue-specific metabolic capacity combined to determine final oil output.

Although key seed developmental regulators such as LEC1, LEC2, FUS3, and ABI3 have been shown to activate WRI1 expression, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this regulation are still incompletely understood. In addition, whether other transcription factors modulate WRI1 expression in response to specific developmental, metabolic, or environmental cues remains an important area for future investigation. The discovery of non-seed-specific regulators controlling EgWRI1 expression in oil palm mesocarp further raises the possibility that distinct tissue-specific regulatory networks govern WRI1 activity in other oil-accumulating plant tissues (Yeap et al. 2017). Comparative analyses of WRI1 promoters across diverse plant species may therefore provide valuable insights into the evolution and diversification of WRI1 regulatory circuits. Another largely unexplored area concerns the potential involvement of epigenetic regulation in controlling WRI1 expression and activity. Epigenetic mechanisms, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and microRNA-mediated regulation, play fundamental roles in coordinating plant development and metabolism (Yamamuro et al. 2016; Lu et al. 2023; Nie et al. 2025). However, whether these processes contribute to WRI1 regulation remains largely unknown. Elucidating the epigenetic landscape associated with WRI1 expression might uncover additional layers of regulatory complexity mediating carbon partitioning and oil biosynthesis.

Recent studies suggest that the activities of WRI1-interacting partners are extensively coordinated through post-translational modifications, highlighting the highly collaborative nature of the WRI1 transcriptional apparatus. Phosphorylation has emerged as a central mechanism modulating WRI1 stability and activity, though its dual roles of phosphorylation in modulating the activity of WRI1 have been proposed (Ma et al. 2016; Kong and Ma, 2018). Future studies will be required to elucidate how diverse kinases and phosphatases fine tune WRI1 function through reversible phosphorylation in response to developmental and environmental signals. Recent discoveries involving WRI1 SUMOylation and deSUMOylation have further expanded our understanding of post-translational control of WRI1 (Huang et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2026). These findings raise important questions regarding how plants dynamically balance WRI1 stability and activity under fluctuating environmental conditions, particularly through coordinated interactions among phosphorylation, SUMOylation, and ubiquitination pathways. In eukaryotic systems, crosstalk among these post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation, SUMOylation, and ubiquitination frequently act as a molecular switch that regulates protein stability, subcellular localization, transcriptional activity, and protein–protein interactions during various cellular processes. However, how these interconnected regulatory mechanisms collectively modulate WRI1 function remains largely unexplored and represents an important direction for future research. Additionally, recent efforts to establish the WRI1 interactome have accelerated the discovery of previously unrecognized WRI1-associated regulators, providing new opportunities to dissect the broader regulatory networks controlling carbon allocation and oil biosynthesis. Continued characterization of these interaction networks will likely reveal additional layers of molecular mechanism underlying WRI1-mediated gene regulation.

Another important research area in WRI1 is the identification and functional characterization of WRI1 target genes beyond those directly involved in de novo fatty acid biosynthesis. Although the AW-box motif has been well established as the primary cis-element recognized by WRI1 (Maeo et al. 2009), emerging evidence suggests that WRI1 regulates additional transcriptional network extending into plant growth, hormone homeostasis, and nutrient responsive programming. For instance, AtWRI1 can bind to the promoter of GH3.3, a gene involved in auxin conjugation and homeostasis, through a non-AW-box cis-element (Kong et al. 2017). In addition, AtWRI1 recognizes AW-box motifs within the promoter regions of auxin transporter PIN genes and a potential role of WRI1 in modulating auxin homeostasis is hence proposed (Kong et al. 2017). Consistent with this hypothesis, overexpression of GmWRI1 in soybean elevates the expression of auxin-associated genes, whereas GmWRI1 knockdown suppresses their expression (Chen et al. 2020). These findings suggest that WRI1 may function beyond lipid metabolism to coordinate broader developmental and hormonal programs. Recent studies revealed that WRI1-mediated regulation of non-lipid targets may involve cooperative interactions with additional transcription factors. Intriguingly, AtWRI1 alone neither activates nor represses GH3.3 expression directly (Kong et al. 2022). Instead, yeast-two-hybrid screening identified TCP20 as a WRI1-interacting partner that binds to the GH3.3 promoter and regulates its expression. Physical interaction between AtWRI1 and TCP20 attenuates GH3.3 expression, revealing a previously unrecognized AtWRI1-TCP20 regulatory module involved in fine-tuning auxin homeostasis (Kong et al. 2022). These findings raise the possibility that WRI1 may participate in diverse transcriptional complexes that regulate developmental and metabolic pathways beyond fatty acid biosynthesis. The functional diversification of WRI1 became even more apparent in a recent study in rice demonstrating that OsWRI1a coordinates systemic growth responses to nitrogen availability (Shen et al. 2026). In shoots, OsWRI1a directly activates nitrogen-mediated tiller growth response 5 (NGR5) expression to promote tillering, whereas in roots it regulates multiple nitrogen metabolism-related genes to improve nitrogen-use efficiency. Remarkably, this work revealed that OsWRI1a contributes to balancing root-to-shoot biomass allocation under varying nitrogen conditions, thereby integrating nutrient signaling with plant growth and productivity (Shen et al. 2026). These findings substantially expand the biological scope of WRI1 function. Although WRI1 has long been considered as one of the most powerful and effective transcriptional regulators for the genetic engineering of vegetable oil production, these recent discoveries indicate that its functions extend far beyond oil biosynthesis alone. Future studies could aim to identify noncanonical WRI1 targets, interacting transcriptional regulators, and alternative cis-elements that will likely uncover additional roles for WRI1 in coordinating lipid metabolism, development, hormone signaling, and environmental adaptation.

Another important area that remains insufficiently explored is the functional diversification of the WRI1 gene family. In addition to AtWRI1, the closely related genes AtWRI3 and AtWRI4 can complement the low-oil phenotype of the wri1 knockout mutant, indicating partial functional redundancy among WRI1-like family members (To et al. 2012). However, unlike AtWRI1, AtWRI3 and AtWRI4 are preferentially expressed in nonseed tissues such as stems and flowers, suggesting that these proteins have evolved specialized functions outside seed oil accumulation. Indeed, both WRI3 and WRI4 participate in supplying acyl precursors for cutin biosynthesis (To et al. 2012), while WRI4 has additionally emerged as an important regulator of cuticular wax biosynthesis in Arabidopsis stems (Park et al. 2016). These findings highlight the broader roles of WRI1-like proteins in regulating lipid-derived protective barriers for environmental adaptation. Interestingly, the wri1 wri3 wri4 triple mutant does not display severe developmental defects, and fatty acid biosynthetic genes remain expressed at basal levels in vegetative tissues (To et al. 2012; Marchive et al. 2014). This observation raises an intriguing possibility that additional transcriptional regulators control the expression of fatty acid biosynthetic genes in vegetative tissues. Recent studies suggest that WRI-like proteins might have undergone substantial functional diversification during evolution, contributing to tissue-specific fatty acid metabolism and specialized metabolite production in Solanaceae species (He et al. 2026). Future investigations aimed at dissecting the distinct and overlapping functions of WRI1-like family members across diverse plant species and tissues will provide important insights into how plants spatially and developmentally modify carbon partitioning, surface lipid production, and storage lipid biosynthesis.

Nearly 3 decades after Focks and Benning first described the wri1 mutant in Plant Physiology, the story of WRI1 remains far from complete. What initially appeared to be a seed oil- deficiency phenotype has become a foundational example of how visible developmental traits can reveal hidden principles of metabolic regulation. The legacy of the original 1998 study is therefore broader than the discovery of 1 transcription factor. It reshaped plant lipid biology by showing that seed oil accumulation is a regulated carbon-partitioning program, and it continues to inspire new approaches for understanding and engineering plant metabolism.
