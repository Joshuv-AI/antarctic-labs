Bernstein-type theorem for stationary hypersurfaces of the Euler-Dierkes-Huisken functional
Hongbin Cui Jiahuan Li Xiaowei Xu

Abstract
. We say that a hypersurface Σ ⊂ ℝ n + 1 \Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1} is α \alpha -stationary if it is a critical point of the Euler-Dierkes-Huisken functional ℰ α ​ ( Σ ) = ∫ Σ | X | α ​ d ​ ℋ n \mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\Sigma)=\int_{\Sigma}|X|^{\alpha}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n} , introduced by Dierkes and Huisken in [ 15 ] . In this paper, we prove that every smooth, complete, connected, embedded α \alpha -stationary hypersurface in ℝ n + 1 \mathbb{R}^{n+1} passing through the origin with α > 0 \alpha>0 is a linear hyperplane.



1.
Introduction

In 1744, L. Euler considered the least moment of inertia for planar curves with fixed endpoints and introduced the functional

\mathcal{E}_{2}(X(t))=\int_{a}^{b}|X^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\,ds,

where
X(t)=(x_{1}(t),x_{2}(t))\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
t\in[a,b]
, and
ds=|X^{\prime}(t)|dt
. M. Mason (
[
26
]
), L. Tonelli (
[
34
]
), C. Carathedory (
[
6
]
) studied regular extremals of a more general functional

\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(X(t))=\int_{a}^{b}|X^{\prime}(t)|^{\alpha}\,ds,

where
\alpha>0
. Recently, U. Dierkes and G. Huisken (
[
15
]
) considered the
n
-dimensional analogue functional

(1.1)

\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\Sigma):=\int_{\Sigma}|X|^{\alpha}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}

for hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, where
X
is the position of a point in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\mathcal{H}^{n}
is the
n
-diemnsional Hausdorff measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. We call (
1.1
) the
Euler Dierkes-Huisken functional
(EDH-functional) in the sequel. The Euler-Lagrange equation of EDH-functional is

(1.2)

H=\alpha\,|X|^{-2}\,\langle X,\nu\rangle,

where
H
is the mean curvature of
\Sigma
, and
\nu
is the unit normal vector field of
\Sigma
.
A hypersurface is said to be
\alpha
-stationary
or
EDH-stationary
if it satisfies (
1.2
) in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}
. We say that an
\alpha
-stationary hypersurface
\Sigma

passes through the origin

0\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
if
0\in\Sigma
and
\Sigma\setminus\{0\}
satisfies (
1.2
). In particular, if
\Sigma=\{(x,u(x))\}
is a graph of a smooth function
u
over

\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then (
1.2
) becomes

(1.3)

\mbox{div}\big(\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}}\big)=\alpha\,\frac{u-\langle x,\nabla u\rangle}{(|x|^{2}+u^{2})\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}},

where
\nabla u
denotes the gradient of
u
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Note that if
\alpha=0
, the EDH-functional reduces to the area functional, and (
1.3
) is precisely the minimal surface equation.

After S. Bernstein (
[
3
]
) proved that a minimal graph over
\mathbb{R}^{2}
must be a plane, it is natural to ask whether a minimal graph over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is necessarily a hyperplane for
n\geq 3
. This question became known as the
Bernstein problem
. The work of Fleming (
[
19
]
), De Giorgi (
[
12
]
), Almgren (
[
1
]
), and J. Simons (
[
33
]
) showed that the Bernstein problem holds for
n\leq 7
. However, E. Bombieri, E. De Giorgi, and E. Giusti (
[
5
]
) constructed counterexamples for
n\geq 8
.

It is known that a minimal graph is a particular class of stable minimal hypersurface. Naturally, this leads to the
stable Bernstein problem
: must a complete, stable minimal hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a hyperplane? Significant progress has been made on this problem. Do Carmo and C.K. Peng (
[
17
]
), D. Fischer-Colbrie and R. Schoen (
[
20
]
), and A.V. Porogelov (
[

]
) gave a positive answer for
n=2
. R. Schoen, L. Simon and S.T. Yau (
[
31
]
), R. Schoen and L.Simon (
[
32
]
) studied the problem under the Euclidean volume assumptions for
n\leq 6
. C. Bellettini (
[
4
]
) developed their approach via De Giorgi’s iteration. Recently, O. Chodosh and Ch. Li
[
7
]
gave a positive answer for
n=3
. Shortly after, two alternative proofs were provided independently by G. Catino, P. Mastrolia and A. Roncoroni
[
9
]
, and by O. Chodosh and Ch. Li (
[
8
]
). Subsequently, O. Chodosh, Ch. Li, P. Minter and D. Stryker (
[
10
]
) applied the strategy in
[
8
]
to solve the problem for
n=4
. L. Mazet (
[
28
]
) settled the case
n=5
. The problem remains open for
n=6
.

The Bernstein problem for hypersurfaces with respect to weighted area functionals has also drawn considerable attention in geometry and analysis. U. Dierkes (
[
13
]
,
[
14

) established Bernstein-type theorems for stable graphs and area-minimizing hypersurfaces associated with the weighted area functional, where the weight is a power of the last coordinate. L. Wang (
[

.

In
[

[

\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
with

\alpha

]
,
[
24
]
, and
[

. In this paper, we prove the following Bernstein-type theorem for
\alpha
-stationary hypersurfaces.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a smooth, complete, connected, embedded
\alpha
-stationary hypersurface passing through the origin with
\alpha>0
, then
\Sigma
is a linear hyperplane.

Remark
. Since any hyperplane that is
\alpha
-stationary must contain the origin, the assumption that
\Sigma
passes through the origin is natural.
The smoothness assumption on
\Sigma
at the origin is essential. The smoothness assumption of
\Sigma
at the origin is essential. Indeed, the graph
\{(x,m|x|)\,|\,x\in\mathbb{R}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is
\alpha
-stationary for
m\neq 0
and
\alpha>0
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, but it is not smooth at the origin. Furthermore, the Simon’s cones
\{(x,y)\,|\,x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell+1},\;|x|=|y|\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\ell+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell+1}
provide examples of
\alpha
-stationary (
[
15
]
) hypersurfaces for any
\alpha>0
, although they are singular at the origin. It is worth noting that neither the stability nor the area-minimizing property is required in the proof of our Bernstein-type theorem.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we show that the Taylor coefficients of an
\alpha
-stationary graph defined by a smooth function
u
vanishes at the origin. Section 3 is dedicated to establishing the local flatness of such graphs. Finally, in Section 4, we complete the proof of the main theorem.

2.
Local estimates for stationary graph

In this section, we prove that a smooth function
u
whose graph is
\alpha
-stationary has vanishing Taylor coefficients at the origin, i.e., all its partial derivatives of every order​ vanish at 0.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
u\in C^{\infty}(B_{r_{0}})
satisfies
u(0)=0
and
\nabla u(0)=0
. If
u
satisfies the equation
(
1.3
)
with
\alpha>0
on
B_{r_{0}}\setminus\{0\}
, then
u
has vanishing Taylor coefficients at the origin. Moreover, for a fixed multi-index
\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we have

|\nabla^{\beta}u|\leq C_{\beta,m}\,|x|^{m},

for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, where
C_{\beta,m}>0
is a constant depends only on
\beta
and
m
.

Proof
. We rewrite equation (
1.3
) as

(2.1)

\big(\delta_{ij}-\frac{u_{i}u_{j}}{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}\big)u_{ij}=\alpha\,\frac{u-\langle x,\nabla u\rangle}{|x|^{2}+u^{2}}

in
B_{r_{0}}\setminus\{0\}
. We will prove that equation (
2.1
) leads to a contradiction if
u
has a finite order term in its Taylor expansion at the origin.

Since
u(0)=0
and
\nabla u(0)=0
, the Taylor expansion of
u
at the origin takes the form

(2.2)

u(x)=P_{k}(x)+o(|x|^{k}),

where
k\geq 2
and
P_{k}(x)
is a non-trivial homogeneous polynomial of degree
k
.
This implies

(2.3)

\nabla u(x)=\nabla P_{k}(x)+o(|x|^{k-1}),\hskip 14.22636pt\nabla^{2}u(x)=\nabla^{2}P_{k}(x)+o(|x|^{k-2}).

Since
k\geq 2
, it follows from (
2.3
) that

\frac{u_{i}u_{j}}{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}\,u_{ij}=o(|x|^{k-2}).

Thus, the left hand side of (
2.1
) is equal to

(2.4)

\Delta P_{k}(x)+o(|x|^{k-2}).

For the right hand side, Euler’s identity for homogeneous polynomials gives

\langle x,\nabla P_{k}(x)\rangle=kP_{k}(x),

and hence

u-\langle x,\nabla u\rangle=(1-k)P_{k}(x)+o(|x|^{k}).

Using
k\geq 2
once more, we obtian

\frac{1}{|x|^{2}+u^{2}}=|x|^{-2}+o(|x|^{-2}).

Thus the right hand side of (
2.1
) is equal to

(2.5)

\alpha(1-k)\frac{P_{k}(x)}{|x|^{2}}+o(|x|^{k-2}).

Substituting (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) into (
2.1
), and comparing the homogeneous terms of degree
k-2
, we obtain

(2.6)

|x|^{2}\Delta P_{k}(x)=\alpha(1-k)P_{k}(x).

We first treat the case
n=1
. Writing
P_{k}(x)=cx^{k}
with
c\neq 0
and
k\geq 2
, we have
|x|^{2}\Delta P_{k}(x)=k(k-1)P_{k}(x)
. Then (
2.6
) gives
k(k-1)=\alpha(1-k)
, which contradicts
k\geq 2
and
\alpha>0
.

We next treat the case
n\geq 2
. By the standard harmonic decomposition of homogeneous polynomials (see Chapter 5 in
[
2
]
),
P_{k}(x)
can be written uniquely as

(2.7)

P_{k}(x)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{[k/2]}|x|^{2i}H_{k-2i}(x),

where
H_{k-2i}(x)
is a homogeneous harmonic polynomial of degree
k-2i
. Since
\Delta H_{k-2i}=0
and

\langle x,\nabla H_{k-2i}(x)\rangle=(k-2i)H_{k-2i}
, we compute

\Delta(|x|^{2i}H_{k-2i})=2i(2i+n-2)|x|^{2i-2}H_{k-2i}+4i(k-2i)|x|^{2i-2}H_{k-2i},

which yields

(2.8)

|x|^{2}\Delta(|x|^{2i}H_{k-2i})=2i(2k-2i+n-2)|x|^{2i}H_{k-2i},

for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,[k/2]\}
. Substituting (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) into (
2.6
), and using uniqueness of harmonic decomposition, we find that the coefficients must satisfy

2i(2k-2i+n-2)=\alpha(1-k)

for some
i\in\{0,\ldots,[k/2]\}
, which contradicts
\alpha>0
and
k\geq 2
. Thus no such solution exists, and the theorem follows from the Taylor expansion of a smooth function.

\Box

Remark
. Theorem
2.1
does not hold​ for a​ minimal graph, i.e., the case
\alpha=0
. However, it holds for some other
\alpha
, such as negative irrationals.
On the other hand, from Theorem
2.1
, the coefficients
a^{ij}(x)

in (
2.1
) can be continuously extended​ to the origin by setting​
a^{ij}(0)=\delta_{ij}
, for smooth functions have vanishing Taylor coefficients.

3.
Local flatness for stationary graph

In this section, we prove the local flatness of smooth
\alpha
-stationary graphs through the origin, using the strong unique continuation result of Ch.L. Lin, G. Nakamura and J.N. Wang in
[
23
]
.

We rewrite (
2.1
) as

(3.1)

a^{ij}(x)u_{ij}+b^{i}(x)u_{i}+c(x)u=0,

where

b^{ij}(x):=\frac{u_{i}u_{j}}{1+|\nabla u|^{2}},\hskip 8.5359pta^{ij}(x):=\delta_{ij}-b^{ij}(x),\hskip 8.5359ptb^{i}(x):=\frac{\alpha x_{i}}{|x|^{2}+u^{2}},\hskip 8.5359ptc(x):=-\frac{\alpha}{|x|^{2}+u^{2}}.

To apply the theory in
[
23
]
, we define the transformation as

(3.2)

w(x):=|x|^{s}u(x),

where
s=\alpha/2
. Clearly,
w(x)
also has vanishing Taylor coefficients at the origin if
u
does.
Away from the origin, a direct computation shows that

(3.3)

u_{i}=|x|^{-s}w_{i}-s|x|^{-s-2}x_{i}w,

and

(3.4)

u_{ij}=|x|^{-s}w_{ij}-s|x|^{-s-2}(x_{i}w_{j}+x_{j}w_{i}+\delta_{ij}w)+s(s+2)|x|^{-s-4}x_{i}x_{j}w.

Substituting (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) into (
3.1
), and multiplying by
|x|^{s}
on both sides, we obtain

(3.5)

a^{ij}(x)w_{ij}+\tilde{b}^{i}(x)w_{i}+\tilde{c}(x)w=0,

where

(3.6)

\tilde{b}^{i}(x)=b^{i}(x)-2s|x|^{-2}a^{ij}(x)x_{j},

and

(3.7)

\tilde{c}=-s|x|^{-2}a^{ij}(x)\delta_{ij}+s(s+2)|x|^{-4}a^{ij}(x)x_{i}x_{j}-s|x|^{-2}b^{i}(x)x_{i}+c(x).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
P(x,D)=a^{ij}(x)\,\partial_{ij}
be the second-order differential operator in
(
3.1
)
. For any smooth
function
u(x)
with vanishing Taylor coefficients at the origin that satisfies
(
3.1
)
, then
w(x):=|x|^{s}u(x)
satisfies

|P(x,D)w|\leq C_{1}\,|x|^{-2}|w|+C_{2}\,|x|^{-1}|\nabla w|,

where
C_{2}\rightarrow 0
as
|x|\rightarrow 0
.

Proof
. Substituting
a^{ij}(x)=\delta_{ij}-b^{ij}(x)
into (
3.6
) and (
3.7
), using the definition of
b^{i}(x)
and
c(x)
, we obtain

(3.8)

\displaystyle\tilde{b}^{i}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2b^{ij}x_{j}}{|x|^{2}}-\frac{\alpha u^{2}x_{i}}{|x|^{2}(|x|^{2}+u^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Big(2|B|+\frac{\alpha u^{2}}{|x|^{2}+u^{2}}\Big)\,\frac{1}{|x|},

where
|B|
is the norm of matrix
B:=(b^{ij}(x))_{n\times n}
, and

(3.9)

\displaystyle\tilde{c}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{s(s-n+2)}{|x|^{2}}-\frac{2s(s+1)}{|x|^{2}+u^{2}}+\frac{sb^{ii}(x)}{|x|^{2}}-\frac{s(s+2)b^{ij}(x)x_{i}x_{j}}{|x|^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C(s)}{|x|^{2}}+\Big(s|\operatorname{tr}(B)|+\frac{s(s+2)|b^{ij}(x)x_{i}x_{j}|}{|x|^{2}}\Big)\,\frac{1}{|x|^{2}},

where
C(s)=s(n+3s+4)
depends only on
\alpha
. Since
u(x)
has vanishing Taylor coefficients at the origin, (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) imply

(3.10)

|\tilde{b}^{i}(x)|\leq\epsilon(|x|)\,|x|^{-1},\hskip 8.5359pt|\tilde{c}(x)|\leq(C(s)+\epsilon(|x|))|x|^{-2},

where
\epsilon(|x|)\rightarrow 0
as
|x|\rightarrow 0
. The assertion now follows from (
3.5
)and (
3.10
).

\Box

To obtain local flatness, we appeal to Theorem 1.2 from
[
23
]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

(
[
23
]
)

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a connected open set containing
0
, where
n\geq 2
. Let
P(x,D)=a^{ij}(x)\partial_{ij}
be an elliptic differential operator on
\Omega
with
a^{ij}(0)
real symmetric matrix and
a^{ij}(x)
Lipschitz on
\Omega
. If
w(x)\in H^{1}_{loc}(\Omega)
is a nontrivial solution satisfying

|P(x,D)w|\leq C_{1}\,|x|^{-2}|w|+C_{2}\,|x|^{-1}|\nabla w|

with
C_{2}
is sufficiently small, then there exist constants
C_{0}>0
, depending only on
n
and
w(x)
, and a constant
m_{0}
depending only on
P(x,D)

, such that

\int_{|x|<r}|w(x)|^{2}dx\geq C_{0}r^{m_{0}},

for sufficiently small

.

Proof
. A detailed proof can be found in Section 3 of
[

.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
u\in C^{\infty}(B_{r})
satisfies
u(0)=0
and
\nabla u(0)=0
. If
u
solves
(
1.3
)
with
\alpha>0
in
B_{r}\setminus\{0\}
, then
u\equiv 0
on
B_{r_{0}}
for sufficiently small
r_{0}
.

Proof
. We first treat the case
n=1
. Set
u(x):=xw(x)
for
x>0
. Clearly,
u(x)
has vanishing Taylor coefficients at the origin, and so do
w(x)
and
w^{\prime}(x)
. Then the equation (
2.1
) becomes

(3.11)

xw^{\prime\prime}(x)+a(x,w,w^{\prime})w^{\prime}(x)=0,

where
a(x,w,w^{\prime})=2+\alpha[1+(w+xw^{\prime})^{2}]/(1+w^{2})>0
is smooth for
x>0
. Equation (
3.11
) implies

(3.12)

w^{\prime}(x)=w^{\prime}(x_{0})e^{-\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\frac{a}{t}\,dt},

for any
x_{0}>0
.
Taking the limit
x\rightarrow 0^{+}
in (
3.12
) yields
w^{\prime}(0)=w^{\prime}(x_{0})e^{\int_{0}^{x_{0}}\frac{a}{t}\,dt}
, which contradicts
w^{\prime}(0)=0
unless
w^{\prime}(x_{0})=0
. Since
x_{0}>0
is arbitrary and
w^{\prime}(0)=0
, it follows that
w^{\prime}(x)\equiv 0
. Consequently,
w(x)\equiv 0
in light of
w(0)=0
, and thus
u(x)\equiv 0
.

We next treat the case
n\geq 2
. It follows from Theorem
2.1
that
u(x)
has vanishing Taylor coefficients at the origin. Clearly,
w(x)=|x|^{s}u(x)
with
s=\alpha/2
shares the same property. That is, there is a constant
C_{m}>0
such that

(3.13)

|w(x)|^{2}\leq C_{m}\,|x|^{m},

for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
We now extend the coefficients
a^{ij}(x)
to
0
by setting
a^{ij}(0)=\delta_{ij}
. The extended coefficients remain Lipschitz in a small ball. Then, Lemma
3.1
ensures​ that
w(x)
satifies the hypotheses of Theorem
3.2
. We claim that
u\equiv 0
in sufficiently small ball. Suppose, to the contrary, that
w(x)
is not identically zero. Then Theorem
3.2
yields

\int_{B_{r}}|w(x)|^{2}dx\geq C_{0}|x|^{m_{0}},

for all sufficiently small
r>0
, which contradicts inequality (
3.13
). Thus,
u(x)\equiv 0
in a sufficiently small neighborhood of the origin.

\Box

4.
Proof of the main theorem

We complete the proof of the main theorem in this section.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a smooth, connected, embedded, complete
\alpha
-stationary hypersurface passing through the origin with
\alpha>0
, then
\Sigma
is a linear hyperplane.

Proof
. Since
\Sigma
is embedded and
0\in\Sigma
, there exists a neighborhood of
0
in
\Sigma
that can be written as a graph over
T_{0}\Sigma
. After rotating
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
if necessary, we identify
T_{0}\Sigma
with
\mathbb{R}^{n}=\{(x,0)\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Consequently,
\Sigma
can be represented as the graph of a smooth function
u(x)
in
B_{r}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
u(0)=0
and
\nabla u(0)=0
, where
u(x)
solves (
2.1
) in
B_{r}(0)\setminus\{0\}
. By Theorem
2.1
and Theorem
3.3
, we conclude that
u(x)\equiv 0
in some
B_{r_{0}}(0)
. Hence,
\Sigma
coincides with the hyperplane
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in a neighborhood of
0
.

Define the set

\mathcal{O}:=\{p\in\Sigma\,|\,\mbox{there exists a neighborhood}\;\mathcal{O}_{p}\subset\Sigma\;\mbox{of}\;p\;\mbox{such that}\;\mathcal{O}_{p}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\}.

Clearly,
\mathcal{O}
is non-empty and open in
\Sigma
. We claim that
\mathcal{O}
is also closed in
\Sigma
. Let
\{p_{i}\}\subset\mathcal{O}
be a sequence coverging to
p\in\Sigma\setminus\{0\}
. Since
p_{i}\rightarrow p
and
p_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it follows that
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover, by the smoothness of
\Sigma
, we have
T_{p}\Sigma=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus, near
p
,
\Sigma

can be represented as the graph of a smooth function
w(x)
defined on
B_{r}(p)
for some
r>0
, which solves (
2.1
).
Since
p_{i}\in\mathcal{O}
, there exists a neighborhood
\mathcal{O}_{p_{i}}
where
w(x)\equiv 0
. As
p_{i}\to p
, this implies that
w(x)
vanishes to infinite order at
p
, i.e., all derivatives of
w(x)
vanish at
p
. Considering the translated function
\tilde{w}(y):=w(y+p)
in
B_{r}(0)
, and applying Theorem
3.3
again, we conclude that
\tilde{w}(y)\equiv 0
in some
B_{r_{0}}(0)
. Hence,
w(x)\equiv 0
in
B_{r_{0}}(p)
, which means
p\in\mathcal{O}
.
An alternative proof follows from the strong unique continuation property for second-order elliptic PDEs (Theorem 1.1 in
[
21
]
by N. Garofalo and F.H. Lin).

Since
\mathcal{O}
is both open and closed in the connected hypersurface
\Sigma
, we have
\Sigma=\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover,
\Sigma
is complete, the Hopf-Rinow theorem implies

\Sigma=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

\Box
