Differential predictive value of inflammation- and coagulation-based composite biomarkers for venous thromboembolism and mortality in patients with NSCLC receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors
Authors: Jianying Liu, Lumin Wang, Xiaotong Xia, Shu Chen, Dawei Yang, Xuefeng Lu, Yijiao Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVenous thromboembolism (VTE) is a clinically important complication in patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Although inflammation and coagulation abnormalities may contribute to both thrombosis and poor prognosis, it remains unclear whether routinely available composite biomarkers have similar or differential predictive value for VTE occurrence and all-cause mortality in this setting.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort included 206 patients with advanced NSCLC who received at least two cycles of ICI therapy between January 2020 and December 2024. Baseline clinical, treatment, laboratory, and conventional thrombosis risk score data were collected. The D-dimer-to-albumin ratio (DAR), platelet-to-hemoglobin ratio (PHR), and fibrinogen-to-prealbumin ratio (FPR) were analyzed as log2-transformed continuous variables. VTE was evaluated using cumulative incidence functions and Fine–Gray competing-risk models with death without prior VTE as a competing event. All-cause mortality was assessed using time-dependent Cox models, with incident VTE modeled as a time-dependent covariate. Exploratory transition-specific Cox models evaluated baseline-to-VTE, baseline-to-death without prior VTE, and VTE-to-death pathways. E-values and proportional hazards diagnostics were used for sensitivity assessment and model checking.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 23.0 months, 29 patients (14.1%) developed VTE, 101 died without prior VTE, and 76 were censored. The median time to VTE was 5.0 months, and the 12-, 24-, and 36-month cumulative incidences were 9.2%, 13.7%, and 14.4%, respectively. Neither the Padua nor Khorana score effectively discriminated VTE risk. In Fine–Gray models, DAR was associated with VTE risk after full adjustment, with a subdistribution hazard ratio of 1.28 per doubling (95% confidence interval, 1.00–1.64; P = 0.048), whereas PHR and FPR were not. In time-dependent Cox models, PHR and FPR remained associated with all-cause mortality, whereas DAR and incident VTE did not. Transition-specific analyses suggested that DAR was more closely related to VTE occurrence, whereas PHR and FPR were more closely related to death without prior VTE.ConclusionsIn patients with advanced NSCLC receiving ICIs, DAR, PHR, and FPR showed differential predictive patterns for VTE and all-cause mortality. Background

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have become a cornerstone of systemic therapy for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), substantially improving survival outcomes in a subset of patients [1]. With the increasing use of ICIs, the identification and risk management of treatment-related complications have become important issues affecting long-term treatment continuity and prognosis. Among these complications, venous thromboembolism (VTE) is common and clinically important in patients with cancer. It can lead to additional hospitalization, increased risk of anticoagulation-related bleeding, and interruption or delay of anticancer therapy, and it often indicates a higher-risk prognostic state [2–4]. However, mortality in patients with NSCLC is not determined solely by thrombotic events, but rather by multiple factors, including tumor burden, treatment response, systemic inflammation, coagulation abnormalities, and nutritional depletion [5–8]. Therefore, in the context of ICI therapy, VTE occurrence and all-cause mortality are clinically related but represent two distinct and important outcomes.

VTE is not uncommon during ICI therapy. In NSCLC populations, Bjørnhart et al. [5] and Alma et al. [9] both reported that approximately 10% of patients developed VTE during ICI treatment. However, the Khorana score is mainly used in patients receiving conventional anticancer therapy, and several ICI cohorts have shown that its predictive performance is suboptimal in this setting [4, 9–12]. In recent years, several laboratory-derived composite ratios have attracted increasing attention, including the D-dimer-to-albumin ratio (DAR), fibrinogen-to-prealbumin ratio (FPR), and platelet-to-hemoglobin ratio (PHR) [13–15]. These markers integrate information on coagulation and fibrinolytic activation, systemic inflammation, nutritional depletion, anemia, and platelet activation, and have shown potential utility in the assessment of thrombotic events and cancer prognosis [14–17]. Inflammation and coagulation activation can promote endothelial injury, platelet activation, and thrombus formation. Meanwhile, persistent inflammation, coagulation-fibrinolysis dysregulation, and nutritional depletion often reflect tumor progression and deterioration of the host systemic condition [18–21]. Therefore, these composite biomarkers may carry information relevant to both VTE risk identification and mortality risk stratification. However, because these markers differ in composition and biological emphasis, their predictive value for VTE occurrence and all-cause mortality may also differ.

At present, whether DAR, FPR, and PHR have differential predictive value for VTE occurrence and all-cause mortality in patients with NSCLC receiving ICI therapy remains insufficiently evaluated. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the predictive performance of these composite biomarkers for the two key outcomes and to evaluate their performance relative to conventional risk scoring tools. By doing so, we sought to clarify their potential roles in thrombotic risk identification and mortality risk stratification, and to provide evidence for individualized risk assessment and clinical management in the setting of ICI therapy.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study collected data from patients with NSCLC who received ICIs at Zhongshan Hospital (Xiamen), Fudan University, between January 1, 2020 and December 31, 2024. The patient selection process is shown in Fig. 1. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) pathologically confirmed unresectable or postoperative recurrent NSCLC; (3) receipt of at least two cycles of ICI therapy; and (4) complete treatment and follow-up data.

Patient selection flow diagram

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) clinically unstable or end-stage major organ dysfunction before ICI initiation, including New York Heart Association class III to IV heart failure, recent acute coronary syndrome or severe arrhythmia requiring hospitalization, recent stroke with severe residual neurological deficits, decompensated liver disease or Child–Pugh class C cirrhosis, end-stage renal disease requiring dialysis or an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2, and other severe organ dysfunction requiring hospitalization, intensive care unit admission, or organ support therapy. Stable and well-controlled chronic comorbidities alone were not considered exclusion criteria. (2) Loss to follow-up or incomplete follow-up data.

A total of 206 patients were ultimately included in the analysis. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital (Xiamen), Fudan University, with the approval number B2024-007.

Data collection

Demographic and disease-related data were collected through the electronic medical record system, including sex, age, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, alcohol use, history of comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, renal disease, and cardiovascular disease, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS), tumor stage, histological subtype, ICI agent, and treatment regimen. Laboratory data were obtained from the hospital laboratory information system. Baseline laboratory results were defined as the most recent test results within 7 days before the initiation of the first ICI treatment. Laboratory tests performed after ICI initiation were not included in the baseline analysis. The collected laboratory parameters included complete blood count, liver function, renal function, fasting blood glucose, coagulation function, and fibrinolytic markers.

The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was calculated as the neutrophil count (× 10⁹/L) divided by the lymphocyte count (× 10⁹/L) [22]. DAR was calculated as the D-dimer level (mg/L) divided by the albumin level (g/L) [13]. FPR was calculated as the fibrinogen level (mg/L) divided by the prealbumin level (mg/L) [14]. PHR was calculated as the platelet count (× 10⁹/L) divided by the hemoglobin level (g/L) [15].

The risk of thrombosis was assessed using the Padua score and the Khorana score. The Padua score includes 11 clinical variables, including active cancer, reduced mobility, previous VTE, known thrombophilic condition, recent surgery or trauma, age ≥ 70 years, heart or respiratory failure, acute myocardial infarction or ischemic stroke, acute infection or rheumatologic disorder, BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2, and ongoing hormonal treatment [23]. A total score of ≥ 4 was defined as high risk. The Khorana score includes five clinical and laboratory variables: cancer site, platelet count ≥ 350 × 10⁹/L, hemoglobin < 100 g/L or use of erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, white blood cell count > 11 × 10⁹/L, and BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2 [12]. Very high-risk cancer sites are assigned 2 points, high-risk cancer sites are assigned 1 point, and each of the remaining variables is assigned 1 point. A total score of ≥ 3 was defined as high risk for thrombosis.

Assessment of thrombotic events

VTE events were retrospectively adjudicated based on objective imaging evidence during ICI treatment. VTE was not assessed through a prespecified systematic screening protocol, such as routine Doppler ultrasonography or pulmonary computed tomography (CT) angiography performed at fixed intervals for all patients. Instead, thrombotic events were identified in the context of routine clinical practice. Patients underwent further diagnostic assessment when thrombotic symptoms were suspected, when abnormal findings were observed during periodic clinical or laboratory follow-up, or when thrombotic events were incidentally detected on clinically indicated imaging examinations. For patients with suspected lower-extremity deep vein thrombosis (DVT), bilateral whole-leg venous color Doppler ultrasonography was performed to assess venous patency. For patients with suspected pulmonary embolism (PE), pulmonary CT angiography was performed as clinically indicated. For venous thrombosis suspected at other sites, contrast-enhanced CT or CT angiography was performed according to the clinical judgment of the treating physicians. All imaging findings were interpreted and confirmed by qualified radiologists. Accordingly, both symptomatic VTE events and asymptomatic VTE events detected during clinically indicated evaluations were included in this study.

Treatment regimens and study endpoints

All patients received at least two cycles of ICI therapy. The ICI agents used in this cohort included tislelizumab, camrelizumab, sintilimab, toripalimab, pembrolizumab, nivolumab, durvalumab, and atezolizumab. All ICIs were administered intravenously at a dose of 200 mg every 3 weeks. Treatment regimens included ICI monotherapy, ICI combined with platinum-based doublet chemotherapy, ICI combined with non-platinum single-agent chemotherapy, and ICI-based regimens combined with anti-angiogenic therapy or radiotherapy.

The primary endpoint was the occurrence of thrombotic events during ICI therapy, including imaging-confirmed DVT, PE, and thrombosis at other sites. The secondary endpoint was all-cause death during follow-up. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from initiation of the first ICI treatment to death from any cause.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation and compared using Student’s t tests, whereas non-normally distributed variables were summarized as median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared using Mann–Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate.

Variables with more than 20% missingness were excluded from multivariable analyses, except for prespecified key biomarkers when applicable. Missing covariate data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations, generating 20 imputed datasets. The imputation model included demographic characteristics, comorbidities, treatment-related variables, laboratory markers, biomarker variables, VTE status, survival status, and follow-up time. Survival times and outcome variables were not imputed. Estimates from imputed datasets were pooled according to Rubin’s rules. The distributions of observed and completed data were compared using density plots, and convergence was assessed using trace plots.

DAR, PHR and FPR were analyzed as log2-transformed continuous variables on their original ratio scale. Therefore, hazard ratios (HRs) and subdistribution hazard ratios (sHRs) for these biomarkers represent the relative change in risk associated with each doubling of the original biomarker value. Median-based dichotomization was used only for exploratory CIF visualization and not for primary regression analyses.

For VTE, death without prior VTE was treated as a competing event. Cumulative incidence functions were used to estimate VTE incidence over time, and Gray’s test was used for exploratory group comparisons. Fine–Gray competing-risk regression was used to estimate sHRs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for VTE. Given the limited number of VTE events, DAR, PHR, and FPR were evaluated in separate Fine–Gray models using a parsimonious sequential adjustment strategy. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 adjusted for age, ECOG performance status (ECOG-PS), tumor stage, and histological subtype. Model 3 additionally adjusted for anti-angiogenic therapy and antiplatelet therapy. Model 4 further adjusted for hypertension and diabetes mellitus. These covariates were selected according to clinical relevance, baseline imbalance, and potential treatment- or comorbidity-related confounding.

For all-cause mortality, incident VTE was incorporated as a time-dependent covariate using the counting-process formulation, with VTE_td switching from 0 to 1 at the time of VTE occurrence. Sequential time-dependent Cox regression models were constructed to evaluate the associations of incident VTE and the three composite biomarkers with mortality. Model 1 included incident VTE as a time-dependent covariate and log2-transformed DAR, PHR, and FPR. Model 2 additionally adjusted for age, ECOG-PS, tumor stage, and histological subtype. Model 3 further adjusted for anti-angiogenic therapy and antiplatelet therapy. Model 4 further adjusted for hypertension and diabetes mellitus.

Exploratory transition-specific Cox models were used to evaluate three clinical pathways: baseline to VTE, baseline to death without prior VTE, and VTE to death. Given the limited number of VTE and post-VTE death events, these analyses were considered hypothesis-generating and were adjusted parsimoniously for age, ECOG-PS, and tumor stage.

Robustness and model diagnostics were assessed as follows. Sequential adjustment models were used to examine whether the associations of VTE and the composite biomarkers were consistent across increasing levels of covariate adjustment. E-values were calculated for statistically significant main findings to assess the potential influence of unmeasured confounding. The E-value represents the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the exposure and the outcome, conditional on measured covariates, to fully explain away the observed association. Larger E-values indicate that stronger unmeasured confounding would be required to negate the observed association. The proportional hazards assumption for Cox models was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals, and diagnostic plots were generated for the main time-dependent Cox model. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.0), with two-tailed P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics and missing data

A total of 206 patients with NSCLC receiving ICIs were included in the study (Fig. 1). The median follow-up time was 23.0 (IQR, 14.0–33.75) months. During follow-up, 29 patients (14.1%) developed VTE, 101 died without prior VTE, and 76 were censored without VTE.

Missingness was generally low for variables included in the main analyses. Among the three composite biomarkers, FPR had the highest missing rate (8.3%), followed by DAR (2.4%), whereas PHR had no missing values. Other variables with missing data included prealbumin, blood glucose, D-dimer, fibrin degradation products, and coagulation parameters. Missing covariate data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations with 20 imputed datasets. Density plots showed broadly similar observed and completed distributions, and trace plots did not suggest major instability of the imputation process (Supplementary Fig. 1). Before imputation, the median values of DAR, PHR, and FPR were 0.014 (IQR, 0.008–0.029), 1.97 (IQR, 1.58–2.53), and 1939.13 (IQR, 1448.48–2686.96), respectively. Because these biomarkers were measured on different scales, they were analyzed as log2-transformed continuous variables, with effect estimates interpreted per doubling of the original biomarker value.

The distributions of conventional thrombosis risk scores were also evaluated. For the Khorana score, most patients were classified as intermediate risk because lung cancer itself contributes one point to the score: 198 patients (96.1%) were categorized as intermediate risk and 8 patients (3.9%) as high risk. For the Padua score, 148 patients (71.8%) were classified as low risk and 58 patients (28.2%) as high risk. The distribution of Khorana categories did not differ between patients with and without VTE (P > 0.999), and the Padua high-risk category was numerically more frequent in the VTE group but without statistical significance (37.9% vs. 26.6%, P = 0.298).

Compared with patients without VTE, those who developed VTE were older [66.0 (IQR, 63.0–72.0) vs. 63.0 (IQR, 56.0–69.0) years, P = 0.010] and had higher proportions of hypertension (37.9% vs. 16.4%, P = 0.014), diabetes mellitus (20.7% vs. 5.6%, P = 0.015), ECOG-PS ≥ 1 (79.3% vs. 38.4%, P < 0.001), stage IV disease (82.8% vs. 56.5%, P = 0.013), and anti-angiogenic therapy (58.6% vs. 36.2%, P = 0.037). Histological subtype distribution also differed between the two groups (P = 0.002). Baseline DAR was higher in the VTE group [0.03 (IQR, 0.01–0.07) vs. 0.01 (IQR, 0.01–0.03), P = 0.005], whereas PHR and FPR did not differ significantly between groups (Table 1).

Cumulative incidence of VTE and risk stratification

Among patients who developed VTE, the median time from ICI initiation to VTE occurrence was 5.0 months (IQR, 2.0–15.0 months). When death without prior VTE was treated as a competing event, the cumulative incidence of VTE was 9.2% at 12 months, 13.7% at 24 months, and 14.4% at 36 months. The overall VTE incidence curve suggested early clustering of events followed by a plateau, while the accumulation of competing deaths during mid-to-late follow-up may have limited the subsequent observation of VTE events (Fig. 2).

The solid vertical line indicates the median time to VTE, and dashed lines indicate the first and third quartiles. B Cumulative incidence function of VTE with death without prior VTE treated as a competing event. Shaded areas represent 95% confidence intervals

In exploratory analyses using median-based biomarker stratification, patients with higher DAR had a significantly greater cumulative incidence of VTE than those with lower DAR (Gray’s test P = 0.029). In contrast, no significant differences in VTE incidence were observed according to PHR (P = 0.866) or FPR (P = 0.277) strata. When conventional thrombosis risk scores were analyzed as categorical variables, neither Padua nor Khorana category significantly discriminated VTE risk (Gray’s test P = 0.194 and P = 0.930, respectively; Fig. 3). As noted above, median-based biomarker stratification was used only for exploratory visualization, whereas the primary regression analyses modeled DAR, PHR, and FPR as continuous log2-transformed variables.

Fine–Gray competing-risk regression for VTE

In Fine–Gray competing-risk models treating death without prior VTE as the competing event, DAR showed the most consistent association with VTE risk across sequential adjustment models. In the unadjusted model, each doubling of DAR was associated with a higher subdistribution hazard of VTE (sHR = 1.42, 95% CI 1.15–1.76, P = 0.001). This association was attenuated after adjustment for age, ECOG-PS, tumor stage, and histological subtype (sHR = 1.22, 95% CI 0.97–1.53, P = 0.091), but remained directionally consistent after further adjustment for anti-angiogenic therapy and antiplatelet therapy (sHR = 1.26, 95% CI 0.99–1.59, P = 0.057). After additional adjustment for hypertension and diabetes mellitus, DAR was significantly associated with VTE risk (sHR = 1.28, 95% CI 1.00–1.64, P = 0.048; Table 2).

These findings suggest that DAR, but not PHR or FPR, was more closely aligned with thrombotic risk in this ICI-treated NSCLC cohort, although the DAR association should be interpreted cautiously given its attenuation across adjustment models (Fig. 4).

Time-dependent Cox regression for all-cause mortality

To avoid immortal time bias, incident VTE was modeled as a time-dependent covariate in Cox regression analyses. In univariable analysis, incident VTE was associated with increased all-cause mortality risk (HR = 1.71, 95% CI 1.01–2.91, P = 0.046). However, this association was attenuated after adjustment for age, ECOG-PS, tumor stage, and histological subtype, and remained non-significant after additional adjustment for treatment-related and comorbidity variables. In the fully adjusted joint biomarker model, VTE was not independently associated with all-cause mortality (HR = 0.92, 95% CI 0.49–1.72, P = 0.787).

In univariable time-dependent Cox analyses, all three composite biomarkers were positively associated with mortality risk. Each doubling of DAR was associated with a higher risk of death (HR = 1.17, 95% CI 1.04–1.32, P = 0.012), with stronger associations observed for PHR (HR = 1.84, 95% CI 1.30–2.61, P < 0.001) and FPR (HR = 1.61, 95% CI 1.24–2.09, P < 0.001). In sequential multivariable models including incident VTE and all three biomarkers, PHR and FPR remained associated with all-cause mortality after full adjustment. Each doubling of PHR (HR = 1.63, 95% CI 1.09–2.45, P = 0.017) and FPR (HR = 1.35, 95% CI 1.01–1.80, P = 0.043) was associated with a higher mortality risk. In contrast, DAR was not associated with mortality in the fully adjusted model (HR = 1.07, 95% CI 0.92–1.23, P = 0.376).

Among the clinical covariates, ECOG-PS 0 remained associated with lower mortality risk (HR = 0.58, 95% CI 0.39–0.88, P = 0.011), whereas stage IV disease showed a borderline association with higher mortality risk (HR = 1.48, 95% CI 0.95–2.30, P = 0.082). Overall, these results suggest that PHR and FPR were more closely related to mortality risk, whereas incident VTE itself did not remain an independent predictor of all-cause mortality after adjustment (Table 3 and Fig. 5).

Exploratory transition-specific analysis

To further explore whether biomarker associations differed across clinical pathways, we constructed exploratory transition-specific Cox models for three transitions: baseline to VTE, baseline to death without prior VTE, and VTE to death (Supplementary Fig. 2). In the baseline-to-VTE transition, DAR was significantly associated with increased VTE risk (HR = 1.30, 95% CI 1.05–1.60, P = 0.014), whereas PHR and FPR were not associated with this transition (PHR: HR = 1.44, 95% CI 0.69–2.98, P = 0.328; FPR: HR = 1.11, 95% CI 0.66–1.85, P = 0.702).

In contrast, for the transition from baseline to death without prior VTE, PHR(HR = 2.15, 95% CI 1.45–3.18, P < 0.001) and FPR (HR = 1.72, 95% CI 1.29–2.29, P < 0.001) were strongly associated with mortality risk. DAR was not significantly associated with this transition (HR = 1.09, 95% CI 0.94–1.25, P = 0.248). None of the three biomarkers was significantly associated with death after VTE.

Overall, these exploratory findings suggest a pathway-specific pattern: DAR was more closely related to the thrombotic transition, whereas PHR and FPR were more closely related to death without prior VTE (Table 4). Given the limited number of VTE and post-VTE death events, these results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Sensitivity analyses and model diagnostics

E-value analyses were performed for statistically significant main findings to assess the potential influence of unmeasured confounding. For the association between DAR and VTE in the Fine–Gray model, the E-value was 1.88 for the point estimate and 1.05 for the confidence limit closest to the null. This suggests that the DAR–VTE association had only modest robustness to potential unmeasured confounding, particularly at the lower bound of the confidence interval. For all-cause mortality, the E-values for PHR and FPR were 2.65 and 2.04 for the point estimates, and 1.41 and 1.10 for the confidence limits closest to the null, respectively. These results suggest that the mortality association of PHR was relatively more robust, whereas the FPR association showed moderate robustness but remained sensitive at the confidence-limit level (Table 5).

Discussion

This study was based on a real-world cohort of patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). We evaluated the occurrence pattern of venous thromboembolism (VTE) and compared the predictive value of routinely available laboratory-derived composite biomarkers for VTE occurrence and all-cause mortality. The results showed that the incidence of VTE during ICI therapy was 14.08%, with a median time to occurrence of 5 months and a 12-month cumulative incidence of 9.2%, which was generally consistent with previous reports in ICI-treated populations [5, 9]. Notably, the association patterns of different composite biomarkers differed between the two key outcomes: the D-dimer-to-albumin ratio (DAR) was mainly associated with VTE risk, whereas the platelet-to-hemoglobin ratio (PHR) and fibrinogen-to-prealbumin ratio (FPR) were more closely related to all-cause mortality. In contrast, the Padua score and Khorana score failed to effectively distinguish VTE risk in this cohort. This may be related to the settings in which these scores were developed: the Khorana score is mainly used in patients receiving conventional anticancer therapy, whereas the Padua score was primarily derived from general medical inpatients and may not fully capture the immune-inflammatory and prothrombotic risk features in the context of ICIs [10, 24]. These findings suggest that, in the setting of ICI therapy, the applicability of conventional VTE risk scores may be limited, and the predictive value of different inflammation- and coagulation-based composite biomarkers for VTE and mortality outcomes may not be consistent.

The association between DAR and VTE occurrence may reflect its ability to capture a more proximal prothrombotic state. VTE depends on proximal prothrombotic processes, including activation of the coagulation system, fibrin formation and degradation, endothelial injury, and platelet activation [12, 19]. As a degradation product of cross-linked fibrin, D-dimer directly reflects ongoing coagulation formation and secondary fibrinolytic activation [25]. Previous studies have also shown that elevated D-dimer levels and dynamic increases in D-dimer may precede clinically apparent VTE events and are associated with subsequent VTE risk in patients with cancer [18, 26, 27]. Low albumin levels not only indicate systemic inflammation, nutritional depletion, and increased disease burden, but are also associated with an increased risk of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. Potential mechanisms may involve reduced plasma colloid osmotic pressure, increased blood viscosity, altered fibrin polymerization, enhanced platelet aggregation, and impaired vascular endothelial homeostasis [28–31]. In addition, in the inflammatory milieu induced by immunotherapy, inflammatory responses may promote coagulation activation and increase D-dimer levels, while also contributing to albumin decline through enhanced catabolism and increased capillary permeability [32, 33]. Therefore, DAR integrates signals related to coagulation-fibrinolysis activation and inflammation or nutritional depletion, and may better reflect the prothrombotic risk background.

By contrast, the relationship of PHR and FPR with VTE may be less direct than that of DAR. Compared with D-dimer, the platelet count in PHR and fibrinogen in FPR more often reflect a platelet-related procoagulant background, fibrin substrate availability, and acute-phase inflammatory responses, rather than established local thrombus formation or fibrin degradation [34, 35]. Platelets are also widely involved in tumor angiogenesis, tumor cell adhesion, and distant metastasis [36]. Reduced hemoglobin levels indicate anemia, hypoxia, chronic wasting, and reduced treatment tolerance, whereas decreased prealbumin reflects malnutrition, inflammatory consumption, and systemic frailty [37, 38]. Therefore, the signals captured by PHR and FPR may be more indicative of chronic inflammation, nutritional depletion, and deterioration of host status, and may better reflect overall disease burden and mortality risk [14, 39, 40]. Transition-specific analysis also supported this outcome-oriented difference: PHR and FPR were mainly associated with death before VTE occurrence, rather than death after VTE.

This finding also helps clarify the relationship between VTE and mortality. When VTE was incorporated into the Cox model as a time-dependent covariate, VTE was associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality in univariable analysis. However, after further adjustment for clinical factors, treatment-related factors, comorbidities, and biomarkers, this association was no longer significant, whereas Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) ≥ 1, PHR, and FPR remained associated with increased mortality risk. Similar to previous studies, VTE may be more appropriately viewed as a marker event of disease severity rather than an independent driver of mortality [41]. Therefore, clinical management should not be limited to the assumption that VTE prevention will necessarily translate into improved survival. Instead, while identifying VTE risk, clinicians should also pay attention to overall mortality risk and related disease background, including underlying disease status, systemic inflammation, and nutritional depletion, and should reasonably arrange anticoagulation strategies to reduce thrombosis-related hospitalization and treatment interruption [42, 43].

This study focused on patients with advanced NSCLC receiving ICIs. Most patients received ICIs combined with chemotherapy, and some patients also received anti-angiogenic therapy. The population characteristics and treatment patterns were generally consistent with the current clinical scenario of immunotherapy-based combination treatment. Therefore, the findings of this study may have certain reference value for VTE risk identification and prognostic stratification in patients with advanced NSCLC in clinical practice. However, this was a single-center study conducted in a Chinese population, and the proportion of male patients was high. Caution is still needed when extrapolating these findings to cohorts receiving ICI monotherapy, cohorts with a higher proportion of female patients, or populations with markedly different clinical characteristics.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively limited sample size and number of VTE events. To reduce the risk of model instability, this study adopted a sequential adjustment strategy and used E-values to assess the sensitivity of the main positive findings to unmeasured confounding. Nevertheless, the findings still need to be validated in larger cohorts. Second, this study was mainly based on single baseline measurements and therefore could not reflect the dynamic evolution of inflammatory and coagulation phenotypes during ICI therapy. Third, this study did not perform systematic VTE screening at fixed intervals, and some asymptomatic VTE events may not have been captured. Fourth, this study did not systematically collect data on immune-related adverse events (irAEs) during immunotherapy. irAEs are usually associated with immune activation and systemic inflammation and may contribute to VTE risk by affecting endothelial function, coagulation activation, and platelet responses [44, 45]. Therefore, this study could not compare the cumulative incidence of VTE between patients with and without irAEs. Finally, although the association between DAR and VTE has certain biological plausibility, its clinical utility still requires further validation in external independent cohorts. Future studies should validate these findings in larger, multicenter, prospective cohorts and should integrate dynamic inflammatory, coagulation, and nutritional markers as well as irAE-related information to further clarify the temporal relationship among immune activation, inflammatory response, nutritional depletion, and coagulation imbalance during ICI therapy, as well as the differential predictive value of these changes for VTE occurrence and all-cause mortality. This may provide evidence for risk stratification and individualized management in the setting of ICI therapy.

Conclusion

Based on a real-world cohort of patients with advanced NSCLC receiving ICIs, this study compared the predictive value of DAR, PHR, and FPR for VTE occurrence and all-cause mortality. The results showed that the incidence of VTE was 14.08%, with events mainly clustered in the early treatment period. DAR was associated with VTE risk, whereas the Padua score and Khorana score failed to effectively distinguish VTE risk. By contrast, PHR and FPR were mainly associated with all-cause mortality risk, and VTE did not show a stable independent predictive effect for mortality after full adjustment. These findings suggest that inflammation- and coagulation-based composite biomarkers may have differential predictive value for VTE and mortality outcomes. Further validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is warranted.
