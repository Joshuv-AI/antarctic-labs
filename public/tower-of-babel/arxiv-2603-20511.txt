“Girl, I’m so Serious”: CARE, a Capability Framework for Reproductive Equity in Human-AI Interaction CCS: Computing methodologies Natural language processing CCS: General and reference Measurement CCS: Human-centered computing Interaction design process and methods
Alice Zhong, Phoebe Chen, Anika Sharma, Kandyce Brennan, Snehalkumar ‘Neil’ S. Gaikwad

Abstract
. Sexual and reproductive health (SRH) remains shaped by structural barriers that leave many without judgment-free information. AI chatbots offer anonymous alternatives, but access alone does not ensure equity when socioeconomic determinants shape whose capabilities these tools expand or constrain. Conventional methods for evaluating human-AI interaction were not designed to capture whether technologies holistically support reproductive autonomy. We introduce CARE, Capability Approach for Reproductive Equity, developing capabilities, functionings, and conversion factors into a Normative Design Lens and an Evaluation Lens for AI in SRH contexts. Evaluating SRH-specific non-LLM chatbots, general-use LLMs, and search engine features along credibility and reasoning, we identify two epistemic harms: source opacity and response rigidity. We conclude with design and evaluation recommendations, participatory auditing strategies, and policy implications for high-stakes domains where AI intersects with inequity.

Sen’s capability approach reframes development not as the accumulation of resources but as the expansion of real freedoms
(
40
;
37
;
39
;
38
;
36
)
. The same principle applies here. A chatbot is a resource, but a resource does not become a capability unless users can convert it into meaningful action. This conversion is shaped by socioeconomic determinants such as health literacy, healthcare access, stigma, and legal constraints that determine whose freedoms these tools actually expand. Current SRH chatbot design reflects resource-centered thinking: tools are built without first asking which capabilities they should expand and for whom. For instance, mainstream SRH chatbots overwhelmingly adopt female personas
(
24
)
, reinforcing social and gender norms that frame reproductive health as solely a women’s concern and excluding users of other genders from the conversation entirely. A chatbot may be available 24/7, but availability is insufficient if users cannot afford follow-up care, lack the literacy to interpret responses, or face stigma that prevents them from asking questions. The question is not whether users have access, but whether these tools expand their real freedom to make informed reproductive decisions.

This reframing demands new approaches to both how SRH technologies are designed and how they are measured. SRH technologies are typically built around information delivery rather than structured around the capabilities users need. Current evaluation methods in HCI and AI assess essential dimensions such as usability, accuracy, and interaction quality
(
26
;
1
)
, but were not designed to measure whether technologies holistically support reproductive health and autonomy. What is missing is a complementary layer connecting design and evaluation to the structural conditions shaping how users convert access into capabilities and capabilities into meaningful outcomes.

To address this gap, we introduce CARE, Capability Approach for Reproductive Equity, a sociotechnical framework grounded in Sen’s capability approach and Martha Nussbaum’s Ten Central Human Capabilities
(
36
;
27
)
. CARE develops capabilities, functionings, and conversion factors into two lenses: a Normative Design Lens that works backward from reproductive wellbeing to identify what technologies should support, and an Evaluation Lens that works forward from resources to measure how they are mediated into capabilities and functionings.

We make three contributions.
First
, we introduce CARE and its two lenses, developing the capability approach for responsible design and evaluation of human-AI interaction in SRH contexts.
Second
, we apply CARE’s Normative Design Lens to establish what SRH technologies should enable, then use its Evaluation Lens to assess existing tools (SRH-specific non-LLM chatbots, general-use LLMs, and search engine features) along credibility and reasoning. This reveals two critical gaps that constitute epistemic harms, structurally embedded design failures that restrict users’ agency to evaluate, contest, and act on health information: lack of source transparency and rigid, verbose responses. These failures operate at a layer that conventional accuracy and usability metrics were not designed to surface.
Third
, we translate these findings into design and evaluation recommendations, participatory auditing strategies, and policy implications for high-stakes domains where AI intersects with inequity.

3.
CARE: A Capability-Based Framework for Design and Evaluation of SRH Technologies

CARE (Figure
1
) builds on Sen’s distinction between capabilities and functionings, mediated by conversion factors, developed through Robeyns’ core (A) and specification (B) modules (see Appendix
A
for details)
(
33
)
. CARE is tailored to SRH in three ways. First, it specifies a capability subset from Nussbaum’s Ten Central Human Capabilities relevant to reproductive contexts. Second, it foregrounds conversion factors specific to SRH, including reproductive stigma, gendered design assumptions, and legal constraints on reproductive autonomy, that would not arise in other health domains with equal force. Third, it defines wellbeing in terms of reproductive freedom rather than general health outcomes. These specifications anchor CARE in SRH while the underlying structure remains adaptable to other sensitive domains.

The framework operates through two lenses, each structured around resources, conversion factors, capabilities, and functionings.
The Normative Design Lens
specifies how SRH tools should be designed by following the capability approach
backwards
: (1) establish wellbeing goals grounded in reproductive freedom, (2) specify desired functionings as target "beings" and "doings," (3) identify capabilities that enable those functionings, (4) determine conversion factors that impact users’ ability to achieve capabilities, and (5) design resources intended to enable those capabilities.
The Evaluation Lens
follows the capability approach
forward
to assess existing technologies: (1) analyze the technology as a resource, (2) examine how conversion factors mediate users’ ability to convert resources into capabilities, (3) evaluate the technology’s impact on the capability set, (4) assess the functionings users actually achieve, and (5) assess the resulting impact on SRH wellbeing.

3.1.
Normative Baseline via CARE’s Design Lens

Applying the Normative Design Lens, we work backward from reproductive freedom (Step 1) to identify desired functionings such as making informed contraceptive decisions, understanding fertility risks, and seeking care without shame (Step 2). These require capabilities including Practical Reason, Bodily Health, Bodily Integrity, and Control over One’s Environment, which we evaluate directly (Step 3). Conversion factors that could constrain these capabilities include low health literacy, reproductive stigma, and lack of trust in AI-generated information (Step 4). Technologies designed to enable these capabilities should therefore provide concise, literacy-calibrated responses with verifiable sources and non-stigmatizing language (Step 5). The standard is not just accuracy but whether users can verify, comprehend, and act on information within their contexts. Table
2
presents the normative mapping of question categories to their associated functionings and capabilities, establishing the standard against which we evaluate existing tools.

3.2.2.
Evaluation Dimensions

CARE’s normative baseline establishes that SRH technologies should enable users to verify, comprehend, and act on health information. We developed this through two measurable dimensions.

Credibility
captures whether users can verify information, mapping onto Practical Reason and Control over One’s Environment, as exercising reproductive autonomy requires traceable, trustworthy sources. We assessed credibility through responses to the trust question and citation analysis, classifying .org, .gov, .edu, and .int domains as reputable sources. This proxy is conservative, as some .com domains host medically reviewed content, but provides a reproducible classification without subjective per-domain judgment.

Reasoning
captures whether users can comprehend and act on information, mapping onto Practical Reason; Bodily Health; and Bodily Integrity; as exercising reproductive control requires understanding the information provided. Two coders independently assessed whether responses directly addressed each query and provided logically coherent, contextually appropriate information, comparing against expert answers from
bedsider.org
. Responses were coded as high (comprehensive and appropriate), moderate (relevant but with gaps), or low (off-topic or contradictory), yielding Cohen’s
\kappa
= 0.78, with disagreements resolved through discussion. We selected these two dimensions because they are the most direct points at which tool design mediates users’ conversion of a resource into a functioning. To capture default behavior, each LLM-based tool was accessed in incognito mode with a new session per query and temperature settings left unadjusted. The same protocol was applied to non-LLM chatbots. Each question was asked once with no follow-up, except where a chatbot explicitly requested clarification, mirroring the majority use pattern where most LLM interactions are single-turn
(
57
)
.

4.
Results

We apply CARE’s Evaluation Lens to SRH-specific non-LLM chatbots, general-use LLMs, and search engine features, examining how each tool’s design choices shape human-AI interaction in ways that impact the capabilities and functionings established in our normative baseline.

4.1.
Credibility

Tools inhibit capabilities for Practical Reason and Control over One’s Environment by being unable to provide reliable citations.
None of the SRH-specific non-LLM chatbots provided specific sources. When asked why the user should trust the tool, responses ranged from nuanced guidance on proper use (BigSis), to misunderstanding the query (Ask Roo), to dismissing the concern entirely (Layla’s Got You, Figure
2
), despite Layla’s responses being sourced from experts
(
4
)
.

All sources cited by general-use LLMs and search engine features varied in quantity and quality. Across all general-use LLM chatbots and search engine features, 540 unique domain names appeared in 1,456 total citations. Of these, 295 (56.63%) were referenced only once. The top 20% of unique domain names accounted for 67% of the total citations. Google AI Mode cited the most sources, averaging 16.6 per query. ChatGPT and Claude failed to cite sources by default when prompted with only the question, so we reprompted with an addendum:
"Please cite sources in your response."
after the question. Claude provided citations for 17 of its 34 responses. However, for two responses, it failed to provide citations, and for the remaining 15, Claude stated it was
unable
to cite sources and attributed the information in its response to its training data (Figure
2
, Appendix
C
). Using
.org
,
.gov
,
.edu
, and
.int
domains as proxies for reputable sources, we found 737 (50.6%) of citations came from such sources (see Table
4
in Appendix
D
for detailed breakdown). Even under this conservative proxy, nearly half of all citations lack institutional backing. Claude cited the highest proportion of reputable sources at 74%, while no other tool exceeded 57% (Figure
3
). Among
.com
citations, the domains most frequently referenced included medically reviewed sites such as healthline.com and webmd.com. We found that social media platforms (YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, Facebook, Quora, and Reddit) comprised 2.68% of all citations. Google AI Overview cited the highest percentage of social media sources (6.57%). These patterns of missing and non-reputable citations constitute an epistemic harm against the normative baseline of verifiable sources enabling practical reason: users cannot reliably distinguish institutionally backed guidance from unvetted content.

4.2.
Reasoning

SRH-specific non-LLM chatbots inhibit capabilities for Practical Reason; Bodily Health; and Bodily Integrity by providing information that is accurate but poorly reasoned.
Two coders measured reasoning by comparing responses against bedsider.org (
\kappa=0.78
). Among the responses, 28.4% fully matched the
bedsider.org
answers (high reasoning), 26.1% partially matched (moderate reasoning), and 45.5% either did not understand, redirected the user, or gave irrelevant answers (low reasoning).

Irrelevant answers occurred even when the chatbot has an appropriate answer in its corpus. For instance, when asked
"Can I get pregnant on my period?"
, Layla’s Got You provided only a general definition of a period despite its corpus containing the response
"You can get pregnant any time you have sex. This means at any time during the month, even on your period. […]"
. These system safety failures constitute an epistemic harm against the normative baseline of literacy-calibrated, comprehensible responses, limiting users’ ability to understand their own bodies and health.
General-use LLMs and search engine features are highly reasoned but limit capabilities for Practical Reason; Bodily Health; and Bodily Integrity through lengthy responses and unexplained jargon.
LLM responses averaged 2.75 to 7.09 times longer than expert-curated answers, creating barriers for users with lower health literacy. Most responses contained undefined medical terminology. For example, ChatGPT, Claude, and Copilot used the term "hymen" without definition, creating comprehension barriers for users unfamiliar with anatomical language. While accurate, these sociotechnical conversion factors limit users’ abilities to achieve associated functionings, particularly for those with lower health literacy.

5.
Discussion

Our evaluation reveals a systematic gap between what CARE’s normative baseline prescribes and what existing SRH technologies deliver. Conventional evaluation would identify these gaps as information quality problems or interface issues. CARE reframes them as failures of human-AI interaction that deny users’ freedom to function, supporting the argument that evaluating generative AI requires social science methods attuned to socially situated outcomes
(
52
;
42
)
. A chatbot that provides uncited medical advice does not simply reflect poor design. It restructures the user’s epistemic relationship with health information, shifting evaluation from
"does the interface work?"
to
"does the interaction expand or constrain users’ real freedoms?"

Conversion factors constrain capabilities across all tool categories.
CARE surfaces two sociotechnical conversion factors, neither of which would register under conventional evaluation. The first is source opacity: without transparent citations, users cannot contest guidance, and guidance that cannot be contested cannot be dependable. Prior work has shown that AI chatbots frequently hallucinate citations
(
6
;
53
;
7
)
; our results present a different problem, as LLM-based tools cited real sources, though most were not reputable. Only 50.6% of 1,456 citations came from reputable domains, a pattern especially concerning where medically unverified SRH content on social media generates higher engagement than vetted sources
(
45
;
56
;
41
)
. The second is response rigidity and verbosity. LLM-based tools prioritize helpfulness through length, likely reflecting RLHF-driven training where longer responses proxy for quality
(
43
)
, burying key information in verbose answers with unexplained jargon. Non-LLM tools have the opposite problem: keyword-dependent answers fail to recognize how different communities phrase the same concerns, producing irrelevant responses even when the correct answer exists in the corpus. Both patterns prevent users with lower health literacy from converting accurate information into understanding, despite evidence that effective SRH communication requires both accessibility for low-literacy users
(
22
)
and flexibility across linguistic contexts
(
32
;
14
)
. These technological failures do not occur in a vacuum. Gendered exclusion from SRH education
(
49
)
and low AI literacy in sensitive health domains
(
20
)
shape how users engage with these tools before any interaction begins, meaning the users most in need of reliable SRH information are precisely those least equipped to compensate for these design failures.

Epistemic Harms and Participatory Auditing.
Together, these failures constitute what we term
epistemic harms
, a category distinct from adjacent concepts. Misinformation is false content that can be corrected by improving training data. Opacity is lack of source attribution that can be addressed by adding citations. Poor explainability is inaccessible reasoning that can be resolved through clearer presentation. Each targets a specific output failure. Epistemic harm is different in kind, not degree. It occurs not when a system produces a bad output, but when a system’s interaction patterns structurally restrict users’ agency to evaluate, contest, and act on information regardless of whether any individual output is accurate. A system exhibits bad information design when its outputs are poor; it produces epistemic harm when its interactions systematically deny users the resources to judge for themselves. Both interactions in Figure
2
illustrate this: one encourages users to stop scrutinizing information, the other leads users to falsely believe verification is impossible. In both cases the information may be accurate, but the interaction reshapes the user’s epistemic agency. This category remains absent from existing harm taxonomies
(
54
;
28
)
, yet it disproportionately affects the users these tools claim to serve.

Design and Evaluation Implications.
Our findings call for three direct design changes: surfacing citations from reputable sources, calibrating response length to expert-curated baselines, and defining medical terminology inline. Beyond these fixes, CARE motivates participatory design with target communities to address stigma as a conversion factor
(
4
)
. For AI evaluation more broadly, CARE contributes three methodological shifts.
First, evaluation should measure capability expansion, not just output quality. By conventional standards, all tools would pass
(
26
;
1
)
, yet CARE reveals failures these standards were not designed to surface. Second, evaluation should account for conversion factors as mediating variables, since the same tool produces different capability outcomes for different users
(
35
)
. Third, evaluation validity requires participatory methods with affected populations, because developer-defined metrics cannot capture harms experienced at the intersection of tool design and users’ lived circumstances
(
52
;
15
;
42
)
.

Current AI safety frameworks
(
54
;
28
)
do not account for epistemic harms, and health regulations like HIPAA address privacy but not the epistemic quality of AI-generated guidance. CARE identifies four policy gaps. First, AI systems providing health guidance should be required to cite reputable sources. Second, participatory auditing should precede deployment, with capability assessments publicly reported. Third, legal frameworks must establish users’ epistemic rights: the right to know the provenance of health guidance, to access underlying sources, and to flag information that contradicts clinical experience. Fourth, because SRH conversion factors vary across cultural, linguistic, and legal settings, CARE’s capability subset should be deliberated with local communities rather than applied universally, supported by practical tools such as capability scorecards and community-led evaluation campaigns where regulatory infrastructure is limited.

6.
Conclusion

CARE demonstrates that responsible AI in sensitive health domains requires more than accurate outputs and functional interfaces. With Capability Approach, we show that existing SRH technologies produce epistemic harms that constrain reproductive autonomy even when tools appear to function as intended, failures invisible to conventional evaluation because they operate at the level of users’ real freedoms, not system performance. The participatory auditing strategies, design recommendations, and policy guidance derived from CARE extend to any domain where AI mediates access to sensitive information and autonomy is at stake. As AI increasingly serves as the first point of contact for health information in stigmatized contexts, frameworks like CARE ensure that expanding access does not come at the cost of the very autonomy these technologies promise to support.

Appendix A
Capability Approach Modules

A-Module: The Non-optional Core

Ag

A1.

Functionings and capabilities as core concepts:
CARE distinguishes between what users actually achieve (functionings) and their real opportunity to achieve it (capabilities).

A2.

Functionings and capabilities are value-neutral categories:
Functionings can be positive (being informed about contraception), negative (receiving stigmatizing responses), or ambiguous (providing care under pressure). Value-neutrality allows CARE to analyze all SRH outcomes. Table
1
presents the capabilities and functionings selected as critical for reproductive freedom.

A3.

Conversion factors:
Personal, social, and environmental factors affect users’ ability to transform resources into functionings. CARE introduces sociotechnical conversion factors to account for how tool design mediates this process, explaining why equal access to SRH technologies does not produce equal capabilities.

A4.

Means-ends distinction:
Resources are means; capabilities and functionings are ends. In CARE, the SRH technology is the resource, and the end is expanding users’ ability to achieve reproductive freedom.

A5.

The evaluative space:
SRH technologies must be evaluated by their impact on what users can be and do, not through technical metrics like usage statistics, shifting focus from system performance to human wellbeing.

A6.

Other dimensions of ultimate value:
CARE integrates Nussbaum’s Ten Central Human Capabilities as its normative foundation.

A7.

Valuing each person as an end:
CARE evaluates impact on individual users, ensuring that analysis does not aggregate away disadvantages faced by marginalized populations.

B-Module: Specification for SRH Technologies

Ag

B1.

Purpose:
To provide a framework for normative design and evaluation of SRH technologies that expand users’ freedom to access, understand, and act on accurate SRH information free from misinformation, stigma, discrimination, or social sanctions.

B2.

Selection of dimensions:
We derived functionings and capabilities by working backward from wellbeing goals. Sexual and reproductive wellbeing encompasses SRH, autonomy and agency, reduced anxiety and harm, and dignity and social inclusion. These goals require key beings (being informed, confident, and respected) and doings (seeking care, making informed choices, and exercising reproductive agency). Drawing on Nussbaum’s Ten Central Human Capabilities, we define SRH-related functionings as shown in Table
1
.

B3.

Human diversity:
Users’ identities (race, gender, disability) shape their conversion factors and capability sets. Ignoring diversity would overlook how SRH technologies might produce unequal outcomes for users facing compounding barriers.

B4.

Agency:
Each user is treated as an independent agent capable of making their own decisions. SRH technologies should support user autonomy by providing options and knowledge rather than prescribing behavior.

B5.

Structural constraints:
CARE considers systemic barriers such as inadequate education and SRH stigma that shape interactions between users and technologies, ensuring analysis accounts for the broader structures within which tools operate.

B6.

Functionings vs. capabilities:
CARE focuses on expanding capabilities as the precondition for achieving functionings, with reproductive freedom as the ultimate goal.

B7.

Meta-theoretical commitment:
CARE adopts a normative approach that centers users from diverse backgrounds and contexts.
