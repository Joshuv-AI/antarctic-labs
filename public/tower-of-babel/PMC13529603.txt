Stepwise management of temporomandibular joint internal derangement: from conservative therapy to arthrocentesis with intra-articular hyaluronic acid and 12.5% dextrose
Authors: Hamzah Sukma Anggoro, Didit Istadi, Pingky Krisna Arinda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Temporomandibular joint (TMJ) internal derangement frequently leads to orofacial pain, joint dysfunction, and limited mandibular movement. Conservative treatment is first, but arthrocentesis may help refractory cases. This case report describes one year of conservative therapy and arthrocentesis with intra-articular hyaluronic acid (HA) and 12.5% dextrose. A 21-year-old woman had right-sided preauricular pain, joint clicking, and intermittent locking for one year. Clinical and magnetic resonance imaging findings confirmed anterior disc displacement with reduction and intermittent locking with osteoarthritis of the right TMJ. After 8 months of physiotherapy, pharmacotherapy, occlusal splint, and diet modification, maximum mouth opening improved from 13 to 36 mm, but symptoms persisted. Two arthrocentesis sessions with prolotherapy were performed: one with HA alone, followed by HA and 12.5% dextrose. At 4-month follow-up, function was restored, symptoms resolved, and joint clicking disappeared. Arthrocentesis with intra-articular HA and 12.5% dextrose relieved pain, improved mouth opening, and restored function in refractory TMJ internal derangement. This case highlights the value of a staged, minimally invasive strategy, though larger controlled studies are needed to validate its efficacy and to standardize treatment protocols. A stepwise approach, from conservative therapy to arthrocentesis with intra-articular HA and 12.5% dextrose, can effectively manage TMJ internal derangement.

I. Introduction

Internal derangement of the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is a common subtype of temporomandibular disorders (TMD) that affects one-third of the population and is usually associated with/without degenerative joint disease (DJD)1. It is characterized by disc displacement relative to the mandibular condyle and articular eminence. The quality of life and masticatory function are substantially impacted by clinical manifestations, which frequently include chronic orofacial pain, restricted mouth opening, and joint sounds2. This condition also represents an early stage of DJD, in which cartilage degradation and synovial inflammation are induced by persistent mechanical stress and disc displacement, ultimately leading to osteoarthritic alterations. The etiology is multifactorial, involving mechanical, functional, and occasionally psychological factors that contribute to disc displacement and altered joint biomechanics3.

Conservative, non-surgical interventions are considered the first-line treatment for TMJ internal derangement. These include physical therapy, dietary modification, pharmacological agents, and occlusal splint therapy4. Occlusal splints, which are often hard acrylic appliances worn across the dental arches, are frequently used to reduce joint loading, redistribute occlusal stresses, diminish parafunctional activity, and possibly reposition the condyle-disc assembly to a more stable configuration. Several studies have reported improvement in TMJ-related pain and functional limitation following regular use of occlusal splints, particularly in the early stages of internal derangement5,6.

However, some patients may continue to experience persistent symptoms despite adhering to long-term conservative treatment protocols. In refractory cases, minimally invasive interventions such as arthrocentesis have been proposed as the next step in the treatment algorithm. Arthrocentesis involves lavage of the superior joint space to remove inflammatory mediators, release adhesions, and enhance intra-articular mobility, frequently resulting in considerable symptomatic relief7. The intra-articular injection of adjunctive agents or prolotherapy may improve the therapeutic efficacy of arthrocentesis. Hyaluronic acid (HA) is widely used to enhance the viscoelasticity of synovial fluid, lubricate joints, and reduce inflammation. It also protects cartilage and reduces friction, making it particularly advantageous in the treatment of osteoarthritis8,9. Similarly, dextrose with concentrations >10% has shown potential in accelerating tissue regeneration, regulating inflammation, and reducing joint pain by inducing a mild regenerative inflammatory response10,11.

Although both HA and dextrose prolotherapy have individually demonstrated beneficial effects in improving joint lubrication, reducing inflammation, and promoting tissue healing, no prior study has reported on its combined intra-articular use for TMJ disorders. This case presents a novel minimally invasive approach, utilizing arthrocentesis followed by intra-articular injection of HA combined with 12.5% dextrose to address refractory TMJ internal derangement. This combined method shows potential clinical benefits in restoring function and relieving pain in refractory TMJ cases, supporting its consideration as an intermediate treatment strategy prior to surgical intervention.

II. Case Report

A 21-year-old woman presented with right preauricular pain, limited mouth opening, and joint clicking, persisting for one year. Mastication aggravated the pain, and intermittent locking occasionally accompanied it. She reported a parafunctional habit of bruxism but had no history of trauma, systemic disease, or psychological stress.

Extraoral examination revealed localized tenderness over the right preauricular area and associated masticatory muscles. Mandibular movement results in pain accompanied by a clicking sound in the right TMJ. The mandibular opening pathway exhibited a transient deviation to the right characterized by restricted motion: pain-free opening measured at 13 mm, maximum unassisted opening at 15 mm, and maximum assisted opening at 17 mm.(Fig. 1) An asymmetric Class III malocclusion was noted intraorally, along with a deep bite and attrition of the anterior mandibular teeth.(Fig. 2) The visual analogue scale (VAS) score was 7/10, indicating severe pain.

Panoramic radiography showed both condyles positioned within their fossae, with a slightly reduced joint space on the right side. Magnetic resonance imaging confirmed anterior disc displacement with reduction (ADDwR) on the right TMJ, accompanied by irregular condylar surface, cortical erosion, and mild joint effusion, consistent with early osteoarthritic changes.(Fig. 3)

According to the Diagnostic criteria for temporomandibular disorders (DC/TMD, 2014), the diagnosis includes ADDwR, right TMJ arthralgia, local myalgia, and early osteoarthritic changes that are consistent with Wilkes stage II and early degenerative alterations.(Fig. 4)

Initial management consisted of conservative therapy, including a soft diet, avoidance of excessive mouth opening, range-of-motion exercises, moist heat therapy, and pharmacologic treatment with ibuprofen 400 mg and eperisone 50 mg. Minimal improvement was achieved, with pain reduction only during medication use. Therefore, a stabilization-type occlusal splint was fabricated and worn for 5 months.(Fig. 5)

During the first week, the splint was used for 2 hours on the first day, with daily increments of 2 hours until reaching 14 hours per day. In the second week, the patient maintained 14 hours of daily use. By the third week, wearing time was gradually extended to full-time use (24 hours daily except during meals) and continued until the end of the fifth month. This therapy reduced pain intensity from severe to mild and increased maximum mouth opening (MMO) to 36 mm, though intermittent clicking persisted.

As symptoms persisted despite conservative therapy, a minimally invasive intervention was performed. The first arthrocentesis utilized 100 mL of normal saline (NS) for irrigation, followed by an intra-articular injection of 1 mL of HA. This procedure led to significant pain reduction (VAS 1) and improved mandibular movement, achieving a pain-free maximum unassisted opening of 45 mm and a maximum assisted opening of 50 mm. Two months later, a second arthrocentesis was conducted with 50 mL NS lavage, followed by intra-articular administration of 1 mL HA and 1 mL of 12.5% dextrose. Arthrocentesis was performed using the standard double-needle technique under local anesthesia, preceded by an auriculotemporal nerve block. After antiseptic preparation, two anatomical reference points were identified and marked along the Holmlund–Hellsing line extending from the lateral canthus to the tragus to guide needle placement. The posterior point was located 10 mm anterior to the tragus and 2 mm below the line, and the anterior point 10 mm further anterior and 10 mm below it. An 18-gauge needle was inserted into the superior joint space at the posterior mark and 100 mL of NS solution was injected to distend the cavity. After irrigation, gentle mandibular movements were carried out to free the disc and release adhesions.(Fig. 6) The procedure was well-tolerated without complications.

During a 4-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with stable mandibular function and no recurrence of clicking or locking. Pain intensity, initially recorded as VAS 7 (severe), progressively decreased and reached VAS 0 at the final evaluation, indicating complete resolution of pain.(Fig. 7) Mandibular movement also returned to normal parameters, with a pain-free and maximum unassisted opening of 45 mm and a maximum assisted opening of 50 mm at the last follow-up.(Fig. 8, 9) A fixed orthodontic appliance with posterior bite risers was subsequently employed to maintain occlusal stability, prevent relapse, and minimize bruxism-related parafunctional activity.(Fig. 10) No adverse events or complications were observed throughout the treatment course. This case report was prepared in accordance with the CAse REport (CARE) Checklist to ensure accuracy and completeness in clinical case documentation.

III. Discussion

TMJ internal derangement is a progressive disorder characterized by altered disc–condyle coordination, inflammation, and early degenerative remodelling of the joint. Its multifactorial etiology includes repetitive microtrauma, occlusal instability, and parafunctional habits such as bruxism, which lead to synovial inflammation and cartilage degradation12,13. In the present case, these factors likely contributed to the patient’s ADDwR, arthralgia, and early osteoarthritic changes.

The management strategy followed a stepwise, minimally invasive protocol aligned with current TMJ management guidelines. Initial therapy emphasized conservative measures, comprising pharmacologic treatment, physiotherapy, and occlusal stabilization, aimed to reduce inflammation, muscle hyperactivity, and joint overloading. These modalities are considered the first-line approach for the management of TMJ internal derangement and arthralgia4.

In this case, a stabilization-type occlusal splint was used for five months with gradual adaptation, effectively redistributing occlusal forces, reducing joint loading, relaxing hyperactive masticatory muscles, and preventing parafunctional activities, including bruxism14. Prior studies show that stabilization splints improve joint function and reduce pain by decreasing muscle co-contraction and promoting a more stable mandibular position. By maintaining a gap between the mandibular condyle and fossa, this splint restores blood supply, decreases intra-articular pressure, and relieves TMJ pain6,15.

While occlusal splints can provide relief by stabilizing the jaw and reducing muscle tension, they do not directly correct intra-articular pathology. In cases of disc displacement or osteoarthritis of the TMJ, splints may alleviate symptoms but cannot reliably restore the disc–condyle relationship or reverse joint degeneration16. In this case, conservative therapy, including the use of an occlusal splint, markedly reduced pain intensity from severe to mild and increased the MMO to 46 mm, although intermittent clicking persisted.

When conservative interventions fail to resolve symptoms, arthrocentesis becomes a feasible subsequent option in the treatment algorithm. This minimally invasive procedure facilitates joint lavage, reduces inflammatory mediators, lyses adhesions, and improves joint mobility. This procedure is effective in reducing pain, increasing MMO, and alleviating joint locking in patients with disc displacement (with or without reduction), osteoarthritis, and post-traumatic arthritis who have not responded to conservative therapy7,17,18. Based on the studies by Rashid and Tawfek16, arthrocentesis produced immediate pain relief and improved joint function by reducing intra-articular effusion and releasing adhesions, while occlusal splints maintained these benefits by decreasing joint load and alleviating muscle spasm. This combination therapy provides both rapid and sustained symptom relief, demonstrating its superiority over splint therapy or arthrocentesis alone in managing TMJ internal derangement. These findings emphasize that, although occlusal splints remain a valuable component, particularly for muscle-related TMD, they are most effective when complemented by minimally invasive intra-articular interventions in cases with structural joint changes.

NS was used as the irrigant in this case due to its isotonic and inert properties, allowing efficient elimination of inflammatory cytokines. Although Ringer’s lactate (RL) more closely resembles physiological extracellular fluid, studies report no significant difference in clinical outcomes between NS and RL for TMJ arthrocentesis18. Tsui et al.17 discovered that arthrocentesis with a smaller lavage volume (<150 mL) may achieve superior outcomes in pain reduction and jaw function than larger-volume irrigation. This might be attributed to reduced procedural trauma, shorter operative time, and more effective concentration of lavage within the superior joint compartment.

Recent evidence suggests that adjunctive intra-articular injections can further enhance the efficacy of arthrocentesis. Agents including HA, platelet-rich plasma (PRP), corticosteroids, or dextrose prolotherapy have demonstrated efficacy in enhancing joint lubrication, reducing inflammatory cytokine activity, and promoting the repair of the articular and capsular tissues. The integration of mechanical lavage with pharmacologic or regenerative adjuvants yields synergistic advantages, leading to enhanced and more enduring therapeutic results than lavage alone. Studies have demonstrated that patients receiving arthrocentesis with intra-articular HA or PRP exhibit greater improvements in pain reduction and MMO compared to those treated solely with lavage18.

In the present case, the initial arthrocentesis using NS lavage followed by intra-articular HA injection provided transient improvement, whereas a second session combining HA with 12.5% dextrose achieved complete and sustained symptom resolution. This enhanced response may be attributed to the complementary mechanisms of both agents: HA provides viscoelastic lubrication, reduces friction, and exerts anti-inflammatory effects, while dextrose promotes fibroblast proliferation, collagen synthesis, and capsular tightening, thereby improving joint stability and function10,19,20.

Recent studies show that HA injections following arthrocentesis significantly improve clinical outcomes in TMJ internal derangement, particularly in patients unresponsive to conservative care17,19. Moreover, dextrose prolotherapy has demonstrated potential as a regenerative adjuvant, with concentrations above 10% stimulating proliferative pathways through the release of growth factors, hence promoting ligament and capsular repair11,20. The combination of HA and dextrose may thus offer synergistic benefits, enhancing both lubrication and biological regeneration.

A key strength of this case lies in the novel combination of intra-articular HA and 12.5% dextrose, which is rarely reported in TMJ literature. Additionally, the utilization of a posterior bite-rising orthodontic appliance further contributed to biomechanical stabilization by maintaining vertical dimension, balancing occlusal forces, and minimizing parafunctional load8,18, which is particularly relevant for patients with deep bite and asymmetric occlusion.

Clinically, this case highlights the value of a comprehensive, non-surgical management pathway that integrates conservative, pharmacologic, and biomechanical strategies before considering surgical intervention. The adjunctive use of regenerative intra-articular agents, particularly the combination of HA and 12.5% dextrose, appears to offer a promising treatment option for patients with early degenerative TMJ changes unresponsive to conventional therapy. These findings support a testable hypothesis that, in TMJ internal derangement with early osteoarthritis, arthrocentesis followed by intra-articular administration of HA combined with 12.5% dextrose may provide superior improvements in pain relief, mandibular mobility, and functional recovery. Future randomized controlled trials with larger cohorts and long-term follow-up are warranted to validate this hypothesis and establish standardized injection protocols.

A structured, stepwise, non-surgical strategy involving conservative therapy followed by arthrocentesis combined with intra-articular HA and 12.5% dextrose can effectively address TMJ internal derangement with early osteoarthritic changes. This minimally invasive, low-risk method provides significant pain reduction and functional improvement in patients unresponsive to conservative treatment, potentially delaying or eliminating the need for surgery.
