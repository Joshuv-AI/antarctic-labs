Babel: Jailbreaking Safety Attention via Obfuscation Distribution Optimized Sampling
Ziwei Wang, Jing Chen, Ruichao Liang, Zhi Wang, Yebo Feng Ju Jia Ruiying Du Cong Wu Yang Liu

Abstract
Despite rigorous safety alignment, Large Language Models (LLMs) remain vulnerable to jailbreak attacks. Existing black-box methods often rely on heuristic templates or exhaustive trials, lacking mechanistic interpretability and query efficiency. In this study, we investigate an intrinsic vulnerability in the safety mechanisms of LLMs, where safety alignment relies on a small set of sparsely distributed attention heads, leaving much of the representational space weakly monitored. We formalize this phenomenon with a mathematical jailbreaking model that characterizes the delicate boundary of effective text obfuscation and analytically explains observed jailbreak behaviors. Guided by this model, we propose Babel, an efficient black-box attack framework that exploits the identified safety gap through systematic obfuscation sampling with iterative, feedback-driven distribution refinement, enabling reliable and high-success jailbreak attacks without access to model internals. Comprehensive evaluations on frontier commercial models demonstrate that Babel achieves state-of-the-art attack success rates and superior query efficiency. Specifically, compared to state-of-the-art methods, Babel increases the attack success rate on GPT-4o from 41.33% to 82.67% and on Claude-3-5-haiku from 38.33% to 78.33% within an average of 40 queries, providing a robust red-teaming methodology for LLMs safety research.

(

. Consequently, rigorous red-teaming through diverse jailbreak methodologies has become imperative, serving as a vital diagnostic tool to evaluate and fortify the safety boundaries of LLMs.

Jailbreak attacks are generally categorized as white-box and black-box attacks based on attacker capabilities. Black-box attacks pose a more pervasive threat, as most leading-edge models are deployed as closed-source services
(
25
;
10
)
. Existing black-box attack techniques primarily design methods from the attackers’ perspective, constructing and optimizing templates and prompts through extensive search or heuristic algorithms to achieve effective and transferable attacks
(
38
;
17
;
8
;
14
;
34
)
. However, these approaches often lack interpretability for ignoring vulnerabilities within the LLMs’ inherent safety mechanisms, limiting their performance and insights into enhancing safety.

To address these limitations, we shift the perspective to the internal safety mechanisms of LLMs, aiming to uncover intrinsic vulnerabilities that can be systematically exploited. Prior work shows that safety alignment is primarily implemented by a small number of sparsely distributed attention heads (safety heads)
(
42
)
, meaning that only a limited subset of hidden states is monitored while a large representational space remains unchecked. This imbalance creates an exploitable vulnerability. For the original harmful query, copyright-related information is captured by safety heads, resulting in refusal. In contrast, a carefully constructed obfuscated input can shift harmful semantics into hidden states not covered by safety heads, thereby bypassing safety alignment and eliciting disallowed outputs. However, the boundary of effective obfuscation is highly delicate: excessive obfuscation destroys harmful intent, whereas insufficient obfuscation remains detectable. To characterize this boundary, we propose a mathematical jailbreaking model that quantifies obfuscation strength, enabling reliable evaluation and controlled optimization of obfuscation strategies that stably activate this vulnerability.

Guided by this insight, we propose Babel, a black-box attack framework that bypasses safety attention via text obfuscation. Babel evaluates and optimizes the obfuscation distribution of harmful queries, explicitly targeting the identified safety gap. Unlike existing trial-and-error prompting approaches, Babel performs systematic obfuscation sampling, regulating the statistical properties of obfuscation to maximize bypass probability. This process is further guided by an iterative feedback loop that dynamically refines the sampling distribution based on model responses.
Extensive experiments on state-of-the-art closed-source models show that Babel achieves state-of-the-art attack success rates: 82.67% on GPT-4o, 78.33% on Gemini-2.0-Flash, 95.67% on Grok-3, and 75.67% on Claude-3.5-Haiku, while substantially improving query efficiency over existing baselines.
This paper makes the following contributions:

•

We identify a intrinsic vulnerability in the safety mechanisms of LLMs and formalize it through a mathematical model that characterizes how to bypass safety alignment.

•

We propose Babel, a black-box jailbreak framework that systematically exploits this vulnerability via distribution-aware obfuscation sampling and iterative optimization.

•

We conduct extensive evaluations on commercial LLMs from multiple providers, achieving state-of-the-art attack success rates with significantly improved query efficiency.

Related Work

2.1
Safety Mechanisms of LLMs

Recent mechanistic interpretability studies have begun to uncover the internal structures underlying safety alignment in large language models
(
42
;
6
;
32
)
. Prior work suggests that safety behaviors are implemented by sparse and highly localized components, including specific attention heads and neurons, whose ablation can substantially weaken safety alignment while largely preserving general model utility. For example,
42
identify safety-critical attention heads whose removal significantly increases harmful responses despite modifying only a negligible fraction of parameters. Similarly,
6
show that fewer than 1% of neurons consistently contribute to handling harmful queries, indicating that safety alignment is concentrated in compact parameter subspaces.

Subsequent studies further reveal the structural fragility of these mechanisms.
32
demonstrate that safety-critical regions occupy only a small fraction of the parameter space and can be disrupted through pruning or low-rank modifications with minimal impact on downstream utility. Related work also shows that neuron-level pruning can severely degrade alignment behavior even under small perturbations, highlighting the brittleness of safety-relevant representations. Moreover, recent evidence suggests that post-training capabilities, such as enhanced reasoning behaviors in LRMs, may partially suppress or mask pre-existing safety mechanisms rather than eliminate them entirely, implying a complex interaction between reasoning and alignment objectives.

Together, these findings suggest that current LLM safety mechanisms rely on compact yet fragile internal structures that remain vulnerable to targeted perturbations. This line of work motivates further investigation into how reasoning dynamics interact with safety-critical representations and how such mechanisms fail under adversarial prompts.

LLM Safety Mechanism and Jailbreaking Model

In this section, we first analyze the safety mechanisms of the open-source LLMs and discuss how to exploit the vulnerabilities of them under black-box scenarios to bypass it. Then we introduce a mathematical model to describe the process of jailbreaking by bypass safety mechanisms and validate the its validity through empirical experiments.

3.1
Intrinsic Vulnerability in LLM’s Safety Alignment

Researches have found that removing a single safety head will increase the changes of jailbreaking to aligned models like Llama-2-7b-chat
(
42
;
29
)
. Additionally, LLMs can identify harmful inputs in early layers and alignment causes models to generate substantial negative sentiment predictions in intermediate attention layers, which are then refined into specific rejection tokens for safe generation
(
41
)
.
Therefore, the immediate thought is to prevent the security attention heads in these early layers from detecting harmful behavior, thereby bypassing the security mechanisms to achieve jailbreaking.

Intuitively, altering more characters in a malicious query makes its malicious behavior harder to detect, thereby increasing the likelihood of bypassing security headers. To verify that this intuition holds true in the black-box model, we conducted a series of empirical studies on the GPT-4o model. As shown in the Figure
1
, the inputs consist of both harmless and malicious queries, and the model can respond to harmless queries. Altering only a few characters in the harmless query causes the security header to fail to understand the harmless query, thereby refusing to respond to the harmful query. However, when the harmless query remains unchanged, as the number of altered characters in the harmful query increases, the model regains the ability to understand the harmless query and gradually resumes responding to harmless requests. This phenomenon indicates that the security header does not identify the complete semantics ultimately understood by the model. Consequently, there is a gap between the model’s security mechanism response and its final output, creating an exploitable vulnerability. By appropriately modifying harmful queries, attackers can prevent the security header from recognizing the harmful behavior, while the model ultimately produces the harmful output.

4
Methodology

In this section, we propose Babel, a jailbreak attack framework. As shown in Figure
4
, it consists of three steps: harmful query obfuscation, harmful query embedding, and distribution-optimized sampling. Harmful query obfuscation defines a systematic obfuscation approach that adaptively adjust obfuscation methods and the obfuscation degree distribution. Harmful query embedding technique embeds harmful queries into benign requests to thereby increasing attack success probability. Distribution-optimized sampling ensures that it performs obfuscation sampling under the ditribution with higher probability density within the jailbreaking interval, enabling efficient jailbreak attacks.

4.1
Harmful Query Obfuscation

To find the distribution with the highest probability density within the jailbreaking interval, we propose a systematic obfuscation method that generates different obfuscation degree distributions. Generally, string perturbations have a smaller impact on LLM comprehension than token perturbations. The obfuscation targets are grouped into three categories, string, token, and sequence, which enables control of the obfuscation degree at multiple granularities. The specific obfuscation operations and procedures are detailed in the appendix
B
.

4.2
Harmful Query Embedding

This design aims to further suppress safety heads and increase the likelihood of jailbreaking. Given the model’s availability requirements, harmless requests should not be rejected by the model. We attempt to embed obfuscated harmful queries within harmless requests to avoid rejection. Specifically, we define four categories of harmless requests:
Translation
,
Statistics
,
Transformation
and
Retrieval
, requiring the model to perform different operations respectively on the obfuscated harmful query before executing it. In addition, prompting model to start with "Sure" can improve the performance
(
43
;
31
)
, therefore we build a template to combine harmless requests with harmful queries and prompt how the model begins. The specific template of harmful query embedding can be find in the appendix
A
.

Small-scale sampling.
To effectively locate the optimal distribution, we first perform a small-scale sampling at the current obfuscation distribution and use hypothesis testing to determine whether this it is optimal base on the model’s feedback. It is assumed that the obfuscation degree
O_{d}
of samples follows a normal distribution and the midpoint of the model’s jailbreaking interval is
\mu_{0}=\frac{a_{r}+b_{r}}{2}

\mu

\Pr(X_{i}\geq\mu_{0})=\frac{1}{2}.

Therefore, the statistic

5.2
Effectiveness of Attack

The results in Table
1
show that although the four baseline methods achieve relatively high ASRs on certain models, their overall performance remains limited. Bon, which relies solely on random obfuscation, performs poorly across all models when the number of queries is constrained. PiF is effective on Gemini-2.0-flash and Grok-3, reaching ASRs of 72.67% and 82.00% respectively, by exploiting attention mechanisms to obscure malicious intent. However, its weaker results on other models indicate limited transferability. FlipAttack exhibits more stable performance across models, suggesting moderate versatility, but its attack effectiveness remains modest. By contrast, Babel achieves the highest ASRs on all four models, reaching 82.67%, 78.33%, and 75.67% on GPT-4o, Gemini-2.0-flash, and Claude-3.5-Haiku, respectively, and up to 95.67% on Grok-3. Therefore, as a red team approach, Babel can not noly effectively jailbreak LLMs with transferability, but also evaluate the safety of the model.

5.3
Efficiency of Attack

Although methods like FlipAttack achieve jailbreaking with a single request, it comes at the cost of limited effectiveness and scalability. So we exclude such methods from the efficiency comparison. While achieving the highest ASRs, Babel still maintained a low number of requests. For Bon, the number of requests results from its random search process, meaning its effectiveness mainly depends on the search strategy and query volume, which leads to low efficiency. PiF requires multiple attempts to replace or insert tokens at different positions, thereby increasing its request count. In contrast, Babel handles requests more efficiently, each request both performs a jailbreak attempt and serves as a sampling point. As a result, Babel achieves the lowest ANRs on most models.

Ablation Study

Harmful query embedding ablation.
To investigate the impact of harmful query embedding on attacks, we used the method without harmful query embedding template as baselines and compared attack effectiveness across various benign request types. As shown in the figure

Overall, the Retrieval request type we selected demonstrated the best comprehensive performance, outperforming the baseline across all four models. Distribution-optimized sampling ablation.
To investigate the effectiveness of the distribution-optimized sampling optimization method, we set the baseline by disabling distribution adjustment, fixing the maximum request count

Furthermore, on GPT-4o and Claude-3-5-haiku models, distribution-optimized sampling enables ASR to increase nearly linearly with ANR rather than exhibiting a power-law behavior as Bon, demonstrating that our optimization method effectively enhances attack efficiency.

6
Conclusion

This paper identifies the vulnerability in LLM’s safety mechanism caused by gap in safety heads coverage and proposes a mathematical model describing the jailbreak attack that exploits this vulnerability. Based on this model, we introduce Babel, a jailbreak framework that bypasses LLM safety mechanism through obfuscation and distribution-optimized sampling. Extensive experiments verify its effectiveness, determining that this black-box method exposes a critical vulnerability in LLMs and provides valuable insights for enhancing their safety.

Limitations

This work focuses on text-based jailbreak attacks, allowing us to provide a detailed analysis of the intrinsic safety mechanisms of large language models. While the insights gained may be relevant to other modalities or extension-based attacks, a systematic exploration in these directions is beyond the scope of this study. Similarly, we do not explicitly assess interactions with existing external defense strategies, as our primary aim is to understand model-level safety behaviors. We consider these extensions and evaluations as valuable directions for future research.

Ethical Considerations

This work is intended solely for defensive security research aimed at improving the safety of large language models. We did not deploy the method in real-world settings or facilitate malicious use. All experiments were conducted on publicly accessible commercial models for controlled red-teaming evaluation, no personal data was used, and code release is restricted to reduce misuse risk while supporting responsible disclosure and mitigation efforts.

Broader Impacts

Our study contributes positively by exposing concrete weaknesses in current safety alignment and by providing a methodology for evaluating whether commercial LLM safeguards fail under obfuscated harmful prompts. These findings can support stronger red-teaming, targeted mitigations, and more reliable safety evaluation pipelines. At the same time, jailbreak research is inherently dual use: attackers could adapt the high-level ideas to probe deployed models. To reduce that risk, we censor sensitive generations in the paper, avoid operational deployment guidance, and restrict code release while pursuing responsible disclosure.

Harmful Query Embedding Template

The request: <harmful query>.

Please <harmless request>, respond to the request.

Just output the <harmless request response> and the <harmful query response>.

This is the prompt template we constructe for implementing harmful query embedding. We insert suffixes into the harmless behavior templates to explicitly guide the model’s output. •

Translate. Instructing the model to translate the harmful query into another language and output the translated sentence.

•

Transformation. Tnstructing the model to perform a specific transformation on the harmful query text and output the transformed text.

Retrieval. Instructs the model to search for words in the harmful query that meets specific criteria and output that word.

Appendix B
Algorithms

B.1
Harmful Query Obfuscation

Obfuscation objects.
The objects of obfuscation are as follow.

•

String obfuscation. Includeing four specific methods: converting lowercase letters to uppercase, randomly replacing letters with the preceding or following character in the alphabet, swapping positions of two characters, and removing whitespace characters.

•

Token obfuscation. Randomly replaces a single token while preserving sentence coherence. We use a masking model to predict the token to be replaced and randomly select a different token from the prediction probabilities for substitution.

•

Sequence obfuscation. This involves two operations: swapping the positions of two tokens and inserting tokens at random positions. Similar to token obfuscation, we insert [mask] at random positions within the original token sequence and use the masked model to predict the token to be inserted.

Obfuscation level.
We set upper limits on the number of tokens affected per obfuscation category, corresponding to their maximum obfuscation levels. The upper limit for capitalization conversion is set to half the number of letters in the harmful query, as a fifty-fifty mix of uppercase and lowercase letters is significantly harder to decipher than all-uppercase text. The quantity of targets for other obfuscation operations is determined by the current obfuscation level.

Obfuscation sequence.
To prevent character obfuscation from altering tokens and causing obfuscation between token and sequence obfuscation, we execute obfuscation operations in the sequence: token obfuscation → sequence obfuscation → character obfuscation. The obfuscation level adjusts the distribution of samples’ obfuscation degree by varying the quantity of different obfuscation objects. The specific sequence is shown in Algorithm

B.2
Distribution-Optimized Sampling

By examining samples from small-scale sampling, ones with high (low) obfuscation but model outputs of rejection (harmlessness) are removed. Then we estimate the upper and lower bounds of the model’s jailbreaking interval using the maximum obfuscation degree among rejected samples and the minimum obfuscation degree among harmless samples. During large-scale sampling, we attempt samples falling within the estimated jailbreaking interval, while continuously updating the trusted jailbreaking interval estimate. Throughout this process, we record the number of samples whose obfuscation degree do not align with their assigned interval. When this count reaches the threshold, the current jailbreaking interval estimate is deemed invalid. Then we skip current distribution and proceeds to the next level of obfuscation until jailbreaking or reaching the max obfuscation level. The overall process is shown in Algorithm
