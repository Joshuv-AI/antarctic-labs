Large Language Model-Enhanced Relational Operators: Taxonomy, Benchmark, and Analysis
Yunxiang Su 1,2,∗ , Tianjing Zeng 1,3,∗ , Zhongjun Ding 1 , Yin Lin 1 , Rong Zhu 1,# , Zhewei Wei 3 , Bolin Ding 1 , Jingren Zhou 1 1 Alibaba Group, 2 Tsinghua University, 3 Renmin University of China

Abstract
. With the development of large language models (LLMs), numerous studies integrate LLMs through operator-like components to enhance relational data processing tasks, e.g. , filters with semantic predicates, knowledge-augmented table imputation, reasoning-driven entity matching and more challenging semantic query processing. These components invoke LLMs while preserving a relational input/output interface, which we refer to as LLM-Enhanced Relational Operators (LROs) . From an operator perspective, unfortunately, these existing LROs suffer from fragmented definition, various implementation strategies and inadequate evaluation benchmarks. To this end, in this paper, we first establish a unified LRO taxonomy to align existing LROs, and categorize them into: Select , Match , Impute , Cluster and Order , along with their operands and implementation variants. Second, we design LroBench , a comprehensive benchmark featuring 290 single-LRO queries and 60 multi-LRO queries, spanning 27 databases across more than 10 domains. LroBench covers all operating logics and operand granularities in its single-LRO workload, and provides challenging multi-LRO queries stratified by query complexity. Based on these, we evaluate individual LROs under various implementations, deriving practical insights into LRO design choices and summarizing our empirical best practices. We further compare the end-to-end performance of existing multi-LRO systems against an LRO suite instantiated with these best practices, in order to investigate how to design an effective LRO set for multi-LRO systems targeting complex semantic queries. Last, to facilitate future work, we outline promising future directions and open-source all benchmark data and evaluation code, available at https://github.com/LROBench/LROBench/ .

2.
Background

2.1.
LLM-Enhanced Relational Operator

A conventional relational algebra operator processes database relations: it accepts one or two relations as input and produces a relation as output
(
14
)
.
We extend this notion to an LLM-enhanced relational operator (LRO),
defined as below.

Definition 0 (
L
LM-Enhanced
R
elational
O
perator,
LRO
).

An LLM-enhanced relational operator (LRO) takes as input one or two relations
\mathcal{R}=\{R\}
or
\{R_{1},R_{2}\}
, an operand granularity
g\in\{\textsf{cell},\textsf{row},\textsf{column},\textsf{table}\}
, and a natural-language requirement
l
.
It represents the input relation(s) as relational elements at granularity
g
,
invokes an LLM to derive a transformation guided by
l
, and materializes the result as an output relation
R^{\prime}
(possibly with a different schema). Formally, we define

\mathsf{LRO}(\mathcal{R},g,l)\mapsto R^{\prime}.

Notably, LROs are closely related to
semantic operators

(
26
)
,
as both leverage LLMs to capture and generate semantic information beyond purely syntactic relational processing.
However, they slightly differ in scope and design: semantic operators typically target heterogeneous data domains (
e.g.
, images or documents), whereas LROs focus strictly on relational data;
LROs further emphasize LLM-based reasoning, numerical computation and external knowledge retrieval within relational workflows,
and operate at multiple relational granularities (cells, rows, columns, or tables).

Example 0.

Figure
1
illustrates three LRO examples. In Figure
1
(a),
LLM_SELECT
takes as input one relation with restaurant records,
performs semantic schema linking (
i.e.
, selects the columns relevant to the restaurant atmosphere),
and outputs a relation whose schema is narrowed to include only the “Description” column.
In Figure
1
(b),
LLM_SELECT
filters rows in the relation by leveraging the LLM’s external knowledge to determine whether a restaurant is located in the Bay Area.
Then,
LLM_ORDER
applies the LLM’s semantic reasoning to rank the restaurants by their taste.

2.2.
Relevant Works on LROs

Numerous existing works have applied LLMs to relations.
In this paper, we focus on systems whose LLM-enhanced components fit the scope of LRO in Definition

Such components typically target data preparation (

Consequently, they are usually not designed to be composed with classical SQL operators or additional LROs.
For example, Figure

2.3.
Motivation and Roadmap

Existing LRO-related systems exhibit diverse operator patterns.
However, despite using different names,
certain operators across different systems share the same underlying operating logic.
For instance, both
NLJoin
in ThalamusDB
(
22
)
and
Merge
in DocETL
(
48
)
join relations by semantic comparison of join keys.
Moreover, operators with different operands may also follow the same logic.
For example, ComEM
(
55
)
can be cast as a standalone LRO for row-level entity matching, logically similar to cell-level join operators such as
NLJOIN
and
Merge
.
This raises our first question:
How many distinct operating logics underlie current LROs?
(
Q1
)

Furthermore, LROs with the same name, functionality and operand may be implemented under various strategies across systems.
For instance, both LOTUS-TAG
(
41
;
5
)
and BlendSQL
(
17
)
support semantic join operations.
However, LOTUS-TAG follows a nested-loop strategy that prompts one pair of values per LLM invocation,
whereas BlendSQL adopts a left-join strategy that prompts one left value together with all right values,
a.k.a.
, a semi-join.
These various implementations induce different prompt contexts and reasoning patterns,
which can affect the accuracy of LLM judgments.
Thus, we raise the second question:
How do different LRO implementations impact their performance?
(
Q2
)

Finally, while Q1–Q2 focus on individual LROs logics and implementations,
real-world applications, especially semantic query processing engines
(
26
)
, are supported by multi-LRO systems with an LRO set.
In such systems, designing a multi-LRO solution requires going beyond optimizing a single LRO in isolation,
and instead understanding how to incorporate multiple LROs into a coherent end-to-end system, each implemented using operator-level best practices.
This raises our third question:
How should multi-LRO systems be designed to incorporate operator-level best practices, and do existing systems do so effectively?
(
Q3
)

Roadmap.

For
Q1
,we develop a unified taxonomy of LRO operating logics to systematically categorize and align diverse LLM-enhanced operators (Section
3
).
For
Q2
, we build a comprehensive benchmark with single-LRO queries to evaluate individual LROs (Section
4
),
and compare various implementations of each LRO type (Section
5
).
For
Q3
, we extend the benchmark with multi-LRO queries (Section
4
) and evaluate current multi-LRO systems against our best practices derived from our taxonomy and operator-level insights (Section
6
).
Together, these results show that our taxonomy and implementation insights inform multi-LRO system design and yield measurable end-to-end gains.

To investigate
Q1
, we first generalize existing LROs in Section
2.2
and establish a unified taxonomy to categorize and align them.
Our taxonomy is organized along three dimensions to ensure its completeness and mutual exclusivity:
(1)
Operating Logic
that are consistent with relational algebra;
(2)
Operand Granularity
that encompasses cell, row, column and table as in Definition
1

; and (3)
Implementation Variant
,
i.e.
, the strategies used to realize an LRO that induce different prompt contexts and reasoning patterns.

Table
1
illustrates our proposed taxonomy along three dimensions.
We abstract operating logics into five mutually exclusive LRO categories (first column in Table
1
):

Select
,
Match
,
Impute
,
Cluster
, and
Order
.
Each category is further classified by operand granularity (second column),
where we list common combinations and omit rare ones (
e.g.

Select
+ Cell,
Match
+ Table).
We provide a brief description of each LRO (third column) and align all the LROs reviewed in Section

to our taxonomy (fourth column).
Implementations are illustrated using colored rectangles,

Select
.

The LLM-enhanced
Select
operator extends traditional SQL’s
SELECT
(row filtering) and
PROJECTION
(column selection) by retrieving subsets of data elements satisfying semantic conditions. Formally, we have

Definition 0 (
Select
).

Given a relation
R
, an operand granularity
g\in\{\textsf{row},\textsf{column},\textsf{table}\}
and a natural-language requirement
l
,

Select
returns the subset of relational objects at granularity
g
that satisfy
l
, i.e.,

\textnormal{{Select}}(R,g,l)=\{\,e\in E_{g}(R)\mid e\models l\,\},

where
E_{g}(R)
denotes the set of relational objects obtained from
R
at granularity
g
; the notation is used throughout.

The functionality of the
Select
operator varies by operand granularity. Row-wise selection (
e.g.
,
sem_filter
in LOTUS-TAG
(
41
;
5
)
) filters records.
For example, as shown in Figure
2
,
selecting “technical companies” from Table (b) returns Microsoft and Google. Column-wise and table-wise selection identifies relevant columns or tables, respectively.
For instance, table-wise selection for “company CEO” retrieves Table (b), and column-wise selection further locates the “CEO” column.

There are mainly two implementations of
Select
:
(1)
LLM-ALL
uses a batched strategy, that packs all candidates in
E_{g}(R)
into a single prompt, submits it to the LLM, and invokes the LLM to directly return the filtered elements.
Currently, only BlendSQL
(
17
)
applies this strategy.
(2)
LLM-ONE
, widely applied in current works
(
3
;
22
;
34
;
47
;
48
;
41
)
,
uses a element-wise strategy
that individually packs each candidate in
E_{g}(R)
, queries the LLM with the semantic condition
l
, and returns a Boolean value (true/false).
After traversing all candidates, elements labeled true are retained.

.

Match
.

The enhanced
Match
operator generalizes SQL’s
JOIN
operation beyond value-based matching to establish a semantical matching relationship between two sets of elements (
e.g.
, cells, rows and columns). Formally, we have,

Definition 0 (
Match
).

Given two relations
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, an operand granularity
g\in\{\textsf{cell},\textsf{row},\textsf{column}\}
and a natural-language requirement
l
,

Match
returns the set of object pairs at granularity
g
that satisfy
l
, i.e.,

\textnormal{{Match}}(R_{1},R_{2},g,l)=\{\,(e_{1},e_{2})\in E_{g}(R_{1})\times E_{g}(R_{2})\mid(e_{1},e_{2})\models l\,\}.

Similarly, the functionality of
Match
also varies by operand types. Cell-wise matching enables semantic table joining (
e.g.
,
LLMJoin
in BlendSQL
(
17
)
).
In Figure
2
, Tables (a) and (b) can be joined on the condition “the Company Name in Table (a) matches the Enterprise in Table (b)” despite lacking primary-foreign keys.
Row-wise matching identifies the same real-world entities,
e.g.
, entity resolution in DataWrangle
(
39
)
.
Column-wise matching detects semantically joinable columns across tables (

schema matching in DataWrangle), exemplified by linking “Company Name” column in Table (a) and “Enterprise” column in Table (b).

Definition 0 (

Given a relation

Given the LRO taxonomy in Section

4.1.
Design Principles

Aligning with our scope
Q1
-
Q3
in Section
2.3
,
and the goal of benchmarking the LLM-enhanced relational operators defined in Definition
1
,
we establish the following design principles:

(1)
Native to Relations
:
All benchmark queries should center on relational data,
involve LLM-enhanced relational operators (
i.e.
,
Select
,
Match
,
Impute
,
Cluster
and
Order
),
and operate on relational operands (
i.e.
, cells, rows, columns and tables).

(2)
Diversity of Queries
:
The benchmark should span diverse LRO types, various operands, multiple data domains.
The benchmark queries should be constructed to cover a broad range of required LLM capabilities (
e.g.
, numerical calculation, world knowledge, semantic reasoning).

(3)
Single-LRO Queries with Full Coverage
(to address
Q2
):
For each LRO type and operand in Table
1
, the benchmark should respectively include a set of single-LRO queries,
enabling fine-grained evaluation and implementation comparison at the operator level.

(4)
Multi-LRO Queries with Stratification
(to resolve
Q3
):
The benchmark should include more challenging multi-LRO queries that require composing multiple LROs within a task,
enabling end-to-end evaluation of multi-LRO systems.
Moreover, these multi-LRO queries should be stratified into difficulty levels (
e.g.
, by hops, LRO numbers) to reveal the capability limits of systems.

4.3.1.
Data Collection

Since LROs are primarily applied to real-world databases with diverse schemas and abundant semantic attributes, we curate our benchmark from such databases.
Specifically, we collect 27 databases from open-source benchmarks and repositories,
with 20 databases from BIRD
(
31
)

These databases span over 10 real-world domains, including sports, movies, software, education, animation, food, and music.

4.3.2.
Query Construction

Following the annotation protocol of BIRD
(
31
)
, three experienced database experts from industry were invited to author and annotate a corpus of benchmark queries.
Each query contains three components:
a declarative question, a corresponding procedural plan, and a ground-truth result
.
The query corpus was split evenly among the three experts.
For each query authored by one expert, the other two experts independently reviewed the question and the procedural plan, and verified the ground-truth result.
Annotations were accepted only after this cross-verification.
Disagreements triggered further examination and revision until consensus was reached.
We manually annotate and verify ground truth instead of relying on LLM outputs.
To keep verification tractable, we limit the sizes of the input tables used in LRO tasks.

As in Section
4.1
, we design two types of queries to support
Q2
and
Q3
.
To study how different implementations affect the same LRO (
Q2
), experts construct
290 Single-LRO Queries
(Section
4.3.3
) that isolate one target LRO.
Moreover, to study how an end-to-end multi-LRO system should incorporate a suite of LROs with operator-level best practices,
and how existing systems perform (
Q3
), experts construct
60 Multi-LRO Queries
(Section
4.3.4
) that require multiple LROs to solve a more challenging task.

To ensure benchmark validity, experienced database experts were instructed to design and refine queries by drawing analogies to real-world workloads.
For example, some queries join “

It is a common practice (also adopted in TAG benchmark

5.2.
Scalability Evaluation

While effectiveness is critical, real-world LRO query tasks may often involve large-scale inputs,
making scalability a key practical concern.
We conduct scalability experiments on two most prevalent tasks for large-scale data: row-wise
Select
for semantic filtering in Figure
6
,
and column-wise
Impute
for augmentation in Figure
7
.
Similar to Sections
5.1.1
and
5.1.3

LLM-ALL
and
LLM-ONE

All experiments run on a “european_football_2” database from BIRD
(
31

with more than 10k records.
Experiments are repeated 10 times under GPT-5 to capture variations (with results shown as box plots),
and each run has a 30-minute timeout.
As ground truth for semantic filtering or imputation over large tables is hard to obtain,
we design two queries whose ground truth can be determined equivalently by deterministic rules:
(i)
“Identify the players born after the fall of the Berlin Wall”
for
Select
,
and (ii)
“Determine the zodiac sign for each player based on their date of birth”
for
Impute
.

The
LLM-ALL
methods degrade rapidly in accuracy and stability,
and fail at relatively small scales (2k–10k tuples) despite input token counts remaining much below the 400k token limit of GPT-5.
At this point, outputs become malformed or syntactically invalid, indicating a breakdown in structured generation.
This reveals a critical gap:

the effective context boundary for reliable reasoning is far smaller than the theoretical maximum
.
We hypothesize that long prompts overload the LLM’s attention modules,
diluting attention on key conditions and disrupting coherent response formation,
even when technically “within limits”.

I6
:

Limited Effective Context Window of
LLM-ALL
.

Although within token limit, LLMs may fail to maintain structured generation under large-scale inputs due to attention dilution.

In contrast,
LLM-ONE

performs fine-grained reasoning and consistently maintains high quality (
e.g.
, F1
=1.0
on
Select
) until it hits the timeout limit, but it incurs higher inference costs than
LLM-ALL
.

As discussed in Section
3
,

and

represent two extreme design points:

However, Figures

This suggests that: these exists a hybrid implementation between the two design extremes (

,

For other base model, we can characterize an LRO design by a model-specific quality–cost curve, with token number on the x-axis and result quality on the y-axis, to find the best balance point. Such detailed explorations are reserved in our future work.

I7

LRO design entails a trade-off that can be cast as optimizing over a model-specific quality–cost curve. Smart batching strategies and fine-tuned LRO implementations integrating

6.2.

6.2.1.
Overall Performance Analysis

Table
11
reports the overall performance of multi-LRO baselines on our multi-LRO query set.
Overall, LLM-planning systems perform poorly (all below 15% accuracy), substantially lagging behind manual-planning systems.
This gap is largely due to failures in the initial planning step, which yield flawed query plans and doom the pipeline to incorrect results.
Even with expert-annotated plans, however, most manual-planning systems (except Palimpzest) still achieve limited accuracy (below 55%).
In contrast, our best-practice approach achieves the best results across all three base models, peaking at 86.67% with GPT-5.

Table

also reports GPT-5 accuracy by difficulty, with 17 easy, 33 medium, and 10 hard queries. Overall, current systems struggle on hard queries.
Notably, no existing LLM-planning system solves any hard query (all 0% accuracy), indicating substantial room for improvement in automatic LRO planning.
Even manual-planning systems, despite using annotated plans,
solve at most 3 of the 10 hard queries and about half of the medium queries.
In contrast, our best-practice baseline achieves 100%, 81.82%, and 80% accuracy on easy, medium, and hard queries, respectively,
due to its full LRO coverage and near-optimal LRO implementations.

I8
:

Early-Stage and Room for Improvement.

Current LLM-planning systems perform poorly on our benchmark (
<
15% accuracy) and fail on complex queries,
as unreliable initial plans derail downstream steps.
Even with expert-annotated plans, most manual-planning systems (except Palimpzest) remain below 55%.
Overall, automatic LRO planning remains early-stage and multi-LRO systems still have substantial room for improvement.

I9
:

Best Practices as Guidelines.

Our best-practice baseline consistently performs best and solves up to 80% of hard queries,
suggesting it may serve as a useful guideline for future systems.

6.2.2.
Performance
vs.
LRO Coverage and Design

We next analyze how LRO coverage and LRO design affect the overall performace.
Consistent with Table
10
,
systems that support broader coverage of our LRO taxonomy and cover more queries generally achieve higher accuracy.
Among LLM-planning systems, Binder and Aryn-Scyamore have the lowest coverage and perform poorly,
whereas SUQL covers 75% of the queries and achieves correspondingly higher accuracy.
Among manual-planning systems, BlendSQL, Palimpzest, and LOTUS-TAG cover all queries in our benchmark and therefore exceed 50% peak accuracy.
DocETL also covers all queries but peaks at around 40%.
In contrast, HQDL and ThalamusDB respectively covers only 75% and 41.7% of the queries, limiting their peak accuracy to around 30%.

How LROs are implemented also matters.
Systems whose LRO implementations more closely follow our best-practice implementations tend to perform better, such as Palimpzest and LOTUS-TAG.
In addition, they both incorporate chain-of-thought reasoning, and Palimpzest also uses a more effective prompt with detailed instructions.
These design choices contribute to Palimpzest’s strong performance (70% accuracy with Qwen3-Max), approaching our best-practice results
but at substantially higher cost (2–5
\times
compared to our best-practice approach).
In contrast, HQDL relies on a single general-purpose LRO
Generate
to handle all tasks, capping its accuracy at around 30%.
HQDL also incurs substantially higher cost (about 10
\times
than our best-practice approach),
suggesting that an overly general, one-fits-all LRO is neither effective nor cost-efficient.
Last, despite limited query coverage (41.7%) and LRO coverage (2 LROs),
ThalamusDB implements them following our best practices (
i.e.
,
LLM-ONE
for
NLFILTER
and
LLM-ALL
for
NLJOIN
) and reaches 30% peak accuracy.
This is comparable to HQDL, despite HQDL’s higher query coverage (75%), highlighting that strong LRO implementations can partially compensate for limited coverage.

Our best-practice baseline, with both comprehensive LRO coverage and experimentally validated LRO designs,
achieves the best performance across all base models, further demonstrating the importance of LRO support and design.

I10
:

LRO Support and Design Effects.

Systems with broader coverage of our LRO taxonomy tend to achieve better performance.
Moreover, LRO implementations that follow our best practices often perform better, with CoT and instruction-rich prompts providing further gains.
In contrast, overly general, one-fits-all LROs are neither effective nor cost-efficient.

6.2.3.
Performance
vs.
Base Model

Base model choice also matters, but no single model always outperforms.
Due to different LRO implementation strategies, prompt styles, and context designs, systems exhibit various preferences for base models.
GPT-5 performs best for SUQL, ThalamusDB, BlendSQL, LOTUS-TAG, and our best-practice baseline,
while Qwen3-Max is more suitable for Palimpzest,
and Claude-Sonnet-4.5 works best for HQDL and DocETL.
Besides performance, different base model choices also impact the monetary costs.
Claude-Sonnet-4.5 is more expensive, due to higher per-token pricing and greater reasoning token usage,
whereas Qwen3-Max is cheaper with lower per-token pricing.

I11
:

Base Model Effects.

Different base models substantially affect system performance and monetary cost.
However, higher-priced models are not necessarily better, and no single model is universally best across all systems.

Conclusion and Outlook

In this paper, we establish the first unified taxonomy for LLM-enhanced relational operators,
along three dimensions: operating logic, operand granularity, and implementation variant.
Based on the taxonomy, we design a comprehensive benchmark
LroBench
,
spanning diverse real-world domains, with both single- and multi-LRO workloads.
We conduct evaluation on individual LROs and multi-LRO systems, deriving practical insights.
For individual LROs, we derive concrete LRO design choices (

-

) and design trade-off (

).
For systems, we highlight their early-stage landscape (

) and our best-practice guidelines (

),
and identify key performance factors: LRO support, LRO design, base model (

).

These insights suggest the need for a more principled design of LROs and their supporting systems.
In the short term,
we expect LRO systems with:
comprehensive LRO sets,
robust LLM planning via reinforcement learning or prompt fine-tuning,
joint optimization of classical SQL and enhanced operators,
and optimal LRO implementations with quality–cost trade-offs.

In the long term, we envision a new paradigm:
LLM-centric databases
,
where databases are rebuilt around LLM-native operators rather than treating LLMs as external plugins.
Such systems may integrate:
(1) Multi-modal unified storage, enabling native management of heterogeneous data.
(2) Semantic-aware indexing, supporting true semantic retrieval beyond traditional indexing or vanilla RAG mechanisms.
(3) Adaptive context management,organizing relevant data into dynamically constructed prompt contexts.
(4) Holistic system-level optimization,
jointly orchestrating storage, indexing, planning, and model invocation.
At the core of this paradigm are LROs, which serve as the abstraction layer that embeds LLM capabilities into query processing.
By systematically formalizing, evaluating and benchmarking LROs,
this work establishes the first unified LRO taxonomy, a comprehensive benchmark
LroBench
and empirical best-practice LRO designs to facilitate future works and systems.
