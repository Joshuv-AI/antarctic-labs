Project Overview
Training and running artificial intelligence (AI) models take large amounts of computing time,
and that time translates into energy use, a fundamental concern for the Department of Energy
(DOE). At Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL), large scientific datasets and models
make training especially costly, and new "reasoning" models and AI agents spend more
compute per answer on increasingly many applications, so we developed methods that remove
redundant data, model components, and computation. These methods halved the computing
time, and so roughly the energy, needed to train a math-reasoning model versus a leading
approach, with essentially no loss of accuracy; beat standard chatbot training's best quality in a
fifth of the training steps, using a technique now in widely used open-source AI software;
generated text up to seven times faster; let a model think longer without writing out extra
words; and found low-cost ways to make image-and-text AI models resist deliberately
manipulated images. Published at leading AI conferences and released openly, our methods
help LLNL and others build and deploy capable AI with less computing time and energy.

Mission Impact
Our project gives LLNL faster, cheaper ways to train and deploy AI on its supercomputers,
including El Capitan, supporting the National Nuclear Security Administration's mission to
develop science and technology tools and capabilities to meet future national security
challenges; by cutting the computing time, and so the electricity, that AI needs, it also helps
address DOE's energy and environmental security missions. Our methods make AI models
resistant to manipulated inputs and guard AI systems with automated safeguards, helping
explore solutions to emerging security challenges associated with technology surprise in
cyberspace, which matters to homeland-security and defense agencies. Several of our methods
and training recipes are used in other Laboratory projects, one is built into widely used open-
source AI software, and our work led to a follow-on pilot project funded by the DOE Office of
Science, brought several interns to the Laboratory, and expanded collaborations with
universities and industry.
