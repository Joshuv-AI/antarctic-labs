Efficacy and safety of propylthiouracil protocols: high-dose monotherapy vs combination of low-dose with iodine
Authors: Nami Suzuki, Jaeduk Yoshimura Noh, Natsuko Watanabe, Ai Yoshihara, Miho Fukushita, Masako Matsumoto, Masakazu Koshibu, Miki Kawai, Aiko Kitao, Kiminori Sugino, Koichi Ito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPropylthiouracil (PTU) use has decreased due to adverse events (AEs), but it is still needed for patients who cannot tolerate thiamazole.ObjectiveTo compare the efficacy and safety of the PTU protocol between high-dose monotherapy and low-dose combination therapy with potassium iodide (KI).MethodsThis is a retrospective observational study including patients who were newly diagnosed with Graves disease (GD) and received PTU as initial therapy between January 2005 and October 2024. Patients who received an initial dose of 300 mg (high-dose group) and those who received 150 mg of PTU combined with 50 mg of KI (low-dose group) were included. The propensity-score matching method was employed to adjust for confounders.ResultsA total of 1171 patients (high-dose: low-dose = 1133: 38, all female) with a median age of 32 (interquartile range [IQR]: 28-36) years were included. Median levels of free-thyroxine (FT4) and thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor antibody were 4.97 (IQR: 3.67-7.31) ng/dL, 12.4 (IQR: 6.4-26.8) IU/L. Adverse events occurred in 294 patients (high-dose: low-dose = 289:5). Free-thyroxine levels were significantly lower at Week 2 in the low-dose group than in the high-dose group (P = .014), but the difference disappeared afterwards. The prevalence of inadequate efficacy with the initial dose was significantly higher in the low-dose group (12.8% vs 35.5%, P = .002).ConclusionDespite a lower rate of efficacy from the initial dose, the low-dose protocol was effective in controlling hyperthyroidism with up-titration in young women with GD, with the potential for fewer AEs.Keywords: Graves disease, propylthiouracil, potassium iodide, anti-thyroid drugs, adverse events

Propylthiouracil (PTU) was the first medication developed for the treatment of Graves disease (GD) more than 7 decades ago, in the 1940s, followed by methimazole (MMI) a few years later [1]. To this day, there are only 3 types of anti-thyroid medications (ATDs) that are commercially available worldwide for GD: PTU, MMI, and carbimazole (CBZ), which is a pro-drug of MMI [2, 3]. Drug-related adverse events (AEs) are the most concerning issue while patients are on ATDs. The most common AEs associated with the use of ATDs are drug-induced liver injury (DILI), agranulocytosis, and urticaria. Even though the incidences are rare for DILI and agranulocytosis, they could be fatal if not identified and treated immediately and properly [4]. In 2011, the US Food and Drug Administration issued a boxed warning that the DILI caused by PTU is a worrisome problem, and that the use of PTU should be restricted to only patients who cannot tolerate MMI, particularly in children and adolescents. As a result, a 2023 global survey reported that only 0.9% of patients with GD were treated with PTU, whereas 84% and 14% were treated with MMI and CBZ, respectively [1]. We previously reported that PTU has a significantly higher incidence of DILI than MMI [5], and that both PTU and MMI have an increased risk of agranulocytosis in a dose-dependent manner, with a significantly higher incidence of agranulocytosis with PTU than with MMI at the same potency [6]. However, PTU is still needed for patients who cannot tolerate or for whom MMI and CBZ are contraindicated, such as patients in the first trimester of pregnancy [2] or those having severe AEs with MMI or CBZ. In addition, our previous report showed that the potassium iodine (KI) combination protocol with lower dose MMI had a lower incidence rate of AEs and required a shorter time to achieve normal thyroid hormone levels [7]. Therefore, a retrospective, observational study was conducted to evaluate the efficacy and safety of a treatment protocol of low-dose (150 mg) PTU combined with KI (50 mg) compared with classic high-dose (300 mg) PTU as initial treatment. The aims of the study were (1) to compare the incidence of AEs due to PTU between the protocols and (2) to compare the efficacy of the protocols.

Materials and methods

Participant

This was a retrospective, single-center, observational study that included patients with newly diagnosed moderate to severe GD who were initially treated with PTU between January 2005 and October 2024 at Ito Hospital. The diagnosis of GD was made based on either the presence of thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor antibody (TRAb)-positive hyperthyroidism or confirmation of hyperthyroidism with diffuse high uptake in the thyroid by radioisotope scan. The treatment strategy, including the dose of ATDs, was determined based on the Japanese guidelines for the management of GD [8]. The initial treatment dosage of PTU 300 mg (high-dose group) or 150 mg combined with 50 mg of KI (low-dose group) was prescribed for patients with moderate to severe hyperthyroidism. The low-dose protocol was implemented in 2010, following the results of our previous study [7]. Exclusion criteria were a history of treatment for GD, current pregnancy, having switched from MMI, taking steroids for any reason, whose initial dose of PTU was neither 150 mg nor 300 mg, or whose follow-up time was <3 months. Patients who had poor medication adherence or who developed AEs with PTU and discontinued PTU were also excluded from the efficacy evaluation.

Treatment protocol

The PTU dose was titrated by 50 mg once the free thyroxine (FT4) level reached reference ranges for both protocols. When FT4 levels did not decrease or reach the normal range with the initial dose, patients required an increase in the PTU dose by 50 mg. For patients treated with low-dose PTU combined with the KI protocol, KI was titrated first; once the FT4 level reached the reference range, the PTU dose was titrated after KI discontinuation. Potassium iodine is a prescription medicine in Japan that contains 38 mg of inorganic iodide in a pill. Because it must be stored in a dry, dark place once the package is opened, KI was titrated by decreasing the frequency of KI administration. Starting with a daily dose of 38 mg, decrease the frequency to every other day (daily dose of 19 mg of inorganic iodine), and then to twice a week (daily dose of 11 mg of inorganic iodine) before discontinuation. Following the Japanese guidelines for management of GD [8], total bilirubin, liver enzymes, and white blood cell counts with differentials were regularly measured at every visit for the detection of DILI and agranulocytosis.

Primary and secondary outcomes

Patients were evaluated at baseline and 2, 4, 6, and 8 weeks after starting treatment. The primary outcome was the incidence rate of AEs caused by the different dosages of PTU. In terms of safety evaluation, AEs of ATD that frequently occur within the first 3 months [9, 10], such as DILI, granulocytopenia (neutrophils <1000/µL), agranulocytosis (neutrophils <500/µL), drug eruption, arthralgia, and fever, were evaluated at each visit. The secondary outcome was the comparison of the efficacy of the protocols, which compared the changes in FT4 levels between the protocols. For efficacy evaluation, the time to FT4 normalization, the prevalence of achieving a normal FT4 level within 4 and 8 weeks after ATD initiation, and the prevalence of being unable to achieve FT4 normalization with the initial dose were evaluated in each protocol group.

Thyroid volume measurement

Ultrasonographic measurements were performed by skilled technicians who routinely conducted these examinations. Thyroid volume was calculated by measuring the length, width, and depth (mm) of each lobe of the thyroid gland using ultrasonography and putting these numbers into the following equation: thyroid volume = ([0.7365 × right lobe length × width × depth] +[0.7412 × left lobe length × width × depth]) – 0.55 [11].

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ito Hospital (approval # 473) in May 2025, and it was confirmed that this research was completed following the Declaration of Helsinki as revised in 2024. The Ethics Committee approved an opt-out consent method, and the opt-out document was made public on our institution's website immediately after approval.

Laboratory methods

Free triiodothyronine (FT3) and FT4 measurements were performed using an ECLusys FT3 (Cat# 07027362, RRID: AB_2925177) and an ECLusys FT4 (Cat# 09043284190, RRID: AB_3095310), the electrochemiluminescence Immunoassay (ECLIA) testing kit (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) for the entire study period, which provides high analytical sensitivity and a wide measurement range. The ECLusys testing kit is the product name of the Elecsys testing kit (Roche Diagnostics) in Japan. The reference ranges for each item were: FT3 2.2-4.3 pg/mL and FT4 0.8-1.6 ng/dL. Thyroid stimulating hormone receptor antibody was measured using the TRAb CT kit (Cat# TCT/60, RRID: AB_3095551; cutoff value <10%) between January 2005 and July 2008 and using the ECLusys TRAb kit (Elecsys Anti-TSHR) (Cat# 08496609190, RRID: AB_3696448; cutoff value <2.0 IU/L) from October 2008 onward. The formula that was calculated by the receiver-operating curve analysis to convert the TRAb CT value to the TRAb ECLusys value was used. For statistical analysis, TRAb values above and below the cutoff levels were assigned specific values: TRAb values <0.8 IU/L were recorded as 0.8 IU/L, and TRAb values >40 IU/L were recorded as 40.0 IU/L. Anti-thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb) and antithyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb) were also measured using the ECLusys Anti-Tg (Cat# 07026919190, RRID: AB_2894922) and Anti-TPO (Cat# 06368590190, RRID: AB_2916057), the ECLIA testing kit, for the entire study period. The cutoff values were TgAb <40 IU/mL and TPOAb <28 IU/mL.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. On univariate analysis, Wilcoxon's rank-sum test was used to compare continuous variables, and Fisher's exact test was used to compare categorical variables.

The propensity score for each case was calculated using a generalized linear model with EZR, based on age, FT3, FT4, and TRAb levels. Because the initial PTU dose was primarily determined based on the biochemical severity of hyperthyroidism, other baseline characteristics, such as the presence of thyroid eye disease, thyroid volume, body weight, and heart rate, were not included in the propensity score calculation. A paired case with the most similar propensity score was selected from the low-dose and high-dose groups (the nearest neighbor method). If the difference in the propensity score between 2 selected cases was <0.05, the pair was included. The pairs were matched in a ratio of 1:4, without duplication. As a model sensitivity analysis, we constructed additional models based on age, FT3, FT4, TRAb levels, and thyroid volumes (Model 1), as well as a second model based on the same variables as the original model (age, FT3, FT4, and TRAb levels) that included those who developed AEs using data up to the time of PTU discontinuation (Model 2).

All analyses were conducted using JMP version 14.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and EZR version 4.3.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing). Missing data were treated as missing.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

The participants consisted of 1171 (all female) patients with a median age of 32 (IQR: 28-36) years and a median follow-up of 92.9 (IQR: 46.4-154.9) months. The median initial values of FT3, FT4, and TRAb were 17.2 (IQR: 12.3-24.2) pg/mL, 4.96 (IQR: 3.67-7.3) ng/dL, and 12.4 (IQR: 6.3-26.7) IU/L, respectively (Table 1). Fourteen patients were TRAb negative, 2 of them were thyroid-stimulating antibody positive, and the remaining 12 patients were diagnosed with RAIU. Of the 1171 patients, 1133 were initially treated with the high-dose PTU protocol, whereas 38 were treated with the low-dose PTU protocol.

Primary outcome

A total of 294 (25.1%) AEs were reported, and the patients were unable to continue PTU therapy. The median time to AE development was 27 (IQR: 14-42) days. The details of the AEs were as follows: DILI 179, drug eruption 69, granulocytopenia 20, drug fever 14, agranulocytosis 7, and arthralgia 5 (Table 2). The incidence rate of AEs in the high-dose group was 25.5% (289 patients), and that in the low-dose group was 13.2% (5 patients). There was no significant difference between the groups (P = .08); however, agranulocytosis and granulocytopenia, one of the most severe life-threatening AEs, occurred only in the high-dose group. When we conducted the same analysis after excluding patients with poor medication adherence, the results remained consistent with those of the original analysis (Table S1), and no statistically significant difference in the incidence of AEs was observed between the groups (P = .12).

Secondary outcome

For the efficacy evaluation of the low-dose protocol, 39 patients with poor medication adherence and 294 patients with AEs were excluded (Fig. 1). Propensity score-matched pairs were identified from the remaining 838 patients (31 in the low-dose group and 807 in the high-dose group), resulting in 124 patients from the high-dose group and 31 patients from the low-dose group. The baseline characteristics of the propensity score-matched pairs are shown in Table 3. Before propensity score matching, the standardized mean difference (SMD) for age was higher than 0.1 (Table 3). However, after the match with a caliper width of 0.05, with a 1:4 ratio, using the nearest neighbor matching method, the differences between the groups were balanced since the SMD of all confounders became <0.1 (Table 3).

FT4 normalization time

The median time to FT4 level normalization was 45 (IQR: 29-70) days in the high-dose group and 35 (IQR: 26-91) days in the low-dose group, with no significant difference (P = .24). The levels of FT4 measured every 2 weeks from PTU initiation were compared between the high-dose and low-dose groups (Fig. 2A and Table 4). The median FT4 level in Week 2 was significantly lower in the low-dose group than in the high-dose group; however, there were no significant differences in Weeks 4, 6, and 8 between the groups. Free-thyroxine normalization achievement rates at Week 4 were 24.2% (30 patients) in the high-dose group and 35.3% (11 patients) in the low-dose group (P = .26), and those at Week 8 were 66.1% (82 patients) in the high-dose group and 67.7% (21 patients) in the low-dose group (P = .80) (Fig. 2B). When we constructed additional models adjusted for other covariates for sensitivity analysis, such as thyroid volume (Model 1), and models that included patients who developed AEs using data up to the time of PTU discontinuation (Model 2), the results were consistent with those of the original model (Tables S2 and S3, Fig. S1) [12]. However, these adjustments did not improve covariate balance.

Insufficient efficacy with the initial dose

Among the 1171 patients, 118 (10.1%) required an increase in PTU dose; 107 (9.4%) were in the high-dose group (1133 patients); and 11 (29.0%) were in the low-dose group (38 patients). When comparing the percentage of patients who did not achieve sufficient efficacy with the initial dose in the matched-pair groups, the proportion was 10.5% (13 patients) in the high-dose group and 35.5% (11 patients) in the low-dose group. The prevalence was significantly higher in the low-dose group (P = .002). Of note, 4 patients developed AEs after an increase in the dose of PTU, and all of them were in the high-dose group.

Discussion

In this retrospective observational study, low-dose PTU in combination with the KI protocol showed an immediate effect on managing hyperthyroidism and fewer AEs in female patients. The rapid effect on decreasing FT4 levels is due to KI, which inhibits thyroid hormone secretion from thyrocytes [13-15] and transiently inhibits the organification of iodine and thyroid hormone synthesis (Wolff–Chaikoff effect) [16]. These mechanisms were proven in normal thyrocytes, but whether the same mechanisms apply to thyrocytes in GD patients is not fully understood. Uchida et al [17] demonstrated in a mouse model of GD that a long-term iodine load suppressed iodine uptake, oxidation, and iodothyronine synthesis, in addition to suppressing hormone secretion that causes the accumulation of T4 in thyrocytes and a decrease in serum thyroid hormone levels. Nagataki et al [18] demonstrated in their clinical study that administering 30 mg of iodine to patients with GD suppressed thyroid iodine uptake and oxidation, increased the T4 level in the thyroid, and increased the thyroidal absolute uptake (AIU). AIU is calculated from the thyroidal clearance of 131I multiplied by serum 127I, and its increase was the opposite result of that seen in the Wolff–Chaikoff effect [7]. These results suggest that the mechanism by which long-term iodine administration decreases serum thyroid hormone levels in GD may differ from the Wolff–Chaikoff effect; however, further study is needed to elucidate these mechanisms in greater detail.

Although the KI combination protocol showed fewer AEs and quicker effect, insufficient efficacy with the initial dose of PTU (150 mg) was seen in 35% of the present patients, requiring an increase in the PTU dose. This insufficiency may not be due to the escape from the Wolff–Chaikoff effect, but an insufficient dose of PTU, since the FT4 levels in Weeks 4, 6, and 8 were not significantly different from those in the high-dose PTU monotherapy group. Therefore, the initial dose of PTU should be reconsidered. Our previous study [6] demonstrated a dose-dependent increase in the incidence of agranulocytosis with PTU. Its incidence with 150 mg was the same as that with 200 mg of PTU, suggesting that 200 mg of PTU may be an option for the initial dose in the combination protocol with KI.

The efficacy of KI in ameliorating thyroid hormone levels is usually limited for a few weeks to months in about 70% of patients [19-21]. Therefore, the guidelines for the management of GD from the American Thyroid Association (ATA) and the European Thyroid Association (ETA) recommend using it for specific situations, such as before surgery [2, 3] and thyroid storm [2]. This strategy was based on a report from Plummer et al in 1923, who used 100-400 mg of iodine to prevent patients with GD from postoperative death. Following this report, Thompson et al demonstrated the efficacy of iodine in controlling hyperthyroidism, using smaller amounts of 6 mg daily to achieve euthyroidism. In 1962, Volpe et al reviewed the previous studies focused on the efficacy of a small amount of iodine, along with their own study, which concluded that administration of 1 mg of iodine could decrease thyroid hormone levels in patients with hyperthyroidism within 48 hours [19]. However, KI is used to treat GD in Japan as a regular medication for its rapid effect and rare AE incidence; as such, the Japanese guidelines for the management of GD recommend the use of KI for severe hyperthyroidism [8]. It is usually used in a combination protocol with ATD [7], or monotherapy as an alternative medicine for ATD when AEs develop [22], or sometimes as an initial therapy for mild GD patients [23-28]. KI monotherapy was successful in controlling thyroid hormone levels in about 50-60% [25, 28], and more than 70% of patients treated with KI monotherapy achieved normal FT4 levels within 2 weeks of KI initiation [28]. However, the remaining 40-50% did not achieve successful hormone control, resulting in a re-elevation of thyroid hormones, mostly within 6 months after starting KI [25]. Recently, a case series of 7 patients with GD who were successfully treated with iodine as an alternative therapy for ATD or as a bridge therapy until surgery was published from the United States [29]. In one of the cases, the dose of PTU could be reduced from 600 to 250 mg after adding KI. These results suggest that KI could be used as a first-line treatment when combined with ATD, not only in Japan, but at least in the US, although some concerns regarding excess iodine exposure should be taken into account, specifically for vulnerable individuals, including patients with autoimmune thyroid diseases [30].

The use of PTU as an initial treatment for GD is less frequent than that of MMI [1], due to the once-daily schedule of MMI [4] and the lower risks of AEs with MMI than with PTU [2, 4]. However, when it comes to pregnancy, particularly in the first trimester, MMI should be avoided. Birth defects due to ATDs were reported from several countries [9, 31-34], and it has been demonstrated that MMI has a higher incidence of inducing specific birth defects, such as aplasia cutis congenital, omphalocele, omphalomesenteric duct anomaly, and esophageal atresia, some of which may require surgical procedures. PTU is also reported to cause specific congenital malformation in about 2.3% [2], such as preauricular sinuses and cysts, and urinary tract abnormalities. However, the severity of the malformations is less severe than with MMI, and therefore, the use of PTU is recommended for the first trimester of pregnancy by the ATA and ETA guidelines [2, 3]. The safety of KI use in pregnant patients also warrants further discussion. Although KI has been used safely in pregnant patients with GD for the control of thyroid function [35, 36], available evidence regarding its efficacy and potential adverse effects is limited to reports from Japan. The potential adverse effects associated with prolonged KI use in patients with GD include limited antithyroid efficacy [25], an increase in goiter [37], sustained hypothyroidism due to failure to escape from the Wolff–Chaikoff effect, and iodine-induced thyrotoxicosis in patients with nodular goiter, particularly in iodine-deficient regions [36]. However, further studies are needed to verify the efficacy and safety of KI as an anti-thyroid agent on a global scale. In the present study cohort, the median age was 32 (IQR of 28-36) years, suggesting the target age of possible pregnancy, which is the reason PTU was chosen as the initial ATD. Since the results do not apply to the wider ranges of age or male patients who cannot tolerate MMI, further investigations to evaluate the efficacy of the KI combined protocol in diverse cohorts will be needed.

The present study has several limitations. First, this study was designed as a retrospective observational study, and the number of patients in the low-dose group was small, consisting only of young female patients. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to male or older patients, as discussed above. In addition, although propensity score–matched pair analysis can minimize potential biases, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. A prospective study with a larger number of patients, including male patients with a wide range of ages, except for children and adolescents, may provide more precise results. Second, interobserver variability was not assessed in ultrasonographical examination; therefore, potential differences between technicians may have influenced the results. Finally, the efficacy of KI is limited and may not last long, and the efficacy may depend on the individuals and their iodine nutrition status. Japan is known for its adequate to high iodine intake due to its culinary culture, and thus, people in Japan may have tolerance to high amounts of iodine. The situation does not apply to iodine-deficient countries, and the efficacy may not be the same in those countries. Therefore, it is crucial to distinguish its effect on each patient.

Conclusion

Despite a lower rate of efficacy from the initial dose of PTU, the low-dose PTU combined with KI protocol was effective in controlling hyperthyroidism with up-titration in young women with GD, with the potential for fewer AEs compared with the high-dose protocol. This suggests that further research confirming the efficacy and safety of this protocol is warranted. Given that the efficacy of KI may vary among individuals, careful monitoring and individualized assessment of treatment response are recommended.

Therefore, due to the sensitive nature of the research, the supporting data are not publicly available. Restrictions apply to the availability of some or all data generated or analyzed during this study to preserve patient confidentiality. The corresponding author will, upon request, provide details of these restrictions and any conditions under which access to certain data may be granted. The statistical code is available upon reasonable request to the corresponding author.
