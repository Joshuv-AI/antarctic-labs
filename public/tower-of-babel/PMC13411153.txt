Antimicrobial Management of Severe Chlamydia psittaci Pneumonia in Adults: A Narrative Review
Authors: Lujun Shao, Gaochao Lv, Yi Yuan, Cheng Xu, Huiyu Tai, Yue Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChlamydia psittaci is an obligate intracellular bacterium and an increasingly recognized cause of severe community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) in adults, with contemporary multicenter Chinese cohorts reporting severe-stratum in-hospital mortality clustering around 8–9% and higher figures in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)-enriched series. β-Lactam therapy lacks reliable activity against this pathogen, yet considerable practice variation persists in the selection, sequencing, and de-escalation of intracellularly active agents in the era of routine metagenomic and targeted next-generation sequencing (mNGS, tNGS). The aim of this review is to provide a phase- and severity-stratified, bedside antimicrobial framework for severe psittacosis in adults—clarifying when to select, continue, switch, or combine intracellularly active agents at the 48- to 72-hour ICU decision points. This narrative review, reported in accordance with the SANRA framework, synthesizes the post-2015 antimicrobial evidence for severe C. psittaci pneumonia in adults and integrates treatment phase (empirical versus targeted), severity context, organ support, and antimicrobial stewardship at the 48- to 72-hour ICU decision points. Drawing on six multicenter cohorts and the largest dedicated multicenter dataset to date (Fang 2026, n = 186; severe-stratum mortality 7/81 = 8.6%), phase-by-severity stratification reconciles apparently discordant tetracycline- and fluoroquinolone-favoring cohort signals. Doxycycline is the preferred targeted backbone in confirmed non-pregnant disease; reflex class-switching is not required when severely ill patients are already improving on an empirical fluoroquinolone at NGS confirmation. Omadacycline is a renal-sparing alternative when acute kidney injury, anticipated continuous renal replacement therapy, or unreliable doxycycline access alters the standard pathway; high-dose tigecycline is reserved for salvage; azithromycin retains its clearest targeted role in pregnancy. Apparent nonresponse at 72 hours should trigger structured reassessment for coinfection, secondary organizing pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, and inadequate antimicrobial exposure before any salvage escalation. Adjunctive corticosteroid use should follow contemporary severe-CAP guidance pending pathogen-specific data from the NCT07352865 adaptive trial. Recommendations are calibrated using GRADE-adapted certainty and strength, with explicit acknowledgment that the comparative evidence base remains overwhelmingly retrospective and geographically concentrated in Chinese tertiary hospitals.

Background

Chlamydia psittaci is an obligate intracellular bacterium with a biphasic developmental cycle that replicates within an inclusion in respiratory epithelial cells and macrophages.1 Human infection follows inhalation of contaminated aerosols from infected birds and most often presents as community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), with high fever, dry cough, relative bradycardia, neuropsychiatric or gastrointestinal symptoms, and transaminase elevation; these clinical features overlap substantially with adenoviral and other atypical pneumonia, and are not by themselves diagnostic.2 The intracellular biology of the organism explains why β-lactam monotherapy is unreliable regardless of clinical severity: the reticulate body does not present the conventional peptidoglycan target exploited by β-lactams, and β-lactam penetration into the intracellular inclusion—and accumulation within respiratory epithelial cells and macrophages—is poor relative to tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, and macrolides (mechanism detailed in Intracellular Biology and Pharmacological Consequences).1

The recognized epidemiology has changed substantially with the routine use of metagenomic and targeted next-generation sequencing (mNGS, tNGS) in respiratory specimens from patients with unexplained severe CAP. Whether this represents a true rise in incidence or improved ascertainment is not yet resolved: the contemporary increase in reported cases is driven substantially by wider mNGS and tNGS use, which detects an obligate intracellular pathogen that conventional culture and serology frequently miss, rather than by a demonstrated increase in the underlying frequency of disease. Against a pre-NGS baseline incidence of approximately 1% of adult CAP,3,4 contemporary multicenter Chinese cohorts have established psittacosis as a clinically important ICU pneumonia, with severe-stratum in-hospital or ICU mortality clustering around 8–9% in severity-comparable denominators (Tang 2023 severe-only 8.9%; Fang 2026 severe stratum 8.6%) and reaching higher figures in more ARDS-enriched cohorts (Liu K 2024 severe stratum 15.4%).5–7 The largest dedicated multicenter cohort to date—186 NGS-confirmed adults across seven Guangdong hospitals, with 81 (43.5%) classified as severe and in-hospital mortality of 7/186 (3.8%) confined to the severe stratum (7/81; 8.6%)—provides the current epidemiological and prescribing-pattern anchor for the framework developed here.7 In a separate Fujian multicenter cohort, ARDS occurred in 41/74 (55.4%) and overall in-hospital mortality was 6/74 (8.1%), with all 6 deaths occurring within the severe stratum (6/39 severe; 0/35 non-severe); deaths are likely concentrated in but not directly reported as restricted to the ARDS subgroup, where the upper-bound estimate is 6/41 (14.6%).6 Xu et al multicenter data illustrate the steep severity gradient within ostensibly similar denominators.8 Recent European signals—the World Health Organization notification of unusual psittacosis activity across Austria, Denmark, Germany, Sweden, and the Netherlands,9 and a Dutch family cluster caused by zoonotic Chlamydia abortus with documented possible human-to-human transmission10—confirm that severe zoonotic chlamydial pneumonia is not exclusive to Asian centers. Beyond Europe, psittacosis remains a nationally notifiable zoonosis in the United States, where slaughter-plant and pet-bird-associated outbreaks continue to be reported,11 and outbreak and surveillance reports continue to identify severe community-acquired cases in Australia.12 These non-Asian signals do not change the antimicrobial framework for confirmed C. psittaci; the geographical concentration of the comparative evidence base is acknowledged again in Limitations.

Tetracyclines remain the conventional targeted class, but contemporary practice is more nuanced.1 Doxycycline is the most strongly supported targeted backbone, respiratory fluoroquinolones are commonly used empirically and may be appropriate continuation therapy, macrolides retain a defined role in pregnancy, and newer tetracycline-class agents (omadacycline, tigecycline) have emerged in renal-sparing, ARDS, and salvage scenarios with evidence dominated by single-arm cohorts and case reports. To our knowledge, and consistent with the only individual-patient-data synthesis of this literature, no randomized trial has compared these classes for severe psittacosis.4 Existing reviews1 have summarized the field broadly without integrating treatment-class selection with diagnostic timing, organ support, and stewardship at the 24- to 72-hour ICU decision points.

Building on this gap, the present review synthesizes the post-2015 antimicrobial evidence for severe C. psittaci pneumonia in adults around three explicit aims. First, to clarify when to use tetracycline-class agents, respiratory fluoroquinolones, macrolides, and newer derivatives, with careful separation of empirical and targeted treatment phases. Second, to integrate antimicrobial selection with diagnostic timing, severity context, and organ support—continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), and vasopressors. Third, to provide a calibrated 48- to 72-hour bedside framework rather than reflex escalation or de-escalation. Pregnancy, immunocompromise, central-nervous-system (CNS) involvement, and coinfection are addressed where they materially change antimicrobial decisions. Pediatric disease is outside scope.

Methods

This narrative review is reported in accordance with the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA).13 A formal systematic or scoping review was not undertaken because the available evidence base is dominated by retrospective cohorts, single-arm case series, and case reports with non-randomized treatment allocation, heterogeneous severity definitions, and inconsistent dose and duration reporting; pooling under these conditions would convey false statistical precision. The review addresses one clinical question: in adults with severe C. psittaci pneumonia, acute hypoxemic respiratory failure (AHRF), ARDS, or ICU-level disease, how should tetracycline-class agents, respiratory fluoroquinolones, macrolides, and newer tetracycline derivatives be selected, sequenced, and de-escalated, with integration of diagnostic timing, organ support, coinfection, pregnancy, and antimicrobial stewardship? Adults aged ≥18 years with confirmed or strongly suspected disease (mNGS, tNGS, polymerase chain reaction, paired serology, or culture) were eligible.

PubMed (MEDLINE), Embase (Elsevier), and Web of Science Core Collection (Clarivate) were searched in parallel from 1 January 2015 to 31 March 2026 using Boolean combinations of pathogen, antimicrobial, and severity terms; the complete strategy, including targeted supplementary PubMed searches for CRRT/ECMO pharmacokinetics, QT safety, severe-CAP corticosteroid trials and guidelines, pregnancy, and coinfection/next-generation sequencing, is provided in Supplementary Table S1, and the structured retrieval workflow is shown in Supplementary Figure S1. Reference lists of all included multicenter cohorts and contemporary reviews were hand-searched, and bibliographic details of every cited reference were verified against PubMed records. Methodological references that pre-date the search window were retained on the basis of irreplaceability rather than recency: the SANRA reporting framework, GRADE evidence-to-recommendation methodology, the only published in-vitro susceptibility dataset specific to C. psittaci (14 European turkey-derived strains), doxycycline pharmacodynamics, and two historical case-level precedents informing the pregnancy and organizing-pneumonia sections (the only published precedent for short-course tetracycline use in gestational psittacosis, and the first report of chlamydial-associated organizing pneumonia).

Evidence was weighted qualitatively: greatest interpretive weight was given to multicenter comparative cohorts and to individual-patient-data syntheses focused on severity (the principal multicenter comparative cohorts informing this framework are summarized in Table 1); single-center cohorts, special-scenario reports, pharmacological references, and society guidelines were used to refine, contextualize, or constrain conclusions. Two cross-cutting dimensions are applied within each antimicrobial class: treatment phase (empirical before pathogen confirmation versus targeted after) and severity context (severe pneumonia versus AHRF/ARDS/mechanical ventilation/shock). No pooled treatment-effect estimate was calculated; quantitative information is reported only as within-study proportions or confidence intervals from the original publications. Recommendation language and certainty/strength descriptors follow GRADE-adapted reasoning14 and are not formal guideline grades; their derivation is detailed in the recommendation-table footnote (Practice-Oriented Recommendations). Because the comparative evidence base is observational, a formal risk-of-bias instrument designed for randomized trials or for systematic reviews (for example, RoB 2 or ROBINS-I) was not applied; such instruments are not a SANRA requirement for narrative reviews and would impose false methodological uniformity on a heterogeneous case-report-and-cohort literature. Study quality was instead appraised qualitatively and applied consistently throughout: comparative multicenter cohorts carried the greatest interpretive weight, and the certainty attached to each conclusion was downgraded for retrospective design, non-randomized treatment allocation, confounding by indication, inconsistent severity definitions, small single-arm samples, and publication bias—the same determinants that calibrate the certainty and strength descriptors of the recommendation table (Practice-Oriented Recommendations).14

Microbiology and Diagnosis: Implications for Antimicrobial Selection

Antimicrobial selection in psittacosis rests on three foundations addressed in turn below: intracellular biology and class-level pharmacology, diagnostic-platform choice, and species-level molecular identification.

Intracellular Biology and Pharmacological Consequences

The biphasic developmental cycle has direct pharmacological consequences. The extracellular elementary body is metabolically inert; the intracellular reticulate body is metabolically active but sequestered within a host-cell inclusion. An effective agent must penetrate both membranes and reach concentrations adequate to inhibit reticulate-body protein synthesis or DNA replication.1 β-lactams are ineffective because the chlamydial cell envelope has reduced peptidoglycan that is not the principal vulnerability of the reticulate body, and because β-lactam penetration into the inclusion and accumulation within respiratory epithelial cells and macrophages is poor relative to tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, and macrolides; β-lactam exposure may additionally induce persistent or aberrant chlamydial forms in vitro. Apparent improvement under β-lactam monotherapy reflects natural fluctuation of the inflammatory syndrome rather than antimicrobial activity.

The intracellular classes differ in accumulation profile. Doxycycline accumulates in macrophages and polymorphonuclear leukocytes at intracellular-to-extracellular ratios in the single- to low-double-digit range and binds the 30S ribosomal subunit,19 whereas azithromycin accumulates in phagocytes and inflamed tissues to substantially greater quantitative extents (tens to several hundred-fold) and binds the 50S subunit; the prolonged tissue retention of azithromycin underlies its short-course efficacy in many atypical-pneumonia regimens but does not in itself overcome the limited cohort-level evidence supporting it as targeted monotherapy in severe non-pregnant disease (Macrolides). Respiratory fluoroquinolones (moxifloxacin, levofloxacin) achieve epithelial-lining-fluid and intracellular concentrations exceeding plasma and inhibit DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV; ciprofloxacin has weaker respiratory pharmacodynamic activity and is not preferred. Omadacycline and tigecycline share doxycycline’s ribosomal target with broader spectrum and altered pharmacokinetics (Tigecycline: High-Dose Salvage and Mixed-Infection Coverage, Omadacycline: A Renal-Sparing Alternative, Not a Doxycycline Replacement). Quantitative in vitro susceptibility data for C. psittaci itself are limited because the organism is a Risk Group 3 pathogen and routine clinical susceptibility testing is not performed; most antimicrobial activity and resistance-mechanism information is extrapolated from C. trachomatis.20 The principal C. psittaci-specific dataset remains the testing of 14 European turkey-derived strains by Butaye et al (doxycycline minimum inhibitory concentrations 0.05–0.2 μg/mL; no acquired resistance),21 with quantitative pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic detail in Supplementary Table S5. Neither the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute nor EUCAST maintains interpretive breakpoints for C. psittaci; antimicrobial selection therefore relies on cohort-level outcome data, intracellular pharmacology, and class toxicity rather than routine susceptibility reporting.

Diagnostic Platforms and Stewardship Implications

mNGS of bronchoalveolar-lavage fluid typically returns clinically actionable results within 24–48 hours of sample receipt, while tNGS panels are generally faster. Faster turnaround does not by itself justify selecting tNGS over mNGS; what matters is whether the result modifies the bedside risk assessment for an intracellular atypical pathogen and whether the broad-spectrum empirical regimen carries a continuing stewardship cost (selection pressure, nephrotoxicity, drug-interaction burden, Clostridioides difficile exposure). When a positive tNGS result for C. psittaci returns in a clinically compatible severe-CAP phenotype with no plausible second pathogen, the empirical β-lactam should be discontinued and a deliberate intracellular agent selected without waiting for confirmatory mNGS—the operational anchor of the stewardship discussion in Treatment Duration and Antimicrobial Stewardship. The principal continuing role of mNGS lies in reassessment of patients who deteriorate, where polymicrobial infection, unexpected fungi, or uncommon zoonotic organisms become more relevant than re-confirming the index pathogen (Apparent Treatment Failure and Salvage Therapy). In the largest contemporary cohort, mNGS predominated in severe disease and tNGS in non-severe presentations,7 reflecting clinician preference for broader pathogen discovery in the sickest patients.

Two interpretive cautions apply. Sequencing read counts are not directly proportional to clinical relevance and vary by laboratory and bioinformatic pipeline; absolute thresholds reported on one platform should not be transposed to another without local validation. Second, neither platform replaces basic supportive microbiology—blood and respiratory cultures, urinary antigen tests for Streptococcus pneumoniae and Legionella pneumophila, and viral panels—which together shape whether a plausible second pathogen is genuinely present and whether broad-spectrum coverage can be safely withdrawn after C. psittaci confirmation.

Resistance and Species-Level Identification

Acquired clinically significant resistance of human C. psittaci isolates to tetracyclines, respiratory fluoroquinolones, or macrolides has not been documented;20 this is partly an artefact of absent routine susceptibility testing, and continued vigilance is warranted, but routine empirical broadening on the basis of presumptive resistance is not currently supported. Where standard targeted therapy fails to produce the expected response, the leading explanations are delayed diagnosis, inadequate intracellular exposure, undetected coinfection, secondary nosocomial infection, and non-infectious complications (Apparent Treatment Failure and Salvage Therapy).

Species-level identification has gained relevance with the detection of related avian Chlamydiaceae in respiratory specimens—the 2025 Dutch outbreak investigation identified avian C. abortus as the cause of a family cluster of community-acquired pneumonia.10 The antimicrobial implications for confirmed C. psittaci pneumonia are unchanged, since tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, and azithromycin retain activity against closely related Chlamydiaceae; nevertheless, laboratories should report at species level wherever possible, and “Chlamydiaceae detected” on a sequencing report should not be equated with C. psittaci without species-level confirmation. No antimicrobial-susceptibility difference has been documented across the major C. psittaci ompA genotypes (A in psittacine-bird-associated, predominantly Asian human disease; B and E/B in pigeon- and European-poultry-associated disease) or against C. abortus, and the framework presented here applies independently of genotype on present evidence.20,21

Tetracycline-Class Therapy

Tetracyclines are the preferred targeted class in confirmed severe C. psittaci pneumonia. The class is heterogeneous: doxycycline is the default backbone in non-pregnant adults; minocycline has a niche in CNS involvement; tigecycline is a salvage or mixed-infection option; and omadacycline is a renal-sparing alternative. Doxycycline as the Default Targeted Backbone

Doxycycline is the preferred targeted antimicrobial for most non-pregnant adults with confirmed or strongly suspected severe C. psittaci pneumonia. The rationale rests on reliable intracellular penetration (Intracellular Biology and Pharmacological Consequences), decades of atypical-pneumonia experience, and supportive cohort-level signals from contemporary mNGS-confirmed multicenter studies; acquired tetracycline resistance has not been documented in human C. psittaci isolates.20 This preference rests on intracellular pharmacology and consistent observational signals rather than on randomized evidence; because no cohort allocated treatment at random, the apparent advantage of doxycycline is susceptible to confounding by indication, differences in disease severity at the point of drug selection, and treatment-selection bias, and the recommendation is therefore made with explicit acknowledgment of these limitations (Limitations).

The strongest cohort-level signal favoring tetracyclines comes from Ni et al Among 122 mNGS-confirmed adults across 15 hospitals, 66/69 (95.7%) of those treated with tetracyclines (overwhelmingly post-mNGS confirmation; only 1/122 received initial empirical tetracycline) achieved a favorable response, compared with 18/58 (31.0%) whose initial empirical antimicrobial therapy was a fluoroquinolone (P < 0.001), with shorter median defervescence in the tetracycline group.15 This contrast must be read in context: only 1/122 patients received initial empirical tetracycline therapy, and most patients counted in the tetracycline group received the drug after pathogen confirmation, frequently following empirical fluoroquinolone exposure that had not produced clinical response. The 66/69 versus 18/58 figures therefore compare predominantly targeted tetracycline therapy with initial empirical fluoroquinolone therapy and are not a randomized head-to-head class comparison; what they robustly support is that β-lactam-based and many empirical fluoroquinolone-based regimens fail to control severe psittacosis at the rates seen with deliberate intracellular therapy after pathogen confirmation.

The 19-center AHRF cohort of Tang et al refines but does not overturn this conclusion. Empirical fluoroquinolone-based therapy was used in 30/45 (66.7%) before mNGS confirmation, with 13/45 receiving β-lactam or antiviral and 2/45 azithromycin; in the 38-patient post-confirmation comparison subset, continued fluoroquinolone (n = 18), fluoroquinolone-to-tetracycline switch (n = 12), and fluoroquinolone–tetracycline combination (n = 8) produced no significant differences in mortality, ICU length of stay, ventilation duration, or CRRT use.5 Read together, Ni and Tang support a single operational rule: a deliberately chosen, intracellularly active agent should be in place by 48–72 hours of severe-CAP care; in most non-pregnant adults that agent is doxycycline, but for severely ill patients already improving on a fluoroquinolone at the time of NGS confirmation, mandatory class-switching is not supported.5,15 Full reconciliation across all six cohorts is developed in Fluoroquinolones.

Doxycycline can be initiated orally or intravenously according to gastrointestinal function, hemodynamic stability, and absorption. A practical adult regimen is 200 mg loading then 100 mg every 12 hours (terminal half-life ≈18–22 h, largely preserved in renal impairment and during hemodialysis);22 the loading dose is required to achieve target tissue concentrations within the first 24–48 h. The drug is highly protein-bound and primarily hepatically cleared, does not require renal dose adjustment, and is suitable in acute kidney injury (AKI) and during CRRT. Mild transaminase elevation is more often disease-related than drug-related and should not by itself prompt agent switching. Drug availability is a non-trivial consideration: intravenous doxycycline is widely available in mainland Chinese tertiary centers but is not consistently available in many European countries and is subject to recurrent shortage in the United States, where intravenous minocycline is commonly substituted (Limitations). Doxycycline should be avoided in pregnancy after the first trimester, in true allergy, and in confirmed CNS involvement, where minocycline is preferable (Minocycline: A Niche Role for CNS Involvement).

Minocycline: A Niche Role for CNS Involvement

Minocycline has a defensible niche in confirmed CNS involvement (meningitis, encephalopathy, cerebral infarction, reversible splenial-lesion syndrome), where its more favorable CNS penetration relative to doxycycline23 is illustrated by a published severe case in which minocycline-based therapy contributed to resolution of Chlamydia psittaci pneumonia complicated by central-nervous-system infection and reversible splenial-lesion syndrome;24 severe Chlamydia psittaci CNS-involved cases have also been managed with tetracycline-, fluoroquinolone-, and macrolide-based combination regimens after metagenomic confirmation of pathogen in both bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and cerebrospinal fluid.25 A reasonable adult regimen is 200 mg loading then 100 mg every 12 hours, individualized by neurological syndrome and cerebrospinal-fluid response. Routine substitution of minocycline for doxycycline in pneumonia without CNS involvement is not supported.

Tigecycline: High-Dose Salvage and Mixed-Infection Coverage

Tigecycline is not routine first-line targeted therapy for severe C. psittaci pneumonia. Its value lies in salvage of patients failing standard tetracycline-class therapy, in suspected polymicrobial infection where its broader Gram-negative and anaerobic spectrum is operationally useful, and in intolerance of both doxycycline and omadacycline. The principal psittacosis-specific signal is the case series of Wang et al, in which patients failing prior moxifloxacin or doxycycline recovered after escalation to tigecycline 100 mg intravenously every 12 hours.26 Its reservation as a last-line agent reflects three considerations: the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) boxed warning of increased all-cause mortality in non-approved indications, a tolerability profile less favorable than doxycycline, and a very broad spectrum whose routine use risks both collateral resistance pressure and masking of an out-of-range pathogen in an unstable patient.

A practical adult salvage regimen is a 200 mg loading dose followed by 100 mg intravenously every 12 hours—double the maintenance dose of the standard community-acquired bacterial pneumonia (CABP) regimen (100 mg load, then 50 mg every 12 hours). The rationale for the higher dose is pharmacokinetic rather than psittacosis-specific: tigecycline attains low maximum serum concentrations and distributes extensively into tissue, and in critical illness an increased volume of distribution, hypoalbuminemia, possible sequestration during extracorporeal support, and a high organism burden further reduce already-marginal plasma exposure, so that the standard 50 mg every 12 hours is frequently inadequate for salvage of severe disease. Hepatic dose reduction is required in Child-Pugh class C disease. Active monitoring for nausea, hepatic dysfunction, fibrinogen reduction, coagulation derangement, and pancreatitis is required. The FDA boxed warning of excess all-cause mortality in non-approved indications reinforces this last-line positioning;27 informed clinical reasoning, documented rationale, and active monitoring should accompany its use.

A conceptual limitation warrants emphasis: tigecycline’s broad spectrum can make an unstable patient appear pharmacologically covered while disguising pathogens outside its range (fungi, certain mycobacteria, viruses) or with limited bedside efficacy (carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales with elevated minimum inhibitory concentrations). Apparent anti-chlamydial failure should therefore prompt diagnostic reassessment for ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), polymicrobial infection, fungal disease, pulmonary embolism, secondary organizing pneumonia, or inadequate source control before tigecycline escalation is interpreted as failure of doxycycline itself.

Omadacycline: A Renal-Sparing Alternative, Not a Doxycycline Replacement

Omadacycline is a newer aminomethylcycline. The standard adult regimen is 200 mg intravenously on day 1 (or 100 mg every 12 hours for two doses), then 100 mg intravenously once daily; oral 300 mg once daily requires strict 4-hour fasting before and 2-hour fasting after dosing, which makes oral transition impractical on continuous enteral nutrition. No dose adjustment is required in moderate renal impairment or in end-stage renal disease on intermittent hemodialysis,28 or in mild hepatic impairment, with reductions recommended in moderate-to-severe hepatic impairment.29 Dedicated continuous renal replacement therapy pharmacokinetic data have not been published; the absence of renal-route elimination supports use without empirical dose adjustment, but clinical monitoring is appropriate.

The two principal cohort-level evidence sources should not be conflated. Wang et al reported on 16 mechanically ventilated ARDS patients receiving omadacycline after mNGS confirmation, of whom 14 recovered and 2 died of multidrug-resistant bacterial superinfection or renal failure rather than clear omadacycline failure;30 this is a single-arm series without a doxycycline comparator and therefore describes feasibility in the ARDS phenotype rather than effectiveness against an alternative. Song et al, in 47 omadacycline-treated psittacosis patients with a typical-CAP comparator, identified the blood-urea-nitrogen-to-albumin ratio and lactate dehydrogenase as predictors of AKI;31 this is evidence of renal-risk profiling, not of omadacycline superiority. Scattered case reports describe successful use in septic shock, multi-organ failure, and KPC-producing Gram-negative coinfection32,33 but are subject to publication bias.

Omadacycline is therefore positioned as a renal-sparing or salvage-compatible tetracycline-class agent rather than a new default first-line therapy. It is most attractive when AKI is present at presentation, when CRRT is anticipated, when intravenous doxycycline access is unreliable, in tolerance or absorption issues, or when polypharmacy with directed Gram-negative therapy in suspected MDR coinfection makes the standard pathway awkward. Off-label use in psittacosis (NMPA approval December 2021 covers community-acquired bacterial pneumonia and acute bacterial skin and skin-structure infections only; FDA approval covers comparable indications) and the cost gradient relative to generic doxycycline should enter stewardship decisions where the two are pharmacodynamically equivalent (Limitations).

Fluoroquinolones

Respiratory fluoroquinolones—principally moxifloxacin and levofloxacin—penetrate cells, cover the common bacterial pathogens of severe CAP, and are familiar empirical agents when atypical pathogens remain on the differential. In confirmed psittacosis, their role should be interpreted by treatment phase: a fluoroquinolone given before NGS confirmation is empirical atypical-pathogen coverage; a fluoroquinolone continued after confirmation is targeted alternative therapy; and a fluoroquinolone combined with doxycycline is a pragmatic ICU strategy in selected scenarios rather than evidence-supported superior therapy. The principal cohort-level studies appear discordant only when the empirical and targeted phases are blurred. Comparative Cohort Evidence presents the comparative evidence; Phase × Severity Reconciliation reconciles it; Agent Selection and Safety addresses agent selection and class-related safety.

Four further cohorts add context. Feng et al reported recovery in all 14 invasively ventilated patients at a single center.16 Empirical therapy was predominantly carbapenem-based; after mNGS confirmation all 14 received a fluoroquinolone-based regimen, but only 4 were on fluoroquinolone monotherapy, with 7 receiving fluoroquinolone plus doxycycline and the remaining 3 fluoroquinolone plus azithromycin or tigecycline. The cohort therefore demonstrates feasibility of fluoroquinolone-based targeted therapy in severe IMV psittacosis but does not isolate a fluoroquinolone-monotherapy signal. Yang M et al observed shorter fever duration and shorter hospital stay with quinolone-containing therapy in 116 mixed-severity adults (severe 53/116 = 45.7%) across 9 hospitals; this signal reflects population case-mix and prescribing culture rather than a phase-stratified comparison, and the discordance with Ni is largely a function of which patients each cohort enrolled and at what phase they were observed.17 Shen et al, in 48 adults (11 ICU/IMV) across 11 hospitals, found shorter defervescence with doxycycline-containing regimens than with fluoroquinolone monotherapy, and no advantage of doxycycline combination over doxycycline monotherapy.18 Fang et al—the largest dedicated multicenter cohort (n = 186 across seven Guangdong hospitals; 81 [43.5%] severe; January 2019–June 2025)—described severity-stratified prescribing (targeted combination therapy 51.9% in severe versus 21.0% in non-severe disease, P < 0.001; respiratory-fluoroquinolone monotherapy 52.4% in non-severe versus 19.8% in severe disease) without a regimen-versus-regimen comparison; its severe-stratum mortality provides the largest contemporary denominator for the figures reported in Background.7

Phase × Severity Reconciliation

The apparent disagreement across these cohorts resolves when treatment phase and severity context are made explicit (Figure 1). Two operational conclusions follow. First, doxycycline-first targeted therapy remains the preferred regimen for non-pregnant adults with confirmed severe psittacosis, on the strength of the Ni and Shen signals and consistent with chlamydial intracellular pharmacology (Intracellular Biology and Pharmacological Consequences). No cohort in the available evidence base shows that fluoroquinolone therapy used as a deliberate targeted post-confirmation regimen produces clearly inferior outcomes in severely ill patients, but none establishes class equivalence in a randomized sense either; the Ni 18/58 figure measures empirical fluoroquinolone effectiveness in the pre-confirmation phase and is not a measure of targeted fluoroquinolone effectiveness in confirmed disease. Second, mandatory class-switching is not supported when a severely ill or invasively ventilated patient is already improving on an empirical fluoroquinolone at the time of NGS confirmation. This rule is anchored on the 38-patient comparison subset of Tang. The Feng cohort provides supportive but qualified evidence: 7 of its 14 patients received a tetracycline alongside the fluoroquinolone after confirmation, so the cohort describes feasibility of fluoroquinolone-based regimens (predominantly with concurrent tetracycline activity) rather than maintained-fluoroquinolone-monotherapy survival. Sample sizes in both cohorts are consistent with feasibility and signal but not with established equivalence, and the rule should not be generalized to fluoroquinolones as the preferred targeted backbone for all severe disease. Fang 2026 anchors prevalence and prescribing patterns rather than the phase × severity reconciliation. Implications for combination therapy are developed in Targeted Combination Therapy After Confirmation.

Agent Selection and Safety

Moxifloxacin (400 mg every 24 hours, intravenously or orally) is generally the most attractive choice in severe psittacosis because of its intracellular activity, once-daily dosing, and primarily non-renal clearance. Levofloxacin is an acceptable alternative but requires renal dose adjustment in moderate-to-severe renal impairment, which is operationally relevant in the AKI-enriched ICU population, where doxycycline or omadacycline (Doxycycline as the Default Targeted Backbone, Omadacycline: A Renal-Sparing Alternative, Not a Doxycycline Replacement) avoid this complication. Ciprofloxacin is not preferred: it has substantially weaker activity against Streptococcus pneumoniae and other typical CAP pathogens that empirical severe-CAP coverage must encompass.

The principal ICU safety consideration is QT-interval prolongation, greatest with co-administration of azithromycin, antiarrhythmic agents, haloperidol, ondansetron, or azole antifungals, or in the presence of hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, structural heart disease, or shock-related myocardial injury. Baseline and follow-up electrocardiography are reasonable in ventilated or vasopressor-dependent patients receiving any QT-prolonging combination, and electrolyte replacement should be active throughout dual atypical-coverage exposure. Outside the defined fluoroquinolone indications in confirmed psittacosis (empirical coverage; continuation when improving; alternative when tetracyclines are contraindicated; pragmatic combination in suspected coinfection or delayed diagnosis with shock—Combination Antibiotic Therapy), doxycycline (Doxycycline as the Default Targeted Backbone) remains the cleaner targeted choice. Macrolides

Macrolides occupy a defined but narrow place in the antimicrobial framework for severe C. psittaci pneumonia: a legitimate role as part of empirical severe-CAP coverage before pathogen identification, a well-established targeted role in pregnancy when tetracyclines are contraindicated and respiratory fluoroquinolones are not preferred, and a small but real role when both classes are unavailable or unsuitable They are not supported as routine targeted therapy for severe non-pregnant adults once C. psittaci is confirmed.

Empirical Role and Non-Pregnant Targeted Use

A β-lactam plus azithromycin is a standard empirical option for severe CAP (Empirical Combination Therapy Before Pathogen Confirmation): the macrolide covers atypical and intracellular organisms (Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Legionella pneumophila, Chlamydia pneumoniae, C. psittaci) while the β-lactam covers extracellular bacterial pathogens.34,35 Once C. psittaci is confirmed and typical bacterial coinfection has been excluded, most non-pregnant adults should switch to doxycycline and discontinue both the β-lactam and the macrolide (Doxycycline as the Default Targeted Backbone, Treatment Duration and Antimicrobial Stewardship). Outside pregnancy, targeted azithromycin monotherapy is reasonable only when tetracyclines are truly contraindicated and respiratory fluoroquinolones carry unacceptable risk, when tetracyclines are inaccessible, or when an unstable QT profile makes a non-fluoroquinolone, non-tetracycline option preferable. A common adult regimen is 500 mg every 24 hours intravenously or orally; failure to defervesce by 72 hours should prompt structured reassessment (Apparent Treatment Failure and Salvage Therapy) rather than automatic extension.

Targeted Azithromycin in Pregnancy

Pregnancy is the clinical scenario in which azithromycin retains its clearest targeted role. Tetracyclines are conventionally avoided after the first trimester, and respiratory fluoroquinolones are avoided unless maternal benefit clearly outweighs fetal concern. Recent successful cases support azithromycin as the first-line targeted regimen for confirmed gestational psittacosis across a range of severity. In a second-trimester case (24 weeks), a 6-day course of intravenous and oral azithromycin 500 mg daily was followed by term delivery;36 in another second-trimester case (25 weeks) presenting with respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation, azithromycin-based targeted therapy guided by tNGS confirmation was also followed by recovery.37 The pregnancy literature is not uniformly favorable: a 2026 series of three fatal gestational cases in Japan was dominated by delayed diagnosis and rapidly progressive multi-organ failure rather than azithromycin pharmacodynamic failure per se.38

Two operational implications follow. First, intravenous or oral azithromycin (500 mg once daily for 7–14 days, response-guided) is the preferred targeted regimen for mild-to-moderate gestational psittacosis. Second, in severe gestational disease deteriorating despite azithromycin, multidisciplinary input from infectious diseases, maternal–fetal medicine, and intensive care is required, with the threshold for short-course tetracycline-class therapy reassessed case-by-case rather than reflexively maintained or reflexively crossed.

The reasoning that may justify short-course tetracycline use in this narrow scenario should be made explicit. Short doxycycline courses (≤10–21 days) have not been shown to cause clinically significant fetal dental or skeletal effects in available human data,39,40 and the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has explicitly endorsed doxycycline as the agent of choice for life-threatening tick-borne rickettsial disease in children and pregnant women on the basis that maternal life-threat outweighs fetal-risk concern in that specific indication.39 This precedent is narrow and disease-specific (acute lethal rickettsial disease), and its extension to severe gestational psittacosis failing azithromycin is supported by an older case-level precedent in which short-course tetracycline therapy was deemed justified after erythromycin failure in third-trimester gestational psittacosis, despite acknowledged fetal concerns;41 given the limited and dated nature of this precedent, any decision to use tetracyclines in pregnancy should still be presented explicitly to the patient, the maternal–fetal-medicine team, and within the medical record.

Macrolide–fluoroquinolone overlap should be avoided where a single-class targeted agent can achieve equivalent coverage. When transition is operationally necessary (eg, empirical β-lactam + azithromycin → fluoroquinolone-containing targeted therapy), the macrolide and the fluoroquinolone should be separated by at least four half-lives of the agent being discontinued, or the prescriber should commit to single-class targeted therapy from the time of pathogen confirmation Baseline and follow-up electrocardiography are reasonable in ventilated or vasopressor-dependent patients receiving any QT-prolonging combination, with active electrolyte replacement throughout. Combination Antibiotic Therapy

Contemporary multicenter practice reflects this distinction: targeted combination therapy is used substantially more often in severe than in non-severe disease, a pattern best understood as prescribing driven by severity and comorbidity rather than by demonstrated superiority.7 In severely ill adults with multiple comorbidities—for example, concurrent septic shock, suspected bacterial coinfection, or established multi-organ dysfunction—a short, closely monitored period of combination therapy can be clinically justified while the diagnostic picture is consolidated, with the intracellularly active agent (usually doxycycline) retained throughout. These are precisely the circumstances that define defensible targeted combination after pathogen confirmation (Targeted Combination Therapy After Confirmation); outside them, optimized monotherapy remains preferred, and any combination should carry a pre-specified de-escalation endpoint.

Three different practices are commonly conflated: empirical combination (β-lactam + respiratory fluoroquinolone or β-lactam + macrolide) before pathogen identification, which is standard severe-CAP practice; targeted combination (most commonly doxycycline + respiratory fluoroquinolone) after C. psittaci confirmation, observationally common but without proven outcome advantage over optimized monotherapy; and short-duration multi-class therapy in exceptional contexts.

Empirical Combination Therapy Before Pathogen Confirmation

A β-lactam plus a respiratory fluoroquinolone or a β-lactam plus a macrolide remains a standard empirical regimen for severe CAP because the β-lactam covers extracellular pathogens while the macrolide or fluoroquinolone covers atypicals while pathogen identification is pending. Psittacosis cannot be reliably distinguished from other severe atypical pneumonia at admission on clinical features alone, even when bird exposure is documented.42,43 Once mNGS or tNGS confirms C. psittaci as the only clinically relevant pathogen and culture, urinary antigen, and viral panel testing do not support typical bacterial coinfection, the β-lactam should be discontinued (Treatment Duration and Antimicrobial Stewardship).

Targeted Combination Therapy After Confirmation

Doxycycline plus a respiratory fluoroquinolone is the most frequently reported targeted combination. Neither Shen18 nor Tang5 showed that doxycycline–fluoroquinolone combination was superior to optimized doxycycline monotherapy. The largest contemporary multicenter cohort (Fang 2026, n = 186) reinforces this asymmetry empirically: targeted combination therapy was used in 51.9% of severe disease versus 21.0% of non-severe disease (P < 0.001), without comparative outcome analysis between strategy arms;7 this pattern reflects prescribing in response to severity (confounding by indication), not therapeutic equivalence. Targeted combination is therefore most defensible when the clinical course is unstable, when diagnosis was substantially delayed, when suspected coinfection has not been excluded, or when an empirical fluoroquinolone is already being tolerated and appears beneficial at NGS confirmation. Triple-class therapy (tetracycline + fluoroquinolone + macrolide) should be reserved for confirmed CNS involvement (Minocycline: A Niche Role for CNS Involvement) and rapidly de-escalated. When any targeted combination is selected, the de-escalation endpoint should be specified at the time the regimen is initiated rather than deferred.

Coinfection and Co-Detection

Co-detection (sequencing reads attributable to a second organism) is not synonymous with coinfection (a clinically active second pathogen). Reported NGS-based co-detection rates from single-center Chinese cohorts (41–73% in unselected mNGS-confirmed psittacosis; 84–92% in severe disease)44,45 almost certainly overstate true polymicrobial disease and are heavily dependent on laboratory pipeline and reporting threshold; absolute figures should not be transposed across laboratories without local validation. The clinical question is whether the second organism explains fever, shock, hypoxemia, the radiographic pattern, or lack of response to appropriate intracellular therapy. A second organism detected at low read count in a patient improving on appropriate intracellular therapy is generally not a second disease; a second organism detected in a clinically compatible context (multilobar consolidation in a poorly responding patient, persistent shock, rising procalcitonin, imaging features inconsistent with isolated atypical pneumonia) requires pathogen-specific therapy alongside maintained intracellular cover for C. psittaci.

Special Critical-Care Scenarios

ICU patients commonly present with combinations of ARDS, septic shock, AKI, hepatic dysfunction, thromboembolic complications, or strong suspicion of polymicrobial coinfection; a substantial minority require CRRT or ECMO. These scenarios modify route, monitoring, and threshold for combination or salvage therapy but do not change the central principle (Microbiology and Diagnosis: Implications for Antimicrobial Selection, Tetracycline-Class Therapy): the C. psittaci component requires an intracellularly active agent—usually doxycycline, with omadacycline, tigecycline, a respiratory fluoroquinolone, or azithromycin selected for defined clinical reasons. ARDS and Invasive Mechanical Ventilation

Doxycycline remains the default targeted backbone in non-pregnant adults. In unstable patients with shock, substantially delayed diagnosis, or unresolved coinfection concern, a temporary doxycycline–moxifloxacin combination is reasonable (Targeted Combination Therapy After Confirmation); routine combination therapy for every ARDS patient is not evidence-supported. Secondary nosocomial infection (multidrug-resistant Gram-negative ventilator-associated pneumonia [VAP] in particular), renal failure, and thromboembolic events appear among fatal courses;30,46 lung-protective ventilation, prone positioning when indicated, VAP prevention, and anticoagulation are therefore as important to outcome as antimicrobial choice.

AKI, Anticipated CRRT, and ECMO

Doxycycline (200 mg loading, then 100 mg every 12 hours) is generally acceptable in CRRT—highly protein-bound, primarily hepatobiliary, with limited hemofiltration removal.22,47 Omadacycline requires no dose adjustment in renal impairment or on intermittent hemodialysis28 and in mild hepatic impairment,29 making it attractive when AKI is established at presentation; CRRT use rests on extrapolation from its non-renal elimination pathway in the absence of dedicated CRRT pharmacokinetic data. For ECMO, no psittacosis-specific drug-concentration data are available; clinical response remains the practical exposure marker, and recommended doses should not be empirically increased in the absence of validated therapeutic drug monitoring.48 Mild transaminase elevation usually reflects disease, not drug toxicity, and should not by itself prompt agent switching.31

Pregnancy, CNS Involvement, Immunocompromise, Coinfection

Pregnancy is addressed in Targeted Azithromycin in Pregnancy (azithromycin first-line; multidisciplinary review when severe and not responding). Confirmed CNS involvement shifts the regimen toward minocycline plus moxifloxacin (Minocycline: A Niche Role for CNS Involvement). Immunocompromise from biologic therapy is a recognized risk factor for severe psittacosis after bird exposure49 but does not justify reflex combination or salvage therapy in patients responding promptly. Coinfection management is in Coinfection and Co-Detection.

Treatment Duration and Antimicrobial Stewardship

No randomized trial has defined optimal duration of therapy. In multicenter cohorts that report duration, most clinically improving severely ill patients receive approximately 10–14 days of targeted intracellular therapy.5,15,50 Total active anti-chlamydial therapy is counted from the first day on which an effective intracellular agent was given at adequate dose. Practical ranges, derived from observational practice rather than psittacosis-specific duration trials, are 10–14 days for uncomplicated severe disease that stabilizes promptly, 14–21 days for ARDS, multilobar consolidation, or extrapulmonary involvement, and 21–28 days for confirmed CNS involvement (the last range derives from analogous intracellular-pathogen experience and should be individualized by neurological syndrome and CSF response).

NGS confirmation should trigger a stewardship review within 24 hours, answering four practical questions: is C. psittaci the only clinically plausible pathogen; is the current regimen intracellularly active; is broad-spectrum β-lactam coverage still justified; and what is the planned total duration? If blood cultures, respiratory cultures, urinary antigen for S. pneumoniae and L. pneumophila, and NGS read interpretation do not support typical bacterial coinfection, the β-lactam should be discontinued and a deliberate intracellular agent selected. The expected early response is defervescence and improving oxygenation within 48–72 hours;5,15,50 C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, interleukin-6, PaO2/FiO2, vasopressor requirement, and ventilator settings can support assessment, but imaging usually lags clinical parameters and should not alone define early treatment failure. Routine repeat mNGS is unnecessary in clinically improving patients; repeat or expanded testing is appropriate when the patient deteriorates, remains febrile beyond 72 hours, or has a high-risk context. The full failure pathway is in Apparent Treatment Failure and Salvage Therapy.

Apparent Treatment Failure and Salvage Therapy

Apparent failure is defined here as persistent or worsening fever, oxygenation, hemodynamic status, or radiographic consolidation at approximately 72 hours after initiation of an appropriate intracellular agent at adequate dose, in a patient with confirmed or strongly suspected C. psittaci pneumonia. Effective intracellular therapy typically produces measurable improvement within 48–72 hours;1,5,15 apparent failure is therefore a trigger for systematic reassessment, not for reflex antimicrobial escalation. Acquired resistance in human C. psittaci has not been documented;20 other explanations dominate the failure pathway.

Exclude Mimics Before Escalating Antimicrobial Therapy

Three categories of apparent failure should be actively excluded before any antimicrobial change (Figure 2). The first is undetected or evolving polymicrobial infection: ventilator-associated pneumonia (most often Klebsiella, Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter, or Stenotrophomonas) is a particularly important consideration in deteriorating ventilated patients, and several fatal outcomes in severe-psittacosis cohorts have been attributed to secondary bacterial infection rather than anti-chlamydial failure;5,46 viral coinfection (SARS-CoV-2, influenza, rhinovirus), invasive pulmonary aspergillosis, and reactivated cytomegalovirus or herpes simplex virus require specific testing in selected patients.51–53 The second is secondary organizing pneumonia, increasingly recognized in C. psittaci pneumonia and characterized by migratory or persistent consolidation, reverse-halo signs, and recurrent inflammatory deterioration after initial microbiological control under appropriate intracellular therapy, with corticosteroid-responsive recovery,54–56 with an earlier precedent for the chlamydial-OP association reported at the genus level.57 The third comprises non-infectious complications and inadequate antimicrobial exposure: pulmonary embolism, fluid-overload pulmonary edema, ventilator-induced lung injury, drug-induced lung injury, secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, and progression of underlying ARDS; gut hypoperfusion or drug-interaction-mediated under-exposure of oral tetracyclines (concurrent antacids, iron, calcium, sucralfate); and prescribed-versus-delivered audit.Figure 2Treatment-failure and salvage pathway for severe Chlamydia psittaci pneumonia. The reassessment work-up should be proportionate to severity: structured clinical review at 72 hours, repeat blood and respiratory cultures, urinary antigen testing, electrocardiography and electrolyte review for dual atypical therapy, chest computed tomography when imaging and oxygenation diverge, repeat or expanded mNGS in deteriorating patients, transbronchial biopsy when organizing pneumonia is plausible, and explicit exclusion of pulmonary embolism in disproportionate hypoxemia or new hemodynamic instability.

Salvage Therapy When True Antimicrobial Failure Remains Likely

If true antimicrobial failure on the initial targeted regimen remains the leading explanation after structured reassessment, salvage strategy is guided by the agent that has failed and the dominant clinical problem. The 72-hour reassessment is the time of decision, not the start of an additional observation period: a patient with objective deterioration at 72 hours should have salvage initiated then; a clinically static patient may reasonably be observed for a further 24 hours with active mimic search. Routine continuation beyond 96 hours of unequivocal nonresponse without an identified mimic is not supported.

For doxycycline failure without an identified mimic, high-dose tigecycline (100 mg intravenously every 12 hours, with an optional 200 mg loading dose extrapolated from critical-care practice for severe infections) is the most clearly described psittacosis-specific salvage option,26 with FDA boxed-warning considerations addressed in Tigecycline: High-Dose Salvage and Mixed-Infection Coverage. Omadacycline (200 mg loading, then 100 mg every 24 hours intravenously) is the cleaner alternative when AKI, anticipated CRRT, doxycycline intolerance, or co-administration of pathogen-directed Gram-negative therapy in suspected MDR coinfection makes the standard pathway awkward.33 For apparent fluoroquinolone failure, the most rational step is usually to add or switch to a tetracycline-class agent (doxycycline first; omadacycline for renal-sparing simplicity; tigecycline only if both are unsuitable); switching among respiratory fluoroquinolones is rarely the right answer in true failure on an appropriately chosen agent. For pregnancy, salvage decisions require multidisciplinary input from maternal–fetal medicine, infectious diseases, and intensive care (Targeted Azithromycin in Pregnancy). For confirmed or strongly suspected secondary organizing pneumonia, salvage is corticosteroid therapy (Corticosteroids and Host-Directed Therapy) rather than antimicrobial escalation.

Adjunctive Corticosteroids and Future Directions

Corticosteroids and Host-Directed Therapy

The role of adjunctive corticosteroids in severe C. psittaci pneumonia is the most important open clinical question for which prospective psittacosis-specific data are imminent. NCT07352865 (Adaptive RCT of Corticosteroids for Severe Chlamydia psittaci Community-Acquired Pneumonia) is a Phase 3 adaptive, randomized, triple-masked trial with three arms (saline placebo; methylprednisolone 0.5 mg·kg−1 intravenously daily; methylprednisolone 1.0 mg·kg−1 intravenously daily); planned enrollment is approximately 100 adults with completion expected in late 2028. NCT07352865 is positioned to identify the optimal corticosteroid dose for severe psittacosis specifically, rather than to settle whether corticosteroids are indicated at all in this etiological subgroup; it shares its three-arm methylprednisolone dose-finding architecture with the broader PANDA-SCAP adaptive platform (NCT07152587), which is currently active for adenovirus- and Pneumocystis jirovecii-induced severe community-acquired pneumonia; closely related pathogen-stratified sub-protocols using the same architecture are under registration for severe influenza (CAPSTONE-IFV, NCT07351500) and severe psittacosis (NCT07352865) (full trial detail in Supplementary Table S3).

Contemporary severe-CAP corticosteroid evidence between 2022 and 2025 is mixed: CAPE-COD reported reduced 28-day mortality with hydrocortisone in severe CAP excluding vasopressor-dependent shock at inclusion;59 ESCAPe was negative on prolonged low-dose methylprednisolone;60 REMAP-CAP was stopped for futility with several interpretive caveats;61,62 an updated Bayesian meta-analysis nonetheless supports a population-level short-term mortality benefit.63 The 2026 ATS update issues a conditional recommendation for systemic corticosteroids in adult severe CAP excluding influenza-related disease;34 IDSA did not co-endorse the broader update, though the corticosteroid recommendation was not among the contested items.58 None of these trials reported a C. psittaci subgroup, and the disease was likely under-represented in CAPE-COD and REMAP-CAP, both of which predate routine mNGS-driven psittacosis detection. Trial-level detail is in Supplementary Table S3.

The implication for the bedside is therefore narrow: a patient with confirmed psittacosis-related severe CAP who meets CAPE-COD severity criteria and is otherwise an appropriate hydrocortisone candidate can reasonably receive hydrocortisone on the existing severe-CAP evidence base; what the field does not have is evidence that this generalizes favorably to higher methylprednisolone doses or longer durations. Two narrower indications are independent of severe-CAP guidance and well established: confirmed or strongly suspected secondary organizing pneumonia complicating C. psittaci infection (Exclude Mimics Before Escalating Antimicrobial Therapy), and enrollment in NCT07352865 or comparable prospective trials when eligibility is met. Pregnancy and breastfeeding are exclusion criteria for NCT07352865; corticosteroid decisions in pregnant patients should be made within multidisciplinary discussion. Outside these defined situations, the dominant management commitments remain prompt and adequate intracellular antimicrobial therapy together with standard supportive ARDS care.

Comparative Effectiveness Research and Registry Needs

The highest-value future antimicrobial trial would stratify prospectively by treatment phase and severity, addressing four questions that observational data cannot answer: in a severely ill patient already improving on empirical fluoroquinolone at NGS confirmation, does mandatory class-switching to doxycycline change outcome; in AKI-enriched severe psittacosis, is omadacycline non-inferior or superior to doxycycline; in delayed diagnosis, does early addition of doxycycline to ongoing fluoroquinolone change outcome compared with switch alone; and what is optimal duration of intracellular therapy in ARDS, multilobar, or extrapulmonary disease? A complementary priority is a prospective international registry of severe C. psittaci pneumonia capturing time from symptom onset to active intracellular therapy, oxygen-support trajectory, NGS read counts and species-level identification, coinfection profile, antimicrobial de-escalation timing, adverse events, and 30- and 90-day outcomes—particularly important as evidence accumulates outside Asia.9,10

Limitations

The available primary evidence is overwhelmingly retrospective, observational, and geographically concentrated in Chinese tertiary hospitals where mNGS uptake has been earliest and broadest. The empirical-versus-targeted treatment-phase reconciliation that anchors Doxycycline as the Default Targeted Backbone, Comparative Cohort Evidence, and Phase × Severity Reconciliation depends predominantly on Ni 2023 (n = 122 / 15 hospitals); Tang 2023, Yang M 2022, Shen 2021, Feng 2026, and Fang 2026 contribute reconciling rather than independently replicating signals, having been performed within the same broad Chinese tertiary-hospital context with substantially overlapping clinical case definitions, laboratory diagnostics, prescribing culture, and drug availability. In particular, the preference for doxycycline as the targeted backbone rests on observational cohorts in which patients receiving tetracyclines and those receiving comparators differed systematically in severity, diagnostic timing, and clinician judgment; its apparent advantage should therefore be read as consistent with, but not proven by, the available evidence. C. psittaci ompA genotype distribution differs between regions (genotype A in psittacine-bird-associated, predominantly Asian human cohorts; B and E/B in pigeon- and European-poultry-associated disease), and avian C. abortus has been documented as a human zoonotic pathogen in northwestern Europe.10 Drug availability and prescribing culture also differ: omadacycline is approved in mainland China and the United States but is not licensed in many European jurisdictions; intravenous doxycycline is not consistently available outside mainland China and is subject to recurrent shortage in the United States, where intravenous minocycline is commonly substituted; tigecycline use patterns vary substantially between regions and institutions.

Treatment allocation in every comparative cohort cited is non-randomized and is shaped by diagnostic timing, severity at antimicrobial selection, drug availability, organ dysfunction, and clinician preference; the empirical-versus-targeted phase distinction controls part but not all of this confounding. Several drug-specific conclusions—particularly for omadacycline, tigecycline, and minocycline in CNS involvement—rest on small single-arm cohorts and case reports subject to publication bias. Outcome definitions, follow-up duration, dose and route reporting, and adverse-event ascertainment are inconsistent across cohorts; quantitative cross-cohort comparisons of effect size are not appropriate.

Recommendations in Table 3 should be read as practice-oriented synthesis rather than as guideline-grade recommendations. Several adjacent topics fall outside the antimicrobial scope: infection-prevention practice in light of documented or suspected human-to-human transmission,10 weight-based dose adjustment in extreme obesity, and detailed therapeutic-drug-monitoring strategies for co-administered antimicrobials during ECMO. Recommendations resting on basic biology (β-lactam inadequacy, structured 72-hour reassessment) generalize without reservation; recommendations resting on Chinese observational comparative effectiveness require local validation in non-Chinese settings.

Conclusions

Severe Chlamydia psittaci pneumonia is an intracellular, NGS-diagnosed, ICU-relevant atypical pneumonia whose outcome depends primarily on early recognition, rapid pathogen confirmation, prompt withdrawal of inactive β-lactam-only therapy, deliberate selection of an intracellularly active targeted agent, and structured reassessment for coinfection and treatment mimics at 48–72 hours. Doxycycline remains the preferred targeted backbone for most non-pregnant adults with confirmed disease—a recommendation grounded in intracellular pharmacology and in consistent but non-randomized observational data, and therefore subject to confounding by indication and treatment-selection bias; respiratory fluoroquinolones are clinically useful empirical agents and acceptable continuation therapy in patients improving before NGS confirmation, but should not displace doxycycline as the default targeted regimen. Omadacycline is the most consistently described renal-sparing tetracycline-class alternative; high-dose tigecycline is reserved for salvage and selected mixed-infection contexts. Azithromycin has its clearest targeted role in pregnancy, with explicit recognition that severe gestational disease may deteriorate despite macrolide therapy and that maternal-life decisions sometimes require tetracycline use under multidisciplinary supervision. Adjunctive corticosteroids are not yet supported as routine therapy for primary psittacosis-related ARDS and should be reserved for confirmed secondary organizing pneumonia or for enrollment in NCT07352865 or comparable prospective trials. Prospective phase-stratified comparative trials and a contemporary international registry of severe psittacosis would refine this framework as evidence accumulates outside Asian tertiary centers.
