Generalized Howe curves of genus 4, 5, and 6 with completely decomposable Jacobians
Ryo Ohashi

Abstract
Superspecial curves are important objects in number theory and algebraic geometry, and the existence in genus g ≥ 4 g\geq 4 remains an open problem for all but finitely many characteristics p > 0 p>0 . As a computational approach to this problem, Kudo-Harashita-Howe (2020) showed that a superspecial curve of genus 4 exists in each characteristic p p with 7 < p < 20000 7<p<20000 . Their method restricted attention to a specific class of curves, known as Howe curves , for which superspeciality is reduced to those of curves of genus at most 2. In this paper, we focus on a more specific class of curves, namely Howe curves whose Jacobians decompose into a product of four elliptic curves. By restricting our attention to such curves, the superspeciality reduces to the supersingularity of elliptic curves, which enables us to construct a superspecial curve of genus 4 more efficiently than Kudo-Harashita-Howe’s method. As our first main result, we confirmed by computer the existence of such superspecial curves of genus 4 in characteristics p p with 20000 < p < 10 6 20000<p<10^{6} . Using a similar approach, we also propose constructions of superspecial curves of genera 5 and 6 from only supersingular elliptic curves. Furthermore, computational experiments establish the existence of superspecial curves of genus 5 (resp. genus 6) in characteristics p p with 13 < p < 10 5 13<p<10^{5}\hskip-0.85358pt (resp. 7 < p < 10 5 7<p<10^{5} ).

Introduction

Throughout this paper, a
curve
always means a non-singular projective variety of dimension one defined over a field of characteristic
p>0
.
A curve is said to be
superspecial
if its Jacobian is isomorphic to a product of supersingular elliptic curves (as unpolarized abelian varieties).
Superspecial curves are important objects not only in number theory but also in applications, including isogeny-based cryptography and algebraic geometry codes.
It is very natural to ask the following question:

Question.
For given
g
and
p
, does there exist a superspecial curve of genus
g
in characteristic
p
?

This question is completely solved for genera
g\leq 3
.
In fact, Deuring
[
2
]
showed that a supersingular elliptic curve (i.e., the case of
g=1
) exists in any characteristic
p>0
.
Also, the non-existence of superspecial curves of genus
g=2
for
p\in\hskip-0.85358pt\{2,3\}
, and the existence of such curves for
p>3
were shown by Ibukiyama, Katsura, and Oort in
[
8
, Theorem 3.3]
.
Ekedahl proved in
[
3
, Theorem 1.1]
that if there exists a superspecial curve of genus
g
in characteristic
p
, then the inequality
2g\leq p^{2}-p
holds, which implies that no superspecial curve of

genus
g=3
exists in characteristic
p=2
.
The existence of superspecial curves of genus
g=3
for
p>2
was shown by Oort
[
19
, Theorem 5.12]
or Ibukiyama
[
7
]
.
On the other hand, the question for genus
g\geq 4
remains open in general
p
.
One of the difficulties in this case lies in the fact that abelian varieties of dimension
g\geq 4
are generally not Jacobians, in contrast to the case
\hskip-0.56905ptg\leq 3
.

In this paper, we restrict our attention to the cases
g\in\hskip-0.85358pt\{4,5,6\}
.
We first review some previous results on the existence of superspecial curves of genus
g=4
in small characteristic
p
.
By Ekedahl’s bound, there is no

superspecial curve of genus 4 for

.
The existence of a superspecial curve of genus

[

by Fuhrmann, Garcia, and Torres, whereas the non-existence for

In addition, Kudo, Harashita, and Howe

Their strategy was to focus on a specific class of genus-

2

To verify the existence of superspecial curves of genus
4

Explicitly, for

2.2
Generalized Howe curves

Let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be hyperelliptic curves of genera
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
which share exactly
r
common Weierstrass points; that is,

\displaystyle C_{1}:y_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=(x-a_{1})(x-a_{2})\cdots(x-a_{r})(x-b_{r+1})\cdots(x-b_{2g_{1}+2}),

\displaystyle C_{2}:y_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=(x-a_{1})(x-a_{2})\cdots(x-a_{r})(x-c_{r+1})\cdots(x-c_{2g_{2}+2}),

where
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 13.0mu}}}{K})
are all distinct.
The desingularization
H
of the fiber product
C_{1}\times_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}C_{2}
is referred to

as a
generalized Howe curve
.
We recall the results by Katsura and Takashima
[
13
, Section 2]

on the genus of a generalized Howe curve
H
and a criterion for
H
to be
hyperelliptic:

Proposition 2.5
.

With the above notation, the genus of
H
is given by
2g_{1}+2g_{2}+1-r
.
Moreover, if the genus of
H
is at least
4
, then
H
is hyperelliptic if and only if
r=g_{1}+g_{2}+1
.

Next, the hyperelliptic involutions of
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
induce the involutions of
H
given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}:(x,y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle\longmapsto(x,y_{1},-y_{2}),

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}:(x,y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle\longmapsto(x,-y_{1},y_{2}),

and we clearly have the isomorphisms
H/\langle\sigma_{i}\rangle\cong C_{i}
for each
i\in\{1,2\}
.
The composition
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}
defines another quotient map
H\rightarrow H/\langle\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle\eqqcolon C_{3}
, where
C_{3}
is the hyperelliptic curve given by

C_{3}:y_{3}^{2}=(x-b_{r+1})\cdots(x-b_{2g_{1}+2})(x-c_{r+1})\cdots(x-c_{2g_{2}+2})

with
y_{3}=y_{1}y_{2}/(x-a_{1})(x-a_{2})\cdots(x-a_{r})
.
We remark that the genus of
C_{3}
is given by
g_{1}+g_{2}+1-r
, and thus the genus of
H
equals the sum of the genera of
C_{1},C_{2}
, and
C_{3}
.
An important property of a generalized Howe curve
H
is that their Jacobians are isogenous to the product of the Jacobians of
C_{1},C_{2}
, and
C_{3}
.

Theorem 2.6

(cf.
[
13
, Theorem 3]
)
.

With the above notation, there exists an isogeny

J(H)\longrightarrow J(C_{1})\times J(C_{2})\times J(C_{3})\vskip-1.99168pt

whose degree is a power of 2.
In particular, the curve
H
is superspecial if and only if
C_{1},C_{2}
, and
C_{3}
are all superspecial.

Now assume that
g_{1}=g_{2}=1
and
r=1
.
In other words, let
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
be elliptic curves defined by

\displaystyle E_{1}:y^{2}

\displaystyle=(x-a)(x-b_{2})(x-b_{3})(x-b_{4}),

(2.2)

\displaystyle E_{2}:y^{2}

\displaystyle=(x-a)(x-c_{2})(x-c_{3})(x-c_{4}),

(2.3)

where
a,b_{2},b_{3},b_{4},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 13.0mu}}}{K})
are pairwise distinct, and define
H
as the desingularization of
E_{1}\times_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}\hskip-0.56905ptE_{2}
.
Proposition
2.5
shows that
H
has genus 4 and is non-hyperelliptic;  such a generalized Howe curve
H
is simply called a
Howe curve
.
By Theorem
2.6
, a Howe curve
H
is superspecial if and only if two elliptic curves
E_{1},E_{2}
are both supersingular and the genus-2 curve

C_{3}:y^{2}=(x-b_{2})(x-b_{3})(x-b_{4})(x-c_{2})(x-c_{3})(x-c_{4})

(2.4)

is superspecial.
Based on this property, Kudo, Harashita, and Howe
[
15
]
proposed an algorithm for generating superspecial Howe curves.
For completeness, we briefly outline their algorithm below:

Step 1.

Choose parameters
(b_{2},b_{3},b_{4},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4})
such that the genus-2 curve (
2.4
) is superspecial.

Step 2.

For each
a
for which the elliptic curve (
2.2
) is supersingular, check whether the elliptic curve (
2.3
) is also supersingular.
If so, output
(a,b_{2},b_{3},b_{4},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4})
.

Step 3.

If no such parameter
a
is found, return to
Step 1
with another choice of
(b_{2},b_{3},b_{4},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4})
.

Each output
(a,b_{2},b_{3},b_{4},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4})
determines a superspecial Howe curve
H
.
Applying the above algorithm, they verified the existence of superspecial Howe curves for every characteristic
p
with
7<p<20000
.
Their implementation is available at the URL cited in
[
15
, Section 6]
.

Remark 2.7
.

The number of superspecial Howe curves in characteristic
p
is expected to be
\varTheta(p^{3})
.
Indeed, since the number of superspecial genus-2 curves
is approximately
\hskip-0.28453ptp^{3}/2880
as shown in
[
8
, Theorem 3.3]
, there are
\varTheta(p^{3})
possible choices of parameters
(b_{2},b_{3},b_{4},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4})
in
Step 1
.
For a fixed
(b_{2},b_{3},b_{4},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4})
, there are approximately
p/2
values of
a
for which the elliptic curve (
2.2
) is supersingular, and the probability that the elliptic curve (
2.3
) is also supersingular is estimated to be about
1/(2p)
.
This heuristic explains why this algorithm is expected to produce many superspecial Howe curves for sufficiently large
p
.

3
Genus-4 curves with completely decomposable Jacobians

In this section, we construct a family of genus-4 curves whose Jacobians are completely decomposable, namely, isogenous to a product of elliptic curves.
More precisely, we restrict our attention to Howe curves such that the genus-2 curve (
2.4
) falls into Case 1 (or its specialization).
The main purpose of this section is to present an algorithm (Algorithm
3.4
) that generates superspecial curves among them.

For parameters
s,t\in\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 13.0mu}}}{K}\!\smallsetminus\!\{0,\pm 1\}
with
s^{2}\neq t^{2}
, we consider two elliptic curves

\displaystyle E_{1}:y^{2}

\displaystyle=(x-1)(x-s)(x-t),

\displaystyle E_{2}:y^{2}

\displaystyle=(x+1)(x+s)(x+t).

We denote by
X_{s,t}
the desingularization of the fiber product
E_{1}\times_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}\hskip-1.13809ptE_{2}
, which is a Howe curve as discussed in Section
2.2
.
In particular, the curve
X_{s,t}
has genus 4 and is non-hyperelliptic.
Moreover, by Theorem
2.6
, the Jacobian of
X_{s,t}
is isogenous to
E_{1}\times E_{2}\times J(C_{3})
, where
C_{3}
is the genus-2 curve defined by

C_{3}:y^{2}=(x^{2}-1)(x^{2}-s^{2})(x^{2}-t^{2}).

Then, it follows from Proposition
2.1
that the Jacobian of
C_{3}
is isogenous to
E_{3}\times E_{4}
, where

\displaystyle E_{3}:y^{2}

\displaystyle=x(x-1)(x-\lambda_{3}),\quad\lambda_{3}\coloneqq\frac{t^{2}-s^{2}}{1-s^{2}},

\displaystyle E_{4}:y^{2}

\displaystyle=x(x-1)(x-\lambda_{4}),\quad\lambda_{4}\coloneqq\frac{t^{2}-s^{2}}{t^{2}(1-s^{2})},

(3.1)

and therefore the Jacobian of
X_{s,t}
is completely decomposable.
Since
E_{2}
is isomorphic to
E_{1}
, we obtain the following proposition:

Proposition 3.1
.

With the above notation, there exists an isogeny
J(X_{s,t})\rightarrow E_{1}^{2}\times E_{3}\times E_{4}
whose degree is a power of
2
.
In particular, the curve
X_{s,t}
is superspecial if and only if
E_{1},E_{3}
, and
E_{4}
are all supersingular.

As a corollary, if the characteristic
p
satisfies
p\equiv 5\pmod{6}
, then there exists a superspecial

X_{s,t}
, which coincides with the one constructed by
Kudo-Harashita-Howe
[
15
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Corollary 3.2
.

If
p\equiv 5\pmod{6}
, then a superspecial curve
X_{s,t}
of genus
4
in characteristic
p
exists.

Proof.

We define
s\coloneqq\omega
and
t\coloneqq\omega^{2}
, where
\omega
denotes a primitive cube root of unity.
Then, the three elliptic curves
E_{1},E_{3}
, and
E_{4}
are given by

\displaystyle E_{1}:y^{2}

\displaystyle=(x-1)(x-\omega)(x-\omega^{2}),

\displaystyle E_{3}:y^{2}

\displaystyle=x(x-1)(x-1-\omega),

\displaystyle E_{4}:y^{2}

\displaystyle=x(x-1)(x-1-\omega^{2}).

The
j
-invariants of these curves are all equal to
0
, and hence they are supersingular (cf.
[
20
, Example V.4.4]
).
Therefore, it follows from Proposition
3.1
that the curve
X_{s,t}
is superspecial.
∎

Next, the elliptic curve
E_{1}
can be written in Legendre form as

E_{1}:y^{2}=x(x-1)(x-\lambda_{1}),\quad\lambda_{1}\coloneqq\frac{t-s}{1-s}\vskip-1.70717pt

by the Möbius transformation
x\mapsto(x-s)/(1-s)
.
Note that the non-singularity of
E_{1},E_{3}
, and
E_{4}
implies that
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4}\neq 0,1
.
Additionally, the parameters
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{3}
satisfy the following conditions:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{3}\neq 0,\\
\lambda_{1}^{2}-\lambda_{3}\neq 0,\\
\lambda_{1}^{2}-2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{3}\neq 0,\\
2\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{1}^{2}-\lambda_{3}\neq 0.\end{array}\right.\vskip-0.56905pt

(3.2)

Indeed, a straightforward computation yields

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-t)(t-s)}{(1-s)(1+s)},

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{2}-\lambda_{3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2s(1-t)(t-s)}{(1-s)^{2}(1+s)},

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{2}-2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2(1-t)(t-s)}{(1-s)^{2}(1+s)},

\displaystyle 2\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{1}^{2}-\lambda_{3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2t(1-t)(t-s)}{(1-s)^{2}(1+s)},

which are all non-zero since
s,t\notin\{0,\pm 1\}
and
s^{2}\neq t^{2}
.
By combining suitable expressions among the above, we can express
s
and
t
in terms of
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{3}
explicitly:

s=\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}-\lambda_{3}}{\lambda_{1}^{2}-2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{3}},\quad t=\frac{2\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{1}^{2}-\lambda_{3}}{\lambda_{1}^{2}-2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{3}}.\vskip-0.56905pt

(3.3)

We now determine the constraints on
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{3}
under which our assumptions imposed to ensure that
C_{3}
is non-singular, namely
s,t\notin\{0,\pm 1\}
and
s^{2}\neq t^{2}
, are satisfied.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{3}\in\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 13.0mu}}}{K}\!\smallsetminus\!\{0,1\}
satisfying the conditions (
3.2
), the corresponding values of
s
and
t
given by (
3.3
) lie in
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 13.0mu}}}{K}\!\smallsetminus\!\{0,\pm 1\}
with
s^{2}\neq t^{2}
.

Proof.

By (
3.2
), both the numerators and the denominator
in (
3.3
) are non-zero; hence
s
and
t
are well-defined
and non-zero.
Also, a straightforward computation yields

\displaystyle s-1

\displaystyle=\frac{2(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{3})}{\lambda_{1}^{2}-2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{3}},

\displaystyle\quad s+1

\displaystyle=\frac{2\lambda_{1}(\lambda_{1}-1)}{\lambda_{1}^{2}-2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{3}},

\displaystyle t-1

\displaystyle=-\frac{2(\lambda_{1}-1)(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{3})}{\lambda_{1}^{2}-2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{3}},

\displaystyle\quad t+1

\displaystyle=\frac{2\lambda_{1}(\lambda_{3}-1)}{\lambda_{1}^{2}-2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{3}},

\displaystyle s-t

\displaystyle=\frac{2\lambda_{1}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{3})}{\lambda_{1}^{2}-2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{3}},

\displaystyle\quad s+t

\displaystyle=\frac{2\lambda_{3}(\lambda_{1}-1)}{\lambda_{1}^{2}-2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{3}},

and each of these values is non-zero.
This implies that
s\neq\pm 1
,
t\neq\pm 1
, and
s^{2}\neq t^{2}
, as desired.
∎

From the above discussion, for given
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{3}\in\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 13.0mu}}}{K}\!\smallsetminus\!\{0,1\}
satisfying the conditions (
3.2
), our curve
X_{s,t}
can be constructed via (
3.3
).
In particular, by choosing
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{3}
so that
E_{1}
and
E_{3}
are supersingular, it follows from Proposition
3.1
that
X_{s,t}
is superspecial if and only if
E_{4}
is also supersingular.
Hence, we can produce superspecial
X_{s,t}
as follows:

Algorithm 3.4
.

Input: A prime
p>5
.

Output: A pair
(s,t)
such that
X_{s,t}
is superspecial in characteristic
p
, or
\bot
.

Step 1.

Compute the set
\mathcal{T}
of values
\lambda\neq 0,1
for which the elliptic curve
y^{2}=x(x-1)(x-\lambda)
in Legendre form is supersingular in characteristic
p
.

Step 2.

For each pair
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{3})\in\mathcal{T}^{2}\hskip-0.56905pt
satisfying the conditions (
3.2
), check whether the elliptic curve (
3.1
) is

supersingular, where
(s,t)
is given by (
3.3
). If so, output
(s,t)
.

Step 3.

If no such
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{3})
is found, output
\bot
.

We mention that, since
\mathcal{T}\hskip-0.56905pt\subset\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}\hskip-0.85358pt
by the result of Auer-Top
[
1
, Proposition 2.2]
, the outputs
s
and
t
lie in
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
.

Also, it is well-known (cf.
[
20
, Theorem V.4.1]
) that the cardinality of
\mathcal{T}
computed in
Step 1
equals
(p-1)/2
.
There are
\varTheta(p^{2})
possible choices of pairs
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{3})
in
Step 2
, and the probability that the elliptic curve (
3.1
) is

supersingular is estimated to be about
1/(2p)
for each
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{3})
.
Therefore, the expected number of superspecial
X_{s,t}
in characteristic
p
is
\varTheta(p)
.
This suggests that, for sufficiently large

, Algorithm
3.4
will produce at

least one superspecial
X_{s,t}
. In fact, within the range of our experiments, such curves are found for all

(see Section
6.1
for details).

Remark 3.5
.

In our experiments, we first run Algorithm
3.4
with
\mathcal{T}
taken to be the set of
\lambda
corresponding to supersingular elliptic curves whose
j
-invariants lie in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
If this search fails to produce a superspecial

,

we then rerun Algorithm

with

as the full set of the
\lambda
corresponding to all supersingular elliptic curves, following the original procedure.
For most

tested in our experiments, this restricted choice of

suffices to yield at least one superspecial

, thereby significantly reducing the execution time required for

.

4
Genus-5 curves with completely decomposable Jacobians

In this section, we construct a family of generalized Howe curves of genus 5, specifically those whose Jacobians are completely decomposable.
Subsequently, we give an algorithm (Algorithm
4.3
) for generating superspecial curves among them.

Consider two genus-2 curves
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
sharing exactly
r=4
Weierstrass points:

\displaystyle\begin{split}C_{1}:y^{2}&=(x-a_{1})(x-a_{2})(x-a_{3})(x-a_{4})(x-b_{1})(x-b_{2}),\\[-4.2679pt]
C_{2}:y^{2}&=(x-a_{1})(x-a_{2})(x-a_{3})(x-a_{4})(x-c_{1})(x-c_{2}),\\[-2.56073pt]
\end{split}

(4.1)

where
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 13.0mu}}}{K})
are all distinct.
The desingularization of the fiber product
C_{1}\times_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}\hskip-0.28453ptC_{2}
is a generalized Howe curve, whose genus is
5
according to Proposition
2.5
.
In the following, we focus on the case where both genus-2 curves
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
fall into Case 3 (or its specialization).
Specifically, for parameters
s,t\in\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 13.0mu}}}{K}\!\smallsetminus\!\{0,\pm 1\}
with
s^{2}\hskip-0.56905pt\neq t^{2}

C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be genus-2 curves defined by

\displaystyle C_{1}:y^{2}

\displaystyle=x(x^{2}-1)(x^{2}-s^{2}),

\displaystyle C_{2}:y^{2}

\displaystyle=x(x^{2}-1)(x^{2}-t^{2}).

We denote by
Y_{s,t}
the generalized Howe curve constructed from these curves.
By Theorem
2.6
, the Jacobian of
Y_{s,t}
is isogenous to
J(C_{1})\hskip-0.28453pt\times\hskip-0.28453ptJ(C_{2})\hskip-0.28453pt\times\hskip-0.28453ptE_{3}
, where
E_{3}
is the elliptic curve defined by

E_{3}:y^{2}=(x^{2}-s^{2})(x^{2}-t^{2}).\vskip-0.56905pt

(4.2)

According to Proposition
2.2
, the Jacobian of each
C_{i}
is isogenous to
E_{i}^{2}
, where

E_{i}:y^{2}=(c_{i}+2)x^{3}-(3c_{i}-10)x^{2}+(3c_{i}-10)x-(c_{i}+2)

with
c_{1}\coloneqq s+1/s
and
c_{2}\coloneqq t+1/t
(we note that these values satisfy
c_{1}^{2}=s^{2}+1/s^{2}+2
and
c_{2}^{2}=t^{2}+1/t^{2}+2
).
Therefore, the Jacobian of
Y_{s,t}
is completely decomposable:

Proposition 4.1
.

With the above notation, there exists an isogeny
\hskip-0.56905ptJ(Y_{s,t})\rightarrow E_{1}^{2}\times E_{2}^{2}\times E_{3}
whose degree is a power of 2.
In particular, the curve
Y_{s,t}
is superspecial if and only if
E_{1},E_{2}
, and
E_{3}
are all supersingular.

Here, we define the polynomial
h(\beta)
by

h(\beta)\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor p/4\rfloor}\binom{(p-1)/2}{\lfloor(p+1)/4\rfloor+i}\hskip-1.42262pt\binom{(p-1)/2}{i}\beta^{i}.\vskip-2.56073pt

(4.3)

According to
[
8
, Proposition 1.9]
, the two genus-2 curves
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are superspecial if and only if
h(s^{2})=0
and
h(t^{2})=0
, respectively.
Hence, provided that
s^{2}
and
t^{2}
are roots of the polynomial (
4.3
), it follows from Theorem
2.6
that our curve
Y_{s,t}
is superspecial if and only if the elliptic curve
E_{3}
is supersingular.
Based on this property, one can construct a
naive
algorithm to generate superspecial
Y_{s,t}
as follows:

Algorithm 4.2
.

Input: A prime
p>5
.

Output: A pair
(s,t)
such that
Y_{s,t}
is superspecial in characteristic
p
, or
\bot
.

Step 1.

Compute the set
\mathcal{T}
of roots
\beta\neq 0,1
of the polynomial (
4.3
).

Step 2.

For each pair
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})\in\mathcal{T}^{2}
with
\beta_{1}\hskip-0.28453pt\neq\beta_{2}
, check whether the elliptic curve
y^{2}=(x^{2}-\beta_{1})(x^{2}-\beta_{2})
is supersingular.
If so, output any
(s,t)
with
s^{2}=\beta_{1}
and
t^{2}=\beta_{2}
.

Step 3.

If no such
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
is found, output
\bot
.

We mention that, by the author’s result
[
17
, Main Theorem A]
, the superspeciality of
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
implies that the outputs
s
and
t
lie in
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}\hskip-0.56905pt
.
Also, it is known from
[
8
, Proposition 1.14]
that the cardinality of
\mathcal{T}
computed in
Step 1
equals
\lfloor p/4\rfloor
.
There are
\varTheta(p^{2})
possible choices of pairs
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
in
Step 2
, and the probability that the elliptic curve
E_{3}:y^{2}=(x^{2}-\beta_{1})(x^{2}-\beta_{2})
is supersingular can be estimated to be approximately
1/(2p)
for each
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
.
Therefore, the expected number of superspecial
Y_{s,t}
in characteristic
p
is
\varTheta(p)
.

However, finding the roots of the polynomial
h(\beta)
is computationally expensive in practice, and therefore Algorithm
4.2
above is not very efficient.
Instead, we explore a way to determine
s
and
t
from the
j
-invariants of
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
.
We recall from Proposition
2.2
that the
j
-invariants of
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are given by

\displaystyle j(E_{1})=64\frac{(3c_{1}-10)^{3}}{(c_{1}-2)(c_{1}+2)^{2}}

\displaystyle=64\frac{(3s-1)^{3}(s-3)^{3}}{(s-1)^{2}(s+1)^{4}},

\displaystyle j(E_{2})=64\frac{(3c_{2}-10)^{3}}{(c_{2}-2)(c_{2}+2)^{2}}

\displaystyle=64\frac{(3t-1)^{3}(t-3)^{3}}{(t-1)^{2}(t+1)^{4}}.

Consequently, for given
j_{1},j_{2}\in\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 13.0mu}}}{K}
, our curve
Y_{s,t}
can be constructed by solving the equations

\displaystyle\begin{split}64(3s-1)^{3}(s-3)^{3}-j_{1}(s-1)^{2}(s+1)^{4}&=0,\\[-3.98337pt]
64(3t-1)^{3}(t-3)^{3}-j_{2}(t-1)^{2}(t+1)^{4}&=0\\[-1.99168pt]
\end{split}

(4.4)

for
s
and
t
, provided that
s,t\notin\hskip-0.28453pt\{0,\pm 1\}
and
s^{2}\hskip-0.56905pt\neq t^{2}
holds.
In particular, by choosing
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
as supersingular
j
-invariants, it follows from Proposition
4.1
that
Y_{s,t}
is superspecial if and only if
E_{3}
is supersingular.
Hence, we can produce superspecial
Y_{s,t}
as follows:

Algorithm 4.3
.

Input: A prime
p>5
.

Output: A pair
(s,t)
such that
Y_{s,t}
is superspecial in characteristic
p
, or
\bot
.

Step 1.

Compute the set
\mathcal{S}
of supersingular
j
-invariants in characteristic
p
.

Step 2.

For each pair
(j_{1},j_{2})\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
, solve the equations (
4.4
), and check whether the elliptic curve (
4.2
) is supersingular for each solution
(s,t)
satisfying
s,t\notin\hskip-0.28453pt\{0,\pm 1\}
and
s^{2}\hskip-0.56905pt\neq t^{2}
.
If so, output
(s,t)
.

Step 3.

If no such
(s,t)
is found, output
\bot
.

As discussed above, the expected number of superspecial
Y_{s,t}
is
\varTheta(p)
, which suggests that Algorithm
4.3
will yield at least one such
Y_{s,t}
for sufficiently large
p
.
In fact, within the range of our experiments, such a curve is found for all
p>137
(see Section
6.2
for details).

5
Genus-6 curves with completely decomposable Jacobians

In this section, we construct a family of generalized Howe curves of genus 6, specifically those whose Jacobians are completely decomposable.
Subsequently, we give an algorithm (Algorithm
5.4
) for generating superspecial curves among them.

Consider two genus-2 curves
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
sharing exactly
r=3
Weierstrass points:

\displaystyle\begin{split}C_{1}:y^{2}&=(x-a_{1})(x-a_{2})(x-a_{3})(x-b_{1})(x-b_{2})(x-b_{3}),\\[-4.2679pt]
C_{2}:y^{2}&=(x-a_{1})(x-a_{2})(x-a_{3})(x-c_{1})(x-c_{2})(x-c_{3}),\\[-2.27621pt]
\end{split}

(5.1)

where
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 13.0mu}}}{K})
are all distinct.
The desingularization of the fiber product
C_{1}\times_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}\hskip-0.28453ptC_{2}
is a generalized Howe curve, whose genus is
6
according to Proposition
2.5
.
In the following, we focus on the case where both genus-2 curves
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
fall into Case 2 (or its specialization).
More explicitly, for parameters
s,t\in\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 13.0mu}}}{K}\hskip-0.85358pt\smallsetminus\hskip-0.85358pt\{0\}
such that
1,s^{6},t^{6}
are pairwise distinct, let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be genus-2 curves defined by

\displaystyle C_{1}:y^{2}

\displaystyle=(x^{3}-1)(x^{3}-s^{6}),

\displaystyle C_{2}:y^{2}

\displaystyle=(x^{3}-1)(x^{3}-t^{6}).

We denote by
Z_{s,t}
the generalized Howe curve constructed from these curves.
By Theorem
2.6
, the Jacobian of
Z_{s,t}
is isogenous to
J(C_{1})\hskip-0.28453pt\times\hskip-0.28453ptJ(C_{2})\hskip-0.28453pt\times\hskip-0.28453ptJ(C_{3})
, where
C_{3}
is the third genus-2 curve defined by

C_{3}:y^{2}=(x^{3}-s^{6})(x^{3}-t^{6}),

which is isomorphic to the genus-2 curve
y^{2}=(x^{3}-1)(x^{3}-s^{6}/t^{6})
via the Möbius transformation
x\mapsto x/t^{2}
.
Here, we prepare the following lemma:

Lemma 5.1
.

If
Z_{s,t}
is superspecial, then both
s
and
t
belong to
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
.

Proof.

Applying the Möbius transformation
x\mapsto\hskip-0.56905pt(1-\omega x)/(x-\omega)
maps the genus-2 curve
C_{1}
to its Rosenhain form
y^{2}=x(x-1)(x-\lambda_{1})(x-\lambda_{2})(x-\lambda_{3})
with

\lambda_{1}\coloneqq-\omega\frac{s^{2}-\omega^{2}}{s^{2}-\omega},\quad\lambda_{2}\coloneqq-\omega\frac{s^{2}-1}{s^{2}-\omega^{2}},\quad\lambda_{3}\coloneqq-\omega\frac{s^{2}-\omega}{s^{2}-1},\vskip-4.2679pt

where
\omega
denotes a primitive cube root of unity (we note that
\omega
lies in
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
, since
\omega^{p^{2}}\hskip-1.99168pt=\omega\cdot(\omega^{3})^{(p^{2}-1)/3}=\omega
).
By Theorem
2.6
, if
Z_{s,t}
is superspecial, then
C_{1}
is also superspecial, which implies that
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
, and
\lambda_{3}
all lie in
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}\hskip-1.42262pt
by
[
17
, Main Theorem A]
.
Hence, a direct computation shows that

s^{2}=\frac{\omega^{2}\hskip-1.42262pt+\hskip-0.28453pt\lambda_{3}}{\omega+\lambda_{3}}\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}.\vskip-1.42262pt

In addition, one can verify that

\displaystyle(1-\lambda_{1})\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=\biggl(-\omega^{2}\frac{s^{2}-1}{s^{2}-\omega}\biggr)\hskip-1.42262pt\biggl(-\omega\frac{s^{2}-1}{s^{2}-\omega^{2}}\biggr)=\frac{(s^{2}-1)^{2}}{(s^{2}-\omega)(s^{2}-\omega^{2})},

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{3s^{2}}{(s^{2}-\omega)(s^{2}-\omega^{2})},

both of which are squares in
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}\hskip-0.56905pt
, again by
[
17
, Main Theorem A]
.
It follows from these equations that

s^{2}=\frac{1}{3}(s^{2}-1)^{2}\cdot\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{(1-\lambda_{1})\lambda_{2}}.\vskip-1.99168pt

Since the right-hand side is a square in
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
,
we conclude that
s\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
.
Following the same procedure for
C_{2}
, we obtain
t\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}\hskip-0.56905pt
, which completes the proof.
∎

According to Proposition
2.3
, the Jacobian of each
C_{i}
is isogenous to
E_{i}^{2}
, where

E_{i}:y^{2}=(c_{i}+2)x^{3}-(3c_{i}-30)x^{2}+(3c_{i}+30)x-(c_{i}-2)

with
c_{1}\coloneqq s^{3}+1/s^{3},\,c_{2}\coloneqq t^{3}+1/t^{3}
, and
c_{3}\coloneqq s^{3}/t^{3}+t^{3}/s^{3}
(this is why we define
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
using
s^{6}
and
t^{6}

instead of
s^{3}
and
t^{3}
; to ensure that each
c_{i}
is a rational function in
s
and
t
).
Therefore, the Jacobian of
Z_{s,t}
is completely decomposable:

Proposition 5.2
.

With the above notation, there exists an isogeny
\hskip-0.56905ptJ(Z_{s,t})\rightarrow E_{1}^{2}\times E_{2}^{2}\times E_{3}^{2}
whose degree is

a product of powers of 2 and 3.
In particular, the curve
Z_{s,t}
is superspecial if and only if
E_{1},E_{2}
, and
E_{3}
are all supersingular.

Here, we define the polynomial
g(\alpha)
by

g(\alpha)\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor p/3\rfloor}\binom{(p-1)/2}{\lfloor(p+1)/6\rfloor+i}\hskip-1.42262pt\binom{(p-1)/2}{i}\alpha^{i}.\vskip-2.56073pt

(5.2)

It follows from
[
8
, Proposition 1.8]
that the three genus-2 curves
C_{1},C_{2}
, and
C_{3}
are superspecial if and only if
g(s^{6})=0,\,g(t^{6})=0
, and
g(s^{6}/t^{6})=0
, respectively.
Hence, by Theorem
2.6
, the curve
Z_{s,t}
is superspecial if and only if
s^{6},t^{6}
, and
s^{6}/t^{6}
are roots of the polynomial (
5.2
).
Based on this property, one can construct a
naive
algorithm to generate superspecial
Z_{s,t}
as follows:

Algorithm 5.3
.

Input: A prime
p>5
.

Output: A pair
(s,t)
such that
Z_{s,t}
is superspecial in characteristic
p
, or
\bot
.

Step 1.

Compute the set
\mathcal{T}
of roots
\alpha\neq 0,1
of the polynomial (
5.2
).

Step 2.

For each pair
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in\mathcal{T}^{2}
with
\alpha_{1}\hskip-0.28453pt\neq\alpha_{2}
, check whether
\alpha_{1}/\alpha_{2}
belongs to the set
\mathcal{T}
.
If so, output any
(s,t)
with
s^{6}=\alpha_{1}
and
t^{6}=\alpha_{2}
.

Step 3.

If no such
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
is found, output
\bot
.

Due to Lemma
5.1
, the outputs
s
and
t
(as well as
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
) lie in
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}\hskip-0.56905pt
.
It follows from
[
8
, Proposition 1.14]
that the cardinality of
\mathcal{T}
computed in
Step 1
equals
\lfloor p/3\rfloor
, which implies that there are
\varTheta(p^{2})
possible choices of pairs
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
in
Step 2
.
The probability that
\alpha_{1}/\alpha_{2}
also lies in
\mathcal{T}
is estimated to be approximately
1/(3p)
for each
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
, and thus the expected number of superspecial
Z_{s,t}
in characteristic
p
is
\varTheta(p)
.

However, finding the roots of the polynomial
g(\alpha)
is computationally expensive in practice, and therefore Algorithm
5.3
above is not very efficient.
Instead, we describe a way to determine
s
and
t
from the
j
-invariants of
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
.
We recall from Proposition
2.3
that the
j
-invariants of
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are given by

\displaystyle j(E_{1})=6912\frac{(2c_{1}-5)^{3}}{(c_{1}-2)(c_{1}+2)^{3}}

\displaystyle=6912\frac{s^{3}(2s^{3}-1)^{3}(s^{3}-2)^{3}}{(s^{3}-1)^{2}(s^{3}+1)^{6}},

\displaystyle j(E_{2})=6912\frac{(2c_{2}-5)^{3}}{(c_{2}-2)(c_{2}+2)^{3}}

\displaystyle=6912\frac{t^{3}(2t^{3}-1)^{3}(t^{3}-2)^{3}}{(t^{3}-1)^{2}(t^{3}+1)^{6}}.

Consequently, for given
j_{1},j_{2}\in\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 13.0mu}}}{K}
, our curve
Z_{s,t}
can be constructed by solving the equations

\displaystyle\begin{split}6912s^{3}(2s^{3}-1)^{3}(s^{3}-2)^{3}-j_{1}(s^{3}-1)^{2}(s^{3}+1)^{6}&=0,\\[-3.41432pt]
6912t^{3}(2t^{3}-1)^{3}(t^{3}-2)^{3}-j_{2}(t^{3}-1)^{2}(t^{3}+1)^{6}&=0\\[-0.85358pt]
\end{split}

(5.3)

for
s^{3}
and
t^{3}
, provided that
s^{3},t^{3}\notin\{0,1\}
and
s^{6}\neq t^{6}
holds.
Choosing
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
as supersingular
j
-invariants, it follows from Proposition
5.2
that
Z_{s,t}
is superspecial if and only if
E_{3}
is supersingular.
Also, the
j
-invariant of
E_{3}
can be computed from
s^{3}
and
t^{3}
via the following equation:

j(E_{3})=6912\frac{(2c_{3}-5)^{3}}{(c_{3}-2)(c_{3}+2)^{3}}=6912\frac{s^{3}t^{3}(2s^{3}-t^{3})^{3}(s^{3}-2t^{3})^{3}}{(s^{3}-t^{3})^{2}(s^{3}+t^{3})^{6}}.

(5.4)

Therefore, we can generate superspecial
Z_{s,t}
as follows.
In practice, it is not necessary to determine
s
and
t
; it suffices to compute only
s^{3}
and
t^{3}
.
For the sake of clarity, however, we describe the following algorithm as outputting
(s,t)
.

Algorithm 5.4
.

Input: A prime
p>5
.

Output: A pair
(s,t)
such that
Z_{s,t}
is superspecial in characteristic
p
, or
\bot
.

Step 1.

Compute the set
\mathcal{S}
of supersingular
j
-invariants in characteristic
p
.

Step 2.

For each pair
(j_{1},j_{2})\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
, solve the equations (
5.3
), and check whether the value (
5.4
) belongs to the set
\mathcal{S}
for each solution
(s,t)
satisfying
s^{3},t^{3}\notin\hskip-0.28453pt\{0,1\}
and
s^{6}\hskip-0.56905pt\neq t^{6}
.
If so, output
(s,t)
.

Step 3.

If no such
(s,t)
is found, output
\bot
.

As discussed above, the expected number of superspecial
Z_{s,t}
is
\varTheta(p)
, which suggests that Algorithm
5.4
will yield at least one such
Z_{s,t}
for sufficiently large
p
.
In fact, within the range of our experiments, such a curve is found for all
p>571
(see Section
6.3
for details).

6.1
The existence of superspecial genus-4 curves

We applied Algorithm

for all primes

\approx

Corollary 6.1
.

There exists a superspecial curve of genus
4
in every characteristic
p
with
7<p<10^{6}
.

Remark 6.2
.

To demonstrate the efficiency of Algorithm
3.4
, we compared its performance with the method of Kudo-Harashita-Howe.
In the range
7<p<20000
, the total execution time of their algorithm was 41,491 seconds (
\approx
11.5 hours), whereas our algorithm required only 29.7 seconds to test all the same primes.
While our algorithm failed to output a superspecial genus-4 curve for
p\in\hskip-0.56905pt\{13,19,73\}
, the time spent on these cases was negligible and had almost no impact on the overall comparison.
Consequently, our algorithm achieves a speedup of approximately 1,400 times compared to the method of Kudo-Harashita-Howe.
