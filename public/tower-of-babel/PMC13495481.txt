Background

Timely access to definitive care is a major determinant of outcome in several time-dependent conditions. This is well established for acute coronary syndromes, particularly STEMI and very high-risk NSTEMI, where system delays and interfacility transfer delays are associated with delayed reperfusion and worse outcomes [1–8]. Similar time-dependent considerations apply to acute cerebrovascular emergencies, especially candidates for reperfusion therapy or neurosurgical intervention [9–15]. Major trauma, aortic emergencies, and selected hyperbaric or toxicological emergencies also require rapid access to specialized definitive care when specific interventions are not available at the referring facility [16–29].

Interfacility transfers represent a critical and often under-structured phase of care in time-dependent conditions. When patients initially present to facilities without access to definitive treatment, transfer delays may substantially offset the benefits of specialized therapies. Helicopter Emergency Medical Services (HEMS) may provide clinical and organizational advantages in selected secondary interfacility transfers by reducing transport time, overcoming geographic or infrastructural barriers, and enabling advanced medical support during transfer [30–32]. This potential role is particularly relevant when ground ambulance transport is limited by distance, terrain, or traffic congestion, and when the receiving facility provides highly specialized and time-dependent interventions such as primary percutaneous coronary intervention, mechanical thrombectomy, neurosurgical procedures, advanced trauma care, endovascular repair, or hyperbaric oxygen therapy [2, 31, 32].

In Italy, although HEMS is widely available, activation criteria for secondary interfacility transfers remain heterogeneous across regions. Italy’s geographically complex terrain—41.6% hills, 35.2% mountains, and 23.2% plains—may further contribute to regional variability in HEMS utilization. This variability may result in differences in access to advanced therapeutic options and inconsistent use of HEMS resources [31–33]. Inappropriate activation may also affect availability for primary missions or other time-critical emergencies, underscoring the need for clear, standardized, and evidence-informed decision-making criteria [31].

While several international systems have developed frameworks to support HEMS utilization, no national consensus in Italy currently exists specifically addressing secondary interfacility transfers. This lack of standardization contributes to variability in clinical practice, inequity in patient management, and potential inefficiencies in resource allocation [30, 34].

This expert consensus document was therefore developed to provide context-informed recommendations to support decision-making regarding HEMS activation for secondary interfacility transfers. By integrating available evidence with expert clinical judgment, the document aims to promote consistency in clinical practice, optimize resource utilization, and support equitable and timely access to definitive care.

Methods

Scope and target audience

This document provides consensus-based recommendations on the use of HEMS in interfacility transfers. It defines the scope of clinical and organizational criteria considered for HEMS activation, including transport time, geographic and logistical factors, and system-level resource constraints.

This consensus focuses on rotor-wing HEMS, which represent the standard modality for interfacility transfers within the Italian EMS system, and on adult secondary interfacility transfers for time-dependent or time-relevant conditions. Fixed-wing aeromedical transport is used in selected long-distance or specialized scenarios but follows different organizational pathways and was therefore considered outside the scope of this document. Neonatal transfers, incubator transport, pediatric transfers, and highly specialized ICU-to-ICU transfers, including patients requiring advanced organ support (e.g., ECMO), were also considered outside the scope, as they involve distinct clinical pathways, dedicated transport systems, and specific organizational frameworks.

This consensus was developed within the context of the Italian EMS–HEMS system and reflects its specific organizational, regulatory, and geographic characteristics. It is not intended to function as a universal European standard; rather, it offers a context-informed decision model that may be adapted to healthcare systems with comparable structures. Differences in governance, dispatch models, infrastructure, and interfacility transfer pathways across countries may require modification of the proposed criteria.

The document is intended for healthcare professionals involved in the activation, coordination, and execution of secondary interfacility transfers, as well as healthcare administrators and policymakers responsible for the organization of emergency medical networks.

Operational context of the Italian HEMS system

In Italy, HEMS is organized at a regional level, and activation pathways may differ across regions.

Primary missions (scene response) are typically activated through the national emergency call system (National Emergency Number, NUE 112), which is routed to the regional EMS dispatch center. Activation generally includes an initial feasibility assessment (e.g., weather and flight conditions), followed by mission authorization and rapid deployment of the HEMS crew.

In contrast, secondary interfacility transfers are usually initiated by the referring hospital (spoke center or local facility). The EMS coordination center evaluates the clinical indication, logistical factors, and resource availability, and determines whether HEMS activation is appropriate.

Final decision-making is performed by the EMS dispatch physician in coordination with the HEMS medical crew, considering clinical urgency, transport feasibility, and operational constraints. Operational capabilities and availability may vary across bases (e.g., night operations and instrument flight capability where applicable).

In Italy, HEMS crews include one or two pilots and a dedicated medical team composed of a physician and a nurse. The physician is trained in anesthesia and intensive care or emergency medicine, while the nurse has specific expertise in emergency and critical care. This configuration enables the provision of advanced airway management, hemodynamic support, and other critical care interventions during transport.

In some regions, additional technical personnel may be part of the crew, particularly for missions involving hoist operations or mountain rescue (e.g., HEMS technical crew members or National Alpine and Speleological Rescue Corps – CNSAS). As such, crew composition may vary depending on mission profile and regional organization.

The Italian emergency care system is based on a hub-and-spoke model, in which advanced treatments are concentrated in tertiary referral centers, while many patients initially present to peripheral hospitals. In this context, interfacility transfer represents a critical step in the care pathway. The relative contribution of HEMS versus ground EMS depends on geographic factors, transport distances, and system organization, and may significantly influence access to definitive care in time-sensitive conditions.

Panel selection and organisation

A panel of Italian experts in pre-hospital emergency medicine, critical care, and HEMS operations was convened to develop this consensus. Panel members were selected through purposive sampling to ensure representation of key domains relevant to interfacility transfer and HEMS operations. Efforts were made to minimize selection bias by including participants from different geographic regions, institutional settings, and professional roles, and by avoiding over-representation of single institutions or local operational models. The panel included clinicians with roles in EMS dispatch coordination, HEMS operational leadership, and active clinical involvement in aeromedical services, as well as individuals with academic and scientific society engagement in emergency and critical care.

This composition reflects the organizational and clinical diversity of the Italian EMS–HEMS system, including representation across Northern, Central, and Southern regions.

Eligibility for panel participation required direct clinical involvement in secondary interfacility transfers and active experience within EMS or HEMS operational systems. Recognized expertise in time-critical emergency care at clinical, organizational, or academic level was also considered. Expertise was defined as a combination of sustained clinical practice, operational responsibility within EMS–HEMS systems, and/or relevant scientific contribution in the field.

Ten clinicians constituted the voting panel and participated in the modified Delphi process. A designated methodologist (G.A.) supervised question formulation, evidence synthesis, and consensus procedures to ensure internal methodological consistency but did not participate in the voting process. Methodological oversight was further supported by the involvement of authors with experience in clinical research design and evidence appraisal.

All ten panelists are included among the listed authors. The remaining authors did not participate in the Delphi voting phase and contributed to study conception, methodological supervision, evidence synthesis, and manuscript drafting and revision. All authors meet the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) authorship criteria.

The panel size was deliberately limited to ensure feasibility, effective discussion, and consistent participation across all phases of the consensus process, while maintaining representation of key domains relevant to HEMS operations and interfacility transfer.

Clinical question formulation

Clinical domains were identified through structured preliminary discussions among panel members, focusing on conditions characterized by high time sensitivity, the need for specialized interventions, or potential benefit from advanced in-transit care. For each domain, key clinical questions were formulated using the PICO framework (Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome) to ensure standardized question structure and internal consistency across statements. Each PICO question was addressed independently within the evidence review and consensus process.

Literature review and evidence synthesis

A scoping review of the literature was conducted to inform statement development. Literature searches were performed across major biomedical databases, including MEDLINE, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library, as well as relevant international guideline repositories and specialty society documents (e.g., ESC, AHA/ASA, ATLS, UHMS).

Search strategies were developed separately for each PICO-defined clinical domain to allow targeted retrieval of relevant evidence. Predefined keywords and Boolean operators were adapted to each database. The review focused on publications from 2010 to 2026 and included randomized trials, observational studies, secondary evidence syntheses, position statements, and international guidelines addressing HEMS utilization, interfacility transfer, and time-dependent emergency conditions. Study selection and data extraction were performed by the coordinating authors in a structured manner and used to inform the narrative synthesis and subsequent panel discussion.

The literature was screened and narratively synthesized to support expert deliberation. Given the objectives of this consensus, the review was not designed as a formal systematic review. No predefined study selection flow diagram, formal risk-of-bias assessment, or PRISMA methodology was applied. The aim of the review was to contextualize available evidence and inform expert discussion rather than to produce a quantitative pooled estimate. The detailed search strategies, including keywords and database-specific queries for each clinical domain, are provided in the Electronic Supplement.

The review also incorporated international literature to contextualize HEMS utilization across different healthcare systems. However, a formal comparative system analysis was not performed, as this was beyond the scope of the present consensus.

Consensus process

A modified Delphi approach was used to develop and validate consensus statements. This approach was designed as a pragmatic, expert-informed process integrating structured discussion with anonymous voting.

This design reflects commonly adopted pragmatic adaptations of Delphi methodology in clinical consensus development.

Draft statements were initially developed by designated panel members based on PICO-defined clinical questions and available literature. All panel members participated both in statement development and in the subsequent anonymous voting process. These statements were circulated to the full panel and discussed in structured sessions to refine wording and resolve ambiguities. Discussions were moderated to ensure balanced participation and to minimize the influence of dominant individuals during the statement refinement phase.

Following this discussion phase, statements were submitted to anonymous online voting by all panel members using a 1–9 Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 9 = strongly agree). Anonymity was preserved during voting to reduce the potential influence of seniority or dominant participants.

A predefined consensus threshold of ≥ 80% agreement was required for statement approval. All statements reached the predefined threshold during the first voting round. The high level of agreement reflects the preliminary structured discussion and iterative refinement of statements prior to voting, which minimized ambiguity and aligned interpretation. No statements required substantial reformulation; therefore, additional Delphi rounds were predefined but not required, as all statements reached the predefined consensus threshold in the first round.

Agreement percentages for each approved statement are reported in Supplementary Table S1.

Consensus statements reflect the integration of available evidence and expert clinical judgment. Given the heterogeneity and largely observational nature of the evidence base across clinical domains, a formal GRADE methodology was not applied. Nevertheless, the supporting rationale and key references underpinning each statement are explicitly described to enhance transparency.

All authors reviewed and approved the final version of the manuscript prior to submission.

This consensus is grounded in a pragmatic, evidence-informed approach integrating available literature and expert clinical judgment through a structured modified Delphi process.

Conflict of interest management

All panel participants were informed about the purpose and structure of the consensus process and agreed to participate. Participation was voluntary, and all contributors consented to the use of anonymized voting data for the development of this manuscript.

All panel members were required to disclose any intellectual or financial conflicts of interest that could potentially influence their participation. No financial compensation was provided for involvement in the project, and no industry funding or external sponsorship was involved in the development of this document. No conflicts of interest were declared.

Background

Time to reperfusion is a major determinant of survival and myocardial salvage in patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) [1]. System-related delays, particularly those related to interfacility transfer, may adversely affect clinical outcomes in time-dependent conditions. Even modest reductions in ischemic time may have clinical relevance; however, activation decisions should balance potential myocardial salvage with overall system efficiency and resource stewardship. Current international guidelines recommend primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) within 120 min from first medical contact [2, 3].

When the expected FMC-to-PCI time via ground transport exceeds this threshold, rapid transfer to a PCI-capable center becomes critical. In geographically remote areas or in the presence of significant logistical barriers, Helicopter Emergency Medical Services (HEMS) may substantially reduce transfer time, facilitate early access to the catheterization laboratory, and allow appropriate monitoring and stabilization during transport [4]. In these settings, HEMS use may be justified when it results in a clinically meaningful reduction in time to reperfusion compared with ground ambulance transfer [5, 6].

The magnitude of clinically meaningful time reduction should be interpreted within the operational and system-level framework described in the RATIONAL-HEMS model.

Interfacility transfer represents a major source of delay in pharmaco-invasive pathways, particularly in regions with long distances or limited road accessibility. In these settings, HEMS may reduce transfer time, facilitate early catheterization laboratory activation, and provide advanced monitoring and hemodynamic support during transport. Background

Very High Risk NSTEMI patients are characterized by a markedly increased short-term risk of mortality and life-threatening complications. According to current international guidelines, the presence of Very High Risk features warrants an immediate invasive strategy, ideally within 2 h from diagnosis [7].

In this subgroup, interfacility transfer delays are clinically relevant, as prolonged time to coronary angiography may exacerbate hemodynamic instability and ischemic burden. Interhospital transfer has been identified as a major contributor to treatment delay in acute coronary syndromes, particularly for patients initially admitted to non–PCI-capable hospitals [5, 6].

In geographically remote areas or in the presence of significant logistical barriers, HEMS may facilitate faster mobilization, direct routing to the catheterization laboratory, and provision of advanced critical care support during transport. In these settings, HEMS may be considered when it results in a clinically meaningful reduction in time to invasive management compared with ground ambulance transfer [5–7].

In this high-risk subgroup, the choice of transport modality should not be based solely on expected time reduction, but also on the level of clinical support required during transfer. Patients with hemodynamic instability, malignant arrhythmias, acute pulmonary edema, or post–cardiac arrest status may require physician-led monitoring, vasoactive support, advanced airway management, and real-time clinical decision-making during transport. In such cases, HEMS may offer an advantage not only in terms of speed but also in terms of the capability to deliver advanced critical care en route [34].

Background

High Risk NSTEMI patients benefit from an early invasive strategy, typically within 24 h from diagnosis, which has been shown to reduce recurrent ischemic events but has a limited impact on short-term mortality [8, 35]. Unlike Very High Risk NSTEMI, these patients are usually hemodynamically stable and do not require immediate (< 2 h) coronary angiography according to current international guidelines [7].

Given the finite availability of HEMS, prioritization of time-critical conditions is essential. In most healthcare settings, early invasive management for High Risk NSTEMI can be safely achieved through organized ground transfer within recommended timelines [35]. Routine use of HEMS in this subgroup is therefore unlikely to confer a meaningful clinical advantage and may negatively affect system efficiency and availability for higher-priority missions.

However, in selected geographic contexts characterized by long distances, complex terrain, or significant logistical barriers, ground transfer may result in substantial delays beyond guideline-recommended timeframes. In such cases, HEMS may be considered when it provides a clinically relevant reduction in time to coronary angiography compared with ground ambulance transfer [5–7].

Background

Low Risk NSTEMI patients do not derive a prognostic benefit from accelerated invasive management in terms of mortality or major ischemic outcomes. International guidelines support a delayed or selective invasive strategy in this subgroup, with coronary angiography typically performed within 72 h when clinically indicated [7, 8, 35, 36].

In most healthcare settings, transfer within this timeframe is generally feasible through planned ground ambulance transport without compromising patient outcomes. Routine use of HEMS in low-risk NSTEMI is therefore unlikely to provide additional clinical benefit, and may impact system efficiency by reducing availability for higher-priority, time-critical missions. HEMS activation should be considered only in exceptional circumstances, such as when ground transfer is not feasible due to geographic or logistical constraints.

Background

Structured prehospital stroke screening tools, such as the Cincinnati Prehospital Stroke Scale (CPSS) and BEFAST (Balance, Eyes, Face, Arms, Speak, Time), support early identification of suspected stroke during initial medical contact. These tools are designed to rapidly detect focal neurological deficits and facilitate timely pathway activation within organized stroke systems of care [37–39].

In the context of secondary interfacility transfers, structured neurological screening is not intended as a diagnostic instrument, but rather as a trigger for early pathway activation and coordination with stroke-capable centers. Early recognition is considered essential for appropriate transfer decisions, including consideration of HEMS in time-dependent scenarios such as candidates for intravenous thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy [40].

Systematic use of standardized stroke screening during initial triage reduces variability, improves communication between referring and receiving centers, and supports timely mobilization of specialized stroke resources within regional networks [41].

Background

Early pre-notification and parallel activation of stroke teams are critical determinants of effective time reduction. HEMS can shorten transfer times in areas with long distances, limited road access, or traffic constraints [9–11]. In drip-and-ship models, initiation of intravenous thrombolysis at a primary stroke center followed by expedited transfer to a thrombectomy-capable center may further optimize time to reperfusion, and HEMS may facilitate this strategy in geographically complex regions [40].

Early administration of 100% oxygen and careful hemodynamic and ventilatory management are critical during transfer [65].

In the Italian geographic context, typical HEMS flight altitudes rarely reach levels associated with clinically significant expansion of intravascular gas bubbles; nevertheless, sea-level cabin pressure should be maintained whenever feasible.

Background

Differentiating between DCI and AGE in the acute setting may be challenging, as clinical presentations can overlap and initial information regarding dive profile or exposure may be incomplete. Importantly, both conditions share the same definitive treatment strategy—early hyperbaric oxygen therapy—and diagnostic uncertainty should not delay transfer to a hyperbaric facility [23, 65].

When HBOT is indicated or potentially indicated, transport decisions should prioritize minimizing time to recompression. Background

Acute exposure to hydrocarbons and industrial solvents may occur in a variety of environmental, occupational, or accidental settings and can result in aspiration pneumonitis, acute lung injury, or central nervous system depression. Severe cases may progress to hypoxemic respiratory failure requiring advanced ventilatory support and intensive care admission. Early recognition of respiratory compromise and timely access to critical care resources are essential to prevent clinical deterioration [66].

Exposure to specific oxidizing aromatic compounds (e.g., aniline, nitrobenzene, and related benzene derivatives), can induce clinically significant methemoglobinemia by oxidizing hemoglobin iron and impairing oxygen delivery. Severe methemoglobinemia may present with cyanosis, altered mental status, cardiovascular instability, or refractory hypoxemia despite supplemental oxygen [67].

Methylene blue is the first-line antidotal therapy and is effective in most cases. However, severe methemoglobinemia refractory to standard treatment, or situations in which methylene blue is contraindicated (e.g., glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency), have been described. In these selected cases, hyperbaric oxygen therapy may be considered as a rescue strategy to improve oxygen delivery and tissue perfusion. When rapid access to advanced ICU support or hyperbaric facilities is limited by distance or logistical constraints, HEMS may reduce transfer time and facilitate timely access to advanced critical care or hyperbaric facilities in geographically or logistically constrained settings [68].

Operational and system-level constraints affecting HEMS secondary transfers

Challenges to implementation and system-level considerations

Despite the potential benefits of HEMS in time-critical interfacility transfers, the implementation of standardized activation criteria is challenged by relevant organizational, logistical, and regulatory constraints. We explicitly acknowledge the marked regional variability of emergency medical systems, infrastructural resources, and governance models, which may limit the uniform applicability of the proposed recommendations. For this reason, the following considerations aim to contextualize the recommendations within real-world operational constraints and to highlight pragmatic mitigation strategies, rather than prescriptive solutions.

Previous national recommendations, including those promoted by SIAARTI, have already highlighted the complexity of integrating clinical indications with heterogeneous organizational and infrastructural contexts, reinforcing the need for context-aware and adaptable implementation strategies [34].

Governance and centralization of HEMS coordination

In several regions, HEMS governance is not fully centralized or seamlessly integrated with EMS dispatch centers responsible for primary missions. Fragmented governance may result in suboptimal prioritization, delayed activation, or inaccurate real-time assessment of asset availability. Potential mitigation strategies include the progressive adoption of integrated dispatch models, in which primary and secondary missions are coordinated within a unified operational framework, supported by shared priority algorithms and real-time resource visibility. Evidence from centralized trauma and cardiac networks suggests that integrated governance may improve both efficiency and equity of access to advanced care.

Regulatory constraints on hospital helipads (ENAC/EASA)

Aviation regulations governing hospital helipads frequently limit direct access to emergency departments or impose operational constraints that may be incompatible with extreme time-critical scenarios. While these regulations are essential for flight safety, their clinical impact should be explicitly considered in pathway design. Potential mitigation strategies include the strategic designation of HEMS-receiving hubs, predefined fast-track ground links between helipads and critical care areas, and early activation of receiving teams to offset unavoidable transfer-related delays [69].

Helipad-to-definitive-care distance and multiple transfers

The frequent need for multiple patient transfers (base → referring hospital → helipad → receiving hospital) introduces cumulative delays and increases the risk of physiological instability and adverse events. Each handover represents a potential safety and time penalty. Potential mitigation strategies include minimizing intermediate stops, standardizing handover protocols, and preferentially routing patients directly to definitive-care centers whenever clinically feasible. From a system perspective, reducing unnecessary inter-hospital steps may yield time savings comparable to those achieved by aerial transport itself [33].

Meteorological limitations, daylight constraints, and base operating hours

Weather conditions, night operations, and base-specific service hours substantially influence real-world HEMS availability, often to a greater extent than anticipated in theoretical models. Decision algorithms should therefore incorporate dynamic assessments of operational availability, rather than assuming constant HEMS readiness. Redundant planning with predefined ground-based alternatives is essential to prevent decision paralysis or unsafe pressure on flight crews [70].

Uneven distribution of helipads within hub-and-spoke networks

The heterogeneous availability of certified helipads across hospitals, with pronounced regional differences, limits standardization and challenges national-level recommendations. While full infrastructural homogenization is unrealistic, regional mapping of helipad accessibility and transparent integration into dispatch decision-support tools may improve predictability and reduce ad hoc decision-making [69].

Variability in HEMS crew skills and experience

Crew composition and clinical competencies in HEMS are generally higher than in standard ground EMS; however, variability in experience and exposure to complex interfacility transfers may exist between operators and regions. Potential mitigation strategies include the definition of minimum competency standards along with targeted training and system-level strategies to ensure adequate exposure to high-complexity interfacility transfers [63].

Taken together, these challenges underscore that the effectiveness of HEMS in secondary interfacility transfers depends not only on clinical indication but also on governance, infrastructure, regulation, and human factors. Importantly, in certain organizational settings, these constraints may reduce or even negate the expected time advantage of HEMS over ground transport. Accordingly, the proposed recommendations and the RATIONAL-HEMS framework are intended to be adaptable, supporting informed local implementation rather than rigid standardization, and to stimulate system-level improvements tailored to regional realities.

Discussion

This Expert Consensus presents a structured and evidence-informed framework for the activation of HEMS in interfacility transfers for time-dependent conditions. The clinical scenarios included—acute coronary syndromes, stroke, trauma, aortic emergencies, and toxicological or hyperbaric emergencies—represent situations in which delays in access to definitive treatment are associated with worse clinical outcomes.

Although the available literature demonstrates heterogeneity across countries and healthcare systems, several consistent themes emerge: [1] early access to specialized centers is associated with improved outcomes; [2, 3, 11, 16] interfacility transfer is a recognized source of delay; and [3] HEMS may help mitigate these delays when ground transfer is prolonged or limited, not only in terms of transport time, but also in relation to operational availability, crew expertise, and the capacity to provide advanced monitoring and critical care interventions during transfer [30, 70].

The consensus aligns with international recommendations emphasizing early reperfusion in STEMI, rapid revascularization in stroke, timely surgical management in trauma and aortic syndromes, and early hyperbaric therapy in selected toxicological conditions.

The RATIONAL-HEMS model proposed herein integrates clinical urgency with logistical considerations, providing a reproducible and transparent decision-making tool. The model also emphasizes resource stewardship, supporting prioritization of HEMS deployment for the highest-yield missions. Decision-making should balance clinical needs, geographic and system constraints, and the operational limitations of HEMS availability.

However, HEMS availability and effectiveness may be influenced by several operational constraints. Weather conditions, visibility, and flight safety considerations may limit deployment. In addition, not all HEMS bases have full instrument flight capability or night operation capacity, and aircraft availability may be affected by concurrent missions, particularly in regions where HEMS resources are heavily engaged in primary emergency response or specialized rescue operations. These factors should be considered when evaluating the potential benefit of HEMS compared with ground transport.

This consensus deliberately focuses on adult interfacility transfers for time-dependent conditions. Pediatric, neonatal/incubator, and highly specialized ICU-to-ICU transfers (e.g., patients requiring ECMO or advanced organ support) were not included, as these populations are typically managed through dedicated transport systems with distinct organizational models and specialized clinical teams. These scenarios involve different decision-making processes, risk profiles, and resource configurations that differ substantially from standard HEMS-based interfacility transfer pathways. Their inclusion within a single framework would have reduced the specificity and applicability of the proposed model; therefore, they warrant dedicated, context-specific consensus efforts.

Importantly, these findings reinforce that the effectiveness of HEMS in interfacility transfers is not determined by transport time alone, but results from the interaction between clinical urgency, transport logistics, system organization, and in-transit care capabilities.

This consensus is intended to support greater harmonization within the Italian EMS–HEMS network and may serve as a foundation for future standardization efforts and outcome-based validation studies. External validation of the RATIONAL-HEMS model in diverse healthcare settings will be important to assess its generalizability and impact on clinical outcomes. Future research should aim to prospectively evaluate the impact of structured HEMS activation frameworks on patient-centered outcomes and system efficiency.

Summary of evidence

A scoping review of the literature (2010–2026) was performed across MEDLINE, EMBASE, the Cochrane Library, and major international guideline repositories to evaluate the role of HEMS in secondary interfacility transfers for time-critical conditions. Available evidence indicates that HEMS may provide clinical benefit in scenarios where: [1] time-to-definitive treatment is strongly associated with improved outcomes; [2] ground transfer is expected to exceed guideline-recommended timelines; and [3] advanced in-transit capabilities may support patient stabilization.

In the context of secondary interfacility transfers, including patients initially presenting to non-PCI or non-comprehensive stroke centers, available evidence suggests a potential role for HEMS in selected time-critical scenarios.

For cardiac emergencies requiring secondary interfacility transfer, earlier access to primary PCI or urgent coronary angiography is associated with improved outcomes in STEMI, post-fibrinolysis patients, and very high-risk NSTEMI. HEMS may reduce time to access interventional cardiology centers.

For ischemic stroke requiring secondary transfer, treatment delays negatively impact functional outcomes, particularly in candidates for intravenous thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy. HEMS may shorten transfer times to stroke centers in geographically challenging regions. In intracerebral hemorrhage, HEMS may be considered when neurosurgical intervention is indicated.

For major trauma, evidence supports expedited transfer to trauma centers capable of damage-control surgery, neurosurgical assessment, or interventional radiology. HEMS may facilitate access to these services and reduce secondary transport delays.

For aortic syndromes, including acute aortic dissection and ruptured or impending rupture aneurysms, time-to-surgery is a major determinant of survival. HEMS may reduce delays to specialized vascular or cardiothoracic centers.

For toxicological and hyperbaric emergencies, such as carbon monoxide poisoning requiring HBOT, DCI, and AGE, earlier HBOT is associated with improved neurological outcomes. HEMS may facilitate access to hyperbaric facilities, particularly in geographically remote areas. Selected hydrocarbon and oxidizing solvent intoxications may also require ICU support or HBOT in severe cases.

Overall, current evidence supports a selective and context-dependent use of HEMS, prioritizing conditions in which rapid access to advanced treatment is associated with potential clinical benefit. The recommendations presented in this consensus reflect the integration of available literature, expert clinical experience, and system-level considerations within the Italian EMS–HEMS structure.

The available evidence is largely observational and context-dependent and should be interpreted accordingly.

Conclusions

This Italian Expert Consensus provides an evidence-informed and clinically oriented framework for the use of HEMS in interfacility transfers for time-dependent or time-relevant conditions. By integrating clinical urgency, logistical variables, and system-level constraints, the RATIONAL-HEMS model offers a structured approach to support decision-making in selected clinical scenarios.

The proposed framework reflects the integration of available evidence—largely observational—and expert clinical judgment within the context of the Italian EMS–HEMS system. As such, it should be interpreted as a decision-support tool rather than a prescriptive standard.

Its application may support more consistent decision-making and more efficient resource utilization, particularly in settings where timely access to definitive care is critical. However, prospective validation is needed to assess its impact on patient-centered outcomes and system performance.
