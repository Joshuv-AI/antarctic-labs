Minimax Optimal Procedures for Joint Detection and Estimation
Dominik Reinhard, Michael Fauß, and Abdelhak M. Zoubir

Abstract
We investigate the problem of jointly testing a pair of composite hypotheses and, depending on the test result, estimating a random parameter under distributional uncertainties. Specifically, it is assumed that the distribution of the data given the parameter of interest, is subject to uncertainty. Both, a Bayesian formulation and a Neyman-Pearson-like formulation, are considered. It is shown that the optimal policy induces an f f -similarity that must be maximized to identify the least favorable distributions. Besides the general results, the implementation is investigated using a band-type uncertainty model. For designing the minimax procedures, existing algorithms are modified to increase convergence speed while maintaining numerical stability. The proposed theory is supplemented by numerical results for both formulations.

Introduction

Detection and estimation are fundamental signal processing tasks that occur in a wide range of applications.
However, in many applications both inference tasks are intrinsically coupled.
That is, the outcome of the hypothesis test would influence the estimation and vice versa.
In radar, for example, one wishes to detect a target and if a target is declared to be present, one aims at estimating some parameters, such as position or velocity of the target

In this example, the outcome of the hypothesis test as well as the outcome of the estimation task are of primary interest and should come with statistical performance guarantees.
Although one could combine an optimal detector and an optimal estimator to approach this problem, the result might not be overall optimal and, therefore, the problem of coupled detection and estimation has to addressed jointly

Besides the scenario mentioned above, coupled detection and estimation occur in various situations.
In communication, for example, one has to detect the presence of a signal and estimate the channel once a signal is detected

Detecting the primary user by the secondary user and estimating possible inference is a main task in cognitive radio

In statistical inference, often a precise statistical model is required that matches the true data generating procedure.

Small deviations from the assumed model may heavily degrade the performance.
In the 1960s, robust statistics arose to overcome this problem.
In robust statistics, one aims to develop methods whose performance is close to optimal under the nominal model while tolerating a certain, predefined amount of distributional uncertainty without breaking down.

II
Minimax Statistical Inference

This section provides the fundamentals of minimax statistical inference used in this work.
Designing minimax optimal procedures can be boiled down to finding a solution of

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\min_{v\in\mathcal{V}}J(u,v)=\min_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}J(u,v)\,,

(1)

where
J(u,v)
is some cost function.
In the equation above,
u
represents the statistical procedure, e.g., it can be a decision rule in the context of hypothesis testing or an estimator in the context of parameter estimation.
The variable
v
on the other hand represents the quantity which is subject to uncertainty, which could, for example, be the distribution of the data itself or just a single parameter of the distribution.
For
Eq.

1
to hold with equality, several conditions on the cost function as well as on the spaces
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
have to be fulfilled.
If equality holds, a saddle point of the form

\displaystyle J(u,v^{\star})\leq J(u^{\star},v^{\star})\leq J(u^{\star},v),\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{U},v\in\mathcal{V}

exists, where
u^{\star}
and
v^{\star}
are the solutions of
Eq.

1
.
Hence, the validity of
Eq.

1
is a consequence of the existence of a saddle point.

The existence of minimax optimal procedures is stated in Sion’s famous minimax theorem.

Theorem 1

(Sion’s minimax theorem
[
30
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
be convex sets.
Then,
Eq.

1
holds
if

1.

J(\cdot,v)
is upper semi-continuous and convex in
u
for every fixed
v\in\mathcal{V}

2.

J(u,\cdot)
is lower semi-continuous and concave in
v
for every fixed
u\in\mathcal{U}

3.

either
\mathcal{U}
or
\mathcal{V}
is compact.

Quantifying the model uncertainties is an essential step prior to the design of robust methods.
There exist various ways to quantify distributional uncertainties in the literature.
In robust statistics, one defines the set of feasible distributions as a neighborhood around some nominal model.
This can, for example, be done by placing an
f
-divergence ball around a nominal model, such as a
Kullback-Leibler (KL)
divergence ball
[
31
]
or an
\alpha
-divergence ball
[
32
]
.
Additionally, the neighborhood may be defined as a mixture of the nominal and an arbitrary contaminating distribution, which is referred to as the
\varepsilon
-contamination model
[
23
,
22
]
.
A different approach, introduced by Kassam
[
33
]
, is to model the set of feasible distributions using the band model.
Here, all densities are assumed to lie between a pre-defined upper and lower bound, see
Fig.

1
for illustration.
Though this band model can, in principle, be placed around a nominal distribution, it is in general non-parametric.
If the upper bound tends to infinity, this model coincides with the
\varepsilon
-contamination model
[
34
]
.
Furthermore, the band model can be used to capture local uncertainties that vary over the state space
[
35
]
.

Illustrative example of a density band model. The set of feasible distributions
\mathcal{P}^{\theta}
depends on the location parameter
\theta
.

Statistical similarity measures, such as
f
-divergences

,
37
,
38
,
39
]
and
f
-dissimilarities

40

,
42
]
as their natural extension to more than two distributions play a central role in statistics and in particular in robust hypothesis testing.
More precisely, the

are given as the minimizer of
all

-divergences in binary hypothesis testing

]
and as the minimizer of a particular

-dissimilarity in more complex setups such as,

-ary testing
[
24
]
or sequential hypothesis testing
[
44
,
45
,
24
]
.

Next, a formal definition of

-(dis)similarities is given.

(

-(dis)similarity

]
)
.

Let
f(s_{1},\ldots,s_{N})

Problem Formulation

In what follows, the system model used in this work is presented.

III-A

System Model

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a random variable that can be generated under two different hypotheses
\mathrm{H}_{0}
and
\mathrm{H}_{1}
.
The occurrence of the hypotheses is random with known prior probabilities
P(\mathrm{H}_{0})
and
P(\mathrm{H}_{1})
, respectively.
Under each hypothesis, the distribution of the data may depend on a random parameter
\Theta_{i}
,
i\in\{0,1\}
, with known distribution.
The triplet (
\mathbf{X}
,
\Theta_{i}
,
\mathrm{H}_{i}
) is defined on the parameter space
E_{\mathbf{X}}\times E_{\Theta_{i}}\times E_{\mathrm{H}_{i}}
.

Hence, the model can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{H}_{0}:&\quad\mathbf{X}\,|\,\theta_{0}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}P(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{0},\theta_{0})\,,\quad\Theta_{0}\sim P(\theta_{0}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{0})\,,\\
\mathrm{H}_{1}:&\quad\mathbf{X}\,|\,\theta_{1}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}P(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{1},\theta_{1})\,,\quad\Theta_{1}\sim P(\theta_{1}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{1})\,.\end{split}

(M1)

For the model
Eq.

M1
, there are three statistical quantities which could be subject to uncertainty, namely, the conditional distribution of the data
P(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{i},\theta_{i})
,
i\in\{0,1\}
, the prior distribution of the parameters
P(\theta_{i}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{i})
,
i\in\{0,1\}
, and the prior probabilities of the hypotheses
P(\mathrm{H}_{i})
,
i\in\{0,1\}
.
In this work, we focus on uncertainty in the conditional distributions of the data, i.e., the likelihoods.
More precisely, we assume that for every
\theta_{i}\in E_{\Theta_{i}}
, the likelihood belongs to some convex uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}_{i,\theta_{i}}
.
Mathematically, that is

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{i},\theta_{i})\in\mathcal{P}_{i,\theta_{i}}\quad\forall i\in\{0,1\},\theta_{i}\in E_{\Theta_{i}}\,.

The choice of this particular uncertainty model can be motivated by the following example.
Consider the linear model

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}=\Theta+\mathbf{V}\,,

where
\Theta
is some random quantity of interest and
\mathbf{V}
is random noise.
Since the noise distribution is often subject to uncertainty, i.e.,
P(\mathbf{v}\,|\,\theta)
is not known completely, deploying an uncertainty model on
P(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\theta_{i})
is a natural choice.

In order to keep the notation compact, the following short notation is introduced
p_{i,\theta_{i}}:=p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{i},\theta_{i})
. Furthermore, constraints of the form
p_{i,\theta_{i}}\in\mathcal{P}_{i,\theta_{i}}
have to hold for all
i\in\{0,1\}
and all
\theta_{i}\in E_{\Theta_{i}}
.

III-B

Fundamentals of Joint Detection and Estimation

In
JDE
, the aim is to infer the data generating hypothesis
\mathrm{H}_{i}
,
i\in\{0,1\}
, as well as the underlying parameter
\theta_{i}
,
i\in\{0,1\}
.
Treating both problems, the one of detection and the one of estimation separately, does not necessarily result in an overall optimal performance
[
3
]
.
Therefore, both problems have to be solved jointly.

A procedure that solves the problem of
JDE
is characterized by a policy
\pi
that comprises of one decision rule
\delta
and two estimators
\hat{\theta}_{0}
and
\hat{\theta}_{1}
.
In this work a
randomized
decision rule
\delta:E_{\mathbf{X}}\rightarrow[0,1]
that maps the observations to the probability of accepting the alternative is considered.

In addition to the decision rule, the policy is equipped with a set of estimators, one for each hypothesis.
More formally, the estimator for the parameter under hypothesis
\mathrm{H}_{i}
is defined as
\hat{\theta}_{i}:E_{\mathbf{X}}\rightarrow E_{\Theta_{i}}
,
i\in\{0,1\}
.
Hence, they map the observations to the respective state space of the parameter.

The set of all feasible decision rules
\Delta_{\delta}
is convex and compact.
Additionally,
\Delta_{i}
, the set of feasible estimators under hypothesis
\mathrm{H}_{i}
is convex and compact, which directly follows from the definition of
E_{\Theta}
.
From that, it follows that the set of feasible policies
\Pi
is also convex and compact.

Next, performance measures for
JDE
are to be discussed.
For the outcome of the decision, the probabilities of falsely rejecting a hypothesis

\displaystyle\begin{split}\alpha^{0}&=\E[\delta\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{0}]=\iint\!\delta p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{0},\theta_{0})p(\theta_{0}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{0})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\mathrm{d}\theta_{0}\,,\\
\alpha^{1}&=\E[1-\delta\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{1}]=\iint\!(1-\delta)p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{1},\theta_{1})p(\theta_{1}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{1})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}\end{split}

(2)

are used as performance measures.
To measure the performance of the estimators, the
mean-squared error (MSE)
is used.
In the case a wrong decision is made, we set the estimation error to zero
2
2

2

Defining the estimation error in case of a wrong decision is often neither meaningful nor practical as, for example, the random parameter under
\mathrm{H}_{1}
might not even exist under the null hypothesis.
.
Mathematically, the estimation error levels under bot hypotheses can be defined as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\beta^{0}&=\E\Bigl[(1-\delta)(\hat{\theta}_{0}-\theta_{0})^{2}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{0}\Bigr]\\
&=\iint(1-\delta)(\hat{\theta}_{0}-\theta_{0})^{2}p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{0},\theta_{0})p(\theta_{0}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{0})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\mathrm{d}\theta_{0}\,,\\
\beta^{1}&=\E\Bigl[\delta(\hat{\theta}_{1}-\theta_{1})^{2}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{1}\Bigr]\\
&=\iint\delta(\hat{\theta}_{1}-\theta_{1})^{2}p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{1},\theta_{1})p(\theta_{1}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{1})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}\,.\end{split}

(3)

Note that the performance measures in
Eq.

2
and
Eq.

3
respectively depend on the policy
\pi
as well as on the conditional densities
p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{0},\theta_{0})
and
p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{1},\theta_{1})
.
To keep the upcoming notation compact, these dependencies are only mentioned explicitly if they are relevant within the context.

III-C

Optimal Joint Detection and Estimation

A variety of problem formulations exist in
JDE
.
In this work, we focus on two different problem formulations.
In the first one, referred to as
Bayesian formulation
in this work, one aims to find a policy that minimizes a weighted sum of detection and estimation errors.
Measuring the inference quality by a linear combination of the individual performance measures is well motivated from a decision theoretic point of view and has been used in the literature
[
46
,
47
,
5
,
13
,
14
]
.

Mathematically, the search for the optimal policy under this formulation can be written as the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\pi}\;J^{\text{B}}(\pi)\,,

(P1)

with

\displaystyle J^{\text{B}}(\pi)=\sum_{i=0}^{1}P(\mathrm{H}_{i})\left(\lambda_{i}\alpha^{i}(\pi)+\mu_{i}\beta^{i}(\pi)\right)\,.

(4)

In
Eq.

4
,
\lambda_{i},\mu_{i}
,
i\in\{0,1\}
, are some fixed, non-negative cost coefficients.
Although the prior probabilities
p(\mathrm{H}_{0})
and
p(\mathrm{H}_{1})
are fixed, and, hence, could be incorporated into the cost coefficients, they are added to end up with a more compact notation later.

In the second formulation, referred to as the
Neyman-Pearson-like formulation
or
NP-like formulation
, one aims to find a policy that minimizes a cost function reflecting the estimation errors, while constraining the error probabilities to not exceed nominal levels.
Such formulations have been used, for example, in
[
3
,
48
,
49
,
50
]
.
In the literature, there exist different ways to model the estimation inaccuracy, such as the maximum of the
MSEs
under
\mathrm{H}_{0}
and
\mathrm{H}_{1}

[
48
]
or as a weighted sum of both
MSEs

[
49
,
50
]
.
In this work, we focus on the latter.

Mathematically, it can be written as the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\min_{\pi}\;J^{\text{NP}}(\pi)\,,\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&\alpha^{0}(\pi)\leq\bar{\alpha}^{0}\,,\quad\alpha^{1}(\pi)\leq\bar{\alpha}^{1}\,,\end{split}

(P2)

with

\displaystyle J^{\text{NP}}(\pi)=P(\mathrm{H}_{0})\mu_{0}\beta^{0}(\pi)+P(\mathrm{H}_{1})\mu_{1}\beta^{1}(\pi)\,.

Note that for
Eq.

P2
to have a solution, it has to hold that
\bar{\alpha}^{1}>\bar{\alpha}^{1}_{\text{NP}}
, where
\bar{\alpha}^{1}_{\text{NP}}
is the Type-II error probability of the
NP
-test with nominal Type-I error level
\bar{\alpha}^{0}

[
3
]
.

III-D

Minimax Optimal Joint Detection and Estimation

After the problem formulation under full model knowledge has been provided, the counterparts of
Eq.

P1
and
Eq.

P2
under model uncertainty can be introduced.

The minimax version of
Eq.

P1
can be described as follows.
We wish to find a policy that minimizes the maximum weighted sum of detection and estimation error levels.
Mathematically, this can be written as the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\pi}\,\max_{P_{i,\theta_{i}}\in\mathcal{P}_{i,\theta_{i}}}\;J^{\text{B}}(\pi,p_{0,\theta_{0}},p_{1,\theta_{1}})\,,

(P
1^{\ast}
)

where the constraints for the maximum operator have to hold for all
i\in\{0,1\}
and all
\theta_{i}\in E_{\Theta_{i}}
.
As a maximization over the set of feasible densities has to be performed, the dependency of the objective on the densities is stated explicitly.
The second and the third argument of
J^{\text{B}}(\pi,\cdot,\cdot)
have to be read as the collection of
P(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{0},\theta_{0})
for all
\theta_{0}\in E_{\Theta_{0}}
and
P(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{1},\theta_{1})
for all
\theta_{1}\in E_{\Theta_{1}}
, respectively.

For the
NP-like formulation
, see
Eq.

P2
, the minimax formulation reads as follows

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\min_{\pi}\;\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}P_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\text{D}}\in\mathcal{P}_{i,\theta_{i}}\\
P_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\text{E}}\in\mathcal{P}_{i,\theta_{i}}\end{subarray}}J^{\text{NP}}(\pi,p_{0,\theta_{0}}^{\text{E}},p_{1,\theta_{1}}^{\text{E}})\,,\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}P_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\text{D}}\in\mathcal{P}_{i,\theta_{i}}\\
P_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\text{E}}\in\mathcal{P}_{i,\theta_{i}}\end{subarray}}\alpha^{0}(\pi,p_{0,\theta_{0}}^{\text{D}})\leq\bar{\alpha}^{0}\,,\\
&\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}P_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\text{D}}\in\mathcal{P}_{i,\theta_{i}}\\
P_{i,\theta_{i}}^{\text{E}}\in\mathcal{P}_{i,\theta_{i}}\end{subarray}}\alpha^{1}(\pi,p_{1,\theta_{1}}^{\text{D}})\leq\bar{\alpha}^{1}\,,\end{split}

(P
2^{\ast}
)

where the constraints for the maximum operators have to hold for all
i\in\{0,1\}
and all
\theta_{i}\in E_{\Theta_{i}}
.
The arguments for the function above have to be read as a family of
p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{i},\theta_{i})
for all
\theta_{i}\in E_{\Theta_{i}}
,
i\in\{0,1\}
.
In the
NP-like formulation
, the objective and the constraint functions have to be maximized separately.
Therefore, one has to maximize over four set of densities, two for maximizing the objective and two for maximizing the constraint functions.

IV
Minimax Solution under the Bayesian Formulation

In this section, the minimax solution under the Bayesian formulation, i.e., the solution of
Eq.

P

1
∗

, is investigated.
Before the characterization of the solution of
Eq.

P

1
∗

can be presented, the applicability of Sion’s minimax theorem has to be verified.
The objective in
Eq.

1
∗

is a linear combination of the four different performance measures.
The error probabilities are linear in the decision rule as well as in the densities
p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{i},\theta_{i})
,
i\in\{0,1\}
.
The

on the other hand are linear in the decision rule, quadratic in the estimators and linear in the densities

,
i\in\{0,1\}
.
This implies that the objective in
Eq.

P

1
∗

is continuous in all arguments, it is convex in the policy

and concave in the densities

,
i\in\{0,1\}
.
Since the set of feasible policies is compact, Sion’s minimax theorem as stated in

1

This implies that finding a solution of
Eq.

P

1
∗

is equivalent to first deriving an optimal policy for a given, but arbitrary set of distributions and subsequently finding the
LFDs
.

First, the optimal solution for a given set of distributions is reviewed.
Next, some important properties of the cost functions are presented.
Finally, a characterization of the
LFDs
and the minimax optimal solution is given.

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&D_{0}^{\text{B}}(\mathbf{x})<D^{\text{B}}_{1}(\mathbf{x})\,,\\
\in[0,1]&D^{\text{B}}_{0}(\mathbf{x})=D^{\text{B}}_{1}(\mathbf{x})\,,\\
1,&D^{\text{B}}_{0}(\mathbf{x})>D^{\text{B}}_{1}(\mathbf{x})\,,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{i}^{\star}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\E_{p_{i,\theta_{i}}}[\Theta_{i}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{i},\mathbf{x}]\,,

with

\displaystyle D^{\text{B}}_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\lambda_{1-i}p(\mathrm{H}_{1-i}\,|\,\mathbf{x})+\mu_{i}p(\mathrm{H}_{i}\,|\,\mathbf{x})\Var_{p_{i,\theta_{i}}}[\Theta_{i}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{i},\mathbf{x}]\,.

It further holds that

\displaystyle J^{\text{B}}(\pi^{\star},p_{0,\theta_{0}},p_{1,\theta_{1}})=\int\min\Bigl\{D_{0}^{\text{B}}(\mathbf{x}),D_{1}^{\text{B}}(\mathbf{x})\Bigr\}p(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,.

The optimal estimator under
\mathrm{H}_{i}
as stated in
Theorem

is the minimum
MSE

The decision rule accepts

if the cost for accepting
\mathrm{H}_{0}

is smaller than the cost for accepting
\mathrm{H}_{1}

The cost of accepting a particular hypothesis comprises of one term reflecting the certainty about the true hypothesis, whereas a second term reflects the uncertainty about the underlying parameter when this hypothesis is accepted.
Here, the coupling of detection and estimation can be seen as the decision takes detection and estimation accuracy into account.

After the optimal policy for some given set of distributions and the characterization of the distributions maximizing the weighted sum of error measures have been presented, the design of minimax optimal procedures is addressed.

The solution of the minimax problem formulation in

\mu_{0}

\mu_{1}

\bar{\alpha}^{1}_{\text{NP}}

NP

\displaystyle\alpha^{0}(\pi^{\star},\boldsymbol{q}^{\text{D}}_{0,\theta_{0}})=\bar{\alpha}^{0}\,,\qquad\alpha^{1}(\pi^{\star},\boldsymbol{q}^{\text{D}}_{1,\theta_{1}})=\bar{\alpha}^{1}.

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}^{\star},\lambda_{1}^{\star}=\argmax_{\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}}\tilde{J}^{\text{NP}}_{\text{u}}(\bm{q};\pi^{\star})-\sum_{i=0}^{1}\lambda_{i}p(\mathrm{H}_{i})\bar{\alpha}^{i}\,.

Theorem

However, the

depend on the optimal policy and vice versa.

To design a minimax procedure in the sense of
Theorem

As an initial step, the optimal procedure for
some

\mathbb{P}_{0}
and
\mathbb{P}_{1}
is designed.
Subsequently, one iteratively calculates the cost maximizing distributions according to
Corollary

This process is repeated until convergence.

Note that this iterative design process cannot be interpreted as iteratively designing an optimal scheme and finding a set of
LFDs

Once an optimal scheme is designed, one can find a set of most similar, or least favorable, distributions.
However, once the coefficients
\lambda_{0}
and
\lambda_{1}
are updated, i.e., an optimal scheme for this new set of densities is designed, the densities are no longer most similar in the sense of

Once the iterative design process converges, the distributions are least favorable in the sense of
Eq.

P

2
∗

VI
Discussion

In
Sections

IV
and
V
, we have shown that the least favorable, or most similar, distributions that lead to a minimax scheme are defined as the maximizer of some
f
-similarity.
This is in line with classic as well as more recent results in the context of minimax robust hypothesis testing.
As one of the earliest important results, the characterization of the
LFDs
of a binary fixed-sample size test by Huber and Strassen
[
43
]
has to be mentioned.
This says that the pair of distributions that simultaneously minimize
all

f
-divergences are the
LFDs
for a binary hypothesis test for all sample sizes and all thresholds.
However, besides the case of binary hypothesis testing, no such strong conditions exist.
Though the results by Huber and Strassen could be extended to
M
-ary hypothesis testing by replacing
f
-divergences by
f
-dissimilarities, there is no set of distributions that jointly minimizes all
f
-dissimilarities.
Intuitively, this can be explained as in the
M
-ary case where there are multiple sources of error, i.e., more than confusing
\mathrm{H}_{0}
and
\mathrm{H}_{1}
.
Hence, one could only find a pair of
LFDs
that maximize a particular sum of weighted error probabilities.
See
[
24
, Sec. VI]
for a discussion.
Additionally, it has been shown in the context of sequential hypothesis testing that the
LFDs
are defined as the maximizer of some
particular

f
-similarity
[
44
,
45
]
.
However, similar to
Section

V
, one has to
jointly
find a policy and the
LFDs
.
Hence, one can see that the results presented in this work are in line with existing results in minimax robust hypothesis testing.
Although this paper only addresses
JDE
for a binary hypothesis testing problem, there are also multiple sources of errors.
Besides confusing
\mathrm{H}_{0}
and
\mathrm{H}_{1}
, also errors during the estimation could occur.
The problem of
JDE
can also be seen as an
M
-ary hypothesis test with an infinite number of hypotheses.
Following this interpretation underpins the fact that the minimax optimal solution only minimize a particular
f
-dissimilarity.
Therefore, it is only natural that a condition as strong as the one derived by Huber and Strassen could not exist. Instead, the set of
LFDs
maximizes a particular
f
-similarity.

VII
Calculation of the Most Similar Distributions

As shown in
Sections

IV
and
V
, the most similar or least favorable distributions are defined as the maximizer of some
f
-similarity, i.e., of some concave function.
As the uncertainty sets are assumed to be convex, the
LFDs
can, in principle, be found by solving a convex optimization problem.
However, the optimization has to be performed over
N

pdfs
, where
N=N_{0}+N_{1}
in the Bayesian or
N=2\cdot N_{0}+2\cdot N_{1}
in the
NP
-like formulation.
In practice,
N
could be in the range of hundreds or thousands, and, hence, solving this problem is highly challenging.
As mentioned earlier, we use the band model for implementing the robust procedure.
Therefore, we resort to an adaptation of
[
51
]
, which is an algorithm tailored to solving problems of the form

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathbfcal{P}}\;\int f(\bm{p})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,,

(17)

where
f(\bm{p})
is a convex function of a vector of
N

pdfs
,
\bm{P}
and
\mathbfcal{P}
are the corresponding distributions and band-type uncertainty sets.
In the context of this work,
f(\cdot)
is replaced by the functions
-\rho^{\text{B}}(\cdot)
and
-\rho^{\text{NP}}(\cdot)
, respectively, so that solving
Eq.

17
is equivalent to maximizing the
f
-similarities introduced in
Sections

IV
and
V
, respectively.
The optimal densities are then approximated iteratively, where in each iteration the optimization problem reduces to a one-dimensional line search for a normalization constant.
Using this normalization constant, the most similar distributions are found by projecting the inverse of the objective’s subdifferential onto the set of feasible densities.
To increase numerical stability, the algorithm presented in
[
51
]
comes with a proximal version.

We propose two modifications for the algorithm as described in
[
51
]
.
First, we replace the Euclidean proximal term by the more general Bregman distance.
Second, we deploy an adaptive weighting scheme to the Bregman distance.

The proximal formulation in
[
51
]
is generalized by replacing the Euclidean term by a Bregman map
h(\cdot,\cdot)

[
52
]
.
Now, the new objective of the proximal algorithm can be written as

\displaystyle\bm{q}^{(k)}=\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathbfcal{P}}\;\int f(\bm{p})+\eta_{k}h\Bigl(\bm{p},\bm{q}^{(k-1)}\Bigr)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}:=\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathbfcal{P}}\tilde{f}(\bm{p})\,,

where
\bm{q}^{(k)}
denotes the solution at the
k
-th iteration and
\eta_{k}
the weight at iteration
k
.

The class of Bregman maps contains various widely used function such as the Euclidean distance or the generalized
KL
-divergence.
In the case where the free variables are valid densities, the latter reduces to the
KL
-divergence.
Since the
KL
-divergence is a widely used statistical distance measure and has some advantages such as reflecting the differences in the tails in an adequate manner, we resort to the
KL
-divergence in this work.
Then, the Bregman map reads

\displaystyle h\Bigl(\bm{p},\bm{q}^{(k-1)}\Bigr)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\int p_{n}(\mathbf{x})\log\left(\frac{p_{n}(\mathbf{x})}{q_{n}^{(k-1)}(\mathbf{x})}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,.

As mentioned above,
[
51
]
relies on calculating the inverse sub-differentials of the objective. For any function
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}^{N}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we denote the partial sub-derivatives by
\tilde{f}_{n}
,
n\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. With the generalized
KL
-divergence, the partial sub-derivative becomes

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{n}(\bm{p})=f_{n}(\bm{p})+\eta_{k}\int\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x})-\log q_{n}^{(k-1)}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,.

The partial sub-derivatives of the objective
f(\bm{p})
depend on whether the Bayesian or the
NP
-like formulation is used and are derived in
Section

-C
.

Instead of using a constant
\eta_{k}
, we propose an exponentially decaying sequence
\{\eta_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
.
This should trade-off convergence speed and numerical stability.
More formally, the sequence is defined as

\displaystyle\eta_{k}=\min\Bigl\{\eta_{0}\rho^{k-k_{\text{init}}}\,,\,\eta_{\text{min}}\Bigr\}\,,

where
\eta_{0}>0
is the initial weight,
\rho\in(0,1)
is a decay factor,
\eta_{\text{min}}
is the minimum weight and
k_{\text{init}}
is the iteration in which the exponential decay starts.
The latter is used to reset the exponential decay to its initial value when running into numerical instability.
We set
k_{\text{init}}=0
so that the sequence
\eta_{k}
decays exponentially until the process ist reset the first time.
Once the algorithm runs into instability, e.g., the algorithm in
[
51
]
is not able to find a valid density,
k_{\text{init}}
is set to the current iteration number.
The idea behind this exponential weighting scheme is as follows.
Instead of most proximal optimization methods, we interpret
\nabla_{k}h(\cdot,\cdot)
as a regularization term.
We further assume that close to the optimal solution, the optimization problem is almost well conditioned, i.e., it does not run into numerical instability.
In early iterations, i.e., when the current solution is far from the optimal one, it requires a lot of regularization to keep the problem well conditioned.
Once the solution moves towards the optimal solution, less regularization is required and too much regularization will require many more iterations until convergence.
To avoid numerical instability, the weight must not fall below
\eta_{\text{min}}
and, in case the algorithm still becomes numerically unstable, the exponential decaying is reset.

IX
Conclusions

The problem of joint detection and estimation under distributional uncertainties has been investigated under a Bayesian and a
NP
-like formulation.
We have shown that the optimal policy induces an
f
-similarity as a statistical similarity measure that is to be maximized by the worst case distributions; irrespective of the type of uncertainty sets.
To design minimax optimal procedures under the band model, we have adopted existing algorithms to increase convergence speed while maintaining numerical stability.
To supplement the proposed theory, numerical results have been provided for the Bayesian and for the
NP
-like formulation.

Proof of Theorem
3

First, it is shown that
\breve{D}_{i}^{\text{B}}(\bar{s}_{0,\theta_{0}},\bar{s}_{1,\theta_{1}})
,
i\in\{0,1\}
, are continuous and homogeneous in the arguments
\bar{s}_{0,\theta_{0}}
and
\bar{s}_{1,\theta_{1}}
.
From the definition of
\breve{D}_{i}^{\text{B}}(s_{0},s_{1})
, one can see that it is a continuous function of
\bar{s}_{0,\theta_{0}}
and
\bar{s}_{1,\theta_{1}}
. It holds that

\displaystyle\breve{D}_{i}^{\text{B}}(c\bar{s}_{0,\theta_{0}},c\bar{s}_{1,\theta_{1}})=c\breve{D}_{i}^{\text{B}}(\bar{s}_{0,\theta_{0}},\bar{s}_{1,\theta_{1}})

for
c>0
.
Furthermore, we can state that

\displaystyle\lim_{c\rightarrow 0}\breve{D}_{i}^{\text{B}}(c\bar{s}_{0,\theta_{0}},c\bar{s}_{1,\theta_{1}})=\lim_{c\rightarrow 0}c\breve{D}_{i}^{\text{B}}(\bar{s}_{0,\theta_{0}},\bar{s}_{1,\theta_{1}})=0\,.

Since the minimum operator preserves these properties, they hold for
\rho^{\text{B}}(\cdot)
as well.
As stated in the beginning of
Section

IV
,
J^{\text{B}}(\pi,p_{0,\theta_{0}},p_{1,\theta_{1}})
is concave in the densities, and, hence the function
\rho^{\text{B}}(\cdot)
is also concave in its arguments. ∎

Proof of Theorem
6

First, for a given policy
\pi^{\star}
, we can see that

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{P}_{0}^{\text{D}}\in\mathbb{P}_{0}\,,\;\boldsymbol{P}_{0}^{\text{E}}\in\mathbb{P}_{0}\\
\boldsymbol{P}_{1}^{\text{D}}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}\,,\;\boldsymbol{P}_{1}^{\text{E}}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}\end{subarray}}\;D_{\rho^{\text{NP}}}(\boldsymbol{P}_{0}^{\text{D}},\boldsymbol{P}_{1}^{\text{D}},\boldsymbol{P}_{0}^{\text{E}},\boldsymbol{P}_{1}^{\text{E}})

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{P}_{0}^{\text{D}}\in\mathbb{P}_{0}\,,\;\boldsymbol{P}_{0}^{\text{E}}\in\mathbb{P}_{0}\\
\boldsymbol{P}_{1}^{\text{D}}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}\,,\;\boldsymbol{P}_{1}^{\text{E}}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}\end{subarray}}\!\!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{1}p(\mathrm{H}_{i})\biggl(\lambda_{i}\alpha^{i}(\pi,\boldsymbol{p}_{i}^{\text{D}})+\mu_{i}\beta^{i}(\pi,\boldsymbol{p}_{i}^{\text{E}})\biggr)\right)

implies that

\displaystyle\max_{\boldsymbol{p}_{i}^{\text{D}}\in\mathbb{P}_{i}}\;\alpha^{i}(\pi,\boldsymbol{p}^{\text{D}}_{i})\,,\quad\max_{\boldsymbol{p}_{i}^{\text{E}}\in\mathbb{P}_{i}}\;\beta^{i}(\pi,\boldsymbol{p}_{i}^{\text{E}})\,,\quad i\in\{0,1\}

as
for a given policy
, there is no coupling in the densities of the individual performance measures.

Assume that there exists a policy
\pi^{\circ}=(\delta^{\circ},\hat{\theta}_{0}^{\circ},\hat{\theta}_{1}^{\circ})
such that

\displaystyle J^{\text{NP}}(\pi^{\circ},\boldsymbol{q}_{0}^{\text{D}},\boldsymbol{q}_{1}^{\text{D}},\boldsymbol{q}_{0}^{\text{E}},\boldsymbol{q}_{1}^{\text{E}})<J^{\text{NP}}(\pi^{\star},\boldsymbol{q}_{0}^{\text{D}},\boldsymbol{q}_{1}^{\text{D}},\boldsymbol{q}_{0}^{\text{E}},\boldsymbol{q}_{1}^{\text{E}})

and

\displaystyle\alpha^{i}(\pi^{\circ},\bm{q}_{{i}}^{\text{D}})\leq\bar{\alpha}^{i}\,,\quad i\in\{0,1\}.

From the above, we can conclude that

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}J^{\text{NP}}(\pi^{\star},\bm{q})+\sum_{i=0}^{1}\lambda_{i}p(\mathrm{H}_{i})\alpha^{i}(\pi^{\star},\bm{q}_{{i}}^{\text{D}})

\displaystyle=J^{\text{NP}}(\pi^{\star},\bm{q})+\sum_{i=0}^{1}\lambda_{i}p(\mathrm{H}_{i})\bar{\alpha}^{i}

\displaystyle\geq J^{\text{NP}}(\pi^{\circ},\bm{q})+\sum_{i=0}^{1}\lambda_{i}p(\mathrm{H}_{i})\alpha^{i}(\pi^{\circ},\bm{q}_{{i}}^{\text{D}})

which contradicts the assumption that the policy stated in
Theorem

4
solves
Eq.

10
.
It is left to show that
Eq.

15
could be obtained by solving
Eq.

16
.
First, it can be shown that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{j}}D_{i}^{\text{NP}}=\begin{cases}p(\mathrm{H}_{1-i})p^{\text{D}}_{1-i}(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{1-i})&j=1-i\,,\\
0&\text{else}\,.\end{cases}

With

\displaystyle{J}^{\text{NP}}_{\text{u}}(\mathbf{x})=(1-\delta(\mathbf{x}))D_{0}^{\text{NP}}(\mathbf{x})+\delta(\mathbf{x})D_{1}^{\text{NP}}(\mathbf{x})

we can, under mild assumptions, conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{0}}{J}^{\text{NP}}_{\text{u}}(\mathbf{x})=\int\delta(\mathbf{x})p(\mathrm{H}_{0})p(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathrm{H}_{0})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=p(\mathrm{H}_{0})\alpha^{0}\,.

Then, it follows that the objective in
Eq.

16
attains an extremum at
\alpha^{0}=\bar{\alpha}^{0}
if
\lambda_{0}
> 0.
If
\lambda_{0}=0
, it acts as a slack variable and it follows that
\alpha^{0}<\bar{\alpha}^{0}
.
With the same line of arguments, it can be shown that a similar relationship holds for
\lambda_{1}
.

-C

Gradient of the Objective

In this section, we use the short-hand notations
\boldsymbol{s}=(\boldsymbol{s}_{0,\theta_{0}},\boldsymbol{s}_{1,\theta_{1}})
or
\boldsymbol{s}=(\boldsymbol{s}_{0,\theta_{0}}^{\text{D}},\boldsymbol{s}_{1,\theta_{1}}^{\text{D}},\boldsymbol{s}_{0,\theta_{0}}^{\text{E}},\boldsymbol{s}_{1,\theta_{1}}^{\text{E}})
to keep the notation compact.
First, the Bayesian formulation as used in
Section

IV
is considered.
The derivative of the cost for accepting hypothesis
\mathrm{H}_{i}
for
i\in\{0,1\}
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}_{i,\theta_{i}}}\tilde{D}_{i}^{\text{B}}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle=\mu_{i}p(\mathrm{H}_{i})\boldsymbol{a}_{i}-2\mu_{i}p(\mathrm{H}_{i})\frac{\boldsymbol{b}_{i}^{\top}s_{i,\theta_{i}}}{\boldsymbol{c}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{s}_{i,\theta_{i}}}\boldsymbol{b}_{i}

\displaystyle\quad+\mu_{i}p(\mathrm{H}_{i})\frac{\bigl(\boldsymbol{b}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{s}_{i,\theta_{i}}\bigr)^{2}}{\bigl(\boldsymbol{c}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{s}_{i,\theta_{i}}\bigr)^{2}}\boldsymbol{c}_{i}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}_{1-i,\theta_{1-i}}}\tilde{D}_{i}^{\text{B}}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1-i}p(\mathrm{H}_{1-i})\boldsymbol{c}_{1-i}

From the definition of the objective

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{s})=-\min\{\tilde{D}_{0}^{\text{B}}(\boldsymbol{s}),\tilde{D}_{1}^{\text{B}}(\boldsymbol{s})\}\,,

we can directly conclude that the final gradient becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{s})}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}_{i,\theta_{i}}}=-\begin{cases}\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}_{i,\theta_{i}}}\tilde{D}_{i}^{\text{B}}(\boldsymbol{s})&\tilde{D}_{i}^{\text{B}}(\boldsymbol{s})<\tilde{D}_{1-i}^{\text{B}}(\boldsymbol{s})\,,\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}_{i,\theta_{i}}}\tilde{D}_{1-i}^{\text{B}}(\boldsymbol{s})&\tilde{D}_{i}^{\text{B}}(\boldsymbol{s})>\tilde{D}_{1-i}^{\text{B}}(\boldsymbol{s})\,.\end{cases}

Second, when using the
NP
-like formulation, the cost for accepting hypothesis
\mathrm{H}_{i}
is given by

However, as the gradient causes practical implementation issues when finding the inverse of the gradient, we replaced the conventional minimum operator by a

where
