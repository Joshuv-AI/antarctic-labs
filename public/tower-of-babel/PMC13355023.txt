Brain mechanisms supporting mindfulness-based chronic pain relief: A randomized, sham-mindfulness meditation controlled mechanistic clinical trial protocol

ABSTRACT
IntroductionChronic low back pain (cLBP) is a leading cause of disability worldwide and is frequently refractory to pharmacological treatment. Mindfulness meditation has been shown to reduce pain through modulation of self-referential, cognitive and affective mechanisms. However, the neural mechanisms supporting the direct and immediate modulation of movement-evoked cLBP by mindfulness meditation as compared to an appropriate placebo-control remain poorly characterized.Methods and analysisThis single-center, sham-mindfulness meditation–controlled mechanistic clinical trial randomized 120 meditation naïve adults with cLBP to a six-session (20 min/session) mindfulness meditation, sham-mindfulness meditation, or book-listening control intervention delivered over two weeks. Perfusion fMRI was acquired at pre- and post-intervention timepoints during pain evocation with the leg raise test (LRT), a standardized orthopedic maneuver used to evoke low back and/or radiating leg pain in individuals with lumbar pathology. The primary outcome was change in whole-brain cerebral blood flow. Secondary outcomes included change in numerical pain ratings (0 = no pain; 10 = worst pain imaginable) corresponding to the LRT. Planned analyses include mixed-effects models examining group-by-time effects on behavioral and neuroimaging outcomes.DiscussionBy integrating a clinically relevant movement-evoked pain paradigm with perfusion-based neuroimaging and a matched sham meditation control, this trial will isolate mindfulness-specific neural mechanisms underlying pain modulation to inform the design of future efficacy-focused clinical trials.Ethics and disseminationThe study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03354585) was approved by the University of California, San Diego Institutional Review Board (IRB#181814). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. At the time of manuscript submission, recruitment and intervention delivery were complete and data analyses ongoing. Study findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and scientific conferences.

Introduction

Chronic low back pain (cLBP), which affects over 1.5 billion individuals, is the most prevalent chronic pain condition worldwide and the leading cause of disability globally [[1], [2], [3]]. A defining and disabling feature of cLBP is movement-evoked pain, in which routine activities such as bending, lifting, or low-flexion movements precipitate acute pain exacerbations [4,5]. These pain flares contribute substantially to functional impairment, healthcare utilization, and reduced quality of life [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Movement-evoked pain is particularly essential to study because it reflects the dynamic, functionally relevant pain that patients most consistently report as disabling, yet standard laboratory and neuroimaging paradigms have historically relied on externally applied noxious stimuli that do not recapitulate this experience. Characterizing the neural mechanisms underlying modulation of movement-evoked clinical pain therefore represents an important and relatively underexplored priority for the field.

Mindfulness meditation is a self-regulated mental training practice characterized by sustained, present-centered, and non-reactive awareness of sensory, cognitive, and affective experiences [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Mindfulness meditation can reduce pain severity and pain-related distress across a range of conditions [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. Brief mindfulness-based training regimens, consisting of three to six sessions, can also reduce cLBP [[20], [21], [22]]. Although these findings are promising, the neural mechanisms supporting mindfulness meditation related modulation of clinical pain remain insufficiently characterized. Moreover, it remains unclear whether observed pain reductions reflect mindfulness-specific neurocognitive processes or more general placebo-related mechanisms [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. To address this challenge, we previously developed and validated a sham-mindfulness meditation intervention that closely matches mindfulness meditation operational procedures (slow breathing; posture) while omitting the mindfulness-specific component of non-reactive attention to breath sensations. Accordingly, the present randomized, sham-mindfulness meditation controlled mechanistic clinical trial (NCT03354585) was designed to characterize the neural processes associated with mindfulness meditation–related modulation of movement-evoked cLBP using the leg raise test (LRT) [[6], [7], [8], [9]], a procedure wherein a participant's affected leg is raised to compress lumbosacral nerve roots and reliably increase pain and engage nociceptive brain regions [23,24].

Our a priori neural hypotheses focused on the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), insula, and thalamus based on convergent evidence from our laboratory [25,26].The OFC is critically involved in the cognitive reappraisal of noxious events and the top-down modulation of pain affect; prior fMRI studies have shown that mindfulness-related analgesia is associated with OFC engagement during the anticipation and experience of pain [25,26]. The thalamus serves as a primary relay hub for nociceptive input, and thalamic deactivation has been observed during mindfulness-mediated pain reduction, consistent with a gating of ascending pain signals [26]. The insula integrates interoceptive and nociceptive signals and has been implicated in the affective and evaluative dimensions of pain and modulation of pain by mindfulness meditation in our prior work [25,26]. The DMN, which encompasses midline cortical regions associated with self-referential processing, shows sustained activation during clinical pain states and has been proposed as an exploratory target in light of recent findings demonstrating that mindfulness-based pain relief, in healthy individuals, is associated with lower pain [23,24,27]. Mindfulness practice is theorized to reduce self-referential rumination and DMN engagement, thereby attenuating the evaluative amplification of pain. Together, these regions form a mechanistically coherent set of targets for examining mindfulness-specific modulation of movement-evoked cLBP during the LRT.

The primary outcome of this study was change in cerebral blood flow (CBF), assessed using perfusion-based functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) during the LRT. Secondary outcomes included numerical rating scale (NRS) pain ratings and resting-state functional connectivity changes assessed using blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) fMRI. This study was not designed or powered to test clinical efficacy; rather, its primary aim was to identify mindfulness-specific neural mechanisms that may inform the design of future efficacy-focused clinical trials.

Methods and Analysis

Study design

This study was a single-center, randomized, sham-mindfulness meditation controlled, mechanistic clinical trial designed to characterize neural mechanisms associated with mindfulness meditation-related modulation of movement-evoked cLBP. The trial employed a three-arm, parallel-group design comparing mindfulness meditation, sham-mindfulness meditation, and a book-listening control. The primary outcome was change in CBF using perfusion-based fMRI during a clinically relevant pain-evocation task. Secondary outcomes included pain ratings and exploratory outcomes included among other measures resting-state functional connectivity and behavioral assessments.

Participants

Adults aged 18–65 years with cLBP of at least three months’ duration were recruited from the greater San Diego area through institutional research registries, clinical referral services, and community advertisements.

Study population

Based on prior work [28,29], we targeted to recruit 54% Non-Hispanic White, 15% Non-Hispanic Black, 17% Hispanic, 7% Asian/Pacific Islander, 1% Native American/American Indian, and 6% other.

Randomization and blinding

After completion of baseline assessments, participants were randomized in a 1:1:1 ratio to mindfulness meditation, sham-mindfulness meditation, or book-listening (control). Randomization was stratified by sex using a computer-generated allocation sequence. Study coordinators responsible for scheduling and randomization were not involved in data collection or analysis.

Leg raise test

The LRT is a standardized orthopedic maneuver used to evoke low back and/or radiating leg pain in individuals with lumbar pathology. During the LRT, the participant lies supine while an examiner passively raises their straightened leg to approximately 30–70 degrees of hip flexion. Tension transmitted through the lumbosacral nerve roots and posterior spinal structures reliably produces or exacerbates back and/or radiating pain in individuals with cLBP. In the present study, the LRT was used as a within-scanner pain-evocation procedure; a positive LRT, defined as a minimum 2-point increase in NRS pain ratings relative to the supine resting baseline, was required to confirm eligibility and to proceed with perfusion fMRI acquisition.

Interventions

All interventions consisted of six sessions, each approximately 20 min in duration, delivered over a two-week period on separate days by trained facilitators.

Mindfulness meditation

Participants in the mindfulness meditation group completed a well-validated mindfulness-based mental training regimen to teach them to independently practice mindfulness meditation [25,26,[30], [31], [32]]. In each of the six intervention sessions, participants were asked to close their eyes, focus on their breath, recognize distracting thoughts and feelings, and “simply let go” of sensory events without judgment. They were taught that perceived sensory and affective events were “momentary”, “fleeting”, and did not require evaluation. In training sessions 1-2, individuals were instructed to focus on the breath occurring at the tip of the nose and follow the “full flow of the breath” and to attend to discursive sensory events without judgment. In session 5, these principles were reiterated, and an audio recording of MRI sounds was played during the final 10 min to familiarize participants with meditating in the MRI. In session 6, participants received no guided training while engaging in mindfulness meditation during an audio recording of the MRI sounds to better prepare them to independently practice meditation in an MRI environment [25,26].

Sham-mindfulness meditation

The purpose of the sham-mindfulness regimen was to lead participants to believe they were practicing mindfulness meditation without the specific instructions related to mindfully attending to the breath in a non-evaluative manner [25,32]. The primary difference between the mindfulness and sham-mindfulness training was the explicit mindfulness-based instructions (e.g., mindful attention to the breath) given only to those in the mindfulness meditation group. The sham-mindfulness group was instructed that they were randomly assigned to the mindfulness meditation condition [25,32]. In each of the six intervention sessions, participants were instructed to close their eyes and take a deep breath “as we sit here in meditation” every 2-3 min. All other aspects (training room, posture, facilitator, time providing instruction) were matched to the mindfulness condition. The MRI scanner sounds were introduced during the last 10 min of session 5. In session 6, participants received no instruction and “meditated” during an audio recording of the MRI sounds.

Book-listening control

As previously employed [25,30], the control group [33,34] listened to an audio recording of The Natural History and Antiquities of Selborne [35] in a quiet room across the six intervention sessions. This group served as a non-manipulation control to the “meditation” regimens, with consistent facilitator attention, setting, and time elapsed across all groups. Subjects were not allowed to sleep, use telephones, or talk during sessions. In the 5th and 6th sessions, the sounds of the MRI scanner were played during book listening, employing custom-made recordings to ensure participants could hear both the audiobook and scanner sounds [25].

Blinding procedures

A study coordinator instructed that participants were randomized to one of two groups (meditation; book listening), i.e., participants in the mindfulness and sham-mindfulness group were told that they had been randomized to the meditation group. Due to the nature of the mindfulness and sham-mindfulness interventions, intervention facilitators could not be blinded. However, research technicians were blinded to mindfulness and sham-mindfulness group assignment during fMRI/behavioral data acquisition.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was change in CBF, measured using perfusion fMRI during the LRT. Analyses focused on whole-brain and a priori regions of interest implicated in pain processing and cognitive-affective regulation, including the thalamus, OFC, and insula. DMN connectivity was examined as a pre-specified exploratory outcome given recent evidence [27]. There were two MRI scanners employed during this RCT: 1) a 3T GE MR750 Discovery scanner (GE Healthcare, Chicago, Il, USA) with a 32-channel head coil; and 2) a 3T Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma scanner (Siemens Healthineers AG, Munich, Germany) with a 32-channel head coil.

For the control group, participants were asked how effectively they listened to the audiobook.

Sample size

Drawing from a prior study [51], which used a similar three-arm design (mindfulness meditation, sham-mindfulness meditation, and book-listening control) in healthy individuals exposed to noxious heat, a conservative statistical threshold (z > 3.1, p < 0.05) [52] was employed to assess contrast of parameter estimates (COPE) on changes in CBF associated with meditation in response to the LRT as compared to rest and the control groups. This prior work yielded (G∗Power 3.1) an effect size of η2 = 0.33, corresponding to a large effect (Cohen's f = 0.70). Thirty participants per group were calculated to achieve approximately 90% power to detect a significant effect (α = 0.05) in a focused pairwise comparison of the primary outcome between the mindfulness meditation and control group. The comparison between mindfulness meditation and sham-mindfulness meditation was analyzed as a secondary comparison at α = 0.05 and the sham-mindfulness meditation vs. book-listening control group comparison was treated as exploratory.

Experimental procedures

Session 1 - Pre-intervention MRI 1: Participants reported to UCSD's CfMRI where all study procedures were explained before they then provided informed consent. For a given participant, all subsequent visits were performed on the same scanner. MRI safety was verified with medical record review. Prior to fMRI scanning, participants were taught to use the VAS to rate pain intensity and unpleasantness in response to twenty 5-s thermal stimuli (35°C-48°C) delivered to the right ventral forearm via the same thermal probe (QST.Lab, Strasburg, FR) employed during fMRI. This psychophysical training was used to train participants to reliably rate their pain to maximize the reproducibility of pain ratings [25,26,53,54]. Participants completed a battery of baseline behavioral and exploratory questionnaires on REDCap. Female volunteers provided a urine sample for a pregnancy test to verify they were not pregnant. After an MRI safety screening, participants were positioned supine on the scanner bed where they were fitted with a respiratory transducer and a finger pulse oximeter [25,26]. Participants were then instructed to remain still and close their eyes during the following procedures: A 1) resting state BOLD fMRI scan, 2) BOLD fMRI acquisition during the administration of the noxious heat series to the back of the calf (heat 1). 3) After the heat series, the thermal probe was removed from the calf, and pain ratings corresponding to the heat were collected. 4) An NRS rating corresponding to participants' low back pain while lying supine was collected (pre-LRT 1). 5) LRT 1 was then performed, and after confirming pain increases of at least 2 NRS points, participants were instructed to “close their eyes and remain still” during perfusion fMRI acquisition. 6) Following 5 min of scanning, NRS pain ratings for the “overall experience of your low back pain from the time your leg was lowered until now” were collected (post-LRT 1). 7) Perfusion fMRI was then performed while participants were lying supine. 8) The anatomical scan was acquired for 5 min 9) Supine NRS ratings were then collected (pre-LRT 2). 10) LRT 2 was performed. 11) Perfusion fMRI was acquired after a 2-point pain increase was confirmed. 12) Post-LRT 2 NRS pain ratings were acquired after 5 min of scanning. 13) Perfusion fMRI was acquired while participants were lying in the supine. 14) A noxious heat series was administered (heat 2) during BOLD acquisition. 15) Participants reported their pain ratings in response to said noxious heat. 16) Participants completed questionnaires, after being removed from the scanner, before concluding the session. Individuals were randomized during this session.

Sessions 2 - 7: Intervention training: Based on their group randomization, participants completed one of the three intervention assignments across six separate training sessions. Completion was defined as at least 4 of six sessions.

Session 8 - Post-intervention MRI 2: After successfully completing their respective intervention, participants returned to UCSD's CfMRI. Participants completed the same battery of questionnaires (REDCap) administered in Session 1. The procedures in Session 8 paralleled those described in Session 1 including a 1) resting state BOLD fMRI scan. 2) BOLD fMRI was acquired during noxious heat administration (heat 3). 3) After the heat series, the thermal probe was removed from the calf, and pain ratings corresponding to the heat were collected. 4) NRS ratings corresponding to participants' low back pain while lying supine were collected (pre-LRT 3). 5) LRT 3 was then performed, and participants were instructed to “close their eyes and remain still” during perfusion fMRI acquisition. 6) NRS pain ratings (post-LRT 3) were collected after 5 min at the completion of this scan. 7) Perfusion fMRI was acquired while lying supine. 8) A 5 min anatomical scan was acquired. 9) Supine NRS ratings were then collected (pre-LRT 4). 10) LRT 4 was performed. 11) Participants in the book-listening control group were instructed to “close their eyes and remain still” during a perfusion MRI scan. Participants in the mindfulness- and sham-mindfulness meditation interventions were instructed to “begin meditating and to continue until the end of the session” during fMRI acquisition. 12) After 5 min of perfusion scanning, NRS pain ratings were collected (post-LRT 4). 13) Perfusion fMRI was acquired while lying supine. 14) A noxious heat series (heat 4) was administered during BOLD fMRI acquisition. 15) Participants reported their pain ratings in response to said noxious heat. 16) Participants completed study questionnaires after the scan and were subsequently dismissed.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis of cerebral blood flow: Voxelwise analyses of LRT-evoked CBF changes were conducted using FSL FEAT. For each participant, a first-level general linear model contrasted CBF during manipulation (LRT4) with rest (LRT3). CBF values from MRI 1 (LRT1 and LRT2) served as an implicit baseline. Second-level analyses combined participants within each group to estimate mean manipulation-related effects. Third-level mixed-effects analyses were performed with FSL FLAME to model both within- and between-subject variance. Scanner type (GE vs. Siemens) was included as a covariate of no interest. Statistical maps were intensity-normalized and thresholded at Z > 3.1, and cluster-corrected at p < 0.05. In addition to testing the main manipulation effect (LRT4>LRT3), higher level modeling also included demeaned change in pain ratings (LRT 4–LRT 3) to assess whether brain responses to the manipulation tracked subjective experience.

Manipulation check for LRT pain: As a manipulation check, a paired samples t-test examined if the LRT produced significant increases in pain after performing LRT 1, in the pre-intervention MRI Session 1, when compared to lying supine.

Pain ratings: Linear mixed-effects models tested for differences in NRS pain report between groups, pre- and post-intervention MRI sessions, and condition (rest vs manipulation). These models were constructed using the lmer function of the “lme4” package applying Satterthwaite's method to approximate effective degrees of freedom and significance values with the “lmerTest” package. LRT-induced pain change, calculated by subtracting post-LRT from pre-LRT NRS pain ratings, served as the dependent variable in these models. Participant was modeled as a random effect, while fixed effects included group (mindfulness meditation vs sham-mindfulness meditation vs book listening control), session (MRI 1 vs MRI 2), condition (LRT 1-rest and LRT 3-rest vs LRT 2-rest and LRT 4-manipulation) and all two- and three-way interactions of group X session X condition. The initial model was interpreted as a type III ANOVA, using the anova function of base r (R core team, 2024), to assess significance of overall interaction effects. This analysis was followed by two post-hoc linear mixed models focused on MRI 1 and MRI 2 to evaluate the group by condition interaction pre- and post-intervention, respectively.

Respiration rate exploratory analyses: Respiration rates were analyzed with exploratory linear mixed-effects models testing for interactions between group (mindfulness meditation, sham-mindfulness, book-listening control), session (Pre-intervention MRI 1 vs. Post-intervention MRI 2), and condition (rest vs. manipulation). When significant group or interaction effects were observed, follow-up analyses restricted to MRI 2 were performed to determine how mindfulness meditation and sham-mindfulness meditation, relative to control, influenced LRT-evoked changes in breathing.

Discussion

Chronic low back pain is a leading cause of disability worldwide, with movement-evoked pain representing a particularly burdensome and functionally limiting component of the condition. Despite growing evidence that mindfulness meditation can reduce chronic pain, the neural mechanisms supporting mindfulness-related modulation of clinically relevant, movement-evoked pain remain insufficiently characterized. This sham-mindfulness meditation-controlled mechanistic RCT was designed to address this gap by integrating a clinically validated pain-evocation paradigm with perfusion-based neuroimaging to isolate mindfulness-specific neural processes underlying pain modulation.

A central strength of the trial is the use of a rigorously developed sham-mindfulness meditation condition that closely matches mindfulness training in structure, expectancy, facilitator interaction, and contextual features while omitting the core attentional component of non-reactive awareness. This design enables mechanistic differentiation between mindfulness-specific neurocognitive processes and nonspecific contextual or expectancy-related effects. By including both a sham-mindfulness condition and an active non-meditative control, the study advances beyond prior designs that have relied solely on passive controls, thereby enhancing interpretability of meditation-related neural effects.

The focus on movement-evoked pain using the LRT further enhances the clinical relevance of this work. The LRT reliably elicits pain flares that mirror those encountered during everyday movement in individuals with cLBP. Coupling this ecologically valid paradigm with perfusion MRI allows for quantification of sustained CBF changes associated with pain processing and modulation, offering a physiologically grounded index of neural activity well suited for tonic pain states. The single-center design facilitates methodological consistency and rigorous control over intervention delivery, neuroimaging acquisition, and pain-evocation procedures, although replication in multisite settings will be valuable for extending generalizability. Blinding of intervention facilitators was not feasible due to the nature of the interventions; however, standardized protocols, matched session characteristics, and blinded outcome assessments for the two meditation conditions were employed to minimize bias.

Several additional limitations warrant consideration. First, the single-center design, while advantageous for methodological consistency, limits the generalizability of findings to other clinical settings, patient populations, and neuroimaging platforms. Replication in multisite trials will be necessary before results can be broadly applied. Second, interventions were delivered via Zoom, which, while expanding access and reflecting real-world scalability, introduces potential variability in participant environment and reduces the degree of direct observational control over session fidelity. Third, the two-week intervention period is brief relative to standard mindfulness-based programs (e.g., Mindfulness-based stress reduction). Thus, whether the observed neural and behavioral effects reflect durable mechanisms or more transient training effects cannot be determined from this design. Fourth, reliance on self-reported pain as a secondary outcome introduces response bias and does not capture objective functional measures of disability. The inclusion of exploratory questionnaires (e.g., PROMIS, Roland Morris) partially mitigates this limitation. Fifth, although the book-listening control equates for time, attention, and facilitator contact, it does not control for expectancy effects in the same way the sham-mindfulness condition does; differences between book-listening and sham-mindfulness should therefore be interpreted with this asymmetry in mind. Sixth, the use of two different MRI scanner models (GE and Siemens) introduces potential sources of signal variance. While scanner type will be included as a covariate of no interest in all neuroimaging analyses, residual scanner effects nonetheless cannot be entirely ruled out.

If mindfulness meditation is found to produce distinct neural changes relative to both sham-mindfulness meditation and book-listening controls, several clinical and research implications would follow. Identifying mindfulness-specific neural signatures of movement-evoked pain modulation would provide a mechanistic rationale for incorporating brief, structured mindfulness training into multimodal pain management programs for individuals with cLBP, a population that frequently does not respond adequately to pharmacological treatment alone. From a research perspective, he neural targets hypothesized here, including OFC and insular engagement and thalamic deactivation, as well as exploratory DMN analyses, could serve as intermediate endpoints or stratification biomarkers in future efficacy-focused trials, enabling more targeted patient selection and outcome prediction. Conversely, null findings, particularly a failure to differentiate mindfulness from sham-mindfulness meditation at the neural level, would have important implications for how the field conceptualizes mindfulness-specific versus nonspecific contributions to pain relief, and would argue for refined mechanistic models and intervention designs. Either outcome advances the evidence base needed to determine whether and how mindfulness-based approaches should be scaled and integrated into clinical care for cLBP.
