Optimizing nitrogen management enhances photosynthesis and alfalfa productivity in jujube-alfalfa intercropping
Authors: Gulinigaer Taxi, Ling Li, Jinbin Wang, Zhengjun Cui, Tiantian Li, Yaru Zhao, Hang Qiao, Sumei Wan, Xiao-Fei Li, Wei Zhang, Hong Li, Guodong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough alfalfa is capable of nitrogen fixation, the optimal nitrogen (N) application rate for jujube-alfalfa intercropping systems in arid oasis regions remains unclear. This study evaluated four N application rates (0, 80, 160, and 240 kg/ha) to determine their effects on alfalfa photosynthesis, yield, and quality. Measurements of gas exchange and chlorophyll fluorescence revealed that the 160 kg/ha treatment (N2) significantly enhanced photosynthetic performance, particularly at the early flowering stage—a critical period when peak photosynthetic activity directly governs biomass accumulation and final yield. At this stage, the net photosynthetic rate (Pn) in the first and second harvests increased by 31.1% and 42.8%, respectively, while the photosynthetic performance index (PIabs) improved by 37.7%–102.2%. Compared with the unfertilized control, the N2 treatment significantly increased fresh forage yield by 23.8%–45.8% and dry matter yield by 18.4%–29.7%; crude protein content rose by up to 9.6% in the second harvest. Yield was significantly positively correlated with Pn, transpiration rate (Tr), the maximum photochemical efficiency of PSII (Fv/Fm), and PIabs. In conclusion, a nitrogen application rate of 160 kg/ha is recommended to maximize alfalfa yield and quality in this intercropping system.Keywords: Intercropping, Nitrogen application rate, Alfalfa, Photosynthetic properties, Fluorescence parameters, Yield qualitySubject terms: Physiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Intercropping improves crop yield, economic returns, soil fertility, and land use efficiency. Research has demonstrated that fruit-grass intercropping systems optimize the micro-environment, which helps reduce the incidence of pests and diseases. Furthermore, these systems mitigate the degradation of soil physicochemical properties, thereby enhancing microbial diversity and enzymatic activities1,2. Furthermore, such systems enhance fruit quality, increase forage biomass, and improve nutritional value, thereby providing both ecological and economic benefits for orchard management3–5.Intercropping with perennial leguminous plants effectively suppresses weed growth, enhances nitrogen fixation, and promotes plant growth and nutrient uptake, thereby improving both the quality and yield of forage6,7.A rational intercropping layout facilitates complementary niches in both space and time, optimizes the canopy structure, and increases the total photosynthetic leaf area. Additionally, by leveraging variations in plant growth durations, these systems can extend the photosynthetic period. This synergy enhances both the yield and quality of the system, aligning with the agricultural objective of high yield and superior quality8.

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) is one of the most widely cultivated leguminous forages globally, recognized for its tolerance to abiotic stresses—such as cold, drought, and saline-alkali conditions—as well as for its high yield, superior quality, capacity for soil improvement, and substantial economic benefits9. Alfalfa, widely known as the ‘King of Forages’10, is primarily cultivated in Northwest China—a region with an arid climate, limited rainfall, and inherently low soil fertility11.Thus, under naturally harsh conditions, achieving both high quality and high yield in alfalfa production remains an urgent challenge. Nitrogen availability directly influences photosynthetic performance, yield, and quality. However, in recent years, excessive reliance on chemical fertilizers has raised concerns over irrational nitrogen fertilization practices. While appropriate nitrogen application can significantly enhance photosynthetic efficiency—thereby promoting growth and improving forage quality—excessive nitrogen use contributes to environmental pollution on one hand, and inhibits photosynthetic characteristics, growth, and development on the other, ultimately leading to reduced yield and compromised quality12,13.Recent studies on nitrogen management in alfalfa have further substantiated the importance of appropriate nitrogen fertilization. Moderate nitrogen application enhances soil nitrogen availability, stimulates root growth and activity, and thereby increases dry matter yield and crude protein content, ultimately improving nutritional quality14.Conversely, excessive nitrogen application suppresses alfalfa growth, diminishes nitrogen use efficiency, induces nutritional imbalances, degrades forage quality, and exacerbates environmental burdens15.This “optimal–excess” pattern is similarly evident in other crops. Nitrogen is intimately linked to crop yield and quality formation16. Moderate nitrogen supply promotes tillering in rice, elevates leaf area index and leaf sheath dry weight, and facilitates grain filling and seed setting, thereby enhancing yield17,18; excessive nitrogen, however, comparably compromises both yield and quality19,20.

The improvement of photosynthetic capacity in a crop is determined not only by the genetic traits of the variety but also by cultivation management practices, including irrigation and fertilization methods21.Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters are key indicators for assessing plant photosynthetic performance, as they reflect the efficiency of light energy capture, conversion, and utilization during photosynthesis22. Nitrogen application rate is a key determinant influencing both the photosynthetic rate and chlorophyll content in alfalfa leaves23.Studies have demonstrated that nitrogen application increases the maximum photochemical efficiency (Fv/Fm) of PSII in wheat leaves by 6.19–8.30%, thereby enhancing the primary electron transport efficiency within PSII reaction centers and consequently improving the photosynthetic capacity of the plants24,25. Rational nitrogen application can markedly improve leaf photosynthetic capacity and apparent quantum efficiency26.Furthermore, studies have indicated that nitrogen fertilization significantly enhances the net photosynthetic rate and stomatal conductance of alfalfa leaves compared to nitrogen-free controls, thereby improving overall photosynthetic performance and stimulating key metabolic enzyme activities27.Recent studies have demonstrated that appropriate nitrogen application rates and rational management can significantly enhance leaf net photosynthetic rate, chlorophyll content, and the maximum photochemical efficiency of PSII, while reducing intercellular CO₂ concentration and non-photochemical quenching28. These modifications improve light energy capture and carbon assimilation capacity. Excessive nitrogen application, however, suppresses the efficient functioning of the photosynthetic apparatus29.Optimization of photosynthetic performance promotes dry matter accumulation and yield formation, while concurrently elevating nitrogen use efficiency30.

Currently, there is a lack of systematic research on the effects of nitrogen application rates on the photosynthetic characteristics, yield, and quality of alfalfa in jujube-alfalfa intercropping systems in the southern Xinjiang oasis region. Although alfalfa possesses nitrogen-fixing capabilities, its natural nitrogen fixation under these intercropping conditions often fails to meet the nitrogen requirements for high-yield, high-quality cultivation. Therefore, this study aims to fill the research gap regarding nitrogen management in this specific cropping system. The objective is to investigate the effects of different nitrogen application rates on alfalfa photosynthetic characteristics, chlorophyll fluorescence parameters, yield, and nutritional quality, and to determine the optimal nitrogen application rate. The research hypothesis posits that an appropriate nitrogen application rate can significantly improve the photosynthetic performance, yield, and quality of alfalfa leaves, whereas excessive nitrogen application will have an inhibitory effect. The findings of this study not only provide a scientific basis for rational nitrogen management in the jujube-alfalfa intercropping system in the southern Xinjiang oases but also offer valuable theoretical and practical guidance for sustainable nitrogen management in similar orchard-pasture intercropping systems in arid and semi-arid regions.

Materials and methods

Experimental field site

This experiment was conducted in a jujube orchard at the Horticultural Experiment Station of Tarim University, located in Alaer City, Xinjiang (40°32′34″ N, 81°18′07″ E; altitude 1015 m). Alaer City lies in the upper reaches of the Tarim River and on the northwestern fringe of the Taklimakan Desert. The region is characterized by a warm-temperate continental arid climate, with an average annual evaporation ranging from 1976.6 to 2558.9 mm. The groundwater table exceeds 3 m in depth, and precipitation is extremely limited, with an annual rainfall averaging between 40.1 and 82.5 mm.The region is characterized by abundant sunshine and substantial light and heat resources, with an average annual sunshine duration of approximately 3199.9 h, a frost-free period ranging from 180 to 224 d, a mean annual temperature of 10.8 °C, and an accumulated temperature ≥ 10 °C typically exceeding 4000 °C.The experimental site is characterized by both high surface evaporation and an arid climate, with prevailing northeasterly winds. Drip irrigation is employed as the primary irrigation method. The soil at the experimental station is classified as sandy loam, and the initial soil fertility characteristics are provided in Table 1.

Experimental design

The experiment was conducted between 2013 and 2022, and yield data were collected in both 2021 and 2022, whereas data related to photosynthetic characteristics and quality were collected in 2022, investigating alfalfa within a jujube-alfalfa intercropping system using a randomized complete block design with a single factor. Four nitrogen (N) application rates were evaluated: 0 (N0), 80 (N1), 160 (N2), and 240 (N3) kg/ha. The experimental hypothesis was that, in the jujube-alfalfa intercropping system, photosynthetic performance and yield of alfalfa would initially increase and then decline with increasing nitrogen application, and that an optimal nitrogen application threshold exists. The rationale for gradient selection was as follows: 0 kg/ha served as the nitrogen-free control; 80 kg/ha represented a low nitrogen level; 160 kg/ha corresponded to the locally recommended nitrogen application rate and was considered the optimal nitrogen level; and 240 kg/ha represented an excessive nitrogen level. Each treatment was replicated three times, resulting in a total of 12 experimental plots. The individual plot size was 42 m² (14 m length × 3 m width). The nitrogen fertilizer used was urea (N: 46%); 50% of N was applied before sowing (12 April 2021 and 15 April 2022) and the remaining 50% was applied after the first harvest (4 July 2021 and 6 July 2022). N fertilizer was applied with irrigation water, and the annual irrigation volume was 4300 m³ hm⁻². The alfalfa was harvested by hand, and artificial weeding was carried out during the crop growth period. At the same time, the redundant buds of the jujube trees were treated. The other management practices that were used were the same as those in the field.

The plant materials used in this study consisted of alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) and jujube (Ziziphus jujuba Mill. cv. Junzao). The jujube orchard was established in 2012 from direct-seeded wild jujube (Ziziphus acidojujuba), which was grafted with the cultivated jujube variety in spring 2014. The trees were planted at a spacing of 1 m × 3 m (plant × row). Drip irrigation was maintained throughout the experimental period, and manual weeding was carried out as necessary. All other management practices followed standard local orchard protocols (see Fig. 1).

Measured parameters

Photosynthetic characteristics of alfalfa

The LI-6400 XT portable photosynthetic measurement system (LI-6400, Li-COR Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA) was used to measure photosynthetic physiological parameters of alfalfa leaves, including net photosynthetic rate (Pn), stomatal conductance (Gs), intercellular CO₂ concentration (Ci), and transpiration rate (Tr). Measurements were conducted at four growth stages of alfalfa: seedling stage (cotyledons emerge and unfold, and the first true leaf is fully expanded); bud stage (the first flower buds differentiate in the leaf axils, with distinct buds visible at the stem apex but no purple petals open); initial flowering stage (5%–10% of plants in the population are flowering, i.e., approximately one in every ten plants has open flowers); full flowering stage (approximately 50% of plants in the population are flowering, with abundant open flowers; stems become markedly thicker and more lignified, and the proportion of leaf area declines). Measurements were taken on sunny days with ample sunlight between 11:00 and 13:00. For each treatment, three plants were selected based on uniform growth, absence of pests and diseases, consistent light exposure, and similar leaf age (the third fully expanded functional leaf from the apex). All measurements were performed under these standardized conditions.

Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters of alfalfa

Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters of alfalfa leaves, including initial fluorescence (F0), maximum fluorescence yield (Fm), the maximum photochemical efficiency of PSII (Fv/Fm), and the photosynthetic performance index (PIabs), were measured using a Ya Xin-1105 portable chlorophyll fluorometer (Yaxin-1105; Beijing Yaxinliyi Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Measurements were conducted at the same four growth stages described in Sect. “Photosynthetic characteristics of alfalfa” (seedling stage, bud stage, initial flowering stage, and full flowering stage). Prior to measurement, leaves were dark-adapted for 30 min. All measurements were taken on clear days between 11:00 and 13:00. For each treatment, three plants were selected based on uniform growth, absence of pests and diseases, consistent light exposure, and the same leaf position (the third fully expanded leaf from the apex).

Alfalfa yield and fresh-dry ratio determination

Alfalfa was harvested twice per year, and the fresh forage yield was determined for each plot. After excluding border rows, three random 1 m × 1 m quadrats were selected per plot to measure fresh weight at a stubble height of 5 cm. All collected samples were placed in a 105 °C oven for 30 min for enzyme deactivation, and then dried at 80 °C until constant weight was achieved. The fresh–dry ratio was calculated as the ratio of fresh weight to dry weight.

Alfalfa nutritional quality

The dried alfalfa samples from each plot were ground, sieved through a 0.425 mm (40-mesh) sieve, and placed in self-sealing bags for the determination of nutrient content.

(1) Crude ash (CA): Determined using the dry ashing method.Place the porcelain crucible in a muffle furnace and heat it at 550 °C for at least 30 min, then cool it to room temperature in a drying oven and weigh it to an accuracy of 0.001 g. Accurately weigh the sample (5 g), place it in the crucible, and heat it slowly on a hot plate until carbonized. Then transfer the crucible to a muffle furnace preheated to 550 °C and calcine for 4 h. Transfer the crucible to a desiccator to cool to room temperature, then weigh it again31.

(2) Crude fat (CF): Determine using the Soxhlet extraction method. Wrap approximately 5 g of the dry sample in filter paper and place it in a Soxhlet extractor. Extract for approximately 6 h at 70 °C using petroleum ether as the solvent. After evaporating the solvent, dry the sample and weigh it. Calculate the crude fat content by dividing the weight of the extracted fat by the initial weight of the sample and multiplying by 100%31.

(3) Crude protein (CP): Determined using a Dumas nitrogen analyzer (Model DN2100 Dumas Nitrogen Analyzer, Nordtec, Beijing, China). Approximately 100 mg of ground alfalfa sample was accurately weighed and placed in an aluminum foil cup. The sample was placed in the combustion chamber and combusted at high temperature (approximately 950–1000 °C) under an oxygen-rich atmosphere, converting all nitrogen-containing compounds into nitrogen oxides, which were subsequently reduced to elemental nitrogen (N₂). Total nitrogen content was determined using a thermal conductivity detector, and crude protein content was calculated by multiplying the total nitrogen content by the standard conversion factor of 6.2532.

Statistical analysis

Microsoft Excel 2021 was chosen to organize and tabulate the raw data. Experimental results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using SPSS 27.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and Duncan’s multiple range test was applied to assess statistical significance at the P < 0.05 level. In correlation analysis, P < 0.05 and P < 0.01 were adopted as the thresholds for statistical significance. Principal component analysis (PCA) and all figures were generated using Origin 2021 software (Origin Lab Corporation, Inc., Northampton, MA, USA). Prior to analysis, the data were standardized, and principal components (PC1 and PC2) with eigenvalues > 1 were extracted to explain the major variance in the dataset. PCA biplots and composite scores were employed to visualize the relationships between different nitrogen treatments and the measured indicators. Planting pattern diagrams were created using Microsoft PowerPoint 2021.

Results

Effects of different nitrogen application rates on photosynthetic characteristics of alfalfa

Effects of different nitrogen application rates on Pn of alfalfa

The variation trends of Pn in leaves of two alfalfa cuttings are shown in Fig. 2. Compared with N0, the N3 treatment significantly reduced Pn by 18.51% and 17.21% in the first and second cuttings, respectively, at the S1 stage. In subsequent growth stages, nitrogen application treatments generally resulted in higher Pn compared to N0. Specifically, in the first cutting, at the S2 stage, Pn values under N1, N2, and N3 were significantly higher than those under N0 by 9.36%, 14.65%, and 21.29%, respectively; at the S3 stage, they were higher by 17.98%, 31.06%, and 10.46%; and at the S4 stage, by 16.07%, 50.91%, and 33.62%. A similar trend was observed in the second cutting: at S2, Pn under N1, N2, and N3 was significantly higher than under N0 by 14.65%, 23.14%, and 33.45%, respectively; at S3, by 26.93%, 42.83%, and 18.41%; and at S4, by 29.75%, 61.42%, and 52.84%.Moreover, for both the first and second cuttings, the N2 treatment resulted in significantly higher values than the other treatments at both the S3 and S4 stages.

Effects of different nitrogen application rates on Ci of alfalfa

Figure 3 illustrates the changes in Ci in alfalfa leaves across both harvests. As the growth period advanced, Ci initially decreased and subsequently increased, reaching its minimum value at the early flowering stage.Compared to the N0 treatment, the Ci under the N2 treatment was significantly higher by 10.47% and 6.93% at the seedling stage of the first and second cuttings, respectively (P < 0.05). During the bud stage of the first cuttings, the Ci in the N0 treatment was significantly higher—by 16.68%—than that in the N2 treatment (P < 0.05). Significant differences were also observed at the early and full flowering stages, where the Ci under N0 exceeded that under N2 by 6.30% and 23.85%, respectively (P < 0.05).Similarly, during the second harvest, the Ci in the N0 treatment was 9.45% higher than that in the N2 treatment at the bud stage (P < 0.05). Similarly significant differences were observed at the early and full flowering stages, where the Ci under N0 exceeded that under N2 by 4.34% and 26.09%, respectively (P < 0.05).Furthermore, the Ci under the N0 treatment was significantly higher than that under the N2 treatment at both the bud and full flowering stages in each harvest.

Effects of different nitrogen application rates on yield and quality of alfalfa

Effects of different nitrogen application rates on yield of alfalfa

Figure 9 presents the fresh weight yield, dry matter yield, and fresh-dry ratio of alfalfa under different nitrogen application rates during 2021–2022. Over the two-year period, both fresh weight yield and dry matter yield initially increased and then declined with increasing nitrogen application rates. In 2022, the N2 treatment achieved the highest fresh weight yield and dry matter yield, with fresh weight yields of 43,367 kg/ha and 37,533 kg/ha in the first and second cuttings, respectively, and dry matter yields of 8,466.67 kg/ha and 8,388.67 kg/ha, respectively. The N0 treatment recorded the lowest yields. Relative to N0, the N2 treatment significantly enhanced fresh weight yield by 45.81% and 31.20% in the first and second cuttings, respectively, and dry matter yield by 25.74% and 29.67%, respectively (P < 0.05). The fresh-dry ratio exhibited an initial increase followed by a decline with increasing nitrogen application rates. In 2022, the N0 treatment displayed the highest fresh-dry ratio, attaining 29.93% and 32.65% in the first and second cuttings, respectively, whereas the N2 treatment exhibited the lowest values of 25.32% and 26.11%, respectively. The fresh-dry ratio in the N3 treatment rebounded significantly relative to N2, increasing by 8.66% and 13.29% in the first and second cuttings, respectively (P < 0.05).

Correlation analysis among photosynthetic characteristics, chlorophyll fluorescence parameters, yield, and quality of alfalfa

To further clarify the relationships between photosynthetic characteristics, chlorophyll fluorescence parameters, yield, and quality of alfalfa under nitrogen application, a correlation analysis was performed using 12 indicators throughout the entire growth period. The results, presented in Fig. 10, indicated that yield was significantly positively correlated with Pn, Tr, PIabs, and Fv/Fm (P < 0.01), and showed a significant positive correlation with F0 (P < 0.05).Among these correlations, the strongest was observed between yield and Fv/Fm, with a correlation coefficient of 0.93. Meanwhile, yield was significantly negatively correlated with Ci (P < 0.01) and significantly negatively correlated with FDR (P < 0.05).

Discussion

The application of nitrogen fertilizer can enhance alfalfa’s photosynthetic capacity by increasing leaf nitrogen content, thereby improving the plant’s stress tolerance33. The results of this study indicate that as nitrogen application rates increased, the Pn, Gs, and Tr of alfalfa leaves all exhibited a trend of initially rising and then declining, reaching a peak under the N2 treatment (160 kg/ha) at the beginning of flowering (Figs. 2 and 4, and 5); meanwhile, Ci showed a significant negative correlation with Pn (Fig. 3). From a physiological perspective, moderate nitrogen application promotes the synthesis of Rubisco in leaves and enhances its activity, thereby increasing carboxylation efficiency and net photosynthetic rate; simultaneously, an adequate nitrogen supply helps maintain normal stomatal regulation, ensuring the smooth diffusion of CO234. A study by Fang et al.35 on buckwheat also indicated that nitrogen application significantly influences leaf photosynthetic capacity and grain yield; both parameters exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease with increasing nitrogen levels, consistent with the findings of this study. Similarly, nitrogen application significantly altered photosynthetic parameters in wheat: as nitrogen levels increased, Pn, Gs, and Tr all initially rose and then declined, peaking under the medium-nitrogen treatment during flowering, while Ci exhibited a trend opposite to that of Pn36, further corroborating the conclusions of this study. Although nitrogen application can promote photosynthesis, excessive application inhibits this process. Under high-nitrogen conditions, carbon-nitrogen metabolism in plants becomes imbalanced; excess nitrogen accumulates as non-protein nitrogen, accelerating leaf senescence. Simultaneously, excess nitrogen stimulates excessive growth of vegetative organs, leading to canopy closure and insufficient light penetration to lower leaves, resulting in a decline in overall photosynthetic efficiency37,38. Moderate nitrogen fertilization can increase Gs, Tr, and Pn; however, when application rates exceed the optimal range, excessive vegetative growth leads to a decline in these photosynthetic parameters, ultimately resulting in reduced yields39. These findings are consistent with the experimental results of this study.

Nitrogen deficiency impairs light energy absorption and reduces the activity of the PSII reaction center40. As a probe for studying photosynthesis, chlorophyll fluorescence can reveal processes such as the absorption, transfer, distribution, and dissipation of light energy within the photosynthetic apparatus during light-dependent reactions41. The results of this study indicate that, with increasing nitrogen fertilizer application rates, chlorophyll fluorescence parameters in alfalfa leaves first increased and then decreased. Moderate nitrogen application significantly increased the values of F0, Fv/Fm, and PIabs, with the highest values observed in the N2 treatment (160 kg/ha). Compared with the control group (N0), the fluorescence parameters under the N2 treatment were significantly improved (Figs. 6, 7 and 8). This is mainly attributed to the fact that an appropriate nitrogen supply maintained the stability and repair efficiency of the D1 protein in the PSII reaction center, ensuring the smooth operation of the photosynthetic electron transport chain42,43. Previous studies have reported that gas exchange and chlorophyll fluorescence kinetic parameters in naked oat leaves also exhibit a trend of initially increasing and then decreasing, with a decline as nitrogen fertilizer application rates increase44, which is consistent with the results of this experiment. Appropriate nitrogen fertilization significantly enhanced Fv/Fm and ΦPSII by increasing the activity of the PSII reaction center23,24; whereas low-nitrogen treatment significantly reduced F0 and Fv/Fm in leaves, thereby impairing light energy transfer and electron transport efficiency in different maize varieties45. This study indicates that leaf parameters, including F0, Fv/Fm, and PIabs, generally exhibit an initial increase followed by a decrease with increasing nitrogen fertilizer application, with fluorescence parameters under medium-nitrogen treatment being higher than those under high-nitrogen treatment. High nitrogen treatments reduce chlorophyll fluorescence properties and the rate of photosynthetic electron transport, impairing light energy capture and conversion efficiency, and ultimately weakening photosynthetic performance46,47. High nitrogen application significantly reduces chlorophyll fluorescence parameters in alfalfa leaves and inhibits the primary conversion of light energy and light energy capture efficiency in the PSII reaction center48. The underlying physiological mechanism is that high nitrogen stress induces a massive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in leaves, leading to oxidative damage that disrupts thylakoid membrane structure and the D1 protein of the PSII reaction center. This accelerates chlorophyll degradation and impedes photosynthetic electron transport, ultimately resulting in significant decreases in Fv/Fm, PIabs, ΦPSII, and net photosynthetic rate, as well as a significant increase in NPQ heat dissipation49; simultaneously, excess nitrogen disrupts the carbon-nitrogen metabolic balance and inhibits nitrogen fixation activity in root nodules, further exacerbating the decline in photosynthetic performance50,51. This is consistent with the findings of the present study.

During its growth and development, alfalfa has high nutrient requirements that often exceed the soil’s natural supply capacity. Therefore, external nutrient supplementation is crucial. As an essential nutrient, the appropriate application of nitrogen can meet al.falfa’s nitrogen requirements, promote leaf expansion and protein synthesis, and ultimately increase yield52. Previous studies have shown that nitrogen application under drought conditions can increase alfalfa yield53. However, excessive nitrogen fertilization may inhibit growth performance54, a finding consistent with the results of this study. Furthermore, nitrogen fertilizer application rates significantly influence crop yield; within the range of 145 to 190 kg/ha, both alfalfa yield and economic returns can be optimized55. Studies have shown that, compared to the control group and the application of 80 kg/ha of nitrogen fertilizer, a rate of 160 kg/ha significantly increased alfalfa fresh weight and dry weight yields56. This study further confirms that alfalfa yield first increases and then decreases with increasing nitrogen fertilizer application rates, peaking at 160 kg/ha. These results indicate that the N₂ treatment (160 kg/ha) promoted alfalfa yield (Fig. 9), which is consistent with previous studies.

Alfalfa is a perennial leguminous forage crop and one of the most widely cultivated and highest-yielding forage species globally57. The results of this study indicate that nitrogen fertilization significantly increased the crude fat, crude ash, and crude protein contents in alfalfa. These parameters initially increased and then decreased with increasing nitrogen application rates, peaking at 160 kg/ha, indicating that this application rate optimizes the nutritional quality of alfalfa (Table 2). Moderate nitrogen application enhances nitrogen uptake and assimilation efficiency in the root system, providing sufficient precursors for amino acid and protein synthesis, thereby increasing crude protein content58. Previous studies have shown that nitrogen application rates exceeding 100 kg/ha can significantly increase the crude protein and crude ash contents of alfalfa; however, excessive nitrogen application may lead to the accumulation of non-protein nitrogen. This portion of nitrogen cannot be converted into true protein, thereby reducing the effective crude protein content while simultaneously inhibiting nodule nitrogenase activity and weakening the plant’s own nitrogen-fixing capacity59. The results of this study are consistent with these findings. Wan et al.60 also reported that as nitrogen application rates increased, the crude protein, crude fat, and crude ash contents of alfalfa first rose and then declined, peaking at 120–160 kg/ha. Under the experimental conditions of this study, a nitrogen application rate of 160 kg/ha effectively increased the crude protein, crude ash, and crude fat contents of alfalfa, thereby enhancing its overall nutritional quality.

Furthermore, in this study, yield was highly significantly positively correlated with Pn, Tr, PIabs, and Fv/Fm; significantly positively correlated with F0; and highly significantly negatively correlated with Ci (Fig. 10). These results indicate that increasing nitrogen fertilizer application promotes increases in Pn, Tr, PIabs, Fv/Fm, and F0 in alfalfa leaves while reducing Ci, thereby improving photosynthetic and chlorophyll fluorescence characteristics, enhancing photosynthetic conversion efficiency, and ultimately leading to increased yield. These findings are consistent with previous studies61,62.

This study is the first to elucidate the comprehensive regulatory effects of nitrogen application rates on the photosynthetic characteristics, fluorescence parameters, yield, and quality of alfalfa in jujube-alfalfa intercropping systems in the oasis region of southern Xinjiang. The optimal nitrogen application rate for this region was identified as 160 kg/ha, and the intrinsic relationships among these indicators were revealed through correlation and principal component analyses. The strengths of this study are as follows: reliance on a long-term field experimental platform (2013–2022) ensured data stability and reliability; the measured indicators encompassed multiple aspects, including photosynthesis, fluorescence, yield, and quality, and the application of multivariate analysis (correlation and principal component analyses) elucidated the patterns of nitrogen effects; and the regional applicability of the findings was clearly defined, thereby enhancing the practical relevance of the study. Certain limitations should also be acknowledged. With the exception of yield data, which were collected in both 2021 and 2022, photosynthetic and quality data were obtained from a single growing season (2022), and the interannual robustness of the conclusions therefore requires further verification. In addition, the experiment focused exclusively on the nitrogen requirements of alfalfa and did not concurrently evaluate the response of jujube trees to nitrogen fertilization within the intercropping system; future research should comprehensively assess the nitrogen demands of both components to optimize fertilization strategies for the entire system. Furthermore, because this study was conducted under the specific climatic and edaphic conditions of the southern Xinjiang oasis region, the optimal nitrogen application rate of 160 kg/ha identified herein requires further validation before extrapolation to jujube-alfalfa intercropping systems in other arid oasis regions.

Conclusion

This study indicates that in the jujube-alfalfa intercropping system of the southern Xinjiang oasis region, an application rate of 160 kg/ha of nitrogen significantly increased alfalfa’s net photosynthetic rate, chlorophyll fluorescence parameters, fresh and dry matter yields, and crude protein content; whereas excessive nitrogen application (240 kg/ha) diminished these beneficial effects. Therefore, 160 kg/ha is the optimal nitrogen application rate for high-yield, high-quality alfalfa in this region. These findings are applicable to the jujube-grass intercropping ecological zone in the oases of southern Xinjiang and provide theoretical and technical support for efficient nitrogen management in similar arid oasis intercropping systems.
