Genetic structure and plumage color candidate genes of Yunnan Partridge duck revealed by whole genome resequencing
Authors: Siyi Tang, Jingjing Qi, Jie Yu, Weihua Jin, Zaimei Yuan, Dinghua Zhang, Hao Yang, Xiaohong Jiao, Mingping Yang, Hehe Liu, Liang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Feather color is an important economic trait and genetic marker in domestic ducks, and its genetic architecture is critical for selective breeding. The Yunnan Partridge duck is an important conserved population in Yunnan Province, China, comprising three plumage color types: spotted, gray, and yellow partridge. However, research on its genetic basis remains limited. This study aimed to elucidate the genetic diversity, population structure, and the genetic basis of hatchling plumage color in the Yunnan Partridge duck. Based on whole-genome sequencing data, the genetic diversity, population structure, and selection signals of the Yunnan Partridge duck population were analyzed, with results as follows: the highest proportion (29.6%) exhibited a minor allele frequency between 0.1 and 0.2. The mean observed heterozygosity (Ho = 0.1993) was lower than the expected (He = 0.2909). IBS and NJ tree supported three genetic clusters. Analysis of runs of homozygosity detected 3,474 fragments, yielding a low mean genomic inbreeding coefficient (FROH) of 0.05493. Based on genome-wide association study (GWAS) analysis, a significant genetic signal associated with plumage color was identified on chromosome 21 within the 2.25–2.4 Mb region, with ASIP determined as the key candidate gene. LD analysis revealed a strong haplotype structure in this region, with the high-frequency haplotypes H1 and H2 (chr21:2334307-2337146) showing highly significant differentiation between speckled and gray-feathered populations, indicating they are the primary genetic determinants of plumage color. In summary, Yunnan Partridge duck exhibits moderate genetic diversity and a low level of inbreeding, with ASIP is the major gene, haplotypes H1 and H2 can distinguish spotted and gray plumage. These findings provide effective molecular markers and a genetic basis for the conservation and targeted breeding of plumage color in the Yunnan Partridge duck.

Introduction

China's diverse geography and climate have fostered exceptionally rich genetic resources in livestock and poultry, constituting a vital component of its biodiversity. The conservation of these resources is crucial for sustainable agriculture development. The Yunnan Partridge duck, a small to medium-sized local Chinese breed valued for its dual-purpose (egg and meat) qualities, combines strong adaptability, stress resistance, and foraging hardiness with high fertility, making it well-suited for free-range systems. During the juvenile stage, the breed exhibits three distinct hatching plumage colors: gray, spotted, and yellow partridge. The plumage matures primarily in the brown Partridge's feathers in adulthood. Although genetic assessment is essential for breed conservation and utilization, and has been extensively applied in many Chinese duck breeds, research on the Yunnan Partridge duck in this area is very limited.

Whole-genome resequencing has become an increasingly vital tool in livestock and poultry conservation, providing robust support for the preservation and utilization of endangered local breeds (Marras et al., 2015; Peripolli et al., 2017; Vanavermaete et al., 2020). This genomic approach enables systematic evaluation of the genetic diversity in the Yunnan Partridge duck, providing a theoretical foundation for its scientific conservation and sustainable utilization. In the absence of mutation, genomic information is stably inherited across generations, allowing individuals to share identical chromosomal segments or haplotypes (Nielsen et al., 2012). In diploid organisms, inherited identical chromosomal segments from shared ancestors can result in runs of homozygosity (ROH), where all loci within these segments become homozygous(Ceballos et al., 2018; Kirin et al., 2010). These segments are also termed chromosome segment homozygosity (CSH) (Hayes and Kelly, 2003). Analyzing variation in ROH length and abundance serves as a valuable indicator for inferring past demographic dynamics, as historical bottlenecks or inbreeding events influence the distribution of runs of homozygosity within a population (Bertolini et al., 2018; Bosse et al., 2012; Cardoso et al., 2018). Genetic assessments based on genome resequencing have been widely conducted in many local duck breeds. For example, RAD sequencing analyses of breeds such as the Liancheng White Duck have elucidated the population structure, genetic diversity, and selection signals of Fujian local ducks (Lin et al., 2022). However, genomic studies on the Yunnan Partridge duck are still lacking.

Chinese indigenous duck breeds display a wide array of plumage colors, with representative examples being the Liancheng White (white plumage), Putian Black (black plumage), and Jinding (partridge plumage) ducks. The genetic basis of these plumage variations is actively being uncovered, multiple association studies have systematically documented correlations between specific genetic variants in genes such as MC1R (Araguas et al., 2018; da Cruz et al., 2020; Miao et al., 2010; Santana et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2020), MITF (Wang et al., 2014),EDNRB (Miwa et al., 2007; Wu et al., 2017),TYR and TYRP1 (Xu et al., 2013), and plumage color phenotypes. For instance, a genome-wide association study (GWAS) using an F2 population from a cross between white Pekin ducks and mallards identified a 6.6 kb insertion in the MITF gene, which almost inhibited its expression and subsequently shut down TYR expression, thereby completely blocking melanin production (Zhou et al., 2018). At present, the genetic basis underlying the specific plumage color types of the Yunnan Partridge duck remains to be fully elucidated.

Therefore, this study aims to achieve three main objectives. First, to assess genetic diversity and population structure, we measured genetic diversity using observed heterozygosity (Ho), expected heterozygosity (He), and minor allele frequency (MAF), and analyzed population structure based on identity-by-state (IBS) distance. Second, to estimate genome-wide inbreeding levels, we quantified individual relatedness using runs-of-homozygosity (ROH)-based inbreeding coefficients (FROH). Finally, to identify key genes and molecular markers associated with feather color, we conducted a genome-wide association study by integrating trait records, detected significantly associated single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), and visualized them using Locus Zoom. In addition, we performed functional annotation of candidate genes within relevant genomic regions to screen for genes regulating melanogenesis and analyzed haplotype structure based on linkage disequilibrium (LD). Collectively, these results delineate the population's genetic architecture and diversity, establish a foundation for germplasm conservation, elucidate the molecular basis of plumage coloration, and advance molecular marker-assisted breeding, thereby offering insights for avian pigmentation genetics.

Materials and methods

Birds phenotypic collection

All Yunnan Partridge ducks used in this study were obtained from a conservation farm in Yunnan Province and were maintained under identical husbandry conditions. A total of 373 ducks were selected for photography. Under the same conditions, full-body photos were taken of the ducks at hatching, 40 days old, and 100 days old, including feather color, beak, and web. Standards were established (different classifications were made according to age, and phenotypic standards between each week of age were correlated), and individual numbers were maintained for individual identification. Blood samples were collected from the brachial vein of 100 Yunnan Partridge ducks and preserved in EDTA-coated tubes at −20°C. All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee (AEWC) of Sichuan Agricultural University (Approval No.2024302119).

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from all samples using the phenol-chloroform method. Based on concentrations measured with a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo. NanoDrop2000C, USA) spectrophotometer, each DNA sample was diluted to 100 ng/μL. DNA integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis.

Whole-genome resequencing and genotyping

High-quality genomic DNA from 100 Yunnan Partridge ducks was used to construct paired-end libraries (150-bp insert size) following the Illumina® HiSeq X-Ten protocol. All qualified libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X-Ten platform at Bio-company (Berry Genomics, Beijing, China), yielding an average sequencing depth of 10× per sample(Li and Durbin, 2009). The resulting data showed high quality, with >96% of bases achieving a Q30 score, uniform base composition, and no significant AT/GC bias. Clean reads were aligned to the duck reference genome ZJU1.0 (GCF_015476345.1) using the Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA aln) (DePristo et al., 2011). SNP variant calling was performed with the GATK HaplotypeCaller to generate a genomic VCF (gVCF) file(Danecek et al., 2011).

Data quality control

Subsequent SNP filtering was conducted using PLINK v1.90 with the following criteria: (1) MAF > 0.05 and < 0.99, (2) maximum missing rate per site < 0.1, and (3) biallelic sites only. After quality control, over 95% of high-quality SNPs were retained, meeting the requirements for downstream analyses. To mitigate potential biases from uneven sequencing coverage and heterozygosity on smaller and sex chromosomes, all autosomal SNPs (chr1–29) were used for population structure analyses, but SNP density plots and principal component analysis (PCA) included all chromosomes.

Genetic diversity analysis

PLINK v1.90 was used to estimate the MAF of the Yunnan Partridge duck population and to calculate individual heterozygosity. The output metrics included the observed number of homozygous loci (O(HOM)), expected number of homozygous loci (E(HOM)), number of non-missing sites (N(NM)), and the inbreeding coefficient (F). Ho and He were derived as follows:HO=1−O(HOM)N(NM)HE=1−E(HOM)N(NM)

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on genome-wide SNP data using GCTA to elucidate population genetic structure. The three predefined plumage phenotypes were used to categorize individuals, and the first two principal components were visualized in R.

Genetic distance analysis in populations

Quality-controlled genome-wide SNPs were used to compute IBS pairwise distances with PLINK v1.90, generating a genetic distance matrix that was subsequently clustered and visualized in R. A neighbor-joining (NJ) phylogenetic tree was constructed from this matrix and visualized using Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) v7.1.

ROH detection and inbreeding coefficient estimation

ROH for each individual were detected using PLINK (version 1.90) with the following parameters: a minimum SNP call rate of 0.9 and a MAF > 0.05 per site; Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium P-value > 1 × 10⁻⁶; a minimum of 50 SNPs per ROH (–homozyg-snp 50) within a sliding window of 50 SNPs (–homozyg-window-snp 50); a maximum of 5 missing SNPs (–homozyg-window-missing 5) and 3 heterozygous SNPs (–homozyg-window-het 3) allowed per sliding window; a minimum ROH length of 1000 kb (–homozyg-kb 1000); a minimum SNP density of 1 SNP per 50 kb (–homozyg-density 50); and a maximum gap of 1000 kb between consecutive SNPs (–homozyg-gap 1000). The proportion of SNPs included in the sliding windows was set to ≥0.05 (–homozyg-window-threshold 0.05). The resulting output files, including detailed information for each ROH segment and summary ROH statistics for each individual, were visualized in R as histograms of ROH length distribution, bar plots of chromosomal ROH distribution, and violin–box plots of inbreeding coefficients.

FROH was calculated using the formula proposed by McQuillan et al. (McQuillan et al., 2008):FROH=∑iLROHiLauto

Where ∑i LROHi is the total length of ROH on autosomes, Lauto is the total length of the autosomes, and FROH is the inbreeding coefficient calculated based on ROH.

Genome-wide association analysis

Association analysis was performed using EMMAX. The linear model for testing each SNP individually was:y=Xα+Zβ+Wμ+e

Where y is the vector of phenotypic observations; Xα denotes the fixed-effect term, including the top three PCA derived from genome-wide SNP data to correct for population stratification; Zβ represents the SNP effect with the allelic substitution effect β; and Wμ indicates the random animal effect, whose variance-covariance structure is defined by the genomic kinship matrix estimated from all SNPs. e is the vector of random residuals. Population structure and relatedness were incorporated as fixed effects to mitigate false positives and enhance statistical power. Significant associations passing the Bonferroni-corrected threshold are visualized using Manhattan and quantile–quantile (Q-Q) plots.

Gene annotation

Significant SNPs associated with plumage color were annotated using SnpEff (Cingolani et al., 2012) based on the duck reference genome ZJU1.0 (GCF_015476345.1). Within candidate regions, individual genotypes were extracted using VCFtools on a Linux system. Pairwise linkage disequilibrium (R²) and association P-values between each locus and the lead SNP were then calculated using PLINK. Locus Zoom plots were generated using the “spectralGP” package in R.

Linkage disequilibrium and haplotype analysis

To delineate the linkage architecture of plumage color-associated candidate regions, nine representative SNPs with R² > 0.6 were selected based on GWAS results. Haplotype blocks within the candidate region were constructed, and inter-SNP linkage disequilibrium was visualized using Haploview.

ROH-based genomic inbreeding estimates

A total of 3,474 runs of ROH were identified in the Yunnan Partridge duck population (n = 100), with an average of 34.74 ROH per individual. ROH length distribution, bar plots of chromosomal ROH distribution, and violin–box plots of FROH are presented in Fig. 2. The peak in Fig. 2A is concentrated within the 40–60 Mb interval, indicating that the majority of individuals have total ROH lengths within this range. The relatively low number of individuals in the higher ROH length range (80–160 Mb) suggests a low proportion of highly inbred individuals. The chromosomal ROH distribution (Fig. 2B) revealed that chromosome 1 exhibited the greatest total ROH length, substantially exceeding all other chromosomes, followed by chromosome 2. Among the 373 ducklings evaluated, 206 (55%) were spotted, 162 (44%) were gray, and 5 (1%) were yellow partridge (YP) (Fig. 3A). Plumage color in Yunnan Partridge ducks differed markedly between sexes by 40 days of age. Males exhibited black- or brown-partridge feathers on the breast and back, with gray-white or gray belly feathers, whereas females were predominantly yellow-partridge. Consequently, subsequent analyses of plumage development were conducted separately for each sex. In females, a pronounced darkening of plumage was observed across the three sampling time points from hatch to 100 days of age. At hatch, plumage was predominantly gray and spotted. By 40 days of age, most individuals displayed light brown-partridge coloration. At 100 days, plumage distribution became strongly concentrated in yellow-partridge and brown-partridge phenotypes. This transition pattern indicates a directional and largely irreversible darkening of plumage during this developmental period in females (Table 1). In males, plumage at hatch was also predominantly gray and spotted. By 40 days of age, most individuals exhibited a “dark” phenotype, characterized by largely black plumage with localized white areas. By 100 days, plumage phenotypes diversified, with dark and light individuals each representing roughly half of the population (Table 2). GWAS-based screening of plumage candidate genes

A chi-square test revealed a significant association between plumage color categories and sex (P < 0.01). Consequently, sex was included as a covariate in the subsequent GWAS to control for its potential effect on plumage color variation. A GWAS was conducted on two plumage color phenotype combinations (spotted vs. gray) in day-old Yunnan Partridge ducklings using whole-genome resequencing data. A significant association signal was identified a significant association signal on chr21 (Fig. 4C). Within this region, genome-wide significant SNPs (–log₁₀(P) > 8.84) spanned approximately 0.3 Mb (chr21: 2,334,307–2,634,307 bp), totaling 150 SNPs. The most significant SNP was located at chr21: 2,334,307 (–log₁₀(P) = 13.80). Conditional analysis was performed using the top SNP (chr21: 2,334,307) as a covariate, which eliminated the original signal peak (Fig. 4C), confirming that this single strong-effect SNP predominantly drove the plumage color association. Gene annotation was performed on SNPs that surpassed the Bonferroni-corrected threshold (Table 3). Fine localization of the plumage color‑associated locus

Given the strong association signal for plumage color, this locus was selected for further investigation. Fine-mapping was performed using LocusZoom analysis centered on the lead SNP (chr21: 2,334,307). The results revealed a cluster of highly linked SNPs within a 2.25–2.4 Mb interval on chromosome 21 (Fig. 5B), defining a candidate functional region for subsequent analysis.Fig. 5Association and localization of the major plumage color locus. A Manhattan plot of chromosome 21 (Fig. 5A) illustrated the distribution of association signals, confirming a single major peak near 2.33 Mb with no other significant peaks across the chromosome. Based on linkage disequilibrium (R² > 0.6), nine representative SNPs were selected within this region. The resulting LD heatmap (Fig. 5C) revealed a tightly linked haplotype block, indicating the presence of a key variant associated with plumage color in this interval.

LD analysis identified a haplotype block spanning chr21:2,334,307-2,337,146 bp, comprising five tightly linked SNPs (chr21:2,334,307, chr21:2,334,948, chr21:2,336,966, chr21:2,337,145, and chr21:2,337,146). Genotype-phenotype association statistics for these SNPs are provided (Supplementary Table S3). Based on their allelic combinations, 11 haplotypes (H1-H11) were defined (Supplementary Table S2). Genotypes within this region were extracted from whole-genome resequencing data using VCFtools. To examine haplotype-phenotype associations, the previously defined sub-categories of spotted (dark, light) and gray (light, medium, dark) were merged into two broad phenotypic groups: spotted and gray. Haplotype-based association analysis revealed that the distribution frequencies of the high-frequency haplotypes H1 and H2 differed (P < 0.001) between the spotted and gray groups, indicating a strong link between haplotype variation in this region and plumage color (Supplementary Table S3).

Discussion

Diverse local livestock and poultry breeds are a crucial genetic resource for breeding, biodiversity conservation, and sustainable poultry production. The Yunnan Partridge duck, an indigenous breed from Yunnan Province, holds significant value for conservation and utilization. In this study, we assessed its genetic structure by evaluating genetic diversity and calculating observed (Ho) and expected (He) heterozygosity within the conservation population. Here, He represents the expected proportion of heterozygous SNPs across the population, whereas Ho reflects the actual proportion of heterozygous genotypes observed in the sampled individuals (Silió et al., 2013). A lower Ho than He heterozygosity suggests possible inbreeding, whereas a higher Ho than He may indicate introgression from other breeds. In this study, the mean He exceeded the mean Ho in the Yunnan Partridge duck conservation population, implying the presence of inbred individuals. Therefore, mating strategies should be optimized to limit further increases in inbreeding.

Most conservation programs for livestock and poultry in China employ closed breeding systems. Consequently, evaluating parameters such as genetic distance, relatedness, and inbreeding coefficients within these populations is critical for their sustainable management (Li et al., 2004). Most individuals in the Yunnan Partridge duck population were distantly related, as indicated by IBS distances, with only a few pairs showing close kinship. The length and distribution of ROH reflect individual inbreeding levels and can be used to infer recent inbreeding history within the population (Browning and Browning, 2012; Keller et al., 2011). Short ROH segments predominated in the Yunnan Partridge duck conservation population, suggesting that recent detrimental inbreeding has been minimal (Zhang et al., 2015). The mean FROH was 0.05493 in the Yunnan Partridge duck conservation population, indicating a low level of recent inbreeding and effective population management. This result indicates that the current conservation population has maintained relatively intact genetic diversity, and the risk of inbreeding is manageable. In practice, the existing population structure can be maintained, and the introduction of external bloodlines should be avoided to preserve genetic uniqueness. For targeted breeding, prioritizing matings between less closely related individuals can help retain favorable genotypes and enhance breeding efficiency.

GWAS of the two plumage color phenotypes identified four protein-coding genes on chromosome 21 associated with plumage color: ASIP, AHCY, RBPJL, and LOC110352121. AHCY is essential for cellular methylation and metabolic homeostasis (Boczki et al., 2024; Vizán et al., 2021). Its deficiency causes embryonic lethality in multiple species and is linked to rare metabolic disorders such as hypermethioninemia. AHCY has also been proposed as a potential therapeutic target in cancer and viral infections. In acute pancreatitis, RBPJL downregulates Arid5a expression by binding to its promoter, thereby inhibiting IL-6/STAT3 signaling and attenuating inflammatory cytokine secretion and pancreatic damage (Lv et al., 2022). ASIP is the most compelling candidate for regulating plumage color. ASIP encodes a paracrine signaling factor secreted by dermal papilla cells that acts locally within the hair follicle to regulate eumelanin synthesis spatiotemporally. Specifically, ASIP expression promotes pheomelanin production, and its absence favors eumelanin synthesis, thereby switching pigment type. Before being identified as a direct ligand of MC1R, ASIP was shown to influence ectodermal patterning in chick embryos. In the feather follicle, it functions as a paracrine signal regulating plumage color (Millar et al., 1995). Thus, ASIP plays a critical role in melanocyte differentiation.

Many research studies support the role of ASIP in avian plumage color. To date, all known ASIP variants associated with avian plumage color are SNPs. For example, eight SNPs in intron 1, intron 2, and the 3′ UTR of ASIP were linked to plumage color in Korean native and white-feathered ducks (Sultana et al., 2018). A novel mutation (c.1826A>T) identified in the ASIP promoter region may influence chicken skin color by modulating the gene's transcriptional activity (Yu et al., 2019). In Japanese quail, plumage color variation has been associated with major structural variants in the ASIP genomic region. A deletion exceeding 90 kb upstream of ASIP, which removes nearly the complete coding sequences of RALY and EIF2, was identified as the causal mutation in pure yellow quail. The expression of ASIP is, in turn, regulated by the promoter of RALY (Nadeau et al., 2008). In 2019, two novel structural mutations at the 5′ end of ASIP were identified in Japanese quail and associated with lighter plumage color: a 141,162 bp deletion in yellow quail, and a 71 kb tandem duplication in light brown quail, which includes an intact ASIP copy and an additional copy within an itch-ASIP fusion gene. (Robic et al., 2019). Functional studies in Japanese quail embryos, via vector-mediated overexpression, indicate that black plumage is likely caused by enhanced MC1R expression, and white plumage correlates with increased ASIP expression (Li et al., 2019). In 2022, transcriptome sequencing followed by qPCR validation revealed significant upregulation of ASIP and DDC in white feathered quail embryos. This finding offers a molecular explanation for the plumage color divergence between white and brown variants (Hu et al., 2022). Regarding coat and plumage color in other species, ASIP is a well-established candidate gene. Polymorphisms such as SNPs, insertions, and deletions in this gene contribute to phenotypic diversity. For example, a study of 167 guinea pigs linked a 4- bp deletion in exon 3 of ASIP (c.181_184delTTCA) to a premature stop codon. This deletion genotype showed complete association with the black coat phenotype and followed an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern (Lai et al., 2019).

This study initiated with an investigation of the genetic structure of local resources, followed by genetic analyses of specific traits, thus offering a referable analytical pipeline for the evaluation and utilization of genetic resources across various regions. The Yunnan Partridge duck harbors a high abundance of ancestral alleles, which may provide important genetic clues regarding gene exchange among duck breeds worldwide and their migratory histories. These findings enhance our understanding of duck domestication and dispersal, while also providing a reference for the systematic evaluation of global domestic duck genetic resources.

Conclusion

Yunnan Partridge Duck has a moderate level of genetic diversity and a low degree of inbreeding, indicating that the current conservation measures are effective. The ASIP gene on chromosome 21 has been identified as a key candidate gene for distinguishing between the spotted and gray feathers of Yunnan Partridge Duck. In addition, haplotypes H1 and H2 can serve as molecular markers for feather color selection. Our findings provide novel insights into the genetic basis of plumage color in the Yunnan Partridge duck. The specific molecular pathways by which ASIP genes regulate plumage color can be further resolved in the future.
