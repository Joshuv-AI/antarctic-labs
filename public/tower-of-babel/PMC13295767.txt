Loneliness and social isolation at the emergency department: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Will Lung Chan, Zesen Gao, Arthur Liu Chuxi, Timothy Rainer, Wilson Wing Chung Tang, Yu Serena Sheung Wing, Zohar Lederman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction Loneliness and social isolation are critical public health issues linked to significant adverse health outcomes and increased healthcare utilisation, including visits to the emergency department (ED). The ED often serves as a primary societal safety net, providing care for vulnerable populations who may be disproportionately affected by these conditions. In fact, loneliness and social isolation might be the underlying reasons they presented to the ED in the first place either consciously or not. For such individuals, the ED encounter may represent a rare point of human contact, yet the stressful and depersonalising nature of the ED environment may paradoxically exacerbate their sense of isolation. Furthermore, ED staff may lack the training and awareness of the scope of the problem to properly screen for loneliness and address it. Yet, the compounded impact of the ED experience on lonely or socially isolated patients and the relationship between loneliness and healthcare utilisation remains poorly understood. This paper presents a protocol for a scoping review designed to systematically map the existing evidence on the experiences of these patients and the perspectives of the clinicians who care for them.Methods and analysisThis scoping review will be conducted following the Arksey and O’Malley methodological framework in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). The findings will be reported according to the Extension to the PRISMA Statement for Reporting Literature Searches in Systematic Reviews (PRISMA-S; see online supplemental file 1). A comprehensive search will be performed across five electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, CNKI) and grey literature sources. Studies published in English or Chinese that address loneliness or social isolation in the ED context, from the perspective of adult or paediatric patients or clinical staff, will be included. A novel, artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted screening process will be utilised for initial relevance assessment, followed by full manual screening and data extraction by two independent reviewers to ensure rigour and mitigate bias. Findings will be synthesised using a narrative approach and thematic analysis to identify key concepts, themes and existing gaps in the literature.Ethics and disseminationAs this study synthesises data from previously published literature, it does not require formal ethical approval. The findings will be disseminated through a manuscript submitted to a peer-reviewed, open-access journal. The aim is to provide an evidence-based roadmap to guide future research, inform policy and support the development of interventions designed to improve care and outcomes for this vulnerable population within the acute care setting.Registration numberThe review has been registered at Open Science Framework, DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/MBVSR.

Introduction

Loneliness, the subjective distress of lacking expected meaningful social connections, is distinct from social isolation, the objective state of having few social ties or infrequent social contact.1 2 Although conceptually different, these phenomena are closely inter-related and are independently associated with a range of serious adverse health outcomes.26 For instance, a 2020 global survey indicated that 24% of the population reported feeling ‘very lonely’ or ‘fairly lonely’.1 While loneliness is often perceived as a problem for older adults, a growing body of evidence highlights that adolescents and young adults represent a particularly vulnerable group, placing them at heightened risk for a lifetime of compounded health consequences.1 2 Loneliness and social isolation are associated with a 29% increased risk of heart disease and a 32% increased risk of stroke.3 Furthermore, these conditions are linked to an elevated risk for depression, anxiety, dementia, suicidality and premature mortality.4610 The cumulative burden of these adverse health outcomes inevitably results in increased healthcare utilisation and carries substantial economic implications.11

The emergency department (ED) serves a unique and essential role at the intersection of acute medical care and public health, often functioning as a primary societal safety net. EDs are mandated to treat all individuals regardless of their insurance status, ability to pay or social circumstances. Consequently, they disproportionately care for society’s most marginalised and vulnerable populations.12 13 Although the evidence linking loneliness and social isolation to general healthcare utilisation is mixed, a clear association has been established with increased hospital readmissions and longer lengths of stay.14 This association, which also correlates significantly with more frequent ED visits, highlights a particular vulnerability among patients who rely on the acute care system.15 16 For these individuals, the ED may function not only as a site for urgent medical treatment but also as a primary, rare or sole point of human connection. As such, the ED represents a critical setting for identifying and potentially mitigating the adverse health effects of loneliness and social isolation.

Rationale

Despite the clear public health significance of loneliness and social isolation, and the ED’s established role as a social safety net, research exploring their direct intersection remains fragmented, underdeveloped and lacking systematic synthesis. Multiple related streams of inquiry have emerged independently, but they have yet to be integrated into a cohesive understanding of the phenomena.

A critical knowledge gap emerges at this intersection. Lonely or socially isolated individuals use the ED more frequently than individuals who are not lonely or socially isolated.15 16 At the same time, the ED environment is known to be stressful for patients who are both unwell and confused about the work routine, and clinicians working in EDs are rarely trained to screen for loneliness or social isolation. Clinicians working in EDs also rarely perceive loneliness and social isolation to fall within their remit of care.17 As far as we are aware, there is no clinical pathway or tool to help these clinicians to screen for and manage loneliness and social isolation. There has largely been little systematic effort to understand the compounded negative impact that potentially occurs when a lonely or socially isolated individual encounters the depersonalising and high-stress ED setting or alternatively the optimal ways ED staff should respond to patients who are potentially lonely or socially isolated. The former experience is likely not merely additive but rather a synergistic interaction that could significantly exacerbate a patient’s distress, anxiety and sense of isolation during a moment of acute medical and emotional need.17 18 The latter means that patients do not receive the ideal treatment they should receive at the ED. These compounded negative experiences could have significant downstream consequences, potentially leading to poorer health outcomes, reduced understanding of or adherence to discharge instructions and a heightened reluctance to seek necessary care in the future, thereby creating a dangerous cycle of unmet needs and escalating health crises. Alternatively, inattention to loneliness and social isolation that act as triggers for ED visits might cause an increase in these visits while failing to address the patients’ actual needs.

Furthermore, the formal perspectives of ED staff regarding these social presentations have not been systematically captured. Understanding how ED staff perceive their ability to identify and manage such patients, the challenges they face, their views on the feasibility of ED-based interventions and the systemic support needed remain a critical but largely unexplored area. A scoping review is, therefore, urgently needed to systematically map the existing body of research. This review will consolidate knowledge on both patient experiences and clinician perspectives to identify key knowledge gaps that must be addressed to inform the development of evidence-based strategies for improving care for this highly vulnerable population. It will specifically lead to the creation (and further validation) of a clinical screening and decision tool to screen and manage loneliness and social isolation at the ED.

Objectives

The objective of this paper is to present a protocol for an AI-facilitated scoping review that maps the existing evidence on the reasons for which lonely or socially isolated individuals are more likely to visit the ED or to repeat visits to the ED, the impact of the ED on patients experiencing loneliness and social isolation as well as the perspectives of ED clinical staff and patients on the ED’s role in managing these patients. The review aims to identify key concepts, prevalent themes and critical gaps in the current knowledge base to guide future research directions.

Methods

This protocol has been developed based on the methodological framework established by Arksey and O’Malley and will be reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines.19 20 The methodology is augmented by a model of an Artificial Intelligence (AI)-Facilitated Triage & Validate: two human reviewers screen abstracts flagged by the AI as ‘Yes’ or ‘Maybe’ and then randomly check 20% of the ‘No’ pile. The study is planned to take place between January 2026 and December 2026. The literature search is ongoing, commencing in January 2026.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Identifying relevant studies

The review has been registered at Open Science Framework, DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/MBVSR. A comprehensive and systematic search strategy has been developed in close collaboration with a university medical librarian (table 2). Two investigators, YSSW and ALC, will independently conduct the search across five major electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science and CNKI), retrieving all records from database inception to 5 March 2025. The strategy will combine the key concepts of ‘loneliness’, ‘social isolation’, ‘emergency department’, ‘adult’ and ‘paediatrics’ using both entry terms and Medical Subject Headings, adapted to the search requirements of each database (online supplemental table S1). Eligibility criteria

As a scoping review, our goal is to cast a wide net while maintaining scientific and academic rigour. For this reason, in addition to including randomised controlled trials and reviews, we will also include published, peer-reviewed or non-peer-reviewed, solicited or non-solicited opinion pieces. Also, for this reason—and since some of the authors are native Chinese speakers—we will include publications in Chinese. Similarly, since we aim to explore the experiences of healthcare providers and patients, we will include qualitative and mixed-methods studies.

Studies, then, will be deemed eligible for inclusion if they meet the following criteria: (1) empirical studies, randomised controlled trials, systematic reviews, meta-analyses and opinion articles; (2) focus on the concepts of loneliness or social isolation within the context of the ED; (3) include either adult or paediatric patient populations or the perspectives of clinicians and (4) are published in either English or Chinese; (5) are qualitative, quantitative or mixed-method studies

Studies will be excluded if they are not relevant to the ED context, do not explicitly discuss loneliness or social isolation or are in a language other than English or Chinese.

Loneliness is most commonly assessed using the Revised University of California Los Angeles (R-UCLA) Loneliness Scale in its various modifications and the De Jong Gierveld Six-item Loneliness Scale. It is either defined as a continuous variable or a categorical variable, defined as >43 in the UCLA, and >1 in the De Jong Gierveld.

Social isolation is most commonly assessed using the Lubben Social Network Scale, Berkman–Syme Social Network Index or the Duke Social Support Index.21

Study selection

All citations identified through the search strategy will be imported into EndNote citation management software, where duplicates will be removed. The study selection will then proceed through a systematic, multistage screening process.

AI-assisted screening

This study will use AI to assist with the screening process. All retrieved titles, abstracts and subsequently full-text articles will be uploaded to a secure, sandboxed environment for initial relevance assessment using a large language model (LLM). Initial relevance assessment will be conducted using a custom-developed AI screening tool leveraging the Microsoft Azure OpenAI Service API, specifically employing the GPT-4.1 model. The model will be guided by a structured prompt that has been codeveloped with the university’s medical librarian (see table 3).

AI, artificial intelligence.

For title and abstract screening, the AI will classify each record as ‘Yes’, ‘No’ or ‘Maybe’. For full-text screening, the output will be ‘Yes’ or ‘No’. Crucially, to assist the research team, all AI decisions will be accompanied by short, bullet-point explanations detailing the specific rationales for its classification based on the prompt. Rather than replacing human judgement, this AI-assisted approach functions as an advanced triage and decision-support system. By preprocessing the literature, prioritising highly relevant records, and extracting key rationales, the AI is expected to significantly reduce the cognitive load and evaluation time per article for the human reviewers. Furthermore, this approach provides a novel opportunity to prospectively evaluate the concordance between LLM-generated decisions and human consensus in the context of a scoping review.

Human validation and full screening

We will employ a novel, AI-facilitated Triage & Validate model, whereby two independent human reviewers, ZL and YSSW, will screen abstracts flagged by the AI as ‘Yes’ or ‘Maybe’, and randomly check 10%–20% of the ‘No’ pile. This approach is intended to optimise reliability and effectiveness while mitigating known limitations and risks associated with the use of AI in evidence synthesis, including algorithmic bias, lack of contextual understanding and the potential for ‘hallucinated’ or inaccurate interpretations.22 23 Any disagreements between the two reviewers will be resolved through discussion and consensus, and if consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer, TR, will make the final decision. The specific reasons for excluding studies at the full-text stage will be documented.

Charting the data

A standardised data extraction form will be developed to systematically extract relevant information from each included study. One reviewer will independently extract the data, and a second reviewer will verify its accuracy. Any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer. Collating, summarising and reporting the results

The literature selection process will be documented using a PRISMA flow diagram.24 The analytical approach will be finalised after data charting is completed for all included studies. We anticipate employing a narrative synthesis to construct a coherent account of the findings. This will involve summarising the evidence textually and integrating the reported impacts of ED visits on lonely and socially isolated patients with the documented perspectives of clinical staff. The aim is to highlight connections, consistencies and contradictions across the literature.

To enhance the depth of this synthesis, a thematic analysis is planned to systematically identify, analyse and report patterns across the dataset.25 This qualitative process will begin with familiarisation, during which the review team will repeatedly read the charted data to become immersed in the findings. Two reviewers will then independently generate initial codes by labelling relevant data segments. These codes will be grouped into potential themes that reflect key concepts related to patient experiences and clinician perspectives. The full team will subsequently review these themes to ensure they are coherent, distinct and representative of the data, refining them through merging, splitting or redefining as needed. Once finalised, each major theme and its associated subthemes will be clearly named and thoroughly defined using the software Elicit.

The results of the scoping review will be presented through a combination of tables, charts and a detailed narrative summary organised around the identified themes. The synthesis will conclude with a discussion that explicitly maps existing knowledge gaps and provides an evidence-based foundation for future research.

Ethics and dissemination

This protocol outlines a scoping review that will synthesise data from published literature. As this study does not involve collecting primary data from human subjects, formal ethical approval was not required. By synthesising evidence on the impact of the ED environment on patients experiencing loneliness and social isolation, alongside the perspectives of clinicians, this review aims to generate a comprehensive overview of the current evidence base. The findings will be disseminated through a manuscript submitted to a peer-reviewed, open-access journal to ensure wide accessibility for the global research community. This synthesis will identify critical knowledge gaps and thereby provide an evidence-based roadmap for future research focused on developing and evaluating interventions to improve outcomes for this population in the demanding ED setting.
