Deciphering the genetic basis of yield components in wheat by integrating hyperspectral-based phenomes
Authors: Sudip Kunwar, Md Ali Babar, Yiannis Ampatzidis, Naeem Khan, Janam Prabhat Acharya, Jordan McBreen, Samuel Adewale, Dipendra Shahi, Gina Brown-Guedira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have advanced crop genetics by the detection of loci controlling complex traits; however, their power is often constrained by the quality and the throughput of phenotypic data. In this study, we integrated hyperspectral and genomics data to investigate the genetic architecture of spectral signatures associated with yield components in wheat. A diverse panel of 341 soft wheat lines was evaluated over three years, and hyperspectral data were collected using a UAV-mounted sensor. Among 273 spectral bands, those most strongly correlated with grain yield (GY), thousand-grain weight (TGW), and grains per unit area (GN) were selected. Principal component analysis was used for dimensionality reduction, and the first principal component (PC1), here defined as the hyperspectral phenome, accounted for 78.9%–97.1% of overall variance. The GWAS using both manual phenotypes and hyperspectral phenomes identified 31 significant marker-trait associations (MTAs), including several pleiotropic loci shared across traits and data types. A notable SNP on chromosome 1A, associated with all three hyperspectral phenomes, was located within a gene specifying a chlorophyll a–b binding protein, a key component of photosynthesis and stress response. Additional MTAs were linked to genes involved in cytochrome P450 metabolism and LRR proteins, highlighting their roles in yield and environmental response. Overall, this study shows that hyperspectral imaging serves as a valuable, high-throughput secondary correlated trait for uncovering novel loci and dissecting the genetic basis of complex yield traits in wheat.

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is a dominant cereal crop on a global scale, supplying approximately one-fifth of human calorie consumption and serving as a primary food source for billions [1]. Despite notable genetic improvements in elite spring wheat lines [2], the overall annual increase in wheat yield remains below 1% [3] and is unlikely to meet the higher demand expected by 2050 [4]. To close this gap, wheat breeding programs must better understand the genetics of grain yield (GY) and the associated major secondary traits. Among the traits, grains per unit area (GN) is among the strongest drivers of yield differences [[5], [6], [7]]. The GN has played a major role in past yield improvements and remains a key target for increasing productivity, especially in yield-limiting environments [8]. Understanding the genetic architecture governing GN is therefore essential to advance breeding of high-yielding wheat cultivars.

Thousand-grain weight (TGW) is another major determinant of GY and reflects the average individual grain weight. Together, GN and TGW usually define most of the variation in GY, making them central traits for breeders. Although both traits are essential, numerous studies have reported a trade-off between GN and TGW, particularly in high-yielding germplasm. For instance, negative correlations between these two traits have been observed in CIMMYT-developed elite breeding lines [9,10], suggesting that enhancement of one trait can compromise the other. Improving wheat yield will require strategies that balance and enhance both GN and TGW at the same time. Large-scale genetic studies can help identify the genomic regions that control these traits, offering valuable markers for selecting lines that combine high grain number with higher per grain weight. Such progress is essential for boosting wheat productivity and ensuring stable food supplies in the future.

Genome-wide association study (GWAS) provides a robust approach to elucidate genetically complex traits by leveraging past recombination events and linkage disequilibrium [11]. In contrast to traditional bi-parental QTL mapping, GWAS utilizes natural variation in diverse germplasm to achieve higher mapping resolution and uncover favorable alleles often undetected by conventional breeding [12]. GWAS has been extensively utilized in wheat recently to elucidate the genetic architecture of key quantitative traits, including GY, biotic and abiotic stress resistance, and grain quality attributes [13,14]. Genotyping technologies have advanced rapidly with the emergence of next-generation sequencing technologies, including genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS) and high-density SNP arrays, enabling the rapid, cost-effective, and genome-wide discovery of molecular markers for high-resolution genetic analysis.

Despite the advances in sequencing technologies that now provide extensive access to high-density genomic markers, large-scale, accurate phenotyping of complex traits remains the most critical bottleneck constraining the resolution and statistical power of GWAS in breeding programs [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. Conventional field phenotyping of yield components, GY, GN, and TGW, relies on labor-intensive, destructive, and the manual measurements that are inherently low-throughput and prone to human subjectivity, severely restricting the population sizes and multi-environment replication achievable in association studies [15,19]. Yield components in wheat are quantitative complex traits governed by many quantitative trail loci (QTLs) necessitating extensive studies to uncover the genomic regions [14,20]. Recent GWAS for these traits in bread wheat have largely been conducted using manually collected data from limited genotypes and single-environment, potentially diminishing the detection of small-effect loci underlying the complex, polygenic architectures of yield components.

High-throughput phenotyping (HTP) has evolved into a prominent tool to overcome the long-standing phenotyping bottleneck in crop genetics and breeding. HTP technologies enable non-destructive, rapid assessment of critical traits in both controlled and field environments through diverse sensor types, including RGB, thermal, multispectral, and hyperspectral cameras, deployed across ground-based (handheld or vehicle-mounted), aerial (Unmanned Aerial Vehicle; UAV or manned aircraft), and satellite platforms [[21], [22], [23]]. These technologies have been widely adopted in wheat breeding programs to quantify key agronomic traits such as yield potential [24,25], plant height [[26], [27], [28]], biomass-partitioning traits [29], and abiotic or biotic stress responses [30,31]. More recently, the integration of HTP-derived phenotypes with GWAS has gained momentum as a strategy to enhance the genetic dissection of complex traits and improve the biological relevance of marker–trait associations [[32], [33], [34], [35]].

Despite rapid progress in UAV-based GWAS studies, several interconnected limitations persist in the field that constrain the genetic dissection of complex traits. Most GWAS studies continue to rely on a narrow set of pre-defined vegetation indices derived from two or few spectral bands, such as NDVI and NDRE, leaving much of the available spectral information unexploited in genetic mapping analyses [36,37]. Similarly, efforts to improve genetic signal detection through UAV-predicted traits have mostly remained confined to a small number of multispectral bands [38]. Although hyperspectral imaging offers substantially higher spectral resolution, its use in wheat GWAS remains limited in both scope and analytical approach. Existing studies have either focused on a single trait like GY while excluding other major yield components such as GN and TGW [39], relied on model-predicted secondary traits rather than direct reflectance measurements as GWAS inputs [40], or been conducted under controlled laboratory conditions rather than large-scale field settings [32]. Collectively, these limitations highlight the need for frameworks that leverage trait-relevant spectral information from UAV-based hyperspectral data as direct phenotypic inputs in field-based GWAS.

In this study, we combined UAV-based hyperspectral reflectance data with genomics to investigate the genetic basis of spectral signatures associated with wheat yield components under field conditions. To our knowledge, no previous work has systematically compared the genetic architecture revealed by manually measured yield components with that derived from UAV-based hyperspectral band-level proxy phenotypes. Our specific objectives were to: (i) identify hyperspectral reflectance bands significantly associated with key yield components; (ii) evaluate the use of these trait-specific spectral profiles as complementary proxy phenotypes in GWAS; and (iii) compare the genetic architecture revealed through GWAS of manually measured traits with that obtained using their corresponding hyperspectral proxies. This integrative framework aims to enhance the knowledge of the genetic basis of spectral–phenotypic relationships and to assess the potential of hyperspectral phenotyping as a scalable, high-throughput tool to support genetic studies of complex traits in wheat breeding programs.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

In total, 341 soft red winter wheat facultative genotypes, consisting of advanced elite lines developed by the SunGrains™ consortium were evaluated in this study. The SunGrains™ consortium includes small grains breeding programs in the southern United States. Over three consecutive growing seasons (2022–2024), field trials were set up at the Plant Science Research and Education Unit (PSREU) in Citra, Florida, USA (29°24′20.02″ N, 82°10′10.02″ W). Experiments were conducted using an incomplete block alpha-lattice design, with two replicates per genotype. The study design consisted of 8, 23, and 28 incomplete blocks per replication in 2022, 2023, and 2024, respectively, with block sizes of 20, 15, and 11 genotypes. A uniform seeding rate of 100 kg ha−1 was applied, and plots consisted of seven rows, each measuring 3.70 m in length and 1.77 m in width. Field operations, including planting, irrigation, fertilization, pesticide application, and harvesting, were conducted with support from the PSREU technical team.

Phenotypic traits

The number of days from planting until 50% of plants in each plot reached flowering was recorded as days to anthesis (DTA), based on the Zadoks growth scale [41]. All rows in each plot were harvested using a plot combine harvester, and the total grain weight was recorded and adjusted to 13% moisture content to calculate GY in g/m2. The TGW was measured by counting thousand kernels using a Seedburo 801 seed counter (AgPoint Precision LLC, Plainfield, IL, USA) and weighing the sample. The GN was estimated by dividing the total grain yield by the average grain weight, as determined from the TGW measurement.

Hyperspectral data acquisition

Hyperspectral images were captured using a Pika L 2.4 hyperspectral sensor (Resonon, Bozeman, MT, USA) mounted on a Matrice 600 Pro hexacopter (DJI, Shenzhen, China). Equipped with a 17 mm focal length lens, the sensor provided a 17.6° FOV and an IFOV of 0.71 milliradians. A single hyperspectral flight was conducted at the grain filling stage (GS70) at around solar noon. Flight operations were managed using the Pix4D Capture application (Pix4D S.A., Prilly, Switzerland).

A light-gray calibration target was set up within the plots to facilitate radiometric correction. The UAV flights were conducted at 30 m altitude, with a flight speed of approximately 1.5 m/s and configured with 85% front and 70% side overlap. Hyperspectral data were processed using Spectronon Pro software for both radiometric calibration and georectification. Plot-level spectral signatures were obtained by manually selecting regions of interest (ROIs), resulting in reflectance values across 273 spectral bands spanning wavelengths from 398.47 to 1000.58 nm. Statistical analysis

Genetic parameters were estimated for both phenotypic data and hyperspectral reflectance data using the “lme4” and “lmerTest” packages [42] in R statistical software version 4.2.2 [43]. First, data were analyzed separately for individual year to identify year-specific correlated spectral bands and to conduct GWAS within individual datasets. Best Linear Unbiased Estimates (BLUEs) of genotypic effects were obtained by fitting a linear mixed model that accounted for days to anthesis (DTA) as a fixed covariate and, experimental design factors as random effects, as specified below:(1)yijkl=μ+gi+β.DTAi+Bj+Rk(j)+Cl(j)+eijklIn this model, yijkl represents the observed value of the phenotypic trait or hyperspectral reflectance in the given band. The term μ is the overall mean, gi is the fixed effect of the genotype (where, i = 1 to 341), and β.DTAi is the covariate effect of days to anthesis (DTA).The term Bj indicates the random effect of the complete block (replicate), Rk(j) indicates the random effects of row nested within replicate, Cl(j) indicates the random effects of column nested within replicate. The residual term eijkl captures unexplained variation not accounted for by the other model components. Adjusted genotypic means (BLUEs), defined as ȳi=μˆ+ĝi where ĝi is the fixed genotype effect solution estimated after accounting for the effect of DTA and experimental design, were obtained using the “emmeans” package in R [44]. These adjusted means were calculated for all yield components and reflectance data in each band of hyperspectral imaging and used in all subsequent analyses including correlation calculations and GWAS. Adjusted means across all years were computed by the combined analysis incorporating year and genotype-by-year interactions as shown in the model below:(2)yijklm=μ+gi+β.DTAi+Em+(gE)im+Bj(m)+Rk(mj)+Cl(mj)+eijklmWhere Em is the effect of the mth year (m = 1 to 3), (gE)im is the effect for the interaction between the ith genotype and the mth year. The other terms denote the same as defined in the above model. For analysis of variance (ANOVA), and broad-sense heritability (H2) calculations, DTA was removed from equation (2). Broad-sense heritability (H2) was calculated by considering all components random and using equation (3) as below:(3)H2=σG2(σG2+σG×E2n+σe2nr)×100%Where σG2 , σG×E2 , and σe2 are genotype, genotype-by-year interaction, and error variances, respectively. Likewise, n is the number of years, and r is the number of replications.

Hyperspectral phenome associated with yield components

Trait-specific hyperspectral datasets were developed by identifying the most informative spectral bands for each yield component—GY, GN, and TGW. For each trait, the 50 spectral bands with the highest absolute Pearson correlation coefficients between adjusted reflectance values and manually assessed phenotypic measurements were selected. The band selection was performed independently for each year's dataset as well as for the combined three-year dataset.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on these 50-band subsets for each trait using “FactoMineR” package in R [45]. The first principal component (PC1) from each PCA, which captured the majority of variation within the selected bands, was extracted and designated as the hyperspectral phenome for that specific trait. These PC1-derived values were then used as alternative phenotypic inputs in GWAS, enabling the integration of spectrally informed phenotyping into genetic mapping analyses (Fig. 2). SNP genotyping, LD analysis, and population structure

Genotyping was done using high-quality genomic DNA isolated from leaves of two-week-old wheat seedlings. DNA extraction followed protocols using the sbeadex™ plant maxi kit on the oKtopure™ automated system, as previously described [24,46]. The GBS libraries were prepared using the PstI and MspI restriction enzymes according to the protocol by Poland et al. [47], and subsequently sequenced on a Novaseq 6000 SP flowcell (Illumina Inc.). The SNPs were called using TASSEL-GBSv2 pipeline version 5.2.35 [48]. During initial quality control, markers exhibiting more than 80% missing data, MAF <5%, or heterozygosity >10% were removed from the dataset. Following marker filtering, a total of 37,394 SNPs remained for downstream analysis. Beagle software (version 5.2) was used to impute missing genotype information [49].

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) was calculated for each sub-genome (A, B, D) and for whole genome using TASSEL v5.0 to calculate pairwise r2 values among SNPs [50]. Genome-wide LD decay was assessed by plotting pairwise r2 against physical distance (Mbp), with an r2 threshold of 0.1 used to estimate decay, following approaches from previous studies [14,51]. Population structure was inferred using DAPC in the R package “adegenet” [52], with the optimal number of clusters identified via the Bayesian Information Criterion.

Genome-wide association study (GWAS)

Two parallel GWAS approaches were employed in this study. In the first approach, best linear unbiased estimates (BLUEs) of manually measured phenotypic traits were used as phenotypic inputs. In the second approach, trait-specific hyperspectral phenome values derived from the BLUEs of spectral reflectance data were used as alternative phenotypes. The general workflow of GWAS analysis is presented in the diagram below (Fig. 2).

GWAS analyses were conducted independently for each trial year and also using a combined dataset across all years. The Bayesian Information and Linkage Disequilibrium Iteratively Nested Keyway (BLINK) model [53], implemented in GAPIT v3 [54], was used for the analysis. To account for population structure, the GWAS models incorporated three principal components. Significant marker–trait associations were determined using an adjusted Bonferroni correction at a 0.05 significance level.

Visualization of SNP distribution, Manhattan plots, and Q-Q plots was performed using the “CMplot” package in R. Genomic inflation factors (λ) were estimated from the distribution of marker-wise test statistics as the ratio of the observed median χ2 statistic to the expected median under the null hypothesis of no association. Although the BLINK model accounts for population structure and relatedness through iterative marker selection, λ was used to further evaluate any residual inflation in the test statistics. Candidate gene identification was conducted by aligning significant SNPs to the Triticum aestivum genome using the International Wheat Genome Consortium (IWGSC) RefSeq v2.1 reference genome assembly. Genes located within or near (250 kb upstream and downstream) significant SNPs were annotated using the Ensembl Plants Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) tool(http://plants.ensembl.org/Triticum_aestivum/Tools/VEP), and putative functions were further characterized based on existing literature. Haplotype block surrounding key loci was identified using solid spine method in Haploview v4.2 [55]. SNPs within 250 kbp of target SNP were extracted for LD analysis, LD block was defined using default parameters. Haplotypes were constructed by concatenating alleles of SNPs within LD block. Haplotypes represented by fewer than five genotypes were excluded. Haplotype-trait associations were evaluated using a linear model where haplotype was included as fixed effect and population structure was controlled by including the first three principal components derived from genome-wide markers.

Results

Weather variation across growing seasons

Despite being conducted at the same location (Citra, FL), the three growing seasons differed considerably in meteorological conditions (Fig. 3). The 2021–22 season was the coldest, with weekly minimum air temperatures dropping to −4.0°C in late January and multiple weeks of near-saturating relative humidity (≥90%), creating conditions favorable for foliar disease pressure. The 2022–23 season experienced an anomalous near-zero solar radiation week in early March, potentially disrupting canopy photosynthesis during grain fill. The 2023–24 season was the warmest and wettest, receiving approximately 70 mm of rainfall within two weeks in January and recording the highest late-season air temperatures (>26°C by early May). Cumulative growing-season rainfall also differed across years, with 2023–24 receiving the greatest total precipitation. Yield-related traits

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed significant effects of genotype and genotype-by-year interactions for all three phenotypic traits evaluated, whereas the main effect of year was not statistically significant (Table S1). Broad-sense heritability estimates (H2) based on combined data across all three years indicated moderate to high genetic control: GN showed H2 = 0.36, GY had H2 = 0.60, and TGW exhibited the highest heritability at H2 = 0.78.

Substantial phenotypic variation was observed among genotypes across all datasets (Fig. 4). In 2022, best linear unbiased estimates (BLUEs) ranged from 114.9 to 697.0 g/m2 for GY (mean = 479.3 g/m2), 6700 to 35,700 grains/m2 for GN (mean = 19,300 grains/m2), and 14.9 to 35.3 g for TGW (mean = 24.8 g). Similar trends were observed in 2023, with GY ranging from 136.7 to 657.1 g/m2 (mean = 405.8 g/m2), GN from 8000 to 27,400 grains/m2 (mean = 17,400 grains/m2), and TGW from 14.9 to 34.2 g (mean = 23.1 g). In 2024, GY values ranged from 222.5 to 666.2 g/m2 (mean = 438.0 g/m2), GN from 8100 to 22,300 grains/m2 (mean = 14,300 grains/m2), and TGW from 20.3 to 45.5 g (mean = 30.7 g). Across the combined dataset, GY ranged from 114.9 to 697.0 g/m2 (mean = 433.7 g/m2), GN from 6670.1 to 35,687.9 grains/m2 (mean = 16,536.9 grains/m2), and TGW from 14.9 to 45.5 g (mean = 26.5 g).Fig. 4Comparison of phenotypic trait performance using adjusted means (BLUEs) across datasets.

Genomic markers data, LD decay, and population structure

After initial quality control, 37,394 SNPs were used for downstream analyses. Marker distribution across the wheat genomes was uneven, with 13,404 markers (35.8%) located on the A genome, 18,826 (50.3%) on the B genome, and 5164 (13.8%) on the D genome (Fig. 7). Markers were represented across all 21 chromosomes, with chromosome 2B harboring the highest number (5155; 13.8% of total markers), while chromosome 4D contained the fewest (195; 0.52%).Fig. 7Ideogram showing single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers density across various sub-genomes and chromosomes.

Ideogram showing single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers density across various sub-genomes and chromosomes.

The DAPC revealed the presence of three genetic clusters, indicating population structure and a degree of admixture within the mapping panel (Fig. S4). The first three principal components (PCs) explained 8.52%, 6.46%, and 4.53% of the genetic variation, respectively.

The LD decay was calculated separately for each genome and for the entire genome using a threshold of r2 = 0.1, beyond which LD was considered indicative of physical linkage (Fig. S5). LD decayed at 3.44 Mbp in the A genome, 16.8 Mbp in the B genome, and 6.01 Mbp in the D genome. Genome-wide LD decay was estimated at 8.75 Mbp.

Genome-wide association studies

The GWAS was conducted using both conventionally measured yield components and their corresponding hyperspectral phenome traits to elucidate the genetic architecture underlying yield formation. Across all traits, a total of 31 significant marker–trait associations (MTAs) were detected, distributed across eight wheat chromosomes (1A, 1B, 2A, 2B, 2D, 3A, 5A, and 7B) (Fig. 8). Of these MTAs, 13 were located on the A genome, 15 on the B genome, and 3 on the D genome, with chromosome 2B harboring the highest number of associations (12 MTAs), indicating a major contribution of this chromosome to yield-related genetic variation.Fig. 8Chromosomal phenogram illustrating significant associations between markers and three phenotypic traits, as well as associated hyperspectral phenomes.

Among the conventionally measured traits, GY was associated with 5 significant MTAs, with individual SNPs explaining 4.4% to 46.1% of the phenotypic variance in adjusted means. The GN was also associated with five MTAs, although with more modest effects (PVE = 2.2%–7.4%), consistent with its polygenic nature. For TGW, 3 MTAs were identified, explaining 4.6% to 41.7% of the phenotypic variance (Table 1). In the hyperspectral phenome-based analyses, Hyp_TGW exhibited the largest number of associations (7 MTAs), with PVE values ranging from 3.3% to 22.0%, highlighting its strong genetic linkage with grain weight–related spectral signatures. Hyp_GN and Hyp_GY were associated with 6 and 5 MTAs, respectively, with individual markers explaining 2.4%–8.8% of the variance for Hyp_GN and 3.3%–13.4% for Hyp_GY. Overall, the hyperspectral phenomes captured genetically meaningful variation aligned with the yield components. Among MTAs associated with hyperspectral phenomes, notable candidate proteins included plant cytochrome P450 monooxygenases, chlorophyll a–b binding proteins, multi-antimicrobial extrusion (MATE) transporters, disease resistance and adaptor proteins, and leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domain proteins involved in signal transduction. Discussion

Wheat GY is a genetically complex trait influenced by many components and their interactions with the environment, making genetic improvement challenging. Among the key yield components, GN and TGW stand out for their strong contribution to final yield. The GN is often considered the primary driver of sink strength, particularly under sink-limited conditions during grain filling, while TGW plays an essential role in determining final grain mass and quality [56]. Both traits, however, are governed by complex genetic architectures and are strongly influenced by G×E interactions [57]. In our study, significant genotype-by-year effects were observed for GY, GN, and TG. The moderate heritability of GN further reflects its sensitivity to environmental variation across the trials. Considerable inter-annual variations in meteorological conditions, together with field rotation across PSREU, Citra, FL, fields differing in soil properties and slope, likely contributed to environmental heterogeneity. Hyperspectral reflectance captures dynamic canopy properties such as leaf water content, chlorophyll concentration, and canopy architecture. As a result, environmental fluctuations can destabilize spectral–trait associations across years. This makes it challenging to identify consistent spectral predictors across environments. We also observed greater reflectance variability around the 940 nm region in 2024 compared to previous years. Nevertheless, the major spectral regions associated with traits remained largely consistent across years.

In this study, we leveraged hyperspectral reflectance data integrated with genomic analysis to uncover the genetic architecture of wheat yield components, demonstrating the potential of spectrally derived phenomics proxies to advance GWAS. To construct the hyperspectral phenome, we first selected the 50 spectral bands most strongly correlated with each yield component. Because hyperspectral datasets contain substantial collinearity, principal component analysis (PCA) was used to reduce dimensionality, with the first principal component (PC1) serving as a latent spectral phenome summarizing the majority of canopy reflectance variation. A potential limitation of this approach is the condensation of broad spectral variation into a single component. This may attenuate some trait-specific spectral information. Nevertheless, this approach preserves most of the biologically relevant information while mitigating the “curse of dimensionality,” that is, a well-recognized challenge in hyperspectral data analysis. Similar latent spectral variables have been utilized successfully as reliable phenotypes for GWAS. For instance, Concepcion et al. [32] recently demonstrated the efficacy of using PC1 derived from hyperspectral reflectance to elucidate the genetic basis of deoxynivalenol (DON) content in wheat, identifying loci that were otherwise elusive using traditional phenotypic screens. Similarly, spectral dimensionality reduction has proven robust in dissecting and predicting complex physiological traits, such as nitrogen response and water use efficiency, by aggregating subtle reflectance shifts across the visible and near-infrared spectrum [58].

We observed moderate variations in the selected wavebands across traits and datasets that influenced the direction of hyperspectral phenome–yield component correlations. Such variation is not unexpected, as spectral–trait relationships are modulated by environmentally induced variation in canopy physiological and structural properties, which alters how spectral signals covary with agronomic traits across seasons [59,60]. The sign reversal observed in the hyperspectral phenome correlations across years reflects the relative proportion of positively versus negatively correlated bands dominating the top 50 selection per dataset, rather than any biological inconsistency, as evidenced by the consistently significant absolute correlation magnitudes across all years. Nevertheless, most selected bands were consistently concentrated in the near-infrared (800–870 nm), followed by the red-edge (695–730 nm) and green (495–570 nm) regions.

The dominance of the NIR wavelengths in our constructed phenome is consistent with their sensitivity to canopy structure and biomass-related traits such as leaf area index (LAI), which are closely linked to yield formation [61]. NIR reflectance is primarily governed by internal leaf scattering within mesophyll air spaces rather than pigment absorption [62,63], making it a strong proxy for structural development influencing grain yield components. Red-edge bands representing the transition between chlorophyll absorption and NIR reflectance, were also consistently selected, reflecting their sensitivity to chlorophyll content and senescence dynamics. These signals are closely associated with "stay-green" traits and photosynthetic persistence during grain filling, which directly affect GN and TGW. Prior studies have similarly demonstrated the utility of red-edge features for tracking senescence and predicting yield and nitrogen uptake across environments [63,64]. Although fewer in number, green bands provide complementary biochemical information related to chlorophyll absorption minima and accessory pigments such as carotenoids and xanthophylls, which are linked to photosynthetic efficiency and stress responses [59,65].

Of the 31 MTAs identified in this study, 13 were associated with manually measured conventional yield components, with 6 located within annotated genes. Notably, two MTAs on chromosome 2B were located within the gene TraesCS2B02G177400, which encodes a Plant Cytochrome P450 Monooxygenase, proteins previously linked to increased GN and resistance to deoxynivalenol (DON) [66]. Similarly, two MTAs on chromosome 2A were found within TraesCS2A02G031500, a gene associated with disease resistance. An additional MTA on 2B was located within genes encoding leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domain superfamily proteins, known for their roles in signal transduction and plant defense [67], including resistance to powdery mildew in wheat [68].

For hyperspectral phenomes associated with yield components, 18 MTAs were identified, of which 8 were located within annotated genes. Notably, SNP (S1A_506474750) on chromosome 1A was associated with all three hyperspectral phenotypes (Hyp_GY, Hyp_GN, and Hyp_TGW) and was located within the gene TraesCS1A02G314800, which encodes a chlorophyll a–b binding (CAB) protein. CAB proteins are integral to the light-harvesting complex of photosystems I and II, facilitating light absorption and energy transfer, and have been linked to improved photosynthetic efficiency, abiotic stress tolerance, and yield in wheat [69,70]. The Cab genes have also been linked to the stay-green phenotype under terminal heat stress, where susceptible genotypes showed stronger downregulation after anthesis compared with stay-green types [71]. Chlorophyll content and senescence dynamics are most sensitively captured in the green and red-edge spectral regions [72]. Variation at this locus therefore likely underlies the observed hyperspectral signals, reflecting differences in photosynthetic performance and grain-filling capacity. Similarly, SNP S2D_175819471 on chromosome 2D, associated with multiple hyperspectral phenomes, was located within TraesCS2D02G214300, encoding a multi-antimicrobial extrusion (MATE) protein. MATE transporters are implicated in detoxification processes and biotic and abiotic stress adaptation in wheat [73]. By contributing to cellular homeostasis and stress tolerance, these genes help maintain canopy function and photosynthetic activity during reproductive development, which is closely reflected in red-edge and near-infrared spectral regions [74,75]. Under stress, genotypes maintaining physiological stability and delayed senescence sustain assimilate production during grain filling, thereby supporting yield formation. The co-localization of this locus with hyperspectral phenomes suggests that spectral reflectance captures biologically meaningful variation linked to stress adaptation and canopy function.

Two SNPs (S2B_152503632 and S2B_152503653) were associated with both hyperspectral phenomes and grain yield and mapped to a gene encoding a cytochrome P450 monooxygenase. Cytochrome P450s form a large enzyme family involved in phytohormone metabolism, oxidative stress responses, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis, and developmental regulation [[76], [77], [78]]. Through their roles in stress signaling and metabolic regulation, variation in these genes may influence chlorophyll retention, photosynthetic efficiency, and canopy structural integrity during grain filling, particularly affecting red-edge and NIR reflectance. These physiological effects can prolong assimilate supply to developing grains, thereby indirectly contributing to yield formation.

In this study, we identified several pleiotropic loci significantly associated with both yield components and hyperspectral phenomes. Notably, SNP S2B_67980420 was significantly associated with GY, GN, and all three hyperspectral phenotypes. LD-based haplotype analysis showed a significant effect of haplotype on these traits, highlighting it as a key genomic region of interest. Several genes, such as TraesCS2B02G106700, TraesCS2B02G106800, TraesCS2B02G106900, TraesCS2B02G107000, TraesCS2B02G107100, and TraesCS2B02G107200 were found in proximity to this locus (Table S7). These genes were annotated for wound-induced protein Wun1-like homologs, a receptor-like kinase, and F-box domain genes, which in wheat are known to mediate stress responses and signal transduction [79,80], suggesting that natural variation at this locus may influence yield components indirectly by enhancing stress tolerance, maintaining photosynthetic performance, and supporting grain development under adverse conditions.

A persistent challenge in wheat yield improvement is the negative trade-off between GN and TGW. Our results reveal loci co-associated with both traits, suggesting some shared genetic regulation that may offer potential entry points to address this trade-off. Beyond S2B_67980420, S2D_175819471 (with annotated gene TraesCS2D02G214300; multi-antimicrobial extrusion protein) and S1A_506474750 (with annotated gene TraesCS1A02G314800; chlorophyll A-B binding protein) were co-associated with both Hyp_GN and Hyp_TGW. However, co-association at the marker level does not necessarily confirm a common causal variant or establish the direction of allelic effects, and whether these loci can improve GY without the conventional GN-TGW penalty requires functional validation across multiple environments. Nevertheless, these co-associated loci provide a useful starting point for fine-mapping studies aimed at genetically dissecting this trade-off.

The co-localization of associations between yield traits and hyperspectral phenomes supports the utility of hyperspectral data in identifying biologically meaningful genomic regions. Multiple hyperspectral phenomes mapped to common loci, demonstrating that these traits can serve as reliable proxies for GWAS of yield components. While the initial identification of informative hyperspectral bands currently relies on manually measured trait data, this limitation is offset by the complementary nature of hyperspectral and conventional phenotyping. As demonstrated in this study, combining both approaches can uncover genetic regions that might be missed by either method alone. Although associations of individual bands may vary across datasets and years, the broad spectral regions selected were mostly consistent, indicating that these regions can serve as robust proxies even when used independently. Strategically, a small, well-characterized subset of a large wheat population can be screened to identify correlated bands. This can accelerate genetic region mapping across the entire population, optimizing resources while expanding genetic discovery. While the maximum spectral–yield correlations observed in this study were moderate, the higher heritability of most spectral bands relative to yield components, together with correlations derived from BLUEs (i.e., adjusted genotypic means), suggests that these spectral traits capture more stable, genetically driven components of canopy physiology. However, to enhance the transferability of spectral associations in multi-environment GWAS, further improvement can be achieved through diverse environment calibration, stability-based selection of spectral bands, and validation in independent populations and environments, which together will strengthen the robustness and breeding utility of the identified spectral signals. Once informative spectral regions are established and validated across multiple studies, they can be applied more broadly to large populations without the need for manual phenotyping, enhancing the efficiency of high-throughput genomic analyses.

Overall, GWAS using hyperspectral phenomes revealed both unique loci and regions overlapping with those identified through manual phenotyping, illustrating the added value of hyperspectral reflectance data in genetic studies. These findings highlight putative candidate genes that could inform the development of molecular markers for improving GY and other yield components in wheat. Given the limited prior research on the use of hyperspectral phenomes in GWAS for yield traits, this study provides an innovative framework for leveraging high-throughput reflectance data to elucidate complex genetic architectures. By integrating hyperspectral phenotyping with conventional measurements, breeding programs can accelerate the identification of candidate loci, expand the scope of genetic discovery, and ultimately enhance selection efficiency for grain yield improvement.

Conclusion

This study illustrates how combining hyperspectral reflectance data with genomic information can help dissect the genetic architecture of complex yield components in wheat, including GY, GN, and TGW. By leveraging both manually measured traits and hyperspectral-derived proxies, we identified significant MTAs, demonstrating that hyperspectral imaging can complement conventional phenotyping in genome-wide association studies. The combined use of these approaches revealed genetic regions that may be overlooked when relying on either method alone. Several pleiotropic loci were consistently associated with both yield traits and hyperspectral phenomes, underscoring their biological relevance and potential for marker-assisted selection. The co-localization of MTAs with genes involved in photosynthesis, stress responses, and developmental processes further highlights the utility of hyperspectral data in uncovering candidate genes for yield-related traits. Overall, these results provide a framework for integrating hyperspectral phenotyping into wheat genetic studies, offering complementary insights that can guide the exploration of genomic regions underlying complex agronomic traits and support their practical application in breeding programs.
