Hemoptysis Secondary to Right Pulmonary Artery Aneurysm in Behçet's Disease: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Dua Nori, Suha Nori, Dania A. Ashoor, Lana M. Dweekat, Reem Shihab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBehçet's disease is a systemic inflammatory vasculitis that can involve vessels of any size, with pulmonary artery aneurysms representing a rare but potentially fatal complication. Hemoptysis is the most common presentation and may recur despite medical or interventional therapy. We report a 38‐year‐old man with Behçet's disease and a history of right pulmonary artery aneurysm treated with endovascular coil embolization and ongoing immunosuppressive therapy, who presented with recurrent hemoptysis. He was managed with supportive care, high‐dose corticosteroid pulse therapy, and continuation of biologic immunosuppressants. Hemoptysis resolved, and follow‐up imaging was planned to monitor disease progression. Pulmonary artery involvement in BD demands a patient‐specific, integrated management strategy combining medical therapy, interventional procedures, and careful monitoring to prevent fatal complications and ensure long‐term disease control.

Introduction

Behçet's disease (BD) is an inflammatory vasculitic disorder that can affect multiple organ systems and involve blood vessels of any type or size. It typically follows a relapsing–remitting course and presents with diverse clinical manifestations [1]. Diagnosis relies on clinical evaluation, as no pathognomonic laboratory test exists. The International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD) in Table 1 provides a standardised scoring system, in which a score of ≥ 4 indicates a definitive diagnosis of BD [2]. A rare but potentially fatal form of BD is pulmonary artery aneurysm (PAA), which is caused by inflammation‐induced vessel wall destruction, resulting in the formation of a pseudoaneurysm. It is a major cause of death in BD patients; it usually affects young males and manifests as massive or recurrent hemoptysis [3]. An interdisciplinary, tailored approach is required to treat this condition effectively. Treatment options for PAA that develop in the context of BD include medical, surgical, or endovascular therapy [4]. The initial treatment for vascular BD is immunosuppressive therapy. The primary goal of medical management of pulmonary aneurysms in BD is to suppress the inflammatory response in order to avoid irreversible end‐organ damage [5]. The European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) guidelines state that cyclophosphamide and glucocorticoids are the first‐line treatments for BD pulmonary and peripheral artery aneurysms [6]. Recurrence is common even though medical treatments have been demonstrated to cause aneurysms to recede [7]. For people with BD who have a ruptured PAA, surgical treatment has traditionally been the standard approach. This may entail a partial or total lobectomy [8]. Given that delayed management carries mortality rates of over 50%, early diagnosis and intervention are crucial [9].

Herein, we report the case of a 38‐year‐old man with BD who presented with recurrent hemoptysis due to a persistent pulmonary artery aneurysm, despite prior endovascular coil embolization and ongoing immunosuppressive therapy.

Case Report

A 38‐year‐old male smoker with a history of chronic hepatitis B diagnosed 3 years earlier and Behçet disease presented to the emergency department with chronic cough and moderate hemoptysis that had worsened the night before admission. The hemoptysis occurred on a background of intermittent, smaller episodes over the preceding month. He denied fever, chest pain, dyspnea, night sweats, recent respiratory infections, trauma, or weight loss.

The first episode of hemoptysis occurred in 2022; however, the patient did not seek medical attention, and the condition progressed from blood streaks with coughing to larger volumes of bleeding. In 2024, he presented to the hospital with hemoptysis and was diagnosed with Behçet disease. This was followed by recurrent painful oral and genital ulcers and pyoderma gangrenosum lesions on both legs.

One year before the current presentation, the patient experienced massive hemoptysis secondary to a right pulmonary artery aneurysm, for which he underwent urgent endovascular coil embolization (Figure 1). Following the procedure, he reported a persistent cough and a foreign body sensation in the airway. He remained clinically stable on maintenance infliximab administered every 2 months, with the last dose given 2 months before the current admission.

CT pulmonary angiography, showing a large aneurysm in the descending branch of the right pulmonary artery. Metallic coils and a streak artefact were noted post‐embolization procedure.

Despite escalation of therapy, including shortening the infliximab dosing interval from every 8 weeks to every 6 weeks and increasing the dose, hemoptysis persisted and progressively worsened. Three months before admission, CT pulmonary angiography demonstrated a hyperdense lobulated area with streak artefact at the level of the right lower interlobar pulmonary artery related to the prior intervention (Figure 2). This finding was associated with faint patchy consolidation in the right lower lung and a 1 cm focal consolidated nodule in the mid left lung zone (Figure 3), likely representing recent focal hemorrhagic episodes, with no evidence of active bleeding. The patient declined hospital admission at that time.

CT scan revealed the previously inserted metallic coil post‐embolization procedure (the red arrow).

Faint patchy consolidation in the right lower lung.

During the most recent episode, he developed significant hemoptysis requiring emergency department admission. On examination, he appeared mildly pale and hypotensive, with a blood pressure of 90 over 50 mmHg, heart rate of 104 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 94% on room air. Chest examination revealed decreased breath sounds over the right lower lung zone, with no crackles or wheezes.

Initial laboratory investigations showed leukocytosis, marked reactive thrombocytosis, and a significant drop in haemoglobin consistent with acute blood loss. Laboratory values included a white blood cell count of 22.8 × 103 per microliter, haemoglobin of 6.9 g per deciliter compared with a baseline of 8.5 g per deciliter, haematocrit of 23.1%, mean corpuscular volume of 64.4 femtoliters, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 50 mm per hour. Coagulation studies were within normal limits, including prothrombin time of 14.7 s, international normalised ratio of 1.17, and activated partial thromboplastin time of 29 s. Hepatitis B surface antigen was negative.

Chest radiography demonstrated a right lung opacity at the site of prior coil embolization with postprocedural changes and no new consolidation or pleural effusion (Figure 4).

Chest X‐ray showing a right‐lung opacity (red arrow) at the site of prior coil embolization (blue arrow).

The patient was managed collaboratively by pulmonology, rheumatology, and interventional radiology teams. Supportive care included intravenous fluids, pantoprazole, nebulized bronchodilators as needed, and transfusion of two units of packed red blood cells. High‐dose corticosteroid pulse therapy with intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1 g daily for 5 days was initiated for a suspected vasculitic flare.

The patient showed steady clinical improvement, with complete resolution of hemoptysis and an increase in haemoglobin to 9.1 g per deciliter. He was discharged with instructions for close rheumatology follow‐up, continuation of immunosuppressive therapy as guided by the treating team, planned CT pulmonary angiography in 2–3 weeks, followed by surveillance imaging every 6 months for 2 years, resumption of infliximab therapy once clinically stable, consideration of cyclophosphamide if indicated, and smoking cessation counselling.

Discussion

One of the prevalent vasculitides with a significant mortality risk is BD. Inflammation of the tunica media's vasa vasorum is the underlying pathology, destroying the media's elastic fibres and causing dilation of the vessel lumen. This results in periadventitial fibrosis and aneurysm formation at the chronic stage of the disease [10]. One pathological sign includes intense infiltration of inflammatory cells, especially around the vasa vasorum. Arterial lesions appear as an occlusion in 35% of patients and as an aneurysm in 65% of patients. The aorta or the pulmonary arteries and their main branches may develop these lesions [11].

One major direct cause of death in BD is pulmonary disorders. At 1 year and 5 years, respectively, the survival rates for BD patients with PAA are 57% and 39% [10]. According to Kural‐Seyashi et al., the death was caused by PAAs 12 years after the BD diagnosis [12]. The role of minimally invasive therapy in controlling life‐threatening bleeding was highlighted by Cantasdemir et al.'s report of two BD patients with ruptured PAA who were successfully treated by emergency endovascular embolisation using n‐butyl‐cyanoacrylate [9]. Our patient presented similarly with recurrent hemoptysis secondary to a right PAA and underwent successful coil embolization. Because of its higher success rates and lower morbidity, endovascular embolisation has largely supplanted surgery as the first‐line treatment for PAA related to BD. Voiriot et al. examined 17 patients and found that 100% of haemoptysis was immediately controlled following embolisation; however, 40% of cases recurred, primarily due to coil use, persistent vasculitis, and the development of systemic or bronchial collaterals. Relapse was also linked to insufficient immunosuppression and incomplete exclusion of the aneurysm sac. Although coils were the most widely used embolic agents, they were linked to a higher recurrence rate than liquid adhesives like NBCA. These results are evident in our case, which shows initial stabilisation following coil embolisation but necessitates ongoing immunosuppression [7]. Despite the effectiveness of endovascular therapy, side effects like airway communication or coil erosion can happen. A patient with BD who experienced a persistent cough and a foreign‐body sensation following embolisation was reported by Law et al.; it was later discovered that the coil had migrated into the bronchus. The causes were suggested to be local necrosis and persistent vascular inflammation [13]. Similar concerns about early erosion are raised by our patient's post‐embolization cough and airway irritation, highlighting the significance of bronchoscopy and follow‐up imaging when symptoms worsen. To manage vasculitis and stop recurrence, long‐term immunosuppressive treatment is still necessary. Aneurysm formation and recurrence have been demonstrated to be decreased by high‐dose corticosteroids in combination with cytotoxic drugs like cyclophosphamide or biologics like infliximab. According to a recent report, a wide‐neck PAA was successfully embolised with a vascular plug and then treated with cyclophosphamide, resulting in full radiologic resolution after a year [14]. Although the EULAR guidelines state that cyclophosphamide and glucocorticoids are the first‐line treatments for BD pulmonary and peripheral artery aneurysms, several reports have documented clinical improvement with tumour necrosis factor‐alpha (TNFα) blocking agents, such as adalimumab or infliximab, either after recommended first‐line agents failed to treat patients with life‐threatening pulmonary aneurysms or as part of first‐line treatment [5]. Nevertheless, they are not recommended for severe vascular diseases in accordance with EULAR guidelines [6]. Though they have demonstrated potential when cyclophosphamide or azathioprine are not working [15]. However, therapeutic decisions in clinical practise are often individualised. In our case, the decision to escalate and modify immunosuppressive therapy was driven by the recurrence of massive hemoptysis with hemodynamic instability, indicating active and life‐threatening pulmonary vascular inflammation, a recognised emergency manifestation of Behçet's disease requiring prompt treatment intensification. Imaging with CT pulmonary angiography demonstrated a new pulmonary artery aneurysm and thrombosis despite maintenance of infliximab, providing objective evidence of disease progression and inadequate control of TNF‐mediated vasculitis. Additionally, as the patient was already established on maintenance infliximab therapy before admission, during hospitalisation, hemoptysis was managed successfully with high‐dose corticosteroid pulse therapy, and the treating team opted to continue infliximab with close follow‐up rather than immediately transition to cyclophosphamide. Anti‐TNF agents, including infliximab, have demonstrated efficacy in severe or refractory vascular Behçet's disease in observational studies and case reports [15, 16] and are increasingly utilised in selected cases [3]. Additionally, given the patient's clinical instability at presentation and underlying comorbidities, the risks and monitoring requirements associated with cyclophosphamide may have influenced the decision to pursue optimization of ongoing biologic therapy. Cyclophosphamide remained a consideration if clinically indicated.

The main symptom of PAA in BD is hemoptysis, which can manifest in different degrees. It is thought to be caused by either in situ thrombosis due to active vasculitis or the rupture of an aneurysm that erodes into a bronchus. The arteries of the right lower lobe and the right and left main pulmonary arteries are where PAAs are most frequently observed [10].

In a multicenter study of 18 refractory BD patients with major vessel involvement, including four with pulmonary aneurysms, anti‐TNFα significantly decreased the 12‐month risk of relapse when compared to traditional immunosuppressants in previous treatment lines (6% versus 41% with p = 0.036) [16]. Hemoptysis recurred in 41% of patients within a year [7]. This is consistent with our patient's case, in which hemoptysis recurred despite prior embolization and ongoing medical therapy.

Although surgical intervention is the standard treatment for ruptured pulmonary artery aneurysms in Behçet's disease, it carries several drawbacks, including the potential need for repeated thoracotomies if new aneurysms develop. Additionally, postoperative recovery may be hindered because the majority of patients are on long‐term corticosteroids, which also increase the risk of infection. Increased pulmonary artery pressure after lobectomy may cause other PAAs to enlarge, which could result in rupture and death because PAAs usually develop bilaterally [8]. Because of these disadvantages and the high rate of surgical therapy death, endovascular therapy may be a viable alternative to surgery in cases of life‐threatening hemoptysis [9, 10]. However, surgery should be performed on patients who have endovascular intervention‐related complications or who do not improve with medical and/or endovascular treatment [5]. Coils, balloon embolization, glue embolization, and stent graft insertion are all used in this therapeutic approach [17].

In our case, the choice of coil embolization was guided by several clinical and anatomical considerations. The patient presented with massive hemoptysis caused by a descending pulmonary artery aneurysm associated with Behçet disease, a condition that carries a substantial risk of rupture and death, necessitating rapid endovascular exclusion of the aneurysm. Because the disease involves active vasculitis, which increases the likelihood of perioperative bleeding, thrombosis, and impaired wound healing, surgical options such as lobectomy or aneurysmectomy were considered high risk, making a minimally invasive endovascular approach more appropriate. In addition, the aneurysm was focal and located in a distal pulmonary artery branch, rendering it technically amenable to selective coil embolization while preserving the surrounding lung parenchyma. Placement of a covered stent was less suitable due to the distal vessel location and the need for prolonged antiplatelet therapy, which could worsen hemoptysis. Furthermore, current literature supports endovascular embolization as a first‐line treatment for Behçet‐related pulmonary artery aneurysms, particularly in patients presenting with hemoptysis, with outcomes that compare favourably to surgical intervention [18].

Untreated PAAs may result in serious complications like aneurysm rupture, pulmonary embolism, and pulmonary hypertension, which raise the risk of death and respiratory failure. In contrast, the mid‐ and long‐term recurrence rates will be 9.8%–57.5% if BAE is used for management [19]. The recurrence rate of endovascular coil embolization is about 30% [20]. Between 40% and 60% of patients with recurrent hemoptysis have to have a lobectomy, undergo secondary embolization, or die [21].

Early post‐discharge imaging with CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) is recommended within 2–4 weeks to confirm aneurysm stability, evaluate treatment response, and exclude the development of new pulmonary artery aneurysms. In patients with vascular involvement secondary to Behçet's disease, early reassessment is critical given the risk of rapid aneurysmal progression or rupture despite apparent clinical improvement [22, 23]. During the first year, continued radiologic surveillance with CTPA every 3–6 months is advised, with shorter intervals (every 3 months) in patients with recent relapse, persistent hemoptysis, large aneurysms, or escalation of immunosuppressive therapy [22, 24]. Once stability is achieved, imaging intervals may be extended to every 6 months. For long‐term follow‐up, imaging every 6–12 months for at least 2–3 years is reasonable and should be individualised according to disease activity, aneurysm characteristics, and response to immunosuppressive therapy [22, 23]. Magnetic resonance angiography may be considered in selected patients to reduce cumulative radiation exposure [23]. Importantly, urgent imaging is warranted at any time in the event of recurrent hemoptysis, unexplained anaemia, new chest pain, hemodynamic instability, or rising inflammatory markers, as these may indicate aneurysm expansion, rupture, or new vascular involvement [24]. In addition to imaging, close multidisciplinary clinical follow‐up is essential. Rheumatology assessment every 4–8 weeks initially, with monitoring of mucocutaneous activity, vascular symptoms, ESR/CRP levels, and complete blood count, allows early detection of relapse and timely adjustment of immunosuppressive therapy [22]. Vascular involvement represents one of the most severe and potentially fatal manifestations of Behçet's disease, underscoring the need for vigilant longitudinal surveillance [23, 24].

In conclusion, pulmonary artery involvement in BD represents a rare but potentially life‐threatening manifestation. Effective management requires a tailored, multidisciplinary approach combining early recognition, aggressive immunosuppressive therapy, interventional procedures, and careful radiologic monitoring. Such an integrated strategy is essential to prevent fatal complications, control recurrent hemoptysis, and optimise long‐term patient outcomes.
