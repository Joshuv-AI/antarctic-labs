Trends in cardiovascular mortality (1999–2023) in the United States: an analysis of underlying causes of death using the CDC WONDER database
Authors: Junsheng Jiang, Lina Qi, Yueyan Mao, Shenggang Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesNational statistics on mortality due to cardiovascular disease (CVD) in the United States remain insufficiently characterized. This study aimed to analyze the long-term trends and subgroup disparities in CVD-related mortality across the US from 1999 to 2023.MethodsWe utilized the CDC WONDER database to identify all US residents with CVD recorded as the only underlying cause of death from 1999 to 2023 (ICD-10 codes: I00-I99). Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) per 100,000 population and average annual percent changes (AAPCs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated via Joinpoint regression analysis (version 5.3.0).ResultsIn the United States, the number of cardiovascular-related deaths decreased from 954,339 in 1999 to 915,973 in 2023. Concurrently, the overall AAMR fell from 350.76 (95% CI: 350.06 to 351.47) in 1999 to 218.32 (95% CI: 217.87 to 218.77) in 2023, reflecting an AAPC of -1.95 (95% CI: -2.36 to -1.55). Notably, AAMR was consistently higher in men compared to women (2023: 263.04 vs. 180.16). An analysis by race indicated that non-Hispanic blacks experienced the highest mortality in 2023 (290.03 (95% CI: 288.35 to 291.72)), followed by whites (223.73 (95% CI: 223.19 to 224.27)), Hispanics (156.65 (95% CI: 155.42 to 157.88)), and individuals from other racial backgrounds (124.21 (95% CI: 122.92 to 125.49)). Regionally, the South exhibited the highest AAMR in 2023 (234.53 (95% CI: 233.77 to 235.30)), succeeded by the Midwest (231.41 (95% CI: 230.39 to 232.42)), the West (197.25 (95% CI: 196.35 to 198.16)), and the Northeast (194.97 (95% CI: 193.99 to 195.96)). Furthermore, the AAMR was most pronounced among individuals aged over 85 years. Between 1999 and 2020, AAMR was notably higher in rural regions compared to urban areas, with a widening gap after 2011.ConclusionThe age-adjusted mortality rate of CVD in the United States continued to decrease from 1999 to 2023, with the highest burden among men, non-Hispanic black, rural areas, and individuals over 85 years of age.Keywords: cardiovascular disease, cdc wonder, children, mortality, average annual percent changes

Background

One in four annual deaths and imposing a substantial public health and economic burden [1–4]. While prior studies have reported long-term declines in U.S. CVD mortality, most lack granularity across demographic/geographic subgroups or extend to post-2020 data [5–8], a critical gap given the COVID-19 pandemic’s potential impact on healthcare access and CVD outcomes [9–12]. Additionally, existing analyses often do not strictly distinguish between underlying and contributing CVD deaths, leading to inconsistent mortality estimates [13].

Given these gaps, this descriptive epidemiological study uses CDC WONDER data on CVD as the underlying cause of death only from 1999 to 2023 to systematically characterize national CVD mortality trends and subgroup disparities in the U.S. This study focuses on population-level trend description—without causal inference—to provide clear, actionable epidemiological data for public health stakeholders [14].

Method

Data sources

The CDC WONDER database offers mortality data covering diverse conditions and classification categories. We systematically queried the Underlying Cause of Death module of the CDC WONDER database for U.S. resident decedents aged ≥ 0 years from 1999 to 2023; CVD-related mortality cases were identified based on CVD as the underlying cause of death only using the International Classification of Diseases, ICD-10: I00-I99. Data on CVD as a contributing cause of death were excluded from all analyses to ensure consistency with the study’s focus on underlying cause-specific mortality. Inclusion criteria: U.S. residents aged ≥ 0 years who died between 1999 and 2023;

CVD recorded as the only underlying cause of death (ICD-10 codes: I00-I99);Complete demographic (gender, race/ethnicity, age group) and geographic (census region, urban-rural status) data.Exclusion criteria:

CVD listed as a contributing (not underlying) cause of death; Missing demographic or geographic information (n = 1,248, < 0.15% of total); Non-U.S. residents or decedents with invalid ICD-10 coding.The extracted data included stratified demographic variables: gender (male/female), age group (< 1 year, 1–4 years, 5–14 years, 15–24 years, 25–34 years, 35–44 years, 45–54 years, 55–64 years, 65–74 years, 75–84 years, ≥ 85 years), and race/ethnicity (NH White, NH Black, Hispanic/Latino, other races). Geographic classification followed the US Census Bureau’s criteria, dividing the US into four regions (Northeast, Midwest, South, West) and further stratifying by urban-rural status based on population thresholds (urban: ≥50,000 residents; rural: <50,000 residents). Decedents with missing demographic (gender, race/ethnicity) or geographic information were excluded (n = 1,248, < 0.15% of total) to minimize selection bias. Annual U.S. population counts were obtained from the CDC WONDER database, which integrates data from the U.S. Census Bureau (decennial censuses) and bridged-race population estimates (for 2000–2023). Bridged-race denominators were used to ensure consistency with racial/ethnic classification in mortality data, as recommended by the National Center for Health Statistics.This study does not include cardiovascular morbidity data, as the core objective is to analyze long-term trends in CVD-related mortality based on underlying cause of death.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate mortality trends and ensure comparability across subgroups, we calculated crude and age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) per 100,000 population, with corresponding 95% CIs. AAMRs were standardized to the 2000 US standard population (CDC-recommended), and crude mortality rates were calculated by dividing the number of CVD deaths (underlying cause only) by the corresponding annual US population.

Temporal trends in AAMR were analyzed using the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.3.0, National Cancer Institute, Bethesda, Maryland). Key model parameters were set as follows: maximum 5 joinpoints allowed, joinpoint number determined via Monte Carlo permutation test (1,000 permutations), AAMRs log-transformed to meet normality assumptions, and model fitting via least squares. Annual percentage change (APC) and average annual percentage change (AAPC) with 95% CIs were calculated for each trend segment and the overall study period. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05, with significant values marked by an asterisk (*) in the results.

Due to the aggregate nature of CDC WONDER data, individual-level adjustment for confounding variables (e.g., comorbidities, treatment modalities) was not possible. Subgroup analyses across demographic and geographic variables were conducted to explore variation in CVD mortality within the national population.

Result

Overall trends

From 1999 to 2023, the total number of CVD-related deaths in the U.S. decreased slightly by 4.02%, from 954,339 to 915,973. The overall age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) for CVD declined markedly from 350.76 per 100,000 population (95% CI: 350.06–351.47) in 1999 to 218.32 per 100,000 (95% CI: 217.87–218.77) in 2023, with a statistically significant average annual percent change (AAPC) of -1.95 (95% CI: -2.36 to -1.55, P < 0.05). A consistent phase-specific trend was observed for the overall AAMR across the study period: 1999–2002 (APC: -2.68, 95% CI: -3.92 to -1.42), 2002–2010 (APC: -4.14, 95% CI: -4.48 to -3.78), 2010–2018 (APC: -0.87, 95% CI: -1.24 to -0.50), 2018–2021 (APC: 2.39, 95% CI: -0.29 to 5.15), and 2021–2023 (APC: -2.74, 95% CI: -5.25 to -0.16). All subsequent stratified analyses highlight only subgroup-specific deviations from this universal phase trend, with no repeated full trend descriptions for individual subgroups (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Overall age-adjusted mortality trends of CVD in the United States, 1999–2023

Gender stratification

Death count changes

Notable gender disparities were observed in absolute CVD death counts (underlying cause only). Female CVD deaths decreased by 15.18% (510,798 in 1999 to 433,254 in 2023), while male CVD deaths increased by 8.83% over the same period (443,541 to 482,719) (Table 1; Fig. 2).

Age-adjusted mortality trends of CVD by sex, 1999–2023

AAMR trends and subgroup deviations

Males maintained consistently higher AAMRs than females throughout the study period, with a 46% higher AAMR in 2023 (males: 263.04 per 100,000, 95% CI: 262.28–263.80; females: 180.16 per 100,000, 95% CI: 179.62–180.71). Both genders had significant long-term AAMR declines (females: AAPC − 2.08, 95% CI: -2.56 to -1.60; males: AAPC − 1.98, 95% CI: -2.42 to -1.54; all P < 0.05). The key deviation from the universal phase trend was a more pronounced AAMR increase in males during 2018–2021 (APC: 3.12, 95% CI: 0.05 to 6.29) compared to females (APC: 1.56, 95% CI: -1.02 to 4.21), with a more rapid rebound decline in males during 2021–2023 (APC: -3.58, 95% CI: -6.89 to -0.27).

Racial/ethnic stratification

Death count changes

CVD death counts showed distinct trends across racial/ethnic groups, with persistent inequities. Non-Hispanic (NH) White individuals accounted for the largest share of CVD deaths but experienced a 13.46% decrease (797,094 to 689,778). In contrast, minority groups had substantial increases in death counts: Hispanic (96.68% increase, 33,851 to 66,579), NH Black (15.01% increase, 104,242 to 119,891), and NH Other (128.78% increase, 15,965 to 36,525) (Table 1; Fig. 3).

Age-adjusted mortality trends of CVD by race/ethnicity, 1999–2023

AAMR rankings and subgroup deviations

2023 AAMR rankings were consistent with long-term disparities: NH Black (290.03 per 100,000, 95% CI: 288.35–291.72) ;NH White (223.73 per 100,000, 95% CI: 223.19–224.27) ;Hispanic (156.65 per 100,000, 95% CI: 155.42–157.88) ;NH Other (124.21 per 100,000, 95% CI: 122.92–125.49). All groups had significant long-term AAMR declines (p < 0.05), with the steepest reduction in NH Other populations (AAPC: -2.52, 95% CI: -3.00 to -2.03), followed by Hispanic (AAPC: -2.33, 95% CI: -2.97 to -1.68), and NH Black/NH White (both AAPC: -1.87).

The critical subgroup deviation was a statistically significant AAMR increase in NH Black populations during 2018–2021 (APC: 3.79, 95% CI: 2.00 to 5.60, p < 0.05), in contrast to non-significant increases in all other racial/ethnic groups.

Census region stratification

Death count changes

CVD death counts varied by U.S. Census Region, with stable regional rankings over the study period. The Northeast (-23.39%) and Midwest (-12.67%) recorded reduced CVD deaths, while the South (7.32%) and West (7.37%) experienced increases. In 2023, the South had the highest number of CVD deaths (371,803), followed by the Midwest (204,616), West (186,180), and Northeast (153,374) (Table 1; Fig. 4).

Age-adjusted mortality trends of CVD by U.S. census region, 1999–2023

AAMR rankings and subgroup deviations

2023 AAMR rankings (underlying cause only) were as follows: the South (234.53 per 100,000, 95% CI: 233.77–235.30) had the highest AAMR, followed by the Midwest (231.41 per 100,000, 95% CI: 230.39–232.42), West (197.25 per 100,000, 95% CI: 196.35–198.16), and Northeast (194.97 per 100,000, 95% CI: 193.99–195.96). All regions had significant long-term AAMR declines (P < 0.05), with the Northeast showing the steepest reduction (AAPC: -2.32, 95% CI: -2.50 to -2.15). The key subgroup deviation was that the Northeast maintained a consistent, statistically significant AAMR decline throughout the study period (2011–2023 APC: -0.84, 95% CI: -1.11 to -0.56, P < 0.05), with no post-2018 AAMR increase—an observation not seen in the South, Midwest, or West.

Urbanization stratification

Death count changes

Metropolitan (urban) and nonmetropolitan (rural) areas exhibited distinct CVD death count trends (underlying cause only). Rural areas had a 17.86% reduction in CVD deaths (193,163 to 158,671), while urban areas showed a minimal 0.51% decrease (761,176 to 757,302). (Table 1; Fig. 5).

Age adjusted mortality trends of CVD by urbanization level, 1999–2023

AAMR comparisons and subgroup deviations

Rural areas had consistently higher AAMRs than urban areas across the entire study period. Urban-rural AAMR trend analyses are limited to 1999–2020 (the latest year with complete data from CDC WONDER). In 2020, rural AAMR (245.36 per 100,000, 95% CI: 243.12–247.60) was significantly higher than urban AAMR (201.89 per 100,000, 95% CI: 200.78–203.00). Both areas had significant long-term AAMR declines (P < 0.05), with an AAPC of -2.27 in urban areas and an AAPC of -1.90 in rural areas.

The primary subgroup deviation was a near-plateau in rural AAMR after 2011 (APC: -0.20, 95% CI: -0.59 to 0.18), which resulted in a persistent and widening urban-rural CVD mortality gap over the study period.

Age stratification

AAMR and death count by age group

CVD mortality showed extreme age-related variation, with the oldest populations bearing the highest mortality burden. All age groups followed the universal long-term AAMR decline, with the exception of non-significant changes in select young and middle age groups(Table 1; Fig. 6).Younger groups (< 1–24 years): This cohort had the lowest CVD mortality overall. The < 1 year age group had the largest percent reduction in deaths (-46.63%) and a significant AAMR decline (AAPC: -2.31, P < 0.05), while the 5–14 year age group had a non-significant AAMR decline (AAPC: -0.69).Middle groups (25–64 years): The 25–34 year age group was the only age category with increased deaths (15.48%) and a non-significant AAMR trend (AAPC: 0.11). The 55–64 year age group had a 30.02% increase in deaths despite a significant AAMR decline (AAPC: -1.24, P < 0.05).Older groups (65 + years): This cohort accounted for over 80% of total CVD deaths in 2023. The 85 + year age group had the highest 2023 AAMR (5,391.58 per 100,000, 95% CI: 5,373.29–5,409.86) and a significant long-term decline (AAPC: -1.92, P < 0.05), with only a 2.19% reduction in death counts over the study period.

Age-adjusted mortality trends of CVD by age group, 1999–2023

Age-specific deviations from the universal phase trend

A consistent deviation across middle and older age groups (25–74 years) was a temporary AAMR increase during 2018–2021, followed by a rebound decline during 2021–2023, with the magnitude of the increase positively associated with age. For the 25–34 year age group, the APC was 5.65 during 2018–2021 and − 7.30 during 2021–2023 (P < 0.05). For the 45–54 year age group, the APC was 4.06 during 2018–2021 (P < 0.05) and − 5.67 during 2021–2023 (P < 0.05). For the 55–64 year age group, the APC was 3.73 during 2018–2021 (P < 0.05) and − 4.28 during 2021–2023 (P < 0.05).

The most prominent age-specific deviation was the stable AAMR in the 85 + year age group after 2012 (APC: 0.07, 95% CI: -0.37 to 0.51), with no post-2018 increase recorded. This made the 85 + year age group the only cohort unaffected by the 2018–2021 mortality inflection observed in other age groups.

Discussion

This study comprehensively analyzed 24-year trends in U.S. cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality using the CDC WONDER database, revealing a complex landscape of overall progress, persistent disparities, and phase-specific shifts. The findings not only confirm a long-term decline in age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) but also highlight critical variations by sex, race/ethnicity, geography, urbanization, and age—insights that are pivotal for refining public health strategies to address remaining CVD burdens. Notably, this is a descriptive ecological epidemiological study, and no causal inferences can be drawn between observed population-level CVD mortality trends and any potential influencing factors. All interpretations of the identified patterns are framed as tentative hypotheses or potential explanations, which require validation in subsequent individual-level analytical research.

The 4.02% reduction in total CVD deaths and 1.95% annual decline in AAMR (1999–2023) align with broader global trends in CVD mortality reduction, reflecting decades of advancements in prevention, diagnosis, and treatment. The initial accelerated decline likely stems from multiple evidence-based interventions: widespread adoption of statins for cholesterol management, improved blood pressure control through antihypertensive medications, reduced smoking prevalence [15–16], and public health campaigns promoting heart-healthy diets and physical activity. Additionally, innovations in acute care—such as early revascularization for myocardial infarction and better management of heart failure—have contributed to reduced short-term mortality from CVD events [17–19].

COVID-19 pandemic and CVD mortality inflection (2018–2021)

The temporary reversal in CVD mortality trends (2018–2021) across most subgroups coincides with the COVID-19 pandemic and may be linked to pandemic-related disruptions to US healthcare systems. The pandemic limited access to routine CVD care (e.g., delayed screenings, interrupted medication adherence) and exposed vulnerabilities in chronic disease management [20]. Indirect pandemic effects—including increased psychological stress, sedentary behavior, and unhealthy dietary patterns—may also have exacerbated modifiable CVD risk factors [21–22]. The subsequent resumption of AAMR decline (2021–2023) may reflect the recovery of healthcare delivery and public health efforts, though the sustainability of this trajectory remains to be confirmed with longer-term follow-up data [23].

Gender disparities

The finding of higher CVD AAMR in males (263.04 per 100,000 in 2023) versus females (180.16 per 100,000) is consistent with established sex differences in CVD, which may be driven by biological, behavioral, and healthcare-seeking factors [24–25]. Biologically, males have higher baseline low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels and are more prone to early atherosclerotic progression, while premenopausal females may benefit from estrogen-mediated cardiovascular protection [26]. Behaviorally, males are more likely to engage in high-risk behaviors (e.g., smoking, heavy alcohol consumption) and less likely to seek timely medical care for CVD symptoms (e.g., chest pain), which may contribute to higher rates of fatal CVD events [27–28].

The 8.83% increase in male CVD deaths is a notable concern and may reflect slower improvements in male adherence to preventive CVD care or a higher burden of emerging risk factors (e.g., type 2 diabetes, obesity) in males [29–30].A recent cohort study confirmed that male sex is an independent predictor of poor medication adherence for CVD prevention, with males 18–25% less likely to adhere to antihypertensive or statin therapies than females [31].

Racial/ethnic disparities

Racial/ethnic disparities in CVD mortality are among the most striking findings of this study: NH Black populations had the highest 2023 AAMR (nearly double that of NH Other groups), a gap that has persisted despite overall national CVD mortality reductions [32–33]. This disparity may be rooted in systemic social and healthcare inequities: NH Black communities face higher poverty rates, limited access to quality healthcare, and greater exposure to adverse social determinants of health (SDOH), including residential segregation, food insecurity, and environmental pollution [34–37].

The substantial increases in CVD deaths among minority groups (Hispanic: +96.68%, NH Other: +128.78%) may partially reflect US population growth in these groups (e.g., 86% growth in the Hispanic population between 2000 and 2020) but also may signal unmet preventive CVD needs [38]. Hispanic individuals, for example, have high rates of “silent” CVD risk factors (e.g., undiagnosed hypertension, insulin resistance), which may be associated with language barriers and cultural stigma around healthcare utilization [39–40].

Geographic and urban-rural disparities

The South’s persistently high CVD AAMR (234.53 per 100,000 in 2023) and the widening urban-rural CVD mortality gap highlight the role of geography in shaping CVD outcomes. The South has higher rates of modifiable CVD risk factors (e.g., smoking, physical inactivity, high-sodium diets) and a higher poverty prevalence than other US regions, with these challenges compounded by a shortage of cardiologists (the South has 30% fewer cardiologists per capita than the Northeast) [41–42].

Rural areas face even greater CVD care challenges: nonmetropolitan areas have higher AAMRs and slower mortality declines (AAPC: -1.90 vs. -2.27 in urban areas) that may be linked to limited access to specialty care, longer travel times to acute care facilities, and higher chronic disease prevalence [43–44]. For example, rural adults are 1.5 times more likely to have hypertension and 20% more likely to die from a heart attack than urban adults⁴⁷. The near-plateau in rural CVD AAMR after 2011 (APC: -0.20) may reflect the closure of over 120 rural US hospitals since 2010 and the outmigration of healthcare providers, leaving rural communities with limited options for chronic CVD management [45–46].

Age-specific vulnerabilities

Age-specific analyses reveal two key vulnerabilities

The 25–34 year age group: The only group with increasing CVD deaths and a non-significant AAMR trend. This trend may be related to rising rates of obesity, type 2 diabetes, and substance use among young adults [47–48]. For example, U.S. obesity rates in 20–39 year olds have tripled since 1980, and type 2 diabetes diagnoses in this age group have increased by 70% since 2000—risk factors that accelerate atherosclerotic disease [48–49].

The 25–34 year age group: The only group with increasing CVD deaths and a non-significant AAMR trend. This trend may be related to rising rates of obesity, type 2 diabetes, and substance use among young adults [47–48]. For example, U.S. obesity rates in 20–39 year olds have tripled since 1980, and type 2 diabetes diagnoses in this age group have increased by 70% since 2000—risk factors that accelerate atherosclerotic disease [48–49].

The 85 + year age group: While this group had the highest AAMR, its stable post-2012 trend suggests resilience to recent mortality shocks. This may reflect improvements in geriatric care—such as better management of polypharmacy and comorbidities—or selection bias [50–51].However, the sheer number of deaths in this group (334,007 in 2023) underscores the need for age-tailored care, such as simplified medication regimens and palliative care for advanced CVD [52].

This study’s strengths include its use of the CDC WONDER database— a large, population-based dataset that ensures external validity—and its comprehensive analysis of trends across multiple demographic and geographic subgroups. The use of Joinpoint regression to calculate AAPCs also allows for the detection of phase-specific shifts in mortality, which is critical for understanding temporal changes. However, several limitations should be noted. First, CVD misclassification on death certificates is unavoidable: CVD may be undercoded as the underlying cause in decedents with multiple comorbidities (e.g., COVID-19) where acute events are prioritized, or overcoded in areas with limited post-mortem diagnostic capacity, potentially impacting observed trends and disparities. Second, demographic/geographic reporting variability across U.S. states (e.g., race/ethnicity classification, urban-rural coding) may introduce minor bias, despite adherence to national guidelines. Third, age adjustment via the 2000 U.S. standard population—a CDC-recommended approach for cross-study comparability—underrepresents the contemporary U.S. older adult population, potentially slightly underestimating the true age-adjusted CVD mortality burden in 2023. Fourth, the study is subject to the ecological fallacy: group-level trends cannot be extrapolated to individual CVD risk, as individual-level data on risk factors, socioeconomic status, and healthcare utilization are unavailable. Fifth, Urban-rural analyses are restricted to 2020 (the latest complete data from CDC WONDER), and conclusions regarding urban-rural disparities do not extend beyond this year. Future studies should incorporate 2021–2023 urban-rural data once available. Finally, pandemic-era coding variability (temporary delays, minor underlying cause assignment changes) may introduce small inconsistencies in 2020–2021 CVD mortality data, though CDC post-hoc validation mitigates this bias.

Conclusion

This descriptive ecological epidemiological study confirms a significant long-term decline in U.S. CVD age-adjusted mortality (underlying cause only) from 1999 to 2023.Severe, persistent disparities in CVD mortality are observed across key U.S. subgroups, with the highest burden in males, non-Hispanic Black populations, the Southern U.S., rural areas, and adults aged ≥ 85 years.
