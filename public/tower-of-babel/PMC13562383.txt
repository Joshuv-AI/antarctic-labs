Pseudoaneurysm of the posterior circumflex humeral artery in an avid volleyball player
Authors: Hela Saidi, Heather Waring, Matthew Bye, Adam Rizkalla, Ashish Bedi, Sunny Sahajwani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Posterior circumflex humeral artery (PCHA) aneurysms are rare, with only 52 documented cases. They are often diagnosed in athletic individuals who participate in sports requiring repetitive overhead motion. We report a case of a 55-year-old man who developed axillary pain and a perceptible mass after playing volleyball. Intraoperatively, the patient was found to have a PCHA pseudoaneurysm and underwent open surgical excision with primary repair of the axillary artery. Despite increased recognition, PCHA aneurysms remain largely underdiagnosed. Heightened awareness may reduce diagnostic delays and help prevent serious complications, such as limb-threatening ischemia.

Aneurysms of the posterior circumflex humeral artery (PCHA) are a rare phenomenon. According to a recent systematic review, 52 cases have been documented in the literature with the vast majority occurring in young, athletic individuals. Most reported cases involve volleyball and baseball players with volleyball players representing the majority. The first reports of PCHA aneurysms were described in the early 1990s in overhead athletes including volleyball and baseball players suggesting a sport-related etiology.1,2 Patients with PCHA aneurysms are often misdiagnosed with symptoms frequently attributed to musculoskeletal injuries. One of the well-documented complications of PCHA aneurysms is thromboembolism which can lead to upper limb ischemia, a potentially devastating consequence if left untreated. Therefore, timely recognition and prompt management of PCHA aneurysms are essential. This case report describes the diagnosis and management of a PCHA pseudoaneurysm in a volleyball player who presented with right upper-extremity pain and a palpable mass, a fairly rare presentation. Institutional Review Board along with patient permission was granted to publish this report.

Case report

The patient was a 55-year-old male gym teacher with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation on anticoagulation and avid volleyball player who initially presented to his primary care physician with a complaint of a palpable mass in his right axilla. The mass was associated with a sharp, shooting pain, tingling, and numbness radiating down his right arm often worse after playing volleyball. He recalled an event that occurred during a volleyball game 2 years prior where he felt a sudden “pop” sensation. Since that time, he had noticed a palpable mass. When his symptoms began to increase in frequency and progressed to occurring with all overhead activity, he decided to seek attention.

At his initial evaluation, his symptoms were thought due to lymph node compression. He was referred for ultrasound assessment with tentative plans to proceed with biopsy. Ultrasound study revealed a complex, mixed echogenicity structure without internal vascular flow immediately adjacent to the right axillary artery thought likely representing a thrombosed pseudoaneurysm (Fig 1). Upon examination by the vascular surgery team, there was a small, palpable, pulsatile mass appreciated in the right axilla and reported localized tenderness with arm rotation. There was no evidence of vascular compromise in the right upper extremity. Computed tomography angiography showed a patent brachial artery with compression of the proximal brachial artery at the level of the proximal humeral shaft by a 2 × 2 cm lesion. The patient underwent a right upper-extremity angiogram via right radial artery access. A micropuncture needle and sheath were used to gain vessel access with glide wire and glide catheter advanced into the subclavian artery. The angiogram was performed with arm abducted and adducted. The subclavian, axillary, brachial, radial, and ulnar arteries were noted to be widely patent; however, contrast extravasation was noted from the axillary artery at the site of the pulsatile mass confirming the presence of a pseudoaneurysm. The decision was then made to proceed with surgical repair.

Intraoperatively, a longitudinal incision made in the axilla overlying the region of the palpable mass. Initially, there was no appreciable aneurysmal dilation of the axillary artery; however, upon further dissection and arm abduction, an aneurysm at the level of the PCHA was discovered (Fig 2). Following systemic heparinization, proximal and distal control of the axillary artery was obtained using vessel loops. The PCHA was dissected down to a segment of normal caliber where it was then ligated proximally and distally with 0 silk ties (Fig 3) and then resected (Fig 4). Back bleeding and forward bleeding were then performed to flush the axillary artery and confirm patency. The arterial stump (Fig 5) of the resected PCHA was next oversewn in a transverse running fashion without narrowing. At the conclusion of the case, the patient had strong, palpable radial, and ulnar pulses and demonstrated excellent grip strength. Histopathologic evaluation of the resected specimen confirmed a pseudoaneurysm of the PCHA. Postoperatively, the patient recovered without complications and was discharged on postoperative day 1. At the 3-month follow-up, the patient reported initial postoperative numbness in the upper extremity that resolved with physical therapy and had returned to playing competitive sports.

Discussion

Due to under diagnosis and likely under-reporting, the true incidence of PCHA aneurysms, especially among athletes remains unclear. Van de Pol et al3 attempted to address this gap in their evaluation of 281 volleyball players in the Netherlands and reported a PCHA aneurysm incidence of 4.6%. Larger observational studies are needed to gain a better understanding of the true incidence of the condition however. This is particularly important as it can inform screening strategies in at-risk athletic populations and timely clinical decision-making in the management of this potentially serious vascular condition.

This report contributes an additional case of PCHA aneurysm with a rare presentation. Damgaard et al4 described a similar case in which a patient developed a PCHA aneurysm following steroid injection for shoulder pain and presented with a nonpulsatile mass initially thought to be a tumor. These two cases underscore the diagnostic challenges PCHA aneurysms can pose. In both instances, the aneurysms presented as nonpulsatile axillary masses misidentified as other pathologies. These cases illustrate how PCHA aneurysms can mimic more common conditions increasing the risk of missed opportunities for timely identification or inappropriate interventions.

Although Atema et al5 and Scrivens et al6 describe various management strategies for PCHA aneurysms, the optimal treatment remains unclear. Reported approaches include surgical resection alone (60%), resection with revascularization (20%), embolization (13.3%), and conservative management (6.7%).7 In this case, endovascular stent grafting was considered but not pursued because of concerns about stent fracture or occlusion from repetitive overhead shoulder motion and dynamic compression within the quadrilateral space.8 Given the patient’s high level of activity, an open surgical approach was considered the more durable option. The contralateral lower extremity was initially prepared for possible vein harvesting, but this was ultimately unnecessary as primary resection was feasible avoiding the additional operative time and morbidity associated with saphenous vein harvest.9 As demonstrated in this case, outcomes following surgical resection are favorable, with most patients (92.3%) returning to sport without functional deficits.7 The outcome of this case supports the use of open surgical management as a reliable and durable treatment strategy for appropriately selected active patients.

Conclusions

This case adds to the growing body of literature demonstrating that open surgical repair of PCHA aneurysms remains a safe and effective treatment strategy. In this patient, operative management resulted in successful excision of the aneurysm with preservation of upper-extremity perfusion and function consistent with previously reported outcomes. Despite advances in the recognition and management of this uncommon vascular condition, significant knowledge gaps remain regarding its true incidence, risk factors, optimal treatment selection, and long-term outcomes. Multicenter registries and collaborative studies will be essential to address this. Increased awareness among clinicians caring for patients who present with upper-extremity ischemia, particularly in those who engage in repetitive overhead activities may facilitate earlier diagnosis and timely intervention ultimately improving patient outcomes.
