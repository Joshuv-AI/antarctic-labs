Normal Forms for Rough Differential Equations
Gideon Chiusole, Christian Kuehn and Maximilian Semenowicz

Abstract
We address the existence of normal forms for rough ordinary differential equations. We assume suitable smoothness and the hyperbolicity of an equilibrium point. In this context, we establish local formal equivalence of the two solution flows generated by a random nonlinear RDE and its linearized version. This provides the foundation for extending normal form theory to rough differential equations.



Mathematics Subject Classification (2020):
60L90, 60L20, 37G05, 60G15

Keywords:
rough differential equations, normal forms, homological equation, nonlinear coordinate transformation.

1
Introduction

Normal form theory, originally initiated in
[
28
]
, aims to simplify a given differential equation through a smooth coordinate transformation, ideally achieving linearity. We work locally in the phase space
\mathbb{R}^{D}
near a fixed point at the origin. Concretely, given a smooth diffeomorphism
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
with
\varphi(0)=0
, that we imagine as a map coming from a corresponding flow, the normal form problem is to find a coordinate transformation
H:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
with
H(0)=0
and
\mathrm{D}H(0)=\mathrm{id}
such that
\psi:=H^{-1}\circ\varphi\circ H
is as simple as possible. The normal form of
\varphi
near
0
, which is the simplest
\psi
we are able to reach, may thus be viewed as a generalization of the Jordan canonical form to the nonlinear setting.

In this work, we study the question how to extend normal form theory to rough ordinary differential equations (RDEs)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=F_{0}(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+F(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}(\omega)

(1.1)

where
\mathbf{W}
denotes a centered Gaussian rough path that is also a rough path cocycle. In particular, we assume that the origin
0
is an equilibrium point and ask, whether there is a coordinate transformation of (
1.1
) to its linearized version

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\mathrm{D}F_{0}(0)Y_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{D}F(0)Y_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}(\omega).

The existing deterministic normal form framework
[
32
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
21
]
must be refined to accommodate the irregularity of rough paths and the randomness of stochastic processes. For more classical stochastic differential equations (SDEs) in the Stratonovich sense, there exist normal form results as present in
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
. The challenge is whether it is possible to transfer the theory to the rough setting, where much broader classes of driving/forcing processes are allowed.

The normal form problem we intend to tackle boils down to the search for near-identity random coordinate transforms
H:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
conjugating two random local flows, over a metric dynamical system
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P,(\theta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}})
with shift
\theta_{t}:\omega(\cdot)\mapsto\omega(\cdot+t)-\omega(t)
on the Wiener space. In our case, these flows are generated by random rough differential equations whose driver
(\mathbf{W}(\omega))_{\omega}
forms a rough path cocycle in the sense of
[
4
]
, linking rough path theory with random dynamical systems theory. The central obstruction to linearization, also appearing in deterministic and stochastic settings, is the possible failure of hyperbolicity. Said hyperbolicity for random dynamical systems is calculated via the Lyapunov spectrum
\mathrm{spec}\left(\Phi\right)
of a linearized flow
\Phi
. The multiplicative ergodic theorem ensures under broad hypotheses the existence and regularity of a Lyapunov spectrum
[
27
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
31
]
,
[
3
]
. If
0\in\mathrm{spec}\left(\Phi\right)
, then hyperbolicity fails and there is a resonance effect generically blocking the transformation to a normal form. Hence, ruling out resonance of all orders is an essential assumption.

As expected, extending normal form theory from SDEs to RDEs introduces further analytical challenges. The effort is justified, though, for many real-world systems are driven by rough, non-martingale noise - such as fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
\in(0,1/2)
, see
[
6
]
,
[
24
]
- where standard stochastic calculus fails
[
11
]
,
[
29
]
,
[
5
]
. The two main obstacles we overcome in this work are:

•

Classical SDE normal form theory uses martingale tools such as the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality; these are unavailable for RDEs. We develop pathwise estimates and Gaussian tail bounds in their place.

•

Coordinate changes are themselves rough in time, as they are built from solutions to RDEs. In particular, they carry the structure of rough paths - a richer object compared to its deterministic or SDE counterparts. This requires again additional technical tools to be developed.

Although we have not discussed all the technical details of the setup yet, let us still state the main result of this work to clarify the final goal.

Theorem 1.1

(Formal Linearization of an RDE)
.

Let
(\mathbf{W}(\omega))_{\omega}\subseteq\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{M})
be a centered Gaussian rough path that is also a weakly geometric rough path cocycle. Furthermore, let

F_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})\cap C^{3}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})
and

F\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))\cap C^{3}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
with
F_{0}(0)=0
and
F(0)=0
.
We consider the RDE (
1.1
)
in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. If the linear cocycle generated by the linearized equation

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\mathrm{D}F_{0}(0)Y_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{D}F(0)Y_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}(\omega)

is non-resonant of all orders
\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
, that is, if the linear RDEs

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\mathrm{ad}_{n}\mathrm{D}F_{0}(0)Y_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{ad}_{n}\mathrm{D}F(0)Y_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}(\omega)

generate hyperbolic linear cocycles for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
,
then there exists a random coordinate transform
H:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
that formally locally conjugates the solution flows of the initial RDE and its linearization, rendering these two equations formally equivalent.

In addition to that, the formal Taylor expansion
H(\omega,x)\approx^{\mathrm{f}}x+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}H^{n}(\omega,x)
is uniquely determined and is composed of coefficients that are controlled rough paths

\displaystyle t\mapsto\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{n}(\theta_{t}\omega):=(H^{n}(\theta_{t}\omega),\dot{H}^{n}(\theta_{t}\omega))\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}(\mathbb{R},H_{n,D}(\mathbb{R}^{D}))

themselves. Both the path and the Gubinelli derivative meet the temperedness condition.

At this point, we should clarify what we mean by "formally". We say that Theorem
1.1
holds true formally because we do not take into account the question of convergence/divergence of the (therefore a priori formal) power series defining the random coordinate transform
H
. Instead, we manipulate the Taylor series coefficient by coefficient and work with underlying algebraic relations. This formal procedure here happens in analogy to the deterministic and, especially, to the stochastic counterpart.
Be aware that proper convergence is already a delicate issue in the deterministic setting. In fact,
[
20
]
proves that for a large amount of coefficient functions, the deterministic Birkhoff normal form is divergent.

The paper is structured as follows. Section 2 recalls some fundamentals of random dynamical systems presented in
[
3
]
, and Section 3 provides a concise reminder of rough path theory in the sense of Gubinelli
[
18
]
, following
[
13
]
.

Sections 4 and 5 establish the analytical framework for the rough normal form. Section 4 develops the necessary calculus for coordinate transformations in the rough path setting, with a central contribution being a rough Itô-Wentzell-type formula stated in Theorem
4.6
, which handles coefficients exhibiting rough time-dependence themselves. Section 4 also bridges rough path theory with random dynamical systems by verifying that RDEs driven by rough path cocycles generate cocycles in the sense of random dynamical system, and that linearized rough flows are eligible for applications of the multiplicative ergodic theorem.

Section 5 presents the main normal form construction, building upon the SDE approach of
[
3
, Section 8.5]
while addressing the analytical challenges posed by rough drivers. A formal power series ansatz of the random coordinate transform
H(\omega,x)\approx^{\mathrm{f}}x+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}H^{n}(\omega,x)
is substituted into the to-be-satisfied conjugation relation, reducing the normal form problem to finding invariant measures corresponding to a recursive hierarchical system of affine RDEs. An important technical task is to extend the moment conditions coined in
[
3
, Lemma 8.5.3]
to the rough setting and to prove that these moment bounds propagate through the hierarchy. Doing so, we inherently have to study Gaussian tail estimates first examined in
[
9
]
. Under the non-resonance assumption, we establish that each stage of the conjugation relation admits a unique tempered stationary solution, constructed via integral series over the Oseledets decomposition. The result is the unique tempered random coordinate transformation
H
that formally linearizes the original RDE.

Background: Random Dynamical Systems

The theory of random dynamical systems provides a framework for the study of dynamical systems (RDS) subject to randomness. A random dynamical system is defined as a measurable cocycle over a metric dynamical system, where the latter models the temporal evolution of the random input itself, and is therefore regarded as the underlying base flow of the probability space. Thereby, this formulation extends classical deterministic dynamical systems to a random setting.

For completeness and to fix the notation, we review the most fundamental concepts of RDS with direct importance to our work. For a detailed examination of the broad field of RDS, we refer the reader to the classic monograph
[
3
]
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
denote a probability space. We use Borel
\sigma
-algebras when dealing with measurability questions in the context of Euclidean spaces. We write
\mathbb{T}
to denote time; in our work, that means
\mathbb{T}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\}
.

Definition 2.1

(
[
3
, Definition 1.1.1]
)
.

Let
(\theta_{t}:\Omega\rightarrow\Omega)_{t\in\mathbb{T}}
be a family of
P
-invariant transformations, that is, they satisfy
P=P\circ\theta_{t}^{-1}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
, which has the following properties:

(i)

(t,\omega)\mapsto\theta_{t}\omega
is measurable,

(ii)

\theta_{0}=\mathrm{id}
,

(iii)

\theta_{t+s}=\theta_{t}\theta_{s}
for all
s,t\in\mathbb{T}
.

The tuple
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P,(\theta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{T}})
is called a metric dynamical system.

Definition 2.2

(
[
3
, Definition 1.1.2]
)
.

A random dynamical system on a finite-dimensional Banach space
\mathbb{V}
over a metric dynamical system
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P,(\theta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{T}})
, or short cocycle, is a measurable map

\displaystyle\varphi:\mathbb{T}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{V},\;(t,\omega,x)\mapsto\varphi(t,\omega,x)

which has the following properties:

(i)

\varphi(0,\omega,\cdot)=\mathrm{id}
for all
\omega\in\Omega
,

(ii)

\varphi(t+s,\omega,x)=\varphi(t,\theta_{s}\omega,\varphi(s,\omega,x))
for all
x\in\mathbb{V}
,
\omega\in\Omega
, and
s,t\in\mathbb{T}
,

(iii)

\varphi(\cdot,\omega,\cdot):\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{V}
is continuous for all
\omega\in\Omega
.

We refer to (ii) as the cocycle property or cocycle identity.

Next, we introduce the important concept of sub-exponential growth of a random variable with respect to a metric dynamical system, which appears in various places in RDS theory.

Definition 2.3

(
[
2
, Definition 3.1]
)
.

We call a
\mathbb{V}
-valued random variable
X
tempered with respect to the metric dynamical system
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P,(\theta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}})
if

\displaystyle\lim_{\left|t\right|\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log^{+}\left|X(\theta_{t}\omega)\right|}{\left|t\right|}=0

holds for almost every
\omega\in\Omega
, where
\log^{+}x:=\max\{\log x,0\}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

Definition 2.4

(
[
2
, Section 3.3]
)
.

A random probability measure
\mu
on
(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathrm{Borel}(\mathbb{R}^{D}))
, given by
\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\omega,x)=\mathrm{d}\mu_{\omega}(x)\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)
, with marginal
P
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
is called invariant for the cocycle
\varphi
if
\varphi(t,\omega)_{*}\mu_{\omega}=\mu_{\theta_{t}\omega}
holds true almost surely and for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where the subscript
∗
denotes the pushforward.

Last, we recall Oseledets’ celebrated multiplicative ergodic theorem (usually abbreviated as MET). We present only the statements that are the most important to our work, but see
[
3
, Section 3.4]
, especially
[
3
, Theorem 3.4.1 and Theorem 3.4.11]
, for a thorough account of MET variants.

Theorem 2.5

(Multiplicative Ergodic Theorem;
[
3
, Theorem 3.4.11]
)
.

Let
\Phi
be a linear cocycle over
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P,(\theta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}})
. Assume the following integrability condition:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\log^{+}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\Phi(t,\omega,0)\right|\right)+\log^{+}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\Phi(t,\omega,0)^{-1}\right|\right)\mathrm{d}P(\omega)<\infty.

Then, there exists a
\theta
-invariant set
\Omega_{0}\in\mathcal{F}
with
P(\Omega_{0})=1
on which there is the random Oseledets splitting of
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, which has the following properties:

(i)

For all
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
, we have the splitting into the Oseledets spaces given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{D}=\bigoplus_{r=1}^{R(\omega)}E_{r}(\omega).

The mapping
\omega\mapsto E_{r}(\omega)
is measurable for each
r=1,\dots,R(\omega)
. The Oseledets subspaces
E_{r}(\omega)
possess dimension
\dim(E_{r}(\omega))=d_{r}(\omega)
, and are invariant under the flow in the sense that
\Phi(t,\omega)E_{r}(\omega)=E_{r}(\theta_{t}\omega)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
r=1,\dots,R(\omega)
. Equivalently, if
\mathrm{pr}^{r}(\omega):\mathbb{R}^{D}\to E_{r}(\omega)
denotes the projection onto
E_{r}(\omega)
along
\bigoplus_{r^{\prime}=1,r^{\prime}\neq r}^{R(\omega)}E_{r^{\prime}}(\omega)
, then
\Phi(t,\omega)\mathrm{pr}^{r}(\omega)=\mathrm{pr}^{r}(\theta_{t}\omega)\Phi(t,\omega)
holds true for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
r=1,\dots,R(\omega)
.

(ii)

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\setminus\{0\}
, the limit
\lim_{\left|t\right|\rightarrow\infty}\log\left|\Phi(t,\omega)x\right|/\left|t\right|
exists and fulfills for
r=1,\dots,R(\omega)
the condition

\displaystyle x\in E_{r}(\omega)\setminus\{0\}\text{ if and only if }\lim_{\left|t\right|\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\left|\Phi(t,\omega)x\right|}{|t|}=\lambda_{r}(\omega),

where the
\lambda_{r}(\omega)
are the so-called Lyapunov exponents constituting the Lyapunov spectrum
\mathrm{spec}\left(\Phi\right):=\{(\lambda_{r}(\cdot),d_{r}(\cdot)):r=1,\dots,R(\cdot)\}
, see also
[
3
, Definition 3.3.8]
. Be aware that sometimes we also just refer to the Lyapunov exponents when talking about the Lyapunov spectrum
\mathrm{spec}\left(\Phi\right):=\{\lambda_{r}(\cdot):r=1,\dots,R(\cdot)\}
.

3
Background: Rough Path Theory

3.1
Rough Paths

Rough path theory, initiated in
[
23
]
, provides a robust analytical framework for studying differential equations driven by highly irregular signals. In particular, the theory extends classical integration and differential equation techniques beyond the semimartingale setting, allowing one to give pathwise meaning to equations driven by rough noise such as fractional Brownian motion
[
13
]
. Central to the theory is the enhancement of a driving signal by its iterated integrals which encode higher-order oscillatory information and, therefore, eliminate obstructions we encounter with standard martingale methods.
In this section, we introduce the key concepts and notation regarding rough paths, following
[
13
]
, to which we refer the reader for a detailed account. We shall refrain from introducing the parameter
\alpha\in(1/3,1/2]
controlling the regularity of a rough path in every statement. Upon further notice,
T\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
denotes a finite time horizon.

Definition 3.1

(
[
13
, Definition 2.1]
)
.

Put
\Delta:=\{(s,t)\in[0,T]^{2}:s<t\}
. An
\alpha
-Hölder rough path (over
\mathbb{V}
) is a pair
\mathbf{W}:=(W,\mathbb{W})
, where the first-order path
W_{\cdot}:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{V}
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\|W\right\|_{\alpha}:=\sup_{(s,t)\in\Delta}\frac{\left|W_{s,t}\right|}{(t-s)^{\alpha}}<\infty,

the second-order process
\mathbb{W}_{\cdot,\cdot}:[0,T]^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{V}\otimes\mathbb{V}
fulfills

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{W}\right\|_{2\alpha}:=\sup_{(s,t)\in\Delta}\frac{\left|\mathbb{W}_{s,t}\right|}{(t-s)^{2\alpha}}<\infty,

and
Chen’s relation, which reads
W_{s,u}\otimes W_{u,t}=\mathbb{W}_{s,t}-\mathbb{W}_{s,u}-\mathbb{W}_{u,t}
for all
s,u,t\in[0,T]
with
s<u<t
, is met.
We denote the space of
\alpha
-Hölder rough paths by
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{V})
. Last, we impose a metric on
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{V})
defined as

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}:\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{V})\times\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{V})\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\;(\mathbf{W},\widetilde{\mathbf{W}})\mapsto\left\|W-\widetilde{W}\right\|_{\alpha}+\left\|\mathbb{W}-\widetilde{\mathbb{W}}\right\|_{2\alpha}.

In turn,
\rho_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}
gives rise to the semi-norm

\displaystyle\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}:\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{V})\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\;\mathbf{W}\mapsto\left\|W\right\|_{\alpha}+\left\|\mathbb{W}\right\|_{2\alpha}.

Rough paths are typically defined on compact intervals, which is mainly due to Hölder continuity being satisfied only on compact intervals as opposed to infinite intervals. A meaningful extension to the entire real line
\mathbb{R}
is given as follows:

Definition 3.2

(
[
4
, Section 1]
)
.

Set
\mathscr{I}_{0}:=\{I\subseteq\mathbb{R}:I\text{ is a compact interval with }0\in I\}
.
We denote by
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{V})
the space of pairs
\mathbf{W}:=(W,\mathbb{W})
, where
W_{\cdot}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{V}
and
\mathbb{W}_{\cdot,\cdot}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{V}\otimes\mathbb{V}
, such that
\mathbf{W}|_{I}:=(W|_{I},\mathbb{W}|_{I\times I})\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}(I,\mathbb{V})
for each
I\in\mathscr{I}_{0}
. We equip
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{V})
with the coarsest topology for which the maps
\mathbf{W}\mapsto\mathbf{W}|_{I}
are continuous for all
I\in\mathscr{I}_{0}
.

When we lift a stochastic process to a rough path, we often call it an enhanced or enriched rough path.
While Definition
3.1
covers driving signals for rough differential equations (RDEs), we still have to deal with a suitable notion for the corresponding sought-after unknowns.

Definition 3.3

(
[
13
, Definition 4.6]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{W}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{V}_{1})
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}:=(Y,Y^{\prime})\in C^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{V}_{2})\times C^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{V}_{1},\mathbb{V}_{2}))
. We say that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}
is an (
\alpha
-Hölder continuous) controlled rough path controlled by
\mathbf{W}
if the remainder
\mathfrak{R}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}
, implicitly defined for
s,t\in\Delta
by

\displaystyle Y_{s,t}=Y^{\prime}_{s}W_{s,t}+\mathfrak{R}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{s,t},

is regular in the sense that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathfrak{R}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\right\|_{2\alpha}:=\sup_{(s,t)\in\Delta}\frac{\left|\mathfrak{R}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{s,t}\right|}{(t-s)^{2\alpha}}<\infty,

where
\Delta:=\{(s,t)\in[0,T]^{2}:s<t\}
.
The space of (
\alpha
-Hölder continuous) controlled rough paths controlled by
\mathbf{W}
is denoted by
\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{W}}^{2\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{V}_{2})
. The path
Y^{\prime}
indeed plays the role of a derivative of
Y
with respect to
\mathbf{W}
and is called the Gubinelli derivative. We endow
\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{W}}^{2\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{V}_{2})
with the semi-norm

\displaystyle\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{W}}^{2\alpha}}:\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{W}}^{2\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{V}_{2})\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\;\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\mapsto\left\|Y^{\prime}\right\|_{\alpha}+\left\|\mathfrak{R}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\right\|_{2\alpha}.

In analogy to Definition
3.2
, we also regard
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{V}_{2})
, given
\mathbf{W}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{V}_{1})
, where this means that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}|_{I}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}|_{I}}(I,\mathbb{V}_{2})
for each compact interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with
0\in I
.

Notation
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
either be a compact interval with
0\in I
or
I=\mathbb{R}
.
We use bold font to denote rough paths. For rough drivers in
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}(I,\mathbb{V}_{1})
, we stick to
\mathbf{W}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}(I,\mathbb{V}_{1})
, exclusively. In turn, we denote controlled rough paths in
\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}(I,\mathbb{V}_{2})
by caligraphic symbols like
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}
for Latin letters and
\boldsymbol{\upvarphi}
for Greek letters. Sometimes, we refer to the explicit pair, too. For the Gubinelli derivative of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}(I,\mathbb{V}_{2})
, we usually write
Y^{\prime}
in Lagrange’s style. If we want to stress that we are dealing with a time-derivative as opposed to differentiation with respect to any other potential argument, we also employ Newton’s notation
\dot{Y}
.

We shall below require the fundamental property of a rough path being (weakly) geometric. The power of this property lies in the closer resemblance to well-known calculi.

Definition 3.4

(
[
13
, Section 2.2]
,
[
10
, Definition 2.2]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{W}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{V})
and
\Delta:=\{(s,t)\in[0,T]^{2}:s<t\}
. We call
\mathbf{W}
weakly geometric if

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{W}_{s,t})=\frac{1}{2}W_{s,t}\otimes W_{s,t}

holds true for all
(s,t)\in\Delta
. Here, given
\mathbb{X}\in\mathbb{V}_{0}\otimes\mathbb{V}_{0}
, we set the symmetric part
2\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{X}):=\mathbb{X}+\mathbb{X}^{T}
and the antisymmetric part
2\mathrm{Anti}(\mathbb{X}):=\mathbb{X}-\mathbb{X}^{T}
. If otherwise
\mathbf{W}
is not weakly geometric, we measure the deviation by the bracket of
\mathbf{W}
that is given by

\displaystyle[\mathbf{W}]_{s,t}:=W_{s,t}\otimes W_{s,t}-2\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{W}_{s,t})

for
(s,t)\in\Delta
. Notice that
[\mathbf{W}]\in C^{2\alpha}(\Delta,\mathbb{V}\otimes\mathbb{V})
.

If there exists a sequence of smooth paths
(W^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq C^{\infty}([0,T],\mathbb{V})
with sequence of canonical lifts
(\mathbf{W}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{V})
defined as in
[
13
, Exercise 2.1]
such that
\mathbf{W}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbf{W}
, that is,
\rho_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}(\mathbf{W}^{n},\mathbf{W})\to 0
, then we call
\mathbf{W}
geometric.

We realize that any geometric rough path is already weakly geometric, as a result of the lifts of smooth paths being weakly geometric. Weakly geometric rough paths will play an important role to us since, like Stratonovich calculus, they imitate first-order calculus quite well, see
[
13
, Section 2.2]
. And, the (enhanced Gaussian) rough paths we will be interested in conveniently/purposefully turn out to be weakly geometric.

We have now encountered the general underlying concepts. Let us transition to the main operations that we have to carry out with (controlled) rough paths. The most basic yet integral operations are controlled integration and composition with regular maps.

Theorem 3.5

(Gubinelli;
[
13
, Theorem 4.10]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{W}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{V}_{1})
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{W}}^{2\alpha}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{V}_{1},\mathbb{V}_{2}))
. Then, for every
s,t\in[0,T]
with
s<t
, and finite partitions
\mathrm{P}\subseteq[s,t]
, the limit

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}Y_{r}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{r}:=\lim_{\left|\mathrm{P}\right|\rightarrow 0}\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathrm{P}}Y_{u}W_{u,v}+Y^{\prime}_{u}\mathbb{W}_{u,v}

exists in
\mathbb{V}_{2}
. In addition to that,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}\in\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{W}}^{2\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{V}_{2})
, where the integral path
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}:=(I,I^{\prime})
is defined by
I:=\int_{0}^{\cdot}Y_{r}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{r}
and
I^{\prime}=Y
. Furthermore, for some constant
C=C(\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}
, the following two estimates hold true:

(i)

For every
s,t\in[0,T]
with
s<t
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{s}^{t}Y_{r}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{r}-Y_{s}W_{s,t}-Y^{\prime}_{s}\mathbb{W}_{s,t}\right|\leq C(\left\|W\right\|_{\alpha}\left\|\mathfrak{R}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\right\|_{2\alpha}+\left\|\mathbb{W}\right\|_{2\alpha}\left\|Y^{\prime}\right\|_{\alpha})\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha}.

(ii)

The map

\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{W}}^{2\alpha}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{V}_{1},\mathbb{V}_{2}))\rightarrow\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{W}}^{2\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{V}_{2}),\;\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}

is continuous and linear and satisfies the bound

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}\right\|_{\mathscr{D}_{\mathbf{W}}^{2\alpha}}\leq\left\|Y\right\|_{\alpha}+\left\|Y^{\prime}\right\|_{\sup}\left\|\mathbb{W}\right\|_{2\alpha}+CT^{\alpha}(\left\|W\right\|_{\alpha}\left\|\mathfrak{R}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\right\|_{2\alpha}+\left\|\mathbb{W}\right\|_{2\alpha}\left\|Y^{\prime}\right\|_{\alpha}).

In particular, thanks to
[
13
, (4.20)]
, (i) and (ii) have the direct consequences

(i’)

\left|\int_{s}^{t}Y_{r}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{r}\right|\leq\left|Y_{s}W_{s,t}\right|+\left|Y^{\prime}_{s}\mathbb{W}_{s,t}\right|+C\left\|\mathbf{W}\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}}\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha}
, and

(ii’)

\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}}\leq C(1+\left\|\mathbf{W}\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}})(\left|Y_{0}^{\prime}\right|+T^{\alpha}\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}})
.

Notation
.

We often write
\int_{0}^{\cdot}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}_{r}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{r}:=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}
for the controlled rough path that comes from controlled integration as in Theorem
3.5
.

Lemma 3.6

(
[
13
, Lemma 7.3]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{W}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{V}_{1})
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathbb{V}_{2})
, and let
f\in C^{2}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbb{V}_{2},\mathbb{V}_{3})
. Then,
f(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}):=(f(Y_{\cdot}),\mathrm{D}f(Y_{\cdot})Y^{\prime}_{\cdot})\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathbb{V}_{3})
. Furthermore, there exists
C=C(T,\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|f(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}})\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}}\leq C\max\left\{1,\left|Y_{0}^{\prime}\right|+\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}}\right\}\left\|f\right\|_{C^{2}_{\mathrm{b}}}(1+\left\|W\right\|_{\alpha})^{2}\left(\left|Y_{0}^{\prime}\right|+\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}}\right).

At last, a uniform choice of
C
over
T\in(0,1]
is valid.

3.2
Rough Differential Equations

We employ an extension of the theory described in
[
13
, Chapter 8]
to RDEs with drift (as opposed to driftless RDEs). Nevertheless, we still refer to
[
13
]
as the extension is straightfoward. For example, one
could include explicit time in an enlarged driving rough path and alter the coefficient function accordingly. Of course, whenever we speak of an RDE like

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=F_{0}(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+F(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t},

assuming well-definedness, we really mean the corresponding integral equation

\displaystyle Y_{t}=Y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}F_{0}(Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}F(Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{s}.

A common well-posedness result is the following:

Theorem 3.7

(
[
13
, Theorem 8.3 and Theorem 8.5]
,
[
16
, Theorem 2.12]
)
.

Fix some initial value
y_{0}\in\mathbb{V}_{2}
. Let
\mathbf{W}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{V}_{1})
, and let
F\in C^{3}(\mathbb{V}_{2},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{V}_{1},\mathbb{V}_{2}))
and
F_{0}\in C^{3}(\mathbb{V}_{2},\mathbb{V}_{2})
.

(i)

Existence and Uniqueness
: Then, there are some
T_{*}\in(0,T]
and some unique
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T_{*}],\mathbb{V}_{2})
with
Y^{\prime}=F(Y)
such that we have

\displaystyle Y_{t}=y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}F_{0}(Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}F(Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{s}

for all
t\in[0,T_{*}]
. Moreover, if
F
and
F_{0}
are linearly growing or
\in C^{3}_{\mathrm{b}}
, then
T_{*}=T
.

(ii)

Continuity of the Solution Map, also Known as Universal Limit Theorem
: Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}
be as in (i). In addition, let
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{V}_{1})
and let
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}}([0,T],\mathbb{V}_{2})
satisfy

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}_{t}=\tilde{y}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}F_{0}(\widetilde{Y}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}F(\widetilde{Y}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{s},

where
t\in[0,T_{*}]
and
\tilde{y}_{0}\in\mathbb{V}_{2}
. Then, it holds

\displaystyle\quad\left\|Y^{\prime}-\widetilde{Y}^{\prime}\right\|_{\alpha}+\left\|\mathfrak{R}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathfrak{R}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}}\right\|_{2\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq Q\left(\alpha,T,\left\|F\right\|_{C^{3}_{\mathrm{b}}},\left\|F_{0}\right\|_{C^{3}_{\mathrm{b}}},\left\|\mathbf{W}\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}},\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}\right)\left(\rho_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}(\mathbf{W},\widetilde{\mathbf{W}})+\left|y_{0}-\tilde{y}_{0}\right|\right),

where
Q:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{6}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
denotes an increasing polynomial. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\left\|Y-\widetilde{Y}\right\|_{\alpha}\leq Q\left(\alpha,T,\left\|F\right\|_{C^{3}_{\mathrm{b}}},\left\|F_{0}\right\|_{C^{3}_{\mathrm{b}}},\left\|\mathbf{W}\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}},\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}\right)\left(\rho_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}(\mathbf{W},\widetilde{\mathbf{W}})+\left|y_{0}-\tilde{y}_{0}\right|\right).

In fact, our requirements on the drift vector field
F_{0}
are rather restrictive and allow for easy relaxation, see
[
13
, Exercise 8.5]
. However, the exact regularity assumptions are of no importance to us, so, for ease of presentation, we align the conditions on
F_{0}
and
F
. Now that we have fixed the rough path fundamentals, we advance to a detailed investigation of RDEs, placing more emphasis on links to our normal form procedure. Specifically, we are interested in the connection to the theory of RDS as presented in
[
3
]
.

4
Rough Itô-Wentzell Formula, Link from Rough Differential Equations to Random Dynamical Systems

In this section, we fix a finite time horizon
T\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Unless otherwise stated, all rough paths and RDEs are considered on the time domain
[0,T]
. Although it would be convenient to choose a fixed driver
\mathbf{W}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{M})
, too, we refrain from doing so to avoid imposing unnecessary a priori-requirements on all results. Instead, we describe the respective conditions on
\mathbf{W}
in each statement.

4.1
A Rough Chain Rule and a Rough Itô-Wentzell-type Formula

Let us quickly present the classical Gubinelli derivative product rule.

Lemma 4.1

(Rough Product Rule;
[
13
, Corollary 7.4]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{W}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{M})
, and
let
\boldsymbol{\Upphi}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{K}))
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{L},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
. Then, we have
\boldsymbol{\Upphi}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{L},\mathbb{R}^{K}))
through
(\Phi Y)_{t}:=\Phi_{t}Y_{t}
and the product rule
(\Phi Y)^{\prime}_{t}:=\Phi_{t}^{\prime}Y_{t}+\Phi_{t}Y^{\prime}_{t}
with the interpretation
\Phi_{t}^{\prime}Y_{t}\in\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{L},\mathbb{R}^{K}))
via
\Phi_{t}^{\prime}Y_{t}v:=\Phi_{t}^{\prime}vY_{t}
for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{\Upphi}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}}\leq\left\|\Phi^{\prime}\right\|_{\sup}\left\|Y\right\|_{\alpha}(1+\left\|W\right\|_{\alpha})+2\left\|Y\right\|_{\sup}\left\|\boldsymbol{\Upphi}\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}}+2\left\|\Phi\right\|_{\sup}\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}}+\left\|\Phi\right\|_{\alpha}\left\|Y^{\prime}\right\|_{\sup}.

Remark 4.2
.

Setting
L=1
and
D=K
, it follows immediately from Lemma
4.1
that a similar result holds true for the regular matrix-vector application
\boldsymbol{\Upphi}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{D})
given controlled rough paths
\boldsymbol{\Upphi}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{D})
.

Our approach to the normal form procedure is structurally based upon the SDE approach discussed in
[
3
, Section 8.5]
. For this reason, we need analogues of cornerstone results of stochastic analysis, namely the Stratonovich chain rule and the Itô formula. This section deals with the generalization of these statements.

Definition 4.3
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the set

\displaystyle\mathbb{N}_{n}^{D}:=\left\{\tau\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{D}:\left|\tau\right|:=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\tau_{d}=n\right\}

of
D
-dimensional multi-indices of length
n
. For
\tau\in\mathbb{N}_{n}^{D}
, we abbreviate
X^{\tau}:=\prod_{d=1}^{D}X_{d}^{\tau_{d}}
. Furthermore, we denote by

\displaystyle H_{n,D}(\mathbb{V}):=\left\{f=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{N}_{n}^{D}}f_{\tau}X^{\tau}:f_{\tau}\in\mathbb{V}\right\}

the vector space of homogeneous polynomials of order
n
in
D
variables with values in
\mathbb{V}
. Note that we adopt the standard notation from algebra to write an abstract polynomial
f
in terms of indeterminates
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{D})
. When dealing with a specific polynomial function
f(\cdot)
, we still refer to it as
f
.

Lemma 4.4
.

For any
n,D\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
H_{n,D}(\mathbb{R}^{D})\simeq\mathbb{R}^{\overline{D}}
for some suitable
\overline{D}\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

For the straightforward proof, see
[
3
, Section 8.1]
.
∎

Lemma 4.5

(Rough Chain Rule)
.

Let
\mathbf{W}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{M})
and let
F:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}
be continuous and satisfy
F(t,\cdot)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})
for all
t\in[0,T]
, with formal Taylor series expansion
F(t,x)\approx^{\mathrm{f}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}F^{n}(t,x)
. Assume that, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and any fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, we have
t\mapsto(F^{n}(t,x),\dot{F}^{n}(t,x))\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{D})
. Here,

\displaystyle F^{n}(t,x):=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{N}_{n}^{D}}F^{n}_{\tau}(t)x^{\tau}\text{ and }\dot{F}^{n}(t,x):=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{N}_{n}^{D}}\dot{F}^{n}_{\tau}(t)x^{\tau},

with
\mathfrak{R}(F^{n}(\cdot,x),\dot{F}^{n}(\cdot,x))_{s,t}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{N}_{n}^{D}}\mathfrak{R}(F^{n}_{\tau}(\cdot),\dot{F}^{n}_{\tau}(\cdot))_{s,t}x^{\tau}
. Then, the following statements hold true:

(i)

On a formal level,

\displaystyle t\mapsto(F(t,x),\dot{F}(t,x)):\approx^{\mathrm{f}}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}F^{n}(t,x),\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\dot{F}^{n}(t,x)\right)\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{D}).

(ii)

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle t\mapsto\left(\mathrm{D}_{x}F^{n}(t,x),\mathrm{D}_{x}\dot{F}^{n}(t,x)\right)\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})).

(iii)

On a formal level,

\displaystyle t\mapsto(\mathrm{D}_{x}F(t,x),(\mathrm{D}_{x}F(\cdot,x))^{\prime}_{t}):\approx^{\mathrm{f}}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{D}_{x}F^{n}(t,x),\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{D}_{x}\dot{F}^{n}(t,x)\right)

is an element of
\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})).

Let now in addition
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{D})
.

(iv)

Then, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle t\mapsto(F^{n}(t,Y_{t}),\dot{F}^{n}(t,Y_{t})+\mathrm{D}_{x}F^{n}(t,Y_{t})Y_{t}^{\prime})\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{D}).

(v)

On a formal level,

\displaystyle t\mapsto

\displaystyle(F(t,Y_{t}),\dot{F}(t,Y_{t})+\mathrm{D}_{x}F(t,Y_{t})Y_{t}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad:\approx^{\mathrm{f}}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}F^{n}(t,Y_{t}),\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\dot{F}^{n}(t,Y_{t})+\mathrm{D}_{x}F^{n}(t,Y_{t})Y_{t}^{\prime}\right)

is an element of
\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{D}).

(vi)

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have that

\displaystyle t\mapsto\left(\mathrm{D}_{x}F^{n}(t,Y_{t})Y_{t}^{\prime},\mathrm{D}_{x}\dot{F}^{n}(t,Y_{t})Y_{t}^{\prime}+\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}F^{n}(t,Y_{t})(Y_{t}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{t}^{\prime})+\mathrm{D}_{x}F^{n}(t,Y_{t})Y_{t}^{\prime\prime}\right)

is an element of
\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D})).

(vii)

On a formal level, with the usual abbreviation of the sum,

\displaystyle t\mapsto

\displaystyle\left(\mathrm{D}_{x}F(t,Y_{t})Y_{t}^{\prime},\mathrm{D}_{x}\dot{F}(t,Y_{t})Y_{t}^{\prime}+\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}F(t,Y_{t})(Y_{t}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{t}^{\prime})+\mathrm{D}_{x}F(t,Y_{t})Y_{t}^{\prime\prime}\right)

is an element of
\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
.

Assume now that already
t\mapsto(F(t,x),\dot{F}(t,x))\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{D})
for any fixed
x
. Then, the statements (v) and (vii) hold true even non-formally. To be precise:

(v’)

We have that

t\mapsto(F(t,Y_{t}),\dot{F}(t,Y_{t})+\mathrm{D}_{x}F(t,Y_{t})Y_{t}^{\prime})\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{D})
and

(vii’)

that
t\mapsto\big(\mathrm{D}_{x}F(t,Y_{t})Y_{t}^{\prime},\mathrm{D}_{x}\dot{F}(t,Y_{t})Y_{t}^{\prime}+\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}F(t,Y_{t})(Y_{t}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{t}^{\prime})+\mathrm{D}_{x}F(t,Y_{t})Y_{t}^{\prime\prime}\big)
is an element of
\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
.

Proof.

The proof is a tedious but routine calculus exercise we refrain from performing here. It is important to realize that it is valid to characterize
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],H_{n,D}(\mathbb{V}))
in terms of its coefficients
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}_{\tau}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathbb{V})
, that is,

\displaystyle H=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{N}_{n}^{D}}H_{\tau,\cdot}X^{\tau}\text{ and }H^{\prime}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{N}_{n}^{D}}H^{\prime}_{\tau,\cdot}X^{\tau}

with
\mathfrak{R}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}_{s,t}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{N}_{n}^{D}}\mathfrak{R}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}_{\tau,s,t}
. See also
[
10
, Section 3]
.
∎

With the chain rule done, we still must establish an Itô-type formula that suits our purposes. The issue we face is the fact that the Itô formula stated in
[
13
, Theorem 7.7]
is not strong enough, because it does not cover coefficients that explicitly depend upon time in a rough way. Thus, the current objective is to expand the result into such a setting. Our model is
[
10
, Theorem 4.1]
, whose proof we upgrade as required by our normal form calculations taking place below.

Theorem 4.6

(Rough Itô-Wentzell Formula)
.

Fix a driver
\mathbf{W}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{M})
. Let
(\eta(\cdot,x),\dot{\eta}(\cdot,x))\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Assume the following conditions on
(\eta,\dot{\eta})
:

(i)

The maps
\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D})),\,x\mapsto(\eta(\cdot,x),\dot{\eta}(\cdot,x))
and
\eta:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D})
are continuous and
\eta(t,\cdot)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
for every
t\in[0,T]
.

(ii)

The map
\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
is continuous and
\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(t,\cdot)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D})))
for every
t\in[0,T]
.

(iii)

The map
\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))),\,x\mapsto(t\mapsto(\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(t,x),(\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(\cdot,x))^{\prime}_{t}))
is continuous.

Let moreover
\eta_{0}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
be continuous and satisfy
\eta_{0}(t,\cdot)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})
for all
t\in[0,T]
. Then, define for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
the controlled rough path
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}(x):=(H(\cdot,x),\dot{H}(\cdot,x))\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{D})
by

\displaystyle H(t,x):=H(0,x)+\int_{0}^{t}\eta_{0}(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\eta(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{s}.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}
enjoys the following properties:

(i)

For any
t\in[0,T]
, we have
H(t,\cdot)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})
.

(ii)

The maps
(t,x)\mapsto H(t,x)
,
(t,x)\mapsto\mathrm{D}_{x}H(t,x)
,
(t,x)\mapsto\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(t,x)
and
(t,x)\mapsto\mathrm{D}_{x}^{3}H(t,x)
are continuous.

As last object, we introduce
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{D})
by setting

\displaystyle Y_{t}:=Y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\Upsilon_{0}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\Upsilon_{s}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{s},

where
\Upsilon_{0}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
is continuous, and
(\Upsilon,\Upsilon^{\prime})\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
.
Then, the Itô-Wentzell-type formula

\displaystyle H(t,Y_{t})

\displaystyle=H(0,Y_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\eta(s,Y_{s})+\mathrm{D}_{x}H(s,Y_{s})\Upsilon_{s}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{s}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\eta_{0}(s,Y_{s})+\mathrm{D}_{x}H(s,Y_{s})\Upsilon_{0}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(s,Y_{s})Y_{s}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}[\mathbf{W}]_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(s,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}[\mathbf{W}]_{s}

holds true, where the last two integrals are understood in the Young sense, see
[
33
]
. In particular, if
\mathbf{W}
is weakly geometric, then these two Young integrals vanish due to
[\mathbf{W}]=0
.

To understand the depth of what is happening, it is crucial to be aware of the fact that a solution to an RDE is considered to have two Gubinelli derivatives in the sense that its Gubinelli derivative possesses a meaningful Gubinelli derivative itself. Let us illuminate this by reviewing the generic RDE
\,\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=F_{0}(Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+F(Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}
, assuming that everything is well-defined. The solution
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{D})
actually reads
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}=(Z,F(Z))
. In addition to that, we know that
(F(Z),\mathrm{D}F(Z)Z^{\prime})=(F(Z),\mathrm{D}F(Z)F(Z))\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
, which explains why it makes sense to talk about
Z^{\prime\prime}=\mathrm{D}F(Z)F(Z)
.

Now, if the integral representations of
H(\cdot,x)
and
Y
in Theorem
4.6
appear as mild solution formulations of suitable RDEs, meaning that
(\eta(\cdot,x),\dot{\eta}(\cdot,x))=(\dot{H}(\cdot,x),\ddot{H}(\cdot,x))
and
(\Upsilon,\Upsilon^{\prime})=(Y^{\prime},Y^{\prime\prime})
, where we could write out the Gubinelli derivatives explicitly given the respective (drift and) diffusion vector fields, then the rough Itô-Wentzell formula is effectively a "mild solution formula"-type result for a certain composition of RDE solvers in terms of their respective drift and diffusion vector fields plus higher-order correction terms in form of Young integrals.

Proof of Theorem
4.6
.

To begin with, notice that
Y^{\prime}=\Upsilon
and
\dot{H}(\cdot,x)=\eta(\cdot,x)
by definition. That being said, let
s,t\in[0,T]
with
s<t
. We start by expanding

\displaystyle H(t,Y_{t})-H(s,Y_{s})=H(t,Y_{t})-H(t,Y_{s})+H(t,Y_{s})-H(s,Y_{s}).

Let us examine both summands separately.

\underline{\textit{Regarding the first summand}}
. A simple spatial Taylor expansion produces

\displaystyle H(t,Y_{t})-H(t,Y_{s})=\mathrm{D}_{x}H(t,Y_{s})Y_{s,t}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(t,Y_{s})(Y_{s,t}\otimes Y_{s,t})+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha}).

We make use of the two assertions

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{x}H(t,Y_{s})Y_{s,t}=\mathrm{D}_{x}H(s,Y_{s})Y_{s,t}+\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(s,Y_{s})Y_{s}^{\prime}(W_{s,t}\otimes W_{s,t})+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha})

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(t,Y_{s})(Y_{s,t}\otimes Y_{s,t})=2\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(s,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})\mathbb{W}_{s,t}+\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(s,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})[\mathbf{W}]_{s,t}+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha}),

whose justifications we postpone to Appendix
A.2
. Substituting these into the Taylor expansion yields

\displaystyle H(t,Y_{t})-H(t,Y_{s})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}_{x}H(s,Y_{s})Y_{s,t}+\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(s,Y_{s})Y_{s}^{\prime}(W_{s,t}\otimes W_{s,t})

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(s,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})\mathbb{W}_{s,t}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(s,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})[\mathbf{W}]_{s,t}+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha}).

According to the definition of
Y
and Theorem
3.5
, we have

\displaystyle Y_{s,t}=\Upsilon_{s}W_{s,t}+\Upsilon_{s}^{\prime}\mathbb{W}_{s,t}+\int_{s}^{t}\Upsilon_{0}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha}),

inserting which lets us advance the previous line to

\displaystyle H(t,Y_{t})-H(t,Y_{s})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}_{x}H(s,Y_{s})\Upsilon_{s}W_{s,t}+\mathrm{D}_{x}H(s,Y_{s})\Upsilon_{s}^{\prime}\mathbb{W}_{s,t}+\mathrm{D}_{x}H(s,Y_{s})\int_{s}^{t}\Upsilon_{0}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(s,Y_{s})Y_{s}^{\prime}(W_{s,t}\otimes W_{s,t})+\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(s,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})\mathbb{W}_{s,t}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(s,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})[\mathbf{W}]_{s,t}+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha}).

(4.1)

Let us now take a different point of view. By Theorem
3.5
and Lemma
4.5
,

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{D}_{x}H(u,Y_{u})\Upsilon_{u}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}_{x}H(s,Y_{s})\Upsilon_{s}W_{s,t}+\Big((\mathrm{D}_{x}H(\cdot,Y_{s}))^{\prime}_{s}\Upsilon_{s}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(s,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes\Upsilon_{s})+\mathrm{D}_{x}H(s,Y_{s})\Upsilon_{s}^{\prime}\Big)\mathbb{W}_{s,t}+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}_{x}H(s,Y_{s})\Upsilon_{s}W_{s,t}+\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(s,Y_{s})\Upsilon_{s}\mathbb{W}_{s,t}^{T}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(s,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})\mathbb{W}_{s,t}+\mathrm{D}_{x}H(s,Y_{s})\Upsilon_{s}^{\prime}\mathbb{W}_{s,t}+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha}),

(4.2)

where, in the last equation, we substituted
Y^{\prime}=\Upsilon
and, more importantly,
recognized the decisive information
(\mathrm{D}_{x}H(\cdot,Y_{s}))^{\prime}_{s}\Upsilon_{s}\mathbb{W}_{s,t}=\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(s,Y_{s})\Upsilon_{s}\mathbb{W}_{s,t}^{T}
. Next, we subtract (
4.2
) from (
4.1
) and recall
W_{s,t}\otimes W_{s,t}=[\mathbf{W}]_{s,t}+2\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{W}_{s,t})
and
Y^{\prime}=\Upsilon
to deduce

\displaystyle\quad H(t,Y_{t})-H(t,Y_{s})-\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{D}_{x}H(u,Y_{u})\Upsilon_{u}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(s,Y_{s})Y_{s}^{\prime}[\mathbf{W}]_{s,t}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(s,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})[\mathbf{W}]_{s,t}+\mathrm{D}_{x}H(s,Y_{s})\int_{s}^{t}\Upsilon_{0}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\quad-\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(s,Y_{s})Y_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbb{W}^{T}_{s,t}-2\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{W}_{s,t}))+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha}).

(4.3)

Furthermore, standard rules for Riemann integration provide

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\mathrm{D}_{x}H(u,Y_{u})\Upsilon_{0}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u-\mathrm{D}_{x}H(s,Y_{s})\int_{s}^{t}\Upsilon_{0}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u=\mathrm{o}(\left|t-s\right|),

so that, ultimately, equation (
4.3
) becomes

\displaystyle\quad H(t,Y_{t})-H(t,Y_{s})-\int_{s}^{t}D_{x}H(u,Y_{u})\Upsilon_{u}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(s,Y_{s})Y_{s}^{\prime}[\mathbf{W}]_{s,t}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(s,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})[\mathbf{W}]_{s,t}+\int_{s}^{t}D_{x}H(u,Y_{u})\Upsilon_{0}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\quad-\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(s,Y_{s})Y_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbb{W}^{T}_{s,t}-2\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{W}_{s,t}))+\mathrm{o}(\left|t-s\right|),

(4.4)

where we made use of
\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha})=\mathrm{o}(\left|t-s\right|)
. Let us now tackle the other term.

\underline{\textit{Regarding the second summand}}
.
Thanks to the definition of
H
and Theorem
3.5
, we again infer

\displaystyle H(t,x)-H(s,x)=\eta(s,x)W_{s,t}+\dot{\eta}(s,x)\mathbb{W}_{s,t}+\int_{s}^{t}\eta_{0}(u,x)\,\mathrm{d}u+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha}).

Meanwhile, Theorem
3.5
and Lemma
4.5
applied to the integral of the concatenation of
\eta
and
Y
instead of just
\eta
produce

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\eta(u,Y_{u})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}=\eta(s,Y_{s})W_{s,t}+\left(\dot{\eta}(s,Y_{s})+\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(s,Y_{s})Y_{s}^{\prime}\right)\mathbb{W}_{s,t}+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha}).

For
x=Y_{s}
, subtracting the second line from the first line results in

\displaystyle H(t,Y_{s})-H(s,Y_{s})-\int_{s}^{t}\eta(u,Y_{u})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}=\int_{s}^{t}\eta_{0}(u,Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}u-\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(s,Y_{s})Y_{s}^{\prime}\mathbb{W}_{s,t}+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha}).

Again, with
\int_{s}^{t}\eta_{0}(u,Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}u=\eta_{0}(s,Y_{s})\left|t-s\right|+\mathrm{o}(\left|t-s\right|)
and
\int_{s}^{t}\eta_{0}(u,Y_{u})\,\mathrm{d}u=\eta_{0}(s,Y_{s})\left|t-s\right|+\mathrm{o}(\left|t-s\right|)
, and
\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha})=\mathrm{o}(\left|t-s\right|)
, a final substitution leads to

\displaystyle H(t,Y_{s})-H(s,Y_{s})-\int_{s}^{t}\eta(u,Y_{u})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}=\int_{s}^{t}\eta_{0}(u,Y_{u})\,\mathrm{d}u-\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(s,Y_{s})Y_{s}^{\prime}\mathbb{W}_{s,t}+\mathrm{o}(\left|t-s\right|).

(4.5)

At this point, we find ourselves in a position to finish the proof by putting together the identities that we have derived thus far. Indeed, writing
\mathrm{P}\subseteq[0,t]
for partitions, identifying a telescopic sum in (1) and inserting (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) in (2), we end up with

\displaystyle H(t,Y_{t})-H(0,Y_{0})

\displaystyle\overset{(1)}{=}\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathrm{P}}(H(v,Y_{v})-H(v,Y_{u}))+(H(v,Y_{u})-H(u,Y_{u}))

\displaystyle\overset{(2)}{=}\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathrm{P}}\bigg(\bigg(\int_{u}^{v}\mathrm{D}_{x}H(w,Y_{w})\Upsilon_{w}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{w}+\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(u,Y_{u})Y_{u}^{\prime}[\mathbf{W}]_{u,v}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(u,Y_{u})(Y_{u}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{u}^{\prime})[\mathbf{W}]_{u,v}+\int_{u}^{v}\mathrm{D}_{x}H(w,Y_{w})\Upsilon_{0}(w)\,\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(u,Y_{u})Y_{u}^{\prime}(\mathbb{W}^{T}_{u,v}-2\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{W}_{u,v}))+\mathrm{o}(\left|v-u\right|)\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+\bigg(\int_{u}^{v}\eta(w,Y_{w})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{w}+\int_{u}^{v}\eta_{0}(w,Y_{w})\,\mathrm{d}w-\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(u,Y_{u})Y_{u}^{\prime}\mathbb{W}_{u,v}+\mathrm{o}(\left|v-u\right|)\bigg)\bigg)

\displaystyle\overset{(3)}{=}\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathrm{P}}\int_{u}^{v}\eta(w,Y_{w})+\mathrm{D}_{x}H(w,Y_{w})\Upsilon_{w}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{w}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathrm{P}}\int_{u}^{v}\eta_{0}(w,Y_{w})+\mathrm{D}_{x}H(w,Y_{w})\Upsilon_{0}(w)\,\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathrm{P}}\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(u,Y_{u})Y_{u}^{\prime}[\mathbf{W}]_{u,v}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(u,Y_{u})(Y_{u}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{u}^{\prime})[\mathbf{W}]_{u,v}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathrm{P}}\mathrm{o}(\left|v-u\right|)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{(4)}\int_{0}^{t}\eta(w,Y_{w})+\mathrm{D}_{x}H(w,Y_{w})\Upsilon_{w}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{w}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\eta_{0}(w,Y_{w})+\mathrm{D}_{x}H(w,Y_{w})\Upsilon_{0}(w)\,\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(w,Y_{w})Y_{w}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}[\mathbf{W}]_{w}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(w,Y_{w})(Y_{w}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{w}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}[\mathbf{W}]_{w},

as desired. In (3), we employed linearity of the controlled integration, as well as
\mathbb{W}_{u,v}+\mathbb{W}^{T}_{u,v}=2\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{W}_{u,v})
. Last, in (4), we summed over all controlled Gubinelli integrals and took the limit
|\mathrm{P}|\rightarrow 0
to obtain the Young integrals, as well as
\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathrm{P}}\mathrm{o}(\left|v-u\right|)\rightarrow 0
.
∎

4.2
Random Dynamical Systems from a Rough Point of View

Definition 4.7

(
[
4
, Definition 2]
)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P,(\theta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}})
be a metric dynamical system. A random rough path
(\mathbf{W}(\omega))_{\omega}\subseteq\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{V})
is called a rough path cocycle if
\mathbf{W}_{t_{1}+s,t_{2}+s}(\omega)=\mathbf{W}_{t_{1},t_{2}}(\theta_{s}\omega)
holds true for every
\omega\in\Omega
and all
s,t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,
t_{1}<t_{2}
.

When the randomness is known from the context, we sometimes just say that
\mathbf{W}
is a rough path cocycle.

Notation
.

Henceforward, we will almost exclusively work with random rough paths
(\mathbf{W}(\omega))_{\omega}\subseteq\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{M})
. While we will constantly indicate the random input
\omega
in the driver
\mathbf{W}(\omega)\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{M})
in the notation, we will not display it always explicitly in the context of controlled rough paths
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}(\mathbb{R},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
. The dependence of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}(\omega)
upon
\omega
will stem from the controlling driver
\mathbf{W}(\omega)
and will always be clear from the context.

We start this section by generalizing a statement discussed in
[
25
, Lemma 3.7]
and
[
4
, Theorem 19]
that deals with the fact that RDEs driven by rough path cocycles generate, similar to the situation with SDEs, a random dynamical system even on all of
\mathbb{R}
. But before, we must quote a useful time-shift rule for controlled integration that shall be featured multiple times throughout this work.

Lemma 4.8

(Time-Shift Rule for Integration;
[
25
, Lemma 3.7]
)
.

Let
(\mathbf{W}(\omega))_{\omega}\subseteq\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{M})
be a rough path cocycle and let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{D})
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}(\mathbb{R},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
. Then, for any
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\int_{0}^{t}Z_{u+s}\,\mathrm{d}u=\int_{s}^{t+s}Z_{u}\,\mathrm{d}u,&t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\\
\int_{t}^{0}Z_{u+s}\,\mathrm{d}u=\int_{t+s}^{s}Z_{u}\,\mathrm{d}u,&t\in\mathbb{R}_{<0}\end{cases},

and also

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\int_{0}^{t}U_{u+s}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}(\theta_{s}\omega)=\int_{s}^{t+s}U_{u}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}(\omega),&t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\\
\int_{t}^{0}U_{u+s}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}(\theta_{s}\omega)=\int_{t+s}^{s}U_{u}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}(\omega),&t\in\mathbb{R}_{<0}\end{cases}.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
(\mathbf{W}(\omega))_{\omega}\subseteq\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{M})
be a rough path cocycle. Then, the RDE
\,\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=G_{0}(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+G(Y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}(\omega)
with
Y_{0}=y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
generates a cocycle on the entire time axis
\mathbb{R}
. Here,
G_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})\cap C^{3}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})
and
G\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))\cap C^{3}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
enjoy the following properties taken from
[
8
, Section 3.1]
that ensure global solubility:

(i)

The drift
G_{0}
is Lipschitz and linearly growing.

(ii)

The derivative of
\mathrm{D}G(\cdot)G(\cdot)
is bounded.

Proof.

Denote the solution flow of the RDE for times
\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by
\varphi
. It was shown on several occasions that
\varphi:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
is a cocycle, see, for instance,
[
4
, Theorem 19]
,
[
25
, Lemma 3.7]
and
[
8
, Lemma 5.8]
.
Thus, the task upon us is to extend
\varphi
to the negative axis
\mathbb{R}_{<0}
.

Remembering the connection between RDEs in Lyons’s original sense and in Gubinelli’s sense, as described in
[
13
, Section 8.8]
, we know from
[
15
, Proposition 11.11]
that
\varphi(t,\omega)
is a
C^{\infty}
-diffeomorphism for every
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and every
\omega
. Hence, reviewing
[
3
, Theorem 1.1.6]
, it is natural to extend
\varphi
to the negative axis by defining
\varphi(t,\omega):=\varphi(-t,\theta_{t}\omega)^{-1}
for each
t\in\mathbb{R}_{<0}
and every
\omega
, which gives rise to a map
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Let us verify that the extended
\varphi
remains a cocycle.

First, we study the cocycle property, dividing the
(s,t)
-plane into six areas:

A_{1}:=\{(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:s,t\geq 0\}
,
A_{2}:=\{(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:s\leq 0,t\geq 0,t\geq-s\}
,
A_{3}:=\{(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:s\leq 0,t\geq 0,t\leq-s\}
,
A_{4}:=\{(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:s,t\leq 0\}
,
A_{5}:=\{(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:s\geq 0,t\leq 0,t\leq-s\}
, and
A_{6}:=\{(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:s\geq 0,t\leq 0,t\geq-s\}
.
We handle each area separately in a case distinction. For
(s,t)\in A_{1}
, we already know that the cocycle identity holds true. Let now
(s,t)\in A_{2}
. Using the cocycle identity for pairs
\in A_{1}
, we compute

\displaystyle\varphi(t,\theta_{s}\omega)\circ\varphi(s,\omega)

\displaystyle=\varphi(t+s-s,\theta_{s}\omega)\circ\varphi(-s,\theta_{s}\omega)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\varphi(t+s,\theta_{-s+s}\omega)\circ\varphi(-s,\theta_{s}\omega)\circ\varphi(-s,\theta_{s}\omega)^{-1}=\varphi(t+s,\omega).

We work out the remaining cases for pairs
\in A_{3},A_{4},A_{5},A_{6}
in a similar fashion.

At last, we realize that the measurability and continuity matters are straightforward consequences of the respective properties of
\varphi|_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
.

This finishes the proof.
∎

It turns out to be quite fruitful to further investigate the form of
\varphi
instead of leaving it in its abstract definition given in Lemma
4.9
. Of course,
\varphi|_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
corresponds to solution flows of the RDE, but, a priori, we do not have a meaningful interpretation of
\varphi|_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{<0}}
with regard to the RDE. Let us close this gap.

Lemma 4.10
.

Under the conditions of Lemma
4.9
, we have a mild formulation of
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
on all of
\mathbb{R}
given as

\displaystyle\varphi(t,\omega,y_{0})=\begin{cases}y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}G_{0}(\varphi(u,\omega,y_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}u+\int_{0}^{t}G(\varphi(u,\omega,y_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}(\omega),&t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\\
y_{0}-\int_{t}^{0}G_{0}(\varphi(u,\omega,y_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}u-\int_{t}^{0}G(\varphi(u,\omega,y_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}(\omega),&t\in\mathbb{R}_{<0}\end{cases}.

That means that, for times
t\in\mathbb{R}_{<0}
, we set
\varphi(t,\omega,y_{0})
to be the unique position in space such that
\varphi(t,\omega,y_{0})+\int_{t}^{0}G_{0}(\varphi(u,\omega,y_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}u+\int_{t}^{0}G(\varphi(u,\omega,y_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}(\omega)=y_{0}
.

Proof.

For non-negative times
\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, the statement is clear by the integral formulation of the solution flow. So, let
t\in\mathbb{R}_{<0}
. We check that the RHS evaluated at
\varphi(-t,\theta_{t}\omega,y_{0})
equals
y_{0}
, so that the RHS really is
\varphi(-t,\theta_{t}\omega)^{-1}=\varphi(t,\omega)
, as defined in Lemma
4.9
. Indeed, we calculate

\displaystyle\text{RHS}|_{\varphi(-t,\theta_{t}\omega,y_{0})}

\displaystyle=\varphi(-t,\theta_{t}\omega,y_{0})-\int_{t}^{0}G_{0}(\varphi(u,\omega,\varphi(-t,\theta_{t}\omega,y_{0})))\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{t}^{0}G(\varphi(u,\omega,\varphi(-t,\theta_{t}\omega,y_{0})))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}(\omega)

\displaystyle\overset{(1)}{=}y_{0}+\int_{0}^{-t}G_{0}(\varphi(u,\theta_{t}\omega,y_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}u+\int_{0}^{-t}G(\varphi(u,\theta_{t}\omega,y_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}(\theta_{t}\omega)

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{t}^{0}G_{0}(\varphi(u,\omega,\varphi(-t,\theta_{t}\omega,y_{0})))\,\mathrm{d}u-\int_{t}^{0}G(\varphi(u,\omega,\varphi(-t,\theta_{t}\omega,y_{0})))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}(\omega)

\displaystyle\overset{(2)}{=}y_{0}+\int_{t}^{0}G_{0}(\varphi(u-t,\theta_{t}\omega,y_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}u+\int_{t}^{0}G(\varphi(u-t,\theta_{t}\omega,y_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}(\omega)

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{t}^{0}G_{0}(\varphi(u-t,\theta_{t}\omega,y_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}u-\int_{t}^{0}G(\varphi(u-t,\theta_{t}\omega,y_{0}))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}(\omega)=y_{0},

where, in (1), we wrote down the mild formulation of the solution flow
\varphi|_{t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
, and, in (2), we employed the time-shift rule from Lemma
4.8
for the first two integrals and used the fact that
\varphi
enjoys the cocycle property, as proven in Lemma
4.9
, in the last two integrands.
∎

Remark 4.11
.

We point out an interesting detail: Upon comparing Lemma
4.10
and
[
3
, Theorem 2.3.39]
, we find that the backward integration at the heart of the latter suddenly vanishes in the former, although we would expect both theories to be more or less parallel in our discussion. Yet, we also know from
[
13
, Section 5.4]
that the time-direction in controlled integration has less of an impact on the outcome, because the second-order process brings in robust extra information missing in classical stochastic integration. We elaborate on this topic in Appendix
A.3
. We explain that the mentioned issue only exists when conducting a superficial analysis, whereas upon closer inspection, we realize that there is no real difference.

Note that linear RDEs generate linear cocycles. Therefore, it makes sense to be interested in whether the MET, Theorem
2.5
, is applicable to such cocycles. But, before we get to the link to the MET, let us shortly deviate to another important tool: Duhamel’s formula, also known as the variation-of-constants formula, for solutions to affine differential equations. Now that we know about the existence of suitable fundamental matrices for linear RDEs, let us derive a version of Duhamel’s formula for affine RDEs.

Remark 4.12
.

Let
(\mathbf{W}(\omega))_{\omega}\subseteq\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{M})
be a rough path cocycle, and let
B_{0}\in\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})
and
B\in\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
.
Throughout this work, we will frequently encounter matrix-valued RDEs of the form
\,\mathrm{d}J_{t}=B_{0}J_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+BJ_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}(\omega)
, that have a phase space given by
\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})
, on a regular basis. The importance of these equations results from the observation that linear cocycles
\Phi
generated by linearized equations
\,\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=B_{0}Y_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+BY_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}(\omega)
actually give rise to solutions of the above matrix-valued RDE themselves. However, before showing this, we must understand the equation, which requires careful interpretation: While the drift
B_{0}J_{t}\in\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})
is harmless, we must remember
\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))\simeq\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D})
in order to give a meaning to
BJ_{t}\in\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
via
BJ_{t}v:=B(\cdot\otimes v)J_{t}
for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
. More elegantly, we understand
B\in\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))\simeq\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
via
Bv:=\sum_{m=1}^{M}B_{m}v^{m}
, where
B_{m}\in\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})
for
m=1,\dots,M
, so that

\displaystyle BJ_{t}v=B(\cdot\otimes v)J_{t}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}B_{m}J_{t}v^{m}.

This quickly results in a one-to-one correspondence between linear vector-valued and matrix-valued RDEs via the fundamental matrix
\Phi
. It is clear that the corresponding solution to
\,\mathrm{d}J_{t}=B_{0}J_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+BJ_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}(\omega)
with
J_{0}=\mathrm{id}
written out properly is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Upphi}(\cdot,\omega):=(\Phi(\cdot,\omega),B\Phi(\cdot,\omega))\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})).

Lemma 4.13

(The Inverse of a Linear Cocycle Solves an RDE)
.

Let
(\mathbf{W}(\omega))_{\omega}\subseteq\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{M})
be a weakly geometric rough path cocycle.
In addition, let
B_{0}\in\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})
and
B\in\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
, and denote by
\Phi
the linear cocycle generated by the linear RDE
\,\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=B_{0}Y_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+BY_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}(\omega)
in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Assume that
\Phi(t,\omega)
is invertible for every
t\in[0,T]
. Then, the following statements hold true:

(i)

We have
t\mapsto(\Phi(t,\omega)^{-1},-\Phi(t,\omega)^{-1}\Phi^{\prime}(t,\omega)\Phi(t,\omega)^{-1})\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
. Here, we interpret
\Phi(t,\omega)^{-1}\Phi^{\prime}(t,\omega)\Phi(t,\omega)^{-1}\in\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
via

\Phi(t,\omega)^{-1}\Phi^{\prime}(t,\omega)\Phi(t,\omega)^{-1}v:=\Phi(t,\omega)^{-1}\Phi^{\prime}(t,\omega)v\Phi(t,\omega)^{-1}
for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
.

(ii)

This controlled rough path is the solution to the linear matrix-valued RDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}J_{t}=-J_{t}B_{0}\,\mathrm{d}t-J_{t}B\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}(\omega)

in
\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})
with initial condition
J_{0}=\mathrm{id}
, where
J_{t}B\in\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
via
J_{t}Bv:=J_{t}B(\cdot\otimes v)=J_{t}\sum_{m=1}^{M}B_{m}v^{m}
for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and suitable
B_{m}\in\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})
,
m=1,\dots,M
.

(iii)

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle t\mapsto\left(\Phi(t,\omega)^{-T},-B^{T}\Phi(t,\omega)^{-T}\right)\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})),

where
B^{T}:=(B_{1}^{T},\dots,B^{T}_{M})
.

(iv)

This controlled rough path solves the linear matrix-valued RDE

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}J_{t}=-B_{0}^{T}J_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t-B^{T}J_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}(\omega)

with initial condition
J_{0}=\mathrm{id}
, which lives in
\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})
. Here,
B^{T}J_{t}\in\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
via
B^{T}J_{t}v:=B^{T}(\cdot\otimes v)J_{t}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}B_{m}^{T}v^{m}J_{t}
for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
.

Proof.

Assertions
(i)
and
(iii)
follow immediately from composition of controlled rough paths with regular functions as in Lemma
3.6
, whereas
(ii)
and
(iv)
are tedious but routine calculations; a key hint is
[
13
, Remark 8.14]
.

∎

Corollary 4.14

(Rough Duhamel Formula)
.

Let
(\mathbf{W}(\omega))_{\omega}\subseteq\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{M})
be a weakly geometric rough path cocycle.
We consider the non-autonomous affine RDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=(B_{0}Y_{t}+Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+(BY_{t}+U_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}(\omega)

in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
with initial condition
Y_{0}=y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, given linear maps
B_{0}\in\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})
and
B\in\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{D})
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
. Denote by
\Phi
the linear cocycle generated by the linearized RDE
\,\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=B_{0}Y_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+BY_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}(\omega)
, and by
\varphi(\cdot,\omega)
the solution flow generated by the initial affine RDE. If
\Phi(t,\omega)
is invertible for all
t\in[0,T]
, then we have the formula

\displaystyle\varphi(\cdot,\omega)y_{0}=\Phi(\cdot,\omega)y_{0}+\Phi(\cdot,\omega)\int_{0}^{\cdot}\Phi(s,\omega)^{-1}Z_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+\Phi(\cdot,\omega)\int_{0}^{\cdot}\Phi(s,\omega)^{-1}U_{s}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{s}(\omega).

Proof.

To begin with, note that
\boldsymbol{\Upphi}(\cdot,\omega)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
and
\boldsymbol{\Upphi}(\cdot,\omega)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{D})
according to Lemma
4.13
and Lemma
4.1
, where the controlled rough path
\boldsymbol{\Upphi}(\cdot,\omega)^{-1}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
is explained in Lemma
4.13
.
We intend to apply Theorem
4.6
. To this end, set

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}:=\left(y_{0}+\int_{0}^{\cdot}\Phi(s,\omega)^{-1}Z_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{\cdot}\Phi(s,\omega)^{-1}U_{s}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{s}(\omega),\Phi(\cdot,\omega)^{-1}U\right)\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{D}).

In addition, let
H:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D},\,(t,x)\mapsto\Phi(t,\omega)x
, so that, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, the controlled rough path
t\mapsto(H(t,x),\dot{H}(t,x))
satisfies the mild solution formulation

\displaystyle H(t,x)=x+\int_{0}^{t}B_{0}H(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}BH(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{s}(\omega).

It is easy to see that all the requirements imposed in Theorem
4.6
are met. Therefore, for all
t\in[0,T]
, we obtain, by definition of
H
, that

\displaystyle\Phi(t,\omega)X_{t}=H(t,X_{t})

\displaystyle=y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}B\Phi(s,\omega)X_{s}+\Phi(s,\omega)\Phi(s,\omega)^{-1}U_{s}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{s}(\omega)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}B_{0}\Phi(s,\omega)X_{s}+\Phi(s,\omega)\Phi(s,\omega)^{-1}Z_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}B_{0}\Phi(s,\omega)X_{s}+Z_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}B\Phi(s,\omega)X_{s}+U_{s}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{s}(\omega).

Thus, the Duhamel representation indeed provides a solution to the initial affine RDE. We conclude by the fact that linear-growth RDEs possess unique solutions.
∎

Remark 4.15
.

All of the above results are easily extended to integrals that take place on the non-positive axis
\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}
. For example, we highlight that, in analogy to Lemma
4.10
, Duhamel’s formula as in Corollary
4.14
possesses for
t\in\mathbb{R}_{<0}
the version

\displaystyle\varphi(t,\omega)y_{0}=\Phi(t,\omega)y_{0}-\Phi(t,\omega)\int_{t}^{0}\Phi(s,\omega)^{-1}Z_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s-\Phi(t,\omega)\int_{t}^{0}\Phi(s,\omega)^{-1}U_{s}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{s}(\omega).

With Duhamel’s formula established, let us return to the connection between the linear cocycle generated by a linear RDE and the MET.

Lemma 4.16
.

Let
(\mathbf{W}(\omega))_{\omega}\subseteq\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{M})
be a weakly geometric rough path cocycle that satisfies
\int_{\Omega}\left\|\mathbf{W}(\omega)\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}^{1/\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)<\infty
on compact intervals.
Furthermore, let
\Phi
be a linear cocycle generated by the linear RDE
\,\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=B_{0}Y_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+BY_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}(\omega)
in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, where
B_{0}\in\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})
and
B\in\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
. Then,
\Phi
meets the integrability condition of the MET, Theorem
2.5
, that is,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\log^{+}\left|\Phi(t,\omega)\right|+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\log^{+}\left|\Phi(t,\omega)^{-1}\right|\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)<\infty.

In particular, the MET automatically applies to
\Phi
.

This extremely convenient fact should not come as a surprise, however, for this result mirrors the SDE situation exactly. In that situation, any linear cocycle generated by a linear SDE instantly satisfies the integrability condition of the MET, as well, see
[
3
, Remark 6.2.12]
.

Proof of Lemma
4.16
.

The proof consists of an application (rather, two applications) of the rough Gronwall statement
[
13
, Proposition 8.13]
. However, first, we need to transform the RDE to work with a driftless equation. To this end, we perform the standard enlargement of the driving signal by embedding time as a new entry to end up with a new driver rough path
\overline{\mathbf{W}}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{1+M})
and the new driftless RDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=(B_{0},B)Y_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\overline{\mathbf{W}}_{t}(\omega),

which is equivalent to the original RDE with drift.

∎

This completes the section on necessary preliminaries required as a basic setting to even start to analyze rough normal forms.

5
Normal Form Theory for Rough Differential Equations

In the rest of this work,
(\mathbf{W}(\omega))_{\omega}\subseteq\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{M})
denotes a fixed enhanced centered Gaussian rough path that is also a weakly geometric rough path cocycle. For details on the construction and central properties, we refer to the classic monographs
[
13

and
[
15

, and to the recent work
[
8

as a prominent example.

Recall from the classical construction that the underlying probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
is given by the two-sided Wiener space, that is,
\Omega=C^{0}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{M})
,
\mathcal{F}
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra and

is the probability measure determined by finite-dimensional Gaussian distributions. In addition, we equip
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
with the filtration
(\mathcal{F}^{t}_{-\infty})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
, where, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}^{t}_{-\infty}
denotes the completion of
\sigma(\{W_{s}:s\in(-\infty,t]\})
, and with the base shift
\theta:\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\to\Omega,\,(t,\omega)\mapsto\theta_{t}\omega:=\omega(\cdot+t)-\omega(t)
. After, if necessary, restricting the filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}^{t}_{-\infty})_{t\in\mathbb{R}},P)
, we are allowed to speak about all
\omega
instead of almost all
\omega
.
Keep in mind also the important aspect from the construction of the enhancement that it preserves adaptedness, that is,
\mathbb{W}_{s,t}
and, hence,

is
\mathcal{F}^{t}_{-\infty}

with

, so the second-order process
\mathbb{W}
and, thus, the entire rough path
\mathbf{W}
is non-anticipative.

Finally, we officially rewind the central object our work revolves around.

Definition 5.1

(
[
3
, Definition 8.2.1]
)
.

We name a measurable map

is a

-diffeomorphism and

In the following, we assume that the reader is familiar with the discussion of the SDE case presented in

. Keep in mind that we intend to parallel the SDE discussion, only accounting for additional technical obstacles. Thus, our first target is to transform the rough normal form problem into the examination of a certain hierarchical system of affine RDEs and its invariant measures.

5.1
Arriving at the Hierarchical System of Affine Rough Differential Equations

\displaystyle\leq\left|y_{2}-\tilde{y}_{2}\right|+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|Z_{t}(\omega,y_{1})-Z_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right|+C\int_{0}^{t}\left|\varphi(s,\omega,y_{1})y_{2}-\varphi(s,\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\tilde{y}_{2}\right|\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\left|\big(B(y_{2}-\tilde{y}_{2})+(U_{0}(\omega,y_{1})-U_{0}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1}))\big)W_{0,t}(\omega)\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\left|\big((B(\varphi(\cdot,\omega,y_{1})y_{2}-\varphi(\cdot,\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\tilde{y}_{2}))^{\prime}_{0}+(U_{0}^{\prime}(\omega,y_{1})-U_{0}^{\prime}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1}))\big)\mathbb{W}_{0,t}(\omega)\right|

\displaystyle\quad+C\left\|\mathbf{W}(\omega)\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}\left\|B(\boldsymbol{\upvarphi}(\cdot,\omega,y_{1})y_{2}-\boldsymbol{\upvarphi}(\cdot,\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\tilde{y}_{2})+\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}(\omega,y_{1})-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right)\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left|y_{2}-\tilde{y}_{2}\right|(1+\left\|\mathbf{W}(\omega)\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}})+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|Z_{t}(\omega,y_{1})-Z_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right|

\displaystyle\quad+C\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|U_{t}(\omega,y_{1})-U_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right|\left\|\mathbf{W}(\omega)\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|U_{t}^{\prime}(\omega,y_{1})-U_{t}^{\prime}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right|\left\|\mathbf{W}(\omega)\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\quad+C\left\|\mathbf{W}(\omega)\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{\upvarphi}(\cdot,\omega,y_{1})y_{2}-\boldsymbol{\upvarphi}(\cdot,\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\tilde{y}_{2}\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}}+\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}(\omega,y_{1})-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}C\left|\varphi(s,\omega,y_{1})y_{2}-\varphi(s,\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\tilde{y}_{2}\right|\,\mathrm{d}s

on a pathwise level. We realize that, in the last line, only the very last integral term still depends upon
t
, whereas the rest is constant in
t
, so that we rightfully consider it as increasing. Employing a classical Gronwall inequality like
[
30
, Lemma 2.7]
, we end up with

\displaystyle\quad\left|\varphi(t,\omega,y_{1})y_{2}-\varphi(t,\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\tilde{y}_{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{Ct}\bigg(C\left|y_{2}-\tilde{y}_{2}\right|(1+\left\|\mathbf{W}(\omega)\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}})+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|Z_{t}(\omega,y_{1})-Z_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right|

\displaystyle\quad+C\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|U_{t}(\omega,y_{1})-U_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right|\left\|\mathbf{W}(\omega)\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|U_{t}^{\prime}(\omega,y_{1})-U_{t}^{\prime}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right|\left\|\mathbf{W}(\omega)\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\quad+C\left\|\mathbf{W}(\omega)\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{\upvarphi}(\cdot,\omega,y_{1})y_{2}-\boldsymbol{\upvarphi}(\cdot,\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\tilde{y}_{2}\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}}+\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}(\omega,y_{1})-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}}\right)\bigg)

for all
t\in[0,1]
.
With the same steps as before and
\mathrm{e}^{Ct}\leq\mathrm{e}^{C}\leq C
, this leads to

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\varphi(t,\omega,y_{1})y_{2}-\varphi(t,\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\tilde{y}_{2}\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)\leq C\left|(y_{1},y_{2})-(\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})\right|^{p/2},

where we also exploited (iii).
This bound is exactly what we intended to show in statement (i).

Regarding
(ii)
. This is the case that is the easiest to deal with. In fact, with (i) established, we see that

\displaystyle\quad\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\varphi^{\prime}(t,\omega,y_{1})y_{2}-\varphi^{\prime}(t,\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\tilde{y}_{2}\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|B\left(\varphi(t,\omega,y_{1})y_{2}-\varphi(t,\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\tilde{y}_{2}\right)+\left(U_{t}(\omega,y_{1})-U_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right)\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\varphi(t,\omega,y_{1})y_{2}-\varphi(t,\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\tilde{y}_{2}\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)

\displaystyle\quad+C\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|U_{t}(\omega,y_{1})-U_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)\leq C\left|(y_{1},y_{2})-(\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2})\right|^{p/2}

already produces the desired inequality, and, thus, in total, concludes the proof.
∎

A.2
Proof of Theorem
4.6
: Two Technical Assertions

Recall that, for
s,t\in[0,T]
with
s<t
, we intend to establish

(i)

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{x}H(t,Y_{s})Y_{s,t}=\mathrm{D}_{x}H(s,Y_{s})Y_{s,t}+\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(s,Y_{s})Y_{s}^{\prime}(W_{s,t}\otimes W_{s,t})+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha})\text{ and}

(ii)

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(t,Y_{s})(Y_{s,t}\otimes Y_{s,t})=2\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(s,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})\mathbb{W}_{s,t}+\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(s,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})[\mathbf{W}]_{s,t}+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha}).

Regarding
(i)
. Performing two Gubinelli expansions, exploiting symmetry of
W_{s,t}\otimes W_{s,t}
with
(\mathrm{D}_{x}H(\cdot,x))^{\prime}=\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(\cdot,x)
, and successively collecting terms of order
=\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha})
, we derive

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{x}H(t,x)Y_{s,t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}_{x}H(s,x)Y_{s,t}+(\mathrm{D}_{x}H(\cdot,x))^{\prime}_{s}W_{s,t}Y_{s,t}+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}_{x}H(s,x)Y_{s,t}+\mathrm{D}_{x}\eta(s,x)Y_{s}^{\prime}(W_{s,t}\otimes W_{s,t})+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha}).

Completing the proof of (i) requires the choice
x=Y_{s}
.

Regarding
(ii)
. By inserting the Gubinelli expansion
Y_{s,t}=Y_{s}^{\prime}W_{s,t}+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{2\alpha})
, it is easy to see that

Y_{s,t}\otimes Y_{s,t}=(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})(W_{s,t}\otimes W_{s,t})+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha}).

Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(t,Y_{s})(Y_{s,t}\otimes Y_{s,t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(t,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})(W_{s,t}\otimes W_{s,t})+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(t,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})[\mathbf{W}]_{s,t}+\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(t,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{W}_{s,t})

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha}),

where we employed
W_{s,t}\otimes W_{s,t}=[\mathbf{W}]_{s,t}+2\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{W}_{s,t})
. Now, it is important to remember that the contraction of a symmetric matrix with an antisymmetric matrix vanishes. Thus, as the Hessian matrix
\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(t,Y_{s})
is symmetric, it eliminates any antisymmetric part, which leads to

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(t,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})\mathbb{W}_{s,t}=\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(t,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{W}_{s,t}).

With this fact in mind, we finally arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(t,Y_{s})(Y_{s,t}\otimes Y_{s,t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(s,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})[\mathbf{W}]_{s,t}+\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(s,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})\mathbb{W}_{s,t}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(t,Y_{s})-\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(s,Y_{s}))(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})[\mathbf{W}]_{s,t}

\displaystyle\quad+(\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(t,Y_{s})-\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(s,Y_{s}))(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})\mathbb{W}_{s,t}+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(s,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})[\mathbf{W}]_{s,t}+\mathrm{D}_{x}^{2}H(s,Y_{s})(Y_{s}^{\prime}\otimes Y_{s}^{\prime})\mathbb{W}_{s,t}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{3\alpha}),

where we made use of the facts that
t\mapsto H(t,x)\in C^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{D})
for every
x
, and that
[\mathbf{W}],\mathbb{W}\in C^{2\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{M}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{M})
. The last equation is precisely statement (ii).

A.3
Further Explanation Regarding Remark
4.11

Remember that, most notably, a major difference is the occurrence of backward integration in the SDE setting, as opposed to usual forward integration in the RDE framework. This mystery is encoded in the fact that controlled integration contains additional information due to the second-order process that is an integral part of the rough path
\mathbf{W}(\omega)
, resulting in the choice of the evaluation point carrying less weight. For more details on controlled backward integration, see
[
13
, Section 5.4]
.

Before we come to the main discussion about whether there is a fundamental difference between Lemma
4.10
and
[
3
, Theorem 2.3.39]
, let us illuminate two elementary claims regarding time-reflected rough paths.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\mathbf{W}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([-T,0],\mathbb{R}^{M})
be a weakly geometric rough path. Then, also its time-reflection
\mathbf{W}^{\leftarrow}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{M})
is weakly geometric, where, for
s,t\in[0,T]
,
s<t
, we define

\displaystyle(W^{\leftarrow}_{s,t},\mathbb{W}_{s,t}^{\leftarrow}):=(-W_{-t,-s},-\mathbb{W}_{-t,-s}+W_{-t,-s}\otimes W_{-t,-s}).

Proof.

The proof is a straightforward computation analogous to
[
13
, Exercise 2.4]
.
∎

Corollary A.2
.

Let
\mathbf{W}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([-T,0],\mathbb{R}^{M})
and
\mathbf{W}^{\leftarrow}\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{M})
be as in Proposition
A.1
. In addition to that, let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}}([-T,0],\mathbb{R}^{D})
. Then, putting
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{\leftarrow}_{t}:=(-Y_{-t},-Y^{\prime}_{-t})
yields a time-reflected controlled rough path
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{\leftarrow}\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}^{\leftarrow}(\omega)}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{D})
.

Proof.

The Hölder-continuity requirements are obviously satisfied. Regarding controlledness, we observe, for
s,t\in[0,T]
,
s<t
, that

\displaystyle Y^{\leftarrow}_{s,t}=Y_{-t,-s}=Y_{-t}^{\prime}W_{-t,-s}+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{2\alpha})=-Y_{-s}^{\prime}W_{s,t}^{\leftarrow}+\mathrm{O}(\left|t-s\right|^{2\alpha}),

which ends the proof.
∎

The next step is to compare the situation from Lemma
4.10
to the SDE situation explained in
[
3
, Theorem 2.3.39]
. To this end, let
W
be a two-sided
\mathbb{R}^{M}
-valued Brownian motion with
W_{0}=0
almost surely, and let
(\mathbf{W}(\omega))_{\omega}\subseteq\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([-T,0],\mathbb{R}^{M})
be the weakly geometric Stratonovich enhancement of
W
. On the one hand, it is clear that the time-reflection
W^{\leftarrow}
given by
W_{t}^{\leftarrow}:=W_{-t}
is still a Brownian motion. On the other hand, according to Proposition
A.1
, for almost every
\omega
, the rough path
\mathbf{W}^{\leftarrow}(\omega)\in\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{M})
is well-defined and has first-order path
W^{\leftarrow}(\omega)
.

In order to ensure that the notions of the integrals are consistent for our purposes, which is essential given our expectation of parallelism between the theories, let us calculate that both versions reduce to the same object. We take an arbitrary suitable integrand process
Y:\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\to\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D})
that we also interpret as being controlled by
\mathbf{W}
, so that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}(\omega):=(Y(\omega),Y^{\prime}(\omega))\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}([-T,0],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
. Furthermore, we define, as proposed in Corollary
A.2
,
Y^{\leftarrow}_{t}(\omega):=-Y_{-t}(\omega)
and
(Y^{\leftarrow})^{\prime}_{t}(\omega):=-Y^{\prime}_{-t}(\omega)
to obtain
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}^{\leftarrow}(\omega)\in\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}^{\leftarrow}(\omega)}([0,T],\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M},\mathbb{R}^{D}))
. It remains to check whether both classical Stratonovich integration and controlled Gubinelli integration produce the same output.

For simplicity of presentation, we work with concrete partitions of
[0,T]
which, for
N\in\mathbb{N}
, are given by
\{0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{N-1}<t_{N}=T\}
.
Obviously, the time-reflected trial points
\{-T=-t_{N}<-t_{N-1}<\dots<-t_{1}<-t_{0}=0\}
partition
[-T,0]
. In the SDE case, we recall
the definitions provided in
[
13
, Section 5.4]
to manipulate

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}Y^{\leftarrow}_{t}\circ\,\mathrm{d}^{+}W^{\leftarrow}_{r}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}Y_{r}^{\leftarrow}\,\mathrm{d}^{+}W^{\leftarrow}_{r}+\frac{1}{2}\langle Y^{\leftarrow},W^{\leftarrow}\rangle_{T}^{+}

\displaystyle=\int_{-T}^{0}Y_{r}\,\mathrm{d}^{-}W_{r}-\frac{1}{2}\langle Y,W\rangle^{-}_{-T}=\int_{-T}^{0}Y_{r}\circ\,\mathrm{d}^{-}W_{r}.

(A.1)

Yet, be aware that equation (
A.1
) only holds true algebraically, whereas on the martingale level, the LHS is forward-adapted while the RHS is backward-adapted.

The same objective in mind, we compute in the RDE case that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}Y_{r}^{\leftarrow}(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}^{\leftarrow}_{r}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\Big(Y_{-t_{n}}(\omega)W_{-t_{n+1},-t_{n}}(\omega)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+Y^{\prime}_{-t_{n}}(\omega)\left(\mathbb{W}_{-t_{n+1},-t_{n}}(\omega)-W_{-t_{n+1},-t_{n}}(\omega)\otimes W_{-t_{n+1},-t_{n}}(\omega)\right)\Big)

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\Big(Y_{-t_{n+1}}(\omega)W_{-t_{n+1},-t_{n}}(\omega)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+Y^{\prime}_{-t_{n+1}}(\omega)\left(\mathbb{W}_{-t_{n+1},-t_{n}}(\omega)-W_{-t_{n+1},-t_{n}}(\omega)\otimes W_{-t_{n+1},-t_{n}}(\omega)\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+Y_{-t_{n+1},-t_{n}}(\omega)W_{-t_{n+1},-t_{n}}(\omega)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+Y^{\prime}_{-t_{n+1},-t_{n}}(\omega)\left(\mathbb{W}_{-t_{n+1},-t_{n}}(\omega)-W_{-t_{n+1},-t_{n}}(\omega)\otimes W_{-t_{n+1},-t_{n}}(\omega)\right)\Big)

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}Y_{-t_{n+1}}(\omega)W_{-t_{n+1},-t_{n}}(\omega)+Y^{\prime}_{-t_{n+1}}(\omega)\mathbb{W}_{-t_{n+1},-t_{n}}(\omega)+\mathrm{O}(\left|t_{n+1}-t_{n}\right|^{3\alpha})

\displaystyle=\int_{-T}^{0}Y_{r}(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{r}(\omega).

(A.2)

In the second-to-last equality, we Gubinelli-expanded
Y_{-t_{n+1},-t_{n}}(\omega)=Y^{\prime}_{-t_{n+1}}(\omega)W_{-t_{n+1},-t_{n}}(\omega)+\mathrm{O}(\left|t_{n+1}-t_{n}\right|^{2\alpha})
and collected terms of order
=\mathrm{O}(\left|t_{n+1}-t_{n}\right|^{3\alpha})
. Furthermore, in the last identity, we made use of
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathrm{O}(\left|t_{n+1}-t_{n}\right|^{3\alpha})=0
. Keep in mind the key aspect that adaptedness constitutes no issue here.

Standard results, such as
[
13
, Corollary 5.2]
, tell us that the LHSs of (
A.1
) and (
A.2
) coincide almost surely, which is why the RHSs do, as well. This, finally, convinces us that the "loss" of backward integration in Lemma
4.10
compared to
[
3
, Theorem 2.3.39]
is merely a subtlety hidden in the definitions and does not really take place.

Complete Proof of Lemma

Recall that
f(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}(\omega,y_{1}))=(f(Z(\omega,y_{1})),\mathrm{D}f(Z(\omega,y_{1}))Z^{\prime}(\omega,y_{1}))
. Smooth mappings pass on adaptedness. Let
p\in[2,\infty)
and choose a compact
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D_{1}}
with
0\in K
with two fixed elements
y_{1},\tilde{y}_{1}\in K
.

Let
i\in\{1,\dots,D_{3}\}
, so that
f^{i}:\mathbb{R}^{D_{2}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a polynomial that we write as
f^{i}=\sum_{\left|\sigma\right|=0}^{\deg f^{i}}f^{i}_{\sigma}X^{\sigma}
for
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{D_{2}}_{0}
and coefficients
f_{\sigma}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. We utilize the general calculus identity
x^{\sigma}-y^{\sigma}=\sum_{d=1}^{D}(x_{d}^{\sigma_{d}}-y_{d}^{\sigma_{d}})\prod_{k=1}^{d-1}x_{k}^{\sigma_{k}}\prod_{l=d+1}^{D}y_{l}^{\sigma_{l}}
that holds for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{D}
. Adding elementary inequalities as well as Hölder’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\quad\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|f^{i}(Z_{t}(\omega,y_{1}))-f^{i}(Z_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1}))\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\sum_{\left|\sigma\right|=0}^{\deg f^{i}}f_{\sigma}^{i}(Z_{t}(\omega,y_{1})^{\sigma}-Z_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})^{\sigma})\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{\left|\sigma\right|=0}^{\deg f^{i}}\sum_{d=1}^{D_{2}}\left(\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|(Z_{t}^{d}(\omega,y_{1})^{\sigma_{d}}-Z_{t}^{d}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})^{\sigma_{d}})\right|^{3p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)\right)^{1/3}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\cdot\left(\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\prod_{k=1}^{d-1}Z_{t}^{k}(\omega,y_{1})^{\sigma_{k}}\right|^{3p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)\right)^{1/3}\left(\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\prod_{l=d+1}^{D_{2}}Z_{t}^{l}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})^{\sigma_{l}}\right|^{3p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)\right)^{1/3}.

The last two factors are easy to control: A simple application of Hölder’s inequality shows that it suffices to bound
\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|Z_{t}^{k}(\omega,y_{1})^{\sigma_{k}}\right|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)
for a suitable
q\in\{np:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
k=1,\dots,D_{2}
. We recall that
y_{1}\in K
, where
K
is a compact set with
0\in K
. With

, we get by means of Lemma
5.2
that the two last factors are bounded by
C
. To study the first factor, we recycle the trick of rewriting

\displaystyle Z_{t}^{d}(\omega,y_{1})^{\sigma_{d}}-Z_{t}^{d}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})^{\sigma_{d}}=(Z_{t}^{d}(\omega,y_{1})-Z_{t}^{d}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1}))\sum_{r=0}^{\sigma_{d}-1}Z_{t}^{d}(\omega,y_{1})^{r}Z_{t}^{d}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})^{\sigma_{d}-1-r}.

With this identity, a similar procedure as before and elementary considerations, we see

\displaystyle\quad\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|(Z_{t}^{d}(\omega,y_{1})^{\sigma_{d}}-Z_{t}^{d}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})^{\sigma_{d}})\right|^{3p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|Z_{t}^{d}(\omega,y_{1})-Z_{t}^{d}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right|^{6p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)\right)^{1/2}

Consequently, it holds

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|f^{i}(Z_{t}(\omega,y_{1}))-f^{i}(Z_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1}))\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)\leq C\left|y_{1}-\tilde{y}_{1}\right|^{p/2}.

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{D_{3}}\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|f^{i}(Z_{t}(\omega,y_{1}))-f^{i}(Z_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1}))\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)\leq C\left|y_{1}-\tilde{y}_{1}\right|^{p/2}.

As a second step, we must derive

Note that elementary inequalities give

\displaystyle\quad\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\mathrm{D}f(Z_{t}(\omega,y_{1}))Z_{t}^{\prime}(\omega,y_{1})-\mathrm{D}f(Z_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1}))Z_{t}^{\prime}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|(\mathrm{D}f(Z_{t}(\omega,y_{1}))-\mathrm{D}f(Z_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})))Z_{t}^{\prime}(\omega,y_{1})\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)

\displaystyle\quad+C\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\mathrm{D}f(Z_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1}))(Z_{t}^{\prime}(\omega,y_{1})-Z_{t}^{\prime}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1}))\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega).

Let us control the second summand. It is obvious that

\displaystyle\left|\mathrm{D}f(Z_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1}))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{D_{3}}\sum_{j=1}^{D_{2}}\left|\partial_{j}f^{i}(Z_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1}))\right|.

But,

By means of Hölder’s inequality, this results in

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\mathrm{D}f(Z_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1}))(Z_{t}^{\prime}(\omega,y_{1})-Z_{t}^{\prime}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1}))\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)\leq C\left|y_{1}-\tilde{y}_{1}\right|^{p/2},

for

, so that Hölder’s inequality tells us to focus on

. For a suitable expansion

, where

and all

\displaystyle\quad\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\mathrm{D}f(Z_{t}(\omega,y_{1}))-\mathrm{D}f(Z_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1}))\right|^{2p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{D_{3}}\sum_{j=1}^{D_{2}}\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\partial_{j}f^{i}(Z_{t}(\omega,y_{1}))-\partial_{j}f^{i}(Z_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1}))\right|^{2p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|\mathrm{D}f(Z_{t}(\omega,y_{1}))Z_{t}^{\prime}(\omega,y_{1})-\mathrm{D}f(Z_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1}))Z_{t}^{\prime}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)\leq C\left|y_{1}-\tilde{y}_{1}\right|^{p/2}.

It remains to establish the third bound

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left\|f(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}(\omega,y_{1}))-f(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1}))\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)\leq C\left|y_{1}-\tilde{y}_{1}\right|^{p/2}.

It is crucial to recall the continuity of composing controlled rough paths with regular functions as seen in
[
13
, Theorem 7.5]
. More precisely, we refer to
[
16
, Proposition 2.3]
. Upon close inspection of the proof of
[
16
, Proposition 2.3]
, we first discover that the constant
M(\dots)
is in fact a polynomial of its inputs, and second that it is enough to substitute
\left\|F\right\|_{C^{3}(\zeta)}
for
\left\|F\right\|_{C^{3}_{\mathrm{b}}}
, both following the notation of
[
16
, Proposition 2.3]
, where
\zeta:=\zeta_{1}\cup\tilde{\zeta}_{1}:=\mathrm{conv}(\mathrm{ran}(Z(\omega,y_{1})))\cup\mathrm{conv}(\mathrm{ran}(Z(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})))
and, for any set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D_{2}}
, we write
\mathrm{conv}(S):=\{(1-\lambda)s_{1}+\lambda s_{2}:s_{1},s_{2}\in S,\lambda\in[0,1]\}
.
We apply the estimate from
[
16
, Proposition 2.3]
, as well as Hölder’s inequality, to obtain

\displaystyle\quad\int_{\Omega}\left\|f(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}(\omega,y_{1}))-f(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1}))\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\Omega}\left\|f\right\|_{C^{3}(\zeta)}^{3p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)\right)^{1/3}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\left(\int_{\Omega}M\left(\left|Z_{0}^{\prime}(\omega,y_{1})\right|,\left|Z_{0}^{\prime}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right|,\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}(\omega,y_{1})\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}},\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}},\left\|\mathbf{W}(\omega)\right\|_{\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}}\right)^{3p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)\right)^{1/3}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\left(\int_{\Omega}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}(\omega,y_{1})-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right\|_{\mathscr{D}^{2\alpha}_{\mathbf{W}(\omega)}}+\left|Z_{0}(\omega,y_{1})-Z_{0}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right|+\left|Z_{0}^{\prime}(\omega,y_{1})-Z_{0}^{\prime}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})\right|\right)^{3p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)\right)^{1/3}.

We must control all three factors. The second factor is
\leq C
by standard procedures and Lemma
5.2
and Proposition
5.3
, since
M(\dots)
is a polynomial of its inputs. Moreover, the third factor is
\leq C\left|y_{1}-\tilde{y}_{1}\right|^{p/2}
by means of usual arguments and the assumption that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}
satisfies
(\mathfrak{C})
. To finish the proof, it is left to establish
\int_{\Omega}\left\|f\right\|_{C^{3}(\zeta)}^{3p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)\leq C
. Notice that
\left\|f\right\|_{C^{3}(\zeta)}\leq C\max_{|\gamma|=0,\dots,3}\sup_{z\in\zeta}\left|\partial^{\gamma}f(z)\right|\leq C\max_{\left|\gamma\right|=0,\dots,3}(\sup_{z\in\zeta_{1}}\left|\partial^{\gamma}f(z)\right|+\sup_{z\in\tilde{\zeta}_{1}}\left|\partial^{\gamma}f(z)\right|)
for
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{D_{2}}
, which gives that the first factor is bounded via

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left\|f\right\|_{C^{3}(\zeta)}^{3p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)

\displaystyle\leq C\max_{\left|\gamma\right|=0,\dots,3}\Bigg(\int_{\Omega}\sup_{s,t\in[0,1]}\sup_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\left|\partial^{\gamma}f((1-\lambda)Z_{s}(\omega,y_{1})+\lambda Z_{t}(\omega,y_{1}))\right|^{3p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{\Omega}\sup_{s,t\in[0,1]}\sup_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\left|\partial^{\gamma}f((1-\lambda)Z_{s}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1})+\lambda Z_{t}(\omega,\tilde{y}_{1}))\right|^{3p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)\Bigg).

Of course, it suffices to show that, for a fixed
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{D_{2}}
with
|\gamma|\in\{0,\dots,3\}
, both of the two remaining summands are bounded by
C
.
The integrands coincide syntactically upon disregarding the tilde, which is why it is enough to study the first summand. As
\partial^{\gamma}f:\mathbb{R}^{D_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}^{D_{3}}
and
[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{D_{2}},\,\lambda\mapsto\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y
are polynomials, so too is
[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{D_{3}},\,\lambda\mapsto\partial^{\gamma}f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)
, where
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{2}}
. We utilize a standard Bernstein basis expansion
that leads to

\displaystyle\partial^{\gamma}f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)=\sum_{i=0}^{\deg\partial^{\gamma}f}\sum_{j=0}^{\deg\partial^{\gamma}f-i}\lambda^{i}(1-\lambda)^{j}(\partial^{\gamma}f)_{i,j}(x,y),

where each
(\partial^{\gamma}f)_{i,j}:\mathbb{R}^{D_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{D_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}^{D_{3}}
is some suitable polynomial.
Inserting this formula into the first summand entails

\displaystyle\quad\int_{\Omega}\sup_{s,t\in[0,1]}\sup_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\left|\partial^{\gamma}f((1-\lambda)Z_{s}(\omega,y_{1})+\lambda Z_{t}(\omega,y_{1}))\right|^{3p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\sup_{s,t\in[0,1]}\sup_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\left|\sum_{i=0}^{\deg\partial^{\gamma}f}\sum_{j=0}^{\deg\partial^{\gamma}f-i}\lambda^{i}(1-\lambda)^{j}(\partial^{\gamma}f)_{i,j}(Z_{s}(\omega,y_{1}),Z_{t}(\omega,y_{1}))\right|^{3p}\,\mathrm{d}P(\omega).

With
\lambda^{i}(1-\lambda)^{j}\in[0,2]
and the fact that
(\partial^{\gamma}f)_{i,j}
is a polynomial, standard procedures via elementary inequalities, Hölder’s inequality and Lemma
5.2
let us conclude that the first summand is
\leq C
, which completes the proof.

A.5

,

: The Invariance Property

For the existence, we have to verify that
\varphi_{1}(t,\omega)\xi_{1}(\omega)=\xi_{1}(\theta_{t}\omega)
, or equivalently that
\mathrm{pr}^{s}_{1}(\theta_{t}\omega)\varphi_{1}(t,\omega)\xi_{1}(\omega)=\xi_{1}^{s}(\theta_{t}\omega)
and
\mathrm{pr}^{u}_{1}(\theta_{t}\omega)\varphi_{1}(t,\omega)\xi_{1}(\omega)=\xi_{1}^{u}(\theta_{t}\omega)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. For brevity, we only deal with the latter, but the former follows by a similar argument.
First, we present the decisive calculation that achieves the invariance claim. This manipulation is quite delicate, which is why we ought to treat it very carefully and slowly. Still, some equality signs might raise some further questions that we will resolve afterwards. Also, we focus on
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
; the case
t\in\mathbb{R}_{<0}
works analogously. We compute

\displaystyle\quad\xi^{u}_{1}(\theta_{t}\omega)

\displaystyle\overset{(1)}{=}-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{pr}^{u}_{1}(\theta_{t}\omega)\left(\Phi_{1}(k,\theta_{t}\omega)^{-1}X^{+}_{1}(\theta_{t+k}\omega)\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(3)}{=}-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{pr}^{u}_{1}(\theta_{t}\omega)\left(\int_{t}^{t+1}\Phi_{1}(u+k-t,\theta_{t}\omega)^{-1}P^{1}_{0}\,\mathrm{d}u+\int_{t}^{t+1}\Phi_{1}(u+k-t,\theta_{t}\omega)^{-1}P^{1}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}(\theta_{k}\omega)\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(4)}{=}-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{pr}^{u}_{1}(\theta_{t}\omega)\left(\int_{t}^{t+1}\Phi_{1}(t,\omega)\Phi_{1}(u+k,\omega)^{-1}P^{1}_{0}\,\mathrm{d}u+\int_{t}^{t+1}\Phi_{1}(t,\omega)\Phi_{1}(u+k,\omega)^{-1}P^{1}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}(\theta_{k}\omega)\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(5)}{=}-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Phi_{1}(t,\omega)\mathrm{pr}^{u}_{1}(\omega)\left(\int_{t}^{t+1}\Phi_{1}(u+k,\omega)^{-1}P^{1}_{0}\,\mathrm{d}u+\int_{t}^{t+1}\Phi_{1}(u+k,\omega)^{-1}P^{1}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}(\theta_{k}\omega)\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(6)}{=}\Phi_{1}(t,\omega)\Bigg(-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{pr}^{u}_{1}(\omega)\left(\int_{0}^{1}\Phi_{1}(u+k,\omega)^{-1}P^{1}_{0}\,\mathrm{d}u+\int_{0}^{1}\Phi_{1}(u+k,\omega)^{-1}P^{1}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}(\theta_{k}\omega)\right)

In (1), we just write down the definition of

For (2), given partitions

\displaystyle\quad\Phi_{1}(k,\theta_{t}\omega)^{-1}X_{1}^{+}(\theta_{t+k}\omega)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathclap{(2.1)}}{=}\Phi_{1}(k,\theta_{t}\omega)^{-1}\bigg(\int_{0}^{1}\Phi_{1}(u,\theta_{t+k}\omega)^{-1}P^{1}_{0}\,\mathrm{d}u+\int_{0}^{1}\Phi_{1}(u,\theta_{t+k}\omega)^{-1}P^{1}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{u}(\theta_{t+k}\omega)\bigg)

Identity (2.1) is just the definition of
