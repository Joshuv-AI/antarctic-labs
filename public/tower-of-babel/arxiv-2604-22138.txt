Global Convergence of Policy Gradient Methods for ReLU Controllers in Linear Quadratic Regulation
Jhojan A. Rodriguez-Gil, César A. Uribe

Abstract
We study the convergence of model-based policy gradient for the deterministic, scalar, discounted linear-quadratic regulator when the controller is an overparameterized one-hidden-layer ReLU network without biases. Although the optimal LQR controller is linear, neural parameterization creates a redundant nonconvex weight space with a possibly asymmetric piecewise-linear controller. We show that this structure can still be analyzed exactly through the two effective gains induced on the positive and negative half-lines. Under suitable random initialization, sufficient width, and a small step size, the model-based policy gradient remains stable, decreases the cost geometrically, and drives the effective gains to the unique optimal scalar LQR gain with high probability.



I
Introduction

The linear quadratic regulator (LQR) is a canonical problem in optimal control because it admits a unique optimal linear state-feedback law and a closed-form solution through the Riccati equation
[
9
,
2
]
. It is also a natural benchmark for reinforcement learning and policy optimization, since one can search directly over a parameterized controller instead of first solving a model-based dynamic program. For the exact class of linear controllers, this viewpoint is now well understood: policy-gradient methods converge globally to the optimal LQR gain under suitable assumptions, and broader analyses identify structural conditions under which policy-gradient objectives have no spurious stationary points or satisfy gradient-dominance properties
[
7
,
1
,
4
]
. Notably, if the policy class is fixed to linear controllers, one can directly parameterize the feedback gain and apply policy-gradient methods that provably converge to the optimal LQR controller under suitable assumptions
[
7
]
. Other results have also addressed the sample complexity of stochastic LQR
[
12
]
, and adaptive control methods with policy gradient
[
18
]
.

The situation is much less understood when the controller is parameterized by a neural network. General neural policy-gradient theory in overparameterized actor–critic settings provides global-optimality or stationary-point guarantees in broader Markov decision processes
[
15
]
, but these results do not address the closed-loop stability issues that are central in control. In the LQR setting, moving beyond linear policies brings other complexities: extending policy search to piecewise-linear controllers can create local minima for standard policy gradient, while positive results in broader nonlinear policy classes typically rely on additional continuation or homotopy mechanisms rather than standard fixed-discount policy gradient
[
5
]
. The closest overparameterized LQR convergence result for neural-network parameterizations that we are aware of concerns linear feedforward networks, not ReLU policies
[
6
]
.

In this paper, we show that a ReLU parameterization admits a controller-level analysis in deterministic, scalar, discounted LQR, and that sufficiently wide policy gradients still preserve stability and converge to the optimal linear LQR controller. This choice is natural because ReLU networks are expressive and piecewise linear
[
16
,
3
]
; in the scalar case, however, it imposes a very specific structure: the controller acts as one gain on
x\geq 0
and another gain on
x<0
. This places the problem outside the linear-controller setting studied in classical LQR policy-gradient analyses
[
7
,
1
,
4
]
. Although the optimal LQR controller remains linear, the ReLU parameterization introduces a redundant, nonconvex weight space and a piecewise-linear controller with sign-dependent closed-loop dynamics. As a result, the main issue is not representability but optimization through a nonlinear parameterization. This is nontrivial for three reasons. First, policy gradient updates the neural weights rather than the effective controller gains directly. Second, the ReLU sign pattern couples the weight dynamics to closed-loop stability, so stability during training must be proved rather than assumed. Third, even though the target controller is linear, the search space includes asymmetric piecewise-linear controllers, and one must show that gradient descent drives the two induced gains to the same optimal value rather than converging to a suboptimal nonlinear policy. The main contributions of this paper are:

•

We characterize the bias-free scalar ReLU controller as a two-gain piecewise-linear policy and derive its controller-level stability and cost geometry. In the safe set, the cost-to-go is piecewise quadratic and can be analyzed directly in terms of the effective gains on the two half-lines.

•

We show that under Gaussian initialization and sufficient width, the induced initial controller is stabilizing with high probability.

•

Under a suitable overparameterized scaling and a sufficiently small step size, we prove that model-based policy gradient preserves stability for all iterates, yields geometric decay of the performance gap, and converges with high probability to the unique optimal linear LQR controller.

and
IV
present controller- and weight-level analyses. Then, Section
V
proves convergence of the sequence of controllers. Section
VI
provides numerical simulations, and Section

II
Problem Formulation and Main Result

Consider the deterministic scalar discrete-time system

x_{t+1}=ax_{t}+bu_{t},\quad t=0,1,2,\dots,

(1)

where
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
is the state,
u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
is the input, and
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
are fixed system parameters. Given an initial state

\begin{array}[]{cl}\underset{u_{0},u_{1},\ldots}{\min}&\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\rho}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\bigl(qx_{t}^{2}+ru_{t}^{2}\bigr)\right]\\[1.99997pt]
\text{s.t.}&x_{t+1}=ax_{t}+bu_{t},\qquad t=0,1,2,\ldots\end{array}

(2)

with
q>0
,
r>0
, and
\gamma\in(0,1)
. We say that a feedback law
\mu:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is stabilizing if the corresponding closed-loop trajectory satisfies
x_{t}\to 0
for every initial condition. For any stabilizing policy
\mu
, the cost-to-go from state

is

J_{\mu}(x)=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\left(qx_{t}^{2}+r\mu(x_{t})^{2}\right),\qquad x_{0}=x.

(3)

Hence, the feedback-design problem can be written as

\displaystyle\underset{\mu\in\mathcal{F}}{\min}\ \mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\rho}\left[J_{\mu}(x_{0})\right]\ \st\ x_{t{+}1}{=}ax_{t}+b\mu(x_{t}),

(4)

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the class of stabilizing deterministic state-feedback laws. For the scalar LQR problem, classical theory implies that the unique optimal policy is linear, namely
\mu^{\star}(x)=K^{\star}x
for the optimal LQR gain
K^{\star}

[
9
,
2
]
.

Assumption 1
.

System (
1
) is controllable, i.e.,
b\neq 0
.

Assumption 2
.

There exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that
|a|\leq 1-\delta_{0}
.

Assumption 3
.

There exists
\sigma_{\rho}>0
such that
\mathbb{E}\!\left[x_{0}^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{x_{0}\geq 0\}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[x_{0}^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{x_{0}<0\}}\right]\triangleq{\sigma_{\rho}^{2}}/{2}
.

Assumption
2
is stronger than stabilizability and is used later to justify random initialization: it guarantees that sufficiently small feedback gains remain stabilizing with a uniform margin, so the policy-gradient iterates can be started inside a safe set. Assumption
3
is exactly the moment condition used later to lower bound the positive- and negative-side occupancy weights.

We parameterize the controller by a bias-free one-hidden-layer ReLU network with width
m
,

\mu_{\theta}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\sigma(w_{i}x),\qquad\sigma(y)=\max\{0,y\},

(5)

where
\theta=\{w_{1},\dots,w_{m},v_{1},\dots,v_{m}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}
. Because the state is scalar and no biases are used, the network has a single breakpoint at the origin. Let
{I}=\{1,\dots,m\}
,
{J}_{1}\coloneqq\{j\in{I}:w_{j}\geq 0\}
, and
{J}_{2}\coloneqq\{j\in{I}:w_{j}<0\}
. Neurons with
w_{j}=0
can be omitted. Then

\mu_{\theta}(x)=\begin{cases}K_{1,\theta}x,&x\geq 0,\\
K_{2,\theta}x,&x<0,\end{cases}

(6)

where
K_{1,\theta}\coloneqq\sum_{j\in{J}_{1}}v_{j}w_{j}
, and
K_{2,\theta}\coloneqq\sum_{j\in{J}_{2}}v_{j}w_{j}
.
Thus, in the scalar bias-free case, the ReLU controller is completely characterized by the two effective gains
(K_{1,\theta},K_{2,\theta})
. Moreover, when
m\geq 2
, this parameterization contains every linear controller
u=Kx
by activating one neuron on each half-line; in particular, it contains the optimal LQR controller
(K^{\star},K^{\star})
.

Example 1
.

Although the controller-level objective is naturally expressed in terms of the two gains
(K_{1,\theta},K_{2,\theta})
, its combination with the ReLU weights is redundant. This redundancy can create flat directions, local degeneracy, and poorly conditioned, plateau-like regions in weight space, so progress in one effective gain need not produce comparable progress in the other
[
5
,
17
,
11
]
. For example,
(K_{1},K_{2})=(1,2)
is realized by
(w_{1},w_{2},v_{1},v_{2})=(1,-1,1,-2)
, and by infinitely many other choices such as
(a,-b,a^{-1},-2b^{-1})
with
a,b>0
. If one sign group is missing at initialization, then one effective gain is locally absent; e.g., if
m=1
and
w_{1}>0
, then locally
K_{2,\theta}=0
, so gradient updates can move only
K_{1,\theta}
until the neuron crosses zero.

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, define the stability-margin set as

\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\coloneqq\left\{\mu_{\theta}:|a+bK_{1,\theta}|\leq 1-\delta,\;|a+bK_{2,\theta}|\leq 1-\delta\right\}.

We also define the safe set
\mathcal{K}_{\overline{\delta}}
with
\overline{\delta}\triangleq\delta/2
. This set serves as the stability buffer in the analysis; later, we show that the relevant policy-gradient iterates remain within it.

Restricting attention to controllers in
\mathcal{K}_{\overline{\delta}}
, the parameterized policy-search problem becomes

\begin{array}[]{cl}\underset{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}}{\min}&\mathcal{J}_{\rho}(\theta)\triangleq\mathcal{J}_{\rho}(\mu_{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\rho}[J_{\mu_{\theta}}(x_{0})]\\[1.99997pt]
\text{s.t.}&x_{t+1}=ax_{t}+b\mu_{\theta}(x_{t}).\end{array}

(7)

Because the optimal linear controller is representable by the ReLU parameterization, the main issue is not expressivity but optimization: policy gradient operates in the redundant and nonconvex weight space
\theta
, rather than directly on the two controller gains. The next section analyzes this controller landscape and its relationship to the induced neural network optimization problem.

(iii) (PL-Inequality) There exists

Proof.

1

For (ii),

\mathcal{C}^{1}

[

holds.
By Proposition

By Lemma

Also, by (i) and (ii),

For (iv),
|K_{i}|\leq K_{\max}

IV
ReLU Landscape

As a next step, we study the neural-network landscape induced by the map
\theta\mapsto(K_{1,\theta},K_{2,\theta})
, where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}
and
K_{1,\theta},K_{2,\theta}\in\mathbb{R}
. In principle, the pair
(K_{1,\theta},K_{2,\theta})
can be represented with only a few parameters. In particular, for system (
1
), one may use
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
. However, the two effective gains are generated by different subsets of neurons, depending on the signs of their first-layer weights. As a consequence, a small network may represent one side well while providing little flexibility on the other.

The next lemma shows that the NTK-scaled Gaussian initialization places the initial controller in the stabilizing set with exponentially high probability.

Lemma 4
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, consider the system (
1
) and the control law (
6
). Initialize the network independently as
w_{j,0}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,{\beta}/{m}\right)
, and
v_{j,0}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,{\alpha}/{m}\right)
, for
j=1,\dots,m
. Fix any
\delta
such that
0<\delta<\delta_{0}
. Then there exist constants
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
C_{1}>0
, depending only on
\alpha,\beta,\delta,\delta_{0}
, and
|b|
, such that for all
m\geq m_{0}
,
\mathbb{P}\left(|a+bK_{1,\theta_{0}}|\leq 1-\delta,\;|a+bK_{2,\theta_{0}}|\leq 1-\delta\right)\geq 1-e^{-C_{1}m}
.

Proof.

Let
\Delta\triangleq\delta_{0}-\delta>0
and
\varepsilon\triangleq\Delta/|b|
. Since
|a|\leq 1-\delta_{0}
, if
|K_{i,\theta_{0}}|\leq\varepsilon
, then
|a+bK_{i,\theta_{0}}|\leq|a|{+}|b|\,|K_{i,\theta_{0}}|\leq(1-\delta_{0})+\Delta{=}1{-}\delta,\ i\in\{1,2\}
. Thus it suffices to control
K_{1,\theta_{0}}
and
K_{2,\theta_{0}}
. Define
X_{j}\triangleq v_{j,0}w_{j,0}\mathds{1}_{\{w_{j,0}\geq 0\}}
and
Y_{j}\triangleq v_{j,0}w_{j,0}\mathds{1}_{\{w_{j,0}<0\}}
. Then
K_{1,\theta_{0}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{j}
and
K_{2,\theta_{0}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}Y_{j}
. Since
v_{j,0}
is independent of
w_{j,0}
and
\mathbb{E}[v_{j,0}]=0
, we have
\mathbb{E}[X_{j}]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{j}]=0
. Moreover,
w_{j,0}
and
v_{j,0}
are centered subgaussian with
\|w_{j,0}\|_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim\sqrt{\beta/m}
and
\|v_{j,0}\|_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim\sqrt{\alpha/m}

\|X_{j}\|_{\psi_{1}},\|Y_{j}\|_{\psi_{1}}\lesssim\sqrt{\alpha\beta}/m

[
14
]

C_{1}>0
, depending only on
\alpha,\beta,\delta,\delta_{0}
, and
|b|
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(|K_{i,\theta_{0}}|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq 2e^{-C_{1}m},\qquad i\in\{1,2\},

(24)

The desired result follows by applying the union bound.

Lemma 5
.

Under the initialization of Lemma
4
, select
\mathcal{R}>0
such that
\mathcal{R}^{2}>\alpha+\beta
. Then there exists
C_{2}>0
such that
\mathbb{P}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big(w_{j,0}^{2}+v_{j,0}^{2}\big)\leq\mathcal{R}^{2}\big)\geq 1-e^{-C_{2}m}
.

Proof.

Given the rule to select the initial weights, we have
\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j,0}^{2}=(\beta/m)\sum_{m}z_{j}^{2}
where
z_{j}\sim Z
, and

is the standard Gaussian distribution. Applying the same analysis
\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_{j,0}^{2}=(\alpha/m)\sum_{j=1}^{m}z_{j}^{2}

[
10

, one can get the upper-tail bound.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\{w_{j,0}\}_{j=1}^{m}
and
\{v_{j,0}\}_{j=1}^{m}
be initialized independently as in Lemma
4
. Fix constants
0<\tau<W<\infty
and

, and define
p\triangleq\big(\Phi\big({W}/{\sqrt{\beta}}\big)-\Phi\big({\tau}/{\sqrt{\beta}}\big)\big)\big(2\Phi\left({V}/{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)-1\big)
. Then, for every
c\in(0,p)
, there exists
C_{3}>0
such that
\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{C}_{1}|\geq cm\right)\geq 1-e^{-C_{3}m}
,
\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{C}_{2}|\geq cm\right)\geq 1-e^{-C_{3}m}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}

\displaystyle{\triangleq}\big\{j\in{I}\big|{\tau}/{\sqrt{m}}\leq w_{j,0}\leq{W}/{\sqrt{m}},\;|v_{j,0}|\leq{V}/{\sqrt{m}}\big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}

\displaystyle\triangleq\big\{j\in{I}\big|{-W}/{\sqrt{m}}\leq w_{j,0}\leq{-\tau}/{\sqrt{m}},\;|v_{j,0}|\leq{V}/{\sqrt{m}}\big\}.

Proof.

Let
X_{j}\triangleq\mathds{1}_{\{j\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\}}
. Since
w_{j,0}=\sqrt{\beta/m}\,Z_{j}^{(w)}
and
v_{j,0}=\sqrt{\alpha/m}\,Z_{j}^{(v)}
, with
Z_{j}^{(w)}
and
Z_{j}^{(v)}
independent standard Gaussians, we have
\mathbb{P}(j\in\mathcal{C}_{1}){=}\mathbb{P}\!\left({\tau}/{\sqrt{m}}{\leq}w_{j,0}{\leq}{W}/{\sqrt{m}}\right)\mathbb{P}\!\left(|v_{j,0}|{\leq}{V}/{\sqrt{m}}\right)=p
. By independence across
j
, the variables
\{X_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
are i.i.d. Bernoulli
(p)
, so
|\mathcal{C}_{1}|=\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{j}\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(m,p)
. Since
c<p
, Chernoff’s inequality yields a constant
C_{3}=C_{3}(c,p)>0
such that
\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{C}_{1}|<cm\right)\leq e^{-C_{3}m}
, hence
\mathbb{P}\!\left(|\mathcal{C}_{1}|\geq cm\right)\geq 1-e^{-C_{3}m}
. The same argument applies to
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. Indeed, by symmetry of the Gaussian law,
\mathbb{P}(j\in\mathcal{C}_{2})=\mathbb{P}(j\in\mathcal{C}_{1})=p
, so
|\mathcal{C}_{2}|\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(m,p)
and therefore
\mathbb{P}\!\left(|\mathcal{C}_{2}|\geq cm\right)\geq 1-e^{-C_{3}m}
.

Lemma
4
shows that
\theta_{0}
yields a stable initial controller, Lemma
5
controls the mass of the initial weigths and
Lemma
6
guarantees the existence of backbone index sets
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}

defined from the initialization. These index sets remain fixed throughout training, although the
corresponding weights
(w_{j,k},v_{j,k})
evolve with
k
. We now define the potentially problematic
neurons by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{w}{\triangleq}\left\{j\in{I}\,\Big|\,|w_{j,0}|{\leq}{\tau_{w}}/{\sqrt{m}}\right\},\ \mathcal{S}_{v}{\triangleq}\left\{j\in{I}\,\Big|\,|v_{j,0}|{>}{V}/{\sqrt{m}}\right\}.

Define the corresponding bad-set masses by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{w}\triangleq\sum_{j=1}^{m}(w_{j,0}^{2}+v_{j,0}^{2})\mathds{1}_{\{j\in\mathcal{S}_{w}\}},\ \mathcal{M}_{v}\triangleq\sum_{j=1}^{m}(w_{j,0}^{2}+v_{j,0}^{2})\mathds{1}_{\{j\in\mathcal{S}_{v}\}}.

For deterministic thresholds
M_{w},M_{v}>0
, define the event

\mathcal{E}_{\Xi}\triangleq\{\mathcal{M}_{w}\leq M_{w},\ \mathcal{M}_{v}\leq M_{v}\}.

Note that
\mathcal{M}_{w}
is defined from
\mathcal{S}_{w}
, not from
\mathcal{C}_{1}
, and both
\mathcal{M}_{w}
and
\mathcal{M}_{v}
are
random sums determined by the initialization.

Lemma 7
.

Suppose the initialization is Gaussian as in Lemma
4
. If
M_{w}>\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{M}_{w}]
and
M_{v}>\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{M}_{v}]
, then there exists a constant
C_{4}>0
such that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\Xi})\geq 1-e^{-C_{4}m}
.

Proof.

Set

Z_{j}^{(w)}\triangleq(w_{j,0}^{2}+v_{j,0}^{2})\mathds{1}_{\{j\in\mathcal{S}_{w}\}},\qquad Z_{j}^{(v)}\triangleq(w_{j,0}^{2}+v_{j,0}^{2})\mathds{1}_{\{j\in\mathcal{S}_{v}\}}.

Since the initialization is i.i.d. across
j
, the variables
\{Z_{j}^{(w)}\}_{j=1}^{m}
and

\{Z_{j}^{(v)}\}_{j=1}^{m}
are i.i.d. nonnegative subexponential random variables. Therefore
Bernstein’s inequality yields constants
c_{w},c_{v}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_{w}>M_{w})\leq e^{-c_{w}m},\qquad\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_{v}>M_{v})\leq e^{-c_{v}m}.

A union bound gives the result.

Remark 1
.

The ReLU derivative is undefined at zero, so we use the convention
\sigma^{\prime}(0)=0
. Under the initialization of Lemma
4
,
w_{j,0}\neq 0
almost surely for every
j
. Thus, we only care if a neuron crosses zero during training.

We now study policy-gradient updates in weight space, i.e., Eq. (
8
). By Lemma
3
, the controller-level objective
\mathcal{J}_{\rho}(\mu)
satisfies a PL inequality in
\mu=(K_{1},K_{2})
. However, its composition with the neural parameterization is nonconvex in
\theta
. The two gradients are related by the chain rule,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\rho}(\theta_{k})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{J}_{\rho}\left(\mu_{\theta_{k}}\right)}{\partial K_{1,\theta_{k}}}\nabla_{\theta}K_{1,\theta_{k}}+\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{J}_{\rho}\left(\mu_{\theta_{k}}\right)}{\partial K_{2,\theta_{k}}}\nabla_{\theta}K_{2,\theta_{k}},

(25)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{J}_{\mu}(\theta_{k})^{\textit{\footnotesize{T}}}\nabla_{\mu}\mathcal{J}_{\rho}(\mu_{\theta_{k}}),

(26)

where
\mathbf{J}_{\mu}(\theta_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2m}
is the Jacobian of the map
\theta\mapsto\mu_{\theta}=(K_{1,\theta},K_{2,\theta})
.
Let
{J}_{1,k}\triangleq\{j:w_{j,k}\geq 0\}
and
{J}_{2,k}\triangleq\{j:w_{j,k}<0\}
, we may write

\frac{\partial K_{1,\theta_{k}}}{\partial w_{j,k}}=v_{j,k}\mathds{1}_{\{j\in{J}_{1,k}\}},\quad\frac{\partial K_{1,\theta_{k}}}{\partial v_{j,k}}=w_{j,k}\mathds{1}_{\{j\in{J}_{1,k}\}},

\frac{\partial K_{2,\theta_{k}}}{\partial w_{j,k}}=v_{j,k}\mathds{1}_{\{j\in{J}_{2,k}\}},\quad\frac{\partial K_{2,\theta_{k}}}{\partial v_{j,k}}=w_{j,k}\mathds{1}_{\{j\in{J}_{2,k}\}}.

Stacking these derivatives gives the stated Jacobian. The row supports are disjoint because
{J}_{1,k}\cap{J}_{2,k}=\emptyset
. By defining
\theta_{k}=[\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\textit{\footnotesize{T}}}\ \mathbf{v}_{k}^{\textit{\footnotesize{T}}}]^{\textit{\footnotesize{T}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}
, with
\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathbf{v}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we get

\mathbf{J}_{\mu}(\theta_{k})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathds{1}_{\{\mathbf{w}_{k}\geq 0\}}\odot\mathbf{v}_{k}&\mathds{1}_{\{\mathbf{w}_{k}\geq 0\}}\odot\mathbf{w}_{k}\\
\mathds{1}_{\{\mathbf{w}_{k}<0\}}\odot\mathbf{v}_{k}&\mathds{1}_{\{\mathbf{w}_{k}<0\}}\odot\mathbf{w}_{k}\end{bmatrix},

where the indicators are understood entrywise. Let
g_{k}\triangleq\nabla_{\mu}\mathcal{J}_{\rho}(\mu_{\theta_{k}})=[g_{1,k},g_{2,k}]^{\textit{\footnotesize{T}}}
, then

\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}_{\rho}(\theta_{k})}{\partial w_{j,k}}=v_{j,k}\Big(g_{1,k}\mathds{1}_{\{j\in{J}_{1,k}\}}+g_{2,k}\mathds{1}_{\{j\in{J}_{2,k}\}}\Big),

\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}_{\rho}(\theta_{k})}{\partial v_{j,k}}=w_{j,k}\Big(g_{1,k}\mathds{1}_{\{j\in{J}_{1,k}\}}+g_{2,k}\mathds{1}_{\{j\in{J}_{2,k}\}}\Big).

Thus, each neuron is updated by the controller-gradient component associated with its current sign. Under (
8
), neurons may cross zero and move from one effective gain to the other. Define
{H}_{1,k}{\triangleq}\{j:w_{j,k}{\geq}0,\ w_{j,k+1}{<}0\}
,
{H}_{2,k}{\triangleq}\{j:w_{j,k}{<}0,\ w_{j,k+1}{\geq}0\}
. We measure this redistribution by the crossing mass

\Xi_{k}\triangleq\sum_{j\in{H}_{1,k}\cup{H}_{2,k}}v_{j,k}^{2}
.
Finally, define the masses on the positive and negative sides by

\mathcal{G}_{1,k}\triangleq\sum_{j\in{J}_{1,k}}(w_{j,k}^{2}+v_{j,k}^{2}),\qquad\mathcal{G}_{2,k}\triangleq\sum_{j\in{J}_{2,k}}(w_{j,k}^{2}+v_{j,k}^{2}).

These quantities will be used to control sign changes and mass redistribution during training. Define the “no-crossing” gains

\widetilde{K}_{1,k+1}\triangleq\sum_{j\in{J}_{1,k}}w_{j,k+1}v_{j,k+1},\quad\widetilde{K}_{2,k+1}\triangleq\sum_{j\in{J}_{2,k}}w_{j,k+1}v_{j,k+1},

the no-crossing controller
\widetilde{\mu}_{k+1}\triangleq[\widetilde{K}_{1,k+1},\widetilde{K}_{2,k+1}]^{\textit{\footnotesize{T}}}
,
and the crossing error vector
\mathbf{e}_{k}\triangleq[K_{1,\theta_{k+1}}-\widetilde{K}_{1,k+1},K_{2,\theta_{k+1}}-\widetilde{K}_{2,k+1}]^{\textit{\footnotesize{T}}}
. Further define
D_{k}\triangleq\mathrm{diag}(\mathcal{G}_{1,k},\mathcal{G}_{2,k})
,
h_{k}\triangleq[K_{1,\theta_{k}}g_{1,k}^{2},K_{2,\theta_{k}}g_{2,k}^{2}]^{\textit{\footnotesize{T}}}
.

Proposition 2
.

With the above notation,
\widetilde{\mu}_{\theta_{k+1}}=\mu_{\theta_{k}}-\eta_{m}D_{k}g_{k}+\eta_{m}^{2}h_{k}
,
and therefore
\mu_{\theta_{k+1}}=\mu_{\theta_{k}}-\eta_{m}D_{k}g_{k}+\eta_{m}^{2}h_{k}+\mathbf{e}_{k}

Proof.

If
j\in{J}_{1,k}
, then
w_{j,k+1}=w_{j,k}-\eta_{m}v_{j,k}g_{1,k}

v_{j,k+1}=v_{j,k}-\eta_{m}w_{j,k}g_{1,k}

w_{j,k+1}v_{j,k+1}=w_{j,k}v_{j,k}-\eta_{m}g_{1,k}(w_{j,k}^{2}+v_{j,k}^{2})+\eta_{m}^{2}g_{1,k}^{2}w_{j,k}v_{j,k}.

Summing over
j\in{J}_{1,k}
, and similarly for
{J}_{2,k}
yields

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{1,k+1}

\displaystyle=K_{1,\theta_{k}}-\eta_{m}\mathcal{G}_{1,k}g_{1,k}+\eta_{m}^{2}K_{1,\theta_{k}}g_{1,k}^{2}

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{2,k+1}

\displaystyle=K_{2,\theta_{k}}-\eta_{m}\mathcal{G}_{2,k}g_{2,k}+\eta_{m}^{2}K_{2,\theta_{k}}g_{2,k}^{2}.

Stacking the two identities proves the first equality, and the second follows from the definition of

\mathbf{e}_{k}
.

Lemma 8
.

Assume
\mu_{\theta_{k}}\in\mathcal{K}_{\overline{\delta}}

\eta_{m}G_{\max}\leq 1

\|\mathbf{e}_{k}\|_{2}\leq 2\sqrt{2}\,\eta_{m}\,\|g_{k}\|_{2}\,\Xi_{k}

Consider

v^{+}=v_{j,k+1}
. Then

,
w^{+}<0

w^{+}=w-h_{k}^{(1)}v

|v^{+}|\leq|v|+|h_{k}^{(1)}|\,|w|\leq(1+|h_{k}^{(1)}|^{2})|v|\leq 2|v|,

The contribution of neuron

\mathbf{e}_{j,k}{=}[{-}w^{+}v^{+},w^{+}v^{+}]^{\textit{\footnotesize{T}}},\ \|\mathbf{e}_{j,k}\|_{2}{=}\sqrt{2}\,|w^{+}v^{+}|{\leq}2\sqrt{2}\,\eta_{m}\,|g_{1,k}|\,v_{j,k}^{2}.

The case

Lemma 9
.

Let

Proof.

Let
g_{t}\triangleq\nabla_{\mu}\mathcal{J}_{\rho}(\mu_{\theta_{t}})
and

r_{j,s}\triangleq\sqrt{w_{j,s}^{2}+v_{j,s}^{2}}
. Writing
\mathbf{z}_{j,s}\triangleq[w_{j,s},v_{j,s}]^{\textit{\footnotesize{T}}}

\mathbf{z}_{j,s+1}=A(h_{i,s})\mathbf{z}_{j,s}

-h&1\end{bmatrix}
,

Iterating gives

Now take

|w_{j,0}|>\tau_{w}/\sqrt{m}
and
|v_{j,0}|\leq V/\sqrt{m}

\tau_{w}

cannot cross zero for any

, for
s=0,\dots,k

\mathcal{E}_{\Xi}

\displaystyle\Xi_{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in{H}_{1,s}\cup{H}_{2,s}}v_{j,s}^{2}\leq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{w}\cup\mathcal{S}_{v}}r_{j,s}^{2}\leq e^{2B}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{w}\cup\mathcal{S}_{v}}r_{j,0}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{2B}(\mathcal{M}_{w}+\mathcal{M}_{v})\leq e^{2B}(M_{w}+M_{v})\triangleq\Xi_{\ast}.

Proposition
2
shows that the contribution of the neurons in
{J}_{i,k}
is dominant in the effective gains at

bounds the norm of

and Lemma

VII
Conclusions and Future Work

This paper studied policy gradient for overparameterized ReLU controllers in the scalar discrete-time LQR problem. Under appropriate assumptions, we proved that, with high probability, the sequence of controllers induced during training converges to the optimal LQR controller. The main contribution is therefore a rigorous convergence guarantee for a nonlinearly parameterized neural policy in a classical control problem. Our analysis clarifies how the ReLU parameterization behaves at both the controller and weight levels, and the numerical experiments are consistent with the theory and illustrate the expected training behavior.

Several directions remain open. An important next step is a model-free extension in which policy gradients are estimated from trajectories generated by the current controller. It would also be valuable to extend the analysis to higher-dimensional systems and to include state and control constraints.
