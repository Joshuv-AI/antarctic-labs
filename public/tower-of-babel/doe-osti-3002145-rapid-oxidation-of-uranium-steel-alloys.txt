 ABSTRACT

This project explored the use of combustion synthesis as a rapid, high-temperature method for oxidizing
uranium-bearing steel alloys. Traditional laboratory-scale synthesis methods often fail to replicate the
thermal and kinetic conditions experienced by real-world particulates, particularly those formed under
rapid quenching or high-temperature scenarios. Combustion synthesis offers a promising alternative by
enabling fast, localized heating and flexible precursor selection. A series of targeted experiments were
conducted using a U₂NiCrFe₄ alloy as the precursor. The alloy was oxidized using combustion synthesis
reactions fueled by uranyl nitrate and glycine, achieving peak temperatures exceeding 1,200 °C.
Postreaction analysis using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), elemental mapping, and Raman
spectroscopy revealed the formation of iron-based oxides, with limited but detectable evidence of
uranium oxide phases such as UO₂.

The results indicate that under the rapid reaction and cooling conditions of combustion synthesis, iron
oxides form preferentially, but uranium oxide formation is kinetically limited. These findings validate
combustion synthesis as a viable method for simulating the oxidation behavior of uranium steels in
extreme environments and lay the groundwork for future studies aimed at enhancing uranium oxide
formation through higher temperatures or modified precursor compositions.

 1) BACKGROUND

A persistent challenge in materials research is bridging the gap between controlled laboratory-scale
synthesis and formation conditions relevant to applied environments. While laboratory-scale synthesis
often yields high-quality materials that provide critical insights into formation mechanisms, applying
these findings to real-world particulates requires significant assumptions. Specifically, replicating the
potential high temperatures, pressures, and metastable states observed in collected particulates is difficult.
Combustion synthesis offers a promising route to simulate some of these conditions in a semicontrolled
form. It enables rapid, high-temperature reactions and allows for flexible precursor selection, including
specific alloys for targeted oxidation studies.

This Laboratory Directed Research and Development project conducted a focused set of experiments to

1. oxidize targeted U-steel alloys under extreme conditions, while capturing quantifiable thermal
 profiles during combustion synthesis and

2. identify the resulting oxide phases formed during rapid, high-temperature oxidation.

The overarching goal was to assess the viability of combustion synthesis as a method for producing
relevant metal-oxide phases from uranium-containing alloys under nonequilibrium, extreme-environment
conditions.

1.1 PREPARATION OF PRECURSOR MATERIALS

Precursor selection was guided by projected relevance to applied projects and identified uranium steel–
based materials as high priorities. Here, we report experiments targeting U₂NiCrFe₄, which was
synthesized by arc melting in an inert environment. The resulting ingot was broken into small pieces,
which were used for combustion oxidation.

1.2 COMBUSTION SYNTHESIS

Combustion syntheses were carried out using an aqueous uranyl nitrate-glycine system. Solid reagents
were dissolved in water in a 1.5 molar ratio of glycine (Gly) to uranyl nitrate (UO2(NO3)2). The resulting
solution was dried overnight yielding a malleable gel in which the precursor metal was incorporated. The
loaded gel was placed into a glass reaction vessel with constant light flowing air and heated using a hot
plate. Reaction temperatures were measured using K-type thermocouples. Figure 1 shows high-speed
imaging of a combustion reaction used within the study, as well as thermograms for both combustion
reactions. Following combustion, the alloy fragment was removed and rinsed with acetone to remove char
contaminants.

1.3 CHARACTERIZATION METHODS

Electron microscopy was performed using a Zeiss Gemini 2 scanning electron microscope equipped with
a backscatter electron detector. Imaging was conducted at an excitation voltage of 20 kV, providing
compositional contrast suitable for identifying phase distributions in the alloy and oxidized samples.

Raman spectroscopy was carried out using a Renishaw InVia system with a 785 nm excitation wavelength
and a 2,400 lines/mm grating, achieving a spectral resolution of approximately 2 cm⁻1. To minimize
sample heating and potential damage, the laser power was maintained at less than 0.5 mW for all
measurements. A pinhole aperture was used to reduce the line focus to a tightly focused spot, and all
spectra were collected using a long-working-distance 50× objective.

 2) OXIDE FORMATION BY COMBUSTION SYNTHESIS

2.1 OXIDE FORMATION VIA COMBUSTION SYNTHESIS

Figure 2 presents SEM images of the U₂NiCrFe₄ alloy before and after combustion synthesis of sample
SCS-2. The precursor material exhibits a multiphase microstructure, evident from the contrast between
brighter and darker regions in the backscattered electron images. Following combustion, three distinct
regions are observed: a bright central region—presumed to be uranium-rich due to its higher atomic
number contrast—a relatively uniform gray matrix, and a darker region likely associated with residual
carbonaceous material from the reaction. Elemental area mapping (Error! Reference source not found.)
confirms a substantial increase in oxygen content postreaction, but the relative proportions of the metallic
elements remain largely unchanged. Detailed compositional analysis of the bright central region was not
pursued because Raman spectroscopy indicated the presence of only magnetite (Fe₃O₄) in all areas
following oxidation.

Figure 2. SEM images of the U₂NiCrFe₄ sample from SCS-2, shown (left) before combustion and (right) after
combustion. Postcombustion images reveal changes in surface morphology consistent with oxidation. Green boxes
 in the lower panel indicate regions where elemental compositions were measured via area mapping.

In the SCS-1 sample, more pronounced compositional heterogeneity is observed across the surface. Three
regions can again be distinguished: a bright edge, a dark interior, and a bright interior region (Figure 3).
Compositional analysis reveals that the edge region most closely resembles the original alloy
composition, suggesting limited oxidation. The dark interior region is enriched in iron and depleted in
other alloying elements, and the bright interior region is comparatively rich in chromium and deficient in
iron. These variations suggest localized differences in oxidation behavior, possibly driven by thermal
gradients, reaction kinetics during combustion, or the precursor heterogeneity.

2.2 RAMAN SPECTROSCOPY ANALYSIS

To determine the specific oxide phases formed on the alloy surface, Raman spectroscopy was employed
using a 785 nm excitation wavelength, with laser power kept at less than 500 µW to avoid thermal
damage. Representative spectra are shown in Figure 4. For the SCS-2 sample, all spectra exhibited a
dominant peak near 670 cm⁻¹, which was occasionally accompanied by a weaker feature around 550 cm⁻¹.
This spectral signature is consistent with the presence of Fe₃O₄ (Testa-Anta, et al. 2019), suggesting that
rapid oxidation under high-temperature conditions favored the formation of this phase.

Figure 4. (Top left) Optical image collected for SCS-2. The boxes indicate the regions where Raman spectra were
collected and are color coded with the Raman spectra shown on the right. (Bottom left) SEM image reproduced for
 SCS-1. The boxes are color coded with the regions where Raman spectra were acquired.

In contrast, the Raman spectra from SCS-1 revealed more complex behavior. The dark interior region
(purple trace) showed features consistent with hematite (Fe2O3), and traces of Fe₃O₄ were still detectable.
The bright interior region also exhibited signatures of both Fe2O3 and Fe₃O₄, but additional peaks
appeared near 730 and 800 cm⁻¹. These peaks have not yet been assigned and do not correspond to
common iron or uranium oxides. Rather, we speculate that these modes correspond to a ternary or higher
order uranium metal oxide.

The bright edge region of the SCS-1 sample displayed Raman features consistent with Fe₃O₄, along with a
minor peak near 445 cm⁻¹. This latter peak aligns with reported values for UO2, suggesting localized
formation of UO2. Notably, the absence of Raman bands near 580 and 1,150 cm⁻¹ may reflect differences
in the oxidation state, crystallinity, or rapid quenching conditions that limited phase evolution.

The oxidation behavior of steels has been extensively studied under controlled conditions (Mamede, et al.
2016). Typically, nickel remains in the metallic state and migrates away from the surface, and the final
outer oxide layer is composed of Fe2O3, often with some chromium substitution on the iron sites. Fe₃O₄ is
generally considered a precursor phase in the transformation to Fe2O3. Most literature studies focus on
oxidation over longer timescales (i.e., minutes) allowing sufficient time for this phase transformation to
occur.

In contrast, the oxidation behavior of uranium-based alloys, particularly U–M systems (where M = Fe, Cr,
Ni), is less well characterized. Some studies have examined compounds such as UNi₂ and UFe₂ (Gouder,
et al. 1992), (Bonino, et al. 2001). In the case of UNi₂, nickel remains metallic and is removed from the
surface, similar to behavior observed in steels. For UFe₂, oxidation results in a mixed oxide layer
containing uranium oxides, iron oxides, and uranium–iron mixed oxides.

Based on these findings, nickel would be expected to be depleted from the surface during oxidation of U-
steels, and the resulting oxide layer would be expected to consist of a mixture of (Fe,Cr)₂O₃, (Fe,Cr)₃O₄,
UO₂₊ₓ, and potentially U(Fe,Cr)O₄. The formation of (Fe,Cr)₃O₄ is likely favored under rapid reaction
conditions, where insufficient time is available for transformation to the more thermodynamically stable
(Fe,Cr)₂O₃. Therefore, the exclusive observation of (Fe,Cr)₃O₄ in the SCS-2 sample suggests either rapid
quenching or that the actual temperature at the sample location was lower than the thermocouple reading
indicated.

In contrast, the oxide phases observed in SCS-1 are more varied. Large regions are consistent with both
(Fe,Cr)₂O₃ and (Fe,Cr)₃O₄, and there is some evidence for the formation of UO₂. The bright interior region
shows a general decrease in nickel-content, consistent with the expectations above. However, no spectral
features corresponding to U(Fe,Cr)O₄ were detected; such a phase would be expected to exhibit a
prominent Raman peak at or below 700 cm⁻¹, depending on its exact composition (Akiyama, et al. 2022).
It is somewhat surprising that uranium oxide phases are less prominent than their iron-based counterparts.
This may be attributed to the short reaction timescales, which likely prevents the system from reaching
equilibrium. The current results suggest that under the conditions investigated, iron-based oxides are
preferentially formed. Future work will explore the effect of achieving higher peak temperatures, which
may promote the formation of more readily detectable uranium oxide phases.

 3) CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrates the feasibility of using combustion synthesis to oxidize uranium-bearing steel
alloys under rapid, high-temperature conditions. Through a series of focused experiments, we
successfully oxidized U₂NiCrFe₄ alloy samples and characterized the resulting oxide phases using SEM,
elemental mapping, and Raman spectroscopy. The results show that combustion synthesis can achieve

temperatures exceeding 1,200 °C and induce rapid quenching, creating conditions relevant to those
encountered in extreme environments.

The oxidation behavior observed in these experiments aligns with expectations based on prior studies of
iron- and uranium-based systems. In particular, the formation of Fe₃O₄ and Fe₂O₃ was confirmed, with
evidence suggesting that rapid reaction kinetics favor the retention of Fe₃O₄. Although uranium oxide
phases were detected in some regions, their presence was limited compared to iron-based oxides. This
disparity likely reflects the short reaction timescales and rapid cooling, which inhibit the formation of
more thermodynamically stable or complex uranium oxide phases.

These findings support the viability of combustion synthesis as a tool for generating relevant oxide
products from uranium alloys under nonequilibrium conditions. Future work will focus on extending the
accessible temperature range and refining precursor compositions to promote the formation of uranium-
rich oxide phases. Such efforts will enhance our ability to simulate and understand the behavior of
actinide-containing materials in extreme environments, which has implications for nuclear forensics,
materials aging, and accident scenario modeling.
