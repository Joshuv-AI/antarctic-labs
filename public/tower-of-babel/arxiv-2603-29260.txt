Unexpected toric Richardson varieties
Eugene Gorsky, Soyeon Kim, Melissa Sherman-Bennett

Abstract
. We prove that an open Richardson variety in the complete flag variety for GL n \mathrm{GL}_{n} is isomorphic to a torus if and only if the corresponding closed Richardson variety is toric. Such toric varieties can be classified in terms of the combinatorics of Bruhat intervals, and include many varieties of dimension larger than n − 1 n-1 . We give a combinatorial description of the corresponding polytopes, and compute several explicit examples.



1.
Introduction

Richardson varieties arise in various contexts in algebraic geometry and combinatorics, including Schubert calculus, total positivity, and cluster algebras. Given two permutations
v,w\in S_{n}
such that
v\leq w
in Bruhat order, the
open Richardson variety

R_{v,w}
is the intersection of the opposite Schubert cell for
v
and the Schubert cell for
w
. Open Richardson varieties are smooth, affine, irreducible varieties, and
\dim R_{v,w}=\ell(w)-\ell(v)
. The
closed Richardson variety

\overline{R}_{v,w}
is the Zariski closure of
R_{v,w}
in the complete flag variety
\mathrm{Fl}(n)
. This paper is concerned with the closed Richardson varieties which are toric varieties. Recall that
\overline{R}_{v,w}
is
\mathbb{T}
-toric for some torus
\mathbb{T}
if there is a
\mathbb{T}
-action on
\overline{R}_{v,w}
with an open orbit. We say
\overline{R}_{v,w}
is toric if it is
\mathbb{T}
-toric for some
\mathbb{T}
.

The
(n-1)
-dimensional torus
T\subset\mathrm{SL}(n)
of diagonal matrices with determinant 1 acts on the flag variety and on all Richardson varieties. It is well-known (see
[
2
,
8
,
15
,
20
,
30
,
37
]
and references therein) that

is
T
-toric if and only if the roots associated to any maximal chain from

to
w
are linearly independent. By construction, all
T
-toric Richardson varieties have dimension at most
n-1
.

It is also possible for a Richardson variety
\overline{R}_{v,w}
to be toric with respect to some other torus
\mathbb{T}
. This situation has been much less studied. As shown in
[
8
, Corollary 3.3]
, if
\overline{R}_{v,w}
is
\mathbb{T}
-toric, then the interval
[v,w]
is a lattice, or, equivalently, has no subintervals which are 2-crowns (a 2-crown is the Bruhat graph of

shown below).

The first main result of this paper is that, if the open Richardson variety
R_{v,w}
is a torus
\mathbb{T}
, then
\overline{R}_{v,w}
is a toric variety.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose the open Richardson variety
R_{v,w}
is isomorphic to a torus
\mathbb{T}
. Then the action of
\mathbb{T}
on
R_{v,w}
extends to the closed Richardson variety
\overline{R}_{v,w}
and
\overline{R}_{v,w}
is a toric variety with respect to
\mathbb{T}
.

We then show that this is in fact a characterization of toric Richardsons, and also provide various equivalent combinatorial characterizations.

Theorem 1.2
.

The following conditions are equivalent:

(a)

The open Richardson variety
R_{v,w}
is a torus.

(b)

The closed Richardson variety
\overline{R}_{v,w}
is a toric variety.

(c)

The Bruhat interval
[v,w]
does not contain a subinterval isomorphic to
S_{3}
.

(d)

The Bruhat interval
[v,w]
is a lattice.

(e)

The interval poset
\{[v^{\prime},w^{\prime}]:[v^{\prime},w^{\prime}]\subset[v,w]\}\cup\{\emptyset\}
, ordered by containment, is a lattice.

We prove (a)
\Leftrightarrow
(b) in Theorem
3.6
and (a)
\Leftrightarrow
(c) in Lemma
3.1
. The equivalence (d)
\Leftrightarrow

One way to understand a toric Richardson

Theorem 1.3
.

The moment polytope

has face lattice isomorphic to

. That is, the

One nice consequence of our results is a characterization of when

Corollary 1.4
.

The following are equivalent:

(a)

The interval poset

of sub-intervals of
[v,w]
is the face lattice of a polytope.

(b)

The Bruhat interval
[v,w]
does not have any 2-crowns.

(c)

The Bruhat interval
[v,w]
is a lattice.

Background

We use the notation

2.1.
Algebraic tori

A torus is an algebraic variety isomorphic to
(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{d}
for some
d
. A specific choice of isomorphism
\mathbb{T}\simeq(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{d}
defines coordinates on
\mathbb{T}
, and coordinate-wise multiplication equips
\mathbb{T}
with a structure of an algebraic group, which we will refer to below as
multiplicative structure
. Note that different choices of coordinates might give different multiplicative structures. A multiplicative structure gives an isomorphism
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{T}]\cong\mathbb{C}[x_{i}^{\pm 1}]
, and the only units in this ring are Laurent monomials in the
x_{i}
.

We will repeatedly use the following simple lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose
\mathbb{T}
is a torus, and
U
is an open subset in
\mathbb{T}
. If
U
is a torus then
U=\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

We have
\dim U=\dim\mathbb{T}=d
. Let us choose some coordinates
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
on
U
and
y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}
on
\mathbb{T}
. The restrictions of
y_{i}
on
U
are invertible hence they are Laurent monomials in
x_{i}
:

(2.1)

y_{i}|_{U}=c_{i}\prod x_{j}^{a_{ij}},\ a_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z},\ c_{i}\neq 0.

Define
y^{\prime}_{i}=y_{i}/c_{i}
and consider the multiplicative structures on
\mathbb{T}
(resp.
U
) defined by
y^{\prime}_{i}
(resp.
x_{i}
). By (
2.1
) the inclusion
U\hookrightarrow\mathbb{T}
is a homomorphism with respect to these multiplicative structures, so
U
is a subgroup of
\mathbb{T}
. Since
\dim U=\dim\mathbb{T}
, we get
U=\mathbb{T}
.
∎

We will need the following easy fact about cluster varieties. We refer to
[
33
]
and references therein for more details and definitions.

Corollary 2.2
.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}
is a cluster algebra. Then
X=\mathrm{Spec}\mathcal{A}
is a torus if and only if all cluster variables in
\mathcal{A}
are frozen.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{s}
be one seed for
\mathcal{A}
. By
[
33
, Section 2.6]
, it corresponds to an open torus
T_{\mathfrak{s}}\subset X
. If all cluster variables in
\mathcal{A}
are frozen then
\mathcal{A}
is the Laurent polynomial algebra in the frozen variables so
T_{\mathfrak{s}}=X
by definition.

Conversely, assume
X
is a torus. Then by Lemma
2.1
we get
X=T_{\mathfrak{s}}
, so all cluster variables are invertible in
\mathcal{A}
and hence frozen by
[
21
, Theorem 1.3]
.
∎

2.2.
Richardson varieties

We refer to
[
36
]
for all background on Richardson varieties and positroid varieties. We fix the Borel subgroup
B\subset\mathrm{SL}(n)
of upper triangular matrices.
We consider the Schubert cell
X^{w}=BwB/B
in the complete flag variety
\mathrm{Fl}(n)\cong\mathrm{SL}(n)/B
, and the opposite Schubert cell
X_{v}=B_{-}vB/B
. These are isomorphic to affine spaces of dimensions
\ell(w)
and
\binom{n}{2}-\ell(v)
respectively.

Definition 2.3
.

The open Richardson variety is defined as the intersection
R_{v,w}=X^{w}\cap X_{v}
. The closed Richardson variety
\overline{R}_{v,w}
is the Zariski closure of
R_{v,w}
inside
\mathrm{Fl}(n)
.

The open Richardson variety
R_{v,w}
is nonempty if and only if
v\leq w
in the Bruhat order. The dimension of both
R_{v,w}
and
\overline{R}_{v,w}
equals
\ell(w)-\ell(v)
. The variety
R_{v,w}
is smooth, affine, and irreducible, and is a cluster variety
[
9
,
17
]
. The variety
\overline{R}_{v,w}
is typically singular
[
27
,
1
]
.
We have decompositions

\overline{R}_{v,w}=\bigsqcup_{[v^{\prime},w^{\prime}]\subset[v,w]}R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{R}_{v,w}=\bigcup_{[v^{\prime},w^{\prime}]\subset[v,w]}\overline{R}_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}}.

We define the
{\tt R}
-polynomial

{\tt R}_{v,w}(q)
as the point count in
R_{v,w}
over the finite field with
q
elements.

Given an
n\times n
nondegenerate matrix
M
with columns
M_{i}
, one can define the flag

\mathcal{F}_{M}=\left\{\langle M_{1}\rangle\subset\langle M_{1},M_{2}\rangle\subset\cdots\subset\langle M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}\rangle=\mathbb{C}^{n}\right\}.

Given
I\in\binom{[n]}{k}
, we define the
flag minor
(or
Plücker coordinate
)
\Delta_{I}=\Delta_{I}(M)
as the
k\times k
-minor of the matrix
M
using rows
I

and the first
k
columns. Choosing a different representative matrix
M^{\prime}
for the same flag yields proportional minors
\Delta_{I}(M^{\prime})=c_{k}\Delta_{I}(M)
where the constant
c_{k}\neq 0
does not depend on
I
but might depend on
k
.

Alternatively, one can think of the projection
\pi_{k}:\mathrm{Fl}(n)\to\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)
which sends the flag
\mathcal{F}_{M}
to the
k
-dimensional subspace
\langle M_{1},\ldots,M_{k}\rangle
. Then the minors
\Delta_{I}(M)
with
I\in\binom{[n]}{k}
are the Plücker coordinates of
\pi_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{M})
.

We will need to know which minors are identically zero on
\overline{R}_{v,w}
and which are not, which is essentially
[
37
, Theorem 7.1]
. Note that
\Delta_{I}
is identically zero on
\overline{R}_{v,w}
if and only if it is identically zero on
R_{v,w}
.

Definition 2.4
.

We define
\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)
as the collection of all
k
-subsets
I\subset[n]
such that the minor
\Delta_{I}
is not identically zero on
R_{v,w}
. We call
\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)
the
k
th constituent
of the interval
[v,w]
.

Note that many different intervals can have the same
k
th constituent, but the following result shows that the collection of all constituents uniquely determines an interval.

Theorem 2.5
.

Fix
I\in\binom{[n]}{k}
. The flag minor
\Delta_{I}
is generically nonzero on the closed Richardson variety
\overline{R}_{v,w}
if and only if there is some
u\in[v,w]
such that
I=u[k]
. In other words,

\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)=\left\{u[k]\ :\ u\in[v,w]\right\}.

Proof.

For a tuple of nested subsets
\mathbf{I}=(I_{1}\subsetneq I_{2}\subsetneq\dots\subsetneq I_{n-1})
, define the
generalized Plücker coordinate
as the product of flag minors
\Delta_{\mathbf{I}}:=\Delta_{I_{1}}\dots\Delta_{I_{n-1}}
. Then

[
37
, Theorem 7.1]
shows that for any point
F
in the positive part
R_{v,w}^{>0}
, the generalized Plucker coordinate
\Delta_{\mathbf{I}}
is nonzero if and only if
\mathbf{I}=(u[1]\subset u[2]\subset\cdots\subset u[n-1])
for some
u\in[v,w]
.

On the other hand, the nonzero flag minors of
F
define a flag matroid. By the Maximality Property for flag matroids
[
6
]
,
any nonzero flag minor
\Delta_{J}
of
F
is a factor of some nonzero generalized Plücker coordinate. This implies that for
F\in R_{v,w}^{>0}
, the flag minor
\Delta_{J}
is nonzero if and only if
J=u[k]
for some
u\in[v,w]
.

Since the positive part
R_{v,w}^{>0}
is Zariski dense in the closed Richardson, this implies the desired statement.
∎

Positroids and positroid varieties

The collection

of Definition

is an example of a matroid known as a
positroid
, and all positroids can be obtained this way by varying

and

. Moreover, we have
\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)=\mathcal{M}_{k}(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})
for a unique

where

is

w^{\prime}(1)<w^{\prime}(2)<\dots<w^{\prime}(k)
and

We will not need the precise definition of a positroid and refer to

First, given a positroid

\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}^{\circ}\subset\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)

This projection is an isomorphism. In particular, we have

\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}^{\circ}\supset\{V\in\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)\ :\ \Delta_{I}(V)\neq 0\ \mathrm{if\ and\ only\ if}\ I\in\mathcal{M}\}.

An open positroid variety is a cluster variety by
[
19
]
. We denote by
\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}
the closure of
\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}^{\circ}
. We have the decomposition

\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}=\bigsqcup_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{M}}\Pi_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}^{\circ}.

Second, it is convenient to encode positroids by

35

Note that the boundary of the disk is not part of the plabic graph.

. A plabic graph is called reduced if it satisfies some technical condition in

.
Note that any plabic forest (plabic graph with no cycles) is automatically reduced.

Definition 2.6
.

Given a plabic graph

, an orientation of

has exactly one edge directed towards

, and each black vertex
v
has exactly one edge directed away from

Given a perfect orientation
\mathcal{O}
on a reduced plabic graph

Third, we need the notion of positroid polytopes. For

Definition 2.7
.

For a positroid
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)

The positroid polytope

Finally we have a lemma which we will make use of later.

Lemma 2.8
.

Suppose
\mathcal{M}
is a positroid such that the open positroid variety
\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}^{\circ}
is a torus. Then the corresponding plabic graph

is a forest.

Proof.

By
[
19
]
the cluster variables for a seed for

correspond to the faces of the plabic graph

then implies that there are no internal faces. Any plabic graph

which has a cycle has an internal face, so this implies
G_{\mathcal{M}}
is a forest.
∎

2.4.
Deodhar decomposition and Marsh-Rietsch parametrization

Choose a reduced expression
\mathbf{w}
for
w\in S_{n}
. The Richardson variety
R_{v,w}
admits a Deodhar decomposition
[
11
]

\bigsqcup_{\sigma}(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{a(\sigma)}\times\mathbb{C}^{b(\sigma)}

where the components are indexed by
distinguished subexpressions

for

. We have that

and is also the unique component which is a torus. We call this component the

Lemma 2.9
.

Fix a reduced word
\mathbf{w}
for
w\in S_{n}
.
The open Richardson
R_{v,w}
is isomorphic to a torus if and only if the Deodhar torus is the unique Deodhar component.

Proof.

The forward direction follows from Lemma

is the union of the Deodhar components.

Let
\phi_{i}:\mathrm{SL}(2)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{SL}(n)

\dot{s}_{i}:=\phi_{i}\left(\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\

Lemma 2.14

Let

.

We proceed by induction on

\mathbf{w}=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{\ell}}

2.5.
Combinatorics of Bruhat intervals

We refer to
[
4
]
for all details on Bruhat order on
S_{n}
. Since the only intervals we will consider in this paper are intervals in the Bruhat order, we will henceforth just say “intervals.” Following
[
4
]
, a
k
-crown is the face poset of a
k
-gon. In particular, the interval
[e,s_{1}s_{2}s_{1}]
is a 2-crown.

We recall the definition of Bruhat interval polytopes following
[
37
, Definition 7.8]
. For
I\subset[n]
, we use the notation
e_{I}:=\sum_{i\in I}e_{i}
for the indicator vector of
I
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 2.15
.

The
Bruhat interval polytope

P(v,w)
is

P(v,w)=\Conv\left((n-1)e_{u(1)}+(n-2)e_{u(2)}+\ldots+e_{u(n-1)}\ :\ u\in[v,w]\right)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}.

More conceptually, recall that
S_{n}
acts on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
u\cdot(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})=(\beta_{u^{-1}(1)},\dots,\beta_{u^{-1}(n)})
. We have that

P(v,w)=\Conv(u\cdot(n-1,n-2,\dots,1,0):u\in[v,w]).

Note that

(n-1)e_{u(1)}+(n-2)e_{u(2)}+\ldots+e_{u(n-1)}=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}e_{u[k]},

and in fact we have a Minkowski sum decomposition
[
37
, Proposition 2.8]

P(v,w)=P_{1}(v,w)+\ldots+P_{n}(v,w)

where

P_{k}(v,w)=\Conv\left(e_{u[k]}\ :\ u\in[v,w]\right)=\Conv\left(e_{I}\ :\ I\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)\right)

are the positroid polytopes for the constituents of
[v,w]
.

Remark 2.16
.

The vertices of
P(v,w)
are given by

(n-1)e_{u(1)}+(n-2)e_{u(2)}+\ldots+e_{u(n-1)}
. Since
e_{u(1)}+\ldots+e_{u(n)}=e_{1}+\ldots+e_{n}
, we can instead consider the shifted Bruhat interval polytope

P(v,w)+e_{1}+\ldots+e_{n}
with vertices

ne_{u(1)}+(n-1)e_{u(2)}+\ldots+2e_{u(n-1)}+e_{n}
. The latter convention was used in
[
37
]
.

2.6.
A lemma on posets

Let
P
be a poset and let
\Int(P)
be the set of intervals of
P
, together with the empty set. We consider
\Int(P)
to be a poset with respect to containment, so
\emptyset
is the minimal element of
\Int(P)
. The following lemma will be useful to us.

Lemma 2.17
.

A finite poset
P
is a lattice if and only if
\Int(P)
is a lattice.

Proof.

Suppose
P
is a lattice with meet
\wedge
and join
\vee
. Consider
[x,p],[y,q]\in\Int(P)
. By the definition of meet and join,
[a,b]
contains
[x,p]
and
[y,q]
if and only if
[a,b]
contains
[x\wedge y,p\vee q]
. So
[x\wedge y,p\vee q]
is the least upper bound of
[x,p]
and
[y,q]
. A similar argument shows that
[x\vee y,p\wedge q]
is the greatest lower bound of
[x,y]
and
[p,q]
. The least upper bound of
\emptyset
and
[x,y]
is
[x,y]
, and the greatest lower bound is
\emptyset
. Thus,
\Int(P)
is a lattice.

If
\Int(P)
is a lattice, then it has a maximal element. This maximal element contains all intervals
[x,x]
so must be the poset
P
. That is,
P
is itself an interval and so must have a minimal element
\hat{0}
and a maximal element
\hat{1}
. The poset
P
is isomorphic to the interval
[[\hat{0},\hat{0}],[\hat{0},\hat{1}]]
in
\Int(P)
. Since subintervals of lattices are lattices,
P
is a lattice.
∎

3.
Toric Richardson varieties

In this section, we characterize the toric Richardsons
\overline{R}_{v,w}
in various ways.

3.1.
On open Richardsons which are tori

We begin with some helpful results on open Richardsons
R_{v,w}
which are tori.

Lemma 3.1
.

The open Richardson variety
R_{v,w}
is a torus if and only if
[v,w]
contains no 2-crowns.

Proof.

It follows from the Deodhar decomposition that the
{\tt R}
-polynomial can be written as

{\tt R}_{v,w}(q)=\sum_{\sigma}(q-1)^{a(\sigma)}q^{b(\sigma)}

where the sum is over distinguished subexpressions of a particular reduced word
\mathbf{w}
for
w
. Recall that
a(\sigma)+2b(\sigma)=\ell(w)-\ell(v)=d
. Let
{\tt m}
be the number of codimension 1 Deodhar components (with
a(\sigma)=d-2,b(\sigma)=1
). Then

{\tt R}_{v,w}(q)=(q-1)^{d}+{\tt m}(q-1)^{d-2}q+\ldots=q^{d}-(d-{\tt m})q^{d-1}+\ldots

The number
{\tt m}
is also the number of mutable variables in a seed for the cluster structure on
R_{v,w}
. By Corollary
2.2
,
R_{v,w}
is a torus if and only if there are no mutable variables, that is
{\tt m}=0
. So
R_{v,w}
is a torus if and only if the coefficient of
q^{d-1}
in
{\tt R}_{v,w}(q)
is
(-d)
. By
[
7
, Theorem 6.3]
(see also
[
4
, Section 5, Exercise 36]
) this is equivalent to the fact that
[v,w]
has no 2-crowns.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Assume that
R_{v,w}
is a torus. Then for all
v\leq v^{\prime}\leq w^{\prime}\leq w
the open Richardson
R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}}
is also a torus.

Proof.

Since
R_{v,w}
is a torus, by Lemma
3.1
the interval
[v,w]
does not contain 2-crowns. Therefore
[v^{\prime},w^{\prime}]
does not contain 2-crowns, and by applying Lemma
3.1
we conclude that
R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}}
is a torus.
∎

We next turn to flag minors when
R_{v,w}
is a torus. Recall from
[
23
, Proposition 5.12]
that for all
v\leq u\leq w
we have a splicing map

\Psi_{v,u,w}:R_{v,u}\times R_{u,w}\to R_{v,w}

which defines an isomorphism between
R_{v,u}\times R_{u,w}
and a principal open subset
U(v,u,w)\subset R_{v,w}
defined by the inequalities

U(v,u,w)=\{\Delta_{u[1]}\Delta_{u[2]}\cdots\Delta_{u[n-1]}\neq 0\}.

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume
R_{v,w}
is a torus
\mathbb{T}
and
v\leq u\leq w
. Then:

a) The map
\Psi_{v,u,w}
is an isomorphism and
U(v,u,w)=R_{v,w}
.

b) For any choice of multiplicative structure on
R_{v,w}
, the minors
\Delta_{u[k]}
are nonzero Laurent monomials.

Proof.

a) By Corollary
3.2
we get that
R_{v,u}
and
R_{u,w}
are tori of dimensions
\ell(u)-\ell(v)
and
\ell(w)-\ell(u)
respectively. Therefore
R_{v,u}\times R_{u,w}\simeq U(v,u,w)
is also a torus of dimension
\ell(w)-\ell(v)
.

On the other hand,
U(v,u,w)
is an open subset in the torus
R_{v,w}
of the same dimension, so by Lemma
2.1
we get
U(v,u,w)=R_{v,w}
.

b) All the minors
\Delta_{u[k]}
are nonzero on
U(v,u,w)
. Since
U(v,u,w)=R_{v,w}
, we conclude
that
\Delta_{u[k]}
are invertible functions on the torus, that is, nonzero monomials.
∎

The above lemma is true regardless of the multiplicative structure we fix on
R_{v,w}
. If we fix a particular multiplicative structure, we obtain additional information. The next lemma discusses flag minors for the multiplicative structure given by Proposition
2.12
. That is, the coordinates of
R_{v,w}
are exactly the MR parameters
\{t_{j}\}_{j\in J^{\circ}}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose
R_{v,w}
is a torus, with multiplicative structure given by Proposition
2.12
. Fix
\mathbf{w}
a reduced expression for
w
.

For any
I\in\binom{[n]}{k}
, either there are no NI path collections
P:I\to[k]
in
G_{v,\mathbf{w}}
and
\Delta_{I}(g)=0
, or there is a unique NI path collection
P:I\to[k]
and

\Delta_{I}(g)=\prod_{b\in P}\wt(b)

where the product is over all bridge edges in
P
.

In particular, the nonzero flag minors of the MR matrix
g
are square-free monic monomials in the MR parameters
\{t_{j}\}_{j\in J^{\circ}}
.

Proof.

By (
2.5
),
\Delta_{I}(g)
is the sum of distinct monic monomials in the MR parameters, one for each NI path collection
P:I\to[k]
. So
\Delta_{I}(g)
is identically zero if and only if there are no such path collections. If
\Delta_{I}(g)
is not identically zero, then by Theorem
2.5
,
I=u[k]
for some
u\in[v,w]
and by Lemma
3.3
,
\Delta_{I}(g)
is a nonzero Laurent monomial in the MR parameters. This is possible only if there is a unique NI path collection
P:I\to[k]
.
∎

3.2.
On closed Richardsons which are toric

In this section, we characterize toric closed Richardson varieties. We start by ruling out some Richardsons.

Lemma 3.5

(
[
8
, Corollary 3.4]
)
.

Assume that
\overline{R}_{v,w}
is a toric variety with respect to some torus action. Then
[v,w]
does not contain 2-crowns.

Proof.

We describe the argument from
[
8
]
for the reader’s convenience. If
\overline{R}_{v,w}
is a toric variety with respect to the action of some torus
\mathbb{T}^{\prime}
then by
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
for all
[v^{\prime},w^{\prime}]
the subvariety
\overline{R}_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}}
is a closed irreducible
\mathbb{T}^{\prime}
-invariant subvariety of
\overline{R}_{v,w}
. Therefore it corresponds to the closure of some face of the corresponding moment polytope, and is a toric variety itself.

On the other hand, if
[v^{\prime},w^{\prime}]
is a 2-crown then one can check as in
[
8
, Corollary 3.3]
that
\overline{R}_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}}
cannot be a toric variety. In particular, there is no polytope whose faces are in inclusion-preserving bijection with the subintervals of a 2-crown. So if
\overline{R}_{v,w}
is toric,
[v,w]
does not contain 2-crowns.
∎

Now we show that all
\overline{R}_{v,w}
that are not ruled out by Lemma
3.5
are in fact toric.

Theorem 3.6
.

We have that
\overline{R}_{v,w}
is a toric variety if and only if
R_{v,w}
is a torus.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
,
R_{v,w}
is a torus if and only if
[v,w]
does not contain 2-crowns. If
R_{v,w}
is not a torus, then
[v,w]
contains a 2-crown and Lemma
3.5
shows that
\overline{R}_{v,w}
is not toric. We now show the other direction, so assume
R_{v,w}
is a torus.

Let us choose some coordinates
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
on
R_{v,w}\simeq(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{d}
. We claim that we may choose these coordinates so that each flag minor
\Delta_{I}
is either identically zero or is a nonzero monic Laurent monomial in
x_{i}
. Indeed, if
I\neq u[k]
for some
u\in[v,w]
then
\Delta_{I}=0
by Theorem
2.5
. Otherwise
\Delta_{I}
is a nonzero Laurent monomial by Lemma
3.3
, and Lemma
3.4
shows that we may assume the Laurent monomials are monic. When
\Delta_{I}\neq 0
, we can write

(3.1)

\Delta_{I}=x_{1}^{m_{I,1}}\cdots x_{d}^{m_{I,d}},\ m_{I,j}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Now consider the composition of maps:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Fl}(n)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{P}^{\binom{n}{k}-1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\jmath}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}

where the horizontal map sends a flag to a collection of its subspaces, and
\alpha
is the Plücker embedding.
The image of
\jmath
is closed and is defined by the incidence Plücker relations (
2.2
). Now consider the composition

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{d}\simeq R_{v,w}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Fl}(n)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{P}^{\binom{n}{k}-1}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\jmath}

We can think of the closed Richardson variety
\overline{R}_{v,w}
as the closure of the image of
\iota
, as the image of
\iota
is automatically contained in the closed subset
\jmath(\mathrm{Fl}(n))
. By the above,
\iota
is given in coordinates by monic Laurent monomials in
x_{i}
, hence

\overline{R}_{v,w}\simeq\overline{\iota(R_{v,w})}

is a toric variety as in
[
10
]
.
∎

Definition 3.7
.

We define the algebraic torus
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}\simeq(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{d}
as the variety
R_{v,w}
with some choice of the multiplicative structure which we fix for the remainder of the section.

We can rephrase Theorem
3.6
as follows.

Corollary 3.8
.

When
R_{v,w}
is a torus, the action of
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
on
R_{v,w}
extends to an action of
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
on
\overline{R}_{v,w}
.

Recall the decomposition

\overline{R}_{v,w}=\bigsqcup_{v\leq v^{\prime}\leq w^{\prime}\leq w}R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}}

of closed Richardson varieties into open Richardson varieties. We show that when
\overline{R}_{v,w}
is toric, this coincides with the decomposition of
\overline{R}_{v,w}
into
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
-orbits.

Lemma 3.9
.

Assume
R_{v,w}
is a torus and define the action of
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
on
\overline{R}_{v,w}
as in Theorem
3.6
. Then for all
v\leq v^{\prime}\leq w^{\prime}\leq w
the subvariety
R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}}\subset\overline{R}_{v,w}
is a
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
-orbit.

Proof.

By
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
if the closed Richardson variety
\overline{R}_{v,w}
is toric for some torus
\mathbb{T}
then all closed Richardson varieties
\overline{R}_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}}
for subintervals are
\mathbb{T}
-invariant subsets. Since

R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}}=\overline{R}_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}}\setminus\bigcup_{[v^{\prime\prime},w^{\prime\prime}]\subset[v^{\prime},w^{\prime}]}\overline{R}_{v^{\prime\prime},w^{\prime\prime}}

we conclude that the open Richardson variety
R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}}
is also
\mathbb{T}
-invariant.

On the other hand, by Corollary
3.2
the variety
R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}}
is a torus itself, and action of the torus
\mathbb{T}
on the torus
R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}}
can have either one or infinitely many orbits.

Since
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
has finitely many orbits in
\overline{R}_{v,w}
, we conclude that
R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}}

is a
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
-orbit.
∎

Now that we know the torus orbits, we can describe the moment polytope of toric Richardsons. We will discuss the moment polytope further in Section
4
.

Corollary 3.10
.

Suppose
\overline{R}_{v,w}
is toric and let
\widehat{P}(v,w)
denote its moment polytope. Then the
s
-dimensional faces of
\widehat{P}(v,w)
are in bijection with the subintervals
[v^{\prime},w^{\prime}]\subset[v,w]
with
\ell(w^{\prime})-\ell(v^{\prime})=s
. The face poset of
\widehat{P}(v,w)
is isomorphic to the poset of subintervals of
[v,w]
ordered by inclusion.

Corollary 3.11
.

The combinatorial type of
\widehat{P}(v,w)
is determined by the poset structure of
[v,w]
.

3.3.
Positroid projections

In this subsection, we collect a few results regarding the projections of open Richardsons which are tori, and closed Richardsons which are toric, to the Grassmannian. By
[
25
, Theorem 5.1]
,
\pi_{k}(\overline{R}_{v,w})
is always a closed positroid variety, but
\pi_{k}(R_{v,w})
may not be an open positroid variety. Among other things, we show that when
R_{v,w}
is a torus,
\pi_{k}(R_{v,w})
is indeed an open positroid variety, and is also a torus. When
\overline{R}_{v,w}
is toric,
\pi_{k}(\overline{R}_{v,w})
is toric with respect to the standard torus.

Consider the projections
\pi_{k}:\mathrm{Fl}(n)\to\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)
from the complete flag variety to the Grassmannian of
k
-planes. Recall the positroid

\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w):=\{u[k]:v\leq u\leq w\}
.
We denote by
\Pi^{\circ}_{k}(v,w):=\Pi^{\circ}_{\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)}
(resp.
\Pi_{k}(v,w):=\Pi_{\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)}
) the corresponding open (resp. closed) positroid variety and by
G_{k}(v,w)
the corresponding plabic graph.

Lemma 3.12
.

Assume that
R_{v,w}
is a torus. Then

\pi_{k}(R_{v,w})
is a dense subset of
\Pi^{\circ}_{k}(v,w)
.

Proof.

The containment
\pi_{k}(R_{v,w})\subset\Pi^{\circ}_{k}(v,w)
is because all points in
\pi_{k}(R_{v,w})
have
\Delta_{I}\neq 0
if and only if
I=u[k]
for some
u\in[v,w]
, by Theorem
2.5
and Lemma
3.3
. This implies that they are contained in
\Pi^{\circ}_{k}(v,w)
. On the other hand, by
[
26
, Theorem 5.1]
we have
\Pi_{k}(v,w)=\pi_{k}(\overline{R}_{v,w})
, so
\pi_{k}(R_{v,w})
is dense in
\Pi_{k}(v,w)
. By the above, it is also dense in
\Pi^{\circ}_{k}(v,w)
.
∎

Lemma 3.13
.

Assume
R_{v,w}
is a torus. Then
\Pi_{k}(v,w)=\pi_{k}(\overline{R}_{v,w})
is a toric variety and the projection
\pi_{k}
is
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
-equivariant.

Proof.

This is immediate from the proof of Theorem
3.6
: all flag minors for
I\in\binom{[n]}{k}
are either zero or Laurent monomials in the coordinates of
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
, and these flag minors are exactly the Plücker coordinates in the projection.
∎

Lemma 3.14
.

Assume that
R_{v,w}
is a torus and
[v^{\prime},w^{\prime}]\subsetneq[v,w]
. Then either
\pi_{k}(R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}})\cap\pi_{k}(R_{v,w})=\emptyset
or
\pi_{k}(R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}})=\pi_{k}(R_{v,w})
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.13
the projection
\pi_{k}
is
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
-equivariant. Assume that a point
p
is contained in the intersection
\pi_{k}(R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}})\cap\pi_{k}(R_{v,w})
.

Since
R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}}
is also a torus by Corollary
3.2
, Lemma
3.12
implies that the image
\pi_{k}(R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}})
is contained in
\Pi^{\circ}_{k}(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})
. Open positroid varieties labeled by different positroids are disjoint. Therefore
\mathcal{M}_{k}(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})=\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)
and
\pi_{k}(R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}})
is dense in
\Pi^{\circ}_{k}(v,w)
. In particular, we have

\dim\Pi_{k}(v,w)=\dim\pi_{k}(R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}})=\dim\pi_{k}(R_{v,w}).

The
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
-orbit
\mathbf{O}
of
p
is a torus which is contained in
\Pi_{k}(v,w)
by Lemma
3.12
and contains both
\pi_{k}(R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}})
and
\pi_{k}(R_{v,w})
by Lemma
3.9
. The above equality of dimensions implies that
\mathbf{O}
is also the same dimension as
\pi_{k}(R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}})
, and
\pi_{k}(R_{v,w})
. By Lemma
2.1
we conclude that
\mathbf{O}=\pi_{k}(R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}})=\pi_{k}(R_{v,w})
.
∎

Theorem 3.15
.

Assume that
R_{v,w}
is a torus. Then:

(a)

We have
\pi_{k}(R_{v,w})=\Pi^{\circ}_{k}(v,w)
.

(b)

The open positroid
\Pi^{\circ}_{k}(v,w)
is a torus and the closed positroid variety is toric with respect to
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
.

(c)

The plabic graph
G_{k}(v,w)
is a forest.

(d)

The closed positroid variety
\Pi_{k}(v,w)=\pi_{k}(\overline{R}_{v,w})
is toric with respect to the standard torus
T
.

Proof.

(a) We have
\overline{R}_{v,w}=\bigsqcup_{v\leq v^{\prime}\leq w^{\prime}\leq w}R_{x,y}
, so

\pi_{k}(\overline{R}_{v,w})=\bigcup_{v\leq v^{\prime}\leq w^{\prime}\leq w}\pi_{k}(R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}}).

By Lemma
3.12
,
\pi_{k}(R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}})
is contained in the positroid
\Pi^{\circ}_{k}(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})
which either coincides with
\Pi^{\circ}_{k}(v,w)
or is disjoint from it. Since
\pi_{k}(\overline{R}_{v,w})
contains
\Pi^{\circ}_{k}(v,w)
, we can write

\Pi^{\circ}_{k}(v,w)=\bigcup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v\leq v^{\prime}\leq w^{\prime}\leq w}}{{\mathcal{M}_{k}(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})=\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)}}}\pi_{k}(R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}})

By Lemma
3.14
the projections
\pi_{k}(R_{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}})
either coincide or do not intersect at all. Since
\Pi^{\circ}_{k}(v,w)
is irreducible, they cannot be disjoint and therefore
\pi_{k}(R_{v,w})=\Pi^{\circ}_{k}(v,w)
, as desired.

(b) Part (a) implies that
\Pi^{\circ}_{k}(v,w)
is a torus, as all nonzero Plücker coordinates are monomials in the coordinates of the torus
R_{v,w}=\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
. The closed positroid is the closure of the image of a monomial map
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}\to\mathbb{P}^{\binom{n}{k}-1}
so is toric with respect to
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
.

(c) The plabic graph
G_{k}(v,w)
is a forest by Lemma
2.8
. This implies (d) because in this case, the dimension of
\Pi_{k}(v,w)
is the same as the dimension of the
T
-moment polytope
P_{k}(v,w)
.
∎

4.
Moment polytopes of toric Richardsons

In this section, we focus on the moment polytope
\widehat{P}(v,w)
of the toric Richardson variety
\overline{R}_{v,w}
. Corollary
3.10
described the face lattice of this polytope, which is the poset of subintervals of
[v,w]
. Here, we give more detail on its geometry.

4.1.
The moment polytopes as Minkowski sums

We fix
R_{v,w}
which is a torus. As in Definition
3.7
, we use
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
to denote
R_{v,w}
with a fixed multiplicative structure. We use
x_{i}
to denote the coordinates of
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
.
Recall that on
R_{v,w}
, if
I\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)
, then by (
3.1
)

\Delta_{I}=x_{1}^{m_{I,1}}\dots x_{d}^{m_{I,d}}

for some
m_{I}=(m_{I,1},\ldots,m_{I,d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Definition 4.1
.

We define the polytope
\widehat{P}_{k}(v,w)
as

\widehat{P}_{k}(v,w)=\Conv(m_{I}:I\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)).

We call this the
k
th summand
for
[v,w]
, for reasons that will be clear momentarily.

Recall that the vectors
m_{I}
are defined up to a shift which might depend on
|I|=k
but not on
I
. This means that the polytopes
\widehat{P}_{k}(v,w)
are also defined up to a shift.

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose
R_{v,w}
is a torus, so by Theorem
3.15

\pi_{k}(\overline{R}_{v,w})=\Pi_{k}(v,w)
is toric.

a)

The moment polytope of
\Pi_{k}(v,w)
with respect to the action of the torus
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
is
\widehat{P}_{k}(v,w)
.

b)

The moment polytope
\widehat{P}(v,w)
of
\overline{R}_{v,w}
is equal to the Minkowski sum
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\widehat{P}_{k}(v,w)
.

Proof.

Both parts follow from the proof of Theorem
3.6
, so we only need to explain the appearance of the Minkowski sum. We consider the Segre embedding

R_{v,w}\xrightarrow{\iota}\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{P}^{\binom{n}{k}-1}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{N},\ N=\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}-1.

The coordinates in the image of
R_{v,w}
are either zero or monomials with exponent vectors

m_{I_{1}}+m_{I_{2}}+\dots+m_{I_{n-1}}

where
I_{k}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)
. Therefore the moment polytope for
\overline{R}_{v,w}
is given by

\Conv\left(m_{I_{1}}+m_{I_{2}}+\dots+m_{I_{n-1}}\ :\ I_{k}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)\right)

=\Conv\left(m_{I_{1}}\ :\ I_{1}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(v,w)\right)+\dots+\Conv\left(m_{I_{n-1}}\ :\ I_{n-1}\in\mathcal{M}_{n-1}(v,w)\right)

=\widehat{P}_{1}(v,w)+\dots+\widehat{P}_{n-1}(v,w).

Here we used the identity
\Conv(A+B)=\Conv(A)+\Conv(B)
.

∎

Corollary 4.3
.

The vertices of
\widehat{P}_{k}(v,w)
are exactly the
m_{I}
for
I\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)
.

Proof.

The vertices of
\widehat{P}_{k}(v,w)
correspond to the
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
-fixed points of
\Pi_{k}(v,w)=\pi_{k}(\overline{R}_{v,w})
. These are the same as
T
-fixed points, and given by coordinate flags for subsets
u[k],\ u\in[v,w]
, equivalently, by
I\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)
.
∎

Recall from Definition
2.7
the definition of the positroid polytope
P_{k}(v,w)
. This polytope lies in the affine hyperplane
\mathcal{H}_{k}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
consisting of vectors with coordinate sum
k
. Alternatively, we can think of
P_{k}(v,w)

as the moment polytope of
\Pi_{k}(v,w)
under the action of the standard torus
T
where a nonzero minor
\Delta_{I}
has weight
e_{I}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

There exist an integer matrix
C\in\mathrm{Mat}(d,n)
and integer vectors
d^{(0)},d^{(1)},\ldots,d^{(k)}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that

C\widehat{P}(v,w)+d^{(0)}=P(v,w),\ C\widehat{P}_{k}(v,w)+d^{(k)}=P_{k}(v,w)

and

Cm_{I}+d^{(k)}=e_{I}\quad\mathrm{for}\ I\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w).

Proof.

The action of the standard torus
T
on
R_{v,w}
defines a homomorphism
T\to\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
which corresponds to the map of lattices
C:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. The action of
T
on
\overline{R}_{v,w}
factors through the action of
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
, and hence we get a commutative diagram of moment maps

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{R}_{v,w}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}^{d}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}^{n}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{\mathbb{T}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{T}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}

and
C\widehat{P}(v,w)
agrees with
P(v,w)
up to translation. The proof of the other statements is similar.
∎

The next lemma shows that the
k
th summand
\widehat{P}_{k}(v,w)
is affinely equivalent to
P_{k}(v,w)
, the positroid polytope for the
k
th constituent of
[v,w]
.

Lemma 4.5
.

For each
k
there exists an integer matrix
A^{(k)}\in\mathrm{Mat}(n,d)
and an integer vector
b^{(k)}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
\widehat{P}_{k}(v,w)=A^{(k)}P_{k}(v,w)+b^{(k)}
. Equivalently, there exist vectors
a^{(k)}_{1},\ldots,a^{(k)}_{n}
and
b^{(k)}
in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that for all
I\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)
,

(4.1)

m_{I}=b^{(k)}+\sum_{i\in I}a^{(k)}_{i}.

This defines an affine equivalence between
P_{k}(v,w)
and
\widehat{P}_{k}(v,w)
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.15
we get that
\Pi^{\circ}_{k}(v,w)
is a torus and the corresponding plabic graph
G_{k}(v,w)
is a forest. All cluster variables in the corresponding cluster algebra are frozen. These cluster variables are Plücker coordinates
\Delta_{I_{1}},\ldots,\Delta_{I_{s}}
corresponding to the
s
faces of
G_{k}(v,w)
(the subsets
I_{1},\dots,I_{s}
are the elements of the
Grassmann necklace
). These Plücker coordinates are algebraically independent. Note also that
\Pi^{\circ}_{k}(v,w)
has dimension
s-1
, and this is the same as the dimension of the polytopes
P_{k}(v,w)
and
\widehat{P}_{k}(v,w)
, as both polytopes are moment map images of
\Pi_{k}(v,w)
with respect to the tori
T
and
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
respectively.

The nonzero Plücker coordinates
\{\Delta_{I}:I\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)\}
of
\Pi^{\circ}_{k}(v,w)
are units; this follows for example from Theorem
3.15
(a). By
[
21
, Theorem 1.3]
, this implies that the nonzero Plücker coordinates are Laurent monomials in the frozen variables
\Delta_{I_{1}},\ldots,\Delta_{I_{s}}
:

\Delta_{I}=\Delta_{I_{1}}^{\ell_{I,1}}\cdots\Delta_{I_{s}}^{\ell_{I,s}}\quad\text{for }\ell_{I,1},\ldots,\ell_{I,s}\in\mathbb{Z}.

By comparing the weights of
T
and
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
we get

(4.2)

e_{I}=\ell_{I,1}e_{I_{1}}+\ldots+\ell_{I,s}e_{I_{s}}\ \quad\text{and}\quad m_{I}=\ell_{I,1}m_{I_{1}}+\ldots+\ell_{I,s}m_{I_{s}}\quad\text{for }I\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w).

The points
e_{I_{1}},\ldots,e_{I_{s}}
span a simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
(otherwise
P_{k}(v,w)
would not be dimension
s-1
). So there exists an affine transformation
\beta\mapsto A^{(k)}\beta+b^{(k)}
from
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
to
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
A^{(k)}e_{I_{j}}+b^{(k)}=m_{I_{j}}
for
j=1,\ldots,s
.
By (
4.2
) we get
A^{(k)}e_{I}+b^{(k)}=m_{I}
for all
I\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)
and

A^{(k)}P_{k}(v,w)+b^{(k)}=\widehat{P}_{k}(v,w).

Let
a^{(k)}_{i}
be the columns of the matrix
A_{k}
, then

A^{(k)}\left(\sum_{i\in I}e_{i}\right)+b^{(k)}=\sum_{i\in I}\left(A^{(k)}e_{i}\right)+b^{(k)}=\sum_{i\in I}a^{(k)}_{i}+b^{(k)},

and the equation (
4.1
) follows. More abstractly, we can say that the matrix
A^{(k)}
defines a homomorphism
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}\to T
and the action of
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
on
\Pi_{k}(v,w)
factors through the action of
T
.

Finally, by Lemma
4.4
for all
I\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(v,w)
we get

C(A^{(k)}e_{I}+b^{(k)})+d^{(k)}=Cm_{I}+d^{(k)}=e_{I}

This implies that
C
and
A^{(k)}
define inverse affine transformations between
P_{k}(v,w)
and
\widehat{P}_{k}(v,w)
, and hence these polytopes are affine equivalent.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

Adding the same vector
a
to all
a_{i}^{(k)}
is equivalent to changing
b^{(k)}
to
b^{(k)}+ka
. This will shift the polytope
\widehat{P}_{k}(v,w)
by a constant vector.

4.2.
Vertices and edges

We already know from Corollary
3.10
that the vertices of
\widehat{P}_{v,w}
are in bijection with the
\mathbb{T}_{v,w}
-fixed points of
\overline{R}_{v,w}
, which are exactly the 0-dimensional open Richardsons
R_{u,u}
with
u\in[v,w]
. The next corollary expresses these vertices in terms of the exponent vectors
m_{I}
and the vectors from Lemma
4.5
.

Corollary 4.7
.

The vertices of the polytope
\widehat{P}(v,w)
are given by

(4.3)

X_{u}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}m_{u[k]}=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}a^{(k)}_{u(i)}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}b^{(k)}.

for
u\in[v,w]
.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.10
the vertices
X_{u}
of
\widehat{P}(v,w)
correspond to the coordinate flags

\mathcal{F}(u)=uB/B

for permutations
u\in[v,w]
. The image of
\mathcal{F}(u)
under the projection
\pi_{k}
is the coordinate subspace which corresponds to the subset
u[k]
and to the vertex
m_{u[k]}
in the polytope
\widehat{P}_{k}(v,w)
. Therefore by the proof of Theorem
4.2
we get

X_{u}=\mu(\mathcal{F}(u))=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\mu_{k}(\pi_{k}(\mathcal{F}(u)))=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}m_{u[k]}.

∎

Next, we study the compatibility between the edge vectors in different constituent polytopes
\widehat{P}_{k}(v,w)
, and the respective matrices
A^{(k)}
. Recall that any edge in the Minkowski sum of polytopes is parallel to an edge in one of the summands, or is the Minkowski sum of parallel edges in different summands. The following result clarifies and strengthens this observation. For two vectors
m,m^{\prime}
, we use the notation
\overline{mm^{\prime}}
for the difference
m^{\prime}-m.

Lemma 4.8
.

Assume that
i<j
,
u^{\prime}=u\cdot(i\ j)
covers
u
in Bruhat order, and
u,u^{\prime}\in[v,w]
. Then for all
i\leq k,k^{\prime}<j
the edge vectors
\overrightarrow{m_{u[k]}m_{u^{\prime}[k]}}
in
\widehat{P}_{k}(v,w)
and
\overrightarrow{m_{u[k^{\prime}]}m_{u^{\prime}[k^{\prime}]}}
in
\widehat{P}_{k^{\prime}}(v,w)
are the same.

Proof.

It is sufficient to prove the statement for
k^{\prime}=k+1<j
. Consider two subsets

I=\{u(1),\ldots,u(i-1),u(i+1),\ldots,u(k)\}

and

J=\{u(1),\ldots,u(i-1),u(i+1),\ldots,u(k),u(i),u(j),u(k+1)\}.

Note that
J\setminus I=\{u(i),u(j),u(k+1)\}
. The incidence Plucker relation (
2.2
) for
I,J
reads as

\pm\Delta_{I\cup u(i)}\Delta_{J\setminus u(i)}\pm\Delta_{I\cup u(j)}\Delta_{J\setminus u(j)}\pm\Delta_{I\cup u(k+1)}\Delta_{J\setminus u(k+1)}=0.

Note that
I\cup u(i)=u[k]
and
J\setminus u(i)=u^{\prime}[k+1]
while

I\cup u(j)=u^{\prime}[k]
and
J\setminus u(j)=u[k+1]
, so in particular both products
\Delta_{I\cup u(i)}\Delta_{J\setminus u(i)}
and
\Delta_{I\cup u(j)}\Delta_{J\setminus u(j)}
are nonzero monomials. Assume that the third product
\Delta_{I\cup u(k+1)}\Delta_{J\setminus u(k+1)}
is also nonzero. By Lemma
3.4
we can assume that all three products are monic monomials, but then the relation is impossible. Therefore
the third term vanishes and we get

(4.4)

\Delta_{u[k]}\Delta_{u^{\prime}[k+1]}=\Delta_{u^{\prime}[k]}\Delta_{u[k+1]}.

By comparing the weights of both sides of (
4.4
) we get the identity:

\sum_{s\in I}a^{(k)}_{s}+a^{(k)}_{u(i)}+\sum_{s\in I}a^{(k+1)}_{s}+a^{(k+1)}_{u(j)}+a^{(k+1)}_{u(k+1)}=\sum_{s\in I}a^{(k)}_{i}+a^{(k)}_{u(j)}+\sum_{s\in I}a^{(k+1)}_{s}+a^{(k+1)}_{u(i)}+a^{(k+1)}_{u(k+1)}.

This implies

(4.5)

a^{(k)}_{u(i)}-a^{(k)}_{u(j)}=a^{(k^{\prime})}_{u(i)}-a^{(k^{\prime})}_{u(j)}.

and the result follows.
∎

Corollary 4.9
.

Assume that
u^{\prime}=u(i\ j)
covers
u
and
u,u^{\prime}\in[v,w]
. Then the edge vector between the corresponding vertices

of

equals

\overrightarrow{X_{u}X_{u^{\prime}}}=(j-i)\left(a^{(i)}_{u(j)}-a^{(i)}_{u(i)}\right),

and in particular each entry of this integral vector is divisible by

.

Remark 4.10
.

We note that each cover relation

also corresponds to a Bruhat interval polytope

which is a single edge, with vertices

and
x_{u}:=u^{\prime}\cdot(n-1,\dots,0)
. One can check that

\overrightarrow{x_{u}x_{u^{\prime}}}=(j-i)(e_{u(j)}-e_{u(i)}).

Remark 4.11
.

The product of positroid varieties
\Pi_{k}(v,w)
is a toric variety with respect to the
(n-1)^{2}
-dimensional torus

, where each copy of the standard torus

acts on the respective factor

The Richardson variety

is cut out in

by the incidence Plücker relations (

). We can interpret

as the maximal subtorus of

which makes the relations (

The tuple of matrices

from Lemma

describes the embedding

. The condition (
4.5
) is one constraint describing the compatibility of

with the incidence Plücker relations, and it would be interesting to find a complete set of such constraints. See

for related constructions.

Next, we consider a 2-dimensional face in the polytope

which corresponds to a rank 2 subinterval

and

Lemma 4.12
.

(a)

Suppose

are all distinct, and

Then

(4.6)

\overrightarrow{X_{u}X_{u_{1}}}=\overrightarrow{X_{u_{2}}X_{u_{3}}},\quad\overrightarrow{X_{u}X_{u_{2}}}=\overrightarrow{X_{u_{1}}X_{u_{3}}},

(b)

Suppose

(4.7)

\overrightarrow{X_{u_{1}}X_{u_{3}}}=\frac{k-j}{j-i}\overrightarrow{X_{u}X_{u_{1}}}+\overrightarrow{X_{u}X_{u_{2}}},\quad\overrightarrow{X_{u_{2}}X_{u_{3}}}=\frac{k-i}{j-i}\overrightarrow{X_{u}X_{u_{1}}}.

Proof.

Part (a) is clear from Corollary
4.9
, let us prove (b).
Note that
u_{3}=u_{1}(j\ k)=u_{2}(i\ k).
Now by Corollary

\overrightarrow{X_{u}X_{u_{1}}}=(j-i)\left(a^{(i)}_{u(j)}-a^{(i)}_{u(i)}\right),\ \overrightarrow{X_{u}X_{u_{2}}}=(k-j)\left(a^{(j)}_{u(k)}-a^{(j)}_{u(j)}\right),\ \overrightarrow{X_{u_{1}}X_{u_{3}}}=(k-j)\left(a^{(j)}_{u(k)}-a^{(j)}_{u(i)}\right),

\overrightarrow{X_{u_{2}}X_{u_{3}}}=(k-i)\left(a^{(i)}_{u(j)}-a^{(i)}_{u(i)}\right)=(k-i)\left(a^{(j)}_{u(j)}-a^{(j)}_{u(i)}\right).

∎

In the recent paper

Definition 5.14
.

We inductively define the permutations

in a one-line notation as follows:

(1)

,

(2)

The authors of
[

Definition 5.15

The main theorem of

Proposition 5.16
.

We have
I=u[k]
for some dyadically well-distributed permutation

(equivalently,

and any basic

-interval

(5.6)

\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2^{j}}\right\rfloor\leq|I\cap T|\leq\left\lceil\frac{k}{2^{j}}\right\rceil.

divides

(n-j)
-interval

Let us prove that (
5.6

can be decomposed into

More generally, define

Lemma 5.22
.

Assume
k=2^{l}\cdot k^{\prime}
where
k^{\prime}
is odd. Then
I\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(n)
if and only if for each basic
(n-l)
-interval
T
one has
|I\cap T|=k^{\prime}
and
I\cap T\in\mathcal{M}_{k^{\prime}}(n-l)
.

Proof.

The condition
|I\cap T|=k^{\prime}
is necessary by (
5.6
) for
j=l
. If
j<l
then
2^{j}
divides
k
and each basic
(n-j)
-interval
T^{\prime}
must contain
2^{l-j}k^{\prime}
elements of
I
. On the other hand,
T^{\prime}
can be decomposed into
2^{l-j}
basic
(n-l)
-intervals and each of them contains
k^{\prime}
elements of
I
, and (
5.6
) follows.

Finally, we need to analyze the case
j>l
. In this case we can decompose each basic
(n-l)
-interval
T
into
2^{j-l}
basic
(n-j)
-intervals
T^{\prime}
, and

\left\lfloor\frac{k^{\prime}}{2^{j-l}}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2^{j}}\right\rfloor\leq|I\cap T|\leq\left\lceil\frac{k}{2^{j}}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{k^{\prime}}{2^{j-l}}\right\rceil

so
I\cap T\in\mathcal{M}_{k^{\prime}}(n-l)
.
∎

Lemma 5.23
.

We have
I\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(n)
if and only if
[2^{n}]\setminus I=\overline{I}\in\mathcal{M}_{2^{n}-k}(n)
.

Proof.

For each basic
(n-j)
-interval
T
we have
|\overline{I}\cap T|=2^{n-j}-|I\cap T|
. On the other hand,

\left\lfloor\frac{2^{n}-k}{2^{j}}\right\rfloor=2^{n-j}-\left\lceil\frac{k}{2^{j}}\right\rceil,\ \left\lceil\frac{2^{n}-k}{2^{j}}\right\rceil=2^{n-j}-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2^{j}}\right\rfloor

and the result follows.
∎

Lemma 5.24
.

Assume
k\leq 2^{L}
with
L<n
. Then the subsets
I\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(n)
can be characterized as follows:

(a)

For each basic
(n-L)
-interval
T
, we have
|I\cap T|\leq 1
.

(b)

Let us identify the set of basic
(n-L)
-intervals with
[2^{L}]
and define a subset
\widetilde{I}\subset[2^{L}]
describing the
(n-L)
-intervals
T
such that
I\cap T\neq\emptyset
.
Then have
I\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(n)
if and only if
\widetilde{I}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(L)
.

Proof.

Since
\frac{k}{2^{L}}\leq 1
, the condition (a) is necessary by (
5.6
) with
j=L
. We need to check the inequalities (
5.6
) for all other
j
.

If
j>L
then
\frac{k}{2^{j}}<1
and (
5.6
) requires that
|I\cap T^{\prime}|\leq 1
for all basic
(n-j)
-intervals
T^{\prime}
. On the other hand, each
T^{\prime}
is contained in some basic
(n-L)
-interval
T
, and
|I\cap T^{\prime}|\leq|I\cap T|\leq 1
.

If
j<L
then each basic
(n-j)
-interval
T^{\prime\prime}
is the union of
2^{L-j}
basic
(n-L)
-intervals. By arguing as in (b), we can associate to
T^{\prime\prime}
a basic
(L-j)
-interval
\widetilde{T^{\prime\prime}}
in
[2^{L}]
. This gives a bijection between
(n-j)
-intervals in
[2^{n}]
and
(L-j)
-intervals in
[2^{L}]
. Now
|T^{\prime\prime}\cap I|=|\widetilde{T^{\prime\prime}}\cap\widetilde{I}|
, so the conditions (
5.6
) for
I
and
\widetilde{I}
are equivalent.
∎
