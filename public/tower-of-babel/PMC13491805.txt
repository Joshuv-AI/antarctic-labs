Boredom proneness and mind wandering in Chinese adolescents: mobile phone addiction as a mediator and sensation seeking as a moderator
Authors: Fang Wang, Ling Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMind wandering is common during adolescence and can interfere with academic performance and psychological well-being. Boredom proneness, a stable tendency to experience dissatisfaction in unstimulating contexts, has been associated with greater mind wandering, yet the mechanisms linking these constructs remain insufficiently understood. This study investigated whether mobile phone addiction mediates the association between boredom proneness and mind wandering, and whether sensation seeking moderates this pathway.MethodData were collected from 3,736 adolescents (mean age = 14.98 years, SD = 1.64) enrolled in 17 schools across Guangdong Province, China. Participants completed validated self-report measures of boredom proneness, mobile phone addiction, sensation seeking, and mind wandering. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted using SPSS 23.0 and Mplus 8.3.ResultsBoredom proneness was positively associated with mind wandering. Mobile phone addiction partially mediated this association, and sensation seeking moderated the pathway between boredom proneness and mobile phone addiction.ConclusionsFindings suggest that mobile phone addiction helps explain the link between boredom proneness and mind wandering, with stronger associations observed among adolescents high in sensation seeking. Given the cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be drawn, but the results highlight potential targets for interventions aimed at regulating boredom and reducing problematic phone use.

Introduction

Mind wandering constitutes an attentional decoupling from immediate task demands toward stimulus-independent mental content that is unrelated to the current environment or activity [1]. Among school-aged children, mind wandering occupies approximately 20% to 39% of daily activities [2, 3], and has been consistently associated with reduced learning outcomes, impaired reading comprehension, and lower overall academic performance [4–6]. Moreover, mind wandering is closely linked to attentional deficits and symptoms of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), highlighting its significance in both typical and atypical cognitive development [7, 8].

Much prior research has focused on adult populations, emphasizing antecedents such as executive control failures [9, 10] and negative affective states [4, 11]. However, developmental mechanisms underlying mind wandering in adolescents remain less well understood. Adolescence is a period of heightened cognitive and emotional reorganization, during which the interplay between attentional control, motivational states, and external environmental demands may uniquely shape the frequency and content of mind wandering.

Recent theoretical work underscores that mind wandering contains both maladaptive and adaptive dimensions, making it important to differentiate these perspectives when examining adolescents’ cognitive processes. On the maladaptive side, mind wandering is often characterized as a disengagement from the external task environment. Adolescents with high boredom proneness—marked by chronic dissatisfaction and sensitivity to under-stimulating contexts—may be particularly susceptible to drifting attention, as they seek alternative sources of cognitive stimulation. Such task-unrelated thinking can interfere with sustained attention, hinder learning, and compromise performance in academically demanding settings. In this sense, boredom proneness may heighten the occurrence of mind wandering episodes that carry clear performance costs.

Conversely, from an adaptive perspective, mind wandering is increasingly understood as a functional cognitive activity. Bortolotti et al. (2024) [12] conceptualize the brain as a predictive engine that constantly constructs internal models to anticipate future events and guide behavior. Mind wandering may therefore serve as a space for adolescents to simulate possibilities, explore imagined scenarios, and rehearse strategies for upcoming challenges, especially when external demands are minimal. Empirical findings further support its constructive role: research using incubation paradigms, including variants of the Unusual Uses Task, shows that periods of internally generated thought can enhance divergent thinking by allowing associative networks to reorganize and integrate prior information [13–15]. These insights suggest that even boredom-triggered mind wandering, though potentially distracting in the short term, may simultaneously cultivate cognitive flexibility, imaginative exploration, and creative problem-solving capacities.

The mediating role of mobile phone addiction

Mobile phones have become a ubiquitous tool for adolescents, offering immediate access to diverse, stimulating, and interactive experiences. In China, the number of mobile phone users reached approximately 1.123 billion by mid-2025, with adolescents accounting for 16.7% of this population (CNNIC, 2025) [16]. Mobile phone addiction (MPA) also referred to as problematic mobile phone use or mobile phone dependence, has emerged as a critical concern, particularly among adolescents, due to its association with physical, psychological, and social impairments [17, 18]. Epidemiological evidence shows that 21.3% of Chinese adolescents meet criteria for mobile phone addiction, markedly higher than in Spain (14.8%) and the United Kingdom (10%) [16, 19], highlighting the public health relevance of this population.

Boredom proneness has consistently been identified as a risk factor for excessive mobile phone use. Billieux et al. (2015) [17] proposed a pathway model in which sensation seeking drives compulsive mobile phone use via an extraversion-related pathway. Since sensation seeking is strongly associated with boredom proneness [20, 21], adolescents prone to boredom may pursue novel and stimulating experiences to alleviate under-arousal states [18, 22]. The multifunctional and customizable features of mobile phones make them particularly effective in fulfilling these needs. Theoretical frameworks further indicate that boredom arises not only from low arousal but also from a perceived lack of meaning in ongoing activities [23]. Consequently, adolescents high in boredom proneness may turn to mobile phones both to escape monotony and to access stimulating or meaningful experiences.

Recent evidence underscores the link between boredom and technology-related addictions. Cannito et al. (2023) [24] demonstrated that boredom predicts internet addiction through attentional biases toward social networking platforms. This finding supports the hypothesis that mobile phone addiction mediates the relationship between boredom proneness and maladaptive cognitive outcomes, including mind wandering. Indeed, boredom has been robustly linked to increased mind wandering, as individuals in bored states are more likely to disengage attention from the present task [1]. Mobile phones provide a readily accessible outlet for such disengagement, and excessive use can fragment attentional resources, impair executive function, and exacerbate mind wandering tendencies [17, 18].

The moderating role of sensation seeking

Sensation seeking, defined as the need for varied, complex, and intense sensory, social, and cognitive experiences [20, 21], is a key predictor of risk-taking and high-arousal behaviors. In the context of digital media, high sensation seeking predicts more frequent and intense smartphone use, including social networking, multimedia consumption, and interactive applications [18]. Mobile phones provide immediate access to novel and stimulating content, particularly in contexts of low external stimulation or perceived boredom. Consequently, adolescents with elevated sensation-seeking tendencies may be more prone to develop mobile phone addiction and experience its associated cognitive consequences.

Sensation seeking may also moderate the relationship between boredom proneness and mobile phone addiction. Adolescents prone to boredom may turn to mobile phones to alleviate under-stimulation, but those high in sensation seeking are particularly motivated to seek intense and novel experiences. Thus, higher levels of sensation seeking are expected to strengthen the pathway from boredom proneness to mobile phone addiction, consistent with prior findings on the interaction between motivational drives and technology use [18, 22].

Adolescence is a developmental stage marked by heightened susceptibility to psychological difficulties. Within the Chinese context, adolescents face distinctive sociocultural and educational demands, including intense academic pressure, extended study hours, and highly structured routines, which often exacerbate stress and restrict adaptive coping opportunities [25, 26]. Such conditions may foster boredom proneness, which has been identified as a significant cognitive–emotional vulnerability linked to maladaptive outcomes. Boredom proneness can trigger increased mind wandering, leading adolescents to disengage from goal-directed activities and to rely on mobile phones as a readily accessible means of cognitive and emotional regulation. While mobile phone use may provide temporary relief, excessive reliance can escalate into mobile phone addiction, which in turn contributes to greater attentional difficulties and psychological distress.

Given these dynamics, it is both timely and necessary to investigate the effect of boredom proneness on mind wandering among Chinese adolescents, with a particular focus on the mediating role of mobile phone addiction and the moderating role of sensation seeking. Cross-cultural evidence indicates that these mechanisms may not fully align with those observed in Western populations, highlighting the importance of situating Chinese adolescents’ behavioral and psychological trajectories within their specific cultural milieu. Accordingly, the present study aims to clarify these relationships, providing a more nuanced understanding of how cognitive tendencies, personality traits, and technology use jointly contribute to adolescent development. Methods

Participants and procedure

Following recommendations by Memon et al. (2019) [27], a priori and post hoc power analyses were conducted using G*Power 3.1 [28] to determine the adequacy of the sample size for detecting moderating effects. Assuming a medium effect size (ƒ² = 0.15), α = 0.05, and power = 0.90, the required minimum sample size was estimated at 2,673 [29]. Post hoc analysis confirmed that the final sample exceeded this threshold, achieving statistical power above 0.99 [30].

Participants were recruited from seventh through ninth grades across eight middle schools and tenth through twelfth grades across nine secondary institutions in Guangdong Province, Southern China. Data were collected over a two-week period (October 10–21, 2022) using Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn/), a widely used and secure Chinese online survey platform. This platform was selected due to its accessibility for large student populations and its ability to ensure standardized administration across multiple schools, though potential biases associated with online self-report data are acknowledged. To enhance data quality, attention checks were embedded, time stamps were recorded, and both unusually short (< 2 min) and excessively long (> 10 min) completion times were flagged.

Initially, 3,953 adolescents participated. Exclusion criteria included patterned responses (e.g., endorsing the same option for all items), invalid completion times, and more than 50% missing data. After applying these criteria, 3,736 valid questionnaires were retained, yielding an effective response rate of 94.51%.

The survey was administered during scheduled class sessions under the supervision of trained research assistants to minimize distractions and ensure compliance with study instructions. Participants accessed the mobile-compatible survey link and were first presented with an informed consent statement (“I voluntarily agree to participate in the survey”). Only those providing affirmative consent proceeded. Anonymity and confidentiality were emphasized, and students were assured that their participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any point without consequence.

Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Huaihua University. The measures employed were validated Chinese-language scales, previously adapted and normed for use with adolescent populations, ensuring cultural appropriateness and linguistic equivalence.

Boredom proneness

The research utilized the Short Boredom Proneness Scale (Struk et al., 2017 [31]). This scale comprises of 8 items designed to measure individual proneness to boredom, such as “I have difficulty entertaining myself.” Survey responses were quantified using a standardized 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Elevated scores reflect greater self-reported boredom propensity. Previous research conducted in China has demonstrated the questionnaire’s reliability and validity [32, 33]. In the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), the results showed that the structural validity of the scale was acceptable, with standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) = 0.05, comparative fit index (CFI) = 0.90, and Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) = 0.89.The scale demonstrated good internal consistency(Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.79).

Mobile phone addiction

To evaluate problematic mobile phone use, this study employed an adapted version of the Internet Addiction Scale originally formulated by Young (1998) [34]. The items were revised to reflect mobile phone usage contexts and reformulated as affirmative statements (e.g., “I prioritize spending time on my mobile phone over completing school assignments.”). The scale was translated into Chinese and back-translated by Hong et al. (2012) [35] to ensure semantic equivalence. The final version consisted of 11 items measuring the frequency of mobile phone-related behaviours (e.g., prolonged use beyond intention), evaluated using a 5-point Likert scale, with endpoints anchored at 1 (not at all) and 5 (always). A mean score of 3.2 or above was employed as the threshold for identifying mobile phone addiction [36]. In the present study, CFA suggested that the structural validity of the scale was acceptable [37] with SRMR = 0.06, CFI = 0.91, and TLI = 0.88. The Cronbach’s α was 0.89.

Sensation seeking

The Sensation Seeking Questionnaire, developed by Steinberg et al. (2008) [38], served as a measure of sensation seeking. To ensure cultural appropriateness, Li et al., (2010) [39] translated the questionnaire into Chinese. This measure evaluates the inclination towards seeking thrill or novelty rather than impulsivity. Previous research conducted with Chinese adolescents [40] has confirmed the reliability and validity of the scale. The questionnaire includes six items, exemplified by the statement, “I enjoy participating in novel and stimulating activities, even when they are somewhat daunting.” Responses were collected using a 6-point Likert scale anchored by 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree), where higher ratings imply greater tendency for sensation seeking. For the present study, CFA suggested that the structural validity of the scale was acceptable with SRMR = 0.06, CFI = 0.90, and TLI = 0.86. The measure also demonstrated a good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.78).

Mind wandering

Mind wandering was evaluated via the Mind Wandering Questionnaire (MWQ) developed by Mrazek et al. (2013) [41]. The MWQ comprise of 20 items designed to capture the frequency of mind wandering experiences (e.g., “A consistent tendency exists for attentional focus to disengage during formal presentations or lectures.”), quantified using a standardized 5-point Likert scale (1 = rarely to 5 = almost constantly), yielding a composite score range of 20–100, with higher composite scores indicating a greater tendency toward mind wandering. In present research, the Chinese version of the MWQ, modified and validated by Carciofo et al. (2014) [42], was utilised. This version demonstrated robust internal consistency, evidenced by a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85 reported in prior research. In the current study, the CFA model generated a good fit, with SRMR = 0.03, CFI = 0.96, TLI = 0.91. Cronbach’s α was 0.84.

Attrition and missing data

Across the study variables, the proportion of missing responses ranged from 0.0% to 20.0%. Little’s Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) test (Little, 1988) [43] indicated that the missingness pattern did not deviate from randomness (χ² (80) = 73.19, p = 0.69), and the normed chi-square value (χ²/df = 0.91 < 3; Bollen, 1989) further supported the adequacy of the MCAR assumption [44]. Given this pattern, all cases were retained for analysis. To evaluate whether missing data introduced systematic bias, we compared adolescents who provided complete data with those who had at least one missing value. Group differences were examined across demographic factors and the primary constructs of the study. The two groups did not differ in age (F (1, 3734) = 0.13, p = 0.72) or socioeconomic status (F (1, 3734) = 0.07, p = 0.80). Similarly, boredom proneness showed no significant group differences (F (1, 3734) = 2.16, p = 0.14). Small statistical differences emerged for mind wandering (F (1, 3734) = 8.01, p = 0.01) and mobile phone addiction (F (1, 3731) = 4.22, p = 0.04), but the associated effect sizes were trivial (η² < 0.02), suggesting minimal practical impact. Sensation seeking did not differ between groups. Taken together, these findings indicate that missing data had minimal influence on the study variables.

Data analysis

Data analysis in the present research was utilizing SPSS 23.0 and Mplus 8.3 To ensure a comprehensive examination of the data, Harman’s single-factor test, descriptive statistics, and Pearson correlation analyses were carried out in SPSS 23.0. The hypothesized model was then tested using the PROCESS macro (Model 14) within SPSS. Furthermore, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed in Mplus 8.3 to evaluate the measurement model’s construct validity.

Results

Common method bias assessment

Data collection via self-reports within a cross-sectional framework increases susceptibility to common method bias, potentially resulting in systematic measurement errors and inflating the observed associations among the four primary constructs. To evaluate the extent of common method variance, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted [45]. The results indicated that the first factor accounted for only 18.74% of the total variance, well below the commonly accepted threshold of 40%, suggesting that common method bias was not a significant concern.

Testing for the mediation effect

As hypothesized, mobile phone addiction was tested as a mediator between boredom proneness and mind wandering. Following standardized statistical procedures, we implemented a modified four-step mediation analysis [46, 47] requiring: (a) a significant direct correlation between boredom proneness and mind wandering; (b) a significant relationship between boredom proneness and mobile phone addiction; (c) a significant correlation between mobile phone addiction and mind wandering when controlling for boredom proneness; and (d) a significant indirect effect of boredom proneness on mind wandering through mobile phone addiction. The final condition was verified using bias-corrected percentile bootstrapping.

Tables 3 and 4 confirm all prerequisites: boredom proneness significantly predicted mind wandering (β = 0.984, 95% CI [0.931, 1.036], p < 0.001, f² = 0.18) and mobile phone addiction (β = 0.489, 95% CI [0.450, 0.528], p < 0.001, f² = 0.19). Mobile phone addiction remained positively associated with mind wandering after controlling for boredom proneness (β = 0.230, 95% CI [0.817, 0.272], p < 0.001, β = 0.18,). And the bias-corrected percentile bootstrap method provided evidence of a noteworthy indirect influence of boredom proneness on mind wandering, mediated by Mobile phone addiction, ab = 0.112, SE = 0.015, 95% CI = [0.085, 0.142]. Testing moderated mediation

A moderated mediation analysis was evaluated with Hayes’ PROCESS macro (Model 7) [47] to examine whether sensation seeking moderates the relationship between boredom proneness and mobile phone addiction. As presented in Table 5, the interaction effect was significant (β = 0.07, 95% CI [0.02, 0.09], p < 0.01, f² = 0.02), indicating that the effect of boredom proneness on mobile phone addiction was conditional on adolescents’ levels of sensation seeking. Simple slopes analysis (Fig. 2) visualized this interaction by plotting the boredom proneness-mobile phone addiction relationship at low (M − 1SD) and high (M + 1SD) sensation seeking levels. Results confirmed a consistently positive association between boredom proneness and mobile phone addiction across both conditions and significantly different from zero for both adolescents with low sensation seeking (M-1SD) and adolescents with high sensation seeking (M + 1SD). But this effect for adolescents with high sensation seeking (βsimple = 0.354, 95% CI [0.292, 0.415], p < 0.001) was weaker than that for respondents with low sensation seeking (βsimple = 0.616, 95% CI [0.553, 0.680], p < 0.001). This pattern suggests that adolescents who are both prone to boredom and high in sensation seeking are at the greatest risk for developing problematic mobile phone use. Interestingly, the moderation effect size was modest, suggesting that although sensation seeking amplifies the boredom and mobile phone addiction pathway, boredom proneness itself remains a robust predictor across different sensation-seeking levels.

Discussion

The present study investigated how boredom proneness contributes to mind wandering among Chinese adolescents, with mobile phone addiction as a mediator and sensation seeking as a moderator. Consistent with prior findings, boredom proneness was positively associated with mind wandering [48, 49]. Adolescents who are more boredom-prone tend to disengage from external tasks and immerse themselves in stimulus-independent thought, especially in monotonous or unstimulating contexts. This aligns with research showing that boredom enhances attentional disengagement and increases the likelihood of mental drift [50].

Importantly, the study demonstrated that mobile phone addiction mediates this relationship. Adolescents prone to boredom appear to use mobile phones as an easily accessible coping mechanism for under-stimulation, yet excessive engagement fragments attention and fosters cognitive lapses. This finding resonates with Cannito et al. (2023) [24], who showed that boredom predicts internet addiction through attentional biases toward social networks. By extending these insights to mobile phones, our study highlights how maladaptive attentional patterns can translate boredom into compulsive digital engagement, which in turn undermines attentional focus and elevates susceptibility to mind wandering.

Beyond attentional processes, the role of visual and sensory features embedded in mobile phones deserves emphasis. Neuromarketing research indicates that color and visual salience strongly capture attention and bias decision-making, particularly under conditions of cognitive strain [51]. Smartphones employ colorful icons, dynamic notifications, and flashing cues that act as persistent bottom-up triggers, diverting attention from ongoing tasks [52]. Moreover, recent evidence on colored valence in lexical decision tasks [12] suggests that the perceptual characteristics of stimuli can modulate cognitive evaluation. Applied to the smartphone context, visually salient cues not only draw attention but may also alter cognitive appraisal of digital content, reinforcing compulsive engagement. Taken together, these insights imply that visual features of smartphones interact with boredom-driven attentional vulnerability, accelerating both addiction and mind wandering.

A further contribution of this study is the nuanced role of sensation seeking. While prior models [17, 18] emphasized sensation seeking as a risk factor for compulsive digital use, the current findings suggest a more complex pattern. Adolescents high in sensation seeking did not uniformly show stronger relationship between boredom and mobile phone addiction effects; instead, the moderating effect revealed that high sensation seekers sometimes displayed weaker boredom-related vulnerability. One possible explanation is that high sensation seekers pursue diverse offline outlets (e.g., sports, peer activities, creative exploration) that compete with digital engagement, thereby diluting the boredom–phone use link. In contrast, adolescents with moderate sensation seeking may lack such compensatory strategies, channeling boredom more directly into phone overuse. This interpretation underscores the importance of considering sensation seeking not as a unidirectional risk factor but as a contextual trait that interacts with environmental affordances.

Finally, the findings can be situated within broader theories of attention and cognitive control. Mind wandering has been linked to the activity of the default mode network (DMN), which supports internally directed thought, imagination, and prospective simulation. From this perspective, boredom may serve as a trigger for DMN-dominated cognition, while mobile phone addiction represents an external distraction that competes with goal-directed networks. Excessive reliance on external stimuli, such as phones, may therefore impair the adaptive aspects of mind wandering—its capacity to foster creativity, planning, and problem-solving (Baird et al., 2012) [13]—and instead exacerbate maladaptive attentional lapses.

Conclusion

This study provides empirical evidence that boredom proneness predicts mind wandering in adolescents, both directly and indirectly through mobile phone addiction. Moreover, sensation seeking moderates this process, revealing that the boredom–mobile phone addiction relationship is conditional on individual motivational traits. By integrating recent cross-cultural and cognitive perspectives, the findings highlight the dual role of mobile phones as both a tool for stimulation and a risk factor for attentional fragmentation. These results should be viewed as exploratory given the cross-sectional design, but they contribute to a growing body of research that seeks to clarify the mechanisms linking boredom, technology use, and adolescent cognition.

Limitations and implications

Several limitations warrant caution. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to trace temporal dynamics between boredom, mobile phone addiction, and mind wandering. Second, reliance on self-report measures may be affected by social desirability or recall bias. Future research should incorporate behavioral or neurocognitive measures of attentional control, such as working memory or inhibitory capacity, to deepen mechanistic insight. Third, the sample consisted of adolescents from southern China, raising questions of cultural specificity. Cross-cultural comparisons have shown variability in the prevalence and expression of digital addiction (Lopez-Fernandez et al., 2017) [19], suggesting that cultural values surrounding technology, academic pressure, and leisure opportunities may shape the observed patterns. Thus, findings may not generalize to adolescents in other sociocultural contexts.

Although the present model accounted for key psychological constructs, it is possible that unmeasured variables may also contribute to the associations observed. Traits such as impulsivity, which is closely linked to risk-taking and problematic technology use (Billieux et al., 2010) [53], or social anxiety, which may increase reliance on digital devices as a coping strategy (Caplan, 2007) [54], could influence both mobile phone addiction and mind wandering. Future research incorporating these constructs, as well as other self-regulation and socio-emotional factors, may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms linking boredom proneness and attentional outcomes.

Despite these limitations, the study has several practical implications. For educators and parents, the findings underscore the importance of fostering adaptive boredom coping strategies, such as creative exploration or structured offline activities, to mitigate reliance on digital devices. Clinicians and school psychologists may consider interventions targeting attentional control, reducing compulsive phone checking, and promoting balanced digital habits. These findings also point to practical avenues for school-based and family-based interventions. For instance, programs that help students develop adaptive boredom-coping strategies, strengthen self-regulation skills, or establish healthier digital habits may reduce reliance on mobile phones and attenuate boredom-related mind wandering. Finally, the recognition of both maladaptive and adaptive facets of mind wandering points to the value of helping adolescents’ harness internally directed cognition for creativity and problem-solving, rather than allowing it to be hijacked by compulsive smartphone use.
