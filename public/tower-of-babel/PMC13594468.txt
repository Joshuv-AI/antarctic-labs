Left ventricular wall thickness and functional recovery after atrial fibrillation ablation in patients with systolic dysfunction
Authors: Masato Okada, Nobuaki Tanaka, Yasushi Koyama, Koji Tanaka, Yuko Hirao, Naoko Miyazaki, Kohei Iwasa, Heitaro Watanabe, Yoshitaka Iwanaga, Atsunori Okamura, Katsuomi Iwakura, Koichi Inoue, Yasushi Sakata

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeft ventricular functional recovery after atrial fibrillation (AF) ablation varies widely in patients with systolic dysfunction.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate whether baseline left ventricular wall thickness (LVWT) and its regional heterogeneity predict recovery of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) after AF ablation.MethodsOf 5535 consecutive patients undergoing first-time AF ablation (2012–2024), 342 with a baseline LVEF of <50% (median age 64 years; 86% male; 19% with previous myocardial infarction) were retrospectively analyzed. Multidetector computed tomography was performed before ablation, and LVWT was quantified using a 16-segment model at end-diastole. Mean LVWT and its standard deviation (SD) across segments were calculated. The primary endpoint was the 6-month change in LVEF (ΔLVEF) on echocardiography.ResultsMedian mean LVWT and SD of LVWT were 7.03 mm (6.19–8.00) and 1.20 mm (0.95–1.50), respectively. LVEF improved from 38.7% (32.0%–44.4%) to 56.0% (44.3%–62.0%) at 6 months (P < .001). In multivariable linear regression, higher mean LVWT was independently associated with greater ΔLVEF (1.72% per 1 mm; 95% confidence interval, 0.83–2.61; P < .001), whereas greater LVWT heterogeneity (SD) was associated with less ΔLVEF (−4.02% per 1 mm; 95% confidence interval, −6.64 to −1.40; P = .003). Patients with reduced thickness and high heterogeneity (mean LVWT <7.0 mm; SD of LVWT >1.2 mm) had the highest incidence of heart failure hospitalization or cardiovascular death.ConclusionBaseline LVWT and its regional heterogeneity were independently associated with postablation functional recovery in patients with a baseline LVEF of <50%. Preprocedural LVWT assessment may help estimate myocardial reserve and expected functional recovery after AF ablation.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Catheter ablation, Computed tomography, Heart failure, Left ventricular wall thickness

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) and heart failure (HF) often coexist, amplify each other, and contribute to left ventricular (LV) systolic dysfunction.1 In this population, AF ablation with restoration of sinus rhythm can improve cardiac function, symptoms, exercise capacity, quality of life, and cardiovascular outcomes.2,3 However, recovery in LV ejection fraction (LVEF) varies widely, and its determinants remain incompletely defined.

Underlying myocardial injury, including fibrosis or scarring, likely contributes to this heterogeneity. Late gadolinium enhancement on cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging is an established marker of replacement fibrosis, and its presence or greater extent has been associated with a lower likelihood of LV functional recovery after AF ablation.4,5 Nevertheless, CMR imaging is often limited by availability, cost, and contraindications, particularly in patients with implanted cardiac devices. In contrast, multidetector computed tomography (MDCT) is frequently performed before AF ablation for anatomic assessment, enabling quantification of LV wall thickness (LVWT) as a noninvasive surrogate of myocardial integrity. Wall thinning may reflect advanced remodeling with myocyte loss and replacement fibrosis, whereas preserved thickness may indicate structurally preserved myocardium with greater potential for functional recovery after rhythm control.

Despite its potential, the association between preprocedural LVWT and postablation recovery of LVEF has not been systematically evaluated. We investigated whether baseline MDCT-derived mean LVWT and regional LVWT heterogeneity are associated with LV functional recovery after AF ablation in patients with an LVEF of <50%.

Methods

Study design and population

This single-center retrospective study included 5535 consecutive patients who underwent first-time AF ablation between January 2012 and September 2024 at Sakurabashi Watanabe Advanced Healthcare Hospital, Osaka, Japan. The present analysis was designed to evaluate the association between baseline MDCT-derived LVWT metrics and LV functional recovery, defined as the change in LVEF (ΔLVEF) from baseline to 6-month follow-up transthoracic echocardiography (TTE). Among the overall cohort, 388 patients (7.0%) had LV systolic dysfunction, defined as a baseline LVEF of <50% assessed by TTE using the biplane modified Simpson method. Of these, 46 were excluded because baseline MDCT was not performed (n = 26), MDCT image quality was inadequate for analysis (n = 2), additional cardiovascular intervention was performed within 6 months after ablation (n = 3), or follow-up TTE data at 6 months (±2 months) were unavailable (n = 15) (Supplemental Figure 1). The final cohort comprised 342 patients.

Written informed consent for ablation and retrospective data use was obtained from all patients. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review board of Sakurabashi Watanabe Advanced Healthcare Hospital (SWH #21-66).

Cardiac MDCT acquisition and image analysis

Preprocedural cardiac MDCT was performed as part of routine care before AF ablation to assess left atrial (LA) anatomy, thrombus, and coronary artery disease. Patients with contraindications to iodinated contrast, such as renal impairment or contrast allergy, did not undergo MDCT. Scans were acquired on a 256-slice scanner (Brilliance iCT, Philips Medical Systems) using electrocardiogram-gated, contrast-enhanced imaging with bolus tracking according to institutional protocols. Scan parameters included a tube voltage of 120 kV and collimation of 2 × 128 × 0.625 mm. A nonionic iodinated contrast medium (Iopamiron 370, Bayer Yakuhin Ltd, Osaka, Japan) was administered via an antecubital vein at a total dose of approximately 0.6 mL/kg, typically at 3–4 mL/s, followed by a saline flush. Detailed acquisition parameters have been described previously.6

MDCT image analysis and LVWT quantification

All image analyses were performed by experienced radiologic technologists who were blinded to clinical and echocardiographic data, using vendor software (Comprehensive Cardiac Analysis, Extended Brilliance Workspace; Philips Medical Systems). Electrocardiogram-gated datasets were reconstructed across the cardiac cycle. After automated chamber segmentation, end-diastolic and end-systolic phases were determined using the LV volume–time curve. LVWT was quantified on end-diastolic short-axis images in all patients using the American Heart Association 16-segment model. For scans acquired during AF, multiple electrocardiogram-gated phases were reconstructed using vendor-specific reconstruction methods for irregular R-R intervals (Supplemental Method 1) and reviewed for image quality. After end-diastole was identified, the reconstruction with the least motion artifact and the clearest endocardial and epicardial borders was selected for LVWT analysis.

Endocardial and epicardial contours were then visually inspected and manually corrected as needed. Within each segment, LVWT was calculated as the mean contour-to-contour distance between the endocardial and epicardial borders and visualized on a bull’s-eye map using 1-mm color increments (Figure 1). AF ablation and follow-up

AF ablation was performed in accordance with contemporary guidelines and our institutional protocol, as previously described.7,8 Pulmonary vein (PV) isolation was the primary strategy and was performed using either radiofrequency energy with an irrigated-tip catheter (Thermocool or QDOT, Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA) or a 28-mm cryoballoon (Arctic Front Advance, Medtronic Inc, Minneapolis, MN). Radiofrequency catheter ablation was performed under the guidance of a 3-dimensional reconstruction image of the LA. Cryoballoon ablation has been performed since 2014 in anatomically eligible patients. The procedural endpoint was PV isolation, which was confirmed by bidirectional conduction block between the LA and the PVs. A cavotricuspid isthmus block line was created if a typical atrial flutter was detected before or during the procedure. After PV isolation, isoproterenol (4–20 μg/min) was administered to identify reconnection and/or the presence of extra-PV triggers. When extra-PV triggers were identified, additional ablation was performed using radiofrequency energy to eliminate these arrhythmogenic sources. Other procedures, such as linear ablation and posterior isolation, were performed at the operator’s discretion.

Follow-up and endpoints

Patients were observed in the hospital for 3 days after AF ablation and discharged when clinically stable. Oral anticoagulation was continued for at least 3 months. Antiarrhythmic drugs were prescribed at the attending physician’s discretion and were generally discontinued after 3 months. Follow-up visits were scheduled at 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months after ablation and every 6 months thereafter. A 12-lead electrocardiogram was obtained at each visit, and TTE and 24-hour Holter monitoring were performed at 6 and 12 months. Patients were instructed to check their pulse regularly and contact the clinic if recurrence was suspected; a portable event recorder (HCG-801, Omron Healthcare Co, Ltd, Kyoto, Japan) was used as needed.

The primary endpoint was ΔLVEF from baseline to 6-month follow-up, assessed by TTE using the modified Simpson method. Secondary clinical endpoints were (1) a composite of HF hospitalization or cardiovascular death and (2) AF recurrence. HF hospitalization was defined as unplanned hospitalization for worsening HF requiring intravenous therapy. Cardiovascular death included death caused by HF, sudden cardiac death, acute myocardial infarction, stroke, or other cardiovascular causes. AF recurrence was defined as any atrial tachyarrhythmia lasting ≥30 seconds occurring beyond 3 months after ablation.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as counts (percentages). Changes in TTE parameters from baseline to 6 months were evaluated using the paired t test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test for continuous variables and McNemar’s test for paired categorical variables. End-diastolic LVWT differences were assessed using the Friedman test across the 16-segment model and, in an exploratory ventricular-level analysis, across basal, mid, and apical levels. Pairwise ventricular-level comparisons were performed using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm adjustment.

Associations between MDCT-derived LVWT metrics (mean LVWT and its SD across 16 segments) and ΔLVEF were assessed using Pearson’s correlation. Potential nonlinear associations between LVWT metrics and ΔLVEF were explored using restricted cubic spline regression with 3 knots, and overall and nonlinearity P values were reported. Multivariable linear regression models were used to evaluate the association between baseline LVWT metrics and ΔLVEF. The primary model included only baseline variables: age, sex, CHADS2 score, AF type, previous myocardial infarction, baseline LA volume index, baseline LVEF, mean LVWT, and SD of LVWT. To assess whether the associations between LVWT metrics and ΔLVEF were influenced by postablation rhythm- or rate-related factors, post hoc sensitivity analyses were performed by additionally adjusting for AF recurrence before follow-up TTE, AF rhythm or heart rate at the 6-month follow-up TTE, or change in heart rate from baseline to 6 months. LVWT metrics were primarily analyzed as continuous variables.

For clinical interpretability, patients were categorized into 4 LVWT phenotypes using thresholds approximating the cohort median (mean LVWT of 7.0 mm and SD of 1.2 mm). Differences in ΔLVEF across phenotypes were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post hoc pairwise comparisons with Holm adjustment. For secondary endpoints, event-free survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Univariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for clinical outcomes according to LVWT phenotype.

Interobserver reproducibility of LVWT measurements was assessed in a subset of 30 randomly selected patients, as described in Supplemental Method 2. Statistical analyses were performed using MedCalc version 23 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium) and R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). No data were missing for variables included in the primary analyses. 2-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

MDCT-derived LVWT parameters

During preprocedural MDCT acquisition, AF rhythm was present in 268 patients (78.4%). End-diastolic LVWT differed significantly among the 16 segments (Friedman test, P < .001; Figure 2). Exploratory analysis by ventricular level showed a significant base-to-apex gradient, with the greatest LVWT in basal segments and the lowest LVWT in apical segments (Supplemental Table 2). The median values of mean LVWT and SD of LVWT were 7.03 mm (6.19–8.00) and 1.20 mm (0.95–1.50), respectively; their distributions are presented in Supplemental Figure 2. Mean LVWT showed only a weak inverse association with baseline LV end-diastolic volume index (r = −0.09; P = .094), whereas SD of LVWT showed no association with this parameter (r = 0.00; P = .987). Interobserver reproducibility was high for both mean LVWT and SD of LVWT (Supplemental Method 2).

Changes in TTE parameters

Follow-up TTE performed at 6 months (±2 months) showed significant changes in rhythm status, heart rate, and LA and LV chamber measurements (Table 2). AF rhythm was documented at the time of TTE in 274 patients (80%) at baseline and in 17 patients (5.0%) at follow-up, whereas heart rate decreased from 97 ± 23 beats/min at baseline to 71 ± 12 beats/min (both P < .001). The LA volume index decreased from 37 (31–46) to 25 mL/m2 (20–33), LV end-diastolic volume decreased from 123 (101–157) to 105 mL (88–129), and LV end-systolic volume decreased from 73 (56–97) to 47 mL (35–65), with a corresponding increase in LVEF from 38.7% (32.0%–44.4%) to 56.0% (44.3%–62.0%) (all P < .001).

Associations between baseline LVWT metrics and ΔLVEF

Baseline LVWT metrics were associated with improvement in LVEF, with greater mean LVWT and lower heterogeneity associated with larger ΔLVEF (Figure 3A and 3B). In the primary multivariable model including baseline variables only (Table 3), higher mean LVWT was independently associated with greater ΔLVEF (regression coefficient 1.72% per 1 mm; 95% CI, 0.83–2.61; P < .001), whereas greater SD of LVWT was independently associated with less improvement (−4.02% per 1 mm; 95% CI, −6.64 to −1.40; P = .003). In post hoc sensitivity analyses, the associations of mean LVWT and SD of LVWT with ΔLVEF were materially unchanged after additional adjustment for AF recurrence before follow-up TTE, AF rhythm during TTE acquisition, heart rate at the 6-month follow-up TTE, or change in heart rate from baseline to 6 months (Supplemental Table 3).

Clinical outcomes

During a median follow-up of 51.7 months (interquartile range 27.5–78.5 months), the composite of HF hospitalization or cardiovascular death occurred in 38 patients (11.1%), including 9 cardiovascular deaths (6 owing to HF, 2 owing to sudden cardiac death, and 1 owing to acute myocardial infarction). AF recurrence occurred in 112 patients (32.7%).

Kaplan–Meier analyses showed significant differences across the 4 LVWT phenotypes for a composite of HF hospitalization or cardiovascular death. The incidence was lowest in the preserved-thickness/low-heterogeneity phenotype and highest in the reduced-thickness/high-heterogeneity phenotype (log-rank P = .018). In contrast, no significant difference was observed in AF recurrence across phenotypes (log-rank P = .735) (Figure 5). Univariable Cox estimates for each phenotype are also presented in Figure 5.

Discussion

Major findings

This study showed that baseline MDCT-derived LVWT metrics were independently associated with subsequent LV functional recovery after first-time AF ablation in patients with an LVEF of <50%. Greater mean LVWT was associated with a larger improvement in LVEF at 6 months, whereas greater regional heterogeneity in LVWT was associated with less improvement, even after adjustment for clinically relevant baseline variables. These associations were materially unchanged in post hoc sensitivity analyses accounting for AF recurrence before follow-up TTE, rhythm status during TTE acquisition, and heart rate–related factors. In exploratory phenotype analyses, patients with preserved thickness and low heterogeneity showed the most favorable functional recovery, whereas those with reduced thickness and high heterogeneity showed the least favorable recovery and the highest incidence of HF hospitalization or cardiovascular death. These findings suggest that MDCT-derived LVWT phenotyping may provide a practical preprocedural marker of myocardial reserve and expected functional recovery after AF ablation.

Mechanistic considerations

The extent of LV functional recovery after AF ablation is likely determined by the balance between reversible rhythm-related dysfunction and underlying myocardial disease. Mean LVWT may reflect the degree of global myocardial preservation. In patients with relatively preserved LVWT, systolic dysfunction may be driven predominantly by AF-related mechanisms, including rapid or irregular ventricular activation, loss of atrial contribution, impaired ventricular filling, and neurohormonal activation. By contrast, lower LVWT may indicate more advanced remodeling with diffuse myocyte loss, myocardial fibrosis, or chronic ischemic injury, thereby limiting the potential for functional recovery even when AF is successfully suppressed. This interpretation is consistent with the concept that AF-mediated cardiomyopathy and underlying structural cardiomyopathy often coexist, making reversibility difficult to predict before ablation.9

Regional heterogeneity in LVWT may provide additional information beyond mean wall thickness. A higher SD of LVWT may reflect nonuniform myocardial injury, such as postinfarction scar, patchy nonischemic fibrosis, or regional remodeling related to underlying cardiomyopathy. It may also indicate spatially variable myocardial reserve, in which preserved and impaired regions coexist. Such heterogeneity may attenuate coordinated LV recovery after restoration of sinus rhythm. Exploratory analyses stratified by previous myocardial infarction showed generally similar directional patterns, suggesting that LVWT metrics may provide information not fully captured by previous myocardial infarction status alone. In this framework, relatively preserved and homogeneous LVWT may suggest a larger reversible AF-mediated component, whereas reduced and heterogeneous LVWT may identify a phenotype in which underlying structural myocardial disease substantially limits recovery after rhythm control.

Relationship to previous studies and myocardial fibrosis

Although MDCT does not directly characterize myocardial tissue composition, LVWT can be interpreted within a broader framework of myocardial fibrosis and viability. CMR studies have shown that ventricular fibrosis or scar, particularly as assessed by late gadolinium enhancement, is associated with a lower likelihood of LV functional recovery after AF ablation.4,5 Similarly, studies in ischemic cardiomyopathy have demonstrated that marked wall thinning is associated with nonviable myocardium and limited recovery after revascularization.10, 11, 12 These observations support the biological plausibility that reduced LVWT may serve as an anatomic marker of limited myocardial reserve.

However, LVWT should not be regarded as a direct substitute for tissue characterization. CMR can characterize complementary aspects of myocardial fibrosis: diffuse interstitial fibrosis is commonly assessed using native T1 or extracellular volume mapping, whereas replacement fibrosis or scar is typically identified by late gadolinium enhancement.13, 14, 15, 16, 17 By contrast, MDCT-derived LVWT reflects the downstream anatomic consequences of myocardial remodeling rather than fibrosis itself. In this regard, previous work in patients undergoing cardiac resynchronization therapy suggested that MDCT-derived LVWT may help stratify reverse remodeling and clinical outcomes.18 The present findings extend this concept to AF ablation, suggesting that preprocedural MDCT may provide incremental information for estimating LV functional recovery in patients with systolic dysfunction without requiring a separate imaging test.

Importantly, our findings do not support the use of a single absolute wall-thickness cutoff to define reversibility. Although median-based thresholds of mean LVWT (7.0 mm) and SD of LVWT (1.2 mm) were used to create clinically interpretable phenotypes, these values were not intended to define normal, preserved, or hypertrophic myocardium. Rather, the categorical analysis was intended to illustrate the combined implications of lower wall thickness and greater regional heterogeneity. Consistent with this continuous framework, restricted cubic spline analyses showed no evidence of nonlinearity, suggesting that the associations between LVWT metrics and LVEF recovery are gradual rather than threshold dependent. Therefore, these median-based thresholds should be regarded as pragmatic descriptors rather than definitive clinical cutoffs. External validation is needed before any cutoff-based approach can be applied in clinical decision making.

Clinical implications

Contemporary guidelines recommend AF ablation in patients with HF and LV systolic dysfunction, particularly when tachycardia- or arrhythmia-induced cardiomyopathy is highly suspected.19,20 However, reliable preprocedural features that identify such patients remain insufficiently established, and the diagnosis is often made retrospectively. Moreover, AF-mediated cardiomyopathy and underlying structural cardiomyopathy often coexist, and conventional etiologic classification alone may not fully capture the potential for reversibility. In this context, MDCT-derived LVWT phenotyping may help refine preprocedural assessment of expected functional recovery after AF ablation. Patients with reduced and heterogeneous LVWT may warrant closer postablation follow-up because of a potentially lower likelihood of recovery, whereas preserved and relatively homogeneous LVWT may indicate greater myocardial reserve and support preprocedural discussions regarding expected recovery.

Study limitations

First, this was a single-center retrospective analysis and may be subject to selection bias. The cohort was limited to patients with a baseline LVEF of <50% who underwent baseline contrast-enhanced MDCT and had follow-up echocardiography at approximately 6 months. Therefore, patients without MDCT or follow-up TTE, including those with renal impairment or advanced HF, may have been underrepresented. In addition, the findings cannot be extrapolated to patients with preserved LVEF, in whom LVWT may reflect different structural phenotypes, and its clinical implications would need to be evaluated using different endpoints. The marked predominance of male patients may also limit the generalizability of the findings to female patients. Second, LVWT measurements may have been influenced by image quality, reconstruction, and phase selection, particularly in patients imaged during AF. Although interobserver reproducibility was high and contours were manually corrected as needed, residual measurement variability, including variability related to manual contour correction, cannot be excluded. Third, although we adjusted for clinically relevant covariates, residual confounding related to HF severity, medical therapy, cardiomyopathy substrate, and procedural heterogeneity cannot be excluded. In addition, specific cardiomyopathy substrates, including hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and infiltrative or storage cardiomyopathies, were uncommon or not systematically screened; therefore, the interpretation of LVWT phenotyping in these subgroups remains uncertain. Fourth, LV functional recovery was assessed at 6 months and may not fully capture later remodeling. Moreover, because AF recurrence was defined only after a 3-month blanking period, the window for evaluating rhythm-related influences before the 6-month follow-up TTE was limited. In addition, continuous rhythm monitoring was not routinely performed; therefore, asymptomatic recurrence may have been underdetected, and AF burden or ventricular rate during recurrent atrial tachyarrhythmias could not be fully assessed. Fifth, the long enrollment period may have introduced temporal confounding related to evolving ablation technology, HF therapy, clinical practice, and MDCT hardware or reconstruction methods. Finally, associations with clinical outcomes should be considered exploratory because event counts were limited, precluding robust multivariable adjustment.

Conclusion

Preprocedural LVWT and its regional heterogeneity, assessed by MDCT, were independently associated with LV functional recovery after AF ablation in patients with a baseline LVEF of <50%. LVWT phenotyping may help estimate myocardial reserve and support preprocedural assessment of expected functional recovery after AF ablation. External validation in independent multicenter cohorts is warranted to confirm the generalizability of these findings.
