Health-related quality of life in community-acquired pneumonia: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Camilla Koch Ryrsø, Maria Hein Hegelund, Daniel Faurholt-Jepsen, Arnold Matovu Dungu, Christian Ritz, Mette Frahm Olsen, Rikke Krogh-Madsen, Birgitte Lindegaard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommunity-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is a leading cause of hospitalization and often results in impaired health-related quality of life (HRQoL). The prognostic value of HRQoL at admission and its recovery trajectory after CAP remain unclear. We investigated associations between HRQoL at admission and adverse outcomes, including ICU admission, rehospitalization, and mortality, and described changes in HRQoL 180 days post-discharge. In this prospective cohort study, 552 patients hospitalized with CAP completed the EQ-5D-5L questionnaire at admission; 262 patients were reassessed 180 days post-discharge. Patients were categorized into tertiles based on the EQ-5D utility index and dimension-specific scores. Associations between HRQoL and outcomes were analyzed using logistic and Cox regression models. The mean EQ-5D utility index at admission was 0.609 ± 0.266. Severe impairments were most common in usual activities (42%) and mobility (30%) dimensions. Lower HRQoL at admission (lowest vs. highest tertile) was associated with an increased risk of rehospitalization at 30 days (aHR 1.92, 95% CI: 1.18–3.13), 90 days (aHR 2.01, 95% CI: 1.33–3.03), and 180 days (aHR 1.82, 95% CI: 1.27–2.59) and an increased risk of mortality at 90 days (aHR 1.91, 95% CI 1.01–3.63) and 180 days (aHR 1.84, 95% CI 1.05–3.20). Severe impairment in the mobility dimension was associated with increased risks of ICU admission, rehospitalization within 30, 90, and 180 days, and mortality within 90 and 180 days, while severe impairment in the self-care dimension was associated with increased risks of rehospitalization within 30, 90, and 180 days and mortality within 180 days. Among survivors who completed the follow-up, HRQoL improved modestly over 180 days (+ 0.109 ± 0.247). Low HRQoL at admission, particularly impairments in the mobility and self-care dimensions, was associated with adverse outcomes in patients with CAP. HRQoL is an independent prognostic marker that may support clinical risk stratification in patients with CAP. Introduction

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is the leading cause of hospitalization due to infectious diseases worldwide, particularly among older adults1,2. Despite therapeutic advances, CAP continues to impose a substantial burden on patients and healthcare systems, with 23–37% of the patients being rehospitalized within 30 days post-discharge3–5. Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is a key indicator of a person’s well-being, encompassing physical, psychological, and social functioning6. The EuroQoL EQ-5D questionnaire is a widely used standardized generic tool to assess HRQoL across five dimensions, including mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression7,8. Maintaining functional ability and independence in activities of daily living is essential for healthy aging and strongly associated with HRQoL9. Older adults hospitalized with CAP often experience functional decline, which is associated with impaired HRQoL in the post-discharge period, which may never return to the pre-hospital level10–12. Low HRQoL has been associated with increased risk of rehospitalization and mortality in both older individuals13,14 and patients hospitalized with CAP4. Even so, information on the prognostic value of low HRQoL at the time of admission as well as changes in HRQoL in the post-discharge period is limited.

We hypothesized that low HRQoL at admission was associated with adverse clinical outcomes in patients with CAP. The overall aim of this study was to investigate the association between HRQoL measured at admission and the risk of admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), rehospitalization, and mortality within 30, 90, and 180 days among patients hospitalized with CAP. As a secondary aim, in a predefined subgroup of patients, we assessed changes in HRQoL from admission to 180 days post-discharge.

Methods

Study population and design

This study is based on data from the Surviving Pneumonia Study Cohort3–5,15,16, a prospective cohort study conducted at the Department of Pulmonary and Infectious Diseases, Copenhagen University Hospital–North Zealand, Denmark. The inclusion period spanned from January 15, 2019, to March 10, 2023. Inclusion criteria were adults (≥ 18 years) hospitalized with CAP. CAP was defined as the presence of a new pulmonary infiltrate on a chest x-ray or CT, accompanied by a minimum of one of the clinical symptoms consistent with CAP, including a temperature < 35.0 or ≥ 38.0 °C, cough, shortness of breath, sputum production, or pleuritic chest pain. Patients were excluded from the current analysis if they had no HRQoL measures at admission or if they were enrolled in a randomized controlled trial (RCT). The study was approved by the Scientific Ethics Committee at the Capital Region of Denmark (August 15, 2018; H-18024256) and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (November 9, 2018; NCT03795662). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki17 and reported following the STROBE guidelines18. Only patients able to provide both oral and written informed consent were included in the present analysis, as completion of the EQ-5D-5L questionnaire was required; consequently, participants enrolled via proxy consent in the Surviving Pneumonia Study Cohort were excluded.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were 90-day rehospitalization and 180-day mortality. Secondary outcomes were admission to the ICU, rehospitalization within 30 and 180 days, and mortality within 30 and 90 days.

Data collection

Data were collected prospectively within 48 h of admission. Patient-reported data were obtained using standardized questionnaires and included HRQoL, smoking history, and alcohol consumption. Anthropometric measurements included weight measured to the nearest 0.1 kg on an electronic scale (Seca, Hamburg, Germany) and self-reported height. Body mass index (BMI) calculated as weight (kg)/height (m)2. BMI was categorized as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), healthy weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥ 30 kg/m2). Demographic characteristics and clinical data were extracted from the electronic medical record.

Clinical data and outcomes

Information on demographic characteristics, living situation (i.e., own home, senior housing, nursing home, or relief/rehabilitation), and comorbidities was collected from the electronic medical records within 48 h of admission. Information on disease severity (i.e., oxygen therapy, high-flow therapy, non-invasive ventilation, mechanical ventilation, ICU admission, and length of stay) was collected from the electronic medical records at discharge. Patients were risk-stratified according to the CURB-65 score into mild (score 0–1), moderate (score 2), or severe (score 3–5) CAP19. The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was used to assess the combined burden of comorbidities20. The outcomes of rehospitalization and mortality were ascertained through the electronic medical records at the end of the study 180 days post-discharge. Rehospitalization was defined as any new hospital admission lasting more than 6 h occurring within 30, 90, and 180 days from discharge. Mortality was defined as all-cause death occurring within 30, 90, and 180 days from the day of hospital admission. The association between HRQoL and rehospitalization and mortality was examined at three predefined time points (30, 90, and 180 days), as these outcomes were hypothesized a priori to differ across them. Thirty-day outcomes reflect early post-discharge vulnerability and are closely related to the severity of CAP and the immediate post-discharge process21,22. In contrast, 90‑day outcomes include subacute recovery and the effectiveness of transitional care23, whereas 180‑day outcomes reflect longer‑term prognosis influenced by recovery, residual disease burden, and chronic health status24.

Health-related quality of life

HRQoL was assessed using the EuroQoL 5-Dimension 5-Level (EQ-5D-5L) questionnaire25, a validated, generic measure of HRQoL with well-established psychometric properties7,26. Patients were instructed to describe their health status “today”, i.e., within 48 h of admission. The EQ-5D-5L measures health across five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each dimension is rated on five response levels (none, slight, moderate, severe, and extreme problems), scored from 1 (no problems) to 5 (extreme problems). Health states range from 11111 (no problems across all dimensions) to 55555 (extreme problems across all dimensions). Responses were converted into a numerical value (utility index), reflecting overall health state using the Danish EQ-5D-5L value set. The Danish EQ-5D-5L utility index ranges from −0.757 for state 55555 to 1.000 for state 111117. Based on the EQ-5D utility index, patients were categorized into tertiles, with the lowest tertile representing the lowest HRQoL. In addition, patients were grouped for each EQ-5D-5L dimension into three categories: no problems (score = 1), mild-to-moderate problems (score = 2–3), and severe problems (score = 4–5). This approach was used to reduce sparsity in the higher severity levels and improve interpretability in the regression analyses, which is consistent with recommended approaches to analyze EQ-5D profile data27. HRQoL measured at admission was collected through a face-to-face interview with the patient within 48 h of admission, whereas HRQoL measured 180 days post-discharge was collected through a phone interview with the patient.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25, Armonk, NY). Continuous variables were reported as means ± standard deviation (SD) or medians with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Binary logistic regression was used to assess the association between the EQ-5D utility index (both continuous and tertile-based, with the highest tertile as reference), EQ-5D dimension scores (best score as reference), and the risk of ICU admission. Kaplan-Meier plots and Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to assess the association between the EQ-5D utility index, EQ-5D dimension scores, and the time to rehospitalization and mortality within 30, 90, and 180 days. In analyses of rehospitalization, death was treated as a censoring event, and thus the estimated hazard ratios should be interpreted as cause-specific hazard ratios under the assumption of independent censoring. The primary analysis was based on an adjusted model including age, sex, CURB-65, CCI, and BMI. As BMI was missing (n = 48, 8.7%), multiple imputations by chained equations were performed. Five datasets with 10 iterations per dataset were generated, under the assumption that data were missing at random. The imputation model included the following predictor variables: age, sex, EQ-5D utility index, and CCI, as well as the outcome variables rehospitalization (30, 90, and 180 days), and mortality (30, 90, and 180 days, ) in accordance with recommendations to include outcome variables in the imputation model to reduce bias28. Baseline characteristics in Table 1 are reported from the original (non-imputed) dataset. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation for all regression analyses. Results from unadjusted and adjusted complete-case models (adjusted for age, sex, CURB-65, CCI, and BMI) are presented in the supplementary material. Results were reported as odds ratios (OR) or hazard ratios (HR) with a corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) and P-value. In a subgroup of patients with follow-up data on HRQoL at 180-days post-discharge, paired sample t-tests were used to explore differences in the EQ-5D utility index measured at admission and 180-days post-discharge. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

All results presented in the result section are from the primary analyses. Of the 897 patients with CAP enrolled in the Surviving Pneumonia Study Cohort, 552 (61.5%) were included in the present study. The remaining 345 patients were excluded: 22 due to withdrawal of consent, 199 due to missing HRQoL data at admission, and 124 due to concurrent participation in an RCT. The study population and reasons for exclusion are illustrated in Figure S1 (supplemental material). A subgroup of 262 patients had follow-up data on HRQoL at 180 days post-discharge (Figure S1). The mean age of the study population was 71 ± 13 years, and 46.4% were female (Table 1). Twenty-four patients (4.3%) required admission to the ICU. The proportion of rehospitalized patients was 19.6% within 30 days, 27.5% within 90 days, and 36.2% within 180 days. Mortality rates were 8.0%, 11.8%, and 15.8% at 30, 90, and 180 days, respectively.

HRQoL at admission

The mean EQ-5D utility index at admission was 0.609 ± 0.266 (Table 1). Across EQ-5D dimensions, severe impairment was most frequently reported in usual activities (42%) and mobility (30%). Severe problems were also reported in the self-care (17%), pain/discomfort (19%), and anxiety/depression dimensions (6%, Table S1).

Association between HRQoL at admission and risk of ICU admission

The risk of ICU admission did not differ between patients in the highest tertile of the EQ-5D utility index compared to those in the middle or lowest tertiles. However, when evaluated as a continuous variable, a decrease in the EQ-5D-5L utility index was associated with an increased risk of ICU admission (Table 2). Dimension-specific analyses showed that severe impairment in the mobility dimension was associated with an increased risk of ICU admission compared to no impairment (Table 3).

Association between HRQoL at admission and risk of rehospitalization

Patients in the middle and lowest tertile of the EQ-5D utility index had an increased risk of rehospitalization compared to those in the highest tertile at 30, 90, and 180 days (Fig. 1A; Table 2). Compared to no impairment, mild-to-moderate and severe impairment in the mobility dimension were associated with an increased risk of rehospitalization at 30, 90, and 180 days (Fig. 2A; Table 4). Compared to no impairment, severe impairment in the self-care dimension was associated with increased risk of rehospitalization at 30 days, while both mild-to-moderate and severe impairment in the self-care dimension were associated with increased risk of rehospitalization at 90 and 180 days (Fig. 2B; Table 4). The risk of rehospitalization did not differ between patients with no impairment and those with mild-to-moderate and severe impairment in the usual activities and anxiety/depression dimensions (Fig. 2C and E; Table 4). Compared to no impairment, severe pain/discomfort was associated with increased risk of rehospitalization at 90 and 180 days (Fig. 2D; Table 4).

Association between HRQoL at admission and mortality

The risk of mortality did not differ across tertiles of the EQ-5D utility index at 30 days. Patients in the lowest tertile of the EQ-5D utility index had an increased risk of mortality at 90 and 180 days (Fig. 1B; Table 2). Compared to no impairment, severe impairment in the mobility dimension was associated with risk of mortality at 90 and 180 days (Fig. 3A; Table 4). Compared to no impairment, mild-to-moderate and severe impairment in the self-care dimension were associated with increased risk of mortality at 180 days (Fig. 3B; Table 4). The risk of mortality did not differ between patients with no impairment and those with mild-to-moderate and severe impairment in the usual activities, pain/discomfort, or anxiety/depression dimensions (Fig. 3C-E; Table 4).

Changes in HRQoL from admission up to 180 days post-discharge

In a subgroup (n = 262), HRQoL improved from admission to 180 days post-discharge, with the mean EQ-5D utility index increasing by 0.109 ± 0.247. Improvements were seen across all EQ-5D dimensions (Table S2).

Discussion

In this large cohort of patients hospitalized with CAP, HRQoL at admission was strongly associated with adverse clinical outcomes, including rehospitalization and mortality. While overall EQ-5D utility tertiles were not associated with increased risk of ICU admission, severe impairment in the mobility dimension was associated with an increased risk. This suggests that functional limitations may reflect underlying frailty or disease severity beyond established clinical scores such as CURB-65. These findings emphasize the prognostic importance of physical function in acute illness and support the potential role of patient-reported outcomes in risk stratification.

Elderly patients hospitalized with CAP frequently present with multimorbidity and clinical instability, making them more vulnerable than the general older population29. Accordingly, in our cohort, HRQoL at admission was markedly lower than the age-matched (≥ 70 years) Danish population norms (0.61 ± 0.27 vs. 0.91 ± 0.14), with a particularly higher prevalence of severe impairments in the mobility, usual activities, and pain/discomfort dimensions (19–42% vs. 1.7–5.2%)7. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating substantial impairment in HRQoL among patients hospitalized with CAP4,10,11. Similar to prior population-based30,31 and CAP-specific studies4, men and women rate their health differently. The female patients in our cohort reported lower HRQoL at admission than male patients, with differences persisting across physical and psychosocial dimensions and appear only to be partly explained by sociodemographic and socioeconomic status30,31.

Lower HRQoL at admission was consistently associated with increased risk of rehospitalization within 30, 90, and 180 days post-discharge in patients with CAP. Dimension-specific analyses demonstrated that impairment in the mobility dimension, but not the self-care dimension, was associated with an increased risk of ICU admission; however, this estimate should be interpreted with caution due to the small number of events, as reflected by the wide confidence interval. In contact, impairments in both dimensions were associated with an increased risk of rehospitalization, independent of comorbidities and CAP severity, indicating that HRQoL provides independent prognostic information. Most studies have examined HRQoL as an outcome following CAP10,11. To our knowledge, our study is the first to examine HRQoL as a prognostic marker in patients hospitalized with CAP without focusing on comorbidity. From the Surviving Pneumonia Study Cohort (the cohort from which our study is derived from), HRQoL has previously been evaluated as a prognostic marker in a subpopulation of patients with COPD hospitalized with CAP4. Therefore, this study is the first to demonstrate that HRQoL measured at admission is associated with increased risks of ICU admission, rehospitalization, and mortality, supporting prior observations that link functional status and frailty to prognosis in CAP3,32. Impairment in the pain/discomfort dimensions also contributed to adverse outcomes, which emphasizes the multidimensional nature of vulnerability in this population. The mortality analyses further confirmed the prognostic relevance of HRQoL. Although HRQoL was not associated with short-term mortality, patients in the lowest EQ-5D utility tertile, as well as those with severe impairment in the mobility and self-care dimensions, had increased risks of death within 90 and 180 days post-discharge. These results are consistent with prior research, which links impairment in functional status and frailty to increased mortality risk among patients with CAP3,32. Further, it aligns with findings from our previous cohort study of patients with COPD hospitalized with CAP4.

Maintaining sufficient functional ability is crucial for preserving independence in activities of daily living and HRQoL among older adults33. Although HRQoL improved from admission to 180 days post-discharge, the modest increase (0.109 ± 0.247) suggests persistent physical and physiological limitations. This pattern aligns with a previous study reporting slow and incomplete recovery in HRQoL following CAP, with impairment persisting for up to 18 months post-discharge34. Nonetheless, this evidence underscores the long-term impact of CAP on patient-perceived health. However, because 180-day HRQoL data only were available for a subgroup of patients (n = 262), the observed improvements in HRQoL over time should be interpreted with caution. The observed improvements may partly reflect survivor bias rather than recovery across the full cohort, as patients lost to follow‑up constituted a more severely ill subgroup with a poorer prognosis (supplementary material Table S3). Collectively, these findings highlight the long-term impact of CAP on patient-perceived health and the need for targeted strategies to improve recovery trajectories.

Acute hospitalization is frequently associated with transient or persistent declines in physical function and functional independence, particularly among older adults. Exercise-based interventions during hospitalization may represent a promising strategy to mitigate these impairments. Prior studies, including our own, have shown that supervised exercise interventions during hospitalization with CAP may improve physical function and HRQoL35 while tending to reduce the risk of 90-day rehospitalization and the number of rehospitalization days36. Within the EQ-5D-5L framework, impairments in dimensions such as mobility and self-care reflect distinct yet interrelated physiological and psychological factors. In this context, dimension-specific HRQoL impairments should be understood as indicators of vulnerability rather than as direct targets for therapeutic interventions. Accordingly, the associations observed in this study are best interpreted as prognostic rather than as mechanistic or prescriptive, and translation of dimension-specific impairments into specific intervention strategies is beyond the scope of the study. Furthermore, randomized controlled trials specifically designed to evaluate intervention efficacy are needed before any clinical recommendations can be made.

The key implication of our findings is that HRQoL measured at admission may provide clinically meaningful prognostic information that complements established risk stratification. Although formal quantification of incremental predictive performance substantiates this observation, this study was not designed to develop or optimize a prognostic prediction model. Rather, we aimed to evaluate the association between HRQoL and clinical outcomes in CAP. Therefore, future studies specifically designed for prediction modeling should evaluate the incremental predictive value of HRQoL using established measures of discrimination and calibration. The integration of HRQoL into clinical decision-making needs further investigation, particularly with respect to its potential to support personalized care and reduce risks of rehospitalization and mortality in patients with CAP. However, several practical considerations must be addressed to enable the implementation of HRQoL measures in CAP. In acute care settings, time constraints, patients’ distress, and competing clinical priorities often complicate the assessment of HRQoL at the initial presentation. Assessment following initial clinical stabilization may be a more feasible approach, which allows the measurement of health status without interfering with urgent care. The mode of administration is also important. Acute illness or cognitive impairment may limit the use of self-administered instruments, despite their efficiency and scalability. In contrast, interviewer-administered assessments can improve completeness and inclusivity but require additional resources. Therefore, a hybrid model, which combines self-administration with targeted assistance, may offer a pragmatic balance to ensure feasibility and data quality. For sustainable implementation, HRQoL measures should be integrated into electronic health record systems. Embedding these measures into clinical workflows or patient-facing digital platforms would enable structured, time-stamped data capture and ensure linkage with clinical variables, which would support both clinical care and secondary uses such as quality improvement and research. As this study was not designed to develop or validate a prediction model, model calibration was not assessed. Future studies should include a comprehensive evaluation of model performance, including both discrimination and calibration, to determine the clinical use of HRQoL-informed risk assessment. Overall, future studies should focus on identifying optimal timing, relevant patient subgroups, and pragmatic approaches for integrating HRQoL assessments into routine pneumonia care, ensuring that such measures complement existing clinical risk stratification without imposing an undue burden on clinical practice.

Strengths and limitations

The study has several strengths. First, its novelty is a key strength, as only one other study by Hegelund et al.4, conducted in a related subpopulation of patients with COPD hospitalized with CAP from the same cohort, has investigated the association between HRQoL at admission and risks of rehospitalization and mortality. The present study extended this work by using a broader CAP population, a larger sample size, and a 180-day follow-up to determine changes in HRQoL among survivors with available follow-up. Second, the study utilized a validated generic HRQoL instrument, enabling a comprehensive assessment of overall health status. Generic tools provide a broader assessment of overall health status and may be more sensitive in capturing the cumulative burden of comorbidities37.

The study also has several limitations. First, there is a potential risk of selection bias, as 38% of the patients from the Surviving Pneumonia Study Cohort were excluded from the current analysis. Comparative analyses of baseline and prognostic characteristics demonstrated that excluded patients had a higher prevalence of ICU admission (9.3% vs. 4.3%, P < 0.01), treatment with high-flow oxygen therapy (12.4% vs. 5.8%, P < 0.01), and mechanical ventilation (4.3% vs. 0.9%, P < 0.01) during hospitalization. In addition, 90-day mortality was significantly higher among the excluded patients compared with those included in the analysis (18.6% vs. 11.6%, P < 0.01; supplementary Table S4). These findings suggest that excluded patients represented a more severely ill subgroup with a worse prognosis. Accordingly, the analytical cohort reflects a relatively more clinically stable subset of patients with CAP. Therefore, we cannot rule out the possibility that the exclusion of these patients, primarily due to missing HRQoL data at admission (n = 199, 22%), may have resulted in an underestimation of the association between low HRQoL and adverse clinical outcomes. Second, the high attrition at 180-day follow-up limits conclusions regarding HRQoL changes over time. Comparative analyses showed that patients without available 180-day HRQoL data were more likely to reside in nursing homes (6.2% vs. 1.5%, P = 0.02), were older (72.6 ± 13.4 vs. 69.6 ± 13.2 years, P < 0.01), had a higher CCI (median 53–7 vs. 43–6, P < 0.01; supplementary Table S3), and experienced longer hospital stays (75–11 vs. 54–8 days, P < 0.01) compared to those who completed follow-up. In addition, by design, patients who died could not undergo the 180-day assessment, and mortality at 30, 90, and 180 days was considerably higher among those without follow‑up data. Collectively, these findings indicate that patients lost to follow‑up represented a more severely ill subgroup with a poorer prognosis. Accordingly, the observed improvement in HRQoL over 180 days should be interpreted as reflecting change among survivors with available follow-up rather than the entire initial cohort. These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution, as the observed improvements may partly reflect survivor bias rather than recovery across the full cohort. Given the systematic differences between patients with and without follow-up, the magnitude of improvement is likely overestimated at the population level. Furthermore, as the cohort overall reflects relatively lower disease severity, the prognostic relevance of HRQoL among the most severely ill patients could not be evaluated. Nonetheless, these findings underscore the impact of CAP on patient-perceived health and highlight the need for targeted strategies to support recovery, particularly among high-risk patient groups. Third, although the overall analyses for ICU admission, rehospitalization, and mortality were based on an adequate number of events, some subgroup analyses, particularly those examining individual EQ‑5D dimensions stratified by severity (no, mild-to-moderate, or severe problems), were based on relatively few events, resulting in wide confidence intervals. These findings should be interpreted with caution and not overinterpreted. Larger studies will be required to more precisely evaluate dimension‑specific associations with prognosis. Fourth, missing data necessitated the use of multiple imputation. While this approach is statistically robust and helps to preserve power, estimates from the imputed analyses differed somewhat from complete-case analyses (Tables S5-S7). Nonetheless, the imputed analyses are considered more reliable, as they allow inclusion of relevant confounders and reduce bias associated with missing covariate data. Fifth, death was treated as a censoring event in the rehospitalization analyses, implying an assumption of independent censoring. As death precludes rehospitalization and may share underlying risk factors, this assumption may not fully hold; therefore, results should be interpreted with caution. Sixth, the incremental predictive value of HRQoL measures beyond established risk models was not assessed; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as demonstrating independent prognostic associations rather than improvements in risk prediction. Finally, this was a single-center study, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other healthcare centers.

Conclusions

Lower HRQoL at admission, particularly impairment in the mobility and self-care dimensions, was associated with increased risks of rehospitalization and mortality in patients hospitalized with CAP. Although modest improvements in HRQoL were observed over 180 days in a subset of patients, substantial limitations persisted. These findings suggest that HRQoL is an independent prognostic marker in patients capable of completing the HRQoL questionnaire (i.e., clinically stable patients), while its applicability in more severely ill patients with CAP remains limited. Future studies are needed to determine how HRQoL measures can be pragmatically integrated into clinical care and whether targeted interventions aimed at supporting recovery may improve long‑term outcomes in patients with CAP.
