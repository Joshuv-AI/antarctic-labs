Association between soluble Klotho levels and calcification propensity T50 in patients with hemodialysis: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Akio Nakashima, Kazuhiko Kato, Arisa Kobayashi, Rena Kawai, Yuriko Shibata, Saya Tanimoto, Chiharu Aizawa, Keita Kimura, Ichiro Ohkido, Takashi Yokoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoluble Klotho is an anti-aging hormone implicated in vascular calcification. The calcification propensity score (T50) is a method used to evaluate the calcification propensity. However, no study has evaluated the association between soluble Klotho and T50. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between soluble Klotho and T50 in patients undergoing hemodialysis. This cross-sectional study recruited patients undergoing hemodialysis. We measured T50 using the turbidimetric method and soluble Klotho using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Multiple regression analysis was used to examine the association between soluble Klotho and T50. In total, 199 patients were included in this study. Patients’ mean age was 65.2 ± 10.2 years, and the median soluble Klotho concentration was 408 (321–525) pg/mL. The serum T50 value was 78 (63–104) minutes. The correlation coefficient between T50 and soluble Klotho was 0.39 (p < 0.001). Multivariate analysis revealed that the T50 value was significantly longer when comparing the group with the lowest and highest Klotho concentrations (β-coefficient = 21.3 [95% confidence interval: 4.5–38.2, p < 0.013]). The analysis of hemodialysis patients revealed for the first time that Klotho is associated with the T50 time. Soluble Klotho may be involved in vascular calcification.Keywords: Klotho, Vascular calcification, Hemodialysis, T50Subject terms: Diseases, Medical research, Nephrology

Introduction

Vascular calcification is still an important problem in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), and its pathogenesis involves the collapse of the balance between facilitators and inhibitors1. Although there are several risk factors of vascular calcification, many remain unknown. CKD-mineral bone disorder (MBD), which is characterized by calcium and phosphorus abnormalities, is considered the main cause of vascular calcification in patients with CKD2, and the relationship between Klotho and vascular calcification has been emphasized.

Klotho is a protein involved in aging-related pathways and serves as a cofactor for fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23), regulating phosphate and vitamin D metabolism3. Klotho exists in two forms in the human body4. Soluble Klotho is present in the blood and has various effects on the body5. A previouss study reported that soluble Klotho affects vascular calcification and that the administration of soluble Klotho attenuates vascular calcification in mice with CKD6,7. However, few clinical studies have reported an association between soluble Klotho and vascular calcification.

The calcification propensity score (T50) is a method for assessing serum calcification propensity using supersaturated calcium and phosphate solutions and a turbidimetric assay8,9. However, no study has analyzed the association between soluble Klotho and T50. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between soluble Klotho and T50 in patients undergoing hemodialysis.

Patients and methods

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional study in August 2018 in collaboration with the Division of Nephrology and Hypertension, Jikei University School of Medicine, Tokyo, Japan, and the Shin-Kashiwa Clinic, Chiba, Japan. The Ethics Committee of the Jikei Institutional Review Board of Jikei University School of Medicine reviewed and approved the study protocol (approval number: 29–292[8908]). The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

Study population and eligibility

Patients aged > 20 years who had undergone maintenance hemodialysis therapy for at least 3 months (3–5 h/session, thrice weekly) at the Shin-Kashiwa Clinic were eligible for inclusion. We excluded patients with acute clinical diseases, such as acute gastrointestinal bleeding, acute coronary syndrome, or severe liver dysfunction. Additionally, individuals unable to provide informed consent due to advanced malignancies, end-stage hepatic failure, or cognitive impairment were excluded, as were those managed with peritoneal dialysis or twice-weekly hemodialysis regimens.

Data collection

Clinical parameters retrieved from medical records included age, sex, body mass index, dialysis vintage, blood pressure, heart rate, and primary kidney disease. We also evaluated concomitant medications using prescription records, focusing on antiplatelet agents, vitamin K antagonists, active vitamin D receptor agonists, calcimimetics, phosphate binders, antihypertensive medications, beta-blockers, and statins. Comorbidities and medication histories were confirmed via comprehensive chart review and structured baseline interviews.

Laboratory measures

Blood samples were collected at study entry, prior to the hemodialysis session, following the longest interdialytic interval. Routine biochemical measurements included serum sodium, potassium, phosphorus, calcium, magnesium, albumin, blood urea nitrogen, alkaline phosphatase, creatinine, hematocrit, intact parathyroid hormone, and C-reactive protein (CRP). The delivered dialysis dose was measured using single-pool Kt/V.

FGF23, soluble Klotho, T50, and 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25OHD) were stored at 4 °C until measurement within 1 week after serum collection. Fetuin-A was measured 6 months after collection using serum stored at − 80 °C. The serum was frozen and not thawed prior to the fetuin measurement. Serum soluble Klotho, intact FGF23, and Fetuin-A levels were measured using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions (IBL International, Tokyo, Japan; KAINOS, Tokyo, Japan; and BioVender R&D, Asheville, NC, USA, respectively).

For T50 measurements, blood samples were collected and stored at 4 °C under standardized conditions and analyzed within one week of collection without freeze–thaw cycles. Measurements were performed in duplicate, and the mean of the duplicates was used for the analysis. T50 measurements were performed according to previously reported methods with minor modifications9,10. Briefly, serum samples were incubated with supersaturated calcium and phosphate solutions under standardized conditions at 36–37 °C, and the transition time from primary to secondary calciprotein particles was determined using a nephelometric apparatus (Nephelostar Plus; BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany), as previously described9,10.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables, while categorical parameters are reported as frequencies and percentages. Soluble Klotho concentrations were categorized into tertiles, and differences between the groups were analyzed using analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Nominal variables were analyzed using the chi-squared test. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations with 20 imputations. Variables clinically relevant and devoid of missing data—specifically age, sex, dialysis vintage, and serum levels of calcium, phosphate, and intact parathyroid hormone—were incorporated into the imputation model.

Multivariable regression analyses for T50 were performed using explanatory variables selected based on previous studies. Covariates were selected from the following domains: patient characteristics (age, sex, dialysis vintage, and comorbidities), nutritional and inflammatory status (albumin, C-reactive protein, and blood urea nitrogen), and bone and mineral metabolism parameters (calcium, phosphate, vitamin D levels, and FGF23)2,8–11. Multiple regression analysis was performed to investigate the association between the T50 values and soluble Klotho levels. We set three models for the multiple regression analysis. Model I was adjusted for age; sex; dialysis vintage; body mass index; diabetes mellitus; levels of hemoglobin, albumin, CRP, and blood urea nitrogen; and history of cardiovascular disease. Model II was adjusted for the variables in model I and levels of calcium, phosphate, magnesium, intact parathyroid hormone (iPTH), FGF23, 25OHD, and active vitamin D analogs; phosphate binders; and calcimimetics. Model III was adjusted for the variables in model II and the Fetuin-A level. To visually clarify the association between T50 values and soluble Klotho levels, we constructed box plots and restricted cubic spline curves. A restricted cubic spline curve was created using the results of model III of the multiple regression analysis. The association between soluble Klotho and T50 was analyzed using multiple regression models within predefined subgroups (age, diabetes mellitus, phosphorus, FGF23, and fetuin-A). The same covariates as those described above were included in each model. Soluble Klotho was treated as a continuous variable, and interaction terms were included to test for effect modification across subgroups. Subgroup analyses were performed for T50 values and soluble Klotho levels. Subgroup analyses were performed on the basis of age (65 years or older or not), presence or absence of diabetes, phosphorus concentration (5.5 mg/dL), FGF23 concentration (4222 pg/mL), and Fetuin-A concentration (18.4 ng/mL). The cutoff values for phosphorus, FGF23, and Fetuin-A concentrations were the median values in this cohort. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA software (version 19.0; STATA Corp., College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics

In total, 199 patients were included in this study. Patients’ mean age was 65.2 ± 10.2 years, and the median dialysis duration was 106 (62–181) months. The median soluble Klotho concentration was 408 (321–525) pg/mL. Patient characteristics are displayed according to the Klotho tertiles in Table 1. There were 133 men (66.8%), and 111 patients (55.8%) had diabetes. The variables related to CKD-MBD were as follows: phosphate level, 5.6 ± 1.3 mg/dL; calcium level, 8.4 ± 0.6 mg/dL; and magnesium level, 2.6 ± 0.3 mg//dL. The median concentrations of FGF23, 25OHD, and Fetuin-A were 1621 (680–4548) pg/mL, 5.8 (4.1–13.3) ng/mL, and 18.4 (14.6–22.9) ng/mL, respectively. Calcium-containing phosphate binders were used in 88 (44.2%) patients, whereas non-calcium-containing phosphate binders were used in 138 (69.3%) patients. A total of 100 (50.2%) patients used calcimimetics. There were no significant differences in age, diabetes mellitus, serum albumin, or CRP levels among the soluble Klotho tertiles. Regarding CKD-MBD-related factors, no significant differences were observed in phosphate, calcium, iPTH 25OHD, and FGF23 levels between the soluble Klotho concentrations. In contrast, Fetuin-A levels increased with soluble Klotho levels.

Soluble Klotho and T50

The serum T50 value was 78 (63–104) minutes. The correlation coefficient between T50 and soluble Klotho was 0.39 (p < 0.001). The T50 values were higher than soluble Klotho levels (Fig. 1). We conducted a multivariate regression analysis for T50 using explanatory variables, including soluble Klotho and known contributing factors (Table 2). When the group with the lowest Klotho concentration was used as the reference, the group with the highest Klotho concentration had a significantly prolonged T50 value (unadjusted: p < 0.001, model I: p < 0.001). The results were similar for model II (which included CKD-MBD factors including 25OHD or FGF23) and model III (which included Fetuin-A levels) (model II: p < 0.001, model III: p < 0.013). We also analyzed Klotho as a continuous variable and found that it was significantly associated with T50 in all models. The restricted cubic spline curve of multiple regression analysis of Model III showed that the association between soluble Klotho and T50 was linear (Fig. 2).

Subgroup analysis of soluble Klotho and T50

We conducted a subgroup analysis of soluble Klotho and T50 and set subgroups for age (65 years), diabetes mellitus, phosphorus level (5.5 mg/dL), FGF23 level (4222 pg/mL), and Fetuin-A level (18.4 ng/mL) (Fig. 3). The association between soluble Klotho and T50 was significant in all subgroups. Although there was a significant interaction with phosphorus (p for interaction, 0.043), the interactions of the other groups were not significant.

Subgroup analysis of T50 values and serum soluble Klotho levels by clinical characteristics. Soluble Klotho was treated as a continuous variable, and multiple regression analysis was performed on T50 to calculate the interactions for each subgroup.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that serum soluble Klotho levels are associated with the calcification propensity score, T50, in patients undergoing hemodialysis and that T50 is positively associated with soluble Klotho after adjustment for known variables that influence its concentration. Additionally, this association was significant after subgroup analysis including FGF23 or Fetuin-A levels. This study is the first to demonstrate an association between serum soluble Klotho and T50.

Several studies have reported the influence of soluble Klotho on vascular calcification, including the Coronary Artery Calcium Score (CACS)11–13 and pulse wave velocity14–16. Many of the previous studies reported that serum soluble Klotho levels are inversely associated with vascular calcification11,13,15,16. Moreover, Wungu et al. performed a meta-analysis on the effectiveness of soluble Klotho for vascular calcification17, and they reviewed the association between soluble Klotho and intima-media thickness or pulse wave velocity in 27,459 individuals, including those with CKD. They reported that FGF23 and Klotho were associated with arterial calcification (r=−0.388 [−0.578 to −0.159], p = 0.001) and intima-media thickness (r=−0.38 [−0.53 to −0.207], p < 0.00001). However, no study has investigated the association between soluble Klotho and T50 until now.

T50 has attracted attention for evaluating susceptibility to vascular calcification using serum samples. Because direct assessment of vascular calcification is not routinely feasible in clinical practice, T50 may serve as a convenient surrogate marker of calcification propensity. Therefore, identifying factors associated with T50 is clinically relevant, and our findings suggest that soluble Klotho may contribute to risk stratification for vascular calcification in patients undergoing hemodialysis. Previous studies have reported that serum T50 values are significantly associated with the CACS18. Additionally, decreased T50 values are associated with cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality in the general population and patients with CKD8,19–21. Previous studies have reported that several factors increase the T50 values; slow down calcification; increase magnesium, calcium, albumin, and pH levels; and decrease Fetuin-A level22. To our best knowledge, this is the first study to report an association between soluble Klotho and T50. After adjusting for factors that affect T50, including calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, pH, and Fetuin-A levels, soluble Klotho was found to be a significant factor.

We categorized Klotho into tertiles for descriptive comparisons; however, consistent associations were also observed when Klotho was analyzed as a continuous variable, supporting the robustness of our results. Clinically, these findings suggest that soluble Klotho may serve as an additional biomarker to identify hemodialysis patients at elevated risk of vascular calcification, potentially improving risk stratification beyond traditional mineral metabolism parameters.

Several studies have reported the mechanism by which soluble Klotho attenuates vascular calcification. First, soluble Klotho inhibits phosphate uptake in vascular smooth muscle cells. Klotho usually interacts with FGF23 and the fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR). In contrast, soluble Klotho stimulates FGF23 in a FGFR 1-dependent manner in bone cells. Hum et al. reported that soluble Klotho attenuated hyperphosphatemia in a mouse model of CKD-mineral bone disorder and prevented vascular calcification in mice without endogenous Klotho protein. These findings suggest that soluble Klotho has activity without FGF23 or FGFR and protects against vascular calcification7. Second, soluble Klotho regulates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and inhibits vascular calcification23. Aprevious study reported that the Wnt/β-catenin signal pathway has an important role in vascular calcification24. Decreased Klotho levels in mammals have been reported to enhance the Wnt signaling pathway and accelerate calcification in tissues throughout the body, including blood vessels25. Third, soluble Klotho suppressed oxidative stress. Soluble Klotho has antioxidant properties and activates nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2). Silencing Nrf2 attenuates the induction of the antioxidant enzymes heme oxygenase and peroxiredoxin-1 and protects against vascular calcification23. Here, we analyzed the association between soluble Klotho and T50 using multivariate analysis and found a significant association. Because the association between soluble Klotho and T50 was significant in the analysis involving FGF-23, it is possible that this relationship was not mediated by FGF23. Because the association between soluble Klotho and T50 remained significant after adjustment for calcium, phosphate, PTH, and FGF23, the relationship may be mediated by mechanisms beyond conventional mineral metabolism parameters.

This study has some limitations. First, as the study design was cross-sectional, we could not establish cause and effect. In addition, a limitation of this study is the lack of a control group with normal renal function. Because both T50 and soluble Klotho levels are influenced by kidney function, direct comparisons with individuals without kidney disease could not be made in the current cohort. Future studies including participants across a broader spectrum of kidney function are warranted. Second, although the value of vascular calcification was evaluated using T50, the CACS, neck echography, and autopsy are more accurate methods for evaluating vascular calcification. However, T50 represents serum calcification propensity and does not directly measure vascular calcification. Imaging-based assessments, such as computed tomography, were not available in this study. Therefore, the relationship between soluble Klotho, T50, and structural vascular calcification should be confirmed in future studies using imaging-based evaluations. T50 is a method for evaluating the calcification propensity and is suitable for predicting vascular calcification. Further studies are required to evaluate the association among soluble Klotho, CACS, and T50 over time. Third, as patients with higher soluble Klotho levels had a longer dialysis vintage and a lower incidence of ischemic heart disease, there is a possibility of survival bias in this study. If possible, it would be desirable to conduct research on patients matched for the same conditions, such as age and dialysis history. Forth, the median T50 value in our cohort was lower than that reported in previous studies26. This difference may be partly explained by assay-related variability and differences in patient characteristics. However, established determinants of T50, including calcium, phosphate, and magnesium, showed expected associations in our cohort, supporting the internal validity of the measurements. Fifth, in the present study, we did not observe significant differences in FGF23, calcium, or phosphate levels across Klotho tertiles. This likely reflects the disrupted Klotho–FGF23 axis in maintenance hemodialysis patients and the strong influence of dialysis-related treatments on mineral metabolism parameters.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that soluble Klotho is associated with T50 in patients undergoing hemodialysis. This association was significant after adjusting for known risk factors of calcification. However, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Thus, future large-scale clinical and interventional studies are needed to clarify the association between soluble Klotho and vascular calcification in patients with CKD.
