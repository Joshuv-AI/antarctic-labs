Developmental coupling between obsessive-compulsive symptoms and psychotic-like experiences in youth: a four-wave longitudinal study
Authors: Wanling Zhang, Na Hu, Yanru Liu, Xuanzi Zhou, Xiao Leng, Lianjingyi Liang, Xiaoxuan Fan, Tianyu Bian, Zixin Li, Hui Xu, Zhi Yang, Ying Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObsessive-compulsive symptoms (OCS) and psychotic-like experiences (PLEs) commonly emerge during late childhood and early adolescence and often co-occur. However, it remains unclear whether this overlap reflects stable between-person differences, within-person longitudinal coupling, or symptom-specific developmental pathways. Using four waves of data from 7,451 participants in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study, we examined OCS–PLEs coupling from late childhood to early adolescence. Parallel-process latent growth models characterized co-developmental trajectories; cross-lagged and random-intercept cross-lagged panel models distinguished between-person from within-person associations; and temporal network analyses identified symptom-level bridge patterns. OCS and PLEs followed distinct but partially coupled developmental trajectories, with higher initial OCS associated with higher initial PLEs and a less favorable trajectory of PLEs over time. Conventional cross-lagged models showed bidirectional associations at the observed-score level, whereas random-intercept models suggested that early associations largely reflected stable between-person differences; only PLEs predicted subsequent increases in OCS from ages 12 to 13 at the within-person level. Symptom-level networks showed earlier OCS-to-PLE links and later more reciprocal coupling, with intrusive thoughts and worry emerging as consistent bridge symptoms. These findings suggest that OCS–PLEs overlap may vary across developmental stages and highlight the importance of developmental timing and symptom-specific assessment in youth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57877-5.Keywords: Obsessive-compulsive symptoms, Psychotic-like experiences, Adolescence, Longitudinal study, Co-occurrenceSubject terms: Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Obsessive-compulsive symptoms (OCS) and psychotic-like experiences (PLEs) are not rare during late childhood and early adolescence and are increasingly studied as developmentally relevant dimensions of youth mental health. Subthreshold OCS refer to obsessive or compulsive symptoms that do not meet full diagnostic criteria for obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD)1. These symptoms are developmentally relevant because obsessive-compulsive phenomena often emerge during childhood, with pediatric OCD showing an early incidence peak around ages 9–102. Although these symptoms may remain below the diagnostic threshold, they can persist over time and have been associated with heightened risk for later OCD, earlier psychosis onset, and broader psychopathology3. PLEs, including attenuated perceptual abnormalities and mild delusion-like beliefs, are subclinical phenomena in the general population and are commonly conceptualized as early expressions along the psychosis continuum, particularly during childhood and adolescence4,5. A systematic review and meta-analysis of population-based studies reported median prevalence estimates of psychotic symptoms of 17% among children aged 9–12 years and 7.5% among adolescents aged 13–18 years, indicating that these experiences are more frequently reported in childhood than in later adolescence6. Epidemiological evidence suggests that OCS and PLEs frequently co-occur in youth, with up to 40% of young people with OCS reporting concurrent PLEs and estimated co-occurrence rates ranging from 25% to 40%7. Youth presenting with overlapping OCS and PLEs may represent a higher-burden subgroup. This interpretation is supported by evidence that OCS are closely linked with anxiety, social isolation, and other symptoms in psychosis-risk populations, and that adolescent psychotic symptoms are associated with more severe, multiple psychopathologies and poorer later social or functional outcomes8,9. Despite this substantial overlap, the developmental nature of OCS–PLEs co-occurrence remains insufficiently understood.

The co-occurrence of OCS and PLEs may reflect partially overlapping cognitive and neurodevelopmental processes, particularly in domains related to cognitive control and reality monitoring10. In the present context, cognitive control refers to a set of executive processes that support goal-directed behavior, including inhibition of prepotent responses and shifting between mental sets, whereas reality monitoring refers to the ability to distinguish internally generated thoughts, images, or perceptions from externally generated events11–13. Difficulties in these processes may be relevant to both obsessive-compulsive and psychosis-like phenomena, including intrusive thoughts, attenuated delusion-like beliefs, anomalous perceptual experiences, and atypical behaviors as captured by symptom-level assessment items. Prior research has proposed a “schizo-obsessive” phenotype to describe individuals with co-occurring obsessive-compulsive and psychosis-spectrum features, suggesting potential shared pathways across these domains14. Cognitive models further suggest that intrusive thoughts may become more persistent or distressing when they are appraised as threatening, personally significant, or difficult to control, and that metacognitive beliefs about the meaning, danger, or controllability of thoughts may maintain these appraisals15,16. In parallel, unusual interpretations of internal experiences may involve attributing internally generated thoughts or perceptions to external or unusually meaningful sources. Repetitive attempts to suppress, control, or neutralize intrusive thoughts may increase their salience and perceived threat value, offering a theoretical account of how obsessive-compulsive and psychosis-like experiences could become linked17. However, theoretical processes have rarely been examined longitudinally in youth, and little is known about how specific OCS and PLE symptoms dynamically interact across development.

From a developmental perspective, the association between OCS and PLEs is unlikely to remain static across late childhood and early adolescence18,19. This period is marked by rapid changes in cognitive control, emotional regulation, and reality-monitoring capacities, alongside heightened vulnerability to emerging psychopathology20,21. Importantly, it also represents a sensitive window during which subclinical symptoms may either remit or consolidate into more persistent forms of mental disorder22–24. As neurocognitive and affective systems reorganize during this transition, the strength and direction of associations between OCS and PLEs may shift across developmental stages4,25,26. Understanding how these symptom domains co-develop during this period may help clarify when their co-occurrence becomes most pronounced and clinically relevant.

Existing longitudinal evidence on the direction of association between OCS and PLEs remains inconsistent. Some studies suggest that OCS confer increased risk for subsequent psychotic experiences27,28, whereas others report the opposite pattern, with PLEs preceding or exacerbating OCS29–31. An important explanation for this inconsistency may be methodological. Conventional cross-lagged panel models (CLPMs) estimate temporal associations between observed variables across repeated assessments, but they do not separate stable between-person differences from within-person fluctuations over time. Thus, cross-lagged associations in conventional CLPMs may partly reflect enduring individual differences rather than time-specific within-person processes. Random-intercept cross-lagged panel models (RI-CLPMs) extend the conventional CLPM by decomposing repeated measures into stable between-person components and within-person deviations from an individual’s expected level32,33. This approach is useful for testing whether increases in one symptom domain at a given wave are followed by subsequent increases in the other symptom domain within the same individual. Distinguishing these sources of variance may clarify whether OCS–PLEs associations primarily reflect stable shared liability across individuals or more proximal within-person temporal coupling34–36.

A further limitation of the existing literature is that both OCS and PLEs are highly heterogeneous constructs. Their overlap is unlikely to be evenly distributed across all symptoms; rather, some symptoms may be more central to cross-domain coupling than others37–39. However, evidence on how specific OCS and PLE symptoms are linked over time remains limited. Aggregating each domain into a total score may obscure meaningful variation in how specific symptoms are linked across development, particularly when certain symptoms differ in their developmental salience or cross-domain relevance40,41. As a result, total-score analyses may fail to capture which symptoms are most closely involved in cross-domain coupling and whether these symptom-level associations change across developmental stages. Symptom-level approaches are therefore needed to identify bridge symptoms linking OCS and PLEs over time42. Temporal network approaches, including cross-lagged panel networks (CLPN), provide a useful framework for examining such symptom-level associations across repeated measurements.

To address these gaps, the present study used four waves of data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study to examine the developmental coupling between OCS and PLEs from late childhood to early adolescence. Specifically, we aimed to: (1) characterize the developmental trajectories of OCS and PLEs and their co-development over time; (2) disentangle between-person and within-person longitudinal associations between OCS and PLEs by comparing conventional CLPMs with RI-CLPMs; and (3) identify symptom-level bridge patterns linking OCS and PLEs across developmental stages using temporal network analysis. By integrating construct-level and symptom-level longitudinal approaches within a developmental framework, this study seeks to clarify whether OCS–PLEs co-occurrence primarily reflects stable between-person differences, within-person longitudinal coupling, or developmentally shifting interactions between specific symptoms. Based on prior developmental and longitudinal evidence, we hypothesized that OCS and PLEs would show correlated developmental trajectories. We further expected that bidirectional associations observed in conventional cross-lagged models would be attenuated after accounting for stable between-person differences in random-intercept models. Finally, we hypothesized that symptom-level coupling between OCS and PLEs would vary across developmental stages, with specific bridge symptoms emerging over time.

Methods

Participants

Data were obtained from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, version 5.0, a large-scale and ongoing nationwide study conducted across 21 research sites in the United States and aimed at tracking the developmental trajectory of 11,868 children aged 9–10 years at baseline. Detailed descriptions of the study design, recruitment strategies, and assessment protocols have been described previously43. The current study used four annual waves of data from baseline through the 3-year follow-up, corresponding approximately to ages 9–13 years. Participants were included if they had available data on OCS, PLEs, and covariates across the analytic waves. After excluding participants with missing study variables or covariate information, the final analytic sample included 7,451 youth. A flowchart of participant selection is shown in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of the study cohort selection and procedure.

Measures

Obsessive-compulsive symptoms

Obsessive-compulsive symptoms (OCS) were assessed using the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL), a parent- or caregiver-reported measure of children’s emotional and behavioral problems. The CBCL includes 113 items rated on a 3-point scale: 0 = not true, 1 = somewhat or sometimes true, and 2 = very true or often true. For construct-level analyses, we used the DSM-oriented obsessive-compulsive problems scale, based on age- and sex-normed T-scores. Prior studies have supported the reliability and validity of the CBCL obsessive-compulsive scale in youth samples44–46. For symptom-level network analyses, individual OCS items were used to examine item-level associations with PLE dimensions.

Psychotic-like experiences

Psychotic-like experiences (PLEs) were assessed using the 21-item Prodromal Questionnaire-Brief Child Version (PQ-BC), a self-report measure designed to assess psychotic-like experiences in children and adolescents47. The PQ-BC has demonstrated acceptable internal consistency and construct validity in pediatric populations48. For each endorsed item, participants rated associated distress on a 5-point scale. A total PLE-with-distress score was calculated by summing distress ratings across endorsed items, consistent with prior ABCD-based work47,48. For symptom-level analyses, PLE items were grouped into three dimensions: thought-related delusions items 1, 5, 8, 12, 14, and 18; unusual or grandiose delusions items 4, 7, 15, and 16; and hallucinations items 2, 3, 9–11, 17, 19, and 20. Items 6, 13, and 21 were excluded from subtype analyses because of ambiguous classification49. Both total PLE scores and subtype-specific dimensions were used to capture overall severity and symptom-level heterogeneity.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in a stepwise manner. First, descriptive statistics for demographic and clinical characteristics, as well as bivariate correlations among study variables, were examined using SPSS version 29 (IBM Corp., USA).

Second, longitudinal measurement invariance of the study variables was tested in Mplus 8.350. Unconditional latent growth models (LGMs), including both linear and nonlinear forms, were then estimated to characterize the trajectories of OCS and PLEs across the four waves. Parallel-process LGM was further used to examine associations between the intercepts and slopes of OCS and PLEs.

Third, to distinguish overall longitudinal associations from within-person dynamic effects, cross-lagged panel models (CLPM) were estimated to assess cross-lagged associations between OCS and PLEs across time, and random-intercept cross-lagged panel models (RI-CLPM) were subsequently fitted to separate stable between-person differences from within-person fluctuations. All models included the following baseline covariates: age, sex, twin status, body mass index (BMI), race, caregiver education, parental marital status, and household income. Model fit was evaluated using χ², CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR, with acceptable fit defined as CFI and TLI ≥ 0.90, RMSEA ≤ 0.08, and SRMR ≤ 0.08.

Fourth, temporal network analysis was conducted in R version 4.1.2 to examine symptom-level bridge patterns between OCS and PLEs across developmental stages. Cross-lagged panel networks (CLPNs) were estimated for three consecutive intervals (T0–T1, T1–T2, and T2–T3). Regularized regressions were conducted using the glmnet package51, and networks were visualized using the qgraph package52. The same baseline covariates included in the longitudinal models were also adjusted for in the network analyses. To estimate the CLPN, autoregressive and cross-lagged effects were obtained through a series of regularized regressions using penalized maximum likelihood estimation with a LASSO penalty. A tuning parameter of γ = 0.5 was applied to increase network sparsity and reduce the risk of overfitting. In-expected influence (IEI) and out-expected influence (OEI) were estimated to capture item directionality. One-step bridge expected influence (BEI) identified bridge items between OCS and PLEs. To aid interpretation, logistic regression coefficients were exponentiated into odds ratios (ORs): OR > 1 indicates a positive association, OR < 1 a negative association, and OR = 1 no association. All network stability and accuracy were evaluated using the bootnet package (version 1.4.3) in R, with 1,000 nonparametric and 1,000 case-drop bootstrap replications. Correlation stability coefficients exceeding 0.5 were interpreted as indicative of reliable network estimation42,53.

Sensitivity analyses

To examine whether the longitudinal OCS–PLEs coupling was driven by non-specific obsessive–compulsive features, we conducted a priori sensitivity analyses on the temporal cross-lagged panel networks. Specifically, we re-estimated the three symptom-level networks (T0–T1, T1–T2, T2–T3) after removing the CBCL OCS items “strange behavior” and “strange ideas” (items OCS 84 and OCS 85) from the node set. All remaining OCS items and the three PLE dimensions (hallucinations, thought delusions, unusual/grandiose delusions) were retained.

Developmental trajectories and co-developmental coupling of OCS and PLEs

Latent growth models indicated distinct but partially coupled developmental trajectories for OCS and PLEs. A linear LGM provided a good fit for OCS (χ² (5) = 82.47, p < 0.001; CFI = 0.995; RMSEA = 0.036), showing a significant linear decline across the four waves. For PLEs, a nonlinear LGM provided the best fit (χ² (1) = 3.91, p = 0.047; CFI = 1.00; RMSEA = 0.016), indicating a steeper early decline followed by subsequent stabilization. In the parallel-process model, higher baseline OCS was associated with higher initial PLEs and a steeper rate of change in PLEs over time (β = 0.11, p < 0.001; β = 0.06, p < 0.001). In contrast, baseline PLEs were not significantly associated with either the initial level or the slope of OCS (Fig. 2). Longitudinal bidirectional associations between OCS and PLEs

Parameter estimates for the CLPM and RI-CLPM are presented in Table S4.

In the conventional CLPM, autoregressive effects were substantial for both OCS (βs = 0.42–0.62) and PLEs (βs = 0.36–0.45). Small but statistically significant bidirectional cross-lagged associations were observed across all intervals. Specifically, higher OCS was associated with higher subsequent PLEs from T0 to T1, T1 to T2, and T2 to T3. Conversely, higher PLEs was associated with higher subsequent OCS across the same intervals. After accounting for stable between-person differences in the RI-CLPM, autoregressive effects were substantially attenuated (OCS: βs = 0.19–0.30; PLEs: βs = 0.08–0.28). All cross-lagged paths from OCS to later PLEs were no longer significant, and the paths from PLEs to later OCS were also non-significant from T0 to T1 and from T1 to T2. Only the path from PLEs at T2 to OCS at T3 remained significant at the within-person level (β = 0.06, SE = 0.024, p < 0.01, Fig. 3).

Network accuracy and stability were acceptable. Confidence intervals around edge weights were relatively narrow (Figure S1), and case-dropping analyses showed adequate stability for OEI, IEI, and BEI, with correlation stability coefficients ranging from 0.67 to 0.75 (Figure S2). Across all intervals, autoregressive edges were stronger than cross-lagged edges, and within-domain stability was generally stronger in the OCS cluster than in the PLE cluster.

In the T0–T1 interval (approximately ages 9–11 years), the strongest cross-domain edges were OCS85 (“Strange ideas”) → Hallucinations (OR = 1.21) and OCS84 (“Strange behavior”) → Hallucinations (OR = 1.12) (Fig. 4a; Table S5). The strongest out-expected influence node was OCS84 (“Strange behavior”; OEI = 2.33), the strongest in-expected influence node was Hallucinations (IEI = 2.37), and the strongest bridge node was also OCS84 (BEI = 2.63) (Fig. 4g; Table S8).

In the T1–T2 interval (approximately ages 11–12 years), the strongest cross-domain edges were OCS84 (“Strange behavior”) → Hallucinations (OR = 1.34), OCS84 (“Strange behavior”) → Thought delusions (OR = 1.16), and OCS09 (“Can’t get their mind off certain thoughts”) → Unusual/grandiose delusions (OR = 1.06) (Fig. 4b; Table S6). The strongest out-expected influence node remained OCS84 (OEI = 2.38), whereas OCS09 emerged as the strongest bridge node (BEI = 1.45) (Fig. 4h; Table S8).

In the T2–T3 interval (approximately ages 12–13 years), the strongest cross-domain edges were Unusual/grandiose delusions → OCS09 (“Can’t get their mind off certain thoughts”) (OR = 1.05) and OCS84 (“Strange behavior”) → Hallucinations (OR = 1.08) (Fig. 4c; Table S7). The strongest out-expected influence node was OCS52 (“Feels too guilty”; OEI = 2.36), whereas the strongest bridge node was OCS112 (“Worries”; BEI = 1.77) (Fig. 4i; Table S8). Notably, cross-domain associations from PLEs to OCS became more evident at this stage, particularly linking delusion-like experiences with intrusive obsessive cognition. At the same time, worry-related symptoms emerged as the most prominent bridge features. Cross-domain edges differed across the three developmental intervals. In the earlier intervals, the strongest cross-domain edges were primarily directed from OCS-related items to PLE dimensions, whereas in the T2–T3 interval, one of the strongest cross-domain edges was directed from unusual/grandiose delusions to OCS09. Detailed edge and centrality difference are shown in Figures S3–S8.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity results are presented in Figures S9–S10 and Table S10. After excluding the OCS items strange behavior and strange ideas, the overall temporal network structure remained largely unchanged. Autoregressive edges within both OCS and PLEs continued to dominate, and the general pattern of early OCS-PLEs associations and later bidirectional connections was preserved. The sensitivity analyses yielded a similar pattern of autoregressive and cross-domain edges after excluding these two CBCL items. OCS09, OCS52, and OCS112 continued to show relatively high bridge expected influence across intervals.

Discussion

This study examined the developmental relationship between OCS and PLEs in youth using complementary construct-level and symptom-level longitudinal approaches. Across four waves, we found that OCS and PLEs were developmentally dynamic and partially overlapping rather than fully independent symptom domains. Higher OCS in late childhood was associated with higher initial PLEs and with a less favorable developmental course of PLEs. In addition, the comparison between conventional CLPM and RI-CLPM suggested that much of the apparent early bidirectional coupling was explained by stable between-person differences, whereas only a later within-person pathway from PLEs to subsequent OCS remained evident in early adolescence. Symptom-level temporal network analyses further showed that cross-domain coupling was developmentally heterogeneous: earlier associations were more strongly oriented from OCS-related symptoms to PLEs, whereas later associations became more reciprocal and included clearer pathways from delusion-like experiences to intrusive obsessive cognition. Across stages, intrusive thoughts and worry emerged as relatively persistent bridge symptoms. Current findings support a staged developmental account of OCS–PLEs overlap and highlight the importance of developmentally informed assessment of co-occurring symptoms in young people.

Developmental coupling and longitudinal associations between OCS and PLEs

The latent growth models indicated distinct but partially coupled developmental trajectories of OCS and PLEs between ages 9 and 13 years. OCS showed a linear decline across waves, whereas PLEs showed a steeper early decline followed by relative stabilization. This pattern is broadly consistent with previous work suggesting that subclinical psychiatric phenomena often peak in late childhood and attenuate with ongoing neurocognitive and emotional maturation24,54,25. Importantly, higher initial OCS was associated with both higher initial PLEs and subsequent change in PLEs, suggesting that early obsessive-compulsive liability may index a broader developmental vulnerability relevant to psychosis-spectrum experiences. One possible interpretation is that early OCS captures dispositional characteristics, such as threat sensitivity, cognitive inflexibility, or maladaptive salience processing, that are also relevant to later PLE expression55,56. In contrast, baseline PLEs were not associated with the developmental course of OCS, suggesting that the developmental coupling between the two domains may be asymmetrical at the population level, with early OCS showing greater relevance to subsequent variation in PLEs than vice versa. Taken together, these findings suggest that early co-development between OCS and PLEs is more consistent with shared vulnerability than with a simple unidirectional developmental sequence.

This interpretation was further clarified by comparing the conventional CLPM and the RI-CLPM. In the conventional CLPM, cross-lagged associations were small but statistically significant across waves, suggesting apparent bidirectional coupling at the observed-score level. However, once stable between-person heterogeneity was taken into account in the RI-CLPM, the associations from OCS to later PLEs were substantially attenuated and became non-significant, whereas only the later association from PLEs to subsequent OCS remained significant at the within-person level. This pattern accords with the well-documented concern that CLPM cross-lagged estimates may largely reflect stable between-person covariance rather than within-person change processes34. Accordingly, the earlier OCS–PLEs associations observed in the conventional model are more plausibly interpreted as reflecting enduring individual differences, namely, that youth with persistently elevated OCS also tend to report elevated PLEs, consistent with shared liability across obsessive-compulsive and psychosis-spectrum phenotypes57,58. By incorporating random intercepts, the RI-CLPM provides a more stringent framework for distinguishing time-invariant between-person differences from occasion-specific within-person deviations and for determining whether temporal associations remain evident after stable trait-like variance is accounted for. Within this framework, the attenuation of the earlier OCS–PLEs associations suggests that the apparent early coupling was largely attributable to stable between-person factors rather than short-term within-person processes.

By contrast, the retained pathway from PLEs to later OCS in early adolescence suggests that, at this stage, cross-domain coupling is not fully explained by stable trait-like differences and may instead reflect a more proximal within-person process59,60. This finding suggests that emerging PLEs may become increasingly tied to the maintenance or intensification of obsessive-compulsive symptomatology during early adolescence. Although age-related changes in introspection and symptom awareness may influence self-reported PLEs, such changes would be expected to occur gradually across waves. The observed developmental shift in the pattern of cross-domain associations therefore more likely reflects a meaningful change in symptom interplay, even though measurement-related influences cannot be entirely excluded61. Overall, the combined trajectory and cross-lagged findings support a staged developmental account in which early OCS–PLEs associations primarily reflect shared vulnerability at the between-person level, whereas later PLEs become more closely linked to subsequent elevations in OCS at the within-person level. This developmental distinction also provides the rationale for examining symptom-level bridge mechanisms in the temporal network analyses.

Symptom-level bridging connectivities across developmental stages

Temporal network analyses further characterized the symptom-level bridge mechanisms underlying the developmental coupling between OCS and PLEs. During the earlier stage (approximately ages 9–12 years), cross-domain associations were more strongly oriented from the OCS cluster toward the PLE cluster. In particular, “strange behavior” and “strange ideas” emerged as the most prominent bridge symptoms, with the strongest cross-domain edges linking these OCS features to hallucination- and delusion-like experiences62,63. Conceptually, these items may reflect broader behavioural dysregulation and atypical ideation accompanied by reduced behavioural flexibility and less efficient reality monitoring, thereby marking symptom configurations that are closely coupled with later anomalous experiences55.

By early adolescence (approximately ages 12–13 years), cross-domain associations became more reciprocal. OCS symptoms reflecting affective dysregulation, such as guilt-related experiences, remained connected with later PLE features, whereas unusual or grandiose delusional experiences showed clearer links with subsequent OCS symptoms, particularly intrusive thoughts (“can’t get mind off certain thoughts”) and worry64. This developmental shift is consistent with the possibility that, as PLEs become more consolidated, aberrant beliefs and unusual interpretations of experience may become increasingly intertwined with obsessive-compulsive cognitive processes, contributing to heightened rumination and affective distress8,60.

Across intervals, “strange behavior,” “can’t get mind off certain thoughts,” and “worries” repeatedly emerged as bridge symptoms linking the OCS and PLE domains. At the same time, these cross-domain associations were observed alongside strong within-domain stability in both symptom clusters (see Supplemental Material). The magnitude and consistency of the autoregressive effects suggest that cross-domain coupling unfolds against the background of two internally organized and relatively persistent symptom systems, rather than isolated symptom fluctuations65,66. Taken together, the temporal network findings complement the construct-level models by showing that the coupling between OCS and PLEs is symptom-specific and developmentally contingent: earlier stages are characterized by stronger OCS-to-PLE bridges, whereas later stages show more reciprocal coupling and clearer links from delusion-like experiences to intrusive obsessive cognition.

Clinical and research implications

The present findings have implications for both developmental psychopathology research and clinical formulation. At the theoretical level, the results suggest that the relationship between OCS and PLEs is better understood as developmentally staged rather than uniformly bidirectional across childhood and early adolescence. In late childhood, their co-occurrence appears to be more consistent with shared vulnerability at the between-person level, whereas in early adolescence PLEs become more closely linked to subsequent elevations in OCS at the within-person level. The symptom-level findings further highlight that this coupling is not diffuse, but organized around specific bridge symptoms, particularly intrusive thoughts and worry, with atypical behavior and ideation appearing more prominent in earlier stages. Together, these findings provide a more refined developmental account of how obsessive-compulsive and psychosis-spectrum phenomena may overlap across late childhood and early adolescence17.

From a clinical perspective, the findings underscore the importance of developmentally informed assessment and symptom-focused intervention. For children presenting with elevated OCS, particularly when accompanied by unusual behavior, atypical ideation, or persistent intrusive thoughts, closer monitoring of broader psychopathological difficulties, including psychosis-like experiences, may be warranted. For adolescents with emerging PLEs, special attention to concurrent intrusive thoughts, guilt-related cognitions, and worry may be clinically useful, given that these symptoms may reflect more tightly coupled cross-domain difficulties during this period. Rather than implying deterministic progression, the present results support the value of identifying bridge symptoms as potential points for early formulation and targeted intervention. Evidence-based approaches, including cognitive-behavioral strategies tailored to obsessive-compulsive and psychosis-like symptom profiles, may help address maladaptive cognitions and emotional dysregulation in a developmentally sensitive manner3. By focusing on bridge symptoms and their developmental patterning, clinicians and educators may be better positioned to deliver earlier and more targeted support for youth experiencing co-occurring OCS and PLE-related difficulties67.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be noted. First, OCS were assessed via parent report whereas PLEs were assessed by child self-report, which may introduce informant-specific biases and incompletely capture clinical complexity68,5. In addition, OCS were measured using the CBCL obsessive-compulsive problems scale rather than a diagnostic OCD interview or an OCD-specific symptom measure. Although this scale has been validated for identifying obsessive-compulsive problems in youth, it is not equivalent to a clinical diagnosis of OCD and may include items that overlap with broader anxiety, internalizing symptoms, or atypical behavior. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting parent-reported obsessive-compulsive problems rather than disorder-level OCD. Developmental changes in introspection and symptom awareness across late childhood and early adolescence may also influence PLE reporting over time, introducing time-varying measurement error and complicating interpretation of temporal ordering61. Second, despite the use of longitudinal models, the observational design does not permit causal inference. Third, the analytic sample was restricted to US youth aged 9–13 years, which may limit generalizability to other developmental periods, cultural contexts, and clinical populations. Future studies should replicate these patterns in diverse cohorts and extend follow-up into later adolescence and early adulthood to test whether reciprocal associations persist or shift with development. Combining multi-informant assessments, OCD-specific symptom measures or diagnostic interviews, longitudinal measurement-invariance testing, and neurobiological data with higher-frequency designs may further clarify mechanisms linking OCS and PLEs and refine intervention targets69.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that the overlap between OCS and PLEs changes across late childhood and early adolescence rather than reflecting a stable association over time. Early co-occurrence appeared to be more consistent with stable between-person differences, whereas later coupling was more closely related to within-person associations from PLEs to subsequent OCS. At the symptom level, intrusive thoughts and worry emerged as relatively consistent bridge symptoms across development. These findings highlight the importance of considering developmental timing and symptom-specific patterns when assessing co-occurring mental health difficulties in youth and may inform more targeted approaches to early identification and intervention.
