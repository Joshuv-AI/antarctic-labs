Program Synthesis for Non-Linear Real Arithmetic: Going Beyond Realizability
S. Akshay, Supratik Chakraborty, R. Govind, Aniruddha R. Joshi

Abstract
We study the problem of synthesizing programs from non-linear real arithmetic ( NRA ) specifications. Existing techniques, such as syntax-guided synthesis ( SyGuS ), fail to synthesize programs when the specification is unrealizable . We argue this is unsatisfactory in many situations, and aim to synthesize programs from arbitrary NRA specifications, such that for any input, the synthesized program either produces outputs satisfying the specification or reports non-existence of any such output. To avoid rounding errors inherent in floating-point arithmetic, we restrict our programs to work on rational inputs and outputs. We first show that our variant of the synthesis problem is as hard as a long-standing open problem in number theory, and that synthesizing loop-free programs from arbitrary NRA specifications with rational inputs and outputs is impossible in general. Second, we present a sound and complete synthesis algorithm for the case where the specification involves a single output variable. We also show that for realizable specifications, a program generated by SyGuS for NRA (real inputs and outputs) serves as a solution to our problem, where inputs and outputs are rationals. Third, we provide a sound (but necessarily incomplete) synthesis algorithm for the general case of specifications. We have implemented our approach in a prototype tool called NQSynth that solves many benchmarks beyond the reach of state-of-the-art SyGuS tools, even when we render the specifications realizable.

adequate to have a program that generates correct outputs whenever the

faithfully reports the absence of correct outputs otherwise.

Related work

Program synthesis from
NRA
specifications with real (not
rational) inputs and outputs is closely related to quantifier
elimination in the existential theory of reals
(ETR)
[
50
]
, and to techniques for finding real roots of
polynomials
[
7
,
30
,
35
,
54
]
. While
existentially quantifying all outputs from a given specification
yields the weakest pre-condition on inputs, for specifications with a
single real output, polynomial root finding techniques can be used to
find disjoint intervals for all admissible real values of the output.
However, such root finding techniques do not distinguish between
rational and irrational roots, and cannot guarantee zero rounding
errors with floating-point implementations. There has been a long and
illustrious line of work on quantifier elimination in ETR, starting
with the seminal work of Tarski
[
50
]
, followed by
Collins’ celebrated CAD algorithm
[
14
,
15
]
, and several
subsequent adaptations
[
8
,
36
,
13
]
. Similarly,
finding real roots of polynomials is an intensely studied topic in
algebraic geometry (see
[
7
]
for an excellent exposition). We leverage this rich line of work in our solution, while being
cognizant of the high complexity of algorithms like CAD, and of the
limitations of polynomial root finding techniques in the rational
setting.

Earlier approaches to program synthesis from
NRA
specifications
can be broadly classified as template-based approaches and
syntax-guided approaches. The work
of
[
47
,
23
,
22
]
present
template-based techniques for synthesizing programs by filling holes
in user-provided sketches or templates. Syntax-guided or
SyGuS

approaches
[
3
,
4
,
21
,
5
]
have
also proven to be very effective with state-of-the-art
competition-winning
SyGuS
tools, such as CVC5
[
6
]
and
DryadSynth
[
26
]
, synthesizing non-trivial programs from a
large class of specifications, but only if the specification is
realizable. Our work neither fits the template-based synthesis
paradigm, nor can it be viewed as an instance of
SyGuS
. Instead,
it makes careful use of a handful of generic modules for reasoning
about polynomials to synthesize programs from specifications that go
beyond the capabilities of existing techniques.

There are also works that synthesize programs from restricted real/integer arithmetic specifications. For example,
[
32
,
33
]
discuss techniques to solve program synthesis for specifications that are combinations of

]
considers specifications given as non-linear constraints over

Preliminaries and Problem statement

Let
\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathbb{Q}
, denote the set of natural numbers,
integers, rational numbers and real numbers, respectively.
Let
\mathbf{X}=\{x_{1},\ldots x_{m}\}
and
\mathbf{Y}=\{y_{1},\ldots y_{n}\}
be disjoint sets of variables, representing program inputs and outputs respectively. A
relational specification
, or simply a
specification
, of a program is a first-order formula
\varphi
with free variables
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{X}\cup\mathbf{Y}
.
In this paper, we focus on specifications in the

quantifier-free theory of non-linear real arithmetic
(
NRA
), where variables are assumed to take values from .

Thus, our specifications are Boolean combinations of polynomial inequalities over real-valued input and output variables. However, for reasons related to rounding errors as explained in Section
1
, we will restrict our discussion to rational input and output values.
We use lower-case letters
v,x,y,z
, possibly with subscripts, to
denote variables, while constants are represented by letters
a,b
.
Sets of variables are denoted as
\mathbf{V}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
, and
similarly for
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z}
. Likewise, sets of constants are denoted

\mathbf{A}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}
. A monomial over
\mathbf{V}
is a (repeated)
product of variables in
\mathbf{V}
, written as
v_{1}^{r_{1}}v_{2}^{r_{2}}\cdots v_{k}^{r_{k}}
, where
r_{1},\ldots r_{k}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. A polynomial
is a rational weighted sum of monomials, i.e.
\sum_{j=1}^{t}a_{j}m_{j}
,
where
a_{j}\in\mathbb{Q}
and
m_{j}
is a monomial. Polynomials over

\mathbf{V}
are represented as
p(\mathbf{V}),q(\mathbf{V}),r(\mathbf{V})
etc.
A polynomial inequality is a constraint of the form
p(\mathbf{V})\bowtie 0
, where
\bowtie~\in\{<,>,\leq,\geq\}
. Polynomial constraints over
\mathbf{V}
are Boolean combinations of polynomial inequalities over
\mathbf{V}
, and are represented as
\varphi(\mathbf{V}),\psi(\mathbf{V})
etc.
For a constant
a
and
v\in\mathbf{V}
, we use
\varphi[v\mapsto a]
to denote the constraint obtained by substituting
a
for
v
in
\varphi
.
If
\mathbf{A}
denotes the tuple of constants
(a_{1},\ldots a_{k})
and
\mathbf{V}
denotes the tuple of variables
(v_{1},\ldots v_{k})
, we use
\varphi[\mathbf{V}\mapsto\mathbf{A}]
or simply
\varphi(\mathbf{A})
to denote
\big(\cdots(\varphi[v_{1}\mapsto a_{1}])\cdots\big)[v_{k}\mapsto a_{k}]
.

Problem Statement.

Given an
NRA
specification

\varphi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
, our goal, as outlined in Sec.
1
, is
to synthesize a terminating program
\mathsf{Prog}
that takes as input
arbitrary rational values (say,
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Q}^{|\mathbf{X}|}
),
and either generates
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{Q}^{|\mathbf{Y}|}
such that
\varphi(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
holds, or reports that no such
\mathbf{B}
exists in
\mathbb{Q}^{|\mathbf{Y}|}
.
In the latter case, we allow our synthesized programs to return a
special symbol
\bot
. A program satisfying this property is said to

realize
the
NRA
specification
\varphi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
.
As we show shortly, synthesizing a program that realizes a
specification in
NRA
is not possible in general, unless some
long-standing problems in number-theory are resolved. Hence, we
propose to develop a synthesis algorithm that is sound but incomplete
in general. In other words, whenever the synthesized program returns

\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{Q}^{|\mathbf{Y}|}
for an input
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Q}^{|\mathbf{X}|}
, then

\varphi(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
indeed holds. However, there may exist some
other
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Q}^{|\mathbf{X}|}
for which the synthesized program
returns
\bot
although
\exists\mathbf{Y}\;\varphi(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y})
holds. For
some sub-classes of specifications, however, we can indeed obtain a
sound and complete synthesis algorithm. Thus, for specifications in
these sub-classes, the synthesized program returns
\bot
for an input

\mathbf{A}
iff
\forall\mathbf{Y}\;\neg\varphi(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y})
holds. The formal
definition of the problem statement has already been given in the
introduction.

A program returns either a special symbol

).

::=

(

)

\mathsf{Prog}

\mathsf{Prog}

\mathsf{LoopStmt}

::=

(
\mathsf{PCnstr}
)

\mathsf{Prog}

It is not hard to see that programs derived from this grammar are Turing powerful (e.g. counter-machines can be encoded).
Let
\mathsf{Prog}
be a program in the language of the grammar
\mathcal{G}
, and
let
\mathbf{X}
be a tuple of variables used in
\mathsf{Prog}
. We use the
notation
\mathsf{Prog}(\mathbf{X})
to denote the fact that
\mathsf{Prog}
does not assign
to any variable in
\mathbf{X}
, and instead uses values of these variables
as inputs. For
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Q}^{|\mathbf{X}|}
, we also use
\mathsf{Prog}(\mathbf{A})
to
denote the result (return value) of running the program
\mathsf{Prog}
with

\mathbf{X}
set to constants in
\mathbf{A}
. A special

\mathsf{LoopStmt}

Note that the above grammar, though simple, is quite expressive. For

, or

Hardness Results and Theoretical Characterizations

In this section, we state our theoretical characterization and hardness results. We start by recalling Hilbert’s tenth problem
[
16
]
which asks, given a Diophantine equation (a polynomial equation with integer coefficients and a finite number of unknowns), whether we can decide if the equation has a solution over integers. This problem is famously undecidable by a sequence of results, which is collectively referred to as the MRDP theorem
[
16
]
. Over reals, on the other hand, this problem is decidable and an algorithm for it follows from Tarski’s quantifier elimination
[
50
]
. However, over rationals, it turns out that this problem is still open. More precisely,
the Hilbert’s tenth problem over rationals, denoted
\mathcal{HTP}_{\mathbb{Q}}
, asks given a polynomial equation with integer or rational coefficients and a finite number of unknowns, whether we can decide if it has a solution over rationals. Despite significant effort in this direction
[
19
]
, this question remains a long-standing open problem. It turns out that the problem defined in the previous section is as hard as
\mathcal{HTP}_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Note that while
\mathcal{HTP}_{\mathbb{Q}}
is a decision problem,
\mathcal{RPS}_{\mathbb{Q}}
is a synthesis problem; hence the statements are suitably framed.

Theorem 3.1

1.

\mathcal{RPS}_{\mathbb{Q}}
is at least as hard as
\mathcal{HTP}_{\mathbb{Q}}
.

2.

\mathcal{HTP}_{\mathbb{Q}}
is at least as hard as
\mathcal{RPS}_{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Proof

1. Given an instance of
\mathcal{HTP}_{\mathbb{Q}}
, i.e., a polynomial equation

\varphi(\mathbf{V})
, we can build the specification
\varphi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})

with empty inputs
\mathbf{X}=\emptyset
and output
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{V}
. This is
an instance of
\mathcal{RPS}_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Now suppose we are able to synthesize a
terminating program
\mathsf{Prog}(\mathbf{X})
for this specification. Then
on running
\mathsf{Prog}
with no inputs (since
\mathbf{X}=\emptyset
), if it
returns
\bot
, this means that
\varphi(\mathbf{V})
has no solution.
If, on the other hand, it returns
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{Q}^{|\mathbf{Y}|}
, then
this is guaranteed to be a solution. Thus,
\mathsf{Prog}
can be used to
build a decision procedure for
\mathcal{HTP}_{\mathbb{Q}}
.

2. In the other direction, given an instance

\varphi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
of
\mathcal{RPS}_{\mathbb{Q}}
, we show below how to solve
it using a decision procedure for
\mathcal{HTP}_{\mathbb{Q}}
as a sub-routine.
W.l.o.g. we assume
\varphi
to be in disjunctive normal form,
i.e. of the form
\bigvee_{i=1}^{k}\psi_{i}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
, where

\psi_{i}
is a conjunction of polynomial inequalities (recall that
negation of a polynomial inequality is itself a polynomial
inequality). We first show that for each such
\psi_{i}
, we can
obtain a single equi-satisfiable polynomial equation. This is done
in three sub-steps.
First, for every strict inequality of the form
x>y
in
\psi_{i}
,
we introduce a fresh rational variable, say
z^{\prime}
, and replace the
strict inequality by the conjunction
(x\geq y)\wedge((x-y)z^{\prime}=1)
. Clearly, this conjunction is satisfiable iff
x>y
is satisfiable. Next, for every sub-formula of the form
x\geq y
in
\psi_{i}
, we introduce four fresh rational variables
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4}
, and then replace this inequality by the equation

x-y=z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{2}+z_{4}^{2}
. Clearly if this equation is
satisfied, then
x-y
must be non-negative. By Lagrange’s
four-square theorem
[
27
]
, we also know that every
non-negative rational is the sum of four rational squares. So
there are always solutions for the new variables as long as

x-y\geq 0
holds. Let
\mathbf{Z}
denote the set of all fresh rational
variables introduced above. Then, the above steps give us a
conjunction of equalities, say
\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})
, such that

\psi_{i}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\equiv\exists\mathbf{Z}\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})
. Finally,
we convert
\psi_{i}^{\prime}
– a conjunction of polynomial equations –
into a single equation. This can be easily done by observing that
for any two polynomial equations
p=0
and
q=0
, the formula

(p=0)\wedge(q=0)
is equivalent to
p^{2}+q^{2}=0
. Thus, after the
above sequence of steps, we obtain a single polynomial equation,
say
\psi_{i}^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})
, such that
\psi_{i}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\equiv\exists\mathbf{Z}\psi_{i}^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})
holds. Note that

\psi_{i}^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})
being a single polynomial equation is also
an instance of
\mathcal{HTP}_{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Now, for any input
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Q}^{|\mathbf{X}|}
, if
\mathcal{HTP}_{\mathbb{Q}}
on

\psi_{i}^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})
answers “No” for all
i\in\{1,\ldots k\}
, then no
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{Q}^{|\mathbf{Y}|}
can satisfy

\varphi(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y})\equiv\bigvee_{i=1}^{k}\psi_{i}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y})
; hence a
program realizing
\varphi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
must return

\bot
. Otherwise, if
\mathcal{HTP}_{\mathbb{Q}}
returns “Yes” for some

\psi_{i}^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})
, then there exists

\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{Q}^{|\mathbf{Y}|+|\mathbf{Z}|}
s.t
\psi(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
holds. Since
this is a rational vector, we can now find it by enumeration.
This gives us a terminating program that realizes

\varphi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
. ∎

The above proof requires enumeration in the final step, and therefore
does not guarantee that the synthesized program is loop-free, even if
we had an
\mathcal{HTP}_{\mathbb{Q}}
oracle. But there could be alternative ways of
solving
\mathcal{RPS}_{\mathbb{Q}}
. We therefore ask: Is it possible to solve
\mathcal{RPS}_{\mathbb{Q}}

using only loop-free programs? The answer turns out to be in the
negative.

Theorem 3.2

\mathcal{RPS}_{\mathbb{Q}}
cannot be solved using loop-free programs.

The proof, as detailed in Appendix
0.B
, proceeds by showing that if
\mathcal{RPS}_{\mathbb{Q}}
can be solved using loop-free programs, then this would imply that the theory of rational arithmetic admits quantifier elimination, and hence decidability. However, by a famous result of Robinson
[
45
]
, we know that this is not the case.

4
Algorithmic procedures for solving
\mathcal{RPS}_{\mathbb{Q}}

Given the hardness results above, a sound and complete algorithm for
solving the
\mathcal{RPS}_{\mathbb{Q}}
problem in its full generality is unlikely. This
motivates us to (a) consider fragments of
NRA
for which we can
design sound and complete synthesis algorithms, and (b) design sound
but incomplete algorithms in general, that perform well in practice.
We start with an interesting observation.

4.1
Solutions of
\mathcal{RPS}
are solutions of
\mathcal{RPS}_{\mathbb{Q}}

We show that every program derivable from grammar
\mathcal{G}
(see
Fig.
1
) that realizes a specification

\varphi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
over
reals
, also realizes

\varphi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
over
rationals
. This immediately
suggests the following result.

Theorem 4.1

Let
\mathcal{L}
be any fragment of
NRA
for which there is a
sound and complete algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{L}}
for solving

\mathcal{RPS}
, i.e. synthesis over reals using grammar
\mathcal{G}
. Then

{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{L}}
also solves
\mathcal{RPS}_{\mathbb{Q}}
, i.e. synthesis over
rationals using
\mathcal{G}
, for fragment
\mathcal{L}
.
Moreover, there exists a fragment of
NRA
for which there does
not exist any sound and complete algorithm for solving
\mathcal{RPS}
,
but for which there is a sound and complete algorithm for solving

\mathcal{RPS}_{\mathbb{Q}}
.

We defer the proof to Appendix
0.D
.
A consequence of Theorem
4.1
is that whenever a
SyGuS
tool synthesizes a program
\mathsf{Prog}
(\mathbf{X})
for a (realizable) specification
\varphi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})

over reals
using
\mathcal{G}
or any fragment of it, we can effectively use
\mathsf{Prog}
(\mathbf{X})
as a solution for the

\mathcal{RPS}_{\mathbb{Q}}
problem. If
\varphi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
is not realizable, we can
first compute the weakest pre-condition
\psi(\mathbf{X})\equiv\exists\mathbf{Y}\,\varphi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
using a quantifier elimination algorithm for
reals. If a
SyGuS
tool can then synthesize a program realizing

\psi(\mathbf{X})\rightarrow\varphi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})

over reals
, then the
same program with a check for satisfaction of
\psi(\mathbf{X})
also serves
as a solution for the
\mathcal{RPS}_{\mathbb{Q}}
problem for
\varphi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
.

4.2
Complete synthesis for Single-Output Specifications

We now propose a sound and complete synthesis procedure for solving

\mathcal{RPS}_{\mathbb{Q}}
when the specification has only one output variable. Thus, the
specification is a formula
\varphi(\mathbf{X},y)
in
NRA
, where
y

is the sole program output. At a high level, the synthesis procedure
separates the reasoning into two cases: (i) finding solutions that
satisfy the constraints with only strict inequalities, and (ii)
finding solutions that arise only on the boundary of non-strict
inequalities. These cases are handled using two classical results from
number theory, namely
Real Root Isolation
(RRI) and the

Rational Root Theorem
(RRT), along with quantifier elimination
from polynomial constraints using
CAD
or variant algorithms. For
notational convenience, we refer to a generic quantifier elimination
procedure using
CAD
as
QEPCAD
. Crucially,
QEPCAD
is used only
while synthesizing the program; it is not invoked during execution of
the synthesized program. We start with an overview of two key results
we rely on.

Real root isolation. (RRI)
[
53
,
12
,
30
]

This refers to a family of algorithms that given a univariate
polynomial, compute a collection of intervals with rational end-points
on the real line, such that each interval contains exactly one real
root of the polynomial (if any exist).
The maximum size of an interval output by RRI, say
\varepsilon>0
,
can be user-specified. Using classical results on lower bounds of
root separation
[
7
,
41
]
, the value of
\varepsilon
can
be chosen (or even iteratively reduced), so that the intervals for the
real roots obtained from RRI are pairwise disjoint. Since the sign of
a polynomial is invariant between its real roots, gaps between the
disjoint intervals output by RRI give intervals of values where the
polynomial’s sign cannot change. Given a specification
\varphi
that
is a conjunction of
strict inequalities
, the above discussion
suggests the following procedure for computing values of
y
, given a
value of
\mathbf{X}
, such that
\varphi
is satisfied. We first
substitute the given value of
\mathbf{X}
in every polynomial

p(\mathbf{X},y)
that appears in a constraint of the form
p(\mathbf{X},y)\bowtie 0~(\bowtie\in\{<,>\})
in
\varphi(\mathbf{X},y)
. This
effectively gives us a set of uni-variate polynomials in
y
. Next,
we construct the product of all these uni-variate polynomials, and
apply RRI with an appropriate value of
\epsilon
to obtain the
interval gaps where the product polynomial’s sign is invariant.
Clearly, none of the factor polynomials change sign in any of these
intervals. By evaluating each factor polynomial at the mid-point of
each such gap interval, we can find a rational value of
y
that
satisfies
\varphi(\mathbf{X},y)
, if one exists.

The above argument relies on the fact that the rationals are dense in

\mathbb{R}
. Specifically, after substituting a rational value for

\mathbf{X}
, if
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are rationals such that every
real
y
in
r_{1}<y<r_{2}
satisfies
\chi(\mathbf{X},y)
, then there exist
rationals between
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
(for instance,

\frac{r_{1}+r_{2}}{2}
) that also satisfy
\chi(\mathbf{X},y)
.
This immediately implies that if a rational value of
\mathbf{X}
satisfies

\exists y\,\chi(\mathbf{X},y)
over the reals, then the same value of

\mathbf{X}
also satisfies
\exists y\,\chi(\mathbf{X},y)
over rationals.
We exploit this property in detecting when
\exists y\,\varphi(\mathbf{X},y)

has a solution over rationals.

Rational root theorem (RRT)
[
20
]
.

The second result that we need is the rational root theorem, which can
be formulated as follows.

Theorem 4.2
(Rational Root Theorem
[
20
]
)

Let
a_{n}x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{1}x+a_{0}=0
be a
polynomial equation, where
a_{0},\ldots a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
a_{0},a_{n}\neq 0
. Then every rational solution
x=\frac{u}{v}

of the equation, where
u
and
v
are relatively prime, satisfies
the conditions: (i)
u
is an integer factor of
a_{0}
, and (ii)

v
is an integer factor of
a_{n}
.

An immediate consequence of Theorem
4.2
is that every
rational solution of the polynomial equation lies in the set

\{\frac{u}{v}\mid u\in\mathsf{IntFactor}(a_{0}),v\in\mathsf{IntFactor}(a_{n})\}
,
where
\mathsf{IntFactor}(m)
is the set of integer factors of
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Since each natural number has finitely many divisors, the set
of candidate rational solutions is finite.

We use these crucial observations to synthesize a program
\mathsf{Prog1OP}_{\varphi}(\mathbf{X})
that realizes
\varphi(\mathbf{X},y)
. Though the polynomials in our specification may use rational coefficients, an inequality involving such a polynomial can be converted to an equivalent inequality with only integer
coefficients by multiplying both sides of the inequality by the least
common multiple of all denominators of rational coefficients. Hence
Theorem
4.2
applies to polynomial equations with rational coefficients as well, i.e., our specification class.

Recall that the formula
\varphi(\mathbf{X},y)
may contain both strict and non-strict polynomial inequalities.
Let
\mathsf{NonStrict}(\varphi)
denote the set of polynomial terms
p
that contain the output variable
y
and occur in non-strict inequalities of the form
p\geq 0
or
p\leq 0
in
\varphi
.
From
\varphi(\mathbf{X},y)
, we can always construct the formula
\widehat{\varphi}(\mathbf{X},y)
obtained by converting every non-strict inequality in
\mathsf{NonStrict}(\varphi)
to a strict inequality. In other words, for every
p\in\mathsf{NonStrict}(\varphi)
, if
p\leq 0
(resp.
p\geq 0
) is a polynomial inequality in
\varphi
, we replace all instances of this inequality in
\varphi
by
p<0
(resp.
p>0
) to get
\widehat{\varphi}(\mathbf{X},y)
. We use
QEPCAD
to obtain
\psi(\mathbf{X})
by eliminating quantifiers from
\exists y\;\widehat{\varphi}(\mathbf{X},y)
. The pseudocode of the program
\mathsf{Prog1OP}_{\varphi}(\mathbf{X})
that realizes
\varphi(\mathbf{X},y)
in

is given in Algorithm

.

4.3
Synthesis for full
NRA

In this section, we present a sound but incomplete procedure that
solves
\mathcal{RPS}_{\mathbb{Q}}
for the general case.
Given the hardness results in Section
3
, we limit
ourselves to providing a sound procedure that nevertheless solves a
large collections of benchmarks (including unrealizable
specifications), as demonstrated by our experiments.

A naive way to solve the multiple output case would be to try to
process it one output at a time by existentially quantifying the
others. However, as explained earlier, existential quantification over
rationals is at least as hard as
\mathcal{HTP}_{\mathbb{Q}}
, and hence we cannot hope for
a practically efficient procedure. Instead, we propose an alternative
approach that first considers a (rational) point that satisfies the
given specification, and then uses this to decompose the synthesis
problem to a collection of single output synthesis sub-problems. The
sound and complete procedure for the one output case described in
Section
4.2
is used to solve these sub-problems
individually, and the solutions composed to incrementally synthesize
the desired program. The process is repeated until the weakest
pre-condition of the given specification is covered, or until the
algorithm times out. The main difficulty of this approach is to
maintain rationality (and correctness) of solutions, while ensuring
progress at each iteration.

In addition, we can also provide a guarantee of partial completeness of the procedure. That is, if the procedure terminates before timing out, then we know that the there is no way to satisfy the specification while violating all the (partial) pre-conditions obtained so far. Hence, the program synthesized by the procedure realizes the post-condition.

not rational, we skip the

solver again until we get a rational solution.

Conclusion

In this paper, we addressed rational program synthesis from polynomial specifications.
We investigated theoretical aspects of the problem, loop-free conditions and links to quantifier elimination.
We then developed algorithms for synthesis, sound and complete for subclasses and an
NRA
-oracle based sound algorithm in general. Our prototype implementation already shows promise of the approach in synthesizing rational programs for polynomial specification. As future work, we would like to know when a particular polynomial specification has a loop-free program, if we can effectively synthesize it.
Finally, a practical bottleneck in this work is the dependence on
QEPCAD
and removing this would be a path towards further applicability.
