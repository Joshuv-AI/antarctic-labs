On the global convergence of gradient descent for wide shallow models with bounded nonlinearities
Romain Petit, Clarice Poon, Gabriel Peyré

Abstract
A surprising phenomenon in the training of neural networks is the ability of gradient descent to find global minimizers of the training loss despite its non-convexity. Following earlier works, we investigate this behavior for wide shallow networks. Existing results essentially cover the case of ReLU activations and the case of sigmoid activations with scalar output weights. We study a large class of models that includes multi-head attention layers and two-layer sigmoid networks with vector output weights. Building upon [ 10 ] , we prove that all non-global minimizers of the training loss are unstable under gradient descent dynamics. Thus, when the initial distribution of the parameters has full support (which includes the popular Gaussian case), and in the many hidden neurons or attention heads limit, continuous-time gradient descent can only converge to global minimizers. Establishing the instability of non-global minimizers corresponds to the construction of an “escaping active set” – we complete the proof of [ 10 ] to construct this set for models with bounded nonlinearities and scalar output weights. We also extend this construction to new cases for models with vector output weights. Finally, we show the well-posedness and the stability with respect to discretization of the mean field training dynamic for sub-Gaussian initializations.



1
Introduction

The training of neural networks leads to the minimization of objective functions that are highly non-convex. As a result, first order optimization algorithms such as gradient descent could fail to reach an approximate minimizer and converge to non-optimal critical points. In certain settings, it has been observed that this behavior is not typical and that gradient descent finds global minimizers of the training loss despite its non-convexity. Among the various explanations that have been proposed, a line of works has focused on the study of the
over-parameterized regime
. The landmark results of
[
10
]
(see also
[
27
,
32
]
) show that, if the model is sufficiently over-parameterized and the initialization densely covers the parameter space, then (continuous-time) gradient descent can only converge to global minimizers of the training loss.

In these works, the over-parameterized models under study are typically fully connected neural networks with a single hidden layer and a large number of hidden neurons. Existing results essentially cover sigmoid activations with scalar output weights and ReLU activations. Besides networks with sigmoid activations and vector output weights, other popular architectures are not covered by these results. This is in particular the case of attention layers, which have become ubiquitous in recent years. In this work, we make a step towards bridging this gap by investigating global convergence results for a large class of over-parameterized models.

1.1
Main problem

We study the training of parameterized models of the form
(1/m)\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Phi(w_{i},\theta_{i})
, where
\Phi
is
linear
in the
w
variable. This setting covers two main examples of interest: transformers with a single multi-head attention layer and two-layer fully connected neural networks. In the first case, the
w
and
\theta
variables respectively correspond to the value matrix and to the query and key matrices. In the second case, they respectively correspond to the parameters of the output layer and to the parameters of the hidden layer.

We analyze the training of such models with continuous-time gradient descent (or gradient flow). To be more precise, we analyze the gradient flow of the function
F_{m}\colon\Omega^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

F_{m}((w_{i},\theta_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m})=\textstyle R((1/m)\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Phi(w_{i},\theta_{i})),

(1)

where
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}
and
R
is a given loss function. The objective
F_{m}
is highly non-convex, and the iterates of gradient descent could get trapped by spurious local minima. Yet, in the large
m
(or
over-parameterized
) regime, gradient descent often converges to global minimizers of
F_{m}
. The main objective of this work is to understand this phenomenon.

Following
[
10
]
, the key object of our analysis is the dynamic one obtains by encoding the parameters of the model as a probability distribution over the parameter space
\Omega
. This evolution turns out to be a
Wasserstein gradient flow
(see
[
33
]
for an introduction to this topic) of a function
F
defined on the set
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
of probability distributions on
\Omega
. By studying the long-time limit of this evolution, we aim to find conditions under which the gradient flow of
F_{m}
converges to a global minimizer.

1.2
Related work

Scaling limits.

It is by now well understood that non-convexity is not necessarily an obstacle when training highly over-parameterized neural networks, including deep ones. Under suitable scaling regimes, local and sometimes global convergence can be established. Different scalings lead to distinct limiting descriptions of training dynamics, with the two most studied cases being the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) regime and the mean-field or “feature learning” regime. These asymptotic viewpoints clarify why optimization succeeds in over-parameterized models, although they may not always align with practical behavior
[
11
,
25
,
36
]
.

Wide networks and NTK.

For nonlinear multilayer perceptrons, a standard scaling is to initialize weights with variance proportional to
1/\sqrt{m}
, where
m
is the width of a hidden layer. In this setting, when the width grows, hidden units move little during training, and in the infinite-width limit, they remain fixed. The resulting dynamics correspond to kernel regression with the Neural Tangent Kernel
[
21
]
. This framework provides a route to proving global convergence in sufficiently wide networks, with refinements depending on data separability and network depth.
For two-layer ReLU networks, convergence has been analyzed under the NTK scaling
[
24
]
. For deeper models, general convergence results have been established
[
1
,
14
,
25
,
37
,
23
,
9
,
29
]
.
This analysis can be refined for residual networks, illustrating how skip connections stabilize training
[
19
,
5
,
38
,
4
]
.

Feature learning regime and mean-field models.

An alternative scaling is to initialize weights with variance
1/m
. In this case, hidden units evolve significantly during training, leading to the so-called mean-field or feature learning regime. Here, the network can be described by a probability measure over neurons, which adapts during training. Unlike NTK, this regime captures representation learning and explains the ability of neural networks to exploit low-dimensional structures in data
[
3
,
18
]
. It also connects neural networks to functional analysis, where associated function spaces can be defined
[
15
]
. The mean-field scaling has been rigorously formalized as the limit of interacting particle systems, yielding a partial differential equation (PDE) of McKean–Vlasov type, which is a gradient flow for the Wasserstein metric
[
10
,
27
,
32
,
35
,
28
]
. This PDE viewpoint allows one to prove global convergence results when the support of the initial distribution is sufficiently broad
[
10
]
. The main goal of our work is to extend the PDE framework (without diffusion and noise injection) and convergence analysis to a broader class of architectures—so as to accommodate attention heads and vector output weights for bounded activation functions—as well as to more general initialization schemes, including non–compactly supported distributions such as Gaussians and, more generally, sub-Gaussian distributions.

Training attention and transformers.

The convergence analysis of transformers and attention mechanisms has followed parallel lines in the NTK regime.
[
22
]
reformulated attention as a linear-time mechanism using kernelization, allowing convergence to be analyzed with tools from fast autoregressive training.
[
12
]
introduced the Performer architecture, which uses random feature approximations to make attention scalable while maintaining convergence properties, and
[
30
]
further developed random feature attention with theoretical guarantees. The feature learning behavior of attention under head scaling remains poorly understood. One focus of this article is to address this gap by uncovering and analyzing the mean-field structure of wide attention layers.

1.3
Contributions

Our main contribution, which is the object of
Section

3
, is the proof that, under suitable assumptions, the Wasserstein gradient flow of
F
can only converge to measures satisfying a certain condition. We establish this result for a large class of models, including multi-head attention layers and two-layer fully connected networks with vector output weights and sigmoid activations.
Section

4
is dedicated to the proof that these two examples fit in our framework. In
[
10
, Appendix C]
, a closely related condition was shown to be satisfied only at global minimizers of
F
for sigmoid networks with scalar output weights, hence providing a global convergence result for the Wasserstein gradient flow. We provide a corrected version of this proof, which contained an erroneous argument. We also investigate the extension of this result to sigmoid networks with vector output weights and attention layers in
Section

3.3
, and conjecture that it holds under mild assumptions.

As the Wasserstein gradient flow of
F
is a key object in our analysis, we also study its well-posedness and stability with respect to discretization in
Section

2
. We improve previous results by proving its existence and uniqueness for all times, as well as a stability estimate, for a larger class of initializations. Our main assumption is that the initial measure is sub-Gaussian (instead of compactly supported for previous works), which includes the popular Gaussian case. In the case of two-layer fully connected networks, in addition to sigmoid activations, our assumptions also cover the popular GELU and SiLU / swish activations.

Regularization.

An important difference with respect to
[
10
]
is that they allow an optional regularization term in the objective. Our choice to cover the unregularized case is motivated by two reasons. First, the potential non-smoothness of the regularizer leads to several additional technical difficulties. Second, in the case of attention layers and fully connected networks with sigmoid activations, the analysis conducted in
[
10
]
would only allow us to treat the case of an
\ell^{1}
regularization of the weights, which does not seem popular. Practitioners rather use weight decay, which corresponds to an
\ell^{2}
squared regularization.

Parameterization of the attention.

Transformers have a complex architecture that relies on a number of crucial operations, such as layer normalization, skip connections, and variants of the classical softmax self-attention. In this work, we focus on a simplified task, that is, the training of a single softmax self-attention layer with a large number of attention heads. Moreover, we study the training of a parameterization that slightly differs from the most standard one. Namely, if
(Q,K,V)
are the query, key, and value matrices of an attention head, its output only depends on
V
and the matrix
A=K^{T}Q
. Optimizing over both factors
(Q,K)
of the product leads to several difficulties, including non-Lipschitz gradients. For this reason, we directly optimize over the variables
(A,V)
and leave the investigation of the usual parameterization to future works.

2
Mean-field training dynamic

In this section, we introduce the evolution one obtains by encoding the parameters of the model as a probability measure on the parameter space. We prove the well-posedness and stability with respect to discretization of this mean-field training dynamic for a broad class of initializations, namely, sub-Gaussian distributions.

2.1
Setting

In all the following, we rely on the following assumption. As shown in
Section

4
below, it covers multi-head attention layers, as well as fully connected networks with a single hidden layer, provided the activation function is differentiable with a bounded and Lipschitz differential. This includes the popular sigmoid, GELU, and SiLU / swish activations (see
[
31
,
16
,
20
]
for more details on these recently proposed activation functions).

Assumption 1
.

{\mathcal{F}}
is a separable Hilbert space and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the closure of a convex open set. The loss
R:{\mathcal{F}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is differentiable with a differential that is Lipschitz on bounded sets and bounded on sublevel sets of
R
. The mapping
\Phi:\Omega\to{\mathcal{F}}
is differentiable, and its differential has the following properties.

(i)

Linear growth:
\|d\Phi(u)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d};{\mathcal{F}})}\lesssim 1+|u|
.

(ii)

Local Lipschitz continuity: for every
r>0
and
u_{1},u_{2}\in B(0,r)
it holds

\|d\Phi(u_{1})-d\Phi(u_{2})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d};{\mathcal{F}})}\lesssim(1+r)|u_{1}-u_{2}|.

Gradient flows.

Gradient flows, which correspond to the vanishing step size limit of gradient descent, are curves of steepest descent. In the case of the objective function
F_{m}
, a gradient flow is a
C^{1}
path
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto(w_{i}(t),\theta_{i}(t))_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m}
such that

(w_{i}^{\prime}(t),\theta_{i}^{\prime}(t))_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m}=-\nabla F_{m}((w_{i}(t),\theta_{i}(t))_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m})

for every
t\geqslant 0
. Under
1
, the objective
F_{m}
is of class
C^{1}
and lower bounded. As a consequence, standard results (see for instance
[
33
]
) imply that, for every initialization
(w_{i}(0),\theta_{i}(0))_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m}\in\Omega^{m}
, there exists a unique gradient flow of
F_{m}
.

Lifting to the space of probability measures.

Following
[
10
]
, we define the function

\displaystyle F\colon\mathcal{P}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\mapsto\begin{cases}R(\int_{\Omega}\Phi d\mu)&\mathrm{if}~\int_{\Omega}\|\Phi(w,\theta)\|_{{\mathcal{F}}}\,d\mu(w,\theta)<+\infty,\\
+\infty&\mathrm{otherwise,}\end{cases}

and notice that the mapping
F_{m}
defined in (
1
) satisfies
F_{m}((w_{i},\theta_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m})=F((1/m)\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{(w_{i},\theta_{i})})
. In other words, the restriction of
F
to empirical distributions coincides with
F_{m}
. The main advantage of this lifting is that it allows to consider models associated with more general parameter distributions, such as distributions with an infinite support.

Wasserstein gradient flow.

In
[
10
, Proposition B.1]
, it is shown that, if
t\mapsto(w_{i}(t),\theta_{i}(t))_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m}
is a gradient flow of
F_{m}
, then
t\mapsto\mu_{t}:=(1/m)\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{(w_{i}(t),\theta_{i}(t)}
is a Wasserstein gradient flow of
F
, that is to say a curve of steepest descent for the geometry induced by the Wasserstein distance on
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
. To be more precise, it holds

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=-\mathrm{div}(\mu_{t}v_{t})~~\mathrm{with}~~v_{t}(u)=-\nabla F^{\prime}(\mu_{t})(u)

(2)

for every
t\geqslant 0
, where
F^{\prime}(\nu):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the function representing the differential of
F
at some
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
. We refer the reader to
Appendix

B
for the precise meaning of (
2
) and to
[
33
]
for more details on Wasserstein gradient flows. When the evolution (
2
) is initialized with a more complicated distribution, proving its long-time existence and stability is more involved. This is the object of the following subsection.

2.2
Well-posedness

Under
1
, we are able to show that the mean field training dynamic is well-posed for every sub-Gaussian intialization.

Theorem 1
.

Under
1
, if
{\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}
is sub-Gaussian, then there exists a unique Wasserstein gradient flow
(\mu_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
of
F
starting from
\mu_{0}
. Moreover, if
\mu_{m,0}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
converges to
\mu_{0}
for the
W_{2}
distance when
m\to+\infty
, then
(\mu_{m,t})_{t\geqslant 0}
converges as
m\to+\infty
to
(\mu_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
. Moreover, there exists
C>0
such that
W_{2}(\mu_{t},\mu_{m,t})\leqslant e^{Ct}W_{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{m,0})
for every

.

The proof of
1
is given in
Appendix

B
. Our proof strategy and our assumptions on the initialization are discussed below.

Assumption on the initialization.

We stress that our condition on the initialization is essentially weaker than that of
[
10
, Proposition 2.5]
, which requires the
w
-marginal of the initial measure to have compact support. In particular, our condition covers the important case of Gaussian initializations. In
Section

3
below, we focus on the case where
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}
and
\Phi:(w,\theta)\mapsto\phi(\theta)w
where
\phi(\theta)
is a linear mapping from
\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}}
to
{\mathcal{F}}
. The
w
variable corresponds to the parameters of the output layer (in the case of fully connected networks) or to the value matrix (in the case of attention layers). The
\theta
variable corresponds to the parameters of the hidden layer (for fully connected networks) or to the query and key matrices (for attention layers). For attention layers or fully connected networks with sigmoid activations, we could deal separately with these two sets of variables and exploit the boundedness of
\phi
to remove the sub-Gaussian assumption on the
\theta
-marginal. However, this leads to more involved computations. For the sake of clarity, we choose to deal with both sets of variables jointly, at the price of requiring that the full distribution is sub-Gaussian.

Proof strategy.

In
[
10
]
, the analog of
1
(which is Proposition 2.5) is proved by relying on the theory of gradient flows in metric spaces developed in
[
2
]
. In contrast, our proof, which allows to remove the compact support assumption, only uses elementary tools and is based on a fixed point argument. We stress that this is made possible because we do not consider a possibly non-smooth regularization term in the objective, contrary to
[
10
]
. Our proof strategy is borrowed from the literature on interacting particle systems (see also
[
8
]
for an application to the analysis of infinitely deep transformers) and uses Dobrushin-type estimates (see for instance,
[
13
,
6
]
). However, the control we have on the velocity field is weaker than in these works. We circumvent this issue by exploiting the sub-Gaussian assumption on the initialization and by controlling the growth of the tails along the evolution.

3
Global convergence

In this section, we consider the case
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}
and
\Phi:(w,\theta)\mapsto\phi(\theta)w
where
\phi(\theta)
is a linear mapping from
\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}}\to{\mathcal{F}}
. We focus on the case where
\phi
is bounded, which covers two-layer fully connected networks with sigmoid activations and multi-head attention layers. We investigate the limit points of the Wasserstein gradient flow of

Existing results.

In
[
10
]
, two different cases are considered depending on the homogeneity of the mapping

is positively
1

\phi(\lambda\theta)=\lambda\phi(\theta)
for every
\lambda>0
. In this case,
\Phi
is positively
2
-homogeneous. This essentially models two-layer networks with ReLU activations. The second case corresponds to the situation where

is bounded, and

is hence only partially
1
-homogeneous. This models two-layer networks with sigmoid activations. In
[
10
, Theorems 3.4 and 3.5]
, it is proved that, in both cases and under suitable assumptions on the initial distribution, the Wasserstein gradient flow of
F
can only converge to global minimizers. This result is established for every
d_{w}\geqslant 1
in the
2
-homogeneous case but only for
d_{w}=1
in the partially
1
-homogeneous case.

Section outline.

In this section, we investigate the extension of the global convergence result mentioned above in the partially
1
-homogeneous setting when
d_{w}>1
. In
Section

3.1
, we leverage the tools introduced in
[
10
, Appendix C]
to prove that under suitable assumptions, the Wasserstein gradient flow of

can only converge to measures satisfying a certain condition. A proof that this condition holds only at global minimizers was proposed in
[

. However, a part of this proof relies on an erroneous argument. In

3.1
Limit points of the training dynamics

We first isolate an abstract instability mechanism for ruling out non-optimal
limit points of the training dynamic. The idea is simple: if the vector field
associated with a candidate limit point
\mu
contains a region of the parameter space where output weights must grow at a uniform rate, then a trajectory cannot
converge to
\mu
in
W_{2}
, because a positive amount of mass would escape to infinity.

Throughout this subsection, we denote by
C_{b}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}};\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}})
the space of bounded differentiable maps whose differential is bounded and Lipschitz. In the case of bounded nonlinearities, the relevant perturbation norm is
\|h\|_{\mathcal{X}}:=\|h\|_{\infty}+\sup_{r\geqslant 0}\|rJ_{h}(r\cdot)\|_{\infty}
, where
J_{h}
denotes the Jacobian matrix of
h
.

Definition 1

(Escaping active set)
.

Let

g\in C_{b}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}};\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}})
.
We say that
g
admits an escaping active set and write
\mathcal{E}(g)
if there exist constants

\varepsilon>0
,

\eta>0
,
and a Borel set

A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}

containing a nonempty open set such that the following holds.

For every absolutely continuous curve

(g_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}\subset C_{b}^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}};\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}})

satisfying

\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\|g_{t}-g\|_{\mathcal{X}}\leqslant\varepsilon,

(3)

and for every initial condition

(w_{0},\theta_{0})\in A
,
the solution of

\begin{cases}\dot{w}_{t}=-g_{t}(\theta_{t}),\\
\dot{\theta}_{t}=-J_{g_{t}}(\theta_{t})^{T}w_{t}\end{cases}

(4)

satisfies

\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}|w_{t}|^{2}\geqslant\eta\qquad\text{for all }t\geqslant 0.

Remark 1
.

If
g\equiv 0
, then
g
does not admit an escaping active set. Indeed, taking

g_{t}=g
for all
t
, every solution of the ordinary differential equation is constant.
Thus the escaping active set property is a genuine non-stationarity condition.

Assumption 2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a separable Hilbert space and let

\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}
.
Assume that the loss

R:\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

is convex and differentiable, with differential Lipschitz on bounded sets and
bounded on sublevel sets of
R
.
Assume also that

\Phi:\Omega\to\mathcal{F}

has the form
\Phi(w,\theta)=\phi(\theta)w
, where
\phi(\theta)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}};\mathcal{F})
is bounded and differentiable, with bounded Lipschitz differential.

Under
2
, the first variation of

F(\mu)=R\left(\int_{\Omega}\Phi\,d\mu\right)

is given by

\textstyle F^{\prime}(\mu)(w,\theta)=\langle R^{\prime}\left(\int_{\Omega}\Phi\,d\mu\right),\phi(\theta)w\rangle_{\mathcal{F}}.

We define

\textstyle g_{\mu}(\theta):=\phi(\theta)^{*}R^{\prime}\left(\int_{\Omega}\Phi\,d\mu\right),

so that

F^{\prime}(\mu)(w,\theta)=\langle g_{\mu}(\theta),w\rangle
.

We are now able to state the following result, which restricts the potential limit points of the training dynamic.

Theorem 2

(Escaping active sets cannot occur at
W_{2}
-limits)
.

Assume that
2
holds. Let
(\mu_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be a Wasserstein gradient
flow of
F
, and assume that
\mu_{0}
has full support. If
W_{2}(\mu_{t},\mu)\to 0
, then

does not admit an escaping active set.

Proof.

We argue by contradiction and assume
g_{\mu}
admits an escaping active set. We have

\textstyle\|g_{\mu_{t}}-g_{\mu}\|_{C^{1}}\leqslant\|\phi\|_{C^{1}}\|R^{\prime}(\int_{\Omega}\Phi d\mu_{t})-R^{\prime}(\int_{\Omega}\Phi d\mu)\|_{{\mathcal{F}}}.

Using the fact that
R^{\prime}
is Lipschitz on bounded sets together with
10
and the convergence of
(\mu_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
towards
\mu
for the
W_{2}
distance, we obtain the existence of

such that
\|g_{\mu_{t}}-g_{\mu}\|_{\infty}+\|J_{g_{\mu_{t}}}-J_{g_{\mu}}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\varepsilon
for every

. Using again that
W_{2}(\mu_{t},\mu)\to 0
when

and that

is Lipschitz, we obtain:

\underset{t\to+\infty}{\mathrm{lim}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}}|w|d\mu_{t}(w,\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}}|w|d\mu(w,\theta).

(5)

But since
\mathcal{E}(g_{\mu})
holds, we also have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}}|w|d\mu_{t}(w,\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}}|w_{t}|d\mu_{t_{0}}(w_{t_{0}},\theta_{t_{0}})

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{A}|w_{t}|d\mu_{t_{0}}(w_{t_{0}},\theta_{t_{0}})

\displaystyle\geqslant\eta(t-t_{0})\mu_{t_{0}}(A).

Using the results of
Appendix

is the pushforward of

by

, where

has full support, the same holds for

∎

3.3
Vector linear parameters

As explained above, the case
d_{w}>1
is not covered in
[
10

. For attention layers, imposing

amounts to dealing with scalar tokens, which is not realistic. As a result, we would like to extend the global convergence theory beyond the case of scalar output parameters.

In this setting, the dynamic of the linear parameters

becomes significantly more complex: they can change direction in addition to growing or shrinking in norm. In
Section

3.3.1
, we explain why a natural generalization of the proof of

Section

, we discuss an alternative construction of the set

. It allows to prove an analog of

when

and

under reasonable assumptions. However, these assumptions do not seem realistic when
d_{\theta}>1
.

We conjecture that

holds when

provided
g
is not identically zero and satisfy some regularity assumptions similar to those of
1
. Proving this would show that the mean field training dynamic can only converge to global minimizers of the training loss, even in the case of sigmoid networks with vector output parameters and multi-head attention layers.

3.3.1
First construction

A natural idea could be to define
A=\{(w,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}\,\rvert\,(w/|w|,\theta)\in K\}
, where

K=\{(v,\theta)\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{w}-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}\,\rvert\,\langle g(\theta),v\rangle\leqslant-\eta\}

and
-\eta
is a regular value of the mapping
h:(v,\theta)\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{w}-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}\mapsto\langle g(\theta),v\rangle
. If

then it holds

\frac{d}{dt}|w_{t}|=-\langle v_{t},g_{t}(\theta_{t})\rangle\geqslant-[\langle g(\theta_{t}),v_{t}\rangle-\varepsilon]\geqslant\eta/2

provided

is small enough. We also have

\frac{d}{dt}\langle g(\theta_{t}),v_{t}\rangle\leqslant-|w_{t}|[|J_{g}(\theta_{t})^{T}v_{t}|^{2}-\|J_{g}\|_{\infty}\varepsilon]-\frac{1}{|w_{t}|}[|\mathrm{proj}_{\{v_{t}\}^{\perp}}(g(\theta_{t}))|^{2}-\|g\|_{\infty}\varepsilon].

We know that

is non-zero on
\partial K
. We would like to guarantee that
\frac{d}{dt}\langle g(\theta_{t}),v_{t}\rangle<0
if

, which would yield that

for every

as soon as

would finally allow us to conclude. The problem is that if

is very small for some

is very large, we cannot say anything about the sign of

4.2.1

The aim of this subsection is to obtain an analog of

Given

) and

5
Conclusion

In this work, we studied the global convergence of gradient descent for wide shallow models with bounded nonlinearities. In the case of models with vector output weights, we reduced this question to a blow-up condition for a family of ordinary differential equations. The main question that we left open is the complete proof that this condition holds under mild assumptions.

There are several natural perspectives to this work. Concerning the training of attention layers, other variants of attention such as masked attention could be considered. An interesting but challenging question is to study the traditional
(Q,K,V)
parameterization, instead of optimizing on
(A,V)
where
A=K^{T}Q
. One could also investigate whether the abstract framework in which our results are proven allows to handle contexts with a variable (and possibly arbitrarily large) size. For training transformers, the Adam algorithm seems to significantly outperform gradient descent. Studying its global convergence properties is a highly interesting challenge. Weight decay (which amounts to a squared
\ell^{2}
regularization on the weights) is also popular and is not covered by our analysis. Finally, activation functions that are smooth approximations of ReLU (such as SiLU / swish and GELU) have become increasingly popular in modern architectures. As they are neither positively
1
-homogeneous nor bounded, they are not covered by our global convergence result or that of
[
10
]
. Investigating whether an analogous property can be proven in this case is particularly interesting.

d\Phi(w,\theta)\cdot(w^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})=\phi(\theta)w^{\prime}+[d\phi(\theta)\cdot\theta^{\prime}]w
. As a result, we obtain that
\|d\Phi(w,\theta)\|\leqslant(\|\phi(\theta)\|^{2}+|w|^{2}\|d\phi(\theta)\|^{2})^{1/2}
. Using that
d\phi
is bounded and Lipschitz, we obtain that
\|d\Phi(u)\|\lesssim 1+|u|
. Now, defining
\Delta=[d\Phi(w_{1},\theta_{1})-d\Phi(w_{2},\theta_{2})]\cdot(w^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})
we also have

\Delta=[\phi(\theta_{1})-\phi(\theta_{2})]w^{\prime}+[(d\phi(\theta_{1})-d\phi(\theta_{2}))\cdot\theta^{\prime}]w_{1}+[d\phi(\theta_{2})\cdot\theta^{\prime}](w_{1}-w_{2}).

As a consequence, we obtain

\|d\Phi(w_{1},\theta_{1})-d\Phi(w_{2},\theta_{2})\|\leqslant\sqrt{\|d\phi\|_{\infty}^{2}|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|^{2}+(\mathrm{Lip}(d\phi)|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}||w_{1}|+\|d\phi\|_{\infty}|w_{1}-w_{2}|)^{2}}

and a direct computation allows to conclude.
∎

Now, we proceed with the proof of
1
, which states that the square loss and the cross-entropy loss are both compatible with
1
and
2
.

Proof of
1
.

We argue as in the proof of
[
10
, Lemma D.1]
. First, a direction computation shows that, in both cases, the integral in the expression of
R(f)
is finite for every
f\in\cal F
. The convexity of
R
directly follows from the fact that, for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{out}}}}
, the mapping
z\mapsto\ell(z,y)
is convex.

In both cases,
z\mapsto\ell(z,y)
is differentiable for every
y
and there exists
L>0
such that, for every
(z_{1},z_{2},y)\in(\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{out}}}})^{3}
, it holds
{|\nabla_{z}\ell(z_{1},y)-\nabla_{z}\ell(z_{2},y)|\leqslant L|z_{1}-z_{2}|}
. To see this, notice that
{\nabla_{z}\ell(z,y)=z-y}
for the square loss so that we can take
L=1
. For the cross-entropy loss, it holds
{\nabla_{z}\ell(z,y)=-y+\sigma(z)}
, where
\sigma
denotes the softmax function. By
8
, we can take
L=2
.

Now, let us prove that the differential of
R
is given by

\textstyle dR(f)\cdot h=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{out}}}}}\langle\nabla_{z}\ell(f(x),y),h(x)\rangle d\rho(x,y).

We have

\displaystyle\Delta(h)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}|R(f+h)-R(f)-dR(f)\cdot h|

\displaystyle=\textstyle|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{out}}}}}[\ell(f(x)+h(x),y)-\ell(f(x),y)-\langle\nabla_{z}\ell(f(x),y),h(x)\rangle]d\rho(x,y)|.

Using that
|\ell(z+z^{\prime},y)-\ell(z,y)-\nabla_{z}\ell(z,y)\cdot z^{\prime}|\leqslant L|z^{\prime}|^{2}/2
, we obtain
\Delta(h)\leqslant(L/2)\|h\|^{2}_{{\mathcal{F}}}
. A similar computation also shows that

L

Finally, we claim that
|\nabla_{z}\ell(z,y)|^{2}\leqslant 2\ell(z,y)
. This is immediate for the square loss. For the cross-entropy loss, since
\rho_{y}
is concentrated on
\mathcal{B}_{k}
with
k={d_{\mathrm{out}}}
, it is enough to show that property when
y\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
, say
y=e_{q}
. We use
|a-1|^{2}\leqslant-\log(a)
if
a\in(0,1)
to obtain

\displaystyle|\sigma(z)-y|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigg|\frac{e^{z_{q}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{z_{j}}}-1\Bigg|^{2}+\sum\limits_{i\neq q}\Bigg|\frac{e^{z_{i}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{z_{j}}}\Bigg|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Bigg|\frac{e^{z_{q}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{z_{j}}}-1\Bigg|^{2}+\Bigg|\sum\limits_{i\neq q}\frac{e^{z_{i}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{z_{j}}}\Bigg|^{2}

\displaystyle|dR(f).h|

\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{out}}}}}|\nabla_{z}\ell(f(x),y)||h(x)|d\rho(x,y)

\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle\leqslant\|h\|_{{\mathcal{F}}}\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{out}}}}}|\nabla_{z}\ell(f(x),y)|^{2}d\rho(x,y)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|h\|_{{\mathcal{F}}}\sqrt{2R(f)},

so that

is bounded on sublevel sets of

.
∎

Lemma 6
.

Assume that
\rho_{x}
has a bounded continuous density with respect to the Lebesgue measure. If
\sigma
is the sigmoid function then when
r\to+\infty
the mapping
\phi_{r}:\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}\mapsto\phi(r\theta)\in\cal F
converges uniformly on
\mathbb{S}^{d_{\theta}-1}
towards
\phi_{\infty}:\theta\mapsto[x\mapsto\mathbf{1}_{\{\theta\cdot x\geqslant 0\}}]
.

Proof.

We fix
\eta,R>0
and split
\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}}
in three sets
E_{\eta}^{i}
(
i=1,2,3
) defined by

E^{1}_{\eta}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}}\,\rvert\,\langle\theta,x\rangle\geqslant\eta\},~E^{2}_{\eta}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}}\,\rvert\,\langle\theta,x\rangle\leqslant-\eta\}~\mathrm{and}~E^{3}_{\eta}=\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}}\setminus(E^{1}_{\eta}\cup E^{2}_{\eta}).

If
x\in E^{1}_{\eta}
then
|\sigma(r\theta\cdot x)-\mathbf{1}_{\{\theta\cdot x\geqslant 0\}}|=|1/(1+e^{-r\theta\cdot x})-1|\leqslant e^{-r\eta}
. If
x\in E^{2}_{\eta}
we also have

|\sigma(r\theta\cdot x)-\mathbf{1}_{\{\theta\cdot x\geqslant 0\}}|=1/(1+e^{-r\theta\cdot x})\leqslant e^{-r\eta}.

As a consequence, we have

\displaystyle\|\phi_{r}(\theta)-\phi_{\infty}(\theta)\|_{{\mathcal{F}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}}}|\sigma(r\theta\cdot x)-\mathbf{1}_{\{\theta\cdot x\geqslant 0\}}|^{2}d\rho_{x}(x)

\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle\leqslant e^{-2r\eta}+\int_{E^{3}_{\eta}}|\sigma(r\theta\cdot x)-\mathbf{1}_{\{\theta\cdot x\geqslant 0\}}|^{2}d\rho_{x}(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{-2r\eta}+4\rho_{x}(E^{3}_{\eta})

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{-2r\eta}+4\rho_{x}(E^{3}_{\eta}\cap B_{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}}(0,R))+4\rho_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}}\setminus B_{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}}(0,R)).

Now, we notice that since
|\theta|=1
, up to a rotation the set
E^{3}_{\eta}\cap B_{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}}(0,R)
is included in the cylinder
(-\eta,\eta)\times B_{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}-1}(0,R)
. As a consequence, we have

\rho_{x}(E^{3}_{\eta}\cap B_{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}}(0,R))\leqslant 2\eta\|\rho_{x}\|_{\infty}\mathcal{L}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}-1}(B_{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}-1}(0,R)).

Taking for instance
\eta=1/\sqrt{r}
, we obtain

\underset{r\to+\infty}{\mathrm{lim~sup}}\underset{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{\theta}-1}}{\mathrm{sup}}\|\phi_{r}(\theta)-\phi_{\infty}(\theta)\|_{{\mathcal{F}}}\leqslant 4\rho_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}}\setminus B_{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}}(0,R)).

As this holds for every
R>0
and the right-hand side converges to
0
as
R\to+\infty
, we obtain the result.
∎

In
[
10
, Appendix D.3]
, the result below is stated under a finite moment assumption on
\rho_{x}
. However, the integrability of
x\mapsto f(x)\rho_{x}(x)x
with respect to the surface measure on the hyperplane
\{\langle\theta,x\rangle\}
cannot be guaranteed without additional assumptions on
\rho_{x}
. For this reason, we introduce an additional polynomial decay assumption under which the result is indeed true.

Lemma 7
.

Assume
\rho_{x}
has a finite moment of order
1
, has a bounded continuous density with respect to the Lebesgue measure and
|\rho_{x}(x)|\leqslant C(1+|x|)^{-p}
for some
C>0
and
p>{d_{\mathrm{in}}}
. Then for every continuous and bounded function
f:\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}}\to\mathbb{R}
, the mapping
g_{f}:\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}}}f(x)\sigma(\langle\theta,x\rangle)d\rho_{x}(x)
is of class
C^{1}
and
\theta\mapsto r\nabla g_{f}(r\theta)
converges uniformly on
\mathbb{S}^{d_{\theta}-1}
towards
\theta\mapsto\int_{\{\langle\theta,x\rangle=0\}}f(x)\rho_{x}(x)xd\mathcal{H}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}-1}(x)
.

Proof.

The gradient of the integrand with respect to
\theta
is
f(x)\sigma^{\prime}(\langle\theta,z\rangle)\rho_{x}(x)x
, whose norm is bounded by
\|f\|_{\infty}\|\sigma^{\prime}\|_{\infty}|x|\rho_{x}(x)
. Since
\rho_{x}
has a finite moment of order
1
, this last function is integrable, which shows that
g_{f}
is of class
C^{1}
and that for every
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}
it holds

\nabla g_{f}(\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}}}f(x)\sigma^{\prime}(\langle\theta,x\rangle)x\rho_{x}(x)dx.

For every
r\geqslant 0
and
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{\theta}-1}
, the co-area formula
[
17
, Theorem 3.11]
yields that the restriction of
x\mapsto f(x)\rho_{x}(x)x
to
\{\langle\theta,x\rangle=s\}
is
\mathcal{H}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}-1}
-integrable for almost every
s\in\mathbb{R}
and

\displaystyle r\nabla g_{f}(r\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}r\sigma^{\prime}(rs)\bigg[\int_{\{\langle\theta,x\rangle=s\}}f(x)\rho_{x}(x)xd\mathcal{H}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}-1}(x)\bigg]ds.

For every
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{\theta}-1}
, since
p>{d_{\mathrm{in}}}
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\{\langle\theta,x\rangle=s\}}|f(x)||\rho_{x}(x)||x|d\mathcal{H}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}-1}(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant\|f\|_{\infty}\int_{\{\langle\theta,x\rangle=s\}}|\rho_{x}(x)||x|d\mathcal{H}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}-1}(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|f\|_{\infty}\int_{\{\langle\theta,x\rangle=s\}}\frac{1}{(1+|x|)^{p-1}}d\mathcal{H}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}-1}(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|f\|_{\infty}\int_{\{y_{1}=0\}}\frac{1}{(1+|y+se_{1}|)^{p-1}}d\mathcal{H}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}-1}(y)

\displaystyle\leqslant C\|f\|_{\infty}\int_{\{y_{1}=0\}}\frac{1}{(1+|y|)^{p-1}}d\mathcal{H}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}-1}(y)

\displaystyle<+\infty,

where we have used the change of variable
y=R_{\theta}^{-1}x-se_{1}
with
R_{\theta}
a rotation with
R_{\theta}e_{1}=\theta
. Applying the dominated convergence theorem, we also obtain that
(\theta,s)\mapsto\int_{\{\langle\theta,x\rangle=s\}}f(x)\rho_{x}(x)xd\mathcal{H}^{{d_{\mathrm{in}}}-1}(x)
is continuous, and hence uniformly continuous on
\mathbb{S}^{d_{\theta}-1}\times[-1,1]
. Since

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}r\sigma^{\prime}(rs)ds=1~~\mathrm{and}~~\int_{-1/\sqrt{r}}^{1/\sqrt{r}}r\sigma^{\prime}(rs)ds\underset{r\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}1,

the uniform boundedness and continuity obtained above allow to conclude.
∎

A.2
Attention layers

In all this subsection, we denote by
\sigma:z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mapsto(e^{z_{i}}/(\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{z_{j}}))_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

Proof of

.

As

Now, we notice that

\displaystyle[d\psi(A).B](X)

\displaystyle=X^{T}(d\sigma(XAx_{n})\cdot(XBx_{n}))

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}(XAx_{n})\bigg(\langle Bx_{n},x_{i}\rangle-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}(XAx_{n})\langle Bx_{n},x_{j}\rangle\bigg)x_{i}.

We have

\displaystyle\Delta(B)^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\|\psi(A+B)-\psi(A)-d\psi(A)\cdot B\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}}|X^{T}(\sigma(X(A+B)x_{n})-\sigma(XAx_{n})-d\sigma(XAx_{n})\cdot(XBx_{n}))|^{2}d\rho_{X}(X)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}}\bigg[\underset{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}{\mathrm{max}}|x_{i}|^{2}\bigg]\Bigg[\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|\sigma_{i}(X(A+B)x_{n})-\sigma_{i}(XAx_{n})-d\sigma_{i}(XAx_{n})\cdot(XBx_{n})|\Bigg]^{2}d\rho_{X}(X).

Now, using that
\sigma_{i}(z+h)-\sigma_{i}(z)-d\sigma_{i}(z)\cdot h=\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)d^{2}\sigma_{i}(z+th)\cdot(h,h)dt

\displaystyle\Delta(B)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}}\bigg[\underset{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}{\mathrm{max}}|x_{i}|^{2}\bigg]\bigg[\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)|d^{2}\sigma(X(A+tB)x_{n})\cdot(XBx_{n},XBx_{n})|_{1}dt\bigg]^{2}d\rho_{X}(X)

\displaystyle\leqslant 9\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}}\bigg[\underset{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}{\mathrm{max}}|x_{i}|^{2}\bigg]|XBx_{n}|_{\infty}^{4}d\rho_{X}(X)\leqslant 9\|B\|^{4}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\underset{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}{\mathrm{max}}|x_{i}|^{10}d\rho_{X}(X).

This shows that
\phi
is differentiable with differential

is bounded and Lipschitz.
∎

Lemma 8
.

For every
z,h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it holds
|d\sigma(z)\cdot h|_{1}\leqslant 2|h|_{\infty}
and
|d^{2}\sigma(z)\cdot(h,h)|_{1}\leqslant 6|h|_{\infty}^{2}
.

Proof.

We have
d\sigma(z)\cdot h=\sigma(z)\odot h-\langle\sigma(z),h\rangle\sigma(z)
. Using the fact that the components of
\sigma(z)
are positive and sum to one, we obtain that

\displaystyle|d\sigma(z)\cdot h|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}(z)|h_{i}-\langle\sigma(z),h\rangle|\leqslant\underset{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}{\mathrm{max}}~|h_{i}-\langle\sigma(z),h\rangle|\leqslant 2|h|_{\infty}.

Now, for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, we have that

d^{2}\sigma_{i}(z)\cdot(h,h)=\sigma_{i}(z)\big[(h_{i}-\langle h,\sigma(z)\rangle)^{2}-\langle\sigma(z),h\odot h\rangle+\langle\sigma(z),h\rangle^{2}\big].

Using again that the components of
\sigma(z)
are positive and sum to one, we obtain that

\displaystyle|d^{2}\sigma(z)\cdot(h,h)|_{1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\underset{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}{\mathrm{max}}~|h_{i}-\langle\sigma(z),h\rangle|^{2}+|\langle\sigma(z),h\odot h\rangle|+\langle\sigma(z),h\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\underset{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}{\mathrm{max}}~\langle\sigma(z),h-h_{i}\mathbf{1}\rangle^{2}+|\langle\sigma(z),h\odot h\rangle|+\langle\sigma(z),h\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\underset{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n}{\mathrm{max}}~|h_{i}-h_{j}|^{2}+2|h|_{\infty}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 6|h|_{\infty}^{2}.

∎

We now turn to the proof of
4

has finite second order moments and the rest of the integrand is bounded. Then, given

for some

Proof of

.

In all the following, we set

. We notice that

\displaystyle|\psi_{r}(A)(X)-\psi_{\infty}(A)(X)|

\displaystyle=\Bigg|\sum\limits_{i\notin\Sigma(z)}\sigma_{r,i}(z)\Bigg(x_{i}-\frac{1}{\#\Sigma(z)}\sum\limits_{j\in\Sigma(z)}x_{j}\Bigg)\Bigg|

B.1
Preliminaries

If
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, we denote by
m_{2}(\mu)=\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2}d\mu(u)
its second order moment. We denote by
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega)
the set of probability measures on
\Omega
that have finite second order moments. We recall that
(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega),W_{2})
is a complete metric space.

Sub-Gaussian norm.

We define the sub-Gaussian norm of a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
by

\textstyle\|\mu\|_{\psi_{2}}=\mathrm{inf}\{c>0\,\rvert\,\int_{\Omega}e^{|u|^{2}/c^{2}}d\mu(u)\leqslant 2\}

and say that it is sub-Gaussian if
\|\mu\|_{\psi_{2}}<+\infty
. We stress that, when
d\geqslant 2
, this definition differs from the usual definition of sub-Gaussianity for random vectors, which can be found for instance in
[
34
, Section 3.4]
. Namely, if
X
is a random vector with law
\mu
, then with our definition

\|\mu\|_{\psi_{2}}=\inf\{c>0\,\rvert\,\mathbb{E}[|X|^{2}/c^{2}]\leqslant 2\},

whereas the sub-Gaussian norm of
X
is usually defined as
\sup_{|v|\leqslant 1}\inf\{c>0\,\rvert\,\mathbb{E}[(v^{T}X)^{2}/c^{2}]\leqslant 2\}
. Our choice is motivated by the following lemma, which shows that with our definition, the pushforward operation by a map with linear growth preserves sub-Gaussianity.

Lemma 9
.

If
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
is sub-Gaussian and
T:\Omega\to\Omega
satisfies
|T(u)|\leqslant C(1+|u|)
for some
C>0
, we have

\|T_{\#}\mu\|_{\psi_{2}}\leqslant 2C\max\Big(\|\mu\|_{\psi_{2}},1/\sqrt{\ln(2)}\Big).

(9)

Proof.

Let
X
be a random vector with law
\mu
. As a result,
|X|
is sub-Gaussian with sub-Gaussian norm
\|\mu\|_{\psi_{2}}
. By
[
34
, Prop. 2.6.1]
, for every
t>0
, we have that
{\mathbb{P}(|X|\geqslant t)\leqslant 2e^{-t^{2}/\|\mu\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}}}
. If
t\geqslant 2C
, then
|T(x)|\geqslant t
implies
|x|\geqslant 1
, which yields
{|T(x)|\leqslant 2C|x|}
. As a result, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|T(X)|\geqslant t)\leqslant\mathbb{P}(2C|X|\geqslant t)\leqslant 2e^{-t^{2}/(2C\|\mu\|_{\psi_{2}})^{2}}.

If
t\leqslant 2C
, we use the trivial bound
\mathbb{P}(|T(X)|\geqslant t)\leqslant 1
. Taking
K=2C\max(\|\mu\|_{\psi_{2}},1/\sqrt{\ln(2)})
, we see that
\mathbb{P}(|T(X)|\geqslant t)\leqslant e^{-t^{2}/K^{2}}
for every
t>0
. Applying the same proposition as above, we obtain the result.
∎

Continuity of the predictor for

The following lemma shows that the measure-to-predictor mapping

Lemma 10

Under

Considering

\displaystyle\textstyle\|\int_{\Omega}\Phi d\mu_{1}-\int_{\Omega}\Phi d\mu_{2}\|

\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle=\|\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}[\Phi(u_{1})-\Phi(u_{2})]d\Pi(u_{1},u_{2})\|

B.2
Core of the proof

In all the following, we work under
1
. We set an initial condition
\bar{\mu}_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega)
and

such that

Truncation of the objective.

We claim that there exists a twice differentiable function
\xi
such that
\xi(x)=x
if

,
g
is (strictly) increasing on
[\alpha,2\alpha]
,
\xi(x)=2\alpha
if

{\|\xi^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 3/2}
and
{\|\xi^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 4/\alpha}
. One can check that a possible choice for

is

\xi(x)=x+\frac{(x-\alpha)(2\alpha-x)(1-\cos(\pi(x-\alpha)/\alpha))}{2\alpha}.

We define

and

. We notice that

on
{\{F\leqslant\alpha\}=\{\tilde{F}\leqslant\alpha\}}

Construction of the flow map.

Let

There exists a unique solution to

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{r}(t)=-\nabla\tilde{F}^{\prime}(\mu_{t})(r(t)),\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant T,\\
&r(0)=u.\end{aligned}\right.

Indeed,

Fixed point argument on

We set an initial condition

\displaystyle\to C([0,T];\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega))

\displaystyle\mapsto(X_{t}[\mu]_{\#}\bar{\mu}_{0})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}.

Construction of a global solution.

Now, we can iterate the previous argument by applying the result again with the updated initial condition

, the same reasoning carries out if we take
K
to be the intersection of a ball with this sublevel set.
we would like to obtain an expansion of the two quantities involved in (
6
) when
\eta\to\eta_{\max}
.

D.1.1
Right-hand side

Let us prove that

\inf_{\theta\in K}\frac{|\langle g(\theta),v\rangle|}{|g(\theta)|}=1-c_{1}^{2}\frac{\eta_{\max}-\eta}{\eta_{\max}}+o(\eta_{\max}-\eta),

(24)

where

c_{1}=\underset{\langle Hx,x\rangle=1}{\sup}|Jx|~~\mathrm{with}~~J=J_{g}(\theta_{*})~~\mathrm{and}~~H=-H_{g}(\theta_{*})[g(\theta_{*})].

Using Taylor’s theorem, we obtain for every
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}

\langle g(\theta),v\rangle-(-\eta_{\max})-\frac{1}{2}\langle H_{g}(\theta_{*})[v](\theta-\theta_{*}),\theta-\theta_{*}\rangle=R_{1}(\theta-\theta_{*})~~\mathrm{with}~~\lim_{\theta\to\theta_{*}}\frac{1}{|\theta-\theta_{*}|^{2}}R_{1}(\theta-\theta_{*})=0.

(25)

This yields

\frac{1}{2}\langle H_{g}(\theta_{*})[v](\theta-\theta_{*}),\theta-\theta_{*}\rangle\leqslant(\eta_{\max}-\eta)+|R_{1}(\theta-\theta_{*})|.

As a result,
\theta^{\prime}:=\theta_{*}+(\theta-\theta_{*})/\sqrt{1+|R_{1}(\theta-\theta_{*})|/(\eta_{\max}-\eta)}
is such that

\frac{1}{2}\langle H_{g}(\theta_{*})[v](\theta^{\prime}-\theta_{*}),\theta^{\prime}-\theta_{*}\rangle\leqslant\eta_{\max}-\eta

and

|\theta^{\prime}-\theta|=|\theta-\theta_{*}|\bigg|1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|R_{1}(\theta-\theta_{*})|/(\eta_{\max}-\eta)}}\bigg|\leqslant c(\eta_{\max}-\eta)^{1/2}\frac{|R_{1}(\theta-\theta_{*})|}{2(\eta_{\max}-\eta)}

provided
\theta\in K
. Since

\frac{|\langle g(\theta),v\rangle|}{|g(\theta)|}=1-\frac{1}{2\eta_{\max}^{2}}|J_{g}(\theta_{*})(\theta-\theta_{*})|^{2}+R_{2}(\theta-\theta_{*})~~\mathrm{with}~~\lim_{\theta\to\theta_{*}}\frac{1}{|\theta-\theta_{*}|^{2}}R_{2}(\theta-\theta_{*})=0,

we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{|\langle g(\theta),v\rangle|}{|g(\theta)|}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{2\eta_{\max}^{2}}|J_{g}(\theta_{*})(\theta-\theta_{*})|^{2}+R_{2}(\theta-\theta_{*})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{2\eta_{\max}^{2}}|J_{g}(\theta_{*})(\theta^{\prime}-\theta_{*})|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\eta_{\max}^{2}}[|J_{g}(\theta_{*})(\theta-\theta_{*})|^{2}-|J_{g}(\theta_{*})(\theta^{\prime}-\theta_{*})|^{2}]+R_{2}(\theta-\theta_{*})

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{2\eta_{\max}^{2}}|J_{g}(\theta_{*})(\theta^{\prime}-\theta_{*})|^{2}-\frac{\|J_{g}\|_{\infty}^{2}}{2\eta_{\max}^{2}}|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|\big[|\theta-\theta_{*}|+|\theta^{\prime}-\theta_{*}|\big]-|R_{2}(\theta-\theta_{*})|.

for every
\theta\in K
. Combining the above yields

\inf_{\theta\in K}\frac{|\langle g(\theta),v\rangle|}{|g(\theta)|}\geqslant\inf\bigg\{1-\frac{1}{2\eta_{\max}^{2}}|J_{g}(\theta_{*})(\theta-\theta_{*})|^{2}\,\bigg\rvert\,\frac{1}{2}\langle H_{g}(\theta_{*})[v](\theta-\theta_{*}),\theta-\theta_{*}\rangle\leqslant\eta_{\max}-\eta\bigg\}+o(\eta_{\max}-\eta).

To obtain the reverse inequality, one can argue in the same way by observing that, if

\frac{1}{2}\langle H_{g}(\theta_{*})[v](\theta-\theta_{*}),\theta-\theta_{*}\rangle\leqslant\eta_{\max}-\eta,

then
|\theta-\theta_{*}|\leqslant\sqrt{2\eta_{\max}(\eta_{\max}-\eta)/\lambda_{\min}(-H_{g}(\theta_{*})[g(\theta_{*})])}
. Now, either
\langle g(\theta),v\rangle\leqslant-\eta
and we are done, or
\langle g(\theta),v\rangle>-\eta
and by the intermediate value theorem there exists
\rho(\theta)\in[0,1]
such that

\langle g(\theta_{*}+\rho(\theta)(\theta-\theta_{*})),v\rangle=-\eta.

We obtain

\displaystyle-\eta=\langle g(\theta_{*}+\rho(\theta)(\theta-\theta_{*})),v\rangle

\displaystyle=-\eta_{\max}+\frac{1}{2}\rho(\theta)^{2}\langle H_{g}(\theta_{*})[v](\theta-\theta_{*}),\theta-\theta_{*}\rangle+R_{1}(\rho(\theta)(\theta-\theta_{*})),

which yields

\rho(\theta)\geqslant\sqrt{1-\frac{|R_{1}(\rho(\theta)(\theta-\theta_{*}))|}{\eta_{\max}-\eta}}~~\mathrm{and}~~|\theta_{*}+\rho(\theta)(\theta-\theta_{*})-\theta|\leqslant|\theta-\theta_{*}|\left[1-\sqrt{1-\frac{|R_{1}(\rho(\theta)(\theta-\theta_{*}))|}{\eta_{\max}-\eta}}\right].

Since
\rho(\theta)\leqslant 1
, we obtain

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta_{\max}-\eta}}\sup\big\{|\theta_{*}+\rho(\theta)(\theta-\theta_{*})-\theta|\,\big\rvert\,(1/2)\langle H_{g}(\theta_{*})[v](\theta-\theta_{*}),\theta-\theta_{*}\rangle\leqslant\eta_{\max}-\eta\big\}\underset{\eta\to\eta_{\max}}{\longrightarrow}0.

We can then conclude as above to finally obtain

\displaystyle\inf_{\theta\in K}\frac{|\langle g(\theta),v\rangle|}{|g(\theta)|}

\displaystyle=\inf\bigg\{1-\frac{1}{2\eta_{\max}^{2}}|J_{g}(\theta_{*})(\theta-\theta_{*})|^{2}\,\bigg\rvert\,\frac{1}{2}\langle H_{g}(\theta_{*})[v](\theta-\theta_{*}),\theta-\theta_{*}\rangle\leqslant\eta_{\max}-\eta\bigg\}+o(\eta_{\max}-\eta)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{2\eta_{\max}^{2}}\sup\bigg\{|J_{g}(\theta_{*})(\theta-\theta_{*})|^{2}\,\bigg\rvert\,\frac{1}{2}\langle H_{g}(\theta_{*})[v](\theta-\theta_{*}),\theta-\theta_{*}\rangle\leqslant\eta_{\max}-\eta\bigg\}+o(\eta_{\max}-\eta)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2\eta_{\max}(\eta_{\max}-\eta)}{2\eta_{\max}^{2}}\sup\big\{|J_{g}(\theta_{*})(\theta-\theta_{*})|^{2}\,\big\rvert\,\langle-H_{g}(\theta_{*})[g(\theta_{*})](\theta-\theta_{*}),\theta-\theta_{*}\rangle\leqslant 1\big\}+o(\eta_{\max}-\eta),

which yields (
24
).

D.1.2
Left-hand side

Let us prove that

\sup_{\theta\in\partial K}\frac{|\mathrm{proj}_{\{v\}^{\perp}}(J_{g}(\theta)J_{g}(\theta)^{T}v)|}{|J_{g}(\theta)J_{g}(\theta)^{T}v|}=1-c_{2}^{2}\frac{\eta_{\max}-\eta}{\eta_{\max}}+o(\eta_{\max}-\eta)

where

c_{2}=\inf_{\langle Hx,x\rangle=1}\frac{|Hx|^{2}}{|JHx|}~~\mathrm{with}~~J=J_{g}(\theta_{*})~~\mathrm{and}~~H=-H_{g}(\theta_{*})[g(\theta_{*})].

Reasoning as above and using (
25
) and

\frac{|\langle J_{g}(\theta)J_{g}(\theta)^{T}v,v\rangle|}{|\mathrm{proj}_{\{v\}^{\perp}}(J_{g}(\theta)J_{g}(\theta)^{T}v)|}=\frac{|H_{g}(\theta_{*})[v](\theta-\theta_{*})|^{2}}{|J_{g}(\theta_{*})H_{g}(\theta_{*})[v](\theta-\theta_{*})|}+R_{3}(\theta-\theta_{*})~~\mathrm{with}~~\lim_{\theta\to\theta_{*}}\frac{1}{|\theta-\theta_{*}|}R_{3}(\theta-\theta_{*})=0,

we obtain

\sup_{\theta\in\partial K}\frac{|\mathrm{proj}_{\{v\}^{\perp}}(J_{g}(\theta)J_{g}(\theta)^{T}v)|}{|J_{g}(\theta)J_{g}(\theta)^{T}v|}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(\mathcal{I}+o((\eta_{\max}-\eta)^{1/2}))^{2}}}

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle=\inf\bigg\{\frac{|H_{g}(\theta_{*})[v](\theta-\theta_{*})|^{2}}{|J_{g}(\theta_{*})H_{g}(\theta_{*})[v](\theta-\theta_{*})|}\,\bigg\rvert\,\frac{1}{2}\langle H_{g}(\theta_{*})[v](\theta-\theta_{*}),\theta-\theta_{*}\rangle=\eta_{\max}-\eta\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2(\eta_{\max}-\eta)}{\eta_{\max}}}\inf\bigg\{\frac{|H_{g}(\theta_{*})[g(\theta_{*})](\theta-\theta_{*})|^{2}}{|J_{g}(\theta_{*})H_{g}(\theta_{*})[g(\theta_{*})](\theta-\theta_{*})|}\,\bigg\rvert\,-\langle H_{g}(\theta_{*})[g(\theta_{*})](\theta-\theta_{*}),\theta-\theta_{*}\rangle=1\bigg\},

which finally yields the result.
