Yearly attained adherence to Mediterranean diet, estimated trajectories of lipid profile from 60 to 80 years, and onset of clinical dyslipidemias in older adults at high cardiovascular risk

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate the association between yearly attained adherence to a Mediterranean diet (MedDiet) and trajectories of triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and the low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C)/HDL-C ratio, as well as the risk of clinically relevant dyslipidemias, in adults aged 60–80 at high cardiovascular risk.MethodsIn 4,954 participants (68.1 ± 5.5 years, 62% women, median follow-up: 4 years) from the Prevención con Dieta Mediterránea trial, trajectories of triglycerides, HDL-C, and LDL-C/HDL-C were compared between participants with high or low cumulative adherence to the MedDiet (≥ 10 vs. <10 points) over time. The association between high MedDiet adherence and the risk of five dyslipidemias (triglycerides ≥ 150 or ≥ 200 mg/dL; low HDL-C; LDL-C ≥ 130 or ≥ 160 mg/dL) was examined using multivariable Cox regressions.ResultsCompared with low adherence to the MedDiet, high adherence was associated with lower triglycerides (differences in the range: -6 to -12 mg/dL), particularly among individuals using triglyceride-lowering medications (-17 to -41 mg/dL). High adherence was also associated with higher HDL-C (+ 0.8 to + 2.3 mg/dL) and lower LDL-C/HDL-C ratio. High adherence to the MedDiet was associated with a lower risk of all dyslipidemias (range: -21% to -45%). One-point increases in adherence were associated with a 48–91% lower dyslipidemia risk among individuals with low adherence (< 8 points).ConclusionIn adults aged 60–80 at high cardiovascular risk, greater long-term adherence to a MedDiet was associated with a better lipid profile and a lower risk of clinically relevant dyslipidemias.Trial registration numberISRCTN35739639 (registration date: Oct 5, 2005).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-026-02999-y.

Introduction

Observational and interventional studies, such as the PREvención con DIeta MEDiterránea (PREDIMED) trial, have shown that following a Mediterranean diet (MedDiet) decreases the risk of developing major cardiovascular events [1, 2]. The MedDiet improves glucose metabolism, blood pressure, adiposity, kidney function, oxidative stress, low-grade inflammation, and biological aging, which in turn contribute to cardioprotection [3, 4]. Regarding the lipid profile, as with other plant-based diets [5, 6], clear short-term benefits for triglycerides and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) have been observed, as well as a decrease in the ratio between low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and HDL-C [7] (benefits for LDL-C are less clear [4, 8, 9], and excessively lowering LDL-C levels in older adults may be dangerous due to increases in all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [10]). However, current evidence has some limitations. First, the role of the MedDiet in the lipid profile over long follow-up periods, particularly in older adults, is not well characterized. Whether the benefits of MedDiet emerge early or later in life remains uncertain, particularly because lipid parameters evolve with age; in older adults, cholesterol levels typically decline over time, whereas triglyceride levels tend to follow a more variable, though often decreasing, trajectory [11, 12]. In addition, it is unknown whether the benefits of a MedDiet are large enough to prevent triglyceride, HDL-C or LDL-C levels from exceeding the thresholds that may prompt clinical decisions such as initiating lipid-lowering medication (clinical dyslipidemias). Finally, better insights into MedDiet benefits can be achieved by investigating dietary adherence over time, since dietary interventions do not show optimal compliance [13]. Adherence to the MedDiet can be measured using rapid tools such as the Mediterranean Diet Adherence Screener (MEDAS) [14]. Its usefulness in trials and the clinical setting lies in its ability to be administered multiple times, offer feedback to participants, and track changes in adherence over time, which provides a stronger link between MedDiet adherence and disease [15]. This approach, which is currently advised in the guidelines for the analysis of randomized trials [16], showed a robust association between high levels of long-term adherence to the MedDiet and a lower incidence of diabetes [17]. Thus, this study aimed to investigate the association of longitudinal adherence to a MedDiet with 20-year lipid profile trajectories and the risk of clinically relevant dyslipidemias in adults and older adults at high cardiovascular risk, in order to provide complementary longitudinal evidence to the short-term lipid-related benefits of MedDiet reported in randomized controlled trials.

Methods

Study population

The study population consisted of participants from the PREDIMED Study. It was a multicenter, randomized, controlled, intervention trial conducted in Spain between 2003 and 2010, aimed at assessing the long-term effects of the MedDiet on the primary prevention of major cardiovascular events in older adults at high cardiovascular risk [1]. Participants were assigned to one of three dietary interventions: a MedDiet enriched with extra-virgin olive oil, a MedDiet enriched with mixed nuts, or a control group who received advice to follow a low-fat diet. Eligible volunteers were either men aged 55–80 years or women aged 60–80 years, free of cardiovascular disease at enrolment but presenting type 2 diabetes or at least three of the following risk factors: tobacco use, hypertension, high LDL-C levels, low HDL-C concentrations, overweight/obesity, and family history of premature coronary heart disease. A total of 7,447 participants were included in the study and were followed yearly.

For this work, data from PREDIMED participants aged 60 and over was analyzed as a prospective cohort. In the trajectory analyses, participants with missing values at baseline in lipid profile measures or MedDiet adherence and those with only one lipid profile measurement were excluded (Fig. 1). In the analyses on the risk of clinically relevant dyslipidemias, participants with the corresponding dyslipidemia at baseline were additionally excluded. This work is reported according to the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology statement.

Adherence to mediterranean diet

Trained dietitians assessed adherence to the MedDiet in the face-to-face yearly study visits using the MEDAS, a validated tool for Spanish adults [14] that assigns 1 point to each of the following 14 items: (1) olive oil as the main cooking/seasoning fat; (2) ≥ 4 tablespoons/day of olive oil; (3) ≥ 3 servings/day of fruit; (4) ≥ 2 servings/day of vegetables; (5) ≥ 3 servings/week of mixed nuts (serving: 30 g); (6) ≥ 3 servings/week of legumes (serving: 150 g, boiled); (7) ≥ 3 servings/week of fish or seafood; (8) wine in moderation (1 glass/day, preferably with meals); (9) < 1 serving/day of red or processed meat; (10) preferring poultry and rabbit to red and processed meat; (11) < 1 serving/day of butter, margarine, or cream; (12) < 1 serving/day of carbonated or sugar-sweetened beverages; (13) < 2 servings/week of non-homemade sweets or pastries; and (14) ≥ 2 dishes/week based on a “sofrito” [14].

Outcomes

In the yearly study visits, triglycerides, total cholesterol, and HDL-C levels were determined in local laboratories using overnight fasting serum samples. Triglycerides, total cholesterol, and HDL-C were analyzed by standard procedures. All facilities satisfied external quality-control requirements and harmonized their measurements [18]. LDL-C was computed according to the Friedewald formula whenever triglycerides were < 300 mg/dL, and it was used to calculate the LDL-C/HDL-C ratio (informative of cardiovascular risk [19, 20]). With these data, an age-indexed dataset was constructed, in which lipid measurements from different participants were aligned by age (60–80 years) for analyses in which chronological age was the time scale. Each participant contributed data only for the ages observed during their follow-up, and these overlapping age segments were combined to build a longitudinal dataset spanning ages 60–80. This strategy does not imply continuous observation of any single participant across the entire age range. The number of measures for triglycerides, HDL-C, and LDL-C/HDL-C available for each age in the dataset is presented in Online Resource 1.

The onset of five clinically relevant dyslipidemia-related outcomes at any time point during follow-up were defined in the study participants: (1) triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL; (2) triglycerides ≥ 200 mg/dL; (3) low HDL-C (< 40 mg/dL in men, < 50 mg/dL in women); (4) LDL-C ≥ 130 mg/dL; and (5) LDL-C ≥ 160 mg/dL [21]. “Onset” was defined as the appearance of the lipid abnormality in participants without the condition at baseline that subsequently persisted until the last visit. For potentially uncertain cases (sequences with occasional reversions), onset was confirmed only if ≥ 75% of all follow-up measurements after the first abnormal value were abnormal [22].

Covariates

Trained clinical personnel collected self-reported baseline data on age (years), sex, recruitment site, educational level (primary, secondary, higher), diabetes (yes/no), hypertension (yes/no), smoking (current, former, or never smoker), body mass index (kg/m2), and use of cholesterol-lowering medications (statins, ezetimibe, and bile acid sequestrants; yes/no) or triglyceride-lowering medications (fibrates and pharmacological doses of omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids; yes/no). Energy intake (kcal/day) was estimated at baseline from a validated 137-item food frequency questionnaire, and leisure-time physical activity (metabolic equivalents of task-minute/day) from a validated Minnesota Leisure-Time Physical Activity Questionnaire [23]. Before the analyses, covariates with non-structural missingness (educational level [0.7%], energy intake [0.3%]) were imputed by random-forest imputation, using all available information from the other variables of the study.

Statistical analyses

Normally distributed continuous variables were described by means and standard deviations (SD), non-normally distributed continuous variables by medians and interquartile ranges, and categorical variables by proportions.

First, the average of all MEDAS scores obtained over follow-up (“cumulative average”) was calculated with a median number of 4 MEDAS scores per participant, including the baseline assessment, and then divided the participants into two groups: those with a cumulative average of < 10 points (“low adherence to a MedDiet”) and those with ≥ 10 points (“high adherence”). In both groups, the trajectories of lipid profile markers in the longitudinal aggregated database spanning ages 60–80 years were modeled using smoothed cubic spline mixed-effects regression models (K + 4 degrees of freedom). Models included an interaction term between age and the MedDiet adherence group to allow for different trajectories between groups [24]. Analyses were adjusted for PREDIMED intervention group, sex, educational level, diabetes, hypertension, smoking habit, body mass index, energy intake, leisure-time physical activity, two propensity scores representing the estimated probabilities of being allocated to the MedDiet groups in the PREDIMED trial, use of cholesterol-lowering medication, and baseline values of triglycerides, HDL-C, and total cholesterol. Analyses on triglycerides were additionally adjusted for triglyceride-lowering medication. Mean trajectories of lipid profile markers over age in the two adherence groups were graphically represented using predicted values and computed the average differences between groups at ages 60, 65, 70, 75 and 80 using linear regression models. As sensitivity analyses, whether differences in the trajectories were attributable to baseline values of each marker (triglycerides: levels < or ≥ 150 mg/dL; HDL-C: low or high; LDL-C/HDL-C: < or ≥ 3), baseline use of lipid-lowering medication, and PREDIMED intervention group was investigated. Models were fitted including an interaction term (“other factor × MedDiet adherence group”) and likelihood ratio tests between the models with and without it were applied. If the interaction was significant (Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold < 0.017 for the three pre-specified interactions), the trajectory analyses were presented in all relevant subgroups.

The association between MedDiet adherence and the risk of dyslipidemias was studied by two approaches. First, the cumulative incidence of each outcome between ages 60–80 for high versus low MedDiet adherence was represented using Kaplan-Meier curves. Confounding in Kaplan-Meier curves was minimized by inverse probability weighting (weights were estimated from a propensity score model that contained the same covariates as in the trajectory analyses), and weighted survival estimates with 95% confidence bands were plotted from age 60 to 80 years. Second, age-as-time-scale, multivariable Cox regression models were used to assess the relationship between the cumulative average of MEDAS score (updated up to the visit immediately preceding the event for cases) and the risk of dyslipidemias. Follow-up began at baseline age and ended at the age of the first event or the age at December 1, 2010, whichever occurred first [22]. Robust variance estimators accounted for intra-cluster correlations. Hazard ratios (HR) were calculated for (i) high versus low MedDiet adherence and (ii) each one-point increase in the MEDAS score. The models were adjusted for the same covariates as in the trajectory analyses. Additionally, in analyses assessing one-point increases in the MEDAS score, linearity was evaluated by comparing the linear term with a smoothed cubic spline (K + 4 degrees of freedom) using likelihood ratio tests. When non-linear relationships were detected, spline-based Cox models (reference: 5 MEDAS points) were used to characterize the association [22]. The adherence value at which the minimum risk of dyslipidemia was reached was identified. Segment-specific slopes were reported for ranges showing the strongest gradient. As sensitivity analyses, effect modification by baseline use of lipid-lowering medication and intervention group was also investigated (Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold < 0.025 for the two pre-specified interactions).

Analyses were conducted in R Software v. 4.3.1. Code for data management and analysis is available at https://github.com/alvarohernaez/MedDiet_adherence_lipids.

Results

Participants

Participants were predominantly older adults (average age 68, 62% women), followed for a median of 4 years. They had a high baseline use of lipid-lowering drugs and a high prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors. Participants with high cumulative adherence to a MedDiet were less likely to be female and more likely to have secondary or higher education. They had lower triglyceride levels, were less likely to use triglyceride-lowering medication, had slightly higher HDL-C levels, and were less likely to have other cardiovascular risk factors (type 2 diabetes, hypertension, obesity, and current smoking). They engaged in more physical activity and consumed more calories (Table 1).

Lipid profile trajectories over time

Lipid parameters changed over age in a manner broadly consistent with previous reports in older adults, who typically show declining cholesterol levels and, less consistently, declining triglyceride levels with aging. Accordingly, the estimates shown below represent between-group differences in these age-related trajectories. Participants with high adherence to a MedDiet, versus those with low adherence, had lower triglyceride levels between ages 60–80 (differences ranging from − 5.7 to -12.1 mg/dL; Fig. 2A and B). All lipid trajectories depended on dyslipidemia status and use of lipid-lowering medication at baseline (Online Resource 2). High MedDiet adherence was linked to lower levels of triglycerides in participants receiving triglyceride-lowering medication (significant differences ranging from − 16.6 to -41.4 mg/dL) (Fig. 2B; Online Resource 3 A-B). Participants who were not receiving triglyceride-lowering medication at baseline showed similar trajectories to those described for the overall population.

HDL-C and LDL-C/HDL-C declined with age. Nevertheless, participants with high adherence showed higher HDL-C (differences ranging from + 0.8 to + 2.3 mg/dL; Fig. 2C-D, Online Resource 3 C-D) and lower LDL-C/HDL-C, particularly between ages 60–75 (Fig. 2E-F, Online Resource 3E-F). As observed for triglycerides, no substantial differences were observed for HDL-C or LDL-C/HDL-C trajectories when analyses were restricted to participants not receiving lipid-lowering medication at baseline.

Although there were significant interactions with PREDIMED intervention group, associations between MedDiet adherence and the evolution of lipid profile with age were similar in both PREDIMED intervention arms (Online Resource 4).

Onset of clinically relevant dyslipidemias

Incidence of dyslipidemias was relatively frequent among study participants (triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL, incidence rate 13.5%, median follow-up time 3.5 years; triglycerides ≥ 200 mg/dL, incidence rate 3.5%, median follow-up time 3.9 years; low HDL-C, incidence rate 20.7%, median follow-up time 3.3 years; LDL-C ≥ 130 mg/dL, incidence rate 19.8%, median follow-up time 3.1 years; LDL-C ≥ 160 mg/dL, incidence rate 6.6%, median follow-up time 3.8 years). High adherence to a MedDiet was associated with a lower risk of onset of all five dyslipidemia-related outcomes studied (high vs. low adherence in multivariable Cox models, HRs ranging from 0.55 to 0.79; Fig. 3). Because all dose-response associations were non-linear, increments in the MEDAS score led to particularly strong decreases in the risk of dyslipidemia among individuals with low MedDiet adherence. In participants with 5–8 MEDAS points, 1-point increases in adherence were linked to a 48–91% lower risk of dyslipidemia (Fig. 4). Beyond this point, the associations between adherence and lower dyslipidemia risk were much less pronounced, and the lowest risks of dyslipidemia were observed around 11–12 MEDAS score points.

Discussion

In adults aged 60–80 at high cardiovascular risk, sustained high adherence to a MedDiet (≥ 10 MEDAS points) was associated with lower triglycerides, particularly among individuals treated with triglyceride-lowering medication, lower LDL-C/HDL-C, and higher HDL-C. These differences were accompanied by a lower age-dependent risk of triglyceride, HDL-C-, and LDL-C-related dyslipidemias. Increases in MedDiet adherence were associated with a lower risk of dyslipidemia particularly among participants with very low adherence.

The MedDiet consistently lowers triglyceride levels in short-term interventions, including PREDIMED [4, 8], and is linked to a lower risk of fibrate use [22]. These findings provide complementary longitudinal evidence over a 20-year horizon in adults and older adults (with triglyceride levels 6–12 mg/dL lower in untreated individuals and up to ~ 40 mg/dL lower in treated individuals). These differences are modest and within the ranges reported in dietary interventions [4], but such changes have been associated with a 2–6% lower risk of major adverse cardiovascular events in the PREDIMED population [25] and genetic and mechanistic evidence support a causal role of triglycerides in the prevention of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [26]. Mechanistically, unsaturated fats in olive oil, nuts, seeds, and fish activate peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-α, which upregulates lipoprotein lipase and carnitine palmitoyl-transferase in skeletal muscle, thereby accelerating triglyceride clearance [27, 28]. The MedDiet’s high fiber content is fermented by gut bacteria, producing short-chain fatty acids, which reach adipocytes, decrease futile lipolysis, lower circulating free fatty acids, and consequently limit hepatic de novo triglyceride synthesis [29, 30]. Finally, short-chain fatty acids and certain phenolic compounds (antioxidants in extra-virgin olive oil, fruits, vegetables, legumes, and nuts) activate adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase, which suppresses transcription factors that drive hepatic triglyceride production [31–33].

A few short-term studies report small LDL-C/HDL-C reductions after MedDiet interventions [7] and link higher adherence to lower statin initiation [22]. This study provides complementary longitudinal evidence across late adulthood that higher MedDiet adherence is associated with lower LDL-C/HDL-C values and a lower incidence of LDL-C-related dyslipidemias. These associations were observed between ages 60–75. This age-specific pattern is relevant because the oldest adults already exhibit age-related LDL-declines [10], and it supports a cautious interpretation of the findings in this group. The combination of low/moderate meat intake in the MedDiet, the cholesterol and bile acid sequestration due to dietary fiber, and the adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase-mediated inhibition of hepatic cholesterol synthesis support its protective role [2, 3, 31–34]. Regarding other cholesterol-related parameters, short-term interventions also report mild HDL-C increases [4, 8]. This study provides complementary longitudinal evidence across late adulthood that higher MedDiet adherence is associated with higher HDL-C levels and a lower incidence of low HDL-C levels. Increases in HDL-C as observed in this study (~ 1–2 mg/dL) are modest, aligned with what has been observed in previous dietary interventions [4], and linked to 2–3% lower coronary risk in classic epidemiological studies [35]. However, these associations should be interpreted cautiously because the causal role of increases in HDL-C levels in preventing cardiovascular disease is increasingly questioned by genetic and mechanistic studies [36]. Mechanistically, several MedDiet bioactive compounds (unsaturated fatty acids, short-chain fatty acids, phenolic antioxidants) stimulate the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-α, upregulate hepatic apolipoprotein A-I synthesis, and thereby raise HDL-C [27, 28, 31–33]. In addition, because HDL-C and triglyceride concentrations are inversely coupled [37], the triglyceride-lowering capacity of MedDiet may contribute to the observed HDL-C increase.

This work also underscores additional clinical implications for dyslipidemia. The non-linear dose-response curves show that raising MEDAS scores from very low (5–8 points) to moderate levels provides the largest benefit, with a plateau beyond 11–12 points. This pattern may help prioritize interventions for individuals with low adherence; however, reinforcement of healthy dietary behaviors remains essential across all levels of adherence [38]. Moreover, the MEDAS questionnaire is a practical clinical tool: its brevity and simplicity make it suitable for integration into primary care workflows, allowing routine monitoring, immediate feedback, and stepwise negotiation of diet changes in settings where time constraints often limit the use of more detailed dietary assessment tools. It has been validated in Spanish and Mediterranean populations and shown to be sensitive to dietary changes [14, 39], and its repeated administration allows monitoring adherence over time [1] and supports personalized dietary counseling in routine practice. The findings may have practical relevance for clinical settings, although they should be interpreted in the context of a structured randomized trial. Dietary counselling was delivered during repeated face-to-face visits by trained professionals, and adherence was assessed prospectively using the MEDAS questionnaire at each visit, which likely reduced recall and reporting biases compared with retrospective or self-administered dietary assessments [40]. In addition, repeated measurements of adherence better capture long-term dietary patterns, which are more relevant to chronic disease risk than single baseline assessments [41]. Nevertheless, the applicability of these findings to real-world conditions should be further evaluated.

Strengths and limitations

The main strengths of this study include its well-characterized sample of older adults at high cardiovascular risk, the repeated prospective assessment of Mediterranean diet adherence and lipid biomarkers, and the use of longitudinal age-indexed analyses to assess lipid trajectories and clinically relevant dyslipidemia onset across late adulthood. However, this study has several limitations. First, it represents a secondary observational analysis derived from a randomized trial and addresses research questions that extend beyond the original primary objective of PREDIMED, which was the prevention of major adverse cardiovascular outcomes. The analyses of cumulative adherence to the MedDiet, lipid trajectories, and dyslipidemia onset extend the primary analyses and suggest a plausible mechanism underlying the long-term preventive effects of the MedDiet on cardiovascular disease. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously within an observational framework. Second, as an observational analysis, this study cannot establish causality, and residual confounding may persist despite extensive adjustment. The conclusions should therefore be interpreted as complementary to existing randomized evidence, as they are consistent with previous intent-to-treat findings from randomized trials, which are theoretically free of residual confounding. This study adds longitudinal evidence suggesting that these favorable associations are maintained over longer time horizons and across aging when adherence is sustained. Third, the database spanning from 60 to 80 years was reconstructed from a discontinuously assembled panel of lipid measurements (each participant contributed data only during the windows in which they attended the trial visits), rather than by observing every individual for the entire period. To obtain unbiased estimates, the linear mixed-effects models and time-to-event analyses implicitly assume that missing lipid values were missing at random and that censoring/loss to follow-up is non-informative after covariate adjustment. Several procedures were implemented to minimize bias: (a) linear mixed-effects spline models use all available repeated lipid measures per participant without requiring every subject to have data at each age point; (b) only participants providing ≥ 2 measures of lipid profile parameters were involved in the analyses; (c) inverse probability weighting in Kaplan-Meier curves rebalanced the sample for potential predictors of visit attendance; (d) age-as-time-scale Cox models treat loss to follow-up as right censoring; and (e) sensitivity analyses produced results that paralleled the main analyses. Nevertheless, prospective studies with complete long-term follow-up and minimal attrition are still required to confirm these patterns. Fourth, trajectory analyses remain vulnerable to reverse causation, as participants with worse lipid profiles could receive recommendations to improve their dietary habits to correct this problem, which could have attenuated the observed protective associations between greater adherence to the MedDiet and a better lipid profile in the data. Fifth, the MEDAS is a short questionnaire that may be affected by measurement error. However, using cumulative averages and administering the tool face-to-face by trained dietitians likely mitigated this bias. Minor analytic variability may also persist despite laboratory harmonization. Sixth, analyses were adjusted for baseline use of lipid-lowering medication as a binary variable (cholesterol-lowering medication in all analyses and triglyceride-lowering medication in triglyceride-related analyses) as a proxy for the use of such medication throughout the whole study. However, detailed information on treatment initiation, discontinuation, dose changes, and adherence during follow-up was not available. Therefore, residual confounding due to time-varying lipid-lowering therapy cannot be excluded, and changes in medication use during follow-up may have partly contributed to the observed associations. Nevertheless, no substantial differences were observed for triglyceride, HDL-C, or LDL-C/HDL-C trajectories when analyses were restricted to participants not receiving lipid-lowering medication at baseline. Seventh, access to detailed psychological, social, or cognitive determinants of dietary adherence, such as motivation, self-efficacy, perceived barriers, or readiness to change, was not available. Future studies should incorporate these variables to better understand who responds well to dietary counselling and how adherence could be improved in those with persistent difficulties [13]. Finally, the results, derived from older adults at high cardiovascular risk in a Mediterranean country, might not be generalizable to other populations.

Conclusion

Long-term adherence to a MedDiet was associated with lower triglycerides and LDL-C/HDL-C, higher HDL-C levels, and a lower risk of related dyslipidemias in older adults at high cardiovascular risk. This study provides complementary longitudinal evidence suggesting that the lipid-related benefits of MedDiet reported in short- and mid-term interventions remain detectable over longer periods and across aging, particularly for hypertriglyceridemia. Dietary counselling based on the MEDAS may be a feasible tool to support cardiovascular prevention in ageing populations. The findings suggest that patients meeting fewer MedDiet recommendations may be a priority group for structured dietary support, where the largest incremental gains may be expected, while reinforcement of healthy dietary behaviors remains relevant across all levels of adherence. Future research should evaluate cost-effectiveness, implementation in non-Mediterranean settings, potential benefits in younger cohorts, and integration of MedDiet counseling, potentially combined with physical activity as part of broader healthy ageing strategies, with pharmacological approaches for dyslipidemia prevention and management.
