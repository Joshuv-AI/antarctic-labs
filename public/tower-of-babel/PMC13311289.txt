Mesalazine-induced nephrolitiasis and crystalluria
Authors: Rafel M. Prieto, Francesca Julià, Flor A. Ordoñez, Antonia Costa-Bauzà, Andrea Cabeza, Myriam Muñoz, Felix Grases
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mesalazine (5-aminosalicylic acid) is an anti-inflammatory drug used for autoimmune diseases. Premature release mesalazine in the small intestine or proximal colon can increase systemic absorption. A 13-year-old with ulcerative colitis treated with Pentasa® at 2 g mesalazine/day developed stones when this dose was doubled. Mesalazine has low water solubility; higher doses raise urinary supersaturation, promoting epitaxial growth of mesalazine crystals that form a stone. In children, lower body weight and reduced urine volume increase this risk. Dose adjustment and adequate hydration are essential, as low urine output raises mesalazine concentration and favors crystallization. Increased fluid intake is strongly recommended.

Introduction

Mesalazine (mesalamine, 5-aminosalicylic acid, 5-ASA) is an anti-inflammatory agent used in the treatment of autoimmune conditions. Thus, mesalazine and its prodrugs like sulfasalazine and olsalazine have been used for several decades for the treatment of inflammatory bowel diseases, mainly in ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease.1,2

Mesalazine has limited water solubility, so it easily reaches high supersaturations in urine, depending on the dose, leading to crystallization. Premature release of pH-dependent mesalazine in the small intestine or proximal colon may increase systemic absorption, resulting in elevated plasma and urinary levels. If renal crystallization occurs gradually, it will appear initially as crystalluria (very cloudy urine) until kidney stones were objectively detected. Thus, in the bibliography, some cases of kidney stones induced by mesalazine 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 or by analogous prodrugs compounds 10,11 have already been described. This case report documents a rare occurrence of mesalazine-induced crystalluria and nephrolithiasis in a pediatric patient, emphasizing the critical importance of dose adjustment and hydration management.

Case presentation

We present the case of a 13-year-old male patient, under outpatient follow-up in Pediatric Nephrology, for episodes of hematuria and lower back pain. At 10 years of age, presented with diarrhoea, rectal bleeding, and abdominal pain. He was diagnosed with diffuse colitis and treated with corticosteroids for several weeks. The clinical picture was consistent with ulcerative colitis, and it was decided to treat him accordingly with Pentasa® 2 g mesalazine sachets: 1 sachet per day (2 g mesalazine/day). Pentasa® sachets contain granules coated with a special polymer that allows controlled (slow) release of mesalazine throughout the intestine, independent of pH.

One month after starting treatment, the patient was referred to the pediatric emergency department for gross hematuria, proteinuria with dysuria, passing grits in the urine, and lower back pain, which appeared after exercise. Kidney ultrasound was initially normal. A few months later, he experienced another episode of visible blood in his urine after engaging in physical exercise. The abdominal ultrasound was repeated, and the renal Doppler showed findings consistent with nutcracker syndrome.

After three years of treatment, a mild outbreak of ulcerative colitis occurred, and it was decided to increase the dose of Pentasa® (2 g of mesalazine/day) to two sachets a day (4 g of mesalazine/day). Four months after starting the new dose of Pentasa®, the patient experienced repeated episodes of visible blood in urine and pain in both lower back areas, which were initially associated with a temporary increase in physical activity. Three months later, after experiencing lower back pain, the patient passed a small kidney stone.

Discussion

We present the case of a 13-year-old patient who had been diagnosed with ulcerative colitis and was being treated with mesalazine. Additionally, ultrasound findings indicated the presence of nutcracker syndrome, which likely complicated the identification of this issue.

Mesalazine's solubility in water is limited, with minimum values at pH between 5.0 and 6.0 (approximately 1.5 mg/mL). Above this pH, solubility increases to 4.0 mg/mL at pH 7.2.6 It should be noted that maintaining a urinary pH above 6.0 is not recommended, as it may facilitate bacterial growth and promote the crystallization of poorly soluble phosphate salts, such as brushite and hydroxyapatite, which can eventually lead to the formation of kidney stones made of these components.

It is important to note that a few months after starting treatment with Pentasa® (mesalazine) 2 g/day, a first episode of hematuria and dysuria accompanied by unidentified crystalluria was detected, precisely after playing sports (possible increase in water loss through sweat), and this type of episode was repeated before increasing the dose of Pentasa® at three years. The first kidney stone appeared shortly after the dose was doubled, reaching a level equivalent to the recommended adult dosage.

The concurrent presence of clinical and ultrasound findings consistent with nutcracker syndrome in the patient complicated the confirmation of the true source of hematuria episodes until kidney stones were objectively detected.

The presence of crystalluria at a mesalazine dose of 2 g/day indicates that the solubility threshold has been reached. Depending on crystal size, microlesions in the urinary epithelium may occur, resulting in hematuria. If, for any reason, such as high proteinuria due nutcracker syndrome, the mesalazine crystals become trapped in a renal calyx, they will grow epitaxially, leading to the formation of a stone.

Even in situations with significant water loss, such as during sports activity, urinary supersaturation is generally not sufficient or prolonged enough to produce a stone at a mesalazine dose of 2 g/day. When the dose is doubled, urinary supersaturation becomes high enough not just to cause isolated crystals (crystalluria), but to promote the organized, epitaxial growth of mesalazine crystals that eventually form a stone. If this stone obstructs the ureter, it can trigger renal colic.

Conclusions

In children, body weight is lower than in adults; therefore, dosing should be adjusted accordingly. It should also be considered that urine volume is lower in children than in adults. Consequently, the urinary concentration of a poorly soluble compound such as mesalazine will be higher due to the reduced urine output, increasing the risk of crystallization. For this reason, a significant increase in fluid intake is strongly recommended in these cases.
