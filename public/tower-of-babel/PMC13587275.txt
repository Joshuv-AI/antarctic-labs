Self-esteem modulates directed forgetting of emotional social feedback
Authors: Meilin Ye, Zengzhen Lin, Yanling Yi, Lei Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccording to self-motivation theories and the mnemic neglect mechanism, differences in self-esteem levels lead to memory bias for social feedback of different valences, and may enable self-esteem to moderate individuals’ directed forgetting of social feedback. However, this hypothesis has not been tested in previous studies. To address this, the present study employed two behavioral experiments to examine the moderating effect of self-esteem on directed forgetting of social feedback.MethodsIn Experiment 1, a directed forgetting paradigm involving valenced (positive or negative) social feedback was used to compare individuals with high versus low self-esteem. Based on Experiment 1, Experiment 2 added a mutual evaluation phase and incorporated intensity information into the valence dimension of social feedback, to further examine the effect of self-esteem on directed forgetting of social feedback.ResultsThe results of Experiment 1 showed that while self-esteem influenced selective memory of social feedback via a mnemic neglect mechanism, it did not significantly affect directed forgetting. The results of Experiment 2 demonstrated that self-esteem significantly moderated directed forgetting, with this effect being intensity-dependent rather than valence-dependent. Specifically, participants with high self-esteem exhibited directed forgetting for neutral and high-intensity feedback (both negative and positive), while showing a reversed pattern for low-intensity negative feedback. By comparison, low‑self‑esteem individuals exhibited no evidence of directed forgetting for emotional social feedback, pointing to new avenues for interventions aimed at social barriers in this population.ConclusionsSelf-esteem can moderate the directed forgetting of social feedback, yet this moderating effect is subject to boundary conditions. Compared with the valence bias and mnemic neglect mechanisms, self-concept integration and updating provide a more compelling account of the conditional moderating effect of self-esteem on the directed forgetting of social feedback.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05223-9.Keywords: Self-esteem, Social feedback, Directed forgetting, Mnemic neglect

Introduction

Social feedback and its memory bias in directed forgetting paradigm

In the context of social interactions, individuals frequently receive social feedback from others, which can vary from appreciation and approval to aversion and rejection. Social feedback functions as a critical evaluative mechanism, encompassing assessments of the self or a target of attention across multiple dimensions, including personal preferences, personality traits, and willingness to interact, and it inherently possesses emotional attributes [1]. As a form of social evaluative information, social feedback is inextricably linked to individual mental health [2]. Prior research has demonstrated that the ability to accurately perceive and effectively utilize social feedback significantly influences emotional states and social engagement [3, 4]. Within this framework, the encoding and retention of social feedback in memory play a pivotal role. Specifically, when confronted with negative social feedback, individuals must intentionally regulate the encoding process and actively engage in the forgetting of negative events to prevent excessive rumination on adverse feedback [5]. Persistent rumination on negative social feedback has been shown to lead to prolonged psychological distress and an increased risk of developing mental disorders [6]. Therefore, exploring memory encoding strategies for different types of social feedback is of particular importance.

The preferential retention of positive over negative social feedback in directed forgetting (DF) paradigms is a notable phenomenon within the realm of social cognition and memory research. This phenomenon was initially documented by Xie et al. [7], who developed an innovative experimental framework known as the Social Judgment and Directed Forgetting (SJ-DF) paradigm. This paradigm integrates social feedback task with DF task, providing a robust method for examining how individuals process and selectively remember or forget social evaluations. In the SJ-DF paradigm, participants are initially exposed to facial images of others, accompanied by social feedback provided by these individuals. Immediately following this exposure, participants receive a “remember” or “forget” cue, instructing them to either encode or suppress the memory of the presented social feedback. After completing the memory encoding phase and a brief intermission, participants undergo a short- or long-delayed memory test, during which they are required to recall the social feedback designated as to-be-remembered (TBR) or to-be-forgotten (TBF). Within this paradigm, individuals consistently exhibit a stronger DF effect for negative social feedback compared to positive feedback. This finding underscores the significant role of positive social feedback in shaping memory processes and suggests that positive feedback may be more readily retained despite instructions to forget.

To further elucidate the underlying neurocognitive mechanisms of this phenomenon, Xie et al. employed event-related potentials (ERPs) [7]. Their results revealed that during the encoding of social feedback, both positive and negative feedback elicited similar anterior N2 components, indicating comparable levels of early inhibitory control. However, positive feedback subsequently induced a larger late positive potential (LPP) amplitude compared to negative feedback. The anterior N2 component in DF paradigms typically reflects early active inhibitory control during memory encoding, while the LPP component is associated with late selective rehearsal processes and a preference for positive self-affirming information [8–10]. Given these findings, Xie et al. proposed that both positive and negative social feedback in the SJ-DF paradigm evoke proactive inhibitory control [7]. However, positive feedback appears to receive more effective selective rehearsal compared to negative feedback, thereby facilitating its preferential retention in memory.

Further neuroimaging studies utilizing the SJ-DF paradigm have identified the inhibitory control network, particularly the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), as a critical brain region regulating the directed forgetting of social feedback [11]. While individuals in the general population can effectively engage in the directed forgetting of negative social feedback, those with depression exhibit a diminished capacity to actively forget such feedback, attributed to dysfunctions within the right prefrontal inhibitory control network [11]. However, the application of transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) to modulate activity in the right DLPFC has been shown to significantly enhance the DF of negative social feedback in individuals with depression [12].

Despite prior research providing preliminary insights into the behavioral manifestations, neural substrates, and individual differences associated with mental illness in the directed forgetting of social feedback among general individuals, the role of self-personality in modulating these processes remains underexplored. To address this gap, the present study will focus on self-esteem—an emotional evaluation dimension of self-concept—to examine whether it influences and modulates the DF effect of social feedback.

Self-esteem moderate directed forgetting of social feedback? A perspective from valence bias and mnemic neglect

Self-esteem is conceptualized as an individual’s evaluative attitude toward the self as a distinct entity, intricately intertwined with personal needs, self-evaluation, and social evaluation, and it exerts a significant influence on social interactions [13]. The sociometer model posits that self-esteem functions as a metric for social feedback and interpersonal relationships, reflecting an individual’s psychological assessment of social acceptance or rejection [14]. It serves to evaluate, monitor, and regulate the quality of interpersonal relationships and motivates individuals to engage in behaviors that maintain social acceptance. Within this theoretical framework, the level of trait self-esteem, as a manifestation of self-evaluation tendencies, encapsulates an individual’s cumulative past social experiences and anticipations of future acceptance and rejection. This level of self-esteem continuously shapes an individual’s perception of social feedback and subsequent social behavior [15, 16].

Two distinct explanatory frameworks currently inform the relationship between self-esteem and emotional social feedback, providing theoretical grounding for examining whether self-esteem moderates the directed forgetting of social feedback. The first framework draws on mechanisms of memory valence bias and mnemic neglect, and the second draws on self-concept integration.

Prior research indicates that individuals with different levels of self-esteem show distinct valence-specific patterns in memory for positive and negative social feedback: high self-esteem (HSE) individuals preferentially remember positive feedback, whereas low self-esteem (LSE) individuals preferentially remember negative feedback [15]. This self-esteem-dependent memory bias can be explained by self-verification and self-affirmation motives.

Self-verification (or self-consistency/self-congruence) reflects a fundamental need to maintain alignment between one’s self-concept and external information [17]. Self-verification theory posits that individuals actively seek feedback confirming their self-concept, even when negative or harmful [17, 18]. Discrepancies between self-views and external feedback activate this motive, prompting adjustments to restore consistency [17, 18]. Thus, positive social feedback aligns with HSE individuals’ self-concept and receives superior encoding, whereas LSE individuals—owing to lower self-competence and self-liking—direct greater attention to negative feedback and failure-related experiences, resulting in elaborative encoding of negative information and a consequent memory bias [19].

Self-affirmation differs fundamentally from self-verification. While self-verification reflects a need for consistency (i.e., prompting individuals to seek self-congruent feedback to maintain a stable self-concept), self-affirmation reflects a need to protect global self-worth: when threatened, individuals affirm the self through various strategies (e.g., reinforcing core values, denying threatening information, seeking positive feedback), thereby alleviating threat and preserving self-esteem [20]. The mnemic neglect mechanism, rooted in self-affirmation, provides a complementary account of valence-specific memory for social feedback. Initially identified in research on motivated forgetting, mnemic neglect denotes a bias whereby individuals remember positive self-relevant events and forget negative ones to regulate emotions and avoid self-threat [21, 22]. This mechanism has been applied to explain the directed forgetting of social feedback, given that the SJ-DF paradigm typically shows preferential retention of positive over negative feedback—a pattern consistent with mnemic neglect [7, 22]. According to the Affirmational Resource Hypothesis (ARH), self-esteem reflects the richness of positive self-knowledge and thus the availability of self-affirmation resources. HSE individuals, possessing greater positive self-knowledge, can more effectively mobilize these resources to buffer against negative social feedback. LSE individuals, by contrast, lack such resources; this deficit heightens defensive responding and amplifies memory biases toward negative social feedback [20, 22, 23].

Based on the mechanisms of valence bias and mnemic neglect, it can be predicted that self-esteem moderates directed forgetting of social feedback. Specifically, HSE individuals—whether driven by self-verification or self-affirmation—tend to maintain a positive and healthy self-concept and to avoid self-threat. They accordingly exhibit a positive bias in memory processing of social feedback and, in a directed forgetting (DF) task, are inclined to intentionally remember positive social feedback while intentionally forgetting negative feedback, thereby demonstrating the DF effect. In contrast, LSE individuals, owing to their preferential encoding of negative information, are predicted to display a DF pattern that differs from that of high self-esteem individuals. Because of their negative self-concept and lack of self-affirmation resources, they develop attentional and memory biases toward negative feedback, which lead them, in a DF task, to more readily intentionally remember negative feedback or to experience difficulty intentionally forgetting it, compared to positive feedback. However, these hypotheses have not yet been empirically tested.

Self-esteem moderate directed forgetting of social feedback? Another perspective derived from the integration of self-concept

Although valence bias and mnemic neglect foreground the role of feedback valence in shaping self-cognition, yet they are not without limitations. Recent evidence indicates that the inherent need for self-concept stability, together with the congruence between feedback and pre-existing self-knowledge (i.e., self-verification), can obscure or attenuate valence effects [24, 25, 26]. Furthermore, valence bias and mnemic neglect offer no explanation for why other emotional dimensions of social feedback (e.g., intensity) also shape self-memory performance [11, 27, 28]. To address these limitations, researchers have proposed a self-concept integration model that does not center on valence. This model posits that individuals preferentially integrate novel social feedback that is consistent with their self-concept [24, 25]. Self-concept updating thus prioritizes structural stability, with a moderate positive bias superimposed, yielding a self-concept that is both stable and positive [25]. On this account, individuals do not merely respond to feedback valence; instead, they evaluate the consistency between social feedback and their self-concept, actively seeking and embracing positive, self-congruent information to promote stability. For self-incongruent information (regardless of valence), self-concept integration engages stability mechanisms to preserve structural integrity and stabilize newly acquired self-knowledge [24, 25, 26].

On this self-concept integration account, valence offers an incomplete picture of how self-esteem moderates memory for social feedback [24, 25]. The decisive factors likely lie in the distinctive self-concept characteristics associated with each self-esteem level. For instance, HSE individuals exhibit not only a positive memory bias but also elevated rejection sensitivity [29, 30], ambiguity aversion [31], and defensive reactivity to social threat [31, 32], whereas LSE individuals are characterized by negative and potentially unstable self-concepts [24, 25, 33]. These properties may well supersede valence in determining how individuals remember and integrate social feedback. This alternative hypothesis also awaits empirical testing within the directed forgetting paradigm for social feedback.

Current experimental design and research hypotheses

In sum, the present study addresses two questions: whether self-esteem moderates directed forgetting of social feedback, and which of two competing frameworks better accounts for this relationship. The valence-based framework holds that valence bias and mnemic neglect are the primary mechanisms through which self-esteem exerts its moderating effect. The self-concept integration framework challenges this view, proposing instead that self-concept characteristics specific to each self-esteem level (rather than valence per se), critically shape self-evaluation and integration processes, thereby determining how self-esteem moderates directed forgetting of social feedback.

In the present study, we conducted two experiments to test these competing frameworks. Experiment 1 used the classic SJ-DF paradigm [7], with individuals explicitly classified as high or low in self-esteem to test the valence-based prediction (Hypothesis 1): a three-way interaction among self-esteem, feedback valence, and directed forgetting memory task, with HSE individuals showing superior intentional memory for positive feedback and LSE individuals showing superior intentional memory for negative feedback. Should this prediction fail, Experiment 2 would introduce the self-concept integration framework by adding an intensity dimension—using a five-level scale (very negative, somewhat negative, neutral, somewhat positive, very positive)—and leveraging differential self-concept updating between HSE and LSE individuals. Experiment 2 (Hypothesis 2) predicted a three-way interaction among self-esteem, social feedback, and directed forgetting task: HSE individuals, characterized by positive-biased yet rejection-sensitive and ambiguity-averse self-concept updating, should preferentially remember certain over ambiguous feedback regardless of valence; LSE individuals, marked by negative and unstable self-concepts, should show no positive bias and may preferentially remember negative feedback via self-verification.

Experiment 1

Method

Participants

This study received ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Normal University (ZJNU) (Protocol ZSRT2024008) and was conducted in compliance with the ethical principles of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The required sample size for the present study was calculated using WebPower (https://webpower.psychstat.org/wiki/), which is a collection of tools for conducting statistical power analysis online [34]. The study employed a two (Self-esteem: HSE vs. LSE) × two (Social feedback: positive vs. negative) × two (Memory task: TBR vs. TBF) mixed experimental design, with Self-esteem as a between-subject variable, while Social feedback and Memory task served as within-subject variables. To achieve a statistical power of 1 − β = 0.8 with a two-tailed significance level of α = 0.05 (nonsphericity correction ε = 1.00), WebPower calculations indicated that a minimum of 35 participants per self-esteem group (total N = 70) was required to detect all main and interaction effects at a high effect size (f = 0.40).

Based on this estimation, an online survey was conducted via an online survey platform named Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn), administering the Rosenberg self-esteem Scale (RSES) and the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) to collect a total of 1,500 responses from undergraduates at ZJNU. Following previous studies [35], an extreme-group approach was adopted, with participants scoring in the top and bottom 27% on the RSES classified into the HSE and LSE groups, respectively [36, 37]. Additionally, to control for the influence of depressive traits, participants with BDI-II scores greater than 13 were excluded from the study. Consequently, a total of 70 participants aged between 17 and 25 were selected, with 35 in the HSE group (9 males) and 35 in the LSE group (12 males). The HSE group had self-esteem scores ranging from 30 to 35 (M = 32.60, SD = 1.94), while the LSE group scored between 19 and 23 (M = 20.68, SD = 1.28). Given the rigorous sample size calculation required by WebPower for mixed-design experiments, we conducted a post-hoc power analysis using the more widely adopted G*Power software (https://www.psychologie.hhu.de/arbeitsgruppen/allgemeine-psychologie-und-arbeitspsychologie/gpower) [38]. The analysis revealed that with the current sample size, a statistical power exceeding 0.99 could be achieved at an effect size of f = 0.25 and a significance level of α = 0.05.

All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, were right-handed, and reported no physical or mental disorders that might affect the experiment. Prior to the experiment, participants provided written informed consents, and they were compensated upon completion of the study.

Given the cultural differences between Chinese and Western contexts, Item 8 was excluded during the scoring process [41]. Higher total scores on this scale indicate higher levels of explicit self-esteem. In the present study, the scale exhibited good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.86).

Procedure

The experimental procedure was programmed using the Psychtoolbox 3.0 toolkit (http://psychtoolbox.org/) on the MATLAB platform and presented on a 23-inch LCD monitor with a resolution of 2560 × 1440 pixels. The study adopted the task paradigm from Xie et al. [7] and consisted of four phases: cover story, social judgment and directed forgetting task, recall phase, and debriefing (see Fig. 1A).

Phase 1: cover story phase

During this phase, participants were informed two days in advance that they would be taking part in a study on first impressions in social interactions. Participants were required to provide identity photo of themselves, which would be evaluated by other participants who would indicate whether they liked the person in the photo.

Phase 2: social judgment and directed forgetting task (SJ-DF)

In this phase, participants completed the SJ-DF task. Each trial began with a jittered fixation cross presented for 200–300 ms, followed by the presentation of a photo. After 1,000 ms, a tick mark (√) or a cross mark (×) appeared below the photo for 2,000 ms as social feedback. Participants were informed in advance that the tick mark indicated that the person in the photo liked them, whereas the cross mark indicated that the person disliked them. Subsequently, a green or a red asterisk was presented on the screen for 3,000 ms as the cue of memory task. A green asterisk instructed participants to remember the previously shown face and the associated feedback, whereas a red asterisk instructed them to forget the previously presented face and feedback (see Fig. 1A). The four groups of facial images were randomly assigned to four experimental conditions (i.e., positive-remember; negative-remember; positive-forgetting; negative-forgetting), with 20 trials per condition, for a total of 80 trials. All trials were presented in a randomized order.

Phase 3: distraction task and recall test

After the SJ-DF task, participants proceeded to the distraction task and recall test. The distraction task consisted of one minute of two-digit addition calculations followed by a two-minute rest period. Immediately after the distraction task, participants completed the recall test. This study focused on the memory encoding characteristics of individuals with different levels of self-esteem under short-delay task conditions rather than on the long-term retention of memory. Therefore, unlike Xie et al. [7], who included a deferred recall test after three days, the present study retained only the short-delay recall test following the distraction task. The recall test procedure also followed Xie et al. [7]: each trial began with a fixation cross presented for 200 ms, followed by a photo that had appeared in the SJ-DF task. Participants were required to recall the social feedback given by the person in the photo within 2,000 ms and respond by pressing the “F” key if they believed the person liked them or the “J” key if they believed the person disliked them. As in the SJ-DF task, 80 photos were presented in a randomized order.

Phase 4: debriefing phase

During this phase, the experimenter primarily engaged in conversation to collect participants’ subjective impressions of the study1 and to mitigate any negative effects arising from the experimental procedure. The researcher reassured participants and explained that the social feedback presented during the experiment was entirely fictitious and that no real individuals had evaluated their photos.

All subjects successfully completed the experiment, with no subjects excluded from subsequent analysis. In this research, all studies, measures, manipulations, and data/participant exclusions are reported in the manuscript or its supplementary materials.

The ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Memory task [F(1, 68) = 13.64, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.17]. Specifically, recall accuracy was significantly higher under the TBR condition (M = 0.58, SD = 0.15) than under the TBF condition (M = 0.53, SD = 0.16), confirming the presence of an overall directed forgetting effect in Experiment 1. However, neither the main effect of Self-esteem [F(1, 68) = 3.37, p = 0.07, ηp² = 0.05] nor that of Social feedback [F(1, 68) = 1.34, p = 0.25, ηp² = 0.02] reached statistical significance.

Additionally, a significant Self-esteem × Social feedback interaction was found [F(1, 68) = 28.20, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.29]. Simple effects analysis revealed that HSE participants recalled more positive social feedback (M = 0.62, SD = 0.10) than negative social feedback (M = 0.46, SD = 0.11), t(34) = 5.19, p < 0.001, d = 0.88. In contrast, LSE participants recalled significantly less positive social feedback (M = 0.51, SD = 0.14) than negative social feedback (M = 0.62, SD = 0.12), t(34) = 2.65, p = 0.012, d = 0.45. That is, HSE individuals exhibited a memory bias for positive feedback, whereas LSE individuals exhibited a memory bias for negative feedback (see Fig. 1B). Besides that, neither the two-way interaction between Self-esteem and Memory task [F(1, 68) = 0.56, p = 0.46, ηp² = 0.01] nor the interaction between Social feedback and Memory task [F(1, 68) = 2.15, p = 0.15, ηp² = 0.03] was significant. Finally, the three-way interaction among Self-esteem, Memory task, and Social feedback did not reach statistical significance [F(1, 68) = 3.11, p = 0.08, ηp² = 0.04]. These results indicate that while self-esteem significantly influenced selective memory biases for social feedback of different valences in Experiment 1, it did not significantly moderate DF effects across valence conditions. Discussion

Using the well-established SJ-DF paradigm, Experiment 1 investigated the moderating role of self-esteem in the directed forgetting of social feedback. The effectiveness of the DF manipulation was confirmed by a significant main effect of Memory task, with higher recall accuracy for TBR than TBF items overall. Beyond replicating this basic effect, the study yielded two novel findings compared to previous work.

First, Experiment 1 employed the DF paradigm for the first time to demonstrate that individuals with different levels of self-esteem indeed exhibit a valence-based memory bias in the processing of social feedback. Specifically, the results of Experiment 1 revealed a significant two-way interaction between Self-esteem and Social feedback, manifested as higher recall of positive social feedback by HSE individuals and higher recall of negative social feedback by LSE individuals. The former pattern aligns with the positive self-concept of HSE individuals, reflecting a positivity bias, whereas the latter is consistent with the negative self-concept of LSE individuals. These results provide valid evidence for the existence of valence bias and mnemic neglect [21, 22]. Notably, although a similar effect has been reported in prior research [15], the present study is the first to demonstrate this phenomenon within the DF paradigm.

Second, while the results of Experiment 1 supported the valence bias and mnemic neglect mechanisms, contrary to the hypothesis of Experiment 1, they failed to provide evidence that self-esteem moderates the directed forgetting of social feedback. Under Hypothesis 1, we predicted that within the SJ-DF paradigm of Experiment 1, individuals with different self-esteem levels would exhibit distinct valence biases when instructed to remember or forget social feedback of varying valence, yielding a significant three-way interaction among Self-esteem, Social feedback valence, and Memory task. However, the results of Experiment 1 only revealed the main effect of Memory task and the two-way interaction between Self-esteem and Social feedback valence. The main effect of Memory task indicated that recall accuracy in the TBR condition was significantly higher than in the TBF condition, confirming that our DF manipulation was effective and validating the existence of the DF effect. The two-way interaction between Self-esteem and Social feedback valence supported the presence of valence bias and mnemic neglect. However, the absence of a three-way interaction among Self-esteem, Social feedback valence, and Memory task indicated that the results did not support the moderating role of self-esteem in the directed forgetting of social feedback. In other words, centering on feedback valence and relying solely on valence bias and mnemic neglect mechanisms cannot predict the moderating effect of self-esteem on the directed forgetting of social feedback.

Based on these findings, we conducted Experiment 2. As noted earlier, Experiment 2 modified the task procedure and feedback information from Experiment 1 to examine whether self-esteem moderates the directed forgetting of social feedback from the perspective of self-concept integration. To this end, two adjustments were introduced. First, we incorporated an intensity dimension alongside feedback valence, replacing the original positive and negative feedback with a five-point scale (i.e., very negative, somewhat negative, neutral, somewhat positive, very positive). This modification was motivated by two considerations. (a) Prior research has demonstrated that the intensity (or arousal) of social feedback significantly influences individuals’ attentional and memory processing of such feedback [11, 27]. (b) Given differences in self-concept, individuals with high versus low self-esteem may exhibit differential self-concept integration when processing social feedback that simultaneously conveys both valence and intensity [11, 27]. Second, we added a mutual evaluation task prior to the encoding task. This served two purposes: (a) to familiarize participants with the multi-level emotional classification of social feedback, and (b) to enhance the interactivity of the experimental context (albeit virtually), thereby indirectly facilitating the updating and integration of participants’ self-concepts. With these adjustments, we sought to re-examine whether self-esteem moderates the directed forgetting of social feedback from the perspective of self-concept integration.

Experiment 2

Methods

Participants

Experiment 2 was conducted as a 2(Self-esteem: high vs. low) × 2(Memory task: TBR vs. TBF) × 5(Social feedback: very negative, somewhat negative, neutral, somewhat positive, and very positive) mixed design. Following the same method used in Experiment 1 for sample size calculation, WebPower indicated that Experiment 2 requires a planned sample of 99 participants. Utilizing the same participant screening methods and inclusion criteria as in Experiment 1, Experiment 2 ultimately recruited 50 HSE participants (13 males) and 50 LSE participants (13 males). The HSE group had self-esteem scores ranging from 28 to 36 (M = 32.54, SD = 2.13), while the LSE group scored between 16 and 24 (M = 21.24, SD = 1.73). Based on a post-hoc power analysis conducted with G*Power, the current sample size achieves a statistical power exceeding 0.99, given an effect size of f = 0.25 and a significance level of α = 0.05.

All participants were undergraduates/graduates at ZJNU, with an age range of 17 to 25 years. They had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, were right-handed, and reported no physical or mental conditions that could affect their performance in the experiment. None of them had previously participated in a similar study. Before the experiment, participants read and signed an informed consent form. They were compensated for their participation after completing the experiment.

Procedure

The procedure of Experiment 2 was similar to that of Experiment 1, except that a mutual evaluation task was inserted between the cover story phase and the SJ-DF task (see Fig. 2A). In this task, participants were required to rate their likability of 10 other people’s photos on a 5-point scale by entering a number from “-2” to “+2,” where − 2 = “very negative”, -1 = “somewhat negative”, 0 = “neutral”, + 1 = “somewhat positive”, and + 2 = “very positive”. After completing this task, participants proceeded to the SJ-DF task, which was essentially the same as in Experiment 1, except that the social feedback likability information was changed from tick mark (√) or cross mark (×) to numbers ranging from “-2” to “+2,” with the same rating criteria as in the social evaluation task. The 10 sets of face photos were randomly assigned to 10 conditions, with 15 trials per condition, totaling 150 trials.

After completing the SJ-DF task, participants were required to perform a distraction task and a memory test. The distraction task was the same as in Experiment 1, requiring participants to perform mathematical calculations. The trial procedure of the memory test was also the same as in Experiment 1, presenting the 150 photos that appeared in the SJ-DF task during the third phase. Participants were required to make a recall judgment about the type of social feedback given by the person in the photo and responded by pressing a key. They had to determine whether the feedback was likable (including “very positive” and “somewhat positive,” press the “F” key), unlikable (including “very negative” and “somewhat negative”, press the “J” key), or neutral (press the “B” key). As in Experiment 1, all measurements and manipulations in this study have been reported, with no subjects excluded from subsequent analysis.

Results

Descriptive results for each condition of Experiment 2 were presented in Table 2. Compared with Experiment 1, the social feedback encoding that participants were required to process in Experiment 2 was more complex, potentially increasing cognitive load and memory difficulty. To examine this possibility, we first compared the accuracy rates on the item recall task between Experiment 1 and Experiment 2 using Welch-corrected independent-samples t-tests. Overall recall accuracy was significantly higher in Experiment 1 (M = 0.55, SD = 0.06) than in Experiment 2 (M = 0.40, SD = 0.04), t = 18.54, df = 116.39, p < 0.001, d = 3.06. Secondly, we conducted a 2 (Self-esteem: HSE vs. LSE) × 2 (Memory task: TBR vs. TBF) × 5 (Social feedback: very negative vs. somewhat negative vs. neutral vs. somewhat positive vs. very positive) three-way repeated-measures ANOVA on recall accuracy. The ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Memory task [F(1, 98) = 8.61, p = 0.004, ηp² = 0.08], with recall accuracy significantly higher in the TBR condition (M = 0.41, SD = 0.06) than in the TBF condition (M = 0.38, SD = 0.05). Additionally, the main effect of Social feedback was also significant [F(4, 392) = 48.36, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.33], with recall accuracy for neutral feedback significantly lower than for all other conditions [very negative vs. neutral: t(99) = 12.69, p < 0.001, d = 1.27; somewhat negative vs. neutral: t(99) = 12.16, p < 0.001, d = 1.22; somewhat positive vs. neutral: t(99) = 8.97, p < 0.001, d = 0.90; very positive vs. neutral: t(99) = 9.73, p < 0.001, d = 0.97; all p-values were Bonferroni-corrected]. No significant differences were found among the four non-neutral feedback levels. The main effect of Self-esteem was not significant [F(1, 98) = 0.02, 1.00 > p = 0.90, ηp² < 0.01].

Regarding interaction effects, the ANOVA revealed a significant Self-esteem × Social feedback interaction [F(4, 392) = 3.69, p = 0.006, ηp² = 0.04], and a significant Memory task × Social feedback interaction [F(4, 392) = 7.52, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.07]. However, the Self-esteem × Memory task interaction was not significant [F(1, 98) = 0.98, p = 0.33, ηp² = 0.01]. Critically, the three-way Self-esteem × Memory task × Social feedback interaction was also significant [F(4, 392) = 4.42, p = 0.002, ηp² = 0.04]. The complete ANOVA statistical results can be found in Tables S3, S4, and S5 and in the Supplementary materials.

To test Hypothesis 2 and decompose the three‑way interaction, we probed this interaction by conducting separate 2 (Memory task: TBR vs. TBF) × 5 (Social feedback: very negative, somewhat negative, neutral, somewhat positive, very positive) repeated‑measures ANOVAs for the HSE and LSE groups, respectively.

For HSE participants, the ANOVA revealed significant main effects of Memory task [F(1, 49) = 8.06, p = 0.007, ηp² = 0.14] and Social feedback [F(4, 196) = 27.04, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.36]. Post‑hoc analyses revealed that, among HSE participants, recall accuracy was significantly higher in the TBR condition than in the TBF condition, and significantly lower in the neutral feedback condition than in the other emotional feedback conditions (ps < 0.001). In addition, the Memory task × Social feedback interaction was significant [F(4, 196) = 9.33, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.16]. Simple effect analysis of this interaction revealed that: (1) recall accuracy for TBR items was significantly higher than for TBF items when social feedback was very positive [t(49) = 3.20, p = 0.002, d = 0.45], very negative [t(49) = 3.97, p < 0.001, d = 0.56], or neutral [t(49) = 2.79, p = 0.007, d = 0.40]. (2) Conversely, when social feedback was somewhat negative, HSE participants showed a reversal of this pattern, with significantly lower accuracy for TBR items than TBF items [t(49) = − 2.39, p = 0.021, d = − 0.34]. However, the difference between TBR and TBF items did not reach significance when social feedback was somewhat positive [t(49) = − 1.88, p = 0.065, d = − 0.27].

In contrast, for LSE participants, the ANOVA only revealed a significant main effect of Social feedback [F(4, 196) = 25.16, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.40]. However, neither the main effect of Memory task [F(1, 49) = 1.81, p = 0.19, ηp² = 0.04], nor the Memory task × Social feedback interaction [F(4, 196) = 1.29, p = 0.28, ηp² = 0.03], was significant. Post‑hoc analyses revealed that, among LSE individuals, recall accuracy was significantly lower in the neutral feedback condition than in the other emotional feedback conditions (ps < 0.001), and significantly higher under very negative feedback than under very positive feedback (p = 0.031) (see Fig. 2; Table 2).

Discussion

Building on the findings of Experiment 1, Experiment 2 implemented two key modifications to the task: (a) multi-level social feedback that integrated valence and intensity, and (b) a mutual evaluation task prior to the main task. These refinements were designed to promote self-concept updating and integration among individuals with different levels of self-esteem, with the goal of further testing whether self-esteem moderates the directed forgetting of social feedback. However, the changes to the task also produced an unintended consequence: compared with Experiment 1, the multi-level social feedback in Experiment 2 increased participants’ cognitive load, resulting in a decline in recall accuracy. Under this new experimental context, Experiment 2 yielded two important results.

First, the results of Experiment 2 partially replicated the core findings on valence bias and mnemic neglect from Experiment (1) Specifically, a main effect of Memory task emerged in Experiment 2 (recall performance was superior for TBR items relative to TBF items), confirming the effectiveness of the DF manipulation within the SJ-DF paradigm. Furthermore, a significant two-way interaction between Memory task and Social feedback was observed. However, the simple effects underlying this interaction shifted: although LSE participants once again displayed a pronounced memory bias for negative feedback—a pattern that remains interpretable through the negativity bias and mnemic neglect mechanisms—the positivity bias exhibited by HSE individuals in Experiment 1 disappeared in Experiment (2) Considering the increased task difficulty and the greater memory load imposed on participants in Experiment 2, while the disappearance of this bias is likely related to the change in experimental task, it also suggests that the positivity bias among HSE individuals is not a rigid mechanism; rather, it can be altered by variations in the experimental task.

Second, Experiment 2 demonstrated that self‑esteem indeed moderates the directed forgetting of social feedback. Specifically, we observed a significant three‑way interaction among Self‑esteem, Memory task, and Social feedback. To probe this interaction, we conducted separate 2 (Memory task: TBR vs. TBF) × 5 (Social feedback: very negative, somewhat negative, neutral, somewhat positive, very positive) repeated‑measures ANOVAs for high‑ and low‑self‑esteem individuals, respectively. These analyses revealed distinct memory patterns between the two groups. In particular, HSE participants exhibited a DF effect for social feedback, albeit with critical boundary conditions. This effect was present only for high‑intensity feedback (very positive/negative) and neutral feedback. In contrast, for low‑intensity negative feedback (“somewhat negative”), we observed a reversed DF effect, such that recall of TBF items was better than recall of TBR items.

The DF effect exhibited by HSE individuals for high-intensity positive feedback aligns with the positivity bias mechanism and with their healthy, positive self-concept, indicating that they indeed preferentially remember feedback that affirms the self [7, 24, 25, 26]. Meanwhile, their directed forgetting of high-intensity negative feedback suggests a tendency to remember high-intensity negative information that is clearly incongruent with the self-concept, which is consistent with their heightened attentional and memory sensitivity to social rejection [29, 30]. Their reversed DF effect for low-intensity negative feedback (i.e., “somewhat negative”), however, is relatively unusual. Previous research has reported reversed DF effects almost exclusively among special populations remembering specific stimulus materials (e.g., depressed patients forgetting negative memories) and has hardly ever documented such an effect among healthy populations [44]. We therefore speculate that this reversed DF effect may represent a memory processing pattern unique to HSE individuals when dealing with particular forms of social feedback. Prior studies have shown that chronic social acceptance may paradoxically heighten HSE individuals’ sensitivity to social rejection. Compared with LSE individuals, they display stronger ambiguity aversion and more pronounced defensive reactions [31, 32]. In Experiment 2, low-intensity negative evaluations (“somewhat dislike”) effectively constituted an incomplete social rejection, prompting them to avoid and forget such information, which ultimately gave rise to the reversed DF effect. Taken together, the memory performance of HSE individuals in Experiment 2 actually supports the influence of self-concept characteristics (sensitivity to social rejection and ambiguity aversion), rather than valence per se, on the processing of social feedback.

Contrary to expectations, although LSE participants in Experiment 2 showed a clear memory bias for negative feedback, the separate 2 (Memory task: TBR vs. TBF) × 5 (Social feedback: very negative, somewhat negative, neutral, somewhat positive, very positive) repeated‑measures ANOVA for this group revealed neither a significant main effect of Memory task nor a significant Memory task × Social feedback interaction; only a main effect of Social feedback was found. These findings suggest that, in the context of Experiment 2, LSE individuals exhibited difficulty with directed forgetting (DF).

We suggest that the DF difficulty observed among LSE individuals may be related to their comparatively poor executive control abilities. As noted earlier, the task context of Experiment 2 required participants to memorize feedback across five categories. Such encoding demands increased task difficulty and imposed a heavy cognitive load, as evidenced by the lower recall accuracy in Experiment 2 relative to Experiment 1. With respect to LSE individuals, prior research has found that this population commonly exhibits cognitive deficits such as poor executive control and inadequate emotion regulation abilities [29, 30]. The ability to intentionally/direct forget emotional stimuli relies precisely on the involvement of these cognitive components [7–11]. In Experiment 2, the high-difficulty memory task and relatively poor executive control abilities likely made it difficult for LSE individuals to direct forgetting of social feedback, with the possibility of floor effects in recall performance. We will elaborate on this issue further in the general discussion.

General Discussion

The present study systematically investigated the influence of self-esteem on the directed forgetting of social feedback through two experiments. Experiment 1 adopted the SJ-DF paradigm and grounded in the mechanisms of valence bias and mnemic neglect, sought to examine the DF effects for positive and negative social feedback among high- and low-self-esteem individuals. The results confirmed that differences in self-esteem can indeed elicit memory biases through these mechanisms, but a moderating role of self-esteem on the directed forgetting of social feedback was not observed. This indicates that centering solely on valence cannot predict the moderating effect of self-esteem on the directed forgetting of social feedback.

Experiment 2 built on Experiment 1 by modifying the task procedure and incorporating both valence and intensity information into the social feedback. Drawing on the self-concept integration model, it re-examined whether self-esteem moderates the directed forgetting of social feedback. Experiment 2 ultimately demonstrated that individuals with different levels of self-esteem exhibit differentiated behavioral patterns in the directed forgetting of social feedback. Based on these findings, we propose that self-esteem can moderate the directed forgetting of social feedback, but this moderating effect is subject to boundary conditions.

For the HSE individuals, they displayed a complex and flexible memory processing pattern in the modified SJ-DF paradigm, capable of producing different intentional memory performances depending on the conditions of the social feedback. These performances were more closely aligned with the characteristics of their healthy and positive self-concept, rather than merely depending on the valence properties of the social feedback [24, 25]. In contrast, LSE individuals exhibited difficulties in the directed forgetting of social feedback under high cognitive load. However, this difficulty does not stem from a valence preference for emotional social feedback, but rather appears to reflect deficits in executive function under high cognitive load [29, 30]. Prior evidence suggests that directed forgetting (as opposed to intentional remembering) involves two temporally distinct processing stages: Stage-1 entails the early active suppression of “to-be-forgotten” items, and Stage-2 involves the late selective rehearsal of target information [7–11]. Key studies have shown that LSE individuals exhibit reduced cognitive control and emotion regulation abilities (e.g., impaired regulation of negative memories and intrusive thoughts), yet paradoxically display an enhanced attentional bias toward social rejection cues [29, 30]. We propose that such impairments may lead to excessive attentional allocation during the perceptual processing of emotional social feedback, thereby disrupting instruction-based cognitive resource allocation. This dysregulation can manifest as deficits in inhibitory control or impaired termination of rehearsal processes [7–11]. Moreover, the increased encoding difficulty in Experiment 2 may have amplified this executive control deficit among low self-esteem (LSE) individuals and eliminated the valence bias in memory, ultimately impairing directed forgetting of emotional social feedback. This account effectively explains why LSE individuals are unable to deploy directed/intentional forgetting strategies to disregard negative social feedback that threatens or damages their self-concept. It offers novel mechanistic insights for understanding and intervening in self-concept distortions, social maladjustment, and comorbid mental health problems in LSE individuals. Future research should prioritize the development of targeted memory encoding interventions, particularly directed forgetting training protocols, as a potential therapeutic avenue for enhancing the mental health of this vulnerable population.

Two limitations of the present study should be noted. First, Experiment 2 did not effectively separate the modifications to the social feedback from the modifications to the task; therefore, Experiment 2 can only indicate that the moderating effect of self-esteem on the directed forgetting of social feedback is conditional, but it cannot further specify which of the two adjustments—and the resultant changes in cognitive components—is responsible for this outcome. Second, in operationalizing self-esteem level, we relied solely on the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale to distinguish explicit high and low self-esteem, without taking into account implicit self-esteem or the heterogeneity of high self-esteem (i.e., the distinction between secure and fragile high self-esteem). Whether these factors influence the directed forgetting of social feedback remains unclear. These limitations constrain the interpretive scope of the present findings, and future research is warranted to address them.

Conclusion

Self-esteem can moderate the directed forgetting of social feedback, yet this moderating effect is subject to boundary conditions. Compared with the valence bias and mnemic neglect mechanisms, self-concept integration and updating provide a more compelling account of the conditional moderating effect of self-esteem on the directed forgetting of social feedback.
