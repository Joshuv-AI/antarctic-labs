Elastic Odyn : Differentiable Optimization for Infeasible Control and Learning in Robotics
Aristotelis Papatheodorou, Jose Rojas, Ioannis Havoutis, Carlos Mastalli \hrefhttps://orcid.org/0000-0002-0725-4279

Abstract
Robotic systems routinely encounter conflicting objectives, modeling errors, and degenerate contact conditions that render quadratic programs (QPs) infeasible. Yet most optimization solvers and differentiable QP layers assume feasibility, leading to numerical failures, unstable gradients, or solver breakdown when constraints cannot be simultaneously satisfied. We present Elastic Odyn , a primal–dual non-interior-point QP solver that handles infeasibility through smooth squared- ℓ 2 \ell_{2} elastic relaxations. The resulting formulation remains well posed under ill-conditioning and degeneracy, supports warm starting, and converges to closest-to-feasible solutions when no feasible point exists. A lightweight refinement stage recovers physically meaningful dual variables from the elastic solution. Building on this framework, we develop Elastic OdynLayer , a differentiable QP layer with stable gradients under infeasibility, and Elastic OdynSQP , an infeasibility-aware SQP method that resolves inconsistent subproblems and intrinsically infeasible optimal control tasks through selective constraint relaxation. We evaluate the framework on benchmark QPs, singular contact mechanics, differentiable parameter identification, and quadrupedal and humanoid trajectory optimization. Across all settings, Elastic Odyn consistently outperforms state-of-the-art elastic QP solvers in robustness, warm-start performance, and convergence reliability, enabling optimization, simulation, control, and learning beyond the feasibility assumptions of existing methods.



I
Introduction

Quadratic programming (QP)
is a fundamental tool across science and engineering.
In robotics and
artificial intelligence (AI)

[
1
]
optimization problems are often large-scale and numerically stiff, placing stringent requirements on solver robustness, scalability, and numerical stability.
Despite its central role, there is currently no
QP
solver that is simultaneously robust to infeasibility, numerically stable for complex problems, and compatible with differentiable optimization.

Robotics provides a particularly demanding setting.
Robots must plan their actions in dynamic and uncertain environments while enforcing safety-critical constraints.
Decisions are made in real time and often over long horizons, leading to high-dimensional optimization problems with several constraints
[
2
]
.
In practice, feasibility is frequently violated due to modeling errors, external disturbances, or conflicting task requirements.
As a result, the quadratic subproblems arising in control pipelines may themselves become infeasible.

Model predictive control (MPC)

[
3
]
computes control actions in a principled way, handling dynamics and constraints by repeatedly solving structured quadratic programs.
However,
MPC
scales poorly and becomes increasingly ill-conditioned with longer prediction horizons.
Moreover,
MPC
solvers are not designed to handle
infeasible constraint sets
gracefully, which can lead to failure or unpredictable behavior in practice
[
4
]
.
Within
sequential quadratic programming (SQP)
methods, infeasibility is typically
addressed through restoration stages
[
5
]
.
In contrast, elastic mechanisms provide a unified and
computationally efficient means of recovering feasibility while resolving the problem.

Differentiable optimization has emerged as a promising paradigm for learning-based methods, where the forward pass solves a constrained problem and the backward pass propagates loss gradients through its solution. These gradients are governed by the
Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT)
system, which couples primal and dual variables and enforces stationarity and complementarity, but not primal feasibility, since intermediate gradient-based iterates may be infeasible. As a result, differentiable optimization layers are highly sensitive to infeasibility and ill-conditioning, exposing a fundamental deficiency of standard differentiable
QP
frameworks.

II
Related Work

Quadratic programming has been extensively studied, giving rise to a wide range of solvers with different trade-offs in scalability, robustness, and differentiability.
We review the main classes of optimization approaches, with a focus on infeasibility handling and differentiable structure.

II-A

Optimization approaches and available solvers

Active-set methods are among the earliest approaches to quadratic programming
[
7
]
. Solvers such as
qpOASES

[
8
]
and the
SQP
solver
Snopt

[
9
]
maintain the active constraint set and solve a sequence of equality-constrained subproblems. They are effective at small to medium scale and warm-startable, but scale poorly as constraint counts grow, due to exponential active-set combinatorics. Discrete active-set changes also induce nonsmooth solution mappings, complicating differentiation and hindering training.

Augmented Lagrangian methods (ALMs)
handle constraints via penalty terms and iterative dual updates, exhibiting strong robustness to rank-deficient equalities and ill-conditioning. Solvers such as
Lancelot

[
10
]
,
Osqp

[
11
]
, and
ProxQP

[
12
]
extend this framework to inequality-constrained problems, typically enforcing inequalities through projection or proximal operators. However, these strategies introduce nonsmoothness detrimental to numerical robustness and gradient-based training. Despite strong theoretical guarantees, reliance on penalty tuning and repeated inner solves limits ALM efficiency in real-time and learning-based settings.

Interior-point methods (IPMs)
dominate large-scale convex and nonlinear optimization. Solvers such as
Ipopt

[
13
]
,
Gurobi

[
14
]
, and
Piqp

[
15
]
use barrier functions and primal–dual
KKT
systems to handle inequalities without explicit active-set enumeration, scaling well in feasible regimes. However, IPMs follow a central path that is well defined only when the problem is strictly feasible, which makes warm starting difficult and causes failures or numerical instabilities when problems become infeasible or nearly infeasible.

Motivated by these limitations,
non-interior-point methods (NIPMs)

[
16
,
6
]
have emerged as alternatives that remain well defined outside the feasible set. Generally, they can be categorized into two families.
Semismooth
methods such as
FBstab

[
17
]
reformulate the
KKT
conditions via
nonlinear complementarity problem (NCP)
functions like Fischer–Burmeister
[
18
]
, with applications in contact simulation and control
[
19
]
.
Path-following
methods instead track smoothed
KKT
systems as the smoothing parameter vanishes, operating robustly under infeasibility
[
20
]
and yielding regular solution mappings well suited to implicit differentiation. Yet no existing method simultaneously addresses infeasibility and robustness within a unified differentiable framework.

II-B

Differentiable optimization and limitations

Differentiable optimization enables training optimization layers within learning pipelines by exposing reverse-mode gradients of the solution map.
OptNet

[
21
]
pioneered this for quadratic programs by analytically differentiating the
KKT
conditions of an interior-point solver, and
cvxpylayers

[
22
]
extends it to general convex programs, but both inherit the strict feasibility requirements and numerical sensitivity of
IPMs
.
QPlayer

[
23
]
, built on
ProxQP

[
12
]
, admits infeasible problems through an elastic
ALM
formulation, but its nonsmooth solution mapping forces differentiation through the
extended conservative Jacobian (ECJ)
framework
[
24
]
and inflates the backward pass with elastic relaxation variables. Tracy and Manchester
[
25
]
recover smooth gradients from a primal–dual
IPM
by relaxing central-path solutions with a tunable smoothing parameter, but inherit barrier pathologies such as parameter sensitivity and poor warm-startability, so their gradients reflect
barrier-induced smoothing
rather than true constrained sensitivities.

Elastic Odyn
is instead designed for the degenerate and ill-conditioned regimes through
barrier–penalty functions
, supporting warm starting and explicitly resolving primal infeasibility. Its differentiable counterpart,
Elastic OdynLayer
, is the first projection-free, fully smooth
QP
layer that yields well-defined derivatives even when no feasible point exists, producing more regular solution mappings than existing layers in the robotics and
AI
regimes where infeasibility and numerical stiffness are routine.

III
Infeasibility in Quadratic Programming

We consider convex quadratic programs of the form

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\mathbf{Q}\,\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}

(1)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b},

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}\,\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{h},

with primal variables
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, symmetric positive semidefinite cost matrix
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, linear cost
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and constraints defined by
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
for the equalities and
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n},\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
for the inequalities. Under standard regularity assumptions such as Slater’s condition, optimal solutions of
Equation

1
are characterized by the
KKT
conditions, derived from the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\mathbf{Q}\,\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b})+\mathbf{z}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{h}),

(2)

where
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are the multipliers associated with the equality and inequality constraints. Combining primal and dual feasibility, complementary slackness, and stationarity, the
KKT
conditions admit the equivalent variational form

\vskip-5.69054pt\mathbf{0}\in\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}+N_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{x}),

(3)

with feasible set
\mathcal{F}\coloneq\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b},\;\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{h}\}
and normal cone
N_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{x})
. This inclusion provides a unified representation that underpins the notions of primal and dual feasibility developed below.

III-A

Primal infeasibility

A point
\mathbf{x}
is primal infeasible if it violates at least one constraint, i.e.,
\mathbf{x}\notin\mathcal{F}
. The geometric infeasibility is captured by
\mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{x},\mathcal{F})=\inf_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|
, but is generally intractable to evaluate. We instead use the computable
constraint violation

\mathrm{viol}(\mathbf{x})=\max\bigl(\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|,\ \|\mathbf{x}-\Pi_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{x})\|\bigr),

(4)

where
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm,
\mathcal{I}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{h}\}
, and
\Pi_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{x})
denotes the Euclidean projection onto
\mathcal{I}
. This quantity vanishes if and only if
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{F}
and provides a tractable surrogate for
\mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{x},\mathcal{F})
.
For
\mathbf{x}\notin\mathcal{F}
, the variational inclusion cannot be satisfied, meaning that the
KKT
inclusion cannot hold:

\mathbf{0}\notin\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}+N_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{x}),

(5)

because the normal cone is empty, i.e.,
N_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{x})=\emptyset\text{ for }\mathbf{x}\notin\mathcal{F}
.
Under standard constraint qualifications, the normal cone admits a representation in terms of dual multipliers

N_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{x})=\{\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{G}^{\intercal}\mathbf{z}\,|\,\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+},\mathbf{z}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{h})=\mathbf{0}\},

(6)

from which the
KKT
conditions follow, with
\mathbf{z}\geq\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{z}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{h})=\mathbf{0}
corresponding to dual feasibility and complementary slackness.

In practical robotics applications, primal infeasibility commonly arises from over-constrained kinematic tasks or conflicting contact conditions.
Moreover, in differentiable
QP
layers, primal feasibility cannot be enforced during training, since intermediate iterates produced by gradient-based updates are generally infeasible.

In the following subsection, we distinguish between violations of dual feasibility at the multiplier level and true dual infeasibility in the optimization-theoretic sense.

III-B

Dual infeasibility

Dual feasibility concerns the existence of Lagrange multipliers
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})
compatible with the normal-cone representation of
Equation

3
. It requires non-negativity of the inequality multipliers
\mathbf{z}\geq\mathbf{0}
, defining the dual set
\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\mid\mathbf{z}\geq\mathbf{0}\}
. A pair
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})
is dual infeasible if
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})\notin\mathcal{D}
, and we quantify the violation by the Euclidean distance to
\mathcal{D}
,

\mathrm{dist}((\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}),\mathcal{D})=\|\mathbf{z}-\Pi_{\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}}(\mathbf{z})\|,

(7)

where
\Pi_{\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}}(\cdot)
denotes the Euclidean projection onto the nonnegative orthant. When
\mathbf{z}\not\geq\mathbf{0}
, the vector
\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{G}^{\intercal}\mathbf{z}
no longer lies in
N_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{x})
, so the stationarity inclusion
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}+N_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{x})
fails to characterize a valid
KKT
point, even when
\mathbf{x}
is primal feasible. Dual feasibility is therefore necessary for the
KKT
system to be well defined.

Under standard regularity assumptions such as Slater’s condition
[
26
]
, dual infeasibility manifests as primal unboundedness, meaning the objective decreases without bound along a feasible direction,
\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{F}}\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\mathbf{Q}\,\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}=-\infty
. When
\mathbf{Q}\succeq\mathbf{0}
, this requires a nonzero
\Delta\mathbf{x}
in the recession cone of
\mathcal{F}
along which the quadratic term vanishes, i.e.
\mathbf{Q}\Delta\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbf{A}\Delta\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbf{G}\Delta\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{0}
, and
\mathbf{c}^{\intercal}\Delta\mathbf{x}<0
. Such directions are rare in well-posed robotics problems and typically signal a modeling pathology, such as missing bounds, or a differentiable
QP
layer whose learned
\mathbf{Q}
has drifted from positive semidefiniteness during training. A small regularization term suffices in both cases. Indefinite
\mathbf{Q}
is qualitatively different, since the problem leaves the convex setting altogether, and the resulting unboundedness reflects a loss of numerical validity rather than dual infeasibility in any meaningful sense.

III-C

About certifying infeasibility

Reliable infeasibility detection is challenging, since residual-based criteria often stagnate in ill-conditioned or degenerate problems. Formal detection relies on Farkas-type certificates
[
27
]
, which prove unsolvability geometrically but are numerically fragile and expensive to compute post hoc. A common alternative is the homogeneous self-dual formulation
[
28
]
, embedding the
KKT
system into a skew-symmetric system where optimality and infeasibility appear as distinct limit points of a single central path. Neither approach aligns with the
best-effort
solutions required in robotics and control, where approximate feasibility and numerical robustness outweigh exact infeasibility proofs.

IV
Elastic Odyn

IV-A

Algorithmic foundations

Elastic formulations address infeasibility by relaxing hard constraints and penalizing violations in the objective. A prototypical elastic
QP
with explicit infeasibility relaxation reads

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x},\,\mathbf{s}_{e},\,\mathbf{s}_{i}}

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}+\tfrac{1}{2\gamma_{e}}\left\lVert\mathbf{s}_{e}\right\rVert^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\gamma_{i}}\left\lVert\mathbf{s}_{i}\right\rVert^{2}

(8)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{s}_{e},\quad\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{h}\leq\mathbf{s}_{i},

where
\mathbf{s}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\mathbf{s}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are elastic relaxation variables relaxing the equality and inequality constraints, and
\gamma_{e},\gamma_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
balance optimality against constraint satisfaction. Closed-form elimination of
\mathbf{s}_{e}
and
\mathbf{s}_{i}
yields the unconstrained problem

\min_{\mathbf{x}}\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}+\tfrac{1}{2\gamma_{e}}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\right\rVert^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\gamma_{i}}\left\lVert(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{h})_{+}\right\rVert^{2},

(9)

with
(\cdot)_{+}
the componentwise positive part. Variationally, the two penalties are Moreau–Yosida regularizations of the indicators
\delta_{\{\mathbf{0}\}}
and
\delta_{\mathbb{R}^{p}_{-}}
composed with the constraint maps,

\min_{\mathbf{x}}\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}+\delta_{\{\mathbf{0}\}}^{\gamma_{e}}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b})+\delta_{\mathbb{R}^{p}_{-}}^{\gamma_{i}}(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{h}),

(10)

where
\delta_{\mathcal{C}}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})\coloneq\min_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}\delta_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})+\tfrac{1}{2\gamma}\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2\gamma}\mathrm{dist}^{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathcal{C})
is the Moreau envelope of
\delta_{\mathcal{C}}

[
29
]
. Its gradient
\nabla\delta_{\mathcal{C}}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})=\gamma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{x}))
is the Yosida approximation of the normal cone
N_{\mathcal{C}}
, so the elastic objective is
C^{1}
with semismooth gradient, and the first-order optimality condition becomes the semismooth stationarity equation

\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}+\tfrac{1}{\gamma_{e}}\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b})+\tfrac{1}{\gamma_{i}}\mathbf{G}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{h})_{+},

(11)

a single-valued surrogate of the original
KKT
inclusion. Whereas
\ell_{1}
penalties enforce constraints exactly at finite parameters but produce nonsmooth, set-valued optimality conditions, the squared
\ell_{2}
penalties we adopt preserve smoothness and yield well-defined proximal
KKT
systems, which are particularly suited to differentiable optimization layers and implicit Newton-type solvers.

IV-B

An elastic path-following NIPM for QP

Elastic Odyn
is a primal–dual
QP
solver based on a non-interior-point formulation that handles both feasible and infeasible
QPs
, supports warm starting, and remains robust to degeneracy. It combines all-shifted
NCP
functions with proximal primal–dual regularization, yielding a smooth, well-conditioned
KKT
system under Slater’s condition alone:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x},\,\mathbf{s},\,\mathbf{s}_{e},\,\mathbf{s}_{i},\,\boldsymbol{\xi}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})-\mu\sum^{p}_{j=1}\log(\xi_{j})+\tfrac{1}{2\gamma_{e}}\|\mathbf{s}_{e}\|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\gamma_{i}}\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|^{2}

(12)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{s}_{e},\quad\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{s}_{i},\quad\mathbf{s}=\boldsymbol{\xi},

with
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\coloneq\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}
, inequality slack
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and consensus variable
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. The barrier acts on
\boldsymbol{\xi}
rather than
\mathbf{s}
, allowing iterates to leave the interior of
\{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{h}\}
while preserving a well-defined primal–dual structure. We refer to this as a
penalty–barrier formulation
.

The associated Lagrangian augments the consensus constraint quadratically,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{E}}(\tilde{\mathbf{U}};\mu,\boldsymbol{\gamma})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})-\mu\sum_{j=1}^{p}\log(\xi_{j})+\tfrac{1}{2\gamma_{e}}\|\mathbf{s}_{e}\|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\gamma_{i}}\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|^{2}

(13)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{s}-\boldsymbol{\xi}\|^{2}-\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{s}-\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle+\mathbf{y}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{s}_{e})+\mathbf{z}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{s}_{i}),

where
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are multipliers for the equality, inequality, and consensus constraints,
\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the barrier parameter, and
\tilde{\mathbf{U}}\coloneq(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s},\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{s}_{e},\mathbf{s}_{i})
.

For robustness under degeneracy, we add proximal penalties on primal and dual variables, yielding the perturbed Lagrangian

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{E}}(

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{U}};\mu,\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\rho},\tilde{\mathbf{U}}_{E})=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{E}}(\tilde{\mathbf{U}};\mu,\boldsymbol{\gamma})+\tfrac{\rho_{d}}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{E}\|^{2}

(14)

\displaystyle-\tfrac{\rho_{e}}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}_{E}\|^{2}-\tfrac{\rho_{i}}{2}\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}_{E}\|^{2}+\tfrac{\rho_{n}}{2}\|\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{s}_{E}\|^{2}

\displaystyle-\tfrac{\rho_{n}}{2}\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_{E}\|^{2}-\tfrac{\rho_{n}}{2}(\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{s}_{E})^{\intercal}(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_{E}),

with reference estimates
\tilde{\mathbf{U}}_{E}\coloneq(\mathbf{x}_{E},\mathbf{s}_{E},\mathbf{w}_{E},\mathbf{y}_{E},\mathbf{z}_{E})
and proximal weights
\boldsymbol{\rho}\coloneq(\rho_{d},\rho_{e},\rho_{i},\rho_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}
. The opposing signs on dual variables reflect the saddle structure of the primal–dual formulation. Its first order optimality conditions in (
15
) form the basis for
Elastic Odyn
’s Newton system.

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{G}^{\intercal}\mathbf{z}+\rho_{d}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{E}),

(15)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{s}_{e}-\rho_{e}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}_{E}),

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{s}_{i}-\rho_{i}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}_{E}),

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{s}-\boldsymbol{\xi}+\tfrac{\rho_{n}}{2}(\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{s}_{E})+\tfrac{\rho_{n}}{2}(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_{E}),

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle=-\mu\,\boldsymbol{\xi}^{-1}-(\mathbf{s}-\boldsymbol{\xi})+\mathbf{w},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{s}}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{s}-\boldsymbol{\xi}+\tfrac{\rho_{n}}{2}(\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{s}_{E})-\tfrac{\rho_{n}}{2}(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_{E})-\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{z},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{s}_{e}}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{y}-\gamma_{e}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{e},\quad\nabla_{\mathbf{s}_{i}}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{E}}=\mathbf{z}-\gamma_{i}^{-1}\mathbf{s}_{i},

IV-C

Elastic Newton system

Following
Odyn
’s reduction strategy
[
6
]
, we first eliminate the consensus variable
\boldsymbol{\xi}
in closed form by substituting
\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{E}}=\mathbf{0}
into
\nabla_{\mathbf{s}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{E}}=\mathbf{0}
, simplifying the corresponding
KKT
residuals. The elastic relaxation variables are likewise eliminated via the identities
\mathbf{s}_{e}\equiv\gamma_{e}\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{s}_{i}\equiv\gamma_{i}\mathbf{z}
in
Equation

15
. This twofold elimination both reduces the system to the hard-constrained dimensions and removes the need for an explicit non-negativity constraint on
\mathbf{s}_{i}
, which inherits non-negativity from the inequality multiplier
\mathbf{z}
.

These reductions yield the following set of reduced elastic
KKT
residuals:

\tilde{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{U};\boldsymbol{\Pi})=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{d}\\
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{e}\\
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\\
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{g}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{G}^{\intercal}\mathbf{z}+\rho_{d}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{E})\\
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}-\gamma_{e}\mathbf{y}-\rho_{e}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}_{E})\\
\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{h}-\gamma_{i}\mathbf{z}-\rho_{i}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}_{E})\\
\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{z};\mu)+\rho_{n}(\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{s}_{E})+\rho_{n}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}_{E})\end{bmatrix}

(16)

where
\boldsymbol{\phi}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
is an
NCP
function,
\mathbf{U}\coloneq\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m+2p}
is the reduced set of primal and dual variables, and
\boldsymbol{\Pi}\coloneq\left(\mu,\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\rho},\mathbf{U}_{E}\right)
denotes the set of parameters of the elastic penalty–barrier formulation.

Equation

16
is then solved by applying Newton’s method:

\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}&\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}&\mathbf{G}^{\intercal}&\\
\mathbf{A}&-\left(\rho_{e}+\gamma_{e}\right)\mathbf{I}&&\\
\mathbf{G}&&-\left(\rho_{i}+\gamma_{i}\right)\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{I}\\
&&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{\mathbf{z}}&\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{\mathbf{s}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\mathbf{x}\\
\Delta\mathbf{y}\\
\Delta\mathbf{z}\\
\Delta\mathbf{s}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{d}\\
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{e}\\
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\\
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{g}\end{bmatrix},

(17)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{\mathbf{z}}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{z}}+\rho_{n}\mathbf{I}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{\mathbf{s}}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{s}}+\rho_{n}\mathbf{I}
are diagonal matrices in which
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
denotes the
NCP
Jacobians.

From
Equation

16
, the elastic formulation penalizes equality and inequality violations through scaled Lagrange multipliers, whereas in
Equation

17
these penalties act as adaptive regularization terms whose magnitudes are governed by the current
KKT
residuals.
Elastic Odyn
factorizes this system and accepts the resulting search directions via a non-monotone line search (see Section IV.D in
[
6
]
).

Barrier, estimates, and elastic penalty updates

The barrier parameter smooths the
NCP
functions and is updated by the centering procedure of
[
30
]
. Proximal estimates are refreshed at each iteration’s end by interpolating between the current iterate
\mathbf{U}
and the next
\mathbf{U}^{+}
, yielding Newton-like behavior under sufficient progress and a more conservative, gradient-descent-like update when convergence degrades
[
6
]
. This adaptive switching mirrors the behavior of Levenberg–Marquardt schemes. The interpolation parameters and the elastic penalties
\gamma_{e},\gamma_{i}
follow a feasibility-driven rule, tightening when progress toward feasibility is sufficient and remaining unchanged otherwise.

IV-D

Stopping Criteria

We combine absolute and relative tolerances to certify convergence in both feasible and infeasible regimes. Unlike standard
QPs
solvers,
Elastic Odyn
’s stopping criteria remain well defined under infeasibility and guarantee convergence to the
closest feasible
problem when exact feasibility cannot be attained.

The primal residuals
(\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{e}\right\rVert_{\infty},\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{i}\right\rVert_{\infty})
and dual residual
\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{d}\right\rVert_{\infty}
are scaled to reflect the elastic relaxation. Primal relative tolerances incorporate
\gamma_{e}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{\infty}
and
\gamma_{i}\|\mathbf{z}\|_{\infty}
, matching the relaxed residuals via the identities
\mathbf{s}_{e}=\gamma_{e}\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{s}_{i}=\gamma_{i}\mathbf{z}
in
Equation

15
. The dual relative tolerance is symmetrically augmented by
\gamma_{e}^{-1}\|\mathbf{s}_{e}\|_{\infty}
and
\gamma_{i}^{-1}\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{\infty}
, ensuring that feasibility and optimality are evaluated consistently with the relaxed problem throughout the iteration.

To certify convergence to the closest-feasible
QP
, we further enforce a least-violation optimality condition,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{c}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\epsilon_{a}+\epsilon_{r}\max\!\Big(\left\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{G}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{s})\right\rVert_{\infty},

(18)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{\infty},\;\left\lVert\mathbf{G}^{\intercal}\mathbf{h}\right\rVert_{\infty},\;\left\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{G}^{\intercal}\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{\infty}\Big),

where
\mathbf{r}_{c}
is the gradient of the squared constraint violation,

\mathbf{r}_{c}=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\right\rVert^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{h}\right\rVert^{2}\Big)

(19)

This certifies optimality in the
least-violation
squared
\ell_{2}
sense. Combined with the elastic primal and dual scalings, these criteria guarantee convergence to the optimally shifted problem while preserving a meaningful primal–dual solution under infeasibility.

IV-E

Warm-started refining stage

The elastic formulation robustly recovers a primal solution for the closest-feasible
QP
, but its dual variables are shaped by the elastic stationarity in
Equation

15
and encode penalized violations rather than true sensitivities of the shifted feasible constraints. These elastic duals therefore lack physical interpretability, making them unsuitable as Lagrange multipliers in contact-physics settings, where multipliers represent quantities like contact forces. We address this with a lightweight refinement stage that warm-starts from the elastic primal, shifts the constraints with the optimal elastic relaxation variables, and re-solves the hard-constrained problem. Converging in only a few iterations (see
Section

V-B
), it yields a well-conditioned primal–dual pair with physically meaningful multipliers and sensitivities consistent with the closest-feasible problem.

IV-F

Differentiable optimization under infeasibility

Elastic OdynLayer
extends differentiable optimization to
infeasible

QPs
. Whereas existing layers
[
21
,
22
]
require strict feasibility and break down when constraints conflict, our layer remains strictly convex and
C^{1}
throughout, exposing infeasibility itself as a differentiable signal so that gradients propagate even when no feasible point exists. We consider the parameterized elastic
QP

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s}_{e},\mathbf{s}_{i}}

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\mathbf{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}+\tfrac{1}{2\gamma_{e}}\|\mathbf{s}_{e}\|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\gamma_{i}}\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|^{2}

(20)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbf{s}_{e},\mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq\mathbf{s}_{i},

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
collects all learnable parameters.

Forward pass

The layer solves (
20
) via
Elastic Odyn
, optionally followed by the refinement stage of
Section

IV-E
. The optimal elastic relaxation variables

\mathbf{s}_{e}^{\star}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{\star}-\mathbf{b},\qquad\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\star}=(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}^{\star}-\mathbf{h})_{+},

serve as differentiable indicators of constraint inconsistency: both vanish in the feasible regime and grow continuously with the degree of violation otherwise.

Backward pass

Gradients with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
combine two complementary contributions: (i)
implicit gradients
through the
KKT
system, and (ii)
elastic relaxation-induced corrections
that propagate sensitivities of any infeasibility-aware loss through
\mathbf{s}_{e}^{\star}
and
\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\star}
. Together they yield stable, well-defined gradients across feasible and infeasible regimes.

IV-F
1
Implicit gradients via the implicit function theorem

From the elastic
KKT
residual in
Equation

16
, the implicit function theorem gives
\partial\mathbf{U}^{\star}/\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}=-[\partial\tilde{\mathbf{r}}/\partial\mathbf{U}^{\star}]^{-1}\,\partial\tilde{\mathbf{r}}/\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}
. To avoid explicit Jacobian inversion, we solve the adjoint system

\left[\frac{\partial\tilde{\mathbf{r}}}{\partial\mathbf{U}^{\star}}\right]^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\lambda}=-\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mathbf{U}^{\star}}

(21)

for
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m+2p}
, yielding the implicit contribution
\partial\ell/\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\intercal}\partial\tilde{\mathbf{r}}/\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}
as in
[
21
]
. Because
Equation

16
remains well conditioned even under infeasibility, this system is solvable without the regularization heuristics required by interior-point-based layers.

IV-F
2
Elastic relaxation-induced gradient corrections

Infeasibility introduces additional gradient pathways through the elastic relaxation variables, with upstream sensitivities
\mathbf{g}_{e}\coloneq\partial\ell/\partial\mathbf{s}_{e}^{\star}
and
\mathbf{g}_{i}\coloneq\partial\ell/\partial\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\star}
. Differentiating the closed-form expressions for
\mathbf{s}_{e}^{\star},\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\star}
yields, for the equality constraints,

\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mathbf{b}}\mathrel{+}=-\mathbf{g}_{e},\qquad\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mathbf{A}}\mathrel{+}=\mathbf{g}_{e}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})^{\intercal},\qquad\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mathbf{x}^{\star}}\mathrel{+}=\mathbf{A}^{\intercal}\mathbf{g}_{e},

(22)

and for the inequality constraints,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mathbf{h}}\mathrel{+}=-(\mathbf{m}\odot\mathbf{g}_{i}),\quad\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mathbf{G}}\mathrel{+}=(\mathbf{m}\odot\mathbf{g}_{i})(\mathbf{x}^{\star})^{\intercal},

(23)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mathbf{x}^{\star}}\mathrel{+}=\mathbf{G}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{m}\odot\mathbf{g}_{i}),\quad\mathbf{m}\coloneq\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{x}^{\star}-\mathbf{h}>\mathbf{0}}.

The mask
\mathbf{m}
activates corrections only on violated inequalities, propagating subgradients through the positive-part nonsmoothness. These accumulate on top of the implicit gradients from the adjoint system, with
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
-contributions flowing into the upstream loss.
In the feasible regime,
\mathbf{s}_{e}^{\star}=\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\star}=\mathbf{0}
and the corrections vanish, recovering the standard
KKT
-based differentiation of
[
21
]
.
Otherwise, they provide a principled gradient signal for losses penalizing inconsistency or driving parameters toward feasibility.

V-C

Contact mechanics

Contact mechanics provide a canonical setting where infeasibility arises frequently. At the acceleration level, sustained contact and impulsive impact are described by the same
QP
,

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{p},\,\mathbf{s}_{i}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{p}_{0}\|_{\mathbf{M}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\gamma_{i}}\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|^{2}

(24)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{s}_{i}\geq\mathbf{0},

differing only in the temporal interpretation of the constraint geometry. The variables specialize to
(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{p}_{0},\mathbf{b})=(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}_{\text{free}},\dot{\mathbf{J}}\mathbf{v})
for sustained contact, with generalized acceleration
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, generalized velocity
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, unconstrained acceleration
\mathbf{a}_{\text{free}}=\mathbf{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\tau}-\mathbf{h})
, and contact Jacobian
\mathbf{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
. For impact,
(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{p}_{0},\mathbf{b})=(\mathbf{v}^{+},\mathbf{v}^{-},e\mathbf{J}\mathbf{v}^{-})
with pre- and post-impact velocities
\mathbf{v}^{-},\mathbf{v}^{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and restitution coefficient
e\in[0,1]
. Sustained contact enforces non-penetration over a finite time step, while impact enforces it instantaneously through impulses. In both cases, infeasibility arises when
\mathbf{J}
becomes rank-deficient or geometrically inconsistent, preventing the constraints from generating admissible reaction forces.

The elastic variable
\mathbf{s}_{i}
relaxes the non-penetration constraint and penalizes infeasibility in an
\ell_{2}
sense. Its complementarity-slackness condition yields the regularized
linear complementarity problem (LCP)

\mathbf{0}\leq\underbrace{(\mathbf{J}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{J}^{\intercal}+\gamma_{i}\mathbf{I})}_{\text{reg. Delassus operator}}\mathbf{z}+\underbrace{\mathbf{J}\mathbf{p}_{0}+\mathbf{b}}_{\text{contact bias}}\perp\mathbf{z}\geq\mathbf{0},

(25)

where
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
collects the contact forces in sustained-contact models or the collision impulses in impact scenarios. The diagonal regularization
\gamma_{i}\mathbf{I}
, together with the proximal-point perturbation on
\mathbf{z}
, resolves rank deficiencies in the Delassus operator and enables robust contact-implicit
MPC

[
31
]
and rigid-body simulation.

We illustrate this on a classical example from Featherstone (Fig. 11.3 in
[
32
]
), where a two-dimensional disc is constrained inside a frictionless tube of equal radius. As the disc reaches the constriction at
x\rightarrow 0^{-}
, the upper and lower contact normals become linearly dependent and
\mathbf{J}
loses rank (see
Figure

2
). At the degenerate configuration
x=0
the contact model is singular. With the tube contracting and no tangential forces available, no admissible contact forces can prevent further penetration.
Elastic Odyn
resolves the singularity by admitting a small penetration beyond
x=0
, which tilts the contact normals away from collinearity and restores Jacobian rank. The impact dynamics are then captured by
Equation

24
.

for infeasible optimal control

We extend

(see Section V in
[

The former produces infeasible QP subproblems despite a feasible nonlinear problem, while the latter arises from conflicting nonlinear constraints. In contrast to standard methods that often stall,
Elastic OdynSQP
robustly handle both cases.

Elastic inner relaxation.
For inconsistent linearizations of otherwise feasible nonlinear problems, the outer loop behaves as standard

, while the inner
Elastic Odyn
resolves infeasible QP subproblems. On a quadrupedal locomotion task with dynamics, contact, torque, and kinematic

Elastic violations enter the merit function as a squared
\ell_{2}
penalty consistent with inner relaxation, while hard residuals are carried by an adaptive
\ell_{1}

\Phi_{\!E}(\mathbf{w})=J(\mathbf{w})+V_{\!E}(\mathbf{w})+\upsilon_{\!H}\,c_{\!H}(\mathbf{w}),

,

, hard residual norm
c_{\!H}
, and elastic penalty
V_{\!E}=\tfrac{1}{2\gamma_{e}}\|\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{el}}\|_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\gamma_{i}}\|[\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{el}}]_{+}\|_{2}^{2}
. The parameters
\gamma_{e}
,
\gamma_{i}
are tightened as elastic feasibility improves, while
\upsilon_{\!H}
follows a generalized Nocedal-Wright rule
[
5
]
whenever

. The predicted elastic decrease along the step,

augments the expected cost decrease in a non-monotone line search that accepts when progress is realistic.

VI
Conclusion

We introduced
Elastic Odyn
, a smooth
\ell_{2}
-based extension of a primal–dual non-interior-point QP solver that remains robust under infeasibility, degeneracy, and ill-conditioning. The method computes closest-to-feasible solutions, supports efficient warm starting, and recovers interpretable dual variables through a lightweight refinement stage. Its smooth formulation further enables stable differentiation through infeasible optimization problems, yielding a differentiable QP layer suitable for learning-based applications. Building on this foundation,
Elastic OdynSQP
extends these capabilities to nonlinear optimal control, enabling reliable convergence in the presence of inconsistent linearizations and intrinsically infeasible tasks. Together, these results establish a unified framework for optimization, simulation, control, and learning beyond the feasibility assumptions of existing methods.
