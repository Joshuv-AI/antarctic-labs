The trap device retrieves the detached stent that is floating within the ascending aorta
Authors: Li-an Liao, Meng Liang, Rong-tai Luo, Hua-qing Yao, Xin-ping Lan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary stent dislodgement is a relatively infrequent iatrogenic complication associated with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). It can result in severe outcomes, including vascular embolism, coronary rupture, malignant arrhythmia, and even death.Case summaryWe reported a patient who previously underwent stent implantation in the left anterior descending artery (LAD) and required a second-stage surgery. During the insertion of a new stent into the left circumflex artery (LCX), the guidewire passed through the mesh of the original stent. This caused the new stent to pull and deform the original stent during advancement. Consequently, the new stent dislodged, and the two stents intertwined and floated in the aorta. Eventually, the two stents were successfully removed using a stent snare, and a new stent was reimplanted in the LAD, thereby avoiding the risk of the dislodged stent falling into other arteries and causing embolism. In our case, the characteristic of the original stent protruding into the left main coronary artery (LM) and the surgeon’s failure to promptly confirm the position of the guidewire were the main causes of this incident, which provide valuable experience for future interventional surgeries.DiscussionWhen the original stent located at the bifurcation of the blood vessel is passed through, preoperative assessment of the spatial relationship between the blood vessels, careful confirmation of the position of the guidewire, and timely analysis of whether stent dislodgement, traction, etc. occurs when advancement is impeded are critical to procedural success.Keywords: Coronary heart disease, Percutaneous coronary intervention, Stent dislodgement

Background

Coronary stent dislodgement is a relatively infrequent yet serious iatrogenic complication of PCI [1], with a reported incidence ranging from 0.2% to 0.5% []. Common aetiologies of stent dislodgement include detachment of the stent from the delivery balloon [3], entrapment and separation of the stent from the balloon during withdrawal [4], and stent deformation [5], which can lead to peeling at the guide catheter opening. Once dislodgement occurs, severe clinical complications such as vascular embolism, rupture causing acute cardiac tamponade [6], and malignant arrhythmias can develop, with potentially fatal consequences. Furthermore, the management of such complications is notably complex.

We report a complex case in which new stent dislodgement and original stent underwent longitudinal stretching and deformation during second-stage PCI. This problem was successfully addressed through a safe and effective method.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old male patient was diagnosed with acute anterior wall myocardial infarction in our department on June 23, 2025. At that time, coronary angiography (CAG) demonstrated subtotal occlusion of the proximal to mid-LAD, severe stenosis of the distal LCX, total occlusion of the obtuse marginal branch, and proximal occlusion of the right coronary artery (RCA). A Firebird2 3.5 mm×33 mm stent was deployed at the LAD (Fig. 1). The patient was readmitted on July 28, 2025, for second-stage PCI of the RCA. Upon admission, a physical examination revealed no abnormalities. Laboratory tests revealed a cTnI concentration of 0.073 ng/ml and a B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) concentration of 235.3 pg/ml. ECG showed sinus rhythm with ST-T changes. Ultrasound cardiogram (UCG) demonstrated an LVDd of 62 mm, LVDs 49 mm, ejection fraction (EF) of 40%, and merged left ventricular apical aneurysm. The admission diagnosis included coronary heart disease, postcoronary stent implantation status, and heart function classified as class III.

Incident Description: On July 31, a CAG was performed. Intraoperatively, the original LAD stent (hereinafter referred to as Stent 1) was found to be patent, severe stenosis was observed in the distal LCX, and there was an occlusion at the origin of the RCA. Through collateral circulation, the distal RCA and its branch shadows were visible. Multiple guidewires (Pilot 50, Pilot 150, Fielder XT, and Gaial) were inserted, but they were unable to traverse the proximal RCA occlusion, resulting in the failure of antegrade recanalization. Consequently, it was decided to first address the LCX lesion and subsequently proceed with a retrograde approach for the RCA. A guidewire was successfully navigated to the distal LCX. Utilizing the guidewire, a Firebird2 2.5 mm×18 mm stent (hereinafter referred to as Stent 2) was inserted. However, during the deployment of Stent 2, it could not be advanced beyond the proximal LCX to reach the distal segment. The decision was made to retract the stent to reassess the situation. During the retraction process, Stent 2 became stuck and could not be withdrawn. Upon applying slight traction to the stent balloon, Stent 2 became dislodged, with the stent visible within the guiding catheter. The entire system was subsequently extracted from the patient; however, Stent 2 was not recovered. A meticulous examination of the image revealed that Stent 2 had traversed the mesh of Stent 1 and had become proximally entangled with it. During the retraction process, the applied force resulted in the deformation of Stent 1 into a linear configuration, causing it to float alongside Stent 2 in the ascending aorta (Fig. 2). The surgeon judged that immediate retrieval of the stent was not feasible. The patient did not experience any discomfort, and vital signs remained stable. Therefore, the surgery was concluded, and a continuous infusion of tirofiban and a regimen of aspirin and clopidogrel for antiplatelet therapy were administered. Upon immediate re-evaluation, cTnI, BNP, ECG and UCG levels did not significantly change compared with those before.

a) Part of the LAD Stent 1 is stretched and deformed; b) LAD Stent 1 is stuck and stretched, causing deformation

Multidisciplinary procedures were conducted; if cardiovascular surgery was to be performed, the procedure involved an incision of the ascending aorta for stent removal and CABG for the LAD, LCX, and RCA, as well as resection of a ventricular aneurysm. The surgery is deemed complex and high risk. The final decision was to perform multicatheter coronary angiography and remove the two stents using a snare device. On August 1, CAG was performed for stent capture. During the procedure, a 6 F EBU3.5 catheter was advanced through the right radial artery to the left coronary sinus, a SION guidewire was positioned in the distal LCX, and a 7 F EBU3.75 guiding catheter was introduced via a right femoral artery sheath to the ascending aorta. Angiography revealed antegrade flow with a TIMI grade III in the LCX and LAD. The proximal segment of LAD Stent 1 was observed to be elongated and narrowed, interlocking with stent 2 and floating in the left coronary sinus and ascending aorta. A snare was introduced via a 7 F EBU3.75 guiding catheter to the ascending aorta, where it captured the overlapping stent site of Stent 1 and Stent 2, subsequently securing them within the guiding catheter (Fig. 3). When the snare was removed, two fully intertwined stents were observed with visible coronary intima on their surfaces (Fig. 4). Angiography evaluation indicated antegrade flow with a TIMI grade III in the LCX and a TIMI grade II in the LAD, with a suspicion of intimal injury but no definitive dissection evident on imaging. Finally, a stent (Firebird2 3.5 mm×33 mm) was successfully inserted in the proximal to the mid-LAD. The distal LCX received balloon dilation (Emerge 2.5 mm×20 mm). During the entire surgical procedure, the patient remained haemodynamically stable and did not experience chest discomfort.

Discussion

In this case, given the ostial LAD lesion, direct placement of Stent 1 posed a significant risk of disturbing the plaque, potentially resulting in vascular occlusion. Therefore, a portion of Stent 1 was designed to slightly protrude into the main trunk, thereby partially covering the opening of the LCX. This configuration increased the risk that, during subsequent guidewire passage through the LAD or LCX, the guidewire might penetrate the mesh of Stent 1. The patient presented with a short LM, and the guidewire passed through the protruding mesh of Stent 1 upon entry into the LCX. In the absence of predilation, Stent 2 was directly delivered, causing the front part of Stent 2 to pass through the mesh while the rear became entangled with the Stent 1 mesh. During the forceful withdrawal of the balloon, Stent 2 became detached and simultaneously pulled on Stent 1, which resulted in its elongation, thinning, deformation, and eventual detachment. Ultimately, the two stents became intertwined and were left floating in the ascending aorta, posing a persistent risk of Stent 2 dislodging into the cerebral artery, renal artery, or other vessels, potentially causing occlusion. Moreover, Stent 1 presented a risk of in-stent thrombosis, which could lead to myocardial infarction [7]. Although we ultimately managed to remove two stents using the trap device, Stent 1 had been implanted for more than one month and had developed neointimal coverage. Forceful removal posed significant risks, including coronary rupture, dissection, no reflow, intimal injury, acute thrombosis, and infection [8]. Following the removal of Stent 1, the patient experienced coronary injury, intimal detachment, and restricted blood flow. Nevertheless, the immediate implantation of a new stent successfully restored normal forwards blood flow. If the doctor can fully assess the spatial relationship between the LCX and Stent 1 in the LAD before surgery and determine the position of the guidewire as early as possible, it may help reduce the risk of stent disengagement. Moreover, when the stent encounters resistance during its advancement to the distal end of the LCX, the surgeon should immediately stop the further advancement operation and promptly check the situation of the guidewire and the stent.

In recent years, with the increase in the number of complex PCI procedures, we frequently come across case reports on stent dislodgement. Numerous studies have shown that preinstalled stents, better cross-sectional designs, and improved stent delivery systems are key factors in reducing the rate of stent dislodgement. Some reports indicate that compared with the pre-expansion strategy, direct implantation of stents is associated with a greater risk of stent detachment, which is related to the increased resistance when the stent passes through the lesion site [9]. Additionally, studies have shown that when attempting to pass through tortuous vessels and severely calcified lesions, the stent may deform [6]. In our case, the direct implantation of Stent 2 without preexpansion caused obstruction, and the stent fell off when the catheter was retracted. In addition, increasingly advanced stent systems will be key factors in reducing stent detachment. Studies have compared the detachment forces of five different types of drug-eluting stents (DESs), namely, Firehawk, Xience Sierra, Orsiro, Resolute Onyx, and Synergy. The results revealed that the peak detachment force of Orsiro was significantly lower than that of all the other stents. Moreover, some studies have indicated that the thinner the stent is, the higher the rate of stent displacement [10, 11].

Once dislodgement occurs, safe retrieval of the dislodged stent is of paramount importance. When the guidewire remains positioned within the dislodged stent, a small balloon can be advanced into the stent and inflated distally to facilitate its withdrawal into the guiding catheter [12]. Currently, the loop snare technique, is a common retrieval method, a snare is advanced via the guiding catheter to approach and encircle the stent, after which the snare is tightened to secure the stent and retract it into the guiding catheter [13]; this was also the technique employed in our case. It has been reported that the original guidewire within the stent may be retained, while a second soft-tipped guidewire is advanced into the distal branch beyond the stent. The two wires are then twisted together approximately 15 times to form a braided structure, and the stent is withdrawn into the guiding catheter through coordinated traction [14]. For dislodged stents that cannot be retrieved, compression of the stent against the coronary vessel wall by implantation of a new stent may be considered [15]; however, this approach is highly risky and is associated with poor prognosis.

From this case, the preventive measures that can be taken in future preoperative preparation include the following: (1) Conduct a comprehensive assessment of the lesion characteristics. Utilize coronary angiography to determine the degrees of vascular calcification and vessel tortuosity, the lesion location, and the presence of in-stent restenosis; estimate resistance during stent passage, and develop a targeted strategy. (2) Adequately prepare the lesion. For calcified lesions, prioritizing preexpansion, rotational atherectomy, laser, or high-pressure balloon dilation is recommended [16] to enhance the pathway and ensure a smooth channel for stent delivery [17]. (3) Select appropriate equipment. Match suitable guides and stents according to vascular anatomy and lesion characteristics to ensure smooth stent delivery to the designated location [18]. (4) Precisely manipulate the equipment. Push the stent in a gentle, steady manner, avoiding forceful forwards or backwards movements. Confirm the coaxial alignment of the guide catheter after manipulation to prevent the catheter tip from scraping the stent. (5) Make timely adjustments. Continuously observe stent position and vascular response via angiography [19]. If the stent encounters resistance or becomes stuck, adjust the catheter position or redilate the lesion first, and do not force the operation.

Conclusions

Coronary stent dislodgement is a serious complication. Severe coronary calcification, vessel curvature, and an outdated stent delivery system and techniques are related risk factors for stent dislodgement. Additionally, the experience and technical expertise of the operating physician during complex procedures is also a key factor. Currently, the method for treating stent dislodgement involves removing the stent through different techniques. Using a snare to retrieve the stent remains an important approach.
