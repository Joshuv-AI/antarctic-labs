Delayed ischemic stroke ten days after aluminum phosphide ingestion in a previously healthy 21-year-old Ethiopian woman: a case report
Authors: Amanuel Mesfin Oljira, Segni Shube Hunduma, Dereje Gurmessa Geleta, Rabira Waktola Gonfa, Dawit Yosef Barkesa, Bisrat Abate Bekele, Eliab Negusse Reda, Dagim Weldegiorgis Fininsa, Aemon Berhane Fissha, Mathewos Tesfaye Yanore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAluminum phosphide poisoning is a highly lethal toxicologic emergency classically dominated by cardiovascular collapse. Delayed ischemic stroke after apparent clinical stabilization is exceptionally rare and mechanistically uncertain.Case presentationA 21-year-old previously healthy Ethiopian woman intentionally ingested five aluminum phosphide tablets. After early gastric lavage, she remained hemodynamically stable during the initial 24 hours and was discharged. Ten days after ingestion, she developed sudden right facial weakness, right hemiparesis, and mild dysarthria. On day 11, her National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score was 11. Contrast-enhanced brain computed tomography demonstrated acute-to-subacute hypodensity in the left lentiform nucleus, caudate head, and anterior limb of the internal capsule. Non-dedicated post-contrast computed tomography showed visual luminal asymmetry/narrowing of the left middle cerebral artery compared with the contralateral side. Evaluation with electrocardiography, 72-hour continuous inpatient telemetry, transthoracic echocardiography with agitated saline study, carotid/vertebral Doppler ultrasonography, cerebrospinal fluid analysis including acid-fast bacilli testing and GeneXpert, human immunodeficiency virus testing, tuberculosis screening, antiphospholipid antibodies repeated at 12 weeks, thrombophilia testing, and autoimmune markers did not identify an alternative cause within the limits of available investigations. Cerebrospinal fluid culture for tuberculosis and other bacteria, computed tomography angiography/magnetic resonance angiography, transcranial Doppler, serial vascular imaging, and prolonged outpatient Holter monitoring were unavailable. She received aspirin, venous thromboembolism prophylaxis, later atorvastatin 80 mg orally once daily, and structured rehabilitation. The modified Rankin Scale score improved from 4 at the 30-day functional assessment to 2 at three and six months.ConclusionsThis case describes delayed deep MCA-territory ischemic stroke temporally associated with aluminum phosphide ingestion. The observed computed tomography (CT) luminal asymmetry is hypothesis-generating only and cannot distinguish toxin-associated vasospasm/arteriopathy from reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome, inflammatory vasculopathy, dissection-related disease, or pseudostenosis artifact. Clinicians should maintain vigilance for delayed focal neurologic deficits after aluminum phosphide exposure and pursue neuroimaging and etiologic evaluation, including dedicated vascular studies and follow-up vascular imaging where available.Key clinical messageDelayed focal ischemic stroke may occur after apparent clinical stabilization following aluminum phosphide ingestion, even without early hemodynamic collapse or structural heart disease. New lateralizing neurologic deficits after phosphide exposure should prompt urgent neuroimaging and structured young-stroke evaluation. In this case, the association with aluminum phosphide remains temporal and biologically plausible but not causal or mechanistically proven, because dedicated vascular imaging, serial angiography, and follow-up vascular imaging were unavailable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13256-026-06403-2.

Background

Aluminum phosphide (AlP) is a solid fumigant widely used for grain storage in many low- and middle-income countries; its low cost and easy accessibility have made it a major agent of deliberate self-poisoning. In Ethiopia, a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis reported a high burden of metallophosphide poisoning among poisoning admissions and substantial mortality [1].

Following ingestion, AlP reacts with gastric moisture and hydrochloric acid to release phosphine gas, which inhibits cytochrome c oxidase, depletes adenosine triphosphate, and generates reactive oxygen species, producing multisystem injury [2, 3]. The typical clinical course is dominated by gastrointestinal irritation, cardiovascular collapse, refractory hypotension, arrhythmias, and respiratory failure within hours. [1, 4] Neurologic complications are less common than cardiovascular manifestations, and delayed focal ischemic stroke after apparent stabilization has been reported only rarely [5–7].

We report a previously healthy young Ethiopian woman who developed delayed deep left MCA/lenticulostriate territory infarction ten days after AlP ingestion. The case is presented in accordance with the CAse REport(CARE)guideline [8]. The diagnostic and mechanistic interpretation is deliberately cautious: no alternative etiology was identified within available testing, but several important differentials could not be formally excluded because of resource limitations.

Case presentation

Patient information

A 21-year-old previously healthy Ethiopian woman of Oromo ethnicity presented after intentional ingestion of five TANPHOS aluminum phosphide tablets, each containing 1.68 g AlP, for a total dose of 8.4 g dissolved in approximately 250 mL water (Fig. 1). This was her first self-harm episode. She had no personal or family history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, human immunodefi ciency virus (HIV) infection, autoimmune or rheumatologic disease, migraine, prior stroke or transient ischemic attack, cardiac disease, arrhythmia, or thromboembolism. She had never used oral contraceptive pills, tobacco, alcohol, or khat.Fig. 1TANPHOS aluminum phosphide product container as presented by the patient. Clinical findings and timeline

The complete timeline of clinical events is presented in Table 1. Ingestion occurred at 14:30 hours on day 0. Within minutes she developed epigastric burning pain and reached a nearby health center within approximately 10 minutes. Gastric lavage was performed approximately 15 minutes after ingestion, and a garlic-like odor consistent with phosphine gas was documented. She received one liter of normal saline with 5% dextrose over eight hours. No activated charcoal, oil-based decontamination, vasopressors, or mechanical ventilation were administered. She remained fully conscious, with Glasgow Coma Scale 15/15, and was hemodynamically and cardiopulmonary stable throughout the initial 24 hours before discharge. She remained asymptomatic from days 1 to 9. On day 10 she developed sudden right facial weakness, right upper- and lower-limb weakness, and mild dysarthria. There was no fever, meningism, headache, thunderclap headache, seizure, or altered mental status. On arrival at our tertiary hospital on day 11, blood pressure was 122/74 mmHg, heart rate 82/minute, respiratory rate 18/minute, temperature 36.8 °C, and oxygen saturation 97% on room air. Neurologic examination showed right upper motor neuron facial palsy, right arm and leg power 1/5, mild dysarthria, preserved language, no visual field deficit, no hemisensory loss, and no neglect. Admission NIHSS was 11.

Diagnostic assessment

Laboratory investigations at presentation are summarized in Table 2. Electrolytes, liver function tests, non-LDL lipid parameters, glucose, blood urea nitrogen, calcium, and magnesium were within reference ranges; only diagnostically relevant laboratory values are presented in Table 2 for conciseness. Antiphospholipid antibodies were negative acutely and again at 12-week repeat testing, supporting exclusion of antiphospholipid syndrome by laboratory criteria. Because infectious/inflammatory vasculopathy was considered, cerebrospinal fluid analysis was performed. CSF acid-fast bacilli testing and GeneXpert were negative. HIV testing and tuberculosis screening were also negative. CSF culture for tuberculosis and other bacteria was not performed because it was unavailable in our setting. Therefore, tuberculous meningitis-associated vasculitis and other infectious meningitis-related vasculopathies were considered less clinically supported but not absolutely excluded.

Cardiac and vascular evaluation

A 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) obtained after admission demonstrated normal sinus rhythm at 80 beats per minute, QTc 420 microseconds, and no ectopy, conduction abnormality, or ST-T abnormality (Fig. 2). The patient underwent continuous inpatient telemetry from admission through 72 hours to screen for occult atrial fibrillation and other dysrhythmias; no atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter was detected. Carotid and vertebral Doppler ultrasonography was normal in the visualized cervical segments, but this does not definitively exclude high-cervical/skull-base internal carotid artery disease, subtle dissection, intracranial arteriopathy, or perforator pathology. Modern young-stroke frameworks emphasize dedicated intracranial and extracranial vascular imaging where available. computed tomography angiography/magnetic resonance angiography head and neck, transcranial Doppler, catheter angiography, and follow-up vascular imaging were not performed because they were unavailable or unaffordable for the patient.

Neuroimaging

Contrast-enhanced brain CT performed on day 11 showed acute-to-subacute hypodensity involving the left lentiform nucleus, left caudate head, and anterior limb of the left internal capsule, consistent with deep left MCA/lenticulostriate territory infarction (Fig. 3). Post-contrast images showed visual luminal asymmetry/narrowing of the left MCA compared with the right (Fig. 3). This was not a dedicated vascular study. Pseudostenosis artifact refers to apparent arterial narrowing on non-dedicated vascular CT caused by contrast timing, vessel orientation, partial-volume averaging, or suboptimal arterial opacification rather than true luminal stenosis. Etiologic classification

The stroke was classified as ischemic stroke of undetermined etiology/cryptogenic within the limits of available investigations and temporally associated with AlP ingestion. The available evidence does not establish causality. The etiologic evaluation and residual uncertainty are summarized in Table 3. The non-dedicated CT luminal asymmetry cannot distinguish true arteriopathy, vasospasm, reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome, inflammatory vasculopathy including tuberculous meningitis-associated vasculitis, dissection-related disease, or artifact. Management and hospital course

Because the patient presented approximately 24 hours after symptom onset, intravenous thrombolysis was not administered. Aspirin 325 mg was given as a loading dose followed by 81 mg orally once daily. Low-molecular-weight heparin 7500 IU subcutaneously twice daily was used for venous thromboembolism prophylaxis. Physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech therapy, and psychiatric follow-up were initiated. Dysphagia resolved by hospital day 5. Right-sided power improved to 3/5 by hospital day 8 and dysarthria resolved. She was discharged on hospital day 10/day 20 from ingestion with NIHSS 4, mRS 3, Barthel index 65/100, right power 3/5, resolved facial weakness, resolved dysarthria, and hemiparetic gait.

Atorvastatin 80 mg orally once daily was not initiated during the earliest inpatient decision-making because the treating team initially classified the stroke as non-atherosclerotic, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol was optimal at 70 mg/dL, and the patient was young without conventional vascular risk factors. During follow-up, atorvastatin 80 mg orally once daily was started and was tolerated. This management decision warrants cautious reflection because statins may have endothelial-stabilizing, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidative effects beyond lipid lowering, which could be relevant in ischemic stroke and in a hypothesized phosphine-related endothelial injury model.

Follow-up and outcomes

Outpatient neurologic, functional, and medication follow-up is summarized in Table 4. At the 30-day functional assessment/window, she was wheelchair-dependent in the community, partially caring for herself, and had not returned to prior activities (mRS 4). At the first outpatient review two weeks after discharge/day 34 from ingestion, right power improved to 4/5 and NIHSS was 2; blood pressure and LDL-C were normal, and she reported no recurrent TIA/stroke symptoms and no headache or thunderclap headache. Aspirin was taken consistently and tolerated without bleeding, dyspepsia, allergy, or other medication-related adverse effects. At 6 weeks/day 51, power remained 4/5 and NIHSS 2, with no recurrence. At 3 months, she walked independently with a hemiparetic gait, NIHSS 2, mRS 2, normal blood pressure, normal LDL-C, and no recurrence. At 6 months, she remained fully ambulatory with hemiparetic gait, NIHSS 2, mRS 2, normal blood pressure and LDL-C, repeat antiphospholipid antibodies were negative, and she continued rehabilitation and psychotherapy. Follow-up CT, CTA, MRA, or TCD was not performed because of financial constraints. Patient perspective

“Surviving the ingestion only to wake up with one side of my body weakened and my speech changed was something I had not anticipated. The right side of my face was different, my arm and leg would not move as I wanted, and my words came out slurred. The slow process of regaining movement—day by day, visit by visit—gave me something to focus on alongside the psychiatric support I was receiving. Being able to walk independently at 6 months was a recovery I had doubted at the time I left the hospital.”

Discussion

This case is clinically notable because delayed deep MCA-territory ischemic stroke occurred ten days after AlP ingestion in a young woman who had no documented hemodynamic collapse, atrial fibrillation/flutter on 72-hour telemetry, cardiomyopathy, or conventional vascular risk factors during the acute phase; the absence of echocardiographic cardiomyopathy is relevant because myocardial dysfunction is a recognized prognostic feature in AlP poisoning [9, 10]. The case supports vigilance for delayed focal neurologic complications after phosphide exposure, but it does not prove causality.

Several biologically plausible but unproven mechanisms may bridge AlP exposure and delayed focal ischemia. Phosphine inhibits mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase, causes adenosine triphosphate (ATP) depletion, and generates reactive oxygen species [2, 3]. Oxidative stress may injure cerebral endothelium, impair nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation, promote vasospasm, activate platelets, and create a prothrombotic endothelial phenotype. In theory, these mechanisms could produce delayed localized vasospasm, perforator dysfunction, or microvascular microthrombi within lenticulostriate branches. Chemical endothelial injury may also evolve over days, providing a possible explanation for delayed onset. However, without CTA/MRA/TCD, serial vascular imaging, or histopathology, these mechanisms remain speculative. Broader AlP treatment literature remains focused on supportive care and investigational antidotal, antioxidant, decontamination, and metabolic strategies rather than delayed cerebrovascular prevention [11–15].

Reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS) is an important alternative mechanism in young adults with ischemic stroke and can be triggered by toxic or vasoactive exposures [16, 17]. Although thunderclap headache is typical, it may be absent, unreported, or not elicited. The absence of headache or thunderclap headache throughout follow-up lowers clinical support for RCVS but does not exclude it. RCVS cannot be formally excluded without dedicated angiographic imaging and follow-up imaging demonstrating reversibility or resolution of segmental vasoconstriction [18].

Tuberculous meningitis-associated vasculitis and other infectious meningitis-related inflammatory vasculopathies also require explicit consideration in this Ethiopian/sub-Saharan African context because tuberculous meningitis (TBM)-associated stroke commonly involves perforating arteries and deep structures including basal ganglia/internal capsule regions [19–21]. In this patient, the absence of fever, meningism, headache, seizure, altered mental status, or progressive neurologic decline; negative HIV and tuberculosis (TB) screening; negative CSF acid-fast bacilli (AFB) and GeneXpert; and functional improvement to modified Rankin Scale (mRS) 2 at six months without anti-tuberculous therapy all argue against active TB meningitis. However, because CSF culture for tuberculosis and other bacteria was unavailable, infectious meningitis-related vasculopathy cannot be excluded with absolute certainty.

The visual MCA luminal asymmetry on post-contrast CT should therefore be interpreted cautiously. It may represent toxin-associated vasospasm or arteriopathy, but it may also reflect RCVS, inflammatory vasculopathy, dissection-related disease, or pseudostenosis artifact. Young-stroke phenotyping frameworks emphasize dedicated intracranial and extracranial vascular imaging and rhythm monitoring when available, especially for strokes of undetermined cause in young adults [17]. In this case, the arteriopathy hypothesis is hypothesis-generating only.

Limitations

This report has important limitations. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with diffusion-weighted imaging was unavailable, so diagnosis rests on CT. CTA/MRA head and neck, transcranial Doppler, catheter angiography, serial vascular imaging, and follow-up CT were not performed; thus true arteriopathy, vasospasm, RCVS, inflammatory vasculopathy, subtle high-cervical/skull-base ICA disease, dissection, and pseudostenosis artifact could not be distinguished. CSF AFB and GeneXpert were negative, but CSF culture for tuberculosis and other bacteria was unavailable; therefore, tuberculous meningitis-associated vasculitis and other infectious meningitis-related vasculopathies cannot be excluded with absolute certainty. Continuous inpatient telemetry for 72 hours did not detect AF/AFL, but prolonged outpatient Holter monitoring was unavailable, leaving residual uncertainty about intermittent paroxysmal arrhythmia. Arterial blood gas and serum lactate were unavailable during the acute poisoning phase. Standardized rehabilitation scales beyond NIHSS, mRS, Barthel index, and Medical Research Council (MRC) power grading were not administered. Atorvastatin 80 mg orally once daily was started later rather than immediately, which should be interpreted in light of evolving management considerations. As a single case report, no causal inference can be drawn.

Conclusion

Delayed ischemic stroke can occur after apparent clinical stabilization following aluminum phosphide ingestion. In this patient, no alternative cause was identified within available investigations, but the mechanism remains uncertain. Visual MCA luminal narrowing on non-dedicated post-contrast CT is hypothesis-generating only and requires angiographic confirmation. Dedicated vascular imaging, serial vascular assessment, CSF microbiological studies where available, and prolonged rhythm monitoring are important to refine etiologic classification in similar young-stroke presentations.
