Multimodal multitask deep learning for grading management system in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Xinyue Liu, Fang Dai, Jiawei Dai, Huoqiang Wang, Qiang Li, Yaokai Wen, Haoyue Guo, Lishu Zhao, Hao Wang, Kandi Xu, Maotao Weng, Siqiong Yao, Hui Lu, Yayi He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate histologic subtyping, tumor node metastasis classification (TNM) staging and prognostic assessment are central to clinical management of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), but remain challenging because of tumor heterogeneity, limited biomarker performance and diagnostic uncertainty. Here we show that a multimodal, multi-task deep learning scoring system (MM-DLS), integrating pretreatment PET/CT images with clinical variables, enables non-invasive prediction of NSCLC subtype, stage and survival risk. We develop and validate MM-DLS in 4,164 patients from multiple centres. In the external validation cohort, MM-DLS achieves area under the receiver operating characteristic curve values of 0.86 for histologic subtype classification and 0.86, 0.86, and 0.88 for stages I–II, III, and IV, respectively. The model also shows consistently strong discrimination for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival across treatment regimens. These results indicate that MM-DLS provides an integrated framework for subtype prediction, staging, and prognostic stratification in NSCLC.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains the foremost cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality globally. Approximately 30–40% of patients present with advanced disease, for whom the 5-year survival is ~5%1. Guideline-based management, integrating histologic subtype, tumor–node–metastasis (TNM) stage, and prognosis, is crucial for optimizing treatment decisions and improving resource utilization. However, current methods are limited by small sample sizes, high heterogeneity, and challenges in repeated sampling2,3. In addition, the expression of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), which is currently used to guide immunotherapy4,5, is affected by many factors such as detection platform, biopsy site, sampling time, and subjectivity of pathological interpretation, leading to limited predictive ability6–8. Only 30%-40% of patients with high expression can achieve durable benefit9. Taken together, these constraints hinder the ability of individualized guideline-based management of patients with NSCLC.

Metabolic imaging methods, such as 18F-FDG PET/CT, have been used to address these challenges but are limited by lack of anatomical detail10. Recently, radiomics methods have also been applied to extract high-dimensional features from imaging datas for prognostic modeling or efficacy prediction11,12. However, most of these methods rely on manual definition of features, which suffer from problems such as poor reproducibility13, and often focus on a single imaging modality, ignoring the synergistic relationship between structural, metabolic, and clinical information14. Additionally, there is a lack of large-scale, multi-center studies comparing models based on combined imaging modalities.

The rise of artificial intelligence, especially deep learning (DL), has brought new opportunities for the diagnosis and treatment of lung cancer15,16. DL models can automatically extract complex and high-level potential features from large-scale images, and have shown excellent performance in tasks such as tumor classification, boundary recognition, and treatment response prediction17–19. Kumar et al. applied transfer learning with ResNet-50, ResNet-101, and EfficientNet-B3 to classify lung cancer subtypes based on CT images, achieving up to 92% accuracy for lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), but without addressing TNM staging or survival prediction20. Ma et al. developed a CT-based radiomic prognostic index to predict the benefit of postoperative radiotherapy in NSCLC patients using a multivariate Cox model, achieving a C-index of 0.703 in the external validation cohort21. Kinoshita et al. constructed an artificial intelligence prognostic model for surgically resected NSCLC using CT radiomics and clinicopathologic features, achieving an AUC of 0.71 for 5-year survival prediction22. To date, there is still a lack of high-dimensional heterogeneous data and multi-task deep models that can truly integrate structural imaging, metabolic parameters, and clinical variables23,24. In particular, there is no widely accepted AI-assisted system that has been shown to be stable and reliable across multi-center cohorts, and that can directly support guideline-based management in real-world practice.

In this work, we address challenges in NSCLC subtyping, staging, and prognostic estimation by developing MM-DLS, a multimodal and multi-task deep learning system based on 4164 patients who underwent pretreatment PET/CT. The model fuses CT structural images, PET metabolic parameters, and key clinical variables, achieving synchronous modeling and prediction of histological subtypes (lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and LUSC), clinical stages (I–II, III, IV), and individualized disease-free survival (DFS)/overall survival (OS) risk, producing a continuous MM-DLS risk score that enables three-tier (low/medium/high) stratification for clinical decision support. In terms of methods, a multimodal feature-sharing mechanism and task-specific branches are introduced into the modeling process to improve the robustness and transferability. In clinical application, MM-DLS aims to provide a high-throughput, non-invasive, and scalable auxiliary evaluation method for integrated subtype and stage assessment, dynamic risk stratification, and individualized prognostic evaluation of advanced NSCLC patients, thereby supporting hierarchical, image-driven management in routine clinical workflows. Results

The MM-DLS framework systematically integrates multi-source heterogeneous data, including high-resolution CT images, PET metabolic indicators such as SUVmax, and clinical variables, to achieve comprehensive guideline-based management of NSCLC. The architecture consists of a lung-lesion joint segmentation module and a multimodal multi-task prediction network. By fusing structural, metabolic, and clinical features, MM-DLS simultaneously predicts histologic subtype, TNM stage, and survival outcomes (DFS and OS) within a unified pipeline. To comprehensively assess prognostic performance, we conducted comparative experiments against three representative published models, all re-trained on our dataset using identical training–validation–test splits for fair comparison. The proposed model achieved superior DFS and OS prediction performance, with C-index values of 0.756 and 0.780, respectively, indicating that multimodal information integration and cross-modal attention fusion improved survival prediction compared with radiomics-based and single-modality deep learning models. We also conducted a comparative study of the lung–lesion joint segmentation model against representative existing models, including CNN-, 3D U-Net-, SAM-, and YOLO-based approaches, which were fully re-trained or fine-tuned using identical data partitions. The proposed model achieved the highest tumor lesion segmentation accuracy, with a Dice similarity coefficient of 0.870 and an IoU of 0.774, and maintained high lung contour segmentation performance, with a Dice similarity coefficient of 0.975 and an IoU of 0.951. Detailed model design and statistical analysis are described in the Methods section, and the ablation, prognostic comparison, and segmentation comparison experiments are presented in the Results section and Table S1 and Table S2.

Figure 2a displays superior discrimination ability in all four sets of data, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.92 on the training set, 0.85 on the validation set, 0.89 on the internal test set, and 0.86 on the external test set. The AUC coefficient of variation (CV) of the four groups is 0.03, indicating elegant generalization performance. The Brier scores are 0.119 (training), 0.158 (validation), 0.139 (internal testing), and 0.139 (external test), respectively, which further support that the model’s reliable prediction probability.

Figure 2b presents PR curves, reflecting the accuracy and recall ability of the model for positive cases (adenocarcinoma) identification. In the training, validation internal and external test sets, the Average Precision (AP) were 0.86, 0.82, 0.85, and 0.81, respectively. The AP of the validation set was the highest, indicating that the model could identify most positive cases while maintaining a low false positive rate on this dataset. The specificity was the highest (0.88) in the validation cohort, and the negative predictive value (NPV) was maintained between 0.76 and 0.80. The AP CV was 0.02.

For reliability, we applied Temperature Scaling (TS) to calibrate the model outputs. Figure 2c displays the calibrated reliability diagrams, with Expected Calibration Error (ECE) values of 0.095 (training), 0.044 (validation), 0.064 (internal test), and 0.025 (external test). Adaptive Calibration Error (ACE) metrics ranged from 0.125 to 0.161 across cohorts, indicating the model’s reliable probability estimates, though calibration varied across datasets.

Figure 2d exploits the decision logic of the SHAP. PET-SUVmax (SHAP = +1.39, P < 0.001) and Lesion Feature (SHAP = +1.18, P < 0.001) were the strongest predictors. In the internal test set (n = 163) of Immune group, DLS stratification significantly distinguishes DFS prognosis of patients (Fig. 5a). The Hazard Ratio (HR) comparisons revealed strong discrimination: Medium vs. Low (HR = 1.18, P = 0.711) and High vs. Low (HR = 6.66, P < 0.001). In the external test set of Immune set (n = 737) (Fig. 5b), the model still maintained robust discrimination performance, with HR (High vs Low) of 8.22, P < 0.001. Similarly, in the Chemo cohort, the model demonstrated stable prognostic ability, with HR for High vs. Low of 19.99 (P < 0.001) in the internal test set (n = 123, Fig. 5c) and 10.34 (P < 0.001) in the external test set (n = 561, Fig. 5d).

Figure S2a evaluates DFS prediction in the Immune cohort. The 1-, 3-, and 5-year AUCs were 0.77, 0.86, and 0.81 (internal) and 0.80, 0.80, and 0.78 (external), with Brier scores consistently below 0.20, indicating robust reliability. For the Chemo cohort (Fig. S2b), AUCs reached 0.85, 0.87, and 0.94 (internal) and 0.82, 0.85, and 0.84 (external). Brier scores ranged from 0.160 to 0.177, confirming the model’s stability and consistent discrimination across short- and long-term outcomes.

To confirm the statistical reliability of the DFS analysis, we systematically evaluated the Proportional Hazards (PH) assumption using Schoenfeld residuals tests. As detailed in Table S4, the assumption held true for all risk group comparisons across both Immune and Chemo cohorts, with P-values for High vs. Low ranging from 0.087 to 0.707, confirming valid hazard ratios for DFS.

SHAP analysis of the combined internal and external test sets (n = 1584; Fig. S3a) revealed that TNM stage (+1.85), pathological subtype (+1.40), and PET SUVmax (+1.22) were the most important features for DFS. These features are consistent with core oncologic factors: TNM stage reflects tumor spread, pathological subtype reflects aggressiveness, and PET SUVmax quantifies metabolic activity. Their prominence in SHAP results reflects the integration of clinical, imaging, and biological factors within the multimodal model, which drives accurate DFS prediction.

Beyond the performance of the MM-DLS itself, stratified analyses based on conventional clinical factors further contextualized its prognostic value for DFS. As shown in Fig. S4, TNM stage (I–II vs. III vs. IV) provided only limited and inconsistent discrimination of DFS across the validation and test cohorts in both Immune and Chemo groups, and histologic subtype (LUAD vs. LUSC) showed no significant prognostic separation (all P > 0.05). These findings are consistent with the SHAP results, in which TNM stage and pathological subtype emerged as important contributors to DFS prediction but gained their discriminative power primarily when integrated with PET, CT-derived, and clinical features. Together, this underscores that conventional staging and histology alone are insufficient for accurate DFS risk stratification, and that embedding them within a multimodal deep learning framework is essential to achieve the markedly improved discrimination observed with the MM-DLS.

Time-dependent ROC analysis evaluated MM-DLS performance for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS (Fig. S2c, d). In the Immune cohort, internal AUCs ranged from 0.84 to 0.91 (Brier: 0.146–0.175), while external AUCs remained stable at 0.78, 0.81, and 0.79 (Brier: 0.190–0.209). Similarly, the Chemo cohort demonstrated high accuracy, with internal AUCs of 0.86, 0.87, and 0.89 (Brier: 0.129–0.162) and robust external performance (AUC: 0.77–0.80; Brier: 0.194–0.213), confirming consistent predictive power across diverse timepoints and datasets.

To ensure the validity of the survival curves comparisons, we further verified the PH assumption for the OS risk stratification (Medium vs. Low and High vs. Low) using Schoenfeld residuals tests. As shown in Table S5, the PH assumption was satisfied for all comparisons across both Immune and Chemo cohorts in internal and external test sets (all P > 0.05). Specifically, P-values for the High-risk vs. Low-risk comparisons ranged from 0.093 to 0.777, indicating no significant violation. This statistical verification confirms that the prognostic distinction between risk groups remains stable and reliable throughout the follow-up period.

The results presented in Fig. S3b revealed that TNM stage (+1.61), pathological subtype (+1.55), and PET SUVmax (+1.35) were the most influential features for OS prediction. TNM staging incorporates the anatomical extent and metastatic potential of the tumor, while the SUVmax reflects the cumulative disease burden. These factors work together on complex biological and clinical processes. Moreover, because TNM staging and pathological classification already integrate all input features, embedding these factors as a central pillar allows for the simultaneous consideration of a large number of other clinical, radiographic, and pathophysiological variables.

A similar pattern was observed for OS. In the conventional stratified analyses (Fig. S5), TNM stage yielded only partial and often non-significant separation of OS curves, and histologic subtype again failed to demonstrate a consistent prognostic effect (all P > 0.05) across Immune and Chemo cohorts. This contrasts with the MM-DLS, in which TNM stage, age, and PET-based volumetric metrics such as MTV were identified as leading contributors to OS prediction in the SHAP analysis, reflecting their enhanced utility when combined with other multimodal features. These results collectively highlight that while TNM stage and pathological subtype are clinically meaningful, they do not provide sufficient prognostic granularity on their own, and that their integration into the MM-DLS architecture enables substantially more precise and stable OS risk stratification than can be achieved by conventional staging systems alone.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and externally validated MM-DLS, a multimodal multi-task deep learning system that integrates pretreatment PET/CT images and clinical data for histologic subtype classification, TNM staging, and survival risk prediction in NSCLC. MM-DLS achieved high-accuracy subtype and stage prediction and showed consistent prognostic performance in patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors plus chemotherapy or chemotherapy alone. These results underscore the potential of integrative imaging-based models for non-invasive risk stratification in NSCLC. With fast inference and an automated end-to-end pipeline, MM-DLS may also serve as an auxiliary decision-support tool in radiology and oncology workflows.

MM-DLS achieved strong discrimination between LUAD and LUSC in the external cohort, reinforcing the concept that pretreatment PET/CT contains identifiable signatures of histologic lineage. The hypermetabolic areas within the tumor may indicate clusters of actively proliferating cancer cells, and the hypometabolic areas may correspond to areas of necrosis or hypoxia26. From a biologic perspective, PET metabolic intensity and lesion-centric CT morphology emerged as dominant contributors, consistent with known subtype-related differences in tumor metabolism and structural features27,28. This provides a mechanistic rationale for why a model that jointly leverages metabolism and structure can outperform single-modality approaches in real-world biopsy-confirmed populations. Clinically, such non-invasive subtype prediction may be useful when biopsy tissue is limited or repeated sampling is difficult, although final diagnosis still relies on pathology. Although large-cell carcinoma and other rare entities were not explicitly modeled due to low prevalence, the current framework is extensible to additional histologies once sufficient labeled data are available, which would further enhance completeness for clinical deployment.

MM-DLS provided consistently good staging performance in groups I-II, III, and IV, indicating that the model captured not only tumor burden but also spatial information related to clinical staging. A key design element is the integration of lesion-lung spatial features and peritumoral lung texture, which may enhance stage discrimination through coding region extension and host tissue response signals that are difficult to quantize by univariate PET measures or lesion-only CT images27,29. Of note, the prediction of stage III was particularly stable, which is biologically plausible because locally advanced tumors tend to exhibit more pronounced metabolic reprogramming and heterogeneous invasion patterns on combined PET/CT30. These findings support the notion that multimodal imaging can complement conventional anatomical staging, especially when integrated into a multitask framework that simultaneously learns signals from histologic and survival monitoring.

In addition to diagnosis and stage, MM-DLS produced a continuous risk score capable of three-tiered stratification of DFS and OS, with clear separation of Kaplan-Meier curves and a large risk gradient between low-risk and high-risk groups. Time-dependent ROC remained high at 1, 3, and 5 years in both treatment cohorts, further demonstrating strong short-to medium-term prognostic accuracy. SHAP analysis suggested that TNM stage, age, and MTV, followed by SUV-based biomarkers and selected clinical variables, were the most influential drivers of OS, consistent with clinical experience that anatomical extent and metabolic burden jointly determine long-term outcomes31.

Beyond the primary lesion, MM-DLS also incorporates peritumoral and surrounding lung features, which may capture additional information related to the local tumor environment and host response. These peritumoral and pulmonary background regions are thought to encode signals related to inflammation, immune cell infiltration, such as CD8⁺T cells and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, and interstitial remodeling, which are associated with therapeutic response and survival15,32. By integrating intratumoral uptake patterns jointly with these peritumoral and lung texture features into a unified risk representation, this model may better capture the underlying tumor immune microenvironment, resulting in more accurate prognostic discrimination than conventional staging alone. Clinically, this risk score may support risk-adapted clinical assessment by identifying patients who may warrant closer follow-up and more careful multidisciplinary evaluation. However, its utility for guiding treatment selection or treatment escalation remains to be established prospectively.

Ablation experiments provide direct evidence that each modality and each CT branch contributes non-redundant prognostic information: removing the lesion module, lung-lesion spatial module, PET feature branch, or clinical variables consistently degraded performance across subtype, stage, DFS, and OS tasks. The full model achieved the best ROC and C-index values (hierarchical classification ROC 0.894; TNM ROC 0.883; DFS C-index 0.756; OS C-index 0.780), while any single-stream variant underperformed. Head-to-head comparisons against representative published models, including the recent habitat imaging-based framework33, further confirm the incremental value of our fusion strategy. While Mu et al. constructed a prognostic model relying on a radiomics pipeline and habitat imaging within a relatively limited dataset, our MM-DLS employs a unified end-to-end deep learning architecture trained on a significantly larger, multi-center cohort. Crucially, unlike their single-task focus on survival, MM-DLS simultaneously outputs histological subtype, TNM stage, and prognostic risk. Consequently, MM-DLS achieved higher C-indices for both DFS and OS with statistically significant margins. Together, these results indicate that multimodal complementarity (structure + metabolism + clinical context) and hierarchical multitask supervision are the main drivers of robustness.

A major strength of this study is the integration of multi-task learning into the MM-DLS framework, enabling simultaneous optimization of multiple clinically relevant endpoints within a shared multimodal feature space34. This approach mimics the standard clinical reasoning pathway: Imaging Phenotype, Histologic Subtype, TNM Stage, and Prognostic Assessment. Unlike standard parallel multi-tasking or simple feature concatenation (late-fusion), this architecture allows upstream diagnostic representations to explicitly guide downstream survival predictions35,36. Unlike parallel multi-tasking or late-fusion methods, this hierarchical architecture ensures that learned features are biologically consistent with disease progression37. Multi-task learning improves data efficiency, reduces overfitting, and enhances model generalizability, especially with high-dimensional multimodal data38,39. Furthermore, the continuous MM-DLS risk score derived from the survival branch can be directly used to stratify patients into low-, medium-, and high-risk groups, thus translating abstract deep learning outputs into clinically interpretable categories. From a workflow perspective40, the unified multi-task architecture streamlines clinical workflows by consolidating staging, classification, and prognostic assessment into a single automated system, offering a practical and efficient tool for integrated subtype assessment, staging, and prognostic evaluation in clinical workflows41,42.

Multiple large prospective cohort studies have shown that anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 ICIs combined with platinum-based chemotherapy could significantly improve OS and PFS in first-line treatment of advanced NSCLC compared with chemotherapy alone6–8. Nevertheless, the patients with TPS ≥ 1% benefit regardless of the level of PD-L1 expression, with no benefit as PD-L1 expression rises6,8,43. In our study, PD-L1 expression did not enhance patient prognosis when ICIs were combined with chemotherapy drugs. However, these analyses were exploratory and based on a subset of patients with available PD-L1 data, which may be underpowered to detect subtle effect modification. One possible explanation is that chemotherapy may alter tumor immunogenicity and the tumor microenvironment, thereby reducing the predictive specificity of PD-L1 as an isolated biomarker44. Thus, these findings highlight the limitations of relying solely on PD-L1 expression for prognostic prediction in patients and further highlight the need for more comprehensive and integrated biomarkers that can reflect the dynamic interplay between tumor biology, host immunity, and therapeutic response. Our results suggest that MM-DLS may provide a more comprehensive framework for prognostic stratification than a single biomarker alone, although its role in informing treatment selection remains to be established in prospective studies.

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, as a retrospective study, we used cross-validation to randomize the data into test, validation, and training cohorts, repeating the process 1000 times to enhance model reproducibility. We also tested the model in an external validation set to demonstrate its robustness. However, prospective confirmatory trials with clinical endpoints are needed. Secondly, genomic features (e.g., EGFR, TMB) were not included due to data heterogeneity in this cohort. Future studies will incorporate these markers to further improve prognostic accuracy. Additionally, all cohorts were from China, and while external validation is robust, calibration may vary in multiethnic or multiregional populations. International validation is necessary before widespread deployment. Lastly, PD-L1 did not significantly stratify DFS/OS in our ICI cohort, but the small sample size limits these findings. Larger cohorts and multivariate models are needed to confirm the predictive value of MM-DLS.

All in all, we have proposed an interpretable, multimodal, and multi-task scoring system that can predict the pathological subtype, TNM stage, and clinical outcome of patients with NSCLC using integrated PET/CT image data. By capturing structural, metabolic, and clinical features, this model addresses key limitations of current systems that rely on fragmented information. This study highlights the potential of advanced AI methods for clinical patient stratification and provides a scalable, reproducible approach for managing NSCLC in diverse populations.

Methods

Patients

This study was approved by the Ethics Committees of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital (Ethics No. K22-241) and Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (Ethics No. KY2023-738-01), with written informed consent obtained from all participants. From February 2017 to June 2020, patients with NSCLC confirmed via percutaneous puncture, bronchoscopy, or surgical biopsy, and with PET/CT images acquired within 1 month before treatment, were enrolled to construct and validate the prognosis model. Clinical follow-up was strictly updated until February 2025 to ensure sufficient observation for long-term survival analysis. Eligible patients were divided into a primary cohort (from the main hospital) and an external test cohort (from an independent hospital). The detailed patient inclusion and exclusion workflow is illustrated in Fig. S6. Exclusion criteria included: (1) inability to receive chemotherapy or immunotherapy; (2) inaccurate pathological diagnosis; (3) poor-quality PET/CT scans. Patients with more than 20% missing clinical data were excluded. For continuous variables with missing values, multiple imputation was performed using the multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE) algorithm. OS was defined as the primary outcome.

The primary cohort included 2866 patients (randomized into training set n = 2293; validation set n = 287, and test set n = 286 at an 8:1:1 ratio), while the external test cohort comprised 1298 patients. Within the primary cohort, patients receiving first-line platinum-based chemotherapy were assigned to the Chemo group (n = 1229), and those undergoing first-line anti-PD-L1/PD-1 immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy were allocated to the Immune group (n = 1637). The external test cohort similarly included an Immune subgroup (n = 737) and a Chemo group (n = 561). Patient baseline information for the two cohorts is provided in Table S7.

Baseline demographics revealed a male predominance across all cohorts (primary cohort: 80.1% male; external test cohort: 71.1% male), consistent with epidemiological patterns of NSCLC. As previous studies have established no inherent gender bias in the biological behavior or treatment response of NSCLC within this context, gender-specific subgroup analyses were not prioritized. Key clinical characteristics—including the number of pulmonary lesions, smoking history, pathological subtypes, TNM stages, and survival outcomes—were well-balanced between the Immune and Chemo groups in both the primary and external cohorts (all P ≥ 0.05), confirming comparable baseline distributions. Detailed demographic and clinical data are summarized in Table S8 (primary cohort) and Table S9 (external test cohort).

IHC assays were conducted by the Dako 22C3 antibody from the Dako company. The PD-L1 expression level was evaluated using the ratio of PD-L1-stained visible tumor cells to all visible tumor cells, i.e., Tumor Proportion Score (TPS). To divide the patient data into groups with no, low, and high expression, 1% and 50% TPS were applied as cut-off values25. Two qualified pathologists scanned the slides.

18F-FDG PET/CT imaging

Before administering FDG injection, the patients must fast for at least 6 hours prior to giving FDG injection and keep their blood glucose stable. After 60 ± 5 min of 18F-FDG (3.7 MBq/kg) intravenous injection, images were obtained by the PET/CT scanner (Biograph 64 system with a 21.6 cm axial field of view, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). SUVmax was defined as the maximum standardized uptake value within the lesion, SUVmean as the average SUV, and SUVpeak as the mean SUV in a small fixed spherical region around the voxel with the highest uptake. MTV was defined as the volume of lesion voxels above a predefined SUV threshold, and TLG was calculated as MTV multiplied by SUVmean. All quantitative parameters (SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVpeak, MTV, TLG) were derived from DICOM-formatted PET/CT images using the Siemens Syngo.via post-processing workstation. The extraction process adhered to the European Association of Nuclear Medicine (EANM) guidelines for tumor imaging45.

Radiomics feature extraction and the nuclear medicine parameters

ROI of CT and PET were imported into the open-source platform, PyRadiomics in Python46, and 107 radiomic features were extracted for each. To find the most accurate prognostic features and avoid overfitting, we used the high-dimensional data regression tool, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) algorithm47. Meanwhile, we performed the 10-fold cross-validation 1000 times and totaled the occurrences of each feature. Additionally, features that appeared 10 or more times were selected to minimize selection bias and increase the stability of the model.

Data feature extraction process

CT imaging data were acquired in DICOM format from clinical institutions and converted to PNG using pydicom48 to preserve essential metadata. A preprocessing step was applied to remove extraneous machine artifacts by masking the lung region using an automatic segmentation algorithm. The images were resized to 512×512 pixels using bilinear interpolation, and pixel intensity values were normalized to a range of [0, 1]. Data augmentation, including random rotations, flips, and elastic deformations, was performed to improve model robustness49. Quality control checks were conducted on a subset of images to ensure consistency50. After preprocessing, the dataset was prepared for feature extraction and model training.

Establishment of MM-DLS

The multi-modal and multi-task deep learning scoring system (MM-DLS) was developed to integrate anatomical imaging, spatial context, handcrafted radiomics, metabolic biomarkers, and clinical variables for comprehensive prognostic modeling in NSCLC (Fig. S7). The fused multimodal representation is simultaneously projected into three independent predictive heads. Two heads estimate risk scores for DFS and OS using Cox proportional hazards regression14, while the third performs binary risk stratification classification using a sigmoid-activated linear layer trained with binary cross-entropy loss. Classification performance is evaluated using the area under the ROC, accuracy, and F1-score. Survival prediction accuracy is assessed using C-index58, calculated by pairwise ranking consistency between predicted risk scores and observed event times.

To optimize model parameters, a composite loss function was minimized, combining Cox loss for survival tasks and binary cross-entropy loss for classification. MM-DLS employs a hybrid multi-task loss strategy for end-to-end optimization. The total loss function comprises: (1) cross-entropy loss for the histological subtype classification task; (2) multi-label classification loss for the TNM staging sub-tasks; and (3) Cox partial likelihood loss for DFS and OS risk prediction. Crucially, instead of fixed manual weights, the contribution of each task to the total loss is balanced using uncertainty weighting. This mechanism allows the model to dynamically learn the task-dependent homoscedastic uncertainty, thereby automatically finding the optimal trade-off between diagnostic accuracy and prognostic discrimination during training. Random rotations, flips, and elastic deformations were applied for data augmentation. After training, model performance was evaluated on independent validation and test cohorts, demonstrating high accuracy across multiple tasks. The core innovation of this architecture lies in its ability to fuse highly heterogeneous data sources through attention-based slice aggregation and joint multimodal integration, yielding robust and interpretable prognostic predictions.

SHAP-based model interpretation

To enhance the interpretability of the developed models, we employed SHAP59 to quantify the contribution of individual input features across different prediction tasks. For the pathological subtype classification (LUAD vs. LUSC) and TNM stage classification (Stage I–II, III, IV), SHAP values were computed using the TreeExplainer framework based on gradient boosting decision trees. In the context of survival analysis, including DFS and OS, SHAP values were derived from deep learning-based Cox proportional hazards models. Specifically, we adopted the DeepSHAP approach to estimate each feature’s impact on the predicted log-risk function60.

To determine whether a feature’s contribution significantly deviated from the null hypothesis, we performed 1,000 random permutations for each feature61. In each iteration, the feature values were randomly shuffled across samples to disrupt their true association with the label, thereby constructing a “null distribution” of SHAP contributions. The P-value was derived by calculating the proportion of permuted importance scores in the null distribution that were greater than or equal to the observed original SHAP value, thus quantifying the significance level beyond random noise. To further evaluate the stability and uncertainty of the model interpretation, we performed 1000 Bootstrap62 resamplings on the training data. For each bootstrapped dataset, SHAP values were recalculated to obtain the Mean, SD, and 95% CI for each feature’s contribution.

The absolute SHAP values were averaged across patients in the test set to assess global feature importance. Features were then ranked and visualized using color-coded bar plots grouped by semantic categories (e.g., PET imaging biomarkers, lesion and anatomical features, smoking history, and demographic variables). This unified framework facilitated consistent interpretability across classification and survival prediction models, allowing for transparent identification of prognostically and diagnostically relevant factors.

Statistics & reproducibility

This retrospective multi-centre study includes 4164 patients with non-small cell lung cancer who underwent pretreatment PET/CT examination. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size; the sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients available during the study period. Patients with poor-quality PET/CT scans, inaccurate pathological diagnosis, inability to receive chemotherapy or immunotherapy, or more than 20% missing clinical data were excluded. Missing values in the remaining continuous clinical variables were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations. In the primary cohort, patients were randomly assigned at the patient level to the training, validation, and internal test sets at an 8:1:1 ratio, whereas the external cohort was used only for independent validation. This was a retrospective observational study, and investigators were not blinded during data curation or outcome assessment.

All calculations and statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.9; Python Software Foundation).

Baseline continuous variables (e.g., age and tumor size) were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were compared between groups using the two-sample Student’s t-test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test (Tables S7–9). Categorical variables (e.g., sex, smoking status, TNM stage, and PD-L1 expression categories) were compared using the chi-square test when all expected cell counts were ≥ 5, and Fisher’s exact test when any expected count was < 5 (Tables S8, 9). Group-level comparisons of PD-L1 expression were performed using regression-based models (Fig. 4).

Classification performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with robustness quantified by the coefficient of variation of AUC (AUC CV) derived from bootstrap resampling (Figs. 2a, 3a–c; Fig. S2). Classification accuracy (ACC) was reported for multi-class classification tasks (Fig. 3a–c). For class-imbalanced settings, precision–recall (PR) curve analysis was conducted, and average precision (AP), AP coefficient of variation (AP CV), specificity, negative predictive value (NPV), and test-set average precision (Test AP) were reported (Figs. 2b, 3a–c). Model calibration was evaluated on probabilistic outputs. Temperature Scaling (TS) was applied as a post-hoc calibration method without altering prediction ranking, and calibration performance was quantified using the Brier score (Fig. 2c; Fig. 7), Expected Calibration Error (ECE), and Adaptive Calibration Error (ACE), with ECE and ACE computed on TS-calibrated probabilities (Figs. 2c, 3a–b).

Survival outcomes were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method, with shaded bands representing 95% confidence intervals of the Kaplan–Meier estimates, and differences between risk groups were assessed using a two-sided log-rank test (Figs. 5, 6). Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. Pairwise comparisons between Medium vs. Low and High vs. Low risk groups were evaluated using two-sided Wald tests (Figs. 5, 6). No adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals tests at both the global model level and the individual covariate level (Tables S4–6). Overall prognostic discrimination was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index) estimated using 1000 bootstrap resamples, and calibration of survival predictions was assessed using the Brier score computed at predefined evaluation time points (Fig. 7). Hazard ratios derived from Cox regression were reported for the overall model and for models incorporating individual clinical features (Fig. 7).

Model interpretability was assessed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). Feature importance was quantified based on SHAP values, and statistical significance of SHAP-derived feature contributions was evaluated using permutation tests, with permutation-test P values reported (Fig. 2d; Figs. S1a–c, S3a–b). The stability of SHAP importance estimates was assessed using bootstrap resampling (Figs. S1d, S3c).

All statistical tests were two-sided unless otherwise specified, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Model reproducibility was supported by evaluation in internal and external cohorts, with bootstrap and permutation analyses used where appropriate.
