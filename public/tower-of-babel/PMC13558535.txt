Treatment Patterns and Burden of Atopic Dermatitis in Adults in the United Arabic Emirates, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Qatar and Turkey: A Real-World Study
Authors: Peter Anderson, Jenny Austin, Mohamed Elrayes, Soniya Rai, Soydan Ince, Mufaddal Alaithan, Fatimah Al-Breiki, Martin Steinhoff, Mohammed Fatani, Emel Bülbül Başkan, Atlal Al-Lafi, James Piercy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionUnderstanding of real-world clinical practice for patients with atopic dermatitis (AD) in the Middle East is limited. We aimed to describe clinical characteristics, treatment patterns and disease burden for adult patients.MethodsData were drawn from the Adelphi Real World AD Disease Specific Programme™, a cross-sectional survey of qualified dermatologists and their patients in the United Arab Emirates, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Qatar and Turkey between September 2022 and March 2023. Dermatologists reported patient demographics, clinical characteristics and treatment history. Patients reported the Patient-Oriented Eczema Measure (POEM), Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI), EQ-5D-5L, and Work Productivity and Activity Impairment questionnaire.ResultsOverall, 209 dermatologists reported data on 736 patients, of whom 437 self-reported data. Dermatologists subjectively determined that 200 patients had ‘mild’, 283 had ‘moderate’ and 253 had ‘severe’ AD at survey. Mean (SD) patient age was 28.8 (8.1) years; 50% were male and 50% female. Overall mean (SD) POEM scores were 9.7 (5.5), DLQI were 9.7 (5.5), EQ-5D-5L were 0.72 (0.29) and overall work impairment was 46.0% (22.3%). Majority were prescribed topical treatments (89%), 18% received systemic immunosuppressants and 13% received biologics.ConclusionsOur findings highlight the disease burden and need to use more efficacious treatment in the Middle East, to improve clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic, pruritic, inflammatory skin disease usually starting in infancy [1] but also affecting a substantial number of adults [2]. AD is commonly associated with other atopic conditions (such as food allergy, asthma, allergic conjunctivitis, nasal polyposis or allergic rhinitis) [3], as well as high level of patient burden including itch, sleep disturbances, anxiety/depression and reduced function/productivity [4].

The prevalence of AD has been well documented in Europe and North America [5], sitting between 10% and 20% of the population, with most patients (90%) reported to experience mild-to-moderate disease [6, 7]. However, recent research has indicated a comparable prevalence in the Middle East [8, 9], with one multinational epidemiology study of adult patients with AD reporting a prevalence of 11.6% in the United Arab Emirates (UAE), 15.3% in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) and 21.6% in Turkey [10].

Despite this, the Middle East remains underrepresented in AD literature [11], potentially stemming from the vast heterogeneity of the region, with prevalence varying within and between countries [10]. Furthermore, reduced physician knowledge of AD, combined with a lack regionally appropriate diagnostic tools and guidelines [7], may also result in underreporting of AD. In a study in the UAE and KSA, rates of AD resulting from using physicians’ diagnoses were much lower than when a validated tool was employed (the International Study of Asthma and Allergies in Childhood [ISAAC] criteria) [2].

As there are currently no objective diagnostic tests available, diagnosis of AD is predominantly based on physician assessment of skin areas affected [12]. Although the presentation of AD is generally similar across racial and ethnic groups, difficulties diagnosing and misclassification of AD may arise from variations in clinical presentation due to skin tone and geographical location [10, 13]. For patients with darker skin, erythema may appear reddish-blue or purple rather than red [14, 15], perifollicular accentuation of lesions may occur, and papulation, scaling and lichenification are more prominent [12, 16, 17]. Patients are also more likely to present with lesions on extensor surfaces, rather than the typical pattern of predominantly flexural lesions [12, 16–19].

The aim of first-line treatment for AD is to alleviate symptoms through hydration with emollients and moisturizers, short-term use of topical corticosteroids (TCS), and long-term maintenance with topical calcineurin inhibitors (TCIs) or crisaborole (a topical nonsteroidal phosphodiesterase 4 inhibitor) [20]. However, cases of moderate and severe AD are often not controlled with topical therapies and more potent treatments are required, such as phototherapy, general systemic immunomodulators (methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil or cyclosporine) or biologics, such as the subcutaneously administered interleukin-4/13 (IL-4/13) receptor antagonist dupilumab.

At the time of survey, the oral Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors abrocitinib (UAE and Turkey), upadacitinib (UAE, KSA and Turkey) and baricitinib (Europe and the Middle East) had recently been approved for use in adult patients with moderate-to-severe AD [7]. The anti-IL13 biologic tralokinumab has also been approved for use in UAE [21].

Given the paucity of data surrounding AD in the Middle East, and the evolving treatment landscape, we aimed to describe clinical characteristics of patients with AD in the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Qatar and Turkey, highlighting current treatment patterns and patient-reported disease burden.

Methods

Study Design

Data were drawn from the Adelphi Real World AD Disease Specific Programme (DSP)™, a multinational, real-world, cross-sectional survey with retrospective data collection of dermatologists and their patients with AD. The survey was conducted in the UAE, KSA, Kuwait, Qatar and Turkey between September 2022 and March 2023. The methodology has been previously described [22, 23], validated [24] and demonstrated to be representative and consistent over time [25].

Participants

Qualified dermatologists were identified from publicly available lists of healthcare professionals and invited to participate in the DSP. For inclusion in the study, dermatologists were required to be actively involved in the management of patients with AD and have a minimum monthly workload of four to seven adult patients with moderate-to-severe disease, including at least one (UAE, Kuwait, Turkey) or two (KSA, Qatar) patients with severe AD. Dermatologists completed a patient record form (PRF) for the next three (UAE), four (Kuwait, KSA, Turkey) or five (Qatar) consecutively consulting eligible patients.

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were aged ≥ 18 years with physician-diagnosed AD and were either currently experiencing, or had a history of, moderate-to-severe disease. All patients for whom a PRF was completed were invited to fill out a patient self-completion form (PSC) on a voluntary basis. The PSC was completed using pen and paper immediately after the consultation and independently of the dermatologist.

Study Variables

Dermatologist-Reported PRF

Dermatologists completed the PRF using a combination of existing patient clinical records, their own judgement and diagnostic skills, and information collected during consultation. Each PRF included details regarding patient demographic and clinical characteristics, disease severity, body surface area (BSA) affected, comorbidities, disease flares, treatment history and current treatments for AD.

Disease severity was determined according to the clinical judgement of consulting dermatologists; the dermatologists were asked for their overall assessment of the severity of AD for each patient based on their own definition of the terms ‘mild’, ‘moderate’ and ‘severe’, consistent with how severity is determined in routine clinical practice.

To supplement this, Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI) scores were also calculated, to provide an assessment of skin signs and determine the extent of skin involvement. EASI scores of 1.1–7.0 indicate mild disease, 7.1–21.0 moderate and 21.1–50.0 severe.

Comorbidities were split into ‘atopic’ and ‘non-atopic’ conditions. Flares were defined as an acute episode/temporary worsening of symptoms, as per clinical judgement of the respondent dermatologist.

Patient-Reported PSC

Patients completed a PSC, self-reporting disease burden and QoL via patient-reported outcome measures, including Patient-Oriented Eczema Measure (POEM), Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI), EQ-5D-5L (Egypt tariff), Work Productivity and Activity Impairment (WPAI) and Itch Numerical Rating Scale (Itch-NRS).

Patient-Reported Outcome Measures

The POEM questionnaire assesses AD symptom frequency over the past week across seven domains (itch, sleep, bleeding, weeping or oozing, cracking, flaking, and dry or rough skin). Total scores range from 0 to 28, with higher scores indicating greater severity [26, 27].

The DLQI is a dermatology-specific questionnaire that measures the impact of skin disease on daily life in the last week across six domains (symptoms and feelings, daily activities, leisure, work and school, personal relationships, and treatment). Total scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater impact on the patient’s life [28, 29].

The EQ-5D-5L assesses general health status across five dimensions (mobility, self-care, pain/discomfort, usual activities and anxiety/depression) [30]. Health utility scores range from 1 (full health) to 0 (a health state equivalent of death), with scores lower than 0 indicating a state worse than death. As tariffs specific to the countries included in this study were not available, the Egypt tariff was determined to be most appropriate [31].

The WPAI questionnaire measures the impact of disease on work productivity and daily activities over the past week, across four items (absenteeism, presenteeism, overall work productivity, and impact on activities). Scores are expressed as a percentage, with higher scores indicating a greater impairment [32].

The Itch-NRS captures patient-reported average itch severity, and worst itch experienced, over the past 24 h on a numerical rating scale from 0 (“no itch”) to 10 (“worst itch imaginable”).

Statistical Analyses

Analyses were descriptive; continuous variables were described using mean and standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables were reported as the frequency and percentage within each category. Missing data was not imputed; therefore base sizes could differ between variables and are given as appropriate.

Compliance with Ethics Guidelines

The DSP fulfils the definition of a market research survey under the EphMRA Code of Conduct and is therefore conducted in accordance with market research, rather than clinical, guidelines. Market research surveys are exempt from requiring institutional review board (IRB) approval; however, the Pearl IRB conducted a methodological review of the atopic dermatitis DSP and provided an exemption (#22-ADRW-154). The DSP was conducted in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964, and its later amendments. Freely given, specific and informed consent was obtained from each respondent to take part in the DSP and for the processing of their personal data. All data provided by dermatologists and patients were anonymized.

The dermatologists were compensated for their participation according to fair market research rates. Dermatologist and patient data were pseudo-anonymized. A code was assigned when data were collected. Upon receipt by ARW, data were pseudo-anonymized again to mitigate against tracing them back to the individual. Data were aggregated before being shared with the subscriber and/or for publication.

Results

Overall, 209 dermatologists (53 in UAE, 51 in KSA, 26 in Kuwait, 11 in Qatar, 68 in Turkey) provided data for 736 patients. The dermatologists determined that 27% of patients (n = 200) had ‘mild’, 38% (n = 283) had ‘moderate’ and 34% (n = 253) had ‘severe’ AD at survey date. A total of 437 patients voluntarily completed a PSC: 92 patients from the UAE, 126 from KSA, 65 from Kuwait, 30 from Qatar and 124 from Turkey.

Patient Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics, split by country and by physician-reported AD severity, are presented in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. Overall, mean (SD) patient age was 28.8 (8.1) years, 50% were male and mean (SD) time since diagnosis was 3.0 (4.5) years. Across countries, mean (SD) patient age ranged from 26.8 (6.1) years in Kuwait to 29.6 (9.9) years in Turkey, and mean (SD) time since diagnosis ranged from 1.6 (1.1) years in Kuwait to 4.4 (5.5) years in Turkey. Across severity groups, time since diagnosis was reported to be 2.9 (5.2) years for mild patients, 2.8 (3.7) years for moderate and 3.3 (4.6) years for severe patients. A total of 546 patients (74%) were diagnosed with any concomitant condition, ranging from 55% of patients (n = 124) in Turkey to 89% of patients (n = 93) in Kuwait. Concomitant conditions were reported in 65.0% of mild, 72.8% of moderate and 83.0% of severe patients. Overall, mean (SD) EASI score (n = 693 patients) was 7.6 (8.2). Mean (SD) EASI score was reported to be 2.0 (3.1) in physician-reported mild patients, 6.1 (4.9) in moderate patients and 13.5 (9.9) in severe patients. Flares

Flaring at survey was reported for 42% of patients (n = 311) overall, ranging from 35% of patients in UAE to 51% of patients in Kuwait. Notably, a total of 64% of severe patients were flaring compared to 38% of moderate and 21% of mild patients. Tables 1 and 2 show the proportion of patients flaring, duration and frequency over 12 months, split by country and physician-reported severity. Of data available from 618 patients, 61% (n = 374) had experienced a flare in the 12 months prior to survey, with patients experiencing a mean (SD) of 3.0 (1.8) flares during that time. Mean flare duration was 10.4 (13.3) days overall. Notably, in KSA, 77% of patients experienced a flare, with a mean (SD) of 3.9 (1.8) flares in the past 12 months.

When considering dermatologist-reported disease severity, 28% of mild and 57% of moderate patients experienced a flare in the 12 months prior to survey compared to 89% of severe patients (Table 2). Mean (SD) number of flares was 2.6 (1.4) among current moderate and 3.6 (1.8) among currently severe patients, and lasted for a mean (SD) of 10.9 (12.5) and 10.3 (14.0) days, respectively.

Comorbidities

The five most common atopic comorbidities by country and by disease severity are given in Fig. 1a and b, respectively. The most common comorbidities experienced by all patients were allergic rhinitis (30% of patients), allergic contact dermatitis (29% of patients), seasonal allergies (21%), chronic hand eczema (10%), urticaria (8%) and asthma (3%). Allergic rhinitis was reported in 46% of patients in Qatar, seasonal allergies were reported in 37% of patients in KSA and urticaria was commonly reported in Kuwait (19% of patients). For patients with dermatologist-reported mild and moderate AD, allergic rhinitis was common (25% and 27% of patients, respectively) alongside seasonal allergies (22% and 17%). Discussion

Although the patient burden associated with AD has been extensively studied in Europe and the USA, data is lacking in the Middle East. This study describes patient clinical characteristics and disease burden in a large cohort of patients across the United Arab Emirates, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Qatar and Turkey, providing real-world insight into AD treatment and management in this region. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this study is the first to quantitatively assess patient-reported burden in this region.

For the overall cohort, the average EASI score indicated most patients had moderate disease. Although there was general alignment between physician-reported severity and EASI score for ‘mild’ and ‘moderate’ patients, dermatologist-reported ‘severe’ patients, on average, did not meet the minimum score threshold of 21.2 for EASI-determined severe disease (mean score 13.5 [9.9]) [33]. A possible reason for this could be that the EASI utilizes erythema (redness) as one of the main indicators to assess disease severity. As erythema may be less prominent in darker skin tones, this may be more difficult to assess, and therefore has the potential to be underreported in our cohort, which was predominantly of Arabic or Turkish ethnicity [14, 15]. Indeed, when using BSA and POEM scores to determine disease severity, dermatologist-reported moderate and severe patients exceeded the 10% BSA threshold indicative of moderate-to-severe disease, with POEM scores also indicating moderately severe disease.

Irrespective of disease severity, a high level of patient burden was reported across all five countries in our study. Most patients had at least one concomitant condition, nearly half of patients were flaring at the time of survey, both atopic and non-atopic comorbidities were commonly reported, and anxiety/depression were reported in 10% of patients. This is in line with previous reports; a study across Eastern Europe, the Middle East and Africa also reported a considerable clinical burden among adult patients with AD, with 36.9% of these patients reporting at least one comorbidity and 89.0% reporting having experienced a flare in a 12-month period [34]. High comorbidity rates were also reported in a study in Saudi Arabia (31.4% of patients) [11], and a systemic global review reported anxiety and depression to be among the most frequently reported burdens associated with AD, averaging at 24.1% of the study population [35].

The heterogeneity of the humanistic and economic burden between Middle Eastern countries has been identified in other studies [36], and is thought to arise from sociocultural, socioeconomic and environmental variation across the region, as well as differences in healthcare policies and access to specialist services [37].

Differences in healthcare frameworks and policy may explain variation across countries. For instance, in Qatar, implementation of the Qatar National Mental Health Strategy (2013–2018) and the Qatar National Mental Health and Wellbeing Strategic Framework (2019–2022) resulted in increased investment in mental health services both within private practice and at community level in the country, leading to better access to resources to address psychological comorbidities [38]. Consequently, anxiety and depression were reported only for 2% and 4% of Qatari patients, respectively, compared to 23% and 17% of patients in KSA, for instance.

Comparable to other studies, we demonstrated that patients still receive largely topical treatment, regardless of their disease severity. It is of note that the 2021 National Saudi Guidelines for the Management of Atopic Dermatitis [39] state that if AD cannot be controlled adequately with appropriate topical treatments and phototherapy, systemic therapy is recommended. Overall, use of advanced therapies was low, with only 13% of patients receiving a biologic and 7% receiving a JAK inhibitor. Notably, use of systemic therapy—indicated for use in patients with severe disease—was used in less than a quarter of physician-reported severe patients overall. Similar low use of advanced therapies was observed in Europe, with one multicountry study reporting that biologic use in severe patients ranged from 8% (UK) to 39% (Italy), while in moderate patients it ranged from 4% (UK) to 7% (Italy) [40]. This underutilization of systemic treatment and reliance on topical therapies may stem from physician lack of experience, as well as variation in the availability of systemic therapies between countries. Out-of-pocket expenses, availability of specialist services, and regional differences in healthcare insurance coverage and government reimbursement may also play a role [34, 41].

Patients self-reported a high level of AD impact on daily life, health-related quality of life, work productivity and everyday activity. Average DLQI, EQ-5D and WPAI scores indicated a moderate-to-severe impact to patients’ quality of life [42, 43] and work productivity [44], with severe patients reporting a substantial impact to daily living. These impacts are likely driven by multiple aspects of the disease, for instance, impact to work productivity and quality of life has been linked to itch severity and is exacerbated by sleep disturbances due to nocturnal itch, with one study correlating presenteeism and absenteeism with severe itching [35].

In our study, itch was reported as particularly burdensome, especially within a 24-h timeframe, irrespective of disease severity. Itching, postulated to be more severe among individuals with darker skin tones [37], may play an even greater part in disease burden, affecting not only daily living but also work productivity and activity. Taken collectively, this indicates a high patient burden associated with AD across the Middle East that likely worsens with disease severity, and an unmet need to effectively treat and manage itch in these patients.

This research is also subject to the limitations associated with any real-world study. The DSP is based on a pseudo-random sample of dermatologists and patients; minimal inclusion criteria governed the selection of participating physicians and participation was influenced by their willingness to complete the survey. Patient eligibility and disease severity was based on the judgement of the respondent dermatologist and not on a formalized diagnostic checklist; however, this is representative of the dermatologists’s real-world classification of their patients. Physicians were not asked about their reasons for prescribing or not prescribing treatment classes; therefore, we cannot draw any conclusions surrounding physicians’ motivations for the low use of advanced therapies in these patients.

Recall bias, a common limitation of surveys, might also have affected responses of both dermatologists and patients. However, to minimize this, the dermatologists did have the ability to refer to the patients’ records while completing the PRF and patients were asked to complete validated patent-reported outcome measures and reported these data consistent with the stated recall periods. While the authors believe the samples collected are sufficient for meaningful analysis of the research objectives, it is recognized that further research may be required in these countries should the exploration of burden in specific sub-populations be required.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated a substantial disease burden in patients with AD in the Middle East, especially among those with severe disease. We also showed that many patients with AD predominantly received only topical treatments, which do not always adequately control their disease. This highlights the need for these patients to have access to more efficacious treatments to improve clinical outcomes and quality of life, which could include advanced therapies such as biologics and JAK inhibitors. Our data support evidence-based decision-making to ensure patients can access efficacious, targeted treatments, to reduce the burden of AD.
