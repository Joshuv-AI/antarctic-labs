Trajectories of Maternal Placental Growth Factor Levels from Early to Late Pregnancy: Prenatal Factors and Adverse Perinatal Outcomes
Authors: Minhui Lv, Yajie Guo, Wenjun Fan, Xiaoli Gao, Lingyi Fang, Huanrong Li, Cha Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKnowledge on longitudinal trajectories of maternal placental growth factor (PlGF) levels, their prenatal factors, and their associations with adverse perinatal outcomes is lacking. We sought to identify PlGF trajectories, examine associations of prenatal factors with these trajectories, and determine whether these trajectories are associated with adverse perinatal outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 438 pregnant women with multiple PlGF measurements. Group-based trajectory modeling identified distinct PlGF trajectories. Logistic and linear regression examined associations of trajectories with prenatal factors and adverse perinatal outcomes.ResultsThree PlGF trajectories were identified. The high (46.6%) and moderate (40.6%) PlGF level trajectories tracked together until the late second trimester, after which the moderate trajectory plateaued and then declined steeply in the third trimester while the high trajectory remained elevated, peaking in the early third trimester before declining gradually. The low PlGF level trajectory (12.8%) maintained the lowest overall levels, rising modestly to an early peak before declining progressively. Uterine fibroids were associated with higher odds of the moderate and low trajectories, as was a history of late adverse obstetric outcomes. Conversely, a history of early pregnancy loss was associated with reduced odds of these trajectories. Additionally, each 1 kg/m2 increase in pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) was associated with higher odds of the low trajectory. Compared with the high trajectory, the low trajectory was associated with increased odds of placental insufficiency, preeclampsia, and preterm delivery, alongside reductions in neonatal birth weight and length. The moderate trajectory was associated with increased odds of preterm delivery before 37 weeks and reduced neonatal birth weight.ConclusionMaternal PlGF trajectories are shaped by prenatal factors. Both moderate and low trajectories markedly increase the odds of adverse perinatal outcomes, underscoring the potential value of dynamic PlGF monitoring for early risk stratification and identification of high-risk pregnancies.Keywords: placental growth factor, trajectory, prenatal factors, adverse perinatal outcomes

Introduction

Pregnancy is a unique physiological state characterized by a series of complex adaptive changes that are essential for fetal survival and development. The placenta plays a pivotal role in this process, serving as a vital organ that forms the maternal-fetal interface.1 The placenta functions to facilitate the exchange of oxygen, nutrients, and metabolic waste products, produce hormones essential for maintaining pregnancy, and modulate immune responses to prevent fetal rejection.2,3 Consequently, normal placental development and function are crucial for fetal well-being and successful pregnancy outcomes.

Placental growth factor (PlGF), a proangiogenic protein of the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) family, rises throughout normal pregnancy, peaks at 28–30 weeks of gestation, and then gradually declines.4 In various placenta-related disorders such as preeclampsia and fetal growth restriction, PlGF levels are markedly reduced.5,6 Additionally, the observation of low circulating PlGF levels in the context of either preeclampsia or fetal growth restriction is known to be closely related to placental pathological outcomes following delivery.7,8 Moreover, the mechanistic association between low circulating PlGF levels and chronic placental ischemia and hypoxia has been well supported.9,10 Therefore, maternal PlGF measurement has the potential to identify patients with both clinically and morphologically significant placental disease. Despite the established role of PlGF in placental-related disorders, several critical gaps remain. First, existing predictive models for adverse pregnancy outcomes predominantly rely on clinical risk factors combined with a single biomarker assessment at a fixed gestational window, limiting their ability to capture the dynamic progression of placental function. Second, while prenatal factors such as maternal age, nulliparity, body mass index (BMI), spontaneous pregnancy, chronic hypertension, and polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) have been reported to significantly influence first-trimester PlGF levels,11 their impact on longitudinal trajectories of PlGF levels remains poorly characterized. Third, whether distinct patterns of PlGF level trajectories differentially predict adverse perinatal outcomes has not been systematically evaluated.

Therefore, this study aims to delineate trajectories of maternal PlGF levels from early to late pregnancy, examine whether prenatal factors modulate maternal PlGF level trajectories, and evaluate the associations between maternal PlGF level trajectories and adverse perinatal outcomes. Trajectory-based monitoring could enable personalized risk stratification across gestation, offering a more dynamic framework than current single-timepoint strategies. Through an in-depth analysis of the longitudinal changes in maternal PlGF levels combined with a comprehensive assessment of prenatal factors, we aim to provide a scientific foundation for antenatal care and offer novel perspectives on the prevention and reduction of adverse perinatal outcomes.

Methods

Setting and Participants

This was a retrospective cohort study of 4555 pregnant women with PlGF measurements from the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Tianjin Medical University General Hospital, between December 2017 and August 2022. From the initial cohort, we first excluded women based on the following criteria: (1) multiple pregnancies; (2) pregnancy termination for non-medical indications or fetal anomalies; and (3) missing prenatal data or pregnancy outcomes, resulting in 4317 pregnant women for inclusion. Medical-record-based PlGF measurements were abstracted at <16, 16–20, 20–24, 24–28, 28–32, 32–36 and ≥36 weeks of gestation. As accurate estimation of trajectories required a minimum of three time points,12 our study excluded pregnant women who had fewer than three PlGF measurements across the specified gestational windows. A total of 438 pregnant women were eligible for the final analysis. The research flowchart is shown in Figure S1.

Measurements

Measurements of Maternal PlGF Levels

Maternal PlGF levels were measured by immunofluorescence assay. Fasting venous blood samples were collected from pregnant women into EDTA anticoagulant tubes. Maternal PlGF levels were quantitatively analyzed with the Lepzi® Quanti PlGF assay on the Lepzi™ Dry Fluorescent Immunoassay Analyzer (T-Meter). The system has a detection limit of 12 pg/mL and a linear correlation coefficient of 0.95 within the range of 12–3000 pg/mL. All procedures were performed by trained and experienced personnel to ensure standardization and data accuracy. All PlGF concentrations measured in this study fell within the quantitative range of the method.

Prenatal Maternal Characteristics

To comprehensively explore prenatal factors potentially associated with maternal PlGF level trajectories, our analytical framework incorporated a broader range of variables. Therefore, we retrospectively extracted prenatal information from medical records, covering maternal age, parity, pre-pregnancy BMI, assisted reproductive technology, chronic hypertension, PCOS, previous pregnancy loss, and uterine fibroids. Previous pregnancy loss was defined as a history of spontaneous loss (including biochemical pregnancy), excluding terminations for fetal anomalies, non-medically indicated terminations, ectopic or molar pregnancies.

Adverse Perinatal Outcomes

We tested whether maternal PlGF level trajectories are associated with placental insufficiency, preeclampsia, fetal distress, placental abruption, pregnancy loss, preterm delivery before 37 weeks, preterm delivery before 34 weeks, birth weight, birth length and Apgar scores (1 min, 5 min, and 10 min). Signs of placental insufficiency include oligohydramnios, fetal growth restriction, abnormal umbilical artery Doppler waveforms, and abnormal fetal heart rate patterns.13 These signs were monitored during prenatal examinations. Preeclampsia was defined according to the guidelines of the International Society for the Study of Hypertension in Pregnancy (ISSHP).14 Fetal distress referred to fetal oxygen deprivation, manifested by signs such as non-reassuring fetal heart rate, decreased fetal movement, and meconium-stained amniotic fluid.15 Placental abruption was defined as the partial or complete separation of the placenta from the uterine wall before delivery.16 Preterm delivery was defined as either spontaneous delivery or medically indicated termination due to gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, placental insufficiency, or placental abruption. Patients with preterm delivery before 34 weeks were classified as an overall group.

Covariates

Adjustments for potential confounders were carried out based on prior knowledge in published literature and plausible direct/indirect association with the outcome variable. For the association between prenatal factors and maternal PlGF level trajectories, the model was adjusted for sex of newborn, aspirin use, and heparin therapy. The association between maternal PlGF level trajectories and adverse perinatal outcomes was adjusted for gestational age at delivery (except in models for preterm birth), maternal age, parity, previous pregnancy loss, pre-pregnancy BMI, chronic hypertension, assisted reproductive technology, sex of newborn, aspirin use, and heparin therapy. Retrospective extraction of maternal medication history (including aspirin and heparin)17,18 was performed from admission records at Tianjin Medical University General Hospital, and sex of newborn19,20 was obtained from birth records.

Statistical Analysis

Determining PlGF Trajectories

Maternal PlGF levels were transformed to their natural logarithm prior to the analysis, due to their marked skewness in distribution. The ln(PlGF) values were analyzed via group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) using the “traj” plugin in STATA/MP 17.0 software to identify change patterns during pregnancy. GBTM classifies individuals with similar developmental patterns within a heterogeneous population, allowing for better subgroup characterization.21,22 The most appropriate trajectory model was determined by a two-stage model selection, taking into account factors such as trajectory shape (eg, linear, quadratic, or cubic) and group number. The number of dimensions and groups was optimized using Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values and clinical interpretability. Five models with one to five trajectories were attempted until the best-fitting model was identified. The performance of the candidate models is listed in Table S1. The objective criteria for selecting the optimal GBTM model included (1) lower absolute values of Bayesian information criterion and Akaike information criterion indicating better model fit, (2) average posterior probability of assignment >0.7 for each trajectory group, (3) each trajectory group accounting for at least 5% of the sample, and (4) simplicity and clinical interpretability of the model. Three trajectories were recognized.

Descriptive and Association Analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted in R V.4.2.2. The significance level for all statistical analyses was set at P<0.05. Descriptive statistics are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables (depending on their distribution), and as frequency and percentage for categorical variables. For clinical characteristics analysis, the statistical differences among PlGF trajectories were tested with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Linear regression models and binary or multinomial logistic regression models were performed to determine the associations of the PlGF trajectories with prenatal factors and adverse perinatal outcomes, adjusted for possible confounding effects. Subsequently, to evaluate potential effect heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were performed based on the same regression model framework: parous women were stratified by a history of late adverse obstetric outcomes, while women with previous pregnancy loss were stratified by the gestational age at loss. The precise definitions are provided in Table S2.

Results

Description of Study Population

A total of 438 pregnant women were included in this study (Figure S1). As indicated in Figure 1, three PlGF-based trajectories were defined: trajectory 1, high PlGF level trajectory; trajectory 2, moderate PlGF level trajectory; and trajectory 3, low PlGF level trajectory. These trajectories accounted for 46.6%, 40.6% and 12.8% of the participants, respectively. Descriptive statistics of these three trajectories are provided in Table 1. Compared with women in the low and moderate PlGF level trajectories, those in the high PlGF level trajectory were more likely to have a longer gestational age at delivery, a lower pre-pregnancy BMI, a history of pregnancy loss, male infants, and heparin therapy, and less likely to be parous or have uterine fibroids (P<0.05). Moreover, those in the low PlGF level trajectory were more likely to experience adverse pregnancy outcomes (such as placental insufficiency, preeclampsia, fetal distress, pregnancy loss, and preterm delivery) and have poorer neonatal outcomes (such as lower birth weight, shorter birth length, and lower Apgar scores) (P<0.05). However, no significant differences were observed in maternal age, gravidity, chronic hypertension, PCOS, assisted reproductive technology, aspirin use, or placental abruption across the three trajectories of maternal PlGF levels (all P>0.05).

Three distinct latent PlGF level trajectories diverge early and remain well-separated throughout pregnancy. All trajectories showed an initial increase followed by a decrease with advancing gestational days. The high (46.6%) and moderate (40.6%) PlGF level trajectories tracked together until the late second trimester, after which the moderate trajectory plateaued and then declined steeply in the third trimester while the high trajectory remained elevated, peaking in the early third trimester before declining gradually. The low PlGF level trajectory (12.8%) maintained the lowest overall levels, rising modestly to an early peak before declining progressively. Note that maternal PlGF levels were natural log-transformed prior to trajectory analysis.

Prenatal Factors Associated with Maternal PlGF Level Trajectories

Table 2 presents the associations of maternal age, parity, previous pregnancy loss, pre-pregnancy BMI, chronic hypertension, PCOS, uterine fibroids, and assisted reproductive technology with the maternal PlGF level trajectories. The high PlGF level trajectory was defined as the reference group. In the model adjusted for potential confounders, parous status and uterine fibroids were associated with increased odds of moderate and low PlGF level trajectories. Specifically, uterine fibroids showed a significantly stronger association with the low PlGF level trajectory (adjusted OR=4.00, 95% CI: 1.62 to 9.85) than with the moderate PlGF level trajectory (adjusted OR=2.69, 95% CI: 1.35 to 5.35). Parous status showed comparable associations with both PlGF level trajectories (moderate PlGF level trajectory: adjusted OR=3.09, 95% CI: 1.72 to 5.56; low PlGF level trajectory: adjusted OR=3.21, 95% CI: 1.46 to 7.05). In contrast, previous pregnancy loss was associated with reduced odds of both PlGF level trajectories (moderate PlGF level trajectory: adjusted OR=0.56, 95% CI: 0.33 to 0.97; low PlGF level trajectory: adjusted OR=0.46, 95% CI: 0.22 to 0.97). Notably, each 1 kg/m2 increase in pre-pregnancy BMI was independently associated only with higher odds of the low PlGF level trajectory (adjusted OR=1.11, 95% CI: 1.02 to 1.21).

Subgroup analyses revealed significant heterogeneity in the effects of parity and previous pregnancy loss on maternal PlGF level trajectories (Table S2). Compared with nulliparous women, parous women with a history of late adverse obstetric outcomes had significantly higher odds of both moderate and low PlGF level trajectories (moderate PlGF level trajectory: adjusted OR=8.28, 95% CI: 2.47 to 27.73; low PlGF level trajectory: adjusted OR=9.24, 95% CI: 2.30 to 37.15). Conversely, compared with no previous pregnancy loss, only a history of early pregnancy loss was associated with significantly lower odds of both moderate and low PlGF level trajectories (moderate PlGF level trajectory: adjusted OR=0.55, 95% CI: 0.32 to 0.95; low PlGF level trajectory: adjusted OR=0.42, 95% CI: 0.19 to 0.92).

Associations of Maternal PlGF Level Trajectories with Adverse Perinatal Outcomes

We further explored the associations of maternal PlGF level trajectories with adverse perinatal outcomes (Table 3). After adjusting for potential confounders, women in the low PlGF level trajectory had significantly higher odds of several adverse pregnancy outcomes compared with those in the high PlGF level trajectory. Specifically, compared with the high PlGF level trajectory, the low PlGF level trajectory was significantly associated with increased odds of placental insufficiency, preeclampsia, preterm delivery before 37 weeks, and preterm delivery before 34 weeks, with adjusted odds ratios of 5.91, 21.14, 37.91, and 44.44, respectively. In contrast, the moderate PlGF level trajectory was associated only with increased odds of preterm delivery before 37 weeks (adjusted OR=3.21, 95% CI: 1.12 to 9.24). Compared with women in the high PlGF level trajectory, those in the moderate and low PlGF level trajectories gave birth to neonates with birth weights reduced by 108.92 g and 419.12 g, respectively. In addition, neonatal birth length was 1.16 cm shorter in the low PlGF level trajectory than in the high PlGF level trajectory.

Discussion

Prenatal Factors and PlGF Trajectory Patterns

Our findings are highly consistent with results from numerous cross-sectional and cohort studies, while offering deeper insights through dynamic trajectory modeling. Consistent with prior research,4,23,24 our findings demonstrate that PlGF concentrations during pregnancy generally exhibit an initial increase followed by a decrease with advancing gestational age (Figure 1). In this study, we further observed that the three trajectories exhibited significant divergence as early as the first trimester and remained separated throughout pregnancy. Normal placental development is dependent on the recruitment of new blood vessels (angiogenesis) occurring throughout the first and second trimesters of pregnancy.25,26 Placental angiogenesis is a highly regulated process designed to facilitate optimal transport of nutrients, oxygen, and metabolic byproducts between a woman and her fetus, and inadequate placental vasculature is a common placental pathology leading to several pregnancy complications.27 PlGF is a proangiogenic member of the VEGF family and is closely associated with early placental development and angiogenesis, such that low PlGF indicates abnormal placental development and heralds placental dysfunction later in pregnancy.4 Accordingly, we speculate that distinct PlGF dynamic trajectories may reflect heterogeneity in vascular development during placental formation. Specifically, the divergence of PlGF levels in early pregnancy may demarcate a critical “time window” for placental development, while their sustained separation suggests divergent paths in placental vascular development. Therefore, identifying the prenatal factors that predispose women to follow these distinct trajectories may provide insight into modifiable and non-modifiable determinants of placental vascular development.

Notably, our study identified that elevated pre-pregnancy BMI was independently associated with higher odds of the low PlGF level trajectory. From a dynamic trajectory perspective, this finding corroborates the conclusion by Beck et al28 that high early pregnancy BMI is associated with lower PlGF concentrations throughout early pregnancy. Additionally, we found that uterine fibroids were associated with longitudinal patterns of persistently lower maternal PlGF levels. We hypothesize that this association may be attributable to certain types of uterine fibroids (eg, submucosal fibroids that significantly affect the uterine cavity environment), which could interfere with placental function by altering the implantation interface and blood supply. Since this study did not conduct a subtype analysis of uterine fibroids, this hypothesis requires validation in future research through comparisons of fibroids with varying locations and sizes.

An intriguing finding was that being parous was significantly associated with higher odds of following either the moderate or the low PlGF level trajectory during pregnancy. This finding contrasts sharply with previous studies that focused on baseline PlGF levels. For example, a large-scale study by Ekelund et al demonstrated that parity was significantly associated with higher PlGF levels in the first trimester.29 Similarly, Kinugawa et al reported that in pregnancies without preeclampsia/superimposed preeclampsia, while PlGF levels were not associated with parity, parous women exhibited significantly lower sFlt-1 levels and sFlt-1/PlGF ratios during the second trimester.30 These findings collectively point to a potential protective effect of parity on placental development. Further subgroup analysis in this study revealed that increased odds associated with parous women were observed only in those with a history of late adverse obstetric outcomes (Table S2). Reijnders et al also identified that a history of placenta-related pregnancy complications was associated with abnormal placental development and function in subsequent pregnancies.31 Therefore, the underlying factor is not parity itself, but pre-existing severe adverse obstetric outcomes, which may persistently impair PlGF secretion in subsequent pregnancies. In contrast, previous pregnancy loss was significantly associated with reduced odds of moderate and low PlGF level trajectories. Subgroup analysis further showed that reduced odds associated with previous pregnancy loss were observed only among pregnant women with a history of early pregnancy loss (Table S2). Studies have shown that approximately half of early pregnancy losses result from chromosomal abnormalities of the conceptus.32 Therefore, we hypothesize that the protective effect may arise from two aspects. First, early pregnancy loss effectively eliminates embryos with significant genetic or developmental abnormalities, allowing the subsequent pregnancy to proceed on a more viable embryonic foundation. Second, the process may induce beneficial adaptation of the maternal immune system,33,34 potentially enhancing pro-angiogenic responses in subsequent pregnancies.35,36 However, complicated or late pregnancy loss usually stems from severe placental dysfunction, and its persistent pathological stimuli (eg, hypoxia) may establish a pathological foundation at the maternal–fetal interface that is difficult to reverse in subsequent pregnancies and therefore fails to confer a comparable protective effect.

Collectively, these findings indicate that maternal PlGF level trajectories are shaped by a combination of modifiable and non-modifiable prenatal factors. Pre-pregnancy BMI reflects metabolic status, uterine fibroids affect the uterine environment, and reproductive history (including parity and previous pregnancy loss) contributes distinctively to trajectory membership. Importantly, the influence of parity and previous pregnancy loss is critically modified by the nature of prior obstetric events, highlighting that a detailed obstetric history is essential for interpreting individual PlGF patterns and guiding early risk stratification. These results underscore the importance of comprehensive prenatal assessment that integrates maternal characteristics and reproductive history to identify, as early as possible, women at heightened risk of following an unfavorable PlGF trajectory, thereby enabling targeted surveillance and timely preventive interventions.

PlGF Trajectory Patterns and Adverse Perinatal Outcomes: Clinical Translation

PlGF has been adopted in clinical practice for two main purposes: first-trimester combined screening (eg, Fetal Medicine Foundation) that incorporates a single PlGF measurement alongside maternal factors and biophysical markers to predict preterm preeclampsia risk;37 and second- or third-trimester sFlt-1/PlGF ratio testing as a diagnostic aid in women with suspected preeclampsia.38 Both applications rely on cross-sectional assessment at specific gestational windows and are primarily designed to predict or rule out individual complications—most notably preeclampsia. However, these single-time-point approaches may not capture the full spectrum of placental dysfunction. Preeclampsia, preterm delivery, and other adverse pregnancy outcomes share a common pathophysiological basis in impaired placental angiogenesis, yet manifest as distinct clinical phenotypes.39 A single PlGF measurement at a fixed gestational age, whether embedded in a multivariable risk algorithm or interpreted as a simple ratio threshold, identifies risk at only one moment in time and targets primarily one clinical endpoint. It may therefore miss women with progressive, subclinical placental compromise that culminates in a different adverse outcome.

Our study addresses this gap by characterizing longitudinal PlGF dynamics throughout pregnancy and examining their associations with a spectrum of adverse perinatal outcomes. Using GBTM, we identified three distinct PlGF patterns that diverged as early as the first trimester. Previous studies have shown that low maternal circulating PlGF levels (<100 pg/mL) are associated with an increased risk of preeclampsia, small for gestational age, and preterm birth.40–42 Importantly, our study not only confirms a strong association between the low PlGF level trajectory and classic placenta-related disorders (eg, placental insufficiency, preeclampsia) but also further reveals that even the moderate PlGF level trajectory is independently associated with preterm delivery before 37 weeks and reduced neonatal birth weight. This suggests that focusing on a single PlGF assessment may be insufficient to identify all pregnancies at risk of placental dysfunction. The moderate trajectory exemplifies this gap: these women might not be flagged by conventional single-time-point strategies, yet they had significantly higher odds of preterm delivery and lower neonatal birth weight. This implies that the longitudinal pattern of PlGF, capturing the trajectory of placental vascular development across gestation rather than its status at one isolated moment, may better reflect functional reserve and progressive placental impairment.

We observed that the high and moderate PlGF level trajectories tracked together until the late second trimester, after which the moderate PlGF level trajectory plateaued and then declined steeply in the third trimester while the high PlGF level trajectory remained elevated, peaking in the early third trimester before declining gradually. This divergence occurs beyond the first-trimester screening window, indicating that a single early assessment is insufficient to reliably distinguish pregnancies following a stable trajectory from those at risk of progressive compromise, with implications for a two-stage screening and monitoring strategy. At first‑trimester screening, the low PlGF level trajectory, which is characterized by the lowest overall levels and rises modestly to an early peak before declining progressively, would likely be captured by existing algorithms. Women identified at this stage may benefit from intensified surveillance, with serial monitoring helping to differentiate sustained low levels from transiently low values. For pregnancies not flagged early, the moderate PlGF level trajectory becomes distinguishable only in the late second to early third trimester, coinciding with the routine third‑trimester assessment window. Serial PlGF measurements from 25 to 32 weeks could be considered to detect the downward inflection before further deterioration, although the optimal interval requires prospective validation.

In summary, this study demonstrates that maternal PlGF levels follow distinct longitudinal trajectory patterns. By characterizing these dynamic patterns, we identify distinct longitudinal profiles that are differentially associated with adverse perinatal outcomes. While our findings suggest that longitudinal PlGF monitoring holds promise for refining risk stratification beyond current single-time-point approaches, prospective validation and formal predictive modeling are essential before trajectory-based assessment can be integrated into routine clinical practice.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study has several strengths. First, we employed trajectory modeling based on longitudinal data to systematically delineate the temporal patterns of maternal PlGF levels from early to late pregnancy. By capturing the overall temporal trend of PlGF as opposed to single time-point measurements, this approach provides a more comprehensive view of placental function dynamics. Second, our study was based on a clinical cohort of 438 pregnant women with multiple longitudinal measurements available, which enhanced the reliability and statistical power of the findings. Third, our study adjusted for multiple potential confounders, such as maternal age, parity, and pre-pregnancy BMI, thereby improving the robustness of the findings.

However, our study also has several limitations. First, the retrospective design relied on medical records, which may be subject to information bias. In addition, some unmeasured or inadequately controlled confounders may exist, including dietary factors, family income, and the specific dosage, timing, or patient adherence of pharmacological treatments (eg, aspirin, heparin), all of which may exert important influences on both PlGF levels and pregnancy outcomes. Second, we only included pregnant women who underwent at least three PlGF measurements, which may introduce selection bias. As shown in Table S3, significant differences existed between the included and excluded cohorts regarding gravidity, parity, use of assisted reproductive technology, and history of pregnancy loss. These differences suggest that the final sample may be systematically biased toward subgroups with higher obstetric risk or greater adherence to medical care, which limits the generalizability of the results. Therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to the broader pregnant population. Third, trajectory modeling has inherent limitations: (1) determining the optimal number of latent trajectories/groups in the study population is a process guided by statistical fit indices and investigator’s discretion, which might overestimate or underestimate the true number of trajectories/groups; (2) individuals are assigned to trajectories/groups based on their highest estimated group-membership probabilities; hence, the identified trajectories/groups should be viewed as approximations of more complex developmental patterns rather than as actual latent developmental categories.43 Fourth, the sample was derived from a single medical center, and the sample size in the low PlGF level trajectory was relatively small (n=56), which may compromise the stability and generalizability of the findings. Therefore, future studies should adopt a multicenter, prospective design that enrolls a more representative population and collects comprehensive covariate data, with long-term follow-up to further validate the clinical significance of maternal PlGF level trajectories and clarify any causal relationships.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that maternal PlGF levels during pregnancy follow distinct dynamic trajectory patterns, which are closely associated with specific maternal characteristics and show strong associations with various adverse perinatal outcomes. PlGF trajectory monitoring therefore holds promise for improving early risk stratification and enabling targeted intervention, but prospective validation is required to confirm its incremental predictive value over existing screening tools.
