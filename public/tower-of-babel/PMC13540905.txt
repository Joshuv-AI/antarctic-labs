A patient-specific induced pluripotent stem cell and neural stem cell resource for angelman syndrome with 15q11.2-q13 deletion
Authors: Song Qu, Yue Wu, Pu Sun, Cui Song, Yanyan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Angelman syndrome (AS) is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder most commonly caused by a maternal deletion of the 15q11.2-q13 region, resulting in loss of ubiquitin protein ligase E3A (UBE3A) function. The development of targeted therapies and a deeper understanding of AS pathogenesis require the availability of genetically accurate human models. Here, we generated and characterized a novel induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) line from peripheral blood mononuclear cells of a female pediatric patient with genetically confirmed deletion-type AS. The iPSC line displayed a normal karyotype, expressed key pluripotency markers, and demonstrated trilineage differentiation potential. Efficient generation of neural stem cells establishes a foundational cellular resource for future investigations of neuronal maturation and disease-specific phenotypes. This well-validated iPSC and NSC resource provides a valuable and clinically relevant platform for elucidating the molecular pathophysiology of AS in a genetically accurate human cellular context and for high-throughput screening of therapeutic compounds targeting the predominant population of patients with AS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57826-2.

Introduction

Angelman syndrome (AS; OMIM 105830) is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder predominantly caused by loss-of-function of the maternally inherited allele of the ubiquitin protein ligase E3A (UBE3A) gene located within the chromosomal region 15q11.2-q131. Four genetic mechanisms may contribute to maternal UBE3A loss of function: (1) maternal deletion of chromosome 15q11.2-13 (approximately 70–75%), (2) variants in the UBE3A gene (10–15%), (3) paternal uniparental disomy (UPD) (1–3%), and (4) imprinting center defects (2–4%). Additionally, 10% of patients present with the clinical characteristics of AS; however, the underlying genetic causes remain undetermined1,2. Neuronal UBE3A expression is almost exclusively maternal because of genomic imprinting. Loss of maternal expression in AS results in a profound deficiency of UBE3A protein. This deficiency impairs synaptic development and remodeling while disrupting the neural circuitry, which underlies the characteristic clinical phenotype of AS1. The deletion subtype is not only the most prevalent genetic cause but is also consistently associated with the most severe clinical manifestations, including a high frequency of intractable seizures and significant impairments in motor and language development1,3. However, the absence of disease-modifying therapies remains a challenge for all individuals with this disorder, regardless of the genetic subtype4. Current clinical management remains entirely supportive, with no etiological therapy available for patients with AS. This highlights the critical need for research models that accurately recapitulate human pathology to elucidate disease mechanisms and facilitate therapeutic screening5.

Animal models of AS have provided crucial mechanistic insights but are inherently limited in recapitulating the human-specific aspects of the disorder6. First, they exhibit significant biological differences from humans and may not accurately reflect disease phenotypes or responses to therapeutic agents. Second, animal models fail to capture the genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity observed in human populations, a limitation further reflected in their inability to fully replicate the complexity of human neural circuitry4,5,7. This creates a major challenge, as the efficacy and safety profiles established in animal models cannot ensure the corresponding outcomes in humans. For example, antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), designed to unsilence the paternal UBE3A allele, have shown robust efficacy in AS mice models7–9. Therefore, confirming the safety and efficacy of these therapies in patients remains a critical step9. Human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) models are critical as they provide a human-relevant platform to overcome this fundamental challenge before advancing into larger clinical trials. Therefore, patient-specific neuronal models allow us to test the safety and efficacy of treatments in human-derived cells. However, obtaining functional neurons directly from patients is neither practical nor ethical10. While postmortem tissues are available, they are inherently limited because they are frozen in time, capturing only the final disease state and thus cannot inform developmental processes11,12.

Human iPSC technology offers a powerful solution for this challenge13. By reprogramming patient blood cells (PBMCs), we can create iPSCs that carry the complete genetic background of the patient, including UBE3A imprinting status. These iPSCs can differentiate into neurons and self-organize into three-dimensional brain organoids, providing a human-based system with considerable potential for both therapeutic screening and mechanistic discovery5,14. These models not only provide an unparalleled platform for therapeutic screening, enabling the direct evaluation of drug efficacy, toxicity, and mechanisms of action in a genetically relevant human cellular context, thereby bridging the gap between animal studies and clinical trials14—but also enable crucial mechanistic discoveries. By recapitulating key aspects of human neurodevelopment, including cellular diversity, spatial organization, and complex cell-cell interactions, brain organoids offer an ideal system for investigating disease-specific developmental pathologies, such as impaired neuronal maturation and circuit formation14–16. Crucially, they provide a unique window into human-specific processes, such as the establishment and maintenance of genomic imprinting at the UBE3A locus, a process known to differ between mice and humans, allowing researchers to explore three-dimensional chromatin architecture and transcriptional regulation during neural fate commitment from neural stem cells (NSCs) to mature neurons17,18.

A review of the existing literature indicates that while iPSC models for AS (including deletion subtypes) have been reported, they are predominantly derived from non-Chinese populations19–21. Focusing on the Chinese population is important given its size and the substantial number of AS cases, which makes it particularly relevant for therapeutic screening. Models from this population will advance the understanding of diverse groups and support the development of precision medicine for Chinese patients. The deletion subtype accounts for the majority of AS cases and is the most severe. Therefore, model development efforts should be expanded to include the genetic backgrounds of diverse AS populations worldwide.

In this study, we aimed to establish and characterize a new iPSC line derived from PBMCs of a pediatric patient with AS carrying a maternal 15q11.2-13 deletion. This study provides a genetically defined, patient-specific cellular resource of the deletion genotype, which is a prevalent AS subtype. The availability of such a resource will enable researchers to elucidate disease mechanisms, facilitate therapeutic screening, and evaluate whether candidate strategies demonstrate efficacy in this defined genetic context.

Methods

Ethics statement, patient selection, and recruitment

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No. [2021] Ethics [Research] No. [279]). A pediatric patient with a preliminary diagnosis of AS, established through clinical evaluation and screening using whole exome sequencing, was recruited. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents of the patient. Peripheral blood was collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid anticoagulant tubes, and PBMCs were isolated for subsequent iPSC generation. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Generation and cultivation of iPSCs

Human iPSCs were generated from peripheral PBMCs obtained from a donor diagnosed with an AS deletion subtype. Reprogramming was performed using a commercial non-integrating sendai virus reprogramming kit (Reproeasy hiPSC reprogramming kit, Cellapy, Cat. CA5002002) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, PBMCs were activated and expanded in PBMCeasy® medium for 6 days. Cells were then transduced with sendai virus vectors expressing OCT4, SOX2, KLF4, and c-MYC (at MOI 5:5:3) and plated. The culture medium was sequentially transitioned from PBMCeasy® medium to a specific reprogramming medium (Reproeasy) and finally to a pluripotent stem cell maintenance medium (PGM1, Cellapy). The entire process was carried out on culture plates pre-coated with the PSCeasy coating solution. Emerging iPSC colonies exhibiting typical ESC-like morphology were manually picked and transferred for expansion under feeder-free conditions. Two primary iPSC clones were obtained. One representative clone (AS-iPSC-1) was selected for in-depth characterization based on typical ESC-like morphology and normal karyotype; the second clone (AS-iPSC-2) showed similar characteristics and its AP staining and immunostaining are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1. The established iPSC lines were routinely passaged using a gentle dissociation reagent (DissoEasy, Cellapy) and maintained in PGM1 medium at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. All experiments in this study used iPSCs between passages 10 and 20.

Additionally, a healthy control iPSC line (Ctrl-iPSC) derived from a 10-year-old female Han Chinese donor was obtained from the same manufacturer (Cellapy), generated with the identical reprogramming kit and maintained under the same conditions; this control was used exclusively as a normal comparator in MS-MLPA analysis to provide reference profiles for biparental diploid copy number and normal methylation patterns at the SNRPN locus.

Characterization of iPSC pluripotency

The pluripotency of the established iPSC lines was verified using a series of standard assays, including assessment of undifferentiated marker expression and functional differentiation potential. Alkaline phosphatase (AP) activity was detected using a commercial staining kit, with positive colonies displaying strong red and purple staining. Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis was performed using cells fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. After permeabilization and blocking, cells were incubated with primary antibodies against pluripotency markers OCT4, SOX2, and NANOG overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. All antibody details (manufacturer, catalog number, host species, and dilution) are provided in Table 1. For quantitative assessment, flow cytometry was performed on dissociated single cells stained with antibodies against the surface markers SSEA4, TRA-1-60, and TRA-1-81. Additionally, the expression of pluripotency-associated genes was confirmed at the transcriptional level by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) using SYBR Green assays. Primer sequences are listed in Table 2. Expression levels were normalized to GAPDH.

In vitro trilineage differentiation potential

Differentiation was carried out in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) unless specified. Specific protocols: (1) Mesoderm induction: cells were treated with 3 µM CHIR99021 (Tocris, 4423) for 24 h, following the protocol by Loh et al., 201622. (2) Endoderm induction: Day 1: 100 ng/mL Activin A (PeproTech) + 3 µM CHIR99021 in RPMI 1640; Days 2–5: 100 ng/mL Activin A + 0.2% FBS (low serum) in RPMI 164023. (3) Ectoderm induction: cells were treated with 10 µM SB431542 (Tocris) + 200 nM LDN-193,189 (Stemgent) in RPMI 1640 for 7 days24. After differentiation, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. We confirmed successful differentiation using IF staining for lineage-specific markers.

Genetic and genomic integrity analysis

Karyotyping was performed on iPSCs during the logarithmic growth phase. The cells were treated with colcemid, subjected to hypotonic treatment, fixed, or G-banded. A minimum of 20 metaphase spreads were analyzed to confirm a normal 46, XX karyotype. Methylation-specific multiplexed ligation probe amplification (MS-MLPA) for AS Diagnosis was conducted using the SALSA MS-MLPA Probemix ME028-C1 according to the manufacturer’s protocol25. Notably, approximately 200 ng of genomic DNA was denatured and hybridized to a probe mix. PCR was performed after ligation and digestion with methylation-sensitive enzymes. Products were separated by capillary electrophoresis. Data analysis was performed using Coffalyser.Net software to determine copy number variations and methylation status of the 15q11.2-q13 region.

Neural differentiation

Neural induction was performed using the NeuroEasy Human Neural Stem Cell Induction Kit (Cellapy, Cat. CA2306100). This commercially available kit was selected for its high efficiency and reproducibility in generating NSCs from hiPSCs within 7 days without an EB stage. Although the exact chemical composition of the kit medium is proprietary per the manufacturer’s policy, the kit’s working principle is based on dual-SMAD inhibition26. Additionally, the robustness of this kit has been independently validated by previous studies27. The induction medium consists of NeuroEasy basal medium supplemented with 2% (v/v) NeuroEasy neural induction supplement. iPSCs at 100% confluence (Day 0) were cultured in this complete induction medium with changes every 48 h. After 6–7 days (Days 6–7), rosette-like structures appeared. Cells were dissociated with NeuroEasy digestion solution and replated in NeuroEasy NSC maintenance medium (STEMCELL Technologies 05750 supplemented with bFGF, EGF, and heparin) to establish stable NSC cultures. NSCs were characterized by IF for Nestin and FOXG1. The resulting NSCs were expanded in this maintenance medium, with medium changes every other day and passaging upon reaching 80–90% confluence.

Mycoplasma detection

Routine mycoplasma testing was performed on all cell lines using a MycoEasy Rapid Mycoplasma Detection Kit (CA6101048; Cellapy). Notably, the concentrated cell culture supernatant was analyzed via PCR using the kit’s positive and negative controls.

Results

Patient clinical characteristics

To establish a patient-specific model, iPSCs were generated from an 11-year-old girl clinically diagnosed with AS. The donor presented with a classic AS phenotype encompassing the core diagnostic triad of severe global developmental delay, movement disorder, and characteristic behavior. Specifically, she exhibited a profound intellectual disability with minimal expressive language, ataxic gait, and motor clumsiness, along with frequent and inappropriate laughter, hyperactivity, and easy excitability. Consistent with the more severe deletion subtype, the patient’s clinical profile also included microcephaly, a history of epilepsy with abnormal EEG findings, and significant sleep-wake cycle disturbances.

Generation and characterization of iPSCs from a female patient with AS carrying a deletion

A central question for modeling AS is whether patient-specific iPSCs can be established to fulfill the standard criteria for pluripotency and, more critically, retain the precise genetic and epigenetic defects of the disease. To address this, we reprogrammed PBMCs from a donor diagnosed with the classic 15q11.2-q13 deletion subtype of AS. Using non-integrating sendai virus reprogramming (Reproeasy kit, Cellapy), we obtained two iPSC clones that adopted a characteristic embryonic stem cell-like morphology with defined borders and prominent nuclei (Fig. 1a, upper panel). A representative AS-iPSC clone (AS-iPSC-1) was selected for in-depth characterization based on typical ESC-like morphology and normal karyotype; the second clone (AS-iPSC-2) showed similar characteristics and its AP staining and immunostaining are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1. AS-iPSC-1 was selected because AS-iPSC-2 showed comparatively slower growth and was therefore reserved as a backup. The data from AS-iPSC-2 demonstrate the reproducibility of the reprogramming. All subsequent experiments, including trilineage differentiation, MS-MLPA analysis, and neural induction, were performed using AS-iPSC-1.

The genomic integrity of the established line was confirmed by karyotyping, which revealed a normal 46, XX chromosomal constitution (Fig. 1b). To validate cell pluripotency, we performed a series of standard assays. The cells stained positive for AP, which is a hallmark of undifferentiated stem cells (Fig. 1a, lower panel). IF analyses and Flow cytometry confirmed that over 90% of the iPSC population robustly expressed the key pluripotency transcription factors, OCT4, SOX2, and NANOG, at the protein level (Fig. 1c, d). All antibodies used are listed in Table 1. The qRT-PCR of parental PBMCs, iPSCs, and NSCs revealed the expected stage-specific expression dynamics: OCT4 and NANOG were specifically upregulated in iPSCs and downregulated upon neural induction, whereas SOX2, which is consistent with its dual role in pluripotency and neural development, remained highly expressed in both iPSCs and NSCs (Fig. 1e). AS-iPSCs efficiently generated derivatives expressing definitive markers of all three germ layers, the ectoderm (Nestin), mesoderm (T/Brachyury), and endoderm (FOXA2), thereby confirming their bona fide pluripotency (Fig. 2a). All the lines were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination (Fig. 2b, with DNA ladder added).

Genetic and epigenetic confirmation of the maternal 15q11.2-q13 deletion

To confirm the molecular pathology of our patient-derived iPSC model, we used MS-MLPA to analyze the imprinted 15q11.2-q13 gene region. Analysis of the AS iPSC line identified a heterozygous ~ 3.9 Mb maternal deletion spanning the 15q11.2-q13 region, which encompasses the critical AS/PWS locus (Fig. 3a, upper panel). Methylation analysis revealed no methylation at the SNRPN promoter (Fig. 3a, lower panel). A complete lack of methylation is a classic epigenetic profile of maternal deletions. When the methylated maternal allele is lost, only the unmethylated paternal allele remains, resulting in a specific pattern. Ctrl-iPSC exhibited the expected biparental contribution to the 15q11.2-q13 region, which is characterized by a normal diploid copy number and a typical methylation pattern (Fig. 3b). Figure 3c shows a schematic diagram comparing the genomic architecture of the 15q11.2-q13 locus between the control and AS alleles, clearly defining the extent of the deletion. These findings confirmed that the established iPSC model faithfully recapitulated the molecular signature of patients with AS carrying deletions.

Efficient neural induction into NSCs

To evaluate the neural differentiation capacity of iPSCs, cells were directed toward a neural lineage. Following induction with NeuroEasy neural induction medium (Cellapy, Cat. CA2306100) for approximately 6–7 days, AS-iPSCs efficiently formed abundant neural rosettes. Subsequently, these rosette structures dissociated and expanded, yielding proliferative cultures (Fig. 4a). IF analysis confirmed that these cells co-expressed the NSCs marker Nestin and the forebrain-specific transcription factor FOXG1 (Fig. 4b). This marker expression profile is consistent with a population of neural stem/progenitor cells with forebrain commitment, thereby establishing a phenotypically relevant cellular foundation for modeling AS pathology28.

Discussion

We successfully established and thoroughly characterized a patient-derived iPSC and NSC resource of AS that recapitulated the molecular basis of the most prevalent deletion subtype, enabling disorder modeling in a human neuronal context. Derived from readily accessible PBMCs of a female patient, this resource fulfills the dual criteria of a reliable experimental tool: it maintains a precise genetic and epigenetic disease profile while exhibiting pluripotent potential and efficient neural differentiation capacity. The following discussion integrates these findings, highlighting the utility of the resource, its advantages for studying neural pathology, its application in therapeutic development, and its importance for future genotype-specific studies.

This study established a novel human iPSC resource for AS from peripheral PBMCs, providing a genetically and phenotypically validated cellular platform that can be obtained and easily expanded13. The AS-iPSC line contained a ~ 3.9 Mb maternal deletion in the 15q11.2-q13 region found in patients, as confirmed by MS-MLPA. This was supported by the definitive epigenetic profile of the complete SNRPN promoter hypomethylation, which recapitulated the donor’s confirmed clinical diagnosis (Fig. 3a, c)25. Such molecular-level genetic precision is fundamental for ensuring phenotypic reproducibility. Furthermore, the pluripotency of the iPSC line was confirmed by (1) positive AP activity (Fig. 1a); (2) robust expression of key markers (OCT4, SOX2, and NANOG) via flow cytometry, IF, and qRT-PCR (Fig. 1c-e); and (3) successful differentiation into derivatives of all three germ layers in a trilineage assay (Fig. 2a). The confirmed genomic integrity (Fig. 1b) and absence of mycoplasma contamination (Fig. 2b) collectively established its suitability as a high-quality research tool.

Based on this validated pluripotency foundation, we established a neural differentiation platform. The iPSCs exhibited robust neural induction, yielding proliferative cultures characterized by neural rosette formation and co-expression of the NSC markers Nestin and FOXG1 (Fig. 4)28. This confirms the generation of a phenotypically relevant population of neural stem/progenitor cells with forebrain commitment28, establishing an essential cellular foundation for investigating neuron-specific pathology. Given that the paternal UBE3A allele is silenced postnatally in neurons, this model enables the precise elucidation of the pathological mechanisms triggered by UBE3A protein loss during neuronal maturation. A key future direction is to further differentiate these NSCs into defined neuronal subtypes29 to assess UBE3A deficiency effects on neuronal function. Such models would allow for cell-type-specific assessment of how UBE3A deficiency affects intrinsic excitability, synaptic function, and network integration, which are core cellular mechanisms underlying the diverse neurological symptoms in AS30,31. The value of this iPSC-based platform extends beyond two-dimensional monolayer cultures. Derived NSCs serve as a critical starting point for generating three-dimensional brain organoids14,15. Brain organoids recapitulate cellular diversity and complex interactions of the developing human brain, offering a powerful system to study UBE3A imprinting dynamics in a patient-specific context14,17,18.

Beyond its use in disease modeling, this iPSC resource also provides a versatile platform for therapeutic screening. It offers an ideal defined genetic background for screening and establishing a defined baseline for the complete UBE3A absence upon neuronal differentiation. This baseline enables a systematic assessment of the efficacy of promising strategies such as ASOs designed to unsilence the paternal UBE3A allele8. Notably, patient-derived neuronal models have been applied in drug discovery for AS, and the antisense oligonucleotide rugonersen is currently in clinical development9,32. In this context, patient-derived brain organoids hold significant promise as a next-generation preclinical screening platform. They allow the evaluation of drug efficacy, toxicity, and mechanisms of action within a more physiologically relevant, human three-dimensional tissue environment, potentially offering a superior bridge between animal studies and clinical trials for neurodevelopmental disorders14.

While these findings highlight the broad utility of this iPSC resource, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the control iPSC line is not a familial matched control; an ideal control would be derived from an unaffected parent or sibling to share the genetic background. While the current healthy donor control permits basic comparative analyses, future studies should incorporate isogenic or familial controls for rigorous phenotyping. Second, neuronal differentiation data are not presented in this resource report. The NSC stage is an essential intermediate, but demonstration of UBE3A-ATS transcript upregulation and UBE3A protein reduction in mature neurons is required to fully validate the disease model. These experiments are planned as the critical next step. Third, the neural induction protocol relies on a commercial kit; while we have now provided the composition and underlying scientific rationale (dual-SMAD inhibition), open-source protocols may offer greater accessibility.

Beyond these technical and resource-specific limitations, it is also important to consider the genetic heterogeneity of AS when interpreting findings from this deletion-specific model. To advance precision medicine for AS, it is essential to construct and characterize models derived from patients with other genotypes, including mutations and imprinting defects. A comparative analysis across such models is critical to determine whether the therapeutic effects are consistent or genotype-specific. Thus, this deletion-specific model serves not only as a tool for its own genotype but also as an essential reference. In contrast to other genotypes, this model helps define the necessity of genotype-specific therapeutic approaches across the AS spectrum. UPD is a critical exception to this framework. The iPSC models from patients with UPD are inherently unsuitable for validating paternal allele reactivation strategies, because both chromosomal copies are of paternal origin. Consequently, any therapeutic strategy aimed at activating the paternal UBE3A allele carries the potential risk of harmful UBE3A overexpression in the context of UPD, which is a significant concern given the established link between UBE3A overdose and autism spectrum disorders7.

In conclusion, we established and comprehensively validated a patient-derived iPSC and NSC resource of the most common and severe forms of AS. This resource faithfully captured the foundational genetics and epigenetics of patients with AS carrying deletions. This provides a well-defined, clinically relevant cellular platform for elucidating disease pathogenesis and accelerating the development of targeted genotype-informed therapies.
