Cultivar dependent responses of olive (Olea europaea L.) to foliar fertilization in fruit quality and mineral composition of leaves and fruits
Authors: Mehmet Doğukan Yılmaz, Mehmet Yaman, Yazgan Tunç, Ömer Faruk Coşkun, Seher Toprak, Metin Gönültaş, Ahmet Sümbül, Ahmet Say, Yusuf Murat Keçe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study examined cultivar-dependent responses to foliar fertilization in olive (Olea europaea L.) by jointly evaluating fruit morphology and skin colour, biochemical quality indicators, and mineral nutrient composition in leaves and fruits. Five cultivars (Arbequina, Frantoio, Ayvalık, Gemlik, and Edincik Su) were tested under five foliar treatments (Control, Bestline, Dermin, UAN-32, and Forceful Amino) in a factorial design. Cultivar, treatment, and their interaction significantly affected fruit size, colour parameters, total phenolic content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC), antioxidant activity (AA), and the accumulation of multiple mineral elements (p < 0.05). Across treatments, UAN-32 increased fruit size overall (mean fruit weight 3.30 g). In contrast, Dermin and Bestline were more frequently associated with higher AA in specific cultivar combinations, indicating that increases in TPC and TFC did not necessarily translate into higher AA. Mineral profiling revealed treatment-related differences in element accumulation and partitioning: Bestline was often linked to higher fruit Fe–Mg–Zn concentrations, Dermin promoted Ca enrichment, and UAN-32 increased fruit K. Principal component analysis (PCA) further supported these patterns by separating cultivar × treatment combinations primarily along a fruit-size axis and a coordinated mineral-nutrient axis. Overall, responses to foliar fertilization were strongly cultivar dependent, highlighting the importance of optimizing cultivar-treatment combinations according to targeted production goals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57360-1.

Introduction

Olive (Olea europaea L.) is among the most important perennial crops of the Mediterranean basin and has high economic value through both table and oil products. Market value and consumer acceptance are largely determined by interrelated traits including fruit size and shape, skin and flesh colour, phenolic composition and antioxidant activity, and mineral nutrient status1,2. In particular, the phenolic fraction is a key driver of sensory properties, oxidative stability, and health-related attributes of olive products3. Because phenolics also contribute to plant defence and stress adaptation (e.g., drought, UV radiation, and biotic pressures), their assessment is most informative when interpreted alongside agronomic management and environmental conditions. The olive phenolic profile is dynamic and shaped by genotype, maturity stage, and growing environment. Secoiridoid derivatives are quantitatively dominant in olive fruits and oils, while simple phenols and flavonoids contribute at variable levels depending on cultivar and ripening stage4. These compounds influence flavour and stability and provide antioxidant protection against oxidative deterioration1. Accordingly, “quality” should be viewed as a multidimensional outcome that integrates morphological attributes, biochemical composition, and nutritional status, rather than being captured by a single trait.

Although many quality attributes are strongly genotype dependent, environmental conditions and orchard management can substantially modulate outcomes. Large-scale studies have reported pronounced genotypic variation in phenolics and highlighted that genotype × year or genotype × environment interactions can be important for specific components4,5. Accordingly, cultivars should be evaluated not only by mean performance but also by the magnitude and direction of their responses across contrasting conditions. Climatic factors (water availability, temperature, light) and practices such as fertilization have been associated with phenolic variability, and dry/hot conditions may increase phenolic accumulation in some contexts6,7. Mineral nutrition is another central lever in olive production, influencing vegetative growth, fruit set, fruit growth, and biochemical quality8. Macro- and micronutrients regulate osmotic adjustment, enzyme activity, membrane integrity, and metabolic fluxes,therefore, evaluating leaf-fruit nutrient partitioning together with quality traits can improve physiological interpretation. In this study, fruit morphology and colour traits were assessed together with total phenolic content, total flavonoid content, antioxidant activity, and mineral profiles measured in both leaves and fruits.

In modern olive cultivation, fertilization management is essential for achieving adequate growth, stable yield, and high-quality products9. Foliar fertilization has gained wider use because it enables rapid, targeted interventions during critical phenological stages10. In calcareous, high-pH soils-common in olive regions-micronutrient availability may be restricted, making foliar treatment a practical complement to soil fertilization in some cases8. Foliar performance depends on formulation chemistry, treatment conditions, and pathways of entry across the leaf surface11,12. However, foliar sprays generally cannot replace soil fertilization for macronutrient demand and are best viewed as supplementary tools8. Importantly, cultivar-specific leaf traits and internal nutrient mobility can cause the same product to elicit different magnitudes and sometimes directions of response among genotypes10, particularly when mineral redistribution and antioxidant metabolism shift concurrently. Foliar fertilizer treatment has the advantage of using low concentrations, providing uniform nutrient distribution within the plant, and eliciting a faster physiological response13,14. To our knowledge, no previous study has simultaneously evaluated fruit morphology, biochemical quality traits, and comprehensive leaf-fruit mineral nutrient profiles under multiple foliar fertilization regimes across olive cultivars, with explicit emphasis on cultivar-dependent responses. To address this gap, the present study aimed to quantify the effects of foliar fertilizer treatments on fruit morphology and color parameters, total phenolic and flavonoid contents, antioxidant activity, and leaf and fruit mineral nutrient concentrations across olive cultivars, and to identify the main sources of variation using PCA.

Materials and methods

Plant material and experimental design

This study was conducted in a commercial olive orchard located in Hatay, Türkiye. Five olive (Olea europaea L.) cultivars—Arbequina, Frantoio, Ayvalık, Gemlik, and Edincik Su—were used as plant material. The trees were 10 years old and established at a planting distance of 4 × 4 m. The orchard was irrigated using a drip irrigation system, and all trees were managed under uniform agronomic practices throughout the experimental period. Pruning, weed control, pest and disease management, and other cultural practices were applied consistently across all experimental units. All trees received the same basal soil fertilization program. Annual fertilizer applications consisted of 500 g N (as urea), 250 g P (as superphosphate), and 500 g K (as potassium sulfate) per tree. Phosphorus and potassium fertilizers were applied in November, whereas nitrogen was applied in split doses in February and May. The experiment was established as a factorial combination of five cultivars and five foliar treatments (5 × 5) in a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replicates. Each experimental unit consisted of one tree, and cultivar × treatment combinations were randomly assigned within each block. To reduce within-block variability, trees of similar vigor and size were selected. The foliar treatments included: Control (water only), Bestline, Dermin, UAN-32, and Forceful Amino. Foliar fertilizer solutions were prepared according to manufacturer recommendations. Bestline, Dermin, and UAN-32 were applied at a concentration of 2.5–3.0 g L⁻1 (250–300 g per 100 L of water), whereas Forceful Amino was applied at 1.0–1.5 g L⁻1 (100–150 g per 100 L of water). Applications were performed using a backpack sprayer. Foliar sprays were applied during the cooler hours of the day (early morning or late afternoon) to minimize evaporation losses and maximize nutrient uptake. Spraying was carried out uniformly over the entire canopy to ensure homogeneous coverage. Foliar applications were conducted at three critical stages corresponding to the fruit oil accumulation period: August 15, September 15, and October 15. Control trees were sprayed with water at the same dates and application volumes. For morphological and biochemical analyses, 60 fruits and 60 leaves were randomly collected from each tree. Sampling was performed from all four cardinal directions and different canopy levels to ensure representativeness. Fruits were harvested at the physiological maturity stage (purple-black skin color), specific to each cultivar, between November 1 and December 30.

Fruit and leaf morphological and color analyses

Fruit width, fruit length, leaf width, leaf length, petiole length, and petiole thickness of the cultivars were measured using a digital caliper (Insize 1104 IP54) with a precision of 0.01 mm and expressed in millimeters. Fruit weight was determined using a digital scale (Precisa, XB 4200C) with a precision of 0.01 g. The color characteristics of the fruits were measured using a Minolta colorimeter according to the CIE L*a*b method.

Biochemical analyses

The fruits were washed with pure water, and the seeds were removed by hand. To prevent thermal degradation of bioactive compounds, the fruit pulp was homogenized in a stainless-steel laboratory blender under cold conditions. 2 g of the homogenized fruit sample was transferred to centrifuge tubes. 30 ml of 80% methanol solution was added to the fruit samples. Fruit samples were centrifuged at 9000 rpm for 1 min at room temperature (Hettich H-1650R, Germany). The final volume was made up to 50 ml with the same solvent and vortexed for 30 s. Fruit samples were centrifuged at 4 °C and 6000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was stored at − 20 °C and used as the main stock extract for olive fruits.

Total Phenolic Content: The total phenolic content of the fruits was determined according to the Folin-Ciocalteu method15. 200 µL of extract taken from the fruit stock was mixed with 1800 µL of distilled water, 1 ml of Folin-Ciocalteu solution diluted 1/10, and 2 ml of 2% Na2CO3. After the mixture was kept in the dark for 2 h, absorbance value was measured at a wavelength of 760 nm using a spectrophotometer. The total phenolic content of the fruits was expressed as mg gallic acid equivalent per 100 g fresh weight (mg GAE/100 g).

Total Flavonoid Content: The total flavonoid content of the fruits was determined according to the method described by Chang et al.16. 500 µL of extract taken from the fruit stock was mixed with 500 µL of distilled water and 100 µL of 5% NaNO3 for 6 min. Then, 100 μL of AlCl3 was added to the solution and left for 6 min. 1 ml of 1 M NaOH was added to the resulting solution, and the final volume was supplemented to 2.5 ml with distilled water. The solution was read spectrophotometerally at a wavelength of 510 nm, and the results were expressed as mg quercetin equivalent per 100 g fresh weight (mg QE/100 g).

Antioxidant Activity: The antioxidant activities of the fruits were determined using the DPPH (1,1-diphenyl-2-picryl-hydrazyl) method described by Brand-Williams et al.17. A 0.2 mM DPPH solution was prepared for analysis. After adding 2900 µL of DPPH solution to 100 µL of fruit extract, the mixture was vortexed for 5 min. The mixture obtained was incubated in the dark for 30 min. To determine the antioxidant activity of the fruits, absorbance values were determined at a wavelength of 517 nm (Asample), and the results were expressed as % inhibition. To calculate antioxidant activity, a blank solution without fruit extract was used as a control, and its absorbance (Ablank) was measured. Leaf and fruit mineral analyses

Leaf and fruit samples were randomly selected from healthy specimens from all parts of the trees. Before analysis, fruits and leaves were cleaned with water and distilled water. The samples were dried at 65–70 °C until they reached a constant weight. The dried samples were ground to a particle size of < 0.5 mm and homogenized. The determination of the nutrient elements in fruits and leaves was performed using the dry combustion method (digestion in a mixture of nitric acid (HNO₃) and perchloric acid (HClO₄) (V/V: 4/1)). Nutrient concentrations in leaves and fruits of the obtained samples were determined using Inductively Coupled Plasma Atomic Emission Spectrometry (ICP-AES, Varian, Liberty Series II; Varian Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA)18.

Statistical analysis

The normality of the data distribution was examined using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was examined using the Levene test. The results showed that the data were suitable for parametric analysis. The effects of cultivar, treatment, and cultivar × treatment interaction were tested using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Mean comparisons were performed using the Tukey test at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS v22.0. The effectiveness of the treatments was evaluated using multivariate analyses (correlation matrix analysis, principal component analysis, and heatmap hierarchical clustering analysis). Correlation matrix analysis was performed using the Origin Pro 2024b software package, while principal component analysis and heatmap hierarchical clustering analysis were performed using the JMP Pro 17.0 software package. Origin Pro 2024b software package was usedf in correlation matrix analysis (CMA) to determine the strength and direction of relationships between variables. To reveal patterns among multiple variables, principal component analysis (PCA) was applied. PCA used the Varimax rotation method with Kaiser normalization to increase the clarity of the components. A two-dimensional biplot representing the first and second principal components (PC1/PC2) was created to visualize the distribution of treatments and associated variables. Heatmap hierarchical clustering analysis was performed using the Ward method based on Euclidean distance coefficients. Before clustering analysis, the data were standardized to reduce variability arising from different units of measurement and to increase comparability between features.

Results

Leaf morphology traits

Significant differences were detected among olive cultivars for leaf width, leaf length, petiole length, and petiole thickness (Table 1). Cultivar effects were highly significant for all traits (p < 0.001), indicating strong cultivar control of leaf morphology. Among the varieties, Frantoio exhibited high values in leaf width (19.22 mm), leaf length (68.29 mm), and petiole thickness (1.48 mm), while Edincik Su variety showed a high value in petiole thickness (1.48 mm). While the effect of fertilizer treatment on leaf width and leaf length was found to be statistically insignificant, it was found to be statistically significant on petiole length and petiole thickness. UAN-32 fertilizer treatment showed a high effect on petiole length (5.60 mm) and petiole thickness (1.38 mm). The cultivar × treatment interaction was significant for leaf width and petiole length (p < 0.05) and highly significant for leaf length and petiole thickness (p < 0.001), confirming cultivar-dependent responses. Although leaf characteristics vary depending on the variety as a genetic trait, fertilizer treatments have shown varying effects depending on the cultivar. The highest increase in leaf characteristics was observed in Arbequina × UAN-32 (34.37%) for leaf width, and in Ayvalık × UAN-32 for leaf length, petiole length, and petiole thickness (33.28%, 23.54%, Fruit morphology and skin colour traits

Fruit weight, fruit width, fruit length, and skin colour parameters (L*, a*, b*) differed significantly among cultivars (Table 2). Cultivar effects were highly significant for fruit weight, width, and length (p < 0.001) and were also significant for colour traits (L* and a*: p < 0.001; b*: p < 0.01). The Edincik Su variety exhibited high values in fruit weight (5.45 g), fruit width (23.54 mm), and fruit length (19.31 mm). Among the fruit peel color values, the L value (33.29) and b* value (6.94) showed higher values in the Arbequina variety, while the a* value (35.60) showed higher values in the Gemlik variety. This indicates that fruit characteristics are genetically determined. Foliar fertilizer treatment significantly affected fruit weight, width, length, and L* (p < 0.01) but did not influence a* or b*. The treatment of UAN-32 for fruit weight (3.30 g), the treatment of Forceful Amino for fruit width (19.60 mm) and fruit length (15.41 mm), and the treatment of Dermin for L* value (29.25) showed the most effective results. The cultivar × treatment interaction was significant for all traits (p < 0.05 to p < 0.001), confirming cultivar-dependent responses. The effects of fertilizer treatments on fruit size and fruit color values varied. The greatest increase in fruit weight was observed in Arbequina × UAN-32 (101.16%), while the lowest increase was observed in Frantoio × Dermin (0.78%). The greatest increase in fruit width and fruit length was observed in Edincik Su × UAN-32 (18.63%, 17.41% respectively). The lowest increase in fruit width was observed in Arbequina × Dermin (0.23%), while the lowest increase in fruit length was observed in Frantoio × Dermin (0.89%). The effect of fertilizer on fruit color values varied according to varieties and fertilizer treatments. The highest increases in fruit color values were observed in Frantoio × Dermin (30.18%) for the L value, Ayvalık × Bestline (60.33%) for the a* value, and Arbequina × Dermin (148.57%) for the b* value. Compared to the control treatment, fertilizer treatments caused increases and decreases in fruit size and fruit color values. Leaf and fruit mineral nutrient contents

Significant effects of cultivar, treatment, and cultivar × treatment interaction were detected for fruit Al, B, Ca, Cr, and Cu contents (p < 0.001 for all; Supplementary Table 1), indicating strong cultivar-dependent modulation of fruit mineral composition by foliar fertilization. The nutrient content of the fruit varied depending on the average of the varieties. The highest values were observed in the Gemlik variety for Al, Ca, and Cr content; in the Edincik Su variety for B content; and in the Ayvalık variety for Cu content. In terms of fertilizer treatments, Bestline showed high levels of Al and Cr, UAN-32 showed high levels of B, Dermin showed high levels of Ca, and Forceful Amino showed high levels of Cu. Fertilizer treatments showed different effects on the fruit element content of the varieties. The highest increases were observed in Gemlik × Bestline (475.30%) for Al content, Ayvalık × UAN-32 (54.32%) for B content, Gemlik × Dermin (1534.01%) for Ca content, Gemlik × Bestline (889.28%) for Cr content, and Frantoio × Forceful Amino (139.41%) for Cu content.

Highly significant effects of cultivar, treatment, and cultivar × treatment interaction were observed for fruit Fe, K, Mg, Mn, and Na contents (p < 0.001 for all; Supplementary Table 2), indicating strong cultivar-dependent modulation of fruit mineral composition by foliar fertilization. The Gemlik variety showed the highest values for Fe, Mg, and Mn content, while the Ayvalık variety showed the highest values for K and Na content. The Bestline treatment had the highest effect on Fe, Mg, and Mn content. The UAN-32 treatment had the highest effect on K content. The Dermin treatment had the highest effect on Na content. The highest increases were observed in Fe, Mg, and Mn content in Gemlik × Bestline (518.67%, 341.59%, and 762.25%, respectively), K content in Ayvalık × UAN-32 (43.07%), and Na content in Arbequina × Dermin (74.17%).

Highly significant effects of cultivar, treatment, and cultivar × treatment interaction were detected for fruit Ni, P, Pb, S, and Zn contents (p < 0.001 for all; Supplementary Table 3), indicating that accumulation of these elements is jointly shaped by genetic background and foliar fertilization with strong cultivar dependence. The Edincik Su variety showed high Ni content, the Arbequina variety showed high P content, and the Gemlik variety showed high Pb, S, and Zn content. Forceful Amino treatment showed high values for Ni content, Dermin treatment showed high values for P and Pb content, and Bestline treatment showed high values for S and Zn content. The highest increases were observed in Ni content (Ayvalık × Dermin 121.91%), P content (Ayvalık × UAN-32 30.40%), Pb content (Frantoio × Dermin 157.14%), S content (Edincik Su × Bestline 61.02%), and Zn content (Gemlik × Bestline 363.37%).

Highly significant effects of cultivar, treatment, and cultivar × treatment interaction were observed for leaf Al, B, Ca, Cr, and Cu contents (p < 0.001 for all; Supplementary Table 4), showing that leaf mineral composition is controlled by genetic background and further modulated by foliar fertilization in a cultivar-dependent manner. Based on the average of the varieties, Al content was higher in the Frantoio variety, B content in the Ayvalık variety, Ca content in the Edincik Su variety, Cr content in the Gemlik variety, and Cu content in the Arbequina variety. The Forceful Amino treatment for Al and B content, the Dermin treatment for Ca and Cu content, and the Bestline treatment for Cr content are the most effective treatments. The highest increases were observed in B, Ca, and Cu content in Gemlik × Dermin (91.51%, 116.10%, and 85.50%, respectively), Al content in Edincik Su × Bestline (57.52%), and Cr content in Gemlik × Bestline (279.45%).

Highly significant effects of cultivar, treatment, and their interaction were observed for leaf Fe, K, Mg, Mn, and Na contents (p < 0.001 for all; Supplementary Table 5), indicating that leaf mineral composition is largely cultivar-driven but is further modulated by foliar fertilization in a cultivar-dependent manner. Based on the average of the varieties, Fe, K, and Mn content were higher in the Frantoio variety, Mg content in the Edincik Su variety, and Na content in the Arbequina variety. The Forceful Amino treatment for Fe, K, and Na content, and the Dermin treatment for Mg and Mn content, are the most effective treatments.

The highest increases were observed in Ayvalık × Dermin (50.17%) for Fe content, in Arbequina × Forceful Amino (164.61%) for K content, in Gemlik × Dermin (102.18%) for Mg content, in Gemlik × Dermin (81.35%) for Mn content, and in Frantoio × Forceful Amino (53.28%) for Na content.

Highly significant effects of cultivar, treatment, and cultivar × treatment were observed for leaf Ni, P, Pb, S, and Zn (p < 0.001 for all; Supplementary Table 6), confirming that leaf mineral composition is primarily cultivar-dependent but can be markedly reshaped by foliar fertilization in a cultivar-specific manner. Based on the average of the varieties, Ni, Pb, and Zn content were higher in the Edincik Su variety, P content in the Arbequina variety, and S content in the Frantoio variety. The Forceful Amino treatment for Ni and Pb content, the Dermin treatment for P and S content, and the Bestline treatment for Zn are the most effective treatments. The highest increases in Ni content were observed in Ayvalık × Forceful Amino (192.80%), P content in Edincik Su × Forceful Amino (59.47%), Pb content in Ayvalık × Forceful Amino (141.07%), S content in Gemlik × Dermin (73.34%), and Zn content in Gemlik × Dermin (170.48%).

Discussion

Foliar fertilization promotes plant growth and increases resistance to environmental stresses; ultimately, this increases product yield and quality19. Foliar fertilizer treatments provide the advantage of rapid and direct uptake of plant nutrients and effective and homogeneous distribution at low treatment doses. In addition to standard fertilizer treatments, foliar fertilization has a positive effect on the nutrient content of olive leaves and fruits, as well as fruit yield and quality. Foliar fertilization is a suitable method for applying highly soluble fertilizers, particularly at low rates20.

The present findings show that fruit size and colour traits, biochemical quality indicators (total phenolic content, total flavonoid content, and total antioxidant activity), and mineral nutrition profiles in olive are primarily determined by genetic background (cultivar). At the same time, foliar treatments exerted significant main effects on many parameters, and the frequent occurrence of significant cultivar × treatment interactions confirms that foliar nutrition responses are not uniform but follow a cultivar-dependent pattern.

Leaves traits

The positive effect of foliar fertilizer treatments on growth characteristics can be attributed to their properties, which facilitate deeper penetration into leaf tissues and promote the efficient transport of nutrients to the plant’s growth centers21,22. Foliar fertilizer treatments improve nutrient uptake and lead to increased plant growth and development. Foliar fertilizer treatments are known to significantly increase leaf size, thereby improving photosynthetic efficiency and enhancing vegetative growth23. From a physiological standpoint, cultivar-dependent foliar responses are expected because the effectiveness of foliar treatments is constrained by sequential processes including solution retention on the leaf surface, cuticular penetration, drying-rewetting dynamics, and translocation to sink organs. These steps vary among cultivars due to differences in leaf surface traits (e.g., cuticle and wax features, stomatal distribution) and sink strength, helping explain why identical foliar treatments may produce divergent outcomes across genotypes24,25. In olive, the strong interaction among formulation chemistry and treatment microclimate further regulates penetration and redistribution, reinforcing cultivar-dependent responses10,24,25.

Pomological traits and production-oriented interpretation

The fruit characteristics of olives are used as important criteria. Fruit characteristics are important parameters for consumer acceptance of table olives26. Pomological results revealed clear differentiation among cultivars in fruit weight, fruit width, and fruit length, indicating that fruit size-one of the most critical attributes for table olive quality-is largely cultivar-driven. These findings underscore cultivar choice as a primary determinant in table olive production strategies. Nevertheless, the significant main effect of foliar treatments indicates that nutritional management can modulate fruit growth within cultivar-specific constraints.

The placement of UAN-32 in the upper group for mean fruit weight (3.30 g), together with its association with higher fruit width and length, suggests that N-based foliar treatments may support growth-related processes, potentially by improving canopy performance and assimilate supply. It is known that nitrogen-based foliar treatments improve canopy performance, thereby increasing assimilation and producing similar growth-promoting effects. Foliar fertilization increases photosynthetic pigments, photosynthesis rates, and carbohydrate accumulation in plants27. This increases the general productivity of the plants. Foliar fertilization with high nitrogen content increases leaf size. This effect increases photosynthetic efficiency and plant growth23. Improvements in fruit characteristics can be attributed to fertilizer treatments that increase photosynthetic efficiency and transport photosynthetic products to the fruit28,29. In addition, high nutrient concentrations in the leaves can significantly affect fruit growth and overall yield30.

Similar growth-promoting effects of foliar nutrient treatments including urea-N have been reported in olive31,32. Mechanistically, this pattern is consistent with nitrogen enhancing photosynthetic capacity, leaf protein content, and carbon assimilation, thereby shifting source-sink dynamics in favour of fruit growth. Olive nutrition studies further indicate that N availability-often evaluated together with K management-can influence fruit growth and size-related traits, although responses depend strongly on phenology, orchard conditions, and cultivar8,33. In addition, foliar N supply (e.g., urea- or nitrate-based formulations) may rapidly improve leaf N status during critical fruit growth stages, particularly when root uptake is constrained, supporting fruit growth dynamics10,34,35. However, the significant cultivar × treatment interaction indicates that both magnitude and direction of response vary among cultivars, so strategies should be optimized in a cultivar-specific manner rather than at the level of a single product.

Fruit colour traits

Significant differences among cultivars for colour parameters indicate that visual traits linked to skin colour and maturity are also strongly cultivar-driven. The absence of significant treatment effects on a* and b*, contrasted with a significant effect on L*, suggests that foliar fertilization may influence appearance primarily along the lightness/brightness axis rather than broadly shifting chromatic components. The increase in L* under Dermin (29.25) may be advantageous for market appearance in some contexts; however, given cultivar × treatment interaction, this effect should be interpreted as cultivar-specific rather than generalized.

Biochemical quality and the TPC/TFC-AA dissociation

Foliar treatments may influence phenolic metabolism not only through improved N or micronutrient supply but also by modulating enzymatic pathways in secondary metabolism. Previous studies indicate that foliar nutrition can affect phenolic profiles via changes in phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) activity and redox-related enzymes, with effects highly dependent on cultivar and timing10. Phenolic compounds are principal determinants of sensory attributes and oxidative stability in olive products, making variability in this fraction central for production objectives1–3. In the present study, strong differentiation among cultivars in biochemical parameters supports a pronounced “cultivar signature”. Ayvalık exhibited the highest total phenolic content (300.89) and antioxidant activity (28.42), Gemlik showed the lowest total phenolics (139.65), and Frantoio had the lowest antioxidant activity (12.80) but the highest total flavonoids (149.98). These patterns align with evidence that genotype is a dominant driver of phenolic metabolism and compound pool size in olive4.

The significant main effects of foliar treatments indicate that foliar nutrition can meaningfully modulate biochemical traits. UAN-32 produced the highest mean total phenolics (271.89) and total flavonoids (139.13), whereas Dermin resulted in the highest antioxidant activity (22.78) and UAN-32 remained in a lower group for antioxidant activity (14.98). This divergence emphasizes that increases in total phenolics do not necessarily translate into proportional increases in antioxidant activity, likely because antioxidant performance depends on phenolic composition (including the relative contribution of secoiridoid derivatives) and assay-specific sensitivity1,3. Although nitrogen fertilizer treatments, which cause a wide change in the activity of enzymes such as PAL, have been reported to have negative effects on phenolic content23,36, the effect of microelements such as zinc, iron, and copper, as well as elements such as boron and potassium, on enzymes related to secondary metabolites positively affects phenolic compounds14,37. Similar findings to our study results have been reported in the literature37,38. The antioxidant capacity of olives varies depending on different components such as carbohydrates, free fatty acids, pigments, and phenolic compounds14,39. While nitrogen fertilizer treatments negatively affect antioxidant activity, fertilizers such as zinc, copper, boron, and potassium have been reported to positively affect antioxidant content14,23,37,38. The findings obtained in this study are corroborated by the literature.

A particularly robust outcome is the highly significant cultivar × treatment interaction for biochemical traits. The highest total phenolic content in Frantoio × Forceful Amino (451.59) and the maximum antioxidant activity in Ayvalık × Dermin (35.17) show that foliar effects can vary in both magnitude and direction depending on cultivar. Notably, increases in TPC and TFC did not consistently translate into higher antioxidant activity, suggesting that foliar inputs may shift phenolic composition and redox-relevant metabolites qualitatively rather than merely enlarging total pools. This dissociation supports the need to interpret bulk phenolic indicators together with cultivar-specific metabolic capacity and nutrient partitioning. This is consistent with reports indicating that, although genotype often dominates phenolic variability, genotype × environment or genotype × management interactions can be strong for selected parameters5. Thus, foliar treatments may interact with cultivar-specific metabolic capacity and foliar uptake/redistribution constraints, yielding high-quality outcomes in specific combinations rather than uniform improvements across cultivars24,25.

Contribution of multivariate analyses

Multivariate analyses (PCA) and relationship patterns (correlation matrices and hierarchical clustering/heatmaps) strengthened biological interpretation. In the combined analysis of fruit morphology, colour, and biochemical traits, strong positive loadings of fruit width, length, and weight on PC1 defined a “fruit size axis,” consistent with strong positive correlations among these traits. This indicates that fruit size components form a shared growth-related module rather than independent traits. PC2, more closely associated with leaf morphology and flesh thickness, suggests that source organ size and tissue structural properties represent a secondary variation axis accompanying fruit development. Partial co-variation between phenolic and colour parameters implies that visual quality and phenolic metabolism can respond in parallel in some contexts, although relationships are modulated by genotype and treatment5,40. Given the increasing reliance on chemometric profiles and the known influence of year/harvest on composition, multi-year validation would strengthen robustness of cultivar × treatment patterns4.

Mineral nutrition as an integrated network and organ-specific partitioning

The variation of mineral nutrient contents in leaves and fruits with cultivar and treatment, together with frequent cultivar × treatment interactions, indicates cultivar-dependent nutrient uptake and inter-organ transport. Mineral dynamics should be interpreted through macronutrients associated with fruit growth and tissue properties (K, Ca, Mg) and micronutrients linked to redox metabolism and enzymatic systems (Fe, Zn, Mn). The clustering of elements with similar loadings in mineral PCA, along with co-varying element groups in correlation and heatmap analyses, supports the view that mineral nutrition is organized as an integrated network rather than independent elemental responses. Antagonistic patterns between K and several divalent cations (e.g., Mg, Mn, Fe) are consistent with competitive interactions, underscoring the need to interpret foliar treatments in terms of nutrient balance rather than absolute increases8. Coordinated behaviour of Mg, Zn, Mn, Fe, and S between leaves and fruits further suggests strong source-sink coupling for these elements under certain cultivar-treatment contexts8,10. The partial decoupling between leaf and fruit mineral profiles is also consistent with evidence that foliar-absorbed nutrients are not uniformly redistributed but are regulated by sink strength, phenological stage, and cultivar-specific transport capacity8,10.

Implications for cultivar-specific, objective-oriented management

Overall, foliar treatments can be differentiated according to production objectives. The observed effects should be interpreted primarily in terms of nutrient composition and physiological function rather than brand identity. Accordingly, no foliar product can be recommended as universally superior across cultivars. For table olive production, the consistent positioning of UAN-32 in the upper group for fruit weight and size parameters suggests potential usefulness in growth-oriented strategies, consistent with reports that urea-N and related inputs can enhance vegetative performance and growth and thereby indirectly support fruit development depending on cultivar and management context31,32. In contrast, for functional quality objectives, Dermin and Bestline improved antioxidant activity in specific cultivars, supporting their potential in quality-oriented strategies3,4. This is consistent with evidence that foliar products containing biostimulant-like components (e.g., amino acids, protein hydrolysates, humic/fulvic substances) can improve physiological performance and quality-related traits in olive, although responses depend strongly on cultivar, timing, and environment10,41,42. Thus, cultivar and target traits should be considered jointly when designing foliar nutrition programs. Taken together, the central message is that responses were strongly cultivar dependent, and treatment effects should be interpreted as cultivar-specific shifts rather than uniform improvements.

Limitations and future perspectives

Although sulfur (S) was included among the reported mineral elements, silicon (Si) and selenium (Se) were not measured or applied; therefore, their effects could not be evaluated directly. Nevertheless, beneficial elements such as Si and Se are known to modulate stress-related metabolism and antioxidant systems in perennial crops, suggesting potential value for future cultivar-targeted foliar programs43,44. In addition, foliar programs including Zn and B have been associated with fruit traits and quality-related parameters under specific conditions, supporting the broader view that cultivar-specific nutrient strategies can influence both growth and quality outcomes45. Because phenolic composition may vary across seasons, multi-year validation under contrasting environmental conditions would improve generalizability6,7. Compound-level phenolic profiling-particularly of secoiridoid derivatives-together with bulk indicators would help clarify the mechanisms underlying the observed dissociation between total phenolics and antioxidant activity1,3. Finally, the present findings are based on a single-year and single-location study; therefore, multi-year and multi-environment validation studies are recommended to confirm the stability of the cultivar × treatment models reported here.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that foliar fertilizer treatments in five olive cultivars significantly affected fruit morphology and color traits, biochemical quality indicators, and leaf-fruit mineral nutrient contents, with responses largely governed by cultivar × treatment interactions. From a pomological perspective, Edincik Su was distinguished by the largest fruits, while UAN-32 induced the most pronounced increases in fruit size components, highlighting its relevance for table olive production objectives. In terms of biochemical quality, Ayvalık showed superior phenolic and antioxidant performance; at the treatment level, UAN-32 was more closely associated with increases in total phenolics and total flavonoids, whereas Dermin and Bestline were more consistently linked to enhanced antioxidant activity in cultivar-specific combinations. Mineral analyses further indicated that foliar fertilization can modify inter-organ nutrient partitioning for selected elements, reinforcing that optimization should be conducted according to the targeted trait and cultivar. The study’s findings reveal that a single fertilizer treatment is not suitable for all olive varieties and that variety-specific fertilization programs are important for yield and quality. These findings demonstrate that fertilization programs in olive cultivation should be optimized according to the targeted quality parameter (fruit size, antioxidant capacity, or specific mineral richness) and the cultivars’ genetic response characteristics.
