On The Complexity of Best-Arm Identification in Non-Stationary Linear Bandits
Leo Maynard-Zhang, Zhihan Xiong, Kevin Jamieson, Maryam Fazel

Abstract
We study the fixed-budget best-arm identification (BAI) problem in non-stationary linear bandits. Concretely, given a fixed time budget T ∈ ℕ T\in\mathbb{N} , finite arm set 𝒳 ⊂ ℝ d \mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} , and a potentially adversarial sequence of unknown parameters { θ t } t = 1 T \{\theta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T} (hence non-stationary), a learner aims to identify the arm with the largest cumulative reward x ∗ = arg ⁡ max x ∈ 𝒳 ​ x ⊤ ​ ∑ t = 1 T θ t x_{*}=\arg\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}x^{\top}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\theta_{t} with high probability. In this setting, it is well-known that uniformly sampling arms from the G-optimal design yields a minimax-optimal error probability of exp ⁡ ( − Θ ⁡ ( T / H G ) ) \exp\left(-\Theta\left(T/H_{G}\right)\right) , where H G H_{G} scales proportionally with the dimension d d . However, this notion of complexity is overly pessimistic, as it is derived from a lower bound in which the arm set consists only of the standard basis vectors, thus masking any potential advantages arising from arm sets with richer geometric structure. To address this, we establish an arm-set-dependent lower bound that, in contrast, holds for any arm set. Motivated by the ideas underlying our lower bound, we propose the Adjacent-optimal design , a specialization of the well-known 𝒳 ​ 𝒴 \mathcal{XY} -optimal design, and develop the Adjacent-BAI algorithm. We prove that the error probability of Adjacent-BAI matches our lower bound up to constants, verifying the tightness of our lower bound, and establishing the arm-set-dependent complexity of this setting.



1
Introduction

We focus on quantifying the difficulty of fixed-budget best-arm identification (BAI) problem in non-stationary linear bandits. The linear bandit framework generalizes the classical multi-armed bandit problem by associating each arm with a feature vector and modeling rewards as linear in an unknown parameter. Algorithms such as UCB and Thompson Sampling are known to perform optimally in the well-studied regret-minimization setting
(
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
)
. However, in the best-arm identification setting, where exploration is more important than cumulative reward, these algorithms are suboptimal
(
Bubeck et al. 2009
)
, thus requiring specialized attention. In the standard BAI setting, a stationary environment is assumed where the rewards of arms are sampled i.i.d
(
Audibert and Bubeck 2010
;
Soare et al. 2014
;
Fiez et al. 2019
)
. However, algorithms designed for these environments can completely fail as soon as this assumption is lifted, for example, in settings where the value of an arm can change at any time step
(
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2018
;
Xiong et al. 2024
)
. An interesting question is
exactly how the difficulty of this setting changes once this stationary assumption is lifted
.

In recent work,
Xiong et al. 2024
partially answered this question by showing that when the arm set is restricted to the standard basis, the difficulty of non-stationary BAI scales proportionally with the dimension. While informative, this result is ultimately unsatisfying as it collapses the linear bandit model back into a multi-armed bandit. Basis arm sets erase correlations between arms, precisely the geometric structure that linear bandits are meant to exploit. With this in mind, we aim to uncover precisely which relationship between arms truly governs the difficulty of non-stationary BAI. Inspired by
Soare et al. 2014
, a natural conjecture is that the pairwise relationships between every arm should be considered when measuring difficulty. However, such a characterization implies that any arm set containing a basis is no easier than the basis itself, and thus no progress is made. To refine this view, we introduce adjacency, a geometric notion that captures which pairs of arms can compete for optimality. Accordingly, we find that the pairwise relationships between adjacent arms are necessary and sufficient to measure the difficulty of non-stationary BAI. Formally, by leveraging the adjacent structure of the arm set, we refine the previous notion of difficulty in non-stationary BAI by establishing a measure of complexity that adapts to the geometry of
any
arm set, which we term as
arm-set-dependent
.

Our contributions.

•

Adjacency and Non-Stationary BAI.
The main novelty of our paper stems from our characterization of non-stationary BAI as a problem of differentiating only between adjacent arms, a notion we formalize in later sections. The intuition for this restriction stems from our central Lemma
1
, which implies that if an arm is better than its adjacent arms, then it is the optimal arm. Accordingly, we introduce a new arm-set-dependent complexity measure
H_{\mathrm{Adjacent}}(\mathcal{X})
and show it strictly refines the minimax-optimal complexity
H_{G}
.

•

Arm-set-dependent lower bound.
We show in Theorem
1
that for fixed arm set
\mathcal{X}
, the error probability of any algorithm must be at least
\exp\left(-O\left(T/H_{\mathrm{Adjacent}}(\mathcal{X})\right)\right)
. Our lower bound builds off of previous works
(
Kaufmann et al. 2016
;
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2018
)
concerning lower bounds in best-arm identification for the multi-armed bandit setting. However, these methods alone are not sufficient for the linear setting, which we address by characterizing the lower bound as an optimization problem in Lemma
3
, and which we solve in Lemma
4
by exploiting the adjacent geometry of the arm set.

•

Matching upper bound.
We introduce the Adjacent-optimal design, a specialization of the well-known
\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}
-optimal design
(
Soare et al. 2014
)
, that instead reduces variance only between adjacent arms. Using the Adjacent-optimal design, we introduce the algorithm
Adjacent-BAI
. Utilizing Lemma
1
, we obtain Theorem
2
showing that for fixed arm set
\mathcal{X}
, the error probability of
Adjacent-BAI
is at most
\exp\left(-\Omega\left(T/H_{\mathrm{Adjacent}}(\mathcal{X})\right)\right)
, verifying the tightness of our lower bound, and establishing
H_{\mathrm{Adjacent}}(\mathcal{X})
as
the arm-set-dependent complexity measure of this setting.

2
Related Work

Best-arm identification (BAI) seeks to identify the optimal arm while ignoring cumulative reward or regret.
Even-Dar et al. 2002
marked the first substantial result in this area and the problem is now studied under two canonical settings: (i) the
fixed-confidence
setting, where the learner attains a prescribed error probability target using as few pulls as possible, and (ii) the
fixed-budget
setting, where the learner minimizes the error probability under a prescribed sampling budget; the latter was introduced by
Bubeck et al. 2009
.

Best-arm identification with fixed confidence.

For multi-armed bandits,
Even-Dar et al. 2002
established the first sample-complexity upper bound, with a nearly matching lower bound later given by
Mannor and Tsitsiklis 2004
. These bounds were subsequently tightened by
Karnin et al. 2013
and
Kaufmann et al. 2016
. Notably,
Kaufmann et al. 2016
derived a general information-theoretic lower bound that underpins many linear-bandit lower bounds
(
Fiez et al. 2019
;
Jedra and Proutiere 2020
;
Camilleri et al. 2021b
)
. In linear bandits,
Soare et al. 2014
proposed the seminal
\mathcal{XY}
-allocation strategy, which has been refined and generalized in follow-up work
(
Tao et al. 2018
;
Xu et al. 2018
;
Degenne et al. 2020
;
Fiez et al. 2019
;
Jedra and Proutiere 2020
;
Katz-Samuels et al. 2020
)
. Nearly matching lower bounds were also established by
Fiez et al. 2019
and
Jedra and Proutiere 2020
.

Best-arm identification with fixed budget.

For multi-armed bandits,
Audibert and Bubeck 2010
provided nearly matching upper and lower bounds, with improvements due to
Karnin et al. 2013
and
Kaufmann et al. 2016
. In contrast, comparatively little is known for linear bandits in this setting. Among existing results,
Katz-Samuels et al. 2020
—via a Gaussian-width analysis—gave the only arm-set-dependent upper bound. Methods based on G-optimal design yield arm-set-
independent
guarantees
(
Azizi et al. 2022
;
Yang and Tan 2022
)
, as does the Bayesian-style approach of
Hoffman et al. 2014
; similarly, a method based on the
\mathcal{XY}
-design in
Alieva et al. 2021
remains arm-set-independent. On the lower-bound side,
Yang and Tan 2022
proved a minimax bound, but to date, there is no arm-set-dependent lower bound for stationary linear bandits in the fixed-budget setting.

Best-arm identification in non-stationary environments.

Compared with regret minimization, non-stationarity has received less attention in BAI. In the fixed-confidence regime, non-stationary multi-armed bandits under models different from ours were studied by
Jamieson and Talwalkar 2016
and
Li et al. 2018
. In the fixed-budget regime,
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2018
analyzed multi-armed bandits from a best-of-both-worlds perspective and established matching upper and lower bounds in a non-stationary setting;
Xiong et al. 2024
extended the upper bounds to linear bandits. However,
Xiong et al. 2024
does not provide lower bounds, and its non-stationary guarantees are arm-set-independent. Our work adopts a non-stationary model similar to
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2018
and
Xiong et al. 2024
and, to the best of our knowledge, presents the first arm-set-dependent lower bound for the fixed-budget setting.

Preliminaries

Notation.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, denote
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
. For a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a positive semidefinite matrix
A\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d}
, denote
\|x\|_{A}=\sqrt{x^{\top}Ax}
as the Mahalanobis norm. For finite set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and distribution
\lambda\in\triangle_{\mathcal{X}}
over
\mathcal{X}
, denote
A(\lambda)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\lambda_{x}xx^{\top}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, denote
\Pi(n)
as the set of all permutations on
[n]
.

Definition 1
.

Given a finite set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\mathcal{P}=\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})
be the convex hull of
\mathcal{X}
, which is a polytope. We say
x\in\mathcal{X}
is an
extreme point
if there exists
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\{x\}=\argmax_{y\in\mathcal{P}}y^{\top}w.

(1)

Equivalently, the extreme points of
\mathcal{X}
are the vertices of
\mathcal{P}
. Two distinct extreme points
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
are
adjacent
if there exists
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\conv(\{x,x^{\prime}\})=\argmax_{y\in\mathcal{P}}y^{\top}w.

(2)

Equivalently,
x
and
x^{\prime}
are adjacent if the line segment connecting them is an edge of
\mathcal{P}
.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{X}}
denote the set of extreme points of
\mathcal{X}
. We denote

\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{X}}:=\left\{(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{X}}\times\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{X}}:x\neq x^{\prime}\right\}

(3)

as the set of all distinct extreme point pairs of
\mathcal{X}
. Further, we denote

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}:=\left\{(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Y}_{\mathcal{X}}:x\text{ is adjacent to }x^{\prime}\right\}

(4)

as the set of all adjacent pairs of
\mathcal{X}
, and, for
x\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{X}}
, we denote

\mathcal{I}^{x}_{\mathcal{X}}:=\left\{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{X}}:(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}\right\}

(5)

as the set of all points adjacent to
x
. It follows from this notation that

(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}\iff\exists~w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ s.t. }\{x,x^{\prime}\}=\argmax_{y\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{X}}}y^{\top}w.

(6)

We omit the
\mathcal{X}
subscript when clear from context.

4
BAI in Non-Stationary Linear Bandits

We adopt a standard model of non-stationary linear bandits with fixed horizon
T\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, given a finite arm set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|\mathcal{X}|=K
and
\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{X})=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, an adversary fixes the parameter sequence
\left\{\theta_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
, which remains unknown to the learner. Then, at each round
t=1,\dots,T
, the learner selects arm
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
and observes a reward
r_{t}=x_{t}^{\top}\theta_{t}+\epsilon_{t}
, where each
\epsilon_{t}
is independent, zero-mean, and 1-sub-Gaussian noise. Together, the arm set
\mathcal{X}
, parameter sequence
\left\{\theta_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
, and distribution of
\left\{\epsilon_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
form an
instance
. The learner’s objective is to identify the hindsight best arm
x_{*}=\argmax_{x\in\mathcal{X}}x^{\top}\overline{\theta}_{T}
, where
\overline{\theta}_{T}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\theta_{t}
, and for simplicity, we assume that this best arm is unique.

A central measure of difficulty in bandit problems is the reward gap between the first and second best arm. Here, we deviate slightly from the standard definition by defining this gap only in terms of extreme points
\mathcal{V}
. This restriction is natural since by definition, non-extreme point arms can never be the best arm, and thus do not affect problem difficulty. Formally, we define the
min-gap

\Delta_{(1)}
as the following:

\Delta_{(1)}:=\min_{x\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{x_{*}\}}(x_{{}_{*}}-x)^{\top}\overline{\theta}_{T}.

(7)

Finally, for event
\mathcal{E}
, we denote
\mathbb{P}_{\left\{\theta_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}}(\mathcal{E})
as the probability of
\mathcal{E}
under instance with parameter sequence
\left\{\theta_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
. Accordingly, we measure algorithmic performance by the error probability
\mathbb{P}_{\left\{\theta_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}}(\widehat{x}\neq x_{*})
, where
\widehat{x}
denotes the arm output by the learner. We omit the dependence on
\left\{\theta_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
when clear from context.

4.1
Minimax-Optimal Complexity in Non-Stationary BAI

In prior work in the non-stationary setting,
Xiong et al. 2024
showed that uniform sampling from the well-known G-optimal design
(
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
)

\lambda^{G}:=\argmin_{\lambda\in\triangle_{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\|x\|^{2}_{A(\lambda)^{-1}}

(8)

leads to a minimax-optimal error probability of

\exp\left(-\Theta\left(\frac{T}{H_{G}\left(\Delta_{(1)}\right)}\right)\right),

(9)

where

H_{G}\left(\Delta_{(1)}\right):=\frac{d}{\Delta_{(1)}^{2}}.

(10)

The relationship between
H_{G}
and the G-optimal design stems from the Kiefer–Wolfowitz Theorem
(
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
)
, which states that

\min_{\lambda\in\triangle_{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\|x\|^{2}_{A(\lambda)^{-1}}=d.

(11)

H_{G}
is minimax-optimal since if the arm set is restricted to the standard basis vectors,
H_{G}
matches the optimal complexity for the multi-armed bandit setting of
H_{\mathrm{UNIF}}=\frac{K}{\Delta_{(1)}^{2}}

(
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2018
)
. However, this notion of complexity is overly pessimistic in the linear bandit setting as it ignores potential advantages arising from arm sets with more complex structure. In contrast, we refine this notion of complexity by establishing an arm-set-dependent complexity that adapts to the geometry of any given arm set.

4.2
Adjacency and Non-Stationary BAI

The main results of our paper are motivated by the following central lemma.

Lemma 1

(Adjacency Lemma)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For any
x\in\mathcal{V}
, there exists
y\in\mathcal{X}
such that
(y-x)^{\top}\theta>0
if and only if there exists
z\in\mathcal{I}^{x}
such that
(z-x)^{\top}\theta>0
.

The formal proof of Lemma
1
is given in Appendix
B
. The idea behind the proof is quite simple: it is well known
(
Ziegler 1995
)
that for a polytope
P
and a vertex
x\in P
,

P\subseteq x+\mathrm{cone}\left(\left\{x^{\prime}-x:x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}^{x}\right\}\right).

(12)

Thus, the quantity
(y-x)^{\top}\theta
must be a conic (non-negative) combination of the points in the set
\left\{(x^{\prime}-x)^{\top}\theta:x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}^{x}\right\}
. Lemma
1
follows immediately from this fact.

One implication of Lemma
1
is that the first and second best arms of any instance must be adjacent. When constructing hard instances for our lower bound in Section
5
, the key strategy is to construct two instances that are hard to distinguish from each other but have different best arms. Specifically, we construct one instance with best arm
x
and second best arm
x^{\prime}
, and another instance with best arm
x^{\prime}
and second best arm
x
. Lemma
1
tells us that to be able to do this,
x
and
x^{\prime}
need to be adjacent. Another implication of Lemma
1
comes from its negation, that is, if an arm is better than all its adjacent arms, then it must be the optimal arm. This implies that when identifying the best arm, we need only accurate comparisons between adjacent arms, a detail we use to design our algorithm with matching upper bound in Section
6
. The main takeaway is that the distinguishability between adjacent arms solely determines the difficulty of identification. Given this, we introduce the following complexity measure

H_{\mathrm{Adjacent}}\left(\mathcal{X},\Delta_{(1)}\right):=\frac{\min_{\lambda\in\triangle_{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}\left\|x-x^{\prime}\right\|^{2}_{A(\lambda)^{-1}}}{\Delta_{(1)}^{2}}.

(13)

By Eq. (
11
), we observe that for any arm set
\mathcal{X}

H_{\mathrm{Adjacent}}\left(\mathcal{X},\Delta_{(1)}\right)\leq 4H_{G}\left(\Delta_{(1)}\right).

(14)

Importantly, the above inequality can be arbitrarily loose for dense arm sets, showing that
H_{\mathrm{Adjacent}}
strictly refines the minimax-optimal complexity of
Xiong et al. 2024
. For example, let
\mathcal{C}(K)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
represent the arm set consisting of
K
uniformly spaced arms on the unit circle. It is not hard to see that

\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}\frac{H_{\mathrm{Adjacent}}\left(\mathcal{C}(K),\Delta_{(1)}\right)}{H_{G}\left(\Delta_{(1)}\right)}=\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\min_{\lambda\in\triangle_{\mathcal{C}(K)}}\max_{(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}(K)}}\left\|x-x^{\prime}\right\|^{2}_{A(\lambda)^{-1}}}{2}=0,

(15)

since as the circle becomes more crowded, the distances between adjacent arms shrink toward zero. In the following sections, we establish
H_{\mathrm{Adjacent}}
as the arm-set-dependent complexity of this setting through a matching error probability lower and upper bound of

\exp\left(-\Theta\left(\frac{T}{H_{\mathrm{Adjacent}}\left(\mathcal{X},\Delta_{(1)}\right)}\right)\right).

(16)

5
Lower Bound of BAI in Non-Stationary Linear Bandits

We present, to the best of our knowledge, the first arm-set-dependent lower bound for BAI in the non-stationary linear bandit setting.

Theorem 1
.

Fix
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Delta_{(1)}>0
. For any algorithm, there exist parameter sequences
\left\{\theta_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
and
\left\{\theta^{\prime}_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
, both with min-gap at least
\Delta_{(1)}
, such that

\max\left\{\mathbb{P}_{\left\{\theta_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}}\left(\widehat{x}\neq x_{*}\right),\mathbb{P}_{\left\{\theta^{\prime}_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}}\left(\widehat{x}\neq x_{*}\right)\right\}\geq\frac{1}{4}\exp\left(-\frac{4T}{H_{\mathrm{Adjacent}}\left(\mathcal{X},\Delta_{(1)}\right)}\right),

(17)

where
H_{\mathrm{Adjacent}}\left(\mathcal{X},\Delta_{(1)}\right)
is given by Eq. (
13
).

The proof is deferred to Appendix
A
, of which we include a sketch in the next subsection. In Section
6
, we present an algorithm that achieves the lower bound in Theorem
1
, verifying the tightness of our bound, and establishing
H_{\mathrm{Adjacent}}
as the arm-set-dependent complexity of this setting.

5.1
Proof Sketch of Theorem
1

In this section, we give a proof sketch of Theorem
1
. We first obtain Lemma
2
, which characterizes the lower bound by the
KL divergence
between two non-stationary multi-armed bandit instances and is a generalization of the stationary lower bound proposed in Lemma 15 of
Kaufmann et al. 2016
. Generalizing the notation from
Kaufmann et al. 2016
, the statement of Lemma
2
is as follows:

Lemma 2
.

Let
\nu=\left\{\nu_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
and
\nu^{\prime}=\left\{\nu^{\prime}_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
be non-stationary bandit models with different best arms. Then

\max\left(\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left(\widehat{k}\neq k_{*}\right),\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{\prime}}\left(\widehat{k}\neq k_{*}\right)\right)\geq\frac{1}{4}\exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}_{\nu}(A_{t}=k)\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{t,k},\nu^{\prime}_{t,k})\right).

(18)

The proof of this lemma is deferred to Appendix
A
and very closely follows that of Lemma 15 in
Kaufmann et al. 2016
. Applying Lemma
2
requires two instances with different best arms, which motivates the following definition.

Definition 2
.

Given fixed arm set
\mathcal{X}
and
\Delta_{(1)}>0
, two parameter sequences
\{\theta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and
\{\theta^{\prime}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
are
feasible
if they have different best arms, and each has min-gap at least
\Delta_{(1)}
.

We now proceed with the proof sketch of Theorem
1
.

First step.

We use Lemma
2
to obtain Lemma
3
which states the following.

Lemma 3

(Optimization-based Lower Bound)
.

Fix
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Delta_{(1)}>0
. For any algorithm, there exist parameter sequences
\left\{\theta_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
and
\left\{\theta^{\prime}_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
, both with min-gap at least
\Delta_{(1)}
, such that

\max\left\{\mathbb{P}_{\left\{\theta_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}}\left(\widehat{x}\neq x_{*}\right),\mathbb{P}_{\left\{\theta^{\prime}_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}}\left(\widehat{x}\neq x_{*}\right)\right\}\geq\frac{1}{4}\exp\left(-Tf\left(\mathcal{X},\Delta_{(1)}\right)\right),

(19)

where

f\left(\mathcal{X},\Delta_{(1)}\right):=\begin{array}[]{rl}\max_{\lambda\in\triangle_{\mathcal{X}}}\min_{(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Y}}\min_{\theta,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}&v^{\top}A(\lambda)v\\
\mbox{\rm subject to}&(x-y)^{\top}\theta\geq\Delta_{(1)}\quad\forall~y\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{x\}\\
&(x^{\prime}-y)^{\top}(\theta+v)\geq\Delta_{(1)}\quad\forall~y\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{x^{\prime}\}\end{array}.

(20)

To prove Lemma
3
, the core idea behind our construction is to split the horizon into two distinct halves, similar to that of
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2018
. Unlike their explicit construction, however, we characterize the hard instance through an optimization problem. At a high level, the first half allows us to minimize the KL divergence of the two instances, while the second half ensures feasibility. Concretely, first, we pick a candidate pair of distinct arms
(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Y}
as potential best arms for the two instances. Then, we proceed to construct the parameter sequences
\{\theta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and
\{\theta^{\prime}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
. For
t\leq\frac{T}{2}
we choose
\theta_{t}=0
1
1

1

The choice of
\theta_{t}=0
is arbitrary, any fixed choice such that
\theta_{t}^{\prime}-\theta_{t}=v
works.
and
\theta_{t}^{\prime}=v
, for perturbation
v
of our choice. Then for
t>\frac{T}{2}
we set
\theta_{t}=\theta_{t}^{\prime}=\theta
for some
\theta
of our choice. Further, we use the noise distribution
\epsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
for each
t
, resulting in a KL divergence between the reward distributions of arm
x
at time step
t
of

\mathrm{KL}\left(\mathcal{N}\left(x^{\top}\theta_{t},1\right),\mathcal{N}\left(x_{t}^{\top}\theta_{t}^{\prime},1\right)\right)=\frac{(x^{\top}(\theta_{t}-\theta^{\prime}_{t}))^{2}}{2}.

(21)

For such a construction, applying Lemma
2
, we obtain the lower bound

\frac{1}{4}\exp\left(-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{t=1}^{T/2}\mathbb{P}_{\{\theta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}}(x_{t}=x)\frac{(x^{\top}v)^{2}}{2}\right).

(22)

An algorithm cannot depend on the future, so for any
t\leq T/2
we have that

\mathbb{P}_{\{\theta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}}(x_{t}=x)=\mathbb{P}_{\{\theta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T/2}}(x_{t}=x).

(23)

Define
\lambda\in\triangle_{\mathcal{X}}
such that for each

\lambda_{x}:=\frac{2\sum_{t=1}^{T/2}\mathbb{P}_{\{\theta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T/2}}(x_{t}=x)}{T}.

(24)

Then, with some algebra, we can show that

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{t=1}^{T/2}\mathbb{P}_{\{\theta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T/2}}(x_{t}=x)\;\frac{(x^{\top}v)^{2}}{2}=\frac{T}{4}v^{\top}A(\lambda)v,

(25)

yielding the objective in
f\left(\mathcal{X},\Delta_{(1)}\right)
. The choice of
\theta
in the second half enforces feasibility with respect to
(x,x^{\prime})
, giving rise to the constraints of
f\left(\mathcal{X},\Delta_{(1)}\right)
. Crucially, since

\{\theta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T/2}
, we may choose them freely without affecting
\lambda
. To obtain the hardest feasible instance, we minimize over arm pairs
(x,x^{\prime})
, perturbations
v
, and parameters
\theta
to make the KL divergence as small as possible. Finally, maximizing over
\lambda
ensures the lower bound holds for any algorithm, concluding the proof of Lemma
3
.

Second step.

We bound the previous optimization problem by solving the following relaxed version of the optimization problem introduced in Lemma
3
:

\widehat{f}\left(\mathcal{X},\Delta_{(1)}\right):=\begin{array}[]{rl}\max_{\lambda\in\triangle_{\mathcal{X}}}\min_{(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}\min_{\theta,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}&v^{\top}A(\lambda)v\\
\mbox{\rm subject to}&(x-y)^{\top}\theta\geq\Delta_{(1)}\quad\forall~y\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{x\}\\
&(x^{\prime}-y)^{\top}(\theta+v)\geq\Delta_{(1)}\quad\forall~y\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{x^{\prime}\}\end{array}.

(26)

This is a relaxation because
\widehat{f}\left(\mathcal{X},\Delta_{(1)}\right)
minimizes only over adjacent pairs
(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}
rather than all extreme point pairs
(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Y}
. Since,
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
, and hence
\widehat{f}\left(\mathcal{X},\Delta_{(1)}\right)\geq f\left(\mathcal{X},\Delta_{(1)}\right)
, we can substitute
f
by
\widehat{f}
in the bound corresponding to Lemma
3
. Requiring
(x,x^{\prime})
to be adjacent is crucial: it enables a closed-form solution of the inner
(\theta,v)
problem, described by the following lemma.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Delta_{(1)}>0
. Consider
\lambda\in\triangle_{\mathcal{X}}
with
A(\lambda)
full rank and
(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}
. We have:

\begin{array}[]{rl}\min_{\theta,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}&v^{\top}A(\lambda)v\\
\mbox{\rm subject to}&(x-y)^{\top}\theta\geq\Delta_{(1)}\quad\forall~y\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{x\}\\
&(x^{\prime}-y)^{\top}(\theta+v)\geq\Delta_{(1)}\quad\forall~y\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{x^{\prime}\}\end{array}=\frac{4\Delta_{(1)}^{2}}{\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}_{A(\lambda)^{-1}}}.

(27)

The ideas behind the proof of Lemma
4
are as follows. Denote
p^{*}:=\frac{4\Delta_{(1)}^{2}}{\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}_{A(\lambda)^{-1}}}.
We first lower bound the optimization problem by
p^{*}
with a simple Cauchy-Schwarz argument. Then, to upper bound the optimization problem by
p^{*}
, we use the key fact that for adjacent
(x,x^{\prime})
, by Eq. (
6
), there exists some
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\{x,x^{\prime}\}=\argmax_{y\in\mathcal{V}}y^{\top}w.

(28)

Thus, there is some
\varepsilon>0
such that
x^{\top}w=x^{\prime\top}w\geq y^{\top}w+\varepsilon
for all
y\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{x,x^{\prime}\}
. We choose
v,u
such that
v^{\top}A(\lambda)^{-1}v=p^{*}
and

(x-x^{\prime})^{\top}u=(x^{\prime}-x)^{\top}(u+v)=\Delta_{(1)}.

(29)

Then we pick
\theta=u+\alpha w
, for some
\alpha
to be chosen. Note that since
x^{\top}w=x^{\prime\top}w
, we have

(x-x^{\prime})^{\top}\theta=(x-x^{\prime})^{\top}u,

(30)

and thus

(x-x^{\prime})^{\top}\theta=(x^{\prime}-x)^{\top}(\theta+v)=\Delta_{(1)},

(31)

regardless of the value of
\alpha
, so this constraint is always satisfied. For any
y\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{x,x^{\prime}\}
we have

(x-y)^{\top}\theta\geq(x-y)^{\top}u+\alpha\varepsilon,

(32)

and

(x^{\prime}-y)^{\top}(\theta+v)\geq(x-y)^{\top}(u+v)+\alpha\varepsilon.

(33)

Hence, we can increase
\alpha
until all other constraints are satisfied, thus
(\theta,v)
is feasible, concluding the proof of Lemma
4
. Combining Lemmas
3
and
4
, we obtain Theorem
1
.

6
Matching Upper Bound

In this section, we first introduce the Adjacent-optimal design, which is a modified version of the well-known
\mathcal{XY}
-optimal design. Based on this new design, we then develop the algorithm
Adjacent-BAI
, and show that it achieves an error probability guarantee that matches the lower bound presented in Section
5
, verifying the tightness of our lower bound, and establishing the arm-set-dependent complexity of this setting.

6.1
The Adjacent-Optimal Design

A central quantity in BAI problems is the
\mathcal{XY}
-optimal design
(
Soare et al. 2014
)
, which aims to uniformly minimize the variance of predictions in directions defined by the pairwise differences between all arms. Formally, the
\mathcal{XY}
-optimal design is defined as

\lambda^{\mathcal{XY}}:=\argmin_{\lambda\in\triangle_{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Y}}\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}_{A(\lambda)^{-1}}.

(34)

The relevance of the
\mathcal{XY}
-optimal design stems from the ranking nature of BAI; identifying the best arm only requires accurate estimates of the relative ordering between arms. Motivated by Lemma
1
, we refine this idea by introducing the
Adjacent-optimal design
, which uniformly minimizes the variance over differences only between adjacent arms. Formally, it is defined as

\lambda^{\mathrm{Adjacent}}:=\argmin_{\lambda\in\triangle_{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}_{A(\lambda)^{-1}}.

(35)

Lemma
1
implies that if an arm is better than all its adjacent arms, then it must be the optimal arm. Thus, when identifying the best arm, only accurate comparisons between adjacent arms are needed. The Adjacent-optimal design embodies this principle: reducing the variance of estimation of the relative ordering between adjacent arms is sufficient for identifying the best arm. Focusing on fewer, more informative directions leads to stronger estimation, which we make use of in the algorithm presented in the next section.

6.2
Adjacent-Optimal Best-Arm Identification (Adjacent-BAI)

We now present the algorithm
Adjacent-BAI
. In the first step, we compute the adjacent set
\mathcal{I}
, for which we provide a polynomial-time procedure in Appendix
C
. We then compute the Adjacent-optimal design
\lambda^{*}
; however, we do not proceed by sampling directly from it. In order to obtain an optimal error rate using random sampling we would require an estimator that (i) admits sub-Gaussian, variance-only error and (ii) does not require a prespecified confidence level
2
2

2

This requirement is unnecessary in the fixed-confidence setting, hence the use of Catoni’s estimator in
Camilleri et al. 2021a
.
. According to
Devroye et al. 2016

Pukelsheim 2006
to obtain a static allocation
\left\{x_{t}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
such that the empirical design matrix
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}
approximates the optimal design matrix
A(\lambda^{*})=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\lambda^{*}_{x}xx^{\top}

3

the following holds:

\max_{(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}\right)^{-1}}^{2}\leq 2\cdot\max_{(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{A(\lambda^{*})^{-1}}^{2}.

(36)

6.3
Proof Sketch of Theorem
2

We outline a proof sketch of Theorem
2
.

First step.

The statistical analysis of Theorem
2
relies primarily on the following lemma.

Lemma 5

(Sub-Gaussian error of least-squares estimator)
.

Let
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
be the estimator computed in Step 9 of Algorithm
1
. For any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have that
z^{\top}(\widehat{\theta}_{T}-\overline{\theta}_{T})
is
3\cdot\|z\|_{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}\right)^{-1}}
-sub-Gaussian.

To obtain Lemma
5
, because the arm choices
x_{\pi(t)}
are dependent, the analysis requires particular care. Denote
A:=\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}
. We begin by expanding the quantity of interest:

z^{\top}(\widehat{\theta}_{T}-\overline{\theta}_{T})=\underbrace{z^{\top}\sum_{t=1}^{T}A^{-1}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}(\theta_{\pi^{-1}(t)}-\overline{\theta}_{T})}_{:=S}+\underbrace{z^{\top}\sum_{t=1}^{T}A^{-1}x_{t}\epsilon_{\pi^{-1}(t)}}_{:=\eta},

(38)

where we note that
\pi^{-1}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\Pi(T))
. Since each
\epsilon_{\pi^{-1}(t)}
is conditionally independent and 1-sub-Gaussian given
\pi^{-1}
, it is straightforward to show that
\eta
is conditionally
\|z\|_{A^{-1}}
-sub-Gaussian given
\pi^{-1}
. Analyzing
S
requires closer attention, since each
\theta_{\pi^{-1}(t)}
is dependent, so we proceed with a martingale analysis. We construct the Doob martingale of
S
,
\{Z_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
, with
Z_{t}=\mathbb{E}[S\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]
, where
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(\pi^{-1}(1),\dots,\pi^{-1}(t))
. The key observation is that, intuitively, each martingale difference
|Z_{t}-Z_{t-1}|
behaves roughly on the order of
\left|z^{\top}A^{-1}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}\theta_{\pi^{-1}(t)}\right|
. Given this, an Azuma inequality-style argument shows that

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda S\right)\right]\leq\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{T}8\left\|z^{\top}A^{-1}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(39)

Observing that

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|z^{\top}A^{-1}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|z\|_{A^{-1}}^{2},

(40)

we obtain that
S
is
\sqrt{8}\|z\|_{A^{-1}}
-sub-Gaussian, completing the proof of Lemma
5
.

Second step.

Finally, we combine Lemmas
1
and
5
to obtain the final bound in Theorem
2
. We aim to bound the error probability

\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{x}\neq x_{*}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\exists x\in\mathcal{X}\text{ s.t. }x^{\top}\widehat{\theta}_{T}>x_{*}^{\top}\widehat{\theta}_{T}\right).

(41)

However, critically, by Lemma
1
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists x\in\mathcal{X}\text{ s.t. }x^{\top}\widehat{\theta}_{T}>x_{*}^{\top}\widehat{\theta}_{T}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\exists x\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}\text{ s.t. }x^{\top}\widehat{\theta}_{T}>x_{*}^{\top}\widehat{\theta}_{T}\right).

(42)

Applying the union bound, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists x\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}\text{ s.t. }x^{\top}\widehat{\theta}_{T}>x_{*}^{\top}\widehat{\theta}_{T}\right)\leq\sum_{x\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}}\mathbb{P}\left((x-x_{*})^{\top}\left(\widehat{\theta}_{T}-\overline{\theta}_{T}\right)>\Delta_{(1)}\right)

(43)

Fix some
x\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}
. Combining Lemma
5
and Hoeffding’s inequality we have

\mathbb{P}\left((x-x_{*})^{\top}\left(\widehat{\theta}_{T}-\overline{\theta}_{T}\right)>\Delta_{(1)}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta_{(1)}^{2}}{2\cdot 3^{2}\cdot\|x-x_{*}\|_{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}\right)^{-1}}^{2}}\right).

(44)

Using that
(x,x_{*})\in\mathcal{I}
, and incorporating the rounding error from Eq. (
36
), we have

\|x-x_{*}\|_{\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}\right)^{-1}}^{2}\leq\frac{2\cdot\max_{(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{A(\lambda^{*})^{-1}}^{2}}{T}.

(45)

Observing that, by definition of
\lambda^{*}

\max_{(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{A(\lambda^{*})^{-1}}^{2}=\min_{\lambda\in\triangle_{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}}\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{A(\lambda)^{-1}}^{2},

(46)

completes the proof of Theorem
2
.

7
Future Work

The most promising future direction is investigating whether adjacency can be leveraged to establish a stronger notion of complexity for the stationary fixed-budget setting. In the stationary fixed-budget setting, currently, no lower bound exists outside of a pessimistic minimax-optimal lower bound derived from a multi-armed bandit reduction
(
Yang and Tan 2022
)
. However, in the stationary fixed-
confidence
setting, it is well-known
(
Soare et al. 2014
;
Fiez et al. 2019
;
Jedra and Proutiere 2020
)
that for arm set
\mathcal{X}
, and parameter vector
\theta
, the instance-optimal sample complexity is of order

\min_{\lambda\in\triangle_{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{*}\}}\frac{\|x_{*}-x\|^{2}_{A(\lambda)^{-1}}}{((x_{*}-x)^{\top}\theta)^{2}}.

(47)

Evaluating the above quantity, we obtain the following proposition:

Proposition 1
.

Given finite set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\min_{\lambda\in\triangle_{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{*}\}}\frac{\|x_{*}-x\|^{2}_{A(\lambda)^{-1}}}{((x_{*}-x)^{\top}\theta)^{2}}=\min_{\lambda\in\triangle_{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{x\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}}\frac{\|x_{*}-x\|^{2}_{A(\lambda)^{-1}}}{((x_{*}-x)^{\top}\theta)^{2}}.

(48)

The proof is deferred to Appendix
D
, which consists of a combination of Eq. (
12
) and Jensen’s inequality. Proposition
1
shows that in the stationary fixed-
confidence
setting, the instance-optimal sample complexity is determined solely by the arms adjacent to
x_{*}
. This suggests that adjacency is fundamentally tied to the difficulty of BAI even in stationary settings. This observation, together with what we have shown in the non-stationary setting, hints towards the possibility of establishing a stronger arm-set-dependent complexity in the stationary fixed-budget setting by exploiting the adjacent geometry of the arm set.

. Analyzing the conditional MGF of
\eta
for some
\lambda
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda z^{\top}\sum_{t=1}^{T}A^{-1}x_{t}\epsilon_{\pi^{-1}(t)}\right)\,\Bigg|\,\pi^{-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda z^{\top}A^{-1}x_{t}\epsilon_{\pi^{-1}(t)}\right)\mid\pi^{-1}\right]

(Cond. Ind. given
\pi^{-1}
)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{t=1}^{T}\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}z^{\top}A^{-1}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}A^{-1}z}{2}\right)

(Cond. 1-sub-G. given
\pi^{-1}
)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}z^{\top}A^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}A^{-1}z}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\|z\|_{A^{-1}}^{2}}{2}\right).

So
\eta
is conditionally
\|z\|_{A^{-1}}
-sub-Gaussian given
\pi^{-1}
. Analyzing the MGF of
z^{\top}\left(\widehat{\theta}_{T}-\overline{\theta}_{T}\right)
, for some
\lambda
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda z^{\top}\left(\widehat{\theta}_{T}-\overline{\theta}_{T}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda S+\lambda\eta\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda S\right)\exp\left(\lambda\eta\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda S\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\eta\right)\mid\pi^{-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda S\right)\right]\cdot\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\|z\|_{A^{-1}}^{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}8\|z\|_{A^{-1}}^{2}}{2}\right)\cdot\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\|z\|_{A^{-1}}^{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}(1+8)\|z\|_{A^{-1}}^{2}}{2}\right).

Thus
z^{\top}\left(\widehat{\theta}_{T}-\overline{\theta}_{T}\right)
is
\sqrt{9}\|z\|_{A^{-1}}
-sub-Gaussian.
∎

Lemma 9
.

Consider fixed sequences
\{x_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
spanning
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\{\theta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, with
\max_{t}\|x_{t}\|\leq B
and
\max_{t}\|\theta_{t}\|\leq M
. Denote
\overline{\theta}_{T}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\theta_{t}
,
A=\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}
, and
\tilde{\theta}_{t}=\theta_{t}-\overline{\theta}_{T}
for each
t\in[T]
. Let
\pi\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\Pi(T))
. For any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have that
z^{\top}\sum_{t=1}^{T}A^{-1}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(t)}
is
\sqrt{8}MB\|z\|_{A^{-1}}
-sub-Gaussian.

Proof.

Fix
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and denote
S=z^{\top}\sum_{t=1}^{T}A^{-1}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(t)}
. For
t\in[T]
define
Z_{t}=\mathbb{E}[S\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]
, with
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(\pi(1),\dots,\pi(t))
. Further define
Z_{0}=\mathbb{E}[S]
. By construction, we have that
\{Z_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
is a martingale and
Z_{T}=S
. Also note that
Z_{0}=\mathbb{E}[S]=0
, since
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{\theta}_{t}=0
. Denote
\Delta_{t}=Z_{t}-Z_{t-1}
. We begin by bounding
|\Delta_{t}|
for some
t<T
. To ease notation, let us denote

a_{t}=x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}A^{-1}z,

(81)

and

\mu_{t}=\frac{1}{T-t+1}\sum_{k\in[T]\setminus\{\pi(1),\dots,\pi(t-1)\}}\tilde{\theta}_{k}.

(82)

Let
t<T
. We have:

\displaystyle Z_{t-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{s}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(s)}+\sum_{s=t}^{T}a_{s}^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{T-t+1}\sum_{k\in[T]\setminus\{\pi(1),\dots,\pi(t-1)\}}\tilde{\theta}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{s}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(s)}+\sum_{s=t}^{T}a_{s}^{\top}\mu_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{s}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(s)}+a_{t}^{\top}\mu_{t}+\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}^{\top}\mu_{t},

and

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t}a_{s}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(s)}+\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{T-t}\sum_{k\in[T]\setminus\{\pi(1),\dots,\pi(t)\}}\tilde{\theta}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{s}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(s)}+a_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(t)}+\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{T-t}\left(\sum_{k\in[T]\setminus\{\pi(1),\dots,\pi(t-1)\}}\tilde{\theta}_{k}-\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(t)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{s}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(s)}+a_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(t)}+\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}^{\top}\left(\frac{(T-t+1)\mu_{t}-\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(t)}}{T-t}\right).

It follows that:

\displaystyle\Delta_{t}

\displaystyle=Z_{t}-Z_{t-1}

\displaystyle=a_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(t)}-a_{t}^{\top}\mu_{t}+\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}^{\top}\left(\frac{(T-t+1)\mu_{t}-\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(t)}}{T-t}-\mu_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=a_{t}^{\top}\left(\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(t)}-\mu_{t}\right)+\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}^{\top}\left(\frac{(T-t+1)\mu_{t}-\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(t)}}{T-t}-\mu_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=a_{t}^{\top}\left(\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(t)}-\mu_{t}\right)+\frac{1}{T-t}\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}^{\top}\left((T-t+1)\mu_{t}-\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(t)}-(T-t)\mu_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=a_{t}^{\top}\left(\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(t)}-\mu_{t}\right)+\frac{1}{T-t}\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}^{\top}\left(\mu_{t}-\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(t)}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(a_{t}-\frac{1}{T-t}\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}\right)^{\top}\left(\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(t)}-\mu_{t}\right).

We have

\displaystyle|\Delta_{t}|

\displaystyle=\left|\left(a_{t}-\frac{1}{T-t}\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}\right)^{\top}\left(\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(t)}-\mu_{t}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|a_{t}-\frac{1}{T-t}\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}\right\|_{2}\cdot\left\|\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(t)}-\mu_{t}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|a_{t}-\frac{1}{T-t}\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}\right\|_{2}\cdot\left\|\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(t)}-\frac{1}{T-t+1}\sum_{k\in[T]\setminus\{\pi(1),\dots,\pi(t-1)\}}\tilde{\theta}_{k}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|a_{t}-\frac{1}{T-t}\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}\right\|_{2}\cdot\left\|\theta_{\pi(t)}-\overline{\theta}_{T}-\frac{1}{T-t+1}\sum_{k\in[T]\setminus\{\pi(1),\dots,\pi(t-1)\}}\theta_{k}+\overline{\theta}_{T}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|a_{t}-\frac{1}{T-t}\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}\right\|_{2}\cdot\left\|\theta_{\pi(t)}-\frac{1}{T-t+1}\sum_{k\in[T]\setminus\{\pi(1),\dots,\pi(t-1)\}}\theta_{k}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|a_{t}-\frac{1}{T-t}\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}\right\|_{2}\cdot\left(\left\|\theta_{\pi(t)}\right\|_{2}+\frac{1}{T-t+1}\sum_{k\in[T]\setminus\{\pi(1),\dots,\pi(t-1)\}}\left\|\theta_{k}\right\|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|a_{t}-\frac{1}{T-t}\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}\right\|_{2}\cdot\left(M+\frac{1}{T-t+1}\sum_{k\in[T]\setminus\{\pi(1),\dots,\pi(t-1)\}}M\right)

\displaystyle=\left\|a_{t}-\frac{1}{T-t}\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}\right\|_{2}\cdot 2M

\displaystyle:=c_{t}.

Bounding
|\Delta_{T}|
we have

\displaystyle|\Delta_{T}|

\displaystyle=|Z_{T}-Z_{T-1}|

\displaystyle=\left|a_{T}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(T)}-a_{T}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{\pi(T)}\right|

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle:=c_{T}.

Note that by construction,
\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=0
for any
t
. For some
\lambda
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda S\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda(Z_{T}-Z_{0})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Delta_{t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\Delta_{t}\right)\cdot\exp\left(\lambda\Delta_{T}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\Delta_{t}\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\Delta_{T}\right)\mid\mathcal{F}_{T-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle(\text{Tower rule})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\Delta_{t}\right)\right]\cdot\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\cdot c_{T}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle(\text{Hoeffding's lemma})

\displaystyle\leq\cdots

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}^{2}\right).

\displaystyle(\text{Induction})

It now only remains to bound:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}^{2}=4M^{2}\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left\|a_{t}-\frac{1}{T-t}\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(83)

For each
t\in[T-1]
denote
d_{t}=a_{t}-\frac{1}{T-t}\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}
, and construct the matrices

\mathbf{a}:=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&\cdots&a_{T}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times T}

(84)

\mathbf{d}:=\begin{bmatrix}d_{1}&\cdots&d_{T-1}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times T-1}.

(85)

Further consider the matrix
D\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T-1}

D_{i,j}:=\begin{cases}1&i=j\\
-\frac{1}{T-j}&i>j\\
0&i<j\end{cases}.

(86)

It follows by construction that
\mathbf{a}D=\mathbf{d}
. Let
D_{i}
denote the
i
th column of
D
. Note that for any
i\in[T-1]
we have

\displaystyle\|D_{i}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=1^{2}+(T-i)\left(\frac{1}{T-i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{1}{T-i}

\displaystyle\leq 2.

Consider
D_{i}
,
D_{j}
, for
i<j
. Note that

\displaystyle D_{i}^{\top}D_{j}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{-1}{T-i}\right)\cdot 1+\sum_{k=j+1}^{T}\left(\frac{-1}{T-i}\right)\left(\frac{-1}{T-j}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{-1}{T-i}\right)+(T-j)\left(\frac{-1}{T-i}\right)\left(\frac{-1}{T-j}\right)

\displaystyle=0.

Thus, each column of
D
is orthogonal. This means

D^{\top}D=\text{diag}\left(\|D_{1}\|_{2}^{2},\dots,\|D_{T-1}\|_{2}^{2}\right)

(87)

and so,

\displaystyle\|D\|_{\text{op}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\max}\left(D^{\top}D\right)

\displaystyle=\max_{i}\|D_{i}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2.

It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle=4M^{2}\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left\|a_{t}-\frac{1}{T-t}\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}a_{s}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=4M^{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{d}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=4M^{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{a}D\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4M^{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{a}\|_{F}^{2}\|D\|_{\text{op}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot 4M^{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{a}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=8M^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|a_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=8M^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}z^{\top}A^{-1}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}A^{-1}z

\displaystyle\leq 8M^{2}B^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}z^{\top}A^{-1}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}A^{-1}z

\displaystyle=8M^{2}B^{2}z^{\top}A^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}A^{-1}z

\displaystyle=8M^{2}B^{2}\|z\|_{A^{-1}}^{2}.

It follows that, for all
\lambda
we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda S\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\cdot 8M^{2}B^{2}\|z\|_{A^{-1}}^{2}\right).

Thus
S
is
\sqrt{8}MB\|z\|_{A^{-1}}
-sub-Gaussian.
∎

Appendix C
Computing The Adjacent Set
\mathcal{I}

Consider
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|\mathcal{X}|=K
. If one has access to the convex hull of
\mathcal{X}
, the adjacent set
\mathcal{I}
can be extracted in
O(|\mathcal{I}|)=O(K^{2})
time. If one does not have access to the convex hull, one can compute it in
O\left(K\log K+K^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\right)
time
[
Chazelle 1993
]
, which is efficient for small
d
. When
d
is large, one can use the following procedure to compute
\mathcal{I}
in time polynomial in
K
and
d
. First center
\mathcal{X}
, that is, denoting
\bar{x}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}x
, compute

\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\leftarrow\left\{x-\bar{x}:x\in\mathcal{X}\right\}.

(88)

This shift ensures
0\in\mathrm{int}(\conv(\mathcal{X}^{\prime}))
, which will be important later on. For each
x\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
solve:

\begin{array}[]{rl}\max_{\epsilon,w}&\epsilon\\
\text{subject to}&x^{\top}w=1\\
&y^{\top}w\leq 1-\epsilon\quad\forall~y\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\setminus\{x\}\end{array}.\quad(\text{LP}1)

Then, check if the resulting
\epsilon>0
. If so, add
x
to the set
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}
. It follows that
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}
is exactly the set of all extreme points of
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
. Now, for each linearly independent pair
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}
solve:

\begin{array}[]{rl}\max_{\epsilon,w}&\epsilon\\
\text{subject to}&x^{\top}w=1\\
&x^{\prime\top}w=1\\
&y^{\top}w\leq 1-\epsilon\quad\forall~y\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}\setminus\{x,x^{\prime}\}\end{array}.\quad(\text{LP}2)

Then, check if the resulting
\epsilon>0
. If so, add
(x,x^{\prime})
to the set
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}
. It follows that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}
is exactly the set of all adjacent pairs of
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
. Since the structure of a polytope is preserved under translation, we have

\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{X}}=\left\{x+\bar{x}:x\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}\right\},

(89)

and

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}}=\left\{(x+\bar{x},x^{\prime}+\bar{x}):(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}\right\}.

(90)

The correctness of LP1 is the following. Consider some
x\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
. Since
0\in\mathrm{int}(\conv(\mathcal{X}^{\prime}))
, any hyperplane strictly supporting
x
must be of the form
\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:y^{\top}h=c\right\}
, for
c>0
and normal vector
h
. Thus
\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:y^{\top}\frac{h}{c}=1\right\}
also strictly supports
x
. Hence, to find whether or not
x
has a strict supporting hyperplane, it suffices to restrict our attention to normal vectors in the set
W(x)=\left\{w:x^{\top}w=1\right\}
. If
\epsilon>0
,
w
corresponds exactly to a strict supporting hyperplane of
x
, and the definition of extreme point is satisfied. If
\epsilon\leq 0
, then no
w\in W(x)
is a strict supporting hyperplane of
x
, and the definition cannot be satisfied. The correctness of LP2 follows analogously, with one extra caveat: checking only linearly independent pairs is sufficient; if
x,x^{\prime}
are linearly dependent then for any
w
we have
x^{\top}w=x^{\prime\top}w\Rightarrow x^{\top}w=0
, hence no
w
strictly supports both
x
and
x^{\prime}
since
0\in\mathrm{int}(\conv(\mathcal{X}^{\prime}))
. Finally, both LP1 and LP2 have
d+1
variables and at most
K
constraints, so each can be solved in
\text{poly}(K,d)
time
[
Karmarkar 1984
]
. Thus, the extreme point procedure takes in total
K\cdot\text{poly}(K,d)
, and the adjacent procedure takes in total
K^{2}\cdot\text{poly}(K,d)
. Thus, the total run time remains
\text{poly}(K,d)
.

Lemma 10
.

Consider
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
M\succ 0
, and let
x_{*}=\argmax_{x\in\mathcal{X}}x^{\top}\theta
. Consider
x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x_{*}\}
. We have

\frac{\|x_{*}-x\|_{M}^{2}}{((x_{*}-x)^{\top}\theta)^{2}}\leq\max_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}}\frac{\|x_{*}-x^{\prime}\|_{M}^{2}}{((x_{*}-x^{\prime})^{\top}\theta)^{2}}.

Proof.

By Lemma 3.6 of
Ziegler 1995
we have
\mathcal{X}\subset x_{*}+\text{cone}\left(\{y-x_{*}:y\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}\}\right)
. Thus
\exists~\alpha_{y}\geq 0
for each
y\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}
, such that
x_{*}-x=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}}\alpha_{y}(x_{*}-y)
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\left|\left|\frac{x_{*}-x}{(x_{*}-x)^{\top}\theta}\right|\right|_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\left|\frac{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}}\alpha_{y}(x_{*}-y)}{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}}\alpha_{x}(x_{*}-x)^{\top}\theta}\right|\right|_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\left|\sum_{y\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}}\frac{\alpha_{y}(x_{*}-y)}{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}}\alpha_{x}(x_{*}-x)^{\top}\theta}\right|\right|_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\left|\sum_{y\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}}\frac{\alpha_{y}(x_{*}-y)^{\top}\theta}{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}}\alpha_{x}(x_{*}-x)^{\top}\theta}\cdot\frac{(x_{*}-y)}{(x_{*}-y)^{\top}\theta}\right|\right|_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}}\frac{\alpha_{y}(x_{*}-y)^{\top}\theta}{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}}\alpha_{x}(x_{*}-x)^{\top}\theta}\cdot\left|\left|\frac{(x_{*}-y)}{(x_{*}-y)^{\top}\theta}\right|\right|_{M}^{2}

(Jensen’s inequality)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}}\frac{\alpha_{y}(x_{*}-y)^{\top}\theta}{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}}\alpha_{x}(x_{*}-x)^{\top}\theta}\cdot\max_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}}\left|\left|\frac{(x_{*}-x^{\prime})}{(x_{*}-x^{\prime})^{\top}\theta}\right|\right|_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=\max_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}}\left|\left|\frac{(x_{*}-x^{\prime})}{(x_{*}-x^{\prime})^{\top}\theta}\right|\right|_{M}^{2}\cdot\sum_{y\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}}\frac{\alpha_{y}(x_{*}-y)^{\top}\theta}{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}}\alpha_{x}(x_{*}-x)^{\top}\theta}

\displaystyle=\max_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}^{x_{*}}}\left|\left|\frac{(x_{*}-x^{\prime})}{(x_{*}-x^{\prime})^{\top}\theta}\right|\right|_{M}^{2}.

∎
