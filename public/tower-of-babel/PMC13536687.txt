Exogenous MeJA confers resistance to Alternaria tenuissima by regulating cell wall components and flavonoids in Paeonia lactiflora
Authors: Zhonghua Lu, Xianfeng Guo, Yulei Jiang, Yihang Sun, Jing Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMethyl jasmonate (MeJA) plays a key role in enhancing plant resistance to a wide range of diseases. However, the specific defense responses it induced and the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood in many plant–pathogen systems. This study aimed to determine whether exogenous MeJA enhances resistance to leaf spot disease caused by Alternaria tenuissima in peony (Paeonia lactiflora) and to elucidate the associated molecular and biochemical mechanisms through integrated transcriptomic and biochemical analyses.ResultsCompared with the control (CK), pretreatment with 25–75 µmol L− 1 MeJA significantly reduced disease severity, with 75 µmol L− 1 identified as the optimal concentration. Peony leaves pretreated with this concentration exhibited a stronger hypersensitive response than the CK, with initial differentiation observed at 24 h after inoculation. Transcriptome sequencing analysis identified 1,017 differentially expressed genes between the MeJA-pretreated and CK groups, primarily associated with cell wall organization, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, terpenoid backbone biosynthesis, and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways. MeJA pretreatment up-regulated the genes involved in phenylpropanoid and lignin biosynthesis, including peroxidase 27 (POD27) and caffeic acid 3-O-methyltransferase (COMT), as well as flavonoid-related genes such as chalcone isomerase (CHI). Genes regulating pectin methylesterification, including pectin methylesterase (PME) and PME inhibitor (PMEI), were also modulated. In addition, transcription factors such as basic leucine zipper (bZIP) and heat shock transcription factor (HSF) were activated. Biochemical analyses confirmed increased levels of major cell wall components (lignin, cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin) and total flavonoids were significantly higher in MeJA-pretreated plants compared with CK plants. A putative model of MeJA-induced defense responses against A. tenuissima was proposed.ConclusionsExogenous MeJA pretreatment enhances resistance to leaf spot disease caused by A. tenuissima in peony by promoting cell wall reinforcement and flavonoid accumulation. These findings highlight the potential of MeJA as a strategy for managing leaf spot disease in peony cultivation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09187-1.Keywords: Alternaria tenuissima; Disease resistance; MeJA, peony; Transcriptome analysis

Introduction

Peony (Paeonia lactiflora) is a traditional and widely cultivated plant in China with high ornamental, medicinal, and economic value [1, 2]. It is grown extensively across most regions of China, except for a few southern provinces. Leaf spot disease caused by the fungal pathogen Alternaria is prevalent in many peony growing areas [3, 4]. This disease reduces both yield and quality of peony products and in severe cases, can lead to plant mortality, thereby posing a substantial threat to commercial peony production [5]. Although developing resistant cultivars is an effective strategy, it remains time-consuming [6]. Current management relies primarily on a combination of cultural practices and chemical control [7]; however, the excessive use of chemical fungicides not only promote pathogen resistance but also pose environmental risks. In contrast, induced resistance, which exploits plant’s innate mechanisms, can reduce dependence on chemical inputs and represents an environmentally sustainable alternative for disease management [6, 8].

Jasmonic acid (JA) and its derivatives, such as methyl jasmonate (MeJA), collectively referred to jasmonates, play critical roles in plant defense against fungal pathogens, particularly necrotrophs [9, 10]. In recent years, jasmonates have attracted increasing attention as effective elicitors in both postharvest agricultural products and field crops. Application of appropriate concentrations of MeJA has been shown to induce resistance in fruits and vegetables, including grape (Vitis vinifera), orange (Citrus sinensis), and potato (Solanum tuberosum), against pathogenic infections [11–13]. Similarly, MeJA enhances disease resistance in field crops, such as wheat against spot blotch caused by Bipolaris sorokiniana and rice against blast disease caused by Magnaporthe oryzae [14, 15]. These findings indicate a broad protective role of exogenous MeJA against diverse fungal pathogens.

Exogenous JA signaling protects plants against pathogens by inducing specific defensive responses. The antifungal activity of JA in potato was enhanced by repressing abscisic acid levels at an early stage of Alternaria solani infection [16]. In contrast, the resistance conferred by MeJA against A. tenuissima in winter jujube was mainly mediated through the regulation of the phenylpropanoid metabolism pathway [17]. In Rosa roxburghii fruit, MeJA treatment enhanced disease resistance mainly by increasing antioxidant enzyme activity and promoting the ascorbate-glutathione cycle and phenolic compound biosynthesis [18]. In Phaseolus vulgaris plants, MeJA application induced systemic defense against Sclerotinia sclerotiorum by increasing transcript levels of genes encoding pathogenicity-related proteins, including chitinases, callose synthase, and F-box family protein-like factors [19]. These findings suggest that the dominant defensive mechanisms triggered by exogenous MeJA vary across specific plant–pathogen interactions.

Despite these advances, the role of exogenous MeJA in regulating resistance to leaf spot disease caused by A. tenuissima in peony remains poorly understood. In particular, the underlying defense mechanisms and key regulatory genes involved have not been fully elucidated. Therefore, this study aimed (1) to evaluate the effect of exogenous MeJA pretreatment on leaf spot disease caused by A. tenuissima in peony and (2) to identify the associated pathways and key regulatory genes through integrated transcriptomic and biochemical analyses.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The Paeonia lactiflora cultivar ‘Da Fugui’ served as the experimental material. Dormant crowns, each with 8–10 healthy buds, were obtained from Heze Jinghe Peony Nursery (Shandong Province, China). In October 2022, the crowns were potted into nonwoven fabric pots (approximately 15 L; 28 cm diameter and 30 cm height) containing a mixed substrate of peat, perlite, and vermiculite in a 3:1:1 (v: v:v) ratio. The plants were cultivated at the Forestry Station of Shandong Agricultural University. In April 2023, approximately 30 days after full leaf expansion, 90 plants with uniform growth were selected for the experiments.

Experimental design

This work comprised two consecutive experiments. The initial experiment aimed to investigate the effect of different concentrations of MeJA pretreatment on pathogen infection. A total of 60 plants were used in this experiment, and they were randomly assigned to 4 groups, each with 3 replicates. MeJA pretreatment was designed based on a preliminary study, in which a broader range of MeJA concentrations—0, 50, 100, 500, and 1,000 µmol L− 1 were tested. Previous results showed that pretreatment with 50 and 100 µmol L− 1 of MeJA evidently induced resistance to A. tenuissima in peony, with a stronger induction effect observed at 50 µmol L− 1 (Fig. S1 in Supplementary material). Therefore, the concentration gradient was further narrowed to 25, 50, and 75 µmol L− 1, designated as MeJA25, MeJA50, and MeJA75, respectively. Distilled water was used as the control (CK) group. Each group of plants was separately sprayed with MeJA or water using a 500 mL pressurized hand sprayer for three consecutive days in the late afternoon (17:00–18:00). Following each treatment, plants were immediately enclosed in a sealed transparent plastic chamber [20]. Two days after final pretreatment, plants were inoculated with the pathogen Alternaria tenuissima strain W23, which was previously isolated and identified from infected peony leaves by Wang (2020) and was stored at -80 °C prior to inoculation [21]. After activation and incubation on potato dextrose agar medium at 28 °C, six fungal disks (6 mm in diameter and approximately 1 mm thick) were inoculated onto the upper leaves of each plant using a 6 mm puncher. Inoculated leaves were wrapped with plastic film to maintain high humidity. Disease symptoms were visually recorded and rated at 72 h after inoculation (hai).

Based on the initial findings, 75 µmol L− 1 MeJA (MeJA75) was identified as the optimal pretreatment concentration. A second experiment was subsequently conducted using the remaining 30 peony plants, which were randomly divided into 2 groups (MeJA75 and CK) with 3 replicates. Pretreatment and inoculation followed the procedures described above. To monitor the hypersensitive response (HR), six pinnate leaves were collected from each treatment group at 0, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 hai. According to the results of the HR examination, leaf samples collected at 24 hai were used for transcriptome sequencing and biochemical analyses. The samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C.

Assessment of disease severity

Disease severity was graded at 72 hai according to the phenotypic symptoms, and the disease index (DI) was calculated using the following formula [21, 22].

Observation of the leaf spot disease progression and HR

The dynamic progression of disease symptoms was monitored at 0, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 hai. Concurrently, peony leaf samples were collected for HR observation using the lactophenol–trypan blue staining method [23].

Transcriptomic analysis

Total RNA was extracted from P. lactiflora leaves using a commercial kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). RNA samples were used for cDNA library construction after quality assessment. The libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform by BioMarker (Beijing Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd, Beijing, China). Low-quality reads were filtered from the raw sequencing data. The remaining clean reads were de novo assembled using the Trinity software. Functional annotation of each unigene was performed using the following databases: NCBI non-redundant protein (NR), Swiss-Prot, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG), Eukaryotic Orthologous Groups (KOG), Gene Ontology (GO), and Protein Families (Pfam) database. The expression level of each unigene was calculated as fragments per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped (FPKM). The Pearson correlation coefficient was utilized to assess the correlation among samples. A heatmap was generated for better visualization.

Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) between groups were identified using the DESeq R package based on the threshold values of a False Discovery Rate (FDR) < 0.05 and | log2 Fold Change (FC)| > 1. DEGs with FC > 25 and Transcription Factors (TFs) associated with plant disease defense were further screened to identify key genes.

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the identified DEGs based on InterProScan (European Bioinformatics Institute, Ingelston, Cambridgeshire, England) and the KEGG Orthology-Based Annotation System (KOBAS) respectively. Terms with a P-adjusted value was less than 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT–PCR) analyses

To verify the transcriptome data and gene expression patterns, 10 DEGs with high expression levels in significantly enriched KEGG pathways were selected for qRT-PCR analysis. Total RNA was extracted from 100 to 200 mg of peony leaves using the RN38-EASYspin Plus Plant RNA kit(Aidlab Biotech, Beijing, China)according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted RNA was subsequently reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using HiFi Script RT Master Mix (CoWin Biosciences, China). The qRT-PCR assay was performed using a SYBR PrimeScript RT-PCR kit (Takara Biotechnology, Beijing, China) on a Bio-RadCFX96TM (Bio-Rad, USA). Each reaction mix consisted of 10 µL of SYBR RT-PCR buffer, 0.5 µL of each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 8.0 µL of sterilized distilled water, and 1.0 µL of cDNA template. The amplification conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, and 60 °C for 30 s. To ensure product specificity, a melting curve analysis was subsequently performed (95 °C for 10 s, 65 °C for 5 s, and 95 °C for 5 s). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method [24], with P. lactiflora actin serving as the internal reference. All Primer sequences for the selected genes were listed in Table S1 (Supplementary material).

Quantification of major cell wall components

Lignin content was measured following the method of Syros et al. [25], using lignin standard (98%; Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) for the standard curve (y = 0.02776x + 0.0068, R² = 0.9889). Cellulose content was determined by iodometric titration [26], while hemicellulose content was calculated according to the method of Xiong et al. [26], using a glucose standard curve (y = 10.54x – 0.045, R² = 0.9973). Pectin content was quantified using the sulfuric acid-carbazole method with a galacturonic acid standard [27] (97%; Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China; y = 0.0127x + 0.0439, R² = 0.9919).

Determination of total flavonoid and terpenoid contents

Total flavonoid content was determined according to the method of Zhang et al. [28], utilizing rutin (≥ 98%; Macklin, Shanghai, China) for the standard curve (y = 10.359x + 0.046, R² = 0.9997). Terpenoid content was measured following the procedure described by Łukowski et al. [29], using linalool (≥ 98%; Macklin, Shanghai, China) as the reference standard (y = 0.0129x + 0.0558, R2 = 0.9997).

Data analysis

All tests were performed with three biological replicates, and data were presented as means ± standard error (SE). Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS 19.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Prior to analysis, data were checked for normality and homogeneity of variance. Significant differences between treatments were evaluated using independent-samples Student’s t-tests or One-way Analysis of Variance followed by Duncan’s multiple range test. Histograms were generated using GraphPad Prism v8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Exogenous MeJA reduces the severity of peony leaf spot disease caused by A. tenuissima

Disease symptoms and the corresponding DIs at 72 hai are illustrated in Fig. 1. The DI values across all three MeJA-pretreated groups were significantly lower than that of the CK group. Notably, the MeJA75 group exhibited the lowest DI, which was only 0.55 times that of the CK group. These results indicated that pretreatment with 25–75 µmol L− 1 MeJA significantly enhanced the resistance of peony leaves against the fungal pathogen A. tenuissima, with 75 µmol L− 1 identified as the most effective concentration.

HR examination

As shown in Fig. 2a, in this round of experiment, CK leaves developed lesions of 0–2 mm at 24 hai, which gradually expanded to 5–6 mm by 96 hai. In contrast, leaves pretreated with MeJA75 did not exhibit lesions until 48 hai, and lesion size showed no significant expansion within 96 hai.

Dynamic changes in phenotype (a) and the hypersensitive response (b) of peony leaves at different time points after inoculation with A. tenuissima. The leaves were examined at 0, 12, 24, 48, 72 and 96 h after inoculation

As illustrated in Fig. 2b, no HR was observed in CK leaves within 24 hai, despite the initiation of pathogen infection. Weak HR appeared at 48 hai, along with continuous fungal colonization. By 72 hai, HR began to subside as the infection progressed, and by 96 hai, HR had completely disappeared, accompanied by continuing lesion expansion. In contrast, pretreated leaves exhibited an HR as early as 12 hai, which became markedly stronger at 24 hai. This intense HR persisted through 48 hai, although minor lesions began to appear. Subsequently, HR gradually vanished by 96 hai. Considering that the most pronounced differences in HR between the two groups occurred at 24 hai, this time point was selected for subsequent transcriptomic and biochemical analyses.

This extensive dataset ensures the coverage and representativeness of the overall gene expression profile in peony plants. From the clean data, 49,244 unigenes were assembled. The correlation coefficients between the three replicates for both the CK and MeJA75 groups exceeded 0.8 (Fig. 3a), demonstrating high reproducibility and ensuring the reliability of subsequent analyses.

Comparative analysis between the CK and MeJA-pretreated groups identified 1,017 DEGs, comprising 482 up-regulated and 535 down-regulated genes. When applying a stricter threshold of FC > 25, 27 DEGs were further screened, including 7 up-regulated DEGs and 20 down-regulated DEGs (Table S3 in Supplementary material). Among these, genes encoding rhamnogalacturonan I rhamnosyltransferase (RRT), peroxidase (POD), probable pectinesterase/pectin methylesterase inhibitor 6 (PMEI6), and pectin methylesterase (PME), were prominent, all of which are associated with cell wall stability.

Furthermore, 23 DEGs encoding TFs were identified (Table S4 in Supplementary material). Specifically, genes encoding basic leucine zipper (bZIP) and heat shock transcription factor (HSF) were exclusively up-regulated after MeJA pretreatment, whereas other TF families exhibited mixed expression patterns. These results suggest a positive correlation between bZIP and HSF expression and peony disease resistance, while other TFs may function as either transcriptional activators or repressors in the defense response.

GO enrichment analysis

GO analysis showed that a total of 1,708 GO terms were associated with the annotated DEGs, of which 27 were significantly enriched (Table S5 in Supplementary material). Figure 2b displays only those terms associated with at least 10 DEGs. In the biological process (BP) category, 65 DEGs were significantly enriched across 5 terms: carbohydrate metabolic process (GO:0005975), pectin catabolism (GO:0045490), carbohydrate transport (GO:0008643), cell wall modification (GO:0042545), and sterol biosynthetic process (GO:0016126). In the cellular component (CC) category, 277 DEGs showed intensive enrichment in 3 terms: integral component of membrane (GO:0016021), extracellular region (GO:0005576), and cell wall (GO:0005618). In the molecular function (MF) category, 249 DEGs were distributed across 19 relatively dispersed terms, such as heme binding (GO:0020037), transmembrane transporter activity (GO:0022857), pectinesterase activity (GO:0030599), and hydroperoxide dehydratase activity (GO:0047987).

Functions related to the cell wall were significantly enriched across all three GO categories. This includes pectin catabolism and cell wall modification in the BP category, cell wall in CC category, and peroxidase/pectinesterase activities in the MF category. These findings strongly suggest that cell wall remodeling plays a pivotal role in the MeJA-induced defense responses of peony leaves against A. tenuissima.

A total of 698 DEGs were mapped to 119 KEGG pathways, with 3 pathways showing significant enrichment: terpenoid backbone biosynthesis (ko00900), phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940), and flavonoid biosynthesis (ko00941) (Fig. 3c).

As shown in Fig. 6, the levels of lignin, pectin, hemicellulose, and cellulose in leaf cell walls were significantly higher in MeJA-pretreated plants than in the CK group at 24 hai. These results suggest that MeJA pretreatment can effectively modulate the accumulation of cell wall components, potentially reinforcing the physical barriers of peony leaves.

Discussion

Impact of exogenous MeJA on peony resistance against A. tenuissima infection

MeJA can induce plant resistance against various pathogens [30]. Exogenous application of MeJA has been shown to reduce disease incidence in multiple plant species, including gray mold in grapes and necrotrophic diseases in tomatoes [11, 31]. However, the optimal concentration of MeJA varies depending on the plant species and, in some cases, on the specific pathogen infecting the same plant species. For example, a concentration of 10 µmol L− 1 MeJA significantly reduced the incidence and progression of gray mold in grapes inoculated with Botrytis cinerea [11]. In barley, a much higher concentration of 1,500 µmol L− 1 MeJA was required to significantly alleviate the severity of leaf spot disease caused by Bipolaris sorokiniana [32]. In another study, 0.1 mM MeJA was found to effectively reduce the severity of necrotrophic disease caused by Alternaria in tomato plants [31]. In alignment with these reports, our findings demonstrated that pretreatment with 50–100 µmol L− 1 MeJA significantly reduced the severity of peony leaf spot disease caused by A. tenuissima, with 75 µmol L− 1 identified as the most effective concentration. These data provide a promising strategy for the field management of peony crops.

Reinforcement of the cell wall contributes to MeJA-induced disease resistance

The plant cell wall serves as the first line of defense against pathogen infection and is mainly composed of cellulose, hemicelluloses, pectin and lignin, as well as proteins [33]. In this work, the role of the cell wall was underscored by both transcriptomic and biochemical evidence: functional GO terms related to the cell wall were significantly enriched, and the four major cell wall components accumulated significantly within 24 hai following MeJA pretreatment (with the exception of cellulose at 0 hai). These results indicate that reinforcement of the plant cell wall is a key mechanism contributing to MeJA-induced resistance against A. tenuissima in peony plants.

As a major structural constituent of the plant cell wall, lignin provides mechanical strength and acts as a robust defensive barrier against pathogens [34]. Our transcriptomic analysis revealed that 24 DEGs between the MeJA-pretreated and CK groups were significantly enriched in the phenylpropanoid biosynthetic pathway—a pathway closely related to lignin synthesis [17]. Among these, nine genes were up-regulated, including those encoding caffeic acid 3-O-methyltransferases (COMT; TRINITY_DN1061_c1_g1, and TRINITY_DN13112_c1_g1) and peroxidases (POD; TRINITY_DN1374_c0_g1, and TRINITY_DN21475_c0_g1). COMT plays an important role in regulating the lignin biosynthesis pathway by catalyzing multi-step reactions, such as the conversion of 5-hydroxyconiferal alcohol to sinapyl alcohol and of caffeyl alcohol to coniferyl alcohol [35, 36]. Using transgenic technology, several COMT genes have been verified to be involved in plant disease resistance and lignin accumulation. For example, TaCOMT-3D gene in wheat enhances resistance to Rhizoctonia cerealis through lignin accumulation [37]. Similarly, OsCOMT5 gene in rice (Oryza sativa) increases lignin accumulation and confers tolerance against Rhizoctonia solani by preventing pathogen penetration of the cell wall [38]. Based on these previous reports, it can be inferred that the two COMTs genes identified here may play a crucial role in MeJA-induced resistance to A. tenuissima in peony plants. Similarly, POD enzymes play a vital role in lignin biosynthesis by facilitating the final oxidative polymerization step [39]. We observed that, among the six DEGs encoding POD, the putative PlPOD27 (TRINITY_DN1374_c0_g1) and PlPOD10 (TRINITY_DN21475_c0_g1) genes were strongly up-regulated, with PlPOD27 showing a striking increase of up to 55.10 times. Considering MeJA-induced resistance was accompanied by lignin accumulation, these two genes are likely to play a predominant role in lignin synthesis and MeJA-induced disease resistance. Similar to COMT, modulation of POD gene can alter lignin content and contribute to disease resistance [34]. Thus, it is reasonable that MeJA-induced resistance to the pathogen is at least partially mediated by lignin accumulation resulting from changes in gene expression, particularly through the up-regulation of PlCOMT1 and PlPOD27.

Apart from lignin, pectin - another major component of the cell wall, also plays a crucial role in the defensive response to invading pathogens, as it is responsible for maintaining cell wall integrity and cell-cell adhesion [40]. The degree of pectin methylesterification (DM) can influence cell wall structure, physiological processes, and plant resistance to pathogens; this process is strictly regulated by the localized expression of specific PMEs and PMEIs [40, 41]. Emerging molecular evidence indicates the crucial roles of PME and PMEI in plant defense [42–44]. For example, overexpression of AtPMEI-1 and AtPMEI-2 genes in Arabidopsis thaliana resulted in higher pectin DM and stronger resistance to the necrotrophic fungus Botrytis cinerea [42]. Conversely, the AtPME3 gene in Arabidopsis acted as a susceptibility factor; by decreasing pectin DM, it enables rapid colonization of host tissue by B. cinerea and P. carotovorum [43]. As shown in Table S3 (Supplementary material), the putative PMEI6 (TRINITY_DN29190_c0_g1)was up-regulated by up to 27.63-fold, whereas PME2 (TRINITY_DN2116_c0_g2) was down-regulated by up to 44.62-fold in MeJA-pretreated plants. Based on these results, it is speculated that PMEI6 and PME2 may contributed to enhancing pectin DM, thereby increasing resistance to the pathogen A. tenuissima.

The results indicated that MeJA-pretreated peony plants exhibited higher cellulose and hemicellulose content compared to the CK group. This suggested that these components, in conjunction with lignin and pectin, collectively reinforced the reinforcement of the cell wall. However, as no relevant pathways were enriched in the KEGG analysis, the specific mechanism by which cellulose and hemicellulose influence disease resistance remain to be elucidated.

MeJA-induced disease resistance was positively related with flavonoid biosynthesis

Flavonoids play an important role in plant defense against biotic stress [45]. For instance, in Cyclocarya parus, flavonoid content in resistant cultivars was significantly higher than in susceptible cultivars; furthermore, flavonoid extracts from this species demonstrated a strong inhibitory effect on the mycelial growth and fungal biomass accumulation of the pathogen Colletotrichum fructicola [46]. Exogenous application of JA signaling compounds often triggers the accumulation of secondary metabolites, including flavonoids [47, 48]. Our findings confirm the stimulatory role of MeJA, as the elevated flavonoid levels paralleled the enhanced disease resistance against A. tenuissima observed in pretreated peony plants.

Curiously, transcriptomic profiling revealed that most key genes in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway were down-regulated, except for CHI genes, which were up-regulated. This expression pattern appears inconsistent with our biochemical measurements—which showed significant flavonoid accumulation following MeJA pretreatment—as well as previous reports [49, 50]. This discrepancy is likely explained by the sampling timeline. For example, genes critical to flavonoid production in Camellia—including phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), 4CL, cinnamoyl-CoA reductases (CCR), CHI, CHS, chalcone by trans cinnamate 4-monooxygenase (C4H), and flavonol synthase (FLS)—showed high expression only 2 h after MeJA pretreatment [49]. Similarly, another study observed that upstream genes in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway (e.g., CHSs, CHIs, and HCTs) were up-regulated, whereas downstream genes (e.g., F3Ms, ANRs, and ANSs) were down-regulated after 6 h of MeJA pretreatment [50]. Notably, gene expression in these studies was detected much earlier (2–6 h after MeJA pretreatment) than in this work (0 and 24 hai, that is, 48 and 72 h after MeJA pretreatment). It can therefore be inferred that these key genes were up-regulated at an earlier time point after MeJA pretreatment, leading to the observed flavonoid accumulation and the subsequent defense response against A. tenuissima.

Similar to flavonoids, terpenoids play a protective role against pathogen infection [51], and exogenous MeJA has been reported to enhance terpenoid biosynthesis and mitigate diseases [52]. In our study, however, all 12 DEGs associated with the terpenoid backbone biosynthesis pathway— including key genes such as HMGCR, dxs, and ispS—were down-regulated, despite the pathway’s significant enrichment in the KEGG analysis. Consistent with the down-regulation profile of these genes, terpenoid content was also reduced at 24 hai in MeJA-pretreated peony leaves compared with the CK leaves. This reduction may be attributed to the relatively late sampling time; since most terpenoids are volatile [53], MeJA-induced terpenoids may have dissipated and were no longer detectable. Alternatively, due to limited evidence, the possibility that MeJA did not effectively induce terpenoid biosynthesis in this specific context, or that lower terpenoid levels may contribute to the defensive response against A. tenuissima, cannot be ruled out. This warrants further investigation.

TFs associated with MeJA-induced disease resistance in peony plants

Concerning the disease resistance mechanism, transcriptional reprogramming of defense-related gene expression has been extensively reported, and many TFs within the regulatory cascade triggering cell wall reinforcement or flavonoid biosynthesis have been characterized [54]. Arabidopsis thaliana myoblastosis 46 (AtMYB46) likely acts as a susceptibility modulator to Botrytis cinerea by integrating cell wall remodeling with the activation of secondary defense responses [55]. Conversely, AtMYB15 is essential for activating lignin biosynthesis genes such as PAL and COMT, and the resulting lignin deposition serves to spatially restrict pathogen infection [56]. In pepper (Capsicum annuum), the MeJA-induced TF CaHsfB2a is a critical positive regulator; its silencing significantly impairs immunity to the pathogenic bacterium Ralstonia solanacearum [57]. In the present study, several types of differentially expressed TFs, such as those from the bZIP, HSF, MYB, and basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) families, were identified. These TFs likely play vital roles in pathogen defense by regulating the expression of key genes (e.g., POD and COMT) involved in the biosynthesis of cell wall components and flavonoids following MeJA pretreatment.

To exert their regulatory function, TFs must bind to specific cis-elements within the promoters of target genes [58]. For example, bZIP factors reportedly bind to G-box motifs in the promoters of lignin-related genes (e.g., POD and COMT) to enhance lignification under stress [59, 60]. Similarly, MYB52 transcriptionally activates PMEI14 and PMEI6 by directly binding to the MYB binding site (MBS) of the promoters, thereby decreasing pectin methylesterification [61]. We therefore suggest the TFs identified here employ comparable mechanisms to modulate cell wall and metabolic defenses.

Proposed model of exogenous MeJA-induced defensive responses to pathogen infection

Based on our findings, we proposed a defense response model in which MeJA enhances disease resistance in peony through modulation of cell wall and flavonoid metabolism (Fig. 8). We hypothesize that exogenous MeJA triggers the endogenous JA signaling pathway—likely via the COI1/JAZ degradation mechanism—thereby releasing TFs such as bZIP and MYB from repression [62]. These activated TFs may then bind to specific promoter elements in the target genes, like G-boxes for bZIP and MBS for MYB; these binding drive the upregulation of POD and COMT for lignin biosynthesis, alter the expression of PMEI and PME for pectin modification, and promote CHI and HCT for flavonoid synthesis. Ultimately, the resulting enhanced cell wall and the accumulation of flavonoids create a dual physical and chemical barrier that effectively restricts A. tenuissima infection.

Conclusions

Pretreatment with MeJA at concentrations of 25–75 µmol L− 1 could induce enhanced resistance to leaf spot disease caused by A. tenuissima in peony plants, with 75 µmol L− 1 producing the strongest induction effect. These findings provide a theoretical basis for developing innovative disease management strategies in peony cultivation. Transcriptomic analysis identified 1,017 DEGs between MeJA75 (75 µmol L⁻¹) pretreated and CK plants following inoculation with A. tenuissima. These genes were primarily enriched in the terpenoid backbone, phenylpropanoid, and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways. Notably, genes such as PlPOD27 and PlPMEI may serve as key regulators of MeJA-induced resistance to A. tenuissima. Biochemical analyses demonstrated significantly increased levels of major cell wall components (lignin, cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin) and total flavonoids were significantly higher in MeJA-pretreated plants. Together, these results indicate that MeJA enhances resistance to leaf spot disease in peony by reinforcing cell wall structure and increasing flavonoid accumulation. Overall, this study provides new insights into the molecular and physiological mechanisms underlying MeJA-induced resistance to leaf spot disease in peony and offers a foundation for future research and practical applications.
