 Abstract
 This article investigates electron bunch density fluctuations in the 1 – 10 µm wavelength range,
focusing on their impact on coherent electron cooling (CEC) in hadron storage rings. In this
study, we compare the shot-noise model with experimental observations using bandwidth-filtered
near-infrared (NIR) optical transition radiation (OTR) photodiode signals with the transverse
bunch size being much larger than the OTR wavelength of interest. The relativistic electron bunch
(γ ≈ 50) parameters are close to those proposed for the Coherent Electron Cooler in the Electron-
Ion Collider (EIC) project. Preliminary feasibility experiments were conducted, and the noise
factors are presented, supported by particle tracking. No major density fluctuations and effective
cooling rate decrease were revealed for EIC CEC design parameters. Additionally, longitudinal
space-charge-induced microbunching for the chicane-compressed bunch was observed with coherent
OTR enhancements up to 100 times, providing further calibration of the measurement method.

I. INTRODUCTION

 The significance of bunch quality in relativistic electron bunches became increasingly
prominent as the allowed density fluctuations, often called ”noise”, approached the Poisson
statistics level (shot noise [1, 2]). Various applications where it is important include high-
energy hadron cooling systems [3, 4] and free-electron lasers (FELs) [5, 6].
 The modulation is defined as the difference between the unperturbed distribution func-
tion, ρ0 (t), and the actual distribution function, ρ(t). The unperturbed distribution ρ0 (t)
neglects the rigidity of the electrons, whereas the actual distribution ρ(t) consists of N elec-
trons arriving at times tj , represented by Dirac delta functions. It is more convenient to
analyze the modulations in the Fourier domain (spectrum):

 In this paper, we are focused on the electron bunch noise in a specific system, EIC CEC,

 The luminosity in colliders depends mainly on bunch intensities and emittances, which in
turn depend on many factors and processes, such as intrabeam scattering [11]. To maintain
hadron bunch quality and high brightness, bunch cooling can be implemented in storage
rings. Currently, two methods are employed: electron cooling [12] and stochastic cooling
[13, 14]. While electron cooling has been extensively studied and widely implemented, its
cooling rate drops significantly with energy increase [15], with the highest hadron energy
cooled by this method being 8.9 GeV [16].
 Stochastic cooling functions as a broad-band feedback system by damping hadron bunch
density fluctuations, detected by a ’pick-up’ device, amplified, and then applied to a ’kicker’
device at an optimal signal phase. Its cooling rate is not strongly dependent on bunch
energy, and is also proportional to the device’s operational frequency [15], making Optical
Stochastic Cooling (OSC) [17], for example, orders of magnitude more effective than mi-
crowave stochastic cooling schemes and even more so than electron cooling at high energies
[18]. Like the OSC method, Coherent Electron Cooling (CEC) is a variation of transit-time
stochastic cooling, where the same electron bunch acts as both the ’pick-up’ modulator
and the kicker [19–21]. Initially, the electron bunch receives a longitudinal density imprint,
shaped by the hadron bunch [22]; this kick is then amplified and transformed into longi-
tudinal density modulation by a chicane, and finally, the hadron bunch is kicked by the
modulated electron bunch with a specific phase shift, achieving cooling. The basic system
scheme, to be built at the Electron Ion Collider (EIC), is depicted in Fig. 1 [23].

 It can be seen from Eq. (5) that the effective cooling time decreases significantly even
when the spectral power in the NIR region is not much larger than the shot level |δρe (ω)|2 =
1/N . Noise comparable to 1/N is called ”low-level” throughout the paper, and its NIR part
presents the main interest of the study.
 The same noise is also relevant in FELs. In self-amplified spontaneous emission (SASE)
and self-seeded FELs the microwave instability starts from the initial noise in the bunch,
leading to the bunch microbunching and resulting in coherent radiation. Hence, the initial
noise within the FEL bandwidth plays a beneficial role [5].Conversely, in externally seeded
FELs, such noise disrupts the seed signal, and reducing noise at the initial seed wavelength
can lower the seed laser power requirements. The dynamics of noise in HGHG FELs was
analyzed in Ref. [25], while its approximate effects on HGHG FELs were studied in Ref. [6].
A more detailed description of noise effect on the radiation from a single undulator is given
in Ref. [26].
 There have been several proposals in the past to suppress the noise, for example, with a
broad-band laser amplifier [27], or in a drift section [28] (and references therein). However,
before suppressing the noise it is necessary to understand its amplitude and spectral de-
composition, as well as its origin. Although the noise exceeding shot level has been already
observed in linear electron accelerators without external wavelength modulation [29], there
is no comprehensive analysis and simulation support for low-level fluctuations, especially in
the range of bunch parameters similar to EIC CEC.

 5

 In this paper we present measurements of the electron bunch density noise level in the 0.5
– 3 µm wavelength range, and compare them with analytical and tracking predictions. We
show that if the bunch has parameters similar to ones in the proposed EIC CEC scheme, it
does not have major fluctuations in the NIR region, while the noise becomes much stronger
if the bunch is longitudinally compressed.
 The article is organized as follows. In sections II, III we give an overview of the exper-
iment: general idea and technical details. It is followed by section IV, devoted to provide
a theoretical basis for predicting and analyzing the experimental data. This data is pre-
sented in section V, alongside with a discussion. Next, the simulations and the tracking
results are briefly described in section VI. Finally, section VII contains a discussion about
the compressed beam and the elevated noise, together with a list of addressed issues and
effects.
 The electron bunch noise influence on the cooling rate at EIC CEC is described in ap-
pendix A. Appendix B provides a detailed study of the optical channel, necessary for exper-
imental data understanding, and appendix C summarizes the main numerical values used
in the article.

II. METHODS

 Density fluctuations in electron bunches are traditionally measured by means of Tran-
sition Radiation (TR). The connection between the density fluctuations in the bunch and
the TR signal has been well established in FELs, where the space charge impedance in a
long linear accelerator, as well as strong compression to a high peak current, often result in
bunch micro-structures [30]. This microbunching strongly amplifies the TR in the optical
range of frequencies, known as coherent optical transition radiation (COTR) [31].

 C. Optical channel

 In order to account for the influence of the light transport channel, shown in Fig. 7, on the
measured photodiode signal, we analyze the optical system formed by two parabolic mirrors.
A rigorous treatment of the transportation requires a simulation using the direct integration
method. However, all the necessary estimations can be obtained using geometrical optics
considerations. They reveal that each point on the OTR screen is imaged onto a unique
point on the photodiode screen, independent of the emission angle. Furthermore, the optical
path length does not depend on the emission angle either, implying that the phase of the
radiation at the photodiode preserves locally the longitudinal coherence present at the OTR
screen.

single-shots. The signal saturation is treated by applying a fit to the rising part, which slope
is proportional to the signal amplitude. An example of an overshot signal is presented in

VII. COMPARISON OF THE RESULTS

 The uncompressed or moderately compressed bunch (l > 2 ps) has no additional noise
above the shot level F = 1, which is supported by both the experiment and the space-charge
particle tracking. This baseline is conserved after the bunch compression, with the coherent
part revealing itself and reaching maximum at the second RF cavity (CC2) phase ϕ = −31◦ .
By comparing the simulated and experimental spectra for compressed bunches, we support
the theoretical estimation of p ≈ 0.08 ± 0.04.
 However, the individual filters’ data are not in agreement. The difference is most likely
caused by the systematic uncertainties of the experiment: the transmission functions of the
elements are not known exactly, and the bunch parameters were not controlled with enough
precision due to a lack of theoretical predictions and tracking at the time of the experiment.
 Nevertheless, other possible issues were also considered. The bunch energy is too high for
wakefields to have such a major impact. Moreover, the characteristic short-range wakefield
wavelength is 5 mm ≫ 1 µm, and the lattice length is too short. Moving further, the bunch
current is not high enough for CSR to noticeably change the bunch dynamics in the NIR
region.

 22

 The signal increase might be explained by another type of radiation, reflected from the
OTR screen. Examples include CSR or Coherent Optical Edge Radiation (COER). The
radiation traveling directly from the bending magnets to the diagnostics system, as well as
any other similar direct radiation, was ruled out by blocking the path. Still, it can couple
to the beam pipe waveguide modes, land on the OTR screen, and be reflected to the optical
channel. For the estimation purposes it can be assumed that all the radiated energy couples
to the waveguide. With this assumption, two radiation candidates have the same power
dependence on the longitudinal bunch distribution of the bunch as the COTR (here we also
assume that the last dipole is the only source of the radiation, and the bunch length change
is small both in the dipole and between the dipole and the OTR source). All is left to do is
to compare the relative amplitude of these three.

VIII. CONCLUSION

 We have constructed and analyzed a low-level noise measurement system, collected the
experimental data, and compared it with theoretical expectations and simulations. An
uncompressed or moderately compressed bunch (l > 2 ps) has no additional noise above
shot level F = 1, that is supported by both experiment and space-charge particle tracking.
The experiment covers the range of wavelengths up to λ = 2.5 µm, while the rest of the EIC
CEC significance range is covered by the simulations.

as the detector sensitivity extends only up to 3 µm. Extending this comparison to a broader

 An estimation of the transverse bunch size effect on coherence loss in an optical channel
was proposed to better align with experimental observations. While this approach proved
to be in agreement with experiment when averaged by all filters, comparison with individual
filters showed some disagreement.
 Future studies could expand the experimental spectral range at higher energies to better
align with the requirements of CEC schemes and FELs. These experiments should have
precise diagnostics of all the measurement elements and bunch parameters.
