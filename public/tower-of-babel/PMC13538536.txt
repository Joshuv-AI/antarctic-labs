Dynamic tensile mechanical properties and energy dissipation characteristics of sandstone with varying degrees of initial damage
Authors: Wenbao Shi, Haoran Shi, Chuanming Li, Chao Qi, Jucai Chang, Aoyun Yan, Feiyang Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo elucidate the influence of pre-existing damage on the dynamic tensile behavior and energy dissipation mechanisms of sandstone, cyclic loading–unloading followed by dynamic Brazilian splitting tests were conducted using a split Hopkinson pressure bar (SHPB) system. X-ray computed tomography (CT) was utilized to quantify the internal microstructural evolution under varying degrees of initial damage. The results reveal a clear intrinsic coupling relationship among initial damage, dynamic tension, and energy evolution: First, cyclic loading exacerbates internal fracture coalescence, significantly increasing the pre-existing damage degree. Second, this initial damage fundamentally deteriorates the rock’s dynamic tensile properties; the dynamic tensile strength decreases markedly as damage intensifies, with highly damaged samples retaining only 54% of the intact strength. Under impact, crack propagation in pre-damaged sandstone remains predominantly tensile, while macroscopic deformations (shear strain and vertical displacement) are significantly amplified. Finally, from an energy perspective, pre-existing internal cracks act as localized energy absorption zones. Consequently, an increase in initial damage drives a higher damage-dissipated energy density and a corresponding reduction in transmitted energy during dynamic failure. This study highlights the failure mechanisms of damaged rock driven by energy dissipation, providing essential theoretical support for the stability evaluation and dynamic disaster early-warning of surrounding rock in deep mining engineering.Keywords: Damage degree, CT scanning, Dynamic tensile properties, Failure characteristics, Energy dissipationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Materials science, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

In recent years, as coal mining in China has progressively transitioned to deeper underground levels, geological conditions have become increasingly complex. Consequently, engineering challenges such as high in situ stress and severe mining-induced disturbances have become more prominent1. During mining operations, such as mechanical coal cutting and blasting excavation, the surrounding rock is inevitably subjected to the superposition of cyclic loading/unloading and dynamic disturbances, leading to varying degrees of cumulative internal damage. This cumulative damage alters the physical and mechanical properties of the rock mass and can trigger dynamic disasters, such as rockbursts and gas outbursts, which pose severe threats to mine safety. Extensive engineering practices and theoretical studies indicate that the failure of roadway surrounding rock is predominantly tensile2–4. Furthermore, the dynamic tensile mechanical response of rock is closely governed by its initial degree of damage. Therefore, investigating the dynamic tensile mechanical properties and failure evolution mechanisms of sandstone with varying degrees of damage is of profound engineering significance for understanding instability mechanisms and developing early warning systems for deep surrounding rock.

Extensive research has been conducted globally on the tensile mechanical properties, damage evolution, and failure characteristics of rocks under various loading conditions. Initially, by deploying split Hopkinson pressure bar (SHPB) systems coupled with high-speed photography and digital image correlation (DIC), standard dynamic behaviors and the approximately logarithmic relationship between impact pressure and average strain rate have been successfully quantified5–7. Moving beyond baseline dynamic loading, advanced constitutive models and non-destructive indicators, such as the Oda damage variable and acoustic emission (AE) parameterization, have been established to numerically formulate creep degradation and structural damage evolution within heterogeneous jointed rock masses8,9. Furthermore, the sensitivity of rock dynamic tensile criteria to inherent anisotropic properties and external operational environments has been comprehensively documented, revealing that bedding plane dip angles, high-temperature thermal treatments, and dynamic loading rates exert strong governing effects on dynamic splitting strength10–12. More recently, the mechanical responses, fatigue accumulation, and thermodynamic characteristics of geomaterials have been investigated under diverse cyclic loading-unloading stress paths13–15.

However, despite these individual milestones, a critical research gap still remains: there is a profound lack of correlation analysis explicitly revealing the intrinsic relationships among pre-damage, dynamic tension, and energy dissipation. Existing literature widely bifurcates prior cyclic fatigue degradation from subsequent high-strain-rate impact failure. Consequently, the scientific question of how quantified microstructural pre-damage regulates the dynamic tensile properties and governs the hot-button mechanisms of localized energy dissipation during sudden failure has not been fully elucidated. In practical deep coal mining and tunnel blasting excavations, the surrounding rock frequently undergoes cyclic loading and unloading—inducing initial damage—before being subjected to dynamic disturbances such as blasting. This complex stress path results in mechanical properties and failure modes that differ significantly from those observed under single loading conditions16–18.To address this gap, the present study focuses on sandstone from the Lianghuai mining area. Different degrees of initial damage were simulated through uniaxial cyclic loading and unloading tests, and the evolution of the internal pore structure was analyzed using X-ray computed tomography (CT) scanning. Subsequently, dynamic splitting tests under various impact loads were conducted using an SHPB platform coupled with a high-speed camera. This approach allowed for the continuous capture and recording of the entire failure process, facilitating a detailed analysis of the dynamic tensile mechanical response and energy dissipation characteristics of the sandstone. Ultimately, the crack development mechanisms under different damage conditions were investigated, providing critical theoretical support for the stability evaluation and damage early-warning of surrounding rock in mining roadways.

Methods

Specimen preparation

The experimental rock samples were collected from the Lianghuai mining area in Anhui Province, which belongs to the nationally planned Lianghuai coal base. Through a rigorous process of coring, cutting, and grinding, the initial materials were fabricated into standard cylindrical specimens measuring 50 mm in diameter and 50 mm in height. Following the cyclic loading and unloading tests (the specific procedures for which are detailed in ‘Cyclic loading and unloading test’), these pre-damaged specimens were re-cored, cut, and polished to produce standard Brazilian disc specimens with a diameter of 50 mm and a thickness of 25 mm. To ensure testing accuracy and reliability, the prepared specimens strictly adhered to the required geometric tolerances19, with the non-parallelism of the end faces controlled to within 0.02 mm and the non-perpendicularity maintained below 0.05 mm. SHPB test system

The experiments were conducted using the split Hopkinson pressure bar (SHPB) test system at Anhui University of Science and Technology, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The system primarily comprises a high-speed camera, an ultra-dynamic strain acquisition instrument, an oscilloscope, a computer, and the Hopkinson pressure bar apparatus.

SHPB and high-speed digital image correlation test system.

The pressure bars, each measuring 50 mm in diameter, consist of a 1000 mm incident bar and a 590 mm transmission bar. Both the pressure bars and the striker are fabricated from high-strength alloy steel, characterized by a density of 7697 kg/m3, an elastic modulus of 210 GPa, and a longitudinal wave velocity of 5190 m/s. The generated shock wave exhibits a sinusoidal profile. During testing, a spindle-shaped striker impacts the incident bar axially at a specified velocity, generating a stress wave within the incident bar. Upon reaching the rock specimen, this incident wave partially reflects back into the incident bar as a reflected stress wave, while the remainder propagates through the specimen into the transmission bar as a transmitted stress wave. Strain gauges affixed to the pressure bars capture the corresponding stress wave signals.

The data acquisition setup relies on the ultra-dynamic strain acquisition instrument, the oscilloscope, and the high-speed camera. To meet the experimental requirements, the framing rate of the MotionPRO high-speed camera was set to 40,000 frames per second (fps) with a resolution of 1024 × 576 prior to testing, ensuring the clear and continuous capture of the entire deformation and failure process of the rock specimens.

For the dynamic splitting tests, three distinct impact gas pressures were established: 0.4 MPa, 0.5 MPa, and 0.6 MPa20. Prior to each impact, the striker was positioned at a consistent location within the high-pressure gas chamber. The distance between the end of the incident bar and the muzzle of the launch chamber was precisely calibrated. This ensured that the initial position and impact stroke of the striker remained constant upon the instantaneous release of the high-pressure gas, thereby guaranteeing a reproducible loading velocity for each respective impact load condition.

Cyclic loading and unloading test

The TYJ-1500 M rock mechanics testing machine was employed to conduct the cyclic loading and unloading tests. Rock specimens with varying degrees of initial damage were prepared by applying different cyclic load peaks.

Preliminary testing revealed that when the peak cyclic load reached 80%σ (where σ denotes the static uniaxial compressive strength of the rock), localized failure occurred at the 10th and 19th cycles in two separate trial specimens. Therefore, to ensure the induction of fatigue damage without causing macroscopic failure, and to clearly distinguish the varying degrees of damage among the specimens, three peak cyclic loads were selected for this study: 0% σ, 67% σ, and 77% σ. The number of loading cycles was uniformly set to 2021,22. While varying the number of cycles can also influence fatigue damage, this study focuses exclusively on the effect of different peak cyclic loads on the resulting damage degree and subsequent mechanical properties, keeping the cycle count constant.

The cyclic loading and unloading path is illustrated in Fig. 3. The loading process was load-controlled at a constant rate of 0.5 kN/s14,22. Following each loading phase to the predetermined peak, the specimen was unloaded to a residual load of 0.5 kN.

During the loading phase, pre-existing microcracks within the rock gradually close under compressive stress. Throughout the repeated loading and unloading cycles, the density of these cracks continuously increases. They coalesce and interconnect to form a complex fracture network. The proliferation of these internal cracks significantly degrades the load-bearing capacity of the rock, reduces its elastic modulus, and consequently leads to the observed gradual attenuation of the longitudinal wave velocity.

CT scanning test

Through CT scanning tests of both undamaged and damaged rock specimens, coupled with three-dimensional visualization and reconstruction using Avizo software, the evolution of the internal microstructural parameters under varying pre-damage conditions was obtained. A ROYMA CT4000 scanner was utilized to conduct high-speed, non-destructive scanning of the specimens. The system provides an image resolution as high as 20.5 µm, ensuring a reliable data foundation for the accurate characterization of the rock’s microstructure.

To illustrate the structural alterations induced by varying degrees of damage, undamaged and pre-damaged specimens were selected for cross-sectional CT scanning at the top (slice 100), middle ( slice 1000), and bottom (slice 1900) sections. The scanning parameters were configured as follows: a voltage of 180 kV, a current of 100 µA, an exposure time of 0.5 s, a resolution of 20.5 µm, 1800 scanning frames, and a total scanning duration of 15 min. Each specimen was scanned continuously from top to bottom, yielding a total of 2000 two-dimensional grayscale image slices per group.

The total fracture volume was quantitatively calculated using Avizo software. First, grayscale thresholding was applied to separate the fractures from the solid rock matrix, converting the 2D slices into binary images. These binary slices were then stacked to reconstruct the 3D fracture network. Finally, the total fracture volume was strictly calculated by multiplying the total number of identified fracture voxels by the physical volume of a single voxel (determined by the 20.5 μm spatial resolution).It should be noted that due to the 20.5 μm spatial resolution of the CT scans, micro-pores and nascent micro-cracks smaller than this threshold cannot be explicitly captured in the reconstructed network. However, since the primary objective of the CT analysis is to characterize the mesoscopic fracture coalescence and volumetric expansion that directly dictate macroscopic structural deterioration, this resolution is sufficient to capture the dominant damage evolution features. Furthermore, the cumulative deteriorating influence of any sub-resolution micro-defects is inherently reflected in the macroscopic mechanical damage variable (Dσ).

Results and discussion

Damage variable analysis

Wave velocity is highly sensitive to micro-defects such as microcracks, whereas macroscopic strength is governed by various mesoscopic mechanisms, including crack propagation, inter-particle friction, and plastic deformation. Consequently, there are fundamental differences in their response mechanisms and sensitivities to structural deterioration, leading to distinct damage evolution rates when evaluated through wave velocity attenuation versus strength degradation under cyclic loading. To characterize the damage evolution of the rock more comprehensively and accurately, this study employs a comparative analysis of two distinct damage variables.

As shown in Table 5, both Dσ and DV increase continuously with the cyclic load level. At a peak cyclic load of 67% σ, the values of Dσ and DV are 0.089 and 0.153, respectively. When the cyclic load increases to 77% σ, Dσ and DV reach 0.111 and 0.204, respectively. These represent the highest pre-existing damage values successfully induced among the experimental groups, indicating that the most severe internal structural deterioration prior to macroscopic failure was achieved at this loading level.

Higher cyclic loading levels correspond to greater energy input into the rock specimen during each loading and unloading cycle. Consequently, a larger portion of this energy is irreversibly dissipated through inter-particle friction, slip, and the initiation of new microcracks. This process accelerates the development and coalescence of microcrack networks, which reduces the effective load-bearing area of the rock (manifested as an increase in Dσ). Simultaneously, the proliferation of open cracks attenuates the elastic modulus (manifested as an increase in DV). The concurrent growth of both damage variables confirms that high-stress cyclic loading and unloading induces severe, irreversible deterioration of the internal rock structure.

In the subsequent sections of this paper, the uniaxial compressive strength damage variable (Dσ) is adopted as the primary metric to characterize the overall damage degree of the rock specimens, with larger Dσ values indicating more severe damage.

Verification of dynamic stress balance

In SHPB testing, achieving dynamic stress equilibrium within the rock specimen prior to failure is a fundamental prerequisite for ensuring the validity of the experimental results. During impact loading, strain gauges mounted on the incident and transmission bars record the transient electrical signals. Following signal conversion, the time-history strain curves of the elastic bars at both ends of the specimen are obtained. Based on these data, a representative dynamic stress equilibrium diagram for the splitting test of a typical rock specimen is plotted, as illustrated in Fig. 6.

Relationship between dynamic tensile strength and damage degree, impact load.

As shown in Table 6, the dynamic tensile strength of the rock specimens under impact loading exhibits a pronounced strain rate effect. Specifically, as the impact load increases, the dynamic tensile strength, average strain rate, and loading rate correspondingly increase. This observation is consistent with the findings of previous studies26,27.

Under different damage states, the dynamic tensile strength of the rock specimens consistently exhibits a strain-rate hardening effect with the increase of the impact load; however, the initial pre-damage exerts a pronounced degradation effect on the magnitude of this dynamic enhancement. Specifically, when the impact load increases from 0.4 MPa to 0.6 MPa, the dynamic tensile strength of the low pre-damage rock specimen (Dσ = 0.089) surges from 5.67 MPa to 19.825 MPa, representing an increment of 250%. Conversely, when the damage escalates to a severe state (Dσ = 0.111), the strength under identical impact conditions only rises from 4.23 MPa to 13.676 MPa, with the increment dropping to 223%. This demonstrates that severe damage disrupts the overall structural integrity of the rock, inducing the scattering and attenuation of internal stress waves, and thereby suppressing the loading-rate hardening effect on the dynamic tensile strength of the rock specimens.

Furthermore, Fig. 7 demonstrates that the dynamic tensile strength of the rock specimens decreases as the degree of initial damage increases. This behavior can be attributed to the microstructural evolution during the cyclic loading and unloading phase. During the initial stages of cyclic loading, pre-existing microcracks and pores within the rock tend to close under compression. However, as the cyclic loading progresses, new microcracks initiate and propagate, leading to the accumulation of internal damage. This progressive structural deterioration degrades the load-bearing capacity of the rock matrix, ultimately resulting in a reduction in its dynamic tensile strength. Consequently, compared to specimens with a damage variable of Dσ = 0.089, those with Dσ = 0.111 contain a higher density of newly formed cracks, exhibit more severe structural deterioration, and correspondingly yield a lower dynamic tensile strength.

Evolution characteristics of strain field

The entire dynamic splitting failure process of the rock specimens across the different groups was captured using a high-speed camera. The acquired images were subsequently analyzed and processed using MATLAB software. A circular area with a localized radius of 24 mm was selected as the area of interest (AOI) for computational analysis, and observation points were strategically deployed in regions exhibiting large deformations. The digital image analysis parameters were configured with a subset radius of 32 pixels and a step size of 1 pixel.

The vertical strain evolution contours of the specimens during the dynamic splitting process under impact loads of 0.4 MPa, 0.5 MPa, and 0.6 MPa were calculated and are illustrated in Fig. 8. As shown in the figure, the distribution of the vertical strain field stabilizes during the dynamic splitting process, exhibiting distinct symmetry along both the X and Y axes. A tensile strain concentration zone emerges in the center of the specimen along the loading direction, further confirming that the rock was in a state of dynamic stress equilibrium during the loading phase.

Vertical strain field distribution of rock samples under different impact loads.

When the specimen is subjected to a radially symmetrical impact load, the central region consistently experiences the maximum tensile strain. Under the continuous influence of this high tensile strain, microcracks initially nucleate in the central area. These cracks subsequently propagate outward along the radial loading direction, ultimately leading to the macroscopic splitting failure of the rock. Notably, this failure process and the associated phenomena closely resemble those observed in the static Brazilian disc test, verifying that the tensile stress perpendicular to the loading direction is the primary driving factor for crack initiation and propagation.

Furthermore, Fig. 8 indicates that the vertical strain of the specimen increases with higher impact loads. The strain concentration zone localizes in the central region and eventually extends radially across the entire specimen. As the impact load increases, the kinetic energy imparted to the rock correspondingly increases. This enhanced energy accelerates the expansion and coalescence of internal microcracks and enlarges the damage zone, resulting in greater axial deformation. Consequently, under impact loads of 0.4 MPa, 0.5 MPa, and 0.6 MPa, the corresponding maximum vertical strains of the rock specimens were recorded as 0.06, 0.10, and 0.14, respectively.

Figures 10 and 11 present the shear strain field distributions of specimens with varying degrees of initial damage and under different impact loads, respectively (with tensile strain defined as positive and compressive strain as negative). Although the peak values in the shear strain concentration zones vary, the maximum values are consistently localized within the central region of the specimen. This pronounced strain concentration indicates that cracks initially propagate in the central area under the combined influence of tensile and shear stresses. Concurrently, while multiple local shear strain concentration zones emerge in the middle of the specimen during impact loading, they do not coalesce to form a dominant, continuous shear band. This observation suggests that crack propagation in the sandstone is predominantly driven by tensile failure.

As shown in Fig. 10, under a constant impact load, the maximum shear strain of the specimen increases with the degree of initial damage. At the moment of initial crack initiation, the maximum shear strains for specimens with Dσ values of 0, 0.089, and 0.111 are 0.00015, 0.002, and 0.003, respectively. At 20 µs, these values rise to 0.004, 0.005, and 0.008, and by 60 µs, they further increase to 0.02, 0.03, and 0.08, respectively. This trend occurs because the pre-damage induced by cyclic loading primarily causes the expansion of internal microcracks and the coalescence of pore structures. This degradation significantly reduces the overall stiffness of the rock while increasing its plastic deformation capacity. Consequently, under the same impact energy, highly damaged specimens—characterized by a more fragile structure and diminished deformation resistance—are more susceptible to inter-particle slip and rapid crack development, thereby generating more pronounced shear strains.

Furthermore, Fig. 11 demonstrates that for a given degree of initial damage, the maximum shear strain increases significantly as the impact load rises. At initial cracking, the maximum shear strains corresponding to impact loads of 0.4 MPa, 0.5 MPa, and 0.6 MPa are 0.003, 0.002, and 0.003, respectively. At 20 µs, these values become 0.005, 0.007, and 0.008, and by 60 µs, they increase to 0.012, 0.03, and 0.08, respectively. These data reveal a clear positive correlation between load and strain: at any given time, a higher impact load generates a greater shear strain. This is primarily because higher impact loads transfer greater kinetic and stress wave energy into the specimen, causing a sharp elevation in the internal stress levels. These high dynamic stresses accelerate the development and coalescence of microcracks, exacerbating inter-particle slip and dislocation within the rock matrix. Simultaneously, the pronounced strain rate effect under high-load conditions diminishes the plastic deformation capacity of the rock, making it more susceptible to brittle shear failure.

Figure 12 presents the vertical displacement field distribution of the rock specimens under varying degrees of initial damage. As illustrated, under a constant impact load, the maximum vertical displacement of the specimens increases consistently with the degree of damage. At the onset of crack initiation, the maximum vertical displacements of specimens with Dσ values of 0, 0.089, and 0.111 are 0.15 mm, 0.2 mm, and 0.3 mm, respectively. By t = 40 µs, these values rise to 0.8 mm, 1.0 mm, and 1.5 mm, respectively. As the cracks continue to propagate, the vertical displacements reach 1.0 mm, 1.5 mm, and 2.0 mm at t = 60 µs. At this stage, the relative displacement differences between the upper and lower ends of the corresponding specimens amount to 1.6 mm, 2.5 mm, and 3.0 mm, respectively.

Mechanistically, this occurs because a large number of microcracks and pores gradually accumulate within the rock matrix during the preliminary cyclic loading and unloading phase. This accumulation compromises the structural integrity and reduces the effective load-bearing area of the specimen, consequently degrading its overall stiffness. As the degree of initial damage increases, the rock’s capacity to resist deformation is progressively diminished. Therefore, under identical impact loading conditions, heavily damaged specimens are significantly more susceptible to vertical deformation, thereby exhibiting greater macroscopic displacement.

The macroscopic deformation induced by initial damage is significantly amplified, corresponding to the severe inelastic sliding of internal microcracks and the nucleation and propagation of large-scale nascent cracks. From a thermodynamic perspective, the intensified internal frictional dislocation and inelastic deformation within the microstructure are bound to consume a massive amount of mechanical work. Consequently, this degradative response of the strain and displacement fields directly dictates the subsequent evolution laws of the dynamic energy dissipation within the system.

Analysis of failure characteristics

The fracture evolution characteristics of specimens under impact loading constitute a primary focus in the study of the dynamic mechanical properties of coal and rock28. During the SHPB tests, the failure process of the rock specimens was recorded using a high-speed camera, and the corresponding failure characteristics were analyzed, as illustrated in Fig. 13.

Splitting failure process of rock samples with different damage degrees.

Figure 13 presents the splitting failure process of rock specimens with varying degrees of initial damage. The initial undeformed state is designated as 0 μs, the onset of crack initiation is captured at 20 μs, and subsequent stages of the splitting process are presented at 20 μs intervals. Under impact loading, a primary crack initially nucleates in the central region of the specimen along the loading axis. Subsequently, numerous secondary microcracks continuously emerge surrounding the primary crack. As the load persists, the primary crack propagates and ultimately penetrates the entire specimen. The overall crack development process exhibits distinct symmetrical characteristics.

As observed in Fig. 13, under identical impact loading conditions, the severity of macroscopic fragmentation exacerbates as the initial damage degree increases. Pre-existing microcracks and pore defects induce severe stress concentrations, accelerate crack propagation, and diminish both the dynamic strength and stiffness of the rock, ultimately culminating in more extensive macroscopic destruction.

Furthermore, the crack initiation frames at 20 μs reveal that a higher degree of initial damage corresponds to a larger initial crack scale. Specimens with substantial initial damage contain a high density of pre-existing microcracks and pores. Upon loading, these defects generate significant stress concentration effects, providing preferential pathways for crack nucleation and propagation. Under external impact loading, energy preferentially concentrates in these pre-damaged zones, supplying the initial cracks with higher driving energy during the nucleation phase. Consequently, these initial cracks develop at a significantly larger scale compared to those in undamaged or less damaged specimens.

For specimens with strength damage variables (Dσ) of 0.089 and 0.111, large fragmented rock blocks emerge in the central region. The heavily damaged specimens contain an abundance of internal microcracks, which serve as the primary zones for energy dissipation and dictate the dominant paths for crack development during dynamic impact. This localized concentration and release of energy facilitate the formation of substantial, discrete rock fragments in the center of the specimen. Conversely, the internal structure of the undamaged specimen (Dσ = 0) is relatively intact and uniform, allowing the impact energy to dissipate more evenly. In this case, cracks initiate randomly and coalesce rapidly, resulting in a more pulverized, crushing failure in the central region rather than the formation of large structural blocks.

Analysis of energy evolution characteristics

According to the principles of thermodynamics, material failure is essentially an energy-driven state instability phenomenon. The deformation and ultimate failure of rock specimens are governed by the combined effects of energy dissipation and energy release29. In the SHPB test, energy propagates through the incident and transmission bars in the form of stress waves. Specifically, the energy carried by the incident, reflected, and transmitted waves is defined as the incident energy (WI), reflected energy (WR), and transmitted energy (WT), respectively. Assuming negligible energy loss at the bar-specimen interfaces during stress wave transmission, the total energy absorbed by the specimen under the action of the impact stress wave is denoted as the absorbed energy (WS). Sandstone dynamic stress–strain curve.

Compaction Stage (Section OA): In this initial stage, the dynamic stress–strain curve of the rock specimen exhibits a convex shape. Under impact loading, the pre-existing microcracks and pores within the specimen are gradually compressed, achieving full compaction at point A. Due to the closure, displacement, and internal friction associated with these initial defects, a fraction of the input energy is converted into dissipated energy; however, the overall energy dissipation during this stage remains relatively low.

Elastic Stage (Section AB): During this phase, a limited number of microcracks begin to initiate and stably propagate under the continuous impact load. Consequently, a portion of the input energy is progressively dissipated, leading to a steady, albeit moderate, increase in the total dissipated energy.

Plastic Yield Stage (Section BC): Point C represents the peak stress on the dynamic stress–strain curve. Throughout this stage, the growth rate of dissipated energy accelerates significantly, accompanied by a rapid increase in the total dissipation amount. This energy surge corresponds to the extensive, large-scale nucleation, propagation, and coalescence of internal cracks within the rock specimen, advancing until they fully penetrate the structure.

Post-Peak Failure Stage (Section CD): At point C, the main macroscopic cracks fully penetrate the specimen, signifying structural failure driven by the rapid accumulation of dissipated energy. In this post-peak stage, the energy dissipation rate continues to increase sharply as the specimen fractures, with the total dissipated energy ultimately reaching its peak value.

Overall, Fig. 14 delineates the dynamic evolution of the rock specimen from microscopic damage accumulation to macroscopic failure from an energy perspective. The continuous increase in dissipated energy fundamentally reflects the progressive development and propagation of internal microcracks. When the rate of energy dissipation accelerates sharply, it indicates the critical transition from microcrack accumulation to the formation of macroscopic fractures. At this juncture, the specimen enters a near-failure state and rapidly loses its overall structural stability.

Figure 15 illustrates the relationship between the energy dissipation density of the rock specimens and their initial degree of damage, while Fig. 16 depicts the relationship between the transmitted energy and the degree of damage. Synthesizing the data from Table .6, Figs. 15, and 16, it is evident that under identical impact loading conditions, an increase in the initial damage degree results in a corresponding increase in energy dissipation density and a significant decrease in transmitted energy. For instance, under an impact load of 0.6 MPa, the energy dissipation density and transmitted energy for the undamaged specimen (Dσ = 0) are 6.847 J/cm3 and 1.248 J, respectively. For the specimen with Dσ = 0.089, these values are 9.859 J/cm3 and 0.502 J, and for the highly damaged specimen (Dσ = 0.111), they are 12.678 J/cm3 and 0.312 J, respectively.

Mechanistically, an increased degree of initial damage corresponds to a higher density of pre-existing internal cracks. Under constant impact loading, this enhanced crack network amplifies energy dissipation mechanisms—such as inter-particle friction, macroscopic crushing, and thermal conversion—thereby increasing the energy dissipation density. Concurrently, the proliferation of internal damage alters the wave impedance of the rock matrix, intensifies stress wave scattering, and degrades the overall wave propagation efficiency. Consequently, a larger proportion of the input energy is absorbed by the damaged regions rather than transmitted through the specimen, leading to a marked reduction in transmitted energy.

Conversely, for specimens with the same degree of initial damage, both the energy dissipation density and the transmitted energy progressively increase with higher impact loads. For example, in highly damaged specimens (Dσ = 0.111), the energy dissipation density and transmitted energy are 3.715 J/cm3 and 0.173 J at 0.4 MPa; 10.579 J/cm3 and 0.268 J at 0.5 MPa; and 12.678 J/cm3 and 0.312 J at 0.6 MPa, respectively. An increase in the impact load yields a significant rise in the total input energy. Under these higher dynamic stresses, internal cracks are more extensively activated, propagated, and subjected to intense frictional sliding. This intensifies the energy dissipation processes, driving up the energy dissipation density. Simultaneously, the higher incident load forces a greater absolute amount of energy to penetrate and propagate through the specimen, resulting in an overall increase in transmitted energy.

Conclusion

Cyclic loading fundamentally alters the mesostructure of sandstone by inducing continuous fatigue, leading to the severe expansion and coalescence of pore structures. This pre-existing internal damage acts as the primary intrinsic driver for the rock’s subsequent vulnerability to dynamic disturbances.

The accumulation of initial damage critically undermines the rock’s macroscopic load-bearing capacity. These findings provide critical empirical data and theoretical support for the stability analysis, non-destructive damage evaluation, and dynamic early-warning monitoring of surrounding rock in mining roadways.
