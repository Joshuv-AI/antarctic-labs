The flexibility of delayed fluoroscopic left ventricular angiography in periprocedural real-time visualization of the lead tip in left bundle branch pacing
Authors: Sung-Hao Huang, Wen-Han Cheng, Hsuan-Ming Tsao, Wen-Liang Lin, Dan-Ying Lee, Shih-Ann Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrent electrocardiography and electrogram (EGM) criteria for confirming left bundle branch (LBB) capture may be inconclusive in certain clinical scenarios. Real-time imaging methods to evaluate lead positioning during LBB pacing (LBBP) remain limited.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the feasibility of delayed fluoroscopic left ventricular (LV) angiography (LVG) for real-time visualization of lead position during LBBP.MethodsPatients undergoing LBB area pacing with delayed LVG were classified as LBBP or LV septal pacing (LVSP) according to EGM-based capture criteria. LBB capture was identified by fascicular potentials, morphology transitions, or a stepwise electrocardiography/EGM algorithm; lead tip–to–LV and right ventricular septal border distances were measured at end-systole and end-diastole, with right ventricular distance representing total lead insertion length.Results76 patients were included (LBBP n = 51; LVSP n = 25). Pacing threshold, impedance, R-wave amplitude, and stimulus-to-V6 LV activation time did not differ significantly between the groups. The QRS onset to V6 LV activation time was shorter in the LBBP group than the LVSP group (33.3 ± 10.6 ms vs 41.4 ± 12.9 ms; P < .05). Delayed LVG demonstrated significantly shorter lead tip–to–LV septal border distances in the LBBP group at both end-diastole (0.93 ± 0.35 mm vs 2.12 ± 0.90 mm) and end-systole (0.87 ± 0.42 mm vs 2.01 ± 0.99 mm; both P < .001), with greater lead insertion depth observed in the LBBP group (13.4 ± 1.2 mm vs 12.9 ± 0.9 mm; P < .05, at end-diastole). No contrast-related renal complications were noted.ConclusionDelayed LVG provides a practical intraprocedural imaging method as an adjunct for real-time visualization of lead position during LBBP, particularly when capture criteria are uncertain.Keywords: Conduction system pacing, Left bundle branch pacing, Helix tip, Fluoroscope, Lead depth, Left bundle branch capture

Introduction

Left bundle branch (LBB) pacing (LBBP) provides physiological pacing with favorable cardiovascular outcomes compared with traditional right ventricular (RV) pacing in patients who are ventricular pacing dependent.1, 2, 3 To achieve optimal LBB capture and improve clinical outcomes,3,4 it is essential to identify electrophysiological (EP) features of the LBB, anatomic landmarks, and serial changes in myocardial current of injury (COI) as the lead crosses the septum.5, 6, 7 Accurately integrating these parameters to guide lead depth, confirm capture, and avoid septal perforation remains a challenge in clinical practice.6,8,9

Recent advances in hierarchical phase-contrast computed tomography (CT) reconstruction have enabled delineation of the conduction anatomy and revealed highly variable conduction axes from the atrioventricular node to the nonbranching bundle and LBB origin.10 This anatomic variability may contribute to inconsistent LBB capture during pacing procedures.5,10 Despite significant progress in high-definition CT-based virtual heart dissection studies in visualizing the conduction system,11 a gap remains in applying this imaging modality to directly guide pacing procedures. At present, CT-based approaches are mainly useful for preprocedural planning or for post-procedural assessment.

Despite the well-established determination of the lead depth on the electrocardiography (ECG) and intracardiac electrogram (EGM)-based LBB capture criteria,12, 13, 14, 15 uncertainties remain in some clinical scenarios. All of the above highlight the unmet clinical need for real-time imaging methods to evaluate LBBP lead depth and detect septal perforation. Intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) offers real-time imaging but is limited by cost.16 The use of transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is mainly limited by suboptimal echo windows and the need for 2 experienced operators.17,18

Because fluoroscopic left ventricular (LV) angiography (LVG) can be performed using standard catheterization equipment, delayed LVG may serve as an adjunctive real-time imaging tool during LBBP implantation. Therefore, we designed a study on delayed LVG aimed at assessing the lead tip–to–LV/RV septal border distances, its impact on LBB capture, and septal perforation during LBBP.

Methods

Study design

The research reported in this article adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki as revised in 2013, and the study was approved by the institutional review board of the Yang Ming Chiao Tung University Hospital, Taiwan; all patients provided a written informed consent. Patients were >18 years of age and met requirements for permanent pacemaker implantation for bradycardia indications. Patients with advanced chronic kidney disease (≥stage 3b) or allergy to contrast medium were excluded. The study cohorts were categorized by EGM-based LBB capture criteria into 2 groups: LV septal pacing (LVSP) and LBBP (Figure 1).

Overview of the study design. ECG = electrocardiography; EGM = electrogram; LBB = left bundle branch; LBBP = left bundle branch pacing; LVSP = left ventricular septal pacing.

Implantation technique

The implantation procedures followed the guidelines for LBBP implantation described by Huang et al19 and the European Heart Rhythm Association clinical consensus statement on LBBP implantation.6 LBBP was performed using the SelectSecure pacing lead (Model 3830, Medtronic Inc, Minneapolis, MN) with a C315 fixed-curve sheath. Tricuspid annulus (TA) angiography was used to assess the RV septal geometry and determine the lead insertion site.20 A unipolar EGM was recorded from the lead tip and displayed on an EP recording system (Bard/Boston Scientific, Lowell, MA). The decision to stop lead advancement (ie, the final lead position) was based on the previously described LBBP assessment algorithm and criteria.6 Delayed LVG was then performed.

LBB capture was confirmed using strict EGM- and ECG-based criteria,14,15 including constant and shortest V5–V6 LV activation time (LVAT) of <75 ms from the pacing stimulus, capture morphology transition during decremental output testing, and presence of LBB/fascicular potential. If these criteria were not met, a stepwise ECG- and EGM-based capture algorithm was applied.21 Patients without definitive EGM-based capture criteria but fulfilling a right bundle branch delay pattern (Qr or qR pattern in lead V1) and meeting ECG-based criteria were classified as LVSP.

Delayed fluoroscopic LVG images

During the proposed LBB capture stage, a 5F pig-tail catheter was inserted into the right atrium via a 7F sheath. This sheath was then used for atrial-lead implantation. Delayed LVG was performed using a power injector (40 mL contrast mixed with 10 mL normal saline) injected over 2 seconds. Image acquisition was delayed by 5 seconds after completion of the contrast injection as contrast drained from the right atrium into the RV and LV, with pacing set at 70 beats/min and cine acquisition at 15 frames/s. Rotational angiography was not used because changing projection angles limit reproducibility of quantitative measurements, and its standard protocol reduced visualization of the LV septal border.

Angiographic measurements were obtained using a Siemens Artis Zee system with a detector spatial resolution of 3.25 line pairs/mm (≈0.3mm). Measurements included the lead tip–to–LV septal border distance, total inserted lead length (lead tip–to–RV septal border distance), and fluoroscopic images of the lead tip perforating into the LV in the left anterior oblique (30°) cranial (20°) view. The distances from the lead tip to the LV and RV borders were measured at the phase of end-diastole (QRS onset) and end-systole (end of T-wave) using ECG gating. Angiographic measurements were performed offline after the procedure using stored cine images, and the analyzed images were verified for accuracy by 2 independent investigators in a blinded fashion. The distance of the lead ring to the TA summit was also measured via right anterior oblique view by another round of RV angiography. The illustration is presented in Figure 2. The safety endpoint was assessed by comparing renal function before and after delayed LVG.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as the means ± standard deviations, and categorical variables are presented as absolute values and percentages. The Student t test and analysis of variance were used for the analysis of continuous data. Fisher’s exact test was used for the analysis of categorical data. Measurement reproducibility was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (Supplemental Table 1). P < .05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 22 for Windows (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY).

A total of 115 patients with bradycardia indications were consecutively enrolled prospectively between February 2022 and June 2025. Patients with advanced chronic kidney disease (n = 32) and those with suboptimal image quality (n = 7) were excluded. 76 patients who underwent LBBP implantation and periprocedural delayed LVG analysis were included (Figure 1).

Patients were mostly elderly (mean age 76.7 ± 9.4 years), with 50% male and an average CHA2DS2-VASc score of 4.3 ± 1.7. 80% of patients had hypertension, half had diabetes, and one-third had heart failure. Baseline demographics were similar between groups except for diabetes, which was more common in the LVSP group than the LBBP group. The primary indication for cardiac implantable electronic device implantation was atrioventricular block (50%), followed by sick sinus syndrome (37%).

The study cohort was divided into 2 groups based on EGM-based LBB capture criteria. 67% of patients (n = 51) met the criteria for LBB capture and were classified as the LBBP group, by the presence of a LBB/fascicular potential (80% [n = 41]), observed capture morphology transition (45% [n = 23]), or the use of a stepwise ECG- and EGM-based capture algorithm (14% [n = 7]). The remaining 25 patients were classified as the LVSP group. Sensed R-wave amplitude, capture threshold, and pacing impedance were not significantly different between the 2 groups. V6 LVAT measured from the pacing stimulus showed no significant difference between the 2 groups (LVSP vs LBBP = 65.0 ± 18.6 ms vs 67.6 ± 13.2 ms; P = .543). However, when V6 LVAT was measured from the onset of the QRS complex, it was significantly shorter in the LBBP group than in the LVSP group (33.3 ± 10.6 ms vs 41.4 ± 12.9 ms; P < .05).

The lead tip–to–LV/RV septal border distances were measured at end-systole and end-diastole using ECG gating. Compared with the LVSP group, the LBBP group exhibited closer proximity of the lead tip to the LV septal border at both end-diastole (LBBP vs LVSP = 0.93 ± 0.35 mm vs 2.12 ± 0.90 mm; P < .001) and end-systole (LBBP vs LVSP = 0.87 ± 0.42 mm vs 2.01 ± 0.99 mm; P < .001). The lead tip–to–RV septal border distance, representing the total inserted lead length, also showed significant differences between the 2 groups. Compared with the LVSP group, the LBBP group demonstrated deeper penetration into the interventricular septum at both end-diastole (LBBP vs LVSP = 13.4 ± 1.2 mm vs 12.9 ± 0.9 mm; P < .05) and end-systole (LBBP vs LVSP = 13.6 ± 0.9 mm vs 13.0 ± 0.9 mm; P < .001).

In most of the LBBP group, LBB capture could be confirmed using EGM-/ECG-based criteria, and LVG in these cases demonstrated that the lead tip was positioned very close to the LV endocardium. In a subset of cases (n = 4 of 51 [8%]), delayed LVG provided additional guidance for lead advancement during LBBP implantation. Regarding the distance from the TA summit to the lead ring, the LBBP group showed a more proximal TA summit than the LVSP group (LBBP vs LVSP = 33.3 ± 8.5 mm vs 40.6 ± 7.1 mm; P < .05). Fluoroscopy time was longer in the LVSP group than in the LBBP group, which may reflect prolonged procedure time and technical challenges in pursuit of LBB capture owing to increased septal thickness, suboptimal sheath support, and difficulty approaching the presumed optimal LBB region.

For the safety endpoint assessment, pre- and postdelayed LVG creatinine levels showed no significant difference (1.00 ± 0.30 mg/dL vs 0.99 ± 0.31 mg/dL). None of the patients developed contrast-associated acute renal failure. Although 1 of the purposes of this study was to use fluoroscopy to detect lead-related septal perforation, none of the patients experienced this complication. However, direct visualization of lead-related septal perforation is an expected capability of delayed LVG.

Discussion

Main findings

A major challenge in LBBP procedures is real-time visualization of lead placement. Our study used delayed LVG to address this limitation by enabling intraprocedural measurement of the lead tip–to–LV/RV septal border distance. The main findings are as follows:

(1)In the LBBP group, the lead tip–to–LV septal border distance was approximately 1 mm or less at both end-diastole and end-systole.

In the LBBP group, the lead tip–to–LV septal border distance was approximately 1 mm or less at both end-diastole and end-systole.

(2)In contrast, the LVSP group had an average distance of approximately 2 mm from the lead tip to the LV septal border.

In contrast, the LVSP group had an average distance of approximately 2 mm from the lead tip to the LV septal border.

(3)Inserted lead depth was roughly 0.5 mm longer in the LBBP group, likely owing to deeper placement.

Inserted lead depth was roughly 0.5 mm longer in the LBBP group, likely owing to deeper placement.

(4)No cases of LV perforation were observed in this cohort. Patients without confirmed LBB capture demonstrated less septal penetration and a greater lead tip–to–LV septal border distance compared with the LBBP group, suggesting that insufficient lead advancement may be a key mechanism underlying failure to achieve LBB capture.

No cases of LV perforation were observed in this cohort. Patients without confirmed LBB capture demonstrated less septal penetration and a greater lead tip–to–LV septal border distance compared with the LBBP group, suggesting that insufficient lead advancement may be a key mechanism underlying failure to achieve LBB capture.

The results indicate that assessing the tip-to-LV/RV border distance may assist in distinguishing LBBP from LVSP, especially when LBB capture criteria are inconclusive.

Insights from post-LBBP implantation imaging studies

Ponnusamy et al22 conducted a CT study for the localization and clinical correlation of LBBP leads (LOCATE LBBP study) in 67 patients. Consistent LBB capture at 6 months was observed in 94% of patients, with a mean distance between the LV blood pool and the lead tip of −0.5 ± 1.8 mm. Lead displacement of >2 mm away from the LV blood pool and the absence of morphology transition during implantation were identified as predictors of LBB capture loss. Ponnusamy et al23 used hierarchical CT to assess the effect of LBBP lead location relative to the conduction tree hierarchy and suggested that a closer pacing lead position relative to the His-Purkinje system or LV endocardium is associated with better functional response. The lead tips in LBBP groups were positioned closer to the LV endocardium than LVSP (0.86 mm vs −0.75 mm).

An echocardiographic study by Peng et al24 demonstrated that the lead tip was significantly closer to the LV septal border in the LBBP group than the LVSP group during both diastole (0.6 ± 0.9 mm vs 3.0 ± 1.6 mm; P < .001) and systole (1.5 ± 1.4 mm vs 4.3 ± 2.6 mm; P < .001). These findings highlighted the importance of real-time intraprocedural monitoring of the lead tip–to–LV septal border distance in LBBP.

Our study showed comparable results in the assessment of lead tip–to–LV septal border distance to confirm LBB capture intraprocedurally rather than through postimplant analysis. A major limitation of CT is that metal artifacts cannot be entirely eliminated, which affects the utility of CT angiography, especially given the small distance between the lead tip and the LV blood pool. In the LOCATE LBBP study, 105 patients underwent CT angiography, but 38 (36%) were excluded from analysis because of metal artifacts.22 In contrast, our study had a lower exclusion rate of 8.4% owing to suboptimal imaging in delayed LVG.

In contrast, lead depth within the interventricular septum is not a perfect predictor of LBB capture when assessed using septography. Su et al25 reported that, in patients with documented LBB potentials, the distance of the lead tip from the RV septal border was 11.2 ± 2.6 mm. In our cohort, leads were advanced further in both groups (average insertion length 13.0∼13.5 mm), with a mean difference of 0.5 mm between LBBP and LVSP groups. However, the AUROC for the lead tip–to–RV septal border distance was only 0.287 (Figure 5), which may reflect variations in septal thickness, lead insertion angle, and lead trajectory.

The LOCATE LBBP study found that 81% of leads were placed in segments 8 and 9 of the American Heart Association 17-segment model, corresponding to the anterior and posterior papillary muscles.22 Owing to the variable distribution of the LBB at the base of these muscles,26 especially in patients with increased wall thickness, delayed LVG may aid in assessing lead positioning near the surrounding papillary muscle area and confirmation of LBB capture.

The proposed indications for delayed LVG

Delayed LVG is not intended to replace ECG-, EGM-, or COI-guided implantation. When LBB capture is clearly confirmed by the implanter based on EGM-based criteria, the role of delayed LVG may be limited. We suggest that delayed LVG may be useful in the following clinical scenarios:

Other real-time imaging modalities for guided LBBP implantation (TTE and ICE)

Yang et al17 demonstrated that TTE-guided LBBP reduced fluoroscopy time by 2.5 minutes compared with radiographic guidance, with similar success and procedure times. A TTE-guided LBBP study conducted by Vijayaraman et al18 demonstrated a 97% success rate in achieving LBB capture using strict criteria compared with 70% using the standard ECG-, EGM-, and COI-guided approach. The lead was advanced under echocardiographic visualization until the tip reached the LV subendocardium. These findings suggest that anatomic imaging guidance is feasible and may be highly effective as an adjunct to conventional implantation methods. Limitations include lead tip artifacts, narrow acoustic windows, and the need for 2 experienced operators.

Kuang et al16 demonstrated that ICE-guided proximal LBBP in 50 patients resulted in shorter fluoroscopic times, fewer lead insertion attempts, and improved LBB trunk pacing compared with non-ICE guidance. ICE offers better visualization of the basal septum and target zone than TTE during LBBP. In ICE-guided cases, LBB capture was assessed when the lead helix was 1–3 mm from the LV septal border, with a lead tip–septal border distance of 1.0 ± 1.4 mm, consistent with our findings. However, the high cost of ICE raises concerns about its routine use.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, patients with advanced kidney disease were excluded owing to contrast use. In addition, because this was a single-arm feasibility study without a control group not undergoing delayed LVG, the incremental benefit of delayed LVG in improving procedural outcomes could not be directly assessed. Second, some image quality was suboptimal owing to contrast dilution. Third, obtaining an individualized fluoroscopic angle for a high-quality image remains challenging owing to contrast within the left atrium overlapping with contrast-enhanced images of the LV. Although a left anterior oblique cranial view may improve visualization of the lead tip relative to the LV septum, optimized imaging angles require further study. Fourth, delayed LVG relies on fixed projection angles, and its accuracy may be under- or overestimated because of suboptimal alignment with the septal plane, septal curvature, and superposition in fixed imaging planes. In contrast, cardiac CT allows multiplanar reformatting, enabling reconstruction of imaging planes perpendicular to the interventricular septum. This facilitates a more accurate assessment of the spatial relationship between the lead tip and the interventricular septum, which may not always be achievable with 2-dimensional fluoroscopy. Fifth, although the angiographic system allows submillimeter measurements, the relatively small difference in total lead insertion depth approaches the limits of measurement precision and should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, the size of the study sample is relatively small; a large-scale study is needed to validate the results of the present study.

Conclusion

Delayed fluoroscopic LVG in LBBP is a flexible and cost-effective tool that enables real-time visualization of lead tip position and its proximity to the LV septal border, aiding implanters in decision making regarding lead advancement without increasing the risk of contrast-associated acute renal failure.

Our findings show that LBBP leads are typically positioned ≤1 mm from the LV septum. These consistent measurements demonstrate that delayed LVG effectively differentiates LBBP from LVSP, especially when capture criteria are uncertain. Its ease of integration may make it a valuable adjunct, particularly in complex cases or for beginner implanters.
