Optimising ICU supply chains: Evidence-based strategies for efficiency, resilience and patient safety—a structured narrative review
Authors: Noor Hasmee, Hema Joshi, Mamita Gurung, Bhupendra Singh, Saman Naseema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInventory management in intensive care units (ICUs) plays a critical role in ensuring uninterrupted patient care, yet it remains an underprioritised component of healthcare operations. This narrative review explores current challenges and evidence-based strategies for optimising ICU supply chains. Issues such as unpredictable demand, manual stock tracking, procurement inefficiencies and limited staff training often contribute to stockouts, expired supplies and delayed interventions. The COVID-19 pandemic further exposed systemic vulnerabilities, emphasising the need for resilient, technology-driven supply systems. A comprehensive literature search (2004–2025) was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science and Google Scholar using keywords related to ICU inventory, artificial intelligence (AI) forecasting, radio frequency identification (RFID), procurement and digital health logistics. Findings were synthesised into thematic categories addressing core problems and interventions. Key strategies include implementing automated tracking systems, adopting AI-driven demand forecasting, standardising procurement policies, training ICU staff in inventory best practices and establishing emergency buffer stock. Emerging technologies such as digital twins and blockchain are highlighted for their potential to enhance transparency, responsiveness and planning accuracy. Additionally, fostering multisourcing supplier partnerships and optimising ICU storage space can further enhance system agility and reduce waste. In conclusion, ICU supply chains must evolve from reactive, manual systems to proactive, data-driven frameworks. Hospitals should invest in digital infrastructure, staff training and ethical procurement models to build resilient, cost-effective and patient-centred supply chains.

Background

Inventory management in intensive care units (ICUs) is a critical yet often overlooked factor in driving patient safety, clinical efficiency and system resilience. Timely access to medications, devices and consumables is essential, especially in time-sensitive ICU interventions. Yet many health systems—especially in low-income and middle-income countries—rely on manual, fragmented inventory systems lacking predictive tools or centralised oversight. Inadequate stock monitoring, poor procurement coordination and limited trained personnel often lead to stockouts, expired drugs or overstocking.13

The COVID-19 pandemic exposed these systemic gaps. ICUs worldwide faced shortages of critical drugs, personal protective equipment (PPE), ventilator parts and dialysis filters.4 Hospitals with real-time tracking and agile procurement adapted better.5 These disparities revealed system-level inefficiencies rooted in reactive inventory models. Even outside pandemics, ICU operations suffer from poor inventory alignment, delayed reorder triggers and disconnected data systems across pharmacy, clinical and procurement teams.6 Unpredictable demand makes optimisation both urgent and complex. Traditional models based on static reorder points often fail to meet ICU needs, leading to resource strain or waste.7 8

A global analysis found over 35% of ICU stock shortages during COVID-19 stemmed from delayed procurement and demand surges.1 These are not isolated issues but signs of systemic failure. Many hospitals—especially in low-resource settings—lack audits, expiration tracking or designated inventory officers.7 8 The cumulative result is a fragile supply chain that fails under pressure and contributes to wastage of resources, expired products and jeopardised care. Existing literature points to solutions such as digital transformation, integration with electronic health records (EHR) and the adoption of just-in-time (JIT) or just-in-case (JIC) inventory strategies.911 However, the translation of these innovations into low-resource ICUs remains limited by cost perceptions, policy inertia and workforce capacity gaps. Addressing these barriers requires a holistic understanding of both structural weaknesses and feasible interventions that support implementation without overburdening frontline staff.

Method

This narrative review followed a structured search of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science and Google Scholar for publications from 2015 to 2025, using terms such as “ICU inventory management”, “hospital procurement systems”, “RFID”, “AI demand forecasting” and “blockchain in healthcare supply chains”. Eligible studies addressed inventory challenges, procurement inefficiencies, technology integration (artificial intelligence (AI), digital twins, Radio Frequency Identification (RFID), ethical sourcing or workforce training in ICUs. Only English-language peer-reviewed articles, reviews, case studies and policy papers were included; non-healthcare, manufacturing-only or opinion pieces were excluded.

Findings were synthesised into nine categories: (1) unpredictable demand and supply chain disruptions, (2) equipment-specific supply chain differences, (3) inventory inaccuracies and manual management, (4) wastage due to expiry and mismanagement, (5) additional inventory management models in healthcare, (6) inadequate institutional support and resource allocation, (7) insufficient staff training and compliance, (8) procurement inefficiencies and non-standardised policies and (9) balancing surplus and shortage in the ICU—reflecting the multifactorial nature of ICU inventory challenges and guiding evidence-based strategies for resilient, technology-enabled systems.

Findings

Unpredictable demand and supply chain disruptions

Unpredictable ICU demand—driven by fluctuating admissions, seasonal outbreaks and crises—complicates forecasting and causes stockouts or overstocking. COVID-19 highlighted these vulnerabilities, with over 60% of US hospitals experiencing major shortages of ventilators, medications and PPE due to rigid procurement and supplier limits.1 Such gaps delayed treatment and increased patient risk. Building resilience requires predictive analytics, diversified suppliers and emergency stockpiles. In a UK pilot, AI-based inventory models reduced emergency stockouts by 45%,2 demonstrating that technology, combined with strategic procurement, can better anticipate surges and maintain supply continuity (see online supplemental file: Practical Insight—Lessons from ICU Supply Challenges during the COVID-19 Crisis).

Equipment-specific supply chain differences

Supply chain considerations differ significantly between categories of ICU equipment. For example, ventilators are high-cost, capital-intensive devices with long lead times and heavy dependence on specialised global manufacturers for components such as turbines and sensors. Their procurement also requires technical expertise for installation, calibration and maintenance, making their logistics and sourcing more complex.12 13 In contrast, consumables such as PPE and syringes, while also critical, typically involve bulk procurement with shorter replenishment cycles and greater reliance on local or regional suppliers.14 Monitoring equipment and diagnostic devices occupy an intermediate position, often being tied to proprietary vendor ecosystems and variable lead times.15 Recognising these distinctions helps in tailoring supply chain models according to equipment class and criticality.

Inventory inaccuracies and manual management

Manual inventory systems remain a major barrier in ICU stock management. Many hospitals rely on paper logs or outdated platforms, limiting real-time visibility and causing stock discrepancies, delayed restocking and avoidable waste. Communication breakdowns further worsen errors.3 In 15 US ICUs, RFID tracking reduced discrepancies by 65% and improved supply availability by 50%.4 Another study found that inaccurate usage records resulted in nearly US$500,000 annually in unaccounted medical supplies and approximately US$2 million in lost patient reimbursement.5 Optimisation models from that analysis recommended adjusting stock levels and cycle counts based on tracking accuracy. Integrating automated systems with EHRs improves inventory accuracy, demand forecasting and timely access to ICU supplies.

Wastage due to expiry and mismanagement

Poor ICU inventory practices often lead to medication and supply wastage due to expiration. Contributing factors include poor stock rotation, rigid bulk purchasing, mismatched order cycles and minimal electronic tracking. A qualitative study in Ethiopia found that 23% of medicine wastage was preventable through first expired, first out (FEFO) stock rotation, better procurement coordination and trained staff.6 Due to pharmacy shortages, nurses without formal training often managed inventory, increasing handling errors. In Bangladesh, 80% of district hospitals lacked bin card systems and relied on manual records, conditions that were tied to frequent stockouts.7 To reduce expiries, hospitals should adopt classification models like Always Better Control (ABC), Vital-Essential-Desirable (VED) (criticality) and XYZ analysis (demand variability). When combined with automation and analytics, these tools align supply with real-time demand. In Indonesia, a digitised model using probabilistic expiry control cut expired stock by 44%.8

Additional inventory management models in healthcare

While JIT offers lean, cost-effective stock control, it may not suit all ICU contexts. Other models include VED for clinical priority, ABC for monetary value, Fast-moving, Slow-moving and Non-moving (FSN) for usage rate, Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) for optimal order size and the Two-Bin system for simple visual reordering. Using these individually or in hybrid form enables planning based on criticality, cost and consumption trends (see online supplemental table S1). Hospitals should tailor strategies to operational needs, ensuring both preparedness and efficiency.16

Inadequate institutional support and resource allocation in intensive care units

Inefficient ICU inventory management stems from workforce shortages, fragmented procurement and poor coordination between administrative and clinical teams, leading to shortages, care delays and staff strain. During COVID-19, resource gaps caused widespread stockouts, exhausted personnel and fragmented oversight. Globally, 47% of frontline workers reported anxiety, with burnout and depression exceeding 35%, driven by inadequate PPE, disorganised logistics and heavy workloads.9 Nurses balancing patient care with inventory duties faced frequent inconsistencies and delays.10 In the UK National Health Service (NHS), centralised procurement with real-time monitoring reduced order delays by 35% and improved ICU supply availability by 42%.11 Solutions include automated procurement, dedicated supply chain staff and stronger cross-department coordination to stabilise inventories and safeguard staff well-being.

Insufficient staff training and compliance

Lack of formal inventory management training among ICU staff—including nurses, orderlies and administrative personnel—remains a major barrier to effective stock control. In a South African regional hospital, only 12 ICU-trained nurses were available, forcing untrained staff to manage inventory and high-acuity care, which led to unsafe practices such as reusing suction catheters and delaying medication restocking.17 Ethiopian hospitals similarly assigned nurses to stockrooms due to pharmacy shortages, often without training in first in, first out (FIFO), FEFO or VED methods.6 In Bangladesh, 83% of inventory staff lacked formal training, and all surveyed facilities relied on manual records; non-compliance with ICU inventory protocols reached 45%.7 18 Addressing these gaps requires structured, ICU-specific training, possibly using Crew Resource Management to improve compliance, along with digital modules on RFID, FIFO and predictive forecasting to reduce preventable disruptions.

Procurement inefficiencies and non-standardised policies

Fragmented and inconsistent procurement in ICUs leads to inefficiency, waste and delays. Without standardised protocols, hospitals face duplicate purchases, incompatible equipment and slow delivery of critical supplies. A review of 12 high-income countries found that high-cost equipment decisions often prioritised cost over clinical value, lacked clear stakeholder roles and were made in silos.19 Within hospital networks, differing ICU equipment preferences delayed integration and disrupted care.20 Standardised purchasing agreements, centralised systems and hospital-based health technology assessments can improve reliability and alignment. In one case, centralised procurement with barcode tracking and improved logistics eliminated 3000 weekly stockouts and increased first-fill rates from 85% to 99%.21 During COVID-19, rigid centralised systems proved vulnerable, while private hospitals using emergency task forces, diversified suppliers and direct engagement with manufacturers managed shortages more effectively.22 Crises also led to inflated pricing, procurement fraud and vendor favouritism.1 Flexible, transparent and ethically grounded procurement—supported by real-time data and compliance safeguards—is crucial to maintaining ICU supply continuity during disruptions.

Balancing surplus and shortage in the intensive care unit

Maintaining the right balance between surplus and shortage in ICU inventory management is crucial for ensuring continuous patient care while keeping operational costs down. Overstocking can cause storage issues, higher holding costs and an increased risk of medication and supply expiration. On the other hand, shortages can delay urgent treatments and raise patient morbidity and mortality risks. One study identified poor demand estimation and fragmented supply chain systems as key factors contributing to both overstocking and stockouts in hospitals.23 A hybrid inventory model that combines just-in-time (JIT) principles with emergency buffer stock strategies and AI-based demand forecasting was recommended to enhance supply chain flexibility and minimise disruptions. By using predictive analytics, sourcing from multiple vendors and maintaining contingency stockpiles, hospitals can create resilient ICU inventory systems that ensure reliable access to vital medical supplies—even during demand spikes or emergencies.

Evidence-based strategies for optimising intensive care unit stock management

To enhance ICU stock management, hospitals must implement evidence-based strategies that improve inventory accuracy, minimise waste and strengthen supply chain resilience.

Implementing automated inventory tracking systems

Manual ICU inventory management is prone to errors, miscalculations and delays, causing shortages or excess stock. Automated systems such as RFID and barcode tracking provide real-time visibility, replenishment alerts and improved accuracy. Hospitals using computerised systems reported significant gains in stock accuracy and efficiency; in one case, point-of-use misreporting caused over $500 000 in supply losses and $2 million in unbilled services.5 Linking digital inventory with EHRs aligns stock levels to patient treatment patterns, ensuring timely access to critical supplies while reducing overstock and expiry risks.

Leverage artificial intelligence-powered demand forecasting

ICU inventory forecasting is challenging due to fluctuating patient volumes, seasonal illness patterns and sudden emergencies. Traditional methods often fail to predict rapid shifts, causing shortages or wasteful overstocking. Integrating AI and machine learning into hospital inventory systems enables dynamic, accurate forecasts by analysing historical use, patient flow and real-time data. Hospitals using AI-driven models saw a 30% drop in shortages and a 28% improvement in turnover rates.24 Embedding AI in enterprise resource planning systems also improved reorder timing and crisis responsiveness.2 By reducing reliance on manual estimates, AI-based forecasting supports leaner inventories while ensuring critical supplies are available when needed.

Standardise procurement policies and centralise supply

Procurement inefficiencies and fragmented supplier contracts cause inconsistent stock, delivery delays and inflated costs in ICUs. Standardising procurement policies improves transparency, cost control and supply continuity. Many hospitals rely on decentralised systems, leading to mismatched stock, poor oversight and non-compliance.19 Structured protocols, digital ordering and regulatory alignment can streamline logistics; facilities adopting these measures reported fewer delays, better visibility and stronger compliance. In Saudi Arabia, formal procurement frameworks shortened lead times and improved vendor reliability,25 while in the UK NHS, centralised procurement with real-time monitoring raised ICU supply availability by 42% and cut fulfilment delays by 35%.11 Strengthening resilience requires long-term vendor contracts, automated purchasing and centralised decision-making for high-value or critical items.

Implement just-in-time inventory models

Traditional hospital inventory systems often lead to excess stock, limited storage and waste from expirations. The JIT approach aligns replenishment with real-time consumption, reducing overstock and shortages. US hospitals using JIT saved $3–$11 million annually by lowering storage needs and improving turnover.23 Automated restocking alerts further enhance efficiency, space use and responsiveness. However, COVID-19 exposed JIT’s limits, as sudden ICU demand surges caused severe shortages of ventilators and PPE.23 A hybrid model—JIT for general consumables with JIC reserves for critical items—is recommended. Simulation-based modelling and predictive analytics can refine forecasts and reduce reliance on distributor-driven restocking,21 enabling routine efficiency while maintaining resilience during crises.

Establishing an emergency buffer stock for critical supplies

While JIT improves efficiency, it leaves hospitals vulnerable in crises. Without buffer stocks, ICU shortages can delay care and increase risk. Facilities holding 3–6 months of reserves for ventilators, PPE and medications maintained better continuity during COVID-19,26 while those relying on real-time procurement faced severe delays. Buffer planning should be data driven, factoring in usage trends, expiry and surge forecasts. During the pandemic, hospitals that bypassed standard channels, partnered directly with manufacturers or pooled supplies fared better.22 Institutionalising buffer policies with automated monitoring and vendor agreements can ensure uninterrupted ICU care.

Train intensive care unit staff in inventory best practices

ICU staff are central to inventory accuracy, yet many nurses and administrative personnel including lack training in procurement, digital systems and stock rotation, leading to discrepancies, mismanagement and delays. Structured training should cover classification systems such as FIFO and VED to prioritise critical items and minimise waste. Digital literacy in RFID, barcode scanning and automated monitoring reduces errors and supports real-time tracking. Ongoing education via e-learning, simulations and competency checks should also address ethical procurement, including contract compliance and fraud prevention, especially during crises.1 Building both technical and ethical skills strengthens transparency, reduces risk and fosters accountability in ICUs.

Adopt digital twin technology for the intensive care unit

Digital twin technology enables real-time simulation of ICU supply chains, allowing administrators to test procurement strategies, forecast shortages and model demand without disrupting operations. These virtual replicas help identify inefficiencies, optimise stock and allocate resources more precisely—essential in high-acuity settings. A bibliometric review reported rising use of digital twins in healthcare and noted they may improve forecasting accuracy and reduce inefficiencies by up to 20%.27 To maximise digital twin impact, hospitals should integrate live data—patient trends, supplier metrics and inventory turnover—to enhance predictive accuracy. When combined with AI forecasting, these platforms can stress-test supply chains, simulate crises and build contingency plans—bolstering ICU resilience. Adopting this technology reduces waste, improves responsiveness and ensures continuous access to life-saving supplies, even during demand surges.27

Use of blockchain for supply chain transparency

Supply chain transparency is essential in ICU inventory management, where ensuring the authenticity, traceability and accountability of critical medical supplies directly impacts patient safety. Blockchain technology provides a decentralised, tamper-proof ledger that records each transaction along a product’s lifecycle—from manufacturing to delivery and final use. This immutable system enhances visibility, enforces procurement integrity and mitigates the risk of counterfeit products, vendor fraud or distribution bias—particularly during emergency scenarios when rapid procurement may bypass traditional oversight mechanisms.

The role of blockchain in reducing unethical practices has been documented, with evidence showing that traceable, auditable records safeguard contract compliance and promote supplier accountability.1 By ensuring that only verified, high-quality supplies enter the ICU inventory, blockchain enhances operational transparency, reinforces institutional trust and protects patient outcomes—especially during crises when procurement agility and accountability are both paramount.

Optimize the warehouse and storage space for the intensive care unit

Optimised warehouse design and systematic stockroom organisation are critical for ensuring inventory accuracy, operational speed and patient safety in ICU environments. Disorganised or congested storage areas frequently lead to misplaced items, delayed retrieval and increased instances of expired stock—issues that can directly compromise the timeliness and quality of critical care. One multihospital study reported that hospitals that restructured their ICU storage spaces experienced a 40% reduction in supply retrieval times and a 32% decrease in inventory expiration rates, demonstrating the measurable benefits of efficient inventory layout.7

ICU warehouse strategies include zoning by urgency, bin cards for real-time tracking, colour-coded labels and FIFO stocking to ensure expiration compliance. Environmental controls—like temperature and humidity regulation—preserve sensitive items. When paired with digital platforms and routine audits, these improvements reduce waste, improve availability and streamline supply chains.

Develop strong supplier partnerships and multisourcing agreements

Relying on a single ICU supplier increases vulnerability during pandemics or trade disruptions, raising risks of procurement failure, cost spikes and delays. Multisourcing through contracts with multiple vetted suppliers diversifies risk; one cross-regional study found it cut procurement failures by 45% and maintained stock during major breakdowns.22 Strategic vendor partnerships also proved valuable in COVID-19, with hospitals holding pre-existing MedTech ties gaining priority access to ventilators and PPE. Providing logistical or procurement support further improved outcomes. Such ‘bridging strategies’ reduce volatility and build trust and should be formalised through performance-based contracts, audits and shared risk frameworks.

Building and testing resilience in intensive care unit supply chains

Beyond optimisation, ICU supply chains must be resilient—able to anticipate, absorb and recover from disruptions. Resilience testing evaluates performance under scenarios such as pandemics, supplier failures, surges and delays. Frameworks like Bhandal et al.27 highlighted the role of digital twin simulations and scenario-based planning in strengthening supply chain resilience and adaptability during disruptions. Digital twins, discussed earlier, allow virtual crisis modelling before real-world implementation. "Resilience can be strengthened through scenario-based procurement, multi-tier supplier reviews and early warning systems via real-time dashboards. Metrics such as Time to Recover and Time to Survive quantify readiness.28 Integrating these with AI-driven analytics supports proactive risk management. Cross-functional coordination among clinical, procurement and logistics teams is equally vital.

By stress-testing systems and planning for uncertainty, hospitals can safeguard uninterrupted ICU care—especially in crisis-prone environments. Additional models for sustainable and efficient intensive care unit inventory

In addition to the digital innovations discussed, several complementary frameworks can support sustainable ICU supply chains. The Efficient Supply Chain model emphasises lean inventory systems, supplier collaboration and waste reduction—particularly effective in stable, high-volume environments.12 However, in ICUs, this model must be adapted to account for clinical unpredictability by incorporating hybrid JIT-JIC principles.

The concept of a Green ICU aligns supply chain optimisation with environmental sustainability, promoting practices like digital inventory, minimal packaging, energy-efficient storage and ethical disposal of medical waste.13 This is especially relevant given the high material consumption in critical care settings.

Finally, Material Flow Analysis (MFA) provides a systems-level tool to map and optimise the flow of materials within hospital networks. By visualising input-output balances and identifying bottlenecks, MFA supports evidence-based decisions on sourcing, distribution and waste reduction.14 Combined with digital platforms, these models enhance both resilience and sustainability.

Future directions for intensive care unit supply chain sustainability and resilience

In addition to established models, several emerging approaches warrant consideration for future ICU supply chain design. Circular economy practices are increasingly applied in healthcare, focusing on safe reuse, recycling and remanufacturing of selected medical devices and consumables to reduce waste and strengthen sustainability.29 Similarly, agile supply chain models emphasise adaptability and rapid responsiveness over cost efficiency, allowing ICUs to adjust quickly to demand surges, such as pandemics or seasonal outbreaks.30 Another promising direction is localised manufacturing, particularly the use of three-dimensional printing to produce consumables, spare parts or even simple devices on demand. This reduces reliance on global suppliers, shortens lead times and enhances resilience during crises.31 Together, these approaches complement digital innovations and traditional strategies, offering a pathway toward more sustainable, flexible and future-ready ICU supply chains.

Conclusion

Optimising ICU inventory is central to patient safety, system resilience and healthcare sustainability. Technology-enabled tracking, AI forecasting, standardised procurement and staff training reduce waste, cut costs and ensure consistent access to critical supplies.

Tools like digital twins, blockchain and JIT models enhance visibility and agility. Buffer stock planning and multisourcing protect against disruptions from pandemics, geopolitical shifts or market volatility.

Realising these gains requires hospital investment in digital tools, cross-functional collaboration and ongoing workforce development. Barriers like regulatory complexity, implementation costs and procedural resistance demand visionary leadership and supportive policy.

The future of ICU inventory lies in predictive, data-driven and ethically governed systems. Embracing innovation today secures life-saving resources for tomorrow—ensuring no patient suffers from a preventable supply failure.
