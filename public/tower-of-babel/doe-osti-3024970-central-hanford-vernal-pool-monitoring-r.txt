 1.0 INTRODUCTION

This report summarizes vernal pool monitoring data collected in calendar year (CY) 2025 and
provides management recommendations. Monitoring efforts occurred on the portion of the
Hanford Site managed by the U.S. Department of Energy, Hanford Field Office (HFO), referred
to herein as Central Hanford. The goal of monitoring is to collect data to evaluate the
conservation status of this imperiled habitat. Monitoring in CY 2025 built on previous efforts, as
reported in HNF-62115, Ecological Monitoring Report Vernal Pools on the Hanford Site.

1.1 BACKGROUND

Vernal pools are shallow ephemeral wetlands that are filled with water during winter and spring,
primarily from rainfall and snowmelt, and typically drying out in the summer. Columbia Plateau
vernal pools are a distinct class of vernal pools recognized in Washington State throughout the
channeled scablands geographic region (NHR 2015-04, Ecological Systems of Washington State.
A Guide to Identification). Four Columbia Plateau vernal pool complexes occur at Central
Hanford: Vernita Grade, Gable Mountain, Gable Butte, and Blind Canyon (Figure 1-1). Each
cluster of pools is situated on top of an impermeable basalt layer that causes water to pool in
shallow depressions during wetter winter seasons. Other isolated small patch wetlands found at
Central Hanford receive vernal moisture and potential seasonal inundation but are not classified
as Columbia Plateau vernal pools and are not discussed in this report. Columbia Plateau vernal
pools are referred as vernal pools throughout this report.
Vernal pools at Central Hanford are highly variable with differences between and within
complexes and temporally throughout seasons. They show wide variations in hydrological
cycles, with some pools filling more frequently and other spanning years between inundations.
The vegetation composition of pools varies widely. Some pools exhibit high diversity of native
plants while others are highly degraded by invasive species. Vegetation composition varies
annually in response to seasonal weather patterns. The natural range of variability among vernal
pools at Central Hanford is not well understood, and this monitoring effort adds a valuable point
of comparison to previous surveys.
Vernal pools support unique assemblages of plants and animals. They are characterized by
concentric zonation of species from the bottom of the pool, which might be barren throughout
the growing season, to the upper pool edge, where facultative wetland species intergrade with
upland species. Vegetation is dominated by annual species, although perennial species
occasionally persist. A mix of upland and wetland associated species can be found throughout
the growing season. Vernal pools are highly diverse and associated with numerous rare plant
species at Central Hanford and regionally. The Central Hanford vernal pools support a regionally
unique plant assemblage not currently described by the United States National Vegetation
Classification System (USNVC 2025) and are distinct from other Columbia Plateau vernal pools
(Bjork and Dunwiddie 2004; Crowe et al. 1994; NHR 2009-11, Assessment of Ecological
Characteristics and Ecological Integrity of Wetlands in Northern Douglas Country,
Washington).
Regionally, Columbia Plateau vernal pools have experienced little direct loss but much
degradation from grazing and agricultural runoff. As such, they are ranked as imperiled (S2S3)
in Washington State (NHR 2015-04). Vernal pools at Central Hanford are not subject to these

threats but have undergone varying levels of degradation from invasive species and potential
upland encroachment. Altered fire regimes may threaten vernal pools, although the effects of fire

Botanical Terms

The term “rare plant” refers to any plant taxon with a state and/or federal conservation listing.
State conservation status is determined by the Washington Natural Heritage Program (WNHP)
(NHR 2024-07, 2024 Washington Vascular Plant Species). Factors considered in determining
state conservation status include abundance, occurrence patterns, vulnerability, threats, existing
protection, and taxonomic distinctness. State rankings include the following:
 • Endangered: In danger of becoming extinct or extirpated from Washington State.
 • Threatened: Likely to become endangered in Washington State.
 • Sensitive: Vulnerable or declining and could become threatened or endangered in
 Washington State.
 • Review List 1: Species for which more information is needed before a conservation
 status can be assigned.
 • Review List 2: Species with unresolved taxonomic questions.
Throughout this report, the terms “native,” “non-native,” “invasive,” “exotic,” and “noxious
weed” are used to describe plant species:
 • Native species are generally considered to be those that evolved in a particular region
 without substantial influence from human activity.
 • Non-native species are those that were relatively recently introduced to a particular
 region outside of their native range, often directly or indirectly as a result of human
 activity. Non-native is a catch all term that includes invasive, exotic, and noxious species.
 • Invasive refers to non-native species with potential to grow aggressively, causing
 ecological or economic harm in their introduced range.
 • Exotic species are non-native species with little to no potential to cause harm in their
 introduced range.
 • Noxious weed is a legal designation given to some non-native species with requirements
 for control at the state level by the Washington State Noxious Weed Control Board
       (NWCB 2021).
The native, invasive, or exotic status is assigned by the WA Invasive Ranking System (WA DNR
2025). Noxious weed lists in many states were developed with a bias towards agricultural weeds,
which has resulted in listing of some species that do not greatly threaten natural areas while
neglecting some invasive plants potentially harmful to natural ecological systems (Quinn et al.
2013); therefore, it is useful to distinguish these categories of non-native species. The taxonomy
used in this report follows plant names found in Flora of the Pacific Northwest: An Illustrated
Manual (Hitchcock 2018), unless updated taxonomy is available from another source.
The term “occurrence” is used throughout this report, referring to spatially distinct locations
where rare plants exist currently or historically. The term refers to rare plant sites that are
currently part of the WNHP rare plant database and to newly recorded rare plant locations that
were submitted for inclusion in the WNHP rare plant database.

1.2 PURPOSE

The purpose of this report is to provide results from the CY 2025 ecological monitoring
conducted at Central Hanford to collect data to ensure compliance with an array of
environmental laws, regulations, and policies governing HFO activities. Under HFO stewardship
on the Hanford Site, ecological monitoring data provides baseline information about the plants,
animals, and habitat, which is required for accurate ecological impact assessment decision
making under the National Environmental Policy Act of 1969 and the Comprehensive
Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act of 1980. In addition, ecological
monitoring helps ensure that HFO, its prime contractors, and other entities conducting activities
on the Hanford Site are in compliance with the Hanford Site Comprehensive Land Use Plan
(DOE/EIS-0222-F). HFO places priority on monitoring plant and animal species or habitats that
have specific regulatory protections or requirements; that are rare and/or declining (federal or
state listed endangered, threatened, or sensitive species); or are of significant interest to federal,
state, or tribal governments or to the public.
The Hanford Site Biological Resources Management Plan (DOE/RL-96-32) is identified by
DOE/EIS-0222-F as the primary implementation control for managing and protecting natural
resources on the Hanford Site. DOE/RL-96-32 provides a mechanism for assuring compliance
with laws protecting biological resources; provides a framework for ensuring that appropriate
biological resource goals, objectives, and tools are in place to ensure HFO remains an effective
steward of the Hanford biological resources; and implements an ecosystem management
approach for onsite biological resources. DOE/RL-96-32 provides a comprehensive direction
that specifies DOE biological resource policies, goals, and objectives.
DOE/RL-96-32 ranks biological resources (Levels 0 to 5), providing a graded approach to
monitoring biological resources based on the level of concern for each resource. Within this
framework, vernal pools are classified as an Irreplaceable Resource (Level 5). A high level of
status monitoring is required for Level 5 resources. HNF-62115 recommends continued
monitoring through years with variable seasonal weather patterns to assess the status of vernal
pools and to increase understanding of the natural range of variation of the pools. The CY 2025
season represented a limited opportunity to monitor above average precipitation conditions.
Characterizing vernal pools on years with above-average precipitation is necessary for meeting
the goals of evaluating vernal pool status and understanding seasonal dynamics.

 2.0 MONITORING METHODS

Surveys targeted all known Columbia Plateau vernal pools to inventory vegetation, characterize
vegetation composition, and evaluate the relative health of vernal pools.
During CY 2025, multiple surveys were conducted throughout the spring and summer to align
with different stages of phenology at the vernal pools. Surveys of all known pools were
conducted in March, May, and June. During each visit, vegetation was inventoried to build a
species list for each pool. A qualitative characterization of each pool was made during each visit,
including notes about hydrology and the extent of the pools. Existing photo points from previous
monitoring efforts were revisited during each visit, and additional photos were taken at some
pools. Rare plants were documented and reported in the Hanford Site Rare Plant Monitoring
Report for Calendar Year 2025 (HNF-72997). Noxious weeds were documented and reported to
the Hanford noxious weed control program.
Vegetation composition was evaluated at a subset of vernal pools in June, when most plant
species are mature. The methods for measuring vegetation composition were rapid and
semi-quantitative, intended to provide a basis for comparison between pools. All vernal pools
that showed signs of inundation and with wetland associated vegetation were monitored. Areas
that had previously been mapped as vernal pools but showed no signs of inundation or wetland
associated vegetation were not monitored. Vegetation cover was estimated visually for each
plant species within the vernal pool area.
The inundated area was smaller for most pools than the areas mapped in CY 2017. Vegetation
was estimated within the current vernally moist area and not estimated to the full extent that was
previously mapped if no wetland associated vegetation was present. Much of the area that was
previously mapped as vernal pools showed no signs of inundation during surveys in March and
contained exclusively upland associated plant species. Species cover was estimated using cover
classes and the mid-point of each cover class was recorded (Table 2-1). Canopy cover is defined
in Daubenmire (1970) as, “the percentage of ground surface included in the vertical projection of
a polygon drawn around the extremities of undisturbed foliage of a plant.” The percentage cover
of bare ground was also estimated.

 3.0 RESULTS

This section summarizes results from vernal pool monitoring in CY 2025. Preliminary surveys

3.1 VERNITA GRADE VERNAL POOL COMPLEX

The Vernita Grade Vernal Pool complex, also known as the Umtanum Vernal Pool complex, is
located on the eastern end of Umtanum Ridge (Figure 3-1). The complex consists of 11 isolated
pools. This system of pools has interconnected runoff channels. An alkaline spring is located
within the complex.

Table 3-5 lists the species recorded at the Central Hanford vernal pools across all years and

vernal pools and wet habitats for each species:
The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers assigns the National Wetland Plant List (UACE 2023) for
the Arid West Region and defines the following wetland indicator status ratings:
 • Obligate (OBL): Almost always occur in wetlands
 • Facultative Wetland (FACW): Usually occurs in wetlands, but may occur in non-
 wetlands
 • Facultative (FAC): Occurs in wetlands and non-wetlands
 • Facultative Upland (FACU): Usually occurs in non-wetlands, but may occur in
 wetlands
 • Upland (UPL): Almost never occurs in wetlands.

 4.0 DISCUSSION

To evaluate the current health and long-term conservation status of Central Hanford’s vernal
pools, it is first necessary to characterize the natural range of variation and establish a long-term
dataset to evaluate trends. This monitoring effort was the third across a 9-year span to collect
vegetation data, building the foundation of a long-term dataset. To characterize the natural range
of variation within vernal pools, different comparisons were made between pools and across
years, which is discussed below.

Vegetation

Of the 27 pools present at Central Hanford, vegetation composition was surveyed at 17 pools in
CY 2025. Pools without vernal pool characteristics or wetland associated vegetation were not
surveyed. Vegetation composition is shown in Table 4-1. Vegetation composition was variable
between pools. Pools ranged from sparsely to highly vegetated and native and non-native cover
varied greatly between pools. There were no clear patterns between the 4 vernal pool complexes
in terms of vegetation cover.

Regional Context

Comparisons of vernal pools throughout the Columbia Plateau region show that Central
Hanford’s pools have a regionally distinctive flora. Columbia Plateau Vernal pools are found
throughout the channeled scablands geographic region across the Columbia Plateau, and several
vegetation community types have been classified (NHR 2015-04); however, none of the plant
associations found at Central Hanford’s vernal pools are described in the current classifications.
Several studies on vegetation composition from nearby Columbia Plateau vernal pools show
distinct differences. The Central Hanford vernal pools share only 15 of the 47 species reported
from the Marcellus vernal pools just 70 miles to the east (Crowe et al. 1994). They differ also
from the vernal pools north of the Hanford Site in Douglas County, which typically have saline
soils (NHR 2009-11). Few halophytes are present at the Central Hanford vernal pools, and the
only observable salt crust is at the spring near the Vernita Grade Vernal Pool complex. The
Central Hanford vernal pools are the only pools in Washington State to host occurrences of the
rare plant spreading pygmy-leaf, a species disjunct from the southern United States. Continued
study of the Central Hanford vernal pools is needed to fully understand their regional
significance.

Threats

Without a long-term dataset, it is difficult to untangle the effect of seasonal variation from the
effects of vernal pool degradation. The high cover of invasive species within some vernal pools
is evidence of degradation, but without a natural baseline for comparison, it is unclear to what
degree invasive species are impacting native vegetation and the vernal pool ecosystem.
Cheatgrass is known to have an aridifying effect, which is likely to reduce available soil moisture
for native vernal pool species. The annual thatch layer produced by cheatgrass is substantial and
may be contributing to soil development within vernal pools and changes in nutrient cycling.
Over time, the yearly buildup of cheatgrass thatch may be contributing to shallowing of vernal
pools and facilitating upland encroachment. Tumbleweed accumulations have been observed in
vernal pools, which may have similar effects.
Various noxious weeds are also present and increasing in some pools, which have the potential to
dominate vernal pools and out-compete native vegetation.
Altered fire regimes have had adverse effects on native vegetation in Western North America,
but the impacts they may have on vernal pools are unknown. The Gable Mountain vernal pools
burned in the 2020 Gable Mountain fire, yet they appear largely unchanged from before the fire.
Lastly, a potential threat facing vernal pools may be elk over-use. The elk population at Central
Hanford has been steadily increasing, and elk are frequent visitors to vernal pools (HNF-71433,
Evaluating Movement Patterns of the Rattlesnake Hills Elk Herd on Hanford for Calendar Years
2019-2024). The effects of elk use on vernal pools are not clear, but elk over-use may degrade
the pools. Herbivory and vegetation trampling may degrade the vernal pools or benefit the pools,
and it may vary situationally. Unauthorized off-road driving poses a similar threat, although this
has only been observed at one vernal pool.

Climate and Weather

Seasonal weather patterns, including rainfall, snowfall, and temperatures, all determine the
formation of vernal pools, subsequently influencing the vegetation composition of the pools.
Duration of standing water and soil moisture potential both influence the presence and
distribution of plants in vernal pools (Zedler 1987; Crowe 1994).
The influence of cumulative precipitation on vernal pools may be the easiest to understand,
because vernal pool formation and extent are clearly correlated with average precipitation. The
vernal pools were first mapped in CY 2017, when precipitation was at its highest level since
CY 1997. The areas mapped in CY 2017 represent the extent of the vernal pools on a
high-precipitation year, but they likely do not represent the maximum extent under extreme
precipitation levels, like those of CY 1997.
The maps from CY 2017 were used as the basis for locating vernal pools in CY 2025.
Precipitation was above average in CY 2025, but lower than in CY 2017, and standing water was
observed in seven of 27 vernal pools in March 2025. An additional 10 of 27 pools showed signs
of vernal moisture and another 10 of 27 pools had no characteristics of a vernal pool.
Precipitation was near average in CY 2018, and no standing water was observed in vernal pools.
It remains possible that additional areas that showed no vernal pool characteristics in CY 2017
could be detected in an extreme precipitation year, such as CY 1997.

Climate Impacts

In general, anthropogenic climate change is expected to adversely affect many native plant
species (Snover et al. 2013). The potential impacts of climate change on vernal pools or vernal
pool flora at Central Hanford are not well understood. The Pacific Northwest has undergone
1.3°F of warming over the past century, which has resulted in changes to the water cycling and
seasonal temperatures. Future climate change is expected to cause increases in average annual
temperature, expansion of the frost-free season (growing season), increases in extreme
precipitation events, and a reduction in snowpack, further altering water cycling (Snover et al.
2013). Continued monitoring of vernal pools at Central Hanford is essential for determining
conservation strategies to address predicted climate outcomes.

 5.0 MANAGEMENT RECOMMENDATIONS

A consistent and thorough monitoring program is essential for documenting the conservation
status of vernal pools and managing these irreplaceable resources accordingly at Central
Hanford. This report highlights the value of a robust vernal pool monitoring program, and the
results of this study increase baseline knowledge of vernal pools at Central Hanford.
In addition to baseline vernal pool monitoring, further study of habitat loss from cheatgrass and
other invasive species is recommended. Cheatgrass encroachment is a major threat to vernal pool
habitats across Central Hanford. Tracking vegetation composition trends informs management
strategies to address this threat, and developing a long-term dataset is necessary to inform those
strategies in response to this threat.
Management of noxious weeds at and surrounding vernal pools is recommended. The specific
interactions between noxious weeds and vernal pool habitats is not well understood. Noxious
weeds are known to be aggressive and harmful competitors, presumably threatening native
species. Noxious weed infestations encountered during vernal pool monitoring were reported to
the Hanford noxious weed control program. Several noxious weed infestations were identified as
urgent threats to vernal pool habits. Infestations of noxious weeds were recorded at each vernal
pool complex. These noxious weeds have high potential to spread to other areas, further
degrading the quality of habitats and impacting native species.

Management Recommendations Summary

 • Conduct routine monitoring at all vernal pool complexes, particularly on years with
 above-average precipitation.
 • Continued surveys for potentially rare animals at the vernal pools such as fairy shrimp
 and spadefoot toads.
 • Develop targeted studies investigating upland encroachment and the long-term
 conservation outlook for vernal pools.
 • Implement a high level of status monitoring for rare plants at vernal pools.
 • Treat noxious weeds at vernal pools.
