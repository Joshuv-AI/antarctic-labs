Dentinal tubule penetration of a bioceramic sealer after different irrigant and activation regimens: An in vitro confocal laser scanning microscopy study
Authors: Nalluri Nikhita, TBVG Raju, Krishna Prasad Parvathaneni, Abitha Seshadri, Gowtam Dev Dondapati, Shikha Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Proper sealer adaptation relies on effective smear layer removal and adequate irrigant penetration. Intratubular entry of sealers is influenced by both the irrigant used and the method of activation. Nevertheless, evidence regarding the impact of newer chelators and activation strategies on bioceramic sealer penetration remains limited.Aim:This study aimed to assess and compare how various irrigating solutions and activation techniques influence the extent to which a bioceramic sealer penetrates the dentinal tubules, as observed through confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM).Materials and Methods:Sixty-three single-rooted teeth were prepared to F3 and randomly assigned to three activation groups (n = 21): sonic, diode laser, and passive ultrasonic. Each group was subdivided by the final irrigant – 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 1-Hydroxyethylidene-1,1-Diphosphonic Acid (HEDP), or 0.2% chitosan (n = 7). After placement of Rhodamine B-labeled bioceramic sealer, apical sections were analyzed using CLSM.Statistical Analysis:Data were examined with one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s post hoc test at a significance level of P < 0.05.Results:Diode laser activation demonstrated significantly greater sealer penetration than sonic and passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI) techniques (P < 0.001). Among the irrigants, EDTA exhibited the highest sealer penetration, particularly when combined with diode laser activation. HEDP and chitosan showed comparatively lower penetration values in most groups; however, under PUI, HEDP demonstrated the greatest sealer penetration compared with the other irrigants.Conclusion:Both irrigant type and activation method significantly influenced bioceramic sealer penetration. Diode laser activation was the most effective, while EDTA remained superior to HEDP and chitosan according to this study.

INTRODUCTION

Successful root canal treatment depends on effective cleaning, disinfection, and three-dimensional obturation.[1] Instrumentation produces a smear layer composed of organic and inorganic debris that can block dentinal tubules and hinder sealer adaptation.[2] Its presence limits irrigant penetration and compromises sealing ability, potentially affecting treatment outcomes.[3]

Sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) is widely used for its antimicrobial and tissue-dissolving properties but cannot remove the inorganic component of the smear layer, especially in the apical region.[45] Therefore, chelating agents such as 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) are used as final irrigants.[6] However, EDTA may cause dentinal erosion, cytotoxicity, and reduced microhardness, prompting the search for alternatives.[7]

1-Hydroxyethylidene-1,1-Diphosphonic Acid (HEDP) is a mild chelator that can be combined with NaOCl, allowing continuous chelation without reducing antimicrobial efficacy.[8] It produces fewer alterations in dentin compared to EDTA, although its effect on sealer penetration is not well established.[9]

Chitosan, a natural polysaccharide, has gained attention due to its biocompatibility, antimicrobial action, and chelating ability.[1011] While it improves smear layer removal and sealer bonding, its influence on sealer penetration, particularly with different activation methods, remains unclear.[11]

Conventional syringe irrigation has a limited ability to reach complex canal anatomy.[12] Activation methods such as sonic and passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI) enhance irrigant distribution through acoustic streaming and cavitation.[1314] Diode lasers have also been introduced, improving irrigation via photothermal and photoacoustic effects.[15]

Sealer penetration into dentinal tubules is an important indicator of smear layer removal and sealing quality.[16] Laser-activated irrigation has been shown to enhance sealer penetration and bonding.[17]

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) enables accurate three-dimensional evaluation of sealer penetration without damaging the specimen.[18] Dureja et al. reported improved wettability of CeraSeal with HEDP.[19]

Previous studies have independently evaluated chelating agents or activation techniques in relation to dentinal tubule penetration of sealers. However, limited evidence exists regarding the comparative effect of EDTA, HEDP, and chitosan when combined with different activation systems on the dentinal tubule penetration of bioceramic sealers.

Therefore, the novelty of the present study lies in the combined evaluation of multiple chelating agents with different irrigant activation techniques and their influence on dentinal tubule penetration of a bioceramic sealer using CLSM.

Aim

The present research sought to determine and contrast the contribution of multiple irrigating regimens and activation strategies on intratubular infiltration of a bioceramic sealer assessed by means of CLSM analysis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (SSDC/IEC/2026/5/S9). As an in vitro study using extracted teeth, informed consent was not required.

Sample size was determined using G*Power(v3.1.9.7; Dusseldorf, Germany) with one-way ANOVA (effect size 0.54, α = 0.05, power = 80%), yielding 63 samples (n = 7/group).

Sixty-three single-rooted extracted teeth were selected. Teeth with fractures, resorption, caries, calcifications, or prior endodontic treatment were excluded. Samples were cleaned and stored in 0.1% thymol until use.

Specimen preparation

Teeth were decoronated at the CEJ under water cooling to standardize length. Working length was set 1 mm short of the apex using a size 10 K-file. Canals were prepared to F3 with ProTaper Universal, with 3% sodium hypochlorite used between instruments.

Grouping of samples

Following preparation, the specimens were assigned at random to three main categories (n = 21) based on the irrigation regimen applied.

Irrigation and activation protocols

Irrigants were delivered into all canals using a syringe–needle system, followed by the respective activation method. In the sonic group (EndoActivator, Dentsply Sirona, USA), the tip was placed 1 mm short of working length and activated for 30 s as per manufacturer instructions.

PUI (Endo 3, Woodpecker, China) was performed using a size 25 tip vibrating freely for 30 s without contacting canal walls.

Diode laser activation (ZOLAR Photon, Canada) was carried out at 970 ± 10 nm, 2 W in continuous mode, using a 200 μm fiber tip positioned 1 mm short of working length for 20 s. Canals were then flushed with saline and dried with sterile paper points.

Sealer placement and storage

The bioceramic sealer (CeraSeal) was mixed thoroughly to obtain a homogeneous mixture with Rhodamine B dye in an approximate ratio of 0.1% (wt) and placed using lentulo spirals for uniform coating. Obturation was then performed, after which the excess gutta-percha at the canal orifice was removed and the remaining material was vertically condensed using a plugger. Access cavities were temporarily sealed. Samples were stored at 37°C in moist conditions for 1 week for complete setting.

Sectioning and confocal laser scanning microscopy

Once the material had fully set, each root was sectioned horizontally in the apical region at a point 3 mm from the apex to obtain slices of nearly 1 mm thickness. The specimens were then evaluated under CLSM to determine the extent of sealer penetration within the dentinal tubules. Visualization of the Rhodamine B-labeled material was performed using suitable excitation and emission parameters.

Measurement of sealer penetration

ImageJ software (Bethesda, MD, USA) was used to measure the linear span between the canal margin and the farthest extent of fluorescent sealer penetration. Each specimen was evaluated by one calibrated examiner, and the procedure was repeated twice to verify consistency. For analysis, the maximum value obtained from each section was recorded [Figure 1].

Statistical analysis

All numerical data were processed with SPSS statistical software (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). Differences among and within the experimental groups were examined through one-way ANOVA, after which Tukey’s post hoc procedure was applied for multiple pairwise evaluations. Significance was defined as a probability value of <0.05.

RESULTS

All 63 samples were available for evaluation, and no specimens were lost during preparation or analysis. Comparison among the different activation techniques revealed the groups exhibited a pronounced disparity in mean penetration depth, reaching high statistical significance (P < 0.001). The diode laser group showed the greatest mean penetration (1142.23 ± 324.21 μm). In contrast, the sonic (757.63 ± 232.29 μm) and PUI groups (755.20 ± 373.73 μm) exhibited lower and nearly comparable values [Table 1 and Graph 1].

CLSM evaluation demonstrated noticeable differences in the depth, pattern, and distribution of sealer penetration among the experimental groups. Specimens treated with diode laser activation showed more extensive and uniform penetration of the bioceramic sealer into the dentinal tubules, with the most prominent fluorescence observed in the EDTA subgroup. Sonic activation exhibited moderate sealer penetration with comparatively less uniform distribution, while PUI demonstrated variable penetration patterns depending on the irrigant used. Among the irrigants, EDTA generally produced deeper and more consistent tubular penetration, whereas HEDP and chitosan showed relatively lesser penetration in most groups. The CLSM images also revealed differences in the continuity and intensity of fluorescence along the canal walls, indicating variations in sealer adaptation and tubular infiltration among the tested groups.

Evaluation of the various combinations of irrigants and activation techniques demonstrated highly significant variation among the subgroups (P < 0.001). The pairing of EDTA with diode laser activation resulted in the highest average penetration depth (1502.86 ± 148.00 μm).

Under sonic activation, EDTA yielded the highest penetration values (1068.06 ± 282.46 μm), whereas HEDP resulted in the lowest (312.08 ± 97.68 μm). In the diode laser group, EDTA again showed superior penetration, followed by HEDP (993.44 ± 95.93 μm) and chitosan (930.42 ± 298.95 μm). Within the PUI groups, the highest penetration was recorded with HEDP (935.57 ± 165.50 μm), followed by chitosan (743.04 ± 263.02 μm) and EDTA (594.30 ± 122.38 μm) [Table 2 and Graph 2].

DISCUSSION

Effective smear layer removal is essential for facilitating sealer penetration into dentinal tubules, thereby improving adaptation and the quality of the apical seal.[13] The extent of intratubular penetration is commonly considered an indirect indicator of smear layer removal, especially in the apical region, where irrigant exchange is limited.

The superior performance of diode laser activation observed in this study may be attributed to laser-induced thermal and acoustic effects that enhance irrigant agitation through vapor bubble formation and collapse, thereby promoting deeper penetration into dentinal tubules. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies on irrigant activation techniques.[13]

However, these results contrast with those of Awati et al., who reported greater sealer penetration with PUI.[14] Such discrepancies may be explained by variations in methodology, including differences in irrigants, sealer types, activation duration, and laser parameters, all of which influence irrigant dynamics and smear layer removal.

Among the irrigants evaluated, EDTA exhibited the highest sealer penetration, particularly when combined with diode laser activation. Its strong chelating action facilitates removal of the inorganic component of the smear layer, increasing dentinal tubule patency and allowing deeper sealer diffusion.[23] These findings are consistent with previous studies using confocal and electron microscopy techniques.[2]

Despite its effectiveness, EDTA is associated with drawbacks such as dentinal erosion and reduction in microhardness, which may compromise tooth structure.[67] This has led to increased interest in alternative chelating agents.

The moderate sealer penetration with HEDP may be due to its weaker chelating ability compared to EDTA. It causes gradual and less aggressive demineralization, resulting in limited smear layer removal and reduced dentinal tubule exposure.[8] Although its compatibility with sodium hypochlorite allows continuous chelation without affecting antimicrobial action,[8] this may reduce its overall effectiveness. Studies have shown that HEDP causes less dentinal erosion than EDTA,[9] which, while preserving dentin, may also restrict sealer penetration. Improved results with passive ultrasonic activation may be attributed to enhanced irrigant movement compensating for its lower chelating strength.[45]

Chitosan showed variable penetration, likely due to its milder chelating action. It removes the smear layer through interaction with calcium ions but is less effective than EDTA in exposing dentinal tubules.[1011] Its tendency to form a bioadhesive layer on dentin may enhance bonding but can limit deeper sealer penetration.[11] In addition, its performance depends on factors such as concentration and application time.[12] Enhanced penetration with diode activation may result from improved irrigant agitation.

The findings of this study differ from those of Morekar et al., who reported superior performance of PUI over diode laser activation.[20] These differences may be attributed to variations in fluid dynamics, as diode laser activation may produce limited lateral irrigant movement compared to ultrasonic techniques.

Intragroup analysis revealed significant differences among irrigants within each activation method. EDTA consistently showed higher penetration in sonic and diode groups, whereas HEDP performed better with passive ultrasonic activation. These findings support the concept that the effectiveness of chelating agents is influenced by the activation method, with ultrasonic agitation enhancing irrigant distribution through acoustic streaming.[45]

CLSM was employed due to its ability to provide accurate, three-dimensional visualization of sealer penetration without altering specimen structure. Compared to scanning electron microscopy, it allows repeated evaluation without dehydration artifacts.

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, diode laser activation in combination with EDTA may be clinically recommended to enhance dentinal tubule penetration of bioceramic sealers and potentially improve the quality of root canal obturation. HEDP may serve as a suitable alternative chelating agent when used with PUI due to its favorable penetration pattern and comparatively conservative effect on dentin. Selection of the irrigant and activation technique should therefore be based on the desired balance between effective smear layer removal, sealer penetration, and preservation of dentin structure.

This study has certain limitations. Being an in vitro investigation, it does not fully replicate clinical conditions. In addition, only the apical third was evaluated, and results may differ in other regions of the canal. Further studies incorporating different concentrations, activation protocols, and clinical models are necessary to validate these findings.

CONCLUSION

Diode laser activation demonstrated superior dentinal tubule penetration of the bioceramic sealer compared with sonic and PUI techniques. Among the chelating agents evaluated, 17% EDTA showed the highest penetration, especially when combined with diode laser activation, whereas HEDP and chitosan exhibited comparatively lower penetration values. HEDP demonstrated better performance with PUI.

The study findings suggest that both the type of chelating agent and the method of irrigant activation significantly affect dentinal tubule penetration of bioceramic sealers. Further in vivo studies are needed to confirm the clinical relevance of these findings
