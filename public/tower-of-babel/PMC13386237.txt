Triiodothyroacetic acid exerts tissue-specific thyromimetic effects without tachycardia and hyperthermia in male mice
Authors: Beate Herrmann, Lena Adam, Rebecca Oelkrug, Jens Mittag
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThyroid hormones regulate cardiovascular functions and energy homeostasis, including thermogenesis, through binding to nuclear thyroid hormone receptors. The thyroid hormone analog 3,3′,5-triiodothyroacetic acid (TRIAC) has recently emerged as a therapeutic candidate for thyroid hormone transporter deficiencies (Allan–Herndon–Dudley syndrome) or thyroid hormone resistance β. Although TRIAC shows promise for these conditions, a comprehensive characterization focusing on heart and body temperature regulation has not been systematically performed in mice.MethodsAs this knowledge is critical for determining its safety profile and therapeutic advantages over conventional thyroid hormone therapy, we administered TRIAC to wild-type mice for 14 days and assessed molecular (real-time PCR, western blot, and immunoassays) and physiological (infrared thermography and blood pressure) responses.ResultsTRIAC treatment reduced circulating thyroxine levels and induced robust upregulation of hepatic thyroid hormone-responsive genes, including Dio1 and Me1, demonstrating classical thyromimetic activity. However, cardiovascular analysis revealed normal blood pressure and heart rate, and there were no signs of cardiac hypertrophy despite specific molecular changes in cardiac gene expression. At the metabolic level, we surprisingly did not observe any effect on brown fat thermogenesis or body temperature, while in muscle, Ucp3 and Gpd2 were reduced.ConclusionTaken together, our comparative in vivo analysis demonstrates variable TRIAC responsiveness across tissues. Under defined conditions, the drug produces defined metabolic responses while avoiding classical hyperthyroid manifestations, such as tachycardia or hyperthermia, thus supporting its potential therapeutic use in thyroid hormone resistance conditions.

Introduction

Thyroid hormones, thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), are essential regulators of metabolism, development, and physiological homeostasis across almost all organ systems (1, 2). These hormones exert their biological effects predominantly through binding to nuclear thyroid hormone receptors (TRα1 and TRβ), which function as ligand-activated transcription factors to regulate gene expression in target tissues (2). The transport of thyroid hormones across cellular membranes is facilitated by specific transporter proteins, among which monocarboxylate transporter 8 (MCT8) plays a crucial role in mediating thyroid hormone uptake and efflux, particularly in the central nervous system (3).

Mutations in the SLC16A2 gene encoding MCT8 result in Allan–Herndon–Dudley syndrome (AHDS), a devastating X-linked disorder characterized by severe intellectual disability, profound motor impairments, and distinctive thyroid function abnormalities (4). Patients typically present with elevated serum T3 levels, low or normal T4 concentrations, and normal thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), creating a paradoxical situation of peripheral thyrotoxicosis combined with central nervous system hypothyroidism due to impaired thyroid hormone transport across brain barriers (4, 5). The clinical manifestations include global developmental delay, spastic quadriplegia, dystonic movements, muscle wasting, and cardiovascular complications, with significantly reduced life expectancy and high mortality rates primarily due to respiratory infections and sudden cardiac death (4).

The pathophysiology of MCT8 deficiency has been extensively studied using mouse models, which faithfully replicate the abnormal serum thyroid hormone profile observed in patients. Mct8 knockout mice exhibit elevated serum T3 and reduced T4 levels, along with increased thyroid hormone concentrations in peripheral tissues but decreased thyroid hormone content in the brain (5). However, these mice do not display the severe neurological phenotype seen in humans, likely due to compensatory thyroid hormone transport mechanisms, particularly through organic anion-transporting polypeptide 1C1 (OATP1C1) at the blood–brain barrier. Consequently, double-knockout mice lacking both MCT8 and OATP1C1 have been developed as more representative models (6), demonstrating profound central nervous system thyroid hormone deficiency, delayed myelination, impaired cerebellar development, and locomotor disabilities that more closely mirror the human condition (7).

Given the limited therapeutic options for AHDS patients, considerable research effort has focused on developing treatment strategies. Conventional thyroid hormone replacement therapy with levothyroxine has proven ineffective and potentially harmful, as it further elevates already high serum T3 levels in the periphery without improving neurological symptoms (8). Therefore, attention has turned to thyroid hormone analogs that can potentially bypass MCT8-dependent transport mechanisms and access target tissues, particularly the brain, to restore thyroid hormone action.

Among the most promising therapeutic candidates is 3,3′,5-triiodothyroacetic acid (TRIAC), an endogenous thyroid hormone metabolite with several advantageous properties for treating MCT8 deficiency (9). TRIAC exhibits high affinity for thyroid hormone receptors and importantly can be transported into cells independently of MCT8 (10). Preclinical studies have demonstrated that TRIAC can prevent abnormal cerebellar development, myelination, and cortical interneuron differentiation when administered to Mct8/Oatp1c1 double-knockout mice during early postnatal development (7). Clinical trials in AHDS patients have shown that TRIAC effectively reduces elevated serum T3 levels and improves peripheral manifestations of thyrotoxicosis (4), but potential neurodevelopmental benefits upon early intervention have not been demonstrated yet. In addition, TRIAC has been used in the treatment of resistance to thyroid hormone β (RTHβ), where it is known to relieve hyperthyroid symptoms, such as increased energy expenditure without exacerbating cardiac thyromimetic effects (11). Consequently, TRIAC has received regulatory approval for RTHβ therapy (9).

However, while thyroid hormone analogs, such as TRIAC, show promise as therapeutic agents for thyroid hormone transporter deficiencies and thyroid hormone resistance β, the tissue-specific effects of TRIAC treatment especially on cardiovascular and metabolic functions remain incompletely characterized. Previous studies have established that thyroid hormones regulate cardiac properties and energy homeostasis through both direct peripheral actions and central mechanisms (12, 13), yet the differential responses of these tissues to TRIAC administration have not been systematically evaluated in a comprehensive manner. Furthermore, it remains unclear whether TRIAC treatment produces classical signs of hyperthyroidism across all target tissues or, at a specific dose, could exhibit selective tissue-specific actions that could inform therapeutic applications. Understanding the multi-organ metabolic and molecular responses to TRIAC is essential for determining its potential as a safer thyroid hormone analog with reduced systemic side effects compared with conventional thyroid hormone therapy.

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of TRIAC’s effects across multiple organ systems in wild-type mice, demonstrating that TRIAC treatment increases food intake and reduces circulating T4 levels while not affecting heart rate or body temperature under the conditions of our paradigm. These findings reveal important insights into TRIAC’s therapeutic profile and support its potential as a targeted treatment approach with distinct advantages over conventional thyroid hormone replacement therapy.

Methods

Animal model

Wild-type C57Bl/6 mice, purchased from Charles River Germany and used at the ages of 3–4 months, were kept in individual ventilated cages on a constant 12 h light:12 h darkness cycle with an ambient temperature of 22 ± 1°C and were continuously allowed free access to food (standard rodent diet) and water. Food and water intake and body weight were recorded daily by weighing. All procedures were approved by the Ministerium für Landwirtschaft, ländliche Räume, Europa und Verbraucherschutz, MLLEV, Schleswig-Holstein, Germany (V243-70506/2015(63-5.15), including amendment). The animals were intraperitoneally (i.p.) injected with 3,3′,5-triiodothyroacetic acid (TRIAC, named TA3, 400 ng/g body weight) or solvent once daily for 14 days. The respective dose was selected based on previous studies showing restored myelination and neuronal development (7). Organ weights were determined postmortem, and all tissue analyses occurred 24 h after the last treatment.

Serum hormone and metabolic measurements

Serum concentrations of total triiodothyronine (T3) and total thyroxine (T4) were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (DNOV053, NovaTec Immundiagnostica GmbH, Germany, and EIA-1781, DRG Instruments, Germany, respectively) following manufacturer’s protocols, as described previously (14). Although these assays were originally validated for human samples, they provided reliable results for murine serum samples, but contrary to the manufacturer’s instructions, the T3 assay displays a high cross-reactivity to TRIAC (data not shown).

Throughout the 14-day treatment period, body mass, food consumption, and water intake were continuously monitored to evaluate metabolic responses to TRIAC administration. Adipose tissue depot masses were determined postmortem and expressed relative to total body weight to quantify tissue-specific changes. Hepatic glycogen concentrations were measured biochemically as described previously (15) and normalized to values from untreated control animals to assess metabolic alterations following TA3 treatment.

Cardiovascular assessment

Hemodynamic parameters, including heart rate, systolic pressure, diastolic pressure, and mean arterial pressure, were recorded to evaluate cardiovascular function. Non-invasive tail-cuff methodology was employed for blood pressure determination (Model SC-100; Hatteras Instrument, Cory, NC), as described previously (16, 17).

Thermal imaging

Infrared thermal imaging was utilized to quantify maximum surface temperatures at distinct anatomical locations, including the inner ear, interscapular brown adipose tissue region, and tail, in animals housed at 22°C ambient temperature. Temperature recordings were obtained using infrared thermography equipment (FLIR systems, T355, Sweden) to assess regional thermal responses to treatment, as described previously (18, 19).

Molecular analysis

Total RNA was extracted from tissue samples using standard protocols and converted to complementary DNA for quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis. Gene expression levels were measured by qPCR using SYBR green with normalization to reference genes cyclophilin (Cyclo) and hypoxanthine phosphoribosyltransferase (HPRT), as described previously (17). Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 1 (see section on Supplementary materials given at the end of the article). For protein analysis, tissue lysates were prepared and subjected to western blot analysis to determine the protein levels of SERCA2 and oxidative phosphorylation complexes. Protein concentrations were quantified using a bicinchoninic acid assay, and immunoblot signals were normalized to total protein content following standard protocols, as described previously (17).

Statistical analysis

Measurements between control and TA3-treated experimental groups were compared using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests. For repeated measurements (cumulative food intake), we used repeated ANOVA with Bonferroni’s correction. No outliers were detected (using GraphPad Prism ROUT test), and thus, no animals were removed from the analysis. Statistical significance was established at P < 0.05 for all comparisons.

Results

To test whether TRIAC treatment affects thyroid hormone levels and metabolic parameters, we performed a 14-day treatment study comparing control wild-type mice without TRIAC (−TA3) treatment and wild-type mice treated with TRIAC (+TA3) as indicated (Fig. 1A). Serum total T3 levels remained unchanged between control and TA3-treated groups, while total T4 levels were significantly reduced in the TA3-treated animals (Fig. 1B, P < 0.001). It needs to be highlighted that we found a substantial cross-reactivity to TRIAC in the used T3 ELISA. Hence, despite the very short half-life of TRIAC (20) and the observation that only 0.015% of the original dose can be found in circulation after 24 h (21), it cannot be fully excluded that at least parts of the reported value could represent TRIAC rather than T3.

In the pituitary, TA3 treatment significantly decreased thyroid-stimulating hormone beta (Tshb) gene expression and significantly increased prolactin (Prl) gene expression (P < 0.001), while growth hormone (Gh), tyrosine hydroxylase (Th), and dopamine D2 receptor (Drd2) expression levels showed no differences between groups (Fig. 1C). Body weight measurements showed no differences between groups at day 1 or day 14 of the treatment period (Fig. 1D). Cumulative food intake progressively increased in TA3-treated animals compared with controls, with significant differences emerging from day 8 onward and becoming highly significant by day 14 (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 1E). Water consumption showed no differences between treatment groups (Fig. 1F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that TRIAC treatment suppresses circulating T4 levels and pituitary Tshb expression while stimulating food intake without affecting body weight.

To examine hepatic thyroid hormone action and metabolic endpoints, we performed quantitative PCR analysis of hepatic gene expression and measured glycogen content in TA3-treated versus control animals. TA3 treatment significantly increased the expression of type I deiodinase (Dio1, P < 0.01), malic enzyme (Me1, P < 0.001), and pyruvate carboxykinase (Pck1, P < 0.05) genes, with Dio1 showing the most pronounced upregulation (Fig. 2A). Thrsp, Pklr, Ldha, and Acaca expression levels were not different between groups (Fig. 2A). Hepatic glycogen content remained unchanged between TA3-treated and control groups (Fig. 2B).

To test for thermoregulatory alterations, we used thermographic analysis, which revealed that inner ear, interscapular brown adipose tissue, and tail temperatures were not different between treatment groups at 22°C ambient temperature (Supplementary Fig. S1).

To investigate cardiovascular effects and molecular changes in cardiac tissue, we performed blood pressure measurements, cardiac weight analysis, and gene expression analysis in control and TA3-treated animals. TA3 treatment had no effect on pulse rate, systolic pressure, diastolic pressure, or mean arterial pressure (Fig. 3A). Heart weight-to-body weight ratios were not different between groups (Fig. 3B). At the molecular level, TA3 treatment significantly increased the expression of hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated channel 2 (Hcn2, P < 0.05) and phospholamban (Pln, P < 0.05) and significantly decreased the α-MHC/β-MHC ratio (P < 0.001), while Hcn4, Atp2a2, Nppb, Ryr2, Adrb1, Adrb2, and Chrm2 showed no differences in expression levels compared with controls (Fig. 3C). In summary, these findings indicate that TRIAC induces specific cardiac gene expression changes without affecting heart rate or weight.

To determine molecular effects on metabolic tissues, we performed tissue weight measurements, gene expression analysis, and protein quantification in gastrocnemius and soleus muscles from control and TA3-treated animals. Soleus muscle weights were not different between groups, while gastrocnemius muscle weights showed a minor decrease (Fig. 4A). In gastrocnemius muscle, uncoupling protein 3 (Ucp3) expression was significantly decreased in TA3-treated animals (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4B). In soleus muscle, the expression of Ucp3 (P < 0.05), Pparδ (P < 0.01), and Gpd2 (P < 0.05) was significantly reduced, while that of Sln, Ryr1, and Dio2 showed no differences between groups (Fig. 4C). SERCA2 protein levels in soleus muscle were not different between groups (Fig. 4D). Oxidative phosphorylation complex proteins showed no differences across the different complexes in TA3-treated versus control samples (Fig. 4E). Adipose tissue depot weights for interscapular brown adipose tissue, inguinal white adipose tissue, and gonadal white adipose tissue were not different between groups (Supplementary Fig. S2A). In brown adipose tissue, Dio2 expression was significantly decreased (P < 0.01) and Adrb3 expression was significantly increased (P < 0.05), while the expression levels of Ucp1 and Tfam showed no differences between groups (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Taken together, these data reveal that TRIAC treatment affects some genes responsible for muscle mitochondrial function and metabolism, but without any obvious effects on adipose tissue mass.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that at the selected dose, TRIAC exhibits tissue-selective thyromimetic activity, producing robust hepatic gene expression changes characteristic of thyroid hormone action while avoiding classical hyperthyroid manifestations, such as tachycardia and cardiac hypertrophy. This selective tissue responsiveness addresses the critical knowledge gap regarding differential organ sensitivity to TRIAC and supports its potential as a safer therapeutic alternative to conventional thyroid hormone replacement in patients with MCT8 deficiency or thyroid hormone resistance β. The findings provide mechanistic insights into TRIAC’s therapeutic window and establish a foundation for understanding how thyroid hormone analogs can achieve beneficial metabolic effects without inducing harmful systemic consequences.

The tissue-selective nature of TRIAC’s thyromimetic effects supports our understanding of thyroid hormone analog pharmacology. In the liver, TRIAC induced robust upregulation of Dio1 and Me1 genes, classic markers of thyroid hormone action, demonstrating preserved hepatic responsiveness to this analog (17). This hepatic activity is consistent with previous studies (22) and clinical observations showing that TRIAC treatment effectively normalizes metabolic parameters in patients with thyroid hormone resistance β, including improvements in cholesterol levels and hepatic function markers (11). The selective activation of hepatic thyroid hormone pathways without corresponding cardiovascular stimulation suggests that TRIAC may preferentially engage TRβ-mediated signaling, which predominates in liver tissue, while having a reduced impact on TRα1-dependent processes that govern cardiac or thermoregulatory functions (1, 13, 23). However, as recent studies have identified multiple transporters capable of facilitating TRIAC uptake, including SLC10A1, SLCO1B1, SLC22A6, SLC22A8, and SLC22A24, which may contribute to tissue-specific distribution patterns and explain the differential organ responses observed (24), it ultimately remains to be seen whether the observed effects are primarily due to differential cellular import or preferential receptor subtype activation.

The paradoxical downregulation of metabolic genes in skeletal muscle provides compelling evidence for tissue-specific mechanisms underlying TRIAC action that extend beyond simple receptor activation. The decreased expression of Ucp3, Pparδ, and Gpd2 in muscle tissue contrasts with the expected hyperthyroid response and may reflect differential transporter utilization as compared to the liver (24). One might speculate that this could be the result of the reduced circulating T4 levels; however, previous studies have indicated that the muscle is more dependent on circulating T3 (25), which was most likely not altered by TRIAC. The muscle-specific downregulation therefore likely represents an indirect compensatory mechanism, potentially through altered liver metabolites or cytokines, that helps explain some of the therapeutic benefits observed in MCT8 deficiency patients who without treatment suffer from excessive peripheral thyroid hormone action that contributes to muscle wasting and metabolic dysfunction (8). Studies in these patients have shown that TRIAC can improve metabolic parameters while avoiding the muscle catabolism typically associated with thyrotoxicosis, suggesting that the observed muscle gene downregulation may be beneficial rather than detrimental.

The preservation of normal cardiovascular hemodynamics despite specific molecular changes in cardiac tissue represents perhaps the most clinically relevant aspect of TRIAC’s tissue selectivity. While the treatment induced significant upregulation of Hcn2 expression and altered the α-MHC/β-MHC ratio, these molecular changes did not translate into the tachycardia or cardiac hypertrophy typically associated with thyroid hormone excess. A similar dissociation between molecular and physiological responses has been observed previously in thyroid hormone resistance α (16) and provides crucial mechanistic insights into TRIAC’s safety profile and therapeutic window. It needs to be kept in mind that central thyroid hormone signaling appears to be particularly important for cardiovascular regulation, as studies in mice lacking thyroid hormone transporters have shown that elevated peripheral thyroid hormone levels alone are insufficient to induce cardiac hypertrophy and hyperthermia without intact central thyroid hormone action (17). Consequently, the preserved cardiovascular stability observed with TRIAC treatment may also indicate that this analog may not effectively act in the hypothalamic regulatory centers, which has also been suggested by previous studies (22, 26, 27).

The increased food intake without immediate weight gain observed in our study provides additional evidence for TRIAC’s unique metabolic profile and suggests altered metabolic efficiency under treatment conditions. This finding aligns with clinical observations in patients with thyroid hormone resistance β, where TRIAC therapy often leads to improved energy balance and weight stability despite normalization of previously elevated metabolic rates (11). The differential responses observed between oxidative and glycolytic muscle types may reflect distinct metabolic demands and transporter expression profiles, as different muscle fiber types may exhibit varying sensitivities to thyroid hormone action.

Recent studies have revealed that browning of white adipose tissue, while inducible by thyroid hormone treatment, does not contribute significantly to thermogenesis or glucose consumption (28). Together with the findings in this study, it suggests that TRIAC’s metabolic effects occur independently of classical thermogenic responses in adipose tissue. This is supported by the general concept that thyroid hormones regulate thermogenesis and energy expenditure through complex synergies between central control and peripheral action in muscle and brown adipose tissue and that altering only one pathway may not be sufficient to result in an overall net change in energy metabolism (29). Although BAT and body temperature were not significantly affected by TRIAC, they showed a tendency to be lower, which together with the reduced Dio2 expression and the increased Adrb3 expression may suggest that the sympathetic stimulation of BAT could have been reduced somewhat (28, 29). Whether this is caused by reduced hypothalamic action of TRIAC (22, 26, 27) or a reduced T4 supply to the brain remains to be established.

Although our study was conducted in healthy wild-type mice rather than MCT8- or TRβ-deficient models, which limits direct translation to the therapeutic context where TRIAC shows greatest clinical promise, the findings provide crucial mechanistic insights into tissue-selective thyroid hormone analog action. While the 14-day treatment duration may not capture long-term adaptive responses or potential cumulative effects that could emerge with chronic administration, and the specific TRIAC dose and delivery route used may not reflect optimal therapeutic regimens for human applications, these limitations do not diminish the fundamental importance of demonstrating tissue-selective thyromimetic activity in a controlled experimental system, especially when considering that most effects of thyrotoxicosis are already clearly present after 14 days (30). Moreover, it needs to be kept in mind that the TRIAC-induced suppression of TSH expression at the pituitary level, which likely contributes to the reduced tT4 levels, may also alter the function of tissues that depend more on T4 rather than T3 or TRIAC. Thus, the role of these shifted thyroid hormone profiles should also be addressed in future studies, especially since we did not directly dissect transporter specificity of all involved tissues. However, as we defined a characteristic in vivo physiological response pattern for TRIAC, our data can be used as groundwork for following mechanistic studies. Long-term safety and efficacy studies in MCT8-deficient animal models will be essential to validate these findings in the relevant disease context and optimize therapeutic protocols (6, 8). However, it needs to be kept in mind that TRIAC transporter expression might be different between mice and humans (24), which might limit the direct translation of findings in preclinical models.

Nevertheless, the fact that TRIAC can at a specific dose elicit tissue specific responses, for instance, in the liver without affecting heart rate, heart weight, or body temperature highlights its unique feature for translational application in thyroid hormone resistance. These advances will ultimately enable the development of more sophisticated thyroid hormone analogs with enhanced tissue selectivity and improved therapeutic indices, revolutionizing the treatment of thyroid hormone-related disorders and opening new avenues for precision endocrine therapy.
