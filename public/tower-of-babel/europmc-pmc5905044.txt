THE PROBLEM OF THE FEEBLE-MINDED.1
By JOHN MACPHERSON, M.D., F.R.C.P.,
Commissioner in Lunacy for Scotland.
I might have entitled this paper the "problems" of the
feeble-minded, for there is not one problem but many.
There are, for example, scientific, social, and political problems,
none of which has, up to the present time, been adequately
solved. It must be apparent to every one who has given
thought to the subject that our views of the social and
political aspects of the question must depend largely upon our
scientific knowledge; but our biological knowledge is, unfor-
tunately, somewhat imperfect, obscured by controversy,
and distorted by ill-considered theories; yet, as I shall
endeavour to submit, there are certain established principles
by which we may be safely guided, which are accepted by
all the contending schools. Our conception of the term
" feeble-minded" requires more precise definition, and to
that end I shall commence my paper with a few remarks on
the relationship of body and mind.

that, as we see it, instinct prevails almost wholly in certain
natural orders, such as the hymenoptera?bees and ants.
When we come to the vertebrates, on the other hand, we
meet with signs indicating a decreasing function of instinct
and an increasing function of intelligence, until in man-?the
chief of the vertebrates?we encounter the highest intelli-
gence, but the minimum amount of instinct possessed by
any animal. In man, mind, in the form of intelligence,
is predominant; but it has sacrificed, or lost, to a large
extent, the element of instinct, which it was perhaps unable to
carry along with it. Not that man, as you all very well
know, is devoid of instinct, for a large part of our life is
instinctive; but, as compared with one of the lower animals,
he is particularly poor in that quality. When I say that man
is less instinctive than the lower animals I do not forget
that so far as concerns the primary instincts of nutrition,
reproduction, and self-preservation, he is, in these respects,
identical with other animals?I merely mean that his mental
operations are more intellectual and less instinctive.
Now, if we were here to inquire what mind is, the answer
would be extremely difficult, and would involve complicated
psychological definitions. It will suffice to say that the
human mind is, predominantly, consciousness, and that human
consciousness is, predominantly, memory. Memory,then,for our
present argument, so far as regards man, may be regarded as
mind. What, then, is the difference between the mind of
man and that of a dog? The difference may be simply
expressed by saying that the brain of a man and of a dog

Memory, then, free and unrestrained, with a comparatively
great deficiency of instinct, are the chief characteristics of
human intelligence. You must not confound this use of
the word "memory" with the ordinary power of recollecting
an event or a place we have once seen wThen it is again
presented to us. It is here used in the wider sense of abstract
thought, of which memory is the chief factor.
From such a definition of human intelligence we are in
a position to estimate more clearly the nature of the varia-
tion which we call " feeble-mindedness," but which would be
better described as mental defect, for not all mental defectives
are feeble in mind, though the majority of them are so. No
such variation in the direction of mental defect can exist
without a corresponding defect in the complicated mechan-
ism of the brain, which is the physical basis of mind. Every
such mental defect is, as Dr. Archdall Reid was the first
to point out, a defect of memory such as I have defined it,
not necessarily of the power of ordinary recollection, in
which we are all more or less defective, but of that power of
freely projecting the memory in all directions, and of linking
together facts in the past with facts in the present, or vice
aversa, and imagining the future; in other words, of correct
reasoning. It will be found, therefore, that every mentally
defective person is defective in memory, using the word
" memory " in this wider sense.
The mentally defective are usually, for convenience, divided
into three classes:?
1. Imbeciles (including idiots).
2. Feeble-minded.
3. Moral imbeciles.

1. Imbeciles as a class are incapable of education in the
ordinary sense. Owing to a physical defect in the develop-
ment of the brain they are unable to exercise that mental
power of memory whereby facts of the present are linked
with facts of the past, and whereby the future can be either
imagined or foreseen. Consequently their reasoning faculties
are, if not in abeyance, greatly restricted, and so far from being
able to take a place in society, society has to think for them
and act for them. But there is something more than this.
They have not only missed the attainment of the average
human intelligence, but as man has dropped to a large extent
in the process of development the instinctive mind of the lower
animals, the imbecile who has also dropped his human
intelligence is at a particuliar disadvantage. Just as a wild
bird, which had for some reason failed to develop the ordinary
powers of flight of its species, would be at the mercy
of four-footed animals of prey, so the imbecile is largely
at the mercy of circumstances, for he possesses neither the
human intelligence nor the animal instinct necessary to
protect himself in the common affairs of life.
2. The feeble-minded are less helpless than the imbecile or
idiot, for they have more intelligence; but their mental
operations are slow or unsteady, and they fall short of
ordinary standards of prudence, independence, and self-control.
Here, again, it is necessary to mention the instincts, this time
the great primary instincts. When the intellectual faculty is
restricted or diminished in a young subject, there is, as a
rule, no corresponding diminution in the strength of the
primary instincts. Consequently these instincts are insuffi-
ciently controlled by the intellect; the person is imperfectly
balanced, and hence arise those trains of evil consequences
which are beginning for the first time to force themselves
into recognition in our day.
3. The moral imbecile has not always been recognised as a
person of defective intellect, but it is now generally admitted
that there is in almost all instances some slight limitation of
intellect. These people, who, though by no means apparently
deficient in ordinary intelligence, display early in life
vicious propensities of an incorrigible or unusual kind,
are characterised not alone by these tendencies, but
chiefly by callousness and cruelty, often of a revolting
kind. Now, unnatural callousness and cruelty indicate most
unquestionably a lack of the power of imagination, which
is a failure to co-ordinate the facts of life with one's own

personal experiences, and therefore a defect in the faculty
which I have described as memory.
When we turn to the question of the origin of mental
defect, we tread upon delicate ground, because every
inch of it has been fiercely contested, and the pitfalls
are innumerable. Before we enter the more debatable
territory, it is advisable to lighten our task, and concentrate
the subject as much as possible. To that end I shall state the
following facts:?
1. The great majority of mental defectives are born
defective. That is to say, in biological language, they are
congenital variations from the species.
2. A certain proportion?probably a larger proportion than
we think?are defective because of injuries or of disease,
or some other cause, operating immediately before or after
birth.
3. A certain proportion are defective because of imperfect
or improper education or training, or because of improper nur-
ture or feeding in infancy or early childhood. In this connec-
tion it must not be forgotten that mental defect may occur for
sufficient reasons at any period of life; thus, in old age, as
the result of senile degeneration. We know that the great
majority of the mentally defective are congenital germinal
variations for the simple reason that, like other variations,
mental defect tends to be transmitted in all races of men,
civilised and savage. Even among the higher vertebrates
lower in the scale than man, corresponding physical anomalies
are not uncommon; not only is the variation transmitted
from parent to offspring, but normal individuals, who are
members of a tainted stock, can and do transmit it. If,
therefore, mental defect can be transmitted, not only directly
from parent to offspring, but intermittently through parents
who are not themselves mentally defective, then there is
no room for doubt that i-t is a germinal variation.
Before we can come to any conclusion as to its origin,
we must consider what is at present known regarding
variations in general, and their origin.
Among the more certain items of knowledge comes first
and foremost the fact, which has been accepted by all men in
all ages, that offspring closely resemble their parents in their
mental and physical characteristics. There is no dispute
on this point, and here we feel ourselves on sure ground.
It is well to bear in mind, however, that closely as the
members of one family resemble their parents they resemble

individuals resemble their parents, and still more their
brothers and sisters, they resemble their own species to a far
greater degree than either?that is to say, that individual

This seems to me a strangely neglected but very significant
fact. Take a Hock of sheep, for instance, and you will

It is the same with human beings of another race. Take
a lot of Chinamen or negroes, and we would find it

our own race it is the chief business of our lives to distinguish
one person from another, often by very minute signs which
we have learned to read with great precision. The fact
remains that the greater points of resemblance are between
the members of a species, and that only the minuter, more
superficial resemblances, lie between members of the same
family. For the present, then, we shall disregard the family
resemblances, and inquire how it is that the members of
a species resemble each other so closely. The answer to
that problem comprises several important hypotheses?
1. The hypothesis of the continuity and the resistance
of the germ plasm.
2. The law of variation.
3. The law of natural selection.

or a litter of dogs, or a brood of fowls, resemble each other

to the risk of extinction. It is an undoubted fact that races

an individual cannot acquire immunity against either of

These three laws of inheritance, which have only been
cursorily and imperfectly sketched, sufficiently explain the sta-
bility of species, the resemblance of the members of a species to
one another, and the reason why, though resemblingone another,
they, nevertheless, within limits, differ from one another.
Variations of this kind are known in biological language as
"fluctuations around the mean" or "continuous variations."'
They are the general rule in nature, but it is necessary
to consider some exceptions.
The chief exception is what is known as "discontinuous
variations " or " mutations." Here the variation is marked
and aberrant; the individual in one or more characters varies
conspicuously from the type. According to the more
advanced Mendelians, all progressive evolution is due to
mutations; the function of continuous variations or fluctua-
tions according to them being solely to maintain the stability
of the existing species or type.
There is something romantic in the Mendelian hypothesis,
for Mendel, as you all know, was a cleric and a monk.
There are nearly fifty years since he published the results of
his great discovery, but for some obscure reason it only
began to attract universal attention about twelve years ago.
It has caused an immense controversy, and is still being
subjected to the most stringent criticism. Mendel's facts have
been amply confirmed; of their scientific accuracy there
remains no doubt. It is with regard to the deductions to
be drawn from them that we must at present maintain an
open mind. Do they or do they not explain all the facts of

Mendel's experiments were made upon peas, the tall and
dwarfed varieties of which were crossed. From the mongrel
seeds thus produced only tall plants developed. When these
latter were self-fertilised about a quarter of the offspring
were found to be dwarfs, three-quarters tall; no plants were
intermediate. Thereafter the dwarfs continued to breed true
as long as the experiments were continued. It seemed as
if their race was purged of the tall variety. About a quarter
of the tall plants also bred true, producing nothing but tall
varieties, so from them the dwarfs seemed to be eliminated.
But the remaining tall plants (50 per cent of the whole)
behaved like their cross-bred parents, producing tall and
dwarf plants in the old proportion, the dwarfs being all pure,
and the tall plants pure and impure. This process went
on indefinitely, or, at any rate, so long as the experiments
lasted. Mendel called the tallness the ''dominant" quality,
and the dwarf the "recessive" quality. Other similar
experiments were made upon the colour of peas with the
same result.
It is upon these extreme variations (" dominant" or
" recessive"), therefore, according to some of the more
extreme exponents of Mendelism, that evolution depends.
These "dominant" and "recessive" qualities are, they hold,
universal in every character possessed by every living thing.
There is no exception; tallness and shortness, colour or the
want of it, beauty and ugliness, health and disease, are
transmitted in accordance with Mendel's great law. But
there is more than this. When once any such quality enters
a stock?let us say, feeble-mindedness, colour blindness,
or some such constitutional variation?it cannot be eradicated
except by the most stringent artificial selection, but goes on
reappearing for ever. This is a very serious doctrine, for, if
it is literally accepted, it involves two almost incredible
alternative conclusions?(a) either that the human race is
doomed to succumb to one or other of these anomalies, for
it is a significant fact that most of these mutations are evil;
or (6) if it is to survive, that it must be subjected to artificial
selection in the same way as prize cattle are artificially bred.
Stated in this way?and I do not think I am travestying the
views of some of the extremer exponents of Mendelism?
the doctrine appears absurd, for it is contrary to experience.

The human race is thousands of years old, and, so far, it
has shown no tendency to degenerate.
There is, however, a serious objection to accepting this
mutation doctrine. The experiments upon which it rests
have been made wholly with cultivated plants, and quickly
bred domestic animals. When a breeder desires to obtain
any quality from a special plant or animal, he breeds
definitely and quickly for that quality, rigidly eliminating
those individuals which do not possess it, and selecting
only those who do. The result is that he forcibly establishes
certain characters which become " dominant."
Again, the tendency to vary on the part of cultivated
plants is proverbial. When plants are removed to cultivated
gardens they immediately begin to manifest changes of
various kinds, while the same plants, in their natural habitats,
have, under the law of natural selection, remained un-
changed for hundreds, perhaps thousands of years. In nature,
therefore, we do not find that mutations manifest themselves
or persist in the same way as they do in highly bred domestic
animals and cultivated plants. Now the human race exists
in nature, not in cultivation, notwithstanding all the poetic
and sentimental opinions to the contrary. We are subject
to the most stringent natural laws, as witness the stature and
the hair and eye-colour of a race like the British.
In normal biparental reproduction, which is universal
among the higher animals, the inheritance is blended,
and the offspring partake fairly equally, for each character,
of the qualities of each parent. There are, perhaps, a few
exceptions, such as eye-colour, which may for the present be
disregarded. In abnormal inheritance, where the variations
are extreme, it is impossible that the characters can blend,
and therefore the inheritance is by mutation. Mental defect
and polydactylism are abnormal extreme variations. It is
impossible, for instance, that a person can have fingers.
It is now opportune to point out the fact that there are
two kinds of mental defect?(1) Modified mental defect,
which arises as a fluctuation; and (2) extreme mental defect,
which arises as a mutation.
With regard to the first form, it is clear that a person may
fall very much below the mean of the intellectual quality of
his race, just as a person may fall much below the mean
stature of his race. Suppose, for a moment, that intelligence
depended upon stature?as, of course, it does not. Then
we should see, in the case of British males, a decreasing

The second form of mental defect is a mutation, an extreme
variation from the racial type, not only in respect of mental
manifestations, but also in respect of bodily conformation.
For typical idiots present many physical anomalies, known
as stigmata of degeneration, some of them so extreme as to

By dividing mental defect into these two classes we
greatly simplify the problem. We cannot hope to modify
variation around the mean, which is a law of our being,
and we must therefore accept it as likely to be ever present
with us.
Extreme mental defect is, as we have seen, a mutation
or a reversion to an earlier type, in the sense that the imper-
fection of the brain, which constitutes it, deprives the
individual of the specially human quality of memory?using
that word in the sense I have applied to it?and as man
has largely dropped his instinctive in favour of intellectual
reasoning, instinct is also lost to a large extent. But the

however unwelcome the advance maybe. Both mental defect

non-adaptive to its environment, and inconsistent with the
law of natural selection. It is, therefore, clear that individuals
so afflicted cannot continue to propagate themselves, and
they therefore become extinct. There never was, and never
can be, a race of six-fingered people, for the tendency to
polydactylism ultimately becomes swamped. "Not at all,"

Two things, then, are plain from what has been said?(1)
Ordinary normal inheritance is characterised by continuous
variations which swing within definite limits around the
mean of the species, and (2) abnormal inheritance is marked
by extreme variations, which arise suddenly or gradually,
which behave for a time as Mendelian characters, but which
are ultimately extruded from the stock by twro concurrent
processes?the regression of the majority of the tainted
members towards the normal, and the final extinction of
the few members who markedly exhibit the abnormality.
As we do not know the cause of the variation which results
in extreme mental defect, we dare not suggest a definite
remedy. It behoves us, however, to beware of certain un-
scientific opinions. We are told, for example, that alcoholism,

Again, if any one accepts certain modern doctrines to the
effect that these extreme variations are " segregated" in
the germ-cells of the race, only waiting for an opportunity to
re-appear, I would ask two questions. Why insist upon this
antiquity ? If they have arisen once, why should they not
be constantly arising ? The second question is, If mental
defect is a " segregated" Mendelian character, it must be
extensively spread throughout the human race; and, I ask,
by what means is it proposed to detect its presence in the
germ-cells of normal persons who ignorantly transmit it ?
Finally, there are some who hold that society in modern
times is being slowly re-cast into two layers?a higher
consisting of the mentally more capable, and a lower consist-
ing of the mentally less capable and the congenitally immoral.
The latter type, which I take to be equivalent to moral
imbeciles, are, they say, deposited in the slums of our great
cities. According to the exponents of this opinion, we
must, if we would save humanity, suppress the growth of
the lower stratum and encourage the fertility of the higher.
In other words, if we wish for the future prosperity of
the race, we must secure the proper mating of the fit, and
prevent the unfit from having children. We are all agreed
on the fact that certain undesirable types should not have
children, e.g., the feeble-minded, the alcoholic, and those who
suffer from obvious constitutional disease. But, apart from
such, who are the " fit," and who are the " unfit" ? There is
a patent fallacy underlying this extraordinary counsel of
perfection. We have seen that the essential difference
between a mentally defective person and an ordinary
individual is a difference in memory; in other words, a
difference in the power of acquiring education or experience.

we conclude that he has a normal brain. No doubt his
capacity for acquiring experience will vary with his ability,
and his ability will vary from that of his fellows as every
other quality varies. But when we pass from physical to
intellectual characters in man we pass into a totally
new sphere; and we must remember, what many people
forget, that this power of acquiring experience is not an
instinct, but an intellectual power. The capacity is expan-
sile, and its expansion depends upon the kind and the
quality of experience it has the opportunity of acquiring.
If you place an infant, the child of modern educated Europeans,
among savages he will grow up a savage; if you place him in
the slums he will become a slum-dweller indistinguishable
from the others. The one will have the morals and intelli-
gence of a savage, the other the morals and intelligence of
his companions of the slums, whatever these may chance
to be. From which I conclude that neither morality nor
intelligence are instincts with which we are born, but
acquirements capable of being made only by the normal
human mind. This new doctrine might perhaps apply to some
society of the lower animals, such as dogs, whose morality is
largely instinctive, and whose reasoning is largely intuitive.
The mind of man is totally different; it is intellectual rather
than intuitive, and because it is intellectual it is capable of
acquiring new experiences and new habits; above all, it
is capable of unceasingly opposing new habits to old ones,
and thus of rising above the domination of any habit.
I have said in the course of this argument that the capacity
for acquiring experience is one thing, and the opportunities
for acquiring the right kind of experience another. A man,
however mentally capable, cannot become learned without
education, and his morality, however it may vary relatively
to that of others, will be of the same type and quality as that
of his neighbours.
We ought in every interest, in the interest of preventing
suffering by the propagation of helpless beings, in the interest
of decency and order, and even in the interest of economy, to
segregate, and, at the same time, tenderly care for the feeble-
minded and the constitutionally diseased. But we need
i not hope, by such means, to eradicate mental or physical
anomalies from the race. If a normal man, sound in bodj^ and
mind, is capable, as I have contended, of developing himself,
given the necessary opportunities, there is very much work
before the eugenist to supply those opportunities of physical
health, common decency of living, and suitable education,
