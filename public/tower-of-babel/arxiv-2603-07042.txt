Asymptotic Behaviors of Global Solutions to Fourth-order Parabolic and Hyperbolic Equations with Dirichlet Boundary Conditions
Wenlong Wu, Yanyan Zhang

Abstract




Abstract

This paper investigates the asymptotic behaviors of global solutions to fourth-order parabolic and hyperbolic equations with Dirichlet boundary conditions. The equations model Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems (MEMS) and are depending on a positive voltage parameter
\lambda
. We establish the convergence of global solutions to an equilibrium, along with the convergence rate estimates. Supporting numerical simulations are presented.

Keywords:
MEMS equation, fourth-order equation, global solution, Lojasiewicz
Simon inequality, convergence rate

Mathematics Subject Classification (2020):
35B40, 35K35, 35L70, 74F15, 74H40

Introduction

Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems are microscopic devices that combine electrostatic effects and precision machining technology. In recent years, MEMS devices have been widely used in electronic devices, aerospace, and medical fields, such as gyroscopes in smartphones, pressure sensors in aviation systems, and pacemakers. Due to the importance of MEMS to science and industry, many engineers have developed a strong interest in its mathematical modeling.

A simplified MEMS device is shown in Figure
1
(see
[
21
, Figure 7.13]
). It consists of a fixed ground plate and a deformable elastic plate with fixed boundaries. When a voltage is applied, the elastic plate deflects towards the ground plate. A common feature of such devices is that when the applied voltage exceeds a certain threshold, the elastic plate may ​touch down​ on the ground plate (this phenomenon is called quenching), resulting in ​pull-in instability of the device.

A simplified MEMS device

Over the past two decades, mathematical research on MEMS equations ​has expanded rapidly. Among these, the following second-order MEMS problem has been widely studied.

u=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\\
u(0,\cdot)=u_{0},\ \ u_{t}(0,\cdot)=u_{1},&x\in\Omega,\end{array}\right.\right.

(1.1)

3.1
Gradient system

In this subsection, we will show that problem (
1.4

Recall that

For convenience, let

R(t)=\left\{e^{-tA}:t\geq 0\right\}.

Then it follows from the dedfinition of analytic semigroup (
[
20

C_{0}
-semigroup.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1

\left\|u\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}\leq\frac{(1-\kappa)^{2}}{C_{0}^{2}},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

Since

\left\|u\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{0}\left\|u\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}\leq 1-\kappa,\quad\forall t\geq 0.

Let

Note that the gradient system consists of three elements that are function space,
C_{0}
-semigroup and Lyapunov function. Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, the function space is

X(\kappa):=\left\{u\in H_{D}^{4}(\Omega):\left\|u\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}\leq\frac{(1-\kappa)^{2}}{C_{0}^{2}}\textup{ in $\Omega$}\right\}.

By the definition of

, we easily get that
X(\kappa)
is a complete metric space. In addition, under the assumptions of Theorem

, we can deduce from Theorem

that

defined by

is

-semigroup.

In addition to
X(\kappa)
and

, we also need to prove the existence of Lyapunov function. We multiply the equation in (
1.4
) by
u_{t}
, and integrate over
\Omega
to get

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{2}B|\Delta u|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}T|\nabla u|^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{1+u}\right)dx+\left\|u_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=0.

(3.1)

The calculation details are as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\Delta^{2}u)u_{t}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\Delta u\Delta u_{t}dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}u_{t}\frac{\partial(\Delta u)}{\partial\nu}dS-\int_{\partial\Omega}\Delta u\frac{\partial(u_{t})}{\partial\nu}dS

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\Delta u\Delta u_{t}dx.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}-(\Delta u)u_{t}dx=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\nabla u_{t}dx-\int_{\partial\Omega}u_{t}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}dS

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\nabla u_{t}dx.

Based on equation (
3.1
), we define the following energy functional:

E(u)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{2}B|\Delta u|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}T|\nabla u|^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{1+u}\right)dx.

(3.2)

We will use the definition
2.1
to show that
E:X(\kappa)\to\mathbb{R}
is a Lyapunov function.

Lemma 3.1
.

E:X(\kappa)\to\mathbb{R}
is a Lyapunov function with respect to
S(t)
.

Proof.

We will prove the lemma in three steps.

(i)

Continuity: Fix
y_{0}\in X(\kappa)
. Suppose that there is sequence
y_{n}\in X(\kappa)
such that
y_{n}\to y_{0}
in
X(\kappa)
. We claim that
E(y_{n})\to E(y_{0})
.

By the assumptions we get
y_{n}\to y_{0}
in
H_{D}^{4}(\Omega)
. Since
H^{4}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
H^{4}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow H^{2}(\Omega)
, we get
y_{n}\to y_{0}
in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and
y_{n}\to y_{0}
in
H_{D}^{2}(\Omega)
. Thus, when
n\to+\infty
,

\displaystyle\left\|y_{n}\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}-\left\|y_{0}\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}\to 0,

\displaystyle\left\|y_{n}-y_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\to 0.

In addition, since
y_{0},y_{n}\in X(\kappa)
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|y_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{0}\left\|y_{0}\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}\leq 1-\kappa,

\displaystyle\left\|y_{n}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{0}\left\|y_{n}\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}\leq 1-\kappa.

A direct calculation shows that

\displaystyle\Bigg|E(y_{n})-E(y_{0})\Bigg|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{2}\left\|y_{n}\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left\|y_{0}\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\lambda}{1+y_{0}}-\frac{\lambda}{1+y_{n}}\right)dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\Bigg|\left\|y_{n}\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}-\left\|y_{0}\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}\Bigg|+\lambda\Bigg|\int_{\Omega}\frac{y_{n}-y_{0}}{(1+y_{0})(1+y_{n})}dx\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\Bigg|\left\|y_{n}\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}-\left\|y_{0}\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}\Bigg|+\lambda\kappa^{-2}|\Omega|\big|y_{n}-y_{0}\big|_{L^{\infty}}.

Therefore, when
y_{n}\to y_{0}
,
E(y_{n})\to E(y_{0})
.

(ii)

Dissipation: By (
3.1
) and the definition of
E
, we immediately get

\frac{d}{dt}E(u(t))=-\left\|u_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq 0.

Therefore for any given
u_{0}\in X(\kappa)
,
E(S(t)u_{0})
is monotone non-increasing in
t
.

(iii)

Lower bound: Since
u_{0}\in X(\kappa)
, we have

\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{0}\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}\leq 1-\kappa.

Then

E(u_{0})\geq-\frac{\lambda|\Omega|}{\kappa},

.

In summary,
E
is a Lyapunov function with respect to
S(t)
.
∎

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2}}+\left\|w\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}Fw^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq|F|\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda(4-\frac{5}{4}\kappa)}{\kappa^{2}(\frac{\kappa}{4})^{2}}\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda\kappa^{-4}(64-20\kappa)\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{10}\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

By Gronwall inequality (参见
[

, Section 1.3.6]
), for all

\big\|w(t)\big\|_{L^{2}}=\big\|u^{(n)}(t)-u(t)\big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq e^{2C_{10}\tilde{T}_{0}}\big\|u_{0}^{(n)}-u_{0}\big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\to 0.

Thus,
u_{n}\to u
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Since
\big\|u^{(n)}\big\|_{H^{5}}
is uniformly boundness, then by Eberlein-Sumulian Theorem, there is a sequence
u^{(nk)}
such that

( “
\rightharpoonup
” denotes the weak convergence in Banach space). Since

,

in

. Note that
u_{n}\to u
in

, we obtain that

. It then follows from weak lower semi continuity of norm(

\big\|u\big\|_{H^{5}}=\big\|\tilde{u}\big\|_{H^{5}}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\big\|u^{(nk)}\big\|_{H^{5}}\leq C_{10}.

Therefore we obtain the uniform boundness of
\left\|\right\|_{H^{5}}
. Since

, for fixed

is relatively compact in

. Therefore we can deduce from Definition

3.2
Lojasiewicz-Simon inequality

We define the
\omega
-limit set by

\omega(u_{0})=\left\{\psi:\exists\,t_{n}\to+\infty\ \text{such that}\,\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|u(t_{n},\cdot)-\psi\right\|_{H_{D}^{4}}=0\right\}.

By lemma
3.2
and Theorem
2.1
,
\omega(u_{0})
is consists of equilibrium, i.e. there are
\psi\in\omega(u_{0})\subset\mathcal{S}
and a sequence
t_{n}\to+\infty
, such that

u(t_{n},\cdot)\to\psi\,\,\text{in $X(\kappa)$}.

Since
H^{4}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we get
u(t_{n},\cdot)\to\psi
in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Let
\varepsilon_{2}=\frac{\kappa}{2}
, then there is
n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that when
n\geq N
,

\left\|u(t_{n})-\psi\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{\kappa}{2}.

By the triangle inequality,

\left\|\psi\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\left\|u(t_{n})\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\leq 1-\frac{\kappa}{2}.

Fix
\psi\in\omega(u_{0})\subset\mathcal{S}
, we consider the linearized problem (
1.4
) at
\psi
:

\left.\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Lw\equiv B\Delta^{2}w-T\Delta w-\frac{2\lambda w}{(1+\psi)^{3}}=0,&x\in\Omega,\\
w=\partial_{\nu}w=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.\right.

(3.20)

For any
h\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, consider the equation

\left.\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Lw+\mu w=h,&x\in\Omega,\\
w=\partial_{\nu}w=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.\right.

(3.21)

and the homogeneous equation

\left.\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Lw+\mu w=0,&x\in\Omega,\\
w=\partial_{\nu}w=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.\right.

(3.22)

Multiplying the equation in (
3.22
) by
w
, then integrating over
\Omega
yields

\int_{\Omega}\left(B|\Delta w|^{2}+T|\nabla w|^{2}+\left(-\frac{2\lambda}{(1+\psi)^{3}}+\mu\right)|w|^{2}\right)dx=0.

Note that
\left\|\psi\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1-\frac{\kappa}{2}
, we have

-\frac{2\lambda}{(1+\psi)^{3}}\geq-16\lambda\kappa^{-3}.

Thus for any constant
\mu>16\lambda\kappa^{-3}
, problem (
3.22
) has only the trivial solution
w\equiv 0
. Then by classical
L^{p}
theory in elliptic equation(
[
9
, Corollary 2.21]
), problem (
3.21
) admits a unique solution
w\in H^{4}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{2}(\Omega)=H_{D}^{4}(\Omega)
. Based on this, we will prove the following Fredholm alternative result:

Lemma 3.3
.

Precisely one of the following two situations holds:

(i)

for any
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, there is unique
w
satisfying

\left.\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Lw=f,&x\in\Omega,\\
w=\partial_{\nu}w=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.\right.

(3.23)

(ii)

there is a weak solution
w\not\equiv 0
satisfying

\left.\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Lw=0,&x\in\Omega,\\
w=\partial_{\nu}w=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.\right.

(3.24)

In addition, if (i) holds, then
Ker(L)=\left\{0\right\}
; If (ii) holds, then
dim(Ker(L))
is finite. Furthermore, the necessary and sufficient condition for the solvability of (
3.23
) is
f\in Ker(L)^{\perp}
.

Proof.

Our proof is motivated by
[
6
, Theorem 6.2.4]
. Let
\gamma>16\lambda\kappa^{-3}
and define the operator
L_{\gamma}w:=Lw+\gamma w
. Then we can deduce from the discussion about (
3.21
) that, for any
h\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
w\in H_{D}^{4}
satisfies

L_{\gamma}w=h.

We rewrite this equation as

w=L_{\gamma}^{-1}h.

Note that
w\in H_{D}^{4}(\Omega)
is the solution to (
3.23
) if and only if

Lw+\gamma w=f+\gamma w,

(3.25)

i.e.

w=L_{\gamma}^{-1}(f+\gamma w).

(3.26)

We rewrite this equation as

w-Kw=g,

(3.27)

where

Kw:=\gamma L_{\gamma}^{-1}w,\quad g:=L_{\gamma}^{-1}f.

(3.28)

We rewrite (
3.26
) as (
3.27
) to employ Fredholm Theorem (
[
6
, Appendix D: Theorem 5]
). We claim that
K:L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
is a linear compact operator.

(1)

Linearity: For any
f_{1},f_{2}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, let
Kf_{1}=w_{1}
,
Kf_{2}=w_{2}
, then we have

\displaystyle K(f_{1}+f_{2})

\displaystyle=K\left(\frac{L_{\gamma}w_{1}}{\gamma}+\frac{L_{\gamma}w_{2}}{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}K(L_{\gamma}(w_{1}+w_{2}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}\gamma L_{\gamma}^{-1}\gamma(w_{1}+w_{2})

\displaystyle=w_{1}+w_{2}=Kf_{1}+Kf_{2}.

(2)

Boundness: for any
f_{3}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, Let
Kf_{3}=w_{3}
, then
w_{3}\in H_{D}^{4}
. Note that
Ker(L_{\gamma})=\left\{0\right\}
, by the priori estimate of ellptic equation, there is a constant
C_{1}>0
such that

\left\|w_{3}\right\|_{H^{4}}\leq C_{1}\left\|f_{3}\right\|_{L^{2}}.

Note that
Kf_{3}=w_{3}
, and
H^{4}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
, then there is a constant
C_{2}>0
such that

\left\|Kf_{3}\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{2}\left\|Kf_{3}\right\|_{H^{4}}\leq C_{1}C_{2}\left\|f_{3}\right\|_{L^{2}}.

Thus
K
is bounded.

(3)

Compactness: Since the embedding
H^{4}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
is compact, we immediately obtain that
K
is compact.

Then by Fredholm Theorem, precisely one of the following two situations holds:

(
\alpha
)

For every
h\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, the equation
w-Kw=h
admits a unique solution
w\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.

(
\beta
)

The equation
w-Kw=0
has nontrivial solution
w\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.

If
(\alpha)
holds, then by (
3.25
)-(
3.28
), problem (
3.23
) admits a unique solution, at this time (ii) does not hold, we obtain
Ker(L)=\left\{0\right\}
. On the other hands, if
(\beta)
hold, then
dim(Ker(L))
is finite. In addition, we need to show that the sufficient and necessary condition for solvability of (
3.23
) is
f\in Ker(L)^{\perp}
. Thus, we prove
L_{\gamma}
defined on
H_{D}^{4}(\Omega)\subset L^{2}(\Omega)
is a self
adjoint operator. In fact we can show that
-L_{\gamma}
is selfadjoint with upper bound 0.(
[
2
, Theorem B.14]
).

(a)

Symmetry: For any
w,v\in D(-L_{\gamma})=H_{D}^{4}(\Omega)
,

\displaystyle(-L_{\gamma}w,v)_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(-B\Delta^{2}w+T\Delta w+\frac{2\lambda w}{(1+\psi)^{3}}-\gamma w\right)vdx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(-B\Delta w\Delta v-T\nabla w\nabla v+\frac{2\lambda wv}{(1+\psi)^{3}}-\gamma wv\right)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(-B\Delta^{2}v+T\Delta v+\frac{2\lambda v}{(1+\psi)^{3}}-\gamma v\right)wdx

\displaystyle=(w,-L_{\gamma}v)_{L^{2}}.

(b)

Upper bound 0: For any
v\in H_{D}^{4}(\Omega)
,

\displaystyle(-L_{\gamma}v,v)_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(-B\Delta^{2}v+T\Delta v+\frac{2\lambda v}{(1+\psi)^{3}}-\gamma v\right)vdx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(-B|\Delta v|^{2}-T|\nabla v|^{2}+\left(\frac{2\lambda}{(1+\psi)^{3}}-\gamma\right)|v|^{2}\right)dx

\displaystyle\leq 0\leq(w,w)_{L^{2}}.

(c)

There is
\lambda>0
such that
Ran(\lambda-(-L_{\gamma}))=L^{2}(\Omega)
(where
Ran
denotes image space), by the discussion above, we can get for all
\mu>0
,
Ran(\mu+L_{\gamma})=L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Therefore we can deduce from
[
2
, Theorem B.14]
that
-L_{\gamma}
is selfadjoint with upper bound 0, which means
-L_{\gamma}
is self-adjoint. Then
K
is self-adjoint. Then by Fredholm Theorem,
Ran(I-K)=Ker(I-K^{*})^{\perp}=Ker(I-K)^{\perp}
. Thus,
Ker(I-K)=\left\{0\right\}
if and only if
Ran(I-K)=L^{2}(\Omega)
. This means the the the sufficient and necessary condition for solvability of (
3.23
) is
f\in Ker(L)^{\perp}
. Lemma
3.3
is proved.
∎

\displaystyle\leq(\left\|M(v)\right\|_{L^{2}}+C^{2}\left\|M(v)\right\|_{L^{2}})\cdot C^{2}\left\|M(v)\right\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{t^{-\frac{\gamma-1}{2}}}{(1+t)^{-\frac{\gamma-1}{2}}}=1.

It follows from (
4.51
) that

\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\int_{t}^{+\infty}\left\|u_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}d\tau\leq c_{5}(1+t)^{-\frac{\theta}{1-2\theta}}.

Theorem
4.2
is proved.
∎

Based on Theorem
4.2
, we can further obtain the convergence rate of the polynomial
\left\|(v,v_{t})\right\|_{\mathbb{H}}
, as shown in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume that
\theta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
is as in Lemma
4.3
, then there are constants
\widetilde{C}>0
,
\gamma=\frac{1}{1-2\theta}>0
and
\widetilde{T}_{0}>0
such that when
t\geq\widetilde{T}_{0}
,

\left\|u_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}+\left\|u-\psi\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}\leq\widetilde{C}(1+t)^{-\gamma}.

(4.52)

Proof.

Recall that
u=v+\psi
, then (
4.52
) is equivalent to

\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}+\left\|v\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}\leq\widetilde{C}(1+t)^{-\gamma}.

(4.53)

Recall that
v
satisfies (
4.24
), we note that

f(v,\psi)=\frac{\lambda(2+u+\psi)}{(1+u)^{2}(1+\psi)^{2}}v.

Let

\tilde{f}=\frac{\lambda(2+u+\psi)}{(1+u)^{2}(1+\psi)^{2}}.

Then
v
satisfies

v_{tt}+v_{t}+B\Delta^{2}v-T\Delta v=\tilde{f}v.

(4.54)

Recall that in subsection
4.1
, we obtain

\left\|\psi\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1-\frac{\kappa}{2}.

By
\left\|u\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1-\kappa
, we obtain that
\tilde{f}
is bounded:

|\tilde{f}|\leq\frac{\lambda(2+1-\kappa+1-\kappa/2)}{(\kappa)^{2}(\kappa/2)^{2}}:=c_{6}.

(4.55)

It is seen from (
4.1
), the following equality holds:

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|v\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}\right)+\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{f}vv_{t}dx.

(4.56)

Since
\tilde{f}
is bounded,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\tilde{f}vv_{t}dx

\displaystyle\leq|\int_{\Omega}\tilde{f}vv_{t}dx|

\displaystyle\leq|\tilde{f}|\int_{\Omega}|vv_{t}|dx

\displaystyle\leq c_{6}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{c_{6}}{2}v^{2}+\frac{1}{2c_{6}}v_{t}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{6}^{2}}{2}\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(4.57)

Combining (
4.56
) and (
4.57
) yields

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\|v\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}\right)+\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq c_{6}^{2}\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(4.58)

Multiplying the equation in (
4.54
) by
v
, then integrating over
\Omega
yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{\Omega}vv_{t}dx\right)+\left\|v\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{f}v^{2}+\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq c_{6}\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(4.59)

Multiplying (
4.59
) by
\frac{1}{2}
, then adding (
4.58
) to the resultant, we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\widetilde{E}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|v\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}\leq(c_{6}^{2}+\frac{c_{6}}{2})\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

(4.60)

where

\widetilde{E}=\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\|v\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}vv_{t}dx.

Adding
\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
on both sides of (
4.60
) yields

\frac{d}{dt}\widetilde{E}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|v\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq(c_{6}^{2}+\frac{c_{6}}{2}+1)\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(4.61)

By Young’s inequality, we get

\widetilde{E}\leq\frac{5}{4}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\|v\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(4.62)

Note that there is
c_{7}=\frac{2}{5}
such that

c_{7}\left(\frac{5}{4}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\|v\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|v\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}+\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(4.63)

By (
4.61
) and (
4.63
), we obtain that

\frac{d}{dt}\widetilde{E}+c_{7}\widetilde{E}\leq(c_{6}^{2}+\frac{c_{6}}{2}+1)\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Since
\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}}
exists polynomial estimation, then by the proof of Theorem
3.2
, we can similarly deduce that, there is
c_{8}>0
,
\gamma=\frac{1}{1-2\theta}
and
T_{1}>0
such that

\widetilde{E}(t)\leq c_{8}^{2}(1+t)^{-2\gamma},\quad t\geq T_{1}.

(4.64)

By the definition of
\widetilde{E}
, we get

\displaystyle\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\|v\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq c_{8}^{2}(1+t)^{-2\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}vv_{t}dx

\displaystyle\leq c_{8}^{2}(1+t)^{-2\gamma}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

By the polynomial estimation of
\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}}
, there is
c_{9}>0
such that

\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\|v\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}^{2}\leq c_{9}^{2}(1+t)^{-2\gamma}.

Thus

\displaystyle\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq c_{9}(1+t)^{-\gamma}

\displaystyle\left\|v\right\|_{H_{D}^{2}}\leq c_{9}(1+t)^{-\gamma}.

Adding the two inequality yields (
4.53
). Theorem
4.3
is proved.
∎

Theorem
1.2
is completly proved.

4.5
Comparison with parabolic MEMS equation

In this subsection, we summarize the two types of problems addressed in Section 3 and 4, highlighting their similarities and differences.

Similarities:

1.

Both types of equations can be formulated as classical Cauchy problems:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\dot{u}+Au=f(u),\\
u(0)=u_{0}.\end{array}\right.

Furthermore, a solution-defined
C_{0}
-semigroup exists:

u(t)=S(t)u_{0}.

2.

Both problems are approached using the same methodology:

Step 1.
Prove the problem defines a gradient system. Utilize properties of gradient systems to establish that the
\omega
-limit set is non-empty and consists of steady-state solutions.

Step 2.
Prove the Lojasiewicz-Simon inequality corresponding to the problem.

Step 3.
Construct an auxiliary function. Apply the Lojasiewicz-Simon inequality to prove convergence of the solution in
L^{2}
. Leverage the relative compactness of the trajectory to extend this convergence to spaces of higher regularity.

Step 4.
Utilize the differential inequality satisfied by the auxiliary function to derive the convergence rate in
L^{2}
.

Step 5.
Utilize the differential inequality satisfied by a new energy functional to extend the convergence rate from
L^{2}
to spaces of higher regularity.

Differences:

1.

When proving the relative compactness of the trajectory, the hyperbolic problem cannot boost the solution’s regularity by leveraging
a priori
estimates for elliptic problems in the same way the parabolic problem can. Consequently, we decompose the operator semigroup and employ Webb’s Theorem
[
25
]
to establish relative compactness of the trajectory.

2.

For a fixed time
t
, the solution spaces differ: the parabolic problem considers
H_{D}^{4}(\Omega)
, while the hyperbolic problem considers
H_{D}^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
. Additionally, the spaces in which the expression
-Au+f(u)
resides within the respective Lojasiewicz-Simon inequalities differ:
L^{2}(\Omega)
for the parabolic problem and
(H_{D}^{2}(\Omega))^{{}^{\prime}}
(the dual space of
H_{D}^{2}(\Omega)
) for the hyperbolic problem.

3.

Due to the favorable regularity of solutions to the parabolic problem, multiplying both sides of the equation by
u_{t}
and integrating over the domain
\Omega
directly yields the desired energy functional. However, for the hyperbolic problem, the insufficient solution regularity necessitates proving the energy dissipation identity using a density argument.
