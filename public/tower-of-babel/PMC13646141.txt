Balancing accuracy, completeness, and efficiency for rice 3D reconstruction through CBAM-UNet-based multi-view segmentation and camera configuration optimization

Haoyang Zhou, Rongjie Chen, Hao Wang, Minglu Li, Yongkang Teng, Shenghao Ye, Menglei Wei, Kun Yu, Pingping Fang, Jing Cao, Fanglin Zhu, Wenyu Zhang, Ting Sun, Min Jiang

Abstract

Multi-view 3D reconstruction has been widely applied in plant phenotyping, but the complex canopy structure of rice plants poses significant challenges for reconstruction accuracy, completene7ss, and efficiency. In this study, we developed an optimized workflow for 3D reconstruction of rice using the self-developed Metatlas V1 multi-view imaging platform combined with the COLMAP + OpenMVS pipeline. A CBAM-UNet-based image segmentation model was used to extract plant regions from complex backgrounds, outperforming thresholding, U-Net, and U-Net++, and increasing the number of reconstructed points. Camera configuration optimization was performed by systematically combining equidistant and greedy strategies, resulting in an optimal six-camera setup (#2, #3, #4, #6, #7, and #8, corresponding to −30°, −15°, 0°, +30°, +45°, and +60° relative to the horizontal viewpoint), which provided complementary views of the canopy inner structure and stem base. This configuration reduced the average nearest-neighbor distance to 0.037–0.066 cm across different varieties and growth stages compared with the full 11-camera setup, retained 73–76% of the points, and decreased reconstruction duration by approximately 70–80%. The strong agreement between point-cloud-derived and manually measured plant height and canopy width (R2 = 0.989 and 0.946, respectively) supported the accuracy of the point-cloud-derived phenotypic measurements. Overall, integrating image segmentation with camera-configuration optimization offers an accurate and efficient solution for high-throughput 3D phenotyping of rice using Metatlas V1, balancing reconstruction accuracy, point-cloud completeness, and computational efficiency, and may provide a methodological reference for camera-configuration design in other rotational multi-view phenotyping platforms.

Introduction

Recent advances in agricultural science and information technologies have propelled plant phenotyping into the high-throughput era (; ). High-throughput phenotyping (HTP) enables rapid, non-destructive acquisition of extensive data on plant morphological, physiological, and biochemical traits, thereby establishing a robust framework for crop growth monitoring, germplasm screening, and genotype-phenotype association studies (; ; ; ). A wide range of optical sensing approaches, including RGB, multispectral, near-infrared (NIR), fluorescence, and hyperspectral imaging, have been extensively utilized for HTP (; ). Moreover, emerging wearable sensor technologies have facilitated in situ and continuous phenotyping (; ). Nevertheless, the phenotypic information derived from these approaches remains predominantly two-dimensional (2D) at the spatial scale of the target crop, limiting their capacity to accurately capture canopy architecture such as leaf inclination and azimuth angle (; ).

In recent years, both terrestrial and airborne LiDAR technologies have been widely employed for three-dimensional (3D) crop reconstruction; however, their relatively high equipment cost, operational complexity, and the sensitivity of point-cloud quality to scanning geometry and canopy occlusion may constrain their broader adoption in agriculture (; ; ). With the continual reduction in industrial camera costs and the rapid advancement of computational capabilities, multi-view imaging has emerged as an accurate and cost-effective alternative for crop 3D reconstruction (; ). Multi-view reconstruction techniques can be broadly classified into explicit modeling, which represents scenes through defined geometric data structures such as point clouds, meshes, or voxels exemplified by the Structure from Motion and Multi-View Stereo (SfM-MVS) pipeline (), and implicit modeling, which encodes geometry using mathematical functions or neural representations as in Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) (). The SfM-MVS pipeline generally includes feature detection, keypoint correspondence, identifying geometrically consistent matches, structure from motion, scale and georeferencing, refinement of parameter values, and multi-view stereo image matching algorithms (). Compared with implicit methods, the SfM-MVS pipeline offers higher interpretability and benefits from a well-established software ecosystem, rendering it the predominant approach for plant 3D phenotyping ().

Despite its widespread adoption, achieving high-precision and computationally efficient SfM-MVS reconstruction remains challenging. Image preprocessing, feature detection and matching, depth-map fusion, and pipeline integration are employed to enhance the reconstruction accuracy and computational efficiency of the SfM-MVS pipeline (). Among these, image preprocessing is a critical step for ensuring input image quality and robust feature matching, encompassing both image segmentation and camera configuration. Previous studies have shown that denoising, background subtraction, and region-of-interest (ROI) extraction can significantly improve dense point-cloud yield and reconstruction quality (; ). The rice canopy, characterized by extensive tillering and long, slender, overlapping leaves, presents greater challenges for 3D reconstruction. Compared to crops such as maize () and soybean (), rice 3D reconstruction requires more viewpoints and higher-quality images, which inevitably increases computational demands and reconstruction time. Therefore, it is crucial to balance reconstruction accuracy and efficiency through image segmentation and optimization of camera poses and numbers.

To address these challenges, we acquired high-quality multi-view images covering a wide range of viewpoints for rice plants at different growth stages using the self-developed Metatlas V1 platform and conducted an optimization study of the image preprocessing workflow. A CBAM-UNet segmentation network was developed by embedding channel and spatial attention modules (CBAM) into the U-Net skip connections to enhance salient feature extraction and fine-detail representation, thereby improving segmentation accuracy and robustness under complex backgrounds. Camera configurations, including variations in poses and numbers, were compared and optimized using Equidistant and Greedy Algorithm strategies. Our study provides an effective approach for balancing reconstruction accuracy and efficiency in SfM-MVS pipeline-based rice 3D reconstruction.

Materials and methods

Experiment design

The experiment was carried out from May to October 2024 at Jiangsu Academy of Agricultural Sciences Wuxi Branch (120.29°E, 31.63°N). Four rice cultivars with distinct plant architectures were used as experimental materials: Hejiang18, Zhongzu143, Nanjing9108, and Guangluai4. Rice seeds were sown on May 15 and grown under dry nursery conditions until the three-leaf stage. On June 18, the seedlings were transplanted into plastic pots (30 cm in height and 33 cm in outer diameter), each containing 15 kg of dry clay loam. Each pot contained one hill consisting of a single plant. Nine plants were grown for each cultivar, resulting in 36 plants in total. At the heading stage, six plants per cultivar exhibiting normal and representative growth and no visible pest or disease damage were selected and labeled R1–R6. These 24 plants, each considered an independent biological replicate, were imaged at both the heading and maturity stages. At the end of tillering, ineffective tillers were suppressed by midseason drainage, and thereafter a 2–5 cm water depth was maintained until one week before maturity, when irrigation was stopped. Prior to transplanting, 8 g of compound fertilizer (15-15-15) was applied per pot as basal fertilizer. Subsequently, 1.9 g of urea was applied as tillering fertilizer, and at the jointing stage, 1.9 g of urea and 1 g of KCl were applied as panicle fertilizer, corresponding to field application rates of 20 kg N, 8 kg P2O5, and 12 kg K2O per mu. Weed, pest, and disease management followed local rice cultivation practices to minimize biotic and abiotic stresses.

Multi-view imaging

To acquire multi-view images of rice plants, we employed the self-developed crop 3D phenotyping platform Metatlas V1 (). The platform consists of a base, a rotary unit (including gears, a turntable, and an imaging arm), camera modules, an industrial computer (IPC), a programmable logic controller (PLC), a servo motor, and a 48 V DC power supply (). The imaging arm comprises a 2.1-m-high perforated vertical column and adjustable horizontal telescopic rods with lengths ranging from 0.5 to 1.3 m. In the configuration used in this study, the arm accommodated 11 cameras mounted at different heights along the column. The correspondence between the camera position indices shown in and their viewing angles is provided in . Camera positions were indexed from 0 to 10, representing a total of 11 camera positions. Each camera module features a 50-megapixel sensor with autofocus, auto-exposure, and rolling shutter, and an imaging distance ranging from 10 cm to infinity.

The rotation parameters of the platform (e.g., motor speed, direction, and angular displacement per image) are controlled in real time by the self-developed software via PLC communication. At each predefined azimuthal position, the system automatically triggers the cameras to capture multi-view image sequences, after which the platform rotates to the next position. In this study, all cameras captured images at horizontal intervals of 15°, with the platform stopping at each position to acquire multi-view image sequences, resulting in a total of 24 captures. The control system coordinates turntable rotation, image acquisition, data storage, and transmission to enable automated multi-view image acquisition.

Multi-view image segmentation

Dataset preparation

Multi-view images of rice plants were manually annotated using the LabelMe software (MIT CSAIL, USA) (). Polygonal masks were drawn around each rice plant to precisely delineate plant regions from the background (). The annotated images were converted into binary masks, where the pixel values of rice plant regions were set to 1, and those of the background were set to 0 (). The resulting dataset comprised 600 annotated images from 24 plants, with six plants per cultivar imaged at both growth stages. For each cultivar–stage combination, the training set contained 60 images from plants R1–R5. Specifically, 55 images were obtained by imaging each plant at one randomly selected horizontal viewing position using all 11 vertical camera positions (#0–#10), and five additional images of R1 were obtained at a second horizontal viewing position using camera positions #1, #3, #4, #6, and #9. This resulted in 480 training images.

Plant R6 of each cultivar was used as the validation set. For each cultivar–stage combination, 11 images of R6 were acquired at one horizontal viewing position using all camera positions (#0–#10), and four additional images were acquired at a second horizontal viewing position using positions #1, #4, #6, and #9, yielding 15 validation images per combination and 120 validation images in total. The dataset was therefore partitioned into training and validation sets at the plant level, yielding an 80:20 training-to-validation ratio by image count. All images from R1–R5 were assigned to the training set, whereas all images from R6 were assigned to the validation set, ensuring plant-level separation between the two subsets. The same fixed partition was used for all segmentation models, and the detailed dataset composition is provided in .

Segmentation model

To address the challenges of variable plant morphology, complex backgrounds, and illumination differences in rice canopy imagery, we adopted a CBAM-UNet segmentation model (). The architecture is based on the classical U-Net network (), which consists of an encoder–decoder structure and skip connections, and is widely used in semantic segmentation tasks. The standard U-Net was enhanced by integrating the Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) ) into each skip connection (). The CBAM module consists of a Channel Attention Module (CAM) and a Spatial Attention Module (SAM), which sequentially refine feature maps by emphasizing informative channels and critical spatial regions, thereby improving segmentation accuracy under varying illumination and background conditions ().

For comparison, three additional segmentation methods were tested: simple thresholding, U-Net, and U-Net++ (). Thresholding distinguished plant regions based on pixel color values. U-Net utilized an encoder–decoder framework with skip connections to capture plant structures across multiple scales. U-Net++ further extended U-Net through dense, multi-level skip connections between encoder and decoder pathways, enhancing feature propagation and fusion for more accurate representation of fine leaf structures and complex canopy morphology.

Model implementation

The segmentation model was implemented in Python 3.8 using the PyTorch 1.10.0 framework with CUDA 11.3 support. All experiments were performed on a Windows 11 workstation equipped with an Intel® CoreTM i9-14900KF CPU (3.20 GHz), 128 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA RTX 4070 GPU (12 GB VRAM). During segmentation, the original RGB images were loaded using OpenCV, resized to 512 × 512 pixels using bilinear interpolation, and converted from BGR to single-channel grayscale images using cv2.COLOR_BGR2GRAY. The grayscale values were converted to float32 and retained in the 0–255 range without normalization or test-time augmentation. The output logits were directly thresholded at 0.5 to generate binary masks (plant = 255; background = 0), which were restored to the original resolution using nearest-neighbor interpolation. During model development, an initial CBAM-UNet run was conducted for 80 epochs to assess convergence. The model was trained using the RMSProp optimizer with an adaptive learning rate initialized at 0.00001 and a batch size of 1. To train the segmentation model, the Binary Cross-Entropy with Logits Loss was adopted, which combines the sigmoid activation and binary cross-entropy loss in a numerically stable formulation. Image post-processing

The segmentation outputs were binary masks. During inference, a copy of each original 6120 × 8160-pixel RGB image was processed as described in Section 2.3.3 to generate a 512 × 512 single-channel input without preserving the original aspect ratio. Each predicted binary mask was subsequently resized to 6120 × 8160 pixels using nearest-neighbor interpolation and applied pixelwise to the corresponding original RGB image. Pixels outside the predicted plant region were set to black, whereas the original RGB values within the plant region were retained. Thus, the outputs of this step were masked RGB images rather than binary images, and these masked RGB images were used as inputs to the SfM-MVS pipeline. Because the RGB images used for reconstruction were not resized or cropped, they retained the original dimensions, aspect ratio, and pixel coordinate system; therefore, no additional rescaling of the camera intrinsics was required.

3D reconstruction and denoising

Images of four rice varieties at the heading and maturity stages, which were not included in the training or validation sets, were used for 3D reconstruction of the rice plants. The reconstruction was performed using an open-source pipeline combining COLMAP (version 3.10) () for sparse reconstruction and camera pose estimation, and OpenMVS (version 2.1.0; https://github.com/cdcseacave/openMVS) for dense MVS reconstruction (). All reconstruction steps were performed using default parameters. Keypoints and descriptors were first extracted from the masked RGB images, and feature correspondences were established across multiple views. COLMAP used the SIMPLE_RADIAL camera model; camera intrinsics and extrinsics were estimated and refined through bundle adjustment to generate a sparse 3D point cloud of the rice plant. The sparse reconstruction and estimated camera parameters were then fed into OpenMVS, where depth maps were computed for each view and subsequently fused to generate a dense 3D point cloud capturing the detailed structure of the plant canopy.

Dense point clouds often contained noise and small artifacts, which could interfere with downstream analyses. To address this, a two-step denoising procedure was applied (). First, point colors were converted from the RGB color space to the HSV color space using OpenCV, and points outside the predefined HSV ranges were removed. The thresholds were set to 0–179 for hue, 43–255 for saturation, and 140–255 for value, following the OpenCV HSV scale. Second, statistical outlier removal was performed using 20 nearest neighbors, and points whose mean distance to neighboring points exceeded 2.0 standard deviations from the global mean were removed. This workflow effectively produced clean and complete point clouds of individual rice plants, suitable for quantitative structural analysis and high-throughput 3D phenotyping.

Camera configuration optimization

Multi-view images captured by 11 cameras on the Metatlas V1 platform were used to optimize camera number and pose. Cameras were numbered #0 to #10 from bottom to top, with camera #4 corresponding to the horizontal (0°) viewpoint. Adjacent cameras were separated by 15°, giving elevation angles of -60°, -45°, -30°, -15°, 0°, +15°, +30°, +45°, +60°, +75°, and +90°for cameras #0 to #10, respectively. Candidate configurations were generated using equidistant and greedy strategies. Three evaluation dimensions—accuracy, completeness, and efficiency—were considered. Within the camera-configuration comparison, accuracy was operationally assessed as relative geometric agreement with the internal 11-camera reference using the average nearest-neighbor distance (ANND), rather than as absolute geometric accuracy. Completeness was indirectly assessed using total point count as a descriptive indicator of point-cloud yield, rather than as an independent metric directly measuring structural completeness. Computational efficiency was evaluated using reconstruction time.

Equidistant strategy

Because feature extraction and matching deteriorate when the angular gap between adjacent cameras exceeds 60° (), the maximum interval was set to 60°. Under the equidistant strategy, cameras were arranged so that the angle between each selected camera and its neighboring camera, relative to the center of the imaging area, was equal. Four interval angles of 15°, 30°, 45°, and 60° were selected to construct camera configurations. The number of cameras was gradually increased from 1 to 11, forming 11 primary groups of configurations. Within each group, camera IDs were selected according to the equidistant strategy based on the interval angle. Using this systematic approach, a total of 116 distinct camera configurations were generated, reflecting the practically achievable positions of the 11 cameras ().

Greedy strategy

The greedy algorithm operates on the principle of making the locally optimal choice at each step with the aim of achieving a globally optimal solution through a sequence of such decisions (; ). The general procedure involves establishing a problem model, defining the greedy selection criteria, constructing the solution set, validating feasibility, and analyzing computational complexity. Unlike the conventional equidistant strategy, the greedy strategy emphasizes local optimization. In this study, the algorithm first determined the optimal position for a single camera, then, based on this selection, identified the optimal two-camera combination. For each subsequent camera, the algorithm iteratively selected the position that, when added to the previously retained stage-wise best-performing camera set, maximized overall reconstruction performance. This stepwise optimization strategy reduced the search and reconstruction burden while allowing each additional camera to be evaluated for its contribution to reconstruction performance. Using this approach, a total of 61 feasible greedy candidate configurations were evaluated (). The complete pseudocode, including the tie-handling and stopping rules, is provided in .

Comprehensive quantitative evaluation

For each candidate configuration containing 1–10 cameras, the point cloud reconstructed using all 11 cameras was used as the corresponding internal reference. To recover its metric scale, an auxiliary point cloud was reconstructed from the corresponding original RGB images without plant segmentation, thereby retaining the cultivation pot. The reconstructed outer diameter of the pot (dpot) was measured across its outer rim in CloudCompare, and the auxiliary cloud was uniformly scaled by 33/dpot, based on the known pot outer diameter of 33 cm. The plant region of the centimeter-scaled auxiliary cloud was then used to transfer the metric scale to the plant-only 11-camera reference cloud, after which each candidate cloud was registered to the corresponding scaled reference cloud. Both the scale-transfer and candidate-to-reference registrations were performed in CloudCompare. Initial alignment was performed by manually selecting at least four pairs of corresponding feature points with the Adjust scale option enabled, followed by refinement using the iterative closest point (ICP) algorithm (). Validation of point-cloud-derived morphological traits

After the optimal imaging configuration and reconstruction workflow were identified, three-dimensional point clouds of rice plants were reconstructed using the selected protocol to validate the accuracy of the point-cloud-derived morphological traits. Results

Performance of CBAM-UNet segmentation model

The training loss of the proposed CBAM-UNet model decreased steadily from 0.2098 at the first epoch to approximately 0.0401 at the 80th epoch, as measured by Binary Cross-Entropy with Logits Loss (). The loss curve plateaued around the 60th epoch, indicating that the model had converged. The validation-set metrics, including mIoU, mPA, and F1, obtained from the 60-, 70-, and 80-epoch checkpoints are summarized in . These metrics showed limited variation across the three checkpoints. Based on the convergence of the training loss and the validation-set performance, the 70-epoch checkpoint was selected for subsequent analyses.

The CBAM-UNet model achieved the highest values for all three semantic segmentation metrics among the four methods evaluated (). Specifically, CBAM-UNet improved mIoU by 32.04, 0.46, and 1.12 percentage points, and mPA by 21.82, 0.34, and 0.65 percentage points, and F1 by 21.39, 0.39, and 0.64 percentage points compared with thresholding, U-Net, and U-Net++, respectively (). In the single-run evaluation reported in , CBAM-UNet achieved a plant-class IoU, precision, and recall of 90.56%, 97.12%, and 93.06%, respectively. Across three independent runs, its mean mIoU was 93.24 ± 0.18%, compared with 92.71 ± 0.40% for U-Net. Plant-class IoU and recall were higher by 0.75 and 0.98 percentage points, respectively, whereas plant-class precision was 0.20 percentage points lower (). In the seed-42 ablation, U-Net + CBAM achieved the highest mIoU, mPA, F1-score, plant-class IoU, and recall, whereas CAM or SAM alone did not improve mIoU over U-Net (). Relative to U-Net, U-Net + CBAM increased the parameter count only slightly from 13.394 to 13.438 M and FLOPs from 124.17 to 124.24 G, while peak GPU memory and inference time increased from 511.4 to 667.4 MB and from 46.37 to 68.08 ms image−1, respectively (). Taking Hejiang18, a compact-type rice, and Guangluai4, a spreading-type rice, as examples, provides a qualitative comparison of plant-structure preservation among the different segmentation methods. Thresholding yields fragmented results with substantial loss of plant structures. U-Net captures the main plant body but fails to preserve fine leaf details, resulting in omissions along leaf edges. U-Net++ improves the continuity of leaf structures relative to U-Net; however, small leaves and tips are still partially missed. In this qualitative comparison, CBAM-UNet better preserves the continuity of both major and fine plant structures, with clearly delineated leaf boundaries and fewer visually apparent missing or fragmented regions. These visual examples are intended to illustrate structural preservation, whereas the performance comparison is quantitatively supported by the mIoU, mPA, and F1-scores reported in . Using the COLMAP + OpenMVS 3D reconstruction framework, we further evaluated the effect of different image segmentation models on the completeness of 3D point clouds. Compared with Thresholding, U-Net, and U-Net++, CBAM-UNet increased the total number of reconstructed points by approximately 63%, 1%, and 2% on average, respectively (). Thus, the increase relative to thresholding was substantial, whereas the gains over U-Net and U-Net++ were modest.

Effect of camera configuration on 3D reconstruction

The ANNDs between the candidate point clouds and their corresponding internal reference point clouds reconstructed using all 11 cameras were analyzed. The coefficient of variation (CV) was high for small camera numbers, reaching 35.32% for a single camera and up to 141.35% for five-camera combinations. From six cameras onward, CV decreased sharply, ranging from 7.13% to 22.93% (). The median ANND decreased progressively with increasing camera number, from 0.635 cm for a single camera to 0.037 cm for ten cameras, indicating progressively closer agreement with the 11-camera internal reference (). Optimal camera configuration

Under both the equidistant and greedy strategies, optimal camera configurations were first evaluated based on ANND and total point count, respectively. The optimal camera IDs were identical for 1 and 7–11 cameras (, ). For 2 and 3 cameras, the equidistant strategy achieved lower ANND and higher point counts than the greedy strategy. In contrast, for 4 and 5 cameras, the greedy strategy outperformed the equidistant strategy in both metrics. For 6 cameras, the equidistant strategy produced lower ANND, whereas the point count was higher under the greedy strategy (, ). Subsequently, all configurations from the two strategies were combined, and the comprehensive score was used to quantify overall performance (). The reconstruction performance increased with the number of cameras from 1 to 6, reaching a maximum score of 0.813 at six cameras. The optimal camera configuration was #2, #3, #4, #6, #7, and #8 ().

To further validate the robustness of the optimal configuration, it was tested across four rice varieties at both the heading and maturity stages using independent data. Visualization of the ANND distribution showed that larger distances mainly occurred at the bottom of the plant and at leaf tips within the inner tillers (). The results of 3D reconstruction with the optimal six-camera configuration within the evaluated candidate set showed that the ANND ranged from 0.037 to 0.066 cm relative to the corresponding 11-camera internal references, indicating close agreement across cultivars and growth stages (). The total number of points reconstructed with six cameras ranged from approximately 4.073 million to 4.882 million, representing about 73.3–76.0% of the points generated using 11 cameras. Reconstruction duration with six cameras ranged from 4.0 to 5.5 min, compared to 18.0–22.0 min for 11 cameras, corresponding to a reduction of approximately 70–80% ().

Accuracy of phenotypic trait extraction using the optimal workflow

To evaluate the phenotypic measurement accuracy of the optimal imaging and reconstruction workflow, plant height and canopy width derived from the reconstructed point clouds were compared with the corresponding manual measurements (). Point-cloud-derived plant height showed close agreement with the manually measured values, with a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.989 and a root mean square error (RMSE) of 2.082 cm. The observations were distributed closely around the 1:1 line across the evaluated range of plant heights.

Point-cloud-derived canopy width also showed strong agreement with the manual measurements, yielding an R2 of 0.946 and an RMSE of 4.604 cm. However, the canopy-width estimates exhibited greater dispersion around the 1:1 line than the plant-height estimates. Overall, the optimal workflow enabled accurate extraction of both morphological traits, with higher measurement accuracy obtained for plant height than for canopy width.

Discussion

Comparison of different segmentation models

Compared with thresholding, all deep learning-based segmentation methods, including U-Net, U-Net++, and CBAM-UNet, show clear advantages (, ). Thresholding relies solely on pixel intensity differences, which makes it highly sensitive to complex backgrounds, varying illumination, and subtle color variations in rice canopy images (). In the present study, lighting conditions differed across viewpoints (), environmental backgrounds contained complex non-plant elements (), and rice color characteristics varied at different growth stages (). As a result, thresholding produced fragmented segmentations and failed to maintain continuous leaf structures, leading to considerable loss of structural information. In contrast, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) such as U-Net and its variants can learn hierarchical and contextual features from training data, enabling robust segmentation under variable lighting and complex morphology, which markedly improves overall segmentation completeness ().

Baseline U-Net, widely used in image segmentation, employs an encoder-decoder architecture with skip connections but has limitations in rice plant segmentation (). Conventional convolution operations in U-Net do not sufficiently capture inter-channel dependencies, making it difficult to emphasize informative regions and suppress background noise, which often leads to indistinct boundaries and omission of fine leaf structures. U-Net++ introduces nested skip connections to improve structural continuity and reduce information loss, enhancing segmentation of larger structures; however, small leaves and leaf tips are still partially missed. In contrast, CBAM-UNet incorporates the CBAM into each skip connection, consisting of a CAM and a SAM. The CAM adaptively weights feature channels based on global average and max pooling, strengthening informative channels, while the SAM generates spatial attention maps by combining pooled spatial features through a convolution, guiding the network to focus on critical regions (). This attention-guided feature fusion may contribute to the visually improved preservation of both major structures and fine details, such as leaf edges and tips, shown in . The repeated-run and ablation results further showed that the combined CBAM variant achieved modest gains over U-Net in most evaluated metrics, whereas CAM or SAM alone did not improve mIoU (, ). Together with the small gains in whole-plant point count over U-Net and U-Net++, these findings indicate an incremental rather than substantial advantage.

Comparison of different camera configurations

When the number of cameras was small, the variation in ANND and point count was substantial (), indicating that different camera combinations strongly affected reconstruction results. For the configuration using one camera (24 azimuthal images), camera #7 at a 45° angle achieved the lowest ANND and highest point count (, ), which may be related to the balance between geometric information capture and perspective provided by this viewing angle. It may allow simultaneous observation of top and side surfaces, potentially providing richer 3D cues and moderate perspective distortion that may aid depth computation. This angle may also preserve stable texture features, potentially enhancing feature matching, and reduce occlusion by covering both frontal and partial side/top surfaces. These possible explanations require further targeted validation. Similarly, in UAV-based RGB imaging with a cross-circling oblique flight route, the tilt angle is generally set to 45° to ensure comprehensive canopy coverage and robust 3D reconstruction (; ).

When the number of cameras increased to two, the ANND decreased sharply, falling below the 0.2 cm threshold and indicating closer agreement with the 11-camera internal reference. This trend is consistent with the benefit of complementary views reported for binocular stereo vision-based plant reconstruction (). Across all configurations within two-camera group, camera pairs with a small inter-angle of 15° yielded the lowest ANND and the highest point count, which may be related to their greater image overlap and its potential contribution to stable feature correspondence and more reliable depth recovery (). As the number of cameras increased, the variation among different camera combinations gradually decreased, indicating that the influence of camera arrangement became less significant than the overall number of cameras. Meanwhile, geometric agreement with the 11-camera internal reference improved and point-cloud yield increased, but at the cost of longer reconstruction duration. Therefore, applying a weighting-based evaluation approach can effectively balance accuracy, completeness, and efficiency.

The optimal camera configuration identified in this study originated from the greedy strategy. Compared with the equidistant strategy, the greedy strategy effectively avoids redundant geometric information and unstable feature matching by adaptively selecting camera angles that contribute most to reconstruction quality. In the equidistant strategy, adjacent cameras often capture highly similar perspectives, leading to redundant depth cues and limited improvements in 3D constraints. Moreover, it neglects variations in canopy structure and illumination, potentially causing feature degradation in certain views. In contrast, the greedy strategy progressively optimizes camera selection based on the reconstruction performance of preceding steps, thereby enhancing geometric complementarity while avoiding ineffective or redundant views. This adaptive optimization may have contributed to the observed favorable balance among reconstruction accuracy, completeness, and computational efficiency.

Robustness of the optimal imaging and reconstruction workflow

The validation across four rice cultivars and two growth stages demonstrated that the optimal imaging and reconstruction workflow was robust for quantitative phenotyping. Point-cloud-derived plant height and canopy width agreed closely with manual measurements, with R2 values of 0.989 and 0.946 and RMSE values of 2.082 and 4.604 cm, respectively. These results indicate that the workflow preserved the major vertical and horizontal dimensions of rice plants with different canopy architectures and developmental states.

This morphological trait validation complements the geometric and computational metrics used for camera-configuration optimization. Although nearest-neighbor distance, point count, and reconstruction time describe reconstruction quality and efficiency, they do not directly confirm the accuracy of phenotypic measurements. The lower accuracy obtained for canopy width than for plant height may be partly attributed to residual background points that were not completely removed during point-cloud denoising. Because canopy width was determined from the maximum horizontal extent of the point cloud, even a small number of residual noise points near the canopy boundary could artificially enlarge the estimated width. Overall, the selected workflow achieved a favorable balance among reconstruction quality, processing efficiency, and phenotypic measurement accuracy. Nevertheless, the validation was based on 24 potted-plant samples acquired under controlled conditions. Further evaluation using larger populations, more diverse plant architectures, and field-grown rice is needed to assess its broader applicability.

Remaining challenges

The use of image segmentation to remove non-plant regions prevents independent calibration with a checkerboard for multi-view imaging. We rely on periodic calibration to determine the intrinsic and extrinsic parameters of the cameras. However, slight shifts in camera positions or angles may occur during rotation, and periodic checkerboard calibration may not fully capture these deviations, potentially introducing errors in the world coordinate system and affecting subsequent phenotypic measurements. To improve calibration reliability and enable evaluation of absolute geometric accuracy, it would be necessary to retain checkerboard or other reference markers alongside the plant pixels during segmentation, enabling accurate calibration without compromising plant feature extraction.

For rice individuals with dense tillers and severe leaf overlap, internal regions of the canopy, particularly the lower leaves, still exhibit holes or noisy points in the reconstructed point clouds (), indicating incomplete or noisy reconstruction in these areas. This reduction in local point-cloud quality is mainly due to occlusion and the restricted camera angles available to capture the bottom of the canopy. Point cloud completion techniques can partially address these gaps (; ), but their effectiveness depends on assumptions about plant structure and may introduce artifacts, so improving internal point cloud quality remains a key challenge.

The current camera configuration optimization was conducted using our self-developed 3D phenotyping platform, Metatlas V1. Since this optimization was specifically designed for the geometry, sensor properties, and imaging workflow of this system, it is uncertain whether the resulting optimal configuration can be directly applied to other multi-view imaging platforms, including UAV-based systems, handheld multi-camera arrays, or fixed field-based camera arrays. In addition, the study focused solely on rice. Crops with substantially different morphologies, such as tall maize with relatively simple canopy structures or shorter soybean with dense canopies, may produce different reconstruction outcomes. Consequently, the generalizability of the optimized camera configuration to other platforms and plant species remains uncertain and warrants further systematic evaluation. Nevertheless, the evaluation framework used here may provide a methodological reference for other multi-view systems by jointly considering relative geometric agreement, point-cloud yield, and computational efficiency.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed an optimized workflow for high-throughput 3D reconstruction of rice plants using the Metatlas V1 multi-view imaging platform. By integrating CBAM-UNet-based image segmentation with a comprehensive camera configuration optimization, which fused equidistant and greedy strategies, we achieved precise and efficient reconstruction of rice plants.

Compared with conventional thresholding, CBAM-UNet improved mIoU by 32.04 percentage points, mPA by 21.82 percentage points, and F1 by 21.39 percentage points. Its improvements over U-Net and U-Net++ were more modest, ranging from 0.46–1.12, 0.34–0.65, and 0.39–0.64 percentage points for mIoU, mPA, and F1, respectively. The model accurately delineated both major and fine leaf structures, preserving continuous leaf edges and tips while minimizing fragmented or missing regions. When used for 3D reconstruction based on segmented multi-view images, CBAM-UNet also yielded higher point counts compared with other models.

Camera configuration had a substantial impact on reconstruction performance. Increasing the number of cameras reduced the ANND and increased point count, while reconstruction time also increased. By combining equidistant and greedy strategies, an optimal six-camera configuration (#2, #3, #4, #6, #7, and #8, corresponding to −30°, −15°, 0°, +30°, +45°, and +60° relative to the horizontal viewpoint) was identified. This setup achieved a low ANND of 0.037–0.066 cm, retained 73–76% of the points produced by the full 11-camera arrangement, and reduced reconstruction duration by approximately 70–80%, effectively balancing reconstruction accuracy, point cloud completeness, and computational efficiency.
