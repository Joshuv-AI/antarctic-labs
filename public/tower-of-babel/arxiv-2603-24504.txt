1.
Introduction

The Hörmander–Bernhardsson extremal function
\varphi
is the unique entire function of
exponential type at most
\pi
such that
\varphi(0)=1
and

\|\varphi\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}
is minimal. Introduced by Hörmander and Bernhardsson

[
5
]
, it is the extremizer in the
L^{1}
point-evaluation problem for the
Paley–Wiener space
PW^{1}
; see also

[
1
,
4
]
for related extremal questions in
Paley–Wiener and Fourier analysis.

Bondarenko, Ortega-Cerdà, Radchenko, and Seip (BOCRS) developed a detailed analytic theory of

\varphi
. They showed that

\varphi(z)=\Phi(z)\Phi(-z),

identified the differential equation satisfied by
\Phi
, derived an odd inverse-power
expansion for the positive zeros
(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, and introduced the two-parameter
family of differential operators

L_{a,b}(f)(z):=z^{2}f^{\prime\prime}(z)+(2z-a)f^{\prime}(z)+b^{2}z^{2}f(z)

governing the associated spectral problem
[
3
]
. In
[
3
, §10.2–§10.3]
they
isolated two arithmetic conjectures. The first was proved in
[
6
]
. The main
purpose of the present paper is to prove the second. After the completion of the present manuscript, Danylo Radchenko informed the author that he and Wadim Zudilin have also obtained an independent proof of Conjecture 2, as part of a more general integrality theorem
[
8
]
. Their approach is different from the determinant-comparison method used here, while the present paper also emphasizes consequences related to the irrationality question for
L_{\tau}(1)
.

BOCRS observed that if
f
is a
\lambda
-eigenfunction of
L_{1,b}
, then the even
Taylor coefficients of
f(z)f(-z)
satisfy the three-term recurrence

u_{-1}=0,\qquad u_{0}=1,

u_{n+1}=\frac{4n+2}{n+1}\bigl(n(n+1)-\lambda\bigr)u_{n}+\frac{4n}{n+1}b^{2}u_{n-1}\qquad(n\geq 0).

Conjecture 2 in
[
3
, §10.3]
asserts that

u_{n}(b,\lambda)\in\mathbb{Z}[b^{2},\lambda]\qquad(n\geq 0).

To work universally, we set
x=b^{2}
and define

\widetilde{u}_{-1}=0,\qquad\widetilde{u}_{0}=1,

\widetilde{u}_{n+1}=\frac{4n+2}{n+1}\bigl(n(n+1)-\lambda\bigr)\widetilde{u}_{n}+\frac{4n}{n+1}x\,\widetilde{u}_{n-1}\qquad(n\geq 0).

The first terms are

\widetilde{u}_{1}=-2\lambda,\qquad\widetilde{u}_{2}=6\lambda^{2}-12\lambda+2x,\qquad\widetilde{u}_{3}=-20\lambda^{3}+160\lambda^{2}-12\lambda x-240\lambda+40x,

already illustrating the unexpected cancellation of denominators.

Our first main result proves that the entire two-parameter family
\widetilde{u}_{n}(x,\lambda)
is integral
before
specialization.

Theorem 1
.

For every
n\geq 0
one has

\widetilde{u}_{n}(x,\lambda)\in\mathbb{Z}[x,\lambda].

Consequently,

u_{n}(b,\lambda)=\widetilde{u}_{n}(b^{2},\lambda)\in\mathbb{Z}[b^{2},\lambda]\subset\mathbb{Z}[b,\lambda]\qquad(n\geq 0).

The second main result identifies the same sequence with exact finite BOCRS compressions. Writing

\widehat{u}_{n}(a,\lambda):=\widetilde{u}_{n}(a^{2},\lambda),

and letting
T_{n}(a,\lambda)
denote the tridiagonal matrix defined by

T_{0}(a,\lambda):=(-\lambda),

and, for
n\geq 1
,

(1.1)

T_{n}(a,\lambda):=\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda&\dfrac{a}{3}&0&\cdots&0\\
-a&2-\lambda&\dfrac{2a}{5}&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&-\dfrac{2a}{3}&6-\lambda&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\dfrac{na}{2n+1}\\
0&\cdots&0&-\dfrac{na}{2n-1}&n(n+1)-\lambda\end{pmatrix}.

Theorem 2

(Exact finite-compression identity)
.

For every
n\geq 0
,

\widehat{u}_{n+1}(a,\lambda)=\binom{2n+2}{n+1}\det T_{n}(a,\lambda).

Thus the BOCRS truncation scheme is not merely numerical: after the canonical
central-binomial scaling, it is exactly the characteristic-polynomial sequence of the
finite principal compressions.

The paper also derives two arithmetic consequences. The rational-slice theorem
(Theorem
16
) shows that if

F_{a,\lambda}(z):=\sum_{n\geq 0}\widehat{u}_{n}(a,\lambda)z^{2n}

is entire, nonpolynomial, and of exponential type at most
2a
, then
a^{2}
and

\lambda
are not both rational. Applied to the Hörmander–Bernhardsson branch, this yields
Corollary
18
:

\left(\frac{\pi}{4C}\right)^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad-\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2C}

are not both rational. Here
C
is the sharp point-evaluation constant and

L_{\tau}(s):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\tau_{n}^{s}}.

A second consequence is a coefficient-ring theorem. If

\varphi(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{2n},

then Corollary
22
shows that

c_{n}\in C^{n}\,\mathbb{Z}[\pi^{2},C,L_{\tau}(1)]\qquad(n\geq 0).

In particular, only even powers of
\pi
can occur in the Taylor coefficients of

\varphi
. This is obtained from a sharp weighted refinement of the universal integrality
statement: every monomial
x^{r}\lambda^{s}
occurring in
\widetilde{u}_{n}(x,\lambda)

satisfies

2r+s\leq n.

Method of proof

The point of the paper is not merely that denominators cancel. The proof identifies the
mechanism that forces the cancellation, and that mechanism is conceptually unexpected in the
BOCRS setting. Integrality phenomena of this kind are often associated with regular-singular
geometry, i.e., Picard–Fuchs equations, diagonals,
G
-functions, or related algebraic
structures. BOCRS explicitly emphasize that Conjecture 2 lies outside that framework: the
differential equation attached to
L_{a,b}
has irregular singularities at
0
and

\infty
, and the conjectured integrality therefore calls for a different explanation

[
3
, §4,§10.3]
. Our contribution is to show that the arithmetic structure is
governed instead by an exact finite-dimensional Legendre compression.

The proof begins by the sign reversal
x=-\beta^{2}
and the normalization

v_{n}(x,\lambda):=\frac{\widetilde{u}_{n}(x,\lambda)}{\binom{2n}{n}}.

After dividing by the central binomial coefficient, the recurrence becomes exactly the
continuant recurrence of a tridiagonal determinant arising from the action of

\mathcal{L}+\beta t-\lambda,\qquad\mathcal{L}:=-\frac{d}{dt}\Bigl((1-t^{2})\frac{d}{dt}\Bigr),

on the scaled Legendre basis
Q_{n}(t)=2^{n}P_{n}(t)
. The same operator, written in the monomial
basis, gives a matrix with entries in
\mathbb{Z}[\beta,\lambda]
. Comparing the two determinant
realizations via change of basis yields

\det M_{n}=-\widetilde{u}_{n}(-\beta^{2},\lambda),

and hence integrality. In particular, the central binomial coefficient is not an auxiliary
normalization: it is the exact change-of-basis factor between the Legendre and monomial
compressions.

Taken together with
[
6
]
, the two arithmetic conjectures isolated in

[
3
, §10.2–§10.3]
are now resolved.

We also emphasize that the BOCRS recurrence is holonomic in the usual sense: after clearing the
denominator
n+1
, it becomes a second-order
P
-recurrence, and its ordinary generating
series is
D
-finite. However, this formal holonomic structure plays no essential role in the
proof. The arithmetic content of Theorem
1
is not a consequence of holonomicity
alone; it comes from an exact finite-dimensional Legendre compression identity, which is
particularly unexpected here because the underlying BOCRS differential equation has irregular
singularities.

Organization of the paper

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
proves
Theorem
1
by the Legendre determinant comparison.
Section
3
develops the BOCRS truncation and continued-fraction
formalism, proves Theorem
15
, and derives the rational-slice
consequences. Section
4
proves the weighted support bound and the
coefficient-ring theorem.

2.
Legendre determinant proof of Theorem
1

2.1.
The universal recurrence

We work with the BOCRS recurrence

(2.1)

u_{-1}=0,\qquad u_{0}=1,

(2.2)

u_{n+1}=\frac{4n+2}{n+1}\bigl(n(n+1)-\lambda\bigr)u_{n}+\frac{4n}{n+1}b^{2}u_{n-1}\qquad(n\geq 0).

Since the parameter
b
appears only through
b^{2}
, it is convenient to introduce a new
variable
x
and define

(2.3)

\widetilde{u}_{-1}=0,\qquad\widetilde{u}_{0}=1,

(2.4)

\widetilde{u}_{n+1}=\frac{4n+2}{n+1}\bigl(n(n+1)-\lambda\bigr)\widetilde{u}_{n}+\frac{4n}{n+1}x\,\widetilde{u}_{n-1}\qquad(n\geq 0).

By uniqueness of recursively defined sequences in
\mathbb{Q}[x,\lambda]
,

u_{n}(b,\lambda)=\widetilde{u}_{n}(b^{2},\lambda)\qquad(n\geq-1).

Thus Theorem
1
implies Conjecture 2 from
[
3
, §10.3]
.

The proof has four steps. First, by Lemma
3
it is enough to prove
integrality after the sign reversal
x=-\beta^{2}
. Second, after dividing by the central
binomial coefficient
C_{n}=\binom{2n}{n}
, the recurrence becomes exactly the continuant
recurrence of a tridiagonal determinant
D_{n}
arising from the action of

\mathcal{L}+\beta t-\lambda
on the scaled Legendre basis
Q_{n}=2^{n}P_{n}
. Third, the same
operator gives an integral matrix
M_{n}
in the monomial basis. Fourth, comparison of the
two determinant realizations through change of basis yields

\det M_{n}=-\widetilde{u}_{n}(-\beta^{2},\lambda),

which forces integrality. The remaining subsections carry out these four steps.

2.2.
Reduction to a quadratic specialization

The first step is a simple algebraic lemma.

Lemma 3
.

Let
f(x,\lambda)\in\mathbb{Q}[x,\lambda]
. If

f(-\beta^{2},\lambda)\in\mathbb{Z}[\beta,\lambda],

then in fact

f(x,\lambda)\in\mathbb{Z}[x,\lambda].

Proof.

Write

f(x,\lambda)=\sum_{r=0}^{m}a_{r}(\lambda)x^{r},\qquad a_{r}(\lambda)\in\mathbb{Q}[\lambda].

Then

f(-\beta^{2},\lambda)=\sum_{r=0}^{m}(-1)^{r}a_{r}(\lambda)\beta^{2r}.

Now
\mathbb{Z}[\beta,\lambda]=\mathbb{Z}[\lambda][\beta]
is a polynomial ring in the indeterminate
\beta
over
\mathbb{Z}[\lambda]
. Hence the coefficients of a polynomial in
\beta
are unique. Since
f(-\beta^{2},\lambda)
lies in
\mathbb{Z}[\lambda][\beta]
, each coefficient of
\beta^{2r}
must belong to
\mathbb{Z}[\lambda]
and each odd coefficient must vanish. Therefore

(-1)^{r}a_{r}(\lambda)\in\mathbb{Z}[\lambda]\qquad(0\leq r\leq m),

so
a_{r}(\lambda)\in\mathbb{Z}[\lambda]
for all
r
. Thus
f(x,\lambda)\in\mathbb{Z}[x,\lambda]
.
∎

By Lemma
3
, Theorem
1
will follow once we prove that

\widetilde{u}_{n}(-\beta^{2},\lambda)\in\mathbb{Z}[\beta,\lambda]\qquad(n\geq 0).

We now turn to the determinantal construction that yields this fact.

2.3.
Scaled Legendre polynomials

Let
P_{n}(t)
denote the classical Legendre polynomial of degree
n
, normalized by the generating function

\sum_{n\geq 0}P_{n}(t)\,z^{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2tz+z^{2}}}.

Equivalently,
P_{n}
is given by Rodrigues’ formula

P_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{2^{n}n!}\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}(t^{2}-1)^{n}.

For our purposes it is convenient to use the scaled polynomials

Q_{n}(t):=2^{n}P_{n}(t)\qquad(n\geq 0).

Then
Q_{n}(t)\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
, and one has the explicit expansion

Q_{n}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}\binom{2n-2k}{n}\,t^{\,n-2k}.

In particular, the leading coefficient of
Q_{n}
is

C_{n}:=\binom{2n}{n}.

The first values are

\displaystyle Q_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle Q_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=2t,

\displaystyle Q_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=6t^{2}-2,

\displaystyle Q_{3}(t)

\displaystyle=20t^{3}-12t,

\displaystyle Q_{4}(t)

\displaystyle=70t^{4}-60t^{2}+6,

\displaystyle Q_{5}(t)

\displaystyle=252t^{5}-280t^{3}+60t.

The Legendre differential equation becomes

-\frac{d}{dt}\!\left((1-t^{2})\frac{d}{dt}Q_{n}(t)\right)=n(n+1)\,Q_{n}(t).

Thus, if we set

\mathcal{L}:=-\frac{d}{dt}\!\left((1-t^{2})\frac{d}{dt}\right),

then

\mathcal{L}Q_{n}=n(n+1)Q_{n}.

Set
Q_{-1}:=0
for convenience. The three-term recurrence for the scaled family is

(n+1)Q_{n+1}(t)=2(2n+1)t\,Q_{n}(t)-4nQ_{n-1}(t)\qquad(n\geq 1),

with initial conditions

Q_{0}(t)=1,\qquad Q_{1}(t)=2t.

With the convention
Q_{-1}=0
, this can be rewritten as

t\,Q_{n}(t)=\frac{n+1}{2(2n+1)}\,Q_{n+1}(t)+\frac{2n}{2n+1}\,Q_{n-1}(t)\qquad(n\geq 0).

It follows that

(\mathcal{L}+\beta t-\lambda)Q_{n}=\bigl(n(n+1)-\lambda\bigr)Q_{n}+\frac{2n\beta}{2n+1}Q_{n-1}+\frac{(n+1)\beta}{2(2n+1)}Q_{n+1}\qquad(n\geq 0).

This is the tridiagonal action used in the determinant comparison argument.

Finally,
Q_{n}
has the parity property

Q_{n}(-t)=(-1)^{n}Q_{n}(t),

so
Q_{n}
is even for
n
even and odd for
n
odd. For standard formulas for Legendre polynomials, see, for example, DLMF, Chapter 18, especially §§18.5, 18.8, and 18.9.

We have shown the following proposition.

Proposition 4
.

For every
n\geq 0
the polynomial
Q_{n}
belongs to
\mathbb{Z}[t]
and has the explicit expansion

(2.5)

Q_{n}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}\binom{2n-2k}{n}t^{n-2k}.

Also set
Q_{-1}:=0
. In particular, the leading coefficient of
Q_{n}
is

(2.6)

C_{n}:=\binom{2n}{n}.

Moreover, with

(2.7)

\mathcal{L}:=-\frac{d}{dt}\Bigl((1-t^{2})\frac{d}{dt}\Bigr),

one has

(2.8)

\mathcal{L}Q_{n}=n(n+1)Q_{n},

and the three-term recurrence

(2.9)

(n+1)Q_{n+1}=2(2n+1)tQ_{n}-4nQ_{n-1}\qquad(n\geq 1).

Equivalently,

(2.10)

tQ_{n}=\frac{n+1}{2(2n+1)}Q_{n+1}+\frac{2n}{2n+1}Q_{n-1}\qquad(n\geq 0).

Consequently,

(2.11)

(\mathcal{L}+\beta t-\lambda)Q_{n}=(n(n+1)-\lambda)Q_{n}+\frac{2n\beta}{2n+1}Q_{n-1}+\frac{(n+1)\beta}{2(2n+1)}Q_{n+1}\qquad(n\geq 0).

2.4.
An integral determinant

Fix

R:=\mathbb{Z}[\beta,\lambda].

For
m\geq 0
we denote by
R[t]_{\leq m}
the
R
-module of polynomials in
t
of degree at most
m
.

For every integer
n\geq 1
define an
R
-linear map

(2.12)

\Phi_{n}:R[t]_{\leq n-1}\oplus R\longrightarrow R[t]_{\leq n},\qquad\Phi_{n}(f,\mu):=(\mathcal{L}+\beta t-\lambda)f-\mu Q_{n}.

Let
e:=(0,1)\in R[t]_{\leq n-1}\oplus R
. We consider the monomial bases

\mathcal{B}_{n}^{\mathrm{dom}}=(1,t,\dots,t^{n-1},e),\qquad\mathcal{B}_{n}^{\mathrm{cod}}=(1,t,\dots,t^{n}).

Let
M_{n}
be the matrix of
\Phi_{n}
with respect to these bases.

Lemma 5
.

For every
n\geq 1
, the matrix
M_{n}
has entries in
R
. In particular,

\det M_{n}\in R.

Proof.

For
m\geq 0
one computes directly from (
2.7
) that

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}+\beta t-\lambda)t^{m}

\displaystyle=-\frac{d}{dt}\bigl((1-t^{2})mt^{m-1}\bigr)+\beta t^{m+1}-\lambda t^{m}

\displaystyle=-\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(mt^{m-1}-mt^{m+1}\bigr)+\beta t^{m+1}-\lambda t^{m}

\displaystyle=-\bigl(m(m-1)t^{m-2}-m(m+1)t^{m}\bigr)+\beta t^{m+1}-\lambda t^{m}

\displaystyle=\beta t^{m+1}+\bigl(m(m+1)-\lambda\bigr)t^{m}-m(m-1)t^{m-2}.

Here the term
t^{m-2}
is absent when
m\leq 1
, because then its coefficient
m(m-1)
is zero. Since also
Q_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}[t]\subset R[t]
by Proposition
4
, each column of
M_{n}
has entries in
R
. Thus
M_{n}\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n+1}(R)
and therefore
\det M_{n}\in R
.
∎

2.5.
The same determinant in the Legendre basis

The matrix
M_{n}
was defined over the ring
R
. To compare it with a matrix written in the
Q
-basis, we now extend scalars to the fraction field

K:=\mathbb{Q}(\beta,\lambda).

Let

\Phi_{n,K}:=\Phi_{n}\otimes_{R}K:K[t]_{\leq n-1}\oplus K\longrightarrow K[t]_{\leq n}.

Because the leading coefficient of
Q_{j}
is the nonzero integer
C_{j}
, the families

\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\mathrm{dom}}=(Q_{0},Q_{1},\dots,Q_{n-1},e),\qquad\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\mathrm{cod}}=(Q_{0},Q_{1},\dots,Q_{n})

are
K
-bases.

Remark 6
.

This scalar extension is essential. Over
R
the family
(Q_{0},Q_{1})
is
not
a basis of
R[t]_{\leq 1}
, since
Q_{1}=2t
and
t\notin RQ_{0}+RQ_{1}
. Thus every change-of-basis argument involving the
Q
-basis must be performed only after passing to a ring in which the integers are invertible; the field
K
is the cleanest choice.

Let
N_{n}
be the matrix of
\Phi_{n,K}
with respect to the bases
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\mathrm{dom}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\mathrm{cod}}
.

Lemma 7
.

For every
n\geq 1
, the matrix
N_{n}
has the block form

N_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}B_{n}&0\\
*&-1\end{pmatrix},

where
B_{n}=(b_{ij})_{0\leq i,j\leq n-1}
is the tridiagonal matrix determined by

\displaystyle b_{jj}

\displaystyle=j(j+1)-\lambda

\displaystyle(0\leq j\leq n-1),

\displaystyle b_{j-1,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{2j\beta}{2j+1}

\displaystyle(1\leq j\leq n-1),

\displaystyle b_{j+1,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{(j+1)\beta}{2(2j+1)}

\displaystyle(0\leq j\leq n-2),

and all other entries equal to
0
. Equivalently,

B_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda&\dfrac{2\beta}{3}&0&\cdots&0\\
\dfrac{\beta}{2}&2-\lambda&\dfrac{4\beta}{5}&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\dfrac{\beta}{3}&6-\lambda&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\dfrac{2(n-1)\beta}{2n-1}\\
0&\cdots&0&\dfrac{(n-1)\beta}{2(2n-3)}&n(n-1)-\lambda\end{pmatrix}.

In particular,

(2.13)

\det N_{n}=-\det B_{n}.

Proof.

For
0\leq j\leq n-1
, equation (
2.11
) gives

\Phi_{n,K}(Q_{j},0)=(j(j+1)-\lambda)Q_{j}+\frac{2j\beta}{2j+1}Q_{j-1}+\frac{(j+1)\beta}{2(2j+1)}Q_{j+1}.

Thus the first
n
columns of
N_{n}
are tridiagonal in the basis
(Q_{0},\dots,Q_{n})
. The last basis vector
e
is sent to

\Phi_{n,K}(0,1)=-Q_{n},

so the last column is zero except for a single entry
-1
in the last row. This proves the asserted block form, and then (
2.13
) follows immediately by expansion along the last column.
∎

For
n\geq 1
set

D_{n}:=\det B_{n},

and define also
D_{0}:=1
(the determinant of the empty matrix).

Lemma 8
.

The determinants
D_{n}
satisfy

(2.14)

D_{n+1}=(n(n+1)-\lambda)D_{n}-\frac{n^{2}\beta^{2}}{(2n-1)(2n+1)}D_{n-1}\qquad(n\geq 1),

with initial values

(2.15)

D_{0}=1,\qquad D_{1}=-\lambda.

Proof.

The value
D_{1}=-\lambda
is immediate from the
1\times 1
matrix
B_{1}=(-\lambda)
.
Now fix
n\geq 1
. Since
B_{n+1}
is tridiagonal, its last row has exactly two
nonzero entries:

\frac{n\beta}{2(2n-1)}\quad\text{in the penultimate column},\qquad n(n+1)-\lambda\quad\text{in the last column}.

Expanding
\det B_{n+1}
along that row gives

D_{n+1}=(n(n+1)-\lambda)D_{n}-\frac{n\beta}{2(2n-1)}\,M_{n+1,n},

where
M_{n+1,n}
is the minor obtained by deleting the last row and the
penultimate column.

In this minor, the last column has a single nonzero entry

\frac{2n\beta}{2n+1}

in its last row. Expanding
M_{n+1,n}
along that last column therefore yields

M_{n+1,n}=\frac{2n\beta}{2n+1}D_{n-1}.

Substituting this into the previous identity, we obtain

D_{n+1}=(n(n+1)-\lambda)D_{n}-\frac{n\beta}{2(2n-1)}\cdot\frac{2n\beta}{2n+1}D_{n-1},

which is exactly (
2.14
).
∎

2.6.
Normalization of the recurrence

We now normalize
\widetilde{u}_{n}
by the central binomial coefficients

C_{n}:=\binom{2n}{n}\qquad(n\geq 0),

already introduced in (
2.6
). Define

(2.16)

v_{n}(x,\lambda):=\frac{\widetilde{u}_{n}(x,\lambda)}{C_{n}}\qquad(n\geq 0).

Lemma 9
.

The sequence
(v_{n})_{n\geq 0}
satisfies

(2.17)

v_{n+1}=(n(n+1)-\lambda)v_{n}+\frac{n^{2}}{(2n-1)(2n+1)}x\,v_{n-1}\qquad(n\geq 1),

with initial values

(2.18)

v_{0}=1,\qquad v_{1}=-\lambda.

Proof.

Since

\frac{C_{n+1}}{C_{n}}=\frac{2(2n+1)}{n+1},

we have

\frac{C_{n}}{C_{n+1}}=\frac{n+1}{2(2n+1)}.

Dividing (
2.4
) by
C_{n+1}
yields

\displaystyle v_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{4n+2}{n+1}\bigl(n(n+1)-\lambda\bigr)\frac{C_{n}}{C_{n+1}}v_{n}+\frac{4n}{n+1}x\frac{C_{n-1}}{C_{n+1}}v_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\bigl(n(n+1)-\lambda\bigr)v_{n}+\frac{4n}{n+1}x\frac{C_{n-1}}{C_{n+1}}v_{n-1}.

A direct calculation gives

\frac{C_{n-1}}{C_{n+1}}=\frac{n(n+1)}{4(2n-1)(2n+1)},

so (
2.17
) follows. Finally,

v_{0}=\frac{\widetilde{u}_{0}}{C_{0}}=1,\qquad v_{1}=\frac{\widetilde{u}_{1}}{C_{1}}=\frac{-2\lambda}{2}=-\lambda.

∎

Corollary 10
.

For every
n\geq 0
,

(2.19)

D_{n}=v_{n}(-\beta^{2},\lambda)=\frac{\widetilde{u}_{n}(-\beta^{2},\lambda)}{C_{n}}.

Proof.

After the specialization
x=-\beta^{2}
, the recurrence (
2.17
) becomes

v_{n+1}(-\beta^{2},\lambda)=(n(n+1)-\lambda)v_{n}(-\beta^{2},\lambda)-\frac{n^{2}\beta^{2}}{(2n-1)(2n+1)}v_{n-1}(-\beta^{2},\lambda)

for
n\geq 1
, with the initial values (
2.18
). By Lemma
8
, this is exactly the recurrence and initial data satisfied by
D_{n}
. Hence
D_{n}=v_{n}(-\beta^{2},\lambda)
for all
n\geq 0
.
∎

2.7.
Comparison of the two determinants

We now compare the monomial-basis matrix
M_{n}
and the Legendre-basis matrix
N_{n}
.

Let

S_{n}\in\operatorname{GL}_{n+1}(K)

be the change-of-basis matrix from
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\mathrm{cod}}=(Q_{0},\dots,Q_{n})
to the monomial basis
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{\mathrm{cod}}=(1,t,\dots,t^{n})
, and let

T_{n}\in\operatorname{GL}_{n+1}(K)

be the change-of-basis matrix from
\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\mathrm{dom}}=(Q_{0},\dots,Q_{n-1},e)
to
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{\mathrm{dom}}=(1,t,\dots,t^{n-1},e)
.

Because
Q_{j}
has degree
j
and leading coefficient
C_{j}
, both
S_{n}
and
T_{n}

are triangular. Their diagonals are

(C_{0},\dots,C_{n})\qquad\text{and}\qquad(C_{0},\dots,C_{n-1},1),

respectively. Hence

(2.20)

\det S_{n}=\prod_{j=0}^{n}C_{j},

and

(2.21)

\det T_{n}=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}C_{j}.

Since
M_{n}
and
N_{n}
represent the same
K
-linear map
\Phi_{n,K}
in different bases, they are related by

(2.22)

M_{n}=S_{n}N_{n}T_{n}^{-1}.

Taking determinants and using (
2.20
)–(
2.22
) yields

(2.23)

\det M_{n}=\frac{\det S_{n}}{\det T_{n}}\det N_{n}=C_{n}\det N_{n}.

Combining (
2.23
) with (
2.13
) and (
2.19
), we obtain

(2.24)

\det M_{n}=-C_{n}D_{n}=-\widetilde{u}_{n}(-\beta^{2},\lambda).

By Lemma
5
, the left-hand side belongs to
R=\mathbb{Z}[\beta,\lambda]
. Therefore

\widetilde{u}_{n}(-\beta^{2},\lambda)\in\mathbb{Z}[\beta,\lambda]\qquad(n\geq 1).

The same conclusion also holds for
n=0
, because
\widetilde{u}_{0}=1
.

Applying Lemma
3
, we conclude that

\widetilde{u}_{n}(x,\lambda)\in\mathbb{Z}[x,\lambda]\qquad(n\geq 0).

This proves Theorem
1
.

3.
BOCRS truncations, continued fractions, and rational slices

For later use, we introduce the specialization

\widehat{u}_{n}(a,\lambda):=\widetilde{u}_{n}(a^{2},\lambda)\qquad(n\geq-1).

Thus

(3.1)

\widehat{u}_{-1}=0,\qquad\widehat{u}_{0}=1,\qquad\widehat{u}_{n+1}=\frac{4n+2}{n+1}\bigl(n(n+1)-\lambda\bigr)\widehat{u}_{n}+\frac{4n}{n+1}a^{2}\widehat{u}_{n-1}\qquad(n\geq 0),

and Theorem
1
gives

(3.2)

\widehat{u}_{n}(a,\lambda)\in\mathbb{Z}[a^{2},\lambda]\qquad(n\geq 0).

Moreover, it is immediate from (
3.1
) that

(3.3)

\deg_{a^{2}}\widehat{u}_{n}\leq\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\Bigr\rfloor,\qquad\deg_{\lambda}\widehat{u}_{n}\leq n\qquad(n\geq 0).

We next recall the BOCRS three-term recurrence in the form used for the decaying branch.

Proposition 11
.

Let
a>0
, let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
(\xi_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a positive decaying solution of

(3.4)

\lambda\xi_{n}=n(n+1)\xi_{n}-a\left(\frac{n}{2n-1}\xi_{n-1}-\frac{n+1}{2n+3}\xi_{n+1}\right),\qquad\xi_{-1}=0.

For
n\geq 0
set

(3.5)

\beta_{n}:=\frac{a^{2}(n+1)^{2}}{(2n+1)(2n+3)},

and for
n\geq 1
define

(3.6)

q_{n}:=\frac{an}{2n-1}\frac{\xi_{n-1}}{\xi_{n}}.

Then

(3.7)

q_{n}=n(n+1)-\lambda+\frac{\beta_{n}}{q_{n+1}}\qquad(n\geq 1).

In particular,
q_{n}\to\infty
, and

(3.8)

q_{1}=2-\lambda+\cfrac{\beta_{1}}{6-\lambda+\cfrac{\beta_{2}}{12-\lambda+\cfrac{\beta_{3}}{20-\lambda+\ddots}}}.

If, in addition, we normalize by
\xi_{0}=1
, then
\lambda>0
and

(3.9)

q_{1}=\frac{a^{2}}{3\lambda}.

Hence

(3.10)

\lambda=\cfrac{a^{2}/3}{2-\lambda+\cfrac{4a^{2}/15}{6-\lambda+\cfrac{9a^{2}/35}{12-\lambda+\ddots}}}.

Proof.

Divide (
3.4
) by
\xi_{n}
to get

\lambda=n(n+1)-q_{n}+\frac{a(n+1)}{2n+3}\frac{\xi_{n+1}}{\xi_{n}}.

By the definition of
q_{n+1}
,

\frac{a(n+1)}{2n+3}\frac{\xi_{n+1}}{\xi_{n}}=\frac{a^{2}(n+1)^{2}}{(2n+1)(2n+3)}\frac{1}{q_{n+1}}=\frac{\beta_{n}}{q_{n+1}},

which gives (
3.7
). Since
q_{n+1}>0
, we have

q_{n}\geq n(n+1)-\lambda,

so
q_{n}\to\infty
. Iterating (
3.7
) therefore gives the continued fraction (
3.8
). Finally, the equation (
3.4
) at
n=0
reads

\lambda\xi_{0}=\frac{a}{3}\xi_{1}.

Since
a>0
and
\xi_{0},\xi_{1}>0
, this shows that
\lambda>0
.
With the normalization
\xi_{0}=1
, we therefore get

q_{1}=a\frac{\xi_{0}}{\xi_{1}}=\frac{a^{2}}{3\lambda},

which is (
3.9
); substituting this into (
3.8
) gives
(
3.10
).
∎

Remark 12
.

For the Hörmander–Bernhardsson branch, the rescaled eigenfunction

h(z):=\Phi\!\left(\frac{z}{2C}\right)

corresponds to the BOCRS decaying branch for
L_{1,a}
at the normalized spectral pair

a_{*}=\frac{\pi}{4C},\qquad\lambda_{*}=-\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2C}.

Hence Proposition
11
applies, and substituting

(a,\lambda)=(a_{*},\lambda_{*})
into (
3.10
) gives

(3.11)

-\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2C}=\cfrac{\pi^{2}/(48C^{2})}{\,2+\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2C}+\cfrac{\pi^{2}/(60C^{2})}{\,6+\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2C}+\cfrac{9\pi^{2}/(560C^{2})}{\,12+\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2C}+\ddots}}}.

Equivalently,

(3.12)

L_{\tau}(1)=-\cfrac{\pi^{2}/(24C)}{\,2+\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2C}+\cfrac{\pi^{2}/(60C^{2})}{\,6+\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2C}+\cfrac{9\pi^{2}/(560C^{2})}{\,12+\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2C}+\ddots}}}.

It is sometimes convenient to rewrite this in terms of the normalized quantities

X:=\frac{C}{\pi},\qquad Y:=\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{C}.

Then (
3.11
) becomes

-\frac{Y}{2}=\cfrac{1/(48X^{2})}{\,2+\frac{Y}{2}+\cfrac{1/(60X^{2})}{\,6+\frac{Y}{2}+\cfrac{9/(560X^{2})}{\,12+\frac{Y}{2}+\ddots}}}.

Thus the BOCRS decaying-branch continued fraction furnishes an implicit relation between the
two normalized constants
C/\pi
and
L_{\tau}(1)/C
.

For each integer
N\geq 1
, define the depth-
N
truncants by

(3.13)

q_{N}^{(N)}:=N(N+1)-\lambda,\qquad q_{n}^{(N)}:=n(n+1)-\lambda+\frac{\beta_{n}}{q_{n+1}^{(N)}}\quad(1\leq n\leq N-1).

Thus
q_{1}^{(N)}
is the finite continued fraction

(3.14)

q_{1}^{(N)}=2-\lambda+\cfrac{\beta_{1}}{6-\lambda+\cfrac{\beta_{2}}{12-\lambda+\cfrac{\beta_{3}}{\ddots+\cfrac{\beta_{N-1}}{N(N+1)-\lambda}}}}.

Write

(3.15)

q_{n}^{(N)}=\frac{K_{n}^{(N)}}{K_{n+1}^{(N)}},

where

(3.16)

K_{N+1}^{(N)}=1,\qquad K_{N}^{(N)}=N(N+1)-\lambda,

and

(3.17)

K_{n}^{(N)}=(n(n+1)-\lambda)K_{n+1}^{(N)}+\beta_{n}K_{n+2}^{(N)}\qquad(1\leq n\leq N-1).

Accordingly, whenever we use
q_{n}^{(N)}
algebraically, we regard it as the rational
function

q_{n}^{(N)}=\frac{K_{n}^{(N)}}{K_{n+1}^{(N)}}\in\mathbb{Q}(a,\lambda).

Its continued-fraction interpretation at a point
(a,\lambda)
requires the successive
denominators to be nonzero.

Proposition 13
.

Define

T_{0}(a,\lambda):=(-\lambda),

and, for
N\geq 1
,

(3.18)

T_{N}(a,\lambda):=\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda&\dfrac{a}{3}&0&\cdots&0\\
-a&2-\lambda&\dfrac{2a}{5}&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&-\dfrac{2a}{3}&6-\lambda&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\dfrac{Na}{2N+1}\\
0&\cdots&0&-\dfrac{Na}{2N-1}&N(N+1)-\lambda\end{pmatrix}.

For
N\geq 1
and
0\leq n\leq N+1
, let
\Delta_{n}^{(N)}
denote the determinant of the
trailing principal submatrix of
T_{N}(a,\lambda)
obtained by deleting the first
n
rows
and columns; in particular,

\Delta_{0}^{(N)}=\det T_{N}(a,\lambda),\qquad\Delta_{N+1}^{(N)}:=1.

Then the following hold.

(i)

One has

\Delta_{n}^{(N)}=K_{n}^{(N)}\qquad(1\leq n\leq N+1).

(ii)

One has

(3.19)

\det T_{N}(a,\lambda)=-\lambda K_{1}^{(N)}+\frac{a^{2}}{3}K_{2}^{(N)}.

Equivalently,

(3.20)

\lambda q_{1}^{(N)}-\frac{a^{2}}{3}=-\frac{\det T_{N}(a,\lambda)}{K_{2}^{(N)}}

as an identity in
\mathbb{Q}(a,\lambda)
. In particular, on the locus
K_{2}^{(N)}\neq 0
, the
equation

(3.21)

\lambda q_{1}^{(N)}=\frac{a^{2}}{3}

is equivalent to

(3.22)

\det T_{N}(a,\lambda)=0.

Proof.

For
0\leq n\leq N-1
, first-row expansion of the trailing principal submatrix starting at
index
n
gives

\Delta_{n}^{(N)}=(n(n+1)-\lambda)\Delta_{n+1}^{(N)}-\frac{a(n+1)}{2n+3}\left(-\frac{a(n+1)}{2n+1}\right)\Delta_{n+2}^{(N)}.

Thus

\Delta_{n}^{(N)}=(n(n+1)-\lambda)\Delta_{n+1}^{(N)}+\beta_{n}\Delta_{n+2}^{(N)}\qquad(0\leq n\leq N-1),

with terminal values

\Delta_{N}^{(N)}=N(N+1)-\lambda,\qquad\Delta_{N+1}^{(N)}=1.

Comparing this with (
3.16
)–(
3.17
), we obtain

\Delta_{n}^{(N)}=K_{n}^{(N)}\qquad(1\leq n\leq N+1),

which proves (i).

Taking
n=0
in the same expansion gives

\det T_{N}(a,\lambda)=-\lambda\Delta_{1}^{(N)}+\beta_{0}\Delta_{2}^{(N)}=-\lambda K_{1}^{(N)}+\frac{a^{2}}{3}K_{2}^{(N)},

which is (
3.19
). Since
q_{1}^{(N)}=K_{1}^{(N)}/K_{2}^{(N)}
in
\mathbb{Q}(a,\lambda)
,

\lambda q_{1}^{(N)}-\frac{a^{2}}{3}=\frac{\lambda K_{1}^{(N)}-\frac{a^{2}}{3}K_{2}^{(N)}}{K_{2}^{(N)}}=-\frac{\det T_{N}(a,\lambda)}{K_{2}^{(N)}},

which is (
3.20
). The pointwise equivalence of
(
3.21
) and (
3.22
) on the locus
K_{2}^{(N)}\neq 0

is now immediate. This proves (ii).

∎

Remark 14
.

The condition
K_{2}^{(N)}\neq 0
in Proposition
13
(ii)
is necessary. For example, when
N=3
,
\lambda=0
, and
a=\pm 2i\sqrt{70}
, one has

K_{2}^{(3)}=(6-\lambda)(12-\lambda)+\frac{9a^{2}}{35}=0,\qquad K_{1}^{(3)}=-896\neq 0,

Theorem 15

(Exact finite-compression identity)
.

For every
N\geq 0
,

\widehat{u}_{N+1}(a,\lambda)=C_{N+1}\det T_{N}(a,\lambda).

Consequently, for every
N\geq 0
,

\widehat{u}_{N+1}(a,\lambda)=0

if and only if

\det T_{N}(a,\lambda)=0.

If
N\geq 1
and
K_{2}^{(N)}(a,\lambda)\neq 0
, then these equations are also equivalent to

\lambda q_{1}^{(N)}=\frac{a^{2}}{3}.

Proof.

Set

E_{-1}:=1,\qquad E_{N}:=\det T_{N}(a,\lambda)\qquad(N\geq 0).

Expanding along the last row of
T_{N}(a,\lambda)
, we get

E_{N}=(N(N+1)-\lambda)E_{N-1}+\frac{N^{2}a^{2}}{(2N-1)(2N+1)}E_{N-2}\qquad(N\geq 1),

with

E_{0}=-\lambda.

Now define

U_{n}:=C_{n}E_{n-1}\qquad(n\geq 0).

Then
U_{0}=1
and
U_{1}=-2\lambda
. Moreover, for
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle U_{n+1}

\displaystyle=C_{n+1}E_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{n+1}}{C_{n}}\bigl(n(n+1)-\lambda\bigr)U_{n}+\frac{C_{n+1}}{C_{n-1}}\frac{n^{2}a^{2}}{(2n-1)(2n+1)}U_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(2n+1)}{n+1}\bigl(n(n+1)-\lambda\bigr)U_{n}+\frac{4n}{n+1}a^{2}U_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{4n+2}{n+1}\bigl(n(n+1)-\lambda\bigr)U_{n}+\frac{4n}{n+1}a^{2}U_{n-1}.

Hence
(U_{n})_{n\geq 0}
satisfies the same recurrence and the same initial conditions as

(\widehat{u}_{n}(a,\lambda))_{n\geq 0}
. Therefore

U_{n}=\widehat{u}_{n}(a,\lambda)\qquad(n\geq 0).

Therefore

U_{n}=\widehat{u}_{n}(a,\lambda)\qquad(n\geq 0),

so

\widehat{u}_{N+1}(a,\lambda)=C_{N+1}\det T_{N}(a,\lambda)\qquad(N\geq 0).

Since
C_{N+1}\neq 0
, this immediately yields

\widehat{u}_{N+1}(a,\lambda)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\det T_{N}(a,\lambda)=0.

If
N\geq 1
and
K_{2}^{(N)}(a,\lambda)\neq 0
, then
Proposition
13
shows that these are further equivalent to

\lambda q_{1}^{(N)}=\frac{a^{2}}{3}.

This completes the proof of Theorem
15
.
∎

We now turn from exact integrality to a first arithmetic consequence. The point is that
Theorem
1
, together with the elementary degree bounds
(
3.3
), produces integral multiples of the coefficients

\widehat{u}_{n}(a,\lambda)
whenever
a^{2}
and
\lambda
are rational. If, in addition,
the associated even generating series has exponential type at most
2a
, then Cauchy’s
estimate forces those integral multiples to decay superexponentially. The only way an integer
sequence can behave in this fashion is that it eventually vanishes. This yields the following
rational-slice obstruction.

Theorem 16
.

Let
a>0
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Assume that the power series

(3.23)

F(z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widehat{u}_{n}(a,\lambda)z^{2n}

defines an entire nonpolynomial function of exponential type at most
2a
. Then
a^{2}
and
\lambda
are not both rational.

Proof.

Suppose, to the contrary, that

a^{2}=\frac{r}{s}\in\mathbb{Q},\qquad\lambda=\frac{p}{q}\in\mathbb{Q},

with
r,s,p,q\in\mathbb{Z}
and
s,q\geq 1
. By (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), we have

(sq)^{n}\widehat{u}_{n}(a,\lambda)\in\mathbb{Z}\qquad(n\geq 0).

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,a)
. Since
F
is entire of exponential type at most
2a
, there exists a constant
A_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

|F(z)|\leq A_{\varepsilon}e^{2(a+\varepsilon)|z|}\qquad(z\in\mathbb{C}).

By Cauchy’s estimate, for every
R>0
,

|\widehat{u}_{n}(a,\lambda)|\leq\frac{\sup_{|z|=R}|F(z)|}{R^{2n}}\leq A_{\varepsilon}\frac{e^{2(a+\varepsilon)R}}{R^{2n}}.

Choosing

R=\frac{n}{a+\varepsilon}

gives

|\widehat{u}_{n}(a,\lambda)|\leq A_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{e(a+\varepsilon)}{n}\right)^{2n}.

Therefore

(sq)^{n}|\widehat{u}_{n}(a,\lambda)|\leq A_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{sq\,e^{2}(a+\varepsilon)^{2}}{n^{2}}\right)^{n}\longrightarrow 0.

Since
(sq)^{n}\widehat{u}_{n}(a,\lambda)
is an integer for every
n
, it follows that

(sq)^{n}\widehat{u}_{n}(a,\lambda)=0

for all sufficiently large
n
. Hence
\widehat{u}_{n}(a,\lambda)=0
for all sufficiently large
n
, and so the power series (
3.23
) is a polynomial, contrary to assumption.
∎

We now apply Theorem
16
to the BOCRS decaying branch. Since BOCRS
formulate the coefficient recurrence in the
L_{1,b}
-normalization, whereas the decaying
branch is naturally written in the
L_{a,1}
-normalization, we first pass to the rescaled
eigenfunction
g(z)=f(az)
. This places the even product in exactly the framework of
Theorem
16
.

Corollary 17
.

Let
f
be the normalized eigenfunction corresponding to the decaying BOCRS branch
with parameter
a>0
and eigenvalue
\lambda
. Assume that
f(z)f(-z)
is not a
polynomial. Then
a^{2}
and
\lambda
are not both rational. In particular, if
a^{2}\in\mathbb{Q}
,
then
\lambda\notin\mathbb{Q}
.

Proof.

By definition of the decaying BOCRS branch,
f
is the normalized
\lambda
-eigenfunction
of
L_{a,1}
. Define

g(z):=f(az).

If
R_{k}f(z):=f(kz)
, then the BOCRS scaling law
[
3
, §4]
gives

L_{1,a}g=L_{1,a}R_{a}f=R_{a}L_{a,1}f=\lambda g.

Also
g(0)=f(0)=1
, so
g
is a normalized eigenfunction of
L_{1,a}
.

Therefore, by
[
3
, §10.3]
,

g(z)g(-z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widehat{u}_{n}(a,\lambda)z^{2n}.

By
[
3
, Corollary 7.2]
, the function
g
is entire of exponential type at most

a
. Hence
g(z)g(-z)
is entire of exponential type at most
2a
.

Since

g(z)g(-z)=f(az)f(-az),

the function
g(z)g(-z)
is a polynomial if and only if
f(z)f(-z)
is a polynomial.
By assumption, it is not a polynomial. Theorem
16
applied to

g(z)g(-z)
now shows that
a^{2}
and
\lambda
are not both rational. The final
assertion is immediate.
∎

We next specialize Corollary
17
to the
Hörmander–Bernhardsson branch. In the notation of
[
3
]
,

denotes the sharp

The BOCRS rescaling identifies the corresponding normalized spectral pair as

Corollary 18
.

For the Hörmander–Bernhardsson branch, the two numbers

\left(\frac{\pi}{4C}\right)^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad-\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2C}

are not both rational. In particular, at least one of
C/\pi
and
L_{\tau}(1)/C
is irrational.

Proof.

By
[
3
, Theorem 1.1]
, the function
\Phi
is the normalized eigenfunction of

L_{1/(2C),\,\pi/2}

with eigenvalue

\lambda_{*}:=-\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2C}.

Set

a_{*}:=\frac{\pi}{4C},\qquad h(z):=\Phi\!\left(\frac{z}{2C}\right).

If
R_{k}f(z):=f(kz)
, then
[
3
, §4]
gives the scaling law

L_{a,b}R_{k}=R_{k}L_{ka,k^{-1}b}.

Applying this with
a=1
,
b=a_{*}
, and

, we obtain

L_{1,a_{*}}h=L_{1,a_{*}}R_{1/(2C)}\Phi=R_{1/(2C)}L_{1/(2C),\,\pi/2}\Phi=\lambda_{*}h.

Moreover
h(0)=\Phi(0)=1
. Hence
h
is a normalized eigenfunction of
L_{1,a_{*}}
, and
therefore

h(z)h(-z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widehat{u}_{n}(a_{*},\lambda_{*})z^{2n}.

Since

h(z)h(-z)=\varphi\!\left(\frac{z}{2C}\right),

and
\varphi
has infinitely many zeros
\pm\tau_{n}
, the function
h(z)h(-z)
is not a

[

,
h
is entire of exponential type at most

, so

is entire of exponential type at most

. Therefore

16
implies that
a_{*}^{2}
and
\lambda_{*}
are not both rational.

If both
C/\pi
and

were rational, then

a_{*}=\frac{\pi}{4C}=\frac{1}{4(C/\pi)}\in\mathbb{Q}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lambda_{*}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{C}\in\mathbb{Q},

which is impossible. Therefore at least one of
C/\pi
and
L_{\tau}(1)/C
is irrational.
∎

Remark 19
.

does not imply that

Remark 20
.

Bondarenko–Ortega-Cerdà–Radchenko–Seip formulate in

, §10]
two arithmetic questions: Conjecture 1, namely

L_{+}(-2k)=\frac{L_{+}(2k)}{(2\pi iC)^{2k}},\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z},

and Conjecture 2, namely the integrality proved in the present paper. The author proved Conjecture 1 in
[
6
, Theorem 1.2]
. Combining his theorem with the BOCRS identities

L_{+}(2)=-4C\,L_{\tau}(1),\qquad\pi^{2}a_{1}=-\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{C},

here
a_{1}
denotes the coefficient
a_{1}
in the BOCRS odd inverse-power expansion of
the positive zeros
(\tau_{n})
; see
[
3
, (1.7)]
. One obtains

L_{+}(-2)=\frac{L_{+}(2)}{(2\pi iC)^{2}}=\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{\pi^{2}C}=-a_{1}.

4.
Weighted arithmetic and the coefficient ring of
\varphi

The integrality theorem admits a sharper refinement: not only are the coefficients integral,
their monomial support is confined to a natural weighted region. This reflects the structure of
the recurrence itself: multiplication by
\lambda
raises the relevant weight by
1
,
whereas multiplication by
x
raises it by
2
but comes from the lower-order term

\widetilde{u}_{n-1}
. The resulting weighted filtration gives the following precise support
bound.

Proposition 21
.

Define a weight on monomials by

w(x)=2,\qquad w(\lambda)=1,\qquad w(x^{r}\lambda^{s})=2r+s.

For a nonzero polynomial

F(x,\lambda)=\sum_{r,s}c_{r,s}x^{r}\lambda^{s}

set

\deg_{w}F:=\max\{2r+s:c_{r,s}\neq 0\},

and
\deg_{w}0:=-\infty
. Then for every
n\geq 0
,

\deg_{w}\widetilde{u}_{n}\leq n.

Equivalently, every monomial
x^{r}\lambda^{s}
occurring in
\widetilde{u}_{n}(x,\lambda)

satisfies

2r+s\leq n.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
n
. For
n=0
, we have
\widetilde{u}_{0}=1
, so

\deg_{w}\widetilde{u}_{0}=0
. For
n=1
, the recurrence (
2.4
) at
n=0

gives
\widetilde{u}_{1}=-2\lambda
, so
\deg_{w}\widetilde{u}_{1}=1
.

Now assume

\deg_{w}\widetilde{u}_{n}\leq n,\qquad\deg_{w}\widetilde{u}_{n-1}\leq n-1

for some
n\geq 1
. From (
2.4
),

\widetilde{u}_{n+1}=\frac{4n+2}{n+1}\bigl(n(n+1)-\lambda\bigr)\widetilde{u}_{n}+\frac{4n}{n+1}x\,\widetilde{u}_{n-1}.

Multiplication by the constant
n(n+1)
does not change the weight, multiplication by

\lambda
increases the weight by
1
, and multiplication by
x
increases the weight by
2
.
Therefore

\deg_{w}\bigl((n(n+1)-\lambda)\widetilde{u}_{n}\bigr)\leq n+1,\qquad\deg_{w}\bigl(x\,\widetilde{u}_{n-1}\bigr)\leq(n-1)+2=n+1.

Hence
\deg_{w}\widetilde{u}_{n+1}\leq n+1
, completing the induction.
∎

This weighted support bound has a concrete consequence for the Taylor coefficients of the
Hörmander–Bernhardsson extremal function. After inserting the normalized parameters

a_{*}^{2}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{16C^{2}},\qquad\lambda_{*}=-\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2C},

the inequality
2r+s\leq n
is exactly what rules out odd powers of
\pi
and forces an
additional factor of
C^{n}
. In this way, the abstract weight bound becomes an explicit
coefficient-ring statement for
\varphi
.

Corollary 22
.

Let

a_{*}:=\frac{\pi}{4C},\qquad\lambda_{*}:=-\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2C},

and write

\varphi(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{2n}.

Then

(4.1)

c_{n}=(2C)^{2n}\widehat{u}_{n}(a_{*},\lambda_{*})\qquad(n\geq 0).

Consequently,

c_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}[\pi^{2},C,L_{\tau}(1)]\qquad(n\geq 0).

In fact, one has the stronger divisibility statement

c_{n}\in C^{n}\,\mathbb{Z}[\pi^{2},C,L_{\tau}(1)]\qquad(n\geq 0).

Proof.

As in the proof of Corollary
18
, set

h(z):=\Phi\!\left(\frac{z}{2C}\right).

Then
h
is a normalized eigenfunction of
L_{1,a_{*}}
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{*}
, where

a_{*}=\frac{\pi}{4C},\qquad\lambda_{*}=-\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2C}.

Hence, by
[
3
, §10.3]
,

h(z)h(-z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widehat{u}_{n}(a_{*},\lambda_{*})z^{2n}.

Since

h(z)h(-z)=\varphi\!\left(\frac{z}{2C}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}\left(\frac{z}{2C}\right)^{2n},

comparing coefficients gives

c_{n}=(2C)^{2n}\widehat{u}_{n}(a_{*},\lambda_{*})\qquad(n\geq 0).

Now write

\widetilde{u}_{n}(x,\lambda)=\sum_{r,s}a_{r,s}x^{r}\lambda^{s}\qquad(a_{r,s}\in\mathbb{Z}).

By Proposition
21
, only pairs
(r,s)
with
2r+s\leq n
occur.
Using

a_{*}^{2}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{16C^{2}},\qquad\lambda_{*}=-\frac{L_{\tau}(1)}{2C},

a monomial
x^{r}\lambda^{s}
contributes to

c_{n}=(2C)^{2n}\widetilde{u}_{n}(a_{*}^{2},\lambda_{*})

the term

(-1)^{s}\,2^{\,2n-4r-s}\,\pi^{2r}\,C^{\,2n-2r-s}\,L_{\tau}(1)^{s}.

Because
2r+s\leq n
, we have

2n-4r-s=(n-2r-s)+(n-2r)\geq 0,\qquad 2n-2r-s=n+(n-2r-s)\geq n.

Hence every such term belongs to

C^{n}\mathbb{Z}[\pi^{2},C,L_{\tau}(1)].

Summing over all
(r,s)
, we obtain

c_{n}\in C^{n}\,\mathbb{Z}[\pi^{2},C,L_{\tau}(1)]\subset\mathbb{Z}[\pi^{2},C,L_{\tau}(1)].\qed

Remark 23
.

BOCRS observe in
[
3
, §10.3]
that the Taylor coefficients of
\varphi
appear to
lie in
\mathbb{Z}[\pi,C,L_{\tau}(1)]
. Corollary
22
sharpens this:
only even powers of
\pi
can occur.
