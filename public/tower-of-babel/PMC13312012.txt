Massive mitral annular calcification with valvular involvement: Multimodality and longitudinal imaging correlation
Authors: Manuela Montatore, Gabriele Fanigliulo, Federica Masino, Ruggiero Tupputi, Roberto Maresca, Francesca Calò, Eluisa Muscogiuri, Giuseppe Guglielmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitral annular calcification is a chronic and progressive process involving the fibrous support of the mitral valve and is commonly encountered as an incidental finding in elderly patients. In rare cases, it may evolve into a massive form with relevant diagnostic and clinical implications. We report the case of a 76-year-old woman with a history of transient ischemic attacks and prior syncopal episodes who presented with progressive exertional dyspnea. Serial imaging over 8 years demonstrated progressive calcification of the mitral annulus, initially visible as a subtle curvilinear density on chest radiography and later becoming a prominent annular structure. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed preserved left ventricular systolic function, mild mitral and tricuspid regurgitation, and moderate-to-severe aortic regurgitation, with extensive calcification of both the mitral and aortic valve apparatus. Cardiac computed tomography provided definitive characterization, demonstrating massive, confluent, high-attenuation calcification of the mitral annulus with bulky crescentic morphology and mild extension toward the left ventricular inflow tract, without an associated soft-tissue component. This case highlights the value of longitudinal and multimodality imaging in recognizing massive mitral annular calcification and underscores its association with systemic cardiovascular risk factors.Keywords: Mitral annular calcification, Cardiac CT, Multimodality imaging, Valvular calcification, Chronic kidney disease

Introduction

Mitral annular calcification (MAC) is a chronic, progressive process characterized by calcium deposition along the fibrous support of the mitral valve. Although traditionally regarded as a passive degenerative phenomenon, it is now recognized as an active, cell-mediated process involving inflammation, lipid deposition, and osteogenic differentiation of valvular interstitial cells [1,2]. The prevalence of MAC increases with age and is strongly associated with cardiovascular risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and chronic kidney disease [3,4].

Beyond being an incidental imaging finding, MAC has important clinical implications, including mitral valve dysfunction, arrhythmias, cerebrovascular events, and increased cardiovascular morbidity [5]. In patients with chronic kidney disease, even mild renal function impairment may contribute to accelerated ectopic calcification through alterations in mineral metabolism and chronic inflammatory pathways [6,7].

While mild-to-moderate MAC is frequently encountered in clinical practice, massive forms are uncommon and may present as bulky mass-like lesions, potentially posing diagnostic challenges and requiring differentiation from other intracardiac pathologies [8]. We report a case of progressive massive MAC documented over 8 years, emphasizing the role of multimodality imaging and longitudinal assessment.

Case presentation

A 76-year-old woman was referred for cardiology evaluation because of progressive exertional dyspnea corresponding to New York Heart Association functional class II-III. Her medical history was notable for transient ischemic attacks and prior syncopal episodes, for which she was being monitored with an implantable loop recorder. She also had a history of hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, mild-to-moderate aortic valvulopathy, and carotid atheromatosis. Coronary angiography performed in 2021 did not demonstrate significant coronary artery disease. A recent 24-hour Holter electrocardiogram showed episodes of ST-segment depression, raising suspicion of myocardial ischemia, although the patient remained hemodynamically stable.

Laboratory evaluation revealed mild chronic kidney disease, with an estimated glomerular filtration rate of approximately 45 mL/min/1.73 m², and borderline alterations of calcium–phosphate metabolism, suggesting a potential contribution to ectopic cardiovascular calcification.

Imaging findings

Retrospective review of prior chest radiographs demonstrated a gradual progression of mitral annular calcification over time. In 2018, a subtle curvilinear calcific density was visible on a single bedside projection, projected over the expected region of the mitral annulus and initially considered incidental (Fig. 1).

Chest radiograph (single bedside projection, 2018) showing a subtle curvilinear calcific density projected over the expected region of the mitral annulus (arrow), initially considered incidental.

On follow-up two-view radiography performed in 2024, the calcification appeared more prominent, thicker, and clearly identifiable along the atrioventricular junction, reflecting progressive annular involvement (Fig. 2).

Follow-up chest radiographs (posteroanterior and lateral views, 2024) demonstrating marked progression of mitral annular calcification, appearing as a thickened, more conspicuous curvilinear calcific structure along the atrioventricular junction (arrows).

Transthoracic echocardiography performed shortly before computed tomography showed normal left ventricular dimensions and preserved systolic function, with an ejection fraction of approximately 55%. Mild mitral and tricuspid regurgitation were observed, along with moderate-to-severe aortic regurgitation. The mitral annulus appeared markedly thickened and hyperechoic, with extensive calcification predominantly involving the posterior annulus and producing significant acoustic shadowing, partially limiting accurate delineation of the full extent of the lesion (Fig. 3).

(A) Color Doppler echocardiography demonstrating left ventricular inflow and left atrium, with associated mild mitral regurgitation and visualization of the adjacent outflow tract/aortic root (arrow), in the setting of extensive annular calcification. (B) Transthoracic echocardiography, parasternal short-axis view at the mitral valve level (“en face” view), demonstrating dense, irregular hyperechoic thickening of the mitral annulus with posterior acoustic shadowing (arrowheads), consistent with extensive calcification. (C) parasternal long-axis view showing left atrium and left ventricle, with marked calcification of the posterior mitral annulus (arrow) extending toward the annular hinge point.

Cardiac computed tomography angiography performed in April 2026 provided definitive anatomical characterization. It demonstrated massive, confluent calcification of the mitral annulus, characterized by a bulky crescentic morphology partially encircling the annulus. The calcifications showed very high attenuation values, exceeding 1000 Hounsfield units, and extended along both posterior and anterior annular segments, with mild protrusion toward the left ventricular inflow tract. No associated soft-tissue component or contrast enhancement was observed. Additional findings included calcific involvement of the aortic valve. The imaging features were consistent with massive mitral annular calcification (Fig. 4).

Cardiac computed tomography angiography (reformatted views: short and long axes) demonstrating massive, confluent, high-attenuation calcification of the mitral annulus (arrows), with crescentic bulky morphology, extension along the annulus, and mild protrusion into the left ventricular inflow tract.

Dedicated calcium quantification analysis was performed using commercially available Smart Calcium software on a dual-energy CT platform (GE HealthCare, Milwaukee, WI). Quantitative analysis demonstrated an extremely elevated mitral annular calcium burden, with an AJ-130 score of 11,557, mass score of 6047, and calcium volume of 3110, confirming the massive extent of annular calcification. In contrast, coronary calcium burden was overall moderate-to-severe but not massive, with a total coronary AJ-130 score of 785, predominantly involving the left anterior descending and circumflex coronary arteries (Table 1, Table 2).

Discussion

This case illustrates a relatively uncommon presentation of mitral annular calcification, characterized by massive, confluent calcification and documented longitudinal progression over several years. The retrospective identification of early annular calcification on chest radiography and its subsequent evolution highlights the importance of longitudinal imaging in selected patients.

MAC is increasingly recognized as an active and multifactorial pathological process. In addition to age-related degeneration, mechanisms such as chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and osteogenic differentiation of valvular interstitial cells have been implicated [2,3]. Systemic factors, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and chronic kidney disease, play a significant role in promoting and accelerating calcification [4,9]. In the present case, the coexistence of multiple cardiovascular risk factors and mild renal impairment suggests a systemic pro-calcific milieu contributing to the severity of annular involvement.

Similar to the conceptual framework proposed in contemporary reviews, this case illustrates how MAC may represent the final common imaging manifestation of multiple converging mechanisms, including age-related degeneration, atherosclerotic cardiovascular burden, increased valvular stress, and abnormalities of mineral metabolism. From an imaging perspective, this case highlights the complementary role of different modalities. Chest radiography, although limited in sensitivity, may provide early clues and allow retrospective comparison over time. Echocardiography remains essential for the evaluation of cardiac function and valvular hemodynamics, but may be limited by acoustic shadowing in the presence of dense calcifications. Cardiac computed tomography represents the reference imaging modality for accurate anatomical characterization, enabling detailed assessment of calcific burden, distribution, and extension, and playing a key role in differentiating MAC from other intracardiac lesions. Dedicated CT calcium quantification demonstrated an extremely elevated mitral annular calcium burden (AJ-130 score: 11,557), objectively confirming the massive extent of annular calcification.

Although calcium quantification software is primarily validated for coronary artery calcium assessment, it provided an additional objective estimation of the exceptionally elevated annular calcific burden in the present case.

The differential diagnosis of a bulky annular lesion includes caseous mitral annular calcification, intracardiac tumors, thrombus, and infective processes, as previously described in the literature [2,3]. Caseous MAC typically demonstrates a central hypodense component corresponding to liquefaction, whereas tumors and infective lesions often show soft-tissue characteristics and contrast enhancement. In this case, the homogeneous high attenuation, absence of a central low-density component, and lack of enhancement strongly support the diagnosis of dense calcific MAC.

Although MAC is often asymptomatic, severe disease may have significant clinical implications. It can contribute to mitral valve dysfunction, interfere with annular dynamics, and, in advanced cases, cause left ventricular inflow obstruction. It has also been associated with arrhythmias, conduction abnormalities, and an increased risk of cerebrovascular events [10,11]. In this patient, dyspnea was likely multifactorial, with a significant contribution from concomitant aortic regurgitation and diffuse valvular disease. Contemporary CT-based assessment of MAC severity may include evaluation of calcium thickness, circumferential extent, trigonal involvement, leaflet extension, and overall calcium burden, parameters that have become increasingly relevant in procedural planning for transcatheter mitral interventions [12].

This case supports the concept of mitral annular calcification not merely as an incidental degenerative finding, but as an imaging marker of systemic cardiovascular disease.

Conclusion

Massive mitral annular calcification represents an uncommon but clinically relevant manifestation of a common degenerative process. This case emphasizes the importance of multimodality and longitudinal imaging in identifying disease progression, accurately characterizing the extent of calcification, and excluding alternative diagnoses. Recognition of this entity is essential, as it may reflect a broader systemic disease process and has potential implications for patient management and risk stratification. Therefore, reporting severe MAC should include not only its anatomical extent, but also its potential significance as a marker of systemic cardiovascular disease and future procedural complexity. In selected patients, extensive MAC should not be regarded merely as an incidental degenerative finding, but rather as a potential marker of advanced systemic calcific cardiovascular disease.
