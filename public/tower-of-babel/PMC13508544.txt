Who knows where to find mental health help on campus? Structural, psychosocial and need-based predictors of knowledge of campus-based mental health services among post-secondary students
Authors: Sarah Brennenstuhl, Lexi Ewing, Sarah Kuburi, Sandy Welsh, Kristin Cleverley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite increasing reports of mental health problems among post-secondary students, only a small minority appear to be using campus-based mental health services. Given this discrepancy, research is required to better understand factors contributing to low service utilization to identify potential barriers to access, including knowledge of where to access services. As knowledge proceeds intention to use service, it represents a key upstream intervention target for student populations.MethodsData comes from the 2022 Canadian Campus Wellbeing Survey based on a large (n = 5578) sample from a single, multi-campus post-secondary institution with comprehensive mental health services. Logistic regression is used to identify structural (e.g., gender, ethnicity), psychosocial (e.g., favourable to professional help seeking) and needs-based (e.g., psychological distress, suicidal ideation) predictors of knowledge of where to access campus-based mental health services. Results are compared with a similar model predicting knowledge of where to access off-campus mental health services.ResultsA third of students did not have knowledge of where to find on-campus MH services; a significantly larger proportion lacked knowledge of off-campus services (45%, P<.001). Being aged 30–35 (vs. <20 years, OR = 0.68; 95% CI= 0.49-0.95), Asian ethnicity (OR = 0.62, 95% CI = 0.53-0.74), international student status (OR = 0.52; 95% CI 0.44-0.62), and being favourable to seeking professional help (OR = 0.56, 95% CI = 0.49-0.64) were associated with lower odds of not knowing where to find MH services on campus. Living off campus (OR = 1.89; 95% CI = 1.53–2.33), having a disability without academic accommodations (OR = 1.27, 95% CI = 1.08–1.50) and higher psychological distress (OR = 1.03, 95% CI = 1.02–1.04) were associated with higher odds of not knowing. Several important differences in predictors were found when comparing knowledge of on- and off-campus MH services.ConclusionA variety of factors are identified as predictors of knowledge of where to find on-campus MH services. Of great importance are the findings regarding need-based factors that underscore the necessity of upstream approaches for fostering greater awareness of services before students develop acute mental health needs and may be less able to acquire the knowledge needed to get help.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14963-8.

Background

Post-secondary students (PSS) are reporting increasing levels of psychological distress and mental health challenges compared to a decade ago [1, 2]. Findings from the World Health Organization World Mental Health Surveys (WMH-ICS) indicate that approximately one in five PSS meet the criteria for a mental health disorder, with more than 80% entering post-secondary studies with a pre-existing diagnosis [3]. These findings suggest a growing demand for campus-based mental health services [1, 4] as a substantial proportion of PSS may require support during their academic careers. Some estimates suggest that only 12%-15% of students use campus-based mental health services [4, 5], and this rate has nearly doubled over the past 10 years [4]. Mental health (MH) service utilization, therefore, remains relatively low compared to the burden of psychological distress and MH disorders reported among student populations. Given this discrepancy, further investigation is needed to better understand the factors contributing to low service utilization and to identify potential barriers to access among PSS.

One factor that has been shown to contribute to low service utilization and act as a barrier to access is students’ lack of knowledge of the MH services available on their own campus [5, 6]. Many studies have assessed some aspect of PSS’ knowledge of campus-based MH services, demonstrating that greater knowledge is associated with an increased likelihood of help-seeking and service use, as well as more positive attitudes and intentions toward care [5–16]. Given the well-established effectiveness of MH treatments in the general population [17], increasing knowledge of available campus-based MH services not only encourages greater utilization but also may contribute to improved MH outcomes among PSS.

Most publicly-funded Canadian post-secondary institutions (PSI) offer at least some MH treatment services on campus [18]. However, previous research suggests that not all PSS have equal knowledge of campus-based MH services despite having access to services based on student status [7, 9, 11, 12]. Awareness campaigns and outreach programs that are typical of many PSIs [18] may not be effective for ensuring all students have knowledge of MH services. Indeed, the majority of students themselves believe that PSIs should increase awareness of MH resources [19]. Given the relationship between knowledge and service use [5, 6, 13], a better understanding of the drivers of knowledge inequalities will help identify (a) which student groups should be targeted for awareness-building interventions, and (b) how those interventions can be best tailored to the student population at risk.

Multi-level frameworks that include distal (i.e. structural), proximal (i.e. psychosocial) and need-based (i.e. clinical) factors are best suited to capture a full range of mental health determinants that can be targeted for intervention [20]. Multi-level models also align with the need for more equity-oriented, whole university approaches in post-secondary MH research contrasting the usual focus on individual-level factors [21–23]. Distal factors include demographics, socioeconomic position (SEP), and institutional factors that may influence familiarity with the MH system, access to information, and extent to which structural barriers such as stigma exist [24–26].

Prior research has explored structural variables in relation to knowledge equalities only in a limited manner. Yorgason and colleagues’ research of undergraduate students from nearly 20 years ago represents one of the only peer-reviewed studies that has modeled multiple predictors of knowledge of campus-based MH services, including structural factors, controlling for need [7]. They found that female-identified students, those who lived on campus and those who had spent more time at the university were more knowledgeable about campus-based MH services over and above mental health needs [7]. While this work was important for mapping out the relationship between multiple variables and knowledge of campus-based MH resources, newer research is needed using a larger sample and including a wider range of important student characteristics.

Structural factors that have yet to be considered at all or in sufficient depth in relation to knowledge inequalities include gender, race/ethnicity, international student status, sexual orientation, age and parental SEP. Limited research looking at gender has shown no differences in knowledge among PSS [9, 11], or higher knowledge among females [7, 16]. Racial/ethnic differences have been found in some studies [7, 11], but not others [16]; a more complete assessment of race/ethnicity using large, diverse samples is required. An older study found that international students have lower knowledge of campus-based counselling services than domestic students [27], but more up-to-date research reflecting the current environment for international students is needed. 2SLGBTQIA+ students have been found to be more likely to seek out services overall, but it is not clear if this is driven by greater knowledge over and above higher need [9, 28]. Finally, while higher-year students have been shown to have more knowledge than lower-year students, it is not clear if this is driven by age or greater exposure to campus [11].

Psychosocial factors that influence whether one pays attention to, seeks out, or retains information about MH services, such as attitudes toward help seeking, should also be considered in relation of knowledge inequalities [13, 29]. Attitudes toward help-seeking are closely related to MH service access and engagement [5, 6, 8, 12], however, as far as we know, they have not been examined as predictors of service knowledge. Other factors that predispose students to interact with the system may include disability status, especially in the post-secondary context, as assessment of students with a MH-related disability by a MH professional to determine need for academic accommodations is recommended [30]. Substance use may have an opposite effect, resulting in disengagement from campus life and lack of information about MH services that might help [31].

Need factors such as perceived symptoms and psychological distress influence the salience of MH services and determine what students might ultimately engage in service use [32]. Alternatively, greater need may be associated with more barriers such as perceived stigma and lower functioning, potentially resulting in avoidance or deficits in processing information [14]. Prior research on the role of psychological distress has been mixed: some studies indicate that students experiencing greater psychological distress are more likely to be aware of available services [7], others suggest they are less likely to have such knowledge [6, 7, 14, 15] and yet others have found no association [10]. To fully tease out the relationship, structural factors associated with need and help seeking attitudes should be consistently controlled.

Building on the existing literature examining knowledge of MH services among PSS, the primary purpose of the present research study is to use a multi-level model of structural, psychosocial and need-based factors to predict knowledge of campus-based MH services in a large, diverse sample of PSS. This is the first study we are aware of to investigate a broad range of variables at multiple levels simultaneously to determine the most salient factors to target for intervention. As a secondary goal, to understand if factors that predict campus-based service knowledge are unique from those that predict MH service knowledge within the wider system, predictors of knowledge about on-campus mental health services will be compared with those related to off-campus MH services. This comparison will help determine if knowledge varies by service context and whether interventions to increase knowledge need to be tailored accordingly. This project aligns with the call to action for more high-quality research on MH systems in the post-secondary context [33].

Methods

Study design and data overview

This study presents a secondary analysis of data from the 2022 Canadian Campus Wellbeing Survey (CCWS; https://www.ccws-becc.ca/). The CCWS is administered at PSIs across Canada and is designed to assess overall student wellbeing and generate data to inform the development, implementation, and evaluation of institutional policies, programs, and practice guidelines that support the mental, physical, and academic wellbeing of PSS. The survey includes measures of mental health service utilization, help-seeking behaviours, and comprehensive student demographic information. Additional details on the CCWS study design, participant consent, methodology, measures, and data access are available in previously published sources [34].

The present study draws on data collected from a single, large, research-intensive university in an urban setting with well-established and comprehensive mental health services available across multiple campuses in diverse settings. Data collection at our PSI was approved by the Research Ethics Board (REB #38626) in 2022. This research project received ethics approval at [institution blinded] [REB #44273].

Participants and procedures

The sample includes CCWS participants from a single large PSI for which the study team had data access. No additional inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied. A random sample of approximately 32,000 direct-entry undergraduate students and 8,000 graduate and second-entry undergraduate domestic and international students of all study levels were invited to participate at the PSI via recruitment emails. PSS voluntarily participated by responding to the invitations. The survey was administered anonymously online and required approximately 15–20 min to complete. At the institutional level, the survey had a 17% response rate, which is consistent with the overall response rate for the 2022 deployment across the 21 institutions of 18.8% [35].

Measures

Outcome variables

Knowledge of campus-based MH services was assessed using the following item: “How much do you agree with the following statement? If I needed to seek professional help for my mental or emotional health, I would know where to access campus-based resources (online or in-person).” Response options ranged from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). For the purposes of the current research, this item was recoded into a binary variable representing those who somewhat (4), agree (5) or strongly agree (6) as having knowledge and those who somewhat disagree (3), disagree (2) or strongly disagree (1) as not having knowledge. To provide context for the primary research objective, a similar item assessing knowledge of off-campus MH services was also used as follows: If I needed to seek professional help for my mental or emotional health, I would know where to go off campus. Response options were dichotomized into a binary variable in a similar manner as described above.

Predictors variables

The following predictor variables were selected based on the expertise of our research team and the literature, where possible.

Structural variables included demographics such as age (< 20, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 34+), ethnicity (Black, Arab/West Asian, East and South Asian, Latin American, white, multiracial, another race), gender identity (woman, man, gender diverse, prefer not to answer), and sexual orientation (heterosexual, 2SLGBTQIA+, prefer not to answer). SEP and background variables included parental socio-economic status ([SES], university degree, no university degree, don’t know/prefer not to answer) and domestic versus international student, with domestic students sub-divided according to if they were born in Canada (1) domestic student – born in Canada (2), domestic student – not born in Canada and (3) international student. Demographic variables were recoded from the original CCWS measures where needed to minimize the impact of low frequency counts in small groups at the institutional level (e.g. gender, race, sexual orientation). Institutional factors included whether the student reported living (1) on campus or (2) off campus and if the student lives out of province (yes, no). Finally, year of study was included as a continuous variable to represent the length of exposure to the campus.

Psychosocial factors included help seeking attitudes assessed by the question: “If you were experiencing serious emotional distress, whom would you talk to about this?” Those who selected the option “professional clinician (e.g., psychologist, counsellor, or psychiatrist)” were coded as favourable to professional help and those that did not select this option were coded as unfavourable to professional help. Whether a student had a self-reported disability (mental health or any other type) was included in this grouping and combined with a question about if they received academic accommodations for the disability to produce a four-level categorial variable including: (1) disability but with no formal academic accommodations (2), disability with formal academic accommodations (3), no disability and (4) prefer not to answer. Since best practices indicate assessment of students with a MH-related disability by a MH professional to determine need for accommodations [34], we considered that presence of disability with accommodations would have a different relationship to service knowledge than having a disability alone. Substance use was also assessed in the psychosocial grouping using three variables. Binge drinking was based on the question: “During the past 30 days, how often have you had 4 or more drinks (female sex) OR 5 or more drinks (male sex) on one occasion?” Students answered the question based on their self-identified sex, reporting that they had met the threshold for binge drinking more than once in the past month were coded as such. Use of pain relievers and/or stimulants for purposes other than intended was coded as such. Intensive cannabis use was coded as using three or more days per week [36]. Due to the sensitive nature of the question, all three substance use variables included a prefer not to answer/do not know response option.

Need variables included level of psychological distress, which was measured using the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K-10), with higher scores indicating more distress [37]. The scale has shown to be valid and reliable in the general population [37] and among PSS populations [38]. The internal consistency of the K-10 was found to be excellent in this sample (α = 0.92). Finally, suicidal ideation was also included as a need variable assessed using the question: “In the past 12 months have you ever seriously thought about committing suicide or taking your own life?” (yes, no, prefer not to answer).

Statistical analysis

All analysis were undertaken in SAS (version 9.4) [39]. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was used to assess significance. The sample was summarized using descriptive statistics, such as means (standard deviations) for continuous variables and frequency counts (percentages) for discrete variables. Sample descriptives were compared to institutional data on the student population where possible.

Multiple logistic regression analysis was used to identify predictors of lack of knowledge of campus-based mental health services. While ordinal logistic regression could have been used to preserve the original specification of the outcome variable, the inclusion of many predictors (with many levels each) lead us to select a more interpretable method. Models were run to predict the odds of not knowing where to find MH services, since we were interested in identifying the group at a greater risk. All variables were entered into the model simultaneously and left in regardless of p value. This was done so that we could tease out how certain factors (e.g., gender) may influence knowledge over-and-above other variables (e.g., psychological distress). Due to a large amount of missing data (range 13%-27% per variable, total across all model variables = 19%), multiple imputation (MI) was used as the primary analytic strategy with the assumption that the data were missing at random. The Proc MI program in SAS was used based on the fully conditional method, which uses a separate conditional distribution for each imputed variable [40]. This method was selected because of the mixture of continuous and categorical variables in the model. The imputation model included all the predictor variables in the logistic regression model. All variables, including the outcome, were used to predict all other variables in the model. A total of 20 datasets were imputed reflecting the percentage of missing data of about 20%. Results are provided for the complete case analysis and the MI analysis for comparison. The analysis strategy was replicated to identify predictors of knowledge of how to access MH services off campus.

As a sensitivity analysis, the outcome variables were recoded to represent a higher threshold of knowledge to include only those who agreed/very much agreed with the idea that they had knowledge of how to access MH services on campus and off campus, respectively. Results of the sensitivity analysis are compared to those of the primary analysis.

Results

Characteristics of the study sample

The sample included 5578 students with data on one or more self-reported variable; 4518 had complete data. The sample description is based on the raw data; amount of missing data for each variable is provided. The largest proportion of students responding to the survey were aged 20–24 (48.1%), followed by those less than age 20 (28.2%). Women made up 68.5% of the sample of respondents, with gender diverse individuals representing less than 5%. One in four student respondents identified as part of the 2SLGBTQIA+ community. Nearly one in two student respondents identified as Asian, including East (e.g., Chinese, Korean) and South (e.g., Indian, Pakistan) Asian (48%), and just over one in four as white (26.8%). A quarter of those survey responders were international students (25.0%). Just over two-thirds of students had a parent with a university degree (67.7%). Demographic and descriptive characteristics of CCWS sample

In comparison to data on students provided by the institution, the proportions responding to the survey were very close to those at the university for international student status (28.7% vs. 25%) [41], proportion identifying as white (24.7% vs. 28.8%), and those identifying as part of the 2SLQBTQIA+ community (21.6% vs. 25%) [42]. Women (49.7% vs. 68.5%) and students with parents who have a university degree (59.3% vs. 67.7%) were over-represented, and Asian students (55.3% vs. 48.0%) were slightly underrepresented [42].

Just over one in 10 student respondents reported living on campus (13.2%) and 3.7% were out of province. Students on average were currently in the 2nd year of their program (SD = 1.3). Fully 37% of PSS were favourable to receiving professional mental health help. About one in 20 (5.5%) indicated using pain relievers or stimulants for purposes other than intended. A similar proportion reported cannabis use (5.1%). About one in five endorsed binge drinking more than once in the past month (20.6%). Just less than a quarter identified as having a disability but did not receive any academic accommodations (23.0%) and one in 10 had a disability with academic accommodation (10.7%). The mean score on the K-10 was 27.8 (SD = 8.7). Fully 13% of students reported that they had seriously thought about suicide in the past 12 months.

Two out of three students (66.6%) at least somewhat agreed with the statement if I needed to seek professional help for my mental or emotional health, I would know where to access campus-based resources (online or in-person). This compares to just over half of students (54.9%) who at least somewhat agreed with the statement if I needed to seek professional help for my mental or emotional health, I would know where to go off campus. The proportion with knowledge where to access off-campus MH service is significantly lower than the on-campus proportion (χ2 = 256.3, p<.001).

When the threshold for knowledge of where to access MH services was raised, 38.2% agreed or very much agreed with the statement about knowing how to access on campus MH services and 33.0% agreed or very much agreed with the statement about knowing how to access off campus MH services. The proportion with knowledge where to access off-campus MH service remained significantly lower than the on-campus proportion (χ2 = 428.6, p<.001).

Structural predictors: Age, ethnicity and international student status were all significant predictors of not knowing how to access MH services on campus. For age, while older students generally had higher odds of knowledge, the exact group that was found to be significantly different varied according to how the outcome was specified. For example, using the lower threshold, 30–34-year-olds had lower odds of not knowing (i.e., higher odds of knowledge) than those < 20 years old (model 1: OR = 0.68; 95% CI= 0.49-0.95). Using the higher threshold, 25–29-year-olds also had lower odds of not knowing (model 2: OR = 0.68, 95% CI = 0.55-0.84). As for race, Asian students had lower odds of not knowing (i.e. higher odds of knowledge) than white students in both models (model 1: OR = 0.62, 95% CI = 0.53-0.74). Similarly, international students had lower odds of not knowing (i.e., higher odds of knowledge) in both models than domestic students born in Canada (model 1: OR = 0.52; 95% CI 0.44-0.62). Living off campus was associated with higher odds of not knowing how to access on campus MH services in both models (model 1: OR = 1.89; 95% CI = 1.53–2.33). Gender, sexual orientation, parental SES and living out of province were not related to knowledge of on-campus MH services when all other variables were considered.

Psychosocial predictors: Being favourable to obtaining professional MH help was associated with lower odds of not knowing (i.e. higher odds of knowledge) how to access MH services on campus in both models (model 1: OR = 0.56, 95% CI = 0.49-0.64). Those with a disability without accommodations had higher odds of not knowing how to access MH services compared to those without a disability (model 1: OR = 1.27, 95% CI = 1.08–1.5). However, using the higher threshold, those with a disability and accommodations had lower odds of not knowing (i.e. higher odds of knowledge; model 2: OR = 0.63; 95% CI = 0.51-0.78).

Need-based predictors: Higher K-10 scores were associated with higher odds of not knowing how to access MH services on campus in both models (model 1: OR = 1.03, 95% CI = 1.02–1.04).

Discussion

Two-thirds of PSS at least somewhat agreed that they knew where to access on campus MH services at a large, urban PSI with a diverse student body. This proportion is significantly higher than that representing students who know where to access MH services off campus, suggesting provision of campus-based MH services may reduce barriers to access, including knowledge of where to go. One third of PSS, however, report that they do not have the knowledge needed to access MH services on campus. As knowledge is foundational to developing intention to seek treatment [43], ensuring students have accurate information about MH services offers a powerful upstream intervention that may lead to improvement in outcomes.

Using a multi-level model including a broad range of structural, psychosocial and need-based variables we identified factors that can be targeted to reduce inequalities in knowledge. Structural factors that consistently predicted knowledge of where to access MH services on campus over and above all other model variables included age, ethnicity, international student status and living on campus. Being aged 30–34 (vs. <20 years) was associated with higher odds of knowing where to find MH services on campus. Being 30 years or older was also associated with higher odds of knowing where to find MH services off campus. The relationship between age and knowledge was found over-and-above program year, suggesting that greater system knowledge comes from more life experience and formal knowledge [44]. Our data suggests that younger students may be at a higher risk for not accessing MH services since they had lower odds of knowledge of services in both campus-based and wider health systems and should be targeted for intervention.

Asian students, including those from East (e.g., China) and South (e.g., India) Asia, had higher odds of knowing where to access MH services on campus compared to white students. A report from another Canadian university demonstrated a similar finding that Chinese students had greatest awareness of MH services on campus compared to other racial groups [11]. At our institution, Asian students make up the largest racial group and it is possible that far-reaching social networks that act as information channels to help facilitate word of mouth, creating social capital and reducing stigma regarding use of MH services on campus, which positively influences help seeking attitudes [44, 45]. Supporting initiatives to develop social networks among smaller ethnic groups or those with less history at the PSI may help grow the social capital needed to positively influence help seeking. Latin American students were found to have higher odds of not knowing where to find MH services off campus, with no differences found compared to white students related to on campus knowledge; Yorgason showed that Latino/a students had proportionately lower knowledge of campus-based resources than other ethnic groups [7]. Asian and Arab/West Asian students were also found to have higher odds of not knowing where to find MH services off campus, but only based on the lower threshold of the outcome. The latter finding mirrors research demonstrating that Asian and West Asian/Arab youth are less likely to have a MH-related service contact than white youth, controlling for MH symptoms and need [46]. Promoting and maintaining awareness of campus-based MH services among diverse groups of students who are less likely to have MH service connections in the wider health care system should be prioritized for reducing overall inequalities in access to MH care.

International students had higher odds of knowing where to access MH services on campus and lower odds of knowing where to access MH services off campus compared to domestic students born in Canada. This contradicts an earlier study that demonstrated international students had lower knowledge of on campus counselling services [27]. At our institution, at least, the finding that international students are more likely to know about on campus MH supports versus off campus underscores how international students’ wellbeing is closely tied to the PSI they attend as they do not have local support systems in place [47, 48]. It will be important for PSIs to closely monitor awareness of on campus MH services among international students to ensure students know where to go given their vulnerability in the wider system.

We did not find a difference in knowledge of on-campus MH services based on gender consistent with some previous research [9, 11]. However, men and gender diverse individuals were found to have higher odds of knowing where to find MH services off campus (based on the higher and lower threshold of the outcome, respectively). For gender diverse students, knowledge of off campus MH services may reflect complexity of the need, especially among those transitioning, which can require more specialized services than that are offered on campus. While a similar argument based on complexity of need could be made for 2SLGBTQIA+ students, we found this group to have higher odds of not knowing where to find MH services off campus and no relationship detected with knowledge of services on campus. This finding suggests that 2SLGBTQIA+ students may be particularly vulnerable given lower knowledge of the wider MH system, especially if they are not using on-campus services, indicating targeted outreach is required. Disaggregation of the 2SLGBTQIA+ umbrella into specific identity-based groups may be critical for effectively targeting subpopulations based on specific MH needs and risks [49].

Consistent with previous research [7], students living on campus were more likely to know where to access MH services on campus. They were also less likely to know where to access MH services off campus. This underscores the importance of residential proximity for predisposing PSS to service knowledge. However, since most students live off campus, residential proximity has limited reach, necessitating other methods for increasing awareness that create the right conditions for supporting knowledge and access [50]. For example, locating services or information about services near major transit hubs on campus may increase awareness and ease of access among commuter students.

Only just over a third of students (37%) reported that they would talk to a professional (e.g., psychologist, counsellor, psychiatrist) about their mental health. This is consistent with findings from the WMH-ICS initiative, which also showed a low proportion of students that were willing to seek treatment (25%), preferring to handle the problem alone [14]. Those favourable to talking with a professional had higher odds of knowing how to access on or off campus MH services. Given efforts to raise awareness and address the stigma of mental health help seeking [51], it is surprising that a larger portion of students did not indicate being favourable to talking to a professional about their MH. Some research, however, shows that students who more strongly identify with their university report greater stigma in professional help seeking on campus [52]. Stigma [5, 6, 14] and confidentiality [12, 53] have been identified as barriers to accessing on-campus MH services in other pan-Canadian research and internationally. Given that the CCWS is a post-secondary-administered survey, it is possible talking about professional help seeking in this context led to under-reporting. PSIs may need to actively challenge stigma around accessing professional help on campus through improving students’ mental health literacy [54]. This could be achieved by reinforcing the independence of campus MH services from core academic aspects of institution (e.g., advertising where to find information on policies related to confidentiality and privacy of health information) and by increasing awareness of MH services positioned as external to the university [52], including high-quality online resources that may be perceived as more private [14]. Past help seeking behaviour predicts future help seeking attitudes. Those with past experience not only have the knowledge of how to access MH services, but their experience can be leveraged within their social networks, as knowing someone who has sought professional help is shown to encourage positive help seeking attitudes [45]. Therefore, student peer support workers, who provide navigation to on campus MH resources from the perspective of a student [55] may help drive positive on-campus help seeking attitudes.

We expected that students with a disability would have higher odds of knowledge of where to access MH resources due to the necessity of receiving the health professional assessment needed to obtain accommodations [30]. Indeed, based on the higher threshold, those with a disability and academic accommodations had higher odds of knowing where to access on campus MH services. They also had higher odds of knowing where to access MH services off campus. However, students with a disability who had not received academic accommodations had higher odds of not knowing where to access MH services on campus when the lower threshold of the outcome was applied. This could reflect lack of knowledge of accommodations in general or stigma around receiving one, especially among those with invisible disabilities (such as those related to MH) [56], both of which could influence MH service knowledge acquisition. While knowledge of MH services appears key to having an accommodation as hypothesized, increasing awareness of on-campus MH services may be particularly helpful for those with a disability who could benefit from accommodation in addition to MH support and enhanced coordination between these services.

Lastly, focusing on need-based variables, those with higher levels of distress were more likely not to have knowledge of where to access on or off campus MH services. This finding may reflect reverse causation whereby those who have accessed MH supports (and therefore know how to) are less likely to have higher levels of distress. While some research has shown that greater psychological distress is associated with knowledge of campus-based MH services [7], other research has found that those with symptoms of anxiety/depression report uncertainty about how to get help on campus [6] and face higher stigma-based barriers that may influence knowledge acquisition [5, 14]. Moreover, higher mental health literacy has been associated with lower psychological distress and better mental health [57, 58]. Indeed, this relationship is thought to be explained by greater help seeking [59]. In other words, despite heightened need, students with acute MH challenges may be least able to navigate themselves to services, necessitating an upstream approach to creating awareness and help-seeking behaviours before students are too highly symptomatic. It is worth noting that the average level of distress in the sample was 27.8 on the K-10, which places the sample in the moderate mental distress category [37], highlighting the importance of monitoring distress in relation to access to MH services.

Students reporting suicidal ideation were no more likely to know where to access MH services on campus than those without. This is consistent with other research focused on service knowledge [10] and studies showing that the majority of students that go on to attempt or complete suicide are not identified on campus [19, 60]. Students reporting suicidal ideation were also not more likely to know how to access MH services off campus (based on the lower threshold of knowledge), which raises concern about how to best meet the needs of high risk students. Students with higher levels of perceived stigma around suicide may experience higher stigma in help seeking. It is also possible that self-perceived need does not align with clinically-assessed need [23]. Again, this suggests the important role of reducing stigma and increasing awareness of the seriousness of suicidal ideation before help-seeking is acutely needed [23].

This study has several strengths including the use of a large sample of diverse students from a PSI offering comprehensive MH services on campus and the examination of a broad range of structural, psychosocial and need-based predictors. However, several limitations are worth noting. First, data is used from one PSI only so while the large sample size suggests generalization to other similar universities is possible, there is likely great variation in levels of knowledge across PSIs nationally (and internationally). This is indicated by the much lower proportion of PSS (28%) who had awareness of how to access on-campus services based on a pan-Canadian study than was reported here [53]. Differences across PSIs, including size, extent to which MH services are offered and advertised on campus and MH climate likely influence knowledge. Therefore, use of data from multiple PSIs and application of multi-level modeling would better support generalizability and help identify what institutional-level factors may be important to consider.

Second, we found some differences in the results according to how we specified the outcome variable, especially in the off-campus model. This suggests that a more robust measure of service knowledge may be required to take this research further. Related, it has shown that students often demonstrate misperceptions about on-campus MH services [12]. This research has no way to validate that students’ reported knowledge is accurate (and therefore could translate into service use), pointing to the need for a measure of service knowledge that assesses accuracy. Related, this research could not determine how students obtain knowledge and therefore while the results provide new direction on who needs to be targeted, they do not as clearly shed light on how knowledge acquisition can be improved. Finally, while the survey sample was broadly representative of the PSI, women and those with parents having a university degree were over-represented, and Asian students were underrepresented. It is unclear how these differences may have impacted the findings. Future research should make use of representative samples across multiple PSIs.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a third of students at a large PSI with a comprehensive network of MH services on campus did not have knowledge of where to find such services. This proportion was significantly smaller than those who did not have knowledge of where to access MH services off campus. This suggests offering services on campus may reduce knowledge-based barriers to access. A variety of structural (e.g., older age, Asian ethnicity, international student status and living on campus) and psychosocial (e.g., favourable to seeking professional help) factors were identified as predictors of having knowledge of on campus MH services over and above need variables. Of great importance are the findings regarding need-based factors, including higher psychological distress, which was associated with lower odds of knowledge, and suicidal ideation, which was not associated with knowledge, that underscore the need for upstream approaches for fostering greater awareness of service options before students develop acute MH needs and are less able to acquire the knowledge needed to get help. Additionally, variation in predictors of on- and off-campus MH service knowledge suggests that groups lacking knowledge of the broader MH system should be targeted to increase awareness of on-campus services to mitigate overall inequalities in access to care.
