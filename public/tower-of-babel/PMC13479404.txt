Correlates of thorough skin self-examination intentions among individuals at risk for skin cancer
Authors: Sanskriti Iyer, David Perez, Divyesh Parthiban, Zhaomeng Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe incidence of skin cancer, including basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and melanoma, is rising in the United States. Thorough skin self-examination (TSSE) is crucial for identifying new or recurrent melanomas. While widely promoted for early detection, variability in its definition and limited research on TSSE intention in at-risk adults indicate an ongoing need to better understand its correlates. This study aimed to examine demographic, psychosocial, attitudinal, and behavioral correlates of TSSE intention among adults at increased risk for skin cancer using the Health Belief Model.MethodsParticipants (n = 319) were recruited via Amazon Mechanical Turk and screened through Qualtrics to meet eligibility criteria for at-risk populations (e.g., skin type, mole count, or family history of melanoma, excluding those with a personal history of skin cancer). A hierarchical multivariable regression analysis was performed to examine the variables associated with TSSE intent.ResultsThe sample was composed of participants with an average age of 37 years, and 56.4% were female, while 60.1% identified as non-Hispanic White. Demographic factors and behavioral history had minimal impact on TSSE intentions, while psychosocial factors and attitudes emerged as strong correlates. In particular, perceived risk of skin cancer (p < 0.05, β = 0.340), perceived self-efficacy (p = 0.021, β = 0.134), skin cancer-related fear (p < 0.05, β = 0.129), and social influences (p < 0.05, β = 0.191) were all significantly associated with TSSE intentions. Attitudes, including perceived benefits (p < 0.05, β = 0.199) and barriers to TSSE (p < 0.05, β = -0.405), were also significantly associated with TSSE intention. The regression model was statistically significant and explained 43.1% of the variance in TSSE intention (R2 = 0.431, p < 0.001).ConclusionThis study highlights perceived risk, perceived self-efficacy, social influences, skin cancer-related fear, and benefits and barriers as important correlates of TSSE intention among individuals at increased risk for skin cancer. These findings have the potential to inform tailored interventions to improve the early detection of skin cancer.Keywords: Skin cancer, Melanoma, Thorough skin self-examination (TSSE), Sun protection, Perceived risk, Health beliefs, Social influences, Early detection

Introduction

Skin cancers, including basal cell carcinoma (BCC), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), and melanoma, are among the most common cancers in the world [23]. Over 1.2 million nonmelanoma (BCC and SCC) and 330,000 melanoma cases were reported worldwide in 2022 [12, 26]. Of these, more than 69,000 deaths were attributed to nonmelanoma skin cancer and nearly 60,000 to melanoma [23]. Incidence rates vary greatly by location. The highest rates occur in places with high ultraviolet (UV) exposure, including Australia, New Zealand, northern Europe, and North America [5].

In the United States (US), skin cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer [7]. It is estimated that 5.4 million new cases of nonmelanoma skin cancer are diagnosed per year, resulting in about 2,000 to 8,000 deaths annually [2]. Melanoma incidence has risen to over 100,000 cases each year, leading to more than 8,000 deaths yearly [3, 18]. Even though melanoma accounts for about 1% of all skin cancer cases, it remains the leading cause of skin cancer deaths due to high rates of metastasis [3].

The increasing burden of skin cancer comes with a notable rise in its economic impact. Nonmelanoma skin cancer alone accounts for 6.1 million cases treated each year [7]. Annual direct medical costs for skin cancer treatment in the US are estimated at about $8.9 billion, indicating high healthcare usage [7]. This rise is due not only to higher incidence rates but also to more frequent use of dermatological services and rising treatment costs per patient. Although management costs for other types of cancer have also increased, skin cancer remains a significant and growing public health challenge in the US and beyond.

Early detection remains an important strategy for reducing the burden and cost of skin cancer. Skin self-examination (SSE) is often recommended to help identify suspicious lesions, particularly among individuals at increased risk for skin cancer. Prior research has suggested that regular thorough skin self-examination (TSSE) may be associated with lower melanoma-related mortality, potentially because it facilitates earlier detection of tumor recurrence between scheduled follow up visits [1]. However, the literature has not used a consistent definition of SSE, making it difficult to compare findings across studies [9]. In many studies, general SSE has been discussed interchangeably with TSSE, a more structured head to toe examination of the entire skin surface for new or changing lesions. This inconsistency may partly explain why reported prevalence estimates have varied across studies [4]. Moreover, prior research has largely focused on melanoma survivors and on actual self-examination behavior rather than intention, which may limit its applicability to cancer-free adults at elevated risk. Studying TSSE intention in this population is important as intention is a central construct in health behavior theories and may help identify modifiable psychosocial and attitudinal targets for interventions that support earlier detection.

Kasl and Cobb define health behavior as “any activity undertaken by a person who believes himself to be healthy for the purpose of preventing disease or detecting disease in an asymptomatic stage” [21]. Change in health behavior is affected by multiple factors including perceived negative and positive effects following a recommended behavior [11]. The Health Belief Model (HBM) offers a helpful framework for understanding these actions, including TSSE, as it explains how individuals’ beliefs may influence their engagement with behaviors related to skin cancer prevention and early detection [11]. HBM constructs that have been proposed to influence the adoption of preventive behaviors include: perceived risk (how susceptible a person believes they are to developing cancer), perceived severity (an individual’s belief about the risks and complications from cancer), cues to action (familiarity with triggers that promote action), perceived benefits (belief in the benefits of preventive behaviors), perceived barriers (anticipated challenges that may prevent an individual from performing preventive behaviors), and self-efficacy (having the confidence to perform preventive behaviors) [13]. Guided by the HBM, this study examined demographic, psychosocial, attitudinal, and behavioral correlates of TSSE intention among adults at increased risk for skin cancer. Specifically, we asked which factors are associated with stronger intention to perform TSSE in this population. Insights gained from this research aim to inform the development of evidence-based interventions to increase TSSE engagement and strengthen skin cancer control efforts.

Methods

Study procedures

Participants were recruited using Amazon Mechanical Turk (Mturk), which is Amazon’s online marketplace for human intelligence tasks, allowing the study to collect data from individuals efficiently through structured tasks and monetary incentives. MTurk has been widely used in psychosocial and public health research and has shown good reliability and data quality with proper screening and quality control procedures [22]. To ensure data integrity, participation was restricted to workers with a 95% approval rate and at least 500 approved tasks. Individuals with MTurk accounts could view the study information on a task page which was created as a “work assignment.” Potential participants were then directed to Qualtrics to complete a brief screening survey. Individuals were eligible for the study if they were 18 years or older, were not undergoing active skin cancer treatment, and met at least one of the following criteria: (1) self-reported very fair skin type, (2) presence of two or more moles larger than a pencil eraser (¼ inch), (3) a first-degree relative (e.g., parent, sibling, or child) with a history of melanoma, or (4) sunburn and skin reaction history. Individuals with a history of skin cancer were excluded. The main study was then conducted using another survey in Qualtrics. Several data-checking procedures were implemented to ensure data quality [22]. During the main survey, instructional attention checks were used to identify inattentive responding, such as requiring participants to select a specific response option. Completion times were also monitored to exclude participants who finished significantly faster than the established median.

Individuals who were eligible for the study were first shown the consent form. After agreeing to participate, they proceeded to complete a survey to evaluate their attitudes, perceptions, and skin cancer-related behaviors such as skin examination. Upon completing the Qualtrics survey, they received a code that allowed them to claim a reward directly through Amazon. This method allowed researchers to provide incentives without collecting personal information to safeguard participant anonymity. The survey took about 20 to 30 min to finish, and each participant was compensated $3.50 for their participation. Approval for the study was granted by the university’s institutional review board as part of a larger research project investigating skin examination and sun protection, as well as attitudes toward, acceptance of, and the design of a digital skin cancer intervention. The present study reports only on the potential correlates of TSSE intent.

Measures

Demographics

Participants reported their age, gender, race/ethnicity, and education level. Skin cancer risk factors were measured via self-reported questionnaires (e.g., eye color, family skin cancer history).

TSSE intent

TSSE was defined as performing a thorough, systematic examination of all areas of the skin, including areas that are difficult to see. In order to distinguish SSE from TSSE, participants were asked whether they “actually look at all the different areas of [their] skin deliberately and systematically,” including 8 key areas outlined by Weinstock et al. [24]. Intent to perform TSSE was assessed using a single item: “How likely do you think it is that you will do a thorough skin self-examination?” rated on a 7-point scale (1 = not at all likely to 7 = very likely).

Psychosocial factors

Knowledge of abnormal skin lesion identification was assessed using seven items. Each item presented an image of a mole and asked participants to indicate whether it represented a potential melanoma. Response options included “yes,” “no,” and “don’t know.” Five of the seven images depicted abnormal lesions. A total knowledge score, ranging from 0 to 7, was computed by summing the number of correct responses for each participant, with “don’t know” coded as incorrect. The mean score was 2.96 with a standard deviation of 1.72.

Perceived self-efficacy in performing TSSE was measured using six items on 5-point confidence scales (1 = not at all confident to 5 = very confident): (1) How confident are you that you know how to examine the skin on your face for signs of skin cancer? (2) How confident are you that you know how to examine the skin on your body for signs of skin cancer? (3) How confident are you that you can have someone help you regularly check the places on your body that you cannot see by yourself? (4) How confident are you that you can use mirrors to help you check the places on your body that you cannot see by yourself? (5) How confident are you that you can tell the difference between a melanoma and normal skin growths? (6) How confident are you that you can conduct a regular, thorough examination of the skin on your entire body to check for skin cancer? The mean score was 3.52 with a standard deviation of 0.78.

Perceived risk of skin cancer (α = 0.836) was assessed using four items on 5-point agreement scales (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree): (1) How would you rate your chances of having melanoma? (2) How would you rate your chances of having melanoma compared to other people at increased risk for melanoma? (3) I feel that I will get melanoma in the future. (4) I feel very vulnerable to having melanoma in the future. The mean score was 2.71 with a standard deviation of 0.93.

Social influences/norms regarding TSSE (α = 0.807) were assessed with three items on 5-point agreement scales (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree): (1) My friends think I should conduct skin self-examination. (2) My family thinks I should conduct skin self-examination. (3) Most people I consider important think I should conduct skin self-examination. The mean score was 3.54 with a standard deviation of 0.84.

Skin cancer-related fear (α = 0.888) was assessed using three items on 5-point scales (1 = not at all to 5 = very): (1) How scared are you about the possibility of getting skin cancer? (2) How frightened are you about the possibility of getting skin cancer? (3) How nervous are you about the possibility of getting skin cancer? The mean score was 2.83 with a standard deviation of 1.05.

Attitudes

Perceived benefits of TSSE (α = 0.826) were measured with eight items on 5-point agreement scales (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree): (1) By doing skin self-examination, I can find spots or growths on my skin that are cancerous or may become cancerous. (2) Doing skin self-examination is a part of overall good health care. (3) Doing regular skin self-examination would help me avoid developing a serious form of skin cancer. Other items truncated for brevity. The mean score was 3.98 with a standard deviation of 0.63.

Perceived barriers to TSSE (α = 0.895) were assessed using ten items on 5-point agreement scales (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree): (1) The thought of finding an abnormal spot or growth makes me quite anxious. (2) Doing skin self-examination would be very embarrassing. (3) It would take too much time to do regular skin self-examination. Other items truncated for brevity. The mean score was 3.23 with a standard deviation of 0.90.

Scoring

Higher scores across all scales reflected greater endorsement of the respective construct.

Behavioral history

Participants were also asked about prior professional or partner-assisted skin examinations (yes/no) with two items: (1) Have you ever had your skin checked for skin cancer from head to toe by a health professional? (2) Have you or a partner ever examined your entire body for skin cancer?

Data analysis

A power analysis for this linear multiple regression model indicated that a minimum sample of 135 participants was required to detect a medium effect size (f2 = 0.15) with 14 predictors at alpha = 0.05 and power = 0.80. The final sample of 319 exceeded this requirement. A hierarchical multivariable regression analysis was conducted to examine the variables associated with TSSE intentions. The analysis was performed in two steps: Step 1 included demographic variables (age, gender, race, and education), while Step 2 included additional attitudinal and cognitive correlates, including knowledge, attitudes, perceived risk, and social influences. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (versions 24/25). Before interpreting the regression results, model assumptions were evaluated. Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factor values. Normality and homogeneity of variance of residuals were examined using residual plots and normal probability plots. No major violations of regression assumptions were identified.

A majority (69.5%) of the participants were either currently enrolled in college or had completed a college degree (Table 1).

Attitudes

Attitudinal variables were also significantly associated with TSSE intention. Perceived benefits (M = 3.98, SD = 0.63, β = 0.199, p < 0.05) was positively associated with the outcome, while perceived barriers to TSSE (M = 3.22, SD = 0.89, β = −0.405, p < 0.05) was negatively associated with the outcome (Tables 1 and 2).

Model summary

To examine whether the correlates explained variance in participants’ intention to perform TSSE, a hierarchical regression analysis was conducted with two models. Model 1, which included only demographic variables, was not statistically significant (F = 1.580, p = 0.165) and explained only 2.5% of the variance in TSSE intention (R2 = 0.025; Table 2). Model 2 was statistically significant (F = 16.159, p < 0.001) and explained 43.1% of the variance in TSSE intention (R2 = 0.431; Table 2). This represented an increase in explained variance of ΔR2 = 0.406 over Model 1.

Discussion

In this study, TSSE intention was more strongly associated with psychosocial and attitudinal factors than with demographic characteristics. This pattern is consistent with prior work suggesting that preventive skin examination behaviors may be shaped less by fixed demographic characteristics and more by how individuals perceive their risk, confidence, and the value of the behavior [1]. In particular, perceived risk emerged as one of the strongest correlates in our model, suggesting that individuals who viewed themselves as more vulnerable to skin cancer were more likely to report stronger intention to perform TSSE [11]. This finding is theoretically consistent with the HBM, in which perceived risk is a central driver of preventive health intention and action. Positive attitudes toward TSSE, reflected by high perceived benefits and low perceived barriers, were also strongly associated with TSSE intent [14]. Such finding suggests that when TSSE is viewed as valuable in reducing skin cancer risk, without substantial inconvenience or discomfort, individuals may be more likely to intend to adopt the behavior. In addition to emphasizing the importance of cognitive appraisal in preventive decision-making, this finding also highlights the significance of addressing structural challenges to TSSE. Additionally, independent of TSSE knowledge, perceived self-efficacy was also a significant correlate, reinforcing the role of confidence in performing TSSE as an important enabling factor within the HBM [11]. These results highlight the HBM’s utility in explaining TSSE adoption by showing the separate but important roles cognitive determinants may play in motivating preventive behavior.

Beyond cognitive constructs, social influences/norms were another key correlate, suggesting that strong social expectations can enhance the likelihood of performing TSSE. This aligns with findings from Weinstock et al. [25], which showed that regular reminders and social cues can effectively promote TSSE. At the same time, prior research also suggests that the nature of social influence may be shifting. Earlier studies often emphasized encouragement from family members, partners, or healthcare providers, while more recent literature has drawn attention to the role of social media and online communication in shaping preventive health attitudes [10]. This distinction is particularly relevant in our study because participants were recruited through MTurk, an online platform, and may therefore be more exposed to digital forms of social messaging than the general population. Our study supports the idea that social influence, whether from peers, family, or media, can drive individuals to engage in TSSE. In addition to the influence of social networks, skin-cancer-related fear also emerged as a significant correlate. This finding emphasizes the importance of affective responses in motivating preventive behavior and suggests that fear may work alongside HBM constructs by increasing perceived risk and prompting action.

Examining the overall model performance provides additional insight into the relative importance of these correlates, particularly since model performance improved significantly between the two stages of analyses. The initial model, which included only demographic variables, had a poor fit and explained only 2.5% of the variance, while the final model, which incorporated cognitive, attitudinal, and social correlates, explained 43.1% of the variance. From a clinical and public health perspective, this suggests that interventions focused only on demographic factors are unlikely to be effective. Instead, strategies that focus on beliefs about risk and social influence may be more effective in improving TSSE-related intentions.

Our findings are further consistent with prior literature showing that preventive skin health practices are often underutilized, even among individuals at elevated risk for nonmelanoma and melanoma skin cancer [6, 16, 19, 20]. In a study by Niu et al. [20], for example, which examined the correlates of sun protection behaviors among Puerto Rican adults, participants demonstrated limited intention to perform TSSE. Their research also showed that participants infrequently engaged in sun protection behaviors, with a general trend of low engagement across the population [20]. Differences across racial and ethnic groups as well as cultural contexts may help explain why correlates of TSSE intention in our study may not fully mirror correlates of sun protection practices reported in earlier work [6, 16, 19, 20]. At the same time, however, TSSE may involve different barriers than those associated with sun-protection behaviors. For example, while sunscreen use or protective clothing may be more strongly shaped by outdoor work demands and environmental exposures, TSSE may depend more on perceived susceptibility, self-efficacy, and comfort with checking the body. This distinction may partially account for why psychosocial and attitudinal variables were especially prominent in our model.

Additionally, although TSSE engagement among melanoma survivors reportedly increased following an online intervention that participants perceived as trustworthy, easy to understand, and helpful, these findings suggest that while TSSE-related behaviors may be modifiable, they may not be directly generalizable to the present study population [17]. Compared with cancer-free adults at increased risk for skin cancer, melanoma survivors are likely to have greater baseline perceived risk, more frequent clinical follow-up, and stronger motivation to monitor for recurrence. These differences may help explain why perceived risk, fear, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, and self-efficacy emerged as particularly salient correlates of TSSE intention in our study.

Overall, this study highlights the importance of designing and testing tailored interventions that consider significant correlates rather than relying solely on demographic factors, which showed limited associative value. With appropriate intervention, TSSE’s negligible cost and accessibility make it especially valuable for long-term adoption, positioning it as a critical tool for skin cancer control efforts. Based on these findings, several specific and actionable recommendations can be proposed for future interventions. These should: (1) provide clear instruction on what TSSE is and how to perform it, (2) emphasize perceived risk through targeted communication, (3) support tools that improve self-efficacy like culturally appropriate step-by-step instructional videos, (4) increase perceived benefits (e.g., cost-effective) via personalized messaging, (5) reduce perceived barriers (e.g., fear, inconvenience) through skill building and technology support, and (6) incorporate TSSE reminders from family, peers, and/or digital applications.

Limitations

The small sample size may limit the generalizability of our findings, as a larger sample might have captured a more diverse range of socioeconomic factors, health beliefs, and behaviors. Use of MTurk might further limit generalizability as participants using MTurk may differ from the general population in age, education, socioeconomic status, and technology use. While MTurk offers quick and affordable access to participants, the sample might overrepresent individuals who are more comfortable with online surveys and more involved in health research, limiting how well the results apply to broader at-risk groups. Incomplete responses from participants may have also introduced bias, particularly if individuals who were less motivated or knowledgeable about skin cancer prevention were more likely to leave questions unanswered. As a result, the data may be skewed, potentially overrepresenting individuals with higher awareness or stronger intent to perform TSSE. Additionally, given our sample’s mean age, which is lower than the average age of first skin cancer diagnosis, our sample may not fully represent the TSSE intentions of older adults who are at increased risk. Older adults may experience unique challenges when performing TSSE that may impact their self-efficacy in engaging with TSSE, including decreased mobility and visual impairment [15]. Moreover, older adults are likely to have greater lifetime ultraviolet exposure and more skin abnormalities, which may increase the complexity and burden of performing TSSE [8]. Consequently, the reasons for engaging in TSSE and its feasibility may differ substantially between younger and older adults. Furthermore, because the sample was predominantly composed of non-Hispanic White individuals, the generalizability of the findings to other racial and ethnic populations may be limited. Underrepresented populations may have different perceptions of skin cancer risk and varying knowledge about what irregular lesions appear across different skin tones. They might also have less access to dermatologic care and resources to address these challenges. As a result, the significance of our findings, including perceived risk and self-efficacy, may vary greatly among racial and ethnic minorities. Beyond this demographic composition, the cross-sectional survey design assessed participants’ beliefs and intentions at a single point in time, limiting the ability to infer causality or assess long-term behavior. In addition, TSSE intention was measured using a single item, which may limit both reliability and construct validity. Since intention may be a multifaceted construct, a single item may not fully capture its complexity and may be more susceptible to random measurement error, potentially affecting the precision and interpretation of the observed associations. Future research should use validated multi-item scales to improve measurement reliability and construct coverage. Lastly, although the scales used in the study all demonstrated good internal consistency, future research should incorporate externally validated measures to improve reliability and facilitate replication.

Conclusion

The findings of this study suggest that TSSE intention among adults at increased risk for skin cancer were more closely associated with psychosocial and attitudinal factors than with demographic characteristics. These findings underscore the importance of developing tailored interventions grounded in theoretical frameworks (e.g., HBM) that strengthen perceived risk and self-efficacy, reduce perceived barriers, reinforce perceived benefits, and incorporate social cues to action in order to promote TSSE and support the early detection of skin cancer. Future research should examine whether targeting these factors can improve not only TSSE intention but also sustained TSSE behavior over time.
