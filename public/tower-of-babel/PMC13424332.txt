Treatment outcomes of robot-assisted rectal cancer surgery with multivisceral resection of adjacent organs
Authors: Ayumi Takaoka, Naoko Irie, Aoi Sugino, Hiroyasu Ishikawa, Takehiro Shiraishi, Tetsuya Ito, Noriyasu Chika, Toru Ishiguro, Takatoshi Matsuyama, Youichi Kumagai, Hideyuki Ishida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Robotic surgery is increasingly adopted for rectal cancer management, including selected patients with locally advanced disease. We evaluated perioperative safety and medium- to long-term outcomes after robot-assisted rectal cancer surgery with multivisceral resection for cT4 rectal cancer in a single high-volume center. We retrospectively reviewed 45 consecutive patients with locally advanced rectal cancer who underwent robot-assisted surgery with multivisceral resection between April 2021 and June 2025. Demographic and clinical variables, surgical and postoperative outcomes, and pathological findings were extracted from medical records. Recurrence-free survival (RFS), overall survival, and local recurrence were assessed. The study included 45 patients (19 females) with a median age of 72 years. Postoperative complications of Clavien–Dindo grade III or higher occurred in 4 patients (8.8%). Pathologically, 21 patients (46.7%) had pT4 disease (pT4a in 2 and pT4b in 19 [42.2%]), and 39 patients (86.7%) achieved RM0 margins. The median follow-up was 23 months. Across stages I–III, RM0 resection was associated with superior 3-year RFS compared with RM1/RMX resection (74.6% vs. 33.3%, p = 0.03). Robot-assisted rectal cancer surgery combined with multivisceral resection can be performed safely, with acceptable midterm outcomes. RM0 status was linked to improved RFS, supporting complete tumor resection as a prognostic determinant even in locally advanced cases.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer remains on the rise globally, accounting for the third-highest incidence and the second-highest cancer-related mortality worldwide. Rectal cancer constitutes approximately 40% of colorectal cancer cases [1]. In patients with advanced rectal cancer, multimodal treatment is commonly implemented, usually involving preoperative chemoradiotherapy followed by systemic chemotherapy.

Current NCCN Guidelines for Rectal Cancer (version 1, 2026) advise neoadjuvant treatment primarily for patients diagnosed with stage II–III locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC), and total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT) is increasingly considered. The PROSPECT trial reported that, relative to standard neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, neoadjuvant chemotherapy with FOLFOX with selective preoperative radiotherapy achieved noninferior outcomes, implying that preoperative radiotherapy may be safely omitted in selected patients [2]. Although the optimal strategy for LARC remains an area of active discussion and new treatment options continue to emerge, complete tumor removal is still regarded as a key determinant of prognosis [3].

For LARC—particularly T4b rectal cancer—outcomes are generally unfavorable, and radical surgery is therefore required. A negative pathological circumferential resection margin (pCRM > 1 mm) is considered essential for reducing the risk of local recurrence. Prior studies have suggested that R0 resection improves survival and may enhance patient quality of life at 6 months after surgery [4, 5]. In T4b rectal cancer, pelvic exenteration and resection of adjacent involved organs are the main procedures used to obtain negative surgical margins [6]. Over the last 15 years, pelvic exenteration has been established as a curative option for recurrent and locally advanced colorectal cancers [7]. However, conventional open surgery for tumors located in the deep pelvis—often necessitating pelvic exenteration or multiorgan resection—can be associated with substantial invasiveness and increased postoperative morbidity and mortality [8, 9].

Minimally invasive approaches, including laparoscopic surgery and robot-assisted surgery, have expanded the feasibility of complex pelvic procedures [10, 11]. In particular, robotic systems may improve operative efficiency through enhanced three-dimensional visualization and increased dexterity enabled by multiarticulated instruments, potentially contributing to higher rates of successful R0 resection [12].

Large randomized trials evaluating laparoscopic surgery for advanced rectal cancer generally excluded T4 disease owing to technical limitations [13–15]. Observational studies have suggested that oncological outcomes after laparoscopic surgery for T4 rectal cancer may be comparable to those achieved with open surgery [16]. Regarding robotic surgery, earlier reports included cases requiring multivisceral resection of adjacent organs [17, 18]; nevertheless, evidence concerning the efficacy and safety of robot-assisted surgery for rectal cancer requiring multivisceral resection remains limited [19].

Given this gap, we examined short- and long-term outcomes and perioperative safety in patients with LARC undergoing robot-assisted rectal cancer surgery with multivisceral resection performed by specialists at a single high-volume tertiary institution.

Materials and methods

Between April 2021 and June 2025, 45 patients diagnosed with locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) underwent robot-assisted rectal cancer surgery with multivisceral resection of adjacent organs at Saitama Medical Center, Saitama Medical University. Consecutive patients were included in the cohort. Surgical indication included total pelvic exenteration, and cases with local recurrence (LR) were excluded. Although some patients were initially diagnosed as cT3 preoperatively, intraoperative findings suggested invasion into adjacent organs, and multivisceral resection was accordingly performed.

We retrospectively collected data on patient characteristics, perioperative outcomes, postoperative outcomes, pathological findings, and survival/prognostic endpoints. Clinical information was extracted from medical records, and histopathological assessment was performed in accordance with the Japanese Society for Cancer of the Colon and Rectum classification of colorectal, appendiceal, and anal carcinoma (2019) [20]. The radial resection margin (RM) on the dissection plane was categorized as RM0 (no residual tumor; all margins negative), RM1 (microscopic residual tumor present at or within 1 mm of the nonperitonealized surface), RM2 (macroscopic residual tumor), or RMX (indeterminate margin status). N status was defined as follows. N0 indicated no regional lymph node metastasis. N1 indicated that the total number of lymph node metastases in pericolic and intermediate nodes was 3 or fewer. N2 indicated that the total number of lymph node metastases in pericolic and intermediate nodes was 4 or more. N3 indicated metastasis to the main lymph nodes; in cases of low rectal cancer, N3 also included metastasis to the main lymph nodes and/or lateral pelvic lymph nodes.

At our institution, preoperative chemoradiotherapy and preoperative chemotherapy were offered only when anticipated tumor shrinkage was expected to enable urinary tract preservation and to avoid the need for an extended resection.

Postoperative surveillance consisted of serum tumor marker testing every 3 months and computed tomography (CT) at 6-month intervals. Adjuvant chemotherapy was administered primarily as 4 or 8 cycles of CAPOX to patients with stage III disease or higher, following the Japanese Society for Colorectal Cancer Treatment Guidelines. The median follow-up period was 23 months (range, 3–50 months).

Approval for the study protocol was granted by the Ethics Committee of Saitama Medical Center, Saitama Medical University (No. 2099-II). The research adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients before participation.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared with the Mann–Whitney U test, while categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test, as appropriate. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) was calculated from the date of surgical resection to the occurrence of recurrence or death due to causes other than the primary cancer. Survival distributions were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were evaluated with the log-rank test.

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Most patients had an American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status of Grade 2 (41 [91.1%]). Preoperative tumor markers showed median carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9) values of 7.7 ng/mL (range, 0.3–414) and 11 U/mL (range, 2–347), respectively. Tumors were predominantly located in the lower rectum (23 [51.1%]), followed by the rectosigmoid (17 [37.8%]). Clinical tumor depth was cT4b in 60.0% of patients; the most frequently suspected adjacent organ involvement was the prostate (n = 10), followed by the uterus (n = 6) and seminal vesicle (n = 4).

With respect to clinical nodal status, cN1 was observed in 17 patients (37.8%), followed by cN3 in 12 (26.7%). Among 12 patients with cN3, preoperative imaging suggested metastasis to the main lymph nodes in 2 patients, and preoperative imaging also suggested lateral pelvic lymph node metastases in all 12 patients. Regarding clinical stage, cStage III accounted for 31 patients (68.9%), whereas cStage IV was identified in 4 patients (8.9%). Two patients were suspected to have para-aortic lymph node metastasis based on preoperative imaging, and the other 2 patients had liver metastases. In the 2 patients with suspected para-aortic lymph node metastasis, para-aortic lymph node dissection was performed concurrently with primary tumor resection. In the 2 patients with liver metastases, re-evaluation after primary tumor resection revealed that the liver lesions were unresectable; therefore, they received chemotherapy as patients with unresectable colorectal cancer. Preoperatively, 4 patients (8.9%) received chemoradiotherapy and 13 patients (28.9%) underwent neoadjuvant chemotherapy. After surgery, adjuvant chemotherapy was administered to 10 patients (22.2%).

Table 2 shows the surgical details. Low anterior resection was the most common index procedure (15 [33.3%]), followed by abdominoperineal resection (13 [28.9%]). Total colectomy was performed in one patient with rectal cancer arising from ulcerative colitis. Conversion to open surgery occurred in 2 patients (4.4%). Among 17 patients who received preoperative treatment, urinary tract preservation was achieved in 15 patients; however, pelvic exenteration was required in the remaining two patients.

Lymphadenectomy was performed with D2 dissection in 9 patients (20.0%) and D3 dissection in 36 patients (80.0%). Lateral lymph node dissection was performed in 12 patients (26.7%), excluding cases of total pelvic exenteration. The degree of pelvic autonomic nerve preservation was recorded using the AN classification (AN4: bilateral total nerve-sparing; AN3: unilateral nerve sacrifice with contralateral preservation; AN2: bilateral partial sacrifice; AN0: bilateral resection without nerve-sparing). AN4 was the predominant category (31 [68.9%]). A diverting stoma was created in 9 patients to avoid a high-risk anastomotic complication.

Multivisceral resection was carried out for various adjacent organs, including the seminal vesicle, bladder, prostate, uterus, vagina, ovary, ductus deferens, ureter, ileum, cecum, external iliac vein, sacrum, and urethra. In total pelvic exenteration cases, the following organs were included as concomitantly resected organs: in males, the ureters, urethra, seminal vesicles, prostate, and bladder; in females, the ureters, urethra, bladder, and uterus. In contrast, in cases with concomitant bladder resection and concomitant prostate resection outside of total pelvic exenteration, all resections were performed as partial resections. In cases of total pelvic exenteration, Pelvic reconstruction was performed in all cases without flap techniques in five cases; however, in one case, an anterolateral thigh muscle flap was used. The median operative time was 391 min (range, 110–1098), and the median estimated blood loss was 46.5 mL (range, 0–1816).

Postoperative outcomes

Postoperative outcomes are presented in Table 3. Clavien–Dindo (CD) grade III complications occurred in 4 patients. Reported complications included bowel obstruction, duodenal perforation, deep surgical site infection, and anastomotic leakage (each in one patient). No CD grade IV or higher complications were observed. Complication rates were comparable between patients who received preoperative therapy and those who did not (11.8% vs. 7.1%, P = 0.62). The median postoperative length of stay was 7 days (range, 7–49).

Pathological features

Table 4 summarizes the pathological findings. A pathological complete response (pCR) was achieved in 2 patients (11.8%) among those who received neoadjuvant treatment. Overall, the final pathological tumor depth was pT2 in 1 patient (2.2%), pT3 in 21 patients (46.7%), pT4a in 2 patients (4.4%), and pT4b in 19 patients (42.2%). In pT4b cases, adjacent organs involved included the prostate, uterus, ileum, vagina, seminal vesicle, bladder, ovary, and vas deferens. The pT4b rate was numerically higher in patients without preoperative therapy compared with those who received preoperative therapy (53.6% vs. 23.5%, P = 0.065).

In terms of nodal status, 26 patients (57.8%) had pN0 disease. Lateral lymph node metastasis was identified in 6 patients, representing 50% of those who underwent lateral lymph node dissection. Approximately half of the patients were at pathological stage II (21 [46.7%]), followed by stage III (16 [35.6%]). Histologically, most tumors were differentiated adenocarcinoma (37 [82.2%]); additional histological types included mucinous adenocarcinoma, signet-ring cell carcinoma, neuroendocrine tumor, neuroendocrine carcinoma, and squamous cell carcinoma.

The median number of harvested lymph nodes was 18 (range, 5–88), and the median number of lateral lymph nodes was 15 (range, 0–30). Lymphatic invasion was present in 13 patients (28.9%), and venous invasion was observed in 33 patients (73.3%). Proximal and distal resection margins were tumor-negative. Regarding radial resection margin status, RM0 was achieved in 39 patients (86.7%). The details of the five RM1 cases were as follows. Case 1 underwent abdominoperineal resection with concomitant prostate resection for a lower rectal tumor after neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and RM1 was identified at the prostate surgical margin. Case 2 underwent abdominoperineal resection with concomitant prostate resection for a lower rectal tumor after chemoradiotherapy, and RM1 was identified at the prostate surgical margin. Case 3 underwent Hartmann’s procedure with concomitant hysterectomy for an upper rectal tumor without prior treatment, and RM1 was identified at the uterus surgical margin. Case 4 underwent abdominoperineal resection with concomitant hysterectomy for a lower rectal tumor after chemoradiotherapy, and RM1 was identified at the site indicated at the posterior dissection margin. Case 5 underwent total pelvic exenteration for a lower rectal tumor without prior treatment, and RM1 was identified at the site indicated at the levator ani muscle surgical margin.

Long-term clinical outcomes

Long-term outcomes were assessed in patients with pathological stage I–III, excluding stage IV cases. Figures 1 and 2 show long-term outcomes after resection. Outcomes stratified by resection margin status (RM0 vs. RM1/RMX) are presented in Fig. 1A–C. The RM0 group included 33 patients, while the RM1/RMX group included 6 patients. The 3-year recurrence-free survival (RFS) rate was higher in the RM0 group than in the RM1/RMX group (74.6% vs. 33.3%, P = 0.03; Fig. 1B). The mean RFS time was 34.6 months (95% CI, 28.97–40.26) in RM0 and 21.2 months (95% CI, 4.54–37.79) in RM1/RMX. In contrast, there were no statistically significant differences in 3-year overall survival (OS) (77.4% vs. 66.7%, P = 0.35; Fig. 1A) or cumulative local recurrence (3.0% vs. 16.7%, P = 0.17; Fig. 1C). The mean OS time was 37.2 months (95% CI, 32.32–42.07) in RM0 and 35.5 months (95% CI, 19.01–51.99) in RM1/RMX. Discussion

In the surgical treatment of LARC requiring multivisceral resection, obtaining a safe and oncologically complete (R0) resection remains a central goal. As far as we are aware, the present investigation represents the first attempt to assess prognosis in relation to pathological outcomes among patients with LARC treated with robot-assisted rectal cancer surgery and multivisceral resection.

First, we assessed perioperative safety using postoperative complication rates and length of hospital stay. Relative to previous publications, our findings appear favorable. Wyatt et al. [21] reported a CD grade III or higher complication rate of 31.6% and a median hospital stay of 9.5 days in patients treated with robot-assisted rectal surgery with multivisceral resection. DiBrito et al. [22] also described a CD grade III or higher complication rate of 11.6%. In our cohort, CD grade III or higher complications occurred in 8.8% of patients, including small-bowel obstruction requiring prolonged decompression, postoperative duodenal perforation requiring operative intervention, deep surgical site infection requiring drainage, and anastomotic dehiscence requiring reoperation. No CD grade IV or higher events were observed, supporting the feasibility of robot-assisted surgery for this setting. The median postoperative hospital stay was 7 days, and most patients completed postoperative care without clinically meaningful deviations from the planned pathway. Although preoperative therapy has been suggested to increase surgical difficulty in prior studies [23, 24], in this study, the presence of preoperative therapy was not associated with a higher perioperative complication rate.

To characterize oncologic performance, we reviewed pathological outcomes and margin status. Wyatt et al. [21] reported an RM0 rate of 71.4%, while DiBrito et al. [22] reported 90.7%. In our series, the overall RM0 rate was 86.7%. In the preoperative therapy group, RM0 was achieved in 82.4%, compared with 89.2% in the non-preoperative therapy group; this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.65). Therefore, even when tumor shrinkage was obtained, preoperative therapy did not translate into a higher likelihood of R0/RM0 resection, which warrants further discussion. TNT has been reported to increase intraoperative injury to the TME plane in some studies [23, 24]. In addition, preoperative therapy may hinder dissection plane recognition or obscure the tumor–non-tumor boundary, potentially limiting the expected impact on oncologic outcomes such as local recurrence.

Regarding tumor biology, the overall pT4b proportion was 42.2% in our cohort. Importantly, because all patients underwent multivisceral resection, all had findings consistent with suspected T4b disease intraoperatively, even though preoperative staging did not identify T4b in every case. Prior reports have described pT4b rates of 15.6%–33% [16, 18, 25], and we therefore consider our observed proportion relatively high. Although CT and magnetic resonance imaging are used to estimate the preoperative depth of invasion, more accurate diagnostic strategies should be explored. Patients receiving preoperative therapy showed a trend toward a lower pT4b rate than those without preoperative therapy (based on our pathological results), suggesting less definite tumor boundaries on imaging and at operation. In addition, preoperative therapy did not prevent the need for extended surgery. Taken together, the optimal indication and timing of preoperative therapy remain to be clarified.

With regard to prognosis, recurrence-free survival, overall survival, and cumulative local recurrence were not significantly different between T3/T4a and T4b patients. Nevertheless, RM0 status was associated with better RFS than RM1/RMX status. These findings suggest that achieving complete resection may positively influence oncologic outcomes even when tumors require multivisceral resection for locally advanced disease. Yamaoka et al. [18] reported a favorable 3-year local recurrence rate of 4.0% following robot-assisted surgery for T4 rectal cancer, and our cohort demonstrated similarly encouraging oncologic outcomes.

It has long been recognized that a positive resection margin negatively affects long-term oncologic results in rectal cancer surgery performed without multivisceral resection. Wibe et al. [26] reported that, among patients treated with open rectal surgery with TME, those with a positive circumferential resection margin (CRM) had higher distant recurrence rates than those with a negative CRM (40% vs. 12%). Birbeck et al. [27] similarly demonstrated better survival in patients with negative CRM compared with those with positive CRM after open rectal surgery, indicating that CRM status can directly predict survival outcomes. Large trials including COLOR II and ALaCaRT, which used laparoscopic approaches, showed that negative CRM can be achieved laparoscopically, thereby supporting the oncologic safety of minimally invasive surgery [14, 15]. Jayne et al. [11] reported that CRM positivity after robot-assisted surgery was noninferior to that after laparoscopic surgery for rectal cancer. However, large-scale evidence clarifying the relationship between CRM status and long-term oncologic outcomes in robotic rectal surgery remains limited.

In open surgery cases requiring multivisceral resection, Lehnert et al. [28] identified R0 resection as a major prognostic factor. Moreover, the PelvEx Collaborative [29] reported that curative en bloc resection may be feasible even in highly locally advanced tumors in appropriately selected patients. For minimally invasive multivisceral resection, specialized-center reports [18, 19, 30] have suggested technical feasibility in selected cases, though these studies have often been limited by small sample sizes. Current evidence has mainly addressed technical feasibility and short-term outcomes, while long-term oncologic data have remained scarce. Robot-assisted surgery offers theoretical advantages such as improved deep-pelvis visualization, articulated instruments, and enhanced precision during anterior dissection. Nonetheless, there is currently no evidence demonstrating superiority over laparoscopy in achieving negative CRM. In addition, obtaining an adequate CRM with robot-assisted surgery may still be challenging in patients with sacral invasion, pelvic sidewall involvement, or bulky tumors. In such circumstances, tactile feedback may influence margin adequacy; therefore, conventional open surgery or the introduction of haptic-enabled robotic systems may further improve surgical outcomes.

This study has several limitations. The design was retrospective and single-center. Second, the number of patients undergoing robot-assisted rectal cancer surgery with multivisceral resection was limited. In this study, we assessed long-term outcomes; however, the follow-up period for median survival was relatively short, with a median of 23 months (range, 3–50 months). In addition, as this was a new surgical procedure, the cohort included patients with relatively short observation periods, which may have influenced the reliability of long-term estimates. The RM1/RMX group was also small (6 of 39 patients), which may limit the precision of the estimation of 3-year survival rates at this time. Median OS time and RFS time were not reached in either group; therefore, the 95% confidence interval for the median was not estimable. Because of the limited number of enrolled cases, we were unable to perform a robust multivariable analysis. Preoperative treatments were selected according to individual patient factors rather than a standardized protocol, which may have introduced treatment heterogeneity, potential confounding, and reduced the generalizability of our findings.

In conclusion, robot-assisted rectal cancer surgery with multivisceral resection of adjacent organs was performed safely and achieved acceptable long-term oncologic outcomes. Larger studies are warranted to further clarify the role of robot-assisted surgery for LARC requiring extended resection.
