Integrative single-cell and machine learning analysis identifies a tumor doubling time-related prognostic signature and therapeutic targets in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Mingchu Zhang, Ziwen Zhao, Qingwen Zhu, Shihan Liang, Limin Sun, Huali Wei, Shiwei Liu, Mengwei Zhao, Lifeng Li, Liang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Integrated single-cell and bulk transcriptomic analyses identified 21 tumor doubling time-related genes in HNSCC.•A seven-gene prognostic signature (CCBE1, MAD2L1, ECT2, BIRC5, ITGA6, ESM1, NUF2) was developed and validated across multiple independent cohorts.•The signature demonstrated competitive prognostic performance compared to 73 previously reported signatures.•Single-cell and pseudotime analyses revealed dynamic TDTRG expression patterns in cycling T cells and epithelial cells.•Venetoclax was identified as a prioritized candidate with predicted high binding affinity, warranting further experimental validation.

Introduction

Globally, HNSCC is currently ranked as the sixth most common cancer, a disease characterized by persistently high rates of treatment failure and a 5‑year survival prognosis that has shown minimal improvement over the past several decades, despite considerable progress in therapeutic strategies [1,2]. This unfavorable prognosis is primarily attributable to late-stage diagnosis, significant therapeutic resistance, and the absence of effective interventions for recurrent or metastatic disease [3]. Extensive molecular heterogeneity, arising from the convergence of environmental exposures (tobacco, alcohol, HPV infection) and intrinsic genetic changes, fundamentally shapes the diverse clinical trajectories and therapeutic responses observed in HNSCC [4]. This complexity poses a substantial obstacle to both precise outcome forecasting and the customization of treatment strategies.

HPV status is a critical determinant of HNSCC biology and clinical behavior. HPV-positive tumors, which account for approximately 30% of cases, are typically characterized by robust immune infiltration and are considered "immune-hot," correlating with significantly better prognosis despite often presenting at late stage [5,6]. In contrast, HPV-negative tumors, associated with tobacco and alcohol exposure, frequently exhibit an "immune-cold" phenotype with sparse lymphocyte infiltration and poorer outcomes [7]. This immunological dichotomy underscores the importance of understanding the tumor microenvironment (TME) composition and its regulatory mechanisms in HNSCC progression and therapeutic response. Recent single-cell transcriptomic studies have further elucidated the cellular landscape underlying this heterogeneity, revealing distinct stromal and immune cell populations that shape the immunological niches within HNSCC [8,9].

A key parameter in oncology, tumor doubling time (TDT)—defined as the interval necessary for a neoplasm to double its volume—constitutes an essential measure of proliferation kinetics and holds substantial clinical relevance for informing surveillance schedules, estimating prognosis, and selecting appropriate therapeutic interventions [10,11]. Longitudinal CT‑based growth rate analyses have classified HNSCC among those malignancies that exhibit rapid expansion, necessitating timely therapeutic intervention, with proliferation kinetics showing correlation to EGFR, Ki67, and CD44 expression levels [12]. A more than sixfold decrease in tumor doubling time has been linked to elevated PTEN expression, underscoring the substantial influence of this tumor suppressor on proliferation kinetics [13]. Despite its established clinical significance, the systematic integration of TDT-related molecular features into prognostic frameworks for HNSCC remains largely unexplored. TDT-related genes (TDTRGs) represent an untapped reservoir of biomarkers with the potential to decode tumor heterogeneity, predict treatment resistance, and reveal critical interactions within the TME that influence disease progression.

An unprecedented view of tumor microenvironment heterogeneity has been made possible through scRNA-seq, a technological breakthrough that has profoundly altered how cellular diversity is interrogated [[14], [15], [16]]. By integrating single-cell with spatial transcriptomics, several recent investigations have uncovered cellular subpopulations linked to particular immunological niches and clinical responses in HNSCC [8,17]. These findings collectively indicate that both the make-up of the cellular milieu and the signaling networks operating among them exert substantial control over disease progression and patient outcomes. Despite this progress, how proliferation kinetics—quantified by TDT—relates to the architectural organization of the tumor microenvironment remains largely unexplored. Elucidating the distribution patterns of TDTRGs across diverse cell lineages and their impact on differentiation pathways may yield fundamental insights into the mechanisms propelling HNSCC pathogenesis.

In this study, we hypothesize that TDTRGs play critical roles in HNSCC progression and TME modulation, and that integrating these genes into a prognostic framework can refine molecular classification and predict therapeutic responses. To address this question, we undertook an extensive single-cell transcriptomic characterization of HNSCC specimens, systematically cataloging cell populations and evaluating expression differences among groups stratified by HPV status. By merging scRNA-seq profiles with bulk RNA-seq data from the TCGA-HNSC cohort, we delineated 21 TDTRGs and mapped their cellular distribution via UCell enrichment scoring, which uncovered marked enrichment within epithelial compartments and proliferating T lymphocytes. Employing this gene set, consensus clustering was applied to delineate two molecular subgroups exhibiting divergent survival trajectories, immune infiltration profiles, and pathway activation states. Thereafter, leveraging the Mime1 package, we deployed 117 machine learning algorithms to construct and validate a parsimonious 7-gene prognostic model across multiple independent datasets—TCGA-HNSC serving as the training cohort, with GSE41613, GSE65858, and ICGC used for external validation. The model was further characterized through single-cell visualization, cell cycle scoring, and pseudotime trajectory analysis of epithelial cells and cycling T cells using Monocle3. Finally, we predicted chemotherapeutic sensitivity via CTRP2, GDSC1, and GDSC2 databases to identify drugs with differential responses between risk groups, followed by molecular docking validation. This integrative multi-omics approach aims to establish a novel TDTRG-based framework for HNSCC molecular classification and prognostic stratification, providing insights into the biological determinants of tumor progression and identifying potential therapeutic vulnerabilities for personalized treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

The transcriptomic data and corresponding clinical information for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), comprising 502 tumor samples and 44 adjacent normal tissues. The RNA-Seq data in FPKM format were converted to transcripts per million (TPM) and subsequently log2-transformed for downstream analyses. For external validation of our prognostic model, we obtained four independent HNSCC microarray datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/): GSE41613 (n = 97), GSE65858 (n = 270), GSE42743 (n = 103), and GSE30784 (n = 58). An additional validation cohort was sourced from the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC) database (LIRI-JP, n = 232). Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were acquired from the HNSCC study reported by Jenkins and colleagues [8].

Defining TDTRGs through correlation with growth rate metrics

To define genes whose expression correlate with proliferation kinetics, we computed Pearson correlation coefficients between transcript levels and tumor doubling time measurements using the GSE54236 cohort, which provides paired expression and TDT data for HNSCC specimens. Genes satisfying the thresholds of |r| > 0.3 and P < 0.001 were designated as tumor doubling time-related genes (TDTRGs). The full list of TDTRGs and their correlation statistics are provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Data analysis and processing for single-cell RNA sequencing

The scRNA-seq data were processed following the analytical pipeline described by Jenkins et al. [8] and the code provided in the accompanying GitHub repository. In brief, quality control, normalization, and integration were performed in R (v4.3.2) using the Seurat package (v5.2.1). Following quality filtering, data normalization and integration were accomplished using Seurat's reciprocal PCA (RPCA) workflow. Inclusion criteria mandated a minimum of 200 detected transcripts per cell and expression of each gene in at least three individual cells. Low-quality cells were excluded based on mitochondrial transcript proportions exceeding the median value plus three times the median absolute deviation—a cutoff that eliminated approximately 20% of the initial population. To minimize the likelihood of capturing multiplet events, cells with >6000 features were also discarded [18]. After integration, principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to the aggregated dataset, followed by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) for two-dimensional visualization. Clustering was executed via shared nearest-neighbor (SNN) graph construction and subsequent application of the FindClusters function. For each resulting cluster, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the FindAllMarkers function with the Wilcoxon rank sum test, requiring expression in at least 25% of cells, a minimum |log2FC| of 0.5, and adjusted P < 0.05. Cell identities were assigned by comparing DEG profiles against established markers from the literature, the CellMarker 2.0 database, and the ACT resource [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. To evaluate the enrichment of TDTRG signatures in specific cell populations, UCell scoring was performed using the AddModuleScore UCell function from the UCell package, with scores calculated based on the average expression levels of the 21 TDTRGs at the single-cell level. Cell cycle scores were computed using the CellCycleScoring function based on canonical G2/M and S phase markers. Pseudotime trajectory analysis of epithelial cells and cycling T cells was conducted using Monocle3 [23,24] to explore the dynamic expression changes of hub genes along differentiation trajectories; cells were ordered in pseudotime, and genes with significant expression changes were identified using the graph_test function.

Differential expression analysis

To assess transcriptional alterations, we implemented two complementary differential expression pipelines. For bulk transcriptomes, the DESeq2 package (v1.36.0) in R was employed to compare HNSCC tumor specimens against normal tissues, applying significance thresholds of |log2FC| > 1.0 and P < 0.05. For single-cell data, the FindMarkers function within Seurat, coupled with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, was used to evaluate expression differences within each cell type across three comparisons: HPV-positive versus HPV-negative tumors, as well as tumor versus normal conditions. Genes fulfilling the same statistical criteria were classified as cell-type-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Protein–protein interaction and functional enrichment analysis

For the 21 candidate genes, protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were generated utilizing the STRING database [25,26], with a confidence threshold set above 0.4 [27]. Visualization of the network was accomplished using Cytoscape (version 3.9.1). In R, enrichment analyses for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were conducted with the clusterProfiler package [[28], [29], [30]] (version 4.6.0) in R. Statistical significance was assigned to GO terms encompassing biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) when the adjusted P-value was below 0.05.

Consensus clustering and molecular subtyping

Using the expression profiles of the 21 TDTRGs, consensus clustering was applied to the TCGA-HNSCC cohort using the ConsensusClusterPlus package [31] (version 1.66.0) with the following parameters: 1000 resampling iterations, pItem = 0.8, pFeature = 0.8, and clustering algorithm = k-means. The best number of clusters (k) was identified by examining the CDF curves and the consensus matrix heatmap. Kaplan–Meier curves and the log-rank test were used to compare overall survival between the two identified subtypes, C1 and C2. The limma package was used for differential expression analysis between subtypes, while the clusterProfiler package [32,33] with MSigDB hallmark gene sets was employed for gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to investigate functional differences. A detailed immune infiltration analysis was performed to further explore the immunological differences between the two molecular subtypes. The immune cell composition in the TCGA-HNSC cohort was estimated using multiple algorithms. Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed with the GSVA package (version 1.44.5) using 29 immune cell signature gene sets from the ImmPort database. The CIBERSORT algorithm (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/) was applied with the LM22 signature matrix to quantify the fractions of 22 immune cell types [34]. Differences in immune infiltration between C1 and C2 subtypes were assessed using the Wilcoxon test.

Machine‑learning modelling for prognostic signature development

With the objective of establishing a reliable prognostic classifier, we assembled a comprehensive machine‑learning framework comprising ten constituent algorithms: random survival forest (RSF), elastic network (Enet), lasso and ridge regression, stepwise Cox regression, CoxBoost, partial least squares regression for Cox (plsRcox), supervised principal components (SuperPC), gradient boosting machine (GBM), and survival support vector machine (survival‑SVM). These were deployed across 117 distinct combinations using the Mime1 package [35]. Model training was conducted on the TCGA-HNSC cohort, with independent validation performed on three external datasets—GSE41613, GSE65858, and ICGC. For each combination, concordance indices (C‑index) were computed for both training and validation sets; the top‑performing models were selected based on their mean C‑index across all validation cohorts. Intersection of genes recurrently chosen by these leading models yielded a final panel of seven hub genes. A risk score was subsequently formulated as a linear weighted sum of these genes, employing coefficients derived from the optimal model. Patients were dichotomized into high‑ and low‑risk groups using the median score as a cutoff. Prognostic discrimination was evaluated via Kaplan–Meier survival analysis (log‑rank test) and time‑dependent ROC curves for 1‑, 3‑, and 5‑year overall survival, generated with the timeROC package [36].

Drug sensitivity prediction and molecular docking

To estimate chemosensitivity, we employed the oncoPredict package (v1.2) [37] to compute half‑maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values for standard anticancer agents in each TCGA-HNSCC specimen, leveraging gene expression profiles together with drug response data from the GDSC1, GDSC2, and CTRP2 repositories. Subsequently, Spearman rank correlations were computed between the expression levels of our hub genes and the predicted IC50s, pinpointing compounds whose efficacy was significantly linked to the risk signature. For structural investigations, three‑dimensional coordinates of the hub proteins were downloaded from the AlphaFold database (https://alphafold.com/), and ligand structures were acquired from PubChem in SDF format. Protein and ligand preprocessing was conducted with AutoDockTools (v1.5.7), and docking simulations were performed using AutoDock Vina (v1.2.7) with an exhaustiveness parameter of 32. The most favorable binding poses—those with the lowest free energy (ΔG)—were retained for visualization, and two‑dimensional interaction maps were rendered with Discovery Studio (v2019).

Cell culture and transfection

Human head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) cell lines FaDu and CAL27, together with normal human oral keratinocytes (HOK), were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling prior to use to ensure experimental validity and reproducibility. Cells were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM; Procell, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Royacel Biotechnology, RYS-KF22) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The culture medium was replaced every 2–3 days. Cells were passaged using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA (Xinsaimei, Suzhou, China) upon reaching 80–90% confluence. For cryopreservation, cells in logarithmic growth phase were harvested, resuspended in cryopreservation medium (Xinsaimei, Suzhou, China), subjected to controlled-rate freezing at −80 °C overnight, and subsequently transferred into liquid nitrogen for long-term storage.

For gene silencing experiments, CAL27 cells were transfected with three ECT2-specific short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs; SH-1, SH-2, and SH-3) or a negative control shRNA (SH—NC). Transfections were performed using a liposome-based transfection reagent (Lipofectamine 3000, Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, CAL27 cells were seeded into appropriate culture plates and transfected at approximately 60–70% confluence. The shRNA plasmids and transfection reagent were separately diluted and subsequently mixed to form lipoplexes, which were added to serum-free medium for 6–8 h. The medium was then replaced with complete culture medium. Knockdown efficiencies of SH-1, SH-2, and SH-3 were evaluated by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) at 48 h post-transfection. The shRNA exhibiting the highest silencing efficiency was selected for subsequent functional assays.The sequences of shRNAs are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

QRT‑PCR

Total RNA was extracted from human oral keratinocytes (HOK), hypopharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma cells (FaDu), and oral squamous cell carcinoma cells (CAL27) using the SPARKeasy Tissue/Cell RNA Rapid Extraction Kit with a genomic DNA clearance column (SparkJade, Shandong, China; Cat. No. AC0202-B), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality, concentration, and purity were evaluated prior to downstream experiments.

Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from total RNA using a commercial reverse transcription kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premixed qPCR Kit (Accurate Biology, Changsha, Hunan, China; Cat. No. AG11701) according to standard procedures.Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method and normalized to GAPDH as an internal reference gene. All experiments were independently performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay kit (Cat. HY-K0301, MCE, New Jersey, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. CAL27 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 10³ cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. Cell viability was evaluated after transfection with ECT2-specific short hairpin RNA (SH-1), negative control shRNA (SH-nc), or without treatment as the normal control group. CAL27 cells were therefore divided into three groups, including control, SH-nc, and SH-1 groups for subsequent analysis. After transfection, cells were maintained under standard culture conditions and collected at 0 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h. At each time point, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2–4 h in the dark. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA), and cell viability was calculated as relative absorbance normalized to the corresponding control group. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times to ensure reproducibility.

EdU incorporation assay

For EdU incorporation assay, CAL27 cells were treated with SH-1, SH-nc, or control conditions under identical experimental settings. DNA synthesis activity was assessed using an EdU incorporation assay kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were incubated with EdU labeling solution, followed by fixation, permeabilization, and fluorescent labeling procedures. The proportion of EdU-positive cells was quantified to evaluate DNA replication activity and cell proliferation capacity.For statistical analysis, all quantitative experiments were performed in triplicate. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software. Comparisons among multiple groups (control, SH-nc, and SH-1) were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by appropriate post hoc tests. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Colony formation assay

Colony formation assays were performed to evaluate the long-term proliferative capacity of CAL27 cells. CAL27 cells transfected with the ECT2-targeting SH-1 exhibiting the highest silencing efficiency, negative control shRNA (SH-nc), or left untreated (control group) were harvested and prepared as single-cell suspensions. Cells were then seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1000 cells per well and cultured under standard conditions for 10–14 days to allow colony formation. The culture medium was replaced every 3 days. At the end of the incubation period, cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min at room temperature. Colonies containing >50 cells were considered valid colonies. Colony numbers were counted under a light microscope and quantified using ImageJ software. Colony formation ability was normalized to the control group. All experiments were independently performed at least three times.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2). Continuous variables between two groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank‑sum test (for non‑normally distributed data) or Student's t‑test (for normally distributed data). To examine differences across multiple groups, we employed one‑way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by appropriate post‑hoc adjustments. For categorical variables, the chi‑square or Fisher's exact test was applied. Survival probabilities were computed using the Kaplan–Meier estimator, with comparisons between groups performed via the log‑rank test [36]. The independent prognostic capacity of both the risk score and clinical parameters was evaluated through univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models. Model discriminatory ability was quantified using Harrell's C‑index. All statistical testing employed a two‑sided approach, and a threshold of P < 0.05 was adopted to denote statistical significance.

Results

Identification of TDTRGs in HNSCC through integrated single-cell and bulk transcriptomic analysis

Following the analytical pipeline described by Jenkins et al. [8], we merged single‑cell RNA sequencing profiles derived from HNSCC specimens to construct a detailed cellular taxonomy. After applying rigorous quality filters, dimensionality reduction, and cluster identification, we resolved 17 transcriptionally discrete populations: B lymphocytes, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, conventional dendritic cells (cDC), proliferating T cells, endothelial and epithelial cells, fibroblasts, lymphatic endothelial cells (LEC), mast cells, cells of the monocyte/macrophage lineage (MonoMac), mural cells, neutrophils, natural killer (NK) cells, plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC), plasma cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs), as depicted via Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) (Fig. 1A). The expression signatures of canonical markers defining each lineage were validated through bubble plots (Fig. 1B). Comparative analysis of cell type proportions across normal mucosa, HPV+ HNSCC, and HPV– HNSCC samples revealed significant alterations in the cellular composition of the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 1C). Relative to normal tissue, both HPV+ and HPV– tumors exhibited marked reductions in B cells, plasma cells, and CD4+ T cells, while showing increased proportions of cDC, cycling T cells, epithelial cells, MonoMac, and Tregs. These compositional changes suggest substantial remodeling of the immune landscape during HNSCC progression, with notable differences between HPV-associated and HPV-negative tumors.

To comprehensively characterize transcriptional alterations across conditions, We conducted differential expression analysis for each cell type across three different comparison scenarios: HPV– HNSCC versus normal, HPV+ HNSCC versus normal, and HPV– HNSCC versus HPV+ HNSCC. The results are presented as multi-group differential volcano plots (Fig. 1D–F), revealing cell type-specific gene expression changes associated with HPV status and malignant transformation.

Concurrently, we conducted bulk RNA-seq differential expression analysis between tumor and normal samples in the TCGA-HNSC cohort using thresholds of |log2FC| > 1 and P < 0.05, visualized as a gradient volcano plot (Fig. 1G). The intersection of tumor-upregulated genes from bulk analysis, tumor-upregulated genes from scRNA-seq analysis (across all cell types), and TDTRGs identified from the GSE54236 correlation analysis yielded 21 candidate genes (Fig. 1H). PPI network analysis of these 21 TDTRGs revealed extensive interconnectivity (Fig. 1I), suggesting coordinated functional roles. Chromosomal localization mapping showed the distribution of these genes across multiple chromosomes (Fig. 1J), and their expression patterns in the TCGA-HNSC cohort were visualized using a heatmap (Fig. 1K). Correlation analysis demonstrated predominantly positive correlations among the majority of these 21 genes (Fig. 1L), indicating potential synergistic interactions in HNSCC pathogenesis.

Mutational landscape of the 21 TDTRGs in HNSCC

We further investigated the mutational landscape of the 21 TDTRGs in HNSCC using the cBioPortal database. Analysis of genetic alterations revealed that ECT2 exhibited the highest mutation frequency (20%), predominantly characterized by amplification events, while the remaining genes showed mutation rates of 7% or lower (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Clinical correlation analysis demonstrated that mutations in these genes were significantly associated with key genomic metrics, including nonsynonymous tumor mutational burden (TMB), total mutation count, and fraction of the genome altered (Supplementary Fig. S2B–D). Notably, samples harboring mutations in the TDTRGs consistently exhibited higher values for all three parameters compared to non-mutated samples, suggesting that genetic alterations in these genes are linked to increased genomic instability in HNSCC.

Employing this enrichment pattern as a stratifying criterion, we partitioned cells into high‑score and low‑score cohorts using the median enrichment value as a threshold, then assessed compositional differences across normal mucosa, HPV‑negative tumours, and HPV‑positive lesions (Fig. 2D–F). Within normal specimens, the high‑score fraction exhibited elevated representation of epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and lymphatic endothelial cells (LEC). In contrast, within both HPV- and HPV+ tumors, the high-score group exhibited elevated fractions of monocyte/macrophage lineage cells (MonoMac), neutrophils, and conventional dendritic cells (cDC), suggesting a shift towards a more inflammatory microenvironment associated with high TDTRG expression.

To elucidate the biological roles of the 21 TDTRGs, we conducted functional enrichment analyses using Disease Ontology (DO), Gene Ontology (GO), and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway database. DO term enrichment highlighted significant links to a range of malignancies, among them head and neck carcinoma, head and neck cancer, laryngeal carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, embryoma, embryonal cancer, thyroid gland carcinoma, thyroid gland cancer, stomach cancer, and germ cell cancer (Fig. 2G). Examination of GO biological processes (BP) revealed that these genes participate principally in chromosome segregation, organelle fission, nuclear division, mitotic nuclear division, nuclear chromosome segregation, sister chromatid segregation, mitotic sister chromatid segregation, regulation of mitotic nuclear division, regulation of chromosome segregation, and kinetochore organization (Fig. 2H). With respect to cellular components (CC), significant enrichment encompassed condensed chromosome, chromosomal region, the centromeric region of chromosomes, condensed chromosome centromeric region, kinetochore, spindle, midbody, spindle pole, mitotic spindle, and spindle midzone (Fig. 2I). Analysis of molecular functions (MF) demonstrated enrichment in tubulin binding, microtubule binding, protein C‑terminus binding, peptidase inhibitor activity, endopeptidase inhibitor activity, integrin binding, growth factor binding, cysteine‑type endopeptidase inhibitor activity, insulin‑like growth factor binding, and insulin‑like growth factor I binding (Fig. 2J). These findings collectively highlight the critical roles of TDTRGs in cell cycle regulation, chromosomal dynamics, and cancer-related signaling pathways.

Consensus clustering identifies two distinct TDTRG-based molecular subtypes with divergent immune landscapes and clinical outcomes

Using the expression profiles of the 21 TDTRGs, consensus clustering was applied to TCGA-HNSC tumour specimens via the ConsensusClusterPlus algorithm [33]. An integrated assessment of the consensus matrix, cumulative distribution function (CDF) curves, CDF delta area metrics, and sample tracking plots led to the selection of K = 2 as the most stable clustering solution, thereby defining two molecular subgroups, designated C1 and C2 (Fig. 3A–D). The immune landscapes of the two TDTRG‑defined subtypes were found to be markedly distinct. Together, these observations point to divergent immune microenvironmental compositions associated with the two molecular subgroups.

The distinct transcriptional programmes of the two subtypes were reflected in their clinical outcomes. Visualisation of the 21 TDTRG expression profiles via heatmap revealed clear segregation between C1 and C2 tumours (Fig. 3I). Prognostically, patients assigned to the C2 subtype experienced a markedly inferior progression‑free interval relative to those in C1 (Fig. 3H, log‑rank P = 0.001). Although overall survival (OS) and disease‑specific survival (DSS) also favoured the C1 group, the differences did not attain statistical significance (Supplementary Fig. S3A, B).

Differential expression analysis between C2 and C1 subtypes identified genes with significant expression changes. The ranked dot plot displays the distribution of differentially expressed genes, with red dots indicating smaller P values and blue dots representing larger P values (Fig. 3J). These findings suggest that the C2 subtype, characterized by poorer prognosis and a distinct immune landscape, exhibits activation of integrin and MET signaling pathways coupled with downregulation of metabolic and drug metabolism pathways.

Following evaluation of all algorithm combinations, the Lasso regression coupled with random survival forest (RSF) emerged as the top-performing model, attaining the highest mean C-index (0.692) across validation cohorts and consequently being designated the optimal approach (Fig. 4A). To elucidate the relative importance of each gene within the prognostic model, we conducted SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis. Consistently, EdU incorporation assays revealed that the proportion of EdU-positive cells was significantly reduced following ECT2 silencing, suggesting impaired DNA synthesis and reduced entry into S phase (Fig. 10D,E). In addition, colony formation assays showed that ECT2 knockdown markedly decreased the clonogenic capacity of CAL27 cells. Both colony number and colony size were significantly reduced compared with the control group, indicating that ECT2 is essential for long-term proliferative and self-renewal potential in HNSCC cells (Fig. 10F,G).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that ECT2 functions as a potent promoter of proliferation in HNSCC cells. Its silencing leads to significant inhibition of cell viability, DNA replication, and clonogenic growth. Importantly, these findings provide functional validation supporting the biological relevance of the TDTRG-derived prognostic signature, suggesting that it reflects a proliferation-dependent transcriptional state in HNSCC. This strengthens the translational significance of the model and links TDTRG-associated gene expression to tumor growth behavior at the cellular level.

Discussion

In this study, we thoroughly analyzed the molecular profile of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma by focusing on genes associated with tumor doubling time, integrating single-cell transcriptomics with bulk RNA-seq data across multiple independent cohorts. This multi-omics approach enabled us to construct a comprehensive cellular atlas of HNSCC, identify 21 TDTRGs with predominant enrichment in cycling T cells and epithelial cells, define two molecular subtypes with divergent immune landscapes and clinical outcomes, and develop a robust seven TDTRGs prognostic signature that demonstrated superior predictive performance compared to 73 previously reported signatures. Furthermore, we validated the cell type-specific expression patterns of these genes at single-cell resolution, elucidated their dynamic regulation during HNSCC progression through pseudotime trajectory analysis, and identified Venetoclax as a high-affinity candidate therapeutic agent targeting multiple hub proteins via molecular docking.

The clinical significance of TDT in HNSCC has been previously established, with studies demonstrating that specific growth rate calculations from serial CT scans identify HNSCC as fast-growing tumors for which treatment must not be delayed, and that clinical tumor growth rates are influenced by EGFR, Ki67, and CD44 expression [13]. Furthermore, cell kinetic parameters such as labeling index have been shown to influence treatment response, with fast-proliferating tumors potentially benefiting from intensified therapeutic regimens [38]. Despite this clinical relevance, the molecular underpinnings linking TDT to HNSCC pathogenesis and tumor microenvironment composition have remained poorly characterized. Our study addresses this gap by providing a transcriptomically defined TDTRG signature and elucidating its cell type-specific distribution, thereby bridging the gap between macroscopic tumor growth kinetics and microscopic cellular heterogeneity.

HPV status is a critical determinant of HNSCC biology, with HPV-positive tumors generally exhibiting better prognosis and distinct immune microenvironments compared to HPV-negative tumors [6,8]. Our single-cell analysis revealed that TDTRG enrichment scores exhibited distinct patterns across HPV-stratified subgroups: in cycling T cells, enrichment was highest in HPV+ tumors, whereas in epithelial cells, scores progressively increased from normal tissue to HPV+ and further to HPV– tumors. This differential enrichment pattern suggests that TDTRGs may play context-dependent roles in HPV-associated versus HPV-negative carcinogenesis. Recent research has emphasized the variability of HPV integration within tumors and its link to genomic instability [39], which may partly explain the differential TDTRG expression patterns observed in our study. The C2 molecular subtype, characterized by poorer prognosis, exhibited activation of MET and integrin signaling pathways coupled with downregulation of metabolic pathways, consistent with the aggressive phenotype of HPV-negative tumors [40]. The increased infiltration of neutrophils and M0 macrophages in C2 tumors, together with concurrent MET and integrin pathway activation, may synergistically foster an immunosuppressive and pro‑metastatic niche, plausibly contributing to the poorer prognosis observed in this subtype.

The development of robust prognostic signatures for HNSCC has been a major focus of translational research, with numerous studies proposing gene panels for risk stratification [41,42]. However, the clinical utility of many such signatures is limited by inadequate external validation and failure to outperform existing clinicopathological parameters. Our study addressed this limitation by employing a rigorous machine learning framework incorporating 117 algorithm combinations and validating the resulting seven-gene signature across four independent cohorts comprising over 1000 patients. The signature demonstrated competitive prognostic performance, ranking first in the TCGA-HNSC and ICGC cohorts when benchmarked against 73 previously reported signatures. Notably, the inclusion of both discovery and validation cohorts from diverse geographical origins enhances the generalizability of our findings, as external geographic validation is essential for demonstrating model reproducibility and robustness [43]. It is noteworthy that the prognostic performance of our seven-gene signature, while robust in the TCGA-HNSC and GSE41613 cohorts, did not reach statistical significance in GSE65858 (P = 0.221) and exhibited only borderline significance in the ICGC cohort (P = 0.057). Several factors may account for this variability. First, the smaller sample sizes of GSE65858 (n = 270) and ICGC (n = 232) relative to TCGA-HNSC (n = 502) may reduce statistical power to detect significant survival differences, particularly when the effect size is modest. Second, differences in patient demographics, treatment protocols, and HPV prevalence across cohorts may modulate the prognostic impact of proliferation-related transcriptional programs. The ICGC cohort, derived from a Japanese population, may have different genetic and environmental backgrounds, while GSE65858 originates from a distinct institutional setting with potential variations in therapeutic strategies. Third, technical differences between RNA-seq (TCGA) and microarray platforms (GSE65858 and ICGC) may affect the comparability of gene expression measurements and risk score calculations. These observations underscore the importance of validating prognostic signatures in large, well-characterized, and uniformly treated prospective cohorts to confirm their generalizability. The lower AUC values in GSE65858 may reflect heterogeneous treatment regimens within this cohort, which could confound survival outcomes and attenuate the prognostic signal. This underscores the need for validation in uniformly treated prospective cohorts to confirm generalizability.

The seven TDTRGs comprising our signature—CCBE1, MAD2L1, ECT2, BIRC5, ITGA6, ESM1, and NUF2—have established roles in cell cycle regulation, chromosomal stability, and tumor progression. The predominant enrichment of these genes in cycling T cells and epithelial cells aligns with their functional annotations in cell division and mitotic processes. Our functional analyses highlight the importance of MAD2L1, ECT2, BIRC5, and NUF2 in the mitotic checkpoint and chromosomal segregation, consistent with the enrichment of cell cycle-related pathways. BIRC5, a well-known apoptosis inhibitor, supports tumor cell survival and is correlated with unfavorable prognosis in several malignancies, such as HNSCC [44]. ECT2, a guanine nucleotide exchange factor, regulates cytokinesis and has been implicated in tumor proliferation and metastasis [45]. NUF2 is a component of the NDC80 kinetochore complex essential for chromosome alignment and segregation, with emerging evidence supporting its oncogenic role in various cancers [46]. ITGA6 encodes integrin α6, which mediates cell-matrix interactions and has been implicated in cancer stem cell maintenance and epithelial plasticity [47]. ESM1 (endocan) is a secreted proteoglycan involved in endothelial cell function and angiogenesis [48], while CCBE1 is essential for lymphangiogenesis and has been linked to tumor metastasis [49]. The coordinated upregulation of these genes in high-risk patients suggests that TDTRG-associated aggressiveness stems from multiple interconnected biological processes centered on proliferation, chromosomal stability, and cellular plasticity within epithelial and cycling T cell compartments.

To further strengthen the biological relevance of the TDTRG-derived signature, we performed experimental validation focusing on ECT2, which exhibited the highest expression level among the candidate genes in HNSCC cell lines and occupied a central position within the TDTRG interaction network. Consistent with our transcriptomic findings, ECT2 expression was markedly elevated in both FaDu and CAL27 cells compared with normal oral keratinocytes. Functional assays further demonstrated that ECT2 silencing significantly suppressed cell viability, DNA synthesis, and clonogenic growth capacity. These observations are in line with previous studies showing that ECT2 functions as an oncogenic guanine nucleotide exchange factor that promotes cell-cycle progression and malignant proliferation in multiple cancer types. Importantly, our results provide direct experimental evidence that the TDTRG signature is not merely a statistical prognostic model but reflects a biologically active proliferative program in HNSCC. Given that cell-cycle regulation emerged as one of the most significantly enriched pathways in our functional analyses, the inhibitory effects observed following ECT2 depletion further support the notion that dysregulated proliferative signaling represents a key molecular basis linking TDTRGs to tumor growth kinetics and unfavorable clinical outcomes.

The observation that cycling T cells exhibited high TDTRG enrichment is particularly noteworthy, as it implicates proliferating immune cells in the TDTRG-associated transcriptional program. Cycling T cells represent an activated population critical for anti-tumor immunity, yet their proliferative state may also render them susceptible to co-option by tumor-promoting signals. The progressive increase in TDTRG expression from normal tissue to HPV+ and further to HPV– tumors in epithelial cells suggests that malignant transformation drives the acquisition of proliferation-associated transcriptional programs, with HPV– tumors exhibiting the most aggressive phenotype. This is consistent with the well-established clinical observation that HPV– tumors have poorer outcomes despite lower baseline proliferation rates, potentially reflecting differences in the underlying biology of cell cycle dysregulation [6].

Pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed dynamic expression patterns of the seven TDTRGs during HNSCC progression, with distinct trajectories along cycling T cell- and epithelial cell-rooted paths. The divergent patterns observed between HPV+ and HPV– groups may reflect differences in underlying driver mutations and immune contexture. In cycling T cells, the elevated expression of BIRC5, ECT2, ITGA6, MAD2L1, and NUF2 in HPV– tumors during early and late pseudotime suggests that these genes contribute to sustained proliferative capacity throughout tumor evolution. In contrast, the mid-pseudotime peaks observed in HPV+ and normal samples may reflect transient activation states that are more tightly regulated. In epithelial cells, the progressive increases in ITGA6, MAD2L1, and NUF2 during early-to-mid pseudotime in HPV– tumors, compared to the high initial expression followed by decline in HPV+ samples, suggests fundamental differences in the temporal regulation of proliferation-associated genes between these subgroups.

Drug sensitivity analysis identified Venetoclax as a high-affinity candidate targeting multiple hub proteins, with binding energies below −9 kcal·mol⁻¹ for ITGA6, ECT2, and NUF2. Venetoclax is a BCL-2 inhibitor approved for hematological malignancies, and its potential repurposing for solid tumors such as HNSCC is an area of active investigation [50]. Notably, recent studies have demonstrated that Venetoclax exhibits antitumor efficacy in HNSCC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models with high Bcl-2 expression, providing direct preclinical evidence for its potential utility in biomarker-selected HNSCC subsets [50]. The predicted strong binding affinity to ITGA6 is especially interesting due to the role of integrin signaling in tumor progression and resistance to therapy. Integrin-targeted therapies have shown promise in preclinical models, and our findings provide a rational basis for further experimental validation of Venetoclax as a potential therapeutic agent in TDTRG-high HNSCC. The high binding affinity to ECT2 and NUF2, both essential mitotic regulators, suggests that Venetoclax may exert pleiotropic effects on cell cycle progression beyond its canonical BCL-2 inhibition, potentially through off-target interactions that could be exploited therapeutically. This hypothesis is consistent with observations that HNSCC tumors typically express low levels of BCL-2 but high levels of other anti-apoptotic proteins such as BCL-xL and MCL-1 [51], suggesting that Venetoclax's activity in our study may involve additional mechanisms beyond BCL-2 inhibition. We also acknowledge that the docking analysis relied on AlphaFold‑predicted protein structures, which, despite their high utility, represent static models and do not fully capture protein flexibility or conformational dynamics. Therefore, the predicted binding affinities should be interpreted as computational estimates requiring experimental validation through biophysical assays such as surface plasmon resonance or isothermal titration calorimetry.

It is important to recognize several limitations of this study. First, although we validated our prognostic signature across multiple independent cohorts, all datasets were retrospectively analyzed and may be subject to selection bias. It is essential to conduct prospective validation in uniformly treated patient cohorts prior to clinical application. Second, the TDTRGs were identified based on correlation analysis in a single dataset (GSE54236), and while we applied stringent statistical thresholds, additional TDTRGs may exist that were not captured by our approach. Third, the definition of TDT-related genes relied on correlation analysis within a single dataset (GSE54236) that provides estimated TDT values rather than direct longitudinal imaging measurements. Although our subsequent functional analyses confirmed strong enrichment in cell-cycle and mitotic pathways—consistent with the concept that proliferation drives tumor doubling—the absence of direct imaging‑based TDT validation limits our ability to conclude that the signature specifically reflects in vivo tumor growth kinetics. Thus, we refer to this signature as reflecting a proliferation‑competent phenotype. Future prospective studies incorporating serial CT or MRI-based growth assessments are necessary to establish a direct link between these genes and actual tumor doubling time. Fourth, the molecular docking predictions, while robust, require experimental validation through in vitro binding assays and functional studies to confirm the therapeutic potential of Venetoclax and other candidate compounds. Fifth, our study focused on transcriptomic profiles and did not incorporate proteomic or metabolomic data, which could provide additional layers of biological insight. Sixth, while we identified cycling T cells as a key compartment for TDTRG expression, the functional significance of TDTRG upregulation in these cells—whether it reflects true T cell proliferation or potential contamination by proliferating tumor cells—requires further investigation through spatial transcriptomics or lineage tracing studies. Finally, the functional roles of individual TDTRGs in HNSCC pathogenesis remain to be fully elucidated through mechanistic experiments, particularly their context-dependent functions in epithelial versus immune compartments.

Conclusion

This study delivers a detailed examination of genes linked to TDT in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, emphasizing their major enrichment in cycling T cells and epithelial cells at single-cell resolution. We established a robust seven-gene prognostic signature with superior predictive performance across multiple independent cohorts and identified Venetoclax as a high-affinity candidate therapeutic agent targeting multiple hub proteins. The discovered molecular subtypes and therapeutic targets provide fresh perspectives on HNSCC diversity and possible paths for personalized treatment approaches. Prospective validation of the prognostic signature, experimental confirmation of the predicted drug–target interactions, and in-depth characterization of TDTRG function in epithelial and cycling T-cell lineages represent critical avenues for future research aimed at fully elucidating their role in HNSCC pathogenesis.
