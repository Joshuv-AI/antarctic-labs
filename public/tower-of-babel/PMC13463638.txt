Lignin enrichment selects specialized fungal communities from contrasting soils
Authors: Vitória Pinheiro Balestrini, Sílvia Belém Gonçalves, Ricardo Henrique Krüger, Betania Ferraz Quirino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite growing interest in microbial lignin degradation, the ecological and taxonomic dynamics of fungal communities enriched under ligninolytic conditions remain poorly understood. We hypothesized that sequential enrichment on lignin would select specialized fungal communities, with assembly shaped by inoculum source, temperature, and lignin type. Fungal consortia from backyard compost soil and a commercial substrate were enriched on alkaline-extracted or Kraft lignin as the sole carbon source at 30°C or 37°C through six successive passages. Internal Transcribed Spacer amplicon sequencing assessed fungal diversity, taxonomic composition, and predicted ecological guilds. Richness and diversity progressively declined, indicating strong selective pressure and enrichment of specialized communities. Community composition was primarily influenced by temperature and inoculum source, whereas lignin type had limited and statistically nonsignificant effects at the genus level. Ascomycota dominated all enriched consortia, while several taxa initially detected in the source materials were progressively lost. Functional predictions indicated a predominance of saprotrophic guilds, although most assignments had moderate or low confidence. These findings highlight the importance of temperature and inoculum source in shaping fungal community assembly during lignin enrichment and provide guidance for designing and optimizing ligninolytic consortia for biotechnological applications.

This study contributes to the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 12 by advancing biological strategies for the sustainable utilization of lignin, a major yet underexploited component of lignocellulosic biomass. By investigating the succession and functional structuring of fungal consortia capable of using lignin as the sole carbon source, this work provides knowledge that supports the development of biotechnological approaches for lignin valorization and biomass residue conversion. These findings may contribute to more efficient resource use, reduced industrial waste streams, and the development of sustainable bio-based production systems. Introduction

Plant biomass is one of the most abundant renewable sources of organic carbon and a key resource for emerging sustainable bioeconomy strategies to reduce dependence on fossil resources and promoting circular use of biological materials (Miao et al. 2025). Lignocellulosic biomass is primarily composed of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, which are intricately associated within the plant cell wall and determine its structural integrity (Peng and Lin 2025). While cellulose and hemicellulose have been extensively exploited for second-generation biofuels and biochemicals, lignin remains underutilized, limiting biomass conversion efficiency (Nargotra et al. 2025).

Lignin, the most abundant aromatic biopolymer on Earth, is a heterogeneous, three-dimensional macromolecule formed by the oxidative coupling of p-hydroxyphenyl, guaiacyl, and syringyl units, which confers high recalcitrance to chemical and enzymatic attack. With the global expansion of lignocellulosic biorefineries, annual lignin generation is projected to exceed 100 million tons (Chen et al. 2024). Lignin´s growing availability is both a challenge given its accumulation, but also an opportunity to develop valorization strategies to high-value chemicals, biofuels, and biomaterials. Biological valorization of lignin offers environmental advantages such as reduced carbon emissions and decreased dependence on fossil-based resources (Rashmi et al. 2024).

In natural ecosystems, microbial degradation plays a central role in lignin turnover, with fungi recognized as the primary drivers of this process. As key decomposers in terrestrial environments, fungi possess unique extracellular oxidative enzyme systems capable of attacking the complex aromatic structure of lignin. These characteristics make fungi essential for carbon cycling and attractive candidates for biotechnological applications. Compared with most bacteria and archaea, which typically degrade lignin-derived aromatic intermediates (Zhang et al. 2025), many filamentous fungi can initiate the oxidative depolymerization of intact lignin through the secretion of powerful extracellular enzymes such as laccases, lignin peroxidases, and manganese peroxidases (Zhang et al. 2025). This enzymatic machinery allows fungi to overcome the structural recalcitrance of lignin. High-throughput sequencing technologies, particularly analyses based on the Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) region, have revolutionized fungal ecology by revealing an unprecedented diversity of fungal taxa and functional guilds (Tedersoo et al. 2017). Despite these advances, the functional potential of many fungal lineages remains poorly understood, particularly with respect to lignin degradation (Lankiewicz et al. 2023).

Understanding which fungal taxa contribute to lignin degradation is critical from both ecological and applied perspectives. Soil fungal communities respond to environmental gradients such as climate and soil pH, with direct implications for carbon cycling under changing environmental conditions (Zeng et al. 2020, Panico et al. 2025). Recent studies also indicate that lignin degradation often involves synergistic interactions between fungi and bacteria, although the mechanisms underlying these interactions remain only partially understood (Miao et al. 2025, Zhang et al. 2025). Identifying ligninolytic fungi can support the development of more efficient and sustainable bioconversion pathways in biorefineries facilitating the transformation of lignocellulosic residues into value-added products.

Here, we applied an enrichment-based metabarcoding approach to investigate the selection and dynamics of fungal communities involved in lignin biodegradation. Microbial consortia capable of using lignin as the sole carbon source were established from two microbial inocula [a commercial soil and backyard (BY) compost soil]. Two cultivation temperatures (30°C and 37°C) and two lignin sources (alkali-extracted lignin and Kraft lignin) were combined to generate eight experimental consortia under defined culture conditions. Six enrichment cycles were performed over 12 weeks. We hypothesized fungal community assembly to be influenced by the different factors evaluated: inoculum source, temperature, and lignin type. Furthermore, based on previous enrichment study of bacterial communities (Mendes et al. 2021), we predicted that lignin source would represent the strongest selective effect on fungal community composition. Community composition across enrichment stages was characterized by sequencing of the ITS region to evaluate how substrate type, temperature, and inoculum source influence fungal community assembly during selective enrichment. This work provides insights into fungal taxa involved in lignin degradation and support the development of sustainable biotechnological strategies for biomass residue valorization, contributing to the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production) and SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation, and Infrastructure).

Materials and methods

Establishment of microbial consortia

As shown in Fig. 1 and as previously described (Mendes et al. 2021), to establish the microbial consortia, two distinct sources of microorganisms were used: a commercial substrate [Miracle-Gro® (MG)] and BY compost soil collected in Alameda County, California (USA). Commercial Kraft lignin (Sigma-Aldrich) and lignin extracted from switchgrass using an alkaline method (BE lignin) were used as carbon sources. The alkaline extraction of lignin was as previously described (Mendes et al. 2021). To establish microbial consortia, eight 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks, each containing 50 mL of M9 minimal medium supplemented with trace elements (Miller 1972) in total were used. Each flask was inoculated with 0.5 g of either MG or BY compost. The carbon source was either 1% (w/v) of Kraft lignin or 20% (v/v) of alkaline-extracted lignin. Cultures were incubated under aerobic conditions at either 30°C or 37°C, with shaking at 200 rpm. The enrichment process consisted of transferring 2 mL of culture into fresh medium under the same conditions every 2 weeks for a total of six passages (DeAngelis et al. 2010). A single enrichment lineage was established for each combination of inoculum source, lignin type, and temperature. At selected time points, 20 mL of each culture were collected, centrifuged at 20 000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C, and the resulting microbial pellet was stored at −80°C until DNA extraction (DeAngelis et al. 2010).

Schematic representation of the enrichment experiment. Eight microbial consortia were enriched using lignin [base-extracted (BE) or Kraft] as the carbon source. Four consortia originated from BY soil and four from soil MG. Cultures were incubated aerobically at 30°C and 37°C, with transfers to fresh medium every 2 weeks. The enrichment lasted six passages (12 weeks). Samples were collected at each passage for DNA extraction and ITS region amplification and sequencing.

Illumina sequencing of ITS rDNA region

To evaluate community succession during enrichment, fungal communities were characterized both before enrichment (original inoculum sources–0PBY and 0 p.m.G) and after the establishment of lignin-enriched consortia using ITS metabarcoding. DNA was extracted using All Prep DNA/RNA kit (QIagen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA was then used as template in polymerase chain reactions (PCR) to amplify the copies of the ITS rDNA using primers FW (ITS9): 5′ GAA CGC AGC RAA IIG YGA 3′ and RV (ITS4): 5′ TCC TCC GCT TAT TGA TAT GC 3′. The fragments were analyzed using Bioanalyzer for quality control. Sequencing was according to JGI guidelines (Joint Genome Institute) on the MiSeq platform (Illumina) by pair end (2 × 150 bp) sequencing by the Joint Genome Institute (Walnut Creek, CA, USA).

Fungal diversity analyses

ITS rDNA amplicon sequences were processed using the DADA2 (v.1.34.0) (Callahan et al. 2016) in RStudio (v.4.4.3) (R Core Team 2025). Files were inspected for quality assessment, and the reads filtered and trimmed with parameters defined for truncation, and removing low-quality reads. The error model was learned for each set of reads, followed by the inference of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), chimeras were removed. The UNITE database was used for taxonomic classification (Abarenkov et al. 2024). Sample metadata was imported and associated with the “phyloseq” object (v.1.50.0) (McMurdie and Holmes 2013), enabling integrated analyses. The α-diversity indices of fungal communities were calculated using the “vegan” package (v.2.7-1) (Oksanen et al. 2025). To analyze the dissimilarity of the consortia, genus-level data from different passages were used for nonmetric Bray–Curtis nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS), using the PERMANOVA test with 999 permutations in the adonis function of the vegan R package. Functional predictions for fungal communities were performed using the FUNGuildR (v.0.3.0) (Nguyen et al. 2016).

Results

Sequencing and taxonomy

A total of 10 experimental groups were analyzed, including the original soils prior to enrichment (0PBY and 0 p.m.G, where 0P indicates passage 0, and BY and MG denote BY soil and MG inoculum sources, respectively) and the lignin-enriched consortia (BYBE30C, MGBE30C, BYBE37C, MGBE37C, BYKRAFT30C, MGKRAFT30C, BYKRAFT37C, and MGKRAFT37C). In these sample codes, BE refers to base-extracted (BE) lignin and KRAFT to Kraft lignin, while 30°C and 37°C denote the incubation temperatures used during enrichment. Sequencing across all consortia generated 7637 ASVs, which were reduced to 3157 ASVs after chimera removal and used for subsequent analyses. Taxonomic assignment revealed different levels of resolution, with 288 ASVs identified at the species level, corresponding to 66 distinct species, the 200 most abundant species are listed in Table 1, while the remaining ASVs were classified at higher taxonomic ranks.

The first passage exhibited substantially higher ASV counts, indicating greater amplicon variant richness. This effect was particularly pronounced in BY-derived consortia, which showed ASV values ranging from 148 to 168, reflecting highly diverse communities when compared to MG-derived consortia, which ranged from 116 to 128 ASVs. This pattern was supported by the Chao1 estimator, which indicated high initial richness, with values exceeding 95.000 in BY and reaching up to 41.688 in MG. These results suggest the presence of a large number of rare taxa in the communities at the first passage.

PD whole tree was also higher during the first passage, especially in BY consortia, with values ranging from 11.16 to 16.55, whereas MG consortia showed lower values, between 4.64 and 7.96. Diversity indices that account for both richness and evenness, namely Shannon and 1-Simpson, displayed relatively high and homogeneous values across treatments in the first passage (Shannon ranging from 3.18 to 4.62; 1-Simpson from 0.796 to 0.821), indicating structurally complex fungal communities.

In contrast, a marked decline in alpha diversity occurred in the last passage across all treatments. ASV counts dropped sharply to values between 12 and 18, representing a reduction of over 90% relative to the first passage. Chao1 estimates ranged from 2.500 to 5.850, suggesting less diverse communities with a reduced contribution of rare taxa. Similarly, PD whole tree values decreased between 0.96 and 3.58, reflecting the selection of a limited number of closely related phylogenetic lineages. Consistently, Shannon and 1-Simpson indices showed pronounced declines, particularly in MG-derived treatments, which exhibited the lowest diversity values overall. The lowest values were observed in the MG_Kraft_37°C treatment (Shannon = 0.131; 1-Simpson = 0.036), indicating dominance by a small number of taxa.

Among the experimental treatments, MG_Kraft_37°C appeared to suffer pronounced loss of fungal diversity, exhibiting the lowest diversity values in the final passage. By the sixth passage, 37°C samples showed reduced diversity in both BY- and MG-derived consortia, resulting in the lowest fungal diversity values and indicating that this temperature imposed additional selective pressure, favoring a more restricted set of organisms. Overall, the enrichment process under lignin-based selection resulted in the reduction of fungal diversity, with the combination of MG inoculum, Kraft lignin, and 37°C leading to simplified microbial communities.

Dynamics of taxonomic profiles during enrichment

To better understand the dynamics of the enrichment process, the taxonomic profiles of the fungal communities were evaluated at the family level across successive passages of the lignin-based enrichment experiment. Microbial consortia derived from BY and MG soils were analyzed separately (Figs 3 and 4). In both soils, a progressive shift in taxonomic composition was observed, characterized by a reduction in overall diversity and an increase in the dominance of a restricted set of fungal families in the later passages. This occurred under all conditions, but the rate of change varied with temperature and lignin type. At 37°C, especially under BE conditions, communities rapidly converged, with dominance established by passages 2–3. In contrast, at 30°C, changes were more gradual and diversity was better maintained.

In the initial soil samples, before enrichment (0PBY), consortia derived from BY soil exhibited a highly diverse taxonomic profile, with multiple fungal families present. Among these, Trichocomaceae showed the highest relative abundance. Over successive passages, directional replacement in taxonomic composition was observed. By the sixth passage, so after the enrichment, BY-derived consortia were characterized by the relative dominance of different families depending on the treatment applied (Fig. 3). At 30°C with BE lignin, the community was primarily composed of Pezizomycotina, Trichosporonaceae, and Nectriaceae. In contrast, the 30°C Kraft lignin treatment was dominated mainly by Nectriaceae and Trichocomaceae. For the 37°C BE lignin treatment, the community was largely dominated by Nectriaceae and Trichocomaceae, along with an unclassified fungal family (Fungi_fam_incertae_sedis). Under 37°C with Kraft lignin, members of Saccharomycetales, Microascaceae, and Aspergillaceae were the most abundant families.

In contrast, consortia derived from MG soil (Fig. 4) exhibited a less diverse taxonomic profile at the initial stage, before enrichment (0MG), when compared to BY soil, with fewer families contributing to community composition. Families such as Pseudeurotiaceae, Cephalothecaceae, Trichomonascaceae, Aspergillaceae, and Hypocreaceae were dominant in the MG community original soil (0MG).

Taxonomic simplification in the MG-derived consortia, after the enrichment, (Fig. 4) occurred more strongly than that observed for BY-derived consortia (Fig. 3) as the enrichment progressed. By the sixth passage, all MG treatments were dominated by few fungal families, although the identity of the dominant families varied depending on the treatment conditions. In the MG consortia at 30°C with BE lignin, the sixth passage community was composed of only three families, namely Trichosporonaceae, Pezizomycotina, and an unclassified fungal family. In the 30°C Kraft lignin treatment, the community was dominated primarily by Cephalothecaceae, with a minor contribution from Trichosporonaceae. In the 37°C BE lignin consortia, the relative abundance was nearly evenly split between Pezizomycotina and the unclassified fungal family. Finally, in the 37°C Kraft lignin treatment, the fungal community became almost exclusively composed of members of the Aspergillaceae family.

The results of the successive lignin-selection enrichment process for both soils indicate progressive taxonomic specialization within the fungal communities, with increasing dominance of specific families. Although this trend was observed for fungal communities derived from both soil sources, it was more pronounced in consortia derived from MG soil.

Influence of factors on fungal selection

Beta diversity of the fungal communities at genus level was assessed using the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity index and visualized through NMDS, with statistical support evaluated by PERMANOVA based on 999 permutations (Table 3). The analysis was conducted at the genus level (Fig. 5). The stress number was 0.144, indicating the NMDS ordering is a good fit. The NMDS ordination revealed an influence of the experimental factors (temperature 30°C/37°C, lignin type kraft/BE and soil BY/MG) on fungal community composition throughout the enrichment process, as well as a distinct separation between communities from the first and sixth passages. Samples from the first passage were more widely dispersed in the NMDS space, reflecting more complex and heterogeneous fungal communities. In contrast, samples from the sixth passage formed a more compact cluster, suggesting increased homogeneity and greater structural similarity among communities after successive rounds of selective enrichment.

Regarding the soil used as inoculum, communities derived from BY soil displayed greater dispersion in the NMDS ordination, whereas MG-derived communities tended to cluster more tightly. In addition, the ordination patterns pointed to an effect of temperature, as samples incubated at 30°C and 37°C tended to form distinct subgroups within each lignin type.

PERMANOVA (Table 3) results showed that temperature explained the largest proportion of variation in community composition (R² = 5.18%, F = 1.28, P = .005). Similarly, soil type (BY vs. MG) had a significant effect on community structure (R² = 4.93%, F = 1.21, P = .020), reinforcing the influence of the initial inoculum on community assembly during enrichment. The enrichment passages themselves also contributed significantly to the observed variation (R² = 2.57%, F = 1.26, P = .023), supporting a directional shift over successive enrichment stages and indicating an adaptive microbial succession process. In contrast, lignin type did not show a statistically significant effect on community composition at the genus level (R² = 4.17%, F = 1.02, P = .331).

Discussion

Taxonomic classification

The ITS region is recognized as the primary genetic marker for fungal taxonomic identification. Due to its high interspecific variability and relatively conserved flanking regions, ITS allows the design of universal primers capable of amplifying a broad diversity of fungal taxa while maintaining resolution for species-level discrimination in many groups (Rani et al. 2026). For this reason, ITS has been proposed as the universal DNA barcode for fungi and it is currently the most used molecular marker in fungal ecology (Naqvi et al. 2025, Rani et al. 2026). In addition, the large number of ITS sequences available in curated databases such as UNITE has greatly improved fungal taxonomic assignment (Abarenkov et al. 2024).

The DADA2 pipeline initially identified 7637 ASVs, which were reduced to 3157 after chimera removal. The high proportion of discarded sequences reflects both the intrinsic complexity of the fungal communities analyzed and the limitations of ITS amplification, a marker characterized by high variability and a strong tendency toward chimera formation. This filtering step was essential to avoid artificial inflation of diversity and erroneous taxonomic assignments (Callahan et al. 2016). Previous methodological studies have demonstrated that environmental fungal ITS datasets are particularly prone to chimera-related artifacts due to the coexistence of highly similar ribosomal templates within complex microbial communities (Kauserud 2023). Increasing community complexity directly elevates the frequency of chimera generation during ITS amplification, especially under conditions involving mixed-template PCR reactions (Bjørnsgaard Aas et al. 2017, Kauserud 2023). In addition, fungal ITS regions exhibit substantial natural variation in sequence length and GC content, factors that can promote incomplete extension and template switching during PCR amplification, leading to artificial recombinant sequences (Francioli et al. 2021).

However, although 58.7% of inferred ASVs were removed during chimera filtering, ~84.5% of sequencing reads were retained in the final dataset. This indicates that most excluded ASVs were represented by few reads and therefore likely corresponded to low-abundance artifacts rather than abundant biological taxa. Such a pattern is expected when chimera removal targets rare PCR-derived sequence artifacts while preserving the majority of biologically informative reads (Callahan et al. 2016).

Importantly, the removal of these sequences likely improved, rather than artificially distorted, downstream ecological analyses. Chimera retention can substantially inflate richness estimators, artificially increase rare ASV counts, and generate spurious ecological patterns by introducing nonbiological sequence variants into diversity calculations (Nilsson et al. 2015, Kauserud 2023). In contrast, the diversity patterns observed in this study remained biologically coherent across independent metrics, including ASV richness, Chao1, Shannon, Simpson, phylogenetic diversity, and NMDS ordination. The progressive reduction in diversity and convergence of fungal communities across enrichment passages were consistently supported by both alpha- and beta-diversity analyses, indicating that the observed ecological trends reflect genuine biological selection rather than bioinformatic artifacts.

Only a fraction of ASVs could be resolved to the species level. Nevertheless, several of the identified taxa have been previously associated with lignin or lignocellulosic degradation. Well-characterized ligninolytic fungi, such as Trichoderma harzianum (Zhai et al. 2025), Aureobasidium pullulans (Driller et al. 2026), and representatives of Aspergillus (including Aspergillus fumigatus) (Zhu et al. 2023), are known producers of oxidative and hydrolytic enzymes involved in lignin transformation. Other detected species, including Phialemonium inflatum (Yoon et al. 2023) and Trichosporon ovoides, are not classically recognized as primary lignin degraders but have been reported in organic matter-rich environments. Their presence likely reflects indirect ecological roles, such as metabolic cross-feeding, succession, or exploitation of degradation by-products released by primary ligninolytic fungi (Rodriguez et al. 2023). Enrichment under lignin selection favors not only organisms that use lignin but also functionally complementary community members.

At the phylum level, dominance of Ascomycota and Basidiomycota is consistent with patterns commonly observed in lignin-enriched systems. Basidiomycota include the canonical white-rot fungi, widely regarded as efficient lignin degraders (Manici et al. 2024). Although Ascomycota are traditionally linked to cellulose and hemicellulose degradation, increasing evidence indicates their participation in the oxidation of lignin-derived aromatic compounds, particularly during early or intermediate stages of decomposition (Ferrari et al. 2021).

It should also be noted that the absence of Glomeromycota, a common taxonomic group in soils, was unexpected given their widespread occurrence in soils and their ecological importance in terrestrial ecosystems. However, most members of Glomeromycota are arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi that establish obligate symbiotic associations with plant roots and depend on host-derived carbon for growth and reproduction (Smith 2009). These fungi exhibit limited saprotrophic capacity and are generally unable to persist under axenic culture conditions lacking living plant tissues (Priyadharshini et al. 2025, Vaishnav and Jansa 2026, Zhong et al. 2026). Because the enrichment system used in this study consisted of liquid minimal medium supplemented exclusively with lignin and lacked plant roots or other host-associated structures, Glomeromycota were likely unable to maintain active growth throughout successive enrichment passages. This interpretation is consistent with the result of FUNGuildR analysis, which revealed low representation of symbiotic trophic modes and a predominance of saprotrophic guilds in the enriched consortia, indicating strong selection against host-dependent fungal lifestyles during successive enrichment.

Alpha diversity of fungal communities

Analysis of alpha diversity across successive passages revealed a pronounced and progressive reduction in fungal richness and diversity, a pattern consistent with selective enrichment processes using lignin. In the first passage, consortia exhibited high values of richness (i.e. observed ASVs and Chao1), phylogenetic diversity (i.e. PD whole tree), and abundance-based diversity (i.e. Shannon and 1-Simpson). In contrast, by the sixth passage, fungal communities were simplified, with a reduction of >90% in ASV numbers and a sharp decline across all diversity metrics.

Lignin is recognized as a recalcitrant natural heteropolymer (Morena and Tzanov 2022, Georgs et al. 2025). This property imposes strong selective pressure, reflect in the progressive loss of diversity observed over successive passages (Moraes et al. 2018). The pronounced decline in alpha diversity observed in this study is in agreement with previous reports employing microbial enrichment strategies (Levy-Booth et al. 2022). Studies targeting the degradation of recalcitrant compounds, such as lignin, crystalline cellulose, or aromatic hydrocarbons, frequently document substantial reductions in richness and diversity following repeated transfers (i.e. passages), associated with the replacement of complex communities by more specialized ones. This pattern has been reported for both bacterial (Mendes et al. 2021) and fungal communities (this study), reinforcing the role of enrichment as a strong ecological filter. Beyond the reduction in fungal diversity, results indicate a clear restructuring of microbial consortia over time. High Shannon and 1-Simpson values in the first passage suggest relatively even communities, with multiple taxa contributing similarly to total abundance. In contrast, the low values observed in the sixth passage, particularly in MG-derived consortia, reflect communities strongly dominated by a few taxa, indicating that enrichment favored organisms with higher competitive fitness under chosen conditions.

Consortia derived from BY soil exhibited greater initial fungal richness and diversity compared to those derived from MG soil. Higher values of observed ASVs, Chao1, and PD whole tree in BY during the first passage indicate more taxonomically and phylogenetically diverse communities. These findings support our hypothesis that lignin-based enrichment acts by progressively selecting a restricted subset of fungi capable of persisting under lignin-driven conditions. The marked reduction in richness, phylogenetic diversity, and evenness throughout the enrichment process indicates directional community assembly toward increasingly specialized fungal consortia. This difference likely reflects the nature of the inoculum, as BY soil is typically more heterogeneous and exposed to diverse organic inputs and microhabitats, whereas commercial soils such as MG tend to harbor more standardized and less diverse microbial assemblages. Greater initial fungal diversity in BY may also confer higher functional redundancy, contributing to the elevated diversity observed in early passages.

Carbon source characteristics also appear to influence fungal diversity patterns. Previous studies have reported that alkali-extracted lignin may retain residual carbohydrates, such as hemicellulose, which can serve as alternative carbon sources depending on extraction efficiency (Nitsos et al. 2016, Jung et al. 2019, Pang et al. 2024). The presence of these more readily assimilable compounds may allow the persistence of nonligninolytic microorganisms contributing to higher richness and diversity. Residual carbohydrate content was not quantified in the present study; therefore, the presence of minor contaminants cannot be excluded.

Incubation temperature further modulated fungal alpha diversity patterns. By the sixth passage, consortia incubated at 37°C generally displayed lower ASV richness and reduced Shannon and 1-Simpson indices compared to those incubated at 30°C, for both BY- and MG-derived communities. Fungi are known to be sensitive to elevated temperatures (Panico et al. 2025), which may have been an additional stressor, further restricting the pool of organisms capable of persisting under such conditions (Zeng et al. 2020). This effect was particularly evident in the MG + Kraft lignin + 37°C treatment, which yielded the lowest diversity values observed, indicative of an extremely simplified and highly dominated fungal community.

Microbial dynamics during enrichment

The overall stabilization observed along the enrichment process indicates that successive enrichment in lignin promoted directional selection, resulting in structurally stable fungal communities in the final passages. This progressive stabilization observed across enrichment passages further supports our hypothesis that lignin enrichment promotes deterministic community assembly. Rather than maintaining the highly diverse structure observed in the original soils, the communities converged toward more stable configurations dominated by a limited number of fungal lineages, suggesting adaptation to the selective conditions imposed by lignin as the sole carbon source. However, the onset of stabilization varied across conditions, occurring earlier under 37°C and more gradually at 30°C. This stability reflects the persistence of a restricted set of dominant families, a pattern commonly reported in enrichment experiments using recalcitrant substrates (Moraes et al. 2018, Levy-Booth et al. 2022). Although uninoculated controls were not included in the experimental design, the progressive and consistent succession observed across passages, together with the convergence toward specialized fungal assemblages, is more consistent with ecological selection than with the random introduction of laboratory contaminants. Nevertheless, the absence of negative controls prevents the complete exclusion of contamination as a potential source of low-abundance taxa.

A comparable stabilization pattern has also been reported in the same enrichment experiments focusing on bacterial communities with the same selective conditions (Mendes et al. 2021). However, in these systems the reduction in alpha diversity tends to be less pronounced for bacterial communities, which may be related to their greater metabolic versatility (Kijpornyongpan et al. 2025, Peng and Lin 2025), capacity for cross-feeding interactions, and broader ecological strategies (Peng and Lin 2025). In contrast, fungal communities involved in lignin degradation are often dominated by a smaller number of highly specialized taxa capable of producing extracellular oxidative enzymes (Kijpornyongpan et al. 2025), leading to stronger competitive exclusion and reduced taxonomic diversity under selective conditions. Additionally, cross-kingdom interactions suggest that fungi primarily initiate lignin breakdown, while bacteria utilize intermediate compounds, further supporting higher bacterial diversity within lignin-degrading consortia (Zhang et al. 2025).

Once the functionally competent assemblage is established, the consortium tends to reach a stable configuration with reduced taxonomic redundancy, suggesting functional optimization for lignin degradation. Temperature significantly influenced the final fungal taxonomic composition of the consortia. Although all treatments converged toward specialized fungal communities, distinct combinations of substrate and temperature favored different fungal groups, demonstrating that selection was not solely temporal but also environmentally driven. Interestingly, at 37°C fungal families such as Aspergillaceae and Microascaceae were predominant, and these are frequently associated with thermotolerance and rapid growth under stressful conditions (Antal et al. 2020).

BE lignin at 30°C supported relatively higher diversity in the final passages, with the coexistence of families such as Trichosporonaceae (Brink et al. 2019), Nectriaceae (Wang et al. 2024), and other representatives of Pezizomycotina (Ferrari et al. 2021). This pattern suggests that BE lignin may allow a broader range of metabolic strategies and functional niches. Temperature further acted as an ecological filter, with 37°C favoring thermotolerant taxa and 30°C permitting slightly more diverse communities, particularly in BY-derived consortia.

Several dominant fungal families identified throughout the enrichment process have documented roles in lignin or aromatic compound degradation. Trichocomaceae (Zhu et al. 2023), widely represented in BY consortia, includes genera such as Aspergillus and Penicillium, known for producing ligninolytic and oxidative enzymes and for metabolizing lignin-derived aromatic compounds (Brink et al. 2019, Zhu et al. 2023). Nectriaceae and Trichosporonaceae were also recurrent, the latter being particularly associated with the assimilation of aromatic intermediates and potentially acting in later stages of lignin degradation (Brink et al. 2019, Wang et al. 2024). Finally, the occurrence of taxa classified as Fungi_fam_incertae_sedis in the final passages highlights the limitations of current fungal reference databases, and suggests the presence of poorly characterized lineages adapted to niches generated during lignin enrichment.

Influence of temperature and lignin type

Beta diversity analysis based on the Bray–Curtis index and visualized by NMDS revealed pronounced shifts in fungal community composition throughout the enrichment process, highlighting the strong influence of environmental factors on consortium structure. The clear separation between samples from the first and sixth passages indicates a consistent process of adaptive microbial succession.

The initially heterogeneous communities progressively became more homogeneous under selective enrichment with lignin as the sole carbon source, as reflected by greater dispersion of first passage samples and tighter clustering observed in the sixth passage. This pattern is consistent with the alpha diversity results observed for bacteria, which indicated a strong simplification of the communities over successive passages (Moraes et al. 2018, Mendes et al. 2021).

PERMANOVA analysis indicated that temperature was the factor explaining the largest proportion of variation in fungal community composition, despite the relatively low R² value expected for complex ecological datasets. Fungi are sensitive to temperature, which directly affects metabolic rates, gene expression, enzyme stability, and competitive interactions (Panico et al. 2025). This contrasts with what was observed for the bacteria present in the consortia (Mendes et al. 2021), in which PERMANOVA analysis identified lignin-type as the experimental factor that best explained variation. Compared to other microorganisms, fungi often exhibit narrower thermal windows for optimal growth, and even small temperature shifts can result in substantial changes in community composition (Panico et al. 2025). The separation observed between samples incubated at 30°C and 37°C, even under the same carbon source, suggests that increased temperature favored thermotolerant genera.

The significant effect of soil type (BY vs. MG) reinforces the importance of the initial inoculum as a determinant of microbial community trajectories. Although enrichment promotes functional convergence, the final taxonomic composition retains a signature of the source environment, reflecting initial differences in diversity, functional redundancy, and metabolic potential. Fungal communities derived from BY soil maintained greater dispersion in the NMDS space, showing higher residual diversity and greater ecological flexibility throughout enrichment. In contrast, MG-derived consortia formed more defined clusters consistent with alpha diversity results and the lower initial complexity of this commercial inoculum. The differences observed between BY- and MG-derived communities may also reflect environmental characteristics of the original inoculum sources that were not evaluated in this study, including soil physicochemical properties such as pH and the environmental conditions under which these communities developed.

These results support the hypothesis that environmental variables influence fungal community assembly during lignin enrichment, with temperature emerging as the strongest driver of community composition, while inoculum source also significantly affected community trajectories. In contrast, contradicting our original hypothesis, lignin type did not exert a statistically significant effect at the genus level, suggesting that substrate-related influences were weaker than initially expected under the experimental conditions evaluated.

Although lignin type did not have a statistically significant effect on fungal community composition in the PERMANOVA analysis, a separation trend was observed in the NMDS ordination. However, this pattern should be interpreted cautiously. The BE substrate consisted of an alkaline extract obtained from switchgrass and was not chemically characterized; therefore, the exact lignin concentration and composition are unknown. Previous studies have demonstrated that alkaline lignin extracts may contain residual carbohydrates and other biomass-derived compounds associated with lignin fractions (Jung et al. 2019). Consequently, differences between Kraft and BE treatments may reflect not only lignin structural characteristics, but also concentration and compositional differences.

Kraft lignin is generally characterized by a higher degree of condensation, chemical modification of phenolic groups, and greater structural complexity (Lahtinen et al. 2021, Zhang et al. 2021), properties that may require more specialized oxidative enzymatic systems for degradation, including laccases and peroxidases (Gałązka et al. 2025). In contrast, alkali-extracted lignin (BE lignin) typically exhibits lower condensation and greater chemical accessibility (Pang et al. 2024). In addition, residual carbohydrates such as hemicelluloses may remain associated with alkali-extracted lignin depending on extraction efficiency (Rinaldi et al. 2016, Mendes et al. 2021, Pang et al. 2024). These compositional differences may partially explain the persistence of a broader range of fungal taxa in some BE enrichments, including organisms potentially utilizing more readily assimilable co-substrates rather than lignin directly.

Guilds

FUNGuildR was used to infer ecological guilds and trophic modes of fungal ASVs, based on their taxonomic assignments (Nguyen et al. 2016). Because guild assignment depends on the level of taxonomic resolution achieved and the availability of ecological information for each taxon, most ASVs in this study were classified within the ‘‘Probable’’ and ‘‘Possible’’ confidence categories. This pattern is commonly reported in metabarcoding studies where a substantial fraction of ASVs cannot be resolved to the species level (Nguyen et al. 2016).

The distribution of ecological guilds revealed a predominance of categories associated with saprotrophy, indicating the presence of fungi capable of exploiting diverse organic substrates and exhibiting considerable ecological flexibility. Guilds related to organic matter decomposition, including Wood Saprotroph, Plant Saprotroph, and Dung Saprotroph, were well represented, which is consistent with the use of lignin as the sole carbon source during the enrichment process. In contrast, symbiotic guilds such as endophytes and mycorrhizal fungi were poorly represented, likely reflecting the absence of host organisms and the selective conditions imposed by liquid culture enrichment.

A considerable proportion of ASVs was assigned to categories such as “Undefined Saprotroph,” reflecting current limitations of ecological fungal databases and metabarcorning methodology. These categories may include organisms involved in organic matter decomposition whose functional traits have not yet been fully described.

The variation in trophic mode distribution among experimental groups suggests that soil type, lignin source, and temperature influenced the functional structure of the enriched communities. Overall, the results indicate that successive enrichment with lignin promoted functional selection toward saprotrophic fungi and taxa with greater ecological plasticity, particularly those classified within combined trophic modes such as Pathotroph–Saprotroph. This trend is consistent with the reductions in taxonomic diversity and the stabilization of fungal communities observed in later enrichment passages, suggesting directional selection toward organisms better adapted to exploit lignin-derived substrates.

Functional interpretations based on FUNGuildR assignments should be considered preliminary, as only a subset of ASVs could be assigned to ecological guilds. Furthermore, these assignments are inferred from taxonomic affiliations rather than direct functional measurements. Nevertheless, most assigned ASVs were classified in the “Probable” or “Highly Probable” confidence categories, providing useful indications of potential ecological roles within the enriched communities. These results should therefore be interpreted as hypotheses regarding community function rather than definitive functional characterization.

Conclusions

Successive enrichment using lignin as the sole carbon source promoted strong ecological selection within fungal communities, resulting in substantial reductions in richness, phylogenetic diversity, and community complexity over six enrichment passages. These changes were accompanied by a progressive shift toward taxonomically simplified and more homogeneous communities, indicating the establishment of specialized fungal consortia adapted to lignin-based cultivation conditions.

The source of the inoculum influenced the initial composition and diversity of fungal communities, with BY soil-derived consortia exhibiting greater richness and heterogeneity than those derived from the commercial substrate. Among the experimental factors evaluated, temperature exerted the strongest influence on fungal community assembly and composition, whereas lignin type showed limited and statistically nonsignificant effects at the genus level. Taxonomic analyses revealed the progressive dominance of a restricted number of fungal lineages, primarily within Ascomycota, suggesting selective enrichment of taxa capable of persisting under lignin-driven conditions.

Functional predictions indicated a predominance of saprotrophic lifestyles in the enriched communities, consistent with selection for fungi associated with the decomposition of complex organic substrates. However, these functional inferences should be interpreted cautiously given the limited proportion of ASVs assigned to ecological guilds and the predominance of moderate-confidence classifications.

Overall, our results support the hypothesis that lignin-based enrichment promotes the selection of specialized fungal communities and demonstrate that temperature is a major driver of fungal community succession under these conditions. The findings of this study provide a valuable resource for future investigations aimed at identifying fungal taxa and enzymatic systems with potential biotechnological applications for lignin valorization and sustainable biomass conversion.
