Galleria mellonella proteomic response to infection with Streptococcus pyogenes
Authors: Corina Abt-Becker, Josefine Burzlaff, Stefan Mikkat, Bernd Kreikemeyer, Nadja Patenge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGalleria mellonella is considered a suitable alternative infection model for studying microbial infections. Unlike conventional animal models, invertebrate models are readily available and not restricted by ethical concerns. Husbandry, maintenance, and handling of G. mellonella larvae are relatively simple. Furthermore, the insect innate immune system, consisting of a humoral and a cellular branch, shares similarities with the mammalian immune system. Streptococcus pyogenes is a strictly human pathogen responsible for a range of clinical manifestations, from superficial uncomplicated infections to severe invasive diseases. Over the past decade, S. pyogenes infection, serotype-specific virulence, and antimicrobial treatment have been studied using G. mellonella larvae. To further validate the G. mellonella infection model, this study investigated the S. pyogenes-specific response of the larvae using a proteomics approach. Infected larvae responded by upregulation of recognition molecules, antimicrobial peptides, and general stress pathways and by activation of the phenol oxidase pathway. In addition, factors known to be involved in the inflammatory response in humans were upregulated, including 15-hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase, macrophage migration inhibitory factor, and nucleobindin-2. In particular, the levels of C-type lectin receptors and C4b-binding protein alpha were elevated. In humans and mice, these proteins are known to interact directly with S. pyogenes during the course of infection. Taken together, these results support the suitability of the G. mellonella model for studying S. pyogenes infection to reduce the use of vertebrate model organisms.KEYWORDS: Galleria mellonella, Streptococcus pyogenes, Group A streptococcus, insect infection model, proteomics, infectious disease

Introduction

Larvae of Galleria mellonella, also known as the greater wax moth, are a popular infection model for investigating bacterial and fungal pathogenesis and for testing the activity and potential toxicity of novel antimicrobial therapies [1,2]. In addition to fewer ethical concerns, the acquisition, husbandry, and maintenance of the larvae are relatively simple compared to mammalian infection model organisms. Therefore, high-throughput approaches are feasible when using the larvae as an infection model [3]. In contrast to other popular invertebrate models, such as the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster or the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, the larvae of G. mellonella can be kept at 37°C. This is a key advantage, because it corresponds to the optimum growth temperature of many human pathogenic organisms [4]. One limitation of the model is the absence of standard experimental protocols and breeding guidelines. Another limitation is that no certified stock centers are providing consistent genotypes of larvae [4,5].

The immune response (IR) of G. mellonella is to some extent similar to the innate IR of vertebrates [6,7]. G. mellonella has a cellular IR represented by hemocytes that are found in the hemolymph and are responsible for phagocytosis, encapsulation, and clotting of pathogens. Recently, immunophenotyping of G. mellonella hemocytes based on human immune cell markers has underlined the analogies between the larvae and the human cellular immune system [8]. In the humoral response of G. mellonella, members of the phenol oxidase pathway, complement-like proteins, and antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are involved [2,9]. However, in the insect immune system, adaptive immune responses are missing. Consequently, the G. mellonella infection model cannot replace mammalian models. Nevertheless, it can be employed for the evaluation of the activity of antimicrobial agents in a complex system. Thereby, it helps to reduce the number of experiments using mammalian model organisms [2]. In the context of the 3 R principles (replacement, reduction, and refinement), specified in Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council of 22 September 2010 on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes, G. mellonella is increasingly used as an alternative model organism. Many laboratories employ G. mellonella to study pathogenic microorganisms, particularly bacteria and fungi [10–12].

Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A streptococcus, GAS) is responsible for various infections ranging from asymptomatic infections to invasive diseases with high morbidity and mortality, causing a high overall burden of disease [13]. Since it is a human-specific pathogen, standard model organisms cannot easily be used to study streptococcal pathogenesis [14,15]. Nevertheless, certain aspects of GAS infection and treatment have been investigated by us and others using G. mellonella [16,17]. The recently published genome sequence of G. mellonella [18] provides the basis for omics analyses, in which data acquired from G. mellonella infection studies can be compared to sequences from humans, mice, or other host organisms.

In this work, a proteomic analysis of larvae infected with S. pyogenes was conducted. Samples of infected larvae and controls were collected over the course of the infection. The IR of larvae included the expected upregulation of recognition molecules, antimicrobial peptides, and general stress-response pathways. Additionally, skeletal muscle proteins, cytokines, and factors with high similarity to the mammalian innate IR were activated. Our results confirm a stable infection of G. mellonella larvae by S. pyogenes leading to a dynamic response with similarities to the proteomic response reported in a murine GAS infection model [19].

Materials and methods

Bacterial strain and culture conditions

S. pyogenes serotype M49 strain 591 [20,21], kindly provided by R. Lütticken (Aachen, Germany), was grown on Columbia blood agar plates (Becton Dickinson, Heidelberg, Germany) and cultured in Todd–Hewitt broth supplemented with 0.5% yeast extract (THY; Oxoid, Wesel, Germany) at 37°C under a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Viability was monitored by CFU/ml determination on agar plates.

Infection of G. mellonella larvae

Larvae of the greater wax moth Galleria mellonella were obtained from Bugs-International GmbH (Irsingen, Germany). The larvae were stored and fed at 37°C in a mixture of the following dietary components: 250 ml honey, 250 ml glycerol, 100 g brewer’s yeast flakes, 100 g powder milk, 750 g wheat bran, and 250 g oat flakes. Larvae with a weight of 0.15–0.2 g were used for infection [10,16]. For infection experiments, S. pyogenes was grown overnight in THY, washed twice in sterile 0.9% NaCl (Roth, Karlsruhe, Germany), and suspended in sterile 0.9% NaCl to a final concentration of 2.5–4 × 108 CFU/ml. Larvae were infected with 2.5–4 × 106 CFU/larva in 10 µl sterile 0.9% NaCl. Bacteria were injected into the hemocoel of the larvae between the last two pairs of pro-legs using a microapplicator (World Precisions Instruments, Sarasota, FL) and a fine-dosage syringe (Omnican-F, 0.01–1 ml, 0.30 × 12 mm; B. Braun AG, Melsungen, Germany). As a mock control, sterile 0.9% NaCl was injected. Larvae were incubated for 7 d, and survival was monitored daily. Larvae were considered alive when they responded to a light touch with tweezers. Pupae were considered alive and were removed from the experiment [10,16].

Determination of the bacterial load in G. mellonella larvae

To measure the bacterial load in infected G. mellonella, larvae were infected as described above. At 4 h, 24 h, 72 h, 96 h, 120 h, 144 h, and 168 h after infection, 5 larvae per time point were cooled for 10 min at 4°C and subsequently homogenized with a glass lysing matrix (2.85–3.3 mm) in the Precellys 24 Tissue Homogenizer (Bertin GmbH, Frankfurt am Main, Germany) at 5000 rpm for 4 × 20 s. Serial dilutions of the homogenate were plated on Columbia agar with 5% sheep blood (BD, Maryland, USA). Colony identity was confirmed on a random basis by colony PCR, using emm-specific primers (forward primer: 5’-ATAAGGAGCATAAAAATGGCT-3’, reverse primer: 5’-AGCTTAGTTTTCTTCTTTGCG-3’).

Proteomics analysis of the IR of infected G. mellonella larvae

In each experiment, 45 larvae were infected with S. pyogenes 591 with 2.5–4 × 106 CFU/larva and 30 larvae were injected with 10 µl 0.9% NaCl (mock control). The CFU/ml in the bacterial suspension was verified for each experiment by serial dilution and plating. Only larvae infected with inocula containing the required dose range were used in the proteome analysis. Hemolymph was extracted after 4 h, 24 h, and 72 h from 15 infected larvae and 10 noninfected larvae per time point. Each experiment was performed in three biological replicates.

Extraction of the hemolymph and separation of the hemocytes

The larvae were incubated at 4°C for 15 min. The head was removed with a scalpel under sterile conditions. Hemolymph was extracted from the larvae, and 30 µl were added to a tube containing an anticoagulant solution (a spatula tip of N-phenylthiourea (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), 1.5 µl EDTA (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) (10 mM), and 1.5 µl sodium citrate (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) (30 mM)) [22]. Per time point, the hemolymph of all 15 infected or 10 uninfected larvae, respectively, was pooled in one tube. The hemocytes were removed from the hemolymph by centrifugation (200 × g, 8°C, 15 min). The supernatant was transferred to a fresh tube and centrifuged at 20,000g and 8°C for 3 min. The resulting supernatant was transferred to a new tube and designated cell-free hemolymph (CFH). The cell pellet was suspended in 0.9%NaCl and centrifuged (200 × g, 8°C, 15 min). The resulting supernatant was discarded. The pellet was suspended in PBS with 5 µM glucose, and the samples were designated hemocyte fraction. The CFH and the hemocyte fraction were stored at −20°C.

Sample preparation for mass spectrometry

The hemolymph was diluted 100-fold with 50 mM Tris/HCl (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), pH 7.5. Protein concentration was measured using the Bio-Rad protein assay (Bio-Rad Munich, Germany). Sample volumes containing 75 µg protein were subjected to methanol/chloroform precipitation as previously described [23]. The precipitate was dissolved in 50 µl digestion buffer containing 1.5% sodium deoxycholate (SDC) (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (ABC) (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and incubated for 5 min at 95°C. Thereafter, 30 µl of 50 mM ABC and 10 µl reduction/alkylation reagent containing 100 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP) (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and 400 mM 2-chloroacetamide (CAM) (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) were added [24]. Reduction/Alkylation was performed for 15 min at 37°C. Then 10 µl of sequencing-grade trypsin (0.1 µg/µl, Promega GmbH, Walldorf, Germany) was added to obtain an enzyme-to-protein ratio of approximately 1:75 in a total volume of 100 µl. Digestion was performed at 37°C for about 16 h.

Hemocytes were lysed in a lysis/digestion buffer containing 2% SDC and 50 mM ABC by incubation at 95°C for 5 min and subsequent sonication using a bath sonicator for 10 min. The protein concentration was measured using the Bio-Rad protein assay (Bio-Rad Munich, Germany). Cell lysates containing 50 µg protein in a volume of 50 µl were supplemented with 30 µl 50 mM ABC and 10 µl reduction/alkylation reagent and further processed for digestion and StageTip purification as described for the hemolymph samples.

Peptides were purified using SDB-RPS StageTips with two layers of Empore SDB-RPS disk material (Sigma-Aldrich) [25]. To avoid acid-induced precipitation of SDC [24], 27 µl of the digest solution corresponding to 20 µg of protein was mixed with 27 µl of isopropanol (Roth, Karlsruhe, Germany) containing 1% trifluoroacetic acid (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) (TFA). Insoluble material was removed by centrifugation before the sample was loaded onto an SDB-RPS StageTip by centrifugation at 1500g. The StageTip was successively washed with 200 µl wash buffer 1 (1% TFA in isopropanol) and 200 µl wash buffer 2 (0.2% TFA in water) before peptides were eluted in LoBind tube (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) with 30 µl elution buffer (1% ammonia (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), 80% acetonitrile (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany)) [26]. Finally, the peptide solutions were vacuum-dried using a centrifugal evaporator and dissolved with 2% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) for mass spectrometric analysis. Peptide concentrations were measured using the Invitrogen Qubit protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Mass spectrometry and data processing

LC–MS was carried out with a nanoAcquity UPLC system (Waters, Manchester, UK) in combination with a Waters Synapt G2-S mass spectrometer via a NanoLockSpray ion source as described in [27]. 40 ng and 120 ng of peptides from hemolymph and hemocytes were injected. About 40 fmol of Hi3 ClpB_ECOLI standard (Waters) were included for absolute quantification. The Synapt G2-S mass spectrometer was used in data-independent mode with ion mobility separation (HDMSE). Alternating scans at low and elevated collision energies, each with a duration of 0.6 s, enabled the acquisition of precursor and fragment ion information, respectively [28]. Samples were analyzed in duplicate.

Raw data processing, protein identification, and label-free quantification were performed using Progenesis QI for proteomics version 4.1 (Nonlinear Dynamics, Newcastle upon Tyne, UK). A database of 18,634 protein sequences from G. mellonella (UniProt release 2022_02) was used for protein identification. The sequences of E. coli chaperone protein ClpB (P63284) and porcine trypsin were included for analysis. Two missing cleavage sites were allowed, oxidation of methionine residues was considered a variable modification, and carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues a fixed modification. The false discovery rate was set to 1%. Peptides were accepted, if they were identified by at least five fragment ions. Peptide ions were required to be identified at least two times within the dataset with a minimum ion score of 6.0. In general, a mass error below 13.0 ppm was accepted, or below 20 ppm for ions with a score exceeding 7.0. Peptide ions needed to comprise a minimum peptide length of six amino acid residues. We included several peptides that were identified based on a single unique peptide. In these cases, the manually evaluated and annotated spectra are provided as supplementary material. Proteins were quantified by the absolute quantification Hi3 method using Hi3 E. coli ClpB standard (Waters) as reference [29]. Results were given as fmol on column. Statistical analyses were performed using Progenesis QI for proteomics version 4.1 (Nonlinear Dynamics, Newcastle upon Tyne, UK).

Functional enrichment analysis

The functional enrichment analysis was performed using g:Profiler with g:SCS multiple testing correction method applying significance threshold of 0.05 (version e113_eg59_p19_f6a03c19) [30].

Data availability

The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE [31] partner repository at http://doi.org/10.6019/PXD054400 with the dataset identifier PXD054400.

Results

In this study, the proteomic response of G. mellonella to infection with S. pyogenes was investigated. G. mellonella was infected with S. pyogenes and proteomics analyses of the CFH and the hemocyte fraction were performed.

Infection of G. mellonella with S. pyogenes and subsequent collection of proteomics samples

First, suitable infection conditions were identified to monitor the IR of G. mellonella over time. Survival rates of the larvae depended on the infection dose (Figure 1(A)). For the proteomics analyses, 2.5–4 × 106 CFU S. pyogenes M49 strain 591 in 10 µl were injected per larva, because a constant mortality rate was observed under these conditions. Following injection with this dose, 85% of the infected larvae died within 7 d, whereas all mock-infected larvae survived the experiment (Figure 1(A)). Four hours postinfection (p.i.), larvae showed already reduced mobility and signs of melanization, which increased over time. After 7 d, the remaining larvae appeared black and showed almost no mobility. To determine the bacterial load over the course of the infection, larvae were infected with 2.5–4 × 106 CFU S. pyogenes, collected at different time points, and homogenized. Subsequently, the serially diluted homogenate was plated on blood agar.

Sample size: n = 120 in all groups except for 0.5–1 × 106 CFU/larva: n = 30. (B) Bacterial load in infected G. mellonella larvae over the course of the experiment. S. pyogenes was identified by colony appearance and hemolysis. For verification, colony PCR was performed with emm-specific primers. The bacterial load of the larvae remained stable for 120 h at around 2–5 × 106 CFU/larva (Figure 1(B)), indicating a persistent infection with S. pyogenes. At 144 h p.i., the majority of larvae had died, and S. pyogenes could only be detected in one individual. After 168 h p.i., no viable bacteria could be re-isolated from the remaining infected larvae. To cover early, intermediate, and late stages of infection, CFH and hemocytes were collected at three different time points. Proteomes of infected and mock-infected larvae from 4, 24, and 72 h p.i. were analyzed by mass spectrometry.

Analysis of the G. mellonella proteome following infection with S. pyogenes

Proteome analyses using data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry (HDMSE) were performed on three independent samples per time point. For each sample, CFH or extracted proteins from hemocytes were combined from 15 infected or 10 mock-infected larvae, respectively.

A total of 223 and 867 proteins or protein groups, whose identification was based on at least two unique peptides, were detected in hemolymph and hemocytes, respectively. There was a large overlap between proteins detected from hemolymph and hemocytes. Only 31 of the 223 proteins detected in the CFH were unique to this fraction (Figure 2(A)).

(A) Total proteins detected by HDMSE. (B) Proteins with significantly altered abundance following infection.

In addition, 27 proteins from hemolymph and 315 proteins from hemocytes were identified based on a single unique peptide. Only seven of these proteins, whose mass spectra were manually validated, were included in the analysis. These include small antimicrobial peptides, e.g. cecropins, which are composed of 31–39 amino acids [32]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed, generating six data points per group, representing three independent replicates for control and infection samples, each analyzed in two HDMSE runs (Suppl. Figure S1).

The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE [31] partner repository at http://doi.org/10.6019/PXD054400 with the dataset identifier PXD054400. Additionally, the data from proteins identified with two or more unique peptides are provided in Table S1.

For further analyses, statistically significant differential protein abundance was defined as a fold change of >2 and a p-value of ≤0.010 (ANOVA). Overall, 423 proteins were differentially abundant following infection. Sixty of those were unique to the CFH fraction, 248 were unique for hemocytes, and 115 proteins were found differentially abundant in both fractions (Figure 2(B)). Among those were proteins responsible for nonself recognition, antimicrobial peptides, and carboxylic ester hydrolases. An overview of the differentially abundant proteins detected in the two fractions during the three phases of infection is depicted in volcano plots (Figure 3). In the early phase of infection (4 h p.i.), 24 proteins in the hemolymph of infected larvae showed significantly changed abundance, 21 of which were upregulated (Figure 3(A)). These proteins included peptidoglycan recognition proteins and serine protease inhibitors (serpins), involved in the regulation of complement activation. After 24 h, 124 proteins occurred in increased amounts in the hemolymph of infected larvae compared to the control group and five proteins occurred in decreased amounts (Figure 3(B)). Prominent among the upregulated proteins were sensory proteins, receptors involved in recognition and opsonization, and AMPs. Finally, 72 h p.i., 100 proteins were upregulated, and 36 proteins were downregulated compared to noninfected larvae (Figure 3(C)). The majority of the proteins with reduced abundance in infected larvae are involved in development, silk production, and metabolism.

In hemocytes, 77 differentially abundant proteins were detected 4 h p.i. (Figure 3(D)). Of those, 59 proteins were upregulated and 18 were downregulated. About 24 h p.i., 167 proteins were up- and 33 proteins were downregulated (Figure 3(E)). Comparable to the data from CFH, recognition receptors, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), and carboxylesterases were highly upregulated. Finally, in hemocytes 72 h p.i., 143 proteins with increased abundance and 116 proteins with decreased abundance compared to mock-infected larvae were identified (Figure 3(F)). In addition to the recognition receptors detected in the CFH, increased levels of three C-type lectin receptors (CLRs) were observed in the hemocyte fraction.

Four hours p.i. samples represent the early IR. Most proteins that were present in increased abundance at 4 h p.i. in infected larvae compared to control samples increased further until 24 h p.i. Examples are chemosensory proteins 8 and 15, peptidoglycan recognition proteins, serine protease inhibitors, and a number of cytoskeleton and muscle components in the CFH. Overall, more proteins were detected in increased amounts at 24 h p.i., some of which belonged to the functional groups mentioned above. Additionally, the amount of β-1,3-glucan recognition proteins, carboxylic ester hydrolases, the majority of antimicrobial peptides, such as gloverin and 6tox, lysozyme, metabolic enzymes, nonfood digestive serine proteases, and heat shock proteins increased during the intermediate IR.

In the later infection phase, at 72 h p.i., many of the proteins whose concentration had increased during the course of infection were still present in high abundance, e.g. recognition proteins, serine protease inhibitors, cytoskeleton and muscle components, and antimicrobial peptides. Additionally, several proteins were downregulated in the late infection phase, including proteins involved in development and metabolic enzymes. Some proteins were downregulated in the hemolymph throughout infection, e.g. the prophenoloxidase and the GILT-like protein 1.

To further understand the functional implications of the proteomic changes, functional enrichment analyses were performed using g:Profiler [30]. Among the prominent pathways enriched in CFH and hemocytes were peptidoglycan metabolism, defense responses, immune responses, and responses to external/biotic stimuli (Figure 4(A,B)). In hemocytes, fatty acid- and lipid-related processes were downregulated at 4 h p.i. (Figure 4(B)). Exclusively, in CFH 24 h p.i., purine- and pyridine-related metabolic processes were highly enriched (Figure S2).

Proteins associated with nonself recognition

Nonself recognition proteins and opsonins were detected in increased abundance following infection with S. pyogenes (Figure 5(A,B)). These included peptidoglycan recognition proteins, β-1,3-glucan recognition proteins, and hemolin. Upregulation of a hemicentin 1-like protein was observed in the CFH. Additionally, C-type lectins were differentially abundant in hemocytes (Figure S14). Toll-like receptor 6 was downregulated at 4 h p.i. in the hemocyte fraction of infected larvae. Seventy-two hours p.i., it was present in amounts comparable to the mock-infected control (Figure S14).

Furthermore, chemosensory proteins and odorant-binding proteins were detected in significantly altered abundances following infection (Figure 5(C,D)). Among them, gustatory receptor candidate 59 strongly increased upon infection. Chemosensory proteins and odorant-binding proteins showed different expression patterns, with some of them being upregulated and others downregulated (Figure 5(C,D)), suggesting distinct roles during infection. Changes of insect chemosensitivity induced by symbionts and pathogens lead to a modification of insect behavior that can be advantageous for the respective microorganism. A review on the influence of microorganisms on insect chemoreception was recently published [33].

Activation of the phenoloxidase system

The phenoloxidase system is activated upon injury and infection. In this process, the prophenoloxidase is cleaved by serine proteases. The resulting phenoloxidase (PO) oxidizes phenols to quinones that are then polymerized to melanin [34,35]. Dopachrome tautomerases also play a role in melanin synthesis [36,37].

Consistently, in the hemolymph of infected larvae, the abundance of prophenoloxidases decreased, while the levels of PO-activating enzymes and l-dopachrome tautomerases increased considerably in the hemolymph and in hemocytes (Figure 6). Furthermore, tyrosine 3-monooxygenase (tyrosine hydroxylase, TH), representing another pathway of melanin production in insects [38], was upregulated 20-fold in hemocytes in the early IR (4 h p.i.) (Figure 6(B)).

Serine proteases (SPs) play an important role in the IR by activating the phenol oxidase system responsible for melanization [39]. In response to infection, four nondietary SPs, including two CLIP-domain SPs (Figure 6(C,D)) and eight SP inhibitors (serpins), were upregulated (Figure 6(E,F)). Two of the serpins are leukocyte elastase inhibitor-like proteins.

Antimicrobial peptides and putative immune-relevant proteins

AMPs play a crucial role in the IR of G. mellonella. The larvae exhibit a pathogen-specific AMP response, since they can recognize different pathogen types [40]. The abundance of 16 AMPs belonging to 10 classes was elevated following streptococcal infection (Figure 7(A,B)). The highest increase was observed for gloverins, cecropins, defensins, the silk protein seroin, and the inducible metalloproteinase inhibitor (IMPI).

Additional proteins with immune-relevant functions were detected, which showed differential abundance (Figure 7(C,D)). Two negative regulators of the IR were upregulated during infection: peroxiredoxin and 15-hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase. During the late stage of infection (72 h p.i.), these proteins were downregulated again. In contrast, the proteins were highly upregulated at 72 h p.i. in the mock-infected larvae. The arginine kinase and the ATPase apyrase, two proteins involved in the regulation of extracellular ATP levels, showed increased abundance during infection. The abundance of an Hdd1-like protein, involved in nodulation, was also increased in the CFH. In the hemocyte fraction of the infected larvae, a C4b-binding protein alpha-like (C4BP) protein was detected in increased amounts. Three probable salivary secreted peptides that are identical to REPAT (REsponse to PAThogens) proteins in other lepidopteran insects, e.g. Spodoptera frugiperda and S. exigua [41,42], were identified in hemocytes and were upregulated over the course of infection. Another protein that was present in elevated amounts in the hemocyte fraction of infected larvae was a fibulin-1-like protein, showing 46% similarity to the human fibulin-5 isoform 1 precursor.

As part of the early IR, an insect cytokine was detected in the hemocyte fraction. The macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) shows a similarity of 54% to its human counterpart and was 12-fold upregulated 4 h p.i.

Restriction of free iron to reduce bacterial growth is an important defense mechanism. Here, iron-storage and transport proteins ferritin and transferrin were present in elevated amounts in the infected larvae (Figure S15).

Detoxification and regulation of reactive oxygen species

Several stress-related proteins were upregulated by G. mellonella in response to GAS infection. Among them, the small heat shock proteins (HSPs) HSP20, HSP21.4, and HSP27.4, as well as HSP70 (Figure 8(A,B)). Additionally, several members of the family of carboxylesterases were significantly upregulated in infected larvae (Figure 8(A,B)).

The regulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) was also affected in the infected larvae (Figure 8(C,D)). Superoxide dismutase, sulfhydryl oxidase, and thioredoxin were upregulated during infection. In contrast, the level of catalase decreased in the hemocyte fraction over the course of the infection. Glutathione-S-transferase levels were also elevated upon infection. Particularly, in the hemocyte fraction of the infected larvae, a high abundance of the enzyme was detected at 4 h p.i., before it decreased over the course of the infection to a level comparable to that of control larvae (Figure 8(D)).

Silk and developmental pathways

The amounts of the antimicrobial silk protein seroin and silk fibroins increased in infected larvae. In the CFH of mock-infected larvae, the levels of these proteins diminished, while they were moderately downregulated in the hemocyte fraction of control larvae (Figure 9(A,B)).

Several proteins responsible for larval growth or metamorphosis increased in noninfected larvae over the course of the experiment, while they remained unchanged or increased only slightly in infected larvae (Figure 9(C/D)). Sericotropin was observed in a highly elevated abundance at 72 h in control larvae, while it remained unaffected over the course of the infection. The abundance of two juvenile hormone (JH)-suppressible proteins increased over time in noninfected larvae, while they were detected in slightly increased amounts in the CFH at 72 h p.i. and even decreased over the course of the infection in the hemocyte fraction. In contrast, a JH-binding protein was drastically downregulated in hemocytes of noninfected larvae at time point 72 h, whereas it remained constant in infected larvae. For JH esterase-like proteins, a strong increase was observed over the course of infection while they were present in constant amounts in control larvae.

The levels of cytoskeletal and muscular proteins were elevated following infection with S. pyogenes. For instance, myosin light chain alkali and tropomyosin-2 were considerably elevated in the infected larvae (Figure S16). While the amounts of several metabolic proteins responsible for carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism increased in control larvae, their amounts remained constant or decreased slightly upon infection (Figure S16). Similar dynamics were observed for the amino acid storage and JH-binding protein hexamerin (Figure S16).

Discussion

In previous studies, we used G. mellonella as an invertebrate infection and treatment model for S. pyogenes and S. pneumoniae [16,43]. Here, we investigated the G. mellonella response to S. pyogenes infection by analyzing changes in the host proteome during infection. Our goal was to yield insight into the activation of immune protection in the invertebrate infection model and compare it to the specific IR to infection by S. pyogenes in other model organisms and the human host.

Therefore, in addition to analyzing the overall proteomic response of infected larvae, which is presented in volcano plots and heatmaps, we focused on alterations in specific groups of proteins, such as stress-related proteins and putative immune-relevant proteins. In the discussion, we point out proteins with similarity to known factors of the immune response of other host organisms, including mice and humans. Some of these proteins play a specific role in the mammalian immune response to S. pyogenes infection. Further, we compare the proteomic response of G. mellonella to the murine proteome response to infection by S. pyogenes. The conceptual novelty of this study lies in the comparison of the larval proteomic response to the immune response of other host organisms and the identification of proteins that are homologous to immune factors activated in the mammalian immune system upon infection with S. pyogenes. These proteins are candidates for a specific response to streptococcal infection, which can be further investigated in future functional studies. The data of this work contribute to the long-term goal of optimizing data transferability between model organisms and between infection models and the human host.

We were able to induce a stable infection and investigated time points 4 p.i., 24 p.i., and 72 p.i. representing early, intermediate, and late responses to infection, respectively. In the CFH extracted from mock-infected larvae and from larvae infected with S. pyogenes, 223 proteins were identified by proteomic analyses. This number is comparable to the 185 proteins that were detected in the CFH extracted from G. mellonella larvae infected with Mycobacterium bovis BCG lux [44]. We additionally identified a total of 867 proteins in the hemocyte fraction of the corresponding samples.

The general immune response of G. mellonella to microbial infection

G. mellonella is used as an infection model for a variety of pathogenic microorganisms, including fungi, Gram-positive, and Gram-negative bacteria with different host specificities. In several studies, the proteomic response to infection has been analyzed, which helps to understand the general immune response that is activated during infection and to identify specific responses to exposure to a particular microorganism. Following infection with S. pyogenes, we observed an elevated abundance of a variety of proteins that have been identified before in previous studies. Key proteins that were highly upregulated upon infection by diverse microbial pathogens are summarized in Table 1.

Key proteins highly upregulated in Galleria mellonella upon microbial infection.

In response to infection with Fusarium oxysporum microconidia, G. mellonella showed differential protein abundance of proteins involved in transport, immune response, storage, oxide-reduction, and catabolism. Specifically, antimicrobial peptides, activators of the phenol oxidase cascade, and lipid-transport proteins were upregulated [45]. Following infection with Candida albicans, cecropin-A, prophenoloxidase-activating proteinase-1, and peptidoglycan recognition-like protein B were upregulated at 6 h p.i. After 24 h, the abundance of muscle proteins, including troponin I, and stress-related proteins, such as glutathione-S-transferase-like protein, were elevated [12]. In the proteomic response to infection of G. mellonella by viable conidia of Aspergillus fumigatus, the abundances of a

gustatory receptor candidate, chemosensory proteins, antimicrobial peptides, peptidoglycan recognition-like protein B, and prophenoloxidase-activating enzyme 3 were elevated [46].

Analysis of the G. mellonella proteome following infection with M. bovis BCG lux revealed upregulation of cecropin, gloverin, hemolin, Hdd-proteins, serpins, phenoloxidase activation enzyme 3, and chemosensory protein 7 [44]. In response to Staphylococcus aureus infection, upregulation of gloverin, cecropins, lysozyme, peptidoglycan recognition-like proteins B, inducible metalloproteinase inhibitor protein, and hemolin was detected [11].

Proteins regulated in G. mellonella in response to infection by S. pyogenes

Following infection with S. pyogenes, peptidoglycan recognition proteins (PRGPs), beta-1,3-glucan binding proteins, and hemolin were upregulated in the CFH and in the hemocyte fraction of infected larvae. These proteins are involved in the binding of peptidoglycans, beta-1,3-glucan, and lipoteichoic acid (LTA) [2]. Additionally, apolipophorin III was significantly upregulated in the CFH. Apolipophorin III facilitates lipid transport, serves as a pattern-recognition molecule, and activates several pathways [47]. It is highly similar to mammalian apolipoprotein E (apoE) that plays a role in Alzheimer’s disease, in part by regulation of the innate IR [48].

C-type lectin receptors (CLRs) were elevated in the hemocytes following infection. CLRs are pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) expressed on human antigen-presenting cells. S. pyogenes specifically interacts with the human CLR langerin (CD207), which is expressed on human Langerhans cells (LCs). LCs are present in the skin epidermis and in the pharynx, both representing major infection sites of S. pyogenes [49,50]. Additionally, the macrophage-inducible C-type lectin (Mincle) recognizes glycolipid antigens from S. pyogenes, which serve as lipoteichoic acid anchors. Mincle is upregulated on neutrophils upon infection in murine and human skin and activates protective immunity against S. pyogenes infection [51]. Disruption of Mincle-mediated detection inhibited the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines in a mouse model of systemic S. pyogenes infection. Consequently, bacteremia and mortality were increased [51].

The abundance of SPs and serpins was upregulated in infected larvae. CLIP domain-containing serine proteases (CLIP SPs) are readily activated following infection. They induce the production of antimicrobial peptides and mediate the activation of prophenoloxidase, which is essential for melanization [52]. Serpins, on the other hand, negatively regulate the innate IR by inhibiting serine proteases. In mammals, leukocyte elastase inhibitor (SERPINB1) acts as an efficient inhibitor of neutrophil SPs, thereby protecting neutrophils from SPs that are released from granules. SERPINB1 supports neutrophil survival during inflammation and sustains the balance of neutrophil reserve [53,54].

Besides the well-studied insect AMPs gloverin and cecropin that are expressed by G. mellonella following infection with Gram-negative and Gram-positive microorganisms [55], other antimicrobial agents and immune-system-related proteins were differentially regulated upon challenge with S. pyogenes. Seroins are small silk proteins with antimicrobial activity, which can be distinguished into three classes [56,57]. In the silkworm, Bombyx mori, all three classes exhibited higher antibacterial efficiency against Gram-positive bacteria compared to Gram-negative bacteria [58], indicating a specific response. By a different defense mechanism, the inducible metalloproteinase inhibitor (IMPI) protects the host from pathogen-secreted metalloproteinases [59,60]. 15-Hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase (15-PGDH) was also elevated upon infection. The anti-inflammatory 15-PGDH metabolizes prostaglandin E2 and acts as a tumor suppressor in humans [61,62].

The innate immunity of insects is in part subject to hormonal control. Particularly, JH is a positive regulator of the immune system, while simultaneously suppressing metamorphosis [63]. Consistently, we observed downregulation of JH-suppressing proteins and upregulation of the JH-binding protein in response to infection with S. pyogenes. In addition to activating the immune response, these changes lead to the inhibition of developmental pathways and represent a major energy allocation. The mechanisms by which insect hormones balance reproduction and immune responses have been recently reviewed [64].

Additionally, proteins that play a role in the cellular IR of other organisms increased in the infected larvae. Nucleobindin-2, which controls calcium homeostasis by regulating calcium storage and release, was induced during infection in the CFH. In the mammalian immune system, nucleobindin-2 (NUCB2)/nesfatin-1 is found in plasmacytoid dendritic cells, in marginal zone B cells, and neutrophils [65]. It is associated with the regulation of the inflammatory response and antitumor immunity [66,67].

A C4b-binding protein alpha (C4BP)-like protein was detected in increased amounts in the hemocyte fraction of infected larvae. C4BP inhibits the classical and lectin pathways of complement activation in humans. The binding of C4BP and factor H in the human host is part of the immune-evasion strategies employed by S. pyogenes [68]. A macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) homolog was upregulated in the hemocytes of the infected larvae 4 h p.i. and decreased over the course of the experiment. MIF is a regulator of the mammalian innate immune system and promotes the pro-inflammatory response of immune cells by counteracting the inhibitory effects of glucocorticoids [69].

Comparison of the proteome response in G. mellonella compared to the murine host proteome response following infection by S. pyogenes

Lapek and coworkers investigated the organ-specific responses during systemic S. pyogenes infection in mice and mapped the IR in a murine organ proteome atlas [19]. In the heart of infected mice, troponin, myosin light chain 3 (Myl3), fatty acid binding protein 3 (Fabp3), and myoglobin (Mb) were upregulated. In the lung, upregulation of nebulin and a network of myosin proteins was observed [19]. We found that troponin, myosin light chain alkali, and Fabp1 were upregulated in the larvae upon infection with S. pyogenes. In Manduca sexta, the muscle was able to support the immune defense by the expression of IR genes and provided increased resource availability to the immune system. In consequence, muscle function was restricted under these conditions [70]. Overall, the role of skeletal muscle in the regulation of the IR has become increasingly evident [71].

In the brain of GAS-infected mice, upregulation of several cytochrome p450 (CYP) proteins related to the metabolism of arachidonic acid was noted [19]. Arachidonic acid is metabolized into both pro- and anti-inflammatory products. We observed upregulation of 15-hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase, which is also involved in arachidonic acid metabolism, upon infection of G. mellonella.

Upregulation of proteins involved in the glutathione metabolic process was determined in the liver of the GAS-infected mice [19]. This response was expected as glutathione is an important antioxidant in protection from reactive oxygen species (ROS) [72]. In our work, a high abundance of glutathione-S-transferase was detected in the infected larvae in the early phase of infection, before it decreased to the level of the control larvae in the course of infection.

Limitations of this study

In this study, we describe the dynamics of protein abundance over time after infection with S. pyogenes. A limitation of this work is that the analysis is based exclusively on proteomic data. Changes in protein abundance were not validated by Western blot analysis or enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays. Furthermore, our data do not allow distinction between a mere stress-related regulation of molecules and an active immune response of G. mellonella. While the role of several of the regulated peptides in the immune system of insects is well established, e.g. antimicrobial peptides, pattern-recognition receptors, and the phenol-oxidase cascade, the function of other proteins is still speculative. For instance, the impact of alterations of the levels of 15-hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase, troponin, or C4BP on the immune response of G. mellonella requires further investigation before conclusions about their physiological relevance in the context of infection can be drawn.

Conclusion

G. mellonella is frequently used as an infection and treatment model for bacteria and fungi. One important reason are similarities of the insect immune response and the mammalian innate immune system. Here, we investigated the proteomic response of G. mellonella larvae to infection with S. pyogenes, an exclusively human pathogen. We observed the induction of components of the general IR, which is known to be activated in G. mellonella following infection by a wide range of pathogens, including pattern-recognition molecules, AMPs, and the phenol-oxidase system. More specifically, proteins were upregulated that are homologous to immune factors activated in the mammalian immune system upon infection, in particular to human proteins known to interact with S. pyogenes. Additionally, the larvae responded by downregulation of several metabolic proteins and proteins involved in developmental pathways. Whether this effect reflects larval resource management or is driven by defense-specific mechanisms requires further investigation. Together, these data support the suitability of G. mellonella as an infection and treatment model for S. pyogenes, which can reduce the number of experiments with mammalian model organisms.
