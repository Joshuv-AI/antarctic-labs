Clinical outcomes of anticoagulated vs nonanticoagulated patients with isolated distal deep venous thrombosis
Authors: Benjamin Wick, Omar Sahibzada, Annie Cherner, Jose Ortiz de Elguea Lizarraga, Junji Tsukagoshi, Mitchell W. Cox, Christine Shokrzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe optimal clinical management of isolated distal deep venous thrombosis (IDDVT) remains controversial as the benefits of routine anticoagulant therapy are not clearly defined amid the risk of bleeding events. Our study aimed to compare clinical outcomes between patients with IDDVT treated with anticoagulation vs those managed conservatively.MethodsWe performed a retrospective analysis of adults (≥18 years old) with IDDVT identified in the TriNetX Research Network from January 1, 2014, to January 1, 2024. Patients who received anticoagulation therapy within 1 week of diagnosis were compared with patients without anticoagulation therapy. Patients were 1:1 propensity score matched for baseline covariates, including demographics and various comorbidities. We calculated and compared odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals of pulmonary embolism (PE), gastrointestinal bleeding, intracranial bleeding at 6 months, and all-cause mortality at both 6 months and 1 year.ResultsPropensity score matching resulted in 4085 patients in each group after matching. The incidence of PE and intracranial bleeding were not significantly different between anticoagulated or nonanticoagulated patients. The risk of gastrointestinal bleeding was significantly higher in patients receiving anticoagulation at 6 months. All-cause mortality was also significantly higher at both 6 months (4.94% vs 2.09%; P < .01) and 1 year (5.85% vs 2.82; P < .01).ConclusionsIn patients with IDDVT, anticoagulation therapy did not decrease the incidence of PE. These findings suggest a more selective approach to IDDVT management rather than routine anticoagulation.

Isolated distal deep venous thrombosis (IDDVT), defined as thrombosis in the deep veins distal to the popliteal vein without involvement of proximal segments, accounts for up to 50% of lower extremity DVT cases.1 Despite its high incidence, the clinical significance of IDDVT is debated due to the variable risk of progression to proximal DVT and pulmonary embolism (PE), with progression rates to proximal segments ranging from 10% to 15%.2 Although the relationship between proximal DVT and PE is well established, the risk of embolization in patients with IDDVT remains unclear. The American College of Chest Physicians (CHEST) currently recommends anticoagulation for at least 3 months for patients with IDDVT and high-risk features such as severe symptoms, proximity to the popliteal vein, unprovoked thrombosis, malignancy, immobility, or a history of venous thromboembolism (VTE).3 For patients without these high-risk features, management may involve serial imaging. However, in the 8% to 15% of patients with IDVTT with observed thrombus propagation on duplex ultrasound examination, anticoagulation may be indicated.4 Likewise, the European Society for Vascular Surgery guidelines recommend deciding on anticoagulation for IDDVT based on symptoms, risk factors for progression, and bleeding risk.5

There is not a substantial body of literature regarding the need for anticoagulation and the length of treatment; therefore, clinical practices still vary significantly when it comes to the treatment of IDDVT. The objective of this study was to compare clinical outcomes in patients diagnosed with IDDVT who were treated with therapeutic anticoagulation vs those managed without therapeutic anticoagulation.

Methods

Data source

We performed a retrospective cohort study of electronic health record data from the TriNetX US Collaborative Network database, which at the time of analysis consisted of 67 health care organizations and more than 115 million unique patients. Available data include patient demographics, diagnostic codes, procedures, and medication histories identified through standardized coding systems such as International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes, Current Procedural Terminology codes, ICD-10 Procedure Coding System codes, Logical Observation Identifier Names and Codes, Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System codes, and RxNorm Technical Documentation codes. Patient information within the TriNetX platform is fully anonymized and does not contain individually identifiable information. As such, this study meets the criteria for exemption from institutional review board review under the US Department of Health and Human Services regulations, 45 CFR 46.104(d)(4). All data within the TriNetX platform are certified to the ISO 27001:2013 information security standard and are fully compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act.

Study population

We identified patients aged 18 years old or older in the TriNetX database with ICD-10 diagnoses of tibial vein DVT (I82.44, I82.449), peroneal vein DVT (I82.45), and calf muscular vein DVT (I82.46) between January 1, 2014, and January 1, 2024. Of these patients, we identified those who underwent anticoagulation treatment (eg, administration of warfarin, heparin, low-molecular-weight heparins, direct oral anticoagulants, or direct thrombin inhibitors) within 1 week of diagnosis, as well as patients with no history of anticoagulation treatment within 1 month of diagnosis. This 1-week window was selected to better approximate early treatment decisions and to reduce misclassification of patients who may have initially undergone surveillance, anticoagulation received later for thrombus propagation, interval deterioration, or other indications. Patients with more proximal thrombus extension, multiple DVTs (ie, concurrent axillary thrombus, PE, iliac thrombus), and an underlying history of thrombophilia or malignancy were excluded.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes included thrombus progression (I82.41, I82.42, I82.43, I82.220), PE (I26), gastrointestinal bleeding (K92.1, K92.2), intracranial bleeding (I60, I61), and mortality assessed at 6 months after the diagnosis of DVT. Secondary outcomes included mortality assessed 1 year after the diagnosis of DVT. Patients with outcomes before the intervention were excluded from statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Propensity score matching was conducted within the TriNetX platform using a 1:1 greedy nearest neighbor algorithm with a caliper width of 0.1 pooled standard deviations. Covariates were selected to represent a large degree of possible demographic and medical cofounders and were analyzed beginning from 1 month before the incidence of IDDVT. Covariate balance between the matched cohorts was assessed using standardized mean differences, with a threshold of 0.1 or lower, indicating an acceptable balance. A comprehensive list of matched covariates is provided in Tables I and II. Time-to-event outcomes were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier survival estimates and compared using the log-rank test. Results

Study population

Our inclusion criteria identified 6828 patients receiving anticoagulant therapy and 4166 patients managed without anticoagulant therapy. Propensity score matching resulted in a total of 4085 patients per cohort. Cohort characteristics before and after propensity score matching are shown in Tables I and II.

Outcomes

At 6 months, there were no statistically significant differences in the rates of PE or intracranial bleeding between groups. However, anticoagulant therapy was associated with a statistically significant increased odds of gastrointestinal bleeding as well as increased mortality. For secondary outcomes, there was a statistically significant increase in mortality at 1 year within the anticoagulation cohort relative to the nonanticoagulation cohort (Table III). Discussion

Despite the CHEST and the European Society for Vascular Surgery recommendations on the treatment for IDDVT, there is significant variability in clinical practice. It has been our experience that many practitioners do not differentiate between IDDVT and more proximal DVT when considering clinical management. Many patients with IDDVT are empirically anticoagulated without clear risk stratification, perhaps due to convenience, concern over progression, or lack of familiarity with surveillance protocols. Although this treatment paradigm may require less diagnostic and imaging follow-up, anticoagulation is not entirely benign. Anticoagulation therapy carries well-documented risks, including gastrointestinal bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, and trauma-related complications—risks that are particularly relevant when the absolute benefit is uncertain.

Although IDDVT has historically been thought to be a lower-risk condition, some studies suggest a potential for progression to adverse outcomes such as recurrent VTE, proximal extension, and post-thrombotic syndrome.1 A large multicenter observational study by Bikdeli et al,6 using data from the RIETE registry, compared patients with IDDVT and proximal DVT who underwent anticoagulation therapy. Patients with IDDVT experienced lower 90-day and 1-year mortality, lower VTE progression, and a reduced incidence of post-thrombotic syndrome. However, as the majority of IDDVT patients in the cohort received anticoagulation, the benefit of therapy vs the natural history of the disease is uncertain. Whether similar favorable outcomes would be observed in untreated patients remains unclear and underscores the need for studies comparing anticoagulated and nonanticoagulated cohorts. Several randomized clinical trials have sought to clarify the role of anticoagulation in IDDVT. In one of the earliest studies, Lagerstedt et al7 demonstrated a lower recurrence rate of IDDVT in patients treated with 3 months of warfarin therapy following initial heparin therapy compared with patients receiving no long-term anticoagulation. However, this study predated modern imaging, risk stratification, and therapeutic techniques. In contrast, a study by Schwarz et al8 demonstrated no difference in progression or PE when comparing 10 days of low-molecular-weight heparin treatment vs compression therapy alone. More recently, CACTUS (Anticoagulant Therapy for Symptomatic Calf Deep Vein Thrombosis) evaluated 6 weeks of nadroparin in low-risk outpatients with symptomatic IDDVT. Their results found no decrease in thrombus propagation or thromboembolic events, but did report a significantly increased risk of bleeding in the anticoagulation group.9,10

In our propensity-matched retrospective cohort study, therapeutic anticoagulation did not decrease the incidence of PE. The incidence of gastrointestinal bleeding at 6 months and all-cause mortality at 6 months and at 1 year were also significantly higher in the anticoagulated group.

Overall, our study is consistent with the current CHEST guidelines and corroborates that anticoagulation for patients with IDDVT should be individualized according to symptom severity, thrombus characteristics, risk of extension, and bleeding risk rather than applied routinely to all patients. Compared with previous studies, our use of the TriNetX database provided an extensive network to conduct a large-scale, multicenter, cohort study in real-world medical practice. Furthermore, the implementation of propensity score matching offered more rigorous control of confounding variables that may alter observed outcomes.

Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. Like other database studies, our data are limited by the nature of the database, which lacks optimal granularity and may be subject to coding inaccuracies. Symptom severity, exact thrombus location, limited mobility, imaging availability, or patient preference are important for making treatment decisions and cannot be accounted for. Additional clinical factors such as inpatient vs outpatient status, recent hospitalization, trauma, thrombus burden, and propagation risk are not reliably captured in TriNetX. Medication-related limitations should also be considered. Anticoagulation exposure was identified using medication records, but medication dose, intended duration, adherence, interruptions, and therapeutic efficacy could not be confirmed. Laboratory measures such as the prothrombin time/international normalized ratio, activated partial thromboplastin time, anti-Xa levels, or heparin levels were not available in a manner sufficient to determine whether individual patients were therapeutically anticoagulated. Similarly, postmatch medication class distribution could not be reliably calculated within TriNetX and the percentage of each anticoagulant used in the treatment cohort is unknown.

The similar incidence of PE between cohorts should also be interpreted cautiously. Several explanations are possible, including the low absolute risk of PE in IDDVT, limited power to detect very small absolute differences, incomplete event capture, and residual confounding by indication. Patients selected for anticoagulation may have had a higher baseline risk for thromboembolic progression due to previously mentioned clinical factors. Immobility, recent hospitalization, and qualitative clinical data are also unavailable in coded databases such as TriNetX. Accordingly, similar PE rates may reflect treatment selection or partial risk mitigation among treated patients at higher risk rather than equivalence between treatment strategies.

Because TriNetX is an electronic health record-based network, PE, bleeding events, hospital encounters, and mortality occurring outside participating health care organizations may not be completely captured. Cause-specific mortality was not available, and the higher all-cause mortality observed in the anticoagulated cohort cannot be attributed to specific events such as anticoagulation, bleeding, PE, or any other mechanism. Accordingly, the observed mortality differences are unlikely to be explained solely by the difference in hemorrhagic complications. Although gastrointestinal bleeding was significantly more common among anticoagulated patients, the magnitude and timing of the mortality difference suggest that residual confounding by baseline illness severity likely contributed. This concern is supported by early separation of the Kaplan-Meier mortality curves, which may indicate baseline differences between cohorts despite propensity score matching, rather than a direct causal effect of anticoagulation.

Finally, due to the retrospective nature of the TriNetX database and limitations in available patient data, we could not evaluate patients in the nonanticoagulated cohort for serial duplex ultrasound surveillance, symptom resolution, or quality of life. Some matching characteristics such as body mass index were available for only a select number of patients and may introduce confounding bias. Coding-based definitions may also misclassify acute vs chronic DVT, symptomatic vs incidental thrombosis, and recurrent vs persistent diagnoses.

Conclusions

In this large retrospective cohort study of patients with IDDVT, therapeutic anticoagulation was not associated with a decrease in PE. These findings support the individualized, risk-stratified management of IDDVT rather than the routine anticoagulation of all patients. Prospective studies are needed to compare structured surveillance with anticoagulation while accounting for symptom severity, thrombus burden, mobility status, anticoagulation duration, and cause-specific mortality.
