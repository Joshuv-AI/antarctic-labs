Age and Stability Trade-offs in Remote Monitoring Systems
Nitya Sathyavageeswaran, Anand D. Sarwate, Roy D. Yates, Narayan Mandayam

Abstract
Timely information is important in a wide variety of Internet of Things (IoT) services in which a shared server must manage two competing tasks: (i) processing a queue of jobs, and (ii) generating status updates to a remote monitor. This creates a fundamental trade-off between queue stability and data freshness. In this work, we model this scheduling decision as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) with the objective of minimizing a weighted sum of the average Age of Information (AoI) and the average queue length. We show that the optimal scheduling strategy is a queue-dependent age threshold which is monotonic. The shape of the switching curve differs according to different priority regimes. Finally, we compare the optimal MDP policy against heuristic policies.



I
Introduction

In modern Internet of Things (IoT) systems, the utility of collected data degrades rapidly over time, making timely information delivery critical
[
1
]
.
As an example, consider an industrial application in which an installed device has a camera that is used to perform continuous high-speed quality control on an assembly line. Image processing is done locally and, to obtain high throughput, the local processor operates in a heavy-traffic regime.
However, the camera must also periodically transmit status updates to a monitor which is tracking the entire production line. Since these updates share the local processor, generating them temporarily degrades the primary image-processing job pipeline to a slower service. The severity of this delay depends on the type of update being sent; transmitting a simple summary update may be fast, but processing a high resolution frame or doing extensive post processing may be slow.

In heavy traffic, even this brief interrupt for an update risks queue instability. This creates a fundamental trade-off between system stability and data freshness at the monitor. In this paper we use the Age of Information (AoI)
[
2
]
metric to quantify the freshness of information at the monitor. An
always-update
policy prioritizes AoI but leads to queue instability, whereas an
always-serve
policy stabilizes the queue but allows AoI to grow unbounded. Balancing these extremes requires a state-aware scheduling approach.

In this paper we formulate the problem of balancing AoI and stability as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) in which a local shared processor must dynamically choose whether to service a queue holding an incoming stream of data processing jobs or generate a fresh status update for the monitor. We use a weighted sum of the average age and the expected queue length as a cost function. We show that the optimal scheduling policy follows a state-aware monotonic switching curve that adapts to different parameter regimes. We empirically compare the optimal policy to two simple heuristics: a Memoryless policy that randomly chooses to send an update, and a Myopic policy that minimizes the expected cost in the next time step. Because our optimal policy does not have a closed form expression, we identify a low-complexity heuristic that approximates the switching curve and demonstrates that it can achieve near-optimal performance.

II-A

Problem Formulation

The scheduler determines the server’s operating mode in each slot. We model the scheduler’s actions over time as a sequence
\mathbf{U}=(U_{1},U_{2},\ldots)
, where

\displaystyle U_{n}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{Generate Update (Update Mode)}\\
0&\text{Serve Queue (Fast Mode)}.\end{cases}

(1)

To use the AoI metric to measure timeliness, let
R_{n}=\max\{k\leq n\colon\text{update generated at }k\text{ delivered}\}
be the generation time of the most recent update at the monitor. The age at the monitor at slot
n
is
A_{n}=n-R_{n}
. The age increases by 1 in every slot where a successful update does not occur.
The average age is defined as
1
1

1

Actions occur slot by slot in discrete time but age is averaged continuously over the slot; when
A_{n}=a
, the average age over slot
n
is
a+1/2
.

\displaystyle\Delta=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(A_{n}+1/2).

(2)

For the data processing job queue, an arrival and departure can occur in the same slot. The evolution of the queue backlog
Q_{n}
is governed by arrivals
X_{n}
and departures
D_{n}
:

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}=Q_{n}-D_{n}+X_{n},

(3)

where the departure probability is given by

\displaystyle P(D_{n}=1\mid U_{n},Q_{n},X_{n})=\begin{cases}\mu(U_{n})&\text{if }Q_{n}+X_{n}>0\\
0&\text{if }Q_{n}+X_{n}=0,\end{cases}

(4)

where
\mu(0)=\mu_{\mathrm{f}}
and
\mu(1)=\mu_{\mathrm{s}}
. Similarly, we define the average queue length as:

\displaystyle\bar{Q}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}Q_{n}.

(5)

Our goal is to minimize the weighted sum of the average age and the average queue length. The average cost is defined as:

\displaystyle\bar{C}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(A_{n}+\frac{1}{2}+wQ_{n}),

(6)

where
w>0
is a weight parameter representing the penalty for queue backlog relative to staleness.

Given these ingredients, we formulate the problem as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). We define the state of the system as
S_{n}=(Q_{n},A_{n})
. The MDP consists of a tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{U},P,C)
:
s=(q,a)\in\mathcal{S}
, where
q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
a\in\mathbb{N}
are the countably infinite set of states, and
u\in\mathcal{U}
is the action defined in (
1
).

We assume job queue arrivals are a rate
\lambda
Bernoulli process, independent of data service and update generation. The transition function
P((q^{\prime},a^{\prime})\mid(q,a),u)
governs the probability of the transition
S_{n}\to S_{n+1}
. Since only one arrival or departure can happen per slot, the transition probabilities
P(q\to q^{\prime}\mid u)
conditioned on the action
U_{n}=u
are given as follows

\displaystyle P(q\rightarrow q^{\prime}\mid u)=\begin{cases}\lambda(1-\mu(u))&q^{\prime}=q+1\\
(1-\lambda)\mu(u)I_{\left\{q>0\right\}}&q^{\prime}=q-1\\
\begin{aligned} &1-\lambda(1-\mu(u))\\
&-(1-\lambda)\mu(u)I_{\left\{q>0\right\}}\end{aligned}&q^{\prime}=q\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(7)

Based on the chosen action, the joint state transitions are:

•

If
u=0
(Serve Queue), the age increases by 1:

\displaystyle P((q^{\prime},a+1)\mid(q,a),0)=P(q\to q^{\prime}\mid 0).

(8)

•

If
u=1
(Generate Update), the age resets to 1 with probability
\mu_{\mathrm{m}}
:

\displaystyle P((q^{\prime},1)\mid(q,a),1)

\displaystyle=\mu_{\mathrm{m}}P(q\to q^{\prime}\mid 1),

(9)

\displaystyle P((q^{\prime},a+1)\mid(q,a),1)

\displaystyle=(1-\mu_{\mathrm{m}})P(q\to q^{\prime}\mid 1).

(10)

We model the immediate cost
C
of taking action
U
in state
S
as

\displaystyle C_{n}=C(S_{n},U_{n})=A_{n}+\frac{1}{2}+wQ_{n}.

(11)

We want to find a policy
f^{*}
that minimizes the long term average cost given by

\displaystyle V_{f}(s)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\Eop_{f}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{N}C(S_{n},U_{n})\middle|S_{0}=s\right].

(12)

III
Heuristic Policies

We first analyze a few heuristic policies to understand the age/queue length tradeoff.

III-A

Memoryless inspection

In this policy, the scheduler makes its decisions independently of the system state. In every slot, the scheduler chooses to generate a status update with fixed probability
p
.
The average age at the monitor for this policy is given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{mem}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p\mu_{\mathrm{m}}}+\frac{1}{2}.

(13)

The detailed derivation of this average age is provided in appendix
VI-A
.
The effective service rate,
\mu_{\mathrm{mem}}
for this policy, which is the probability of a job departure from the queue in a slot is given by

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{mem}}

\displaystyle=p\mu_{\mathrm{s}}+(1-p)\mu_{\mathrm{f}}.

(14)

The queue evolves as a discrete-time Geo/Geo/1 queue. Using standard results for discrete-time Markov queues
[
17
]
, the steady-state average queue length is given by

\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{\mathrm{mem}}=\frac{\lambda(1-\mu_{\mathrm{mem}})}{\mu_{\mathrm{mem}}-\lambda}.

(15)

A fundamental requirement for the system is to maintain queue stability, ensuring that the average queue length
\bar{Q}
remains finite. Given the arrival rate
\lambda
and the effective service rate
\mu_{\mathrm{mem}}
, the system is stable if and only if
\lambda<\mu_{\mathrm{mem}}
. This implies that the update probability must satisfy

\displaystyle p<p_{\max}\triangleq\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{f}}-\lambda}{\mu_{\mathrm{f}}-\mu_{\mathrm{s}}}.

(16)

From (
13
), we see that the average age decreases as the update probability
p
increases. However, a higher
p
reduces the effective service rate which in turn increases the queue backlog. This competing dynamic implies there is a unique update probability
p^{*}
that optimally resolves this tradeoff, which is given in the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

For the Memoryless policy where updates are chosen with probability
p
in each slot, the probability
p^{*}
that minimizes the average cost
\bar{C}(p)
is

\displaystyle p^{*}=\min\left(1,\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{f}}-\lambda}{\Delta\mu+\sqrt{w\lambda(1-\lambda)\mu_{\mathrm{m}}\Delta\mu}}\right),

(17)

where
\Delta\mu=\mu_{\mathrm{f}}-\mu_{\mathrm{s}}
,
\lambda<\mu_{\mathrm{f}}
. If
\lambda\geq\mu_{\mathrm{f}}
, no stable policy exists.

The proof is provided in Appendix
VI-B
.

III-B

Myopic policy

In this policy the scheduler minimizes the expected cost in the next time slot. At any time
n
with state
S_{n}=(q,a)
, the scheduler selects the action
u\in\{0,1\}
that minimizes the one-step expected cost
\Eop\left[C_{n+1}\mid S_{n}=(q,a),U_{n}=u\right]
.

By evaluating the expected next-step costs for updating versus serving the queue (detailed in Appendix
VI-C
), we find that the Myopic scheduler behaves as a deterministic threshold policy. It generates a status update (
U_{n}=1
) if the current age
A_{n}=a
satisfies

\displaystyle a>\begin{cases}\frac{w\lambda(\mu_{\mathrm{f}}-\mu_{\mathrm{s}})}{\mu_{\mathrm{m}}}&\text{if }q=0\\
\frac{w(\mu_{\mathrm{f}}-\mu_{\mathrm{s}})}{\mu_{\mathrm{m}}}&\text{if }q>0.\end{cases}

(18)

Otherwise, the scheduler chooses to serve the queue (
U_{n}=0
). We denote the deterministic age threshold for a back-logged queue (
q>0
) as
a^{*}_{\mathrm{myopic}}=w(\mu_{\mathrm{f}}-\mu_{\mathrm{s}})/\mu_{\mathrm{m}}
.

To establish the stability of the Myopic policy, a standard approach would rely on Foster’s stability criterion
[
18
]
using a Lyapunov function such as
L(Q_{n},A_{n})=Q_{n}
. Basic versions of this criterion require the expected one-step drift to be strictly negative outside a finite set of states. However, under the Myopic Policy when
A_{n}>K
, the expected one-step queue drift,
\lambda-\mu_{\mathrm{s}}
is strictly greater than zero.
Also the age process lacks autonomy since the threshold changes when
Q_{n}=0
. To resolve this we adopt a methodology inspired by Yates et al.
[
19
]
and construct a fictitious autonomous age process. This lets us establish queue ergodicity using the Markov-modulated framework of Foss et al.
[
20
]
.

Theorem 1

(Myopic Stability region)
.

Under the Myopic scheduling policy, the queue is stable (positive recurrent) if the arrival rate satisfies the following condition:

\displaystyle\lambda<\frac{K\mu_{\mathrm{f}}\mu_{\mathrm{m}}+\mu_{\mathrm{s}}}{K\mu_{\mathrm{m}}+1},

(19)

where
K=\lfloor a^{*}_{\mathrm{myopic}}\rfloor
is the deterministic age threshold for a backlogged queue.

We construct a fictitious system applying a constant threshold
K
, letting age operate as an independent renewal cycle. By renewal reward theory, its effective service rate is

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{K\mu_{\mathrm{f}}+\mu_{\mathrm{s}}/\mu_{\mathrm{m}}}{K+1/\mu_{\mathrm{m}}}=\frac{K\mu_{\mathrm{f}}\mu_{\mathrm{m}}+\mu_{\mathrm{s}}}{K\mu_{\mathrm{m}}+1}.

(20)

By Corollary 1 of Foss et al.
[
20
]
, this independent system is stable if
\lambda<\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}
. Since the true Myopic policy clears backlogged jobs at least as fast as the fictitious system, it is inherently stable (full proof in Appendix
VI-D
).

IV
Optimal Policy Analysis

We now turn to finding the optimal policy
f^{*}
which minimizes the long term average cost in (
12
). We first define the discounted cost
V_{f,\beta}(s)
as follows:

\displaystyle V_{f,\beta}(s)=\Eop_{f}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta^{t}C(S_{t},U_{t})\middle|S_{0}=s\right],

(21)

where
s=(q,a)
is the initial system state and
\beta\in(0,1)
is the discount factor. We define the optimal discounted value function

\displaystyle V_{\beta}(s)=\inf_{f}V_{f,\beta}(s).

(22)

If the value function
V_{\beta}(s)
is finite, it satisfies the following discounted cost optimality equation:

\displaystyle V_{\beta}(s)

\displaystyle=\min_{u\in\{0,1\}}\Bigl(C(s,u)+\beta\sum_{s^{\prime}}P_{ss^{\prime}}(u)V_{\beta}(s^{\prime})\Bigr),

(23)

for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
, where
P_{ss^{\prime}}(u)
is the transition probability from state
s
to
s^{\prime}
under action
u
.

We can define a value iteration
V_{\beta,n}(s)
recursively as

\displaystyle V_{\beta,n}(q,a)

\displaystyle\quad=\min_{u\in\{0,1\}}\Bigl(C((q,a),u)+\beta\sum_{s^{\prime}}P_{ss^{\prime}}(u)V_{\beta,n-1}(s^{\prime})\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad=a+wq+\min\Bigl(\beta\Eop\left[V_{\beta,n-1}(Q^{\prime}_{0},a+1)\right],

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\beta\mu_{\mathrm{m}}\Eop\left[V_{\beta,n-1}(Q^{\prime}_{1},1)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\beta(1-\mu_{\mathrm{m}})\Eop\left[V_{\beta,n-1}(Q^{\prime}_{1},a+1)\right]\Bigr),

(24)

for any
n\geq 1
, with
V_{\beta,0}(s)=0
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
\beta\in(0,1)
, where
Q^{\prime}_{0}
and
Q^{\prime}_{1}
denote the next-slot queue lengths under Fast Mode and Update Mode, respectively.

Under Sennott’s conditions
[
21
]
(verified in Appendix
VI-E
), there exists a differential cost function
h(s)
and a stationary deterministic policy
f^{*}
satisfying the average cost optimality equation

\displaystyle g+h(s)=\min_{u\in\{0,1\}}\Bigl(C(s,u)+\sum_{s^{\prime}}P_{ss^{\prime}}(u)h(s^{\prime})\Bigr),

(25)

for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
, where
g
is the minimum average cost.

Remark 1

(Stability of the Optimal Policy)
.

Since the immediate cost function explicitly penalizes the queue length (
C(S_{n},U_{n})=A_{n}+1/2+wQ_{n}
with
w>0
), any policy that yields a finite average cost must inherently maintain a finite average queue length. In Section
III-A
we see that whenever
\lambda<\mu_{\mathrm{f}}
, a Memoryless policy with update probability
p<p_{\max}
stabilizes the queue and achieves a finite average cost. By definition, the optimal policy
f^{*}
achieves an average cost no greater than this Memoryless baseline. Therefore, the optimal policy guarantees a finite average queue length, and ensures strict system stability for any
\lambda<\mu_{\mathrm{f}}
.

IV-A

Relative Value Iteration (RVI)

To numerically evaluate the optimal policy
f^{*}
, we truncate the state space to a finite grid
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{trunc}}=\{0,\dots,Q_{\max}\}\times\{1,\dots,A_{\max}\}
. The optimal policy for the truncated MDP is identical to that of the original infinite MDP as
Q_{\max},A_{\max}\to\infty
, provided that the truncated MDP is
unichain

[
22
]
. Our truncated MDP satisfies this unichain condition because, under any stationary policy, the Markov chain consists of a single recurrent class. The state
(0,1)
can be reached from all other states
(q,a)\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{trunc}}
. This is because, from any state, there is a strictly positive probability of having a finite sequence of zero arrivals coupled with consecutive fast service completions, followed by a successful update.

We get the exact optimal policy over
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{trunc}}
by applying RVI
[
22
]
. The value function is initially set to
V_{0}(s)=0
for all states
s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{trunc}}
. At each iteration
n\geq 0
, we update:

\displaystyle V_{n+1}(s)

\displaystyle=\min_{u}\left[C(s,u)+\Eop\left[V_{n}(s^{\prime})\right]\right]-V_{n}(s_{\mathrm{ref}}),

(26)

where
s_{\mathrm{ref}}=(0,1)
is a fixed reference state. The iteration stops when the condition
\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{trunc}}}|V_{n+1}(s)-V_{n}(s)|<\epsilon
is satisfied.

which partitions the state space into two distinct operating regions. The blue region below the boundary indicates states where the optimal action is to serve the queue in Fast Mode (
u=0
) to clear backlog, and the red region above the boundary indicates states where the optimal action is to generate an update (

) to prioritize data freshness. As the penalty for queue backlog
w
increases, this monotonic boundary (age threshold) shifts upward, which indicates the scheduler’s hesitance to interrupt queue service for status updates.

IV-B

Low-Complexity Square-Root Heuristic

While the exact MDP formulation yields an optimal policy
f^{*}
, storing an infinite-sized lookup table is impractical for implementation on resource-constrained edge devices. Our empirical evaluations using RVI reveal that the optimal switching boundary exhibits a distinct square-root shape. To reduce the computational complexity, we propose a low-complexity heuristic based on a square-root curve.

We define the Square-Root Heuristic, denoted as
f_{\mathrm{sqrt}}
, which maps the current system state
S_{n}=(q,a)
to an action
u\in\{0,1\}
as follows:

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{sqrt}}(q,a)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }a>m\sqrt{wq}+b\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(27)

where
m>0
and
b\geq 0
are tunable parameters.

Here,
b
represents the baseline age tolerance of the system when the queue is empty (
q=0
). The parameter
m
governs the scheduler’s sensitivity to queue backlog. A larger
m
dictates a steeper initial penalty for the queued jobs, delaying status updates to prioritize queue stability. As shown by the dashed lines in Fig.
2
, and Fig.
3
, this heuristic uses just two parameters to approximate the optimal policy, avoiding the need for the lookup table over the infinite space. The optimal parameters
(m^{*},b^{*})
can be determined offline by applying ordinary least squares regression to the exact MDP switching boundary. Alternatively, for systems where computing the full MDP is computationally prohibitive, these parameters can be found directly via a low-dimensional grid search over the target stability region.

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied the fundamental trade-off between data freshness and queue stability in remote monitoring IoT systems. We formulate the scheduling problem as a Markov Decision Process, and show that the optimal policy for minimizing a weighted sum of Age of Information (AoI) and queue length follows a state-aware, monotonic switching curve. Our numerical evaluations reveal that this optimal boundary is well-approximated by a low-complexity square-root heuristic, except when the update channel is severely constrained. Furthermore, we compared the optimal approach against Memoryless and Myopic baselines, illustrating that state-aware scheduling not only improves the Pareto frontier but also drastically expands the stable operating region of the system.

VI-C

Derivation of the Myopic Threshold

To find the optimal myopic action, we first evaluate the expected next state age and queue length under each action. For the age evolution, conditioned on the current age
A_{n}=a
, we have:

\displaystyle\Eop\left[A_{n+1}\mid A_{n}=a,U_{n}=0\right]

\displaystyle=a+1,

(39)

\displaystyle\Eop\left[A_{n+1}\mid A_{n}=a,U_{n}=1\right]

\displaystyle=1\cdot\mu_{\mathrm{m}}+(a+1)(1-\mu_{\mathrm{m}})

\displaystyle=a+1-a\mu_{\mathrm{m}}.

(40)

For the expected queue length, the evolution depends on whether the queue is currently empty or has jobs waiting in line:

\displaystyle\Eop\left[Q_{n+1}\mid Q_{n}=q,U_{n}=u\right]

\displaystyle=q+\lambda-\mu(u)\quad\text{for }q>0,

(41)

\displaystyle\Eop\left[Q_{n+1}\mid Q_{n}=0,U_{n}=u\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda(1-\mu(u)).

(42)

The Myopic scheduler chooses to generate an update (
u=1
) if the expected next-step cost of updating is strictly less than the expected next-step cost of serving the queue:

\displaystyle\Eop\left[C_{n+1}\mid u=1\right]<\Eop\left[C_{n+1}\mid u=0\right].

(43)

We evaluate (
43
) for two cases based on the queue backlog.

Case 1 (
q>0
): Substituting (
39
), (
40
), and (
41
) into (
43
), we get:

\displaystyle(a+1-a\mu_{\mathrm{m}})+w(q+\lambda-\mu_{\mathrm{s}})

\displaystyle\quad<(a+1)+w(q+\lambda-\mu_{\mathrm{f}}).

(44)

Simplifying this expression yields the age threshold for a backlogged queue:

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle>\frac{w(\mu_{\mathrm{f}}-\mu_{\mathrm{s}})}{\mu_{\mathrm{m}}}.

(45)

Case 2 (
q=0
): Similarly, substituting the expected values for an empty queue (
42
) into (
43
) yields:

\displaystyle(a+1-a\mu_{\mathrm{m}})+w(\lambda-\lambda\mu_{\mathrm{s}})

\displaystyle\quad<(a+1)+w(\lambda-\lambda\mu_{\mathrm{f}}).

(46)

Simplifying this expression provides the threshold when the queue is empty:

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle>\frac{w\lambda(\mu_{\mathrm{f}}-\mu_{\mathrm{s}})}{\mu_{\mathrm{m}}}.

(47)

VI-D

Stability Analysis of the Myopic Policy

We establish the stability of the Myopic policy by applying Corollary 1 from Foss et al.
[
20
]
. We model the system as a Markov-modulated process
Z_{n}=(A_{n},Q_{n})
, where the age process
A_{n}
evolves autonomously but modulates the evolution of the queue
Q_{n}
. To account for the boundary conditions at
Q_{n}=0
, we introduce a
fictitious system

(\tilde{A}_{n},\tilde{Q}_{n})
where the scheduler applies a constant age threshold
K=\lfloor\frac{w(\mu_{\mathrm{f}}-\mu_{\mathrm{s}})}{\mu_{\mathrm{m}}}\rfloor
regardless of the queue state.

Following the framework in Foss et al.
[
20
]
, we verify the essential conditions for the stability of the fictitious system:

•

Condition A (Modulator Ergodicity): The age process
\{\tilde{A}_{n}\}
must be ergodic. In the fictitious system, the transition probabilities for the age are

\displaystyle P(\tilde{A}_{n+1}=a^{\prime}\mid\tilde{A}_{n}=a)

\displaystyle\quad=\begin{cases}1&a\leq K,a^{\prime}=a+1,\\
\mu_{\mathrm{m}}&a>K,a^{\prime}=1,\\
1-\mu_{\mathrm{m}}&a>K,a^{\prime}=a+1.\end{cases}

(48)

The age process
\{\tilde{A}_{n}\}
follows a renewal cycle that resets to state 1 with finite expected time
\Eop\left[\tau\right]=K+1/\mu_{\mathrm{m}}
with every successful update. Thus the age process is positive recurrent and has a unique steady-state distribution
\pi(a)
.

•

Condition B1 (Bounded Drift): There exists a constant
U
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{a,q}\Eop\left[|\tilde{Q}_{n+1}-\tilde{Q}_{n}|\mid\tilde{A}_{n}=a,\tilde{Q}_{n}=q\right]\leq U.

(49)

In our discrete-time model,
Q_{n+1}=(Q_{n}-D_{n}+X_{n})^{+}
. Since arrivals
X_{n}\in\{0,1\}
and departures
D_{n}\in\{0,1\}
,
|Q_{n+1}-Q_{n}|<=1
. Thus, the condition is satisfied with
U=1
.

•

Limiting Drift: We define the Lyapunov function
L_{2}(q)=q
and the conditional expected increment for the fictitious system as

\displaystyle D(a,q)=\Eop\left[L_{2}(\tilde{Q}_{n+1})-L_{2}(\tilde{Q}_{n})\mid\tilde{A}_{n}=a,\tilde{Q}_{n}=q\right].

(50)

This condition requires the existence of a limiting drift function
f(a)
dependent only on the modulator, such that

\displaystyle\lim_{q\to\infty}D(a,q)=f(a).

(51)

For our discrete-time system, when
\tilde{Q}_{n}\geq 1
, departures occur with probability
\mu(u)
and arrivals with probability
\lambda
. Under the Myopic policy, the service rate
\mu(u)
is deterministic for a given age
a
. Thus, the increment is strictly independent of
q
for all
q\geq 1
, yielding the exact limit

\displaystyle f(a)=\lambda-\mu(u)=\begin{cases}\lambda-\mu_{\mathrm{f}}&a\leq K,\\
\lambda-\mu_{\mathrm{s}}&a>K.\end{cases}

(52)

Since
D(a,q)=f(a)
for all
q\geq 1
, the conditional expected increment converges trivially in the
L_{1}
norm to the limiting drift function
f(a)
as
q\to\infty
.

By verifying the above conditions we establish that the fictitious joint process
(\tilde{A}_{n},\tilde{Q}_{n})
is stable (positive recurrent) if the average drift over the stationary distribution
\pi(a)
is negative:

\displaystyle\bar{D}=\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}f(a)\pi(a)<0

(53)

The stationary probabilities for the fictitious age process are
\pi(a)=\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{m}}}{K\mu_{\mathrm{m}}+1}
for
a\in\{1,\dots,K\}
. The probability of the system being in Update Mode (
a>K
) is
\pi_{\text{Update}}=\frac{1}{K\mu_{\mathrm{m}}+1}
. The stability condition becomes

\displaystyle\left(\frac{K\mu_{\mathrm{m}}}{K\mu_{\mathrm{m}}+1}\right)(\lambda-\mu_{\mathrm{f}})+\left(\frac{1}{K\mu_{\mathrm{m}}+1}\right)(\lambda-\mu_{\mathrm{s}})<0.

(54)

Rearranging terms yields the stability bound

\displaystyle\lambda<\frac{K\mu_{\mathrm{m}}\mu_{\mathrm{f}}+\mu_{\mathrm{s}}}{K\mu_{\mathrm{m}}+1}.

(55)

The original Myopic policy uses an age threshold
K_{0}<K
when
Q_{n}=0
. This lower threshold only increases the frequency of updates when the queue is already empty, which does not decrease the service rate of backlogged jobs. Consider both the original system
Q_{n}
and the fictitious system
\tilde{Q}_{n}
experiencing identical job arrivals. By design, the fictitious system provides a lower effective service rate. Thus its queue length acts as a strict upper bound for the original queue, ensuring
Q_{n}\leq\tilde{Q}_{n}
for all time slots
n
. Since the fictitious queue is proven to be positive recurrent, the smaller original system is inherently bounded and therefore positive recurrent as well.

Lemma 1
.

For every state
s=(q,a)
and discount factor
\beta\in(0,1)
, the discounted value function

is finite.

Proof.

By Sennott
[
21
]
, it is sufficient to show that there exists at least one stationary policy

that induces an irreducible, ergodic Markov chain yielding a finite expected average cost:

\displaystyle\sum_{s}\pi_{s}C(s,f(s))<\infty,

(56)

where
\pi_{s}
is the steady-state distribution of the Markov chain.

Consider the Memoryless inspection policy where the scheduler triggers an update with a fixed probability
p
in every slot. We choose
p
such that
0<p<\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{f}}-\lambda}{\mu_{\mathrm{f}}-\mu_{\mathrm{s}}}
.

Under this policy, the queue service rate is decoupled from the state and becomes a constant effective rate
\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}=p\mu_{\mathrm{s}}+(1-p)\mu_{\mathrm{f}}
. Our choice of
p
guarantees that the arrival rate is strictly less than the service rate (
\lambda<\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}
). Therefore, the queue evolves as a stable discrete-time Geo/Geo/1 queue. This queue is an irreducible, positive recurrent Markov chain with a finite steady-state expected length
\Eop\left[Q\right]<\infty
.

Simultaneously, the age process
A_{n}
resets to
1
with a strictly positive probability
p\mu_{\mathrm{m}}
in every slot, and increases by
1
otherwise. This forms a geometric renewal process. The age process is also positive recurrent with a finite steady-state expected age
\Eop\left[A\right]=\frac{1}{p\mu_{\mathrm{m}}}+\frac{1}{2}<\infty
.

The queue and age processes are positive recurrent and any state
(q,a)
can be reached (e.g., the queue can empty and the age can reset in a finite number of steps with positive probability), thus the joint Markov chain is irreducible and ergodic. The expected steady-state cost is

\displaystyle\sum_{s}\pi_{s}C(s,u)

\displaystyle=\Eop\left[A\right]+w\Eop\left[Q\right]<\infty.

(57)

Since there exists a policy with a finite average cost, the discounted cost

is finite for all
s
and
\beta
.
