The Burden of Transthyretin Amyloid Cardiomyopathy on Caregivers and Patients in Japan: A Cross‑Sectional Real‑World Survey
Authors: Hiroaki Kitaoka, Miwa Enami, Kazumasa Kamei, Jun Hamada, Cynthia Khanji, Jade Garratt-Wheeldon, Jack Wright, Akira Yuasa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTransthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) is a progressive disease predominantly affecting older men and is associated with increasing care needs. Previous international studies have reported that, as ATTR-CM progresses, caregivers experience substantial physical and emotional burden, as well as productivity impairment. However, real-world evidence on caregiver burden in Japan remains limited. This study aimed to characterize multidimensional caregiver and patient burden in Japan.MethodsData were drawn from the Adelphi ATTR Disease Specific Programme™, a cross-sectional survey of cardiologists and their consulting patients with ATTR-CM, conducted in Japan between October 2024 and January 2025. Cardiologists completed patient record forms, including patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and caregiving status. Patients and their non-professional caregivers also completed voluntary self-reported questionnaires. Caregiver outcomes included caregiving time, work productivity, and activity impairment (WPAI), and Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI) scores. Patient health status was assessed using the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ). Analyses were descriptive.ResultsTwenty-five physicians provided data for 120 patients (mean age 74.7 years; 78.3% male). Over half of patients (55.9%) had a caregiver, most commonly a spouse (72.6%). Caregivers (n = 22; mean age 64.0 years; 95.5% female) primarily lived with the patient (81.8%). Caregivers reported a median (interquartile range) of 20.0 (5.0–40.0) hours/week caregiving, and experienced substantial overall work productivity impairment [36.2% (SD 37.3%)] and daily activity impairment [47.7% (SD 31.5%)]. Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Previous international studies have reported that as transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) progresses, caregivers experience substantial physical and emotional burden, alongside productivity impairment.Although caregiver burden in ATTR‑CM has been documented internationally, real‑world evidence specific to Japan remains limited.This study aimed to describe the multidimensional burden of ATTR-CM on both patients and their caregivers in Japan using real-world data.What was learned from the study?In this real‑world Japanese survey, informal caregivers, who were predominantly female spouses caring for older male patients, experienced substantial burden including caregiving time commitments and activity impairment.These findings underscore the importance of considering caregiver impact when managing ATTR-CM, as well as the need to utilize supportive care within the Japanese healthcare and social care context to reduce household-level burden in the future.

Introduction

Transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) is a progressive and life-threatening disorder caused by the deposition of misfolded transthyretin protein in the myocardium, leading to heart failure and increased mortality [1]. The timely diagnosis of ATTR-CM remains challenging, with patients often experiencing prolonged intervals between symptom onset and definitive diagnosis [2, 3]. Clinical manifestations frequently overlap with other cardiovascular conditions, including hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, ischemic heart disease, and hypertensive heart disease [3, 4]. This results in many patients being diagnosed at an advanced stage, when irreversible structural and functional damage has already occurred.

As of 2019, the number of patients with ATTR-CM in Japan was estimated to be approximately 1500–1800, including 1468–1798 patients with wild-type transthyretin (TTR) and 50–61 patients with variant TTR [5]. Although ATTR-CM has traditionally been regarded as a rare disease, recent advances in imaging-based diagnostic techniques and updates to diagnostic criteria have led to an increase in the number of diagnosed cases, particularly of wild-type ATTR-CM. This increase likely reflects improved detection of previously unrecognized diseases rather than a true rise in incidence, suggesting that a substantial proportion of patients may have historically remained undiagnosed [6, 7].

Early diagnosis and timely treatment are essential not only for preventing disease progression and severe complications in patients with ATTR-CM but also for reducing the burden on caregivers. Importantly, early intervention, including disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) such as tafamidis, can improve prognosis, preserve patient autonomy, and mitigate caregiver burden [8]. However, a recent analysis of a Japanese claims database reported a delay between the onset of cardiac and extracardiac comorbidities and subsequent ATTR-CM diagnosis, highlighting persistent diagnostic challenges in clinical practice [9].

The more advanced disease that can result from diagnostic challenges may lead patients to experience substantial declines in quality of life (QoL), which may include deterioration in psychological well-being, such as anxiety and depression [10]. Disease severity, as assessed by patient-reported outcome measures such as the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ), is correlated with QoL [11], and patient health status measured by the KCCQ has also been shown to reflect broader domains of disease severity and frailty rather than symptom burden alone [12].

Prior international studies have also demonstrated that caregiver burden in ATTR-CM is closely linked to patients’ health status, including KCCQ scores, as well as specific symptom profiles and loss of independence in activities of daily living [11, 13–15]. However, most of this evidence, both for patients and caregivers, derives from non-Japanese populations. The extent to which these relationships translate to the Japanese context, where caregiving structures, family roles, and health and long-term care systems differ, remains insufficiently characterized.

To address this gap, the present study aims to describe the multidimensional burden of ATTR-CM on both patients and their caregivers in a real-world Japanese setting.

Methods

Study Design

A secondary data analysis was conducted using the Adelphi Real World ATTR Disease Specific Programme (DSP)™, a cross-sectional survey, with elements of retrospective data collection, of physicians, their patients, and caregivers. The data presented in this study were collected in Japan between October 2024 and January 2025. The DSP methodology has been previously described [16, 17], validated [18], and demonstrated to be representative and consistent over time [19], and is well-established, with studies implemented globally across many disease areas.

Physicians were recruited to participate by local fieldwork agents, following the completion of a short screening questionnaire. Physicians were eligible to participate if they were specialists in cardiology and were actively treating at least one patient with ATTR in a typical month. In Japan, patients with ATTR‑CM are predominantly managed by cardiologists in accordance with national clinical guidelines [1].

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they had a confirmed diagnosis of ATTR amyloidosis cardiomyopathy or a mixed cardiomyopathy–polyneuropathy phenotype and were aged 18 years or over at the time of the survey. Where appropriate, non-professional caregivers were included if they attended consultation with an eligible patient.

Data Collection

Physicians were invited to complete a patient record form for up to 10 consecutively consulting patients who visited them for routine care. The patient record form contained detailed questions on patient demographics and clinical characteristics including the New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification [20] and caregiver requirements.

Each patient for whom the physician provided a patient record form was then invited to independently complete a voluntary patient-reported questionnaire, and upon agreement provided their informed consent to participate. Patient-reported questionnaires contained detailed questions on QoL and several standardized patient-reported outcome measures, including the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment (WPAI) questionnaire [21] and the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) [22].

At the time of consultation, if present, non-professional caregivers of the same patients for whom the physicians completed a patient record form were invited to independently complete a voluntary caregiver self-completion survey, following separate informed consent. These surveys contain detailed self-reported information from the caregiver’s perspective regarding their experiences and the impact of the patient’s condition on their own life, as well as the caregiver WPAI and Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI) questionnaire [23].

Outcome Measures

As part of the patient-reported questionnaire, patients were asked to rate the extent to which ATTR-related problems negatively affected their QoL. For each item, responses were recorded on a five-point Likert scale using the following options: not at all, slightly, moderately, significantly, and extremely.

The NYHA classification system evaluates the severity of heart failure based on a patients physical limitations [15]. It includes four classes, ranging from Class I (no limitation) to Class IV (heart failure symptoms occur even at rest).

The WPAI questionnaire assesses work and activity impairment over the 7 days prior to survey. It consists of four domains: absenteeism (work time missed), presenteeism (impairment while working), overall work impairment, and overall activity impairment (activities not including work) [21]. Scores are expressed as impairment percentages and range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater levels of impairment.

The KCCQ is a 23-item self-administered instrument that measures health status in patients with heart failure. It consists of an Overall Summary Score, Total Summary Score, and Clinical Summary Score, as well as domain-specific scores: (1) physical limitation (6 items), which assesses the extent to which heart failure limits physical activities; (2) symptom frequency (4 items), which reflects how often patients experience symptoms such as shortness of breath and fatigue; (3) symptom burden (3 items), which evaluates how bothersome these symptoms are; (4) symptom stability (1 item), which assesses changes in symptoms over time; (5) self-efficacy (2 items), which assesses patients perceived ability to manage their heart failure; (6) quality of life (3 items), which captures the patients perceived impact of heart failure on enjoyment of life; and (7) social limitation (4 items), which measures the degree to which heart failure interferes with social interactions and activities [22]. Each domain score is calculated by summing item responses within the domain and transforming them to a standardized scale ranging from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better health status. The Total Symptom Score is calculated as the mean of the symptom frequency and symptom burden scores. The Clinical Summary Score is calculated as the mean of the physical limitation and total symptom scores. The Overall Summary Score is calculated as the mean of the physical limitation, total symptom, quality of life, and social limitation scores, and similarly ranges from 0 to 100.

The ZBI is a 22-item self-reported questionnaire which measures the burden experienced by caregivers. ZBI questions comprise 5 domains: (1) guilt (2 items), reflecting feelings of inadequacy or self-blame; (2) burden in the relationship (6 items), capturing strain in the caregiver-patient relationship; (3) emotional well-being (7 items), assessing psychological impact such as stress and frustration; (4) social and family life (4 items), measuring the impact on social activities and relationships; (5) finances (1 item); and (6) loss of control over one’s life (4 items), reflecting feelings of being overwhelmed. These domains are derived from overlapping item groupings rather than mutually exclusive subscales. Summary constructs of personal strain (12 items) and role strain (6 items) were additionally derived from overlapping subsets of items [23]. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (“never”) to 4 (“almost always”), with total scores ranging from 0 to 88 and higher scores indicating greater caregiver burden. Scores are interpreted as follows: 0–20 no to mild burden, 21–40 mild to moderate burden, 41–60 moderate to severe burden, and ≥ 61 severe burden.

All patient and caregiver questionnaires (ZBI, WPAI, KCCQ) were administered in their officially validated Japanese-language versions to ensure cultural and linguistic appropriateness.

Data Analysis

Analyses were descriptive. Mean and standard deviation (SD) were calculated for continuous variables, and frequency counts and percentages for categorical variables. Data were also stratified descriptively by NYHA class, and patients were grouped into NYHA Class I, II, and III+ .

All analyses were conducted in UNICOM® Intelligence Reporter version 7.5.1. Missing data were not imputed; therefore, the number of patients for each analysis could vary from variable to variable and was reported separately for each analysis.

Ethical Approval

Participants provided informed consent at the time of data collection using a checkbox. Data were collected in such a way that patients and physicians could not be identified directly. Physician and patient data were pseudo-anonymized, and a code was assigned when data were collected. Data were aggregated and de-identified before being shared for analysis and publication.

Data collection was undertaken in line with European Pharmaceutical Marketing Research Association guidelines [24]. This research was submitted to and received ethical exemption from the Pearl Institutional Review Board (IRB study number 2024–0328). Each survey was performed in full accordance with relevant legislation at the time of data collection, including the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects [25], the US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act 1996 [26], and Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act legislation [27].

Results

Study Population

Twenty-five physicians participated in the survey, most of whom were general cardiologists (84.0%), followed by heart failure specialists (12.0%). The participating physicians provided data for 120 patients with ATTR-CM, of whom 32 (26.7%) completed the patient self‑completion questionnaire. Among 62 patients identified as having a caregiver, 22 caregivers (35.5%) completed the caregiver survey. The overall study population and measured outcomes are illustrated in Fig. 1. Among patients for whom data were available (n = 111), physicians reported that 55.9% had a caregiver. Of these patients (n = 62), 72.6% were receiving care and support from their partner or spouse, 22.6% from their child aged over 18 years old, and 8.1% from their parent or guardian. Physicians reported that 12.9% (n = 8) of patients were receiving care from a professional caregiver. When stratified by NYHA class, 43.3% of patients in class I, 56.9% in class II, and 75.0% of patients in class III+ were reported to have a caregiver. Whereas 0% of patients in NYHA class I were receiving care from a professional caregiver, 13.5% in class II and 25.0% in class III or higher were receiving care from a professional caregiver.

Among patients for whom data were available on activities of daily living (n = 58), physicians reported that patients were receiving help from their caregiver with transportation (58.6%), ambulating (44.8%), managing medications (41.4%), shopping and meal preparation (31.0%), and house cleaning and home maintenance (31.0%). Other activities of daily living for which caregivers were providing help included feeding (20.7%), toileting (19.0%), dressing (17.2%), personal hygiene (17.2%), and continence (17.2%).

Reasons for Treatment Choice

Physicians were asked about the key reasons that influenced the prescription of their patients ATTR DMT treatment (Fig. 4). Among patients prescribed DMTs at the time of survey (n = 99), the top reasons for treatment choice included intention to slow/delay disease progression (96.0%), good tolerability (70.7%), and intention to maintain QoL (64.6%). Discussion

Based on physician-, patient- and caregiver-reported data, we found a high reliance on informal caregiving, resulting in work and activity impairment for both patients and caregivers, as well as negative impacts on QoL. Notably, the patient population was predominantly older adults (mean age of 74.7 years), which amplifies vulnerability to functional declines and frailty. While caregiver-related results should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size, the findings nonetheless highlight the dual burden of ATTR-CM on patients and caregivers.

High Reliance on Informal Caregiving Among Older Adults

We found that over half of patients (55.9%) had a caregiver, most commonly a partner or spouse, indicating that caregiving responsibilities are largely managed within households. Caregiver involvement varied across NYHA classes, with 43.3% of patients in class I, 56.9% of those in class II, and 75% of those in class III or higher having a caregiver. Only 12.9% of patients received support from professional caregivers; notably, all patients who were receiving professional support were NYHA class II or higher. This NYHA-dependent pattern is consistent with international evidence showing increasing care needs with worsening functional status of patients in ATTR-CM [28, 29].

Caregiver Burden, and Productivity Loss and Household-Level Impact

Caregivers had a mean age of 64.0 years, were predominantly female (95.5%), and most (81.8%) were living with the patient. This distribution, the male predominance of ATTR-CM and the common role of spouses as caregivers, aligns with observations from previous Japanese and international studies [11, 30, 31]. The median weekly caregiving time in our study was 20.0 h, despite our cohort including a large proportion of early-stage patients (NYHA I/II). In contrast, a previous study conducted across seven countries found a median of 4.5 h overall (2.0 h for NYHA class I/II and 17.5 h for NYHA class III) [29]. However, given our cross-sectional design and the small caregiver sample, this observation should be interpreted with caution, and further longitudinal research is needed to confirm whether significant caregiver burden develops early in the disease course. The limited use of professional services in the present study may reflect cultural expectations of family caregiving as well as the structure of social support and long-term care systems in Japan [32], and suggests that most of the burden of ATTR-CM is absorbed within households rather than by formal care systems. Historically, long‑term care in Japan has relied heavily on family members—particularly spouses and daughters‑in‑law—and, although public long‑term care insurance was introduced in 2000 to mitigate this burden, informal caregiving within households remains a central pillar of care provision [32].

This time commitment may contribute to disruptions in daily activities (caregivers reported a mean activity impairment of 47.7%) and work productivity loss among caregivers who are employed (36.2%). This finding should be interpreted in the context of the dual burden experienced by caregivers, who balance household responsibilities and employment, while also managing their own health problems (42.9%). These combined challenges suggest that long caregiving hours and limited external support may affect their physical and mental strain, further impacting the caregiver’s ability to maintain both household and work-related roles. A previous international study targeting patients with DMT‑naïve ATTR‑CM and their caregivers reported that 35% of caregivers experienced at least mild ZBI burden [29], whereas our cohort showed that 85.0% experienced at least mild ZBI burden, including more caregivers in the moderate to severe and severe categories of ZBI. These findings should be interpreted with caution, as differences in country, study period, treatment status, and healthcare and caregiving systems may influence observed caregiving patterns and burden.

In the present study, the mean KCCQ overall summary score was 65.1, which was broadly consistent with that reported in other ATTR-CM clinical trial and observational populations [28–31]. Despite this, caregivers in our cohort reported ZBI scores and caregiving time commitments that were not consistent with those described in previous international studies. This finding suggests that caregiver burden is not determined solely by disease severity, and that factors such as healthcare system characteristics, availability of formal care services, and caregiving structures may substantially modify the relationship. In context to this, Cappelli et al. reported from an international real‑world survey of patients with untreated ATTR‑CM and their caregivers that the KCCQ overall summary score was the strongest independent predictor of caregiver burden as measured by the ZBI [23]. While this highlights the importance of patient‑perceived symptom burden at the individual level, our findings suggest that the magnitude of caregiver burden associated with a given level of patient health status may vary across countries and care settings. Together, these results indicate that caregiver burden in ATTR‑CM reflects not only patient health status but also the broader healthcare and caregiving environment. Further studies are needed to better understand how these contextual factors shape caregiver experience across different systems.

ATTR-CM has been reported to be underdiagnosed and to have a higher prevalence than previously recognized, and the number of patients diagnosed with ATTR-CM is expected to increase further in the future in Japan [33, 34]. Consequently, both the number of caregivers and their burden are likely to rise, underscoring the need not only for early diagnosis and treatment of ATTR-CM but also for strengthening existing support for caregivers.

Comprehensive Approaches to Maintaining Patient Independence and Reducing Functional Decline

Patients in the present study reported a moderate or greater impact on QoL from tiredness/low energy (59.4%) and loss of independence (62.5%), which suggests a substantial deterioration in day-to-day functioning. Fatigue, reduced physical functioning, and role limitations are consistently reported as drivers of impaired QoL among patients with ATTR-CM [11].

While DMTs have demonstrated significant benefits in improving prognosis, several clinical trials have also shown DMTs to slow the deterioration of KCCQ scores in patients with ATTR‑CM [35–38]. In the present study, physicians frequently cited maintaining QoL (64.6%) and preserving the ability to perform activities of daily living (47.5%) as important reasons for treatment selection, in addition to slowing disease progression (96.0%). This indicates that treatment goals extend beyond survival to encompass daily functioning and QoL. Consistent with these goals, the need for assistance with daily activities increased with disease severity, particularly from NYHA class II onward, highlighting the functional burden of ATTR‑CM and the importance of early intervention. A previous Japanese real-world study reported that exercise tolerance still declines over time in older patients receiving treatment, likely due to frailty progression [39]. This emphasizes that, in addition to pharmacologic therapy, supportive strategies such as cardiac rehabilitation, nutritional support, and multidisciplinary care should be considered early, especially for older patients, to maintain functional capacity and QoL [39, 40].

Limitations

While this real-world study provides important insights into aspects beyond the scope of clinical trials, including the burden experienced by patients and caregivers, several important limitations should be acknowledged. First, the DSP did not employ random selection of physicians or patients. Although participating physicians were required to meet predefined eligibility criteria, participation was voluntary and dependent on physicians’ willingness to complete the survey, which may have introduced selection bias. Second, physicians were instructed to recruit consecutive patients to minimize selection bias; however, patients with more frequent healthcare visits and/or greater diseases severity, may have been overrepresented in the study population. In addition, as with all questionnaire-based studies, recall bias cannot be ruled out because some responses relied on participants’ memories. However, the data used in this study were collected during routine patient appointments, which may have helped reduce this likelihood. Third, only a subset of patients and caregivers completed the voluntary self‑reported questionnaires, which may introduce selection bias and limit the representativeness of patient‑ and caregiver‑reported outcomes. As a result, the estimates of patient and caregiver burden may be subject to variability and should be interpreted with caution. Fourth, although the cohort consisted predominantly of older adults, individual nursing care level and frailty assessments were not performed, which limits our ability to fully capture the overall burden of ATTR-CM, particularly among older patients. Finally, as a descriptive, cross-sectional study, this analysis was not designed to evaluate causal relationships between disease severity, patient outcomes, and caregiver burden. Therefore, any results should not be interpreted as causal. Taken together, these limitations may affect the generalizability of the findings, and therefore future studies with larger sample sizes and longitudinal designs are warranted to confirm these results. Despite such limitations, real-world studies play an important role in highlighting areas of concern which are not adequately addressed in clinical trials, such as patient and caregiver burden.

Conclusion

This real-world study describes the multi-dimensional burden of ATTR-CM among a sample of patients and their informal caregivers in Japan. Our findings indicate that caregivers, who were predominantly female spouses caring for older male patients, experienced substantial time commitments and activity impairment. Although this study was descriptive in nature, with a relatively small sample size, the results demonstrate caregiver burden in a real‑world Japanese setting, where caregiving structures and long‑term care systems differ from those reported in prior international studies. These findings underscore the importance of early diagnosis and timely treatment of ATTR‑CM, alongside strengthening existing support for caregivers, to reduce the overall burden of the disease in Japan.

Ethical Approval

Participants provided informed consent at the time of data collection using a checkbox. Data were collected in such a way that patients and physicians could not be identified directly. Physician and patient data were pseudo-anonymized, and a code was assigned when data were collected. Data were aggregated and deidentified before being shared for analysis and publication. Data collection was undertaken in line with European Pharmaceutical Marketing Research Association guidelines. This research was submitted to and received ethical exemption from the Pearl Institutional Review Board (IRB study number 2024–0328). Each survey was performed in full accordance with relevant legislation at the time of data collection, including the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects, the US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act 1996, and Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act legislation.
